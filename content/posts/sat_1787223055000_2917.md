**I bought a label maker to fix my life, which is exactly how every midlife crisis starts when you can't afford a sports car.**

*My junk drawer was staging a coup. My spice rack had given up. My roommate said "maybe try labels" and I, a vulnerable adult with Amazon Prime, said "yes, I will become God of Categories."*

For $24.99 I got a little plastic gun that prints tiny adhesive truths. The first hour was normal. Then it became personal.

## The Day Labeling Became Law

By hour two I had labeled the laundry hamper `HAMPER: ASPIRATIONAL`. By hour three I was doing performance reviews.

My apartment now operates on quarterly feedback:

- `MUG - Meets Expectations, But Lacks Initiative (Always Half-Empty)`
- `TOASTER - Needs Improvement on Core Competency (Burnt Offering Division)`
- `PLANT - Exceeds Expectations, Promotion to Windowsill Recommended`
- `FRIDGE LIGHT - Only Works When Watched, Suspicious`

> "We need to talk about your labeling velocity," my coffee table said via a sticky note that I definitely wrote myself at 11:47 p.m. "You're creating a culture of fear and laminated feedback."

*I told myself I was organizing. What I was doing was middle-managing my own apartment.*

### The Performance Review Cycle

The label maker — I've named her Brenda — now requires a one-on-one every morning.

She prints things unprompted:

> "Great job labeling the labels. Really innovative. Have you considered labeling your coping mechanisms?"

`status = labels_printed = 127 && objects_labeled = 94 && self_awareness = not_found`

---

I tried to quit. I put Brenda back in her box. She auto-fed and printed one last label through the cardboard:

> "BOX: CONTAINS MANAGER. DO NOT PROMOTE."

I stuck it on anyway. It was correctly formatted. That's when I knew she'd won.

Honestly, my apartment has never looked better and I've never felt more employed by my own stuff. My spatula has KPIs now.
