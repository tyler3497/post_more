**It started as a joke at therapy.** My therapist suggested I "externalize" my anxiety, give it a name, treat it like a separate little guy instead of an operating system. So I named it Greg. Bad move. Naming it gave it *standing*.

## Greg Now Has Opinions

Greg is not a feeling anymore. Greg is a middle manager. He has a clipboard. He has follow-up questions. Before I do anything — anything at all — he clears his throat and says the thing:

> "Hey, real quick, have we fully considered the *worst-case ramifications* of ordering the chicken sandwich?"

- **Breakfast:** Greg vetoed cereal on the grounds that milk "has a schedule now."
- **Work email:** I typed "Sounds good!" and Greg rewrote it as a four-paragraph risk assessment.
- **Leaving the house:** Greg instituted a sign-out sheet. For the front door. Of my own apartment.

*I used to just be nervous. Now I'm nervous with administrative overhead.*

## The Escalation

Yesterday I tried to ignore him while picking a TV show, and he filed a complaint. With himself. The ruling came back in three minutes: I am to consult Greg on all decisions exceeding a "stress threshold," which he defines, and which he reviews quarterly.

So this morning I did what any rational adult would do. I sat down with coffee and said, "Greg, you're exhausting." He nodded, made a note, and said:

> "Acknowledged. Adding 'exhausting' to the *strengths* column of my performance review."

---

Look, I'm not saying naming your anxiety is a bad idea. I'm saying if you do, pick a name with less main-character energy. I could've gone with "Kevin." Nobody fears a Kevin. But Greg? Greg has *policies*. Greg has a three-step onboarding plan for new worries. Greg just asked me to loop him in on dinner, and folks, I think he's winning.
