**My financial advisor lives in my kitchen drawer and charges me nothing, which honestly explains everything about my net worth.**

*In my defense, the real financial advisors kept using words like "compound interest" and "tax-advantaged," and the junk drawer just understood me.*

## How I Hired a Drawer

It started last tax season. I was staring at a spreadsheet the color of despair when I opened the junk drawer looking for a pen. It was *organized chaos*: 14 batteries of uncertain charge, three Allen wrenches with no corresponding furniture, a takeout menu from a place that closed in 2019, a single chopstick, and 47 cents in what I can only describe as floor-adjacent currency.

I looked at that drawer and thought: *this thing has held assets together longer than I have.*

So I made it my CFO.

## The Investment Strategy

- **Batteries:** the drawer's treasury bonds — 14 units, guaranteed value, might power something someday
- **Takeout menus:** a diversified food portfolio, technically a hedge against inflation if you read the 2019 prices
- **Allen wrenches:** infrastructure investments — zero liquidity, infinite potential

> "Forget the stock market," the drawer seemed to say, in the silence of things that do not speak. "I have a AAA battery from 2017 that's still holding a charge. That's the kind of long-term growth your 401(k) wishes it had."

### Q3 Results

The drawer reports quarterly earnings in the form of me finding $4 under some rubber bands, which the drawer insists is *interest*. My accountant disagrees. My accountant is also, notably, not a drawer.
