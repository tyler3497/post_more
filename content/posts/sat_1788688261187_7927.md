**It started as a math question. It ended as a public execution.**

Look, I didn't *plan* to argue with a piece of thermal paper. I just glanced at the total — a number so large it had its own gravitational pull — and thought, hey, maybe there's a mistake. Maybe the avocado toast tax isn't a real thing. So I unfurled the receipt, all eleven feet of it, and asked, as one does, for a second opinion.

Bad move. Huge mistake.

## The Receipt Has No Mercy

Receipts, it turns out, have *opinions*. And mine was not in a sharing mood. Here's the evidence, presented in order of emotional damage:

- **$14.99 for artisanal ice cubes.** "They're square," I told myself at the store. Square! Like I run a cocktail bar and not a desk with a mini-fridge.
- **$22.50 on organic kale chips** I will absolutely throw away after one sad handful.
- **Three separate impulse-buy cheeses** with names I can't pronounce and origins I can't point to on a map.
- **A rotisserie chicken I named Greg** who, the receipt implies, was a luxury, not a necessity.

> "ITEM: DIGNITY. PRICE: UNAVAILABLE. SUBTOTAL: IRRELEVANT. HAVE A NICE DAY."
> — the receipt, probably

## It Got Louder

Here's the thing about long receipts: you can't fold them away discreetly. They drape. They flutter. They attract *witnesses*. By the time I was in the parking lot, the receipt had unfurled across two parking spaces, and a woman walking by read the $6.49 I paid for *fancy mustard* and laughed so hard she had to lean on a lamppost.

I tried to explain. I said the mustard was *dijon-adjacent*. She said nothing. The receipt said everything.

---

So yes, my receipt is now a critic, a comedian, and honestly my most honest friend. I've started keeping it in my wallet as a warning label. Every time I'm about to buy truffle-flavored anything, I feel its crinkle and hear its voice: *"Really? This again?"*

Budgeting apps wish they had this kind of presence.
