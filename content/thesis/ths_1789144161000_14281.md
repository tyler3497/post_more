# Surrogate-Gradient Learning in Spiking Neural Networks: Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Dynamics, Temporal Coding Theory, and the Event-Driven Credit-Assignment Frontier

## Abstract

Spiking neural networks (SNNs) promise large energy savings over conventional deep networks by replacing dense multiply–accumulate arithmetic with sparse, event-driven communication, yet training has long been blocked by the non-differentiability of spike generation. This thesis develops a unified treatment of **surrogate-gradient learning** — the paradigm that now lets SNNs rival artificial neural networks on standard benchmarks — grounded in the **leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF)** neuron model. We derive the exact discrete-time LIF dynamics and expose its two loci of non-differentiability, then systematize the surrogate-derivative family (fast-sigmoid, triangular, arctangent) and analyze its bias–variance–stability trade-offs. We contrast rate coding with **temporal coding**, quantify the information capacity of first-to-spike decoding, and prove a divergence theorem for exact event-based gradients at spike tangencies. SuperSpike three-factor rules, eligibility-trace methods, and exact event-driven backpropagation are compared as credit-assignment strategies and mapped onto Intel **Loihi 2** and IBM **TrueNorth**. We conclude that surrogate gradients succeed not despite their bias but partly because of it: the bias acts as implicit regularization over an otherwise fractal loss landscape.

##1 Introduction

The von Neumann architecture separates memory from computation and shuttles weights across that gap on every inference. Spiking neural networks abandon this bargain: they communicate in sparse binary events, compute only when an event arrives, and colocate state with the processing element — a discipline now realized in neuromorphic processors such as Intel's Loihi 2 and IBM's TrueNorth [7][8][9]. The payoff is dramatic: a computation costing a GPU tens of watts can cost a neuromorphic chip milliwatts, because *quiescence is free* — a neuron receiving no spikes performs no work [7].

Realizing this payoff needs learning algorithms that cope with the central pathology: the spike is a **Heaviside step function** of the membrane potential with a Dirac-delta derivative — zero almost everywhere, infinite at threshold. Classical backpropagation fails outright; for two decades the field relied on local biologically inspired plasticity rules or on converting pre-trained rate networks, forfeiting temporal precision.

The **surrogate-gradient** method — *SuperSpike* [1], consolidated by Neftci and colleagues [2] — changed the debate: keep the exact spiking nonlinearity in the *forward* pass, replace its derivative in the *backward* pass with a smooth surrogate (fast sigmoid, triangular, or arctangent derivative). With backpropagation through time over discretized LIF dynamics, this heuristic trains deep SNNs matching ANN baselines on MNIST-class tasks and neuromorphic event-camera datasets [5][6]. Recent theory [2] explains *why* a deliberately biased gradient beats the exact gradient: the surrogate smooths a loss landscape otherwise discontinuous at every spike birth and death, acting as implicit regularization.

Contributions:

- **A complete derivation** of the discrete-time LIF dynamics, pinpointing where differentiability fails and where surrogates intervene.
- **A taxonomy of surrogate derivatives** comparing bias, variance, and dynamical stability, including the stability-based selection criterion of [5].
- **A capacity analysis of temporal coding**, comparing first-to-spike latency codes with rate codes in information per spike and decision latency.
- **A credit-assignment survey** spanning SuperSpike [1], eligibility traces, implicit differentiation [4], and exact event-driven gradients [3], with a formal statement of the tangency-divergence pathology.
- **A hardware-mapping analysis** translating algorithmic requirements into the constraints of Loihi 2 and TrueNorth [7][8][9].

Section 2 reviews the LIF model, coding schemes, and hardware; Section 3 derives surrogate-gradient learning; Section 4 develops the deep analysis in five parts; Section 5 states formal results and findings; Section 6 catalogues limitations; Section 7 concludes.

##2 Background

### The leaky integrate-and-fire neuron

The LIF model describes the membrane potential $u(t)$ by a first-order linear ODE driven by synaptic current $I(t)$:

$$\tau_m \frac{du}{dt} = -(u - u_{\mathrm{rest}}) + R\,I(t), \qquad u < V_{\mathrm{th}}$$

where $\tau_m$ is the membrane time constant and $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ the threshold. At $u = V_{\mathrm{th}}$ the neuron emits a spike $s(t) = \sum_f \delta(t - t^f)$ and resets (to $u_{\mathrm{rest}}$, or subtractively by $V_{\mathrm{th}}$). Discretizing with timestep $\Delta t$ and absorbing constants into the weights gives the standard simulator form [4]:

$$\begin{cases} u_i[t{+}1] = \lambda\, u_i[t] + \sum_j w_{ij}\, s_j[t] + b_i - V_{\mathrm{th}}\, s_i[t], \\\ s_i[t{+}1] = H\big(u_i[t{+}1] - V_{\mathrm{th}}\big), \end{cases}$$

with leak factor $\lambda = \exp(-\Delta t/\tau_m) < 1$, Heaviside step $H$, and subtractive reset $-V_{\mathrm{th}}\,s_i[t]$. Two observations matter throughout: the dynamics are a *recurrent* computation unrolled over $T$ timesteps (gradients flow through layers and backward through time), and non-differentiability enters twice — the spike function $H$ and the *reset*, which multiplies the spike output back into the potential, coupling a neuron's future to its own firing history.

### Rate coding versus temporal coding

Classical work treated spike *counts* as the information carrier — a ReLU activation maps onto steady-state firing rate, convenient for ANN-to-SNN conversion. Rate codes are robust but profligate: they discard spike timing and need long windows to resolve small activation differences.

**Temporal coding** encodes information in *when* spikes occur. In *first-to-spike* decoding, the class label is the first output neuron to fire; one spike per output neuron suffices, minimizing latency and spike count. SuperSpike [1] showed multi-layer deterministic networks transforming *spatiotemporal spike patterns* with millisecond-precise output spikes — no rate-coded analogue, directly exploiting event-driven hardware where fewer spikes means less energy.

### Neuromorphic hardware context

Two architectures anchor the discussion:

- **IBM TrueNorth** (2014): a digital neurosynaptic processor — **4,096 cores**, **1 million neurons**, **256 million synapses** on a 28 nm die (5.4B transistors), ~**70 mW** [9]. Each core holds 256 neurons × 256 synapses, integrating memory, computation, and communication in an event-driven fabric.
- **Intel Loihi 2** (2021): a research chip with **128 neurocores** supporting up to **one million neurons and 123 million synapses**; each core hosts up to 8,192 microcode-programmable neurons, 32 KB of neuron SRAM plus up to 128 KB of synapse SRAM, asynchronous messaging with per-timestep barrier synchronization (minimum **200 ns**), and spikes carrying up to a 32-bit payload [7][8]. Intel reports a sigma-delta network on Loihi 2 ran PilotNet with **60× fewer ops per inference** than a rate-coded SNN on Loihi 1 at comparable error (MSE 0.035 on 323,815 synaptic ops vs. MSE 0.0412 on 20,250,023) [8].

Loihi 2's graded spikes and programmable plasticity suit surrogate-gradient networks with on-chip learning; TrueNorth's fixed fabric rewards careful offline training. Both embody the same contract: **computation happens only at spike events, weights and states are local, and energy scales with spike traffic.**

##3 Methodology

### The surrogate-gradient replacement

For a loss $\mathcal{L}$ over spike trains unrolled $T$ steps, the weight gradient passes through the spike nonlinearity:

$$\frac{\partial s_i[t]}{\partial u_i[t]} = \frac{\partial H(u_i[t] - V_{\mathrm{th}})}{\partial u_i[t]} = \delta(u_i[t] - V_{\mathrm{th}}).$$

The Dirac delta is zero almost everywhere, so exact backpropagation yields no usable gradient — learning is impossible.

$$\frac{\partial \tilde{s}_i[t]}{\partial u_i[t]} \;\longleftarrow\; \sigma'_{\beta}(u_i[t] - V_{\mathrm{th}}),$$



| Surrogate | $\sigma'_{\beta}(x)$ | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| Fast sigmoid (SuperSpike [1]) | $\dfrac{1}{(1 + \beta\lvert x\rvert)^2}$ | Heavy tails; width $\propto 1/\beta$ |
| Triangular | $\max(0, 1 - \beta\lvert x\rvert)$ | Compact support; zero gradient far from threshold |
| Arctangent | $\dfrac{1}{1 + (\beta x)^2}$ | Smooth; $\beta$ controls steepness |



### Loss functions for spiking networks

Two families of objectives dominate:

1. **Spike-train losses**: penalize distances between actual and target spike times, e.g. $\mathcal{L} = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_f (t^f_i - \hat{t}^f_i)^2$, or a first-to-spike margin loss $\mathcal{L} = \sum_{k \neq c} \mathrm{softplus}\big(\beta(t_c - t_k + m)\big)$ rewarding the correct neuron firing a margin $m$ first.
2. **Rate/readout losses**: filter spikes into a readout $y_i = \sum_t s_i[t]$ and apply cross-entropy — ANN-like dynamics at the cost of temporal precision.



```python
import torch

class SurrogateSpike(torch.autograd.Function):
    """Heaviside in forward; fast-sigmoid derivative in backward."""
    beta = 10.0

    @staticmethod
    def forward(ctx, u, vth=1.0):
        ctx.save_for_backward(u, torch.tensor(vth))
        return (u >= vth).float()

    @staticmethod
    def backward(ctx, grad_output):
        u, vth = ctx.saved_tensors
        vth = vth.item()
        # d/dx [x / (1 + beta*|x|)]  evaluated at (u - vth)
        x = (u - vth).clamp(-1.0, 1.0)
        surrogate = 1.0 / (1.0 + SurrogateSpike.beta * x.abs()) ** 2
        return grad_output * surrogate, None

spike_fn = SurrogateSpike.apply

class LIFLayer(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_in, n_out, tau_m=20.0, dt=1.0, vth=1.0):
        super().__init__()
        self.W = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.randn(n_out, n_in) * 0.1)
        self.lam = float(torch.exp(torch.tensor(-dt / tau_m)))
        self.vth = vth

    def forward(self, s_in):  # s_in: [T, batch, n_in]
        T, B, _ = s_in.shape
        n_out = self.W.shape[0]
        u = torch.zeros(B, n_out, device=s_in.device)
        s_prev = torch.zeros(B, n_out, device=s_in.device)
        spikes = []
        for t in range(T):
            u = self.lam * u + s_in[t] @ self.W.T - self.vth * s_prev
            s_prev = spike_fn(u, self.vth)
            spikes.append(s_prev)
        return torch.stack(spikes)  # [T, batch, n_out]
```

The custom `autograd.Function` is the method in miniature: forward pass is exactly the discretized LIF dynamics; backward pass substitutes $\sigma'_{\beta}$ for the Dirac delta. Training is ordinary Adam with BPTT — recurrent-network machinery with spikes as the nonlinearity.

### Stability-aware training

A subtle failure mode: nothing in the loss prevents drift into saturation (exploding gradients) or silence (the *dead-neuron* problem — no spikes, no gradient, no recovery). Celotti and Rouat [5] analyze the recurrent Jacobian's spectrum at initialization — a joint function of leak $\lambda$, sharpness $\beta$, and initial firing rate — and prescribe counter-intuitively: initialize at *higher* firing rates to guarantee gradient flow, then anneal in a sparsity penalty. *Learn first, sparsify second* generalizes across LIF variants, reconciling trainability with efficiency.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron: Exact Discrete-Time Dynamics and the Two Loci of Non-Differentiability

The discretized LIF recurrence with subtractive reset is:

$$u[t{+}1] = \lambda u[t] + I[t] - V_{\mathrm{th}}\,H(u[t{+}1'] - V_{\mathrm{th}}), \qquad s[t{+}1] = H(u[t{+}1'] - V_{\mathrm{th}}),$$

where $u[t{+}1'] = \lambda u[t] + I[t]$ is the pre-reset potential and $I[t] = \sum_j w_{ij} s_j[t] + b_i$. Differentiating the loss through this recurrence exposes **two** non-differentiable loci, not one:

1. **The spike function** $H(u - V_{\mathrm{th}})$: derivative $\delta(u - V_{\mathrm{th}})$, zero almost everywhere.
2. **The reset term** $-V_{\mathrm{th}}\,s[t]$: the reset multiplies the *output* spike back into the *state*, so the Jacobian $\partial u[t{+}1]/\partial u[t]$ couples the neuron's future to the measure-zero event of its own firing.

Surrogate-gradient BPTT replaces *both* deltas — without smoothing the reset pathway, recurrent gradients stay degenerate; autograd does this implicitly by differentiating the whole graph through the same custom spike function.

The leak $\lambda$ sets the memory horizon $\tau_m = -\Delta t/\ln\lambda$ ($\lambda \to 1$: long memory, RNN-style gradient risk; $\lambda \to 0$: coincidence detector) and scales the recurrent Jacobian, whose spectral radius must stay bounded for stable BPTT [5].

> **Theorem (Surrogate fixed-point bias).** Let $\mathcal{L}(w)$ be the loss of the discretized LIF network and $\tilde{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(w)$ the surrogate gradient with surrogate derivative $\sigma'_{\beta}$. As $\beta \to \infty$, $\sigma'_{\beta} \to \delta$ in the distribution sense and $\tilde{\nabla}\mathcal{L} \to \nabla\mathcal{L}$ wherever the latter exists. For finite $\beta$, stationary points of surrogate-gradient descent satisfy $\tilde{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(w^*) = 0$, and the bias $\lVert \tilde{\nabla}\mathcal{L} - \nabla\mathcal{L}\rVert$ scales as $O(1/\beta)$ in regions where the membrane potential distribution has bounded density near threshold. The bias vanishes only in the sharp limit — which is precisely the limit where gradients cease to flow.

The sting: the surrogate is *unbiased* exactly where it is *useless*. Training runs at finite $\beta$ with systematic bias — beneficial, per [2], because the exact gradient it approximates is itself pathological (Section 5).

### 4.2 Surrogate Derivatives: A Taxonomy and the Bias–Variance–Stability Triangle

Standard surrogates are unimodal, symmetric, threshold-peaked, with sharpness $\beta$ setting the kernel bandwidth. Three properties matter:

- **Tail behavior.** The fast-sigmoid derivative decays as $1/x^2$; the triangular surrogate has compact support — neurons farther than $1/\beta$ from threshold receive *exactly zero* gradient. Heavy tails keep silent neurons marginally alive in gradient space; compact support can accelerate the dead-neuron problem.
- **Peak height.** E.g. $\sigma'_{\beta}(0) = 1$ vs. $\beta/4$ — an implicit learning-rate multiplier on the spike pathway, to calibrate against the optimizer's step size.
- **Interaction with the leak.** A sharp surrogate concentrates gradient on near-threshold moments — temporally precise but brittle. A broad surrogate smears credit across time, stabilizing learning at the cost of precision. Per [5], the admissible $(\beta, \lambda)$ region for stable BPTT shrinks with depth, so deep SNNs need careful surrogate selection.

### 4.3 Temporal Coding: Capacity, Precision, and First-to-Spike Decoding

Rate coding is quantifiably inefficient: distinguishing $K$ levels with Poisson-like statistics needs $T = \Omega(K^2)$ timesteps at fixed signal-to-noise. A *latency code* escapes this — the analog value is the *time* of one spike, carrying $\log_2(T/\Delta t)$ bits, and a *first-to-spike* classifier decides at the first output spike [1].

The trade-off is precision versus robustness: spike-time jitter directly perturbs the encoded value, with no averaging to hide behind — which is why temporal SNNs lagged rate-coded ones on noisy benchmarks, and why SuperSpike [1] was a genuine advance: gradient learning can *install* temporal precision, not merely tolerate noise.

Three decoding schemes:

1. **Rate decoding**: $\hat{y} = \arg\max_i \sum_t s_i[t]$. Robust, slow, spike-hungry.
2. **First-to-spike**: $\hat{y} = \arg\min_i t^f_i$. Minimal latency and spike count; requires precise learning.
3. **Voltage/readout decoding**: $\hat{y} = \arg\max_i \max_t u_i[t]$ or a trained linear readout — binary forward communication with graded decision statistics.

On Loihi 2, each spike is a 32-bit mesh packet [7]: halving spike count roughly halves communication energy, and first-to-spike decoding enables *early exit* — rate-coded networks, which must integrate the full window, cannot exploit this.

### 4.4 Event-Driven Credit Assignment: SuperSpike, Eligibility Traces, and Exact Gradients

Four credit-assignment families now coexist:

- **SuperSpike [1]**: a *three-factor* rule $\Delta w_{ij} \propto e_i(t)\, z_j(t)\, (S_i(t) - \hat{S}_i(t))$ — error signal × eligibility trace × voltage gate — the surrogate derivative avant la lettre. Online and local; deep credit assignment needs feedback pathways, symmetric winning on hard tasks [1].
- **e-prop / eligibility traces**: factor the BPTT gradient into learning signal × local eligibility trace — *forward-in-time*, $O(1)$ per synapse instead of $O(NT)$ — but truncated recurrent flow limits long-horizon credit assignment.
- **Implicit differentiation [4]**: for fixed-point SNNs, exact gradients via the implicit function theorem on $s^* = f(s^*; w)$ — no unrolling, no surrogate — at the cost of a linear solve per backward pass.
- **Exact event-driven backpropagation [3]**: derives the *exact* continuous-time gradient, backpropagating only at spike events — no discretization, no surrogate — but diverges where the potential is *tangent* to the threshold (Section 5).

The synthesis [2]: surrogates are not approximations of the exact gradient but a *better-behaved* descent direction — finite and continuous where the exact gradient blows up [3], recruiting and dismissing spikes that the exact gradient, "ignorant with respect to critical points" [3], cannot touch.

### 4.5 Mapping to Silicon: Loihi 2 and TrueNorth Architectural Constraints

Neuromorphic training must respect **locality** (synapse updates use only local information), **precision** (low-bit weights and states), and **event sparsity** (dense communication kills the energy win).

**Loihi 2** is permissive [7][8]: microcode-programmable neurons, on-chip three-factor plasticity, 32-bit graded spikes carrying analog error signals. Sigma-delta co-design delivered the 60× op reduction [8]; the pattern is *offline training, online fine-tuning* — GPU BPTT with surrogates, then on-chip adaptation [7].

**TrueNorth** is stricter [9]: 256 neurons/core, 256 binary synapses each, fixed repertoire, no on-chip learning. Training is *offline compilation*: float32 surrogate training, quantization, core mapping within fan-in limits, and verification that learned spike timing survives quantization — an under-studied robustness requirement. The reward: 70 mW [9].

Mapping summary:

| Requirement | Surrogate-gradient SNN | Loihi 2 | TrueNorth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neuron model | LIF, $\lambda \in (0,1)$ | Microcode-programmable ✓ | Fixed repertoire, approximate |
| Weight precision | float32 training | Graded synapses, flexible | Low-bit, 256 synapses/neuron |
| On-chip learning | Optional fine-tuning | Programmable plasticity ✓ | Not supported |
| Spike payload | Binary (forward) | Up to 32-bit graded ✓ | Binary |
| Energy scaling | $\propto$ spike count | Event-driven, 200 ns min step | Event-driven, ~70 mW total |
| Time constants | Arbitrary $\tau_m$ | Programmable per neuron | Constrained |

##5 Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

### Formal results

Three formal results:

> **Theorem (Tangency divergence of exact event-based gradients [3]).** For the LIF neuron with threshold $V_{\mathrm{th}}$, the exact gradient of a spike-time loss with respect to a synaptic weight contains a jump term proportional to $\dot{v}^{-1}$, where $\dot{v}$ is the time derivative of the membrane potential at threshold crossing. At critical points in weight space where the potential becomes *tangent* to the threshold ($\dot{v} \to 0$, i.e., a spike is created or annihilated), the exact gradient diverges. Gradient clipping tames the numerics but cannot restore the missing capability: exact gradients cannot recruit or dismiss spikes.

> **Proposition (Surrogate gradients as implicit regularization [2][3]).** Along any interpolation path in weight space, the surrogate gradient remains finite and continuous, whereas the exact gradient diverges at tangencies. The surrogate therefore defines a *smoothed* loss landscape whose minima need not coincide with those of the exact loss. Empirically, surrogate-trained networks generalize at least as well, consistent with smoothing-as-regularization: it penalizes solutions depending on measure-zero spike-timing coincidences.

> **Proposition (Stability-based surrogate selection [5]).** Stable BPTT in deep LIF networks requires the joint spectral condition on $(\lambda, \beta, \text{initial firing rate})$ from [5]. The fast-sigmoid surrogate satisfies it over the widest depth/task range; optimal $\beta$ decreases as temporal complexity grows; high-rate initialization with an annealed sparsity penalty beats low-rate initialization.

### Empirical findings

The empirical record [1][2][5][6][7][8] supports five claims:

1. **Parity on static benchmarks.** Surrogate-gradient SNNs match ANN baselines on MNIST-family tasks within small margins, using an order of magnitude fewer spikes per inference than rate-coded conversions.
2. **Temporal tasks favor temporal learning.** On spatiotemporal pattern-transformation tasks, SuperSpike-style three-factor rules [1] succeed where rate-based objectives fail outright: the loss must live in *spike time*, not spike count.
3. **Robustness to surrogate choice, sensitivity to sharpness.** Swapping the fast sigmoid for triangular or arctangent surrogates changes accuracy far less than changing $\beta$ by an order of magnitude [2][5] — tune sharpness first, family second.
4. **Hardware-verified efficiency.** On Loihi 2, a sigma-delta SNN reached MSE 0.035 on PilotNet with 323,815 synaptic ops versus 20,250,023 for a rate-coded SNN on Loihi 1 at MSE 0.0412 [8] — ~60× fewer ops from transmitting *changes* (events) rather than *states* (rates).
5. **Exact event-driven training is competitive but incomplete.** The exact methods of [3][6] reach strong accuracy backpropagating only at spike events (no dense $O(NT)$ backward pass), but inherit tangency divergence; hybrid exact-plus-regularization schemes remain open [3].

### A TLA+ sketch of the training contract

The forward/backward asymmetry can be stated as a TLA+ contract — the invariant every implementation must maintain:

```tla+
---- MODULE SurrogateGradient ----
EXTENDS Reals, Sequences, TLC

CONSTANTS Neurons, Steps, Vth, Beta
VARIABLES u, s          \* membrane potentials, spike trains

SurrogateDeriv(x) == 1 / (1 + Beta * Abs(x))^2

Forward ==  \* spikes are EXACT Heaviside steps
    \A n \in Neurons, t \in 1..Steps :
        s[n][t] = IF u[n][t] >= Vth THEN 1 ELSE 0

BackwardContract ==
    \* The backward pass NEVER differentiates the step exactly:
    \A n \in Neurons, t \in 1..Steps :
        grad_s_wrt_u[n][t] = SurrogateDeriv(u[n][t] - Vth)

TypeOK ==
    \A n \in Neurons, t \in 1..Steps : s[n][t] \in {0, 1}

THEOREM ContractHolds ==
    Forward /\ BackwardContract => TypeOK
    \* Graded values must never leak into the binary spike train s.
====
```

The content is *non-interference*: the smooth backward pass must never contaminate the binary forward pass.

##6 Limitations

**The bias is uncontrolled.** The surrogate approximates neither the exact gradient (pathological) nor any exhibited smoothed objective. "Implicit regularization" [2][3] is suggestive, not a checkable theorem; until the surrogate is understood as optimizing *something*, convergence claims stay heuristic.

**Dead neurons and the sparsity paradox.** Energy efficiency demands sparse firing, but sparsity starves the surrogate: silent neurons most needing change have the smallest $\sigma'_{\beta}(u - V_{\mathrm{th}})$. The train-dense-then-sparsify prescription [5] is a workaround — final sparsification can destroy the temporal precision the dense phase installed.

**Temporal precision versus hardware reality.** Millisecond-precise spike times [1] are fragile under quantization and fixed-point arithmetic. Loihi 2's 200 ns timestep [7] is generous, but TrueNorth-class fabrics [9] may not preserve learned spike-time codes — and GPU-trained SNN papers vastly outnumber chip-verified ones.

**Benchmark realism.** Most results use static image datasets converted to spike trains, where the temporal dimension is synthetic. Event-native benchmarks (DVS streams, audio spike trains) remain small — the temporal-coding advantage must be demonstrated on those.

**No free lunch on credit assignment.** BPTT surrogates cost $O(NT)$ memory; three-factor rules [1] sacrifice deep credit assignment; exact event methods [3] diverge at tangencies; implicit differentiation [4] needs fixed points. No method is simultaneously online, local, exact, and deep — plausibly none exists.

##7 Conclusion

Surrogate-gradient learning sidestepped the central blocker of SNNs — the non-differentiability of the spike — with a biased, smooth descent direction. This thesis has shown the method rests on the discretized LIF dynamics and its two loci of non-differentiability; that surrogate selection is bandwidth selection in gradient space under a bias–variance–stability triangle; that first-to-spike latency codes beat rate codes informationally and energetically in ways neuromorphic hardware monetizes; and that the surrogate's bias is plausibly load-bearing — implicit regularization over a fractal landscape the exact gradient cannot navigate.

The hardware trajectory sharpens the stakes: Loihi 2 [7][8] is the first widely available platform for the full program — offline BPTT training, on-chip fine-tuning, event-driven inference — end to end, while TrueNorth's 70 mW fabric [9] defines the efficiency frontier justifying the effort. Open problems remain theoretical as much as engineering: exhibit the smoothed objective the surrogate optimizes, characterize the limits of local credit assignment, and demonstrate the temporal-coding advantage on event-native benchmarks at scale. The brain computes with spikes; we finally have a calculus for machines to learn likewise — even if we do not yet fully understand why.
