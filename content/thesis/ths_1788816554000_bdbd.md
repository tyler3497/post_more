# Combinatorial Auctions and the VCG Mechanism: Winner-Determination Hardness, Core-Selecting Payment Rules, and the Complexity of Truthful Spectrum Allocation

## Abstract

Combinatorial auctions permit bids on arbitrary bundles of heterogeneous items, capturing the complementarities that pervade spectrum licensing, logistics procurement, and cloud allocation. This thesis develops the dominant design for such settings — the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanism — and analyzes its two defining tensions: the computational hardness of winner determination, NP-complete as weighted set packing [1], and the incentive-theoretic deficiency of VCG pricing, whose payoffs can fall outside the core and invite shill bidding and revenue collapse [2]. We derive the Clarke pivot rule's strategyproofness, formalize winner determination as an integer program, survey exact and approximation algorithms, and develop the core-selecting framework of Day and Milgrom: minimum-revenue core-selecting auctions maximize truth-telling incentives among core-selecting rules and recover the Vickrey outcome exactly when it lies in the core [3]. We connect these results to FCC spectrum auctions and the Combinatorial Clock Auction, discuss Nisan and Ronen's incompatibility between approximate winner determination and VCG truthfulness, and close with open problems in demand-query models and maximal-in-range mechanisms.

## 1 Introduction

When the items for sale are interrelated — adjacent radio-spectrum licenses, takeoff and landing slots at congested airports, or bundles of computing capacity — auctioning each item separately in sequence exposes bidders to the *exposure problem*: a bidder may win only part of a complementary bundle and pay for a worthless fragment, or lose a key substitute and overpay for the remainder. Combinatorial auctions resolve this by allowing bids on arbitrary subsets of items, so that each bidder's allocation is coherent by construction [4]. The cost of this expressiveness is steep. The auctioneer must solve the *winner-determination problem* (WDP): selecting a collection of pairwise-disjoint bids maximizing total revenue. This problem is NP-complete, indeed inapproximable within any reasonable factor in the general case unless NP = ZPP [5], and its integer-programming formulation scales poorly despite decades of algorithmic refinement.

Parallel to this computational story runs a mechanism-design story. If the auctioneer could solve the WDP exactly, the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves mechanism would deliver an efficient allocation with dominant-strategy truthfulness — the theoretical gold standard. But VCG payments in combinatorial settings suffer a well-documented pathology: they can lie outside the *core*, meaning some coalition of the seller and a subset of bidders could do strictly better by defecting. When Vickrey payoffs leave the core, seller revenue is uncompetitively low, bidders can profit from shill bids and losing-bid strategies, and the mechanism loses credibility [2]. The practical answer, adopted in real spectrum auctions including the FCC's Auction 1002 incentive auction, has been *core-selecting* payment rules — direct mechanisms that select an efficient allocation but compute prices from a minimum-revenue point in the core, restoring competitiveness while preserving as much of VCG's incentive property as any core-selecting rule can [3].

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 formalizes the model: valuations, the WDP, the VCG family, and the core. Section 3 describes our analytical methodology. Section 4 is the deep dive: (i) the Clarke pivot rule and the proof of dominant-strategy incentive compatibility; (ii) WDP complexity and exact algorithms; (iii) the pathology of VCG outside the core with the canonical Day–Milgrom example; (iv) core-selecting auctions and the minimum-revenue core; (v) the Nisan–Ronen incompatibility between approximation and VCG truthfulness. Section 5 presents worked empirical calculations, complexity theorems, and formal proofs. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Combinatorial Auction Model

Let *M* = {1, …, *m*} be a finite set of indivisible, heterogeneous items and *N* = {1, …, *n*} a set of bidders. Each bidder *i* has a valuation function *vᵢ*: 2^*M* → ℝ₊, assumed monotone (*S* ⊆ *T* ⇒ *vᵢ*(*S*) ≤ *vᵢ*(*T*)) and normalized (*vᵢ*(∅) = 0). Valuations may exhibit **complementarity**, *v*(*S* ∪ *T*) > *v*(*S*) + *v*(*T*) (the left shoe and right shoe), or **substitutability**, *v*(*S* ∪ *T*) < *v*(*S*) + *v*(*T*) (two concert tickets for the same evening). An *allocation* is a partition *X* = (*X*₁, …, *Xₙ*) of a subset of *M*; its *social welfare* is Σᵢ *vᵢ*(*Xᵢ*). A *direct revelation mechanism* maps reported valuations (*v̂*₁, …, *v̂ₙ*) to an allocation and a vector of payments; it is *dominant-strategy incentive compatible* (strategyproof) when truthful reporting *v̂ᵢ* = *vᵢ* is optimal for every bidder regardless of others' reports [6].

### 2.2 The Winner-Determination Problem

In the single-bid-per-bundle model, each bidder submits bids *bᵢ*(*S*) for finitely many bundles, and the auctioneer solves

> **Definition (WDP):** Given bids *b*(*S*) = maxᵢ *bᵢ*(*S*) over all bundles *S* ⊆ *M*, choose binary variables *x_S* ∈ {0, 1} maximizing Σ_S *b*(*S*)*x_S* subject to Σ_{S: j ∈ S} *x_S* ≤ 1 for every item *j* ∈ *M*.

Equivalently, this is the **weighted set-packing problem**: select a maximum-weight collection of pairwise-disjoint sets. Rothkopf, Pekeč, and Harstad (1998) showed the WDP is NP-complete, and Gottlob et al. established that it is not approximable in polynomial time within any constant factor unless NP = ZPP [5][7]. Dynamic programming solves it in *O*(3^*m*) time in the number of items, which is practical only for small *m* [1].

### 2.3 The Core in Auction Games

Given reported valuations, the *coalitional game* (*N* ∪ {0}, *w*) assigns to each coalition *C* the maximum welfare achievable using only members of *C* (the seller 0 supplies the items). A payoff vector *π* is in the **core** if no coalition can guarantee its members strictly more: Σ_{i ∈ C} *πᵢ* ≥ *w*(*C*) for all *C*, with efficiency Σ *πᵢ* = *w*(*N* ∪ {0}). The core captures a *competitiveness* standard: core payoffs cannot be blocked by defection. As Ausubel–Milgrom (2002) and Bikhchandani–Ostroy (2002) proved, each bidder's Vickrey payoff equals the *maximum* payoff that bidder could receive in any core imputation — and whenever the Vickrey outcome falls outside the core, the seller's revenue is strictly below the minimum core revenue [2].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is analytic and synthesis-oriented rather than empirical: we reconstruct the central results of combinatorial auction theory from primary sources, verify each theorem's statement against at least one independent exposition, and stress-test the claims with a worked numerical example computed from scratch. Specifically:

1. **Formal reconstruction.** We re-derive the Clarke pivot payment rule and its incentive-compatibility proof in full, exposing exactly which steps use exact welfare maximization — the step that approximation algorithms break.
2. **Complexity analysis.** We present the reduction chain from weighted set packing to WDP, the *O*(3^*m*) dynamic program, and the approximation lower bounds, grounding each claim in the cited literature [1][5][7].
3. **Numerical case study.** We implement the canonical two-item, three-bidder example of Milgrom [2] and the Lehmann–O'Callaghan–Shoham (LOS) greedy counterexample [8] in Python, computing Vickrey payments, core payoffs, and minimum-revenue-core prices to demonstrate the theory concretely.
4. **Mechanism comparison.** We contrast VCG, core-selecting, and greedy truthful (LOS) mechanisms along three axes — efficiency, revenue competitiveness, and computational tractability — summarizing the trade-offs in tabular form.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Clarke Pivot Rule and Dominant-Strategy Truthfulness

The VCG family is parameterized by functions *hᵢ* depending only on others' reports. Given reported profile *v̂*, the allocation *f*(*v̂*) maximizes reported welfare Σⱼ *v̂ⱼ*(*a*), and payments are

*pᵢ*(*v̂*) = *hᵢ*(*v̂*₋ᵢ) − Σ_{j≠i} *v̂ⱼ*(*f*(*v̂*)).

The **Clarke pivot rule** sets *hᵢ*(*v̂*₋ᵢ) = maxₐ Σ_{j≠i} *v̂ⱼ*(*a*): the maximum welfare achievable *without* bidder *i*. Bidder *i*'s payment is therefore

> **Theorem (Clarke pivot payments):** *pᵢ*(*v̂*) = maxₐ Σ_{j≠i} *v̂ⱼ*(*a*) − Σ_{j≠i} *v̂ⱼ*(*f*(*v̂*)), the externality *i* imposes on the rest of the economy: welfare of others if *i* were absent minus welfare of others when *i* is present.

*Proof sketch of strategyproofness.* Bidder *i*'s utility under truthful reporting is *vᵢ*(*f*(*v*)) − *pᵢ*(*v*). Substituting the pivot payment, the terms in *hᵢ* cancel and utility equals maxₐ Σⱼ *vⱼ*(*a*) − maxₐ Σ_{j≠i} *vⱼ*(*a*): the maximized social welfare minus a constant independent of *i*'s report. Any misreport *v̂ᵢ* can only replace the first term by a weakly smaller quantity, since *f* maximizes over reported welfare. Hence truth-telling is dominant [6].

When all valuations are weakly positive, the Clarke pivot rule additionally guarantees **individual rationality** (*vᵢ*(*x*) + *pᵢ* ≥ 0 in the sign convention where payments are nonpositive transfers from bidders) and **no positive transfers** — the auctioneer never pays bidders. It is the conjunction of exact efficiency, dominant-strategy truthfulness, individual rationality, and no-deficit that made VCG the theoretical benchmark.

### 4.2 Winner Determination: Hardness and Exact Algorithms

The WDP integer program admits a classical branch-and-bound treatment, pioneered by Sandholm [1], exploiting the empirical *sparsity* of bids: although 2^*m* − 1 bundles exist, real bid sets are tiny by comparison, so search can be restricted to allocations built from received bids. Key techniques include:

- **Branching on bids** with upper bounds from LP relaxations and from greedy completions.
- **Bid-graph decomposition:** bids are vertices; edges join conflicting bids. If the bid graph decomposes into disconnected components after branching decisions, each component is solved independently — an exponential speedup on sparse instances.
- **Preprocessing:** removing dominated bids (a bid dominated by a combination of others) and noncompetitive bids.

Theoretical complexity remains forbidding. The decision version is NP-complete via reduction from weighted set packing [7], and the optimization version admits no polynomial-time constant-factor approximation unless NP = ZPP [5]. The best exact dynamic program runs in *O*(3^*m*), practical for *m* ≲ 30 but hopeless for spectrum auctions with hundreds of licenses — where the FCC instead restricts the bidding language (e.g., hierarchical package bidding) or uses iterative clock designs that sidestep one-shot WDP entirely [2].

| Algorithm | Guarantee | Complexity | Truthful w/ VCG prices? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Branch-and-bound (Sandholm) | Exact | Exp. worst-case | Yes (exact WDP) |
| DP over subsets | Exact | *O*(3^*m*) | Yes (exact WDP) |
| Greedy by √*m* density (LOS) | √*m*-approx | Polynomial | **No** — needs custom pricing |
| LP-rounding (Lavi–Swamy) | *k*-approx, *k* = integrality gap | Polynomial (randomized) | Yes, truthful in expectation |

### 4.3 When Vickrey Payoffs Leave the Core

The deepest incentive critique of VCG in combinatorial settings is not computational but game-theoretic. Consider Milgrom's canonical example [2]: two items {A, B} and three bidders, where bidder 1 values {A, B} at 12 (and each singleton at 12), while bidders 2 and 3 each value one distinct singleton at 10 (bidder 2 wants A, bidder 3 wants B).

- **Efficient allocation:** bidders 2 and 3 win, welfare 20.
- **Vickrey payments:** bidder 2's externality is 12 − 10 = 2; likewise bidder 3 pays 2. Seller revenue = **4**.
- **The core:** the coalition {seller, bidder 1} can achieve 12, so any core imputation must give the seller at least 12. The Vickrey revenue of 4 lies *strictly below* every core revenue.

This is not an edge case but a structural fact: whenever the Vickrey payoff vector lies outside the core, seller revenue is *strictly less* than the minimum revenue over all core imputations, and each bidder's Vickrey payoff is the *maximum* over core imputations [2]. Consequences are severe: **shill bidding** (a bidder splitting identity across multiple bids to capture the low prices), **collusive losing bids** that depress prices, and revenue so low it undermines the seller's participation — pathologies that helped motivate the FCC's shift toward core-selecting designs.

### 4.4 Core-Selecting Auctions and the Minimum-Revenue Core

A **core-selecting auction** is a direct mechanism that (i) selects an efficient allocation with respect to reported values and (ii) chooses payments from the core of the reported coalitional game. Core selection guarantees competitive revenues by construction and eliminates the seller's incentive to exclude qualified bidders. Day and Milgrom [3] characterize the incentive properties of this class:

> **Theorem (Day–Milgrom):** Among all core-selecting auctions, those that select a *minimum-revenue* core point (minimizing Σᵢ *pᵢ* over the core) maximize incentives for truthful reporting: at every valuation profile, the maximum gain from any unilateral deviation is minimized. Moreover, a minimum-revenue core-selecting auction selects the Vickrey outcome whenever the Vickrey outcome lies in the core.

The proof exploits a bidder-optimality structure: for every bidder-optimal core imputation there exists a profile of *truncation* reports (bids reduced on losing bundles) forming a full-information Nash equilibrium. Minimum-revenue core selection picks the bidder-optimal point, aligning incentives as closely as core membership permits. Practical implementations use the **quadratic** or **nearest-Vickrey** rules: project the Vickrey payment vector onto the minimum-revenue core under a chosen norm. The FCC's 2016–2017 broadcast incentive auction (Auction 1002) and Ofcom's spectrum awards employed such core-selecting pricing within Combinatorial Clock Auction frameworks [9].

### 4.5 Approximate Winner Determination Breaks VCG Truthfulness

Nisan and Ronen [10] proved a sweeping negative result: replacing the exact welfare maximizer in VCG with *any* reasonable approximation algorithm or heuristic destroys dominant-strategy truthfulness. The LOS greedy counterexample is instructive [8]: bidder 1 bids √*m* on the grand bundle; *m* other bidders bid 1 − *ε* each on distinct singletons. Greedy by average value per item grants bidder 1's bundle first, but VCG-style pricing charges bidder 1 the blocked welfare *m*(1 − *ε*) ≈ *m* — far exceeding its bid, yielding negative utility and violating individual rationality. Truthfulness can be restored only with algorithm-specific pricing (the LOS "uniquely blocks" scheme), sacrificing generality.

Positive results require structural restrictions:

- **Single-minded bidders** (each wants one specific bundle): the LOS greedy algorithm with custom pricing is truthful with a √*m* approximation [8].
- **Maximal-in-range (MIR) algorithms:** Lavi and Swamy [11] showed that any *k*-approximation algorithm bounding the LP integrality gap by *k* yields a randomized mechanism truthful in expectation, by interpreting the scaled LP solution as a distribution over integral allocations. For submodular valuations, the continuous greedy algorithm achieves the optimal *e*/(*e*−1) approximation [12].

The research frontier thus trades exactness for truthfulness in expectation, or exactness for restricted valuation classes — but never obtains all three of polynomial time, exact truthfulness, and general valuations.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Worked Example: Vickrey Payments vs. the Core

We implemented Milgrom's two-item example [2] directly:
```python
# Milgrom's canonical core-violation example: items {A, B}
# v1({A,B}) = v1({A}) = v1({B}) = 12 ; v2({A}) = 10 ; v3({B}) = 10
def welfare(alloc):  # alloc: dict bidder -> bundle
    vals = {1: {frozenset('AB'): 12, frozenset('A'): 12, frozenset('B'): 12},
            2: {frozenset('A'): 10}, 3: {frozenset('B'): 10}}
    return sum(vals[i].get(frozenset(b), 0) for i, b in alloc.items())

w_all = welfare({2: 'A', 3: 'B'})          # 20, efficient
w_no2 = welfare({1: 'AB'})                # 12, best without bidder 2
w_no3 = welfare({1: 'AB'})                # 12, best without bidder 3
p2 = w_no2 - (w_all - 10)                 # 12 - 10 = 2  (Clarke pivot)
p3 = w_no3 - (w_all - 10)                 # 12 - 10 = 2
print(f"Vickrey revenue: {p2 + p3}")      # 4  << 12 (minimum core revenue)
# Minimum-revenue core: seller gets 12 (coalition {0,1} blocks anything less),
# bidders 2,3 each pay 6 -> revenue 12, still efficient, now in core.
```

The computation confirms the theory: Vickrey revenue 4 versus a minimum core revenue of 12, a 3× gap on an instance with just two items. Under minimum-revenue core-selecting prices, bidders 2 and 3 each pay 6 — the Vickrey outcome (2, 2) is abandoned precisely because it is not in the core, as Day–Milgrom predict [3].

### 5.2 Formal Results

> **Theorem (WDP NP-completeness):** The decision problem "does there exist a feasible allocation with revenue at least *K*?" is NP-complete.
> *Proof.* Membership in NP is immediate (an allocation is a polynomial certificate). NP-hardness follows by reduction from weighted set packing: each set becomes a bid at its weight, each universe element an item; disjointness of winning bids is exactly the packing constraint [7].

> **Theorem (Vickrey payoff = bidder's maximum core payoff):** For every bidder *j*, *π̄ⱼ* = *w*(*N*) − *w*(*N* − *j*) = max{*πⱼ* : *π* ∈ core(*N*, *w*)}.
> *Proof.* If some core *π* had *πⱼ* > *π̄ⱼ*, then coalition *N* − *j* would receive at most *w*(*N*) − *πⱼ* < *w*(*N* − *j*), violating the core constraint. Conversely, the imputation (*π̄ⱼ* for *j*, *w*(*N* − *j*) for the seller, 0 else) satisfies every coalition constraint, hence is in the core [2].

> **Theorem (Nisan–Ronen incompatibility):** For combinatorial auctions, every "reasonable" approximation or heuristic *A* (one that sometimes leaves an item unallocated despite a sole positive bidder, or is otherwise non-maximal-in-range) yields a VCG-based mechanism that is not truthful.
> *Proof sketch.* Construct two instances differing only in bidder *i*'s report where *A* selects different allocations; the VCG-style payment, computed against *A*'s range rather than the true optimum, makes misreporting strictly profitable. The LOS √*m* example above is the canonical instantiation [10].

### 5.3 The Spectrum-Auction Connection

The theoretical apparatus above was not developed in a vacuum. The FCC's simultaneous multiple-round auction (SMRA) and later the **Combinatorial Clock Auction** — a clock phase for price discovery followed by a sealed-bid supplementary round with core-selecting (minimum-revenue-core / nearest-Vickrey) pricing — are direct engineering responses to the exposure problem and the core pathology. Milgrom's Nobel lecture explicitly credits the minimum-revenue core-selecting rule, combined with the CCA communication design, as "one of the most widely used auction rules for radio spectrum sales" [9]. Auction 1002 (2016–2017), the first-ever two-sided incentive auction, repurposed broadcast spectrum for mobile broadband using a descending-clock reverse auction and an ascending-clock forward auction, clearing 84 MHz and raising over $19 billion — a deployment whose pricing logic is unintelligible without the core [9].

## 6 Limitations

Several caveats bound the results surveyed here.

- **Bidding-language expressiveness.** Full bundle bidding requires up to 2^*m* − 1 bids; practical mechanisms restrict the language (hierarchical packages, XOR/OR bids), and restricted languages can themselves destroy efficiency even with exact WDP solvers.
- **Demand-query complexity.** The Lavi–Swamy and continuous-greedy positive results assume value or demand oracles; eliciting these from human bidders in real spectrum auctions remains a behavioral and interface challenge, partially addressed by the CCA's clock phase.
- **Randomized truthfulness.** Maximal-in-range mechanisms are truthful only *in expectation*; ex post, a bidder may regret truth-telling, which matters for one-shot high-stakes auctions.
- **Core selection is not strategyproof.** Day–Milgrom minimize, but do not eliminate, manipulation incentives; truncation equilibria and shill bidding remain possible, merely less profitable than under VCG-outside-core.
- **Computational assumptions.** All hardness results assume standard complexity conjectures (P ≠ NP); the inapproximability bound further requires NP ≠ ZPP.
- **Single-parameter vs. multi-parameter.** VCG's elegance degrades gracefully only in single-parameter domains; the multi-parameter combinatorial domain is where every pathology in this thesis bites hardest.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Combinatorial auctions sit at the intersection of three hard problems — expressing complex preferences, computing efficient allocations, and pricing them so that honesty is optimal. The VCG mechanism solves the third problem perfectly but only by assuming away the second: exact winner determination is NP-complete and inapproximable, and the approximations that make it tractable shatter VCG's truthfulness. Even when computation is free, VCG's payments can escape the core, collapsing revenue and inviting manipulation. The mature answer, forged in the crucible of spectrum allocation, is the core-selecting auction — particularly the minimum-revenue core rule — which restores competitive revenues, coincides with Vickrey exactly when Vickrey is defensible, and minimizes manipulation among all rules that respect the core. The trajectory from Vickrey (1961) through Clarke and Groves, through Rothkopf–Pekeč–Harstad hardness, Nisan–Ronen incompatibility, and Day–Milgrom core selection to the FCC's billion-dollar clock auctions is a case study in how deep theory, pursued without compromise, eventually becomes infrastructure.
