# Programmable Bootstrapping in Fully Homomorphic Encryption: Blind Rotation, the CGGI Torus Framework, and Lookup-Table Evaluation at Negligible Marginal Cost

## Abstract

Fully homomorphic encryption permits arbitrary computation over encrypted data, but every operation inflates ciphertext noise, which must periodically be refreshed by *bootstrapping*: homomorphically evaluating the decryption circuit. In the TFHE line of work [1][2], bootstrapping is not merely a noise refresh. Through the *blind rotation* of a test polynomial, a single bootstrap simultaneously evaluates an arbitrary univariate function encoded as a lookup table — *programmable bootstrapping* (PBS) [4]. This thesis develops PBS from first principles: the torus encoding and TLWE/TRLWE/TRGSW ciphertext hierarchy, modulus switching from $\mathbb{T}^{n+1}$ to $\mathbb{Z}/2N$, the blind-rotation loop of iterated CMux gates over the external product, noiseless sample extraction, and the key-switching layer. We analyze per-stage noise propagation, explain why only *negacyclic* functions are natively supported and how the padding-bit convention (and its recent removal [4]) extends the function class, survey circuit bootstrapping, and benchmark the evolution from 690 ms FHEW bootstraps to 6.4 ms single-core implementations [2][5]. In TFHE, the marginal cost of nonlinear computation is zero: any small-precision univariate function is free with every noise refresh.

## 1. Introduction

The foundational promise of fully homomorphic encryption — computing on data without ever decrypting it — was established by Gentry in 2009, but his blueprint left bootstrapping — the homomorphic evaluation of the decryption circuit — orders of magnitude too slow for practical use. Every FHE ciphertext carries *noise*, a small random error term that grows with each homomorphic operation; if the noise outgrows the decryption radius, the plaintext is lost forever. Bootstrapping refreshes a noisy ciphertext to a fixed, low-noise envelope, enabling *unbounded* depth — but at a cost that has historically dominated every FHE deployment.

Two distinct philosophies of bootstrapping have since emerged. In the *leveled-to-bootstrapped* families (BGV, BFV, CKKS), bootstrapping is a heavyweight, high-throughput operation applied infrequently: it linearizes the decryption circuit using homomorphic number-theoretic transforms and modulus switching, refreshing thousands of packed slots at once [3]. In the *gate-bootstrapping* philosophy pioneered by Ducas and Micciancio FHEW and revolutionized by Chillotti et al. [2], bootstrapping is a *lightweight, per-gate* operation: every binary gate is followed by a fast noise refresh, so circuit depth is unbounded from the start. TFHE's gate bootstrap runs in roughly 13 milliseconds on a single core [8], and — crucially — the refresh carries a free bonus: during blind rotation, the test polynomial can encode an *arbitrary lookup table*, so a univariate function of the encrypted input is evaluated *at the same time* as the noise is reduced [1][4]. This is **programmable bootstrapping** (PBS).

The implications are profound. In most FHE schemes, nonlinear functions (comparisons, ReLUs, S-boxes) are the expensive operations, requiring polynomial approximations or garbled-circuit hybrids. In TFHE, any univariate function over a small message space costs *exactly the same* as a no-op refresh. This thesis provides a self-contained treatment of that mechanism: the torus-based ciphertext hierarchy of [1], the blind-rotation algorithm and its correctness theorems, the external product and its asymmetric noise propagation, the negacyclicity constraint and the padding-bit encoding that circumvents it, the noise accounting behind parameter selection, and the extensions — circuit bootstrapping, larger-precision PBS without padding [4], block-binary key optimizations [5], and multi-output LUT frameworks [6] — that define the current frontier.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Torus and LWE over $\mathbb{T}$

Classical LWE operates over a finite modulus $q$: a ciphertext is a pair $(\mathbf{a}, b) \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n \times \mathbb{Z}_q$ with $b = \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle + m \cdot \Delta + e$, where $\mathbf{s} \in \{0,1\}^n$ is the binary secret, $m$ the message, $\Delta$ a scaling factor, and $e$ a small noise term. TFHE reformulates everything over the *real torus* $\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$, the additive group of reals modulo 1 [1]. Working modulo 1 eliminates $q$ from the abstraction: modulus switching becomes *rounding* a torus element to the discrete subgroup $\frac{1}{2N}\mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z}$.

The ciphertext hierarchy of [1] consists of three types:

- **TLWE** (torus LWE): $(\mathbf{a}, b) \in \mathbb{T}^n \times \mathbb{T}$ with phase $\phi = b - \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle = m + e$, encrypting a scalar message $m \in \mathbb{T}$.
- **TRLWE** (torus RLWE): $(A(X), B(X))$ with polynomial entries in $\mathbb{T}_N[X] = \mathbb{T}[X]/(X^N+1)$, encrypting a *polynomial* message $\mu(X) \in \mathbb{T}_N[X]$. The ring is *negacyclic*: $X^N = -1$, a property the entire bootstrapping construction exploits.
- **TRGSW** (torus GSW): a vector of $(k+1)\ell$ TRLWE samples encrypting $s \cdot H$, where $H$ is the gadget matrix with powers of the decomposition base $B_g$. TRGSW ciphertexts support the *external product* $\square$ with TRLWE ciphertexts, the workhorse of blind rotation.

The decomposition parameters $(B_g, \ell)$ — gadget base and decomposition length — control the precision of the *approximate* gadget decomposition [2]: exact decomposition is replaced by an approximate variant whose rounding error is folded into the noise analysis.

### 2.2 The External Product and Its Noise Asymmetry

The external product $\mathrm{ExtProd}(C, c) = C \boxdot c$ takes a TRGSW sample $C$ encrypting $\mu_C$ and a TRLWE sample $c$ encrypting $\mu_c$, and returns a TRLWE sample encrypting $\mu_C \cdot \mu_c$ [1][2]. Its decisive property is *asymmetric* noise propagation: the noise of the output depends *linearly* on the noise of the TRLWE operand but only on the *message* of the TRGSW operand (plus a fixed decomposition error), not on the TRGSW noise. This asymmetry is what makes blind rotation feasible — the bootstrapping key consists of TRGSW encryptions of secret-key bits, and iterating the external product $n$ times does not compound TRGSW noise exponentially.

> **Theorem:** (External product noise, [1] Thm. 4.1). Let $C \in \mathrm{TRGSW}(\mu_C)$ and $c \in \mathrm{TRLWE}(\mu_c)$. Then $C \boxdot c \in \mathrm{TRLWE}(\mu_C \mu_c)$ with noise variance bounded by a term linear in $\mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{Err}(c))$ plus a fixed gadget-decomposition error independent of $\mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{Err}(C))$.

### 2.3 From FHEW to TFHE: The 2016 Speedup

Ducas and Micciancio's FHEW (2015) achieved the first sub-second bootstrapping (under 1 second, 1 GB bootstrapping key) via a homomorphic *accumulator* over the ring [3]. Chillotti et al. [2] reformulated FHEW's entire accumulator in terms of the external product between a GSW (TRGSW) and an LWE (TRLWE) ciphertext — a single primitive replacing FHEW's bespoke machinery. The result: bootstrapping in **under 0.1 seconds**, a **50$\times$** speedup, and a bootstrapping key shrunk from **1 GB to 24 MB** at the same security level [2]. This external-product viewpoint also explained, for the first time, the *unique asymmetry* in GSW noise propagation.

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is a *theoretical-analytical* work. Rather than proposing a new scheme, we reconstruct the programmable-bootstrapping pipeline of [1] as a sequence of well-specified algorithmic transformations, prove (or precisely state, with pointers) the correctness and noise theorems that govern each stage, and validate the framework against published benchmarks from five independent implementation efforts [2][4][5][6][7]. Our methodology has three components:

1. **Formal reconstruction.** We present gate bootstrapping in unified notation: modulus switching $\mathbb{T}^{n+1} \to (\mathbb{Z}/2N)^{n+1}$, the blind-rotation loop as iterated $\mathrm{CMux}$ over TRGSW key material, and sample extraction as a noiseless projection. Each step's input/output ciphertext type and noise budget is tracked explicitly.
2. **Constraint analysis.** We characterize *exactly* which functions PBS can evaluate natively (negacyclic functions), prove why the constraint arises from $X^N = -1$, and compare the remedies: the MSB padding bit [1] and padding-free constructions [4].
3. **Empirical triangulation.** We collect published timings, key sizes, and parameter sets across the TFHE library [8], the WoP-PBS implementation [4], block-binary keys [5], and the TFHE-rs/Zama ecosystem [7], normalizing where possible to single-core latency and bits of security, to quantify the engineering trajectory of the construction.

All complexity claims are stated for the standard parameter regime $N \in \{1024, 2048\}$, $n \in \{500, 630\}$, with $\lambda \ge 110$–$152$ bits of security under ideal-lattice assumptions [1][2].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Modulus Switching: From the Torus to $\mathbb{Z}/2N$

Gate bootstrapping begins with a TLWE sample $c = (\mathbf{a}, b) \in \mathbb{T}^{n+1}$ encrypting a binary message $x \in \{0, 1\}$ as $x \cdot \frac{1}{2}$ (antipodal encoding: 0 maps to $0$, 1 maps to $\frac{1}{2}$) with noise $e$. The blind rotation operates in the *discrete* world $\mathbb{Z}/2N$, so the first step rescales each coordinate:

$$\tilde{a}_i = \left\lfloor a_i \cdot 2N \right\rceil, \qquad \tilde{b} = \left\lfloor b \cdot 2N \right\rceil.$$

The rescaled phase is $\tilde{\phi} = \tilde{b} - \sum_i s_i \tilde{a}_i \approx 2N \cdot \phi \pmod{2N}$. Because $\phi = x/2 + e$, we have $\tilde{\phi} \approx N \cdot x + 2N \cdot e$: the *message bit* has been promoted to the most significant region (a shift by $N$), while the noise occupies the least significant bits [1]. Correctness of the whole pipeline requires $|e| < \frac{1}{4} - \delta$ for a small rounding slack $\delta$ — the noise must not cross the decision boundary at $\frac{1}{4}$ [1]. This single inequality is the *noise contract* that every subsequent stage must preserve; it is also why gate bootstrapping refreshes after *every* gate rather than amortizing over a circuit.

### 4.2 Blind Rotation: The CMux Loop and the Accumulator

The heart of bootstrapping is **blind rotation**: homomorphically rotating a known test polynomial $v(X)$ by an *encrypted* amount $\rho = \tilde{b} - \sum_i s_i \tilde{a}_i \pmod{2N}$, using only TRGSW encryptions $C_i$ of the secret bits $s_i$ (the *bootstrapping key*). The algorithm [1, §4.3] is:

```
BlindRotate(c, (ã_1..ã_n, b̃), (C_1..C_n)):
    ACC ← X^{-b̃} · c                      # public rotation by known b̃
    for i = 1..n:
        ACC ← CMux(C_i, X^{ã_i} · ACC, ACC)  # secret rotation iff s_i = 1
    return ACC
```

where $\mathrm{CMux}(C, c_1, c_0) = C \boxdot (c_1 - c_0) + c_0$ selects between two TRLWE ciphertexts according to the TRGSW-encrypted bit [1]. Each iteration multiplies the accumulator by $X^{\tilde{a}_i s_i}$ — applying the secret-dependent rotation *blindly*. After the loop, the accumulator encrypts $X^{-\rho} \cdot v(X)$, i.e., the test polynomial rotated by minus the (encrypted) phase.

> **Theorem:** (Blind rotation correctness, [1] Thm. 4.3). The output is a TRLWE encryption of $X^{-\rho} \cdot v$ where $\rho = \tilde{b} - \sum_i s_i \tilde{a}_i \pmod{2N}$, with noise variance growing *linearly* in $n$ (the number of CMux iterations), each contributing one external-product noise term.

The linearity is the crux: because the external product's noise depends on the TRLWE operand rather than the TRGSW key material, the loop's total noise is $n \times$ (one CMux noise unit) — manageable for $n \approx 500$–$630$. The dominant cost is also here: $n$ external products, each requiring $(k+1)\ell$ NTT/FFT polynomial multiplications, which is why the FFT over the anticyclic ring — with AVX/AVX2/FMA vectorization in the reference library [8] — determines wall-clock performance.

### 4.3 Test-Polynomial Encoding, Negacyclicity, and the Padding Bit

Blind rotation is only useful because of *what* it rotates. For binary gate bootstrapping, the test polynomial is chosen as

$$v(X) = (1 + X + \cdots + X^{N-1}) \cdot X^{N/2} \cdot \mu_1,$$

with $\mu_1 \in \mathbb{T}$ the target output encoding. A short calculation using $X^N = -1$ shows that the constant term of $X^{-\rho} \cdot v$ equals $+\mu_1$ when the encrypted phase satisfies $|\phi| > \frac{1}{4} + \delta$ (message 1) and $-\mu_1$ otherwise (message 0) — the accumulator's constant term performs *thresholding* of the noisy phase [1, Thm. 6.2]. Sample extraction then reads off this constant coefficient *without adding any noise* [1]:

$$\mathrm{SampleExtract}(\mathrm{TRLWE}(\mu)) \to \mathrm{TLWE}(\mu_0),$$

a noiseless projection from the polynomial ring to a scalar LWE sample.

For **programmable** bootstrapping, the test polynomial generalizes to an arbitrary *lookup table*. Given a univariate function $f: \mathbb{Z}_p \to \mathbb{T}$, its values are encoded redundantly as the coefficients of $v(X) = \sum_j f(\lfloor j p / 2N \rfloor) X^j$; after blind rotation and extraction, the output encrypts $f(m)$ — the function is evaluated *for free* during the refresh [4][6]. But the negacyclicity of the ring imposes a hard constraint: because $X^{N+j} = -X^j$, only *negacyclic* functions, satisfying $f(x + p/2) = -f(x)$, can be encoded [6, App. A.3]. Arbitrary functions require one **padding bit** of zero in the MSB of the message, guaranteeing $\tilde{\phi} < N$ so the negacyclic wrap-around is never exercised [1][6].

Two recent lines of work attack this limitation. Chillotti, Ligier, Orfila, and Tap [4] introduced *programmable bootstrapping without padding* (WoP-PBS): by incorporating a BFV-style multiplication into the TFHE framework (justified by a careful noise analysis) and generalizing the bootstrap to select *any chunk of bits* of the plaintext, they evaluate many LUTs simultaneously and bootstrap large-precision integers with far smaller parameters — improving circuit bootstrapping as well. Complementary multi-output frameworks such as manyLUT and *Tetris* [6] pack several functions into one PBS, amortizing the bootstrap across outputs.

### 4.4 Key Switching and Circuit Bootstrapping: TLWE $\to$ TRGSW

Blind rotation plus sample extraction yields a TLWE ciphertext under the *accumulator* key — a different secret than the caller's. A final **key-switching** step (Theorems 4.1–4.2 of [1]) maps the ciphertext back under the original key and dimension using a public keyswitching key, at the cost of one more bounded noise term. This completes *gate bootstrapping* (Algorithm 10 of [1]).

Some applications need more: converting a TLWE ciphertext into a **TRGSW** ciphertext (e.g., to feed a subsequent blind rotation as key material, or to evaluate a CMux). **Circuit bootstrapping** [1, Alg. 6] performs this TLWE $\to$ TRGSW conversion while refreshing noise, via $\ell$ parallel gate bootstraps followed by $(k+1)\ell$ *private* keyswitches that assemble the output TRGSW sample coefficient by coefficient. The original proof of concept ran in **0.137 seconds** with 152-bit-security parameter sets ($N = 2048$, $B_g = 2$, $\ell = 4$ at level 2) [1].

### 4.5 Noise Propagation and the Correctness Bound

The end-to-end noise of gate bootstrapping decomposes additively across stages, and each term is *independent of the input noise* — this is precisely why bootstrapping refreshes [1]:

| Stage | Noise contribution | Scales with |
|---|---|---|
| Input TLWE noise | $2N \cdot e_{\mathrm{in}}$ | input (must satisfy $\|e\| < 1/4 - \delta$) |
| Modulus-switch rounding | $\approx n/4 + 1/4$ variance units | key dimension $n$ |
| Blind rotation ($n$ CMux) | $n \times$ external-product noise | $n$, gadget params $(B_g, \ell)$ |
| Sample extraction | **zero** | — |
| Key switching | keyswitch noise | keyswitch params $(t)$ |

The output noise is a *fixed* envelope determined solely by the parameters, so a bootstrapped ciphertext can immediately undergo further homomorphic operations. The parameter-selection methodology of [1, §7] directly relates the security parameter $\lambda$ to the LWE error rate and the entropy of the (binary) secret key, and experimental validation confirms that the output noise is Gaussian and matches the average-case variance theorems even after very deep circuits [1].

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

We summarize the published record:

| Milestone | Bootstrap latency | Bootstrapping key size | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| FHEW (Ducas–Micciancio, 2015) | $\approx$ 690 ms | $\approx$ 1 GB | [2] |
| TFHE / CGGI16 (Asiacrypt 2016) | $<$ 100 ms | 24 MB | [2] |
| TFHE library, binary gate | $\approx$ 13 ms / core | — | [8] |
| TFHE library, MUX gate | $\approx$ 26 ms (13 ms on 2 cores) | — | [8] |
| Block-binary keys (2023) | 10.5 ms $\to$ **6.4 ms** | 109 MB $\to$ 60 MB | [5] |
| Circuit bootstrapping (CGGI17) | 0.137 s (TLWE $\to$ TRGSW) | — | [1] |

Several observations emerge. **First**, the CGGI16 reformulation was a double win: latency and key size improved *simultaneously* (50$\times$ faster, 40$\times$ smaller) because the external product both simplified the algorithm and enabled the approximate gadget decomposition [2]. **Second**, subsequent gains have come from *key-structure* innovations: Min, Seo, and Song's block-binary secret distribution — the secret expressed as a concatenation of Hamming-weight-$\le$-1 blocks — simplifies the blind-rotation loop and, with a modified gadget decomposition, cuts both latency (6.4 ms) and key-switching noise at the same security level [5]. **Third**, at the ecosystem level, Zama's TFHE-rs reimplements the scheme in Rust with programmable bootstrapping as a first-class primitive, adds short-integer and full $u_{16}$–$u_{64}$ arithmetic by grouping small LWE ciphertexts, and provides ciphertext/server-key compression [7]. **Fourth**, the comparative study of [3] situates TFHE precisely: DM/CGGI-style bootstrapping achieves the *smallest latency* and is the *only* family offering programmable bootstrapping, while CKKS offers the highest throughput for approximate arithmetic and BGV/BFV offer higher small-integer throughput without the programmability.

On the theoretical side, the guarantees are unusually complete: worst-case and average-case noise theorems with experimentally validated independence heuristics [1], a direct security-to-error-rate parameterization [2], and formal treatments of the full PBS pipeline including failure-probability bounds in threshold settings [6].

The following Python sketch illustrates the core encoding idea — constructing a test polynomial for a lookup table over a small message space, the operation at the heart of every PBS:

```python
import numpy as np

def test_polynomial(lut, N, delta):
    """Encode a lookup table as a (redundant) test polynomial.
    lut: list of 2p values defining a negacyclic function f
    Returns coefficients v_j = delta * f(floor(j * p / (2N)))).
    """
    p = len(lut) // 2          # half-domain; negacyclic: f(x+p) = -f(x)
    v = np.zeros(2 * N)
    for j in range(2 * N):
        idx = (j * p) // (2 * N)
        v[j] = delta * lut[idx % (2 * p)]
    return v                    # blind rotation computes X^{-rho} * v

# Example: 2-bit ReLU-like LUT, negacyclic on Z_4
negacyclic_lut = [0, 1, 2, 1, 0, -1, -2, -1]  # f(x+4) = -f(x)
v = test_polynomial(negacyclic_lut, N=1024, delta=1/64)
assert np.allclose(v[1024:2048], -v[0:1024])  # negacyclicity of X^N = -1
```

## 6. Limitations

Programmable bootstrapping is powerful but not universal:

**Precision ceiling.** PBS natively handles small message spaces — typically 1 to 8 bits per bootstrap [7]. Larger integers must be decomposed into blocks (radix representations) with carry management across bootstraps, as in TFHE-rs's integer arithmetic [7]; WoP-PBS [4] raises the ceiling substantially but does not eliminate it.

**Negacyclicity.** Only negacyclic functions are directly encodable; arbitrary functions cost a padding bit (halving the usable message space) or require padding-free machinery [4][6] with its own parameter costs. Multivariate functions are not directly supported — they must be decomposed into univariate stages.

**No SIMD.** Unlike BGV/BFV/CKKS, TFHE ciphertexts encrypt a *single* small message; there is no batching across slots. Throughput-oriented workloads favor the packed schemes [3], while TFHE dominates latency-sensitive, branching-heavy, small-integer computation.

**Key material and circular security.** The bootstrapping key encrypts the secret key under itself (circular security assumption), is tens of megabytes [5], and must be transmitted to the evaluator. Threshold variants that distribute bootstrapping across parties [6] add interaction rounds and noise from partial decryptions.

**Side channels and failure probability.** Practical deployments must consider timing variability of FFT-based polynomial arithmetic, and every PBS carries a tiny correctness-failure probability governed by the noise-bound analysis — parameters must be chosen so failures are cryptographically negligible, not merely rare [1][5].

## 7. Conclusion

Programmable bootstrapping transformed bootstrapping from a necessary evil into a computational *asset*. The CGGI framework's insight — that the external product between TRGSW and TRLWE ciphertexts can drive a blind rotation of a lookup-table-encoded test polynomial — collapsed noise refresh, function evaluation, and key switching into a single pipeline whose marginal cost of evaluating *any* univariate function is zero [1][2]. A decade of refinement has carried the construction from 690 ms bootstraps with gigabyte keys to 6.4 ms single-core PBS with 60 MB keys [5], extended it to padding-free multi-bit precision [4] and multi-output LUTs [6], and productized it in maintained Rust libraries [7].

The deeper lesson is architectural: TFHE's gate-bootstrapping discipline — refresh early, refresh always — converts the noise-management problem from a global circuit-depth analysis into a *local* per-gate contract ($|e| < 1/4 - \delta$), and then exploits that locality to smuggle arbitrary nonlinear computation into the refresh for free. As FHE moves toward deployment in private inference, encrypted databases, and threshold key management, the schemes that win will be those whose cheapest operation is also their most expressive. Programmable bootstrapping is, at present, the clearest realization of that ideal.
