# Equality Saturation and E-Graph Rewriting for Compiler Optimization: egg, Cranelift ISLE, and the Elimination of the Phase-Ordering Problem

## Abstract

Traditional compiler optimizers apply rewrites destructively and in a fixed order, so the profitability of any one transformation cannot be judged against the transformations it forecloses — the *phase-ordering problem*. Equality saturation solves this by representing, in a single compact structure, the entire space of programs reachable under a set of equational axioms, and then extracting the globally best program according to a cost model. The enabling data structure is the **e-graph**, a congruence-closure representation of an equivalence relation over terms, introduced by Nelson in his 1980 PhD thesis and later popularized for theorem proving in Detlefs, Nelson, and Saxe's Simplify. Tate, Stepp, Tatlock, and Lerner coined *equality saturation* in 2009, but the technique remained impractical until Willsey et al.'s **egg** library (POPL 2021) introduced *rebuilding* — deferred, amortized invariant restoration — and *e-class analyses* for cooperating semantic analyses. This article traces that arc: from congruence closure and superoptimization (Denali, STOKE, Souper) through egg's algorithmic contributions, through Cranelift's ISLE rewriting DSL and the egglog unification of Datalog with equality saturation, to case studies in floating-point accuracy (Herbie), CAD decompilation (Spire), tensor-graph superoptimization (Tensat/TASO), and vectorizer synthesis. We present the core invariants, the saturation algorithm, and extraction, state formal correctness properties, survey empirical results including reported speedups of up to three orders of magnitude in rewrite throughput, and close with the technique's intrinsic limitations — rule-set explosion, analysis-soundness obligations, and the undecidability that lurks behind non-terminating axiom systems [1][2][3][4][5][6][7].

## 1. Introduction

Compiler engineers have long organized optimizers as pipelines of *passes*: constant folding, strength reduction, loop unrolling, vectorization, inlining, each a destructive rewrite of the intermediate representation (IR). This organization is modular and comprehensible, but it forces every pass to commit irrevocably. Consider the textbook example from the **egg** paper [1]:

$$\text{optimize}\left(\frac{a \times 2}{2}\right)$$

A strength-reduction pass rewrites $a \times 2 \to a \ll 1$ before any algebraic simplification observes the expression, producing $(a \ll 1)/2$ and permanently losing the opportunity to cancel the two factors of $2$. Any order of passes is wrong for some program: this is the **phase-ordering problem**, studied empirically since Whitfield and Soffa's exploration of optimization-space search. The deeper pathology is *information loss*: a destructive rewrite discards the term it replaced, so later passes — and the profitability heuristic — can never reconsider it.

Superoptimizers attacked the problem from the other end. Massalin's original superoptimizer, and later **Denali** (Joshi, Nelson, and Randall), **STOKE** (Schkufza, Sharma, and Aiken), and **Souper** (Sasnauskas et al.) [6], search for *the* optimal instruction sequence for a given specification. These systems find remarkably good code — Souper's synthesized rewrites shipped, hand-implemented, in LLVM and MSVC, and as an automated pass it shrank a Clang binary by roughly 4.4% — but their search is fundamentally enumerative: candidate *programs*, not candidate *derivations*, and it scales only to straight-line fragments of tens of instructions.

**Equality saturation** occupies the middle ground. Rather than choosing one rewrite order, it applies *all* rewrites *simultaneously*, accumulating every reachable equivalent term in an e-graph and deferring the choice of final program to a global extraction step. Tate et al. formalized this architecture in 2009 [2]: optimizations become *equality analyses* that only *add* information to a common IR, and a profitability heuristic selects among fully optimized candidates at the end. The vision was compelling; the machinery was not ready. E-graphs were designed in the late 1970s for theorem proving, not for the add-heavy, merge-heavy workload of saturation, and every attempt to scale the technique hit either asymptotic or engineering walls.

The breakthrough came with **egg** (Willsey et al., POPL 2021) [1]. Two contributions made equality saturation practical and flexible:

1. **Rebuilding**: an amortized technique that restores e-graph invariants only at selected points in the saturation loop, yielding asymptotic speedups over the eager congruence-closure maintenance of prior systems.
2. **E-class analyses**: a general mechanism integrating domain-specific analyses (constant evaluation, interval bounds, cost models) into the e-graph with a stated invariant, replacing ad hoc manipulation.

Built on egg, case studies achieved reported speedups of up to 3000× in rewrite throughput, across floating-point accuracy (Herbie), linear algebra kernels, and 3D CAD program decompilation (Spire). The ideas have since radiated outward: **Cranelift's ISLE** DSL brings ordered term rewriting with backtracking into a production WebAssembly compiler; **egglog** (Zhang et al., PLDI 2023) [4] unifies Datalog-style fixpoint reasoning with equality saturation; and **Tensat** brings saturation to tensor-graph superoptimization for deep learning compilers.

This article develops the theory and practice of equality saturation from first principles: e-graphs and congruence closure (§2), the saturation algorithm and its modern engineering (§3), the deep mechanisms that make it fast (§4), its formal properties and empirical record (§5), and its limits (§6).

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Terms, congruences, and the word problem

Let $\Sigma$ be a ranked signature and $T(\Sigma)$ the set of ground terms. Given a finite set $E$ of equations $l \approx r$, the **word problem** asks whether $E \models s \approx t$ for ground terms $s, t$. For uninterpreted function symbols, the semantic relation is the least congruence containing $E$: an equivalence relation $\equiv$ such that $s_i \equiv t_i$ for all $i$ implies $f(s_1, \dots, s_n) \equiv f(t_1, \dots, t_n)$.

> **Theorem:** (Decidability of ground word problems) For a finite set of ground equations over uninterpreted symbols, the word problem is decidable in $O(n \log n)$ time (Downey–Sethi–Tarjan; Nelson–Oppen union-find with congruence closure).

The algorithmic heart is **congruence closure**: maintain a partition of terms into equivalence classes, repeatedly merging classes whose members become congruent. Nelson's 1980 thesis introduced the **e-graph** as the data structure making this efficient [3]: terms are hash-consed into *e-nodes* $f(c_1, \dots, c_n)$ whose children are *e-classes* (equivalence classes of e-nodes), so that structurally shared subterms are stored once and congruence propagates through the DAG.

### 2.2 From theorem proving to optimization

E-graphs spent two decades in theorem provers — notably **Simplify** (Detlefs, Nelson, Saxe), where they discharged verification conditions by saturating ground equalities. In the 2000s, researchers repurposed them for program optimization [3]: the compiler populates an e-graph with *many equivalent programs* via axiomatic rewrites, then extracts the best. Tate et al. [2] named the procedure **equality saturation** and built **Peggy**, a Java optimizer on program expression graphs (PEGs), demonstrating both intricate optimization discovery and translation validation of a realistic optimizer.

The algorithm, at its simplest:

1. Insert the input term into an empty e-graph.
2. Repeatedly: for each rewrite rule $l \to r$, *e-match* the pattern $l$ against the e-graph; for every match, add the instantiated right-hand side to the same e-class as the match.
3. Stop at saturation (no rule adds new information), a timeout, or an iteration/node budget.
4. **Extract** the lowest-cost term represented by the root e-class, using a user-supplied cost function.

Crucially, step 2 is *monotone*: e-graphs only grow. Nothing is ever deleted, so no rewrite choice forecloses any other. The phase-ordering problem is not solved by finding the right order — it is *dissolved*, because order no longer matters: all orders' outcomes coexist in the e-graph.

### 2.3 Term rewriting and its discontents

Classical term rewriting applies $l \to r$ by destructive replacement: find a subterm matching $l\sigma$, replace it with $r\sigma$. Confluence (Church–Rosser) guarantees order-independence *if* the system is confluent, but compiler rule sets are almost never confluent, and even confluent systems commit to a *single* normal form rather than the *cheapest* of many equivalent forms. Equality saturation generalizes rewriting from "find a normal form" to "enumerate the equivalence class, then optimize over it."

### 2.4 The superoptimization lineage

It is instructive to contrast equality saturation with its cousins:

| System | Search object | Verification | Scale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Massalin superoptimizer | Instruction sequences (enumerated) | Testing | ~10 instructions |
| **Denali** (Joshi et al.) | E-graph + axioms + solver | Theorem prover | Straight-line code |
| **STOKE** (Schkufza et al.) | Programs (MCMC sampling) | SMT + fuzzing | Loop-free x86-64 |
| **Souper** (Sasnauskas et al.) [6] | LLVM IR fragments (enumerative synthesis) | Z3 | Peephole-scale |
| **Equality saturation** [1][2] | Equivalence classes of terms | Axioms (assumed sound) | Whole functions / graphs |

Souper is the closest relative: it synthesizes *new* rewrite rules verified by SMT, which can then feed an equality-saturation engine's rule set. STOKE searches *program space* stochastically; equality saturation searches *derivation space* exhaustively up to a budget. Denali was, in retrospect, the first equality-saturation superoptimizer — it used e-graphs with axioms and a solver-guided search — but its performance engineering predated the modern techniques.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The e-graph, precisely

An e-graph $G = (U, M, \text{lookup})$ consists of:

- A set $U$ of **e-classes**, each a set of **e-nodes**;
- A union-find structure $M$ over e-class ids maintaining the congruence;
- A hash-cons map from canonical e-nodes $f(c_1, \dots, c_n)$ (with $c_i$ canonical class ids) to their e-class, enforcing that *congruent terms share one e-class* — the **congruence invariant**.

Two operations drive everything:

- **add**$(t)$: insert term $t$, creating e-nodes bottom-up, merging with any congruent existing e-node.
- **merge**$(a, b)$: union two e-classes, then repair congruence: if $f(\dots, a, \dots)$ and $f(\dots, b, \dots)$ now have congruent children, they must merge too. This upward repair is congruence closure.

> **Theorem:** (E-graph soundness) Every two terms in the same e-class are provably equal under the inserted equations. *Proof sketch.* By induction on the derivation: `add` inserts syntactic instances; `merge` unions classes only for asserted equalities (rewrite applications) or congruence consequences thereof. ∎

Completeness is relative: the e-graph proves exactly the equalities derivable from the rules *applied within the budget*. Saturation is a semi-decision procedure for the word problem under the given axiom set.

### 3.2 The saturation loop and e-matching

The equality-saturation loop interleaves two phases:

```
loop {
    matches = e_match_all(rules, egraph)   // read phase
    for (l -> r, subst) in matches { egraph.union(l[subst], r[subst]) }  // write phase
    if saturated or budget_exceeded { break }
    egraph.rebuild()                       // restore invariants
}
```

**E-matching** is the performance-critical operation: finding all substitutions $\sigma$ such that pattern $l\sigma$ is represented in the e-graph. Naively this is subgraph matching against a compressed structure; egg compiles patterns to a small virtual machine and uses the e-graph's internal indices. Zhang et al. observed that e-matching *is* conjunctive query evaluation over the e-graph viewed as a relational database — the insight that led to egglog [4].

Rules in egg are ordinary Rust values, written with a macro DSL:

```rust
use egg::{rewrite as rw, *};

define_language! {
    enum Math {
        Num(i32),
        "+" = Add([Id; 2]),
        "*" = Mul([Id; 2]),
        "<<" = Shl([Id; 2]),
        "/" = Div([Id; 2]),
        Var(Symbol),
    }
}

let rules: &[Rewrite<Math, ()>] = &[
    rw!("comm-add"; "(+ ?a ?b)" => "(+ ?b ?a)"),
    rw!("assoc-mul"; "(* ?a (* ?b ?c))" => "(* (* ?a ?b) ?c)"),
    rw!("mul-two"; "(* ?a 2)" => "(<< ?a 1)"),
    rw!("cancel-div"; "(/ (* ?a ?b) ?b)" => "?a"
        if is_not_zero("?b")),
];
```

Note the conditional rewrite: `is_not_zero` is a *predicate over an e-class analysis*, not syntax — the bridge to §4.2. Running the runner:

```rust
let runner = Runner::default()
    .with_expr(&"( / (* a 2) 2)".parse().unwrap())
    .with_node_limit(10_000)
    .run(rules);
let extractor = Extractor::new(&runner.egraph, AstSize);
let (cost, best) = extractor.find_best(runner.roots[0]);
assert_eq!(best.to_string(), "a");
```

Both $(a \ll 1)/2$ and the cancelled $a$ coexist in the e-graph; the extractor picks $a$ by the `AstSize` cost model. No phase ordering decision was ever made.

### 3.3 Extraction: optimization as a separate concern

Extraction selects, from each e-class, the minimum-cost represented term for a *local* cost function $c$: $c(f(t_1, \dots, t_n)) = w(f) + \sum c(t_i)$. Because cost is local, the optimal choice per e-class composes into a globally optimal term via dynamic programming — a bottom-up pass over the e-graph's DAG. This cleanly separates *correctness* (which terms are equivalent) from *profitability* (which is cheapest), the exact separation the phase-ordering problem destroys. Extraction is also where domain knowledge enters: Herbie uses sampled error estimates as costs; vectorizers use instruction-count or latency models; Tensat uses predicted kernel runtimes.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Rebuilding: deferred invariant restoration

The classical e-graph maintains the congruence invariant *eagerly*: every `merge` immediately repairs all affected parents. Under saturation workloads — thousands of merges per iteration — this is catastrophic: a single merge can trigger cascading repair work, and the same e-node may be re-canonicalized many times per iteration.

**Rebuilding** defers the work. Merges during the write phase only union the classes (recording them in a worklist); once per iteration, `rebuild` processes the worklist, re-canonicalizing e-nodes and merging newly congruent ones until fixpoint. Because each e-node is re-canonicalized at most once per rebuild, the amortized cost drops dramatically — Willsey et al. report this as an *asymptotic* speedup in practice, the single largest factor in egg's performance [1][7].

```haskell
-- conceptual model of deferred rebuilding
rebuild :: EGraph -> EGraph
rebuild g = fixpoint repairWorklist g
  where
    repairWorklist g =
      let dirty = takeWorklist g
          g'    = foldl' recanonicalize g dirty
      in if null (newMerges g') then g' else repairWorklist g'
```

The invariant maintained is weaker *within* an iteration (e-matching must tolerate slightly stale canonical ids, handled by resolving through the union-find at match time) and fully restored at iteration boundaries. This is the classic systems trade: a weaker temporary invariant, amortized restoration, dramatically better throughput.

### 4.2 E-class analyses: semantics inside the e-graph

Purely syntactic rewrites cannot express "fold $x + 0$ only when the *other* operand is the constant $0$" — wait, they can; but they cannot express "rewrite using the *value* of a subexpression" (constant folding needs evaluation) or "apply only if this interval is non-negative." Prior systems handled this with ad hoc side tables that rewrites consulted and mutated, breaking the e-graph's invariants in subtle ways.

**E-class analyses** attach a lattice domain $D$ to every e-class, computed bottom-up from e-nodes: $d(c) = \bigsqcup_{n \in c} \text{make}(n)$. The analysis is maintained across merges by joining, and — this is the key invariant — the analysis data of a class may only *grow* (in lattice order) as the e-graph grows. Rewrites can then:

- **Read** analysis data in conditions (`if is_not_zero("?b")` above reads a constant-folding analysis);
- **Write** new e-nodes computed from analysis data (e.g., inserting the folded constant).

Because analysis values are monotone in e-graph growth and merges join them, the analysis/e-graph co-evolution is sound by construction. egg's paper demonstrates analyses for constant folding, interval arithmetic, and free-variable sets; Herbie's egg-based rewriter uses a *sampling-based error estimate* analysis to guide extraction toward numerically stable forms [1].

### 4.3 ISLE: rewriting discipline in a production compiler

**Cranelift** — the WebAssembly code generator used by Wasmtime — faced a concrete phase-ordering-adjacent problem in instruction selection: lowering rules interact (a rule for `iadd` with an immediate vs. a rule for a full `iadd`), and hand-ordered rule priority lists are brittle. Its answer is **ISLE** (Instruction Selection with Lowering Expressions), a term-rewriting DSL embedded in the compiler.

ISLE rules are *ordered*: the first matching rule wins, with backtracking across nested matches. This is deliberately *not* equality saturation — instruction selection must commit to one lowering per node, and predictable, auditable lowering matters more than global optimality there. But ISLE's design borrows saturation's central lesson: express lowering as *declarative rewrite rules over terms* with explicit priorities, rather than imperative pattern-matching code, so rule interactions are visible and checkable. It demonstrates the spectrum: full equality saturation where global search pays (peephole optimizers, vectorizers), disciplined ordered rewriting where predictability pays (instruction selection).

### 4.4 Case studies: saturation in the wild

**Herbie and floating-point accuracy.** Herbie (Panchekha et al., PLDI 2015) [5] improves floating-point accuracy by up to 60 recovered bits on textbook examples, using a database of algebraic rewrites plus series expansions and regime inference. Reimplementing Herbie's rewriting core on egg yielded the headline performance result: rebuilding and e-class analyses made the saturation engine up to **3000× faster** on its workload [1], turning an overnight batch tool toward interactive use.

**Spire and CAD decompilation.** Spire (Nandi et al.) recovers high-level, editable CAD programs from low-level geometry via *inverse transformations* — rewrites run "backwards" from meshes to parametric models. Equality saturation is natural here because inverse rules are highly non-confluent: many candidate programs explain the same geometry, and the e-graph compactly represents all of them until a simplicity cost model extracts the best [1].

**Tensat, TASO, and tensor graphs.** Deep-learning compilers optimize compute graphs with graph substitutions. TASO generates substitutions automatically via enumerative synthesis; **Tensat** applies equality saturation over tensor graphs with egg, exploring combinations of substitutions that fixed-order application would miss. The cost model is learned or profiled kernel latency — extraction then literally selects the fastest *provably equivalent* graph.

**Vectorization.** The same pattern applies to SIMD synthesis: vectorization rewrites (packing scalar ops into vector lanes) interact badly with scalar simplifications under fixed ordering. Saturation-based vectorizers keep both scalar and vectorized forms in the e-graph and extract by a target cost model — the approach explored in equality-saturation vectorizers for DSPs and Halide-style pipelines.

### 4.5 egglog: the Datalog unification

**egglog** (Zhang et al., PLDI 2023) [4] observes that e-matching is conjunctive query evaluation and that saturation is fixpoint computation — i.e., equality saturation *is* Datalog with equality. egglog unifies the two: relations, rules with semi-naïve incremental evaluation, lattice-based merging of derived facts, *and* e-graphs with congruence closure and extraction, in one engine. The payoff is bidirectional: Datalog analyses (e.g., a unification-based pointer analysis) gain efficient equality reasoning, and EqSat applications gain incremental execution and cooperating analyses for free. Its evaluation reimplemented both a pointer analysis and Herbie's rewriter, reporting systems that were faster, simpler, and — tellingly — fixed bugs present in the originals. Conceptually, egglog completes the arc: from Nelson's e-graphs as a *decision procedure*, through egg's e-graphs as an *optimization engine*, to egglog's e-graphs as a *general fixpoint programming substrate*.

## 5. Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

**Formal properties.** The core theorems of the framework are:

> **Theorem:** (Saturation soundness) Every term extracted from the root e-class is provably equal to the input term under the rewrite rules. *Follows from e-graph soundness (§3.1): extraction only selects among terms in the root class.* ∎

> **Theorem:** (Extraction optimality) For a local cost function $c$, bottom-up extraction returns the minimum-$c$ term represented in the e-graph. *Proof by induction on term height: the optimal term rooted at $f$ with children in classes $c_i$ combines the optimal choices per class, and locality makes the combination optimal.* ∎

Note what is *not* claimed: the extracted term is optimal only *among represented terms*. Global optimality requires saturation to completion, which is undecidable in general (see §6).

**Empirical record.** The egg paper's evaluation [1] reports: (i) rebuilding provides asymptotic speedups over eager congruence maintenance, measured across the standard `math` rewrite benchmark; (ii) the Herbie case study ran up to **3000× faster** than the prior implementation; (iii) case studies in floating-point accuracy, linear-algebra kernel optimization, and CAD decompilation each reached state-of-the-art results with substantially less engineering effort than bespoke solvers. Tate et al. [2] demonstrated practical time/space overhead and successful translation validation of a realistic Java optimizer. Souper [6], the closest superoptimizing relative, found optimizations that shipped in LLVM and MSVC and shrank a Clang binary by ~4.4% as an automated pass — evidence for the *complementary* claim that saturation engines should consume machine-synthesized rules. The semantic-foundations literature [3] has since put the chase/eqsat correspondence on rigorous footing, proving connections between saturation termination and the Skolem chase from database theory.

## 6. Limitations

**Rule-set explosion.** Equality saturation trades the phase-ordering problem for a *capacity* problem. Associativity and commutativity generate combinatorially many equivalent forms; without node limits, timeouts, or rule scheduling, the e-graph exhausts memory long before saturation. egg's `Runner` exposes iteration limits, node limits, and timeouts, and practitioners schedule expansive rules carefully — but this reintroduces a *soft* ordering problem through the back door: which rules to run, and when, affects which optima are discovered within budget.

**Soundness is the user's burden.** The e-graph guarantees that extracted terms are equal *under the asserted rules*; it cannot check that the rules themselves are sound. An incorrect rewrite silently poisons every extraction. Conditional rewrites depending on e-class analyses additionally require the analysis to be a sound abstraction — a non-trivial proof obligation per domain (interval arithmetic for division guards, overflow semantics for bit-vector rewrites).

**Undecidability and non-termination.** With a Turing-complete rule set, saturation need not terminate, and no complete termination checker exists. Timeouts make the system practically useful but theoretically incomplete: extraction optimality holds only relative to the explored fragment.

**Cost-model fidelity.** Extraction is only as good as its cost function. Local cost functions cannot express global properties (register pressure, instruction-cache effects) without encoding them as analyses, and a cost model that disagrees with hardware reality will "optimize" programs into slower ones — the classic pitfall, now automated.

**Expressiveness boundaries.** E-graphs represent *equivalence over terms*; control flow, mutable state, and binding structure require encodings (PEGs, explicit state tokens, De Bruijn indices) that complicate rules and bloat the graph. egglog's relational view helps, but reasoning about loops and memory remains harder than peephole-scale arithmetic.

## 7. Conclusion

Equality saturation reframes compiler optimization from *choosing rewrites* to *accumulating equivalences*: the e-graph compactly represents the space of programs reachable under a set of axioms, and extraction selects the best by a global cost model. The phase-ordering problem — an artifact of destructive, ordered rewriting — dissolves when rewrites only add information. The technique's history is a forty-year arc from Nelson's congruence-closure data structure [3], through Simplify's theorem proving and Tate et al.'s Peggy [2], to the engineering breakthroughs of egg — rebuilding and e-class analyses [1][7] — and onward to ISLE's disciplined rewriting in Cranelift, egglog's unification with Datalog [4], and saturation-based superoptimization of tensor graphs and floating-point code [5][6].

The practical lesson is architectural: *separate equivalence from profitability*. Let rewrites assert what is equal; let analyses compute what is known; let extraction decide what is best. Systems that respect this separation — egg, egglog, and their descendants — have repeatedly turned weeks of bespoke solver engineering into declarative rule sets with competitive or better results. The open problems are now about scale and trust: taming rule-set explosion with learned scheduling, verifying rewrite rules and analyses, and extending saturation gracefully to stateful, control-rich programs. The phase-ordering problem is solved in principle; making its solution routine is the work ahead.
