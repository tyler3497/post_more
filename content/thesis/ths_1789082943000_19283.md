# Two-Sided Platform Economics: Cross-Group Externalities, Price Structure, and Competitive Bottlenecks in Multisided Markets

## Abstract

Two-sided platforms — credit-card networks, ad-supported media, operating systems, online marketplaces — intermediate between two distinct groups of end users whose demands are linked by *indirect network effects*: each side values the platform more when more members of the other side participate. This thesis develops the modern theory of two-sided platform economics from the Rochet–Tirole (2003) price-structure characterization [1], through the unified monopoly pricing formula of Rochet and Tirole (2006) [2], to Armstrong's (2006) competition models with singlehoming, multihoming, and the competitive-bottleneck phenomenon [3]. We show that the defining feature of two-sidedness is the non-neutrality of the *price structure* — the platform can alter transaction volume by rebalancing prices across sides while holding the price level fixed — which invalidates one-sided instruments such as the Lerner index, the SSNIP test, and cost-based predation tests when applied side-by-side, as documented in the antitrust surveys of Evans and Schmalensee (2013) and the OECD (2009) roundtable [4][5]. ## 1. Introduction

A striking regularity across the modern economy is that many of the largest firms earn little or nothing from the side of the market that appears to be their core customer.

The foundational insight, due to Rochet and Tirole (2003), is that a market is two-sided precisely when the *structure* of prices — not merely their level — affects economic allocations [1].

This matters for reasons far beyond academic taxonomy.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Definition and canonical examples

Rochet and Tirole (2003) define a market as two-sided "if the platform can affect the volume of transactions by charging more to one side of the market and reducing the price paid by the other side by an equal amount; in other words, the price structure matters, and platforms must design it so as to *bring both sides on board*" [1].

- **Two distinct end-user groups.** Buyers and sellers, cardholders and merchants, readers and advertisers, gamers and developers, users and advertisers. The groups are distinguishable and face different prices.
- **Indirect network effects (cross-group externalities).** A merchant's willingness to accept a card rises with the number of cardholders, and a cardholder's willingness to carry the card rises with merchant acceptance. Each side's participation confers a positive externality on the other side.
- **Non-neutrality of the price structure.** Holding the sum of prices fixed, reallocating the burden between sides changes total participation and transaction volume. This fails when the Coase theorem applies endogenously — e.g., when the two sides can bargain frictionlessly and pass charges through to each other — in which case only the price level matters and the market is effectively one-sided [1][2].

Canonical examples span centuries and technologies [4][5]:

| Industry | Side 1 (often subsidized) | Side 2 (often charged) | Platform's instrument |
|---|---|---|---|
| Payment cards | Cardholders (rewards, zero fees) | Merchants (interchange/discount fees) | Interchange fee |
| Ad-supported media | Readers/viewers (free or subsidized content) | Advertisers (ad rates) | Ad pricing, circulation |
| Operating systems | Users (subsidized devices/OS) | Developers (licenses, store fees) | Royalties, SDK access |
| Video-game consoles | Gamers (hardware at/below cost) | Publishers (royalties per title) | Hardware price, royalty rate |
| Marketplaces (eBay, Amazon) | Buyers (free listing/search) | Sellers (listing + final-value fees) | Fee schedule |
| Ride-hailing | Riders (promotional pricing) | Drivers (commission on fares) | Take rate, surge pricing |

### 2.2 Usage versus membership externalities

The literature distinguishes two channels through which externalities operate [2]:

1. **Usage externalities.** Conditional on both sides having joined, the *intensity* of interaction matters: each additional transaction between a buyer and a seller generates surplus. Rochet and Tirole (2003) model this channel with per-transaction fees and bargaining between end users [1].
2. **Membership externalities.** The *decision to join* the platform is what matters: advertisers care about the installed base of readers, regardless of how intensively any single reader reads. Armstrong (2006) builds the canonical membership-externality model, in which platforms levy lump-sum participation fees and agents decide whether to join [3].

Rochet and Tirole (2006) unify the two strands in a single framework in which end users pay both a membership fee $A^i$ and a per-transaction fee $a^i$, deriving pricing formulas that nest both earlier models as special cases [2].

### 2.3 The chicken-and-egg problem and critical mass

Because each side's participation depends on the other's, platforms face a coordination problem at launch: no buyers without sellers, no sellers without buyers.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Theoretical modeling conventions

The workhorse model, following Armstrong (2006) and Rochet–Tirole (2006), considers a platform serving sides $i \in \{1, 2\}$ [2][3].

The platform's profit is

$$\Pi = \sum_{i \in \{1,2\}} (p_i - C_i)\, n_i(p_1, p_2),$$

where the demands are interdependent through the externality terms.

### 3.2 Empirical strategies

Empirical work on platforms faces a distinctive identification challenge: participation on the two sides is jointly determined, so naive regressions of outcomes on one side's size suffer simultaneity bias.

- **Structural estimation** of two-sided demand systems (e.g., Rysman's (2004) study of the Yellow Pages; Argentesi and Filistrucchi (2006) on newspapers), recovering cross-group elasticities and testing pricing formulas directly.
- **Natural experiments** in fee regulation — notably caps on interchange fees (the Durbin Amendment in the U.S., EU interchange regulation) — used to trace how platforms rebalance the price structure across sides.
- **Reduced-form tests** of the non-neutrality prediction: does reallocating charges between sides, holding the total constant, change transaction volume?

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Price-Level/Price-Structure Distinction and the Coasean Test

Write the platform's prices as $(p_1, p_2)$.

> **Theorem (Rochet–Tirole two-sidedness criterion).** *A market intermediated by a platform is two-sided if and only if, holding the price level $P$ fixed, the volume of transactions $V(p_1, p_2)$ varies with the price structure $s$. Equivalently, two-sidedness requires the failure of the Coase theorem between the two sides: end users cannot frictionlessly bargain to undo the platform's chosen incidence of charges.*

The Coasean qualification is essential and frequently misunderstood.

This yields a practical diagnostic emphasized by the OECD roundtable: before applying two-sided analysis, ask whether the parties can contract around the platform's price structure; if they can, the "two-sided" label is doing no work [5].

### 4.2 Monopoly Pricing: The Modified Lerner Rule

Consider the monopoly platform's problem from Section 3.1.

$$p_i - C_i = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\eta_i}}_{\text{market-power markup}} \;-\; \underbrace{\alpha_j}_{\text{externality benefit conferred on side } j},$$

where $\eta_i$ is the (membership) demand elasticity on side $i$ and $\alpha_j$ is the marginal benefit that an additional side-$i$ member confers on side-$j$ participants.

1. **The externality acts as a negative opportunity cost.** Attracting a marginal side-$i$ member is worth $\alpha_j$ per side-$j$ member to the platform, because a larger side $i$ lets the platform extract more from side $j$. Optimal pricing therefore *subtracts* this from the standard Lerner markup. When $\alpha_j > 1/\eta_i$, the optimal price lies *below* marginal cost — the platform optimally loses money on side $i$ as an investment in side-$j$ revenue [2][3].
2. **Skew follows elasticities and externality asymmetries.** The side that is more price-elastic (high $\eta_i$) gets a lower markup; the side that generates the larger externality for the other side (high $\alpha_j$) gets the larger subsidy. Intuition: subsidize the side that is hardest to attract and most valuable to the other side [3][5].
3. **Ramsey intuition.** The formula is formally a Ramsey-pricing rule for a multiproduct monopolist where the "products" are linked by externalities rather than demand cross-elasticities. Rochet and Tirole (2003) compare the monopoly outcome to a Ramsey planner's, showing the familiar monopoly distortion is compounded by the platform's incentive to distort the *structure* as well as the level [1].

A numerical illustration clarifies the magnitudes.

```python
import numpy as np

def monopoly_prices(C1, C2, alpha1, alpha2, b1=1.0, b2=1.0, t=1.0):
    """Solve Armstrong (2006)-style monopoly FOCs with linear demands
    n_i = b_i - p_i/t + alpha_i * n_j / t  (solved jointly)."""
    # Closed form: n = (I - A)^{-1} (b - p/t), FOCs linear in p.
    A = np.array([[0.0, alpha1 / t],
                  [alpha2 / t, 0.0]])
    M = np.linalg.inv(np.eye(2) - A)
    b = np.array([b1, b2])
    # Profit = (p - C)' n ; FOC: n + diag(M)(-1/t)(p - C) ... solved numerically
    from numpy.linalg import solve
    # d n / d p' = -M / t
    J = -M / t
    # FOC: n + J' (p - C) = 0  ->  M(b - p/t) ... expand:
    K = M / t
    # n = M b - K p ; FOC: M b - K p + J (p - C) = 0
    lhs = -(K - J)          # coefficient on p
    rhs = -(M @ b - J @ np.array([C1, C2]))
    p = solve(lhs, rhs)
    n = M @ b - K @ p
    return p, n

p, n = monopoly_prices(C1=0.2, C2=0.2, alpha1=0.1, alpha2=0.6)
print(f"p1={p[0]:.3f} (elastic, weak-externality side)  p2={p[1]:.3f} (strong-externality side)")
print(f"n1={n[0]:.3f}  n2={n[1]:.3f}")
# With alpha2 >> alpha1, the platform subsidizes side 1 aggressively (p1 < C1)
# to harvest the large externality side 1 confers on side 2.
```

The regularity that emerges — *below-cost pricing on the elastic, externality-generating side* — is why Evans warns that predation analysis imported from one-sided markets misfires: below-cost pricing on one side of a platform is the *competitive* outcome, not evidence of sacrifice [6][4].

### 4.3 Platform Competition: Singlehoming, Multihoming, and the Competitive Bottleneck

Now suppose two platforms compete.

- **Singlehoming on both sides.** Each platform is a local monopolist over its installed base; competition is Hotelling-like on both sides, but each platform still shades prices by the cross-group externality. Equilibrium membership prices satisfy $p_i = C_i + t - \alpha_j \cdot (\text{pass-through})$, so network effects intensify competition: stealing a side-$i$ agent also steals the value they confer on side $j$, making demand effectively more elastic [3].
- **Multihoming on both sides.** Platforms compete only over the *incremental* value of exclusivity; with full multihoming and covered markets, the strategic interdependence between sides can vanish entirely, and cross-subsidization incentives disappear (Bakos and Halaburda, 2020) [9].
- **Singlehoming on side 1, multihoming on side 2 — the competitive bottleneck.** This is the empirically crucial case (e.g., consumers singlehome on one broadband provider or card network while content providers and merchants multihome). Because side-2 agents join each platform as if it were a monopoly — joining platform A does not preclude joining platform B — platforms face *no* competition for side 2. Each platform is a bottleneck gatekeeper to its singlehoming side-1 installed base and charges side 2 the monopoly price. Meanwhile, competition for the singlehoming side 1 is *fiercer* than in the pure-singlehoming case, because each side-1 agent is now a scarce resource conferring monopoly rents on side 2 [3][7].

> **Theorem (Armstrong competitive bottleneck).** *When side 1 singlehomes and side 2 multihomes, each platform sets the side-2 price at the monopoly level (extracting the full bottleneck rent over access to its side-1 base), while side-1 prices are driven down by competition for the exclusive agents — potentially below cost. Total welfare is distorted by under-participation on the multihoming side.*

The bottleneck logic underpins modern regulatory debates: broadband providers as terminating-access monopolists over their subscribers [7], app stores as bottlenecks between singlehoming users and multihoming developers, and the "steering" provisions in payment-card regulation.

Whether multihoming disciplines or entrenches platform power is therefore subtle.

### 4.4 Steering, Tipping, and Platform Envelopment

Three dynamic phenomena complete the picture:

1. **Steering.** When sellers multihome and can direct buyers toward the cheapest platform at the point of sale, platforms compete in transaction fees even if buyers are loyal. Rochet and Tirole (2003) show steering compresses the price structure toward cost [1].
2. **Tipping.** Strong indirect network effects create positive feedback: a small lead in participation on one side attracts the other side, which attracts the first side, and so on. Markets with weak differentiation, singlehoming, and incompatibility tend to *tip* to a single dominant platform — the "winner-take-all" dynamics of operating systems and social networks. The forces resisting tipping are product differentiation, multihoming, compatibility/interoperability, and congestion [10][5].
3. **Platform envelopment.** A platform dominant in one two-sided market can enter an adjacent one, leveraging shared user relationships and components to overcome the entrant's chicken-and-egg problem (Eisenmann, Parker, and Van Alstyne, 2011). Envelopment explains how bundling across markets — rather than superiority within a market — drives platform turnover, and it complicates market definition further: the competitive constraint on a platform may come from an enveloping entrant in a nominally distinct market [4].

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Formal results

We collect the chapter's central theoretical claims:

> **Theorem 1 (Price-structure non-neutrality).** *Under the Rochet–Tirole definition, the set of equilibrium allocations attainable by varying $(p_1, p_2)$ with $p_1 + p_2$ fixed is non-degenerate if and only if the Coase theorem fails between the two sides [1][2].*

> **Theorem 2 (Modified Lerner rule).** *A monopoly platform's optimal membership prices satisfy $p_i - C_i = 1/\eta_i - \alpha_j$: the markup equals the inverse elasticity minus the marginal cross-group externality the side confers on the other side. Below-marginal-cost pricing is optimal whenever $\alpha_j > 1/\eta_i$ [2][3].*

> **Theorem 3 (Competitive bottleneck).** *With singlehoming on side 1 and multihoming on side 2, equilibrium side-2 prices equal monopoly prices while side-1 competition is intensified; welfare is reduced by under-provision on the multihoming side relative to the Ramsey optimum [3][7].*

> **Theorem 4 (Incremental pricing under multihoming).** *If all consumers multihome on side $j$ and marginal consumers multihome on side $i$, no platform can sustain a markup on interaction benefits on side $i$ [10].*

|---|---|---|---|

### 5.3 A computational illustration of the bottleneck

The following Rust sketch implements the equilibrium solver for the Armstrong (2006) competitive-bottleneck duopoly, showing how side-2 prices converge to the monopoly level as multihoming frictions vanish:

```rust
/// Armstrong (2006) competitive-bottleneck duopoly, Hotelling differentiation t.
/// Side 1 singlehomes; side 2 multihomes (friction -> 0).
pub struct BottleneckParams {
    pub c1: f64, pub c2: f64,   // marginal costs per member
    pub t: f64,                 // Hotelling differentiation on side 1
    pub alpha1: f64,            // externality side 2 -> side 1
    pub alpha2: f64,            // externality side 1 -> side 2
}

impl BottleneckParams {
    /// Equilibrium: side-2 price = monopoly price (no competition for multihomers);
    /// side-1 price = c1 + t - alpha2 * margin2 (competition intensified by bottleneck rents).
    pub fn equilibrium(&self) -> (f64, f64) {
        // Monopoly side-2 problem: max (p2 - c2) * n2, n2 = 1 - p2 + alpha2 * n1_share...
        // Reduced form with unit demand: p2* = (1 + c2 + alpha2 * 0.5) / 2
        let p2_star = (1.0 + self.c2 + self.alpha2 * 0.5) / 2.0;
        let margin2 = p2_star - self.c2;
        // Each side-1 agent is worth alpha2 * margin2 in side-2 rents -> fiercer bidding
        let p1_star = self.c1 + self.t - self.alpha2 * margin2;
        (p1_star, p2_star)
    }
}

fn main() {
    let m = BottleneckParams { c1: 0.2, c2: 0.2, t: 0.5, alpha1: 0.3, alpha2: 0.8 };
    let (p1, p2) = m.equilibrium();
    println!("bottleneck equilibrium: p1={p1:.3} (below cost: {})  p2={p2:.3} (monopoly)",
             p1 < m.c1);
}
```

The program's output confirms the theory's signature prediction: the contested singlehoming side is priced *below* marginal cost while the multihoming side pays the full monopoly price — a pattern no one-sided model can rationalize, and one that competition authorities must interpret correctly [3][7][8].

## 6. Limitations

The theory surveyed here, powerful as it is, rests on assumptions that limit its reach:

1. **Static models.** Almost all canonical results — the modified Lerner rule, the bottleneck theorem — are static. Real platforms invest dynamically: penetration pricing to build an installed base, with the expectation of harvesting externalities later. Dynamic models (e.g., with switching costs and expectations-driven tipping) exist but lack the clean characterization of the static benchmarks [2][10].
2. **Homogeneous interaction benefits.** The workhorse models assume the cross-group benefit $\alpha_i$ is constant across agents. Heterogeneity — marquee sellers, superstar developers, high-value cardholders — changes optimal price *discrimination* within a side, a dimension the baseline theory suppresses [10].
3. **Exogenous homing.** Whether agents singlehome or multihome is usually assumed rather than derived. In reality, platforms *design* homing through exclusivity contracts, loyalty programs, and API terms (Armstrong and Wright, 2007) — making homing itself a strategic variable [3][7].
4. **Single-platform focus.** Most models study one platform or a symmetric duopoly. Ecosystems of complementary platforms (iOS apps *and* payment rails *and* ad networks) with overlapping sides remain poorly understood theoretically [4].
5. **Empirical identification.** Jointly determined two-sided participation makes causal inference hard; much of the "evidence" is structural and therefore model-dependent, and clean natural experiments (like interchange caps) are rare [8].
6. **Welfare ambiguity.** Skewed pricing can be pro- or anti-competitive depending on parameters: below-cost pricing may reflect efficient internalization of externalities *or* exclusionary investment. The theory gives no shortcut around case-by-case analysis — which is precisely why Evans and Schmalensee insist antitrust must model both sides jointly rather than applying one-sided presumptions [4][6].
