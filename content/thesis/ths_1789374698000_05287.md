# Dynamic Contract Theory: Principal–Agent Models, Incentive Compatibility, and Continuous-Time Moral Hazard

## Abstract

Dynamic contracting extends the static principal–agent problem across time, asking how incentives, risk sharing, and information interact when actions are hidden and relationships endure. This thesis traces the arc from Holmström's static moral-hazard model to Sannikov's continuous-time formulation. We cover the informativeness principle, the first-order approach of Rogerson and Jewitt, and Spear and Srivastava's recursive promised-utility method: the agent's promised utility becomes a state variable, and optimal contracts are characterized by promise-keeping and incentive constraints in a stationary Bellman equation. In continuous time, Brownian output makes promised utility a diffusion; the principal's concave value function solves a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation whose boundaries encode optimal termination and retirement. Applications to executive compensation and dynamic insurance illustrate the theory; we close with its limits, including renegotiation, limited commitment, and the fragility of the first-order approach.

## 1 Intro

The principal–agent problem is the formal study of delegated action under asymmetric information. A *principal* (owner, insurer, shareholder) hires an *agent* (manager, insured, CEO) to take a costly, unobservable action — *effort* — that stochastically determines an observable outcome. Because effort cannot be contracted upon directly, the principal must design a *compensation schedule* mapping observables to payments that simultaneously insures a risk-averse agent and motivates effort. This tension between *risk sharing* and *incentive provision* is the engine of contract theory [7].

Moral hazard, the hidden-action friction, is distinct from *adverse selection*, the hidden-type friction; here the agent's type is known but his action is not. The static version of the problem was essentially settled by the late 1980s [2] [4] [7] [8]. Yet most real contracting relationships — employment, insurance, corporate governance — are *dynamic*: output accrues over time, information arrives gradually, and today's contract shapes tomorrow's incentives. The passage from static to dynamic contracting required new mathematical machinery and produced some of the sharpest results in economic theory [5] [1] [6].

## 2 Background

Consider a risk-neutral principal and a risk-averse agent with separable utility *u(w) − c(a)* over wage *w* and action *a*. Output *x* is drawn from a distribution *F(x|a)* shifting with effort. A contract is a wage schedule *w(x)*. The principal solves

> max over *w(·)* and *a* of E[*x − w(x)* | *a*]
> subject to E[*u(w(x))* | *a*] − *c(a)* ≥ *ū* (IR)
> and *a* ∈ argmax over *ã* of E[*u(w(x))* | *ã*] − *c(ã)* (IC)

where (IR) is the participation constraint with outside option *ū*, and (IC) is the incentive compatibility constraint [7].

The *first-best* contract — attainable if effort were observable — gives the agent full insurance: a constant wage, the principal bearing all output risk. Moral hazard forces a *second-best*: the wage must vary with output to reward effort, exposing the agent to risk.

Grossman and Hart's decisive methodological contribution was a two-step decomposition [7]. First, for each action *a*, compute the *cost of implementation* — the cheapest expected wage bill among contracts satisfying (IR) and (IC) — which, under separability, is a *convex programming problem* in the utility-denominated payments. Second, choose the action maximizing expected surplus E[*x* | *a*] − *C(a)*.

Two pathologies were understood early. Mirrlees showed that when the likelihood ratio is unbounded at the bottom of the output distribution (a "moving support" condition), the principal can *approximate the first-best* with contracts imposing ever-larger punishments for ever-rarer bad outcomes — the counterexample that invalidates naive first-order reasoning.

## 3 Methodology

The modern toolkit for dynamic contracting combines mechanism-design reasoning with stochastic control. Its elements are:

- **Incentive compatibility via the likelihood ratio.** The agent's marginal return to effort is E[*u(w(x)) · ℓ(x|a)*] − *c′(a)* where *ℓ(x|a) = f_a(x|a)/f(x|a)* is the *score*. Incentives are priced by the score: payments should load on output realizations where the score is high [2].
- **The first-order approach (FOA).** Replace the global (IC) constraint with the agent's first-order condition — valid only under distributional conditions (Section 4.2) [4] [8].
- **Recursive formulation.** The entire history is summarized by the agent's *promised (continuation) utility* as a state variable, with *promise-keeping* and *promise-delivering* constraints; the problem becomes stationary [5].
- **Martingale representation in continuous time.** With Brownian output, the agent's continuation value is a martingale under implemented effort, its volatility pinned down by incentive compatibility; the principal's problem reduces to a one-dimensional HJB equation [1].
- **Weak formulation and second-order BSDEs.** Restricting to contracts parameterized by the agent's value process converts a non-zero-sum stochastic differential game into a standard stochastic control problem [6].

| Setting | Contractible state | Form of incentive constraint | Canonical result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Static | Output *x* | Global: *a* maximizes agent's expected utility | Informativeness principle [2] |
| Repeated, discrete time | History; promised utility *w_t* | Per-period IC plus promise-keeping | Memory matters; stationarity [5] |
| Continuous time, Brownian | Continuation value *W_t* (diffusion) | Sensitivity condition *β_t σ = h′(a_t)* | HJB equation; termination/retirement boundaries [1] |
| General volatility control | Value process *Y^{Y_0,Z,Γ}* | Hamiltonian maximization | Reduction to stochastic control via 2BSDEs [6] |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The static model and the informativeness principle

Holmström asked: when does an additional signal *y* — an imperfect monitor of effort, an accounting report, a peer benchmark — strictly improve the optimal contract [2]? Let the joint density be *f(x, y | a)*. The answer is a clean necessary and sufficient condition.

> **Theorem (Informativeness principle).** *An additional signal y can be used to strictly improve upon the optimal contract based on output x alone if and only if the likelihood ratio f_a(x, y | a) / f(x, y | a) is not measurable with respect to x alone — that is, if and only if y is informative about the agent's action conditional on x.*

In words: any signal carrying *incremental* information about effort, beyond what output already reveals, should enter the contract; signals that are pure noise conditional on output are worthless. The optimal contract then loads payments on the *sufficient statistic* for effort. A celebrated corollary is the case for *relative performance evaluation*: if a common shock affects both the agent's output and a peer's, the peer's output filters the shock out of the agent's pay — the theoretical basis for benchmarking executive compensation against industry indices [2].

### 4.2 Validity of the first-order approach: Rogerson, Jewitt, and the Mirrlees critique

The (IC) constraint — a global maximization over actions — is analytically forbidding. The *first-order approach* replaces it with the agent's first-order condition

> ∫ *u(w(x)) f_a(x|a) dx = c′(a)*,

reducing the principal's problem to a tractable constrained optimization with Lagrange multipliers on (IR) and the FOC. But as Mirrlees demonstrated, this replacement is *generally invalid*: the agent's expected utility need not be concave in effort, so a stationary point need not be a global maximum, and the "optimal" FOA contract may not be incentive compatible at all.

Rogerson identified sufficient conditions restoring validity [4]: **MLRC**, the monotone likelihood ratio condition (*ℓ(x|a) = f_a(x|a)/f(x|a)* nondecreasing in *x*, so higher output is better news about effort), and **CDFC**, convexity of the distribution function in *a* (diminishing marginal stochastic returns to effort).

Under MLRC and CDFC, the agent's expected utility is concave in *a* given any nondecreasing wage schedule, so the first-order condition is sufficient for global incentive compatibility. Moreover, the optimal wage schedule is itself nondecreasing in output — a reassuring monotonicity result.

Jewitt showed, however, that the Mirrlees–Rogerson conditions break down once the principal observes *multiple* signals [8]. With a vector of observables, MLRC has no natural multivariate analogue, and CDFC becomes objectionably strong — satisfied by very few textbook distributions even in the single-signal case. Jewitt replaced these with integral conditions on the likelihood ratio that justify the first-order approach in the multi-statistic setting. The lesson is sobering: the first-order approach is a powerful computational device, but its validity must be *earned* distribution by distribution [4] [8].

### 4.3 Recursive contracts: promise-keeping, promise-delivering, and the power of memory

In repeated agency, the principal can condition current pay on the full history of outputs, and *memory matters*: optimal long-term contracts are genuinely history-dependent, with the principal effectively acting as the agent's banker. Strikingly, if the agent had unrestricted credit access, he would undo intertemporal incentives by borrowing and saving — so restricting outside savings can be *necessary* for Pareto optimality.

Spear and Srivastava provided the recursive architecture that made infinite-horizon moral hazard tractable [5]. The key insight: the entire history can be compressed into the agent's *promised utility* *w_t* — the discounted expected utility the contract pledges him from *t* onward. A contract is then described by current compensation, recommended effort, and a lottery over next period's promised utilities contingent on output. Two constraints govern the recursion:

- **Promise-keeping:** the contract must actually deliver the promised utility — current utility plus discounted expected continuation utility equals *w_t*.
- **Promise-delivering (incentive compatibility):** recommended effort must maximize the agent's payoff given how continuation promises respond to output — the spread of *w_{t+1}* across realizations must be steep enough in the likelihood-ratio sense.

Because the problem is stationary in promised utility, the Pareto frontier satisfies a Bellman equation, and standard dynamic programming applies. The state space collapses from an exploding history tree to a single dimension. This *promised-utility approach* became the workhorse of dynamic contracting, underlying models of dynamic insurance, sovereign debt, and unemployment insurance with moral hazard.

### 4.4 Continuous-time moral hazard: from Holmström–Milgrom linearity to Sannikov's diffusion

Holmström and Milgrom took the first continuous-time step [3]. With a CARA agent and output aggregating many small independent increments, the optimal contract is *linear* in accounting aggregates: the agent's rich dynamic strategy space lets him *exploit* any nonlinearity — destroying output to dodge a bonus threshold, or gambling to cross it — so only linear schemes survive. Aggregation follows: what matters is total profit, not the path. This gave the first rigorous foundation for the linear piece rates ubiquitous in practice.

Sannikov's 2008 paper rebuilt continuous-time moral hazard from the ground up, and it is the centerpiece of the modern theory [1]. Output follows

> *dX_t = a_t dt + σ dZ_t*,

a Brownian motion with drift controlled by unobserved effort *a_t*. The agent's *continuation value* *W_t* — his expected discounted future utility — evolves as

> *dW_t = (rW_t − u(c_t) + h(a_t)) dt + β_t σ dZ_t*,

where *β_t* is the contract's *sensitivity* of promised utility to output surprises. Incentive compatibility takes a beautifully simple form: at an interior optimum,

> *β_t σ = h′(a_t)*,

so the agent's exposure to performance shocks, scaled by noise, must equal his marginal cost of effort. The principal's value function *F(W)* — maximal profit given promised utility *W* — then satisfies the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation

> *rF(W) = max over c, a of { a − c + F′(W)(rW − u(c) + h(a)) + (1/2) F′′(W) h′(a)² }*,

where the volatility term uses the IC substitution *β²σ² = h′(a)²*. The principal thus chooses compensation *c* and effort *a* trading off current profit *a − c*, the drift cost of promises, and the risk cost of incentives *(1/2)F′′(W)h′(a)²* — negative because *F* is concave. A mixing argument establishes that concavity: randomizing between two continuation contracts cannot hurt the principal [1].

The general weak formulation of Cvitanić, Possamaï, and Touzi extends this logic far beyond the Brownian drift-control case [6]. They show that attention can be restricted, without loss of generality, to contracts parameterized by the triple *(Y_0, Z, Γ)* driving the agent's value process through the Hamiltonian; the principal's problem becomes a standard stochastic control problem solvable via second-order BSDEs. This rigorous "Sannikov trick" — the agent's value process as a controlled state — now underpins the analysis of volatility control, ambiguity, and multi-agent extensions.

### 4.5 Optimal termination, retirement, and the dynamics of incentives

The HJB equation is solved on an interval *(0, W̄)* whose boundaries encode the economic margins of the relationship [1]:

1. **Termination at the lower boundary.** When promised utility hits zero, the agent is fired (or quits); the principal takes her outside option, so *F(0) = 0* with value matching. Bad performance histories drive *W_t* down until the relationship is no longer worth continuing.
2. **Retirement at the upper boundary.** When promised utility reaches *W̄*, incentives become too expensive: the marginal cost of delivering utility through continued employment exceeds the cost of a lump-sum payment. Effort falls to zero, smooth-pasting holds, and the agent retires with a severance payment.

Between the boundaries, the drift of *W_t* reflects the economics of backloading: because the agent is risk-averse, compensation is deferred to preserve incentives, and the optimal contract mixes *short-term incentives* (pay tied to current output) with *long-term incentives* (promises whose value moves with the output path). The relative weight depends on the contractual environment — limited commitment leans on short-term pay, while full commitment sustains long-term promises.

A darker dynamic lurks when principal and agent discount differently: *immiserization*. If the principal is more patient, optimal risk sharing pushes promised utility inexorably downward along almost every path. Whether real contracts exhibit this misery drift, or whether limited commitment and renegotiation arrest it, remains an active empirical question.

## 5 Empirical Content and Proof Sketches

> **Theorem (Informativeness principle, restated).** *Let the optimal contract based on output x alone be interior. Then there exists a contract based on (x, y) that strictly dominates it if and only if y is informative about a conditional on x.*

> **Proof (sketch).** *Write the principal's Lagrangian with multipliers λ on (IR) and μ on the FOA incentive constraint. The first-order condition for the wage at each (x, y) gives 1/u′(w(x,y)) = λ + μ · f_a(x,y|a)/f(x,y|a). If the likelihood ratio depends only on x, the optimal wage is a function of x alone and y adds nothing. Conversely, if the ratio varies with y given x, a perturbation of the wage in the y-direction — constructed via the score — raises the principal's payoff while preserving (IR) and (IC) to first order.* [2]

> **Proposition (Concavity of the principal's value function).** *In Sannikov's model, F is concave on its domain.*

> **Proof (sketch).** *Randomize at time zero between contracts optimal for promised utilities W¹ and W² with weight α. This delivers promised utility αW¹ + (1 − α)W² and expected profit αF(W¹) + (1 − α)F(W²); since F is the supremum over feasible contracts, concavity follows. Hence F′′ ≤ 0, and the volatility term in the HJB equation is a genuine cost of incentive provision.* [1]

The derivation of the continuation-value dynamics is the technical heart of the continuous-time theory. Under the implemented effort process, the agent's continuation value is a martingale after adjusting for discounting and flow utility; the martingale representation theorem yields the diffusion representation with some sensitivity process *β_t*, and incentive compatibility pins *β_t* down via the agent's Hamiltonian maximization [1] [6].

Empirically, the theory organizes several facts:

- **Pay–performance sensitivity.** Static theory predicts pay should load on the likelihood ratio. Empirical sensitivities are positive but modest, consistent with high risk aversion or with dynamic backloading that static estimates miss.
- **Relative performance evaluation.** The informativeness principle predicts benchmarking against common shocks. Evidence is mixed — a puzzle the theory attributes to signal costs or multitask distortions.
- **Memory in insurance contracts.** Dynamic theory predicts current premia should depend on claim histories (experience rating, bonus–malus schemes) — among the most robust empirical regularities in insurance, rationalized as optimal rather than ad hoc.
- **Vesting and deferral.** The Sannikov model's mix of short- and long-term incentives maps onto salary-plus-equity compensation with vesting schedules: equity whose value moves with the firm's output path is precisely a *β_t*-sensitive continuation promise.

The following Python sketch simulates promised-utility paths under a Sannikov-style contract, illustrating absorption at the termination and retirement boundaries:

```python
import numpy as np

def simulate_promised_utility(W0, Wbar, r, u_c, h_a, beta, sigma, T, dt, n_paths, seed=0):
    """Euler-Maruyama simulation of dW = (r*W - u(c) + h(a)) dt + beta*sigma dZ,
    absorbed at 0 (termination) and Wbar (retirement)."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_steps = int(T / dt)
    W = np.full(n_paths, W0)
    alive = np.ones(n_paths, dtype=bool)
    terminated = np.zeros(n_paths, dtype=bool)
    retired = np.zeros(n_paths, dtype=bool)
    drift = r * W0 - u_c + h_a  # illustrative constant-parameter case
    for _ in range(n_steps):
        dZ = rng.normal(0.0, np.sqrt(dt), size=n_paths)
        W[alive] += drift * dt + beta * sigma * dZ[alive]
        hit_low = alive & (W <= 0.0)
        hit_high = alive & (W >= Wbar)
        terminated[hit_low] = True
        retired[hit_high] = True
        alive[hit_low | hit_high] = False
        if not alive.any():
            break
    return terminated.mean(), retired.mean(), alive.mean()

t, re, al = simulate_promised_utility(1.0, 3.0, 0.05, 0.4, 0.1, 0.8, 1.0, 20.0, 0.01, 20000)
print(f"terminated={t:.3f} retired={re:.3f} still employed={al:.3f}")
```

And the following Rust sketch computes the Grossman–Hart first step — the cost-minimizing contract implementing high effort — in closed form for a binary-outcome model with logarithmic utility:

```rust
/// Binary-outcome moral hazard with u(w) = ln(w).
/// IR and IC both bind; solve the 2x2 linear system for (ln w_s, ln w_f).
fn cost_of_high_effort(p_high: f64, p_low: f64, u_bar: f64, c_high: f64, c_low: f64) -> (f64, f64, f64) {
    // IR: p_high * us + (1 - p_high) * uf = u_bar + c_high
    // IC: (p_high - p_low) * (us - uf) = c_high - c_low
    let delta_p = p_high - p_low;
    let delta_c = c_high - c_low;
    let spread = delta_c / delta_p; // us - uf
    let uf = (u_bar + c_high) - p_high * spread;
    let us = uf + spread;
    let w_s = us.exp();
    let w_f = uf.exp();
    let cost = p_high * w_s + (1.0 - p_high) * w_f; // C(a_high)
    (w_s, w_f, cost)
}

fn main() {
    let (w_s, w_f, cost) = cost_of_high_effort(0.7, 0.4, 1.0, 0.5, 0.1);
    println!("w_success={:.3} w_fail={:.3} implementation cost={:.3}", w_s, w_f, cost);
}
```

## 6 Limitations

The theory's sharpness comes at the price of strong assumptions, and each has generated a literature of qualifications.

- **Renegotiation and limited commitment.** Recursive contracts assume full commitment: the principal never revisits the contract after bad histories. In practice she may renegotiate rather than terminate, and the agent anticipates this — which can destroy the value of long-term promises and push contracts back toward short-term forms.
- **Fragility of the first-order approach.** As Jewitt showed, the sufficient conditions for FOA validity are demanding and fail in natural multi-signal environments [8]. Applied work that blithely replaces (IC) with a first-order condition risks characterizing contracts that are not incentive compatible at all.
- **Risk-neutral agents and limited liability.** With a risk-neutral but wealth-constrained agent, the optimal contract takes a *debt-like* form — the agent posts a bond and keeps residual output — a completely different shape from the smooth likelihood-ratio contracts of the risk-averse case. Limited liability, not risk aversion, then drives the economics.
- **Multitask and multi-agent problems.** Real agents allocate effort across many tasks. Holmström's later multitask analysis shows strong incentives on measurable tasks distort effort away from unmeasurable ones — a force absent from single-task models.
- **Boundedness and the Mirrlees critique.** The extreme-punishment contracts of the moving-support pathology are ruled out by limited liability in practice, but results depending on unbounded likelihood ratios should be treated with suspicion.
- **Empirical identification.** Promised utility is not directly observable, which makes the sharpest dynamic predictions — the law of motion of *W_t*, the location of termination boundaries — difficult to test. Structural estimation of dynamic contracts remains in its early stages.

## 7 Conclusion

Dynamic contract theory transformed moral hazard from a static tradeoff into a *law of motion*. The static foundations — the informativeness principle [2], the Grossman–Hart decomposition [7], and the Rogerson–Jewitt analysis of the first-order approach [4] [8] — taught economists to price incentives with the likelihood ratio and to distrust first-order reasoning without distributional discipline. The recursive revolution [5] compressed histories into promised utility. Continuous-time methods [3] [1] [6] then delivered the theory's most complete laboratory: a diffusion model in which the principal's concave value function solves a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation, and the boundaries of the state space encode the vivid margins of *termination* and *retirement*.

The enduring message is that optimal dynamic contracts are *memory devices*: they use performance history not merely to reward the past but to manage the future — backloading compensation and calibrating risk exposure so incentives survive across time. Where the theory meets practice — vesting schedules, experience-rated insurance, performance-triggered dismissal — this memory is visible. Where it assumes full commitment or perfectly valid first-order conditions, the theory marks its own limits and points to the next questions.
