# Oblivious Data Structures at the Bandwidth Frontier: Pointer-Based OMaps, Hierarchical ORAM Backends, and Lower Bounds on Access-Pattern Hiding

## Abstract

Hiding *what* data is stored is routine; hiding *how* it is accessed is not. Oblivious RAM (ORAM) compiles arbitrary programs into access patterns statistically independent of their inputs, at a bandwidth cost. Oblivious data structures (ODS) go further: rather than obliviously simulating an entire address space, they hide the access patterns of specific structures — maps, stacks, priority queues — exploiting the *predictability* of their access sequences to beat generic ORAM. This thesis traces the arc from the Goldreich–Ostrovsky hierarchical construction through the tree-based revolution of Path ORAM and the near-optimal deterministic eviction of Circuit ORAM, then develops the pointer-based and locality-based techniques by which oblivious AVL trees, oblivious hash tables, and modern oblivious maps (OMaps) are built on these backends. We analyze the bandwidth landscape — O(log² N) per OMap operation classically, O(log² N / log log N) for hash-table-hybrid designs, against the fundamental Ω(log N) lower bound for online ORAMs [1][2][5] — and examine the frontier: closing the gap between oblivious search trees and optimal maps, and the engineering of ODS inside encrypted databases such as ObliDB [8].

## 1. Introduction

The access pattern of a program is a powerful side channel. An adversary who observes *which* memory locations are read or written — even when every value is encrypted — can infer search queries, key presence, join selectivities, and control flow. The classic demonstration is statistical: repeated accesses to the same encrypted record reveal that a database query touched a "hot" key, and access-frequency analysis against public background knowledge can de-anonymize the query itself [1]. Oblivious RAM, introduced by Goldreich and Ostrovsky in the context of software protection [1], is the foundational countermeasure: a compiler that transforms any RAM program into one whose access sequence is indistinguishable from a function of the program's *length* alone.

For two decades ORAM remained theoretical, with overheads in the tens of thousands. The last fifteen years compressed that by three orders of magnitude [6], carrying ORAM into secure processors, encrypted databases, and MPC. A parallel line asked whether full ORAM generality is necessary: many applications need only one data structure, whose access patterns are partially *predictable*. This observation, formalized by Wang et al. [6], yields oblivious counterparts that beat naive ORAM simulation by exploiting structural locality.

This thesis is organized as a vertical slice through that history. Section 2 reviews the hierarchical ORAM paradigm of Goldreich and Ostrovsky and its refinements. Section 3 introduces the two ODS construction techniques and the security definitions they target. Section 4 — the deep dive — examines Path ORAM's stash-and-evict dynamics, Circuit ORAM's deterministic eviction schedule, oblivious AVL-tree OMaps, and modern hash-table-hybrid OMaps. Section 5 consolidates the empirical and provable results: stash-overflow tail bounds, bandwidth-complexity tables, and the Ω(log N) lower bounds for online ORAMs [2][5]. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The ORAM Security Definition

An ORAM is a randomized machine with a small trusted client (the CPU) and a large untrusted server (the memory). On an input sequence of logical operations *y = (op₁, …, op_m)*, the ORAM produces a physical access sequence *A(y)*. Security requires that for any two input sequences *y, y′* of equal length, the distributions of *A(y)* and *A(y′)* are computationally (or statistically) indistinguishable. The *bandwidth overhead* is the multiplicative factor: physical blocks transferred per logical block accessed.

> **Theorem (Goldreich–Ostrovsky lower bound, informal):** Any *balls-and-bins*, statistically secure ORAM with O(1) client storage incurs Ω(log N) amortized bandwidth overhead [1].

Boyle and Naor [4] identified two caveats that shaped a decade of research: the bound needs the *balls-and-bins* model (blocks only shuffled, never re-encoded) and *statistical* security. Computationally secure ORAMs that re-encode data — e.g., via homomorphic encryption — beat it asymptotically, at the price of heavy server computation [5].

### 2.2 The Hierarchical Construction

Goldreich and Ostrovsky's original ORAM [1] organizes server storage as a hierarchy of *levels* 0…L, level ℓ holding up to 2^ℓ blocks in hash buckets. A read scans one bucket per level for the freshest copy; the block is re-inserted at level 0 with a fresh tag, and full levels are periodically *reshuffled* into the next via oblivious sorting, assigning new independent hash positions. Between reshuffles, each probed bucket holds at most one copy of the target, so the probe sequence is a tuple of independent pseudorandom bucket indices.

The original construction achieves O(log³ N) amortized bandwidth overhead: each access scans O(log N) buckets, and the amortized reshuffle cost is O(log² N) [1]. Refinements followed — Goodrich and Mitzenmacher reached O(log² N) [3], Kushilevitz, Lu, and Ostrovsky O(log² N / log log N) — foreshadowing the tree-based revolution.

### 2.3 From Hierarchies to Trees

Shi et al. introduced the *tree-based* ORAM paradigm [7]: server storage is a binary tree whose nodes are *buckets* of Z blocks; every block is assigned a random leaf, and the invariant holds that a block is either in its leaf's *path* buckets or in a small client-side *stash*. An access reads a whole path, remaps the target to a fresh leaf, and *evicts* stash blocks back down the path. The accessed path is always uniformly random — independent of the logical address — so obliviousness is immediate; the hard problem becomes *stash management*.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Two ODS Techniques

Wang et al. [6] observe that simulating an entire address space with ORAM is overkill when the target is a data structure with predictable access patterns. They propose two techniques:

1. **The pointer-based technique.** Take the ordinary pointer-based algorithm — e.g., AVL-tree insertion — and replace every pointer dereference with an ORAM access. If the plain algorithm touches *T* nodes, the oblivious version costs *T × (ORAM overhead)*: for AVL, *O(log N) × O(log N) = O(log² N)* per operation.

2. **The locality-based technique.** Exploit *spatial locality*: accesses guaranteed to fall within a small window (e.g., scanning an array segment) can use an oblivious *shuffle* of the window instead of ORAM, yielding better bounds for sorting-adjacent primitives, memory allocators, and graph algorithms on low-doubling-dimension graphs.

The ODS security definition mirrors ORAM's: the physical access pattern must be simulatable from the *sequence of operation types and public parameters* — not the keys. A crucial subtlety is *operation-type hiding*: Insert and Find must induce indistinguishable patterns, achieved by padding the cheaper operation with dummy ORAM accesses.

### 3.2 ODS as an ORAM Backend Consumer

A recurring theme is that ODS and ORAM research are coupled: every improvement in ORAM bandwidth, stash bounds, or eviction determinism propagates multiplicatively into OMap performance. The table below summarizes the bandwidth landscape the deep dive justifies:

| Construction | Bandwidth overhead per op | Client storage | Security |
|---|---|---|---|
| GO96 hierarchical [1] | O(log³ N) amortized | O(1) blocks | statistical, balls-and-bins |
| Goodrich–Mitzenmacher [3] | O(log² N) amortized | O(1) | statistical |
| Path ORAM, recursive (Z=4) [7] | O(log² N), O(log N) for D = Ω(log² N) | O(log N)·ω(1) blocks | statistical |
| Circuit ORAM [2] | O(D log N)·ω(1), D = Ω(log² N) | O(D) | statistical |
| OptORAMa (computational) | O(log N) | O(1) | computational |
| AVL-ODS OMap [6] | O(log² N) bandwidth | O(1) + ORAM client | statistical |
| OMIX++ OMap (VLDB'24) | O(log² N) bandwidth, O(log N) rounds | — | statistical |
| Hash-hybrid OMap (VLDB'25) [9] | O(log² N / log log N) bandwidth | — | statistical |
| **Lower bound (online ORAM) [5]** | **Ω(log N)** | — | **computational** |

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Path ORAM: Stash Dynamics and the Eviction Invariant

Path ORAM [7] is disarmingly simple. The server holds a binary tree of height *L = ⌈log₂ N⌉*; each node stores a bucket of *Z* blocks (Z = 4 in practice). The client holds a *position map* (logical address → random leaf label) and a *stash* (a small buffer of blocks read but not yet evicted). The core invariant: *every block is either on the path from the root to its assigned leaf, or in the stash.*

```
Access(op, a, data*):                      # Path ORAM, one logical access
    x      ← position[a]                   # old leaf label
    position[a] ←$ {0,1}^L                 # fresh random leaf (remap)
    S      ← ReadPath(x)                   # read Z·L blocks; oblivious: x random
    data   ← block a from S (if present)
    S      ← S ∪ {(a, x', data*)}          # insert/overwrite with new label
    WritePath(x, Evict(S, x))              # push blocks down path x greedily
```

*Obliviousness argument.* The only server-visible values are the accessed paths *x₁, x₂, …*. Every accessed block is remapped to a fresh uniform leaf *before* its old path is revealed, so each *xᵢ* is uniform and independent of the logical address sequence — the transcript is simulatable without the input.

*Stash analysis.* Greedy eviction can fail to place blocks; an overflowing stash breaks the invariant. Stefanov et al. analyze an *infinite ORAM* process with a per-subtree Chernoff argument: for bucket size Z ≥ 4, *Pr[stash > R] ≤ 14 · 0.6^R*, so ~80–100 stash blocks suffice for 2⁻⁸⁰ failure probability [7]. This reduction of a global invariant to per-subtree load statistics is the technical heart of Path ORAM and the template for all subsequent tree-based proofs.

*Recursion.* The position map's *N* entries are stored in a smaller Path ORAM (each block packing *χ = D/log N* labels), recursively. For the important regime *D = Ω(log² N)* the recursion depth is *O(1)* and total bandwidth is *O(log N)* blocks per access [7][2].

### 4.2 Circuit ORAM: Deterministic Eviction and Near-Optimality

Path ORAM's eviction is *opportunistic*: blocks trickle down the accessed path whenever the greedy rule permits. Circuit ORAM [2] replaces opportunism with a *deterministic two-pass eviction schedule* independent of stash contents, yielding two dividends: the access pattern's *circuit complexity* (relevant to secure-computation embeddings) drops dramatically, and the bandwidth constant approaches the lower bound. Each access: (1) reads the path to the block's leaf into the stash; (2) runs a deterministic eviction pass along a *fixed, precomputed* schedule (a reverse-lexicographic sweep), moving each stash block as deep as legally possible; (3) writes back the touched paths.

The key innovation is a *metadata-only* pre-pass: the client first reads only the (small) headers of buckets along the eviction schedule, computes the full eviction plan locally, then performs data movement in a second pass. This two-pass structure is what makes Circuit ORAM's *circuit* representation compact — the original motivation, since the construction was designed for embedding inside garbled-circuit MPC where every server-side branch is expensive [2].

> **Theorem (Circuit ORAM bandwidth [2]):** With block size *D = Ω(log² N)*, Circuit ORAM achieves *O(D log N)·ω(1)* bandwidth and *O(log N)·ω(1)* online round-trips per access, with *negl(N)* statistical failure probability and *O(D)* client storage.

For the regime *D = N^ε* (large blocks), recursion depth becomes *O(1)* and bandwidth is *α·log N* for arbitrary super-constant *α = ω(1)* — matching the Ω(log N) lower bound up to the *ω(1)* factor. This near-tightness shows the logarithmic barrier is the true complexity of online oblivious access, not an artifact of weak constructions [2][4].

### 4.3 Pointer-Based OMaps: The Oblivious AVL Tree

An *oblivious map* (OMap) supports `Insert(k, v)`, `Find(k)`, and `Delete(k)` on key–value pairs with the guarantee that the access pattern leaks nothing about the keys beyond the operation count. The canonical construction, due to Wang et al. [6], applies the pointer-based technique to an AVL tree:

- Each AVL node (key, value, child pointers, balance factor) is stored as one block in an underlying ORAM, addressed by a *node id*.
- `Find(k)` walks from the root — one ORAM access per node, key comparison locally — padding to worst-case depth with dummy reads after early termination, hiding the key's rank.
- `Insert(k, v)` performs the same walk, then unwinds with at most *O(log N)* rotations, each a constant number of ORAM accesses (read the nodes, rewrite pointers, update balance factors).
- *Type hiding* executes the *union* of access patterns: every operation does a full root-to-leaf walk plus a full unwind with dummy rotations, so Insert and Find are indistinguishable.

The bandwidth is *(tree depth) × (ORAM overhead) = O(log N) × O(log N) = O(log² N)* per operation [6]. This multiplicative structure is the central cost equation of pointer-based ODS — and why ODS research tracks ORAM research so closely: halving the ORAM overhead halves every OMap operation.

Subsequent work refined the tree rather than the backend. Roche et al.'s **HIRB** (history-independent B-tree) increases fanout to reduce depth [9]; **ObliDB** [8] instantiates oblivious B⁺-tree indexes inside an SGX enclave, reporting point queries 7.6× faster than the HIRB baseline on million-row tables; and **OMIX++** (VLDB 2024) optimizes the AVL variant to *O(log² N)* bandwidth with *O(log N)* interaction rounds.

### 4.4 Beyond Search Trees: Hash-Table-Hybrid OMaps

Search-tree OMaps inherit a structural tax: every operation must traverse *Ω(log N)* tree levels, each costing an ORAM access. Liu et al. [9] observe that this design philosophy is *self-limiting* — oblivious search trees face a super-logarithmic bandwidth lower bound — and propose the first framework that *combines an oblivious hash table with a search tree*:

- The bulk of key–value pairs live in an **oblivious hash table** (cuckoo hashing with a stash, à la the ODS hash table of [6]), supporting expected-*O(1)* ORAM accesses per lookup.
- A small **overflow structure** — an oblivious search tree — absorbs the hash table's stash and failure cases, preserving worst-case guarantees.
- The two structures are queried in a *fixed, data-independent* pattern (always probe both), so the server cannot tell whether the key was found in the table or the tree.

This yields *O(log² N / log log N)* communication bandwidth with *O(log N / log log N)* rounds [9] — the first asymptotic improvement over the search-tree-only line in a decade. The same paper introduces **DAORAM**, a recursive tree-based ORAM tuned for hash-table workloads (deterministic resets, tighter constants) [10], illustrating the feedback loop in the other direction: ODS workloads driving ORAM design.

A minimal sketch of the pointer-based access pattern, in the style the ODS implementations use, clarifies the padding discipline:

```python
def omap_find(omap, key):
    node_id = omap.root_id
    for _ in range(omap.max_depth):          # FIXED depth: pads early exit
        node = oram.access(node_id)          # physical path: random, oblivious
        if node.is_dummy or node.key == key:
            result, node_id = node.value, DUMMY_ID
        elif key < node.key:
            node_id = node.left
        else:
            node_id = node.right
    return result
```

```haskell
-- Eviction legality: a block labeled with leaf x may rest in any bucket
-- on path(x) at or below its deepest legal depth. Path ORAM and Circuit
-- ORAM differ only in *scheduling* -- when this predicate is exercised.
canPlace :: Leaf -> Depth -> Bucket -> Bool
canPlace leaf d bucket = bucket `onPath` leaf && depth bucket >= d
```

The Haskell predicate captures the invariant both constructions maintain; Path ORAM exercises it opportunistically along the accessed path, Circuit ORAM along a deterministic precomputed schedule.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Provable Bounds

The security proofs here share a skeleton: (i) the transcript is a function of fresh randomness independent of the input (path labels, hash tags); (ii) a stochastic-process argument bounds the failure event (stash overflow, hash-table overload). Three landmarks:

1. **Path ORAM stash bound** [7]: *Pr[stash > R] ≤ 14 · 0.6^R* for bucket size *Z = 4*. The proof couples the real execution to an infinite-capacity variant and applies a Chernoff bound per subtree — a technique now standard in tree-ORAM analysis.

2. **Circuit ORAM near-tightness** [2]: *O(D log N)·ω(1)* bandwidth for *D = Ω(log² N)*, i.e., the GO lower bound is tight up to a sub-polynomial factor in the large-block regime.

3. **Online-ORAM lower bound** [5]: Larsen and Nielsen prove *Ω(log N)* bandwidth overhead for *any* online ORAM — computationally secure, arbitrary encoding — closing the loophole Boyle and Naor identified in the original GO bound [4]. Hubáček et al. strengthen this: even obfuscating the *boundaries* between input operations (variable-length access sequences) cannot beat Ω(log N) [11].

> **Theorem (Larsen–Nielsen [5], informal):** Any ORAM that processes operations online, with server storage *n* and any word size, has bandwidth overhead Ω(log n), even under computational security and arbitrary data representation. The bound matches known constructions when the block size is Ω(log² n).

### 5.2 Reported Empirical Performance

Asymptotics compress three orders of magnitude of engineering. Representative reported numbers:

- **Path ORAM**: with *Z = 4* and a ~100-block stash, failure probability below 2⁻⁸⁰; per-access bandwidth ≈ *8·L* blocks (path read + write at data and position-map levels) [7].
- **ObliDB** [8]: on 10⁶-row tables, point selection 7.6× and insert/delete 3× faster than HIRB+vORAM — though still an order of magnitude slower than plaintext MySQL, a gap the authors argue is dwarfed by wide-area latency.
- **VLDB'25 hash-hybrid OMap** [9]: implemented and benchmarked against OMIX++ and the optimized AVL OMap; the hash-table framework significantly outperforms prior OMAPs in bandwidth and rounds, validating the asymptotic analysis experimentally.

### 5.3 The ODS Cost Equation, Validated

Across all constructions, measured OMap bandwidth follows the predicted multiplicative form *(structural depth) × (ORAM overhead)*, confirming the pointer-based analysis is tight in practice and that backend improvements (Path → Circuit → DAORAM) propagate directly into ODS performance [6][9][10].

## 6. Limitations

**The logarithmic barrier is real.** The Larsen–Nielsen bound [5] says no online ORAM beats Ω(log N); since every known OMap performs Ω(1) ORAM accesses per operation, OMaps inherit an Ω(log N) floor, and current constructions sit at O(log² N) to O(log² N / log log N). Whether an OMap can reach *O(log N)* — matching the ORAM floor — remains open; the oblivious-search-tree lower bound suggests search-tree-only designs cannot, which is precisely the motivation for hash-table hybrids [9].

**Security-model gaps.** The strongest bounds assume *statistical* security in the balls-and-bins model; computational ORAMs with server-side homomorphic computation evade the lower bound but pay in compute [5]. Most ODS deployments (e.g., ObliDB [8]) additionally trust a hardware enclave — a qualitatively different assumption whose side-channel surface (page faults, cache timing) the ODS abstraction does not model.

**Expressiveness limits.** The pointer-based technique requires the underlying algorithm's memory-access *count* to be input-independent (or padded to be); algorithms with data-dependent iteration counts (e.g., while-loops over linked structures) need restructuring, and not all data structures admit efficient oblivious counterparts — oblivious *graphs* with dynamic edge sets remain substantially more expensive than oblivious maps [6].

**Concurrency and persistence.** The constructions surveyed are single-client and volatile: concurrent oblivious access (OPRAM [12]) multiplies costs, and crash-consistent oblivious storage is largely unexplored.

## 7. Conclusion

Oblivious data structures sit at the intersection of two programs: the thirty-year effort to minimize ORAM bandwidth, from Goldreich–Ostrovsky's O(log³ N) hierarchy [1] through Path ORAM's stash analysis [7] to Circuit ORAM's near-optimal deterministic eviction [2]; and the insight that data structures with predictable access patterns need not pay for full address-space obliviousness [6]. The pointer-based technique turns every ORAM advance into an OMap advance multiplicatively, while hash-table hybrids [9] show that rethinking the *data structure* — not just the *memory* — can break decade-old barriers. The Ω(log N) lower bound for online ORAMs [5] sets the floor; the gap to the O(log² N / log log N) state of the art is the field's central open problem, and closing it will require the co-design this thesis documents: backends and data structures engineered together, proofs and benchmarks advancing in lockstep.
