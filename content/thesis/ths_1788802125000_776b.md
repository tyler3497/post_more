# Stateful Hash-Based Signatures for Secure Firmware Updates: XMSS and LMS Merkle Trees, Multi-Tree Hypertrees, State-Cloning Hazards, NIST SP 800-208, and Side-Channel Hardening

## Abstract

Stateful hash-based signatures (HBS) are the only post-quantum signature family whose security rests solely on the preimage and second-preimage resistance of a hash function, with exact, tight, decades-old reductions. This thesis develops an engineering account of the two standardized stateful schemes — XMSS (RFC 8391) and LMS (RFC 8554) — and their multi-tree variants XMSS^MT and HSS, as the signing primitive for post-quantum firmware-update pipelines. We analyze the WOTS+ and LM-OTS one-time blocks, Merkle authentication layers, and BDS tree traversal; then the central deployment hazard, *state cloning*, where VM snapshots, process forking, or lost non-volatile writes silently re-use one-time keys and void the security proof, with the state-reservation discipline of McGrew et al. as countermeasure. We formalize the NIST SP 800-208 profile — hardware-confined signing, non-exportable keys, approved parameter sets — and the NSA CNSA 2.0 mandate prescribing LMS/XMSS for firmware signing. Finally we address physical attacks: timing and power-analysis leakage from WOTS+ chaining and PRF key derivation, and the constant-time, masking, and recomputation countermeasures for hardened signers. Quantitative comparisons of sizes and costs across parameter sets are given, with limitations stated precisely.

## 1 Introduction

Firmware is the most durable code an adversary can compromise and the hardest to remediate. A forged image signed under broken assumptions — by cryptanalysis or a large-scale quantum computer running Shor's algorithm — undermines every device in the field at once, with recovery sometimes requiring physical access. NSA's CNSA 2.0 therefore singles out software and firmware signing as the use case where quantum-resistant migration is *urgent*: it prescribes the stateful hash-based schemes of NIST SP 800-208, LMS and XMSS, and asks vendors to begin adoption immediately [7][8].

Stateful hash-based signatures are the oldest post-quantum idea in existence — Lamport's one-time signatures (1979), Merkle's tree (1979/1989), Winternitz's one-time compression — yet their deployment discipline is the least forgiving of any standardized primitive. The entire security argument collapses to one operational invariant:

> **Theorem (informal):** A stateful hash-based signature scheme is existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack *if and only if* no one-time secret key is ever used to sign two distinct messages, and the underlying hash functions satisfy their stated preimage/second-preimage properties.

The "only if" direction is what makes these schemes *stateful*: the signer must maintain, persist, and never regress a counter identifying the next unused one-time key. Every known catastrophic failure of a stateful deployment — from virtual-machine snapshot restores to `fork()`-induced state duplication to write-back caches that lose the counter increment on power failure — is a violation of this invariant, not a break of any hash function [5][6].

This thesis is written for the engineer building the firmware-signing service: choosing between XMSS and LMS, sizing Merkle trees for a product line's lifetime, placing keys in hardware, surviving state-cloning hazards, and hardening the signer against side channels. We proceed from the cryptographic core (WOTS+, Merkle trees, hypertrees, traversal) through the operational core (state management, SP 800-208, CNSA 2.0) to the physical core (side-channel hardening), closing with quantitative comparisons, limitations, and open problems.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Lamport to Winternitz

Lamport's one-time signature reveals one secret nonce per digest bit; Winternitz's optimization signs *w*-bit digits instead, each digit *b* selecting the *b*-th hash-chain iterate — shrinking signatures ~*w*-fold at *2^w − 1* hashes per chain. Merkle's contribution was authenticating many one-time public keys under one short root via a binary hash tree, so one public key vouches for *2^h* one-time keys at height *h* [3].

**WOTS+** (Hülsing) replaces unkeyed iteration with a *keyed* chaining function whose key and bitmask are PRF-derived per iteration from a public seed, keyed to a 32-byte address (ADRS) encoding the chain's hypertree position [1][2]. This permits the tight proof under *second-preimage resistance* rather than collision resistance — decisive, since Grover degrades collision resistance quadratically but second-preimage resistance only linearly. **LM-OTS** (RFC 8554) instead prefixes every iteration with domain-separation constants and address fields (*I*, *q*, chain, counter), reducing to *collision resistance* of SHA-256 [3].

### 2.2 The statefulness contract

A stateful scheme is a quadruple *(KeyGen, Reserve, Sign, Verify)* (McGrew, Curcio, Fluhrer): signing consumes a reserved one-time key and the private state advances monotonically [5]. The verifier is stateless, but the signer must guarantee (i) *persistence* (the counter survives crashes), (ii) *monotonicity* (it never regresses), and (iii) *exclusivity* (no clone ever holds the same counter). SP 800-208 codifies the consequence: key and signature generation *shall* occur inside hardware modules that forbid export of secret keying material [4].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a specification-grounded engineering analysis, not an experimental measurement campaign. Our method is:

1. **Specification analysis.** We work from the normative texts — RFC 8391 [1], RFC 8554 [2], NIST SP 800-208 [4] — extracting algorithms, parameter registries, and state-management requirements.
2. **Reduction review.** We trace claims to their proofs — Hülsing's tight WOTS+ proof [9], the XMSS EUF-CMA reduction [10], the LMS analysis [3] — recording which hash-function properties each step consumes.
3. **Threat modeling.** We model a canonical firmware pipeline (offline HSM root, per-product/release trees, bootloader verification) and enumerate state-cloning vectors against the mitigations of [5][6].
4. **Quantitative derivation.** Sizes and asymptotic key-generation costs are derived from the RFC parameter sets and cross-checked against published figures.
5. **Side-channel review.** We survey power/timing analyses of hash-based signing [11] to locate WOTS+/PRF leakage and the countermeasures that compose with stateful operation.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 WOTS+ and the XMSS single tree

WOTS+ signs an *n*-byte digest with Winternitz parameter *w ∈ {4, 16}*: the digest becomes *len₁ = ⌈8n / log₂ w⌉* base-*w* digits, and a checksum *C = Σᵢ (w − 1 − bᵢ)* is appended as *len₂* further digits, defeating forgeries that lower a chain position (lowering *bᵢ* only *increases* the checksum). For *n = 32, w = 16*: *len₁ = 64*, *len₂ = 3*, *len = 67* chains [1].

![WOTS+ Winternitz one-time signature chaining](public/thesis/ths_1788802125000_776b-1.webp)

The chaining function — input *X*, start index *i*, step count *s*:

```haskell
-- WOTS+ chaining function (RFC 8391, Algorithm 2), simplified
chain :: Bytes -> Int -> Int -> Seed -> ADRS -> Bytes
chain x i 0 _ _ = x
chain x i s seed adrs
  | i + s > w - 1 = error "chain overflow"
  | otherwise =
      let tmp  = chain x i (s-1) seed adrs
          adrs' = setHashAddress (i + s - 1) adrs
          key  = prf seed (setKeyAndMask 0 adrs')   -- per-iteration key
          bm   = prf seed (setKeyAndMask 1 adrs')   -- per-iteration bitmask
      in f key (tmp `xor` bm)
```

Two PRF calls per iteration buy the tight proof: each *F* call is independently keyed and masked, and the ADRS structure (layer, tree, key-pair, chain, hash addresses) domain-separates every hash call in the hypertree, so a collision for one position cannot be transplanted [1][9].

XMSS places *2^h* WOTS+ key pairs under one binary tree: leaves are *L-tree*-compressed WOTS+ public keys, internal nodes are *H(key, (left ⊕ bmask) ∥ (right ⊕ bmask))* (the Bellare–Rogaway XOR-tree) with PRF-derived keys and bitmasks. The public key is *(root, SEED)* — 68 bytes for *n = 32*. Signing at leaf *idx*: hash *M* with fresh randomizer *r* via *H_msg*, sign with the WOTS+ key at *idx*, attach the *h* authentication-path siblings, increment *idx* [1].

Recomputing the authentication path naively costs *O(2^h)* per signature; the **BDS traversal algorithm** (Buchmann–Dahmen–Szydlo) reduces this to *O(h)* time and *O(h)* stored nodes via a stack of tail nodes and scheduled subtree computation [10].

### 4.2 LMS, LM-OTS, and the HSS hierarchy

LMS (RFC 8554) is the conservative sibling: every hash call is SHA-256 (or SHA-256/192 / SHAKE256 under SP 800-208), with domain separation by explicit prefixes. An LM-OTS private key is *p* random *n*-byte strings; the public key iterates each *2^w − 1* times through *H(I ∥ u32str(q) ∥ u16str(i) ∥ u8str(j) ∥ x)* with 16-byte tree identifier *I*. Signing hashes the message with randomizer *C* under *D_MESG*, splits into base-*2^w* digits plus checksum, and releases the chain nodes [2].

The LMS tree is classical Merkle with heights restricted to *h ∈ {5, 10, 15, 20, 25}*. The **Hierarchical Signature System (HSS)** stacks *L* LMS trees, each signing the public key below it; only active trees are instantiated, so child key pairs are generated lazily, and an HSS signature carries *Nspk = L − 1* signed public keys plus the message signature [2].

For firmware, the hierarchy mirrors the organization: an offline level-0 tree signs per-product level-1 trees, which sign per-release level-2 trees that sign images. The root signs rarely — exactly the access pattern stateful schemes demand [4].

![LMS/HSS hierarchical signing pipeline for firmware updates](public/thesis/ths_1788802125000_776b-3.webp)

### 4.3 XMSS^MT: hypertrees and the capacity question

XMSS^MT splits the tree into *d* layers of height *h/d* for total capacity *2^h*: the bottom layer signs messages, higher layers sign the root below, and a signature concatenates *d* XMSS signatures plus one *idx* and randomizer *r* [1]. Key generation parallelizes per subtree, but signature size grows linearly in *d* (≈ 2.5 KB per layer at *n = 32, w = 16*).

Capacity planning is a first-class activity: an LMS tree at *h = 20* signs ~10⁶ images; XMSS^MT at *h = 60, d = 3* signs 2⁶⁰. The operative question is how many signatures can be *securely counted* — the counter must stay monotonic across the key's lifetime, including HSM replacements and disaster recovery. Over-provisioning height raises key-generation cost without substituting for state custody [4][5].

### 4.4 State-cloning hazards and the reservation discipline

The proof assumes a single, crash-consistent, unclonable signer. Real infrastructure violates this routinely [5][6]:

| Cloning vector | Mechanism | Effect on the counter |
|---|---|---|
| VM snapshot / restore | Backup restored after further signing | Counter regresses to snapshot value |
| Live VM cloning | Two live instances share one key | Both instances hold identical counters |
| `fork()` of signing process | Child inherits in-RAM state | Parent and child sign with same index |
| Write-back cache loss | Power failure before `fsync` of counter | Increment silently lost; index reused |
| Backup restore (DR) | Stale key backup brought online | Counter regresses to backup time |

![State-cloning hazard in stateful hash-based signatures](public/thesis/ths_1788802125000_776b-2.webp)

Any single row is a complete break: two signatures under one WOTS+/LM-OTS key let an attacker combine chain positions digit-wise and forge arbitrary digests. The countermeasure is **reservation** (McGrew, Curcio, Fluhrer): split signing into *reserve-then-sign*, atomically allocating and persisting the next index before any signature bytes exist, with keys pre-generated into a pool behind a single-writer service [5]. SP 800-208 goes further, *requiring* a hardware module with a rollback-protected monotonic counter and no key-export path [4]. Verifiers that archive issued signatures can also check for index reuse — the OTS index is public in every signature — making retrospective detection trivial [5].

Firmware's operational profile is unusually favorable: signing events are rare, centralized, and auditable — which is why CNSA 2.0 selects stateful HBS *for firmware and software signing* while deeming them unsuitable for general use [7][8]. Note the restriction: for NSS, NSA approves **only** single-tree LMS and XMSS; HSS and XMSS^MT are explicitly *not* allowed, and the preferred set is LMS with SHA-256/192 [7].

### 4.5 Side-channel hardening of the signer

The HSM answers cloning, but the signer still computes over secrets — the master seed *S* and chain starting points. Leakage surfaces [11]:

1. **Variable-iteration chaining.** WOTS+ signing iterates each chain a digest-dependent number of steps. The count is public, but a naive loop leaks it through timing, complicating constant-time auditing and interacting with iteration-skipping faults.
2. **PRF key derivation.** Each chain step invokes *PRF(SEED, ADRS)* twice. *SEED* is public, but the master secret *S* behind *PRF(S, ·)* is long-lived and used thousands of times — the classic DPA target, requiring masking or secret-sharing on exposed hardware.
3. **BDS traversal state.** The deterministic computation schedule of the traversal stack can anchor template attacks against interleaved PRF calls under precise EM probing.
4. **Fault attacks.** Skipping a chain iteration or corrupting the checksum yields a signature over a related digest — the hash-based analogue of faulting an ECDSA nonce. Genet's caching countermeasure (storing intermediate WOTS+ nodes, cross-checking recomputation) targets stateful schemes like XMSS^MT specifically [11].

The prescription: fixed-iteration constant-time loops (always iterate *w − 1* and select), masked or split-secret PRF evaluation for *S*, redundant recomputation of the checksum path with comparison before release — all inside the same hardware boundary that enforces state monotonicity [4][11].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Security reductions (proof summary)

- **WOTS+** is strongly EUF-CMA if *F* is a second-preimage-resistant, undetectable one-way function family and *PRF* is pseudorandom; the reduction is tight, so parameters follow directly from the bound [9].
- **XMSS** is EUF-CMA under the same assumptions on *(F, PRF, H, H_msg)*; the original Buchmann et al. design is additionally *forward-secure* with a forward-secure PRG, while the RFC 8391 profile derives all keys deterministically from one seed — trading forward security for a compact secret key [1][10].
- **LMS/HSS** is EUF-CMA assuming *collision resistance* of SHA-256 — stronger than XMSS needs, but with a longer cryptanalytic track record [2][3].

### 5.2 Sizes and costs across standard parameter sets

Sizes below follow directly from the specifications (OTS signature + authentication path + headers) and match published measurements [1][2][4].

| Scheme / parameter set | Capacity | Signature | Public key | Key-gen cost (hash calls, order) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LMS-SHA256-M32-H5 + LMOTS-W4 | 32 | ≈ 2.4 KB | 52 B | ~2⁵ · 68 chains |
| LMS-SHA256-M32-H10 + LMOTS-W8 | 1,024 | **1,420 B** | 52 B | ~2¹⁰ · 33 chains |
| LMS-SHA256-M32-H20 + LMOTS-W8 | ~10⁶ | ≈ 1.7 KB | 52 B | ~2²⁰ · 33 chains (hours; use HSS) |
| XMSS-SHA2_10_256 (w=16) | 1,024 | **2,500 B** | 68 B | 2¹⁰ WOTS+ keygens |
| XMSS-SHA2_20_256 (w=16) | ~10⁶ | ≈ 2.8 KB | 68 B | 2²⁰ WOTS+ keygens (minutes) |
| XMSS^MT-SHA2_20/2_256 (d=2) | ~10⁶ | **4,964 B** | 68 B | 2 · 2¹⁰ subtree keygens |

Verification costs a few thousand hashes — well within bootloader budgets — which is why these schemes fit constrained *verifiers* while *signing* belongs in a data-center HSM. Key generation dominates cost and motivates HSS/XMSS^MT: lazy subtree generation converts infeasible up-front *O(2^h)* into amortized *O(2^{h/L})* per level [2][4].

### 5.3 The firmware-pipeline security argument

Composing the pieces yields the end-to-end claim a certification authority actually needs:

> **Theorem (deployment):** If (i) the instance uses an SP 800-208-approved parameter set, (ii) all signing occurs in a non-exporting hardware module with a rollback-protected monotonic counter implementing reserve-then-sign, (iii) WOTS+/PRF evaluation is constant-time and masked, and (iv) verifiers pin the root key in immutable boot ROM, then firmware forgery requires breaking the hash function's second-preimage (XMSS) or collision resistance (LMS), or physically extracting HSM secrets.

No step of this argument invokes number-theoretic hardness; every assumption is either a symmetric-primitive property believed to survive quantum cryptanalysis (Grover gives at most a quadratic speedup, countered by 256-bit outputs) or a hardware-custody property [4][8].

## 6 Limitations

1. **State is a single point of catastrophic failure.** A stateful key has no safe backup story: any restored backup is a potential forgery oracle. Disaster recovery must mean generating a *new* key and re-certifying the root [5][6].
2. **Not general-purpose.** Both restrict these schemes to controlled signing environments (firmware, software releases). TLS, email, and interactive protocols are out of scope — the state discipline cannot be assured there [4][8].
3. **Capacity is finite.** A height-*h* tree signs at most 2^h messages, then the key is dead. Under-provisioning strands a product line; over-provisioning (single-tree LMS at *h = 25*) makes key generation infeasible without HSS [2].
4. **CNSA 2.0 forbids multi-tree variants for NSS.** HSS and XMSS^MT — the mechanisms that make large capacities practical — are not approved for National Security Systems; only single-tree LMS and XMSS are [7].
5. **Signature sizes strain small transports.** A 1.4–5 KB signature dwarfs 64-byte ECDSA; bootloader parsers and update manifests must be re-engineered [1][2].
6. **The HSM mandate is expensive and centralizing.** It rules out pure-software signers — correct for security, but it concentrates signing capability in a few costly devices [4].
7. **Forward security is not automatic.** The RFC 8391 seed-derived profile does not inherit the original design's forward security; seed compromise exposes all past and future keys — layer key rotation on top if needed [1][10].
8. **Side-channel literature is thinner than for lattices.** Masking and fault-attack guidance for WOTS+ chaining is sparse versus ML-DSA's; high-assurance signers extrapolate from general hash-based work [11].

## 7 Conclusion

Stateful hash-based signatures are uniquely positioned: the most conservatively secure signatures we know, standardized earliest (RFC 8391 in 2018, RFC 8554 in 2019, SP 800-208 in 2020), mandated most urgently (CNSA 2.0 for firmware/software signing) — yet demanding the most disciplined operation of any approved primitive. The cryptography is essentially solved: WOTS+ gives tight reductions to second-preimage resistance; hypertrees scale capacity to 2⁶⁰ and beyond; BDS traversal and HSS lazy keygen make signing practical. The remaining problems are operational and physical: never reusing a one-time key across crashes, clones, forks, and restores; confining signing to non-exporting hardware with monotonic counters; hardening chain evaluation against timing, power, and fault adversaries.

For the firmware engineer, the actionable summary: use LMS-SHA-256/192 (NSA's preferred set) or XMSS-SHA-256 at a tree height matched to the product line's lifetime releases, with margin. Keep the key in a validated HSM implementing reserve-then-sign on a rollback-protected counter; treat backups as key-replacement events, never restores. Verify in immutable boot ROM with CAVP-validated code. Harden WOTS+ chaining to constant time and mask the master seed. Then the update channel rests on nothing more exotic than the preimage resistance of SHA-256 — an assumption that has survived every classical and quantum attack for two decades [1][2][4][7].
