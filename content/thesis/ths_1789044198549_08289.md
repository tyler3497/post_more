# Denotation and Dynamics: The Semantics of Probabilistic Programs, Bayesian Inference Algorithms, and the Architecture of Probabilistic Programming Languages

## Abstract

Probabilistic programming languages (PPLs) treat *programs as specifications of probability distributions* and inference as *automatic conditioning*, unifying model specification, semantics, and computation. This thesis examines the field along three axes. First, we survey the **semantics of probabilistic programs**, from Kozen's measure-transformer semantics through modern denotational accounts, including the treatment of higher-order functions, continuous distributions, and soft constraints established by Staton et al. Second, we analyze **Bayesian inference algorithms** for probabilistic programs: Metropolis–Hastings on execution traces, sequential Monte Carlo and particle MCMC, and stochastic variational inference with the reparameterization estimator, including the No-U-Turn Sampler that made gradient-based turnkey inference practical. Third, we examine PPL **design** — restricted first-order languages (Stan, BUGS) versus universal higher-order languages (Church, Venture, Anglican, Pyro, Gen) — and the trade-offs between expressiveness and inference generality. We argue that a probabilistic program's meaning is best understood as the *composition of its denotation as a measure transformer and the operational contract of its inference engine*.

## 1 Introduction

A probabilistic program is, at once, a *generative story* and a *queryable artifact*. Consider the following: a short program samples a coin's bias from a Beta prior, flips the coin, and conditions on an observed sequence of heads. The program's *meaning* is the posterior distribution over the bias — a mathematical object — while its *execution* is whatever algorithm the language runtime deploys to approximate that posterior. That these two aspects can and should be separated is the foundational insight of probabilistic programming: the modeler writes the *what*, and the inference engine supplies the *how*.

This separation was not always obvious. Early systems such as BUGS [7] fused the model specification language with a specific inference strategy (Gibbs sampling over a static graphical model). The modern PPL literature, by contrast, treats the *semantics of the modeling language* and the *correctness of inference* as distinct, composable research problems. On the semantics side, the challenge is to give a mathematically precise meaning to programs that combine ordinary higher-order computation with random sampling, conditioning, and even nested queries — programs whose random choices may be unbounded in number and whose types may include distributions over functions themselves. On the inference side, the challenge is to design *generic* algorithms — Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), sequential Monte Carlo (SMC), variational inference (VI) — that operate on the execution traces of arbitrary programs and provably target the distribution the program denotes.

This thesis integrates these strands. We begin with the semantic foundations (Section 2), develop a precise account of the inference algorithms that give probabilistic programs their operational life (Sections 3–4), present empirical and theoretical results (Section 5), and close with the hard limitations that still constrain the field (Section 6).

The central thesis is this: **a probabilistic program denotes a measure transformer, and every design decision in a PPL — its primitives, its type system, its inference backends — is a decision about which class of measure transformers can be written and how efficiently the induced conditioning problem can be solved.**

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Bayesian models to probabilistic programs

Classical Bayesian modeling proceeds in three steps: (i) write down a joint distribution $p(x, y)$ over latent variables $x$ and observations $y$; (ii) condition on the data, $p(x \mid y) \propto p(y \mid x)p(x)$; (iii) compute expectations under the posterior. A probabilistic program generalizes step (i): instead of a closed-form density, the model is an *executable stochastic process* — a program that, when run, produces a random execution trace. Conditioning is expressed in the program itself, through constructs conventionally named `observe`, `score`, or `factor`.

The earliest rigorous semantics for such programs is due to Kozen [8], who interpreted a probabilistic program as a **measure transformer**: a function mapping an input probability measure (over initial states) to an output measure (over final states), with conditioning corresponding to renormalization. This view already separates the *denotation* of the program from any particular simulation strategy.

### 2.2 Church and universal probabilistic programming

Goodman et al. introduced **Church** [3], a universal PPL built on the Lisp model of the $\lambda$-calculus, containing a pure Lisp as its deterministic subset. The semantics of Church is defined in terms of *evaluation histories* — the random choices made during evaluation — and conditional distributions over such histories. Church's distinctive contributions include:

- **Viewing evaluation as sampling and `query` as conditional sampling**: any program denotes a distribution, and `query` reifies the conditional distribution given observed predicates.
- **The stochastic memoizer** (`mem`): a construct letting separate evaluations share generative history, which enables concise description of nonparametric models such as the Dirichlet process mixture.
- **Generic inference**: exact and approximate Monte Carlo schemes implementing `query`, so that *any* Church program can be run without special-purpose inference code.

Because Church is Turing-complete, a Church program may make an unbounded (even infinite) number of random choices; its "random variables" are not statically enumerable. This universality is precisely what breaks the static graphical-model compilation used by BUGS-like systems and motivates trace-based inference.

### 2.3 The first-order / higher-order distinction

Van de Meent et al. [5] draw the field's most load-bearing taxonomic line:

- **First-order PPLs (FOPPLs)** — Stan, BUGS, Infer.NET — restrict random choices to fixed positions, guaranteeing that every program induces a model with a *finite* set of random variables. These languages compile to a static density function and exploit gradients and conjugacy aggressively.
- **Universal (higher-order) PPLs** — Church, Venture, Anglican, WebPPL, Pyro, Gen, Turing.jl — allow random choices in any statement or expression. Models may have stochastic support (the set of latent variables itself is random), and inference must be fundamentally *generative*, operating on execution traces.

This thesis is primarily concerned with the universal class, while using Stan as the canonical example of what restriction buys: automatic differentiation plus the No-U-Turn Sampler [2] yields inference so reliable it is nearly turnkey.

### 2.4 Bayesian inference in one paragraph

Given a program denoting an unnormalized density $\gamma(x) = p(y \mid x)p(x)$ over latent variables $x$, the posterior is $\pi(x) = \gamma(x)/Z$ with $Z = \int \gamma$. Inference algorithms approximate $\pi$ or expectations $\mathbb{E}_\pi[f]$. Three families dominate: **MCMC**, which constructs a Markov chain with stationary distribution $\pi$; **SMC**, which propagates a population of weighted particles through a sequence of intermediate distributions; and **VI**, which optimizes a tractable family $q_\phi$ to minimize $\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi \,\|\, \pi)$, equivalently maximizing the evidence lower bound (ELBO).

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is threefold, mirroring the structure of the field itself.

**Semantic analysis.** We formalize a core probabilistic metalanguage in the style of Staton et al. [1], with three primitive effect operations:

| Construct | Operational reading | Denotational reading |
|---|---|---|
| `sample(t)` | Draw from the distribution denoted by `t` | Kleisli extension of the Giry monad |
| `score(t)` | Multiply the trace weight by the positive real `t` | Density reweighting of the current measure |
| `norm(u)` | Run inference on the subprogram `u` | Normalization: $\mu \mapsto \mu / \mu(\top)$ |

The denotation of a program is a **s-finite kernel** between measurable spaces; `score` multiplies by a density and `norm` renormalizes, turning soft constraints into genuine conditional distributions. We examine how this semantics validates the program equations underlying SMC [1].

**Algorithmic analysis.** For each inference family we derive the estimator, state its correctness theorem, and analyze its computational profile: asymptotic variance, gradient requirements, and behavior under stochastic support.

**Design-space analysis.** We compare six representative PPLs along the axes of expressiveness, inference strategy, and programmability of inference, and we implement a small single-site Metropolis–Hastings engine over execution traces in Python to make the trace-based view concrete.

> **Theorem:** (Measure-transformer compositionality.) *If $\llbracket e_1 \rrbrow$ and $\llbracket e_2 \rrbrow$ are the kernel denotations of two program fragments, then $\llbracket e_1; e_2 \rrbrow = \llbracket e_1 \rrbrow \mathbin{;} \llbracket e_2 \rrbrow$, Kleisli composition in the category of s-finite kernels. In particular, the meaning of a probabilistic program is determined compositionally from the meanings of its parts, and two programs with equal denotations admit the same posterior under any correct inference algorithm.*

This is the formal content of the slogan "semantics-preserving program transformations denote the same distribution" [5, Ch. 1], and it is what licenses compiler optimizations — common-subexpression elimination, conjugacy-aware simplification, algebraic manipulation of densities — in systems like Hakaru [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Denotational semantics: the Giry monad and s-finite kernels

The cleanest modern account interprets probabilistic programs in the **Giry monad** $\mathcal{G}$ on measurable spaces: a distribution over $X$ is a probability measure, and sequencing `x <- sample(d); k(x)` is Kleisli composition. Deterministic functions embed via the Dirac unit $\eta(x) = \delta_x$. Conditioning via `score` requires leaving the probability monad: scores multiply measures by densities, producing *s-finite* (countable sums of finite) measures, and `norm` projects back to probability measures.

Staton et al. [1] show that this structure supports the full expressive wish-list — higher-order functions, continuous distributions, soft constraints, and even *nested* `norm` (queries within queries, the probabilistic analogue of nested database queries) — and they prove **soundness, adequacy, and termination** of the metalanguage with respect to both an operational semantics (stochastic labelled transition systems, where sampled variables read as dynamically allocated read-only variables) and the denotational semantics (functor categories over measurable spaces). Their semantics validates non-trivial equations, for instance that a certain SMC-style resampling scheme is equivalent to plain importance weighting — exactly the kind of equation an inference-engine author needs to know is true.

Borgström et al. [7] give a complementary **measure transformer semantics** for a first-order Bayesian language, compiling programs to density functions over a fixed vector of variables; this is the semantic story behind Stan-style systems, where the program *is* the log-density and inference is a numerical problem on $\mathbb{R}^n$.

### 4.2 Trace-based MCMC: single-site Metropolis–Hastings

For universal languages, the dominant exact-in-the-limit strategy is MCMC over **execution traces**. A trace $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ records every random choice; the program induces an unnormalized density $\gamma(\mathbf{x}) = \prod_i p_i(x_i \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \cdot \prod_j \ell_j(\mathbf{x})$ combining sample densities and observe/score likelihoods. Wingate et al. [4] showed that a remarkably simple algorithm — pick one random choice $x_k$ uniformly, propose $x_k' \sim q(\cdot \mid x_k)$, re-execute the program's continuation deterministically, and accept with probability

$$\alpha = \min\left(1,\, \frac{\gamma(\mathbf{x}')\, q(x_k \mid x_k')}{\gamma(\mathbf{x})\, q(x_k' \mid x_k)}\right)$$

— is a valid Metropolis–Hastings kernel on the space of traces, *even when the proposal changes the number of random choices* (stochastic support), because choices absent from one trace simply contribute density 1 to the other. Venture [6] industrializes this idea with **probabilistic execution traces (PETs)** — graph-structured histories capturing conditional, existential, and exchangeable dependencies — and **scaffolds**, partitions of the trace that factor global inference into coherent local subproblems amenable to blocked Gibbs, particle, and variational updates. The stochastic procedure interface (SPI) encapsulates primitives analogously to CPTs in a Bayes net, supporting custom control flow and likelihood-free simulators.

```python
def single_site_mh(trace, log_gamma, propose, n_iters):
    """Generic single-site Metropolis-Hastings over execution traces."""
    cur = trace
    cur_lp = log_gamma(cur)
    samples = []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        # 1. choose a random choice uniformly and propose a change
        k = random.randrange(len(cur))
        prop = list(cur)
        prop[k] = propose(cur[k])
        # 2. re-execute downstream choices deterministically (omitted:
        #    in a real PPL this re-runs the program continuation)
        prop_lp = log_gamma(prop)
        # 3. Metropolis-Hastings accept/reject on the trace space
        if math.log(random.random()) < prop_lp - cur_lp:
            cur, cur_lp = prop, prop_lp
        samples.append(tuple(cur))
    return samples
```

The beauty of this scheme is its *generality*: the same kernel applies to any program in the language. Its curse is *mixing*: single-site proposals wander slowly through high-dimensional, correlated posteriors, which is why the field simultaneously pursued gradient-based methods.

### 4.3 Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and the No-U-Turn Sampler

For differentiable models — the FOPPL sweet spot — **Hamiltonian Monte Carlo** (HMC) suppresses random-walk behavior by simulating Hamiltonian dynamics on an augmented phase space $(q, p)$ with potential energy $U(q) = -\log \gamma(q)$ and kinetic energy $K(p) = \tfrac{1}{2}p^\top M^{-1} p$. Leapfrog integration proposes long, coherent moves that are accepted with high probability, yielding the dramatic efficiency gains that made Stan practical [2].

But HMC demands two tuning parameters: step size $\epsilon$ and trajectory length $L$. Too small an $L$ recovers random-walk behavior; too large wastes computation. Hoffman and Gelman [2] introduced the **No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS)**, which builds a binary tree of leapfrog steps by recursive doubling and stops when the trajectory begins to retrace itself — the "U-turn" condition $(q^+ - q^-)\cdot p^- < 0$ or $(q^+ - q^-)\cdot p^+ < 0$ — while slice-sampling a candidate point from the visited set so that detailed balance is preserved. Step size is adapted on the fly by primal–dual averaging toward a target acceptance statistic $\delta$ (default $0.65$ for HMC, $0.6$–$0.8$ for NUTS in practice). The result is a *turnkey* sampler: no hand-tuning, yet empirically at least as efficient as optimally tuned HMC, and on some targets (multivariate normal, stochastic volatility) better by factors of $1.5$–$2$ in effective sample size per gradient evaluation [2]. NUTS is the engine behind Stan's reputation for "just works" inference, and its success shaped PPL design: every serious FOPPL now ships an adaptive HMC variant as its default.

### 4.4 Sequential Monte Carlo and particle MCMC

**Sequential Monte Carlo** attacks the problem from the importance-sampling direction. Given a sequence of distributions $\pi_0, \dots, \pi_T = \pi$ bridging prior to posterior (or data arriving sequentially), SMC maintains $N$ weighted particles, propagating, weighting by incremental likelihood ratios, and *resampling* when the effective sample size collapses. For probabilistic programs, the "sequence" is the program's own execution order: Anglican pioneered SMC over program traces, treating each `sample`/`observe` as a filtering step [5].

SMC's killer application inside PPLs is **particle MCMC**: particle Gibbs and particle marginal Metropolis–Hastings use an SMC sweep as a high-dimensional proposal inside an outer MCMC loop, and remain exact — they target $\pi$ for *any* fixed particle count $N$ [4]. This compositionality (SMC inside MCMC inside a programmable-inference language) is exactly what Venture's scaffold machinery [6] and Gen's combinators were built to express.

> **Theorem:** (SMC unbiasedness and consistency.) *Under standard integrability conditions, the SMC normalizing-constant estimator $\hat{Z}_N$ is unbiased, $\mathbb{E}[\hat{Z}_N] = Z$, and the particle approximation $\hat{\pi}_N = \sum_i w_i \delta_{x_i}$ converges weakly to $\pi$ almost surely as $N \to \infty$.*

### 4.5 Variational inference, reparameterization, and amortization

**Variational inference** trades asymptotic exactness for speed: choose $q_\phi$ from a tractable family and maximize the ELBO

$$\mathcal{L}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[\log p(x, y) - \log q_\phi(x)] \le \log p(y),$$

with equality iff $q_\phi$ equals the posterior. The modern era of VI in PPLs begins with Kingma and Welling [4]: the **reparameterization trick** writes $x = g_\phi(\epsilon)$ with $\epsilon \sim p(\epsilon)$ fixed, so that

$$\nabla_\phi\, \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[f(x)] = \mathbb{E}_{p(\epsilon)}[\nabla_\phi f(g_\phi(\epsilon))],$$

a low-variance pathwise gradient estimator that makes the ELBO amenable to stochastic gradient ascent and — crucially — to *amortization*: parameterize $q_\phi(x \mid y)$ with a neural recognition network shared across data points, yielding the variational autoencoder. Pyro [6] is architected around this idea: models are Python functions with `sample`/`param` primitives, guides are inference programs, and **effect handlers** (the `poutine` system) non-invasively reinterpret model execution — tracing, replaying, blocking sites, enumerating discrete variables — so that SVI, importance sampling, and MCMC compose as program transformations rather than compiler passes. Black-box VI [5] extends the reach further with score-function (REINFORCE) estimators $\nabla_\phi \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[f] = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[f \cdot \nabla_\phi \log q_\phi]$, applicable whenever $\log q_\phi$ is differentiable even if $f$ is not — at the cost of higher variance, tamed in practice by control variates and Rao-Blackwellization.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

**NUTS vs. tuned HMC.** Hoffman and Gelman [2] benchmark on four targets — multivariate normal (MVN), logistic regression (LR), hierarchical logistic regression (HLR), and stochastic volatility (SV) — measuring effective sample size (ESS) per gradient evaluation. Optimal HMC trajectory lengths vary by a factor of ~100 across targets (from $L \approx 18$ for MVN down to very short for SV); NUTS, with no trajectory tuning, matches or exceeds the best HMC configuration on all four, producing effectively independent samples ~2× more efficiently on MVN and ~1.5× on SV. The dual-averaging target $\delta = 0.6$ is a robust default across targets.

**Trace-MH correctness.** The single-site MH kernel of Section 4.2 satisfies detailed balance with respect to $\pi(\mathbf{x}) \propto \gamma(\mathbf{x})$ on the union of trace spaces of all lengths, because the proposal over the differing coordinates is symmetric in the extended space and the acceptance ratio telescopes to the density ratio [4]. Ergodicity follows when the per-site proposals have full support.

**SMC program equations.** Staton et al. [1] prove denotationally that resampling-based SMC computes the same normalized distribution as the naive importance-weighted semantics — a *program equation* validated by the semantics, not by Monte Carlo asymptotics. This is the payoff of the semantic program: inference algorithms become theorems about programs.

**VI amortization at scale.** Kingma and Welling [4] demonstrate stochastic-gradient variational Bayes scaling to large i.i.d. datasets with per-datapoint continuous latents — a regime where per-datapoint MCMC is infeasible — with the recognition network amortizing inference cost across the dataset. The ELBO gap to the true marginal likelihood quantifies the approximation error, and is exactly the KL divergence being minimized.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Differentiability and stochastic support.** Gradient-based engines (HMC/NUTS, reparameterized VI) require a fixed-dimensional, differentiable log-density. Universal programs with stochastic branching induce densities that are discontinuous — or not even defined on a common space — across traces. Pyro's enumeration and Gen's involutive MCMC are partial answers; a fully general, efficient gradient estimator for arbitrary universal programs remains open.

2. **Inference is not compositional in practice.** The denotation composes; the *efficiency* of inference does not. A program assembled from well-behaved fragments can still defeat every generic algorithm (multimodal posteriors, funnel geometries, stiff likelihoods), forcing the modeler back into inference programming — Venture's [6] raison d'être.

3. **Nested queries and normalization.** `norm` inside `sample` — inference within the model — is semantically delicate (Staton et al. [1] treat it; naive implementations are unsound), and nested SMC/MCMC estimators introduce bias that only vanishes asymptotically in the inner particle/iteration count [5].

4. **Undecidability of program equivalence.** Because universal PPLs are Turing-complete, questions like "do these two programs denote the same distribution?" are undecidable in general; semantic reasoning scales only for restricted fragments (finite discrete models admit exact compilation via variable elimination or knowledge compilation [5]).

5. **The ELBO gap and VI bias.** Variational approximations are asymptotically biased; in misspecified or highly correlated models the bias can dominate, and diagnosing it requires exactly the expensive MCMC one hoped to avoid.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Probabilistic programming is the discipline of making Bayes' rule *executable*: a program denotes a measure transformer, `score` reweights it, `norm` conditions it, and the inference backend — whether trace-MCMC, SMC, NUTS, or amortized VI — computes with the resulting posterior. The semantic line of work from Kozen [8] through Staton et al. [1] tells us what programs *mean*; the algorithmic line from Church's generic `query` [3] through Venture's programmable inference [6], NUTS [2], and Pyro's effect handlers tells us how that meaning is *computed*. The enduring design tension is between universality and tractability: every construct that makes a language more expressive — higher-order distributions, stochastic memoization, nested queries, stochastic support — makes some inference algorithm's job harder, and every restriction that makes inference turnkey — fixed support, differentiability, conjugacy — shrinks the class of models one can write. Progress in the field, we argue, will come not from resolving this tension but from *programming* it: languages in which the inference strategy is itself a first-class, composable, and semantically justified artifact.
