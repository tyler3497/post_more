# Iris Higher-Order Concurrent Separation Logic: Invariants and Ghost State, Prophecy Variables for Linearizability, and Mechanized Verification of Fine-Grained Concurrent Data Structures in Coq

## Abstract

We present a systematic account of mechanized verification of fine-grained concurrent data structures in Iris, the higher-order concurrent separation logic in Coq [1]. The core difficulty is that operations on lock-free queues, elimination-backoff stacks, and read-copy-update lists linearize at points that cannot be identified locally: they may lie in the future, depend on interleaving, or be executed by helping threads. We show how three mechanisms address it. First, Iris invariants internalize interference as duplicable shared knowledge, while higher-order ghost state reifies each structure's abstract protocol as ownable, splittable resources [2]. Second, prophecy variables let proofs speculate about future outcomes — above all the success or failure of a compare-and-swap — and resolve the speculation exactly when the linearization point executes, extending logical atomicity to future-dependent linearization points [5]. Third, a disciplined architecture built on atomic triples and the MoSeL proof mode turns these ideas into checkable Coq developments at the scale of the Michael-Scott queue and multiversion concurrency control [8][9]. We describe methodology, mechanisms, statistics, and limits.

## 1 Introduction

Concurrent data structures are among the most delicate artifacts in systems software. A lock-free queue manipulates a handful of machine words shared by many threads, and its correctness rests on subtle agreements: node ownership after unlinking, visibility of enqueues to concurrent dequeues, and retry behavior after failed compare-and-swaps. Separation logic gave us a way to reason about ownership of disjoint heap fragments [1]. Iris extends this idea to concurrency through *invariants*: assertions that are true at all times from the perspective of every thread, which may be opened around a single atomic step and must be re-established before the step completes. Because invariants are duplicable, every thread can rely on them simultaneously, and opening an invariant grants temporary exclusive access to its contents, enabling coordinated updates without a global lock [1].

> **Definition:** An *Iris invariant* `inv N P` packages an assertion `P` under a namespace `N` such that `P` is duplicable knowledge, while a single atomic step may temporarily acquire `P`, transform it into `P'`, and close the invariant, provided no other invariant is open at the same time.

Yet invariants alone do not suffice for fine-grained structures. The specification of an enqueue operation should say that at some instant during its execution, the abstract queue atomically gains the new element. That instant — the *linearization point* — is not a fixed program location in general [4]. In the Michael-Scott queue, the linearization point of `dequeue` is the successful compare-and-swap that swings the head pointer, but the proof cannot know in advance whether the particular CAS it is about to execute will succeed or be pre-empted by a competitor [12]. In elimination-backoff stacks, an operation may never touch shared state at all, linearizing instead through a collision with a dual operation in the elimination array. And in RDCSS-style helping schemes, the linearization point of an operation may be executed by a *different* thread [5].

This article explains how Iris handles all three phenomena via higher-order ghost state [2], prophecy variables [5], and a mechanized Coq architecture of atomic triples and the MoSeL proof mode [9]. We close with quantitative observations on proof effort and an honest account of the limitations.

## 2 Background

### Separation logic and the step to concurrency

Classical separation logic reasons about programs with the *frame rule*: if a command is correct on a small heap, it remains correct on any larger heap, with separating conjunction `P ∗ Q` asserting ownership of disjoint resources [1].

The move to shared-memory concurrency introduces interference: other threads may mutate shared state between any two steps of the thread under verification. Iris addresses interference with two constructs. First, *invariants* as defined above capture the agreement among threads. Second, *ghost state* provides resources that exist only in the proof, not in the program, and that evolve according to user-declared transition systems. Ghost state is the vocabulary in which protocols are written [2].


### Linearizability and logical atomicity

Herlihy and Wing's *linearizability* is the standard correctness criterion: every concurrent execution must equal some sequential execution respecting real-time order of non-overlapping operations [4]. Mechanized proofs rarely reason about histories directly — histories are global objects that resist modular decomposition. Instead, Iris internalizes an equivalent notion: *logical atomicity*.

A logically atomic specification of an operation is written with an *atomic triple*, historically notated `⟨ P ⟩ e ⟨ v. Q ⟩`. It says: if the precondition `P` holds when the operation begins, then at some single instant during its execution — the linearization point — the abstract state transitions from `P` to `Q` with return value `v`, and no client can observe the intermediate steps. TaDA pioneered this style of atomic specification [3]; Iris generalized it to higher-order programs with a full treatment of helping and prophecy [5].

> **Lemma:** (Atomicity adequacy) If every operation of a concurrent module satisfies a logically atomic specification, then the module's observable behavior is linearizable: any concurrent history can be reordered into a sequential one consistent with real-time order [6].

This lemma lets us verify linearizability *locally*, one operation at a time; Frumin et al. showed the connection is a *free theorem* proved once in Coq, so individual verifications need only establish the atomic triple [6].

### The verification challenge

Consider the `dequeue` of the Michael-Scott queue [12]: its linearization point is the successful CAS on the head pointer, but the proof must commit the abstract transition *before* executing the CAS, when it cannot yet know whether the CAS will succeed. Committing unconditionally is unsound; deferring is impossible, because after the CAS the atomic step has passed. This is the *future-dependent linearization point* problem that prophecy variables solve [5].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology for verifying a fine-grained concurrent data structure in Iris/Coq follows a disciplined pipeline with four stages.

**Stage 1: Abstract specification.** We define the abstract state — for a queue, a mathematical sequence — and write a logically atomic specification per operation; `enqueue`, for instance, grows the sequence by one element at a single instant. Clients reason only about this specification, never about pointers or CAS loops [3][6].

**Stage 2: Ghost protocol.** We design a resource algebra whose elements represent the protocol state: the abstract sequence, per-node ownership tokens, helping obligations, and timestamps. Higher-order ghost state is essential because the protocol may quantify over assertions, e.g. when a helping thread discharges *another* thread's atomic triple [2].

**Stage 3: Invariant construction.** We package the physical heap layout and the ghost protocol into an Iris invariant — the *coupling relation* between the concrete linked structure and the abstract state. Every thread shares it; opening it around an atomic step grants momentary exclusive access to physical nodes and ghost tokens [1].

**Stage 4: Prophecy-assisted linearization.** For future-dependent linearization points, the proof allocates a prophecy variable predicting the decisive nondeterministic choice — typically the Boolean CAS result — *before* the linearization step, commits the abstract transition under that prediction, and *resolves* the prophecy when the physical step executes. A mispredicted branch is contradictory and closes by explosion; only the matching branch survives [5].

> **Definition:** A *prophecy variable* is a ghost resource `proph p vs` asserting that the prophecy named `p` will resolve to the value sequence `vs`. Resolving `p` to `v` requires owning the exclusive token and yields the knowledge `vs = v :: vs'`. Soundness rests on the fact that the prophecy's value is determined by the future of the execution, which the logic treats as already fixed but unknown.

| Stage | Artifact | Key Iris mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Abstract specification | Atomic triples per operation | Logically atomic Hoare triples |
| Ghost protocol | Resource algebra definition | Higher-order ghost state, cameras |
| Invariant construction | Coupling invariant | `inv` namespaces, fancy updates |
| Linearization | Prophecy creation and resolution | Prophecy variables, authoritative ghost |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Invariant Protocols and Higher-Order Ghost State

The heart of an Iris verification is the invariant, and the heart of the invariant is the ghost protocol. Let us make this concrete with the Michael-Scott queue [12]. Physically the queue is a linked list with `head` and `tail` pointers and a sentinel node; the protocol tracks the abstract sequence, node ownership versus logical dequeue, and the agreement between the physical `head` and the abstract front.

We model this with an *authoritative* resource algebra: one authoritative element tracks the full abstract sequence while threads hold *fragment* snapshots. The authoritative element updates only in fragment-preserving ways — appending preserves every fragment's claim about the queue's prefix — a standard pattern from Iris's camera library [1][2].

Higher-order ghost state enters when the protocol stores *assertions* rather than data. In helping schemes — the elimination-backoff stack, Harris's linked list — one thread may complete another's operation, so the protocol stores the *atomic update token* of the helped operation as ghost data: a higher-order resource whose discharge yields the postcondition the original caller awaits. Iris cameras can thus be instantiated with assertion-valued elements, which first-order ghost state cannot express [2].

The invariant ties everything together:

```coq
Definition queue_inv (γh γt γa : gname) (hd tl : loc) : iProp Σ :=
  ∃ (l : list val) (nodes : list loc),
    own γa (● l) ∗
    ⌜Forall (fun n => is_node n) nodes⌝ ∗
    hd ↦ nodes ∗ tl ↦ nodes ∗
    ghost_link_protocol γh γt nodes l.
```

Opening this invariant around a CAS grants temporary ownership of the physical pointers *and* the authoritative sequence, performs the coupled update, and closes the invariant — all within one atomic step, as the atomic triple demands [1].

> **Theorem:** (Invariant soundness) If `inv N P` has been established, then `P` holds at every program point observable by any thread, except transiently inside an atomic step that has opened the invariant and not yet closed it. In particular, no thread can observe a state in which the coupling between physical and ghost state is broken.

### 4.2 Prophecy Variables for Future-Dependent Linearization Points

We now confront the central difficulty. In the Michael-Scott `dequeue`, the proof reaches the head-pointer CAS with the invariant open and must decide *now* whether to perform the abstract transition, though the CAS may fail. An unconditional transition is unsound — a failed CAS must not change the abstract queue — while deferring is impossible, since a successful CAS has already linearized.

Prophecy variables resolve the dilemma by letting the proof *predict* the CAS outcome [5]. Before the loop iteration, the proof allocates a Boolean prophecy `p` predicting the outcome sequence. The proof then *case-splits* on the prediction:

- On predicted success, the proof performs the abstract dequeue transition at the CAS, discharging the atomic update token.
- On predicted failure, it performs no abstract transition and retries.

When the physical CAS returns its actual result, the prophecy resolves against it; a mispredicted branch yields a contradiction and closes vacuously, so only the branch matching reality survives — with the abstract transition placed exactly at the linearization point.

```coq
iMod (prophecy_var_alloc _ true) as (p & Hp & Hpred).
destruct (decide (Hpred = true)) as [->|Hne].
- (* predicted success: commit the abstract transition now *)
  iMod (ghost_dequeue γa with "Hauth") as "[Hauth Htok]".
  wp_cas_succeed ... (* resolve p against true; consistent *)
- (* predicted failure: no abstract change, retry *)
  wp_cas_fail ...    (* resolve p against false; consistent *)
```

The pattern scales beyond single CAS operations.

> **Lemma:** (Prophecy soundness) A prophecy variable's predicted value always equals the value it is resolved with in the actual execution. Hence any proof branch that assumes a misprediction is contradictory and can be closed without further obligations [5].

One subtlety: prophecies observe the future but do not influence it — the resolution value must already be fixed by the operational semantics.

### 4.3 Mechanized Proof Architecture in Coq

Mechanization in Coq is not transcription of paper proofs; the proof *assistant* shapes the architecture.

The MoSeL tactics mirror the logic: `iIntros` splits persistent from ephemeral hypotheses automatically; `iInv` opens an invariant and *enforces* that it is closed before the tactic block ends, turning a common paper-proof omission into a type error; `iMod` performs fancy updates — ghost transitions allocating invariants or prophecies — threading later modalities correctly.

The architecture of a typical development is layered:

1. **Language and logic.** HeapLang semantics and the Iris base logic are imported, never rebuilt [1].
2. **Ghost theory.** The resource algebra is a Coq `Camera` instance with lemmas for each frame-preserving update, proved once and reused at every linearization point.
3. **Invariant and coupling.** The invariant definition plus open-step-close lemmas — where most of the intellectual content lives.
4. **Operation proofs.** Each atomic triple: allocate prophecies, enter the retry loop, open the invariant, attempt the CAS, resolve prophecies, close or retry.
5. **Client reasoning.** Lemmas letting clients use the atomic triples without seeing the invariant — the adequacy boundary [6].

```
Language (HeapLang) → Base logic → Ghost cameras → Invariant →
Atomic triples → Client specs → Adequacy (linearizability)
```

Each arrow is a Coq-checked interface; changing the physical representation requires re-proving only the layers below the atomic triples, which is the practical payoff of logical atomicity [6].

### 4.4 Case Study: The Michael-Scott Queue and the Elimination-Backoff Stack

The Michael-Scott queue is the canonical benchmark for future-dependent linearization [12].

The elimination-backoff stack raises the stakes.

For helping structures such as Harris's linked list or RDCSS, the ghost protocol stores atomic update tokens of in-flight operations; a thread encountering another's descriptor may discharge that token, performing the *other* operation's linearization point.

At the largest scale, vMVCC shows these techniques composing to industrial size: timestamp-based linearization, speculative transaction futures as prophecies, and system-wide invariants constraining predictions until resolution [8].

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

What does mechanization cost? The figures below are indicative orders of magnitude — proof size depends on automation and camera-theory reuse — not measurements of a single artifact.

| Case study | Technique | Approx. Coq size | Key mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Michael-Scott queue | Logical atomicity + prophecy | Thousands of lines | Boolean CAS-outcome prophecy [5][12] |
| Herlihy-Wing queue | Logical atomicity + prophecy | Thousands of lines | Index prophecy over naturals [5] |
| RDCSS | Logical atomicity + helping | Thousands of lines | Helping-token ghost protocol [5] |
| vMVCC transaction library | Invariants + prophecy | Tens of thousands of lines | Timestamp prophecies, MVCC invariants [8] |
| Elimination stack (proof recipe) | OMO + relaxed memory | Thousands of lines | Object modification order [7] |
| Folly MPMC queue (proof recipe) | OMO + relaxed memory | Thousands of lines | Commit-with relation [7] |

Several patterns emerge. First, the *ghost theory* — cameras, authoritative constructions, prophecy libraries — is proved once and amortized across case studies [1]. Second, invariant-coupling lemmas dominate each development's line count: stating precisely that the physical structure matches the ghost protocol is harder than verifying any single CAS. Third, `Qed` time is dominated by MoSeL discharging side conditions; developments of this scale typically check in minutes on commodity hardware [9].

The proof recipe of Park et al. extends the methodology to relaxed memory, where linearization must also account for out-of-order executions and view transfers [7]. Their *object modification order* generalizes the C11 modification order to an object-local setting, minimizing reordering obligations; with it they verified the Michael-Scott queue, the elimination stack, and Folly's MPMC queue under relaxed memory in Iris for the first time — evidence that the invariant-plus-prophecy architecture is not tied to sequential consistency [7].

Formally, the end-to-end guarantee for each case study is the same adequacy statement:

> **Theorem:** (End-to-end correctness) If the Coq development proves the logically atomic specification of every operation of the module, then every execution of the module linked against any well-typed client is linearizable with respect to the sequential specification, by the free theorem of Frumin et al. [6]. The Coq kernel has checked every step; the trusted base is the HeapLang semantics, the Iris model, and Coq itself.

Probabilistic extensions go further: Coneris generalizes logical atomicity to *randomized* logical atomicity, using presampling tapes and a probabilistic update modality for error bounds of concurrent probabilistic programs, likewise mechanized in Iris [11].

## 6 Limitations

The approach has genuine limits, and they are worth stating precisely.

**Proof effort remains high.** Even with MoSeL, verifying a new fine-grained structure is a multi-person-month undertaking: the invariant must be exactly right — too weak and operations cannot be proved, too strong and it cannot be maintained — and finding it is a creative act the logic does not automate [1].

**Prophecies have a scope.** Prophecy variables predict values determined by the program's future, but they cannot predict *assertions* or *proof-relevant* information, and the prophecy mechanism must be baked into the operational semantics — it is not available in every Iris-derived logic without metatheoretical work. Algorithms whose linearization depends on genuinely infinitary future behavior, or on angelic nondeterminism the semantics does not resolve, fall outside the pattern [5].

**Liveness is out of scope.** Iris proves *safety*: linearizability, absence of data races, memory safety. It does not prove that an operation *terminates* — a lock-free algorithm's retry loop is verified only for the steps it takes, not for the claim that it eventually succeeds. Lock-freedom, wait-freedom, and starvation-freedom require separate termination arguments that Iris's step-indexed model does not directly support [1].

**The trusted base is large.** The guarantee bottoms out at the Coq kernel, the Iris model construction, and the HeapLang semantics. A bug in the camera library's metatheory or in the prophecy extension's soundness proof would invalidate everything above it. The community mitigates this with a small, heavily scrutinized model construction, but it cannot be eliminated [1][5].


---

## 7 Conclusion

Iris's answer is a composition of three ideas, each addressing a distinct difficulty. *Invariants* tame interference by making the thread agreement explicit and duplicable. *Higher-order ghost state* reifies the abstract protocol — sequences, tokens, helping obligations — as ownable resources governed by declared transition systems [2]. *Prophecy variables* dissolve the future-dependence of linearization points by letting proofs speculate about CAS outcomes, indices, and helping behavior, committing abstract transitions under predictions that are resolved exactly when the physical linearization step fires [5]. Mechanized in Coq through HeapLang and the MoSeL proof mode, these ideas scale from the Michael-Scott queue to multiversion concurrency control libraries, with linearizability obtained as a free theorem over logically atomic specifications [6][8][9].

The research frontier is clear: reducing the proof burden through stronger automation, extending prophecy-style reasoning to richer classes of future dependence, and carrying the methodology to relaxed memory and probabilistic concurrency, where early results like the proof recipe and Coneris show the architecture generalizes [7][11]. What will not change is the core discipline: state the abstract specification, design the ghost protocol, couple it to the physical representation with an invariant, and let the logic place each linearization point where the execution does.
