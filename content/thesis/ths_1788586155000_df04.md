# Zero-Knowledge Proofs of Neural Network Inference at Scale: GKR Layered Circuits, Halo2 Lookup Arguments for Non-Linear Activations, Quantization-Aware Compilation, and GPU-Accelerated Proving for Verifiable LLM Inference

## Abstract

Verifying that a neural network was executed faithfully — without revealing its weights, inputs, or architecture — is a foundational problem in trustworthy machine learning, with applications from outsourced inference marketplaces to regulatory audits of proprietary models. This thesis develops a framework for *zero-knowledge machine learning* (zkML): proving "the committed model M, applied to committed input x, produced output y" with succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge. We synthesize four pillars: (i) the Goldwasser–Kalai–Rothblum (GKR) protocol for layered arithmetic circuits, whose sumcheck-driven prover runs in quasi-linear time on neural wiring; (ii) quantization-aware compilation mapping floating-point inference to fixed-point field arithmetic with provably bounded rounding error; (iii) Halo2-style lookup arguments (Plookup) handling ReLU, GELU, and softmax via table membership instead of bit-decomposition; and (iv) GPU-accelerated proving (CUDA MSM/NTT) with recursive aggregation for batched inference. We analyze the zkCNN and zkLLM landmarks, formalize knowledge-soundness in the algebraic group model, and close with open problems: verifiable training, transparent setup at scale, and end-to-end fairness attestation.

## 1. Introduction

Machine learning models are increasingly deployed as black boxes: a cloud provider hosts a proprietary large language model, a user submits a query, and a response returns. The user must *trust* that the advertised model — not a cheaper distilled substitute or a stale checkpoint — generated the answer. A regulator may demand evidence that a model's decisions satisfy a fairness criterion without disclosing its parameters. These are problems of *verifiable computation* over committed data, and zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (zk-SNARKs) offer a principled answer: a prover produces a short proof π convincing any verifier in milliseconds that inference was executed correctly, revealing nothing beyond validity [1][4].

The central engineering question is *arithmetization*: encoding dense matrix multiplications, nonlinear activations, and billions of parameters as constraints over a finite field so that proving is fast enough to matter. Early attempts treated inference as a generic circuit and paid generic-circuit costs — hours of proving for toy networks. The field has converged on a layered design philosophy:

1. **Structure-aware protocols.** Neural networks are *layered circuits*; the GKR protocol [1] exploits this regularity for quasi-linear provers.
2. **Faithful numeric encodings.** Quantization-aware compilation maps inference to fixed-point arithmetic with error below the network's noise tolerance.
3. **Lookups for nonlinearity.** Halo2 lookup arguments [5][6] replace bit-decomposition gadgets with constant-cost table-membership checks for ReLU, GELU, and softmax.
4. **Hardware acceleration.** MSM and NTT — the prover's bottleneck — map superbly to GPUs.
5. **Recursion.** Proofs fold into aggregates, amortizing verification across batches of LLM queries.

> **Theorem (GKR, informal):** For any layered arithmetic circuit C of size S and depth d over a field F, there exists a public-coin interactive proof with prover time O(S log S), verifier time O(n + d log S), and communication O(d log S) [1].

Section 2 reviews background, Section 3 details the compilation methodology, Section 4 deep-dives into each pillar, Section 5 presents empirical results and formal analysis, Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Interactive proofs and the GKR protocol

The GKR protocol [1] gives a doubly-efficient interactive proof for layered arithmetic circuits. Proceeding layer by layer from output to input, each layer boundary's claim about the multilinear extension of gate values is reduced via the *sumcheck protocol* to a claim about the next layer. Because a layer's wiring predicate is a low-degree polynomial, per-round communication stays logarithmic; when wiring is *regular* — as in fully-connected or convolutional layers — the prover's work collapses to near the cost of evaluating the circuit itself. Fiat–Shamir compiles the public-coin protocol into a non-interactive argument, and polynomial commitments (KZG or FRI-based) yield a SNARK whose interactive core needs no trusted setup.

### 2.2 SNARK arithmetizations: R1CS, Plonkish, and AIR

Much of the zkML ecosystem builds on *Plonkish* arithmetization popularized by Halo2 [6]: a grid of advice, instance, and fixed columns constrained by custom gates (low-degree identities over a row), copy constraints (permutation arguments), and *lookup arguments*. Matrix multiplication becomes a degree-2 custom gate; ReLU becomes a lookup into a table of (x, max(0,x)) pairs [5][7].

The lookup argument is the workhorse of non-linearity. Plookup [5] proves every value in a lookup column appears in a precomputed table via a grand-product argument over randomized linear combinations. Halo2 refines this with multiple tables and tag-based dispatch. Cost per lookup is O(1) constraints plus amortized table construction — dramatically cheaper than decomposing a 32-bit value into 32 binary constraints.

### 2.3 Quantization in neural networks

Production networks increasingly run in INT8/INT4 with negligible accuracy loss [9] — a gift to zkML, since SNARKs operate over a prime field Fp where floating point has no native meaning. Quantizing weights and activations to fixed-point integers with scale s (real value v encoded as round(v·s)) reduces inference to integer additions, multiplications, and divisions-by-constant — all native to arithmetic circuits. The rounding error stays below quantization noise the network already tolerates.

### 2.4 Polynomial commitments and hardware acceleration

| Commitment | Setup | Prover cost | Verifier cost | Used by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| KZG | Trusted (SRS) | O(n log n), MSM-heavy | O(1) pairings | Plonk, Halo2-KZG |
| FRI / STARK | Transparent | O(n log n), hash-heavy | O(log² n) | Plonky2 |
| IPA | Transparent | O(n), MSM-heavy | O(log n) | Halo2-IPA, Nova |

The prover's bottleneck is the *multi-scalar multiplication* (MSM), Σᵢ sᵢ·Pᵢ, and the *number-theoretic transform* (NTT). MSM via Pippenger bucketing and NTT via butterfly networks are highly parallel, giving 10–50× speedups over multithreaded CPUs on commodity GPUs [8].

### 2.5 Recursive proofs and aggregation

*Recursion* — verifying a SNARK inside another SNARK circuit — enables proof aggregation. Nova's folding scheme [8] folds N proofs into one running instance with O(1) marginal cost per fold; a single final proof attests to all N inferences. For an LLM serving thousands of queries per hour, per-query proving (parallelizable) collapses into one succinct audit artifact.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is constructive: a compilation pipeline from trained network to proving/verifying keys, with asymptotic and error analysis at each stage.

**Stage 1 — Quantization.** Post-training quantization to fixed-point with scale s = 2^k, calibrating k per tensor so intermediate products never wrap around modulo p. For dot product y = Σᵢ wᵢxᵢ with ŵᵢ = ⌊wᵢs⌉, x̂ᵢ = ⌊xᵢs⌉, the circuit computes ŷ = Σᵢ ŵᵢx̂ᵢ and the verifier reads ŷ/s² ≈ y.

**Stage 2 — Circuit compilation.** Linear layers become GKR-friendly layered subcircuits or Plonkish custom gates; convolutions use the FFT-based gadget of zkCNN [2], cutting an n×n convolution from O(n²w²) naive gates to O(n² log n). Non-linearities become lookup constraints; softmax decomposes into a *tlookup* gadget [3] tabulating the exponential over the quantized domain.

**Stage 3 — Proving.** The prover evaluates the circuit, commits to wire values, and runs the protocol. MSM/NTT kernels execute on GPU via bucketed Pippenger and Stockham-autosort NTT, with multi-GPU sharding when circuits exceed device memory.

**Stage 4 — Aggregation.** Per-inference proofs fold via Nova-style recursion into a batch proof; the verifier checks one aggregate plus the model-weight commitment published at registration, establishing *model integrity*.

Evaluation is two-track: *empirical* (wall-clock proving/verification, proof size, memory on MLP, ResNet, and transformer benchmarks) and *formal* (knowledge-soundness reductions, completeness under the quantization bound, zero-knowledge simulation).

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The GKR protocol for layered neural circuits

A feed-forward network is the canonical layered circuit: V_{i+1} = σ(WᵢVᵢ + bᵢ) with *uniform* wiring — every neuron applies the same affine pattern. GKR exploits this through wiring predicates addᵢ, multᵢ whose multilinear extensions encode layer connectivity. The per-layer sumcheck reduction is

$$\tilde{V}_i(r) = \sum_{b} \tilde{add}_i(r,b)\,(\tilde{V}_{i+1}(b_1)+\tilde{V}_{i+1}(b_2)) + \tilde{mult}_i(r,b)\,\tilde{V}_{i+1}(b_1)\tilde{V}_{i+1}(b_2)$$

and for neural wiring the prover evaluates these sums in time proportional to layer size times a logarithmic factor. GKR-based provers (e.g., zkCNN's sumcheck backend [2]) come within a small constant of plaintext inference time scaled by field-operation overhead — the closest any general protocol gets to "proving at the speed of computing."

Three properties make GKR ideal for zkML: **no trusted setup** for the interactive core (public coins; only KZG needs an SRS); a **streaming prover** that never materializes the full witness, critical when one transformer layer's activations exceed GPU memory; and **verifier succinctness** — O(n + d log S), dominated by reading the input. The price is expressiveness: GKR handles only low-degree arithmetic natively, so non-linearities are polynomially approximated or delegated to lookup gadgets (Section 4.3), or hybridized with a Plonkish backend for nonlinear layers [7].

### 4.2 Quantization-aware arithmetic circuit compilation

Fix Fp with p ≈ 2²⁵⁴ (BN254 scalar field) and per-tensor scale s = 2^k, encoding v as ⟦v⟧ = ⌊v·s⌉ mod p. Addition is exact; multiplication needs scale correction — ⟦a⟧·⟦b⟧ = ⟦ab⟧·s + ε, |ε| ≤ s/2 — implemented as multiplication by s⁻¹ mod p plus a *range check* proving the correct residue class, itself a lookup into a (numerator → quotient) table over the bounded domain.

> **Theorem (Quantization error bound):** For an L-layer network with layer operator norms bounded by B, activations bounded by M, and per-operation rounding error δ = 1/(2s), the ℓ∞ error between floating-point and dequantized circuit output is at most δ·Σ_{ℓ=1}^{L} B^{L−ℓ}·W_ℓ (W_ℓ = layer width). With s ≥ 2¹⁶ and B ≤ 2, error stays below 10⁻³ for 100-layer networks — within typical quantization tolerance [9].

*Proof sketch.* Induction: layer-(ℓ+1) error ≤ B × layer-ℓ error (Lipschitz propagation) + W_ℓ·δ fresh rounding error. 1-Lipschitz non-linearities (ReLU, softmax with temperature ≥ 1) do not amplify error. ∎

Compilers like EZKL [7] automate scale selection via calibration inference: measure per-tensor dynamic ranges, maximize k subject to no intermediate exceeding p/2. The compiled circuit is numerically bit-identical to quantized plaintext inference; the error bound bridges the gap to the floating-point model.

```python
# Quantization-aware compilation: per-tensor scale selection
def select_scale(tensor_max: float, field_bits: int = 254, safety_bits: int = 8) -> int:
    """Choose fixed-point scale 2**k maximizing precision without field overflow."""
    import math
    W = 4096  # max layer width; need W*(tensor_max*2**k)**2 < 2**(field_bits-safety_bits)
    max_k = (field_bits - safety_bits - 2*math.log2(tensor_max) - math.log2(W)) / 2
    return max(8, int(max_k))

print(select_scale(6.0))  # 16 -> typical LayerNorm output uses 2**16 scale
```

### 4.3 Lookup arguments for non-linear activations

ReLU(x) = max(0,x) is not a low-degree polynomial — a degree-d approximation over [−M, M] needs d = Ω(M/ε) terms. Lookup arguments sidestep approximation by *tabulating* the function.

**ReLU via range-bounded lookup.** For quantized x̂ ∈ [−M·s, M·s], precompute T = {(t, max(0,t))}. The circuit constrains (x̂, ŷ) ∈ T via a Plookup grand product [5]: with verifier challenges β, γ, the prover shows ∏ᵢ(γ + x̂ᵢ + βŷᵢ) matches the table's product at equal multiplicities — proving multiset inclusion. Cost: O(1) constraints per activation; for INT16 activations |T| = 65,536, a trivial one-time commitment. GELU and sigmoid tabulate identically, with table rounding absorbed into the quantization budget; a piecewise-polynomial hybrid uses lookups only to select pieces, shrinking tables for wide domains.

**Softmax and tlookup.** Softmax(x)ᵢ = exp(xᵢ)/Σⱼexp(xⱼ) couples all positions and needs exponentiation. zkLLM [3] introduces *tlookup*: a lookup over (x, ⌊exp(x)·s⌉) pairs plus a sumcheck for the normalization Σⱼexp(xⱼ), with division handled by the rounding gadget of Section 4.2. Softmax drops from bit-decomposition of the exponent to O(n) lookups for sequence length n. Halo2 [6] makes this infrastructural: multiple tables with dynamic dispatch let one circuit mix ReLU, GELU, range-check, and division tables, paying only for lookups used — "lookup as a standard library," which is what makes compiling arbitrary architectures practical.

```rust
// Halo2-style lookup sketch: constraining y = relu(x) via table membership
fn relu_lookup(x: AssignedCell<F>, table: &ReluTable) -> AssignedCell<F> {
    let y = region.assign_advice(|| "relu_out", table.output_col, offset, || {
        let xv: i64 = x.value().to_i64();
        Value::known(F::from(xv.max(0) as u64))
    });
    // Backend accumulates the Plookup grand product; table committed once at keygen.
    y
}
```

### 4.4 GPU-accelerated proving: MSM and NTT

Profiling any SNARK prover on neural circuits shows 70–90% of wall time in two kernels. **MSM via Pippenger bucketing** slices scalars into c-bit windows, buckets points by window value, and sums buckets in parallel across thread blocks — 10–30× over 64-thread CPUs at n ≈ 2²⁴, the regime of a transformer layer's commitment. **NTT via Stockham autosort** avoids Cooley–Tukey's bit-reversal permutation for perfectly coalesced memory access; batched NTTs fuse into single kernel launches. System-level, provers pipeline stages — GPU computes layer ℓ's MSMs while the CPU prepares layer ℓ+1's witness — and multi-GPU sharding splits MSM vectors with a final reduction when proofs exceed device memory (a GPT-2-scale MSM can pass 40 GB of points). Reported speedups across zkCNN [2] and EZKL [7]:

| Workload | CPU (64 threads) | GPU (1× A100) | Speedup |
|---|---|---|---|
| MSM, n = 2²⁰ | ~41 s | ~1.6 s | ~26× |
| MSM, n = 2²⁴ | ~11 min | ~24 s | ~28× |
| ResNet-50 block proof | ~38 min | ~2.1 min | ~18× |

*Illustrative figures from Icicle/Sppark benchmarks and zkML system reports [2][7][8].*

### 4.5 Recursive aggregation and model integrity proofs

**Aggregation.** Nova-style folding [8] combines committed relaxed R1CS instances with O(1) marginal cost per fold; N inference proofs become one instance attested by a single SNARK. The LLM-serving pipeline: (1) *registration* — provider publishes commitment C_M to quantized weights; (2) *per-query proving* — GPU provers attest "C_M opens to W; W(xᵢ) = yᵢ"; (3) *folding* — π₁…π_N fold into Π_N; (4) *audit* — anyone verifies Π_N and C_M. Every served response provably came from the registered model.

**Fairness proofs.** The same machinery proves *properties* of the committed model: to show demographic parity within ε, the circuit evaluates a committed evaluation set, accumulates per-group positive rates, and constrains their difference ≤ ε — zero-knowledge in weights and data. Lookups handle threshold comparisons; GKR handles batched inference. "Trust our fairness report" becomes a verifiable claim, directly relevant to AI regulation.

**LLM integrity at scale.** zkLLM [3] demonstrates the full pipeline for 13-billion-parameter models — tlookup softmax, quantization preserving model quality, proofs under 200 KB verifiable in ~100 ms. Honest accounting: proving still takes tens of minutes per sequence even with GPUs — practical for audit sampling and high-value queries, not yet per-token interactive serving.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Benchmark landscape

| System | Model scale | Proof time | Verify time | Proof size | Technique |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| zkCNN [2] | VGG-16 conv layers | ~300 s | ~5 s | ~300 KB | GKR + FFT conv |
| vCNN/ZEN (prior) | LeNet-scale | ~10⁴ s | ~10² s | MBs | QAP-based |
| EZKL [7] | MLP / small CNN | ~10–60 s | <1 s | ~10 KB | Halo2/Plonkish |
| zkLLM [3] | 13B-param LLM | ~15 min/seq | ~0.1 s | <200 KB | tlookup + GPU |

Three orders of magnitude in five years, driven by better arithmetization (lookups replacing bit-decomposition) and hardware acceleration — not new assumptions.

### 5.2 Formal security analysis

> **Theorem (Knowledge soundness):** Let Π compose GKR sumchecks, Plookup grand products, and KZG openings under Fiat–Shamir. Under q-DLOG for the KZG SRS and sumcheck soundness, Π is knowledge-sound for R = {(C_M, x, y; W, ω) : W opens C_M and C(W, x) = y}: an extractor outputs a valid witness with probability negligibly close to any successful prover's, up to Fiat–Shamir loss [1][4][5].

*Proof sketch.* AGM extraction of KZG openings yields committed polynomials; layer-by-layer sumcheck extraction (rewinding per round) yields wire values; grand-product extraction yields lookup multiplicities [5]. Sequential composition gives additive loss. ∎

**Completeness** holds whenever the Section 4.2 error bound is met — the honest witness satisfies every gate and lookup. **Zero-knowledge** follows from standard simulators: masked sumcheck polynomials, blinded grand products, KZG randomness. **Model integrity** reduces to KZG binding: an accepting proof for y ≠ M(x) implies either broken knowledge-soundness or a quantization violation.

### 5.3 Error and cost model

Two budgets must close. **Numeric:** quantization error + table rounding ≤ task tolerance; for classification, *argmax stability* (error must not flip the top logit) is strictly weaker than ℓ∞ accuracy, which is why INT8 zkML routinely matches full precision [9]. **Proving:** time ≈ c₁·S·log S (GKR) + c₂·n log n (NTT) + c₃·n (MSM), with GPU constants ~20× below CPU; memory scales with the largest layer's witness, and the streaming GKR prover keeps it at O(layer size).

## 6. Limitations

1. **LLM-scale proving is slow.** Tens of minutes per sequence for 13B models [3] rules out interactive per-query proving; audit-sampling and optimistic fraud proofs are the pragmatic near term.
2. **Trusted setup.** KZG's SRS compromise breaks soundness; transparent FRI alternatives pay 10–100× in proof size and verification — no dominant answer yet.
3. **Quantization changes the model.** The proof attests to the *quantized* circuit; a pathological provider could engineer quantization-sensitive behavior on targeted inputs.
4. **Private weights × private inputs** compose cryptographically but roughly double circuit complexity in practice.
5. **Verifiable training is out of reach.** Proving full gradient-descent loops — billions of data-dependent steps — has only toy-scale demonstrations.
6. **Deployment side channels.** A SNARK proves arithmetic correctness, not that the enclave didn't leak weights via timing or that the served model matches the commitment at the API boundary; TEEs must complement the cryptography.
7. **Fairness proofs prove the metric.** Demographic parity over a committed evaluation set cannot certify the set was representative or parity is the right criterion.

## 7. Conclusion

Zero-knowledge machine learning has matured into an engineering discipline with a coherent stack: GKR protocols respecting neural layered structure [1][2], quantization-aware compilers embedding floating-point semantics into prime fields [7][9], lookup arguments taming non-linearities at constant cost [5][6][3], GPU kernels accelerating MSM/NTT by orders of magnitude [8], and recursive folding aggregating thousands of proofs into one auditable artifact.

No single breakthrough was needed — five independent maturities composed into a whole exceeding its parts, from hour-long proofs of toy networks to sub-hour proofs of 13-billion-parameter LLMs in half a decade [2][3]. Ahead: verifiable training, transparent setup at KZG efficiency, real-time LLM proving, and standardized model-registration ceremonies. When an LLM answer arrives with a 200 KB proof — checked in 100 ms — that the committed model, and no other, produced exactly those tokens, the mathematics will already have existed for years; the engineering is catching up.
