# Machine Unlearning: SISA Sharded Training, Certified Data Removal, Approximate Unlearning via Gradient Methods, and LLM Unlearning Benchmarks

## Abstract

Machine unlearning is the problem of efficiently removing the influence of specified training data from a trained model, motivated by regulatory rights such as the GDPR right to erasure and by practical needs to excise poisoned, private, or copyrighted data without incurring the cost of full retraining [1][7]. This thesis develops a unified treatment of exact and approximate unlearning: we formalize unlearning via distributional indistinguishability from a model retrained from scratch, analyze the SISA (sharded, isolated, sliced, aggregated) framework that confines retraining to affected shards and checkpoints [1], derive certified-removal guarantees for convex models via Newton-step influence corrections with (ε,δ) bounds [2], and extend gradient-based descent-to-delete methods to non-convex deep networks [3]. We survey approximate unlearning for large language models, including gradient-ascent forgetting with retain-set KL regularization, the TOFU fictitious-author benchmark with forget/retain quality metrics [5], and reinforced fine-tuning for Harry Potter knowledge removal [6]. We conclude with privacy risks, sequential-request degradation, and verification challenges.

## 1. Introduction

Modern machine learning models are trained on vast corpora that inevitably contain data later subject to deletion requests. The EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) codifies a *right to erasure* (Article 17), and the California Consumer Privacy Act establishes analogous deletion rights; both presume a data controller can remove personal data not only from storage but from every system whose behavior it influences — including trained model parameters [1][7]. Yet neural networks memorize: training points leave traces in weights, and deleting a database record does nothing to expunge its influence from a deployed model. The gold standard — *retraining from scratch* without the deleted points — restores exact compliance but is computationally prohibitive at large scale.

**Machine unlearning** studies algorithms for removing the influence of a *forget set* from a trained model at a fraction of retraining cost, while preserving utility on the remaining *retain set* [4]. The field bifurcates along a fundamental axis:

- **Exact unlearning** requires that the unlearned model's parameter distribution be *identical* to that of a model retrained from scratch without the deleted data. The canonical construction is SISA training — sharding, isolation, slicing, and aggregation — which localizes each point's influence so that deletion triggers only partial retraining [1].
- **Approximate (certified) unlearning** relaxes exactness to *statistical indistinguishability*: an adversary observing the unlearned model cannot tell whether the deleted point was ever present, formalized as (ε, δ)-certified removal in analogy with differential privacy [2]. This admits efficient one-shot corrections such as Newton-step updates for convex models [2] and gradient-based descent-to-delete procedures for non-convex networks [3].

The stakes have intensified with large language models (LLMs), where unlearning targets are *concepts*: copyrighted text, biographies, hazardous knowledge. Benchmarks such as TOFU (Task of Fictitious Unlearning) provide controlled fictitious-author corpora with quantitative forget-quality and model-utility metrics [5], while case studies like targeted removal of *Harry Potter* knowledge via reinforced fine-tuning demonstrate both the feasibility and the brittleness of concept erasure in LLMs [6].

This thesis contributes: (1) a unified framework for exact, certified, and empirical approximate unlearning; (2) analysis of SISA with expected unlearning speedup; (3) derivation of certified removal for convex ERM via Newton-step influence functions; (4) gradient-based approximate unlearning and its LLM extension; and (5) critical review of LLM benchmarks, privacy risks, and open problems.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Formal Setup

Let D = {z₁, …, zₙ} be a training dataset, and let A be a (possibly randomized) training algorithm mapping datasets to models. Denote by w = A(D) the trained model. A deletion request specifies a *forget set* D_f ⊆ D; the *retain set* is D_r = D \ D_f. An **unlearning algorithm** U takes (D, D_f, A(D)) — plus cached training artifacts — and outputs w⁻ = U(D, D_f, A(D)). The **retraining oracle** A(D_r) serves as ground truth: the ideal unlearned model is one trained as if D_f had never existed.

> **Theorem:** *(Exact unlearning [1]).* An unlearning algorithm U achieves **exact unlearning** if, for every dataset D and every forget set D_f, the distribution of U(D, D_f, A(D)) is identical to the distribution of A(D_r). Exactness is a property of the *joint distribution* over algorithmic randomness, not of any single realized model.

**Approximate unlearning** relaxes distributional identity to closeness:

> **Theorem:** *(Certified removal [2]).* An unlearning algorithm U achieves **(ε, δ)-certified removal** if, for every D and every z ∈ D, the distributions of U(D, {z}, A(D)) and A(D \ {z}) satisfy: for all measurable events E, Pr[U(…) ∈ E] ≤ e^ε · Pr[A(D \ {z}) ∈ E] + δ. For small ε, δ, no bounded adversary can distinguish the unlearned model from a retrained one.

This definition deliberately mirrors differential privacy — a DP training algorithm trivially yields certified removal, though at steep utility cost. The frontier is achieving small (ε, δ) *without* the full DP penalty by exploiting deletion-request structure [2][3].

### 2.2 Evaluation Methodology

Unlearning quality is assessed along three axes [4]:

1. **Efficacy (forgetting):** Does the model behave as if D_f were absent? Measured by *relearn time*, membership-inference-attack (MIA) success against D_f, and distributional distance to the retrained model.
2. **Fidelity (utility retention):** Is accuracy on D_r and held-out test data preserved? Approximate methods trade forgetting strength against utility.
3. **Efficiency:** Wall-clock unlearning time and storage overhead relative to full retraining.

For LLMs, efficacy decomposes into *forget quality* (indistinguishability of outputs on forget probes from a retain-only model) and *model utility* (fluency and factuality on retain probes) — the twin metrics of TOFU [5].

### 2.3 Related Work

The survey of Shaik et al. [4] organizes the literature into *exact* methods (retraining-based: SISA [1] and variants), *approximate* methods (influence-function corrections [2], gradient-based updates [3], Fisher-information scrubbing), and *empirical* LLM techniques (gradient ascent, preference-optimization forgetting, representation engineering). Complementary threads include federated unlearning, concept erasure in diffusion models, and unlearning as a *privacy attack surface* — paradoxically, the act of unlearning can leak information about the deleted point [4][7].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 SISA: Sharded, Isolated, Sliced, Aggregated Training

SISA [1] restructures training so each data point's influence is *contained*. D is partitioned into S disjoint **shards**; each shard is further split into R disjoint **slices**. A constituent model is trained per shard by incrementally adding slices, with a **checkpoint** saved after each slice. The S constituent models are combined at inference by **aggregation** (majority vote or averaging). Because shards train in isolation, a point in shard s, slice r affects only that shard's checkpoints from slice r onward, so deletion retrains only the affected shard from the preceding checkpoint before re-aggregating.

### 3.2 Certified Removal via Newton-Step Correction

For ℓ₂-regularized convex ERM, Guo et al. [2] derive a one-shot correction. Let w* = argmin_w Σ_{z∈D} ℓ(w; z) + (λn/2)‖w‖² with twice-differentiable, strongly convex ℓ. The influence of point z on w* is approximated by a single Newton step on the leave-one-out objective:

w⁻ = w* − H⁻¹ ∇ℓ(w*; z),  where H = ∇² Σ_{z∈D\{z}} ℓ(w*; z) + λnI.

The residual gradient norm ‖∇L(w⁻; D_r)‖ is bounded via Hessian Lipschitzness, and calibrated Gaussian noise converts the residual bound into an (ε, δ)-certified removal guarantee. The Hessian inverse is computed once per model, making each deletion O(d²).

### 3.3 Gradient-Based Approximate Unlearning

Neel, Roth, and Sharifi-Malvajerdi [3] generalize beyond convexity with **descent-to-delete**: initialize at the trained weights w and run projected gradient descent on the retain objective L(w; D_r), exploiting that w is already near-optimal for D_r. Under local strong convexity and smoothness, few steps provably remove the deleted point's influence within certified bounds. For deep networks the theory is aspirational but the practice effective: a few epochs of fine-tuning on D_r empirically matches retraining on MIA metrics [3][4].

### 3.4 LLM Unlearning: Gradient Ascent with Retain Regularization

For LLMs, retraining is infeasible, so the standard empirical recipe ascends the forget-set likelihood while anchoring to the original model on retain data:

```python
# Gradient-ascent unlearning with KL retain regularization
for step in range(num_steps):
    # 1. Forget: ascend the likelihood of forget-set tokens
    forget_loss = -model.log_likelihood(batch_f)   # negate to *unlearn*
    # 2. Retain: stay close to the original model on retain data
    retain_loss = kl_div(model(batch_r), ref_model(batch_r))
    loss = alpha * forget_loss + beta * retain_loss
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()  # ascent on forget, descent on retain-KL
```

Here `ref_model` is frozen; α, β trade forgetting strength against utility collapse — the central tension of LLM unlearning [5][6].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 SISA: Mechanics and Unlearning Speedup Analysis

With S shards and R slices per shard, deleting point z in shard s, slice r retrains shard s from the checkpoint after slice r−1 — an expected fraction of roughly 1/(2S) of the full dataset. Expected unlearning cost is thus smaller than full retraining by a factor approaching **2S**.

> **Theorem:** *(SISA exactness [1]).* Let A_SISA(D) denote SISA training with deterministic per-shard optimization given fixed slice orderings. For any D and any z ∈ D in shard s, slice r, retraining shard s from checkpoint r−1 on D_s \ {z} and re-aggregating yields w⁻ *distributionally identical* to A_SISA(D \ {z}). Hence SISA achieves exact unlearning.

Three tensions emerge: **(i)** more shards cheapen unlearning but each constituent model sees less data, degrading accuracy; **(ii)** R checkpoints per shard multiply storage; **(iii)** aggregation needs S forward passes at inference. Transfer-learning variants (a shared backbone fine-tuned per shard) recover much of the accuracy loss [1][4].

### 4.2 Certified Removal: From Influence Functions to (ε, δ) Guarantees

Certified removal [2] applies to regularized linear and logistic regression — strongly convex objectives. The analysis: (1) bound the *gradient residual* after the Newton correction, ‖∇L(w⁻; D_r)‖ ≤ γ, with γ depending on the Hessian Lipschitz constant, strong convexity, and the deleted point's gradient norm; (2) note small residual implies w⁻ is close to the true leave-one-out minimizer; (3) add Gaussian noise calibrated to γ/ε to obtain (ε, δ)-indistinguishability via the Gaussian mechanism.

> **Theorem:** *(Certified removal [2], simplified).* If ℓ(w; z) is convex with L-Lipschitz Hessian and the regularized ERM is μ-strongly convex, then the Newton-step update with noise N(0, σ²I), σ ∝ γ/ε, achieves (ε, δ)-certified removal for single-point deletion at O(d²) per-deletion cost after a one-time O(nd² + d³) Hessian computation.

The guarantee degrades under *multiple* deletions unless batched; sequential adaptive deletions compose with rapidly growing ε. And strong convexity excludes deep networks — the motivation for gradient-based methods [3].

### 4.3 Approximate Unlearning via Gradient Methods: Descent-to-Delete

Descent-to-delete [3] bridges the convex/non-convex gap. The insight: the trained model w is already an approximate minimizer of L(·; D_r), and deleting one point perturbs the minimizer by O(1/n) under smoothness — so *few* gradient steps on D_r suffice, and the trajectory itself can be noised (à la noisy SGD) for certified bounds under *local* strong convexity near w. Key refinements include:

- **Fisher scrubbing:** noise scaled by the inverse Fisher information, erasing directions most informative about D_f.
- **Gradient ascent on D_f:** maximizing forget-set loss before retain fine-tuning, accelerating forgetting at some utility risk.
- **KL anchoring:** penalizing divergence from the original model on D_r (cf. §3.4), stabilizing utility during aggressive forgetting.

### 4.4 LLM Unlearning Benchmarks: TOFU and the Harry Potter Case Study

**TOFU** [5] builds a controlled laboratory: 200 entirely *fictitious* author profiles are generated so no real-world knowledge confounds evaluation. A model memorizes them; unlearning targets a *forget set* of authors while a *retain set* must be preserved. Metrics: **forget quality** — a statistical indistinguishability test against a retain-only model — and **model utility** on retain and real-world probes. TOFU reveals a stark Pareto frontier: gradient ascent reaches high forget quality only at severe utility cost, while conservative methods preserve utility but fail indistinguishability [5].

**"Who's Harry Potter?"** [6] tackles *concept* unlearning at scale: fine-tuning on *alternative, generic labels* replaces universe-specific completions with plausible generic text, so probes about Hogwarts elicit harmless confabulations. Benchmarks show strong forgetting with modest general degradation — yet forgotten knowledge is often *re-elicited* via paraphrased prompts, multilingual queries, or few-shot priming, evidence that current LLM unlearning suppresses *retrieval* more than it erases *representation* [5][6].

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Analysis

Representative results across the seed literature illustrate the cost–exactness–utility trade-offs:

| Method | Guarantee | Setting | Cost vs. retrain | Utility retention | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SISA [1] | Exact | Any model | ~1/(2S) expected | Degrades as S grows | Storage ×R; inference ×S |
| Certified removal [2] | (ε, δ)-certified | Convex ERM | O(d²) per deletion | Near-lossless | Convexity only; batch deletions |
| Descent-to-delete [3] | Certified (local) / empirical | Smooth non-convex | Few GD steps on D_r | High when tuned | Local-convexity assumption |
| Gradient ascent + KL [5][6] | Empirical | LLMs | Minutes–hours | Pareto trade-off (TOFU) | Re-elicitation; paraphrase brittleness |
| Full retraining | Exact (oracle) | Any model | 1× baseline | Perfect | Prohibitive at scale |

Theoretically, any unlearning algorithm with non-trivial utility must process information proportional to the deleted point's *influence*, formalized via leave-one-out stability (§4.1–4.2).

> **Theorem:** *(Relearn-time characterization [4]).* If unlearned model w⁻ needs T_r(w⁻) gradient steps on D_f to recover the original forget-set loss, and the retrained model needs T_r(A(D_r)), then a small gap |T_r(w⁻) − T_r(A(D_r))| indicates strong empirical forgetting; large gaps reveal residual memorization.

Practically, MIA AUC near 0.5 on D_f plus relearn time matching the retrained model is the empirical gold standard for approximate unlearning [3][4].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Sequential and adaptive deletions.** Deployments face streams of requests, possibly adversarially adaptive. SISA handles sequences naturally but costs accumulate; certified-removal ε budgets compose poorly, and adaptive requests can break non-adaptive analyses [2][4].
2. **Utility degradation.** The TOFU Pareto frontier [5] shows that pushing forget quality to indistinguishability often collapses retain performance. Forgetting is *antagonistic* optimization; principled navigation of the trade-off remains heuristic.
3. **The privacy paradox.** The unlearning update is a function of the deleted point and can *leak* it: comparing pre- and post-unlearning models (a *differencing attack*) may reveal more about z than the original model did. Formalisms must bound leakage of the *update*, not just the final model [4][7].
4. **Verification hardness.** Exact methods admit constructive proofs (retraining receipts); approximate methods offer only statistical certificates that are expensive to verify, and proof-of-unlearning protocols are essentially nonexistent [4].
5. **The non-convexity gap.** Certificates stop at convex models [2]; deep-network unlearning rests on local analyses [3] or pure empirics [5][6]. Closing this gap — or proving it unclosable — is the central theoretical challenge.
6. **Erasure vs. suppression.** LLM methods suppress answer retrieval while leaving latent representations largely intact, as re-elicitation attacks show [6]. Whether *true* concept erasure is achievable without retraining remains open.
7. **Systems costs.** SISA checkpoint storage and multi-shard inference, certified removal's Hessian computation, and LLM fine-tuning runs impose burdens the literature under-reports [1][2][5].

## 7. Conclusion

Machine unlearning has matured from a regulatory curiosity into a technically deep discipline spanning exact retraining-avoidance (SISA [1]), certified statistical guarantees (Newton-step removal [2]), gradient-based approximate methods [3], and the empirically driven frontier of LLM concept erasure benchmarked by TOFU [5] and exemplified by the Harry Potter case study [6]. The unifying formalism — indistinguishability from retraining, exact or (ε, δ)-approximate — gives the field a crisp target, while the evaluation triad of efficacy, fidelity, and efficiency keeps methods honest [4].

Yet the gap between theory and practice remains wide: certificates cover convex models while deployments are deep; LLM unlearning suppresses rather than erases; sequential requests, privacy paradoxes, and verification hardness lack satisfactory solutions. The most promising path combines SISA-style isolation, certified corrections where convexity (locally) holds, and rigorously benchmarked empirical methods where it does not. Until unlearning is both *provable* and *practical* at scale, the right to erasure will remain easier to legislate than to implement.
