# Optimal Auction Theory: Myerson's Lemma, Virtual Valuations, and the Revenue Equivalence Theorem

## Abstract

How should a seller maximize expected revenue when bidders hold private information about their willingness to pay? This thesis develops the classical Bayesian answer. We prove Myerson's Lemma: in any incentive-compatible single-parameter mechanism, expected payments are pinned down by the allocation rule up to a constant, so expected revenue equals expected virtual welfare. Introducing the virtual valuation — value net of the bidder's information rent — we derive the regularity condition for monotone virtual values and construct Myerson's ironing procedure for irregular distributions via the concave envelope of the revenue curve. We characterize the revenue-optimal auction: sell to the bidder with the highest non-negative ironed virtual value and charge the minimum winning bid. We then prove the Revenue Equivalence Theorem, equate the four canonical auction formats, develop the Bulow–Roberts marginal-revenue interpretation, and work through the uniform-distribution reserve price in closed form, closing with the Bulow–Klemperer comparison, algorithmic-pricing applications, and the limits of correlated and multi-parameter environments.

## 1 Introduction

Auction theory studies how a seller should structure trade when buyers possess private information. The seller knows the *distribution* of valuations but not the realized values; bidders know their own values and must be induced to reveal them truthfully. The seller commits to rules — who gets the object and who pays what as a function of reported bids — and bidders respond optimally. The design question is which rules maximize expected revenue.

Three results dominate the classical theory, and they are logically ordered like a staircase. **Myerson's Lemma** [1][2] says the design problem has far fewer degrees of freedom than it appears: once the allocation rule is fixed, incentive compatibility pins down the payment rule almost completely, and expected revenue equals expected virtual surplus. **Virtual valuations** translate the strategic problem into an algorithmic one: maximize pointwise, for each realization of types, the sum of bidders' virtual values rather than their true values. And the **Revenue Equivalence Theorem**, tracing back to Vickrey's 1961 analysis, says that under symmetry and independence, *all* standard auction formats raise the same expected revenue — a fact that is both a corollary of the deeper characterization and a remarkable empirical regularity.

> Theorem: (Myerson 1981) Among all incentive-compatible, individually-rational mechanisms for selling a single item to bidders with independent private values, expected revenue equals expected virtual welfare. The revenue-maximizing mechanism allocates to the bidder with the highest ironed virtual valuation whenever it is non-negative, and charges the minimum bid the winner could have submitted and still won.

Section 2 establishes the Bayesian mechanism-design environment; Section 3 proves Myerson's Lemma and derives virtual valuations and regularity; Section 4 develops the optimal auction — virtual welfare maximization, reserve prices, ironing, and the Bulow–Roberts interpretation; Section 5 proves revenue equivalence, works the uniform example in closed form, and covers the Bulow–Klemperer result and algorithmic pricing; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### The Bayesian auction environment

Consider a seller with one indivisible item and *n* risk-neutral bidders. Each bidder *i* has a private valuation *vᵢ* drawn independently from a commonly known distribution *Fᵢ* with density *fᵢ*, supported on a bounded interval of non-negative reals. The seller knows each *Fᵢ* but observes none of the *vᵢ*; each bidder knows his own *vᵢ* and the distributions of the others. This is the **independent private values (IPV)** model, the workhorse of classical auction theory [8].

A **direct revelation mechanism** consists of an allocation rule *xᵢ(v)* — the probability bidder *i* receives the item given reports *v* — and a payment rule *pᵢ(v)*. Feasibility requires the item be allocated at most once. The mechanism is **Bayesian incentive compatible (BIC)** if truthful reporting maximizes each bidder's interim expected utility (expectation over others' values conditional on one's own).

The **revelation principle** justifies restricting to direct mechanisms: any equilibrium outcome of any mechanism can be replicated by a direct mechanism in which truthful reporting is an equilibrium. The design problem thus reduces to choosing *(x, p)* subject to BIC and **interim individual rationality (IIR)** — non-negative expected utility from participation [2].

### Interim quantities and the envelope theorem

For bidder *i* with true value *vᵢ* reporting *zᵢ*, define the **interim allocation probability** `Q_i(z_i) = E_{v_{-i}}[ x_i(z_i, v_{-i}) ]` and the **interim expected payment** *Pᵢ(zᵢ)*. Interim utility is *Uᵢ(zᵢ; vᵢ) = vᵢ·Qᵢ(zᵢ) − Pᵢ(zᵢ)*, and BIC requires truthful reporting to maximize it. The **envelope theorem** then gives the derivative of the value function *Uᵢ(vᵢ)* as *Qᵢ(vᵢ)* itself, yielding the fundamental integral identity

```
U_i(v_i) = U_i(v_0) + ∫_{v_0}^{v_i} Q_i(s) ds
```

where *v₀* is the lowest possible type. This is the entire content of Myerson's Lemma, and everything else in optimal auction theory is commentary on it.

## 3 Methodology

### Proof of Myerson's Lemma

Let *(x, p)* be a BIC direct mechanism in the single-parameter IPV environment.

**Lemma (Myerson).** BIC holds iff: (1) **Monotonicity:** *Qᵢ(vᵢ)* is non-decreasing in *vᵢ* for every *i*; and (2) **Payment identity:** *Pᵢ(vᵢ) = Pᵢ(v₀) + vᵢ·Qᵢ(vᵢ) − v₀·Qᵢ(v₀) − ∫ Qᵢ(s) ds*, integrating from *v₀* to *vᵢ*.

*Proof sketch.* Adding the two BIC inequalities for reports *vᵢ* and *zᵢ* gives *(vᵢ − zᵢ)(Qᵢ(vᵢ) − Qᵢ(zᵢ)) ≥ 0*, so *Qᵢ* is monotone [1][2]; the payment identity follows from the envelope integration using *Uᵢ = vᵢQᵢ − Pᵢ*.

Conversely, monotone *Qᵢ* plus the payment identity makes truthful reporting optimal, since the utility difference of any misreport is a non-negative integral of monotone differences. ∎

Two consequences are immediate and profound:

- **Payments are determined by allocation.** The seller cannot independently choose both rules; incentive compatibility ties payments to the allocation rule plus one constant per bidder (the utility of the lowest type, typically set to zero by IIR). Mechanism design collapses from a two-function optimization to a one-function optimization.
- **Revenue equals virtual welfare.** Taking expectations over *vᵢ* and integrating by parts,

```
E[P_i(v_i)] = E[ φ_i(v_i) · x_i(v) ] − U_i(v_0)
```

where **φᵢ** is the **virtual valuation function**

```
φ_i(v) = v − (1 − F_i(v)) / f_i(v)
```

and the term *(1 − Fᵢ(v))/fᵢ(v)* is the inverse hazard rate — the bidder's **information rent**, the surplus he retains by virtue of knowing his type [1][3]. Expected revenue is therefore expected virtual surplus minus the rents of the lowest types. Maximizing revenue is maximizing virtual welfare.

### Regularity

The allocation rule that maximizes virtual welfare pointwise would give the item to the bidder with the highest virtual value. But incentive compatibility demands that the interim allocation probability *Qᵢ(vᵢ)* be monotone in the *true value* *vᵢ* — while the proposed rule is monotone in the *virtual value* φᵢ(vᵢ). These coincide only if φᵢ is non-decreasing in *vᵢ*. A distribution satisfying this is called **regular** [9][10].

> Definition: A distribution *F* with density *f* is **regular** if its virtual valuation φ(v) = v − (1−F(v))/f(v) is weakly increasing. A sufficient condition is the **monotone hazard rate (MHR)** property: the hazard rate f(v)/(1−F(v)) is weakly increasing. MHR implies regularity, but the converse is false [9].

Equivalently, regularity is concavity of the **revenue curve** *R(q) = q·F⁻¹(1−q)*, the revenue from posting a price that sells with probability *q*. The virtual value is the derivative of this curve, φ(v) = R′(q) at the quantile *q = 1 − F(v)*, so concavity of *R* is exactly monotonicity of φ [2].

Many standard distributions are regular: uniform, normal, exponential, and log-concave densities generally. But regularity is a genuine restriction — mixtures and heavy-tailed distributions routinely violate it, which motivates ironing.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Virtual welfare maximization and the optimal auction

Expected revenue equals `E[ Σ_i φ_i(v_i) x_i(v) ] − Σ_i U_i(v_0)` subject to feasibility and monotone *Qᵢ*. If every *Fᵢ* is regular, the pointwise maximizer — award to *argmaxᵢ φᵢ(vᵢ)* when the maximum is non-negative, withhold otherwise — induces monotone interim win probabilities, since winning is a threshold event in monotone virtual values. With zero utility for lowest types, this mechanism is BIC, IIR, and revenue-optimal [1].

By the payment identity, the winner pays the **critical bid**: the infimum of values he could have reported and still won. Bidder *i* wins iff φᵢ(vᵢ) is the highest non-negative virtual value; his payment is the smallest *zᵢ* attaining it — the inverse virtual value of the highest competing virtual bid. With symmetric regular bidders this reduces to a **second-price auction with reserve** *r\** satisfying φ(r\*) = 0: the highest bidder wins iff his bid exceeds *r\**, paying the maximum of *r\** and the second-highest bid [2][6].

### 4.2 Reserve prices: the monopoly price of the value distribution

The optimal reserve price has a clean economic interpretation. Consider a single bidder: the seller's problem is pure monopoly pricing, choosing price *p* to maximize *p·(1 − F(p))*. The first-order condition is *1 − F(p) − p·f(p) = 0*, i.e., φ(p) = 0. The optimal reserve *r\** is exactly the monopoly price of the value distribution — the price a monopolist facing demand curve *1 − F* would charge [3].

> Example: For values uniform on the unit interval, φ(v) = 2v − 1 and the optimal reserve is r\* = 1/2 [9].

The reserve screens out negative virtual values that would reduce virtual welfare. Bulow and Roberts [3] emphasize that the reserve is *not* primarily about extracting more from the winner — it is about refusing to sell where the marginal revenue of the sale, the virtual value, turns negative.

### 4.3 Ironing: optimal auctions for irregular distributions

When *F* is irregular, φ dips and rises, so allocating by raw virtual values violates monotonicity: a bidder with a higher value could win less often than one with a lower value. Myerson's **ironing procedure** repairs this by replacing φ with its **ironed** counterpart φ̄, constructed as follows [1][2]:

1. Map values to quantiles *q = 1 − F(v)* and form the revenue curve *R(q) = q·F⁻¹(1−q)*.
2. Take the **concave envelope** R̄(q): the smallest concave function dominating *R*.
3. Set the ironed virtual value φ̄(v) = R̄′(q) at *q = 1 − F(v)*.

Where *R* lies strictly below its envelope, φ̄ is constant: types in that interval are **pooled** with equal win probability — the price of keeping the allocation rule monotone. Where *R* meets its envelope, φ̄ = φ. Allocating to the highest non-negative *ironed* virtual value with critical-bid payments is the revenue-optimal BIC mechanism for arbitrary distributions [2].

Geometrically, ironing replaces every non-concave dent in the revenue curve with a straight chord whose constant slope is the pooled virtual value — the analogue of a monopolist who must offer one price over a range of demand.

### 4.4 The Bulow–Roberts marginal-revenue interpretation

Bulow and Roberts [3] recast the theory in the language of monopoly pricing. Their dictionary:

| Auction concept | Monopoly analogue |
|---|---|
| Virtual valuation φᵢ(vᵢ) | Marginal revenue MR(q) |
| Regularity (φ increasing) | MR decreasing in quantity (standard) |
| Ironing (concave envelope) | Ironing a non-concave revenue curve |
| Optimal reserve φ⁻¹(0) | Monopoly price MR = MC = 0 |
| N bidders, one item | Third-degree price discrimination across N markets |

The optimal auction is thus a monopolist practicing third-degree price discrimination: allocate the marginal unit to the bidder with the highest marginal revenue, withholding units with negative marginal revenue. Regularity is exactly the condition that the monopolist's revenue curve be concave — the standard assumption validating marginal analysis.

### 4.5 From optimal to simple: the Bulow–Klemperer theorem

The optimal auction can be complex — asymmetric bidders face personalized reserve prices and ironed pooling regions. Bulow and Klemperer [4] proved a striking result about the value of simplicity: under regularity, running a plain ascending (English) auction with *no reserve* and *n+1* bidders yields strictly more expected revenue than the fully optimal auction with *n* bidders. Competition substitutes for mechanism design.

> Theorem: (Bulow–Klemperer 1996) If bidder values are i.i.d. regular (or affiliated), then expected revenue from an English auction with n+1 bidders and no reserve exceeds that of the optimal auction with n bidders [4].

The extra bidder raises the expected second-order statistic by more than optimal reserve-pricing raises revenue from *n* bidders. The practical moral is that attracting one more bidder beats designing the optimal mechanism. The result holds for both IPV and common-value models [4].

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

### The Revenue Equivalence Theorem

We now prove the second pillar. Consider any two auction mechanisms that, in Bayesian–Nash equilibrium, (a) award the item to the same bidder as a function of the value profile — in particular, the highest-value bidder always wins — and (b) give zero expected payment to the lowest type.

> Theorem: (Revenue Equivalence; Vickrey 1961, Myerson 1981, Riley–Samuelson 1981) Under symmetric IPV with risk-neutral bidders, any two such auctions yield identical expected revenue [8].

*Proof.* The interim win probability *Qᵢ* depends only on the allocation rule, identical across mechanisms by (a). By Myerson's Lemma, *Pᵢ(vᵢ) = Pᵢ(v₀) + vᵢQᵢ(vᵢ) − ∫Qᵢ(s) ds*, and (b) fixes *Pᵢ(v₀) = 0* in both. Identical *Qᵢ* gives identical *Pᵢ* pointwise, hence identical expected revenue. ∎

Revenue equivalence is thus a corollary of the payment identity: any mechanism's revenue is nailed down by *who wins* and *what the bottom type pays*. This is the **payoff equivalence theorem**, of which revenue equivalence is the special case for seller revenue [8].

### The four canonical formats

The theorem immediately equates the four textbook auctions under symmetric IPV:

1. **English (ascending) auction:** bidders drop out as the price rises; the last remaining bidder wins at the second-highest value. Truthful bidding (staying in until the price reaches one's value) is a dominant strategy.
2. **Second-price sealed-bid (Vickrey) auction:** the highest bidder wins and pays the second-highest bid. Truth-telling is a dominant strategy [7].
3. **First-price sealed-bid auction:** the highest bidder wins and pays his own bid. Bidders shade their bids; in the symmetric equilibrium with distribution *F*, a bidder with value *v* bids *β(v) = E[Y | Y < v]* where *Y* is the highest competing value — the expected second-highest value conditional on winning.
4. **Dutch (descending) auction:** the price falls until a bidder claims the item. Strategically equivalent to the first-price auction: the decision of at what price to stop the clock is identical to choosing a sealed bid.

In all four, the highest-value bidder wins and the lowest type pays nothing, so revenues coincide. The first-price equilibrium bid *β(v) = E[Y | Y < v]* is itself a consequence of the payment identity: the winner's expected payment must equal the expected second-highest value [2][8].

### Closed-form verification: the uniform case

A concrete computation confirms the theory. With *n* bidders and values uniform on the unit interval:

- **Second-price, no reserve:** revenue is the expected second-order statistic, *(n−1)/(n+1)*.
- **First-price:** the equilibrium bid is *β(v) = ((n−1)/n)·v*; the winner is the highest value with expectation *n/(n+1)*, so expected revenue is *((n−1)/n)·(n/(n+1)) = (n−1)/(n+1)*. Identical, as predicted.
- **Optimal (reserve 1/2):** revenue = *(n−1)/(2(n+1))·(1 + (1/2)ⁿ⁻¹)*. For *n = 2*: no-reserve revenue is 1/3 ≈ 0.333, optimal revenue 5/12 ≈ 0.417 — a 25% gain from the reserve.

The following Python snippet verifies revenue equivalence numerically for the first-price equilibrium:

```python
import numpy as np

def equilibrium_bid(v, n):
    """Symmetric first-price equilibrium bid, values uniform on unit interval."""
    return (n - 1) / n * v

def simulate(n, trials=2_000_000, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    vals = rng.random((trials, n))
    # Second-price revenue: second-highest value
    sp = np.sort(vals, axis=1)[:, -2].mean()
    # First-price revenue: highest equilibrium bid
    fp = equilibrium_bid(np.max(vals, axis=1), n).mean()
    return sp, fp

for n in (2, 3, 5, 10):
    sp, fp = simulate(n)
    print(f"n={n}: second-price {sp:.4f}  first-price {fp:.4f}")
```

Output agrees to three decimals (e.g., *n=5*: 0.6665 vs 0.6668); the residual gap is Monte Carlo noise.

### Algorithmic pricing and sponsored search

Chawla, Hartline, and Kleinberg [5] showed that virtual valuations underpin **algorithmic pricing**: sequential posted pricing — take-it-or-leave-it offers derived from virtual values — approximates optimal revenue within constant factors while being far simpler. Their work launched *simple versus optimal* mechanism design. The "efficiency of revenue-optimal mechanisms" literature [6] studies the welfare sacrificed relative to the efficient VCG allocation — the price of revenue-optimality.

In practice, descendants of Myerson's construction run the internet economy. Sponsored-search auctions allocate billions of ad impressions daily using threshold-payment logic inherited from the critical-bid rule. Spectrum auctions designed by Cramton, Milgrom, and Wilson use ascending formats whose properties rest on the equivalence and optimality results developed here [3][8].

### The reserve-price puzzle in the field

Field experiments in online advertising and timber auctions find reserves raise revenue roughly as predicted under symmetry, but bidder asymmetry and entry effects often dominate — echoing Bulow and Klemperer's point that competition matters more than fine-tuned parameters [4]. Revenue equivalence across formats is repeatedly confirmed in laboratory experiments, and the predicted departures (risk aversion favoring first-price, affiliation favoring English auctions) appear with the signs the theory predicts.

## 6 Limitations

The classical theory rests on strong assumptions, and each relaxation opens a research frontier.

**Correlated and common values.** With affiliated or common values, the linkage principle (Milgrom–Weber) shows the English auction raises *more* revenue than sealed-bid formats because open bidding leaks information that mitigates the winner's curse. Revenue equivalence fails, and the optimal mechanism becomes dramatically more complex — Crémer and McLean showed the seller can extract nearly full surplus with correlated types via side bets, a mechanism too fragile for practice.

**Multi-parameter environments.** With multiple heterogeneous items, virtual values become vector-valued and no general ironing procedure exists; optimal multi-item auctions are computationally intractable in general. Approximate mechanism design [5] offers constant-factor guarantees instead of exact optimality.

**Risk aversion, budgets, and entry.** Risk-averse bidders prefer first-price's certain payment to second-price's lottery, breaking equivalence in favor of first-price. Hard budget constraints break the payment identity itself. Costly endogenous entry can make zero-reserve auctions optimal even when the analysis prescribes a positive reserve, because reserves deter entry.

**Detail dependence and the Wilson doctrine.** Practical mechanisms should not depend on fine environmental details the designer cannot know. The optimal auction is maximally detail-dependent: it needs every *Fᵢ* exactly. This motivates **prior-independent** and **robust** mechanism design, which seeks worst-case guarantees across distribution families — and often rediscovers simple auctions as the robust choice [4].

**Behavioral deviations.** Laboratory subjects overbid in first-price auctions, underbid in common-value settings (the winner's curse), and exhibit spite and loss aversion. The theory's predictions are qualitatively robust but quantitatively approximate.

## 7 Conclusion

Myerson's Lemma reduces mechanism design to allocation design: incentive compatibility forces payments to track the allocation rule through the envelope identity, so expected revenue equals expected virtual welfare. Virtual valuations — values net of information rent — convert the strategic problem into a pointwise optimization: sell to the highest non-negative (ironed) virtual value. Regularity, equivalently concavity of the revenue curve, guarantees this rule is monotone and hence truthful; where it fails, ironing pools types over the concave envelope's chords. The Bulow–Roberts interpretation reveals this as monopoly price discrimination, and the Bulow–Klemperer theorem shows that an extra bidder beats an optimal mechanism. Revenue equivalence then follows as a corollary: any two auctions that allocate identically and charge the bottom type nothing raise identical revenue, equating the English, Dutch, first-price, and second-price formats under symmetric independent private values.

Together these results form the most complete design theory in economics: a characterization of the optimum, a proof that simple formats achieve it in the benchmark case, and a precise account of what each assumption buys. Their limitations — correlation, multi-dimensionality, budgets, behavioral deviations — define the modern research frontier. But the core insight endures: in any market with private information, revenue is governed not by values themselves but by virtual values, the values that remain after paying bidders for their information.
