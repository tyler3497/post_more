# The abc Conjecture, Szpiro's Conjecture, and Vojta's Dictionary: One Arithmetic Principle in Three Guises

## Abstract

The *abc* conjecture of Masser and Oesterlé (1985) asserts that for coprime positive integers satisfying a + b = c, the radical of abc — the product of its distinct prime divisors — cannot be much smaller than c: for every epsilon greater than zero, all but finitely many such triples satisfy c <= C(epsilon) rad(abc)^{1+epsilon} [1][9]. Born from attempts to understand Szpiro's discriminant-conductor inequality for elliptic curves, the conjecture is equivalent to a modified form of Szpiro's conjecture and would imply Roth's theorem, Faltings' theorem, and an asymptotic Fermat's Last Theorem [2][4]. Vojta's dictionary with Nevanlinna theory recasts *abc* as a Second Main Theorem with truncated counting functions on the projective line [5][7]. This article develops the radical and the quality invariant, proves the *abc*-Szpiro equivalence via Frey-Hellegouarch curves, surveys computational evidence and unconditional partial results, and gives a neutral account of Mochizuki's claimed inter-universal Teichmüller proof, which the mathematical community has not accepted [8].

## 1 Introduction

Few statements in number theory combine such elementary packaging with such sweeping consequences. The *abc* conjecture, formulated independently by David Masser and Joseph Oesterlé in 1985 in the course of their attempts to understand a conjecture of Lucien Szpiro about elliptic curves, concerns nothing more exotic than three positive integers with a + b = c [1][4]. Its claim is that addition and multiplication cannot conspire too efficiently: when a and b are divisible by high powers of small primes, their sum c tends to be divisible only by small powers of large primes, so the "squarefree kernel" of the triple stays large relative to c. Dorian Goldfeld called it "the most important unsolved problem in Diophantine analysis," a judgment echoed across the subsequent literature [4].

What makes *abc* extraordinary is its position at the intersection of three deep programs. Through the Frey-Hellegouarch construction, it is interchangeable with a modified form of Szpiro's conjecture bounding the discriminant of an elliptic curve by its conductor [2]. Through Paul Vojta's dictionary, it is the exact arithmetic analogue of Nevanlinna's Second Main Theorem with truncated counting functions — the one-dimensional case of a conjectural theory encompassing Roth's theorem, Schmidt's subspace theorem, and Faltings' theorem [5][6][7]. And it has been the target of one of the most dramatic episodes in modern mathematics: Shinichi Mochizuki's 2012 claimed proof via inter-universal Teichmüller theory, which the broader community has not accepted [8].

Section 2 fixes notation and the precise statements; Section 3 describes the methods — heights, Frey curves, the Nevanlinna analogy, and computation. Section 4 develops the Szpiro equivalence, the quality invariant, Vojta's dictionary, and the status of the claimed proof. Section 5 presents computational evidence and unconditional partial results, Section 6 the limitations, and Section 7 a conclusion.

## 2 Background

> **Definition:** For a nonzero integer n, the *radical* (or squarefree kernel) is rad(n) = product of p over p dividing n, the product taken over distinct prime divisors. Thus rad(72) = rad(2^3 . 3^2) = 6.

An *abc triple* is a triple (a, b, c) of positive integers with a + b = c and gcd(a, b, c) = 1. Coprimality is essential: without it, infinite families such as 2^n + 2^n = 2^{n+1} would trivially violate any bound, since rad(2^n . 2^n . 2^{n+1}) = 2 while c grows without bound.

> **Conjecture (*abc*, Masser-Oesterlé):** For every epsilon > 0 there exists a constant C(epsilon) such that every coprime triple a + b = c satisfies c <= C(epsilon) rad(abc)^{1+epsilon} [1].

Equivalently, for each fixed epsilon there are only finitely many triples violating the inequality. The conjecture admits several useful reformulations, summarized below.

| Formulation | Statement |
|---|---|
| Strong (standard) | For every epsilon > 0, finitely many coprime a + b = c with c > C(epsilon) rad(abc)^{1+epsilon} [1] |
| Weak | There exists lambda_1 > 0 such that for any lambda < lambda_1, finitely many primitive solutions with rad(abc) <= max(a,b,c)^lambda [9] |
| Quality form | The quality q(a,b,c) = log c / log rad(abc) satisfies liminf q = 1 as c -> infinity over coprime triples [9] |
| Epsilon-free | The exponent lambda_1 in the weak form equals 1 [9] |

The *quality* q(a, b, c) = log c / log rad(abc) measures how close a triple comes to violating the conjecture: q > 1 means c > rad(abc), and the conjecture is exactly the assertion that q is bounded above by 1 + epsilon eventually. Lagarias and Soundararajan formalized this via the *abc-exponent* lambda_1, the liminf of log rad(abc) / log max(a,b,c); the strong conjecture is lambda_1 = 1 [9].

Two remarks put the conjecture in perspective. First, it has a *proven* polynomial analogue. Mason's theorem (1983), rediscovered by Stothers, states that for coprime polynomials a, b, c over a field of characteristic zero with a + b = c, the maximum degree is bounded by deg rad(abc) - 1, where rad now means the product of distinct monic irreducible factors [1]. The passage from polynomials to integers — from a proven theorem to perhaps the deepest open problem in Diophantine analysis — is the whole difficulty.

> **Theorem (Mason-Stothers):** Let a, b, c be coprime nonzero polynomials over a field of characteristic zero with a + b = c and not all derivatives zero. Then max(deg a, deg b, deg c) <= deg rad(abc) - 1.

Second, the conjecture's consequences are enormous. It implies an *asymptotic* Fermat's Last Theorem: for a putative solution x^n + y^n = z^n with coprime x, y, z, one has rad(x^n y^n z^n) = rad(xyz) <= xyz < z^3, so that z^n <= C(epsilon) z^{3(1+epsilon)}, impossible for large n and large z [4]. It likewise implies Roth's theorem, Faltings' theorem, Tijdeman's theorem, the Fermat-Catalan conjecture, and strong forms of results on linear forms in logarithms [4][7].

## 3 Methodology

Four interlocking methods dominate the study of *abc*.

**Heights.** The modern language is that of Weil heights, measuring the arithmetic complexity of a rational point. The inequality c <= C(epsilon) rad(abc)^{1+epsilon} is a height inequality on the projective line with the divisor (0) + (1) + (infinity) removed, and the radical is essentially the exponential of the truncated counting function. This viewpoint is what makes Vojta's dictionary possible [7].

**Frey-Hellegouarch curves.** To a coprime triple a + b = c one attaches E: y^2 = x(x - a)(x + b). Its minimal discriminant is Delta = 16 a^2 b^2 c^2 and its conductor N_E divides a small multiple of rad(abc) [2]. This *Frey curve* translates the multiplicative data of the triple into the arithmetic geometry of an elliptic curve — the bridge along which *abc* and Szpiro's conjecture exchange information.

**The Nevanlinna analogy.** Osgood observed in 1981 that Roth's theorem is formally identical to Nevanlinna's Second Main Theorem; Vojta's 1987 thesis built this into an explicit dictionary between the two fields [7]. In this dictionary, *abc* is the one-dimensional, truncated case of a general conjectural Second Main Theorem for varieties — which is why progress on Vojta's conjectures and on *abc* illuminate each other [5][6].

**Computation.** Exhaustive search for high-quality triples tests the conjecture's plausibility. Nitaj's 1993 algorithms find triples with large quality lambda = log c / log rad(abc) and large Szpiro ratio sigma = log |Delta| / log N via approximation methods for A x^n - B y^n = C z, and distributed projects have enumerated triples far beyond naive reach [3]. Section 5 reports such computations.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Szpiro's discriminant-conductor inequality and the equivalence

Lucien Szpiro conjectured in 1981 that the arithmetic complexity of an elliptic curve over the rationals, measured by its minimal discriminant, is controlled by the set of primes of bad reduction, measured by the conductor [2]:

> **Conjecture (Szpiro):** For every epsilon > 0 there exists C(epsilon) such that every elliptic curve E over Q with minimal discriminant Delta and conductor f satisfies |Delta| <= C(epsilon) f^{6+epsilon}.

The *modified* Szpiro conjecture replaces the discriminant by the natural modular invariants: max(|c_4|^3, |c_6|^2) <= C(epsilon) f^{6+epsilon}, where c_4, c_6 are the invariants from Tate's algorithm [2]. It is the modified form that is equivalent to *abc* — a theorem of Oesterlé.

The bridge is the Frey-Hellegouarch curve. Given a coprime triple a + b = c, form E: y^2 = x(x - a)(x + b). A short computation gives c_4 = 16(a^2 + ab + b^2) and Delta = 16 a^2 b^2 c^2, while E is semistable away from 2, so its conductor f divides a small multiple of rad(abc). Since a^2 + ab + b^2 >= c^2 / 3, we have |c_4|^3 >= 16^3 c^6 / 27, and modified Szpiro applied to E gives c^6 bounded by a constant multiple of rad(abc)^{6+epsilon} — precisely *abc* with epsilon/6. Conversely, from a semistable elliptic curve the identity c_4^3 - c_6^2 = 1728 Delta furnishes, after dividing by the gcd, a coprime triple to which *abc* applies; since the triple's radical is controlled by the conductor, one recovers |Delta| <= C(epsilon) f^{6+epsilon} [2][4]. The exponent 6 is best possible, as Masser showed.

Thus *abc* and Szpiro's conjecture are two formulations of a single principle: *the discriminant-like quantity is bounded by the conductor-like quantity to the power 1 + epsilon*, the exponent 6 reflecting the weights of the modular forms involved. This equivalence is why the conjecture "originated as the outcome of attempts by Oesterlé and Masser to understand Szpiro's conjecture about elliptic curves" [4].

### 4.2 Quality, the c-radical, and the computational frontier

The quality q(a, b, c) = log c / log rad(abc) turns the conjecture into a question about a single real invariant. Triples with q > 1 are called *abc hits*; the conjecture asserts that for every epsilon > 0, only finitely many hits have q > 1 + epsilon. The known record, due to Reyssat, is 2 + 109 . 3^10 = 23^5, with q approximately 1.6299 [3]. Its radical is 2 . 3 . 109 . 23 = 15042, while c = 6436343 — the radical is strikingly small because a, b, c are each divisible by high powers of tiny primes.

Nitaj's algorithms systematically produce such triples by solving A x^n - B y^n = C z with small |z|, generating both high-quality *abc* triples and semistable elliptic curves with large Szpiro ratio sigma = log |Delta| / log N [3]. The table below shows the highest-quality triples with c at most 3000 found by direct search, illustrating how rare large quality is:

| Triple a + b = c | rad(abc) | Quality q |
|---|---|---|
| 1 + 2400 = 2401 = 7^4 | 210 | 1.4557 |
| 3 + 125 = 128 = 2^7 | 30 | 1.4266 |
| 625 + 2048 = 2673 = 3^5 . 11 | 330 | 1.3607 |

No triple below 3000 exceeds q = 1.46, and the known global record stands near 1.63 — consistent with the conjecture's prediction that quality cannot stay large, though no finite computation can confirm an asymptotic claim. Distributed efforts in the style of the ABC@home project, using algorithms descended from Nitaj's, have enumerated hits far beyond naive search range [3].

### 4.3 Vojta's dictionary: Nevanlinna theory meets Diophantine approximation

The deepest context for *abc* is Vojta's dictionary between Nevanlinna's value distribution theory in complex analysis and Diophantine approximation in number theory [7]:

| Nevanlinna theory (complex analysis) | Diophantine approximation (number theory) |
|---|---|
| Meromorphic function f: C -> P^1 | Rational point x on P^1(k) |
| Nevanlinna characteristic T(r, f) | Weil height h(x) |
| Counting function N(r, a) | Truncated counting of prime divisors |
| Proximity function m(r, a) | Local heights at finitely many places |
| First Main Theorem | Artin-Whaples product formula |
| Second Main Theorem | Roth's theorem |
| Second Main Theorem *with truncation* | The *abc* conjecture |

The dictionary was first glimpsed by Osgood in 1981 and developed systematically in Vojta's 1987 thesis, which formulated far-reaching conjectures unifying Roth's theorem, Schmidt's subspace theorem, and Faltings' theorem as the unramified special cases of a single Second Main Theorem for varieties [7].

Vojta's general conjecture bounds a sum of local heights plus the canonical height by epsilon times an ample height; its special cases include Mordell's conjecture as proved by Faltings and Schmidt's subspace theorem, and it implies both Lang's conjectures on rational points and the Masser-Oesterlé *abc* conjecture [6].

### 4.4 Inter-universal Teichmüller theory and the status of the claimed proof

In August 2012, Shinichi Mochizuki of Kyoto University posted four preprints developing inter-universal Teichmüller (IUT) theory — "an arithmetic version of Teichmüller theory for number fields equipped with an elliptic curve" — and claiming as a consequence a proof of the *abc* conjecture [8].

The series was accepted for publication in *Publications of the Research Institute for Mathematical Sciences* in 2021 — a journal of Mochizuki's own institute, of which he was editor-in-chief — but this did not shift expert opinion [8].

The neutral, factual summary: a proof has been *claimed*, published, and defended by its author and a small circle of supporters; the broader mathematical community has *not* accepted it, and *abc* remains an open conjecture [8].

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Partial Proofs

### 5.1 Computing quality

The following script reproduces the quality computations of Section 4.2: it sieves radicals up to a bound, scans coprime triples, and evaluates the record triple. For coprime a, b with a + b = c, the three numbers are pairwise coprime, so rad(abc) = rad(a) rad(b) rad(c).

```python
import math

def spf_sieve(limit):
    spf = list(range(limit + 1))
    for p in range(2, int(limit ** 0.5) + 1):
        if spf[p] == p:
            for m in range(p * p, limit + 1, p):
                if spf[m] == m:
                    spf[m] = p
    return spf

def rad_of(n, spf):
    r, prev = 1, 0
    while n > 1:
        p = spf[n]
        if p != prev:
            r *= p
            prev = p
        while n % p == 0:
            n //= p
    return r

N = 3000
spf = spf_sieve(N)
R = [0] + [rad_of(i, spf) for i in range(1, N + 1)]

best = []
for c in range(3, N + 1):
    for a in range(1, c // 2 + 1):
        b = c - a
        if math.gcd(a, b) != 1:
            continue
        q = math.log(c) / math.log(R[a] * R[b] * R[c])
        if q > 1.35:
            best.append((q, a, b, c))
best.sort(reverse=True)
for q, a, b, c in best:
    print(f"q = {q:.4f}   {a} + {b} = {c}")

# The Reyssat record triple: 2 + 109 * 3^10 = 23^5
a, b, c = 2, 109 * 3 ** 10, 23 ** 5
print("record q =", math.log(c) / math.log(2 * 3 * 109 * 23))
```

Output:

```
q = 1.4557   1 + 2400 = 2401
q = 1.4266   3 + 125 = 128
q = 1.3607   625 + 2048 = 2673
record q = 1.6299116841270482
```

The same computation in Haskell, emphasizing the radical as a pure function on prime factorizations:

```haskell
-- | Distinct prime divisors via trial division.
distinctPrimes :: Integer -> [Integer]
distinctPrimes n = go n 2
  where
    go 1 _ = []
    go m p
      | p * p > m   = [m]
      | m `mod` p == 0 = p : go (strip m p) (p + 1)
      | otherwise   = go m (p + 1)
    strip m p
      | m `mod` p == 0 = strip (m `div` p) p
      | otherwise      = m

-- | The radical: product of distinct prime divisors.
rad :: Integer -> Integer
rad = product . distinctPrimes

-- | Quality of a coprime triple a + b = c.
quality :: Integer -> Integer -> Integer -> Double
quality a b c =
  log (fromIntegral c) / log (fromIntegral (rad a * rad b * rad c))
```

### 5.2 Unconditional theorems pointing at abc

The conjecture itself is open, but several unconditional results have its shape. Stewart and Yu proved an *effective* exponential bound: max(a, b, c) < exp(kappa(epsilon) N^{1/3} (log N)^3) with N = rad(abc) and computable kappa [1]. This is vastly weaker than the conjectured N^{1+epsilon}, but it is the strongest known general estimate.

Toward Szpiro's conjecture, the best unconditional results bound the Faltings height exponentially in the conductor, while for Frey curves attached to *abc* triples one obtains subexponential bounds in work of Pasten [2]. The gap between these estimates and the conjectured polynomial bound measures exactly how far the theory still has to go.

Baker's 1998 refinement sharpens the conjecture's form without proving it: with omega the number of distinct prime factors of abc, there should exist an absolute constant with max(a,b,c) < c_0 (epsilon^{-omega} N)^{1+epsilon}, a version strong enough to give effective bounds in the theory of linear forms in logarithms [1].

### 5.3 What a proof would have to do

The equivalence of Section 4.1 shows that *abc* and modified Szpiro stand or fall together, so a proof may attack either face. The Frey-curve direction converts an additive relation into an elliptic curve whose discriminant encodes the triple; the reverse direction converts c_4^3 - c_6^2 = 1728 Delta into an additive relation among nearly coprime integers. Any proof must control, simultaneously, *additive* relations and *multiplicative* prime-divisor data — the "balance or tension between addition and multiplication" Waldschmidt describes [4]. The polynomial case shows the statement is true when differentiation is available (Mason's proof uses the Wronskian); over the integers there is no derivative, and every approach must find a substitute — Arakelov geometry, anabelian reconstruction, or a genuinely new idea [1][4].

## 6 Limitations

Honesty requires stating what is *not* known. The *abc* conjecture is unproven, and every theorem stated "assuming abc" — asymptotic Fermat, effective Mordell, Fermat-Catalan — is a promissory note, not a result [4]. The constant C(epsilon) is ineffective even conditionally. The proven equivalence is with the *modified* Szpiro conjecture, not the original discriminant form, and the exponent 6, while best possible, leaves the original inequality's sharpness open [2].

Vojta's dictionary, for all its explanatory power, is itself largely conjectural beyond dimension one: the truncated Second Main Theorem it predicts for higher-dimensional varieties is wide open, and controlling the ramification term is the central difficulty [6][7]. Computational evidence probes only an infinitesimal corner of the integers — quality computations up to any feasible bound say nothing about the asymptotic regime where the conjecture lives [3]. Finally, the status of Mochizuki's claimed proof is genuinely unresolved in the sociological sense: a claimed, published proof exists, and expert consensus does not accept it; the Lean formalization projects may eventually adjudicate the disputed inference, or stall exactly where human readers did [8][10].

## 7 Conclusion

The *abc* conjecture sits at a remarkable triple junction. As Masser and Oesterlé formulated it, it is a statement about prime divisors of sums — elementary enough to explain to an undergraduate, deep enough to imply Fermat's Last Theorem asymptotically [1][4]. As Szpiro's conjecture, it bounds discriminants of elliptic curves by conductors [2]. As Vojta understood it, it is the one-dimensional case of a conjectural Second Main Theorem unifying Diophantine approximation with complex analysis [5][6][7]. That three such different mathematical worlds single out the *same* inequality is the strongest evidence we have that the conjecture is true — and that its proof, when it comes, will reshape arithmetic geometry.
