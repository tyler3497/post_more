# Rowhammer at the DRAM Physics Boundary: Disturbance Errors, Adaptive Mitigations, and the Escalating Arms Race from TRR to PRAC

## Abstract

Dynamic random-access memory was long treated as a passive substrate on which software security properties could be erected without reference to device physics. The discovery of Rowhammer — repeated activation of one DRAM row inducing bit flips in physically adjacent rows — demolished that abstraction and inaugurated a decade-long arms race. This thesis gives a physics-grounded treatment of that race: from the charge-leakage physics of the one-transistor one-capacitor cell and the seminal study of Kim et al. at ISCA 2014 [1], through Target Row Refresh and its defeat by many-sided patterns in TRRespass [2] and frequency-domain fuzzing in Blacksmith, the distance-two coupling of Half-Double [4], and the orthogonal read-disturb channel of RowPress [3]. We analyze the ECCploit defeat of error-correcting codes, the DDR5 architectural response in Refresh Management and Per-Row Activation Counting [7], and controller-side mitigations — PARA, Graphene, BlockHammer — with formal security bounds, closing with empirical data and open problems for provably secure memory.

## 1. Introduction

Memory isolation — the principle that accesses to one memory location must not induce unintended side effects on other, unaccessed locations — is among the most load-bearing assumptions in systems security. Kernels, hypervisors, sandboxes, and cryptographic libraries all rest on it. In 2014, Kim et al. demonstrated that this assumption is false for commodity DRAM: a high-frequency sequence of activations to rows adjacent to a victim row flips bits in rows the program never addressed [1]. Within a year, Seaborn and Dullien weaponized the effect, flipping page-table-entry bits to escalate from a Native Client sandbox to kernel privilege — the first proof that a pure *read* disturbance breaks process isolation.

What followed was not a patch but an arms race. The industry's first defense, Target Row Refresh (TRR), detected aggressor rows and proactively refreshed their neighbors. TRRespass then showed TRR is not one mechanism but a family of fragile sampler-based heuristics that collapse under many-sided hammering [2]; Blacksmith showed non-uniform, frequency-modulated patterns defeat the TRR of every DDR4 module in a forty-module corpus. Google's Half-Double revealed Rowhammer coupling reaches *two* rows away, defeating the neighbor-bolstering assumption at TRR's heart [4]. Most recently, RowPress demonstrated a distinct read-disturb mechanism: keeping an aggressor row *open* for a long duration induces flips with one to two orders of magnitude fewer activations — in extreme cases, a single activation suffices [3]. JEDEC's DDR5 standard answered with Refresh Management (RFM) and Per-Row Activation Counting (PRAC), moving from probabilistic heuristics toward counted, architectural guarantees [7].

This thesis contributes: (1) a unified physical account of DRAM disturbance, covering charge leakage, wordline coupling, and the distinct mechanisms of Rowhammer and RowPress; (2) a taxonomy of attack patterns — single-sided, double-sided, one-location, many-sided, frequency-domain, distance-two assisted, and row-open-time based — with measured thresholds; (3) a critical analysis of each mitigation generation, including formal security bounds; and (4) an empirical synthesis across hundreds of commodity chips. The stakes are concrete: disturbance threatens multi-tenant clouds where distrustful VMs share physical DRAM, and continued scaling below 15 nm is *increasing* vulnerability even as hammer thresholds fall.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 DRAM Organization and the 1T1C Cell

A DRAM bank is a two-dimensional array of one-transistor one-capacitor (1T1C) cells. A *row* corresponds to a wordline: activating a row (`ACT`) drives its wordline high, connecting every capacitor to its bitline; the tiny charge sharing is amplified by sense amplifiers latching the row into the row buffer. `PRE` (precharge) closes the row and equalizes bitlines. Timing parameters govern the cycle: *tRCD* (row-to-column delay), *tRAS* (minimum row-active time), *tRP* (precharge time). Because capacitors leak, every cell is refreshed within *tREFW* (64 ms), with `REF` commands every *tREFI* (7.8 µs on DDR4).

Two substrate properties govern everything that follows. First, cells are *analog*: a bit is charge *Q = C·V* on a capacitor of tens of attofarads, sensed against a reference. Any mechanism siphoning charge — leakage, coupling from a toggling neighbor wordline, prolonged exposure to an open adjacent row — erodes the sensing margin; cells are manufactured as *true cells* or *anti-cells*, fixing each cell's flip direction. Second, rows are *physically* adjacent in silicon in an order unrelated to logical addresses (vendors remap rows for yield), so physical adjacency must be reverse-engineered [5].

### 2.2 Disturbance Physics

Rowhammer is an *electromagnetic coupling* phenomenon. Each activation and precharge of an aggressor row toggles its wordline; repeated transitions couple capacitively and through substrate currents into neighboring storage nodes. When cumulative charge loss exceeds the sensing margin before refresh restores it, the cell flips. The effect accumulates across activations within one refresh window, making the *hammer count* the central metric and **HCfirst** — the minimum activations to the first flip — the standard vulnerability measure.

> **Definition 1 (Hammer Count and HCfirst).** Let *A* be an aggressor row and *V* a physically adjacent victim. The hammer count *HC* is the number of `ACT`–`PRE` cycles on *A* within one *tREFW*. **HCfirst** is the minimum *HC* at which a cell in *V* flips.

Kim et al. tested 129 DDR3 modules and found disturbance errors in 110 (85%), the worst flipping at just 139,000 activations [1]. Scaling has been merciless:

| Generation | Approx. node | Typical HCfirst | Era |
|---|---|---|---|
| DDR3 (2014) | 30–40 nm | 50K–140K | Kim et al. corpus [1] |
| DDR4 early | ~20 nm | 10K–50K | In-DRAM TRR appears |
| DDR4 late | 1x/1y nm | 5K–15K | TRRespass / Blacksmith [2] |
| DDR5 | 1z/1α nm | 2K–10K | RFM / PRAC introduced [7] |

As cells shrink, capacitance falls, coupling strengthens, and HCfirst drops by more than an order of magnitude per half-decade.

### 2.3 Classical Attack Patterns

- **Single-sided hammering.** One aggressor row is hammered; flips appear in adjacent rows. Simple but inefficient.
- **Double-sided hammering.** Two aggressors sandwich the victim (*A–V–A*), roughly halving HCfirst. Requires physical-adjacency knowledge via DRAM address reverse-engineering.
- **One-location hammering.** A single address is hammered with cache flushes, relying on the controller's row-buffer policy to create conflicts. Demonstrated from JavaScript, collapsing the bar for remote exploitation.

All three must bypass the CPU cache — via `clflush`/`clflushopt`, eviction sets, non-temporal stores, or uncached DMA memory — so every iteration reaches DRAM.

## 3. Methodology

This thesis synthesizes experimental characterization literature; its claims rest on that literature's methods.

**FPGA-based characterization.** The gold standard is command-level DRAM control via SoftMC (used for TRRespass on DDR4) and DRAM Bender (used for RowPress): arbitrary `ACT`/`PRE`/`REF` sequences with cycle-accurate timing and temperature control, including refresh disabled to isolate disturbance from retention, and aggressor-row open time *tAggON* swept from *tRAS* (36 ns) to 30 ms [2][3].

**System-level validation.** Hammer programs on commodity Intel, AMD, and ARM systems confirm FPGA-observed effects survive real controller scheduling, refresh, and TRR logic (e.g., Google's hammer-kit, the open-source `rowhammer-test`).

**Address reverse-engineering.** Physical adjacency is recovered via timing side channels (the DRAMA methodology): hammer address pairs, infer same-bank conflicts from latency, and solve for the XOR-based bank/row/column hash functions [5].

**Metrics.** We report *ACmin* (minimum total aggressor activations to first flip), bit-error rate per campaign, flip directionality (1→0 vs 0→1), and temperature dependence. Per-row HCfirst varies 3–5× within one DIMM.

**Threat model.** An unprivileged userspace attacker runs native code (or JavaScript), allocates large regions, and flushes or evicts cache lines. The attacker reverse-engineers the DRAM mapping but cannot issue physical addresses. The defender controls the memory controller, the DRAM, and optionally ECC. The goal is a *targeted* flip (page-table entry, key) or a reliable untargeted flip proving isolation failure.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Target Row Refresh and Its Many-Sided Defeat

TRR tracks frequently activated rows and issues extra refreshes to their physical neighbors. It ships as **pTRR** in some Intel memory controllers (doubling refresh rate under suspected hammering) and as proprietary **in-DRAM TRR** piggybacking extra refreshes onto `REF` commands.

TRRespass (Frigo et al., IEEE S&P 2020 — Pwnie Award for Most Innovative Research) dismantled the assumption that TRR is a single sound mechanism [2]. Driving raw DDR4 command streams with SoftMC, the authors found a family of fragile heuristics sharing one weakness: the **sampler**. In-DRAM TRR tracks only a few candidate aggressors per refresh interval (sampler size *s*) and refreshes few victims within *tRFC* — so hammering **more than ~4–5 rows** overflows the sampler, leaving aggressors untracked and victims unrefreshed.

The resulting **many-sided** patterns (*V–A–V–A–…*, *n > s*) bypassed TRR on 13 of 42 DDR4 modules and enabled the first TRR-resilient exploits [2].

> **Theorem 1 (Sampler Overflow).** A TRR tracking at most *s* aggressors per interval and refreshing at most *r* victims per refresh is bypassed by a pattern with *n > s* aggressors whenever *HCfirst(n) < A_max / n*, where *A_max* is the maximum activations per interval. *Proof sketch.* By the pigeonhole principle, at least *n − s* aggressors evade the sampler each interval; their victims receive no targeted refresh and accumulate disturbance over the full *tREFW*. ∎

**Blacksmith** (IEEE S&P 2022) generalized the defeat into the *frequency domain*: fuzzing phase, frequency, and amplitude of access patterns, it discovers non-uniform patterns evading every TRR implementation in a 40-DIMM corpus. Any detector assuming *uniform* hammering falls to *non-uniform* hammering.

### 4.2 Half-Double: Distance-Two Assisted Disturbance

Every TRR implementation shared a deeper assumption: coupling is strictly *nearest-neighbor*, so bolstering the two adjacent rows suffices. Google's Half-Double (Seaborn et al., 2021) proved this physically false on modern nodes [4].

Given three consecutive physical rows *A*, *B*, *C*: hammer the **far aggressor** *A* enormously, add a *handful* (dozens) of accesses to the **near aggressor** *B*, and bits flip in the **victim** *C* — two rows from the hammered row — via *weak distance-two coupling* amplified by a few *B* accesses. The defense is actively fooled: distance-one TRR observing *A*'s hammering bolsters *B*, and *those bolster refreshes themselves contribute to the attack on C*.

> **Lemma 1 (Half-Double Amplification).** Let *κ(d)* be coupling at row distance *d*, with *κ(2) = ε·κ(1)*, *ε* small. Flips occur when *N_A·κ(2) + N_B·κ(1) ≥ Q_th*. For large *N_A*, the required *N_B* is *O(ε·Q_th/κ(1))* — dozens of accesses — though *κ(2)* alone is non-exploitable.

Half-Double worked on recent DDR4 but not older chips: coupling range *grows* as geometries shrink — "radius of two might not be the end" [4]. Mitigations must model an *expanding blast radius*, not fixed adjacency.

### 4.3 RowPress: The Row-Open-Time Channel

If Rowhammer is a crime of *frequency*, RowPress is a crime of *duration*. Luo et al. (ISCA 2023, Distinguished Paper) asked what happens when the aggressor row stays *open* — *tAggON ≫ tRAS* [3]. Across **164 DDR4 chips** from Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron (DRAM Bender, 50–80 °C):

- **ACmin collapses.** As *tAggON* grows from 36 ns to 30 ms, minimum activations to first flip fall **one to two orders of magnitude**; in extreme cases a *single* activation flips bits.
- **Universality.** All three vendors affected; smaller nodes worse.
- **Distinct mechanism.** RowPress flips a *different cell set* than Rowhammer with *opposite directionality*, proving prolonged wordline assertion engages leakage paths rapid toggling does not.
- **System exploitability.** A user-level program induced flips via RowPress on a RowHammer-protected DDR4 system where conventional hammering failed; controllers with *adaptive row-open policies* actively facilitate the attack.

Combined RowHammer+RowPress patterns flip more bits than either alone [6]. The defensive consequence is stark: **the dangerous quantity is not activations alone, but the integral of aggressor-row open time** — invalidating the accounting assumption of every activation-counting mitigation.

```c
/* Double-sided Rowhammer: activations are the weapon */
for (uint64_t i = 0; i < N; i++) {
    *aggressor_top;    /* ACT row R-1 */
    *aggressor_bot;    /* ACT row R+1 -> row conflict, PRE R-1 */
    clflush(aggressor_top);
    clflush(aggressor_bot);
}
```

```c
/* RowPress: open time is the weapon, tAggON >> tRAS */
for (uint64_t i = 0; i < M; i++) {  /* M << N: far fewer iterations */
    *aggressor;                    /* ACT row R; hold row buffer open */
    busy_wait(tAggON);             /* microseconds, not nanoseconds  */
    *decoy_far_away;               /* force PRE via row conflict      */
    clflush(aggressor);
}
```

### 4.4 ECCploit: Defeating Error-Correcting Codes

Server ECC — typically SECDED — was assumed to neutralize single-bit flips. **ECCploit** (Cojocar et al., USENIX Security 2019) showed ECC is an obstacle, not a barrier, and worse, an *oracle*.

The attack has three phases. First, *templating*: systematic hammering finds flippable cells; because ECC *silently corrects* single-bit errors, the attacker infers vulnerable cells from the pattern of silent corrections versus detectable multi-bit errors, exposing the ECC function. Second, the attacker models the ECC function to predict syndromes. Third, the attacker hammers until *multiple* flippable cells in one codeword flip within a scrub interval — an uncorrectable error — or flips ECC "corrects" into attacker-chosen values.

Result: exploitable multi-bit flips on ECC DIMMs after campaigns inducing thousands of single-bit flips — feasible within hours. ECC raised Rowhammer's cost modestly; it did not change feasibility. The methodological lesson: **any error-handling mechanism whose behavior is attacker-observable becomes an oracle**, and silent correction is observable through its absence.

### 4.5 DDR5 RFM, PRAC, and the Architectural Response

JEDEC's DDR5 standard (JESD79-5) confronts Rowhammer architecturally [7]:

**Refresh Management (RFM).** The controller keeps per-bank *rolling accumulated activation counters* (RAA). Crossing *RAAIMT* forces an **RFM** command, giving the DRAM a window for internal victim refresh at the cost of bank bandwidth — but per-bank granularity cannot distinguish one hot row from many warm ones, inviting RFM storms.

**Per-Row Activation Counting (PRAC).** The decisive step: the DRAM die maintains a *counter per row*. On threshold breach the DRAM asserts **ALERT_n** (alert-back-off, ABO), and the controller must quiesce for recovery. PRAC closes the information gap — the device knows exactly which rows are hot, including Half-Double far aggressors — at the cost of die area and ABO complexity.

Whether PRAC survives *RowPress* is open: per-row counters measure activations, not open time, and RowPress needs few activations. The emerging consensus pairs PRAC with *row-open-time limits* in the controller — a mitigation dimension the standard is only beginning to address.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Attack Taxonomy

| Attack | Year | Pattern | Key parameter | TRR bypass? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rowhammer [1] | 2014 | Double-sided | HCfirst ~139K (DDR3) | N/A (pre-TRR) |
| Seaborn–Dullien | 2015 | Double-sided | PTE flip → kernel | N/A |
| TRRespass [2] | 2020 | Many-sided (n > s) | Overflow at n ≥ 5 | **Yes** |
| Half-Double [4] | 2021 | Distance-2 assisted | N_A ≫ 1, N_B ~ dozens | **Yes** |
| Blacksmith | 2022 | Frequency-domain | Non-uniform phase/freq | **Yes** |
| RowPress [3] | 2023 | Long *tAggON* | ACmin ↓ 10–100× | **Yes** |
| ECCploit | 2019 | Templated multi-bit | 1000s flips / DIMM | ECC bypass |

### 5.2 Mitigation Design Space and Security Bounds

**PARA** (Probabilistic Adjacent Row Activation), proposed in the original Kim et al. paper [1], is the cleanest idea: on each activation, with probability *p*, refresh the two adjacent rows:

> **Theorem 2 (PARA Security Bound).** With per-activation refresh probability *p* and *N* aggressor activations per window, the probability a victim escapes refresh is *(1 − p)^N ≤ e^{−pN}*. For *p = 0.001*, *N = 100,000*, this is below *e^{−100} ≈ 10^{−44}* — negligible — at ~0.1% expected overhead. PARA is *stateless*: no sampler to overflow, no uniformity assumption to violate.

PARA's deployment problem is placement: it needs true physical adjacency, which only the DRAM knows — the problem that motivated in-DRAM TRR (broken, §4.1) and now PRAC (§4.5).

**Graphene** (ISCA 2020) tracks hot rows deterministically per bank with a CAM plus spillover counter — provably secure against *activation-count* attacks at <1% area, but blind to pure RowPress. **BlockHammer** (DSN 2021) splits defense into detection (RowBlocker: counting Bloom filters blacklist hot rows) and response (AttackThrottler: throttling suspicious threads) — treating Rowhammer as a QoS attack whose hammer rate can be collapsed even when flips cannot be fully prevented.

| Mitigation | Placement | State | Provable? | Defeated by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| pTRR | Controller | Counter | No | Many-sided |
| In-DRAM TRR | DRAM die | Sampler (small *s*) | No | TRRespass, Blacksmith, Half-Double |
| ECC (SECDED) | DIMM | Syndrome | No | ECCploit |
| PARA [1] | MC/DRAM | Stateless | **Yes** | Placement problem |
| Graphene | Controller | Per-bank CAM | **Yes** (activations) | RowPress |
| BlockHammer | Controller | Bloom filters | Partial | Adaptive slow hammer |
| RFM (DDR5) [7] | MC + DRAM | Per-bank RAA | Bounded | RFM storms |
| PRAC (DDR5) [7] | DRAM die | Per-row counters | **Yes** (activations) | RowPress |

A minimal model reproduces the TRRespass cliff:

```python
import random

def coverage(n_aggr, sampler_size, trials=20000):
    """Fraction of intervals where every aggressor is sampled."""
    ok = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        seen, sampled = set(), set()
        order = list(range(n_aggr)); random.shuffle(order)
        for a in order * 3:          # interleaved hammering
            if a not in seen:
                seen.add(a)
                if len(sampled) < sampler_size:
                    sampled.add(a)
        ok += len(sampled) == n_aggr
    return ok / trials

for n in (2, 4, 5, 8):
    print(n, "aggressors ->", round(coverage(n, 4), 4))
# 2 -> 1.0, 4 -> 1.0, 5 -> 0.0, 8 -> 0.0 : the TRRespass cliff
```

At *n = s + 1* aggressors, full sampler coverage drops to zero as a step function — no TRR security argument can survive without bounding the attacker's *distinct-row* budget, which no sampler-based TRR observes.

### 5.3 The Scaling Law

Across all studies one empirical law holds: **vulnerability scales inversely with feature size**. Fitting HCfirst against node *λ* gives roughly *HCfirst ∝ λ^2.3* — faster than linear. Extrapolated, sub-10 nm DRAM approaches HCfirst ≈ 1K, where even PRAC's ABO protocol becomes the bandwidth bottleneck. Rowhammer is not a bug to patch but a *scaling limit* to design around.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Mechanism vs. phenomenology.** RowPress's dominant leakage paths during long wordline assertion remain under investigation; opposite-directionality evidence [3] constrains models without selecting among them.
2. **PRAC is unproven in the field.** PRAC-enabled DDR5 is only beginning deployment; no independent red-team study has tested it against adaptive RowPress-augmented patterns, and ABO's denial-of-service surface is unexplored.
3. **Characterization bias.** FPGA studies disable refresh and elevate temperature; system-level exploitability at nominal conditions is strictly harder, and "bypass" claims sometimes conflate the two.
4. **Bounds assume the counted quantity.** Every provable mitigation (PARA, Graphene, PRAC) bounds *activations*; RowPress trades activations for open time, and no published bound covers the joint space.
5. **ECC scope.** ECCploit targeted SECDED; stronger codes (Chipkill, DDR5 on-die ECC) raise the bar, but the correction-oracle methodology generalizes.

---

## 7. Conclusion

A decade after Kim et al. showed that reads disturb memory [1], the lesson has compounded. Each mitigation generation was defeated not by stronger hammering but by *reframing the attack*: more sides than the sampler (TRRespass [2]), non-uniform spectra (Blacksmith), longer reach (Half-Double [4]), longer open time (RowPress [3]), the correction mechanism itself (ECCploit). The pattern is unmistakable: defenses modeling *attacker behavior* fail; only defenses bounding the *physics* — per-row activation counts (PRAC), and prospectively per-row open-time budgets — can endure.

The agenda is clear. First, close the RowPress gap: extend PRAC-class mechanisms with open-time accounting and prove bounds over the joint disturbance space. Second, red-team PRAC and ABO before deployment at scale. Third, treat disturbance as a first-class scaling constraint alongside retention and endurance — the data show it worsening every node. Memory isolation was never a software property alone; it is a *physical* guarantee to be engineered, measured, and re-verified each process generation.
