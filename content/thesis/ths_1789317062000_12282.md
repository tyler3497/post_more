# The Geometry of Deep Learning Loss Landscapes: Mode Connectivity, Hessian Eigenspectra, and the Edge of Stability

## Abstract

Modern deep neural networks optimize non-convex objectives over parameter spaces of millions of dimensions, yet first-order methods reliably find solutions that generalize. This dissertation develops a unified geometric account of *why* optimization succeeds by integrating three converging lines of inquiry. First, we survey **mode connectivity**: independently trained minima are joined by simple low-loss curves, contradicting the classical picture of isolated valleys [1][2]. Second, we analyze **Hessian eigenspectra**: the loss curvature decomposes into a massive near-zero bulk with a small number of large, data-dependent outliers [4][5]. Third, we examine the **edge of stability**: full-batch gradient descent drives sharpness to the stability limit $2/\eta$ and trains there, in flagrant violation of classical descent theory [6][7]. We present a reproducible methodology combining filter-normalized landscape visualization [3], curve-finding optimization, Lanczos-based spectral density estimation, and sharpness tracking, and report stylized empirical results on CIFAR-scale benchmarks together with formal results linking spectral outliers to Gauss-Newton structure. The synthesis reveals a single coherent mechanism: overparameterization creates flat, connected low-loss manifolds whose residual curvature concentrates along a handful of directions that optimization dynamics actively regulate.

## 1. Introduction

The success of deep learning poses a paradox that has resisted decades of optimization theory. Training a neural network means minimizing a highly non-convex loss function $L(\theta)$ over a parameter space with millions to billions of dimensions. Classical theory offers little comfort: non-convex optimization admits exponentially many local minima and proliferating saddles. Yet in practice, first-order methods reliably converge to solutions that generalize remarkably well.

This dissertation argues that the paradox dissolves once we understand the *geometry* of the loss landscape itself. Three empirical discoveries have fundamentally revised the classical picture:

1. **Minima are connected, not isolated.** Garipov et al. [1] and Draxler et al. [2] demonstrated that independently trained networks can be joined by simple curves along which training and test loss remain nearly constant. The landscape is better understood as a connected low-loss *manifold* than as a collection of isolated valleys.
2. **Curvature concentrates along few directions.** The Hessian of the loss at trained solutions exhibits a striking spectral structure: an overwhelming bulk of eigenvalues clustered near zero, plus a small set of outliers that depend on the data distribution [4][5]. The effective dimensionality of the landscape is far lower than the parameter count.
3. **Optimization operates at the boundary of instability.** Cohen et al. [6] showed that full-batch gradient descent does not monotonically descend, as textbooks prescribe, but rather *progressively sharpens* the landscape until the maximum Hessian eigenvalue $\lambda_{\max}$ hovers just above $2/\eta$, the classical stability limit, and then trains at this "edge of stability" indefinitely.

These findings are not three separate curiosities. As we shall develop, they are three views of a single geometric mechanism. Overparameterization furnishes vast flat directions (the zero-bulk of the Hessian); continuous symmetries and redundant parameterizations stitch minima into connected manifolds; and optimization dynamics, rather than converging to the flat interior of these manifolds, are attracted to their sharpest accessible boundary — the edge of stability — where the few remaining large-curvature directions are dynamically regulated.

The remainder of this work is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews background: loss landscapes, Hessian spectra, stability of gradient descent, and visualization. Section 3 presents a unified empirical methodology. Section 4 develops the three pillars in depth and synthesizes them. Section 5 reports stylized empirical results and formal theorems. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Loss landscapes and the classical picture

Given a dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ and a network $f_\theta$, the empirical risk is

$$L(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(f_\theta(x_i), y_i) + \lambda R(\theta),$$

where $\ell$ is a per-example loss (cross-entropy or squared error) and $R$ a regularizer. For deep networks, $L$ is non-convex, and the classical worry — dating to early work on spin-glass models of neural loss surfaces — was that gradient methods would become trapped in poor local minima. Empirical practice refuted the worry, but the *reason* remained obscure.

A critical technical obstacle to understanding landscape geometry is **scale invariance**. Dinh et al. famously showed that one can rescale layers of a ReLU network to make any minimum appear arbitrarily sharp or flat without changing the function it computes. Any geometric claim must therefore be invariant to such reparameterizations. Li et al. [3] addressed this with **filter normalization**: when perturbing weights along a direction $d$, each filter of $d$ is rescaled to match the Frobenius norm of the corresponding filter of $\theta$. This removes the scaling freedom and makes visualizations of $L(\theta + \alpha d_1 + \beta d_2)$ meaningfully comparable across architectures.

### 2.2 The Hessian and its spectrum

Local geometry around a point $\theta$ is captured by the Hessian $H(\theta) = \nabla^2 L(\theta)$. Its eigenvalues describe curvature along principal directions: large positive eigenvalues indicate sharp valleys, negative eigenvalues indicate saddles, and near-zero eigenvalues indicate flat directions. For a network with $d$ parameters the full Hessian is far too large to form explicitly, but its spectral *density* can be estimated with matrix-free methods (see Section 3.3).

Sagun, Bottou, and LeCun [4] were among the first to compute Hessian spectra before and after training, finding a consistent two-part structure: a **bulk** concentrated near zero and **edges** (outliers) scattered away from zero. They interpreted the bulk as evidence of massive overparameterization and the outliers as data-dependent structure.

### 2.3 Stability of gradient descent

For gradient descent $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla L(\theta_t)$, classical theory requires $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}(H)$ for local convergence near a minimum — the **stability limit**. The quantity $\lambda_{\max}(H)$, the *sharpness*, governs the fastest oscillation the discrete dynamics can tolerate. The conventional presumption was that successful training keeps $\lambda_{\max} < 2/\eta$ throughout.

### 2.4 Ensembling and the function-space view

If minima are connected by low-loss paths, one can harvest diverse models along a single trajectory — the insight behind Fast Geometric Ensembling [1] — turning a geometric fact into an algorithmic advantage: averaging weights along trajectories works because the averaged point remains in the low-loss region.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology unifies four empirical probes, each targeting one aspect of landscape geometry. All experiments described are designed to run on CIFAR-10/100 with standard convolutional architectures (VGG-style, ResNet), consistent with the cited literature.

### 3.1 Filter-normalized visualization

Following Li et al. [3], we visualize 2D slices $g(\alpha, \beta) = L(\theta^* + \alpha d_1 + \beta d_2)$ where $d_1, d_2$ are random Gaussian directions with per-filter normalization:

```python
def filter_normalized_direction(theta, direction):
    """Rescale each filter of `direction` to match theta's filter norms."""
    d = [torch.zeros_like(w) for w in theta]
    for l, (w, v) in enumerate(zip(theta, direction)):
        for f in range(w.shape[0]):  # per-filter normalization
            w_norm = w[f].norm()
            v_norm = v[f].norm().clamp_min(1e-10)
            d[l][f] = v[f] * (w_norm / v_norm)
    return d
```

Grid evaluation over a range of $\alpha, \beta$ (typically a few dozen points per axis) yields contour plots that reveal valley width, barrier height, and asymmetry. The key comparison enabled by normalization is *across architectures*: skip connections visibly flatten chaotic landscapes into smooth, nearly convex bowls [3].

### 3.2 Curve finding for mode connectivity

To test whether two independently trained minima $\theta_a, \theta_b$ are connected, we parameterize a curve $\phi_\omega(t)$ with $t$ ranging over the unit interval, $\phi_\omega(0) = \theta_a$, $\phi_\omega(1) = \theta_b$, and minimize the expected loss along the curve. Garipov et al. [1] use Bézier curves and polygonal chains with a single bend point; Draxler et al. [2] relax from linear interpolation to general continuous paths. The bend point $\omega$ is trained with the same optimizer used for the network. A successful curve satisfies $\max_t L(\phi_\omega(t)) \approx L(\theta_a) \approx L(\theta_b)$ — the *barrier* along the path is negligible.

### 3.3 Lanczos quadrature for spectral densities

The Hessian spectral density $\rho(\lambda) = \frac{1}{d}\sum_i \delta(\lambda - \lambda_i)$ is estimated without forming $H$ via **stochastic Lanczos quadrature** [5]: Hessian-vector products come from automatic differentiation (the Pearlmutter trick), a Krylov subspace is built from random starting vectors, and the Ritz values are weighted to approximate $\rho$. A few dozen Lanczos iterations with a handful of random restarts suffice to resolve the bulk and the outliers on CIFAR-scale models.

### 3.4 Sharpness tracking during training

To detect the edge of stability [6], we track $\lambda_{\max}(H(\theta_t))$ — estimated by power iteration with Hessian-vector products — alongside the training loss at every epoch of full-batch gradient descent for a range of step sizes $\eta$. The diagnostic signature is *progressive sharpening* followed by a plateau just above $2/\eta$, with non-monotonic short-timescale loss that decreases over long timescales.

| Probe | Quantity measured | Key method | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visualization | 2D loss slices | Filter normalization | [3] |
| Connectivity | Barrier along curves | Bézier / polygonal chains | [1][2] |
| Curvature | Spectral density $\rho(\lambda)$ | Lanczos quadrature | [4][5] |
| Dynamics | Sharpness $\lambda_{\max}(t)$ | Power iteration | [6][7] |

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Mode connectivity: minima as points on a manifold

The central empirical claim of Garipov et al. [1] is startling in its simplicity: the optima found by independent training runs are connected by a polygonal chain with *only one bend*, along which training and test accuracy stay nearly constant. Draxler et al. [2] independently constructed continuous paths between minima of ResNets and DenseNets on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 that are "essentially flat" in both train and test landscapes, arguing that the sub-level set $\{\theta : L(\theta) \le c\}$ forms a single connected component for low thresholds $c$.

Several refinements followed. When two runs share an early training trajectory, even *linear* interpolation between the resulting minima incurs no barrier — linear mode connectivity. Entezari and collaborators traced residual barriers to permutation symmetries: neurons can be permuted to align two networks before interpolating. Benton et al. extended curves to **simplicial complexes** — multi-dimensional volumes of low loss containing arbitrarily many independently trained modes.

The theoretical upshot is a change of ontology. Minima are points on a **connected low-loss manifold** whose existence is plausibly explained by overparameterization (vast null spaces of the Hessian) and exact or approximate parameter-space symmetries. The practical upshot is cheap, diverse ensembling from a single trajectory [1].

> **Theorem (informal synthesis):** In overparameterized networks, the low-loss region is generically path-connected; the barriers reported in earlier work were artifacts of restricting attention to *linear* segments between minima rather than general curves.

### 4.2 Hessian eigenspectra: a bulk of flatness with data-dependent outliers

Sagun et al. [4] computed full Hessian spectra on small networks and reported the now-canonical decomposition: a bulk of eigenvalues concentrated around zero, and a small number of outliers scattered away from zero. The bulk grows with overparameterization — it measures the *redundant* degrees of freedom — while the outliers depend on the data, with roughly $K$ large outliers on $K$-class problems, suggesting the outliers encode class-level structure.

Ghorbani et al. [5] scaled this analysis to modern architectures (VGG, ResNet) on ImageNet-scale tasks using Lanczos quadrature, confirming and sharpening the picture:

- **Negative eigenvalues vanish early.** After a few hundred optimization steps, large negative eigenvalues disappear; the spectrum becomes almost entirely non-negative, with the vast majority of eigenvalues close to zero.
- **Batch normalization reshapes the spectrum.** The emergence of outliers is highly dependent on normalization layers; without batch normalization, the outlier structure differs markedly.
- **The Hessian is dominated by the Gauss-Newton component.** Decomposing $H = G + E$ into the Gauss-Newton matrix $G$ (second-order only in the loss, first-order in the network) and the residual $E$, the spectra of $H$ and $G$ nearly coincide. Since $G$ is a Gram matrix of per-example Jacobians, its outliers are controlled by the input-output Jacobian of the network — connecting curvature to the network's *function*, not just its parameters.

This last point is the bridge to generalization theory: flatness-based bounds and sharpness-aware minimization all implicitly exploit the fact that only a few directions are sharp, and those directions are tied to how the network processes data. The bulk — often exceeding 99.99% of eigenvalues [5] — represents directions in which the loss is locally constant: the flat floor of the connected manifold of Section 4.1.

### 4.3 The edge of stability: optimization at the brink

Cohen et al. [6] trained networks with *full-batch* gradient descent — deliberately, to remove the confounding effects of stochastic noise — and observed behavior that contradicts textbook optimization: **progressive sharpening** (whenever $\lambda_{\max}(H) < 2/\eta$, the sharpness *rises* throughout training), **stabilization at the boundary** (once $\lambda_{\max}$ reaches $2/\eta$, it hovers just above this value — the **edge of stability**), and **non-monotonic but convergent loss** (the loss oscillates on short timescales yet decreases reliably over long timescales).

This is not a quirk of one architecture: it holds across MLPs, CNNs, ResNets, and Transformers, across datasets and losses. It refutes the presumption that gradient descent operates in the stable regime $\lambda_{\max} < 2/\eta$, and with it, much of the classical convergence analysis applied to neural networks.

Arora et al. [7] provided the first rigorous theoretical account: in the edge-of-stability phase, the non-smooth dynamics of gradient descent induce an **implicit regularization** — the iterates evolve along a deterministic flow on the manifold of minimizers that *minimizes* the sharpness $\lambda_{\max}$ itself. The dynamics don't just find *a* minimum; they find one as flat as the step size permits, connecting the edge of stability directly to the flat-minima generalization hypothesis.

> **Theorem (informal, Arora et al. [7]):** For normalized gradient descent and related variants, the edge-of-stability dynamics provably converge to a flow on the minimizer manifold that minimizes the top Hessian eigenvalue, yielding an implicit bias toward flat solutions.

### 4.4 Synthesis: one mechanism, three views

The three phenomena interlock. Overparameterization creates a Hessian with a massive near-zero bulk [4][5]; these flat directions are what make low-loss connectivity possible, since curves between minima can route through the null space of curvature [1][2]. But the dynamics do not settle into the flat interior. Progressive sharpening [6] drives the sharpness — the handful of outlier directions — upward until it hits the stability boundary imposed by the step size. At that boundary, Arora et al.'s implicit regularization [7] selects the flattest minima compatible with the step size.

Put differently: **mode connectivity describes the *space* of solutions, the Hessian spectrum describes its *local shape*, and the edge of stability describes the *dynamics* that navigate it.** The classical picture — isolated sharp valleys found by monotone descent — is wrong on all three counts.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Formal Results

### 5.1 Stylized experimental protocol

We describe a consolidated protocol, faithful to the cited works, for reproducing the three phenomena on CIFAR-10 with a ResNet-18. Train two independent models to convergence with SGD, recording minima $\theta_a, \theta_b$. Then: **(1) Connectivity:** fit a one-bend polygonal chain between $\theta_a$ and $\theta_b$ by minimizing expected loss along the curve [1]; report the barrier $B = \max_t L(\phi(t)) - \max(L(\theta_a), L(\theta_b))$. **(2) Spectrum:** compute the Hessian spectral density at $\theta_a$ via stochastic Lanczos quadrature with 100 iterations and 10 random probes [5]; report the bulk fraction and outlier count. **(3) Dynamics:** rerun training with full-batch gradient descent at step sizes $\eta$ spanning two orders of magnitude; track $\lambda_{\max}(t)$ by power iteration and test the edge-of-stability signature [6].

### 5.2 Representative findings

Representative outcomes across the literature:

| Phenomenon | Measurement | Typical result |
|---|---|---|
| Mode connectivity | Barrier $B$ along one-bend chain | $< 0.5\%$ of endpoint loss [1][2] |
| Connectivity (linear) | Barrier along straight segment | Large unless shared trunk/permutation aligned |
| Hessian bulk | Fraction of eigenvalues near zero | $> 99.9\%$ [5] |
| Hessian outliers | Large positive eigenvalues | $\approx K$ (number of classes) [4] |
| Negative curvature | Large negative eigenvalues late in training | Vanish after early phase [5] |
| Edge of stability | $\lambda_{\max}$ vs. $2/\eta$ late in training | Hovers just above $2/\eta$ [6] |
| Progressive sharpening | Trend of $\lambda_{\max}$ while below $2/\eta$ | Monotone increase [6] |

### 5.3 Formal results

We state two formal results that anchor the empirical picture. First, the Gauss-Newton decomposition explains the outlier structure:

> **Theorem (Gauss-Newton outlier structure, after [5]):** For the cross-entropy loss, write $H = G + E$ where $G$ is the Gauss-Newton matrix. Then $G$ is positive semidefinite with rank at most $nC$ ($n$ examples, $C$ classes), and empirically $\mathrm{spec}(H) \approx \mathrm{spec}(G)$: the outliers of the Hessian coincide with the outliers of $G$, which are controlled by the singular values of the network's input-output Jacobian.

Second, the edge-of-stability analysis of Arora et al. [7] gives a dynamical counterpart:

> **Theorem (implicit regularization at EoS, after [7]):** Gradient descent with constant step size $\eta$, once it reaches the edge of stability, tracks a limiting flow constrained to the manifold of minimizers along which the sharpness $\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^2 L)$ is non-increasing; the dynamics therefore exhibit an implicit bias toward minima whose sharpness is minimal subject to the stability constraint.

A minimal reference implementation of the sharpness tracker used in Section 5.1 is:

```python
def estimate_sharpness(loss_fn, params, iters=50):
    """Power iteration for lambda_max of the Hessian via Hv products."""
    v = [torch.randn_like(p) for p in params]
    v = normalize(v)
    for _ in range(iters):
        grads = torch.autograd.grad(loss_fn(), params, create_graph=True)
        flat_g = torch.cat([g.reshape(-1) for g in grads])
        flat_v = torch.cat([x.reshape(-1) for x in v])
        hv = torch.autograd.grad(flat_g @ flat_v, params, retain_graph=True)
        v = normalize(hv)
    # Rayleigh quotient
    return float((torch.cat([x.reshape(-1) for x in v]) @
                  torch.cat([h.reshape(-1) for h in hv])))
```

## 6. Limitations

- **Scale gap.** The sharpest results (full Hessian spectra [4], exhaustive curve-finding [2]) were obtained on CIFAR-scale models. Whether mode connectivity and clean bulk-plus-outlier spectra persist at the scale of frontier language models — where the loss is next-token prediction over trillions of tokens — remains only partially explored.
- **Stochasticity.** The edge-of-stability analysis [6][7] concerns *full-batch* gradient descent, yet practitioners train with small-batch SGD and adaptive methods. Stochastic noise interacts with sharpness in ways the deterministic theory does not capture.
- **Causality of flatness and generalization.** The correlation between flat minima and generalization is well documented [3], but counterexamples exist: reparameterization can change apparent sharpness without changing the function [3]. The field still lacks a universally accepted *causal* theory.
- **Non-convex global structure.** Mode connectivity establishes path-connectedness of low-loss regions, but says little about *optimization complexity*: how long curves must be, how hard they are to find, or whether gradient-based curve-finding can fail on harder problems.
- **Architectural dependence.** Batch normalization, skip connections, and attention each reshape the landscape dramatically [3][5]. A unified theory must explain *why* these architectural choices help, not merely observe that they do.

## 7. Conclusion

The geometry of deep learning loss landscapes has been transformed from a subject of speculation into an empirical science. Minima are connected by low-loss pathways [1][2]; curvature concentrates along a handful of data-dependent directions while the vast majority of directions are flat [4][5]; and gradient descent operates not in the comfortable interior of the stable regime but at its very edge, where progressive sharpening meets the stability limit $2/\eta$ and implicit regularization selects flat solutions [6][7].

The synthesis offered here — overparameterization furnishes flat connected manifolds, spectral outliers encode the data-dependent signal, and edge-of-stability dynamics regulate those outliers — explains the founding paradox: optimization succeeds not *despite* non-convexity but *because* the non-convex landscape of overparameterized models is geometrically benign in precisely the ways classical theory failed to anticipate.

Open directions abound: extending edge-of-stability theory to stochastic and adaptive optimizers, characterizing connectivity at foundation-model scale, and converting geometric insight into next-generation training algorithms. The loss landscape, once feared as a rugged wilderness, increasingly looks like a well-charted terrain whose remaining mysteries are among the most exciting in machine learning.
