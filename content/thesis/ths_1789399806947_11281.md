# The Conformal Bootstrap: Crossing Symmetry, the Operator Product Expansion, and Rigorous Bounds on Conformal Field Theory Data

## Abstract

The conformal bootstrap is a non-perturbative framework that constrains conformal field theories using only symmetry, unitarity, and the associativity of the operator product expansion. Expanding four-point correlation functions in conformal blocks and demanding crossing symmetry yields infinite families of sum rules whose coefficients are squares of operator product expansion coefficients, hence non-negative in unitary theories. Searching for linear functionals that act positively on all but a distinguished contribution converts physical consistency into a convex optimization problem, solvable by linear and semidefinite programming. This program has produced rigorous bounds on operator dimensions and, applied to the three-dimensional Ising conformal field theory, the most precise determinations of the critical exponents eta, nu, and omega ever obtained, in agreement with Monte Carlo and renormalization-group estimates. This article develops the bootstrap from its axiomatic foundations through conformal-block technology, the numerical linear-functional method and its SDPB implementation, mixed-correlator islands, and the analytic lightcone bootstrap, closing with the method's limitations and open frontiers.

## 1. Introduction

Quantum field theories at second-order phase transitions, and certain strongly coupled gauge theories, enjoy an enlarged symmetry: in addition to Poincaré invariance and scale invariance, they are invariant under the full conformal group. A *conformal field theory* (CFT) is specified, in the bootstrap philosophy, not by a Lagrangian but by its *CFT data*: the spectrum of local primary operators with their scaling dimensions and spins, and the operator product expansion (OPE) coefficients that govern how operators multiply. The central claim of the bootstrap is that symmetry plus internal consistency — the associativity of the OPE, known as *crossing symmetry* — may suffice to determine this data, sometimes uniquely.

The idea is old. In the early 1970s, Polyakov proposed a "non-Hamiltonian" approach to critical phenomena in which the OPE and its associativity replace the Lagrangian as the starting point [9]. Ferrara, Gatto, and Grillo, and independently Mack, developed the representation theory of the conformal group and the covariant OPE in the same era [9]. In two dimensions the program triumphed: Belavin, Polyakov, and Zamolodchikov used the infinite-dimensional Virasoro algebra to solve the minimal models exactly, and Moore and Seiberg systematized rational CFT. In higher dimensions, however, the bootstrap stalled for decades. The obstruction was technical rather than conceptual: nobody knew how to compute *conformal blocks* — the kinematical functions packaging the contribution of an entire conformal multiplet to a four-point function — in closed form above two dimensions.

The logjam broke in 2001, when Dolan and Osborn derived explicit expressions for scalar four-point conformal blocks in four dimensions, nearly factorizing in lightcone coordinates [2]. Armed with these blocks, Rattazzi, Rychkov, Tonni, and Vichi revived the higher-dimensional bootstrap in 2008: they showed that crossing symmetry of a single four-point function of a scalar operator implies a rigorous upper bound on the dimension of the lowest scalar appearing in its OPE, a bound obtained by linear programming over a truncated space of derivatives [1]. The method was rapidly generalized — to three dimensions and the Ising model by El-Showk, Paulos, Poland, Rychkov, Simmons-Duffin, and Vichi in 2012, where a striking "kink" in the bound appeared near the known Ising exponents [3]; to semidefinite programming by Poland, Simmons-Duffin, and Vichi; and to arbitrary precision at large scale with the open-source solver SDPB by Simmons-Duffin in 2015 [5]. The mixed-correlator analysis of Kos, Poland, Simmons-Duffin, and Vichi in 2016 carved out an isolated "island" in parameter space containing the three-dimensional Ising CFT, delivering critical exponents more precise than any Monte Carlo or renormalization-group determination [6].

In parallel, an *analytic* bootstrap emerged. Fitzpatrick, Kaplan, Poland, and Simmons-Duffin, and Komargodski and Zhiboedov, showed in 2012 that the lightcone limit of crossing implies universal large-spin systematics: every CFT contains infinite towers of double-twist operators whose dimensions approach those of generalized free fields [7]. This article presents both branches — the numerical linear-functional machinery and the analytic lightcone program — as facets of a single principle: crossing symmetry is a rigid constraint, and rigidity is information.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Axioms of conformal field theory

A unitary CFT in *d* spacetime dimensions is defined by a Hilbert space carrying a unitary representation of the conformal group *SO(d+1,1)* (in Euclidean signature), a distinguished vacuum, and a set of local operators. Each local operator belongs to a conformal multiplet headed by a *primary* operator of scaling dimension Delta and spin ell, annihilated by the special conformal generators; acting with momentum generators produces the *descendants*. Unitarity imposes lower bounds on dimensions:

- Delta >= (d-2)/2 for scalars,
- Delta >= ell + d - 2 for spinning operators with ell > 0,

with the stress tensor saturating Delta = d at spin 2 in any local CFT. These are the *unitarity bounds*, and they will play a starring role: they restrict the spectrum over which the bootstrap sums, making positivity arguments possible.

### 2.2 The operator product expansion

The OPE asserts that the product of two local operators at nearby points can be expanded as a sum over local operators at one of the points, with a *nonzero radius of convergence* equal to the distance to the nearest other insertion:

> **Axiom (OPE associativity):** Operator products are associative inside correlation functions wherever the OPE converges. Different orders of performing the OPE on a four-point function must agree.

Conformal symmetry fixes two- and three-point functions of primaries up to constants; the three-point constants lambda_{ijk} are the dynamical OPE coefficients. Applying the OPE twice inside a four-point function gives the *conformal block decomposition*. For four identical scalars sigma of dimension Delta_sigma, with cross ratios *u* and *v* built from the insertion positions, one finds [3]

g(u, v) = sum_{Delta, ell} p_{Delta,ell} G_{Delta,ell}(u, v),  p_{Delta,ell} = lambda_{sigma sigma O}^2 >= 0,

where the sum runs over primaries in the sigma x sigma OPE (even spins only, by Bose symmetry), G_{Delta,ell} are the *conformal blocks*, and positivity of p_{Delta,ell} follows from unitarity with a real operator basis.

### 2.3 Conformal blocks

A conformal block G_{Delta,ell}(u,v) packages the contribution of an entire conformal multiplet — primary plus all descendants — and is fixed purely by conformal kinematics. It is convenient to trade (u, v) for complex variables (z, zbar) with u = z zbar and v = (1-z)(1-zbar). Dolan and Osborn showed that in even dimensions the scalar blocks nearly factorize; in four dimensions [2]:

g_{Delta,ell}(u,v) = (z zbar / (z - zbar)) [k_{Delta+ell}(z) k_{Delta-ell-2}(zbar) - (z <-> zbar)],

with k_beta(z) = z^{beta/2} 2F1(beta/2, beta/2, beta; z). In general dimension, blocks are computed via radial coordinates: the map rho = z / (1 + sqrt(1-z))^2 sends the cut z-plane to the unit disk, and the block admits a rapidly convergent expansion g_{Delta,ell} = sum_{m,n} c_{m,n} rho^{Delta+m} eta^n whose coefficients follow from the Casimir differential equation [4]. Crucially for numerics, the block as a function of Delta has only poles at predictable locations Delta_{m,n} below the unitarity bound, with positive residues — a fact exploited to build rational approximations for semidefinite programming [5].

### 2.4 Crossing symmetry

Performing the OPE in the (12)(34) channel versus the (14)(23) channel must give the same four-point function. For identical scalars this yields the *crossing equation* [3]:

v^{Delta_sigma} g(u,v) = u^{Delta_sigma} g(v,u),

or, subtracting the identity contribution and defining the crossing vector F_{Delta,ell} = v^{Delta_sigma} G_{Delta,ell}(u,v) - u^{Delta_sigma} G_{Delta,ell}(v,u):

sum'_{Delta,ell} p_{Delta,ell} F_{Delta,ell}(u,v) = -(F_{identity})(u,v),

where the primed sum excludes the identity. This is the master equation of the numerical bootstrap: a single functional equation encoding infinitely many constraints, with positive coefficients p_{Delta,ell} >= 0.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 From crossing to exclusion: the linear functional method

Suppose we conjecture that no scalar operator in the sigma x sigma OPE has dimension below some gap Delta_gap. To *rule out* this conjecture, it suffices to find a linear functional alpha acting on functions of (u,v) such that:

1. **Normalization:** alpha(F_{identity}) = 1 (or another convenient nonzero value);
2. **Positivity:** alpha(F_{Delta,ell}) >= 0 for every (Delta, ell) allowed by unitarity and the gap assumption, at every spin.

Applying alpha to the crossing sum rule then gives 1 = -sum' p_{Delta,ell} alpha(F_{Delta,ell}) <= 0, a contradiction — because every p_{Delta,ell} is non-negative. Hence the gap assumption is false: some scalar must lie below Delta_gap. This is a *proof by contradiction powered by convex duality*, and it converts physics into optimization: the strongest bound comes from the functional that exists at the largest Delta_gap.

> **Theorem (Functional exclusion):** Let S be a set of operator quantum numbers consistent with unitarity. If there exists a linear functional alpha with alpha(F_{identity}) > 0 and alpha(F_{Delta,ell}) >= 0 for all (Delta, ell) in S, then no unitary CFT can have its entire non-identity spectrum contained in S. The spectrum must contain an operator outside S.

In practice, alpha is taken as a linear combination of derivatives evaluated at the crossing-symmetric point z = zbar = 1/2, truncated at total derivative order Lambda. Rattazzi et al. used precisely this Taylor truncation with linear programming [1]; the bounds strengthen monotonically as Lambda grows.

### 3.2 Semidefinite programming formulation

Positivity for all Delta above the unitarity bound is a polynomial positivity constraint. Using the pole structure of blocks, one writes derivatives of blocks in the factorized form [5]

d^m dz^m d^n dzbar^n G_{Delta,ell} = P^{(m,n)}_{ell}(Delta) chi_ell(Delta),

where P is a polynomial in Delta and chi_ell(Delta) > 0 for all Delta above the unitarity bound. Then alpha(F_{Delta,ell}) >= 0 becomes a polynomial matrix inequality in Delta at each spin — exactly a *semidefinite program*: maximize the functional's objective subject to polynomial matrix positivity. Simmons-Duffin's SDPB solves this primal-dual problem with arbitrary-precision arithmetic, parallelized over hundreds of cores via MPI and the Elemental library, and it dramatically outperformed general-purpose SDP solvers on bootstrap problems [5].

A minimal sketch of the logic — a toy one-dimensional gap bound using sampled positivity — looks like this in Python:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog

def toy_gap_bound(n_derivs=6, gap=1.2, n_sample=400):
    """Illustrative sketch: search for a derivative functional ruling out
    a scalar gap `gap` in a 1d crossing problem. Positivity is enforced
    on a dense sample of dimensions (a real bootstrap uses SDP)."""
    deltas = np.linspace(gap, gap + 20.0, n_sample)
    # F[d, k] = k-th derivative of a mock crossing vector at the symmetric point
    z0 = 0.5
    F = np.array([[((-1) ** k) * np.exp(-d) * d ** k
                   for k in range(n_derivs)] for d in deltas])
    F_id = np.array([(-1) ** k * 0.5 ** k for k in range(n_derivs)])
    # linprog: find alpha with alpha . F_id = 1, alpha . F[d] >= 0
    c = np.zeros(n_derivs)
    A_eq = F_id.reshape(1, -1)
    b_eq = np.ones(1)
    A_ub = -F          # -(alpha . F[d]) <= 0  <=>  alpha . F[d] >= 0
    b_ub = np.zeros(n_sample)
    res = linprog(c, A_ub=A_ub, b_ub=b_ub, A_eq=A_eq, b_eq=b_eq,
                  bounds=[(-10, 10)] * n_derivs, method="highs")
    return res.success  # True  <=>  gap assumption refuted
```

The real implementation replaces sampled positivity with exact polynomial SDP constraints and the mock kernel with true conformal blocks [10].

### 3.3 The computational pipeline

A modern numerical bootstrap run proceeds in stages:

1. **Block tables:** compute derivatives of conformal blocks at z = zbar = 1/2 to order Lambda, for spins up to ell_max, using recursion relations in radial coordinates [4].
2. **Rational approximation:** fit each spin's Delta-dependence by polynomials times a positive prefactor, with rigorous control of the approximation error [5].
3. **SDP assembly:** build the polynomial matrix program encoding normalization plus positivity above the assumed gaps.
4. **Solve:** run SDPB; a dual-feasible solution is a *certificate* that the assumed spectrum is impossible, while primal feasibility exhibits an approximate crossing solution.
5. **Scan:** binary-search or grid-scan the parameter of interest (a gap, a pair of dimensions, an OPE coefficient ratio) to map the boundary of the allowed region.

An alternative "determinant" or Gliozzi method dispenses with unitarity: it truncates the crossing equations to finitely many operators and demands a vanishing determinant, which can locate non-unitary theories such as the Yang-Lee edge singularity at the cost of generality [9].

In Haskell-flavored pseudocode, the functional itself is simply a linear map on the Taylor jet of the crossing vector:

```haskell
-- A crossing functional: linear combination of derivatives at z = zbar = 1/2
type DerivOrder = (Int, Int)
type Functional = [(DerivOrder, Double)]

applyFunctional :: Functional -> (DerivOrder -> Double) -> Double
applyFunctional coeffs jet = sum [c * jet d | (d, c) <- coeffs]

-- Exclusion: positive on the identity jet, non-negative on every allowed block jet
excludes :: Functional -> Double -> [Double] -> Bool
excludes alpha fId blockJets =
    applyFunctional alpha (const 0) >= 0  -- placeholder shape
    && True  -- real check: alpha(F_{Delta,l}) >= 0 for all allowed (Delta, l)
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Conformal blocks: Casimir equation, poles, and recursion

Conformal blocks are eigenfunctions of the conformal Casimir operators with eigenvalue determined by (Delta, ell). Solving the resulting differential equation directly is impractical at high derivative order, so the field relies on two structural facts. First, as a function of Delta, each block is meromorphic with poles only at the locations Delta_{m,n} where a descendant becomes null — all below the unitarity bound — and the residues are proportional to blocks of shifted quantum numbers [4]. This yields recursion relations that generate high-order derivatives from seed data. Second, in radial coordinates the expansion in rho converges exponentially fast inside the unit disk, and the crossing-symmetric point z = 1/2 maps to rho = 3 - 2 sqrt(2) approx 0.1716, deep inside the disk, so modest truncation suffices [10]. Together these give block tables accurate to hundreds of digits, the bedrock on which rigorous bounds stand.

### 4.2 The linear functional as a theorem prover

A subtle point deserves emphasis: when the SDP is solved with exact rational arithmetic and the polynomial approximations carry rigorous error bounds, a dual-feasible functional is not a numerical hint but a *mathematical proof* that the assumed CFT data cannot exist. The 2015 SDPB computation, for instance, reported its 3D Ising bounds as rigorous, with the tiny quoted uncertainties reflecting the binary-search resolution rather than statistical error [5].

### 4.3 Mixed correlators and the Ising island

A single correlator leaves a one-parameter family of theories along its bound. The breakthrough of 2016 was to bootstrap the *system* of correlators {<sigma sigma sigma sigma>, <sigma sigma epsilon epsilon>, <epsilon epsilon epsilon epsilon>}, where sigma is Z2-odd and epsilon is the leading Z2-even scalar. The mixed crossing equations couple the spectra with a matrix of OPE coefficients that must be positive semidefinite — a larger SDP with far more bite [6]. Scanning the plane of (Delta_sigma, Delta_epsilon) and demanding a gap above epsilon for the next Z2-even scalar carves the allowed region down to a tiny isolated *island*. Increasing the derivative order Lambda shrinks the island onto the Ising point; the subleading scalar dimension Delta_3 then fixes the correction-to-scaling exponent omega = Delta_3 - 3.

### 4.4 The analytic bootstrap and the lightcone

The numerical bootstrap expands around the Euclidean point z = zbar = 1/2, but crossing also has a Lorentzian regime: the *lightcone limit* z -> 1 with zbar fixed, where two operators become null-separated. In this limit, the identity operator dominates one channel, and reproducing it in the crossed channel requires an infinite tower of large-spin operators — the *double-twist families* — with twists accumulating at tau = Delta - ell -> 2 Delta_phi + 2n for integer n [7]. Subleading corrections are controlled by the lowest-twist operators in the crossed channel: an operator of twist tau_min shifts double-twist data by powers of the inverse conformal spin, organizing an asymptotic large-spin expansion. Simmons-Duffin's lightcone analysis of the 3D Ising CFT solved this expansion to all orders asymptotically and matched about a hundred low-twist operators against numerics, showing that the same crossing equations govern both the Euclidean numerics and the Lorentzian analytics [7].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

The flagship application is the three-dimensional Ising CFT — the universality class of the liquid-vapor critical point and uniaxial magnets. The SDPB computation of 2015 obtained [5]:

- Delta_sigma = 0.518151(6),
- Delta_epsilon = 1.41264(6),

from a single correlator with a rigorous exclusion certificate.

- eta = 2 Delta_sigma - 1 = 0.036298(2),
- nu = 1 / (3 - Delta_epsilon) = 0.629971(4),
- omega = 0.82968(23),

the most precise values ever reported, surpassing Monte Carlo and renormalization-group estimates.

| Method | nu | eta | omega |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conformal bootstrap (2016) | 0.629971(4) | 0.036298(2) | 0.82968(23) |
| Monte Carlo (2010) | 0.63002(10) | 0.03627(10) | 0.832(6) |
| Renormalization group (1998) | 0.6304(13) | 0.0335(25) | 0.799(11) |
| Binary fluid experiment (2009) | 0.629(3) | 0.032(13) | — |

Beyond Ising, the bootstrap has produced: kinks identifying the O(N) vector models for N = 1, 2, 3 across dimensions [4]; rigorous lower bounds on the central charge c_T; bounds on the 6D (2,0) superconformal theory; exclusion plots mapping the space of 4D CFTs with no relevant singlet deformations; and constraints on gauge theories and defect CFTs [9].

## 6. Limitations

The bootstrap's strength — freedom from Lagrangians — is also its weakness. Key limitations include:

- **Assumed gaps:** most bounds assume a gap in some sector (e.g., only one relevant Z2-even scalar). If the assumption fails for the target theory, the "bound" does not apply to it. Truly assumption-free results are rarer and weaker.
- **Truncation:** derivative order Lambda and spin cutoff ell_max are finite; bounds are extrapolated in Lambda, and the extrapolation, while empirically stable, is not itself rigorous.
- **Upper bounds, not constructions:** exclusion carves allowed regions but does not construct the CFT. The extremal spectrum is a candidate, not a proof of existence.
- **Unitarity dependence:** the positivity underpinning the SDP fails for non-unitary theories; the Gliozzi determinant alternative requires guessing the low spectrum in advance [9].
- **Computational cost:** high-Lambda mixed-correlator SDPs demand supercomputer-scale resources; SDPB's arbitrary precision is powerful but expensive [5].
- **Analytic asymptotics:** the lightcone expansion is asymptotic in spin and needs low-twist data as input; it constrains tails of spectra, not the deep infrared [7].
- **No dynamics:** the bootstrap cannot tell you *why* a CFT exists or compute its Lagrangian description; it is a theory of constraints, and constraints alone do not always close.

## 7. Conclusion

Fifty years after Polyakov's non-Hamiltonian proposal, the conformal bootstrap has matured from a speculative program into the sharpest known tool for solving strongly coupled conformal field theories [9]. Its logic is austere: the OPE must be associative, unitarity makes OPE coefficients squares, and therefore any linear functional positive on all blocks but one yields a theorem. Implemented through semidefinite programming at arbitrary precision, this logic pins down the 3D Ising critical exponents beyond the reach of Monte Carlo [6]; pursued analytically in the lightcone limit, it reveals universal large-spin towers in every CFT [7]. Open frontiers include mixed systems with global symmetries and fermions, the S-matrix bootstrap for gapped theories, holographic CFTs at large central charge, and the dream of a fully rigorous, assumption-free classification. The bootstrap does not ask what a quantum field theory is *made of* — it asks what consistency *allows*, and finds that the answer is remarkably little.
