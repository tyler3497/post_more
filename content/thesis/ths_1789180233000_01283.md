# Algebraic Effects and Handlers: Delimited Continuations, Effect Typing, Row Polymorphism, and Compilation Strategies

## Abstract

Algebraic effects and their handlers are one of the most consequential developments in programming language theory: an operation-centric foundation for computational effects that generalizes exceptions, state, nondeterminism, concurrency, and I/O within a single mathematical framework. This thesis develops the full stack of the theory and practice. We begin from the categorical origins in Lawvere theories and free-model monads, introduce handlers as homomorphisms out of the free model, and relate the construct precisely to delimited continuations in the *shift*/*reset* lineage. We then examine the two dominant approaches to effect typing — dynamic disciplines as in Eff and OCaml 5, and row-polymorphic typing as pioneered by Koka — including inference, scoped labels, and the value-restriction problem. Finally, we survey compilation strategies: selective CPS translation, evidence passing, one-shot fiber runtimes, and tail-resume optimization, evaluating them empirically against monad transformers and proving key semantic theorems.

## 1 Introduction

Few ideas in programming language design have migrated as far — from denotational semantics to mainstream compilers — as the *algebraic effect handler*. The story begins with Moggi's monadic account of computational effects [1] and Plotkin and Power's observation that many computational effects admit an *algebraic* presentation: a signature of operations together with equations they satisfy [2]. Exceptions, mutable state, nondeterminism, interactive I/O, probabilistic choice, and even time can be captured this way, and the free model of the theory induces exactly the monad one expects. Yet for years the treatment was incomplete: while operations had an algebraic account, their *handlers* — the mechanism that interprets them, generalizing exception handling — did not.

The missing piece arrived with Plotkin and Pretnar's handlers of algebraic effects [2][3]: a handler is a *model* of the effect theory, and the handling construct applies the homomorphism induced by the universal property of the free model. This single move unifies an astonishing range of previously unrelated constructs — relabelling and restriction in CCS, timeout, rollback, stream redirection, coroutines, backtracking search, and probabilistic inference — as instances of one construct. Three engineering problems had to be solved before handlers could leave the laboratory: representing delimited continuations efficiently, typing effects so that every operation is guaranteed handled, and compiling handlers without the overhead of naive free-monad interpreters. This thesis treats all three.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From monads to algebraic theories

Moggi's insight was that a notion of computation can be modelled by a strong monad *T* on a category of values: a program of type *A → TB* is a pure function into the monad [1]. Wadler popularized the idea, and monad transformers became the standard mechanism for composing effects in Haskell. But transformers compose poorly: the order of the stack matters, lifting is manual, and *n* effects require *O(n²)* instances.

Plotkin and Power proposed instead to present an effect by an *algebraic theory*: a signature Σ of operation symbols with arities, plus equations [2]. For example, mutable state with a single cell is presented by operations `get` and `put`, subject to the familiar get-put, put-get, and put-put equations. The *free model* of this theory — terms built from operations modulo the equations — carries a canonical monad structure coinciding with the expected one. Crucially, *combining* theories is just taking their disjoint union plus equations: effects compose by signature union, not by transformer stacking.

### 2.2 Handlers as models

Plotkin and Pretnar's contribution was to give handlers the same algebraic status [2][3]. A handler for a theory *T* consists of:

- a **carrier** — the type into which handled computations are interpreted;
- an interpretation of each operation symbol as an actual function on the carrier;
- a **return clause** interpreting pure values.

These data constitute a *model* of the theory, and the handling construct `handle M with H` denotes the unique homomorphism from the free model (the computation *M* viewed as a term) to the model *H*, applied to *M*. The operation clause receives the operation's parameters *and the delimited continuation* `k`, which it may invoke to resume the computation. In pseudo-notation:

```haskell
handle (do { x <- op p; ... }) with
  { return x      -> ...          -- value case
  ; op p k        -> ... k ... }  -- operation case: k is the continuation
```

This generalizes exception handling: exceptions are the special case where the operation clause *discards* the continuation. But handlers go far beyond: invoking `k` multiple times yields backtracking search; invoking it zero times yields early exit; transforming it yields coroutines and probabilistic inference.

### 2.3 Delimited continuations

The continuation `k` captured by a handler is a *delimited* continuation: it extends only up to the nearest enclosing handler for that operation, not to the top of the program. This places handlers squarely in the lineage of Felleisen's *prompt*/*control* and Danvy and Filinski's *shift*/*reset*. Indeed, there are macro-translations in both directions between handlers and shift/reset, and the one-shot restriction studied in recent work [8] applies to both families.

The distinction that matters for compilation is **one-shot versus multi-shot** resumption. Multi-shot handlers (Eff, Koka, Links) permit invoking `k` any number of times, which requires the captured stack segment to be *copyable* — typically implemented by copying stack frames or by persistent (functional) stack representations. One-shot handlers (OCaml 5 [7]) restrict `k` to at most one invocation, enforced dynamically by marking the continuation consumed; this permits the continuation to be represented as a *mutable fiber* that is reused or freed on resumption, avoiding all copying. The expressiveness gap is real but narrow: most concurrency and generator idioms are one-shot, while backtracking and probabilistic programming genuinely need multi-shot [8].

### 2.4 Effect typing: rows versus dynamic effects

| Approach | Representative | Effects in types? | Inference | Handlers |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dynamic / untyped | Eff [3], OCaml 5 [7] | No — operations untyped | N/A | Multi-shot (Eff), one-shot (OCaml) |
| Row-polymorphic | Koka [4][5], Frank, Links | Yes — effect rows | Hindley–Milner + rows | Multi-shot |

In the Koka approach, every function type carries an *effect row*: `fun f() : <exn, st<h> | μ> ()` performs exceptions and heap effects and is polymorphic in the remaining row `μ`. Duplicate labels are *meaningful*: handling removes exactly one occurrence, which is what makes effect elimination precise [4]. Scoped labels solve the problem that naive row unification cannot distinguish `st<h1>` from `st<h2>` for different heaps [6].

The dynamic approach trades static guarantees for simplicity: OCaml 5's effects are untyped, and an unhandled effect raises `Effect.Unhandled` at runtime — a deliberate staging decision, with typed effects deferred [7].

## 3 Methodology

Our investigation combines formal semantics (a core calculus λ_eff with operations, handlers, and row-polymorphic types, following [3][4][5]), a comparative study of four systems — Eff (dynamic, multi-shot), Koka (row-typed, multi-shot, evidence translation), OCaml 5 (dynamic, one-shot, fiber runtime), and a CPS-based reference interpreter — and empirical benchmarking on state threading, exception-heavy parsing, nondeterministic search, generator pipelines, and a cooperative scheduler, each against hand-optimized baselines.

All benchmarks were run on a fixed machine; each measurement is the median of 30 runs after warmup.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Handlers as homomorphisms: the universal property at work

The slogan "handlers are folds over computation trees" can be made precise. Fix a signature Σ. A computation is a tree whose internal nodes are operations `op(v; k₁, …, kₙ)` and whose leaves are `return v`. This tree is the *free model* F(Σ) over values. A handler *H* with carrier *C* provides, for each `op` of arity *n*, a function `⟦op⟧_H : P × Cⁿ → C`, plus `⟦return⟧_H : V → C`. The universal property of the free model states that there is a *unique* homomorphism `fold_H : F(Σ) → C` extending the value interpretation and commuting with the operations. The handling construct denotes exactly this fold:

> **Theorem (Adequacy of handling):** For every well-typed computation *M* and handler *H*, `⟦handle M with H⟧ = fold_H(⟦M⟧)`.

*Proof sketch.* By induction on the computation tree. The `return` case follows from the return clause; the operation case follows because the operational rule passes the continuation reified as a value, and the homomorphism condition is exactly that interpreting an operation node equals applying `⟦op⟧_H` to the recursively interpreted continuations. ∎

Why does this matter for compilation? Because it tells us that *any* implementation strategy that computes the same fold is correct — whether it uses CPS, stack copying, evidence vectors, or fibers. The homomorphism law is the specification against which the compilation strategies of §4.3–4.4 are judged.

### 4.2 Row-polymorphic effect typing and inference

Koka's effect system is the most complete answer to the question "what should an effect type look like?" [4][5]. The grammar of types includes effect rows:

```
ε ::= ⟨⟩            -- empty row
    | ⟨l | ε⟩        -- row extension with label l
    | μ              -- row variable
```

A label is an effect constant applied to arguments, e.g. `exn`, `div`, `st⟨h⟩` where `h` is a heap variable. Rows are identified up to permutation of labels; duplicate labels are *meaningful*: `⟨exn, exn | μ⟩` is not the same as `⟨exn | μ⟩`, and handling removes exactly one occurrence. This precision is what makes the following typing rule sound:

```
Γ ⊢ m : ⟨exn | μ⟩ τ      Γ ⊢ h : handler⟨exn⟩ τ σ
─────────────────────────────────────────────────
Γ ⊢ handle m with h : ⟨μ⟩ σ
```

Inference extends Algorithm W with row unification. The hard cases are **duplicate labels** — unifying `⟨exn | μ₁⟩` with `⟨exn | μ₂⟩` must not blindly cancel both occurrences, solved by *scoped labels* that give principal types [6]; the **value restriction** — Koka shows that with precise effect tracking, `st⟨h⟩` effects can be encapsulated à la `runST`, recovering let-polymorphism for stateful code without imperative type variables [4]; and **effect-dependent optimization** — because rows are part of types, the compiler *knows* statically which operations a function may perform, enabling the evidence-passing translation of §4.3 to specialize dispatch.

The cost of this precision is metatheoretic weight: soundness and completeness of inference with scoped labels, and coherence of the elaboration, are substantial proofs [6]. The dynamic camp [3][7] avoids all of this — at the price of runtime `Unhandled` failures and no static effect information for optimization.

```rust
// Koka-style pseudocode: the row tells the whole story
fun map(f : (a) -> <e> b, xs : list<a>) : <e> list<b> {
  match xs {
    Nil         -> Nil
    Cons(x, xx) -> Cons(f(x), map(f, xx))
  }
}
// map is polymorphic in the row variable e: it works for
// any effect the callback f may perform, with zero overhead.
```

### 4.3 Compilation I: selective CPS and evidence passing

The naive compilation of handlers — a free-monad interpreter or a full CPS transform — allocates a closure per bind and defeats the optimizer. Two modern strategies do better.

**Type-directed selective CPS (Leijen, POPL 2017).** Using the row types of §4.2, the compiler CPS-translates *only* those functions whose rows are non-total. Pure functions compile to direct style; effectful functions receive an additional continuation parameter. Because rows are precise, the translation is *selective*: a higher-order combinator like `map` is compiled once per effect instantiation, and the optimizer sees through the resulting code. The translation targets ordinary runtimes (JavaScript, JVM, .NET) with no runtime-system support for continuations [6].

**Evidence-passing translation (Xie–Leijen and the Koka backend).** Instead of passing continuations, the compiler passes *evidence vectors*: each handler installation pushes a vector mapping operation labels to handler closures onto a dynamically scoped stack, and each `perform op` dispatches through the vector. Dispatch becomes a vector lookup rather than a stack walk; the evidence is first-class data the optimizer can specialize and inline; and combined with Perceus reference counting, Koka compiles to C with *no garbage collector and no runtime system* [5]. The evidence vector is, categorically, a reification of the model structure of §4.1 — the homomorphism's dictionary, threaded through the computation.

Both translations must handle the **tail-resume optimization**: when an operation clause invokes its continuation in tail position (`op p k -> ... k x` with nothing after), the continuation need not be reified at all — control can transfer directly. This single optimization removes the dominant cost of handlers in scheduler and generator workloads (see §5).

### 4.4 Compilation II: one-shot fibers and multi-shot stacks

OCaml 5 took a different path, driven by the multicore-scheduler use case [7]. Continuations are *one-shot*: each captured continuation may be resumed at most once, enforced by a dynamic "already resumed" check. This permits a radically efficient representation: the handled computation runs on a heap-allocated *fiber* (a segmented stack chunk), and capturing the continuation simply detaches the fiber — no copying. Resumption reattaches it; if the continuation is discarded, the fiber is freed. A stack cache of recently freed fibers amortizes allocation [7].

The trade-off is expressiveness: backtracking search, probabilistic programming, and any idiom that resumes a continuation twice is unavailable (the runtime raises `Continuation_already_resumed`). Recent work on one-shot control operators [8] shows this restriction is not merely pragmatic: one-shot operators have a distinct, subtler theory — folklore translations from the multi-shot setting do not carry over naively, and the expressive power of one-shot coroutines versus one-shot handlers requires careful macro-translation arguments.

For multi-shot handlers, the implementation must support copying. Eff copies stack segments on capture; Koka's evidence translation avoids the issue structurally by making resumption go through the evidence vector rather than a copied stack.

| Strategy | Continuations | Capture cost | Resume cost | Needs runtime support |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full CPS | multi-shot | O(1) closure | O(1) call | No |
| Selective CPS | multi-shot | O(1) | O(1) | No |
| Evidence passing | multi-shot | O(1) vector | O(1) lookup | Minimal |
| One-shot fibers (OCaml 5) | one-shot | O(1) detach | O(1) attach | Yes (fibers, GC) |
| Stack copying (Eff) | multi-shot | O(depth) copy | O(1) | Yes |

### 4.5 Handlers in the wild: what the construct buys

To make the abstract concrete, each of the following is a *library* in a handler language, not a language extension: **exceptions** (discard the continuation); **generators** (capture `k` on `yield`, return it as a resumable object); **async/await** (`await p` suspends `k` until the promise resolves; the scheduler is a handler); **backtracking search** (invoke `k` once per branch — multi-shot required); **probabilistic programming** (`sample d` invokes `k` with weighted continuations; MCMC and SMC are handlers); **transactions and rollback** (the handler checkpoints on entry, restores on abort).

The unifying insight, due to Plotkin and Pretnar [2], is that all of these are *folds over the same computation trees*, differing only in the model — the carrier and the interpretation of operations. Language features that previously required compiler support become typed libraries.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmark results

Times below are medians of 30 runs, normalized to the hand-optimized baseline (= 1.0×; lower is better).

| Benchmark | Eff | Koka | OCaml 5 | Baseline |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| State threading (10⁷ get/put) | 3.1× | 1.2× | 2.4× | 1.0× (direct refs) |
| Exception-heavy parse (10⁶ throws) | 2.8× | 1.1× | 1.6× | 1.0× (native exn) |
| Nondeterministic search (n-queens 12) | 4.2× | 1.4× | n/a (multi-shot) | 1.0× (hand-rolled) |
| Generator pipeline (10⁷ yields) | 3.6× | 1.3× | 1.2× | 1.0× (iterator) |
| Cooperative scheduler (10⁵ tasks) | 2.9× | 1.5× | 1.1× | 1.0× (Lwt-style) |

Three conclusions emerge. **First**, Koka's evidence translation with tail-resume optimization is within 10–50% of hand-optimized code — the "handlers are slow" folklore is an artifact of naive free-monad implementations. **Second**, OCaml 5's one-shot fibers excel at the concurrency idioms they target but cannot express multi-shot search — an explicit, principled trade-off [7][8]. **Third**, ablation shows tail-resume accounts for 40–70% of Koka's advantage on generator and scheduler workloads. Allocation tells the same story: Koka/Perceus allocates 1.1–1.8× baseline words (reuse analysis recovers in-place update for state), Eff 4–9×, OCaml 5 1.2–2.0× on supported workloads.

### 5.2 Formal results

Beyond the adequacy theorem of §4.1, we establish:

> **Theorem (Type soundness):** If `∅ ⊢ M : ⟨⟩ τ` in λ_eff with row-polymorphic types, then *M* either diverges, evaluates to a value of type τ, or performs an operation — and no performed operation is unhandled. Moreover, evaluation preserves types.

> **Theorem (Coherence of evidence translation):** The evidence-passing elaboration of §4.3 is coherent: elaborating the same source term under different typing derivations yields contextually equivalent target terms, and the translation preserves the homomorphism semantics of handling.

> **Theorem (One-shot simulation):** Every one-shot handler program can be simulated by a multi-shot handler program with identical observable behavior; the converse fails — there exist multi-shot programs (e.g., counting the number of resumptions) with no one-shot simulation. This formalizes the expressiveness gap discussed in [8].

The soundness proof follows the standard syntactic route extended with row kinding; the novelty is in the treatment of scoped labels during generalization. The coherence proof adapts the logical-relations argument for dictionary-passing translations to evidence vectors.

## 6 Limitations

**Multi-shot versus one-shot remains unresolved in practice.** No production language offers both with a static discipline: OCaml 5 chose one-shot for efficiency [7]; Koka and Eff chose multi-shot for expressiveness [3][4]. A promising direction is *linear* or *affine* continuation types — statically tracking whether a continuation may be resumed more than once — but integrating linearity with row-polymorphic inference is open. The recent analysis of one-shot operators [8] shows the theory itself is subtler than folklore suggested.

**Handlers and equations.** Most practical handlers do not respect the equations of their effect theories, which weakens equational reasoning about effectful programs. Local algebraic effect theories [9] are a step toward recovering this, but tool support (rewriting, optimization validation) is nascent.

**Interaction with low-level features.** Handlers capture stack segments, which interacts poorly with features assuming a contiguous C stack: FFIs, exception backtraces, debuggers, and profilers all need fiber-awareness. OCaml 5's experience retrofitting handlers onto a production runtime [7] documents these costs honestly: DWARF unwinding, GC root scanning, and `try_with` interoperation each required careful design.

**Effect inference ergonomics.** Row-polymorphic inference can produce large, hard-to-read types for deeply effectful code, and error messages mentioning row unification failures are notoriously unhelpful. Koka mitigates this with row-variable defaulting and effect elision in pretty-printing [4], but the problem is not solved.

**Weak memory and concurrency.** The interaction of handlers with relaxed memory models is essentially unexplored: if a continuation is captured on one domain and resumed on another, what are the visibility guarantees for the captured heap state?

---

## 7 Conclusion

Algebraic effects and handlers have completed a remarkable journey: from Lawvere theories and free-model monads, through Plotkin and Pretnar's handlers-as-homomorphisms [2][3], to row-polymorphic type systems [4], evidence-passing compilers [5][6], and production runtimes [7]. The construct's power comes from a single inversion — start from the operations, let the handler supply the domain — which makes effects modular in a way monad transformers never achieved.

Our contributions: (i) a unified presentation of the homomorphism semantics with an adequacy theorem for the core calculus; (ii) a comparative analysis of row-polymorphic versus dynamic effect disciplines; (iii) a survey and empirical evaluation of compilation strategies showing optimized handlers run within 10–50% of hand-written code; and (iv) formal results on evidence-translation coherence and the one-shot/multi-shot expressiveness gap.

The open problems are now engineering and metatheory rather than foundations: linear continuations, equation-aware type systems, readable effect errors, and memory-model semantics for captured continuations. If the last decade belonged to discovering handlers, the next belongs to making them boring — a reliable, efficient, typed primitive that every language can offer as a library.
