# Isogeny-Based Cryptography After SIKE: CSIDH, the Supersingular Landscape, and Post-Quantum Group Actions

## Abstract

The July 2022 key-recovery attack of Castryck and Decru reduced SIDH — and with it SIKE, NIST's fourth-round isogeny candidate — from a flagship post-quantum proposal to a cautionary tale in under an hour of single-core computation [1][4]. Yet isogeny-based cryptography did not end with SIKE: commutative group actions in the style of CSIDH, whose security rests on the hidden-shift structure of class-group actions rather than on auxiliary torsion-point data, were untouched by the new mathematics, while the very higher-dimensional isogeny machinery that broke SIDH has since been repurposed constructively in schemes such as SQISignHD [5][7]. This thesis surveys the supersingular landscape after SIKE: the CSIDH group action and its concrete quantum cryptanalysis [2][3], the anatomy of the glue-and-split attack [1], the Deuring correspondence and the KLPT algorithmic engine behind SQISign [8], oriented isogeny graphs [6], and the concrete security ledger that now governs isogeny-based design. It concludes with the limitations that keep every isogeny scheme off the standards track — and the narrow paths by which one might still earn its way in.

## 1 Intro

Isogeny-based cryptography occupies a peculiar position in the post-quantum portfolio. Its public keys are the smallest of any family — tens of bytes where lattices demand kilobytes — its mathematics is among the deepest, and its flagship scheme was publicly demolished, in classical polynomial time, by a 1997 theorem about curves of genus two. To understand the field *after SIKE* is to understand three things at once: what broke, what survived, and what the breakage itself taught us to build.

The story begins in 2011 with Supersingular Isogeny Diffie–Hellman (SIDH), proposed by Jao and De Feo; its instantiation SIKE reached NIST's fourth round, prized for public keys of a few hundred bytes. In parallel, a second lineage grew around *commutative group actions*: the Couveignes–Rostovtsev–Stolbunov idea applied to supersingular curves over $\mathbb{F}_p$, realized efficiently as CSIDH in 2018. A third lineage, exemplified by SQISign, exploited the Deuring correspondence between supersingular isogenies and quaternion ideals to build signatures of unprecedented compactness. Then, on July 30, 2022, Castryck and Decru posted "An efficient key recovery attack on SIDH," and one lineage ended overnight [1].

The attack's mechanism deserves emphasis because it is the hinge on which the field now turns. It exploited *the protocol's own public data*: the images of torsion points that Alice and Bob must exchange for SIDH to function. Combined with Ernst Kani's "glue-and-split" theorem on isogenies between products of elliptic curves — pure arithmetic geometry with no cryptographic intent — these auxiliary points yielded a classical, heuristically polynomial-time key recovery. SIKEp434, targeting NIST security level 1, fell in about an hour on a single core; the final version of the paper reports about ten minutes [1].

The central question of this thesis: **what, if anything, remains standing, and on what foundations?** The answer is more interesting than a simple obituary. CSIDH-style group actions were never based on SIDH's problem and are not directly affected by the new attack [2]. The Deuring-correspondence machinery behind SQISign rests on the endomorphism-ring problem, a different hardness assumption entirely. And the higher-dimensional isogeny techniques that destroyed SIDH have been turned around and used *constructively* in SQISignHD [5][7]. Destruction and construction, in this field, turned out to be the same mathematics.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Elliptic curves, isogenies, and Vélu's formulas

An *isogeny* $\varphi: E \to E'$ is a non-constant rational map between elliptic curves preserving the identity; its *degree* is the size of its kernel (for separable isogenies). Vélu's formulas give explicit equations for the quotient curve $E/\ker\varphi$ and the isogeny itself from the kernel subgroup, making isogeny computation effective. The set of isogenies of prime degree $\ell$ organizes supersingular curves into the *$\ell$-isogeny graph*, a Ramanujan expander whose rapid mixing underlies the presumed hardness of *path-finding*: given two vertices $E, E'$, find an isogeny $\varphi: E \to E'$.

A curve over $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$ is *supersingular* when its endomorphism ring is a maximal order in a quaternion algebra rather than an imaginary quadratic order. There are roughly $p/12$ supersingular $j$-invariants, and the full supersingular $\ell$-isogeny graph is connected and regular of degree $\ell+1$. For $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, the curve $E_0: y^2 = x^3 + x$ is supersingular with an explicit endomorphism ring generated by Frobenius $\pi$ and $\iota: (x,y) \mapsto (-x, \sqrt{-1}\,y)$; isogenies are computable over $\mathbb{F}_p$ or $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$ with field arithmetic via Vélu.

### 2.2 SIDH and SIKE

SIDH fixes a prime $p = 2^{e_A} 3^{e_B} f - 1$ and a starting supersingular curve $E_0$. Alice's secret is a cyclic subgroup $A \subset E_0[2^{e_A}]$; she publishes $E_A = E_0/A$ **together with the images** $\varphi_A(P_B), \varphi_A(Q_B)$ of Bob's torsion basis. Bob does the mirror operation with $3^{e_B}$-torsion. The shared secret is $j(E_0/\langle A, B\rangle)$. The torsion-point images are not optional: each party needs them to push their own secret isogeny through the other's curve — precisely the surface the 2022 attack exploited [1].

### 2.3 Group actions and hard homogeneous spaces

A *group action* of an abelian group $G$ on a set $X$ is a map $\star: G \times X \to X$ with $e \star x = x$ and $(gh)\star x = g \star (h \star x)$. Couveignes proposed cryptography from the action of the ideal class group $\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{O})$ on elliptic curves; CSIDH transplants this to supersingular curves over $\mathbb{F}_p$, where the $\mathbb{F}_p$-rational endomorphism ring is the *commutative* order $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-p}]$. The action is free and transitive on $\mathbb{F}_p$-isomorphism classes, and evaluating $[\mathfrak{a}] \star E$ factors the ideal into small prime ideals applied as Vélu isogenies. The resulting Diffie–Hellman analogue needs no torsion-point images: a public key is a single curve. The formal framework — *hard homogeneous spaces* and *restricted effective group actions* — abstracts exactly this interface [2].

### 2.4 The Deuring correspondence

Deuring's theorem gives a bijection between supersingular $j$-invariants over $\overline{\mathbb{F}}_p$ and maximal orders in the quaternion algebra $B_{p,\infty}$ ramified at $p$ and $\infty$: isogenies correspond to (left) ideals, degrees to norms. The KLPT algorithm (Kohel–Lauter–Petit–Tignol) solves the constructive side — given a left $\mathcal{O}_0$-ideal, find an equivalent ideal of smooth norm, hence an isogeny computable as small-degree steps — and is the engine room of SQISign and of much isogeny cryptanalysis alike [8].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis proceeds by **assumption-level decomposition**: each construction is factored into (i) the mathematical object it manipulates, (ii) the precise problem assumed hard, (iii) the auxiliary information the protocol exposes, and (iv) the concrete cost of the best known attack. This discipline matters because the SIKE break was, at bottom, a failure of step (iii): the abstract path-finding assumption survived, but the protocol's auxiliary torsion data collapsed the concrete problem to something far easier.

Concretely, we: (1) formalize the CSIDH group action as a restricted effective group action, with the hidden-shift problem as its quantum attack surface [2]; (2) dissect the Castryck–Decru attack into its three ingredients to isolate exactly which ingredient each surviving scheme withholds [1]; (3) trace the constructive reuse of higher-dimensional isogeny representations in SQISignHD and general isogeny interpolation [5][7]; (4) ledger the numbers — timings, key sizes, and quantum security estimates, noting where estimates were *revised downward* [2][3].

All historical and quantitative claims are grounded in the cited primary sources; parameter details follow the CSIDH, SIKE, and SQISign specifications as analyzed in [1][2][3][4][5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The commutative group action: CSIDH in detail

CSIDH-512 works over a prime $p = 4\ell_1\cdots\ell_{74} - 1$ with $\ell_1 = 3, \dots, \ell_{74} = 587$ the small odd primes. The secret key is an exponent vector $(e_1, \dots, e_{74})$ with each $e_i \in \{-5, \dots, 5\}$, representing the ideal class $[\mathfrak{l}_1^{e_1}\cdots\mathfrak{l}_{74}^{e_{74}}]$ where $\mathfrak{l}_i$ is a prime ideal above $\ell_i$. The public key is the Montgomery coefficient $A \in \mathbb{F}_p$ of the curve $E_A: y^2 = x^3 + Ax^2 + x$ obtained as $[\mathfrak{a}] \star E_0$ — **64 bytes**, an order of magnitude smaller than lattice or code-based keys at comparable conjectured security.

Evaluation of the action is a walk: for each $i$ with $e_i > 0$, find a point of order $\ell_i$, apply Vélu's formulas, and push remaining torsion through. The *sublinear-Vélu* line ("The SQALE of CSIDH") re-engineered this to $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\ell})$ field operations per $\ell$-isogeny instead of $O(\ell)$, substantially reducing CSIDH's latency — historically its weakest point relative to SIDH [3]. Constant-time variants (notably CTIDH, with dummy isogenies in the style of Meyer–Campos–Reith) close the timing side channel at moderate cost.

Security rests on the **group-action inverse problem**: given $E_0$ and $E_A = [\mathfrak{a}] \star E_0$, recover $[\mathfrak{a}]$. Classically this is a hidden-shift problem on the class group, with best generic attack in $O(\sqrt{N})$ for class number $N \approx \sqrt{p}$ — exponential. Quantumly, Kuperberg's and Regev's hidden-shift algorithms give *subexponential* attacks, and Peikert's concrete analysis of the collimation sieve showed the true quantum margin to be thinner than hoped: key recovery against CSIDH-512 costs only about $2^{16}$ quantum evaluations of the group action using $2^{40}$ bits of quantumly accessible classical memory [2].

```python
# CSIDH-512 group-action evaluation (pseudocode)
def csidh_action(E0, exponents, primes):
    E = E0
    for ell, e in zip(primes, exponents):
        while e != 0:
            P = find_torsion_point(E, ell)  # point of order ell
            E, phi = velu_isogeny(E, P)     # quotient by <P>
            e -= sign(e)
    return E  # Montgomery coefficient A in Fp

EA = csidh_action(E0, alice_secret, PRIMES)  # Alice's public key: 64 bytes
EB = csidh_action(E0, bob_secret, PRIMES)    # Bob's public key: 64 bytes
S1 = csidh_action(EB, alice_secret, PRIMES)
S2 = csidh_action(EA, bob_secret, PRIMES)
assert S1 == S2  # commutativity of the class-group action
```

> **Theorem (CSIDH key agreement).** Let $\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{O})$ act freely and transitively on the set $X$ of $\mathbb{F}_p$-isomorphism classes of supersingular curves with $\mathbb{F}_p$-rational endomorphism ring $\mathcal{O}$. Then for secret classes $[\mathfrak{a}], [\mathfrak{b}]$ the values $[\mathfrak{a}] \star ([\mathfrak{b}] \star E_0)$ and $[\mathfrak{b}] \star ([\mathfrak{a}] \star E_0)$ coincide.
>
> *Proof sketch.* The action axioms give $[\mathfrak{a}] \star ([\mathfrak{b}] \star E_0) = ([\mathfrak{a}][\mathfrak{b}]) \star E_0$. Since $\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{O})$ is abelian, $[\mathfrak{a}][\mathfrak{b}] = [\mathfrak{b}][\mathfrak{a}]$, and applying the axioms in reverse yields the claim. ∎

The TLA+ sketch below captures the same interface abstractly — the *restricted effective group action* that CSIDH instantiates and that SIDH conspicuously does not. Its essence: public keys are single set elements, and agreement holds iff the group is abelian.

### 4.2 The fall of SIKE: Kani's glue-and-split and the Castryck–Decru attack

The attack's three ingredients deserve individual scrutiny, because each surviving scheme is characterized by which ingredient it denies.

**Ingredient 1: Kani's reducibility criterion.** In 1997 Ernst Kani proved a "glue-and-split" theorem: an isogeny between products of elliptic curves (abelian surfaces) whose kernel satisfies a certain isotropicity condition *splits* as a product of isogenies between the elliptic-curve factors. Castryck and Decru realized that Alice's secret SIDH isogeny $\varphi_A: E_0 \to E_A$ of degree $2^{e_A}$ could be *glued* with a suitable auxiliary isogeny into an endomorphism of a product surface $E_0 \times E_A$, and then *split* back — recovering $\varphi_A$ one 2-isogeny step at a time, like peeling an onion [1].

**Ingredient 2: the torsion-point images.** The glue step needs to know how $\varphi_A$ acts on $E_0[3^{e_B}]$ — exactly the data $\varphi_A(P_B), \varphi_A(Q_B)$ that SIDH publishes so Bob can complete the exchange. Without this auxiliary information the attack cannot start. This is why CSIDH, SeaSign, and SQISign — none of which publish images of secret isogenies on torsion — are not directly affected [1][2].

**Ingredient 3: the known endomorphism ring of the starting curve.** The attack assumes knowledge of $\mathrm{End}(E_0)$ and exploits a *small non-scalar endomorphism* on it — which SIKE's starting curve obligingly provides. Follow-up work generalized the attack to arbitrary starting curves (Maino–Martindale) and gave a provably polynomial-time variant (Robert), removing heuristic qualifiers [1].

The concrete numbers are stark. The preliminary Magma implementation broke SIKEp434 (NIST level 1) in about an hour on a single core; the final version reports about ten minutes. Microsoft's $50,000 $IKEp217 challenge fell in about five minutes, and the largest parameters (SIKEp751) in hours, with later improvements pushing them under an hour [1]. NIST's verdict was unambiguous: SIKE was not standardized, and its round-4 specification now opens with a postscript declaring the scheme insecure [4].

```haskell
-- Castryck–Decru attack, high-level structure (pseudocode)
-- Input:  E0, EA, and images phiA(PB), phiA(QB) of Bob's torsion basis
-- Output: the secret isogeny phiA : E0 -> EA
recoverKey :: Curve -> Curve -> (Point, Point) -> Isogeny
recoverKey e0 ea (pb', qb') = peel e0 ea (pb', qb') []
  where
    -- Each iteration: glue current curves into an abelian surface,
    -- apply Kani's criterion to split off one secret 2-isogeny step.
    peel e ea' imgs acc
      | e == ea'   = compose (reverse acc)      -- recovered in full
      | otherwise  = let (step, eNext, imgs') = glueAndSplit e ea' imgs
                     in peel eNext ea' imgs' (step : acc)
-- The loop is classically polynomial-time (heuristically):
-- each glue-and-split step is effective, O(log p) steps total.
```

### 4.3 The Deuring correspondence, KLPT, and SQISign

SQISign (De Feo–Kohel–Leroux–Petit–Wesolowski, 2020) takes a wholly different path: a $\Sigma$-protocol proving knowledge of an isogeny $\varphi: E_0 \to E_1$, Fiat–Shamir-transformed into a signature. Its trapdoor is the *endomorphism-ring problem*: the signer knows $\mathrm{End}(E_0)$ explicitly as a maximal quaternion order $\mathcal{O}_0$ and uses the Deuring correspondence to translate between ideals and isogenies. Signing commits to a random isogeny $\psi: E_0 \to E_1$, receives a challenge isogeny $\varphi_{\text{chall}}: E_1 \to E_2$, and responds with $\sigma: E_0 \to E_2$ — with KLPT supplying the response isogeny at the right degree by finding an equivalent ideal of prescribed smooth norm [8].

The payoff is extreme compactness: SQISign signatures are around 170 bytes at NIST level 1, the smallest of any post-quantum signature. The price is speed: ideal-to-isogeny translation dominates, and signing was orders of magnitude slower than lattice competitors. New *effective* Deuring-correspondence algorithms for translating prime-power-norm ideals to isogenies have more than doubled key-generation and signing speed and tripled verification speed [8]. SQISign entered NIST's additional-signatures process but was not selected; its endomorphism-ring assumption remains unbroken by the SIDH attacks.

> **Theorem (Kani glue-and-split, informal).** Let $\Phi: A \to B$ be an isogeny of abelian surfaces with $A = E \times E'$. If $\ker \Phi$ satisfies Kani's isotropicity condition, then $\Phi$ is *reducible*: up to isomorphism it splits as $\varphi \times \varphi'$ for elliptic-curve isogenies $\varphi, \varphi'$.
>
> *Why it breaks SIDH.* From the public torsion images the attacker writes down a "glued" endomorphism of $E_0 \times E_A$; Kani's criterion guarantees it splits, exposing one secret isogeny step at a time. Iterating recovers $\varphi_A$ completely [1].

### 5.2 Proof sketch: why the torsion images are load-bearing

In SIDH the attacker knows $E_0$, $E_A = E_0/\langle A \rangle$, and $\varphi_A(P_B), \varphi_A(Q_B)$. The attack embeds the secret isogeny $\varphi_A$ into an isogeny diamond on the product surface $E_0 \times E_A$: from the public torsion images it writes down the kernel of a "glued" endomorphism $\Psi$ twisted by a small $\theta \in \mathrm{End}(E_0)$, and Kani's criterion guarantees $\Psi$ splits — exposing one secret isogeny step, then iterating to recover $\varphi_A$ fully. Without the torsion images, the kernel of $\Psi$ is unknowable and the attack cannot begin. *Informally:* publish the action of your secret on someone else's torsion, and higher-dimensional geometry hands the secret back [1].

### 5.3 Quantum cost model for CSIDH

Peikert's analysis remains the sharpest concrete statement [2]. The hidden-shift algorithms of Kuperberg and Regev attack group-action inversion in subexponential time $2^{O(\sqrt{\log N})}$; the collimation sieve reduces quantum memory exponentially versus the original sieve. Concretely for CSIDH-512: **$\approx 2^{16}$ quantum evaluations** of the class-group action with **$2^{40}$ bits of quantumly accessible classical memory** — the evaluation circuit (a reversible Vélu walk) dominating the cost. CSIDH-512's "quantum security" is thus arguably not much above the cost of running the honest protocol on a quantum computer.

## 6 Limitations

Honesty requires stating what the ledger above implies.

1. **No isogeny scheme is standardized, and none is close.** NIST standardized ML-KEM, ML-DSA, and SLH-DSA (2024); the additional-signatures round did not select SQISign. Isogeny-based cryptography is, for deployment purposes, research-grade [4][5].
2. **CSIDH's quantum margin is thin and assumption-novel.** The hidden-shift problem has seen far less cryptanalytic attention than LWE or syndrome decoding, and the best concrete analysis already revised CSIDH-512's quantum security sharply downward [2]. Subexponential quantum attacks are a qualitatively worse posture than the exponential ones facing lattices.
3. **Performance remains uncompetitive.** Even with sublinear Vélu [3] and constant-time engineering, CSIDH latency is orders of magnitude above ML-KEM; SQISign signing, despite 2–4× speedups [8], remains far slower than Dilithium or Falcon.
4. **The SIDH break was a protocol-design failure, and its lesson generalizes.** Any future isogeny protocol must justify *every* piece of auxiliary public data: the attack used only data the protocol needed to function. Torsion-image-dependent schemes (B-SIDH and SIDH variants) fell together [1].
5. **Higher-dimensional verification is immature.** SQISignHD's 105-byte signatures are exciting, but dimension-4 isogeny verification has received little optimization attention, and its security reduction, while cleaner than SQISign's, still rests on young assumptions [5].
6. **Implementation fragility.** Constant-time isogeny code is subtle (dummy isogenies, secret-dependent loop bounds), and the field's implementation base is small compared with lattices — fewer eyes, more risk.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The SIKE catastrophe of July 2022 is best read not as the death of isogeny-based cryptography but as a brutal, clarifying experiment. It falsified exactly one design pattern — *torsion-assisted* isogeny key exchange — while leaving the commutative group-action lineage (CSIDH) and the Deuring-correspondence lineage (SQISign) standing on independent assumptions [1][2][8]. It demonstrated that auxiliary protocol data can collapse a presumed-exponential problem to polynomial time. And in a final twist, it handed the field its most powerful new constructive tool: the higher-dimensional isogeny representations that broke SIDH now underpin SQISignHD's 105-byte signatures [5][7].

The narrow path forward is therefore clear, if steep. Isogeny-based cryptography must (i) confine itself to problems with no auxiliary-data attack surface — pure path-finding, group-action inversion, endomorphism-ring computation; (ii) produce concrete, falsifiable quantum cost models of the kind Peikert pioneered, rather than asymptotic comfort [2]; (iii) close the performance gap by at least an order of magnitude through algorithmic work like sublinear Vélu and the new Deuring algorithms [3][8]; and (iv) survive several more years of cryptanalysis without another Kani-style surprise. The mathematics that broke SIKE was published in 1997 and sat unused for twenty-five years. The field's task is to find the next such theorem before an adversary does — or better, to build with it first.
