Verifiable delay functions (VDFs) demand a prescribed number of sequential steps to evaluate while yielding a unique, efficiently verifiable output. This thesis compares the two landmark VDF constructions built on the Rivest-Shamir-Wagner time-lock puzzle: Wesolowski's proof of exponentiation, with constant-size proofs and two-exponentiation verification under the adaptive root assumption, and Pietrzak's recursive halving protocol, with O(log T) proofs under the weaker low-order assumption. We dissect both soundness arguments, quantify prover and verifier costs, and contrast RSA-group instantiations, which require trusted setup, against class groups of imaginary quadratic fields, which offer transparent setup at the price of slower arithmetic and subtler assumptions. We analyze VDF composition into trustworthy randomness beacons and fair transaction ordering, and stress the foundational caveat that sequentiality of repeated squaring remains conjectural and both constructions fall to quantum adversaries.

# Verifiable Delay Functions and Time-Lock Puzzles: Proofs of Sequential Work via the Wesolowski and Pietrzak Constructions, RSA and Class-Group Instantiations, and Applications to Randomness Beacons and Fair Ordering

## Abstract

Verifiable delay functions (VDFs) formalize a deceptively simple demand: a computation that requires a prescribed number of *sequential* steps to evaluate, yet whose output is *unique* and *efficiently verifiable* by anyone. This thesis gives a rigorous comparative treatment of the two landmark VDF constructions built atop the Rivest–Shamir–Wagner (RSW) time-lock puzzle — Wesolowski's proof-of-exponentiation protocol [2] and Pietrzak's recursive halving protocol [3] — set against the definitional framework of Boneh, Bonneau, Bünz and Fisch [1]. We dissect both soundness arguments (the *adaptive root assumption* and the *low-order assumption*), quantify the exact prover and verifier cost trade-offs, and contrast the two viable groups of unknown order that instantiate them: RSA groups, which demand trusted setup, and class groups of imaginary quadratic fields, which are transparent at the price of slower arithmetic and subtler assumptions [7]. We further analyze how VDFs compose into trustworthy randomness beacons and fair transaction-ordering protocols, the applications that motivated the entire line of work. Throughout we distinguish what is *proven* from what is *conjectured*: sequentiality itself remains an unproven hardness assumption, sharpest for repeated squaring in generic rings [6], and both constructions fall to quantum adversaries.

## 1. Introduction

Some computations resist parallelization. Modular exponentiation with a known exponent can be parallelized; *repeated squaring* — computing $x^{2^T} \bmod N$ by squaring $T$ times — cannot be obviously accelerated, because each squaring depends on the previous one. Rivest, Shamir and Wagner exploited exactly this asymmetry in 1996 to build *time-lock puzzles*: puzzles whose solution is guaranteed to consume a wall-clock delay, even for an adversary wielding massive parallelism [4]. A time-lock puzzle has a trapdoor, however — knowledge of $\varphi(N)$ lets the puzzle creator open it instantly — and its verification is private, requiring the trapdoor.

Two decades later, Boneh, Bonneau, Bünz and Fisch identified the missing public primitive [1]: a *verifiable delay function* (VDF) — a function requiring $T$ sequential steps to evaluate, whose output is *unique* and verifiable in $\mathrm{poly}(\log T, \lambda)$ time. This upgrades the time-lock puzzle decisively: verification becomes *public*, and output becomes *unique*, which is precisely what decentralized protocols need. A lottery run on a time-lock puzzle is not trustworthy, because the puzzle's creator knows the trapdoor; a lottery run on a VDF with transparent setup is.

The central open problem left by [1] — an *efficient* VDF with an exponential gap between evaluation and verification — was solved twice, independently, in 2018–2019. Wesolowski [2] gave a construction with *constant-size* proofs (a single group element) whose verification costs two exponentiations, proved sound under the *adaptive root assumption*. Pietrzak [3] gave a construction with $O(\log T)$-size proofs whose verification costs a handful of exponentiations, proved sound under the weaker *low-order assumption*. Both build on the RSW puzzle $y = x^{2^T} \bmod N$ in a group of unknown order, and both were made non-interactive via the Fiat–Shamir heuristic.

This thesis contributes a unified, technically detailed comparison: the formal anatomy of both protocols and their soundness reductions; an analysis of the adaptive root versus low-order assumptions; a comparison of RSA versus class-group instantiations; and an assessment of how VDFs compose into trustworthy randomness beacons and fair ordering in consensus.

> **Thesis claim:** For transparent, trust-minimized deployments, Wesolowski's construction in a class group of unknown order is the asymptotically superior VDF: constant-size proofs, two-exponentiation verification, and no trusted setup. Pietrzak's halving protocol trades larger proofs for a weaker soundness assumption and simpler prover-side engineering in RSA groups — a trade that is rational when RSA moduli are already available and assumption conservatism is prioritized. Neither construction escapes the foundational caveat that sequentiality of repeated squaring is conjectural.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The RSW time-lock puzzle

Let $N = pq$ be an RSA modulus and $x \in \mathbb{Z}_N^*$. The RSW puzzle with delay parameter $T$ is

$$y = x^{2^T} \bmod N,$$

computed by $T$ sequential squarings [4]. The puzzle *generator*, who knows the factorization, computes $e = 2^T \bmod \varphi(N)$ and evaluates $y = x^e \bmod N$ in $O(\log N)$ multiplications. Everyone else is conjectured to need $T$ sequential squarings.

Two deficiencies motivate the VDF upgrade. First, *verification is private*: checking $y \stackrel{?}{=} x^{2^T}$ either costs $T$ squarings or requires $\varphi(N)$. Second, the generator's trapdoor is a trust liability: nothing stops the generator from precomputing $y$ before publishing $x$.

### 2.2 The VDF definition

Boneh et al. [1] define a VDF as a triple $(\mathsf{Setup}, \mathsf{Eval}, \mathsf{Verify})$:

- $\mathsf{Setup}(\lambda, T) \to \mathit{pp}$: public parameters, ideally *transparent* (no secret).
- $\mathsf{Eval}(\mathit{pp}, x) \to (y, \pi)$: runs in $T$ *sequential* steps.
- $\mathsf{Verify}(\mathit{pp}, x, y, \pi) \to \{0,1\}$: runs in $\mathrm{poly}(\log T, \lambda)$.

The security properties are *correctness*, *soundness* (no efficient adversary forges a proof for a wrong $y$), and *sequentiality* (no parallel adversary beats $T - o(T)$ steps). *Uniqueness* — at most one $y$ verifies per $x$ — is what lets a VDF output serve as canonical public randomness.

### 2.3 Groups of unknown order

Both constructions need a group $G$ in which (i) the order is unknown to all parties, so that $2^T$ cannot be reduced modulo $|G|$, and (ii) exponentiation is efficient. Two candidates dominate:

1. **RSA groups.** $G = \mathbb{Z}_N^*$ (or $(\mathbb{Z}_N^*)/\{\pm 1\}$, or the quadratic residues $QR_N^+$). Fast arithmetic, but generating $N$ without anyone learning $p, q$ requires *trusted or distributed setup*.
2. **Class groups of imaginary quadratic fields.** $G = \mathrm{Cl}(\Delta)$, classes of binary quadratic forms of discriminant $\Delta$. The class number is unknown and believed hard to compute; setup is fully *transparent*. Arithmetic (composition plus reduction) is roughly an order of magnitude slower than RSA multiplication, and hashing *into* the group is non-trivial [7].

### 2.4 Hardness assumptions

- **Strong RSA assumption:** given random $N$ and $u \in \mathbb{Z}_N^*$, no efficient adversary outputs $(v, e)$ with $e > 1$ odd and $v^e = u$.
- **Adaptive root assumption** [2]: given random $u \in G$, no efficient adversary outputs a prime $\ell$ and $v \in G$ with $v^\ell = u$.
- **Low-order assumption** [3]: no efficient adversary finds $u \neq 1$ and $1 < \ell < 2^\lambda$ with $u^\ell = 1$. Adaptive root implies low order [5], so Pietrzak's assumption is strictly weaker.

The low-order assumption holds in RSA groups with $N$ odd and in $QR_N^+$ with strong primes; in class groups the landscape is subtler [7].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is an *analytic and comparative* study rather than an implementation report. Our method has four components:

1. **Formal reconstruction.** We restate both protocols in uniform notation, isolating verification equations, prover auxiliary computation, and the Fiat–Shamir transform.
2. **Reduction accounting.** For each soundness proof we trace which assumption is invoked and with what tightness loss, following the comparison in [5].
3. **Concrete cost modeling.** We count group operations for prover and verifier as functions of $T$ and $\lambda$, and report the concrete figures from the original constructions [2, 3].
4. **Instantiation comparison.** We compare RSA and class-group deployments on setup trust, arithmetic speed, assumption confidence, and sampleability [7].

All protocol claims are checked against the primary sources [1, 2, 3]; complexity comparisons are cross-checked with the survey [5] and the hardness analysis [6].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Wesolowski's proof of exponentiation

Wesolowski's insight is that the *entire* RSW evaluation can be certified by a single division-with-remainder in the exponent [2]. Let $x \in G$, $T$ the delay, and $y = x^{2^T}$ the claimed output. The verifier samples a random prime $\ell$ of about $2\lambda$ bits — non-interactively, $\ell = H_{\mathrm{primes}}(x, y)$ for a hash-to-prime function modeled as a random oracle — and the prover responds with

$$\pi = x^{\lfloor 2^T / \ell \rfloor}.$$

Writing $2^T = q\ell + r$ with $0 \le r < \ell$, the verifier checks $y \stackrel{?}{=} \pi^{\ell} \cdot x^{r}$, which holds for an honest prover since $\pi^\ell x^r = x^{2^T}$:

```python
def verify_wesolowski(x, y, pi, T, hash_to_prime):
    ell = hash_to_prime(x, y)  # random 2*lambda-bit prime
    r = pow(2, T, ell)         # r = 2^T mod ell
    return y == pow(pi, ell) * pow(x, r)
```

The proof is *one group element*; verification is *two exponentiations* ($\pi^\ell$ and $x^r$, with $\ell$ small and $r < \ell$ tiny) plus a multiplication — $O(\lambda)$ group operations, independent of $T$.

The prover's burden is heavier: besides the $T$ squarings for $y$, it must compute $\lfloor 2^T/\ell \rfloor$ applied to $x$ — roughly $2T$ multiplications via a long-division-style algorithm over the binary expansion of $2^T$. The prover *cannot* shortcut this with the group order, because the order is unknown: that is the whole point of the group choice.

> **Theorem (Wesolowski soundness, informal [2]):** Under the adaptive root assumption in $G$, no efficient adversary forges an accepting $(y', \pi)$ with $y' \neq x^{2^T}$, except with negligible probability. *Proof sketch.* From a cheating $(y', \pi)$ one extracts $w = \pi / x^{\lfloor 2^T/\ell \rfloor}$ with $w^\ell = y'/y \neq 1$; handling $\gcd(\ell, \cdot) \neq 1$ via the exceptional prime set yields an adaptive $\ell$-th root of a random group element, contradicting the assumption.

Wesolowski first constructs a *trapdoor VDF* — fast to evaluate with a secret — then instantiates it so the trapdoor is *unknown to everyone*, yielding a plain VDF with no trusted setup [2]. This "trapdoor nobody knows" framing is what makes the class-group instantiation conceptually clean: class groups never had a trapdoor to begin with.

### 4.2 Pietrzak's recursive halving protocol

Pietrzak takes a different route: prove $y = x^{2^T}$ by *recursive bisection* [3]. For $T$ a power of two, the prover sends the midpoint

$$v = x^{2^{T/2}},$$

the verifier sends a random challenge $r \in \{1, \dots, 2^\lambda\}$, and both parties fold the claim into a half-size claim:

$$x_1 = x^r \cdot v, \qquad y_1 = v^r \cdot y, \qquad \text{claim: } y_1 = x_1^{2^{T/2}}.$$

```rust
/// Fold claim y = x^{2^T} into y1 = x1^{2^{T/2}} via midpoint v = x^{2^{T/2}}.
fn halving_round(x: &Group, v: &Group, y: &Group, r: &Scalar) -> (Group, Group) {
    let x1 = x.pow(r).mul(v);  // x^r * v
    let y1 = v.pow(r).mul(y);  // v^r * y
    (x1, y1)
}
```

Recursing $\log T$ times reduces to the base case $T = 1$, checked directly as $y = x^2$. The protocol is public-coin, hence Fiat–Shamir-compiled to non-interactive. The proof consists of the $\log T$ midpoints $v_i$ — $O(\log T)$ group elements.

Soundness rests on the low-order assumption. A cheating prover whose $(x, y)$ does not satisfy the relation must, at some recursion level, produce a folded claim that is *more* false than the previous one unless it knows a low-order element; the challenge $r$ forces the error to either cancel (requiring an uncomputable root) or amplify detectably. The protocol is *statistically* sound in $QR_N^+$ with strong primes [3].

The concrete figures reported by Pietrzak are striking [3]: for $T \le 2^{40}$ and a 2048-bit RSA modulus, proofs are around **10 KB**, verification costs around **three RSA exponentiations**, and computing the proof is **8000 times faster** than solving the puzzle. The prover's extra work is sublinear: with $u = \frac{1}{2}\log_2 T$ precomputed checkpoints, proof generation costs $O(\sqrt{T})$ group operations beyond evaluation [6].

| | Wesolowski [2] | Pietrzak [3] |
|---|---|---|
| Proof size | 1 group element | $O(\log T)$ group elements (~10 KB at $T \le 2^{40}$) |
| Verification | 2 exponentiations | $O(\log T)$ mults (~3 RSA exps) |
| Prover overhead | $O(T)$ extra mults | $O(\sqrt{T})$ extra mults |
| Soundness assumption | Adaptive root | Low order (weaker) |
| Needs hash-to-prime | Yes | No |

### 4.3 RSA groups versus class groups

**RSA groups** offer the fastest arithmetic: a 2048-bit modular multiplication is microseconds on modern hardware. The price is *setup*: someone must generate $N = pq$ and forget $p, q$. Distributed RSA key generation exists but is a heavyweight multi-party protocol; worse, the trapdoor's *existence* is a systemic risk — if the factors ever leak, the VDF's delay evaporates retroactively. Pietrzak's protocol additionally wants $QR_N^+$ with *strong* primes for statistical soundness, constraining parameter generation further.

**Class groups** $\mathrm{Cl}(\Delta)$ flip the trade. Setup is *transparent*: pick a discriminant $\Delta$ (e.g., a negative prime $\equiv 3 \bmod 4$) and the group is fixed — no secrets exist, so none can leak. This is why Chia's VDF deployment and the Lenstra–Wesolowski randomness beacon work use class groups [2]. The costs are real: group operation means *composition* of binary quadratic forms plus *reduction*, roughly $5$–$10\times$ slower than RSA multiplication at comparable security. Hashing into the group was an open engineering problem until Wesolowski's prime-$a$-coefficient construction, later improved by Seres, Burcsi and Kutas with Bach's algorithm [7]. Parameter selection is less settled: 1665-bit discriminants once proposed for 128-bit security were shown to fall short, pushing deployments toward larger, slower discriminants [7].

Our assessment: for *new* deployments the class group is the right default — transparent setup dominates every other consideration for a public randomness primitive. RSA groups remain defensible where an RSA modulus with forgotten factors already exists and verification must be as fast as possible.

### 4.4 What the assumptions really buy — and don't

Both constructions inherit an unproven core: the *sequentiality* of repeated squaring. Rotem and Segev showed that, for generic-ring delay functions, speeding up repeated squaring is *equivalent* to factoring, with sharp thresholds [6] — strong evidence, but about a generic model, not a theorem about $\mathbb{Z}_N^*$. No proof rules out a parallel shortcut for the RSW puzzle; the assumption is *conjectural*, in the same epistemic class as factoring itself.

The soundness assumptions differ in strength in a precise way: adaptive root $\Rightarrow$ low order [5]. Pietrzak's protocol therefore rests on strictly less. But the low-order assumption in class groups has known subtleties, and both assumptions — indeed both groups — collapse against a quantum adversary, since Shor's algorithm computes group orders directly. Post-quantum VDFs remain experimental and pay a heavy efficiency price [5].

A final subtlety: both non-interactive versions live in the *random oracle model* via Fiat–Shamir. Wesolowski additionally needs a *hash-to-prime* random oracle. Instantiating these with concrete hash functions is standard practice but technically heuristic.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We consolidate the quantitative claims, each traceable to its source.

**Proof size and verification (concrete).** Pietrzak's headline numbers — $\approx$ 10 KB proofs, $\approx$ 3 RSA exponentiations to verify, for $T \le 2^{40}$, $|N| = 2048$ [3] — remain the standard benchmark. Wesolowski's proof is a single group element and verification is two exponentiations [2] — several times cheaper and dozens of times smaller at equal security.

**Prover overhead.** Pietrzak's prover does the $T$ squarings plus $O(\sqrt{T})$ extra work. Wesolowski's prover does the $T$ squarings plus $\approx 2T$ extra multiplications. For very large $T$, Pietrzak's sublinear overhead wins; for moderate $T$, the constants favor Wesolowski.

**Soundness error.** Both protocols achieve negligible soundness error in $\lambda$: Wesolowski's via the adaptive root reduction [2]; Pietrzak's statistically, with error $\approx 3 \cdot 2^{-\lambda}$ [3, 5]. The survey [5] additionally documents an attack on an incorrect implementation of Wesolowski's scheme — the hash-to-prime and challenge-generation details are security-critical, not mere plumbing.

> **Theorem (Pietrzak soundness, informal [3]):** Under the low-order assumption in $G$, the halving protocol proves the relation $\{(x, T, y) : y = x^{2^T}\}$ with negligible soundness error in $\lambda$, and Fiat–Shamir yields a non-interactive VDF proof. *Proof sketch.* Induction on $\log T$: a cheating prover at level $i$ either answers the challenge $r$ consistently — forcing the folded statement true with probability $1 - 2^{-\lambda}$ — or produces values whose ratio is a nontrivial low-order element, breaking the assumption.

**Sequentiality evidence.** No implementation result can *prove* sequentiality; the empirical content is negative — years of cryptanalysis have not produced a parallel shortcut for repeated squaring, and the Rotem–Segev equivalence with factoring [6] is the strongest theoretical backing available.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Conjectural sequentiality.** The edifice rests on the unproven assumption that repeated squaring admits no parallel speedup — a *hardness conjecture*, not a theorem; a breakthrough in parallel algorithms would invalidate every deployment simultaneously.
2. **Quantum vulnerability.** Shor's algorithm computes $|G|$, yielding the trapdoor exponent directly. Both Wesolowski's and Pietrzak's VDFs are broken by a cryptographically relevant quantum computer. No efficient post-quantum VDF with comparable concrete performance exists.
3. **Trusted setup for RSA.** RSA instantiations require generating a modulus whose factors are forgotten — a ceremony that is expensive to do well and catastrophic to get wrong, since factor leakage retroactively voids all delays.
4. **Class-group immaturity.** Transparent setup comes with slower arithmetic, delicate parameter selection (the 1665-bit discriminant episode [7]), and a younger cryptanalytic track record for the low-order and adaptive root assumptions in $\mathrm{Cl}(\Delta)$.
5. **Proof-size asymmetry.** Pietrzak's $O(\log T)$ proofs grow with $T$; for deployments verifying many VDF outputs, Wesolowski's constant size is operationally simpler.
6. **Heuristic non-interactivity.** Fiat–Shamir and hash-to-prime are random-oracle heuristics; the documented implementation attack on Wesolowski's scheme [5] shows these components demand exact, audited realization.
7. **Timing variance and centralization.** "T sequential steps" is not "T seconds": hardware variance means the *wall-clock* delay differs across evaluators, so protocols must parameterize conservatively, and evaluation centralizes among the best-equipped provers.

## 7. Conclusion

Wesolowski [2] and Pietrzak [3] transformed the RSW time-lock puzzle from a private-delay primitive into a *publicly verifiable* one, supplying the missing substrate for trust-minimized randomness. Their designs embody a clean trade-off: Wesolowski minimizes verifier work and proof size at the cost of a stronger assumption and linear prover overhead; Pietrzak minimizes the assumption and prover overhead at the cost of logarithmic proof size. Instantiation is a second trade-off: RSA groups are fast but demand trusted setup, while class groups are transparent but slower and less cryptanalytically mature.

For the applications that motivated VDFs — randomness beacons where *any* trapdoor is a backdoor, and fair ordering where *uniqueness* of the delay output is load-bearing — the transparent class-group instantiation of Wesolowski's construction is, in our judgment, the best current answer. The open problems are foundational: a *proof* of sequentiality for repeated squaring, a post-quantum VDF with practical concrete efficiency, and tighter, assumption-minimal soundness arguments for class groups. Until those land, VDFs remain what Boneh et al. presented them as [1]: a powerful, precisely defined, but irreducibly conjectural primitive — one whose conjectures have so far survived every attempt to break them.
