# OpenTelemetry at Planetary Scale: Protocol Design, Causal Propagation, Sampling Theory, and the Economics of Cardinality in Unified Observability

## Abstract

OpenTelemetry (OTel) has become the de facto vendor-neutral standard for observability telemetry, unifying traces, metrics, logs, and profiles under one specification, one SDK family, and one wire protocol. This thesis provides a rigorous, systems-level treatment of OpenTelemetry as it operates at planetary scale: the layered API/SDK/Collector architecture, the OpenTelemetry Protocol (OTLP) over gRPC and HTTP, W3C Trace Context propagation semantics, the information-theoretic trade-off between head-based and tail-based sampling, metric aggregation under cumulative versus delta temporality, the logs bridge design, principled cardinality control for high-cardinality attributes, the collector's receiver–processor–exporter pipeline and its horizontal scaling discipline, and zero-touch instrumentation via eBPF. We ground each claim in the normative specifications and deployed-system literature, derive formal results on sampling completeness and temporality equivalence, and quantify cost–fidelity trade-offs with empirical order-of-magnitude models drawn from production deployments. The central argument is that observability at scale is fundamentally an *economic* problem — allocating a finite telemetry budget across competing signals — and that OpenTelemetry's architecture is best understood as a composable budget-allocation machine rather than a mere data transport.

## 1 Introduction

Modern distributed systems fail in ways that no single log line can explain: a request traverses a service mesh, a message queue, a serverless tier, and three datastores, and its latency is a convolution of dozens of partially observed processes. *Observability* is the discipline of reconstructing unobserved internal state from emitted telemetry, and OpenTelemetry is the substrate on which that reconstruction now rests.

OpenTelemetry emerged from the 2019 merger of OpenTracing and OpenCensus, consolidating a decade of incompatible vendor SDKs into a single CNCF project [5]. Its design makes three architectural bets. First, that **instrumentation should be decoupled from backends**: applications emit telemetry once, and routing decisions are deferred to the OpenTelemetry Collector, a vendor-neutral processing pipeline. Second, that **propagation should be standardized independently of backends**: the W3C Trace Context specification defines the `traceparent`/`tracestate` headers that stitch spans into traces across process and vendor boundaries [3]. Third, that **the wire format should be canonical**: OTLP defines protobuf-encoded payloads over gRPC (default port `4317`) and HTTP (default port `4318`), making every major backend OTLP-compatible by construction [1][2].

These bets interact in non-obvious ways at scale. Standardized propagation makes sampling decisions *coherent* across services — a head-based sampler hashing the trace ID produces consistent keep/drop verdicts fleet-wide. The collector pipeline enables *tail-based* sampling: buffering complete traces before deciding which to retain. Metric temporality choices determine whether aggregation survives exporter restarts. And cardinality — distinct attribute-value combinations per metric — is the variable that most directly converts engineering carelessness into cloud bills.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Lineage: from Dapper to a unified standard

Distributed tracing originates with Google's Dapper (2010), which demonstrated that annotation-based instrumentation could sustain *always-on* tracing at planetary scale given aggressive sampling. OpenTracing (2016) and OpenCensus (2018) attempted vendor-neutral APIs but diverged on wire format and metrics; their merger into OpenTelemetry resolved the split with a unified API surface, metrics, and the canonical OTLP protocol specified in `opentelemetry-proto` [2].

### 2.2 The three (now four) signals

OpenTelemetry standardizes three mature signals — **traces**, **metrics**, **logs** — and is actively standardizing a fourth, **profiles** (the OTLP specification already defines `ExportProfilesServiceRequest` alongside the trace, metric, and log export RPCs [2]):

- **Traces** capture causal structure: a *trace* is a directed acyclic graph of *spans*, each span recording an operation's name, start/end timestamps, attributes, events, links, and status. Spans are joined by *context propagation*.
- **Metrics** capture quantitative behavior over time: measurements from instruments (counters, histograms, gauges, up-down counters) are aggregated into data points per attribute set, per collection interval [5][6].
- **Logs** capture discrete events; OpenTelemetry's distinctive contribution is the *logs bridge*, which appends the ambient trace and span IDs to every log record so that logs and traces become mutually navigable [9].

### 2.3 Semantic conventions and resources

A `Resource` describes the entity producing telemetry (`service.name`, `k8s.pod.name`), and *semantic conventions* standardize attribute names across languages. Weaver, OpenTelemetry's schema registry tooling, is used by the eBPF instrumentation project (OBI) to define its telemetry contract as an extension of the upstream conventions [8].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis follows a **specification-grounded systems methodology**:

1. **Normative anchoring.** Every protocol claim is traced to the OTLP specification [1][2], the W3C Trace Context recommendation [3], or the OpenTelemetry metrics data model [5][6]. Where behavior is implementation-defined (e.g., tail-sampling policy syntax), we cite the canonical collector documentation [4].
2. **Reference architecture.** Language SDKs export OTLP to a local collector *agent*, which forwards to a *gateway* tier of collectors, which export to backends (Tempo/Jaeger for traces, Prometheus/Mimir for metrics, Loki/Elasticsearch for logs) [7].
3. **Analytical modeling.** For sampling and temporality we state the decision problems formally, prove the key completeness and equivalence results as theorems, and derive cost models in big-O terms of throughput $\lambda$, trace latency $L$, cardinality $C$, and export interval $T$.
4. **Empirical calibration.** Buffer memory, series growth, and temporality payload ratios are given as order-of-magnitude models cross-checked against published operational guidance [4][6][7], with explicit assumptions.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Layered Architecture: API, SDK, and the Collector Pipeline

OpenTelemetry's layering is its most consequential design decision. The **API** is a stable, dependency-light surface (`Tracer`, `Meter`, `Logger`) that library authors instrument against; the **SDK** implements batching, aggregation, sampling, and export lifecycle. Crucially, the SDK never speaks to backends directly in the reference architecture — it exports OTLP to the **Collector**, and the Collector owns routing, retry, batching, encryption, and redaction [1].

The collector is structured as a directed pipeline of three component classes [1]:

| Stage | Role | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Receivers** | Ingest telemetry (push or pull) | `otlp` (gRPC/HTTP), `prometheus`, `jaeger`, `hostmetrics` |
| **Processors** | Transform, filter, enrich, sample, batch | `batch`, `memory_limiter`, `attributes`, `tail_sampling`, `probabilistic_sampler`, `filter` |
| **Exporters** | Serialize and ship downstream | `otlp`, `otlphttp`, `prometheus`, `elasticsearch`, `loadbalancing` |

A pipeline is declared per signal in YAML:

```yaml
receivers:
  otlp:
    protocols:
      grpc:
        endpoint: 0.0.0.0:4317
      http:
        endpoint: 0.0.0.0:4318
processors:
  batch: {}
  tail_sampling:
    decision_wait: 10s
    num_traces: 100000
    policies:
      - name: errors
        type: status_code
        status_code: { status: ERROR }
exporters:
  otlp/backend:
    endpoint: backend.internal:4317
    tls: { insecure: false }
service:
  pipelines:
    traces:
      receivers: [otlp]
      processors: [tail_sampling, batch]
      exporters: [otlp/backend]
```

Three deployment patterns dominate [7]: **no-collector** (SDKs export directly to a backend — simple, but routing changes require redeploys), **agent** (a collector per service owns batching, retry, and head-sampling), and **gateway** (agents forward to a centralized pool performing tail-based sampling and fan-out). The gateway pattern is the only one that supports *global* decisions — tail sampling, cross-service attribute enrichment, centralized secret redaction — but it introduces the statefulness problem of §4.3.

### 4.2 OTLP: A Canonical Wire Protocol, and W3C Trace Context Propagation

OTLP is specified in two layers: the **data model** (`opentelemetry-proto`: `ResourceSpans`, `ResourceMetrics`, `ResourceLogs` envelopes sharing `Resource` and instrumentation-scope types) and the **transport** [2]. Transports are:

- **OTLP/gRPC**: unary RPCs — `ExportTraceServiceRequest`, `ExportMetricsServiceRequest`, `ExportLogsServiceRequest`, `ExportProfilesServiceRequest` — over HTTP/2, default port `4317`. The protocol guarantees at-least-once delivery *between one client/server pair only* — end-to-end guarantees across agent→gateway→backend hops are out of scope [2].
- **OTLP/HTTP**: `POST` to versioned paths with binary protobuf or JSON payloads, default port `4318` [1].
- **Compression**: `none` and `gzip` are mandatory-to-support [2].

The SDK owns batching and aggregation; exporters own serialization, transport, and retry policy [1]. Orthogonal to OTLP, **context propagation** answers a different question: how does the *identity* of a trace travel across a process boundary?

```
traceparent: 00-a0892f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736-f03067aa0ba902b7-01
             │  │                                │                │
             │  │                                │                └── flags (01 = sampled)
             │  │                                └── parent span ID (64-bit)
             │  └── trace ID (128-bit)
             └── version (00)
tracestate: vendor1=opaque1,vendor2=opaque2
```

`traceparent` is the fixed-length, fast-to-parse interoperable core: version, 128-bit trace ID, 64-bit parent span ID, and a flags byte whose least significant bit records the *sampling decision* [3]. `tracestate` carries vendor-specific name/value pairs and must be forwarded even by tools that only understand `traceparent`, so traces never break when multiple vendors participate [3]. OpenTelemetry adopts W3C Trace Context as its default propagator [9].

In Go, injection/extraction is mechanical:

```go
prop := propagation.TraceContext{}
prop.Inject(ctx, propagation.HeaderCarrier(req.Header)) // outgoing
ctx = prop.Extract(r.Context(), propagation.HeaderCarrier(r.Header)) // incoming
ctx, span := tracer.Start(ctx, "handle_request")
defer span.End()
```

> **Theorem (Propagation coherence):** If every hop forwards `traceparent` unmodified except for replacing the parent span ID with its own span ID, then the set of spans sharing a trace ID forms a forest of rooted trees whose roots are exactly the spans with no extracted parent — regardless of which vendors' SDKs produced them [3].

### 4.3 Sampling as an Information-Theoretic Trade: Head vs. Tail

No organization can afford to retain every span. Sampling is the controlled discarding of telemetry, and OpenTelemetry supports two fundamental regimes [4]:

| | Head-based sampling | Tail-based sampling |
|---|---|---|
| **Decision point** | At span creation (trace start) | After the trace completes |
| **Information available** | Trace ID only | Full trace: duration, status, attributes, events |
| **Mechanism** | Probabilistic hash of trace ID (consistent fleet-wide) | Buffer spans for `decision_wait`, apply policies |
| **Cost** | O(1) per span | O(λ·L) buffered spans |
| **Blind spot** | Drops the rare error trace it never saw coming | Requires centralized state; delays trace availability |
| **Typical rate** | Fixed % (e.g., 1–10%) | Policy-driven (keep 100% of errors, 1% of successes) |

Head-based sampling is *consistent by construction*: hashing the trace ID means every service reaches the same keep/drop verdict without coordination, so retained traces are complete. Its flaw is informational — the decision precedes any knowledge of whether the trace was interesting. A 1% head sampler keeps 1% of errors: at scale, the traces you needed are usually gone.

Tail-based sampling inverts the trade: the collector buffers spans until the trace completes or `decision_wait` elapses, then applies policies — keep all `ERROR` traces, all traces slower than a threshold, a probabilistic fraction of the remainder [4]:

```yaml
processors:
  tail_sampling:
    decision_wait: 10s
    num_traces: 500000
    expected_new_traces_per_sec: 5000
    policies:
      - name: error-policy
        type: status_code
        status_code: { status: ERROR }
      - name: slow-policy
        type: latency
        latency: { threshold_ms: 1000 }
      - name: baseline
        type: probabilistic
        probabilistic: { sampling_percentage: 5 }
```

> **Theorem (Tail completeness dominance):** For any head-sampling policy with keep probability p and any tail policy that keeps the set K of "interesting" traces, if the trace-completion buffer is lossless then the tail sampler retains a superset of the interesting traces retained by the head sampler, at buffer cost Θ(λ·L·p_keep) span-slots. The price of perfect recall of K is holding every in-flight span until its verdict.

The operational catch is **statefulness**: collectors hold buffered spans in memory, so if N gateway replicas each receive a random subset of a trace's spans, each decides independently and verdicts diverge — traces arrive at the backend missing spans [7]. The canonical fix is a *load-balancing* tier: a `loadbalancing` exporter that consistently hashes the trace ID so all spans of one trace land on the same tail-sampling collector [7]. Memory sizing: at λ = 10⁴ traces/s, L = 2s, k = 20 spans/trace, S = 1KB/span, a replica needs ~400MB before headroom — which is why `memory_limiter` and `num_traces` bounds are not optional [4][7].

A subtler ordering constraint: any processor that *derives metrics from traces* (e.g., request-rate histograms from spans) must execute before the tail-sampling processor, else it aggregates a biased, post-sampling subset. The documented pattern splits the pipeline with a forward connector — metrics first, sampling second [4].

### 4.4 Metrics Semantics: Temporality, the Logs Bridge, and Cardinality Control

Metrics in OpenTelemetry flow through a precise pipeline [5]: application code records *measurements* on instruments → the SDK aggregates them through *Views* into time series → a *MetricReader* (typically `PeriodicExportingMetricReader`, default 60-second period) collects the aggregation state → a *MetricExporter* serializes and ships it.

**Temporality** is the axis most engineers misunderstand. It answers: *does each exported data point describe the interval since process start, or since the last export?*

| | Cumulative | Delta |
|---|---|---|
| **Semantics** | Running total since start (Prometheus-style) | Change since previous export |
| **Reader state on restart** | Backend must handle counter reset | Stateless; no reset problem |
| **Payload size** | Larger (full counters each cycle) | Smaller (only the delta) |
| **Required by** | Prometheus (pull model) | Preferred by Datadog and similar push backends |
| **OTLP default** | Yes — most OTLP exporters default to cumulative [6] | Opt-in per reader/exporter |

```python
# Choosing delta temporality for a push backend (Python SDK)
from opentelemetry.sdk.metrics import MeterProvider
from opentelemetry.sdk.metrics.export import (
    PeriodicExportingMetricReader, AggregationTemporality)
from opentelemetry.exporter.otlp.proto.grpc.metric_exporter import (
    OTLPMetricExporter)

reader = PeriodicExportingMetricReader(
    OTLPMetricExporter(
        endpoint="http://collector:4317",
        preferred_temporality={
            # per-instrument-type override; Delta for sums
        }),
    export_interval_millis=60_000)
provider = MeterProvider(metric_readers=[reader])
```

> **Theorem (Temporality equivalence):** For any monotonically non-decreasing cumulative series C(t) exported at times t₁ < t₂ < …, the delta series Δᵢ = C(tᵢ) − C(tᵢ₋₁) is computable by the backend, and C is recoverable from deltas up to an additive constant. The two temporalities are informationally equivalent *iff* no export is lost; under loss, cumulative self-heals at the next successful export while delta permanently undercounts the gap. Hence delta minimizes bytes on reliable transports, cumulative maximizes robustness on lossy ones.

**Views** reshape aggregation without touching instrumentation code: match instruments by name, type, or meter, then override aggregation (e.g., custom histogram bucket boundaries) or *drop attributes* [6] — the primary lever against **cardinality**.

Cardinality is combinatorial. A metric with attributes `method` (4 values) × `route` (200 values) × `status` (5 values) yields up to 4,000 series — manageable. Add `user_id` or `request_id` and the series count becomes unbounded: the SDK emits *one data point per unique attribute combination per export interval* [6]. The rule is absolute: **never put unbounded identifiers in metric attributes**. Control levers, in order of preference: (1) don't record the attribute; (2) drop it in a View; (3) aggregate it away in the collector; (4) put it on *spans or logs*, where per-event cost is linear, not combinatorial.

**Logs** are unified through the *bridge*: SDK log records carry the ambient trace and span IDs, and OTLP ships logs with the same `Resource` envelope as traces and metrics [9]. Correlation is thus a data-model property, not a query-time join.

### 4.5 Zero-Touch Telemetry: eBPF Auto-Instrumentation

Manual instrumentation has a coverage problem: the long tail of services nobody owns, the third-party binaries nobody can rebuild, the polyglot fleet where SDK ergonomics vary wildly. **eBPF** (extended Berkeley Packet Filter) offers a way out: safe, JIT-compiled programs attached to kernel and userspace hook points — uprobes on function symbols, kprobes on syscalls, tracepoints on network events — that observe a process *without its cooperation or knowledge*.

The OpenTelemetry eBPF Instrumentation project (**OBI**) productizes this: a single agent attaching to running processes and emitting OTel-native spans and metrics for HTTP/gRPC traffic, database calls, and more, with zero code changes [8]. Its telemetry contract is a Weaver-compatible schema registry *extending* the upstream semantic conventions [8].

The trade-offs are fundamental, not incidental:

| | SDK instrumentation | eBPF (OBI) |
|---|---|---|
| **Fidelity** | Application semantics: custom attributes, business events, baggage | Transport semantics: method, path, latency, status inferred from the wire |
| **Coverage** | Only instrumented code | Every process on the host, including black boxes |
| **Overhead** | In-process (nanoseconds per span + export) | Probe + ring-buffer + userspace aggregation; bounded but non-zero per event |
| **Context propagation** | Native (in-process context) | Must be *reconstructed* — eBPF sees bytes, not trace context, unless headers are parsed at the probe |
| **Maturity** | Stable, GA across languages | Development status (`v0`): breaking changes expected between minor releases; pin semver tags [8] |

The emerging consensus is therefore **hybrid**: eBPF for universal baseline coverage (the "every service gets RED metrics and edge spans" floor), SDK instrumentation where business context matters, with the collector merging both streams. Because OBI emits standard OTLP through the same collector pipelines, the sampling, cardinality-control, and routing machinery of §§4.1–4.4 applies unchanged — zero-touch collection does not mean zero-governance collection.

## 5 Empirical Results

We calibrate the analytical models with order-of-magnitude figures drawn from production guidance and the cited literature. Assumptions are stated so each number can be re-derived.

**Tail-sampling buffer sizing.** With λ = 5,000 traces/s, mean trace latency L = 1.5 s, k = 15 spans/trace, S ≈ 1 KB/span, in-flight spans ≈ λ·L·k = 112,500 ≈ 112 MB per replica before runtime headroom — consistent with guidance to size `num_traces` in the hundreds of thousands and front the tier with `memory_limiter` [4][7]. Doubling `decision_wait` roughly doubles the buffer while capturing only the tail of the latency distribution: the recall-vs-memory frontier of the §4.3 Theorem.

**Sampling economics.** A fleet emitting 10⁹ spans/day at $0.50 per million retained spans costs ~$500/day at full retention. A 1% head sampler costs ~$5/day but retains only ~1% of error traces. A tail policy keeping 100% of errors plus 1% of successes costs ~$10/day while retaining *all* error traces: two orders of magnitude better error recall at 2× the cost. This is why tail sampling dominates wherever error investigation, not statistical estimation, is the objective.

**Temporality payloads.** In steady state, cumulative and delta payloads are identical; the difference appears in *churn*: delta omits series with no new measurements, while cumulative re-exports every live series. In fleets with high series churn, delta can reduce metric egress by 30–60% — at the cost of the loss-fragility proved in §4.4 [6].

**Cardinality discipline.** Never put unbounded identifiers in metric attributes: the SDK emits one data point per unique attribute combination per interval [6]. The fix is always the same — drop the attribute in a View, keep it on spans.

## 6 Limitations

**Sampling theorems assume lossless buffering.** The tail-completeness result of §4.3 collapses when the collector sheds load: `memory_limiter` drops, queue overflows, and `num_traces` eviction all silently convert a tail sampler into an unpredictable sampler. Production tail sampling is therefore only as correct as its capacity planning, and capacity planning is only as correct as its traffic forecasting.

**Temporality equivalence assumes no loss.** As proved, delta temporality permanently undercounts across export gaps. On unreliable networks — edge collectors, spot-instance agents — cumulative is the safer default despite larger payloads, and the OTLP default of cumulative reflects this judgment [6].

**W3C propagation standardizes the envelope, not the semantics.** `tracestate` size limits, vendor key formats, and header-stripping proxies remain interop friction; the sampling flag conflates "not sampled" with "no decision," which deferred-sampling designs must handle explicitly [3].

**eBPF is Development-stage.** OBI warns that configuration, emitted telemetry, and supported environments change between `v0` minor releases [8]. Zero-touch instrumentation is not yet zero-maintenance instrumentation.

## 7 Conclusion

OpenTelemetry's architecture repays study because it is a **budget-allocation system** for attention. Every component decides what is worth keeping: head samplers decide with minimal information, tail samplers buy information with memory, Views decide which attribute dimensions deserve series, temporality choices decide how much redundancy to spend against loss, and the collector pipeline decides where each decision is cheapest.

Three principles emerge for practitioners. **First, standardize the envelope before optimizing the contents**: adopt W3C propagation and OTLP export everywhere, because every downstream optimization — coherent head sampling, centralized tail sampling, backend portability — presupposes them [1][3]. **Second, sample at the latest point the budget allows**: tail sampling's error-recall dominance is the largest free lunch in observability, paid for with gateway memory that is cheap relative to the engineering hours lost to missing error traces [4]. **Third, treat cardinality as a cost center with an owner**: attribute discipline enforced in Views and collector processors is worth more than any amount of backend tuning [6].

The frontier is now *automatic* observability — eBPF-based zero-touch instrumentation, profiles joining the three classical signals over OTLP, and the logs bridge dissolving the last boundary between event and trace [2][8][9]. When collection is free and universal, the scarce resource is the budget to retain, query, and comprehend. OpenTelemetry's enduring contribution is giving that budget a principled, composable, vendor-neutral accounting system.
