# Certified Satisfiability Solving: DRAT/LRAT Proof Logging, Formally Verified Proof Checkers, and the Engineering of Conflict-Driven Clause Learning

## Abstract

Modern conflict-driven clause learning (CDCL) SAT solvers decide the satisfiability of industrial formulas with millions of variables, yet the correctness of their answers has historically rested on blind trust in tens of thousands of lines of highly optimized, heuristic code. This article develops the theory and engineering of *certified* SAT solving: the end-to-end pipeline in which a solver emits a machine-checkable proof of unsatisfiability, an elaborator compresses and annotates that proof, and a *formally verified* checker — itself proved correct inside a theorem prover such as CakeML, Coq, or ACL2 — validates the certificate down to the logical kernel. We cover the CDCL core (implication graphs, first-UIP clause learning, VSIDS and LRB branching), the DRAT and LRAT proof formats and their underlying asymmetric-tautology and propagation-redundancy criteria, the standard DRAT-trim elaboration pipeline that turns search-heavy RAT checks into hint-guided LRAT verification, inprocessing techniques that complicate proof emission, and parallel portfolio solvers that must merge distributed clause-sharing traces into a single checkable certificate. We argue that the combination of untrusted high-performance solving with verified checking now constitutes the practical gold standard for trustworthy automated reasoning.

## 1. Introduction

The Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) is the canonical NP-complete problem, and its solvers are among the most consequential artifacts of automated reasoning: they underpin hardware verification, bounded model checking, planning, configuration, and — through SAT-based computer algebra — mathematical results such as the Boolean Pythagorean triples problem [1]. A peculiar epistemic asymmetry characterizes the field. A satisfiable answer is self-certifying: the solver exhibits an assignment, checkable in linear time. An unsatisfiable answer, by contrast, historically required *trust* — trust that the solver's search, its clause database management, its inprocessing, and its restart policy were all bug-free. That trust was misplaced more than once: subtle soundness bugs in competition-winning solvers have silently produced wrong answers on industrial benchmarks.

The response of the community was *certification*: solvers must emit a proof of unsatisfiability in a checkable clausal proof format, and the certificate must be independently validated [1]. Two developments made this practical at scale. First, the **DRAT** (Deletion Resolution Asymmetric Tautology) format gave solvers an easy-to-emit, expressive proof language whose checking cost is decoupled from solving cost [2]. Second, the **LRAT** (Linear RAT) format augmented DRAT with *hints* — explicit pointers to the unit clauses needed for each inference — so that checking becomes a guided, near-linear procedure amenable to *formal verification* [8]. The modern pipeline is therefore: an unverified, aggressively optimized solver emits DRAT; an unverified but efficient elaborator (DRAT-trim) converts and compresses it into LRAT; and a *verified* checker, with its soundness proved in CakeML, Coq, or ACL2, performs the final validation [3][6]. The trusted computing base collapses to the theorem prover's kernel.

This article traces that full stack: the CDCL engine and its branching heuristics (§2), the proof-theoretic foundations of DRAT/LRAT and propagation redundancy (§3), the elaboration and verified-checking pipeline (§4), inprocessing and parallelism as certification challenges (§5), and an empirical/theoretical evaluation with limitations and conclusions (§6–§8).

## 2. Background

### CDCL: the modern SAT engine

Conflict-driven clause learning refines the Davis–Putnam–Logemann–Loveland (DPLL) backtracking search with *learning from failure*. A CDCL solver maintains a partial assignment under a trail of decision literals and propagated literals. Unit propagation (Boolean constraint propagation, BCP) applies the unit rule exhaustively using the two-watched-literal scheme. When propagation falsifies a clause — a *conflict* — the solver analyzes the *implication graph*: a directed acyclic graph whose vertices are assigned literals and whose edges record the antecedent clauses that forced each propagation [6].

**Conflict analysis** resolves the conflict clause against antecedent clauses in reverse chronological order until the *first unique implication point* (first-UIP) is reached: the first dominator of the conflict node on the current decision level. The resulting *asserting clause* is added to the learnt database, and the solver backjumps to the assertion level rather than chronological backtracking. The 1-UIP scheme is both empirically dominant and theoretically justified: learnt clauses derived this way correspond to resolution derivations, placing CDCL (with restarts) within the power of general resolution, while practical extensions — extended resolution steps via inprocessing — reach far beyond it [2][4].

### Branching heuristics: VSIDS, CHB, LRB

The *decision heuristic* selects the next unassigned variable. **VSIDS** (Variable State Independent Decaying Sum), originating in Chaff, maintains an activity score per variable: each variable appearing in a learnt clause is additively bumped, and periodically all scores are multiplicatively decayed [6]. Liang et al. gave VSIDS its first rigorous mathematical characterization, showing that its rankings correlate strongly with *exponentially smoothed temporal graph centrality* over the temporal variable incidence graph of learnt clauses: VSIDS provably selects high-centrality *bridge variables* connecting communities of the formula's community structure [6].

**LRB** (Learning Rate Branching) reframes branching as an online optimization problem: maximize the *learning rate*, the propensity of a variable to participate in learnt-clause generation. Liang, Ganesh, Poupart, and Czarnecki model variable selection as a multi-armed bandit and apply *exponential recency-weighted average* to each variable's conflict-participation history, augmented with reason-side rate and locality corrections. On 1975 application and hard combinatorial instances from SAT competitions 2009–2014, LRB outperformed both VSIDS and the conflict-history-based CHB heuristic [7]. Modern solvers (Kissat, CaDiCaL, CryptoMiniSat) typically hybridize these ideas, e.g., alternating phases or using LRB for the first phase of search.

| Heuristic | Update rule | Key idea | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| VSIDS | additive bump + multiplicative decay | recency-weighted conflict participation | robust, simple, temporal focus [6] |
| CHB | conflict-history EMA per variable | reward variables in recent conflicts | cheap, effective on crafted instances |
| LRB | exponential recency-weighted average of learning rate | maximize clause-generation propensity as bandit problem | best on application tracks, SAT 2016 [7] |

### Restarts, inprocessing, and the solver as proof producer

CDCL solvers restart search periodically (Luby, geometric, or machine-learned restart policies [5]) while retaining learnt clauses, and they apply *inprocessing* — simplification during search: variable elimination, subsumption, blocked-clause elimination, probing, and vivification [4]. Inprocessing is the certification headache of §5: every simplification must be reflected in the emitted proof, or the proof checker will reject a sound derivation.

## 3. Methodology

### Proof-theoretic core: RAT, DRAT, PR

A clausal proof is a sequence of clause additions and deletions starting from the input formula *F*, ending with the empty clause ⊥. The classical criterion is *RUP* (reverse unit propagation): clause *C* is RUP w.r.t. *F* if unit-propagating *F ∧ ¬C* derives conflict, i.e., *F ⊨ C* is witnessed by propagation alone. RUP captures only logical consequence via unit propagation; solvers routinely learn clauses that are *not* RUP consequences — e.g., from blocked-clause elimination or symmetry reasoning — motivating *interference-based* criteria.

> **Theorem (RAT criterion).** A clause *C* has the *resolution asymmetric tautology* (RAT) property w.r.t. formula *F* on pivot literal *ℓ ∈ C* if, for every clause *D ∈ F* containing *¬ℓ*, the resolvent *(C \ {ℓ}) ∪ (D \ {¬ℓ})* is an asymmetric tautology (implied by *F* via unit propagation). Adding a RAT clause preserves satisfiability of *F* [2].

**DRAT** combines RAT clause additions with *deletion* steps: a line `d C` removes clause *C* from the active set. Deletions are satisfiability-monotone (removing clauses can only make a formula *more* satisfiable), so they need no justification, yet they are essential for performance: they bound the active clause set and enable *backward checking*, where the proof is validated in reverse from the empty clause, marking only the clauses actually needed — typically a small *core* of the full trace [5]. RAT generalizes RUP, blocked clauses, and extended resolution (with new variables), making DRAT the *lingua franca* of SAT certification: proof logging has been mandatory in the SAT Competition since 2013, and formally verified checking since 2017 [1].

**Propagation redundancy (PR)**, introduced by Heule, Kiesl, and Biere, pushes expressiveness further: a clause *C* is PR w.r.t. *F* if there exists a *witness* partial assignment *ω* such that *F* restricted by *ω* unit-propagates to subsume *C*'s obligations. PR yields *polynomial-size* refutations of the pigeonhole principle without new variables — exponentially shorter than any RAT-only proof [2] — and its linear-hint variant **LPR** extends LRAT with witness annotations so verified checkers can validate PR steps efficiently [3].

### From DRAT to LRAT: elaboration

Checking a RAT addition naively requires *proof search*: the checker must rediscover, by unit propagation, the chain of clauses establishing the asymmetric-tautology property for every resolvent. This search is exactly what makes verified RAT checking slow and its correctness proof painful [8]. **LRAT** eliminates the search by attaching *hints* to each addition: the IDs of the unit clauses that witness each propagation step, in order. The standard trusted pipeline is therefore a division of labor [8]:

1. **Emit (untrusted, fast):** the solver logs DRAT — easy to emit, since the solver already knows each learnt clause but not necessarily a minimal hint set.
2. **Elaborate (untrusted, efficient):** DRAT-trim replays the proof, performs backward checking, extracts an unsatisfiable core, and *computes hints*, emitting LRAT [1][5].
3. **Verify (trusted, proved correct):** a verified LRAT checker — in CakeML [3], Coq [8], or ACL2 — follows the hints deterministically and accepts or rejects.

Because the verified checker re-verifies everything the elaborator claims, the elaborator need not be trusted: any bug in DRAT-trim produces an LRAT proof the verified checker rejects, failing *safe* (rejecting a valid proof is a completeness failure, never a soundness hole).

> **Theorem (LRAT soundness, verified).** The CakeML-verified LRAT checker `cake_lpr` is proved correct in higher-order logic: if it accepts a proof for formula *F*, then *F* is unsatisfiable. The proof connects the checker's operational semantics to a deep embedding of propositional logic, with the machine-code verification extending the guarantee to the executed binary [3].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The elaboration pipeline in detail

DRAT-trim's backward checking is the workhorse of certification. Given formula *F* and proof *P* ending in ⊥, it processes *P* in reverse. The empty clause is marked; for each marked addition, the checker verifies the RAT/RUP property against currently active clauses and marks every clause participating in the verification; deletions deactivate clauses. Unmarked clauses are never validated. The marked subset — the *core* — is both an optimized proof and, restricted to *F*, an *unsatisfiable core* [5]. Heule et al. extended this machinery to *clausal proof optimization*: multiple cores extractable from one proof, with graph-trimming applications that reduced symmetry-breaking proofs substantially [5].

The verified end of the pipeline has been realized in three major theorem provers:

- **Coq** — Cruz-Filipe et al.'s verified LRAT checker extracts an efficient OCaml checker from a Coq soundness proof, demonstrating that hint-guided checking is both fast and trustworthy [8].
- **ACL2** — Wetzler, Heule, and Hunt verified a checker for extended-resolution-style proofs, used to certify the 200 TB Boolean Pythagorean triples proof [1].
- **CakeML** — Tan, Heule, and Myreen's `cake_lpr` goes furthest: the LRAT/LPR checker is verified *down to machine code*, and supports *compositional* UNSAT checking, where cube-and-conquer subproofs (each ending in the negation of a failed cube's assumptions) are stitched into one verified verdict [3].

The CakeML compositional approach deserves emphasis. In cube-and-conquer, *F* is partitioned by cubes *G₁…Gₙ*; incremental solvers emit DRAT proofs for each *F ∧ Gᵢ* ending in *¬Hᵢ* (the negation of the failed assumptions actually used). A top-level proof then shows the cubes partition the space. Tan et al. modified DRAT-trim to convert these *partial* proofs into LRAT and check the composition in verified CakeML code — the first verified checker handling realistic divide-and-conquer solving [3].

### 4.2 Branching heuristics as certified-proof influencers

Branching does not affect soundness — any complete CDCL search emits a valid proof — but it dramatically affects *proof size and shape*. VSIDS's temporal focus means learnt clauses cluster around bridge variables, producing proofs whose core concentrates on community boundaries [6]. LRB's bandit formulation maximizes learning rate, which empirically correlates with shorter proofs on application instances: more conflicts per decision means more learning per unit of search, and each learnt clause is a proof step [7]. A subtle consequence: heuristic choice changes the *hint structure* of the LRAT proof, and hence verified-checking time. Proofs from LRB-driven solvers tend to have shorter, denser hint chains — cheaper to check per step — an effect visible in the SAT Competition's certified tracks.

```python
# Sketch: verified-style LRAT addition check (hint-guided, no search)
def check_lrat_addition(active_clauses, clause, hints):
    # hints: ordered list of clause IDs witnessing unit propagations
    units = set()
    for hid in hints:
        # each hinted clause must become unit under current assignment
        c = active_clauses[hid]
        unassigned = [l for l in c if l not in units_neg(units)]
        assert len(unassigned) == 1, "hint must identify a unit clause"
        units.add(unassigned[0])
    # after applying all hints, clause must be RUP: negating it + units -> conflict
    assert propagates_to_conflict(active_clauses, negate(clause), units)
    return True
```

The point of the sketch: *no search* — the checker follows hints linearly, which is precisely what makes the algorithm simple enough to verify in a theorem prover [3][8].

### 4.3 Inprocessing and proof logging

Inprocessing rules — variable elimination (Davis–Putnam resolution), subsumption, self-subsuming resolution, blocked-clause elimination, equivalent-literal substitution — are *satisfiability-preserving* transformations applied during search [4]. Each must be proof-logged: variable elimination of *x* adds all resolvents on *x* (each RAT-checkable) and deletes the parent clauses; blocked-clause elimination adds clauses with the *blocked* property (a special case of RAT); equivalent-literal substitution is logged via RAT steps on the binary equivalence clauses.

The engineering tension is real: inprocessing can consume 30–50% of solver time and its proof steps can dominate proof size, yet *disabling* inprocessing for certifiability cripples performance. Modern solvers (CaDiCaL, Kissat) therefore implement *proof-aware inprocessing*: every simplifier is co-designed with its DRAT emission, and DRAT-trim's backward checking later discards the simplification steps irrelevant to the final core [5].

### 4.4 Parallel portfolio solving and distributed proofs

Portfolio solvers run diverse CDCL configurations (different heuristics, seeds, restart policies) in parallel with *clause sharing*: learnt clauses migrate between workers, accelerating all of them. Certifying this is nontrivial — the "proof" is a distributed trace of clause exchanges [8]. Heule et al.'s approach for distributed clause-sharing solvers exploits LRAT hints to reconstruct *dependencies*: each worker logs its local DRAT trace; a merger uses hint information to order clause additions into a single linear proof, resolving the fundamental problem that a clause used by worker *B* may have been learnt by worker *A* at a "later" local time [8]. The merged DRAT proof is then elaborated and verified exactly as in the sequential pipeline, and the approach scales to the clause-sharing architectures (HordeSAT-style, Mallob) now dominant in parallel SAT.

```tla
------------------------------- MODULE PortfolioCert -------------------------------
\* Abstract spec: distributed clause-sharing proof must linearize to valid LRAT
EXTENDS Naturals, Sequences
CONSTANTS Workers, Formula
VARIABLES localProofs, mergedProof, activeClauses
Init == /\ localProofs \in [Workers -> Seq(ClauseOp)]
        /\ mergedProof = << >>
        /\ activeClauses = Formula
AddClause(w, c, deps) ==
    \* clause c from worker w may be appended only after all its hint-deps are active
    /\ \A d \in deps : d \in activeClauses
    /\ mergedProof' = Append(mergedProof, <<w, c, deps>>)
    /\ activeClauses' = activeClauses \cup {c}
    /\ UNCHANGED localProofs
THEOREM Soundness == [] (mergedProofValid => Unsatisfiable(Formula))
=====================================================================================
```

## 5. Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

The certified pipeline has been validated at extraordinary scale. The Boolean Pythagorean triples proof — 200 TB of DRAT, checked by the ACL2-verified checker — remains the largest formal proof object ever constructed [1]. The Erdős discrepancy proof (13 GB DRAT, verified via DRAT-trim) demonstrated that backward checking reduces real proofs to cores an order of magnitude smaller [1]. On the heuristic side, the LRB evaluation over 1975 competition instances showed consistent gains over VSIDS/CHB on application tracks, and CryptoMiniSat+LRB solved strictly more instances than its VSIDS variant [7]. For proof systems, the PR-based pigeonhole solver produces DRAT proofs of length ≈ 3.3n³, the shortest known, versus the exponential lower bound for RAT without new variables [2] — a concrete, measured separation between proof systems that solvers actually emit.

Verified-checker performance is the remaining quantitative question. The CakeML `cake_lpr` checker verifies LRAT at speeds within a small constant factor of unverified C checkers, and the Coq-extracted checker of Cruz-Filipe et al. handles competition-scale proofs [3][8]. The dominant cost in practice is *elaboration* (DRAT→LRAT in DRAT-trim), not verified checking — an acceptable trade since elaboration is untrusted and can be optimized freely.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Completeness failures are silent.** A verified checker rejects invalid proofs but cannot diagnose *why* a solver's DRAT is malformed; debugging proof-logging bugs in inprocessing remains laborious.
2. **Proof size.** DRAT proofs routinely exceed the input formula by 10–1000×; the 200 TB Pythagorean proof strained storage and I/O [1]. PR/LPR mitigate this asymptotically [2][3], but solver support for emitting PR steps is still nascent.
3. **Elaboration trust gap.** DRAT-trim is unverified; a bug can only cause rejection (safe), but competition pipelines add latency and a large unverified component to the *workflow*, if not the trust base.
4. **Parallel proof merging** assumes cooperative logging; adversarial or crashed workers can produce unmergeable traces, and current mergers handle clause-sharing but not full search-space partitioning with dynamic load balancing [8].
5. **Satisfiable answers** remain uncertified beyond the checkable assignment — no proof-theoretic machinery is needed, but also none exists for *why* a solver concluded SAT quickly.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Certified SAT solving has converged on a clean architectural separation: *untrusted performance* (the CDCL solver with VSIDS/LRB branching, restarts, inprocessing, and portfolio parallelism) behind a *verified interface* (DRAT emission → DRAT-trim elaboration → LRAT/LPR checking proved correct in CakeML, Coq, or ACL2). The proof-theoretic advances — RAT, DRAT, and propagation redundancy — give the interface its expressiveness, with exponential separations (PR vs. RAT on pigeonhole [2]) showing the format is not merely bureaucratic but mathematically substantive. The heuristic advances — VSIDS's graph-centrality characterization [6] and LRB's bandit formulation [7] — determine the shape and size of the certificates produced. And the verified checkers [3][8] collapse the trusted base to a theorem-prover kernel, making the solver's 100,000 lines of heuristics *irrelevant to soundness*. For any application where a wrong UNSAT answer has consequences — hardware verification, mathematical proof, safety-critical configuration — this pipeline is now the standard of evidence. The open frontier is shrinking the certificates themselves: emitting PR natively, verifying elaboration, and certifying the full parallel portfolio without a sequential bottleneck.
