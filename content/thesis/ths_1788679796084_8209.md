# From Action Potentials to Intent: Signal Acquisition, Population Decoding, and Closed-Loop Adaptation in Intracortical Brain–Computer Interfaces

## Abstract

Intracortical brain–computer interfaces (iBCIs) translate the spiking activity of cortical neuronal populations directly into control signals for prosthetic effectors, restoring communication and movement to individuals with tetraplegia. This thesis develops the complete engineering stack of a modern iBCI: chronic signal acquisition with Utah microelectrode arrays and electrocorticographic grids, spike sorting and robust feature extraction under nonstationary conditions, population decoding via Kalman filters, point-process filters, and optimal linear estimators, and closed-loop decoder adaptation that co-optimizes the machine with the learning user. It then extends the architecture to bidirectional interfaces in which intracortical microstimulation delivers artificial somatosensory feedback, analyzes information transfer rate and clinical safety evidence from the BrainGate trials, and confronts the dominant failure modes — biological encapsulation, material degradation, and recording instability — that bound long-term performance. The synthesis argues that the future of clinical iBCIs lies not in any single decoder, but in adaptive, fully implanted, bidirectional systems whose signal processing is designed explicitly for the closed loop.

## 1. Introduction

In tetraplegia from cervical spinal cord injury or advanced ALS, motor cortex remains intact while downstream pathways are destroyed. An intracortical BCI records directly from this source of motor intent, bypassing the spinal cord to drive a cursor, robotic limb, or functional electrical stimulation [1]. Unlike scalp EEG, intracortical arrays resolve individual action potentials at kilohertz rates — the highest-bandwidth control signals in clinical neuroengineering.

The BrainGate pilot trials (FDA investigational device exemption, 2004) have shown that participants with longstanding tetraplegia can operate cursors and robotic arms from motor cortical spikes recorded a thousand days after implantation [1], with a safety profile across 14 participants and 12,000+ implant-days comparable to approved implanted neurological devices [2]. Yet performance lags the native limb, calibration is burdensome, and the interface degrades over years. Closing these gaps requires treating the BCI as a *closed-loop control system* in which brain, decoder, and effector co-adapt [3].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the historical and physiological foundations. Section 3 formalizes the standard methodology: acquisition, spike sorting, feature extraction, decoding, and adaptation. Section 4 develops five deep topics — the Utah array and its alternatives, spike sorting under drift, the mathematics of population decoding, closed-loop decoder adaptation, and bidirectional stimulation. Section 5 presents empirical results and the formal results that justify the methods. Section 6 catalogs limitations and open problems. Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

Neural decoding descends from Fetz (1969), who showed monkeys could volitionally modulate single motor-cortical neurons, and Georgopoulos et al. (1986), whose *population vector* — preferred directions weighted by firing rates — predicts reach direction and founds the population-coding paradigm. The first closed-loop BCIs (late 1990s–2000s) then revealed that the brain adapts to the decoder, forming a stable neural map when the mapping is held constant [3].

On hardware, the Utah array — a 10 × 10 bed of silicon needles from Normann's group at the University of Utah, commercialized by Blackrock Microsystems — became the clinical workhorse: 100 shanks, 1.0–1.5 mm long on a 400 µm pitch, sampling layer IV/V at 30 kHz per channel. The first BrainGate human implants (Hochberg et al., 2006) proved motor cortical spikes persist years after paralysis and can drive a 2D cursor [1]. Electrocorticography (ECoG) — subdural grids recording cortical-surface high-gamma (70–200 Hz) power — trades single-neuron resolution for lower surgical risk and typically greater chronic stability.

Three signal classes dominate: **sorted single-unit activity (SUA)**, **threshold-crossing multiunit activity (MUA)**, and **local field potentials (LFP)**. SUA gives the cleanest tuning but is fragile across days; threshold MUA (every event at −4.5× RMS) is the robust BrainGate canonical feature [1]; spike-band power (Hilbert envelope of 300–1000 Hz) is the most drift-resilient chronic alternative.

## 3. Methodology

A deployed iBCI executes a stereotyped pipeline, formalized here and examined in depth in Section 4.

1. **Acquisition.** 96 channels at 30 kHz, band-passed (250–7500 Hz spikes; 0.1–250 Hz LFP), common-average referenced. Impedances near 1 kHz track health; >~1 MΩ flags dead or encapsulated sites.
2. **Spike detection and sorting.** Threshold crossings are aligned and clustered in waveform feature space (typically PCA); units are validated by refractory violations (<1–2% of intervals under ~1.5 ms) and metrics like isolation distance and L-ratio.
3. **Feature construction.** Spikes binned at 10–50 ms form rate vectors **y**_t ∈ ℝ^C, optionally concatenated with 100–200 ms of history.
4. **Decoding.** Maps features to kinematic state **x**_t (position, velocity, sometimes acceleration): *Kalman filter* (linear-Gaussian state-space recursion), *optimal linear estimator* (Wiener filter on lagged rates), *point-process filters* (recursive Bayesian updates from spike conditional intensities).
5. **Closed-loop adaptation.** Decoder parameters are re-estimated during use — *closed-loop decoder adaptation* (CLDA) — from error signals inferred via task goals or extracted from the brain [3].
6. **Bidirectional extension.** A second array in somatosensory cortex delivers ICMS — charge-balanced biphasic pulse trains — evoking artificial tactile percepts [5].
7. **Evaluation.** Target acquisition rate, time-to-target, and Fitts-law information transfer rate (bits/s); safety via adverse-event surveillance [2].

> **Theorem:** *(Kalman optimality.)* For a linear state-space model with Gaussian process and observation noise, the Kalman filter's recursive estimate **x̂**_{t|t} is the minimum-mean-squared-error estimator of the state given all observations to time *t*, with error covariance evolving deterministically, independent of the data.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Signal Acquisition and the Utah Microelectrode Array

The Utah array trades invasiveness for yield: 100 silicon shanks on a 4 × 4 mm footprint penetrate ~1.5 mm to layer V, each parylene-C insulated except a Pt or SIROF tip that defines the recording site (SIROF also permits safe stimulation [5]). Ninety-six shanks are wire-bonded via 25 µm gold-alloy wires to a percutaneous pedestal — the familiar 96-channel stream.

The chain is unforgiving: ±1 mV at the tip is amplified and band-passed (250–7500 Hz), a hardware refractory period (~0.5 ms) suppresses double-counting, thresholds sit at −4.5× RMS per channel (negative crossings only), and common-average referencing removes correlated artifact.

Two pressures are reshaping acquisition: the percutaneous connector — the dominant source of device-related adverse events — motivates fully implanted wireless systems (BrainGate wireless transmitter; high-channel-count commercial efforts such as Neuralink's N1), and the data bottleneck (hundreds of GB/day at 96 × 30 kHz) demands on-implant spike detection and feature compression.

| Array technology | Channel count | Resolution | Typical chronic yield |
|---|---|---|---|
| Utah microelectrode array | 96 (10×10 − 4) | Single units | Declines over months–years [6][7] |
| Floating microwire arrays (FMA) | 32–128 | Single/multiunit | Comparable, insertion-dependent |
| ECoG surface grids | 32–256 | High-gamma field | Generally more stable, coarser |
| Next-gen (threads, CMOS probes) | 10³–10⁴ | Single units | Under clinical evaluation |

### 4.2 Spike Sorting and Single-Unit Isolation

Spike sorting assigns each waveform to a putative neuron via the classical pipeline — detect, align, extract features (PCA/wavelets), cluster (k-means, mixtures, Kilosort-style template matching), curate — but chronic iBCIs exposed its central weakness: *the units do not stay put*. Micromotion, gliosis, and material degradation reshape waveforms over days; macaque studies find only a subset of Utah-array units stable over days to weeks even when waveform and interspike-interval statistics are jointly tracked.

Hence the pragmatic turn to unsorted features. Threshold-crossing MUA discards identity for robustness: the decoder never commits to which neuron fired, so drift degrades performance gracefully. Spike-band power goes further, replacing discrete events with a continuous energy envelope that survives unit turnover entirely. The trade-off: SUA maximizes information per channel when isolation holds; MUA and spike-band power maximize information *over time*.

```python
# Canonical chronic-iBCI feature pipeline (per channel, per 20 ms bin)
import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import butter, filtfilt, hilbert

def extract_features(raw, fs=30000, bin_ms=20):
    b, a = butter(4, [250/(fs/2), 7500/(fs/2)], btype='band')
    filt = filtfilt(b, a, raw)
    rms = np.sqrt(np.mean(filt**2))
    spikes = (filt < -4.5 * rms)          # threshold crossings (BrainGate canonical)
    # spike-band power: Hilbert envelope of 300-1000 Hz
    b2, a2 = butter(4, [300/(fs/2), 1000/(fs/2)], btype='band')
    sbp = np.abs(hilbert(filtfilt(b2, a2, raw)))
    n = int(fs * bin_ms / 1000)
    mua_rate = spikes[:len(spikes)//n*n].reshape(-1, n).sum(axis=1)
    sbp_mean = sbp[:len(sbp)//n*n].reshape(-1, n).mean(axis=1)
    return mua_rate, sbp_mean
```

> **Theorem:** *(Sorting-free sufficiency.)* When single-unit identity is unidentifiable due to drift, the binned threshold-crossing count is a sufficient statistic for the pooled conditional intensity of the local population; decoders linear in these counts lose no *population-rate* information relative to perfect sorting, at the cost of unit-specific tuning structure.

### 4.3 Population Decoding: Kalman Filters, Point-Process Filters, and Optimal Linear Estimators

Population decoding inverts the encoding map: from population rates **y**_t estimate kinematic state **x**_t. The Kalman formulation posits a linear-Gaussian state-space model

**x**_t = **A** **x**_{t−1} + **w**_t,  **y**_t = **H** **x**_t + **q**_t,

where **A** captures movement smoothness, **H** is the tuning matrix fit by least squares, and **w**_t, **q**_t are Gaussian noise. The filter alternates prediction (**x̂**_{t|t−1} = **A** **x̂**_{t−1|t−1}) and update steps driven by the Kalman gain, with an explicit uncertainty (covariance) downstream controllers can exploit. Deployments typically use 50–100 ms bins and a ~100 ms neural-to-kinematic lag [1][5].

The *optimal linear estimator* (Wiener filter) regresses kinematics directly onto lagged rates — simpler, but lacking the temporal prior that smooths Kalman trajectories. *Point-process filters* instead model each channel as a point process with conditional intensity λ_c(t|**x**_t, history) and update exactly at spike times; their timing advantage over a well-tuned Kalman filter has been modest in clinical data at higher computational cost.

The landmark advance was the *recalibrated feedback intention-trained Kalman filter* (ReFIT-KF) [4]. Decoders trained on open-loop data suffer systematic mismatch, because neural activity during closed-loop control differs from training activity. ReFIT-KF makes two corrections: *intention estimation* — recalibration velocities are rotated toward the known target, encoding that the subject always intends to move at the goal — and joint decoding of *position and velocity*. In monkeys, ReFIT-KF doubled standard-Kalman performance, reaching 75–85% of native arm speed with far less overshoot [4]: *control-aware* design beats offline reconstruction.

```python
# Discrete Kalman filter: core recursion used in iBCI velocity decoders
def kalman_update(x, P, y, A, H, W, Q):
    # predict
    x_pred = A @ x
    P_pred = A @ P @ A.T + W
    # update
    S = H @ P_pred @ H.T + Q            # innovation covariance
    K = P_pred @ H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)  # Kalman gain
    x_new = x_pred + K @ (y - H @ x_pred)
    P_new = (np.eye(len(x)) - K @ H) @ P_pred
    return x_new, P_new
```

### 4.4 Closed-Loop Decoder Adaptation

Even the best decoder decays: tuning drifts, units drop out, and the user's internal model evolves. *Closed-loop decoder adaptation* (CLDA) updates parameters *during use*, using error signals inferred without ground-truth movement [3]. The canonical signal is the task goal: the cursor-to-target vector defines presumed intended velocity, and its discrepancy with decoder output drives recursive-least-squares or gradient updates on the observation matrix **H**.

ReFIT-KF is CLDA's most successful instantiation: its recalibration *is* a closed-loop adaptation step, refitting **H** to intention-estimated data collected under the current decoder [4]. More aggressive schemes adapt sample-by-sample, extracting error signals from the brain itself — error-related potentials or predicted-vs-observed sensory discrepancies — so user learning and machine learning converge to a joint optimum [3].

The stakes are clinical: daily recalibration is a major usability burden. A BCI maintaining performance across days with minimal recalibration — via CLDA plus drift-robust features like spike-band power — crosses from laboratory demonstration to assistive device.

> **Theorem:** *(CLDA convergence sketch.)* If intended kinematics are identifiable from task goals and the decoder is linear in parameters with persistently exciting features, recursive-least-squares CLDA drives parameter error to a neighborhood of zero whose radius scales with intention-estimation noise — which is why better intention models (as in ReFIT) directly improve adapted performance.

### 4.5 Bidirectional Interfaces and Intracortical Microstimulation

Vision alone is a poor substitute for touch: deafferented patients show markedly degraded dexterity. Bidirectional BCIs close the sensory loop with *intracortical microstimulation* (ICMS) — charge-balanced, cathodal-first biphasic pulse trains (20–300 Hz, 1–100 µA, 200 µs phases) evoking punctate tactile percepts in the somatotopic map [5].

Stimulation saturates recording amplifiers, so systems blank or interpolate artifacts, interleave stimulation and recording, or filter artifacts while decoding continuously — demonstrated across 119 sessions over 13 months in NHPs [5]. There, a Utah array in Brodmann's area 1 delivered tactile feedback about a virtual hand while parietal arrays (AIP, PRR, area 5d) drove a Kalman decoder, and ICMS feedback outperformed auditory sensory substitution [5]. Patterned ICMS can convey texture, contact force, and location nearly as intuitively as mechanical touch.

Human translation is underway: microstimulation through iridium-oxide Utah arrays in human somatosensory cortex shows stable or improving detection thresholds over time [7]. Remaining barriers: spatial resolution (current spread over hundreds of microns, far coarser than natural touch), perceptual stability, and stimulation-accelerated tip erosion — observed on explants without measurable recording degradation in the studied cohort [7].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

The BrainGate trials report performance and safety with unusual candor, giving the field a concrete empirical record.

**Longevity of control.** At 1000 days post-implant, a BrainGate participant yielded spikes on 41 of 96 electrodes; a daily-retrained Kalman velocity decoder plus linear discriminant click classifier achieved 91.3% correct target acquisition [1] — proof that motor cortical signals persist for years after paralysis.

**Decoder design beats data volume.** ReFIT-KF held population and implant constant, changed only the algorithm, and doubled speed and accuracy — 75–85% of native arm speed, with the decisive gain being the ability to *stop* on target [4].

**Clinical safety.** Across 14 participants (mean 872 days; 12,203 implant-days): 68 device-related adverse events, 6 serious — mostly connector-site skin irritation — with no brain infections, no removals, and no permanently increased disability [2], a profile comparable to deep brain stimulation.

**Bidirectional feasibility.** Simultaneous PPC decoding and S1 microstimulation across 119 sessions over 13 months, with ICMS tactile cues outperforming sensory substitution [5].

**The degradation ledger.** Six explanted human arrays (987 vs. 182 days) show time-dependent parylene-C cracking, Pt/IrOx tip erosion, and denser fibrotic encapsulation correlating with declining peak-to-peak voltages [7]. In rats, single-unit yield falls from ~53% of electrodes at week 1 to ~13% at week 24, with abiotic failures (wire breakage, insulation degradation) compounding biological ones [6]; fewer than half of electrodes remain active after 4 weeks and ~18% after a year [8]. Meningeal encapsulation can eject the device entirely — the leading chronic failure mode in NHPs at ~30% [7].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Nonstationarity.** Units drift, appear, and vanish; no once-trained decoder lasts. CLDA and robust features mitigate but do not solve it, and misestimated error signals can destabilize continuous adaptation [3].
2. **Single-unit isolation depreciates.** The migration from SUA to MUA to spike-band power concedes that chronic identity tracking may be unwinnable with rigid arrays [6].
3. **Bidirectionality is coarse.** ICMS recruits tissue indiscriminately; naturalistic touch needs higher-density or patterned stimulation with validated perceptual stability.
4. **Bandwidth and power.** Wireless implants must stream or compress thousands of channels within thermal/power budgets; on-chip spike detection remains clinically immature.
5. **Biocompatibility is unsolved.** Gliosis, encapsulation, and material degradation form a coupled cascade; biomimetic coatings (e.g., L1CAM) help only transiently [6], and flexible probes lack decades-long data.
6. **Evaluation gaps.** Bit rates are reported on stereotyped center-out tasks that flatter decoders; real-world self-paced multi-effector use is rarely quantified.
7. **Access and ethics.** Invasive, expensive, and limited to trial participants; equitable access, device abandonment, cognitive liberty, and agency over co-adaptive systems remain largely unaddressed.

## 7. Conclusion

The intracortical BCI has matured from curiosity to clinical technology with multi-year performance and an acceptable safety profile [1][2], driven by compounding engineering: better arrays, spike-sorting pragmatism, control-aware decoders [4], adaptation in the loop [3], and first bidirectional systems [5]. The binding constraints are now biological — foreign-body response, signal drift, coarse artificial sensation — rather than algorithmic. The path forward is integrative: decades-tolerated interfaces, drift-robust features with continuously adapting decoders, high-resolution bidirectional stimulation, and fully implanted electronics — a device a paralyzed person uses daily, without recalibration, to act and to feel. The action potential is only the beginning; the field's thesis is that *intent*, properly decoded and properly answered, can be restored.
