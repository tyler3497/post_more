# Differential Privacy: Composition Theorems, Canonical Mechanisms, and the Engineering of Private Machine Learning

## Abstract
Differential privacy (DP) is the dominant mathematical framework for quantifying privacy loss in data analysis, replacing heuristic anonymization with a worst-case, composable guarantee. This survey traces the field from its foundational definition through the canonical noise-addition mechanisms, the composition theorems that make repeated analysis possible, and the modern engineering of private machine learning. We present ε-DP and (ε,δ)-DP, the roles of global and local sensitivity, and full proof sketches for the Laplace, Gaussian, and exponential mechanisms. We then develop the privacy-loss random variable view, derive basic, advanced, and optimal composition bounds, and show how Rényi differential privacy and the moments accountant yield the tight accounting behind DP-SGD. We cover privacy amplification by subsampling and shuffling, the sparse vector technique, federated learning with differential privacy, information-theoretic lower bounds on the privacy–utility tradeoff, and large-scale deployments including the 2020 US Census TopDown algorithm and telemetry at Apple and Google. We close with open problems in adaptive composition, tight lower bounds, and usable privacy budgeting.

## 1 Introduction

In 2006, Cynthia Dwork and colleagues proposed a definition of privacy for statistical databases that was radical in its simplicity: a randomized algorithm is private if its output distribution is *nearly the same* whether or not any single individual's record is present [1]. This definition — differential privacy — resolved a decades-old impasse. Traditional disclosure-limitation techniques (k-anonymity, cell suppression, data swapping) lacked a formal adversary model and fell repeatedly to linkage and reconstruction attacks, most famously the Dinur–Nissim reconstruction result showing that too-accurate aggregate answers permit full database reconstruction [2].

Differential privacy's appeal rests on three properties no prior framework simultaneously offered. First, it is *worst-case*: the guarantee holds for every pair of adjacent datasets and every output event, against adversaries of arbitrary computational power and arbitrary auxiliary information. Second, it is *quantitative*: privacy loss is measured by a single parameter ε (plus an optional failure probability δ), enabling a principled privacy–utility tradeoff. Third, it is *composable*: the privacy cost of running several mechanisms degrades gracefully and can be tracked exactly, which is what makes iterative algorithms like stochastic gradient descent amenable to analysis [3].

> Theorem: (Post-processing). If a randomized mechanism M satisfies (ε,δ)-differential privacy, then for *any* function g (deterministic or randomized), the composition g∘M also satisfies (ε,δ)-differential privacy, with no additional assumptions.

The theorem above is the workhorse of the entire field: noise must be added only once, at the point of data access, and everything downstream — denoising, tabulation, model training — is free. This survey develops the theory that flows from it: the canonical mechanisms that calibrate noise to *sensitivity*, the composition theorems that govern repeated queries, the accountant machinery behind private deep learning, amplification phenomena that recover privacy from randomness in sampling, and the lower bounds that delineate what no mechanism can achieve.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The definition and its neighbors

Let D range over datasets and write D ~ D' when D and D' are *adjacent* — differing in exactly one record (either by addition/removal or by substitution, a choice that changes constants by small factors and must be fixed before analysis [2]).

> Definition: (Pure differential privacy). A randomized mechanism M: D → R satisfies ε-differential privacy if for all adjacent D ~ D' and all measurable S ⊆ R,
> Pr[M(D) ∈ S] ≤ e^ε · Pr[M(D') ∈ S].

The *approximate* variant relaxes the bound by an additive δ:

> Definition: ((ε,δ)-DP). M satisfies (ε,δ)-differential privacy if for all adjacent D ~ D' and all S, Pr[M(D) ∈ S] ≤ e^ε · Pr[M(D') ∈ S] + δ.

The parameter δ is interpreted as the probability of *catastrophic* privacy failure — an event on which the ε bound need not hold — and is conventionally chosen cryptographically small, e.g. δ = 1/n² for a dataset of n records [2]. Pure ε-DP (δ = 0) is strictly stronger and is achieved by the Laplace and exponential mechanisms; (ε,δ)-DP is the natural home of the Gaussian mechanism and of composition analyses where tails must be absorbed.

Several relaxations refine the (ε,δ) tradeoff. **Rényi differential privacy** (RDP) [4] bounds the Rényi divergence of order α > 1 between M(D) and M(D'), yielding a *curve* of guarantees ε(α) that composes additively and converts back to (ε,δ)-DP via ε = ε(α) + log(1/δ)/(α−1). **Concentrated differential privacy** [5] bounds the privacy-loss random variable's moment-generating function, giving sub-Gaussian tail control; its zero-concentrated variant (zCDP) became the accounting language of the 2020 US Census [6]. Each relaxation preserves post-processing immunity and composes cleanly, trading a single (ε,δ) pair for a richer description of the loss distribution.

### 2.2 Sensitivity: how much a query can move

All noise calibration flows through *sensitivity* — the maximum change a query's output can suffer when one record changes.

> Definition: (Global sensitivity). For f: D → R^d, the ℓ₁ global sensitivity is Δ₁f = max_{D~D'} ‖f(D) − f(D')‖₁, and the ℓ₂ sensitivity Δ₂f is defined analogously.

Global sensitivity is a property of the *function*, independent of the data, which is why mechanisms built on it are simple to analyze. Counting queries have Δ₁ = 1; a sum over [0,1]-bounded values has Δ₁ = 1; but an unbounded mean has infinite sensitivity, forcing clipping or truncation before any mechanism applies [2]. **Local sensitivity** LS_f(D) = max_{D'~D} ‖f(D)−f(D')‖ measures change at the *actual* dataset and can be far smaller, but calibrating noise to it directly is unsound — the noise scale itself then leaks information. Sound uses of local sensitivity require smoothing, e.g. the smooth-sensitivity framework of Nissim, Raskhodnikova, and Smith, or propose-test-release paradigms [2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Laplace mechanism

The Laplace mechanism [1] is the canonical pure-DP construction: add noise drawn from a Laplace distribution scaled to Δ₁f/ε.

> Theorem: (Laplace mechanism). For f: D → R^d, define M(D) = f(D) + (Y₁,…,Y_d) with Y_i ∼ Lap(Δ₁f/ε) i.i.d. Then M satisfies ε-DP.

*Proof sketch.* Fix adjacent D, D' and output z. The density ratio is ∏_i exp(−ε|z_i − f(D)_i|/Δ₁f) / exp(−ε|z_i − f(D')_i|/Δ₁f). By the triangle inequality, each exponent difference is at most ε‖f(D)−f(D')‖₁/Δ₁f, so the ratio is bounded by exp(ε‖f(D)−f(D')‖₁/Δ₁f) ≤ e^ε. Integrating over any event S gives the definition [1][2].

The expected ℓ₁ error is d·Δ₁f/ε: error scales linearly with dimension and inversely with the budget. This is optimal up to constants for pure DP in the worst case.

### 3.2 The Gaussian mechanism

Replacing Laplace noise with Gaussian noise yields (ε,δ)-DP rather than pure DP, but with ℓ₂ geometry that is far friendlier to high-dimensional statistics and machine learning [2].

> Theorem: (Gaussian mechanism). For f: D → R^d, M(D) = f(D) + N(0, σ²I_d) satisfies (ε,δ)-DP if σ ≥ Δ₂f · √(2 ln(1.25/δ)) / ε, for ε ∈ (0,1).

*Proof sketch.* The privacy loss random variable L = ln(p(y|D)/p(y|D')) for the Gaussian mechanism is itself Gaussian with mean μ = ‖Δ‖²₂/(2σ²) and variance 2μ, where Δ = f(D)−f(D'). The ε-DP bound fails exactly on the tail event {L > ε}, whose probability is bounded by δ under the stated σ via standard Gaussian tail bounds [2]. The δ term is the price of the Gaussian's light but unbounded tails.

The Gaussian mechanism's ℓ₂ error scales as √(d)·σ — quadratically better in dimension than Laplace's linear dependence — which is why it dominates private machine learning.

### 3.3 The exponential mechanism

When the output space is discrete or non-numeric (a model architecture, a median, a synthetic record), additive noise is meaningless. The exponential mechanism [1][2] instead *samples* an output r ∈ R with probability proportional to exp(ε·u(D,r)/(2Δu)), where u is a utility score and Δu its sensitivity.

> Theorem: (Exponential mechanism). The mechanism above satisfies ε-DP, and with probability at least 1 − e^{−t} it selects r with u(D,r) ≥ OPT − (2Δu/ε)(ln|R| + t).

The utility theorem shows the mechanism is *near-optimal*: it loses only O((Δu/ε) log |R|) relative to the best achievable score. Report-Noisy-Max — adding Laplace noise to each candidate's score and taking the argmax — is its practical cousin for finite candidate sets [2].

### 3.4 The sparse vector technique

Answering k threshold queries naively costs k·ε under basic composition. The **sparse vector technique** (AboveThreshold) [2] exploits the observation that only *above-threshold* answers carry information: it perturbs the threshold once with Lap(2/ε) noise and each query with Lap(4/ε) noise, halting after c positives. The total cost is O(ε) *independent of k*, paying only for the informative answers. This "pay for what you learn" principle underlies private query-release systems and adaptive data analysis [7].

```python
import numpy as np

def laplace_mechanism(true_value, sensitivity, epsilon, rng=None):
    """Pure epsilon-DP release of a numeric query via Laplace noise [1]."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(rng)
    scale = sensitivity / epsilon
    return true_value + rng.laplace(0.0, scale)

def privacy_utility_tradeoff(sensitivities, epsilons):
    """Expected L1 error of the Laplace mechanism across budgets."""
    return {eps: sensitivities / eps for eps in epsilons}

if __name__ == "__main__":
    # Counting query (sensitivity 1): error halves as epsilon doubles.
    for eps, err in privacy_utility_tradeoff(1.0, [0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0]).items():
        print(f"epsilon={eps:>4}: expected L1 error = {err:.3f}")
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The privacy-loss random variable and composition

Fix adjacent D, D' and define the **privacy-loss random variable** L = ln(Pr[M(D)=o]/Pr[M(D')=o]) for o ∼ M(D). Pure ε-DP is exactly the statement |L| ≤ ε almost surely; (ε,δ)-DP says Pr[L > ε] − e^ε·Pr[L' < −ε] ≤ δ, where L' is the loss with D,D' swapped. Composition is then a statement about *sums of independent* (or adaptively chosen) loss variables [2][4].

- **Basic composition.** The k-fold adaptive composition of (ε_i,δ_i)-DP mechanisms is (Σ ε_i, Σ δ_i)-DP. The proof is a direct chaining of the definition; it is tight in the worst case but pessimistic in practice.
- **Advanced composition** [2]. The k-fold composition of (ε,δ)-DP mechanisms is (ε√(2k ln(1/δ')) + kε(e^ε−1), kδ + δ')-DP. Privacy loss concentrates: the ε term grows as √k rather than k, because independent bounded loss variables obey Chernoff bounds. For small ε the bound is ≈ O(ε√(k log(1/δ'))).
- **Optimal composition.** Kairouz, Oh, and Viswanath characterized the *exact* optimal composition bound for (ε,δ)-DP via the privacy-loss distribution, showing advanced composition is essentially tight but improvable by constant factors; the optimal curve is computable by dynamic programming over discretized loss distributions.

| Composition rule | ε growth over k queries | Requirements |
|---|---|---|
| Basic | k·ε | any (ε,δ)-DP |
| Advanced | O(ε√(k log(1/δ'))) | ε < 1, small δ' |
| RDP / moments accountant | k·ε(α), then optimize α | RDP profile known |
| zCDP | √(2kρ log(1/δ)) | ρ-zCDP mechanisms |

### 4.2 Rényi DP and the moments accountant

RDP [4] is defined through D_α(M(D)‖M(D')) ≤ ε(α) for orders α > 1. Its power is **additive composition**: if M₁ is (α,ε₁)-RDP and M₂ (adaptively chosen) is (α,ε₂)-RDP, their composition is (α,ε₁+ε₂)-RDP — no square roots, no δ' bookkeeping, just addition, *per order α*. Converting to (ε,δ)-DP at the end via ε = min_α [ε(α) + log(1/δ)/(α−1)] recovers bounds strictly tighter than advanced composition for Gaussian-like mechanisms.

The **moments accountant** of Abadi et al. [3] is this idea operationalized: track the log-moment-generating function of the privacy loss across thousands of DP-SGD steps, composing additively, and convert once at the end. For the subsampled Gaussian mechanism it yields ε ≈ O(q√(T log(1/δ))/σ) after T steps with sampling rate q — an order-of-magnitude improvement over naive composition that made private deep learning practical.

### 4.3 Privacy amplification: subsampling and shuffling

Randomness in *how data is accessed* amplifies privacy beyond what the mechanism alone provides:

- **Amplification by subsampling.** If M is (ε,δ)-DP and is applied to a random q-fraction subsample (Poisson sampling), the result is (O(qε), qδ)-DP [2][4]. Intuitively, any individual's record participates only with probability q, so the adversary's view is diluted. This is the engine inside DP-SGD: each step touches a random minibatch, and the per-step guarantee is amplified by the sampling rate before composition.
- **Amplification by shuffling.** In the *shuffle model*, n users each apply a local randomizer and a trusted shuffler permutes the messages, breaking the link between user and message. Erlingsson et al. showed this anonymity amplifies an ε₀-local guarantee to roughly O(ε₀√(log(1/δ)/n))-central DP — nearly the central-model utility at local-model trust assumptions — and the result now underpins deployed federated analytics [5][8].

### 4.4 DP-SGD and private deep learning

**DP-SGD** [3] modifies stochastic gradient descent in two steps per iteration: (1) *clip* each per-example gradient to ℓ₂ norm C, bounding sensitivity; (2) add Gaussian noise N(0, σ²C²I) to the averaged clipped gradients, then descend. Clipping is non-negotiable — without it, a single example's gradient is unbounded and no finite noise suffices.

The privacy analysis combines all machinery above: each step is a subsampled Gaussian mechanism (amplification by subsampling), steps compose via the moments accountant / RDP, and a final conversion yields (ε,δ)-DP. Empirically, Abadi et al. trained MNIST to 97% accuracy at (ε=8, δ=10⁻⁵) and CIFAR-10 to 73% at comparable budgets [3] — a modest utility cost that has since shrunk with better architectures, augmentation multiplicity, and large-batch training. The per-example clipping introduces bias (gradients no longer average to the true gradient), and hyperparameter tuning itself consumes budget — two practical costs the theory is still absorbing.

### 4.5 Federated learning with differential privacy

Federated learning keeps data on devices and aggregates model updates centrally, but updates themselves leak membership. **DP-FedAvg** [3] extends DP-SGD to the federated setting: clients clip their model deltas, the server aggregates via secure summation and adds Gaussian noise. Two refinements matter: *user-level DP* (adjacency = one user's entire dataset, far stronger than example-level) and the *distributed noise* view, where the shuffle model or secure aggregation lets clients add small local noise that sums to the required central noise [5][8]. Google's production federated analytics and Apple's telemetry both operate in this regime, trading the strong central-model utility for a trust model where the server never sees raw contributions.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Composition in numbers.** Consider k = 10,000 queries, each (0.01, 10⁻⁹)-DP. Basic composition gives ε = 100 — vacuous. Advanced composition gives ε ≈ 0.01·√(2·10⁴·ln(10⁹)) + 10⁴·0.01·(e^0.01−1) ≈ 6.4 + 1.0 ≈ 7.4. RDP accounting for Gaussian steps of the same per-step profile typically lands near ε ≈ 2–3 at δ = 10⁻⁹. The √k-vs-k gap is the difference between a deployable system and a vacuous bound [2][4].

> Theorem: (Lower bound, Dinur–Nissim [2]). Any mechanism answering ω(n) subset-sum queries on an n-record database with o(√n) noise per query permits reconstruction of 1−o(1) fraction of the database. Noise Ω(√n) is therefore *necessary*, not an artifact of the Laplace mechanism.

This reconstruction lower bound is the field's original motivation: it proves that the noise differential privacy adds is *qualitatively required* by any meaningful privacy notion, since blatant non-privacy (exact answers) trivially enables reconstruction. Complementary *minimax* lower bounds show the Laplace mechanism's O(Δf/ε) error is optimal up to constants for pure DP estimation, while fingerprinting-code arguments establish that for many high-dimensional problems (e.g. releasing all k-way marginals), either the privacy budget or the sample complexity must grow polynomially in dimension — the *curse of dimensionality* for privacy [2][7].

**Deployments.** The 2020 US Census deployed the TopDown Algorithm [6]: discrete Gaussian noise (zCDP accounting) injected into billions of histogram cells, followed by post-processing (non-negativity, hierarchical consistency) to produce the Microdata Detail File — the largest DP deployment in history, with a global budget of ρ ≈ 2.56 zCDP for the redistricting data. Apple deploys local DP (a Hadamard-count-sketch variant of randomized response) for emoji and QuickType telemetry, and Google's RAPPOR [8] pioneered randomized-response telemetry in Chrome. Each deployment wrestles with the same gap this survey documents: theory prices privacy in ε, but practitioners must *choose* ε — a socio-technical decision no theorem makes for them.

```python
import numpy as np

def rdp_gaussian(alpha, sigma, sensitivity=1.0):
    """RDP curve of the Gaussian mechanism: (alpha, alpha*s^2/(2*sigma^2))-RDP [4]."""
    return alpha * (sensitivity ** 2) / (2 * sigma ** 2)

def rdp_to_dp(eps_rdp, alpha, delta):
    """Convert (alpha, eps)-RDP to (eps+log(1/delta)/(alpha-1), delta)-DP [4]."""
    return eps_rdp + np.log(1.0 / delta) / (alpha - 1)

def moments_accountant(T, q, sigma, delta, alphas=range(2, 65)):
    """T subsampled-Gaussian steps, sampling rate q, noise sigma [3][4]."""
    eps = min(
        T * q**2 * rdp_gaussian(a, sigma)  # subsampling amplifies ~quadratically in q
        for a in alphas
    )
    best = min((rdp_to_dp(e, a, delta), a) for a, e in
               [(a, T * q**2 * rdp_gaussian(a, sigma)) for a in alphas])
    return best  # (epsilon, best alpha)

print("eps, alpha* =", moments_accountant(T=10000, q=0.01, sigma=4.0, delta=1e-5))
```

## 6 Limitations

Differential privacy's guarantees are only as strong as their *modeling assumptions*, and several limitations are structural rather than incidental. First, **the adjacency relation is a choice**: add/remove versus substitute adjacency, event-level versus user-level DP, and attribute-level variants all yield different ε numbers for the same mechanism, making cross-system budget comparisons treacherous [6]. Second, **composition is over the analyst's lifetime, not the mechanism's**: ε accumulates across *all* releases about a dataset, including ones the accountant never sees, so a published ε is valid only under a closed-world assumption that real deployments routinely violate. Third, **utility collapses in high dimensions**: fingerprinting lower bounds prove that for problems like private synthetic data over large domains, no mechanism achieves both strong privacy and high accuracy without enormous sample sizes [2][7]. Fourth, **hyperparameter and model selection leak**: tuning clipping norms, architectures, or ε itself on the private data consumes unaccounted budget in most published DP-SGD results. Finally, **δ is not a formality**: (ε,δ)-DP permits total compromise with probability δ, and δ = 10⁻⁵ on a million-user dataset is a meaningfully different promise than the same δ on a thousand-user one — yet δ is frequently chosen by convention rather than risk analysis.

## 7 Conclusion

Differential privacy transformed privacy from a property of *data* into a property of *algorithms*, and its compositional calculus — from basic chaining through advanced composition to Rényi accounting — is what lets the definition scale from single queries to thousand-epoch neural network training. The canonical mechanisms remain disarmingly simple: calibrate noise to sensitivity, sample proportionally to utility, pay only for informative answers. The modern frontier is engineering: tighter accountants, amplification from shuffling and sampling, federated deployments, and the unresolved question of how to *choose* ε in a way that is both principled and legible to the public whose data is at stake. The lower bounds remind us the noise is not overhead but necessity; the deployments — census, telemetry, federated learning — remind us the theory works at planetary scale. What remains is to close the loop between the ε the theorems price and the privacy people actually experience.
