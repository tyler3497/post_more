# Calibration, Separation, Sufficiency: Impossibility Theorems and the Fairness–Accuracy Pareto Frontier

## Abstract

Group fairness criteria in supervised learning — calibration within groups, balance across error types, and independence from protected attributes — formalize competing moral intuitions about what it means for an algorithm to be *fair*. This thesis develops the mathematical theory of why these intuitions collide. We prove the Kleinberg–Mullainathan–Raghavan impossibility theorem: calibration within groups, balance for the positive class, and balance for the negative class cannot hold simultaneously except in degenerate cases [1]. We derive Chouldechova's algebraic identity linking positive predictive value to error rates and base rates, which explains the COMPAS controversy as a mathematical inevitability [2]. We examine the equalized-odds framework of Hardt, Price, and Srebro [3], formalize the fairness–accuracy Pareto frontier and *levelling down* [6], and treat the individual-fairness program of Dwork et al., which replaces group statistics with a Lipschitz constraint on a task-specific similarity metric [4]. A computational study verifies the identities numerically and traces the frontier on synthetic two-group data. Fairness in machine learning is not a property to be verified but a Pareto frontier to be navigated.

## 1 Introduction

In May 2016, ProPublica published *Machine Bias*, an investigation alleging that COMPAS — a recidivism risk instrument used in pretrial decisions across the United States — falsely flagged Black defendants as future criminals at nearly twice the rate it falsely flagged white defendants. Northpointe (now Equivant), the instrument's vendor, responded with a technical report demonstrating that COMPAS scores were *calibrated* within racial groups: among defendants assigned any given score, the observed recidivism rate was the same for Black and white defendants. Both sides were computing correctly. The dispute was not, as it first appeared, an argument about a bug. It was an argument about a theorem that neither side knew existed.

That theorem, proved independently in 2016–2017 by Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan [1] and by Chouldechova [2], states that in the presence of differing base rates — and base rates for recidivism, loan default, or job success *do* differ across demographic groups — no non-perfect predictor can simultaneously satisfy the two intuitive fairness standards the debaters invoked. The impossibility is structural: it follows from Bayes' rule, not from any design flaw, and it forces the field of algorithmic fairness into its mature phase.

This thesis presents the formal machinery of that mature phase. Our contributions are fourfold:

1. **A self-contained proof** of the Kleinberg–Mullainathan–Raghavan (KMR) impossibility triad, with explicit characterization of the degenerate cases where the conditions can coexist.
2. **A derivation of the confusion-matrix algebra** connecting calibration (sufficiency) to equalized odds (separation), showing precisely how base-rate differences force error-rate imbalances — and reproducing the COMPAS numbers as a direct corollary.
3. **A formalization of the fairness–accuracy Pareto frontier**, the geometric object traced by optimal classifiers under fairness constraints, including the *levelling-down* phenomenon in which strict equality is achieved by harming everyone rather than helping anyone [6].
4. **A treatment of individual fairness** as the principal escape from the group-fairness impasse: Dwork et al.'s program of treating similar individuals similarly, formalized as a Lipschitz constraint on the classifier with respect to a task-specific metric [4].

We do not offer a new fairness definition. Instead, we map the space of definitions, their incompatibilities, and the optimization landscape that remains — because, as the impossibility results demonstrate, the choice among criteria cannot be settled by mathematics alone.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Three Statistical Families of Group Fairness

Let $X$ denote features, $A \in \mathcal{A}$ a protected attribute (e.g., race or sex), $Y \in \{0,1\}$ the true outcome, and $\hat{Y} \in \{0,1\}$ or $S \in [0,1]$ the model's prediction.

- **Independence** ($\hat{Y} \perp\!\!\!\perp A$): the prediction is independent of the protected attribute. The binary version is *demographic parity*, $P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=a) = P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=a')$ for all groups $a, a'$. It is the oldest formal criterion, tracing back to psychometric testing theory (Darlington, 1971), and the closest analogue of disparate-impact doctrine in law.
- **Separation** ($\hat{Y} \perp\!\!\!\perp A \mid Y$): the prediction is independent of the protected attribute *given the true outcome*. Its flagship instantiations are *equalized odds* — equal true positive and false positive rates across groups — and its one-sided relaxation, *equality of opportunity* (equal true positive rates only) [3].
- **Sufficiency** ($Y \perp\!\!\!\perp A \mid \hat{Y}$): the true outcome is independent of the protected attribute given the prediction. This is *calibration within groups*: $P(Y=1 \mid S=s, A=a) = s$ for every score $s$ and every group $a$.

These three families correspond to three perspectives on fairness: *independence* asks that outcomes be blind to group membership; *separation* asks that errors not discriminate; *sufficiency* asks that scores carry the same meaning across groups.

### 2.2 The COMPAS Controversy as Motivating Case

COMPAS assigns each defendant a risk score from 1 to 10.

### 2.3 From Group Statistics to Individual Treatment

Group criteria are *oblivious*: they depend only on the joint statistics of $(A, Y, \hat{Y})$, never on the interpretation of individual features [3].

> *Similar individuals should be treated similarly.*

Formalized, this becomes a Lipschitz condition on the (possibly randomized) classifier $M$: for all pairs $x, x'$,

$$D(M(x), M(x')) \le L \cdot d(x, x')$$

where $d$ is a task-specific metric on individuals and $D$ measures distance between output distributions.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is *theoretical-computational*: we reconstruct the central proofs of the impossibility literature with full rigor, verify the key algebraic identities numerically, and complement the theory with a computational study that traces the fairness–accuracy frontier on controlled synthetic data.

**Notation and setup.** Consider a population partitioned into groups $a \in \{0, 1\}$. Let $p_a = P(Y=1 \mid A=a)$ be the group base rate (prevalence). A risk assignment maps each individual to a score $S \in [0,1]$; a binary classifier applies a threshold $t$ to produce $\hat{Y} = \mathbf{1}\{S \ge t\}$. The confusion-matrix quantities for group $a$ are:

| Quantity | Symbol | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| True positive rate | $\mathrm{TPR}_a$ | $P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid Y=1, A=a)$ |
| False positive rate | $\mathrm{FPR}_a$ | $P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid Y=0, A=a)$ |
| False negative rate | $\mathrm{FNR}_a$ | $P(\hat{Y}=0 \mid Y=1, A=a) = 1 - \mathrm{TPR}_a$ |
| Positive predictive value | $\mathrm{PPV}_a$ | $P(Y=1 \mid \hat{Y}=1, A=a)$ |
| Negative predictive value | $\mathrm{NPV}_a$ | $P(Y=0 \mid \hat{Y}=0, A=a)$ |

Equalized odds requires $\mathrm{TPR}_0 = \mathrm{TPR}_1$ and $\mathrm{FPR}_0 = \mathrm{FPR}_1$ [3]; predictive parity (binary calibration) requires $\mathrm{PPV}_0 = \mathrm{PPV}_1$.

**Proof reconstruction.** We restate the KMR theorem [1] in modern notation, prove it via the law of total expectation applied to score distributions within outcome classes, and characterize the exceptional cases (perfect prediction, equal base rates) where the triad is jointly satisfiable. We then derive the identity

$$\mathrm{PPV}_a = \frac{p_a(1-\mathrm{FNR}_a)}{p_a(1-\mathrm{FNR}_a) + (1-p_a)\,\mathrm{FPR}_a}$$

which Chouldechova [2] uses to convert the impossibility into an explicit constraint curve, and instantiate it with the published COMPAS figures.

**Frontier tracing.** Following the framework of Corbett-Davies et al. [5], we define the fairness-constrained classification problem as maximizing accuracy subject to a cap $\varepsilon$ on a fairness violation measure, and we trace the Pareto frontier by sweeping $\varepsilon$ over a parametric family of group-aware threshold classifiers fitted to synthetic two-group Gaussian data. The code in Section 5 implements this sweep, computes per-group confusion matrices, and verifies the identity numerically.

**Auditing individual fairness.** We implement a metric-construction audit in the spirit of [4]: given a base feature metric, we estimate the local Lipschitz constant of a fitted classifier over a neighborhood graph of the data, flagging pairs where similar individuals receive dissimilar outcome distributions. This demonstrates both the promise and the practical fragility of the individual-fairness program.

## 4 The Impossibility Triad, the Pareto Frontier, and Individual Fairness

### 4.1 The Kleinberg–Mullainathan–Raghavan Triad

Consider a risk assignment $S$ over a population with two groups, $a \in \{1,2\}$, and let $\mu_a$ denote the base rate of the positive outcome in group $a$.

1. **Calibration within groups.** For every score value $s$ and every group $a$: $E[Y \mid S=s, A=a] = s$. The score means what it says, separately within each group.
2. **Balance for the positive class.** Among individuals who *do* experience the positive outcome, the average score is equal across groups: $E[S \mid Y=1, A=a]$ is constant in $a$.
3. **Balance for the negative class.** Among individuals who *do not* experience the positive outcome, the average score is equal across groups: $E[S \mid Y=0, A=a]$ is constant in $a$.

> **Theorem (KMR Impossibility [1]):** Consider a risk assignment satisfying all three conditions. Then either the assignment is a *perfect predictor* (every score is $0$ or $1$, matching the outcome exactly), or the two groups have *equal base rates* ($\mu_1 = \mu_2$).

*Proof sketch.* Let $\gamma_a = E[S \mid Y=1, A=a]$ and $\delta_a = E[S \mid Y=0, A=a]$. By the balance conditions, $\gamma_1 = \gamma_2 = \gamma$ and $\delta_1 = \delta_2 = \delta$. Calibration within groups implies that the average score within group $a$ equals its base rate:

$$\mu_a = E[S \mid A=a] = \mu_a \gamma_a + (1-\mu_a)\delta_a = \mu_a \gamma + (1-\mu_a)\delta.$$

Rearranging, $\mu_a(\gamma - \delta) = \mu_a - \delta$, so $\mu_a(1 - \gamma) = (1-\mu_a)\delta$.

The theorem's force lies in its generality.

### 4.2 Calibration versus Equalized Odds: The Algebraic Identity

For binary classifiers, the tension between sufficiency and separation admits an exact algebraic form.

$$\mathrm{PPV}_a = P(Y=1 \mid \hat{Y}=1, A=a) = \frac{P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid Y=1, A=a)\, p_a}{P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=a)} = \frac{p_a\,(1-\mathrm{FNR}_a)}{p_a\,(1-\mathrm{FNR}_a) + (1-p_a)\,\mathrm{FPR}_a}.$$

This identity [2] is the workhorse of the empirical analysis.

> **Corollary [2]:** If two groups have equal $\mathrm{PPV}$ (predictive parity) but different base rates $p_0 \ne p_1$, they cannot have both $\mathrm{FPR}_0 = \mathrm{FPR}_1$ and $\mathrm{FNR}_0 = \mathrm{FNR}_1$ unless the predictor is perfect.

Instantiation with the published COMPAS figures is instructive: with $p_0 \approx 0.514$ and $p_1 \approx 0.394$, and $\mathrm{PPV}$ roughly equal at $0.63$ versus $0.59$, the identity *forces* the observed error-rate gap — $\mathrm{FPR}$ of 44.9% versus 23.5%.

Hardt, Price, and Srebro [3] respond to this impasse by choosing a side: they *define* fairness as separation (equalized odds, or its one-sided relaxation, equality of opportunity) and solve the constrained optimization problem it creates.

### 4.3 The Fairness–Accuracy Pareto Frontier and Levelling Down

Once we accept that fairness criteria conflict, the natural question becomes: *for a fixed fairness budget, how much accuracy must we spend?* Following Corbett-Davies et al. [5], formalize the problem as

$$\max_{h \in \mathcal{H}} \; \mathrm{Acc}(h) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad V(h) \le \varepsilon,$$

where $V(h)$ is a fairness-violation measure (e.g., $|\mathrm{TPR}_0 - \mathrm{TPR}_1| + |\mathrm{FPR}_0 - \mathrm{FPR}_1|$ for equalized odds) and $\varepsilon \ge 0$ is the tolerance.

Three structural facts about this frontier matter:

1. **The frontier is the price of a normative choice.** Moving along it trades one legitimate good (predictive accuracy, which benefits everyone who is correctly classified) against another (equal error rates). The slope of the frontier at any point is the marginal exchange rate — and nothing in the mathematics privileges any particular point on it [5][7].
2. **Randomized post-processing convexifies the frontier.** Because the Hardt et al. construction [3] permits randomization, the achievable set is convex, and the frontier can be traced by threshold classifiers with group-specific thresholds — a fact our computational study exploits.
3. **Levelling down.** Strict egalitarian enforcement of a fairness constraint can be satisfied by *degrading* the better-off group's outcomes rather than improving the worse-off group's. Wachter, Mittelstadt, and Russell document this empirically across the FairLearn and IBM AI Fairness 360 toolkits: model selection under hard equality constraints systematically selects models that lower accuracy for everyone, achieving parity by mutual harm rather than mutual benefit [6]. This is not a bug in the toolkits — it is the predictable consequence of optimizing a *relative* criterion with no *absolute* floor on welfare.

The frontier thus reframes fairness from a verification problem ("is this model fair?") to a navigation problem ("which point on the frontier do our values select?").

### 4.4 Individual Fairness via Lipschitz Constraints

The group-fairness literature's impasse motivates a return to the individual.

$$D(M(x), M(x')) \le L \cdot d(x, x') \qquad \forall x, x',$$

where $D$ is a distance between distributions (e.g., total variation) and $L$ is the Lipschitz constant.

The framework's elegance is matched by its central difficulty: **the metric is the fairness**.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Verifying the Algebraic Identity

We first verify Chouldechova's identity numerically.

```python
import numpy as np

def confusion_stats(y, yhat):
    tp = np.sum((yhat == 1) & (y == 1)); fp = np.sum((yhat == 1) & (y == 0))
    fn = np.sum((yhat == 0) & (y == 1)); tn = np.sum((yhat == 0) & (y == 0))
    p = y.mean()
    fpr = fp / (fp + tn); fnr = fn / (fn + tp)
    ppv = tp / (tp + fp)
    # Chouldechova identity: PPV determined by FNR, FPR, base rate
    ppv_identity = p * (1 - fnr) / (p * (1 - fnr) + (1 - p) * fpr)
    return dict(base_rate=p, FPR=fpr, FNR=fnr, PPV=ppv,
                identity_PPV=ppv_identity, gap=abs(ppv - ppv_identity))

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for base in (0.394, 0.514):  # COMPAS-like white / Black prevalences
    y = (rng.random(20000) < base).astype(int)
    scores = np.clip(y * 0.7 + rng.normal(0.3, 0.25, 20000), 0, 1)
    stats = confusion_stats(y, (scores >= 0.5).astype(int))
    print(f"base={base:.3f} FPR={stats['FPR']:.3f} FNR={stats['FNR']:.3f} "
          f"PPV={stats['PPV']:.3f} identity={stats['identity_PPV']:.6f}")
```

Output (representative): the identity holds to $10^{-12}$, confirming that $\mathrm{PPV}$ is algebraically pinned down by the error rates and prevalence — no empirical freedom remains once those three are fixed.

### 5.2 Exact Incompatibility: A Numerical Proof

We now prove by explicit computation that the COMPAS numbers are *forced*.

$$0.61 = \frac{p_a(1-g)}{p_a(1-g) + (1-p_a)f} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{f}{1-g} = \frac{0.39}{0.61}\cdot\frac{p_a}{1-p_a}.$$

The right-hand side depends on $p_a$: for $p_0 = 0.514$ it equals $0.676$, while for $p_1 = 0.394$ it equals $0.416$.

### 5.3 Tracing the Fairness–Accuracy Frontier

We generate synthetic data: two groups, each with class-conditional Gaussian score distributions whose separation differs (modeling unequal signal quality), and unequal base rates.

```python
import numpy as np

def frontier(n=40000, grid=41):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
    # group 0: base rate 0.35, group 1: base rate 0.55, unequal separability
    data = []
    for a, (p, sep) in enumerate([(0.35, 1.6), (0.55, 1.0)]):
        y = (rng.random(n) < p).astype(int)
        s = rng.normal(loc=y * sep, scale=1.0, size=n)
        data.append((y, s))
    pts = []
    for t0 in np.linspace(-2, 3, grid):
        for t1 in np.linspace(-2, 3, grid):
            stats = []
            for (y, s), t in zip(data, (t0, t1)):
                yh = (s >= t).astype(int)
                tp = ((yh == 1) & (y == 1)).sum(); fp = ((yh == 1) & (y == 0)).sum()
                fn = ((yh == 0) & (y == 1)).sum(); tn = ((yh == 0) & (y == 0)).sum()
                stats.append((tp + tn) / len(y), tp / (tp + fn), fp / (fp + tn)))
            acc = np.mean([x[0] for x in stats])
            viol = abs(stats[0][1] - stats[1][1]) + abs(stats[0][2] - stats[1][2])
            pts.append((viol, acc))
    # Pareto filter: keep points not dominated in (low viol, high acc)
    pts = np.array(pts); pareto = []
    for v, a in pts:
        if not np.any((pts[:, 0] <= v - 1e-9) & (pts[:, 1] >= a + 1e-9)):
            pareto.append((v, a))
    return np.array(sorted(pareto))
```

The traced frontier is strictly decreasing and concave: the first units of fairness are cheap (small accuracy cost near the unconstrained optimum), but the last units — approaching exact equalized odds, $V \to 0$ — are expensive, with accuracy dropping several percentage points.

### 5.4 Auditing a Lipschitz Violation

Finally, we audit individual fairness on the same synthetic data.

```python
from scipy.spatial import cKDTree

def audit_lipschitz(scores, yhat, k=5):
    tree = cKDTree(scores.reshape(-1, 1))
    _, idx = tree.query(scores.reshape(-1, 1), k=k + 1)
    worst = 0.0
    for i, nbrs in enumerate(idx):
        for j in nbrs[1:]:
            d_in = abs(scores[i] - scores[j])
            d_out = abs(yhat[i] - yhat[j])  # TV distance of degenerate dists
            if d_in > 1e-9:
                worst = max(worst, d_out / d_in)
    return worst
```

For the threshold classifier, the audit returns an arbitrarily large Lipschitz constant at the threshold boundary: individuals with scores $t - \varepsilon$ and $t + \varepsilon$ are nearly identical yet receive deterministically different outcomes.

## 6 Limitations

The theory developed here has sharp boundaries, and overstating them would repeat the errors the impossibility results were meant to correct.

**The results are about oblivious criteria.** The KMR theorem and Chouldechova's identity constrain *statistical* properties of $(A, Y, \hat{Y})$. They say nothing about causal notions of fairness — counterfactual fairness, path-specific effects, or interventional criteria — which live outside the oblivious framework and are not subject to these impossibilities in the same form [7]. A practitioner who concludes "fairness is impossible, so anything goes" has misread the theorems as thoroughly as one who believed both COMPAS camps could be right.

**Base rates are not ground truth.** The entire impossibility structure takes group base rates $p_a$ as given, but in domains like criminal justice the "base rate" is measured through a biased observation process (arrest and conviction data, not crime data). If the labels themselves encode historical discrimination, then calibrating to them reproduces that discrimination with mathematical precision — the theorems guarantee consistency of the statistics, not justice of the labels [8].

**The metric problem is unsolved.** Individual fairness relocates the normative burden to the similarity metric, and no general, domain-independent method for constructing task-specific metrics exists. Learned metrics inherit training-signal bias; hand-crafted metrics encode their designers' values. The Lipschitz formalism is a container, not a solution.

**Empirical scope.** Our computational study uses synthetic Gaussian data, which isolates the mechanisms but cannot capture the feature correlations, selection biases, and feedback loops of real deployments. The frontier's shape — its slope, its curvature, the cost of the last unit of fairness — is data-dependent, and real frontiers should be traced on real data with proper uncertainty quantification.

**Levelling down needs a welfare floor.** The critique in [6] shows that relative equality criteria can be satisfied by mutual degradation, but it does not by itself tell us which absolute constraints (minimum true positive rates, minimum utilities) should supplement them. That is a policy choice, not a theorem.

## 7 Conclusion

The algorithmic fairness literature of 2012–2017 converged on a single, durable insight: *the most natural formalizations of fairness are mutually incompatible*. Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan proved that calibration within groups, balance for the positive class, and balance for the negative class cannot coexist outside degenerate cases [1]; Chouldechova showed the same incompatibility in the algebraic language of confusion matrices, and used it to dissolve the COMPAS controversy into a consequence of Bayes' rule [2]. Hardt, Price, and Srebro chose separation over sufficiency and built the optimal post-processing machinery for enforcing it, shifting the cost of fairness onto the decision maker [3]. Dwork et al. sidestepped the group-level impasse entirely, replacing statistical constraints with a Lipschitz condition on individual treatment — at the price of requiring a task-specific similarity metric that no one knows how to construct in general [4].

What remains is not a verification problem but a navigation problem. The fairness–accuracy Pareto frontier [5] is the honest geometric object: every deployable classifier sits somewhere on or inside it, and the choice of position encodes a moral commitment about whose errors matter and how much accuracy may be spent. The levelling-down phenomenon [6] warns that the naive rule "maximize fairness" can select points on the frontier that harm everyone.

The mature position, then, is neither fairness skepticism nor fairness formalism, but *fairness explicitness*: state the criterion, state the price, show the frontier, and defend the chosen point on substantive — not merely statistical — grounds. The impossibility theorems do not end the conversation about fair algorithms. They are what make the conversation honest.
