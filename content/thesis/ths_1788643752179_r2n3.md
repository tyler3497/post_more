# Factor-Graph SLAM: Incremental Smoothing with iSAM2, Robust Loop Closure, and the Bayes Tree

## Abstract

This thesis treats simultaneous localization and mapping as probabilistic inference on factor graphs, emphasizing *incremental* smoothing: measurements arrive as a stream and an updated estimate of the full trajectory and map must be available in real time. We develop the factor-graph MAP formulation, reduce it to nonlinear least squares, and show how variable elimination yields the sparse square-root information matrix and, equivalently, the Bayes tree — a directed structure encoding the posterior as conditional densities [1][2]. We analyze iSAM2's two innovations: *fluid relinearization*, which relinearizes only variables that moved beyond threshold, and *incremental variable reordering*, which keeps the tree shallow without periodic batch steps [1]. We formalize loop-closure detection as place recognition, quantify how a single false-positive loop closure catastrophically warps the MAP estimate, and survey robust back-ends — M-estimators, switchable constraints [3][4], max-mixtures, and graduated non-convexity. We report benchmark methodology (Victoria Park, Manhattan world, KITTI), convergence results, and open problems: globality certificates, certifiable perception, and lifelong sparsification.

## 1 Introduction

Simultaneous localization and mapping is the problem of building a map of an unknown environment while concurrently estimating the sensor platform's pose within it — the estimation core of mobile robotics. The field progressed from EKF-based filtering [5], through particle-filter Rao-Blackwellization, to the current consensus — full *smoothing* — in which every measurement constrains every pose ever visited. Smoothing re-exploits past measurements, closes loops over long horizons, and attains the Bayesian optimum given data association. Its cost is computational: batch smoothing re-factors the entire problem at every step, incompatible with online operation on graphs that grow unboundedly with time.

Incremental smoothing resolves this tension. The insight, developed most completely in the iSAM2 algorithm [1], is that a new measurement is *local*: it invalidates only the small sub-portion of the factorized posterior that depends on the variables it touches. The correct data structure for exploiting this locality is the Bayes tree, a directed tree in which each node is a clique of variables equipped with a conditional density, and the joint posterior is the product of these conditionals [1][2]. Updating the tree after a new measurement means cutting out the affected cliques, re-eliminating the small orphaned sub-graph, and grafting the result back — an operation whose cost scales with the *size of the change*, not the size of the map. Fluid relinearization avoids relinearizing the whole graph when only a few variables moved, and incremental reordering maintains good elimination orders on the fly, eliminating the periodic batch steps of earlier iSAM.

But incremental efficiency is worthless without *robustness*. The dominant real-world failure mode is the false-positive loop closure: a place-recognition front-end confidently declaring two distant poses co-located, injecting a wildly wrong factor into the graph. A single such factor warps the entire MAP estimate catastrophically [3]. Any serious treatment of factor-graph SLAM must therefore pair the incremental optimizer with (i) loop-closure detection tuned for near-zero false positives, and (ii) a robust back-end that identifies and disables erroneous constraints *during* optimization. This thesis addresses all three — factor-graph formulation, iSAM2 incremental inference, and robust loop closure — as one coherent estimation pipeline.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The SLAM posterior as a factor graph

Let $\mathbf{X} = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ denote the unknowns: robot poses $x_i \in SE(3)$ (or $SE(2)$) and landmark positions $l_j \in \mathbb{R}^3$. Let $\mathbf{Z} = \{z_1, \dots, z_m\}$ be the measurements: odometry, scan matches, bearing/range observations, GPS fixes, loop-closure constraints. A *factor graph* is a bipartite graph $G = (\mathcal{F}, \mathcal{X}, \mathcal{E})$ whose factor nodes $f_i \in \mathcal{F}$ and variable nodes $x_j \in \mathcal{X}$ are connected when $f_i$ depends on $x_j$, encoding the posterior factorization

$$P(\mathbf{X} \mid \mathbf{Z}) \propto \prod_{i=1}^{m} f_i(\mathbf{X}_i) \qquad \mathbf{X}_i \subseteq \mathbf{X}.$$

Each factor is a probabilistic measurement model. Under Gaussian noise, $f_i(\mathbf{X}_i) \propto \exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|h_i(\mathbf{X}_i) - z_i\|_{\Sigma_i}^2)$ with measurement function $h_i$ and covariance $\Sigma_i$, and MAP estimation reduces to *nonlinear least squares*:

$$\mathbf{X}^* = \arg\min_{\mathbf{X}} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \|h_i(\mathbf{X}_i) - z_i\|_{\Sigma_i}^2.$$

This is the unifying formulation: pose-graph optimization, bundle adjustment, and full SLAM are all instances of the same least-squares problem on different factor graphs. The Georgia Tech Smoothing and Mapping (GTSAM) library implements exactly this abstraction, treating factor graphs and Bayes networks — rather than sparse matrices — as the underlying computing paradigm [5].

### 2.2 Linearization and the square-root information matrix

Nonlinear least squares is solved iteratively. At linearization point $\mathbf{X}^0$, $h_i(\mathbf{X}_i) \approx h_i(\mathbf{X}^0_i) + \mathbf{H}_i \Delta\mathbf{X}_i$, giving the linear problem $\Delta\mathbf{X}^* = \arg\min \|\mathbf{A}\,\Delta\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{b}\|_2^2$, where $\mathbf{A}$ stacks the whitened Jacobians $\Sigma_i^{-1/2}\mathbf{H}_i$ and $\mathbf{b}$ the whitened residuals. The QR factorization $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{Q}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R} \\ \mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}$ yields the upper-triangular system $\mathbf{R}\,\Delta\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{d}$, solved by back-substitution — more stable than forming the normal equations $\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A}\,\Delta\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{b}$. The matrix $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A}$ is the *information matrix*; $\mathbf{R}$ is its square root. The sparsity of $\mathbf{R}$ — and hence the cost of factorization and back-substitution — is governed by the *variable elimination ordering*. Finding an ordering that minimizes fill-in is NP-hard, but heuristics such as COLAMD (column approximate minimum degree) are highly effective on SLAM graphs, whose chordal sparsity arises from the local structure of the measurement process [1].

### 2.3 Variable elimination and the Bayes tree

Variable elimination is the graphical-model dual of matrix factorization. Eliminating $x_j$ forms the product of all factors adjacent to it, marginalizes $x_j$ out, and records the *conditional density* $P(x_j \mid S_j)$, where $S_j$ is the *separator* — variables adjacent to $x_j$ at elimination time. Eliminating all variables in order $\pi$ factorizes the posterior as $P(\mathbf{X}) = \prod_{j=1}^{n} P(x_{\pi_j} \mid S_{\pi_j})$.

Kaess et al. [1] observed that this factorization is naturally represented as a *Bayes tree*: a directed tree whose nodes are cliques $C_k = \{F_k : S_k\}$ (frontal variables plus separator), with edges from each clique to the clique containing its separator. The Bayes tree maps *directly* onto the square-root information matrix: each clique corresponds to a block row of $\mathbf{R}$, and the conditional densities encode exactly the same information as $\mathbf{R}$ [1][2].

> **Theorem (Bayes tree–square-root equivalence, Kaess et al. [1]):** The Bayes tree obtained by variable elimination under ordering $\pi$ encodes the same joint density as the posterior factor graph, and its clique conditional densities are in one-to-one correspondence with the block rows of the square-root information matrix $\mathbf{R}$ obtained by QR factorization under the same ordering. Solving the tree by back-substitution is algebraically identical to solving $\mathbf{R}\Delta\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{d}$.

This equivalence is the foundation of everything that follows: *editing the graphical model edits the factorization*, and the Bayes tree makes that editing local, explicit, and probabilistically interpretable.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Problem statement

We consider online SLAM: at each time step $t$, new factors $\mathcal{F}'$ and possibly new variables $\mathcal{X}'$ arrive. We must maintain the MAP estimate and its uncertainty with per-step cost that does *not* grow with total graph size. The naive batch approach relinearizes and re-factors everything — $O(n^{1.5})$–$O(n^2)$ per step for typical SLAM sparsity — rapidly becoming infeasible.

### 3.2 The iSAM2 update cycle

iSAM2 [1] maintains the Bayes tree of the current linearized posterior and processes each new measurement through the following cycle:

1. **Add factors.** New factors $\mathcal{F}'$ are attached to the graph. The variables they involve identify the *affected* Bayes-tree cliques: those on the paths from the involved variables' cliques up to the root. Affected cliques are detached as *orphan sub-trees*; their variables plus new variables form a small sub-graph.
2. **Relinearize selectively (fluid relinearization).** Only variables whose estimate deviated from the linearization point by more than threshold $\beta$ are relinearized, along with their Markov blanket. This avoids the periodic batch relinearization of iSAM (2008), previously the dominant cost.
3. **Reorder incrementally.** The orphaned sub-graph is re-eliminated with a fresh ordering (constrained COLAMD) applied *only to affected variables*, treating the rest of the tree as fixed context.
4. **Graft and solve.** The re-eliminated fragment is re-attached; cached densities in untouched sub-trees are reused verbatim. Partial back-substitution updates changed variables, and only variables whose delta exceeds threshold $\alpha$ trigger propagation to descendants.

The net effect: per-step complexity scales with the *magnitude of the update* — a handful of cliques for odometry, more for a large loop closure — rather than with map size [1].

### 3.3 Loop-closure detection (front-end)

A loop closure is a factor connecting two temporally distant poses, asserted when the robot re-observes a previously mapped place. Detection is a *place-recognition* problem: retrieve candidate past observations and geometrically verify them. Modern pipelines combine appearance-based retrieval (bag-of-visual-words over ORB features, or learned global descriptors such as NetVLAD, scored with TF-IDF) with geometric verification (RANSAC-estimated fundamental matrices or PnP); candidates failing verification are discarded before reaching the back-end.

The operative design principle is *asymmetric risk*: a missed loop closure merely forfeits information, while a false positive can destroy the entire estimate (Section 4.4). Front-ends are therefore tuned for near-zero false-positive rates at the cost of recall — and the back-end is hardened anyway, because in perceptually aliased environments (corridors, parking structures, tunnels) false positives are unavoidable.

### 3.4 Robust back-end formulations

Given a factor graph possibly contaminated by outlier loop closures, the back-end must produce a correct estimate regardless. We implement and compare three families, all compatible with the iSAM2 Bayes-tree machinery:

1. **M-estimators / robust kernels.** Replace the quadratic cost $\|r\|^2$ with a robust loss $\rho(\|r\|)$ (Huber, Cauchy, Geman–McClure) whose influence function saturates, bounding the damage of any single residual. Implemented via iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) with weights $w(r) = \rho'(r)/r$. Simple and local, but non-convex losses admit poor local minima, and even Huber is overwhelmed by gross outliers at high contamination rates [3].
2. **Switchable constraints (Sünderhauf & Protzel [3]).** Each loop-closure factor $f_e$ is augmented with a latent *switch variable* $s_e \in [0,1]$ optimized jointly with the poses:
   $$\min_{\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{s}} \sum_{\text{odom}} \|r_e\|_{\Sigma_e}^2 + \sum_{\text{loops}} s_e^2 \|r_e\|_{\Sigma_e}^2 + \sum_{\text{loops}} \gamma_e (1 - s_e)^2.$$
   The penalty term $\gamma_e(1-s_e)^2$ encodes the prior belief that the loop is valid ($s_e \approx 1$); during optimization, inconsistent loops are *switched off* ($s_e \to 0$). The switches live in the same factor graph, so iSAM2 optimizes them incrementally with no structural change. Evaluated on Manhattan-world graphs with up to 1,000 injected false loop closures, where plain Huber fails [3][4].
3. **Max-mixtures and graduated non-convexity.** Max-mixtures model each loop as a mixture of a tight inlier and a broad outlier component, selecting the better-explaining mode per iteration. Graduated non-convexity (GNC), as in riSAM, solves a sequence from convex to the target non-convex loss, widening the convergence basin [4]. A comparison of switchable constraints, max-mixtures, and RRR appears in [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Anatomy of a Bayes-tree update

Consider poses $x_1 \dots x_5$ with landmarks $l_1, l_2$ and a loop closure between $x_5$ and $x_1$. Under a typical ordering (landmarks first, then poses in reverse temporal order), elimination yields cliques such as $C_1 = \{x_5 : x_4, x_1\}$, $C_2 = \{x_4 : x_3, x_1\}$, up to the root. When odometry $x_5 \to x_6$ arrives, only cliques containing $x_5$ — a small suffix of the tree — are affected; all landmark sub-trees are untouched and their cached densities reused. When instead the loop closure $x_6 \leftrightarrow x_1$ arrives, the affected region spans from $x_6$'s clique to the root, touching a larger but still bounded fragment. This is the precise sense in which iSAM2's cost tracks *information flow*, not graph size: only the Markov blanket of new evidence is recomputed [1].

### 4.2 Fluid relinearization vs. periodic batch steps

Nonlinearity is the enemy of incrementality: as estimates drift from linearization points, the linear approximation degrades. iSAM (2008) handled this with *periodic batch steps* — every $k$ steps, relinearize and re-factor everything, causing latency spikes unacceptable for real-time control. Fluid relinearization [1] instead tracks, per variable, the deviation $\|\Delta x_j\|$ between the current estimate and its linearization point; only variables exceeding threshold $\beta$ (plus their Markov blanket) are relinearized. On Victoria Park and Intel datasets this keeps error bounded while relinearizing a small fraction of variables per step, exposing a smooth speed–accuracy Pareto frontier [1].

### 4.3 Incremental variable reordering

The elimination ordering determines fill-in, and a fixed ordering degrades as the graph grows: variables added late under an early ordering can force dense separators. iSAM2 reorders *only the affected variables* at each update, using constrained COLAMD on the orphaned sub-graph while treating the rest of the tree as fixed context [1]. This keeps the Bayes tree balanced — the root stays near recently added variables, cliques stay small — and update times remain nearly constant on Manhattan and City10000 datasets where batch reordering would dominate [1].

### 4.4 The catastrophic loop closure: a quantitative account

Why does one false loop closure destroy the estimate? In least squares, the MAP solution satisfies $\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A}\,\Delta\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{b}$; a single factor with residual $r_{\text{out}}$ and information $\Omega_{\text{out}}$ contributes $\Omega_{\text{out}} r_{\text{out}}$ to the right-hand side. Because the loss is quadratic, the outlier's influence is *unbounded* in $\|r_{\text{out}}\|$: a confident-but-wrong loop closure (small $\Sigma$, large residual) dominates the gradient and drags the whole trajectory toward the lie. Sünderhauf and Protzel [3] exhibit this on Manhattan world: 3,500 poses, 2,099 true loop closures, plus 100 injected false ones — under 5% contamination — suffice to make a standard back-end (g2o with Huber) converge to a topologically wrong map, while switchable constraints recover ground truth exactly. A general theorem of robust statistics underlies the experiment: *least squares has a breakdown point of zero* — a single adversarial observation can move the estimate arbitrarily far.

### 4.5 Switch variables inside the Bayes tree

A notable elegance of switchable constraints: no new inference machinery is required. Each switch $s_e$ is another continuous variable in the factor graph, connected by the loop factor (ternary over $x_i, x_j, s_e$) and its prior factor $\gamma_e(1-s_e)^2$. The Bayes tree contains switch variables in cliques alongside poses; elimination marginalizes them like any other variable. The joint optimization is non-convex, but initialized at $s_e = 1$ (trust all loops) it behaves well: consistent loops keep $s_e \approx 1$ because the data supports them, while inconsistent loops are driven to $s_e \approx 0$ because no pose configuration can satisfy them cheaply [3][4]. The Vertigo library provides reference C++ implementations of switchable constraints and max-mixtures for g2o and GTSAM, plus the spoiled benchmark datasets [4].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 Correctness properties

iSAM2 inherits the following guarantees from its construction [1]:

> **Theorem (Incremental exactness):** For a linear Gaussian factor graph, the iSAM2 update produces exactly the batch MAP estimate and exact marginal covariances at every step, since Bayes-tree editing is algebraically equivalent to updating the QR factorization.

> **Theorem (Locality of updates):** The cliques requiring re-elimination after adding factors $\mathcal{F}'$ are exactly the union of root paths of cliques containing variables adjacent to $\mathcal{F}'$. No other clique's conditional density changes.

For nonlinear graphs, iSAM2 is an *approximate* incremental optimizer: fluid relinearization bounds the linearization error, and [1] measures the normalized $\chi^2$ against periodic-batch baselines, showing negligible accuracy loss at large speedups.

### 5.2 Benchmark methodology and results

We evaluate on the canonical 2D/3D pose-graph and SLAM datasets used across the cited literature:

| Dataset | Poses | Loop closures | Key property | Reported result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Victoria Park | ~7k | car/bearing | Real, large | Matches batch $\chi^2$; ~10× faster [1] |
| Intel Research Lab | ~900 | laser | Real, indoor | Near-constant update time [1] |
| Manhattan world | 3,500 | 2,099 + outliers | Synthetic, spoiled | Recovers GT with 100–1,000 false loops [3] |
| City10000 / Sphere | 10,000 | dense | Large synthetic | Incremental reordering keeps cliques small [1] |
| KITTI odometry | varies | visual loops | Real, driving | Smoothing reduces drift [5] |

From [1]: on Victoria Park, iSAM2's per-step update stays well under the sensor period while batch re-factorization grows superlinearly; recommended $\alpha$/$\beta$ settings achieve batch-identical $\chi^2$ at a fraction of the cost. From [3]: across random, local, random-group, and local-group outlier-injection policies, switchable constraints converge to ground-truth topology in every trial where M-estimator baselines collapse; the ICRA-2013 comparison [4] extends this to max-mixtures and RRR, finding switchable constraints and max-mixtures broadly comparable and both superior to non-robust baselines.

### 5.3 Reference implementation sketch

The following Python sketch (using the GTSAM Python bindings [6]) illustrates the core pattern — building a pose-graph factor graph with a robust loop closure and solving it incrementally:

```python
import gtsam
import numpy as np

graph = gtsam.NonlinearFactorGraph()
initial = gtsam.Values()
noise_odom = gtsam.noiseModel.Diagonal.Sigmas(np.array([0.05, 0.05, 0.01]))

for i in range(N):
    initial.insert(i, gtsam.Pose2(0.0, i * 1.0, 0.0))
    if i > 0:
        graph.add(gtsam.BetweenFactorPose2(i - 1, i,
                    gtsam.Pose2(0.0, 1.0, 0.0), noise_odom))
graph.add(gtsam.PriorFactorPose2(0, gtsam.Pose2(0, 0, 0),   # anchors gauge freedom
          gtsam.noiseModel.Diagonal.Sigmas(np.ones(3) * 1e-6)))

huber = gtsam.noiseModel.Robust.Create(                       # robust loop closure
    gtsam.noiseModel.mEstimator.Huber.Create(1.345),
    gtsam.noiseModel.Diagonal.Sigmas(np.array([0.1, 0.1, 0.02])))
graph.add(gtsam.BetweenFactorPose2(N - 1, 0, gtsam.Pose2(0, 0, 0), huber))

isam = gtsam.ISAM2(gtsam.ISAM2Params())   # fluid relinearization + incremental reorder
isam.update(graph, initial)
result = isam.calculateEstimate()
```

For switchable constraints, each loop factor is replaced by a factor over $(x_i, x_j, s_e)$ plus a prior on $s_e$; the same `ISAM2.update` optimizes poses and switches jointly, since switches are ordinary graph variables [3][5].

## 6 Limitations, Open Problems, and Future Work

**Linearization fragility.** Fluid relinearization bounds error only when the linearization point stays within the trust region of the true posterior mode. Bearing-only SLAM, range-only initialization, and monocular visual SLAM with unknown scale all exhibit regimes where first-order approximations fail qualitatively; incremental second-order or sampling-based corrections inside the Bayes tree are promising but immature.

**Lifelong operation and graph growth.** iSAM2's per-step cost is bounded by the update size, but memory grows without bound: every pose and landmark persists forever. Long-term autonomy demands *sparsification* — principled marginalization of old variables — and *multi-session* map management. Fixed-lag smoothing [5] bounds the active window but sacrifices distant loop closures; reconciling fixed-lag efficiency with full-smoothing accuracy is an active frontier.

**Local minima and globality certificates.** iSAM2 converges to a *local* optimum; poor initialization (e.g., after long open-loop traverse) can trap it far from the global MAP. Certifiably correct perception (SE-Sync and successors) provides *a posteriori* optimality certificates via semidefinite relaxation, but integrating them into an incremental Bayes-tree framework without sacrificing real-time performance remains open.

**Data association outside the MAP.** The factor-graph formulation conditions on data association. Switchable constraints and max-mixtures soften this boundary by making association *part of* the optimization [3][4], but assume the candidate set is given. Joint inference over continuous geometry and discrete association is supported in principle by GTSAM's hybrid machinery [5], yet scaling it to city-scale graphs is unsolved.

**Front-end/back-end co-design.** The most practical direction: learned place recognition with calibrated uncertainty, feeding probabilistic loop-closure *candidates* (not binary decisions) into a robust incremental back-end, with graduated non-convexity widening the convergence basin [4]. The Bayes tree is the right substrate — it already supports incremental, anytime inference — but the field has yet to fully exploit it for joint perception-estimation loops.

## 7 Conclusion

Factor-graph SLAM reduced mapping to its essence: MAP inference on a sparse graphical model, solved by nonlinear least squares. The Bayes tree revealed that this inference has a local, editable structure — a new measurement perturbs only a small fragment of the factorized posterior — and iSAM2 turned that observation into a practical algorithm via fluid relinearization and incremental reordering, achieving batch-quality estimates at incremental cost with no periodic batch steps [1][2]. Robust loop closure — switchable constraints [3], max-mixtures, graduated non-convexity [4] — hardened the pipeline against false-positive loop closures that dominate real-world failures, with experiments showing recovery from contamination levels that destroy conventional back-ends. The open problems — globality certificates, joint discrete-continuous inference, lifelong sparsification — are substantial, but they are now problems *within* a mature, unifying framework. For the practitioner the message is concrete: formulate the posterior as a factor graph, maintain it as a Bayes tree, update it incrementally, and distrust every loop closure until the optimizer itself vouches for it.
