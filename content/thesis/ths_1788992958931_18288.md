# Constructing Static Single Assignment Form and Modern Register Allocation: Dominance-Frontier Placement, Graph Coloring, Linear Scan, and SSA Deconstruction

## Abstract

Static single assignment (SSA) form and register allocation are the two pillars of modern compiler back ends, yet they are rarely treated as a single engineering discipline. This thesis unifies them. We present the classical dominance-frontier algorithm for φ-function placement of Cytron, Ferrante, Rosen, Wegman, and Zadeck [1], contrast it with the on-the-fly construction of Braun, Buchwald, Hack, Leißa, Mallon, and Zwinkau that builds minimal, pruned SSA directly from an abstract syntax tree without prior analysis [6], and then develop the two dominant register allocation paradigms that consume SSA: Chaitin's graph-coloring allocator and its Briggs refinement [2][3][4], and Poletto and Sarkar's linear-scan allocator for just-in-time compilation [5]. We close the loop with SSA deconstruction — the lost-copy and swap problems of φ elimination — and the theorem that SSA interference graphs are chordal, making coloring polynomial. The result is a coherent account of how programs enter SSA, how their values are bound to a finite register file, and how SSA is faithfully destroyed again.

---

## 1. Introduction

Every optimizing compiler must answer two questions. First, how should the unbounded temporaries of the intermediate representation be *named* so that data flow is explicit and analyzable? Second, how should those temporaries be *bound* to the finite set of machine registers so that the program runs? The first question is answered by static single assignment (SSA) form; the second by register allocation. The two are coupled: SSA makes data-flow facts local and sparse, register allocation consumes those facts, and the allocator's output must finally be translated *out* of SSA before machine code emission.

SSA form imposes a single, ruthless discipline: **every variable is assigned exactly once** [1]. Where control flow merges — at loop headers, after conditionals — the single-assignment rule would break, because two different definitions of a variable can reach the same use. SSA repairs this with *φ-functions* (phi-nodes), notional merges placed at join points:



The φ-function is a *fiction* — no machine instruction corresponds to it — but an extraordinarily productive one. Because each use has exactly one reaching definition, def-use chains become trivial: follow the subscript. Optimizations that once required iterative data-flow analysis — constant propagation, value numbering, dead-code elimination — become *sparse* algorithms that walk def-use edges directly [1].

Register allocation then maps the unbounded SSA names onto *K* physical registers, spilling to memory when the program's register pressure exceeds capacity. The classical formulation, due to Chaitin and colleagues [2], models simultaneously-live temporaries as an *interference graph* and allocation as graph coloring. The modern fast alternative, linear scan [5], trades a little code quality for dramatically lower compile time — the bargain that makes just-in-time compilation feasible.

This thesis proceeds in four movements. Section 2 fixes terminology. Section 3 states our method: each algorithm presented operationally, with worked examples and complexity analysis, grounded in the primary literature. Section 4 is the deep dive: dominance-frontier φ placement, on-the-fly SSA construction, graph-coloring allocation, linear-scan allocation, and SSA deconstruction. Section 5 gathers the empirical record; Section 6 is honest about the limits. The through-line is a single claim: **SSA construction and register allocation are one pipeline, and understanding either in isolation is understanding neither.**

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Control-flow graphs, dominators, and liveness

A *control-flow graph* (CFG) has basic blocks as nodes and control transfers as edges. Node *d* **dominates** node *n* if every path from the entry to *n* passes through *d*. A variable is **live** at a point if its value may be read on some path before being overwritten. The *interference graph* has one node per live range and an edge between simultaneously-live ranges — values that cannot share a register [2].

### 2.2 The flavors of SSA

Not all SSA is equal. The literature distinguishes three principal flavors [1]:

- **Minimal SSA**: φ-functions are placed at every join point in the *iterated dominance frontier* of each variable's definitions. Simple to compute, but inserts φ-functions that may be dead.
- **Pruned SSA**: eliminates φ-functions for variables that are not live at the join point, requiring a liveness analysis.
- **Semi-pruned SSA**: a cheaper approximation — φ-functions are placed only for variables *used outside their defining block* ("global" names), avoiding the full liveness computation.

The trade is compile time versus IR size: LLVM's `mem2reg` effectively produces pruned SSA, while many JIT front ends accept semi-pruned form.

### 2.3 Register allocation as coloring

> **Theorem (Chaitin et al. [2]):** Register allocation for *K* registers is equivalent to *K*-coloring the interference graph: assign each node a color (register) such that no adjacent nodes share a color; nodes that cannot be colored are *spilled* to memory.

Graph coloring is NP-complete in general, so allocators are heuristic: *simplify* (remove nodes of degree < K), *spill* (choose a victim when stuck), *select* (assign colors in reverse removal order), and *coalesce* (merge move-related nodes to eliminate copies) [2][4].

| Allocator family | Time complexity | Code quality | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Graph coloring (Chaitin–Briggs) | Superlinear (iterated) | High | Ahead-of-time compilers (GCC, LLVM greedy) |
| Linear scan (Poletto–Sarkar) | Near-linear | Within ~12% of coloring [5] | JITs (HotSpot client, V8) |
| Chordal/SSA coloring | Polynomial coloring | Optimal *coloring*, heuristic spill | Research SSA allocators |

## 3. Methodology

Our method is *reconstructive exposition*: each algorithm is presented as its authors specified it, exercised on a hand-verifiable example, and annotated with the asymptotic argument behind its design. We draw exclusively on peer-reviewed primary sources, distinguishing *theorems* (proved in the cited work), *measurements* (reported there), and *folklore* (widely repeated but unproven). Where competing variants exist — Cytron versus Braun construction, Chaitin versus Briggs spilling — we present both and state when each wins. Pseudocode is Python-like; no claim depends on unimplemented machinery.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Dominance frontiers and φ-function placement

The classical construction of Cytron, Ferrante, Rosen, Wegman, and Zadeck [1] proceeds in two phases: **φ-placement** and **renaming**.

**Definition.** The *dominance frontier* of a CFG node *n*, written *DF(n)*, is the set of nodes *m* such that *n* dominates some predecessor of *m* but does *not* strictly dominate *m*. Intuitively, *DF(n)* is the set of join points "just beyond" *n*'s dominance — exactly where a definition in *n* can first meet a competing definition from another path.

φ-placement iterates this idea to a fixed point. For a variable *v* defined in the set of blocks *Defs(v)*, compute the *iterated dominance frontier* *IDF(Defs(v))*: start with *DF* of each definition block, add those frontier blocks as new definition sites (each will hold a φ for *v*), and repeat until closure. A φ-function for *v* is then placed at every block in *IDF(Defs(v))* [1]. The fixed point terminates because the block set is finite, and the algorithm is efficient because dominance frontiers are computed once per CFG, not once per variable, using the dominator tree:



This runs in time proportional to the output size — effectively linear in practice [1].

**Renaming** then walks the dominator tree depth-first, maintaining a stack of current versions per variable. Each definition pushes a fresh subscripted name; each use is rewritten to the stack top; on leaving a block, the pushed names are popped. φ-operands are filled in when the walk visits each predecessor. The stack discipline is what makes renaming *linear*: every definition is pushed and popped exactly once.

> **Theorem (Cytron et al. [1]):** The algorithm places φ-functions at precisely the join points of the iterated dominance frontier of each variable's definitions, and renaming assigns each use its unique reaching definition. The resulting program is in minimal SSA form.

*Worked miniature.* In `i = 0; while i < n: i = i + 1`, the loop header lies in the entry block's dominance frontier, so a φ is placed there: `i₁ = φ(i₀, i₂)`, with the back-edge definition `i₂ = i₁ + 1` feeding its second operand. Minimal, exact, mechanical.

### 4.2 On-the-fly construction à la Braun et al.

Cytron's algorithm presupposes a CFG, dominator tree, and dominance frontiers — analyses that must run before a single φ is placed. Braun, Buchwald, Hack, Leißa, Mallon, and Zwinkau [6] asked whether SSA could be constructed *during* translation from the abstract syntax tree, with no prior analysis at all. Their answer is the algorithm now used by Cranelift, libfirm, and countless teaching compilers.

The core data structure is `currentDef[var][block]`: the latest definition of `var` visible at the end of `block`. Three mutually recursive operations do all the work:



The subtlety is *sealing*: a block seals once all predecessors are known, which for reducible CFGs happens naturally during translation. Incomplete φ-nodes in unsealed blocks are completed when the block seals. Then `tryRemoveTrivialPhi` deletes φ-nodes whose operands are all the same value (or itself) — recursively, since removing one φ can trivialize another. This single cleanup pass is what converts the naive construction into **minimal and pruned** SSA: dead φ-functions never survive [6].

> **Theorem (Braun et al. [6]):** For reducible control flow, the algorithm produces minimal, pruned SSA form. Its runtime is on par with Cytron et al.'s algorithm, while the implementation is roughly *375 lines versus 1141* — a threefold reduction in compiler complexity.

The engineering moral: SSA construction need not be a "phase" at all — it can be a *discipline of the translator*, maintained invariantly, permitting SSA-based optimizations to run *during* construction [6].

### 4.3 Graph-coloring register allocation: from Chaitin to Briggs

Chaitin and colleagues [2] reduced register allocation to graph coloring and, crucially, gave a *heuristic* that works: **simplify–spill–select**. While some node has degree < *K* (fewer than *K* neighbors), remove it and push it on a stack. If no such node exists, choose a *spill candidate* — Chaitin's metric was spill cost divided by degree — remove it, and continue. Then pop the stack in reverse, assigning each node a color unused by its already-colored neighbors. Nodes that cannot be colored are spilled: loads and stores are inserted, and allocation repeats [2][3].

The 1982 refinement [3] added *coalescing* — merging a move's source and destination when the merged node keeps degree < *K*, deleting the move — and integrated spilling into the simplify loop. Briggs, Cooper, and Torczon [4] then fixed Chaitin's two great weaknesses:

1. **Optimistic coloring.** Chaitin spilled any node that could not be removed during simplify. Briggs observed that a node removed *as a spill candidate* might still be colorable during select, because its neighbors may receive the same color. Deferring the spill decision to select time strictly reduces spilling [4].
2. **Conservative coalescing refined.** Briggs's coalescing criterion — merge *u, v* only if the merged node has fewer than *K* neighbors of significant degree — is safe: it cannot turn a colorable graph uncolorable. Iterated coalescing (George–Appel) repeats simplify/coalesce to a fixed point for further gains.



The deeper theoretical point belongs to the SSA era: **the interference graph of a strict SSA program is chordal**, hence *perfect* — its chromatic number equals its clique size, and greedy coloring along a *perfect elimination order* is optimal and polynomial [4][6]. Under SSA, the NP-complete coloring step *evaporates*; what remains hard is spilling and coalescing. This is why modern allocators keep the program in SSA through allocation and only deconstruct afterward.

### 4.4 Linear-scan allocation: speed as a design goal

Graph coloring is excellent but slow — superlinear, iterated, with repeated interference-graph rebuilds. For just-in-time compilers, where compile time *is* run time, Poletto and Sarkar [5] proposed an algorithm that is nearly linear and dramatically simpler: **linear scan**.

The insight is to replace live *ranges* over the CFG with live *intervals* over a linear order of instructions. Each temporary's lifetime becomes *[start, end)*; two temporaries interfere iff their intervals overlap. Allocation is then a single sweep:

1. Compute live intervals and sort by increasing start point.
2. Maintain the *active* set: intervals that overlap the current point, sorted by end point.
3. For each interval in order: *expire* active intervals that ended before this start (freeing their registers), then assign a free register — or spill, typically the interval with the farthest end point ("farthest-first" spilling).



Traub, Holloway, and Smith's *second-chance binpacking* invests more compile time for better code: it splits live ranges at selected points instead of spilling whole intervals, keeping hot portions in registers. Poletto and Sarkar measured linear scan as *several times faster* than a fast graph-coloring allocator while producing code *within 12%* of an aggressive iterated-coalescing allocator on all but two benchmarks [5] — the bargain that put linear scan into HotSpot's client compiler, V8's early back ends, and LLVM's fast allocator.

> **Proof sketch (correctness of the sweep [5]):** The active set when interval *i* is processed contains exactly the intervals overlapping *i*'s start point, whose registers are therefore unavailable. Assigning any free register — or spilling when none is free — preserves the invariant that overlapping intervals hold distinct registers. Expiry is safe because an interval ending before *i* starts cannot overlap *i*.

The price of intervals is precision: a temporary live in two disjoint regions of the CFG occupies one interval spanning the gap, artificially inflating register pressure. Lifetime *holes* and interval splitting are the standard remedies.

### 4.5 Translating out of SSA: deconstruction, copies, and the lost-copy problem

No machine executes φ-functions, so SSA must be destroyed before code emission. The naive translation — replace each φ `x₀ = φ(x₁, …, xₙ)` with copies `x₀ ← xᵢ` at the end of each predecessor block *i* — is *wrong* in two classic ways:

- **The lost-copy problem.** If a φ-operand's source block also defines a variable read by another φ in the same block, sequential copies can overwrite a value before it is read (e.g., two φ-nodes implementing `a ← b` and `b ← a` simultaneously). The fix is *parallel copies*, executed simultaneously or sequentialized with cycle-breaking temporaries [1].
- **The swap problem.** A generalization: φ-nodes can induce arbitrary permutations of values across an edge, requiring temporary registers to break cycles.

Additionally, copies must be placed on *edges*, not in blocks: if a predecessor has multiple successors (a critical edge), the copy cannot go at the end of the predecessor block without corrupting the other path. The standard remedy is **critical-edge splitting** — inserting an empty block on the edge — or the *conventional SSA* (CSSA) discipline, which constrains φ-related variables to a single congruence class so that copies become simple renamings.

Coalescing then removes the inserted copies wherever source and destination can share a register — the same conservative coalescing of §4.3, now applied to φ-generated moves. Deconstruction is thus not an afterthought but a second allocation problem, and getting it wrong miscompiles programs: every SSA-based compiler implements parallel-copy sequentialization (Rideau–Serpette–Leroy's verified algorithm ships in CompCert as `Parmov`).

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

The literature's quantitative record, restricted to claims with published measurements:

- **Linear scan vs. graph coloring [5].** On the Machine SUIF back end, linear scan compiled *several times faster* than a fast graph-coloring allocator performing no coalescing, while generated code was within 12% of an aggressive iterated-coalescing allocator on all but two benchmarks. Simple alternatives (e.g., allocating the *K* most-used variables) produced code *several times slower* — speed of compilation must not be confused with triviality of the algorithm.
- **On-the-fly SSA construction [6].** The Braun et al. algorithm matches Cytron et al.'s runtime on SPEC CPU2000 while reducing implementation size from ~1141 to ~375 lines of code and guaranteeing minimal, pruned SSA for reducible CFGs.
- **Improvements to graph coloring [4].** Briggs, Cooper, and Torczon report that optimistic coloring plus conservative coalescing strictly reduces spill code and copy instructions relative to Chaitin's allocator across their benchmark suite.
- **Chordal coloring theorem.** For strict SSA programs the interference graph is chordal, hence optimally colorable in polynomial time by greedy elimination ordering. The NP-completeness Chaitin et al. [2] exhibited therefore attaches, under SSA, *only* to spilling and coalescing decisions.

> **Proof (sketch): SSA interference graphs are chordal.** In strict SSA each use is dominated by its definition, so live ranges correspond to subtrees of the dominator tree — and the intersection graph of subtrees of a tree is chordal. Greedy coloring along a perfect elimination order then uses exactly *ω(G)* colors, where *ω* is the clique number — optimal.

## 6. Limitations

Honesty requires stating what this pipeline does *not* solve.

1. **Spilling remains NP-complete**, even under SSA [2]. Chordality tames coloring, not spill choice; optimal spill placement is intractable, and all production allocators use heuristics (farthest-interval, cost/degree, Chow–Hennessy priority).
2. **Coalescing is NP-hard** in general and interacts adversarially with coloring: aggressive coalescing can make a colorable graph uncolorable, which is why conservative criteria [4] deliberately leave copies behind.
3. **Irreducible control flow** defeats the sealing discipline of on-the-fly construction [6], requiring node splitting or fallback to Cytron-style construction.
4. **Linear scan's interval approximation** overestimates lifetimes across control-flow gaps, inflating register pressure where a graph-coloring allocator would see disjoint ranges; lifetime holes mitigate but do not eliminate this.
5. **SSA deconstruction can reintroduce the copies** that SSA-based optimization removed; final code quality depends on the deconstruction-time coalescer as much as on the allocator.
6. **Machine constraints** — register classes, aliasing (e.g., `al`/`ax`/`eax`/`rax` overlap on x86-64), calling conventions, fixed-register instructions — turn pure coloring into a *constrained* problem the textbook formulations idealize away.

---

## 7. Conclusion

We have traced a single value's journey through a modern back end: born as a definition in the AST, renamed into SSA by dominance frontiers or on-the-fly construction [1][6], bound to a physical register by graph coloring or linear scan [2][3][4][5], and finally released from SSA by parallel-copy deconstruction. The unifying insight is that **SSA is not merely an optimization IR but an allocation IR**: its chordal interference graphs make coloring tractable, its φ-functions name the merge points where allocation decisions matter, and its deconstruction is itself a small allocation problem. Compiler engineers who treat construction, allocation, and deconstruction as three chapters of one story — rather than three separate passes owned by three separate teams — build back ends that are simpler, faster, and measurably better. The φ-function, a fiction no machine executes, remains the most productive fiction in compilation.
