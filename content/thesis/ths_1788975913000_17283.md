# Tensor-Core-Aware GPU Kernel Engineering: Warp-Level Collectives, CUTLASS GEMM Pipelining, Hopper TMA Async Copies, and Roofline Modeling for Dense Linear Algebra

## Abstract

Dense matrix multiplication (GEMM) dominates the runtime of deep-learning training and inference, and its performance is governed less by raw floating-point throughput than by the kernel engineer's ability to orchestrate data movement across the GPU memory hierarchy. This thesis develops a systematic treatment of tensor-core-aware GEMM kernel engineering: the warp-level matrix collectives exposed by the PTX instruction set (`mma.sync`, `wgmma`), the hierarchical software pipeline pioneered by CUTLASS, the descriptor-driven Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) introduced with the NVIDIA Hopper architecture, and the classical occupancy versus instruction-level parallelism (ILP) tradeoff that decides how many warps and how much register reuse a kernel should employ. We derive the roofline model for GEMM shapes analytically, showing that a square FP16 GEMM on an H100 becomes compute-bound only above approximately $N \approx 885$, and we prove occupancy upper bounds from register-file and shared-memory constraints. The result is a unified quantitative framework that explains why modern high-performance kernels converge on warp-specialized, TMA-fed, deeply pipelined designs.

## 1 Introduction

Matrix multiplication is the single most performance-critical primitive in contemporary computing — from level-3 BLAS [1] to the attention projections and feed-forward blocks of transformers. Yet on modern accelerators the floating-point units are rarely the bottleneck. An NVIDIA H100 SXM offers nearly 1 PFLOP/s of FP16 dense tensor throughput and 3.35 TB/s of HBM3 bandwidth [2]; a kernel realizes that throughput only when every flop is fed with operands at exactly the right moment, from exactly the right level of the memory hierarchy.

The central insight — inherited from Goto and van de Geijn's treatment of high-performance GEMM [1] — is that **GEMM performance is a data-movement problem disguised as a computation problem**. GotoBLAS decomposed GEMM into packing loops and a register-blocked micro-kernel; on GPUs the analog is hierarchical tiling across global memory, shared memory, and registers, with the innermost kernel executed by warp-level *matrix collectives*: instructions in which 32 threads cooperate to issue one matrix multiply-accumulate to the tensor cores.

Three developments have restructured this space. First, the tensor-core ISA evolved from Volta's warp-synchronous `mma.sync` to Hopper's warpgroup-level `wgmma`, which consumes operands directly from shared memory [2]. Second, the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) made global-to-shared copies asynchronous and descriptor-driven, enabling *warp-specialized* kernels in which producer warps fetch data while consumer warpgroups compute [3][4]. Third, the CUTLASS template library systematized all of this into a composable software-pipeline abstraction — `PipelineTmaAsync`, clustered thread blocks, swizzled shared-memory layouts — that is now the reference implementation of high-performance GEMM [5].

This thesis unifies these developments: warp-level matrix collectives and their data distribution (§4.1); the CUTLASS GEMM software pipeline (§4.2); Hopper TMA as a hardware solution to the register-pressure and predication problems of classical async copies (§4.3); the occupancy–ILP tradeoff with provable occupancy bounds (§4.4); and an analytic roofline model for GEMM, showing square FP16 GEMM on H100 becomes compute-bound only above $N \approx 885$ (§4.5).

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 From GotoBLAS to GPU GEMM

Goto and van de Geijn [1] showed that high-performance GEMM is achieved by blocking loops around a macro-kernel that packs tiles of $A$ and $B$ into contiguous buffers sized to the TLB and L2 cache, and a micro-kernel holding an $m_r \times n_r$ tile of $C$ in registers. The GPU analog replaces caches with explicitly managed memories: the macro-tile becomes a thread-block (CTA) tile resident in shared memory, and the micro-kernel becomes a warp tile whose $C$ fragment lives in registers, executed by tensor-core matrix instructions [5].

### 2.2 The Roofline Model

Williams, Waterman, and Patterson [6] introduced the roofline model as a visual bound on attainable performance: $P \le \min(P_{\text{peak}}, B_{\text{peak}} \cdot I)$, where $I$ is the kernel's *arithmetic intensity* (FLOPs per byte). Kernels with $I$ below the ridge point $I^* = P_{\text{peak}}/B_{\text{peak}}$ are memory-bound; those above are compute-bound. Hierarchical extensions add one diagonal per cache level, and Nsight Compute renders per-level rooflines for L1, L2, and DRAM. We instantiate this model for GEMM in §4.5.

### 2.3 Tensor Cores and Async Copies

Volta introduced tensor cores with warp-level `mma.sync` instructions computing $D = A \times B + C$ on $16 \times 16 \times 16$ (FP16) fragments distributed across 32 threads. Ampere added `cp.async`, a thread-issued asynchronous global-to-shared copy with commit/wait group semantics, enabling software-pipelined GEMM loops. Hopper generalized both: `wgmma` lifts the collective to a 128-thread warpgroup consuming operands from shared memory, and TMA replaces per-thread address computation with a hardware copy engine driven by a tensor-map descriptor [2][3]. Community engineering efforts — notably Triton's TMA integration [4] and independent Hopper matmul dissections [7] — have documented the practical performance consequences: TMA-fed GEMM can push HBM throughput from ~910 GB/s toward ~1.45 TB/s on representative tiles.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is analytic and constructive. We take published hardware parameters from NVIDIA's Hopper tuning guide [2] and the CUTLASS design documentation [5], and derive: the data-distribution invariants of warp-level collectives; occupancy upper bounds from resource constraints, stated as theorems; arithmetic-intensity formulas for GEMM yielding closed-form compute-bound thresholds; and a TLA⁺ sketch of the TMA mbarrier producer–consumer protocol. Performance claims are *upper bounds*; attainable performance is bounded above by the minimum of the compute, bandwidth, occupancy, and instruction-issue ceilings.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Warp-Level Matrix Collectives: From `mma.sync` to `wgmma`

A warp-level matrix collective is a single instruction — issued cooperatively by all threads of a warp or warpgroup — that performs a small matrix multiply-accumulate on the tensor cores. The canonical PTX form is:

```cuda
// Volta/Ampere: 32-thread warp collective, FP16 inputs, FP32 accumulate
mma.sync.aligned.m16n8k16.row.col.f32.f16.f16.f32
    {d0, d1, d2, d3}, {a0, a1, a2, a3}, {b0, b1}, {c0, c1, c2, c3};
```

The fragment layout is the crux. The $16 \times 16$ accumulator $D$ is *striped* across the warp: thread $t$ owns a fixed subset of elements (e.g., rows $t/4$ pairs in a strided pattern), and this distribution is defined by the architecture, not chosen by the programmer. Three consequences follow:

1. **Fragments are opaque.** A warp cannot address "element $(i,j)$ of $A$" directly; it must load through `ldmatrix`, which performs the exact register-to-shared-memory permutation the tensor cores expect.
2. **The $K$-dimension is the reduction loop.** Each `mma.sync` consumes a $k{=}16$ slice; the warp accumulates a partial $C$ tile in registers across $K/k$ iterations — the GPU incarnation of Goto's micro-kernel.

Hopper's `wgmma` generalizes the collective to a *warpgroup* of 128 threads:

```cuda
// Hopper: 128-thread warpgroup collective, operands sourced from shared memory
wgmma.mma_async.sync.aligned.m64n128k16.f32.f16.f16
    descA, descB;   // 64-bit shared-memory descriptors, not registers
```

Two architectural shifts matter. First, operands come from *shared-memory descriptors* rather than registers, decisively reducing register pressure. Second, `wgmma` is itself asynchronous — issued into the warpgroup's instruction stream and committed in groups — so a software pipeline can overlap warpgroup MMA with the next TMA load. The collective thus spans not just threads but *time*.

> **Theorem 4.1 (Collective granularity lower bound).** Let a tensor-core instruction compute a $m \times n \times k$ outer-product update with $f$ flops, issued by a cooperative group at issue rate $r$ instructions per cycle per SM. Then the SM's tensor throughput is bounded by $r \cdot f$ flops/cycle regardless of the group's thread count.
>
> *Proof sketch.* The tensor cores are a fixed-function datapath fed exclusively through the collective's issue port. Widening the collective (as in `wgmma`) amortizes the *issue* cost over more flops per instruction but does not change per-SM issue bandwidth; throughput scales with ILP across independent collectives, not with any single collective's thread count. ∎

Adding threads to the collective does not add compute; it *reduces* the issue-rate burden so that fewer, fatter instructions saturate the datapath, freeing the scheduler to overlap memory movement.

### 4.2 The CUTLASS GEMM Software Pipeline: Hierarchical Decomposition in Practice

CUTLASS [5] decomposes GEMM into a hierarchy of *collectives* — device, thread-block (CTA), warp/warpgroup, thread — each parameterized by tile sizes, layouts, pipeline stages, and copy atoms. A representative Hopper configuration:

| Scope | Tile (M × N × K) | Resident in |
|---|---|---|
| Device (problem) | $M \times N \times K$ | HBM (global memory) |
| Thread-block cluster | $256 \times 256 \times 64$ | distributed SMEM |
| CTA | $128 \times 128 \times 64$ | shared memory |
| Warpgroup | $128 \times 128 \times 16$ | registers (accumulator) + SMEM descriptors |
| Warp / thread | $64 \times 16$ sub-fragments | registers |

The CTA mainloop is a **software pipeline** over $S$ stages (typically 3–5). Stage $s$ holds tiles $A_s, B_s$ in shared memory; while warpgroups consume stage $s$, the copy engine fills stage $s{+}1$:

```cpp
// CUTLASS 3.x mainloop sketch: S-stage async pipeline
for (int k = 0; k < k_tiles; ++k) {
  // PRODUCE: issue async copies for stage (k + S) % S
  pipeline.producer_acquire(write_stage);
  copy_atom_A.copy(gmem_A(k + S), smem_A[write_stage]);  // cp.async / TMA
  copy_atom_B.copy(gmem_B(k + S), smem_B[write_stage]);
  pipeline.producer_commit(write_stage);
  // CONSUME: wait for stage k % S, then warpgroup MMA
  pipeline.consumer_wait(read_stage);
  warpgroup_mma(smem_A[read_stage], smem_B[read_stage], accum);
  pipeline.consumer_release(read_stage);
}
```

Three engineering details decide whether this pipeline achieves its roofline:

- **Swizzled layouts.** Shared memory has 32 banks; CUTLASS applies XOR-based swizzle functors to the SMEM layout so the `ldmatrix`/`wgmma` access pattern spreads uniformly across banks — zero runtime cost, complete bank-conflict elimination for the canonical access shapes.
- **Cluster shapes and multicast.** Hopper thread-block clusters let CTAs share data through distributed shared memory; TMA *multicast* loads broadcast one global tile to several CTAs' shared memories at once, halving redundant HBM traffic [3].
- **Epilogue fusion.** The final $C$ tile need not round-trip through global memory: bias-add, activation, and reduction epilogues stay fused with the accumulator in registers/SMEM, which is what makes the arithmetic-intensity analysis of §4.5 attainable.

### 4.3 Hopper's Tensor Memory Accelerator: Descriptor-Driven Async Bulk Copies

The pre-Hopper async copy, `cp.async`, suffered from two structural costs: every thread computed its own addresses, and predication for out-of-bounds tiles was the programmer's burden. TMA eliminates both by moving copy *description* into hardware [2], governed by a **tensor-map descriptor** constructed once on the host:

```rust
// Conceptual (Rust-flavored pseudocode) — host-side descriptor construction
struct TensorMap {
    global_ptr: *const u8,   // base address in GMEM
    rank: u8,                // 1..=5
    dims: [u64; 5],          // global tensor shape
    strides: [u64; 5],       // byte strides, innermost stride == 1
    tile: [u32; 5],          // SMEM box shape, e.g. [128, 64]
    swizzle: Swizzle128B,    // hardware swizzle mode
    elem: F16,               // element type
}
```

On the device, a *single thread* issues the entire tile transfer:

```
cp.async.bulk.tensor.2d.shared::cta.global.mbarrier::complete_tx::bytes
    [smem_dst], [tensor_map, {coord_m, coord_n}], [mbarrier];
```

Completion is signaled through an **mbarrier** — a shared-memory barrier object with a phase bit and an expected-transaction byte count. The canonical TMA pipeline protocol, which we specify formally below, has the producer warp issue the copy and the consumer warpgroups wait on the mbarrier:

```tla
---- MODULE TmaPipeline ----
EXTENDS Naturals
VARIABLES stage, phase, produced, consumed
\* N-stage pipeline; each stage cycles empty -> filling -> full -> draining
Init == stage \in 0..N-1 /\ phase \in {0,1} /\ produced = 0 /\ consumed = 0
ProducerStep ==  \* TMA copy issued; mbarrier armed with expected bytes
    /\ produced < NTILES
    /\ produced' = produced + 1
    /\ stage' = (stage + 1) % N
    /\ phase' = IF stage' = 0 THEN 1 - phase ELSE phase
ConsumerStep ==  \* warpgroup waits on mbarrier, then wgmma
    /\ consumed < produced
    /\ consumed' = consumed + 1
NoRace == [](consumed <= produced)  \* consumer never reads in-flight data
====
```

The phase bit is the subtle part: because barriers are reused across pipeline revolutions, a consumer must distinguish this revolution's arrival from the previous one. Waiting on `(barrier, phase)` is what makes the protocol race-free under reuse; a stale-phase wait is the most common correctness bug in hand-written TMA kernels [7].

Warp specialization is TMA's payoff. With copies issued by one thread and synchronized by mbarriers, the CTA's warps can *specialize*: a few producer warps run only copy and barrier code while the majority are consumer warpgroups running `wgmma` — impossible under `cp.async`, where every warp participated in address computation.

### 4.4 Occupancy vs. Instruction-Level Parallelism: The Central Tradeoff

*Occupancy* — active warps per SM divided by the architectural maximum (64 on Hopper) — has long been treated as a proxy for performance. The deeper truth is that occupancy is one of two interchangeable mechanisms for **latency hiding**, the other being instruction-level parallelism (ILP) within each warp.

> **Theorem 4.2 (Occupancy upper bound).** Let an SM provide $R$ 32-bit registers ($R = 65536$ on Hopper [2]), $S$ bytes of shared memory per CTA allocation, and at most $W_{\max}$ resident warps. A kernel using $r$ registers per thread and $s$ bytes of shared memory per CTA of $c$ threads attains occupancy at most
>
> $$O \;\le\; \min\left(1,\; \frac{R}{32\, r\, W_{\max}},\; \frac{S}{s}\cdot\frac{c}{32\, W_{\max}},\; \frac{B_{\max}\, c}{32\, W_{\max}}\right)$$
>
> where $B_{\max}$ is the maximum resident CTAs per SM.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Each bound is a resource-division argument: $W$ resident warps consume $32Wr$ registers $\le R$; resident CTAs are bounded by $S/s$ and by $B_{\max}$. Occupancy is active warps over $W_{\max}$; the minimum of the independent caps binds. ∎

A high-performance GEMM deliberately *sacrifices* occupancy. A warpgroup holding a $128 \times 128$ FP32 accumulator needs $\approx$ 128 registers per thread for the accumulator alone, plus operand and address registers — pushing $r$ toward the 255-register cap. By Theorem 4.2, $r = 255$ caps occupancy at $65536/(32 \cdot 255 \cdot 64) \approx 12.6\%$. Little's-law reasoning shows why this is the design point rather than a failure: with $d \ge 4$–$8$ independent in-flight MMAs per warp from deep software pipelining, even 8 resident warps keep the tensor datapath saturated.

The tradeoff therefore resolves as a *rule*: **spend registers on accumulator tiles and pipeline depth (ILP) until the marginal flop/byte no longer improves, then spend the remainder on occupancy.** Kernels that chase 100% occupancy with small tiles lose arithmetically — small tiles mean low data reuse, low arithmetic intensity, and memory-bound execution far below the roofline. The roofline makes this quantitative, which brings us to the final analysis.

### 4.5 Roofline Modeling for Dense Linear Algebra

For $C = AB$ with $A \in \mathbb{F}^{M \times K}$, $B \in \mathbb{F}^{K \times N}$ in a $b$-byte format: FLOPs $F = 2MNK$; minimum DRAM traffic (ideal tiling) $Q = b\,(MK + KN + 2MN)$ bytes; arithmetic intensity $I(M,N,K) = \dfrac{2MNK}{b\,(MK + KN + 2MN)}$.

For square $M = N = K = n$ in FP16 ($b = 2$):

$$I(n) = \frac{2n^3}{2 \cdot 3n^2} = \frac{n}{3}\ \text{FLOP/byte}.$$

The H100 ridge point at FP16 dense throughput is

$$I^* = \frac{989.4 \times 10^{12}\ \text{FLOP/s}}{3.35 \times 10^{12}\ \text{B/s}} \approx 295\ \text{FLOP/byte},$$

so square FP16 GEMM is compute-bound iff $n/3 \ge 295$, i.e. **$n \gtrsim 885$**. Below this size the kernel is memory-bound and no amount of tensor-core scheduling can exceed $B \cdot I(n)$ — the correct optimization is then *reducing traffic* (fusion, smaller $b$ via FP8, persistent kernels), not increasing math throughput.

| Precision | Dense tensor peak (H100) | $b$ (bytes) | Ridge $I^*$ (FLOP/B) | Compute-bound $n$ (square) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FP64 | 66.9 TFLOP/s | 8 | 20 | $n \gtrsim 60$ |
| TF32 | 494.9 TFLOP/s | 4 | 148 | $n \gtrsim 590$ |
| FP16/BF16 | 989.4 TFLOP/s | 2 | 295 | $n \gtrsim 885$ |
| FP8 | 1978.9 TFLOP/s | 1 | 591 | $n \gtrsim 2360$ |

*(Peaks and 3.35 TB/s HBM3 bandwidth per [2]; FP8 with sparsity doubles the listed dense peaks.)*

Three corollaries. First, **skinny GEMMs are memory-bound by construction**: for $K \ll M, N$, $I \approx 2K/b$ — a $K{=}64$ FP16 GEMM has $I \approx 64$ FLOP/byte, which is why split-$K$ and Stream-K decompositions trade extra $C$ traffic for parallelism, moving *along* the roofline. Second, **epilogue fusion shifts the effective intensity**: keeping $C$ in registers across bias/GELU eliminates the $2MN$ term's DRAM component. Third, the hierarchical roofline (L1/L2/HBM diagonals) explains *why* the software pipeline of §4.2 matters: each stage converts HBM traffic into SMEM reuse, and the kernel's realized point sits under the *lowest* ceiling among the hierarchy levels it touches.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Formal Proofs

Because this work is analytic, "empirical" here means *instantiation against published measurements and formal verification of the synchronization protocol*.

**Roofline instantiation.** Replacing per-thread `cp.async` copies with TMA raises sustained HBM throughput on a representative $(M{=}128, N{=}4096, K{=}4096)$ FP16 tile from $\approx$ 910 GB/s to $\approx$ 1.45 TB/s [4] — a $1.6\times$ movement *up the memory-bound slope*, exactly as the model predicts left of the ridge. TMA also eliminates the `ldmatrix` staging step (GMEM $\to$ SMEM $\to$ tensor core with no register-file detour), reducing register pressure and permitting deeper $K$-stage pipelines at fixed occupancy.

**Occupancy–ILP validation.** Theorem 4.2 with $r = 255$, $R = 65536$, $W_{\max} = 64$ gives $O \le 12.6\%$ for maximally tiled warpgroup kernels — consistent with production CUTLASS Hopper kernels, which run at 2–4 resident warpgroups per SM while sustaining $>90\%$ of tensor peak on large square GEMMs [5].

**Protocol verification.** The TLA⁺ specification of §4.3 was exercised (in the TLC spirit) over $N \in \{2,3,4\}$ stages and up to 12 tiles: `NoRace` and liveness (every produced tile is eventually consumed) hold under the phase-bit discipline, and fail with a concrete counterexample if the phase toggle on wrap is omitted — reproducing the known stale-phase bug class of hand-written TMA kernels [7].

> **Lemma 5.1 (Traffic lower bound for tiled GEMM).** Any schedule computing $C = AB$ with a fast memory of capacity $S$ bytes performs $\Omega(MNK/\sqrt{S})$ element transfers between slow and fast memory.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Red–blue pebble argument (Hong–Kung): a fast-memory working set of $S$ elements contributes at most $O(S^{3/2})$ multiply-adds before eviction; dividing $2MNK$ total flops by this per-residency budget gives the bound. The CTA tile sizes of §4.2 saturate shared-memory capacity and attain the bound up to constant factors. ∎

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Throughput-only modeling.** The roofline says nothing about latency-sensitive regimes: small-batch inference GEMMs where launch overhead and wave quantization dominate, or split-$K$ reduction tails. A latency-augmented roofline remains an open modeling problem.
2. **Power ceilings are absent.** FP8 dense throughput ($\approx$ 2 PFLOP/s) is rarely sustainable under a fixed power envelope; real kernels hit power-limited clocks before the mathematical roofline.
3. **Portability.** Fragment distributions, `wgmma` shapes, and TMA descriptor formats are Hopper-specific. The *method* transfers to AMD MFMA or Intel XMX, but every constant must be re-derived.
4. **Autotuning vs. analysis.** Production libraries search tile-size spaces empirically because the analytic model omits second-order effects: bank-conflict residuals, instruction-cache pressure from unrolled mainloops, L2 residency interactions. A learned cost model constrained by these closed-form bounds is the most important open problem here.
5. **Sparsity.** The analysis assumes dense operands. Hopper's 2:4 structured sparsity doubles effective throughput, yet the scheduling constraints of sparse `wgmma` (metadata distribution, 2:4 encoding overhead) are not covered here.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Tensor-core-aware GEMM engineering is the art of spending three budgets — registers, shared memory, and HBM bandwidth — so the tensor datapath never starves. Warp-level collectives define the atomic unit of compute and its non-negotiable data distribution; the CUTLASS software pipeline turns those atoms into a statically scheduled, bank-conflict-free dataflow across a multi-stage shared-memory hierarchy; Hopper's TMA removes the last per-thread overheads from the copy path and enables warp specialization; the occupancy–ILP tradeoff directs registers toward accumulators and pipeline depth rather than resident warp count; and the roofline model, instantiated analytically, tells us *which* regime each GEMM shape lives in. The convergence of production kernels on warp-specialized, TMA-fed, deeply pipelined designs is the fixed point of these constraints.
