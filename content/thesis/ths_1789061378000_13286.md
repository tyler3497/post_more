# Speculative Decoding for Large Language Model Inference: Draft Models, Rejection Sampling, and Lossless Acceleration

## Abstract

Autoregressive decoding of large language models is limited not by arithmetic throughput but by memory bandwidth: emitting *K* tokens demands *K* sequential passes over the full parameter tensor, leaving accelerator compute largely idle [1]. Speculative decoding breaks this serialization by proposing candidate tokens with an inexpensive approximation model and verifying them in a single parallel pass of the large target model, accepting proposals through a modified rejection-sampling test that provably preserves the target's output distribution [1][2]. We present the draft–verify loop, the acceptance criterion min(1, *p(x)/q(x)*) with resampling from norm(max(0, *p − q*)), and the expected-throughput analysis in terms of the acceptance rate *α* and draft length *γ*. We survey the modern drafting taxonomy — independent drafters, dependent-head methods such as Medusa [3] and EAGLE [4], self-drafting by layer skipping, and draft-free Jacobi and lookahead schemes [5] — unified by the recent survey literature [6]. Reported speedups of 2–3.5× are lossless up to hardware numerics, yet batch size, sampling temperature, draft overhead, and serving constraints bound real deployments.

## 1 Introduction

The defining performance characteristic of contemporary LLM inference is a mismatch between where the time goes and where the arithmetic goes. Each decoding step must stream all *P* parameters from high-bandwidth memory into the accelerator's cache before computing a single token. Decoding is therefore **memory-bandwidth bound**: per-token latency is, to first order, model size divided by memory bandwidth, while matrix-multiply capacity sits idle [2]. Generating *K* tokens thus requires *K* serial runs of the model [1].

Two observations motivate speculative decoding. First, hard language-modeling tasks decompose into easier subtasks that small, efficient models approximate well: the next few tokens of a summary, a code completion, or a chat reply are often predictable by a model one to two orders of magnitude smaller than the target [1]. Second, the very parallelism that decoding wastes can be redeployed: scoring a short continuation of *K* candidate tokens in parallel costs roughly the same wall-clock time as scoring a single token, because the weights are loaded once and the sequence dimension parallelizes across the idle compute [2]. Speculative decoding — introduced independently by Leviathan, Kalman, and Matias [1] and by Chen et al. [2] in early 2023 — combines these insights into an algorithm that samples *multiple* tokens per target-model invocation **without changing the output distribution**.

The core loop is conceptually simple. A cheap *draft* (approximation) model *M_q* autoregressively proposes *γ* candidate tokens. The expensive *target* model *M_p* then scores all *γ* + 1 positions (the *γ* drafts plus one bonus position) in a single forward pass. Each draft token *x* is accepted with probability min(1, *p(x)/q(x)*); the first rejection triggers resampling from the adjusted distribution norm(max(0, *p − q*)) and terminates the round [2]. The resulting token stream is provably distributed exactly as if the target model had been sampled directly — a property the literature calls *losslessness* — while the number of expensive serial steps falls by a factor of 2–3 in practice [1][2].

> **Theorem (Losslessness, Leviathan et al. [1]):** The sequence of tokens produced by speculative decoding is distributed identically to the sequence obtained by sampling each token directly from the target model *M_p*.

What began as a two-model trick has since grown into a substantial research program, comprehensively taxonomized in the survey of Xia et al. [6]: independent versus self-drafting strategies, tree-structured candidate verification, feature-level drafters (EAGLE [4]), multi-head parallel predictors (Medusa [3]), draft-free Jacobi iteration schemes (Lookahead decoding [5]), and single-model stream-fused variants [7]. This article presents the full arc — the algorithm, its proof, the throughput theory, the drafting taxonomy, the empirical record, and the limits that remain.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The memory wall in autoregressive decoding

A decoder-only transformer factorizes *p(x₁…x_T) = ∏ᵢ p(xᵢ | x_<i)* and samples each conditional in turn, loading the full weight tensor and KV cache per step. At batch size 1, arithmetic intensity is so low that the GPU waits on memory transfers; Chen et al. [2] measure that scoring *γ* short continuations in parallel costs about the same as sampling one token, since weight loading is amortized once. This is the physical fact all of speculative decoding exploits: *per-step FLOPs can be traded for fewer steps* [5].

### 2.2 Classical rejection sampling

Rejection sampling draws exact samples from a target distribution *p* using a proposal *q* that is cheap to sample. The textbook scheme accepts a proposal *x ∼ q* with probability *p(x)/(M·q(x))* for a bound *M ≥ sup p/q*, and the accepted samples are distributed exactly as *p*. Speculative decoding adapts this idea to the sequential setting: the draft model plays the role of *q*, the target model the role of *p*, and the acceptance test is applied token-by-token along the proposed continuation [1][2]. Because the test needs *p(x)* at every draft position, a single parallel target pass supplies all the required densities — this is why the method's verification phase is naturally parallel.

### 2.3 Greedy versus stochastic decoding

Two regimes matter. Under *greedy* decoding, acceptance is deterministic — a draft token is accepted iff it equals the target's argmax — and output is bit-for-bit identical to target-only greedy generation [1]. Under *stochastic* sampling, Chen et al.'s scheme [2] preserves the *distribution*: speculative and direct samples are statistically indistinguishable up to hardware numerics. Throughout, "acceptance rate *α*" denotes the per-token acceptance probability, which degrades as sampling temperature rises.

### 2.4 Related acceleration paradigms

Speculative decoding is orthogonal to — and composable with — quantization, pruning, and distillation [6], and distinct from *non-autoregressive* generation, which changes the output distribution. The Jacobi-decoding line [5] achieves parallelism *without any draft model* by reformulating greedy decoding as a nonlinear system solved by Jacobi fixed-point iteration (§4.4).

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The draft–verify loop

Let *M_p* be the target model with conditional distributions *pᵢ(·) = p(· | prefix, x₁…xᵢ₋₁)* and *M_q* the draft model with *qᵢ(·)*. Fix a draft length *γ ≥ 1*. One round of speculative sampling proceeds as follows [2]:

1. **Draft.** Sample *γ* tokens autoregressively from the draft model: *xᵢ ∼ qᵢ(·)* for *i = 1…γ*.
2. **Verify.** Run the target model *once* on the concatenation *prefix + x₁…x_γ* to obtain *p₁…p_{γ+1}* in parallel (position *γ+1* supplies the "bonus" distribution).
3. **Accept/reject.** For *i = 1…γ* in order, draw *rᵢ ∼ Uniform(0,1)* and accept *xᵢ* if

   *rᵢ < min(1, pᵢ(xᵢ) / qᵢ(xᵢ))*.

   Let *n* be the number of consecutively accepted tokens.
4. **Correct.** If *n < γ* (some *x_{n+1}* rejected), sample one final token *t ∼ norm(max(0, p_{n+1} − q_{n+1}))* and emit *x₁…x_n, t*. If all *γ* drafts are accepted, sample the bonus token *t ∼ p_{γ+1}* and emit *x₁…x_γ, t*.

Every round therefore emits between 1 and *γ+1* tokens while invoking the target model exactly once: the draft phase costs *γ* cheap serial steps, and verification costs one expensive parallel step.

```python
def speculative_sampling_round(prefix, draft_model, target_model, gamma):
    """One draft-verify round. Returns the accepted token block."""
    # 1. Draft: gamma cheap autoregressive steps
    drafts, q_dists = [], []
    ctx = prefix
    for _ in range(gamma):
        q = draft_model.next_dist(ctx)          # q_i(·)
        x = sample(q)
        drafts.append(x); q_dists.append(q); ctx = ctx + [x]
    # 2. Verify: ONE parallel target pass over all positions
    p_dists = target_model.batch_dists(prefix, drafts)  # p_1..p_{gamma+1}
    # 3-4. Accept/reject with resampling on first rejection
    out = []
    for i, (x, q) in enumerate(zip(drafts, q_dists)):
        p = p_dists[i]
        if random() < min(1.0, p[x] / q[x]):
            out.append(x)                        # accepted
        else:
            out.append(sample(norm(max(0, p - q))))  # corrected, stop
            return out
    out.append(sample(p_dists[gamma]))            # bonus token
    return out
```

### 3.2 Why the acceptance test preserves the target distribution

The scheme is sequential *modified* rejection sampling [2]: at each position, *x ∼ q* is accepted with probability min(1, *p(x)/q(x)*), else resampled from norm(max(0, *p − q*)). A short calculation (§5.1) shows the emitted token is distributed exactly as *p* — the draft model introduces no bias [1][2] — and left-to-right conditioning extends this to the full sequence by induction.

> **Theorem (Distribution preservation, Chen et al. [2]):** For any prefix, the block of tokens emitted by one speculative-sampling round has exactly the same distribution as the block produced by sampling the target model *M_p* autoregressively for the same number of steps (up to hardware numerics).

### 3.3 Throughput theory: acceptance rate and draft length

Let *α ∈ [0,1]* be the expected per-token acceptance probability (assumed stationary for analysis) and let *c ∈ (0,1)* be the relative cost of one draft-model step versus one target-model step. The number of tokens emitted per round is *min(n+1, γ+1)* where *n ∼ Geometric*-like with *P(n ≥ k) = αᵏ*; its expectation is the truncated geometric sum

*E[#tokens] = Σ_{k=0}^{γ} αᵏ = (1 − α^{γ+1}) / (1 − α)*.

A round costs *γ·c + 1* target-equivalent steps, yielding the wall-clock improvement factor derived by Leviathan et al. [1]:

*Speedup(γ) = (1 − α^{γ+1}) / ((1 − α)(γ·c + 1))*.

Three consequences follow. **First**, speedup is monotone in *α*: draft quality is the dominant lever, which is why draft-model *alignment* with the target matters more than raw draft-model perplexity [6]. **Second**, the optimal draft length *γ** balances diminishing acceptance (*α^γ* decays) against linear draft cost; in practice *γ ∈ [3, 8]* is typical [1][2][6]. **Third**, if *c → 0* (free drafting, e.g. retrieval-based drafts), the speedup approaches *1/(1−α)*, the mean run length of accepted tokens. This formula also explains the failure mode: when *α* is small and *c* non-negligible, speculation can *slow down* decoding, so production systems adapt *γ* dynamically [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Draft models: alignment, cost models, and distillation

The original formulation uses an *independent* draft model: a small LM from the same model family as the target, sharing the tokenizer, so that no retraining is required [1]. Leviathan et al. demonstrate with T5-small drafting for T5-XXL, reporting 2–3× speedups with identical outputs [1]; Chen et al. use a small Chinchilla variant to draft for the 70B target, reaching 2–2.5× on HumanEval and XSum in a distributed TPU setup [2].

A key finding [6] is that *draft accuracy for speculative decoding is not language-modeling accuracy*: what matters is the expected acceptance probability *α*, i.e. the total-variation alignment between draft and target conditionals. Distilled or wide-shallow drafters systematically beat off-the-shelf small LMs of equal latency, because draft-phase cost is dominated by depth (serial steps) while width parallelizes [6].

### 4.2 Verification via modified rejection sampling

Two refinements matter in practice. **Typical acceptance** (Medusa [3]) generalizes the test for approximately aligned draft heads, trading strict losslessness for higher acceptance. **Multi-candidate verification** extends the test to draft *trees*: one target pass scores every node in parallel via a tree-attention mask, and the accepted prefix is the longest surviving root-to-leaf path [3][4]; the resampling correction at the first divergence point preserves losslessness whenever the standard test is used [4].

### 4.3 Tree-structured drafts and self-drafting: Medusa, EAGLE, and layer skipping

**Medusa** [3] eliminates the separate draft model entirely. *K* lightweight decoding heads are attached to the target's final hidden state; head *k* predicts the token at position *t+k+1* in parallel. Candidate continuations are assembled into a tree and verified with tree attention in one target pass. Two training recipes exist: *Medusa-1* fine-tunes only the heads on a frozen backbone (lossless), while *Medusa-2* jointly fine-tunes heads and backbone for higher head accuracy at the cost of a special training recipe [3]. Reported speedups are 2.2–2.8× on Vicuna-class models [3][6].

**EAGLE** [4] observes that autoregression is *easier at the feature level than at the token level*: predicting the second-to-top-layer feature vector *f_{i+1}* is a smoother regression problem than predicting the discrete token *t_{i+1}*. A tiny draft module (a single decoder layer) autoregressively predicts *f_{i+1}* from *(f_i, t_{i+1})* — the token sequence shifted one step ahead, which resolves the feature-level uncertainty — and the LM head converts features to draft tokens [4]. Combined with dynamic tree drafting, EAGLE achieves ~3× on MT-bench and 2.7–3.5× latency speedup on LLaMA2-Chat 70B, doubling throughput while preserving the output distribution [4].

**Self-speculative decoding** goes further: the draft model *is* the target model with a subset of intermediate layers skipped. Zhang et al. (2023) select the skipped layers by Bayesian optimization to maximize draft speed at acceptable accuracy loss, then verify with the full model [6]. Related designs include *Kangaroo* (a shallow sub-network plus a lightweight adapter bridging the representation gap), *LayerSkip* (early-exit drafting at dynamic depths), and *SPEED* (parameter sharing across decoder layers so early predictions reuse computation) [6]. The appeal is deployment simplicity — one set of weights in memory — at the price of draft accuracy that degrades once too many layers are dropped [6].

### 4.4 Draft-free speculation: Jacobi decoding and Lookahead decoding

Jacobi decoding reformulates greedy decoding as solving the nonlinear system *yᵢ = argmax p(· | y_<i, x)* via fixed-point iteration: starting from a random *n*-token guess, all positions are updated in parallel each iteration, and the fixed point provably equals the greedy autoregressive output [5]. Vanilla Jacobi decoding barely accelerates in practice (~1.05×), because one wrong early token poisons later positions along the trajectory.

**Lookahead decoding** [5] rescues the idea with two branches. The *lookahead branch* runs Jacobi-style iterations inside a fixed 2-D window (*W* lookahead steps × *N* positions), harvesting disjoint *n*-grams from the trajectory into a pool. The *verification branch* then verifies promising cached *n*-grams against the true prefix in parallel, integrating accepted ones into the output. No draft model, no training data, no distribution change: Lookahead decoding reports up to 1.8× on MT-bench and 4× with strong scaling across multiple GPUs on code completion [5]. It exemplifies the "trade per-step log(FLOPs) for fewer steps" principle in its purest form.

### 4.5 Single-model streaming and retrieval drafts

**Speculative streaming** [7] fuses speculation and verification into one stream: a single model trained with a multi-token objective concurrently speculates and verifies, removing the two-model memory footprint [7]. **Retrieval-based drafting** replaces the neural drafter with datastore lookup over the prompt and history — *c ≈ 0* in the throughput formula — exact-lossless and strong in retrieval-heavy domains, weak elsewhere [6]. Xia et al. [6] organize these as points in a design space spanned by *draft source*, *verification structure* (linear vs. tree), and *acceptance rule*.

## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Formal proof of losslessness (sequential rejection sampling)

We formalize the §3.2 argument. Fix a prefix and let *p, q* be the target and draft next-token distributions.

> **Theorem:** Let *x ∼ q*. Accept *x* with probability *min(1, p(x)/q(x))*; otherwise emit *x′ ∼ r* where *r(v) = max(0, p(v) − q(v)) / Z* and *Z = Σ_v max(0, p(v) − q(v))*. Then the emitted token has distribution exactly *p*.

*Proof.* The emission probability of token *v* decomposes into the accept and reject paths:

*P(emit v) = q(v)·min(1, p(v)/q(v)) + P(reject)·r(v)*.

Now *q(v)·min(1, p(v)/q(v)) = min(q(v), p(v))*. The rejection probability is

*P(reject) = Σ_v q(v)·(1 − min(1, p(v)/q(v))) = Σ_v max(0, q(v) − p(v))*.

Since *Σ_v (q(v) − p(v)) = 0*, we have *Σ_v max(0, q(v)−p(v)) = Σ_v max(0, p(v)−q(v)) = Z*, so *P(reject) = Z* and the second term equals *max(0, p(v) − q(v))*. Summing: *min(q(v),p(v)) + max(0, p(v)−q(v)) = p(v)*. ∎

By induction over positions — each step's prefix distribution matches the target's by the induction hypothesis — the whole emitted block matches autoregressive sampling from *M_p* [1][2]. In floating point, the equality holds *within hardware numerics*; Chen et al. verify empirically that benchmark metrics (XSum ROUGE, HumanEval pass@k) are at parity between speculative and direct sampling [2].

### 5.2 Empirical record

| Method | Draft source | Lossless | Reported speedup | Setting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Speculative decoding [1] | T5-small (independent) | Yes | 2–3× | T5-XXL, T5X |
| Speculative sampling [2] | Small Chinchilla (independent) | Yes (up to numerics) | 2–2.5× | Chinchilla 70B, HumanEval/XSum, TPU |
| Medusa-1 / Medusa-2 [3] | *K* decoding heads | Yes / approx. | 2.2–2.8× | Vicuna 7B/13B/33B |
| EAGLE [4] | Feature-level draft module + tree | Yes | 2.7–3.5× (3× MT-bench) | LLaMA2-Chat 70B; 160 tok/s on 13B (RTX 3090) |
| Lookahead decoding [5] | Jacobi *n*-gram pool (draft-free) | Yes | 1.8× (4× multi-GPU) | MT-bench; code completion |
| Speculative streaming [7] | Single fused model | Yes | ~2× | Stream-fused, no auxiliary model |

Several patterns emerge [6]. **First**, *α* is task-dependent: low-entropy generation (code, summarization) yields *α ≈ 0.7–0.9* and large speedups; high-temperature chat yields less. **Second**, tree verification beats linear drafts at fixed budgets, since the verifier scores multiple continuations per position for nearly the same parallel cost [3][4]. **Third**, feature-level drafting (EAGLE) defines the lossless Pareto frontier, because predicting continuous features is easier than predicting discrete tokens [4]. **Fourth**, benchmark metrics hold at parity, confirming losslessness in practice [1][2][3][4].

## 6 Limitations

1. **The batch-size wall.** The entire speedup argument rests on decoding being memory-bandwidth bound. As batch size grows, arithmetic intensity rises and decoding becomes compute bound; the "free" parallel verification is no longer free, and speedups shrink toward 1×. Speculative decoding is therefore primarily a *low-batch, latency-sensitive* serving optimization [2][6].
2. **Draft overhead and the *c* term.** Every round pays *γ* serial draft steps. If the draft model is too large relative to the target (large *c*), or *α* is too low, the throughput formula of §3.3 predicts slowdowns. Adaptive draft-length controllers mitigate but do not eliminate this [6].
3. **Temperature and entropy sensitivity.** Acceptance probability *α = E[min(1, p/q)]* degrades as sampling temperature rises, because the draft and target distributions diverge. High-temperature creative generation benefits least; greedy and low-temperature sampling benefit most [1][2].
4. **Numerics and "exact" losslessness.** The distribution-preservation proofs assume exact arithmetic. In practice, different computation graphs (parallel verification vs. serial sampling) produce different floating-point rounding, so outputs match *within hardware numerics* rather than bit-for-bit under stochastic sampling [2].
5. **Long-context and KV-cache pressure.** Tree-structured drafts multiply the KV cache footprint during verification, and self-speculative methods must manage two cache views (draft-skipped vs. full). At very long contexts, verification — not drafting — becomes the bottleneck [6].
6. **Engineering complexity.** Tree attention masks, adaptive draft-length scheduling, and continuous batching all interact with speculative decoding, and Medusa-2's joint fine-tuning needs a careful recipe to avoid perturbing base-model capabilities [3][6].

## 7 Conclusion

Speculative decoding reframes LLM inference as *converting idle parallel compute into fewer sequential steps*. Its core is small — a draft model proposes, a target model verifies in parallel, and modified rejection sampling guarantees the output distribution is unchanged [1][2] — yet the field has built a rich design space around it: independent drafters [1][2], multi-head predictors [3], feature-level drafters [4], self-drafting [6], draft-free Jacobi schemes [5], and stream-fused models [7], systematized by recent surveys [6].

The verdict is clear: 2–3.5× latency reductions are achievable, losslessly, in the low-batch regime where serving latency hurts. The remaining challenges are systems challenges — batching, KV-cache management, adaptive draft-length control, and draft–target co-design. As accelerators widen the gap between memory bandwidth and compute, the logic of speculation only strengthens: whenever weights are expensive to load and FLOPs are cheap, *guessing cheaply and verifying in parallel* is the rational way to decode.
