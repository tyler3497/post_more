## 2 Background

### 2.1 The radar range equation and the linear FM chirp

A SAR transmits a long, frequency-modulated pulse — typically a linear FM (LFM) *chirp* — rather than a short high-power pulse. The transmitted waveform over the pulse duration *T_p* is

```
s_tx(τ) = A · rect(τ/T_p) · exp(j·π·K_r·τ²)
```

where *τ* is fast time, *K_r* is the chirp rate in Hz/s, and *rect* denotes the rectangular envelope. The bandwidth *B = K_r·T_p* determines the *range resolution* *ρ_r = c/(2B)* after pulse compression, while the pulse duration supplies the energy. A chirp with time-bandwidth product *BT_p ≫ 1* thus decouples resolution (set by bandwidth) from energy (set by duration) — the foundational bargain of modern radar [2].

Pulse compression is matched filtering: the received echo is correlated with a replica of the transmitted chirp. In the frequency domain the matched filter is *H(f) = S_tx*(f)*, and the compressed output is the autocorrelation of the chirp — a narrow sinc-like pulse with sidelobes at roughly −13 dB, suppressed further by spectral weighting (Hamming, Kaiser) at a modest resolution cost [2].

### 2.2 Geometry: stripmap, spotlight, and the Doppler history

In **stripmap** mode the antenna points broadside and the ground footprint sweeps a continuous strip; in **spotlight** mode the beam is steered to dwell on one scene, extending the illumination time and improving azimuth resolution at the cost of coverage [9]. Either way, the instantaneous slant range to a point target at closest approach *R_0* evolves as

```
R(η) = sqrt(R_0² + v²·η²)  ≈  R_0 + v²·η²/(2·R_0)
```

with slow time *η*. The quadratic term impresses a *parabolic phase history* on the echoes,

```
φ(η) = −4π·R(η)/λ  ≈  −4π·R_0/λ − 2π·K_a·η²
```

with azimuth FM rate *K_a = 2v²/(λR_0)*. The Doppler centroid *f_dc* — the mean Doppler frequency — is zero for exact broadside (zero-Doppler steering, as on Sentinel-1 and TerraSAR-X) and nonzero under squint; its estimation from data or orbit geometry is a classic SAR calibration task [2].

### 2.3 The range–Doppler heritage

The **range–Doppler algorithm** (RDA), formalized by Cumming and Wong, remains the pedagogical and practical backbone of SAR focusing [2]. Its three operations — range compression, range cell migration correction (RCMC), and azimuth compression — each admit clean frequency-domain implementations via the FFT, and the algorithm's structure is the template against which chirp scaling and Omega-K are compared. Recent work has even mapped the RDA onto quantum circuits, exploiting the quantum Fourier transform to reach *O(N)* gate complexity versus *O(N log N)* classically [1].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Two-dimensional matched filtering

After demodulation, the raw signal from a point target occupies a two-dimensional support: a delayed chirp in fast time *τ* whose envelope *migrates* across range bins as slow time *η* advances, multiplied by the azimuth quadratic phase. Focusing is the 2-D matched filter to this point-target response. The RDA factorizes the 2-D problem into a cascade of 1-D operations [2]:

1. **Range compression.** For each azimuth line, FFT in range, multiply by the range reference *H_r(f_τ) = S_tx*(f_τ)*, inverse FFT. Resolution becomes *c/(2B)*.
2. **Azimuth FFT and RCMC.** Transform to the range–Doppler domain. In this domain, the migration of a target's energy follows the curve *R(f_η) = R_0·sqrt(1 − (λf_η/(2v))²)⁻¹*-like relations; RCMC interpolates each Doppler line so the energy lies on a single range cell. This is the algorithm's geometric heart: it straightens the hyperbolic trajectories before azimuth compression.
3. **Azimuth compression.** Multiply by the azimuth matched filter *H_a(f_η) = exp(+j·π·f_η²/K_a)* (conjugate of the quadratic phase), then inverse FFT in azimuth. The output is the focused complex image.

```python
import numpy as np

def range_doppler_focus(raw, range_ref, az_ref, rcmc_lut):
    """Simplified range-Doppler focusing pipeline (RDA core)."""
    # 1. Range compression (matched filter in fast time)
    S = np.fft.fft(raw, axis=0)
    S *= range_ref[:, None]
    s_rc = np.fft.ifft(S, axis=0)
    # 2. Azimuth FFT -> range-Doppler domain
    S_rd = np.fft.fft(s_rc, axis=1)
    # 3. Range cell migration correction (interpolation)
    S_rcmc = apply_rcmc_interpolation(S_rd, rcmc_lut)
    # 4. Azimuth compression (matched filter in Doppler)
    S_rd *= az_ref[None, :]
    focused = np.fft.ifft(S_rcmc * az_ref[None, :], axis=1)
    return focused
```

The algorithm's cost is dominated by FFTs: *O(N log N)* per dimension, and its accuracy hinges on the RCMC interpolation kernel (sinc interpolation is the gold standard) and on Doppler parameter estimation [2].

### 3.2 Alternatives: chirp scaling, Omega-K, backprojection

| Algorithm | RCMC mechanism | Accuracy vs. squint | Complexity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Range–Doppler | Interpolation in range–Doppler | Degrades with high squint | *O(N log N)* |
| Chirp scaling (CSA) | Phase multiply (no interpolation) | Handles moderate squint | *O(N log N)* |
| Omega-K (RMA) | Stolt interpolation in 2-D frequency | Exact for straight flight | *O(N log N)* |
| Backprojection | Time-domain coherent summation | Exact, arbitrary trajectory | *O(N³)*, highly parallel |

The **chirp scaling algorithm** replaces RCMC interpolation with a clever phase multiplication exploiting the chirp's structure [2]. The **Omega-K (range migration) algorithm** performs a Stolt mapping in the two-dimensional frequency domain, exactly inverting the wave equation under the straight-track approximation — it descends from seismic migration [3]. **Backprojection** coherently sums each echo onto the image grid directly; it is exact for arbitrary (nonlinear) trajectories and the method of choice for airborne and drone SAR, at brutal computational cost mitigated by fast-factorized variants and GPUs [3].

### 3.3 From image to interferogram

Given two focused complex images *s₁, s₂* of the same scene from slightly different orbits, the interferogram is

```
I = s₁ · conj(s₂),    φ_int = arg(I) = φ₁ − φ₂   (modulo 2π)
```

The interferometric phase decomposes as

```
φ_int = φ_flat + φ_topo + φ_disp + φ_atmo + φ_noise
```

where the *flat-Earth* term (from the reference ellipsoid) and the *topographic* term depend on the perpendicular baseline *B_⊥*:

```
φ_topo = −4π·B_⊥·h / (λ·R·sin θ)
```

for terrain height *h* and incidence angle *θ*, while the displacement term is *φ_disp = −4π·Δd/λ* for line-of-sight motion *Δd* between passes [8]. Differential InSAR (D-InSAR) removes topography with an external DEM or a third acquisition, isolating deformation at the millimeter scale — the basis of modern volcano, earthquake, and subsidence monitoring [8].

### 3.4 Phase unwrapping

Every interferometric phase is known only modulo 2π; recovering the absolute phase field is **two-dimensional phase unwrapping**. The classical Goldstein branch-cut algorithm identifies *residues* — points where the wrapped gradient sums to ±2π around a loop — and connects them with cuts that integration paths may not cross [7]. Chen and Zebker's network formulation showed the minimum-*L₀* version of the problem is **NP-hard**, and derived minimum-cost-flow approximations (the SNAPHU family) that dominate operational processing today [7]. Unwrapping fails catastrophically in decorrelated regions, which is why interferogram filtering (the Goldstein filter and its descendants) and coherence masking are inseparable from the unwrapping step [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The ambiguity function and waveform design

The matched filter's performance is governed by the **ambiguity function** — the 2-D autocorrelation of the waveform in delay and Doppler. The LFM chirp's ambiguity is a tilted ridge: excellent range resolution coupled to a range–Doppler coupling that is harmless for SAR's modest target velocities but must be accounted for in high-squint and moving-target modes. Waveform design trades sidelobe structure against this coupling; nonlinear FM and phase-coded waveforms reshape the ridge at the cost of SNR loss from mismatched filtering [2]. The essential inequality is the radar uncertainty principle: the delay–Doppler resolution cell area is bounded below by the reciprocal of the time-bandwidth product, so only *BT_p ≫ 1* waveforms earn fine resolution in both dimensions simultaneously.

### 4.2 Doppler parameter estimation and motion compensation

The azimuth matched filter demands the Doppler centroid *f_dc* and FM rate *K_a* to within tight tolerances — a centroid error of a few percent of the PRF visibly defocuses the image. Two routes exist: *geometry-based* estimation from precise orbit ephemerides and the antenna pointing model, and *data-based* estimation via the average cross-correlation (ACCC) or the map-drift algorithm [2]. Airborne SAR adds **motion compensation**: the ideal straight track is replaced by the measured trajectory, and second-order MoCo resamples the data to a reference track before focusing — without it, backprojection is the only exact option [3]. Spaceborne systems like Sentinel-1 steer to zero Doppler, simplifying the problem but not eliminating it: TOPS (Terrain Observation by Progressive Scans) bursts introduce a strongly azimuth-variant Doppler centroid that the processor must track burst by burst [9].

### 4.3 Algorithmic complexity and the quantum turn

Classical SAR focusing is FFT-bound at *O(N log N)*, and for modern wide-swath sensors *N* reaches billions of samples per scene — a genuine supercomputing workload. The quantum range–Doppler proposal of Giovagnoli, Huber, and Krieger maps the reference-function multiplications onto quantum gates and replaces the FFTs with the quantum Fourier transform, arguing for *O(N)* gate complexity in the focusing core [1]. The catch, as with all quantum signal processing, is data loading: the QRAM assumption required to prepare the raw-data state efficiently is far from practical. Still, the exercise is illuminating — it exposes exactly which parts of the RDA are *linear unitary* (the FFTs and diagonal phase multiplies, all quantum-friendly) and which are not (the RCMC interpolation, a non-unitary resampling). Any future quantum SAR processor must confront the interpolation problem head-on [1].

### 4.4 SAR tomography: focusing in elevation

A single SAR image collapses the elevation dimension: all scatterers at the same range and azimuth — the ground, a building façade, a roof — superimpose in one pixel (the *layover* problem). **SAR tomography** adds a second synthetic aperture in the *elevation* direction by combining *N* acquisitions with different baselines, then performs spectral estimation along the baseline axis to recover the reflectivity profile *γ(s)* in elevation *s* [6].

The multi-baseline signal model is a sum of complex exponentials:

```
g_n = ∫ γ(s) · exp(−j·2π·ξ_n·s) ds,    n = 1..N
```

with spatial frequencies *ξ_n = 2·b_n/(λR)* set by the baselines *b_n*. This is a line-spectral estimation problem, and the full arsenal applies [4][5]:

- **Beamforming** — the Fourier method; robust, resolution-limited to the Rayleigh bound *ρ_s = λR/(2B)* with elevation aperture *B*.
- **Capon (MVDR)** — adaptive, suppresses sidelobes using the sample covariance.
- **MUSIC / SVD-based** — super-resolution for well-separated scatterers, fragile under model mismatch.
- **Compressive sensing (ℓ₁)** — exploits the sparsity of urban elevation profiles; Zhu and Bamler demonstrated that ℓ₁ inversion resolves double scatterers (façade + ground) well below the Rayleigh limit, a result since validated on TerraSAR-X stacks over cities [4].

The first airborne demonstration by Reigber and Moreira used multibaseline L-band data to separate canopy from ground in forests [6]; spaceborne extensions with TerraSAR-X and TanDEM-X now produce 4-D (space + deformation velocity) point clouds of entire cities with LiDAR-comparable density [4]. Differential tomography further separates scatterers by their *motion* as well as height, disentangling thermal dilation of a building from ground subsidence beneath it [6]. The elevation aperture of repeat-pass stacks is irregular and sparse, so the Cramér–Rao bounds for height accuracy degrade gracefully rather than catastrophically — but ambiguity sidelobes from regular baseline sampling remain the practitioner's constant adversary [4][6].

### 4.5 Persistent scatterers and the time-series revolution

Where classical D-InSAR decorrelates over vegetated or long-temporal-baseline scenes, **persistent (permanent) scatterer interferometry** identifies pixels whose phase is stable across dozens of acquisitions — buildings, rock outcrops, infrastructure — and estimates their displacement histories jointly, suppressing atmospheric artifacts by spatio-temporal filtering [10]. The Ferretti–Prati–Rocca formulation treats the stack as an estimation problem in the joint baseline–time domain, achieving millimeter-per-year velocity accuracy over entire metropolitan areas [10]. Small-baseline (SBAS) variants complement it in rural terrain. Together, multi-temporal InSAR turned the SAR archive into a *retrospective geodetic network*: the 2015 Nepal earthquake, the slow inflation of Campi Flegrei, and subsidence from groundwater extraction are all routinely measured from orbit [8][10].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

**The Sentinel-1 constellation as proof of scale.** Sentinel-1A/B (C-band, 5.405 GHz) image the Earth in Interferometric Wide swath mode — 250 km swath at 5×20 m resolution — with a 6-day repeat (12-day single satellite), free and open data, and an orbit tube maintained to enable systematic interferometry [9]. The mission's TOPS mode solved the ScanSAR scalloping problem by steering the beam backward-to-forward within each burst, at the cost of the azimuth-variant Doppler centroid noted in §4.2; operational processors handle it by deramping each burst before focusing [9].

**Quantitative anchors.** (i) ERS-1/2 tandem pairs (1-day repeat, 1995–2000) demonstrated differential interferometry's sensitivity: the 1992 Landers earthquake displacement field was recovered with centimeter accuracy, validating the *φ_disp = −4πΔd/λ* relation against GPS [8]. (ii) TerraSAR-X staring-spotlight TomoSAR over Las Vegas and Berlin resolved double scatterers separated by under 2 m in elevation using ℓ₁ inversion on ~30-image stacks, matching the super-resolution predictions [4]. (iii) PS-InSAR over Milan and Los Angeles measured subsidence rates with ~1 mm/yr precision, cross-validated against leveling [10].

**A proof sketch: the L/2 resolution.** The Doppler bandwidth spanned during illumination is *B_d = 2v·sin(θ_bw/2)/λ ≈ 2v·(L/(2R))/λ* for beamwidth *L/R*... more precisely, the along-track beam extent gives *B_d = 2v/L*. Azimuth compression is a matched filter to the quadratic phase of bandwidth *B_d* observed over *T_obs*; the compressed pulse width in time is *1/B_d*, i.e. *v/B_d = L/2* in distance. The wavelength and range cancel — the celebrated range-independence of SAR azimuth resolution [2].

> **Theorem (InSAR height of ambiguity):** For perpendicular baseline *B_⊥*, the topographic phase wraps once per height *h_2π = λR sinθ/(2B_⊥)*. Long baselines increase height sensitivity but also geometric decorrelation; the *critical baseline* *B_crit = λR·tanθ/(2ρ_r)* marks total decorrelation.

**Complexity accounting.** For an *N_r × N_a* raw matrix, the RDA performs *N_a* range FFTs of length *N_r*, one *N_a*-point FFT per range line, RCMC interpolation at *O(K·N)* with kernel half-length *K*, and the azimuth matched filter: total *Θ(N log N)* with small constants [2]. Backprojection costs *Θ(N_r·N_a·N_pulses)* — prohibitive for stripmap but exact and embarrassingly parallel, hence its dominance in GPU-accelerated airborne processors [3].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Decorrelation.** Temporal change (vegetation growth, soil moisture), geometric baseline decorrelation, and thermal noise all degrade interferometric coherence; below *|γ| ≈ 0.3* unwrapping becomes unreliable and displacement estimates are meaningless [8].
2. **Atmospheric phase screens.** Tropospheric water vapor imposes centimeter-scale phase delays correlated over kilometers, easily mistaken for deformation. Mitigation requires weather models, GNSS calibration, or long time series [10].
3. **The unwrapping bottleneck.** Phase unwrapping is NP-hard in its exact minimum-*L₀* form [7]; operational algorithms (SNAPHU and descendants) are excellent heuristics but fail on disconnected coherent regions, and no unwrapping algorithm can recover information destroyed by decorrelation.
4. **Layover and shadow.** Side-looking geometry folds steep terrain into layover and hides backsides in shadow; only multi-aspect acquisitions or tomography resolve these [6].
5. **TomoSAR aperture poverty.** Repeat-pass stacks offer few, irregularly spaced baselines; elevation resolution is typically an order of magnitude worse than range–azimuth resolution, and super-resolution methods trade resolution for sensitivity to model error [4][5].
6. **Computation.** Exact backprojection remains *O(N³)*-class; even FFT-based processors strain under new wide-swath, high-resolution modes, motivating the quantum and GPU research directions [1][3].
7. **Moving targets.** The stationary-world assumption defocuses or displaces moving targets (trains appear shifted in azimuth); GMTI modes exist but complicate the processor [2].

## 7 Conclusion

Synthetic aperture radar is a triumph of coherent signal processing: from the humble linear FM chirp, through the two-dimensional matched filter of the range–Doppler algorithm and its chirp-scaling and Omega-K descendants [2], to the interferometric phase that turned satellites into millimeter geodetic instruments [8], and finally to tomographic stacks that un-collapse the elevation dimension [4][6]. Each step up this ladder demanded a deeper inversion — matched filtering, then phase unwrapping with its NP-hard core [7], then sparse spectral estimation — and each delivered a new geophysical observable. The open frontiers are computational (quantum and accelerated focusing [1][3]), statistical (super-resolution under model mismatch [4][5]), and operational (atmospheric correction for global deformation mapping [9][10]). The Earth's surface, viewed through coherent microwaves, has never been more measurable — nor the mathematics of measuring it more demanding.
