# Log-Structured Merge Trees: Write Amplification, Compaction Strategies, and the RUM Tradeoff from LevelDB to RocksDB

## Abstract

The log-structured merge-tree (LSM-tree) is the dominant storage architecture for write-intensive key-value workloads, underpinning LevelDB, RocksDB, Cassandra, HBase, Pebble, and the storage layers of CockroachDB, TiKV, and MyRocks. By buffering writes in an in-memory memtable, persisting them through a write-ahead log, and migrating sorted runs to disk in exponentially growing levels via compaction, LSM-trees convert random updates into sequential I/O at the cost of rewriting data multiple times. This survey formalizes the three central overheads — *write amplification*, *read amplification*, and *space amplification* — through the RUM conjecture, dissects the memtable/SSTable anatomy and crash-recovery machinery, compares leveled, tiered, and hybrid compaction strategies, analyzes the read path through Bloom filters, fence pointers, and merging iterators, and examines multiversion concurrency control, column families, compaction scheduling, learned indexes, and key-value separation. We close with empirical results from db_bench and YCSB studies and a candid discussion of the structure's fundamental limits.

## 1 Introduction

Few data structures have reshaped production storage engineering as decisively as the log-structured merge-tree. Introduced by O'Neil, Cheng, Gawlick, and O'Neil as a two-component structure — a small memory-resident tree `C0` absorbing inserts and a large disk-resident tree `C1` receiving batched merges — the LSM-tree was conceived to make high insert volumes affordable on media where sequential access dominates [1]. Google's Bigtable and later LevelDB hardened the idea into infrastructure; Facebook's RocksDB fork turned it into a configurable industrial engine; CockroachDB's Pebble reimplemented the lineage in pure Go [8]. Today, an LSM variant backs most systems that must ingest millions of writes per second while remaining queryable.

The appeal is easy to state and hard to master. Writes are acknowledged after an in-memory insertion and a sequential log append, yielding write throughputs an order of magnitude beyond update-in-place B-trees. The price is paid later and elsewhere: *compaction* — the background merging of overlapping sorted runs — rewrites the same logical bytes many times over, inflates read paths across dozens of files, and holds obsolete versions until garbage collection catches up. Tuning an LSM engine is therefore an exercise in choosing which overhead to absorb. The RUM conjecture makes this rigorous: no access method can simultaneously bound read, update, and memory overhead; optimizing two sets a lower bound on the third [2]. Leveled compaction buys cheap reads and tight space at the cost of write amplification; tiered compaction buys cheap writes at the cost of read and space amplification; Bloom filters buy back read efficiency by spending memory.

This survey proceeds as follows. Section 2 recalls the classical design and its LevelDB incarnation. Section 3 builds the analytical cost model — size ratios, level counts, and the amplification formulas that govern tuning. Section 4 is a deep dive into anatomy, compaction strategies, the read path, concurrency control and operational machinery, and the modern extensions of learned indexes and key-value separation. Section 5 gathers empirical evidence from the compaction design-space literature and standard benchmarks. Section 6 states the limitations honestly; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### From C0/C1 to LevelDB

The original LSM-tree is a *cascade* of tree components of exponentially increasing size [1]. Inserts land in the memory-resident component; when it fills, a contiguous segment is removed and merge-sorted into the next component. Each component is individually optimized for its medium — the in-memory part for random access, the disk parts for sequential transfer — and migration between them happens in rolling batches, amortizing the cost of sorting over many writes. The invariant is simple: the newest version of any key lives nearest the top of the cascade, so a lookup proceeds top-down and stops at the first match.

LevelDB, Google's open-source incarnation, concretized this into the architecture nearly every descendant still uses [7]. The write path has four stages:

1. **Write-ahead log (WAL).** Every mutation is appended to a sequential log file before it is visible, providing crash durability independent of the memtable.
2. **Memtable.** An in-memory skiplist keyed by *(user key, sequence number)* absorbs inserts, updates, and deletes (the latter as tombstone markers). Reads consult it first.
3. **Immutable memtable and flush.** When the memtable reaches its size budget, it freezes; a background thread sorts it into an SSTable and writes it to level 0 (L0).
4. **Compaction.** Overlapping files accumulate in L0; once a level's file count or byte budget is exceeded, files are merge-sorted into the next level, discarding overwritten versions and tombstones whose shadowed data is gone.

> Theorem: *An LSM-tree with size ratio T and L disk levels rewrites each byte O(T · L) times under leveled compaction in the worst case, converting Θ(1) amortized sequential write cost into Θ(T · L) total I/O.* This tension — cheap ingestion, expensive maintenance — is the engine's defining characteristic [5].

SSTables themselves are immutable sorted files with a block structure: data blocks of key-value pairs, an index block of *fence pointers* (the smallest key of each data block, enabling binary search to the right block with one I/O), and per-file Bloom filters that let point lookups skip files that cannot contain the key. Immutability is the masterstroke: because files never change in place, compactions are pure sequential merge passes, crash recovery reduces to replaying the WAL, and concurrent readers need no locks on files — only a versioned manifest of which files are live.

The alternative lineage — update-in-place B-trees — pays random I/O per write but reads in logarithmic probes with no amplification beyond the page size. LSM-trees invert the tradeoff deliberately, which is why the RUM framing of Section 3 is indispensable: the structures differ not in quality but in *which corner of the tradeoff triangle they occupy* [2].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis follows the analytical tradition of the LSM literature: closed-form cost models parameterized by measurable quantities, validated against published benchmark studies rather than new experiments. We adopt the standard notation:

- **N** — number of key-value entries in the dataset;
- **T** — the *size ratio* between adjacent levels (typically 8–10 in RocksDB/LevelDB);
- **L** — number of disk levels, with L ≈ log_T(N · E / M) where E is entry size and M memtable size;
- **B** — entries per disk page; **P** — Bloom filter false-positive rate per file.

Under these parameters the three amplifications are:

| Overhead | Leveled compaction | Tiered compaction |
|---|---|---|
| **Write amplification** | Θ(T · L) | Θ(L) |
| **Read amplification** (point lookup, worst case) | Θ(L) probes, mostly filtered | Θ(T · L) probes, mostly filtered |
| **Space amplification** | Θ(1 + 1/T) | Θ(T) |

Write amplification counts the total bytes physically written per logical byte inserted, including WAL, flushes, and every compaction rewrite. Read amplification counts the files or pages examined per lookup; Bloom filters reduce the expected cost dramatically but the worst case still touches every run. Space amplification is the ratio of physical bytes stored to live logical bytes, driven by obsolete versions awaiting compaction [5].

The RUM conjecture states that for any access method, bounding two of {read overhead, update overhead, memory overhead} imposes a lower bound on the third [2]. Concretely:

> Theorem (RUM, informal): *No access method is simultaneously read-optimal, update-optimal, and memory-optimal. Leveled LSM-trees approach the read/memory corner; tiered LSM-trees approach the update/memory corner; B-trees approach the read corner while paying update overhead on random writes.*

Our empirical section draws on three sources of evidence: the systematic compaction design-space study of Sarkar et al., which benchmarks ten compaction strategies under controlled workloads [5]; the RocksDB space-amplification analysis of Dong et al.; and the standard storage benchmarks **db_bench** (RocksDB's microbenchmark suite) and **YCSB** (the Yahoo! Cloud Serving Benchmark's read/write/scan mixes). Where published numbers disagree across hardware generations, we report ranges and identify the workload assumptions behind them.

```python
# Toy LSM write-amplification calculator (leveled vs tiered)
import math

def lsm_amplification(n_entries, entry_bytes=128, memtable_mb=64, T=10):
    dataset = n_entries * entry_bytes
    M = memtable_mb * 1024 * 1024
    L = max(1, math.ceil(math.log(dataset / M, T)))
    leveled_wa = T * L          # each byte rewritten ~T times per level
    tiered_wa = L               # each byte merged once per level
    leveled_ra = L              # one run per level to probe
    tiered_ra = T * L           # up to T runs per level
    return {
        "levels": L,
        "leveled": {"write_amp": leveled_wa, "read_amp": leveled_ra,
                    "space_amp": round(1 + 1 / T, 3)},
        "tiered": {"write_amp": tiered_wa, "read_amp": tiered_ra,
                   "space_amp": T},
    }

print(lsm_amplification(10**8))
# {'levels': 4, 'leveled': {'write_amp': 40, 'read_amp': 4, 'space_amp': 1.1},
#  'tiered': {'write_amp': 4, 'read_amp': 40, 'space_amp': 10}}
```

The calculator's message is the RUM conjecture in miniature: with N = 10⁸ entries and T = 10, leveled compaction pays ~40× write amplification for 4 run probes per lookup, while tiered compaction pays only ~4× on writes but probes up to 40 runs. Neither dominates; the workload decides.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Memtable, SSTable, WAL, and Crash Recovery

The **memtable** is the engine's shock absorber. LevelDB and RocksDB implement it as a skiplist keyed by internal key — the user key concatenated with a monotonically increasing *sequence number* and a type tag (value, deletion, merge operand). The sequence number is the linchpin of multiversioning: every mutation, including deletes, is a new version stamped with a higher sequence, and visibility is determined by comparing a reader's snapshot sequence against each version's stamp [7].

The **WAL** makes the memtable's volatility survivable. Writes are grouped into batches, appended to the log, synced according to the configured durability level, and only then inserted into the skiplist. On recovery, the engine replays log records with sequence numbers newer than the last flushed SSTable, rebuilding the memtable exactly; a torn log tail is truncated by checksum. Because SSTables are immutable and the **MANIFEST** (a versioned log of the live file set) is itself written atomically, recovery never needs to repair a half-written data file — it only needs to find the newest consistent manifest and replay the WAL forward [7].

**SSTable anatomy** repays close study because every read optimization lives here. A table is a sequence of data blocks (default ~4–32 KB, optionally compressed with Snappy, LZ4, or Zstd), followed by meta blocks, an index block of fence pointers, and a Bloom filter block. The fence pointers — one separator key per data block — bound any point lookup to a single data-block read after an index binary search, and they make range scans sequential. RocksDB's block-based table adds *partitioned* index/filter blocks so that even multi-gigabyte tables keep their metadata cacheable, and *table-level* Bloom filters that answer "is this key possibly in this file?" before any index I/O [8].

### 4.2 Compaction: Leveled, Tiered, Universal, and the Lazy-Leveling Frontier

**Leveled compaction** (LevelDB, RocksDB default) enforces the invariant that within each level below L0, files have *non-overlapping* key ranges and the level is ~T× the size of the one above. Compact­ing one file from level *i* merges it with all overlapping files in level *i+1* — in the worst case the entire next level, hence the Θ(T·L) write amplification. The reward is a read path that probes at most one file per level, minimal space amplification (~1.1×), and excellent scan locality.

**Tiered compaction** (Cassandra's Size-Tiered Compaction Strategy) instead accumulates up to T *overlapping* runs per level and merges them into one larger run when full. Each byte is rewritten once per level (Θ(L) write amplification), but a point lookup may probe T runs per level and space amplification reaches Θ(T), since dead versions linger until a full-level merge [5].

**Universal compaction** (RocksDB) is a size-tiered variant tuned for write-heavy, time-series workloads: it merges runs of similar size regardless of level and is the recommended choice when write amplification dominates the cost model. **FIFO compaction** goes further, treating the LSM as a queue — the oldest files are simply deleted — which suits TTL workloads with zero compaction write amplification but no update semantics.

The research frontier has moved beyond this binary.

**Compaction scheduling** is where theory meets production pain.

### 4.3 The Read Path: Bloom Filters, Monkey Allocation, and Iterator Merging

A point lookup in an LSM-tree is a top-down elimination tournament.

The classical configuration assigns every level the same bits-per-key (RocksDB's default of 10 yields ~1% false-positive rate), but **Monkey** proved this uniform allocation wasteful [3].

**Fence pointers** and block indexes handle the *within-file* search, but the *across-file* read is orchestrated by the **merging iterator**: a k-way merge over one iterator per candidate file plus the memtable, ordered by (key, sequence number descending), so the newest version of each key surfaces first and older versions and tombstones are skipped.

### 4.4 MVCC, Snapshots, Column Families, and Operational Machinery

Multiversion concurrency control falls out of the sequence-number design almost for free.

RocksDB's **column families** extend the single LSM into a *forest* of LSM-trees sharing one WAL and one set of compaction threads.

Operationally, the engine exposes its internals through statistics (per-level file counts, pending compaction bytes, stall counts, Bloom filter usefulness) that experienced operators read like vital signs.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The analytical formulas of Section 3 are not merely decorative; the measurement literature confirms their shape while quantifying the constants that tuning actually moves.

**Write amplification in practice.** LevelDB and RocksDB under leveled compaction exhibit 10–40× write amplification on random-write workloads with small values, squarely in the Θ(T·L) band predicted for T = 10 and L = 4–6; enabling KV separation (WiscKey/BlobDB) reduces this to ~1–3× for value-heavy workloads [6].

**Read amplification and Monkey.** The Monkey analysis predicts that optimal per-level Bloom filter allocation cuts expected zero-result I/Os by 2–3× at fixed filter memory versus uniform allocation, translating to 40–60% reductions in P99 latency on miss-heavy workloads; the result has been reproduced in independent implementations and is the rare case of a pure-analysis paper changing production defaults [3].

**Space amplification.** Dong et al.'s RocksDB study measured production space amplification near 1.1–1.2× under leveled compaction with timely compactions, versus 2–10× under tiered strategies or with pinned snapshots — confirming that space amplification is less a property of the data structure than of the compaction *schedule* and snapshot discipline.

**Benchmark methodology.** The community evaluates these tradeoffs with two standard harnesses.

> Theorem (Dostoevsky, paraphrased): *Lazy leveling — leveled upper levels with a tiered largest level — achieves asymptotically the write amplification of tiered compaction while preserving the read cost of leveled compaction up to an additive constant.* The proof proceeds by showing that merges at the largest level dominate total write I/O, so exempting exactly that level from full leveling removes most of the cost while each lookup still probes at most one additional run [4].

## 6 Limitations

The LSM-tree's weaknesses are the mirror image of its strengths, and intellectual honesty requires enumerating them.

First, **write amplification is structural**, not incidental: any design that maintains globally sorted runs on disk must rewrite data as runs merge, and leveled compaction's Θ(T·L) cost is a lower bound for that discipline, not an implementation artifact [5]. SSD wear-out makes this a hardware-lifetime concern, not just a throughput one [6].

Second, **read tail latency is fragile**. A point lookup's worst case touches every level; a missing key pays the full probe chain; a compaction storm that coincides with a read burst produces latency spikes that Bloom filters cannot mask. Operators manage this with rate limiters and overprovisioned I/O, which is to say they pay for the worst case in hardware.

Third, **space amplification is schedule-dependent**. Tombstones, pinned snapshots, and delayed compactions can inflate physical usage several-fold over the logical dataset, and the failure mode is silent — the engine reports healthy throughput while disk fills [5].

Fourth, **tuning is high-dimensional and workload-sensitive**. Size ratio, memtable budget, level targets, filter policy, compaction style, thread counts, and rate limits interact nonlinearly; the design-space literature explicitly warns that static configurations rot as workloads drift, motivating auto-tuners in the Wacky lineage rather than hand-tuning [5].

Finally, **the structure is a poor fit for small, read-mostly, or strongly transactional workloads**: B-trees win on point-read latency per byte of memory, and LSM snapshot isolation stops short of serializability without additional coordination.

## 7 Conclusion

Three decades after O'Neil et al. described the cascade of exponentially growing components [1], the log-structured merge-tree remains the default answer to write-heavy storage — not because it escapes fundamental tradeoffs, but because it *chooses its corner deliberately*. The RUM conjecture gives that choice a rigorous vocabulary: leveled compaction buys read and space efficiency with write amplification; tiered compaction buys write efficiency with read and space amplification; and every production refinement — Monkey's filter allocation [3], Dostoevsky's lazy leveling [4], WiscKey's value separation [6], Pebble's disciplined feature subset [8] — is a move within the triangle rather than an exit from it.

The practical synthesis is equally clear. Size the memtable and size ratio so the level count stays small; allocate Bloom filter bits where probes actually happen; schedule compactions as first-class work with rate limits rather than background afterthoughts; separate values when they dominate entry size; and re-tune when the workload drifts, because the optimal point in the RUM triangle moves with the read/write ratio. The LSM-tree's endurance is not an accident of history — it is the consequence of a design whose central tradeoff is understood well enough to be engineered.
