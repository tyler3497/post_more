# Incremental Stream Computation: Naiad's Timely Dataflow, Differential Dataflow, and the DBSP Formalism

## Abstract

We survey the intellectual lineage that transformed incremental computation from a collection of ad hoc caching strategies into a principled theory of streaming dataflow. Beginning with **Naiad** [1], the SOSP 2013 system by Murray et al., we examine *timely dataflow*, a computational model in which stateful vertices exchange logically timestamped messages over a directed graph that may contain nested cycles, coordinated by a distributed *progress-tracking protocol* over partially ordered timestamps. We then develop **differential dataflow** [3], McSherry et al.'s generalization of incremental computation to arbitrarily nested iteration, in which collections evolve as multisets of *(data, time, diff)* triples and operators act on *differences* rather than on states. Finally, we present the **DBSP** formalism of Budiu et al. [5], which recasts incremental view maintenance as differentiation and integration of streams over discrete time, yielding a compact operator algebra and a general incrementalization theorem for rich query languages. We compare these designs with Apache Flink and Spark Structured Streaming, and survey production evidence from Materialize and Feldera, concluding with open limitations in fault tolerance, memory footprint, and optimizer integration.

## 1. Introduction

Modern data infrastructure faces a three-way tension. Batch processors deliver high throughput but poor latency; stream processors deliver low latency but struggle with iteration and stateful aggregation; graph and ML frameworks iterate efficiently but cannot easily absorb streaming updates. The classical response has been *specialization*: a different engine per workload. Naiad [1] rejected this compromise. Presented at SOSP 2013 by Derek Murray, Frank McSherry, Rebecca Isaacs, Michael Isard, Paul Barham, and Martín Abadi, Naiad introduced **timely dataflow**, a single computational model claimed to combine the throughput of batch systems, the latency of stream systems, and the expressive power of iterative and incremental computation.

Two design decisions make timely dataflow remarkable. First, timestamps are drawn from a **partially ordered set** rather than a total order, so independent input epochs and loop iterations proceed concurrently without global coordination. Second, a lightweight **distributed progress-tracking protocol** computes each vertex's *frontier* — whether any message at or before a given timestamp could still arrive — enabling batch-like *consistency guarantees* under fully asynchronous execution.

Out of Naiad grew **differential dataflow** [3], presented at CIDR 2013 by McSherry, Murray, Isaacs, and Isard. Differential computation extends traditional incremental computation in two ways: computation state varies over a partially ordered set of *versions* rather than a totally ordered sequence, and the updates needed to reconstruct any version are retained in an indexed *difference trace* rather than folded into a "current" state and discarded. The consequence: iterative algorithms like strongly connected components can be maintained incrementally under changing inputs, with additions and deletions handled *symmetrically*.

A decade later, Budiu, McSherry, Ryzhyk, and Tannen [5] proposed **DBSP**, a formalism that distills differential dataflow into a small, mathematically rigorous operator algebra inspired by discrete digital signal processing. DBSP models databases as streams, views as stream operators, and incremental view maintenance (IVM) as *differentiation* followed by *integration*: given a query *Q*, its incremental version Δ*Q* satisfies *Q*(*I*(*x*)) = *I*(Δ*Q*(*x*)). The DBSP paper [5] received the **VLDB 2023 best paper award** and the **2024 ACM SIGMOD Research Highlights award**, and its Rust runtime powers Feldera's incremental SQL engine.

This thesis reconstructs these three systems from first principles, proves their central claims, compares them against contemporary streaming engines, and identifies where the theory still falls short.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Incremental view maintenance

Incremental view maintenance (IVM) is among the oldest problems in database theory [5]. Given a query *Q* over a database *D* and an update Δ*D*, the goal is to compute *Q*(*D* ⊕ Δ*D*) from *Q*(*D*) and Δ*D* in time proportional to the *change*, not the database. Classical solutions — counting algorithms, DRed, and their descendants — handle restricted fragments of the relational algebra and often treat deletions as second-class citizens: deleting a tuple may require re-derivation of everything it entailed, the *deletion problem* of Datalog materialization. Differential dataflow's central practical claim is that additions and deletions cost the same, because both are merely signed updates in a difference multiset.

### 2.2 The batch–stream–iteration trichotomy

Pre-Naiad systems partitioned the design space along three axes:

| Axis | Batch (MapReduce) | Streaming (Storm, S4) | Iterative (Pregel, Spark) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Latency | seconds–hours | milliseconds | seconds–minutes |
| Iteration | barrier-synchronized, expensive | unsupported | bulk-synchronous |
| Consistency | deterministic outputs | at-least-once, approximate | deterministic per superstep |
| Incremental updates | recompute | append-only windows | recompute or coarse deltas |

Naiad's thesis: these are not three systems but three *configurations* of one — timely dataflow subsumes batch, streaming, and iteration by varying the timestamp partial order and graph topology [1].

### 2.3 Partial orders and frontiers

A timestamp is a tuple *(e, i₁, …, iₖ)* where *e* is the input epoch and each *iⱼ* the iteration counter of the *j*-th nested loop, ordered by the product order. Timestamps from different epochs or incomparable iterations are *incomparable* — and incomparability is precisely what licenses concurrent execution: no ordering constraint, no coordination.

The **frontier** of a vertex is the set of minimal timestamps at which it might still receive messages. Naiad's progress-tracking protocol maintains conservative frontier approximations in a distributed fashion using *pointstamps* — *(location, timestamp)* pairs — letting each worker determine locally when a timestamp is *complete* [1].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is an **analytic reconstruction** rather than an empirical benchmark study. Our method is:

1. **Primary-source reconstruction.** We recover definitions — timely graphs, notifications, pointstamps, difference traces, the Δ and *I* operators — directly from the Naiad [1], differential dataflow [3], and DBSP [5] papers, cross-checked against publicly archived PDFs.
2. **Formal consolidation.** We restate the three systems' core theorems in uniform notation so their relationships (generalization, simplification, re-derivation) become visible.
3. **Comparative analysis.** We contrast the timely/differential/DBSP stack with Apache Flink and Spark Structured Streaming along six dimensions: timestamp model, iteration, incremental algebra, state representation, consistency, and production maturity.
4. **Evidence survey.** We collect reported evaluations and production deployments (Materialize, Feldera) to ground the theory in practice.

We do not re-run the original benchmarks; performance claims are attributed to their sources.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Timely Dataflow: Coordination Without Coordination

A timely dataflow program is a directed graph of **stateful operators** exchanging **timestamped messages**, distinguished by three primitives:

- **Structured loops.** Cycles pass through *loop-context* operators that extend timestamps with a fresh iteration coordinate, so data circulating in a loop is *distinguishable* from data entering it.
- **Asynchronous vertices.** A vertex may send messages at any time *t′ ≥ t* upon receiving a message at time *t*, with no global barrier — different epochs and loop iterations execute simultaneously.
- **Notifications.** A vertex may request a *notification* for time *t*: a promise it will be invoked once all messages with timestamp ≤ *t* have been delivered, giving blocking semantics inside a non-blocking runtime.

The **progress-tracking protocol** [1] is the key mechanism. Each worker tracks outstanding pointstamps *(target location, timestamp)* — undelivered messages plus operator "capabilities" to send future messages. Workers periodically exchange summaries and compute each vertex's *frontier*, the greatest lower bound of timestamps that might still produce messages. When the frontier advances past *t*, the vertex's notification for *t* fires. The protocol is fully distributed, leaderless, and its overhead scales with graph size and timestamp dimensionality, not data volume.

```rust
// Illustrative timely dataflow program (timely-dataflow Rust crate).
use timely::dataflow::operators::{Input, Inspect, Probe};

fn main() {
    timely::execute_from_args(std::env::args(), |worker| {
        let mut probe = timely::dataflow::operators::probe::Handle::new();
        let mut input = worker.dataflow(|scope| {
            let (input, stream) = scope.new_input::<u64>();
            stream.inspect(|x| println!("seen: {x}")).probe_with(&mut probe);
            input
        });
        for epoch in 0..10u64 {
            input.send(epoch);
            input.advance_to(epoch + 1);   // move the input frontier
            while probe.less_than(input.time()) { worker.step(); }
        }
    }).unwrap();
}
```

> **Theorem 1 (Notification soundness, Naiad [1]).** *A vertex receives its notification for timestamp t only after all messages with timestamps ≤ t addressed to it have been delivered, and no message with timestamp ≤ t will ever subsequently arrive.*

*Proof sketch.* The frontier is a lower bound on the timestamps of all outstanding pointstamps. A pointstamp *(v, t′)* is outstanding exactly when a message to *v* at time *t′*, or the capability to produce one, exists somewhere in the system. Notifications for *t* fire only when the frontier strictly dominates *t* — equivalently, no future message can bear a timestamp ≤ *t*. ∎

The practical upshot, reported in the paper's evaluation, is that a *single* Naiad deployment matched or beat specialized batch, streaming, and graph systems on their home workloads [1].

### 4.2 Differential Dataflow: Computation on Differences

Differential dataflow [3] is a *programming model* layered on timely dataflow. Its key move is to stop thinking of a collection as a *state* and start thinking of it as a *function of time*:

> *A collection C is a multiset of triples (data, time, diff), where diff ∈ ℤ records insertions (+1) and retractions (−1) at partially ordered logical times.*

The *value* of the collection at time *t* is the accumulation of all diffs at times ≤ *t*. Operators are defined *directly* on the difference representation, and the framework guarantees that applying operators to differences and then accumulating yields the same result as accumulating first — provided the operators are *linear* in the appropriate sense.

The operator suite mirrors relational algebra but speaks "diff":

- **`map` / `flat_map` / `filter`** — stateless; applied pointwise to each update.
- **`join`** — the crown jewel. It maintains indexed difference traces of both inputs per key and emits output updates from *co-occurring* input updates, never re-scanning full inputs: work is proportional to the *changes*. The implementation uses *arrangements* — shared, indexed, lazily-maintained keyed representations — so many downstream operators share one index.
- **`iterate`** — fixed-point iteration over a *nested* timestamp dimension. Each round is a new incomparable timestamp, so rounds pipeline concurrently, and the loop's output is itself a differential collection.
- **`consolidate`** — compacts update multisets by canceling opposite-signed diffs for identical *(data, time)* pairs, bounding trace memory.
- **`distinct` / `count` / `reduce`** — aggregation over difference multisets where retractions correctly *decrement* aggregates — the symmetric deletion handling classical IVM lacks.

```python
# A minimal difference-trace: the heart of differential dataflow,
# distilled to a multiset of (data, time, diff) triples.
from collections import Counter

class DifferenceTrace:
    def __init__(self):
        self.updates = Counter()          # (data, time) -> net diff

    def update(self, data, time, diff: int):
        self.updates[(data, time)] += diff

    def consolidate(self):
        # Cancel +1/-1 pairs; drop zeros to bound memory.
        self.updates = Counter({k: v for k, v in self.updates.items() if v != 0})

    def reconstruct(self, time):
        # C(t) = sum of diffs at times <= t  (the "accumulation" semantics)
        out = Counter()
        for (data, t), v in self.updates.items():
            if t <= time and v != 0:
                out[data] += v
        return {d: v for d, v in out.items() if v != 0}
```

Why partially ordered *versions* instead of a sequence? Consider iterative reachability under two concurrent input changes. With a total order, the second change must wait for the first's fixed point to converge. With a product order *(epoch, iteration)*, the two epochs' iterations are *incomparable* and their fixed-point computations *pipeline*: iteration *i* of epoch 2 reuses arrangement state built by iteration *i* of epoch 1, because differential operators share work across incomparable times automatically. This is the sense in which differential dataflow "extends traditional incremental computation to allow arbitrarily nested iteration" [3].

The CIDR 2013 evaluation's headline result: incrementally maintained **strongly connected components** on a live Twitter-scale graph — a computation the authors describe as previously intractable at interactive timescales — updated in milliseconds per batch of edge changes [3].

### 4.3 The DBSP Formalism: Incremental Computation as Calculus

DBSP [5] asks a disarmingly simple question: *what if incremental computation were just calculus on streams?* Borrow the machinery of discrete digital signal processing — streams, the delay operator *z⁻¹*, differentiation, integration — and apply it to databases.

**Definitions.** Fix discrete time *t ∈ ℕ*. A *stream* *s : ℕ → A* maps time to values forming a **commutative group** (relations under multiset union/difference qualify — the algebraic license for signed updates):

- **Differentiation** Δ: (Δ*s*)[*t*] = *s*[*t*] − *s*[*t*−1]. The derivative of a stream is the stream of *changes*.
- **Integration** *I*: (*I s*)[*t*] = Σ_{i≤t} *s*[*i*]; *I*(Δ*s*) = *s*.
- **Delay** *z⁻¹*: (*z⁻¹ s*)[*t*] = *s*[*t*−1], the unit delay for feedback loops (recursion).

A **DBSP circuit** is a composition of *lifted* operators — ordinary functions on values, lifted pointwise to streams — plus Δ, *I*, and *z⁻¹*. A database is the stream *DB*[*t*] of snapshots after *t* transactions; the transaction log is its derivative Δ*DB*.

> **Theorem 2 (General incrementalization, DBSP [5]).** *For every DBSP circuit Q built from lifted operators, Δ, I, and z⁻¹, there exists an incremental circuit ΔQ — constructed by mechanically pushing Δ inward — such that Q(I(x)) = I(ΔQ(x)) for all input streams x.*

*Proof sketch (structural induction, after [5]).* For a lifted stateless operator *f*, its output change at time *t* depends only on *s*[*t*] and *s*[*t*−1], both recoverable from the differentiated input plus one stored delay. For *z⁻¹*, differentiation commutes trivially. For nested circuits, the stream chain rule Δ(*g* ∘ *f*) = Δ*g* ∘ Δ*f* completes the induction. The construction is *syntax-directed* — hence it covers relational algebra, aggregation, flatmap/unnest, and monotone and non-monotone recursion, all expressible in DBSP [5]. ∎

**First**, IVM becomes a *compilation pass*, not a per-operator research project. **Second**, the formalism explains *why* differential dataflow works: DD's difference traces are streams, its operators are Δ-pushed circuits, and consolidation is algebraic simplification of Δ*s*. **Third**, the group-structure requirement pinpoints exactly which computations incrementalize cleanly: those whose values support signed differences.

### 4.4 Implementation Lineages: Timely in Rust, Materialize, Feldera

Frank McSherry reimplemented timely dataflow and differential dataflow in **Rust**, exploiting ownership and zero-cost abstractions for fine-grained, allocation-heavy operator logic. The Rust runtime uses *cooperative scheduling* within workers and lock-free exchange between them; arrangements became carefully laid-out indexed structures rather than garbage-collected maps.

**Materialize** commercialized the stack: timely dataflow as the distributed execution substrate, differential dataflow as the incremental operator library, and a PostgreSQL-compatible SQL planner on top. Its signature abstraction, the *arrangement*, is DD's shared index made first-class — a key → *(time, diff)* structure shared across views so *N* views over one source maintain *one* index, with a persist layer for durability beneath the in-memory traces. **Feldera** took the second path: a from-scratch **DBSP runtime in Rust** compiling SQL directly to DBSP circuits, then to incremental circuits via Theorem 2 — the same incrementalization pass serving relational, streaming, and recursive queries uniformly.

The table below situates these systems against mainstream streaming engines:

| Dimension | Spark Structured Streaming | Apache Flink | Timely / Differential (Materialize) | DBSP (Feldera) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time model | micro-batch epochs (total order) | event-time watermarks (total order) | partially ordered *(epoch, iteration…)* | discrete stream time *t ∈ ℕ* |
| Iteration | driver-side loops, coarse | limited (bulk/stream iterations) | nested loop contexts, pipelined | recursion via *z⁻¹* delay |
| Incremental algebra | stateful operators, manual | keyed state + timers, manual | automatic: operators on differences | automatic: Δ-pushing compilation |
| Deletions | retraction support varies | retractions via changelog streams | symmetric with insertions | group structure ⇒ symmetric |
| State backend | in-memory / RocksDB | RocksDB / heap | in-memory arrangements (+ persist) | in-memory traces (+ storage) |
| Consistency | exactly-once via checkpointing | exactly-once via checkpointing | frontier-based notifications | stream identity *Q*(*I*(*x*)) = *I*(Δ*Q*(*x*)) |
| Query language | DataFrame/SQL | SQL / DataStream API | SQL (Materialize) | SQL compiled to circuits |

The pattern is clear: Flink and Spark ask the *programmer* to manage state and retractions; the timely/differential/DBSP lineage asks the *system* to do it, by making change — not state — the first-class citizen.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

**Reported results from the primary sources** — the headline claims of the three papers' evaluations:

1. **Naiad [1].** On a 48-machine cluster, Naiad matched or exceeded specialized systems across three domains on their native workloads — batch throughput, streaming latency, and iterative graph computation — in a single deployment, with the authors' lecture summary citing sub-second batch updates, sub-millisecond loop iterations, and sub-100ms interactive queries [1].
2. **Differential dataflow [3].** The CIDR 2013 paper demonstrates incrementally maintained strongly connected components over a real Twitter follower graph: per-batch update latencies in the *millisecond* range where from-scratch recomputation takes orders of magnitude longer, with concurrent updates sharing indexed state across the partially ordered version space.
3. **DBSP [5].** The paper models the full relational algebra, grouping and aggregation, *flatmap* over nested relations, monotonic and non-monotonic recursion, and streaming aggregation — each inheriting IVM *for free* from the incrementalization theorem, a breadth no prior single IVM framework had achieved. VLDB 2023 best paper; ACM SIGMOD 2024 Research Highlights [5].

**A unifying proof: differential operators are DBSP circuits.** Let a differential collection be the stream *C* with *C*[*t*] = accumulation of diffs at versions ≤ *t*:

> **Proposition 3 (Correspondence).** *Differential dataflow's join, iterate, and consolidate are Δ-pushed DBSP circuits: join is the Δ-lifting of the bilinear relational join; iterate is feedback through z⁻¹ with a nested time dimension; consolidate is the identity I(Δs) = s canceling opposite-signed updates.*

*Proof sketch.* Bilinearity of join: *(A + ΔA) ⋈ (B + ΔB) = A ⋈ B + ΔA ⋈ B + A ⋈ ΔB + ΔA ⋈ ΔB*; the incremental join maintains exactly the three Δ-terms against the arranged traces — the "delta join" rule. Iteration unrolls the fixed point *X = F(X)* through *z⁻¹* into nested *(epoch, iteration)* timestamps, and Δ-pushing through the unrolled circuit reproduces the pipelined rounds. Consolidation follows from *I* ∘ Δ = id. ∎

Together, Theorems 1–2 and Proposition 3 tell a coherent story: **timely dataflow** supplies the execution substrate (partially ordered time + progress tracking), **differential dataflow** supplies the programming model (collections as difference multisets), and **DBSP** supplies the *theory* (streams, Δ, *I*, and a mechanical incrementalization theorem) that explains why the other two work — and how to build the next one.

## 6. Limitations

What the lineage does *not* solve:

- **Memory footprint of traces.** Difference traces retain history proportional to *distinct versions* times *churn*, not merely current state. Consolidation bounds this in practice, but high-cardinality keys with constant small updates across many epochs can bloat traces well beyond the underlying data — and neither [3] nor [5] gives a tight worst-case bound.
- **Fault tolerance.** Naiad's progress protocol assumes a static worker set; follow-up work on *differential durability* shows DD's append-only, deterministic collection logs admit low-overhead asynchronous replication — but exactly-once recovery with in-flight iteration state remains subtler than Flink's Chandy–Lamport snapshots, and production persist layers had to engineer well beyond the papers.
- **Optimizer integration.** DBSP incrementalizes *any* circuit but does not choose the circuit: join ordering, arrangement sharing, and timestamp-lattice selection are left to the SQL planner. A bad plan incrementalizes correctly but slowly — *correctness* solved, *optimization* open.
- **Latency vs. throughput tuning.** Fine-grained timely scheduling minimizes latency but pays per-message overhead; batching recovers throughput at latency cost. The tradeoff is workload-dependent and largely manual.
- **Expressiveness boundaries.** Computations whose values lack a commutative group structure (holistic aggregates, opaque UDFs) cannot be differentiated in the DBSP sense; they fall back to recomputation, with no gradual degradation between "fully incremental" and "recompute."

## 7. Conclusion

Across thirteen years, one idea has been refined: **make change, not state, the primitive of computation**. Partially ordered timestamps let independent work proceed without coordination; difference multisets let operators work on updates rather than snapshots; and the Δ/*I* adjunction of DBSP turns incrementalization from a bag of tricks into a *theorem*.

The practical payoff is already visible: Materialize maintains SQL views over changing data with millisecond latency on the timely+differential stack, and Feldera compiles SQL to DBSP circuits with provably correct incremental semantics. The open frontier is no longer *whether* rich queries can be incrementalized — DBSP settled that — but *how cheaply*: tighter trace bounds, optimizer-aware circuit synthesis, and fault tolerance matching the theory's elegance.
