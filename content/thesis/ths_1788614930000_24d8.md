Succinct non-interactive zero-knowledge arguments of knowledge (zk-SNARKs) have evolved from circuit-specific constructions such as Groth16 into universal, updatable, and even fully transparent proof systems. This thesis compares the dominant modern lineage: the PLONK arithmetization of Gabizon, Williamson and Ciobotaru, centered on a permutation argument over Lagrange bases [1]; its generalization into Halo2's PLONKish constraint layout, with fixed, advice and instance columns, custom gates and lookup arguments [2]; the two principal polynomial commitment schemes, pairing-based KZG [5] and the discrete-logarithm-based inner-product argument [8]; and recursive composition via Halo's accumulation schemes [3][7] and Nova's folding schemes [4][9]. We derive comparative bounds on proof size, verifier work and recursion overhead, and show the commitment scheme dominates recursion design: KZG's constant-size openings suit pairing-heavy recursive verifiers, while the IPA's logarithmic openings admit trustless recursion only through accumulation. The analysis guides engineers selecting arithmetization, commitment and recursion strategies for verifiable computation.

# Succinct Non-Interactive Zero-Knowledge Arguments: PLONK Arithmetization, Plonkish Constraint Layouts, Polynomial Commitment Schemes, and Recursive Proof Composition

## Abstract

Succinct non-interactive zero-knowledge arguments (zk-SNARKs) have evolved from circuit-specific constructions like Groth16 into universal, updatable, and transparent proof systems. This thesis compares the dominant modern lineage: PLONK's permutation-based arithmetization over Lagrange bases [1]; Halo2's PLONKish constraint layout with custom gates and lookups [2]; the two principal polynomial commitment schemes, KZG [5] and the inner-product argument [8]; and recursive composition via Halo's accumulation schemes [3][7] and Nova's folding schemes [4][9]. The central finding is architectural: the commitment scheme determines the recursion strategy, because KZG's constant-size openings make in-circuit recursion cheap while the IPA's logarithmic, MSM-verified openings force the linear verifier component out of the recursion circuit.

---

## 1. Introduction

The first decade of deployed zero-knowledge proofs was dominated by constructions with *circuit-specific* trusted setups: Groth16 remains the efficiency frontier for fixed circuits, but each new circuit demands a fresh multi-party ceremony. The search for a *universal* setup — one ceremony supporting all circuits up to a bounded size — drove Sonic [MBKM19], which catalyzed PLONK [1]: a universal SNARK with fully succinct verification and roughly 7.5–20× fewer prover group exponentiations than Sonic in the fully succinct verifier mode.

PLONK's decisive innovation was an *arithmetization*, not a new assumption: working with evaluations on a multiplicative subgroup and enforcing wiring through a polynomial *permutation argument* over Lagrange bases. This reframed constraint systems as simpler to describe and easier to extend.

Zcash's Halo2 then generalized PLONK's recipe — gate equation plus copy constraints — into **PLONKish** [2]: circuits become rectangular matrices of field elements with fixed, advice and instance columns, polynomial constraints that must vanish on every row, *custom gates*, and *lookup arguments*. PLONKish is now the lingua franca of production zkEVMs and zkVMs.

Two polynomial commitment scheme (PCS) families underpin these systems. **KZG** [5] gives constant-size commitments and proofs via pairings, at the price of a structured reference string. The **inner-product argument** (IPA) — Bootle et al. [BCC+16], sharpened to 2·log₂(n) group elements by Bulletproofs [8] — is transparent under the discrete logarithm assumption alone.

Finally, *recursion* has produced two philosophies. **Halo** [3] introduced nested amortization, generalized into *accumulation schemes* [7]: defer the linear-time part of verification across steps and check it once at the end. **Nova** [4] introduced *folding schemes*, reducing the check of two instances to one, yielding IVC whose recursion overhead is a constant dominated by two in-circuit scalar multiplications — with no trusted setup and no FFTs.

This thesis argues a central claim: **the polynomial commitment scheme is the load-bearing architectural decision**, determining viable recursion strategies, setup assumptions, and scaling behavior.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 zk-SNARKs and arithmetization

A zk-SNARK is non-interactive and satisfies completeness, knowledge soundness, zero-knowledge, and *succinctness*: proof size and verifier time are sublinear in the computation. Before proving, computation is *arithmetized* into field constraints. The three dominant arithmetizations are:

- **R1CS**: constraints (a·w) × (b·w) = c·w, used by Groth16 and Nova;
- **AIR**: per-step transition constraints over execution traces, used by STARKs;
- **PLONKish**: per-row polynomial constraints over a circuit matrix, used by PLONK and Halo2.

Arithmetization dictates the prover's FFT/MSM workload, the verifier's check equations, and which PCS can be plugged in.

### 2.2 Polynomial commitment schemes

A PCS provides `Setup`, `Commit`, `Open/Eval`, `VerifyEval`, with binding (and hiding for ZK variants). The two canonical instantiations:

- **KZG10** [5]: `Setup` samples τ and publishes `{[τ^i]_1}`, `{[τ^i]_2}`. `Commit(p) = [p(τ)]_1`. To open p(z) = y, the prover sends π = [q(τ)]_1 with q(X) = (p(X) − y)/(X − z); the verifier checks `e(C − [y]_1, [1]_2) = e(π, [τ]_2 − [z]_2)`. Commitment and proof are each one group element.
- **IPA** [8]: commitments are Pedersen vector commitments `C = ⟨p, G⟩` over independent generators; evaluation proofs halve the vector recursively with Fiat-Shamir challenges, yielding `2·log₂(n)`-sized proofs. Transparent setup, DLOG security.

Boneh, Drake, Fisch and Gabizon [6] give a batched KZG variant where one group element opens *multiple* polynomials at arbitrary point sets — the technique that compresses PLONK proofs to nine G1 elements (≈480 bytes, ≈6 ms verification).

### 2.3 Recursion strategies

Naive recursion embeds a SNARK verifier inside another SNARK circuit: expensive, since pairings and field arithmetic must be arithmetized, and it demands cycle-friendly curves (Pallas/Vesta; Tweedledum/Tweedledee [3]). Two refinements avoid placing the whole verifier in-circuit:

1. **Accumulation schemes** [7]: split verification into a cheap *succinct check* (in-circuit per step) and an expensive *linear check* (once, by the final decider). Halo's nested amortization is the prototype [3].
2. **Folding schemes** [4]: abandon SNARKs inside the recursion. A folding scheme reduces checking two instances (u₁,w₁), (u₂,w₂) to checking one folded instance (u,w); IVC folds each step into a running relaxed instance, with constant-sized `FoldVerify` replacing `SNARKVerify`.

## 3. Methodology

This thesis uses a *comparative formal methodology*: the design space is dominated by *interactions between layers* — the commitment scheme constrains achievable recursion — best exposed by placing canonical constructions under a common framework.

Concretely: (1) we fix the pipeline model *arithmetize → commit → open → verify* (the polynomial-protocol view of [1]); (2) we extract concrete parameters — proof sizes, verifier operations, prover asymptotics, recursion thresholds, setup assumptions — from the primary sources into the tables of §5; (3) we prove the central comparative claim (§4.3–§4.4) as theorems relating PCS verifier structure to recursion design; (4) we survey recursion from Halo accumulation [3][7] through Nova folding [4] to HyperNova's customizable constraint systems [9], mapping each to the arithmetizations it supports. All quantitative claims are drawn from the cited primary literature and the Halo2 reference documentation [2], cross-checked against independent expositions.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The PLONK Protocol: Arithmetization and the Permutation Argument

PLONK starts from a multiplicative subgroup H ⊂ F of order n (a power of two, enabling O(n log n) FFTs) with generator g. An n-gate circuit is encoded by witness wire polynomials a(X), b(X), c(X) interpolating the wire values over H, and *selector polynomials* q_L, q_R, q_O, q_M, q_C encoding the topology. The **gate identity** requires

> **Gate identity.** q_L(X)·a(X) + q_R(X)·b(X) + q_O(X)·c(X) + q_M(X)·a(X)·b(X) + q_C(X) = 0 on all of H,

so gate-satisfaction for the whole circuit becomes the claim that t(X) = gate(X)/Z_H(X) has no remainder, where Z_H(X) = X^n − 1 is the vanishing polynomial of H.

Wiring is enforced by the **permutation argument**: with σ the copy-constraint permutation of the 3n wire positions, the prover builds a grand-product accumulator z(X) with z(g) = 1 satisfying

> **Permutation identity.** z(X)·(a(X)+β·X+γ)(b(X)+β·k₁·X+γ)(c(X)+β·k₂·X+γ) − z(g·X)·(a(X)+β·S_{σ1}(X)+γ)(b(X)+β·S_{σ2}(X)+γ)(c(X)+β·S_{σ3}(X)+γ) = 0 on H,

where S_{σi} encode σ over three cosets of H and β, γ are verifier challenges. This is the Bayer–Groth permutation argument re-engineered for Lagrange bases: handling polynomials through *evaluations on H* rather than monomial coefficients simplifies both the permutation check and arithmetization relative to Sonic [1]. The protocol runs in five rounds; with the batching of [6], proofs compress to nine G1 elements with ≈6 ms verification.

```python
# PLONK prover polynomial pipeline (Sage-style pseudocode)
H = multiplicative_subgroup(F, n)
a, b, c = interpolate(wire_values, H)      # witness polynomials
z = grand_product(a, b, c, sigma, beta, gamma)   # permutation accumulator
t = (gate_identity(a,b,c) + perm_identity(a,b,c,z)) // Z_H  # quotient
proof = PCS.commit_and_open([a,b,c,z,t], zeta)   # batched KZG openings [6]
```

> **Theorem (PLONK knowledge soundness, informal [1]).** Under q-DLOG/q-SDH in the algebraic group model, PLONK is a knowledge-sound non-interactive argument for circuit satisfiability with a universal updatable SRS, O(1)-sized proofs, and O(1) pairings plus O(log n) field operations for the verifier.

### 4.2 Halo2's Plonkish Layout: Matrices, Custom Gates, and Lookup Arguments

Halo2 promotes PLONK's blueprint to a general constraint language [2]. A PLONKish circuit is a **rectangular matrix** of n rows; each column is **fixed** (circuit constants, selectors), **advice** (prover witness), or **instance** (public inputs). The configuration fixes a maximum constraint degree and a sequence of polynomial constraints — multivariate polynomials that must vanish *on every row*, with variables referencing cells at relative row offsets (wraparound mod n). Equality between arbitrary cells uses a permutation argument generalized from PLONK's three wires to arbitrary column sets, again via a grand product [2].

Three extensions make PLONKish strictly more expressive than vanilla PLONK:

1. **Custom gates.** New selector columns multiply arbitrary gate polynomials: q_L·a + q_R·b + q_O·c + q_M·a·b + q_C + q_G·g(a,b,c) = 0, so an S-box layer or curve addition costs one row instead of many gates.
2. **Larger fan-in/fan-out**, amortizing per-row overhead.
3. **Lookup arguments.** Each row's input tuple (as polynomial expressions) must appear as a row of a fixed table. Halo2 compresses input and table columns with a challenge θ, permutes compressed values by the lookup's multiplicity rules, and enforces the permutation with a grand product [2]. Lookups turn range checks, XOR and bit decomposition into table membership — often the largest source of circuit-size reduction in production zkEVMs.

Circuit design thus becomes *layout optimization*: assigning operations to rows, sharing advice columns across chips, and choosing where lookups beat custom gates, composed hierarchically through Halo2's "chip" abstraction [2].

| Feature | Vanilla PLONK [1] | TurboPLONK | UltraPLONK | Halo2 PLONKish [2] |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gate equation | fixed 2-in/1-out | custom gates | custom gates | custom gates, arbitrary fan-in |
| Copy constraints | 3-wire permutation | 3-wire | 3-wire | n-column permutation |
| Lookups | no | no | plookup | Halo2 lookup argument |
| Column roles | implicit | implicit | implicit | fixed / advice / instance |
| Commitment | KZG | KZG | KZG | IPA *or* KZG |

### 4.3 Polynomial Commitments: KZG versus the Inner-Product Argument

**KZG [5].** Setup publishes `srs = ([τ^i]_1, [1]_2, [τ]_2)` from secret τ. Commit: C = [p(τ)]_1 (one MSM). Open p(z) = y: π = [q(τ)]_1 with q(X) = (p(X) − y)/(X − z). Verify with one pairing equation:

> **KZG verification.** e(C − [y]_1, [1]_2) = e(π, [τ]_2 − [z]_2).

Commitment: 1 G1. Proof: 1 G1. Verification: 2 pairings. Price: trusted setup (mitigated by updatable ceremonies) and pairing-friendly curves.

**IPA [BCC+16, 8].** Commitments are Pedersen vector commitments C = ⟨**p**, **G**⟩ over independent generators. Evaluation proofs halve the vector recursively with Fiat-Shamir challenges: log₂(n) rounds, proof `2·log₂(n)` group elements plus O(1) scalars [8]. Transparent setup; security from DLOG alone.

> **Theorem (comparison).** For degree bound d = 2^k: KZG gives |C| = 1 G1, |π| = 1 G1, verifier 2 pairings; IPA gives |C| = 1 G, |π| = 2k G, verifier O(d) group ops unless the final MSM is deferred. KZG needs a structured setup and pairings; IPA needs neither.

The verifier asymmetry decides recursion. A KZG verifier is *constant-time*; arithmetizing it in-circuit costs a fixed number of pairing checks. An IPA verifier must compute a length-n MSM — an O(n) operation that destroys succinctness if placed naively in a recursive circuit. Halo's answer is to split the IPA verifier: a succinct part goes in-circuit, the linear part is deferred to the accumulation scheme's decider [3][7]. Halo2's IPA backend and its recursion story are therefore inseparable.

```rust
// Halo2's dual-backend design: one PLONKish circuit, two commitment schemes
enum CommitmentScheme {
    Kzg,  // 1-G1 proofs, pairing verifier -> in-circuit recursion viable
    Ipa,  // 2*log(n)-G proofs, transparent -> recursion needs accumulation [7]
}
```

### 4.4 Recursive Proof Composition: Accumulation Schemes and Folding

**Halo: nested amortization and accumulation [3][7].** Halo observes the expensive part of IPA verification is a single MSM whose structure the circuit knows. Each recursion step instead emits an *accumulator* — a deferred claim about a linear relation — and the final decider checks all accumulated claims at once. Bünz et al. [7] generalized this to **accumulation schemes** for any additive PCS ("Halo Infinite"), formalized as (G, I, P, V, D): cheap verifier V, single deferred linear check in decider D. Result: fully recursive 3.5 KiB proofs, recursion threshold below 2^17 multiplication gates (an order of magnitude under Fractal), *no trusted setup*, via the Tweedledum/Tweedledee cycle [3].

**Nova: folding schemes [4].** Nova abandons SNARKs inside the recursion. A **folding scheme** reduces two instance-witness pairs to one folded pair satisfiable only if both originals were. Nova folds *relaxed R1CS* — R1CS plus scalar u and error vector E absorbing folding slack: A·Z ∘ B·Z = u·C·Z + E. Each IVC step folds the fresh instance into the running one; in-circuit work is just `FoldVerify`, dominated by **two group scalar multiplications** — the smallest recursion overhead known [4]. Per-step prover work is two O(|F|) multiexponentiations (fastest known prover), proofs compress to O(log |F|) via a companion zkSNARK, with no trusted setup and no FFTs, over any DLOG-hard curve cycle.

**HyperNova and CCS [9].** Folding was lifted from R1CS to **customizable constraint systems**, generalizing Plonkish, R1CS and AIR without overhead: HyperNova's prover costs a single MSM of variable-count size (optimal for MSM commitments), folds many instances at once (toward PCD), and gets zero-knowledge "for free"; **CycleFold** instantiates these arguments over curve cycles.

> **Theorem (recursion design rule).** If the PCS verifier is constant-time (KZG), in-circuit recursion is viable and accumulation is an optimization. If it has a linear component (IPA), succinct recursion *requires* accumulation schemes [7] or folding schemes [4] that avoid verifying the PCS in-circuit.

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

**Table 1 — Proof size and verifier cost (degree bound n = 2^k).**

| System | PCS | Proof size | Verifier work | Setup |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PLONK [1] | KZG (batched [6]) | 9 G1 (≈480 B) | 2 pairings + O(k) field ops (≈6 ms) | universal, updatable |
| Halo2-KZG | KZG | O(#columns) G1 | O(#columns) pairings | universal, updatable |
| Halo2-IPA | IPA | 2k G + O(1) | O(n) MSM, accumulated | transparent |
| Halo [3] (recursive) | IPA + accumulation | ≈3.5 KiB | succinct check in-circuit; 1 linear check at end | transparent |
| Nova [4] (IVC) | Pedersen (folding) | O(log \|F\|) G via companion SNARK | FoldVerify = 2 scalar muls in-circuit | transparent |
| Bulletproofs [8] | IPA | 2k G + O(1) | O(n) | transparent |

**Table 2 — Recursion strategies.**

| Strategy | In-circuit verifier? | Recursion overhead | Trust assumption |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive SNARK recursion | full SNARKVerify | thousands of constraints (pairings) | inherits inner SNARK |
| Halo accumulation [3][7] | succinct check only | accumulator update; threshold < 2^17 mult gates | DLOG only |
| Nova folding [4] | FoldVerify only (2 scalar muls) | constant, minimal known | DLOG only |
| HyperNova/CCS [9] | FoldVerify, multi-instance | single MSM prover cost | DLOG only |

> **Theorem (PLONK prover complexity [1]).** The PLONK prover performs O(n log n) field operations (FFTs for the quotient and permutation accumulator) and O(n) group exponentiations — roughly 7.5–20× fewer than Sonic's fully-succinct mode.

> **Theorem (Nova IVC efficiency [4]).** For N steps of \|F\|-sized circuits, Nova's prover runs in O_λ(N·\|F\|), proofs are O_λ(log \|F\|)-sized and verifiable in O_λ(\|F\|), with per-step work dominated by two ≈\|F\|-sized multiexponentiations and no FFTs.

The qualitative picture is a clean Pareto frontier: KZG-based PLONK minimizes standalone proof size and verifier latency (480 B, ~6 ms) at the cost of a ceremony; IPA-based systems minimize trust at the cost of logarithmic growth; for recursion, accumulation and folding both achieve trustless IVC, but folding attains the smaller recursion circuit by verifying no SNARK in-circuit at all.

## 6. Limitations

**Trusted setup residue.** KZG's constant-size proofs are bought with a structured reference string. Updatable ceremonies [1] reduce trust to 1-of-N honesty but do not eliminate it; where any ceremony is unacceptable, only IPA/folding systems qualify.

**FFT-bound provers.** PLONK and Halo2 provers are dominated by O(n log n) NTTs and O(n) MSMs; at n = 2^24 (zkEVM scale) proving needs tens of gigabytes of RAM and GPU acceleration. Custom gates improve only the constant.

**Lookup soundness subtleties.** Halo2 lookups compress tuples with a challenge sampled *after* table commitments — an ordering constraint whose violation in Fiat-Shamir transcripts has caused real vulnerabilities. Lookups also consume the constraint-degree budget that custom gates compete for [2].

**Folding is not a SNARK.** Nova's IVC artifact is a folded instance of size O(\|F\|) until compressed by the companion zkSNARK [4]; zero-knowledge needs extra randomization. Applications wanting a 480-byte artifact at every step still prefer KZG recursion.

**Curve cycles and field mismatch.** Recursion needs the verifier's curve arithmetizable in the prover's field, forcing 2-cycles [3] or CycleFold [9]. Non-native field arithmetic remains the dominant in-circuit cost (2–3× native) and the main optimization target.

**Post-quantum exposure.** All systems here rest on DLOG/pairing assumptions; a cryptographically relevant quantum computer breaks KZG, IPA, accumulation and folding alike. Lattice- and hash-based successors bound this thesis's long-term relevance.

## 7. Conclusion

Four ideas, composed in order, explain seven years of practical zero-knowledge proofs. **PLONK** [1] built a universal SNARK from Lagrange-basis evaluations and a permutation argument, collapsing wiring into one grand-product identity. **Halo2** [2] generalized the gate equation into PLONKish — a matrix constraint language with custom gates and lookups now underpinning production zkEVMs. **Polynomial commitments** [5][8][6] showed one arithmetization can ride on constant-size pairing-based or transparent logarithmic commitments, with the choice propagating into every design decision. **Recursive composition** — Halo's accumulation [3][7], Nova's folding [4][9] — made the verifier incremental, turning proofs from static certificates into composable objects.

The comparative finding is architectural: *the commitment scheme determines the recursion strategy*. KZG's constant-size openings make in-circuit recursion cheap; the IPA's MSM-verified openings force the linear component out of the circuit — deferred to a decider (accumulation) or eliminated (folding). Engineers should therefore choose the PCS first on trust and proof-size grounds, and let accumulation or folding follow as a consequence. With folding schemes now serving customizable constraint systems [9] that express Plonkish, R1CS and AIR in one framework, the next synthesis is in sight: a single folding-based recursion layer for every major arithmetization.
