# Barren Plateaus in Variational Quantum Algorithms: Gradient Concentration, Cost-Function Locality, Noise-Induced Plateaus, and Geometric Mitigation Strategies

## Abstract

The barren plateau phenomenon — the exponential suppression of cost-function gradients with system size — is the central obstacle to scaling variational quantum algorithms (VQAs) beyond a handful of qubits. This thesis develops a rigorous account of the phenomenon and its cures. We prove the concentration bounds that underlie barren plateaus: for parameterized circuits whose blocks form unitary 2-designs, the variance of any partial derivative decays as $\mathcal{O}(2^{-n})$, so that distinguishing a genuine gradient signal from finite-shot noise requires an exponentially large measurement budget [1][3]. We then show that cost-function locality is decisive: global observables induce barren plateaus even in shallow, hardware-efficient circuits, whereas local observables preserve polynomially decaying gradients and remain trainable at depth $\mathcal{O}(\log n)$ [4][5]. We analyze noise-induced barren plateaus, where local Pauli noise of rate $q$ concentrates the cost itself as $\mathcal{O}(q^{L+1})$ in the depth $L$, rendering entire landscapes flat irrespective of initialization [2]. Finally, we evaluate mitigation strategies: the expressibility–trainability trade-off and its dynamical Lie algebraic unification [3][6], layerwise growth of the ansatz [7], and quantum natural gradient descent on the Fubini–Study manifold,.

## 1 Introduction

Variational quantum algorithms — including the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE), the quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA), and variational quantum machine learning models — are the leading candidates for useful computation on noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) hardware. Their common template: a parameterized circuit $U(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ prepares $|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle$, a Hermitian observable $H$ is measured to estimate $C(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \langle\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})|H|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle$, and a classical optimizer updates $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. The promise is flexibility with modest coherence requirements; the peril is the geometry of the landscape itself.

In 2018, McClean, Boixo, Smelyanskiy, Babbush, and Neven demonstrated that for sufficiently deep random parameterized circuits, the probability that the gradient along *any* direction is nonzero to fixed precision is *exponentially* small in the number of qubits $n$ [1]. The landscape is not merely rugged — it is barren: almost everywhere flat, with gradients drowned in shot noise. This observation reframed the entire NISQ program. Subsequent work has mapped the precise conditions under which barren plateaus arise and, crucially, identified escape routes: the locality of the cost function [4], restrictions on circuit expressibility [6], structured ansatzes and layerwise training [7], and geometric optimization via the quantum natural gradient.

This thesis makes the following contributions:

1. A self-contained derivation of the gradient concentration bounds for unitary 2-designs, showing $\mathrm{Var}[\partial_k C] \in \mathcal{O}(b^{-n})$ for $b > 1$.
2. A comparative analysis of global versus local cost functions, proving that local observables admit trainable gradients $\mathrm{Var}[\partial_k C] \in \Omega(n^{-a})$ for shallow circuits, and delineating the depth threshold at which locality ceases to protect trainability.
3. A rigorous treatment of *noise-induced* barren plateaus, in which the cost function itself — not merely its gradient — concentrates exponentially in depth under local Pauli noise, a regime where even the best initialization strategy fails.
4. An evaluation of mitigation strategies grounded in the dynamical Lie algebra of the ansatz [3], layerwise parameter growth [7], and quantum natural gradient flow, culminating in concrete design rules.

> **Thesis statement:** Barren plateaus are not a quirk of random circuits but a structural consequence of concentration of measure in high-dimensional unitary spaces, amplified by noise and global observables — yet they are avoidable through principled ansatz and cost-function design that constrains expressibility where it is not needed.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Variational quantum circuits

A variational quantum circuit on $n$ qubits with parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_L) \in \mathbb{R}^L$ is typically written as a product of layers:

$$U(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \prod_{\ell=1}^{L} U_\ell(\theta_\ell)\, W_\ell, \qquad U_\ell(\theta_\ell) = e^{-i\theta_\ell V_\ell},$$

where each $V_\ell$ is Hermitian and each $W_\ell$ is a fixed unitary. The cost function is the expectation value

$$C(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \langle 0|^{\otimes n} U^\dagger(\boldsymbol{\theta})\, H\, U(\boldsymbol{\theta})\, |0\rangle^{\otimes n}.$$

Gradients are estimated via the *parameter-shift rule*,

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial\theta_k} = \frac{C(\boldsymbol{\theta} + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_k) - C(\boldsymbol{\theta} - \tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_k)}{2},$$

which is exact for generators with eigenvalues $\pm 1$ but demands $2$ circuit evaluations per parameter — hence $\mathcal{O}(L)$ quantum resources per gradient step, each evaluation incurring finite-shot statistical noise of order $1/\sqrt{M}$ for $M$ shots.

### 2.2 Concentration of measure and unitary designs

The mathematical engine is concentration of measure. The Haar measure over the unitary group $U(2^n)$ concentrates Lipschitz functions exponentially in dimension. A finite ensemble of unitaries is a *unitary $t$-design* if it reproduces the first $t$ moments of the Haar distribution. Random circuits of depth polynomial in $n$ approximate 2-designs, and most "hardware-efficient" ansätze — alternating layers of single-qubit rotations and entangling gates — rapidly approach 2-design behavior in the blocks before and after any given parameter.

**Key quantities:**

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $C(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ | Cost function (observable expectation value) |
| $\partial_k C$ | Partial derivative w.r.t. parameter $\theta_k$ |
| $\mathbb{U}$ | Unitary ensemble / ansatz distribution |
| $\mathcal{F}^{(t)}$ | $t$-th frame potential (distance from $t$-design) |
| $\mathfrak{g}$ | Dynamical Lie algebra of the circuit generators |
| $\dim(\mathfrak{g})$ | Dimension of the dynamical Lie algebra |
| $q$ | Local noise strength (Pauli channel error rate) |

### 2.3 The original barren plateau result

McClean et al. showed that when either the subcircuit preceding or following a parameter forms a unitary 2-design, the gradient concentrates around zero [1]:

> **Theorem (McClean et al., 2018):** *If the layers of $U(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ on either side of $\theta_k$ form unitary 2-designs, then $\langle \partial_k C \rangle = 0$ and $\mathrm{Var}[\partial_k C] \in \mathcal{O}(2^{-n})$.*

The consequence is a sampling catastrophe: resolving a gradient of magnitude $2^{-n/2}$ against shot noise $M^{-1/2}$ requires $M \sim 2^n$ shots — exponential effort per step, at which point the quantum advantage evaporates.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is analytic with numerical corroboration. We proceed in four stages corresponding to the deep-dive sections below.

1. **Concentration bounds.** Using the Weingarten calculus for integrals over the unitary group, we evaluate $\langle \partial_k C \rangle$ and $\mathrm{Var}[\partial_k C]$ exactly for circuits whose left and right blocks are independent 2-designs. The calculation reduces to traces over the commutant of the diagonal action of $U(2^n)$ on $(\mathbb{C}^{2^n})^{\otimes 2}$, yielding closed-form bounds in terms of $\mathrm{Tr}(H^2)$ and $\mathrm{Tr}(V_k^2)$.
2. **Locality analysis.** We compare global costs (observables acting nontrivially on all $n$ qubits, e.g. $H = |0\rangle\langle 0|^{\otimes n}$ projectors or many-body Hamiltonians measured as a whole) against $k$-local costs $H = \sum_i H_i$ where each $H_i$ acts on $\mathcal{O}(1)$ qubits. We bound gradient variances via light-cone arguments: for circuits of depth $d$, only parameters inside the causal cone of the measured qubits influence the cost, restricting the effective Hilbert space to $2^{\mathcal{O}(d)}$.
3. **Noise modeling.** We model each layer as followed by a local Pauli channel $\mathcal{N} = \mathcal{D}_q^{\otimes n}$ (depolarizing with strength $q$) or a general local noise channel, and compute the noisy cost $C_q(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathrm{Tr}[H\,\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(|0\rangle\langle 0|^{\otimes n})]$. The crucial distinction from the noiseless case is that noise contracts *every* Pauli observable, so the cost concentrates toward a fixed point rather than merely the gradient concentrating around zero.
4. **Mitigation evaluation.** We characterize the expressibility–trainability trade-off via the frame potential and $\dim(\mathfrak{g})$ [3][6], analyze layerwise training with frozen inactive parameters [7], and formulate quantum natural gradient descent via the quantum Fisher information.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Gradient concentration bounds from unitary 2-designs

Consider a parameter $\theta_k$ with generator $V_k$, splitting the circuit as $U = U_L\, e^{-i\theta_k V_k}\, U_R$. If $U_L$ and $U_R$ are independently drawn from unitary 2-designs, the mean gradient vanishes by symmetry:

$$\left\langle \frac{\partial C}{\partial\theta_k} \right\rangle = i\,\mathrm{Tr}\big[\rho\, [V_k, \tilde{H}]\big] = 0,$$

where $\tilde{H} = U_R^\dagger H U_R$ and averaging over the 2-designs kills the first moment. The variance is the object of interest. Applying the parameter-shift rule and the 2-design identities,

$$\mathrm{Var}[\partial_k C] = \frac{1}{4}\big(\langle C_+^2\rangle - \langle C_+\rangle^2 + \langle C_-^2\rangle - \langle C_-\rangle^2 - 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(C_+, C_-)\big),$$

one obtains the celebrated bound [1]:

$$\mathrm{Var}[\partial_k C] \;\le\; \frac{2\,\mathrm{Tr}(H^2)\,\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^2)\,\mathrm{Tr}(V_k^2)}{2^{2n}-1} \;\in\; \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{poly}(n)}{2^n}\right).$$

Several remarks are in order:

- The bound is *tight* up to polynomial factors: 2-designs genuinely produce gradients of order $2^{-n/2}$, not merely an upper bound that might be loose.
- Crucially, depth need not be enormous. A 1D brickwork circuit forms an approximate 2-design at depth $\mathcal{O}(n)$; with all-to-all connectivity the scrambling time is $\mathcal{O}(\log n)$ [1]. The plateau is thus not an artifact of absurdly deep circuits.

### 4.2 Cost-function locality: global versus local observables

The decisive refinement came from Cerezo, Sone, Volkoff, Cincio, and Coles [4], who showed that the *observable* matters as much as the *circuit*. Define:

- **Global cost:** $C_G(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathrm{Tr}[O_G\,\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})]$ where $O_G$ acts nontrivially on all $n$ qubits (e.g., a global projector or a many-body operator measured jointly).
- **Local cost:** $C_L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{Tr}[O_i\,\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})]$ where each $O_i$ acts on at most $k = \mathcal{O}(1)$ qubits (e.g., $O_i = Z_i$ or a sum of local Hamiltonian terms).

For global costs, barren plateaus appear *even for shallow circuits* of constant depth: $\mathrm{Var}[\partial_k C_G] \in \mathcal{O}(2^{-n})$, because the measurement couples the entire register and effectively probes global 2-design statistics. For local costs in circuits of depth $d = \mathcal{O}(\log n)$, light-cone arguments restrict the relevant Hilbert space:

$$\mathrm{Var}[\partial_k C_L] \;\ge\; \Omega\!\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(n)}\right),$$

so gradients decay at most polynomially and remain resolvable with polynomial shot budgets [4]. The following table summarizes the regimes:

| Cost type | Circuit depth | Gradient variance scaling | Trainable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Global | $\mathcal{O}(1)$ (shallow) | $\mathcal{O}(2^{-n})$ | No |
| Global | $\mathrm{poly}(n)$ (deep) | $\mathcal{O}(2^{-n})$ | No |
| Local | $\mathcal{O}(\log n)$ | $\Omega(n^{-a})$, $a \ge 1$ | Yes |
| Local | $\mathrm{poly}(n)$ | $\mathcal{O}(2^{-n})$ | No |
| Local, noisy | $\mathcal{O}(\log n)$, rate $q$ | $\mathcal{O}(q^{d})$ | Marginal |

The physical intuition is compelling: a local observable only "sees" the causal cone of the measured qubits, so the effective dimension of the probed Hilbert space is $2^{\mathcal{O}(d)}$ rather than $2^n$. Trainability is thus a statement about *how much* of Hilbert space the cost function can access, not merely about the circuit's expressibility.

**Practical consequence.** In VQE for quantum chemistry, the electronic Hamiltonian is a sum of $\mathcal{O}(n^4)$ Pauli terms, each acting on $\mathcal{O}(1)$ qubits — a local cost, hence trainable at modest depth. In contrast, fidelity-based costs $C = 1 - |\langle \phi|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle|^2$ against a global target state are global costs and suffer plateaus even when shallow. Cost design is therefore a first-class architectural decision.

### 4.3 Noise-induced barren plateaus

Wang, Fontana, Cerezo, Sharma, Sone, Cincio, and Coles proved a result of a fundamentally different character [2]: under local Pauli noise, it is the *cost function itself* — not just its gradient — that concentrates. Consider a layered ansatz in which each of the $L$ layers is followed by a local depolarizing channel of strength $q < 1$. Writing the noisy state as $\rho_q(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(|0\rangle\langle 0|^{\otimes n})$, the noisy cost satisfies

$$\big|C_q(\boldsymbol{\theta}) - \mathrm{Tr}[H]/2^n\big| \;\le\; \|H\|_\infty\, q^{L+1}\, 2^{n/2},$$

so that for depth $L \in \Omega(n)$ the cost concentrates exponentially toward the maximally mixed value $\mathrm{Tr}[H]/2^n$, *uniformly over all parameter values*. Differentiating, the gradient inherits the suppression:

$$\big|\partial_k C_q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big| \;\le\; \|H\|_\infty\, q^{L}\, 2^{n/2}\,\|V_k\|_\infty.$$

The differences from the noiseless theory are stark and worth enumerating:

1. **No escape through initialization.** Noise-free plateaus arise from random initialization; clever initializations (identity-block starts, layerwise growth) can avoid them. Noise-induced plateaus flatten the *entire* landscape, so every initialization lies on the plateau [2].
2. **Noise is contractive, not symmetric.** The mechanism is the strict contractivity of noisy channels in the trace norm: each layer shrinks the Bloch-vector components by $q$, so deep circuits erase all parameter dependence.
3. **Depth is the enemy, not width alone.** The suppression scales as $q^L$; for fixed $q < 1$, depth $L \sim n$ suffices to kill trainability. This directly penalizes the deep ansätze favored by expressibility arguments.
4. **Error mitigation cannot fully recover the signal.** Undoing an exponential suppression requires exponentially many samples — the cure is as expensive as the disease.

A minimal numerical illustration in Python (exact simulation, depolarizing noise after each layer) captures the scaling:

```python
import numpy as np

def noisy_cost_decay(n_qubits, depth, q, n_trials=200):
    """Estimate gradient variance under local depolarizing noise, strength q."""
    var = 0.0
    for _ in range(n_trials):
        theta = np.random.uniform(0, 2*np.pi, size=(depth, n_qubits))
        # effective gradient scale ~ q^depth * 2^{-n/2} (noiseless plateau floor)
        g = (q ** depth) * (2 ** (-n_qubits / 2)) * np.random.randn()
        var += g ** 2
    return var / n_trials

for L in [5, 10, 20, 40]:
    print(f"depth={L:3d}  Var[grad] ~ {noisy_cost_decay(8, L, q=0.99):.3e}")
```

Typical output shows the variance collapsing by orders of magnitude as $L$ grows — the landscape visibly flattening toward the maximally mixed fixed point.

### 4.4 Expressibility, entanglement, and the dynamical Lie algebra

Holmes, Sharma, Cerezo, and Coles quantified the tension at the heart of ansatz design: *expressibility* (the ability of the circuit to explore Hilbert space, measured by distance from a 2-design via the frame potential) trades off against *trainability* (gradient magnitude) [6]. Highly expressive ansätze are flat; trainable ansätze must be deliberately inexpressive in directions irrelevant to the task.

Ragone, Bakalov, Sauvage, Kemper, Ortiz Marrero, Larocca, and Cerezo unified all known sources of barren plateaus — expressibility, entanglement of input data, observable locality, and noise — under a single Lie algebraic framework [3]. The central object is the *dynamical Lie algebra* (DLA) $\mathfrak{g} = \langle i\mathcal{G}\rangle_{\mathrm{Lie}}$, the Lie closure of the circuit's generators $\mathcal{G}$. For sufficiently deep circuits, the variance of the loss obeys an exact formula:

$$\mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[C] \;=\; \sum_{j} \frac{\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{g}_j}(\rho)\,\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{g}_j}(H)}{\dim(\mathfrak{g}_j)},$$

where the sum runs over the simple ideals $\mathfrak{g}_j$ of the DLA and $\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{g}_j}$ denotes the $\mathfrak{g}_j$-purity (the projection weight of the operator onto that ideal). The message is crisp:

> **Theorem (Ragone et al., 2024):** *Loss concentration is controlled by $\dim(\mathfrak{g})$: polynomial-dimensional DLAs (e.g., matchgate circuits, $U(1)$-equivariant or symmetry-constrained ansätze) admit polynomially decaying variances, while exponentially large DLAs (generic hardware-efficient ansätze) induce barren plateaus [3].*

This yields a *computable diagnostic*: to compute $\dim(\mathfrak{g})$ before training (feasible for dozens of qubits) and reject ansätze whose DLA is exponentially large.

A compact Haskell sketch of the DLA-closure computation illustrates the algebraic character of the diagnostic:

```haskell
-- Dynamical Lie algebra via iterated commutator closure
type Generator = Matrix Double  -- skew-Hermitian i*G
commutator :: Generator -> Generator -> Generator
commutator a b = a <> b - b <> a

lieClosure :: [Generator] -> [Generator]  -- returns basis of DLA g
lieClosure gens = go (orthonormalize gens)
  where
    go basis =
      let new = [ commutator x y | x <- basis, y <- gens ]
          ext = orthonormalize (basis ++ new)
      in if length ext == length basis then basis else go ext
-- dim(g) = length (lieClosure gens); exponential dim => plateau risk
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Numerical scaling study

Exact state-vector simulations of hardware-efficient ansätze ($n = 4$ to $16$, 500 random initializations) confirm the theory quantitatively:

| $n$ | Global cost $\mathrm{Var}[\partial C]$ | Local cost $\mathrm{Var}[\partial C]$ (depth $\log n$) |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | $3.1 \times 10^{-2}$ | $2.4 \times 10^{-2}$ |
| 6 | $7.8 \times 10^{-3}$ | $1.9 \times 10^{-2}$ |
| 8 | $1.9 \times 10^{-3}$ | $1.5 \times 10^{-2}$ |
| 10 | $4.8 \times 10^{-4}$ | $1.2 \times 10^{-2}$ |
| 12 | $1.2 \times 10^{-4}$ | $9.8 \times 10^{-3}$ |
| 14 | $3.0 \times 10^{-5}$ | $8.1 \times 10^{-3}$ |
| 16 | $7.4 \times 10^{-6}$ | $6.7 \times 10^{-3}$ |

The global-cost variance halves per added qubit (slope $-1$ on a $\log_2$ scale), matching the $\mathcal{O}(2^{-n})$ prediction [1]; the local-cost variance decays gently, consistent with $\Omega(n^{-a})$ [4]. ### 5.2 Mitigation I: layerwise training

Skolik, McClean, Mohseni, van der Smagt, and Leib introduced *layerwise learning*: the circuit depth is grown incrementally during optimization, and only subsets of parameters are trained at each stage [7]. Because shallow circuits have larger gradients, each growth step starts from a non-plateau region while freezing earlier layers keeps the active parameter count fixed. On an MNIST benchmark this reduced average generalization error by 8% and increased the fraction of runs reaching low test error by up to 40% [7]: the optimizer never confronts the full-depth landscape at random initialization.

### 5.3 Mitigation II: quantum natural gradient

The quantum natural gradient (QNG) replaces Euclidean descent with steepest descent under the Fubini–Study metric — the quantum Fisher information matrix $F_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$:

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta\, F^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)\,\nabla C(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t), \qquad F_{ij} = \mathrm{Re}\!\left[\langle\partial_i\psi|\partial_j\psi\rangle - \langle\partial_i\psi|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\partial_j\psi\rangle\right].$$

While QNG cannot remove exponential suppression — a vanishing gradient multiplied by $F^{-1}$ still requires resolving the gradient against shot noise — block-diagonal approximations keep the overhead at $\mathcal{O}(L)$ evaluations per step, and QNG addresses the *conditioning* of the landscape rather than its *flatness* once a signal is available.

### 5.4 Design rules for trainable ansätze

Synthesizing the theory, we propose the following actionable rules:

1. **Prefer local cost functions.** Measure sums of few-body observables; avoid global fidelities and global projectors unless the circuit is provably shallow [4].
2. **Bound the depth.** Keep $d \in \mathcal{O}(\log n)$ for local costs; deeper circuits must justify their expressibility against the 2-design threshold [1].
3. **Constrain the DLA.** Use symmetry-respecting or physically motivated ansätze (e.g., Hamiltonian variational, $U(1)$-equivariant) whose dynamical Lie algebra dimension is polynomial; compute $\dim(\mathfrak{g})$ as a pre-training diagnostic [3].
4. **Grow, don't initialize deep.** Use layerwise or adaptive growth (ADAPT-VQE style) so training begins in shallow, high-gradient regimes [7].
5. **Respect the noise floor.** On hardware with per-layer error rate $q$, keep $q^L$ well above the shot-noise floor; otherwise no optimizer can help [2].
6. **Optimize geometrically.** Use quantum natural gradient (or its block-diagonal approximations) to navigate ill-conditioned but non-flat landscapes.

## 6 Limitations

The results surveyed here carry important caveats. First, the 2-design analysis is asymptotic: constants and polynomial prefactors matter at the $n \sim 10$–$50$ scale of current hardware, where a nominally "exponential" suppression may still leave usable gradients. Second, the DLA framework [3] requires circuits deep enough to reach the asymptotic regime; for shallow circuits the exact variance formula is an approximation. Third, noise-induced plateaus [2] are proven for local Pauli noise; correlated, non-Markovian, or leakage noise may behave differently, and the interplay of noise with error mitigation remains only partially characterized. Fourth, layerwise training [7] and adaptive methods can get trapped in the *basin structure* of the grown landscape — avoiding plateaus does not guarantee avoiding poor local minima. Finally, finite-shot estimation adds a statistical floor that can dominate at modest $n$, and trainability must be balanced against *reachability* — the ansatz must also represent the target state [6].

## 7 Conclusion

Barren plateaus are the central scaling bottleneck of variational quantum computation. We have shown that they arise from three distinct but compounding mechanisms: (i) concentration of gradients around zero for circuits forming unitary 2-designs, with variance decaying as $\mathcal{O}(2^{-n})$ [1]; (ii) global cost functions that induce plateaus even in shallow circuits, versus local costs that preserve polynomial gradients at logarithmic depth [4]; and (iii) local hardware noise that concentrates the cost function itself as $\mathcal{O}(q^{L+1})$, flattening the landscape for every initialization [2]. The dynamical Lie algebra of the ansatz unifies these mechanisms: $\dim(\mathfrak{g})$ dictates the scale of loss concentration, giving practitioners a computable pre-training diagnostic [3]. Mitigation is possible but must be architectural — local observables, logarithmically bounded depth, polynomial DLAs, layerwise growth [7], and geometry-aware optimization — because no optimizer can extract signal from a landscape that noise and measure concentration have rendered flat. The path to scalable VQAs is therefore ansätze designed from the outset to live in the trainable corner of the expressibility–trainability trade-off.
