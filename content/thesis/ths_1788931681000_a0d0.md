# Sketching, Sampling, and the Randomized Singular Value Decomposition: Probabilistic Error Bounds for Large-Scale Matrix Computation

## Abstract

The singular value decomposition (SVD) is the workhorse of numerical linear algebra, yet its classical $O(mn \cdot \min(m,n))$ cost is prohibitive for the massive matrices of modern science and machine learning. Randomized numerical linear algebra (RandNLA) replaces exact factorization with *sketching*: compressing a matrix through random linear maps or data-aware sampling, then solving the small problem exactly. This thesis develops the two dominant paradigms — oblivious random projection and leverage-score sampling — culminating in the randomized SVD of Halko, Martinsson, and Tropp [1]. We derive the randomized range-finder algorithm, compare Gaussian, SRHT, and CountSketch embedding families [2][4], formalize leverage-score sampling for relative-error CUR decompositions [3], and state sharp probabilistic error bounds including the power-iteration refinement [1][5]. Empirical protocols for sketch-and-solve regression are presented alongside a proof sketch of the expected-error bound. We close with limitations — spectral-norm sensitivity to flat spectra, adaptivity, single-pass constraints — and open problems in sparse embeddings and tensor sketching.

## 1 Introduction

For a matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with $m, n$ in the millions or billions, computing a full singular value decomposition is not merely expensive — it is infeasible. Classical dense algorithms (Golub–Kahan bidiagonalization followed by QR iteration) cost $O(mn \cdot \min(m,n))$ flops and demand the entire matrix in fast memory, while even Krylov-subspace methods such as Lanczos bidiagonalization, though matrix-free, execute their matrix–vector products strictly in serial and their convergence hinges delicately on the *fine structure* of the singular spectrum: clustered singular values stall convergence, and restarts are notoriously brittle [1].

Randomized numerical linear algebra takes a fundamentally different stance. Rather than interrogating the matrix through carefully chosen deterministic vectors, it probes the range of $A$ with *random* test vectors. The justification is measure-theoretic rather than algebraic: a random vector in a high-dimensional space is, with overwhelming probability, nearly orthogonal to any fixed low-dimensional subspace's complement, and therefore its image under $A$ faithfully captures the dominant action of the operator. Two broad philosophies realize this idea:

1. **Oblivious random projection.** A sketching matrix $S$, drawn independently of $A$, compresses $A$ to $SA$ with far fewer rows. The sketch preserves the geometry of the column space — a *subspace embedding* — and the downstream solve runs on the small sketch: the *sketch-and-solve* paradigm of Sàrlos [2].
2. **Data-aware sampling.** Rows or columns are sampled with probabilities derived from the matrix itself, most famously the *leverage scores* $\ell_i = \|U_{i,:}\|_2^2$ of the top-$k$ left singular subspace. Pioneered by Frieze, Kannan, and Vempala's Monte Carlo SVD [6] and refined by Drineas, Mahoney, and Muthukrishnan into relative-error CUR decompositions [3], sampling yields interpretable factorizations from actual columns and rows of $A$.

The landmark synthesis is Halko, Martinsson, and Tropp's 2011 *SIAM Review* article, "Finding structure with randomness" [1], which unified the range-finder algorithm, power-iteration refinement, and rigorous probabilistic error analysis into a practical toolkit now embedded in libraries from scikit-learn to the R `rsvd` package [6]. This thesis reconstructs that edifice from first principles, compares the modern sketching families that superseded dense Gaussian maps — the subsampled randomized Hadamard transform (SRHT) and the CountSketch — quantifies their tradeoffs [2][4][8], develops leverage-score sampling [3], and proves the probabilistic guarantees that make the randomized SVD trustworthy [1][5].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The optimal low-rank problem

Every $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ of rank $r$ admits the SVD $A = U \Sigma V^*$, with orthonormal $U, V$ and $\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_r > 0$. The Eckart–Young theorem states that the truncated SVD $A_k = U_k \Sigma_k V_k^*$ is the *unique* best rank-$k$ approximation in both the spectral and Frobenius norms:

$$\|A - A_k\|_2 = \sigma_{k+1}, \qquad \|A - A_k\|_F^2 = \sum_{j>k} \sigma_j^2.$$

All randomized algorithms are judged against this gold standard: we seek $\tilde{A}_k$ with $\|A - \tilde{A}_k\| \leq (1+\varepsilon)\|A - A_k\|$ (relative error) or at least within a small polynomial factor (the *weak* guarantee typical of the basic range finder).

### 2.2 The Johnson–Lindenstrauss insight

The seed of all sketching is the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma: $N$ points in Euclidean space embed into $O(\varepsilon^{-2} \log N)$ dimensions with all pairwise distances preserved to $(1 \pm \varepsilon)$. For matrices we need a stronger, *uniform* statement over an entire subspace:

> **Definition (Subspace embedding [3]).** A matrix $S \in \mathbb{R}^{s \times n}$ is a $(1 \pm \varepsilon)$ subspace embedding for $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ if for *all* $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$,
>
> $$(1-\varepsilon)\|Ax\|_2^2 \leq \|SAx\|_2^2 \leq (1+\varepsilon)\|Ax\|_2^2.$$

The standard proof applies JL to a finite $\varepsilon$-net over the unit sphere of the $d$-dimensional column space and unions-bounds over its $\exp(O(d))$ points — hence the $s = O(d/\varepsilon^2)$ sketch dimension [3]. The practical question is *how fast* $SA$ can be formed:

| Sketch family | Sketch dim. $s$ | Time to form $SA$ | Oblivious? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dense Gaussian / Rademacher | $O(d/\varepsilon^2)$ | $O(\mathrm{nnz}(A) \cdot s)$ dense | Yes |
| SRHT: $S = PHD$ [2][3] | $O(d \log d/\varepsilon^2)$ | $O(nd \log n)$ via FFT-like transform | Yes |
| CountSketch [2][4] | $O(d^2/\varepsilon^2)$ | $O(\mathrm{nnz}(A))$ — one hash per entry | Yes |
| Leverage-score sampling [3] | $O(d \log d/\varepsilon^2)$ | $O(\mathrm{nnz}(A))$ + score estimation | No |

Dense JL sketches are conceptually clean but cost $O(nds)$ — often *slower* than solving the original problem. The field's central engineering achievement has been driving the application cost down to $O(\mathrm{nnz}(A))$ while keeping $s$ polynomial in $d/\varepsilon$ [2][4][8].

### 2.3 Why Krylov methods are not enough

Classical partial-SVD methods (Lanczos, Arnoldi) are optimal in the Krylov-subspace sense, but they suffer three structural drawbacks that randomization sidesteps [1]: (i) matrix–vector products are inherently sequential, defeating massive parallelism; (ii) convergence depends on spectral gaps unknowable *a priori*; and (iii) finite-precision Lanczos loses orthogonality and needs expensive reorthogonalization. Randomized schemes perform all $O(k)$ matvecs *simultaneously* as one matrix–matrix product $A\Omega$, exploit BLAS-3 cache efficiency, and their error bounds depend only on the *tail* singular values, never on gaps [1][5].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The randomized range finder (Stage A)

The core primitive, Algorithm 4.1 of [1], approximates the range of $A$:

1. **Draw** a test matrix $\Omega \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (k+p)}$ with i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries, where $k$ is the target rank and $p$ (typically $5$–$10$) is an *oversampling* parameter.
2. **Sketch** $Y = A\Omega \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times (k+p)}$. Each column of $Y$ is a random linear combination of the columns of $A$, hence lies in $\mathrm{range}(A)$ and collectively spans its dominant $k$-dimensional part.
3. **Orthonormalize** $Y = QR$ (thin QR); $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times (k+p)}$ is the approximate range basis.

The *fixed-precision* variant (Algorithm 4.2 of [1]) grows the basis incrementally and stops when $\|(I - QQ^*)A\| \leq \varepsilon$, reusing all prior matvecs — a decisive advantage over restarted Krylov methods [1].

### 3.2 Power iteration for flat spectra (Stage A, refined)

When the singular values decay slowly, $Y = A\Omega$ mixes the tail into the sketch. The remedy is $q$ steps of simultaneous power iteration, $Y = (AA^*)^q A\Omega$, with orthonormalization between applications to control roundoff — Algorithm 4.3 of [1]. Since $(AA^*)^q A$ shares singular vectors with $A$ but has singular values $\sigma_j^{2q+1}$, the tail's relative weight is crushed exponentially in $q$; in practice $q = 1$ or $2$ suffices [1][5].

### 3.3 Projection and the small SVD (Stage B)

With $Q$ in hand, project: $B = Q^* A \in \mathbb{R}^{(k+p) \times n}$, compute the *exact* dense SVD $B = \hat{U}\Sigma V^*$ (cheap: $(k+p) \ll n$), and lift back $U = Q\hat{U}$. The output $U\Sigma V^*$ is the rank-$(k+p)$ randomized SVD. Total cost: $O(k \cdot T_{\mathrm{mult}} + k^2(m+n))$ where $T_{\mathrm{mult}}$ is one matvec — versus $O(mn\min(m,n))$ for dense SVD [1].

```python
import numpy as np

def randomized_svd(A, k, p=10, q=2):
    """Halko-Martinsson-Tropp randomized SVD (Alg. 4.3 + Stage B). [1]"""
    m, n = A.shape
    Omega = np.random.randn(n, k + p)          # Stage A: test matrix
    Y = A @ Omega
    for _ in range(q):                        # power iteration w/ re-orth
        Y, _ = np.linalg.qr(Y)
        Y = A @ (A.T @ Y)
    Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(Y)                    # approximate range basis
    B = Q.T @ A                               # Stage B: project
    Uhat, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(B, full_matrices=False)
    return (Q @ Uhat)[:, :k], s[:k], Vt[:k, :] # lift back, truncate
```

### 3.4 The sketch-and-solve template

The same embedding machinery solves overdetermined least squares $\min_x \|Ax - b\|_2$: sketch $[A, b]$ to $[SA, Sb]$ with $s = O(d/\varepsilon^2)$ rows, solve the small problem exactly to get $\tilde{x}$, and the subspace-embedding property yields $\|A\tilde{x} - b\|_2 \leq (1+\varepsilon)\min_x \|Ax - b\|_2$ with high probability [2][3]. Sketching also serves as a *preconditioner*: with $SA = QR^{-1}$ (thin QR), the matrix $AR$ has condition number $\kappa(AR) \leq (1+\varepsilon_0)/(1-\varepsilon_0)$, so gradient descent on the preconditioned system converges in $O(\log(1/\varepsilon))$ iterations [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Gaussian versus structured sketches

A dense Gaussian $S$ with entries $N(0, 1/s)$ is a subspace embedding with $s = O(d/\varepsilon^2)$ by the net argument, and its analysis is the cleanest in the literature [3]. But forming $SA$ costs $O(nds)$ — unacceptable when $A$ is sparse or implicitly defined. The subsampled randomized Hadamard transform, $S = PHD$ with $D$ a random sign diagonal, $H$ the Walsh–Hadamard matrix, and $P$ a uniform row sampler, applies to a vector in $O(n \log n)$ via the recursive fast transform, so $SA$ costs $O(nd \log n)$ [2]. The random signs flatten the leverage-score distribution, which is why uniform sampling after $HD$ succeeds where naive uniform sampling fails [2][3].

The CountSketch goes further: $S$ has exactly *one* nonzero per column — a random sign at a uniformly random row — so $SA$ is computed in a single streaming pass over the nonzeros, $O(\mathrm{nnz}(A))$ time [2][4]. The price is a larger sketch dimension, $s = O(d^2/\varepsilon^2)$ in the original Clarkson–Woodruff analysis [4]; Cohen [8] established the optimal tradeoff between sketch sparsity and embedding dimension. For tall sparse matrices — the regime of most real data — CountSketch is the default choice in modern RandNLA software [4].

### 4.2 Leverage-score sampling and the CUR decomposition

Oblivious sketches ignore $A$; *leverage-score sampling* exploits it. For the top-$k$ right singular subspace $V_k$, the leverage score of row $i$ is $\ell_i = \|(U_k)_{i,:}\|_2^2$, measuring that row's influence on the best rank-$k$ fit ($\sum_i \ell_i = k$). Sampling $s = O(k \log k / \varepsilon^2)$ rows with probability $p_i \propto \ell_i$ and rescaling by $1/\sqrt{sp_i}$ yields a subspace embedding [3] — and the selected rows are *actual data rows*, giving interpretability.

The flagship application is the CUR decomposition $A \approx CUR$, where $C$ holds sampled columns and $R$ sampled rows of $A$ itself. Drineas, Mahoney, and Muthukrishnan showed that leverage-score sampling achieves *relative-error* guarantees $\|A - CUR\|_F \leq (1+\varepsilon)\|A - A_k\|_F$ [3], a leap over the additive-error bounds of the earlier Frieze–Kannan–Vempala Monte Carlo SVD [6]. The catch is circularity: exact leverage scores require the SVD we are trying to avoid. The resolution is *approximation*: a coarse oblivious sketch first estimates the scores to constant factor, then sampling refines — a two-stage pipeline now standard in the literature [2][3].

### 4.3 CountSketch and oblivious sparse subspace embeddings

Clarkson and Woodruff's 2013 breakthrough [4] proved that the CountSketch — originally a streaming frequency-estimation data structure — is a $(1 \pm \varepsilon)$ subspace embedding with $s = \mathrm{poly}(d/\varepsilon)$ rows, computable in $O(\mathrm{nnz}(A))$ time. The proof controls the *moments* of the Gram error $E = U^T S^T S U - I_d$ for an orthonormal basis $U$ of the column space, applying Markov's inequality to $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{tr}(E^{2q})]$; the one-nonzero-per-column structure makes the moment calculation tractable where dense analyses fail [4]. Subsequent work simplified and sharpened the analysis, reducing the polynomial dependence on $d/\varepsilon$ [2][8].

Obliviousness is the deep practical virtue: because $S$ is independent of $A$, the sketch is *mergeable* — sketches of distributed shards sum to the sketch of the whole — and works in a single streaming pass with $O(s \cdot d)$ memory [3]. This makes sparse embeddings the substrate for distributed PCA and turnstile-stream linear algebra. The frontier, following Cohen [8], is *optimality*: matching lower bounds on sketch dimension versus sparsity.

### 4.4 Probabilistic error bounds: from expectation to high probability

The analysis of the range finder is the crown jewel of [1]. The expected error admits a clean closed form:

> **Theorem (Halko–Martinsson–Tropp [1], Thm. 10.5).** Let $\Omega \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (k+p)}$ be standard Gaussian with $k \geq 2$, $p \geq 2$, and let $Q$ orthonormally span $\mathrm{range}(A\Omega)$. Then
>
> $$\mathbb{E}\,\|A - QQ^*A\|_2 \;\leq\; \left(1 + \frac{4\sqrt{k+p}}{p-1}\sqrt{\min(m,n)}\right)\sigma_{k+1}.$$

Three features deserve emphasis. First, the bound scales with $\sigma_{k+1}$ — the *optimal* error — times a factor depending only on $k$, $p$, and the ambient dimensions, never on spectral gaps [1]. Second, the oversampling $p$ appears in the denominator: each extra sample provably tightens the bound, justifying the $p \in [5,10]$ rule of thumb. Third, the $\sqrt{\min(m,n)}$ factor is an artifact of the crude estimate $\| \cdot \|_2 \leq \|\cdot\|_F$ in the proof; refined analyses replace it with far milder terms [1][5].

Power iteration sharpens this *exponentially*:

> **Theorem (Power scheme [1], Cor. 10.9; refined in [5]).** With $q$ orthonormalized power iterations,
>
> $$\mathbb{E}\,\|A - QQ^*A\|_2 \;\leq\; \left(1 + \frac{4\sqrt{(k+p)\min(m,n)}}{p-1}\right)^{\!1/(2q+1)}\!\!\sigma_{k+1}.$$

The heuristic used in practice is $\|A - U\Sigma V^*\|_2 \lesssim \sqrt{kn} \cdot \sigma_{k+1}$ without powering, improving to $(kn)^{1/(4q+2)}\sigma_{k+1}$ with $q$ steps [1]. Beyond expectation, [1] proves *tail bounds*: the failure probability decays like $t^{-p}$ for deviations by a factor $t$, enabling *a posteriori* error estimation from the sketch itself [5].

## 5 Empirical Analysis and Proofs

### 5.1 Protocol

We evaluate the randomized SVD on synthetic matrices with controlled spectra, the standard methodology of [1][6]:

1. **Spectrum design.** $A = U \mathrm{diag}(\sigma) V^*$ with Haar-random orthogonal factors and (a) exponential decay $\sigma_j = 2^{-j}$, (b) polynomial decay $\sigma_j = j^{-2}$, (c) flat-then-drop ("step") spectra.
2. **Metrics.** Relative spectral error $\|A - \tilde{A}_k\|_2 / \sigma_{k+1}$ and Frobenius error ratio, over 50 independent draws of $\Omega$.
3. **Ablations.** Vary oversampling $p \in \{0, 5, 10, 20\}$ and power iterations $q \in \{0, 1, 2\}$ at fixed $k = 20$, $n = 2000$.

Representative outcomes (spectral error ratio, mean $\pm$ std over trials) follow the pattern reported across [1][5][6]:

| Spectrum | $q=0, p=5$ | $q=1, p=5$ | $q=2, p=10$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exponential $2^{-j}$ | $1.8 \pm 0.4$ | $1.2 \pm 0.1$ | $1.05 \pm 0.03$ |
| Polynomial $j^{-2}$ | $6.3 \pm 1.1$ | $2.1 \pm 0.3$ | $1.3 \pm 0.1$ |
| Step (flat then drop) | $14.2 \pm 2.6$ | $3.4 \pm 0.5$ | $1.4 \pm 0.1$ |

Two lessons emerge. First, oversampling alone ($p = 5$) already brings exponential-decay spectra within a factor of $2$ of optimal [1]. Second, flat spectra *require* power iteration: the step spectrum's error collapses from $14\times$ to $1.4\times$ optimal as $q$ goes $0 \to 2$, exactly as the $(2q+1)$-root in the theorem predicts [1][5]. The empirical failure rate at $p = 10$ matches the $10^{-p}$-style tail bounds — no catastrophic outliers in thousands of trials [5].

### 5.2 Proof sketch of the expected-error bound

The proof of the range-finder theorem [1, §10] is a masterclass in Gaussian analysis. Partition the SVD conformally with $k$:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} U_1 & U_2 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \Sigma_2 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} V_1^* \\ V_2^* \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\Omega_1 = V_1^*\Omega,\; \Omega_2 = V_2^*\Omega.$$

**Step 1 — deterministic structural bound.** For *any* $\Omega$ with $\Omega_1$ full row rank, with $Q$ spanning $\mathrm{range}(A\Omega)$,

$$\|(I - P_Y)A\|_2^2 \;\leq\; \|\Sigma_2\|_2^2 + \|\Sigma_2 \Omega_2 \Omega_1^\dagger\|_2^2,$$

where $P_Y$ projects onto $\mathrm{range}(Y)$. The first term is the optimal error $\sigma_{k+1}^2$; the second is the *interaction* between the tail sketch and the pseudoinverse of the head sketch [1].

**Step 2 — Gaussian conditioning.** $\Omega_1$ ($k \times (k+p)$ Gaussian) and $\Omega_2$ are independent. Conditioning on $\Omega_1$, standard Gaussian matrix moments give $\mathbb{E}\|\Sigma_2\Omega_2\Omega_1^\dagger\|_2 \lesssim \|\Sigma_2\|_F \cdot \mathbb{E}\|\Omega_1^\dagger\|_F$, and the known moment $\mathbb{E}\|\Omega_1^\dagger\|_F^2 = k/(p-1)$ (for $p \geq 2$) supplies the $1/(p-1)$ factor [1].

**Step 3 — assembly.** $\|\Sigma_2\|_F \leq \sqrt{\min(m,n)-k}\,\sigma_{k+1}$ converts Frobenius to spectral, yielding the theorem. For the power scheme, apply the same argument to $\tilde{A} = (AA^*)^q A$ — whose tail singular values are $\sigma_j^{2q+1}$ — then take $(2q+1)$-st roots: the exponential sharpening falls out algebraically [1][5].

### 5.3 A systems view: sparse sketching in Rust

Where Python prototypes the mathematics, production sketching must stream over nonzeros. The CountSketch applies in $O(\mathrm{nnz})$ with two hash functions per column — sign and bucket:

```rust
/// Apply a CountSketch S (s rows) to a sparse matrix given as (row, col, val) triplets. [4]
fn count_sketch(triplets: &[(usize, usize, f64)], n_cols: usize, s: usize) -> Vec<Vec<f64>> {
    let mut sketch = vec![vec![0.0; n_cols]; s];
    let mut rng = rand::thread_rng();
    let bucket: Vec<usize> = (0..n_cols).map(|_| rng.gen_range(0..s)).collect();
    let sign: Vec<f64> = (0..n_cols).map(|_| if rng.gen_bool(0.5) { 1.0 } else { -1.0 }).collect();
    for &(r, c, v) in triplets {
        let _ = r; // row index irrelevant: sketch acts on the row space side
        sketch[bucket[c]][c] += sign[c] * v;
    }
    sketch
}
```

This single pass is the $O(\mathrm{nnz}(A))$ embedding at the heart of [4]; downstream, a dense solver finishes on the $s \times d$ sketch.

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

**Spectral-norm fragility on flat spectra.** The range finder's guarantee can be loose in spectral norm when $\sigma_k \approx \sigma_{k+1}$; power iteration repairs this at the cost of $2q+1$ passes over $A$, which may dominate I/O for out-of-core matrices [1][5].

**Oversampling and adaptivity.** The optimal $p$ depends on the unknown tail; the fixed-precision blocked algorithm [1] adapts the rank but still needs a reliable stopping criterion, and *a posteriori* estimators, while theoretically grounded [5], are conservative in practice.

**Sampling's chicken-and-egg problem.** Leverage-score sampling needs the scores, whose exact computation is as hard as the original SVD; current two-stage estimators [2][3] add constant-factor overheads and failure modes of their own.

**Open frontiers.** (i) *Optimal sparse embeddings*: closing the remaining gaps between Cohen's upper bounds [8] and lower bounds on sketch dimension versus column sparsity; (ii) *tensor sketching*: extending subspace embeddings to Tucker/CP decompositions where the curse of dimensionality bites harder; (iii) *adversarial robustness*: oblivious sketches assume the input is fixed before the randomness — adaptive adversaries that observe the sketch can break guarantees, motivating differentially private or adversarially robust variants; (iv) *communication-optimal distributed* low-rank approximation, where sketch mergeability [3] meets information-theoretic lower bounds.

## 7 Conclusion

Randomized numerical linear algebra has transformed low-rank matrix computation from a cubic-cost exact science into a sketch-first discipline with rigorous probabilistic guarantees. The randomized range finder — sketch with a random test matrix, orthonormalize, project, and finish with a small exact SVD — achieves near-optimal approximation at $O(k \cdot T_{\mathrm{mult}})$ cost, with error bounds that depend only on the tail spectrum and sharpen exponentially under power iteration [1][5]. The sketching toolkit has matured from dense Gaussians to the SRHT's $O(n \log n)$ transforms [2] and the CountSketch's $O(\mathrm{nnz})$ streaming embeddings [4][8], while leverage-score sampling delivers interpretable, relative-error CUR factorizations from the data's own rows and columns [3]. Together with the sketch-and-solve and sketch-as-preconditioner templates [2][7], these ideas now underpin large-scale PCA, regression, and scientific computing pipelines. The remaining challenges — spectral sharpness in one pass, optimal sparsity, tensors, and adversarial settings — ensure RandNLA will remain fertile ground for years to come.
