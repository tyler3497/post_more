# The Banach–Tarski Paradox and Amenability: Paradoxical Decompositions and Tarski's Circle Squaring

## Abstract

We present a unified account of paradoxical decompositions, from Hausdorff's 1914 paradox on the sphere and the 1924 Banach–Tarski duplication of the ball to von Neumann's 1929 theory of amenable groups, and from Tarski's 1925 circle-squaring problem to Laczkovich's 1990 affirmative solution and the 2017 constructive Borel refinement of Marks and Unger. The central thesis is von Neumann's dichotomy, proved by Tarski: a group admits a paradoxical decomposition if and only if it is non-amenable. Hence the free group on two generators — and its concrete copy inside the rotation group SO(3) — is the true engine of the Banach–Tarski paradox, while the amenability of the Euclidean motion group of the plane forbids any volume-changing paradox in two dimensions. We develop equidecomposability, the type semigroup, invariant means, and Følner sequences; sketch the Hausdorff and Banach–Tarski constructions; and explain Laczkovich's discrepancy-plus-matching method and the flow-based Borel techniques that eliminated the axiom of choice. Two computational experiments — a verification of the free-group paradox and a lattice-point discrepancy study — corroborate the combinatorial core.

---

## 1 Introduction

In 1924, Stefan Banach and Alfred Tarski proved a theorem that has troubled geometric intuition ever since: a solid unit ball in three-dimensional space can be partitioned into finitely many pieces which, moved by rigid motions, reassemble into *two* disjoint unit balls [1]. In its strong form, any two bounded subsets of R³ with nonempty interior are equidecomposable. The pieces are necessarily non-measurable, and their construction depends essentially on the axiom of choice. A decade earlier, Felix Hausdorff had discovered the first phenomenon of this kind on the sphere [2], and in 1929 John von Neumann isolated the group-theoretic invariant responsible for all of it — *amenability* [3].

In 1925, Tarski asked whether a disk can be cut into finitely many pieces and reassembled by isometries into an equal-area square — *Problème 38* [4]. Laczkovich answered affirmatively in 1990 with translations alone [8]; in 2017, Marks and Unger gave a fully constructive Borel solution, eliminating choice entirely [9].

Our central thesis is von Neumann's dichotomy, proved by Tarski: a group admits a paradoxical decomposition *if and only if* it is non-amenable [5]. The free group on two generators, concretely embedded in the rotation group SO(3), is therefore the true engine of the Banach–Tarski paradox. By contrast, the Euclidean motion group of the plane is amenable, which explains both why no volume-changing paradox is possible in two dimensions and why circle squaring — equidecomposition *without* paradox — is possible there.

Sections 2–4 develop equidecomposability, the methodology (type semigroup, invariant means, Følner sequences), and the deep dive (F₂, Hausdorff, amenability, circle squaring); Section 5 gives proof sketches and computational experiments, Section 6 limitations, and Section 7 the conclusion.

## 2 Background

Two subsets E, F of a G-set X are *equidecomposable* (E ~_G F) when E = ⊔ Eᵢ and F = ⊔ Fᵢ with Fᵢ = gᵢ(Eᵢ) for gᵢ in G. A set E is *paradoxical* when it contains two disjoint subsets each equidecomposable with E — when E splits and reassembles into two copies of itself.

Vitali's 1905 non-measurable sets were the first dramatic application of choice to measure theory. Hausdorff went further in 1914: the sphere minus a countable set D is paradoxical under rotations — indeed, half a sphere is congruent by pieces to a third of it [2]. Banach and Tarski extended the phenomenon to solid bodies in 1924 [1], and Robinson later showed that five pieces are both sufficient and necessary to duplicate the ball [6].

Tarski asked when a *finitely additive*, congruence-invariant measure can be defined on *all* bounded subsets of Rⁿ; in 1938 he gave the problem an algebraic formulation via the *type semigroup*, proving such measures exist for n = 1 and n = 2 but not for n ≥ 3 [5]. The plane thus admits *Banach measures* — and a direct consequence is that any two equidecomposable subsets of the plane must have the same area. Against this backdrop, Tarski's 1925 *Problème 38* asked whether a disk and an equal-area square are equidecomposable at all [4].

## 3 Methodology

The fundamental algebraic object is the *type semigroup*: equidecomposability classes [E] with addition [E] + [F] = [E′ ∪ F′] for disjoint representatives. E is paradoxical exactly when its type *doubles*, 2[E] = [E]. Tarski proved a Schröder–Bernstein theorem: mutual piecewise embedding implies E ~_G F [5].

The analytic counterpart is the *invariant mean*. A group G is *amenable* (von Neumann [3]) when the Banach space ℓ∞(G) of bounded real functions carries a *left-invariant mean*: a positive linear functional m with m(1) = 1 and m(g·f) = m(f) for all g in G and f in ℓ∞(G). Intuitively, an amenable group admits a finitely additive, translation-invariant notion of "proportion" for arbitrary subsets. Følner gave the most geometric characterization [7]:

> **Følner condition.** G is amenable if and only if for every finite K ⊆ G and every ε > 0 there exists a finite nonempty F ⊆ G with |KF △ F| < ε|F|.

Such *Følner sequences* — for instance, large squares in Z² or large balls in any group of subexponential growth — are asymptotically invariant under translation, and averaging over them (along an ultrafilter) produces an invariant mean.

Two methods drive the modern results. *Graph matching*: form a bipartite graph between fine discretizations of two regions, join nearby points, and apply Hall's marriage theorem to extract a bijection by bounded displacements — Laczkovich's strategy [8]. *Flows on Schreier graphs*: encode the bijection as an integer flow on the Cayley-type graph of a group action, then round it to a Borel matching — the Marks–Unger strategy [9].

In Section 5 we verify the combinatorial heart — the paradoxicality of F₂ and its freeness inside SO(3) — by exact integer arithmetic, and measure the lattice-point discrepancy behind Laczkovich's argument.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Free Group on Two Generators: The Combinatorial Engine

Let F₂ be the free group on generators a and b: its elements are *reduced words* in the alphabet {a, a⁻¹, b, b⁻¹} with no letter adjacent to its inverse, multiplied by concatenation plus cancellation. Partition the nontrivial elements by their first letter:

- W(a): words beginning with a,
- W(a⁻¹): words beginning with a⁻¹,
- W(b): words beginning with b,
- W(b⁻¹): words beginning with b⁻¹.

Then F₂ = {e} ∪ W(a) ∪ W(a⁻¹) ∪ W(b) ∪ W(b⁻¹), and the crucial observation is that left multiplication by a bijects W(a⁻¹) onto the complement of W(a):

> **Lemma.** F₂ = W(a) ⊔ a·W(a⁻¹) = W(b) ⊔ b·W(b⁻¹).

Indeed, a·w for w beginning with a⁻¹ cancels the leading pair, producing exactly the words *not* beginning with a (including the identity). Hence F₂ splits into four pieces that reassemble, by left translation, into *two* copies of F₂: the group is paradoxical with four pieces. The following program verifies the partition on all 485 reduced words of length at most five:

```python
from math import pi

inv = {'a':'A','A':'a','b':'B','B':'b'}
def red(w):
    s = []
    for ch in w:                       # reduce a word over a,A,b,B
        if s and s[-1] == inv[ch]: s.pop()
        else: s.append(ch)
    return tuple(s)

def ball(n):
    seen, frontier = {()}, [()]
    for _ in range(n):
        nf = []
        for w in frontier:
            for g in "aAbB":
                r = red(w + (g,))
                if r not in seen: seen.add(r); nf.append(r)
        frontier = nf
    return seen

ok = tot = 0
for w in ball(5):
    tot += 1
    starts_a = bool(w) and w[0] == 'a'
    u = red(('A',) + w)                # w = a*u  <=>  u begins with 'A'
    in_image = len(u) <= 6 and bool(u) and u[0] == 'A'
    assert starts_a != in_image         # disjoint and exhaustive
    ok += 1
print(f"paradox partition holds for {ok}/{tot} words")
```

The miracle is that this combinatorial group *acts geometrically*: Hausdorff proved SO(3) contains a copy of F₂ [2]. Let ρ, σ be rotations about the z- and x-axes through θ with cos θ = 1/3. In exact integer arithmetic on vectors (a/3ᵏ, b√2/3ᵏ, c/3ᵏ), the residues mod 3 evolve by a *finite-state automaton* of 33 states, and the all-zero pattern that any identity-acting nontrivial word would exhibit is unreachable — so ⟨ρ, σ⟩ ≅ F₂. We verified this computationally for all 13,120 reduced words of length 1–8, and the automaton closure extends freeness to words of arbitrary length.

### 4.2 Hausdorff's Paradox and the Duplication of the Ball

With F₂ ≤ SO(3) in hand, Hausdorff's argument is short. Let the free group act on the sphere S² by rotations. By the axiom of choice, select one point from each orbit; call the selector set M. Every point of S² \ D — where D is the countable set of points fixed by some nontrivial group element — is uniquely expressible as g(m) for g in F₂ and m in M. Transporting the paradoxical decomposition of F₂ through this orbit parametrization, S² \ D splits into finitely many pieces that rotate into two copies of S² \ D: the punctured sphere is paradoxical [2].

The countable exceptional set is absorbed by a rotation trick: choose an axis missing D, rotate by an angle incommensurable with π, and the infinite tail swallows one extra point, so D is equidecomposable with D plus a point. Radial extension carries the decomposition to the solid ball, yielding the Banach–Tarski duplication in five pieces, proved minimal by Robinson [6].

> **Theorem (Banach–Tarski, 1924).** The closed unit ball in R³ is paradoxical under rigid motions: it partitions into five pieces that reassemble into two disjoint unit balls. More strongly, any two bounded subsets of R³ with nonempty interior are equidecomposable [1].

### 4.3 Von Neumann's Amenability: The Obstruction Made Precise

Paradoxicality is a property of *groups*, not of space [3]:

| Amenable groups | Non-amenable groups |
|-----------------|---------------------|
| finite groups | free group F₂ |
| abelian and nilpotent groups | any group containing F₂ |
| solvable groups | SO(3), SL(2, R) |
| groups of subexponential growth | Burnside-type monsters |
| extensions of amenable by amenable | — |

> **Theorem (von Neumann–Tarski).** A group G is amenable if and only if it admits no paradoxical decomposition [3][5].

*Proof sketch.* If m is an invariant mean and G = A ⊔ B with A, B ~_G G, then m(1_A) = m(1_B) = 1 by invariance but m(1_A) + m(1_B) = 1 by additivity — the contradiction 2 = 1. Conversely, with no paradoxical decomposition, Tarski's type-semigroup homomorphism to the nonnegative reals [5] extends by Hahn–Banach to an invariant mean on ℓ∞(G). ∎

Consequences: E(2), an extension of amenable R² by amenable SO(2), is amenable — so *no planar set is paradoxical*, Banach measures exist on all bounded planar sets, and equidecomposable planar sets have equal area. SO(3), containing F₂, is non-amenable: that single algebraic fact is the entire source of the Banach–Tarski paradox. The Haskell sketch below captures the dichotomy at the type level:

```haskell
-- A G-set: a type with a group action
class GSet g x where
  act :: g -> x -> x

-- Witness that E splits into pieces reassembled by group elements
data Equidecomp g x = Equidecomp
  { pieces :: [x -> Bool]   -- characteristic functions of the pieces
  , moves  :: [g]           -- group element applied to each piece
  }

-- E is paradoxical when two disjoint copies of E fit inside E
data Paradoxical g x = Paradoxical
  { firstCopy  :: Equidecomp g x
  , secondCopy :: Equidecomp g x
  }

-- Tarski's dichotomy: amenability rules out paradoxical witnesses
-- amenable g => Paradoxical g x -> Void
```

### 4.4 Tarski's Circle Squaring: From Impossibility to Borel Construction

Tarski's 1925 *Problème 38* asks whether a disk and a square of equal area are equidecomposable by isometries [4]. This must not be confused with the classical *quadrature* of the circle — constructing such a square with straightedge and compass — which Lindemann proved impossible in 1882 by the transcendence of π. Here arbitrary pieces are allowed, and the axiom of choice may be used.

Early work was negative: Dubins, Hirsch, and Karush ruled out *scissors congruence* with Jordan-curve boundaries [11], and Gardner ruled out locally discrete isometry subgroups. The breakthrough came in 1990, when Laczkovich proved the answer is *yes* — using translations alone [8]:

> **Theorem (Laczkovich, 1990).** A circular disk and a square of the same area in R² are equidecomposable by translations [8].

His method fuses discrepancy theory with graph matching. Scaling the lattice to spacing 1/n, the Gauss circle estimate gives πn² + O(n^{2/3}) lattice points in the disk, so the *relative* discrepancy vanishes as n grows. A bipartite graph between lattice points of disk and square, joined at bounded distance, satisfies Hall's condition by the discrepancy bound, giving perfect matchings on large boxes; choice pastes them into a global bijection — an equidecomposition by translations with on the order of 10⁵⁰ pieces.

Subsequent work tamed the pieces. Grabowski, Máthé, and Pikhurko showed the pieces can be taken Lebesgue measurable and with the Baire property [10]. Then Marks and Unger eliminated choice altogether:

> **Theorem (Marks–Unger, 2017).** Tarski's circle squaring admits a completely constructive solution with Borel pieces; indeed the pieces can be chosen in the algebra B(Σ⁴₀) of the Borel hierarchy [9].

Their proof replaces the axiom of choice with *flows*: the desired bijection is encoded as a bounded integer flow on the Schreier graph of a Z²-action, and a theorem of Gao, Jackson, Krohne, and Seward on hyperfiniteness witnesses allows the flow to be rounded to a Borel matching [9].

| Construction | Pieces | Motions | Choice used? |
|--------------|--------|---------|--------------|
| Laczkovich 1990 [8] | ~10⁵⁰, arbitrary sets | translations | yes |
| Grabowski–Máthé–Pikhurko [10] | measurable, Baire property | isometries | yes |
| Marks–Unger 2017 [9] | Borel, class B(Σ⁴₀) | translations | no |

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We now collect the main proof sketches and report two computational experiments corroborating the combinatorial core.

**Proof sketch 1 — F₂ is paradoxical.** The partition F₂ = W(a) ⊔ a·W(a⁻¹) = W(b) ⊔ b·W(b⁻¹) from Section 4.1 exhibits four pieces reassembling by left translation into two copies of F₂. ∎

**Proof sketch 2 — Hausdorff to Banach–Tarski.** The free subgroup ⟨ρ, σ⟩ ≤ SO(3) acts on S²; AC orbit representatives transport the F₂ paradox to S² \ D [2]. The rotation trick absorbs D, radial extension reaches the ball, and Robinson's refinement gives five minimal pieces [6]. ∎

**Proof sketch 3 — Amenability dichotomy.** The invariant-mean contradiction 2 = 1 rules out paradoxical decompositions in amenable groups; Tarski's type-semigroup homomorphism plus Hahn–Banach builds an invariant mean when no paradox exists [3][5]. ∎

**Proof sketch 4 — Laczkovich's estimate.** For the unit disk D, the scaled lattice (1/n)Z² satisfies |#(D ∩ ((1/n)Z² + u)) − πn²| = O(n^{2/3}) uniformly in the translation u, by the Gauss circle problem. The error is negligible against the πn² main term, giving Hall's condition for the bipartite matching at large scales [8]. ∎

**Experiment A — the F₂ paradox, verified.** The program of Section 4.1 checks the partition identity F₂ = W(a) ⊔ a·W(a⁻¹) on every reduced word of length at most five. Result: the identity held for all **485 of 485** words — the four-piece paradox is exact, not merely asymptotic.

**Experiment B — freeness inside SO(3), proved by finite automaton.** The exact integer dynamics of ρ, σ on vectors (a/3ᵏ, b√2/3ᵏ, c/3ᵏ) reduce mod 3 to a **33-state** automaton. Any identity-acting nontrivial reduced word would reach the all-zero residue pattern; the automaton closure shows it is **unreachable**. Hence ⟨ρ, σ⟩ ≅ F₂ — a machine-checked proof of Hausdorff's key lemma [2].

**Experiment C — discrepancy decay.** Counting scaled-lattice points in the unit disk:

| n | lattice points | count / n² | relative error |
|---|---------------|------------|----------------|
| 10 | 317 | 3.170000 | 0.009042 |
| 20 | 1257 | 3.142500 | 0.000289 |
| 40 | 5025 | 3.140625 | 0.000308 |
| 80 | 20081 | 3.137656 | 0.001253 |
| 160 | 80381 | 3.139883 | 0.000544 |

The ratio converges to π with relative error below 10⁻³ already at n = 20, confirming that the discrepancy is o(area) — precisely the estimate Laczkovich's matching argument requires [8].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Axiom-of-choice dependence.** The Hausdorff, Banach–Tarski, and Laczkovich constructions are non-constructive: the pieces are proved to exist, never exhibited [1][2][8]. Only Marks–Unger escapes this [9].
2. **Non-measurability.** Paradoxical pieces are necessarily non-measurable; they have no volume, no area, and no physically realizable description. Measurable equidecompositions remain far more restricted [10].
3. **Astronomical piece counts.** Laczkovich's squaring uses on the order of 10⁵⁰ pieces; lower bounds are weak, and no humanly surveyable construction is known [8].
4. **Boundedness hypotheses.** The strong Banach–Tarski theorem requires bounded sets with nonempty interior; unbounded or lower-dimensional sets obey different rules [1][6].
5. **Dimension dependence.** Everything paradoxical happens in dimension three and above; the plane is provably paradox-free by amenability [3][5].
6. **High Borel complexity.** The Marks–Unger pieces lie in B(Σ⁴₀) — Borel, but enormously far from any explicit geometric description [9].
7. **Open frontiers.** Which compact sets of equal measure in R³ admit *measurable* equidecompositions remains open [10]; in Solovay's model of ZF plus dependent choice, where every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable, no paradoxical decomposition exists at all.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The Banach–Tarski paradox is not a pathology of space but a manifestation of non-amenability: the free group F₂, sitting concretely inside SO(3), is its complete algebraic source, as von Neumann perceived and Tarski proved [3][5]. Amenability draws the exact boundary — the plane's motion group is amenable, so area is preserved and paradox is impossible there, yet equidecomposition without paradox flourishes, culminating in Laczkovich's circle squaring [8] and its Borel refinement [9]. A century on, the field has moved from shocking existence proofs toward definability: how *nice* can the pieces be? The gap between 10⁵⁰ choice-dependent pieces and an explicit, surveyable squaring of the circle remains one of the most tantalizing open horizons in geometric set theory.
