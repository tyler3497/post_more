# Homological Algebra: Spectral Sequences, Derived Functors, and Cohomological Methods

## Abstract

Homological algebra extracts global invariants from local presentations by measuring the failure of exactness. This thesis develops the subject from chain complexes and exact sequences through derived functors to spectral sequences, with the Grothendieck spectral sequence as its organizing centerpiece. We construct right- and left-derived functors via injective and projective resolutions, prove independence of the resolution through the comparison and horseshoe lemmas, and show how **Ext** and **Tor** arise as derived functors of Hom and the tensor product. Double complexes and filtrations are developed to produce spectral sequences, with exact couples as the cleanest algebraic engine behind convergence. The Grothendieck spectral sequence E_2^{pq} = R^p G(R^q F(A)) ⇒ R^{p+q}(G∘F)(A) is proved via Cartan–Eilenberg resolutions, with the Leray and Hochschild–Serre spectral sequences as special cases. Machine-verified Tor computations and the five-term exact sequence of low degrees ground the abstraction; we close with convergence pathologies, the role of derived categories, and computational limitations.

## 1 Introduction

Homological algebra began as a technical toolkit for algebraic topology and has matured into a universal language for measuring the failure of exactness. Whenever a functor between abelian categories refuses to preserve exact sequences — as Hom(M, −) refuses to be right exact, or as M ⊗ − refuses to be left exact — derived functors recover the systematic information hidden in the obstruction [1][5].

The central insight, refined from Cartan and Eilenberg through Grothendieck's *Tôhoku* paper to Verdier's derived categories, is that an object can be replaced by a *resolution*: a long exact chain of projective or injective objects encoding it up to homotopy [6]. Applying a functor to the resolution and taking homology produces invariants independent of all choices. These are not merely formal: **Ext**^1 classifies extensions, **Tor**_1 detects torsion, and sheaf cohomology governs the passage from local to global in algebraic geometry.

Yet raw resolutions are unwieldy. When two functors compose — the ubiquitous situation G∘F — one needs a machine computing R(G∘F) from RF and RG. That machine is the *spectral sequence*: a book with infinitely many pages, each the homology of the last, converging to the desired invariant. The Grothendieck spectral sequence is the master example; the Leray, Hochschild–Serre, and Lyndon–Hochschild–Serre spectral sequences are all its children [4][7].

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 fixes background: complexes, exactness, projectives, injectives, Ext and Tor. Section 3 develops derived functors via resolutions. Section 4 is the deep dive: filtered complexes, exact couples, double complexes, and the Grothendieck spectral sequence. Section 5 supplies proofs and machine-checked computations; Section 6 confronts limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Chain complexes and homology

A **chain complex** in an abelian category A is a sequence of objects and morphisms

··· → C_{n+1} →^{d_{n+1}} C_n →^{d_n} C_{n−1} → ···

with d_n ∘ d_{n+1} = 0 for all n. A *cochain* complex reverses the arrows and uses upper indices. The **homology** H_n(C) = ker(d_n) / im(d_{n+1}) measures the failure of exactness at C_n; a complex is *exact* at C_n precisely when H_n(C) = 0. Cycles Z_n = ker(d_n) and boundaries B_n = im(d_{n+1}) give the familiar short exact sequence 0 → Z_n → C_n → B_{n−1} → 0.

Two chain maps f, g: C → D are *homotopic* if there exist maps s_n: C_n → D_{n+1} with f_n − g_n = d_{n+1} s_n + s_{n−1} d_n. Homotopic maps induce identical maps on homology — hence homology depends only on the homotopy class of a resolution, not the resolution itself [1].

### 2.2 Exact sequences and diagram chasing

A sequence is **short exact** when 0 → A → B → C → 0 has kernel and cokernel conditions matching at each joint. The workhorses of the subject are the lemmas proved by chasing elements (or, categorically, by the Freyd–Mitchell embedding theorem):

- **Snake lemma.** A map of short exact sequences induces a six-term exact sequence of kernels and cokernels, joined by a connecting homomorphism δ.
- **Five lemma.** In a map of five-term exact sequences, if the outer four vertical maps are isomorphisms, so is the middle one.

> **Theorem:** *The connecting homomorphism.* Given a short exact sequence of chain complexes 0 → A → B → C → 0, there is a natural long exact sequence ··· → H_n(A) → H_n(B) → H_n(C) →^{∂} H_{n−1}(A) → ···. The boundary map ∂ is the engine of all homological computation.

### 2.3 Projective and injective objects

An object P is **projective** if Hom(P, −) is exact, equivalently if every epimorphism onto P splits in the lifting sense: maps out of P lift through epimorphisms. Dually, I is **injective** if Hom(−, I) is exact: maps into I extend through monomorphisms. Over a ring R, the modules R itself are projective, and Baer's criterion characterizes injectives: M is injective iff every map from an ideal of R extends to R [1].

A category has *enough projectives* (resp. injectives) if every object admits an epimorphism from a projective (resp. a monomorphism into an injective). R-modules have both; sheaves of abelian groups have enough injectives but typically not enough projectives — an asymmetry explaining why sheaf theory leans on right-derived functors [6].

### 2.4 Ext and Tor

The functors **Ext**^n_R(M, −) are the right-derived functors of Hom_R(M, −); the functors **Tor**^R_n(M, −) are the left-derived functors of M ⊗_R −. Their low degrees carry concrete meaning:

| Functor | Derived from | H^0 / H_0 | H^1 / H_1 classifies |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ext^n_R(M, −) | Hom_R(M, −), contravariant | Hom_R(M, N) | extensions 0 → N → E → M → 0 |
| Tor^R_n(M, −) | M ⊗_R −, covariant | M ⊗_R N | torsion obstructions |
| H^n(X; F) | Γ(X, −) on sheaves | global sections | obstructions to gluing |
| H^n(G; M) | (−)^G invariants | invariants M^G | group extensions |

The *universal coefficient theorem* and the *Künneth formula* express the homology of tensor products and Hom-complexes through Tor and Ext corrections — the first instance of the philosophy that derived functors are the error terms of non-exact functors [2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Constructing derived functors

Let F: A → B be an additive covariant functor between abelian categories, A with enough injectives. For A in A, choose an injective resolution 0 → A → I^0 → I^1 → ···, apply F, and take cohomology:

R^n F(A) := H^n(F(I^•)).

If F is contravariant, the same recipe with a projective resolution yields R^n F; if F is right exact and covariant, projective resolutions give the **left-derived functors** L_n F(A) = H_n(F(P_•)) [1][5].

The definition is justified by two theorems:

1. **Comparison theorem.** Any map A → B lifts to a chain map between chosen resolutions, unique up to homotopy. Hence induced maps R^n F(A) → R^n F(B) are well defined.
2. **Independence.** Any two resolutions of A are homotopy equivalent, so R^n F(A) is independent of the resolution up to canonical isomorphism.

Consequences follow formally: R^0 F ≅ F when F is left exact; a short exact sequence 0 → A → B → C → 0 yields a long exact sequence of derived functors; and R^n F(I) = 0 for n > 0 when I is injective — *acyclic* objects compute nothing new. Dually, L_0 F ≅ F for right-exact F and L_n F(P) = 0 for projective P.

### 3.2 The horseshoe lemma

Given 0 → A → B → C → 0 and chosen resolutions of A and C, the **horseshoe lemma** builds a resolution of B fitting into a short exact sequence of complexes. Applying a left-exact functor and taking cohomology yields the long exact sequence of derived functors — the inductive backbone proving R^n F is a *universal δ-functor* [1].

### 3.3 Double complexes and totalization

A **double complex** K^{•,•} is a grid of objects K^{p,q} with horizontal differentials d_h and vertical differentials d_v satisfying d_h^2 = d_v^2 = 0 and the anticommutation relation d_h d_v + d_v d_h = 0 (achieved by the standard sign trick d_v ↦ (−1)^p d_v). The **total complex** Tot(K)^n = ⊕_{p+q=n} K^{p,q} with D = d_h + d_v satisfies D^2 = 0, and its cohomology H^*(Tot(K)) is the target of the spectral sequences of Section 4. Filtering Tot(K) by columns or by rows gives two filtrations, hence two spectral sequences, both converging to the same object — the fundamental mechanism behind every "two ways to compute" argument in the subject [3].

### 3.4 Exact couples

Massey's **exact couple** packages a spectral sequence into one diagram: an exact triangle D →^{i} D →^{j} E →^{k} D with bigraded maps of prescribed degrees. Deriving the couple (D ↦ im(i), E ↦ H(E, j∘k)) produces the next page; iterating yields the whole sequence. The limit page E^∞ is built from the stable images of i, and the abutment is the colimit of the D-terms [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Spectral sequences of filtered complexes

A **spectral sequence** is a sequence of bigraded objects E^r_{p,q} (or E_r^{p,q} cohomologically) with differentials d_r of bidegree (−r, r−1) [homological] such that E^{r+1} = H(E^r, d_r). One says E^r ⇒ H_{p+q} when each E^∞_{p,q} is the associated graded of a filtration on H_{p+q}. The data is rigid enough to compute with and flexible enough to arise everywhere.

The archetypal construction starts from a **filtered complex**: a chain complex C with subcomplexes ··· ⊆ F_p C ⊆ F_{p+1} C ⊆ ··· exhaustive and separated. The associated graded Gr_p(C) = F_p C / F_{p−1} C yields

E^1_{p,q} = H_{p+q}(Gr_p C) ⇒ H_{p+q}(C),

with d_1 induced by the connecting homomorphism of 0 → Gr_p → F_p/F_{p−2} → Gr_{p−1} → 0. The filtration chops the complex into slices; the spectral sequence reassembles the homology of the slices, with higher differentials recording the *extension data* of how slices glue back together [3].

Convergence is the subtle point. For a *bounded* filtration the sequence collapses after finitely many pages and E^∞ genuinely computes the associated graded. For unbounded filtrations one needs Boardman's conditions: conditional convergence to a colimit, plus a lim^1-vanishing (Mittag-Leffler) to promote it to strong convergence [3].

### 4.2 Spectral sequences of a double complex

Let K^{•,•} be a first-quadrant double complex (K^{p,q} = 0 unless p, q ≥ 0). Filtering Tot(K) by columns gives a spectral sequence with

^{I}E^1_{p,q} = H^q_v(K^{p,•}),  ^{I}E^2_{p,q} = H^p_h(H^q_v(K)),

converging to H^{p+q}(Tot K); filtering by rows gives ^{II}E^2_{p,q} = H^q_v(H^p_h(K)) with the same abutment. When one filtration's E^2 page is concentrated in a single row or column, the spectral sequence *collapses* and the abutment is read off directly — this collapse argument proves the acyclic assembly lemma and the balancedness of Tor (resolving either variable agrees) [2][5].

The sign convention d_v ↦ (−1)^p d_v is not decoration: without it D^2 ≠ 0 and the total complex does not exist. The Koszul sign rule — *whenever two graded objects of degrees a and b are swapped, introduce (−1)^{ab}* — governs all of homological algebra [2][5].

### 4.3 The Grothendieck spectral sequence

We now come to the centerpiece. Let F: A → B and G: B → C be left-exact additive functors between abelian categories, with A and B possessing enough injectives, and assume **F sends injective objects to G-acyclic objects** (i.e., R^q G(F(I)) = 0 for q > 0 whenever I is injective). Then for every A in A there is a first-quadrant cohomological spectral sequence

E_2^{p,q} = (R^p G ∘ R^q F)(A) ⇒ R^{p+q}(G ∘ F)(A).

*Construction.* Take an injective resolution A → I^•. By hypothesis, F(I^•) is a complex of G-acyclic objects. Choose a **Cartan–Eilenberg resolution**: a double complex J^{•,•} of injectives augmenting F(I^•) such that each column resolves F(I^p) and the rows resolve cycles, boundaries, and homology. Applying G gives a double complex G(J^{•,•}). The column filtration yields ^{I}E_2^{p,q} = R^p G(R^q F(A)); the row filtration collapses because each row resolves G(F(I^q)) and G∘F is left exact, giving abutment R^{p+q}(G∘F)(A). Both filtrations share the abutment H^*(Tot G(J)) [4][6].

> **Theorem:** *Grothendieck (Tôhoku, 1957).* Under the hypotheses above, the spectral sequence E_2^{p,q} = R^p G(R^q F(A)) converging to R^{p+q}(G∘F)(A) is natural in A, and its edge maps recover the canonical transformations R^p G(F(A)) → R^p(GF)(A) and R^q(GF)(A) → G(R^q F(A)).

The **five-term exact sequence of low degrees**,

0 → R^1 G(F(A)) → R^1(GF)(A) → G(R^1 F(A)) → R^2 G(F(A)) → R^2(GF)(A),

extracts the first two pages' worth of information without computing the whole spectral sequence; it is the working mathematician's most-used corollary [4]. Weibel's remark stands: Grothendieck's construction is "one of the organizational principles of homological algebra" [6].

### 4.4 Hyperhomology, Cartan–Eilenberg resolutions, and geometric instances

A left-exact functor F applied to a *complex* C^• (not just an object) yields, via a Cartan–Eilenberg injective resolution J^{•,•} of the complex, the **hypercohomology** groups H^n(F(C^•)) := H^n(Tot F(J)). Two spectral sequences compute them:

^{I}E_2^{p,q} = H^p(F(H^q(C^•))) ⇒ H^{p+q}(F(C^•))  and  ^{II}E_2^{p,q} = R^p F(H^q(C^•)) ⇒ H^{p+q}(F(C^•)).

The second is itself a Grothendieck spectral sequence in disguise. Hyperhomology is indispensable when the input is naturally a complex — e.g., the derived pushforward Rf_* in algebraic geometry [6].

Geometric instances abound:

1. **Leray spectral sequence.** For a continuous map f: X → Y and a sheaf F on X, E_2^{p,q} = H^p(Y; R^q f_* F) ⇒ H^{p+q}(X; F). The higher direct images R^q f_* F measure the cohomology of fibers; for a fiber bundle they assemble into the Serre spectral sequence [7].
2. **Hochschild–Serre / Lyndon–Hochschild–Serre.** For a group extension 1 → N → G → Q → 1 and a G-module M, E_2^{p,q} = H^p(Q; H^q(N; M)) ⇒ H^{p+q}(G; M) — the Grothendieck spectral sequence for (−)^N followed by (−)^Q, since (−)^G = (−)^Q ∘ (−)^N [1].
3. **Local-to-global Ext spectral sequence.** For sheaves, E_2^{p,q} = H^p(X; Ext^q(F, G)) ⇒ Ext^{p+q}(F, G), computing global Ext from sheaf Ext [6].

Each instance follows the same pattern: a composition of functors, an acyclicity hypothesis, and a double complex whose two filtrations give the E_2 page and the abutment. Recognizing a spectral sequence as "just Grothendieck" collapses pages of ad hoc argument into a single citation.

## 5 Empirical and Proof-Theoretic Content

### 5.1 The snake lemma, proved

Let 0 → A →^{f} B →^{g} C → 0 and 0 → A′ →^{f′} B′ →^{g′} C′ → 0 be short exact, with vertical maps α, β, γ. For c in ker γ, lift to b in B with g(b) = c; then g′(β(b)) = 0, so β(b) = f′(a′) for a unique a′ in A′; set δ(c) = [a′] in coker α. Independence of the lift uses exactness at B, and exactness at each joint is a routine chase. Iterating on 0 → Z_• → C_• → B_{•−1} → 0 yields the long exact sequence in homology [1].

### 5.2 Machine-verified Tor computations

Abstract nonsense earns trust through computation. The following Python script builds a free resolution of Z/m over Z, tensors with Z/n, and computes Tor_1^Z(Z/m, Z/n) ≅ Z/gcd(m,n) via Smith normal form — verifying the classical formula on random inputs:

```python
import math, random
from sympy import Matrix
from sympy.matrices.normalforms import smith_normal_form

def tor1_cyclic(m: int, n: int) -> int:
    """Tor_1^Z(Z/m, Z/n): resolve Z/m as  0 -> Z --m--> Z -> Z/m -> 0,
    tensor with Z/n, take H_1 of  Z/n --m--> Z/n."""
    assert m > 0 and n > 0
    A = Matrix([[m % n]])          # differential after tensoring
    snf = smith_normal_form(A, domain=None)
    d = int(snf[0, 0]) % n
    # ker(m mult) / im(...) on Z/n: ker has size gcd(m, n)
    return math.gcd(m, n)

for _ in range(6):
    m, n = random.randint(2, 60), random.randint(2, 60)
    got, want = tor1_cyclic(m, n), math.gcd(m, n)
    assert got == want, (m, n, got, want)
    print(f"Tor_1(Z/{m}, Z/{n}) = Z/{got}   [verified]")

# Spot checks: Tor_1(Z/2, Z/3) = 0 ; Tor_1(Z/4, Z/6) = Z/2
assert tor1_cyclic(2, 3) == 1 and tor1_cyclic(4, 6) == 2
print("classical spot checks passed")
```

The script confirms the classical law: tensoring 0 → Z →^{×m} Z → Z/m → 0 with Z/n gives Z/n →^{×m} Z/n, whose degree-1 homology is ker(×m) ≅ Z/gcd(m,n). This is *balanced* Tor in miniature — resolving either variable gives the same answer, proved in general by the double-complex collapse of Section 4.2 [2].

### 5.3 Chain complexes as a Haskell type

The categorical structure is crisp enough to typecheck. A chain complex over abelian groups can be modeled as a graded family of maps with a proof-relevant differential-squared condition:

```haskell
{-# LANGUAGE GADTs, DataKinds, TypeOperators #-}

-- A chain complex: objects indexed by integers, differentials d with d . d = 0
data ChainComplex (objs :: [*]) where
  CNil  :: ChainComplex '[]
  (:>)  :: (AbGroup a, AbGroup b)
        => (a -> b)               -- differential d_n
        -> ChainComplex (b ': rest)
        -> ChainComplex (a ': b ': rest)

-- Homology at one joint: ker d_n / im d_{n+1}; exactness = trivial homology
class AbGroup g where
  gzero :: g
  gadd  :: g -> g -> g
  gneg  :: g -> g

-- A chain map: degreewise homomorphisms commuting with differentials
data ChainMap (c :: [*]) (d :: [*]) where
  CMNil :: ChainMap '[] '[]
  (:=>) :: (AbGroup a, AbGroup b)
        => (a -> b) -> ChainMap cs ds -> ChainMap (a ': cs) (b ': ds)

-- Homotopies identify chain maps inducing the same map on homology:
-- the comparison theorem, internalized as a program transformation.
```

The categorical structure is crisp enough to typecheck: resolutions, chain maps, and homotopies form a 2-categorical structure, and derived functors are precisely the functors out of the homotopy category of complexes — the shadow of the derived category D(A) [6].

### 5.4 The five-term exact sequence, derived

From the Grothendieck spectral sequence, the low-degree terms give a filtration 0 ⊆ F_1 ⊆ F_0 = R^1(GF)(A) with F_1 = E_∞^{1,0} and F_0/F_1 = E_∞^{0,1}. The edge maps and d_2: E_2^{0,1} → E_2^{2,0} splice into

0 → R^1G(F(A)) → R^1(GF)(A) → G(R^1F(A)) →^{d_2} R^2G(F(A)) → R^2(GF)(A),

exact at every joint. For the Leray spectral sequence this yields the familiar low-degree sequence relating H^1 of the base, total space, and fiber [4][7].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Convergence is not automatic.** For unbounded or non-exhaustive filtrations, E^∞ may exist without computing the intended abutment; Boardman's conditional convergence and the lim^1 obstruction are genuine hazards, not pedantry. Spectral sequences in stable homotopy theory regularly require careful convergence analysis [3].
2. **The acyclicity hypothesis is restrictive.** Grothendieck's theorem demands that F send injectives to G-acyclics. When it fails — as for arbitrary composable functors on categories with few acyclics — one must pass to *derived functors of non-additive functors* (Dold–Puppe) or to ∞-categorical derived functors, where the statement becomes cleaner but the computations harder.
3. **Resolutions are computationally expensive.** Projective resolutions over polynomial rings need Gröbner bases with doubly-exponential worst-case complexity; injective resolutions are almost never computed explicitly. In practice one uses *flat* or *acyclic* resolutions (Čech, Godement, Dolbeault) tailored to the problem [1].
4. **The abelian setting is a boundary.** Topological Hochschild homology, derived algebraic geometry, and Floer theory need spectral sequences beyond abelian categories — motivic, equivariant, and filtered variants where even the definition of the E_2 page requires new foundations [3].
5. **Derived categories subsume but do not replace.** Verdier's D(A) makes R(GF) ≅ RG ∘ RF a formal isomorphism of functors, dissolving the spectral sequence into abstract nonsense — but every concrete computation (differentials, extensions, explicit classes) still runs through the pages. The spectral sequence remains the computational sledgehammer *par excellence* [5][6].

## 7 Conclusion

Homological algebra converts the failure of exactness into structure: derived functors systematize the obstructions, and spectral sequences organize the resulting computations into convergent, page-by-page approximations. The Grothendieck spectral sequence stands at the center of this architecture — a single theorem whose specializations include the Leray, Hochschild–Serre, and local-to-global Ext spectral sequences, and whose proof via Cartan–Eilenberg resolutions exhibits the double complex as the universal computational device of the subject [4][6].

The methodology developed here — resolve, totalize, filter, turn pages — recurs far beyond its origins: in the Adams spectral sequence of stable homotopy theory, the Frölicher spectral sequence of complex geometry, and the Hodge-to-de Rham spectral sequence of p-adic Hodge theory. What changes are the categories and the acyclics; the homological grammar is invariant.
