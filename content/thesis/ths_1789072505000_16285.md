# Reed–Solomon Codes and the Guruswami–Sudan List Decoder: Interpolation, Factorization, and Capacity-Achieving List Recovery

## Abstract

Reed–Solomon (RS) codes are the most deployed family of algebraic error-correcting codes, yet for nearly four decades their efficient decoding was confined to half the minimum distance. This article develops the theory that broke that barrier: Madhu Sudan's 1997 interpolation-based list decoder, the Guruswami–Sudan framework of 1999 that introduced *multiplicities* to reach the Johnson bound of $n-\sqrt{nk}$ errors, the Parvaresh–Vardy correlated-polynomial construction, and the folded Reed–Solomon codes of Guruswami and Rudra that finally achieved *list-decoding capacity* — correcting a $1-R-\varepsilon$ fraction of errors at rate $R$ in polynomial time. We present the encoding as polynomial evaluation, the classical Berlekamp–Welch unique decoder, the interpolation-plus-factorization paradigm with its counting argument, the geometry of folded and multiplicity codes, the role of *list recovery* in alphabet reduction, and applications from optical media to distributed storage and probabilistically checkable proofs. Numerical comparisons of decoding radii, a worked interpolation sketch, and a discussion of current limitations — alphabet growth, list-size blowup, and derandomization — complete the picture.

## 1 Introduction

Few objects in theoretical computer science combine mathematical elegance with industrial ubiquity as completely as Reed–Solomon codes. Introduced by Irving Reed and Gustave Solomon in 1960 as *polynomial codes over certain finite fields* [1], they encode a message as the coefficients of a low-degree polynomial and transmit its evaluations at a set of field elements. Because a nonzero polynomial of degree less than $k$ has at most $k-1$ roots, any two distinct codewords differ in at least $n-k+1$ positions: RS codes meet the Singleton bound with equality and are therefore *maximum distance separable* (MDS). This optimality, together with efficient decoding, made them the backbone of the compact disc, DVD, QR codes, and satellite links [10].

Yet a fundamental ceiling constrained RS decoding for almost forty years. The classical algorithms — syndrome-based Berlekamp–Massey decoding and the Berlekamp–Welch rational interpolation method [2] — correct only up to $\lfloor (d-1)/2 \rfloor$ errors, half the minimum distance. Elias had already observed in 1957 that *list decoding* — returning all codewords within a larger radius rather than insisting on uniqueness — could in principle reach far beyond this bound [11]. But no efficient list decoder for RS codes existed until 1997, when Sudan showed that decoding could be recast as *bivariate interpolation followed by factorization* [3]. Two years later, Guruswami and Sudan added the decisive ingredient of *multiplicities*, pushing the radius all the way to the Johnson bound $n - \sqrt{nk}$ [4].

This article is a self-contained exposition of that arc and its culmination: the capacity-achieving folded Reed–Solomon codes of Guruswami and Rudra [6], the linear-algebraic refinements of Guruswami and Wang [7], multiplicity codes [8], and the notion of list recovery that ties the story together.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Polynomial evaluation codes

Fix a finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$ and $n \le q$ distinct *evaluation points* $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n \in \mathbb{F}_q$. The Reed–Solomon code $\mathrm{RS}_q(n,k)$ consists of all vectors

$$\mathcal{C} = \left\{ (f(\alpha_1), \dots, f(\alpha_n)) : f \in \mathbb{F}_q[x],\ \deg f < k \right\}.$$

It is a linear $[n, k, d]_q$ code with the following elementary but decisive properties:

- **Dimension.** The evaluation map is injective for $\deg f < k \le n$, so $\dim \mathcal{C} = k$ and the rate is $R = k/n$.
- **Distance.** A nonzero polynomial of degree at most $k-1$ vanishes at most $k-1$ times, so every nonzero codeword has weight at least $n-k+1$. Hence $d = n-k+1$, meeting the Singleton bound: RS codes are MDS [1, 10].
- **Alphabet constraint.** The construction requires $q \ge n$; the alphabet must grow at least linearly with the block length.

### 2.2 Unique decoding: the Berlekamp–Welch decoder

Suppose the received word is $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_n)$ with at most $e$ errors. Berlekamp and Welch (1986) observed that one may search simultaneously for an *error-locator* polynomial $E(x)$ of degree at most $e$ and a polynomial $N(x) = f(x)E(x)$ of degree less than $k+e$ satisfying the *key equations*

$$N(\alpha_i) = y_i E(\alpha_i) \qquad \text{for all } i = 1, \dots, n.$$

These are $n$ homogeneous linear equations in $(k+e) + (e+1)$ unknowns. Whenever $n > k + 2e$, i.e., $e \le (n-k)/2 = (d-1)/2$, a nonzero solution exists, and $f = N/E$ recovers the message [2]. The decoder is correct, polynomial-time, and *unique* — but it is provably confined to half the minimum distance, since beyond $d/2$ two codewords may both lie within the decoding ball.

### 2.3 List decoding and the Johnson bound

*List decoding* relaxes the problem: given a radius $\tau$, return **all** codewords within Hamming distance $\tau$ of the received word. The output is useful only if the list is small. The Johnson bound guarantees that for RS codes, any Hamming ball of radius below $n - \sqrt{nk}$ contains only polynomially many codewords [4, 11]. In agreement terms: from agreement $t > \sqrt{nk}$ onward, the list is tractable. The central question of the field for decades was whether this radius could be *achieved efficiently* — and eventually, whether the far larger *capacity* radius $n(1-R)$ could be.

## 3 Methodology

The methodology of this article is analytic and expository. We reconstruct the interpolation-based list-decoding paradigm from first principles:

1. **Counting arguments.** Each decoder is presented as a dimension-versus-constraints argument: choose a space of bivariate polynomials large enough that a nonzero element must vanish (possibly with multiplicity) on all received points.
2. **The factorization lemma.** A univariate polynomial $R(x) = Q(x, f(x))$ of bounded degree with more roots than its degree is identically zero; hence $y - f(x)$ divides $Q(x,y)$, reducing decoding to bivariate factorization.
3. **Parameter optimization.** We track how Sudan's choice ($m = 1$) and the Guruswami–Sudan choice (multiplicity $m \to \infty$) trade interpolation degree against decoding radius.
4. **Structural generalizations.** We examine how correlating message polynomials (Parvaresh–Vardy), bundling symbols (folded RS), and adding derivative information (multiplicity codes) push the radius to capacity.

> **Theorem:** *(Johnson bound for RS codes).* For $\mathrm{RS}_q(n,k)$, any Hamming ball of radius strictly less than $n - \sqrt{nk}$ contains at most $O(n^2)$ codewords [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Sudan's interpolation breakthrough (1997)

Sudan's insight was to decouple decoding into two algebraic steps [3]. Given the received points $(\alpha_i, y_i)$:

**Step 1 — Interpolation.** Find a nonzero bivariate polynomial $Q(x,y) = \sum q_{ij} x^i y^j$ of $(1, k-1)$-*weighted degree* at most $D$ — meaning $q_{ij} \ne 0 \Rightarrow i + (k-1)j \le D$ — such that $Q(\alpha_i, y_i) = 0$ for all $i$. The number of available monomials is approximately $D^2 / (2(k-1))$; choosing $D = \lceil \sqrt{2n(k-1)} \rceil$ makes this exceed the $n$ interpolation constraints, so a nonzero $Q$ exists and is found by Gaussian elimination.

**Step 2 — Factorization.** For any message polynomial $f$ of degree $< k$ agreeing with $\mathbf{y}$ on $t$ positions, the univariate polynomial $R(x) = Q(x, f(x))$ has degree at most $D$ yet vanishes at those $t$ points. If $t > D$, then $R \equiv 0$, so $y - f(x)$ divides $Q(x,y)$ in $\mathbb{F}_q[x,y]$. Factoring $Q$ and collecting factors of the form $y - f(x)$ yields every candidate.

The result: all codewords with agreement $t > \sqrt{2kn}$ are recovered — a list decoder for up to $n - \sqrt{2kn}$ errors, i.e., an error *fraction* of $1 - \sqrt{2R}$. For low rates this already exceeds half the minimum distance, the first efficient decoder to do so [3].

### 4.2 Multiplicities and the Guruswami–Sudan framework (1999)

The Guruswami–Sudan refinement is conceptually small and quantitatively decisive: require $Q$ to vanish **with multiplicity** $m$ at each received point [4]. A zero of multiplicity $m$ imposes $\binom{m+1}{2}$ linear constraints per point (all Hasse derivatives of order below $m$ vanish), for $n\binom{m+1}{2}$ constraints total. The monomial count is unchanged, so the weighted degree must grow to $D \approx m\sqrt{n(k-1)}$.

The payoff comes in the factorization lemma: if $Q$ vanishes with multiplicity $m$ at $(\alpha_i, y_i)$ and $f(\alpha_i) = y_i$, then $x - \alpha_i$ divides $R(x) = Q(x, f(x))$ with multiplicity at least $m$. Hence $R \equiv 0$ as soon as $mt > D$, i.e., agreement

$$t > \frac{D}{m} \approx \sqrt{n(k-1)}$$

suffices. Letting $m$ grow polynomially in $n$ drives the radius to $n - \sqrt{nk}$ errors — an error fraction of $1 - \sqrt{R}$ — attaining the Johnson bound for *every* rate $R$ [4]. The list size remains polynomial, bounded by the $y$-degree of $Q$.

> **Theorem:** *(Guruswami–Sudan [4]).* For every $t > \sqrt{n(k-1)}$ there is a polynomial-time algorithm that, given any received word, lists all $f$ with $\deg f < k$ agreeing with it on at least $t$ positions.

The bound $1 - \sqrt{R}$ strictly improves on unique decoding $(1-R)/2$ at all rates, since $(1-\sqrt{R})^2 > 0$. Sudan's original radius, by contrast, exceeds $(1-R)/2$ only for $R < 3 - 2\sqrt{2} \approx 0.172$ — a fact that makes the multiplicity innovation essential rather than incremental.

### 4.3 Parvaresh–Vardy codes: correlated polynomials

To go *beyond* the Johnson bound, Parvaresh and Vardy (2005) changed the code rather than the decoder [5]. Their construction encodes not just $f$ but also polynomials algebraically *correlated* with it — for instance $f_1 = f^d \bmod E$ for an irreducible $E$ — evaluating all of them at each point. The decoder interpolates a *multivariate* polynomial $Q(x, y_1, \dots, y_s)$ through the received tuples; the algebraic relations $y_{j+1} = y_j^d$ among the variables collapse what would otherwise be an exponentially large solution set down to a polynomial-size list. This was the first polynomial-time decoder beating the Guruswami–Sudan radius — but the extra correlated evaluations cost rate, confining the improvement to low rates (at most $1/16$) and keeping the optimal $1-R$ radius out of reach [5].

### 4.4 Folded Reed–Solomon codes and list-decoding capacity

Guruswami and Rudra (2008) realized that the rate loss of Parvaresh–Vardy could be eliminated by a simple *repackaging*: the order-$s$ **folded** Reed–Solomon code views an ordinary RS codeword over the larger alphabet $\mathbb{F}_q^s$, bundling $s$ consecutive evaluations into one symbol [6]. With a primitive element $\gamma \in \mathbb{F}_q$, the $i$-th folded symbol of $f$ is

$$\left( f(\gamma^{is}),\, f(\gamma^{is+1}),\, \dots,\, f(\gamma^{is+s-1}) \right) \in \mathbb{F}_q^s.$$

Folding is *exactly* the RS code seen through a coarser lens — the rate is unchanged. Yet the decoder can now exploit correlated structure across the bundle: interpolating a multivariate polynomial and using the relation between $f(x)$ and $f(\gamma x)$ yields, for $s$ sufficiently large, list decoding up to a fraction $1 - R - \varepsilon$ of errors [6]. This meets the information-theoretic *list-decoding capacity*: no code of rate $R$ can be list-decoded (with subexponential lists) beyond $1-R$. It was the first explicit capacity-achieving family, resolving a central open problem in algorithmic coding theory.

Two companion ideas in the same work deserve emphasis. First, **list recovery** — given, at each position, a *set* of $\ell$ candidate symbols, recover all codewords consistent with many of them — is the engine behind the alphabet-size reduction from $q^{O(1/\varepsilon)}$ to constant via expander-based concatenation [6]. Second, concatenating folded RS codes with suitable inner codes yields explicit *binary* codes list-decodable up to the Zyablov bound, twice the radius of standard generalized-minimum-distance decoding [6].

### 4.5 Linear-algebraic decoding and multiplicity codes

The original folded-RS decoder required root-finding over extension fields. Guruswami and Wang (2013) showed the entire algorithm can be made *linear-algebraic*: interpolate a polynomial $Q$ with a linear structure, solve one linear system for interpolation and a second small one to isolate the candidate subspace, and prune — no extension fields, quadratic time apart from pruning [7].

A parallel line, **multiplicity codes**, enriches each codeword symbol with the value of $f$ *and its Hasse derivatives* at each point. Introduced by Kopparty, Saraf, and Yekhanin as high-rate locally decodable codes [8], they were later shown to be list-decodable up to capacity as well, giving a second, structurally distinct capacity-achieving family. Together with folded RS codes, they demonstrate that the interpolation paradigm is not tied to one code family but is a general method for extracting algebraic redundancy from noisy evaluations.

### 5.2 Proof sketch of the factorization lemma

The engine of every interpolation decoder is the following elementary fact.

> **Lemma:** Let $Q(x,y) \in \mathbb{F}_q[x,y]$ have $(1,k-1)$-weighted degree at most $D$ and vanish with multiplicity $m$ at the points $(\alpha_i, y_i)$. If $f \in \mathbb{F}_q[x]$ has $\deg f < k$ and $f(\alpha_i) = y_i$ on a set $T$ with $|T| > D/m$, then $y - f(x)$ divides $Q(x,y)$.

*Proof.* Put $R(x) = Q(x, f(x))$. Substituting $y = f(x)$ replaces each monomial $x^i y^j$ by $x^{i + j\deg f}$, so $\deg R \le D$. At each $i \in T$, the multiplicity-$m$ vanishing of $Q$ at $(\alpha_i, f(\alpha_i))$ forces $(x-\alpha_i)^m \mid R(x)$. The $\alpha_i$ are distinct, so $\prod_{i \in T}(x-\alpha_i)^m \mid R(x)$, a divisor of degree $m|T| > D \ge \deg R$; hence $R \equiv 0$. Viewing $Q$ as a polynomial in $y$ over $\mathbb{F}_q[x]$, $f(x)$ is a root, so $y - f(x) \mid Q(x,y)$. ∎

### 5.3 A concrete interpolation sketch

The following Python sketch implements Sudan's interpolation step (multiplicity $m=1$) over a prime field, exhibiting the dimension-counting argument in code:

```python
import math

def sudan_monomials(n, k):
    """All (i, j) with (1, k-1)-weighted degree <= D, where #monomials > n."""
    D = math.isqrt(2 * n * (k - 1))          # floor(sqrt(2n(k-1)))
    mons = [(i, j) for j in range(D // (k - 1) + 1)
            for i in range(D - (k - 1) * j + 1)]
    assert len(mons) > n, "need more monomials than points"
    return mons, D

def interpolation_matrix(xs, ys, k, p):
    """Constraint matrix M with M[t, (i,j)] = x_t^i * y_t^j (mod p)."""
    mons, D = sudan_monomials(len(xs), k)
    M = [[pow(x, i, p) * pow(y, j, p) % p for (i, j) in mons]
         for x, y in zip(xs, ys)]
    return M, mons, D
    # A nonzero vector in ker(M) (Gaussian elimination over GF(p))
    # gives the coefficients of Q; ker is nontrivial since
    # #columns = #monomials > n = #rows.
```

Factoring the resulting $Q$ over $\mathbb{F}_p[x,y]$ and collecting factors $y - f(x)$ with $\deg f < k$ completes the decoder [3, 9].

## 6 Limitations

The theory is not without sharp edges:

- **Alphabet size.** Plain RS codes need $q \ge n$; folded RS codes need $q^s$ with $s$ growing as the gap $\varepsilon \to 0$, i.e., alphabet size $n^{O(1/\varepsilon^2)}$ for radius $1-R-\varepsilon$. Constant-alphabet capacity is recovered only through concatenation machinery [6].
- **List size.** Folded-RS decoders naturally produce lists of size $n^{O(1/\varepsilon)}$; reducing this to a constant independent of $n$ requires pre-encoding with subspace-evasive sets [6, 7]. Moreover, *plain* RS codes provably cannot be list-decoded with polynomial lists much beyond the Johnson radius — the bound is essentially tight for the code family itself.
- **Randomness.** Until recently, the factorization step of Sudan's and the Guruswami–Sudan decoder was randomized, since deterministic bivariate factorization over finite fields in $\mathrm{poly}(d, \log q)$ time remains open even for quadratics. Chatterjee, Harsha, and Kumar (2025) derandomized the specific factorization instances arising in list decoding, yielding the first deterministic $\mathrm{poly}(n, \log q)$-time decoders for all finite fields [9].
- **Complexity constants.** The polynomial running times hide large exponents in $1/\varepsilon$; recent work on *fast* list decoding (e.g., Goyal–Harsha–Kumar–Shankar, FOCS 2024) aims at near-linear time, but practical implementations still lag the theory [11].
- **Unique vs. list.** List decoding returns a *set*; disambiguating the true message requires side information (e.g., CRCs), which real systems such as QR codes and distributed storage provide out-of-band [10].

## 7 Conclusion

The trajectory from Reed and Solomon's 1960 polynomial codes [1] to capacity-achieving list decoding [6] is one of the great arcs of coding theory. Sudan's recasting of decoding as interpolation plus factorization [3] broke the half-the-minimum-distance barrier that had stood for nearly forty years; the Guruswami–Sudan introduction of multiplicities [4] carried the radius to the Johnson bound $n - \sqrt{nk}$ for all rates; Parvaresh–Vardy codes [5] showed the bound could be beaten by restructuring the code; and folded Reed–Solomon codes [6] reached the information-theoretic limit $1-R$, with multiplicity codes [8] and linear-algebraic refinements [7] broadening the method into a general paradigm. List recovery emerged as the unifying generalization, powering alphabet reduction and connecting list decoding to pseudorandomness and complexity theory [6]. With derandomization now achieved [9] and fast decoders under active development [11], the interpolation paradigm that began as a clever trick has become the foundation of modern algebraic list decoding — and the reason Reed–Solomon codes, sixty-five years on, remain at the center of both theory and practice, from QR codes and archival storage to probabilistically checkable proofs [3, 10].
