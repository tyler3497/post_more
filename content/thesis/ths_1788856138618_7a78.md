# Stackelberg Security Games: Algorithmic Game Theory for Defender–Attacker Resource Allocation

## Abstract

We present a unified treatment of *Stackelberg security games* (SSGs), the algorithmic game-theoretic framework that has carried game theory into airport terminals, harbors, metro systems, and national parks. In a security game, the defender (the Stackelberg leader) commits to a randomized allocation of scarce resources over targets, and the attacker (the follower) observes that strategy — through surveillance or pattern detection — before selecting a target. We develop the model from first principles, define the *Strong Stackelberg Equilibrium* (SSE), and analyze the principal exact solution methods: the multiple-linear-program method, the compact mixed-integer linear programming formulation **DOBSS** [1], and the column-generation schemes **ORIGAMI** and **ERASER** [3]. We extend the analysis to *Bayesian* Stackelberg games with attacker-type uncertainty, minimax-regret strategies under payoff intervals (**ARROW**), boundedly rational adversaries (**SUQR**), and learning dynamics in repeated *green* security games. Finally, we survey the deployed record — **ARMOR**, **IRIS**, **PROTECT**, **TRUSTS**, and **PAWS** [4][5][7] — and close with the fundamental limitations of the paradigm.

---

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The defender's dilemma

Security, in the physical and cyber worlds alike, is a *resource-allocation problem under adversarial response*: fewer officers than city blocks, fewer canine teams than terminals, fewer rangers than poaching hotspots. The defender cannot protect everything at once, so it must *randomize*: assign coverage probabilities to targets so as to remain unpredictable to an adversary who is watching.

Classical simultaneous-move game theory, centered on Nash equilibrium, mischaracterizes this interaction: the defender publishes (through observable behavior) its strategy *first*, and the attacker moves *second* with knowledge of the defender's mixed strategy. This is the leadership structure of a **Stackelberg game** [6][8], and the resulting framework — *Stackelberg security games* — has become one of the most successful applications of algorithmic game theory ever fielded [7][5].

### 1.2 Historical arc

The intellectual lineage runs from von Stackelberg's 1934 model of market leadership, through Conitzer and Sandholm's (2006) result on computing optimal commitment strategies [8], to the watershed sequence of papers from the Teamcore research group: the DOBSS solver (Paruchuri et al., 2008) [1], the ORIGAMI algorithm (Kiekintveld et al., 2009), the ERASER column-generation scheme [3], and the ARMOR deployment at LAX in August 2007 [2][7]. Milind Tambe's monograph *Security and Game Theory* (2011) consolidated the field and documented the lessons of the first generation of deployments [9]. Since then, the framework has been extended to *green security games* for wildlife and fisheries protection [4][6], robust and behavioral variants under payoff uncertainty [6], and large-scale formulations with tight LP relaxations [3].

### 1.3 Scope and contributions of this thesis

This thesis develops the theory of Stackelberg security games at a level sufficient to implement, analyze, and extend the state of the art:

1. A formal model of security games and the **Strong Stackelberg Equilibrium** as the solution concept, with existence and tie-breaking arguments.
2. The exact solution machinery: the multiple-LP method, the **DOBSS** compact MILP [1], and the **ORIGAMI**/**ERASER**/**Mipp-S** formulations [3].
3. Attacker uncertainty through *Bayesian* Stackelberg games, interval-payoff minimax regret (**ARROW**), and bounded-rationality models (**SUQR**) [6].
4. Learning dynamics in *repeated* security games — green security games, **SHARP**, and adaptive patrol strategies [4][6].
5. A survey of the deployed systems and the measured algorithmic gains, with explicit discussion of where the theory breaks down in practice.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Formal model of a security game

A *(non-Bayesian) security game* is defined by the following primitives:

- A set of **targets** $T = \{1, \ldots, n\}$, each a potential attack site.
- A set of **defender resources** $R$, with $|R| = m < n$; each resource can be assigned to cover at most one target at a time (more generally, to a *schedule* — a feasible subset of targets).
- A **coverage vector** $\mathbf{c} = (c_1, \ldots, c_n) \in [0,1]^n$, where $c_t$ is the probability that target $t$ is covered. Not every vector in the cube is feasible; the feasible set $\mathcal{C}$ is the image of a distribution over schedules, i.e., $\mathcal{C}$ is a polytope.
- **Payoff structure.** For each target $t$: defender reward/penalty $R^d_t, P^d_t$ and attacker reward/penalty $R^a_t, P^a_t$. Conditional on $t$ being attacked with coverage $c_t$:
$$U^d_t(c_t) = c_t R^d_t + (1 - c_t) P^d_t, \qquad U^a_t(c_t) = c_t P^a_t + (1 - c_t) R^a_t.$$

The game proceeds in two stages: the defender commits to $\mathbf{c} \in \mathcal{C}$ (realized via a mixed strategy over schedules); the attacker observes $\mathbf{c}$ and attacks a target maximizing $U^a_t(c_t)$.

> **Definition (Strong Stackelberg Equilibrium).** A *Stackelberg equilibrium* is a pair $(\mathbf{c}^*, t^*)$ with $t^* \in \arg\max_t U^a_t(c_t^*)$ and $\mathbf{c}^*$ maximizing the defender's utility under the attacker's choice rule. It is *strong* (SSE) if the attacker breaks ties in the defender's favor.

The SSE is the standard solution concept in security games; its key analytical convenience is that the leader's problem reduces to optimization over its own strategy space with an embedded best-response constraint [1][8].

### 2.2 Computational complexity

Computing an SSE is **NP-hard** in general security games: the attacker's choice of target partitions the defender's feasible polytope into regions defined by which target is the attacker's best response, and the defender's utility is piecewise-linear but *non-convex* across that partition [8]. Yet — and this is the pivot on which the entire field turns — the problem admits compact and practically fast exact algorithms, because the follower's action set (targets) is small and the best-response constraint is linear conditional on the attacked target.

### 2.3 The multiple-LP method

Conitzer and Sandholm (2006) showed that an SSE can be computed by solving *one linear program per candidate attacked target* $t$ [8]: maximize the defender's utility $U^d_t(c_t)$ subject to $\mathbf{c} \in \mathcal{C}$ and the *incentive constraints*
$$U^a_t(c_t) \geq U^a_{t'}(c_{t'}) \quad \forall\, t' \in T,$$
which force $t$ to be a best response. Solving $n$ LPs and retaining the best yields the SSE, with ties broken in the defender's favor by construction. The multiple-LP method is exact and simple, but scales poorly: each LP carries the full schedule polytope, and $L$ attacker *types* blow up the Harsanyi transformation — a limitation that motivated DOBSS [1].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Bayesian Stackelberg games

In a *Bayesian Stackelberg game* the defender faces a finite set of attacker types $\Lambda = \{1, \ldots, L\}$ with prior probabilities $p^\lambda$, payoff parameters $(R^{a,\lambda}_t, P^{a,\lambda}_t)$, and type-specific best responses $t^\lambda(\mathbf{c})$ [1]. The defender maximizes expected utility
$$\sum_{\lambda \in \Lambda} p^\lambda \, U^d_{t^\lambda(\mathbf{c})}\!\left(c_{t^\lambda(\mathbf{c})}\right),$$
with each type's incentive constraints enforced separately.

### 3.2 The DOBSS compact MILP

DOBSS (Decomposed Optimal Bayesian Stackelberg Solver) [1] starts from a mixed-integer *quadratic* program (MIQP): with defender mixed strategy $\mathbf{x}$ over pure strategies $X$ and binary indicators $q^\lambda_j$ for type $\lambda$'s choice of action $j$ among $Q$,
$$\max_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{q}} \sum_{\lambda,i,j} p^\lambda R^\lambda_{ij}\, x_i q^\lambda_j$$
subject to probability-simplex and best-response big-$M$ constraints. The quadratic objective is linearized by the change of variables
$$z^\lambda_{ij} = x_i \, q^\lambda_j,$$
yielding a compact MILP with $XQL$ continuous variables, $QL$ binary variables, and $4QL + 2XL + 2L$ constraints — an $O(Q^L)$ reduction in total work relative to the multiple-LP method [1]. Branch-and-bound explores at most $O(Q^L)$ nodes, but in practice prunes aggressively.

> **Theorem (DOBSS complexity [1]).** *DOBSS's worst-case work is $O(Q^L \cdot XQL)$, versus $O(Q^L \cdot (X + Q^L))$ for the multiple-LP method — a reduction of order $Q^L$. The MIQP and MILP are equivalent under $z^\lambda_{ij} = x_i q^\lambda_j$.*

### 3.3 Column generation: ERASER, ORIGAMI, Mipp-S

Where DOBSS is a compact formulation over the full schedule space, **ERASER** [3] exploits the observation that optimal SSEs can be found in *marginal coverage space*: solve a master LP over coverage variables, then *generate* the realizing schedules via a slave column-generation problem. **ORIGAMI** handles identical fully protective resources by greedy level-covering in $O(n^2)$ time (later linear) [3]. The recent **Mipp-S** formulation tightens the LP relaxation substantially: its average LP gap is 36–52% lower than ERASER's and 30–49% lower than SDOBSS's across instance classes [3].

### 3.4 Behavioral and robust extensions

Real adversaries are not perfectly rational. The **SUQR** (Subjective Utility Quantal Response) model [6] replaces strict best response with a softmax over attacker utilities augmented by target features:
$$\hat{U}^a_t(\mathbf{x}) = w_1 x_t + w_2 R^a_t + w_3 P^a_t + w_4 \Phi_t,$$
with weights learned from data and $\Phi_t$ a target feature such as distance.

### 3.5 Repeated games and green security games

In wildlife and fisheries protection, defender–attacker interaction is *repeated*: poachers place snares season after season, and rangers adapt patrols as data accrues. **Green security games** (GSGs) [4][6] model this as a repeated Stackelberg game with learning: poacher behavior models are re-estimated every few months from recovered snare data, and new randomized patrols generated. **SHARP** adds *spatio-temporal* reasoning, capturing how poaching pressure shifts across the landscape in response to past patrols [4].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Strong Stackelberg Equilibrium: structure and tie-breaking

The *strength* of the SSE — the follower breaking ties for the leader — is not an arbitrary convenience. When the attacker is indifferent between targets $t_1$ and $t_2$, the defender can perturb coverage by an arbitrarily small $\varepsilon$ to make $t_1$ uniquely optimal, and continuity of $U^d_t$ in $\mathbf{c}$ implies the perturbed solution is arbitrarily close to the tie-breaking one. Hence the SSE is the *closure* of achievable outcomes under infinitesimal perturbations.

> **Theorem (SSE as limit of perturbed games).** *For any security game with continuous expected utilities, the SSE value is approached by strategies inducing unique attacker best responses. Consequently, tie-breaking in the defender's favor is without loss of generality.*

Practically, the multiple-LP construction realizes this automatically: solving the LP for target $t$ and comparing values across $t$ implements the favorable tie-break at no extra cost [8]. This is a rare and fortunate alignment — in general Stackelberg games, optimal tie-breaking can be computationally expensive.

### 4.2 DOBSS: from MIQP to compact MILP

The DOBSS derivation is the template for much subsequent work [1]. The full MIQP carries the objective $\sum_{\lambda,i,j} p^\lambda R^\lambda_{ij} x_i q^\lambda_j$ with simplex constraints $\sum_i x_i = 1$, $\sum_j q^\lambda_j = 1$, and big-$M$ optimality constraints

$$0 \le a^\lambda - \sum_i C^\lambda_{ij}\Big(\sum_h z^\lambda_{ih}\Big) \le (1 - q^\lambda_j) M \quad \forall \lambda, j,$$

where $a^\lambda$ is type $\lambda$'s maximum attacker utility.
$$\sum_{j \in Q} z^\lambda_{ij} = \sum_{j \in Q} z^1_{ij}, \qquad z^\lambda_{ij} \le 1, \qquad q^\lambda_j \le \sum_{i \in X} z^\lambda_{ij} \le 1,$$
which enforce consistency of the defender's marginal strategy across types. The resulting MILP has $XQL$ continuous variables and only $QL$ binary variables — exponentially fewer binaries than a naïve discretization — and empirically DOBSS is *orders of magnitude* faster than the multiple-LP approach on randomly generated games and LAX-scale instances [1][2].

```python
# Multiple-LP method for SSE (Conitzer & Sandholm 2006): for each attacked
# target t, maximize U_d[t](c) s.t. coverage feasible and t is best response.
import numpy as np

def sse_multiple_lp(Rd, Pd, Ra, Pa, C_vertices):
    n = len(Rd)
    best_val, best = -np.inf, None
    for t in range(n):
        for v in C_vertices:          # vertices of the schedule polytope
            c = np.array(v)
            Ua = c * Pa + (1 - c) * Ra
            if Ua[t] >= Ua.max() - 1e-9:      # t is a best response
                val = Pd[t] + c[t] * (Rd[t] - Pd[t])
                if val > best_val:
                    best_val, best = val, (t, c)
    return best_val, best
```

The snippet above implements the multiple-LP logic by vertex enumeration for pedagogy; production solvers use the DOBSS MILP or ERASER column generation rather than enumerating the (exponential) schedule polytope [1][3].

### 4.3 ERASER, ORIGAMI, and the perfect-formulation race

ERASER's core insight is a *separation of concerns*: the attacker only ever observes marginal coverage $\mathbf{c}$, never the schedule distribution. The master problem optimizes over $\mathbf{c}$ with the best-response constraint for the assumed attacked target, and the slave problem asks whether a schedule distribution realizing $\mathbf{c}$ exists — classical Dantzig–Wolfe column generation converging to an exact SSE [3].

ORIGAMI attacks the problem from the opposite end: a *combinatorial algorithm* for the structured case of identical, fully protective resources. It sorts targets by attacker reward and "levels" the attacker's expected utility across a prefix of the target list — a water-filling procedure in $O(n^2)$ time [3], with a later linear-time refinement. The deep lesson is that *identical resources induce a threshold structure* in optimal solutions, a property that general MILP solvers rediscover only through expensive branching.

The 2023–2024 perfect-formulation work [3] sharpened this picture by comparing the *LP relaxation gaps* of ERASER, SDOBSS, and Mipp-S. Mipp-S's relaxation is provably tighter for single-target protection, and empirically the gaps are dramatic: 36.08% lower than ERASER and 30.5% lower than SDOBSS without variability; 52.01% and 49.4% with variability [3]. Tighter relaxations mean stronger dual bounds, less branching, and — for the first time — the realistic prospect of solving continent-scale infrastructure protection games to optimality.

```haskell
-- ORIGAMI's level-covering idea: water-fill coverage so the attacker's
-- expected utility is equal across the attacked prefix of targets.
levelCover :: [Double] -> Double -> [Double]
levelCover rewards budget = go (sort rewards) budget []
  where go [] _ acc = acc
        go (r:rest) b acc =
          let k = fromIntegral (length rest + 1)
          in if b >= k * r then go rest (b - k*r) (r:acc)
             else acc ++ replicate (length rest + 1) (b / k)
```

### 4.4 Bayesian games, payoff uncertainty, and minimax regret

The Bayesian extension is what makes the theory *deployable*: at LAX, ARMOR's defender faces a distribution over attacker *types* (capabilities, preferences, risk attitudes), and the Bayesian SSE optimizes against the mixture [1][2][7], handled natively by DOBSS through the type-indexed variables $z^\lambda_{ij}$.

Even the type-conditioned payoffs are uncertain. **ARROW** [6] takes interval uncertainty as primitive: $R^a_t \in [R^a_{\min}(t), R^a_{\max}(t)]$, $P^a_t \in [P^a_{\min}(t), P^a_{\max}(t)]$. The defender's *behavioral regret* — the loss from playing $\mathbf{x}$ instead of the ex-post optimal $\mathbf{x}'$ — is minimized over the worst case in the uncertainty set, using the SUQR behavioral model in place of the rational best response. The resulting minimax-behavioral-regret formulation degrades gracefully when elicited payoffs are wrong, which field experience shows they invariably are [6].

| Uncertainty model | Primitive | Solution concept | Representative algorithm |
|---|---|---|---|
| Known types, exact payoffs | Prior $p^\lambda$ over types | Bayesian SSE | DOBSS [1] |
| Interval payoffs | $[R^a_{\min}, R^a_{\max}]$, $[P^a_{\min}, P^a_{\max}]$ | Minimax behavioral regret | ARROW [6] |
| Bounded rationality | SUQR weights $(w_1,\dots,w_4)$ | Quantal-response equilibrium | SUQR learning [6] |

### 4.5 Learning in repeated games and green security

The green security game (GSG) [4][6] is the repeated-game extension that field deployments demanded: poachers probe, rangers patrol, snares are found or missed, and the cycle repeats. PAWS operationalizes the loop [4]:

1. **Discretize** the area into grid cells with key access points (KAPs) and route segments as shortest paths between KAPs.
2. **Learn** a poacher behavior model from recovered-snare data, using the SUQR form with spatial features (distance to boundary, animal density, slope) [4][6].
3. **Solve** the security game with a cutting-plane approach (master: ARROW for payoff uncertainty; slave: column generation of new patrol routes) [4].
4. **Deploy**, collect data over a season, and **re-learn** [4].

The repeated interaction also admits *regret-based* learning guarantees: a no-regret online algorithm against the empirical sequence of attacker actions converges in average utility to the game value against any stationary attacker mixture — a robustness property static SSE computation cannot provide [6]. Uganda field tests (spring 2014 onward) confirm PAWS-generated patrols outperform rangers' manual plans and naïve uniform randomization [4][5].

## 5. Empirical Evidence and Proofs

### 5.1 Algorithmic speedups

DOBSS solved Bayesian Stackelberg games *orders of magnitude* faster than the multiple-LP method by replacing $Q^L$ LPs over the Harsanyi-transformed game with a single compact MILP whose branch-and-bound explores only a fraction of the worst-case tree [1]. ERASER beat SDOBSS on schedule-based instances [3], and Mipp-S cut LP relaxation gaps by 36–52% relative to both, directly reducing branch-and-bound nodes [3].

### 5.2 Proof sketch: DOBSS MILP–MIQP equivalence

> **Proposition (Paruchuri et al. [1]).** *Problems (5) (MIQP) and (7) (MILP) are equivalent under $z^\lambda_{ij} = x_i q^\lambda_j$.*

*Proof sketch.* ($\Rightarrow$) Let $(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{q}^\lambda, \mathbf{a})$ be feasible for (5) and define $z^\lambda_{ij} = x_i q^\lambda_j$. Then $\sum_j z^\lambda_{ij} = x_i$ is independent of $\lambda$, satisfying the cross-type consistency constraints; the big-$M$ constraints transfer since $z^\lambda_{ih}$ sums to $x_i$, and objectives coincide. ($\Leftarrow$) Given feasible $(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{q}, \mathbf{a})$ for (7), set $x_i = \sum_j z^\lambda_{ij}$; the constraints $q^\lambda_j \le \sum_i z^\lambda_{ij} \le 1$ and $\sum_j z^\lambda_{ij} = x_i$ force $z^\lambda_{ij} = x_i q^\lambda_j$ on the support of $q^\lambda_j$, so feasibility and objective transfer back. ∎

## 6. Limitations

The theory's successes should not obscure its boundaries.

1. **Computational hardness persists.** SSE computation is NP-hard, and while DOBSS/ERASER/Mipp-S handle hundreds of targets and types, truly massive domains (continental rail networks, city-scale camera networks) remain out of reach of exact methods [8][3].
2. **The rational-adversary idealization.** The SSE assumes the attacker perfectly observes $\mathbf{c}$ and best-responds. Real adversaries observe *noisily*, respond *boundedly rationally*, and may not know the game at all; over-fitting to a rational model can produce brittle strategies [6].
3. **Payoff elicitation is the bottleneck.** Every payoff number must come from expert judgment or historical data, and these numbers are unreliable — the ARROW/interval machinery exists precisely for this reason [6].
4. **Schedule feasibility vs. marginal coverage.** The marginal-coverage abstraction assumes any $\mathbf{c} \in \mathcal{C}$ is realizable; with routing constraints, time windows, and shift handovers, the *realizable* set is smaller and harder to characterize [4].
5. **Fairness concerns.** SSE maximizes the defender's expected utility, which can concentrate coverage on high-value targets and starve low-value ones — unacceptable when targets are neighborhoods. Fairness-constrained SSGs are an active area [3].
6. **Repeated-game modeling gaps.** Green security games assume the poacher population is stationary between re-learning windows; in reality, adversaries adapt *within* a window, and the defender's learning induces a non-stationary environment — a coupled learning problem with few guarantees [4][6].

## 7. Conclusion

Stackelberg security games transformed security resource allocation from an art of expert intuition into a science of commitment, observation, and optimization. The theoretical core — the Strong Stackelberg Equilibrium, the DOBSS compact MILP, the ERASER/ORIGAMI column-generation schemes, and their Bayesian, robust, and behavioral extensions — is now mature enough to be taught as a closed chapter of algorithmic game theory [1][3][6][8]. The deployment record — ARMOR, IRIS, PROTECT, TRUSTS, GUARDS, PAWS — is unmatched by any other application of non-cooperative game theory [2][4][5][7]. The frontier has moved to where the classical assumptions break: massive-scale approximation, fairness-constrained equilibria, coupled learning in repeated games, and the security of interacting AI agents [8].
