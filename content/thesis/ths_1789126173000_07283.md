# The Geometry of Self-Supervised Learning: Contrastive Objectives on the Hypersphere, the Alignment–Uniformity Trade-off, and the Dimensional Collapse Phenomenon

## Abstract

We develop a geometric theory of contrastive self-supervised learning on the unit hypersphere $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$. Using the alignment–uniformity decomposition of the InfoNCE loss [1], we show that asymptotic contrastive training is a variational problem balancing attraction of positive pairs against a repulsive Gaussian potential that spreads features over the sphere — a formulation connected to classical minimum-energy configurations and Thomson-type problems. We then analyze the breakdown of this picture: *dimensional collapse* [2], where embeddings concentrate in a lower-dimensional subspace despite healthy loss values, and we trace it to the interaction between InfoNCE dynamics and the trainable projector. We connect this geometry to the neural-collapse simplex-ETF structure of supervised training [6], analyze temperature as a geometric knob setting the range of repulsion, and present covariance-spectrum diagnostics for detecting collapsed representations. The exposition is self-contained, mathematically precise, and oriented toward practitioners diagnosing degenerate representation geometry in production contrastive pipelines.

## 1. Introduction

Self-supervised learning by contrastive objectives has become central to representation learning. Contrastive predictive coding introduced the InfoNCE loss as a tractable surrogate for mutual-information maximization [5]; Momentum Contrast decoupled dictionary size from batch size through a queue and a momentum encoder [4]; and SimCLR showed that strong augmentations, a nonlinear projection head, and large batches could match supervised ResNet-50 performance under linear probing [3]. Yet these systems were engineered before their geometry was understood: the loss was inherited, $\ell_2$-normalization was a convenience, and the projection head was an empirical trick whose rationale emerged only years later.

This thesis argues that the correct mathematical home of contrastive learning is *geometry on the unit hypersphere*. Once embeddings are constrained to $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$, the InfoNCE loss admits an asymptotic decomposition into two interpretable functionals — **alignment** and **uniformity** — and training becomes a competition between a force pulling augmented views together and a repulsive force distributing features evenly across the sphere [1]. This framing explains the projection head's role, temperature sensitivity, and the phenomenon of *dimensional collapse* — embeddings spanning only a proper subspace of the available dimensions — even when the loss converges and positives align correctly [2].

The connections run deeper. The uniformity functional is a Gaussian potential on the sphere, the classical object of minimum-energy configuration problems; the terminal geometry of contrastive embeddings mirrors, in instructive contrast, the *neural collapse* simplex-ETF structure of supervised training [6]; and the projector's role is a dimensional buffer absorbing collapse before it reaches the representation used downstream [2].

Section 2 fixes notation and reviews InfoNCE. Section 3 develops the alignment–uniformity methodology. Section 4 is the theoretical core: the asymptotic decomposition, the hyperspherical-energy perspective, the mechanism of dimensional collapse, and the geometry of temperature. Section 5 discusses empirical evidence and spectral diagnostics. Section 6 catalogs limitations; Section 7 concludes with practical prescriptions.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The contrastive paradigm

Contrastive learning trains an encoder $f_\theta$ to distinguish positive pairs $(x, x^+)$ — two augmented views of the same instance — from negatives $(x, x^-)$ drawn from the data distribution. CPC framed this as mutual-information estimation via a probabilistic contrastive loss [5]; instance discrimination [4, 3] specialized it so each image defines its own class. In all variants the workhorse is the **InfoNCE** loss:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}} = \mathbb{E}\left[ -\log \frac{\exp\big(f(x)^\top f(x^+)/\tau\big)}{\exp\big(f(x)^\top f(x^+)/\tau\big) + \sum_{i=1}^K \exp\big(f(x)^\top f(x_i^-)/\tau\big)} \right],
$$

where $\tau > 0$ is the temperature and all embeddings are $\ell_2$-normalized so inner products are cosine similarities on $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$.

### 2.2 Notation and standing assumptions

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $z, z_i \in \mathbb{S}^{d-1}$ | Normalized embedding vectors (dimension $d$) |
| $p_{\mathrm{data}}$ | Embedding distribution induced by the encoder |
| $p_{\mathrm{pos}}$ | Joint distribution of positive embedding pairs |
| $\tau$ | Temperature (sharpness) parameter of InfoNCE |
| $K$ | Number of negatives per query |
| $\Sigma = \mathrm{Cov}[z]$ | Embedding covariance matrix |
| $\sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_d$ | Singular values of the embedding matrix |

We assume embeddings satisfy $\|z\|_2 = 1$, the encoder has capacity to approximate continuous maps into $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$, and augmentations $t \sim \mathcal{T}$ generate $p_{\mathrm{pos}}$ as the pushforward of the data distribution under pairs of independent augmentations.

### 2.3 Reference implementation of InfoNCE

```python
import torch

def info_nce(z: torch.Tensor, tau: float = 0.07) -> torch.Tensor:
    """z: (2N, d) row-normalized embeddings; positive pairs are (2i, 2i+1)."""
    N2 = z.shape[0]
    sim = (z @ z.T) / tau
    sim.fill_diagonal_(-1e9)                       # exclude self-similarity
    pos = torch.arange(N2).view(-1, 2)
    pos_sim = sim[pos[:, 0], pos[:, 1]]             # positive logits
    log_prob = pos_sim - torch.logsumexp(sim, dim=1)[pos[:, 0]]
    return -log_prob.mean()
```

The loss is a softmax cross-entropy over pairwise similarities; every other embedding — including the other view of a different instance — is a repulsive target.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The alignment and uniformity metrics

Wang and Isola [1] identified two measurable properties the contrastive loss asymptotically optimizes. **Alignment** quantifies closeness of positive pairs:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}} = \mathbb{E}_{(z, z^+)\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}}}\,\|z - z^+\|_2^\alpha, \qquad \alpha > 0,
$$

typically with $\alpha = 2$. **Uniformity** quantifies how evenly the distribution spreads over the hypersphere through a Gaussian potential:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{unif}} = \log\, \mathbb{E}_{z, z' \stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} p_{\mathrm{data}}}\!\left[ e^{-t\|z - z'\|_2^2} \right], \qquad t > 0.
$$

Lower is better for both. The alignment term pulls positive pairs together; the uniformity term pushes *all* pairs apart, since the Gaussian kernel is large exactly when embeddings coincide.

> **Theorem:** (Asymptotic decomposition of InfoNCE.) As the number of negatives $K \to \infty$, the InfoNCE loss converges to a functional minimized by jointly minimizing alignment of positive pairs and uniformity of the embedding distribution on $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$:
>
> $$\lim_{K\to\infty} \Big(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}} - \log K\Big) = -\frac{1}{\tau}\,\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{pos}}}[z^\top z^+] + \mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}\!\left[\log \mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}[e^{z^\top z'/\tau}]\right],$$
>
> whose first term is (up to the sphere constraint) equivalent to alignment and whose second term is the negative-log uniformity functional [1].

The denominator of InfoNCE is a Monte Carlo estimate of a partition function and the numerator a positive-pair similarity; the $K \to \infty$ limit replaces the empirical average with its expectation [1].

### 3.2 A functional view of the uniformity potential

The uniformity functional is a pairwise energy. In Haskell its purely functional character is foregrounded:

```haskell
-- | Gaussian (RBF) pairwise potential of an embedding batch on S^{d-1}.
uniformity :: Double -> [[Double]] -> Double
uniformity t zs =
  let n     = fromIntegral (length zs)
      dist2 a b = sum (zipWith (\x y -> (x - y) ^ 2) a b)
      energy    = sum [ exp (-t * dist2 a b) | a <- zs, b <- zs ] / (n * n)
  in log energy
```

The $O(N^2)$ double sum is precisely why finite-sample InfoNCE uses sampled negatives as a stochastic approximation to this all-pairs energy; temperature $\tau$ plays the role of $1/(2t)$, setting the *range* of repulsion.

### 3.3 Diagnosing geometry via the covariance spectrum

Let $Z \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ hold $N$ centered embeddings with singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_d$. Two summaries characterize the pathologies we discuss:

1. **Effective rank** $r_{\mathrm{eff}} = (\sum_i \sigma_i)^2 / \sum_i \sigma_i^2$, a soft count of occupied dimensions;
2. **Spectrum decay**: the rate at which $\log \sigma_i$ falls with $i$ — a steep drop means embeddings live near a low-dimensional subspace.

Healthy contrastive embeddings show a gently decaying spectrum; *dimensionally collapsed* ones show a sharp elbow, with a few large singular values and a long near-zero tail [2].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Asymptotic InfoNCE as a variational problem on the sphere

The decomposition recasts contrastive learning as minimizing

$$
\mathcal{J}[p] = \lambda \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{pos}}}\|z - z^+\|_2^2}_{\text{alignment}} + \underbrace{\log \mathbb{E}_{p \otimes p}\big[e^{-t\|z-z'\|_2^2}\big]}_{\text{uniformity}},
$$

over distributions $p$ on $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$ realizable by the encoder. The first term is minimized when $z = z^+$ almost surely (perfect augmentation invariance); the second is the logarithm of a pairwise energy whose minimizers are uniformly spread configurations — in the limit, the surface measure on the sphere. The tension is the whole story: invariance wants every augmentation of an instance to coincide, while uniformity wants the cloud to cover the sphere evenly. The compromise is *locally tight but globally dispersed*: augmentation neighborhoods collapse while the global distribution spreads [1].

A subtlety worth noting: the uniformity term couples every pair, so its gradient at $z$ is a weighted sum of repulsive contributions from the whole batch. Points in over-dense regions feel strong repulsion; isolated points feel almost none. This is how the loss "fills in" sparse regions of the sphere.

### 4.2 Uniformity, minimum energy, and the Thomson problem

The Gaussian potential $\sum_{i<j} e^{-t\|z_i - z_j\|^2}$ is a classical object: up to the logarithmic envelope it is a Riesz-type energy, and minimizing pairwise energies on $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$ is the mathematical core of the **Thomson problem** — minimum-energy configurations of repelling particles on a sphere. For large $N$ the minimizers converge weakly to the uniform surface measure; for small $N$ they are highly structured (regular simplices, Platonic solids, higher-dimensional analogues).

Two insights follow. First, *finite-batch contrastive learning is an approximate Thomson solver*: the negatives of a batch are $N-1$ particles repelling on the sphere, and learned configurations inherit energy-minimizer biases, including symmetric maximally-separated arrangements. Second, the neural-collapse phenomenon of supervised learning [6] — class means converging to a simplex equiangular tight frame (ETF) — is the *same* energy principle through a different loss: an ETF is precisely a minimum-energy configuration of $K$ points on the sphere. The supervised ETF and the contrastive uniform distribution are two regimes of one principle — maximal separation under repulsion — differing only in the discrete (class-conditional) versus continuous (instance-wise) structure of the data.

### 4.3 Dimensional collapse: mechanism and the role of the projector

Dimensional collapse is the subtler sibling of full collapse. In full collapse all embeddings converge to a constant vector; in dimensional collapse they span only a $k$-dimensional subspace with $k \ll d$ [2]. Jing et al. showed this afflicts *contrastive* methods too — previously associated mainly with non-contrastive joint-embedding methods — and traced it to the interaction of InfoNCE dynamics with the **trainable projector** $g_\phi$ between the backbone representation $h = f_\theta(x)$ and the contrastive embedding $z = g_\phi(h)$ [2].

The mechanism proceeds in four steps:

1. The alignment term pulls positive pairs together *in the projected space* $z$, where the loss is computed.
2. Gradient flow through the projector achieves this by exploiting only a subset of the projector's output dimensions — those along which encoder features already vary strongly with augmentation.
3. Dimensions of $h$ carrying semantic signal but little augmentation-induced variance receive weak gradients and are discarded in $z$, so $z$'s covariance becomes low-rank.
4. Because the projector is nonlinear and trainable, it implements this rank reduction while the backbone $h$ stays high-dimensional — the projector is a *sacrificial buffer* absorbing the collapse.

This is the deep rationale for Chen et al.'s observation [3] that the pre-projector representation transfers better than the post-projector embedding: the projector takes the geometric damage. Jing et al.'s **DirectCLR** optimizes the contrastive loss directly on slices of the backbone representation, removing the trainable projector and forcing uniformity pressure onto the representation itself; on ImageNet it outperforms SimCLR with a trainable linear projector [2]. The lesson generalizes: any component between the representation and the loss can serve as a collapse sink, so spectra must be measured at *every* pipeline stage, not only at the loss.

The following Rust sketch computes the singular spectrum and effective rank — the diagnostic at the heart of this analysis:

```rust
/// Effective rank of an embedding batch from its covariance spectrum.
pub fn effective_rank(singular_values: &[f64]) -> f64 {
    let sum: f64 = singular_values.iter().sum();
    let sum_sq: f64 = singular_values.iter().map(|s| s * s).sum();
    if sum_sq == 0.0 { 0.0 } else { (sum * sum) / sum_sq }
}

/// Fraction of variance captured by the top-k singular values.
pub fn variance_explained(singular_values: &[f64], k: usize) -> f64 {
    let total: f64 = singular_values.iter().map(|s| s * s).sum();
    let top_k: f64 = singular_values.iter().take(k).map(|s| s * s).sum();
    if total == 0.0 { 0.0 } else { top_k / total }
}
```

A collapsed embedding with $d = 2048$ may show $r_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 40$–$120$ and 95% variance in the top few dozen components, while a healthy one shows $r_{\mathrm{eff}}$ in the hundreds with gentle decay [2].

### 4.4 Temperature as a geometric control knob

Temperature $\tau$ corresponds to $1/(2t)$: it sets the *range* of repulsion. Small $\tau$ means large $t$, a *short-range* repulsion — embeddings feel each other only when close, so the loss tolerates large-scale non-uniformity (clusters separated by empty regions) as long as local neighborhoods are tight; the optimum approaches well-separated point masses, i.e., strong clustering. Large $\tau$ means long-range repulsion: every pair repels, the energy is minimized only by genuinely uniform coverage, but positive-pair attraction weakens because the softmax flattens.

This predicts the well-known temperature sensitivity: too small and the representation fragments into tight, semantically arbitrary clusters (alignment dominates); too large and positives fail to bind (uniformity dominates and the loss cannot distinguish signal from noise). The optimum is where the *range of repulsion* matches the *scale of the augmentation neighborhood* — quantifiable by comparing $\tau$ to typical $\|z - z^+\|^2$ under the augmentation distribution. Practitioners should therefore tune $\tau$ jointly with augmentation strength: stronger augmentations (larger positive-pair distances) demand proportionally larger $\tau$ to keep the two forces balanced.

The invariance requirement can be stated as a temporal-logic specification — a healthy geometry must avoid *both* full collapse and total fragmentation at all times:

```tla
---------------------------- MODULE AugInvariance ----------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals

CONSTANTS Embeddings, Tau, Epsilon

VARIABLES z  \* z[e] in S^{d-1}: current embedding of instance e

AugNeighbors(e) == { e2 \in Embeddings : Dist(z[e], z[e2]) < Epsilon }

TypeOK == \A e \in Embeddings : Norm(z[e]) = 1

NoCollapse == \A e \in Embeddings : AugNeighbors(e) /= Embeddings
NoFragment == \A e \in Embeddings : AugNeighbors(e) /= {}

Spec == TypeOK /\ []NoCollapse /\ []NoFragment
=============================================================================
```

### 4.5 The three-scale geometry of a trained model

A trained contrastive model exhibits a characteristic three-scale geometry: at the finest scale, augmentation neighborhoods are tight (low $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}$); at the intermediate scale, semantic neighborhoods — instances sharing class or content — form soft clusters inherited from the data manifold; at the global scale, the cloud covers the sphere with near-uniform density (low $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{unif}}$). Dimensional collapse degrades the third scale first: the cloud flattens into a subspace, the spectrum develops its elbow, and downstream linear probes lose the fine-grained features in the discarded dimensions [2]. Monitoring $r_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and the spectrum elbow during training gives an early warning long before linear-probe accuracy degrades.

## 5. Empirical Results

**Protocol.** The standard evaluation is *linear probing*: the encoder is frozen and a linear classifier trained on the (pre-projector) representation. Alignment and uniformity are estimated from batch statistics [1]; dimensional health from the covariance singular spectrum [2].

**Key findings from the literature:**

1. **Alignment and uniformity predict downstream performance.** Both metrics correlate strongly with linear-probe accuracy across vision and language benchmarks, and directly optimizing their weighted sum matches or exceeds standard contrastive training — evidence the decomposition captures causally relevant geometry [1].
2. **SimCLR's recipe.** Strong augmentation composition, a nonlinear projection head, and large batches yield 76.5% top-1 linear accuracy on ImageNet with ResNet-50, matching supervised performance; the pre-projector representation transfers substantially better [3].
3. **MoCo's dictionary view.** A queue plus momentum encoder decouples negatives from batch size, reaching 60.6% top-1 on ImageNet with ResNet-50 and transferring strongly to detection and segmentation — uniformity pressure depends on the *number and consistency* of negatives [4].
4. **Dimensional collapse is real in contrastive models.** SimCLR-style embeddings show pronounced low-rank structure attributable to the trainable projector; projector-free DirectCLR recovers full-rank representations and outperforms the projector-based baseline on ImageNet [2].
5. **The supervised analogue.** In supervised training, within-class variability vanishes (NC1), class means converge to a simplex ETF (NC2), and classifiers become self-dual to the means (NC3) [6] — the terminal geometry of cross-entropy on the sphere, and the instance-level counterpart of contrastive uniformity.

| CPC [5] | Future prediction | In-batch | — (multi-domain) | InfoNCE as MI bound |
| MoCo [4] | Instance discrimination | Queue (65K) | 60.6% | Consistent dictionary |

## 6. Limitations

- **Asymptotics versus practice.** The decomposition holds in the $K \to \infty$ limit [1]; practical $K$ (hundreds to tens of thousands) introduces finite-sample bias in the partition-function estimate that interacts with temperature in ways the asymptotic theory does not capture.
- **Augmentation dependence.** The theory conditions on $p_{\mathrm{pos}}$. If augmentations destroy semantic content, alignment to them is actively harmful, and no uniformity term recovers the lost signal.
- **Modality specificity.** The cleanest results are in vision. In language and audio, where "views" are harder to define, the hyperspherical picture is less settled and uniformity can conflict with discrete data structure.
- **Uniformity is not always desirable.** For strongly hierarchical data, perfect uniformity destroys the neighborhood relations downstream tasks need; the loss optimum and the transfer optimum can diverge under distribution shift.
- **Diagnostics are correlational.** Spectrum elbows and effective rank flag *symptoms*; causal attribution to the projector, while well supported [2], can be confounded by optimizer dynamics, weight decay, and batch-normalization statistics.
- **Non-contrastive methods are out of scope.** BYOL, SimSiam, and VICReg avoid explicit negatives and lie outside the InfoNCE decomposition; their collapse modes require separate analysis.

## 7. Conclusion

Contrastive self-supervised learning is best understood not as mutual-information estimation but as *geometric optimization on the hypersphere*: InfoNCE asymptotically decomposes into an alignment term binding augmented views and a uniformity term spreading embeddings via a classical repulsive potential [1, 5]. This framing unifies the engineering lore — the projection head as collapse buffer [3, 2], temperature as the range of repulsion, large negative pools as better Thomson solvers [4] — and predicts dimensional collapse, whose spectral signatures practitioners can monitor with the covariance tools of Section 3.3 [2]. The parallel with neural collapse [6] suggests maximal-separation geometry under repulsive potentials is a general principle of deep representation learning, differing across paradigms only in separation granularity. The prescriptions are concrete: measure alignment, uniformity, and effective rank at every pipeline stage; tune temperature against augmentation strength; treat the projector as a diagnostic window; and be suspicious of any embedding whose spectrum has an elbow. The sphere is unforgiving of wasted dimensions.
