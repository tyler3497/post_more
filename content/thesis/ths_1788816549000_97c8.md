# HotStuff and the Linearization of Byzantine Consensus: Three-Phase Voting, Quorum-Certificate Pipelining, and Responsive View-Change in Partial Synchrony

## Abstract

HotStuff is a leader-based Byzantine fault-tolerant replication protocol that reconciles two properties long thought to be in tension: communication complexity linear in the number of replicas, and *optimistic responsiveness*—progress at the pace of actual network delay. Presented by Yin, Malkhi, Reiter, Gueta, and Abraham [1], HotStuff achieves this in the partially synchronous model through three innovations: (i) replacing all-to-all broadcast with leader-centric vote collection and *quorum certificates* (QCs) built from threshold signatures, yielding O(n) messages per decision; (ii) a three-phase voting rule—*prepare*, *pre-commit*, *commit*—whose phases share an identical communication pattern, permitting *chained pipelining*; and (iii) a *pacemaker* rotating leaders every view with linear re-synchronization cost. This thesis reconstructs the design from classical BFT foundations, gives a rigorous account of the safety and liveness arguments, analyzes threshold-signature infrastructure, surveys the evolution into Fast-HotStuff [5], HotStuff-2, and HotStuff-1 [6], and discusses empirical evaluation and limitations. HotStuff underpins Diem/LibraBFT, Aptos, and Hyperliquid [4, 7].

## 1 Introduction

The problem of Byzantine fault-tolerant state-machine replication—reaching agreement among n = 3f + 1 replicas despite f adversarial nodes—has been a central concern of distributed systems since the Byzantine Generals formulation [2]. Practical BFT replication, as crystallized by Castro and Liskov's PBFT [3], demonstrated that consensus could sustain thousands of requests per second on modest clusters. Yet PBFT's communication profile was quadratic: every phase required all-to-all multicast, so each decision cost O(n²) authenticators on the wire. View changes were worse—O(n²) to O(n³) depending on the accounting—making leader failover the perennial Achilles' heel of implementations and the graveyard of several production efforts.

For two decades, the field regarded this quadratic footprint as the price of *responsiveness*: protocols that move at the speed of the actual network, rather than waiting out worst-case delay bounds, seemingly required replicas to exchange votes directly with one another. HotStuff [1] falsified that assumption. Its central claim is disarmingly simple: **make every phase look the same**. If prepare, pre-commit, and commit all reduce to the identical one-to-all/all-to-one communication pattern—a leader broadcasting a proposal and collecting 2f + 1 threshold-signed votes into a quorum certificate—then linearity falls out naturally, and the phases can be *pipelined* so that a single round serves triple duty. The result is the first partially synchronous BFT protocol to achieve both O(n) communication and responsiveness.

The significance of this result is not purely asymptotic. HotStuff was designed explicitly "in the lens of blockchain," where consortia envision committees of hundreds of validators and where rotating leadership is a fairness and decentralization requirement, not an exoticism. The protocol became the consensus core of Meta's Diem (formerly Libra) project via LibraBFT, and its descendants power Aptos and Hyperliquid mainnets today [4]. Understanding HotStuff is therefore not only understanding a theorem; it is understanding the architecture of contemporary high-value ledgers.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Partial synchrony and responsiveness

HotStuff operates in the *partial synchrony* model of Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer [8]: there exists an unknown Global Stabilization Time (GST) and an unknown delay bound Δ such that, after GST, every message between correct replicas arrives within Δ. Crucially, the protocol never needs to know Δ or GST; safety holds in fully asynchronous executions, and liveness is guaranteed only after GST. This distinction matters because the alternative—synchronous protocols—buy liveness at the cost of fragility: a single misestimated bound causes permanent failure.

Within this model, *responsiveness* is the property that a correct leader drives the protocol forward at the speed of actual message delivery, without waiting for any timeout in the common case [1]. PBFT is not responsive: its view-change machinery and certain phase transitions hinge on timers calibrated to Δ, so latency degrades to worst-case bounds precisely when performance matters most. Tendermint improves on PBFT but retains three sequential gossip-based phases with timing dependencies. HotStuff's ambition is responsiveness *with* linearity—a combination no prior partially synchronous protocol had achieved [1].

### 2.2 The classical lineage: DLS, PBFT, Tendermint, Casper

One of HotStuff's most intellectually satisfying contributions is the *common framework* in which it re-expresses prior protocols. Yin et al. [1] demonstrate that DLS, PBFT, Tendermint, and Casper FFG can all be cast in a single vocabulary of phases, votes, and quorum certificates, exposing exactly where each pays its quadratic tax:

| Protocol | Phases | Comm. per decision | View change | Responsive? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT [3] | pre-prepare, prepare, commit | O(n²) | O(n²)–O(n³) | No |
| Tendermint | propose, prevote, precommit | O(n²) gossip | round-robin, O(n²) | Partial |
| Casper FFG | justify/finalize epochs | O(n²) | — (finality gadget) | No |
| HotStuff [1] | prepare, pre-commit, commit | **O(n)** | **O(n)** | **Yes** |
| HotStuff-2 | prepare, commit | **O(n)** | **O(n)** | **Yes** |

The table's lesson is that the quadratic term lives in the *all-to-all* vote dissemination of the prepare and commit phases. SBFT [9] had already shown that threshold signatures could linearize the common path, but its view change remained quadratic and it was not responsive. HotStuff's insight was that linearity and responsiveness share a common prerequisite: phase uniformity.

### 2.3 Threshold signatures and quorum certificates

The cryptographic workhorse is the (k, n)-threshold signature scheme, instantiated in practice with BLS signatures [10]. Each replica holds a share of a joint secret; any k = 2f + 1 partial signatures on the same message combine into a single compact signature that verifies against the joint public key. A *quorum certificate* (QC) is such a combined signature over a leader's proposal, and its defining property is succinctness: a QC is O(1) in size regardless of n, so a leader can attach proof of quorum support to a broadcast without retransmitting O(n) signatures.

> Theorem: *QC succinctness.* A quorum certificate over k partial signatures has size independent of n (a single BLS signature plus a bitmap or aggregate identifier), so carrying quorum evidence in a leader broadcast costs O(1) bytes.

This primitive converts vote *dissemination*—which was the quadratic bottleneck—into vote *collection*: votes flow replica → leader, and quorum evidence flows leader → replicas. The leader does linear work per phase (sending n messages, verifying n signatures), and every other replica does constant work. Total: O(n) authenticators per decision.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 System model

We assume n = 3f + 1 replicas, of which at most f are Byzantine—capable of arbitrary, coordinated deviation. Correct replicas follow the protocol; all messages are authenticated (digital signatures), so the adversary cannot forge a correct replica's vote. Network is partially synchronous per [8]. The protocol proceeds in *views*, each with a designated leader given by a rotating function (e.g., round-robin); view v + 1 begins after view v's leader either drives a decision or is suspected faulty. A *pacemaker* module (Section 4.3) handles view synchronization.

### 3.2 Data structures

The protocol's state revolves around two structures:

- **Node (proposal):** a leader's proposal for view v, containing a batch of client commands and a reference to the parent QC (in the chained variant, the parent block's QC is embedded on-chain, enabling external verification [7]).
- **Quorum certificate (QC):** a threshold signature over (view, node-id), proving that 2f + 1 replicas voted for that node in that view. A *genericQC* is the highest QC known to a replica; replicas also maintain a *lockedQC*—the QC of the highest pre-committed node—used in the voting safety rule.

### 3.3 The voting safety rule

The invariant that guarantees safety is enforced locally by each correct replica through a simple predicate on incoming proposals:

> Theorem: *Voting rule.* A correct replica votes for a proposal p in view v only if (a) p extends the branch of the replica's lockedQC (i.e., p's parent chain includes the locked node), and (b) p's view exceeds the replica's current view. This rule guarantees that no two conflicting nodes can both obtain QCs in the same or later views once a node is committed.

The two-chain structure of the commit rule (Section 4.1) makes this local predicate sufficient: a committed node implies a lockedQC that blocks any future conflicting quorum.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The basic protocol: three-phase voting

Basic HotStuff (non-pipelined) proceeds through three voting phases per decision, each with the *same* communication pattern:

1. **Prepare.** The leader of view v broadcasts a proposal carrying the highest QC it knows (collected from new-view messages). Replicas check the voting rule; if satisfied, they send prepare-votes to the leader. On collecting 2f + 1 votes, the leader forms a *prepareQC*.
2. **Pre-commit.** The leader broadcasts the prepareQC. Replicas verify it, update their genericQC, and—crucially—*lock* on the prepareQC's node (updating lockedQC) before sending pre-commit votes. The leader aggregates these into a *precommitQC*.
3. **Commit.** The leader broadcasts the precommitQC. Replicas respond with commit-votes; the leader forms a *commitQC* and broadcasts it. A replica that sees a commitQC *commits* (executes) the node.

Why three phases instead of PBFT's two? The extra phase is the price of responsiveness under leader rotation. Because the leader changes every view, a new leader must learn the highest prepared value *and* be certain that no conflicting value could have committed. The pre-commit phase, combined with the lock, provides exactly this: once a precommitQC exists, 2f + 1 replicas are locked, so any future quorum intersects the locked set, and the new leader's proposal must extend the locked branch. In Casper/Streamlet-style two-phase variants, responsiveness is lost because replicas must wait a full timeout to rule out a hidden commit [11].

The cost of the basic variant is latency: three sequential round trips per decision. But the uniformity of phases is what enables the chained optimization.

### 4.2 Chained HotStuff: QC pipelining and the three-chain rule

The chained variant observes that since every phase has the same shape—leader broadcasts a QC, replicas vote, leader forms the next QC—the phases of *consecutive views* can overlap. View v's leader proposes a block extending view v − 1's block; the prepare-phase of view v simultaneously serves as the pre-commit phase of view v − 1's block and the commit phase of view v − 2's block. Four blocks are effectively in flight at once, and the protocol achieves the throughput of one decision per round trip while retaining three-phase safety.

The commit rule becomes beautifully simple:

> Theorem: *Three-chain commit rule.* When a replica observes three blocks B₁, B₂, B₃ with consecutive views such that each carries the QC of its parent (B₁ ← B₂ ← B₃, views v, v+1, v+2), then B₁ is committed, along with its entire prefix chain.

Equivalently, a *two-chain* (prepareQC formed on a block) sets the lock, and a *three-chain* finalizes. This is the same finalization rule as Streamlet [11], but HotStuff adds linearity: replicas send votes only to the next leader rather than gossiping, so per-block cost is n broadcasts + n votes = O(n) [7].

### 4.3 The pacemaker: linear, responsive view change

View change—moving to a new leader after a suspected failure—is where classical protocols paid their quadratic tax. HotStuff's *pacemaker* achieves O(n) view change with an elegant trick: new-view messages are sent *to the next leader*, not broadcast. Each replica sends its highest prepareQC to the leader of view v + 1 (one message each, O(n) total); the leader selects the highest QC among the 2f + 1 it receives and proposes extending it. Because any two quorums intersect in at least one correct replica, the selected QC is at least as recent as any that could have committed—safety is preserved, and no all-to-all exchange is needed.

Responsiveness of the pacemaker requires care: after GST, replicas must enter views in a synchronized-enough fashion that a correct leader's view overlaps with 2f + 1 correct replicas for long enough to drive three phases. The pacemaker achieves this with a standard doubling-timeout scheme—each replica doubles its view timer until progress is made—so that eventually a correct leader enjoys a sufficiently long, synchronized view. The result: **linear view change with liveness**, the first such construction in partial synchrony [1].

### 4.4 Formalizing optimistic responsiveness

HotStuff's responsiveness deserves a precise statement, because the term is often used loosely:

> Theorem: *Optimistic responsiveness.* After GST, if the leader of view v is correct and 2f + 1 correct replicas remain in view v, then the leader drives a decision in time proportional to the actual network delay δ (not the bound Δ), specifically within O(δ) of the view's start.

The mechanism is simple but crucial: the leader advances each phase as soon as 2f + 1 votes arrive, rather than waiting for a timeout. This is what the Fast-HotStuff authors identify as the property worth preserving even while reducing phase count [5]. Note the honest accounting in the BFT survey literature [12]: in the worst case of cascading faulty leaders, HotStuff takes O(f·n) messages across O(n) views—quadratic total—before a correct leader stabilizes. Linearity is a *per-view, common-case* guarantee; the worst case over f consecutive faulty leaders is inherently O(n²), and no rotating-leader protocol can do better.

### 4.5 The lineage: Fast-HotStuff, HotStuff-2, HotStuff-1

The HotStuff framework proved fertile, spawning a family of refinements:

- **Fast-HotStuff** [5] reduces latency by collapsing to a two-chain commit rule while remaining resilient to forking attacks, at the cost of a small overhead on the block during the "unhappy path" (primary failure).
- **HotStuff-2** (Malkhi & Nayak, 2023) shows that two phases suffice for responsiveness *without* extra block overhead, through a more careful view-change keyed on the highest locked QC.
- **HotStuff-1** [6] attacks latency from a different direction: *speculative* early finality confirmations to clients after a single phase, resolving the "prefix speculation dilemma" of streamlined protocols, and adding *slotting* so leaders can drive multiple decisions per view—cutting two network hops relative to HotStuff-2 while preserving linear complexity under faults.

def on_proposal(replica, block):
    # block = {view, parent_qc, payload}; parent_qc = (view, qc_sig)

        return  # violates safety rule: does not extend locked branch
    if block.view <= replica.generic_qc.view:
        return  # stale view

        replica.locked_qc = block.parent_qc        # two-chain => lock

def on_votes(leader, view):

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Evaluation: the original deployment

Yin et al. [1] implemented HotStuff and deployed it on a network of **over 100 replicas**, comparing directly against BFT-SMaRt, the production-hardened PBFT implementation. The headline result: HotStuff achieves **throughput and latency comparable to BFT-SMaRt** while reducing the leader-failover communication footprint from quadratic to linear. Independent evaluation of the pipelined variant [13] confirmed the throughput story: overlapping blocks in flight multiplies effective throughput relative to the basic three-round-trips-per-decision protocol. Production telemetry from HotStuff-derived mainnets (Aptos, Hyperliquid) shows block-time distributions dominated by quorum-formation latency rather than timeout behavior—a signature of responsive, pipelined operation [4].

### 5.2 Safety proof sketch

> Theorem: *Safety.* If two correct replicas commit blocks B and B′, then B and B′ do not conflict (one extends the other).

*Proof sketch.* Suppose B commits at view v via a three-chain B ← B₂ ← B₃ (views v, v+1, v+2). Forming the prepareQC for B₂ required 2f + 1 pre-commit votes on B, so 2f + 1 replicas locked on B's branch (lockedQC ≥ v). Consider any later view v′ > v + 2 in which a conflicting block B* obtains a prepareQC. That QC required 2f + 1 votes; by quorum intersection, at least one correct replica r voted both to lock on B's branch and for B*. But a correct replica votes for a proposal only if it extends its locked branch—contradiction, since B* conflicts with B. ∎

The argument's elegance lies in its locality: global safety reduces to the local voting rule plus quorum intersection, with no reasoning about message timing.

### 5.3 Liveness proof sketch

> Theorem: *Liveness.* After GST, the protocol commits new client commands infinitely often.

*Proof sketch.* The pacemaker's doubling timers guarantee that eventually some view v with a correct leader lasts long enough (≥ c·δ for the needed round trips) with 2f + 1 correct replicas present. In that view, the leader collects 2f + 1 new-view messages, proposes extending the highest QC, and—by the voting rule—every correct replica votes, since the proposal extends their locked branches (the highest QC is ≥ any lock, by quorum intersection over the 2f + 1 new-view senders). Three consecutive such views form a three-chain; the head commits. ∎

## 6 Limitations

An honest accounting of HotStuff must acknowledge where the design pays its costs:

1. **Cryptographic infrastructure.** Linearity depends on threshold signatures. BLS setup requires distributed key generation (DKG), and the leader verifies O(n) signatures per phase—a computational bottleneck at large n, mitigated by batching but not eliminated.
2. **Worst-case quadratic behavior.** The O(n) guarantee is per-view. With f consecutive faulty leaders, the protocol burns O(n) views × O(n) messages = O(n²) before stabilizing [12]. Pipelining interacts poorly with faults: a faulty leader's garbage block occupies pipeline slots, and the three-view commit latency means a fault's damage is discovered late.
3. **Latency in small deployments.** For n ≤ 10, PBFT's two phases often beat HotStuff's three in absolute latency; the asymptotic advantage only materializes at scale. HotStuff-2 and Fast-HotStuff exist precisely to claw back this constant.
4. **Forking attacks.** Fast-HotStuff [5] documents the *forking attack*: a Byzantine leader can propose voting-rule-compliant blocks that prevent commit, exploiting pipelining. The three-phase rule is immune, but two-phase variants must add explicit forking resilience.
5. **Speculation hazards.** HotStuff-1's early confirmations [6] are safe only because of the prefix-speculation analysis; naive speculation in pipelined protocols can confirm prefixes that later get orphaned.
6. **Leader rotation cost.** Rotating every view is elegant for chain quality and DoS resistance, but forfeits the stable-leader fast path that lets PBFT-class protocols skip view-change overhead during long fault-free runs; slotting [6] partially recovers this.

## 7 Conclusion

HotStuff's enduring contribution is a design principle rather than a single protocol: **uniform phases plus succinct quorum evidence yields linear, responsive Byzantine consensus**. By making prepare, pre-commit, and commit share one communication pattern, Yin et al. simultaneously solved the linearity problem (via threshold-signature QCs and leader-centric collection), the pipelining problem (identical phases overlap naturally into chained HotStuff), and the view-change problem (the pacemaker's O(n) re-synchronization). The framework's expressive power—recasting DLS, PBFT, Tendermint, and Casper in common terms—clarified the field's understanding of where quadratic costs originate and why responsiveness had seemed to require them.

The protocol's afterlife confirms its importance: Fast-HotStuff, HotStuff-2, and HotStuff-1 refine the latency–complexity tradeoff; LibraBFT/Diem, Aptos, and Hyperliquid carry its core into production at significant economic scale [4, 7]. Open questions remain—post-quantum threshold signatures to replace BLS, tighter worst-case bounds under adaptive adversaries, and formal verification of the full chained implementation in proof assistants—but the central thesis stands: Byzantine consensus need not choose between scaling linearly and moving at the speed of the network.
