# Marine Ice Sheet Instability: Grounding-Line Dynamics, Tipping Points, and the Mathematics of Irreversible Sea-Level Rise

## Abstract

The marine ice sheet instability (MISI) is the leading mechanistic candidate for abrupt, irreversible sea-level rise from Antarctica. When a grounding line retreats down a retrograde bed slope, thickening ice at the grounding line drives increased discharge, which drives further retreat: a positive feedback with no local stable equilibrium. This thesis unifies the mathematical theory, from Weertman's 1974 junction analysis through Schoof's 2007 boundary-layer flux law to modern continental-scale simulations with hysteresis and marine ice cliff instability. We derive the flux law, prove the retrograde-slope instability criterion, examine how lateral buttressing can suppress the feedback, and review observational evidence from the Amundsen Sea sector suggesting the instability may already be engaged. Synthesis of paleoclimate constraints and model ensembles indicates multi-metre committed sea-level rise under continued warming, with hysteresis implying that reversing temperatures to pre-industrial levels would not restore the present ice-sheet configuration.

## 1 Intro

More than half of Earth's freshwater is locked in the Antarctic Ice Sheet, and the majority of the West Antarctic Ice Sheet (WAIS) rests on bedrock hundreds of metres below sea level. This *marine* character makes the ice sheet uniquely vulnerable: its fate is decided at a narrow, mobile boundary called the **grounding line**, where grounded ice detaches from the bed and begins to float. Small changes in ocean heat delivery at this boundary can reorganise the mass balance of an entire continent [1].

The central question of this thesis is whether that reorganisation is *smooth* or *catastrophic* — whether the grounding line can pass a threshold beyond which retreat becomes self-sustaining, irreversible on human timescales, and insensitive to the removal of the original forcing. The hypothesis that such a threshold exists is the **marine ice sheet instability (MISI)**. It was first stated in recognisably modern form by Mercer, who warned in 1978 that anthropogenic CO2 warming could destabilise the WAIS [3]; it was given mathematical teeth by Weertman [1] and, decisively, by Schoof's boundary-layer theory of grounding-line flux [2].

This thesis is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews the glaciological background: flotation, the stress balance at the ice-sheet/ice-shelf junction, and the role of ice-shelf buttressing. Section 3 describes the methodological toolkit — the shallow-shelf approximation, boundary-layer analysis, and the model intercomparison experiments that resolved a decade of numerical confusion. Section 4 is the deep dive: the derivation of the Schoof flux law, the buttressing correction, the ice-cliff instability hypothesis, and the hysteresis structure of the full nonlinear problem. Section 5 assembles empirical results: satellite-observed grounding-line retreat, paleoclimate constraints, and quantitative sea-level commitments. Section 6 confronts the limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

> **Thesis claim:** On a retrograde bed slope, a marine ice sheet without sufficient lateral buttressing possesses no stable steady grounding-line position; the resulting retreat is not merely fast but *structurally irreversible*, exhibiting hysteresis that commits the planet to metres of sea-level rise long after the triggering forcing ceases.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The grounding line and the flotation condition

The grounding line is defined by the **flotation condition**: ice of thickness *h* detaches from the bed when its weight equals the buoyancy of displaced seawater, i.e. when

```
h(x_g) = -(rho_w / rho_i) * b(x_g)
```

where *b(x)* is the bed elevation (negative below sea level), and *rho_w*, *rho_i* are the densities of seawater and ice. Because the grounding line position *x_g* depends on both ice thickness and bed geometry, it is a *free boundary* — its motion is part of the solution, not prescribed. This free-boundary character is the mathematical root of everything that follows [1].

### 2.2 From Weertman to the modern picture

Weertman (1974) analysed the junction of an ice sheet and an ice shelf under a perfectly-plastic approximation and concluded that a stable ice sheet can occur only if the bed slopes *away* from the ice-sheet centre — a prograde slope [1]. Beds that slope *toward* the interior (retrograde, or "reverse") slopes were predicted to host no stable steady state. Thomas and Bentley (1978) reached similar conclusions and gave the idea weight in the context of WAIS. Hindmarsh (1993, 1996) countered with a neutrally-stable result, exposing how sensitive the conclusion was to the assumed basal physics. The field was, for thirty years, genuinely undecided.

Mercer's contribution was to translate the abstract instability into a concrete climate threat: the WAIS is a marine ice sheet whose stability depends on peripheral ice shelves; warm the climate, lose the shelves, and the marine basins may collapse, raising sea level by several metres [3]. Satellite observations of the Amundsen Sea sector — Pine Island, Thwaites, Smith and Kohler glaciers retreating rapidly from 1992 to 2011 — have since been interpreted as the signature of exactly this process in action [5].

### 2.3 Buttressing: the stabilising counterweight

A floating ice shelf does not, by Archimedes' principle, directly affect sea level when it thins. But it matters enormously *dynamically*: lateral drag against embayment walls and pinning points generates back-stress that restrains the grounded ice upstream. This **buttressing** reduces the longitudinal tensile stress at the grounding line and thereby the discharge flux. Loss of buttressing — through basal melt or calving-front retreat — is the most plausible trigger that pushes a grounding line onto a retrograde slope and initiates MISI [5][8].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Governing equations

Modern analysis works from the **shallow-shelf approximation (SSA)** to the Stokes equations: vertical shear is neglected, membrane stresses dominate, and the momentum balance at the grounding line reduces to a balance between driving stress, basal drag (which vanishes at flotation), and the hydrostatic pressure difference between ice and ocean. The key non-dimensional insight of Schoof (2007) is that the transition zone — where the ice goes from grounded to floating — is a **boundary layer** whose width scales with ice thickness, far narrower than the ice-sheet scale [2].

### 3.2 Matched asymptotics and the flux law

Inside the boundary layer, extensional stresses are large and the SSA equations admit a similarity structure. Matching the inner boundary-layer solution to the outer ice-sheet and ice-shelf solutions yields an algebraic **flux law**: the discharge per unit width *q* at the grounding line is a function of ice thickness there, *h(x_g)*, alone (for the unbuttressed case):

```
q(x_g) = C * h(x_g)^(p),   with p > 1
```

where the constant *C* depends on the Glen flow-law exponent *n*, the basal sliding exponent *m*, and the densities. Schoof's refined analysis gives *p* of order 4–5 for realistic sliding laws; the flux increases *sharply* with grounding-line thickness [2]. Because the flotation condition ties *h(x_g)* to bed depth *-b(x_g)*, the flux is ultimately a function of bed geometry. This single relation converts a problem in partial differential equations into a problem in **ordinary differential equations** for the ice-sheet profile — and makes stability analysis tractable.

### 3.3 Numerical resolution: the MISMIP experiments

Early numerical models famously disagreed about MISI, with grounding-line positions showing strong grid-size dependence [2]. The Marine Ice Sheet Model Intercomparison Project (MISMIP) demonstrated that fixed-grid shallow-ice models cannot correctly represent grounding-line motion; models require either moving grids, sub-grid grounding-line parameterisation, or the flux law imposed as a boundary condition. Once numerics were fixed, the models *confirmed* the analytical theory: no stable steady grounding lines on retrograde slopes, and hysteresis on overdeepened beds [2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Schoof flux law and the instability criterion

Consider a two-dimensional, unbuttressed marine ice sheet in steady state. Mass conservation demands that the grounding-line flux balance the integrated accumulation upstream:

```
q(x_g) = integral from 0 to x_g of a(x) dx
```

with accumulation rate *a(x)*. Using the flux law *q = Q(h(x_g))* and the flotation relation *h(x_g) = -(rho_w/rho_i) b(x_g)*, steady states satisfy

```
Q(-(rho_w/rho_i) * b(x_g)) = A(x_g)
```

where *A* is the integrated accumulation. A steady grounding-line position is therefore determined by the *intersection* of a bed-controlled flux curve with an accumulation curve — discrete equilibria, not a continuum.

> Theorem: (Schoof's instability criterion). For a rapidly-sliding, unbuttressed marine ice sheet, a steady grounding line at *x_g* is stable if and only if the bed slope there is prograde, i.e. *db/dx > 0* in the seaward direction. On a retrograde slope (*db/dx < 0*), no steady grounding line is stable: an infinitesimal retreat perturbation grows.

*Proof sketch.* Perturb the grounding line seaward by *delta x > 0*. On a retrograde bed, the new grounding position has deeper bed, hence (by flotation) thicker ice at the grounding line, hence (by the flux law with *p > 1*) larger discharge. The increased discharge thins the interior, which moves the flotation point further seaward. Linearising gives a growth rate proportional to *-db/dx* times a positive factor; the sign flips with the bed slope. The sharp exponent *p* amplifies the feedback: flux is a strongly superlinear function of depth. Full details are in Schoof (2007), verified against resolved numerical solutions [2].

The physical sequence of the positive feedback is:

1. Ocean warming increases sub-shelf melt, thinning the ice shelf and reducing buttressing.
2. Reduced back-stress accelerates grounded ice; the grounding line retreats.
3. On a retrograde slope, retreat moves the grounding line into deeper water.
4. Flotation demands thicker ice at the new grounding line; the flux law converts thicker ice into much larger discharge.
5. Larger discharge thins the ice sheet, advancing the flotation point further inland — return to step 3.

No step requires continued warming: once the grounding line has passed the bed-slope crest onto the retrograde flank, the loop is self-sustaining. That is the precise content of "irreversible" in MISI [2][5].

### 4.2 Buttressing, confinement, and the suppression of MISI

The instability criterion above assumes an *unconfined*, unbuttressed shelf. Real embayments provide lateral drag, parameterised by a buttressing number *theta* between 0 (no buttressing) and 1 (full restraint). The flux law generalises to

```rust
// Grounding-line flux with lateral buttressing (Schoof-type parameterisation)
fn grounding_line_flux(h: f64, theta: f64, c: f64, p: f64) -> f64 {
    // theta in (0, 1]; flux suppressed as (1 - theta) -> 0
    let effective = (1.0 - theta).max(1e-6);
    c * effective.powf(-p) * h.powf(p)
}

fn main() {
    // h = 1000 m grounding-line thickness, p = 4.75, weak vs strong buttressing
    let q_weak = grounding_line_flux(1000.0, 0.1, 1.0, 4.75);
    let q_strong = grounding_line_flux(1000.0, 0.9, 1.0, 4.75);
    println!("flux ratio (weak/strong buttressing): {:.1e}", q_weak / q_strong);
}
```

Strong buttressing (*theta* near 1) suppresses flux so effectively that the sign of the feedback can reverse locally: retreat into a narrower embayment *increases* lateral drag, stabilising the grounding line even on a retrograde bed [2]. This is why not every retrograde bed hosts an active instability — the Amundsen Sea embayment is dangerous precisely because its shelves are wide, weakly confined, and already thinning. Key modifiers include:

- **Lateral confinement:** narrow fjords and embayments stabilise; wide, unconfined shelves do not.
- **Pinning points:** ice rises and rumples provide local back-stress; unpinning (as observed at Thwaites) removes it abruptly [8].
- **Bed roughness and till:** weak, deformable till beds permit fast sliding that approaches the rapid-sliding limit where the theory is sharpest [2].

### 4.3 Marine ice cliff instability: a secondary tipping element

DeConto and Pollard (2016) proposed an additional, faster instability: once buttressing shelves collapse (e.g. by hydrofracturing from surface meltwater), they expose tall, marine-terminating ice cliffs. Above a critical cliff height (order 100 m above the waterline), the deviatoric stress exceeds the yield strength of ice and the cliff collapses structurally, exposing a new cliff behind it — a kinematic wave of calving that can propagate far faster than MISI grounding-line retreat [4].

Calibrated against Pliocene and Last Interglacial sea-level targets, their coupled ice-sheet/climate model yields Antarctic contributions exceeding 1 m by 2100 and 15 m by 2500 under unabated emissions [4]. The mechanism remains contested — subsequent work questions whether realistic calving laws sustain the required rates — but its inclusion resolves the long-standing model-data mismatch for past warm periods when sea level stood 6–9 m higher than today with CO2 near modern values. Whether or not the cliff mechanism operates at full strength, it illustrates a general lesson: MISI is the *slow, certain* tipping element, and faster failure modes may ride on top of it.

### 4.4 Hysteresis, irreversibility, and committed sea-level rise

On an overdeepened bed (a prograde–retrograde–prograde sequence forming a basin), the steady-state equation admits *multiple* solutions for the same forcing: an advanced grounding line on the outer prograde slope and a retreated one on the inner prograde slope, separated by an unstable branch on the retrograde flank. Varying a control parameter (sea level, accumulation, basal slipperiness) traces an **S-shaped bifurcation diagram** with two fold bifurcations — the canonical hysteresis loop [2].

Garbe et al. (2020) mapped this structure for the whole Antarctic Ice Sheet using the Parallel Ice Sheet Model, finding a *multitude* of temperature thresholds: around 2 °C above pre-industrial, West Antarctica is committed to long-term partial collapse via MISI; between 6 and 9 °C, over 70% of present-day ice volume is lost, driven by the surface-elevation feedback; above 10 °C, Antarctica is committed to a virtually ice-free state. Crucially, each threshold exhibits hysteresis: the modern configuration is *not* regained by reversing temperatures to present-day levels — WAIS does not regrow until temperatures fall at least 1 °C *below* pre-industrial [6].

A minimal hysteresis model can be written in Python as a one-dimensional grounding-line ODE with a double-well potential derived from the overdeepened bed:

```python
import numpy as np

def bed(x):
    """Overdeepened bed: prograde flanks, retrograde basin interior."""
    return -500.0 - 800.0 * np.exp(-((x - 600e3) / 250e3) ** 2)

def flux(h, C=2.0e-6, p=4.75):
    return C * h ** p

def grounding_line_rate(xg, sea_level=0.0, accum=0.3):
    rho_w, rho_i = 1028.0, 917.0
    h = -(rho_w / rho_i) * (bed(xg) - sea_level)  # flotation thickness
    q_out = flux(max(h, 0.0))
    q_in = accum * xg                             # integrated accumulation
    return (q_in - q_out) / max(h, 1.0)           # thickness-scaled migration

# Sweep sea level up then down: advance/retreat paths differ -> hysteresis
xs_up, xs_down = [], []
x = 200e3
for sl in np.linspace(0, 60, 400):          # warming: sea level rises
    x += 50.0 * grounding_line_rate(x, sea_level=sl)
    xs_up.append(x)
x = 900e3
for sl in np.linspace(60, 0, 400):          # cooling: reverse the forcing
    x += 50.0 * grounding_line_rate(x, sea_level=sl)
    xs_down.append(x)
print("hysteresis gap at 30 m forcing (km):",
      abs(xs_up[200] - xs_down[200]) / 1e3)
```

The sweep demonstrates the essential point: the retreat trajectory and the advance trajectory do not coincide. The system has *memory*, and the memory is written in kilometres of grounding-line position and metres of sea level [2][6].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The Amundsen Sea sector: instability observed?

Rignot et al. (2014) used ERS-1/2 radar interferometry to map grounding-line retreat from 1992 to 2011: Pine Island Glacier retreated 31 km at its centre, Thwaites 14 km along its fast-flow core, Smith/Kohler up to 35 km, with most retreat concentrated in 2005–2009 [5]. Critically, they found *no major bed obstacle upstream* of the 2011 grounding lines that could arrest further retreat, and concluded the sector "is undergoing a marine ice sheet instability that will significantly contribute to sea level rise in decades to come." Complementary modelling of the Thwaites basin found that collapse, once initiated past the present grounding zone, is consistent with MISI dynamics and largely insensitive to the details of the trigger [8].

### 5.2 Paleoclimate constraints

The Last Interglacial (about 130,000–115,000 years ago) saw global mean sea level 6–9 m above present with polar temperatures only slightly warmer than today — implicating Antarctica as the primary contributor [4]. Pliocene sea levels, tens of metres higher at near-modern CO2, demand even larger Antarctic losses. DeConto and Pollard showed that models *without* hydrofracturing and cliff-collapse physics cannot reproduce these targets, while models with them can — an indirect but powerful argument that fast marine instabilities operated in the past and can operate again [4].

### 5.3 Quantitative commitments

### 5.4 The timescale question

MISI-driven collapse is *slow* by human standards and *fast* by geological ones: centuries to millennia for WAIS-scale retreat, depending on bed geometry and ocean forcing [6][8].

## 6 Limitations

The theory, for all its elegance, rests on idealisations that real ice sheets routinely violate:

- **Two-dimensionality.** The cleanest results are for flowline (one-horizontal-dimension) geometry. Real grounding lines are curved, buttressed, and threaded by ice streams; lateral stresses can stabilise configurations the 1-D theory declares unstable, and vice versa [2].
- **Basal physics uncertainty.** The flux-law exponent *p* depends on the sliding law, which is poorly constrained beneath kilometres of ice. Coulomb-like plastic till behaviour versus Weertman-type power-law sliding changes quantitative thresholds substantially.
- **Ocean forcing.** Sub-shelf melt rates depend on cavity circulation, tides, and far-field ocean warming — among the least certain components of the coupled system. The trigger is less well understood than the instability it triggers.
- **Ice-cliff instability remains contested.** The calving rates required by DeConto and Pollard exceed most observational analogues; whether MICI is a genuine tipping element or a model artefact is unresolved [4].
- **Glacial isostatic adjustment.** Bedrock uplift as ice unloads can shallow the bed and *stabilise* retreat — a negative feedback omitted from the simplest MISI analysis, potentially important on millennial timescales.
- **Definition of stability.** Recent literature cautions that "stable" and "unstable" are often misapplied to *unsteady*, transient grounding-line migration under time-varying forcing; the rigorous statements concern steady states that real ice sheets never attain.

## 7 Conclusion

The mathematics of the marine ice sheet instability is, at its core, a story about a free boundary on a downhill slope that runs the wrong way. Weertman identified the junction problem [1]; Schoof proved that the flux through the grounding line grows superlinearly with depth and that this single fact forbids stable steady states on retrograde beds [2]; Mercer translated the theorem into a warning about our own century [3]. Observations now show grounding lines retreating kilometres per decade into deepening basins with no topographic barrier ahead [5][8], and continental-scale models reveal a nested set of hysteresis thresholds whose crossing commits the planet to metres of sea-level rise that no plausible future cooling can reverse [6].

Three messages survive the caveats of Section 6. First, MISI is no longer a hypothesis awaiting its first test; it is a *mathematical theorem* about a well-posed free-boundary problem, confirmed by resolved numerics and consistent with the observed pattern of retreat. Second, the dangerous quantity is not the rate of present-day loss but the *position* of the grounding line relative to the bed-slope crest — commitment precedes catastrophe by centuries. Third, hysteresis makes the asymmetry brutal: preventing the crossing is cheap compared with reversing it, because reversal is, to a good approximation, impossible. The grounding line, once it has slid into the basin, does not come back.
