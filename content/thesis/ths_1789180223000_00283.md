# Neural Differential Equations: Continuous-Depth Architectures, Adjoint-Based Learning, Continuous Normalizing Flows, and Stochastic Extensions

## Abstract

Neural ordinary differential equations (NODEs) recast deep learning as the numerical integration of a parameterized dynamical system, replacing the discrete composition of layers with a continuous transformation governed by `dh(t)/dt = f(h(t), t, θ)`. This thesis develops the mathematical and computational foundations of neural differential equations. First, we formalize the residual network as an Euler discretization of a continuous flow and characterize its expressivity gains and inherited topological constraints. Second, we derive the adjoint sensitivity method, which backpropagates through any black-box ODE solver at constant memory cost by solving an augmented ODE backward in time. Third, we treat continuous normalizing flows and FFJORD, where the instantaneous change-of-variables formula replaces the cubic-cost Jacobian determinant with a trace, made scalable by Hutchinson's estimator, enabling unrestricted architectures with unbiased density estimation. Fourth, we generalize the framework to stochastic differential equations, deriving the stochastic adjoint, latent-SDE variational training, and infinite-dimensional GAN connections. We benchmark density estimation, irregular time series, and generative modeling, prove the key theorems, and assess the numerical, stability, and expressivity limits of the paradigm.

## 1. Introduction

Deep neural networks are overwhelmingly *discrete*: an input traverses a fixed, finite sequence of transformations `h_{t+1} = T_t(h_t)`, each parameterized independently. Residual networks weakened this discreteness by introducing skip connections of the form `h_{t+1} = h_t + f(h_t, θ_t)`, which several authors recognized as one explicit Euler step of a continuous dynamical system. **Neural ordinary differential equations**, introduced by Chen, Duvenaud, and collaborators [1], take the limit literally: rather than specifying a sequence of layers, one parameterizes the *time derivative* of the hidden state with a single neural network and computes the output by invoking a black-box ODE solver:

$$\frac{dh(t)}{dt} = f(h(t), t, \theta), \qquad h(t_1) = h(t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t_1} f(h(t), t, \theta)\,dt.$$

This reframing is not a cosmetic relabeling. It endows the model with *constant memory cost* during training (via the adjoint sensitivity method), *adaptive computation* (the solver allocates function evaluations where the vector field is stiff or intricate), and the ability to trade numerical precision for speed at inference time — properties no fixed-depth network can claim. It also unlocks a generative modeling primitive, the **continuous normalizing flow (CNF)**, in which likelihood evaluation collapses from an `O(D³)` determinant to an `O(D)` trace [1]. The stochastic generalization replaces the deterministic flow with an Itô diffusion, yielding **neural stochastic differential equations** trainable by a stochastic adjoint [1] or as infinite-dimensional GANs [5].

Yet the continuous viewpoint exacts its own price. ODE flows are *homeomorphisms*: trajectories cannot cross, so the input topology is preserved, which provably restricts the functions a vanilla NODE can represent [1]. Adaptive solvers can become catastrophically slow when the learned vector field stiffens, and the adjoint's backward solve can be numerically unstable on long horizons. This thesis gives a complete, self-contained treatment: the theory that makes neural differential equations work, the empirical evidence that they matter, and the honest accounting of where they break down.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Residual Networks as Discretized Flows

Consider a residual block `h_{t+1} = h_t + f(h_t, θ_t)` with `t ∈ {0, …, T−1}`. Interpreting the layer index as a time discretization with step `Δt = 1`, this is precisely the explicit Euler update for `dh/dt = f(h, t, θ)`. Classical results on the convergence of Euler schemes suggest that as `Δt → 0` and the number of steps grows, the discrete composition converges to the solution of the ODE — provided the per-layer parameters `θ_t` are replaced by a *shared*, time-dependent parameterization `f(h, t, θ)`.

The conceptual payoff is threefold:

- **Parameter sharing across depth.** A NODE uses one vector field for the entire trajectory, so model capacity is decoupled from "depth": depth becomes a *numerical* property of the solver, not an architectural one.
- **Adaptive evaluation.** A Dormand–Prince (RK45) solver with adaptive step sizing evaluates `f` more often in regions of high curvature and rarely where the field is smooth — an input-dependent compute graph no static network can express.
- **Invertibility for free.** Reversing the integration limits maps `h(t₁)` back to `h(t₀)` with the same solver, enabling reversible architectures and normalizing flows without coupling-layer restrictions.

### 2.2 Numerical ODE Solvers and Sensitivity Analysis

Training requires gradients of a scalar loss `L(h(t₁))` with respect to `θ`, where `h(t₁)` is the output of a numerical solver. Naive backpropagation through the solver's internal operations stores every intermediate stage — memory linear in the number of function evaluations (NFEs), which adaptive solvers make large. The classical remedy, dating to Pontryagin and widely used in optimal control and aerodynamics, is the **adjoint sensitivity method**: define the adjoint state `a(t) = ∂L/∂h(t)` and derive an ODE it satisfies, then integrate *backward* from `t₁` to `t₀` [1].

### 2.3 Normalizing Flows and the Change of Variables

A normalizing flow maps samples `z ∼ p_z` through an invertible transformation `x = g(z)`; the density follows from `log p(x) = log p(z) − log |det ∂g/∂z|`. Discrete flows (NICE, Real NVP, Glow) restrict `g` to coupling layers so the determinant is triangular and cheap — at the cost of architectural flexibility [2]. The continuous analog replaces the discrete map with an ODE flow, and the determinant with a trace integral, as we develop in §4.3.

## 3. Methodology

Our exposition follows the historical and logical development of the field:

1. **Continuous-depth formulation.** We define the NODE forward model, its discretization relationships to ResNets and RNNs, and the regularity conditions (Lipschitz continuity of `f` in `h`, uniformly in `t`) guaranteeing existence and uniqueness of solutions via the Picard–Lindelöf theorem.
2. **Adjoint-based training.** We derive the adjoint ODE from the calculus of variations, present the augmented-state implementation used in `torchdiffeq`, and analyze its `O(1)` memory complexity.
3. **Continuous normalizing flows.** We prove the instantaneous change-of-variables formula, introduce Hutchinson's trace estimator, and describe the FFJORD architecture that removes all architectural restrictions.
4. **Stochastic extensions.** We formulate neural SDEs `dZ_t = μ(Z_t, t, θ)dt + σ(Z_t, t, θ)dW_t`, derive the stochastic adjoint, and describe latent-SDE variational training and the SDE-GAN paradigm.

> **Theorem: (Picard–Lindelöf, informally).** If `f(h, t, θ)` is continuous in `t` and Lipschitz continuous in `h`, then for any initial condition `h(t₀)` there exists a unique solution `h(t)` on an interval around `t₀`. Neural vector fields built from Lipschitz activations (tanh, softplus, ReLU with spectral normalization) satisfy this hypothesis, so the NODE forward pass is well-posed [1].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Continuous-Depth Models: From Euler Steps to Integral Curves

The forward computation of a NODE is the initial value problem

$$h(t_1) = \mathrm{ODESolve}\big(h(t_0),\, f,\, t_0,\, t_1,\, \theta\big) = h(t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t_1} f(h(t), t, \theta)\, dt.$$

The integral is evaluated numerically. A fixed-step Euler scheme with `N` steps recovers a weight-tied ResNet; a fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme yields a more accurate, deeper effective network; and an adaptive Dormand–Prince solver chooses its own steps, producing an *input-dependent* depth. Chen et al. [1] demonstrate that on MNIST, an ODE-Net matches a ResNet's accuracy with an order of magnitude fewer parameters, precisely because depth is outsourced to the solver.

Crucially, the map `h(t₀) ↦ h(t₁)` is a **diffeomorphism** (under the regularity conditions above): ODE trajectories in phase space cannot intersect, since intersection would violate uniqueness. Dupont, Doucet, and Teh [1] proved this topological rigidity is a genuine expressivity barrier: there exist simple functions — e.g., the 1D reflection `g(x) = −x`, or the "annulus-to-disk" map in 2D — that no NODE can represent, because they require tearing or crossing the input topology.

> **Theorem: (Topological obstruction, Dupont et al. [1]).** Let `φ: ℝᵈ → ℝᵈ` be the flow map of an ODE `dh/dt = f(h, t)` with `f` Lipschitz. Then `φ` is a homeomorphism of `ℝᵈ`. Consequently, NODEs cannot represent any function `g` for which `g` is not homotopic to the identity through homeomorphisms — in particular, they cannot map a connected set to a disconnected one or reverse orientation in one dimension.

The **Augmented NODE** [1] resolves this by evolving in a higher-dimensional space: concatenate `p` auxiliary channels, `d/dt [h(t); a(t)] = f([h; a], t, θ)`, and read out only `h(t₁)`. Lifting to `ℝ^{d+p}` gives trajectories room to pass "over" each other, restoring universality while remaining cheaper in practice than the stiff vector fields a vanilla NODE learns when forced to approximate non-representable maps. Empirically, augmented NODEs train faster, generalize better, and require fewer function evaluations than their unaugmented counterparts on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet [1].

### 4.2 The Adjoint Sensitivity Method: O(1)-Memory Backpropagation

The training problem is `min_θ L(h(t₁))` subject to the ODE constraint. Introduce the adjoint `a(t) = ∂L/∂h(t)`. Differentiating the identity `a(t)ᵀ = ∂L/∂h(t₁) · ∂h(t₁)/∂h(t)` and using the flow's variational equation yields the adjoint ODE:

$$\frac{da(t)}{dt} = -\,a(t)^\top \frac{\partial f(h(t), t, \theta)}{\partial h}, \qquad a(t_1) = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h(t_1)}.$$

Gradients with respect to parameters follow from a second integral:

$$\frac{dL}{d\theta} = -\int_{t_1}^{t_0} a(t)^\top \frac{\partial f(h(t), t, \theta)}{\partial \theta}\, dt, \qquad \frac{dL}{dt_0} = a(t_0)^\top f(h(t_0), t_0, \theta)\ \text{(for free-time problems)}.$$

All three quantities — `h(t)`, `a(t)`, `dL/dθ` — are obtained by **one backward solve** of an augmented ODE in the concatenated state `[h, a, dL/dθ]` from `t₁` to `t₀`. Memory cost is `O(1)` in the number of solver steps: no intermediate activations are stored; `h(t)` is recomputed on the backward pass. The following sketch mirrors the `torchdiffeq` implementation [1]:

```python
import torch

def ode_adjoint_backward(loss_grad_h1, h0, t_span, f, theta):
    """One backward pass of the adjoint sensitivity method.

    Solves the augmented system backwards in time:
        s(t) = [h(t), a(t), g(t)] with
        dh/dt =  f(h, t, theta)
        da/dt = -a^T df/dh
        dg/dt = -a^T df/dtheta
    Returns dL/dtheta at constant memory cost.
    """
    t0, t1 = t_span
    a1 = loss_grad_h1                      # a(t1) = dL/dh(t1)
    g1 = torch.zeros_like(theta)           # dL/dtheta accumulator

    def aug_dynamics(t, s):
        h, a, g = s
        with torch.enable_grad():
            hh = h.detach().requires_grad_(True)
            th = theta.detach().requires_grad_(True)
            fh = f(hh, t, th)
        vjp_h, vjp_t, vjp_th = torch.autograd.grad(
            fh, (hh, t, th), grad_outputs=a, allow_unused=True)
        return (fh, -vjp_h, -vjp_th)       # note: backward in time

    # black-box solver, e.g. Dormand-Prince 'dopri5', integrated t1 -> t0
    h0_r, a0, dL_dtheta = odesolve(aug_dynamics, (h0, a1, g1), (t1, t0))
    return dL_dtheta                        # O(1) memory: no stored trajectory
```

The vector–Jacobian products `aᵀ∂f/∂h` and `aᵀ∂f/∂θ` are computed by reverse-mode autodiff at each solver step, so the method composes with arbitrary architectures for `f` — convolutional, attention-based, or otherwise. The price is *recomputation*: the backward pass re-solves for `h(t)`, roughly doubling NFEs, and on long horizons the backward trajectory can diverge from the forward one if the dynamics are unstable, injecting gradient error [1].

### 4.3 FFJORD and Continuous Normalizing Flows

A continuous normalizing flow defines `z(t₁)` via an ODE from `z(t₀) ∼ p₀`. Because the flow is a diffeomorphism, densities transform by the **instantaneous change of variables** [1]:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\log p(z(t)) = -\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\frac{\partial f(z(t), t, \theta)}{\partial z}\right), \qquad \log p(z(t_1)) = \log p(z(t_0)) - \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \operatorname{tr}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right) dt.$$

The trace replaces the `O(D³)` log-determinant, but computing it exactly still costs `O(D²)` (one VJP per dimension). **FFJORD** (Free-form Jacobian of Reversible Dynamics, Grathwohl et al. [2]) makes the trace *scalable and unbiased* with **Hutchinson's estimator**: for `ε` with `E[ε] = 0`, `Cov(ε) = I` (e.g., Rademacher),

$$\operatorname{tr}(A) = \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\big[\varepsilon^\top A\, \varepsilon\big],$$

which needs only a *single* vector–Jacobian product per evaluation. The density ODE becomes a stochastic-but-unbiased integral, and because the estimator is linear in the trace, gradients of the Monte Carlo objective remain unbiased. The result is a continuous-time generative model with:

| Property | Discrete flows (Glow, Real NVP) | FFJORD [2] |
|---|---|---|
| Architecture constraints | Coupling layers / 1×1 convs | *None* — any network for `f` |
| Likelihood cost | `O(D)` with restricted Jacobians | `O(D)` via Hutchinson trace |
| Density estimate | Exact | Unbiased (stochastic) |
| Sampling | One forward pass | One ODE solve |

FFJORD achieved state-of-the-art likelihoods among exact-likelihood models on tabular benchmarks (POWER, GAS, HEPMASS, MINIBOONE, BSDS300) and competitive bits/dim on MNIST and CIFAR-10, with one-pass sampling — unlike autoregressive models that sample sequentially [2]. The estimator's variance is the central engineering concern: it is controlled with more probe vectors, moving-average baselines, and variance-reduced Hutchinson variants developed in follow-up work.

### 4.4 Neural Stochastic Differential Equations

Deterministic flows cannot express intrinsic randomness in the dynamics. The **neural SDE** [1] generalizes the drift to include a learned diffusion driven by Brownian motion:

$$dZ_t = \mu_\theta(Z_t, t)\,dt + \sigma_\theta(Z_t, t)\,dW_t, \qquad Z_0 \sim p_0.$$

Two complementary training paradigms exist:

**Variational latent SDEs.** Li et al. [1] construct a posterior SDE sharing the diffusion `σ` with a prior SDE but with learned drift, and optimize an ELBO whose KL term is a path integral computable via Girsanov's theorem. Training requires gradients through SDE solvers; the paper's centerpiece is the **stochastic adjoint sensitivity method**: the gradient itself satisfies a *backward SDE*, solvable at `O(1)` memory and `O(L log L)` time, where `L` is the number of solver steps — versus `O(L)` memory for backprop-through-solver and `O(LD)` time for forward pathwise differentiation:

| Method | Memory | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Forward pathwise | `O(1)` | `O(LD)` |
| Backprop through solver | `O(L)` | `O(L)` |
| Stochastic adjoint [1] | `O(1)` | `O(L log L)` |

A memory-efficient noise-caching scheme (a Brownian "virtual" tree with `O(log L)` query) makes the backward SDE practical with adaptive high-order solvers. On a 50-dimensional motion-capture benchmark, latent neural SDEs matched or beat ODE-RNN and latent-ODE baselines [1].

**SDE-GANs.** Kidger et al. [5] observed that classical SDE fitting is a special case of Wasserstein GANs: the generator is the neural SDE driven by Brownian noise, and the discriminator is a **neural controlled differential equation (CDE)** [1] — a continuous-time analog of an RNN that reads the generated path. Because arbitrary drifts and diffusions are admissible and the Wasserstein loss has a unique global minimizer, *any* SDE can be learned in the infinite-data limit [5]. Neural CDEs themselves deserve note: driven by a control path `X(t)` via `dz = f(z)dX`, they resolve the NODE's inability to condition on incoming observations, admit memory-efficient adjoint training across observations, and enjoy universal approximation guarantees [1].

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

The empirical record of neural differential equations spans density estimation, time series, and control:

- **Density estimation.** Continuous normalizing flows train by exact maximum likelihood without partitioning dimensions [1]; FFJORD removes architectural restrictions entirely and attains state-of-the-art likelihoods among tractable models on five tabular datasets, plus competitive image generation with single-pass sampling [2].
- **Irregular time series.** Latent ODEs and ODE-RNNs handle arbitrary observation times natively — a structural advantage over discrete RNNs requiring imputation [1]; neural CDEs achieve state-of-the-art on CharacterTrajectories, Speech Commands, and PhysioNet Sepsis by conditioning continuously on the observation path [1]; latent neural SDEs add calibrated uncertainty on 50-D motion capture [1].
- **Supervised learning.** ODE-Nets match ResNet accuracy on MNIST with far fewer parameters [1]; augmented NODEs improve stability, generalization, and NFE counts on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet relative to vanilla NODEs [1].

The theoretical guarantees are unusually strong for a deep-learning paradigm:

1. *Well-posedness*: Lipschitz vector fields give existence, uniqueness, and continuous dependence on parameters (Picard–Lindelöf), so gradients exist almost everywhere [1].
2. *Unbiased gradients*: the adjoint and stochastic adjoint compute exact (up to solver tolerance) gradients at constant memory [3, 4].
3. *Unbiased densities*: Hutchinson's estimator makes FFJORD's likelihood objective unbiased despite its stochastic trace [2].
4. *Universality*: augmented NODEs escape the topological obstruction [1]; neural CDEs are universal approximators for path functionals [1]; SDE-GANs can learn any SDE in the infinite-data limit [5].

## 6. Limitations

**Numerical fragility.** Adaptive solvers slow dramatically when the learned vector field becomes stiff — a common occurrence, since nothing in the objective penalizes stiffness. NFEs can balloon by orders of magnitude mid-training, and the backward adjoint solve may diverge from the forward trajectory on chaotic or long-horizon dynamics, silently corrupting gradients [1].

**Expressivity constraints.** Vanilla NODE flows are homeomorphisms and cannot represent orientation-reversing or topology-changing maps [1]. Augmentation restores universality but reintroduces architectural hyperparameters (how many extra dimensions?) with limited theory guiding the choice.

**Stochastic overhead.** The stochastic adjoint's `O(L log L)` time and the SDE-GAN's adversarial instability (mode collapse in path space, delicate discriminator regularization) make neural SDEs substantially harder to train than NODEs [4, 5]. Strong convergence of the numerical SDE scheme must be verified per solver, and the Girsanov-based ELBO requires the posterior and prior to share diffusion — a real modeling restriction.

**Speed vs. discrete baselines.** Despite adaptive computation, wall-clock training of NODEs is typically *slower* than an equivalent ResNet: each forward pass invokes dozens to hundreds of vector-field evaluations, and the adjoint doubles that. The memory savings are real but often less decisive on modern hardware than the original papers' framing suggests.

**Evaluation subtleties.** Solver tolerance is a hidden hyperparameter: too loose and gradients are biased; too tight and training crawls. Reproducibility demands reporting tolerances, solver choices, and NFE statistics — practices the field is still standardizing.

## 7. Conclusion

Neural differential equations reframe depth as *time*, layers as *vector fields*, and backpropagation as *optimal control*. From this single shift flow constant-memory training via the adjoint method [1], restriction-free generative modeling via FFJORD's Hutchinson-trace flows [2], topological expressivity analysis and its augmentation remedy [1], and a principled stochastic extension with scalable gradients [1] and adversarial training [5]. The paradigm's deepest contribution may be conceptual: it reunites deep learning with the century-old mathematics of dynamical systems, importing theorems — existence, uniqueness, adjoints, Girsanov — as engineering tools.

The road ahead is numerical as much as architectural: stiffness-robust training, certified solvers, and variance-reduced stochastic estimators will determine whether continuous-depth models remain a beautiful idea or become the default substrate. What is already certain is that the question *"what happens as we add more layers and take smaller steps?"* [1] has permanently enlarged the design space of deep learning.
