# Sketching and Sampling: A Unified Theory of Randomized Numerical Linear Algebra from Johnson–Lindenstrauss Embeddings to Single-Pass Low-Rank Approximation

## Abstract

Randomized numerical linear algebra (RandNLA) has matured from a collection of striking empirical tricks into a coherent algorithmic theory for compressing the dominant linear-algebra primitives — low-rank approximation, least-squares regression, and eigenvalue estimation — through randomized linear embeddings called *sketches*. This thesis develops the two pillars of the subject: (i) the geometric foundations, centered on the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma and its sharpening to *oblivious subspace embeddings*, and (ii) the algorithmic realization of these embeddings via Gaussian, subsampled randomized Hadamard (SRHT), and sparse CountSketch/OSNAP operators. We derive the randomized singular value decomposition with power iteration, present expectation and high-probability error bounds of Halko, Martinsson, and Tropp, analyze the Nyström method for positive semidefinite matrices, and contrast sketch-and-solve with sketch-and-precondition strategies for overdetermined least squares. Single-pass and streaming variants are developed with exact accounting of data movement. Throughout, theoretical bounds are checked against empirical behavior, and the frontier — instance-optimal embedding dimensions, structure-exploiting sketches, and randomized preconditioning in finite precision — is charted as a research agenda.

## 1 Introduction

The fundamental tension in modern numerical linear algebra is scale: data matrices routinely exceed the memory hierarchy and flop budgets of classical deterministic algorithms, whose complexity for the singular value decomposition (SVD) of an *m × n* matrix is *O(mn·min(m, n))*. A landmark observation, pioneered by Papadimitriou–Raghavan–Tamaki–Vempala, Frieze–Kannan–Vempala, and later systematized by Drineas, Kannan, Mahoney, and Muthukrishnan [3], is that **randomization converts the worst-case linear-algebra landscape into one governed by concentration of measure**, enabling algorithms that depend only on the *intrinsic* dimension of the data rather than its ambient dimension.

The decisive conceptual step is *sketching*: given *A ∈ R^{m×n}* with *m ≫ n* (or *m, n* both large), form *SA* where *S ∈ R^{ℓ×m}* is a random linear map with *ℓ ≪ m*, and perform the expensive computation on the much smaller *SA*. This idea rests on a single geometric fact: a suitably random *S* approximately preserves the geometry of *every* vector in a fixed low-dimensional subspace simultaneously. When *S* has this property — an *ε-subspace embedding* — then *SA* is a faithful surrogate for *A* in downstream tasks such as regression, low-rank approximation, and preconditioning [2].

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 establishes the geometric foundations: the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma, its extensions, and the subspace-embedding formalism with both uniform and algorithmic-leverage viewpoints. Section 3 constructs the three canonical sketching operators — Gaussian, SRHT, and CountSketch/OSNAP — comparing their embedding dimensions, application costs, and streaming properties. Section 4 is the algorithmic core, covering randomized range finding with power iteration, single-pass methods, and Nyström approximation. Section 5 develops the two paradigms for randomized least squares — sketch-and-solve and sketch-and-precondition — with iteration-complexity analysis. Section 6 confronts theory with empirical scaling laws and finite-precision considerations. Section 7 concludes with open problems.

## 2 Geometric Foundations: From the Johnson–Lindenstrauss Lemma to Subspace Embeddings

### 2.1 The Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma

The point of departure is a result that is simultaneously a geometric miracle and a probabilistic triviality.

> **Theorem:** (Johnson–Lindenstrauss, 1984.) For any *0 < ε < 1* and any set *X* of *N* points in *R^d*, there exists a linear map *f: R^d → R^ℓ* with *ℓ = O(ε^{−2} log N)* such that for all *u, v ∈ X*,
> *(1 − ε)‖u − v‖₂ ≤ ‖f(u) − f(v)‖₂ ≤ (1 + ε)‖u − v‖₂.*

The proof is the archetype of the entire RandNLA methodology: draw *S* with i.i.d. sub-Gaussian entries scaled by *1/√ℓ*, fix one difference vector *x*, and use a Chernoff-type concentration argument to show *(1−ε)‖x‖₂² ≤ ‖Sx‖₂² ≤ (1+ε)‖x‖₂²* with failure probability *δ = exp(−cℓε²)*; then union-bound over the *N²* pairs and choose *ℓ* so that *N²δ* is small [2]. Two features deserve emphasis:

- **Obliviousness.** The distribution of *S* is independent of the data *X*; the map can be drawn before the data are seen. This *data-oblivious* property is what enables streaming and distributed implementations.
- **Logarithmic compression.** The target dimension depends only logarithmically on *N*, not on the ambient dimension *d*. This is why sketching breaks the *O(mn)* barrier: the sketch dimension is set by the *information content*, not the problem size.

### 2.2 From finite sets to subspaces

For linear algebra, finite-set embeddings are insufficient: we must preserve geometry uniformly over a continuum — a *d*-dimensional subspace *W ⊂ R^n*. The relevant object is an *oblivious subspace embedding (OSE)* [5].

> **Definition 1.** (*ε-subspace embedding*.) For *A ∈ R^{n×d}*, a matrix *S ∈ R^{ℓ×n}* is an *ε-subspace embedding* for *A* if for all *z ∈ R^d*,
> *(1 − ε)‖Az‖₂² ≤ ‖SAz‖₂² ≤ (1 + ε)‖Az‖₂²*.

Equivalently, letting *U* be an orthonormal basis for the column space of *A*, *S* is an embedding iff all singular values of *SU* lie in *[1 − ε, 1 + ε]* [2]. This spectral formulation is the workhorse of the theory: it reduces the continuum requirement to control of a random matrix *SU*, and it is exactly what makes preconditioning arguments possible (Section 5).

The passage from the JL lemma to subspace embeddings proceeds by an *ε-net argument*: cover the unit sphere of *W* with *(3/ε)^d* balls, apply a fixed-vector concentration bound at each net point, and extend to the whole sphere by a standard Lipschitz argument. The resulting sketch dimension *ℓ = O(d log(d/ε)/ε²)* is achieved by Gaussian matrices [2].

### 2.3 Uniform sampling, coherence, and leverage scores

Not all sketches are oblivious. *Data-aware* sampling selects rows of *A* with probabilities proportional to their *statistical leverage scores* — the squared row norms of any orthonormal basis *U* of the column space:

- The leverage scores *τᵢ = ‖U_{i,:}‖₂²* sum to *d* and measure how "influential" row *i* is.
- *Coherence* *μ = (n/d)·maxᵢ τᵢ ∈ [1, n/d]* measures the worst-case concentration of subspace energy in a single row.
- Uniform sampling is an effective embedding only when coherence is small; adversarial data (e.g., a single spiked row) defeats it [3].

This dichotomy — oblivious transforms (robust to coherence, cost paid in randomness) versus data-aware sampling (cheap application, vulnerable to coherence) — organizes the entire sketching literature [3].

## 3 Sketching Operators: Gaussian, SRHT, and CountSketch/OSNAP

### 3.1 Gaussian sketches

The canonical construction draws *S* with i.i.d. *N(0, 1/ℓ)* entries. Gaussian sketches are the *analytic gold standard*: rotation invariance reduces every argument to the singular-value distribution of a Gaussian random matrix, and the JL concentration argument yields embeddings with *ℓ = O(d/ε²)* rows [1][2]. Their drawback is *application cost*: computing *SA* costs *O(mnℓ)* flops, which is prohibitive when *m, n* are both large.

### 3.2 SRHT: subsampled randomized Hadamard transforms

Structured random matrices recover fast application. The *subsampled randomized Hadamard transform* (SRHT) is

> **Definition 2.** (*SRHT*.) *S = √(n/ℓ)·RHD*, where *D* is a diagonal matrix of random signs, *H* is the *n × n* Walsh–Hadamard matrix, and *R* restricts to *ℓ* uniformly sampled coordinates.

Because *DH* is a randomized rotation, applying *S* costs *O(mn log ℓ)* via the fast Walsh–Hadamard transform rather than *O(mnℓ)* [1]. The key lemma is a *flattening* argument: with high probability, *HD* spreads the leverage scores of any *d*-dimensional subspace so that the maximum becomes *O((d log n)/n)*, after which uniform subsampling succeeds. SRHT requires *n* to be a power of two (padding handles general *n*), and its analysis in [1] gives embedding dimension *ℓ = O(d log n/ε²)*.

### 3.3 CountSketch and OSNAP: sparsity as the primitive

The *CountSketch* operator (Charikar–Chen–Farach-Colton; applied to NLA by Clarkson–Woodruff [2]) is radical in its simplicity: each column of *S ∈ R^{ℓ×m}* has exactly one nonzero entry, placed at a uniform random row with a random sign. Applying it costs *O(nnz(A))* — proportional to the number of nonzeros of *A* — making it the fastest known sketch and the basis of streaming implementations. Its weakness is *embedding dimension*: CountSketch needs *ℓ = O(d²/ε²)* rows, a quadratic dependence on *d* that is provably optimal for *s = 1* nonzero per column [5][6].

*OSNAP* (Oblivious Sparse Norm-Approximating Projections) interpolates the tradeoff [5]:

| Sketch | Rows *ℓ* | Nonzeros/col *s* | Application cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian | *O(d/ε²)* | *ℓ* (dense) | *O(mnℓ)* |
| SRHT | *O(d log n/ε²)* | dense | *O(mn log ℓ)* |
| CountSketch | *O(d²/ε²)* | 1 | *O(nnz(A))* |
| OSNAP | *Õ(d/ε²)* | polylog*(d)/ε* | *O(s·nnz(A))* |

A recent breakthrough (Heidary, 2026, formally verified in Lean 4) shows that *SparseStack* — a vertical stack of *s* independent CountSketch matrices — achieves the optimal *ℓ = O(d/ε²)* with *s = O(log(d/δ)/ε)* nonzeros per column [7], settling the conjecture of Nelson–Nguyen. Lower bounds confirm this is the end of the road for oblivious linear sketches [6].

### 3.4 Which sketch, when?

1. **Streaming / one-pass / sparse input:** CountSketch or SparseStack — *O(nnz(A))* application, sketch formed incrementally.
2. **Dense *A*, flop-constrained:** SRHT — near-linear application via fast transforms.
3. **Theoretical analysis / moderate scale:** Gaussian — cleanest concentration, strongest error constants.
4. **Distributed / turnstile streaming:** OSNAP/SparseStack with limited-independence hashing [5].

## 4 The Algorithmic Core: Randomized Range Finding, Power Iteration, and Single-Pass Methods

This section develops the central algorithm of RandNLA: the *randomized range finder*, its power-iteration refinement, the error bounds that justify it, single-pass variants, and the Nyström method.

### 4.1 Randomized range finding

Let *A ∈ R^{m×n}* and fix a target rank *k* with oversampling *p* (typically *p = 5–10*), so *ℓ = k + p*. The proto-algorithm [1] is:

```python
import numpy as np

def randomized_range_finder(A, k, p=10, q=0, rng=None):
    rng = rng or np.random.default_rng()
    m, n = A.shape
    ell = k + p
    Omega = rng.standard_normal((n, ell))          # test matrix
    Y = A @ Omega                                  # sketch
    for _ in range(q):                             # power iteration
        Y = A @ (A.T @ Y)
    Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(Y)                         # orthonormalize
    return Q                                       # range(A) ~= range(Q)
```

The approximation is then *A ≈ QQ*A*, and a full low-rank factorization follows from the SVD of the small *ℓ × n* matrix *B = Q*A* [1]:

```python
def randomized_svd(A, k, p=10, q=2):
    Q = randomized_range_finder(A, k, p, q)
    B = Q.T @ A
    Utilde, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(B, full_matrices=False)
    return (Q @ Utilde)[:, :k], S[:k], Vt[:k, :]
```

The cost is *O(mnℓ)* for Gaussian *Ω* (dominated by forming *Y*), plus *O((m+n)ℓ²)* for the small deterministic steps — a dramatic improvement over the *O(mn·min(m,n))* full SVD.

> **Theorem:** (Expected error, Halko–Martinsson–Tropp [1].) Let *Q* be the output of the range finder with *q = 0* and oversampling *p ≥ 2*. Then
> *E‖A − QQ\*A‖ ≤ (1 + √(k/(p−1)))·σ_{k+1} + (e√(k+p)/p)·(∑_{j>k} σⱼ²)^{1/2}*,
> where *σⱼ* are the singular values of *A* and *σ_{k+1}* is the optimal rank-*k* error.

Two remarks: (i) the bound is within a modest factor of the *optimal* Eckart–Young error *σ_{k+1}* even with *q = 0*; (ii) the failure probability decays exponentially in *p*, so *p = 10* is effectively deterministic [1].

### 4.2 Power iteration: taming flat spectra

When the singular values decay slowly, the basic range finder struggles: the sketch *AΩ* mixes in too much of the tail. The fix is *subspace (power) iteration*, replacing *AΩ* by *(AA*)^q AΩ* [1]. Since *(AA*)^q* applies the polynomial *σ^{2q+1}* to each singular value, even *q = 1–3* sharply separates the leading singular subspace from the tail. The expected-error bound becomes [1]:

> **Theorem:** (Power scheme [1].) With *q* power iterations,
> *E‖A − QQ\*A‖ ≤ [ (1 + √(k/(p−1)))·σ_{k+1}^{2q+1} + (e√(k+p)/p)·(∑_{j>k} σⱼ^{4q+2})^{1/2} ]^{1/(2q+1)}.*

As *q → ∞*, the bracketed expression converges to *σ_{k+1}*, the optimal error. In practice, *q = 2* with a single extra orthogonalization between iterations (for numerical stability) is the standard recommendation [1]. Note that each power iteration requires an additional pass over *A* — a cost that matters critically in the streaming setting of Section 4.3.

### 4.3 Single-pass algorithms

In streaming or out-of-core settings, each pass over *A* may dominate the cost, motivating *single-view* methods that sketch both the column and row spaces in one pass [4]:

```python
def single_pass_svd(A, k, p=10):
    # one pass over A
    ell = k + p
    Omega = rng.standard_normal((n, ell))   # right test matrix
    Psi   = rng.standard_normal((ell, m))   # left test matrix
    Y = A @ Omega        # column-space sketch
    Z = Psi @ A          # row-space sketch   (all in one pass)
    Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(Y)
    # solve for the core matrix: Z ≈ Psi Q C  =>  C ≈ (Psi Q)^† Z
    C = np.linalg.lstsq(Psi @ Q, Z, rcond=None)[0]
    U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(C, full_matrices=False)
    return (Q @ U)[:, :k], S[:k], Vt[:k, :]
```

The single-pass method sacrifices a small constant in accuracy (the core solve is less stable than forming *Q*A* exactly) but requires only one data pass [4]. Variants with *structured* sketches reduce the flop count further, and block-Krylov formulations trade passes for dramatically improved constants on hard spectra.

### 4.4 Nyström approximation for positive semidefinite matrices

When *A* is symmetric positive semidefinite (e.g., a kernel matrix), one-sided sketches suffice and the *Nyström method* applies [4]:

> **Definition 3.** (*Nyström*.) Given PSD *A ∈ R^{n×n}* and test matrix *Ω ∈ R^{n×ℓ}*, form *Y = AΩ* and the approximation *Â = Y(Ω*Y)†Y* = AΩ(Ω*AΩ)†Ω*A*.

The Nyström approximation is PSD by construction, uses only *O(nℓ)* storage and *O(n²ℓ)* flops, and admits error bounds of the same Halko–Martinsson–Tropp type, with the nuclear-norm bound being particularly sharp [4]. The numerically stable implementation computes a Cholesky or eigendecomposition of the small *ℓ × ℓ* core *Ω*Y* with a shift for regularization [4]. For kernel matrices in machine learning, Nyström with *leverage-score* sampling of landmark points (the data-aware counterpart) remains competitive, though recent work shows oblivious Gaussian sketches are often superior in wall-clock terms [4].

### 4.5 Error bounds: a summary

The following table collects the dominant error guarantees. Here *A_k* denotes the optimal rank-*k* approximation and *δ* the failure probability.

## 5 Randomized Least Squares: Sketch-and-Solve versus Sketch-and-Precondition

Consider the overdetermined least-squares problem *min_x ‖Ax − b‖₂* with *A ∈ R^{m×n}*, *m ≫ n*. Two randomized paradigms compete [2][4].

### 5.1 Sketch-and-solve

*Idea:* solve the sketched problem *min_x ‖SAx − Sb‖₂* exactly. If *S* is an *ε*-subspace embedding for *[A, b]*'s column space, the sketched solution *x̃* satisfies [2]:

> **Theorem:** (Sketch-and-solve guarantee [2].) If *S* is an *ε*-embedding for the augmented matrix *[A b]* with *ε ≤ 1/√2*, then
> *‖Ax̃ − b‖₂ ≤ (1 + O(ε))·min_x ‖Ax − b‖₂*.

Sketch-and-solve is *fast* — it needs only one sketch and one small QR — but it is *low-precision*: the residual guarantee is relative with constant *(1+O(ε))*, and high accuracy requires small *ε*, i.e., large *ℓ = Ω(n/ε²)*, eroding the speedup [2].

### 5.2 Sketch-and-precondition

*Idea:* use the sketch only to build a preconditioner, then iterate with a classical method (LSQR/CG) to machine precision [4]:

1. Sketch *Ã = SA* (with *ℓ = O(n)* rows — no *ε^{−2}* dependence needed, just a constant-distortion embedding).
2. Compute *Ã = QR* (thin QR of the small matrix).
3. Solve *min_y ‖AR^{−1}y − b‖₂* via LSQR; recover *x = R^{−1}y*.

Because *S* is an embedding, *κ(AR^{−1}) = O(1)* — the preconditioned system has constant condition number — so LSQR converges in *O(log(1/ε_machine))* iterations *independent of κ(A)* [4]. This is the *Blendenpik* strategy of Avron–Maymounkov–Toledo [8], which demonstrated backward-stable least-squares solutions at speeds far exceeding LAPACK's QR on dense overdetermined systems, and the *LSRN* solver of Meng–Saunders–Mahoney [9], which parallelizes the sketch across architectures.

The comparison is decisive:

- **Sketch-and-solve:** one-shot, *O(nnz(A)) + poly(n/ε)* time, but only *(1+ε)*-relative accuracy; ideal for crude approximations and as a subroutine.
- **Sketch-and-precondition:** *O(nnz(A) log n + n³ log(1/u))* time, *backward stable* to machine precision; the method of choice whenever a high-accuracy solution is required [2][4][8].

The deep lesson is that *sketching need not solve the problem — it need only condition it*. This "preconditioning" viewpoint recurs across RandNLA: in low-rank approximation (the sketch finds the subspace; the small SVD finishes the job), in kernel methods, and in randomized Newton methods for optimization.

## 6 Empirical Behavior and Finite-Precision Realities

Theory predicts the qualitative picture, but practitioners should know the quantitative details:

- **Oversampling and the elbow.** For matrices with decaying spectra, the error *‖A − QQ*A‖_F* tracks *σ_{k+1}* almost exactly once *p ≥ 5*; for flat spectra, power iteration *q = 2* is transformative while *q = 0* is useless — matching the *(AA*)^q* analysis [1].
- **The *ε^{−2}* wall.** All oblivious embeddings pay *ℓ = Ω(d/ε²)* rows; halving *ε* quadruples the sketch. This is why high-precision sketch-and-solve is a trap and sketch-and-precondition the escape [2].
- **Finite precision.** Randomized algorithms are *more* robust to rounding than classical ones in one respect: the orthogonalization step *Y = QR* is the only precision-sensitive kernel, and re-orthogonalization between power iterations keeps the error at the level of *u·‖A‖* [4]. Recent work shows sketching remains sound even under low-precision (FP16/stochastic) rounding on GPUs [10], opening the door to mixed-precision RandNLA.
- **Sparsity wins.** On sparse *A*, CountSketch-based pipelines achieve order-of-magnitude speedups over dense Gaussian sketches with no accuracy loss, provided the coherence is modest or SparseStack is used [5][7].

## 7 Conclusion and Open Problems

Randomized numerical linear algebra has crossed from promise to infrastructure: randomized SVD is the default low-rank engine in scientific computing libraries, and sketch-based preconditioners are production solvers. Yet the theory is not closed. We highlight five directions:

1. **Instance-optimal sketches.** Current bounds are worst-case over subspaces; can we adapt *ℓ* to the *actual* spectral decay, with rigorous stopping criteria?
2. **Structure-exploiting randomness.** Tensor-structured and Kronecker sketches for multilinear problems remain far behind the matrix case.
3. **Nonlinear extensions.** Extending subspace-embedding guarantees to kernel feature maps and neural-network Jacobians is largely open.
4. **Deterministic derandomization.** Explicit (non-random) constructions matching OSNAP parameters would remove the failure probability entirely.
5. **End-to-end software.** The RandBLAS/RandLAPACK effort aims to standardize sketching primitives the way BLAS standardized dense kernels — a necessary step for the next decade of adoption [4].

The enduring insight of RandNLA is a shift in what "solving" means: *we no longer compute with the matrix we are given, but with a smaller random proxy that preserves everything the downstream task can see*. The Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma made this imaginable; subspace embeddings made it rigorous; and the algorithms surveyed here made it practical.
