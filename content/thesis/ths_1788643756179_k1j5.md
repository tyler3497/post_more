# Tokamak Disruption Mitigation: Runaway Electron Avalanches, Shattered Pellet Injection, and MHD Stability Limits in ITER-Scale Plasmas

## Abstract

Plasma disruptions are the most hazardous off-normal event for ITER: at full performance (15 MA, >300 MJ thermal and >1 GJ magnetic stored energy) a disruption unfolds in milliseconds. The thermal quench ejects plasma energy onto plasma-facing components, the current quench drives extreme electromagnetic forces via eddy and halo currents, and the large inductive electric field can convert much of the plasma current into a relativistic runaway electron beam that melts the first wall on localized impact. This thesis treats tokamak disruption mitigation with emphasis on runaway electron avalanche physics, shattered pellet injection (SPI) as the ITER baseline technology, and MHD stability limits bounding mitigation effectiveness. We derive the Rosenbluth–Putvinski avalanche growth rate with its Hesslow extension to partially ionized plasmas, analyze Dreicer and hot-tail seed mechanisms, describe the ITER DMS architecture, review multi-machine SPI experiments and JOREK 3D MHD modeling, and quantify the current-quench, radiation-asymmetry, and radiation-fraction constraints every scenario must satisfy. We conclude that while SPI substantially reduces thermal and electromagnetic loads, complete runaway avoidance at ITER scale remains unresolved, requiring staged injection, optimized fragmentation, and validated nonlinear MHD models.

## 1 Introduction

The tokamak approach to magnetic confinement fusion achieves scientific viability only if the plasma can be confined stably for durations relevant to power production. At reactor scale, however, the plasma is susceptible to **disruptions**: sudden, global losses of confinement in which the stored thermal energy is exhausted in milliseconds (the *thermal quench*, TQ) and the plasma current subsequently decays (the *current quench*, CQ). On ITER they represent a credible threat to machine integrity, via a threefold damage mechanism: (1) conducted and radiated thermal loads from the TQ can melt or crack plasma-facing components (PFCs); (2) the rapid current decay induces eddy currents and drives *halo currents* in vessel structures, producing electromagnetic forces that can deform in-vessel components; and (3) the large toroidal electric field during the CQ can accelerate electrons to relativistic energies, forming a *runaway electron* (RE) beam that deposits megajoules in a millimeter-scale footprint, causing localized melting [1][2].

The ITER Organization, together with the ITER Disruption Mitigation System (DMS) task force, has elevated disruption mitigation to a plant-level design requirement: every ITER disruption must be mitigated, with targets on current quench time (~50–150 ms), conducted heat load fraction (below ~10% of thermal energy, under ~20 MJ conducted [3]), and suppression of the runaway electron beam [2]. The baseline technology is **shattered pellet injection (SPI)**: cryogenically frozen pellets of deuterium, neon, or mixtures are accelerated to hundreds of meters per second and shattered against a miter bend, producing high-velocity shards that penetrate and cool the plasma, radiate its energy, and raise electron density to suppress runaway generation [4][5].

This thesis develops the physics chain that makes disruption mitigation at ITER scale simultaneously *necessary* and *extraordinarily difficult*: the runaway avalanche amplifying a tiny seed by tens of orders of magnitude [6]; the SPI hardware delivering impurity mass on the millisecond timescale [7]; and the MHD stability limits — tearing modes, kink modes, flux-surface stochastization — that both enable mitigation and frustrate it [8].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Anatomy of a tokamak disruption

A major disruption typically proceeds through three phases. First, a **pre-disruption phase** in which an MHD instability — most commonly a locked *m = 2 / n = 1* tearing mode — grows, or a vertical displacement event (VDE) drives the plasma into the wall. Second, the **thermal quench**: flux surfaces become globally stochastic and parallel heat transport dumps core thermal energy to the edge in ~1 ms on present machines (6–10 ms predicted for ITER) [3]. Third, the **current quench**: the cold, resistive post-TQ plasma decays with an *L/R* time set by cold resistivity and wall time.

> **Theorem:** For a post-thermal-quench plasma of electron temperature $T_e$ and effective charge $Z_{eff}$, the current quench time scales as $\tau_{CQ} \propto T_e^{3/2} / (Z_{eff} \ln\Lambda)$; ITER requires $50~\mathrm{ms} \lesssim \tau_{CQ} \lesssim 150~\mathrm{ms}$ to simultaneously limit halo-current forces and vessel eddy-current loading [2].

The bounds on the current quench time encode a genuine physical tension: a *fast* current quench produces large $dI_p/dt$, driving destructive eddy currents and halo currents (with toroidal peaking factors that can concentrate forces), while a *slow* current quench prolongs the period of high toroidal electric field $E_\parallel$, maximizing the window for runaway electron generation. Mitigation must therefore thread a narrow corridor.

### 2.2 Runaway electrons: from seed to beam

An electron *runs away* when the accelerating force from a parallel electric field exceeds collisional drag. The **Dreicer field**

$$E_D = \frac{n_e e^3 \ln\Lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0^2 m_e c^2}$$

sets the scale: for $E_\parallel > E_D$ the whole distribution runs away; in the relevant regime $E_\parallel \ll E_D$, only the high-energy tail overcomes friction. The Connor–Hastie **critical field** $E_c$ is the threshold above which a test electron of arbitrary energy gains energy indefinitely. Two primary seed mechanisms matter [6][9]:

- **Dreicer generation**: velocity-space diffusion of thermal electrons across the runaway separatrix during the high-$E_\parallel$ CQ phase; the rate is exponentially sensitive to $E_\parallel / E_D$.
- **Hot-tail generation**: during the TQ the bulk cools in ~1 ms, but suprathermal electrons have slowing-down times ($\tau_s \propto v^3$) exceeding the cooling time, so a non-Maxwellian hot tail survives and converts directly into runaways as $E_\parallel$ rises. In ITER-scale modeling this is the dominant primary mechanism [9][10].

Secondary generation — the **runaway avalanche** — is the mechanism that elevates runaways from a curiosity to an existential threat. A relativistic runaway electron undergoing a close Coulomb collision with a thermal electron can knock the latter into the runaway region (Møller scattering). Rosenbluth and Putvinski [11] showed that this produces *exponential* growth of the runaway population:

$$\frac{dn_{RE}}{dt} = \frac{n_{RE}}{\tau_{av}}, \qquad \tau_{av}^{-1} \approx \frac{E_\parallel / E_c - 1}{\tau_{rel} \ln\Lambda}\sqrt{\frac{\pi \varphi}{3(Z_{eff}+5)}}$$

where $\tau_{rel}$ is the relativistic collision time and $\varphi$ a geometric factor of order unity. The avalanche multiplication over a CQ is $\exp(\int dt/\tau_{av})$, reaching $10^{10}$–$10^{35}$ for ITER: even a single-electron seed can, in principle, convert the entire plasma current into a runaway beam. Crucially, Hesslow *et al.* [6] showed that in **partially ionized** plasmas — exactly the state produced by impurity-injection mitigation — bound electrons of incompletely stripped neon/argon add knock-on targets while screening reduces friction only partially. The avalanche growth rate in weakly ionized plasmas can thus be *tens of orders of magnitude larger* than fully-ionized estimates: impurity injection raises $E_c$ through density while simultaneously supercharging the avalanche it seeks to suppress. This paradox is at the heart of ITER DMS design.

### 2.3 Massive material injection and shattered pellet injection

Two delivery technologies dominate: **massive gas injection (MGI)** — a fast valve releasing ~$10^{23}$–$10^{24}$ noble-gas atoms at the edge, with limited penetration — and **shattered pellet injection (SPI)** [4][5]. SPI delivers solid cryogenic shards at 100–600 m/s that penetrate to mid-radius or deeper before ablating, giving faster, more uniform assimilation. ITER baseline pellets are 28.5 mm in diameter (L/D ~2), desublimated in a pipe-gun barrel, dislodged by a mechanical punch or propellant, characterized in a microwave cavity, and shattered on an S-bend (20° impact angle in the JET prototype) [4][7].

Species selection encodes the mitigation trade-offs:

| Species / mixture | Primary function | Key trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Pure D₂ | Dilution, density rise, RE avoidance (no immediate TQ) | Slow TQ, weak radiation, long pre-TQ |
| Ne/D₂ mixture | Radiative TQ, thermal load mitigation | Accelerates CQ; hot-tail/avalanche seed risk |
| Ar/D₂ mixture | Strong radiation, RE dissipation | High-Z; CQ too fast if overdosed |
| H₂/Ne (ITER baseline) | Balanced TQ + CQ control | Requires precise dosing and 2-stage timing |

*Table 1: SPI species and their mitigation roles. The central design tension: radiating impurities tame thermal loads but shorten the current quench and can amplify the avalanche; pure deuterium avoids runaways but radiates poorly.*

### 2.4 MHD stability limits

Mitigation unfolds inside a violently MHD-unstable plasma, and the MHD response both enables and limits mitigation [8]:

1. **Tearing modes** ($m/n = 2/1$ dominant) grow as the current profile contracts during edge cooling; island overlap (Chirikov criterion) stochastizes flux surfaces and the TQ ensues.
2. **External kink modes** ($n = 1$) driven by current profile and wall proximity produce asymmetric halo currents whose toroidal peaking damages the vessel.
3. **Stochastization** during the TQ is double-edged: it spreads conducted heat and *deconfines* runaway seeds (aiding RE avoidance), but if a beam has already formed in the re-healed core, stochastic fields drive its rapid localized loss — the *benign vs. malign termination* problem.

JOREK 3D nonlinear MHD simulations of D₂ SPI into JET H-mode plasmas show pure-deuterium shards cooling the edge without immediate global current-density contraction, delaying the TQ by ~9 ms (vs. 1–2 ms for impurity pellets) [8]. For ITER, JOREK and INDEX modeling predicts 6–10 ms TQ durations with slow cold-front propagation — a direct consequence of machine size — reshaping pellet requirements [3].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in four stages: (i) analytic runaway generation rates from the relativistic Fokker–Planck equation with a Hesslow-type partial-screening collision operator; (ii) zero-dimensional CQ modeling coupling the inductive field $E_\parallel(t)$ to the avalanche growth rate; (iii) review of ITER DMS hardware and fragment size/velocity distributions from shatter-test campaigns and SPI experiments on DIII-D, JET, KSTAR, and AUG [4][7]; and (iv) synthesis of 3D nonlinear MHD (JOREK) and integrated disruption modeling (DREAM) results to bound ITER-scale performance [8][9][10].

A minimal avalanche model is instructive. Consider the coupled system for ohmic current $I_\Omega$ and runaway current $I_{RE}$:

```python
import numpy as np

def avalanche_cq(E_over_Ec, t, n0=1.0, lnL=15.0, tau_rel=2e-3, Zeff=2.0):
    """
    Integrate Rosenbluth-Putvinski avalanche growth during a current quench.
    E_over_Ec : array of E_parallel/E_crit vs time
    tau_rel   : relativistic collision time [s]
    Returns runaway density amplification factor.
    """
    phi = 1.0
    gamma = (E_over_Ec - 1.0) / (tau_rel * lnL) * np.sqrt(np.pi * phi / (3.0 * (Zeff + 5.0)))
    gamma = np.maximum(gamma, 0.0)          # growth only when E > E_c
    dt = np.gradient(t)
    exponent = np.sum(gamma * dt)
    return np.exp(exponent)                 # multiplication factor

t = np.linspace(0, 0.1, 10001)              # 100 ms current quench
E = 30.0 * np.exp(-t / 0.03)                # E/E_c decaying after TQ
print(f"Avalanche multiplication: {avalanche_cq(E, t):.2e}")
```

For ITER-relevant $E_\parallel/E_c \sim 20$–$50$ sustained over tens of milliseconds, this model yields multiplication factors of $10^{15}$–$10^{35}$ — the *seed-insensitive regime*, where the final runaway current is set by available poloidal flux, not seed magnitude [9][10]. The Hesslow correction multiplies the integrand by a partial-ionization factor that, for mitigation-relevant neon densities ($\sim 10^{20}$ m$^{-3}$), increases the exponent by tens of orders of magnitude over fully ionized estimates [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Dreicer–hot-tail–avalanche chain in ITER conditions

The three generation mechanisms operate in sequence and must be evaluated jointly. During the TQ ($t_{TQ} \sim 1$ ms on JET, 6–10 ms predicted for ITER), the bulk cools from ~10 keV to tens of eV. Electrons above a few keV have slowing-down times exceeding $t_{TQ}$ and form the **hot tail**; as flux surfaces stochastize, part of the tail is lost, but the remainder becomes the runaway seed when $E_\parallel$ rises during the CQ [9][10]. Dreicer generation adds seeds continuously during the CQ, and the avalanche multiplies the combined seed.

> **Lemma (seed insensitivity):** If $\int \gamma_{av}\,dt \gtrsim 25$ (multiplication $\gtrsim 10^{10}$), the final runaway current depends only logarithmically on the seed; mitigation must therefore reduce the *avalanche exponent itself* (via density, $E_\parallel$ control, or RE transport losses), not merely the seed.

DREAM simulations of unmitigated ITER-scale disruptions confirm the seed-insensitive regime: hot-tail seeds of wildly different magnitudes converge to multi-MA runaway beams, and only extreme density increases ($n_e \gtrsim 10^{22}$ m$^{-3}$, far above SPI-deliverable assimilation) push $E_\parallel/E_c$ below unity [9][10].

### 4.2 Shattered pellet injection: from pipe gun to fragment plume

The ITER DMS reference design comprises 27 injectors (24 upper-port, 3 equatorial-port) for toroidal coverage and redundancy. Each forms a cylindrical pellet by desublimating low-pressure gas (<100 mbar) onto a cryogenic cold zone, dislodges it with a mechanical punch or fast gas valve, accelerates it to 200–600 m/s, and characterizes pellet integrity, mass, and velocity in a microwave cavity before it strikes the shattering bend [4][7].

Fragmentation physics governs everything downstream. Fraunhofer EMI shatter tests show broad, approximately log-normal fragment size distributions, with mean fragment size decreasing at higher impact velocity and steeper impact angle; small fragments ablate fast and deposit at the edge, while surviving large fragments penetrate deeper [12]. The *assimilation fraction* and *deposition profile* jointly determine whether the TQ triggers promptly at the edge or after deep penetration. Multi-machine analysis shows energy-loss duration increasing with machine size and scaling with the injected-neon-to-stored-energy ratio — a scaling law directly informing ITER pellet sizing [3].

### 4.3 Two-stage injection: diluting before radiating

The Hesslow paradox — impurities that mitigate the TQ amplify the avalanche — motivates **two-stage SPI**: a first pure-deuterium pellet dilutes the plasma (raising $n_e$ and hence $E_c$, cooling without a prompt TQ), followed by a mixed Ne/D₂ or Ar/D₂ pellet that radiates thermal energy once density is high enough to keep $E_\parallel/E_c$ below the avalanche threshold [8][10]. JOREK simulations of D₂ SPI in JET discharge #96874 validate the first stage: the ~9 ms pre-TQ window lets the current density evolve slowly, avoiding the abrupt $j$-profile contraction of impurity pellets [8]. DREAM modeling of two-stage schemes for STEP (>20 MA, even more avalanche-prone than ITER) shows hot-tail seed reduction cutting the final RE current from ~16 MA to ~13 MA — an improvement, but not avoidance [10]. Two-stage injection is therefore *necessary but insufficient* alone; it must combine with optimized fragment distributions and active RE loss mechanisms (passive 3D coils, benign termination via controlled MHD).

### 4.4 Radiation asymmetry and electromagnetic load control

Mitigation is not only about total energy but about *where and how* it goes. JET SPI experiments measured toroidal and poloidal peaking factors (TPF, PPF) of radiated power during mitigated disruptions: strongly asymmetric radiation (TPF > 2) concentrates megajoules onto limited wall areas, threatening stainless-steel port plugs even where the first wall is tungsten [13]. Experiments with error-field correction coils imposing $n = 1$ fields showed the radiation pattern can be deliberately manipulated — directly relevant to ITER, where ~80% of thermal energy is expected to be lost during the TQ [13].

Electromagnetic loads are controlled through the CQ window: SPI dosing is tuned so cold-plasma resistivity yields $\tau_{CQ}$ in the 50–150 ms band, keeping halo-current fractions and eddy-current forces within vessel limits [2]. Over-injection of high-Z material shortens $\tau_{CQ}$ below the floor (excessive forces); under-injection leaves it too long (maximal avalanche). The acceptable dosing window narrows with machine size.

### 4.5 MHD stability limits on mitigation itself

MHD imposes hard limits on mitigation itself. The TQ *requires* island overlap and stochastization — but the same stochastic fields deconfine runaway seeds, so the promptness of the TQ (set by fragment deposition and $q = 2$ surface dynamics) controls the surviving seed [8]. Conversely, once a beam forms, its termination is governed by flux-surface re-healing and kink/tearing growth in the beam itself; NIMROD simulations of dispersive shell pellets show an inside-out TQ can leave edge flux surfaces surprisingly robust, in which case runaway losses are *not* expected — thermal mitigation succeeds but runaways are not deconfined [14]. Post-mitigation MHD stability is therefore an active design variable, not a passive background.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

We consolidate the quantitative evidence base:

1. **Avalanche amplification (theory):** Rosenbluth–Putvinski growth rates integrated over ITER CQ parameters give multiplication factors $10^{10}$–$10^{35}$ [11]; Hesslow *et al.* show partial ionization adds tens of orders of magnitude for mitigation-relevant impurity densities [6].
2. **SPI thermal mitigation (experiment):** JET SPI campaigns (three-barrel system, 4.6–12.5 mm pellets, H/D/Ne/Ar) demonstrated thermal load mitigation, CQ control, and RE dissipation/avoidance across a wide parameter range; radiation asymmetry characterized via dual bolometer arrays [7][13].
3. **Scaling with machine size (multi-device):** Energy-loss duration (TQ proxy) increases with machine size across J-TEXT, KSTAR, AUG, DIII-D, and JET, and scales with injected-neon-to-stored-energy ratio — supporting 6–10 ms TQ predictions for ITER [3].
4. **D₂ SPI pre-TQ physics (simulation vs. experiment):** JOREK reproduces the ~9 ms cooling phase of JET #96874 D₂ SPI and the absence of global $j$-contraction, validating the two-stage concept's first step [8].
5. **RE avoidance limits (integrated modeling):** DREAM scans for STEP find no Ar/D₂ mixture avoiding runaways within CQ/radiation constraints; two-stage SPI reduces but does not eliminate the beam (13 MA residual) [10].
6. **Fragment distributions (experiment + simulation):** Fraunhofer EMI shatter tests plus numerical fragmentation models provide realistic size/velocity distributions as inputs to MHD modeling — a prerequisite for credible ITER predictions [12].

> **Proposition:** Under seed-insensitive avalanche conditions, the set of simultaneously achievable mitigation targets (conducted fraction < 10%, $50 < \tau_{CQ} < 150$ ms, RE current < 150 kA equivalent) is empty for single-stage impurity SPI at ITER scale; it becomes non-empty only with staged injection plus engineered RE transport losses. *Status: supported by [6][8][10], not yet demonstrated experimentally at scale.*

## 6 Limitations, Open Problems, and Future Work

- **The Hesslow paradox is unresolved.** No validated scheme simultaneously achieves prompt radiative TQ and avalanche suppression at ITER scale; required densities ($\gtrsim 10^{22}$ m$^{-3}$) exceed demonstrated SPI assimilation [6][10].
- **Fragment distribution uncertainty.** ITER predictions inherit the full uncertainty of shatter-test extrapolations to 28.5 mm pellets at 200–600 m/s; assimilation fractions remain model-dependent [12].
- **Radiation asymmetry at ITER energy density.** JET TPF/PPF scalings may not extrapolate to ITER's 350 MJ thermal energy; port-plug survival is unproven [13].
- **Runaway beam termination.** Benign termination via controlled MHD and passive 3D coil concepts are pre-design; the physics of RE–MHD interaction in the re-healed core is poorly validated [10][14].
- **MHD model validation.** JOREK/NIMROD/DREAM predictions for ITER TQ duration (6–10 ms) and cold-front propagation await experimental confirmation; the multi-machine database is the primary validation vehicle [3][8].
- **DMS reliability.** The ITER DMS must fire reliably on every disruption precursor with millisecond latency; 27-injector redundancy, pellet formation reliability in a radiation environment, and false-trigger consequences are engineering risks beyond plasma physics [4].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Tokamak disruption mitigation at ITER scale is a problem of *simultaneous constraints*: the TQ must be radiated symmetrically, the CQ must decay neither too fast nor too slow, and the runaway avalanche — amplified by tens of orders of magnitude in the partially ionized plasmas mitigation creates — must be suppressed below wall-damage thresholds. Shattered pellet injection, the ITER DMS baseline, demonstrably addresses the first two constraints across multiple devices, supported by increasingly validated 3D MHD modeling [3][4][7][8][13]. The third, runaway avoidance, remains the outstanding grand challenge: theory [6][11], integrated modeling [9][10], and the seed-insensitive avalanche all indicate single-stage impurity injection cannot suffice, and that two-stage schemes, optimized fragmentation, and engineered runaway transport losses must be developed in concert. The path to a disruption-tolerant ITER runs through the Hesslow paradox — and its resolution will determine whether fusion power plants can survive their own worst milliseconds.
