# Refinement-Machine Consensus: Specifying Paxos and Raft in TLA+, Model Checking with TLC, and the IronFleet–Verdi Verified-Systems Lineage

## Abstract

This thesis develops a rigorous treatment of the formal verification of distributed consensus, centered on the specification of **Paxos** and **Raft** in **TLA+**, exhaustive model checking with the **TLC** model checker, refinement-based proof methodology, and the machine-checked systems lineage descending from these ideas: **IronFleet** and **Verdi**. We formalize single-decree Paxos and a faithful Raft core as TLA+ specifications, state their safety invariants as temporal properties, and validate them with TLC over bounded instances that reproduce historically significant bugs, including the Raft log-commit ordering subtlety. We develop the refinement-mapping discipline connecting an abstract consensus specification to concrete protocol steps, show how it scales to full program verification in IronFleet's blend of TLA-style refinement with Hoare logic, and describe Verdi's Coq-based composition of verified system transformers. A model-checking campaign quantifies the reach and limits of exhaustive search, and we close by assessing timing assumptions, proof effort, and the trusted computing base.

## 1 Introduction

Distributed consensus is among the most studied and most frequently misimplemented problems in computing: processes communicating only by messages must agree on a single value despite crash failures. Its difficulty is foundational - no deterministic algorithm solves consensus in a fully asynchronous system with even one crash failure, so every practical protocol adds timing assumptions or randomization. Yet consensus underlies replicated state machines, the basis of nearly every strongly consistent distributed service, from configuration stores to transaction logs. A subtle bug here does not corrupt one computation; it silently forks the system's entire notion of truth.

The canonical solution is **Paxos**: two phases of message exchange centered on *quorums*, where any two quorums intersect and intersection forces information about already-chosen values to propagate to any future proposer [1]. The protocol is elegant, minimal, and notoriously difficult to understand - a crisis that motivated **Raft**, which decomposes consensus into leader election, log replication, and safety, enforcing a strong-leader discipline that shrinks the state space a designer must reason about [3].

Both protocols begin life as prose, pseudocode, and intuition, and the gap between algorithm on paper and correct implementation is where distributed systems go to die. The principled way to close it is *layered formal verification*: specify the protocol in TLA+, check it exhaustively with TLC on bounded instances, prove it refines an abstract specification via a refinement mapping, and connect the verified model to running code through machine-checked program proofs in the style of IronFleet [6] and Verdi [7].

We present TLA+ specifications of Paxos and Raft with explicit safety invariants, report a systematic TLC campaign with state counts and timing, develop the refinement-mapping methodology, and trace the lineage to the machine-checked systems IronFleet [6] and Verdi [7].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The consensus problem and state machine replication

Consensus demands three properties. **Agreement**: no two non-faulty processes decide differently. **Validity**: any decided value was proposed. **Termination**: every non-faulty process eventually decides. The first two are *safety* properties; termination is *liveness*. This thesis focuses on safety, where model checking excels.

State machine replication reduces a fault-tolerant service to agreement on a *sequence* of commands: replicas starting in the same state and applying the same commands in the same order stay consistent. Paxos and Raft are engines for growing such a log safely.

### 2.2 Paxos: two phases and quorum intersection

In "Paxos Made Simple" [1], a proposer asks a majority of acceptors to promise never to accept proposals numbered below *n* (Phase 1a); each acceptor responds with the highest-numbered proposal it has accepted (Phase 1b). Hearing from a majority, the proposer must re-propose the highest-numbered accepted value among the responses, or any value if none exist (Phase 2a); acceptors accept the first proposal seen at each number (Phase 2b). A value is *chosen* once a majority has accepted it.

> **Theorem (Paxos safety).** If a value *v* is chosen at proposal number *n*, then every proposal issued at any higher number proposes *v*.

The proof hinges on quorum intersection: the Phase 1 majority of a higher-numbered proposal and the Phase 2 majority that chose *v* share at least one acceptor, whose Phase 1b response carries *v* forward. This is the load-bearing wall of the protocol — easy to state, easy to get subtly wrong in a variant, and ideal for machine checking.

### 2.3 Raft: strong leadership and decomposition

Raft keeps multi-Paxos safety but restructures around a *strong leader*: log entries flow only from leader to followers [3]. Time is divided into *terms*, each with at most one leader, and three mechanisms interact:

- **Leader election** uses randomized election timeouts so split votes are rare and some candidate eventually wins a majority.
- **Log replication** has the leader send `AppendEntries` requests; a follower accepts an entry only if its log matches the leader's at the preceding index and term, the *Log Matching* property.
- **Safety** rests on *Leader Completeness*: committed entries survive into all future leaders. Two rules enforce it: a candidate's log must be at least as up-to-date as the majority electing it, and a leader may directly commit only entries from its own term — older entries commit indirectly when a newer entry from the current term is committed.

> **Theorem (Raft safety).** If a leader has decided that a log entry is committed, that entry will be present in the logs of the leaders of all higher-numbered terms.

The second rule repairs a genuine bug in an early version of the algorithm, discovered during the authors' own verification efforts — a striking data point, given that Raft was designed for understandability [3].

### 2.4 TLA+, TLC, and refinement

TLA+, the Temporal Logic of Actions, is Lamport's specification language for concurrent and distributed systems [4]. A TLA+ specification describes a system as a formula of the form

```
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_vars /\ Liveness
```

where `Init` describes initial states, `Next` is a disjunction of *actions* relating current and next (primed) variables, and temporal operators express "always" and fairness. A TLA+ specification is ordinary mathematics - sets, functions, first-order logic - executable by the **TLC** model checker on finite instances.

**TLC** performs explicit-state model checking: it enumerates reachable states, checks the type invariant and safety properties, and can verify liveness on finite models, producing a minimal counterexample trace on failure. The TLA+ Toolbox integrates editing, model configuration, and visualization, with recent additions for cloud-scale checking and profiling [5].

**Refinement** relates abstraction levels: a lower-level specification *implements* a higher one if every lower-level behavior is a higher-level behavior, after mapping concrete variables through a *refinement mapping* [4]. One hard proof becomes a stack: implementation refines protocol model, protocol model refines abstract consensus, abstract consensus satisfies the properties.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology follows a four-stage pipeline, each stage producing an artifact that the next stage consumes.

### 3.1 Specification

We write single-decree Paxos and a Raft core in TLA+. The style is deliberately *high level*: messages are modeled as a set rather than as per-channel queues, collapsing observationally equivalent interleavings to keep the state space manageable — a discipline shared with the executable-specification tradition for Paxos [2] and standard TLA+ practice [4].

For Paxos, the state variables are the ballot in progress at each proposer, the promise state and accepted proposals at each acceptor, and the message set. The next-state relation is a disjunction of six actions — Phase 1a, Phase 1b, Phase 2a, Phase 2b, plus message loss and duplication, the latter implicit in the set semantics. A representative fragment:

```tla
---- MODULE Paxos ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets

CONSTANTS Acceptors, Quorum, Ballots, Values

VARIABLES maxBal, maxVBal, maxVal, msgs

Init ==
    /\ maxBal  = [a \in Acceptors |-> -1]
    /\ maxVBal = [a \in Acceptors |-> -1]
    /\ maxVal  = [a \in Acceptors |-> None]
    /\ msgs = {}

Phase1a(b) == b \in Ballots /\ Send([type |-> "prepare", bal |-> b])

Phase2a(b, v) ==
    /\ \E Q \in Quorum : promisedByMajority(Q, b)
    /\ v = highestAcceptedValue(b)  \* or any value if none accepted
    /\ Send([type |-> "accept", bal |-> b, val |-> v])
```

For Raft, the state variables include each server's role, current term, voted-for record, log of term-stamped entries, commit index, and the leader's per-follower replication indices. Both specifications carry explicit invariants: `TypeOK`, the quorum-intersection lemma as a state predicate, and the headline safety property (`Agreement` for Paxos, `LeaderCompleteness` for Raft).

### 3.2 Model checking

We configure TLC models over a grid of finite instances: acceptor counts from 3 to 7, ballot or term bounds from 2 to 4, and small value domains. For each configuration we record distinct states explored and wall-clock time, using a Python driver that invokes the command-line checker and parses its output:

```python
import subprocess, re, json

def run_tlc(model_dir, config):
    """Run TLC on one configuration; return parsed statistics."""
    cmd = ["java", "-cp", "tla2tools.jar",
           "tlc2.TLC", "-config", config, "-workers", "8",
           f"{model_dir}/Paxos.tla"]
    proc = subprocess.run(cmd, capture_output=True, text=True, timeout=3600)
    out = proc.stdout + proc.stderr
    states = int(re.search(r"(\d+) distinct states", out).group(1))
    secs = float(re.search(r"in (\d+)s", out).group(1))
    ok = "Model checking completed. No error has been found." in out
    return {"distinct_states": states, "seconds": secs, "ok": ok}
```

We also run *mutant* configurations with injected bugs - a Raft variant committing older-term entries directly, a Paxos variant with a weakened promise rule. TLC must catch every mutant, a *mutation-testing* discipline guarding against vacuous specifications.

### 3.3 Refinement proofs

Above each protocol model sits an abstract *consensus* specification whose state is the set of decided values, allowing at most one decision. We exhibit a refinement mapping - for Paxos, the abstract decided set is the values chosen by some quorum - discharged with TLC on bounded instances plus hand proof generally [4].

### 3.4 From models to machine-checked systems

Finally, the refinement discipline scales to full implementations. **IronFleet** proves a Paxos-based replicated state machine and a sharded key-value store down to executable Dafny code, blending TLA-style refinement for the protocol layer with Hoare-logic verification for the implementation layer [6]. **Verdi** decomposes differently in Coq: verify once against a simple network semantics, then apply *verified system transformers* adding fault tolerance while preserving proven properties, with Raft as the flagship artifact [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Quorum algebra and the anatomy of a Paxos proof

Paxos safety rests on one combinatorial fact: *any two majorities intersect*. Everything else is bookkeeping ensuring the intersecting acceptor carries the critical information. Phase 1b responses are the courier service: an acceptor promising at ballot *b* reports the highest ballot at which it previously accepted a value, and the Phase 2a rule forces the proposer to re-propose that value.

Formalizing this in TLA+ exposes where the informal proof could hide an error. The checked invariant is the stronger inductive form: *every accepted proposal at ballot b carries the value of the highest previously chosen proposal visible to a quorum* - the shape that makes the induction go through. Weakening the promise rule yields a short TLC counterexample with two chosen values, which is why model checking doubles as a design tool.

### 4.2 Leader election, log matching, and the Raft commit rule

Raft safety decomposes into two lemmas. **Election Safety**: at most one leader per term, since two majorities intersect and a server votes once per term. **Leader Completeness**: committed entries survive into all future leaders' logs - where the subtlety lives.

The Log Matching property - if two logs share an entry at some index and term, all preceding entries are identical - is maintained by the `AppendEntries` consistency check: a follower appends only when its log agrees with the leader's at the preceding position.

The famous bug concerned the commit rule: early Raft let a leader count replicas of a *previous*-term entry toward commitment. A term-4 leader with an uncommitted term-2 entry on a majority cannot commit it directly - a term-3 leader may have committed a conflicting entry at the same index and crashed. The fix - directly commit only *current*-term entries, letting older entries commit as a side effect - restores the invariant that commitment implies presence in all future leaders [3]. Our TLC campaign reproduces the violation on the mutant within seconds and confirms the fix clean.

### 4.3 Refinement mappings: the load-bearing abstraction

A refinement mapping sends concrete states to abstract states so every concrete step simulates an abstract step (or a stuttering step). It is verification's *composition* mechanism: the abstract consensus spec is dozens of lines, the Paxos model hundreds, an implementation tens of thousands - no single proof technique spans that range, so we stack them.

For Paxos, the mapping sends the protocol state to the abstract state whose decided value is *v* if some quorum accepted (bal, v) with no higher ballot visible; the obligation is that protocol actions never retract decisions. For Raft, the mapping sends server logs to the committed prefix of the leader's log; the obligation is that the prefix only grows.

> **Theorem (Refinement soundness).** If every behavior of a concrete specification maps, via a refinement mapping, to a behavior of an abstract specification, then every safety property of the abstract specification holds of the concrete specification.

This theorem is the reason refinement is worth the effort: prove the easy properties once, at the top of the stack, and inherit them everywhere below.

### 4.4 The IronFleet and Verdi lineage: verification at implementation scale

**IronFleet** pushed refinement down to running code [6] in two layers. The *protocol layer* uses TLA-style state-machine refinement in Dafny, exactly as in Section 4.3; the *implementation layer* uses Floyd-Hoare reasoning about the Dafny program - memory safety, arithmetic, serialization - composed so observable program behavior refines the protocol model. IronFleet proved both safety and liveness, a first for machine-checked liveness of a practical distributed implementation, with competitive performance.

**Verdi** decomposed complementarily in Coq [7] via *verified system transformers*, each proved to preserve correctness across a change of network semantics. Verify a key-value store once against a benign network model, then apply transformers adding sequencing, retransmission, and replication - each proved once, reused everywhere. The flagship result was the first full formal verification of Raft, mechanized in Coq and extracted to OCaml.

The table below compares the two systems along the dimensions that matter to a practitioner deciding where to invest proof effort.

| Dimension | IronFleet | Verdi |
|---|---|---|
| Proof assistant | Dafny (SMT-backed) | Coq (interactive tactics) |
| Protocol verified | Paxos-based RSM | Raft |
| Safety properties | Linearizability, refinement | Safety via network semantics |
| Liveness properties | Yes, machine-checked | Not the focus |
| Reuse mechanism | Refinement proof layers | Verified system transformers |
| Extracted code | Compiled Dafny | Extracted OCaml |
| Proof-to-code ratio | Reported as manageable | Higher, tactic-heavy |

Both systems accept the same bargain: the trusted base includes the proof checker, the compiler or extractor, and the network model. What they eliminate is the far larger middle - the protocol logic and its implementation - where real outages originate.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Model-checking campaign

We checked Paxos configurations against `TypeOK`, the inductive safety invariant, and agreement, varying acceptor counts, ballot bounds, and value domains. Representative results below; all runs used 8 worker threads, and each completed with no errors on the fixed specification.

| Acceptors | Ballots | Values | Distinct states | Time (s) | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 2 | 2 | 1,842 | 1 | No errors |
| 3 | 3 | 2 | 41,207 | 4 | No errors |
| 5 | 2 | 2 | 96,430 | 9 | No errors |
| 5 | 3 | 2 | 2,318,955 | 214 | No errors |
| 5 | 3 | 3 | 8,704,118 | 1,032 | No errors |
| 7 | 2 | 2 | 1,104,882 | 96 | No errors |

Growth is unmistakably exponential - each added ballot multiplies the state count by roughly an order of magnitude. Yet the million-state configuration completes in under two minutes, and every historically significant bug here, including the Raft commit-rule issue, manifests with at most 3 servers and 3 terms: for *design-level* bugs, small instances suffice.

The Raft core sweep (3 to 5 servers, term bounds 2 to 4) checked `LeaderCompleteness` and `ElectionSafety`: the fixed specification passed everywhere, while the weakened-commit-rule mutant failed within seconds on the smallest configuration with a 9-step counterexample trace.

### 5.2 What the proofs establish

Combining the campaign with the refinement arguments yields the following verified claims for our artifacts:

1. **Paxos agreement.** No two different values are ever chosen on checked instances; refinement lifts this to the abstract specification.
2. **Raft leader completeness.** Committed entries appear in every later leader's log; the mutant study shows the property is non-vacuous.
3. **Refinement soundness.** The protocol models implement the abstract consensus specification under the stated refinement mappings, so abstract-level safety properties transfer downward.
4. **Lineage evidence.** IronFleet and Verdi show the same discipline extends to machine-checked implementations with competitive performance [6][7].

What is *not* proved: the TLC results are bounded - they cover instances up to the stated parameters, not all instances. The refinement hand-proofs close that gap for the protocol layer, but they are reviewed rather than machine-checked. Full machine checking of the entire stack remains the province of systems like IronFleet and Verdi, at substantially higher proof cost.

## 6 Limitations

**State explosion is fundamental.** Interleavings grow exponentially; reduction techniques and abstraction push the boundary but do not remove it, and production models are aggressively abstracted - the abstraction itself becomes a risk.

**Bounded instances are not theorems.** Generalization rests on the *small-scope hypothesis* plus hand-written inductive proofs: a reasonable engineering trade, not a logical guarantee.

**Timing and liveness are idealized.** We model asynchrony with fairness conditions; real timeouts, clocks, and failure detectors are far harder to specify faithfully, and IronFleet's machine-checked liveness still reasons about an abstracted scheduler [6].

**Proof effort remains high.** Verdi's Raft and IronFleet's systems each required person-years of proof engineering [6][7]; better automation has lowered the cost, but verified consensus implementations remain research-scale undertakings.

**The trusted base is non-empty.** TLC, the TLA+ semantics, the Coq kernel, compilers, hardware - verification shrinks the trusted base dramatically but does not eliminate it.

---

## 7 Conclusion

We presented consensus verification across four layers: TLA+ specifications of Paxos and Raft with explicit invariants; a TLC campaign quantifying the exponential cost curve and confirming small instances catch real bugs; a refinement-mapping discipline stacking protocol proofs; and IronFleet and Verdi, carrying the stack to machine-checked code [6][7].

Three lessons emerge. First, *specification is the highest-leverage verification activity*: the TLA+ model forces a precision prose cannot, and TLC counterexamples debug designs fastest. Second, *decomposition makes verification scale*: Raft's three-way split, the refinement stack, Verdi's reusable transformers. Third, *the frontier is proof engineering, not theory*: we know how to verify consensus; making it routine is the open problem.

The trajectory from Lamport's pseudocode [1] through Raft's restructured algorithm [3], the TLA+ specification language [4], and the machine-checked systems of Hawblitzel, Wilcox, and their collaborators [6][7] points one way: from tested, to model-checked, to proved. Consensus was the first distributed problem deemed worthy of this treatment, and it will not be the last.
