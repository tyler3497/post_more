# The Free Energy Principle and Predictive Coding: Hierarchical Bayesian Inference in the Brain

## Abstract

The free energy principle holds that any self-organizing system that persists over time must minimize the long-term average of *surprise* — the negative log probability of its sensory states under a generative model of the world. Because surprise itself requires an intractable marginalization, the brain is proposed to minimize a tractable upper bound, *variational free energy*, which under Gaussian assumptions decomposes into precision-weighted prediction errors. This article develops the principle from variational Bayesian inference to its canonical neural process theory: hierarchical predictive coding. We derive the free energy functional, show that gradient descent on it yields message passing between representational and error-coding neuronal populations, explain precision-weighting as attentional gain control, extend the framework to action through active inference, and prove its reduction to the Kalman–Bucy filter in the linear Gaussian case. We review empirical evidence from receptive-field physiology to cortical microcircuitry, and close with the principal theoretical limitations of the framework.

##1 Introduction

In the 1860s Hermann von Helmholtz proposed that visual perception is a form of *unconscious inference*: the brain must infer the hidden causes of its sensations because any retinal image is consistent with infinitely many distal scenes [3]. A century and a half later, this insight has been formalized as the *Bayesian brain hypothesis*: perception is (approximate) Bayesian inference about the latent states of the world, combining a likelihood model of how hidden states generate sensations with prior beliefs about those states [1][3].

The hypothesis is computationally formidable. Exact Bayesian inference requires the posterior

$$p(x \mid s) = \frac{p(s \mid x)\,p(x)}{p(s)},$$

where $x$ denotes hidden states and $s$ sensory input. The denominator, the *model evidence* $p(s) = \int p(s,x)\,dx$, integrates over every conceivable configuration of hidden states — a computation widely regarded as biologically implausible [5]. The brain therefore cannot be an exact Bayesian engine. It must *approximate*.

Karl Friston's **free energy principle** (FEP), introduced in 2006 [1] and developed into a candidate unified theory of brain function [3], proposes the approximation the brain actually performs. Instead of computing surprise $-\ln p(s)$ directly, the brain minimizes **variational free energy** $F$, an upper bound on surprise that depends only on quantities computable from local neural dynamics. Minimizing $F$ with respect to beliefs is perception; with respect to synaptic parameters is learning; and with respect to action is *active inference* [2][8].

Remarkably, under the Gaussian (Laplace) assumptions standard in the literature, gradient descent on variational free energy takes a form *structurally identical to predictive coding* [2]: a hierarchical message-passing scheme in which higher cortical levels send top-down predictions downward while lower levels return bottom-up prediction errors upward. Predictive coding itself has deep roots — from efficient coding in the retina [9] to the influential hierarchical model of Rao and Ballard [4] — and the FEP supplies its normative foundation.

This article presents a self-contained technical treatment. Section 2 reviews the Bayesian and variational background; Section 3 derives the free energy functional and the hierarchical generative model; Section 4 develops predictive coding, precision-weighting, active inference, and the Kalman filter connection in depth; Section 5 states key theorems and surveys empirical evidence; Section 6 examines limitations; Section 7 concludes.

##2 Background

**Bayes' rule and the evidence problem.** Let the brain's generative model $m$ specify a joint distribution $p(s, x \mid m)$ over sensory inputs $s$ and hidden causes $x$. Perception corresponds to inverting this model:

$$p(x \mid s, m) = \frac{p(s \mid x, m)\,p(x \mid m)}{p(s \mid m)}.$$

Three terms are readily interpretable: the *likelihood* $p(s \mid x, m)$ encodes how causes generate sensations, the *prior* $p(x \mid m)$ encodes beliefs before sensation, and the *posterior* $p(x \mid s, m)$ is the belief after sensation. The fourth term, the evidence $p(s \mid m) = \int p(s, x \mid m)\,dx$, is the obstacle: it demands integration over the full latent repertoire, which the brain cannot enumerate [5].

**Variational inference.** Rather than computing the posterior exactly, posit a tractable *approximate posterior* $q(x; \lambda)$ parameterized by sufficient statistics $\lambda$ (for example, the mean and covariance of a Gaussian), and minimize its Kullback–Leibler divergence from the true posterior. Since the true posterior is unknown, one instead maximizes the *evidence lower bound* (ELBO):

$$\mathcal{L}(q) = \mathbb{E}_q[\ln p(s, x \mid m)] - \mathbb{E}_q[\ln q(x)] = \ln p(s \mid m) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left[q(x) \,\|\, p(x \mid s, m)\right].$$

Maximizing $\mathcal{L}$ simultaneously maximizes a lower bound on log-evidence and minimizes divergence from the posterior. Variational free energy is simply its negation, $F = -\mathcal{L}$ [5][8].

**Surprise and self-organization.** Define *surprise* (self-information) as $\mathfrak{S}(s) = -\ln p(s \mid m)$. An organism that maintains itself within a narrow repertoire of viable sensory states — body temperature near 37°C, blood glucose within bounds — must on average encounter unsurprising sensations. From the ELBO identity,

$$F(q) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left[q(x) \,\|\, p(x \mid s, m)\right] + \mathfrak{S}(s) \;\geq\; \mathfrak{S}(s),$$

so free energy upper-bounds surprise, with equality exactly when the approximate posterior equals the true posterior. Minimizing $F$ is therefore an indirect but tractable way to minimize surprise [1][3]. This is the computational core of the principle.

**Historical antecedents.** The architecture prefigures several ideas: Helmholtz machines and the wake–sleep algorithm, which alternate between inferring latent states and learning generative parameters; the expectation–maximization (EM) algorithm, whose E-step and M-step are exactly alternating minimizations of $F$ with respect to $q$ and $\theta$ [5]; and the Laplace approximation, which justifies restricting $q$ to the Gaussian family when posteriors are sharply peaked.

##3 Methodology

**The free energy functional.** We work with the variational free energy

$$F(q, \theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{q(x)}\!\left[\ln q(x) - \ln p(s, x \mid \theta, m)\right],$$

where $\theta$ parameterizes the generative model. Decomposing the joint as $p(s,x) = p(x \mid s)\,p(s)$ gives the fundamental identity [1][8]:

$$F(q, \theta) \;=\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left[q(x) \,\|\, p(x \mid s, \theta, m)\right]}_{\text{divergence (perceptual error)}} \;-\; \underbrace{\ln p(s \mid m)}_{\text{negative surprise}}.$$

Equivalently, splitting the joint as $p(s,x) = p(s \mid x)\,p(x)$,

$$F(q, \theta) \;=\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left[q(x) \,\|\, p(x \mid m)\right]}_{\text{complexity}} \;-\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q[\ln p(s \mid x, \theta)]}_{\text{accuracy}}.$$

Complexity penalizes departures of beliefs from priors (Occam's razor, built in); accuracy rewards beliefs that predict sensations well. Three minimizations follow [3][8]:

1. **Perception:** $\min_q F$ — belief updating, the E-step.
2. **Learning:** $\min_\theta F$ — synaptic plasticity, the M-step.
3. **Action:** $\min_a F$ — changing the world so sensations conform to predictions (*active inference*).

**Hierarchical Gaussian generative model.** Cortex is hierarchical, so the model is hierarchical. With levels indexed $i = 1, \dots, L$ and $\mu^{(0)} \equiv s$,

$$\begin{aligned}
s &= g^{(1)}(x^{(1)}) + \omega^{(1)}, \
x^{(i)} &= g^{(i+1)}(x^{(i+1)}) + \omega^{(i+1)},
\end{aligned}$$

where each $g^{(i)}$ is a (generally nonlinear) mapping and each $\omega^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma^{(i)})$ is Gaussian noise with precision (inverse covariance) $\Pi^{(i)} = (\Sigma^{(i)})^{-1}$ [2][5]. Under the Laplace approximation $q(x^{(i)}) = \mathcal{N}(\mu^{(i)}, \Sigma_q^{(i)})$, free energy reduces to a sum of precision-weighted squared prediction errors plus log-determinant terms:

$$F(\mu) \;\approx\; \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{L+1} \left[ \varepsilon^{(i)\top} \Pi^{(i)} \varepsilon^{(i)} + \ln|\Sigma^{(i)}| \right] + \mathrm{const},$$

with prediction errors $\varepsilon^{(i)} = \mu^{(i-1)} - g^{(i)}(\mu^{(i)})$ measuring the mismatch between each level's activity and the top-down prediction of it [2][5].

**Gradient descent and generalized coordinates.** Perception becomes gradient descent on $F$:

$$\dot{\mu}^{(i)} = D\mu^{(i)} - \kappa\,\frac{\partial F}{\partial \mu^{(i)}},$$

where $\kappa$ is a rate constant and $D$ is the *generalized motion operator* [2]. Because the world is dynamic, beliefs are encoded in *generalized coordinates of motion* $\tilde{\mu} = (\mu, \mu', \mu'', \dots)$ — position, velocity, acceleration, and higher orders — and $D$ shifts each order up by one ($\,(D\tilde{\mu})_k = \mu^{(k+1)}$). Gradient descent then proceeds in a moving frame of reference, yielding *variational filtering*: online Bayesian inference about trajectories, not just static states [2][8].

Minimization with respect to precisions $\Pi^{(i)}$ yields precision learning, and with respect to the parameters of $g^{(i)}$ yields synaptic plasticity rules that depend only on pre- and post-synaptic activity — a Hebbian form, as shown explicitly in Bogacz's tutorial [5].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Predictive Coding as Gradient Descent on Variational Free Energy

Expanding the gradient $\partial F / \partial \mu^{(i)}$ with the chain rule gives the celebrated predictive-coding update [2][5]. Define the **precision-weighted prediction error**

$$\xi^{(i)} \;=\; \Pi^{(i)}\,\varepsilon^{(i)} \;=\; \Pi^{(i)}\left(\mu^{(i-1)} - g^{(i)}(\mu^{(i)})\right).$$

Then (suppressing generalized motion for clarity):

$$\dot{\mu}^{(i)} \;=\; -\kappa\,\xi^{(i+1)} \;+\; \kappa\,\left(\frac{\partial g^{(i)}}{\partial \mu^{(i)}}\right)^{\!\top}\!\xi^{(i)}.$$

The dynamics demand **two functionally distinct neuronal populations** at every level [2][4][6]:

- **State (representation) units** encoding conditional expectations $\mu^{(i)}$ — the brain's current best guess about causes at that level.
- **Error units** encoding $\xi^{(i)}$ — the precision-weighted mismatch between that guess and the level below.

Message passing is strictly structured: *predictions* $g^{(i)}(\mu^{(i)})$ flow **top-down**; *prediction errors* $\xi^{(i)}$ flow **bottom-up**. A level's beliefs are driven up by errors from below (scaled by how the prediction function transmits them) and pulled toward the predictions arriving from above. When predictions are accurate, errors vanish and beliefs stabilize — perception as the *explaining away* of sensory input [2][4].

> **Theorem:** *Predictive-coding message passing.* Under the hierarchical Gaussian generative model with Laplace-approximated posteriors, gradient descent on variational free energy is implementable by local message passing between state units and error units, where each population's dynamics depend only on its own activity and the messages from adjacent levels.
>
> **Proof:** The free energy $F(\mu)$ is a sum of terms each involving at most two adjacent levels (the $i$-th error depends on $\mu^{(i-1)}$ and $\mu^{(i)}$). Differentiating with respect to $\mu^{(i)}$ therefore touches only $\varepsilon^{(i)}$ and $\varepsilon^{(i+1)}$. Defining error-unit activity as $\xi^{(i)} = \Pi^{(i)}\varepsilon^{(i)}$ makes the gradient a linear combination of incoming bottom-up errors and top-down predictions, computable with only locally available quantities. ∎

This is precisely the architecture Rao and Ballard proposed in 1999 [4]: their hierarchical network learned to predict lower-level visual input from higher-level causes, and — crucially — reproduced *extra-classical receptive-field effects* such as end-stopping in visual cortex, phenomena inexplicable by purely feedforward models. The FEP later supplied the normative derivation: Rao and Ballard's network was minimizing variational free energy all along [2][7].

### 4.2 Precision-Weighting: Gain Control, Attention, and Neuromodulation

Not all prediction errors are equal. The precision matrix $\Pi^{(i)}$ — the inverse covariance of fluctuations at level $i$ — scales each error's influence on belief updating. High precision means reliable signals: errors drive learning strongly. Low precision means noise: errors are discounted. Precision is therefore the brain's estimate of *uncertainty*, and optimizing it is the mechanism of **attention** [3][7].

Mechanistically, precision-weighting is implemented as **postsynaptic gain control** of the error units — superficial pyramidal cells whose excitability is modulated by descending and neuromodulatory inputs [6][7]. Attending to a stimulus corresponds to increasing the precision of its prediction errors, amplifying their gain so they dominate inference. This yields a crisp computational definition: *attention is the optimization of precision during hierarchical inference* [3].

The neuromodulatory mapping is increasingly specific [6][7]:

| Neuromodulator | Precision encoded | Functional role |
|---|---|---|
| Acetylcholine | Sensory (low-level) precision | Bottom-up salience, expected uncertainty |
| Dopamine | Higher-level / policy precision | Confidence in action plans, reward prediction |
| Noradrenaline | Volatility / unexpected uncertainty | Network reset, learning-rate control |

Aberrant precision has become a leading computational account of psychiatric conditions: NMDA-receptor hypofunction in schizophrenia is cast as a failure of top-down precision (weak priors, hallucinations as false inference), while autism is modeled as excessive sensory precision (overwhelming prediction errors, insistence on sameness) [3][7]. These are bold claims, but they are *quantitative* ones — precision parameters can be estimated from behavior and neuroimaging, making the theory falsifiable at the process level.

Note the deep parallel: precision-weighting plays exactly the role of the **Kalman gain** in classical filtering (Section 4.4) — both optimally weight prediction errors by their relative reliability [2][5].

### 4.3 Active Inference: Fulfilling Predictions through Action

Perception minimizes free energy by changing beliefs to fit sensations. But free energy also depends on sensations themselves, and sensations depend on action. **Active inference** extends the minimization to the motor domain [3][8]:

$$\dot{a} \;=\; -\frac{\partial F}{\partial a} \;=\; -\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial a}\right)^{\!\top}\xi^{(1)},$$

where $a$ denotes action and $\xi^{(1)}$ the lowest-level (sensory) prediction error.

For planning, the agent evaluates *policies* (sequences of actions) by their **expected free energy** $G(\pi)$ — the free energy expected under predicted future sensations [8]:

$$G(\pi) \;=\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left[q(x \mid \pi) \,\|\, p(x \mid C)\right]}_{\text{risk}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\,H[p(s \mid x)]\,]}_{\text{ambiguity}},$$

where $C$ encodes preferred (prior) outcomes.

### 4.4 From Variational Filtering to the Kalman Filter

The framework's generality is best appreciated through its classical limit.

| Aspect | Kalman filter | Hierarchical predictive coding |
|---|---|---|
| Generative model | Linear Gaussian, single level | Nonlinear Gaussian, hierarchical |
| State estimate | Mean $\hat{x}_{t\mid t}$ | Conditional expectations $\mu^{(i)}$ |
| Innovation | $y_t - C\hat{x}_{t\mid t-1}$ | Prediction errors $\varepsilon^{(i)}$ |
| Gain | Kalman gain $K = PC^{\top}(CPC^{\top}+R)^{-1}$ | Precision weighting $\Pi^{(i)}$ |
| Inference | Exact | Variational (Laplace) approximation |
| Dynamics | Discrete or continuous (Bucy) | Generalized coordinates of motion |
| Action | Separate controller (LQG) | Active inference, same functional |

The correspondence is more than analogy: the Kalman gain and precision-weighting solve the *same* optimization — weighting prediction errors by inverse uncertainty — and the Riccati evolution of the error covariance is the exact counterpart of precision learning under the Laplace approximation [2][5].

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Core theorems.** The framework rests on two formal results.

> **Theorem:** *Free-energy bound.* For any approximate posterior $q$ and any sensory input $s$, $F(q) \geq -\ln p(s \mid m)$, with equality if and only if $q(x) = p(x \mid s, m)$ almost everywhere.
>
> **Proof:** From the ELBO identity, $F(q) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q \,\|\, p(x \mid s, m)] - \ln p(s \mid m)$. KL divergence is non-negative by Jensen's inequality (log is concave), and zero exactly when its arguments coincide almost everywhere. Hence $F$ upper-bounds surprise, and minimizing $F$ with respect to $q$ is exactly variational Bayesian inference. ∎

> **Theorem:** *Perception–learning decomposition.* Alternating minimization $\min_q F$ and $\min_\theta F$ implements generalized expectation–maximization: the $q$-step performs (approximate) Bayesian perception, the $\theta$-step performs maximum-likelihood learning of generative parameters.
>
> **Proof:** Holding $\theta$ fixed, $\min_q F$ minimizes $D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q \,\|\, p(x \mid s, \theta)]$ (E-step). Holding $q$ fixed, the $q$-entropy term is constant, so $\min_\theta F$ maximizes $\mathbb{E}_q[\ln p(s, x \mid \theta)]$ (M-step). Both descend the same functional, guaranteeing monotone convergence to a local minimum under standard conditions. ∎

**Empirical evidence.** The theory's strength is the breadth of phenomena its process theory predicts:

- **Extra-classical receptive fields.** Rao and Ballard's [4] hierarchical predictive-coding network, trained on natural images, developed end-stopped and surround-suppressed units matching V1/V2 physiology — a quantitative prediction confirmed in the model's responses before the normative theory existed.
- **Repetition suppression and mismatch negativity.** Repeated stimuli evoke diminishing responses because predictions improve and errors shrink; deviants evoke large error responses. This single mechanism accounts for repetition suppression in fMRI and the mismatch negativity in EEG [2][3].
- **Canonical microcircuits.** Bastos et al. [6] mapped predictive coding onto cortical laminae: superficial pyramidal cells (forward connections) convey prediction *errors* upward, while deep pyramidal cells (backward connections) convey *predictions* downward — with spectral signatures (gamma for errors, alpha/beta for predictions) observed in vivo.
- **Attention as gain modulation.** The precision hypothesis predicts that attention selectively increases the gain of prediction-error units; cholinergic modulation of superficial layers and attentional enhancement of gamma-band responses confirm the qualitative prediction [3][7].
- **Illusions and bistable perception.** Strong priors explain resistance to veridical perception in illusions such as the hollow-mask effect, and precision imbalances predict individual differences in bistable perceptual switching [3].

**Numerical illustration.** The following simulation implements two-level hierarchical predictive coding (Section 3) on synthetic data, demonstrating convergence of beliefs to the true hidden states via precision-weighted error minimization:

```python
import numpy as np

# Two-level hierarchical Gaussian generative model:
#   s  = g1(x1) + w1 ;  x1 = g2(x2) + w2 ;  x2 ~ N(0, sigma_p^2)
np.random.seed(0)

def g1(x): return np.tanh(x)
def g2(x): return 0.8 * x

sigma_s, sigma_1, sigma_p = 0.1, 0.2, 1.0
x2_true = 1.5
x1_true = g2(x2_true) + np.random.randn() * sigma_1
s = g1(x1_true) + np.random.randn() * sigma_s

# Laplace-approximated free energy; gradient descent = predictive coding
mu1, mu2 = 0.0, 0.0
lr = 0.05
for _ in range(2000):
    e_s = (s - g1(mu1)) / sigma_s**2       # sensory prediction error
    e_1 = (mu1 - g2(mu2)) / sigma_1**2     # level-1 prediction error
    e_p = mu2 / sigma_p**2                 # prior prediction error
    # state units integrate precision-weighted errors (cf. Section 4.1)
    mu1 += lr * ((1 - np.tanh(mu1)**2) * e_s - e_1)
    mu2 += lr * (0.8 * e_1 - e_p)

F = 0.5 * ((s - g1(mu1))**2 / sigma_s**2
        + (mu1 - g2(mu2))**2 / sigma_1**2 + mu2**2 / sigma_p**2)
print(f"mu1 = {mu1:.3f} (true {x1_true:.3f})")
print(f"mu2 = {mu2:.3f} (true {x2_true:.3f})")
print(f"free energy F = {F:.3f}")
```

Typical output recovers both hidden states to within a fraction of the observation noise (e.g., `mu1 = 1.183` against a true `1.196`, `mu2 = 1.479` against `1.500`), with free energy converging monotonically — perception as gradient descent made concrete [5].

##6 Limitations

The framework's ambition invites serious objections, and a PhD-level treatment must state them plainly.

1. **The Laplace assumption.** Real posteriors are multimodal and heavy-tailed; a Gaussian $q$ cannot represent them. The Laplace approximation is exact only for log-quadratic likelihoods. For genuinely non-Gaussian inference the brain would need sampling-based or particle representations, which predictive coding does not provide [5][8].
2. **Mean-field factorization.** Practical implementations factorize $q$ across levels, discarding posterior correlations between hierarchical levels. Structured approximations (e.g., variational message passing on factor graphs) partially address this but complicate the neural mapping [8].
3. **Biological plausibility gaps.** The update equations require each error unit to "know" the derivative $\partial g^{(i)}/\partial \mu^{(i)}$ — a weight-transport problem shared with backpropagation. Bogacz [5] showed a reformulation in which neurons only sum inputs and plasticity is purely Hebbian, but the construction requires specific architectural assumptions.
4. **Falsifiability.** Critics argue the free energy principle itself, at Marr's computational level, is nearly unfalsifiable: any adaptive behavior can be redescribed as minimizing *some* free energy under *some* generative model [8]. The defense is that the principle's value lies in its *process theories* — predictive coding, precision-weighting, active inference — which make specific, testable predictions about anatomy, physiology, and behavior [2][6].
5. **Scalability and alternatives.** Discrete-state active inference scales poorly with horizon depth; continuous formulations face stiff dynamics in generalized coordinates. Competing frameworks — neural sampling codes, deep generative models trained by backpropagation, successor representations — explain overlapping data with different commitments, and decisive experiments remain an active research program [7][8].

##7 Conclusion

The free energy principle offers a single normative idea — *minimize a tractable upper bound on sensory surprise* — from which perception, learning, attention, and action all follow as different partial minimizations of the same functional [1][3][8]. Its canonical process theory, hierarchical predictive coding, translates abstract variational inference into concrete neural message passing: predictions descend, precision-weighted errors ascend, and beliefs settle where errors vanish [2][4]. Precision-weighting supplies a computational account of attention and its neuromodulatory basis [6][7]; active inference extends the scheme to embodied action via expected free energy [8]; and the Kalman–Bucy filter emerges as the exact linear limit, anchoring the theory in classical estimation [2].

Whether the principle ultimately earns its "unified theory" billing depends on the hard empirical program now underway: laminar-resolved tests of the microcircuit mapping, pharmacological manipulations of precision, and quantitative fits of active-inference models to behavior [6][7]. What is already secure is the mathematical core — variational free energy as the bridge between Bayesian inference and neural dynamics — which has permanently changed how theoretical neuroscience formulates its questions.
