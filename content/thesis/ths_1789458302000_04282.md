# Arithmetic Dynamics: Canonical Heights, the Dynamical Mordell–Lang Conjecture, and Equidistribution of Preperiodic Points

## Abstract

Arithmetic dynamics studies the number-theoretic properties of points under iteration of rational maps on projective varieties. This thesis surveys three intertwined pillars of the field: the canonical height of Call and Silverman, the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture, and the equidistribution of preperiodic points. We present the Tate-style limit construction of the canonical height, its characterization of preperiodic points as the zero set of an arithmetic dynamical system, and its decomposition into local Green functions. We then formulate the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture, which constrains the intersection of an orbit with a subvariety, and recount its proof for étale maps and endomorphisms of projective space by Bell, Ghioca, and Tucker via p-adic quasiperiodicity. Finally, we develop the equidistribution theory of Yuan, Favre–Rivera-Letelier, and others, showing how Galois orbits of preperiodic points converge to the measure of maximal entropy, and connect these results to the dynamical Manin–Mumford and André–Oort phenomena.

##1 Intro

The classical theory of Diophantine geometry asks how rational and algebraic points sit inside algebraic varieties: how many there are, how large they are, and how they distribute. Arithmetic dynamics [1][2] imports this machinery into the world of discrete dynamical systems. Where complex dynamics studies iteration as a topological or analytic phenomenon — Julia sets, entropy, bifurcations — arithmetic dynamics studies iteration as a *number-theoretic* phenomenon: given a rational map φ defined over a number field K and a point P defined over the algebraic closure, what can be said about the arithmetic complexity of the forward orbit O_φ(P)?

Three questions dominate the landscape, and this thesis is organized around them.

1. **Height.** How does the arithmetic complexity of φ^n(P) grow with n? The answer, due to Call and Silverman, is the *canonical height* ĥ_φ, a function obtained by renormalizing the naive Weil height along the orbit. Its kernel is precisely the set of preperiodic points, a *dynamical Kronecker theorem* that anchors everything else [10][2].
2. **Orbits versus subvarieties.** If V is a subvariety of the ambient space, how often can the orbit of a wandering point land on V? The *dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture* asserts that this intersection is finite unless V contains a periodic subvariety, and it has been proved for étale maps and endomorphisms of projective space by Bell, Ghioca, and Tucker [4], using deep p-adic dynamics.
3. **Equidistribution.** Where do preperiodic points *accumulate*? Galois orbits of preperiodic points equidistribute with respect to the measure of maximal entropy μ_φ, by theorems of Yuan, Chambert-Loir, and Favre–Rivera-Letelier [3][6]. This arithmetic equidistribution yields rigidity: two rational maps sharing infinitely many preperiodic points share their measures [3].

These pillars are not independent. The canonical height is the analytic backbone of the equidistribution theorems; the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture controls the return sets that equidistribution would otherwise leave ambiguous; and all three feed the grand open problems — the uniform boundedness conjecture of Morton and Silverman, the dynamical Lang and Lehmer conjectures on canonical heights, and the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture.

##2 Background

### Height functions in Diophantine geometry

Let K be a number field and let h denote the absolute logarithmic Weil height on projective space P^N(¯K), normalized so that h is functorial and satisfies the Northcott property: the set of points of bounded height and bounded degree is finite [1]. The height measures arithmetic complexity: rational integers are measured by their size, rational numbers p/q by log max(|p|,|q|) in lowest terms, and algebraic points by their minimal polynomials.

The prototype of a *canonical* height is the Néron–Tate height on an abelian variety A. If φ = [2] is the doubling map on an elliptic curve E and h a Weil height, then the limit

ĥ(P) = lim_{n→∞} 4^{-n} h(φ^n(P))

exists, satisfies ĥ(φ(P)) = 4ĥ(P), differs from h by a bounded amount, and vanishes exactly on torsion points [1]. Call and Silverman realized that this construction generalizes to any *polarized dynamical system* (V, φ, D) with φ^*D linearly equivalent to dD for some real number d > 1. The same Tate limit produces the canonical height ĥ_φ [10].

> **Theorem:** *(Call–Silverman)* Let φ: P^N → P^N be a morphism of degree d ≥ 2 defined over ¯Q. There exists a unique function ĥ_φ: P^N(¯Q) → R_{≥0} such that (i) ĥ_φ(φ(P)) = d·ĥ_φ(P), (ii) ĥ_φ = h + O(1), and (iii) ĥ_φ(P) = 0 if and only if P is preperiodic for φ [10][2].

Part (iii) is the dynamical analogue of Kronecker's theorem and is the single most used fact in arithmetic dynamics.

### Dynamics over number fields

A point P is *preperiodic* if its forward orbit O_φ(P) = {φ^n(P) : n ≥ 0} is finite; it is *wandering* if the orbit is infinite. Rational maps of degree at least two over Q have only finitely many rational preperiodic points [2], but the uniform boundedness conjecture of Morton and Silverman asks whether the total number is bounded by a constant depending only on the degree and the dimension — a dynamical analogue of Merel's theorem on torsion of elliptic curves. Even for quadratic polynomials over Q, this remains open.

### Classical analogues

The three dynamical pillars mirror three classical Diophantine achievements:

| Classical result | Dynamical analogue | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Faltings' theorem (Mordell conjecture) | Dynamical Mordell–Lang | Proved for étale maps, P^N endomorphisms [4] |
| Manin–Mumford (torsion points) | Dynamical Manin–Mumford | Proved for (P^1)^n products; recent A^2 theorem [5] |
| Equidistribution (Szpiro–Ullmo–Zhang) | Equidistribution of preperiodic points | General theorems by Yuan et al. [6] |

Understanding the analogy — and where it breaks down — is the engine of the field. A famous breakdown: the original dynamical Manin–Mumford statement is *false*, as shown by Ghioca, Tucker, and Zhang, who produced a polarized endomorphism of E × E for which the diagonal contains infinitely many preperiodic points without being periodic [5]. The corrected conjecture requires excluding "special" maps such as Lattès maps and isogenies.

##3 Methodology

Our method is synthetic: we combine local-to-global height theory, p-adic analytic dynamics, and measure-theoretic equidistribution to give a unified account of the three pillars. Concretely, the methodology rests on three techniques.

*The Tate limit and telescoping sums.* The existence of the canonical height is proved by showing that the sequence d^{-n} h(φ^n(P)) is Cauchy. The key estimate is h(φ(P)) = d·h(P) + O(1), which follows from functoriality of the Weil height applied to a lift of φ to affine space. The error telescopes: |d^{-(n+1)}h(φ^{n+1}(P)) − d^{-n}h(φ^n(P))| decays geometrically, so the limit exists and inherits the transformation law.

*Local decomposition.* The canonical height splits as a sum over places:

ĥ_φ(P) = (1/[K:Q]) · Σ_v n_v λ̂_{φ,v}(P)

where λ̂_{φ,v} are local canonical heights, limits of v-adic escape rates [2]. For archimedean v, λ̂ is the Green function of the filled Julia set; for nonarchimedean v, it is defined via Berkovich analytic geometry. This decomposition is what connects the canonical height to the invariant measure: the archimedean local height is a potential for μ_φ, and small-height points equidistribute because their local contributions nearly cancel.

*p-adic quasiperiodicity and Skolem–Mahler–Lech.* The dynamical Mordell–Lang proof for étale maps reduces, after passing to a prime of good reduction, to studying orbits in p-adic disks where φ is analytically conjugate to a translation t ↦ t + 1 on a quasiperiodicity domain (Rivera-Letelier). On such a disk, the condition φ^n(x) ∈ V becomes the vanishing of a p-adic analytic function in n, and the classical Skolem–Mahler–Lech theorem on zeros of linear recurrences forces the return set to be a finite union of arithmetic progressions [4].

##4 Deep Dive

###4.1 The canonical height and the dynamical Kronecker theorem

Fix φ: P^N → P^N of degree d ≥ 2 over a number field K, and a Weil height h. The construction proceeds in four stages.

```python
def canonical_height(phi, P, d, height, terms=40):
    """Tate-limit approximation of the Call-Silverman canonical height."""
    Q, total = P, height(P)
    for n in range(1, terms + 1):
        Q = phi(Q)
        total = height(Q) / (d ** n)
    return total
```

**Stage 1 — the telescoping estimate.** There exists C = C(φ, h) with |h(φ(Q)) − d·h(Q)| ≤ C for all Q. Hence the sequence a_n = d^{-n}h(φ^n(P)) satisfies |a_{n+1} − a_n| ≤ C·d^{-(n+1)}, a summable geometric tail, so a_n converges [1][10].

**Stage 2 — functoriality.** The limit ĥ_φ satisfies ĥ_φ(φ(P)) = d·ĥ_φ(P) and |ĥ_φ − h| ≤ C/(d − 1). In particular, ĥ_φ is independent of the chosen Weil height up to the stated bound, and conjugate dynamical systems have conjugate canonical heights.

**Stage 3 — the zero set.** If P is preperiodic, then the orbit is finite, so h(φ^n(P)) is bounded and d^{-n}h(φ^n(P)) → 0. Conversely, if ĥ_φ(P) = 0, then ĥ_φ(φ^n(P)) = d^nĥ_φ(P) = 0 for all n, so the forward orbit has canonical height zero, hence bounded Weil height; by Northcott, the orbit is finite and P is preperiodic. This is the *dynamical Kronecker theorem* [10].

**Stage 4 — local decomposition.** The global height decomposes into local heights λ̂_{φ,v}, and for polynomials the local height at v is the limit of d^{-n} log max(|φ^n(β)|_v, 1) [2]. For archimedean places this recovers the dynamical Green function whose Laplacian is the measure of maximal entropy μ_φ — the bridge to equidistribution.

The canonical height also drives the major open problems. The *dynamical Lang conjecture* predicts a uniform positive lower bound for ĥ_φ(P) over wandering points P (with constant depending only on the degree and the field); the *dynamical Lehmer conjecture* predicts an asymptotically optimal lower bound in terms of the degree of P, in analogy with Lehmer's problem on Mahler measures [9][10]. Strong statistical evidence for the dynamical Lang conjecture was obtained by Le Boudec and Mavraki, who proved an optimal statistical version for random polynomials ordered by height [9].

```haskell
-- The canonical height as a fixed point of a renormalization operator.
canonicalHeight :: RationalMap -> Point -> Double
canonicalHeight phi p =
  let d = degree phi
      orbit = iterate phi p
      approx n = weilHeight (orbit !! n) / fromIntegral (d ^ n)
  in limit approx  -- Cauchy by the telescoping estimate
```

###4.2 The dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture

Faltings' theorem says that on an abelian variety, the intersection of a finitely generated subgroup with a subvariety containing no abelian subvariety is finite. Replacing the group by a *wandering orbit* yields the dynamical analogue [2]:

> **Conjecture:** *(Dynamical Mordell–Lang)* Let φ: P^N → P^N be a morphism of degree at least 2, let P be a wandering point, and let X be an irreducible subvariety containing no φ-periodic subvariety of positive dimension. Then X ∩ O_φ(P) is finite [2].

More is true in the known cases: the *return set* {n : φ^n(P) ∈ X} is a finite union of arithmetic progressions. The progression structure handles the periodic subvarieties: if X contains a periodic component, the orbit can meet it infinitely often but only along arithmetic progressions.

The conjecture is known in several important cases:

- **Étale maps.** Bell, Ghioca, and Tucker proved it for étale self-maps of quasiprojective varieties, using the p-adic quasiperiodicity method described above [4].
- **Endomorphisms of P^N.** The same authors extended the method to all endomorphisms of projective space, the central case.
- **Polynomial maps of A^2.** Xie proved it for polynomial endomorphisms of the affine plane, using Berkovich-space analysis near points at infinity (Favre–Jonsson techniques) [8].
- **Split maps.** For maps of the form Φ(x, y) = (f(x), g(y)) on A^2 with V a line, Ghioca, Tucker, and Zieve proved it via Siegel's theorem on integral points [8].

The p-adic proof is worth sketching. After reduction modulo a suitable prime, one studies the orbit inside residue disks. On a *quasiperiodicity disk* U, some iterate f^k is analytically conjugate to the translation t ↦ t + 1 via a bijective rigid analytic map h. Writing z_n = f^{nk+ℓ}(x), the condition z_n ∈ V becomes G(n) = 0 for a p-adic analytic function G of the integer variable n. By a p-adic analytic lemma, either G is identically zero on a residue class (giving an arithmetic progression) or its zeros are isolated and finite [4]. This reduces dynamics to the classical Skolem–Mahler–Lech theorem.

```tla+
---- MODULE DynamicalMordellLang ----

VARIABLES n
ReturnSet == { n \in Nat : phi^[n](P) \in X }
\* Conjecture: ReturnSet is a finite union of arithmetic progressions,
\* unless X contains a periodic subvariety of positive dimension.
FiniteUnionOfProgressions(S) ==
  \E k \in Nat : \E a \in [1..k -> Nat], b \in [1..k -> Nat] :
    S = UNION { { a[i] + b[i]*m : m \in Nat } : i \in 1..k }
====
```

###4.3 Equidistribution of preperiodic points

The third pillar is the deepest analytically. To each rational map f of degree d ≥ 2 over C is attached the *measure of maximal entropy* μ_f, supported on the Julia set, with entropy log d. The arithmetic theorem is:

> **Theorem:** *(Baker–Rumely, Chambert-Loir, Favre–Rivera-Letelier, Yuan)* Let f ∈ Q(z) have degree d ≥ 2 and let (x_n) be an infinite sequence of preperiodic points. Then the Galois-average measures converge weakly to the equilibrium measure:
>
> (1/[Q(x_n):Q]) · Σ_{y ∈ Gal(x_n)} δ_y → μ_f  in P^1(C) [3].

The reason μ_f appears is the local height decomposition: the Call–Silverman canonical height satisfies the hypotheses of Yuan's general equidistribution theorem for points of small height, and the archimedean local height is a potential for μ_f [3][6]. Since preperiodic points have canonical height zero, any infinite sequence of them is a sequence of small points, and the theorem applies. Dujardin's notes give a clean exposition of this deduction and derive a striking rigidity corollary [3]:

> **Corollary:** *(Rigidity)* If f and g are rational maps over Q of degree at least 2 sharing infinitely many common preperiodic points, then μ_f = μ_g, and in particular their Julia sets coincide [3].

This rigidity is the dynamical shadow of unlikely-intersection phenomena: Baker and DeMarco used equidistribution of postcritically finite parameters to attack the dynamical André–Oort conjecture, proving that in a family of rational maps, parameters where two marked critical points are simultaneously preperiodic lie on special subvarieties [3][7]. DeMarco's theorem on the variation of the canonical height in families — improving the error term in the Call–Silverman estimate to O(1) — is the technical engine behind these unlikely-intersection results [7].

###4.4 The dynamical Manin–Mumford and André–Oort frontier

The dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture asks: if X ⊂ (P^1)^n contains infinitely many preperiodic points of a product map, is X preperiodic? For (P^1)^n with non-Lattès coordinate maps, Ghioca, Tucker, and Zhang proved it [5]. But the general polarized case fails: on E × E with f(x, y) = (5x, (3 + 4i)y), torsion (hence preperiodic) points are Zariski dense in the diagonal, which is not periodic [5]. The corrected conjecture adds a "non-special" hypothesis.

A recent breakthrough: Dujardin, Favre, and Ruggiero proved the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture for regular polynomial maps of A^2 and irreducible curves avoiding superattracting orbits at infinity, over any field of characteristic zero [5]. Their proof blends complex dynamics (currents, bifurcation measures) with arithmetic equidistribution — exactly the fusion of pillars two and three.

Meanwhile, the *arithmetic degree* α_f(x) = lim max(1, h_L(f^n(x)))^{1/n} and the Kawaguchi–Silverman conjecture relate orbit growth to dynamical degrees, and convergence of the arithmetic degree is known for orbits satisfying the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture — another bridge between the pillars [7].

##5 Empirical/Proofs

We collect the key proven results and the evidence for the open conjectures.

**Proved.**

- *Dynamical Kronecker theorem.* ĥ_φ(P) = 0 iff P is preperiodic, for polarized endomorphisms over number fields [10][2]. This is the workhorse of the subject.
- *Dynamical Mordell–Lang.* Proved for étale maps and endomorphisms of P^N (Bell–Ghioca–Tucker) [4], for polynomial maps of A^2 (Xie) [8], and for split maps with V a line (Ghioca–Tucker–Zieve) [8].
- *Equidistribution of preperiodic points.* Galois orbits of any infinite sequence of preperiodic points equidistribute toward μ_f [3][6]; the same holds for small points in Yuan's arithmetic bigness framework [6].
- *Dynamical Manin–Mumford.* Proved for product maps on (P^1)^n (Ghioca–Tucker–Zhang) and for regular polynomial maps of A^2 (Dujardin–Favre–Ruggiero) [5].
- *Dynamical André–Oort.* Baker–DeMarco for the moduli space of rational maps with marked critical points; Favre–Gauthier and Ghioca–Ye for cubic polynomials [3].

**Evidence for the open conjectures.**

- *Uniform boundedness (Morton–Silverman).* Le Boudec and Mavraki proved that the *average* number of rational preperiodic points of polynomials ordered by height is zero — a strong statistical version [9]. Extensive computational searches for small degrees support the conjecture [9].
- *Dynamical Lang conjecture.* The same authors proved an optimal statistical version: the dynamical successive minima of random polynomials behave as the conjecture predicts [9].
- *Dynamical Lehmer conjecture.* Evidence comes from lower bounds for canonical heights via finite-tree and Hubbard-tree combinatorics (Thurston core entropy) for univariate polynomials [10].

**A worked computation.** For f(z) = z^2 − 2 over Q, the canonical height can be computed in closed form. Since f^n(z) + f^{-n}-type identities hold, one finds ĥ_f(z) = h(z) + log 2 for |z| large, and in fact ĥ_f(z) = lim_{n→∞} 2^{-n} log max(|f^n(z)|, 1) + c. Rational preperiodic points are exactly the points z = ζ + ζ^{-1} with ζ a root of unity — the Chebyshev case — all of canonical height zero, illustrating the dynamical Kronecker theorem concretely [1].

##6 Limitations

The theory has sharp boundaries, and several are structural rather than technical.

1. **Degree one and automorphisms.** The canonical height construction collapses for automorphisms (d = 1): the Tate limit does not renormalize, and indeed on abelian varieties the Néron–Tate height uses the group law rather than iteration. Dynamical Mordell–Lang for automorphisms of surfaces in positive characteristic requires separate methods [8].
2. **The special-map obstruction.** Lattès maps, power maps, and Chebyshev maps admit infinitely many preperiodic subvarieties and violate naive formulations of dynamical Manin–Mumford; the Ghioca–Tucker–Zhang counterexample on E × E shows that any general conjecture must exclude these "special" maps explicitly [5].
3. **Effectivity.** The p-adic proof of dynamical Mordell–Lang is ineffective: it gives no bound on the size of the finite intersection X ∩ O_φ(P), because the Skolem–Mahler–Lech step is nonconstructive. Effective versions remain largely open.
4. **Positive characteristic.** Most equidistribution theorems rely on archimedean potential theory or Yuan's arithmetic bigness over number fields; the function-field and positive-characteristic analogues are fragmentary, and the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture in characteristic p has counterexamples without extra hypotheses [8].
5. **Higher dimensions.** Equidistribution is well developed for polarized endomorphisms, but for dominant rational maps that are not morphisms (indeterminacy), the invariant measure theory and the canonical height both become substantially harder; the Kawaguchi–Silverman conjecture on arithmetic degrees remains open in general [7].
6. **Computational limits.** Verifying the dynamical Lang or Lehmer conjectures computationally requires searching enormous parameter spaces; genetic-algorithm approaches have been proposed to find extreme examples, but exhaustive search is infeasible beyond small degrees [9].

##7 Conclusion

The canonical height, the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture, and the equidistribution of preperiodic points form a single coherent picture: *the canonical height measures arithmetic complexity along orbits, the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture says orbits meet subvarieties only finitely often, and equidistribution says the arithmetically simplest points — the preperiodic ones — spread uniformly according to the measure of maximal entropy.* Each pillar reinforces the others: equidistribution theorems are proved by applying Yuan's machinery to the canonical height; the arithmetic degree converges on orbits satisfying dynamical Mordell–Lang; and unlikely-intersection results like dynamical André–Oort emerge from equidistribution in parameter space.

The open frontiers — uniform boundedness, the dynamical Lang and Lehmer conjectures, the full dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture, and effectivity — are among the deepest problems in modern number theory. What is remarkable is how much has already been achieved by translating classical Diophantine geometry into dynamics: Faltings' theorem became Bell–Ghioca–Tucker, Manin–Mumford became Ghioca–Tucker–Zhang and Dujardin–Favre–Ruggiero, and Szpiro–Ullmo–Zhang became Yuan. The dictionary between arithmetic geometry and dynamics continues to generate theorems in both directions, and the canonical height remains its central entry.
