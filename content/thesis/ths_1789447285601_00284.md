# Bootstrapping in Fully Homomorphic Encryption: TFHE Programmable Bootstrapping, Modulus Switching, and Noise Management in CKKS and BGV

## Abstract

Bootstrapping — the homomorphic evaluation of a scheme's own decryption circuit — is the operation that converts leveled homomorphic encryption into *fully* homomorphic encryption by refreshing ciphertext noise. This thesis compares the three dominant modern bootstrapping paradigms along a unified noise-management framework: **TFHE** blind-rotation gate bootstrapping with programmable functional evaluation via lookup tables, **CKKS** approximate bootstrapping through ModRaise, homomorphic DFT, and sine-polynomial modulus reduction, and **BGV/BFV** digit-extraction bootstrapping built on modulus switching. We derive the noise algebra of each, quantify their latency, throughput, level consumption, and precision tradeoffs using published benchmark data, and prove noise-bound guarantees for the TFHE refresh. The analysis shows there is no universally optimal refresh: TFHE minimizes per-gate latency and enables arbitrary univariate functions, CKKS maximizes amortized throughput on real-number vectors, and BGV/BFV serve exact small-integer arithmetic at intermediate throughput without functional bootstrapping.

##1 Introduction

Every lattice-based homomorphic encryption scheme embeds a small random *noise* term in each ciphertext. Homomorphic addition grows noise additively; homomorphic multiplication grows it roughly quadratically, and deep circuits drive it exponentially [1]. Decryption fails once the noise crosses a threshold determined by the ciphertext modulus, so a scheme that evaluates a circuit of multiplicative depth \(L\) must provision a modulus chain of \(L\) levels — this is **leveled** homomorphic encryption. The price of depth is concrete: the modulus bit-length, and hence the ring dimension required for security, grows with \(L\).

Gentry's 2009 breakthrough was **bootstrapping**: if the scheme can homomorphically evaluate its own decryption circuit (plus one extra operation), then a noisy ciphertext can be *refreshed* — converted into a fresh ciphertext encrypting the same plaintext with small noise — enabling unbounded depth [2]. The refreshed ciphertext carries the noise of a *fresh* encryption, independent of the noise accumulated before the refresh, at the cost of evaluating decryption under encryption.

Modern practice has diverged into three families whose bootstrapping strategies differ radically:

- **TFHE / CGGI** (torus-based): bootstrap after *every* gate. A fast *blind rotation* refreshes the ciphertext in roughly ten milliseconds while optionally evaluating an arbitrary univariate function through a lookup table — *programmable bootstrapping* (PBS) [3][6].
- **CKKS** (approximate arithmetic): bootstrap infrequently and in bulk. A four-stage pipeline (ModRaise, homomorphic inverse-DFT, polynomial sine approximation, homomorphic DFT) refreshes thousands of slots at once, at the cost of many consumed levels and approximate precision [1][4][5].
- **BGV / BFV** (exact arithmetic): bootstrap rarely, using *digit extraction* recurrences under a modulus-switching chain, achieving high throughput on vectors of small integers but offering no functional bootstrapping [2].

> **Theorem (Bootstrappability criterion).** *A homomorphic scheme is bootstrappable if its decryption circuit has depth strictly less than the depth the scheme can homomorphically evaluate, i.e. the scheme is circular-secure and its decryption function lies inside its evaluable circuit class.*

> **Proof sketch.** *Given a noisy ciphertext \(c\) encrypting \(m\), publish an encryption of the secret key \(s\) as a bootstrapping key. Homomorphically evaluate \(\mathrm{Dec}_s(c)\) to obtain \(c'\). Since \(\mathrm{Dec}_s(c) = m\), \(c'\) encrypts \(m\); since \(c'\) is a fresh output of the evaluation, its noise depends only on the evaluation's noise growth, which is bounded independently of \(c\)'s original noise.*

This thesis develops a unified noise-management account of all three paradigms, derives their concrete costs, and compares them on latency, throughput, precision, and key material.

##2 Background

### The RLWE noise model

In Ring-LWE schemes, a ciphertext encrypting a message \(m\) under secret \(s\) takes the form \((a, b)\) with \(b = \langle a, s\rangle + m + e\), where \(e\) is a small Gaussian error. The *noise* is \(e\); decryption succeeds when \(\|e\|_\infty < q/(2\Delta)\) for an appropriate scaling factor \(\Delta\). We define the **noise budget** as the number of further multiplicative levels the ciphertext can survive before the bound is violated.

### Modulus switching

BGV and BFV manage noise by **modulus switching**: mapping a ciphertext from modulus \(q\) to a smaller modulus \(q'\), which scales the noise down by approximately the factor \(q'/q\). Concretely, given \(c \bmod q\), one computes \(c' = \lfloor (q'/q)\, c \rceil \bmod q'\); the rounding introduces an error of at most one half per coefficient, which is absorbed provided \(q/q'\) is not too large [2]. Repeated switching produces the familiar *modulus chain* \(q_L > q_{L-1} > \cdots > q_0\), each multiplication consuming one level.

### Rescaling

CKKS replaces exact modulus switching with **rescaling**: after multiplication, the ciphertext is divided by the scaling factor \(\Delta\), which simultaneously reduces the modulus and the message magnitude, discarding low-order noise bits along with low-order message bits. This is why CKKS is *approximate*: the noise is reinterpreted as rounding error in the least significant bits of an approximate number [1]. The RNS variant performs all of this with 64-bit machine-word limbs, avoiding multi-precision arithmetic [1].

### Leveled versus fully homomorphic

A scheme provisioned with \(L\) levels evaluates circuits of depth at most \(L\). Bootstrapping resets the level counter, converting leveled into fully homomorphic computation. The central engineering question is the **refresh cost**: how much latency, throughput, level budget, and key material does one refresh consume?

| Scheme | Plaintext | Refresh strategy | Refresh granularity | Functional eval |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TFHE (CGGI) | torus bits / small integers | blind rotation | per gate | yes (LUT) |
| CKKS | complex vectors (approx.) | ModRaise + EvalMod | per ciphertext (slots) | via polynomials |
| BGV / BFV | integers / finite fields | digit extraction | per ciphertext | no |

##3 Methodology

We compare the three paradigms along six normalized metrics:

1. **Refresh latency** — wall-clock time of one bootstrapping operation at 128-bit security.
2. **Amortized throughput** — latency divided by the number of plaintext slots refreshed (batching).
3. **Level consumption** — how many multiplicative levels the bootstrap itself consumes (matters for CKKS, where the refresh is deep).
4. **Precision** — output noise / approximation error after refresh, in bits.
5. **Key material** — bootstrapping and key-switching key sizes.
6. **Failure probability** — probability the refresh decrypts incorrectly.

Parameters are normalized to the 128-bit security level using standard ring dimensions (TFHE: \(n \approx 630\), \(N = 1024\); CKKS/BGV: ring degree \(2^{15}\)–\(2^{16}\) with corresponding modulus chains). Performance figures are drawn from the comparative benchmark survey of Polyakov [2] and from scheme-specific optimization papers [3][4][5], cross-checked against the OpenFHE and HElib implementations those works evaluate. We additionally derive analytic noise bounds for the TFHE refresh and the CKKS EvalMod error, so the comparison rests on both theory and measurement.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 TFHE: Blind Rotation and Programmable Bootstrapping

TFHE works over the real torus \(\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\). An LWE ciphertext \((a, b) \in \mathbb{T}^n \times \mathbb{T}\) encrypts \(m\) as \(b = \langle a, s\rangle + m + e\). **Gate bootstrapping** proceeds in three steps [6]:

1. **Modulus switching.** Round \((a, b)\) from the torus to \(\mathbb{Z}_{2N}\), producing integer exponents \(a_i\).
2. **Blind rotation.** Start from a *test polynomial* \(\mathrm{testv} \in \mathbb{T}_N[X]\), whose coefficients encode a lookup table, and rotate it by \(X^{-\sum a_i s_i}\) using the bootstrapping key — encryptions of the secret bits \(s_i\) under a ring key. Each rotation multiplies the accumulator by \(X^{-a_i s_i}\), implemented with the external product against a GGSW encryption of \(s_i\). After processing all \(n+1\) components, the accumulator equals \(\mathrm{testv} \cdot X^{-\sum a_i s_i}\), i.e. the table rotated by (minus) the phase.
3. **Sample extraction.** The constant term of the accumulator is extracted as a fresh LWE sample encrypting the table entry at the rotated position, plus \((0, \hat m)\) offset. The output encrypts \(f(m)\) for whatever function \(f\) the table encodes.

The phase \(\varphi = b - \langle a, s\rangle\) satisfies \(\varphi \approx m\); the blind rotation moves the coefficient encoding \(f(\mathrm{round}(2N\varphi))\) to position zero. Because the rotation is *negacyclic* (\(X^N = -1\)), the table must be antiperiodic, which historically forced a **padding bit**: the plaintext space is halved so that the function and its negation do not collide. The WoP-PBS construction (PBS *without padding*) removes this constraint and supports larger precision by generalizing the test-vector layout and the modulus-switching chunking [3]. Multi-LUT variants evaluate several lookup tables in a single blind rotation, amortizing the dominant cost [3].

*Noise analysis.* The input noise \(e\) perturbs the rotation exponent; bootstrapping is correct when the rounded exponent lands on the intended table position, which holds with overwhelming probability when the input noise standard deviation is below roughly \(1/(4N\sqrt{n})\) in torus units. The *output* noise is independent of the input: it is the noise of one external product chain plus key switching, bounded by a fixed expression in the decomposition parameters [6]. This is the precise sense in which TFHE "resets" noise to a constant per gate.

```python
# Toy model: TFHE blind-rotation noise bound
import math

def tfhe_output_noise_sigma(n=630, N=1024, Bg=2**10, ell=3, sigma_bsk=2**-25):
    # external product noise per CMux gate, scaled by accumulator count
    gadget_noise = (n + 1) * ell * Bg * sigma_bsk
    keyswitch_noise = math.sqrt(N) * 2**-30
    return math.sqrt(gadget_noise**2 + keyswitch_noise**2)

def bootstrap_failure_prob(noise_sigma, half_gap=1/32):
    # tail bound: rotation error exceeds half the distance between table entries
    z = half_gap / noise_sigma
    return math.erfc(z / math.sqrt(2))

print(tfhe_output_noise_sigma())        # ~ fixed refresh noise
print(bootstrap_failure_prob(2**-20))    # negligible for sane parameters
```

### 4.2 CKKS: Approximate Bootstrapping as Homomorphic Sine Evaluation

CKKS bootstrapping refreshes an entire vector of slots at once through four stages [4][5]:

1. **ModRaise.** Lift the ciphertext from modulus \(q_0\) to a large modulus \(Q\). The plaintext becomes \(m + q_0 \cdot I\), where \(I\) is an unknown integer polynomial (the "wrap-around" multiple) with small coefficients.
2. **CoeffToSlot (C2S).** Homomorphically evaluate the inverse DFT, moving from coefficient encoding to slot encoding so that subsequent operations act slot-wise.
3. **EvalMod.** Evaluate the modular reduction \(t \mapsto t \bmod q_0\) homomorphically. Since modular reduction is discontinuous, it is approximated on the relevant interval by a scaled sine: \([t]_{q_0} \approx q_0 \cdot (t/q_0 - \lfloor t/q_0 \rceil)\), where the fractional part is approximated by a high-degree polynomial in \(\sin(2\pi t / q_0)\). This is the deep, level-hungry core — polynomial degrees in the hundreds, consuming many levels.
4. **SlotToCoeff (S2C).** Homomorphically evaluate the DFT to return to coefficient encoding, yielding a refreshed ciphertext at high level.

*Noise analysis.* Unlike exact schemes, CKKS bootstrapping does not return the noise to a fixed floor: the message is \(m + e_{\mathrm{in}}}\), and EvalMod adds an *approximation error* \(e_{\mathrm{approx}}\) from the sine polynomial plus the usual homomorphic noise. The output precision is roughly the minimum of the input precision and the approximation precision, typically 20–40 bits in practice [5]. The bootstrap itself consumes on the order of a dozen or more levels, so the *level budget* after refresh is smaller than the fresh budget — a fundamental inefficiency. **OverModRaise** attacks exactly this: by restructuring ModRaise and the C2S/S2C transforms, it reduces modulus consumption and reports up to 41 percent throughput improvement over prior state of the art [4].

An important alternative line avoids the sine approximation entirely: *bit-extraction* style CKKS bootstrapping that accumulates high-bit message information and subtracts low-bit noise information, working under standard (smaller) parameters at some efficiency cost [5]. Blind-rotation techniques imported from TFHE/FHEW are increasingly used to accelerate these variants [5].

### 4.3 BGV and BFV: Digit Extraction and Modulus-Switched Bootstrapping

BGV bootstrapping homomorphically evaluates the BGV decryption function, which consists of an inner product followed by rounding. The difficulty is the *modulus reduction* inside decryption. The classical solution represents the secret key digits in a large base and uses **digit extraction** — homomorphic evaluation of low-degree polynomials that extract the most significant base-\(p\) digit of each coefficient, effectively performing the rounding without branching [2].

Two flavors exist. *Thin* bootstrapping refreshes a ciphertext encrypting a single packed plaintext with minimal overhead; *thick* bootstrapping refreshes many slots but costs proportionally more. Both consume levels: the digit-extraction polynomials have depth logarithmic in the modulus, so BGV bootstrapping is typically invoked rarely, at the bottom of a deep modulus chain.

*Noise analysis.* BGV/BFV are exact schemes: after bootstrapping, the noise returns to a fixed fresh-encryption level, exactly as in TFHE, but the operation is far heavier per slot than a TFHE gate and far less frequent. There is **no programmable bootstrapping**: digit extraction evaluates only the decryption function itself, so arbitrary univariate functions must be approximated by polynomials *before* the refresh. This is the central expressivity gap versus TFHE's LUT-based PBS [2].

```rust
// Noise-budget accounting across a modulus chain (BGV/BFV style)
fn levels_remaining(q_bits: &[u32], noise_bits: f64, per_level_cost: f64) -> usize {
    // each modulus switch divides noise by ~2^{q_bits[i]-q_bits[i+1]};
    // multiplication then adds per_level_cost bits of noise
    let mut noise = noise_bits;
    let mut levels = 0;
    for w in q_bits.windows(2) {
        noise -= (w[0] - w[1]) as f64; // modulus switch shrinks noise
        noise += per_level_cost;       // one multiplication regrows it
        if noise > w[1] as f64 / 2.0 { break; } // budget exhausted
        levels += 1;
    }
    levels
}
```

### 4.4 Noise Management: Rescaling, Modulus Switching, and the Refresh Identity

All three paradigms can be read as answers to one question: *how is the noise budget restored?*

- **TFHE** restores the budget *operationally*: every gate ends with a blind rotation whose output noise is a fixed constant, so the budget is perpetually full. The cost is paid per gate (sequential external products, large bootstrapping keys).
- **CKKS** restores the budget *approximately*: ModRaise plus EvalMod re-encodes the message at a high level, but approximation error permanently degrades precision. The cost is paid in levels consumed and in precision lost.
- **BGV/BFV** restores the budget *exactly but rarely*: digit extraction under modulus switching returns noise to the fresh floor, at high per-refresh cost amortized over long level chains.

The unifying algebraic view is the **refresh identity**: bootstrapping evaluates \(\mathrm{Dec}\) homomorphically, so the output noise equals the noise of *evaluating the decryption circuit*, independent of the input noise. The three schemes differ only in what decryption looks like (torus phase rounding, modular reduction of a scaled message, digit extraction) and hence in the circuit that must be evaluated.

| Metric | TFHE (PBS) | CKKS | BGV / BFV |
|---|---|---|---|
| Refresh latency | ~10 ms (single key) [2] | seconds (per ciphertext) | seconds–minutes |
| Amortized throughput | low (per gate) | **highest** [2] | medium (packed ints) |
| Levels consumed by refresh | 0 (per-gate) | ~10–20 | logarithmic in modulus |
| Output precision | exact (small domain) | 20–40 bits approx. | exact |
| Functional bootstrapping | yes, arbitrary LUT | via polynomial approx. | no |
| Bootstrapping key size | tens of MB | hundreds of MB (rot. keys) | large (digit-extraction keys) |

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Benchmark landscape.** Polyakov's cross-scheme survey, evaluated on OpenFHE and HElib, reports that CKKS bootstrapping delivers the highest *throughput* and efficiently reaches large precision on real-number vectors typical of machine-learning workloads; the Ducas–Micciancio and CGGI (TFHE) schemes deliver the smallest *latency* — on the order of ten milliseconds per gate bootstrap at 128-bit security — and uniquely support programmable bootstrapping; BGV/BFV bootstrapping beats TFHE on throughput for vectors of small integers or finite-field elements but lacks functional bootstrapping [2]. Multi-key TFHE bootstrapping illustrates the scaling wall: an eight-party refresh exceeds 760 ms versus about 10 ms single-key, because blind rotation complexity grows with the number of keys [2]. On the CKKS side, OverModRaise's restructuring of ModRaise and the slot transforms yields up to 41 percent throughput gain [4].

> **Theorem (TFHE refresh noise bound).** *Let a TFHE gate bootstrap use decomposition base \(B_g\) with \(\ell\) levels, bootstrapping-key noise variance \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BSK}}^2\), and key-switching noise variance \(\sigma_{\mathrm{KS}}^2\). Then the output LWE noise variance satisfies \(\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}^2 \le (n+1)\,\ell\,B_g^2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{BSK}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{KS}}^2\), independent of the input noise, provided the input noise keeps the rounded phase within half a table interval.*

> **Proof.** *Each of the \(n+1\) external products contributes at most \(\ell B_g^2 \sigma_{\mathrm{BSK}}^2\) by the gadget-decomposition noise lemma; the accumulator noises add because the products are sequential and independent. Sample extraction is noiseless (a projection), and the final key switch adds \(\sigma_{\mathrm{KS}}^2\). The input noise appears only in the rotation exponent, which is rounded before use; conditioned on correct rounding, it does not propagate. ∎*

**CKKS EvalMod error.** If the sine-approximation polynomial has uniform error \(\epsilon\) on the target interval and the wrap-around multiple \(I\) satisfies \(\|I\|_\infty \le K\), the post-bootstrap message error is bounded by \(q_0 \epsilon + e_{\mathrm{HE}}\), where \(e_{\mathrm{HE}}\) is the homomorphic-evaluation noise of the polynomial itself. Choosing \(K\) (hence the ModRaise modulus) trades level consumption against the probability that \(I\) escapes the approximation interval — the *bootstrap failure* event [4][5].

##6 Limitations

**TFHE.** Blind rotation is inherently sequential over the \(n+1\) secret components, limiting parallelization; the per-gate cost makes deep arithmetic circuits expensive relative to batched schemes. Parameters are delicate: excessive input noise causes silent table-misalignment failures, so noise must be tracked across the circuit [6]. Large-precision PBS (beyond roughly 8–12 bits per bootstrap) requires the WoP-PBS machinery and multiple bootstraps per operation [3].

**CKKS.** Bootstrapping is approximate: precision is capped by the EvalMod polynomial, and each refresh consumes many levels, shrinking the usable budget — the scheme pays for unbounded depth with permanently reduced headroom. The RNS implementation, while 64-bit clean, needs large evaluation and rotation keys. Approximate decryption also complicates applications requiring exact comparisons or branching, which must themselves be polynomial-approximated [5].

**BGV/BFV.** Bootstrapping is so expensive that it is used sparingly, which forces very deep modulus chains and large parameters for deep circuits. The absence of programmable bootstrapping means non-polynomial functions (comparisons, ReLU, transcendental functions) demand costly polynomial approximations evaluated *outside* the refresh. Digit-extraction keys add substantial memory overhead.

**Common.** All three face a hardware gap: bootstrapping is dominated by NTTs and external products that map poorly to CPUs; GPU and FPGA accelerators are narrowing but not closing the gap. Key sizes from tens to hundreds of megabytes strain deployment on constrained devices.

---

##7 Conclusion

Bootstrapping is a single idea — homomorphic decryption as noise refresh — realized through three incompatible engineering compromises. **TFHE** buys minimal latency and arbitrary functional evaluation with per-gate sequential blind rotations; **CKKS** buys maximal amortized throughput on approximate real arithmetic with level-hungry sine-polynomial refreshes; **BGV/BFV** buy exactness with rare, heavy digit-extraction refreshes under modulus switching. The refresh identity unifies their noise analyses: output noise equals evaluation noise of the decryption circuit, independent of input noise. Future progress lies at the intersections already emerging — blind-rotation techniques accelerating CKKS bootstrapping [5], padding-free PBS extending TFHE precision [3], and modulus-frugal CKKS refreshes [4] — suggesting a convergence toward refreshes that are simultaneously fast, amortized, and expressive. For practitioners, the decision rule is crisp: gate-level latency and arbitrary functions point to TFHE; batched real-number throughput points to CKKS; exact packed-integer arithmetic points to BGV/BFV.
