# Randomized Numerical Linear Algebra: Johnson–Lindenstrauss Sketching, CUR Decompositions, and Low-Rank Approximation

## Abstract

Randomized numerical linear algebra (RandNLA) has reshaped how large-scale linear-algebraic computation is performed, replacing the dense, exact orthogonal factorizations of classical numerical analysis with probabilistic primitives — *sketching*, *range finding*, and *column/row sampling* — that trade minuscule, rigorously quantified losses of accuracy for dramatic gains in speed, memory locality, and parallelizability. This thesis develops the modern theory from first principles. We begin with the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma and its algorithmic refinement into *oblivious subspace embeddings*: Gaussian maps, subsampled randomized Hadamard transforms (SRHT), CountSketch, and OSNAP, each achieving the (1 ± ε) norm-preservation property with an explicit embedding dimension and apply time. We then present the randomized singular value decomposition in the Halko–Martinsson–Tropp framework, proving expected-error bounds and describing the power-iteration refinement that tames slowly decaying spectra. We analyze CUR and interpolative decompositions built on leverage-score sampling, obtaining (1 + ε) relative-error Frobenius bounds via the Drineas–Mahoney–Muthukrishnan program [4], and we treat sketch-and-solve least squares through input-sparsity embeddings [3]. Empirical evidence and complexity tables are presented throughout, demonstrating that sketching reduces O(mnk) dense work to O(mn log k) or O(nnz(A)) regimes while preserving solution quality, thereby explaining the adoption of randomized methods in data science, scientific computing, and machine learning pipelines.

## 1 Introduction

For most of the twentieth century, numerical linear algebra was dominated by deterministic, orthogonal factorizations: the QR decomposition, the singular value decomposition (SVD), and Gaussian elimination with pivoting. These algorithms are backward stable, exquisitely well understood, and — for the problem sizes they were designed for — optimal. But the data matrices of the twenty-first century routinely have 10^6–10^9 rows and comparable column counts; classical dense algorithms whose running times scale as O(mn·min(m, n)) or whose memory footprints demand many passes over the data are simply no longer viable. The field's response, crystallizing around 2006–2011, was to import ideas from theoretical computer science and randomized algorithms: rather than computing the *exact* best low-rank approximation, we compute a *probabilistically near-exact* one, orders of magnitude faster.

Three intellectual strands fused into modern RandNLA. The first is the **Johnson–Lindenstrauss (JL) lemma** [1], which states that n points in arbitrary dimension can be embedded into O(ε^−2 log n) dimensions by a random linear map while preserving all pairwise distances within (1 ± ε). The second is **column-sampling and low-rank approximation theory**: Drineas, Kannan, and Mahoney showed in a series of papers beginning in 2006 that sampling columns proportionally to their squared norms (and later, to their *leverage scores*) yields additive-error and then relative-error approximations to the SVD and to matrix products [5]. The third is the **randomized range finder** of Halko, Martinsson, and Tropp [2], which identifies a subspace capturing "most of the action" of a matrix via a single random projection, then compresses the matrix to that subspace before doing exact small-scale computation.

> **Theorem:** *(Randomized SVD framework, informal).* Let A ∈ ℝ^{m×n} and let k be the target rank. With an oversampling parameter p ≥ 2 and Ω a Gaussian random matrix, Algorithm 1 (below) returns a rank-(k+p) approximation Â satisfying ‖A − Â‖ ≤ (1 + 15√((k+p)/(p−1))) · σ_{k+1}(A) in expectation, where σ_{k+1} is the (k+1)-st singular value. Power iteration improves the bound to decay as (σ_{k+1}/σ_k)^{2q+1} for q iterations.

What makes this theorem remarkable is that the error is measured against the *optimal* rank-k approximation A_k (whose error is exactly σ_{k+1}), the constant is small and explicit, and the algorithm needs only a constant number of passes over A. This thesis develops these ideas in full: the geometry of sketching (§2), the construction of subspace embeddings (§3), randomized range finding and the SVD (### 4.1), CUR and interpolative decompositions (### 4.2), leverage-score sampling (### 4.3), and least-squares sketching (### 4.4), followed by empirical results, proofs (§5), limitations (§6), and conclusions (§7).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Singular Value Decomposition and Low-Rank Approximation

The SVD A = UΣV^T, with U ∈ ℝ^{m×r}, V ∈ ℝ^{n×r} orthonormal and Σ = diag(σ_1 ≥ σ_2 ≥ ⋯ ≥ σ_r > 0), provides the optimal rank-k approximation A_k = U_k Σ_k V_k^T in every unitarily invariant norm [2]. Its cost — O(mn·min(m,n)) via Golub–Kahan bidiagonalization — is the baseline that randomized methods must beat. Two facts from matrix analysis underpin everything that follows:

- **Range invariance:** A_k = P_{U_k}A, where P_{U_k} is the orthogonal projector onto the top-k left singular subspace. Any orthonormal Q whose range *contains* U_k yields A ≈ QQ^T A with near-optimal error.
- **Pythagorean splitting:** ‖A − QQ^T A‖_F^2 = ‖(I − QQ^T)UΣV^T‖_F^2 ≤ ‖Σ_2‖_F^2 + ‖(I − QQ^T)U_1‖_F^2 · ‖Σ_1‖_F^2, decoupling the *tail* (irreducible) error from the *subspace-estimation* error. Randomization controls the latter.

### 2.2 Concentration and Random Projections

The JL lemma [1] rests on the concentration of Lipschitz functions of Gaussian (or sub-Gaussian) random vectors: if G ∈ ℝ^{s×d} has i.i.d. N(0,1/s) entries and x is fixed, then ‖Gx‖_2^2 concentrates sharply around ‖x‖_2^2, with deviations decaying exponentially in s. A union bound over a finite set of points then yields the classic dimension s = O(ε^−2 log n). For *subspace* embedding — preserving ‖Ax‖ for all x simultaneously, i.e., for a d-dimensional subspace — the union bound is replaced by a net argument over the unit sphere, producing s = O(ε^−2 d log d) for dense sub-Gaussian maps, later improved to s = Θ(ε^−2 d) for optimal constructions [3]. The essential trade-off is between embedding dimension, failure probability δ, distortion ε, and the time to apply the map — the subject of §3.

### 2.3 Computational Models

Classical flop counts assume dense matrices. Modern complexity accounting instead centers on **nnz(A)** (the number of nonzero entries), the number of *passes* over data stored on slow media, and cache/multicore behavior. Dense Gaussian maps cost O(mn·s) to apply — sometimes *slower* than a classical algorithm. Hence the field's obsession with *fast* embeddings: SRHT applies in O(mn log s) time, while CountSketch achieves the theoretically optimal O(nnz(A)) time [3]. These distinctions are not asymptotic trivia; they determine whether sketching wins in practice.

## 3 Methodology

The methodological core of RandNLA is a three-stage pattern that recurs across applications:

1. **Sketch:** compress A into SA (or AS), where S is a randomized embedding with favorable dimension, apply time, and distortion guarantees.
2. **Solve the small problem:** perform exact classical computation (SVD, QR, solve) on the sketch, whose size depends only on the intrinsic rank/dimension, not on m, n.
3. **Lift back:** transfer the small solution to the original space, often with one more sketch for validation.

Our exposition follows this pattern. We define an *oblivious ℓ_2 subspace embedding* precisely, survey the major constructions with their guarantees, and then deploy them inside the randomized SVD, CUR, and least-squares pipelines. Throughout, "oblivious" means the distribution of S is independent of A — crucial for streaming and single-pass settings, where S can be generated before seeing the data.

> **Definition:** *(ε-subspace embedding).* Let A ∈ ℝ^{m×n} have rank at most d. A random matrix S ∈ ℝ^{s×m} is an (ε, δ)-oblivious ℓ_2 subspace embedding if, with probability at least 1 − δ over the draw of S, ‖SAx‖_2 = (1 ± ε)‖Ax‖_2 for *all* x ∈ ℝ^n simultaneously.

Equivalently, (SA)^T(SA) ≈_ε A^T A in the Löwner order: (1−ε)A^T A ⪯ (SA)^T(SA) ⪯ (1+ε)A^T A. This *spectral approximation* property is what makes embeddings composable — chaining two embeddings multiplies their distortions — and it is the single most-used fact in RandNLA algorithm design [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Randomized SVD: The Halko–Martinsson–Tropp Framework

The randomized SVD proceeds in two stages. First, **range finding**: draw Ω ∈ ℝ^{n×(k+p)} with i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries and form Y = AΩ; orthonormalize to obtain Q. Second, **small factorization**: form the tiny (k+p)×n matrix B = Q^T A, compute its SVD B = ŨΣV^T, and set U = QŨ [2].

```python
import numpy as np

def randomized_svd(A, k, p=10, q=2):
    """Halko-Martinsson-Tropp randomized SVD with power iterations."""
    m, n = A.shape
    Omega = np.random.randn(n, k + p)          # stage 1: sketch
    Y = A @ Omega
    for _ in range(q):                          # power iterations
        Y = A @ (A.T @ Y)
    Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(Y)                      # orthonormal basis
    B = Q.T @ A                                 # stage 2: compress
    Utilde, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(B, full_matrices=False)
    return (Q @ Utilde)[:, :k], s[:k], Vt[:k, :] # rank-k truncation
```

The analysis decomposes cleanly. Let Q be the orthonormal factor of Y and write the error as ‖A − QQ^T A‖. The key structural lemma [2, Theorem 10.5] shows that for *any* deterministic projector P, ‖A − QQ^T A‖ ≤ ‖(I − P)Σ_1 V_1^T Ω (V_2^T Ω)^†‖ + ‖(I−P)Σ_2‖, and averaging over Ω yields the bound quoted in §1. Oversampling (p ≈ 10) makes the pseudoinverse term benign; power iteration multiplies the effective spectral gap to the (2q+1)-st power, so even matrices with flat spectra converge [7]. For dense A the total cost is O(mn log k) versus O(mnk) for classical partial SVD — the speedup is roughly a factor of k, the very quantity being computed.

### 4.2 CUR and Interpolative Decompositions

While the SVD produces factors that are dense linear combinations of rows/columns, many applications (genetics, text mining, recommender systems) demand *interpretable* factors expressed in terms of actual data. The **CUR decomposition** writes A ≈ CUR, where C contains a small number of actual columns of A, R contains actual rows, and U is a small connector matrix [4]. Formally, given index sets J (columns) and I (rows), C = A(:,J), R = A(I,:), W = A(I,J), and U = W^† (the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse), with A ≈ C W^† R. The *interpolative decomposition* is the special case A ≈ C X with X = (C^+ A) containing a k×k identity submatrix, so that the factorization *interpolates* the selected columns exactly.

The randomized column-selection program of Drineas, Mahoney, and Muthukrishnan [4] achieves the gold standard:

> **Theorem:** *(Relative-error CUR [4]).* Given A ∈ ℝ^{m×n} and rank parameter k, there is an algorithm selecting c = O(k^2 ε^−2 log(k/ε)) columns C and r = O(c ε^−2 log c) rows R such that, with probability at least 1 − δ, ‖A − CUR‖_F ≤ (1 + ε)‖A − A_k‖_F.

The proof is a two-stage sampling argument. **Stage 1** selects columns with probabilities proportional to the *leverage scores* ℓ_j = ‖(V_k)_j‖_2^2 of the top-k right singular subspace, guaranteeing ‖A − CC^+A‖_F ≤ (1+ε)‖A − A_k‖_F. **Stage 2** samples rows of [C; A] adaptively to bound ‖A − CC^+AR^+R‖_F. The crucial innovation is that sampling *exactly* proportionally to leverage scores requires knowing V_k — but leverage scores can be *approximated* in O(nnz(A) log n) + poly(k) time via sketching [3], making the whole pipeline sublinear in the dense cost of A. Later work reduced the column count to O(k ε^−1 log k) (Boutsidis–Woodruff–Zhong) and the row count likewise, approaching the information-theoretic limit.

### 4.3 Leverage-Score Sampling and Statistical Leverage

The *leverage score* of row i of A (with orthonormal basis U ∈ ℝ^{m×d}) is h_i = ‖U_{i,:}‖_2^2; ∑_i h_i = d. Leverage scores measure how influential each row is on the column space — uniform sampling fails catastrophically on matrices where a single row carries the signal (consider a diagonal matrix with one huge entry). Sampling rows with probability p_i ∝ h_i and rescaling by 1/√(s p_i) yields a subspace embedding with s = O(d log d/ε^2), via a matrix Bernstein argument [6]. Moreover, the scores themselves can be *estimated* by a fast sketch: compute SA with a CountSketch S, take the QR of the small SA = QR̃, and read off approximate leverage scores from R̃ applied to a second sketch — achieving constant-factor approximations to all scores in O(nnz(A) log n) time [3]. This closes the loop: sketching makes leverage sampling practical, and leverage sampling yields interpretable approximations.

### 4.4 Subspace Embeddings: The Sketch Zoo

| Method | Dimension s (for d-dim subspace) | Apply time to A | Oblivious? | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dense Gaussian | O(ε^−2 d) | O(mn·s) | Yes | Optimal dimension; slow apply |
| SRHT (Ailon–Chazelle) | O(ε^−2 d log d) | O(mn log s) | Yes | Needs n a power of 2 (pad); great in practice |
| CountSketch (Clarkson–Woodruff) | O(ε^−2 d^2) (improved: O(ε^−2 d log d)) | **O(nnz(A))** | Yes | Optimal apply time; larger dimension |
| OSNAP (Nelson–Nguyên) | O(ε^−2 d log d) | O(nnz(A)·log d) | Yes | Column-sparsity trade-off |
| Leverage-score sampling | O(ε^−2 d log d) | O(nnz(A) log n) + poly | No (data-dependent) | Interpretable; needs score estimates |

The engineering consensus [7] is pragmatic: **CountSketch for the first (large) sketch** because of its O(nnz) apply cost, followed by a dense Gaussian or SRHT *second* sketch to reduce the CountSketch dimension down to the optimal O(ε^−2 d) — the "sketch of a sketch." The composition lemma for subspace embeddings guarantees the product is still an embedding with distortion (1±ε_1)(1±ε_2). This two-level design is what production implementations (e.g., in RandLAPACK and the `scipy.sparse` ecosystem) actually deploy.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Proof Sketch: Randomized Range Finder Error Bound

We sketch the proof of the expected-error bound from [2, Theorem 10.6], the field's central result. Let A = UΣV^T, partition V = [V_1 V_2] with V_1 ∈ ℝ^{n×k}, and write Ω_1 = V_1^T Ω, Ω_2 = V_2^T Ω. Assuming Ω_1 has full row rank (probability 1 for Gaussian Ω), define the (non-orthonormal) approximator Z = AΩ Ω_1^† V_1^T; then range(Z) ⊆ range(AΩ) = range(Q), so ‖A − QQ^T A‖ ≤ ‖A − Z‖. Expanding,

‖A − Z‖ ≤ ‖Σ_2 Ω_2 Ω_1^†‖ + ‖Σ_2‖,

where the inequality uses ‖(I − ·)Σ_1‖ structure and unitary invariance. Taking expectations: E[‖Σ_2 Ω_2 Ω_1^†‖_F^2] = E[‖Ω_1^†‖_F^2]·‖Σ_2‖_F^2 by independence of Ω_1, Ω_2 and rotational invariance of the Gaussian, and E[‖Ω_1^†‖_F^2] = k/(p−1) for p ≥ 2 (an exact inverse-Wishart calculation). Jensen's inequality converts the Frobenius bound to the spectral bound quoted in §1, completing the proof. ∎

### Empirical Validation

The randomized SVD has been validated extensively [2,7]: on matrices with exponentially decaying spectra (σ_j = 2^−j), even q = 0 power iterations with p = 10 oversampling reproduce the first k singular values to machine precision; on flat-spectrum matrices (σ_j = 1/j), q = 2–3 iterations close the gap to the deterministic SVD's error. In timing studies on 10^5 × 10^5 sparse matrices, sketch-based rank-k approximation runs 5–20× faster than ARPACK-style Krylov methods at matched accuracy, with the advantage growing on multicore hardware because the sketch phase (Y = AΩ) is embarrassingly parallel [7]. For least squares, Clarkson–Woodruff's input-sparsity algorithm [3] solves n × d systems (n ≫ d) in O(nnz(A)) + poly(d/ε) time — essentially the cost of *reading* the data — versus O(nd^2) for a dense QR, and their experiments show it is competitive with highly optimized LAPACK on sparse instances. CUR decompositions on gene-expression and document-term matrices confirm the (1+ε) relative-error behavior and, importantly, recover semantically meaningful columns (marker genes, topical documents) that the SVD's dense factors obscure [4].

## 6 Limitations

No honest account of RandNLA can omit its failure modes.

- **Constants and the ε^−2 tax.** JL-type bounds scale as ε^−2; for ε = 0.1 this is a factor of 100, and the constants in front (from net arguments) are often large. Sketching is a tool for *moderate* accuracy — relative error 10^−2 to 10^−6 — not for the 10^−12 accuracy of double-precision LAPACK. High-precision iterative refinement on top of a sketch is possible but delicate [6].
- **Tall-skinny vs. general matrices.** Most sketching theory targets n ≫ d (regression) or low-rank approximation. General square linear systems, eigenvalue problems, and structured factorizations (e.g., preserving positive semidefiniteness) require additional machinery, and naive sketching can destroy structure that matters downstream.
- **Failure probability and adversarial inputs.** Guarantees hold with probability 1 − δ over the random sketch; derandomization is largely open. Oblivious sketches are robust to *fixed* inputs but not to inputs chosen *after* seeing the sketch — a real concern in adaptive optimization loops, where fresh randomness must be drawn each iteration.
- **Communication and pass efficiency.** Single-pass and streaming algorithms exist [2], but power iteration costs 2q+1 passes; on data too large for fast memory, each pass is expensive, and the accuracy–pass trade-off can favor classical Krylov methods in the few-pass regime.
- **Interpretability vs. optimality.** CUR's relative-error bounds are weaker in the constants and sample complexity than the SVD's exact optimality; choosing C, R columns well requires approximate leverage scores, whose own estimation introduces error. In practice the gap is small [7], but theory lags.

## 7 Conclusion

Randomized numerical linear algebra represents a genuine paradigm shift: by injecting carefully calibrated randomness at the *front* of a computation — through subspace embeddings, random range finders, and leverage-score sampling — we reduce problems that once demanded O(mnk) dense arithmetic and O(k) data passes to regimes of O(nnz(A)) work and constant passes, while sacrificing only a rigorously bounded, typically negligible, factor in accuracy. The three pillars surveyed here — the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma and its fast oblivious embeddings (Gaussian, SRHT, CountSketch, OSNAP) [1,3,6]; the Halko–Martinsson–Tropp randomized SVD with its sharp expected-error theory [2]; and the CUR/interpolative decomposition program with relative-error guarantees via leverage-score sampling [4,5] — together form a coherent algorithmic toolkit whose adoption in scientific computing, data analysis, and machine learning continues to accelerate. The practical lesson, validated across implementations [7], is a two-level sketching discipline: a sparse first sketch for speed, a dense second sketch for dimension optimality, and exact classical computation on the result. Open challenges — high-precision sketching, structure preservation, derandomization, and tighter sample complexity for CUR — ensure that the next decade of RandNLA will be as productive as the last.
