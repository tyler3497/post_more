# The Chomsky Hierarchy Revisited: Formal Language Theory from Pumping Lemmas to Mildly Context-Sensitive Grammars and the Structure of Natural Language

## Abstract

This thesis develops a unified account of the Chomsky hierarchy — the classification of formal grammars into regular, context-free, context-sensitive, and unrestricted families — and its central role in computational linguistics. Beginning with Noam Chomsky's 1956 formulation of rewrite grammars and their automata-theoretic counterparts, we reconstruct the strict containment chain that organizes formal languages by generative power, and survey the classical correspondence between production restrictions and machine models. We then give detailed treatments of the pumping lemmas for regular and context-free languages, the principal diagnostic instruments for separating the classes, and prove the Chomsky–Schützenberger representation theorem, which exhibits every context-free language as a homomorphic image of the intersection of a Dyck language with a regular language. The second half confronts the hierarchy with natural language: we recount Shieber's 1985 proof that Swiss-German cross-serial dependencies refute context-freeness, and show how tree-adjoining grammars and combinatory categorial grammars occupy the mildly context-sensitive region — strictly beyond context-free yet polynomially parsable. We close on the open question of exactly where natural languages sit.

## 1 Intro

Few ideas in computer science have the organizing power of the **Chomsky hierarchy**. Proposed by Noam Chomsky in the mid-1950s, it classifies formal grammars into four nested types — *regular*, *context-free*, *context-sensitive*, and *unrestricted* — by restricting the shape of their productions, and shows that each restriction corresponds precisely to a class of abstract machines [1]. The hierarchy was born from linguistics: Chomsky's original motivation was to characterize the generative capacity required for natural language syntax, and his first major result in the area was that English is not a finite-state (regular) language [1]. Yet the theory rapidly outgrew its linguistic cradle to become the backbone of compiler design, complexity theory, and the mathematical study of computation itself.

The hierarchy raises two kinds of questions. *Internal* questions concern the mathematics of the classes: how do we prove strict separation, what canonical representatives do the classes admit, and which operations preserve membership? *External* questions concern placement: where, in this tower of expressive power, do actual natural languages live? The second question turned out to be far harder than the first. It took nearly three decades of research — culminating in the mid-1980s with the Swiss-German arguments of Huybregts, Shieber, and Culy — to show that natural languages are not context-free, and the effort gave birth to the entire subfield of **mildly context-sensitive** grammar formalisms [6][7].

This thesis proceeds as follows: Section 2 fixes the hierarchy and its machine correspondence; Section 3 describes the proof-theoretic methodology of pumping arguments, closure properties, and representation theorems; Section 4 dives deep into the containment structure, the pumping lemmas, the Chomsky–Schützenberger theorem, and mild context-sensitivity; Section 5 works through the Swiss-German proof and surveys parsing complexity; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

> **Thesis statement:** The Chomsky hierarchy is not merely a taxonomy of string sets but a theory of *structure*: its classes are separated by canonical combinatorial invariants (pumping, growth, semilinearity), unified by representation theorems, and empirically probed by natural language — which sits, tantalizingly, just outside context-freeness.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Grammars, productions, and the four types

A **grammar** is a tuple *G = (N, T, P, S)* where *N* is a finite set of nonterminals, *T* a finite set of terminals disjoint from *N*, *P* a finite set of productions *α → β* with *α ∈ (N ∪ T)* containing at least one nonterminal and *β ∈ (N ∪ T)\**, and *S ∈ N* the start symbol. The **language** *L(G)* is the set of terminal strings derivable from *S*. Chomsky's classification restricts the *form* of productions [1]:

| Type | Name | Production form | Recognizer | Membership problem |
|------|------|----------------|------------|--------------------|
| 3 | Regular | *A → aB*, *A → a* | Finite automaton (DFA/NFA) | Linear, *O(n)* |
| 2 | Context-free | *A → α* (single nonterminal LHS) | Pushdown automaton (PDA) | Polynomial, *O(n³)* |
| 1 | Context-sensitive | *αAβ → αγβ*, *\|γ\| ≥ 1* (non-contracting) | Linear bounded automaton | PSPACE-complete |
| 0 | Unrestricted | *α → β* (no restriction) | Turing machine | Undecidable (RE) |

The inclusions are strict: every regular language is context-free, every context-free language is context-sensitive, and every context-sensitive language is recursively enumerable — with canonical witnesses separating each level [1]. Type-1 grammars are required to be non-contracting (*\|α\| ≤ \|β\|*), which is what makes the membership problem decidable: derivations of a string of length *n* never need sentential forms longer than *n*, so a linear bounded automaton can explore them in bounded space.

### 2.2 Why the hierarchy matters to linguistics

Chomsky's original argument that English is not regular used *center embedding*: constructions of the form "the rat the cat chased ate the cheese" exhibit unbounded nested dependencies of the pattern *aⁿbⁿ*, and a finite automaton — which has only finitely many states and hence no unbounded counter — cannot recognize them [1]. This style of argument, ruling out a class by exhibiting a dependency pattern it cannot express, became the template for all subsequent placement results, culminating in the Swiss-German refutation of context-freeness in 1985 [6].

### 2.3 Dyck languages and canonical representatives

The canonical context-free languages are the **Dyck languages** *Dₖ* of correctly balanced parentheses over *k* bracket types — the archetypal non-regular CFL. Their significance is not merely pedagogical: the Chomsky–Schützenberger theorem shows they are *universal* for the class in a precise sense (Section 4.3) [3][5].

## 3 Methodology

Our method is the classical proof-theoretic toolkit of formal language theory, applied in three stages:

1. **Separation by pumping.** To show a language *L* is not in class *C*, we assume membership and derive a contradiction from the *pumping lemma* of *C* — a combinatorial invariant stating that long strings in *C* contain a pumpable segment. The regular pumping lemma handles finite-state limits; the context-free pumping lemma (Bar-Hillel, Perles, and Shamir) handles the single-stack limit. These lemmas are *necessary* conditions, so failure to pump refutes membership.
2. **Separation by closure.** Classes are closed under operations (homomorphism, intersection with regular languages, inverse homomorphism for CFLs). Shieber's Swiss-German proof is the exemplar: assuming Swiss German is context-free, apply a homomorphism and a regular intersection — both closure operations — to obtain a language known to be non-context-free, a contradiction [6].
3. **Unification by representation.** Rather than treating each CFL as sui generis, the Chomsky–Schützenberger theorem reduces the entire class to two ingredients — a Dyck language and a regular language — combined by intersection and homomorphism [3][5]. Modern work extends this representation quantitatively (weighted languages over semirings) [2] and categorically (grammars as functors of multicategories, parse-tree shapes as contour words) [3].

Throughout, we focus on *weak generative capacity* — claims about string sets, which are what the hierarchy adjudicates — turning to structural (strong) capacity only where TAG's motivation demands it.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The containment tower and its machine mirror

The deepest fact about the hierarchy is the **Chomsky machine correspondence**: each production restriction matches a resource-bounded machine.

- *Type 3 ↔ finite automata.* A right-linear grammar's nonterminals are exactly the states of an NFA; productions *A → aB* are transitions.
- *Type 2 ↔ pushdown automata.* A single stack supplies the unbounded memory that finite control lacks, enabling the nested *aⁿbⁿ* dependencies.
- *Type 1 ↔ linear bounded automata.* The non-contracting condition means derivations fit in space linear in the input — the machine never needs more tape than the input occupies.
- *Type 0 ↔ Turing machines.* Unrestricted rewriting is computation itself; the halting problem makes membership undecidable.

> **Theorem:** The inclusions REG ⊂ CFL ⊂ CSL ⊂ RE are all strict.

*Proof sketch.* *{aⁿbⁿ}* is context-free but not regular (regular pumping lemma). *{aⁿbⁿcⁿ}* is context-sensitive but not context-free (CFL pumping lemma). *{aⁿ : n is prime}* or the set of valid Turing machine computations are RE but not context-sensitive (a counting/diagonal argument: CSL membership is decidable, so any undecidable RE language separates the classes). ∎

A Haskell encoding makes the grammar side concrete without any low-level machinery:

```haskell
-- Sentential forms and one-step derivation
type Symbol = Either String String   -- Left: nonterminal, Right: terminal

data Grammar = Grammar
  { nonterminals :: [String]
  , terminals    :: [String]
  , productions  :: [([Symbol], [Symbol])]
  , start        :: String
  }

-- A production is context-free iff its LHS is a single nonterminal
isContextFree :: Grammar -> Bool
isContextFree g = all lhsOk (productions g)
  where lhsOk (lhs, _) = case lhs of
          [Left _] -> True
          _        -> False

-- A production is context-sensitive iff it is non-contracting
isNonContracting :: Grammar -> Bool
isNonContracting g = all ok (productions g)
  where ok (lhs, rhs) = length lhs <= length rhs
```

The point is philosophical as much as technical: *restricting syntax restricts computation*, and the four classical machine models are what you get when you ask what memory a restricted rewriter needs.

### 4.2 Pumping lemmas: the anatomy of a separation

Pumping lemmas convert the *resource limits* of a machine class into *combinatorial properties* of its languages.

> **Theorem (Pumping lemma for regular languages):** For every regular language *L* there is a pumping length *p ≥ 1* such that every *s ∈ L* with *\|s\| ≥ p* splits as *s = xyz* with *\|xy\| ≤ p*, *\|y\| > 0*, and *xyⁱz ∈ L* for all *i ≥ 0*.

The proof is the pigeonhole principle: a DFA with *p* states, reading *p* symbols, revisits a state; the loop between visits is *y*, pumpable forever. The classic application: *{aⁿbⁿ}* is not regular, since any split puts *y* inside one block (by *\|xy\| ≤ p*), and pumping unbalances the counts.

> **Theorem (Pumping lemma for context-free languages):** For every CFL *L* there is a pumping length *p* such that every *s ∈ L* with *\|s\| ≥ p* splits as *s = uvwxy* with *\|vwx\| ≤ p*, *\|vx\| > 0*, and *uvⁱwxⁱy ∈ L* for all *i ≥ 0*.

Here the pigeonhole argument climbs a *parse tree*: in a grammar in Chomsky normal form, a tree yielding a long string has a long root-to-leaf path, so some nonterminal repeats along it. The two pumped segments *v* and *x* sit in the two subtrees of the repeated nonterminal — hence the *twin* pumping that preserves nesting. The classic application: *{aⁿbⁿcⁿ}* is not context-free, because *v* and *x* together can span at most two of the three blocks (by *\|vwx\| ≤ p*), so pumping destroys the triple equality.

Both lemmas are one-directional: satisfying the conclusion never *proves* membership. They are refutation instruments, and refutation is exactly what the placement program needs.

### 4.3 The Chomsky–Schützenberger representation theorem

If the pumping lemmas are the hierarchy's scalpels, the **Chomsky–Schützenberger theorem** is its keystone — a single structural fact from which the whole context-free class can be rebuilt.

> **Theorem (Chomsky–Schützenberger representation):** A language *L* is context-free if and only if there exist a matched alphabet *T ∪ T̄*, the Dyck language *D_T* of correctly nested brackets over it, a regular language *R*, and a homomorphism *h* such that *L = h(D_T ∩ R)* [3][5].

The intuition is beautiful. Take any CFG *G*. Its derivations have *shapes* — parse trees — and the contour of a parse tree (walk around it, recording an opening bracket when descending into a node and a closing bracket when ascending) is a Dyck word: every descent is matched by an ascent. Collect all such contours into a Dyck language over an alphabet whose bracket types are the productions of *G*; then a finite automaton *R* checks that adjacent brackets correspond to compatible productions (parent–child nonterminal agreement), exactly like a PDA's finite control checking its stack discipline. Finally, the homomorphism *h* reads off the terminal yield, erasing the structural brackets. Thus: **every CFL is a relabeled, regular-filtered Dyck language** [5].

Droste and Vogler proved a *quantitative* version for weighted languages over semirings and lattices, showing they coincide with the languages of weighted pushdown automata [2]. Melliès and Zeilberger gave a *categorical* proof, viewing CFGs as functors of multicategories and replacing Dyck words by tree contour words — revealing clean categorical explanations for both the regular-intersection closure step and the universality of Dyck languages [3].

A compact Python demonstration of the Dyck-intersection idea:

```python
def dyck_words(bracket_pairs, max_pairs):
    # generate all correctly nested words up to max_pairs pairs (Catalan objects)
    results = set()
    def extend(open_stack, built, pairs_used):
        if pairs_used == max_pairs and not open_stack:
            results.add(tuple(built))
            return
        if pairs_used < max_pairs:
            for o, c in bracket_pairs:
                extend(open_stack + [c], built + [o], pairs_used + 1)
        if open_stack:
            extend(open_stack[:-1], built + [open_stack[-1]], pairs_used)
    extend([], [], 0)
    return results

def homomorphic_image(words, h):
    # h maps each symbol to a string; extend homomorphically
    return {tuple(sym for w in word for sym in h[w]) for word in words}

pairs = [("(", ")"), ("[", "]")]
D = dyck_words(pairs, 3)
h = {"(": ("a",), ")": ("a",), "[": ("b",), "]": ("b",)}
image = homomorphic_image(D, h)
"""Every image word has the form a^(2i) b^(2j): a sublanguage of a*b*,
illustrating how homomorphism + Dyck structure yields patterned CFLs."""
print(sorted("".join(w) for w in image)[:8])
```

The philosophical moral: context-freeness is *not* an irreducible property of strings. It factors into pure *structure* (the Dyck language, recording tree shapes) and pure *finiteness* (the regular filter, recording local compatibility). Everything else is relabeling.

### 4.4 Mild context-sensitivity: TAG, CCG, and the edge of natural language

The hierarchy's Type 1 is a vast, wild territory: CSL membership is PSPACE-complete, and CSLs include pathological specimens like the set of prime-length strings or squares — languages no linguist would attribute to a human grammar. If natural languages are not context-free, they surely do not leap all the way to arbitrary context-sensitivity. Joshi's proposal was to identify an intermediate zone — **mildly context-sensitive languages** — characterized informally by four properties [7]:

1. *Limited cross-serial dependencies* — the class must handle the attested crossing patterns (e.g., *aⁿbⁿcⁿ*) but need not handle arbitrary permutations.
2. *Constant growth* — sentence lengths cannot have arbitrarily large gaps; each sentence extends to another by adding bounded material.
3. *Polynomial parsing* — recognition must be tractable, ideally in low-degree polynomial time.
4. *Semilinearity* — the Parikh image (letter-count vectors) of the language is semilinear, i.e., definable in Presburger arithmetic.

It is understood that every mildly context-sensitive class contains all CFLs [7]. Remarkably, several independently invented formalisms converge on exactly this zone:

- **Tree-adjoining grammars (TAG)** (Joshi, 1985): the elementary objects are *trees*, combined by *substitution* and *adjunction*. Adjunction splices an auxiliary tree into the interior of another, which is what generates the cross-serial *aⁿbⁿcⁿdⁿ* pattern. TAG parses in *O(n⁶)* [4].
- **Combinatory categorial grammars (CCG)** (Steedman): lexical categories combine via combinatory rules — forward/backward application, composition, type-raising — grounded in combinatory logic. CCG is strictly more powerful than CFG yet far below CSL [8].
- **Linear indexed grammars** and **head grammars**: different machinery, same weak generative capacity.

The landmark equivalence result — Joshi, Vijay-Shanker, and Weir (1991) — proved that TAG, CCG, linear indexed grammars, and head grammars are *weakly equivalent*: they generate the same class of string languages, despite utterly different derivations [8]. This convergence is the strongest evidence that mild context-sensitivity is a *natural kind*, not an artifact of one notation. The most general such framework is linear context-free rewriting systems (LCFRS), equivalently multiple context-free grammars (MCFG) [7].

A caveat: there is still no single generally accepted *formal* definition of mild context-sensitivity — Joshi's characterization mixes grammar properties with language properties, and alternatives (e.g., Kallmeyer's language-based definition) differ at the margins [7].

## 5 Empirical Results

### 5.1 The Swiss-German refutation, reconstructed

The decisive empirical result is Shieber's 1985 proof that Swiss German is not weakly context-free [6]. The construction uses subordinate clauses with cross-serial verb–object dependencies:

> *Jan säit das mer d'chind em Hans es huus lönd hälfed aastriiche*
> ("John said that we let the children help Hans paint the house")

Case marking makes the dependencies overt: *hälfe* (help) governs dative (*em Hans*), while *lönd* (let) and *aastriiche* (paint) govern accusative (*d'chind*, *es huus*). The dependency arcs *cross* — *N₁ N₂ N₃ V₁ V₂ V₃* — rather than nest.

Shieber's proof is a closure argument. Define a homomorphism *f* mapping noun phrases to *a, b, x* and verbs to *c, d, y* according to case, and let *r = wa\*b\*xc\*d\*y* be regular. If Swiss German were context-free, then *f(SG)* would be context-free (CFLs are closed under homomorphism), and *f(SG) ∩ r = {waᵐbⁿxcᵐdⁿy}* would be context-free (CFLs are closed under intersection with regular languages). But that language is a padded copy of *{aᵐbⁿcᵐdⁿ}*, which the CFL pumping lemma refutes: any *v, x* with *\|vwx\| ≤ p* spans at most two blocks, so pumping breaks the *m = m*, *n = n* equalities. Contradiction — Swiss German is not context-free [6]. Huybregts and Culy obtained the same conclusion independently via Dutch cross-serial constructions and Bambara reduplication, which is why the result is regarded as robust rather than a quirk of one analysis [4].

### 5.2 Parsing complexity across the boundary

| Formalism | Weak class vs CFL | Recognition complexity |
|-----------|-------------------|------------------------|
| CFG / PDA | = CFL | *O(n³)* (CYK, Earley) |
| TAG | ⊋ CFL, mildly CS | *O(n⁶)* (Vijay-Shanker & Weir) |
| CCG | ⊋ CFL, mildly CS | polynomial (variants *O(n³)*–*O(n⁶)*) [8] |
| LCFRS(k) | fan-out *k* hierarchy | *O(n^{3k})* |
| CSL / LBA | ⊋ TAG languages | PSPACE-complete |

Two nuances matter. First, Shieber himself noted that cross-serial dependencies beyond *triple* embedding become unprocessable by native speakers — competence may exceed performance, and a finitary *performance* model could suffice for the attested fragment [6][4]. Second, TAG's *O(n⁶)* bound, while polynomial, motivated work on practical mildly context-sensitive parsing, including the restricted well-nested LCFRS/MCFG subclasses with better constants [7].

## 6 Limitations

1. **No canonical definition of mild context-sensitivity.** Joshi's four properties remain a "rough characterization" in his own words; grammar-based and language-based formalizations disagree at the margins, and LCFRS/MCFG — the most general framework — fails to preserve some characteristic TAG properties [7].
2. **Weak vs. strong generative capacity.** The hierarchy classifies *string sets*. TAG was motivated by *structural descriptions* (derived trees), and weak equivalence can mask deep derivational differences [4].
3. **Empirical fragility.** The Swiss-German argument depends on unbounded cross-serial embedding being grammatical; processing limits mean the crucial data live at the edge of acceptability [6]. Michaelis and Kracht's Old Georgian argument against minimalist grammars shows how such claims can hinge on uncheckable recursion assumptions [4].
4. **The categorical frontier is young.** The Melliès–Zeilberger reframing of the Chomsky–Schützenberger theorem is mathematically compelling but has not yet yielded new parsing algorithms; its payoff is currently conceptual [3].
5. **Performance vs. competence.** If human parsing is bounded and incremental, the *weakly* non-context-free constructions may never be exercised, reopening whether the extra power is linguistically real or an artifact of idealization [4].

---

## 7 Conclusion

The Chomsky hierarchy endures because it is simultaneously a theory of *grammars*, a theory of *machines*, and a theory of *structure*. The pumping lemmas give us the tools to separate its levels; the Chomsky–Schützenberger theorem shows the context-free level to be, at bottom, Dyck structure plus finite control plus relabeling [3][5]; and the Swiss-German results show that natural language overflows the context-free level by exactly the margin — crossing dependencies — that tree-adjoining and combinatory categorial grammars were built to capture [6][7][8].

The open question is placement with precision. The mildly context-sensitive consensus — natural languages live in the TAG/CCG/LCFRS zone, with polynomial parsing, constant growth, and semilinearity — is the best-supported hypothesis we have [4][7]. But it rests on an informal characterization, on acceptability judgments at the edge of processability, and on a convergence of formalisms that could yet be coincidence. Seventy years after "Three models for the description of language," the hierarchy still does what Chomsky designed it to do: it turns "how complex is language?" into a sequence of precise, refutable, mathematical bets [1].
