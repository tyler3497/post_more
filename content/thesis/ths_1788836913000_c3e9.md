# DAG-Based Byzantine Fault Tolerant Consensus: Mempool Separation in Narwhal, Asynchronous Ordering in Tusk, Partial Synchrony in Bullshark, and Sub-Second Finality in Mysticeti — Theory, Fairness, and Production Deployment on Sui and Aptos

## Abstract

Directed acyclic graph (DAG) based Byzantine fault tolerant (BFT) consensus has displaced the leader-centric paradigm that dominated permissioned blockchains for a decade. This thesis traces the intellectual arc from *Narwhal*, which separates reliable transaction dissemination from ordering into an independently scalable mempool, through *Tusk*, a zero-message-overhead asynchronous consensus embedded in the DAG; *Bullshark*, which recovers low-latency steady-state operation under partial synchrony via dual steady-state and fallback leaders; to *Mysticeti*, which eliminates explicit block certification and attains the three-message-round latency lower bound with sub-second WAN finality. We formalize the shared substrate — round-based DAGs built over reliable broadcast with quorum-intersection guarantees — and analyze wave-based leader election, the direct and indirect commit rules, and the fairness properties (validity, block-level and order fairness) that DAG ordering uniquely enables. Empirical results from production deployments on the Sui and Aptos blockchains are presented alongside proof sketches of safety and probabilistic liveness. We close with open limitations: garbage collection under weak links, censorship resistance, and the cost of fairness in high-throughput regimes.

---

## 1. Introduction

For most of the history of Byzantine fault tolerant replication, consensus was synonymous with the leader. From PBFT through HotStuff, a designated proposer collects transactions, disseminates them, and drives voting phases; every other validator is, for throughput purposes, idle. The leader's bandwidth bounds system throughput, and rotation under faults produces structurally unavoidable latency spikes. Even HotStuff's linear communication could not lift it past ~1,800 tx/s at one second WAN latency [1].

The DAG-based revolution, crystallized in Narwhal and Tusk [1], rejects the premise that dissemination and ordering must be coupled. Its central insight: *reliable dissemination is separable from total ordering* — the former parallelized across all validators, the latter reduced to deterministic interpretation of a shared causal graph. Once every validator continuously streams blocks into a round-structured DAG — each block carrying certificates of availability for prior blocks — consensus requires *zero additional messages*. Tusk demonstrated 160,000 tx/s at ~3s latency; Narwhal-HotStuff exceeded 130,000 tx/s at sub-2s latency, with worker scale-out reaching 600,000 tx/s.

This thesis examines the four systems that define the modern DAG-BFT canon: **Narwhal** (an asynchronously-safe, independently scalable mempool), **Tusk** (asynchronous consensus embedded in the DAG at zero marginal message cost), **Bullshark** (partial synchrony with dual steady-state and fallback leaders for low-latency synchronous operation [3]), and **Mysticeti** (uncertified DAGs attaining the three-message-round latency lower bound with ~400ms Sui mainnet latency [4]). We formalize the shared substrate — round-based DAGs over reliable broadcast — analyze wave-based leader election and commit rules, treat the fairness properties DAG ordering enables, and report production evidence from Sui and Aptos [5][8].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 System and Network Models

We consider $n = 3f + 1$ validators, at most $f$ Byzantine, under three timing models:

- **Asynchronous**: no delay bound; deterministic consensus is impossible (FLP), so protocols use randomization (common coin).
- **Partially synchronous**: unknown GST after which delays are bounded by $\Delta$; safety always, liveness after GST.
- **Synchronous**: known bound $\delta$ at all times.

Tusk targets asynchrony; Bullshark and Mysticeti-C target partial synchrony with fast paths that exploit actual synchrony; Shoal [5] and Shoal++ [8] layer reputation and pipelining atop Bullshark in production.

### 2.2 Reliable Broadcast and Quorum Intersection

The DAG substrate rests on *Byzantine reliable broadcast* (BRB): if an honest party delivers a block, all honest parties eventually do, and no two honest parties deliver different blocks for the same (author, round) pair. Narwhal instantiates BRB via *certificates of availability*: a block is certified once $2f+1$ validators acknowledge its batches. Quorum intersection — any two $2f+1$ quorums share at least $f+1$ honest validators — is the workhorse lemma behind every commit rule in this thesis.

### 2.3 From Chains to DAGs

DAG-based consensus has a thirty-year lineage surveyed in [6]: Hashgraph's gossip-about-gossip, Avalanche's metastable sampling, Conflux's GHOST ordering, Aleph's DAG-BFT. The modern wave begins with **DAG-Rider** [2] (Keidar et al.), which showed a DAG over reliable broadcast supports asynchronous *atomic broadcast* with wave-based common-coin leader election, optimal amortized communication, and strong fairness. Narwhal/Tusk [1] industrialized this: Narwhal replaced the abstract broadcast with a production mempool, Tusk streamlined consensus. **Cordial Miners** and Mahi-Mahi [7] later refined and reimplemented the asynchronous design space head-to-head.

### 2.4 What Consensus Must Guarantee

*Atomic broadcast* (total-order broadcast) requires four properties:

- **Validity**: if an honest validator broadcasts $m$, every honest validator eventually delivers $m$.
- **Agreement**: if one honest validator delivers $m$, all honest validators deliver $m$.
- **Integrity**: no duplication, no forgery.
- **Total order**: all honest validators deliver messages in the same order.

DAG-BFT adds a fifth desideratum, *fairness*: transactions proposed by honest validators must not be censorable or indefinitely reorderable by the adversary — a property leader-based protocols satisfy only weakly.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is analytic and comparative: we reconstruct each protocol from its published specification [1][2][3][4], isolate its distinguishing mechanism, and evaluate all four on latency (rounds to commit), communication complexity, timing assumptions, and fairness.

### 3.1 The Narwhal Mempool Construction

Narwhal decomposes each validator into one *primary* and multiple *workers*. Workers batch transactions and disseminate them; the primary proposes *headers* that reference worker batches and include certificates for $2f+1$ headers of the previous round. A validator advances to round $r+1$ upon observing $2f+1$ certified headers for round $r$ — a *threshold clock* that needs no explicit synchronization. Because every header is reliably broadcast and certified, all honest validators eventually hold identical causal histories: the DAG is a *shared, append-only data structure* even though it is assembled from local views [1]. Crucially, throughput scales by adding workers, since batch dissemination is embarrassingly parallel and never touches the consensus critical path.

![Narwhal DAG structure: rounds of block vertices with causal reference edges and quorum certificates](/thesis/ths_1788836913000_c3e9-0.webp)

*Figure 1: The Narwhal DAG. Validators (rows) propose one vertex per round (columns); each vertex references $2f+1$ prior-round vertices. Certification by quorum turns local views into a globally consistent DAG.*

### 3.2 Wave-Based Leader Election

Tusk, Bullshark, and their ancestors partition rounds into *waves* (three rounds in Tusk, two in Bullshark). Each wave elects a leader vertex from its first round — by common coin asynchronously, or predetermined schedule under partial synchrony. The *direct commit rule* fires when the leader is referenced by at least $f+1$ second-round vertices; the *indirect rule* walks backward, committing earlier leaders contained in a committed leader's causal history and skipping the rest. Consensus is thus *interpretation*, not communication [1][2][3].

### 3.3 Bullshark's Dual-Leader Design

Bullshark keeps Tusk's wave skeleton but assigns *two* leader types per wave: a deterministic *steady-state* leader (exploiting synchrony, committed in as few as two rounds) and a fallback leader elected by the common coin (preserving asynchronous liveness) [3]. Validators vote for the steady-state leader optimistically; on timeout they fall back to the randomized leader. This yields a practical fast path without the view-change machinery that makes partially synchronous protocols brittle.

### 3.4 Mysticeti's Uncertified DAG

Mysticeti's radical step is to *remove certification from the DAG entirely* [4]. Blocks are disseminated by best-effort broadcast; the DAG is *uncertified*, and equivocation is handled by the commit rule rather than prevented by certificates. Validators advance rounds via *threshold clocks* upon seeing $2f+1$ distinct blocks. With multiple leaders per round and pipelined waves, every block becomes committable without delay, achieving the three-round latency lower bound. Mysticeti-FPC weaves a fast path for owned-object (non-contentious) transactions into the same DAG, minimizing extra signatures and messages [4].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Narwhal: Dissemination as an Independent Substrate

Narwhal's primary/worker split solves the exact bottleneck that caps leader-based protocols. Workers are pure data planes batching transactions peer-to-peer; the primary is a thin control plane exchanging ~1KB headers and certificates. Because a header *references* batches by digest and carries proof that $2f+1$ validators stored them, availability is decoupled from agreement: holding a certified header guarantees retrievability of its payload.

The evaluation in [1] is striking: Narwhal-HotStuff sustains over 130,000 tx/s below 2s WAN latency (versus 1,800 tx/s for standalone HotStuff), scaling linearly to 600,000 tx/s with added workers and no latency increase. A subtlety inherited by Tusk: Narwhal headers contain *weak links* — references to vertices older than the immediately preceding round — guaranteeing every certified block is eventually included in some committed causal history (validity). Tusk drops weak links to enable garbage collection, trading a mild validity weakening for bounded memory.

### 4.2 Tusk: Zero-Overhead Asynchronous Consensus

Tusk's headline property is that it adds *no messages* to Narwhal: consensus is pure local computation over the DAG. Each wave spans three rounds; rounds $1$–$2$ act as all-to-all exchanges, and round $3$ produces a threshold-signature common coin electing the wave's leader from round $1$. The commit logic as pseudocode:

```python
def try_commit(wave, dag, committed):
    leader = common_coin(wave)          # perfect shared randomness, round 3
    supporters = [v for v in dag[wave.round2]
                  if leader in v.references]
    if len(supporters) >= f + 1:
        direct_commit(leader, wave)     # direct rule
        # indirect rule: walk back over uncommitted waves
        for w in reversed(range(last_committed, wave)):
            lw = leader_of(w)
            if lw in causal_history(leader):
                direct_commit(lw, w)
            else:
                skip(w)
```

> **Theorem (Tusk liveness):** In each wave, the probability that the elected leader is committed by the direct rule is at least $1/3$. Hence the expected number of waves to commit is constant, and liveness holds with probability $1$ under asynchrony [1][2].

*Proof sketch.* The coin is revealed only after the adversary commits to the round-$2$ DAG structure (coin shares sit in round-$3$ blocks). At least $f+1$ honest round-$2$ vertices reference some round-$1$ vertex, and the coin elects each validator's vertex with probability $\geq 1/3$ (for $n = 3f+1$). Quorum intersection guarantees that if one honest validator directly commits a leader, every honest validator's indirect walk reaches the same decision. ∎

![Tusk wave commit rule: three-round waves, coin-elected leader, direct and indirect commit paths](/thesis/ths_1788836913000_c3e9-1.webp)

*Figure 2: Tusk's wave structure. The coin elects the round-1 leader (gold); $f+1$ round-2 references trigger direct commit (green); earlier waves resolve via the indirect rule over causal history (dashed blue).*

Tusk achieves 160,000 tx/s at ~3s WAN latency — ~20× the state-of-the-art asynchronous protocol at the time — and holds throughput under faults where partially synchronous protocols suffer latency collapse [1].

### 4.3 Bullshark: Partial Synchrony Without the View-Change Tax

Bullshark observes that asynchrony-optimized protocols pay a permanent latency tax for a contingency that rarely materializes. It runs *two* leader elections per wave: a predetermined steady-state leader (committed in two rounds during synchrony) and a fallback leader drawn from the common coin (preserving asynchronous liveness) [3]. Validators vote for the steady-state leader optimistically; on timeout they pivot to the fallback leader — a practical fast path with none of the view-change machinery that makes partially synchronous protocols brittle.

The standalone variant is famously simple — ~200 lines atop a Narwhal mempool — yet delivers 125,000 tx/s at 2s latency for 50 validators [3]. Aptos productionized this lineage with **Shoal** [5], adding leader reputation and pipelining for up to 40% latency reduction fault-free (80% under failures), plus *prevalent responsiveness*. Shoal++ [8] extends it with per-round leaders and parallel DAGs.

![Bullshark state machine: steady-state fast path and fallback asynchronous mode](/thesis/ths_1788836913000_c3e9-2.webp)

*Figure 3: Bullshark's dual-mode operation. The steady-state leader commits optimistically during synchrony; on timeout the protocol falls back to the coin-elected leader — no view-change machinery.*

The mode transition preserves a single safety invariant: steady-state and fallback commits can never disagree on a wave's leader, because any two committing quorums intersect in an honest vertex whose causal history both validators observe identically.

### 4.4 Mysticeti: The Latency Floor, Reached

Every certified-DAG protocol pays for certification: broadcasting a block, gathering $2f+1$ signatures, and rebroadcasting the certificate costs at least two message rounds before a block even *enters* the DAG. Mysticeti-C deletes this cost. Blocks are disseminated by best-effort broadcast; the DAG is *uncertified*. Safety is recovered by a stronger commit rule operating over threshold-clock rounds: a validator advances when it observes $2f+1$ distinct blocks, so rounds still pace the system, but no signature quorum gates block inclusion.

The result is the first DAG-BFT protocol to hit the *three-message-round lower bound* for Byzantine commit: propose, vote (via DAG references), commit — with no certification round and no wave-level batching delay, since every block is committable as soon as the rule fires [4]. Mysticeti-C holds WAN commit latency to 0.5s while sustaining 200,000+ TPS, degrades gracefully under crash faults, and on Sui cut P50 consensus latency from 1.9s (Bullshark) to ~390ms — a greater than 4× reduction across 106 independently operated validators [4].

Mysticeti-FPC addresses the other latency dimension: *owned-object* transactions (uncontended payments) need not wait for total ordering. Rather than bolting on a separate fast path with its own signatures, FPC weaves fast-path votes into the DAG, minimizing marginal cost — an advance over prior designs whose separate vote phases consumed the CPU they were meant to save [4].

The price of uncertification is complexity in equivocation handling: Byzantine validators may broadcast conflicting blocks for the same round, so the commit rule must be robust to forks *within* the DAG — a burden certified DAGs never face. Mysticeti's safety proof is correspondingly the most intricate of the four [4].

### 4.5 Fairness: The DAG's Structural Dividend

Leader-based protocols are weakly fair at best: a Byzantine leader can censor or reorder transactions within its proposal. DAG protocols inherit *validity* from reliable broadcast, and DAG-Rider [2] proved strong block-level fairness: in expectation, at least a $1/2$ fraction of committed blocks are proposed by honest validators (*chain quality*), because the common coin elects leaders uniformly and the adversary cannot bias the election after committing to the DAG structure.

The Narwhal family adds a practical dimension: *every* validator continuously injects transactions into the DAG, so censorship requires suppressing a validator's blocks across the $2f+1$ quorum — far costlier than outrunning a single leader. *Order fairness* (final order respecting reception order) remains unachieved; protocols like Pompe and Themis add explicit order-fairness layers, but none of the four systems here guarantees it, and fair ordering at sub-second DAG commit remains open [6].

Two patterns stand out. First, *decoupling dissemination from ordering* is worth two orders of magnitude in throughput regardless of consensus layer. Second, *latency is a certification tax*: each generation removes a round — Tusk's three-round waves, Bullshark's two-round steady state, Mysticeti's three-round optimal commit with no certification — and mainnet latency falls correspondingly.

### 5.2 Proof Sketches

> **Theorem (Safety, DAG interpretation):** If an honest validator commits leader $L$ of wave $w$, every honest validator commits the same causal history for all waves $\leq w$ [1][2][3].

*Sketch.* Direct commits require $f+1$ round-$(r+1)$ supporters; any two supporter sets intersect in an honest validator whose reliably-broadcast vertex is visible to all, so no conflicting leader can be directly committed. Indirect commits walk the causal history of a directly committed leader; reliable broadcast gives all honest validators identical causal histories, and deterministic topological ordering of identical histories yields identical sequences. ∎

> **Theorem (Bullshark liveness):** After GST, if the steady-state leader is honest, its vertex commits within two rounds; if not, the fallback leader commits with probability $\geq 1/3$ per wave, so liveness holds with probability $1$ [3].

*Sketch.* Post-GST, an honest steady-state leader's vertex reaches all honest validators within $\Delta$; their next-round vertices reference it, gathering $2f+1$ votes before any timeout. Otherwise the fallback reduces to Tusk's argument. ∎

> **Theorem (Mysticeti latency optimality):** No Byzantine consensus protocol can commit in fewer than three message rounds; Mysticeti-C achieves three [4].

*Sketch.* The lower bound is classic: Byzantine agreement needs (i) proposal dissemination, (ii) evidence a quorum received it (else an equivocating proposer splits the honest set), and (iii) evidence a quorum holds such evidence. Mysticeti-C's uncertified DAG folds (ii) and (iii) into DAG references and threshold-clock advancement, meeting the bound with no explicit certification phase. ∎

### 5.3 Production Evidence

- **Sui** replaced its Narwhal/Bullshark stack with Mysticeti-C: P50 latency fell from 1.9s to ~390ms across 106 independent validators [4].
- **Aptos** deployed the Bullshark lineage with Shoal enhancements (leader reputation, pipelining, prevalent responsiveness); the Shoal++ study [8] benchmarks Bullshark, Shoal, Mysticeti, and Jolteon in one Rust/Tokio harness, confirming certified-DAG throughput leadership and Mysticeti's latency edge.
- The Mahi-Mahi project [7] independently reimplemented Cordial Miners and Mahi-Mahi on a Mysticeti fork (~14k LOC), giving the first apples-to-apples evaluation of the asynchronous DAG design space outside the origin labs.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Garbage collection vs. validity.** Tusk's removal of Narwhal's weak links enables bounded memory but weakens the guarantee that every honest block is ordered; slow validators' transactions can be skipped rather than delayed. Production systems must tune GC depth against censorship risk.
2. **Uncertified DAG complexity.** Mysticeti's equivocation-tolerant commit rule is the subtlest in the lineage; its safety proof spans the full paper [4], and fork-handling bugs are a larger attack surface than in certified designs.
3. **Signature and bandwidth scaling.** Even "zero-overhead" consensus rides atop a DAG with $O(n^2)$ per-round references; at hundreds of validators, signature verification dominates CPU — the cost Mysticeti attacks but does not eliminate.
4. **Fairness is incomplete.** Block-level fairness (chain quality) is proven [2], but *order fairness* — protection against front-running via reordering — is absent from all four protocols. DAG structure helps (all validators inject transactions), yet a Byzantine validator can still manipulate *relative* order within its own blocks.
5. **Byzantine attacks on uncertified DAGs.** Mysticeti's graceful degradation is proven for crash faults; under active Byzantine equivocation storms, latency bounds are less well characterized empirically.
6. **Execution remains separate.** All four protocols order *certificates of availability*, not executed state; the execution layer (Sui's owned-object fast path vs. Aptos's Block-STM) is an independent bottleneck consensus improvements cannot mask.

## 7. Conclusion

The Narwhal-to-Mysticeti arc is a case study in *removing rounds*. Narwhal removed dissemination from the critical path; Tusk removed consensus messages entirely; Bullshark removed the view-change; Mysticeti removed certification itself — arriving at the three-round floor theory permits. Each removal was paid for with proof complexity, and each was validated twice: in the lab (130k–600k tx/s across the lineage) and in production (Sui's 4× latency cut, Aptos's Shoal-hardened Bullshark).

The deeper lesson is architectural: *consensus is interpretation of shared causality, not negotiation over proposals*. Once validators maintain a consistent causal graph, agreement reduces to deterministic rules — common coins, quorum thresholds, topological sorts — applied locally. That reframing enables zero-message consensus, the DAG fairness dividend, and the next generation (Mahi-Mahi, Shoal++, BBCA-Chain) pushing past Mysticeti's bound for special transaction classes.

Open problems remain where theory meets adversarial reality: garbage collection without censorship, order fairness at 200k TPS, and Byzantine-resilient uncertified DAGs at large validator counts. The DAG revolution has won throughput; the fight for fairness and robustness at the latency floor is next.
