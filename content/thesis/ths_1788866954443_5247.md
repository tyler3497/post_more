# Differential Dataflow and Timely Dataflow: Incremental View Maintenance with Partially-Ordered Timestamps, Arrange Operators, and Streaming SQL in Materialize

## Abstract

Modern analytical workloads demand answers that are both *fresh* and *consistent*: results must reflect the latest data, yet remain correct under concurrent updates. Differential dataflow, introduced by McSherry, Murray, Isaacs, and Isard in 2013 [2], provides a unified computational model for incremental computation that generalizes both stream processing and iterative batch algorithms. Built atop timely dataflow — a framework in which every message carries a partially-ordered logical timestamp and operators receive explicit progress notifications [1,3] — differential dataflow represents collections as multisets whose changes are decomposed into timestamped differences. This formulation reduces incremental maintenance of arbitrary operators, including joins and nested iteration, to the same primitives used for streaming, enabling fixed-point computations to react to input changes in time proportional to the size of the *difference* rather than the data. The `arrange` operator and its shared arrangements permit multiple downstream joins to reuse a single indexed trace [2,4], a capability exploited at industrial scale by Materialize, a PostgreSQL-compatible streaming SQL database whose materialized views are maintained incrementally as differential dataflows [5,6]. This thesis develops the theory of partially-ordered timestamps, consolidation, and arrangement sharing; surveys the Materialize system architecture; and compares differential dataflow with Apache Flink, Spark Structured Streaming, and kSQLDB, quantifying the regimes in which incrementally-maintained views outperform recomputation.

## 1. Introduction

The classical dichotomy between *batch processing* and *stream processing* has long forced practitioners into an unsatisfying compromise: recompute entire results on fresh snapshots, or accept weakened semantics (windows, approximate answers, eventual consistency) in exchange for low latency. Incremental view maintenance (IVM), a decades-old database technique [5], promises a third path — maintain results under updates with work proportional to the *change* — but traditional IVM handles only restricted classes of queries (typically first-order, non-recursive) and struggles with joins over multiple evolving relations.

Differential dataflow dissolves this boundary by observing that *streaming*, *incremental computation*, and *iteration* are instances of a single mechanism: maintaining a collection $C$ as it evolves over a partially-ordered domain of *times*. In differential dataflow, a collection is a multiset of records; a change to the collection is a multiset of *differences* $\delta C$; and any operator $f$ satisfies the distributivity-like identity

$$f(C + \delta C) = f(C) + f(\delta C)$$

when $f$ is linear in the appropriate sense, and more generally can be evaluated incrementally through consolidation of differences. Critically, the "time" domain need not be a total order: timely dataflow's partially-ordered timestamps allow loop iterations and input epochs to be *incomparable*, so that the fixed point of an iterative computation and the arrival of new data are processed concurrently rather than serially [1,3].

This thesis develops the theory of partially-ordered timestamps, consolidation, and arrangement sharing; surveys the Materialize system architecture; and compares differential dataflow with Flink, Spark Structured Streaming, and kSQLDB, quantifying the regimes in which incrementally-maintained views outperform recomputation.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From Naiad to Timely Dataflow

Naiad, presented at SOSP 2013 by Murray, McSherry, Isaacs, Isard, Barham, and Abadi [1], was conceived to unify three regimes — high-throughput batch processing, low-latency stream processing, and iterative/incremental computation — in a single distributed system. Prior systems excelled in at most two: MapReduce and Dryad handled batch but not iteration or low latency; streaming engines handled low latency but not iteration; and graph systems like Pregel handled iteration but neither streaming nor low latency well.

Naiad's key insight was to generalize *time*. Instead of the totally-ordered event times of conventional streaming, Naiad introduced **timely dataflow**: a computational model in which the dataflow graph may contain cycles, every message carries a *logical timestamp* drawn from a partial order, and vertices receive explicit notifications when they have received all messages at or below a given timestamp. The open-source extraction of this runtime, **timely dataflow** (Rust), and its companion library **differential dataflow**, are maintained under the TimelyDataflow GitHub organization and remain the substrate of Materialize [4].

> **Theorem: (Progress safety).** In timely dataflow, a vertex notified that no further messages with timestamp $\leq t$ will arrive will never subsequently receive such a message.
> **Proof:** The distributed progress-tracking protocol maintains, at each worker, a frontier of *pointstamps* $(node, t)$ — the set of capabilities that could still produce messages. Capabilities are transferred conservatively along edges; a notification is delivered only when the frontier of possible message times has advanced beyond $t$. Since capabilities cannot be forged and are only downgraded through explicit operator action, the invariant holds by induction on the protocol steps. ∎

### 2.2 Incremental Computation and the Need for Differences

Traditional incremental computation maintains $f(C)$ given $C \to C'$ by computing a delta rule. This works for simple operators but breaks down for iteration: recomputing a fixpoint from scratch after each change is prohibitive, and hand-derived delta rules for recursive queries are notoriously error-prone. Differential dataflow's innovation [2] was to represent the *entire computation history* — inputs, intermediate results, and iteration rounds — as differences indexed by partially-ordered times, so that "recomputing the fixpoint" and "processing a new input batch" become the *same* operation: consolidate differences and propagate.

### 2.3 Related Systems

Apache Flink implements streaming with event-time watermarks and RocksDB-backed stateful operators, but iterative computations (`IterativeStream`) are second-class and joins are windowed or temporal. Spark Structured Streaming micro-batches with watermark-based state eviction; multi-way joins over unbounded history are impractical. kSQLDB layers streaming SQL over Kafka with changelog semantics but limited join expressiveness and no shared-index amortization. Materialize, by contrast, maintains full-history joins and shares arrangements across views [5,6].

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds on three levels. *Theoretically*, we reconstruct the formal semantics of timely and differential dataflow from the primary sources [1,2,3]: product-order timestamps, the consolidation invariant, and arrangement sharing. *Architecturally*, we decompose Materialize into its coordinator, compute clusters, and storage layers, tracing a SQL query from parse to maintained dataflow [4,6]. *Comparatively*, we evaluate differential dataflow against Flink, Spark Structured Streaming, and kSQLDB on latency, state management, join expressiveness, and consistency [5]. Code illustrations use the public Rust `timely` and `differential-dataflow` crates; empirical claims cite published evaluations.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Partially-Ordered Logical Timestamps and Progress Tracking

The foundational move of timely dataflow [1,3] is to replace totally-ordered event time with a **partial order** $(T, \le)$ of logical times. A timestamp in Naiad has the form

$$t = (e, i_1, i_2, \dots, i_k)$$

where $e$ is the input *epoch* and each $i_j$ is the iteration counter of the $j$-th enclosing loop; the order is the product order: $t \le t'$ iff every coordinate of $t$ is $\le$ the corresponding coordinate of $t'$. Two timestamps from *different* epochs or *incomparable* loop iterations may be unordered relative to each other, which is precisely what permits concurrent execution: an operator processing iteration 5 of epoch 3 need not wait for iteration 2 of epoch 7, because neither could causally affect the other.

Progress tracking is the mechanism that makes this safe. Each worker tracks **pointstamps** — pairs $(location, time)$ naming a place where messages with that timestamp might still be produced. A worker's *frontier* is the minimal antichain of pointstamps summarizing outstanding work. The protocol works as follows:

1. **Capabilities.** An operator holds *capabilities* (tokens) for timestamps it may still emit. Capabilities can be *downgraded* (moved to a greater timestamp) or *dropped*, but never created from nothing — every capability is accounted for.
2. **Propagation.** When a worker sends a message with timestamp $t$ along an edge, the recipient's progress tracker records that $(target, t)$ is outstanding.
3. **Notification.** An operator that has *requested* a notification at time $t$ receives it exactly when the frontier of all pointstamps that *could result in* $(operator, t)$ has advanced strictly beyond $t$ — i.e., no future message could bear a timestamp $\le t$.

The relation "could result in" is the subtle core of the formal model [3]: it is the transitive closure over dataflow edges of the operator-specific rule mapping input timestamps to possible output timestamps. For most operators (map, filter, join) the rule is the identity on timestamps — outputs bear the timestamps of their inputs — which keeps the relation simple; ingress/egress nodes of loops adjust iteration coordinates.

In Rust, this machinery is exposed directly:

```rust
use timely::dataflow::operators::{Input, Inspect, Probe};
use timely::dataflow::operators::generic::operator::Operator;

fn main() {
    timely::execute_from_args(std::env::args(), |worker| {
        let mut input = worker.dataflow(|scope| {
            let (input, stream) = scope.new_input::<u64>();
            // A stateful operator that requests notifications:
            // it buffers inputs and flushes when the frontier advances.
            let probe = stream
                .inspect(|x| println!("saw {:?}", x))
                .probe();
            input
        });
        // Advance the input frontier; notifications fire downstream.
        for epoch in 0..10u64 {
            input.send(epoch);
            input.advance_to(epoch + 1);
            while probe.less_than(input.time()) { worker.step(); }
        }
    }).unwrap();
}
```

Here `advance_to` retracts the input's capability, which propagates through the progress protocol and eventually delivers notifications to stateful operators — the precise mechanism by which differential dataflow's consolidation is triggered [1,4].

### 4.2 Differential Collections: Multisets, Differences, and Iteration

Differential dataflow [2] lifts timely dataflow from streams of *records* to streams of *differences*. A **collection** $C$ is a multiset — a function from records to multiplicities — represented as a stream of triples $(data, time, diff)$, where $diff \in \mathbb{Z}$ is the change in multiplicity of $data$ at logical time $time$. The collection at time $t$ is recovered by summation:

$$C_t(data) = \sum_{t' \le t} \delta C(data, t')$$

where $\delta C$ is the difference stream. Every operator is then defined on difference streams. For a *linear* operator $L$ (map, filter, concat),

$$L(C + \delta C) = L(C) + L(\delta C)$$

so the operator simply applies itself to each difference — no state required. The interesting operators are the *bilinear* and *stateful* ones:

- **Join.** For $C \bowtie D$, the difference rule is the discrete product rule:

  $$(C + \delta C) \bowtie (D + \delta D) = C \bowtie D + (\delta C \bowtie D) + (C \bowtie \delta D) + (\delta C \bowtie \delta D).$$

  The join operator must therefore retain (indexed) state for $C$ and $D$ — this is the role of *arrangements* (§4.3) — and on each new difference, probe the *accumulated* opposite side. Crucially, the product-order timestamps mean that differences from independent epochs/iterations are *unordered* and can be processed without serializing on each other [2].

- **Iteration.** A loop is expressed with `Variable`, the differential fixed-point construct. Timestamps gain an additional coordinate for the loop counter, and the least fixed point is computed by repeatedly feeding outputs back as inputs at increasing iteration coordinates. Because timestamps are partially ordered, iteration *rounds* for different input epochs proceed concurrently: epoch 5's iteration 3 and epoch 6's iteration 1 are incomparable and may execute simultaneously [1,2].

```rust
use differential_dataflow::input::InputSession;
use differential_dataflow::operators::{Consolidate, Join, Iterate};

fn reachable(edges: &Collection<G, (u32, u32)>, nodes: &Collection<G, (u32, u32)>) {
    edges.iterate(|reach| {
        let next = reach
            .map(|(x, _y)| x)
            .join(&edges.map(|(x, y)| (x, y)))
            .map(|(_x, (y, z))| (y, z));
        // Fixed point: nodes reachable in one more hop, minus already known.
        reach.concat(&next).distinct()
    });
}
```

This program — transitive closure maintained incrementally — was intractable for prior incremental systems; in differential dataflow it is a few lines, and updates to `edges` propagate through the *difference* of the fixed point in time proportional to the change, not the graph size [2].

> **Theorem: (Consolidation correctness).** For any collection history $H = \{(d, t, \delta)\}$, replacing any subset of updates with their sum at a timestamp $\le$ all their timestamps preserves $C_t$ for every $t$.
> **Proof:** $C_t$ is defined as the sum over $t' \le t$; addition of multiplicities is commutative and associative, and the replacement only regroups summands whose timestamps all precede or equal the consolidation timestamp. ∎

Consolidation is what bounds state: once the timely frontier advances past $t$, all differences at times $\le t$ may be merged into a single consolidated batch, and *retractions* (negative multiplicities) cancel against their positive counterparts [2,4].

### 4.3 Arrange Operators and Shared Arrangements

The `arrange` operator is differential dataflow's answer to state management [2,4]. `arrange_by_key` converts a collection into an **arrangement**: a persistently indexed, consolidated trace of the collection's history, keyed for efficient lookup. A join against an arranged collection becomes a *lookup join*: probe the arrangement with the keys of incoming differences, rather than shuffling both sides.

The decisive optimization is **sharing**. An arrangement is a *named, reference-counted* data structure: if ten materialized views each join against the same base table on the same key, the table is arranged *once* and all ten joins share the trace. In Materialize, this is exposed to users as *indexes* (`CREATE INDEX`), and the optimizer aggressively reuses arrangements across views [4,6]:

```rust
// Arrange once, join many times — the arrangement is shared.
let edges_by_src = edges.arrange_by_key();
let nodes_by_id  = nodes.arrange_by_key();

let hop1 = reach.join_map(&edges_by_src, |_k, a, b| (*a, *b));
let hop2 = hop1.join_map(&edges_by_src,  |_k, a, b| (*a, *b)); // reuses arrangement
```

Without sharing, $k$ joins against the same relation each maintain their own index: $O(k \cdot n)$ memory and $O(k)$ redundant consolidation work. With sharing, memory is $O(n + k \cdot \Delta)$ and each update is consolidated once, then fanned out to all readers. This is the structural reason Materialize can maintain hundreds of views over common base tables where per-query stream processors would each rebuild state [5].

| Mechanism | Per-join state | Update fan-out | Memory (k joins, n records) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Independent join state (Flink default) | yes | per-operator | $O(k \cdot n)$ |
| Shared arrangement (differential) | shared trace | consolidate once | $O(n + k \cdot \Delta)$ |
| Recomputation (batch) | none | full rescan | $O(n)$ transient, $O(k \cdot n)$ work |

### 4.4 Materialize: Streaming SQL on Differential Dataflow

Materialize [4,6] is a PostgreSQL-wire-compatible streaming SQL database whose execution engine *is* differential dataflow. The architecture decomposes into layers:

1. **Coordinator / adapter layer.** Accepts PostgreSQL-protocol connections, parses SQL, maintains the catalog (sources, tables, views, indexes, sinks), and plans queries. Planning produces a dataflow description (a `DataflowDesc`) rather than a Volcano-style iterator tree [5].
2. **Compute clusters.** One or more timely dataflow workers execute the dataflow. Each `CREATE MATERIALIZED VIEW` compiles to differential operators; `CREATE INDEX` materializes a shared arrangement. Compute is sharded by key across workers, and *every* operator runs on *every* worker (cooperative scheduling), unlike Flink's operator-parallelism model [4].
3. **Storage layer.** Sources (Kafka, PostgreSQL CDC via Debezium, S3, webhooks) ingest as timestamped difference streams; recent versions separate storage onto object stores (S3) with a local cache, so compute clusters can scale or restart without losing history [6].
4. **Consistency.** Materialize provides *strict serializability* for reads: every query result corresponds to some recent, totally-ordered commit point of the underlying streams (via `mz_now()` and timestamp selection), so clients never observe partial or out-of-order updates [6].

The dataflow for a query such as

```sql
CREATE MATERIALIZED VIEW winning_bids AS
SELECT DISTINCT ON (auction_id) *
FROM bids ORDER BY auction_id, amount DESC;
```

compiles to a differential dataflow with a `top_k` arrangement operator; each new bid arrives as a difference $(\text{bid}, t, +1)$, the arrangement maintains the per-auction maximum incrementally, and readers observe updates with millisecond latency [4,5].

### 4.5 Comparison with Flink, Spark Structured Streaming, and kSQLDB

The fundamental trade-off is *state versus expressiveness*. Differential dataflow keeps indexed history in memory (bounded by consolidation and compaction), buying $O(\Delta)$ incremental updates and first-class iteration [1,2,3]. Flink bounds state with watermarks and windowed joins, restricting expressiveness but capping memory for infinite streams; iteration is second-class. Spark Structured Streaming micro-batches with watermark-based eviction and no native iteration. kSQLDB offers changelog semantics over Kafka with limited joins and no cross-query index sharing. Materialize's bet — validated by production deployments — is that where history *fits* (or tiers to S3), incrementally maintained SQL views with strict serializability dominate both batch recomputation and windowed streaming [5,6].

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Published Evaluation Highlights

The Naiad SOSP paper [1] reports sub-millisecond iteration latencies with batch updates under one second, outperforming specialized systems in their own domains. The differential dataflow CIDR paper [2] demonstrates incrementally maintained strongly-connected components and PageRank on changing graphs — workloads previous systems could not express incrementally — with update times proportional to the *change* in the graph. Materialize's public benchmarks report millisecond-latency view maintenance for TPC-H-style queries, with per-update work scaling in the delta [6].

### 5.2 Asymptotic Analysis

Consider a join $C \bowtie D$ with $|C| = n$, $|D| = m$, maintained under a batch of $\Delta$ new differences.

- **Recomputation (batch):** $O(n \cdot m)$ work per batch (hash join), regardless of $\Delta$.
- **Differential with arrangements:** consolidate $\Delta$ ($O(\Delta \log \Delta)$), probe the arranged opposite side ($O(\Delta \cdot \text{selectivity})$), emit output differences. Total: $O(\Delta \log (n + m))$ amortized, plus one-time $O(n \log n + m \log m)$ arrangement build [2,4].
- **Flink windowed join:** $O(\Delta \cdot w)$ where $w$ is the windowed state size; unbounded-history joins degenerate to $O(\Delta \cdot (n + m))$ with RocksDB I/O.

For iterative computations with $r$ rounds to convergence, differential dataflow's product-order timestamps allow the $r$ rounds of *independent* epochs to overlap; the critical path for a single epoch remains $O(r \cdot \text{round cost})$, but throughput scales with the number of incomparable timestamps in flight [1,3].

> **Theorem: (Work proportionality).** For any dataflow composed of linear operators, joins over arranged collections, and `iterate` fixed points, the work to process an update batch $\Delta$ is $O(|\Delta| \cdot \mathrm{polylog}(N) + |O|)$, where $N$ is the accumulated state size and $|O|$ the number of changed output records.
> **Proof sketch:** Linear operators touch only differences. Each join probes arrangements in $O(\log N)$ per difference and emits only genuinely new output differences. Iteration advances the loop counter coordinate; differences at iteration $i$ depend only on differences at iterations $< i$ (in the product order), so each difference is processed once per round in which it participates, and consolidation merges rounds once the frontier passes. Summation over rounds telescopes to the stated bound. ∎

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Memory footprint.** Arrangements retain indexed history; consolidation bounds it, but workloads with large, ever-growing state (e.g., unbounded clickstreams without natural retraction) can exhaust memory. Materialize mitigates this with S3-tiered storage and *compaction* (dropping history before a chosen frontier at the cost of losing the ability to answer "as of" queries), but the tension between expressiveness and bounded state is fundamental [6].
2. **Non-monotonic operators.** `distinct`, `top_k`, and antijoins can amplify small input changes into large output changes (one retraction can promote an entirely new top-$k$ set), degrading $O(|\Delta|)$ proportionality to $O(|\text{affected}|)$.
3. **Recursion through negation.** Stratified semantics are required when iteration interacts with non-monotonic operators; unstratified programs may not have well-defined least fixed points, and the system must reject or approximate them.
4. **Fault tolerance.** Naiad's original design included checkpoint/restore [1]; the open-source timely runtime leaves durability to the embedding system (Materialize persists source data and replays dataflows on restart [6]).
5. **Optimizer complexity.** Choosing *which* arrangements to share, when to consolidate, and how to order joins in a dataflow that cannot be re-planned dynamically is an open optimization problem; adaptive re-optimization of running dataflows remains research territory [5].
6. **Skew.** Key-sharded cooperative scheduling is vulnerable to hot keys; unlike Flink's operator parallelism, there is no easy "split the hot key" escape hatch within a single arrangement.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Differential dataflow, grounded in timely dataflow's partially-ordered timestamps and progress tracking [1,3], makes streaming updates, incremental maintenance, and iterative fixed-point computation *the same operation* on difference streams [2]. Shared arrangements turn this theory into an engineering advantage — amortizing indexed state across many queries — which Materialize productizes as incrementally maintained SQL views with strict serializability [4,5,6]. It trades bounded-state guarantees for full-history expressiveness and $O(\Delta)$ updates, favoring operational-analytics workloads; memory-bounded arrangements and adaptive re-optimization remain open research.
