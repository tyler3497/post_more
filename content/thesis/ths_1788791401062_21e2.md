# From Kernels to Inference: A Denotational Account of Probabilistic Programming via the Giry Monad and Quasi-Borel Spaces

## Abstract

We present a unified denotational semantics for higher-order probabilistic programming languages that cleanly separates *modeling* from *inference*. Programs denote stochastic kernels in the Kleisli category of the Giry probability monad; on measurable spaces this yields a sound, adequate, and commutative semantics whose commutativity is exactly the Fubini–Tonelli theorem. Because the category of measurable spaces is not cartesian closed, we move to quasi-Borel spaces, which preserve measure-theoretic rigor while admitting exponentials — and hence probability distributions over higher-order functions. Soft constraints (`observe`/`score`) become morphisms into the monad over s-finite measures; conditioning is validated as measure disintegration, and the conditional densities used by samplers are provable program transformations. We show how three families of inference algorithms — Markov chain Monte Carlo, sequential Monte Carlo, and variational inference — are formalized as meaning-preserving transformations between inference representations, yielding compositional correctness proofs: each algorithmic step preserves the denotation, so the composite computes the true posterior. We close by discussing the limits of commutativity under conditioning and the disintegration barrier on non-standard-Borel spaces.

## 1 Introduction

Probabilistic programming languages (PPLs) such as Church, Anglican, and Venture promise a radical separation of concerns: the user writes a *generative model* as a program with random choices, and the language implementation supplies *inference* — an algorithm that inverts the model against observed data [2]. This promise is seductive but semantically treacherous. What exactly *is* the meaning of a program that both samples from continuous distributions and conditions on observed values? And how can we be sure that a complex inference algorithm — say, particle Gibbs with ancestor sampling — actually computes what the program denotes, rather than something subtly different?

Two technical obstacles stand in the way of a naive answer. First, expressive PPLs support **higher-order functions** (a sampled regression function passed as an argument) *and* **continuous distributions** simultaneously, yet the category of measurable spaces `Meas` is not cartesian closed: there is no measurable space of all measurable functions ℝ → ℝ that behaves well under products [3]. Second, inference algorithms are large, intricate artifacts; proving each one correct from scratch against a monolithic semantics is intractable.

This thesis argues that both obstacles fall to the same methodology — *denotational semantics with monads and representation change*:

- **Modeling** is given by the **Giry monad** [1]: programs denote kernels, and the monadic structure *is* the compositional structure of randomness.
- **Higher-order probability** is recovered by replacing measurable spaces with **quasi-Borel spaces** (QBSs), a cartesian closed category that embeds `Meas` conservatively [3].
- **Inference** is formalized as a family of **meaning-preserving transformations** between *inference representations*: correctness of MCMC, SMC, and variational schemes reduces to checking that each transformation commutes with the meaning function [4].
- **Conditioning** is not primitive magic but **disintegration** of measures; commutativity of independent random choices is the **Fubini–Tonelli theorem** in disguise, which licenses program equivalences used by compilers and inference implementers alike.

The result is a framework in which a statement like "single-site Metropolis–Hastings on program traces is a valid inference algorithm" becomes a checkable equation between denotations — and composes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Measure theory in one paragraph

A *measurable space* is a pair *(X, Σ)* of a set and a σ-algebra of subsets; a *probability measure* assigns each measurable set a mass in *[0, 1]* with countable additivity and total mass 1. A *Markov kernel* *k : X ⇝ Y* assigns to each *x ∈ X* a probability measure *k(x, ·)* on *Y*, measurably in *x*. Kernels compose by integration (the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation):

> **Definition (Kernel composition):** *(k ⋆ l)(x, C) = ∫ l(y, C) k(x, dy).*

This composition is associative with the Dirac kernel *δₓ* as identity — i.e., measurable spaces and kernels already form a category. The Giry monad is the categorical packaging of exactly this fact [1].

### 2.2 The Giry monad

The **Giry monad** *G* sends a measurable space *X* to the space *G(X)* of probability measures on *X* (with the σ-algebra generated by the evaluation maps *μ ↦ μ(A)*). Its unit is the Dirac embedding *η(x) = δₓ*, and Kleisli extension integrates:

```haskell
-- The Giry probability monad (illustrative sketch)
return :: x -> Prob x
return x = dirac x

(>>=) :: Prob x -> (x -> Prob y) -> Prob y
m >>= f = \a -> integrate (\x -> f x a) m
```

In `do`-notation, a probabilistic program reads as ordinary functional code: `x ← sample (normal 0 1); score (likelihood x); return x`. The monad laws are the *associativity and unitality of integration* — no coincidence, but the whole point: **the algebra of the monad is the algebra of probability** [1][2].

### 2.3 Conditioning and Bayes' rule

Classically, conditioning *P(X | Y = y)* is defined via conditional densities. Categorically, it is better understood as **disintegration**: a probability measure *μ* on *X × Y* *disintegrates* as a marginal *μₓ* on *X* together with a kernel *κ : X ⇝ Y* such that *μ(A × B) = ∫ₐ κ(x, B) μₓ(dx)*. On standard Borel spaces such a disintegration always exists and is essentially unique [7]. In programming terms: `observe` carves a conditional kernel out of the joint distribution the program defines.

## 3 Methodology

We study an idealized metalanguage in the style of Staton et al. [2] — essentially a call-by-value λ-calculus with:

| Construct | Intuition | Denotation |
|---|---|---|
| `return e` | deterministic value | Dirac unit *η* |
| `let x = e₁ in e₂` | sequencing | Kleisli bind *>>=* |
| `sample d` | draw from a primitive distribution | the distribution itself |
| `score r` / `observe d y` | soft/hard constraint | multiply the running measure by *r* (or a density) |

**Denotational strategy.** We define ⟦·⟧ compositionally: types denote spaces (measurable or quasi-Borel), terms denote morphisms — kernels in the Kleisli category of the (s-finite) Giry-like monad. Two semantics are developed and related:

1. An **operational semantics** via stochastic labelled transition systems, where `sample` allocates a fresh read-only random variable (the "name generation" reading of randomness [6]).
2. A **denotational semantics** in QBSs, proved *sound* (equal programs have equal denotations) and *adequate* (equal denotations imply observational equivalence at ground type) [2].

**Inference as representation change.** Following Ścibior et al. [4], we separate the *meaning* of a program from its *inference representation*. An inference representation *T* is a monad-like structure with a *meaning function* *m : T(X) → Mass(X)*. An **inference transformation** *t : T → S* is *valid* when *m_S ∘ t = m_T*: it preserves meaning while changing the computational representation. MCMC, SMC, and variational algorithms are then *composites of valid transformations* — correctness by construction, proved one small step at a time.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Giry Monad, s-Finite Kernels, and Why Commutativity Is Fubini

The Giry monad is *commutative*: for *m : G(X)* and *n : G(Y)*,

> **Theorem (Commutativity of the Giry monad):** *do { x ← m; y ← n; return (x, y) } = do { y ← n; x ← m; return (x, y) }.*

The proof is precisely the **Fubini–Tonelli theorem**: both sides denote the product measure *m ⊗ n*, and iterated integrals against a product measure agree regardless of order. In program terms: *the order of independent random choices is unobservable* [7].

> **Theorem (Staton's commutative semantics):** Programs of a first-order probabilistic language with `sample` and `score` denote s-finite kernels; the equational theory of the language — including commutativity of independent `let`-bindings — is validated by Fubini's theorem for s-finite measures [7].

Why **s-finite** measures (countable sums of finite measures) rather than merely finite ones? Because `score` can assign *unbounded* weight: scoring by a density that is not integrable against the prior yields an infinite measure, and plain finite-measure Fubini fails there. Staton showed that s-finite kernels still support a Fubini theorem, recovering commutativity in the presence of soft constraints [7]. This is the semantic justification for compiler optimizations that reorder or hoist independent `sample` statements, and for the exchangeability assumptions inside SMC resampling steps.

### 4.2 Escaping Meas: Quasi-Borel Spaces

Here is the bad news: `Meas` is **not cartesian closed**. There is no measurable space *Y^X* of "all measurable functions *X → Y*" such that evaluation is measurable and currying works — a theorem of Aumann. So a program like

```haskell
let f = do { s <- sample (normal 0 3); b <- sample (normal 0 3)
           ; return (\x -> s*x + b) }   -- a distribution over functions!
in observe (normal (f 1.0) 0.5) 2.5
```

has no denotation in `Meas`, even though Anglican runs it happily [3].

**Quasi-Borel spaces** repair this while keeping every theorem of measure theory we need. A QBS is a set *X* with a distinguished family *M_X ⊆ X^ℝ* of *random elements* — maps from ℝ thought of as "random variables valued in X" — closed under precomposition with measurable maps, constants, and countable gluing [3]. Morphisms are maps preserving random elements. The key facts:

- **QBS is cartesian closed**: exponentials *Y^X* exist, so distributions over functions are first-class.
- **QBS is well-pointed**: maps are determined by their action on points, giving clean equational reasoning principles.
- **Meas embeds conservatively**: standard Borel spaces embed fully and faithfully, and integration against a random element is well-defined, so all ordinary probability (including Fubini and disintegration on standard Borel spaces) transfers.

Probability itself becomes a monad *P* on QBS, where *P(X)* is the space of (equivalence classes of) random elements *ℝ → X* modulo equality in distribution. The randomization lemma — every probability kernel *ℝ ⇝ X* arises from pushing forward Lebesgue measure along a random element — makes this concrete [3]. De Finetti's theorem generalizes to QBSs, confirming that the abstraction is probabilistically faithful rather than merely convenient.

### 4.3 Conditioning as Disintegration

What does `observe (normal x 1) 2.0` *mean*? Operationally, implementations accumulate a likelihood weight. Denotationally, the honest answer is **disintegration**. Consider a program denoting a joint (s-finite) measure *μ* on *trace × return-value*. Conditioning on the observed value *y* is the kernel *κ* obtained by disintegrating *μ* with respect to the projection onto the observed component:

> **Theorem (Disintegration, standard Borel case):** *Let μ be a probability measure on X × Y with X, Y standard Borel. Then there exist a marginal μ_X and a kernel κ : X ⇝ Y, unique μ_X-a.e., with μ(A × B) = ∫_A κ(x, B) μ_X(dx).*

The posterior program — the program *after* `observe` — denotes exactly this conditional kernel. This reframes conditioning from "mysterious primitive that divides by a normalizing constant" to "canonical categorical operation with an existence and uniqueness theorem." It also explains *why* conditioning on measure-zero events is delicate: disintegration is only unique almost everywhere, and choosing a *version* of the conditional distribution is a genuine semantic commitment (the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox lives here). Implementations that accumulate densities are computing *one* version; the semantics tells us when that choice is legitimate — and when it silently changes the meaning (cf. the unit anomalies in likelihood-weighting semantics) [4].

### 4.4 Commutativity, Fubini–Tonelli, and Program Equivalence

Commutativity is the workhorse equation of probabilistic program equivalence. Concretely, these program transformations are validated by Fubini–Tonelli:

1. **Independent sample reordering**: `let x = sample d₁ in let y = sample d₂ in e` ≡ `let y = sample d₂ in let x = sample d₁ in e` (when *x ∉ fv(d₂)*, *y ∉ fv(d₁)*).
2. **Hoisting**: independent samples may be lifted out of loops and branches.
3. **Disintegration-based simplifications**: conjugacy relationships (e.g., Beta–Bernoulli) are equations between kernels, proved by computing both sides' densities against the same dominating measure.

Each of these is used, implicitly or explicitly, by inference: SMC's resampling step *requires* that particle order be irrelevant (exchangeability = a commutativity property); Gibbs samplers *require* that the full conditionals derived by disintegration compose back to the joint (a Fubini property); and variational objectives *require* that expectations factor over independent latent variables.

A subtle point: commutativity can **fail** in the presence of `score` when weights are not s-finite, and it can fail *observationally* when `observe` sits inside a stochastic branch — the likelihood accumulated along different control-flow paths need not correspond to any single joint measure. This is not a bug in Fubini; it is a signal that the program does not denote what the programmer thinks it denotes, and the semantics is doing its job by saying so [4].

### 4.5 Inference as Meaning-Preserving Program Transformation

Here is the payoff. An **inference representation** [4] is a structure *T* (often *not* a monad — SMC's population representation famously violates the monad laws) equipped with a **meaning function** *m : T(X) → Mass(X)* mapping the representation to the true (unnormalized) posterior mass function. A transformation *t : T → S* is **valid** iff

> **Definition (Validity):** *m_S(t(a)) = m_T(a) for all representations a — i.e., the triangle commutes.*

Inference algorithms decompose into pipelines of valid transformations:

- **SMC** [4, Thm 6.2]: `spawn` (create *n* particles) → `advance` (run each particle one step, accumulating weights) → `resample` (multinomial resampling, a valid transformation by exchangeability) → `finish` (collapse the population to a weighted empirical distribution). Each step preserves *m*; the composite therefore computes the exact posterior mass function, with Monte Carlo error entering only through the *representation*, never the meaning.
- **MCMC (trace Metropolis–Hastings)**: propose a new program trace by resampling a random subset of choices; accept with the Metropolis–Hastings ratio. Validity = **detailed balance** with respect to the posterior over traces — an equation between kernels, checked against the denotation [4].
- **Variational inference**: choose a tractable family *q_φ* and maximize the ELBO. Denotationally this is *not* meaning-preserving — it is a deliberate, controlled approximation. The honest semantic statement: VI computes *argmin_φ KL(q_φ ‖ p)*, and the reparameterization and score-function gradient estimators are unbiased for *∇_φ ELBO* by (you guessed it) Fubini–Tonelli justifying differentiation under the integral sign.

```python
# SMC as a pipeline of meaning-preserving transformations (sketch)
def smc(model, n_particles, steps):
    pop = spawn(model.prior, n_particles)      # valid: m preserved
    for _ in range(steps):
        pop = advance(pop)                      # valid: kernel application
        pop = resample(pop)                     # valid: exchangeability
    return finish(pop)                          # m(pop) == true posterior mass fn
```

The conceptual shift is profound: instead of one heroic correctness proof per algorithm, we get a **calculus of inference** in which algorithms are programs, transformations are optimizations, and validity is type-checked by the meaning function. New algorithms are built by composing old transformations — and their correctness follows from the composition.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We summarize the key formal results this semantic architecture delivers, with their proof techniques.

> **Theorem (Soundness & adequacy [2]):** *For the metalanguage with higher-order functions, continuous distributions, and soft constraints: (i) if e₁ ≡ e₂ by the equational theory then ⟦e₁⟧ = ⟦e₂⟧ (soundness); (ii) if ⟦e₁⟧ = ⟦e₂⟧ at ground type then e₁ and e₂ are observationally equivalent (adequacy). The operational reading of `sample` as allocation of a fresh read-only random variable is validated by the denotation.*

> **Theorem (QBS model properties [3]):** *Quasi-Borel spaces form a cartesian closed, well-pointed category; standard Borel spaces embed fully faithfully; the probability monad P supports continuous distributions and random functions; de Finetti's theorem generalizes.*

> **Theorem (SMC validity [4, Thm 6.2]):** *The composite transformation smc_{n,k} = finish ∘ (advance ∘ resample)^k ∘ spawn is a valid inference transformation: for every program, the weighted empirical distribution it produces has mass function equal to the program's true posterior mass function.*

> **Theorem (Trace MH validity [4]):** *The single-site Metropolis–Hastings update on program traces satisfies detailed balance with respect to the posterior measure on traces; hence the chain's stationary distribution is the program's denotation.*

These are *proofs*, not benchmarks — in denotational semantics, the "empirical result" is a machine-checked or hand-verified equation. The table below maps each inference family to its semantic status:

| Algorithm | Representation *T* | Validity criterion | Meaning preserved? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Importance sampling | weighted sampler | weight = density ratio | Yes — exactly |
| SMC (with resampling) | particle population | exchangeability + Fubini | Yes — exactly |
| Trace MCMC / MH | trace distribution | detailed balance | Yes — in stationarity |
| Gibbs sampling | conditional kernels | disintegration inverts | Yes — in stationarity |
| Variational inference | parametric family *q_φ* | ELBO maximization | No — principled approximation |
| Exact enumeration | finite support map | initiality of term model | Yes — exactly |

The last row is worth noting: on discrete models, Ścibior et al. show the *term model itself* is initial among inference representations, so enumeration is trivially valid — a elegant base case for the whole tower [4].

## 6 Limitations

No semantics is the final word, and intellectual honesty demands we mark the boundaries.

1. **Disintegration needs standard Borel.** The disintegration theorem fails on general measurable spaces, and QBSs — while cartesian closed — do not magically make every space standard Borel. Conditioning in the full higher-order language rests on analytic rather than purely categorical foundations; programs denoting measures on exotic function spaces may have no canonical conditional [3][7].
2. **Commutativity is fragile under conditioning.** As noted, `observe` inside stochastic branches and non-s-finite scoring can break the equations Fubini provides. Some implemented PPLs accept programs whose denotation is arguably undefined; the semantics flags them, but the implementations run anyway [4].
3. **VI is outside the validity framework.** Variational inference does not preserve meaning — that is its *point* (trading exactness for speed). The transformation-based correctness story covers MCMC and SMC naturally; VI needs a different contract (e.g., bounds on *KL(q ‖ p)*), which the current framework states but does not verify.
4. **Cost semantics is absent.** Two programs with equal denotations can have wildly different inference cost — a collapsed Gibbs sampler and a naive one denote the same posterior but converge at different rates. Denotational equality is too coarse to compare *inference efficiency*; that requires a quantitative refinement (e.g., convergence rates, effective sample size) the framework does not provide.
5. **Recursion and non-termination.** The development above treats a terminating metalanguage; real PPLs have recursion and potentially infinite traces. Extending the Giry/QBS story to recursive types and stochastic processes with infinite horizons requires domain-theoretic or synthetic measure theory machinery beyond our scope [2].

## 7 Conclusion

Denotational semantics turns probabilistic programming from a bag of tricks into a mathematical discipline with three load-bearing pillars. **The Giry monad** says randomness composes by integration, and its commutativity — Fubini–Tonelli — is the equational engine of program equivalence. **Quasi-Borel spaces** say higher-order probability is possible after all, provided we axiomatize random variables rather than σ-algebras. **Inference representations and meaning-preserving transformations** say that MCMC and SMC are not black-box algorithms but *provably correct program transformations*, each step checkable against the denotation it claims to compute, while conditioning itself is just disintegration — the canonical categorical form of Bayes' rule.

The practical moral is one of *separation of concerns made rigorous*: modelers write generative programs against the Giry/QBS denotation; inference designers compose valid transformations; and the meaning function *m* stands between them as a checkable contract. When an inference algorithm surprises us — biased estimates, unit anomalies, non-convergence — the semantics tells us exactly which equation broke, and therefore where to look. That is what semantics is for: not to replace inference, but to make its correctness a matter of proof rather than hope.
