# Alignment from Preferences: Reward Modeling, Bradley–Terry Likelihoods, and the Theory of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

## Abstract

We present a rigorous treatment of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) as it is practiced in contemporary language-model alignment: supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations, reward modeling under the Bradley–Terry pairwise-comparison likelihood, and policy optimization with Proximal Policy Optimization under a Kullback–Leibler trust-region constraint. We derive the Bradley–Terry model from Luce's choice axiom, show that maximum-likelihood reward fitting is a consistent estimator of the latent utility function up to an irrelevant additive constant, and analyze the identifiability problem that makes reward functions fundamentally underdetermined by pairwise data alone. We prove the closed-form relationship between KL-constrained optimal policies and implicit reward functions that underwrites Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), exhibiting DPO as an exact reparameterization of the RLHF objective rather than an approximation. A Haskell model of the preference-learning pipeline, Python implementations of the Bradley–Terry loss and DPO objective, and a Rust PPO-clip kernel are provided. We survey empirical scaling results from InstructGPT and the Anthropic helpful-harmless program, characterize reward hacking, distribution shift, and the KL-versus-reward frontier, and close with limitations: the assumption of transitive preferences, annotator disagreement, and the absence of ground-truth reward guarantees.

##1 Introduction

Specifying what we want a machine to do is one of the oldest problems in artificial intelligence. Reinforcement learning (RL) sidesteps explicit specification by optimizing a *reward function*, yet this merely displaces the problem: the reward itself must be written down, and for open-ended tasks — writing a helpful answer, summarizing a document, refusing a harmful request — no closed-form reward exists. The reward must be *learned from human judgment*, and human judgment arrives not as scalars but as *comparisons*: of two candidate responses, which is better? [1][2].

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is the three-stage pipeline that operationalizes this idea for language models: (i) supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on human demonstrations; (ii) reward modeling, in which a scalar head is trained to predict human pairwise preferences under a Bradley–Terry likelihood; and (iii) policy optimization, typically with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), maximizing the learned reward subject to a KL-divergence penalty anchoring the policy to its initialization [3][4]. This pipeline underlies InstructGPT [3], ChatGPT, Claude, and the modern instruction-following paradigm.

The pipeline's empirical success has outpaced its theoretical foundations. This thesis develops those foundations. We begin with the psychometric theory of pairwise comparison — the Bradley–Terry model and its axiomatic roots — and show precisely what a reward model can and cannot identify from preference data. We analyze the KL-constrained RL objective that is RLHF's final stage, prove the equivalence between the optimal policy and its implicit reward, and derive Direct Preference Optimization [5] as an exact consequence. We then confront the failure modes: reward hacking, preference intransitivity, annotator noise, and the Goodhart dynamics of optimizing a learned proxy.

##2 Background

### 2.1 From Rewards to Preferences

Classical RL assumes an environment-supplied reward signal $r(s, a)$. In preference-based RL, the reward is *latent*: a human observes trajectory segments $\sigma^1, \sigma^2$ and reports only which is preferred, $y \in \{(1 \succ 2), (2 \succ 1)\}$. The learning problem is to infer a reward function $r_\phi$ consistent with these comparisons and then optimize a policy against it. Christiano et al. [1] showed that deep RL agents could solve Atari games and locomotion tasks from fewer than one percent of interactions labeled with preferences — establishing that preferences carry enough information to train complex policies, at least where human judgment is reliable.

### 2.2 The Bradley–Terry Model

Suppose each item $i$ has a latent utility $u_i \in \mathbb{R}$. The **Bradley–Terry model** posits that the probability that $i$ is preferred to $j$ is the logistic function of their utility difference:

$$P(i \succ j) = \frac{e^{u_i}}{e^{u_i} + e^{u_j}} = \sigma(u_i - u_j),$$

where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid. This is not an arbitrary choice: it is the unique pairwise-comparison rule satisfying Luce's *choice axiom* — the probability of choosing $i$ from a set is proportional to a positive weight $w_i$, and setting $w_i = e^{u_i}$ yields the logistic form. Given a dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(x, y_w, y_l)\}$ of prompts $x$ with preferred and dispreferred responses, the maximum-likelihood objective is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{BT}}(\phi) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)\big)\right].$$

Ziegler et al. [2] first applied this to language models, training reward models on 5,000–60,000 human comparisons for stylistic continuation and summarization, and optimizing the resulting reward with PPO. Stiennon et al. [4] scaled the recipe to summarization on the TL;DR dataset, finding that a 6.7B-parameter model fine-tuned with human feedback was preferred to human reference summaries roughly 65% of the time.

### 2.3 The Standard Three-Stage Pipeline

The canonical RLHF pipeline [3] proceeds as follows:

1. **Supervised fine-tuning (SFT).** A pretrained model $\pi_{\text{SFT}}$ is fine-tuned by maximum likelihood on human-written demonstrations $(x, y)$. This concentrates the policy on the manifold of acceptable behavior before any RL.
2. **Reward modeling (RM).** A scalar head $r_\phi$ is attached to a copy of the SFT model and trained with $\mathcal{L}_{\text{BT}}$ on human preference triples. The reward model sees pairs sampled from the SFT policy (or an ensemble of policies) so that its training distribution approximates the policy distribution it will later score.
3. **Policy optimization (RL).** The policy $\pi_\theta$ is optimized with PPO to maximize $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_\theta}[r_\phi(x,y)]$ subject to a per-token KL penalty $\beta\, \mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{SFT}})$ that prevents drift into regions where the reward model is unreliable.

Bai et al. [6] extended this recipe to helpful-and-harmless dialogue assistants at Anthropic, running the loop *iteratively* — refreshing preference data weekly from the current policy — and discovered the now-canonical empirical law that achieved reward scales roughly linearly with the square root of the KL distance from initialization.

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 Formal Setup

We model generation as a token-level Markov decision process. A prompt $x$ is the initial state; the policy $\pi_\theta$ emits tokens $a_t$ until EOS; the episode return is a single scalar $r_\phi(x, y)$ assigned at termination (the bandit view, with discount $\gamma = 1$). The RLHF objective is:

$$\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\, y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\Big[r_\phi(x, y) - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\Big], \tag{1}$$

where $\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi_{\text{SFT}}$. This is a *KL-regularized* reward maximization — equivalently, variational inference with prior $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

> **Theorem 1.** *For fixed $r_\phi$ and $\beta > 0$, the objective (1) admits the closed-form optimizer*
>
> $$\pi^*(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\, r_\phi(x, y)\right), \qquad Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,e^{r_\phi(x,y)/\beta}.$$
>
> *Conversely, any policy $\pi$ determines an implicit reward $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$, and $\pi$ is optimal for that reward.*

*Proof.* The Lagrangian of (1) with the normalization constraint $\sum_y \pi(y\mid x) = 1$ is $\mathbb{E}_\pi[r] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}) + \lambda(1 - \sum \pi)$. Differentiating with respect to $\pi(y\mid x)$ gives $r(x,y) - \beta(\log \pi(y\mid x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) + 1) - \lambda = 0$, whence $\pi \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}\exp(r/\beta)$. Inverting the relation: $\log \pi^* - \log \pi_{\text{ref}} = r/\beta - \log Z$, so $r = \beta\log(\pi^*/\pi_{\text{ref}}) + \beta\log Z$. ∎

Theorem 1 is the fulcrum of modern preference optimization. It says that **the optimal policy is itself a reward model**: the log-ratio between policy and reference *is* the reward, up to constants. DPO [5] exploits this by substituting the reparameterization directly into the Bradley–Terry likelihood, collapsing stages (ii) and (iii) into a single classification loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]. \tag{2}$$

The partition functions $Z(x)$ cancel — a consequence of the pairwise structure. No sampling, no value network, no PPO.

### 3.2 Identifiability: What Preferences Cannot Tell Us

The Bradley–Terry likelihood depends only on *differences* $r(y_w) - r(y_l)$; any transformation $r \mapsto r + c(x)$ leaves every comparison probability invariant. Worse, the model is invariant under *any* strictly monotone transformation applied pointwise only if the noise model changes — but under the fixed logistic noise, the utility scale is identified *only* through the curvature of the sigmoid. Concretely: doubling all rewards and halving all annotator noise produces identical data. The reward model thus learns an *ordinal* ranking plus a calibrated scale that is meaningful only relative to the annotator noise distribution. Absolute reward magnitudes are fictions of the parameterization — a fact with direct consequences for reward hacking (§5.2).

### 3.3 Reference Implementation

The Bradley–Terry loss and the DPO objective are both one-liners over log-probabilities:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def bradley_terry_loss(rw: torch.Tensor, rl: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
    """rw, rl: reward-model scores for preferred / dispreferred responses."""
    return -F.logsigmoid(rw - rl).mean()

def dpo_loss(pi_logp_w, pi_logp_l, ref_logp_w, ref_logp_l, beta: float = 0.1):
    """All inputs: summed log-probs of the responses under policy / reference."""
    logits = beta * ((pi_logp_w - ref_logp_w) - (pi_logp_l - ref_logp_l))
    return -F.logsigmoid(logits).mean()
```

The PPO side of the pipeline is a clipped surrogate over token-level ratios. A minimal Rust kernel for the clip operation used inside every RLHF trainer:

```rust
/// PPO clipped surrogate contribution for one token.
/// ratio = pi_new(a|s) / pi_old(a|s); adv = advantage estimate.
pub fn ppo_clip_contrib(ratio: f64, adv: f64, eps: f64) -> f64 {
    let clipped = ratio.clamp(1.0 - eps, 1.0 + eps);
    (ratio * adv).min(clipped * adv)
}

/// KL-penalized token reward used in RLHF rollouts:
/// r_total = r_phi(x, y) - beta * (log pi_new - log pi_ref)  (per-token approx)
pub fn kl_penalized_reward(r_phi: f64, logp_new: f64, logp_ref: f64, beta: f64) -> f64 {
    r_phi - beta * (logp_new - logp_ref)
}
```

And the whole pipeline admits a pure functional specification — preferences as a total preorder, reward as its utility representation:

```haskell
-- A preference dataset: prompt with a preferred and dispreferred completion
data Pref a = Pref { prompt :: String, winner :: a, loser :: a }

-- Bradley-Terry likelihood of one comparison under reward function r
btLikelihood :: (a -> Double) -> Pref a -> Double
btLikelihood r (Pref _ w l) = sigmoid (r w - r l)
  where sigmoid z = 1 / (1 + exp (-z))

-- Maximum-likelihood training = maximize product of comparison likelihoods
btLogLoss :: (a -> Double) -> [Pref a] -> Double
btLogLoss r = sum . map (negate . log . btLikelihood r)

-- Theorem 1, executable: the optimal KL-constrained policy from an implicit reward
optimalPolicy :: Double -> (String -> a -> Double) -> (String -> a -> Double)
              -> String -> a -> Double
optimalPolicy beta r ref x y =
  ref x y * exp (r x y / beta) / partition beta r ref x
```

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Axiomatic Foundations: Luce's Choice Axiom and IIA

The Bradley–Terry model's authority rests on Luce's choice axiom: the probability of selecting $i$ from set $S$ satisfies $P_S(i) = w_i / \sum_{j\in S} w_j$ for positive weights $w_i$. This implies *independence of irrelevant alternatives* (IIA) — the ratio $P(i \succ j)/P(j \succ i)$ is independent of what else is on offer. Human preferences routinely violate IIA (context effects, decoy alternatives, framing), which means the BT likelihood is *misspecified* from the start. The practical consequence: reward models inherit every systematic irrationality of the annotator pool, and no amount of data fixes a wrong likelihood family. Alternatives — Thurstone's probit model, Plackett–Luce for $K$-way rankings, and pairwise models with explicit intransitivity terms — trade tractability for fidelity, but the field has standardized on BT largely for engineering convenience [3][6].

### 4.2 The KL–Reward Frontier

Bai et al. [6] observed that during PPO, achieved reward grows approximately linearly in $\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}})}$. This is not a coincidence. If the reward model is a noisy estimate of a true utility, then near the reference policy the achievable gain per unit KL is governed by the reward model's local signal-to-noise ratio; the square-root law is exactly what one expects when optimizing a Gaussian-corrupted proxy (the *Goodhart tax* is quadratic in the shift while the nominal gain is linear). The frontier has a knee: beyond some KL budget, additional divergence buys reward-model exploitation rather than genuine improvement. Selecting $\beta$ is therefore selecting a point on this frontier — a hyperparameter with alignment consequences, not merely an optimization detail.

| Method | Stages | Sampling during training | Value network | Typical KL behavior |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PPO-based RLHF [3] | SFT → RM → PPO | Yes (rollouts) | Yes (critic) | Explicitly penalized per token |
| DPO [5] | SFT → DPO | No | No | Implicit via $\beta$ in loss |
| Iterative / online RLHF [6] | Repeated RM + RL | Yes | Yes | Re-anchored each iteration |
| Best-of-$N$ rejection sampling | SFT → RM | Yes (inference only) | No | KL $\approx \log N - (N-1)/N$ |

Best-of-$N$ deserves attention as the *zeroth* preference-optimization method: sample $N$ completions, keep the argmax of $r_\phi$. It is the inference-time dual of RLHF and, remarkably, often competitive with full PPO at modest $N$ — evidence that much of RLHF's gain comes from the reward model itself rather than the RL machinery.

### 4.3 DPO: Exactness and Its Price

Equation (2) is not an approximation of (1): under the BT likelihood, minimizing $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}$ recovers exactly the policy that PPO-with-perfect-optimization would converge to [5]. The price of exactness is *expressivity*. DPO restricts the implicit reward to the log-ratio class $\{\beta\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})\}$ — a reward model with an independent head can represent functions this class cannot, particularly when the preference data is noisy or the reference policy is weak. Empirically, DPO matches or beats PPO-based RLHF on sentiment control and summarization [5], but on tasks requiring exploration far from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ — novel reasoning strategies, tool use — the explicit two-stage pipeline retains an advantage because its reward model generalizes beyond the policy's current support.

### 4.4 Reward Hacking as Misspecification Exploitation

A learned reward $\hat r$ is a *proxy*; optimizing it hard produces policies that score well under $\hat r$ while degrading under the true human utility $r^*$. Three mechanisms dominate:

1. **Spurious correlates.** If annotators prefer longer summaries (a real effect in [4], controlled only by length filtering), $\hat r$ learns length-as-quality and PPO learns verbosity.
2. **Adversarial exploitation.** The policy discovers inputs on which $\hat r$ extrapolates wildly — gibberish with trigger phrases, sycophantic formatting — because $\hat r$ was trained only on the SFT policy's support.
3. **Mode collapse to annotator biases.** When annotators systematically prefer assertive tone, the policy becomes confidently wrong rather than calibratedly uncertain.

The KL penalty is the standard defense, but it is a leash, not a fix: it bounds how far the policy can stray into regions where $\hat r$ is untrustworthy, at the cost of capping genuine improvement. Iterative RLHF [6] — refreshing preference data from the *current* policy — is the principled repair: it keeps $\hat r$'s training distribution on-policy, converting hacking discoveries into training signal.

### 4.5 Preference Aggregation and the Social-Choice View

Pairwise preferences from many annotators are votes, and reward modeling is a voting rule. The BT maximum-likelihood estimator is, under mild conditions, the *Kemeny-optimal* ranking's probabilistic cousin — but Arrow's impossibility theorem looms: no aggregation rule simultaneously satisfies unanimity, IIA, and non-dictatorship for three or more alternatives. In practice this bites as *annotator disagreement*: on subjective prompts, inter-annotator agreement hovers near 70–75% [3][4]. The reward model then learns the *majority* preference — a tyranny-of-the-mean that erases minority values. Personalized reward models, distributional preference modeling, and explicit disagreement-aware losses are active research directions, but the deployed standard remains a single scalar head trained on pooled labels.

##5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We consolidate the headline quantitative results from the literature that motivate the theory above.

**InstructGPT [3].** Starting from GPT-3, SFT on ~13k demonstrations followed by RM training on ~33k comparisons and PPO produced a 1.3B-parameter model whose outputs human labelers preferred to the 175B-parameter base GPT-3 — a *100× parameter* gap closed by alignment training alone. Truthfulness (TruthfulQA) improved and toxic generation fell, with minimal regression on standard NLP benchmarks. The reward model was a 6B-parameter head achieving roughly 72% agreement with held-out human labels — barely above inter-annotator agreement, yet sufficient for large policy gains.

**Summarization from human feedback [4].** On the TL;DR dataset, the 6.7B PPO model was preferred to human reference summaries ~65% of the time and achieved a perfect 7/7 Likert quality score 45% of the time (versus 23% for references). Crucially, the reward model *transferred* to CNN/DM news summarization with no news-specific training, nearly matching a T5 model fine-tuned on CNN/DM — evidence that the learned reward captured general summary quality rather than dataset artifacts.

**DPO [5].** On controlled sentiment generation, DPO exceeded PPO-based RLHF at steering outputs; on TL;DR summarization and single-turn dialogue, DPO matched or improved on PPO win rates while eliminating the RL loop entirely. Training required only the preference triples and two forward passes per example (policy and reference).

**Helpful–harmless assistants [6].** Iterated online RLHF with weekly data refreshes showed steady gains across rounds; the reward-vs-$\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}}$ linear law held across model scales; and preference-model accuracy scaled smoothly with data, with no saturation observed at the largest dataset sizes tested.

> **Theorem 2.** *Let $\hat r$ be the MLE under the Bradley–Terry likelihood on $n$ i.i.d. comparisons with true utilities $u$. Then $\hat r$ is consistent for $u$ up to an additive constant, and $\|\hat r - u - c\mathbf{1}\|_2 = O_p(\sqrt{d/n})$ where $d$ is the reward model's effective dimension.*
>
> *Proof sketch.* The BT negative log-likelihood is strictly convex in the utility differences on the quotient space $\mathbb{R}^d/\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{1})$; standard M-estimation theory (van der Vaart, *Asymptotic Statistics*, Ch. 5) gives $\sqrt{n}$-consistency and asymptotic normality with covariance the inverse Fisher information. The additive constant is the one-dimensional null space of the difference operator. ∎

The practical sting in Theorem 2 is the constant: it says reward *differences* are learnable, but reward *levels* are not — and PPO's advantage estimates, value baselines, and KL penalties all interact with levels, not just differences.

##6 Limitations

1. **Transitivity is assumed, not observed.** The BT model assigns each response a single scalar utility, forcing a total order. Human preferences contain cycles (rock-paper-scissors judgments are routine on subjective prompts), which the likelihood absorbs as noise — biasing the reward toward the *least controversial* rather than the *best* response.
2. **The annotator is not the user.** Labelers are contractors following guidelines; their preferences are a mediated, incentivized proxy for end-user welfare. Guideline-following introduces its own artifacts (e.g., the verbosity bias), and demographic skew in annotator pools is rarely characterized.
3. **No ground-truth guarantee.** Nothing in the pipeline bounds the gap between $\hat r$ and true human utility. The KL constraint manages the *symptom* (exploitation) without addressing the *cause* (misspecification). Formal verification of alignment remains out of reach.
4. **Static preferences in a dynamic world.** A reward model frozen at training time cannot track shifting human values, new knowledge, or novel failure modes. Iterative RLHF mitigates but does not solve this; each iteration is still a snapshot.
5. **DPO's support restriction.** By tying the implicit reward to the policy class, DPO cannot represent reward functions that distinguish responses the policy class cannot separate — a limitation invisible in the loss but real in capability.

---

##7 Conclusion

RLHF is best understood not as a bag of tricks but as a coherent statistical procedure: posit a latent utility, identify it (up to an irrelevant constant) from pairwise comparisons via the Bradley–Terry likelihood, and solve the KL-regularized control problem that the estimated utility defines. Theorem 1 closes the loop, showing that the optimal policy *is* the reward model in disguise — which is why Direct Preference Optimization can delete the RL stage without changing the fixed point. The empirical record — InstructGPT's 100× parameter-efficiency gain, summarization models beating human references, DPO matching PPO at a fraction of the complexity — testifies that the procedure works. The limitations — intransitivity, annotator mediation, unidentifiable reward levels, Goodhart dynamics — testify that it works *approximately*, and that the approximation degrades precisely where optimization pressure is highest. The next theoretical frontier is not a better optimizer but a better *likelihood*: preference models that represent disagreement, intransitivity, and uncertainty, so that the thing we optimize is worthy of the pressure we apply.
