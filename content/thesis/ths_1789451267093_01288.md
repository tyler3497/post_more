# Seafloor Geodesy and Subduction Zone Coupling: GNSS-Acoustic Observations, Slow Slip Events, and Tsunami Hazard Assessment

## Abstract

The world's most hazardous faults lie offshore, where conventional satellite geodesy cannot observe them because seawater absorbs and scatters radio waves. The combination of the Global Navigation Satellite System with underwater acoustic ranging (GNSS-A) overcomes this blind spot: a sea-surface platform is positioned kinematically by GNSS while acoustic signals range it to an array of seafloor transponders, yielding absolute seafloor positions with one to two centimeters of horizontal precision in the best modern implementations [10]. Deployed for more than two decades by the Japan Coast Guard's Hydrographic and Oceanographic Department along the Nankai Trough, Sagami Trough, and Japan Trench, GNSS-A has exposed strongly heterogeneous interseismic coupling, previously unknown regions of high slip-deficit rate critical for tsunami mitigation, and shallow slow slip events correlated with very-low-frequency seismicity [1][2]. Decadal observations now confine the constantly locked seismogenic zone mainly to ten to twenty kilometers depth, and network-optimization analysis for Cascadia shows one well-placed seafloor station can deliver roughly thirty times the information gain of the best onshore station [3][5].

## 1 Introduction

Earthquakes and tsunamis that originate at subduction zones have repeatedly produced the costliest and deadliest natural disasters of the modern era. The 1944 Tonankai (magnitude 7.9) and 1946 Nankaido (magnitude 8.0) earthquakes ruptured segments of the Nankai Trough, and the 2011 Tohoku earthquake (moment magnitude 9.0) moved the seafloor near the Japan Trench by tens of meters horizontally and generated a tsunami that overwhelmed coastal defenses designed from an underestimate of offshore slip [1][8]. A single quantity governs whether such events remain locked-in strain or dissipate harmlessly: the *coupling* of the plate interface, that is, the fraction of plate convergence that is stored as elastic strain rather than released by aseismic creep.

For half a century, coupling has been inferred from land-based geodetic networks, satellite interferometry, and seismic catalogs. The difficulty is geometric and unforgiving. In most subduction zones the locked megathrust lies tens to hundreds of kilometers offshore, seaward of the coastline, while geodetic stations sit on land. Inversion for offshore slip from onshore displacement is an ill-posed problem: the elastic Green's functions decay with distance, so a wide family of offshore slip distributions can explain identical onshore data. The tsunami-relevant quantity, the slip-deficit rate within a few tens of kilometers of the trench, is precisely the quantity least constrained by land data.

Seafloor geodesy changes the observation geometry at its root. Because electromagnetic waves cannot propagate through seawater, absolute positioning of the seafloor must be mediated by sound. The GNSS-acoustic (GNSS-A) technique, proposed by Spiess in the mid-1980s and matured by the Japan Coast Guard from the mid-1990s onward, couples radio-wave GNSS positioning above the sea surface with acoustic-wave ranging below it [4][6]. A survey vessel (and increasingly, uncrewed surface vehicles) holding station above a seafloor transponder array becomes a moving geodetic monument whose three-dimensional position is known at the centimeter level; the transponder positions, estimated from thousands of acoustic travel-time measurements, inherit that precision. Campaigns repeated over years resolve interseismic velocities, coseismic steps, postseismic transients, and slow slip events directly above the seismogenic zone.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the subduction-zone earthquake cycle and the phenomenology of slow slip. Section 3 formalizes the GNSS-A forward problem, the sound-speed structure estimation that dominates its error budget, and the inversion of displacement rates into coupling. Section 4 develops five deep technical threads: the forward problem and sound-speed gradient extraction, heterogeneous coupling at the Nankai Trough, the slow-slip spectrum and the slip budget, the coupling-to-tsunami-hazard pathway, and optimal network design. Section 5 presents the empirical record: decadal Nankai results, the Tohoku coseismic and postseismic seafloor record, and information-theoretic network gains. Sections 6 and 7 assess limitations and draw conclusions for the next generation of offshore observatories.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The subduction earthquake cycle

A convergent plate boundary stores energy when the interface between the subducting slab and the overriding plate is *locked*: friction prevents relative motion, and the overriding forearc is dragged landward and downward while convergence continues at the plate rate. The accumulating elastic strain is quantified by the **slip-deficit rate (SDR)**, the difference between the long-term plate convergence rate and the actual aseismic slip rate on the interface. Regions of positive SDR are the candidate source regions of future megathrust earthquakes. When the accumulated stress exceeds the frictional strength, the interface ruptures in seconds to minutes (the coseismic phase), followed by months to years of **afterslip** on surrounding conditionally stable regions and **viscoelastic relaxation** of the mantle wedge and oceanic asthenosphere.

Laboratory friction and field observation motivate a partition of the interface into regimes: **velocity-weakening** patches capable of unstable, seismic rupture; **velocity-strengthening** regions that creep stably; and a transitional, conditionally stable regime hosting slow slip. The spatial arrangement of these regimes — the *coupling map* — is the central unknown of subduction-zone hazard.

### 2.2 Slow earthquakes: the aseismic spectrum

Slow slip events (SSEs) release strain over hours to months with negligible seismic radiation, spanning moment magnitudes from about 5 to above 7.5. They form a continuum with related phenomena: non-volcanic tremor, low-frequency earthquakes, and very-low-frequency (VLF) events [10]. Along the Nankai Trough, short-term SSEs recur on the deep interface downdip of the locked zone with characteristic intervals of months to years, while shallow SSEs occur near the trench in the updip region most relevant to tsunami generation. Along the Ryukyu Trench, systematic GNSS analysis identified 130 probable and 93 possible short-term SSEs of moment magnitude 5.6 to 6.8 between 1997 and 2013, with a notable population of shallow events at depths of twenty kilometers or less — a depth range where the Nankai Trough shows no such events — and distributions clearly modulated by subducting-plate topography [13]. The empirical moment-duration scaling of slow events departs from the cubic scaling of regular earthquakes, indicating distinct physics: rate-and-state friction with near-neutral velocity dependence, elevated pore-fluid pressure, and dilatancy strengthening.

Critically for hazard, SSEs *subtract from the slip budget*. If a patch slips aseismically at rate *v_sse*, only *v_plate − v_sse* accumulates as deficit. A coupling model that ignores SSEs overestimates future seismic moment; one that mislocates them misplaces the locked asperities that will rupture.

### 2.3 Why offshore observations are decisive

Consider the forward model for surface displacement **d** from interface slip **s**:

> **Theorem (offshore resolution deficit):** For a megathrust whose trench lies a distance *L* offshore of the nearest geodetic station, the sensitivity of onshore displacement to trench-proximal slip decays approximately as *L^−3* in a homogeneous elastic half-space. Hence the null space of the onshore-only inverse problem concentrates exactly on the tsunami-relevant updip region.

The practical consequence was demonstrated before 2011 off Tohoku: onshore-only inversions placed the locked zone deep and underestimated the shallow slip potential. Seafloor GPS-A stations recorded the coseismic truth — horizontal displacements reaching 74 meters and vertical displacements up to 5 meters near the trench axis [8], with repeated bathymetry showing nearly 50 meters of horizontal motion of the lower trench slope [8]. The shallow megathrust, long assumed to creep or to host only modest slip, had in fact accumulated an enormous deficit and released it in a single event. No onshore network could have falsified the pre-2011 models; only seafloor data could, and did, after the fact. The lesson for Nankai, Cascadia, and every other offshore megathrust is direct: hazard models without offshore geodetic constraint are underdetermined where it matters most.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The GNSS-A observation principle

A GNSS-A site comprises an array of typically three to six acoustic transponders cemented or moored on the seafloor, with the array diameter chosen to match the water depth (kilometers in the deep ocean). A surface platform — historically a research vessel, increasingly a wave glider or uncrewed aerial-marine vehicle [6] — traverses a survey pattern above the array while:

1. **Kinematic GNSS positioning** determines the platform antenna position at centimeter precision, corrected for attitude (pitch, roll, heading) to transfer the antenna position to the hull-mounted acoustic transducer.
2. **Acoustic ranging** measures round-trip travel times between the transducer and each transponder, thousands of shots per campaign epoch, at acoustic frequencies chosen to balance range resolution against attenuation.
3. **Sound-speed profiling** provides a reference sound-speed profile *v_0(z)* from expendable or shipboard CTD casts, because range equals travel time multiplied by a sound speed that varies with temperature, pressure, and salinity.

The seafloor transponder coordinates and a parameterized sound-speed structure (SSS) are estimated jointly by nonlinear least squares. In compact notation, with observed travel times **t**, platform positions **x_p**, transponder positions **x_s**, and SSS parameters **θ**:

```python
def gnss_a_residual(t_obs, x_platform, x_transponder, theta_sss):
    """Travel-time residual for one acoustic shot."""
    # acoustic range via ray tracing through sound-speed model
    rng = ray_trace_range(x_platform, x_transponder, theta_sss)
    t_pred = 2.0 * rng / sound_speed_along_path(theta_sss)
    return t_obs - t_pred

# Gauss-Newton update over transponder positions + SSS parameters
dx = solve(J.T @ W @ J + lam * R, J.T @ W @ residuals)
```

The Jacobian **J** couples transponder-position partials with SSS partials; the regularization **R** encodes prior smoothness of the sound-speed field [4].

### 3.2 The sound-speed structure problem

The SSS is the dominant error source. Lateral gradients of sound speed — internal waves, eddies, fronts — at kilometer scales systematically bias travel times. A decisive methodological advance extracts the *gradient field* of the SSS directly from travel-time residuals: because each shot samples a different azimuth and the residuals of a correct model are uncorrelated with shot geometry, any azimuthal structure in the residuals maps to horizontal SSS gradients [10]. This empirical gradient extraction improved positioning accuracy to about one to two centimeters in the best cases and, as a byproduct, yields oceanographic data on kilometer-scale sound-speed structure. Recent work evaluates ocean physics models as priors for the SSS, a promising route to further suppressing this error term [6].

Error budget (representative deep-ocean campaign epoch):

| Error source | Magnitude | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| GNSS kinematic positioning | 1–3 cm | Multi-constellation, long baselines, PPP-AR |
| Transducer/attitude transfer | 1–2 cm | Fiber-optic gyro + motion sensor fusion |
| SSS vertical profile error | 1–5 cm | CTD casts, joint SSS estimation |
| SSS horizontal gradients | 2–10 cm | Residual gradient extraction [10] |
| Transponder clock drift | sub-cm | Long-baseline differencing |
| Multipath / seafloor scattering | 1–2 cm | Array geometry, shot stacking |

### 3.3 From positions to coupling: the inverse problem

Repeated campaigns yield a seafloor position time series per site. The interseismic velocity is the linear trend after removing coseismic steps (e.g., the 2011 Tohoku offsets), postseismic decay, and SSE transients. These velocities, combined with onshore GNSS, enter a Bayesian inversion for the SDR distribution on a discretized plate interface using elastic dislocation Green's functions. The inversion is regularized by spatial smoothing and informed by a prior coupling model; resolution is quantified by the model resolution matrix and, increasingly, by information-theoretic experimental design [5].

A complementary technique, **direct-path acoustic ranging**, deploys paired instruments across the trench axis and measures baseline-length change continuously with precision better than two centimeters per year, directly testing whether shallow near-trench slip is locked or creeping [7]. Seafloor **absolute pressure gauges** in cabled networks (notably DONET/DONET2 off the Kii Peninsula) add the vertical component: pressure records coseismic uplift and, at higher frequencies, the tsunami itself [11][12].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The forward problem and sound-speed gradient extraction

The forward problem is a coupled estimation: transponder geometry and the ocean's sound-speed field are both unknown, and both enter every travel-time observation.

### 4.2 Heterogeneous coupling at the Nankai Trough

Six seafloor sites observed from 2006 to early 2011 revealed a heterogeneous coupling pattern with three salient features [1]:

- **Strong coupling (above 75%)** in the easternmost area, covering not only the anticipated Tokai earthquake source region but also the Paleo-Zenisu ridge — a subducting topographic high whose surroundings, paradoxically, remain locked.
- **Moderate coupling (50% to 75%)** near the trench axis in the Kumano-nada Sea, within the shallow portion of the 1944 Tonankai source region.
- **Weakened coupling (about 50%)** seaward of the 1946 Nankaido source region off Cape Muroto-zaki, plausibly caused by a subducting seamount.

The seamount result is nuanced rather than universal: whether a subducting seamount strengthens or weakens coupling depends on its geometry and on the frictional parameters of the surrounding interface [1].

### 4.3 The slow-slip spectrum and the slip budget

GNSS-A campaigns at roughly four epochs per year, sustained over a decade, resolve not only the secular trend but its interruptions.

The slip-budget arithmetic is unforgiving.

### 4.4 From coupling to tsunami hazard

Tsunami amplitude is controlled primarily by the vertical component of seafloor displacement and its spatial wavelength, both of which are maximized by large slip on the shallow, near-trench megathrust — exactly the region resolved by GNSS-A and invisible to onshore networks.

For early warning, offshore observations buy the one commodity that matters: time.

### 4.5 Optimal network design and the information economics of the seafloor

Seafloor geodesy is expensive — ship time, transponder servicing, and data recovery cost orders of magnitude more per station than onshore GNSS — so station placement must be treated as an experimental-design problem.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

**Decadal Nankai coupling and its transience.** The Nankai Trough is the only subduction zone in the world with high-density, decadal-scale seafloor observations of both horizontal and vertical components at sufficient frequency [3]. Analyzing long-term slip-deficit-rate variations, Yokota and colleagues constrained the *constantly locked* area — the portion of the interface that remains locked through SSE cycles — to depths of mainly ten to twenty kilometers, with SDR changes occurring throughout the entire shallow side adjacent to the locked area [3]. A snapshot coupling map, however precise, is therefore insufficient: the frictional state of the shallow megathrust is a time-dependent quantity, and hazard models must be conditioned on where the interface stands in its slow-slip-modulated cycle.

**The Tohoku coseismic and postseismic seafloor record.** The 2011 Tohoku earthquake remains the largest event ever captured by seafloor geodesy in and around its source region. Beyond the headline coseismic displacements, the extended postseismic GPS-A network along the Japan Trench revealed pronounced along-trench structure in displacement rates: distinct *landward* rates within the primary rupture area, evidencing the dominance of viscoelastic relaxation; remarkable *trenchward* rates south of the rupture area, indicating rapid afterslip; and slight trenchward rates to the north [9]. These spatial patterns simultaneously constrain viscoelastic structure, afterslip distribution, and the relocking of the plate interface — a decomposition impossible from onshore data alone, where all three signals project onto the same coastal stations.

**Quantitative detection thresholds.** The mature JHOD system resolves shallow SSEs and coupling heterogeneity at the one-to-two-centimeter level with campaign frequencies near four per year [10]; direct-path acoustic ranging across the Japan Trench achieves baseline-rate precision below two centimeters per year, sufficient to test for the presence or absence of shallow interplate motion directly [7]. At these thresholds, a decade of observation distinguishes a locked trench (slip deficit accumulating at the full plate rate) from a creeping one (deficit near zero) at the several-sigma level — the exact discrimination on which tsunami hazard scenarios hinge.

**Information gain, quantified.** The Cascadia design study's headline number — a well-placed seafloor observation worth up to thirty onshore stations in entropy reduction — has a concrete operational reading: for fixed budget, offshore expansion dominates onshore densification for every offshore hazard objective tested [5]. This inverts the historical funding logic that built dense onshore networks first; the marginal geodetic dollar now buys more hazard information offshore.

## 6 Limitations

Seafloor geodesy remains a young, expensive, and imperfectly sampled discipline, and its results must be read with the following caveats.

- **Temporal aliasing.** Campaign frequencies of a few epochs per year cannot resolve short-term SSEs or separate seasonal oceanographic signals from tectonic transients without long baselines; the deep Nankai short-term SSE cycle in particular is undersampled by seafloor campaigns alone.
- **Sound-speed systematics.** Despite gradient extraction and ocean-model priors, unmodeled SSS structure remains the largest error term, and its correlation structure can mimic small tectonic signals if the survey geometry is unfavorable [6][10].
- **Reference-frame and vertical precision.** Vertical seafloor positioning is intrinsically noisier than horizontal in the GNSS-A geometry; absolute pressure gauges help but measure pressure, not geometric height, conflating ocean mass, steric, and tectonic signals.
- **Inversion non-uniqueness.** Coupling inversions still depend on smoothing choices, elastic structure assumptions (homogeneous half-space versus heterogeneous 3D), and the poorly known geometry of the shallow plate interface; different regularizations shift SDR between adjacent patches.
- **Sparse spatial coverage.** Even at Nankai, seafloor sites are tens of kilometers apart — coarse relative to the kilometer-scale segmentation suggested by VLF and seamount distributions. Cascadia, Chile, Sumatra, and the Aleutians have far sparser or no sustained offshore networks [5].
- **Cost and continuity.** Ship-based campaigns are vulnerable to funding gaps; a single missed epoch degrades transient detection for years. Autonomous platforms mitigate but do not yet eliminate this fragility.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Seafloor geodesy has transformed subduction-zone science from extrapolation to observation. GNSS-A measurements have shown that interplate coupling is heterogeneous at the scale of seamounts and ridges, complementary to shallow slow-earthquake activity, and variable in time on the shallow megathrust adjacent to the locked zone [1][2][3][10]. The 2011 Tohoku earthquake demonstrated both the cost of offshore ignorance — tens of meters of unanticipated shallow slip — and the power of offshore measurement to record it [8][9]. The path forward is clear: densify seafloor networks at the trench-proximal hanging wall where information gain is maximal [5], transition to autonomous and cabled platforms for temporal continuity [6][12], assimilate offshore pressure data into real-time tsunami forecasting [11], and treat coupling as the time-dependent quantity that decadal observations reveal it to be [3]. The next magnitude-9 earthquake will rupture a fault that is today accumulating a measurable slip deficit on the seafloor; whether we are watching it accumulate is now an engineering and funding decision, not a physical impossibility.
