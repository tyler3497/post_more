# Perovskite–Silicon Tandem Photovoltaics: Defect Passivation, Halide Segregation Kinetics, and the Path Beyond 33% Power Conversion Efficiency

## Abstract

Monolithic perovskite–silicon tandems have crossed the single-junction Shockley–Queisser ceiling in certified laboratory cells, with two-terminal devices reaching **35.5%** power conversion efficiency and a credible roadmap toward the **~43%** detailed-balance tandem limit [3][6]. This article develops the physics and materials science behind that ascent. We revisit the detailed-balance argument for the *1.68 eV / 1.12 eV* bandgap pairing and the current-matching constraint that governs two-terminal architectures; we systematize the defect chemistry of wide-bandgap mixed-halide perovskites — undercoordinated lead, halide vacancies and interstitials — and the passivation toolkit (Lewis-base coordination, two-dimensional capping layers, alkali-metal additives, self-assembled monolayer contacts) that has lifted quasi-Fermi-level splittings toward the radiative limit; and we analyze the kinetics of photo-induced halide segregation, the reversible demixing first reported by Hoke and co-workers [4], as a coupled ionic–electronic instability whose timescale, activation energy and reversibility dictate top-cell voltage. Synthesizing certified record data from LONGi and Oxford PV [5][6] with the NREL hybrid-tandem record tables [7], we argue that the remaining gap between 35.5% and 40%+ is no longer an optical problem but a defect-kinetic one — and we identify the passivation and compositional strategies most likely to close it.

## 1 Introduction

Photovoltaics is the cheapest source of new electricity in most of the world, and crystalline silicon supplies more than 95% of it. Yet silicon is a mature technology: record single-junction cells now sit within a few absolute efficiency points of the ~29.4% Auger-limited ceiling for the 1.12 eV bandgap, and the entire single-junction paradigm is bounded by the **33.7%** Shockley–Queisser limit at one sun [3][6]. Incremental gains — larger wafers, better metallization, heterojunction contacts — continue to reduce cost, but they cannot change the thermodynamics. The only demonstrated route to step-change efficiency at standard illumination is the *tandem*: stacking absorbers of different bandgaps so that high-energy photons are converted at high voltage in a wide-gap top cell while low-energy photons are harvested below.

Metal-halide perovskites are the near-ideal tandem partner for silicon. Their bandgap is continuously tunable from ~1.2 to ~3.0 eV through halide and cation alloying [4]; their absorption coefficients exceed 10⁵ cm⁻¹, so sub-micron films suffice; and their charge-carrier diffusion lengths, famously shown to exceed one micrometer in mixed-halide compositions, justify planar thin-film architectures [2]. The field's trajectory — from Kojima's 3.8% sensitized cells in 2009 [1] to certified 35.5% monolithic tandems in 2026 [6] — is the steepest learning curve in the history of photovoltaics, now formally tracked by NREL under a dedicated *Hybrid Tandems* record category [7].

But the top cell is also the tandem's weakest link. Wide-bandgap mixed bromide–iodide perovskites, the compositions needed for the ideal ~1.68 eV top absorber, suffer from two entangled pathologies: **defect-mediated non-radiative recombination**, which caps the open-circuit voltage far below its radiative limit, and **photo-induced halide segregation**, in which illumination drives reversible demixing into iodide-rich and bromide-rich domains that pin the emission — and the voltage — to a low-gap trap phase [4]. This article treats these not as separate topics but as one coupled problem. Section 2 establishes the detailed-balance and architectural background; Section 3 describes our analytical methodology; Section 4 develops the deep theory across four subsections; Section 5 confronts theory with certified empirical results; Section 6 states the limitations honestly; and Section 7 maps the road past 40%.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Detailed balance and why tandems exist

In 1961, Shockley and Queisser computed the limiting efficiency of a *p–n* junction under the principle of detailed balance: every recombination event not extracted as current must be balanced, in the ideal case, by radiative emission alone.

For a two-junction device the detailed-balance ceiling rises to roughly **42–44%** at one sun, a result established by De Vos in 1980 for tandem geometries and routinely quoted by industry as "over 43%" for perovskite-on-silicon versus "less than 30%" for silicon alone [5][6].

### 2.2 Architectures: 2T, 4T, and the current-matching constraint

Tandems are built in three electrical topologies:

1. **Two-terminal (2T) monolithic** — subcells grown in one stack, series-connected through a recombination junction. One substrate, one encapsulation, but the subcells must be *current-matched*: the stack current is the minimum of the two subcell currents.
2. **Four-terminal (4T) mechanically stacked** — subcells fabricated independently and wired separately. No current matching, at the price of extra substrates, transparent electrodes and wiring.
3. **Three-terminal (3T)** — a compromise with a common middle contact, pursued for its tolerance to spectral mismatch.

The industry has converged on **2T monolithic** devices for manufacturability, which makes current matching the central device-design constraint and elevates the optical and electronic quality of the wide-gap top cell to first-order importance.

### 2.3 A compressed history

The perovskite story begins with Kojima *et al.* (2009), who sensitized mesoporous TiO₂ with CH₃NH₃PbBr₃ and CH₃NH₃PbI₃ nanocrystals, reaching 3.8% with spectral response to 800 nm and a striking 0.96 V photovoltage from the bromide composition [1].

## 3 Methodology

This article is an analytical synthesis, not an experimental report.

- **Literature synthesis** of the primary record: the founding papers [1][2][3][4], certified industrial records [5][6], and the NREL record-chart methodology [7], cross-checked against a modern exposition of the Shockley–Queisser model [8].
- **Quantitative detailed-balance modeling.** We implement the standard radiative-limit integration over the AM1.5G spectrum in Python (§4.1) to derive short-circuit currents, radiative open-circuit voltages and the tandem ceiling for the 1.68 eV / 1.12 eV pair from first principles.
- **Kinetic analysis of segregation.** We cast photo-induced halide demixing as a coupled ionic–electronic rate process, extracting timescales and activation-energy scales from the Hoke phenomenology [4] and modeling vacancy-mediated hopping with a kinetic Monte Carlo sketch in Rust (§4.3).
- **Formal specification of device constraints.** We express the series current-matching invariant and the segregation state machine in TLA⁺ (§4.3), making the logical structure of 2T design rules machine-checkable.

Our figures of merit throughout are the **quasi-Fermi-level splitting (QFLS)** of the top-cell absorber, the **external photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY)**, the **V_OC deficit** relative to the radiative limit, and the **Urbach energy** as a disorder metric.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Detailed-Balance Case for the 1.68 eV / 1.12 eV Pair

> **Theorem: (Detailed balance, Shockley–Queisser 1961).** *In steady state, an ideal photovoltaic absorber in the radiative limit emits exactly as many photons as it absorbs above its bandgap minus those extracted as current. The open-circuit voltage is therefore bounded by the radiative value* $V_{OC}^{rad} = \frac{kT}{q}\ln\!\left(\frac{J_{SC}}{J_0} + 1\right)$*, where $J_0$ is the dark radiative saturation current. Any non-radiative recombination increases $J_0$ and depresses $V_{OC}$ logarithmically — the entire game of defect passivation is the reduction of $J_0$.*

For a 2T tandem, the subcells are in electrical series, so the stack obeys a hard constraint:

$$J_{tandem} = \min(J_{top},\, J_{bottom}), \qquad V_{tandem} = V_{top} + V_{bottom}$$

Current matching is therefore an *optical design* problem as much as an electronic one: the top-cell thickness, bandgap and parasitic absorption must be tuned so that the filtered bottom cell generates the same current as the top.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.constants import h, c, k, e

def planck_photon_flux(E_eV, T=6000.0):
    """Photon flux per unit energy from a blackbody (photons / m^2 / s / eV)."""
    E = E_eV * e
    return (2 * np.pi * E**2) / (h**3 * c**2) / (np.exp(E / (k * T)) - 1) / e

def detailed_balance(Eg_eV, T_sun=6000.0, T_cell=300.0, concentration=1.0):
    E = np.linspace(Eg_eV, 4.5, 4000)
    phi_sun = planck_photon_flux(E, T_sun) * concentration * 2.16e-5  # dilution
    phi_cell = planck_photon_flux(E, T_cell)
    Jsc = e * np.trapz(phi_sun, E)          # A / m^2
    J0 = e * np.trapz(phi_cell, E)           # radiative dark current
    Voc_rad = (k * T_cell / e) * np.log(Jsc / J0 + 1)
    return Jsc / 10.0, Voc_rad              # mA/cm^2, V

for Eg in (1.12, 1.34, 1.68):
    Jsc, Voc = detailed_balance(Eg)
    print(f"Eg={Eg:.2f} eV -> Jsc={Jsc:.1f} mA/cm^2, Voc(rad)={Voc:.2f} V")
```

Applied to the tandem pair, the arithmetic is illuminating rather than merely impressive.

### 4.2 Defect Chemistry and Passivation of Wide-Bandgap Perovskites

Wide-bandgap compositions — typically FA/Cs-based lead halides with 20–40% bromide on the X-site, e.g.

The defect inventory that matters for tandems is short and specific:

| Defect | Physical character | Typical passivation strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Undercoordinated Pb²⁺ at surfaces/grain boundaries | Lewis-acid electron trap | Lewis bases: thiophene, pyridine, alkylammonium halides |
| Halide vacancy $V_I / V_{Br}$ | Mobile ionic defect; non-radiative center | Halide-rich stoichiometry; K⁺/Rb⁺ immobilization |
| Interstitial halide $X_i$ | Deep trap in Br-rich lattices | Compositional tuning; 2D capping barriers |
| A-site cation disorder / vacancies | Band-edge fluctuations (Urbach tail) | Cs⁺/FA⁺/MA⁺ triple-cation alloying |
| Interface recombination at transport layers | QFLS loss at contacts | Self-assembled monolayers (2PACz, MeO-2PACz) |

Two quantitative relations govern how passivation translates into voltage.

$$q \cdot V_{OC}^{imp} = \Delta\mu = \Delta\mu^{rad} + kT \ln(\mathrm{PLQY}_{ext})$$

so that every order of magnitude gained in external photoluminescence quantum yield is worth ~60 mV at room temperature.

### 4.3 Kinetics of Photo-Induced Halide Segregation

The most consequential instability in wide-gap perovskites was reported by Hoke *et al.* (2015): under constant ~1-sun illumination, the photoluminescence of (CH₃NH₃)Pb(BrₓI₁₋ₓ)₃ films develops a new **red-shifted peak at 1.68 eV within less than a minute**, accompanied by increased sub-bandgap absorption near ~1.7 eV and a splitting of X-ray diffraction peaks into two crystalline phases — and all of it **reverses within minutes in the dark** [4].

> **Theorem: (Segregation driving force).** *Let $\Delta G_{mix} < 0$ be the free energy of mixing of the homogeneous alloy and $\Delta G_{loc}$ the free-energy gain from localizing a photogenerated carrier into a low-gap iodide-rich nucleus. Under illumination at carrier density $n$, segregation proceeds whenever $n \cdot |\Delta G_{loc}| > |\Delta G_{mix}|$ per nucleation site. The effect is therefore intrinsically photo-driven: it vanishes in the dark, where only $\Delta G_{mix}$ operates, explaining the observed reversibility.*

Microscopically, the kinetics are those of **vacancy-mediated halide hopping**.

```rust
// Halide vacancy hopping on a 2D perovskite X-site sublattice (sketch).
// Hop rate follows Arrhenius kinetics; illumination lowers the barrier
// in the vicinity of trapped carriers, nucleating I-rich domains.
struct Lattice { sites: Vec<f64>, t: f64 } // site halide identity: 0.0 = I, 1.0 = Br

impl Lattice {
    fn hop_rate(&self, barrier_ev: f64, temp_k: f64, illuminated: bool) -> f64 {
        let kb_t = 8.617e-5 * temp_k;                 // eV
        let b = if illuminated { barrier_ev - 0.15 }   // carrier-assisted lowering
                else { barrier_ev };
        1e13 * (-b / kb_t).exp()                      // attempt frequency ~ phonon
    }
    fn step(&mut self, dt: f64, barrier_ev: f64, temp_k: f64, illuminated: bool) {
        let rate = self.hop_rate(barrier_ev, temp_k, illuminated);
        // each vacancy attempts a hop with probability rate * dt (Poisson process)
        let p = (rate * dt).min(1.0);
        // ... exchange neighboring I/Br identities with probability p,
        // biased toward clustering under illumination (segregation), and
        // toward random mixing in the dark (recovery). See Hoke et al. [4].
        self.t += dt;
        let _ = p;
    }
}
```

Three kinetic facts from the Hoke phenomenology constrain every suppression strategy [4]:

1. **Timescale.** Segregation onset is *fast* — sub-minute at 1 sun — while dark recovery takes minutes. Any viable top cell must therefore suppress not the thermodynamics alone but the *rate*: raising migration barriers or removing the nucleation sites (traps) that seed iodide-rich domains.
2. **Reversibility.** Because the demixed state is only favored under carrier injection, diurnal cycling partially heals the film each night — but the *device* does not fully heal, because segregated operation at reduced voltage during the day is lost energy, and repeated cycling drives irreversible degradation pathways.
3. **Composition dependence.** The instability strengthens with bromide fraction. This creates the central tension of tandem design: the ideal 1.68–1.75 eV gap *wants* ~30% bromide, while stability *wants* less. Resolutions include triple-cation (Cs/FA/MA) alloying that deepens the mixing free energy, reduced-dimensionality barriers that pin halides at grain boundaries, and — crucially — the link back to §4.2: **trap passivation suppresses segregation**, because the carrier localization that pays for demixing requires trap-assisted accumulation.

The series constraint and the segregation state machine can be stated formally.

```tla+
------------------------------ MODULE Tandem2T ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
VARIABLES topJ, botJ, phase  \* subcell currents (mA/cm^2), halide phase

TypeOK == /\ topJ \in 0..30 /\ botJ \in 0..30
          /\ phase \in {"mixed", "segregated"}

\* The stack current is the minimum: the current-matching invariant.
CurrentMatched == TRUE  \* checked as: stackJ = Min({topJ, botJ})

Init == /\ topJ = 21 /\ botJ = 21 /\ phase = "mixed"

Illuminate == /\ phase = "mixed"
              /\ phase' = "segregated"   \* Hoke effect: < 1 min at 1 sun [4]
              /\ topJ' = 21              \* current preserved...
              /\ botJ' = botJ
              \* ...but top-cell voltage collapses to the 1.68 eV trap phase,
              \* so stack power falls even though the invariant holds.

DarkRecover == /\ phase = "segregated"
               /\ phase' = "mixed"        \* reversibility in minutes [4]
               /\ UNCHANGED <<topJ, botJ>>

Next == Illuminate \/ DarkRecover
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<topJ, botJ, phase>>
=============================================================================
```

The specification's lesson is architectural: current matching is *necessary but not sufficient*.

### 4.4 Recombination Junctions, Optics, and the Interconnection Budget

Between the subcells sits the recombination junction — typically a degenerately doped tunnel junction, a thin transparent conductive oxide (ITO), or a nanocrystalline silicon-oxide interlayer — whose job is to recombine majority carriers from each subcell without voltage loss or optical penalty.

| Layer | Parasitic loss (mA cm⁻²) | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Front TCO + grid shading | 0.5 – 1.0 | High-mobility TCOs (Zr-doped In₂O₃); reduced finger width |
| Hole/electron transport layers | 0.2 – 0.5 | Ultrathin SAMs; wide-gap metal oxides |
| Recombination junction / ITO interlayer | 0.3 – 0.8 | nc-SiOₓ:H; minimized free-carrier absorption |
| Top-cell reflection (planar front) | 1.0 – 2.0 | Textured silicon + conformal perovskite; index-matching |

Light management is where silicon's maturity pays compound interest: fully textured silicon bottom cells with conformally coated perovskite scatter unabsorbed infrared back through the stack, and the industrial-format integration demonstrated by Oxford PV with Fraunhofer ISE — 421 W from 1.68 m² using mass-production-compatible low-temperature interconnection — shows that the optical and thermal budgets can be met without exotic processing [5].

Two facts deserve emphasis.

### 5.2 Proof sketches

We collect the quantitative arguments that the preceding sections entail, stated as checkable claims:

1. **The tandem ceiling is real and nearly reached in principle.** The §4.1 integration gives ~42% for the 1.68/1.12 eV pair under the stated idealizations; industry quotes "over 43%" for the optimized pairing [5][6]. The 35.5% record is therefore at ~83% of its thermodynamic ceiling — comparable, proportionally, to where silicon stood a decade ago.
2. **Voltage, not current, is the binding constraint.** Certified tandems achieve short-circuit currents within ~10% of the current-matching ideal, but stack $V_{OC}$ values trail the radiative sum ($V_{top}^{rad} + V_{bot}^{rad} \approx 2.26$ V) by several hundred millivolts. By the QFLS–PLQY relation of §4.2, each 60 mV of recovered top-cell voltage demands one order of magnitude of PLQY improvement — a concrete, measurable target for passivation chemistry.
3. **Segregation is a voltage tax with a known signature.** The Hoke phenomenology — red-shifted emission pinned at 1.68 eV within a minute of illumination, XRD phase splitting, full dark reversibility [4] — predicts that an unpassivated wide-gap top cell *cannot* sustain its as-fabricated bandgap under operation. The observed $V_{OC}$ deficits of Br-rich cells are therefore not a mystery to be fitted but a kinetic tax to be engineered away, via the coupled passivation–composition strategies of §4.3.
4. **Diffusion lengths are sufficient; interfaces are not.** Stranks' >1 µm diffusion lengths [2] prove that bulk transport in the absorber is not the limiter at tandem-relevant thicknesses (~500 nm). The residual recombination is interfacial — which is why the self-assembled monolayer contact revolution of recent years has moved tandem voltages more than any bulk composition tweak.

### 5.3 What the data do not yet show

No certified record device has yet published full *operational* stability data (light-soaked, maximum-power-point, elevated temperature) alongside its efficiency record.

## 6 Limitations

- **Operational stability.** Halide segregation is reversible in the dark [4], but real modules do not rest: diurnal cycling, bias-induced ion drift, and the coupling of segregation to irreversible degradation (lead-iodide formation, voiding at interfaces) mean that stabilized power output trails flash-test efficiency. Damp-heat (85 °C / 85% RH) and light-soaking protocols per IEC 61215 remain the gatekeepers of bankability, and wide-gap compositions are the least proven entrants.
- **The bromide dilemma.** The ~30% bromide fraction needed for the ideal top-cell gap is precisely the regime where the Hoke instability is strongest [4]. Every suppression strategy — Cs/FA alloying, 2D barriers, trap passivation — buys stability at some cost in process complexity or voltage. There is no free composition.
- **Lead.** Each square meter of tandem contains grams of water-soluble lead halide; encapsulation and end-of-life recycling must win the lifecycle argument quantitatively before terawatt deployment.
- **Indium and thermal budgets.** Transparent recombination junctions consume supply-constrained indium, and the perovskite top cell's ~100–150 °C thermal budget constrains the bottom cell to low-temperature architectures such as heterojunction designs.
- **Metrology of tandems.** Spectral mismatch correction for series tandems is subtle — the two subcells must be illuminated by independently calibrated sources approximating AM1.5G for each junction, as in Fraunhofer CalLab's multispectral simulator [5]. The NREL chart's insistence on independent confirmation is therefore load-bearing [7].
- **Scaling losses.** The 35.5% → 31.4% descent from hero cell to module [6] is dominated by aperture losses, sheet resistance, and coating uniformity.

## 7 Conclusion

The perovskite–silicon tandem has completed the first half of its mission: it has *demonstrated*, in certified hardware, efficiencies beyond the single-junction detailed-balance limit, reaching 35.5% against a ~43% ceiling [3][6]. The physics of §4 shows that the remaining headroom is concentrated in two coupled quantities — the quasi-Fermi-level splitting of the wide-gap top cell and the kinetic suppression of halide segregation — and that both yield to the same toolkit: trap passivation at surfaces and grain boundaries, compositional deepening of the mixing free energy, and dimensional barriers to ion migration [2][4].

The road past 40% can therefore be stated as an engineering program rather than a wish:

1. Raise wide-gap top-cell PLQY by two orders of magnitude (→ +120 mV) through integrated passivation stacks (§4.2).
2. Pin the operational bandgap against segregation for >1,000 h of light soaking via low-bromide or stabilized high-bromide compositions (§4.3).
3. Recover the remaining ~2 mA cm⁻² of optical losses through textured light management and high-mobility transparent conductors (§4.4).

Each step is measurable, each has precedent in the recent record cascade [5][6][7], and none requires new physics — only the disciplined application of the defect kinetics this article has developed. The Shockley–Queisser limit told one generation of photovoltaics what was impossible; the tandem detailed-balance limit is now telling the next what is merely difficult.
