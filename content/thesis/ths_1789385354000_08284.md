# Homomorphism Counts and the Weisfeiler–Leman Hierarchy: A Fine-Grained Theory of Graph Neural Network Expressivity

## Abstract

The expressive power of message-passing graph neural networks is bounded above by the 1-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman test, a combinatorial color-refinement procedure whose blind spots — non-isomorphic regular graphs, cycle-counting failures, the Cai–Fürer–Immerman family — are inherited verbatim by every standard GNN. Escaping this ceiling has produced a zoo of higher-order architectures: k-GNNs, invariant tensor networks, subgraph GNNs, and cellular models. This thesis unifies the landscape through a single mathematical object: the vector of homomorphism counts from small pattern graphs into the input. We show that k-WL indistinguishability coincides exactly with agreement of homomorphism counts over all patterns of treewidth at most k, that the subgraph-GNN zoo collapses into six subgraph-WL equivalence classes with a tight gap to folklore 2-WL, and that the newest "loopy" refinements escape the classical hierarchy by counting cactus homomorphisms locally. The homomorphism-counting lens does not merely taxonomize the field — it predicts which substructures any given architecture can and cannot detect, and it exposes the precise sense in which scaling expressivity trades the treewidth of the countable against the polynomial cost of the counting.

## 1 Intro

Graph neural networks succeeded by turning the *message-passing* primitive — aggregate your neighbors, update your state, repeat — into a differentiable program. But theory soon delivered a sobering characterization: the most widely used GNNs cannot distinguish any pair of graphs that the 1968 Weisfeiler–Leman color-refinement test cannot distinguish [1]. Depth does not help. Width does not help. Attention does not help. The ceiling is combinatorial, not parametric.

The response was a decade of architectural invention: k-GNNs lifting message passing to vertex tuples [2], sparse variants chasing scalability [5], subgraph GNNs forming a parallel hierarchy [3], and "loopy" refinements surpassing the k-WL ladder at linear cost [4] — with quantum and canonicalization approaches attacking from different computational models [6][7].

What this literature lacked, until recently, was a common yardstick finer than the WL test itself. The WL hierarchy tells you *that* one architecture is stronger than another; it does not tell you *what* each architecture can actually see. **Homomorphism counts** provide that yardstick. A homomorphism from a pattern graph F into a target G is an edge-preserving map of vertices — a flexible notion of "F occurs in G" generalizing subgraph counting. The vector of all homomorphism counts, as F ranges over small patterns, is the native coordinate system of graph expressivity: two graphs are indistinguishable to k-WL *if and only if* they agree on homomorphism counts from every pattern of treewidth at most k. Under this lens, every known architecture is characterized by the family of patterns it can count — and every impossibility result becomes a statement about which patterns' counts are provably out of reach.

This thesis argues three claims. First, the homomorphism-counting correspondence (Dvořák 2010; Dell, Grohe, Rattan 2018) is the right foundation for GNN expressivity theory, subsuming the pebble-game and counting-logic viewpoints. Second, the subgraph-GNN literature is fully explained by this correspondence: Zhang et al.'s complete six-class hierarchy [3] is a theorem about which homomorphism families each subgraph policy unlocks. Third, the hierarchy is not the end of the story: loopy refinement [4] counts homomorphisms from *cactus* graphs that no fixed level of k-WL can count, proving that the classical ladder is not the expressivity ceiling it was assumed to be.

## 2 Background

### The Weisfeiler–Leman tests

The 1-dimensional WL test, also called *color refinement*, assigns every vertex an initial color from its features, then iteratively recolors each vertex by the multiset of its neighbors' colors, hashed injectively. It stabilizes in at most n iterations on an n-vertex graph, and two graphs are 1-WL indistinguishable when their stabilized color histograms coincide. It is fast, canonical, and famously incomplete: it cannot separate regular graphs of the same degree and order, cannot count cycles, and is defeated by the Cai–Fürer–Immerman (CFI) construction of non-isomorphic graphs agreeing on every bounded-variable counting property.

The k-dimensional generalization colors *k-tuples* of vertices. Two flavors matter. The *oblivious* k-WL refines tuple colors using, for each position, the multiset of colors from substituting every vertex into that position. The *folklore* k-FWL uses a finer neighborhood considering each substitution directly. The distinction is not pedantry — 2-FWL is strictly stronger than 2-WL, and the folklore variant is the one matching homomorphism indistinguishability over bounded-treewidth patterns.

### Message-passing neural networks

A message-passing neural network (MPNN) computes each vertex's layer-(l+1) representation by aggregating the multiset of its neighbors' layer-l representations and combining with its own:

*h(v) at layer l+1 = UPDATE( h(v) at layer l, AGGREGATE over neighbors u of h(u) at layer l )*

Xu et al. proved the decisive correspondence [1]: if the aggregate and update functions are injective, the MPNN's layer-wise representations refine exactly the 1-WL coloring, and conversely no MPNN — regardless of depth, width, or nonlinearity — can separate graphs that 1-WL cannot. The Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN), which uses a multilayer perceptron over summed neighbor features, is the canonical architecture achieving the bound: *as powerful as 1-WL, and no more*. Graph convolutional networks and GraphSAGE, with mean or max aggregators, are strictly weaker, since non-injective aggregation collapses multisets that 1-WL would keep apart.

### Logic, games, and treewidth

The WL hierarchy has two classical reformulations that the neural story inherits. In *descriptive complexity*, k-WL indistinguishability coincides with equivalence in the k-variable fragment of first-order logic with counting quantifiers. In *combinatorial game theory*, it coincides with Duplicator winning the k-pebble bijective game. The third reformulation — homomorphism indistinguishability — is the one that makes the theory quantitative. A graph's *treewidth* measures how close it is to a tree; the patterns a test can count are exactly those of bounded treewidth.

## 3 Methodology

The methodology of this thesis is *characterization by indistinguishability relations*. Rather than studying architectures directly, we study the equivalence relations they induce — G is equivalent to H when no network in the class can assign them different outputs — and identify each with a combinatorial or logical relation. This is justified by a meta-theorem: for all architecture classes considered here, the neural indistinguishability relation coincides with the corresponding WL-variant relation at generic parameters [2][3].

We work with three tools:

- **Homomorphism-count vectors.** For a pattern F and target G, hom(F, G) is the number of edge-preserving vertex maps from F to G. The *homomorphism profile* of G over a family F is the vector of these counts; G and H agree on all profiles over F exactly when they are indistinguishable by the corresponding test.
- **Treewidth as the expressivity parameter.** Dvořák and Dell, Grohe, and Rattan proved that folklore k-WL indistinguishability coincides with agreement of hom(F, ·) over all F of treewidth at most k. Climbing from k to k plus 1 buys exactly the patterns of treewidth k plus 1.
- **Pebble games and counting logic as proof technology.** Strict separations are proved by exhibiting CFI-like graph pairs that the k-pebble game cannot tell apart but the (k+1)-pebble game can; we use these as black boxes, with the homomorphism view carrying the intuition.

Our "experiments" are therefore *theorem-shaped*: each correspondence stated as a formal claim with a proof sketch, validated by small computational demonstrations — exact homomorphism counts computed by brute force and matrix algebra on benchmark graph pairs.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Homomorphism Counts as the Common Currency

> **Theorem (Homomorphism characterization of the WL hierarchy).** Two graphs G and H are indistinguishable by the folklore k-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman test if and only if hom(F, G) = hom(F, H) for every graph F of treewidth at most k.

This is the master theorem of the thesis. Its power lies in converting an abstract indistinguishability claim into a concrete, checkable, *quantitative* statement about substructure statistics. To ask "can architecture A distinguish graphs G and H?" is to ask "is there a pattern F of treewidth within A's budget whose homomorphism counts differ on G and H?"

The correspondence explains the most famous blind spots of message passing. A triangle has treewidth 2, so 1-WL — and hence every MPNN and GIN — *cannot* count triangles: the precise reason GNNs struggle with molecular graphs, where aromatic rings carry the chemistry. A path on any number of vertices has treewidth 1, so paths are countable at level 1 — which is why 1-WL separates trees correctly (it is *complete* on trees).

| Test / architecture | Max pattern treewidth | Counts triangles? | Counts 4-cycles? | Counts 5-cycles? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1-WL / MPNN / GIN [1] | 1 | No | No | No |
| 2-FWL / 2-GNN [2] | 2 | Yes | Yes | No |
| k-FWL / k-GNN [2] | k | iff tw ≤ k | iff tw ≤ k | iff tw ≤ k |
| Subgraph (SSWL) [3] | mixed | Yes | Yes | Limited |
| r-loopy WL [4] | cactus family | Yes | Yes | Yes (to r+2) |

The table's last row previews the surprise of Section 4.4: loopy refinement counts *some* patterns of arbitrarily high treewidth — 5-cycles have treewidth 2, but longer cactus patterns exceed any fixed bound — while remaining weaker than k-WL on other patterns. The hierarchy of treewidth budgets is therefore not the whole story; the *shape* of the countable family matters too.

An important subtlety: the theorem concerns the *folklore* variant. The oblivious k-WL corresponds instead to homomorphism counts over patterns of *pathwidth* at most k, a stricter family — which is why the two variants induce genuinely different neural architectures, and why confusing them has caused real errors in published expressivity claims.

### 4.2 The Oblivious and Folklore Ladders

With the homomorphism currency in hand, the classical hierarchy becomes transparent. Each rung k of the folklore ladder adds exactly the patterns of treewidth k: at k = 1 we count trees and forests; at k = 2 we add series-parallel patterns including all cycles; and so on. The ladder is strict at every rung: for each k there exist CFI-constructed graph pairs agreeing on all treewidth-k homomorphism counts but differing on some treewidth-(k+1) pattern. No fixed k suffices for all graphs.

The neural realizations were introduced by Morris et al. [2]. A k-GNN operates on k-tuples of vertices with tuple-adjacency defined by substitution neighborhoods, and its power matches k-WL. The cost is the obvious one: all k-tuples need memory n to the k, which is why dense k-GNNs are a theoretical instrument rather than a practical tool. Two responses emerged. *Invariant and equivariant graph networks* realize the folklore ladder with tensor operations of order k — same expressivity, same asymptotic cost, cleaner algebra. And *sparse and local* variants [5] restrict attention to a subset of tuples, gaining scalability and sometimes *strictly more* distinguishing power, because the locality prior breaks symmetries the dense version preserves.

The strictness of the ladder has a direct neural reading: for every fixed k, there exist molecular-scale graphs that a k-GNN provably cannot distinguish but a (k+1)-GNN can.

### 4.3 Subgraph GNNs and the SWL Hierarchy

Subgraph GNNs take a different route around the 1-WL ceiling. Instead of lifting to tuples, they run a standard MPNN on a *bag of subgraphs* — one per vertex with that vertex marked, or deleted — and aggregate the results. Zhang et al. resolved the resulting confusion completely [3]: they introduced *subgraph WL tests* mirroring each subgraph policy and proved that every node-based subgraph GNN falls into exactly **six** SWL equivalence classes, strictly ordered, with a maximal class SSWL. The homomorphism lens explains *why*: each class corresponds to a specific family of countable patterns, and the class inclusions are pattern-family inclusions.

The analysis also reveals the family's ceiling: a provable, tight gap between even the strongest subgraph GNN and folklore 2-WL [3] — certain biconnectivity properties are expressible at 2-FWL but in no SWL class. Experiments on ZINC confirm the practical bite: SSWL-inspired architectures outperform prior subgraph designs despite their simplicity, because the maximal class counts the chemically relevant small patterns [3].

### 4.4 Breaking the Ladder: Loopy Refinement and Homomorphism-Explicit Models

Paolino et al.'s *r-loopy WL* [4] augments color refinement with messages collected along *paths between neighboring vertices* rather than just from the neighbors themselves. The key theorems: r-loopy WL counts cycles up to length r plus 2, and can homomorphism-count *any cactus graph* whose cycles have length at most r plus 2. A cactus is a graph in which every edge belongs to at most one simple cycle — a family including arbitrarily long chains of rings, hence patterns of *unbounded* treewidth.

This is a genuine escape from the hierarchy: 1-loopy WL already separates infinitely many graph pairs that no subgraph k-GNN can separate, at forward cost linear in neighborhood sizes on sparse graphs [4]. The homomorphism view makes the mechanism crisp — loopy refinement counts a *non-treewidth-bounded* family (cacti) that the classical ladder reaches only by climbing arbitrarily high. It is incomparable with k-WL rather than above it: some patterns countable at low k-WL escape loopy WL. The ladder was never the whole space; it was one axis.

A complementary direction makes homomorphism counts *explicit*. Barceló et al.'s F-Hom-GNNs inject homomorphism counts from a chosen pattern family directly as features, and 1-loopy WL strictly dominates these models: anything an F-Hom-GNN expresses with cycle patterns, loopy refinement expresses natively [4]. Two 2026 developments attack from outside the WL paradigm. Quantum message-passing networks sit at a *chosen* WL level set by a particle-number parameter, rising above the 1-WL ceiling with trainability guarantees on up to 56 qubits [6]. And canonicalization approaches observe that WL is incomplete even on graphs with simple spectra — where isomorphism is easy — and recover completeness by canonicalizing first, sidestepping the hierarchy rather than climbing it [7]. The WL ladder is one expressivity axis; homomorphism families are the coordinates.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

We validate the homomorphism-counting correspondence computationally. The Python routine below computes exact homomorphism counts from small patterns by brute-force vertex maps, demonstrating the signature 1-WL blind spot: the 6-cycle and the disjoint union of two triangles agree on all treewidth-1 pattern counts but differ on triangle counts.

```python
import itertools
import numpy as np

def hom_count(pattern_edges, target_edges, n_target):
    """Exact homomorphism count from pattern to target by enumeration."""
    n_pattern = 1 + max(max(e) for e in pattern_edges)
    count = 0
    target_adj = {e for e in target_edges} | {(v, u) for (u, v) in target_edges}
    for mapping in itertools.product(range(n_target), repeat=n_pattern):
        if all((mapping[u], mapping[v]) in target_adj for (u, v) in pattern_edges):
            count += 1
    return count

def cycle_edges(k):
    return [(i, (i + 1) % k) for i in range(k)]

c6 = cycle_edges(6)
two_tri = cycle_edges(3) + [(u + 3, v + 3) for (u, v) in cycle_edges(3)]

path3 = [(0, 1), (1, 2)]          # treewidth 1
triangle = cycle_edges(3)          # treewidth 2

for name, target, n in [("C6", c6, 6), ("2xK3", two_tri, 6)]:
    p3 = hom_count(path3, target, n)
    tri = hom_count(triangle, target, n)
    print(f"{name}: hom(P3)={p3}  hom(K3)={tri}")
```

The output is the theory made concrete: both graphs report identical P3 counts (24 each — treewidth 1, within the 1-WL budget) while the triangle counts differ sharply (0 for the 6-cycle, which is bipartite and admits no closed odd walk, versus 12 for the two triangles) — the exact quantitative signature of a pair that 1-WL conflates and 2-FWL separates. The same routine, run on CFI pairs, reproduces the hierarchy's strictness: agreement extends to higher-treewidth patterns exactly as the pebble-game analysis predicts. For larger patterns, matrix algebra replaces brute force — hom(C_k, G) is the trace of the k-th adjacency power — and a compact Haskell sketch shows how little machinery the 1-WL ceiling requires:

```haskell
-- One step of 1-WL color refinement; colors are integers, multisets sorted lists
refineStep :: [[Int]] -> [Int] -> [Int]
refineStep adj colors = compress
  [ (c, sort [colors !! u | u <- adj !! v]) | (v, c) <- zip [0..] colors ]
  where
    compress labeled = map snd $ zip [0..] $ map head $ group $ sort labeled
    sort = Data.List.sort
    group = Data.List.group

-- The stable coloring is the least fixed point of refineStep
wlStable :: [[Int]] -> [Int]
wlStable adj = until (\c -> refineStep adj c == c) (refineStep adj) init
  where init = replicate (length adj) 0
```

Empirically, the benchmarks line up behind the theory. The CFI graphs remain the standard stress test: any architecture claiming to exceed k-WL must separate the corresponding CFI pair, and the quantum GNN of [6] validates its hierarchy placement on exactly these graphs before touching molecules. On real data, the ZINC results for SSWL-inspired subgraph GNNs [3] and QM9 regression gains for higher-order models [2][5] show that countable patterns — small cycles, functional-group motifs — are precisely the ones whose counts correlate with target properties. The correspondence predicts *which* datasets benefit from *which* rung.

## 6 Limitations

The homomorphism-counting framework is a theory of *distinguishing power*, and distinguishing power is not the same as learning. Four limitations deserve emphasis.

First, **countability is not learnability**. The theorems characterize what an architecture *can* represent at generic parameters; they say nothing about whether gradient descent *finds* those parameters or whether finite training data suffices. A k-GNN that can count triangles in principle may never learn to do so from a few thousand molecules.

Second, **the treewidth budget is worst-case, not typical**. Real graphs — molecules, social networks, code graphs — have small treewidth on average, which is why 1-WL and MPNNs work as well as they do. The hierarchy's strictness is witnessed by adversarial CFI constructions unlike deployed data. For many applications the 1-WL ceiling is rarely the binding constraint; oversmoothing, oversquashing, and optimization pathology usually bind first.

Third, **counting is expensive and the constants matter**. Exact homomorphism counting is #P-hard in general; the treewidth-parameterized polynomial algorithms hide biting constants — a 2-GNN needs quadratic memory in the number of vertices, ruling out large sparse graphs. Sparse and local variants [5] and loopy refinement [4] mitigate this, but every mitigation sacrifices some pattern family: there is no free expressivity.

Fourth, **the framework is graph-isomorphism-centric**. It measures what a network can say about *unlabeled structure*, but deployed GNNs lean on node and edge features, positional encodings, and domain priors. Random features, distance encodings, and canonicalization [7] separate graphs no WL level can — by breaking symmetry with information the pure theory forbids. A complete account of practical expressivity must integrate these symmetry-breaking resources.

## 7 Conclusion

The expressivity theory of graph neural networks, viewed through homomorphism counts, resolves into a single quantitative picture. Every architecture is characterized by the family of pattern graphs whose homomorphism counts it can compute; the classical Weisfeiler–Leman hierarchy is the special case where that family is "all patterns of treewidth at most k"; and the major innovations of the last decade are moves in this pattern-family space — k-GNNs climbing the treewidth axis [2], sparse variants trading density for locality [5], subgraph GNNs unlocking marked-vertex families up to a provable six-class ceiling [3], and loopy refinement escaping the axis into cactus-counting territory [4].

Three lessons stand out. First, *expressivity is a budget, not a property*: denominated in the treewidth and shape of countable patterns, always spent against polynomial cost. Second, *the hierarchy is an axis, not the space*: incomparable extensions like loopy WL prove that WL-indistinguishability was never the only structural similarity worth neuralizing. Third, *theory predicts practice when it is quantitative*: the homomorphism lens names the substructures each architecture can see — triangles, short cycles, biconnected motifs — and those names are exactly the features chemists and network scientists care about.

The frontier is now clear. Closing the gap between countability and learnability: which homomorphism features does gradient descent actually extract? Integrating symmetry-breaking resources — positional encodings, canonicalization [7], quantum circuits [6] — into the same quantitative framework. And pushing the new non-hierarchical axes: if cactus homomorphisms are countable at linear cost, which other pattern families admit local counting schemes? The WL hierarchy organized the first decade of GNN theory. Homomorphism counting is organizing the second.
