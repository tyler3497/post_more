# Differentially Private Deep Learning: DP-SGD, Privacy Accounting, and the Utility Frontier

## Abstract

Training deep neural networks on sensitive data demands rigorous privacy guarantees that survive post-processing and adaptive composition. Differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) remains the dominant paradigm, clipping per-example gradients to bound sensitivity and injecting calibrated Gaussian noise at each update [1]. This thesis unifies the theoretical and practical landscape of private deep learning: the mechanics of per-example clipping, Rényi and Gaussian differential privacy accounting with subsampling amplification [3][8], and the modern engineering stack — large-batch training, augmentation multiplicity, automatic clipping [6], and pre-training — that has closed the utility gap on benchmarks such as CIFAR-10 and ImageNet [5]. We analyze why privacy disproportionately taxes underrepresented subpopulations and long-tailed data [9], derive the privacy-utility scaling laws governing noise multiplier, batch size, and step count, and survey alternatives including PATE [4] and federated discrete-Gaussian mechanisms [10]. We conclude with open problems in hyperparameter tuning under privacy, adaptive clipping theory, and tight accounting for realistic training pipelines.

## 1. Introduction

Modern deep learning is hungry for data, and the most valuable data — medical records, financial transactions, private communications — is precisely the data whose misuse carries the gravest consequences. Classical anonymization techniques have repeatedly collapsed under linkage attacks, motivating a mathematical definition of privacy that is robust to arbitrary side information. **Differential privacy (DP)** [2] provides such a definition: a randomized algorithm is differentially private if its output distribution is nearly indistinguishable whether or not any single individual's record is included in the input. The guarantee is *information-theoretic* in flavor, composes gracefully, and is immune to post-processing — properties no heuristic anonymization can claim.

Yet differential privacy was born in the world of low-dimensional statistics — histograms, contingency tables, and linear queries — where the *sensitivity* of a function (how much one record can change the output) is small and easily bounded. Deep learning breaks this mold. A single training example can, through the nonlinear dynamics of optimization, move model parameters by an amount that is difficult to bound *a priori*. The breakthrough of Abadi et al. [1] was to *impose* a bound: clip each per-example gradient to a fixed norm $C$, thereby capping the influence of any one record, then add Gaussian noise calibrated to $C$. The resulting algorithm, **DP-SGD**, trains neural networks with a provable $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP guarantee.

> **Theorem (Privacy of DP-SGD, informal):** Let each iteration sample a batch with Poisson subsampling rate $q$, clip per-example gradients to norm $C$, and add noise $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 C^2 I)$. Then after $T$ steps the mechanism satisfies $(\alpha, \varepsilon)$-Rényi DP with $\varepsilon$ computed by the moments accountant [1][3].

The decade since has transformed DP-SGD from a proof of concept — one that lost tens of accuracy points on CIFAR-10 — into a practical technology achieving near-non-private accuracy on ImageNet [5]. This thesis charts that journey and the theory that made it possible. Our contributions are organizational and analytical rather than algorithmic: we present a unified treatment of (i) the clipping-noise mechanism and its variants, (ii) privacy accounting from basic composition through Rényi DP [3], Gaussian DP [8], and numerical accountants, (iii) the empirical scaling laws of the privacy-utility tradeoff, and (iv) the fairness and systems challenges that remain.

The stakes are concrete. Regulatory regimes increasingly demand demonstrable privacy protection for machine learning on personal data, and heuristic defenses — gradient compression, dropout, early stopping — offer no guarantees against a determined adversary with auxiliary information [2]. DP-SGD is, at present, the only scalable method for training deep networks with a *provable, composable* privacy bound. Understanding exactly what it costs, where it fails, and how far engineering can push it is therefore a question of both scientific and societal importance.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Differential privacy: definitions

A randomized mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-differential privacy if for all neighboring datasets $D, D'$ differing in one record and all measurable output sets $S$:

$$ \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon} \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S] + \delta. $$

The parameter $\varepsilon$ is the *privacy budget*: smaller means stronger privacy. The additive $\delta$ permits a small probability of catastrophic privacy failure and is conventionally chosen cryptographically small, e.g. $\delta \ll 1/n$ for a dataset of $n$ records [2].

The workhorse of DP mechanism design is the **Gaussian mechanism**: for a function $f$ with $\ell_2$-sensitivity $\Delta_2 = \max_{D \sim D'} \|f(D) - f(D')\|_2$, releasing $f(D) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 \Delta_2^2 I)$ satisfies $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP for $\sigma$ calibrated to $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ [2]. The entire challenge of private deep learning reduces to *controlling the sensitivity of a gradient step*.

### 2.2 Stochastic gradient descent and its sensitivity problem

Standard SGD updates parameters $\theta$ via $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i \in B} \nabla \ell(\theta_t; x_i)$. The per-example gradient $\nabla \ell(\theta_t; x_i)$ has no a priori norm bound — a mislabeled outlier or adversarial example can produce an arbitrarily large gradient, giving the function unbounded sensitivity. Naive noise addition would require infinite noise. Clipping is the standard remedy: replace each gradient $g_i$ by $g_i \cdot \min(1, C/\|g_i\|_2)$, forcing $\|g_i\|_2 \le C$ and hence bounding the batch gradient's sensitivity by $C/|B|$ [1].

### 2.3 Composition and amplification

Training runs for thousands of steps, each touching the data. **Composition theorems** bound the total privacy loss: naive composition adds $\varepsilon$ linearly, while advanced composition gives $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})$ growth [2]. Tighter still are the *moments accountant* [1] and Rényi DP [3], which track the Rényi divergence between output distributions and yield substantially smaller $\varepsilon$ for the Gaussian mechanism under composition.

**Privacy amplification by subsampling** is the second pillar: if each step touches only a random $q$-fraction of the data (Poisson subsampling), the per-step guarantee amplifies roughly by a factor of $q$ [1][3]. This is why DP-SGD samples each example independently with probability $q = |B|/n$ rather than using fixed shuffled batches — though in practice, shuffling is often used with a conservative accounting correction.

| Accounting method | Composition | Handles subsampling | Typical tightness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive / advanced composition [2] | $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})$ | Weakly | Loose |
| Moments accountant [1] | RDP-based | Yes (Poisson) | Moderate |
| Rényi DP [3] | Exact RDP | Yes | Tight |
| Gaussian DP / $f$-DP [8] | Exact tradeoff fn | Yes | Tightest analytic |
| Numerical (PLD) accountants | Exact (discretized) | Yes | Near-optimal |

## 3. Methodology

Our treatment is organized around the canonical DP-SGD template and its analysis pipeline. We first present the algorithm in full generality, then develop the three analytical lenses — *mechanism*, *accounting*, and *utility* — through which every private training method must be evaluated.

**The DP-SGD template.** Given dataset $D$ of $n$ examples, loss $\ell$, clipping norm $C$, noise multiplier $\sigma$, batch sampling rate $q$, learning rate $\eta$, and steps $T$:

```python
def dp_sgd_step(theta, batch, C, sigma, eta):
    grads = [clip(grad(loss, theta, x), C) for x in batch]  # per-example clip
    g = sum(grads) / len(batch)
    g_noisy = g + torch.normal(0, sigma * C / len(batch), g.shape)
    return theta - eta * g_noisy
```

Each step is a subsampled Gaussian mechanism; the full run's privacy follows from composition plus amplification [1][3]. Our methodology for evaluating any variant is: (1) identify the per-step mechanism and its Rényi DP curve, (2) compose over $T$ steps with the correct subsampling model, (3) convert to $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP, and (4) measure utility as test accuracy at fixed $(\varepsilon, \delta)$.

**Analytical approach.** We derive scaling relationships between $(\sigma, q, T)$ and the final $\varepsilon$ using RDP composition, and relate the *effective noise* $\sigma C / |B|$ to optimization dynamics. We survey empirical results from the literature under a consistent evaluation protocol (fixed $\delta = 10^{-5}$ or $1/n$, matched architectures) and isolate the individual contributions of large batches, augmentation multiplicity, and pre-training [5][6][9].

**Scope.** We focus on centralized training with a trusted curator. We treat federated and distributed variants [10] and alternative paradigms such as PATE [4] as points of comparison rather than primary subjects.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The clipping operation: bias, geometry, and automatic variants

Clipping is DP-SGD's most consequential design choice, and its least theoretically understood. The operation $g \mapsto g \cdot \min(1, C/\|g\|)$ introduces **bias**: the expected clipped gradient is not the true gradient, and the bias grows as $C$ shrinks relative to typical gradient norms. Early work treated $C$ as a hyperparameter requiring expensive private tuning; too large a $C$ inflates the noise scale $\sigma C$, while too small a $C$ destroys signal through bias [1][7].

Two insights rehabilitated clipping. First, in overparameterized networks, gradient norms concentrate in a predictable range during training, so a fixed moderate $C$ (e.g., $1.0$) is often near-optimal [5]. Second, **automatic clipping** [6] replaces the hard norm bound with a per-example normalization, $g_i / (\|g_i\|_2 + \gamma)$, which adaptively bounds sensitivity without a tuning-sensitive threshold. Bu et al. prove that automatic clipping preserves convergence guarantees while removing $C$ from the hyperparameter search entirely [6] — a significant practical advance, since hyperparameter tuning itself consumes privacy budget.

Geometrically, clipping reshapes the loss landscape's effective geometry: directions with large gradients are attenuated, which interacts nontrivially with adaptive optimizers. DP-Adam and DP-LAMB adapt the noise-aware update, but the interplay between clipping bias and second-moment estimation remains only partially characterized [7].

### 4.2 Rényi DP accounting and the moments accountant

The moments accountant [1] tracks $\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda \cdot \text{privacy loss})]$ across compositions, yielding far tighter bounds than advanced composition. Mironov's Rényi DP [3] provided the clean formalism: a mechanism is $(\alpha, \varepsilon)$-RDP if the Rényi divergence of order $\alpha$ between outputs on neighboring datasets is at most $\varepsilon$. RDP composes by *addition* of $\varepsilon$ values and converts to $(\varepsilon + \frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\alpha-1}, \delta)$-DP.

For the subsampled Gaussian mechanism, the RDP curve has a closed form in terms of $\sigma$ and $q$ [3]. The accountant's tightness matters enormously in practice: on identical training runs, RDP accounting can report $\varepsilon$ values 2–5× smaller than advanced composition, which directly translates to either stronger claimed privacy or — equivalently — less noise for the same $\varepsilon$ budget.

More recent work has pushed further. **Gaussian DP** [8] characterizes privacy via the hypothesis-testing tradeoff function, giving an *exact* composition law for Gaussian mechanisms that dominates RDP bounds. Numerical privacy-loss-distribution (PLD) accountants discretize the loss distribution and compose by convolution, achieving near-optimal tightness at the cost of more computation. The practical takeaway: accounting is no longer the binding constraint — mechanism design and optimization are.

### 4.3 Amplification by subsampling: Poisson vs. shuffle

Amplification theorems are sharpest for **Poisson subsampling**, where each example joins the batch independently with probability $q$ [3]. Real training pipelines, however, typically *shuffle* the dataset once per epoch and take fixed-size batches. The amplification properties of shuffling are subtler — recent analyses show shuffle amplification is real but weaker than Poisson, and several widely used accountants conservatively treat shuffled batches as Poisson, which is *not* strictly valid.

This theory–practice gap has prompted two responses. Some implementations switched to genuine Poisson sampling (accepting variable batch sizes and systems complexity). Others developed shuffle-specific accountants with rigorous bounds. For practitioners, the lesson is concrete: *the accountant must match the sampling scheme*, and mismatches silently invalidate the reported $\varepsilon$. Auditing frameworks that empirically lower-bound privacy leakage via membership-inference attacks provide a valuable independent check [7].

### 4.4 The utility frontier: scale, augmentation, and pre-training

The most dramatic progress in private deep learning has come not from new mechanisms but from *scale-aware engineering*. De et al. [5] demonstrated that DP-SGD with very large batches (up to 16k), augmentation multiplicity (averaging gradients over multiple augmentations of each example *before* clipping, which reduces variance without extra privacy cost), and careful normalization (group norm instead of batch norm, whose batch statistics break per-example independence) achieves 81.4% top-1 on ImageNet at $\varepsilon = 8$ — within a few points of non-private training.

Three principles emerge:

1. **Large batches reduce effective noise.** The noise added per step scales as $\sigma C/|B|$; increasing $|B|$ at fixed $\sigma$ directly reduces optimization noise. Privacy accounting favors many small steps, but utility favors fewer large steps — the optimum balances the two [5][7].
2. **Augmentation multiplicity is nearly free utility.** Averaging $K$ augmentations per example before clipping keeps sensitivity unchanged (it is still one example's contribution) while reducing gradient variance [5].
3. **Public pre-training changes the game.** Fine-tuning a model pre-trained on public data requires far fewer private steps, slashing the privacy budget. The strongest current results all rely on public pre-training, raising the question of what "private learning" means when the features come from public data [9].

> **Theorem (Utility scaling, informal):** Under standard smoothness assumptions, DP-SGD with appropriate $(\eta, C, \sigma)$ achieves excess risk scaling as $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{d}/(n\varepsilon) + 1/\sqrt{n})$ up to clipping-bias terms, where $d$ is the dimension — exhibiting the curse of dimensionality that makes private deep learning fundamentally harder than private convex optimization [2][7].

## 5. Empirical Analysis and Theoretical Guarantees

The empirical literature now offers a reasonably complete map of the privacy-utility Pareto frontier. On **CIFAR-10**, DP-SGD reaches ~95% accuracy at $\varepsilon = 8$ with pre-training, versus ~99% non-private; training *from scratch* privately still costs 5–10 points [5][6]. On **ImageNet**, the gap at $\varepsilon = 8$ has narrowed to roughly 3–5 points with large-batch training and augmentation multiplicity [5]. These numbers represent a revolution relative to 2016, when private CIFAR-10 accuracy languished near 70%.

Theoretical guarantees have kept pace on the convex side: for convex Lipschitz losses, DP-SGD variants achieve the optimal $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{d}/(n\varepsilon))$ excess-risk rate [2]. For non-convex deep networks, guarantees are necessarily weaker — convergence to approximate stationary points with rates degraded by the clipping bias and noise variance [7]. The gap between convex theory and non-convex practice remains the field's central open theoretical problem.

A critical empirical finding concerns **disparate impact**: DP-SGD degrades accuracy disproportionately on underrepresented classes and subgroups [9]. The mechanism is intuitive — clipping penalizes examples with large gradients, which are disproportionately the rare, hard, or minority examples the model has not yet fit — but the consequence is serious: privacy can amplify unfairness. Tramèr and Boneh show the effect is large and robust, and that it persists across architectures [9]. Mitigations (group-aware clipping, reweighting) are nascent.

Membership-inference evaluations complement the formal guarantees: models trained with DP-SGD at small $\varepsilon$ empirically resist state-of-the-art inference attacks, and the *empirical* privacy leakage is typically well below the theoretical bound — suggesting current accounting, while much improved, remains conservative [7].

## 6. Limitations

**The privacy budget is spent opaquely.** Every hyperparameter choice informed by private validation data consumes budget, yet most published results tune extensively and report only the final training run's $\varepsilon$ [7]. Honest end-to-end accounting including tuning remains rare and would substantially inflate reported $\varepsilon$ values.

**Clipping bias is poorly understood.** The interaction of clipping with adaptive optimizers, normalization layers, and heavy-tailed gradient distributions lacks a complete theory; automatic clipping [6] helps practically but its theory is young.

**Fairness costs are real.** The disparate degradation of minority-subgroup accuracy under DP-SGD [9] is a first-order concern for deployment in high-stakes domains, with no fully satisfactory mitigation.

**Threat-model mismatch.** DP protects against *membership inference* from released parameters, but real deployments face broader threats — data reconstruction from gradients in federated settings [10], property inference, and poisoning — that $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP alone does not address.

**Computational overhead.** Per-example gradient computation (even with efficient vectorized implementations) and large-batch requirements make DP-SGD 2–10× more expensive than standard training, a meaningful barrier for large models.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Differentially private deep learning has matured from a theoretical curiosity into a deployable technology. The core recipe — per-example clipping plus Gaussian noise [1], composed and amplified via Rényi DP accounting [3] — has proven remarkably durable, while the utility frontier has been transformed by large-batch training, augmentation multiplicity, and public pre-training [5][6]. Tight accounting via Gaussian DP [8] and numerical methods has removed pessimism from privacy measurement, leaving optimization and fairness as the binding constraints.

The road ahead is clear. We need honest end-to-end privacy accounting that includes hyperparameter tuning; a theory of clipping bias in adaptive non-convex optimization; mitigations for the disparate impact of privacy noise [9]; and alternatives — PATE-style transfer [4], federated mechanisms [10] — evaluated on equal footing. The ultimate goal is private learning whose cost, in accuracy and in fairness, is small enough that *not* using it becomes indefensible. We are closer than ever, but not there yet.
