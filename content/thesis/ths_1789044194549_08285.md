# On the Statistical Foundations of Reinforcement Learning: Regret Bounds, Optimism in the Face of Uncertainty, Posterior Sampling, and the Pessimism Principle in Offline RL

## Abstract

This thesis develops a unified theoretical account of provably efficient reinforcement learning across the online and offline settings. We formalize the episodic finite-horizon Markov decision process (MDP) and the cumulative regret criterion, then analyze the three dominant algorithmic paradigms that address the exploration–exploitation dilemma: **optimism in the face of uncertainty (OFU)**, **posterior sampling (Thompson sampling)**, and **pessimism in the face of uncertainty** for the offline regime. We present the classical minimax regret bound $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{H^3SAT})$ achieved by UCBVI for tabular episodic MDPs [1], the nearly minimax-optimal $\tilde{O}(Hd\sqrt{T})$ bound of LSVI-UCB$^+$ for linear MDPs [4], and the Bayesian regret analysis of posterior sampling for RL (PSRL) [2], contrasting frequentist high-probability guarantees with Bayesian expected regret. In the offline setting, where no further interaction is permitted, we show how pessimistic value iteration (PEVI) flips the sign of exploration bonuses to obtain minimax-optimal suboptimality bounds under only partial coverage assumptions [3, 5]. We further discuss instance-dependent and tail characterizations of regret [6], the spurious-correlation phenomenon unique to offline data, and the open problems at the frontier: instance-optimality, model-free optimality, and representation learning beyond linearity.

## 1 Introduction

Reinforcement learning formalizes sequential decision-making under uncertainty: an agent interacts with an environment modeled as a Markov decision process (MDP), receives rewards, and seeks to maximize long-term return. The central difficulty — the *exploration–exploitation dilemma* — is that the environment's dynamics are unknown, so the agent must balance exploiting actions known to yield high reward against exploring poorly understood actions that might yield more.

Modern RL theory seeks *provably efficient* algorithms: procedures whose sample complexity or cumulative regret can be bounded by polynomial functions of the problem parameters (states $S$, actions $A$, horizon $H$, feature dimension $d$, episodes $K$) *before* the algorithm ever runs. Three principles dominate this landscape:

1. **Optimism in the face of uncertainty (OFU):** act as if the environment were the most favorable among all statistically plausible candidates. Implemented via *upper confidence bounds (UCB)* added as exploration bonuses [1].
2. **Posterior sampling (Thompson sampling):** maintain a posterior over environments and act optimally for a single random sample — exploration emerges from the *variance* of sampled policies rather than from optimism [2].
3. **Pessimism in the face of uncertainty:** in *offline* RL, where no further interaction is possible, flip the sign of the bonus and act as if the environment were the least favorable among plausible candidates, guarding against distributional shift [3].

This thesis surveys the regret-bound machinery underlying these paradigms, traces the convergence of the tabular minimax rates, extends the analysis to linear function approximation, and formalizes the offline counterpart. We emphasize *why* the bounds take the shapes they do — the role of self-normalized concentration, elliptical potentials, Bayesian posterior symmetry, and concentrability coefficients — rather than merely listing results.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Episodic finite-horizon MDPs

An episodic finite-horizon MDP is a tuple $\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, H, \{P_h\}, \{r_h\})$, where $\mathcal{S}$ is the state space with $|\mathcal{S}| = S$, $\mathcal{A}$ is the action space with $|\mathcal{A}| = A$, $H$ is the episode horizon, $P_h(\cdot \mid s, a)$ is the transition kernel at stage $h$, and $r_h(s, a) \in [0, 1]$ is the mean reward. The agent interacts for $K$ episodes; total steps $T = KH$.

For a policy $\pi = (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_H)$, the value and action-value functions are:

$$V_h^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\left[\sum_{h'=h}^H r_{h'}(s_{h'}, a_{h'}) \,\middle|\, s_h = s\right], \qquad Q_h^\pi(s,a) = r_h(s,a) + \mathbb{E}_{s'\sim P_h(\cdot|s,a)}[V_{h+1}^\pi(s')].$$

The optimal value $V_h^\star = \max_\pi V_h^\pi$ satisfies the Bellman optimality equations. The performance of an online algorithm playing policies $\pi^k$ in episode $k$ is measured by **cumulative regret**:

$$\mathrm{Regret}(K) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \left(V_1^\star(s_1^k) - V_1^{\pi^k}(s_1^k)\right).$$

A *sublinear* regret bound, $\mathrm{Regret}(K) = \tilde{O}(\sqrt{K})$ (the $\tilde{O}$ notation hides polylogarithmic factors), implies the average per-episode suboptimality vanishes: the agent provably converges to optimal behavior. The minimax rate — the best any algorithm can guarantee on the hardest MDP in the class — for tabular episodic MDPs is $\Omega(\sqrt{H^3SAT})$ in the stage-dependent setting [6], and matching upper bounds are now known [1].

### 2.2 Key analytical tools

Three technical ingredients recur throughout modern RL theory:

- **Concentration of the empirical model.** Hoeffding and Bernstein inequalities bound the deviation of empirical transitions $\hat{P}_h$ from $P_h$. The sharper Bernstein form incorporates the *empirical variance* of value estimates, which is essential for the optimal $H$-dependence [1].
- **Self-normalized bounds.** In linear settings, the ridge-regression estimator $\hat{\theta} = \Lambda^{-1}\sum_t \phi_t y_t$ with $\Lambda = \lambda I + \sum_t \phi_t\phi_t^\top$ satisfies $|\langle \phi, \hat{\theta} - \theta^\star\rangle| \le \beta \lVert \phi\rVert_{\Lambda^{-1}}$ uniformly over all directions, with $\beta \approx \tilde{O}(d)$. The radius $\lVert \phi\rVert_{\Lambda^{-1}}$ shrinks precisely when new directions are explored [4].
- **Elliptical potential lemma.** $\sum_{t=1}^T \min(1, \lVert \phi_t\rVert_{\Lambda_t^{-1}}^2) \le 2d\log(1 + T/\lambda)$. This converts per-step uncertainty radii into a $\tilde{O}(d\sqrt{T})$ total, and is the engine behind all linear-MDP regret bounds.

### 2.3 The linear MDP

A linear MDP assumes a known feature map $\phi: \mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A} \to \mathbb{R}^d$ such that for signed measures $\mu_h$ and vectors $\theta_h$,

$$P_h(s'\mid s,a) = \langle \phi(s,a), \mu_h(s')\rangle, \qquad r_h(s,a) = \langle \phi(s,a), \theta_h\rangle.$$

Under this structure, $Q_h^\star(s,a) = \langle \phi(s,a), w_h^\star\rangle$ is itself linear, enabling least-squares value iteration (LSVI) with provable guarantees [4].

## 3 Methodology

We analyze the three paradigms through a common lens: each constructs, from data collected so far, a *plausible set* $\mathcal{P}_k$ of environments consistent with observations, then selects a policy by an extremal or randomized rule over this set:

| Paradigm | Selection rule over plausible set | Exploration driver | Regret type |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Optimism (OFU)** | $\arg\max_{\pi, \mathcal{M}\in\mathcal{P}_k} V^{\pi,\mathcal{M}}$ | Upper confidence bonuses | Frequentist, high-probability |
| **Posterior sampling** | Sample $\mathcal{M}_k \sim \mathrm{Post}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{H}_k)$, then $\arg\max_\pi V^{\pi,\mathcal{M}_k}$ | Variance of sampled policies | Bayesian expected |
| **Pessimism (offline)** | $\arg\max_{\pi} \min_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathcal{P}} V^{\pi,\mathcal{M}}$ (LCB) | Uncertainty *penalty* | Frequentist suboptimality |

Our methodology is theoretical: we state the flagship theorems of each paradigm, sketch the proof architectures that produce the rates, and compare the resulting sample-complexity and regret guarantees. Numerical illustrations are computed for the canonical scaling regimes.

### 3.1 The optimism template

Optimistic algorithms maintain upper confidence estimates $Q_{k,h} \ge Q_h^\star$ (with high probability) and act greedily: $a_{k,h} = \arg\max_a Q_{k,h}(s_{k,h}, a)$. The regret then telescopes:

$$\mathrm{Regret}(K) \le \sum_{k,h} \underbrace{\text{bonus}_{k,h}(s_{k,h}, a_{k,h})}_{\text{uncertainty radius}} + \text{martingale noise}.$$

Hence the entire problem reduces to controlling the sum of uncertainty radii — precisely what the elliptical potential lemma and count-based arguments accomplish. Bernstein-type bonuses additionally scale with the empirical standard deviation of $V_{h+1}^\star$, replacing an $H$ factor with the (smaller) variance of near-optimal trajectories [1].

### 3.2 The posterior sampling template

PSRL [2] avoids confidence sets entirely. With prior $\phi$ over MDPs and history $\mathcal{H}_k$, the agent samples $\mathcal{M}_k \sim \phi(\cdot \mid \mathcal{H}_k)$ and executes $\pi^k = \pi^\star(\mathcal{M}_k)$. The key identity, valid for *any* $\sigma(\mathcal{H}_k)$-measurable quantity, is posterior symmetry:

$$\mathbb{E}\left[V_1^{\star}(\mathcal{M}) \mid \mathcal{H}_k\right] = \mathbb{E}\left[V_1^{\pi^k}(\mathcal{M}_k) \mid \mathcal{H}_k\right].$$

That is, the sampled MDP and the true MDP are identically distributed given the history — so expected regret equals the expected *one-step Bellman error* of the sampled model, which concentration controls. No union bound over models is needed; optimism is replaced by probability matching.

### 3.3 The pessimism template (offline)

In offline RL the agent receives a fixed dataset $\mathcal{D}$ collected by a behavior policy $\mu$, with no further interaction. Optimism is dangerous here: it extrapolates high value onto state–actions absent from $\mathcal{D}$, where estimates are pure noise. Pessimistic value iteration (PEVI) [3] instead subtracts an uncertainty quantifier $\Gamma_h(s,a)$:

$$\hat{Q}_h(s,a) = \hat{r}_h(s,a) + \hat{P}_h \hat{V}_{h+1}(s,a) - \Gamma_h(s,a), \qquad \hat{\pi} = \arg\max_\pi \hat{V}_1^\pi.$$

The penalty $\Gamma_h$ is exactly the exploration bonus of the online setting with flipped sign — making PEVI "easily implementable and compatible with general function approximators" [3]. The learned policy is then a *best effort* given the data: no other policy can be certified better.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Tabular minimax optimality: UCBVI

Azar, Osband, and Munos [1] proved the first minimax-optimal regret bound for episodic tabular RL. Their algorithm, UCBVI, performs model-based value iteration with Bernstein-style exploration bonuses:

$$b_{k,h}(s,a) \;\propto\; \sqrt{\frac{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{s'\sim\hat{P}_{k,h}(\cdot|s,a)}(\bar{V}_{k,h+1}(s'))\cdot L}{N_k(s,a)}} + \frac{HL}{N_k(s,a)},$$

where $N_k(s,a)$ is the visit count and $L$ a logarithmic factor.

> **Theorem (UCBVI, Azar et al. 2017):** With probability at least $1-\delta$, the regret of UCBVI with Bernstein bonuses satisfies
> $$\mathrm{Regret}(K) = \tilde{O}\left(\sqrt{H^3 SAK} + H^3S^2A\right).$$
> The leading term matches the information-theoretic lower bound $\Omega(\sqrt{H^3 SAK})$, so UCBVI is minimax-optimal up to logarithmic factors [1].

Two innovations made this possible. First, concentration is applied to the *optimal value function as a whole* rather than to individual transition probabilities, improving the $S$-dependence. Second, Bernstein bonuses exploit the empirical variance, improving the $H$-dependence: the total variance of a trajectory is bounded by $H^2$ in the worst case but is typically much smaller (the *law of total variance* for MDPs). Subsequent refinements (EULER, refined UCBVI analyses) improved lower-order terms and constants, and recent work characterizes even the full *tail distribution* of UCBVI-style regret, revealing a two-regime structure — sub-Gaussian up to an instance-dependent scale, then sub-Weibull beyond it [6].

### 4.2 Linear function approximation: LSVI-UCB and LSVI-UCB$^+$

Jin et al. introduced the linear MDP and LSVI-UCB, the first provably efficient algorithm for RL with linear function approximation, achieving $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{d^3H^4K})$ regret. A $\sqrt{Hd}$ gap to the $\Omega(Hd\sqrt{T})$ lower bound persisted until Hu et al.'s LSVI-UCB$^+$ [4], which combines:

1. **Weighted ridge regression**, down-weighting high-variance targets (heteroscedastic noise);
2. **A Bernstein-type exploration bonus** with a novel self-normalized concentration bound "with conservatism on elliptical potentials";
3. A refined correction-term analysis handling the bias of the weighted estimator.

> **Theorem (LSVI-UCB$^+$, Hu et al. 2022):** For episodic inhomogeneous linear MDPs, LSVI-UCB$^+$ achieves
> $$\mathrm{Regret}(K) = \tilde{O}\left(Hd\sqrt{T}\right), \qquad T = KH,$$
> which is minimax-optimal up to logarithmic factors, closing the $\sqrt{Hd}$ gap [4].

The proof's core is a new Bernstein self-normalized inequality: for the weighted design matrix, the estimation error in *any* direction $\phi$ is controlled by $\lVert \phi\rVert_{\Lambda^{-1}}$ times a variance-aware radius, and summing over episodes via the elliptical potential lemma yields the $d\sqrt{T}$ scaling. Variants (LSVI-UCB$^{++}$) achieve the same rate with improved computational properties, and the framework extends to linear mixture MDPs.

### 4.3 Posterior sampling: Thompson sampling for MDPs

Osband, Russo, and Van Roy's PSRL [2] remains the canonical non-optimistic algorithm with finite-time regret bounds. The Bayesian regret,

$$\mathrm{BayesRegret}(K, \phi) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}\sim\phi}\left[\sum_{k=1}^K \big(V_1^\star(s_1^k; \mathcal{M}) - V_1^{\pi^k}(s_1^k; \mathcal{M})\big)\right],$$

averages over a prior $\phi$ on MDPs — appropriate when prior knowledge exists and average-case performance matters.

> **Theorem (PSRL, Osband et al. 2013):** For tabular episodic MDPs with episode length $\tau$, PSRL satisfies
> $$\mathrm{BayesRegret}(T, \phi) = \tilde{O}\left(\tau S\sqrt{AT}\right).$$
> This was among the first sublinear regret bounds for any algorithm *not* based on optimism [2].

The analysis is strikingly elegant: posterior symmetry makes the expected instantaneous regret equal to the expected Bellman error of the *sampled* model, and standard concentration bounds the latter. Empirically, PSRL "significantly outperforms existing algorithms with similar regret bounds" because it explores proportionally to posterior uncertainty rather than worst-case confidence widths — it is *probability matching*, concentrating exploration where it matters. Later work extended PSRL to eluder-dimension settings ($\tilde{O}(\sqrt{d_K d_E T})$, the first unified model-based bounds scaling with dimensionality rather than cardinality), linear-quadratic control, and kernelized MDPs. A model-free analogue, randomized least-squares value iteration (RLSVI), injects noise into value targets to approximate posterior sampling without an explicit model.

### 4.4 Offline RL: pessimism and partial coverage

The offline setting replaces regret with *suboptimality*: $\mathrm{SubOpt}(\hat{\pi}) = V_1^\star - V_1^{\hat{\pi}}$, where $\hat{\pi}$ is learned from a fixed dataset $\mathcal{D}$ of $n$ trajectories. Classical analyses required *sufficient coverage* — finite concentrability coefficients $C^\star = \max_{h,s,a} d_h^\star(s,a)/d_h^\mu(s,a)$ bounding the density ratio between the optimal policy's visitation and the behavior policy's. Such assumptions are rarely verifiable and often false.

Jin, Yang, and Wang's PEVI [3] removed the coverage requirement:

> **Theorem (PEVI, Jin et al. 2021):** Without assuming sufficient coverage, PEVI with a valid uncertainty quantifier $\Gamma_h$ satisfies, with high probability,
> $$\mathrm{SubOpt}(\hat{\pi}) \;\lesssim\; \sum_{h=1}^H \mathbb{E}_{\pi^\star}\big[\Gamma_h(s_h, a_h)\big].$$
> Specialized to linear MDPs, this matches the information-theoretic lower bound up to dimension and horizon factors — pessimism is minimax-optimal [3].

The bound is *data-dependent*: suboptimality scales with uncertainty *along the optimal policy's trajectory*, not uniformly. If the dataset covers what $\pi^\star$ needs, learning succeeds; otherwise the algorithm degrades gracefully rather than hallucinating. The analysis identifies *spurious correlation* as the core pathology pessimism cures: value estimates at poorly covered $(s,a)$ pairs correlate with noise in ways that fool optimistic or naive algorithms, and subtracting $\Gamma_h$ provably eliminates this effect.

Rashidinejad et al. [5] sharpened the picture by studying a weak concentrability coefficient measuring deviation from the *behavior* policy to the *expert* policy alone, showing that lower-confidence-bound (LCB) algorithms interpolate smoothly between imitation learning (near-expert data, fast rates) and offline RL (broad data, standard rates) — and are adaptively optimal across the data-composition spectrum in bandits and nearly so in MDPs.

### 4.5 Instance-dependence, tails, and the frontier

Worst-case bounds are only the beginning. Recent work derives *instance-dependent* guarantees scaling with the MDP's suboptimality gaps $\Delta$ — e.g., gap-dependent bounds of order $\sum_{s,a} H^3\log K/\Delta(s,a)$ — which are exponentially smaller on "easy" instances. The tail analysis of UCBVI [6] goes further, characterizing $\Pr(\mathrm{Regret}_K \ge x)$ itself: a sub-Gaussian tail from an instance-dependent scale $m_K$ to a threshold, then a sub-Weibull tail beyond. These results show that optimism is not merely optimal in expectation — its entire regret *distribution* is controlled by instance geometry.

Open frontiers include: (i) instance-optimal (not just gap-adaptive) algorithms; (ii) provably efficient *model-free* algorithms matching model-based rates in full generality; (iii) beyond linearity — general function approximation via Bellman-eluder dimension and bilinear classes; (iv) offline RL with *confounded* or partially observed data, where pessimism must be combined with causal identification; and (v) unifying online optimism and offline pessimism into a single "uncertainty-aware" principle with a sign flip determined by the interaction budget.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We complement the theory with a scaling illustration and a compact proof sketch of the optimism regret decomposition — the single argument from which nearly all OFU bounds flow.

### 5.1 Scaling comparison of flagship bounds

Consider a tabular MDP with $S = 100$, $A = 4$, $H = 20$, and $K = 10^5$ episodes ($T = 2\times10^6$ steps). Leading-order regret scales:

| Algorithm | Setting | Leading regret order | Numerical scale ($\times$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| UCRL2 (Jaksch et al.) | Tabular, avg. reward | $\tilde{O}(DS\sqrt{AT})$ | $\sim D\cdot 10^2\sqrt{4\cdot 2{\times}10^6}\approx 2.8{\times}10^5 D$ |
| UCBVI [1] | Tabular episodic | $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{H^3SAT})$ | $\sqrt{8000\cdot100\cdot4\cdot2{\times}10^6}\approx 8{\times}10^7$ |
| PSRL [2] | Tabular episodic (Bayesian) | $\tilde{O}(\tau S\sqrt{AT})$ | $20\cdot100\sqrt{4\cdot2{\times}10^6}\approx 5.7{\times}10^6$ |
| LSVI-UCB$^+$ [4] | Linear MDP, $d=50$ | $\tilde{O}(Hd\sqrt{T})$ | $20\cdot50\sqrt{2{\times}10^6}\approx 1.4{\times}10^6$ |
| PEVI [3] | Offline linear, $n$ trajs | $\tilde{O}(dH^{1.5}/\sqrt{n})$ subopt. | $50\cdot89/\sqrt{n}\approx 4450/\sqrt{n}$ |

The table makes two facts vivid: optimism's confidence widths are wider than posterior sampling's typical exploration (frequentist worst-case vs. Bayesian average-case), and linear structure collapses the $S$-dependence into $d$.

> **Theorem (Optimism regret decomposition):** Suppose an algorithm maintains $Q_{k,h} \ge Q_h^\star$ entrywise with probability $\ge 1-\delta$, with $Q_{k,h} - Q_h^\star \le 2\,b_{k,h}$ on the visited trajectory. Then
> $$\mathrm{Regret}(K) \le 2\sum_{k=1}^K\sum_{h=1}^H b_{k,h}(s_{k,h},a_{k,h}) + O(H\sqrt{T\log(1/\delta)}).$$

*Proof sketch.* Optimism gives $V_1^\star(s_1^k) \le V_{k,1}(s_1^k)$ where $V_{k,h} = \max_a Q_{k,h}(\cdot,a)$. Then
$V_{k,1} - V_1^{\pi^k} = (Q_{k,1} - Q_1^{\pi^k})(s_1^k, a_1^k) \le 2b_{k,1} + \mathbb{E}_{s'}[V_{k,2}(s') - V_2^{\pi^k}(s')]$,
and unrolling over $h$ telescopes the value differences into a sum of bonuses plus a martingale difference sequence bounded by Azuma–Hoeffding. ∎

A minimal Python sketch of the UCBVI-style bonus computation illustrates the mechanics:

```python
import numpy as np

def ucbvi_bonus(N, emp_var, H, L):
    """Bernstein-style exploration bonus for (s, a) with N visits."""
    if N == 0:
        return H  # maximal optimism for unseen pairs
    return np.sqrt(2 * emp_var * L / N) + 7 * H * L / (3 * N)

def pessimistic_penalty(N, emp_var, H, L):
    """PEVI penalty: identical quantifier, flipped sign (offline RL)."""
    return ucbvi_bonus(N, emp_var, H, L)

# Example: well-covered vs. poorly covered state-action pairs
L = np.log(1e6)
for N in [10000, 100, 1]:
    b = ucbvi_bonus(N, emp_var=4.0, H=20, L=L)
    print(f"N={N:6d}  bonus={b:6.2f}  pessimistic value shift=-{b:.2f}")
```

Output shows bonuses decaying as $1/\sqrt{N}$: optimism *adds* this to encourage visits, pessimism *subtracts* it to discourage trusting thin data — the same quantifier, opposite sign, opposite regime.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Linearity is restrictive.** The linear MDP assumption requires a *known* feature map under which both transitions and rewards are linear — rarely true in practice. Extensions to general function approximation (Bellman-eluder dimension, bilinear classes) give bounds scaling with abstract complexity measures that are often unverifiable or vacuous for neural networks.
2. **Worst-case bounds can be vacuous.** A $\tilde{O}(Hd\sqrt{T})$ guarantee is vacuous until $T \gg H^2d^2$; constants and lower-order terms ($H^3S^2A$ in UCBVI) dominate at practical sample sizes. Instance-dependent bounds mitigate this but require unknown gaps.
3. **Computation vs. statistics.** Optimistic planning over confidence sets (e.g., extended value iteration over plausible MDPs) can be computationally intractable; practical implementations approximate it. PSRL requires posterior sampling and exact planning in the sampled model — feasible for conjugate tabular priors, hard generally.
4. **Bayesian vs. frequentist mismatch.** PSRL's guarantees average over the prior; a misspecified prior yields no frequentist protection. Conversely, OFU's worst-case guarantees can be overly conservative, under-exploring in benign environments.
5. **Offline coverage is fundamental.** Pessimism is "best effort," not magic: if the dataset contains no information about $\pi^\star$'s trajectory, no algorithm can do well — the lower bounds bite. Partial coverage assumptions are weaker than uniform coverage but still untestable from data alone.
6. **Stationarity and realizability.** All bounds above assume stationary MDPs and (for linear results) exact realizability. Non-stationary, confounded, or partially observed settings demand substantially different machinery.

## 7 Conclusion

The theoretical foundations of reinforcement learning rest on a remarkably coherent duality: **optimism** drives provably efficient online exploration, **posterior sampling** achieves comparable guarantees through probability matching rather than confidence widths, and **pessimism** — optimism's sign-flipped mirror — delivers minimax-optimal offline learning under partial coverage. The minimax rates for tabular ($\tilde{O}(\sqrt{H^3SAT})$) and linear ($\tilde{O}(Hd\sqrt{T})$) episodic RL are now closed [1, 4]; the Bayesian regret of PSRL matches the same order in expectation [2]; and PEVI shows that subtracting the exploration bonus is both necessary and sufficient for offline efficiency [3], with LCB-style algorithms adapting across the imitation-to-offline spectrum [5]. Instance-dependent and tail characterizations [6] are now refining these worst-case statements into geometry-aware guarantees. The remaining frontier — general function approximation, computational tractability, and unification of the online/offline principles — will determine whether these elegant bounds translate into the next generation of reliable, deployable RL systems.
