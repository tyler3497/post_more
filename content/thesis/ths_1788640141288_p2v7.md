# Model-Based Congestion Control: BBR, Packet Conservation, and Kelly's Utility-Maximization Framework

## Abstract

Congestion control is the mechanism by which the Internet converts an uncoordinated population of packet senders into a stable, efficient, and approximately fair allocation of network capacity. This thesis traces the arc of the field from Van Jacobson's 1988 congestion-avoidance architecture — additive-increase/multiplicative-decrease (AIMD) dynamics driven by packet loss — through F. P. Kelly's utility-maximization framework, which recast congestion control as distributed convex optimization and supplied the mathematical foundation for fairness analysis, to the contemporary paradigm of model-based control exemplified by Google's Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time (BBR) algorithm. We analyze the theoretical foundations of loss-based control, the packet-conservation principle and Kleinrock's optimal operating point, and the decomposition of the network-wide utility problem into source and link subproblems coordinated by congestion "shadow prices." The core of the work is a detailed exposition of BBR versions 1, 2, and 3: the BtlBw/RTprop path model, the Startup/Drain/ProbeBW/ProbeRTT state machine, and the corrections addressing BBRv1's unfairness against CUBIC and its shallow-buffer losses. We evaluate the measurement-theoretic obstacles of model-based control — the joint-identifiability problem that prevents simultaneous estimation of bottleneck bandwidth and minimum delay — and survey empirical findings from the literature on BBR–CUBIC fairness, bufferbloat, and wireless performance. We conclude that Kelly's framework endures as the normative theory against which algorithms are judged, while model-based control represents the most consequential departure from the AIMD orthodoxy in three decades, and we identify open questions at the intersection of control theory, measurement, and fairness.

## 1. Introduction

In October 1986 the Internet experienced its first "congestion collapse": a 32 Kbit/s transcontinental path saw throughput fall to 40 bits per second as overloaded gateways discarded packets, triggering synchronized retransmissions that consumed capacity without delivering useful work [2]. Van Jacobson's 1988 paper *Congestion Avoidance and Control* — introducing slow start, congestion avoidance, and the packet-conservation principle — became the de facto control law of the global Internet for nearly thirty years [2].

Jacobson's algorithm is *loss-based*: it interprets packet loss as the congestion signal and responds with multiplicative window reduction. This entangles two logically distinct questions — *how fast should I send?* and *how do I know the network is congested?* — answering the second with a crude proxy and the first with the AIMD (additive-increase/multiplicative-decrease) law of Jacobson and Karels. Chiu and Jain later proved that among linear window-update schemes, only AIMD converges to fairness and efficiency simultaneously.

Two intellectual movements subsequently reshaped the field. First, Frank Kelly's 1997–1998 work on charging and rate control reinterpreted congestion control as *distributed optimization*: the Internet implicitly solves a system problem maximizing aggregate user utility subject to capacity constraints, with congestion prices (Lagrange multipliers) as the coordinating signal [3][4]. Kelly's framework supplied, for the first time, a normative criterion — *α-fairness*, including proportional fairness — against which any algorithm could be judged, and connected TCP's equilibrium to a well-posed convex program.

Second, beginning in 2016, Neal Cardwell, Yuchung Cheng, Soheil Hassas Yeganeh, and Van Jacobson himself proposed BBR (Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time), a *model-based* congestion control that abandons loss as the congestion signal entirely [1][8]. BBR explicitly estimates the two physical parameters of the path — bottleneck bandwidth (BtlBw) and minimum round-trip propagation delay (RTprop) — and operates at Kleinrock's optimal operating point: sending at exactly the bottleneck rate with exactly one bandwidth–delay product (BDP) of data in flight. On Google's backbone and YouTube, BBR delivered reported throughput gains of 2–25× over CUBIC and median global RTT reductions of 53% [1].

This thesis develops all three strands and their interconnections. We present the classical AIMD architecture and its AIMD convergence theory, derive Kelly's utility-maximization decomposition and its equilibrium characterization of TCP variants, and then give a comprehensive treatment of BBR: its measurement methodology, state machine, pacing architecture, the fairness and loss-pathology problems of BBRv1, and the mitigations of BBRv2 and BBRv3. We close with a critical evaluation of model-based control theory, its open measurement problems, and the unresolved tension between model-based efficiency and loss-based coexistence.

> **Theorem:** *Any distributed congestion-control algorithm whose window updates are linear in the congestion signal converges to efficiency and fairness only if its update law is additive-increase and multiplicative-decrease (AIMD), up to the fairness metric induced by the signal's aggregation across flows.*

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Jacobson's Congestion Architecture (1988)

Jacobson's contribution comprised four mechanisms, three of which survive in modern TCP [2]: **slow start** (exponential window growth for rapid bandwidth acquisition), **congestion avoidance** (`cwnd += 1/cwnd` per ACK — additive increase of one segment per RTT), and **fast retransmit/fast recovery** (1990), allowing recovery from isolated losses without collapsing the window.
- The **packet-conservation principle**: a new packet is not injected into the network until an old packet leaves — implemented as "clocking" new transmissions on the arrival of acknowledgments.

The conservation principle deserves emphasis: the network is stable *iff* senders preserve the invariant that in-flight packets are held constant except during deliberate probing [2]. Slow start deliberately violates conservation transiently; the steady state is conservative. Every loss-based algorithm since — Tahoe, Reno, NewReno, CUBIC — lives inside this envelope.

### 2.2 AIMD and Its Convergence Theory

Chiu and Jain (1989) modeled *n* users sharing a bottleneck with synchronous feedback. Their central result: **efficiency** (total load equals capacity) and **fairness** (equal shares) are simultaneously achievable only when the increase is additive and the decrease is multiplicative — AIMD. TCP's standard `cwnd` update — add `1/cwnd` per ACK, halve on loss — is the canonical AIMD instance, fair among flows with equal RTTs because each flow's window increments by the same additive constant per RTT.

### 2.3 TCP Vegas: The First Delay-Based Model

Lawrence Brakmo and Larry Peterson's TCP Vegas (1995) was the first serious attempt to use *delay* rather than *loss* as the congestion signal [6]. Vegas measures `BaseRTT` and estimates queueing via `Diff = (cwnd/BaseRTT − cwnd/RTT) × BaseRTT`, holding `α ≤ Diff ≤ β` packets queued (typical `α = 1`, `β = 3`). It anticipates congestion before loss, but was systematically starved by coexisting loss-based flows that filled the buffers Vegas kept empty, and its `BaseRTT` estimate was corrupted by persistent queueing — the same joint-identifiability problem BBR later confronted. BBR can be read as Vegas rebuilt on sturdier foundations: explicit bandwidth modeling, pacing, and periodic re-measurement.

### 2.4 Bufferbloat and the Standing-Queue Pathology

Gettys and Nichols' bufferbloat diagnosis (2011) identified the failure mode that motivates model-based control most directly: excessively large router buffers absorb loss-based senders' overshoot, converting bursty loss into *standing queues* and latency measured in seconds. A loss-based algorithm in a bloated buffer oscillates between queue overflow and underutilization — and its cure, the model-based approach, is to stop relying on the loss signal at all.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Kelly's System Problem

Consider a network with links `J` of capacity `c_j` and users (routes) `R`; user `r` traverses links given by the routing matrix `A`, where `a_{jr} = 1` if route `r` uses link `j` [3][4]. Each user has rate `x_r ≥ 0` and a strictly concave, increasing, differentiable utility `U_r(x_r)`. The *system problem* is:

```
SYSTEM:  maximize   Σ_{r∈R} U_r(x_r)
         subject to Σ_{r: j∈r} x_r ≤ c_j,  ∀j ∈ J
                    x_r ≥ 0,              ∀r ∈ R
```

This is a convex program with a unique optimizer `x*`. Introducing Lagrange multipliers (shadow prices) `μ_j ≥ 0` per link, the KKT conditions give the equilibrium characterization:

```
U_r'(x_r*) = q_r* = Σ_{j∈r} μ_j*     for x_r* > 0
```

i.e., each user's marginal utility equals the sum of the shadow prices along its route. Kelly's *user problem* then decentralizes the system: each source, observing only the aggregate price `q_r` on its path, maximizes `U_r(x_r) − q_r·x_r`, yielding `x_r = (U_r')^{−1}(q_r)` [3]. The network decomposes into a **source algorithm** (primal: adjust rate in response to observed price) and a **link algorithm** (dual: adjust price in response to overload) — gradient descent on the Lagrangian whose stability Kelly, Maulloo, and Tan analyzed via Lyapunov methods [4]. The framework's power is normative: the family `U_r(x_r) = w_r·x_r^{1−α}/(1−α)` yields *α-fairness*, with `α → 1` giving proportional fairness, `α = 2` TCP fairness, and `α → ∞` max-min fairness.

### 3.2 The TCP Equilibrium as Utility Maximization

Low, Peterson, and Wang (2002) showed that TCP Reno's equilibrium maximizes `Σ_r (−1/(β_r·x_r))` — a specific α-fair objective (`α = 2`) [3][5] — while TCP Vegas maximizes `Σ_r (α_r·d_r·log x_r)` (proportional fairness). The mapping is exact: *every* end-to-end congestion control implicitly maximizes some aggregate utility; the question is only *which* utility, and whether the network's implicit objective is one we would choose.

### 3.3 BBR's Path Model and Kleinrock's Operating Point

Cardwell et al. observe that any path is fully characterized, for control purposes, by two parameters: **BtlBw**, the bottleneck bandwidth (maximum sustainable delivery rate), and **RTprop**, the round-trip propagation time (minimum achievable RTT, i.e., delay with no queueing).

The optimal operating point is then immediate: `pacing rate = BtlBw`, `in-flight data = BDP = BtlBw × RTprop`. This is Kleinrock's optimal operating point (1979): full throughput with minimal delay — one BDP in flight, zero queue. Delivery-rate vs. delay plots show throughput climbing linearly with in-flight data until BtlBw, then flat, while delay is flat at RTprop until the BDP knee, then rises linearly. Loss-based algorithms operate at the *cliff* (they fill buffers until loss); BBR aims at the *knee*.

### 3.4 The Measurement Methodology: Max and Min Filters

BBR estimates `BtlBw` as the **maximum observed delivery rate** over a sliding window (10 RTTs) and `RTprop` as the **minimum observed RTT** over a longer window (10 s). The max/min filter design is principled: transient queueing can only *reduce* observed delivery rate and *inflate* observed RTT, so the extrema are the least-contaminated estimates of the physical parameters — provided the filters observe an uncongested instant at least once per window. The entire BBR state machine exists to guarantee, approximately, that such instants occur.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Joint-Identifiability Problem

> **Theorem:** *BtlBw and RTprop cannot be measured simultaneously. Measuring BtlBw requires saturating the bottleneck (in-flight ≥ BDP), which creates queue and inflates RTT above RTprop; measuring RTprop requires draining the pipe (in-flight ≤ BDP), which prevents saturation and underestimates BtlBw.*

This is the central measurement-theoretic constraint of model-based control: any (BtlBw, RTprop) estimator faces a structural bias, and BBR's answer is *temporal multiplexing* — spend most time near the operating point, periodically overshoot to probe bandwidth (ProbeBW), and periodically drain to re-measure RTprop (ProbeRTT). The control problem becomes a measurement-scheduling problem.

### 4.2 The BBRv1 State Machine

BBRv1 cycles through four states [1][8]:

| State | Purpose | Pacing gain | cwnd gain | Exit condition |
|-------|---------|-------------|-----------|----------------|
| **Startup** | Exponentially find BtlBw | 2/ln 2 ≈ 2.89 | 2/ln 2 ≈ 2.89 | 3 rounds with <25% BtlBw growth |
| **Drain** | Remove queue built in Startup | ln 2 / 2 ≈ 0.35 | 2/ln 2 ≈ 2.89 | In-flight ≤ BDP |
| **ProbeBW** | Steady-state bandwidth probing | cycles [1.25, 0.75, 1×6] | 2 | — (majority state) |
| **ProbeRTT** | Re-measure RTprop | — | 4 MSS | 200 ms + 1 RTT with no new min |

**Startup** doubles the sending rate per RTT until delivery-rate growth stalls (three consecutive rounds below 25% growth), deliberately overshooting into queue. **Drain** then reverses the gain (`ln 2/2`) to empty the queue. **ProbeBW**, the steady state, cycles the pacing gain through `[1.25, 0.75, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1]` — one RTT probing for more bandwidth, one RTT draining that queue, six RTTs cruising at exactly BtlBw. **ProbeRTT** fires if 10 seconds pass without a fresh RTprop sample: cwnd collapses to 4 MSS for ~200 ms plus one RTT, draining the pipe so the minimum-RTT filter observes a clean sample.

Pacing is essential: BBR never transmits in ACK-clocked bursts; it spreads packets at `pacing_rate = pacing_gain × BtlBw` using the Linux fq qdisc. Rate is controlled directly; the window merely caps in-flight data.

### 4.3 BBRv1's Pathologies: Fairness and Loss

BBRv1's deployment success was accompanied by three documented pathologies:

1. **Inter-protocol unfairness (BBR vs. CUBIC).** Ware et al. (IMC 2019) and the TUM reproduction [7] found a single BBR flow competing with 16 CUBIC flows capturing ~40% of bandwidth against a fair share of ~6%. BBR's ProbeBW overshoot injects queue that CUBIC treats as available capacity, while BBR ignores the resulting loss; CUBIC backs off multiplicatively on loss that BBR caused, and BBR never backs off at all.
2. **Intra-protocol unfairness (BBR vs. BBR).** Flows with different RTTs converge to unequal shares, and ProbeRTT requires *all* competing flows to drain simultaneously to observe a true RTprop; unsynchronized probes leave residual queue, inflating latecomers' estimates and locking in advantage for early flows.
3. **Shallow-buffer excess loss.** Because Startup overshoots to `2/ln 2 × BDP` before detecting stalled growth, BBRv1 suffers high retransmission rates when the bottleneck buffer holds less than ~1.5 BDP [7].

### 4.4 BBRv2: Adding Explicit Congestion Signals

BBRv2 (first published 2019, IETF draft [8]) retains the BtlBw/RTprop model but adds the feedback channels BBRv1 ignored, making it a hybrid model/signal controller: **ECN** as a secondary signal (reducing the BtlBw estimate when marking rates rise), a **loss-rate cap** (~2%) that treats excess loss as congestion, **inflight bounds** (`inflight_hi`/`inflight_lo` learned from loss/ECN events) capping ProbeBW overshoot, and **loss-sensitive Startup** that exits on loss-rate thresholds rather than only on stalled bandwidth growth. Empirically, BBRv2 coexists markedly better with CUBIC and Reno, at the cost of some of BBRv1's aggressiveness: it is, deliberately, closer to the loss-based equilibrium.

### 4.5 BBRv3: Refinement and the Linux Mainline

BBRv3 (merged into the Linux kernel in 2023, version 6.x) continues the BBRv2 architecture with retuned parameters and improved Startup behavior: faster bandwidth convergence and better handling of high-jitter wireless paths. The trajectory v1 → v2 → v3 is instructive: pure model-based control proved unworkable in a mixed-protocol Internet, and each revision has reintroduced more of the explicit-feedback machinery that model-based control was supposed to supersede — suggesting the stable endpoint is *model-guided, signal-corrected* control rather than signal-free control.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 BBRv1 Deployment Results

Cardwell et al. [1] reported BBR's production deployment across Google's B4 WAN and YouTube: throughput improvements of 2–25× over CUBIC on lossy or high-delay paths, and a 53% reduction in median global RTT (80% in developing regions). The gains concentrate where the loss-based model is most wrong: wireless and long-haul paths where random loss collapses CUBIC's window, and bloated buffers where standing queue inflates delay.

### 5.2 Independent Reproduction

The TUM reproducibility study [7] confirmed BBR's throughput advantage while quantifying the pathologies: retransmission rates exceeding CUBIC's in shallow buffers, ProbeRTT synchronization failures, and inter-protocol unfairness against CUBIC. A PLOS ONE study of BBR-n [9] derived revised startup gains analytically for Wi-Fi 5, finding the default startup gain `4·ln 2 ≈ 2.773` suboptimal under 802.11ac frame aggregation — evidence that BBR's constants are tuned for wired WANs and degrade on wireless media.

### 5.3 Kelly's Framework: Analytical Results

Kelly's decomposition yields theorems, not just measurements [3][4]: the system problem admits a unique maximizer characterized by the KKT conditions; the primal-dual dynamics `ẋ_r = κ_r(w_r − x_r q_r)`, `μ̇_j = [y_j − c_j]⁺` are globally asymptotically stable under mild conditions (Lyapunov analysis), even with bounded heterogeneous delays; and weighted proportional fairness emerges from logarithmic utilities, with the α-fair family interpolating between throughput-maximization (`α → 0`), proportional fairness (`α = 1`), TCP fairness (`α = 2`), and max-min fairness (`α → ∞`).


```python
import numpy as np

C = 10.0
w = np.array([1.0, 2.0])
kappa, mu = 0.5, 0.1
x = np.array([1.0, 1.0])

for t in range(2000):
    mu = max(0.0, mu + 0.05 * (x.sum() - C))
    x = np.maximum(x + kappa * (w - x * mu), 1e-6)

print("rates:", x, "sum:", x.sum())
# -> x = [3.33, 6.67]: shares proportional to willingness to pay
```

The simulation converges to `x_r = w_r/μ` with `x_1 + x_2 = C` — the proportionally fair allocation, demonstrating that decentralized price-taking sources and a single price-updating link jointly solve the system problem with no global coordination.

### 5.4 Fairness Comparison: Summary of Empirical Findings

| Scenario | CUBIC–CUBIC | BBRv1–CUBIC | BBRv1–BBRv1 | BBRv2–CUBIC |
|----------|-------------|-------------|-------------|-------------|
| Equal RTT, deep buffer | Fair (AIMD) | BBR dominates (~40% vs 6% fair share) | RTT-dependent skew | Near-fair |
| Shallow buffer (<1.5 BDP) | Fair, low loss | BBR high retransmissions | High retransmissions | Bounded by inflight_hi |
| High random loss (wireless) | Collapse | BBR 2–25× better | BBR better | BBRv2 better, less aggressive |
| Bufferbloat path | Standing queue | Low delay (knee operation) | Low delay | Low delay |

## 6. Limitations and Threats to Validity

**The model may be unidentifiable in practice.** The joint-identifiability problem (Section 4.1) is fundamental: any estimator of (BtlBw, RTprop) from (delivery rate, RTT) samples faces a bias–variance frontier, and BBR's ProbeRTT schedule (10 s) is an arbitrary compromise. On paths with rapidly varying capacity (millimeter-wave, LEO satellite handoffs), the model goes stale faster than the probing schedule refreshes it.

**Fairness remains protocol-relative.** BBRv2/v3 improve coexistence with CUBIC, but "fairness" between a model-based and a loss-based flow has no canonical definition: the two optimize different objectives (knee operation vs. buffer filling). Kelly's framework judges equilibria against utility functions, but BBR's equilibrium — when it exists — maximizes no known aggregate utility; it is a fixed point of a measurement procedure, not of an optimization.

**The utility framework's own idealizations.** Kelly's analysis assumes price-taking (non-strategic) users, fluid rates, and no packet-level dynamics; real TCP's ACK clocking, timeouts, and burstiness violate the fluid model. The celebrated stability proofs cover linearized dynamics around equilibrium and say little about transient behavior — which is where congestion collapse lives.


**Security and incentive compatibility.** Model-based control trusts the sender's measurements; a malicious or miscalibrated sender can claim arbitrary BtlBw. Loss-based control's signals (loss, ECN) are at least generated by the network.

## 7. Conclusion

The history of congestion control is the history of the congestion *signal*. Jacobson's 1988 architecture [2] chose loss, and AIMD dynamics made that choice fair and efficient under idealized conditions. Kelly's utility-maximization framework [3][4] then showed that any such algorithm implicitly solves an optimization problem — supplying the field with its first normative theory and its vocabulary of fairness. BBR [1][8] chose a different signal: the physical parameters of the path itself, estimated by direct measurement and exploited by operating at Kleinrock's knee.

BBR's lesson is twofold. First, model-based control works: on the paths where loss-based control fails most dramatically — lossy wireless, bloated buffers, intercontinental delay — explicit modeling delivers order-of-magnitude improvements. Second, pure model-based control does not survive contact with a mixed-protocol Internet: BBRv1's unfairness against CUBIC, its self-unfairness, and its shallow-buffer losses forced the reintroduction of precisely the explicit signals (ECN, loss thresholds) the model was meant to replace.

Kelly's framework endures because it asks the right question — *what allocation should the network converge to?* — while BBR answers a different one — *how should a single flow find the knee?* The field's central open problem is their synthesis: a model-based controller whose equilibrium is provably the maximizer of a stated, defensible utility function, robust to strategic senders and identifiable from feasible measurements.
