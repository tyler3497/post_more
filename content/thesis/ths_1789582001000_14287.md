# Neural Radiance Fields: Volume Rendering, Positional Encoding, and the Geometry of Novel View Synthesis

## Abstract

Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) recast novel view synthesis as the joint problem of learning and rendering a continuous volumetric scene function. A single multilayer perceptron maps a five-dimensional coordinate — spatial position plus viewing direction — to emitted radiance and volume density, and classic volume rendering integrates these quantities along camera rays into photorealistic images. We expound the two mechanisms that make this work: a quadrature scheme turning the emission-absorption integral into a differentiable weighted sum of samples, and a Fourier positional encoding that defeats the spectral bias of coordinate networks so fine detail can be represented. We analyze hierarchical importance sampling, the integrated encoding of mip-NeRF, the multiresolution hash encodings of instant-NGP, and the explicit Gaussian primitives of 3D Gaussian Splatting within one sampling-and-integration framework. Results on the Blender and LLFF benchmarks, with reported peak signal-to-noise ratios, anchor the theory. We close with the fundamental limits: per-ray integration cost, calibrated-pose requirements, the static-scene assumption, and the tension between implicit continuity and explicit editability.

##1 Introduction

Novel view synthesis, rendering a scene from viewpoints never observed, sits at the intersection of geometry, optics, and learning. Classical approaches split into two camps: image-based rendering, which interpolates photographs but collapses on complex view-dependent appearance, and explicit reconstruction, which commits to meshes or point clouds before appearance is ever modeled. Both separate geometry estimation from appearance modeling, and both pay for it in fragile intermediate representations. Neural Radiance Fields, introduced by Mildenhall and collaborators at ECCV 2020 [1], reject the separation entirely. The scene is a single continuous function

```
F: (x, d) -> (c, sigma)
```

where spatial position **x** and viewing direction **d** determine emitted RGB color **c** and differential volume density **sigma**. This five-dimensional field is a fully connected network with no convolutions, optimized by gradient descent so its volume renderings match a sparse set of photographs with known camera poses. No depth supervision, no silhouettes, no mesh topology: only pixels and poses.

Three properties of this construction structure this thesis:

1. **The rendering operator is classical.** Emission-absorption volume rendering [6] predates deep learning by decades. Its integral is differentiable almost everywhere in the field parameters, so photometric error backpropagates through ray integration directly into the network weights.
2. **The representation is spectrally shaped.** Coordinate networks suffer from spectral bias, fitting low frequencies long before high ones. Positional encoding lifts the input into a Fourier basis, rebalancing the optimization landscape so sharp geometry and texture can emerge [2].
3. **The sampler matters as much as the field.** A continuous field is useless without a discretization concentrating computation where the integral's mass lives. Hierarchical sampling, conical frustums, and hash grids are all answers to the same quadrature problem.

##2 Background

### 2.1 The emission-absorption model of light transport

In a participating medium, radiance along a ray obeys a first-order ODE balancing emission against extinction. For a camera ray **r**(t) = **o** + t**d**, the expected color is

```
C(r) = integral T(t) * sigma(r(t)) * c(r(t), d) dt
T(t) = exp(- integral sigma(r(s)) ds)
```

with the inner integral running from the near bound to t. Transmittance T(t) is the probability the ray reaches t unabsorbed; sigma(r(t)) is the differential termination probability. This is the classical emission-absorption model of Kajiya and Von Herzen [6], rooted in radiative transfer. It ignores multiple scattering, treating the scene as a self-emitting, self-absorbing continuum — exactly the right fidelity for view synthesis: surfaces are regions of high density where transmittance collapses over a short interval, while empty space contributes nothing.

Two facts are load-bearing. First, the integral is *linear* in the color field weighted by transmittance, so gradients decompose cleanly along each ray. Second, its evaluation needs only point queries of sigma and c, never surface intersection tests, making it the natural rendering operator for an implicit representation.

### 2.2 Implicit neural representations and spectral bias

Before NeRF, coordinate networks memorized three-dimensional shapes as signed distance or occupancy fields, but they exposed a stubborn phenomenon: trained on low-dimensional coordinates, networks fit smooth, low-frequency functions far more readily than sharp, high-frequency ones. The neural tangent kernel analysis of Tancik et al. [2] made this *spectral bias* precise: a standard multilayer perceptron's kernel decays rapidly with frequency, so gradient descent converges on high-frequency components orders of magnitude more slowly.

For a radiance field, spectral bias is fatal, since texture, thin structures, and crisp silhouettes are all high-frequency content. Raw coordinates therefore yield renderings correct in the large and blurred in the small, a failure confirmed by the original ablations [1]. The remedy, detailed in Section 3.2, is a deterministic Fourier feature map lifting each scalar coordinate into a bank of sinusoids spanning several octaves [2].

### 2.3 Camera geometry and calibration

NeRF assumes known camera poses, typically from structure-from-motion systems such as COLMAP. Each training image carries an intrinsic matrix and extrinsic parameters defining the ray origin **o** and direction **d** per pixel. Pose quality bounds reconstruction quality: miscalibration smears every ray's supervision, and the purely photometric field invents semi-transparent haze to explain inconsistencies. Later work jointly optimizes poses alongside the field [7], but the baseline treats calibration as fixed ground truth.

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 The radiance field network

The canonical NeRF network is deliberately unremarkable: eight fully connected layers of width 256 with ReLU activations on the encoded spatial coordinate, then a branch concatenating the encoded viewing direction through one more layer to RGB color. Density sigma emerges from the position-only trunk, so occupancy cannot depend on viewing direction, the correct physical prior. View-dependent color captures specular highlights and anisotropic reflectance with no explicit material model.

> **Theorem:** *Density-view independence.* Let the density head depend only on the encoded position and the color head depend on position and direction. Then any view-consistent surface, defined as a closed two-manifold with a fixed bidirectional reflectance, is in the hypothesis class of the model.

> **Proof:** Concentrate high density in a thin shell around the manifold, which the trunk can represent by universal approximation. On the shell, the directional head approximates the surface reflectance over the sphere of directions, transmittance collapses within the shell, and the integral reduces to the surface term. Density never varies with direction by architecture, so the construction is admissible.

Formally, the mapping is F_theta(gamma(**x**), gamma(**d**)) = (**c**, sigma), where gamma is the positional encoding defined below and theta denotes the network parameters. The final color passes through a sigmoid, constraining outputs to the valid display range, while density passes through a ReLU to enforce nonnegativity.

### 3.2 Positional encoding as a Fourier lifting

Applied to each scalar coordinate p, the encoding is

```
gamma(p) = (sin(2^0 pi p), cos(2^0 pi p), ..., sin(2^{L-1} pi p), cos(2^{L-1} pi p))
```

with L = 10 for positions and L = 4 for directions, yielding 60- and 24-dimensional codes. Tancik et al. [2] showed this map flattens the neural tangent kernel's spectrum across the encoded bands, defeating spectral bias: gradient descent sees high-frequency residuals with the same effective step size as low-frequency ones.

Intuitively, the encoding is a fixed multiscale dictionary: the first layer becomes a learned Fourier series at dyadic frequencies, with data-dependent coefficients from deeper layers. The original ablations [1] are decisive: removing the encoding collapses fine detail and costs several decibels of PSNR on every test scene.

### 3.3 Differentiable volume rendering by quadrature

The rendering integral has no closed form for a neural field, so it is discretized by stratified sampling: the ray interval is partitioned into N evenly spaced bins, one sample drawn uniformly within each,

```
t_i ~ Uniform(near + (i-1)/N * (far - near), near + i/N * (far - near))
```

which guarantees full-interval coverage while keeping the estimator unbiased. Treating sigma and c as piecewise constant on segments of width delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i gives the quadrature

```
C_hat(r) = sum_i T_i (1 - exp(-sigma_i delta_i)) c_i
T_i = exp(- sum_{j < i} sigma_j delta_j)
```

The weight w_i = T_i (1 - exp(-sigma_i delta_i)) is the probability the ray terminates in segment i; the weights are nonnegative and sum to at most one, so the rendered color is a convex combination of sample colors, the discrete emission-absorption model. Every operation is differentiable, so the photometric loss

```
L = sum_r (||C_hat_c(r) - C_gt(r)||^2 + ||C_hat_f(r) - C_gt(r)||^2)
```

backpropagates through both coarse and fine renderings. Training uses 4096 rays per batch, Adam with a learning rate decaying from 5e-4 to 5e-5, and 100,000 to 300,000 iterations [1].

### 3.4 Hierarchical sampling

Uniform sampling wastes most evaluations in empty space, where sigma is zero. NeRF trains two networks: a *coarse* network at 64 stratified samples produces weights w_i, normalized into a piecewise-constant distribution along the ray, and a *fine* network evaluates 128 samples drawn from it by inverse transform sampling, concentrating computation near surfaces. Both renderings contribute to the loss, forcing the coarse network to learn a usable proposal.

This is importance sampling with the proposal learned jointly with the integrand. The coarse-to-fine ratio trades bias against variance: too few coarse samples and thin structures are missed; too many and the fine stage is starved.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The quadrature weights are an alpha-compositing law

The quadrature's algebraic form unifies volume rendering with classical alpha compositing. With alpha_i = 1 - exp(-sigma_i delta_i) and T_i = product over j < i of (1 - alpha_j),

```
C_hat = sum_i (product_{j<i} (1 - alpha_j)) alpha_i c_i
```

is front-to-back alpha blending with per-segment opacity alpha_i. NeRF is thus a machine predicting alpha and color at arbitrary points; everything else is bookkeeping. This also explains semi-transparent floaters: under inconsistent supervision, the optimizer prefers distributing small alpha values across many segments to committing to a sharp surface, producing the haze of under-constrained reconstructions.

### 4.2 Why Fourier features work: a kernel perspective

The neural tangent kernel result of [2] is the theoretical core of the method's representational power. In the infinite-width limit, gradient descent evolves the function under the kernel k(**x**, **x'**) = grad f(**x**) dot grad f(**x'**). For a standard perceptron on raw coordinates, this kernel's high-frequency eigenvalues decay rapidly, so fine detail converges slowly. The Fourier map gamma replaces it with k(gamma(**x**), gamma(**x'**)), approximately stationary with bandwidth set by the highest encoded frequency 2^{L-1}. Learning becomes band-limited kernel regression, and L = 10 sets the representational ceiling; pushing it higher invites overfitting to noise and view-inconsistent hallucination, which is why directions use the smaller L = 4.

> **Theorem:** *Bandwidth control.* For the Fourier map with maximum frequency 2^{L-1}, the induced stationary kernel has negligible mass beyond that frequency, so components above the cutoff converge no faster than the kernel's tail permits.

> **Proof sketch:** By Bochner's theorem, the composed kernel is a sum of cosines at the encoded frequencies; absent frequencies contribute only through the base kernel's tail, which decays exponentially in the frequency gap, bounding the convergence rate of any component above the cutoff.

### 4.3 Hierarchical sampling as learned importance sampling

The coarse network's normalized weights define a proposal distribution q(t) along the ray, from which fine samples are drawn. The joint loss penalizes the coarse rendering too, so the cheapest way to reduce its error is to place mass where the true weights are large, which is exactly the variance-optimal importance sampling proposal. In practice this brackets surfaces within a few dozen samples, which is why 64 coarse samples suffice where a uniform grid would need thousands. The loop is self-reinforcing but fragile: early collapse of the coarse network to a uniform proposal starves the fine stage, so the training schedule keeps the initial learning rate high and the coarse distribution diffuse while geometry is uncertain.

### 4.4 From cones to hash grids: mip-NeRF and instant-NGP

NeRF samples infinitesimal rays, but a pixel's footprint covers a cone, so multiscale rendering shimmers and blurs. Mip-NeRF [3] replaces rays with cones and point encodings with *integrated* positional encodings: the expected value of gamma over each conical frustum, approximated as a multivariate Gaussian with a closed-form encoding of its mean and covariance. One multiscale network replaces the coarse-fine pair, gaining roughly 2 dB on the multiscale Blender benchmark while removing the near and far bound sensitivity of the original.

Instant neural graphics primitives [4] attack the orthogonal bottleneck: the MLP itself. A multiresolution hash table stores trainable features at grid vertices across resolution levels; each query interpolates and concatenates per-level features, and a tiny two-layer network decodes them. Hash collisions between distant points are resolved implicitly by gradient averaging and prove harmless at scale. Training drops from hours to seconds and inference reaches real time, at the cost of tens of megabytes of tables and a less smooth field. The lesson is architectural: the *encoding* carries the representational burden, and the network can shrink once the encoding is expressive enough.

| Method | Encoding | Network depth | Train time (scene) | Multiscale PSNR (Blender) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NeRF [1] | Fourier, L=10 | 8 x 256 | ~12 hours | ~31.0 dB |
| mip-NeRF [3] | Integrated PE | 8 x 256 | ~12 hours | ~33.1 dB |
| Instant NGP [4] | Hash grid, 16 levels | 2 x 64 | ~5 seconds | ~32.6 dB |
| 3D Gaussian Splatting [5] | None (explicit) | None | ~30 minutes | ~33.1 dB |

### 4.5 The explicit counterrevolution: 3D Gaussian Splatting

3D Gaussian Splatting [5] abandons the implicit field: the scene is a set of anisotropic Gaussians with position, covariance, opacity, and spherical-harmonic colors, optimized by gradient descent and rasterized by a tile-based splatter. Rendering becomes projection and sorting at hundreds of frames per second, and the representation is explicit enough to edit and compress directly, matching or exceeding mip-NeRF quality with tens of minutes of training.

The comparison clarifies what the MLP bought: infinite resolution and topological freedom with no primitive budget. The Gaussian set buys speed and explicitness at the cost of a primitive count that must grow with scene complexity and a densification heuristic that is effectively learned meshing. Current research hybrids the two, using fields to initialize or regularize primitives, suggesting the dichotomy is a point on a spectrum rather than a final verdict.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

Evaluation uses two datasets from the original paper [1]: the *Blender* synthetic set (eight scenes, 100 training views on the upper hemisphere, 200 test views) and the *LLFF* set (eight real forward-facing scenes posed by COLMAP). Quality is reported as PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS.

1. **Blender synthetic.** NeRF reports 31.01 dB mean PSNR against 26.50 dB for the strongest prior baseline (Scene Representation Networks), with SSIM 0.947 and LPIPS 0.081 [1]; the margin is largest on view-dependent materials, where the directional color head is decisive.
2. **LLFF real scenes.** NeRF achieves 26.50 dB against 24.82 dB for local light field fusion [1]; imperfect poses, exposure variation, and unbounded backgrounds all degrade scores relative to synthetic data.
3. **Multiscale robustness.** mip-NeRF [3] reports 33.09 dB versus 31.20 dB for single-scale NeRF on the multiscale Blender set, with roughly half the parameters by eliminating the coarse network.
4. **Speed.** Instant NGP [4] reaches comparable quality in about five seconds on one GPU, four orders of magnitude faster than the original, and renders in real time.

> **Theorem:** *Quadrature consistency.* As the number of stratified samples N tends to infinity with fixed near and far bounds, the quadrature rendering converges to the true volume rendering integral for continuous density and color fields.

> **Proof:** Piecewise-constant interpolation of the continuous fields converges uniformly as segment width tends to zero, which stratified sampling guarantees almost surely. Discrete transmittance is the Riemann sum of the continuous optical depth, the exponential is uniformly continuous on the bounded density range, and the weighted sum is a Riemann sum of the integrand.

> **Theorem:** *View-consistency identifiability.* If the training views densely cover the viewing sphere around a bounded scene and the field has sufficient capacity, the global minimizer of the photometric loss reproduces the true radiance field on all observed rays.

> **Proof sketch:** Zero loss means every rendered training ray matches its observation. Under dense directional coverage, the rendering operator restricted to observed rays is injective over band-limited fields up to the encoding cutoff, since the weight functions span the representable frequencies. The global minimizer thus coincides with the true field on the observed support.

The second theorem's hypotheses are deliberately strong: dense spherical coverage and a well-posed inverse problem. Real captures violate both, which is why practical reconstructions exhibit the floater artifacts and view-extrapolation failures documented in Section 6. The theorems describe the method's ceiling, not its typical operating point.

##6 Limitations

- **Integration cost.** Rendering one 800-by-800 image with the original model needs hundreds of millions of network evaluations: 192 samples per ray over 640,000 rays. Even with hash encodings [4] or explicit primitives [5], volumetric integration costs orders of magnitude more than rasterization at comparable quality.
- **Calibrated poses.** The baseline assumes known cameras, and pose error becomes blurred or duplicated geometry. Joint pose-and-field optimization [7] helps but introduces a nonconvex bundle adjustment prone to local minima under large initial error.
- **Static scenes.** The field has no time argument. Dynamic extensions multiply data requirements and reintroduce the correspondence problem the static formulation sidesteps.
- **Unbounded scenes.** The near-far parameterization assumes a bounded volume. Inverted-sphere parameterizations and per-image appearance embeddings absorb background and exposure change [8], but each adds complexity the core formulation avoids.
- **Editability.** An MLP's weights are a global, entangled code with no notion of an object to move or relight. Explicit successors [5] recover editability by abandoning the implicit representation: a genuine tradeoff, not an implementation detail.
- **Aliasing and hallucination.** The encoding cutoff is double-edged: too low loses detail, too high invents view-inconsistent texture to explain noise. Mip-NeRF [3] fixes scale aliasing but not the identifiability gap of sparse-view capture.

---

##7 Conclusion

NeRF endures not because any component is novel — volume rendering [6], Fourier features [2], and importance sampling are all classical — but because their composition solves what none can alone: photorealistic novel view synthesis from sparse photographs with no geometric supervision. Its contribution is architectural: the rendering integral as the differentiable bridge between pixels and parameters, the Fourier encoding as the cure for the optimizer's spectral myopia, and the learned proposal as the mechanism making integration affordable.

Subsequent history is a systematic relaxation of the original's costs: mip-NeRF [3] fixed the sampling geometry, instant-NGP [4] shrank the network to a decoder, and Gaussian Splatting [5] traded the implicit field for explicit primitives and won back real-time rendering. Each preserved the core insight that appearance should be optimized through a physically grounded rendering operator, not regressed directly. Open problems persist: calibration-free capture, dynamic scenes, unbounded environments, and representations simultaneously continuous, editable, and fast. The radiance field is not the final answer to view synthesis, but it is the formulation every successor must now be measured against.
