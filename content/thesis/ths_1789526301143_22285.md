# Drift-Diffusion Dynamics and the Neural Economics of Choice: Optimal Stopping, Collapsing Bounds, and the Circuits of Perceptual Decision-Making

## Abstract

Perceptual decisions are deliberations rendered observable: how does a brain convert a noisy stream of sensory samples into a categorical commitment, and when should it stop sampling? This thesis develops the decision-neuroscience account from first principles: the drift-diffusion model as the continuous limit of Wald's sequential probability ratio test, the statistical optimality of the resulting stopping rule, and the economics of the speed-accuracy tradeoff. We then complicate the classical picture with collapsing bounds and urgency signals as the optimal policy under sampling costs, signatures of time-varying thresholds in reaction-time data, and the neural instantiation of accumulation in lateral intraparietal cortex, frontal eye fields, and cortico-basal ganglia loops that implement the decision threshold. Finally, we situate bounded-rationality strategies as resource-rational computations: given hard limits on time, noise, and metabolic cost, apparently suboptimal heuristics emerge as optimal solutions under constraints.

##1 Intro

Every perceptual decision is a wager made under uncertainty. A radiologist scans a mammogram, a tennis umpire tracks a serve, a monkey judges the net direction of flickering dots: in each case the observer receives a stream of *noisy* evidence, knows the stream will never become perfectly clear, and must decide both *what* to choose and *when* to stop looking. Reaction time and accuracy are not separate outcomes of separate mechanisms; they are the two visible faces of a single accumulation process that runs until a termination criterion is met [1][3].

The dominant theoretical framework for this process, the **drift-diffusion model (DDM)**, posits that momentary evidence is integrated into a decision variable that wanders stochastically toward one of two absorbing boundaries, each boundary standing for one choice alternative. The model's power lies in the unity it achieves: a single mechanism generates full reaction-time distributions for correct and error responses, the speed-accuracy tradeoff as the movement of one parameter (boundary separation), and the effect of bias as a shift of the starting point [3].

But the DDM is not merely a descriptive curve-fitting device. Under Gaussian evidence, it implements the statistically optimal algorithm for sequential choice — the **sequential probability ratio test (SPRT)** of Wald — which achieves any given accuracy in the minimum possible expected time, or equivalently any given speed at the maximum possible accuracy [2]. That a brain circuit might approximate an optimal stopping rule raises the central questions of this thesis: *Is the DDM the right computation? Is it the computation the brain actually performs? And under what constraints is it optimal?*

The answers turn out to be subtle. The classical model with fixed bounds is optimal only when sampling is free; once sampling carries a cost — attentional effort, metabolic expense, opportunity cost of delayed reward — the optimal bound *collapses* over time, and the brain appears to implement this collapse through a growing **urgency signal** added to accumulating evidence [7]. Meanwhile, single-unit recordings have identified candidate accumulators in parietal and frontal cortex and a threshold-setting circuit in the basal ganglia [4][6][8]. The final movement of this thesis is to reframe these findings within **resource-rational analysis**: the hypothesis that cognition is optimal *given* its computational constraints, which recasts bounded rationality not as failure but as solution [10].

##2 Background

### 2.1 The drift-diffusion model

Consider a two-alternative forced-choice (TAFC) task: a random-dot kinematogram in which some fraction of dots drift coherently left or right, and the observer must report the net direction as quickly as possible. On each trial the observer begins at a starting point *z* strictly between two boundaries at 0 and *a* (in the symmetric parametrization, at *-a* and *+a*). Momentary evidence arrives as a stochastic increment: signal plus noise. The decision variable *x(t)* evolves as a **Wiener process with drift**,

$$
dx = v\,dt + s\,dW, \qquad x(0) = z,$$

where *v* is the drift rate (mean rate of evidence accumulation, proportional to stimulus strength), *s* scales the diffusion noise, and *dW* is the Wiener increment. The process is absorbed when it first reaches either boundary; the boundary reached determines the choice, and the first-passage time determines the decision time. Observed reaction time is decision time plus a **nondecision time** *Ter* that absorbs stimulus encoding and motor execution [1][3].

The model's parameters map transparently onto psychological constructs:

- **Drift rate *v*** indexes the quality of the information — stimulus coherence, perceptual sensitivity, task difficulty. Harder stimuli produce smaller drift magnitudes and longer, more error-prone decisions.
- **Boundary separation *a*** implements the *speed-accuracy tradeoff*: wide bounds demand more evidence, slowing responses while suppressing errors; narrow bounds do the reverse [3].
- **Starting point *z*** encodes prior bias or asymmetric payoffs. A starting point nearer one boundary yields fast, error-prone responses toward that alternative even at zero drift.
- **Across-trial variability** in drift rate, starting point, and nondecision time (the "full" DDM) captures the empirically observed asymmetry between correct and error reaction-time distributions: variability in drift makes errors slower than corrects; variability in starting point makes them faster [3].

> Theorem: (Ratcliff, 1978) For fixed drift and diffusion, the first-passage-time densities of the Wiener process with two absorbing boundaries jointly determine the full joint distribution of choice and response time, including the conditional distributions of error latencies that distinguish the model from any pure-threshold or deadline account [1][3].

### 2.2 Wald's SPRT and optimal stopping

The optimality of the DDM traces back to Abraham Wald's 1940s theory of sequential analysis. Suppose observations arrive one at a time, each drawn from one of two hypothesized distributions *H1* or *H2*, and after each observation the decision maker may either stop and declare a hypothesis or pay for another observation. Wald's **sequential probability ratio test** accumulates the log-likelihood ratio

$$
\Lambda_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \frac{P(y_i \mid H1)}{P(y_i \mid H2)}\,,$$

and stops the first time $\Lambda_n$ exits the interval $(-A, A)$, choosing the hypothesis whose side was crossed [2].

> Theorem: (Wald and Wolfowitz, 1948) Among all sequential tests with error probabilities bounded by prescribed levels, the SPRT minimizes the expected sample size under both hypotheses. In the Gaussian continuum limit, the SPRT converges to the DDM with boundaries at $\pm A$ [2].

This theorem is the normative bedrock of the field: *if* the brain accumulates the log-likelihood ratio of its sensory observations, *then* terminating at fixed bounds is not a heuristic but the optimal stopping rule — the fastest possible way to reach a given accuracy, or the most accurate possible way at a given speed. Bogacz and colleagues showed that most accumulator models of TAFC decisions reduce to this SPRT-DDM equivalence, and that the optimal boundary setting is the one that maximizes **reward rate**: the fraction of correct responses per unit time, penalizing both errors and the time spent deliberating and waiting through intertrial intervals [2].

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 Fitting the DDM to behavior

The DDM's first-passage densities admit closed forms (infinite series of Gaussian terms), so maximum-likelihood fits to joint choice-RT data are routine. A canonical fitting protocol constrains parameters across conditions: drift rate varies freely with stimulus strength (coherence), while boundary separation, starting point, and nondecision time are shared across conditions unless a manipulation targets caution, bias, or encoding. The model is then asked to reproduce not just mean RT and accuracy but the full RT distributions — typically displayed as quantile-probability plots — for every coherence level and both correct and error responses. A successful fit reproduces the characteristic fan of distributions: stronger stimuli yield faster, tighter, more accurate RTs; weaker stimuli yield slow, dispersed distributions with substantial error mass [3].

```python
# Schematic: one DDM trial (Euler-Maruyama), two absorbing bounds
import numpy as np
def ddm_trial(v, a, z, s=1.0, dt=0.001, t_max=3.0, Ter=0.3):
    x = z
    t = 0.0
    while 0.0 < x < a and t < t_max:
        x += v * dt + s * np.sqrt(dt) * np.random.randn()
        t += dt
    choice = 1 if x >= a else 0
    return choice, t + Ter
```

### 3.2 Dynamic programming for optimal bounds under sampling costs

The fixed-bound policy is optimal only when observations are free. Drugowitsch and colleagues generalized the problem: suppose each unit of deliberation time carries a cost *c(t)* that may itself grow with elapsed time (attentional fatigue), and an incorrect choice forfeits reward while incurring penalty and intertrial delay. The optimal policy is then found by **dynamic programming** — Bellman-optimal stopping over the belief state (the posterior log-odds of the hypotheses) and elapsed time. The solution is a time-varying bound: the decision maker should demand less evidence the longer deliberation has already lasted, because the marginal value of further sampling is eventually outweighed by its cost [7].

### 3.3 Single-unit and circuit interrogation

Neural tests of the accumulation hypothesis use the random-dot motion task in macaques, with one of two saccade targets placed in the response field of the recorded neuron. If the neuron carries a decision variable, its firing rate should (a) build up at a rate proportional to motion coherence, (b) predict the animal's choice on a trial-by-trial basis, (c) reach a common level at decision time regardless of coherence, and (d) evolve during the period in which the decision is formed rather than merely at response execution [4]. Biophysical network models then ask which circuit motif — leaky integration with recurrent excitation in cortex, or threshold gating in the basal ganglia — reproduces these dynamics [8].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Speed-accuracy tradeoffs and the economics of the boundary

The speed-accuracy tradeoff is the most replicated law in the field: instruct observers to emphasize speed and they respond faster and err more; emphasize accuracy and the reverse follows. In DDM language this is boundary separation, and the model's quantitative prediction is sharp — lowering *a* speeds responses but increases error probability monotonically [3].

The normative question is which boundary is *right*. Bogacz et al. derived optimal boundaries for several objective functions: pure accuracy per unit time, accuracy with differential payoffs, and the **reward rate**

$$
RR(a) = \frac{\text{fraction correct}}{\langle T_{\text{decision}}(a)\rangle + T_{\text{iti}} + P_{\text{err}}(a) \, T_{\text{pen}}},$$

which treats time as the scarce resource it is. The reward-rate curve is an inverted U: tiny boundaries generate fast but near-chance responding, enormous boundaries generate near-perfect but glacial responding, and the optimum sits in between. Empirically, humans and monkeys adjust their boundaries in roughly the optimal direction when payoffs, penalties, or intertrial intervals change, though with systematic deviations — typically undershooting the optimal boundary when the optimum is high, consistent with an intrinsic cost of deliberation the experimenter did not program [2].

That mismatch motivated the collapsing-bound theory: perhaps observers are not mis-setting a fixed boundary but *optimally setting a time-varying one* [7].

### 4.2 Collapsing bounds and urgency signals

If sampling carries a cost that grows with time — sustained attention is effortful, and in natural environments delay risks losing the opportunity entirely — the optimal stopping boundary falls as deliberation proceeds. Drugowitsch et al. formalized this by estimating the cost function from behavior: fitting the full RT distributions of monkeys and humans with a dynamic-programming model in which *c(t)* is inferred rather than assumed. The estimated cost is flat for a brief initial period and then rises, and the inferred bound collapses accordingly; the model reproduces time-dependent choice accuracy, conditional accuracy given stimulus strength, and the full RT distributions [7].

The neural translation of a collapsing bound is elegant. Two racing accumulators (one per alternative) with a *fixed* firing-rate threshold behave exactly like a single diffusion process with *collapsing* symmetric bounds if a common, time-dependent **urgency signal** *u(t)* is added to both accumulators: adding *u(t)* to each accumulator is mathematically equivalent to lowering each bound by *u(t)* [7]. And indeed, recordings in LIP show precisely this: alongside coherence-dependent accumulation, firing rates exhibit a coherence-*independent* upward drift over the course of the trial — an urgency signal that pushes all neurons toward threshold as time elapses [6]. Urgency thus reconciles two observations that seemed contradictory: the decision threshold (in firing-rate terms) is fixed, yet the effective decision bound (in evidence terms) collapses.

> Result: (Drugowitsch et al., 2012) The inferred cost of sampling rises over elapsed decision time, producing an optimal bound that collapses; the model predicts a difficulty-independent urgency component in neural activity, matching the coherence-independent drift observed in parietal recordings [6][7].

The collapsing-bound view also clarifies a long-standing empirical debate. Under fixed bounds, time-dependent accuracy should be flat once conditioned on decision time; under collapsing bounds, accuracy *declines* for long RTs, because late decisions are made on weaker accumulated evidence. Both patterns occur in data, and the cost-of-sampling framework explains when each should dominate: costs of deliberation determine the collapse rate [7].

### 4.3 Neural correlates: LIP, FEF, and the decision variable

The search for a neural decision variable converged on the **lateral intraparietal area (LIP)**.

The **frontal eye field (FEF)** and superior colliculus sit downstream, closer to the motor output: movement-related neurons there exhibit a fixed firing-rate threshold at saccade initiation, consistent with the view that the commitment event — the bound crossing — is read out by oculomotor structures while the graded deliberation lives in parietal and prefrontal circuits [5].

A crucial architectural question is whether the accumulator is one-dimensional (relative evidence, as in the DDM) or two racing accumulators (absolute evidence per alternative).

### 4.4 The threshold in the basal ganglia

If cortex accumulates, who sets the threshold?

> Result: (Lo and Wang, 2006) A biophysically detailed cortico-basal ganglia model reproduces the full pattern of choice and RT data, including the speed-accuracy tradeoff, with the decision threshold set by the dynamics of the basal ganglia output nuclei rather than by any single cortical firing-rate level [8].

This circuit view makes distinctive predictions confirmed in later work: manipulations that bias striatal pathways (dopamine agonists, subthalamic stimulation) shift decision thresholds without altering the rate of evidence accumulation, and human imaging shows striatal and pre-supplementary motor activity scaling with time pressure — the neural signature of a lowered bound under urgency [9].

### 4.5 Bounded rationality and resource-rational analysis

A persistent embarrassment for optimality accounts is that humans deviate from the optimal policies the models prescribe: they undershoot optimal boundaries, overweight recent evidence, and satisfice rather than maximize.

Under this lens, several "biases" in perceptual choice become signatures of constrained optimality.

The methodological moral is that optimality claims must name their constraints.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

**Result 1 — SPRT optimality.** Wald and Wolfowitz proved the SPRT minimizes expected sample size among tests achieving given error rates; in the Gaussian continuum limit this is the DDM with symmetric fixed bounds [2]. Bogacz et al. showed that the dominant accumulator models of TAFC reduce to this SPRT-DDM, and derived the reward-rate-optimal boundary setting, confirming that speed-accuracy instructions move the boundary in the predicted direction in both humans and monkeys [2].

**Result 2 — LIP as accumulator.** Shadlen and Newsome demonstrated that LIP firing rates during motion discrimination ramp with coherence-proportional slopes, predict choice, and converge at commitment — the neural signature of a decision variable [4]. Roitman and Shadlen's reaction-time task showed single-trial activity consistent with a diffusion process terminating at threshold [5].

**Result 3 — Race plus urgency in multi-alternative choice.** With four alternatives, LIP responses favor independent accumulators per alternative with a common, coherence-independent urgency signal over a single relative-evidence diffusion — while remaining behaviorally equivalent to a collapsing-bound DDM [6].

**Result 4 — Collapsing bounds from sampling costs.** Dynamic-programming fits to monkey and human RT distributions infer a cost of sampling that is initially flat and then rises; the implied optimal bound collapses, and the theory predicts the difficulty-independent urgency component observed in parietal activity [7].

**Result 5 — Basal ganglia threshold.** The Lo–Wang cortico-basal ganglia model generates the full choice-RT pattern with the threshold implemented in basal ganglia output dynamics; dopaminergic and pathway-specific manipulations shift the bound without changing accumulation rate, a dissociation later confirmed across species [8][9].

**Result 6 — Cross-species convergence.** Hanks and Summerfield's review shows that the accumulation-to-bound framework, with urgency and threshold-setting circuits, describes perceptual decisions in rodents, monkeys, and humans with the same computational skeleton, despite wide differences in neural implementation [9].

##6 Limitations

1. **The Gaussian, stationary evidence assumption.** The SPRT-DDM equivalence requires independent, identically distributed Gaussian samples with known drift. Real sensory evidence is nonstationary — attention waxes and wanes, stimuli flicker — and the optimal policy under nonstationarity is not a simple bound, collapsing or otherwise. The brain's actual stopping rule under realistic input statistics is unknown.
2. **Identifiability of collapsing bounds.** Fixed-bound models with across-trial variability in drift and starting point can mimic many signatures attributed to collapsing bounds, and vice versa. Model comparison hinges on fine structure of conditional accuracy functions and is sensitive to assumptions about the cost function's form [7].
3. **Where exactly is the bound?** The accumulator-versus-threshold division of labor between cortex and basal ganglia is a useful cartoon, but the bound may be distributed: recurrent cortical dynamics, striatal gating, and collicular burst thresholds could each contribute a stage of the commitment cascade. Causal, cell-type-specific tests of the Lo–Wang mechanism remain sparse [8].
4. **Urgency versus evidence-independent drive.** The coherence-independent drift in LIP could reflect a genuine optimal urgency signal, but it could also reflect arousal, motor preparation, or reward anticipation that merely correlates with elapsed time. Disentangling normative urgency from incidental time-on-task signals requires manipulations that move the two apart [6][7].
5. **Resource-rationality's flexibility.** Any behavior can be rendered "optimal" by positing sufficiently exotic constraints, which threatens to make resource-rational analysis unfalsifiable. The program earns its keep only when inferred costs are measured independently — neurally or across tasks — rather than fitted post hoc [10].
6. **Beyond two alternatives.** Much of the optimality theory is developed for binary choice; real decisions involve many alternatives, sequential dependencies, and hierarchical structure. The multi-alternative race-plus-urgency picture is a start, not a general theory [6].

##7 Conclusion

The decision-neuroscience program has achieved something rare: a continuous chain from a normative theorem to a circuit mechanism. Wald's SPRT proves what the optimal sequential decision looks like; the drift-diffusion model shows that optimal rule has a simple neural-computable form; single-unit physiology finds accumulation-to-bound dynamics in parietal and frontal cortex with the bound implemented through cortico-basal ganglia loops; and the deviations from fixed-bound optimality — collapsing bounds, urgency signals — turn out to be optimal themselves once the cost of sampling is admitted into the objective. Resource-rational analysis generalizes the moral: apparent irrationality is usually unmodeled cost, and the scientific task is to infer the brain's true constraints rather than to grade it against an unconstrained ideal.

The open frontiers are empirical. We need causal tests of the threshold circuit that go beyond correlation, independent neural measures of the brain's internal cost of deliberation, and a multi-alternative, nonstationary generalization of optimal stopping that matches the richness of real choice. If the last four decades are any guide, the mathematics of optimal stopping will continue to be the sharpest lens we have for reading the neural dynamics of deliberation — provided we remember that the brain's objective function includes costs the experimenter never scheduled.
