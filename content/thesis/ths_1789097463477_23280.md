# Bootstrapping in Fully Homomorphic Encryption: CKKS Approximate Arithmetic, TFHE Programmable Bootstrapping, and the Economics of Noise

## Abstract

Bootstrapping—the homomorphic decryption of a ciphertext under an encrypted key that refreshes noise without exposing plaintext—remains the central operation of fully homomorphic encryption (FHE). This thesis develops a unified treatment of bootstrapping across the two dominant FHE paradigms: the Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song (CKKS) scheme for *approximate* arithmetic over complex vectors, and the torus-based TFHE family for *exact* boolean and functional computation. We review the LWE/RLWE foundations that both families share, derive the noise dynamics that make modulus switching, rescaling, and key switching mandatory, and dissect the two canonical bootstrap circuits: CKKS bootstrapping (modulus raising, homomorphic DFT, polynomial modular reduction) and TFHE programmable bootstrapping (blind rotation over a test polynomial, sample extraction, key switching). We formalize 128-bit security parameter selection via the lattice-estimator framework, present representative latency benchmarks on commodity hardware, and quantify the NTT/RNS optimizations that dominate practical cost. Finally, we assess limitations—CKKS's approximate-noise security tension and TFHE's amortization gap—and argue that bootstrapping is best understood not as an occasional refresh but as the priced currency of encrypted computation.

## 1. Introduction

Gentry's 2009 construction showed that a *somewhat* homomorphic encryption scheme, if it can homomorphically evaluate its own decryption circuit, becomes *fully* homomorphic by periodically refreshing noise [1]. The refresh—bootstrapping—squashes accumulated noise back to a fresh-encryption level, removing the depth bound on encrypted circuits. Seventeen years later, bootstrapping is no longer a theoretical existence proof: it is a heavily engineered subroutine with millisecond-to-second latency, and its cost profile determines which applications FHE can serve.

The landscape is dominated by two families with complementary philosophies. **CKKS** treats noise as part of the message and targets *approximate* arithmetic: vectors of up to \(N/2\) complex numbers packed into a single ciphertext are added, multiplied, and rotated in SIMD fashion, with each multiplication followed by *rescaling* that rounds off low-order bits [2]. Its bootstrapping is a heavy homomorphic evaluation of the decryption function (tens of levels, thousands of NTTs), currently costing seconds per ciphertext but amortized over thousands of slots. **TFHE**, built on (T)RLWE over the torus, refreshes *every* gate: blind rotation evaluates a programmable function encoded in a test polynomial, sample extraction recovers a single LWE sample, and key switching restores parameters—achieving per-gate bootstrapping in roughly 10 ms at 128-bit security [5]. This speed comes at the price of scalar rather than vector computation.

This thesis unifies these perspectives. The central claim is simple: noise is the scarce resource of encrypted computation, and bootstrapping—whether CKKS's periodic, amortized refresh or TFHE's per-gate rhythm—is the market mechanism that reprices it. Understanding the bootstrap circuit is understanding FHE performance.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 LWE and RLWE

All schemes considered here rest on the Learning With Errors (LWE) problem and its ring variant (RLWE). Given a secret \(s \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n\), samples \((a, b = \langle a, s\rangle + e \bmod q)\) with \(a\) uniform and \(e\) drawn from a narrow Gaussian \(\chi\) are computationally indistinguishable from uniform pairs. RLWE replaces \(\mathbb{Z}_q^n\) with the polynomial ring \(R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^N + 1)\), where \(N\) is a power of two. Best-known attacks are lattice attacks (primal and dual hybrid attacks) solved via BKZ lattice reduction; concrete security is estimated with the lattice estimator, which converts an instance \((N, \log q, \sigma)\) into a security level in bits given a BKZ block size \(\beta\) and a core-SVP cost model (e.g., \(2^{0.292\beta}\) classically) [2].

A fresh RLWE ciphertext is \((a, b = a s + m + e)\); decryption computes \(b - as = m + e \bmod q\) and rounds off \(e\). Noise growth is the entire story of leveled FHE:

> **Theorem (noise budget):** Let \(\|e\|_\infty \le B\). A homomorphic addition yields noise at most \(2B\); a homomorphic multiplication yields noise proportional to \(B^2 \cdot N\) (naively) or \(B \cdot N\) after relinearization. The computation fails once \(\|e\|_\infty \ge q/2t\), where \(t\) is the plaintext modulus or, in CKKS, the scale boundary.

### 2.2 Modulus switching, rescaling, and key switching

Three generic operations manage the modulus/noise economy:

- **Modulus switching** maps a ciphertext from modulus \(q\) to \(q' < q\), scaling noise by \(q'/q\) (plus rounding noise). It is the BGV/BFV depth engine: each level sheds one modulus prime.
- **Rescaling** is CKKS's analog: after multiplying scaled messages \(\Delta m_1 \cdot \Delta m_2\) (scale \(\Delta^2\)), the ciphertext is divided by \(\Delta\), restoring scale \(\Delta\) at the cost of discarding one modulus prime [2].
- **Key switching** converts a ciphertext under key \(s_1\) to one under \(s_2\) given an encrypted key \(s_2^{-1}(s_1)\); used after relinearization (degree-2 → degree-1) and after Galois automorphisms (rotations). Noise is controlled by decomposing \(a\) into \(\ell = \lceil\log_{B_{ks}} q\rceil\) digits and multiplying by gadget-encrypted key components—key-switching noise scales with the gadget base \(B_{ks}\).

A subtlety: CKKS ciphertexts in modern libraries live in the **RNS** (residue number system) representation, where \(q = \prod_i q_i\) with each \(q_i\) a machine-word prime; all arithmetic is per-limb, and modulus switching is just dropping a limb [7].

### 2.3 The NTT bottleneck

Polynomial multiplication in \(R_q\) is implemented via the Number Theoretic Transform, giving \(O(N \log N)\) complexity per limb rather than \(O(N^2)\). A single CKKS bootstrap performs *thousands* of NTTs/INTTs (each homomorphic DFT is an \(N \times N\) linear transform evaluated via baby-step giant-step over automorphisms), and blind rotation in TFHE is \(n\) sequential external products, each involving NTTs of TRGSW components. The NTT is therefore the atomic cost of bootstrapping; vectorization (RVV, AVX-512), GPU offload, and hardware accelerators report 10–1000× speedups concentrated in this kernel [7][8].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis follows the analytic-benchmark method of the FHE performance literature:

1. **Analytic noise model.** We adopt the componentwise noise analysis of [2], which gives estimates within 0.01 bits of experimental noise measurements, to track precision loss through each bootstrap stage.
2. **Parameter selection.** We fix 128-bit classical security per the HomomorphicEncryption.org standard and the lattice estimator: for RLWE, \(N = 2^{16}\) with \(\log q \le 881\) (or \(N = 2^{15}\), \(\log q \le 438\)); for TFHE, dimension \(n \approx 630\)–1024 with torus parameters tuned to the same block-size budget [5].
3. **Latency model.** We report representative single-threaded CPU latencies from the open literature (OpenFHE/Lattigo for CKKS, TFHE-rs/concrete for TFHE) and amortization: CKKS throughput is divided by slot count (\(N/2\)), TFHE throughput by the number of gates processed [4][6].
4. **Comparative accounting.** We compare CKKS amortized bootstrap, TFHE per-gate bootstrap, and the BFV-via-CKKS hybrid of [9] on three axes: latency per plaintext unit, precision delivered, and key-size overhead.

We do not claim novel asymptotic results; the contribution is a consolidated, numerically grounded comparison that a practitioner can use for parameter selection.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 CKKS approximate arithmetic and rescaling

CKKS encodes a vector \(z \in \mathbb{C}^{N/2}\) into a plaintext polynomial \(m = \lfloor \Delta \cdot \tau^{-1}(z) \rceil \in R\), where \(\tau\) is the canonical embedding and \(\Delta = 2^p\) is the scaling factor (typically \(p \in [30, 60]\)) [2]. Encryption yields \(\text{ct} = (a, as + m + e)\) with \(\|e\|_\infty \ll \Delta\), so decryption returns \(m + e \approx \Delta \cdot z\)—the noise is *part of the message*, like round-off in floating point.

Multiplication is where the scheme earns its keep. Given \(\text{ct}_i\) with plaintext \(m_i = \Delta z_i + e_i\):

\[
\text{ct}_{\text{mult}} = \text{ct}_1 \otimes \text{ct}_2 \;\Rightarrow\; m = \Delta^2 z_1 z_2 + O(\Delta \cdot \|e\|)
\]

Rescaling by \(\Delta\) gives \(\text{ct}_{\text{rs}} = \lfloor \Delta^{-1} \text{ct}_{\text{mult}} \rceil \bmod q/\Delta\), with plaintext \(\Delta z_1 z_2 + e_{\text{rs}}\) and fresh rounding noise \(\|e_{\text{rs}}\| \approx \|e\| + \tau \sqrt{N}/2\). Each rescale consumes one RNS prime from the modulus chain \(q = q_0 q_1 \cdots q_L\). When the chain is exhausted (\(l = 0\)), no further multiplications are possible—unless bootstrapping restores a large modulus.

```python
# Leveled CKKS noise budget (RNS chain, one prime per level)
def rescale_budget(levels, logDelta=40, logq0=60):
    """Remaining noise-free bits after `levels` multiplications."""
    # each rescale adds ~sqrt(N)/2 rounding + consumes one prime
    noise_bits = 0.5 * (levels + 1)  # heuristic growth, cf. [2]
    return logq0 - noise_bits - 1    # decryptable margin
```

### 4.2 CKKS bootstrapping: modulus raising and EvalMod

Cheon et al.'s bootstrap (2018) treats the CKKS decryption function itself as the circuit to evaluate [1]. Given \(\text{ct}\) at level 0 with modulus \(q_0\), the server holds an *encrypted* secret key and proceeds:

1. **ModRaise.** Lift \(\text{ct}\) from modulus \(q_0\) to a large modulus \(Q \gg q_0\) (trivially, by embedding coefficients). Now \(\langle \text{ct}, sk \rangle = m + e + q_0 \cdot I\), where \(I \in R\) is the unknown "wrap-around" polynomial—the decryption must reduce modulo \(q_0\).
2. **CoeffsToSlots.** Apply the homomorphic discrete Fourier transform so that slots carry the *coefficients* of \(m + e + q_0 I\). This is the most expensive step: a linear transform of dimension \(N\) evaluated via BSGS with \(O(\sqrt{N})\) rotations and key-switching operations [3].
3. **EvalMod.** Homomorphically evaluate the modular reduction \(t \mapsto [t]_{q_0}\) slotwise by approximating \(t - q_0 \lfloor t/q_0 \rceil\) with a scaled sine: \([\langle ct, sk \rangle]_{q_0} \approx \tfrac{q_0}{2\pi}\sin(\tfrac{2\pi}{q_0}(\cdot))\). The sine is approximated by a high-degree polynomial (Chebyshev/Remez or multi-interval Remez) composed with repeated squaring [3][4].
4. **SlotsToCoeffs.** Inverse homomorphic DFT, restoring coefficient packing. Output: a ciphertext at high level with noise \(\approx 2^{-r}\) relative precision, where \(r\) is the bootstrap precision (typically 8–30 bits).

Precision is the crux: each bootstrap introduces an error of about \(2^{-r}\); applications requiring exactness need \(r \ge 30\) (noise-flooding for IND-CPA-D security also demands ~30 bits) [1]. Recent work achieves high-precision EvalMod with odd sine series and iterative squaring, cutting bootstrap error by 25% versus the Eurocrypt'22 baseline [3]; roots-of-unity variants reduce CoeffsToSlots/EvalMod latency for the same precision [4].

### 4.3 TFHE programmable bootstrapping and blind rotation

TFHE takes the opposite approach: make bootstrapping so cheap it can run after *every* gate. A TLWE ciphertext is \((a, b = \langle a, s \rangle + \mu + e)\) with message \(\mu \in \mathbb{T}\) (the torus), secret \(s \in \{0,1\}^n\). The bootstrap evaluates *any* function \(f\) with one blind rotation [5]:

1. **BlindRotate.** Start from a noiseless test polynomial \(v(X) = \sum_j f(j/2N') X^j\) encoding the desired function as a lookup table. Given the phase \(\phi = b - \langle a, s \rangle \approx \mu\), compute \(ACC = v \cdot X^{-\tilde{\phi}} \cdot \prod_i (X^{a_i s_i})\) via \(n\) sequential *external products* \(\text{TRGSW}(s_i) \boxdot ACC\), each rotating the accumulator by the encrypted secret bit. After the loop, the constant term of \(ACC\) encodes \(f(\mu)\) [5][6].
2. **SampleExtract.** Pull one coefficient of the resulting TRLWE ciphertext to obtain a fresh TLWE encryption of \(f(\mu)\)—this step is essentially free (a copy).
3. **KeySwitch.** Switch from the RLWE key back to the LWE key with a key-switching key, yielding a ciphertext at the original parameter set with fresh, small noise.

"Programmable" means the test polynomial can encode arbitrary negacyclic functions: ReLU, sign, S-boxes, or integer arithmetic via digit decomposition. Integer-wise functional bootstrapping generalizes the test-vector coefficients so that a single PBS evaluates any one-variable function over integers, enabling secure integer arithmetic without booleanification [6].

Complexity: blind rotation is \(O(n)\) external products, each \(O(N \log N)\); with \(n \approx 630\), \(N = 1024\), a gate bootstrap is ~10 ms single-threaded at 128-bit security [5][6]. Memory: bootstrapping keys are tens of MB (TRGSW encryptions of each secret bit), with recent FHEW-like variants reaching <3 MB and 3 ms [1].

### 4.4 Noise management: the full economy

Bootstrapping refreshes noise but never for free; the surrounding operations must keep noise bounded between refreshes:

| Operation | Noise effect | Controls |
|---|---|---|
| HAdd | additive, \(\|e_1\|+\|e_2\|\) | none needed |
| HMult (CKKS) | scale \(\Delta^2\), noise \(\approx \Delta\|e\|\) | rescale by \(\Delta\) |
| Relinearization | \(+O(\ell \cdot B_{ks})\) | gadget base, digit count |
| Rotation (Galois) | key-switch noise \(+O(\ell_{rot} B)\) | hoisting, baby-step giant-step |
| Modulus switch | scales noise by \(q'/q\) | rounding (CKKS) vs exact (BGV/BFV) |
| Bootstrap (CKKS) | resets to \(2^{-r}\) precision | EvalMod degree, DFT precision |
| Bootstrap (TFHE) | resets to fresh-LWE level | external-product decomposition base |

*Table 1. Noise economy of the core FHE operations. All bounds are asymptotic; concrete values come from [2].*

Key switching deserves emphasis because it is the hidden tax on every non-trivial operation. A naive key switch from \(s_1\) to \(s_2\) needs \(n\) RLWE ciphertexts; the gadget (RGSW/decomposition) variant trades digit count \(\ell\) against noise: noise grows as \(\ell \cdot B_{ks} \cdot \|a\|\), while key size grows as \(\ell\). Hybrid key switching (used in OpenFHE) splits the modulus to balance the two [7].

### 4.5 NTT optimizations and RNS decomposition

Because every operation above is ultimately polynomial arithmetic, the NTT dominates wall-clock time. Optimizations:

- **RNS decomposition.** Writing \(q = \prod_i q_i\) with 60-bit primes lets all arithmetic use 64-bit integer units; modulus switching drops a prime, and the inverse-CRT lifts only when needed [7].
- **Lazy reduction / Harvey butterflies.** Skipping modular reductions inside the NTT butterfly when intermediate values fit in 128 bits saves ~20% of multiply instructions.
- **Vectorization and hardware.** RVV vectorization of the NTT/INTT yields up to 27× speedup on the kernel and ~1.94× on end-to-end CKKS-with-bootstrapping in OpenFHE [7]; GPU/FPGA accelerators report 10–1000× on the NTT and whole-bootstrapping speedups of 5–47× on homomorphic multiply/relinearize pipelines [8].
- **Hoisted rotations.** In CKKS, many rotations share the same input; hoisting performs one NTT of the input and reuses it across all rotation keys, cutting CoeffsToSlots cost by ~30%.

The practical takeaway: a CKKS bootstrap's latency is roughly *number of NTTs × NTT latency per limb*, and algorithmic progress (roots-of-unity EvalMod, digit-extraction variants) competes with engineering progress (kernels, accelerators) for the same budget.

Two theorems worth stating explicitly:

> **Theorem (CKKS bootstrap precision):** Under the componentwise noise model of [2], the CKKS bootstrap with EvalMod polynomial of degree \(d\) and \(k\) squarings returns a ciphertext whose decryption error is bounded by \(2^{-r} + O(\epsilon_{\text{DFT}})\), where \(r\) grows with \(d \cdot 2^k\) and \(\epsilon_{\text{DFT}}\) is the homomorphic-DFT rounding error. Theoretical estimates match experiment within 0.01 bits.

> **Theorem (TFHE bootstrap correctness):** If the input LWE noise satisfies \(\|e\| < 1/(4N')\) (half the torus distance between adjacent test-polynomial slots), blind rotation followed by SampleExtract and KeySwitch yields an encryption of \(f(\mu)\) with noise independent of the input noise [5][6].

### 5.3 Reading the tables

The tables expose the fundamental trade: CKKS amortizes a *seconds-long* bootstrap over *tens of thousands* of slots (effective cost per plaintext: sub-millisecond), while TFHE pays *milliseconds* per gate but computes *exactly* with programmable functions. Neither dominates: SIMD approximate workloads (neural-network inference, statistics) belong to CKKS; boolean circuits and exact integer logic belong to TFHE. Hybrids—BFV bootstrapping implemented *through* CKKS bootstrapping [9]—show that the CKKS bootstrap is becoming a general-purpose refresh primitive beyond approximate arithmetic.

## 6. Limitations

1. **CKKS approximate-noise security tension.** CKKS decryption noise leaks information about the secret key; Li–Micciancio showed CKKS is not IND-CPA-D secure without noise flooding (~30 bits), which forces high bootstrap precision and larger parameters [1]. Exact-CKKS variants [2] recover exactness but sacrifice the approximate-arithmetic performance advantage.
2. **TFHE amortization gap.** A 10 ms gate bootstrap is fast for a gate and slow for a circuit: evaluating a 32-bit multiplier costs hundreds of PBS (~seconds), while CKKS evaluates thousands of such multiplications in one bootstrap's amortization window. SIMD-izing TFHE (multi-bit PBS, circuit bootstrapping) narrows but does not close this gap [6].
3. **Key-size bloat.** CKKS bootstrap keys (rotation keys for the DFT, EvalMod relinearization keys) run to hundreds of MB; TFHE bootstrapping keys are tens of MB per gate type. Key distribution and memory bandwidth, not just FLOPs, bound throughput.
4. **NTT-centric cost.** All progress funnels through polynomial multiplication; memory-bound NTTs on large \(N\) resist the embarrassingly parallel scaling that GPUs want, and blind rotation's *sequential* external products are inherently hard to parallelize [1][8].
5. **Benchmark incomparability.** Published latencies use different parameter sets, security models (classical vs quantum estimators), and precision targets; cross-paper comparisons (like Table 3) are indicative, not rigorous.

## 7. Conclusion

Bootstrapping is the operation that converts somewhat-homomorphic encryption into fully homomorphic encryption, and its engineering determines what FHE is *for*. This thesis traced the two mature realizations: CKKS bootstrapping, which evaluates decryption homomorphically through modulus raising, homomorphic DFTs, and polynomial modular reduction to refresh tens of thousands of approximate slots at once; and TFHE programmable bootstrapping, which uses blind rotation over a test polynomial to evaluate arbitrary functions gate-by-gate in milliseconds. Both rest on the same LWE/RLWE hardness, the same noise economy (modulus switching, rescaling, key switching), and the same computational kernel (the NTT over RNS limbs). Their differences—approximate versus exact, amortized versus per-gate, seconds versus milliseconds—are not accidents but consequences of where each scheme spends its noise budget.

The practical frontier is now parameterization: choosing \((N, \log q, \Delta)\) for CKKS or \((n, N, \sigma)\) for TFHE to hit 128-bit security while meeting a latency target, with the noise model of [2] and the lattice estimator as the standard tools. Open directions include exact-CKKS with IND-CPA-D security, low-latency EvalMod via roots of unity [4], compact-key FHEW variants, and hardware co-design of the NTT [7][8]. The long arc is clear: bootstrapping is getting cheaper by roughly an order of magnitude every few years, and each order of magnitude unlocks a new class of applications—from encrypted statistics today to encrypted general computation tomorrow.
