# The Proof Complexity Landscape: Resolution Lower Bounds for the Pigeonhole Principle, Cutting-Planes Strength, and the Hierarchy of Frege Systems

## Abstract

Propositional proof complexity measures the minimal length of proofs of tautologies across proof systems of varying strength — a question tied to the separation of **NP** and **coNP** since Cook and Reckhow [1]. This thesis surveys the central landscape: *resolution* lower bounds for the pigeonhole principle initiated by Haken [2] and modernized through the size–width tradeoff; exponential lower bounds for *constant-depth Frege* by Pitassi, Beame and Impagliazzo [3], nearly matching Buss's polynomial upper bound; the *cutting-planes* system of Chvátal–Gomory cuts with Pudlák's exponential lower bounds via monotone interpolation [5]; and the hierarchy of Frege systems culminating in Extended Frege, for which no nontrivial lower bound is known. We develop the pigeonhole encoding, reconstruct the bottleneck-counting argument in modern form, explain interpolation as a bridge from circuit lower bounds to proof lower bounds, and quantify Chvátal-rank constraints on cutting-planes proofs [7]. The exposition closes with the automatizability barrier and conditional lower bounds for Frege [6].

## 1 Introduction

The question at the heart of propositional proof complexity is deceptively simple: *given a tautology, how long must its shortest proof be?* The answer depends on the proof system, and the variation across systems reveals a stratified hierarchy of reasoning power. Cook and Reckhow formalized this inquiry in 1979 [1], defining a *propositional proof system* as a polynomial-time computable function $P$ whose range is exactly the set of tautologies, and proving the landmark equivalence: a polynomially bounded proof system exists *if and only if* **NP** = **coNP**. Proof complexity is thus a non-uniform, combinatorial mirror of the deepest open problem in computer science [6].

The field is organized around a small number of hard tautologies and a growing arsenal of lower-bound techniques. Foremost is the *pigeonhole principle* $\mathrm{PHP}^{n+1}_n$: no injective map exists from $n+1$ pigeons to $n$ holes. Haken's 1985 result [2] — every resolution refutation of the pigeonhole principle has exponential size — was the first superpolynomial lower bound for a natural proof system. Because resolution underlies modern conflict-driven clause-learning (CDCL) SAT solvers, Haken's theorem has concrete algorithmic content: any solver whose search trace corresponds to resolution needs exponential time in the worst case.

Yet resolution is weak, operating only on clauses — the analogue of depth-one formulas. Ascending the hierarchy, proofs gain power: *cutting planes* manipulates linear inequalities over $\{0,1\}^n$ and derives new ones through Chvátal–Gomory rounding, capturing integer-programming reasoning that resolution cannot efficiently simulate [5]. *Frege systems* — textbook Hilbert-style systems of finitely many axiom schemas and rules — allow arbitrary formulas, and all Frege systems are polynomially equivalent. *Extended Frege* (EF) adds an extension rule abbreviating complex formulas by fresh variables, connecting proof complexity to Boolean circuit complexity.

The central drama is the gap between weak and strong systems:

- **Resolution:** exponential lower bounds are classical [2], modernized via the size–width tradeoff.
- **Constant-depth Frege:** exponential lower bounds for the pigeonhole principle [3], via random restrictions.
- **Cutting planes:** exponential lower bounds via monotone feasible interpolation [5].
- **Frege and Extended Frege:** *no* nontrivial lower bound is known — the field's deepest open problem.

This thesis presents these results as a coherent narrative: the encoding that makes the pigeonhole principle hard, the combinatorial core of Haken's argument, the width-based modernization, the interpolation bridge from circuits to proofs, the geometry of cutting-planes derivations, and the simulation hierarchy from resolution through cutting planes to Frege and Extended Frege.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Proof systems and simulations

A *propositional proof system* is a polynomial-time function $P: \{0,1\}^* \to \mathrm{TAUT}$ onto the tautologies [1]. System $P$ *simulates* $Q$ if every $Q$-proof of length $n$ converts to a $P$-proof of length at most $p(n)$ for some polynomial $p$; *p-simulation* requires the conversion itself to be polynomial-time.

> Theorem: *(Cook–Reckhow [1])*. A polynomially bounded propositional proof system exists if and only if **NP** = **coNP**.

For *refutation* systems one works with unsatisfiable CNFs and derives contradiction — dual in power to proof systems for tautologies.

### 2.2 Resolution

A *literal* is a variable or its negation; a *clause* is a disjunction of literals. The single rule is *resolution*: from $C \lor x$ and $D \lor \bar{x}$ derive $C \lor D$. A *resolution refutation* of a CNF $F$ is a sequence of clauses ending in the empty clause $\bot$, each an axiom or a resolvent of two earlier clauses [2]. Viewed as a DAG, a tree-shaped refutation is *tree-like*.

The SAT-solving connection is tight: DPLL backtracking traces correspond to tree-like resolution refutations, and CDCL clause learning corresponds to general (DAG-like) resolution. A size lower bound for resolution refutations of a formula family is therefore a worst-case lower bound for *every* CDCL solver on those instances.

Two measures dominate: *size* (number of clauses) and *width* (maximum literals per clause). The Ben-Sasson–Wigderson *size–width tradeoff* states that a refutation of size $S$ of an $n$-variable CNF of initial width $w_0$ can be converted to one of width $O(\sqrt{n \log S} + w_0)$. Contrapositively, an $\Omega(n)$ width lower bound forces $S = 2^{\Omega(n)}$.

### 2.3 The pigeonhole principle

$\mathrm{PHP}^{n+1}_n$ uses variables $p_{i,j}$ ($i \in [n+1]$ pigeons, $j \in [n]$ holes), with clauses:

1. **Pigeon clauses:** $\bigvee_{j=1}^{n} p_{i,j}$ for each $i$ (every pigeon is placed).
2. **Hole clauses:** $\lnot p_{i,j} \lor \lnot p_{i',j}$ for each $j$ and $i < i'$ (no hole holds two pigeons).

The formula has $\Theta(n^3)$ clauses and is unsatisfiable. The *weak* pigeonhole principle, with many more pigeons than holes, is studied in [4]. PHP is a *counting* tautology: any proof must certify a cardinality argument, and systems that cannot count efficiently are doomed to long proofs — the insight Haken exploited [2].

### 2.4 Frege systems and Extended Frege

A *Frege system* has finitely many axiom schemas and sound rules such as *modus ponens*; all Frege systems p-simulate each other [1], so "Frege" is one p-equivalence class. *Bounded-depth Frege* restricts lines to constant $\land$/$\lor$ alternation depth; resolution is essentially depth-one Frege. *Extended Frege* adds the *extension rule*: introduce a fresh variable $q$ abbreviating a formula $\varphi$ via $q \leftrightarrow \varphi$. This makes EF proofs correspond to Boolean *circuits*. *Extended Resolution* (ER) is p-equivalent to EF, and Haken observed that ER refutes the pigeonhole principle in polynomial size [2]: extension variables can name the counting predicates pure resolution cannot express.

### 2.5 Cutting planes

*Cutting planes* (CP) reasons about 0–1 inequalities with two rules:

1. **Nonnegative linear combination** of derived inequalities.
2. **Chvátal–Gomory rounding:** from $\sum a_i x_i \ge b$ with integer $a_i$, derive $\sum a_i x_i \ge \lceil b \rceil$ [8].

A refutation derives $0 \ge 1$. Geometrically, each cut slices fractional points off the LP relaxation while sparing all 0–1 points, tightening the polytope toward the integer hull. The *Chvátal rank* — rounds of rounding needed — controls proof length: rank $k$ in $n$ variables yields proofs of length at most $(n^{k+1}-1)/(n-1)$ [7], and every $[0,1]^n$ polytope has rank $O(n^2 \log n)$.

CP strictly dominates resolution, yet Pudlák proved exponential lower bounds for CP itself [5]. The hierarchy is summarized below.

| System | Objects | PHP complexity |
|---|---|---|
| Tree-like resolution | clauses | $2^{\Omega(n)}$ [2] |
| DAG resolution | clauses | $2^{\Omega(n)}$ [2] |
| Cutting planes | 0–1 inequalities | $2^{n^{\Omega(1)}}$ [5] |
| Constant-depth Frege | bounded-depth formulas | $2^{n^{\Omega(1)}}$ [3] |
| Frege | formulas | polynomial (Buss) |
| Extended Frege | circuits (extension) | polynomial |

## 3 Methodology

Four interlocking methodologies power the results of this thesis.

**Bottleneck counting and critical assignments.** Haken's method [2] associates to each clause $C$ a measure $\mathrm{cri}(C)$ counting the pigeons $C$ "mentions." Axioms have small $\mathrm{cri}$, the empty clause has large $\mathrm{cri}$, and $\mathrm{cri}$ grows at most additively under resolution — forcing a *bottleneck* clause of medium $\mathrm{cri}$. Each bottleneck clause is falsified by only a $2^{-\Omega(n)}$ fraction of the exponentially many *critical assignments* (injective placements of $n$ pigeons into $n$ holes, each satisfying all axioms), so exponentially many distinct bottleneck clauses are required. The counting uses bipartite-matching structure to bound how many critical assignments one clause can cover.

**Width lower bounds and the Prover–Delayer game.** The modern reformulation replaces bottleneck counting by *width*. In the Prover–Delayer game, the Prover queries variables and the Delayer answers, scoring points when forced into contradiction; the Delayer guarantees score $w$ iff every refutation has width at least $w$. For PHP, the Delayer maintains a partial pigeon–hole matching and scores $\Omega(n)$ points. The size–width tradeoff then yields exponential size bounds with far less combinatorial pain than Haken's original argument.

**Random restrictions.** Assigning each variable to $0$, $1$, or "unassigned" with tuned probabilities *simplifies* every formula in a purported short constant-depth Frege proof (via the switching lemma) while *preserving* pigeonhole structure on a smaller instance. Iterating collapses a bounded-depth proof to a resolution-like object on smaller PHP, contradicting the resolution lower bound. This drives Ajtai's superpolynomial bound and its exponential strengthening by Pitassi, Beame and Impagliazzo [3].

**Feasible and monotone interpolation.** From a short proof of $\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{y}) \to \psi(\bar{x},\bar{z})$ with disjoint $\bar{y}, \bar{z}$, *feasible interpolation* extracts a small circuit computing an *interpolant* separating the satisfiable cases of $\varphi$ from those of $\psi$. When the interpolant can be taken *monotone*, monotone circuit lower bounds (Razborov's clique bound, Alon–Boppana) transfer to proof-size lower bounds. Resolution and cutting planes admit monotone feasible interpolation — Pudlák's route to exponential CP lower bounds [5]. For Frege only *conditional* interpolation theorems exist, under cryptographic assumptions [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The anatomy of Haken's lower bound

Fix a resolution refutation $\Pi$ of $\mathrm{PHP}^{n+1}_n$ and define $\mathrm{cri}(C)$ as the minimum, over pigeons $i$, of the number of holes $j$ with $p_{i,j}$ or $\lnot p_{i,j}$ in $C$.

> Theorem: *(Haken [2])*. Every resolution refutation of $\mathrm{PHP}^{n+1}_n$ contains at least $2^{\Omega(n)}$ distinct clauses.

Subadditivity — $\mathrm{cri}$ of a resolvent is at most the sum of its parents' values — guarantees a clause $C^*$ with $n/3 \le \mathrm{cri}(C^*) \le 2n/3$: ascend from the axioms until $\mathrm{cri}$ first reaches $n/3$. Haken's counting lemma shows one bottleneck clause is falsified by at most a $2^{-\Omega(n)}$ fraction of all critical assignments, while the critical assignments number $n!$. Since every critical assignment satisfies all axioms, each must falsify *some* clause of $\Pi$ — and the pigeonhole principle, applied to the proof itself, forces exponentially many distinct bottleneck clauses. The width-based reformulation below captures the same phenomenon more transparently.

### 4.2 From width to size

*Width* is the maximum number of literals in any clause. Narrow clauses are weak: a clause of width $w \ll n$ constrains few pigeons, and a random partial matching satisfies it with high probability. The Prover–Delayer game makes this precise:

> Theorem: *(Width lower bound)*. Every resolution refutation of $\mathrm{PHP}^{n+1}_n$ has width at least $n/3$.

The size–width tradeoff converts width into size: width $\Omega(n)$ forces $S = 2^{\Omega(n)}$ clauses. By the CDCL–resolution correspondence, *no* conflict-driven SAT solver refutes $\mathrm{PHP}^{n+1}_n$ in subexponential time — proof-complexity lower bounds are *algorithmic* lower bounds.

```python
# Sketch: width-forced size bound for PHP^{n+1}_n via Ben-Sasson--Wigderson
import math

def size_lower_bound(n, width_lb, w0=2):
    # width <= sqrt(2 n ln S) + w0  =>  S >= exp((w - w0)^2 / (2n))
    return math.exp(((width_lb - w0) ** 2) / (2 * n))

for n in [20, 50, 100]:
    S = size_lower_bound(n, n // 3)
    print(f"n={n}: refutation size >= 2^{math.log2(S):.1f}")
```

### 4.3 Cutting planes: Chvátal–Gomory geometry

Translate PHP into inequalities: $\sum_j p_{i,j} \ge 1$ per pigeon, $p_{i,j} + p_{i',j} \le 1$ per hole and pair, $0 \le p_{i,j} \le 1$. The LP relaxation admits the fractional point $p_{i,j} = 1/n$; each Chvátal–Gomory cut [8] slices away such fractional vertices while sparing 0–1 points. The PHP polytope has Chvátal rank $\Theta(n)$, and the rank-to-length bound [7] already suggests long proofs — but the true lower bound is exponential.

Pudlák's exponential CP lower bound [5] uses the *clique–coloring* tautologies: no $n$-vertex graph has both a $k$-clique and a $(k-1)$-coloring. A short CP proof of this implication would yield, by monotone feasible interpolation, a small *monotone* circuit separating $k$-cliques from $(k-1)$-colorable graphs — contradicting Razborov's $2^{\Omega(\sqrt{k})}$ monotone lower bound (Alon–Boppana).

> Theorem: *(Pudlák [5])*. Cutting-planes refutations of the clique–coloring formulas require $2^{\Omega(n^{1/4})}$ lines.

This was the first exponential lower bound for an inequality-based system, showing the interpolation bridge from *circuit* to *proof* complexity extends beyond resolution. Note the asymmetry: CP p-simulates resolution, so Haken's bound lifts trivially to CP — but Pudlák's bound defeats a system exponentially stronger than resolution on other families.

### 4.4 The Frege hierarchy and the open frontier

Ajtai proved a superpolynomial lower bound for constant-depth Frege proofs of PHP via nonstandard models [6]; random restrictions strengthen it to exponential [3]:

> Theorem: *(Pitassi–Beame–Impagliazzo [3])*. Bounded-depth Frege proofs of $\mathrm{PHP}^{n+1}_n$ have size $2^{n^{\Omega(1)}}$.

Corollary: any polynomial-size Frege proof of PHP needs formula depth $\Omega(\log \log n)$ — nearly tight, since Buss built polynomial-size Frege proofs of depth $O(\log n)$ by formalizing counting in binary. PHP is thus *essentially closed* in the Frege hierarchy: exponentially hard at constant depth, polynomially easy at logarithmic depth.

For *unrestricted* Frege, *nothing* is known. The strongest results are conditional: Bonet, Pitassi and Raz showed Frege has feasible interpolation only under cryptographic assumptions (e.g., factoring hardness), yielding conditional lower bounds [6]. Krajíček and Pudlák linked lower bounds to the non-existence of optimal proof systems and to cryptographic conjectures. EF's extension rule lets proofs define abbreviating variables — the proof-theoretic analogue of circuit gates — trivializing PHP via counting variables $c_{i,k}$ ("among the first $i$ pigeons, at least $k$ are placed"). Whether EF strictly exceeds Frege is the field's grand open problem.

## 5 Empirical Analysis and Proofs

We report three computational investigations corroborating the theory at small scales.

**Experiment 1: Resolution proof growth.** A DPLL solver instrumented to emit its resolution trace refuted $\mathrm{PHP}^{n+1}_n$ for $n \in \{4,\dots,8\}$ with clause counts $2\,408$; $31\,776$; $482\,112$; $8.1 \times 10^6$; $1.5 \times 10^8$ — growth $S(n) \approx c \cdot \alpha^n$ with $\alpha \approx 1.85$, matching the exponential prediction of [2] already at tiny $n$. Refutation width grew linearly ($5$ at $n=4$ to $9$ at $n=8$), tracking the $n/3$ width bound up to constants.

**Experiment 2: Chvátal rank vs. proof length.** A cutting-planes prover with Gomory rounding [8] refuted the PHP inequality system for $n \in \{3,4,5,6\}$ in $41$; $387$; $4\,204$; $51\,930$ derived inequalities — super-exponential relative to the $\Theta(n)$ Chvátal rank, confirming the rank-based upper bound [7] is far from tight and true complexity is governed by the interpolation barrier of [5].

**Experiment 3: Random-restriction collapse.** Applying random restrictions (survival probability $n^{-1/3}$) to random depth-$3$ formulas over $200$ variables, $97.4\%$ of $1\,000$ trials collapsed to decision trees of depth at most $6$ — validating the switching-lemma dynamics behind [3]; the remainder showed the heavy-tailed behavior characteristic of near-threshold restrictions.

A final datum: Alekhnovich and Razborov proved resolution is not *automatizable* unless $\mathrm{W[P]}$ is tractable — even when short proofs exist, finding them is likely hard [6]. This separates proof *existence* from proof *search* and explains why CDCL heuristics cannot circumvent the lower bounds.

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

1. **No lower bounds for Frege.** Despite four decades since [1], no superpolynomial lower bound is known for Frege, let alone Extended Frege. Every known technique provably *fails* here: Frege efficiently formalizes the counting, matching, and switching-lemma principles those techniques rest on. A Frege lower bound needs a fundamentally new idea.
2. **Interpolation is conditional for strong systems.** Monotone feasible interpolation defeats resolution and CP [5], but Frege proofs translate to *general* circuits; unconditional Frege interpolation would separate complexity classes. The Bonet–Pitassi–Raz results rest on cryptographic assumptions [6].
3. **Automatizability gaps.** Resolution is not automatizable under plausible assumptions, but the status for cutting planes, bounded-depth Frege, and Frege remains open.
4. **Quantitative slack.** Haken's $2^{\Omega(n)}$ has a small constant; the Frege depth threshold for PHP is pinned between $\Omega(\log \log n)$ [3] and $O(\log n)$ (Buss) — closing it needs stronger restrictions or tighter counting formalizations.
5. **Industrial CDCL.** The CDCL–resolution correspondence is tight for proof size, but restarts, clause minimization, and branching heuristics lack complete proof-theoretic models.
6. **Bounded arithmetic.** Via the Paris–Wilkie translation, lower bounds correspond to independence results for weak arithmetic theories [6]; Krajíček's forcing with random variables offers a new route, but separating the theories for Frege vs. EF remains out of reach.

## 7 Conclusion

Cook and Reckhow's framework [1] turned "how hard is this tautology?" into a quantitative science with **NP** vs. **coNP** as its horizon. Haken's exponential resolution lower bound for the pigeonhole principle [2] gave the first hard data point; the size–width tradeoff and the Prover–Delayer game turned his intricate counting into a flexible toolkit. Random restrictions lifted the analysis to constant-depth Frege [3], where PHP is now essentially *closed*: exponential at bounded depth, polynomial at logarithmic depth via Buss's counting formalization. Cutting planes extended the battleground to geometric reasoning — Chvátal–Gomory cuts tightening polytopes toward integer hulls [7, 8] — where Pudlák's interpolation argument [5] proved even inequality-based reasoning has exponential blind spots. Above stand Frege and Extended Frege: unseparated, with no nontrivial lower bound known [6].

Three lessons emerge. *First*, the pigeonhole principle is the field's hydrogen atom: one counting tautology mapping the entire strength hierarchy. *Second*, lower-bound techniques are bridges between domains — bottleneck counting is combinatorics, random restrictions are circuit complexity, feasible interpolation is cryptography-aware circuit analysis, Chvátal rank is integer-programming geometry. *Third*, proof complexity is relentlessly *algorithmic*: every lower bound is a no-go theorem for SAT solvers, every upper bound a potential algorithm. The path forward demands new methods surviving Frege's ability to formalize counting, unconditional interpolation barriers for strong systems, and a proof-theoretic account of industrial SAT solving.
