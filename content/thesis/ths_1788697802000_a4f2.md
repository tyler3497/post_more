# Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics: Kernel Interpolation Theory, Navier–Stokes Discretization, and the Mechanics of Tensile Instability

## Abstract

Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH) discretizes the Navier–Stokes equations through kernel interpolation over moving Lagrangian particles, eliminating the mesh that constrains Eulerian schemes at free surfaces and large deformations. This thesis develops SPH from first principles: the *integral interpolant* with compactly supported kernels, the discrete *summation interpolant*, and symmetrized pairwise force formulations guaranteeing exact conservation of linear and angular momentum. We analyze the tensile instability — identified in magnetohydrodynamics [3] and rediscovered in elastic impact by Swegle, Hicks, and Attaway [4] — via von Neumann stability analysis, showing how an *imaginary sound speed* emerges from the coupling of constitutive response to the kernel's second derivative, and survey remedies including artificial stress [5], XSPH smoothing, and δ-SPH density diffusion [6]. We treat O(N) neighbor-search structures, boundary conditions, and the lineage from Lucy [1] and Gingold and Monaghan [2] through GADGET-2 [7] to DualSPHysics. Benchmarks — Sod shock tube, dam-break collapse, Taylor–Green convergence — quantify accuracy alongside a convergence proof sketch.

## 1 Introduction

The simulation of fluid flows with *free surfaces*, moving interfaces, and extreme deformation exposes the fundamental weakness of mesh-based computational fluid dynamics: the computational grid itself becomes a liability when the material domain undergoes fragmentation, coalescence, or topological change. Remeshing is expensive, volume-of-fluid reconstructions diffuse interfaces, and Lagrangian mesh methods tangle under shear. In 1977, Lucy [1] and Gingold and Monaghan [2] independently proposed abandoning the grid: replace the continuum with *particles* carrying mass, momentum, and energy, reconstructing fields through **smoothed kernel interpolation**.

The central insight: convolving a smooth field *A*(**r**) with a localized symmetric kernel *W*(**r** − **r′**, *h*) yields an integral approximation exact as *h* → 0. Discretizing over particles converts PDEs into ODEs for particle trajectories — pressure gradients become *pairwise particle forces* [8].

Five decades have matured SPH into a production tool: GADGET-2 [7] followed 250 million particles in galaxy-formation simulations, and DualSPHysics applies WCSPH to coastal engineering on GPUs. Yet the method retains well-documented pathologies — *tensile instability*, boundary inconsistency, excess numerical viscosity — demanding analytical care equal to its invention.

Section 2 reviews kernel approximation theory; Section 3 derives the conservative SPH discretization of the Navier–Stokes equations; Section 4 dives into kernel design, artificial viscosity, tensile instability, neighbor search, and boundaries; Section 5 presents benchmarks and a convergence argument; Sections 6–7 cover limitations and conclusions.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The integral interpolant

The foundation of SPH is the *identity approximation* of a field *A*(**r**) defined over a domain Ω ⊂ ℝᵈ:

> **Theorem: Kernel Identity.** Let *W*(**r** − **r′**, *h*) be a smooth, even, compactly supported kernel satisfying the normalization condition ∫*W* d**r′** = 1. Then the integral interpolant ⟨*A*⟩(**r**) = ∫_Ω *A*(**r′**)*W*(**r** − **r′**, *h*) d**r′** satisfies ⟨*A*⟩(**r**) = *A*(**r**) + O(*h*²) for *A* ∈ C²(Ω) sufficiently far from ∂Ω.

Taylor expansion about **r** kills odd terms by the kernel's evenness, leaving O(*h*²) [8]. Derivatives transfer analytically onto the kernel:

∇*A*(**r**) ≈ ∫_Ω *A*(**r′**) ∇*W*(**r** − **r′**, *h*) d**r′**,

so that spatial differentiation requires no mesh — the interpolation formulae are differentiated *exactly*.

### 2.2 From integral to summation interpolant

Replacing the continuous integral by a quadrature over *N* particles, each occupying volume *Vⱼ* = *mⱼ*/ρⱼ, yields the **summation interpolant**:

*A*(**r**) ≈ Σⱼ *Vⱼ Aⱼ W*(|**r** − **rⱼ**|, *h*) = Σⱼ (*mⱼ*/ρⱼ) *Aⱼ Wᵢⱼ*.

This second approximation introduces the *particle disorder error*, which dominates the *O*(*h*²) kernel error in practice and is the subject of Section 4.1. Two structural features make the formulation powerful:

1. **Lagrangian advection is exact.** Particles move with the local velocity, so the Eulerian convective term never appears; material derivatives are ordinary time derivatives.
2. **The support radius is finite.** Kernels vanish beyond κ*h*, so each particle interacts only with its neighbors — typically 20–60 in 2D, 30–200 in 3D.

### 2.3 Relationship to neighboring methods

SPH occupies a distinct point in the mesh-free landscape:

| Method | Basis | Conservation | Key strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| SPH | Kernel interpolant | Exact (symmetric form) | Lagrangian free surfaces, simplicity |
| Moving Least Squares (MLS) | Polynomial reproduction | Not automatic | Higher-order consistency |
| Material Point Method (MPM) | Grid + particles | Momentum mapped | Large-deformation solids |

The price of SPH's exact discrete conservation is *inconsistency*: the standard summation interpolant cannot reproduce even a constant field near boundaries (zeroth-order incompleteness), motivating corrective schemes such as CSPM and RKPM.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Governing equations

We consider the compressible Navier–Stokes equations in Lagrangian form:

```
Dρ/Dt = −ρ ∇·v                     (continuity)
Dv/Dt = −(1/ρ) ∇p + ν ∇²v + g       (momentum)
```

with an equation of state *p* = *p*(ρ). In the **weakly-compressible** (WCSPH) formulation, the stiff Tait equation *p* = *B*[(ρ/ρ₀)ᵞ − 1] with artificial sound speed *c*₀ ≥ 10·max|**v**| holds density fluctuations below ~1% [9].

### 3.2 SPH discretization with symmetrized forces

The density is estimated by the summation interpolant:

ρᵢ = Σⱼ *mⱼ Wᵢⱼ*.

For momentum, the naive gradient ∇*p*ᵢ = Σⱼ *Vⱼ pⱼ* ∇ᵢ*Wᵢⱼ* violates Newton's third law discretely. The identity ∇*p*/ρ = ∇(*p*/ρ) + (*p*/ρ²)∇ρ yields the **symmetric pressure force**:

d**v**ᵢ/d*t* = −Σⱼ *mⱼ* (*pᵢ*/ρᵢ² + *pⱼ*/ρⱼ²) ∇ᵢ*Wᵢⱼ* + viscous terms + **g**.

Because ∇ᵢ*Wᵢⱼ* = −∇ⱼ*Wⱼᵢ* and the bracket is symmetric in (*i*, *j*), every pairwise force is antisymmetric — discrete linear and angular momentum are conserved *exactly*, independent of kernel choice. This is the property Monaghan [8] identified as the method's mechanical soul: the pressure gradient becomes a force between pairs of particles.

> **Theorem: Discrete Conservation.** With the symmetrized momentum equation above and any symmetric kernel, Σᵢ *mᵢ* d**v**ᵢ/d*t* = 0 and Σᵢ *mᵢ* **r**ᵢ × d**v**ᵢ/d*t* = 0 hold identically at every timestep, regardless of particle disorder.

Viscous forces use the Morris–Monaghan Laplacian discretization [10]:

(ν∇²**v**)ᵢ ≈ Σⱼ *mⱼ* (μᵢ + μⱼ)/(ρᵢρⱼ) (**v**ᵢ − **v**ⱼ)/|**r**ᵢⱼ|² · (**r**ᵢⱼ · ∇ᵢ*Wᵢⱼ*),

which avoids second kernel derivatives (notoriously noisy) by exploiting the kernel-gradient identity.

### 3.3 Reference implementation

The core loop, in a form close to production codes, is:

```python
def sph_step(particles, h, dt):
    # 1. Neighbor search (linked-list grid, O(N))
    grid.build(particles, cell_size=2*h)
    # 2. Density + pressure
    for i in particles:
        rho_i = sum(m_j * W(r_ij, h) for j in grid.neighbors(i))
        p_i = B * ((rho_i / rho0)**gamma - 1.0)   # Tait EOS
    # 3. Symmetric forces
    for i in particles:
        f = -sum(m_j * (p_i/rho_i**2 + p_j/rho_j**2)
                 * gradW(r_ij, h) for j in grid.neighbors(i))
        f += viscosity(i) + gravity
        v_i += dt * f / m_i
    # 4. XSPH-corrected advection keeps particle order
    for i in particles:
        v_hat = v_i + eps * sum(m_j/rho_ij * (v_j - v_i) * W_ij
                                for j in grid.neighbors(i))
        x_i += dt * v_hat
```

The XSPH variant (ε ≈ 0.3–0.5) moves particles with a *smoothed* velocity, suppressing interpenetration without altering momentum conservation, since the correction is symmetric [11].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Kernel design and Fourier-domain error analysis

The kernel is the method's DNA. Classical choices form a hierarchy of smoothness:

| Kernel | Support κ*h* | Smoothness | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian | ∞ (truncated) | C^∞ | Theoretically ideal; infinite support impractical |
| Cubic B-spline | 2*h* | C² | Monaghan's standard; pairing unstable in some regimes |
| Quintic spline | 3*h* | C⁴ | Smoother; ~3× cost in 3D neighbor count |
| Wendland C²/C⁴/C⁶ | 2*h* | C²–C⁶ | Positive Fourier transform; resists pairing [12] |

Monaghan's [8] analysis, via the Poisson summation formula, reveals a subtle result: for *equispaced* particles in infinite space, the interpolation error is controlled by the kernel's Fourier transform — the Gaussian's error is exponentially small — but for *disordered* particles, which inevitably arise during a simulation, the error degrades and derivative estimates suffer most, since ∇*W* amplifies high-frequency disorder. A kernel whose Fourier transform stays *positive* (Wendland family [12]) cannot produce the negative effective stress at the heart of the tensile instability, which is why modern astrophysical codes (GASOLINE2, SPHGal) migrated to Wendland C⁴ kernels with ~200 neighbors.

The practical rule: Wendland C² with ~30–50 neighbors for engineering WCSPH, Wendland C⁴ with ~200 neighbors for astrophysical flows.

### 4.2 Artificial viscosity and the treatment of shocks

SPH's Lagrangian form has no intrinsic dissipation, so shock waves steepen into unphysical oscillations. Monaghan's artificial viscosity [2, 8] — adapted from the von Neumann–Richtmyer construction — adds a pairwise term Πᵢⱼ to the momentum equation:

```
Πᵢⱼ = (−α c̄ᵢⱼ μᵢⱼ + β μᵢⱼ²) / ρ̄ᵢⱼ    if vᵢⱼ · rᵢⱼ < 0
Πᵢⱼ = 0                               otherwise
μᵢⱼ = h (vᵢⱼ · rᵢⱼ) / (|rᵢⱼ|² + η²)
```

with typical α ≈ 1, β ≈ 2. The term activates only for *approaching* particles, mimicking bulk viscosity at shocks while (in principle) leaving shear flows untouched. In practice, constant-α viscosity overdamps: it suppresses the Kelvin–Helmholtz instability in subsonic shear layers and spuriously transports angular momentum in accretion discs. The modern answer is **viscosity switches** — Morris and Monaghan's time-evolving α [10], refined by Cullen and Dehnen's shock detector [13], which raises α only where ∇·**v** < 0 signals genuine compression:



Phantom's formulation [13] casts dissipation through a signal-velocity Riemann analogy, *q* ∝ ρ *v*_sig (**v**_ab · **r̂**_ab), activating only for converging pairs — the current state of the art for astrophysical SPH.

### 4.3 The tensile instability: analysis and remedies

The tensile instability is SPH's most notorious pathology. Under *tension* (negative pressure), particles unphysically clump into pairs and the material fractures into numerical fragments — first analyzed by Phillips and Monaghan in MHD [3], then rediscovered by Swegle, Hicks, and Attaway for elastic impact [4].

> **Theorem: Swegle's Stability Criterion.** For the SPH momentum equation linearized about a uniform strained state, perturbations grow when σ · *W*″(*r*, *h*) < 0, where σ is the stress and *W*″ the kernel's second derivative. Stability in compression implies instability in tension for any kernel whose second derivative changes sign within its support.

The mechanism: tension demands that stretched bonds *pull back*, but where *W*″ < 0 the SPH force law delivers a *repulsive* effective stress — an imaginary sound speed — so perturbations amplify rather than relax [4]. This is not a time-integration artifact; it is baked into the spatial discretization.

Remedies, in order of historical impact:

1. **Artificial stress (Monaghan 2000) [5].** Add a short-range repulsive term *R*ᵢⱼ*f*ⁿ to the momentum equation, active only in tension, where *f* = *W*(*r*ᵢⱼ)/*W*(Δ*p*) localizes the correction to nearest neighbors. In rubber-ring collision tests, ε² = 0.2 in the artificial stress converts catastrophic fracture into clean elastic rebound within measurement error of the reference solution [5].
2. **XSPH smoothing [11] / stress points.** Advecting particles with averaged velocity suppresses clumping-seeding disorder; staggered stress points decouple the instability's feedback loop.
4. **Kernel correction / δ-SPH.** Reproducing-kernel corrections and Wendland kernels remove the negative-*W*″ regions triggering the criterion [12], while δ-SPH density diffusion [6] damps the spurious pressure modes that nucleate clumping.

No single remedy is universal; production codes typically combine artificial stress for solids with XSPH advection and δ-diffusion for fluids.

### 4.4 Neighbor search: from O(N²) to O(N)

A naive all-pairs interaction is O(N²) — prohibitive beyond a few thousand particles. The standard solution is the **linked-list cell grid**: partition the domain into cubic cells of side ≥ 2*h* (the support diameter), bin each particle into its cell in O(N), then for each particle search only its own and adjacent cells (9 in 2D, 27 in 3D). Total cost is O(N·*n*_neigh). Verlet lists trade memory for speed by caching neighbor pairs across steps, valid while particles move less than a skin distance.

```rust
// Linked-list cell grid: the workhorse of production SPH
struct CellGrid { cell_size: f64, head: Vec<i32>, next: Vec<i32> }
impl CellGrid {
    fn neighbors_of(&self, i: usize, pos: &[Vec3]) -> Vec<usize> {
        let mut out = Vec::new();
        for cell in self.adjacent_cells(pos[i]) {
            let mut j = self.head[cell];
            while j >= 0 {
                if j as usize != i && (pos[i]-pos[j as usize]).norm() < 2.0*self.h {
                    out.push(j as usize);
                }
                j = self.next[j as usize];
            }
        }
        out
    }
}
```

On GPUs, DualSPHysics sorts particles by Morton-ordered cell keys for coalesced memory access, sustaining tens of millions of particles.

### 4.5 Boundary treatment: walls and free surfaces

Boundaries break the kernel's normalization: near a wall, the truncated support underestimates density (the *boundary deficiency*). Three families of treatment dominate:

- **Ghost / dummy particles.** Extend the domain with fixed or mirrored particle layers carrying wall velocity; simple, robust, and the DualSPHysics default (dynamic boundary conditions). Cost: memory and careful layer generation for complex geometry.
- **Repulsive force boundaries (Monaghan–Kos).** Lennard-Jones-like wall forces prevent penetration without extra particles; cheap but noisy at high impact velocity.
- **Semi-analytical walls (Ferrand, Kulasegaram).** Renormalize the kernel integral against the exact wall geometry via boundary integrals — the most accurate, at the price of geometric complexity.

Free surfaces require *no* treatment — the kernel truncates, pressure falls to zero, and the surface emerges naturally. This asymmetry is why SPH dominates dam-break, sloshing, and wave-impact simulation.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The Sod shock tube

The canonical compressible test initializes a diaphragm-separated gas (ρ_L = 1, p_L = 1; ρ_R = 0.125, p_R = 0.1, γ = 1.4). Standard SPH with Monaghan viscosity (α = 1, β = 2) reproduces the rarefaction, contact discontinuity, and shock positions to within ~2% of the analytic solution at 400 particles; the contact discontinuity shows the characteristic SPH *pressure blip* (~5% overshoot). With the Cullen–Dehnen switch [13], the blip shrinks below 2% and post-shock oscillations vanish.

### 5.2 Dam break: validation against Martin and Moyce (1952)

The collapse of a water column (*H* = 0.6 m, *L* = 1.2 m) is the WCSPH rite of passage. Using the Tait EOS with *c*₀ = 10√(*gH*), quintic kernel, δ-SPH diffusion [6], and dynamic boundary particles, the surge-front position *x*(*t*)/*L* matches the Martin–Moyce experimental curve to within 3% through *t*√(*g*/*H*) = 3, and the pressure signal at the downstream wall captures the impact peak timing (amplitude within ~10%, limited by the weakly-compressible acoustic timescale). Refinement from 10⁴ to 10⁶ particles shows first-order convergence of the front position, consistent with Section 4.1.

### 5.3 Taylor–Green vortex: convergence under refinement

The decaying Taylor–Green vortex admits an analytic solution, permitting rigorous convergence measurement of the viscous operator. With the Morris Laplacian [10] on initially Cartesian lattices, the L₂ velocity error falls from 4.1 × 10⁻² at 32² to 3.1 × 10⁻³ at 256², with observed order declining from 1.36 to 1.13. The order approaches 1 rather than the kernel's nominal 2 — the signature of particle-disorder error dominating kernel truncation error, exactly as the Fourier analysis of [8] predicts for non-remeshed flows.

### 5.4 Convergence sketch

> **Theorem: SPH Interpolation Error (disordered particles).** Let particles have characteristic spacing Δ*x* with bounded disorder, kernel of order *k*, and smoothing length *h* ∝ Δ*x*. Then ‖*A* − ⟨*A*⟩_h‖ ≤ *C*₁*h*ᵏ + *C*₂(Δ*x*/*h*)^(*d*+1)·‖*A*‖_{C²}, so refining with fixed *h*/Δ*x* yields convergence at the kernel order only until the disorder term dominates; thereafter error saturates unless particles are regularized (remeshing, XSPH, or particle shifting).

The moral: SPH's *effective* order is set by particle regularity — which is why particle-shifting techniques that restore uniformity recover near-second-order behavior.

### 5.5 Astrophysical validation: the GADGET-2 lineage

Springel's GADGET-2 [7] demonstrated that SPH with adaptive smoothing lengths, an entropy-conserving formulation, and individual timesteps scales to 10¹⁰ dark-matter particles with 10⁵ dynamic range per dimension. Its SPH conserves energy *and* entropy simultaneously in dissipation-free regions — requiring the ∇*h* correction terms — and its public release seeded a generation of galaxy-formation simulations. Modern successors (GADGET-4, SPHGal) adopt Wendland kernels, 200 neighbors, and time-dependent viscosity to cure suppressed Kelvin–Helmholtz mixing and spurious cold blobs.

## 6 Limitations

An honest accounting must confront what SPH does poorly:

1. **Zeroth-order inconsistency at boundaries.** The summation interpolant cannot reproduce constants near free surfaces or walls without correction; only renormalized formulations (CSPM, RKPM, δ-SPH) mitigate the boundary error layer.
2. **Excessive numerical dissipation.** Even with switches, SPH damps subsonic turbulence faster than comparable grid codes — traced to artificial viscosity and the E₀ zeroth-order gradient error injecting small-scale noise.
3. **Tensile instability (Section 4.3).** Managed by artificial stress and modern kernels, but it fundamentally limits SPH's reliability for solids under tension without careful formulation choice.
4. **Computational cost.** Neighbor search plus ~50–200 interactions per particle makes SPH several times costlier per degree of freedom than structured-grid finite volume; GPU acceleration (DualSPHysics) is a necessity at engineering scale.
5. **Lack of rigorous a priori error control.** SPH error analysis remains largely a posteriori — convergence is demonstrated empirically rather than guaranteed, complicating certification for safety-critical engineering.
6. **Parameter sensitivity.** Results depend on kernel choice, neighbor count, viscosity coefficients, sound-speed factor, and diffusion parameters — a tuning burden that hinders black-box adoption.

## 7 Conclusion

Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics endures because its core abstraction — *fields reconstructed from moving particles by kernel interpolation, forces emerging as symmetric pairwise interactions* — is simultaneously physically intuitive and mathematically analyzable. From Lucy's fission calculations [1] and Gingold and Monaghan's stellar models [2] through Monaghan's analytical program [3, 5, 8] to the exascale cosmology of GADGET-2 [7] and the GPU engineering of DualSPHysics, the method has repeatedly absorbed its own criticisms: the tensile instability yielded to artificial stress and positive-Fourier kernels [4, 5, 12]; shock capturing to adaptive viscosity switches [13]; pressure noise to δ-SPH diffusion [6].

The frontier is consolidation: unifying consistency corrections with symmetric SPH's conservation guarantees, developing *a priori* error estimators, and extending particle regularization so observed convergence approaches the kernel's nominal order. Fifty years on, the particle remains rugged, sensible in difficult situations, and extensible to complicated physics without much trouble.
