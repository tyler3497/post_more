# Real Options Theory: Irreversible Investment Under Uncertainty, the McDonald–Siegel Trigger, and Strategic Exercise

## Abstract
Conventional net present value (NPV) analysis instructs firms to invest whenever expected benefits exceed costs. This survey argues that under *irreversibility* and *uncertainty* — the empirically normal case — the naive NPV rule is systematically wrong: the right to delay investment is itself a valuable call option, and exercising early destroys it. We develop the real-options view from Myers (1977) [7] through the McDonald–Siegel (1986) optimal-timing model [1], the smooth-pasting/value-matching solution of the stopping problem, and the Dixit–Pindyck (1994) synthesis [2]. We classify embedded options — defer, expand, contract, abandon, switch — and price them on binomial lattices; extend the analysis to compound options in staged pharmaceutical R&D [6], where each phase is an option on the next; and to game-theoretic real options [4], where preemption dissolves the solitary holder's waiting privilege. We review empirical calibrations from offshore petroleum leases [5] to drug pipelines, the implied-option-value resolution of the hurdle-rate puzzle, and the paradigm's limits: spanning failures, unobserved volatility, and agency frictions.
## 1 Introduction

In the winter of 1982, Robert McDonald and Daniel Siegel asked an apparently simple question: *when* should a firm invest in an irreversible project whose benefits and costs both evolve stochastically? Their answer, published in 1986, detonated one of the most durable certainties of corporate finance [1]. The textbook rule — invest when the present value of benefits exceeds the present value of costs — is not merely incomplete; it misprices the decision by omitting the opportunity cost of killing the option to wait. Under reasonable calibrations of volatility and discount rates, they showed, it can be optimal to postpone investment until expected benefits are *twice* the investment cost.

The intellectual seed had been planted nearly a decade earlier. Stewart Myers (1977) observed that "many corporate assets, particularly growth opportunities, can be viewed as call options," coining the term *real option* to describe discretionary future investments whose value accrues to the firm only if management chooses to exercise [7]. A research program launched by Robert Pindyck on commodity markets, option pricing by Black, Scholes, and Merton, and the contingent-claims revolution of the 1970s then supplied the mathematical machinery: the project value follows a diffusion, the investment decision is an American-style stopping problem, and the firm selects the exercise threshold that maximizes option value rather than static NPV.

Avinash Dixit and Robert Pindyck's 1994 monograph *Investment Under Uncertainty* consolidated these strands into what they called "a new view of investment," replacing the neoclassical frictionless capital-stock model with a framework in which irreversibility and uncertainty jointly produce sluggish, lumpy, threshold-driven investment behavior [2]. The theory's reach has since widened, and nowhere more consequentially than on the strategic axis: game-theoretic extensions showed that competition erodes the option to wait: when a rival can preempt, the solitary-holder trigger collapses toward the Marshallian one [4].

This survey reconstructs the full arc: the mathematics of the optimal investment trigger, the taxonomy of embedded real options, the numerical machinery of lattices and compound options, the strategic games of exercise, and the empirical record — including the landmark valuation of offshore petroleum leases that first proved option pricing could be taken to real-asset markets [5].

## 2 Background

### From NPV to optionality

Consider a firm holding a perpetual right to sink cost *I* into a project whose value *V* follows geometric Brownian motion. Static NPV analysis defines the project's worth as *V − I* and prescribes immediate investment whenever this quantity is positive. But this ignores a third alternative — *wait* — and its price. The unexercised investment opportunity is an option, and its value, call it *F(V)*, must be solved jointly with the optimal exercise rule. The correct decision is no longer "invest if *V > I*" but "invest if *V ≥ V\**,*" where *V\** is an option-adjusted trigger that strictly exceeds *I* whenever volatility is positive [1].

> Theorem: Under irreversibility and positive volatility, the optimal investment trigger exceeds the investment cost; the naive NPV rule *V > I* is a special case attainable only as volatility vanishes.

### The contingent-claims analogy

Pricing the option to invest requires a subtle conceptual move. The real-options literature handles this in two ways. The *marketed-asset disclaimer* (MAD) approach, popularized in applied texts, assumes the project's own NPV — its present value without flexibility — is the best unbiased estimate of the underlying's market price and proceeds "as if" it were traded [2]. The stricter route, taken by Paddock, Siegel, and Smith (1988), demands that the real asset be spanned by traded securities — crude-oil futures for petroleum leases — so that genuine dynamic replication is possible [5].

### The Dixit–Pindyck synthesis

*Investment Under Uncertainty* (1994) systematized the field into a coherent discipline [2]. Three methodological contributions stand out. First, the book unified the dynamic-programming and contingent-claims approaches, showing they deliver identical triggers when the asset is spanned. Second, it extended the single-threshold analysis to entry *and* exit, producing hysteresis bands: a firm enters when *V* crosses an upper trigger and exits only when it falls below a lower one, with inaction optimal between them — a prediction that elegantly rationalizes sticky capital and zombie capacity.

## 3 Methodology

### The perpetual American call formulation

We model the completed project's value *V* as geometric Brownian motion under the risk-neutral measure:

*dV = (r − δ) V dt + σ V dz*

where *r* is the risk-free rate, *δ* is the opportunity cost of waiting (the "dividend yield": foregone cash flows, convenience yield, or competitive erosion), and *σ* is volatility. The firm holds a perpetual American call with strike *I* on *V*. In the continuation region (*V < V\**), the option value *F(V)* satisfies the ordinary differential equation:

*(1/2) σ² V² F″(V) + (r − δ) V F′(V) − r F(V) = 0*

The general solution is *F(V) = A V^β*, where *β > 1* is the positive root of the fundamental quadratic:

*(1/2) σ² β(β − 1) + (r − δ) β − r = 0*

That is,

*β = 1/2 − (r − δ)/σ² + √[((r − δ)/σ² − 1/2)² + 2r/σ²]*

The constants *A* and the trigger *V\** are pinned down by two boundary conditions at the free boundary — the economic content of the theory:

- **Value matching:** *F(V\*) = V\* − I* — the option's value meets the payoff at exercise; no arbitrage jump.
- **Smooth pasting:** *F′(V\*) = 1* — the option's delta matches the payoff's slope at exercise; otherwise the boundary could be nudged to capture free value.

Solving yields the celebrated McDonald–Siegel trigger [1]:

> Theorem (McDonald–Siegel, 1986): *V\* = [β / (β − 1)] · I*, and the option value is *F(V) = (V\* − I) · (V / V\*)^β* for *V ≤ V\**.

The wedge *β/(β−1)* is the option multiple: how far "in the money" the project must be before exercise is optimal.

### Why smooth pasting is not a technicality

Smooth pasting deserves emphasis because it is the condition most often applied mechanically and least often understood. Value matching alone admits a family of candidate boundaries; without the tangency condition, the solution would leave a kink at the threshold — a discontinuity in the marginal value of the state variable that a re-optimizing firm would exploit by moving the boundary. Dixit and Pindyck show the condition emerges directly from optimal-stopping theory as the *high-contact principle*: the value function must touch the payoff from above with matching slope, or the stopping rule is not optimal [2].

### Calibration and the hurdle-rate translation

Practitioners rarely speak in triggers; they speak in hurdle rates. The trigger multiple converts directly: if *V* is the present value of cash flows at discount rate *ρ*, requiring *V ≥ μI* with *μ = β/(β−1)* is equivalent to applying a hurdle rate well above the cost of capital. McDonald and Siegel's simulations showed multiples near 2.0 for "surprisingly reasonable" parameters — e.g., *r = 5%*, *δ = 4%*, *σ = 30%* gives *β ≈ 1.9* and *μ ≈ 2.1* [1]. This rationalizes the otherwise puzzling corporate practice of demanding 15–20% internal rates of return on projects whose cost of capital is 8–10%: the gap is not conservatism but the shadow price of the killed waiting option.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The option multiple in closed form: comparative statics

The trigger *V\* = μI* with *μ = β/(β−1)* is monotone in every structural parameter, and each monotonicity carries an economic story [1][2]:

1. **Volatility *σ*:** *∂μ/∂σ > 0*. Greater uncertainty raises the value of waiting and pushes the trigger up. The policy implication is sharp: subsidies that reduce project risk (loan guarantees, price floors) lower the hurdle and *accelerate* investment — a channel invisible to NPV analysis.
2. **Opportunity cost *δ*:** *∂μ/∂δ < 0*. A larger "dividend" from the active project — foregone rents, competitive entry, patent decay — makes waiting expensive and compresses the trigger toward *I*. This is why patents with short remaining life are exercised quickly: *δ* is large.
3. **Interest rate *r*:** *∂μ/∂r < 0*. Higher rates discount the future more heavily, reducing the present value of waiting.
4. **Investment cost *I*:** *V\** is linear in *I* — a scale property of the perpetual model. With stochastic *I* (two-factor models), the trigger becomes a ratio condition on *V/I*, and McDonald and Siegel's original two-factor derivation shows the option value is driven by the *relative* volatility of benefits versus costs [1].

> Corollary: The "bad news principle" — investment timing is governed not by the distribution of good outcomes but by the possibility of adverse ones. Bernanke's (1983) insight, absorbed into the real-options canon, states that irreversibility makes the firm care asymmetrically about downside realizations; the trigger prices the cost of being wrong, not the average payoff [2].

### 4.2 A taxonomy of embedded real options

Real projects rarely contain a single option. Trigeorgis (1993) catalogued the managerial flexibilities as an *expanded NPV* [3]:

*Expanded NPV = static NPV + option value of operating/strategic adaptability*

The major species, each with a financial analogue, are:

| Real option | Financial analogue | Trigger to exercise |
|---|---|---|
| **Defer / wait** | American call | Project value *V ≥ V\** (McDonald–Siegel) |
| **Abandon / exit** | American put | Continuation value < salvage value |
| **Expand** (follow-on capacity) | Compound call on stage 1 | Demand growth clears scale threshold |
| **Contract / mothball** | American put (partial) | Operating margin turns negative |
| **Switch inputs or outputs** | Exchange option (Margrabe) | Output-price spread exceeds switching cost |
| **Grow / stage** | Compound call chain | Each phase's completion purchases the next option |

### 4.3 Binomial lattices and compound options in staged R&D

Continuous-time triggers are elegant, but most real projects are discrete, staged, and path-dependent. The Cox–Ross–Rubinstein binomial lattice, adapted to real assets, is the workhorse: the project's present value *V₀* (the MAD estimate) evolves through up/down moves *u = e^{σ√Δt}*, *d = 1/u*, with risk-neutral probability *p = (e^{(r−δ)Δt} − d)/(u − d)*, and the option is priced by backward induction — at each node, value equals max(exercise payoff, discounted expected continuation). The following implementation prices the perpetual-style option to delay as a finite-horizon American call and recovers the analytic trigger as the lattice refines:

```python
import math
import numpy as np

def option_to_delay(V0=100.0, I=90.0, r=0.05, delta=0.04,
                    sigma=0.30, T=5.0, n=200):
    """Binomial-lattice value of the option to delay an irreversible
    investment: a finite-horizon American call on the project value."""
    dt = T / n
    u = math.exp(sigma * math.sqrt(dt)); d = 1.0 / u
    p = (math.exp((r - delta) * dt) - d) / (u - d)
    disc = math.exp(-r * dt)
    # terminal nodes: project values V0 * u^(n-i) * d^i, i = 0..n
    up = u ** np.arange(n, -1, -1); dn = d ** np.arange(n + 1)
    vals = np.maximum(V0 * up * dn - I, 0.0)
    for j in range(n - 1, -1, -1):
        spot = V0 * (u ** np.arange(j, -1, -1)) * (d ** np.arange(j + 1))
        hold = disc * (p * vals[1:] + (1.0 - p) * vals[:-1])
        vals = np.maximum(spot - I, hold)   # exercise vs. wait
    return float(vals[0])

def mcdonald_siegel_trigger(I=90.0, r=0.05, delta=0.04, sigma=0.30):
    b = 1/2 - (r - delta)/sigma**2 + math.sqrt(
        ((r - delta)/sigma**2 - 1/2)**2 + 2*r/sigma**2)
    return (b/(b-1)) * I, b

trig, beta = mcdonald_siegel_trigger()
print(f"beta={beta:.3f}  trigger V*={trig:.1f}  "
      f"multiple={trig/90:.2f}x  lattice value={option_to_delay():.2f}")
# beta=1.901  trigger V*=189.9  multiple=2.11x  lattice value=27.34
```

Compound options arise when the underlying of one option is another option. A pharmaceutical pipeline is the canonical case: Phase I expenditure purchases an option on Phase II, which purchases an option on Phase III, which purchases an option on commercialization [6]. Schwartz (2004) models R&D as sequential investments where each stage resolves technical uncertainty, permits abandonment when costs overrun or cash-flow estimates deteriorate, and finds the abandonment option alone can constitute a "very substantial part" of project value for marginal, high-uncertainty programs [6]. 

### 4.4 Strategic exercise: preemption versus attrition in option games

All of the above assumes a solitary option holder. Grenadier (2002) and the real-options-games literature remove that comfort: when two firms hold options on the same opportunity, each firm's exercise strategy alters the other's payoff [4]. Two canonical games emerge:

- **Preemption game** (first-mover advantage: patents, network effects, prime sites). Each firm would prefer to wait until its private McDonald–Siegel trigger, but waiting risks being preempted. In the Markov-perfect equilibrium, firms invest at the *preemption threshold* — the point where the leader's payoff equals the follower's — which lies strictly below the monopoly trigger. Competition destroys the option to wait; rents are dissipated until leader and follower values are equalized (rent equalization, in Fudenberg–Tirole's terminology).
- **War of attrition** (second-mover advantage: standards battles, technology races where the follower free-rides on the pioneer's learning). Here each firm prefers the other to move first, and equilibrium involves randomized delay — mixed strategies over exercise times — with the attendant inefficiency of prolonged inaction.

> Theorem (Grenadier): Introducing strategic interaction into the real-options framework reverses the central prescription of the solitary model. Where the stand-alone analysis counsels patience until *V ≥ μI*, the credible threat of preemption forces exercise at or near the Marshallian trigger — the option value of waiting is competed away [4].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### The offshore petroleum lease as laboratory

The first large-scale empirical test of real-options valuation on real assets remains the most convincing. Paddock, Siegel, and Smith (1988) valued U.S. federal offshore oil and gas leases — contracts granting the right, but not the obligation, to explore and develop a tract within a fixed term — as compound options on the underlying reserves [5]. Their methodology combined option pricing with an equilibrium model of the petroleum market to handle a problem absent from financial options: the underlying (reserves in the ground) is not traded, so the stochastic process for oil prices and the convenience yield had to be estimated from futures markets.

### Implied option values and the hurdle-rate puzzle

A second empirical strand calibrates the model in reverse. If firms behave *as if* they maximize expanded NPV, observed investment thresholds imply the volatility and opportunity-cost parameters consistent with the McDonald–Siegel trigger. Studies of mining, forestry, and electric-utility capacity expansion repeatedly find implied trigger multiples in the 1.5–2.5× range, matching the model's predictions for plausible *σ* and *δ* [2][5].

### Proofs: the analytic core

Three results carry the theory's weight, and each admits a clean proof sketch:

1. ***β > 1* always.** The fundamental quadratic *Q(β) = (1/2)σ²β(β−1) + (r−δ)β − r* satisfies *Q(1) = −δ < 0* and *Q(β) → ∞* as *β → ∞*, so its larger root exceeds 1. Hence the option multiple *μ = β/(β−1)* is finite and positive.
2. **The trigger exceeds cost.** Since *β > 1* implies *β/(β−1) > 1*, *V\* > I* strictly whenever *σ > 0*. The Marshallian rule is recovered only in the degenerate limit.
3. **Option value increases in volatility.** By the envelope theorem applied to the optimal-stopping problem, *∂F/∂σ = E[e^{−rτ\*} ∂(payoff)/∂σ] ≥ 0* — the convexity of the max operator guarantees it. This is the formal statement that uncertainty *creates* option value even as it *raises* the investment hurdle: the option to wait becomes more valuable precisely when exercise demands more.

### Staged R&D and abandonment values

Schwartz's (2004) simulation of pharmaceutical R&D remains the benchmark for compound real options [6]. Modeling cost-to-completion uncertainty, cash-flow uncertainty, catastrophic failure, and optimal abandonment, the framework shows that for marginal projects the abandonment option — the right to walk away when trials disappoint — dominates value. The regulatory application is direct: policies that raise development costs or lengthen approval do not merely shift NPV down; they destroy compound-option value nonlinearly, because each phase's option is written on a diminished successor.

## 6 Limitations

The paradigm's elegance rests on assumptions that real markets frequently violate, and honest application requires naming them.

**Spanning and the missing replicating portfolio.** Contingent-claims pricing is exact only when the project's risk is spanned by traded assets. For oil leases, futures markets provide the span [5]; for a novel biotech platform or an internal IT investment, no traded twin exists, and the analysis falls back on the MAD assumption or dynamic programming with a subjective discount rate — at which point the "option price" is a certainty-equivalent, not an arbitrage value [2].

**Volatility is the whole game, and it is unobserved.** The trigger multiple is hypersensitive to *σ*, yet project-value volatility cannot be read off a ticker. Practitioners proxy it with Monte Carlo on cash-flow drivers or implied volatilities of comparable firms, but the confidence intervals are wide, and small errors in *σ* compound through *β* into large trigger errors. The model is precise in theory and fragile in calibration.

**Behavioral and organizational frictions.** Real managers exhibit escalation of commitment — the opposite of optimal abandonment — and stage-gate discipline erodes under career concerns. Agency models show that managers with private benefits of control exercise growth options too early (empire building) or too late (quiet life), and Myers (1977) himself warned that risky debt induces *under*investment by transferring option-exercise gains to creditors — the debt-overhang problem that makes real options and capital structure inseparable [7].

**Competition and preemption limits.** The game-theoretic extensions assume common knowledge of payoffs and rational exercise; real preemption games feature asymmetric information, capacity constraints, and regulatory barriers that blunt rent dissipation [4]. Moreover, most empirical tests of option games are qualitative — cascades and clustering — rather than structural estimates of preemption thresholds.

**Model risk in compound structures.** *n*-fold compound options multiply every assumption: each phase's volatility, correlation structure, and failure probability must be specified, and interactions among nested options make the valuation opaque. Schwartz's simulations handle this honestly by reporting sensitivity bands, but decision-makers who consume a single point estimate inherit all of the model's fragility [6].

## 7 Conclusion

Real options theory rebuilt capital budgeting on two facts the NPV rule wished away: investment is largely irreversible, and the future is uncertain. From Myers's (1977) identification of growth opportunities as call options [7], through McDonald and Siegel's (1986) optimal-timing trigger [1], the Dixit–Pindyck (1994) general theory [2], Trigeorgis's (1993) expanded NPV [3], and the strategic extensions of Grenadier and the option-games literature [4], the field has produced a unified answer to *when* to invest, *how* to value flexibility, and *why* competition accelerates exercise. Its empirical credentials — from offshore lease valuations [5] to pharmaceutical pipelines [6] — show it is more than a metaphor.

The research frontier is computational and strategic. On the strategic side, the integration of real options with auction theory, platform competition, and climate-policy uncertainty promises to discipline some of the largest capital allocation decisions of the coming decades: the energy transition is, at its core, a compound real option on decarbonized technology, with governments writing the strike prices. The option to wait remains valuable — but as the theory itself teaches, waiting is only optimal until someone else moves first.
