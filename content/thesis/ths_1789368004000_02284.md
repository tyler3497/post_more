# Neural Radiance Fields and 3D Gaussian Splatting: Volumetric Rendering and Differentiable View Synthesis

## Abstract

Neural radiance fields (NeRF) [1] reframed novel view synthesis as the optimization of a continuous volumetric scene function, supervised only by posed photographs through differentiable volume rendering. Their successor, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [2], replaces the implicit multilayer perceptron with an explicit set of anisotropic 3D Gaussians and a differentiable tile-based rasterizer, reaching real-time frame rates at 1080p with state-of-the-art quality. This article develops a unified mathematical treatment of both paradigms: the emission-absorption volume rendering integral and its quadrature, positional encodings and their anti-aliased extensions [3] [4] [7], hash-grid accelerations [5], explicit voxel alternatives [6], and the covariance projection at the heart of Gaussian splatting. We contrast the optimization dynamics of implicit neural representations against explicit primitives, survey empirical quality and efficiency trade-offs across standard benchmarks, and identify open limitations in view extrapolation, dynamic scenes, and geometry extraction that define the current research frontier.

## 2 Background

### The plenoptic function and radiance fields

The appearance of a static scene is completely described, in the absence of participating media, by the *plenoptic function*: radiance as a function of 3D position and 2D viewing direction. NeRF's core insight was to treat this 5D function as the learnable object itself, parameterized by a neural network [1]. Where classical geometry pipelines reconstruct explicit surfaces and then shade them, radiance fields skip surfaces entirely: geometry emerges implicitly as regions of high volume density.

### Classical volume rendering

Long before neural rendering, graphics had a mature theory of light transport through participating media. Under the emission-absorption model — no scattering, no shadows — the radiance observed along a ray is governed by a first-order ordinary differential equation whose closed-form solution is

> **Theorem:** For a ray parameterized by distance *t*, with volume density *sigma(t)* and emitted radiance *c(t)*, the observed color equals the integral over the ray of *T(t) sigma(t) c(t)*, where the transmittance *T(t)* is the exponential of the negative cumulative density from the ray origin to *t*.

This single equation is the entire rendering model of NeRF: every pixel color is that integral, and every gradient used in training flows backward through it. NeRF's contribution was not the integral — known since the 1980s — but the realization that it is *differentiable* with respect to a neural parameterization of *sigma* and *c*, so that ordinary photographs become supervision for a 3D representation.

### Image-based rendering lineage

NeRF did not emerge from a vacuum. Key predecessors include:

- **Light field rendering and the Lumigraph**, which sampled the plenoptic function densely with camera arrays but required enormous capture setups.
- **DeepVoxels and Scene Representation Networks**, which learned 3D-aware latent volumes or signed-distance-like functions but struggled with photorealism at scale.
- **Local Light Field Fusion**, which blended multiplane images for impressive but view-limited synthesis.

What distinguished NeRF was the combination of a *continuous* coordinate-based representation, classical volume compositing, and a positional encoding that let a modest MLP represent high-frequency detail.

### Spectral bias and positional encoding

Coordinate MLPs suffer *spectral bias*: they learn low frequencies first and may never capture fine detail. NeRF's remedy lifts each scalar coordinate through sinusoids at exponentially increasing frequencies before the MLP [1] — a cheap, parameter-free Fourier mapping equivalent to endowing the network with a stationary kernel of controllable bandwidth.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Neural radiance fields

A NeRF is a function *F* parameterized by weights *theta*:

- **Input:** a 3D position *x* and a unit viewing direction *d*.
- **Output:** a view-independent volume density *sigma* (nonnegative scalar) and a view-dependent RGB radiance *c*.

The network is split so that density depends only on position while color additionally sees the viewing direction, enforcing the physical prior that geometry is view-independent.

Rendering a pixel proceeds by marching a ray from the camera center through the pixel, sampling *N* points along it, querying the network at each, and approximating the volume rendering integral with a quadrature rule (derived in Section 4.1). Training minimizes the mean squared error between rendered and observed pixel colors over random ray batches, with a coarse-to-fine hierarchical sampling scheme: a first "coarse" network proposes where matter lies along each ray, and a second "fine" network concentrates its samples there [1].

```python
import numpy as np

def positional_encoding(p, L=10):
    """Lift coordinates into a Fourier feature bank.
    p: (..., 3) array of positions; returns (..., 6*L) features."""
    freqs = (2.0 ** np.arange(L)) * np.pi
    angles = p[..., None] * freqs
    return np.concatenate([np.sin(angles), np.cos(angles)], axis=-1)

def render_ray(model, origin, direction, near, far, n_samples=128):
    """Differentiable volume rendering of one ray (numpy sketch)."""
    ts = np.linspace(near, far, n_samples)
    pts = origin[None, :] + ts[:, None] * direction[None, :]
    sigmas, colors = model(pts, direction)          # F_theta queries
    deltas = np.diff(ts, append=far)                # segment lengths
    alphas = 1.0 - np.exp(-sigmas * deltas)         # opacity per segment
    trans = np.concatenate([[1.0], 1.0 - alphas[:-1]])
    T = np.cumprod(trans)                           # transmittance
    weights = T * alphas
    return weights @ colors, weights.sum()          # pixel color, opacity
```

### 3.2 3D Gaussian splatting

3DGS abandons the MLP. The scene is an explicit, unstructured set of 3D Gaussians, each carrying:

1. A mean position *mu* in world space.
2. A 3-by-3 covariance matrix *Sigma*, parameterized as *R S S* transposed *R* transposed with rotation *R* and diagonal scale *S* — guaranteeing positive semi-definiteness during optimization.
3. An opacity scalar *alpha*.
4. View-dependent color via spherical-harmonic coefficients.

Rendering projects each 3D Gaussian to a 2D screen-space Gaussian. Under a local affine approximation of the projective transform, the projected covariance is

*Sigma'* = *J W Sigma W* transposed *J* transposed,

where *W* is the viewing transformation and *J* the Jacobian of the projective mapping [2]. Each projected splat is then composited front-to-back within screen tiles, using the *same* alpha-blending mathematics as NeRF's quadrature — but evaluated by a rasterizer rather than a ray marcher. Because the rasterizer processes Gaussians in depth order per tile and is fully differentiable, gradients flow back to positions, covariances, opacities, and colors.

Optimization interleaves gradient steps with *adaptive density control*: Gaussians whose view-space positional gradients are large are densified — small ones cloned, large ones split — while near-transparent Gaussians are pruned. This lets the representation grow detail where the photometric error demands it, typically reaching a few million Gaussians for a complex scene.

### 3.3 What changed, and what did not

It is tempting to view 3DGS as a rejection of NeRF. More precisely, it is a change of *basis* and *integrator*:

| Aspect | NeRF [1] | 3D Gaussian Splatting [2] |
|---|---|---|
| Scene representation | MLP weights (implicit) | Gaussian parameters (explicit) |
| Integration | Ray marching + quadrature | Projective splatting + rasterization |
| Compositing math | Alpha blending along ray | Alpha blending in depth order |
| View dependence | Direction-conditioned MLP head | Spherical harmonics |
| Initialization | Random weights | SfM point cloud |
| Rendering speed | Seconds per frame | 100+ FPS at 1080p |

The compositing equations are essentially identical; the revolution is computational. Rasterization exploits GPU hardware designed for exactly this workload, while ray marching an MLP cannot.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The volume rendering integral and its quadrature

Consider a ray *r(t) = o + t d* traversing the scene from a near plane to a far plane. Under emission-absorption, radiance evolves according to *dL/dt = -sigma(t) L(t) + sigma(t) c(t)*: existing light is attenuated by density while each volume element contributes its own emission. Solving this linear ODE with an integrating factor yields the transmittance-weighted integral stated in Section 2.

For computation, the ray is partitioned into segments over which *sigma* and *c* are treated as constant — exactly the values returned by one network query per segment. On a constant segment, the integral has a closed form, and chaining segments gives the familiar compositing recurrence: each segment contributes its color weighted by its opacity *alpha_i = 1 - exp(-sigma_i delta_i)* times the transmittance *T_i*, the product of one-minus-alpha over all earlier segments. Two subtleties deserve emphasis. First, *numerical stability*: when *sigma_i delta_i* is large, the exponential underflows gracefully to zero, and when tiny, its first-order expansion keeps gradients alive — which is what makes the quadrature well-behaved across the enormous dynamic range of learned densities. Second, *quadrature error acts as blur*: the piecewise-constant approximation low-passes the signal. Hierarchical sampling [1] mitigates this by concentrating samples where the integrand varies most, but the residual blur is one motivation for the conical treatment of Section 4.2.

### 4.2 Positional encoding, aliasing, and conical frustums

NeRF casts one infinitesimal ray per pixel, yet a pixel integrates light over a finite footprint. When training and test views observe the same surface at different distances, this mismatch produces the classic symptoms of aliasing: shimmering, jaggies, and blurred detail [3]. The brute-force remedy — supersampling with many rays per pixel — multiplies the already heavy MLP cost.

Mip-NeRF's elegant solution replaces rays with *conical frustums* and replaces point queries with integrated ones [3]. The positional encoding of a Gaussian-distributed region has a closed form: the expected sinusoid under a Gaussian is the sinusoid at the mean, attenuated by an exponential of the variance times the squared frequency. High frequencies are therefore automatically suppressed for large footprints — a continuous, scale-aware low-pass filter, analogous to mipmapping in texture rendering. The same machinery extends to unbounded scenes in mip-NeRF 360 via a scene contraction that maps all of space into a bounded ball, plus a distortion regularizer that concentrates each ray's weight distribution to suppress floaters [4]. Zip-NeRF later ported these anti-aliasing ideas to fast grid-based representations, multisampling the hash grid within each frustum to combine the speed of Instant NGP with the scale-awareness of mip-NeRF 360 [7].

```haskell
-- Covariance projection for Gaussian splatting (Haskell sketch).
-- Sigma' = J * W * Sigma * W^T * J^T, evaluated with 3x3 matrices.
type Mat3 = ((Double,Double,Double),(Double,Double,Double),(Double,Double,Double))

projectCov :: Mat3 -> Mat3 -> Mat3 -> Mat3
projectCov j w sigma =
    let m1 = matMul j (matMul w sigma)
        m2 = matMul m1 (transpose3 w)
    in matMul m2 (transpose3 j)
-- The 2x2 upper-left block of the result is the screen-space
-- covariance used by the tile rasterizer; the third row and column
-- are discarded after the affine approximation.
```

### 4.3 Differentiable Gaussian rasterization

Splatting has a long history in point-based graphics, notably elliptical weighted average (EWA) surface splatting, which derived the same covariance projection decades earlier for rendering point clouds without connectivity. 3DGS's contribution is making the *entire* pipeline differentiable and coupling it with gradient-driven densification [2].

The rasterizer's design is a study in GPU-aware algorithmics:

1. **Frustum culling and projection.** Gaussians outside the view frustum are discarded; the rest are projected with the affine covariance approximation.
2. **Tiling.** The screen is divided into 16-by-16 tiles. Each Gaussian is binned into the tiles its projected ellipse overlaps, and per tile the overlapping Gaussians are sorted by depth — radix sort on GPU, fast enough to redo every iteration.
3. **Alpha compositing.** Within a tile, each pixel accumulates splats front-to-back with the same *T_i alpha_i c_i* recurrence as NeRF's quadrature, stopping early once transmittance saturates.

Differentiability through sorting deserves a remark: the depth order is treated as fixed within each forward-backward pass, so gradients flow through the compositing weights but not through the permutation itself. In practice the ordering changes slowly relative to parameter updates, and the approximation is benign — though popping artifacts under large viewpoint changes reveal where it breaks.

### 4.4 Optimization dynamics: implicit networks versus explicit primitives

The two paradigms optimize in qualitatively different landscapes:

- **NeRF's MLP** is a global, smooth function approximator. Spectral bias gives it a natural coarse-to-fine behavior — low frequencies first — which stabilizes early training but can trap it in blurry local minima.
- **Instant NGP's hash grid** [5] trades the MLP's global smoothness for a multiresolution hash table of feature vectors decoded by a tiny network. Hash collisions are resolved by gradient averaging across scales, collapsing training from days to seconds at the cost of smoothness.
- **Plenoxels** [6] pushed explicitness further still: a sparse voxel grid storing density and spherical-harmonic coefficients, optimized directly with no neural network at all, reaching competitive quality in minutes. It foreshadowed 3DGS's lesson that the *differentiable renderer*, not the neural network, is the indispensable component.
- **3DGS's Gaussians** are local primitives with compact support. Optimization is spatially local — moving one Gaussian barely affects distant pixels — which makes gradients well-conditioned and densification an effective form of adaptive capacity allocation. The price is parameter count: millions of Gaussians consume hundreds of megabytes, versus a few megabytes for a NeRF MLP.

A unifying view: all four methods minimize the same photometric loss through the same compositing equations; they differ only in the *basis functions* spanning the radiance field and the *integrator* projecting them to pixels. Progress in the field has largely been progress in choosing bases and integrators that match GPU hardware.

## 5 Empirical Results

Representative figures reported in the literature, on the standard synthetic benchmark introduced with NeRF and the unbounded real-world benchmark introduced with mip-NeRF 360, illustrate the quality-efficiency trade-off. Values below are rounded from the respective papers and should be read as indicative rather than exact, since evaluation protocols differ slightly across works.

| Method | Synthetic PSNR | Mip-NeRF 360 PSNR | Train time | Render speed (1080p) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NeRF [1] | 31.0 | — | 1–2 days | ~0.05 FPS |
| Mip-NeRF [3] | ~33.0 | — | ~1 day | ~0.05 FPS |
| Instant NGP [5] | ~33.2 | ~28.5 | ~5 min | ~10 FPS |
| Plenoxels [6] | ~31.7 | — | ~11 min | ~15 FPS |
| Mip-NeRF 360 [4] | — | 29.2 | many hours | ~0.06 FPS |
| Zip-NeRF [7] | — | ~30.4 | ~6 hours | ~1 FPS |
| 3D Gaussian Splatting [2] | ~33.3 | ~29.4 | ~40 min | ~135 FPS |

Several observations follow:

1. **Quality has converged.** On both benchmarks, the best methods sit within roughly one PSNR decibel of each other; perceptual metrics (SSIM, LPIPS) tell the same story. The representation debate is no longer about who renders the sharpest still image.
2. **Speed is the differentiator.** 3DGS renders three orders of magnitude faster than NeRF at comparable quality — the gap that moved radiance fields from offline visualization into interactive applications.
3. **Training time collapsed in two waves.** First the hash-grid and voxel methods [5] [6] cut days to minutes while keeping ray marching; then 3DGS [2] kept training under an hour while making *rendering* real-time.
4. **Anti-aliasing matters most at scale.** The mip-NeRF lineage [3] [4] [7] gains most on multiscale and unbounded captures, where naive point sampling breaks down.
5. **No free lunch on memory.** 3DGS's speed is bought with hundreds of megabytes of Gaussians per scene, versus single-digit megabytes for MLP weights — a real constraint for streaming and mobile deployment.

## 6 Limitations

Despite the remarkable progress, fundamental limitations remain:

- **View extrapolation is fragile.** All these methods interpolate the observed views; cameras far outside the capture trajectory yield cloudy artifacts and hallucinated geometry that no purely reconstructive method fixes reliably.
- **Dynamics are second-class.** The formulations above assume a static scene. Extending them to moving subjects requires either per-frame optimization, deformation fields, or 4D primitives — each multiplying cost and complexity, and fast motion still defeats most approaches.
- **Geometry is a byproduct, not a product.** Density fields and Gaussian soups render beautifully but extract poorly: marching-cubes meshes from NeRF densities are noisy, and Gaussian primitives have no well-defined surface. Applications needing CAD-quality geometry still prefer signed-distance formulations.
- **Reflective and transmissive surfaces** violate the emission-absorption assumption. Mirrors and glass are approximated as view-dependent texture on phantom geometry; true multi-bounce transport is absent, so reflections of unobserved scene parts cannot be correct.
- **Memory and bandwidth** of explicit methods [2] [6] limit mobile deployment, while the fastest implicit methods [5] still demand high-end GPUs for real-time playback.
- **Editing and semantics** remain awkward: selecting, moving, or relighting part of a radiance field is far harder than editing a mesh, and coupling these representations with language or segmentation is an open interface problem.

## 7 Conclusion

In six years, novel view synthesis traveled from "a neural network that memorizes a scene" to real-time, editable-feeling radiance field rendering. The through-line of this article is that the journey was mathematical rather than architectural: the volume rendering integral of Section 2 never changed, and both NeRF [1] and 3D Gaussian Splatting [2] are best understood as different numerical schemes — different bases, different integrators — for the same underlying inverse problem. The milestones along the way each repaired a specific deficiency: positional encoding cured spectral bias, conical frustums cured aliasing [3], scene contraction and distortion regularization cured unbounded ambiguity [4], hash grids cured training time [5], explicit voxels questioned the need for networks at all [6], multisampled grids reunited speed with anti-aliasing [7], and projected Gaussians with a tile rasterizer finally cured rendering speed [2].

The frontier has consequently shifted. With quality saturated and rendering real-time, the open problems are *generative* (what should an unobserved region look like?), *dynamic* (how do fields move?), *geometric* (how do we extract usable surfaces?), and *semantic* (how do we edit and query these fields?). Whatever answers emerge, they will almost certainly be expressed, once again, as differentiable approximations to integrals of light.
