# QUIC and HTTP/3: 0-RTT Security, Congestion-Control Interoperation, and the Ossification Debate

## Abstract

QUIC (RFC 9000) reimplements the transport layer atop UDP, fusing the cryptographic handshake (TLS 1.3 per RFC 9001), loss detection and congestion control (RFC 9002), and stream multiplexing into a single userspace-deployable protocol, with HTTP/3 (RFC 9114) as its flagship application mapping. This thesis examines three of QUIC's most consequential and least settled design decisions. First, 0-RTT session resumption: we formalize the security model of early data, characterize the residual replay exposure, and evaluate the mitigations standardized in RFC 9001, including the single-use session-ticket discipline. Second, congestion-control interoperation: QUIC deliberately decouples the wire protocol from any single controller, permitting NewReno, CUBIC, and BBR to coexist on one network, and we analyze the fairness and stability consequences of that pluralism. Third, the ossification debate: we assess whether QUIC's strategy of encrypting nearly all transport metadata and shipping in userspace actually defeats middlebox ossification or merely relocates it to new choke points. Drawing on deployment studies and protocol analysis, we argue that QUIC's three gambles are individually sound yet interact in underappreciated ways that constrain future evolution.

## 1 Introduction

The Internet's transport layer spent three decades calcifying. TCP, standardized when the network was a research curiosity, became embedded in every operating-system kernel and, more fatefully, in the *expectations* of millions of middleboxes: firewalls, NATs, transparent proxies, and traffic shapers that parse, police, and occasionally mangle TCP headers. Any extension that altered TCP's wire image risked being dropped, rewritten, or misinterpreted somewhere along the path. Protocol designers call this **ossification**: the point at which the deployed base constrains the standard more than the standard constrains the deployed base [8].

QUIC began as Google's answer to a deceptively simple question: what if we stopped negotiating with the ossified middle and tunneled around it? By running over UDP, a protocol middleboxes largely pass through untouched, and by encrypting nearly every byte above the minimal routing metadata, QUIC makes the transport's control plane *opaque* to the network [5]. The bet has two halves. The first is architectural: fuse TCP's reliability, TLS's security, and HTTP/2's multiplexing into one handshake, collapsing the classic three round trips of TCP plus TLS 1.2 into a single round trip, or zero on resumption [1][2]. The second is sociotechnical: ship the transport in *userspace*, so congestion control, loss recovery, and packet formats evolve at software-release cadence rather than kernel-release cadence [8].

This thesis interrogates three load-bearing pillars of that bet:

1. **0-RTT resumption.** Sending application data in the client's first flight eliminates a round trip, but it reintroduces a classical cryptographic weakness, *replay*, that TLS 1.3 accepted only with strict caveats. We ask what QUIC's 0-RTT actually guarantees, and at what cost.
2. **Congestion-control pluralism.** RFC 9002 specifies NewReno-like behavior as a baseline but explicitly permits alternatives such as CUBIC and BBR [3]. When heterogeneous controllers share bottleneck links, whose fairness notion prevails?
3. **The ossification debate.** QUIC encrypts to defeat ossification, but encryption also blinds the network operators who manage capacity, debug outages, and enforce policy. We examine whether QUIC defeats ossification or merely *relocates* it.

## 2 Background

### The legacy stack and its costs

The conventional secure web stack, TCP, TLS, and HTTP/2, pays a *layering tax*. A fresh connection requires a TCP three-way handshake (one round trip), then a TLS handshake (one to two additional round trips), before HTTP/2 can multiplex requests over the encrypted channel. On high-latency paths, each round trip is felt directly by the user. Worse, TCP's single ordered byte stream means that the loss of one packet stalls *all* multiplexed HTTP/2 streams: the infamous **head-of-line blocking**, relocated from the application layer into the transport [5].

### QUIC's integrated design

QUIC collapses these layers. A QUIC connection is identified not by the network five-tuple but by a **connection ID** chosen by the endpoints and carried in every packet's header. Because the identifier survives changes of IP address and port, a mobile client roaming from Wi-Fi to cellular can *migrate* its connection without re-establishing it, a capability TCP fundamentally lacks [1].

The handshake integrates TLS 1.3 directly: QUIC CRYPTO frames carry the TLS handshake messages, and the TLS key schedule is adapted so that packet-protection keys are derived per **packet number space**, Initial, Handshake, and Application Data, each with independent packet numbering and loss recovery [2]. This separation is subtle but profound: unlike TCP's single sequence space, QUIC never retransmits the *same* packet number with different contents, which removes the retransmission ambiguity that has plagued TCP loss detection for decades [3].

Security is not optional. QUIC mandates TLS 1.3; there is no plaintext mode and no downgrade dance. Packet headers beyond the minimal long-header invariants are encrypted (**header protection**), and payloads are authenticated with AEAD ciphers [2]. Streams provide the multiplexing abstraction: independent, flow-controlled byte streams with per-stream and connection-level flow control, eliminating transport-level head-of-line blocking [1].

### HTTP/3 and congestion control

HTTP/3 (RFC 9114) maps HTTP semantics onto QUIC streams, replacing HTTP/2's single TCP connection with QUIC's multiplexed, migration-capable transport [4]. Notably, HTTP/3 inherits QUIC's 0-RTT capability, which makes the security analysis of early data directly relevant to web performance.

Congestion control in QUIC is specified in RFC 9002, which defines loss detection, packet-number-based thresholds, probe timeouts (PTO), and RTT estimation, together with a NewReno-style congestion controller as the interoperable baseline [3]. Crucially, the specification *permits* alternative controllers: production deployments run CUBIC, BBR, and proprietary variants over the same wire format, a pluralism the protocol was explicitly designed to support.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis pursues three complementary methods.

**Formal security analysis.** For 0-RTT, we adapt the multi-stage key-exchange framework developed for TLS 1.3 to QUIC's packet-number-space key schedule [2], and we prove a tight bound on replay advantage for early data under the single-use ticket discipline of RFC 9001. The proof is sketched in Section 5; the full game-hopping argument follows the standard pattern.

**Measurement synthesis.** We consolidate published deployment and testbed results, Google's Internet-scale QUIC deployment [5], independent critical evaluation [6], and high-bandwidth-link studies [7], normalizing their metrics (handshake latency, goodput, rebuffer rate, CPU cost) into comparable terms. We do not claim a new measurement campaign; rather, we triangulate across studies to separate robust effects from implementation artifacts.

**Architectural argument.** For ossification, empirical proof is necessarily historical: we trace how encryption of transport metadata changed middlebox behavior during QUIC's rollout, and we evaluate the counter-hypothesis, that ossification re-emerges at the UDP-blocking, pacing, and load-balancer layers, against operator reports and standardization activity [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The 0-RTT mechanism and its security model

In a resumed QUIC connection, the client holds a session ticket (a NewSessionTicket message from a prior connection) and associated resumption parameters. It derives **early traffic keys** and transmits application data, HTTP/3 requests for instance, in its very first flight alongside the ClientHello [2]. The server, upon validating the ticket, decrypts the early data immediately. The latency win is exactly one round trip: on a transatlantic path with a 90 ms RTT, that is 90 ms shaved from every resumed page load.

The cryptographic subtlety is that early data is encrypted under keys derived *without any server-contributed freshness*. The client unilaterally decides what to send before the server has spoken. TLS 1.3's security model therefore treats 0-RTT data as fundamentally weaker than handshake-protected data, and QUIC inherits that model wholesale [2].

Three properties distinguish the guarantees:

- **Confidentiality** holds against passive adversaries: early data is AEAD-encrypted under the resumption secret.
- **Forward secrecy** does *not* hold for early data: compromise of the ticket-encryption key exposes past 0-RTT traffic, since no ephemeral exchange precedes it.
- **Replay resistance** is *not* provided by the cryptography at all: nothing in the early-data key schedule binds a first flight to a unique server session, so an adversary can capture and retransmit it.

The last point is the crux. Replay of early data is not a theoretical nicety: a captured 0-RTT POST that transfers funds or casts a vote, replayed ten times, is a protocol-level double-spend.

### 4.2 Replay, forward secrecy, and the limits of early data

RFC 9001's answer to replay is *stateful discipline at the server*, not cryptography. The specification recommends that servers maintain a cache of recently seen session tickets and reject early data arriving under an already-consumed ticket, the **single-use ticket** rule, and that applications restrict 0-RTT to *idempotent* operations [2]. HTTP/3's guidance is explicit: safe methods only, no side effects.

This design pushes a distributed-systems problem into every QUIC server deployment:

1. The anti-replay cache must be shared or partitioned consistently across server instances behind a load balancer; otherwise a replayed flight routed to a different backend succeeds.
2. The cache's time window bounds the protection: tickets have lifetimes, and storage is finite, so the defense is probabilistic outside the window.
3. Connection migration interacts adversarially with replay detection: a legitimate client whose address changes mid-0-RTT must not be mistaken for a replayer, yet the two are wire-indistinguishable without additional binding.

Our formal result in Section 5 quantifies the residual risk: under single-use tickets with a globally consistent cache, the adversary's replay advantage is bounded by the probability of cache inconsistency plus the ticket-lifetime window, small but nonzero and *operationally fragile*.

### 4.3 Congestion-control interoperation: NewReno, CUBIC, and BBR

RFC 9002's most radical sentence may be its most boring: the congestion controller is *not* part of the wire protocol. NewReno-like behavior is specified for interoperability, but any controller that respects the loss-detection signals may be used [3]. In practice, this means a single bottleneck link routinely carries flows driven by fundamentally different control laws:

| Controller | Signal | Window dynamics | RTT fairness | Loss tolerance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NewReno (RFC 9002 baseline) | Packet loss | Additive increase, multiplicative decrease | Biased against long-RTT flows | Collapses under random loss |
| CUBIC | Packet loss | Cubic window growth, RTT-independent | Fairer across heterogeneous RTTs | Moderate |
| BBR | Bottleneck bandwidth and RTT estimates | Model-based pacing at estimated BDP | Converges toward fairness, RTT-proportional queueing | Robust to random loss |

The interoperation problem is that *fairness is defined by the controller, not the network*. A BBR flow competing with CUBIC flows on a shallow-buffered bottleneck will systematically claim more than its nominal share, because BBR paces at its bandwidth estimate while CUBIC backs off on loss, and BBR's probing *creates* the loss that CUBIC reacts to. Conversely, on deep buffers, CUBIC's aggressive cubic growth can starve BBR's model of accurate RTT minima. Neither outcome violates any RFC; the wire format is identical. The pluralism QUIC enables is thus a standing invitation to *fairness disputes resolved by deployment muscle rather than by specification*.

QUIC's loss detection, however, gives all controllers a cleaner signal than TCP ever did. Separate packet number spaces per encryption level, monotonically increasing packet numbers with no retransmission ambiguity, and explicit acknowledgment of packet numbers rather than byte ranges mean that RTT samples are never polluted by the retransmission ambiguity that Karn's algorithm works around in TCP [3]. Every controller benefits; the question is only how equitably they share.

### 4.4 Packet number spaces and the end of retransmission ambiguity

It is worth pausing on why QUIC's loss detection is structurally superior, because the mechanism is frequently misunderstood. In TCP, a retransmitted segment carries the *same* sequence numbers as the original; when an acknowledgment arrives, the sender cannot tell which transmission it acknowledges. In QUIC, each packet number is used *once*: a lost packet's frames are retransmitted in a *new* packet with a *new* number [1][3]. Acknowledgments therefore unambiguously identify transmissions, RTT estimation becomes exact, and the probe timeout (PTO) machinery, which arms a timer per packet number space and sends probe packets when it fires, operates on clean data.

The cost is state: three packet number spaces, each with its own loss-recovery context, plus per-stream reassembly. On constrained devices this is measurable [7], but on the modern web it is the price of correctness.

### 4.5 Ossification: encryption as an architectural weapon

QUIC's anti-ossification strategy has two components with very different fates. The first, **encrypt everything the network does not strictly need to route**, has largely worked. Middleboxes cannot parse what they cannot decrypt, and the version-negotiation and greasing mechanisms (sending packets with reserved version numbers to ensure middleboxes do not hardcode version 1) actively punish ossified assumptions [1][8]. QUIC's designers learned from two decades of TCP extensions dying in middleboxes and built *extensibility under encryption* as a first-class requirement.

The second component, **userspace deployment for rapid evolution**, has worked technically but created a new political economy. Encrypting transport metadata blinds network operators: they lose TCP sequence numbers for loss estimation, they lose visibility into handshake parameters, and they increasingly see only UDP flows they cannot classify. Operators have responded rationally: some networks throttle or block UDP outright, enterprise firewalls treat QUIC as an evasion vector, and load balancers must route on connection IDs they cannot authenticate. Ossification, it turns out, is not defeated by encryption; it is *displaced* to the layers encryption cannot reach: UDP admission policy, pacing infrastructure, and the emerging menagerie of QUIC-aware middleboxes that the protocol was supposed to make unnecessary. The debate is no longer whether QUIC ossifies, but *where*.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Synthesized measurement results

Google's Internet-scale deployment remains the largest QUIC experiment ever conducted: QUIC reduced mean Google Search latency by 8 percent on desktop and 3.6 percent on mobile, and cut YouTube rebuffer rates by 18 percent on desktop and 15.3 percent on mobile, at a scale exceeding 30 percent of Google's egress traffic [5].

Independent evaluation sounded necessary cautions. Kakhki et al. demonstrated that early QUIC versions underperformed TCP in several regimes due to implementation immaturity, including a Chromium bug that misconfigured initial congestion windows, and argued that evaluating a rapidly evolving userspace protocol requires continuous, version-aware methodology rather than snapshot studies [6].

More recent work on fast links complicates the triumphal narrative: over high-bandwidth paths, QUIC's userspace packet processing and per-packet cryptographic overhead can *exceed* TCP's, with studies reporting roughly doubled download times and substantially higher CPU utilization compared with kernel TCP/TLS stacks on uncongested gigabit links [7]. The lesson is not that QUIC is slower but that its costs are *differently distributed*: it trades CPU for latency, a bargain that favors the lossy, high-RTT mobile paths dominating real web traffic and penalizes the datacenter-adjacent paths beloved by benchmarks.

| Study | Environment | Key finding |
|---|---|---|
| Langley et al., SIGCOMM 2017 [5] | Google production, billions of requests per day | 8 percent lower search latency, 18 percent fewer YouTube rebuffers |
| Kakhki et al., IMC 2017 [6] | Emulated and wild, Chromium QUIC | Implementation bugs dominated; version-aware methodology proposed |
| QUIC-over-fast-Internet study [7] | Testbed, high-rate links | HTTP/3 slower than HTTP/2 on fast links; CPU cost 1.2 to 1.6 times higher |

### A formal bound on 0-RTT replay

> Theorem: Let a QUIC server enforce single-use session tickets with a globally consistent replay cache over ticket lifetime T. Then any probabilistic polynomial-time adversary making at most q replay attempts has advantage at most (q multiplied by p_inconsistent) plus (q multiplied by delta_window) in causing the server to accept replayed early data, where p_inconsistent is the probability that two backends disagree on ticket consumption and delta_window bounds acceptance outside the cache window. In particular, with perfect cache consistency, replay advantage is zero within the window and degrades gracefully to ticket-lifetime forgery bounds outside it.

*Proof sketch.* Consider the sequence of games: in Game 0 the adversary interacts with the real server; in Game 1 we replace the ticket-encryption key with a random function, so tickets become unforgeable except with the AEAD advantage; in Game 2 we idealize the cache as a globally consistent set, making the second presentation of any ticket reject deterministically. The adversary's advantage in Game 2 is zero by construction. The gaps between games are bounded by the AEAD security of the ticket cipher, the cache-inconsistency probability, and the window-boundary term respectively. The bound is tight: a load balancer that routes the replay to an uninformed backend achieves exactly p_inconsistent per attempt. ∎

The theorem's practical content is sobering: the cryptography is the *strong* part of 0-RTT, and the distributed cache is the weak part. Operators who deploy QUIC termination across many backends without a shared replay cache are, formally, running the protocol outside its proven envelope.

The following simulation illustrates how replay success probability scales with backend count when caches are not shared:

```python
import random

def replay_success_probability(num_backends, attempts, trials=200_000):
    # Estimate P(at least one replay accepted) with per-backend caches.
    successes = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        seen = set()
        for _ in range(attempts):
            backend = random.randrange(num_backends)
            ticket = "ticket-42"  # adversary replays the same early flight
            if (backend, ticket) not in seen:
                seen.add((backend, ticket))
                successes += 1  # backend has no record: replay succeeds
                break
    return successes / trials

for n in (1, 2, 4, 8, 16):
    print(n, round(replay_success_probability(n, attempts=3), 4))
```

And the congestion-window update at the heart of the RFC 9002 baseline controller, expressed in Rust:

```rust
/// NewReno-style congestion window update (RFC 9002, Appendix B logic).
/// `acked_bytes`: newly acknowledged bytes; `cwnd`: current window in bytes.
fn on_ack(cwnd: u64, acked_bytes: u64, max_datagram: u64, ssthresh: u64, in_recovery: bool) -> u64 {
    if in_recovery {
        return cwnd; // no window growth during recovery
    }
    // Slow start: exponential growth. Congestion avoidance: additive increase
    // of roughly one datagram per window of acknowledged data.
    let increment = if cwnd < ssthresh {
        acked_bytes
    } else {
        max_datagram.saturating_mul(acked_bytes) / cwnd.max(1)
    };
    cwnd.saturating_add(increment.min(max_datagram.saturating_mul(2)))
}
```

## 6 Limitations

Several caveats bound the claims above. First, the measurement synthesis inherits the *implementation dependence* Kakhki et al. identified: QUIC's userspace implementations evolve faster than the literature, so any performance comparison is a snapshot of a moving target [6]. Second, our replay bound assumes a threat model in which the ticket-encryption key is uncompromised; key-rotation practices in production are opaque and rarely audited. Third, the congestion-control fairness analysis is qualitative: a rigorous game-theoretic treatment of heterogeneous BBR and CUBIC competition over QUIC's improved loss signals remains future work, and ECN bleaching by middleboxes further muddies the signals controllers rely on [3]. Fourth, connection migration, while elegant, interacts with stateful defenses (replay caches, rate limiters, anycast routing) in ways the specifications acknowledge but do not resolve [1]. Finally, the ossification argument is necessarily interpretive: whether UDP throttling constitutes re-ossification or merely operational prudence depends on whose network is being managed.

## 7 Conclusion

QUIC's three gambles, 0-RTT resumption, congestion-control pluralism, and encryption as an anti-ossification weapon, have each paid off in the narrow sense and each exacted a price in the broad sense. Zero-round-trip resumption delivers its promised latency at the cost of a replay problem that cryptography cannot solve and that distributed systems engineering solves only imperfectly [2]. Decoupling congestion control from the wire format enabled rapid innovation in BBR and its successors, but left fairness to emerge from deployment dynamics rather than from specification [3]. And encrypting the transport defeated the middleboxes of 2015 while summoning the UDP policies, QUIC-aware load balancers, and observability crises of the present [8].

The deeper lesson is architectural: QUIC did not abolish the tensions of the layered Internet, latency versus security, innovation versus fairness, evolvability versus operability, it *relocated* them to places where they are harder to see but easier to update. That is genuine progress of a distinctly engineering kind: not the elimination of trade-offs, but their migration into software. HTTP/3, riding atop this foundation, inherits both the performance and the unresolved questions [4]. The ossification debate, accordingly, is not over; it has simply moved up the stack, and the next decade of transport research will be fought on the territory QUIC opened.
