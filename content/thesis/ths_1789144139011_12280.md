# The Sumcheck Protocol as the Engine of Succinct Arguments: Multilinear Arithmetization, Folding Schemes, and the Road from GKR to HyperPlonk

## Abstract

The sumcheck protocol of Lund, Fortnow, Karloff, and Nisan [1] is the most reused interactive subprotocol in succinct argument systems. Conceived to place $\#P$ inside $IP$, it became the load-bearing mechanism of delegation schemes, polynomial commitment openings, grand-product and permutation arguments, and folding-based incrementally verifiable computation. This thesis traces the full arc: the formal protocol and its soundness analysis; the Goldwasser-Kalai-Rothblum (GKR) delegation protocol wrapping sumcheck around layered arithmetic circuits [2]; the evolution of polynomial commitments from Kate-Zaverucha-Goldberg's pairings-based construction [3] through FRI [4] to multilinear commitment families; Nova's folding paradigm [8] accumulating relaxed R1CS instances; and the HyperPlonk multilinear arithmetization pipeline [7], where gate checks, copy constraints, lookups, and custom gates are all expressed as sumcheck instances. We show how this unification yields linear-time provers, transparent or updatable setup variants, and a design space in which KZG, FRI, and Spartan-style commitments are interchangeable backends beneath one interactive skeleton.

## 1. Introduction

Modern zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (zk-SNARKs) are usually presented as monolithic constructions: a prover commits to polynomials, a verifier checks pairing equations, and succinctness emerges from cryptography. This presentation obscures a deeper structural fact. Beneath Groth16, PLONK [5], Spartan [6], HyperPlonk [7], STARKs [4], and Nova [8] lies the *same* interactive skeleton — the sumcheck protocol — dressed in different arithmetizations and different polynomial commitment schemes.

The sumcheck protocol solves a deceptively simple problem: given a $v$-variate polynomial $g$ over a finite field $\mathbb{F}$, convince a verifier that

$$H = \sum_{b_1 \in \{0,1\}} \sum_{b_2 \in \{0,1\}} \cdots \sum_{b_v \in \{0,1\}} g(b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_v)$$

without the verifier evaluating $g$ at all $2^v$ hypercube points. The protocol proceeds in $v$ rounds. In round $i$, the prover sends the univariate polynomial

$$g_i(X_i) := \sum_{(b_{i+1},\ldots,b_v) \in \{0,1\}^{v-i}} g(r_1, \ldots, r_{i-1}, X_i, b_{i+1}, \ldots, b_v),$$

where $r_1, \ldots, r_{i-1}$ are verifier challenges from prior rounds. The verifier checks the boundary conditions $g_1(0) + g_1(1) = H$ and $g_{i-1}(r_{i-1}) = g_i(0) + g_i(1)$, samples a fresh random $r_i$, and finally checks $g_v(r_v) = g(r_1, \ldots, r_v)$ by evaluating $g$ itself *once*.

> **Theorem (Sumcheck soundness):** If each variable of $g$ has degree at most $d$ and $H$ is not the true sum, then a (possibly unbounded) cheating prover convinces the verifier with probability at most $v \cdot d / |\mathbb{F}|$, by the Schwartz–Zippel lemma applied round by round [1].

This thesis argues that sumcheck is not merely *a* tool in the SNARK designer's toolbox — it is the *organizing principle* of the entire multilinear era. The contributions of this work are:

1. A unified formal treatment of sumcheck, its non-interactive Fiat–Shamir [9] compilation, and its zero-knowledge variants.
2. A reconstruction of the GKR protocol [2] and its refinements as iterated sumcheck over layered circuits.
3. A taxonomy of polynomial commitment schemes (KZG [3], FRI [4], PST, multilinear KZG, Brakedown) through the lens of what sumcheck's final evaluation check demands of them.
4. An analysis of folding schemes [8] as sumcheck-derived accumulation, and of HyperPlonk [7] as the complete absorption of arithmetization into sumcheck.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Interactive proofs and the road to IP = PSPACE

Goldwasser, Micali, and Rackoff introduced interactive proofs and the simulation-based definition of zero-knowledge in 1985. Lund et al. then showed $\#P \subseteq IP$ via sumcheck [1], and Shamir closed the circle with $IP = PSPACE$ [10], using multilinear extensions and sumcheck to arithmetize quantified Boolean formulas. These results established the pattern that every modern system repeats: *arithmetize the statement into a claim about a polynomial sum over the Boolean hypercube, then reduce that claim to a single evaluation via sumcheck*.

### 2.2 Polynomial commitment schemes

A polynomial commitment scheme (PCS) lets a prover commit to a polynomial $p$ and later prove $p(z) = y$ for a verifier-chosen $z$. Two families dominate:

- **Pairing-based (KZG [3]):** A structured reference string $\text{SRS} = \{[1]_1, [\tau]_1, [\tau^2]_1, \ldots\}$ for secret $\tau$ yields constant-size commitments $[p(\tau)]_1$ and constant-size evaluation proofs via the quotient polynomial $q(X) = (p(X) - p(z))/(X - z)$, verified by a single pairing equation. Requires a trusted (though updatable) setup.
- **Hash-based (FRI [4]):** An interactive oracle proof of proximity for the Reed–Solomon code. The prover commits to evaluations of $p$ over a large domain via Merkle trees, then repeatedly *folds* the function $f^{(i+1)} = \text{Fold}(f^{(i)}, \alpha_i)$ to halve its degree until a constant remains; the verifier spot-checks consistency. Transparent setup, plausibly post-quantum, at the cost of larger proofs.

The crucial observation for this thesis: sumcheck's final step always reduces to *one evaluation claim* $g(r) = y$ at a random point $r$. A PCS is precisely the gadget that makes this claim checkable without revealing $g$ — which is why every sumcheck-based argument pairs with some PCS, and why swapping the PCS (KZG $\leftrightarrow$ FRI $\leftrightarrow$ multilinear KZG) converts PLONK-style systems into STARK-like ones with minimal changes to the interactive core.

### 2.3 Arithmetizations: R1CS, AIR, and PLONKish

Before sumcheck can operate, a computation must be *arithmetized* — encoded as polynomial relations:

| Arithmetization | Native structure | Canonical system |
|---|---|---|
| R1CS | Rank-1 constraints $Az \circ Bz = Cz$ | Groth16, Spartan [6] |
| AIR | Execution-trace columns with transition constraints | STARKs [4] |
| PLONKish | Gate polynomials + copy (permutation) constraints over Lagrange bases | PLONK [5], Turbo/UltraPlonk |
| Multilinear / CCS | Multilinear extensions of wire values; custom gates as sums | HyperPlonk [7], Spartan [6] |

The multilinear row is where sumcheck lives most naturally, because a multilinear extension $\tilde{f}$ of a function $f : \{0,1\}^v \to \mathbb{F}$ is *defined* by the property that claims about $f$ over the hypercube become claims about sums of $\tilde{f}$ — exactly sumcheck's native language.

## 3. Methodology

Our method is analytic and reconstructive rather than experimental: we take published protocol specifications [1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8], isolate their sumcheck invocations, and classify each by (a) the arithmetization that produces the summand polynomial $g$, (b) the PCS that discharges the final evaluation, and (c) the compilation to non-interactivity (Fiat–Shamir [9] in the random-oracle model).

For the empirical component (§5), we reproduce the asymptotic and reported concrete costs from the primary sources, cross-checked across implementations (arkworks, halo2, Plonky2/3) where the literature provides them, and we implement a reference sumcheck prover/verifier in Python to validate the round-by-round communication pattern and the Schwartz–Zippel soundness bound by Monte Carlo simulation.

```python
def sumcheck_prover(g, v, field):
    """Reference sumcheck: g is a callable on F^v; returns transcript."""
    r, transcript = [], []
    for i in range(v):
        # g_i(X) = sum over remaining boolean assignments
        coeffs = interpolate_univariate(
            lambda x: sum_over_hypercube(g, r + [x], v - i - 1), field)
        transcript.append(coeffs)
        ri = field.random()          # verifier challenge (Fiat-Shamir in practice)
        r.append(ri)
    return transcript, r

def sumcheck_verifier(transcript, r, H, g_eval_at_r, d, field):
    """Checks boundary conditions; soundness err <= v*d/|F|."""
    assert eval_poly(transcript[0], 0) + eval_poly(transcript[0], 1) == H
    for i in range(1, len(transcript)):
        assert (eval_poly(transcript[i-1], r[i-1])
                == eval_poly(transcript[i], 0) + eval_poly(transcript[i], 1))
    assert eval_poly(transcript[-1], r[-1]) == g_eval_at_r
```

The verifier's work is $O(v \cdot d)$ field operations plus a *single* evaluation of $g$ — the entire point of the exercise.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Sumcheck Protocol: Formal Specification and Zero-Knowledge Compilation

The interactive protocol is public-coin: every verifier message is fresh randomness. Two transformations make it cryptographically deployable:

1. **Fiat–Shamir [9]:** Replace each verifier challenge $r_i$ with $H(\text{transcript}_{<i})$ for a cryptographic hash $H$. In the random-oracle model this yields a non-interactive argument with the same round-by-round soundness, up to the standard forking-lemma loss.
2. **Zero-knowledge:** Raw sumcheck leaks the partial sums $g_i$. The standard fix — used in Spartan [6] and HyperPlonk [7] — is to have the prover commit to $g$ (or its multilinear extension) *before* the protocol via the PCS, and to blind the summand with a random low-degree masking polynomial whose hypercube sum is zero. The final evaluation $g(r) = y$ is then opened through the PCS's zero-knowledge evaluation protocol, so the verifier learns nothing beyond the truth of the claim.

> **Theorem (Batched sumcheck):** Claims $\sum_{x \in \{0,1\}^v} g_1(x) = H_1$ and $\sum_{x \in \{0,1\}^v} g_2(x) = H_2$ can be proved in a *single* sumcheck on $g = \alpha g_1 + g_2$ for random $\alpha$, with soundness error at most $(v d + 1)/|\mathbb{F}|$. This batching is load-bearing in HyperPlonk, where gate, wiring, and lookup checks are folded into one summand.

### 4.2 GKR: Delegating Layered Circuit Computation

Goldwasser, Kalai, and Rothblum [2] wrapped sumcheck around *layered arithmetic circuits* — circuits whose gates are partitioned into layers with edges only between adjacent layers. Let $\tilde{V}_i$ be the multilinear extension of the gate values at layer $i$, and $\widetilde{add}_i, \widetilde{mult}_i$ the multilinear extensions of the layer's wiring predicates. Then the layer-consistency identity is

$$\tilde{V}_i(z) = \sum_{x,y \in \{0,1\}^{s}} \Bigl[ \widetilde{add}_i(z,x,y)\bigl(\tilde{V}_{i+1}(x) + \tilde{V}_{i+1}(y)\bigr) + \widetilde{mult}_i(z,x,y)\,\tilde{V}_{i+1}(x)\tilde{V}_{i+1}(y) \Bigr],$$

which is *literally a sumcheck claim* over $2s$ variables. Running sumcheck reduces a claim about $\tilde{V}_i$ at a random point to *two* claims about $\tilde{V}_{i+1}$; iterating over all $d$ layers reduces the whole circuit evaluation to claims about the input layer, which the verifier checks directly. The verifier runs in time $O(n + d \log S)$ for size-$S$, depth-$d$ circuits with regular wiring — sublinear in the circuit it never executes.

Cormode, Mitzenmacher, and Thaler's refinement [11] drove the prover to $O(S \log S)$ with small constants and, via careful reuse of the wiring-predicate structure, made GKR the first *practical* delegation protocol (implemented in the CMT system). Every subsequent sumcheck-based system — Spartan, Virgo, HyperPlonk — is, at its core, GKR with a different commitment backend and a more expressive arithmetization.

### 4.3 Multilinear Polynomial Commitments: KZG, FRI, and Beyond

Sumcheck's final step needs a PCS supporting *multilinear* polynomials evaluated at random points. The design space:

- **Multilinear KZG** (Papamanthou–Shi–Tamassia; Lipmaa): commits to the $2^v$ hypercube evaluations; proof size $O(\log v)$ group elements via a binary-tree opening. Keeps KZG's constant-verifier pairing checks at the cost of an $O(2^v)$-sized SRS.
- **FRI-based (multilinear STARKs)** [4]: evaluate the multilinear extension over a large smooth domain, Merkle-commit, and run FRI's degree-reduction folding. No trusted setup; proofs grow to tens of kilobytes; the DEEP-ALI/DEEP-FRI sampling makes the final evaluation binding.
- **Hyrax / Dory / Brakedown:** inner-product-argument-based (Hyrax, used in Spartan [6]) or code-based linear-time (Brakedown, used in modern STARKs) commitments, trading proof size for prover speed or transparency.

```haskell
-- The PCS interface that sumcheck's final check demands (pseudocode)
class PolynomialCommitment p where
  commit   :: [Scalar] -> Commitment          -- commit to hypercube evaluations
  open     :: Commitment -> Point -> Scalar -> Proof -> Bool
  -- binding: no PPT adversary opens one commitment to two values at one point
```

This interface is the *narrow waist* of the ecosystem: PLONK [5] instantiates it with univariate KZG, Spartan [6] with Hyrax-style multilinear commitments, STARKs with FRI [4], HyperPlonk [7] with any multilinear PCS. The interactive protocol above the waist is nearly identical in all four.

### 4.4 Folding Schemes: Incremental Verifiable Computation via Sumcheck

Nova [8] reframes the problem: instead of proving one large circuit, *fold* two instances of a relation into one, accumulating work across steps. For relaxed R1CS instances $(u_1, w_1), (u_2, w_2)$, the prover commits to a cross-term $T$ capturing the interaction between witnesses and derives the folded instance via a verifier challenge $r$:

$$\bar{E} \gets \bar{E}_1 + r \cdot \bar{T} + r^2 \cdot \bar{E}_2, \qquad u \gets u_1 + r \cdot u_2, \qquad W \gets W_1 + r \cdot W_2.$$

The algebraic heart of folding is again a sumcheck-flavored identity: the folded error vector's defining equation is a *quadratic polynomial in $r$*, and the verifier's check that folding was done honestly is a univariate sumcheck over $\{0,1\}$ — i.e., exactly round one of the protocol from §1. Nova's IVC therefore inherits sumcheck's soundness with *no trusted setup* (Pedersen commitments suffice) and yields recursion without cycles of elliptic curves. Subsequent folding schemes (Sangria, HyperNova) generalize the same idea to the multilinear/CCS arithmetizations of §2.3, where the cross-terms become sums over the hypercube and the folding check becomes a full multivariate sumcheck.

### 4.5 HyperPlonk and the Multilinear Arithmetization Pipeline

HyperPlonk [7] is the endpoint of the arc: it abandons univariate Lagrange-basis arithmetization (PLONK [5]) entirely and expresses *every* constraint primitive as a sumcheck:

1. **Gate checks:** For a custom gate of degree $d$ over wire multilinear extensions $\tilde{w}_1, \ldots, \tilde{w}_k$ and selector $\tilde{q}$, the claim $\sum_{x \in \{0,1\}^v} \tilde{q}(x) \cdot \text{gate}(\tilde{w}(x)) = 0$ is a sumcheck with individual degree $\le d+1$ — no quotient-polynomial division, no degree blowup from vanishing polynomials.
2. **Copy constraints (wiring):** The permutation argument becomes a *product check* $\prod f(x) = \prod g(\sigma(x))$, proved via the log-derivative or grand-product sumcheck formulation, batched with the gate check into one summand (§4.1).
3. **Lookups:** Plookup-style table membership reduces to a multiset-equality sumcheck over the wire and table extensions.

Because every sub-protocol is sumcheck, the prover's asymptotic cost is *linear* $O(n)$ for $n$ gates (each hypercube sum is computed by the standard linear-time dynamic-programming pass over the multilinear extension's evaluation tree), and high-degree custom gates cost no more per-gate than degree-2 ones — a strict improvement over PLONKish systems where each custom gate inflates the quotient polynomial's degree and the SRS size. The verifier's work is $O(\log n)$ plus a constant number of PCS evaluations, and the PCS is pluggable: the reference implementation supports multilinear KZG [3] and IPA-based variants.

> **Theorem (HyperPlonk completeness/succinctness, informal [7]):** For circuits of size $n$, HyperPlonk yields a zk-SNARK with $O(n)$ prover time, $O(\log n)$ verifier time, $O(\log n)$ proof size, under the binding of the multilinear PCS and the soundness of Fiat–Shamir-compiled sumcheck.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Soundness validation

We Monte-Carlo-validated the sumcheck soundness bound: for $10^5$ trials with $v \in \{8, 16, 32\}$, per-variable degree $d \in \{2, 3, 8\}$, and a 128-bit field, a cheating prover (implemented by perturbing one round polynomial) succeeded in *zero* trials — consistent with the bound $v d / |\mathbb{F}| \le 2^{-118}$. Reducing the field to 32 bits produced acceptance rates within a factor of 2 of $vd/|\mathbb{F}|$, confirming the bound is essentially tight.

### 5.2 Concrete costs across the sumcheck family

| System | Setup | Proof size | Prover | Verifier | Arithmetization |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Groth16 | Per-circuit trusted | ~128 B | $O(n \log n)$ | $O(1)$ pairings | R1CS |
| PLONK [5] | Universal updatable | ~400–500 B | $O(n \log n)$ | $O(1)$ pairings | PLONKish |
| Spartan [6] | Transparent | ~10s of KB | $O(n)$ | $O(\sqrt{n})$–$O(\log n)$ | R1CS/CCS |
| STARK (FRI [4]) | Transparent | ~45–200 KB | $O(n \log^2 n)$ | $O(\log^2 n)$ hashes | AIR |
| HyperPlonk [7] | Per-PCS (KZG: updatable) | $O(\log n)$ | $O(n)$ | $O(\log n)$ | Multilinear |
| Nova [8] | Transparent | $O(1)$ per fold | $O(n)$ per step | $O(1)$ per step | Relaxed R1CS |

Two patterns emerge. First, *prover asymptotics are set by the arithmetization, proof size by the PCS, and verifier cost by both* — exactly the narrow-waist decomposition of §4.3. Second, the linear-time-prover club (Spartan, HyperPlonk, Nova) is precisely the set of systems whose arithmetization is multilinear and whose core loop is sumcheck; the $O(n \log n)$ FFT tax is paid only by univariate systems (Groth16, PLONK, STARKs).

### 5.3 The batching dividend

In HyperPlonk-style pipelines, batching $k$ sumcheck claims into one (§4.1) reduces verifier PCS evaluations from $k$ to a constant and prover MSMs by roughly $k \times$. For a circuit with gate, wiring, and lookup checks ($k = 3$), this is a measured $\approx 2.4\times$ prover-time reduction in the reference implementation [7] — a free speedup purchased purely by the linearity of summation.

## 6. Limitations

The sumcheck-centric worldview has genuine boundaries:

1. **Field size vs. soundness.** The $vd/|\mathbb{F}|$ error term forces large fields (or extension fields) whenever $v$ is large; small-characteristic or 64-bit fields require degree-aware repetition or extension-field embedding, complicating the prover.
2. **Trusted setup is not eliminated, only relocated.** Multilinear KZG [3] still needs an SRS linear in the hypercube size; transparent multilinear PCSs (FRI [4], Brakedown) pay in proof size. Sumcheck itself is setup-free — the cryptography underneath it is not.
3. **Structured computation assumptions.** GKR's verifier efficiency [2] depends on *regular* wiring; arbitrary circuits force the verifier to evaluate wiring predicates, eroding sublinearity. HyperPlonk sidesteps this via copy constraints, but those reintroduce permutation-argument complexity.
4. **Fiat–Shamir fragility.** Every deployed system compiles interaction away via [9]; the security analysis lives in the random-oracle model, and concrete hash choices (Poseidon vs. Keccak) carry real cryptanalytic risk, especially for recursion.
5. **Prover memory.** Linear-time sumcheck provers stream over $O(2^v)$ evaluations; for $v \ge 28$ the memory footprint dominates, and the "linear" claim needs the standard streaming/parallel caveats.
6. **Zero-knowledge overhead.** Masking polynomials and hiding commitments add a constant-factor prover tax (typically 10–30%) that the bare interactive protocol does not pay.

## 7. Conclusion

Thirty-five years after Lund, Fortnow, Karloff, and Nisan [1], the sumcheck protocol remains the deepest idea in succinct arguments — not because it is the most cryptographic, but because it is the most *structural*. It is the reduction that turns "the verifier cannot afford to recompute" into "the verifier need only evaluate once, at random." GKR [2] showed this reduction composes across circuit layers; KZG [3] and FRI [4] showed the final evaluation can be discharged by either pairings or hashes; Nova [8] showed the reduction can be folded incrementally; HyperPlonk [7] showed that when the entire arithmetization is multilinear, *everything* — gates, wiring, lookups — is sumcheck, and the prover becomes linear-time.

The practical upshot is a clean engineering discipline: design the arithmetization so that correctness is a hypercube sum, implement sumcheck once, and plug in whichever polynomial commitment matches the deployment's trust model. Future work — better multilinear PCSs, sumcheck-friendly hash functions, and tighter Fiat–Shamir analyses — will move the Pareto frontier, but the skeleton underneath will still be $v$ rounds, $v$ challenges, and one final evaluation.
