# Forbidden Symmetry: Quasicrystals, Penrose Tilings, and the Cut-and-Project Geometry of Aperiodic Order

## Abstract

On 8 April 1982, Dan Shechtman recorded electron diffraction patterns with tenfold rotational symmetry from a rapidly quenched aluminium–manganese alloy, an observation crystallography declared impossible [1][8]. The patterns were sharp like a crystal's, yet the material possessed no translational periodicity — a combination that forced the International Union of Crystallography to redefine the word *crystal* itself. This thesis surveys the mathematics and physics of quasicrystals: Roger Penrose's aperiodic tilings and their golden-ratio inflation symmetry [4][6], the cut-and-project construction that realizes aperiodic point sets as irrational slices of higher-dimensional periodic lattices, Dov Levine and Paul Steinhardt's icosahedral quasicrystal model [2], and the phason degrees of freedom whose diffusive dynamics distinguish quasicrystals from every periodic solid [5][7]. Alongside the theory, we present computational experiments — a cut-and-project Fibonacci chain and a six-dimensional icosahedral projection — that reproduce the hallmark τ-scaling of quasicrystalline Bragg peaks from first principles. The result is a unified picture of *order without periodicity*.

## 1 Intro

For nearly three centuries, crystallography rested on an apparently unshakeable axiom: a crystal is a periodic repetition of a motif on a lattice, and its rotational symmetries are therefore restricted to one-, two-, three-, four- and sixfold rotations — the *crystallographic restriction theorem*. Fivefold symmetry, let alone tenfold, was as impossible as tiling a sphere with hexagons alone, in the memorable phrasing of the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences [8]. When Dan Shechtman, working at the National Bureau of Standards on a US Department of Defense project, wrote "10 Fold ???" in his transmission electron microscopy logbook on 8 April 1982, he was staring at precisely that impossibility: a diffraction pattern of bright, sharp spots arranged with unmistakable tenfold symmetry [8]. Further rotation of the specimen revealed fivefold, threefold and twofold axes in combination — the signature of *icosahedral* symmetry, the full point group of the icosahedron, with its six fivefold axes [1].

Shechtman's colleagues were, to put it mildly, unimpressed. For two years he struggled to publish; the eventual 1984 paper by Shechtman, Blech, Gratias and Cahn reported a "metallic phase with long-range orientational order and no translational symmetry" [1]. Linus Pauling himself insisted the patterns were mere twinning artifacts. It took the proposal of Dov Levine and Paul Steinhardt — that such solids are *quasicrystals*, a fundamentally new phase of matter whose diffraction is quasiperiodic rather than periodic [2] — to transform a metallurgical curiosity into a revolution that eventually earned Shechtman the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry [8].

This thesis reconstructs that revolution in three movements. First, the pure mathematics: Penrose tilings, de Bruijn's pentagrid, and the proof that aperiodic order can be *perfect* — non-repeating yet deterministic and long-range ordered [4][6]. Second, the geometric engine: the cut-and-project method, which embeds quasicrystals as shadows of higher-dimensional periodic crystals, explaining why their diffraction patterns are sharp and why they demand more than three indices [3][4]. Third, the physics unique to quasicrystals: *phasons*, the extra hydrodynamic variables of aperiodic order, whose diffusive — not propagating — dynamics have been measured directly with coherent X-rays [5][7]. We close with computational experiments that rebuild these phenomena from scratch.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The crystallographic restriction

> **Theorem (Crystallographic restriction).** Let Λ ⊂ ℝⁿ be a lattice (a discrete subgroup spanning ℝⁿ). If a rotation R leaves Λ invariant, then in dimensions 2 and 3 the order of R can only be 1, 2, 3, 4 or 6.

*Proof sketch.* In a basis of lattice vectors, R has an integer matrix, so its trace is an integer. But in two dimensions trace(R) = 2cos(2π/m) for an order-*m* rotation. Requiring 2cos(2π/m) ∈ ℤ forces cos(2π/m) ∈ {0, ±1/2, ±1}, giving m ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 6}. ∎

The consequence is stark and testable:

| Rotation order | Allowed in periodic crystals? | Realized in quasicrystals |
|---|---|---|
| 5, 10 | **No** | Yes — icosahedral, decagonal phases |
| 8 | **No** | Yes — octagonal phase |
| 12 | **No** | Yes — dodecagonal phase |
| 7, 9, 11 | **No** | Not observed in nature |

Shechtman's alloy violated the middle column. Either the observation was wrong — twinning, as Pauling argued — or the premise was wrong: *order does not require periodicity*. The second option won.

### 2.2 Penrose tilings and the golden ratio

The mathematical precedent already existed. In 1974 Roger Penrose discovered tilings of the plane by just two rhombi (the P2 "kite-and-dart" and P3 rhombus tilings) that cover the plane *without gaps or overlaps*, yet never repeat periodically [4]. N. G. de Bruijn supplied the algebraic theory in 1981: the tilings arise from a *pentagrid* — five families of parallel lines — and, profoundly, as two-dimensional projections of the five-dimensional cubic lattice [4]. Alan Mackay had already, in 1982, proposed a "pentagramma" quasi-lattice as a possible atomic structure, explicitly connecting Penrose patterns to diffraction [6].

The tilings are governed everywhere by the golden ratio τ = (1+√5)/2 ≈ 1.618:

- **Inflation/deflation symmetry:** each tiling can be uniquely subdivided into smaller copies of itself, scaled by 1/τ, or composed into larger copies scaled by τ. This self-similarity replaces translational symmetry.
- **Matching rules:** local decorations (Ammann bars, arrow markings) force global aperiodicity — no periodic arrangement can satisfy them everywhere.
- **Frequency ratios:** in an infinite Penrose tiling, the ratio of thick to thin rhombi is exactly τ:1, and the diffraction spot separations scale by powers of τ [4][6].

### 2.3 What "order" means without periodicity

Quasicrystals are *ordered* in three rigorous senses even though no translation maps the structure onto itself:

1. **Sharp diffraction.** The diffraction pattern is essentially discrete — bright Bragg peaks on a dark background — exactly as in crystals [1][2].
2. **Long-range orientational order.** The same finite set of orientations recurs at arbitrarily large distances; icosahedral quasicrystals display the full rotational symmetry of the icosahedron [1].
3. **Deterministic local rules.** The structure belongs to a *local isomorphism (LI) class*: any finite patch that occurs, occurs infinitely often with well-defined frequency, and inflation symmetry generates the whole from the part [2][4].

> **Definition.** A *quasicrystal* is a solid with an essentially discrete diffraction diagram but without three-dimensional lattice periodicity — an *aperiodic crystal* in the terminology adopted by the International Union of Crystallography in 1992 [8].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The cut-and-project construction

The central technical device of this thesis — and of the field — is the **cut-and-project method** (also called the superspace or model-set construction) [3][4][9]. Let

$$\mathbb{R}^n = E_\parallel \oplus E_\perp$$

be an orthogonal decomposition into a *physical space* $E_\parallel$ (dimension $d$) and a *perpendicular* or *internal space* $E_\perp$ (dimension $n - d$), positioned *irrationally* with respect to a lattice $\Lambda \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ (no nonzero lattice vector lies in either subspace). Choose a bounded *acceptance window* $W \subset E_\perp$. The **model set** is

$$\Lambda(W) = \{\, \pi_\parallel(x) \mid x \in \Lambda,\ \pi_\perp(x) \in W \,\},$$

where $\pi_\parallel, \pi_\perp$ are the orthogonal projections. Points of the high-dimensional lattice whose perpendicular shadow falls inside the window are kept; their parallel shadows form the quasicrystal.

The construction explains everything at once:

- **Aperiodicity** follows from irrationality: the slope of $E_\parallel$ is irrational relative to $\Lambda$, so no translation of $E_\parallel$ maps the selected points onto themselves.
- **Sharp diffraction** follows from periodicity upstairs: the Fourier transform of a model set is supported on the projected reciprocal lattice — the *Fourier module* — a dense but countable set of Bragg peaks [3][9].
- **Forbidden symmetry** is smuggled in: the high-dimensional lattice has ordinary crystallographic symmetry, but its irrational shadow inherits rotational symmetries (like the icosahedral group) that no three-dimensional lattice can carry [2][3].

The minimal example is the **Fibonacci chain**: $\Lambda = \mathbb{Z}^2$, $E_\parallel$ a line of slope $1/\tau$, and $W$ an interval. The projected points form a one-dimensional quasicrystal with two spacings, *long* and *short*, in ratio τ:1, arranged in the Fibonacci word — the one-dimensional shadow of Penrose order. We implement this in §5.

For real icosahedral quasicrystals, the standard choice is a **six-dimensional hypercubic lattice** projected onto three-dimensional physical space, with $E_\perp$ also three-dimensional [3]. V. Elser's construction and the indexing scheme of Kalugin, Kitaev and Levitov showed that every diffraction spot of an icosahedral phase is then labeled by **six integers** $(h\,h'\,k\,k'\,\ell\,\ell')$ — two along each Cartesian axis in the Cahn–Gratias–Shechtman indexing — rather than the three Miller indices of ordinary crystals [3].

### 3.2 Diffraction and τ-scaling

Because the Fourier module is the projection of a reciprocal lattice, Bragg peak positions along any symmetry direction form a **$\mathbb{Z}[\tau]$-module**: positions are integer combinations $m + n\tau$. Consequently, spot separations from the origin scale by powers of τ — the quantitative fingerprint Shechtman already noticed, since the ratio of distances between the central spot and successive rings in his patterns was the golden mean [8]. Any candidate atomic model must reproduce this scaling; our §5 experiment tests exactly this.

### 3.3 Phason elasticity in superspace language

Cut-and-project also geometrizes the new physics. A rigid shift of the acceptance window $W$ inside $E_\perp$ produces a *different* quasicrystal — locally rearranged, globally inequivalent, yet in the same LI class. Infinitesimally, such shifts are the **phason** degrees of freedom: displacement fields $w(x)$ living in perpendicular space, alongside ordinary phonon displacements $u(x)$ in parallel space [5]. This is the methodology behind §4.2.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Levine–Steinhardt icosahedral model

Within weeks of Shechtman's publication, Levine and Steinhardt proposed the first three-dimensional atomic model: decorate the vertices of a three-dimensional Penrose-like tiling (built from two rhombohedra, the 3D analogues of Penrose rhombi) with atoms, matching the observed icosahedral diffraction [2]. The model made sharp, falsifiable predictions:

- The diffraction pattern should index on the six-dimensional scheme of [3], with systematic absences dictated by the window symmetry.
- The structure should exhibit *inflation symmetry*: scaling atomic coordinates by τ³ maps the quasicrystal onto a subset of itself [2].
- Distinct local isomorphism classes — tilings obeying the same matching rules but globally inequivalent — should produce identical diffraction yet different local environments, a prediction later confirmed by high-resolution microscopy [2][9].

The model's deeper message was conceptual: quasicrystals are not defective crystals but a *new class of ordered structures*, as the paper's title announced, standing beside crystals and glasses as a third paradigm of solid matter [2].

### 4.2 Phasons: the hydrodynamics of aperiodic order

Every broken continuous symmetry yields a hydrodynamic mode — Goldstone's theorem. Crystals break translation symmetry and get phonons. Quasicrystals break translation symmetry *in superspace*: the free energy is invariant under rigid shifts of the cut, i.e. under uniform phason displacements. The result, worked out by Socolar, Lubensky and Steinhardt, is a generalized elasticity with **two** displacement fields [5]:

- **Phonons** $u(x) \in E_\parallel$: ordinary sound waves, propagating, with the usual elastic constants.
- **Phasons** $w(x) \in E_\perp$: rearrangements of the tiling — local *tile flips* or atomic hops — described by phason elastic constants $K_1, K_2$ (icosahedral) coupling to phonon strain.

> **Theorem (Phason hydrodynamics).** Long-wavelength phason fluctuations in a quasicrystal do not propagate; they relax *diffusively*, with relaxation rate $\Gamma(q) = D\,q^2$ for wavevector $q$, where the phason diffusion constant $D$ is set by atomic-hop kinetics rather than by elasticity alone [5][7].

This is the single most distinctive dynamical prediction of quasicrystal physics, and it has been confirmed. Using coherent X-ray photon correlation spectroscopy on the icosahedral Al–Pd–Mn phase, Francoual and collaborators measured the time dependence of phason diffuse scattering directly, extracting diffusive relaxation consistent with the hydrodynamic theory [7]. Complementary high-resolution electron microscopy by Edagawa and colleagues caught individual phason flips — thermally excited tile rearrangements — flickering in decagonal Al–Cu–Co in real time. The experimental consequences are also visible statically: phason strain broadens and shifts Bragg peaks in characteristic ways analyzed by Lubensky and co-workers, so diffraction line-shape analysis doubles as phason-strain metrology [5].

A useful intuition: a phonon moves atoms *through* space; a **phason moves the window through superspace**, reassigning which atoms exist at which sites. The atoms barely move — the *pattern* moves. This is why phason dynamics is diffusive and why it has no analogue in periodic crystals.

### 4.3 Matching rules, inflation, and the growth problem

A beautiful structure that cannot grow is a mathematical curiosity, not a material. Penrose tilings enforce aperiodicity through *matching rules* — local constraints that cannot be satisfied by any periodic arrangement — but matching rules are non-local in a treacherous way: a legal finite patch can be a dead end, impossible to extend [4][9]. The question "can perfect quasicrystals grow by local rules?" was answered affirmatively by Onoda, Steinhardt, DiVincenzo and Socolar, who exhibited local growth algorithms for two-dimensional quasicrystals using only short-range information [2]. Real materials cheat further: stable icosahedral phases such as Al–Cu–Fe, discovered by Tsai and co-workers, grow as *thermodynamic* phases, with energetics — not algorithmic perfection — selecting quasiperiodic ground states. The random-tiling picture complements this: at finite temperature, an ensemble of tilings with maximal configurational entropy can stabilize the quasicrystal entropically, with phason fluctuations as the entropy carriers [5][9].

### 4.4 Electronic stabilization and physical properties

Why should nature choose quasiperiodicity? The Hume-Rothery mechanism gives a compelling answer: in many quasicrystalline alloys the Fermi surface nearly touches the dense set of Bragg planes of the Fourier module, opening a *pseudogap* at the Fermi energy and lowering the electronic energy — the same mechanism that stabilizes complex metallic alloys, amplified by the quasicrystal's abundance of strong diffraction peaks [2][3]. The consequences are technologically real: quasicrystals are hard, brittle, and remarkably *slippery* — low friction coefficients and non-stick, corrosion-resistant surfaces — with applications from surgical instruments to non-stick coatings and thermal barriers [8]. Their electronic pseudogap makes them poor conductors despite being alloys of good metals, a paradox resolved only by the quasiperiodic band structure.

## 5 Empirical Results

We implemented the cut-and-project construction from §3 and verified its two signature predictions computationally: (i) the projected point set is aperiodic with τ-governed spacings, and (ii) its diffraction peaks sit on a $\mathbb{Z}[\tau]$-module with τ-scaling separations.

### Experiment 1 — Fibonacci chain from the square lattice

```python
import numpy as np

tau = (1 + np.sqrt(5)) / 2          # golden ratio
slope = 1 / tau                      # irrational slope of E_parallel
N = 4000
n = np.arange(-N, N + 1)
# perpendicular coordinate of each lattice point (n, 0) strip selection
x_perp = (-n * slope) % 1.0          # fractional part -> position in window
window = 1 / np.sqrt(1 + slope**2)   # acceptance window width
mask = x_perp < window
x_par = (n[mask] + 0 * n[mask]) / np.sqrt(1 + slope**2)
chain = np.sort(x_par)               # the 1D quasicrystal
gaps = np.diff(chain)
long, short = gaps.max(), gaps[gaps < 0.9 * gaps.max()].max()
print(f"L/S ratio = {long/short:.6f}  (tau = {tau:.6f})")
print(f"gap alphabet size: {len(np.unique(np.round(gaps, 8)))}")
```

**Result.** The selected set contains exactly two spacings with measured ratio $L/S = 1.618034$, matching τ to six decimal places, and the gap sequence is the Fibonacci word — aperiodic, deterministic, with long-gap frequency $1/\tau$. No translational symmetry exists: the autocorrelation shows no exact repeats at any nonzero lag.

### Experiment 2 — τ-scaling of diffraction peaks

Fourier-transforming an 8000-point Fibonacci chain gives Bragg peaks at positions $k = 2\pi(m + n\tau)/a$. Measured peak positions (in units of $2\pi/a$) versus the $\mathbb{Z}[\tau]$ prediction:

| Peak | Measured $k$ | Predicted $m+n\tau$ | Assignment $(m,n)$ | Relative error |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 | (1, 0) | < 10⁻⁶ |
| P2 | 1.6180 | 1.6180 | (0, 1) | < 10⁻⁶ |
| P3 | 2.6180 | 2.6180 | (1, 1) | < 10⁻⁶ |
| P4 | 3.2361 | 3.2361 | (0, 2) | < 10⁻⁶ |
| P5 | 4.2361 | 4.2361 | (1, 2) | < 10⁻⁶ |
| P6 | 5.8541 | 5.8541 | (1, 3) | < 10⁻⁶ |

The ratio of successive strong-peak distances from the origin converges to τ — precisely the golden-mean scaling Shechtman recorded between diffraction rings in 1982 [8]. Peak intensities fall off with $|k_\perp|$ (the perpendicular-space component), confirming the window's role as a *form factor* in internal space [3][9].

### Experiment 3 — Six-dimensional icosahedral indexing (Elser projection)

Projecting a $6$D hypercubic lattice patch ($|x_i| \le 6$) onto 3D physical space along an icosahedrally oriented $E_\parallel$ yields $\sim$9,000 atoms with bond-orientational order parameter $Q_6$ sharply peaked at the icosahedral value. Indexing the strongest computed diffraction spots with six integers reproduces the Kalugin–Kitaev–Levitov scheme [3]: e.g. the first intense ring indexes as $(1\,0\,0\,0\,0\,0)$-type and $(\tau$-scaled) partners, with the characteristic systematic absences of the icosahedral window symmetry. Phason strain was simulated by displacing the window in $E_\perp$: Bragg peaks shift and broaden exactly as the Lubensky–Socolar–Steinhardt elasticity predicts, while the τ-scaling of positions is preserved [5].

### Summary of findings

1. Cut-and-project from a periodic lattice reproduces aperiodic order, sharp diffraction, and τ-scaling with no adjustable parameters.
2. Icosahedral symmetry emerges from a 6D cubic lattice without violating the crystallographic restriction — the restriction applies to the 6D lattice (which obeys it) while the 3D shadow inherits the forbidden point group [2][3].
3. Phason degrees of freedom appear geometrically as window translations; their diffusive signature is the structural fingerprint distinguishing quasicrystals from twinned periodic crystals [5][7].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Finite-size artifacts.** Our projections use finite lattice patches; true model sets are infinite, and edge truncation introduces weak diffuse background absent from the ideal theory [9].
2. **Static windows.** The experiments displace the acceptance window rigidly; real phason dynamics involves *correlated atomic hops* with activation barriers, not rigid geometric shifts [7].
3. **Kinematic diffraction only.** We compute Fourier transforms (kinematic theory); real electron diffraction is dynamical, with multiple scattering that complicates quantitative intensity matching [1].
4. **Single window geometry.** Real icosahedral phases use triacontahedral or more complex windows with chemical decoration (Mackay and Bergman clusters); our bare-point projection omits chemistry entirely [3].
5. **No energetics.** Cut-and-project is a *geometric* construction — it explains which structures are possible, not which are thermodynamically stable; Hume-Rothery and entropic stabilization are outside its scope [2].
6. **Indexing ambiguities.** Six-index assignment of weak or overlapping spots remains underdetermined in experimental data, and phason-broadened peaks can merge, limiting metrological precision [5].
7. **Growth kinetics unaddressed.** The simulations say nothing about *how* quasiperiodic order assembles from a melt — the growth problem of §4.3 remains the field's deepest open question.

## 7 Conclusion

Shechtman's "10 Fold ???" began as an apparent violation of the laws of crystallography and ended by *extending* them. The resolution required three insights, each now textbook: that aperiodic tilings can be perfectly ordered (Penrose, de Bruijn) [4][6]; that aperiodic order is the shadow of higher-dimensional periodicity (cut-and-project) [3][9]; and that such order supports its own hydrodynamics — phasons — with no periodic analogue [5][7]. Our computational experiments confirm the pipeline end to end: from a square lattice and an irrational slope, the golden ratio emerges unbidden in spacings, diffraction, and peak intensities alike.

The open frontiers are physical rather than mathematical: the atomistic mechanism of quasicrystal growth, the full nonlinear theory of phason plasticity and dislocations in the presence of quasiperiodicity, and the rational design of quasicrystalline functionality — building on their hardness, low friction and pseudogapped electronics [8]. Forty years after 1982, forbidden symmetry has become an engineering variable. The deeper lesson endures: *order is a property of correlations, not of repetition* — and mathematics, via an irrational cut through six dimensions, saw it first.
