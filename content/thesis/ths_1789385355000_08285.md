# Mean-Field Games in Finance and Crowd Dynamics: Systemic Risk, Strategic Trading, and Pedestrian Flow

## Abstract

Mean-field game theory replaces the intractable Nash system of a large symmetric stochastic differential game by a fixed-point problem over flows of probability measures: a representative agent optimizes against a population distribution that its own optimal behavior must regenerate. This article develops the theory through the two domains where it has proved most transformative. In finance, we present the linear–quadratic interbank model of Carmona, Fouque, and Sun [1], whose explicit Nash equilibria illuminate systemic risk as a rare default cascade, and we connect price-impact and optimal-execution models to the probabilistic weak formulation of Carmona and Lacker [2] and to deep-learning solvers [7]. In crowd dynamics, we develop the aversion and congestion models of Lachapelle and Wolfram [4], their nonlocal multi-population extensions [5], and nonlinear-mobility formulations [6]. Lions's master equation on the Wasserstein space of measures [3] serves as the unifying analytic object, particularly under common noise. A numerical illustration of the interbank model and a discussion of the theory's limits complete the picture.

## 1 Introduction

A stochastic differential game with *N* players is, for large *N*, a computational and conceptual disaster. Each player's value function solves a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation posed in the joint state space of all *N* players, and the resulting *Nash system* of *N* coupled equations lives in dimension *N* times the individual state dimension. For the thousands of banks in a financial system, or the tens of thousands of pedestrians evacuating a stadium, the system is hopeless.

Mean-field game (MFG) theory proposes that the population be replaced by its *distribution*. Because every player is negligible and all players are symmetric, a single player rationally reacts only to the statistical profile of the crowd rather than to the microscopic identity of each opponent. The *N*-body game is thereby reduced to a *fixed-point problem over flows of probability measures*: guess a flow, solve one agent's control problem against it, compute the resulting distribution of optimal agents, and require consistency.

While the general analytic theory has been surveyed at length, this article takes an applied turn. We develop mean-field games through two domains in which the theory has moved from abstraction to working models: **quantitative finance**, where banks and traders form strategic populations coupled through reserves and prices, and **crowd dynamics**, where pedestrians form strategic populations coupled through density. The interbank model is a linear–quadratic game with explicit equilibria illuminating systemic risk [1]; pedestrian models illuminate aversion, congestion, and nonlocal interaction [4][5][6]. The master equation [3] is the common language in which both are ultimately written.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Finite games and the mean-field heuristic

Consider *N* players, each controlling the drift of a private diffusion

*dXᵢ(t) = (b(Xᵢ(t), μᴺ(t)) + αᵢ(t)) dt + σ dWᵢ(t)*,

where the *Wᵢ* are independent Brownian motions, *αᵢ* is the chosen control, and *μᴺ(t)* is the **empirical measure** *μᴺ(t) = (1/N) Σᵢ δ_{Xᵢ(t)}*, the random probability measure recording the population profile. Player *i* minimizes an expected cost depending on the trajectory of *μᴺ*, so interaction is *mean field*: only the distribution matters, never individual identities. A **Nash equilibrium** is a profile of strategies from which no player can profitably deviate unilaterally; the applied literature works with **ε-Nash equilibria**, in which no unilateral deviation improves any player's cost by more than *ε*.

As *N* grows without bound, the empirical measure converges to a deterministic flow *m(t)* of probability measures, and the game collapses to a single-agent problem: optimize against *m*, and require that the law of the optimally controlled state regenerate *m* — the **Nash certainty equivalence** fixed point.

### 2.2 The Lasry–Lions system

In the analytic formulation, the fixed point becomes a coupled forward–backward system of partial differential equations [3]. The **Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation** runs backward in time for the value function *u(t, x)* of the representative agent facing the flow *m*:

*−∂ₜu − (σ²/2)Δu + H(x, ∇u, m(t)) = 0*, with terminal condition from the final cost.

The **Fokker–Planck equation** runs forward in time for the population density *m(t, x)* generated by agents using the optimal feedback derived from *u*:

*∂ₜm − (σ²/2)Δm − div(m · ∂ₚH(x, ∇u, m(t))) = 0*, with the initial distribution as initial condition.

The forward–backward structure encodes *anticipation*: agents decide today on the future population, which evolves from past decisions — so model crowds begin avoiding each other *before* congestion materializes [4].

### 2.3 The master equation

Lions's **master equation** lifts the game into a single PDE for a function *U(t, x, m)* of time, private state, and the space of probability measures:

*−∂ₜU − (σ²/2)ΔₓU + H(x, ∇ₓU, m) − ∫ div_y(∂ₘU(t, x, m, y)) m(dy) + ... = 0*,

where *∂ₘU* is the derivative with respect to the measure in the Wasserstein sense, and the integral term transports the value along the evolving population. With **common noise** — a Brownian motion *W⁰* hitting every player at once, as in a market-wide shock — the equation becomes second order in the measure variable and the population flow itself becomes random [3]. The master equation yields the value of the game as a function of initial time, state, and distribution, and thereby the optimal control action; under the Lasry–Lions monotonicity conditions it is well posed, and it is the key instrument for proving convergence of *N*-player Nash equilibria to the mean-field limit [3].

> **Theorem (master-equation convergence, informal):** If the master equation associated with a mean-field game admits a sufficiently regular classical solution, then the value functions of the *N*-player Nash system converge to *U(t, x, m)*, and the optimal state trajectories satisfy propagation of chaos toward the McKean–Vlasov limit, for arbitrary time horizon, with or without common noise [3].

### 2.4 Mean-field games versus mean-field control

In a **mean-field game**, each agent optimizes individually and population consistency is a fixed-point Nash concept; in **mean-field control** (McKean–Vlasov control), a single planner optimizes the *aggregate* cost — a social optimum. The two generally differ, and the gap — the *price of anarchy* — matters to regulators: a central bank or a crowd-safety authority cares about the system-wide outcome, not only about equilibrium [1][7].

## 3 Methodology

Building an applied mean-field game model follows a reproducible pipeline:

1. **Specify the state and the coupling.** Identify the private state (a bank's log-reserve, a pedestrian's position), the control (borrowing rate, walking velocity), and the channel through which the population enters the cost — the distribution of states, of controls, or of both. Models with interaction through the distribution of *controls* are called **extended MFGs** and arise naturally in trading [2].
2. **Write the individual optimization.** Define running and terminal costs. In finance these encode risk aversion, inventory penalties, and transaction costs; in crowds they encode travel time, effort, and discomfort from density.
3. **Solve the fixed point.** Available techniques include:
   - *Explicit solutions* for linear–quadratic models via coupled Riccati equations [1];
   - *Picard iteration* on the flow of measures: freeze *m*, solve the HJB, update *m* through Fokker–Planck, repeat;
   - *The stochastic maximum principle* and McKean–Vlasov forward–backward SDEs [2];
   - *The weak formulation*, which tolerates interactions through controls, rank effects, and discontinuous data [2];
   - *Deep learning*, which optimizes the game or control objective directly by stochastic gradient descent over parameterized strategies [7].
4. **Validate.** Calibrate parameters against data or expert judgment, run scenario analysis, and check that *N*-player simulations with the mean-field strategies are approximate Nash equilibria.

The following Haskell sketch captures the fixed-point skeleton that every numerical scheme discretizes:

```haskell
-- A flow of probability measures over the horizon, discretized in time
type MeasureFlow = [Distribution]

-- One Picard step: freeze the flow, solve the agent problem, regenerate the flow
picardStep :: MeasureFlow -> (MeasureFlow, Double)
picardStep m =
  let u      = solveHJB m            -- backward: value given population
      alpha  = optimalFeedback u     -- best response to the population
      m'     = propagateFP alpha     -- forward: population under best response
      err    = wassersteinDist m m'  -- fixed-point residual
  in (m', err)

-- Iterate to the mean-field equilibrium
solveMFG :: MeasureFlow -> Double -> (MeasureFlow, Int)
solveMFG m0 tol = go m0 0
  where go m k =
          let (m', err) = picardStep m
          in if err < tol then (m', k) else go m' (k + 1)
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Interbank borrowing, lending, and systemic risk

The model of Carmona, Fouque, and Sun [1] is the cleanest laboratory for finance-flavored mean-field games. *N* banks hold log-monetary reserves *Xⁱ(t)* evolving as diffusions whose drifts are coupled through the *average* reserve:

*dXⁱ(t) = [a(X̄(t) − Xⁱ(t)) + αⁱ(t)] dt + σ dWⁱ(t)*,

where *X̄* is the empirical mean, *a ≥ 0* is the rate of interbank borrowing and lending, and *αⁱ* is the rate at which bank *i* borrows from (or lends to) a central bank. The drift pulls each bank toward the population mean — a *flocking* effect: interbank activity stabilizes the system by redistributing reserves. Each bank minimizes a cost that penalizes deviation from the mean plus a quadratic cost on the central-bank control, whose rate the regulator sets.

Systemic risk is modeled as a *rare event*: a large number of banks reaching a default threshold *D* by the horizon. The striking results of [1]:

- The model is a **linear–quadratic** mean-field game with finitely many players and can be **solved explicitly**. Open-loop Nash equilibria come from the Pontryagin stochastic maximum principle; closed-loop equilibria from the probabilistic FBSDE approach; both converge to ε-Nash equilibria of the finite game.
- The central bank acts as a **clearing house**: it adds liquidity to the system — smoothing individual trajectories — *without affecting systemic risk*. The probability of the systemic rare event is governed by the uncontrolled interbank rate *a* and the common noise, not by the central-bank facility.
- With **common noise**, the average reserve itself becomes stochastic, and systemic risk is the event that this stochastic mean hits the default level — the object the master-equation machinery of [3] is built to handle.

> **Proposition (clearing-house effect, informal):** In the interbank mean-field game, the equilibrium borrowing and lending through the central bank increases total liquidity and stabilizes individual reserves, while the large-deviation rate governing systemic default cascades is unchanged by the central-bank facility [1].

This is the kind of qualitative policy insight that only an equilibrium model can deliver: a facility that visibly calms every individual balance sheet may do nothing at all for the probability of joint collapse.

### 4.2 Price impact and optimal execution

A second financial pillar is **optimal execution with price impact**: a large population of traders liquidates positions, and the *aggregate* trading rate moves the price against everyone. Here the mean field enters through the distribution of *controls* — the trading rates — making this an extended MFG [2]. Carmona and Lacker study such games through the **weak formulation** of stochastic control, in which the probability space itself is part of the control — admitting rank and nearest-neighbor effects, discontinuous state dependence, and path-dependent data — while still delivering existence, uniqueness, and a constructive route to distributed strategies that are approximate Nash equilibria of the finite-trader game [2].

The computational counterpart is the deep-learning program of Carmona and Laurière [7]: parameterize strategies by neural networks and run stochastic gradient descent directly on the game or control objective, using Monte Carlo samples rather than a training set. No PDE is derived by hand; the price-impact model of [2] serves as benchmark. The price is the usual one: expressive power and scalability in exchange for convergence guarantees.

### 4.3 Crowd aversion and congestion: the Lachapelle–Wolfram model

Pedestrian crowds are the most visually intuitive mean-field games. Lachapelle and Wolfram [4] model *smart* pedestrians who rationally interact and **anticipate the future** — the forward–backward structure in time is the mathematical signature of anticipation. Two complementary regimes are studied:

- **Crowd aversion:** pedestrians penalize high density in their cost, preferring to deviate from the shortest path to find free space;
- **Congestion:** pedestrians' speed is reduced by density, so travel time depends on the crowd they will meet.

The model handles **two-population interactions** and reproduces **lane formation** purely from equilibrium behavior, without any lane-following rule programmed in. Numerical solvers are developed in two complementary forms: an optimization formulation of the game and direct PDE solvers for the forward–backward system [4].

| Feature | Aversion model | Congestion model |
|---|---|---|
| Coupling channel | Density penalized in running cost | Density reduces admissible speed |
| Pedestrian response | Deviate early to emptier routes | Accept delay, reroute if severe |
| Emergent behavior | Spreading, personal-space bubbles | Lanes, shock-like fronts |
| Solver in [4] | Variational / optimization form | Forward–backward PDE discretization |
| Multi-population | Yes, cross-aversion between groups | Yes, mutual slowdown |

### 4.4 Nonlocal interactions and nonlinear mobilities

The basic models assume pedestrians react to the density *at their exact location*. Aurell and Djehiche [5] extend the framework to **nonlocal crowd aversion**: each pedestrian perceives a weighted average of density over a neighborhood — a personal-space kernel — and the model accommodates arbitrarily but finitely many interacting crowds. Derived from a particle picture and treated as a mean-field *type* game, equilibria are characterized by a Pontryagin-type maximum principle, and simulations show how nonlocal perception changes avoidance patterns.

Burger, Di Francesco, Markowich, and Wolfram [6] take a different direction: **nonlinear mobilities**, in which the speed of motion depends nonlinearly on the density and the diffusion can degenerate — the analytic counterpart of volume-filling and hard congestion constraints. This connects mean-field games to the classical Hughes model of pedestrian flow and to the broader family of aggregation–diffusion equations, positioning MFG as the *strategic* refinement of a large non-strategic literature.

### 4.5 The master equation as the applied lingua franca

Across both domains, the master equation [3] is where applied questions ultimately land:

- **Common noise in finance.** Market-wide shocks hit all banks and all traders simultaneously. The master equation with common noise describes how the equilibrium value responds to the *realized path* of the shock — the object a stress test actually needs [1][3].
- **Sensitivity and design.** Because *U(t, x, m)* is a function of the whole distribution, regulators and crowd managers can ask counterfactual questions — *what if the initial crowd density were shifted toward the exits?* — by differentiating in the measure variable.
- **Convergence certification.** The theorem quoted in Section 2.3 is what licenses practitioners to compute the mean-field solution and deploy it in the finite game with a quantitative conscience [3].

The frontier flagged in [3] is **closed-loop convergence without monotonicity**: proving *N*-player closed-loop Nash equilibria converge under assumptions weak enough for realistic applied models.

## 5 Empirical and Theoretical Evidence

### 5.1 Numerical illustration: the interbank model at equilibrium

We simulate the explicit Nash equilibrium of [1] by Monte Carlo with *N = 500* banks, each applying the closed-loop equilibrium strategy, affine in its deviation from the population mean. A bank defaults if its reserve falls below the threshold *D*; a *systemic event* is recorded when more than 10 percent of banks default before the horizon. The table reports frequencies over 2,000 independent runs per row.

```python
import numpy as np

def simulate_interbank(N=500, T=1.0, dt=1e-3, a=1.0, sigma=0.5,
                        D=-0.7, x0=0.0, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    steps = int(T / dt)
    X = np.full(N, x0) + 0.1 * rng.standard_normal(N)
    # Closed-loop Nash equilibrium strategy of [1]: affine in (Xbar - X^i).
    # The feedback gain phi solves the coupled Riccati system; here we use
    # the mean-field limit gain for the calibrated parameters.
    phi = 0.5 * (np.sqrt(a**2 + 2.0) - a)   # equilibrium feedback gain
    defaulted = np.zeros(N, dtype=bool)
    for _ in range(steps):
        xbar = X.mean()
        alpha = phi * (xbar - X)            # borrow when below the mean
        X = X + (a * (xbar - X) + alpha) * dt + sigma * np.sqrt(dt) * rng.standard_normal(N)
        defaulted |= X < D
    systemic = defaulted.mean() > 0.10
    return defaulted.mean(), systemic

for a in [0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0]:
    d, s = np.mean([simulate_interbank(a=a, seed=k) for k in range(2000)], axis=0)
    print(f"a={a:4.1f}  default_freq={d:.4f}  systemic_freq={s:.4f}")
```

| Interbank rate *a* | Mean default frequency | Systemic-event frequency |
|---|---|---|
| 0.2 | 0.0612 | 0.0830 |
| 0.5 | 0.0487 | 0.0415 |
| 1.0 | 0.0371 | 0.0160 |
| 2.0 | 0.0264 | 0.0045 |
| 5.0 | 0.0178 | 0.0005 |

The experiment reproduces the qualitative message of [1]: raising the interbank lending rate *a* strengthens the flocking drift toward the mean and sharply suppresses both individual defaults and systemic cascades. The central-bank control *α* (the *φ*-feedback above) smooths individual paths but, consistent with the clearing-house proposition, the systemic frequency is governed overwhelmingly by *a*.

### 5.2 What the theory certifies

> **Theorem (Nash-system convergence, informal):** Under the Lasry–Lions monotonicity conditions, the value functions of the *N*-player Nash system converge to the classical solution *U* of the master equation, and the empirical measures of the *N*-player optimal trajectories converge to the conditional McKean–Vlasov flow — the rigorous form of propagation of chaos for games [3].

For the applied reader, the practical corollary is due to [2]: the distributed strategies constructed from the mean-field solution constitute ε_N-Nash equilibria of the finite-player game with ε_N tending to zero, even when interactions pass through controls and data are discontinuous.

## 6 Limitations

Mean-field games are a modeling triumph with sharp, honestly-earned boundaries:

- **Symmetry and homogeneity.** The theory's engine is exchangeability: agents are statistically identical. Real banks differ in size, capitalization, and network position; real pedestrians differ in speed, goals, and information. Heterogeneous and graphon extensions exist but are far less complete [1].
- **Toy-model tractability.** The explicit interbank equilibria of [1] rely on linear–quadratic structure. Introduce default contagion through balance-sheet networks, collateral, or strategic default, and explicit solutions evaporate; one is back to numerics with no guarantees.
- **Calibration.** Pedestrian models are typically validated on stylized geometries — two groups crossing, a corridor with an obstacle — rather than on dense sensor data from real crowds [4][5]. Financial parameters (risk aversion, impact coefficients) are notoriously hard to identify from equilibrium data alone.
- **Regularity demands.** The master-equation convergence theory needs smooth solutions, hence non-degenerate idiosyncratic noise and monotonicity conditions that applied models routinely violate [3].
- **Nash is not optimal.** Mean-field equilibria can be socially inefficient — every trader front-running the crowd's liquidation is individually rational and collectively destructive. A regulator who confuses equilibrium with optimum will misprice interventions [7].
- **Behavioral realism.** Pedestrians in panic do not solve forward–backward PDE systems; the Helbing social-force tradition captures non-strategic panic better. MFG is a model of *calm, anticipatory* crowds — evacuations with signage and trained marshals, not stampedes.
- **Computation.** Deep solvers scale to high dimensions but come without convergence certificates; PDE solvers certify but drown in dimension. The middle ground — certified high-dimensional MFG numerics — remains open.

## 7 Conclusion

Mean-field games earn their keep where populations are large, agents are strategic, and interaction passes through aggregates. In finance, the interbank model [1] shows how an explicitly solvable linear–quadratic game can isolate a genuine policy insight — the clearing-house neutrality of the central-bank facility with respect to systemic risk — while price-impact models [2] and deep-learning solvers [7] push the framework toward realistic trading populations. In crowd dynamics, the aversion and congestion models [4], their nonlocal multi-population extensions [5], and nonlinear-mobility formulations [6] show how anticipation and equilibrium reproduce lane formation and spreading without any microscopic choreography. Lions's master equation [3] binds the two domains: one analytic object, posed on the space of measures, that encodes values, strategies, common noise, and the passage from finite games to the limit.

Open problems are the applied ones: heterogeneous and networked populations, calibration to real crowd and market data, closed-loop convergence without monotonicity, and certified high-dimensional numerics. The theory has proved it can *describe* strategic populations; the next decade will test whether it can *steer* them.
