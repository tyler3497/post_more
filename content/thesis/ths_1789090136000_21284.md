# Learned Index Structures and the Cost-Based Optimizer: Recursive Model Indexes, PGM-Index Worst-Case Bounds, ALEX Updatability, and the Learned Cardinality Frontier

## Abstract

Indexes are models. This premise, introduced by Kraska et al. [1], reframes the B-tree as a piecewise-constant approximation to the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of a sorted key set, and asks whether regression models can approximate that CDF more compactly and evaluate it faster than pointer chasing through cache-resident nodes. The answer has spawned an entire research program: the two-stage recursive model index (RMI), the optimal piecewise-linear PGM-index with provable worst-case bounds [2,3], the updatable ALEX structure with gapped arrays and adaptive expansion [4], and, orthogonally, learned cardinality estimators and learned plan optimizers (Neo [6], Bao [5]) that attack the classical cost-based optimizer's oldest weakness — selectivity estimation. This article develops the theory from first principles, derives the error-bounded lookup procedure and the space–time tradeoffs, surveys the empirical SOSD benchmark evidence [7], and confronts the central unresolved tension: learned structures deliver spectacular average-case compression and lookup speed, but classic worst-case guarantees, robustness to distribution shift, adversarial poisoning [8], and update costs remain only partially solved. We conclude with a synthesis of hybrid designs that combine learned approximation with fallback guarantees.

## 1. Introduction

For four decades, the B-tree and its variants have been the undisputed backbone of ordered indexing. Their worst-case *O*(log<sub>B</sub> *n*) lookup bound, their amortized update costs, and their distribution-independent behavior made them the default choice in every serious database engine. The cost-based query optimizer that sits above them — a legacy of Selinger's System R work — has enjoyed a similar hegemony, despite its chronic dependence on cardinality estimates that are routinely wrong by orders of magnitude.

Two parallel revolutions now challenge this status quo. The **learned index** program observes that a sorted array's index function *pos*(*k*) = rank(*k*) is precisely the empirical CDF scaled by *n*, and that any regression model *f̂* with a bounded error *ε* can serve as an index: evaluate *f̂*(*k*), then finish with a bounded local search in [*pos* − *ε*, *pos* + *ε*] [1]. The **learned optimizer** program observes that the optimizer's cost model and cardinality estimator are just functions from (query, data statistics) to a scalar, and that neural networks trained on executed plans can predict them more accurately than decades-old histograms — or can bypass prediction entirely and learn plan selection as a bandit problem [5,6].

Both programs share a methodology and a vulnerability. The methodology: replace a hand-designed algorithmic component with a learned model, and correct the model's residual error with a cheap verification step. The vulnerability: models are distribution-dependent, whereas classical guarantees are not. A B-tree is *O*(log *n*) for *every* key set; a learned index is fast when the CDF is smooth and the workload matches training. This article is an extended meditation on that tension, covering the major constructions, the theoretical results that partially close the gap, and the engineering evidence from the Search on Sorted Data (SOSD) benchmark [7].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The index as a model

Consider a static sorted array *A*[0…*n*−1] of keys. Define the rank function

```
rank(k) = |{ x ∈ A : x < k }|
```

A B-tree index implements this function by storing a hierarchy of separators: at each level, a key *k* is routed by comparisons down to a leaf page, after which a binary search within the page completes the lookup. From the "indexes are models" viewpoint [1], the B-tree is a piecewise-constant model of the CDF with *O*(*n*/*B*) pieces (one per leaf page), evaluated by *O*(log<sub>B</sub> *n*) comparisons.

A **learned index** replaces this with an arbitrary regression model *f̂*: *K* → [0, *n*] trained to minimize max|*f̂*(*k*) − rank(*k*)| over the key set (or a weighted/average variant). The lookup procedure becomes:

1. *p* ← ⌊*f̂*(*k*)⌋ (model evaluation, ideally *O*(1)),
2. search *A* in [*p* − *ε*, *p* + *ε*] with binary or exponential search (*O*(log *ε*)).

The total cost is *O*(eval(*f̂*) + log *ε*). If *f̂* is a small neural network or a linear model, evaluation is a handful of FLOPs — far cheaper than the cache misses of B-tree traversal — and the model occupies kilobytes rather than the B-tree's megabytes.

> **Theorem:** (Error-bounded lookup.) Let *f̂*: *K* → ℝ satisfy |*f̂*(*k*) − rank(*k*)| ≤ *ε* for all *k* in the indexed set. Then point lookup by evaluating *f̂* and binary-searching the interval [*f̂*(*k*) − *ε*, *f̂*(*k*) + *ε*] returns the correct rank in *O*(eval(*f̂*) + log *ε*) time, with correctness independent of the model's statistical properties. The error bound, not the model, is the contract.

This is the foundational decoupling of the field: *accuracy of the model determines performance; the verified error bound determines correctness*.

### 2.2 Cost-based optimization and its Achilles' heel

The classical optimizer enumerates join orders and access paths via dynamic programming, costing each candidate plan with a cost model *C*(*p*) = Σ operator costs, each depending on estimated intermediate cardinalities. Leis et al. demonstrated that cardinality estimation errors — not enumeration or costing — dominate optimizer failures on real workloads. Learned cardinality estimators (deep autoregressive models, sum-product networks) and learned optimizers attack precisely this layer: Neo [6] learns a value network over plan trees via reinforcement learning, while Bao [5] treats hint selection as a contextual bandit, steering an unmodified PostgreSQL toward better plans through trial and feedback.

### 2.3 The classical guarantees we are trying to keep

Any serious learned index must be measured against three classical properties:

- **Worst-case lookup bounds:** B-trees guarantee *O*(log<sub>B</sub> *n*) regardless of data distribution.
- **Update efficiency:** amortized *O*(log *n*) inserts/deletes with bounded space amplification.
- **Distribution independence:** performance does not degrade on adversarial or shifted data.

The history of learned indexes is, to a large extent, the history of recovering these properties one by one.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The recursive model index (RMI)

The original construction of Kraska et al. [1] is a two-stage (in general, *L*-stage) hierarchy. Stage 0 is a single model mapping the key domain to a leaf-model index; each stage-*i* model routes to one of several stage-(*i*+1) models; the final stage predicts the array position. Formally, an *L*-level RMI partitions the key space recursively: model *m*<sub>ℓ,j</sub> is trained on the subset of keys routed to it, and its prediction error *ε*<sub>ℓ,j</sub> is recorded. The root's error determines which child to consult; the leaf's error determines the final search window.

```
def rmi_lookup(key):
    idx = 0
    for level in range(L - 1):          # routing stages
        idx = int(models[level][idx](key))
    pos = models[L-1][idx](key)        # leaf prediction
    lo, hi = pos - eps[L-1][idx], pos + eps[L-1][idx]
    return binary_search(A, key, lo, hi)
```

Training is a top-down procedure: fit the root model on all (*k*, rank(*k*)) pairs, partition keys by predicted position into *M* buckets, fit one model per bucket, and recurse. Model families in the original work include linear regression, polynomial regression, and small feed-forward neural networks (e.g., 16–32 hidden units). The key engineering insight is that *different regions of the CDF have different smoothness*, so a single global model is dominated by a hierarchy of local ones.

### 3.2 From heuristics to optimality: the PGM-index

The RMI's weakness is theoretical: nothing bounds the number of models, their error, or the resulting space. Ferragina and Vinciguerra [2] recast index construction as a **geometric** problem — optimal piecewise-linear *ε*-approximation of the CDF — and derived the PGM-index, the first learned index with provable worst-case bounds.

The construction rests on a linear-time streaming algorithm for the *minimum-cardinality* *ε*-approximate piecewise linear approximation (PLA): given sorted points (*k*<sub>i</sub>, *i*) and error *ε*, it produces the fewest segments such that every point lies within vertical distance *ε* of its segment. This is achieved via the classic convex-hull trick over the feasible slope interval. The PGM-index then builds a hierarchy *bottom-up*: fit an optimal PLA over the keys; take the first key of each segment as a new key set; recurse until a single segment remains. Each level shrinks geometrically, giving *O*(log *n*) levels with *O*(*m*) total segments.

> **Theorem:** (PGM-index bounds [2].) A PGM-index over *n* keys with error parameter *ε* occupies *O*(*m*) space where *m* is the optimal PLA segment count, and answers predecessor and range queries in *O*(log *m* + log(*n*/*m*)) worst-case time. With *ε* = Θ(*B*), lookup matches the B-tree's *O*(log<sub>B</sub> *n*) while space drops from *O*(*n*/*B*) to *O*(*n*/*B*²).

The paper further introduces a distribution-aware variant, a compressed variant exploiting repetitiveness among segments, and a multicriteria auto-tuning variant — plus a fully-dynamic version via the logarithmic method with amortized *O*(log *n*) updates [2].

### 3.3 Why they work: the stochastic-process view

Ferragina, Lillo, and Vinciguerra [3] supplied the missing mathematical foundation: under a generative model where key gaps are i.i.d.

### 3.4 Making them updatable: ALEX

Static learned indexes are elegant but useless for OLTP.

1. **Gapped arrays:** data nodes store records in arrays with deliberate gaps, so inserts land near the model-predicted position with *O*(1) shifts amortized, rather than triggering immediate reorganization.
2. **Model-based inserts:** new keys are placed at the position the current model predicts, preserving the accuracy of the model under inserts drawn from a similar distribution.
3. **Adaptive expansion and splitting:** cost models monitor each node's lookup/insert efficiency; when a node's model degrades (e.g., under distribution shift), ALEX retrains it, expands its gapped array, or splits it — sideways for range growth, downwards for density.

ALEX reports up to 2.2× the read performance of the original RMI at 15× smaller size, and up to 4.1× over B-trees on mixed read-write workloads [4].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The geometry of the CDF approximation

The PGM-index's PLA construction deserves a closer look, because it converts an ML heuristic into an algorithms problem with an exact solution.

A compact Haskell sketch of the core idea (feasible slope-interval maintenance):

```haskell
-- Maintain the feasible slope interval [sLo, sHi] for the current segment.
-- A point (x, y) with error eps restricts slopes via the strip
--   |s * x + t - y| <= eps  for some intercept t.
extend :: Double -> Double -> Double -> SegState -> Maybe SegState
extend x y eps st =
  let sLo' = max (sLo st) ((y - eps - tHi st) / x)
      sHi' = min (sHi st) ((y + eps - tLo st) / x)
  in if sLo' <= sHi' then Just st { sLo = sLo', sHi = sHi' }
                    else Nothing  -- start a new segment
```

The recursion over segment-start keys then yields the multi-level index.

### 4.2 Error models: max-error vs. average-error, and why it matters

RMI-style constructions minimize (or bound) different error metrics, and the choice has deep consequences:

| Error metric | Lookup cost | Construction | Robustness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Max error *ε* (PGM) | *O*(log *ε*) bounded window | Optimal PLA, linear time | Worst-case guarantee |
| Mean absolute error (RMI) | Unbounded in theory; small in practice | Regression (fast) | No formal guarantee |
| Quantile error (learned) | Bounded with high probability | Distribution-dependent | Degrades under shift |

Max-error constructions buy their guarantees with conservatism: a single outlier key can force a new segment, inflating the model.

### 4.3 Updates, distribution shift, and the ALEX cost models

The hardest problem in learned indexing is not lookup — it is *change*.

1. **Expected lookup cost:** a function of the node's model error and the exponential-search behavior from predicted positions.
2. **Expected insert cost:** a function of gap density, shift distance, and the probability of structural operations (expansion/split/retrain).

When the empirical insert cost exceeds a threshold relative to the node's statistics at build time, ALEX triggers retraining or restructuring.

### 4.4 The optimizer stack: where learning actually pays

Learned cardinality estimation and learned plan selection deserve separate treatment because the optimizer's failure modes differ from the index's.

The empirical record is instructive:

- On the Join Order Benchmark (JOB), learned optimizers consistently beat PostgreSQL by 20–50% in total workload time once trained [5,6].
- Training cost is the tax: Neo requires hundreds of executed plans per query template; Bao amortizes this online but still needs exploration budget.
- **Data drift is the shared enemy**: both index models and optimizer models assume the future resembles the past. Retraining policies are now a first-class research problem.

### 4.5 Benchmarking reality: SOSD

The Search on Sorted Data benchmark [7] brought methodological discipline to a field drowning in cherry-picked microbenchmarks.

- Learned indexes (RMI variants, PGM-index, ALEX) *do* beat the best traditional structures on most real datasets, often by 1.5–3× in lookup latency at 10–1000× smaller memory footprints, with wins concentrating on smooth-CDF datasets (timestamps, IDs).
- On "hard" datasets with highly irregular CDFs, the advantage shrinks to parity; build times and update throughput remain the traditional structures' stronghold.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We now consolidate the quantitative claims from the primary literature into a single comparative view. All numbers below are as reported in the cited papers; hardware differs across studies, so treat cross-paper comparisons as indicative rather than exact.

| Structure | Lookup vs. B-tree | Size vs. B-tree | Updates | Worst-case bound |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RMI (Kraska et al. [1]) | up to 3× faster | ~10× smaller | none (static) | none |
| PGM-index [2] | parity ±20% | 83× smaller (static); 1140× (dynamic) | amortized *O*(log *n*) | *O*(log *m* + log(*n*/*m*)) |
| ALEX [4] | up to 2.2× vs RMI; 4.1× vs B-tree mixed | 15× (read); up to 2000× | yes, adaptive | amortized, distribution-dependent |
| B-tree (baseline) | 1× | 1× | *O*(log *n*) | *O*(log<sub>B</sub> *n*) |

> **Theorem:** (Space–time separation [2,3].) For key sets whose gaps have bounded *σ*/*μ*, a learned index achieves B-tree-equivalent lookup time in *o*(*n*/*B*) space — asymptotically *less* space than any comparison-based structure that stores Ω(1) metadata per *B* keys. The separation is information-theoretic: the model exploits the *compressibility of the distribution*, which classical structures ignore by design.

A Rust sketch of the error-bounded lookup at the heart of every learned index clarifies how little code the "learned" part actually requires once the model and its error bound are fixed:

```rust
/// Error-bounded lookup: the model proposes, the bound disposes.
fn learned_lookup<M: Model>(model: &M, eps: usize, a: &[u64], key: u64) -> Option<usize> {
    let pred = model.predict(key) as isize;
    let lo = pred.saturating_sub(eps as isize).max(0) as usize;
    let hi = (pred + eps as isize).min(a.len() as isize - 1) as usize;
    // Exponential search from the prediction exploits model accuracy;
    // binary search over [lo, hi] preserves the worst-case bound.
    exponential_search_then_binary(a, key, pred.clamp(lo as isize, hi as isize) as usize, lo, hi)
}
```

For learned optimizers, the headline empirical results are: Bao improves PostgreSQL workload latency by roughly 20–50% on standard benchmarks with online training [5]; Neo matches or exceeds expert-tuned optimizers after sufficient training episodes [6]. Both, however, report sensitivity to workload shift — the optimizer analogue of the index's distribution-shift problem.

A Python experiment anyone can run illustrates the core phenomenon — that a simple linear model over a smooth CDF beats binary search's cache behavior:

```python
import numpy as np, time

n = 10_000_00
keys = np.sort(np.random.default_rng(0).integers(0, 2**40, n))
# Linear model of the CDF: pos ≈ slope * key + intercept
slope, intercept = np.polyfit(keys, np.arange(n), 1)
preds = (slope * keys + intercept).astype(int)
max_err = int(np.max(np.abs(preds - np.arange(n))))
print(f"max error ε = {max_err}  ({100*max_err/n:.3f}% of n)")

def model_lookup(k):
    p = int(slope * k + intercept)
    lo, hi = max(0, p - max_err), min(n - 1, p + max_err)
    return lo + int(np.searchsorted(keys[lo:hi+1], k))
```

On uniform keys, *ε*/*n* is typically well under 1%, so the final binary search spans a tiny, cache-resident window — the entire mechanism of the learned index in ten lines.

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires dwelling on what remains broken.

**1. Distribution shift and adversarial inputs.** Every learned index is a bet that the future resembles the training data. ALEX's retraining policies mitigate gradual drift [4], but abrupt shifts — bulk loads of out-of-range keys, adversarial insertion orders — can collapse performance. Worse, the vulnerability is now formalized: poisoning attacks on the PGM-index show that inserting just 10% adversarial keys can inflate the segment count (and thus index size) by up to 120×, exploiting the very optimality of the PLA objective [8]. The objective that makes the PGM-index elegant also makes it attackable.

**2. The update gap is narrowed, not closed.** ALEX handles mixed workloads well, but write-heavy workloads with shifting distributions still favor B-trees in several SOSD configurations [7]. The logarithmic-method dynamic PGM-index [2] has clean amortized bounds but pays constant-factor overheads that matter in practice.

**3. Multi-dimensional and string keys.** The CDF trick is inherently one-dimensional. Extensions to multi-dimensional data (Tsunami, learned Z-order curves) and variable-length strings exist but inherit all the fragility of the 1-D case plus the curse of dimensionality. Classical structures (R-trees, tries) remain far more robust here.

**4. Learned optimizers and the training tax.** Neo's [6] training cost — hundreds of plan executions per template — is prohibitive for ad-hoc workloads. Bao [5] reduces this but still requires exploration budget and a stable workload to amortize it. Neither handles schema changes or major data redistribution gracefully; both are, at present, *workload* optimizers rather than *database* optimizers.

**5. Worst-case guarantees remain second-class.** The PGM-index [2] is the honorable exception with genuine worst-case bounds. For RMI-style, ALEX-style, and neural cardinality estimators, "works well on our datasets" is still doing heavy lifting. The database community's skepticism — earned over decades of pathological workloads — will not be dispelled by average-case benchmarks alone.

**6. Benchmark gaming and the reproducibility gap.** Early learned-index papers compared against unoptimized B-tree baselines; SOSD [7] corrected this, but the field still lacks agreed-upon update-heavy and adversarial benchmarks. Until poisoning-robustness and shift-robustness are standard benchmark dimensions, claims of practicality should be read with caution.

## 7. Conclusion

The learned-index program has matured from a provocative position paper [1] into a subfield with real theory, real systems, and real deployments. Its intellectual contributions are durable regardless of adoption: the *indexes-are-models* reframing, the reduction of index construction to optimal CDF approximation [2], the stochastic-process explanation of *why* learning helps [3], and the demonstration that updateability and adaptivity can be engineered into learned structures [4]. The learned-optimizer program [5,6] extends the same philosophy up the stack, attacking cardinality estimation — the optimizer's original sin — with data-driven models.

The central tension, however, is unresolved and likely permanent: **learning exploits regularity; guarantees assume its absence.** The most promising direction is therefore not pure learning but *hybridization* — structures like the PGM-index that are learned in construction yet classical in their contracts, optimizers like Bao that learn within the guardrails of a traditional planner, and benchmarking cultures like SOSD that force honest comparison. The next decade will belong to whoever makes learned components as boringly reliable as the B-tree — at which point, paradoxically, we may stop calling them "learned" at all.
