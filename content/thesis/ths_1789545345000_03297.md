# Exoplanet Detection and Characterization: Transit Photometry, Radial Velocities, and Atmospheric Retrieval

## Abstract

Since the 1995 discovery of 51 Pegasi b, the census of confirmed exoplanets has grown beyond 5,900 worlds, transforming planetary science from speculation into a statistical and spectroscopic discipline. This thesis reviews the four observational pillars — transit photometry, radial velocities, direct imaging, and gravitational microlensing and astrometry — and the inferential machinery that converts raw time series into masses, radii, and atmospheric compositions. We derive the geometric transit probability and the Doppler semi-amplitude, survey light-curve modeling with limb darkening, stellar-activity mitigation via Gaussian processes, and high-contrast imaging with coronagraphy and angular differential imaging. We then examine atmospheric retrieval: Bayesian inversion of transmission and emission spectra exemplified by JWST observations of WASP-39b, LHS 1140 b, and GJ 9827 d. The mass–radius diagram and habitability metrics are presented as the synthesis of these techniques, alongside their fundamental limits and open problems.

##1 Introduction

The detection of a Jupiter-mass companion to the Sun-like star 51 Pegasi in 1995, recognized with the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics shared by Mayor and Queloz, inaugurated the modern era of exoplanet science. Within three decades, wide-field photometric surveys — *Kepler*, *K2*, *TESS*, and ground-based networks such as WASP and HATNet — together with precision Doppler spectrographs have expanded the confirmed catalog to several thousand planets and several thousand additional candidates awaiting validation. This census has rewritten planetary demographics: hot Jupiters exist where theory forbade them, compact multi-planet systems of sub-Neptunes are common around cool dwarfs, and the size distribution of small planets exhibits a pronounced *radius valley* separating rocky super-Earths from volatile-rich mini-Neptunes.

Yet detection is only the first step. A measured flux dip yields a *radius*; a measured Doppler wobble yields a *minimum mass*; only their combination yields a *bulk density*, and only spectroscopy yields a *composition*. Characterization therefore rests on an interlocking suite of techniques, each with distinct selection biases, noise floors, and degeneracies. This thesis develops the theory and practice of the field's principal methods — transit photometry, radial velocities, direct imaging, microlensing, and astrometry — and culminates in atmospheric retrieval, the Bayesian inversion framework that JWST has elevated from an art to a quantitative science.

Three themes recur throughout. **First**, no single method is complete: transits favor large planets on short orbits, radial velocities favor massive planets around quiet stars, imaging favors young self-luminous planets at wide separations, and microlensing samples the cold outer regions of planetary systems that are otherwise inaccessible. **Second**, stellar signals are the perennial adversary — limb darkening shapes every light curve, magnetic activity mimics or masks every Doppler signal, and unocculted spots contaminate every transmission spectrum. **Third**, the modern workflow is probabilistic: from Markov chain Monte Carlo fits of light curves to nested-sampling retrievals of atmospheric spectra, uncertainty quantification is inseparable from measurement.

##2 Background

An exoplanet detection technique is fully described by three quantities: the *observable* it measures, the *parameter combination* that observable constrains, and the *selection function* that determines which planets it can find. The table below summarizes the five mature methods.

| Method | Primary observables | Parameter yield | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Transit photometry | periodic flux dips, depth and duration | radius, period, inclination, scale height | enormous multiplex, atmospheric spectra | geometric alignment bias, astrophysical false positives |
| Radial velocities | Doppler semi-amplitude and period | minimum mass, eccentricity, period | many targets, non-transiting systems | stellar activity jitter, mass–inclination degeneracy |
| Direct imaging | resolved flux, colors, spectra, astrometry | luminosity, orbit, atmospheric composition | face-on orbits, young planets, wide separations | extreme contrast, inner working angle |
| Gravitational microlensing | transient magnification anomalies | mass ratio, projected separation | cold planets, faint and distant hosts | non-repeating events, mass–distance degeneracy |
| Astrometry | stellar positional wobble | true mass, full orbital elements | breaks the inclination degeneracy | long baselines, sub-milliarcsecond precision |

Transit photometry has been the most prolific discovery engine: the *Kepler* mission alone contributed the majority of known planets, and the transit method's power is amplified by the rich parameter set extractable from transiting planets, especially when combined with radial velocity observations [1]. A transit light curve encodes the planet-to-star radius ratio through the depth, the orbital period through recurrence, the scaled semi-major axis through the duration, and the impact parameter through the ingress and egress shape. When the transit chord samples the limb-darkened stellar disk, the curvature of the light curve constrains the stellar limb-darkening coefficients and refines the radius ratio.

The radial velocity method, by contrast, measures the line-of-sight reflex motion of the host star. Its observable is the Keplerian semi-amplitude *K*, which depends on the planet mass, the stellar mass, the period, the eccentricity, and the orbital inclination. Because only the projected quantity *m sin i* enters, radial velocities alone leave the inclination ambiguous; transits, astrometry, or direct imaging resolve it. Stellar activity — spots, plages, granulation, and magnetic cycles — imprints quasi-periodic signals that can mimic planets at the level of meters per second down to centimeters per second, making activity modeling the central methodological challenge of the Doppler field.

Direct imaging trades the statistical abundance of transits for direct photons: resolved images yield photometry, colors, and spectra of the planet itself, most sensitively for young, hot, self-luminous giants at tens of astronomical units. Gravitational microlensing and astrometry probe complementary niches — cold low-mass planets around faint stars, and true dynamical masses from stellar wobbles measured by *Gaia* and by interferometric facilities.

##3 Methodology

The methodological core of exoplanet characterization is *forward modeling plus Bayesian inference*: a physical model predicts observables from parameters, and a likelihood function compares predictions with data while nuisance parameters absorb instrumental and stellar systematics.

**Transit light-curve modeling.** The standard analytic framework of Mandel and Agol (2002) computes the flux blocked by an opaque disk crossing a limb-darkened star for arbitrary impact parameter, enabling rapid evaluation inside Markov chain Monte Carlo samplers. The quadratic limb-darkening law, in which the surface brightness falls as a quadratic function of the cosine of the emergent angle, is the workhorse parameterization; its coefficients may be fixed from stellar atmosphere models or fitted freely, trading bias against variance. Transit timing variations — deviations of individual mid-transit times from a linear ephemeris — betray additional planets or moons through their gravitational perturbations, with sensitivity to masses far below the radial velocity floor in resonant configurations.

**Radial velocity orbit fitting.** The stellar radial velocity is modeled as a sum of Keplerian signals plus a correlated noise term. Modern practice represents stellar activity with Gaussian process regression, using quasi-periodic covariance kernels whose hyperparameters are constrained jointly by the velocities and by spectroscopic activity indicators such as the cross-correlation function width, bisector span, and chromospheric emission indices. The multi-dimensional Gaussian process formalism propagates information from activity tracers into the velocity model, disentangling spot-induced modulations from genuine orbital signals.

**High-contrast imaging.** Starlight suppression combines extreme adaptive optics, coronagraphic masks, and differential post-processing. Angular differential imaging exploits field rotation to distinguish quasi-static speckles from a co-moving companion, and algorithms such as LOCI and KLIP build data-driven point-spread-function models from the science frames themselves.

**Atmospheric retrieval.** Retrieval inverts a spectrum into atmospheric properties — temperature structure, molecular abundances, cloud parameters, and reference radius — by coupling a radiative transfer forward model to a Bayesian sampler such as nested sampling. Because a transmission spectrum supplies only a handful of independent observables for many unknowns, as emphasized in systematic retrieval studies [8], priors and regularization are not optional embellishments but constitutive parts of the measurement. Modern frameworks cascade retrieval stages, starting with free-chemistry models and progressing to physically motivated constraints [7].

The following Python sketch illustrates how the fundamental observables combine into physical parameters:

```python
import numpy as np

G = 6.67430e-11          # gravitational constant, SI
R_sun = 6.957e8          # meters

def transit_depth(rp, rs):
    """Fractional flux dip for a central transit."""
    return (rp / rs) ** 2

def transit_duration_days(period_d, rs, a, b=0.0):
    """Approximate full transit duration for a circular orbit."""
    period_s = period_d * 86400.0
    return (period_s / np.pi) * (rs / a) * np.sqrt(max(0.0, 1.0 - b**2)) / 86400.0

def rv_semi_amplitude(mp_kg, mstar_kg, period_d, ecc=0.0, sini=1.0):
    """Doppler semi-amplitude K in m/s for a circular-ish orbit."""
    period_s = period_d * 86400.0
    k = (2.0 * np.pi * G / period_s) ** (1.0 / 3.0)
    return k * mp_kg * sini / (mstar_kg ** (2.0 / 3.0) * np.sqrt(1.0 - ecc**2))

def equilibrium_temperature(tstar, rs, a, albedo=0.3):
    """Zero-dimensional equilibrium temperature in kelvin."""
    return tstar * np.sqrt(rs / (2.0 * a)) * (1.0 - albedo) ** 0.25

# Earth analog around a Sun-like star:
print("depth ppm:", 1e6 * transit_depth(6.371e6, R_sun))
print("K (m/s):", rv_semi_amplitude(5.972e24, 1.989e30, 365.25))
print("Teq (K):", equilibrium_temperature(5778.0, R_sun, 1.496e11))
```

The Earth–Sun analog produces a transit depth of only about 84 parts per million and a Doppler signal of roughly 9 centimeters per second — numbers that define the frontier of both photometric and spectroscopic precision.
##4 Exoplanet Science: A Deep Dive

### 4.1 Transit Photometry and Light-Curve Inversion

The transit method's enduring dominance rests on a simple geometric fact: when a planet crosses the stellar disk, the fractional flux decrement equals the area ratio of planet to star, modulated by limb darkening and diluted by any unresolved flux in the aperture. For a central transit of a uniformly bright disk, the depth is *(Rp / Rstar)^2*; limb darkening rounds the flat bottom and softens the ingress and egress shoulders, while a nonzero impact parameter *b* shortens the chord and the total duration. Because the stellar density can be inferred from the transit duration and period under Kepler's third law, the light curve itself constrains the host star — a fact exploited in *photoeccentric* analyses of eccentric orbits.

Survey design, as reviewed comprehensively for the transit method [1], balances five factors: sample size, temporal coverage, photometric precision, target brightness, and the detection algorithm.

Beyond discovery, transits enable *transit timing variations* (TTVs).

> **Theorem:** *Geometric transit probability.* For a planet on a circular orbit of semi-major axis *a* around a star of radius *Rstar*, the probability that a randomly oriented observer witnesses a transit is *p = (Rstar + Rp) / a*, approximately *Rstar / a* when the planet is small. *Proof sketch:* a transit requires the orbital inclination to satisfy a bound on the cosine of the inclination set by the stellar and planetary radii; integrating the uniform distribution of orientations over the allowed band yields the stated ratio. For an Earth–Sun analog this probability is below one half of one percent, which is why transit surveys must monitor hundreds of thousands of stars.

### 4.2 The Radial-Velocity Method and Stellar Activity

The Doppler technique measures the star's line-of-sight velocity through shifts of thousands of spectral lines, cross-correlated against a template or fitted with a forward model of the instrumental profile.

The semi-amplitude relation governs survey sensitivity:

> **Lemma:** *Doppler semi-amplitude.* For a planet of mass *mp* orbiting a star of mass *Mstar* with period *P*, eccentricity *e*, and inclination *i*, the stellar radial velocity semi-amplitude is *K = (2πG / P)^(1/3) · mp sin i / (Mstar^(2/3) · sqrt(1 − e^2))*. The observable is therefore the *minimum mass* *mp sin i*; the true mass follows only when the inclination is known from transits, astrometry, or imaging.

Stellar activity remains the dominant noise source.

1. **Chromatic velocities** — measuring the Doppler shift separately in blue and red spectral regions, since spot-induced signals are wavelength dependent while planetary signals are achromatic.
2. **Activity indicators** — the bisector inverse span, the full width at half maximum of the cross-correlation function, and chromospheric indices track magnetic variability and constrain the Gaussian process hyperparameters.
3. **Line-by-line analysis** — rejecting spectral lines most sensitive to activity and weighting the remainder by their Doppler information content.
4. **Extended baselines** — separating the coherent Keplerian signal from quasi-periodic activity through sheer phase coverage.

These advances enabled the detection of a candidate sub-Earth around Proxima Centauri with a semi-amplitude of only 39 ± 7 centimeters per second and a minimum mass of about 0.26 Earth masses — roughly twice the mass of Mars — demonstrating that the Doppler method is approaching the regime of true Earth analogs around the nearest stars, even if stellar activity still sets the practical floor for Sun-like hosts.

### 4.3 Direct Imaging: Coronagraphy and High-Contrast Spectroscopy

Direct imaging resolves the planet as a distinct source of photons, yielding photometry, low-resolution spectra, and astrometry unobtainable by indirect means.

The Gemini Planet Imager Exoplanet Survey demonstrated the method's quantitative maturity by combining direct imaging astrometry of β Pictoris b with *Hipparcos* and *Gaia* absolute astrometry in a joint orbit fit, deriving a dynamical mass consistent with hot-start evolutionary models and measuring a nonzero eccentricity that disfavors circular orbits [3].

The frontier is spectroscopic.

### 4.4 Atmospheric Retrieval, the Mass–Radius Diagram, and Habitability

Atmospheric retrieval is the inversion of an observed spectrum into the physical state of the atmosphere: the pressure–temperature profile, molecular mixing ratios, cloud and haze properties, and the reference radius at a fixed pressure.

The James Webb Space Telescope has revolutionized the data side.

JWST is now pushing into the small-planet regime where habitability questions live.

The mass–radius diagram synthesizes transits and radial velocities into bulk densities, the zeroth-order composition diagnostic.

Habitability assessment layers additional metrics onto this foundation: the incident flux relative to Earth, the equilibrium temperature, the habitable zone boundaries from climate models, and observational figures of merit such as the transmission and emission spectroscopy metrics that rank targets for atmospheric follow-up.

##5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Proofs

The mature results of the field can be stated as quantitative achievements of each technique, anchored in the literature:

- **Transit surveys** have measured radii for thousands of planets, established the radius valley, and delivered the targets that make atmospheric characterization possible; the method's parameter yield and survey design principles are codified in the standard review literature [1].
- **Precision Doppler spectroscopy** reaches below 1 meter per second routinely and tens of centimeters per second in the best campaigns: ESPRESSO measured Proxima b at 1.17 Earth minimum masses and isolated a 39 centimeter-per-second candidate sub-Earth signal using Gaussian process activity modeling [2].
- **Direct imaging plus astrometry** delivers dynamical masses independent of evolutionary models: the β Pictoris b joint fit yields masses consistent with hot-start predictions and an eccentricity of 0.12 that excludes circular orbits [3].
- **JWST transmission spectroscopy** detects molecules at tens of sigma in giants [6], rules out hydrogen envelopes on temperate sub-Neptunes, and confronts the stellar contamination problem head-on [4], while the first steam-world atmosphere demonstrates retrieval of a heavy-molecule-dominated envelope [5].
- **Retrieval frameworks** now cascade from free-chemistry to physically constrained models with full covariance treatment, formalizing the information content of spectra and the degeneracies that limit it [7], in the tradition of systematic retrieval analyses that mapped how each spectral feature constrains each atmospheric parameter [8].

> **Theorem:** *Scale height and transmission signal.* The amplitude of spectral features in a transmission spectrum scales with the atmospheric scale height *H = kT / (μg)*, where *k* is Boltzmann's constant, *T* the temperature, *μ* the mean molecular mass, and *g* the surface gravity. The characteristic signal is approximately *2 · H · Rp / Rstar^2* per scale height probed. *Consequence:* hydrogen-dominated atmospheres produce features hundreds of parts per million in size for hot Jupiters, while high mean molecular weight atmospheres — water or nitrogen dominated — shrink the signal by an order of magnitude, which is precisely why the heavy atmospheres of small planets are the hardest and most prized retrieval targets.

A second theoretical pillar concerns survey completeness. Because the transit probability falls as the inverse of the orbital distance while the radial velocity amplitude falls as the inverse cube root of the period, the two methods sample nearly orthogonal slices of parameter space; occurrence-rate studies must correct each slice by its own detection efficiency before any statement about the true planet population is meaningful. This complementarity is not an inconvenience but the design principle of the field: the planets most amenable to atmospheric characterization — transiting, with measured masses — are exactly those found at the intersection of the methods.

##6 Limitations and Open Problems

For all its progress, exoplanet characterization faces limits that are fundamental rather than merely technical. **Stellar contamination** is foremost: unocculted spots and faculae imprint wavelength-dependent slopes on transmission spectra — the transit light source effect — that can mimic or mask molecular features at the tens of parts per million level, and the LHS 1140 b analysis shows that even quiet, slowly rotating M dwarfs require explicit heterogeneity modeling [4]. Disentangling stellar and planetary signals may ultimately demand simultaneous high-resolution stellar spectroscopy or out-of-transit monitoring at comparable precision.

**Retrieval degeneracies** persist even with exquisite data. Clouds and hazes truncate the pressure probed by transmission spectra, the reference radius trades against abundances, and the mean molecular weight of the background atmosphere is only weakly constrained without broad wavelength coverage. Bayesian evidence comparisons help, but model selection among families of cloud parameterizations remains partly subjective.

**The contrast wall** limits direct imaging to young or massive planets: reaching the ten-billion-to-one contrast needed for a reflected-light Earth analog requires space-based coronagraphy or starshades, and the inner working angle excludes the habitable zones of all but the nearest stars even for thirty-meter-class telescopes. **Microlensing** finds cold planets in abundance but never repeats, leaving masses entangled with lens distances. And **radial velocities** remain activity-limited for Sun-like stars at the 9 centimeter-per-second level of a true Earth analog — a frontier that may require understanding stellar surface physics at a level the field has not yet achieved.

##7 Conclusion

Exoplanet detection and characterization has matured from the single-planet discovery of 51 Pegasi b into a quantitative science of populations and atmospheres. Transit photometry supplies radii and, through light-curve inversion, orbital architecture; radial velocities supply masses and, through activity-aware modeling, access to the smallest planets; direct imaging supplies photons from the planets themselves and, with astrometry, dynamical masses; microlensing and astrometry complete the census at wide separations. Atmospheric retrieval binds these threads, converting spectra into compositions — from the photochemistry of WASP-39b to the steam envelope of GJ 9827 d to the contested air of LHS 1140 b — and the mass–radius diagram translates the results into interior structure and habitability context.

The open problems are worthy of the progress: stellar contamination at the parts-per-million level, retrieval degeneracies under cloudy skies, the contrast wall before true Earth twins, and the Doppler floor set by stellar magnetism. Each is being attacked with new instruments, new algorithms, and the statistical power of ever-larger samples. If the first thirty years of the field taught us that planets are ubiquitous and diverse beyond expectation, the task of the next thirty is to read their atmospheres — and, one day, to recognize in a spectrum the signature of a living world.
