# From Gentry's Blueprint to TFHE: Fully Homomorphic Encryption from Ideal Lattices to Practical Encrypted Computation

## Abstract

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) — evaluating arbitrary circuits on ciphertexts without decrypting — advanced in one decade from open problem to deployable technology. This thesis reconstructs that arc: Craig Gentry's 2009 blueprint, in which a *somewhat* homomorphic scheme over ideal lattices is lifted to full homomorphism by bootstrapping, whose correctness reduces to homomorphically evaluating the scheme's own decryption circuit [1]. We trace the second generation — BGV and BFV over (ring) learning-with-errors, whose *modulus switching* and *relinearization* enable leveled circuits without bootstrapping [2][4] — and the third: GSW's approximate-eigenvector noise analysis [3]; Ducas–Micciancio's FHEW, bootstrapping under one second [5]; and the TFHE family of Chillotti, Gama, Georgieva and Izabachène, which cut bootstrapping to milliseconds via the external product on the torus and enabled *programmable* bootstrapping [6][7]. We analyze Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song (CKKS) for approximate arithmetic, where rescaling keeps modulus growth linear [8], and close with empirical performance, deployment limitations, and open problems — compact multi-key FHE, sub-millisecond bootstrapping, and standardization — defining the frontier.

## 1 Introduction

In 1978, Rivest, Adleman and Dertouzos asked whether a third party could compute on encrypted data without learning either inputs or outputs — the *privacy homomorphism* problem. For thirty-one years the answer was partial: RSA and ElGamal supported a single operation, Paillier supported addition, and Boneh–Goh–Nissim supported unlimited additions but only one multiplication. The obstacle was *noise management*: every technique for homomorphic evaluation degraded ciphertext quality, and no mechanism recovered it.

Craig Gentry's 2009 dissertation shattered the impasse with a construction of striking self-reference: if a somewhat homomorphic scheme can evaluate its *own decryption function* — plus one extra operation — then it can refresh its own ciphertexts, resetting their noise and enabling unbounded computation [1]. This *bootstrapping theorem* turned the central difficulty into parameter engineering. Its architecture has survived every subsequent advance; essentially all known FHE constructions follow Gentry's two-stage blueprint of a somewhat homomorphic scheme plus a refresh mechanism [9].

Yet the field's history is equally a story of *making the blueprint cheap*. Gentry's original instantiation bootstrapped in tens of minutes with gigabyte keys. The second generation (Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan, 2011; Fan–Vercauteren, 2012) replaced ideal-lattice assumptions with (ring) learning-with-errors, introduced *modulus switching* to trade modulus size for noise in a controlled descent, and thereby obtained *leveled* FHE: arbitrary circuits of a-priori bounded depth, without any bootstrapping at all [2][4]. The third generation inverted the economics again: GSW (2013) linearized noise growth through the *approximate eigenvector* method [3], and then the FHEW/TFHE line (2015–2020) made bootstrapping so fast — first under a second [5], then under a tenth of a second [6] — that the optimal regime became bootstrapping after *every* gate. In parallel, CKKS (2017) abandoned exactness, embracing approximate arithmetic whose rescaling keeps modulus growth linear [8].

> **Theorem:** (Gentry's bootstrapping theorem, informal). Let $\mathcal{E}$ be a somewhat homomorphic encryption scheme whose decryption circuit $D$ has multiplicative depth at most $(\log\log N - \log\log n - 1)$, where $N$ is the maximum tolerable noise and $n$ the fresh-encryption noise. If $\mathcal{E}$ additionally supports one NAND gate homomorphically beyond its own decryption depth, then there exists a fully homomorphic encryption scheme $\mathcal{E}^\ast$ derived from $\mathcal{E}$ via the *Recrypt* (bootstrapping) transformation. [1]

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Lattices and the Learning-With-Errors problem

A lattice $\mathcal{L} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is the integer span of a basis $B$; lattice cryptography rests on the conjectured hardness (even for quantum algorithms) of problems like shortest and closest vector — the post-quantum motivation beneath the entire field.

The workhorse assumption of modern FHE is the **Learning With Errors** (LWE) problem of Regev (2005): given samples $(\mathbf{a}_i, b_i = \langle \mathbf{a}_i, \mathbf{s} \rangle + e_i)$ with $\mathbf{a}_i$ uniform and $e_i$ drawn from a narrow Gaussian, recover the secret $\mathbf{s}$. Its ring variant (RLWE) works over $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^N+1)$, enabling SIMD *batching* of $N/2$ plaintext slots per ciphertext. Every scheme below elaborates the following template:

$$\mathrm{Enc}_{\mathbf{s}}(m) = (\mathbf{a},\, b = \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle + e + \Delta \cdot m),$$

where $\Delta$ is a scaling factor separating message from noise, and decryption succeeds iff the accumulated noise $|e| < \Delta/2$ (or $< q/4$ in torus formulations). Homomorphic addition adds noise; homomorphic multiplication *multiplies* it. The central engineering quantity is therefore the **noise budget**: how much computation a ciphertext tolerates before $|e|$ crosses the threshold and decryption fails.

### 2.2 The noise model and the depth barrier

Naive noise growth is doubly exponential in multiplicative depth $L$, since each multiplication squares both dimension and noise. BGV's *modulus switching* [2] — switching a ciphertext from modulus $q$ to a smaller modulus $p$ by scaling and rounding — converts this to single-exponential growth, after which *relinearization* (key switching) restores the original dimension. The price is parameter growth: the modulus chain $q_0 > q_1 > \dots > q_L$ must absorb $L$ levels, so leveled FHE requires the circuit depth in advance.

Bootstrapping escapes this bargain entirely. Given a ciphertext $c$ with high noise decryptable under $\mathbf{s}_1$, one encrypts $\mathbf{s}_1$ under a fresh key $\mathbf{s}_2$ (the *bootstrapping key*, invoking a *circular security* assumption), and homomorphically evaluates the decryption circuit on the encrypted key bits:

$$\mathrm{Recrypt}(c) = \mathrm{Eval}_{\mathrm{pk}_2}\big(D,\, \mathrm{Enc}_{\mathrm{pk}_2}(\mathbf{s}_1),\, c\big) = \mathrm{Enc}_{\mathbf{s}_2}(m).$$

The output's noise depends only on the noise growth of the *decryption circuit* — a fixed, small circuit — not on the input noise. Bootstrapping is thus a *noise reset*: an unbounded-depth evaluator emerges from a bounded-depth one [1][9].

### 2.3 Security foundations and circular security

A subtlety of bootstrapping is the publication of $\mathrm{Enc}_{\mathrm{pk}_2}(\mathbf{s}_1)$ — and, in key-switching-heavy variants, of $\mathrm{Enc}_{\mathrm{pk}}(\mathbf{s})$ under its *own* key. This demands **circular (KDM) security**: security when encrypting key-dependent messages. No reduction from LWE to circular security is known; it stands as the field's most durable nonstandard assumption, accepted pragmatically because no attack exploiting it has emerged in fifteen years of cryptanalysis. The HomomorphicEncryption.org community security standard (2018, updated 2021) codifies 128-bit parameter sets with these assumptions explicit.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis is comparative-constructive: for each generation we isolate the **noise-growth recurrence** (which determines whether bootstrapping is needed and at what cost), quantify the **decryption-circuit depth** (Gentry's theorem makes bootstrappability a property of decryption), and benchmark published bootstrapping timings and ciphertext sizes at $\lambda \approx 128$.

To ground the noise model, we present a minimal (insecure, toy-parameter) LWE cryptosystem in Python, faithful in structure to the real schemes. The invariant `|e| < Delta/2` is the entire game: every technique below — modulus switching, relinearization, the GSW approximate eigenvector trick, the torus external product — is a device for keeping this inequality true across more computation.

```python
import numpy as np

def keygen(n=256, q=4096):
    s = np.random.randint(0, 2, n)          # binary secret
    return s

def encrypt(s, m, q=4096, sigma=3.2, Delta=None):
    n = len(s)
    Delta = Delta or q // 4
    a = np.random.randint(0, q, n)
    e = int(np.random.normal(0, sigma))     # LWE noise
    b = (a @ s + e + Delta * m) % q
    return (a, b, Delta)

def decrypt(s, ct, q=4096):
    a, b, Delta = ct
    v = (b - a @ s) % q
    # centered lift, then round away the noise
    v = v - q if v > q // 2 else v
    return round(v / Delta) % 2

s = keygen()
c1, c2 = encrypt(s, 1), encrypt(s, 1)
# homomorphic XOR ~ addition mod 2 (noise adds: e1 + e2)
a, b = (c1[0] + c2[0]) % 4096, (c1[1] + c2[1]) % 4096
assert decrypt(s, (a, b, c1[2])) == 0       # 1 + 1 = 0 mod 2, noise doubled
```

The depth–noise tradeoff across generations admits a compact functional summary:

```haskell

```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Gentry's blueprint: ideal lattices, squashing, and the first bootstrapping

Gentry's 2009 construction rests on **ideal lattices** — lattices closed under ring multiplication, so products of lattice points give a natural homomorphism from ring operations to lattice operations [1]. A bit $m$ encrypts as $\mathbf{c} = m + 2\mathbf{r} + J$-noise inside an ideal $J$; the secret key is a short basis, the public key a long one, and decryption reduces $\mathbf{c}$ modulo the good basis and reads parity. Ciphertext addition and multiplication follow ideal arithmetic; noise roughly squares per multiplication, so only shallow circuits survive.

With squashing, decryption depth drops below the scheme's homomorphic capacity, the bootstrappability condition is met, and Recrypt yields full homomorphism [1] — at staggering cost: the subset sum had to be sparse enough while resisting lattice attacks, demanding dimension $n \approx 2^{15}$ and gigabyte keys. The blueprint was proven correct; making it *practical* became the field's decade-long project.

### 4.2 The second generation: BGV, BFV, and leveled homomorphism without bootstrapping

Brakerski, Gentry and Vaikuntanathan (2011) rebuilt the blueprint on standard **(R)LWE** assumptions, discarding ideal lattices and squashing, with two innovations [2].

**Modulus switching.** Given a ciphertext $(a, b)$ modulo $q$ with noise $e$, define $(a', b') = \lfloor (p/q) \cdot (a, b) \rceil$ modulo a smaller $p$. The new noise is approximately $(p/q)e$ plus an $O(\sqrt{n})$ rounding term — *noise shrinks with the modulus*. With a descending chain $q_L > q_{L-1} > \dots > q_0$ and one switch per multiplication, the noise-to-modulus ratio stays invariant: exponential absolute noise growth becomes *linear* growth in required modulus bit-length. The scheme is *leveled*: it evaluates any circuit of depth at most $L$, fixed at key generation.

**Key switching / relinearization.** Tensoring two ciphertexts yields one decryptable only under the tensor secret $\mathbf{s} \otimes \mathbf{s}$ — dimension $n^2$. Key switching publishes encryptions of the tensor key's components under a fresh linear key and collapses the ciphertext back to dimension $n$, at additive noise cost controlled by the decomposition base.

Fan and Vercauteren (2012) gave a scale-invariant variant (BFV) with cleaner noise analysis — the default for exact integer arithmetic in SEAL and OpenFHE [4].

| Scheme | Year | Assumption | Noise control | Bootstrapping needed? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gentry | 2009 | Ideal lattices (+ sparse subset sum) | Squashing + Recrypt | Yes — definitional |
| BGV | 2011 | (R)LWE | Modulus switching, leveled | No (leveled); optional |
| BFV | 2012 | RLWE | Scale-invariant variant | No (leveled); optional |
| GSW | 2013 | LWE | Approximate eigenvector | Optional |
| FHEW | 2015 | LWE (+ ring) | Gate bootstrapping | Yes — every gate |
| TFHE | 2016–2020 | LWE/RLWE on the torus | External product | Yes — every gate |
| CKKS | 2017 | RLWE | Rescaling | Optional (expensive) |

### 4.3 GSW, FHEW, and the inversion of the bootstrapping economics

Gentry, Sahai and Waters (2013) recast ciphertexts so decryption is *linear* in the secret key with a twist: $C \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mu \cdot \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{e}$ — an **approximate eigenvector** relation with message $\mu$ as eigenvalue [3]. Multiplication becomes matrix multiplication with *asymmetric* noise growth: multiplying a low-noise ciphertext by a high-noise one adds noise proportional only to the low-noise factor — polynomial in depth, not exponential. No relinearization keys are needed, and homomorphic NAND becomes cheap enough to contemplate bootstrapping after every gate.

Ducas and Micciancio (2015) executed that inversion. Building on the Alperin-Sheriff–Peikert variant, FHEW evaluates boolean circuits gate by gate, bootstrapping after each gate via a homomorphic *accumulator*: the phase of an LWE sample is rounded by rotating a test polynomial. Their headline result — bootstrapping in **under one second** (~690 ms, versus tens of minutes in 2009) — changed the calculus: if bootstrapping is the cheapest noise-management tool, use it everywhere and abandon leveled parameter planning [5].

### 4.4 TFHE: the torus, the external product, and programmable bootstrapping

Chillotti, Gama, Georgieva and Izabachène (2016) generalized FHEW onto the **real torus** $\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$, unifying LWE, RLWE and GSW samples as one structure with continuous messages [6]. The engine is the **external product** $\boxdot$ between a TGSW and a TLWE/TRLWE ciphertext: it multiplies plaintexts while noise grows only *additively* in the TGSW noise times the message bound. Gate bootstrapping then runs in three phases — **blind rotation** (homomorphically compute $X^{-\phi} \cdot \mathrm{testv}$ in an accumulator via one CMux per secret-key bit), **sample extraction** (pull a TLWE sample from the accumulator), and **key switching** — all driven by the bootstrapping key.

The full journal version (2020) adds *circuit bootstrapping* — converting TLWE ciphertexts into TRLWE/TRGSW form so leveled operations compose with gate bootstrapping — and packed operations amortizing many gates; measured bootstrapping fell from **under 0.1 seconds** [6] to roughly **10 milliseconds** in optimized implementations (TFHE-rs, Concrete) [7].

The deepest TFHE innovation is **programmable bootstrapping** (Chillotti–Joye–Paillier, CSCML 2021): the test polynomial in blind rotation can encode *any* univariate function $f$, so bootstrapping simultaneously refreshes the ciphertext *and* evaluates $f$ on the plaintext — neural-network activations, comparisons, lookup tables become free side-effects of the mandatory noise refresh, making TFHE the leading platform for encrypted ML inference.

A Rust sketch of the blind-rotation core (simplified; real code manages decomposition bases and gadget vectors):

```rust
// ACC <- X^{-phi} * testv, one CMux per secret bit (toy sketch)
fn blind_rotate(acc: &mut Trlwe, a: &[i32], bk: &[Trgsw], testv: &Polynomial) {
    // start from testv * X^{b} (b = TLWE body term)
    *acc = trlwe_of_polynomial(testv);
    for (ai, bki) in a.iter().zip(bk.iter()) {
        // CMux(bki, X^{ai} * acc, acc): select rotated or not, homomorphically
        let rotated = acc.mul_by_monomial(*ai);
        *acc = cmux(bki, &rotated, acc);   // external product inside
    }
    // acc now encrypts testv * X^{-phi}; extract TLWE sample, key-switch, done
}
```

### 4.5 CKKS: approximate arithmetic and the rescaling insight

Cheon, Kim, Kim and Song (2017) asked a heretical question: what if the plaintext need not be exact [8]? Machine learning and statistics tolerate rounding error; insisting on exact decryption was a self-imposed tax. CKKS encrypts vectors $\mathbf{m} \in \mathbb{C}^{N/2}$ via the **canonical embedding** $\sigma: R \to \mathbb{C}^{N/2}$, scaled by $\Delta$:

$$\mathrm{Ecd}(\mathbf{m}) = \lfloor \Delta \cdot \sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{m}) \rceil \in R.$$

Because $\sigma$ is an isometric ring homomorphism, encoding is almost homomorphic: $\mathrm{Ecd}(\mathbf{m}_1)\cdot\mathrm{Ecd}(\mathbf{m}_2) \approx \Delta \cdot \mathrm{Ecd}(\mathbf{m}_1 \odot \mathbf{m}_2)$. The **rescaling** operation $\mathrm{RS}(c) = \lfloor \Delta^{-1} c \rceil \bmod q/\Delta$ divides out the extra $\Delta$, rounding the least significant bits exactly as floating-point rounds its mantissa. The precision analysis is the paper's crown jewel: CKKS loses *at most one bit more* precision than unencrypted floating-point evaluation, and required modulus bit-length grows only *linearly* with depth [8]. CKKS bootstrapping (Cheon et al., 2018) exists but is heavy — homomorphic modular reduction via sine/cosine approximation at ~20 bits of precision — an active optimization literature [9].

A TLA+ sketch of the rescaling invariant a model checker would verify of any CKKS implementation:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE CKKSRescale ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
CONSTANTS Delta, L
VARIABLES level, noise, plaintext
Init == level = L /\ noise < Delta / 2 /\ plaintext \in Real
Rescale ==
    /\ level > 0
    /\ level' = level - 1
    /\ plaintext' = plaintext / Delta
    /\ noise' <= noise / Delta + 0.5     \* rounding error, bounded
    /\ noise' < Delta / 2               \* invariant preserved
Next == Rescale
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<level, noise, plaintext>>
=============================================================================
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The bootstrapping cost curve, 2009–2025

Bootstrapping latency at $\approx$128-bit security has improved roughly six orders of magnitude:

| Year | Scheme / implementation | Bootstrapping latency | Ciphertext size (order) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2009 | Gentry (theory) | tens of minutes (est.) | GB-scale keys |
| 2011 | Gentry–Halevi impl. | ~30 min (large params) | GB |
| 2015 | FHEW [5] | ~690 ms | KB |
| 2016 | TFHE [6] | < 100 ms | KB |
| 2020 | TFHE (concrete lib) | ~10–30 ms | ~KB per gate ciphertext |
| 2024 | TFHE-rs / Concrete (Zama), GPU/FPGA ports | ~10 ms gate bootstrap, lower amortized | KB |

The 2016 TFHE paper's measured "less than 0.1 seconds" [6] crossed the threshold where bootstrapping per gate becomes an engineering default. Programmable bootstrapping then amortized *arbitrary univariate functions* into the same ~10 ms budget, enabling encrypted neural-network inference inconceivable under leveled exact schemes. The workload split is now stable: exact schemes (BFV/BGV) for encrypted databases and private set intersection, TFHE for boolean and comparison-heavy workloads, CKKS for real-valued encrypted ML [8][9].

### 5.2 Proof sketches

> **Theorem:** (GSW asymmetric noise growth [3]). The product of GSW ciphertexts encrypts $\mu_1\mu_2$ with noise $\approx \mu_2 e_1 + e_2 \cdot \mathrm{poly}(n)$ — asymmetric in the operands.

*Proof sketch.* From $C_i v = \mu_i v + e_i$: $(C_1 C_2) v = \mu_1\mu_2 v + \mu_2 e_1 + C_1 e_2$. Since $C_1$ has small entries (bit decomposition), $\|C_1 e_2\| \le \|e_2\|\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n)$, and the dominant term is $\mu_2 e_1$ — asymmetric in the operands. For boolean $\mu_2 \in \{0,1\}$, multiplying by a fresh low-noise ciphertext adds almost no noise. ∎

> **Theorem:** (CKKS precision bound [8]). For a circuit of depth $L$, CKKS decryption error exceeds unencrypted floating-point evaluation error by at most one bit, provided $\log q_L = O(L \log \Delta)$.

*Proof sketch.* Each rescaling contributes rounding error bounded by half a ULP relative to the current scale $\Delta$; encoding contributes one rounding; encryption noise is $O(\sigma\sqrt{N}) \ll \Delta$. By induction over levels, total error is $(L+1)$ roundings of relative size $2^{-\log\Delta}$ — the same count as floating-point evaluation plus one. ∎

## 6 Limitations

**The circular-security assumption endures.** Every bootstrapped deployment publishes key-dependent encryptions. Fifteen years of cryptanalysis have produced no attack, but no reduction from LWE exists; a break would collapse every bootstrapped FHE deployment simultaneously — a correlated risk under-discussed in applied work.

**Ciphertext expansion and throughput.** Even at ~10 ms per gate bootstrap, TFHE evaluates boolean circuits roughly $10^6$–$10^9\times$ slower than plaintext; a 64-bit encrypted multiplication costs thousands of gate bootstraps, and ciphertexts are kilobytes per *bit* in gate-bootstrapping mode. CKKS amortizes via batching ($N/2$ slots per ciphertext), but its bootstrapping remains precision-limited (~20 bits [9]) and expensive.

**The single-key model.** Standard FHE assumes all inputs encrypted under one key. Multi-key FHE exists in theory, but ciphertext size grows with the number of keys and no implementation has seen wide deployment; threshold FHE is the pragmatic substitute.

**Side channels and malicious servers.** Deployed FHE provides *circuit privacy* only in the semi-honest model; a malicious evaluator can deviate undetectably, and software timing/memory-access side channels remain an active hardening area.

**Standardization lag.** FHE has no finalized NIST standard; the HomomorphicEncryption.org community standard is voluntary, and parameters across libraries (SEAL, OpenFHE, TFHE-rs, Lattigo) are not fully interoperable.

## 7 Conclusion

The trajectory from Gentry's 2009 blueprint to TFHE shows how a *conceptual* breakthrough — bootstrapping as noise reset [1] — creates a decades-long *engineering* program: modulus switching made bootstrapping optional [2], the approximate eigenvector made it cheap per gate [3][5], the torus external product made it fast [6][7], programmability made it expressive, and rescaling made approximate arithmetic nearly free [8]. Bootstrapping latency fell from tens of minutes to ~10 milliseconds; the question shifted from "can we compute on encrypted data?" to "which computations justify the overhead?"

The frontier now lies in three directions: **hardware** (DARPA's DPRIVE program and commercial accelerators target sub-millisecond bootstrapping via ASICs), **multi-key and threshold FHE** (removing the single-key assumption without linear blowup), and **theory** (proving circular security from LWE, or bootstrapping without it — the one assumption Gentry's blueprint never discharged). The grail, it turns out, was a starting line.
