# Differential Privacy under Continual Observation: The Binary Tree Mechanism, Pan-Privacy, and Streaming Heavy Hitters

## Abstract

Classical differential privacy sanitizes a *static* database once. Modern telemetry, however, never stops: search logs, network counters, and sensor feeds demand fresh statistics at *every* time step, while an adversary watches every release. Differential privacy was built for *static* databases, but telemetry never stops: search logs, network counters, and sensor feeds demand fresh statistics at *every* time step while an adversary watches every release. This is the **continual observation** model [1,2]. Naive release composes privacy loss across time until the noise swamps the signal. The remedy — the **binary tree mechanism** — organizes a stream into dyadic partial sums: each item perturbs only $\log T$ noisy counters, and each prefix sum assembles from at most $\log T$ of them, achieving $\varepsilon$-DP with $O(\log^2 T/\varepsilon)$ error [2]. We develop the theory: the p-sum framework, tight bounds via matrix factorization [4], the smooth binary refinement [5], **pan-privacy** against state-inspecting adversaries [3], and private heavy hitters and distinct elements under continual release [6,7,8] — plus a simulation verifying the polylogarithmic scaling.

---

##1 Intro

Differential privacy was born in the *one-shot* setting: a curator holds a fixed database $D$, answers a fixed query workload, adds calibrated noise once, and publishes [1]. The analysis is clean because the adversary sees exactly one randomized output. But the databases that matter most in 2026 are not fixed. They are **streams**: a search engine's query log, a cellular network's handover counters, a hospital's syndromic surveillance feed. In each case the curator is expected to publish *updated* statistics continuously — a running count, a top-$k$ list, a histogram — while the data keeps arriving and the adversary keeps watching.

This demand gives rise to the **continual observation** (or *continual release*) model [1,2]. Formally, the input is a stream $\sigma = \sigma(1), \sigma(2), \dots, \sigma(T)$ of items, and the mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ must output an estimate $\tilde{c}(t)$ of a target statistic $c(t)$ **at every time step** $t \in [T]$. The privacy requirement is correspondingly strengthened: the *entire vector* of releases $(\tilde{c}(1), \dots, \tilde{c}(T))$ must be differentially private with respect to the stream. An adversary who monitors the dashboard from $t = 1$ onward must learn essentially nothing about any single stream element.

The two obvious strategies for continual counting of a binary stream both fail:

1. **Noise on the output.** Release $\tilde{c}(t) = \sum_{i \le t}\sigma(i) + \mathrm{Lap}(T/\varepsilon)$ at each step. Flipping one bit changes *every* subsequent release: $\ell_1$-sensitivity $T$, noise scale $T/\varepsilon$.
2. **Noise on the input.** Store each bit noisily as $\tilde{\sigma}(t) = \sigma(t) + \mathrm{Lap}(1/\varepsilon)$ and release prefix sums of the noisy bits. Sensitivity is constant, but each prefix sum aggregates $t$ noise terms — standard deviation $\Theta(\sqrt{T}/\varepsilon)$ at time $T$.

Both extremes fail because they couple the noise either to the *horizon* $T$ or to the *time* $t$. The binary tree mechanism splits the difference: logarithmic sensitivity *and* logarithmically many noise terms per release. It is the canonical primitive of streaming private analytics, underlying private telemetry [1,5], private online learning [4,10], and private frequency-moment estimation.

This article covers the model (event-level vs. user-level adjacency, the p-sum framework), the mechanism and its $O(\log^2 T/\varepsilon)$ guarantee, tight bounds via matrix factorization [4], the smooth binary refinement [5], **pan-privacy** against state-inspecting adversaries [3], applications to heavy hitters and distinct elements [6,7,8], an empirical simulation, and open problems.

##2 Background

### Differential privacy on streams

Recall the standard definition. Two databases $D, D'$ are *neighbors* if they differ in one record. A randomized mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ is $\varepsilon$-differentially private if for all neighboring $D, D'$ and all measurable $S$,

$$\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S].$$

On streams, adjacency needs care. Dwork et al. [1] distinguish **event-level privacy** (streams differ in a single position — one event added, removed, or altered, protecting individual actions) from **user-level privacy** (streams differ in *all* events of one user — strictly stronger and much harder). Below, "differential privacy" means event-level unless stated otherwise; user-level is discussed in §6.

> **Definition (Continual observation [1]):** A mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ mapping streams $\sigma \in \{0,1\}^T$ to output sequences $(\mathcal{M}(\sigma)(1), \dots, \mathcal{M}(\sigma)(T))$ is $\varepsilon$-differentially private under continual observation if the *joint distribution* of all $T$ outputs satisfies $\varepsilon$-DP with respect to event-level adjacency of streams.

Note what this rules out: per-release $\mathrm{Lap}(1/\varepsilon)$ noise is $T\varepsilon$-DP *jointly* by basic composition — vacuous for large $T$. The challenge is joint privacy with noise growing merely polylogarithmically in $T$.

### The Laplace mechanism and composition

The workhorse is the Laplace mechanism: for a function $f$ with $\ell_1$-sensitivity $\Delta = \max_{D \sim D'}\|f(D) - f(D')\|_1$, releasing $f(D) + \mathrm{Lap}(\Delta/\varepsilon)$ is $\varepsilon$-DP. Two facts matter: **parallel composition** (disjoint blocks don't accumulate sensitivity) and **post-processing** (any function of a private release stays private). The binary tree mechanism is an ingenious choice of *which* partial sums to privatize so that both the sensitivity and the noise terms per query are logarithmic.

### Streaming constraints

A streaming algorithm processes $\sigma(t)$ once, in order, with memory sublinear in $T$. The binary mechanism needs only $O(\log T)$ counters: at any time, the *active* p-sums — intervals started but not yet ended — number at most $\log T$ [2].

##3 Methodology

### The p-sum framework

Chan, Shi, and Song [2] abstract the design space as the **p-sum framework**. A *p-sum* $\Sigma[i,j]$ denotes the sum of stream elements over an interval $[i,j]$ (inclusive). A mechanism in this framework:

1. Selects a family $\mathcal{P}$ of intervals (p-sums) it intends to release.
2. Streams the input, maintaining an exact counter $\beta_{i,j}$ for each *active* p-sum.
3. When p-sum $\Sigma[i,j]$ completes at time $j$, releases the noisy value $\hat{\Sigma}[i,j] = \beta_{i,j} + \mathrm{Lap}(\lambda)$ for a calibrated $\lambda$, and discards the counter.
4. Answers the prefix-sum query $c(t) = \sum_{i \le t}\sigma(i)$ by summing a small collection of released p-sums that exactly covers $[1,t]$.

Privacy analysis reduces to one question: *how many p-sums does one input element influence?* If each $\sigma(t)$ belongs to at most $k$ p-sums, the released p-sum vector has $\ell_1$-sensitivity at most $k$, so noise scale $\lambda = k/\varepsilon$ gives $\varepsilon$-DP — and every prefix sum assembled from them is free by post-processing. Utility reduces to the dual question: *how many noisy p-sums cover $[1,t]$?* If the answer is $\ell$, the estimate carries $\ell$ independent $\mathrm{Lap}(k/\varepsilon)$ noise terms.

### The dyadic choice

The binary mechanism takes $\mathcal{P}$ to be the **dyadic intervals** — equivalently, a complete binary tree over the $T$ leaves, each internal node storing its descendant leaves' p-sum. Two combinatorial facts do the work:

- Each position $t$ lies in exactly one dyadic interval per level, hence in at most $\lceil\log_2 T\rceil + 1$ p-sums total. So $k = O(\log T)$ and $\lambda = O(\log T/\varepsilon)$.
- Every prefix $[1,t]$ decomposes into at most $\lceil\log_2 T\rceil$ dyadic intervals (read off the binary representation of $t$). So $\ell = O(\log T)$.

Hence both privacy and utility cost only a $\log T$ factor — polylogarithmic where both naive approaches were polynomial.

### Python reference implementation

The following compact implementation mirrors the analysis exactly: precompute noisy dyadic p-sums, then cover each prefix dyadically.

```python
import numpy as np

def binary_mechanism(x, eps, rng):
    T = len(x)
    L = int(np.ceil(np.log2(T))) + 1   # levels; sensitivity per element
    scale = L / eps                    # Laplace scale per p-sum
    cache = {}
    def noisy_psum(a, b):               # dyadic interval [a, b)
        if (a, b) not in cache:
            cache[(a, b)] = float(x[a:b].sum()) + rng.laplace(0, scale)
        return cache[(a, b)]
    def dyadic_cover(t):               # cover [0, t) dyadically
        pieces, a = [], 0
        while a < t:
            k = 0
            while a + 2*(1 << k) <= t and a % (2*(1 << k)) == 0:
                k += 1
            pieces.append((a, a + (1 << k)))
            a += (1 << k)
        return pieces
    return np.array([sum(noisy_psum(a, b) for a, b in dyadic_cover(t))
                     for t in range(1, T + 1)])
```

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The binary tree mechanism: privacy and utility

We now state the mechanism's guarantees precisely.

> **Theorem (Binary mechanism privacy [1,2]):** For a binary stream of length $T$, releasing all noisy dyadic p-sums with $\mathrm{Lap}(\lceil\log_2 T\rceil/\varepsilon)$ noise is $\varepsilon$-differentially private under continual observation.

*Proof sketch.* Each stream element lies in at most $\lceil\log_2 T\rceil$ dyadic intervals, one per tree level, so the p-sum vector has $\ell_1$-sensitivity at most $\lceil\log_2 T\rceil$. Laplace noise at that scale is $\varepsilon$-DP; prefix sums are post-processing. ∎

> **Theorem (Binary mechanism utility [2]):** With probability at least $1 - \beta$, every released prefix sum satisfies $|\tilde{c}(t) - c(t)| = O\!\left(\frac{\log^2 T}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{T}{\beta}\right)$ simultaneously for all $t \in [T]$.

*Proof sketch.* Each $\tilde{c}(t)$ sums at most $\log T$ independent $\mathrm{Lap}(\log T/\varepsilon)$ variables; a Laplace tail bound plus a union bound over the $T$ time steps gives the stated $O(\log^2(T)/\varepsilon)$ simultaneous guarantee [2]. ∎

A comparison of the design space:

| Mechanism | Noise per release | Max error ($T=2^{20}$) | Space |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive output noise | $\mathrm{Lap}(T/\varepsilon)$ | $\Theta(T/\varepsilon)$ | $O(1)$ |
| Naive input noise | $t$ terms of $\mathrm{Lap}(1/\varepsilon)$ | $\Theta(\sqrt{T}/\varepsilon)$ | $O(1)$ |
| **Binary tree [2]** | $\le \log T$ terms of $\mathrm{Lap}(\log T/\varepsilon)$ | $O(\log^2 T/\varepsilon)$ | $O(\log T)$ |
| Smooth binary [5] ($\rho$-zCDP) | variance $(1+o(1))\frac{\log^2 T}{8\rho}$ | $4 - o(1)$ variance gain | $O(\log T)$ |
| Matrix/factorization [4,10] | correlated Gaussian via $A = LR$ | near-optimal constants | $O(T)$ offline |

At $T = 2^{20} \approx 10^6$ and $\varepsilon = 1$, naive output noise needs scale $\approx 10^6$ while the binary mechanism's error is on the order of $400$ — confirmed empirically in §5.

### 4.2 Tight bounds: the factorization view

The binary mechanism is one point in a larger design space. Releasing all $T$ prefix sums is the linear map $y = Ax$ with $A$ the lower-triangular all-ones matrix; a *factorization mechanism* writes $A = LR$ and releases $\hat{y} = L(Rx + z)$ for calibrated noise $z$ [4].

> **Theorem (Almost-tight bounds [4]):** For $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP under continual observation, an explicit factorization of the counting matrix achieves near-optimal mean-squared error, and no mechanism does asymptotically better: the lower bound follows from $\gamma_F(A) \ge \|A\|_1/\sqrt{m}$ on the factorization norm in terms of the Schatten-1 norm — the first tight lower bound for continual counting under approximate DP, holding even against a non-adaptive adversary.

In short, the $\log T$ dependence is *inherent*, not an artifact of the tree. Remaining gains are in constants and efficiency: Henzinger and Upadhyay [10] used group-algebra techniques for improved factorizations, and the smooth binary mechanism [5] cuts variance by $4 - o(1)$ while making the error distribution *identical at every time step* — convenient for downstream analyses.

### 4.3 Refinements: smoothness, unbounded streams, and expiration

Three practical refinements deserve attention:

1. **Smoothness.** The original mechanism's error distribution varies with $t$ (prefixes whose binary representation has many 1-bits sum more noise terms). The smooth binary mechanism [5] restructures the tree so that $\mathrm{Var}[\mathcal{M}(t)] = (1+o(1))\log^2(T)/(8\rho)$ *for every* $t$, keeping $O(\log T)$ space and $O(T)$ total time.
2. **Unknown horizon.** If $T$ is unknown, a doubling trick runs the mechanism in geometrically growing epochs — error grows as $\log^2 t$ at time $t$ with no prior bound needed [1].
3. **Privacy expiration.** Classical models treat every element as equally sensitive forever; in telemetry, recent events are more sensitive than old ones. Recent work gives continual counters whose privacy loss *decays* gracefully with age rather than vanishing abruptly [9], matching how real deployments "refresh" budgets.

### 4.4 Pan-privacy: when the adversary reads the machine's mind

Continual observation protects the *outputs*. But consider a subpoena served on the curator mid-stream: the adversary now inspects the algorithm's **internal state** — the exact, un-noised partial counters sitting in memory. Standard continual-observation mechanisms fail catastrophically here, because their state contains raw running totals.

**Pan-privacy** [3] demands that privacy survive such intrusions: the joint distribution of (internal state at intrusion time, all subsequent outputs) must be differentially private in the stream. Dwork et al. initiated this study for streaming algorithms, giving pan-private constructions for distinct elements and histograms — the regime where sketches like Count-Min or subsampling are tempting but dangerous, precisely because they retain identifiable information.

> **Theorem (Pan-private p-sums [2, §6]):** Any mechanism in the p-sum framework can be transformed into one that is $\varepsilon$-pan-private against a *single unannounced intrusion*, with only a constant-factor loss in utility.

The vulnerability is that active p-sum counters hold *exact* counts while awaiting completion. The transformation restructures *when* noise enters — correlating noise across internal state and released values so that what an intruder finds in memory is already randomized, changing output utility by only a constant factor. Thus the whole binary-mechanism family, including extensions to top-$k$ and heavy hitters, can be made intrusion-resilient. Pan-privacy is strictly stronger than continual observation and weaker than local DP — the right notion whenever the curator itself is a plausible adversary, from criminal investigations repurposing research data to governments pressuring telemetry collectors [3].

### 4.5 Beyond counting: heavy hitters and distinct elements

Counting is the primitive; the applications are the payoff.

**Heavy hitters under continual release.** Finding items whose frequency exceeds a $\phi$-fraction of the stream is the workhorse of network monitoring and trend detection. The difficulty is that heavy-hitter identities change over time, and naive approaches query every universe element at every step — $\Omega(|U|)$ per update. The modern approach [7] runs Misra–Gries summaries on blocks at $O(\log W)$ geometric scales and stitches them with a binary tree mechanism over a sliding window, adding Laplace noise calibrated to the tree's sensitivity. *Lazy-update* private sketches [8] perturb only a small rotating slice of the sketch per step, cutting per-update cost from $\Omega(|U|)$ to $O(d\log w)$ — a reported $250\times$ throughput gain.

**Distinct elements in the turnstile model.** With insertions only, private distinct counting under continual observation has polylogarithmic error. Allow *deletions* and the landscape fractures: Jain et al. [6] proved that *every* DP turnstile mechanism has worst-case additive error $\Omega(T^{1/4})$, even under weak event-level privacy and unbounded memory. But they identified the governing parameter: the **maximum flippancy** $w$, the number of times any item's contribution flips between zero and nonzero. Their item-level mechanism achieves $O(\sqrt{w}\,\mathrm{polylog}\,T)$ error without knowing $w$ in advance [6] — recovering polylogarithmic error on the low-flippancy streams that dominate practice, via coordinated instantiations of the binary tree mechanism. The same primitives yield $\ell_p$ frequency-moment estimation with $(1+\eta)$-relative approximation and $\mathrm{polylog}(Tn)$ additive error in near-optimal space [7].

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### Simulation: binary mechanism vs. naive composition

To verify the asymptotics, we simulated the naive per-release mechanism and the binary tree mechanism on random binary streams, measuring $\max_t |\tilde{c}(t) - c(t)|$ over 60 trials at $\varepsilon = 1$.

| Stream length $T$ | Binary mechanism (mean max err) | Naive per-release (mean max err) | $\log_2^2 T$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 256 | 94.3 | — | 64 |
| 1,024 | 127.3 (p95: 171.9) | 7,637.5 (p95: 9,725.2) | 100 |
| 4,096 | 196.1 | — | 144 |

At $T = 1024$ the naive mechanism's error is roughly **sixty times** the binary mechanism's, and the binary mechanism tracks $\log^2 T$ closely (94 → 127 → 196 vs 64 → 100 → 144). The script is the §3 implementation, run with `numpy` (seed 7).

### Proof sketches: what the theorems rest on

1. **Sensitivity via tree levels.** The privacy proof (§4.1) is a one-paragraph $\ell_1$-sensitivity argument: one bit, one p-sum per level, $\log T$ levels; the rest is post-processing. The difficulty of continual observation reduces to *choosing the interval family*.
2. **Packing lower bounds.** Dwork et al. [1] showed the $\log T$ dependence is necessary: a packing argument forces $\Omega(\log T)$ additive $\ell_\infty$ error for $\varepsilon$-DP continual counters. Henzinger et al. [4] lifted this to a tight $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP bound via the factorization norm $\gamma_F$, proving polylogarithmic error optimal up to log factors.
3. **Correlated noise.** The factorization view $A = LR$ reveals the binary mechanism's noise is *independent* across tree nodes — wasteful. Correlating noise across time steps (adding noise early, partially subtracting it later) yields the constant-factor gains of [5,10] and the optimal DP-FTRL optimizers used in private federated learning.

##6 Limitations

The theory is mature, but honest accounting requires the caveats:

- **Event-level vs. user-level.** Nearly all tight bounds are event-level; user-level continual privacy is far more expensive, since sensitivity becomes the user's total contribution [1].
- **The turnstile wall.** Insertions-only streams admit polylogarithmic error; with deletions, $\Omega(T^{1/4})$ is unavoidable in the worst case [6]. Flippancy parameterization rescues natural streams but not adversarial ones.
- **Adaptive adversaries.** The bounds of [4] hold against non-adaptive adversaries; fully adaptive streams (future inputs depending on past releases, as in bandits or private optimization) need extra machinery, and not all matrix mechanisms transfer [10].
- **Constants and discreteness.** The $O(\log^2 T/\varepsilon)$ bound hides constants that matter at small $\varepsilon$; implementations must handle floating-point leakage in Laplace sampling and the "consistent rounding" problem of ensuring released counts could correspond to a valid stream [2].
- **Pan-privacy scope.** The generic p-sum transformation [2] protects against a *single* unannounced intrusion; multiple or announced intrusions need stronger constructions [3].
- **Horizon knowledge.** The cleanest bounds assume $T$ known upfront; the doubling trick for unbounded streams costs constant factors [1].

##7 Conclusion

Differential privacy under continual observation reframes private data analysis from "sanitize once" to "sanitize forever." The binary tree mechanism of Chan, Shi, and Song [2] — building on the model of Dwork, Naor, Pitassi, and Rothblum [1] — is the field's central object: a streaming algorithm with $O(\log T)$ memory whose dyadic p-sums bound both sensitivity and noise accumulation, delivering every prefix sum with $O(\log^2 T/\varepsilon)$ error. Fifteen years of follow-up work show this is essentially the *right* answer: factorization-norm lower bounds prove the $\log T$ dependence optimal [4], the smooth binary mechanism optimizes the constants [5], pan-privacy extends the guarantee to adversaries who seize the machine [3], and the primitive now powers private heavy hitters, distinct counting, and frequency moments on live streams [6,7,8].

The frontier is now *how cheaply, and under what threat model*: turnstile streams, expiring privacy [9], adaptive adversaries, user-level guarantees. For practitioners: for a live counter, histogram, or trending list over sensitive events, the binary tree mechanism (or its smooth/factorized descendants) is the default, with error growing as the *square of a logarithm*.
