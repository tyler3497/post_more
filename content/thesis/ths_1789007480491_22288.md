# The Diffie-Hellman Problem Family: CDH, DDH, GDH, and the Generic Group Model

## Abstract

Fifty years after Diffie and Hellman's "New Directions in Cryptography" [1], the three problems born of their key exchange — the discrete logarithm (DL), the computational Diffie-Hellman (CDH), and the decisional Diffie-Hellman (DDH) problems — remain the central hardness assumptions of public-key cryptography. This thesis surveys the complete problem family, including the gap Diffie-Hellman (GDH) problem of Okamoto and Pointcheval [2] and the variations studied by Bao, Deng, and Zhu [3]. We reconstruct the reduction lattice relating DL, CDH, DDH, and GDH; present the Maurer–Wolf program on the DL/CDH equivalence question [4]; formalize the gap setting in which DDH is easy yet CDH is hard, the foundation of pairing-based cryptography [5]; and develop the generic group model of Shoup [6] and its refinement by Maurer and Wolf [7], proving that generic adversaries require Ω(√q) operations against these problems in prime-order groups. We close with the separations that remain open — most notably whether CDH implies DDH — and why this question resists fifty years of effort.

---

## 1. Introduction

In November 1976, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published "New Directions in Cryptography" in the *IEEE Transactions on Information Theory*, inventing public-key cryptography and, as its centerpiece, the Diffie-Hellman key exchange [1]. Two parties, Alice and Bob, agree on a cyclic group *G* with generator *g*; Alice publishes *g^x*, Bob publishes *g^y*, and both compute the shared secret *g^{xy}*. The protocol's elegance masks a subtlety that has occupied cryptographers ever since: *what exactly is assumed to be hard?*

The exchange admits at least three distinct computational tasks of increasing subtlety. Recovering *x* from *g^x* is the *discrete logarithm* problem. Computing the shared secret *g^{xy}* from the transcript *(g^x, g^y)* is the *computational* Diffie-Hellman problem. Merely *distinguishing* the real shared secret from random — the question of whether the key exchange leaks even a single bit — is the *decisional* Diffie-Hellman problem. Boneh called DDH "a gold mine," surveying its applications and open problems at ANTS 1998 [8]; the subsequent decades only deepened the metaphor.

This thesis maps the entire family. We define each problem with exact advantage formulations, reconstruct the web of reductions between them (the *reduction lattice*), explain why DDH can be easy while CDH remains hard (the *gap* setting), and develop the *generic group model* — the cleanest theoretical tool we have for arguing that these problems are genuinely hard. Along the way we cover the Maurer–Wolf program on DL/CDH equivalence [4], the Okamoto–Pointcheval gap problems [2], and the variants of CDH/DDH classified by Bao, Deng, and Zhu [3].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The three classical problems

Let *G* be a cyclic group of prime order *q* with generator *g*. All algorithms are probabilistic polynomial-time (PPT) in the security parameter λ, where *q ≈ 2^λ*.

> **Definition (DL):** The *discrete logarithm* problem is: given *(g, h)* with *h = g^x* for uniform *x ← ℤ_q*, output *x*.

> **Definition (CDH):** The *computational Diffie-Hellman* problem is: given *(g, g^x, g^y)* for uniform *x, y ← ℤ_q*, output *g^{xy}*.

> **Definition (DDH):** The *decisional Diffie-Hellman* problem is: given *(g, g^x, g^y, g^z)*, distinguish the case *z = xy* from the case *z ← ℤ_q* uniform, with non-negligible advantage.

The trivial implications structure everything that follows: a DL solver immediately breaks CDH (recover *x*, then compute *(g^y)^x*), and a CDH solver immediately breaks DDH (compute the real secret and compare). Contrapositively, DDH hardness implies CDH hardness, and CDH hardness implies DL hardness. The converse directions — whether breaking CDH is as hard as computing discrete logs, and whether breaking CDH implies breaking DDH — are the subject of the Maurer–Wolf program [4] and remain partially open.

### 2.2 Where DDH holds, and where it fails

DDH is *not* hard in every group. In *ℤ_p^∗* it fails catastrophically: the Legendre symbol leaks quadratic residuosity, so *g^{xy}* can be distinguished from random whenever the parity of the exponents interacts with the group structure. Concretely, in the full multiplicative group, the least significant bits of the shared secret are efficiently computable. Boneh's survey [8] catalogues these pathologies and the standard remedies: work in the prime-order subgroup of *ℤ_p^∗* (e.g., *p = 2q + 1* safe prime, generator of the order-*q* subgroup), or in elliptic-curve groups with no efficiently computable pairing.

DDH's fragility is also its power. Naor and Reingold built efficient pseudorandom functions directly from DDH (STOC 1997), and ElGamal encryption's semantic security reduces to DDH. Every application must therefore be *group-aware*: the assumption is inseparable from the concrete group family.

### 2.3 The gap problem

Okamoto and Pointcheval introduced a new class of assumptions at PKC 2001 [2]: the *gap problems*. The **gap Diffie-Hellman (GDH)** problem asks an adversary to solve CDH *given a DDH oracle*. In most groups this oracle is useless and GDH collapses to CDH; but in *gap groups* — groups where DDH is easy but CDH is presumed hard — the oracle models genuine additional power the adversary might possess, and GDH is the honest formulation of security.

The canonical gap groups arise from bilinear pairings. Given a non-degenerate bilinear map *e: G × G → G_T*, DDH in *G* becomes easy: on input *(g^x, g^y, g^z)*, check whether *e(g^x, g^y) = e(g, g^z)*; bilinearity makes this exactly the DDH test. Yet CDH in *G* can remain hard. Boneh and Franklin's identity-based encryption [5] and the short-signature schemes that followed are all built on this asymmetry — assuming CDH (or its bilinear cousin, BDH) is hard precisely where DDH is trivial.

## 3. Methodology

Comparing cryptographic assumptions is an exercise in *reductions*. We write *A ≤ B* when a solver for *B* can be converted, in polynomial time, into a solver for *A* — breaking *B* is *at least as hard* as breaking *A*, and assuming *A* hard is the *weaker* (safer) assumption. A reduction's *tightness* — the ratio between the assumed hardness and the proven hardness — determines concrete key sizes; a non-tight reduction can silently demand doubling the security parameter.

This thesis uses three methodological instruments:

1. **The reduction lattice.** We arrange DL, CDH, DDH, GDH, and their variants as a partially ordered set under polynomial-time reduction, distinguishing known implications from open ones.
2. **The generic group model (GGM).** Introduced by Shoup at Eurocrypt 1997 [6], the GGM formalizes "an algorithm that works in any group" — the adversary receives group elements only as random opaque encodings and must interact through a group-operation oracle. Lower bounds in the GGM rule out *entire classes* of attacks at once: they show that no algorithm exploiting only the group structure can succeed. Maurer and Wolf extended the technique to prove lower bounds on generic reductions themselves [7].
3. **Granularity.** Following Bao, Deng, and Zhu [3], assumptions are parameterized by *granularity* — the probability space over which the group, generator, and challenge are drawn. High-granularity assumptions fix the group and draw only the challenge randomly; low-granularity assumptions draw the group itself. Reductions must respect granularity, and some equivalences hold only at high granularity.

We work asymptotically (security parameter λ) except where concrete bounds are stated, and we assume prime-order groups unless noted.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The reduction lattice: DL, CDH, DDH

The known implications form a chain with one solid link and two conjectural ones:

$$\text{DDH hard} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{CDH hard} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{DL hard}$$

Both implications are trivial by contrapositive, as noted in §2.1. Everything interesting is in the reverse directions.

**CDH vs. DL.** Maurer (Crypto 1994) and Maurer–Wolf (Crypto 1996, full version in *SIAM Journal on Computing* 1999) launched the systematic study of whether breaking the Diffie-Hellman protocol is equivalent to computing discrete logarithms [4]. Their results are conditional but striking: given a short auxiliary string *S* (of length *2 log |G|*) depending only on the group order, breaking DH and computing discrete logs are polynomial-time equivalent — under a plausible smoothness assumption about numbers in short intervals. In particular, if every prime factor *p* of the group order has *p − 1* or *p + 1* smooth, the equivalence holds unconditionally and efficiently. The auxiliary string "appears to be of no help" for the discrete logarithm itself, yet bridges the gap. Whether *unconditional* equivalence holds remains open: no one has proved CDH ≡ DL, and no one has separated them in the standard model.

**CDH vs. DDH.** The other direction is murkier still. No reduction from DDH to CDH is known in general — solving *g^{xy}* does not obviously help *recognize* it. In gap groups the separation is real by construction (DDH easy, CDH hard). In groups where DDH is believed hard, we simply do not know whether a CDH-breaker must also break DDH. This asymmetry drives protocol design: schemes proven under DDH (ElGamal, Naor–Reingold PRFs) are preferred over CDH-based ones where possible, because DDH is the strictly stronger assumption.

### 4.2 Gap Diffie-Hellman and the pairing revolution

The gap setting deserves its own treatment because it inverts the usual intuition. Okamoto and Pointcheval defined, for any relation *f*, the *gap problem* of *f*: solve the computational version given an oracle for the decisional version [2]. For the Diffie-Hellman relation *f_DH(g^a, g^b, g^c) = 1 ⟺ c = ab*, this yields GDH: compute *g^{ab}* from *(g^a, g^b)* with access to a DDH oracle.

Gap groups turned out to be abundant in pairing-friendly elliptic curves. The pairing *e* decides DDH, as shown in §2.3, and the *bilinear Diffie-Hellman* (BDH) problem — given *(P, aP, bP, cP)*, compute *e(P,P)^{abc}* — became the workhorse assumption of pairing-based cryptography. Boneh and Franklin's IBE is secure under (a variant of) BDH in the random-oracle model [5]. Boldyreva's threshold, multisignature, and blind-signature schemes are built directly on the gap-DH-group signature paradigm, and the Okamoto–Pointcheval paper itself received the PKC Test-of-Time Award, cementing the gap framework's foundational status.

The methodology lesson: *assumptions must be stated relative to oracles the adversary plausibly has*. GDH is not "CDH plus a cheat" — in pairing groups, the DDH oracle is efficiently realizable, so GDH is the *honest* assumption.

### 4.3 The generic group model

Shoup's generic group model [6] is the sharpest tool for arguing that DH-family problems resist all "structure-oblivious" attacks. The setup: the adversary receives group elements as *random encodings* — arbitrary bit-strings *σ(x)* assigned by an injective random map *σ: ℤ_q → {0,1}^∗* — and may query a group-operation oracle that, given encodings of *x* and *y*, returns *σ(x ± y)*. The adversary never sees the actual exponents; it manipulates opaque handles.

> **Theorem:** *(Shoup, Eurocrypt 1997 [6])* Let *G* be a group of prime order *q*. Any generic algorithm making at most *T* group-operation queries solves the discrete logarithm problem, or the computational Diffie-Hellman problem, with probability at most *O(T²/q)*. In particular, Ω(√q) operations are necessary.

The proof's engine is the *polynomial representation*: every encoding the adversary ever holds corresponds to an affine linear polynomial *f_i* in the unknown input exponents, and the adversary can only succeed by producing a *collision* *f_i(a) = f_j(a)* for distinct polynomials at the random input point *a* — an event the Schwartz–Zippel lemma bounds by *O(T²/q)*. The bound is tight: baby-step/giant-step and Pollard's rho achieve *O(√q)* generically.

Maurer and Wolf pushed the model further at Eurocrypt 1998 [7]. Their headline result is a *separation inside the GGM*: for groups whose order has multiple large prime factors, the discrete logarithm and Diffie-Hellman problems are *not* computationally equivalent with respect to generic reductions — no generic algorithm reduces DL to CDH in that setting. They also showed no generic algorithm computes *p*-th roots efficiently when *p²* divides the group order. The message is nuanced: the GGM both *justifies* our confidence (Ω(√q) lower bounds) and *bounds* what generic reasoning can ever prove (some equivalences genuinely fail generically).

### 4.4 Variations: square, inverse, divisible, and granularity

Bao, Deng, and Zhu [3] classified the DH problem's variants at high granularity (fixed group, random challenge):

| Problem | Input | Task |
|---|---|---|
| CDH | *g^x, g^y* | compute *g^{xy}* |
| Square DH | *g^x* | compute *g^{x²}* |
| Inverse DH | *g^x* | compute *g^{x⁻¹}* |
| Divisible DH | *g^x, g^y* | compute *g^{x/y}* |

They proved all three computational variants *equivalent* to CDH with *optimal* (tight) reductions. For the decisional side — single-sample vs. multi-sample DDH and the "matching" DH formulation of Handschuh, Tsiounis, and Yung — all variations are equivalent *except* the strong DDH (SDDH) variant, whose status they left open. This is a useful methodological template: before inventing a new assumption, check whether it collapses to a known one.

A related line concerns *bit security*: Boneh and Venkatesan (Crypto 1996) showed, via the hidden-number problem, that computing the most significant bits of a Diffie-Hellman secret is as hard as computing the entire secret [9]. Partial information does not come cheap.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

The GGM lower bound is not merely asymptotic folklore; its core combinatorial claim — that random linear polynomials over *ℤ_q* collide with probability *O(T²/q)* — is directly testable. The following simulation instantiates Shoup's argument: *T* random affine polynomials in two unknowns over a prime field, counting collisions at a random evaluation point.

```python
import random

def trial(q: int, T: int) -> bool:
    # random affine polynomials f_i(x, y) = a_i*x + b_i*y + c_i
    polys = [(random.randrange(q), random.randrange(q), random.randrange(q))
             for _ in range(T)]
    x, y = random.randrange(q), random.randrange(q)
    seen = set()
    for a, b, c in polys:
        v = (a * x + b * y + c) % q   # sigma(f_i(x, y)): the "encoding"
        if v in seen:
            return True               # collision => generic attack succeeds
        seen.add(v)
    return False

def estimate(q, T, n=2000):
    return sum(trial(q, T) for _ in range(n)) / n

q = 1_000_003
for T in (100, 316, 1000):
    print(f"T={T:5d}  empirical collision rate={estimate(q, T):.4f}  "
          f"T^2/q={T*T/q:.4f}")
```

Typical output confirms the quadratic scaling:

```
T=  100  empirical collision rate=0.0050  T^2/q=0.0100
T=  316  empirical collision rate=0.0490  T^2/q=0.0999
T= 1000  empirical collision rate=0.4010  T^2/q=1.0000
```

The empirical rate tracks *T²/q* (up to the constant from Schwartz–Zippel), and collisions become likely only when *T ≈ √q* — exactly the Ω(√q) lower bound of [6]. The simulation is, of course, a sanity check rather than a proof: it validates the *combinatorial core* of Shoup's argument, while the full theorem additionally requires that the adversary's view be simulatable from the polynomials alone.

> **Theorem:** *(Maurer–Wolf, Crypto 1996 / SIAM J. Computing 1999 [4])* Let *G* be cyclic of known order with smooth *p ± 1* structure as specified. Then there exist polynomial-time reductions in both directions between the computational Diffie-Hellman problem and the discrete logarithm problem in *G*, given auxiliary input depending only on *|G|*.

The theorem's *empirical* content is practical: for standard groups (safe-prime subgroups, standard elliptic curves), the auxiliary input is efficiently constructible, so implementers may treat CDH and DL as equivalent *for those groups* — while remembering the equivalence is not known in general.

Group selection guidance, distilled:

| Group family | DDH | CDH | DL | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *ℤ_p^∗* (full) | ✗ easy | hard? | hard | Legendre symbol breaks DDH [8] |
| Order-*q* subgroup of *ℤ_p^∗* | ✓ believed | ✓ believed | ✓ believed | standard discrete-log setting |
| EC group, no pairing | ✓ believed | ✓ believed | ✓ believed | short keys, generic attacks only |
| Pairing-friendly EC (gap group) | ✗ easy via *e* | ✓ believed | ✓ believed | GDH/BDH setting [2][5] |

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **CDH vs. DL, unconditionally.** The Maurer–Wolf equivalence needs a smoothness assumption or auxiliary input [4]. Is breaking Diffie-Hellman *equivalent* to computing discrete logarithms in the plain model? Fifty years on, neither proof nor counterexample exists. Maurer and Wolf's generic-model separation [7] shows the equivalence fails for *some* group families against *generic* reductions — but says nothing about the standard model.

2. **CDH vs. DDH.** Does breaking CDH imply breaking DDH in groups where DDH is believed hard? No reduction is known; gap groups show the implication is false *in general*, but the interesting case — prime-order groups with no pairing — is wide open.

3. **Tightness and the GGM's blind spots.** GGM lower bounds [6] rule out generic attacks but say nothing about *index calculus* in *ℤ_p^∗* or *MOV/FR reductions* that exploit pairings — precisely the non-generic attacks that matter most in practice. A "provably secure in the GGM" scheme can still fall to a representation-specific attack.

4. **The algebraic group model and beyond.** Newer idealizations (the AGM, 2018) give adversaries more power — requiring them to "explain" group elements as combinations of inputs — and reprove some GGM results with tighter constants. Whether the AGM can separate CDH from DDH, or prove equivalences the GGM cannot, is active research.

5. **Quantum adversaries.** Shor's algorithm breaks DL, CDH, DDH, and GDH uniformly; the entire family falls together. The taxonomy's practical relevance ends at the post-quantum boundary — but understanding *which* classical assumption each protocol needs remains essential for migration planning.

6. **Strong DDH.** Bao, Deng, and Zhu left open whether the strong decisional variant (SDDH) is equivalent to standard DDH [3]. A resolution either way would tidy a loose end in the decisional taxonomy.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The Diffie-Hellman problem family is a study in how much structure hides inside one key exchange. From the three classical problems — DL, CDH, DDH [1][8] — we obtain a reduction lattice whose solid links are trivial and whose missing links have resisted half a century of attack. The Maurer–Wolf program [4] showed CDH and DL equivalent under plausible conditions; Okamoto and Pointcheval [2] showed that adding a DDH oracle creates a meaningful new problem with real instantiations in pairing groups [5]; Shoup [6] and Maurer–Wolf [7] showed that generic adversaries need Ω(√q) work and that some equivalences fail even generically; and Bao, Deng, and Zhu [3] collapsed the zoo of CDH variants to a single assumption.

The enduring lesson is methodological: *name the exact problem, the exact group, and the exact oracle access*. "Diffie-Hellman is hard" is not an assumption — it is a slogan. CDH in a safe-prime subgroup, DDH in a pairing-free elliptic curve, GDH in a gap group, BDH in a pairing-friendly curve: these are assumptions, and the security of modern cryptography rests on keeping them distinct.
