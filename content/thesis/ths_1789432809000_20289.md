# Curvature, Cut Loci, and Regularity: The Ma–Trudinger–Wang Theory of Optimal Transport on Riemannian Manifolds

## Abstract

This article surveys the Ma–Trudinger–Wang (MTW) regularity theory for optimal transport with the squared geodesic cost on Riemannian manifolds. We review the Monge–Kantorovich formulation, c-convex potentials, the c-exponential map, and the cut locus; derive the Monge–Ampère-type equation and the fourth-order MTW tensor; and explain Loeper's theorem that non-negative MTW curvature — which for $c = d^2/2$ reduces to the sectional curvature — is necessary for regularity, so that negative curvature forces discontinuity even for smooth data. We cover sufficiency (smooth and Hölder regularity with explicit exponents), the pseudo-Riemannian reformulation of Kim and McCann, stability of MTW under perturbations of the sphere, geometric control of the cut locus, and the partial-regularity theorem of De Philippis and Figalli, which salvages smoothness on full-measure sets even when MTW fails. A comparative table of geometries and extensions to mechanical Lagrangians and unbalanced transport round out the survey; we close with the theory's sharp limitations and open problems.

##1 Introduction

The Monge–Kantorovich optimal transportation problem asks for the cheapest way to reshape one probability distribution into another. On Euclidean space with quadratic cost, the theory is a triumph of convex analysis: Brenier's theorem represents the unique optimal map as the gradient of a convex potential, and Caffarelli's regularity theory turns convexity into smoothness. On a **Riemannian manifold**, however, the cost function is the squared geodesic distance, and curvature intrudes at every step. The squared distance is not smooth on the cut locus; the exponential map need not be injective; and, most subtly, the optimal map may fail to be even continuous for perfectly smooth data.

The decisive advance came in 2005 when Ma, Trudinger and Wang identified a **fourth-order differential condition** on the cost function — now the Ma–Trudinger–Wang (MTW) condition — that is essentially necessary and sufficient for regularity [3]. When the cost is the squared Riemannian distance, the MTW tensor collapses to the sectional curvature, and Loeper proved that non-negative MTW curvature is genuinely necessary: on any manifold with a point of negative sectional curvature, one can build smooth densities whose optimal map is discontinuous [1]. This article surveys the interplay between curvature and regularity, from c-convex geometry and the cut locus to Hölder estimates, partial regularity, and the pseudo-Riemannian reformulation of Kim and McCann [4].

##2 Background

Let $(M, g)$ be a smooth compact Riemannian manifold without boundary, equipped with its geodesic distance $d$. Given two probability measures $\mu = \rho \, dx$ and $\nu = \bar\rho \, d\bar x$, the Monge problem with quadratic cost is

> *Find a measurable map $T : M \to M$ with $T_\#\mu = \nu$ minimizing $\int_M c(x, T(x)) \, d\mu(x)$, where $c(x, y) = d(x, y)^2 / 2$.*

The fundamental existence result is due to Brenier in Euclidean space and to McCann on manifolds: under mild absolute-continuity hypotheses, there is a unique optimal map, and it takes the form $T(x) = \exp_x(-\nabla \phi(x))$ for a **c-convex potential** $\phi$ [7]. A function $\phi : M \to \mathbb{R}$ is c-convex when $\phi(x) = \sup_y (-c(x, y) - \phi^c(y))$ for its c-transform $\phi^c$, in exact analogy with convex functions as suprema of affine functions.

The central geometric objects of the theory are:

- **The c-subdifferential** $\partial_c \phi(x)$: the set of $y$ attaining the supremum above. Where $\phi$ is differentiable, this set is a singleton and $T(x) = \text{c-exp}_x(\nabla \phi(x))$, with the **c-exponential map** $\text{c-exp}_x(v)$ defined as the unique $y$ with $-D_x c(x, y) = v$.
- **c-segments**: curves $(y_t)$ with base point $x_0$ along which $t \mapsto -D_x c(x_0, y_t)$ is an affine segment. They are the transport-theoretic analogues of line segments.
- **The cut locus** $\text{Cut}(x)$ and the **injectivity domain** $I(x) \subset T_x M$: the cost $c$ is smooth precisely on $M \times M \setminus \text{Cut}$, so every regularity argument must keep the transport away from the cut locus, or else understand it.

Because $d^2$ fails to be $C^1$ across the cut locus, even formulating the problem requires care; throughout, classical results assume the data supported on domains $\Omega \times \bar\Omega$ contained in the smooth region [7].

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Monge–Ampère-type equation

Formally differentiating the push-forward condition $T_\#\mu = \nu$ with $T(x) = \text{c-exp}_x(\nabla \phi(x))$ yields a **Monge–Ampère-type equation** of the form

```
det( M(x, grad phi) + D^2 phi ) = f(x)
```

where $M$ depends on the cost and $f$ encodes the densities. In the Euclidean quadratic case this reduces to $\det D^2\phi = f$, but on manifolds the matrix $M$ carries curvature information through the derivatives of the exponential map.

### 3.2 The Ma–Trudinger–Wang tensor

Ma, Trudinger and Wang's strategy, reported in [3], was to differentiate this equation **twice** and derive an a priori estimate for the second derivatives of $\phi$. In the computation a term appears whose sign must be controlled; this term is the **MTW tensor**, a fourth-order object built from the mixed derivatives of the cost:

$$\mathfrak{S}(x, y)(\xi, \eta) = \sum c_{ij,rs} c^{r,k} c^{s,l} \, \xi^i \xi^j \eta_k \eta_l,$$

evaluated at $(x, y)$ with the standard raised/lowered index conventions. The **MTW condition** demands $\mathfrak{S}(x, y)(\xi, \eta) \ge 0$ whenever $\xi$ and $\eta$ are **c-orthogonal** (i.e. $\xi^i \eta_i = 0$ in the appropriate pairing). A strong version asks for strict positivity away from vanishing vectors [3][8].

The theory is developed under structural conditions on the cost, usually denoted:

| Condition | Content |
|---|---|
| (A0) | $c$ is $C^4$ smooth on the relevant domain |
| (A1) | $y \mapsto D_x c(x, y)$ is injective (twist condition) |
| (A2) | Mixed derivative $D_{xy}^2 c$ is invertible |
| (A3) / (A3w) | The MTW tensor is (strictly) non-negative on c-orthogonal pairs |

### 3.3 The pseudo-Riemannian reformulation

Kim and McCann recast the entire theory in invariant geometric language [4]: the cost $c$ induces a natural **pseudo-Riemannian metric of signature $(n, n)$** on the product $M \times \bar M$. In this picture, the MTW tensor becomes exactly the **sectional curvature of null planes**, and the MTW condition is non-negativity of null sectional curvature. This formulation makes the general covariance of the theory manifest and yields a direct, elementary proof of the maximum principle at the heart of Loeper's Hölder theory, relaxing his hypotheses and opening the door to global settings such as submersions and tensor products [4].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The MTW tensor on Riemannian manifolds

For $c = d^2/2$ on a Riemannian manifold, the fourth-order MTW tensor simplifies dramatically. Loeper showed that in the direction of a vanishing second vector, the MTW curvature equals the **sectional curvature** up to the standard area factor [1]:

$$\text{MTW}(u, 0, w) = K(u, w)\,(\lvert u\rvert^2 \lvert w\rvert^2 - \langle u, w\rangle^2).$$

Consequently, the weak MTW condition **implies non-negative sectional curvature** — but it is far stronger. Up to now the MTW condition is known to hold only for very special manifolds: round spheres, their products, their quotients and Riemannian submersions, and small perturbations thereof [2][6]. In particular:

- It **fails at every point** on any manifold with negative sectional curvature [1][2].
- It fails on sufficiently flat ellipsoids, even though they are positively curved [2][5].
- For the logarithmic cost $c(x,y) = -\log\cos d(x,y)$ arising in unbalanced transport, the MTW weak condition holds on the unit sphere but fails on Euclidean and hyperbolic space and on spheres of radius larger than one [8].

Kim's lectures give the complete computational framework for $c = d^2/2$ in normal coordinates, showing how the fourth derivatives of the distance function assemble into the MTW tensor, and why the cut locus must be excised from the domain [7].

### 4.2 The cut locus and nonfocal domains

Because $d^2$ is singular on $\text{Cut}$, the optimal map can only be analyzed where the transport stays inside the smooth region. This is the role of **nonfocal domains**: a domain is nonfocal if no minimizing geodesic from it to the target encounters a focal point. A remarkable discovery of the recent literature is that the MTW condition itself exerts **geometric control over the cut locus**: strict MTW implies that injectivity domains are uniformly convex, and the shape of the cut locus is stable under perturbations of the metric [6]. Figalli, Rifford and Villani established simplified analytic expressions for the MTW tensor on surfaces and uncovered a direct link between the MTW tensor and the curvature of the tangent focal locus near the tangent cut locus [5].

On the two-sphere, Figalli and Rifford proved the first regularity result that tolerates perturbations of the metric with no extra assumptions on the measures: any $C^2$-small deformation of the round sphere satisfies the regularity condition, and as a byproduct **all injectivity domains on such perturbations are strictly convex** — a purely geometric statement of independent interest [6].

### 4.3 Necessity: discontinuity without MTW

Loeper's fundamental negative result states that the weak MTW condition is **necessary** for regularity [1]. His argument is geometric: non-negativity of the cost-sectional curvature is equivalent to the **connectedness of the contact set** between any c-convex function and any of its supporting functions. If MTW fails at one point, one can construct smooth, strictly positive densities on domains satisfying all convexity hypotheses for which the optimal map is **discontinuous** [1][2].

On Riemannian manifolds this has a stark corollary: since MTW implies non-negative sectional curvature, on any manifold possessing a point of negative curvature there exist smooth data with discontinuous optimal maps [1]. Discontinuity is not a pathology of rough densities — it is forced by geometry.

### 4.4 Sufficiency: Hölder and smooth regularity

When MTW holds together with suitable c-convexity of the supports, the optimal map is regular. The landmark results, surveyed in [3], are:

1. **Smooth regularity**: if the MTW condition holds strictly, the densities are smooth and bounded away from zero, and the injectivity domains $(\exp_x)^{-1}(\text{supp } \nu)$ are uniformly convex, then the potential $\phi$ is smooth and $T$ is a smooth diffeomorphism between the supports [3].
2. **Hölder regularity**: Loeper proved that under the strong MTW condition, with only bounded-above source density and bounded-below target density plus convex injectivity domains, the potential is $C^{1,\alpha}$ with an **explicit exponent** $\alpha = 1/(4n-1)$, whence $T$ is $C^{0,\alpha}$. Liu later improved the exponent to the optimal $\alpha = 1/(2n-1)$ [3]. Remarkably, this regularity exceeds what the classical Monge–Ampère equation yields under comparable hypotheses — the MTW structure is genuinely stronger than convexity alone [1].

### 4.5 Beyond MTW: partial regularity

When MTW fails — the generic situation — De Philippis and Figalli proved a **partial regularity** theorem of striking generality [2]: there exist open sets $X'$ and $Y'$ of full measure on which the optimal map is a smooth diffeomorphism. In other words, even on negatively curved manifolds where global regularity is impossible, the singular set has measure zero. This result subsumes earlier Euclidean work and demonstrates that the failure of MTW localizes the pathology rather than destroying the theory [2].

A useful quantitative summary of the landscape:

| Geometry | Sectional curvature | MTW (for $d^2/2$) | Regularity verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Round sphere, products, quotients | Positive | Holds | Smooth maps [1][6] |
| Small perturbations of the sphere | Positive | Holds | Smooth, stable [5][6] |
| Flat ellipsoid | Positive | Fails | Discontinuous examples exist [2][5] |
| Negative curvature anywhere | Negative somewhere | Fails everywhere | Discontinuous examples exist [1] |
| General manifold | Arbitrary | Fails | Partial regularity on full-measure sets [2] |

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The "empirical" content of this theory is computational and perturbative. Three lines of work stand out:

**Stability under perturbations.** The MTW condition is stable under $C^2$ perturbations of the metric near the sphere [5][6], and a synthetic formulation yields stability even under the extremely weak notion of Gromov–Hausdorff convergence [7]. This means regularity on the sphere is not a fragile coincidence but an open condition in the space of metrics — a fact with direct consequences for numerical optimal transport on near-spherical domains.

**Explicit Hölder exponents.** Loeper's theory is quantitative: the modulus of continuity of $T$ is controlled explicitly by the dimension and the densities, with the sharp exponent $1/(2n-1)$ [3]. Such explicit bounds are rare in fully nonlinear PDE and are what make the theory usable for a priori estimates in discretization schemes.

**Extensions of the cost.** The pseudo-Riemannian viewpoint extends the MTW machinery beyond $d^2/2$ to natural mechanical Lagrangians [9] and to unbalanced transport with the logarithmic cost on constant-curvature spaces, where the MTW weak/strong conditions can be characterized by explicit computable functions [8].

The following Python snippet illustrates the MTW tensor computation for $c = d^2/2$ in normal coordinates on a surface of constant curvature $K$, following the formulas in [5]:

```python
import sympy as sp

def mtw_surface(K, u, w):
    # On a surface of constant Gaussian curvature K, the MTW curvature
    # for cost c = d^2/2 reduces (up to the area factor) to K itself.
    u1, u2, w1, w2 = sp.symbols('u1 u2 w1 w2')
    area2 = (u[0]*w[1] - u[1]*w[0])**2
    return sp.simplify(K * area2)

print(mtw_surface(sp.Symbol('K'), (1, 0), (0, 1)))  # K * 1  -> nonnegative iff K >= 0
```

And the same structural fact in Haskell, as a pure function on curvature:

```haskell
-- MTW curvature on a surface of constant curvature K
mtwCurvature :: Double -> (Double, Double) -> (Double, Double) -> Double
mtwCurvature k (u1, u2) (w1, w2) = k * (u1 * w2 - u2 * w1) ^ 2
-- Weak MTW holds iff k >= 0, recovering Loeper's theorem [1].
```

##6 Limitations

The theory has sharp boundaries, and they are worth stating plainly:

- **Geometric restrictiveness.** The MTW condition holds only on spheres, their products and quotients, submersions, and perturbations [2]. For most manifolds of interest — tori with flat spots, surfaces of mixed curvature, general symmetric spaces — global regularity simply does not hold, and the map can be discontinuous for smooth data.
- **The cut locus remains mysterious.** The MTW tensor is a fourth-order, nonlocal object whose behavior near the cut locus is poorly understood [7]. Keeping the transport away from $\text{Cut}$ requires nonfocality assumptions that are hard to verify in practice.
- **No characterization.** It is unknown whether MTW plus c-convexity is also necessary in full generality, and no complete list of manifolds satisfying MTW exists beyond the known families.
- **Computational gap.** Verifying the MTW tensor numerically requires fourth derivatives of the distance function; outside constant-curvature models this is prohibitively expensive, limiting direct applications in computational optimal transport.

---

##7 Conclusion

The Ma–Trudinger–Wang theory reveals optimal transport as a **geometric** subject in the deepest sense: the smoothness of the cheapest rearrangement is dictated by a curvature tensor built from fourth derivatives of the cost, and for the canonical cost $d^2/2$ this tensor is the sectional curvature itself [1][3]. Loeper's necessity theorem draws a hard line — negative curvature forces discontinuity — while the sufficiency results, the pseudo-Riemannian reformulation of Kim and McCann [4], the stability theorems on perturbed spheres [5][6], and the partial-regularity breakthrough of De Philippis and Figalli [2] show how much structure survives even when the line is crossed. The cut locus stands as the principal remaining frontier: understanding it better would unlock regularity on the manifolds where transport is actually used. Until then, the MTW tensor remains the definitive diagnostic — a single fourth-order quantity that decides whether curvature permits smoothness or mandates singularity.
