# Phonon Hydrodynamics and Second Sound in Solids: The Guyer–Krumhansl Equation, Ballistic-to-Hydrodynamic Transport Regimes, and the Observation of Second Sound in Graphite

## Abstract

Heat in nonmetallic crystals is carried by lattice vibrations, or phonons, whose collective behavior ranges from ballistic flight to ordinary diffusion. Between these limits lies phonon hydrodynamics, a regime in which momentum-conserving normal (N) scattering dominates over momentum-destroying umklapp (R) scattering and the phonon gas behaves as a viscous fluid. This article develops the full arc of that regime: the linearized Peierls–Boltzmann equation with the Callaway collision operator, its multiscale reduction to the Guyer–Krumhansl (GK) heat-transport equation, the derivation of the celebrated GK window condition 1/τ_R ≪ ω ≪ 1/τ_N for undamped propagation, and the classification of ballistic, hydrodynamic, and diffusive transport on the (τ_N, τ_R, L) parameter map. We distinguish drifted from driftless second sound, derive the Debye-limit sound speed c_2 = v/√3, survey heat-vortex and thermal-viscosity predictions from the two-dimensional GK analysis, and review the decisive transient-thermal-grating experiments that observed second sound in graphite above 100 K. Numerical estimates, a Python window-condition calculator, and formal TLA+ regime specifications accompany the analysis, ending with a candid assessment of limitations in the relaxon, boundary-scattering, and strong-anharmonicity corners of the theory.

## 1 Intro

Fourier's law, **q** = −κ∇T, is one of the most successful phenomenological closures in all of physics — and it is wrong in an interesting way. It predicts that a temperature disturbance propagates with infinite speed: a heat pulse applied at one point of a crystal is felt, in exponentially attenuated form, everywhere else *instantaneously*. This is the familiar paradox of parabolic diffusion; in most macroscopic settings the transient hyperbolic correction dies before it can be observed [9].

Phonons, the quanta of lattice vibration, complicate this tidy picture. In a sufficiently pure crystal at sufficiently low temperature, phonon mean free paths can stretch to macroscopic dimensions, and the distinction between *types* of phonon–phonon scattering becomes decisive. Normal (N) processes conserve total crystal momentum; they thermalize the phonon gas *without* degrading its heat flux. Umklapp (R, resistive) processes destroy crystal momentum and are the microscopic origin of finite thermal conductivity. When N scattering is fast and R scattering is slow, the phonon distribution collapses onto a **displaced Bose–Einstein distribution** characterized by a local temperature *and* a drift velocity **u**(r, t), precisely as a classical fluid is characterized by temperature and flow velocity. Heat then propagates not as a diffusing blob but as a **damped temperature wave** — *second sound* — in direct analogy to the entropy waves of superfluid helium, which lent the phenomenon its name.

The theoretical foundation of this regime was laid by Guyer and Krumhansl in two landmark 1966 papers [1, 2]: the first solving the linearized phonon Boltzmann equation by eigenfunction expansion of the N-process collision operator, the second deriving coupled macroscopic equations for the temperature and drift-velocity fields. Their analysis produced the **window condition** — the narrow frequency interval in which second sound can propagate with minimal damping — and thereby guided the first experimental observations in solid helium, NaF, and bismuth [9]. For four decades second sound remained a cryogenic curiosity. That era ended in 2019, when transient thermal grating measurements revealed wavelike heat transport in **graphite at temperatures above 100 K** [4], with ab initio calculations predicting hydrodynamic signatures up to near room temperature on micron scales.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Peierls–Boltzmann framework

The starting point is the phonon Boltzmann transport equation (BTE) for the distribution function f_K(**r**, t), the local density of phonons in mode K = (**k**, s) with wave vector **k** and branch index s [1]:

> **Theorem:** The linearized collision operator of the phonon BTE, decomposed into normal and resistive components C = C_N + C_R, admits a null-space spanned by the collisional invariants of energy and crystal momentum; the drifted Bose–Einstein distribution n(u, T) annihilates C_N exactly, making it the natural local-equilibrium state of the hydrodynamic regime [2, 7].

In equilibrium, f⁰ = [exp(ℏω_K/k_BT) − 1]⁻¹. The linearized deviation χ_K = f_K − f⁰ obeys ∂χ/∂t + **v**_K·∇χ = C[χ], where **v**_K = ∇_kω_K is the group velocity. 
### 2.2 The Callaway model

Callaway's 1959 model [3] splits the collision operator into two relaxation-time channels:

| Channel | Conserves | Relaxation time | Physical role |
|---------|-----------|----------------|---------------|
| Normal (N) | Energy + crystal momentum | τ_N | Drives distribution toward *displaced* equilibrium n(T, **u**) |
| Resistive (R) | Energy only | τ_R | Drives distribution toward ordinary equilibrium n(T), damps **u** |

Formally, C[χ] ≈ −(f − n(T, **u**))/τ_N − (f − n(T))/τ_R. The displaced distribution n(T, **u**) = [exp((ℏω_K − ℏ**k**·**u**)/k_BT) − 1]⁻¹ carries crystal momentum; N processes relax χ toward it rapidly when τ_N is short, while the slower R processes drain the drift velocity toward zero. The ratio τ_R/τ_N, together with the sample length L and the relevant frequency ω, completely classifies the transport regime — this is the parameter map at the heart of Section 4.

### 2.3 Historical observations

The GK window condition directed the search for second sound toward crystals where N scattering overwhelmingly dominates R scattering [9]:

1. **Solid ⁴He** (Ackerman, Bertman, Fairbank, and Guyer, 1966) — heat pulses near 0.5–0.8 K; the first unambiguous second sound.
2. **Solid ³He** (Ackerman and Overton, 1969) — confirmation in the fermionic isotope.
3. **NaF** (McNelly, Jackson, and Walker, 1970) — second sound near 10–20 K; the first observation in a "conventional" dielectric crystal [6].
4. **Bi** (Narayanamurti and Dynes, 1972) — semimetal, ~1–4 K.
5. **Graphite** (Huberman et al., 2019) — second sound above 100 K on 5–20 μm scales via transient thermal grating [4].

The graphite result, nearly five decades after NaF, redefined the field's ambitions: hydrodynamics is not exclusively cryogenic.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is the classical kinetic-to-continuum reduction program, executed with modern notation and supplemented by numerical evaluation and formal regime specification.

1. **Kinetic level.** Linearize the phonon BTE about equilibrium; adopt the Callaway two-channel collision operator [3]; expand χ in eigenfunctions of C_N following Guyer and Krumhansl [1]. The zeroth eigenfunction (uniform in **k**) yields the energy balance; the first (∝ **k**) yields the momentum balance.

2. **Hydrodynamic closure.** Take moments of the BTE weighted by ℏω_K (energy) and ℏ**k** (momentum). The N-process-dominated regime admits closure in the local fields T(**r**, t) and **u**(**r**, t), producing coupled PDEs — the energy balance ρcṪ + ∇·**q** = 0 and the **Guyer–Krumhansl heat-flux equation**
   τ_R **q̇** + **q** = −κ∇T + μ²[∇²**q** + 2∇(∇·**q**)],
   where μ² ∝ v²τ_Nτ_R is the nonlocal (viscous) coefficient [2].

3. **Dispersion analysis.** Seek plane-wave solutions T, **q** ∝ exp[i(**k**·**r** − ωt)] to obtain the second-sound dispersion relation ω(k), identify the window condition for weakly damped propagation, and extract the sound speed c₂.

4. **Regime classification.** Parameterize transport by the Knudsen-like ratios ℓ_N/L and ℓ_R/L (ℓ = vτ), construct the ballistic–hydrodynamic–diffusive map, and encode the transition logic as a formal TLA+ state machine for unambiguous regime assignment.

```python
import numpy as np

def second_sound_window(tau_N, tau_R, v=2.0e4):
    # Guyer-Krumhansl window: 1/tau_R << omega << 1/tau_N;
    # returns (omega_lo, omega_hi, c2) with c2 = v/sqrt(3) in the Debye limit.
    omega_lo, omega_hi = 1.0 / tau_R, 1.0 / tau_N
    c2 = v / np.sqrt(3.0)
    return omega_lo, omega_hi, c2

def damping_ratio(omega, tau_N, tau_R):
    # Dimensionless damping of the GK temperature wave.
    return 0.5 * (omega * tau_N + 1.0 / (omega * tau_R))

# NaF near 15 K: tau_N ~ 1e-10 s, tau_R ~ 1e-8 s  [6]
lo, hi, c2 = second_sound_window(1e-10, 1e-8)
print(f"window: {lo:.1e} < omega < {hi:.1e} rad/s, c2 = {c2:.2e} m/s")
print(f"damping at 1e9 rad/s: {damping_ratio(1e9, 1e-10, 1e-8):.3f}")
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Guyer–Krumhansl equation and the window condition

Eliminating the drift velocity from the coupled moment equations yields, in the GK reduction, a single evolution equation for the heat flux with three distinct terms: a relaxation (Maxwell–Cattaneo–Vernotte inertia) τ_R**q̇**, the Fourier drag **q** = −κ∇T, and the **nonlocal viscous term** μ²[∇²**q** + 2∇(∇·**q**)] [2]. The nonlocal term is the signature of hydrodynamics — it is absent from the Cattaneo equation and it encodes momentum diffusion within the phonon gas, i.e., *thermal viscosity*.

For longitudinal plane waves the dispersion relation reduces to

> **Theorem:** In the Debye limit with τ_N ≪ τ_R, the GK equations admit a weakly damped temperature-wave pole with phase velocity c₂² = v²/3, provided the excitation frequency satisfies the **Guyer–Krumhansl window condition** 1/τ_R ≪ ω ≪ 1/τ_N. Outside this window the pole merges with either the diffusive or the ballistic continuum [2].

The physics of the window is transparent: at frequencies below 1/τ_R, umklapp scattering has time to destroy the drift velocity within one oscillation, so the wave is overdamped into ordinary diffusion; at frequencies above 1/τ_N, even N scattering is too slow to establish the displaced distribution, so phonons fly ballistically and the collective wave never forms. Only in the window does the phonon gas sustain a coherent, momentum-carrying oscillation. The mathematical analysis of the GK equation — well-posedness, finite-difference discretization, and analytic solutions for laser-flash geometries — has been developed extensively as a non-Fourier heat conduction model in its own right [9].

### 4.2 Ballistic, hydrodynamic, and diffusive regimes

The three transport regimes are distinguished by comparing the N and R mean free paths ℓ_N = vτ_N, ℓ_R = vτ_R with the characteristic length L (sample size, grating period, or heater dimension):

| Regime | Condition | Heat signature |
|--------|-----------|----------------|
| **Ballistic** | L ≪ ℓ_N, ℓ_R | Heat pulses arrive at sound speeds v_L, v_T; no collective wave |
| **Hydrodynamic** | ℓ_N ≪ L ≪ ℓ_R (and ω in GK window) | Second sound at c₂ = v/√3; Poiseuille-like phonon flow; heat vortices |
| **Diffusive** | L ≫ ℓ_R | Fourier law; κ from Callaway theory |

A single crystal traverses all three as temperature rises: at the lowest temperatures scattering is negligible (ballistic); at intermediate temperatures N processes turn on while umklapp remains frozen out (hydrodynamic — the classic NaF and helium experiments [6, 9]); at high temperatures umklapp dominates and diffusion reigns. In heat-pulse experiments the regimes are directly visible as separate arrivals: a ballistic precursor at the ordinary sound speeds, followed by the slower second-sound pulse at c₂, followed by the diffusive tail [9].

### 4.3 Drifted versus driftless second sound

Guyer and Krumhansl's wave is **drifted** second sound: the temperature oscillation rides on a genuine drift velocity **u** of the phonon distribution, and the mode's damping is set by \u03c4_R [2]. Hardy showed in 1970 that a second, subtler wave \u2014 **driftless** second sound \u2014 can exist even when the drift velocity has relaxed: it arises from the slow decay of the heat flux itself, a collective mode of the *energy* density that survives when momentum has been destroyed but the distribution remains far from equilibrium in its higher moments [7]. Driftless second sound is more strongly damped and harder to observe, but it completes the spectrum: the linearized BTE supports a hierarchy of damped temperature-wave poles, with the GK pole the most robust.

The distinction matters for interpretation. In the graphite transient-grating experiments, the observed ringing of the thermal-grating decay is consistent with the drifted GK pole: the oscillation frequency scales linearly with the grating wave vector q = 2\u03c0/\u039b, \u03c9 \u2248 c\u2082q, the smoking gun of a propagating wave rather than diffusive relaxation [4]. A purely diffusive signal would decay monotonically as exp(−αq²t); the measured non-monotonic decay with a q-linear frequency is the wave.

### 4.4 Thermal viscosity, heat vortices, and the 2D Guyer–Krumhansl equation

The nonlocal term μ²[∇²**q** + 2∇(∇·**q**)] is formally identical to the viscous stress divergence in the linearized Navier–Stokes equations, with μ² playing the role of a kinematic thermal viscosity. This analogy is not cosmetic: extending the GK derivation to two-dimensional materials with the Callaway model and both linear and quadratic (flexural ZA) phonon dispersions yields a 2D GK equation whose transport coefficients — viscosity, second-sound velocity — become temperature-dependent, unlike the Debye-model constants [8]. The striking prediction is **heat vortices**: steady-state circulation patterns of the heat flux in graphene nanoribbons, the thermal analogue of viscous electron backflow.

### 4.5 Detecting the wave: transient thermal grating

The modern observation tool is the **transient thermal grating (TTG)** technique [4]: two crossed pump laser pulses interfere to imprint a sinusoidal temperature profile T(x) = T₀ + ΔT cos(qx) with period Λ = 2π/q on the sample surface; a probe beam diffracted from the resulting thermal (and acoustic) grating tracks its decay. In the diffusive regime the grating amplitude decays exponentially with rate αq². In the hydrodynamic window, the GK equation predicts the grating rings: the amplitude follows a *damped oscillation*, cos(c₂qt)·exp(−t/2τ_R), superimposed on diffusion. Huberman et al. observed exactly this non-monotonic decay in graphite for grating periods Λ = 5–20 μm at 85–120 K, with oscillation frequencies linear in q and consistent with ab initio phonon-BTE simulations predicting c₂ on these scales [4]. ```haskell
-- Damped GK temperature wave: grating amplitude A(t) for wavevector q
data GKParams = GKParams { c2 :: Double, tauR :: Double, alpha :: Double }

gratingAmplitude :: GKParams -> Double -> Double -> Double
gratingAmplitude p q t =
  let omega = c2 p * q                       -- second-sound frequency
      gamma = 1.0 / (2.0 * tauR p) + alpha p * q * q  -- GK + diffusive damping
  in exp (-gamma * t) * cos (omega * t)

-- q-linear frequency is the smoking gun: diffusive decay has no oscillation
frequencies :: GKParams -> [Double] -> [Double]
frequencies p = map ((c2 p *))
```

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The empirical record now spans six decades and four orders of magnitude in temperature:

1. **Solid helium (1966–1969).** Heat-pulse measurements in solid ⁴He near 0.5–0.8 K showed a slow temperature pulse propagating at c₂ ≈ v/√3 alongside the ordinary ballistic longitudinal and transverse arrivals — the first direct observation, quantitatively matching the GK prediction derived from the same crystals' thermal-conductivity data [2, 9]. The pulse width narrowed with increasing temperature as N scattering strengthened, exactly as the window condition requires.

2. **NaF (1970).** McNelly, Jackson, and Walker drove heat pulses through NaF crystals at 10–20 K and resolved second sound at ~13.5 K, where L/ℓ_N ≈ 50 placed the crystal deep in the hydrodynamic corner of the regime map; collinear versus non-collinear N processes were shown to play distinct roles, with large-angle scattering controlling the hydrodynamic relaxation rate [6]. Thermal-conductivity peaks and second-sound velocities yielded mutually consistent τ_N and τ_R.

3. **Bismuth (1972).** Semimetallic Bi extended the phenomenon beyond pure dielectrics, with second sound near 1–4 K [9].

4. **Graphite (2019).** Transient thermal grating on 10 mm² graphite samples with Λ = 5–20 μm revealed non-monotonic, q-linear-ringing thermal decay at 85–120 K — second sound above 100 K [4]. Ab initio solutions of the full phonon BTE predicted wavelike transport on ~1 μm scales up to near room temperature, in qualitative agreement. This remains the highest-temperature observation of second sound in any bulk crystal.

5. **Graphene (theory, 2015–).** First-principles calculations predict dominant N scattering and hydrodynamic transport in suspended graphene across a wide temperature range, with second-sound signatures potentially surviving to room temperature [5]; the 2D GK analysis adds quantitative predictions for temperature-dependent thermal viscosity and heat vortices [8].

*Numerical cross-check.* For NaF at 15 K with τ_N ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ s, τ_R ≈ 10⁻⁸ s, v ≈ 6×10³ m/s: the GK window spans 10⁸ ≪ ω ≪ 10¹⁰ rad/s, c₂ ≈ 3.5×10³ m/s, and at ω = 10⁹ rad/s the damping ratio ½(ωτ_N + 1/ωτ_R) ≈ 0.1 — a clean, weakly damped wave [6]. ## 6 Limitations

The GK program is powerful but bounded, and an honest account must state the boundaries:

- **The Callaway approximation.** The two-channel relaxation-time model is a drastic simplification of the true scattering integral: it assigns a single τ_N and τ_R to all modes, ignoring the strong mode-dependence of three-phonon rates, the special role of flexural phonons, and four-phonon processes that matter at high temperature [3, 5]. Quantitative predictions for real materials require the full ab initio BTE.
- **Boundary scattering.** The GK derivation assumes bulk-dominated scattering; in nanostructures, diffuse boundary scattering destroys crystal momentum at the walls, shrinking or closing the window. Most "hydrodynamic" signatures in confined geometries are really in the ballistic–hydrodynamic crossover.
- **Linearization.** The displaced distribution and the GK equation are linearized about equilibrium; large temperature gradients, shock-like pulses, and the nonlinear regime of strong drive fall outside the theory. Nonlinear phonon hydrodynamics remains largely open.
- **Driftless and relaxon corrections.** Hardy's driftless pole [7] and the modern relaxon-based viscous heat equations show that the GK closure captures only the slowest non-equilibrium moments; in materials where several relaxon lifetimes are comparable, a single (τ_R, μ²) pair is insufficient.
- **Interpretive ambiguity.** Non-monotonic TTG decays can, in principle, arise from ballistic–diffusive superposition without true hydrodynamics; the q-linear frequency test and ab initio BTE agreement are what elevate the graphite result from suggestive to decisive [4].
- **Temperature ceiling.** Even in graphite, umklapp eventually wins: the window closes as τ_R → τ_N at high T, and no bulk crystal has shown second sound at 300 K to date.

## 7 Conclusion

Phonon hydrodynamics transforms our picture of heat conduction from a random walk into a fluid mechanics. Starting from the linearized Peierls–Boltzmann equation, the Callaway collision operator's separation of momentum-conserving and momentum-destroying scattering [3] permits a controlled reduction to the Guyer–Krumhansl equations [1, 2] — a heat-flux evolution law with genuine inertia and genuine viscosity. The theory's central, falsifiable product is the window condition 1/τ_R ≪ ω ≪ 1/τ_N, which predicted where second sound could exist and was vindicated from solid helium to NaF to bismuth [6, 9], lay dormant as a cryogenic specialty for decades, and then spectacularly re-emerged in graphite above 100 K [4] with ab initio support pointing toward room-temperature hydrodynamics on micron scales. The modern extensions — driftless second sound [7], the 2D GK equation with its temperature-dependent viscosity and heat vortices [8], and hydrodynamic transport in graphene [5] — show the framework still generating predictions. Quantitative frontiers remain: full scattering physics beyond Callaway, mapping the ballistic–hydrodynamic crossover in real nanodevices, and determining how far up in temperature the phonon fluid can flow. If the trajectory from 0.5 K to 100 K is any guide, the answer may reshape microscale thermal engineering.
