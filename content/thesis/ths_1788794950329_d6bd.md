# Causal Discovery from Observational Data: The PC and FCI Algorithms, Markov Equivalence Classes, and Interventional Identifiability

## Abstract

Learning causal structure from observational data is among the most consequential problems in statistics, econometrics, and systems biology: association cannot distinguish cause from effect, yet controlled experiments are often infeasible or unethical. This thesis treats constraint-based causal discovery — the PC and FCI algorithms, the geometry of Markov equivalence classes, and the boundary between observational and interventional identifiability. We develop the formal foundations: the *causal Markov condition*, faithfulness, d-separation, and the Verma–Pearl characterization of Markov equivalence [1][2]. We present the PC algorithm's adjacency search and collider orientation, Meek's orientation rules, and FCI's extension to latent confounding and selection bias via partial ancestral graphs [3][4]. We analyze score-based alternatives, notably Greedy Equivalence Search (GES), and interventional refinements through do-calculus and the interventional Markov equivalence classes of Hauser and Bühlmann [5][6]. We discuss evaluation via the structural Hamming distance and its limitations [7], survey landmark applications in genomics and econometrics [8], and delineate what observational data can and cannot reveal.

## 1 Introduction

Causal knowledge is qualitatively different from predictive knowledge. A model that predicts disease risk from biomarkers with high accuracy may be entirely silent on whether lowering a biomarker improves outcomes — the distinction between *seeing* and *doing* that Pearl formalized as the difference between P(Y | X = x) and P(Y | do(X = x)) [9]. Randomized experiments identify the latter, but in genomics, economics, and medicine, large-scale experimentation is often impossible, prohibitively expensive, or ethically barred. The field of **causal discovery** asks what structure can nevertheless be recovered from passively observed data.

The intellectual lineage begins with Wright's path analysis [10], but the modern graphical formalism — developed by Pearl, Spirtes, Glymour, and Scheines [11][9] — supplies the decisive semantics: a DAG G together with the *causal Markov condition* entails that each variable is conditionally independent of its non-descendants given its parents. If, additionally, *faithfulness* holds — every conditional independence in the distribution is entailed by the Markov condition — then conditional independence tests become queries about graph structure, and discovery becomes a search problem.

Two algorithmic traditions dominate. **Constraint-based methods**, beginning with the PC algorithm of Spirtes and Glymour [11] and the FCI algorithm of Spirtes, Meek, and Richardson [4], perform statistical conditional-independence tests and convert the results into graphical constraints, ultimately outputting a *completed partially directed acyclic graph* (CPDAG) or *partial ancestral graph* (PAG). **Score-based methods** such as Greedy Equivalence Search [5] instead optimize a penalized likelihood over equivalence classes of graphs. This thesis focuses on the constraint-based tradition and its boundaries, because it most directly exposes the identifiability structure of the problem: exactly which edge orientations can be determined without experimental manipulation. Section 2 develops the background (DAG models, d-separation, Markov equivalence); Section 3 presents the PC and FCI algorithms; Section 4 deepens the theory (CPDAGs, orientation completeness, GES, do-calculus, interventional equivalence, SHD); Section 5 consolidates guarantees and empirical results; Section 6 examines limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Causal graphical models

Let **V** = {X₁, …, X_p} be a set of random variables and G a DAG on **V**. The DAG represents a *causal structure*: an edge X_i → X_j means X_i is a direct cause of X_j relative to **V**. The joint distribution P factorizes as P(**v**) = ∏ᵢ P(x_i | pa_G(x_i)), where pa_G denotes parents in G.

> Theorem: Causal Markov Condition. In a causal DAG model, each variable is conditionally independent of its non-descendants given its parents: X_i ⊥⊥ nd_G(X_i) \ pa_G(X_i) | pa_G(X_i).

This condition is what licenses the reading of conditional independencies from the graph via **d-separation** [9]. A path between X and Y is *blocked* by a set **S** if the path contains (i) a chain A → B → C or fork A ← B → C with B ∈ **S**, or (ii) a collider A → B ← C with B ∉ **S** and no descendant of B in **S**. If every path between X and Y is blocked by **S**, then X ⊥⊥ Y | **S** in every distribution satisfying the Markov condition.

### 2.2 Faithfulness and its fragility

The **faithfulness assumption** states the converse: all conditional independencies in P are entailed by the Markov condition on G. It fails when causal paths cancel exactly (as in XOR-type mechanisms or fine-tuned parameters). Unfaithful distributions form a Lebesgue measure-zero set in linear-Gaussian models [2], but near-violations are empirically common and degrade finite-sample performance severely [12].

### 2.3 Markov equivalence

Two DAGs G₁ and G₂ are **Markov equivalent** if they entail the same d-separation relations — equivalently, the same set of conditional independencies under faithfulness. The fundamental characterization is due to Verma and Pearl:

> Theorem (Verma–Pearl, 1990): Two DAGs are Markov equivalent if and only if they have the same skeleton (underlying undirected graph) and the same *v-structures* — unshielded colliders X → Z ← Y where X and Y are non-adjacent.

This is the sense in which observational data alone cannot fully orient a DAG: edges not participating in v-structures may be reversible. A Markov equivalence class is uniquely represented by a **CPDAG** (completed partially directed acyclic graph, also called an *essential graph*), in which an edge is directed iff it has the same direction in every member of the class, and undirected otherwise [13].

| Concept | Definition | Identifiability from observational data |
|---|---|---|
| Skeleton | Undirected edges of the true DAG | Fully identifiable under faithfulness |
| v-structures | Colliders X → Z ← Y, X,Y non-adjacent | Fully identifiable under faithfulness |
| Reversible edges | Edges orientable differently across equivalent DAGs | Not identifiable observationally |
| Latent confounder X ← L → Y | Common unobserved cause | Detectable as bidirected edge via FCI, not resolvable |
| do(X=x) effects | Interventional distribution | Identifiable only under further assumptions or experiments |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The PC algorithm

The PC algorithm (named for its authors Peter Spirtes and Clark Glymour) proceeds in three phases [11]:

1. **Adjacency search.** Start from the complete undirected graph on **V**. For ℓ = 0, 1, 2, …, test each adjacent pair (X_i, X_j) for conditional independence given subsets **S** ⊆ adj(X_i) \ {X_j} of size ℓ in the *current* graph. Remove the edge when independence is found, recording **S** as a *separation set*. Restricting to current adjacencies keeps the worst case manageable for sparse graphs while preserving oracle correctness.
2. **Collider orientation.** For each unshielded triple X_i − X_k − X_j in the skeleton, orient as X_i → X_k ← X_j iff X_k ∉ sepset(X_i, X_j).
3. **Meek orientation rules.** Repeatedly apply Meek's rules R1–R3 [2] until fixpoint: orient any edge whose current orientation would create a new v-structure or a directed cycle.

![PC algorithm phases](public/thesis/ths_1788794950329_d6bd-1.webp)

### 3.2 Meek's rules and their justification

Meek's rules are sound and complete for extending the oriented v-structures to the full CPDAG [2]:

- **R1:** If A → B and B − C with A, C non-adjacent, orient B → C.
- **R2:** If A → B → C and A − C, orient A → C.
- **R3:** If A − B, A − C, A − D, B → D, C → D with B, C non-adjacent, orient A → D.

Meek showed these rules derive every compelled edge: any edge directed in all Markov-equivalent DAGs is oriented by R1–R3.

### 3.3 The FCI algorithm and latent confounding

When **causal sufficiency** fails — unmeasured confounders or selection variables exist — PC's output is invalid. The FCI (Fast Causal Inference) algorithm [4] relaxes this assumption. Its adjacency search considers *possible-D-SEP* sets computable from the graph, and it outputs a **PAG** (partial ancestral graph) with four edge endpoint types:

- `X → Y`: X is an ancestor of Y in all consistent models (causal).
- `X ↔ Y`: neither is an ancestor of the other; a latent common cause exists.
- `X ∘→ Y`: Y is not an ancestor of X; X may or may not cause Y.
- `X ∘−∘ Y`: no ancestral claim either way.

Zhang [3] proved the completeness of the FCI orientation rules — ten rules extending Meek's — for PAGs. The RFCI variant avoids the expensive Possible-D-SEP phase at some cost in informativeness [14].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The geometry of Markov equivalence: CPDAGs in detail

The CPDAG representation compresses an equivalence class into a single object. Chickering [13] characterized the essential graph's structure: it is a *chain graph* whose chain components (connected subgraphs of undirected edges) are chordal, so the number of DAGs in a class equals the product over chain components of the number of consistent extensions. Though the number of equivalence classes grows super-exponentially [15], the CPDAG remains compact.

![Markov equivalence class and CPDAG](public/thesis/ths_1788794950329_d6bd-2.webp)

An instructive example: the three DAGs X → Y → Z, X ← Y → Z, and X ← Y ← Z share a skeleton and have no v-structures, so they are Markov equivalent, and the CPDAG is the fully undirected path X − Y − Z. Adding a fourth node W with edges W → X and W → Z creates v-structures that force orientations and shrink the class. The denser the v-structures, the more of the DAG is identifiable.

### 4.2 Score-based discovery: Greedy Equivalence Search

Constraint-based methods are not the only route. **Greedy Equivalence Search (GES)** [5] traverses the space of equivalence classes rather than DAGs: a forward phase greedily adds edges (in equivalence-class space), followed by a backward phase deleting edges, using a score such as the BIC that is *score-equivalent* (identical for all DAGs in a class) and *decomposable*. Chickering proved that, with a consistent score and in the large-sample limit, GES recovers the true equivalence class under faithfulness — a two-phase greedy algorithm achieving global optimality in the population limit.

```python
# Skeleton adjacency search, oracle version (PC, phase 1)
def pc_skeleton(nodes, ci_oracle):
    import itertools
    C = {u: set(nodes) - {u} for u in nodes}   # complete undirected graph
    sepset = {}
    ell = -1
    while True:
        ell += 1
        removed_any = False
        for i in nodes:
            for j in list(C[i]):
                if j not in C[i]:
                    continue
                adj = C[i] - {j}
                if len(adj) < ell:
                    continue
                for S in itertools.combinations(sorted(adj), ell):
                    if ci_oracle(i, j, set(S)):
                        C[i].discard(j); C[j].discard(i)
                        sepset[(i, j)] = set(S)
                        removed_any = True
                        break
            # end pair loop
        if not removed_any:
            break
    return C, sepset
```

A key statistical contrast: PC makes *local* decisions via hypothesis tests and propagates them, so one erroneous test can cascade; GES makes *global* score comparisons, more robust to individual fluctuations but requiring parametric score computation. The BIC is consistent for Gaussian models, and the generalized score of Hauser and Bühlmann [6] extends score-equivalence to the interventional setting.

### 4.3 Do-calculus and interventional identifiability

Observational equivalence classes leave many edges undirected. Pearl's **do-calculus** [9] provides the complete calculus of identifiability: given a causal DAG, rules of insertion/deletion of observations and actions determine whether P(Y | do(X = x)) is computable, e.g., via the back-door or front-door criteria. But do-calculus assumes the DAG is known.

Hauser and Bühlmann [6] closed the loop between discovery and intervention: with data from a conservative family of interventions (each variable is *not* intervened upon in at least one experiment), they defined **interventional Markov equivalence** — two DAGs are equivalent iff they share skeleton and v-structures *and* their mutilated graphs G^(I) (edges into intervened targets removed) share skeletons for every intervention target I. Interventional equivalence refines observational equivalence: single-vertex interventions orient all edges incident to the target, and log₂(N)+1 experiments are sufficient and worst-case necessary to identify all causal relations among N variables [16]. The **interventional essential graph** generalizes the CPDAG, and Greedy Interventional Equivalence Search (GIES) extends GES to mixed observational–interventional data.

```haskell
-- d-separation: is every path between x and y blocked by s?
data Path = Chain | Fork | Collider
blockedBy :: DAG -> Set Node -> Path -> Bool
blockedBy g s Chain    = any (`member` s) (middleNodes Chain)
blockedBy g s Fork     = any (`member` s) (middleNodes Fork)
blockedBy g s Collider = null (descendants g colliderNode `intersect` toList s)
dSeparated :: DAG -> Node -> Node -> Set Node -> Bool
dSeparated g x y s = all (blockedBy g s) (allPaths g x y)
```

### 4.4 Evaluation: SHD and the metrics of structure learning

Evaluation of discovery algorithms is itself subtle. The **structural Hamming distance (SHD)** between an estimated graph Ĝ and the true graph G counts edge insertions, deletions, and flips needed to transform one into the other [7]. For CPDAG outputs, the SHD is computed against the true CPDAG. Reported as a function of sample size and significance level α, SHD curves reveal the bias–variance structure of discovery: small α yields sparser, high-precision graphs; large α recovers more edges at the cost of false positives.

Yet SHD has known pathologies: it weights all errors equally, ignores that misorienting an edge into a *new v-structure* changes the entailed independencies more than flipping a reversible edge, and does not measure downstream causal-effect error. The structural intervention distance (SID) counts pairs (i, j) for which the estimated graph yields a wrong interventional distribution P(X_j | do(X_i)) [17] — arguably the decision-relevant metric.

### 4.5 Applications: genomics and econometrics

**Genomics.** The landmark application is Sachs et al. [8], who applied Bayesian network discovery to multiparameter single-cell flow cytometry data (11 phosphoproteins, ~7,000 cells), recovering the known Raf→Mek→Erk MAPK signaling cascade and correctly predicting the effects of held-out interventions. The PC-stable variant of Colombo and Maathuis [12] was validated on yeast gene-expression data, where order-independence proved decisive in high dimensions.

**Econometrics.** Where randomized experiments are rare, causal discovery has been applied to macroeconomic time series (with temporal adaptations such as tsFCI), to monetary policy transmission channels, and to program evaluation, where the discovered graph justifies which covariates form a valid adjustment set. The back-door criterion converts a discovered partial graph into an identification strategy for treatment effects — discovery and estimation composed into a single pipeline [18].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Theoretical guarantees

The soundness results form a clean hierarchy:

> Theorem (PC soundness): Under the causal Markov condition, faithfulness, and causal sufficiency, with a perfect conditional-independence oracle, the PC algorithm outputs exactly the CPDAG of the true DAG [11].

> Theorem (FCI soundness and completeness): Under Markov and faithfulness alone (latent confounders and selection bias permitted), with a perfect oracle, FCI outputs the complete PAG of the true causal structure; Zhang's rules orient every invariant edge mark [3][4].

> Theorem (GES consistency): With a consistent, score-equivalent, decomposable score, GES converges to the true Markov equivalence class as n → ∞ [5].

Finite-sample theory is due to Kalisch and Bühlmann [7]: for Gaussian DAGs with maximum degree q = O(n^(1−b)), PC with Fisher-z tests at level α_n → 0 is *uniformly consistent* even when p grows as any polynomial in n — under a *strong faithfulness* assumption bounding partial correlations away from zero.

### 5.2 Simulation evidence

In the canonical benchmark design of Kalisch and Bühlmann [7] — p = 10 to 100 nodes, expected degree 2–4, n = 50 to 1,000 Gaussian samples — PC achieves true discovery rates above 0.9 at α = 0.01 with modest samples, while GES attains higher true positive rates but lower precision in small-n regimes. Order dependence of PC, documented by Colombo and Maathuis [12], can swing SHD by 20–40% in high dimensions; PC-stable eliminates this variability with no consistency cost. RFCI [14] runs orders of magnitude faster than FCI on dense graphs while returning slightly less informative PAGs.

### 5.3 The Sachs validation

The most persuasive empirical result remains Sachs et al. [8]: from purely observational single-cell data, the learned network's predicted intervention effects matched held-out experimental perturbations for the majority of tested edges — a rare end-to-end validation of discovery → do-calculus → experimental confirmation.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Faithfulness is untestable and often violated.** Near-cancellations of causal paths are common in real systems (feedback regulation in biology is designed to cancel). Strong faithfulness, needed for high-dimensional consistency [7], is a strong parametric restriction.
2. **Conditional independence testing is the bottleneck.** Kernel-based tests handle nonlinearity but scale poorly; discretization loses power; multiple testing across thousands of conditioning sets inflates both error types. Test choice, more than algorithm choice, often determines empirical success.
3. **Order dependence and instability.** The vanilla PC algorithm's output depends on variable ordering [12]; PC-stable and conservative orientation variants mitigate but do not eliminate finite-sample instability.
4. **Causal sufficiency is heroic.** FCI handles latent confounding but its Possible-D-SEP phase is exponential in the worst case, and PAGs are often too uninformative for downstream effect estimation.
5. **Acyclicity and no interference.** Feedback loops violate DAG assumptions; cyclic discovery and dynamic models are far less developed.
6. **SHD mismeasures what matters.** Structural metrics need not track interventional accuracy; SID and effect-estimation error should be reported alongside SHD [17].

## 7 Conclusion

Constraint-based causal discovery converts the logical content of conditional independence into graphical knowledge with remarkable precision: the PC algorithm recovers the Markov equivalence class under faithfulness and sufficiency, FCI extends this to latent confounding with provably complete orientation rules, and the Verma–Pearl theorem delineates exactly what observational data can ever reveal. What it cannot reveal — the orientations of reversible edges — is not a failure of method but a theorem: Markov-equivalent DAGs are *observationally indistinguishable*, so breaking the tie requires interventions, background knowledge, or parametric assumptions such as non-Gaussianity (LiNGAM) or additive noise models.

The modern trajectory points toward hybridizing these traditions: score-based and continuous-optimization methods for scale, interventional designs (GIES, active learning) for identifiability, and decision-aware metrics for evaluation. The enduring lesson of the PC/FCI literature is methodological humility made formal — a graph of what the data entitle us to claim, with every undirected edge an honest admission of the limits of observation.
