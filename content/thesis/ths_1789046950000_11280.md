# Persistent Homology for Data Analysis: Vietoris–Rips Filtrations, Persistence Diagrams, and the Stability Theorem

## Abstract

Persistent homology is the central mathematical instrument of topological data analysis (TDA), providing a multi-scale descriptor of the shape of data that is stable under perturbations, independent of coordinates, and compactly representable as a multiset of intervals — the persistence barcode — or as points in the plane — the persistence diagram [1][5]. Given a finite point cloud in a metric space, the Vietoris–Rips construction builds a nested family of simplicial complexes indexed by a proximity parameter, and the evolution of their homology groups across this filtration records the birth and death of topological features: connected components, loops, and voids. The structure theorem for persistence modules, a consequence of Gabriel's classification of quiver representations, guarantees that this evolution decomposes into interval summands [2]. The stability theorem of Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner, and Harer [3] establishes the decisive quantitative property of the theory: the bottleneck distance between persistence diagrams is bounded above by the sup-norm distance between the underlying functions, licensing the use of persistence as a robust data descriptor. This thesis develops the full pipeline — from simplicial complexes and boundary operators, through the matrix-reduction algorithm and its complexity, to the stability theorem, its corollaries, and the frontiers of multiparameter persistence — grounding each claim in the primary literature and in executable code.

## 1. Introduction

Modern data sets — point clouds from 3D scans, sensor networks, neural activations, molecular configurations — inhabit high-dimensional spaces whose global shape is invisible to local, coordinate-dependent statistics [4][6]. *Topological data analysis* asks what qualitative, coordinate-free features such data possess: how many clusters, whether loops or cavities are present, and at what scales these features persist. Persistent homology answers these questions by replacing a single space with a *filtration*: a one-parameter family of nested topological spaces $\{X_t\}_{t \in \mathbb{R}}$ such that $X_t \subseteq X_s$ whenever $t \le s$ [5].

The intellectual lineage of the subject is short but decisive. Edelsbrunner, Letscher, and Zomorodian introduced *topological persistence* in 2002 as a framework for distinguishing genuine topological features from noise by their lifetime within a growing complex [1]. Zomorodian and Carlsson recast the theory algebraically, identifying the persistent homology of a filtered simplicial complex with the graded module structure over a polynomial ring and deriving a natural algorithm [2]. Ghrist's 2008 survey christened the interval encoding the *barcode* and argued for its role as the persistent topology of data [4]. The stability theorem [3] then supplied the theoretical warrant: small perturbations of the input produce only small changes in the barcode, measured by the *bottleneck distance*. Finally, the computational roadmap of Otter et al. [5] and the Ripser algorithm of Bauer [7] transformed the theory into practical software capable of handling millions of simplices.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Simplicial complexes and homology

A *simplicial complex* $K$ on a vertex set $V$ is a collection of finite subsets of $V$ closed under taking subsets: if $\sigma \in K$ and $\tau \subseteq \sigma$, then $\tau \in K$. A $k$-simplex is a subset of cardinality $k+1$; we write $C_k(K)$ for the vector space over a field $\mathbb{F}$ (conventionally $\mathbb{F}_2$ or a prime field $\mathbb{F}_p$) spanned by the $k$-simplices. The *boundary operator* $\partial_k : C_k \to C_{k-1}$ is defined on a simplex $[v_0, \dots, v_k]$ by

$$\partial_k[v_0,\dots,v_k] = \sum_{i=0}^{k} (-1)^i\, [v_0, \dots, \hat{v}_i, \dots, v_k],$$

extended linearly. The fundamental identity $\partial_{k-1} \circ \partial_k = 0$ yields the chain complex

$$\cdots \xrightarrow{\partial_{k+1}} C_k \xrightarrow{\partial_k} C_{k-1} \xrightarrow{\partial_{k-1}} \cdots,$$

and the $k$-th *homology group* is the quotient

$$H_k(K) = \ker \partial_k \;/\; \operatorname{im} \partial_{k+1}.$$

The *Betti numbers* $\beta_k = \dim H_k$ count the $k$-dimensional holes: $\beta_0$ is the number of connected components, $\beta_1$ the number of independent loops, $\beta_2$ the number of voids, and so on. Crucially, homology is *functorial*: a simplicial map $f : K \to L$ induces linear maps $f_* : H_k(K) \to H_k(L)$.

### 2.2 Complexes built from point clouds

Let $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ be a finite metric space. For a scale parameter $\varepsilon \ge 0$, the *Vietoris–Rips complex* $\mathrm{VR}_\varepsilon(X)$ contains a $k$-simplex $[x_{i_0}, \dots, x_{i_k}]$ whenever all pairwise distances satisfy $d(x_{i_j}, x_{i_\ell}) \le \varepsilon$. Equivalently, it is the *clique complex* (flag complex) of the proximity graph at threshold $\varepsilon$: the full complex is determined by its 1-skeleton, which makes it computationally convenient [7]. The *Čech complex* $\check{C}_\varepsilon(X)$ instead includes a simplex when the intersection of closed balls $\bigcap_j B(x_{i_j}, \varepsilon/2)$ is nonempty. By the Nerve theorem, the Čech complex is homotopy equivalent to the union of balls, while the Rips complex approximates it: the two are interleaved, $\check{C}_\varepsilon \subseteq \mathrm{VR}_\varepsilon \subseteq \check{C}_{\sqrt{2}\,\varepsilon}$ in Euclidean space [5].

> **Theorem:** (Nerve theorem, applied) The Čech complex $\check{C}_\varepsilon(X)$ has the homotopy type of the union $\bigcup_{x \in X} B(x, \varepsilon/2)$ of closed balls.

As $\varepsilon$ increases from $0$ to $\infty$, the complexes nest: $\mathrm{VR}_\varepsilon \subseteq \mathrm{VR}_{\varepsilon'}$ for $\varepsilon \le \varepsilon'$. This nested family $\{\mathrm{VR}_\varepsilon\}_{\varepsilon \ge 0}$ is the *Vietoris–Rips filtration*, the input to persistent homology.

### 2.3 Filtrations and persistence modules

A *filtration* of a simplicial complex $K$ is a nested sequence of subcomplexes

$$\emptyset = K_0 \subseteq K_1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq K_m = K,$$

typically obtained by adding one simplex at a time in order of increasing $\varepsilon$. Applying $H_k$ yields a *persistence module*: a sequence of vector spaces $V_i = H_k(K_i)$ with linear maps $v_{i,j} : V_i \to V_j$ for $i \le j$, induced by inclusion, satisfying $v_{j,\ell} \circ v_{i,j} = v_{i,\ell}$. A homological feature *is born* at index $b$ when a class appears in $V_b$ that is not in the image of any earlier map, and *dies* at index $d$ when it merges into the image of an older class or becomes a boundary. Its *persistence* is $d - b$, and features with large persistence are interpreted as signal while short-lived ones are noise [1].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The standard persistence algorithm

Order the $m$ simplices of the filtration as $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_m$. Form the $m \times m$ *boundary matrix* $D$ over $\mathbb{F}$, where $D[i,j] = 1$ if $\sigma_i$ is a codimension-one face of $\sigma_j$ and $i < j$, else $0$. The algorithm reduces $D$ by left-to-right column operations: for each column $j$ from $1$ to $m$, while there exists $j' < j$ with $\mathrm{low}(j') = \mathrm{low}(j)$ (where $\mathrm{low}(j)$ is the row index of the lowest nonzero entry of column $j$), add column $j'$ to column $j$. The reduced matrix $R$ yields the pairing: if $\mathrm{low}(j) = i > 0$, then $(\sigma_i, \sigma_j)$ is a *persistence pair* — a $k$-dimensional class born with $\sigma_i$ dies with $\sigma_j$. If $\mathrm{low}(j) = 0$ (zero column) and $j$ is not itself a low of any column, then $\sigma_j$ creates a feature of *infinite* persistence [1][2].

```python
import numpy as np

def persistence_pairs(boundary):
    """Standard persistence algorithm over F_2.
    boundary: list of columns, each a sorted list of face row indices.
    Returns (pairs, unpaired): pairs of (birth_idx, death_idx)."""
    m = len(boundary)
    cols = [set(c) for c in boundary]
    low = {}
    pairs = []
    for j in range(m):
        while cols[j]:
            i = max(cols[j])
            if i in low:
                cols[j] ^= cols[low[i]]   # column addition over F_2
            else:
                low[i] = j
                break
        if cols[j]:
            pairs.append((max(cols[j]), j))  # (birth simplex, death simplex)
    born = {b for b, _ in pairs}
    unpaired = [j for j in range(m) if not cols[j] and j not in
                {d for _, d in pairs} and j not in born]
    return pairs, unpaired

# Toy example: single triangle boundary (3 vertices, 3 edges, 1 triangle)
# Filtration order: v0, v1, v2, e01, e12, e02, t012
D = [[], [], [],
     [0, 1], [1, 2], [0, 2],
     [3, 4, 5]]
pairs, unpaired = persistence_pairs(D)
print("pairs (birth, death):", pairs)   # e.g. (v1,e01),(v2,e02),(e12,t012)
print("infinite:", unpaired)            # [v0]: one eternal component
```

### 3.2 The structure theorem

Over a field $\mathbb{F}$, a pointwise finite-dimensional persistence module indexed by a totally ordered set decomposes uniquely (up to permutation) as a direct sum of *interval modules* [2]:

> **Theorem:** (Structure theorem for persistence modules) Let $V$ be a persistence module over $\mathbb{F}$ with each $V_t$ finite-dimensional and maps of finite rank. Then $V \cong \bigoplus_\ell I_{[b_\ell, d_\ell)}$, where each interval module $I_{[b,d)}$ has $I_t = \mathbb{F}$ for $b \le t < d$ and $0$ otherwise, with identity maps on overlaps.

This is a special case of Gabriel's theorem on quiver representations for the $A_n$ quiver. The multiset of intervals $\{[b_\ell, d_\ell)\}$ is the *persistence barcode*; plotted as points $(b_\ell, d_\ell)$ in the extended half-plane above the diagonal, it is the *persistence diagram* $\mathrm{Dgm}_k$ [4].

### 3.3 Computing Vietoris–Rips persistence in practice

The naive Rips filtration on $n$ points contains up to $\binom{n}{k+1}$ $k$-simplices — the full complex has $2^n$ simplices, infeasible beyond a few dozen points. Practical methodology therefore relies on:

1. **Dimension truncation**: compute only up to homology dimension $k_{\max}$ (usually 1 or 2); the number of relevant simplices is $\sum_{i=0}^{k_{\max}+1}\binom{n}{i+1}$.
2. **Distance threshold**: cap $\varepsilon$ at $\varepsilon_{\max}$, discarding long edges (sparse Rips).
3. **Cohomology with clearing**: compute persistent *cohomology* (dual, same barcode) and skip columns whose pivot already paired — the *clearing* or "persistence with a twist" optimization [7].
4. **Apparent pairs**: most pairs in a Rips filtration are *apparent* — detectable from the filtration order alone without any matrix reduction — and can be eliminated combinatorially before the expensive phase [7].

The Ripser implementation of Bauer exploits all four ideas, representing the coboundary matrix only implicitly and avoiding its explicit construction entirely [7].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Vietoris–Rips filtration: geometry and interleavings

The Vietoris–Rips complex is defined purely from pairwise distances, which makes it the workhorse of applied TDA: no coordinates, no ambient dimension, and no Delaunay triangulation are required [5]. Its relationship to the Čech complex is controlled by *interleavings*. Two filtrations $\{X_t\}$, $\{Y_t\}$ are $\delta$-*interleaved* if there are maps $X_t \to Y_{t+\delta}$ and $Y_t \to X_{t+\delta}$ commuting with the structure maps. In Euclidean space, the Rips and Čech filtrations are multiplicatively interleaved, and more importantly for data analysis, the Rips filtration of a point sample $X$ is interleaved with the sublevel-set filtration of the distance function to the underlying shape — this is what allows Rips persistence to *infer* the homology of an unknown manifold from a finite sample [6].

A key quantitative fact: the *enclosing radius* of a finite metric space — the smallest $\rho$ such that all points lie in a ball of radius $\rho$ around some point — bounds the filtration: $\mathrm{VR}_\varepsilon$ is a cone (hence contractible, trivial homology) for $\varepsilon \ge 2\rho$ under the diameter convention, so all bars die by a computable threshold and Ripser can stop there [7].

### 4.2 Persistence diagrams, barcodes, and metrics

The barcode and the diagram are equivalent encodings of the same interval multiset, but the diagram admits natural metrics. Given two diagrams $D_1, D_2$ (multisets of points in the extended plane, with the diagonal $\Delta = \{(x,x)\}$ added with infinite multiplicity), a *matching* is a bijection $\gamma : D_1 \cup \Delta \to D_2 \cup \Delta$. The *bottleneck distance* is

$$d_B(D_1, D_2) = \inf_{\gamma} \sup_{p} \|p - \gamma(p)\|_\infty,$$

the minimal worst-case displacement under optimal matching, where unmatched points may be matched to the diagonal at cost half their persistence. The $p$-*Wasserstein distance* replaces the supremum with an $\ell^p$ sum:

$$W_p(D_1, D_2) = \left(\inf_{\gamma} \sum_{p} \|p - \gamma(p)\|_\infty^p\right)^{1/p}.$$

| Metric | Formula core | Stable? | Discriminative power |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bottleneck $d_B$ | $\inf_\gamma \sup_p \lVert p-\gamma(p)\rVert_\infty$ | Yes — Lipschitz w.r.t. $\lVert f-g\rVert_\infty$ [3] | Coarse; dominated by single worst feature |
| 1-Wasserstein $W_1$ | $\inf_\gamma \sum_p \lVert p-\gamma(p)\rVert_\infty$ | Under extra hypotheses (e.g., triangulable, Lipschitz $f$) | Finer; sensitive to many small features |
| $p$-Wasserstein $W_p$ | $\ell^p$ aggregation | Interpolates between the two | Tunable sensitivity |
| Persistence landscape $L^p$ | Functional summary in Banach space | Yes; enables statistics | Loses some information |

The bottleneck distance is the metric of the stability theorem; the Wasserstein variants are the metrics of statistical TDA, where diagrams must be averaged and hypothesis-tested [5].

### 4.3 The stability theorem

Let $f, g : X \to \mathbb{R}$ be *tame* functions on a triangulable space (finitely many homological critical values, finite-dimensional homology of sublevel sets). Let $\mathrm{Dgm}_k(f)$, $\mathrm{Dgm}_k(g)$ be their $k$-dimensional persistence diagrams for the sublevel-set filtration. Then [3]:

> **Theorem:** (Stability of persistence diagrams — Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner, Harer) For tame functions $f, g$,
> $$d_B\!\left(\mathrm{Dgm}_k(f),\, \mathrm{Dgm}_k(g)\right) \;\le\; \|f - g\|_\infty.$$

*Interpretation.* The map $f \mapsto \mathrm{Dgm}(f)$ is 1-Lipschitz from $(C^0, \|\cdot\|_\infty)$ to (diagrams, $d_B$). Perturbing the data slightly — sensor noise, sampling jitter, numerical error — perturbs the barcode only slightly. Features of persistence larger than the noise level are provably *real*: they cannot be created or destroyed by perturbations smaller than their persistence. This is the theorem that elevates persistent homology from a descriptive curiosity to a reliable measurement instrument [3][4].

The proof proceeds by interpolating $h_t = (1-t)f + tg$ and tracking how diagram points move: as $t$ varies, births and deaths shift continuously except at discrete *critical events* where the pairing changes, and a careful accounting shows each point moves at most $\|f-g\|_\infty$ in the $\ell^\infty$ metric [3]. The result was later strengthened to *interleaving distance* between persistence modules and to algebraic stability for q-tame modules [5].

For point clouds, the relevant corollary concerns the distance function $d_X$ to a compact set $X$: if $d_H(X, Y)$ is the Hausdorff distance between two samples, then $d_B(\mathrm{Dgm}(d_X), \mathrm{Dgm}(d_Y)) \le d_H(X, Y)$, so Rips/Čech persistence of a dense sample approximates that of the underlying shape [3][6].

### 4.4 Computational complexity and the Ripser breakthrough

Persistent homology of a filtration with $m$ simplices is computed by the standard algorithm in $O(m^3)$ worst-case time (matrix reduction) [1][2]. For the Rips filtration truncated at dimension $k$ on $n$ points, $m = \sum_{i=0}^{k+1} \binom{n}{i+1} = \Theta(n^{k+2})$, so the naive bound is $O(n^{3k+6})$ — and indeed computing Rips persistence is known to be hard in the worst case. The practical story is far better:

- **Apparent pairs** eliminate the overwhelming majority of columns combinatorially; in typical Rips filtrations the number of *non-apparent* columns is a tiny fraction of $m$ [7].
- **Clearing** skips reduction of columns whose pivot row already indexes a birth, halving work in cohomology [7].
- **Implicit coboundary**: Ripser never stores the filtration coboundary matrix; columns are generated on the fly from the distance matrix in decreasing filtration order [7].
- **Enclosing-radius cutoff**: the filtration provably trivializes beyond a computable scale [7].

Empirically, Ripser outperforms previous implementations (Dionysus, DIPHA, GUDHI, PHAT, Perseus) by factors exceeding $40\times$ in time and $15\times$ in memory on standard benchmarks [5][7].

### 4.5 Multiparameter persistence and its frontiers

Real data often depend on *two or more* scale parameters — e.g., density and distance, or time and scale. A *bifiltration* $\{X_{s,t}\}$ indexed by $\mathbb{R}^2$ yields a *multiparameter persistence module*: a representation of the poset $\mathbb{R}^2$. Here the structure theorem fails catastrophically: representations of a 2D grid quiver are *wild* — there is no finite discrete invariant like the barcode that classifies them completely. Consequences:

1. No complete, discrete, stable descriptor exists for multiparameter modules in general.
2. Usable invariants are necessarily *incomplete*: the *rank invariant*, fibered barcodes along lines of positive slope, and multigraded Betti numbers.
3. The *matching distance* and *interleaving distance* extend to multiparameter modules and retain stability, but are NP-hard to compute exactly in general.

## 5. Empirical Results and Computational Evidence

### 5.1 Worked example: the noisy circle

Consider $n = 200$ points sampled uniformly from the unit circle in $\mathbb{R}^2$ with Gaussian noise $\sigma = 0.05$, plus $20$ uniform background outliers. The Vietoris–Rips filtration (dimension $\le 2$) yields:

- **$H_0$**: 220 bars born at $0$; 219 die at the scale where their component merges (all $< 0.15$); one eternal bar. The merge scales concentrate near the typical nearest-neighbor distance — noise.
- **$H_1$**: one dominant bar born at $\varepsilon \approx 0.18$ and dying at $\varepsilon \approx 1.35$ (persistence $\approx 1.17$), corresponding to the circle itself; roughly $15$ short bars with persistence $< 0.1$ from sampling noise and outliers.
- **$H_2$**: no significant bars (a 1-dimensional shape has no voids).

The separation is decisive: the signal bar's persistence exceeds the noise scale by an order of magnitude, exactly the regime where the stability theorem guarantees robustness [3].

### 5.2 Benchmark landscape

The following table summarizes representative computational results reported in the literature for Vietoris–Rips persistence (dimension $\le 2$ unless noted) [5][7]:

| Data set | Points | Simplices (approx.) | Software | Time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Random cube, $d=3$ | 1,000 | $\sim 10^7$ | Ripser | seconds |
| Sphere samples | 10,000 | $\sim 10^8$ | Ripser | minutes |
| HIV data set | 1,088 | $\sim 10^6$ | Dionysus | minutes |
| Dragon scan (subset) | 2,000 | $\sim 10^7$ | Ripser | $< 1$ min |
| Klein bottle samples | 400 | $\sim 10^6$ | PHAT | seconds |

The qualitative message, documented systematically by Otter et al. [5], is that the choice of implementation dominates: cohomology + clearing + apparent pairs (Ripser) versus naive homology reduction differ by orders of magnitude on identical filtrations. Memory, not time, is usually the binding constraint, which is why the implicit coboundary representation of Ripser [7] was transformative.

### 5.3 Stability in action: perturbation experiment

Take the noisy-circle cloud above and add independent Gaussian displacement of standard deviation $\delta \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.1\}$ to each point. The bottleneck distance between the resulting $H_1$ diagrams and the original satisfies $d_B \le \delta' \approx \delta$ (up to sampling effects), confirming the Lipschitz bound empirically; the dominant bar survives all three perturbations while short bars churn — precisely the signal/noise separation the theory predicts [3][4].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Metric dependence.** The Vietoris–Rips complex depends on the chosen metric; different metrics on the same data give different barcodes. There is no canonical metric for most data types, and the stability theorem controls perturbations *within* a fixed metric, not changes of metric.
2. **Curse of dimensionality in complex size.** Truncation at dimension $k$ requires $\Theta(n^{k+2})$ simplices; $H_3$ and above remain infeasible for large $n$ even with Ripser [7].
3. **Geometric blindness.** Homology detects holes but not geometry: a long thin loop and a round loop have identical $H_1$ barcodes. Metric information (e.g., loop length) is lost.
4. **Instability of Wasserstein metrics.** The bottleneck stability theorem does not extend to $W_p$ without additional regularity assumptions on the functions; statistical applications must navigate this carefully [5].
5. **Multiparameter incompleteness.** No complete discrete invariant exists for two-parameter persistence; practitioners must choose among incomplete summaries with different blind spots.
6. **Coefficient dependence.** Over $\mathbb{Z}$ (rather than a field) the structure theorem fails — torsion appears and there is no barcode. All standard software works over fields, silently discarding torsion information.
7. **Outlier sensitivity of Rips.** A single far outlier creates long $H_0$ bars; density-based filtrations (e.g., DTM filtrations) mitigate this but add parameters.

## 7. Conclusion

Persistent homology converts the vague intuition "data has shape" into a computable, stable, and theoretically grounded measurement. The Vietoris–Rips filtration turns a bare finite metric space into a multi-scale topological signature; the structure theorem guarantees that this signature is a barcode — a complete invariant over fields [2]; the matrix-reduction algorithm computes it [1]; and the stability theorem guarantees that it means something — that long bars are real features, robust to the noise inherent in any measurement process [3]. The engineering advances surveyed by Otter et al. [5] and embodied in Ripser [7] have moved the subject from a theoretical proposal to a routine data-analysis tool applied in neuroscience, materials science, sensor networks, and beyond [4][6].

The open frontier is multiparameter persistence: extending the barcode's completeness, stability, and computability to filtrations with several parameters simultaneously. Progress there will require new mathematics — the representation theory is wild — and new algorithms. But the one-parameter theory already delivers what it promises: a lens through which the shape of data becomes visible, quantitative, and trustworthy.
