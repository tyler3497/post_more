# Stellar Structure and Evolution: Nuclear Reaction Networks, the Equations of Stellar Structure, and Supernova Progenitors

## Abstract

We present a graduate-level treatment of stellar structure and evolution, uniting three pillars of modern stellar astrophysics: the nuclear reaction networks that power stars, the four canonical equations of stellar structure that govern hydrostatic interiors, and the fate of massive stars as core-collapse supernova progenitors. Beginning from the proton–proton chain, the CNO cycle, and the triple-alpha process, we derive the temperature sensitivities that decide which fuel burns where and show how these energy generation rates couple to the hydrostatic, mass-continuity, energy-generation, and energy-transport equations. We develop the Schwarzschild criterion, the radiative and adiabatic gradients, and opacity's role in interiors, with polytropic Lane–Emden models as analytic references. We then trace advanced burning stages — carbon, neon, oxygen, and silicon burning — through photodisintegration and nuclear statistical equilibrium to the formation of the inert iron core, whose collapse at nuclear density drives the neutrino-mediated core-collapse supernova mechanism. Finally, we survey progenitor mass ranges, the observed II-P / IIb / Ib / Ic spectroscopic sequence, the red-supergiant problem, and multi-dimensional explosion models, connecting theory to direct progenitor detections and light-curve modeling.

## 1. Introduction

A star is a self-gravitating sphere of plasma in which gravity is balanced, over most of its lifetime, by pressure gradients sustained by nuclear fusion in its core. This conceals one of the richest boundary-value problems in physics: a coupled system of nonlinear ODEs whose microphysical inputs — nuclear cross sections, opacities, equations of state — span particle physics and plasma theory. Understanding stars requires simultaneous mastery of the *macroscopic* structure equations and the *microscopic* reaction networks that feed them energy.

The modern theory crystallized in the early-to-mid twentieth century: Eddington's radiative models (1916–1926) showed opacity controls luminosity [4]; Chandrasekhar (1931) derived the $1.44\,M_\odot$ white-dwarf limit [3]; Bethe and von Weizsäcker (1938–1939) identified the pp chain and CNO cycle [1]; and the 1957 B²FH paper tied cosmic chemical evolution to successive burning stages in massive stars [2].

We proceed as follows: §2 background, §3 the structure equations and network formalism, §4 a deep dive (burning networks, microphysics, convection, advanced stages), §5 supernova progenitors and the explosion mechanism, §6 limitations, §7 conclusions.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Observational context

The Hertzsprung–Russell (HR) diagram — luminosity versus effective temperature — organizes stellar astrophysics: main sequence (core H burning), giants/supergiants upper right, white dwarfs lower left. A star's ZAMS mass (plus metallicity and rotation) determines its trajectory.

Supernovae are classified spectroscopically. **Type II** shows hydrogen: **II-P** (plateau light curve), **II-L** (linear decline), **IIb** (early H fading to He-dominated). **Type Ib** lacks H but shows He; **Type Ic** lacks both. The H-deficient (stripped-envelope) classes come from progenitors that lost envelopes via winds or binary interaction [5][6].

### 2.2 Historical arc

The key conceptual milestones are:

1. **1916–1926**: Eddington derives the mass–luminosity relation from radiative transport.
2. **1931**: Chandrasekhar derives the limiting mass $M_{\rm Ch} \simeq 1.44\,M_\odot$ for degenerate white dwarfs.
3. **1938–1939**: Bethe identifies the pp chain and the CNO cycle; the CNO cycle's $\epsilon_{\rm CNO} \propto T^{17}$ explains its dominance above $\sim 1.3\,M_\odot$.
4. **1952–1954**: Salpeter proposes the triple-alpha process; Hoyle predicts the $7.654$ MeV resonance in $^{12}$C required to make it fast enough — a celebrated anthropic prediction.
5. **1957**: B²FH maps heavy-element synthesis onto hydrostatic and explosive burning stages.
6. **1966–present**: the neutrino-driven core-collapse paradigm matures; modern 3D simulations with SASI and neutrino-driven convection explode progenitors self-consistently.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The four equations of stellar structure

In Lagrangian (mass) coordinates, with $M_r$ the mass interior to radius $r$, the structure of a non-rotating, spherically symmetric star in hydrostatic equilibrium is governed by four first-order ODEs (mass continuity, hydrostatic equilibrium, energy generation, energy transport):

$$\frac{dM_r}{dr} = 4\pi r^2 \rho \tag{3.1}$$

$$\frac{dP}{dr} = -\frac{GM_r \rho}{r^2} \tag{3.2}$$

$$\frac{dL_r}{dr} = 4\pi r^2 \rho\,\epsilon \tag{3.3}$$

$$\frac{dT}{dr} = -\frac{3\kappa \rho L_r}{16\pi a c\, r^2 T^3} \quad \text{(radiative zones)} \tag{3.4a}$$

$$\frac{dT}{dr} = \left(1 - \frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\frac{T}{P}\frac{dP}{dr} \quad \text{(convective zones)} \tag{3.4b}$$

where $\rho$ is density, $P$ pressure, $L_r$ interior luminosity, $\epsilon$ the specific nuclear energy generation rate (plus gravitational and neutrino terms), $\kappa$ the Rosseland mean opacity, and $a$ the radiation constant.

> **Theorem:** For given mass and composition, the structure equations plus boundary conditions ($M_r = L_r = 0$ at $r = 0$; $P = T = 0$, $M_r = M$, $L_r = L$ at the surface) determine the star uniquely — the content of the Vogt–Russell theorem, modulo mass loss and other complications.

It is often convenient to work with logarithmic gradients. The **radiative gradient**

$$\nabla_{\rm rad} \equiv \left(\frac{d\ln T}{d\ln P}\right)_{\rm rad} = \frac{3\kappa L_r P}{16\pi a c\, G M_r T^4}, \tag{3.5}$$

competes with the adiabatic gradient $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ from the equation of state; the actual gradient is $\nabla = \min(\nabla_{\rm rad}, \nabla_{\rm ad})$ in the Schwarzschild picture.

> **Theorem (Schwarzschild criterion):** A layer is convectively unstable if and only if $\nabla_{\rm rad} > \nabla_{\rm ad}$. The Ledoux criterion generalizes this to composition gradients: instability requires $\nabla_{\rm rad} > \nabla_{\rm ad} + \frac{\phi}{\delta}\nabla_\mu$.

### 3.2 Nuclear reaction network formalism

Let $Y_i = X_i/A_i$ be the molar abundance of species $i$. The network evolves as

$$\frac{dY_i}{dt} = \sum_j c_{ij}\,\lambda_j Y_j + \sum_{j,k} c_{ijk}\,\rho N_A \langle\sigma v\rangle_{jk}\, Y_j Y_k + \sum_{j,k,l} c_{ijkl}\,\rho^2 N_A^2 \langle\sigma v\rangle_{jkl}\, Y_j Y_k Y_l, \tag{3.6}$$

with stoichiometric coefficients $c$, decay constants $\lambda$, and thermally averaged cross sections $\langle\sigma v\rangle$. The energy generation rate follows from the mass differences, $\epsilon = \sum r\,Q - \epsilon_\nu$, where $Q$ is the energy release and $\epsilon_\nu$ the neutrino-loss term — neutrinos escape freely from normal stars, carrying a few percent of pp-chain energy but essentially all of a core-collapse supernova's binding energy.

Tunneling through the Coulomb barrier (the Gamow peak) gives $\epsilon \propto T^\nu$ with $\nu \approx 4$ (pp chain), $\nu \approx 17$–$20$ (CNO cycle), and $\nu \approx 40$ (triple-alpha near $10^8$ K) [1][2].

Modern stellar evolution uses Henyey-type relaxation codes solving the structure equations implicitly over an adaptive mesh, coupled to networks of dozens to thousands of isotopes. ## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The hydrogen- and helium-burning networks

Hydrogen burning proceeds through two distinct networks whose relative importance is decided by temperature and metallicity.

**The proton–proton chain.** In the Sun ($T_c \approx 1.57 \times 10^7$ K), the pp chain dominates. Its principal branch (pp I):

$$p + p \to d + e^+ + \nu_e; \quad d + p \to {}^3{\rm He} + \gamma; \quad {}^3{\rm He} + {}^3{\rm He} \to {}^4{\rm He} + 2p,$$

net $4p \to {}^4{\rm He} + 2e^+ + 2\nu_e$. Of $26.73$ MeV liberated, neutrinos carry $\sim 1.2$ MeV away, leaving **$26.2$ MeV deposited** ($E_{pp} = 6.3 \times 10^{18}\ {\rm erg\,g^{-1}}$) [1]. The pp II/pp III branches (via $^7$Be electron capture and $^8$B decay) dominate the detectable solar neutrino flux: $^8$B neutrinos reach $14.06$ MeV versus $0.420$ MeV for pp neutrinos [4].

**The CNO cycle.** At $T \gtrsim 1.7 \times 10^7$ K — in stars above $\sim 1.3\,M_\odot$ — the CNO cycle overtakes the pp chain ($\epsilon_{\rm CNO} \propto T^{17}$ versus $\epsilon_{pp} \propto T^4$). Carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen act as *catalysts*:

$${}^{12}{\rm C}(p,\gamma){}^{13}{\rm N}(e^+\nu_e){}^{13}{\rm C}(p,\gamma){}^{14}{\rm N}(p,\gamma){}^{15}{\rm O}(e^+\nu_e){}^{15}{\rm N}(p,\alpha){}^{12}{\rm C},$$

net $4p \to {}^4{\rm He} + 2e^+ + 2\nu_e$, $25.0$ MeV deposited (larger neutrino losses: $E_{\rm CNO} = 6.0 \times 10^{18}\ {\rm erg\,g^{-1}}$) [1][2]. In steady state, $^{14}$N accumulates because $^{14}{\rm N}(p,\gamma)^{15}{\rm O}$ is the bottleneck — a diagnostic seen in massive-star spectra.

**The triple-alpha process.** Hydrogen burning leaves helium "ash" that cannot burn at $10^7$ K: no stable nuclei exist with mass 5 or 8. Only at $T \sim 10^8$ K, in contracting red-giant cores, does the equilibrium concentration of the ${}^8{\rm Be}$ resonance ($\tau \approx 2.6 \times 10^{-16}$ s — fleeting, yet long versus the $\sim 10^{-20}$ s alpha-crossing time) suffice for a second alpha capture [2]. The capture lands on the **Hoyle state**, the $0^+$ resonance in $^{12}$C at $7.654$ MeV, predicted by Hoyle in 1954 precisely because stellar carbon production demanded it. The net $3\,{}^4{\rm He} \to {}^{12}{\rm C} + \gamma$ releases $7.28$ MeV ($E_{3\alpha} = 6.0 \times 10^{17}\ {\rm erg\,g^{-1}}$) [1][3]; the $\epsilon_{3\alpha} \propto T^{40}$ dependence makes helium burning explosively temperature-sensitive — the origin of the helium flash in degenerate cores.

Where alphas are abundant, ${}^{12}{\rm C}(\alpha,\gamma){}^{16}{\rm O}$ follows. The resulting $^{16}$O/$^{12}$C ratio ($\approx 2$ at solar metallicity) is exquisitely sensitive to this cross section and remains a leading uncertainty in stellar modeling, propagating into white-dwarf cooling ages and supernova progenitor structures [2].

The principal burning stages and their energetics are summarized below [3]:

| Fuel | Process | $T$ ($10^6$ K) | Products | Energy (MeV/nucleon) |
|------|---------|----------------|----------|----------------------|
| H | pp chain | $\sim 6$–$15$ | $^4$He | 6.55 |
| H | CNO cycle | $\gtrsim 15$–$20$ | $^4$He | 6.25 |
| He | triple-$\alpha$ | $\sim 100$ | $^{12}$C, $^{16}$O | 0.61 |
| C | $^{12}$C+$^{12}$C | $\sim 600$ | O, Ne, Na, Mg | 0.54 |
| O | $^{16}$O+$^{16}$O | $\sim 1000$ | Mg, S, P, Si | $\sim 0.3$ |
| Si | photodisintegration + captures | $\sim 3000$ | Co, Fe, Ni | $\sim 0.18$ |

Energy per nucleon declines down the table: each fuel is more tightly bound than the last, and the sequence terminates at the iron peak, where fusion yields no more energy.

### 4.2 Equation of state, opacity, and polytropic models

The structure equations close with an **equation of state** $P(\rho, T, X_i)$ and **opacity** $\kappa(\rho, T, X_i)$. Stellar interiors are fully ionized, $P = \rho k T/\mu m_u + aT^4/3$; in degenerate cores Fermi–Dirac statistics give $P \propto \rho^{5/3}$ (non-relativistic) or $P \propto \rho^{4/3}$ (relativistic), the latter yielding the Chandrasekhar limit

$$M_{\rm Ch} = \frac{5.83}{\mu_e^2}\,M_\odot \simeq 1.44\,M_\odot \quad (\mu_e = 2), \tag{4.2}$$

the maximum mass supportable by electron degeneracy pressure [3].

Opacity is dominated by bound–free and free–free absorption (Kramers' law $\kappa \propto \rho T^{-3.5}$), electron scattering ($\kappa_{\rm es} \approx 0.2(1+X)$), and H$^-$ at low $T$; modern OPAL/OP tables tabulate $\kappa(\rho, T, X_i)$, and the iron opacity bump near $2 \times 10^5$ K drives $\beta$ Cephei pulsations.

**Polytropes** ($P = K\rho^{1+1/n}$) reduce the structure equations to the Lane–Emden equation, the analytic laboratory of stellar structure:

$$\frac{1}{\xi^2}\frac{d}{d\xi}\left(\xi^2 \frac{d\theta}{d\xi}\right) + \theta^n = 0, \qquad \theta(0) = 1,\ \theta'(0) = 0. \tag{4.3}$$

$n = 3/2$ describes fully convective stars and non-relativistic white dwarfs; $n = 3$ approximates the Eddington standard model and relativistic white dwarfs. A minimal solver:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

def lane_emden(n, xi_max=15.0):
    """Integrate the Lane-Emden equation; returns (xi, theta)."""
    def f(xi, y):
        th, dth = y
        if xi < 1e-8:
            return [dth, -th**n / 3.0]
        return [dth, -2.0*dth/xi - max(th, 0.0)**n]
    sol = solve_ivp(f, (0, xi_max), (1.0, 0.0), max_step=0.01,
                    events=lambda xi, y: y[0])
    return sol.t, sol.y[0]

xi, theta = lane_emden(3.0)
xi1 = xi[-1]          # first zero: stellar surface
print(f"n=3 polytrope surface at xi1 = {xi1:.4f}")  # ~6.8969
```

```haskell
-- Lane-Emden right-hand side for polytropic index n
laneEmden :: Double -> Double -> (Double, Double) -> (Double, Double)
laneEmden n xi (th, dth)
  | xi < 1e-8   = (dth, -th**n / 3.0)
  | otherwise   = (dth, -2*dth/xi - max th 0**n)
```

### 4.3 Convection, semiconvection, and mixing

Where the Schwarzschild criterion is violated, bulk fluid motion carries the flux. Mixing-length theory (MLT) parameterizes this with $\alpha_{\rm MLT} = \ell/H_P \sim 1.6$–$2.0$ (solar-calibrated), now benchmarked against 3D radiation-hydrodynamics. Convection also *mixes* composition, setting core sizes, lifetimes, and final fates.

Three mixing phenomena deserve emphasis:

- **Convective core overshoot**: inertia carries eddies beyond the Schwarzschild boundary, enlarging the fuel reservoir — a leading uncertainty in massive-star core masses.
- **Semiconvection**: in the Ledoux regime (stabilizing $\mu$-gradients, destabilizing thermal gradients), slow layered mixing redistributes composition on thermal timescales.
- **Rotational mixing**: meridional circulation and shear transport angular momentum and chemicals; rotating models produce larger helium cores and chemically homogeneous evolution at low metallicity — a proposed long-GRB channel.

A compact statement of the convective flux in MLT form:

> **Theorem (mixing-length scaling):** In efficient convection ($\nabla \approx \nabla_{\rm ad}$), $F_{\rm conv} \propto \rho c_P T (g\delta)^{1/2} \ell^2 H_P^{-3/2} (\nabla - \nabla_{\rm ad})^{3/2}$; the superadiabaticity adjusts so convection carries whatever flux radiation cannot.

### 4.4 Advanced burning stages and the iron core

After helium exhaustion, the CO core contracts, and the outcome bifurcates by mass:

- **$M_{\rm ZAMS} \lesssim 8\,M_\odot$**: the CO core becomes degenerate; the remnant is a C/O white dwarf below $M_{\rm Ch}$.
- **$8 \lesssim M_{\rm ZAMS}/M_\odot \lesssim 10$**: O/Ne white dwarfs, or electron-capture supernovae when $^{20}$Ne/$^{24}$Mg capture electrons and remove pressure support.
- **$M_{\rm ZAMS} \gtrsim 10\,M_\odot$**: full advanced burning. Carbon burning ($T \sim 6 \times 10^8$ K) yields O, Ne, Na, Mg; neon burning proceeds partly by photodisintegration ${}^{20}{\rm Ne}(\gamma,\alpha){}^{16}{\rm O}$ plus alpha captures; oxygen burning ($T \sim 10^9$ K) yields Si, S, Ar, Ca; silicon burning ($T \sim 3 \times 10^9$ K) is a *photodisintegration–rearrangement equilibrium* relaxing to **nuclear statistical equilibrium (NSE)** peaked at the iron peak ($^{56}$Ni $\to$ $^{56}$Co $\to$ $^{56}$Fe) [3][2].

Because each stage burns faster than the last (carbon burning lasts centuries, silicon burning *days*), the pre-collapse core is an onion-shell structure: an inert $\sim 1.4\,M_\odot$ Fe core surrounded by Si, O, Ne, C, He, and H shells. Fusion of iron-peak nuclei is endothermic, so the core — supported by electron degeneracy — cannot generate energy. As silicon-shell burning adds mass, the core approaches $M_{\rm Ch}$ while electron captures ($p + e^- \to n + \nu_e$) and photodisintegration sap pressure and energy; collapse begins when the effective Chandrasekhar mass falls below the core mass, proceeding on a millisecond free-fall timescale.

The stiffness of coupled burning — rates spanning ~40 orders of magnitude — is why production codes (MESA, KEPLER) use implicit Newton/Bader–Deuflhard solves rather than explicit stepping:

```rust
// Explicit Euler step of a 2-species toy network: dY/dt = -lambda(T) * Y
// Production codes (MESA, KEPLER) use implicit Bader-Deuflhard / Newton
// solves because rates span ~40 orders of magnitude.
fn network_step(y: &mut [f64], lambda: f64, dt: f64) {
    for yi in y.iter_mut() {
        *yi += -lambda * *yi * dt;   // stable only if dt << 1/lambda
    }
}
```

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The collapse and the neutrino mechanism

When the iron core exceeds the effective Chandrasekhar limit, collapse proceeds in five stages:

1. **Collapse**: the inner core falls homologously until nuclear saturation density, $\rho \sim 2.7 \times 10^{17}\ {\rm kg\,m^{-3}}$. Neutrino mean free paths shrink from parsecs to kilometers; neutrinos become trapped [4].
2. **Bounce**: the nuclear equation of state stiffens; the inner core rebounds, launching a shock at $\sim 10$–$20$ km.
3. **Shock stall**: dissociation of iron-group nuclei and neutrino losses sap $\sim 10^{51}$ erg, stalling the shock at $100$–$200$ km within $\sim 100$ ms.
4. **Revival**: in the delayed neutrino-heating paradigm, $\nu_e$/$\bar\nu_e$ absorption in the gain region revives the shock. Neutrino-driven convection and SASI ($\ell = 1, 2$ sloshing modes) break spherical symmetry; modern 3D codes (PROMETHEUS-VERTEX, FORNAX, CHIMERA) now explode $9$–$25\,M_\odot$ progenitors self-consistently, though the explodability landscape versus progenitor compactness remains debated.
5. **Aftermath**: $\sim 99\%$ of the binding energy ($\sim 3 \times 10^{53}$ erg) escapes as neutrinos over $\sim 10$ s — confirmed by the 24 SN 1987A events — while $\sim 1\%$ ($\sim 10^{51}$ erg) powers the optical display. The remnant is a neutron star, or a black hole if fallback pushes it past $\sim 2$–$2.5\,M_\odot$.

> **Theorem (energy partition):** Forming a neutron star liberates $E_b \sim 3GM^2/5R \sim 3 \times 10^{53}\ {\rm erg}$, two orders of magnitude above the explosion kinetic energy. A core-collapse supernova is a *neutrino* event with an optical trace, not the reverse.

### 5.2 Progenitor mass ranges and the spectroscopic sequence

The solar-metallicity ZAMS-mass-to-supernova mapping:

| ZAMS mass ($M_\odot$) | Final state | Typical SN type |
|----------------------|-------------|-----------------|
| $< 8$ | C/O or O/Ne white dwarf | (none; AGB mass loss) |
| $8$–$10$ | O/Ne WD or electron-capture SN | faint II-P / transient |
| $10$–$25$ | neutron star | II-P, II-L, IIb |
| $25$–$35$ | neutron star / black hole (winds strip H) | Ib, Ic |
| $> 35$ | Wolf–Rayet $\to$ black hole | Ic (broad-lined), hypernova/GRB |
| $140$–$300$ | complete disruption | pair-instability SN |

Three empirical pillars constrain this mapping [5][6]:

- **Direct progenitor detections**: archival HST imaging identifies II-P progenitors as red supergiants of $8$–$17\,M_\odot$. The absence of higher-mass RSG progenitors — the "red supergiant problem" — suggests stars above $\sim 20\,M_\odot$ collapse quietly or explode as other SN types [5].
- **Spatial statistics**: SN positions correlated with H$\alpha$ emission show II-P events tracking $\gtrsim 9\,M_\odot$ RSGs, Ic events tracking $\gtrsim 20\,M_\odot$ Wolf–Rayet stars, and IIb/II-L matching $\sim 15\,M_\odot$ stars; Type Ib distributions favor a dominant *binary-stripping* channel over single-star winds [5].
- **The stripped-envelope continuum**: IIb $\to$ Ib $\to$ Ic form a continuum of decreasing residual envelope mass, set by metallicity-dependent winds, eruptive loss, and Roche-lobe overflow. Ultra-stripped supernovae — helium stars stripped to $\sim 1.5\,M_\odot$ cores barely above $M_{\rm Ch}$ — yield fast, faint transients (ejecta $< 0.1\,M_\odot$) and likely progenitors of merging double neutron stars [7].

### 5.3 Nucleosynthesis yields as proof

Elemental abundances are the fossil record of stellar burning. Quantitative successes:

- **Solar $^{16}$O/$^{12}$C $\approx 2$** and the $s$-process pattern, reproduced with the adopted $^{12}{\rm C}(\alpha,\gamma)^{16}{\rm O}$ rate [2].
- **$^{56}$Ni-powered light curves**: Arnett-type fits give $M(^{56}{\rm Ni}) \sim 10^{-3}$–$0.5\,M_\odot$, confirming explosive silicon burning in the ejecta [6].
- **Presolar grains**: meteoritic SiC and oxide grains carry isotopic anomalies (e.g., $^{44}$Ti, a 60-yr half-life explosive product) matching supernova nucleosynthesis calculations.
- **SN 1987A**: its neutrino burst, light curve, and ring validated the core-collapse paradigm; its blue-supergiant progenitor Sk $-69^\circ$202 showed binary history can reshape pre-supernova structure.

## 6. Limitations

The principal limitations:

1. **Convection and mixing**: MLT is a parameterization; overshoot, semiconvection, and rotational-mixing prescriptions shift core masses by tens of percent, moving the predicted black-hole boundary.
2. **Mass loss**: RSG and Wolf–Rayet wind rates are uncertain at the factor-of-two level (clumping), and eruptive pre-supernova mass loss is not predicted ab initio by any 1D code.
3. **Nuclear rates**: the $^{12}{\rm C}(\alpha,\gamma)^{16}{\rm O}$ rate, key C/O-burning cross sections, and $pf$-shell electron-capture rates propagate into core sizes and explodability.
4. **The explosion mechanism**: 3D neutrino-driven explosions succeed for many progenitors, but which stars explode versus collapse to black holes remains model-dependent.
5. **Binarity**: most massive stars are born in binaries, yet most progenitor grids are single-star models; stripping, mergers, and common-envelope phases reshape the IIb/Ib/Ic map [6][7].
6. **Dimensionality**: 1D evolution cannot capture turbulent O/Si-shell convection, whose perturbations seed explosion asymmetries.

## 7. Conclusion

From proton tunneling in the solar core to the neutrino fireball of collapse, stellar evolution is one narrative: a few structure equations, an intricate web of nuclear reactions. The structure equations determine *how* a star holds itself up; the reaction networks — pp chain, CNO cycle, triple-alpha, and the advanced stages — determine *what it burns and for how long*; the iron core's degeneracy-driven collapse determines *how it dies*.

The field is now data-rich: direct progenitor detections, gravitational-wave observations of compact mergers, and 3D explosion simulations jointly constrain the ZAMS-mass-to-supernova mapping. The outstanding problems — convective boundary mixing, wind mass loss, the explosion criterion, binarity — are quantitative, and each is yielding to better microphysics, multidimensional simulation, and observation.
