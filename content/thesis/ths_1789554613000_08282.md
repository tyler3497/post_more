# Neural Operators on Banach Spaces: DeepONet Branch–Trunk Architectures, Fourier Neural Operators, and Universal Approximation of Parametric PDE Solution Maps

## Abstract

Operator learning approximates maps between infinite-dimensional function spaces rather than between finite vectors. This article develops the foundations of neural operators — deep models that learn continuous operators such as the solution map of a parametric PDE. We start from the Chen–Chen universal approximation theorem for nonlinear operators [4], which motivates the branch–trunk factorization of DeepONet [1]. We derive the Fourier Neural Operator [2] as a discretization-invariant parameterization of integral-kernel layers in the neural-operator framework of Kovachki et al. [3], explain its quasi-linear FFT complexity and spectral inductive bias, and survey approximation and error-bound theory [7]. Physics-informed extensions — physics-informed DeepONet [6] and physics-informed neural operators (PINO) [5] — are presented as answers to the data cost and optimization fragility of purely supervised and purely physics-driven regimes. Benchmarks from the PDEBench suite [8] contextualize reported three-orders-of-magnitude speedups over classical solvers. Throughout, we separate rigorously established results from open problems and practical limitations.

## 1 Intro

The history of numerical analysis is, to a large extent, the history of solving one partial differential equation at a time. A finite-element practitioner chooses a mesh, assembles a stiffness matrix for a *specific* coefficient field, boundary condition, and source term, and solves. Change the coefficient field and the computation must be repeated almost from scratch. This paradigm is unassailable for single-instance accuracy, but it is poorly matched to the many-query settings that dominate modern science and engineering: design optimization, uncertainty quantification, inverse problems, and real-time control all require the repeated evaluation of an entire *family* of PDEs parameterized by functional inputs.

**Operator learning** proposes a different contract. Instead of approximating a single solution function, we approximate the *solution operator* — the map $\mathcal{G}$ that carries an input function $a$ (a coefficient, a source, an initial condition) to the corresponding output function $u = \mathcal{G}(a)$. Once such an operator is learned from data, evaluating it for a new input function costs only a forward pass of a neural network, amortizing the expensive training phase across arbitrarily many downstream queries [2,3].

This idea is older than it appears. The theoretical seed was planted in 1995 by Chen and Chen, who proved that neural networks are universal approximators not only of functions but of *continuous nonlinear operators* between spaces of functions [4]. Their theorem — that for any $\epsilon > 0$ and any continuous operator on a compact set of functions there exist sensor locations and network weights achieving uniform error below $\epsilon$ — lay largely dormant until it was operationalized by DeepONet [1], whose branch–trunk architecture is a direct constructive realization of the Chen–Chen factorization.

A second line of attack reformulated the problem as *neural operators*: iterative maps on function spaces built from linear integral kernels composed with pointwise nonlinearities, deliberately designed to be discretization-invariant so that the same parameters transfer across mesh resolutions [3]. The Fourier Neural Operator instantiates the kernel in frequency space, achieving state-of-the-art accuracy on turbulent flows with zero-shot super-resolution [2].

This article provides a self-contained, PhD-level treatment of these developments:

1. The functional-analytic setting: Banach spaces of functions, compactness, and the precise statement of the universal approximation theorem for operators.
2. The DeepONet architecture: branch and trunk networks, the inner-product factorization, and physics-informed variants [1,6].
3. The neural-operator formalism: integral-kernel layers, four efficient parameterizations, and the Fourier realization [2,3,7].
4. Hybrid training: physics-informed neural operators (PINO) that combine coarse data with fine-resolution PDE residuals [5].
5. Empirical evidence: benchmarks on Burgers, Darcy, and Navier–Stokes equations, and the PDEBench evaluation suite [8].
6. Limitations and open problems: non-periodic domains, inverse problems, rigorous error bounds, and the curse of parametric dimensionality.

## 2 Background

### Function spaces and the operator-learning problem

Let $D \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ be a bounded domain and let $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{U}$ be Banach spaces of functions defined on $D$ — for example $L^2(D)$, $H^1(D)$, or $C(D)$ equipped with their standard norms. We consider a continuous, generally nonlinear operator

$$\mathcal{G}^\dagger : \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{U}$$

that we wish to approximate by a parametric family $\mathcal{G}_\theta$. In the PDE context, $\mathcal{G}^\dagger$ is the solution operator of a parametric family of equations: given an input function $a \in \mathcal{A}$ (say, a diffusion coefficient), $u = \mathcal{G}^\dagger(a)$ is the solution of the corresponding PDE. The learning problem is to find parameters $\theta$ minimizing an empirical risk over input–output function pairs $(a_j, u_j)$ drawn from some distribution on $\mathcal{A}$ [3].

> Theorem: (Chen–Chen universal approximation for operators [4]). Let $\sigma$ be a continuous non-polynomial activation function, let $X$ be a Banach space, let $K_1 \subset X$ and $K_2 \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ be compact, let $V \subset C(K_1)$ be compact, and let $\mathcal{G} : V \to C(K_2)$ be a continuous nonlinear operator. Then for every $\epsilon > 0$ there exist positive integers $n, p, m$, constants $c_i^k, \xi_{ij}^k, \theta_i^k, \zeta_k \in \mathbb{R}$, $w_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$, and sensor points $x_j \in K_1$ such that the shallow network
> $$\Bigg|\mathcal{G}(u)(y) - \sum_{k=1}^{p}\sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i^k \,\sigma\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m} \xi_{ij}^k u(x_j) + \theta_i^k\right)\sigma(w_k \cdot y + \zeta_k)\Bigg| < \epsilon$$
> holds uniformly for all $u \in V$ and $y \in K_2$.

The structure of this approximant is crucial: the inner term depends only on *finitely many sensor evaluations* $u(x_1), \dots, u(x_m)$ of the input function, while the outer term depends only on the query point $y$ at which the output function is evaluated. This factorization is exactly what DeepONet implements with deep networks in place of the shallow sums [1].

### From finite to infinite dimensions

Classical universal approximation concerns maps $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$. Operator approximation lifts the domain and codomain to infinite dimensions. Compactness of the input-function set $V$ is the essential hypothesis: it tames the infinite-dimensional input so that finitely many sensors suffice. In practice, the distribution of input functions in a PDE family (e.g., Gaussian random fields with a fixed covariance) concentrates near such compact sets, which is why the theory, though stated for worst-case compacta, has practical bite [3].

### Related paradigms

It is worth distinguishing operator learning from adjacent frameworks:

- **Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs)** approximate one solution function of one PDE instance by minimizing the PDE residual; they do not learn across instances and their optimization is notoriously fragile on multi-scale dynamics [5].
- **Classical reduced-order models** (proper orthogonal decomposition, reduced-basis methods) also build input–output maps, but they are linear in the trial space and typically intrusive, requiring access to the discretized PDE operators [3].
- **Neural operators** are non-intrusive (data-driven), nonlinear, and discretization-invariant: the parameters learned on one mesh apply directly to finer meshes, a property called *zero-shot super-resolution* [2].

## 3 Methodology

### The DeepONet factorization

DeepONet [1] realizes the Chen–Chen factorization with two deep networks. The *branch network* $g : \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^p$ encodes the input function $u$ through its values at $m$ fixed sensor points; the *trunk network* $f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^p$ encodes the query coordinate $y$. The operator approximant is the inner product

$$\mathcal{G}_\theta(u)(y) = \langle g_\theta(u(x_1),\dots,u(x_m)),\, f_\theta(y) \rangle = \sum_{k=1}^{p} b_k(u)\, t_k(y).$$

A generalized theorem in [1] shows that $g$ and $f$ may be any sufficiently expressive architectures — fully connected, convolutional, or recurrent — and the inner product of their $p$-dimensional outputs still yields universal approximation of continuous operators. The integer $p$ plays the role of a learned spectral rank: the trunk outputs $\{t_k\}$ act as a data-adapted basis of the output function space, while the branch outputs $\{b_k\}$ are the corresponding expansion coefficients determined by the input function.

Training is supervised: given $N$ input functions and $q$ query points per function, the loss is the mean-squared error between $\mathcal{G}_\theta(u^{(n)})(y_j^{(n)})$ and the observed output values. Because the trunk can be evaluated at arbitrary coordinates, DeepONet is naturally mesh-free at query time.

### The neural-operator layer

The framework of Kovachki and coauthors [3] builds operators by iterating *neural-operator layers* on function spaces. Starting from a lifted representation $v_0 = P(a)$ (a pointwise map into a higher-dimensional channel space), each layer applies

$$v_{t+1}(x) = \sigma\!\left( W v_t(x) + \int_D \kappa_\theta(x, y)\, v_t(y)\, dy + b(x) \right),$$

where $W$ is a local linear map, $\kappa_\theta$ is a learnable kernel, and $\sigma$ acts pointwise. A final projection $Q$ maps back to the output function space. This construction is deliberately parallel to a standard feedforward network, with matrix multiplication replaced by kernel integration.

Four efficient parameterizations of the kernel make this practical [3]:

1. **Graph neural operators**: the integral is approximated on a graph discretization with message passing, suitable for irregular domains.
2. **Multipole graph neural operators**: a fast-multipole-style hierarchical decomposition for long-range interactions.
3. **Low-rank neural operators**: the kernel is factored as $\kappa(x,y) \approx \sum_k \phi_k(x)\psi_k(y)$, reducing cost to linear in the grid size.
4. **Fourier neural operators**: the kernel is parameterized directly in Fourier space, which we now examine in detail.

### The Fourier parameterization

Assume for the moment that $D$ is the periodic torus. By the convolution theorem, a translation-invariant kernel acts by pointwise multiplication in Fourier space:

$$\int_D \kappa(x - y)\, v_t(y)\, dy \quad \xrightarrow{\;\mathcal{F}\;}\quad \widehat{\kappa}(k) \cdot \widehat{v}_t(k).$$

The Fourier Neural Operator [2] learns the multipliers $R(k) = \widehat{\kappa}(k)$ directly as complex-valued weight tensors, truncated to the lowest $k_{\max}$ Fourier modes. Each layer therefore computes

$$v_{t+1} = \sigma\!\left( W v_t + \mathcal{F}^{-1}(R \cdot \mathcal{F}(v_t)) \right),$$

implemented with the fast Fourier transform in $O(n \log n)$ time for $n$ grid points. Truncation to low modes acts as a learned spectral filter; the retained modes capture the energetic scales of the solution while higher modes are reconstructed implicitly through the nonlinearity and the local $W$ path.

**Discretization invariance** follows because the parameters $R$ live in frequency space, independent of the spatial grid: a model trained on a $64 \times 64$ grid evaluates directly on $256 \times 256$ by simply extending the Fourier basis — zero-shot super-resolution [2,3].

### Physics-informed training

Purely supervised operator learning requires large datasets of high-fidelity PDE solutions, which can be prohibitively expensive. Two hybrid strategies address this.

**Physics-informed DeepONet** [6] augments the data loss with a PDE-residual loss computed by automatic differentiation through the branch–trunk factorization:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}}(\theta) + \lambda\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{BC/IC}}(\theta),$$

where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}}$ penalizes the residual $\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{G}_\theta(u)) - f$ at collocation points.

**Physics-informed neural operators (PINO)** [5] combine coarse-resolution training data with PDE constraints imposed at a *higher* resolution, exploiting the discretization invariance of the FNO backbone. PINO is the first hybrid to succeed with no training data at all on challenging multi-scale systems such as Kolmogorov flows, where PINNs fail to converge due to optimization pathology. A test-time optimization phase further refines the operator ansatz for each query instance, blending the operator-learning and function-optimization paradigms.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Why the Branch–Trunk Factorization Is Canonical

The inner-product form $\sum_k b_k(u)\, t_k(y)$ is not an arbitrary architectural choice; it is the finite-rank separation of variables suggested by the Chen–Chen theorem [4].

A subtle consequence is that DeepONet's query flexibility comes at a price: the trunk must be expressive enough to represent the full output manifold, and the rank $p$ must be chosen large enough to capture it.

### 4.2 Spectral Bias and the Fourier Inductive Prior

The FNO's truncation to low Fourier modes is an *inductive bias*, not merely a computational convenience.

But this bias cuts both ways.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class SpectralConv2d(nn.Module):
    """One Fourier kernel layer: FFT -> multiply modes -> inverse FFT."""
    def __init__(self, in_channels, out_channels, modes1, modes2):
        super().__init__()
        self.modes1, self.modes2 = modes1, modes2
        scale = 1.0 / (in_channels * out_channels)
        # Learnable complex multipliers R(k) for the retained modes
        self.weights = nn.Parameter(
            scale * torch.rand(in_channels, out_channels,
                               modes1, modes2, dtype=torch.cfloat))

    def forward(self, x):
        # x: (batch, in_channels, nx, ny)
        x_ft = torch.fft.rfft2(x, norm="ortho")
        out_ft = torch.zeros(x.shape[0], self.weights.shape[1],
                             x.shape[-2], x.shape[-1] // 2 + 1,
                             dtype=torch.cfloat, device=x.device)
        # Pointwise multiplication on the retained low modes only
        out_ft[:, :, :self.modes1, :self.modes2] = torch.einsum(
            "bixy,ioxy->boxy",
            x_ft[:, :, :self.modes1, :self.modes2], self.weights)
        return torch.fft.irfft2(out_ft, s=(x.shape[-2], x.shape[-1]),
                                norm="ortho")
```

The complete FNO stacks several such layers with a local linear path $W$ and a pointwise nonlinearity between them; lifting $P$ and projection $Q$ are $1 \times 1$ convolutions applied pointwise in physical space [2].

### 4.3 Discretization Invariance as a Design Principle

The most consequential property of neural operators is that *the model is a map between function spaces, not between vectors*.

1. **Resolution transfer.** Train on coarse grids where data generation is cheap; evaluate on fine grids at inference.
2. **Mesh-agnostic inference.** Graph and multipole variants extend the principle to unstructured meshes and point clouds [3].
3. **Consistent refinement.** PINO's multi-resolution loss [5] — coarse data plus fine PDE residuals — is only meaningful because the operator is well-defined across discretizations.

This stands in sharp contrast to ordinary convolutional surrogates, whose weights are tied to a fixed grid and which must be retrained (or interpolated, with artifacts) when the resolution changes.

### 4.4 The Optimization Landscape: PINO and Test-Time Refinement

A persistent criticism of physics-driven learning is optimization fragility: minimizing PDE residuals over a single instance (as in PINNs) often stalls in poor local minima for stiff or multi-scale problems [5].

### 4.5 Benchmarks and the Speedup Claim

The empirical case for neural operators rests on three canonical benchmarks [2,3]:

| PDE | Input function | Output | Reported result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1D viscous Burgers | Initial condition $u_0$ | Solution at time $T$ | FNO beats prior ML baselines; ~1000x faster than spectral solver |
| 2D Darcy flow | Permeability field $a(x)$ | Pressure field $u(x)$ | Sub-1% relative $L^2$ error; strong zero-shot super-resolution |
| 2D Navier–Stokes (turbulent) | Initial vorticity | Vorticity at time $T$ | First ML method to model turbulent regime; 3 orders of magnitude faster |

The "three orders of magnitude" speedup [2] compares *amortized inference* against a classical solver run from scratch per instance; it excludes the one-time data-generation and training costs, which can be substantial.

The PDEBench suite [8] has since systematized this comparison across a wider range of PDEs (including wave propagation, compressible flow, and reaction–diffusion systems), forward and inverse problems, and multiple baseline architectures, providing the community's most comprehensive scoreboard to date.

```haskell
-- The operator-learning pipeline as a typed functional sketch
type Field d = [Double]            -- discretized function on domain d
type Operator a u = Field a -> Field u

branchNet :: Field Sensor -> [Double]   -- u |-> coefficients b_k(u)
trunkNet  :: Coord -> [Double]          -- y |-> basis values t_k(y)

deepONet :: Field Sensor -> Coord -> Double
deepONet u y = sum (zipWith (*) (branchNet u) (trunkNet y))

fourierLayer :: Weights -> Field D -> Field D
fourierLayer (w, r) v =
  pointwiseNonlinear (w |.| v  +  inverseFFT (r .*. fft v))
```

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### What is rigorously known

The theoretical landscape has three pillars:

1. **Chen–Chen (1995)** [4]: universal approximation of continuous operators by the branch–trunk form, with sensors on compact input sets.
2. **Kovachki et al. (2023)** [3]: universal approximation for the general neural-operator architecture (integral kernels plus nonlinearities), plus discretization invariance of the parameterization.
3. **Kovachki, Lanthaler, and Mishra (2021)** [7]: universal approximation *and* error bounds specifically for Fourier neural operators, quantifying how the truncation level and network size control the approximation error for operators of given smoothness.

Together these results put operator learning on footing comparable to classical approximation theory: we know the hypothesis class is rich enough, and we have initial quantitative rates.

### Empirical scaling behavior

Across the reported benchmarks, several robust patterns emerge [2,3,8]:

- **Data scaling.** Test error decreases predictably with the number of training input–output pairs, with FNO typically requiring fewer samples than DeepONet on periodic problems — consistent with the Fourier prior matching the problem structure.
- **Resolution scaling.** Error is approximately resolution-independent once the training grid resolves the input distribution's dominant scales; zero-shot super-resolution to finer grids introduces negligible additional error on smooth problems [2].
- **Mode truncation.** Retaining too few Fourier modes degrades accuracy on problems with fine-scale features; the sweet spot (commonly 12–20 modes per dimension in the benchmarks) balances expressivity against overfitting and cost [2].

### A worked error decomposition

Following the analysis in [7], the total error of a trained FNO can be decomposed conceptually as

$$\|\mathcal{G}^\dagger - \mathcal{G}_\theta\| \;\le\; \underbrace{\|\mathcal{G}^\dagger - \mathcal{G}^\star\|}_{\text{approximation}} \;+\; \underbrace{\|\mathcal{G}^\star - \mathcal{G}_{\hat\theta}\|}_{\text{estimation}} \;+\; \underbrace{\|\mathcal{G}_{\hat\theta} - \mathcal{G}_\theta\|}_{\text{optimization}},$$

where $\mathcal{G}^\star$ is the best-in-class approximant and $\hat\theta$ the empirical risk minimizer. The approximation term is controlled by universality and smoothness-dependent rates [7]; the estimation term by sample complexity, which for operator learning depends on the covering numbers of the input-function distribution; the optimization term is the least understood — though the PINO two-phase procedure [5] can be viewed as a pragmatic attack on exactly this component.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating plainly where these methods fall short.

1. **Non-periodic and complex geometries.** The vanilla FNO assumes a periodic rectangular domain; extensions to arbitrary domains (e.g., geometry-informed variants, graph neural operators) exist but are less mature and often less accurate than the periodic baseline [2,3].
2. **Data cost.** Supervised operator learning needs many high-fidelity PDE solves for training. Physics-informed variants [5,6] mitigate this but introduce harder optimization and have their own failure modes.
3. **No error certificates.** Unlike adaptive finite elements, neural operators provide no a posteriori error estimator; the error-bound theory [7] is asymptotic and not computable in practice. For safety-critical applications this is a serious gap.
4. **Inverse problems and non-smooth operators.** Most theory and benchmarks concern forward solution operators. Inverse maps (e.g., from measurements to coefficients) are often discontinuous or ill-posed, and operator networks struggle accordingly [8].
5. **The curse of parametric dimensionality.** Approximation rates degrade as the effective dimension of the input-function distribution grows; high-dimensional stochastic parameterizations remain challenging despite promising early results.
6. **Training instability at scale.** Very deep operator networks can suffer from the same gradient pathologies as other deep architectures, compounded by the cost of FFTs or large graph message-passing at high resolution.
7. **Speedup accounting.** Reported speedups compare inference against per-instance classical solves and omit data generation and training; the breakeven point depends strongly on the number of downstream queries.

## 7 Conclusion

Neural operators mark a genuine conceptual advance: they move deep learning for PDEs from *function approximation* to *operator approximation*, with architectures — DeepONet's branch–trunk factorization [1] and the Fourier neural operator's spectral kernels [2] — that are disciplined by, rather than merely inspired by, the underlying approximation theory [4,3,7]. Discretization invariance turns a trained model into a reusable asset across resolutions, and physics-informed hybrids [5,6] are beginning to break the dependence on massive labeled datasets.

The honest assessment is that operator learning is now the method of choice for many-query, moderate-accuracy surrogate modeling of PDE families, while classical solvers retain the edge for single-instance, certified, high-accuracy computation. The most important open problems — computable error bounds, robust handling of complex geometries and inverse maps, and a sharper understanding of optimization in the physics-informed regime — are mathematical as much as algorithmic. The next decade of progress will likely come from analysts and architects working in tandem, much as the last decade's did.
