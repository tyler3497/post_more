# Online Convex Optimization and Regret Minimization: Hedge, Follow-the-Regularized-Leader, and the Geometry of Sequential Prediction

## Abstract

Online convex optimization (OCO) provides the dominant theoretical framework for sequential decision-making under adversarial uncertainty, unifying expert advice, online classification, portfolio selection, and large-scale stochastic optimization. This thesis develops the theory from first principles: the prediction-with-expert-advice protocol of Freund and Schapire, whose Hedge algorithm achieves $O(\sqrt{T \log N})$ regret against the best expert in hindsight; Zinkevich's online gradient descent (OGD), the first algorithm attaining the optimal $O(\sqrt{T})$ regret for general convex losses; and the Follow-the-Regularized-Leader (FTRL) and online mirror descent (OMD) frameworks of Shalev-Shwartz, Hazan, and others, which recover Hedge and OGD as instances of entropic and Euclidean regularization. We prove the key regret bounds with full derivations, establish matching $\Omega(\sqrt{T})$ lower bounds, characterize how regularizer choice encodes the geometry of the decision set, and survey adaptive refinements (AdaGrad, decreasing Hedge, parameter-free methods) that remove dependence on prior knowledge of the horizon and loss scales. The synthesis reveals a single organizing principle: regret minimization is stabilized empirical risk minimization, and geometry dictates the constants.

## 1 Introduction

Consider a learner who must act repeatedly in an environment that is, for modeling purposes, *adversarial*. On each round $t = 1, \dots, T$, the learner first commits to a decision $x_t$ from a convex set $\mathcal{K} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$; only then is a convex loss function $f_t : \mathcal{K} \to \mathbb{R}$ revealed, and the learner suffers $f_t(x_t)$. The sequence of losses may be chosen adversarially, even with full knowledge of the learner's strategy, subject only to the convexity and regularity of each $f_t$. There is no statistical assumption, no stationary distribution, no stochastic model: the theory must hold for the *worst case*.

This setting, introduced by Zinkevich [1] as **online convex optimization**, subsumes several classical problems: **prediction with expert advice** [2,3] (OCO over the simplex with linear losses), **online classification** with the hinge loss, **portfolio selection** [4], and **stochastic optimization** — an online algorithm with regret $R_T$ converts via online-to-batch reduction [5] into a batch optimizer with expected suboptimality $R_T/T$.

The canonical performance measure is **regret**:

$$
\mathrm{Reg}_T \triangleq \sum_{t=1}^{T} f_t(x_t) - \min_{x \in \mathcal{K}} \sum_{t=1}^{T} f_t(x).
$$

A learner is *no-regret* if $\mathrm{Reg}_T = o(T)$; per-round regret then vanishes asymptotically. The central quantitative question is the *rate*: how fast can regret grow? The answer for convex Lipschitz losses is $\Theta(\sqrt{T})$: Zinkevich's online gradient descent achieves $O(\sqrt{T})$, and this is optimal [1,6].

This thesis argues that the apparently disparate algorithms — Hedge (multiplicative weights), online gradient descent, mirror descent, dual averaging — are facets of one principle: **Follow-the-Regularized-Leader**.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The OCO Protocol

Formally, online convex optimization is the repeated game:

1. Learner plays $x_t \in \mathcal{K}$ (compact convex, diameter $D$ under norm $\|\cdot\|$).
2. Adversary reveals convex $f_t : \mathcal{K} \to \mathbb{R}$ with $\|\nabla f_t(x)\|_* \le G$ for all $x \in \mathcal{K}$, where $\|\cdot\|_*$ is the dual norm.
3. Learner incurs loss $f_t(x_t)$ and observes (at least) a subgradient $g_t \in \partial f_t(x_t)$.

This is the *full-information* setting — the entire loss function, or at least a subgradient at the played point, is revealed after the fact. It must be distinguished from the bandit setting, where only $f_t(x_t)$ is observed; bandit convex optimization is strictly harder (optimal rates are $T^{2/3}$ or worse in the adversarial case) [7]. Our focus is full information, where the minimax rate $\Theta(\sqrt{T})$ is attainable and tight.

### 2.2 Key Mathematical Tools

Three analytical tools recur throughout regret analysis: **convexity linearization** ($f_t(x_t) - f_t(x^*) \le \langle g_t, x_t - x^*\rangle$, reducing regret to the linear case [1,8]); the **potential/telescoping argument** (bounding per-round regret by a potential difference plus a penalty); and **Bregman divergences**, which generalize squared Euclidean distance and KL divergence for non-Euclidean stability analysis [9].

### 2.3 The Expert-Advice Special Case

In prediction with expert advice [2], the decision set is the simplex $\Delta_N = \{p \ge 0 : \sum_i p_i = 1\}$ and losses are linear: $f_t(p) = \langle \ell_t, p\rangle$ with $\ell_t \in [0,1]^N$. The comparator class is the set of single experts (vertices of the simplex), so

$$
\mathrm{Reg}_T = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \langle \ell_t, p_t\rangle - \min_{i \in [N]} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \ell_t(i).
$$

This special case, though finite-dimensional, exhibits the full richness of the theory and motivated the multiplicative-weights family that would later be recognized as an instance of FTRL with entropy regularization [10].

## 3 Methodology

We proceed by the following program:

1. **Hedge as prototype.** Derive the $O(\sqrt{T \log N})$ regret bound for Hedge via the weight-potential argument, establishing the multiplicative-weights paradigm and its optimality up to constants for the expert setting [2,11].
2. **Online gradient descent.** Prove Zinkevich's $O(DG\sqrt{T})$ bound with a Euclidean telescoping argument [1], and explain why the rate is minimax-optimal.
3. **FTRL and mirror descent.** Present Follow-the-Regularized-Leader and online mirror descent as the unifying frameworks [8,10,12]; show that quadratic regularization yields OGD while entropy regularization over the simplex yields Hedge; give the general FTRL regret bound in terms of regularizer properties.
4. **Geometry and adaptivity.** Explain how the choice of regularizer must match the decision-set geometry, and how adaptive schemes (AdaGrad [13], decreasing learning rates, parameter-free methods [14]) remove the need to know $T$ or $G$ in advance.
5. **Empirics and connections.** Summarize computational evidence and the links to boosting, game solving, and online-to-batch conversion [5].

All regret bounds are worst-case, over *all* adversarial loss sequences consistent with the regularity assumptions.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Hedge and Multiplicative Weights

The **Hedge** algorithm of Freund and Schapire [2] maintains weights $w_{t,i} > 0$ over experts, initialized at $w_{1,i} = 1$, plays $p_{t,i} = w_{t,i}/\sum_j w_{t,j}$, and updates

```python
# Hedge / multiplicative weights, per round t
eta = math.sqrt(math.log(N) / T)          # learning rate
p = w / w.sum()                            # distribution over experts
learner_loss = p @ ell_t                    # expected loss
w = w * np.exp(-eta * ell_t)                # multiplicative update
```

> **Theorem: (Hedge regret bound, Freund–Schapire 1997).** With $\eta = \sqrt{(\ln N)/T}$ and losses in $[0,1]$, Hedge satisfies
>
> $$
> \sum_{t=1}^{T} \langle \ell_t, p_t\rangle - \min_{i} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \ell_t(i) \le 2\sqrt{T \ln N}.
> $$
>
> More precisely, for any fixed $\eta \in (0,1]$,
>
> $$
> \mathrm{Reg}_T \le \frac{\ln N}{\eta} + \eta \sum_{t=1}^{T} \langle \ell_t^2, p_t\rangle \le \frac{\ln N}{\eta} + \eta T.
> $$

> **Proof:** Define the potential $\Phi_t = \sum_i w_{t,i}$ with $w_{t,i} = \exp(-\eta \sum_{s < t} \ell_s(i))$. Then
>
> $$
> \Phi_{t+1} = \sum_i w_{t,i} e^{-\eta \ell_t(i)} \le \sum_i w_{t,i}\left(1 - \eta \ell_t(i) + \tfrac{\eta^2}{2}\ell_t(i)^2\right)
> $$
>
> using $e^{-x} \le 1 - x + x^2/2$ for $x \ge 0$. Dividing by $\Phi_t$ and taking logarithms,
>
> $$
> \ln \Phi_{t+1} - \ln \Phi_t \le -\eta \langle \ell_t, p_t\rangle + \tfrac{\eta^2}{2} \langle \ell_t^2, p_t\rangle.
> $$
>
> Summing over $t$ telescopes the left side to $\ln \Phi_{T+1} - \ln N$. Since $\Phi_{T+1} \ge \max_i w_{T+1,i} = \exp(-\eta \min_i \sum_t \ell_t(i))$, we get $\ln \Phi_{T+1} \ge -\eta \min_i \sum_t \ell_t(i)$. Rearranging and dividing by $\eta$ yields the claimed bound. $\square$

The rate is tight: there is an $\Omega(\sqrt{T \log N})$ lower bound [11], and with $N = 2$ this recovers the general $\Omega(\sqrt{T})$ lower bound for OCO. Second-order refinements (AdaHedge, Squint, Decreasing Hedge [15,16]) remove the need to know $T$ in advance and adapt to easy instances via variance terms.

### 4.2 Online Gradient Descent and the Zinkevich Bound

For general convex $\mathcal{K} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, **online gradient descent** [1] plays $x_{t+1} = \Pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x_t - \eta g_t)$ with $g_t \in \partial f_t(x_t)$:

> **Theorem: (Zinkevich 2003).** Let $\mathcal{K}$ have Euclidean diameter $D$ and each $f_t$ be $G$-Lipschitz. With $\eta = D/(G\sqrt{T})$,
>
> $$
> \mathrm{Reg}_T \le \frac{DG}{2\eta} \cdot \eta^2 \cdot 2 \ldots \quad \text{more precisely} \quad \mathrm{Reg}_T \le DG\sqrt{T}.
> $$

> **Proof:** Fix any comparator $x^* \in \mathcal{K}$ and write $g_t \in \partial f_t(x_t)$. By convexity, $f_t(x_t) - f_t(x^*) \le \langle g_t, x_t - x^*\rangle$. Non-expansiveness of the Euclidean projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}$ gives
>
> $$
> \|x_{t+1} - x^*\|^2 \le \|x_t - \eta g_t - x^*\|^2 = \|x_t - x^*\|^2 - 2\eta \langle g_t, x_t - x^*\rangle + \eta^2 \|g_t\|^2.
> $$
>
> Rearranging,
>
> $$
> \langle g_t, x_t - x^*\rangle \le \frac{\|x_t - x^*\|^2 - \|x_{t+1} - x^*\|^2}{2\eta} + \frac{\eta}{2}\|g_t\|^2.
> $$
>
> Summing over $t = 1, \dots, T$, the first term telescopes: it is at most $D^2/(2\eta)$ since $\|x_1 - x^*\|^2 \le D^2$ and the final term is nonpositive. The second term is at most $\eta T G^2/2$. Setting $\eta = D/(G\sqrt{T})$ balances the two terms, giving $\mathrm{Reg}_T \le DG\sqrt{T}$. $\square$

Two observations. First, the proof *reduces convex losses to linear losses* at the outset; OGD effectively runs on the sequence $\tilde{f}_t(x) = \langle g_t, x\rangle$. Second, the diameter $D$ and gradient bound $G$ enter symmetrically — a harbinger of the general FTRL analysis, where they become the dual-norm diameter and the regularizer's range.

### 4.3 Follow-the-Regularized-Leader: The Unifying Framework

The naive **Follow-the-Leader** (FTL), $x_{t+1} = \arg\min_{x \in \mathcal{K}} \sum_{s=1}^{t} f_s(x)$, is unstable: a small change in the cumulative loss can swing the minimizer across the set, and an adversary can force linear regret. **Follow-the-Regularized-Leader** [8,10] stabilizes FTL by penalizing deviation:

$$
x_{t+1} = \underset{x \in \mathcal{K}}{\arg\min}\; \left\langle \sum_{s=1}^{t} g_s, x \right\rangle + \frac{1}{\eta} R(x),
$$

where $R : \mathcal{K} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a *strongly convex regularizer* and $\eta > 0$ the learning rate. (The "lazy" projection-free form above, with linearized losses, is the version whose analysis is cleanest [8]; the eager/proximal variants are algorithmically equivalent up to the stability terms.)

> **Theorem: (FTRL regret bound).** Let $R$ be $1$-strongly convex with respect to norm $\|\cdot\|$, and let $D_R^2 = \max_{x,y \in \mathcal{K}} (R(x) - R(y))$ be its range. Then FTRL with parameter $\eta$ satisfies
>
> $$
> \mathrm{Reg}_T \le \frac{D_R^2}{\eta} + \frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \|g_t\|_*^2.
> $$

The proof proceeds via the **FTL–BTL lemma** [12]: regret is bounded by the regularizer range plus the sum of "stability" terms $\langle g_t, x_t - x_{t+1}\rangle$, and strong convexity of $R$ converts each stability term into $\tfrac{\eta}{2}\|g_t\|_*^2$ [8,10]. The structure exactly mirrors the OGD proof, with $\|x_t - x^*\|^2$ replaced by the Bregman geometry of $R$.

The unification is now transparent:

| Algorithm | Decision set | Regularizer $R$ | Norm | Regret bound |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| OGD | Euclidean ball / general $\mathcal{K}$ | $\tfrac{1}{2}\\|x\\|_2^2$ | $\ell_2$ | $O(DG\sqrt{T})$ |
| Hedge | Simplex $\Delta_N$ | $\sum_i x_i \ln x_i$ (neg. entropy) | $\ell_1$ | $O(\sqrt{T \log N})$ |
| $p$-norm methods | $\ell_p$ ball | $\tfrac{1}{2}\\|x\\|_p^2$ | $\ell_p$ | $O(\sqrt{T \log n})$–type |

Indeed, FTRL with negative-entropy regularization over $\Delta_N$ yields closed-form updates

$$
x_{t+1,i} = \frac{\exp(-\eta \sum_{s=1}^{t} g_{s,i})}{\sum_j \exp(-\eta \sum_{s=1}^{t} g_{s,j})},
$$

*exactly the Hedge update* [2,10]: entropy is 1-strongly convex w.r.t. $\|\cdot\|_1$ with range $\log N$ over the simplex, so the $\log N$ in Hedge's bound is precisely $D_R^2$ [9].

### 4.4 Mirror Descent and the Geometry of Regularization

**Online mirror descent** (OMD) [9,18] is the "eager" twin of FTRL. Given a mirror map $\nabla\psi$ for a strongly convex $\psi$, each round performs a dual step followed by a Bregman projection:

```haskell
-- Online Mirror Descent (schematic)
-- psi: strongly convex potential; B_psi: Bregman divergence
step :: Vector -> Vector -> Vector
step x_t g_t =
  let y    = gradInv psi (grad psi x_t - eta * g_t)  -- dual (mirror) step
  in  argmin (\x -> bregmanDiv psi x y) feasibleSet  -- Bregman projection
```

McMahan [10] proved the celebrated **equivalence theorem**: FTRL and (lazy) mirror descent produce identical iterates under the same regularizer, and their regret analyses coincide. The distinction is implementational — FTRL re-solves an optimization over the full history each round (often in closed form), while OMD takes a single incremental step. The duality viewpoint underlying this correspondence is developed further in [18].

The deeper message is geometric. Regret bounds always take the form

$$
\mathrm{Reg}_T \lesssim \underbrace{\sqrt{D_R^2 \cdot \sum_t \|g_t\|_*^2}}_{\text{geometry} \times \text{data}},
$$

so the algorithm designer's job is to choose $R$ whose strong convexity matches the norm in which gradients are small and whose range matches the set's size [8,13]. Canonical pairings:

- $\ell_2$ geometry: $R(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|x\|_2^2$, strong convexity modulus $1$ w.r.t. $\ell_2$ — right for Euclidean balls.
- $\ell_1$/simplex geometry: negative entropy, $1$-strongly convex w.r.t. $\ell_1$ — right for the simplex, where $\|g_t\|_\infty \le 1$ but $\|g_t\|_2$ could be $\sqrt{N}$.
- $\ell_\infty$ geometry: $R(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|x\|_p^2$ with $p = 2\ln n$, exploiting $\|x\|_\infty \le \|x\|_p \le e\|x\|_\infty$ — right for the hypercube.

A mismatch is costly: running OGD ($\ell_2$ geometry) on the simplex with $N$ experts yields $O(\sqrt{TN})$ regret, exponentially worse than Hedge's $O(\sqrt{T \log N})$, because the $\ell_2$-diameter of $\Delta_N$ is $O(1)$ while gradients can have $\ell_2$ norm $\sqrt{N}$ [8].

### 4.5 Adaptivity: AdaGrad, Decreasing Rates, and Parameter-Free Learning

The bounds above assume knowledge of $T$ (to tune $\eta$) and of $G$ (to scale it). **Adaptivity** removes these requirements:

**Decreasing learning rates.** $\eta_t = D/(G\sqrt{t})$ achieves $O(DG\sqrt{T})$ without knowing $T$ [1,8]; second-order variants adapt to easy instances [15][16].
- **AdaGrad** [13] adapts *per coordinate*: with $G_t = \sum_{s \le t} g_s g_s^\top$, the update $x_{t+1} = \Pi_{\mathcal{K}}^{G_t^{1/2}}(x_t - \eta G_t^{-1/2} g_t)$ attains regret $O(\sum_i \sqrt{\sum_t g_{t,i}^2})$, dramatically better than $O(DG\sqrt{T})$ when gradients are sparse. The full-matrix variant adapts to arbitrary correlation structure at $O(n^2)$ cost per round.
The **doubling trick** restarts the algorithm on geometrically growing epochs, preserving the asymptotic rate without knowing $T$ [6].
**Parameter-free methods** [14] achieve $O(\|u\|\sqrt{T \log(1 + \|u\|T)})$ regret against *every* comparator $u$ simultaneously, with no norm-bound knowledge.

These refinements keep the minimax rate while making algorithms genuinely deployable: modern deep-learning optimizers (Adam, AdamW) are direct descendants of the AdaGrad/FTRL lineage [13].

### 5.2 The Lower Bound Argument

> **Theorem: (Minimax lower bound).** For any (possibly randomized) online algorithm, there exists a sequence of convex $G$-Lipschitz losses on a set of diameter $D$ with expected regret $\Omega(DG\sqrt{T})$ [1,6].

> **Proof:** (Sketch.) Take $\mathcal{K} = [-D/2, D/2] \subset \mathbb{R}$ and i.i.d. Rademacher signs $\sigma_t \in \{\pm 1\}$, with $f_t(x) = G\sigma_t x$. For any deterministic algorithm, $\mathbb{E}[\sum_t f_t(x_t)] = 0$ by symmetry, while $\min_{x \in \mathcal{K}} \sum_t f_t(x) = -\tfrac{DG}{2}|\sum_t \sigma_t|$. By the Khintchine inequality, $\mathbb{E}|\sum_t \sigma_t| \ge \sqrt{T/2}$, giving expected regret $\Omega(DG\sqrt{T})$. Yao's minimax principle extends this to randomized algorithms. $\square$

The matching upper and lower bounds close the fundamental question for convex Lipschitz OCO: $\Theta(\sqrt{T})$ is the minimax rate, and OGD/FTRL attain it.

### 5.3 Computational Footprint

A practical virtue of these algorithms is their per-round cost: OGD needs one gradient evaluation and one projection; Hedge needs $O(N)$ weight updates; entropic FTRL over the simplex is closed-form. The online-to-batch conversion [5] turns any such algorithm into a stochastic optimizer: running OGD on i.i.d. losses and averaging iterates yields $\mathbb{E}[f(\bar{x}_T)] - \min f \le O(DG/\sqrt{T})$, recovering the optimal rate of stochastic gradient descent.

### 5.4 Connections: Boosting, Games, and Beyond

- **Boosting.** The multiplicative-weights analysis of Hedge is the analytic core of AdaBoost: Freund and Schapire's boosting algorithm *is* Hedge applied to the zero-sum game between the booster and the weak learner [3]. The $O(\sqrt{T \log N})$ bound becomes a margin guarantee.
- **Game solving.** In a zero-sum game with payoff matrix $A$, if both players run no-regret algorithms, their average strategies converge to Nash equilibrium at rate $O(1/\sqrt{T})$ [12]. Optimistic mirror descent [19] accelerates this to $O(1/T)$ in the self-play setting, underpinning modern equilibrium solvers.
- **Portfolio theory.** Cover's universal portfolio [4] is the expert-advice problem with logarithmic loss; the $O(\sqrt{T \log N})$ analysis yields wealth guarantees competitive with the best constantly-rebalanced portfolio.

## 6 Limitations

The theory, for all its elegance, has sharp boundaries:

1. **Convexity is load-bearing.** Every upper bound here uses $f_t(x_t) - f_t(x^*) \le \langle g_t, x_t - x^*\rangle$. For non-convex losses, sublinear regret is impossible in general; one must retreat to convex surrogates, local regret notions, or randomization [7].
2. **Full information is assumed.** The $\Theta(\sqrt{T})$ rate collapses under bandit feedback, where the learner observes only $f_t(x_t)$: the optimal adversarial rate is $\tilde{\Theta}(T^{2/3})$ for general convex losses (and $\tilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{T})$ only for special cases like linear losses via Exp3-style algorithms) [7].
3. **Static regret is weak.** Competing with the best *fixed* comparator in hindsight is a low bar in non-stationary environments. *Dynamic regret* (against time-varying comparators) and *adaptive regret* (against the best comparator on every interval) are stronger notions with their own algorithmic machinery [20].
4. **Constants matter in practice.** The minimax rate hides constants that depend on geometry; a poorly chosen regularizer yields vacuous bounds even when the rate is "optimal." Parameter-free and adaptive methods mitigate but do not eliminate this brittleness.
5. **Computational cost of projections.** OGD's Euclidean projection onto a general convex $\mathcal{K}$ can be expensive; projection-free alternatives (e.g., Frank–Wolfe-style online algorithms) trade regret for cheaper iterations.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Online convex optimization distills sequential prediction to its essence: *stability*. Follow-the-Leader fails because it is unstable; every successful algorithm — Hedge, OGD, mirror descent, FTRL, AdaGrad — is FTL plus a stabilizing penalty, and the penalty's geometry determines the regret bound's constants. Zinkevich's $O(\sqrt{T})$ bound [1] and its matching lower bound settle the minimax rate; the FTRL/mirror-descent unification [8,10] shows that the choice of algorithm is really a choice of geometry; and the adaptive refinements [13,14,15,16] make the theory practical.

The framework's reach — from boosting [3] to game solving [19] to stochastic optimization [5] — suggests that regret minimization is one of learning theory's organizing principles. Open frontiers include optimal dynamic regret, bandit convex optimization, and the non-convex extensions that modern deep learning demands. But the classical picture presented here, two decades after Zinkevich, remains the foundation on which all of it is built.
