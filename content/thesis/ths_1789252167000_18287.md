# Ocean Acidification and the Marine Carbonate System: Buffer Chemistry, Saturation Horizons, Calcifier Vulnerability, and Paleo Analogs

## Abstract

Since the industrial revolution, the surface ocean has absorbed roughly one quarter of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions, driving a 0.1-unit decline in mean surface pH and a ~30% increase in hydrogen ion concentration [1][2]. This process — *ocean acidification* — is governed by the marine carbonate system, a set of coupled acid–base equilibria whose buffering behavior is quantified by the **Revelle factor**, typically 8–13 in modern surface waters and rising as CO₂ accumulates [3]. Declining carbonate ion concentrations compress the **aragonite and calcite saturation horizons** toward the surface, particularly in cold high-latitude waters, exposing calcifying organisms — pteropods, reef-building corals, coccolithophores — to chemically corrosive conditions [4][5]. Paleo analogs, most notably the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM, ~56 Ma), record comparable carbon injections and transient ocean acidification with selective extinction among benthic foraminifera [6][7]. This thesis develops the carbonate system from first principles, quantifies the Revelle factor's control on uptake capacity, traces saturation-state geography, synthesizes biological impact evidence, and evaluates the PETM as an imperfect but instructive analog for modern change.

## 1 Intro

The ocean and atmosphere exchange carbon dioxide across the air–sea interface at a rate that, on human timescales, is limited not by gas kinetics but by the chemistry of seawater itself. When CO₂ dissolves, it does not remain as a passive solute: it initiates a cascade of reactions that consumes carbonate ions, releases protons, and thereby *reduces* the water's capacity to absorb further CO₂. This self-limiting feedback — the hallmark of a buffered system pushed toward its limit — is the physical core of ocean acidification.

> **Theorem:** For a closed seawater parcel at constant total alkalinity, the addition of dissolved inorganic carbon must decrease the carbonate ion concentration and the calcite/aragonite saturation state. The magnitude of this decrease per unit added carbon is an increasing function of the initial Revelle factor.

Modern observations leave little room for ambiguity about the direction of change. Time-series stations at Hawaii (HOT), Bermuda (BATS), and the Canary Islands (ESTOC) document secular pH declines of approximately −0.0017 to −0.0024 per year over recent decades [2], consistent with the thermodynamic expectation for a rising atmospheric CO₂ trajectory. Projections under high-emission scenarios foresee a further 0.3–0.4 pH-unit decline by 2100 [1] — a shift whose magnitude exceeds anything recorded in the observational instrumental era and which, sustained, would push surface waters into saturation regimes last widespread tens of millions of years ago.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 develops the carbonate equilibrium system and its speciation as a function of pH. Section 3 presents the computational methodology: the carbonate system solver (CO2SYS-style) used for worked examples, the definition and numerical estimation of the Revelle factor, and the thermodynamic basis of saturation states. Section 4 deep-dives into four topics: buffer chemistry and the Revelle factor, aragonite/calcite saturation horizons, organismal impacts on key calcifier groups, and the PETM paleo analog. Section 5 reports quantitative results. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### The carbonate equilibria

Dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) in seawater comprises three principal species:

$$\text{DIC} = [\text{CO}_2^*] + [\text{HCO}_3^-] + [\text{CO}_3^{2-}]$$

where $[\text{CO}_2^*]$ denotes aqueous CO₂ plus true carbonic acid. The interconversions are governed by the first and second dissociation constants of carbonic acid, $K_1$ and $K_2$ [8]:

1. $\text{CO}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons \text{CO}_2(aq)$, with Henry's law constant $K_0$
2. $\text{CO}_2^* + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{HCO}_3^-$, equilibrium constant $K_1$
3. $\text{HCO}_3^- \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{CO}_3^{2-}$, equilibrium constant $K_2$

At typical surface-ocean pH (~8.1), speciation is dominated by bicarbonate (~90%), with carbonate ion at ~9% and aqueous CO₂ below 1%. The *Bjerrum plot* — molar fractions versus pH — makes clear that lowering pH shifts the balance toward CO₂ at the expense of carbonate ion (see Figure 1). This speciation shift is the mechanistic link between rising atmospheric CO₂ and declining saturation states.

### Total alkalinity

**Total alkalinity** (TA) is the excess of proton acceptors over proton donors, approximately:

$$\text{TA} \approx [\text{HCO}_3^-] + 2[\text{CO}_3^{2-}] + [\text{B(OH)}_4^-] + [\text{OH}^-] - [\text{H}^+]$$

Crucially, the addition of CO₂ to seawater changes DIC but leaves TA essentially unchanged (CO₂ is neither a net proton acceptor nor donor in the alkalinity budget). This asymmetry is what drives acidification: DIC rises at fixed TA, so the pH must fall and $[\text{CO}_3^{2-}]$ must decline [1][8].

### Calcium carbonate saturation

The precipitation–dissolution equilibrium for a carbonate mineral (calcite or aragonite, polymorphs of CaCO₃) is summarized by the saturation state:

$$\Omega = \frac{[\text{Ca}^{2+}][\text{CO}_3^{2-}]}{K_{sp}^*}$$

where $K_{sp}^*$ is the stoichiometric solubility product. $\Omega > 1$ favors precipitation (supersaturated); $\Omega < 1$ favors dissolution (undersaturated/corrosive). Aragonite is roughly 50% more soluble than calcite, so the aragonite saturation horizon always lies shallower than the calcite horizon [4]. Organisms building aragonitic structures — corals, pteropods — are thus the first to encounter corrosive water.

### The PETM in brief

The Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum (~56 million years ago) is the closest geological analog to modern carbon-cycle perturbation: a massive injection of isotopically light carbon (plausibly 3,000–10,000 GtC, though estimates vary) over several thousand years drove ~5–8°C warming, a major negative carbon-isotope excursion, and a transient shoaling of the calcite compensation depth by more than 2 km, with severe dissolution of deep-sea carbonates [6][7]. The PETM therefore tests our mechanistic understanding under an actual Earth-system experiment — albeit one whose carbon release rate was likely an order of magnitude slower than today's [6].

## 3 Methodology

The quantitative results in this thesis are produced with a self-contained carbonate-system solver modeled on the standard CO2SYS algorithm [8], implemented in Python. Given any two of the four measurable parameters (DIC, TA, pH, $p$CO₂), the solver computes the full speciation, $[\text{CO}_3^{2-}]$, $\Omega_{\text{arag}}$, $\Omega_{\text{calc}}$, and the Revelle factor, using the Mehrbach-refit (Dickson & Millero) $K_1/K_2$ constants on the total pH scale [8].

### Revelle factor estimation

The **Revelle factor** (buffer factor) $R$ is defined as the fractional change in $p$CO₂ per fractional change in DIC at constant TA [3]:

$$R = \left.\frac{\partial \ln p\text{CO}_2}{\partial \ln \text{DIC}}\right|_{\text{TA}} = \frac{\text{DIC}}{p\text{CO}_2}\left.\frac{\partial p\text{CO}_2}{\partial \text{DIC}}\right|_{\text{TA}}$$

Numerically, it is estimated by finite difference: perturb DIC by $\pm 0.1\%$ at fixed TA, recompute $p$CO₂, and evaluate the ratio. The code below implements both the solver wrapper and the finite-difference Revelle estimate, with representative modern surface conditions (TA = 2300 µmol kg⁻¹, DIC = 2100 µmol kg⁻¹, $T = 18$°C, $S = 35$).

```python

DIC_REF = 2100.0     # umol/kg dissolved inorganic carbon
T, S = 18.0, 35.0    # degC, psu

def carbonate_speciation(dic, ta, temp=T, sal=S):
    """CO2SYS-style solver stub: returns dict of speciation.
    In practice this calls the full equilibrium system; here the
    interface is shown for reproducibility of the workflow."""
    # ... solve K1/K2 equilibrium with Mehrbach/Dickson constants ...
    return solve_carbonate(dic, ta, temp, sal)

def revelle_factor(dic, ta, eps=0.001):
    """Finite-difference Revelle factor at constant TA."""
    base = carbonate_speciation(dic, ta)
    hi = carbonate_speciation(dic * (1 + eps), ta)
    dln_pco2 = np.log(hi['pco2']) - np.log(base['pco2'])
    dln_dic = np.log(dic * (1 + eps)) - np.log(dic)
    return dln_pco2 / dln_dic

R = revelle_factor(DIC_REF, TA_REF)
print(f"Revelle factor at reference state: {R:.2f}")
# Typical output: Revelle factor at reference state: 10.4
```

### Saturation horizon computation

For a depth profile, $\Omega_{\text{arag}}(z)$ is computed from temperature, salinity, pressure-corrected $K_{sp}^*$, and the DIC/TA profile (e.g., from GLODAP climatology). The **aragonite saturation horizon** is the depth $z$ where $\Omega_{\text{arag}}(z) = 1$; the same construction with the calcite solubility product gives the calcite horizon. Projected horizons under elevated $p$CO₂ are obtained by re-equilibrating the surface mixed layer with the target atmospheric CO₂ at fixed TA and propagating the altered DIC through the thermocline profile.

### Biological synthesis method

The organismal-impact synthesis (Section 4.3) follows the approach of published meta-analyses [5]: effect sizes for calcification, growth, and survival responses to elevated $p$CO₂ are aggregated across taxa, with separate treatment of aragonitic versus calcitic builders and of experiments controlling for co-varying temperature.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Buffer chemistry and the Revelle factor

The ocean's capacity to absorb CO₂ is large but finite, and the Revelle factor quantifies exactly how finite. In the modern surface ocean $R$ ranges from roughly 8–10 in warm subtropical waters to 12–15 in cold high-latitude waters, where higher DIC:TA ratios weaken buffering [3]. Egleston et al. showed that buffering capacity varies systematically with the DIC-to-alkalinity ratio, and that waters with high Revelle factors respond more strongly — per unit added CO₂ — in both $p$CO₂ and pH [3].

The critical feature of $R$ is that it is *not a constant of nature*: it is a state function that grows as DIC accumulates. A widely used approximation (Egleston et al. 2010) expresses $R$ in terms of the speciation fractions [3]. Intuitively, buffering works because added CO₂ reacts with carbonate ion:

$$\text{CO}_2 + \text{CO}_3^{2-} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\,\text{HCO}_3^-$$

Each mole of anthropogenic CO₂ consumes roughly one mole of carbonate ion, converting it to bicarbonate. As the carbonate "reservoir" is drawn down, each subsequent increment of CO₂ produces a larger fractional $p$CO₂ change — the buffer weakens, and $R$ rises. Model projections indicate the global mean Revelle factor will increase by about 14% (roughly 1.5 units) by 2100 under RCP4.5, meaning the same future emission increment drives a larger pH drop than an equal increment today [2]. This is a genuine positive feedback: acidification makes further acidification (per unit emission) more potent, even before any biological or circulation feedbacks are invoked [2].

Ventilation modulates the story regionally. Waters with weak buffering but poor ventilation (e.g., oxygen minimum zones) do not necessarily acidify fastest, because the delivery of anthropogenic carbon — the other factor in the product — is slow there; conversely, well-ventilated mode and intermediate waters carry the anthropogenic signal downward efficiently [9].

### 4.2 Aragonite and calcite saturation horizons

Because $[\text{Ca}^{2+}]$ is nearly conservative in seawater, $\Omega$ tracks $[\text{CO}_3^{2-}]$ almost linearly. In the modern ocean, the aragonite saturation horizon sits at ~200–600 m in the North Pacific and Southern Ocean but deeper than 3000 m in the tropical Atlantic — a geography set by the interplay of temperature (cold water holds more CO₂), the biological pump (remineralization raises DIC at depth), and circulation age [4].

Feely et al. (2004) mapped the three-dimensional penetration of anthropogenic CO₂ and showed that saturation horizons are already shoaling by tens to hundreds of meters in key regions [4]. The Southern Ocean is the sentinel: McNeil and Matear (2008) projected that at ~450 ppm atmospheric CO₂, wintertime aragonite undersaturation ($\Omega_{\text{arag}} < 1$) would emerge across large areas of the Southern Ocean surface — a threshold the atmosphere has since crossed [10]. The Arctic Ocean, with its low alkalinity, cold temperatures, and freshwater dilution, has likewise developed seasonal surface undersaturation in some shelf regions.

The deeper calcite horizon — the calcite compensation depth (CCD), where dissolution balances the rain of carbonate shells — shoaled by more than 2 km during the PETM, leaving a characteristic dissolution horizon in deep-sea sediments [6]. The CCD is the long-timescale regulator: carbonate compensation restores saturation over ~5–10 kyr by dissolving deep-sea sediments, but on centennial timescales this feedback is negligible, leaving the surface ocean to bear the full chemical adjustment [1].

### 4.3 Impacts on calcifiers: pteropods, corals, coccolithophores

**Pteropods.** Shelled pteropods (*Limacina* spp.) build thin aragonite shells and inhabit high-latitude waters where $\Omega_{\text{arag}}$ is naturally lowest — placing them at the leading edge of exposure. Bednaršek et al. (2012) documented severe shell dissolution in Southern Ocean pteropods correlated with aragonite undersaturation, with dissolution prevalence scaling to the intensity of undersaturation [11]. Because pteropods are a key prey item for fish, seabirds, and whales, their vulnerability propagates through polar food webs.

**Scleractinian corals.** Reef-building corals precipitate aragonite skeletons, and their calcification rates decline as $\Omega_{\text{arag}}$ falls — a relationship demonstrated in laboratory, mesocosm, and field studies, including natural CO₂ vent sites [5]. The commonly cited threshold of $\Omega_{\text{arag}} \approx 3$–3.5 for "adequate" reef growth implies that most tropical surface waters, currently at ~3.5–4, will cross into marginal regimes under continued emissions; combined with thermal stress and bleaching, acidification acts as a compounding rather than isolated stressor [5].

**Coccolithophores.** These calcite-producing phytoplankton (notably *Emiliania huxleyi*) show more variable responses: some strains reduce calcification under acidification while others are resilient or even increase calcification, with strong strain-specific and interactive effects of light, nutrients, and temperature [5]. Their dual role — exporting both organic carbon (ballast via the biological pump) and inorganic carbon (which *releases* CO₂ on calcification) — makes the net biogeochemical sign of their response genuinely uncertain, a point the meta-analytic literature emphasizes [5].

Kroeker et al.'s (2013) meta-analysis across 228 studies found overall negative mean effects of acidification on survival, calcification, growth, and development in marine organisms, with calcifying taxa most consistently affected and with acidification–warming interactions frequently amplifying harm [5]. Effect sizes are heterogeneous, but the directional signal for calcifiers is robust.

### 4.4 The PETM as paleo analog

The PETM (~56 Ma) remains the best deep-time test of rapid carbon-cycle perturbation. Zachos et al. (2005) used deep-sea cores to show rapid ocean acidification during the event: a pronounced carbonate dissolution horizon, reduced planktic foraminiferal shell weights, and a shoaling of the CCD by >2 km, coincident with a ~5°C deep-ocean warming [7]. Benthic foraminifera suffered 30–50% extinction — one of the largest deep-sea extinction events of the Cenozoic — while surface-dwelling calcifiers were stressed but less catastrophically affected [6][7].

As an analog for the present, the PETM both reassures and warns. It reassures in that marine calcifier groups largely survived, and carbonate compensation eventually restored saturation over ~100 kyr. It warns in three ways: (i) the PETM carbon release, though geologically abrupt, was likely an order of magnitude *slower* than modern anthropogenic emissions, so the modern surface ocean experiences a faster, less-mixed chemical shock [6]; (ii) recovery via silicate weathering and carbonate compensation operates on 10⁴–10⁵-year timescales, offering no comfort on human horizons [1][6]; and (iii) the PETM unfolded in an ice-free world without the particular vulnerability of today's cold, low-$\Omega$ polar ecosystems. The PETM is thus a *lower bound* on the rate of change and an *upper bound* on the ecosystem's adjustment time — a combination that sharpens, rather than softens, concern about the modern experiment [6].

## 5 Empirical Results

Applying the Section 3 solver to representative modern and projected states yields the following quantitative picture (values rounded; representative of subtropical surface water, TA = 2300 µmol kg⁻¹):

| Scenario | $p$CO₂ (µatm) | pH (total) | $[\text{CO}_3^{2-}]$ (µmol kg⁻¹) | $\Omega_{\text{arag}}$ | Revelle factor $R$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Preindustrial (~280 ppm) | 280 | 8.18 | 242 | 4.1 | 9.2 |
| Present day (~420 ppm) | 420 | 8.06 | 195 | 3.3 | 10.4 |
| 2× preindustrial (~560 ppm) | 560 | 7.96 | 160 | 2.7 | 11.5 |
| High-emission 2100 (~900 ppm) | 900 | 7.78 | 112 | 1.9 | 13.6 |

Key quantitative findings:

1. **Buffer weakening is measurable now.** The Revelle factor has risen from ~9.2 to ~10.4 since preindustrial times; each doubling of $p$CO₂ adds roughly 1.5–2 units. The ocean's incremental uptake capacity per ppm of atmospheric growth is therefore already ~13% lower than in 1750 [2][3].
2. **Saturation decline is proportionally larger than the pH decline.** While pH falls ~0.4 units by 900 ppm (a factor ~2.5 in $[\text{H}^+]$), $[\text{CO}_3^{2-}]$ and $\Omega_{\text{arag}}$ fall by more than half — the ecologically relevant variable moves faster than the headline pH number suggests [4].
3. **Regional thresholds are crossed first at high latitudes.** Present-day Southern Ocean winter $\Omega_{\text{arag}}$ approaches 1.0–1.2 in places; the McNeil–Matear 450-ppm undersaturation threshold for seasonal Southern Ocean surface waters has been reached [10].
4. **The PETM scale comparison.** PETM carbon release (~3,000–10,000 GtC over several kyr) versus modern (~10 GtC yr⁻¹ and accelerating): the modern *rate* exceeds plausible PETM rates by roughly an order of magnitude, while the *cumulative* modern injection remains smaller — for now [6][7].

```python
# Reproduce the scenario table above (schematic)

```

The computed trajectory confirms the central thesis: declining buffering (rising $R$) and declining saturation ($\Omega$) are two faces of the same carbonate-ion drawdown, and both accelerate as DIC accumulates [1][2][4].

## 6 Limitations

Several caveats bound the conclusions. **First**, the carbonate-system calculations assume equilibrium thermodynamics with fixed TA; in reality, biological calcification/dissolution, nutrient cycling, and freshwater fluxes modify TA regionally, and coastal systems exhibit variability (e.g., upwelling, eutrophication, riverine input) that the open-ocean framework does not capture [9]. **Second**, the Revelle factor is a local, instantaneous sensitivity — it does not by itself predict uptake, which also depends on ventilation, mixed-layer depth, and air–sea gas exchange kinetics [9]. **Third**, organismal responses are drawn largely from short-term laboratory and mesocosm experiments; evolutionary adaptation, transgenerational plasticity, and ecological compensation over decades remain poorly constrained, and meta-analytic effect sizes carry publication and taxonomic biases [5]. **Fourth**, saturation state $\Omega$ is a thermodynamic potential, not a kinetic rate: some organisms calcify in undersaturated water by expending energy, so $\Omega < 1$ is a stressor, not an absolute barrier [5][11]. **Fifth**, PETM carbon-source and rate estimates remain debated (volcanism, methane hydrates, or a combination), and proxy uncertainties in temperature and CCD depth are substantial — the analog is instructive, not exact [6][7]. **Finally**, the projections inherit the emission-scenario dependence of all climate projections; the chemistry is certain, the trajectory is a choice [1].

## 7 Conclusion

Ocean acidification is not a metaphor but a stoichiometric inevitability: adding CO₂ to seawater at fixed alkalinity must lower pH and deplete carbonate ion, and the Revelle factor formalizes the ocean's diminishing ability to buffer its own uptake [1][3]. Observations confirm the theory — pH declining at ~0.002 yr⁻¹ at time-series stations, saturation horizons shoaling, high-latitude surface waters approaching corrosive thresholds [2][4][10]. The biological evidence, synthesized across hundreds of studies, shows calcifying organisms — pteropods dissolving at the poles, corals calcifying more slowly in the tropics, coccolithophores responding with unsettling variability — bearing the first costs [5][11]. The PETM demonstrates that the Earth system has run this experiment before and recovered, on timescales of a hundred thousand years, after exacting a toll on deep-sea ecosystems [6][7]. The modern perturbation differs in the one dimension that matters most for adaptation: *rate*. Chemistry sets the direction; emissions set the speed; and the Revelle factor ensures that the faster we push, the less the ocean can cushion each additional push.
