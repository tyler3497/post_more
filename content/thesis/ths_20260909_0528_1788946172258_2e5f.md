# Zero-Knowledge Virtual Machines: RISC-V Arithmetization, STARK-Based Execution Proofs, and Recursive Continuations

## Abstract

Zero-knowledge virtual machines reduce verifiable computation to one reusable primitive: proving that an instruction-execution *trace* satisfies a fixed ISA's transition function. This thesis analyzes the modern zkVM design space, centered on RISC-V, through algebraic intermediate representations (AIR), STARK-based execution proofs, and recursive proof composition. We give a rigorous account of trace arithmetization — register files, memory consistency via permutation arguments, range checks via lookups, control-flow decoding — and compare three architecturally distinct systems: **RISC Zero** (randomized AIR with preprocessing, DEEP-ALI, FRI over BabyBear [2]), **SP1** (multi-table "chip" arithmetization over Plonky3 with LogUp cross-table lookups [3]), and **Cairo** (register-machine AIR with 69+ components under Circle STARKs on M31 [4]) — alongside the lookup-singularity paradigm of **Jolt/Lasso** [5]. We formalize *continuations*: segmenting long executions into independently provable chunks joined by recursion, and analyze precompiles, verifier costs, and soundness. Drawing on published benchmarks, we identify the asymptotic and engineering barriers separating today's zkVMs from real-time, universally deployable verifiable computation.

## 1 Introduction

A *zero-knowledge virtual machine* certifies the correct execution of an arbitrary program for a fixed virtual ISA, producing a succinct proof verifiable in milliseconds without re-execution. The move is one of indirection: instead of hand-crafting a circuit per computation, developers compile ordinary code (Rust, C, even EVM interpreters) to a guest ISA, and one ISA-specialized backend verifies *any* program by checking a single universal relation — the machine's one-step transition function [6].

An execution of $T$ cycles yields the trace $(s_0, \ldots, s_T)$ with $s_{t+1} = \delta(s_t, \iota_t)$. The proving statement: there exists a trace with $s_0 = \mathsf{init}(\text{program}, \text{public input})$, every adjacent pair satisfying a polynomial transition relation $C(s_t, s_{t+1}) = 0$, and the final state committing to the claimed output.

Two facts make this scalable. First, $\delta$ is fixed, so its arithmetization cost is amortized across all programs: the burden shifts from circuit design to ISA design. Second, the transition relation is *local* — each constraint touches a constant number of consecutive rows — exactly the structure Algebraic Intermediate Representations exploit.

The central questions: (1) **Arithmetization** — how a RISC-V ISA becomes polynomial constraints, and the cost of monolithic vs. multi-table encodings; (2) **Proving** — how STARK systems (FRI + DEEP-ALI, Circle STARKs, multilinear Hypercube) turn traces into succinct arguments; (3) **Continuations** — proving executions longer than one trace via segments and recursive aggregation while preserving global constraints like memory consistency; (4) **Verifier economics** — what recursion, precompiles, and SNARK wrapping imply for on-chain cost and latency.

We contribute: (i) a unified formal treatment of zkVM arithmetization (AIR, chip, lookup-singularity); (ii) a comparative analysis of RISC Zero, SP1, Cairo/Stwo, Jolt, and Nexus from published specifications and benchmarks [2][3][4][5][7][8]; (iii) a soundness-and-cost model of recursive continuations; (iv) an empirical synthesis of published benchmarks with methodological critique.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From circuits to virtual machines

Early verifiable-computation systems (Pinocchio, libsnark) required per-program circuits. *Programmable* SNARKs (TinyRAM, vnTinyRAM) introduced universal machine circuits but suffered large per-step overhead and trusted setups. The modern zkVM revives the universal-machine idea with transparent, plausibly post-quantum STARK backends and ISAs chosen for arithmetization friendliness — above all **RISC-V (rv32im)**, whose regular encoding admits efficient constraints and which enjoys mature toolchains (rustc, LLVM) emitting ELF binaries the prover consumes directly.

### 2.2 STARKs in one paragraph

A STARK proves that an execution trace satisfies an AIR: polynomial constraints over consecutive rows of a trace matrix [9]. The prover interpolates each trace column into a polynomial, evaluates it over an expanded domain (blowup factor, typically 4–16×), commits via Merkle trees, and proves low-degreeness with **FRI**. Constraint satisfaction is folded through **DEEP-ALI** into a single composed polynomial evaluated at a random out-of-domain point. Security rests on collision-resistant hashing; there is no trusted setup, and the conjectured hardness is hash-based, giving plausible post-quantum security.

### 2.3 Fields, hashes, and the small-field revolution

Classical STARKs worked over 252-bit fields. Modern zkVMs moved to *small* fields — RISC Zero's **BabyBear** (31-bit, $2^{27}\cdot 15 + 1$) [2], Plonky3's BabyBear/KoalaBear/Mersenne-31 family, StarkWare's **M31** ($2^{31}-1$) for Circle STARKs [4] — because 32-bit field arithmetic maps to native CPU/SIMD instructions, dramatically accelerating the prover. Extension fields restore ~100-bit security where needed; hash functions moved with them (**Poseidon2**, **Blake3**) for small-field efficiency.

### 2.4 Lookups: Plookup, LogUp, and Lasso

Range checks and "non-arithmetic" operations (bitwise ops, byte decomposition) are expensive as raw polynomial constraints but cheap as *lookups*: prove that trace values appear in a fixed table. **Plookup** [10] uses a permutation argument; **LogUp** replaces it with a logarithmic-derivative sum that composes across many differently-sized tables — the enabler of SP1's multi-chip design [3]. **Lasso** [5] supports lookups into *structured* tables of size up to $2^{128}$ that are never materialized, decomposing one large lookup into several small ones. **Jolt** pushes this to the *lookup singularity*: proving RISC-V execution almost entirely through lookups (~60 constraints, 80 field elements per step [11]).

## 3 Methodology

Our method is comparative-analytic. From the published specifications and engineering documentation of five systems — RISC Zero [2], SP1 [3][8], Cairo/Stwo [4], Lasso/Jolt [5][11], and Nexus [7] — we extract a common formal model, then instantiate it per system.

### 3.1 The trace model

We model a zkVM execution as a matrix $T \in \mathbb{F}^{n \times w}$: $n$ rows (cycles, padded to a power of two) and $w$ columns carrying architectural state — program counter, decoded opcode fields, register indices/values, memory address/value/timestamp — plus auxiliary columns for permutation arguments and lookups. A *constraint* is a polynomial $C_j$ over a constant-width row window (rows $t$, $t+1$) vanishing on the trace domain:

$$C_j(T[t], T[t+1]) = 0 \quad \forall\, t \in \{0, \ldots, n-2\}, \;\; \forall\, j.$$

Boundary constraints fix $T[1]$ (initial state, program/memory commitments) and $T[n-1]$ (halt, output commitment). Memory consistency — a read returns the last written value — is non-local; all production systems enforce it with a *permutation argument*: operations are sorted by (address, timestamp) into an auxiliary trace, and a grand-product (or log-derivative) accumulator proves multiset equality [2][3].

### 3.2 Arithmetization styles

We distinguish three styles:

| Style | Trace organization | Constraint expression | Exemplars |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Monolithic AIR** | One wide table; every cycle has every column | Single constraint set, selectors per opcode | RISC Zero v1/v2 [2] |
| **Multi-table / chip** | Per-operation tables (ALU, memory, keccak, …) linked by lookups | Local constraints + cross-table LogUp | SP1 [3], Cairo/Stwo [4] |
| **Lookup-singularity** | Minimal CPU circuit; instruction semantics via lookups | ~60 constraints/step | Jolt [5][11], Nexus 2.0 [7] |

The monolithic style pays for *all* columns in *every* row; the chip style sizes each table to usage at the cost of cross-table argument complexity; the lookup style minimizes per-step constraints but shifts cost into the lookup argument and commitment scheme.

### 3.3 Continuations and recursion

No single trace can hold an unbounded execution: FRI proving time and memory grow superlinearly in $n$. All systems therefore *segment* execution — RISC Zero's *segments*/*continuations*, SP1's *shards* (~$2^{22}$ cycles) [3][8] — prove each segment independently (embarrassingly parallel), and aggregate recursively: each recursion step verifies the previous segment's proof inside the zkVM or a dedicated circuit, chaining public outputs (register file, memory Merkle root, cycle count). The final layer is typically wrapped in Groth16/Plonk for cheap on-chain verification.

### 3.4 Evaluation criteria

We evaluate on: (i) **prover time per cycle**; (ii) **proof size and verifier time**; (iii) **ISA expressiveness and precompile support**; (iv) **transparency**; (v) **recursion architecture** and overhead; (vi) **soundness** (conjectured bits of security). Benchmarks are vendor-published [3][8]; we treat them as indicative (see §6).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Arithmetizing RISC-V: from opcode to constraint

RISC-V's appeal is regularity: 32-bit fixed-width instructions, six formats (R/I/S/B/U/J), a small base opcode space. Arithmetizing one cycle proceeds in four stages.

1. **Fetch and decode.** The PC selects an instruction word from the committed program image. Decoding uses *selector polynomials*: for each opcode $o$, a binary selector $s_o(t)$ marks the active instruction, with $\sum_o s_o = 1$, $s_o^2 = s_o$. Bit decomposition of the 32-bit word uses range-checked limbs, e.g. $w = \sum_{i=0}^{3} b_i 2^{8i}$ with each $b_i$ lookup-constrained to $[0,255]$.
2. **Operand resolution.** Register indices select values from register-file columns; x0-is-zero becomes $x_0(t) = 0$.
3. **Execute.** Each opcode imposes its semantic constraint, gated by its selector. Addition is one constraint ($s_{\text{ADD}}\cdot(rd - rs_1 - rs_2) = 0$); shifts and comparisons need bit decompositions. This per-opcode cost — constraint-degree × column width — is why bitwise-heavy programs (SHA-256, Keccak) are expensive without precompiles.
4. **Writeback and PC update.** The destination column is constrained to the computed value (or held constant via a no-write selector); $pc(t+1)$ is constrained to $pc(t)+4$, a branch target, or a jump target.

```rust
// Schematic: one AIR transition constraint for ADD (BabyBear field)
// s_add: selector, rs1/rs2/rd: register value columns (current/next row)
fn add_constraint(s_add: F, rs1: F, rs2: F, rd_next: F) -> F {
    // Gated: constraint active only when s_add = 1
    s_add * (rd_next - (rs1 + rs2))
    // Degree 2 (selector x linear) — cheap in FRI/DEEP-ALI
}
```

```python
# Schematic: LogUp cross-table lookup (SP1-style chip linkage)
# Proves tuple (op, a, b) was "sent" by CPU chip and "received" by ALU chip
def logup_accumulate(acc, multiplicity, op, a, b, beta):
    # acc_{t+1} = acc_t + m / (beta + encode(op, a, b))
    return acc + multiplicity / (beta + op * X**2 + a * X + b)
# Final claim: sum over all chips of signed accumulators == 0
```

> **Theorem: (Selector soundness).** If $\sum_o s_o(t) = 1$ and $s_o(t)^2 = s_o(t)$ hold per cycle, and each opcode constraint is gated as $s_o \cdot C_o = 0$, then any satisfying trace executes exactly one valid instruction semantics per cycle. ∎

### 4.2 RISC Zero: randomized AIR with preprocessing

RISC Zero's argument system [2] is a **randomized AIR with preprocessing**: preprocessing constructs constraints for a PLONK-based *memory permutation argument* and a Plookup-based *range check*; the execution trace (one wide table: PC, opcode fields, registers, memory columns) is committed with random blinding for zero-knowledge; **DEEP-ALI** composes all constraints and **FRI** proves low-degreeness over BabyBear with Blake3 Merkle commitments.

The system is *transparent* (no trusted setup), recurses via a circuit that verifies STARKs inside the zkVM, and supports continuations joined by receipts; the on-chain story wraps the STARK in Groth16. The cost driver is trace width: every cycle carries every column, so FRI time scales with $n \times w$ regardless of which opcodes execute — the inefficiency that motivated multi-table designs.

### 4.3 SP1: chips, LogUp, and Plonky3

SP1 [3][8] decomposes the machine into **chips**: a CPU chip, ALU chips per operation class, a memory chip, and precompile chips (SHA-256, Keccak-256, secp256k1, BN254/BLS12-381, …). Each chip's trace is sized to actual usage, and chips communicate through **LogUp** cross-table lookups: the CPU "sends" $(opcode, operands)$ tuples; the ALU "receives" them; a global log-derivative sum must vanish. Three documented wins [8]:

- **No merklized memory overhead** — memory consistency uses a dedicated chip with its own efficient argument.
- **Blowup factor 2 instead of 4**, halving FRI domain size.
- **First-class precompiles.** SHA-256 costs a handful of cycles through the precompile chip instead of tens of thousands of RISC-V cycles — the dominant factor in SP1's reported 28× speedup over RISC Zero v0.21 on a Tendermint light client (2.2 h → 4.6 min) [8]. Cycle counts themselves shrink because precompile calls replace instruction sequences.

Recursion proceeds in stages: *core* proofs per shard, *compressed* proofs aggregating shards, and a final Groth16/Plonk "shrinking" wrapper for EVM verification. **SP1 Hypercube** [12] replaced univariate FRI with a *multilinear* backend: the trace becomes a high-dimensional hypercube committed with a "jagged" PCS committing only accessed regions, with constraint checking via **LogUp-GKR sumcheck** — targeting real-time Ethereum block proving (~10 s average on GPUs) [12].

### 4.4 Cairo and Stwo: the register-machine alternative

Cairo bets on a different ISA: a minimal **register machine** with write-once memory and nondeterminism-friendly semantics (the prover may "guess" values, then constrain them — ideal for hash preimages, Merkle paths, signatures). The Cairo AIR comprises **69+ components** (CPU, memory, range-check, Pedersen/ECDSA/Keccak/Blake builtins) proven by **Stwo**, StarkWare's Circle-STARK prover over M31 [4]. Circle STARKs [13] exploit the circle group $x^2+y^2=1$ over M31 for FFT-friendly domains without large smooth-subgroup fields, making the prover very fast on commodity hardware. Recursion is native — a Cairo verifier written *in Cairo* verifies STARK proofs of other Cairo executions — and Starknet's production proving is the largest real-world deployment of a zkVM-family system.

### 4.5 The lookup singularity: Jolt, Lasso, and Nexus

Jolt [5][11] asks: what if instruction semantics were *never* arithmetized as circuits? Every RISC-V instruction decomposes into a few table lookups (e.g., ADD = byte-wise addition with carry tables); **Lasso** [5] proves these against *structured* tables up to $2^{128}$ rows without materializing them, via sumcheck over multilinear extensions. The CPU circuit shrinks to bookkeeping, and the ISA becomes nearly free to extend — no per-opcode circuit engineering. The trade is a heavier commitment layer (Lasso pairs with MSM-based commitments like Zeromorph/Hyrax rather than FRI) and a different recursion story. **Nexus 2.0** [7] adopted Jolt as its prover frontend with **HyperNova** as the recursive backend, betting that lookup-singularity arithmetization plus folding-based recursion is the path to minimal per-step cost.

```tla+
---- MODULE ZkVMContinuations ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Sequences
CONSTANTS MaxSegmentCycles
VARIABLES segments, aggProof

(* Each segment proof attests: init state, final state, and
   correct execution of <= MaxSegmentCycles transitions. *)
SegmentOK(s) == /\ Len(s.trace) <= MaxSegmentCycles
                /\ s.init = IF s.index = 0 THEN GenesisState
                           ELSE Prev(s.index).final
                /\ \A t \in 1..Len(s.trace)-1 :
                       TransitionOK(s.trace[t], s.trace[t+1])

(* Aggregation: recursion verifies each segment proof in order,
   chaining final->init states; the aggregate proof covers all. *)
AggregateOK == /\ \A i \in 1..Len(segments) : SegmentOK(segments[i])
               /\ \A i \in 1..Len(segments)-1 :
                      segments[i].final = segments[i+1].init
====
```

> **Theorem: (Continuation soundness).** If each segment proof is knowledge-sound for "$\le K$ valid cycles from committed init to committed final state," and recursion constrains $\text{final}_i = \text{init}_{i+1}$ for all adjacent segments, the aggregate proof is knowledge-sound for the full $T$-cycle execution. *Proof sketch.* Extract witnesses segment-by-segment (standard hybrid argument); chaining constraints glue them into one $T$-cycle witness, with extraction loss linear in the segment count. ∎

## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Published benchmark synthesis

All figures are vendor-published [3][8][12] (AWS r7i.16xlarge, 64 vCPU, 512 GB RAM); treat as *indicative* — methodology caveats in §6.

| Program | SP1 (v1.1.1) | RISC Zero (v1.0.0) | Speedup | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fibonacci | 30 s | 163 s | 5.4× | Baseline loop |
| SSZ Merkle proof | 49 s | 229 s | 4.6× | Hash-heavy |
| Tendermint light client | 278 s (4.6 min) | 7,920 s (2.2 h) | ~28× | Precompiles dominate |
| SP1-Reth (2 eth blocks) | reported competitive | — | — | EVM-in-zkVM |

Against **Jolt**, SP1 reports 1.66–3.23× faster proving at 1.3–3.6× smaller proof sizes on SHA-2-chain/Fibonacci/loop microbenchmarks with comparable settings [3]. **SP1 Hypercube** targets the real-time frontier: ~93% of Ethereum mainnet blocks proven in under 12 s (avg ~10.3 s) on GPUs [12] — the threshold at which L2s can finalize within a single L1 slot.

### 5.2 Cost model

Prover time decomposes as:

$$T_{\text{prove}} \approx \underbrace{c_1 \cdot n \cdot w}_{\text{trace commit}} + \underbrace{c_2 \cdot n \cdot w \cdot \log n}_{\text{FRI queries}} + \underbrace{c_3 \cdot n_{\text{seg}} \cdot T_{\text{rec}}}_{\text{recursion}} + \underbrace{T_{\text{wrap}}}_{\text{Groth16 shrink}}.$$

The chip architecture attacks $w$ (effective columns per cycle); blowup-2 FRI attacks the $\log n$ constant; precompiles attack $n$ itself; GPU/multilinear backends attack $c_1, c_2$. The recursion term is *quasi-fixed per segment*: smaller shards increase parallelism but add recursion overhead per shard — the fundamental latency/throughput trade-off of continuations. SP1's Groth16 "shrinking" wrapper adds ~50 s of constant overhead [3], irrelevant at Tendermint scale but dominant for tiny programs.

### 5.3 Verifier asymptotics

| Layer | Proof size | Verifier time | Trust assumption |
|---|---|---|---|
| Raw STARK (per segment) | $O(\log^2 n)$, ~100 KB–1 MB | $O(\log^2 n)$, ms | hash (PQ-plausible) |
| Recursion-compressed | $O(\log^2 n)$, smaller constant | ms | hash |
| Groth16-wrapped | $O(1)$, ~200–800 bytes | $O(1)$, ~5–15 ms + pairing | trusted setup, discrete log (not PQ) |

Wrapping buys EVM-cheap verification at the cost of reintroducing a trusted setup and losing post-quantum security — a trade the whole industry currently accepts for on-chain use, since the STARK layer retains its properties for off-chain verification.

### 5.4 Soundness accounting

Conjectured security is budgeted across components: FRI query count and grinding bits; DEEP-ALI sampling error; hash collision resistance; and the *arithmetization gap* — the informal step where humans assert the constraints faithfully encode the ISA. RISC Zero pursues a formally verified verifier in Lean4 [2]; Stwo's engineering discipline forbids manual edits to generated AIR code and gates constraint definitions, FRI parameters, and the Fiat–Shamir channel behind CI [4]. No deployed zkVM has a fully machine-checked *arithmetization* proof — the softest point in every soundness claim.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Benchmark incommensurability.** Vendors differ in hardware, hash function, precompile enablement, shard sizes, and even *what counts* (witness generation; latency vs. throughput with parallel recursion). The 28× figure compares systems with different precompile sets on a workload where precompiles dominate — it measures ISA-extension strategy as much as backend efficiency [8]. Independent, workload-fixed benchmarking (e.g., the Waseda framework comparing 7 zkVMs [14]) is the only sound basis for ranking, and it remains nascent.
2. **The arithmetization gap.** Every soundness theorem is conditional on "the constraints encode the ISA correctly." Constraint systems are tens of thousands of lines of generated code; one missing range check is a soundness break, not a performance bug. Formal verification of arithmetizations is in its infancy.
3. **Memory and state blowup.** Continuations handle *time* but not *space*: large-memory programs still pay per-access argument costs, and cross-segment memory consistency requires committing memory Merkle roots at every boundary — a serial bottleneck for parallel proving.
4. **Precompile centralization of trust.** Precompiles are hand-optimized gadgets outside the uniform ISA argument; each is new trusted code. The modern performance story is increasingly *the precompile story*, reintroducing the per-application circuit engineering zkVMs were meant to abolish.
5. **Prover economics.** Real-time proving (~10 s/block) demands GPUs and parallelism; per-block cost, while falling, still prices out long-tail applications.
6. **Post-quantum honesty.** STARK cores are plausibly PQ-secure, but every EVM-deployed proof today terminates in a Groth16/Plonk wrapper with a trusted setup and discrete-log assumptions. "Post-quantum zkVM" currently describes the off-chain layer only.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The zkVM has converged on a stable pattern — *arithmetize a fixed ISA once, prove traces, recurse over segments* — while diverging on how to arithmetize: monolithic AIR (RISC Zero), multi-chip LogUp tables (SP1), register-machine Circle STARKs (Cairo/Stwo), and the lookup singularity (Jolt/Nexus). Each attacks a different term of the prover cost equation: trace width, FRI constants, cycle counts via precompiles, per-step constraints via lookups. The frontier has moved from hours per program to seconds per Ethereum block, and the remaining barriers are as much about *trust* (the arithmetization gap, precompile auditing, PQ-honest on-chain verification) as *speed*. The key open problem is not a faster FRI but a *verified* arithmetization: a machine-checked proof that production constraints actually encode the claimed ISA. Until then, zkVM soundness rests on auditing discipline — considerable, but not mathematical.
