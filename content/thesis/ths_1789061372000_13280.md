# Channel Polarization and Polar Codes: Arikan's Construction, Successive-Cancellation Decoding, and the Path to Capacity

## Abstract

Polar codes, introduced by Erdal Arikan in 2009 [1], are the first class of codes provably achieving the symmetric capacity of arbitrary binary-input discrete memoryless channels with an explicit construction and *O(N log N)* encoding and decoding complexity. This thesis presents a self-contained doctoral-level treatment of channel polarization: the recursive channel combining-and-splitting transform that synthesizes *N* extremal synthetic channels from *N* copies of a raw channel *W*, concentrating mutual information so that a fraction approaching the symmetric capacity *I(W)* of the synthetic channels becomes nearly noiseless while the remainder becomes nearly useless [1][2]. We develop the Bhattacharyya-parameter recursions, the martingale convergence proof of the polarization theorem, and the successive-cancellation decoder with its block-error bound. We then analyze polarization exponents and larger kernels [3], successive-cancellation list decoding [5][6], efficient code construction via channel degrading and upgrading [4], finite-length scaling behavior, and the landmark adoption of polar codes for 5G New Radio control channels [7]. We conclude with open limitations, including the suboptimal scaling exponent of the 2x2 kernel, latency of sequential decoding, and directions toward improved constructions.

## 1 Introduction

In 1948, Claude Shannon proved that reliable communication over a noisy channel is possible at any rate below capacity [1]. For six decades afterward, the central embarrassment of coding theory was the gap between *existence* and *construction*: random-coding arguments guaranteed capacity-achieving codes, but no explicitly constructed family with efficient encoding and decoding was *proven* to achieve capacity on arbitrary channels. Turbo codes (1993) and LDPC codes approached capacity empirically, yet their capacity-achieving proofs required carefully optimized degree distributions and held only asymptotically under idealized decoding assumptions.

Arikan's 2009 paper changed this landscape decisively [1]. Channel polarization showed that a simple, deterministic, recursive transform — built from nothing more than the 2x2 kernel *F = [[1,0],[1,1]]* — converts *N = 2^n* independent copies of any binary-input discrete memoryless channel (B-DMC) *W* into *N* synthetic channels *{W_N^(i)}* that polarize: as *N* grows, a fraction approaching *I(W)* of them becomes essentially noiseless while the rest becomes essentially useless. Transmitting data only on the good synthetic channels, with the rest *frozen* to known values, yields codes whose rate approaches *I(W)* and whose block-error probability vanishes under a successive-cancellation (SC) decoder of complexity *O(N log N)*.

This thesis develops the full theory. Section 2 fixes notation and the Bhattacharyya reliability measure. Section 3 presents the combining-and-splitting methodology. Section 4 proves the polarization theorem via martingale convergence, analyzes the SC decoder, studies polarization exponents and kernel design, and treats practical construction and list decoding. Section 5 collects the key quantitative results, from error-exponent bounds to 5G standardization. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Binary-input memoryless channels and symmetric capacity

A **binary-input discrete memoryless channel** (B-DMC) *W: X -> Y* has input alphabet *X = {0,1}*, output alphabet *Y*, and transition probabilities *W(y|x)*. Its **symmetric capacity** is the mutual information under uniform inputs:

*I(W) = sum_{x in {0,1}} sum_{y in Y} (1/2) W(y|x) log_2 [ W(y|x) / ((1/2)W(y|0) + (1/2)W(y|1)) ]*

This is the highest rate achievable when the two input letters are used with equal probability; for *symmetric* channels it equals the Shannon capacity.

Arikan's analysis is driven by a second functional, the **Bhattacharyya parameter**:

*Z(W) = sum_{y in Y} sqrt(W(y|0) W(y|1))*

The parameter *Z(W)* measures reliability: *Z(W) ~ 0* means the channel is nearly noiseless, *Z(W) ~ 1* means it is nearly useless. It upper-bounds the maximum-likelihood decision error for one uncoded bit, which makes it the natural currency for tracking polarization.

> **Example (Binary Erasure Channel).** For *BEC(eps)*, *I(W) = 1 - eps* and *Z(W) = eps*. The erasure channel is the laboratory in which polarization can be computed in closed form, and it recurs throughout this thesis.

### 2.2 The pre-polar landscape

Before 2009, the coding-theoretic frontier was dominated by *graph-based* codes — turbo and LDPC families — whose belief-propagation decoders performed spectacularly in simulation. Yet proving that an *explicitly constructed* sequence achieves capacity with *polynomial* decoding complexity on *every* symmetric B-DMC remained open. Arikan's contribution was not incremental: polarization is a *structural* phenomenon of the channel transform itself, and the SC decoder exploits exactly the recursive structure of the construction, yielding the first complete, rigorous, low-complexity capacity-achieving scheme [1].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Channel combining

Take two independent copies of *W* and combine them with a single XOR. Given bits *u_1, u_2*, transmit *x_1 = u_1 xor u_2* and *x_2 = u_2* through the two copies, producing *y_1, y_2*. This defines the combined channel *W_2(y_1, y_2 | u_1, u_2) = W(y_1 | u_1 xor u_2) W(y_2 | u_2)*. The combining operation is the 2x2 kernel

```
F = [ 1  0 ]
    [ 1  1 ]
```

applied as *(x_1, x_2) = (u_1, u_2) F*. Recursing, *W_{2N}* is built from two independent copies of *W_N* with the same kernel, and the overall generator matrix is *G_N = B_N F^{ox n}*, where *F^{ox n}* is the *n*-th Kronecker power of *F* and *B_N* is the bit-reversal permutation. Encoding is *x_1^N = u_1^N G_N*, computable in *O(N log N)* by the butterfly (fast Walsh-Hadamard-like) structure [1].

### 3.2 Channel splitting

Splitting decomposes *W_N* back into *N* **synthetic bit-channels**, one per input position:

*W_N^(i)(y_1^N, u_1^{i-1} | u_i) = sum_{u_{i+1}^N} (1/2^{N-1}) W_N(y_1^N | u_1^N)*

The *i*-th synthetic channel sees the channel outputs *and* all previous decisions *u_1^{i-1}* as side information — precisely the view of a successive decoder. For *N = 2*, splitting yields the celebrated **minus** and **plus** channels:

- *W^-(y_1,y_2 | u_1) = sum_{u_2} (1/2) W(y_1|u_1 xor u_2) W(y_2|u_2)*
- *W^+(y_1,y_2,u_1 | u_2) = (1/2) W(y_1|u_1 xor u_2) W(y_2|u_2)*

The single-step recursions governing polarization are [1]:

| Quantity | Minus channel | Plus channel |
|---|---|---|
| Mutual information | *I(W^-) <= I(W) <= I(W^+)* | *I(W^-) + I(W^+) = 2 I(W)* |
| Bhattacharyya | *Z(W^-) <= 2Z(W) - Z(W)^2* | *Z(W^+) = Z(W)^2* |

Capacity is *conserved* (*I(W^-) + I(W^+) = 2I(W)*) but *redistributed*: the plus channel is strictly better and the minus channel strictly worse than *W* (unless *W* is already extremal). The Bhattacharyya parameter is *squared* on the plus branch — doubly-exponential improvement under iteration — while degrading only mildly on the minus branch. For the BEC, the minus inequality is an equality, *Z(W^-) = 2eps - eps^2*, which is why the BEC admits exact analysis.

### 3.3 Code construction from polarized channels

Sort the *N* synthetic channels by *Z(W_N^(i))*. Choose an information set *A* of the *K* most reliable indices; its complement *A^c* is **frozen** to fixed values (conventionally zero) known to the decoder. The polar code is the set *{u_1^N G_N : u_{A^c} = 0}*, a linear block code of rate *K/N*. Construction therefore reduces to *ranking* the synthetic channels — a nontrivial computational problem addressed in Section 4.4 [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The polarization theorem: a martingale proof

Consider the random process obtained by starting from *W_0 = W* and, at each step, replacing the current channel by *W^-* or *W^+* with probability 1/2 each. Define *I_n = I(W_n)* and *Z_n = Z(W_n)*.

> **Theorem (Channel polarization [1]).** *For any B-DMC W, as n -> infinity, I_n converges almost surely to a {0,1}-valued random variable I_inf with E[I_inf] = I(W). Equivalently, for any delta > 0, the fraction of indices i with I(W_N^(i)) in (1-delta, 1] tends to I(W), and the fraction with I(W_N^(i)) in [0, delta) tends to 1 - I(W).*

*Proof sketch.* The sequence *{I_n}* is a bounded **martingale**: *E[I_{n+1} | W_n] = (I(W_n^-) + I(W_n^+))/2 = I(W_n)* by capacity conservation. Bounded martingales converge almost surely and in *L^1*. The limit must be extremal: whenever *I_n* is bounded away from *{0,1}*, the variance injected by the splitting step is bounded away from zero (the plus/minus channels are strictly separated in mutual information for non-extremal channels), so *I_n* cannot settle in the interior. Hence *I_inf* takes values in *{0,1}*, and *E[I_inf] = E[I_0] = I(W)*. The same argument applied to *Z_n* — a supermartingale converging to the same limit — shows the *reliability* polarizes too, which is what the decoder needs. ∎

This is the conceptual heart of the theory: **polarization is a conservation law plus a strict improvement step, iterated until extremality is forced.**

### 4.2 Successive-cancellation decoding

The SC decoder estimates *u_1, ..., u_N* sequentially. When decoding position *i*, all previous decisions *u^_1^{i-1}* are available; frozen positions are set to their known values, and information positions are decided by maximum likelihood on *W_N^(i)*:

*u^_i = argmax_{u in {0,1}} W_N^(i)(y_1^N, u^_1^{i-1} | u)*

The likelihoods recurse through the factor graph. In log-likelihood-ratio (LLR) form, with *L* denoting LLRs, the two node updates are [5][8]:

- **f-node (minus):** *f(a, b) ~ sign(a) sign(b) min(|a|, |b|)*
- **g-node (plus):** *g(a, b, u_s) = (-1)^{u_s} a + b*

where *u_s* is the already-decided partial sum. A compact recursive implementation:

```python
import numpy as np

def f_node(a, b):
    """Minus-node LLR update (min-sum approximation of the exact boxplus)."""
    return np.sign(a) * np.sign(b) * np.minimum(np.abs(a), np.abs(b))

def g_node(a, b, u_s):
    """Plus-node LLR update given the decided partial sum u_s."""
    return b + ((-1) ** u_s) * a

def sc_decode(llr, frozen):
    n = len(llr)
    u_hat = np.zeros(n, dtype=int)
    def recurse(node_llr, idx):
        m = len(node_llr)
        if m == 1:
            i = int(next(iter(idx)))
            llr0 = float(next(iter(node_llr)))
            u_hat[i] = 0 if frozen[i] else (0 if llr0 >= 0 else 1)
            return np.array([u_hat[i]])
        a, b = node_llr[:m // 2], node_llr[m // 2:]
        left = recurse(f_node(a, b), idx[:m // 2])
        right = recurse(g_node(a, b, left), idx[m // 2:])
        return np.concatenate([left ^ right, right])
    recurse(llr, np.arange(n))
    return u_hat
```

Each of the *N* positions triggers *O(log N)* node updates, for total complexity *O(N log N)* and *O(N)* memory [1][8]. By the union bound over the information set,

> **Theorem (SC error bound [1]).** *For a polar code with information set A, the block-error probability under SC decoding satisfies P_e(N, A) <= sum_{i in A} Z(W_N^(i)).*

Arikan showed this sum is *O(N^{-1/4})* for any fixed rate *R < I(W)*; Arikan and Telatar sharpened the asymptotic dramatically [2]:

> **Theorem (Rate of polarization [2]).** *For any B-DMC W and any rate R < I(W), the block-error probability under SC decoding satisfies P_e <= 2^{-N^beta} for any beta < 1/2, for all sufficiently large N.*

The exponent *1/2* is the **polarization exponent** of the 2x2 kernel — the best error decay SC decoding can extract from this construction.

### 4.3 Polarization exponents and larger kernels

Is the 2x2 kernel optimal? Korada, Sasoglu, and Urbanke generalized polarization to *l x l* kernels and characterized each kernel's exponent [3]. Their findings:

1. Any *l x l* matrix, no column permutation of which is upper triangular, polarizes symmetric B-DMCs.
2. The exponent of a kernel is determined by its **partial distances**; the 2x2 kernel *F* achieves exponent *1/2*.
3. **No** matrix of size *l < 15* has exponent exceeding *1/2*.
4. A BCH-code-based construction yields kernels whose exponents approach *1* as *l* grows, exceeding *1/2* already at *l = 16*.

| Kernel | Size | Exponent | Construction cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arikan *F* | 2x2 | 1/2 | *O(N log N)* |
| Optimal small kernels | < 15x15 | 1/2 (max) | higher constants |
| BCH-based kernels [3] | >= 16x16 | -> 1 | large alphabet blowup |

The catch is decoding complexity: larger kernels make the SC node updates exponentially more expensive in *l*, so the 2x2 kernel remains the practical choice — a tension between asymptotic exponent and implementability that persists in the literature [3].

### 4.4 Construction algorithms and list decoding

**The construction problem.** The synthetic channels *W_N^(i)* have output alphabets of size *|Y|^N* — exponential in the blocklength — so exact reliability ranking is intractable for general channels. Three practical approaches emerged:

- **Density evolution** (Mori-Tanaka): track LLR densities through the recursion via discretized convolutions, linear in *N* but numerically delicate [4].
- **Gaussian approximation:** assume LLRs stay Gaussian and track only their means — fast and accurate enough for the AWGN channel.
- **Channel degrading/upgrading (Tal-Vardy) [4]:** approximate each synthetic channel by a *degraded* and an *upgraded* version with capped alphabet size *mu*, sandwiching the true channel. The two approximations are provably close and computable in *O(N mu^{2} log mu)*, giving the first rigorous polynomial-time construction for general B-DMCs with rate within *eps* of capacity.

**Successive-cancellation list (SCL) decoding.** SC decoding is greedy: one wrong early decision cascades. Tal and Vardy generalized SC to maintain *L* candidate paths, splitting each path at every information bit and pruning to the *L* most likely survivors — complexity *O(L N log N)* via lazy copying on the decoding tree [5]. Appending a CRC and selecting the most likely CRC-passing path (**CRC-aided SCL**) closes most of the gap to maximum-likelihood decoding at short blocklengths with modest *L* (e.g., *L = 8..32*). Fazeli, Vardy, and Yao proved that SCL *coincides* with ML decoding once *L >= 2^kappa*, where *kappa* — the **mixing factor** — counts information bits preceding the last frozen bit [6]. SCL thus interpolates smoothly between SC (*L = 1*) and ML (*L = 2^kappa*), and its capacity-achievability was formally established alongside precoded (e.g., CRC-concatenated) polar variants.

## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

**Closed-form polarization on the BEC.** For *BEC(0.5)* at *N = 8*, the Bhattacharyya parameters of the synthetic channels (best to worst) are:

| Index *i* | 8 | 7 | 6 | 4 | 5 | 3 | 2 | 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Z(W_8^(i))* | 0.0039 | 0.1211 | 0.1914 | 0.3164 | 0.6836 | 0.8086 | 0.8789 | 0.9961 |
| *I(W_8^(i))* | 0.9961 | 0.8789 | 0.8086 | 0.6836 | 0.3164 | 0.1914 | 0.1211 | 0.0039 |

Even at *N = 8*, polarization is visible: the best channel is nearly perfect, the worst nearly useless. A rate-1/2 code takes *A = {4, 6, 7, 8}*; the union bound gives *P_e <= 0.6328* — loose at this tiny length, but the Arikan-Telatar *2^{-N^beta}* decay takes over rapidly as *N* grows [2].

**Decoder performance landscape.** The following summarizes the proven and observed behavior of polar decoding algorithms:

| Decoder | Complexity | Block-error behavior | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| SC | *O(N log N)* | *<= 2^{-N^beta}*, *beta < 1/2* [2] | Capacity-achieving [1] |
| SCL(*L*) | *O(L N log N)* | Approaches ML as *L* grows [5][6] | *L = 2^kappa* equals ML [6] |
| CRC-aided SCL | *O(L N log N)* | Near-ML at short *N* | 5G control-channel choice [7] |

**Finite-length scaling.** How fast does the gap to capacity close? The finite-length scaling literature reports that the gap scales as *N^{-1/mu}* with scaling exponent *mu ~ 3.627* for the 2x2 kernel — markedly worse than the *mu = 2* of random coding. This quantifies polar codes' main practical weakness: excellent asymptotics, sluggish finite-length convergence, partially remedied by CRC-aided SCL.

**Standardization: 5G New Radio.** In November 2016, at 3GPP RAN1 meeting #87, polar codes — championed principally by Huawei, which had demonstrated 27 Gbps prototype links — were adopted as the channel coding scheme for **5G NR control channels** (PDCCH, PUCCH, and PBCH), specified in 3GPP TS 38.212 [7]. This was the first large-scale commercial deployment of a provably capacity-achieving code family, using CRC-aided SCL decoding with puncturing, shortening, and rate matching to handle arbitrary blocklengths beyond powers of two.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Suboptimal finite-length performance.** The polarization exponent *1/2* and scaling exponent *mu ~ 3.627* mean plain SC decoding needs very large *N* to approach capacity; at short-to-moderate lengths, LDPC and turbo codes outperform it. CRC-aided SCL compensates but multiplies complexity by *L*.
2. **Sequential decoding latency.** SC/SCL decide bits one at a time — an inherently serial *O(N)*-step process. High-throughput implementations require speculative or partitioned architectures, and the latency-throughput tradeoff remains an active hardware research area [5].
3. **Error propagation.** A single early wrong decision under SC corrupts all subsequent decisions; the decoder has no natural mechanism to recover, which is precisely why list decoding was invented [5][6].
4. **Non-universality.** The frozen set is tailored to a specific channel; a code designed for one SNR can perform poorly on another. Universal polar constructions exist but pay with substantially larger blocklengths.
5. **Power-of-two blocklengths.** The Kronecker construction natively yields *N = 2^n*; practical systems need puncturing/shortening/rate-matching machinery (as in 5G [7]), which complicates the polarization analysis.
6. **Construction cost for general channels.** Exact ranking is intractable; practitioners rely on approximations (density evolution, Gaussian approximation, degrading/upgrading [4]) whose accuracy must be validated per channel.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Channel polarization resolved the oldest open problem in constructive coding theory: an *explicit* family of codes, with *O(N log N)* encoding and decoding, *provably* achieving the symmetric capacity of every binary-input discrete memoryless channel [1][2]. The theory's elegance — a single 2x2 kernel, a martingale convergence proof, a recursive decoder — belies its depth: exponent characterizations [3], efficient constructions [4], list decoding [5][6], and finite-length scaling each opened substantial subfields. Its adoption into 5G NR control channels [7] marks the rare transition of an information-theoretic breakthrough into global infrastructure within a decade. The remaining challenges — finite-length scaling, decoding latency, universality, and kernel design — define the current research frontier, but the central insight stands: **capacity can be achieved not by fighting noise symmetrically, but by polarizing it into channels that are either perfect or worthless, and using only the perfect ones.**
