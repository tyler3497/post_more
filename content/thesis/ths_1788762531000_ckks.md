# The Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song Scheme for Approximate Homomorphic Arithmetic: Canonical Encoding, RNS Rescaling, Bootstrapping, and Secure Neural Inference

## Abstract

We present a comprehensive doctoral-level treatment of the Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song (CKKS) homomorphic encryption scheme for arithmetic of approximate numbers [1]. We develop the full pipeline from complex-valued slot packing via the canonical embedding of the cyclotomic ring, through RLWE encryption, homomorphic multiplication with relinearization, and the residue-number-system (RNS) rescaling procedure that keeps ciphertext scales aligned at a cost of at most one bit of precision beyond ordinary floating-point arithmetic. We give a detailed account of CKKS bootstrapping — the homomorphic SlotToCoeff transform, ModRaise, and the EvalMod step built on the scaled-sine approximation of modular reduction [3] — and survey the modern line of optimizations culminating in non-sparse-key [9] and multi-GPU refreshes. We analyze error propagation, parameter selection under current security guidelines [8], and the scheme's role in privacy-preserving machine-learning inference, where CKKS remains the dominant fully homomorphic construction for computation over real numbers [5][6][7].

## 1 Introduction

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) has long promised computation on encrypted data, but the dominant second-generation schemes — BGV, BFV, and their predecessors — operate over *exact* integer arithmetic. Real-world numerical workloads, from financial modeling to neural-network inference, are instead expressed in floating point: they tolerate small rounding errors by construction. The Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song scheme, introduced at ASIACRYPT 2017 and initially released as the HEAAN library [1], exploits this observation by treating *approximation error as part of the ciphertext noise*. Rather than fighting RLWE noise to achieve exact decryption, CKKS folds the rounding error into the noise budget and delivers approximate decryption of complex-valued messages — arithmetic on approximate numbers, natively.

This design decision buys three decisive advantages. First, *rescaling*: after each homomorphic multiplication, the ciphertext modulus is divided down by the scaling factor, so the bit-length of the modulus grows only linearly with circuit depth rather than exponentially [1][5]. Second, *precision*: the cumulative approximation error after an entire computation is bounded by the circuit depth and exceeds ordinary floating-point error by at most one additional bit. Third, *usability*: vectors of complex numbers are packed into a single ciphertext and processed in SIMD fashion, matching the vectorized idiom of modern machine learning.

We develop the scheme from first principles: canonical encoding (4.1), RNS rescaling (4.2), key switching (4.3), bootstrapping (4.4), and error management with encrypted ML inference (4.5), followed by empirical results and proofs (5), limitations (6), and conclusions (7).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Ring-LWE and the cyclotomic ring

CKKS inherits its hardness from the *ring learning with errors* (RLWE) problem over the $2N$-th cyclotomic ring $R = \mathbb{Z}[X]/(X^N + 1)$, with $N$ a power of two [1][5]. A secret $s \in R$ with small coefficients, uniform $a \in R_q = R/qR$, and small error $e$ drawn from a discrete Gaussian yield samples $(a, b = -a s + e) \bmod q$, indistinguishable from uniform under RLWE. The plaintext space is $R$ itself, interpreted as an approximation to a vector of complex numbers through the canonical embedding.

The *canonical embedding* $\sigma: R \to \mathbb{C}^{N/2}$ maps a polynomial $m(X)$ to its evaluations at the primitive $2N$-th roots of unity $\zeta, \zeta^3, \dots, \zeta^{2N-1}$ with $\zeta = e^{\pi i / N}$, taking only the $N/2$ roots with positive imaginary part; the remaining evaluations are their complex conjugates, preserving Hermiticity for real-coefficient polynomials [1]. Because $\sigma$ is a ring homomorphism from $R$ into $\mathbb{C}^{N/2}$ (component-wise addition and multiplication), polynomial arithmetic in $R$ is *exactly* slot-wise arithmetic on complex vectors — the algebraic fact that makes SIMD packing possible.

### 2.2 From BGV to approximate arithmetic

BGV-style schemes embed the message in the high-order bits of a plaintext polynomial and clear noise through modulus switching, requiring exact recovery [2]. CKKS instead treats the *message as the signal riding on top of small noise*, in the same way floating-point significands ride atop rounding error. Decryption returns $m + e'$, where $e'$ is small relative to a scaling factor $\Delta$; dividing out $\Delta$ yields the message plus an error of magnitude roughly $e'/\Delta$. When $\Delta = 2^{40}$ or $2^{50}$, tens of bits of precision survive, matching double-precision idioms for most ML workloads [6][10].

### 2.3 The RNS optimization line

Naive implementations of CKKS need multi-precision arithmetic on moduli of thousands of bits. The *full RNS variant* [2] restructures the ciphertext modulus as $Q = \prod_{i=0}^{L} q_i$ with 60-bit word-sized primes, representing each ring element by its residues $(x \bmod q_i)_i$ and evaluating all polynomial multiplications through the Number Theoretic Transform (NTT) in each limb. Rescaling becomes division by one RNS prime at a time, and modulus switching is performed approximately without ever composing the full modulus — everything stays in 64-bit arithmetic. This RNS-CKKS is the basis of every modern implementation (HEAAN, SEAL, Lattigo, OpenFHE) and of the bootstrapping literature surveyed here [9].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is analytic and comparative. We formalize the CKKS algorithms — $\mathsf{Encode}$, $\mathsf{Decode}$, $\mathsf{Enc}$, $\mathsf{Dec}$, $\mathsf{Add}$, $\mathsf{Mult}$, $\mathsf{Relin}$, $\mathsf{Rescale}$, $\mathsf{Rotate}$, $\mathsf{Bootstrap}$ — over $R_q$ with explicit scale bookkeeping, following the ASIACRYPT 2017 construction [1] and its full-RNS refinement [2]. We derive the rescaling invariant ($\text{scale} \approx \Delta$ at every level) and the noise-growth recurrences that determine parameter selection. For bootstrapping we follow the Cheon–Han–Kim–Kim–Song pipeline [3] and the subsequent EvalMod line [9], comparing asymptotic costs (homomorphic multiplications, depth consumption, precision) across generations. Empirical evidence is drawn from published bootstrapping benchmarks [3], the CKKS bootstrapping survey's unified *Utility* comparison [9], encrypted ML studies [6][7][8], and the 2025 security guidelines [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The canonical embedding and complex SIMD encoding

Let $N = 2^k$ and $R = \mathbb{Z}[X]/(X^N+1)$. The *canonical embedding* is

$$\sigma(m) = (m(\zeta), m(\zeta^3), \dots, m(\zeta^{2N-1})) \in \mathbb{C}^{N/2},$$

for $m \in R$ with real coefficients (complex-coefficient variants conjugate accordingly) [1]. Encoding a vector $z \in \mathbb{C}^{N/2}$ at scale $\Delta$ computes the inverse embedding and rounds:

$$\mathsf{Encode}(z; \Delta) = \left\lfloor \Delta \cdot \sigma^{-1}(z) \right\rceil \in R.$$

Decoding reverses it: $\mathsf{Decode}(m; \Delta) = \sigma(m)/\Delta$. The rounding introduces an *encoding error* $e_{\text{enc}}$ with $\|e_{\text{enc}}\|_\infty^{\text{can}} \le N/\sqrt{12}$ in the canonical norm, negligible once divided by $\Delta \ge 2^{30}$ [1]. Because $\sigma$ is a ring homomorphism, encoding is homomorphic: $\mathsf{Decode}(m_1 + m_2) \approx z_1 + z_2$ and $\mathsf{Decode}(m_1 \cdot m_2) \approx z_1 \odot z_2$ (slot-wise product), with errors bounded by the encoding precision.

> Theorem: (Canonical embedding homomorphism [1]). The map $\sigma: R \to \mathbb{C}^{N/2}$ is an injective ring homomorphism for polynomials with real coefficients; consequently slot-wise addition and multiplication of complex vectors correspond exactly to polynomial addition and multiplication in $R$.

In practice the inverse embedding is evaluated by a *homomorphic-style discrete Fourier transform*: the Vandermonde matrix of $\sigma$ is the DFT matrix, so encoding and decoding reduce to FFTs of size $N$, costing $O(N \log N)$ [1]. Unused slots are simply zero-filled; a ciphertext of ring dimension $N$ carries $N/2$ complex slots, e.g. $N = 2^{16}$ yields $32{,}768$ slots per ciphertext.

A toy Python encoding illustrates the pipeline:

```python
import cmath, math

def canonical_encode(z, N, Delta):
    # sigma^{-1} via inverse DFT on the primitive 2N-th roots
    n2 = N // 2
    zeta = cmath.exp(1j * math.pi / N)
    # build full conjugate-symmetric spectrum
    w = z + [c.conjugate() for c in reversed(z)]
    coeffs = []
    for k in range(N):
        s = sum(w[j] * zeta ** (-(2*j+1)*k) for j in range(N)) / N
        coeffs.append(round((Delta * s).real))
    return coeffs  # integer polynomial m(X)

def canonical_decode(coeffs, N, Delta):
    n2 = N // 2
    zeta = cmath.exp(1j * math.pi / N)
    return [sum(coeffs[k] * zeta ** ((2*j+1)*k) for k in range(N)) / Delta
            for j in range(n2)]
```

The homomorphism structure in Haskell:

```haskell
-- Cyclotomic ring R = Z[X]/(X^N + 1); canonical embedding into C^(N/2)
type Slot = Complex Double
type Poly = [Integer]

sigma :: Int -> Poly -> [Slot]
sigma n m = [ eval (zeta n ^^ (2*j+1)) m | j <- [0 .. n `div` 2 - 1] ]
  where eval z = sum . zipWith (\c k -> fromInteger c * z^k) m <*> [0..]

encode :: Int -> Double -> [Slot] -> Poly
encode n delta z = map (round . (* delta) . realPart) (ifftSpectrum n z)
```

Rotations of slots are Galois automorphisms: $X \mapsto X^{2k+1}$ permutes the roots of unity and hence cyclically shifts slots, enabling the *linear transforms* (matrix–vector products) that dominate neural-network evaluation [7]. Each rotation requires a Galois key and a key-switch, discussed in Section 4.3.

### 4.2 The RNS modulus chain and rescaling

A fresh ciphertext encrypts $m$ at scale $\Delta$ modulo $Q_L = \prod_{i=0}^{L} q_i$. Homomorphic multiplication of ciphertexts at scale $\Delta$ produces a ciphertext at scale $\Delta^2$ — the scale *squares*, and without intervention it would grow doubly-exponentially with depth. The CKKS *rescaling* procedure divides by the last modulus factor and rounds [1][2]:

$$\mathsf{RS}(ct) = \left\lfloor \frac{ct}{q_l} \right\rceil \bmod Q_{l-1}, \qquad Q_l = \prod_{i=0}^{l} q_i,$$

restoring scale $\approx \Delta$ while consuming one level of the modulus chain. Choosing $q_i \approx \Delta$ for $i \ge 1$ keeps the invariant $\text{scale} \approx \Delta$ at every level. The rounding error of rescaling is $O(\sqrt{N}/q_l)$ in the canonical norm — a *fixed, tiny* additive error per multiplication, not one that compounds multiplicatively. This is the analytic heart of the scheme:

> Theorem: (Rescaling precision bound [1]). After evaluating a circuit of multiplicative depth $L$, the accumulated approximation error of CKKS exceeds the error of unencrypted floating-point arithmetic on the same circuit by at most one bit, provided the rescaling moduli satisfy $q_i \approx \Delta$.

The full-RNS variant [2] is what makes this practical. Ciphertext moduli are chosen as products of NTT-friendly 60-bit primes; every operation is performed limb-wise with lazy reduction, and the *approximate modulus switching* drops the top RNS limb without ever reconstructing the integer $Q_l$. A Rust sketch of the modulus-chain bookkeeping:

```rust
/// RNS modulus chain for CKKS: Q_l = prod_{i=0}^{l} q_i
pub struct ModulusChain { pub primes: Vec<u64>, pub level: usize }

impl ModulusChain {
    /// Rescale: drop the top limb, dividing scale by q_level.
    pub fn rescale(&mut self, ct: &mut Ciphertext) {
        assert!(self.level > 0);
        let q = self.primes[self.level];
        for limb in ct.limbs.iter_mut() {
            // approximate division by q with rounding, limb-wise
            *limb = mod_switch_down(*limb, q, self.level);
        }
        self.level -= 1;
        ct.scale /= q as f64; // invariant: scale ~= Delta
    }
}
```

Representative parameter sets (128-bit security per [8]):

| $N$ | $\log Q$ (bits) | $\log \Delta$ | Levels $L$ | Slots | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $2^{13}$ | 218 | 40 | 4 | 4,096 | Shallow circuits, logistic regression |
| $2^{14}$ | 438 | 50 | 7 | 8,192 | Medium-depth ML, bootstrapping dev |
| $2^{15}$ | 881 | 60 | 13 | 16,384 | Bootstrapping, deep CNN layers |
| $2^{16}$ | 1,767 | 60 | 28 | 32,768 | Full bootstrapping, large models |

Larger $N$ buys more levels and slots at the cost of NTT time growing as $O(N \log N)$ per limb [10].

### 4.3 Key switching: relinearization and rotations

Tensoring two ciphertexts $ct_1 = (b_1, a_1)$, $ct_2 = (b_2, a_2)$ yields a degree-2 ciphertext $(d_0, d_1, d_2)$ with $d_0 = b_1 b_2$, $d_1 = a_1 b_2 + a_2 b_1$, $d_2 = a_1 a_2$ [1]. *Relinearization* collapses the quadratic term using an evaluation key $evk = (b', a')$ encrypting $s^2$ under $s$:

$$ct' = (d_0, d_1) + \left\lfloor P^{-1} \cdot d_2 \cdot evk \right\rceil \bmod Q_l,$$

where the special modulus $P$ (product of auxiliary primes) controls key-switching noise via digit decomposition [2][3]. The same key-switching primitive implements *rotations*: applying the Galois automorphism $X \mapsto X^{5^k}$ to the ciphertext permutes slots, then key-switching back to the original secret key yields $\mathsf{Rotate}(ct, k)$. Rotations plus plaintext–ciphertext multiplications give arbitrary linear transforms — the workhorse of encrypted matrix arithmetic and convolutional layers [7].

Asymptotic costs per operation (ring dimension $N$, $l$ remaining RNS limbs) [2][9]:

| Operation | NTTs | Key-switches | Depth cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Add / plaintext-mult | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Multiply + relin | $O(l)$ | 1 | 1 |
| Rescale | $O(l)$ | 0 | 0 (consumes a level) |
| Rotate | $O(l)$ | 1 | 0 |
| Bootstrap | $O(L \log N)$ | $O(\log N)$ | consumes $\approx L_{\text{boot}}$ |

### 4.4 Bootstrapping: CoeffToSlot, ModRaise, and EvalMod

When the modulus chain is exhausted, bootstrapping refreshes the ciphertext: it homomorphically evaluates the decryption circuit to strip away the modulus wrap and re-encrypt at a high level [3]. CKKS bootstrapping is approximate by design and decomposes into four stages:

1. **ModRaise.** Raise the ciphertext from modulus $q_0$ to the full $Q_L$ (trivially, by interpreting coefficients in the larger ring). The plaintext becomes $m + q_0 \cdot I(X)$, where $I(X)$ is a small integer polynomial recording how many times each coefficient wrapped around $q_0$.
2. **CoeffToSlot (homomorphic DFT).** A homomorphic linear transform moves the *coefficients* of $m + q_0 I$ into the *slots*, using the same DFT matrix as encoding but evaluated under encryption with rotation keys. Cost: $O(\log N)$ rotations via baby-step giant-step [3][9].
3. **EvalMod.** Remove the $q_0 \cdot I$ term *slot-wise* by approximating the modular reduction $t \mapsto t - q_0 \lfloor t/q_0 \rceil$ with a scaled sine: $[t]_{q_0} \approx \frac{q_0}{2\pi} \sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{q_0}\right)$, evaluated as a Taylor or Chebyshev polynomial composed $r$ times (each composition doubling the effective approximation range) [3]. This is the depth bottleneck: each sine iteration costs one multiplication.
4. **SlotToCoeff (inverse homomorphic DFT).** Move the cleaned values back from slots to coefficients, yielding an encryption of $m$ at level $L - L_{\text{boot}}$.

> Theorem: (Sine approximation for EvalMod [3]). For $|t| \le K q_0$, the $r$-fold composition of the degree-$(2d+1)$ Taylor approximation of $\frac{q_0}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi t/q_0)$ approximates $[t]_{q_0}$ with error decaying exponentially in $r$ and polynomially in $d$, at a cost of $r$ multiplicative levels.

The original implementation refreshed 128 slots at 12 bits of precision in 139.8 seconds (amortized 1.1 s/slot) [3]. The subsequent line — improved sine/polynomial approximations, *level-collision* techniques, non-sparse keys [9], and multi-GPU refreshes [4] have since reduced bootstrapping to seconds at higher precision, unified under the survey's *Utility* metric [9].

### 4.5 Error management and encrypted machine-learning inference

Total error in a CKKS computation has four sources: *encoding rounding* ($\approx 2^{-\Delta}$ relative), *RLWE noise* from encryption and key-switching, *rescaling rounding* (one $O(\sqrt{N}/q_l)$ term per multiplication [1]), and *bootstrapping approximation error* (dominated by EvalMod, typically $2^{-15}$ to $2^{-25}$ relative per refresh [9]). The practitioner controls all four through $\Delta$, $N$, the chain, and the EvalMod depth/degree. Noise flooding at decryption (adding large noise before revealing a result) restores IND-CPA$^D$ security when the adversary sees decrypted outputs, provided bootstrapping precision exceeds about 30 bits [9][8].

This error model is precisely what makes CKKS the scheme of choice for *encrypted inference*: neural networks are already approximate, trained with noisy gradients and evaluated with quantized weights. The literature demonstrates the pipeline end to end — polynomial approximations of activations (square, low-degree ReLU/sigmoid fits), matrix products via rotations, and bootstrapping between layers [6][7]. Reported results include wildfire-dataset models at 94% plaintext-matching accuracy [6], CNNs reaching 98.3% on CIFAR-10 and 80.2% top-1 on ImageNet via ciphertext sharding [7], and automated encrypted deep-learning frameworks [8]. Rescaling keeps one inference pass within a modest level budget, while bootstrapping enables arbitrary-depth models [3][9].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The foundational empirical claims of [1] are twofold, and both have held up under a decade of scrutiny. First, the *linear-modulus theorem*: because rescaling divides the modulus by $\Delta$ after each multiplication, $\log Q$ grows as $O(L \cdot \log \Delta)$ — linear in depth — versus the exponential growth of exact schemes. Second, the *one-bit theorem*: precision loss versus unencrypted floating point is bounded by a single bit over the whole circuit. The proof tracks the canonical-norm error through each operation: encryption adds $B_{\text{enc}}$, multiplication roughly squares the relative error before rescaling divides it back, and rescaling adds a fixed $B_{\text{rs}}$; induction over depth gives $B_{\text{total}} \le L \cdot B_{\text{rs}} + B_{\text{enc}}$, and with $q_i \approx \Delta$ the ratio $B_{\text{total}}/\Delta$ stays within one bit of the floating-point baseline [1][5].

For bootstrapping, [3] proves correctness of sine-based EvalMod, with error after $r$ compositions bounded by the ratio of the coefficient bound to the sine's linear region. The survey [9] documents order-of-magnitude speedups since the original 139.8 s benchmark, and [8] recommends non-sparse secrets with explicit Hamming-weight bounds for CKKS bootstrapping variants.

On the applications side, the record is consistent: encrypted inference achieves plaintext-matching accuracy whenever the approximation budget ($\Delta$, EvalMod precision) is sized to the model's noise tolerance [6][7]. The remaining gap is *latency*, not accuracy — encrypted inference runs $10^4$–$10^6\times$ slower than plaintext, dominated by bootstrapping and rotations [7][10].

## 6 Limitations

CKKS's approximations are also its constraints. **First, no exactness**: equality tests, comparisons, and branching on encrypted values are impossible; every algorithm must be arithmetized into additions and multiplications, and non-polynomial functions (ReLU, max-pooling, division) require polynomial approximation with attendant accuracy/depth trade-offs [6][7]. **Second, depth economics**: each multiplication consumes a level, and bootstrapping — while asymptotically a fix — costs orders of magnitude more than ordinary operations and itself consumes 15+ levels, so circuits must be carefully *leveled* to amortize refreshes [3][9]. **Third, parameter rigidity**: $(N, Q, \Delta)$ must be fixed before the computation; mis-sizing wastes performance or breaks correctness, and current guidance [8] narrows previously popular choices such as sparse secrets. **Fourth, key material**: rotation and bootstrapping keys are large (gigabytes for full slot coverage at $N = 2^{16}$), and key-switching dominates memory bandwidth [10]. **Fifth, the decryption oracle problem**: CKKS is IND-CPA secure, but releasing many decrypted approximate results can leak the secret key unless noise flooding is applied — a subtle, frequently mishandled requirement [8][9].

## 7 Conclusion

The CKKS scheme reconceived homomorphic encryption around a simple insight: when the application already tolerates approximation, the cryptosystem should too. By packing complex vectors through the canonical embedding, managing scale with RNS rescaling at a provable one-bit precision cost, and refreshing ciphertexts with sine-based bootstrapping, CKKS delivers the only practical FHE path for real-number computation today. Its limitations — arithmetization burden, depth economics, key sizes, and the care required around approximate decryption — define an active research frontier spanning better EvalMod approximations, hardware acceleration, and tightened security guidance. For privacy-preserving machine learning, scientific simulation, and any workload where floating-point semantics are native, approximate homomorphic encryption is not a compromise: it is the correct abstraction.
