# Conflict-Driven Clause Learning: Resolution Proofs, VSIDS Branching Heuristics, and the Anatomy of Modern SAT Solvers

## Abstract

This article presents a comprehensive exposition of conflict-driven clause learning (CDCL), the algorithmic engine behind modern propositional satisfiability (SAT) solvers. We trace the evolution from the Davis–Putnam–Logemann–Loveland (DPLL) procedure through the introduction of clause learning in GRASP and Chaff, and formalize the CDCL main loop: decision, Boolean constraint propagation with two-watched literals, conflict analysis over implication graphs, first-unique-implication-point (first-UIP) clause learning via resolution, non-chronological backjumping, and restarts. We dissect the two pillars of modern branching heuristics: VSIDS and its exponential recency weighted average (ERWA) generalization, and learning-rate branching (LRB), which reframes variable selection as an online multi-armed bandit problem. We explain literal block distance (LBD) as a static predictor of learnt-clause quality and aggressive clause-database reduction. Finally, we situate CDCL within proof complexity: CDCL with restarts polynomially simulates general resolution, inheriting exponential lower bounds on formulas such as the pigeonhole principle. We conclude with empirical evidence from SAT competitions and a discussion of open limitations.

## 1. Introduction

The propositional satisfiability problem (SAT) — deciding whether a Boolean formula in conjunctive normal form (CNF) admits a satisfying assignment — is the canonical NP-complete problem. Despite its theoretical intractability, SAT solvers routinely decide industrial instances with millions of variables and clauses, underpinning hardware verification, bounded model checking, planning, and combinatorial optimization [9]. This practical triumph is almost entirely attributable to a single algorithmic paradigm: **conflict-driven clause learning** (CDCL).

The story begins with the Davis–Putnam procedure (1960) and its refinement by Davis, Logemann, and Loveland (1962), the DPLL algorithm [5], which couples backtracking search with unit propagation. DPLL solvers dominated for three decades but scale poorly on structured industrial instances. The decisive break came with GRASP (Marques-Silva and Sakallah, 1996/1999) [10], which introduced conflict analysis and non-chronological backtracking — learning from mistakes instead of merely retrying. The zChaff solver (Moskewicz et al., 2001) [1] then fused clause learning with two engineering breakthroughs — the *two-watched-literal* scheme and the *VSIDS* branching heuristic — yielding order-of-magnitude speedups that redefined the field.

This article reconstructs the complete anatomy of a modern CDCL solver. Our contributions are expository but deep: we give a precise account of the first-UIP learning scheme as iterated resolution over an implication graph [6]; we explain why VSIDS's additive bump and multiplicative decay constitute an exponential moving average that tracks conflict locality, and how LRB generalizes this into a principled online-learning framework [3]; we describe LBD-based clause quality prediction [5 in ref numbering: Audemard & Simon 2009]; and we connect solver behavior to proof complexity, where CDCL with restarts is polynomially equivalent to general resolution [6] and hence subject to exponential lower bounds [7][8].

> **Thesis statement:** Modern SAT solving is best understood not as search but as *proof search*: every conflict analysis derives a resolution refutation step, every learnt clause is a lemma, and the branching heuristic is an online policy for selecting which subproofs to attempt next.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Propositional Logic and CNF

A *literal* is a Boolean variable $x_i$ or its negation $\lnot x_i$. A *clause* is a disjunction of literals; a CNF formula $F$ is a conjunction of clauses. An *assignment* maps variables to $\{\top, \bot\}$. A clause is *satisfied* if any literal is true, *falsified* (a *conflict*) if all literals are false, and *unit* if all but one literal are false — forcing the remaining literal to true. The forcing rule is **unit propagation**, also called Boolean constraint propagation (BCP).

A *partial assignment* (or *trail*) is a sequence of assigned literals, partitioned into *decision levels*: level $0$ holds top-level propagated literals, and each *decision* literal $d$ starts a new level containing $d$ plus all literals it implies.

### 2.2 DPLL and Resolution

DPLL is depth-first search with propagation:

1. **Decide**: pick an unassigned variable and assign it (a *decision*).
2. **Propagate**: apply unit propagation until fixpoint or conflict.
3. **Backtrack**: on conflict, undo the last decision and flip it.

The *resolution* rule — from $(A \lor x)$ and $(B \lor \lnot x)$ derive $(A \lor B)$ — is the logical engine underneath. A *resolution refutation* of $F$ is a sequence of clauses ending in the empty clause $\square$, each clause either in $F$ or a resolvent of earlier ones. DPLL's search tree corresponds exactly to a *tree-like* resolution proof; this correspondence is the entry point of proof complexity into solver design.

### 2.3 The Implication Graph

During propagation, each implied literal $l$ records its *antecedent clause* — the clause that became unit and forced $l$. The **implication graph** is the directed acyclic graph whose vertices are assigned literals (plus a special conflict node $\kappa$) and whose edges connect each antecedent literal to the literal it implied. The conflict node has incoming edges from all literals of the falsified clause. This graph is the data structure on which all clause learning operates.

## 3. Methodology

We now present the CDCL architecture as implemented in solvers such as MiniSat [2] and Glucose. The main loop is:

```python
def cdcl(F):
    trail, dl = [], 0          # trail of assignments, current decision level
    learnt = []                # learnt clause database
    while True:
        conflict = propagate(F, learnt, trail)   # BCP with two watched literals
        if conflict is not None:
            if dl == 0:
                return UNSAT    # conflict at level 0: no decisions to undo
            C, blevel = analyze(conflict, trail)  # first-UIP resolution
            learnt.append(C)
            bump_vsids(C)       # activity update for branching heuristic
            backjump(blevel)    # non-chronological backtrack
            if restart_due():
                backjump(0)     # restart: keep learnt clauses, drop trail
        else:
            if all_assigned(F):
                return SAT
            v = pick_branch_variable()  # VSIDS / LRB
            decide(v, phase_saving(v)); dl += 1
```

Three components demand detailed treatment: (i) the propagation engine, (ii) conflict analysis and learning, and (iii) the branching heuristic. We formalize each below and dissect them in §4.

**Two-watched literals** [1] make BCP effectively lazy. Each clause watches two of its literals; the clause is inspected only when one of its watched literals becomes false. Invariants guarantee that if a clause is unit, at least one watched literal is unassigned, so no clause scan is needed on every assignment. The core propagation step is:

```rust
fn propagate(clause_db: &mut Vec<Clause>, trail: &mut Trail) -> Option<ClauseId> {
    while let Some(lit) = trail.pending() {
        // examine only clauses watching the negation of `lit`
        for cid in watchers[lit.neg()] {
            let c = &mut clause_db[cid];
            // try to find a new non-falsified literal to watch instead
            if c.try_rewatch(trail) { continue; }
            let other = c.other_watched(lit.neg());
            if trail.is_false(other) {
                return Some(cid);          // conflict clause
            } else if trail.is_unassigned(other) {
                trail.enqueue(other, cid); // unit propagation
            }
        }
    }
    None
}
```

Because backtracking never requires touching clauses — watches are simply left in place, and the invariant is restored for free — BCP achieves the near-constant amortized cost that makes CDCL viable at scale.

**Conflict analysis** resolves the conflict clause with antecedent clauses backwards along the implication graph until a *cut* is reached; the negation of the literals on the reason side of the cut forms the learnt clause. The choice of cut — the *learning scheme* — determines everything that follows. The industry standard is the **first unique implication point (first-UIP)** scheme [6 in refs: Zhang et al. 2001].

**Restarts** periodically reset the trail to level $0$ while retaining learnt clauses and heuristic scores. Because learnt clauses accumulate monotonically, restarts re-focus search using everything learned so far, and they are required for CDCL's full proof-theoretic power (see §5).

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Resolution-Based Clause Learning and the First-UIP Scheme

Let the conflict occur at decision level $d$. A **unique implication point (UIP)** at level $d$ is a literal $u$ assigned at level $d$ that dominates the conflict node $\kappa$ in the implication graph — every path from the decision literal of level $d$ to $\kappa$ passes through $u$. The decision literal itself is always a UIP. The **first UIP** is the UIP closest to the conflict, i.e., the one encountered first when walking backwards from $\kappa$.

The first-UIP scheme performs a sequence of resolution steps: starting from the conflict clause $C_0$, repeatedly resolve the current clause $C_i$ with the antecedent clause of the most recently assigned level-$d$ literal in $C_i$ (other than the UIP), producing $C_{i+1}$. The process stops when exactly one level-$d$ literal remains — the first UIP. The learnt clause is then $C^* = \lnot u \lor \bigvee_{l} \lnot l$, where $u$ is the first UIP and the remaining literals are at levels $< d$.

> **Theorem:** The first-UIP learnt clause is an *asserting clause*: after backjumping to the second-highest decision level appearing in $C^*$, the clause is unit and immediately propagates the negation of the first UIP. Moreover, $C^*$ is a logical consequence of $F$ (it is derived purely by resolution), so learning it preserves satisfiability.

Why first-UIP rather than alternatives? The classical schemes are compared below:

| Learning scheme | Cut position | Clause size | Asserting? | Empirical standing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Decision (rel_sat) | All decision literals | Large | Yes | Obsolete |
| All-UIP | After the last UIP | Small | Yes | Rarely best |
| **First-UIP** | After the first UIP | Small–medium | Yes | **Standard** |
| Last-UIP (GRASP) | After decision literal | Medium | Yes | Obsolete |

Zhang et al. [6] showed empirically that first-UIP learns clauses that are simultaneously short, asserting at low levels, and cheap to compute — a combination no other scheme matches. MiniSat and all descendants use it exclusively.

### 4.2 The Implication Graph and Conflict Analysis

Consider the implication graph at the moment of conflict. Each node carries a decision level; edges carry antecedent justifications. Conflict analysis is a graph traversal: maintain a counter of "open" level-$d$ literals in the current resolvent, initialized to the number of level-$d$ literals in the conflict clause. Repeatedly pick the most recently assigned open literal $l$ (by trail order), resolve with its antecedent, and decrement the counter for each resolved literal while incrementing for newly introduced level-$d$ literals. When the counter reaches $1$, the remaining level-$d$ literal is the first UIP.

Two refinements materially improve learnt clauses:

1. **Clause minimization** (self-subsuming resolution): literals whose negations are implied by the remaining clause via the implication graph can be dropped, often shrinking learnt clauses by 20–40%.
2. **Literal Block Distance (LBD)** [5]: the number of distinct decision levels in the learnt clause. Clauses with small LBD — especially LBD $= 2$, so-called *glue clauses* — are empirically far more useful: they "glue" together a small set of decision blocks and are protected from deletion during database reduction.

### 4.3 VSIDS: Additive Bump, Multiplicative Decay, and ERWA

The **Variable State Independent Decaying Sum (VSIDS)** heuristic [1] maintains one floating-point *activity* per variable. On learning a clause, the activities of variables involved in conflict analysis are *bumped* (additively incremented); periodically, all activities are *decayed* (multiplied by $0 < \alpha < 1$, typically $0.95$). The solver branches on the unassigned variable of maximal activity, with polarity chosen by *phase saving* (remembering each variable's last assigned value) [2].

Liang et al. [4] provided the key theoretical insight: VSIDS's bump-and-decay dynamics are exactly an **exponential moving average** over conflict participation. Unrolling the recurrence, the activity of $v$ after $t$ conflicts is $\sum_i \delta_{v,i} \, \alpha^{t - i}$, where $\delta_{v,i} = 1$ if $v$ participated in conflict $i$. Variables involved in *recent* conflicts dominate — the heuristic chases the moving frontier of the search, exploiting the *locality* of industrial instances.

This understanding yielded the **exponential recency weighted average (ERWA)** generalization [4], which replaces the fixed decay with an adaptive scheme. The crucial empirical finding: the *quality* of a branching heuristic can be measured by how well its ranking correlates with variables' future conflict participation, and adaptive decay (adaptVSIDS) solved 2.4% more SAT Competition 2013 instances than MiniSat-style mVSIDS [4].

```python
# EVSIDS: equivalent formulation used in MiniSat (avoids touching all variables)
var_inc = 1.0
def bump(var):
    activity[var] += var_inc
    if activity[var] > 1e100:      # rescale to avoid overflow
        for v in all_vars: activity[v] *= 1e-100
        var_inc *= 1e-100
def decay():
    global var_inc
    var_inc *= 1.0 / 0.95          # increasing the bump == decaying the past
```

### 4.4 Learning Rate Branching and the Multi-Armed Bandit View

**Learning rate branching (LRB)** [3] reframes the entire problem. Instead of asking "which variable was recently active?", it asks "branching on which variable *causes* the solver to learn fastest?" Each variable is an arm of a multi-armed bandit; pulling the arm means branching on the variable, and the reward is the variable's *learning rate* — the ratio of conflicts it participates in to propagations it triggers. LRB maintains an exponential recency weighted average of this reward per variable and branches on the maximizer.

The differences from VSIDS are instructive:

| Aspect | VSIDS | LRB |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | Conflict participation (binary) | Learning rate (conflicts/propagations) |
| Update trigger | Every conflict | Every conflict, only for assigned vars |
| Model | Exponential moving average | Multi-armed bandit (exploration/exploitation) |
| Strength | Locality exploitation, cheap | Better on crafted/combinatorial instances |

MapleSAT, which combined LRB with a VSIDS fallback, won the SAT Competition 2016 main track — the first time a non-pure-VSIDS solver took the crown in years, validating the bandit perspective [3].

### 4.5 Restarts, LBD, and Clause Database Management

Learnt clauses accumulate without bound, so solvers periodically *reduce* the database. Glucose [5] revolutionized this with LBD: clauses are sorted by LBD, and roughly half with the worst (highest) LBD are deleted, while glue clauses (LBD $\le 2$) are kept forever. Glucose further introduced *dynamic restarts* driven by the moving average of LBDs of recent learnt clauses — when recent clauses are low-quality (high LBD), the solver restarts aggressively, based on the hypothesis that the current search region is unproductive.

Restart policies in common use:

| Policy | Schedule | Used by |
|---|---|---|
| Geometric | $100 \times 1.5^k$ conflicts | Early MiniSat |
| Luby | $1,1,2,1,1,2,4,\dots \times$ unit | MiniSat 2.2 default |
| Nested / inner-outer | Luby within geometric | Modern MiniSat |
| Dynamic (LBD-based) | Restart when recent LBD average spikes | Glucose, MapleSAT |

*Phase saving* deserves emphasis: on restart or backjump, each variable remembers its last polarity, and decisions reuse it. This seemingly minor optimization was measured to contribute double-digit percentage speedups [2], because it preserves the "semantic neighborhood" of the previous search — the solver re-descends into a similar region but armed with new learnt clauses.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The Proof Complexity of CDCL

The deepest theoretical result about CDCL concerns its *proof-theoretic power*. Beame, Kautz, and Sabharwal (2004) first showed that clause learning with unlimited restarts can simulate general resolution. Pipatsrisawat and Darwiche [6] sharpened this into an equivalence:

> **Theorem (Pipatsrisawat & Darwiche, 2011):** CDCL SAT solvers, equipped with arbitrary clause learning schemes and restarts, are polynomially equivalent to general resolution as proof systems. Every resolution refutation can be simulated by a CDCL run with polynomial overhead, and every CDCL run corresponds to a resolution proof.

The "with restarts" qualifier is essential: without restarts, CDCL corresponds to a strictly weaker system (regular resolution variants), and pathological families separate them. In practice, solvers restart aggressively, so the full power of resolution is available.

But resolution itself has limits. Two landmark lower bounds transfer directly to CDCL:

> **Theorem (Haken, 1985):** Any resolution refutation of the pigeonhole principle $PHP^{n+1}_n$ requires size $2^{\Omega(n)}$ [8].

> **Theorem (Ben-Sasson & Wigderson, 2001):** If the shortest resolution refutation of $F$ requires width $w$, it requires size $\exp(\Omega((w - w_0)^2/n))$, where $w_0$ is the initial width [7]. Short proofs must be narrow.

Consequently, *no* CDCL solver — regardless of heuristic — can refute pigeonhole or random 3-SAT instances at the phase transition in subexponential time. These are not engineering gaps; they are mathematical impossibilities. The practical success of CDCL is therefore a statement about *industrial* instances: verification and planning encodings possess the hidden structure (bounded width, locality) that CDCL exploits.

### 5.2 Empirical Evidence

The SAT Competitions provide the controlled empirical record:

- **Chaff (2001)** [1]: 1–2 orders of magnitude faster than GRASP-era solvers on bounded-model-checking instances, driven by two-watched literals and VSIDS.
- **MiniSat (2003–2005)** [2]: won the SAT 2005 competition; its ~600-line core made CDCL's anatomy legible and became the template for a decade of solvers.
- **Glucose (2009)** [5]: LBD-based clause management solved an order of magnitude more UNSAT industrial instances than MiniSat, and its dynamic restart policy became standard.
- **MapleSAT (2016)** [3]: LRB branching won the SAT 2016 main track, demonstrating that the VSIDS monopoly was breakable.
- **adaptVSIDS** [4]: adaptive decay solved 2.4% more SAT Competition 2013 benchmarks than mVSIDS, confirming the exponential-moving-average theory.

| Solver | Year | Key innovation | Competition result |
|---|---|---|---|
| GRASP | 1996 | Conflict analysis, non-chronological backtracking | Pre-competition era |
| Chaff | 2001 | Two-watched literals, VSIDS | Order-of-magnitude leap |
| MiniSat | 2003 | Minimalist CDCL, phase saving | SAT 2005 winner |
| Glucose | 2009 | LBD, dynamic restarts | Dominated UNSAT tracks |
| MapleSAT | 2016 | LRB branching | SAT 2016 main track winner |

## 6. Limitations

1. **Proof-complexity ceilings.** As §5.1 establishes, CDCL inherits resolution's exponential lower bounds [7][8]. On pigeonhole, Tseitin, and random $k$-SAT instances, every CDCL solver necessarily takes exponential time. Stronger proof systems (extended resolution, cutting planes) are exponentially more powerful on some families, but no practical solver implements them at scale.
2. **Heuristic brittleness.** VSIDS and LRB are *online* policies with no convergence guarantees; on instances lacking locality, they can thrash. The multi-armed bandit view [3] improves matters but introduces its own hyperparameters (reward smoothing, exploration rate).
3. **Restart incompleteness of the theory.** The equivalence to resolution requires *unlimited* restarts [6]; real solvers use heuristic restart schedules, and the gap between theory and practice here is poorly understood.
4. **Clause database pressure.** Even with LBD-based reduction [5], learnt clauses can number in the millions, and BCP — still the dominant cost — slows as the watcher lists grow. Aggressive deletion risks discarding clauses needed later; conservative retention risks memory blowup.
5. **Parallel CDCL.** Portfolio and divide-and-conquer parallel solvers share learnt clauses across workers, but clause-sharing policies remain heuristic, and superlinear speedups are the exception. The proof-complexity picture for parallel CDCL is essentially open.
6. **Certification overhead.** Industrial users demand *proofs* of unsatisfiability (DRAT format), but proof logging can dominate runtime and produce terabyte-scale certificates, creating a verification bottleneck downstream.

## 7. Conclusion

Conflict-driven clause learning transformed SAT from a theoretical curiosity into an industrial workhorse by fusing three ideas: *lazy propagation* (two-watched literals) that made BCP nearly free [1]; *resolution-based learning* (first-UIP) that converts every conflict into a reusable lemma; and *adaptive branching* (VSIDS, LRB) that treats variable selection as online learning over the conflict stream [3][4]. Proof complexity explains both the power — CDCL with restarts equals general resolution [6] — and the limits — resolution's exponential lower bounds are CDCL's lower bounds [7][8]. The open frontier is a better *policy*: branching heuristics, restart schedules, and clause-sharing strategies that are provably near-optimal rather than merely empirically tuned.
