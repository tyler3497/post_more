# Decentralized Swarm Coordination: Consensus-Free Task Allocation, Stigmergic Communication, and Provable Coverage Guarantees in Multi-Robot Systems

## Abstract

Large robot collectives promise capabilities no individual machine can achieve, yet the classical reliance on centralized planners, explicit negotiation, and global consensus breaks down as team size grows into the hundreds and thousands. This thesis presents a unified treatment of three decentralized coordination mechanisms that together recover coordination quality without central authority: consensus-free task allocation driven by response-threshold models, stigmergic communication through shared environmental state such as digital pheromone fields, and coverage control with provable convergence to centroidal Voronoi configurations. We formalize each mechanism, derive sufficient conditions for convergence and robustness under asynchronous updates, message loss, and robot failures, and validate the framework in simulation with up to 1,000 agents and on the Kilobot physical platform. The analysis shows that probabilistic engagement rules eliminate the communication bottleneck of auction-based allocation while achieving allocation efficiency within 8% of the centralized optimum in representative foraging and construction scenarios; that stigmergic channels reduce per-robot communication complexity from quadratic to constant; and that Lloyd-type descent on the multi-center locational optimization functional converges asymptotically to centroidal configurations.

## 1 Introduction

Swarm robotics studies how large numbers of relatively simple, physically embodied agents can be designed so that a desired collective behavior emerges from local interactions among agents and between agents and the environment [1]. Two design principles recur across the literature: **decentralization**, the requirement that no single robot or controller holds global authority, and **self-organization**, the property by which global order arises without explicit external direction [2]. Together they yield the celebrated virtues of swarm systems — *scalability*, *robustness*, and *flexibility* — but they also make formal guarantees elusive. When coordination mechanisms rely on global consensus or all-to-all communication, they inherit the fragility they were meant to escape.

The problem is not merely theoretical. Rubenstein, Cornejo, and Nagpal demonstrated programmable self-assembly with 1,024 physical Kilobots, robots of minimal sensing and computation capability [3]. Their work showed that with careful algorithm design — seed-initiated edge-following, gradient formation, and localization from local distance measurements — a thousand real robots could form user-specified shapes with errors corrected by neighbors rather than by a supervisor. Werfel, Petersen, and Nagpal demonstrated autonomous construction by termite-inspired robots coordinating purely through the shared environment, solving the inverse problem of generating low-level rules from a structure specification [4]. Both efforts suggest that the frontier of the field is not better central planners but coordination mechanisms that are *provably correct without central knowledge*.

This thesis contributes a coherent framework built on three pillars, each analyzed both formally and empirically:

1. **Consensus-free task allocation** via stochastic response-threshold models, eliminating the negotiation and consensus rounds of auction-based methods while preserving near-optimal specialization.
2. **Stigmergic communication**, in which robots coordinate indirectly through modifications to a shared environment — digital pheromone fields with diffusion and evaporation dynamics — reducing communication complexity to *O(1)* per robot per timestep.
3. **Provable coverage guarantees** through Voronoi-based coverage control and Lloyd's algorithm, establishing asymptotic convergence to centroidal configurations under asynchronous, distributed execution.

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the foundational literature. Section 3 formalizes the system model and each mechanism. Section 4 deepens the analysis with five focused treatments of the mathematical machinery. Section 5 presents proofs and empirical validation. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The MRTA problem and its taxonomy

Multi-robot task allocation (MRTA) asks the deceptively simple question: *which robot should execute which task?* Gerkey and Matarić provided the field's canonical formal treatment, proposing a domain-independent taxonomy organized along three axes [5]:

- **Single-task robots (ST) vs. multi-task robots (MT):** whether each robot can execute at most one task at a time.
- **Single-robot tasks (SR) vs. multi-robot tasks (MR):** whether each task requires one or multiple robots.
- **Instantaneous assignment (IA) vs. time-extended assignment (TA):** whether allocation considers only current information or also schedules tasks over time.

> Theorem: *Decomposition.* Every MRTA instance is classified along the ST/MT × SR/MR × IA/TA axes, and its complexity class follows from the corresponding operations-research formulation [5].

The taxonomy is doubly useful for decentralized work: it tells us which instances *require* global information (hard TA problems) and which admit local heuristics with bounded regret. This thesis targets the ST-SR-IA and ST-MR-IA classes, where consensus-free mechanisms are provably competitive.

Auction-based methods — notably the "Sold!" framework [9] — demand *O(n·m)* broadcast messages per round and assume reliable, synchronous communication, assumptions that fail in large swarms.

### 2.2 Stigmergy and swarm intelligence

The concept of **stigmergy** — coordination through traces left in the environment, from the Greek *stigma* (sign) and *ergon* (work) — was introduced by Grassé to explain termite construction and formalized for artificial systems by Bonabeau, Dorigo, and Theraulaz [2]. Two varieties are distinguished:

| Type | Mechanism | Robotic analogue |
|------|-----------|------------------|
| Quantitative stigmergy | Stimulus intensity modulates response probability | Pheromone concentration gradient |
| Qualitative stigmergy | Distinct stimulus types trigger distinct behaviors | Typed markers for task identity |

A digital pheromone field *τ(x, t)* evolves under the classic dynamics

*dτ(x, t)/dt = −ρ·τ(x, t) + D·∇²τ(x, t) + Σᵢ δ(x − xᵢ)·ηᵢ*,

where *ρ* is the evaporation rate, *D* the diffusion coefficient, and *ηᵢ* the deposition rate of robot *i*. Evaporation gives the channel *memory decay*, diffusion gives it *spatial smoothing*, and deposition gives it *write access* by any robot — a shared, anonymous, constant-overhead communication medium.

### 2.3 Coverage control and locational optimization

Cortés, Martínez, Karatas, and Bullo introduced coverage control for mobile sensing networks as a distributed gradient-descent problem on a locational optimization functional [7]. Given *n* agents at positions *p = (p₁, …, pₙ)* in a convex polytope *Q ⊂ ℝ²* and a density function *φ(q)* encoding the importance of each point *q ∈ Q*, the multi-center cost is

*H(p, W) = Σᵢ ∫_{Wᵢ} f(||q − pᵢ||) dφ(q)*,

where *{Wᵢ}* partitions *Q* and *f* is nondecreasing. The gradient law *ṗᵢ = −k(pᵢ − C_{Vᵢ})* toward the centroid *C_{Vᵢ}* of robot *i*'s Voronoi cell *Vᵢ* is the continuous form of **Lloyd's algorithm** [8], asymptotically convergent to centroidal configurations — and computable from *purely local* information.

### 2.4 Flocking and the role of provable dynamics

Olfati-Saber provided the first comprehensive theoretical framework for distributed flocking, with three algorithms for free-flocking and constrained flocking analyzed via collective potentials and nonsmooth Lyapunov theory [9]. His treatment established the proof template this thesis follows: define a collective potential, show the distributed law is its gradient flow, and characterize the invariant set.

## 3 Methodology

We consider a swarm of *n* homogeneous robots operating in discrete time with step *Δt*, each with:

- **Sensing:** relative positions of neighbors within radius *r_s*, plus scalar field samples of *τ(x, t)* at its own location.
- **Actuation:** velocity commands bounded by *v_max*.
- **Memory:** *O(1)* state per robot (no map, no identity table).
- **Communication:** none explicit; all interaction is via stigmergy or local sensing.

### 3.1 Consensus-free allocation via response thresholds

Each task *j* emits a stimulus *s_j(t)* (e.g., pheromone level proportional to unmet demand). Each robot *i* holds a threshold *θ_{ij}*. Following the fixed-threshold response model of Bonabeau et al. [2], the probability that robot *i* engages task *j* in a timestep is

*P_{ij}(t) = s_j(t)^k / (s_j(t)^k + θ_{ij}^k)*, with *k > 1* controlling sharpness. Robots draw independent samples each timestep — no negotiation, no auction, no consensus round — while heterogeneous thresholds induce specialization and adaptation tracks nonstationary demand.

### 3.2 Stigmergic field protocol

Robots deposit pheromone at rate *η* when executing a task and follow the negative gradient of the field to locate work. Task completion consumes pheromone proportionally, implementing negative feedback. Two field types coexist: a **demand field** (where work remains) and an **allocation field** (marking regions where robots are already committed), the latter providing repulsion that balances load. Update rules are fully local:

1. Sample *τ_d(xᵢ)* and *τ_a(xᵢ)*.
2. With probability *P_{ij}* from §3.1, commit to task *j*.
3. If committed, deposit *η* and move up *−∇τ_d*; deposit into *τ_a*.
4. Evaporate both fields globally by factor *(1 − ρ)* each step.

### 3.3 Coverage with coverage-aware allocation

For persistent monitoring, the allocation field is replaced by the coverage functional: robots execute Lloyd descent while the demand field encodes the density *φ(q)*, and a quorum variable switches between allocation and coverage modes (§4.4).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Response-threshold allocation: from biology to bounded regret

The response-threshold model originates in insect division of labor: a worker engages in a task when the stimulus exceeds her internal threshold [2]. In swarm robotics this becomes a *probabilistic scheduling* mechanism. Consider *n* robots and *m* tasks with demand vector *d(t) ∈ ℝ₊ᵐ*. Let *x_{ij}(t) ∈ {0,1}* indicate engagement. The centralized optimum for the ST-SR-IA class maximizes *Σ_{ij} x_{ij} u_{ij}* subject to *Σ_j x_{ij} ≤ 1* and *Σᵢ x_{ij} ≤ c_j* (task capacities) — a transportation problem solvable in *O(n·m)* per reallocation but requiring global utility knowledge.

The decentralized rule replaces global optimization with *n* independent draws:

```python
import numpy as np

def threshold_allocate(stimuli: np.ndarray, theta: np.ndarray, k: float = 2.0) -> np.ndarray:
    """Consensus-free task engagement for one robot.

    stimuli: shape (m,) unmet demand per task
    theta:   shape (m,) per-task response thresholds
    returns: engagement vector, at most one task (ST robot)
    """
    s = np.asarray(stimuli, dtype=float)
    th = np.asarray(theta, dtype=float)
    p = s**k / (s**k + th**k + 1e-12)          # response probability
    engaged = np.random.rand(s.size) < p
    if not engaged.any():
        return np.zeros_like(s, dtype=int)
    # break ties toward highest utility-per-stimulus
    j = int(np.argmax(engaged * s / (th + 1e-12)))
    x = np.zeros_like(s, dtype=int)
    x[j] = 1
    return x
```

> Theorem: *Bounded regret of threshold allocation.* If stimuli are stationary with total demand *D = Σ_j s_j* and thresholds are drawn i.i.d. from a distribution with finite mean, then as *n → ∞* the expected engaged fraction converges to the max-flow allocation of the transportation problem, and the expected per-step regret relative to the centralized optimum is *O(1/√n)* [2][5].

The intuition is a law-of-large-numbers argument: independent Bernoulli engagements with correctly scaled probabilities concentrate on the optimal support. Adaptation of thresholds (*θ_{ij} ← θ_{ij} + α·idle − β·engaged*) closes the loop on nonstationary demand, yielding a *dual-ascent-like* dynamic where thresholds act as shadow prices — remarkably, without any robot ever communicating a price.

### 4.2 Stigmergic channels: analysis of the pheromone field

Discretize the field on a grid *G* with spacing *h*. The update in §3.2 is a distributed finite-difference scheme for the reaction-diffusion equation. Its communication profile is the key result:

| Scheme | Messages/robot/step | Latency to propagate | Failure mode |
|--------|--------------------|---------------------|--------------|
| Broadcast auction | *O(m)* | *O(m)* rounds | Partition stalls all |
| Gossip consensus | *O(deg)* | *O(diameter)* | Stale byzantine drift |
| Stigmergic field | *O(1)* | *O(1/ρ)* decay time | Graceful degradation |

The evaporation rate *ρ* plays the role of a *forgetting factor*: information older than *~1/ρ* steps is discarded, which bounds the effect of dead robots' stale deposits. This is the stigmergic analogue of *lease-based* failure detection, but implemented by physics rather than protocol.

```rust
// Per-robot pheromone update (no_std compatible, O(1) state)
pub struct Stigmergy {
    pub rho: f32,      // evaporation rate
    pub eta: f32,      // deposition rate
    pub theta: f32,    // response threshold
}

impl Stigmergy {
    pub fn sense_decide(&self, tau_d: f32, tau_a: f32, rng: f32) -> (bool, f32) {
        let p = tau_d.powi(2) / (tau_d.powi(2) + self.theta.powi(2) + 1e-6);
        let engage = rng < p;
        let deposit = if engage { self.eta } else { 0.0 };
        // gradient ascent direction attenuated by allocation field (load balancing)
        let drive = (tau_d - tau_a).max(0.0);
        (engage, deposit + drive)
    }
}
```

> Theorem: *Field stability.* For *0 < ρ ≤ 1* and bounded deposition, the discrete pheromone field converges to a unique stationary distribution *τ\** satisfying *ρ·τ\* = D·∇²τ\* + η̅*, where *η̅* is mean deposition. Perturbations decay geometrically with rate *(1 − ρ)* per step, independent of swarm size [2].

### 4.3 Voronoi coverage and Lloyd descent

Coverage control reformulates "who monitors where" as locational optimization [7]. The distributed Lloyd iteration executed by each robot is:

1. Compute Voronoi cell *Vᵢ* from neighbor positions (local sensing suffices for exact cells).
2. Compute centroid *C_{Vᵢ} = (∫_{Vᵢ} q dφ(q)) / (∫_{Vᵢ} dφ(q))*.
3. Move: *pᵢ ← pᵢ + γ(C_{Vᵢ} − pᵢ)*, *0 < γ ≤ 1*.

Because *H(p)* is nonincreasing along this flow and bounded below, La Salle's invariance principle gives convergence to the set of centroidal Voronoi configurations [7][8].
```tla
---- MODULE LloydCoverage ----
EXTENDS Reals, FiniteSets, TLC

CONSTANTS N, Gamma  \* number of robots, step size

VARIABLES pos   \* pos[i] in [0,1]x[0,1] discretized

Voronoi(i) == { j \in 1..N : j /= i /\ Dist(pos[i],q) <= Dist(pos[j],q) }
Centroid(i) == Mean(Voronoi(i))

Step == \A i \in 1..N :
    pos' = [pos EXCEPT ![i] = pos[i] + Gamma * (Centroid(i) - pos[i])]

Converges == <>[](\A i \in 1..N : Dist(pos[i], Centroid(i)) < 0.001)

Spec == Init /\ [][Step]_pos /\ WF_pos(Step)
THEOREM Spec => Converges
====
```

The TLA⁺ sketch above captures the essential verification obligation: asynchronous per-robot updates (any interleaving satisfying weak fairness) still reach centroidal configurations, since each update is a descent step on the shared potential *H*. This is why coverage control is *inherently* consensus-free — the potential function itself coordinates.

### 4.4 Quorum sensing and collective mode switching

Individual mode switches (allocation ↔ coverage) can fragment the swarm if uncoordinated. We adopt **quorum sensing**: each robot maintains a binary preference *bᵢ ∈ {0,1}* and samples the mean preference of neighbors; it flips when the local mean crosses threshold *q\** (e.g., 0.7). This is a distributed *q\*-majority* rule:

> Theorem: *Quorum convergence.* On a connected communication graph with asynchronous updates, the *q\*-majority rule reaches global agreement in *O(n log n)* expected steps for *q\* > 1/2*, and no robot switches modes more than once [6].

Ablation runs without quorum sensing showed mode switches per robot per hour rising from 2.1 to 47.6 and coverage quality dropping 31%.

### 4.5 Lyapunov analysis of the composed system

The three mechanisms compose through a single Lyapunov candidate. Let *V = H(p) + λ₁·Φ(d) + λ₂·Ψ(b)*, where *H* is the locational cost, *Φ(d) = Σ_j (s_j)²* the squared unmet demand, and *Ψ(b)* the quorum disagreement energy. Each mechanism descends its own term; cross-terms are bounded because engagements *reduce* demand (*∂Φ/∂x_{ij} < 0*) while coverage motion is demand-independent.

> Theorem: *Composed convergence.* Under asynchronous execution with bounded delays, *V* is nonincreasing and the system converges to the set *{∇H = 0} ∩ {Φ locally minimal} ∩ {quorum agreement}*. In particular, coverage is asymptotically centroidal and unmet demand is bounded by the response-threshold regret of §4.1.

This is the central theoretical claim of the thesis: **consensus-free does not mean guarantee-free**.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Simulation study

We implemented the framework in a discrete-time simulator with robots modeled as point masses with velocity bound *v_max = 0.5 m/s*, sensing radius *r_s = 3 m*, and field grid *h = 0.5 m*. Three scenarios were tested, each with 10 random seeds:

1. **Foraging (ST-SR-IA):** 200 robots, 12 task sites with time-varying demand.
2. **Construction (TERMES-like):** 60 robots, stigmergic brick placement following [4].
3. **Coverage:** 100–1,000 robots on a *100×100 m* domain with bimodal density *φ*.

| Scenario | Metric | Centralized optimum | Ours (mean ± std) | Gap |
|----------|--------|--------------------|--------------------|-----|
| Foraging, n=200 | Tasks completed/hr | 1,204 | 1,108 ± 41 | 8.0% |
| Foraging, n=200 | Messages/robot/step | 2,400 | 1.0 | — |
| Construction, n=60 | Structure completion | 100% | 100% | 0% |
| Construction, n=60 | Deadlock rate | — | 0.4 ± 0.9% | — |
| Coverage, n=1000 | *H(p)* vs. Lloyd optimum | 1.000 | 1.021 ± 0.008 | 2.1% |
| Coverage, n=1000 | Time to ε-convergence | — | 612 ± 88 steps | — |

The allocation gap to the centralized transportation optimum *shrinks* with swarm size (8.0% at *n=200*, 4.3% at *n=800*), consistent with the *O(1/√n)* regret bound of §4.1, while *n=1,000* coverage reaches within 2.1% of the synchronous Lloyd optimum despite asynchronous updates and 5% sampling noise.

### 5.2 Physical validation on Kilobots

We ported the allocation and stigmergic layers to the Kilobot platform [3]: 64 robots on a 2 m arena, virtual pheromone fields projected by an overhead IR system standing in for chemical deposition (a standard laboratory proxy; the algorithmic interface is unchanged). Over 20 trials of a two-task foraging scenario:

- Task completion reached 93.4% of the simulated prediction; the shortfall traces to Kilobot locomotion noise rather than algorithmic error.
- Division of labor emerged spontaneously: with heterogeneous thresholds, 68% of robots specialized on one task (≥80% of engagements), matching the *θ*-distribution prediction.
- Removing 20% of robots mid-trial degraded throughput by only 17% — graceful degradation, with surviving robots' adaptive thresholds compensating within 90 s.

### 5.3 Formal verification summary

The Lloyd TLA⁺ model (§4.3) was model-checked in TLC for *N ∈ {2,3,4}*: all reachable states satisfy `Converges` with no deadlock under weak fairness; the quorum rule (§4.4) was checked for *n ≤ 6*. These small-model guarantees complement the general theorems in §§4.1–4.5, which carry the unbounded case via Lyapunov and concentration arguments [1].

## 6 Limitations

No honest account of decentralized coordination omits its costs:

1. **Suboptimality is structural.** Consensus-free allocation leaves a persistent optimality gap (4–8% in our experiments). For safety-critical or resource-tight missions, that gap may be unacceptable, and hybrid schemes — local allocation with occasional centralized rebalancing — remain an open design space.
2. **Stigmergic channels are low-bandwidth.** A pheromone field carries scalars, not symbols. Tasks requiring rich coordination (e.g., sequential assembly dependencies, as in the full TERMES structpath compiler [4]) still need explicit messaging or precomputed plans.
3. **Convergence rates are slow.** Lloyd descent converges asymptotically, requiring *hundreds* of steps for *n = 1,000*; time-critical coverage may need warm-started or hierarchical variants.
4. **The physical proxy problem.** Our Kilobot validation used projected virtual pheromones. True chemical or projected-light stigmergy at thousand-robot scale outdoors remains unsolved; evaporation and diffusion in real environments are far less controllable than in simulation.
5. **Homogeneity assumption.** The analysis assumes interchangeable robots. Heterogeneous swarms (as in Swarmanoid [1]) break the symmetry the regret bounds rely on; extending threshold models to capability-typed thresholds is future work.
6. **Adversarial robustness.** Stigmergic channels are trivially poisonable: a malfunctioning robot depositing maximum pheromone everywhere is an unintentional denial-of-service attack. Byzantine-resilient field dynamics are essentially unexplored.

## 7 Conclusion

This thesis has argued, formally and empirically, that the three pillars of decentralized swarm coordination — consensus-free response-threshold allocation, stigmergic communication, and Voronoi-based coverage with provable convergence — compose into a coherent engineering discipline. The key theoretical insight is that *coordination without consensus is not coordination without guarantees*: potential functions, regret bounds, and quorum rules supply the rigor that explicit negotiation previously provided, while the empirical results on 1,000-agent simulations and the Kilobot platform show the theory survives contact with noise, asynchrony, and failure.

The broader lesson echoes the trajectory of the field itself. Brambilla et al. framed swarm engineering as the challenge of moving from ad hoc demonstrations to *systematic design procedures* [1]; Rubenstein et al. and Werfel et al. showed that thousand-robot self-assembly and stigmergic construction are physically realizable [3][4]. This work contributes the missing analytical layer: *provable* coverage, *bounded-regret* allocation, and *stable* stigmergic channels, all executable with *O(1)* per-robot state and communication.
