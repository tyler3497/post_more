# Factor-Graph Formulations of Simultaneous Localization and Mapping: Incremental Smoothing via the iSAM2 Bayes Tree and Robust Loop Closure through Dynamic Covariance Scaling

## Abstract

Simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) is formulated as inference over a sparse factor graph, and the classical batch perspective is increasingly inadequate for long-duration autonomy. This thesis reconstructs the SLAM pipeline around the incremental-smoothing paradigm: the factor graph encodes Gaussian measurement models; the Bayes tree renders sparse matrix factorization as a set of editable conditional densities; and the iSAM2 algorithm exploits that structure for fluid relinearization and incremental variable reordering without periodic batch resets. Perceptual aliasing guarantees that loop-closure hypotheses contain false positives, so the work continues through the robust estimation literature: switchable constraints that make graph topology itself an optimization variable, dynamic covariance scaling that reweights suspect edges by their chi-squared error without enlarging the state, max-mixtures that embed failure modes inside a Bayesian factor, and graduated non-convexity with the scale-invariant kernel of riSAM. A worked comparison on benchmark pose graphs demonstrates that robust incremental inference can recover correct trajectories under outlier contamination while preserving online efficiency. The thesis concludes with open problems: certification, multi-hypothesis data association at scale, and principled uncertainty quantification in lifelong operation.

##1 Intro

A mobile robot that must operate for hours or days in an unknown environment faces two entangled problems: it must know where it is, and it must build a map of what it has seen. These two problems are inseparable — pose cannot be estimated without a map, and the map cannot be built without pose. *Simultaneous localization and mapping* (SLAM) names this coupling, and the modern formulation treats it as probabilistic inference over a joint state of robot trajectory and environment structure [1].

**Filtering** maintains a posterior over only the most recent pose, marginalizing the past into a dense covariance; **smoothing** retains the whole trajectory and re-optimizes it. Smoothing has won decisively because it preserves the *sparsity* of the graphical model, and sparse problems are dramatically cheaper to solve [1][3].

The graphical model in question is the **factor graph**: a bipartite representation with variable nodes (poses, landmarks) and factor nodes (measurement likelihoods) [1]. Every SLAM algorithm of consequence — batch sparse Cholesky solvers, incremental smoothers, robust back-ends — can be understood as operations on this graph. This thesis argues that three ideas form the essential core of modern SLAM practice:

1. *Incremental factorization*, in which the Bayes tree converts abstract matrix updates into local edits of conditional densities [2][3];
2. *Robustification*, in which outlier loop closures are tamed by reweighting, switching, or kernel schedules rather than by perfect front-end data association [4][5][6][7];
3. *Evaluation discipline*, in which accuracy and runtime are measured jointly on public benchmarks rather than on tuned demonstrations.

What follows develops each in turn: nonlinear-least-squares background, the iSAM2 methodology, a five-part deep dive, an empirical comparison, and closing limitations.

##2 Background

### The MAP formulation

Assume a robot with trajectory variables x_1 through x_n and landmark variables l_1 through l_m, together written as X. Sensor measurements z_1 through z_k arrive as odometry, range-bearing, or visual reprojection constraints. Under Gaussian noise assumptions, the maximum *a posteriori* (MAP) estimate factorizes as a product of measurement likelihoods and priors:

```
X* = argmax_X p(X|Z) = argmax_X p(X) prod_k p(z_k | X_k)
```

where X_k denotes the small subset of variables involved in measurement k. Taking negative logarithms converts the problem into **nonlinear least squares**:

```
X* = argmin_X sum_k || h_k(X_k) - z_k ||^2_{Sigma_k}
```

with measurement function h_k and covariance Sigma_k, and ||·||_Sigma the Mahalanobis norm. Linearizing each factor around a current estimate X_0 yields a sparse linear system A delta = b, where the rows of A are the Jacobians J_k premultiplied by the whitened noise model, and the structure of A is dictated entirely by the graph connectivity [1].

### The square-root information matrix

Rather than forming A^T A, modern solvers factor the whitened Jacobian directly: QR gives A = Q R, and back-substitution on the upper-triangular **square-root information matrix** R recovers the correction [3]. The sparsity of R is SLAM's central computational resource — the graph mirrored in linear algebra. The *elimination ordering* determines fill-in; column approximate minimum degree (COLAMD) keeps it modest on chain-like problems.

> **Theorem:** *For a linear Gaussian factor graph with elimination ordering o, the Bayes net obtained by eliminating variables in order o is equivalent to the QR factorization of the Jacobian computed with the same ordering; the cliques of the Bayes tree correspond to the rows of the upper-triangular factor R.* (cf. [3])

### From batch to incremental

The **iSAM** family observes that the square-root information matrix can be *updated* rather than recomputed: new measurement rows are appended to R and eliminated with Givens rotations [2]. This yields fully incremental operation, except for two practical needs — periodic variable reordering to control fill-in, and periodic relinearization to prevent inconsistency — implemented as expensive *batch resets*. The Bayes tree was introduced precisely to eliminate them [2][3].

##3 Methodology

This thesis follows the *incremental smoothing and mapping* methodology end to end:

1. **Model.** Construct the SLAM problem as a factor graph: variable nodes for poses and landmarks, factor nodes for odometry, landmark observations, loop closures, and priors [1].
2. **Eliminate.** Convert the factor graph into a Bayes net by variable elimination, then compress into the **Bayes tree** — a directed clique tree in which each clique encodes a conditional density p(frontal | separator) [3].
3. **Update incrementally.** On arrival of new factors, detach the affected subtrees, re-eliminate the union of factors, and reattach — an operation whose cost is confined to the affected portion of the tree [2].
4. **Relinearize selectively.** Monitor linearization-point deviation; relinearize only variables whose delta exceeds a threshold (fluid relinearization), avoiding the batch reset [2].
5. **Robustify.** Wrap suspect factors — principally loop closures — in robust mechanisms (switchable constraints, dynamic covariance scaling, graduated non-convexity) so that false-positive detections are down-weighted or disabled rather than poisoning the estimate [4][5][7].
6. **Validate.** Measure absolute trajectory error and runtime on public pose-graph benchmarks under controlled outlier injection.

The key architectural insight is that steps 2–4 operate on *one* data structure. The Bayes tree is simultaneously the factorization, the variable-reordering device, and the relinearization scheduler; adding factors, reordering variables, and relinearizing all become tree-editing operations [2][3].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Factor graphs: the modeling language

The factor graph is the *lingua franca* of modern robot perception [1]. Its power is representational economy: a single bipartite graph specifies the measurement model, the sparsity pattern of the linearized system, and the conditional-independence structure that every inference algorithm will exploit. Consider a canonical visual-inertial example: pose nodes x_t connected by IMU preintegration factors, landmark nodes l_j connected to the poses that observed them by reprojection factors. Adding a loop closure is, in this language, nothing more than adding an edge between two distant pose nodes — the graph formulation makes the *structural* impact of a loop closure explicit, namely that the elimination ordering that was efficient for a chain is suddenly poor for a graph with long-range edges.

The formulation buys **modularity** (a new sensor is a new factor type), **visible sparsity** (the information pattern is the graph adjacency), and **backend uniformity** (batch, incremental, and robust solvers all consume the same graph and differ only in scheduling) [1].

GTSAM is the reference implementation: factor graphs and Bayes networks are the computing paradigm rather than sparse matrices, and iSAM2 is implemented as tree surgery on the Bayes tree [1][2].

```python
# Conceptual sketch: building a tiny factor graph (GTSAM-style pseudocode)
graph = FactorGraph()
for t in range(1, N):
    graph.add(OdometryFactor(x[t-1], x[t], odom[t], odom_noise))
for (t, j, z) in observations:
    graph.add(ProjectionFactor(x[t], l[j], z, cam_noise))
graph.add(PriorFactor(x_first, prior_pose, prior_noise))  # fixes the gauge
result = graph.optimize_iSAM2(initial)  # incremental Bayes-tree updates
```

### 4.2 The Bayes tree: factorization as tree surgery

The Bayes tree is a directed clique tree that encodes the same factored density as the Bayes net obtained by elimination, but with a crucial property: incremental updates correspond to *local* edits [3]. When new factors arrive, only the cliques on paths from the affected variables to the root need to be detached and re-eliminated; the remainder of the tree — typically the overwhelming majority — is untouched. The following Haskell sketch captures the structure:

```haskell
-- A Bayes tree clique: frontal variables, separator variables, conditional density
data Clique = Clique
  { frontal   :: [Variable]
  , separator :: [Variable]
  , density   :: Conditional  -- p(frontal | separator), square-root form
  }

data BayesTree = Node Clique [BayesTree]  -- children; root holds the prior anchor

-- Incremental update: re-eliminate only the affected subtree
update :: [Factor] -> BayesTree -> BayesTree
update newFactors tree =
  let affected  = topSubtree (variablesIn newFactors) tree
      detached  = detach affected tree
      reElim    = eliminate (factorsIn affected ++ newFactors)  -- small QR
  in reattach reElim detached
```

Two properties make the Bayes tree the right structure for incremental SLAM: *partial state updates* are exact (cliques below the affected subtree are untouched), and the tree exposes the elimination ordering as an explicit, editable object.

### 4.3 Fluid relinearization and incremental variable reordering

The original iSAM maintained its square-root information matrix incrementally but still required periodic batch resets for reordering and relinearization [2]. iSAM2 replaces both with local operations:

- **Incremental variable reordering.** In the last variable to be eliminated (nearest the root), reordering has no fill-in consequences below it. iSAM2 exploits this: it pushes the most recently accessed variables toward the root of the Bayes tree before re-eliminating the affected subtree, obtaining nearly the benefit of a global COLAMD ordering at a fraction of the cost [2].
- **Fluid relinearization.** Rather than relinearizing the whole graph on a schedule, iSAM2 tracks each variable's deviation delta from its linearization point. Only variables whose deviation exceeds a threshold are relinearized; their cliques are updated in the tree, and the rest of the factorization is reused verbatim. The algorithm is therefore *fully* incremental: there is no operation whose cost scales with the whole problem [2].

Kaess and colleagues show iSAM2 matching batch accuracy while per-update cost stays nearly constant on chain-like trajectories, growing only mildly when loop closures widen the affected subtree [2][3].

```rust
// Fluid relinearization check (pseudocode in Rust style)
fn needs_relinearization(delta: &Vector, threshold: f64) -> bool {
    delta.iter().any(|&d| d.abs() > threshold)
}

fn isam2_step(tree: &mut BayesTree, new_factors: &[Factor]) {
    let affected = tree.mark_affected(new_factors);        // upward marking
    tree.reorder_recent_to_root(&affected);                 // incremental ordering
    for clique in affected.cliques() {
        if needs_relinearization(&clique.delta, THETA) {
            clique.relinearize();                            // fluid: only the changed ones
        }
    }
    tree.re_eliminate(&affected);                           // local QR re-factor
}
```

### 4.4 Robust cost design: switchable constraints and dynamic covariance scaling

Loop closures are the lifeblood of SLAM — and its most common cause of death. Perceptual aliasing makes false-positive loop detections unavoidable: corridors, parking garages, and office floors all look alike to a place-recognition module. A single false loop closure with a confident covariance can catastrophically distort a least-squares estimate, because the quadratic cost grows without bound and the optimizer bends the whole map to satisfy the lie [5][6].

Two philosophies address this. The **explicit** approach, *switchable constraints* [5], augments the graph: each suspect loop-closure factor receives a switch variable s in the open interval between zero and one, the factor's information is scaled by s, and a prior pulls s toward one. The optimizer then jointly estimates poses *and* which loop closures to trust. False loops are effectively turned off; true loops retain full weight. Evaluations showed the method surviving one thousand false-positive loop closures on standard datasets [5].

The **implicit** approach, *dynamic covariance scaling* (DCS) [4], avoids extra variables entirely. The insight is that a robust kernel can be implemented as an *iteratively reweighted* least squares: after each iteration, every suspect factor's covariance is inflated (its information down-weighted) as a function of its current chi-squared error. A factor with enormous error gets its information driven toward zero — dynamic, data-driven gating — while factors with small errors are untouched. Because no switch variables are added, DCS integrates into any existing backend with minimal surgery and converges in fewer iterations than switchable constraints on several benchmarks [4].

| Method | Extra variables | Robustness mechanism | Reported behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plain least squares | None | None | Fails catastrophically on outliers [5] |
| Huber kernel | None | Linear growth beyond threshold | Handles mild outliers; breaks on confident false loops |
| Switchable constraints [5] | One switch per suspect edge | Joint optimization of topology | Rejects 1000+ false loops; slower |
| Dynamic covariance scaling [4] | None | Iterative reweighting by chi-squared error | Fast; few extra iterations |
| Max-mixtures [6] | None | Failure mode inside the factor | Integrated Bayesian treatment |
| riSAM / GNC [7] | Per-edge weights | Graduated non-convexity schedule | Near-batch robustness, online |

In short: switchable constraints *explain* outliers with latent variables, DCS *discounts* them with adaptive weights — both are half-quadratic optimization, alternating a weighting update with the state update.

### 4.5 Graduated non-convexity, max-mixtures, and the road to riSAM

Robust kernels come in a spectrum from convex (Huber) to strongly non-convex (Geman–McClure, truncated least squares). Non-convex kernels reject outliers decisively but create local minima — exactly the failure mode that poor initialization also creates. **Graduated non-convexity** (GNC) resolves the tension by homotopy: optimize a convex surrogate first, then gradually morph the cost toward the target non-convex kernel, tracking the solution through the deformation [7].

**riSAM** brings GNC into the incremental setting [7]: an efficient GNC schedule for the Bayes tree, a *scale-invariant graduated kernel* that behaves consistently across rotations and translations (which live on incomparable units), and a trust-region optimizer for the incremental graduated steps. On benchmarks with injected outlier loop closures it achieves online efficiency while matching or exceeding offline batch robustness [7].

A complementary line, **max-mixtures** [6], embeds the failure hypothesis inside the factor: each measurement is a mixture of a nominal Gaussian and a broad null hypothesis, and inference takes the max (not the sum) of the components. The graph stays compact — no switch variables, no hypothesis trees — yet the optimizer can *choose* the null hypothesis for a bad loop closure; the Intel dataset recovers correctly with a hundred injected false loops [6].

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### The DCS reweighting rule

DCS is iteratively reweighted least squares on a robust kernel [4]: for whitened residual e_i(X) with chi_i^2 = ||e_i||^2, each suspect factor's information Omega_i is replaced by s_i^2 Omega_i, with

```
s_i = min(1, 2 Phi / (chi_i^2 + Phi))   -- Phi: kernel width parameter
```

For small errors (chi_i^2 << Phi), s_i ≈ 1 and the factor is treated normally. For large errors, s_i decays inversely with the error, so the factor's influence vanishes smoothly. Choosing Phi = 1 is reported to work across datasets with comparable sensitivity to switchable constraints [4].

```python
import numpy as np

def dcs_weights(residuals, Phi=1.0):
    """Dynamic covariance scaling weights for suspect loop-closure factors."""
    chi2 = np.sum(residuals**2, axis=1)
    s = np.minimum(1.0, 2.0 * Phi / (chi2 + Phi))
    return s  # multiply each factor's information matrix by s**2

def dcs_optimize(graph, suspect_ids, max_iter=10, Phi=1.0):
    X = graph.initial_guess()
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        r = graph.whitened_residuals(X, suspect_ids)
        w = dcs_weights(r, Phi)
        graph.apply_scaling(suspect_ids, w)
        X = graph.gauss_newton_step(X)   # standard sparse solve on R
        if graph.converged(X):
            break
    return X
```

### A worked comparison

Consider the standard pose-graph benchmarks — Manhattan, City10000, Intel, Sphere2500 — corrupted with injected false loop closures, following the protocols of [4][5]. The qualitative picture, consistent across the cited studies, is:

1. **Plain least squares** collapses: even one confident false loop closure bends the whole trajectory [5][6].
2. **Switchable constraints** recover the true trajectory under up to a thousand injected outliers, at the cost of extra variables and slower convergence [4][5].
3. **DCS** matches that robustness with fewer iterations and no added state, and additionally aids convergence under poor initialization by damping far-from-zero Jacobians [4].
4. **riSAM** attains batch-level robustness at online per-update cost [7].
5. **Max-mixtures** recovers correct maps with a hundred injected false loops on Intel with no front-end validation at all [6].

> **Theorem:** *Under the DCS scaling rule above, the sequence of iterates is a majorization–minimization scheme for a bounded robust cost; each iteration monotonically decreases the robust objective, and the weights of factors with unbounded residuals tend to zero.* (Proof sketch: the quadratic surrogate with scaled information majorizes the robust kernel by construction; see the half-quadratic analysis in [4].)

### When robustness still fails

All these methods can fail when the outlier *ratio* is extreme or false loops are *mutually consistent*: a cluster of agreeing false closures can outvote the true structure, and no local robustifier distinguishes a consistent lie from the truth [5][6]. The ambiguity is information-theoretic — the data alone cannot resolve it.

##6 Limitations

- **Gaussianity is a straitjacket.** The entire factor-graph/QR/iSAM2 edifice assumes Gaussian measurement noise. Heavy-tailed sensor behavior is patched by robust kernels, but the underlying probabilistic semantics become approximate; uncertainty estimates after robustification are optimistic [1][7].
- **Consistency of incremental relinearization.** Fluid relinearization is a heuristic: thresholds control a trade-off between cost and accuracy, and no general guarantee ensures the incremental solution stays within a bounded distance of the batch optimum on adversarial trajectories [2].
- **Ordering dependence.** Bayes-tree efficiency depends on variable ordering; incremental reordering is approximate, and pathological loop-closure patterns can still produce dense separators and large affected subtrees [3].
- **Outlier-model mismatch.** Switchable constraints, DCS, and GNC all assume outliers are sparse relative to inliers. Correlated false positives — repeated perceptual aliasing in symmetric environments — defeat every method surveyed here [5][6].
- **Certification gap.** None of these incremental robust methods certify global optimality. Certifiably correct pose-graph optimization via semidefinite relaxation exists but remains largely a batch, offline technology.
- **Evaluation realism.** Benchmark protocols inject *random* false loops, but real perceptual aliasing produces *structured* false loops that are harder and less studied [4][5].

---

##7 Conclusion

The factor graph is the right abstraction for SLAM: it makes sparsity visible, sensors modular, and every solver a variation on factorization [1]. The Bayes tree turns that factorization into a living data structure in which measurement insertion, variable reordering, and relinearization are all local edits — the basis of iSAM2's genuinely incremental operation with no periodic batch resets [2][3]. Robustness, the other half of the story, is not a front-end problem to be solved once but a permanent property the backend must maintain: switchable constraints make topology an optimization variable [5], dynamic covariance scaling reweights by evidence without enlarging the state [4], max-mixtures internalize failure hypotheses [6], and graduated non-convexity with scale-invariant kernels brings robust global reasoning into the online regime [7].

The open frontier is *trustworthy* incremental SLAM: estimators that are simultaneously incremental, robust to structured outliers, and able to bound their own error. Until then, the combination of factor-graph modeling, Bayes-tree incremental inference, and graduated robust estimation remains the state of the art.
