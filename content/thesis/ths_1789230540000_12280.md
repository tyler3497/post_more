# Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces: The Representer Theorem, Mercer's Spectral Expansion, Spectral Bias, and Scalable Approximation via Random Fourier Features and the Nyström Method

## Abstract

Reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS) underpin classical kernel machines — ridge regression, support vector machines, Gaussian processes. Three results anchor the theory: the representer theorem, which collapses infinite-dimensional optimization to a finite linear system; Mercer's theorem, which diagonalizes any continuous positive-definite kernel in the eigenbasis of its integral operator; and Bochner's theorem, which re-expresses shift-invariant kernels as Fourier transforms of finite measures, unlocking data-independent randomization. This thesis develops these pillars and examines two contemporary consequences. First, *spectral bias*: gradient-based learning — in kernel gradient flow and in deep networks via the neural tangent kernel — fits targets from low to high frequencies, governed by the eigenvalue decay of the Mercer spectrum. Second, *scalability*: when the kernel matrix is too large to form, random Fourier features approximate shift-invariant kernels by explicit finite-dimensional maps with uniform guarantees, while the Nyström method builds data-adaptive low-rank approximations with relative-error spectral bounds. We survey proofs, error rates, and design trade-offs, and close with open limitations: kernel selection, the curse of dimensionality, and the statistical-computational frontier.

## 1 Introduction

Few mathematical structures have crossed as many disciplinary boundaries as the *reproducing kernel Hilbert space*. Born in early twentieth-century analysis as a device for integral equations [1], the RKHS became, in the early 2000s, the central object of machine learning theory: the hypothesis class in which regularized empirical risk minimization provably works. The kernel trick — evaluating inner products in an infinite-dimensional space by computing a scalar function $k(x, x')$ — allowed linear algorithms to learn nonlinear functions without ever representing the feature map.

Yet the classical theory carries two apparent impossibilities. First, minimizing over an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space seems intractable — but the **representer theorem** shows every regularized-risk minimizer admits a finite expansion in kernel sections at the training points [3][4]. Second, the kernel is abstractly defined; **Mercer's theorem** makes it concrete by expanding it in the eigenbasis of the Hilbert–Schmidt integral operator, exposing the *spectral* structure that controls approximation error, statistical rates, and the training dynamics of gradient-based learning [1][8].

The modern story begins where the classical one ends. Infinite-dimensional hypothesis classes and $O(n^3)$ kernel matrices do not scale. Two approximation paradigms emerged. **Random Fourier features**, introduced by Rahimi and Recht [11], replace the implicit kernel map with an explicit low-dimensional randomized map, justified by Bochner's characterization of shift-invariant kernels. The **Nyström method**, adapted to kernel machines by Williams and Seeger [9], restricts learning to the subspace spanned by $m$ subsampled landmarks, with randomized-analysis spectral guarantees [10].

Parallel to these computational developments, a *phenomenological* discovery reshaped our understanding of learning. Rahaman et al. [6] and Xu et al. [7] showed that deep networks trained by gradient descent exhibit **spectral bias** (the *frequency principle*): they fit low-frequency components before high-frequency ones. In the infinite-width limit this is exactly Mercer eigen-dynamics of gradient flow on the neural tangent kernel — the same mathematics that governs kernel regression. Spectral bias is thus a general consequence of RKHS geometry and gradient dynamics, not a quirk of deep networks.

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 fixes notation and background. Section 3 develops the representer theorem, including its classical proof and modern extensions to deep and operator-valued settings. Section 4 contains the deep dive: Mercer's theorem and eigenvalue decay (§4.1), spectral bias in kernel and neural learning (§4.2), random Fourier features (§4.3), and the Nyström method (§4.4). Section 5 states key approximation and statistical guarantees and sketches a reproducible experimental protocol. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes. All cited claims are anchored to verifiable sources [1]–[12].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Hilbert spaces and positive-definite kernels

Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a real Hilbert space of functions $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ on a nonempty set $\mathcal{X}$. The defining property of an RKHS is *reproducibility*: point evaluation is a continuous linear functional.

> **Definition (RKHS).** A Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ of functions on $\mathcal{X}$ is a *reproducing kernel Hilbert space* if for every $x \in \mathcal{X}$ there exists a unique $k_x \in \mathcal{H}$ such that $f(x) = \langle f, k_x \rangle_{\mathcal{H}}$ for all $f \in \mathcal{H}$.

The function $k(x, y) := \langle k_y, k_x \rangle_{\mathcal{H}}$ is the *reproducing kernel*. By the Moore–Aronszajn theorem, every symmetric **positive-definite** (psd) kernel — one satisfying $\sum_{i,j} c_i c_j k(x_i, x_j) \ge 0$ for all finite $\{x_i\}$, $\{c_i\}$ — generates a unique RKHS for which it is the reproducing kernel, and conversely every RKHS has a unique reproducing kernel. The **feature map** $\phi(x) := k_x = k(\cdot, x)$ satisfies the *kernel identity* $k(x, y) = \langle \phi(x), \phi(y) \rangle_{\mathcal{H}}$, which is the entire content of the kernel trick: nonlinear structure in $\mathcal{X}$ becomes linear structure in $\mathcal{H}$.

Canonical kernels on $\mathbb{R}^d$ include:

| Kernel | Formula | RKHS character |
|---|---|---|
| Gaussian (RBF) | $\exp(-\gamma \lVert x - x' \rVert^2)$ | Analytic functions; universal |
| Matérn-$\nu$ | $\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}(\sqrt{2\nu}r)^\nu K_\nu(\sqrt{2\nu}r)$ | Sobolev space $H^{\nu + d/2}$ |
| Polynomial (degree $p$) | $(\langle x, x' \rangle + c)^p$ | Finite-dimensional polynomials |
| Laplace | $\exp(-\gamma \lVert x - x' \rVert_1)$ | Continuous, less smooth than RBF |

Here $r = \lVert x - x' \rVert / \ell$ with length-scale $\ell$. Universality — density of the RKHS in $C(\mathcal{X})$ on compact $\mathcal{X}$ — holds for the Gaussian and Laplace kernels [8].

### 2.2 Regularized empirical risk minimization

Given data $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ and a loss $\ell: \mathcal{Y} \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}_+$, the **regularized risk** is

$$
\mathcal{R}_\lambda(f) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(y_i, f(x_i)) + \lambda \lVert f \rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^2, \qquad f \in \mathcal{H}, \ \lambda > 0.
$$

The minimizer balances data fit against RKHS norm — *Tikhonov regularization* — where $\lVert f \rVert_{\mathcal{H}}$ measures complexity in the geometry of $\mathcal{H}$. The subtlety: how does one minimize over an infinite-dimensional space at all?

## 3 Theoretical Framework: The Representer Theorem

### 3.1 The classical result

The representer theorem, in its modern general form, is due to Schölkopf, Herbrich, and Smola (2001); the spline and regularization roots go back further, notably to Wahba's monograph [3].

> **Theorem (Representer).** Let $\mathcal{H}$ be an RKHS with kernel $k$ on $\mathcal{X}$, let $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty\}$ be an arbitrary cost function depending on $f$ only through its values $(f(x_1), \dots, f(x_n))$ at fixed points $x_i \in \mathcal{X}$, and let $g: \mathbb{R}_+ \to \mathbb{R}$ be strictly increasing. Then any minimizer of
>
> $$J(f) = L(f(x_1), \dots, f(x_n)) + g(\lVert f \rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^2)$$
>
> admits a representation $f^\star(\cdot) = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i k(\cdot, x_i)$ for some $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^n$. If $g$ is strictly increasing, every minimizer has this form.

*Proof sketch.* Decompose any $f \in \mathcal{H}$ as $f = f_\parallel + f_\perp$, where $f_\parallel$ lies in $\mathrm{span}\{k_{x_1}, \dots, k_{x_n}\}$ and $f_\perp$ is orthogonal to this span. By the reproducing property, $f_\perp(x_i) = \langle f_\perp, k_{x_i} \rangle = 0$ for all $i$, so the data-dependent term $L$ is unchanged by dropping $f_\perp$. Meanwhile $\lVert f \rVert^2 = \lVert f_\parallel \rVert^2 + \lVert f_\perp \rVert^2 \ge \lVert f_\parallel \rVert^2$, with equality iff $f_\perp = 0$; since $g$ is strictly increasing, any component orthogonal to the data span strictly increases the objective without improving the fit. Hence minimizers live in the span. ∎

The consequence is computational: kernel ridge regression, SVMs, and kernel logistic regression all reduce to $n$-dimensional problems over the Gram matrix $K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)$.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Mercer's Theorem and the Spectral Anatomy of Kernels

Mercer's theorem (1909) is the spectral decomposition of the kernel [1][2].

> **Theorem (Mercer).** There exists an orthonormal system $\{e_j\}$ of continuous eigenfunctions of $T_k$ with non-negative eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots \ge 0$ such that
>
> $$k(x, y) = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \lambda_j e_j(x) e_j(y),$$
>
> with convergence absolute and uniform on $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X}$.

This is strictly stronger than the $L_2$-convergence granted by the spectral theorem: uniform convergence requires continuity *and* positive-definiteness, licensing term-by-term integration, kernel PCA, and the feature map $\phi(x) = (\sqrt{\lambda_j} e_j(x))_{j \ge 1}$ into $\ell^2$ [1].

The RKHS admits the *Mercer characterization* $\mathcal{H} = \{ f = \sum_j a_j \sqrt{\lambda_j} e_j : \sum_j a_j^2 < \infty \}$ with $\lVert f \rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^2 = \sum_j \langle f, e_j \rangle_{L_2}^2 / \lambda_j$: functions whose $L_2$ coefficients decay slowly relative to $\lambda_j$ are *expensive* in RKHS norm.

- **Gaussian kernel on $[-1,1]^d$:** eigenvalues decay *exponentially*, $\lambda_j \asymp \exp(-c j^{1/d})$. The RKHS contains analytic functions; approximating a target whose spectrum has heavy high-frequency tails requires exponentially many terms — the Mercer spectrum prices complexity.
- **Matérn-$\nu$ kernel:** eigenvalues decay *polynomially*, $\lambda_j \asymp j^{-2\nu/d - 1}$. The RKHS is the Sobolev space $H^{\nu + d/2}$; $\nu$ tunes smoothness directly.

The Karhunen–Loève expansion of a Gaussian process with covariance $k$ is exactly this eigen-decomposition; Mercer expansions also govern minimax statistical rates for kernel regression [8].

### 4.2 Spectral Bias: Why Learning Proceeds from Low to High Frequency

Consider gradient flow on the kernel-ridge objective with squared loss: $\dot{f}_t = -\eta\, T_K (f_t - y)$, where $T_K$ is the empirical integral operator.

$$r_j(t) = r_j(0)\, \exp(-\eta \hat{\lambda}_j t).$$

Large-eigenvalue (smooth) modes converge exponentially faster than small-eigenvalue (high-frequency) modes.

In the infinite-width limit this is an identity: the neural tangent kernel (NTK) of a wide network is fixed at initialization, and training is kernel gradient flow in the NTK's RKHS, with per-mode convergence rate proportional to its eigenvalue [6][7].

1. **Implicit regularization.** Early stopping in gradient flow selects smooth, low-frequency hypotheses — the empirical reason over-parameterized models generalize despite memorizing capacity.
2. **A curse for high-frequency targets.** Learners targeting oscillatory functions must fight the Mercer spectrum; convergence stalls along the tail. Remedies include Fourier feature embeddings and frequency-adaptive weighting, all motivated by the eigen-dynamics above.

### 4.3 Random Fourier Features: Bochner's Theorem and Data-Independent Approximation

The classical kernel matrix costs $O(n^2)$ memory and $O(n^3)$ inversion.

> **Theorem (Bochner).** A continuous shift-invariant kernel $k(x, y) = \kappa(x - y)$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$ is positive-definite iff $\kappa$ is the Fourier transform of a finite non-negative measure $\mu$: $\kappa(\delta) = \int e^{i \omega^\top \delta} \, d\mu(\omega)$.

For the Gaussian kernel with bandwidth $\sigma$, $\mu$ is Gaussian with covariance $\sigma^{-2} I$.

Learning then becomes *linear* in $\mathbb{R}^D$: form $Z \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times D}$, solve ridge regression in $O(n D^2 + D^3)$ — versus $O(n^3)$.

```python
import numpy as np

def rff_map(X, D, sigma, rng=None):
    """Random Fourier features for the Gaussian kernel k(x,y)=exp(-||x-y||^2/(2 sigma^2))."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(rng)
    n, d = X.shape
    W = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0 / sigma, size=(d, D))   # omega ~ N(0, sigma^-2 I)
    b = rng.uniform(0.0, 2.0 * np.pi, size=D)
    Z = np.cos(X @ W + b) * np.sqrt(2.0 / D)
    return Z  # E[Z Z^T] -> K elementwise as D -> inf

# Kernel ridge regression becomes linear ridge on Z: O(n D^2) instead of O(n^3)
Z = rff_map(X_train, D=4096, sigma=1.0, rng=0)
theta = np.linalg.solve(Z.T @ Z + n * lam * np.eye(Z.shape[1]), Z.T @ y_train)
pred = rff_map(X_test, D=4096, sigma=1.0, rng=0) @ theta
```

### 4.4 The Nyström Method: Data-Adaptive Low-Rank Approximation

The Nyström method, originating in integral equations (E.

Partition the Gram matrix as

$$K = \begin{bmatrix} K_{mm} & K_{mn} \\ K_{nm} & K_{nn}' \end{bmatrix},$$

where the first block corresponds to the $m$ landmarks.

$$\widetilde{K} = K_{nm} K_{mm}^{-1} K_{mn} = C W^{-1} C^\top, \qquad C = K_{nm} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}, \; W = K_{mm}.$$

Equivalently — and this is the RKHS interpretation [9] — it restricts hypotheses to the subspace $\mathcal{H}_m = \mathrm{span}\{k_{x_{i_1}}, \dots, k_{x_{i_m}}\}$ while fitting all $n$ labels.

$$\tilde{\lambda}_i = \frac{n}{m} \hat{\lambda}_i, \qquad \tilde{u}_i = \sqrt{\frac{m}{n}}\, \frac{1}{\hat{\lambda}_i}\, K_{nm} \hat{u}_i,$$

where $(\hat{\lambda}_i, \hat{u}_i)$ is the eigensystem of $K_{mm}$ — a direct discretization of the Mercer eigenproblem restricted to landmarks.

Sampling strategy matters.

| | Random Fourier features | Nyström |
|---|---|---|
| Approximation target | Kernel *function* (Bochner) | Kernel *matrix* (Mercer eigenstructure) |
| Data dependence | None (drawn from spectral measure) | Adaptive (landmarks from data) |
| Effective rank | $D$ features, dense | $m$ landmarks, rank-$m$ |
| Complexity | $O(nD^2 + D^3)$ | $O(n m^2 + m^3)$ |
| Strength | Streaming-friendly, simple | Better when spectrum decays fast; leverage sampling |
| Weakness | High variance at fixed $D$ | $O(nm)$ kernel evaluations; landmark choice matters |

The two methods are complementary: RFF approximates the Mercer expansion *stochastically and globally*, while Nyström approximates it *deterministically on a data-chosen subspace* [9][10][11].

```haskell
-- Nyström landmark reconstruction in a functional style:
-- given landmark block W (m x m) and cross block C (n x m),
-- form the rank-m approximation  K_tilde = C * W^{-1} * C^T
nystrom :: Matrix -> Matrix -> Matrix
nystrom c w = c <> pinv w <> transpose c
  where pinv = pseudoInverse  -- regularized: (W + eps*I)^{-1} in practice
-- Choosing landmarks by ridge leverage scores concentrates mass
-- where the empirical Mercer operator has large eigenvalues.
```

## 5 Empirical and Formal Results

### 5.1 Key guarantees

We collect the principal theorems, stated informally but faithfully to the sources.

> **Theorem (RFF uniform convergence [11][12]).** Let $k(x,y) = \kappa(x-y)$ be a continuous shift-invariant psd kernel on a compact $\mathcal{M} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ with $\kappa(0) = 1$. For the map $z$ above, with probability at least $1 - 2^8 (\sigma \mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})/\epsilon)^2 \exp(-D\epsilon^2 / 8(d+2))$,
>
> $$\sup_{x,y \in \mathcal{M}} \bigl|z(x)^\top z(y) - k(x,y)\bigr| < \epsilon.$$

The $O(1/\sqrt{D})$ rate is dimension-sensitive only through the covering number — the classic Monte Carlo blessing.

> **Theorem (Nyström relative-error bound [10]).** Let $K$ be psd with best rank-$k$ approximation $K_k$. There exist randomized landmark-sampling schemes using $m = O(k \log k / \epsilon^2)$ columns such that with constant probability $\lVert K - \widetilde{K} \rVert_2 \le (1 + \epsilon)\, \lVert K - K_k \rVert_2$.

This elevates Nyström from a heuristic to a *relative-error* spectral approximation: it is nearly as good as the optimal rank-$k$ truncation, which by the Eckart–Young theorem is exactly the truncated Mercer expansion.

> **Theorem (Kernel gradient-flow spectral dynamics [6][7]).** Under gradient flow on squared loss in the RKHS of $k$, the residual along the $j$-th Mercer eigenfunction decays as $\exp(-\eta \hat{\lambda}_j t)$. Consequently, after time $t$, modes with $\hat{\lambda}_j \ll 1/(\eta t)$ are essentially unlearned.

The last result is the formal shadow of spectral bias: learning *speed* is the Mercer spectrum. For the Gaussian kernel, $\hat{\lambda}_j$ decays exponentially, so high-frequency residuals persist for exponentially long times — the mathematical reason networks stall on oscillatory targets and why wide networks generalize via early stopping.

### 5.2 Reproducible experimental protocol

A minimal experiment distinguishing the three regimes (bias, RFF error, Nyström error) can be run in minutes:

1. **Target:** $f^\star(x) = \sin(2\pi x) + 0.3 \sin(40\pi x)$ on $[0,1]$, $n = 2000$ points, Gaussian noise $\sigma = 0.05$. The low and high components separate spectral-bias dynamics.
2. **Kernel ridge regression** (Gaussian kernel, $\ell = 0.1$, $\lambda = 10^{-4}$): track the Fourier amplitude of the residual at frequencies $1$ and $20$ across epochs — the low mode collapses in tens of epochs; the high mode persists [6][7].
3. **RFF sweep:** $D \in \{64, 256, 1024, 4096\}$; measure $\sup_{x,y} |z(x)^\top z(y) - k(x,y)|$ and test MSE — error decays $\sim 1/\sqrt{D}$ [11][12].
4. **Nyström sweep:** $m \in \{32, 128, 512\}$ landmarks, uniform vs. ridge-leverage sampling; measure $\lVert K - \widetilde{K} \rVert_2 / \lVert K \rVert_2$ — leverage sampling dominates under fast spectral decay [9][10].

Expected qualitative outcome: RFF error is Monte Carlo noise in $D$, uniform across frequencies; Nyström error concentrates on the spectral tail; spectral bias appears only in the *training dynamics*, not the approximations — a clean experimental separation of approximation error from optimization bias.

## 6 Limitations

**Kernel selection remains an art.** The Mercer spectrum — hence generalization, optimization speed, and approximation cost — is fixed once $k$ is chosen, yet principled selection is largely heuristic. Multiple-kernel and deep kernel learning [4] partially automate this but reintroduce non-convexity.

**Curse of dimensionality.** Bochner's uniform bound degrades through the covering number of $\mathcal{M}$; in high dimensions $D$ must be enormous for uniform RFF approximation, and the Gaussian kernel itself concentrates. Nyström leverage scores likewise become expensive to compute exactly.

**Beyond shift-invariance.** RFF applies only to shift-invariant kernels; polynomial, string, and graph kernels need other machinery (e.g., Tensor Sketch, random binning features). The Nyström method is more general but data-bound: $O(nm)$ kernel evaluations still hurt when $k$ itself is costly.

**Spectral bias is a double-edged sword.** The low-frequency-first dynamics aid generalization on natural data but provably hinder high-frequency targets; current mitigations (positional encodings, Fourier features as *inputs*) are effective but lack the clean theoretical footing of the kernel-flow analysis [6][7].

**Statistical–computational gaps.** Relative-error Nyström bounds [10] are near-optimal for matrix approximation, but the *statistical* price of replacing $K$ by $\widetilde{K}$ in ridge regression — excess risk versus compute — is only partially characterized, and optimal landmark counts for a given risk target remain an active frontier.

## 7 Conclusion

The reproducing kernel Hilbert space earns its central place through a triad of ideas. The **representer theorem** turns infinite-dimensional optimization into finite linear algebra [3][4][5]. **Mercer's theorem** turns the abstract kernel into a spectral expansion, whose eigenvalue decay prices the complexity of functions and — through gradient-flow dynamics — the speed at which they are learned [1][2][8]. **Bochner's theorem** turns shift-invariant kernels into expectations over random frequencies, making kernel approximation a Monte Carlo problem with explicit finite-dimensional maps [11][12]. **Spectral bias** [6][7] is the dynamical face of the Mercer spectrum: gradient-based learning, in a classical RKHS or in the neural tangent kernel of a wide network, sweeps the spectrum from large eigenvalues to small, from low frequencies to high.

On the computational side, **random Fourier features** and the **Nyström method** embody the two natural responses to the $O(n^3)$ barrier: stochastic, data-independent approximation of the kernel function versus deterministic, data-adaptive approximation of the kernel matrix — each with rigorous error control [9][10][11].

The open questions — principled kernel selection, high-dimensional approximation, the excess-risk cost of sketching, and taming spectral bias for high-frequency learning — ensure that the RKHS, a century after Mercer's expansion [1], remains a living research program rather than a closed chapter.
