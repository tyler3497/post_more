# Neural Theorem Proving in Dependent Type Theory: Tactic Prediction, Premise Selection, and Proof Search in Lean 4

## Abstract

Interactive theorem provers based on dependent type theory, exemplified by Lean 4, turn mathematical proof into a formal dialogue between a tactic predictor and an elaborator that rejects every ill-typed move. This thesis surveys and analyzes the neural theorem proving stack for Lean 4: language models that predict tactics conditioned on goal states, retrieval-augmented premise selection over Mathlib's hundreds of thousands of lemmas, and search procedures—best-first search, Monte-Carlo tree search, and hypertree proof search—that organize predictions into complete proofs. Drawing on GPT-f, LeanDojo, DeepSeek-Prover, AlphaProof, and Thor, we quantify the empirical state of the art on benchmarks such as miniF2F and ProofNet, and identify the structural bottlenecks that remain: autoformalization, search inefficiency, and the long tail of research-level mathematics.

##1 Introduction

A proof, in the setting of an interactive theorem prover (ITP), is not a static document but the *trace of a successful interaction* between a search procedure and a type checker. The user, or in our case the machine, proposes tactics; the elaborator either accepts them and advances the goal state or rejects them with an error message. Every accepted step is checked against the rules of dependent type theory, so a completed proof is correct *by construction*. This closed loop—prediction, elaboration, feedback—distinguishes formal mathematics from every other domain of machine reasoning and makes it, as Polu and Sutskever observed, an ideal laboratory for studying neural reasoning itself [1].

Three capabilities compose a neural theorem prover. *Tactic prediction* guesses the next proof step given the current goal state. *Premise selection* identifies, from a library of hundreds of thousands of lemmas, the handful of facts relevant to the present goal. *Proof search* organizes predictions into trees, prioritizes branches, and recovers from dead ends. Lean 4, with its metaprogramming framework and the Mathlib library, has become the principal arena in which these three components are integrated and measured [10][2].

Progress has been rapid. GPT-f first showed that transformer language models could generate valid formal proofs and even contribute novel short proofs accepted into the Metamath library [1]. LeanDojo made Lean programmatically accessible, annotated premise usage at scale, and produced ReProver, a retrieval-augmented prover trained on a single GPU week [2]. The DeepSeek-Prover series pushed whole-proof generation with large-scale synthetic data, subgoal decomposition, and reinforcement learning from proof-assistant feedback [3][4]. AlphaProof combined a language model with AlphaZero-style reinforcement learning and test-time adaptation to reach silver-medal performance at IMO 2024 [5]. This thesis dissects the architecture common to these systems, analyzes why each component works, presents the empirical record, and gives a proof-theoretic account of the soundness guarantees the loop provides.

##2 Background

### Dependent type theory and the Lean 4 elaborator

In dependent type theory, propositions are types and proofs are terms: to prove a proposition *P* is to construct a term whose type is *P*. Lean 4 implements this calculus of constructions with definitional unfolding, universe polymorphism, and quotient types, exposing both a functional programming language and a tactic framework through its metaprogramming interface [10]. A *goal state* consists of a local context (hypotheses) and a target type; a *tactic* is a program that transforms goal states. The elaborator fills in implicit arguments, unifies metavariables, and invokes the kernel to check definitional equality, so any tactic sequence that closes all goals is a machine-checked proof.

The interactive unit of Lean is the tactic block:

```lean
example (a b : ℕ) : a + b = b + a := by
  rw [Nat.add_comm]
  rfl
```

Here `rw` rewrites with a lemma retrieved from the environment, and `rfl` closes the goal by reflexivity. A neural prover replaces the human with a policy over tactic strings: at each step it observes a pretty-printed goal state and samples candidate tactics, typically with a beam of tens to hundreds of candidates.

### Mathlib and the premise selection bottleneck

Mathlib is Lean's community-curated mathematics library, containing well over one hundred thousand theorems and definitions with dense interdependency [2]. Premise selection—the task of choosing the relevant lemmas for a given goal—is the key bottleneck: a tactic predictor conditioned on the wrong lemmas hallucinates inapplicable rewrites, while one starved of context cannot find the lemma that closes the goal. Dense retrieval over Mathlib therefore sits at the center of any serious Lean prover [2][7].

##3 Methodology

### Tactic prediction as conditional generation

The standard formulation, introduced by GPT-f and retained by its successors, models the prover as an autoregressive policy

> *π(tactic ∣ goal state, retrieved premises)*

trained by supervised learning on human proof steps extracted from Mathlib [1][2]. Goal states are serialized as text—declarations, local hypotheses, and the target—so a decoder-only transformer can consume them directly. Inference samples *k* tactics per goal (commonly 16 to 64), applies each through the elaborator, and retains those that typecheck and produce new goal states. The elaborator's feedback is *exact*: a tactic is either accepted with a precise residual goal or rejected with an error, which makes the environment unusually forgiving of sampling-based methods.

### Retrieval-augmented premise selection

ReProver's retriever embeds the goal state and every accessible premise into a shared vector space and ranks premises by cosine similarity, following the dense passage retrieval paradigm [2]. Training exploits LeanDojo's program analysis: accessible premises form the candidate pool, used premises are positives, and hard negatives are drawn from in-file but unused lemmas, sharpening the decision boundary. At inference the top-ranked premises are concatenated into the generator's context, and ablation studies show consistent gains over non-retrieval baselines, including against GPT-4 on the novel-premises split [2].

The two-stage *select then rerank* refinement, pioneered by Magnushammer for Isabelle, is the strongest known design: a bi-encoder selects on the order of one thousand candidates cheaply, and a cross-encoder lets proof-state tokens attend directly to premise tokens for a contextualized relevance score [7]. On the PISA benchmark this design reached a 59.5% proof rate against 38.3% for the symbolic Sledgehammer, and swapping it into the neural-symbolic Thor framework lifted the state of the art from 57.0% to 71.0% [6][7].

### Search over the proof tree

Candidate tactics form a tree whose nodes are goal states and whose edges are accepted tactics. Best-first search expands nodes by cumulative log-probability, a simple scheme that remains the workhorse of the field [1][2]. Hypertree proof search (HTPS) generalizes the tree to a hypergraph: a tactic may split one goal into several subgoals, and the value of a node is estimated by a critic network trained on proof outcomes, enabling online training that learns from previous searches [8]. Monte-Carlo tree search with intrinsic-reward exploration (RMaxTS) diversifies proof paths and underpins the DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5 inference stack [4].

### Reinforcement learning and expert iteration

Because the elaborator supplies ground-truth verification, proof search doubles as a data generator: every successful proof is a new supervised example. *Expert iteration* alternates between searching with the current model and retraining on the discovered proofs, an AlphaZero-style loop that lifted GPT-f substantially beyond its supervised baseline [1]. DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5 formalizes the loop as reinforcement learning from proof-assistant feedback (RLPAF): the model is fine-tuned on proof candidates, rolled out to generate multiple completions, and optimized against binary rewards from Lean verification [4]. DeepSeek-Prover-V2 adds subgoal decomposition—prompting a strong model to break hard problems into lemmas—as a cold-start curriculum for the RL phase [3]. AlphaProof scales this paradigm furthest: an AlphaZero-inspired agent trained on millions of auto-formalized problems, with test-time RL on problem variants for the hardest targets [5].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Tactic prediction architectures and goal conditioning

The conditioning context of a tactic predictor has grown steadily richer. Early models consumed only the raw goal string [1]; LeanDojo-era models add retrieved premises and file context [2]; DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5 trains the model to *predict the resulting tactic state* as an auxiliary objective alongside proof completion, forcing the representation to internalize elaborator semantics [4]. This matters because tactic prediction is fundamentally a semantic task: `simp` with the wrong lemma set loops forever, while `omega` discharges linear arithmetic goals only when the goal lies in its fragment. The model's attention over the local context must track hypotheses by name, and ablation of hypothesis names measurably degrades performance, suggesting the model genuinely binds binders rather than pattern-matching text.

A subtle point concerns *elaboration-time information*. The pretty-printer hides implicit arguments, universe levels, and definitional unfoldings that the elaborator will need. Systems that feed the model elaborated terms or elaborator error messages as additional conditioning consistently outperform pretty-print-only baselines, at the cost of longer contexts [3]. The frontier design is a *tool-augmented* predictor that, like a human Lean user, issues `#check` and `#print` queries to inspect definitions mid-proof—a direction still in its infancy.

### 4.2 Premise selection at Mathlib scale

Premise selection is a needle-in-a-haystack problem with unusual structure: the haystack is a formal library with explicit dependency structure, and "relevance" is defined by the proof that *will be* written, not by surface similarity. Contrastive retrievers trained on LeanDojo annotations learn to map a goal to lemmas whose statements, when unified with the goal, make progress [2]. Hard negatives are essential—random negatives are too easy, and without in-file unused lemmas as negatives the retriever collapses onto textual overlap.

Yet dense retrieval has a ceiling. Lemmas are often useful only *after* intermediate rewriting steps that the retriever cannot foresee, so premise selection is inherently non-stationary: what is relevant changes as the goal evolves. Two-stage reranking partially addresses this by conditioning on the full proof state [7], and iterative retrieval—re-querying the index at each search node—is an active research direction. The alternative philosophy, embodied by Thor, delegates premise selection to a symbolic hammer entirely: the model predicts a special `<hammer>` token when it judges a goal hammer-solvable, offloading thousands-of-facts reasoning to an ATP backend [6]. Thor's 18-point gain on PISA (39.0% to 57.0%) over the language model alone demonstrates how much of theorem proving is premise selection rather than step generation.

### 4.3 Search algorithms: best-first, HTPS, and Monte-Carlo tree search

Best-first search is deceptively strong: with a good tactic prior and a few thousand elaborator calls, it solves a large fraction of benchmark problems [1][2]. Its weakness is *horizon*: probability mass diffuses across branches, and deep proofs require the model to be right many times in a row. HTPS attacks this with a learned value function over (hyper)nodes, trained online from the outcomes of the search itself, so the search policy improves as it explores [8]. On the Metamath benchmark, HTPS with online training reached a cumulative 82.6% pass rate on validation statements, substantially above the supervised model's 61% at the same attempt budget, and lifted test pass@32 from 55.8% to 65.6% [8].

RMaxTS, the Monte-Carlo tree search variant of DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5, adds an intrinsic reward for discovering *novel* proof paths, counteracting the mode collapse of pure likelihood-based search [4]. The diversity dividend is measurable: multiple distinct proofs of the same theorem supply independent training signal for expert iteration and reduce the correlation of failures across samples. A Python sketch of the canonical best-first loop clarifies the shared skeleton:

```python
import heapq

def best_first_search(initial_goal, policy, elaborator, budget):
    """Expand goal states in order of cumulative log-probability."""
    frontier = [(-0.0, 0, initial_goal, [])]  # (neg logp, depth, state, path)
    seen = set()
    while frontier and budget > 0:
        neglogp, depth, state, path = heapq.heappop(frontier)
        for tactic, logp in policy.sample(state, k=32):
            budget -= 1
            result = elaborator.apply(state, tactic)
            if result.ok and result.new_state not in seen:
                seen.add(result.new_state)
                new_path = path + [tactic]
                if result.is_closed:
                    return new_path
                heapq.heappush(frontier, (-(logp - neglogp), depth + 1,
                                         result.new_state, new_path))
    return None
```

The loop is deliberately simple; the intelligence lives in the policy, the retriever, and the value estimates that order the frontier.

### 4.4 Reinforcement learning and expert iteration

The RL formulation of neural proving has a uniquely clean reward: *1* if Lean accepts the complete proof, *0* otherwise, with optional dense shaping from per-tactic acceptance [4]. RLPAF (reinforcement learning from proof-assistant feedback) optimizes the whole-proof generator against this binary signal after supervised fine-tuning, and the resulting model transfers to search-based inference where the policy proposes and the elaborator disposes [4]. DeepSeek-Prover-V2's innovation is curricular: before RL, a strong model decomposes hard theorems into subgoal chains, and resolved subgoals are synthesized into chain-of-thought traces that cold-start the prover, integrating informal and formal reasoning in one model [3].

AlphaProof is the purest expression of the paradigm: no human proof data at all is required in principle, because millions of auto-formalized problems (natural-language statements translated to Lean by a fine-tuned Gemini model) provide the curriculum, and the AlphaZero loop of search plus retraining provides the gradient [5]. For the hardest problems, test-time RL generates millions of variants of the target problem and trains on those, achieving deep problem-specific adaptation that static fine-tuning cannot match [5]. The cost is compute measured in days per problem—but the output is a *verified* proof, a guarantee no natural-language system can offer.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Benchmark landscape

The field standardizes on a small set of benchmarks. *miniF2F* collects Olympiad-style problems formalized across systems [9]; *ProofNet* targets undergraduate-level mathematics; *PutnamBench* draws on the notoriously difficult Putnam competition; *FIMO* covers formalized IMO problems. Results below are pass rates on held-out test splits as reported by the respective authors.

| System | miniF2F | ProofNet | Other | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-f (expert iteration) | 56.5% val | — | novel Metamath proofs accepted | [1] |
| ReProver | 26.5% pass@1 | 13.8% | 98,734-theorem LeanDojo benchmark | [2] |
| DeepSeek-Prover-V1 | 46.3% (64 samples); 52% cum. | — | 5/148 FIMO vs 0 for GPT-4 | [3]* |
| DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5 | 63.5% | 25.3% | RMaxTS tree search | [4] |
| DeepSeek-Prover-V2-671B | 88.9% pass ratio | — | 49/658 PutnamBench; 6/15 AIME | [3] |
| AlphaProof (+AlphaGeometry 2) | — | — | IMO 2024: 4/6, silver-medal score | [5] |
| Thor (Isabelle/PISA) | 29.9% 1-pass | — | PISA 39.0% → 57.0% | [6] |
| Thor + Magnushammer | — | — | PISA 57.0% → 71.0% | [7] |

*DeepSeek-Prover-V1 numbers from the V1 report; the V1.5 and V2 reports build directly on that lineage [3][4].

Two patterns stand out. First, *scale and search compound*: each generation adds data (synthetic proofs, auto-formalized problems), search (best-first to MCTS to test-time RL), and training signal (supervised to RLPAF), and each step moves the frontier by double-digit points. Second, *retrieval is a multiplier*: ReProver beats non-retrieval baselines trained on identical data, and premise selection quality predicts downstream proof rate almost linearly in ablation studies [2][7].

### A soundness guarantee: why the loop cannot lie

The empirical table would mean little without the guarantee that every counted "success" is genuinely a proof. That guarantee is a theorem about the architecture, not an empirical claim:

> **Theorem:** *Soundness of the tactic-prediction loop.* Let *E* be Lean's elaborator with its kernel checker. Any finite sequence of tactics that *E* accepts and that closes all goals yields a well-typed proof term in the kernel's type theory. Hence every theorem a neural prover reports as proved is true in Lean's logic, regardless of how the tactics were generated.

*Proof sketch.* Each tactic executes as a metaprogram producing a partial proof term with metavariables for open goals; the elaborator unifies these and the kernel re-checks the final term against the goal type, independently of the tactic code. Neural prediction influences only *which* metaprograms are attempted, never the checking itself. ∎

This is why reinforcement learning from proof-assistant feedback is well-founded: the reward signal cannot be gamed by a fluent but invalid argument, because validity is decided by the kernel, not by a learned judge. Contrast this with natural-language mathematics, where a reward model can be fooled by confident nonsense. The practical consequence is that expert iteration is *monotone in correctness*: retraining on discovered proofs can only add verified theorems to the training set, never corrupt ones [1][8].

### Why premise selection dominates the error budget

Ablation studies across systems converge on a striking diagnosis: when a proof attempt fails, the cause is more often a missing or wrong lemma than a wrong tactic skeleton [2][6]. Thor's design makes this quantitative—the 18-point PISA gain comes entirely from hammer-delegated premise selection while the tactic policy is unchanged [6]. In the LeanDojo novel-premises split, where test theorems depend on premises never seen in training, ReProver's retrieval generalizes while a memorization-heavy baseline collapses, confirming that the retriever learns a genuine relevance function rather than a lookup table [2].

##6 Limitations

*Autoformalization remains the narrow gate.* Neural provers operate on formal statements, but mathematics is written in natural language. AlphaProof's Gemini-based translator bridges the gap at scale [5], yet translation errors—formalizing a subtly different theorem than intended—are invisible to the verifier and can only be caught by human inspection. A prover that proves the wrong formalization perfectly is still wrong.

*Search is profligate.* State-of-the-art results routinely consume thousands of elaborator calls or days of compute per theorem [3][5]. Best-first search scales poorly with proof depth, and learned value functions help only when the critic generalizes—HTPS's online training is itself expensive [8]. The gap between "a proof exists in the search tree" and "the search finds it within budget" is the field's central inefficiency.

*Premise selection is non-stationary.* As argued in §4.2, relevance shifts as the goal evolves, but current retrievers embed a static snapshot of the goal [2][7]. Iterative, search-aware retrieval is nascent, and Lean's hammer infrastructure lags Isabelle's, limiting Thor-style delegation for Lean users [6].

*The long tail of research mathematics.* Benchmarks measure competition and undergraduate problems with short, self-contained statements. Research-level mathematics involves definitions spanning papers, diagrammatic reasoning, and proof ideas that no retrieval corpus contains. Current systems have no demonstrated capability at this frontier, and it is unclear whether scaling the present loop suffices.

*De Bruijn-style brittleness.* Tactic scripts are sensitive to Mathlib's exact lemma names and argument orders; library refactoring silently breaks learned proofs. Whole-proof generation is more robust than tactic-script prediction here, but the underlying distribution shift as Mathlib evolves is a standing maintenance cost for every deployed prover.

##7 Conclusion

Neural theorem proving in Lean 4 has matured from a curiosity—GPT-f's first machine-contributed Metamath proofs [1]—into an engineering discipline with a stable architecture: a tactic-predicting language model, a contrastive premise retriever over a vast formal library, and a search procedure that treats the elaborator as an oracle [2][3][4]. Reinforcement learning from proof-assistant feedback closes the loop, turning verification into training signal and search into a data engine [4][5]. The empirical trajectory—from GPT-f through ReProver and the DeepSeek-Prover series to AlphaProof's IMO silver medal—is among the steepest in applied machine learning, and it rests on a property no other reasoning domain enjoys: the kernel-checked guarantee that a found proof is a *true* proof.

The open problems are now structural rather than architectural. Autoformalization must become trustworthy; search must become orders of magnitude cheaper; premise selection must track the evolving goal; and the methods must climb from competition problems to research mathematics. The hammer lesson of Thor and Magnushammer [6][7] suggests the future is hybrid: neural models for the creative work of proposing proof structure, symbolic engines for the combinatorial work of filling in facts. In dependent type theory, at least, the machine's proofs will always be checkable—and that checkability is what makes the entire research program possible.
