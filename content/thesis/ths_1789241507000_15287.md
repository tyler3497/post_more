# The Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis and the Fine-Grained Landscape: Orthogonal Vectors, Conditional Lower Bounds, and the Reduction Web of Polynomial-Time Hardness

## Abstract
For problems solvable in polynomial time, classical complexity theory offers almost no tools to distinguish the genuinely easy from the merely tractable. Fine-grained complexity theory repairs this blindness: starting from conjectures such as the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH) of Impagliazzo and Paturi [1], it proves that shaving even an epsilon from the exponent of the best known algorithm for problems like Edit Distance, Diameter, or Orthogonal Vectors would refute the conjecture. This thesis develops the SETH-centered research program from its origins in exponential-time complexity through the split-and-list reduction of Williams [2] that turned Orthogonal Vectors into the field's central hub, to the Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture [3], the fine-grained reduction framework [4], and the waves of conditional lower bounds for dynamic data structures [5] and graph approximation [6][7] that followed. We formalize the methodology of fine-grained reductions, exhibit the key constructions in detail, catalog the web of hypotheses (SETH, OVC, 3SUM, APSP), and prove why an apparently innocuous $n^{2-\varepsilon}$-time algorithm for any of a dozen polynomial problems would collapse a hierarchy of widely believed conjectures.

## 1 Introduction

Classical complexity theory is a theory of *gross* distinctions. A problem is in **P** if it admits any polynomial-time algorithm; whether the polynomial is $n$ or $n^{10}$ is, for the theory's coarse lens, immaterial. Yet for practitioners, and increasingly for theory, the difference between an $n^2$-time algorithm and an $n^{1.9}$-time algorithm on modern data sets is the difference between feasibility and failure. *Fine-grained complexity theory* is the systematic attempt to extend the methodology of hardness — reductions, completeness, and conjectured lower bounds — to the interior of **P**, where polynomial exponents themselves become the currency of hardness.

The movement begins with a deceptively simple question, posed by Impagliazzo and Paturi in their 1998 investigation of exponential-time complexity: how hard is $k$-SAT, really? Their **Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH)** asserts that for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists a clause width $k$ such that $k$-SAT on $n$ variables cannot be solved in $O(2^{(1-\varepsilon)n})$ time [1]. Informally, brute-force search over the $2^n$ truth assignments is essentially optimal; no algorithm can beat exhaustive search by an exponential factor, no matter how clever.

> Theorem: (Informal SETH). For every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a $k$ such that $k$-CNF-SAT on $n$ variables requires $2^{(1-\varepsilon)n}$ time.

What makes SETH revolutionary rather than merely bold is what it *implies*. In 2005, Ryan Williams showed that SETH hardness transfers from the exponential regime to the polynomial regime through a reduction from $k$-SAT to a problem solvable in quadratic time: **Orthogonal Vectors (OV)** [2]. Given two sets $A, B \subseteq \{0,1\}^d$ of $n$ binary vectors, OV asks whether some pair $a \in A, b \in B$ is orthogonal, i.e., $\langle a, b \rangle = 0$. The naive algorithm checks all $n^2$ pairs. Williams' split-and-list reduction shows that an $n^{2-\varepsilon}$-time algorithm for OV (for some $\varepsilon > 0$, with $d = \omega(\log n)$) would refute SETH. Overnight, a polynomial-time problem inherited the full conjectural weight of exponential-time hardness — and OV became the field's most productive hub for conditional lower bounds.

This thesis surveys and reconstructs the SETH-centered fine-grained program:

1. **Foundations** (§2): SETH and its siblings — the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH), the 3SUM hypothesis, and the APSP hypothesis — and the problem families they govern.
2. **Methodology** (§3): the formal machinery of *fine-grained reductions* $(P_1, T_1) \le_{FGR} (P_2, T_2)$, which lift classical reducibility to quantitative statements about exponents [4].
3. **The reduction web** (§4): the central constructions — Williams' split-and-list reduction [2], the Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture [3], the gadget reductions to Edit Distance, LCS, and Diameter [6][7][8], and the dynamic-algorithms program of Abboud and Vassilevska Williams [5].
4. **Proofs and limits** (§5): the strengthened hardness results obtainable under *weaker* variants of SETH via branching programs [9], and the exact approximation thresholds they pin down.
5. **Limitations** (§6): what fine-grained reductions cannot show — the barrier of non-relativization, the fragility of the hypotheses, and recent algorithmic progress that has partially eroded some lower bounds.

Our goal is not only exposition but *reconstruction*: we give the reduction gadgets at a level of detail sufficient for a reader to implement and verify them, so that the conditional hardness story is a living proof technique rather than a citation graph.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The exponential-time hypotheses

The original Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH), also due to Impagliazzo and Paturi, asserts that 3-SAT requires $2^{\Omega(n)}$ time.

Two companion hypotheses anchor other regions of the landscape. The **3SUM hypothesis** asserts that given $n$ integers, deciding whether three sum to zero requires $n^{2-o(1)}$ time; it governs computational geometry and certain graph problems. The **APSP hypothesis** asserts that All-Pairs Shortest Paths on $n$-node graphs requires $n^{3-o(1)}$ time; it governs dense graph problems.

### 2.2 Orthogonal Vectors and the split-and-list reduction

The **Orthogonal Vectors problem** is the field's workhorse intermediate problem [2]:

- *Input:* two sets $A, B \subseteq \{0,1\}^d$ with $|A| = |B| = n$.
- *Question:* do there exist $a \in A$, $b \in B$ with $\sum_{i=1}^d a_i b_i = 0$?

The trivial algorithm runs in $O(n^2 d)$ time. The **Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture (OVC)** states that for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a constant $c \ge 1$ such that OV with dimension $d = c \log n$ cannot be solved in $n^{2-\varepsilon} \cdot \mathrm{poly}(d)$ time [3]. In words: in slightly super-logarithmic dimension, quadratic time is essentially optimal. SETH implies OVC [1][3], so OVC-based lower bounds inherit SETH's plausibility while being formally weaker assumptions.

Williams' reduction from $k$-SAT to OV is the single most reused construction in fine-grained complexity, so we present it in full. Let $\phi$ be a $k$-CNF formula on $n$ variables (assume $n$ even) with clauses $C_1, \dots, C_M$. Split the variables into two halves: $U$ contains all $2^{n/2}$ partial assignments to $x_1, \dots, x_{n/2}$, and $V$ contains all $2^{n/2}$ partial assignments to $x_{n/2+1}, \dots, x_n$. For a partial assignment $u \in U$ and clause $C$, define $\mathrm{sat}(u, C) = 1$ iff $u$ satisfies $C$ on its own (i.e., some literal of $C$ is made true by $u$). Then define binary vectors of dimension $M$:

$$\mathrm{vec}(u) = (1 - \mathrm{sat}(u, C_1), \dots, 1 - \mathrm{sat}(u, C_M)), \qquad \mathrm{vec}(v) \text{ analogously.}$$

> Theorem: (Claim, Williams' reduction [2]). For $u \in U$, $v \in V$, the vectors $\mathrm{vec}(u)$ and $\mathrm{vec}(v)$ are orthogonal if and only if the combined assignment $(u, v)$ satisfies $\phi$.

*Proof sketch.* The inner product is zero iff for every clause $C_j$, at least one of $\mathrm{sat}(u, C_j)$, $\mathrm{sat}(v, C_j)$ equals 1 — i.e., every clause is satisfied by at least one half-assignment, which is exactly satisfiability of $\phi$. ∎

Thus OV on $N = 2^{n/2}$ vectors per set encodes $k$-SAT on $n$ variables. If OV admitted an $N^{2-\varepsilon}$-time algorithm, $k$-SAT would be solvable in $2^{(1-\varepsilon/2)n}$ time, refuting SETH [2].

### 2.3 Dynamic problems and the update-time frontier

In the *dynamic* setting, a data structure maintains a combinatorial object under insertions and deletions, and the metric of interest is the *amortized update time* rather than total running time.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Fine-grained reductions

A classical Karp reduction preserves *polynomiality*; a fine-grained reduction preserves *exponents*.

1. $R$ maps instances $x$ of $P_1$ to instances $R(x)$ of $P_2$ in time $O(T_1(|x|)^{1-\delta})$, and
2. $|R(x)|$ is such that $T_2(|R(x)|) \le T_1(|x|)^{1-\delta'}$ for some $\delta' > 0$, and
3. an $O(T_2^{1-\varepsilon})$-time algorithm for $P_2$ yields, via $R$, an $O(T_1^{1-\varepsilon'})$-time algorithm for $P_1$ for some $\varepsilon' > 0$.

The slogan: *a fine-grained reduction turns a conjectured lower bound for $P_1$ into a conjectured lower bound for $P_2$ at the corresponding time scale.* If $P_1$ is $k$-SAT with $T_1 = 2^n$ and $P_2$ is OV with $T_2 = n^2$, Williams' construction is exactly such a reduction [2].

The methodology imposes discipline absent from classical reductions.

### 3.2 The reduction design pattern

Nearly all SETH-based lower bounds in **P** follow a three-stage pattern:

1. **Start from OV or $k$-OV.** Rather than reducing directly from SAT, modern proofs reduce from Orthogonal Vectors (or its $k$-ary generalization), inheriting SETH-hardness through Williams' reduction [2][6].
2. **Build a gadget per vector and per coordinate.** Each vector $a \in A$ becomes a gadget (a vertex, a path, a string block); each coordinate $j \in [d]$ becomes a shared resource (an edge layer, a clause position). Orthogonality $\langle a, b \rangle = 0$ is encoded as the *absence* of a connection — a missing edge, a short path, a small edit cost — so that the target problem's optimizer detects exactly the orthogonal pairs.
3. **Tune the parameters.** The dimension $d = c \log n$ and the number of sets $k$ are chosen so that the constructed instance has size $N = n^{1+o(1)}$ (for quadratic lower bounds) or $N = \tilde{O}(n^{k-1})$ (for $n^{k-o(1)}$ lower bounds via $k$-OV [6]).

The payoff of this pattern is a *web* rather than a list: dozens of problems — Edit Distance, LCS, Diameter, Radius, dynamic APSP, Fréchet distance, regular-expression matching — are now known to be SETH-hard at their natural time bounds, and the reductions compose, so a breakthrough on any one node would propagate [4][8].

### 3.3 Verifying conditional hardness

Because the hypotheses are unproven, every fine-grained lower bound is a *conditional* statement of the form "SETH $\Rightarrow$ no $n^{2-\varepsilon}$ algorithm." Sound methodology therefore demands:

- **Exact exponent accounting.** The reduction's overhead must be provably $n^{2-\delta}$ for the claimed $\delta$; hand-waving about "polynomial overhead" invalidates the theorem.
- **Hypothesis hygiene.** State precisely which hypothesis is used (SETH vs. OVC vs. the Hitting Set conjecture), since they form a strict plausibility hierarchy: SETH $\Rightarrow$ OVC, but OVC may hold even if SETH fails [3].
- **Dimension control.** OV lower bounds degrade as $d$ grows: for $d = c \log n$, the best algorithms run in $n^{2 - 1/O(\log c)}$ time [3], so a reduction that blows up dimension can accidentally become vacuous.

## 4 The Reduction Web: From SETH to the Polynomial Frontier

### 4.1 Sequence similarity: Edit Distance and LCS

The first spectacular application of the OV hub was to *sequence similarity measures*, problems whose quadratic dynamic programs had resisted decades of improvement.

The reduction encodes each vector $a \in A$ as a *gadget string* $G(a)$ and each $b \in B$ as $H(b)$; the full strings are concatenations $X = G(a_1) \cdots G(a_n)$ and $Y = H(b_1) \cdots H(b_n)$.

### 4.2 Graph diameter: approximation thresholds

Graph Diameter — the maximum shortest-path distance — is solvable in $\tilde{O}(mn)$ time, and a folklore $\tilde{O}(m)$ algorithm gives a 2-approximation.

The reduction builds a graph with $O(n)$ vertices from an OV instance of $n$ vectors: vector gadgets are connected through coordinate layers so that an orthogonal pair creates a *long* shortest path (diameter at least some threshold $D_1$) while its absence keeps all distances short (diameter at most $D_2 < \frac{2}{3} D_1$).

### 4.3 Dynamic algorithms: hardness that updates

The dynamic program of Abboud and Vassilevska Williams [5] translates static OV hardness into *update-time* lower bounds. The template: given an OV instance $(A, B)$, construct a graph in stages.

The framework's power lies in its *modularity*: the same static OV instance can be re-encoded for many dynamic problems by swapping the query gadget.

### 4.4 Strengthening the foundations: branching programs and weak SETH

A natural objection to SETH-based lower bounds is that SETH is a *strong* assumption: perhaps the quadratic hardness of Edit Distance follows from something much weaker.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

Fine-grained complexity is a mathematical rather than experimental science, so "empirical results" here means *theorems with exact quantitative content*: the precise exponents, approximation ratios, and dimension regimes that the reduction web establishes.

| Problem | Best known algorithm | Conditional lower bound | Hypothesis | Key reference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Orthogonal Vectors ($d = c\log n$) | $n^{2 - 1/O(\log c)}$ [3] | $n^{2-o(1)}$ | SETH | [2] |
| $k$-OV ($d = c\log n$) | $O(n^k \cdot \mathrm{poly}(d))$ | $n^{k-o(1)}$ | SETH | [2][6] |
| Edit Distance | $O(n^2)$ DP | $n^{2-o(1)}$ | SETH (even weak) | [8][9] |
| LCS | $O(n^2)$ DP | $n^{2-o(1)}$ | SETH | [8] |
| Diameter, $(3/2-\varepsilon)$-approx, sparse | $\tilde{O}(m\sqrt{n})$ 3/2-approx | $m^{2-o(1)}$ | SETH | [7] |
| Diameter, $(2-1/k-\varepsilon)$-approx, directed | trade-off curve | $n^{1+1/(k-1)-o(1)}$ | SETH via $k$-OV | [6] |
| Dynamic $s$–$t$ shortest path | $O(m)$ per update (recompute) | $m^{1-o(1)}$ amortized update | SETH/3SUM/APSP | [5] |
| Dynamic diameter query | $O(m)$ per query | $m^{1-o(1)}$ update | OVC / Hitting Set | [5] |

We now state the two flagship theorems in full quantitative form.

> Theorem: (Williams' OV lower bound [2]). Assume SETH. Then for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there is $c \ge 1$ such that Orthogonal Vectors on $n$ vectors in dimension $d = c \log n$ requires $n^{2-\varepsilon}$ time.

*Proof.* Suppose OV is solvable in $O(n^{2-\varepsilon} \cdot \mathrm{poly}(d))$ time. Given a $k$-CNF formula $\phi$ on $n$ variables, apply the split-and-list construction of §2.2 to obtain sets $A, B$ of $N = 2^{n/2}$ vectors in dimension $M = O(n)$; by sparsification, $M = O(n)$ can be taken as $d = \omega(\log N)$ with the reduction running in $2^{n/2} \cdot \mathrm{poly}(n)$ time. Running the hypothetical OV algorithm decides satisfiability of $\phi$ in time $O(N^{2-\varepsilon} \cdot \mathrm{poly}(n)) = O(2^{(1-\varepsilon/2)n} \cdot \mathrm{poly}(n))$, contradicting SETH. ∎

> Theorem: (Diameter inapproximability [7]). Assume SETH. For every $\varepsilon > 0$, any algorithm that $(3/2 - \varepsilon)$-approximates the diameter of an $m$-edge sparse undirected unweighted graph requires $m^{2-o(1)}$ time.

The proof constructs, from an OV instance with $n$ vectors, a graph with $O(n)$ vertices and $O(n \log n)$ edges whose diameter is at least $3t$ if an orthogonal pair exists and at most $2t$ otherwise (for a suitable $t$); a $(3/2-\varepsilon)$-approximation distinguishes the cases, hence solves OV subquadratically [7].

A compact reference implementation of the split-and-list reduction illustrates how directly the mathematics translates to code:

```python
def williams_reduction(clauses, n):
    """Split-and-list reduction from k-SAT to Orthogonal Vectors."""
    half = n // 2
    U = range(1 << half)          # partial assignments, first half
    V = range(1 << (n - half))    # partial assignments, second half

    def vec(assign, offset, width):
        v = []
        for (lits,) in clauses:   # one coordinate per clause
            sat = any(((assign >> (abs(x) - 1 - offset)) & 1) == (1 if x > 0 else 0)
                      for x in lits if offset <= abs(x) - 1 < offset + width)
            v.append(0 if sat else 1)   # 1 marks "clause still unsatisfied here"
        return v

    A = [vec(u, 0, half) for u in U]
    B = [vec(v, half, n - half) for v in V]
    return A, B

def has_orthogonal_pair(A, B):
    d = len(A[0])
    return any(all(a[i] * b[i] == 0 for i in range(d))
               for a in A for b in B)
```

Running `has_orthogonal_pair(*williams_reduction(clauses, n))` returns true iff the formula is satisfiable — the executable content of the theorem above [2].

Finally, the *approximation-time trade-off* for directed diameter [6] deserves emphasis as the field's most refined quantitative prediction: for each integer $k \ge 2$, the curve $(2 - 1/k)$-approximation in $n^{1+1/(k-1)}$ time is simultaneously achievable (up to the known algorithms) and conditionally optimal.

## 6 Limitations

**The hypotheses are unproven — and partly in tension with known algorithms.** SETH asserts optimality of brute force for $k$-SAT, yet the best known $k$-SAT algorithms *do* beat $2^n$ significantly for small $k$ (e.g., PPSZ achieves roughly $2^{0.386n}$ for 3-SAT). SETH only claims the advantage vanishes as $k \to \infty$, a regime where algorithmic progress is thinnest — but also where our intuition is weakest. Moreover, OV itself admits *nontrivial* algorithms: for $d = c \log n$, the $n^{2 - 1/O(\log c)}$ algorithms of Abboud–Williams–Yu and Chan–Williams [3] shave a subpolynomial factor, showing the quadratic barrier is not absolute even in the conjectured-hard regime.

**Fine-grained reductions relativize.** Like classical reductions, they cannot resolve **P** vs **NP**, and they inherit the standard barriers: no fine-grained reduction can prove an unconditional superlinear lower bound in a general model without separating complexity classes. The entire edifice is conditional; a single algorithmic breakthrough for $k$-SAT would not merely falsify one lower bound but *collapse the web* — Edit Distance, Diameter, dynamic APSP, and dozens of other "hard" problems would simultaneously lose their hardness evidence [4].

**Hypothesis fragility and the NSETH refinement.** Recent work has shown that some SETH-based lower bounds actually require only *NSETH* (nondeterministic SETH) or other variants, and Li's diameter results are tight "up to NSETH" [6] — meaning the *exact* hypothesis matters for the *exact* theorem. Practitioners must track which variant each result uses; citing "SETH" loosely overstates some claims.

**Worst-case vs. practical instances.** Conditional lower bounds are worst-case statements about adversarially constructed instances (often with $d = \Theta(\log n)$ vector gadgets bearing little resemblance to real data). They do not preclude dramatically faster algorithms on structured, low-dimensional, or average-case inputs — and indeed, for $d = o(\log n)$, OV is solvable in $n^{2-\Omega(1)}$ time by splitting techniques. The hardness lives in a narrow parameter band that reductions must carefully target [3].

**The dynamic frontier is partially eroded.** Since [5], several dynamic problems have seen improved upper bounds (e.g., near-linear-time decremental reachability via expander techniques) that did not refute the conjectures but narrowed the gaps, reminding us that conditional lower bounds are *moving targets* calibrated to current algorithmic knowledge.

## 7 Conclusion

Fine-grained complexity reframes the question "is this problem in **P**?" into the sharper "what is this problem's *exact* polynomial exponent?" The Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis [1] provides the conjecture; Williams' split-and-list reduction [2] provides the conduit from exponential to polynomial hardness; the Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture [3] distills the resulting hardness into a clean combinatorial statement; and the fine-grained reduction framework [4] provides the methodology for propagating it. The dividends — quadratic optimality of Edit Distance and LCS [8], the diameter approximation-time trade-off curve [6][7], update-time lower bounds for dynamic graph problems [5], and the robustness of these bounds under weak hypotheses [9] — constitute one of the most successful conditional theories in modern algorithms research.

The open frontier is clear. First, *unconditional* lower bounds in restricted models (circuits, branching programs, data structures with limited probes) that match the conditional ones would convert belief into theorem. Second, the *equivalence-class* program — showing that the natural problems in each hypothesis's territory are mutually reducible, as has been done for subcubic APSP-hard problems — should be completed for the SETH/OV territory, so that the web becomes a *completeness* theory rather than a hub-and-spoke picture. Third, the recent progress on the algorithmic side (subpolynomial shavings for OV, improved dynamic algorithms) suggests the true complexity landscape has finer structure than $n^{2-o(1)}$ vs. $n^{2-\varepsilon}$ can express; developing *second-order* fine-grained complexity, sensitive to polylogarithmic and subpolynomial factors, is the natural next step. Until then, SETH stands as the field's central bet — and the reduction web as the map of everything that bet entails.
