# Photonic Neural Networks on Silicon: Mach–Zehnder Meshes, In-Memory Weight Programming, and Optical Nonlinearities

## Abstract

Silicon photonics promises neural network accelerators in which linear algebra happens at the speed of light. Coherent architectures encode matrix-vector products in the interference of guided optical modes: any weight matrix is factored by singular value decomposition into two unitary meshes of Mach–Zehnder interferometers flanking a diagonal bank of tunable attenuators, so that a trained layer executes in effectively constant time and near-zero energy once programmed. Incoherent architectures trade phase information for parallelism, using wavelength-division multiplexing and nonvolatile phase-change memory to reach tera-MAC-per-second throughputs. Both families stand or fall on the same bottleneck: a cascadable, low-power optical nonlinearity to serve as the activation function. This article develops the theory of MZI mesh synthesis from Reck to Clements decompositions, surveys electro-optic, saturable-absorber, resonator-bistable, and phase-change nonlinearities, explains in-situ training by the adjoint-variable method, and evaluates reported results including vowel recognition and MNIST convolution, concluding with the scaling limits that define the research frontier.

##1 Intro

Deep neural networks are, arithmetically, machines for multiplying matrices. Training and inference both reduce overwhelmingly to general matrix multiply (GEMM) and its vector special case, and the energy cost of moving operands between memory and arithmetic units — the von Neumann bottleneck — now dominates the power budget of large models. Silicon photonics offers a radically different trade: once a set of phase shifters is programmed, an entire matrix-vector product is executed by the propagation of light through an interferometer mesh, at the latency of the time of flight and at an energy cost set by the detectors rather than the arithmetic. This is not a marginal improvement; for dense linear layers the optical approach promises orders-of-magnitude gains in throughput per watt [7].

Two architectural families divide the field. *Coherent* photonic neural networks encode both amplitude and phase of the optical field, using cascaded Mach–Zehnder interferometer (MZI) meshes to realize arbitrary complex-valued unitary transforms exactly as prescribed by the Reck and Clements decompositions [1][2]. *Incoherent* networks discard phase, multiplexing many wavelength channels through banks of tunable weights realized in nonvolatile phase-change materials, and have already demonstrated tera-scale multiply-accumulate (MAC) rates [4]. Both families share the same Achilles heel: the optical nonlinearity that must play the role of the activation function. Linear optics alone can only ever compute linear maps, so the depth and expressive power of a photonic network is set by the quality of its nonlinear units [8].

This article develops the complete stack of a photonic neural layer. We begin with the mathematics of universal MZI meshes and their singular-value-decomposition programming model, survey the physical mechanisms available for optical nonlinearities — electro-optic modulation, saturable absorption, resonator bistability, and phase-change switching — explain how networks can be trained *in situ* by the optical adjoint-variable method rather than on a digital twin, and evaluate the empirical record against fundamental scaling limits.

##2 Background

The intellectual origin of programmable photonic linear algebra is the 1994 result of Reck and colleagues, who showed that any discrete unitary operator on N optical modes can be realized as a triangular cascade of two-mode beam splitter transformations [1]. Each beam splitter, paired with phase shifters, implements a tunable rotation of a two-dimensional subspace, and nulling matrix elements one column at a time factorizes an arbitrary unitary into a product of such local operations. The construction is universal but geometrically awkward: in a triangular mesh, different optical paths traverse different numbers of components, so loss and fabrication error accumulate unevenly across modes.

Clements and collaborators removed this asymmetry in 2016 with the rectangular, or square, mesh design: the same N(N−1)/2 beam splitters are rearranged so that every input-to-output path crosses nearly the same number of MZIs, equalizing loss and making the circuit far more robust to correlated fabrication error [2]. The rectangular layout is now the standard cell of coherent photonic processors, and self-configuration algorithms exist that program it using only external sources and detectors, suppressing fabrication error by a quadratic factor [6].

A single MZI cell combines two nominally 50:50 directional couplers with thermo-optic or electro-optic phase shifters on its two arms. The internal phase θ sets the splitting ratio — a tunable beam splitter — while the external phase φ sets a relative phase between the output modes. Cascaded, these cells act as Givens rotations on neighboring waveguide modes; the mesh as a whole is a programmable unitary whose 2N² real degrees of freedom are exactly the parameter count of the unitary group U(N).

Weight matrices in a neural layer are not unitary, so the mesh is used in a singular value decomposition (SVD) configuration: an arbitrary real matrix W factors as UΣVᵀ, with U and Vᵀ implemented as two unitary meshes and Σ realized as a diagonal bank of variable optical attenuators that scale each singular mode independently [3]. Inputs are encoded on coherent carriers from an on-chip laser or a soliton frequency comb, computation proceeds by interference, and outputs are read by germanium photodetectors followed by analog-to-digital conversion [4].

Incoherent architectures take a different route: they give up phase encoding entirely and instead exploit wavelength-division multiplexing. A single soliton microcomb supplies dozens of mutually incoherent wavelength channels; each channel passes a phase-change memory cell whose transmission encodes one weight; a passive combining tree sums the channels; and a balanced photodetector pair produces the signed MAC result [4]. Nonvolatility is the decisive advantage — weights persist without holding power — while the cost is the loss of the complex-valued representations that coherent meshes provide for free.

##3 Methodology

A photonic neural layer implements the familiar affine-plus-nonlinearity map, but with the linear part executed by light. Concretely, for an input vector x encoded as optical field amplitudes on N waveguides, the layer computes y = f(Wx + b), where W is programmed into the photonic fabric and f is an optical or electro-optic nonlinearity.

**Programming the linear unit.** Given a trained weight matrix W, its SVD W = UΣVᵀ is computed digitally once. The unitaries U and Vᵀ are each translated into MZI phase settings by the Clements nulling algorithm, which walks through the matrix zeroing elements with successive Givens rotations and records the corresponding θ and φ for every cell. The singular values σ are normalized to the unit interval and mapped onto the attenuation settings of the diagonal bank: a value near one leaves the mode untouched, a value near zero extinguishes it. Because attenuation can only reduce amplitude, overall scale is carried by the input laser power and the detector gain, an important calibration detail [3].

> Theorem: *Universal linear synthesis.* Any N-by-N complex matrix W admits a decomposition W = UΣV† with U and V† unitary and Σ diagonal and nonnegative. Each unitary is realizable with N(N−1)/2 MZIs in a Clements rectangular mesh [2], and Σ is realizable with N tunable attenuators. Hence any linear map on N modes is exactly representable, up to global scale, by the three-section photonic fabric. The special case of unitary W (for example, orthogonal weight parametrizations) needs only one mesh [1].

**The forward pass.** The physical protocol proceeds as follows:

1. Encode the input vector as coherent amplitudes on the input waveguides, using modulators driven by digital-to-analog converters.
2. Propagate through the Vᵀ mesh, the attenuator bank, and the U mesh; interference performs the full matrix-vector product in the time of flight, on the order of tens of picoseconds.
3. Apply the optical nonlinearity f to each output mode — for example, a saturable absorber or an electro-optic feedback stage.
4. Detect with photodiodes, digitize, and pass the result to the next layer or the readout.

**In-situ training.** Rather than simulating the chip and copying phases over, the adjoint-variable method trains the physical device directly [5]. A forward pass records the field at every phase shifter; an error signal is then injected backward through the mesh; and the gradient of the loss with respect to each phase setting is obtained from intensity measurements combining the forward and adjoint fields at that shifter. No digital model of the fabrication imperfections is required — the device differentiates itself. The following sketch shows the offline part of the protocol, computing target mesh angles from a trained weight matrix:

```python
import numpy as np

def program_layer(W):
    """Return mesh phases (U, V) and attenuations (s) for a weight matrix W."""
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(W, full_matrices=True)
    s = s / s.max()          # normalize singular values to the unit interval
    theta_U, phi_U = clements_decompose(U)    # Givens nulling, rectangular mesh
    theta_V, phi_V = clements_decompose(Vt)
    return (theta_U, phi_U), s, (theta_V, phi_V)

def forward_optical(x, phases_U, s, phases_V):
    """Idealized coherent forward pass: y = U diag(s) V^T x."""
    U = clements_synthesize(*phases_U)
    V = clements_synthesize(*phases_V)
    return U @ (s[:, None] * (V.T @ x))
```

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Coherent linear units: from Reck to Clements

The Reck triangular mesh [1] and the Clements rectangular mesh [2] implement the same group — the full unitary group U(N) — but their error statistics differ sharply. In the triangular layout, the longest path crosses 2N−3 MZIs while the shortest crosses only a few, so a fabrication bias in beam splitter ratio corrupts some matrix elements far more than others. The rectangular layout balances every path to within one MZI of the mean depth of N, which both equalizes insertion loss and converts correlated splitter error into a benign global effect that calibration can absorb. Recent self-configuration results strengthen this further: a rectangular mesh can be programmed to a target unitary using only external measurements, with residual error scaling quadratically better than naive programming, removing what was once considered a hard limit on mesh size [6]. The price of universality is area: N(N−1)/2 MZIs per unitary section, so a full SVD layer costs O(N²) components and thermo-optic phase shifters that each dissipate milliwatts of holding power — the dominant energy term in large coherent meshes and the main argument for nonvolatile alternatives.

### 4.2 Optical nonlinearities as activation functions

A photonic network without nonlinearity is a single linear map no matter how many layers it has, so the activation function is where the physics gets difficult. Four mechanisms dominate the literature, and their measured transfer curves tell the story [8]:

- *Electro-optic modulation.* Tapping a fraction of the optical power to drive a modulator yields a smooth sigmoidal transmission curve as a function of input power — a natural optical sigmoid or tanh. It is fast and cascadable but requires an optical-to-electrical-to-optical conversion at every neuron, surrendering some of the energy advantage.
- *Saturable absorption.* Materials whose absorption bleaches at high intensity produce a concave saturating curve, the optical analogue of a softplus. Fully passive and ultrafast, but the contrast between low and high transmission is modest, limiting the sharpness of the decision boundary.
- *Microring resonator bistability.* A resonator with a Kerr or thermal nonlinearity exhibits hysteresis: the up-sweep and down-sweep transmission curves differ, giving a history-dependent, step-like activation. The bistability provides a form of optical memory, but the hysteresis loop complicates training, since the function is no longer single-valued.
- *Phase-change switching.* Cells of Ge2Sb2Te5 (GST) switch between amorphous and crystalline states with large contrast in both refractive index and absorption, and can be set to multiple intermediate levels. The transfer curve is stepwise and nonvolatile, making GST cells attractive both as weights and as thresholding nonlinearities, at the cost of slower switching and finite write endurance.

| Mechanism | Transfer shape | All-optical | Speed | Key drawback |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electro-optic modulator | Sigmoidal | No (O/E/O) | Tens of GHz | Conversion energy per neuron |
| Saturable absorber | Concave saturating | Yes | Femtosecond | Low contrast |
| Microring bistability | Hysteretic step | Yes | GHz (thermal/Kerr) | History dependence |
| Phase-change (GST) | Stepwise, multilevel | Yes | MHz–GHz | Write endurance, speed |

No single mechanism yet satisfies all desiderata — cascadability, low threshold power, speed, and a clean differentiable curve — which is why hybrid electro-optic activations remain the pragmatic choice in most system demonstrations [8].

### 4.3 Incoherent architectures: WDM tensor cores and phase-change memory

Incoherent photonic tensor cores abandon phase encoding and recover massive parallelism through wavelength multiplexing [4]. A soliton Kerr frequency comb generated in a microresonator supplies a grid of equally spaced, mutually incoherent carriers from a single pump laser. Each carrier is modulated with one element of the input vector, passes a GST phase-change cell whose transmission encodes the corresponding weight, and all channels are combined in a passive waveguide tree. Because the wavelengths are incoherent, powers — not fields — add, and a balanced photodetector pair produces the signed weighted sum directly as a photocurrent: y equals the sum over channels of input times weight, the optical MAC. Feldmann and colleagues demonstrated this architecture performing convolutions at rates exceeding a tera-MAC per second with 14 GHz modulation bandwidth, using the nonvolatility of the PCM cells to hold a full kernel bank without static power [4]. Signed weights are handled by splitting each weight across the two arms of the balanced detector. The architecture scales in the wavelength dimension rather than the spatial one: adding channels costs comb lines and PCM cells, not additional meshes, which is why incoherent designs currently lead on raw throughput even though they cannot exploit the complex-valued representations natural to coherent meshes.

### 4.4 Precision, noise, and calibration

Analog photonic computation trades digital precision for speed, and the exchange rate is set by noise. In coherent meshes the dominant error sources are beam splitter ratio deviations from the ideal 50:50, phase shifter miscalibration, and thermal crosstalk between neighboring heaters; each contributes phase errors that compound across the O(N) optical depth of the mesh. Demonstrated systems typically achieve an effective precision of 4 to 8 bits per weight, adequate for inference on models trained with quantization awareness but short of the 16-plus bits of digital accelerators [7][8]. Incoherent systems face a different noise budget: relative intensity noise of the comb lines, PCM programming stochasticity, and detector shot noise, with precision limited by the number of distinguishable GST transmission levels and the extinction ratio of the modulators. Calibration is therefore a first-class design problem. Self-configuration algorithms that program rectangular meshes from external measurements alone suppress fabrication error quadratically [6], and in-situ training goes further by optimizing the physical hardware directly against the task loss, automatically absorbing every static imperfection into the learned phases [5]. The residual, irreducible costs sit at the boundaries: digital-to-analog converters to encode inputs, analog-to-digital converters to read outputs, and the lasers themselves, whose wall-plug efficiency bounds the system-level energy per operation.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

The empirical record of photonic neural networks is a ladder of increasing scale. The milestones below are the results most often used to calibrate claims:

| Demonstration | Architecture | Task | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shen et al., 2017 [3] | Coherent, 56-MZI SVD mesh | 4-vowel recognition | 76.7% experimental accuracy |
| Hughes et al., 2018 [5] | Coherent mesh, simulated | In-situ adjoint training | Gradient exactness verified numerically |
| Feldmann et al., 2021 [4] | Incoherent WDM + PCM tensor core | MNIST convolution | 95.3% accuracy at >1 TMAC/s |
| Self-configuration, 2022 [6] | Rectangular mesh, algorithmic | Unitary programming | Quadratic error suppression vs naive |

Three quantitative facts deserve emphasis. First, the *complexity* argument: an N-by-N matrix-vector product that costs O(N²) digital operations executes in a single photonic time step, so the speedup grows with problem size; the 76.7% vowel result used a 4-by-4 mesh, and the path to larger meshes is limited by loss and area rather than by any algorithmic barrier [3][7]. Second, the *throughput* argument: the Feldmann tensor core reached tera-MAC-per-second rates because wavelength parallelism multiplies the per-channel modulation bandwidth — 16 comb lines at 14 GHz already exceed a trillion operations per second, and the computation itself is passive transmission through non-resonant components [4]. Third, the *training* argument: Hughes and colleagues proved that the gradient of any loss with respect to every tunable phase in the mesh can be recovered exactly from intensity measurements of the forward and adjoint fields, establishing in-situ backpropagation as the optical analogue of reverse-mode differentiation and removing the need for a faithful digital twin of the imperfect chip [5].

> Theorem: *Exact in-situ gradients.* For a photonic network whose tunable parameters enter through local phase shifts, the derivative of a scalar loss with respect to each parameter equals (up to a known prefactor) the imaginary part of the overlap between the forward-propagated field and the adjoint field backpropagated from the output error, evaluated at that parameter. Both fields are physical and measurable, so gradient descent can be executed on the hardware itself [5].

Against these results must be weighed the reviews' consensus: coherent systems have demonstrated the cleanest mathematics but the smallest scales, while incoherent systems have demonstrated the largest throughputs but coarser precision, and neither has yet demonstrated an end-to-end trained deep network competitive with digital baselines on a production workload [7][8].

##6 Limitations

The limitations of photonic neural networks are physical, not conceptual, and they cluster around five points. **Loss** accumulates through every component: each MZI contributes a fraction of a decibel, each grating coupler several decibels, and a deep coherent mesh quickly demands optical amplification that erases the energy advantage. **Phase sensitivity** makes coherent meshes exquisite sensors of their own temperature; thermal crosstalk between phase shifters and drift over minutes to hours require active stabilization or frequent recalibration. **Area scaling** is quadratic: a universal N-mode unitary needs N(N−1)/2 MZIs, each hundreds of micrometers long on a silicon photonics process, so a 64-mode mesh already spans a sizable chip. **Nonlinearity** remains the deepest problem — every demonstrated optical activation is either weak, slow, power-hungry, or non-cascadable, and the most capable systems still fall back on electro-optic conversion at each layer [8]. **Precision and I/O** bound the system: 4-to-8-bit effective weight precision, ADC/DAC energy at every layer boundary, and laser wall-plug efficiency together determine whether the optical core's theoretical advantage survives contact with a real computing stack [7]. None of these is a no-go theorem, but collectively they explain why photonics currently accelerates specific linear workloads rather than replacing digital deep learning wholesale.

##7 Conclusion

Photonic neural networks recast the dominant operation of deep learning — the matrix-vector product — as the free propagation of light through a programmed interferometer mesh or a multiplexed weight bank. The mathematics is settled: Reck and Clements decompositions give universal coherent linear units [1][2], singular value decomposition maps arbitrary trained weights onto the fabric [3], the adjoint-variable method provides exact in-situ gradients [5], and self-configuration tames fabrication error [6]. The physics of scale is demonstrated: vowel recognition on a 56-MZI coherent chip [3] and tera-MAC-per-second convolution on a phase-change WDM tensor core [4]. What remains is the engineering of the missing middle — a fast, low-power, cascadable optical nonlinearity; deeper meshes with manageable loss; and system architectures whose boundary conversion costs do not devour the optical advantage [7][8]. The field's trajectory suggests not a replacement of electronics but a division of labor: photonics for the dense linear algebra at the heart of inference, electronics for control, memory, and everything nonlinear that light cannot yet do cheaply. That hybrid, executed well, is where the orders-of-magnitude gains live.
