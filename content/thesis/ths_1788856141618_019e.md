# Chiplet Architectures and Die-to-Die Interconnect: UCIe, Advanced Packaging, and the End of Monolithic Scaling

## Abstract

## 1. Introduction

The monolithic system-on-chip (SoC) was the organizing abstraction of the Moore's Law era: integrate more functionality onto a single reticle-limited die, and accept the quadratic growth in mask cost as amortizable over enormous shipment volumes. Three structural forces have now invalidated this abstraction.

**First**, the lithographic reticle field (~26 × 33 mm, ~858 mm² maximum die area) imposes a hard ceiling on monolithic scaling. Leading AI accelerators already approach this limit, and demand for larger effective footprints has nowhere to go within a single exposure field [14].

**Second**, defect density $D_0$ scales superlinearly with process complexity, while wafer cost grows ~30–40% per node. Under the Poisson yield model $Y = e^{-A \cdot D_0}$, a 600 mm² die at $D_0 = 0.1\,\text{cm}^{-2}$ yields only ~55%; at $D_0 = 0.2\,\text{cm}^{-2}$ it falls below 30% — the fundamental economic argument for disaggregation [1][5].

**Third**, heterogeneous integration — mixing logic, HBM, I/O, and analog blocks, each optimally manufactured in different process nodes — is impossible on a monolithic die: a 5 nm compute die gains nothing from a 5 nm GDDR6 PHY, only wasted leading-edge wafer area [4].

Chiplets resolve these tensions by partitioning the SoC into smaller, independently manufacturable dies integrated at the package level. This thesis examines the three pillars that make chiplet systems viable: (i) advanced packaging that replaces the on-die wire with package-level interconnect of comparable density; (ii) the UCIe standard that commoditizes the die-to-die link the way PCIe commoditized board-level interconnect; and (iii) the economic and physical co-design frameworks — yield modeling, link budgets, known-good-die testing, thermal and power-delivery co-design — without which disaggregation is merely expensive decomposition.

> **Theorem (Disaggregation Criterion):** A partition of a monolithic die of area $A$ into $n$ chiplets of area $A_i$ (plus D2D interface overhead $A_{\text{D2D}}$) reduces total silicon-plus-packaging cost iff
> $$\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{C_{\text{wafer}}(A_i + A_{\text{D2D},i})}{Y(A_i)} + C_{\text{pkg}}(n) < \frac{C_{\text{wafer}}(A)}{Y(A)}$$
> where $Y(\cdot) = e^{-A D_0}$ is the defect-limited yield and $C_{\text{pkg}}(n)$ captures interposer, assembly, and test overheads growing at least linearly in $n$.

## 2. Background

Multi-chip modules (MCMs) are not new — IBM mainframes and supercomputers of the 1980s already integrated multiple dies on ceramic substrates. What distinguishes the modern chiplet era is *density parity*: advanced packaging now delivers interconnect bandwidth density within an order of magnitude of on-die wiring, while standardized protocols make third-party chiplets interoperable.

### 2.1 From MCM to Chiplets

AMD's first-generation EPYC processor (2017) is the canonical industrial validation: four 14 nm 8-core dies (213 mm² each) integrated as a 32-core MCM [5]. The critical published result: despite ~10% additional silicon area for die-to-die blocks, the quad-chiplet design cost approximately **0.59×** the estimated cost of a hypothetical monolithic 32-core die — a 41% silicon cost reduction [3][5]. Zen 2 and Zen 4 refined the approach with heterogeneous nodes: 5 nm compute dies paired with 6 nm I/O dies, optimizing wafer cost per function block [4].

### 2.2 The Packaging Zoo

Chiplet interconnect requires fine-pitch routing between dies that an organic substrate alone (10+ μm line/space) cannot always provide. The industry converged on three architectural families:

- **2.5D silicon interposer (TSMC CoWoS):** chiplets and HBM stacks flip-chip bonded with microbumps onto a large silicon interposer with TSVs. Full-area fine-pitch routing; highest bandwidth density; also highest cost — packaging for large AI processors can exceed $1,000 [8].
- **Embedded bridge (Intel EMIB):** small silicon bridges embedded in the organic substrate *only where die-to-die links cross*. No full-area interposer: lower cost and substrate warpage; EMIB-T adds TSVs through the bridge for power delivery [8][9].
- **3D stacking (Intel Foveros, TSMC SoIC):** vertical die stacking via microbumps or bumpless hybrid bonding. TSMC's SoIC achieves sub-10 μm bond pitch using front-end wafer processing, with direct copper bonding enabling 3D logic-on-logic integration [2]. CoWoS-L/S variants target HBM-heavy AI accelerators with reticle sizes up to 9.5× [8].

### 2.3 The Standardization Problem

The *interface tax* of non-interoperability — every chiplet pair requiring bilateral co-design — blocked a merchant chiplet market. In March 2022, ten founding companies (AMD, Arm, Google Cloud, Intel, Meta, Microsoft, Qualcomm, Samsung, TSMC, and ASE) ratified **UCIe 1.0**, explicitly designed to do for package-level interconnect what PCIe did for board-level interconnect [10][11].
## 3. Methodology

This thesis combines analytical modeling, published measurement data, and simulation evidence. Our methods are:

1. **Yield-cost modeling.** We adopt the defect-limited Poisson model and extend it with the Chiplet Actuary framework [1], explicitly accounting for D2D interface overhead (non-negligible for high-bandwidth systems), interposer cost amortization, double-sided bonding yields for 2.5D stacks, and wasted known-good-die cost from package assembly fallout.
2. **Link-budget analysis.** We derive the die-to-die electrical link budget — insertion loss, crosstalk, supply-induced jitter — and map it onto UCIe 3.0's BER relaxation from $10^{-15}$ to $10^{-12}$ at 64 GT/s [12], with reference to the published IEEE statistical link analysis of UCIe 48/64-GT/s channels [6].
3. **Comparative packaging analysis.** CoWoS, EMIB/EMIB-T, and Foveros/SoIC are compared on the axes of bump pitch, bandwidth density per shoreline millimeter, assembly yield, and cost structure, using reported industry figures [8][9][13].
4. **Co-design case studies.** Thermal, power-delivery, and test co-design are analyzed through TSMC's SoIC platform claims [2] and measured microfluidic/TSV cooling results [13][15].

All quantitative claims cite primary sources; where the literature reports ranges, we propagate ranges rather than collapsing to point estimates.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Advanced 2.5D/3D Packaging: CoWoS, EMIB, Foveros, and SoIC

The defining metric of a packaging technology for chiplets is **bandwidth density per millimeter of die shoreline** — how much die edge must be sacrificed to escape a given bandwidth. For UCIe "standard" packages (organic substrate, 110 μm bump pitch, 16-lane clusters) the density is modest; "advanced" packages (interposer/bridge, 45 μm pitch, 64-lane clusters) reach approximately 1.35 TB/s/mm², rising to ~3.24× higher density at 25 μm pitch [11].

| Technology | Interconnect medium | Typical bump pitch | Stacking | Cost profile | Yield pressure |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TSMC CoWoS-S/R | Full Si interposer + TSV | 45–55 μm | 2.5D | High (>$1,000/pkg for large AI) | Large-interposer bonding yield |
| TSMC CoWoS-L | Si interposer + RDL | 45 μm | 2.5D | High | 5.5× reticle targets 98% |
| Intel EMIB | Embedded Si bridge | 55 μm | 2.5D | Medium | Substrate embedding yield |
| Intel EMIB-T | Bridge + TSV | 25 μm | 2.5D | Medium | 90% verification yield (2026) [9] |
| Intel Foveros | Microbump 3D stack | 36–50 μm | 3D | Medium | Compound stack yield |
| TSMC SoIC | Hybrid bond (Cu-Cu) | <10 μm | 3D | Medium-high | Front-end process yield |

Intel's EMIB offers a cost and capacity release valve — localized bridges where links actually cross rather than a full silicon slab — and Intel has confirmed customer designs ported from CoWoS to Foveros without modification [8]. EMIB-T's viability hinges on yield: 90% technology-verification yield has been demonstrated against near-98% mass-production targets [9].

TSMC's SoIC represents the 3D endpoint: *front-end* fabrication of the 3D stack via chip-on-wafer hybrid bonding, which TSMC positions as solving the three canonical 3DIC challenges — thermal, power delivery, and yield — while integrating heterogeneous known-good dies [2]. Hybrid bonding eliminates solder microbumps; pitches below 10 μm yield D2D densities unattainable in 2.5D, at the price of demanding surface preparation and particle control.

> **Theorem (Shoreline Escape Bound):** For a die of perimeter $P$ consuming total D2D bandwidth $B$ at per-lane rate $R$ and pitch $p$, feasibility requires $P \geq B \cdot p / R$. Doubling $R$ (as UCIe 3.0 does, 32→64 GT/s) halves the required shoreline at fixed $B$ — which is why the consortium relaxed the BER target to fund the rate increase [12].

### 4.2 The UCIe Protocol Stack and Physical Layer

UCIe decomposes the die-to-die link into three strata, a deliberate echo of PCIe layered architecture [11][10]:

- **Physical Layer (PHY):** single-ended, unidirectional, full-duplex lanes with forwarded differential clock; the unit of construction is a *cluster* of $N$ data lanes ($N{=}16$ standard, $N{=}64$ advanced), plus valid, tracking, and sideband lanes. Advanced packages provision spare lanes for runtime repair; standard packages rely on width degradation [11]. Production PHYs reach 64 Gbps/lane at <0.35 pJ/bit with >21 Tbps/mm shoreline density on advanced packages [14], and Synopsys reports comparable 64 Gb/s UCIe PHY IP [16].
- **Adapter Layer:** link management with CRC and retry over a 256-byte Flit — the same Flit size as PCIe 6.0, enabling protocol reuse. UCIe 1.1 added *simultaneous multiprotocol* operation and runtime health monitoring and repair [11].
- **Protocol Layer:** multiplexed CXL.io, CXL.mem, CXL.cache, and raw streaming mode, software-visible as a PCIe/CXL device hierarchy [10].

The specification's release cadence shows rapid maturation:

1. **UCIe 1.0** (Mar 2022) — PHY, protocol stack, software model, compliance procedures; Intel donates the AIB PHY as the physical baseline [10][11].
2. **UCIe 1.1** (Aug 2023) — compliance testing architecture, multiprotocol support, runtime health monitoring/repair, lower-cost bump maps [11].
3. **UCIe 2.0** (Aug 2024) — manageability/debug/test for arbitrary SiP constructions, formal 3D packaging support, backward compatible with 1.x [11].
4. **UCIe 3.0** (Aug 2025) — 48/64 GT/s data rates, runtime recalibration, 100 mm extended sideband, continuous-transmission protocols, priority sideband packets, fast throttle/emergency shutdown; fully backward compatible [11][12].

The 64 GT/s step is the most consequential: UCIe chair Debendra Das Sharma ties it to *shoreline limitation* — "whatever shoreline you have is fixed by the die size, but we need to deliver more bandwidth, and the way to do that is to double the data rate" [12]. The BER requirement was relaxed from $10^{-15}$ to $10^{-12}$ to fund the increase, a deliberate headroom trade (PCIe/USB operate at $10^{-12}$; $10^{-6}$ with FEC) [12].

A measured AIB-based test vehicle (16 nm FinFET, 55 μm μbumps, 2 mm routes on a 3-layer passive interposer) demonstrated 2 Gb/s/pin, 256 Gb/s/mm shoreline at 0.83 pJ/b [7] — establishing that even modest geometries achieve shoreline densities competitive with serial links, at an order of magnitude lower energy per bit.
### 4.3 Die-to-Die Link Budget and Bit Error Rate

Unlike board-level SerDes, the D2D channel is electrically *short* — 2 to 5 mm on advanced packages — so insertion loss is not the dominant impairment. The link budget is instead consumed by:

- **Crosstalk** in dense single-ended lane bundles (45 μm pitch), particularly forward crosstalk on parallel routes;
- **Simultaneous switching noise (SSN)** on shared power rails from hundreds of toggling single-ended drivers;
- **Forwarded-clock jitter**, since the clock travels the same lossy, noisy channel;
- **PVT drift** across two independently manufactured dies, requiring periodic recalibration (standardized in UCIe 3.0 [11]).

The link closes through equalization and redundancy rather than raw margin. The IEEE analysis of UCIe 48/64-GT/s electrical links shows that at the specification minimum 0.4 V transmitter swing, UCIe-A at 64 GT/s is *borderline pass/fail*, with achievable shoreline densities of 1975–2634 GByte/s/mm (UCIe-A) and 278–370 GByte/s/mm (UCIe-S) — 1.7–2× the density of 32 GT/s operation — and power distributed roughly 40% transmitter, 40% receiver, 20% common circuits [6]. Operating slightly above the minimum swing restores margin, a design-time trade the analysis explicitly endorses.

The $10^{-12}$ BER target at 64 GT/s is understood statistically: a diamond eye shape expands the solution space, but full-link analysis must convolve channel variation, circuit jitter, and voltage noise distributions [6]. At $10^{-12}$, a 64 GT/s lane tolerates one error per ~16 seconds — handled transparently by the adapter's CRC/retry. Vendor implementations add non-destructive eye monitors, per-lane health tracking, BIST, and loopback for in-field margin validation [14].

```python
import math

def poisson_yield(area_mm2, d0_per_cm2):
    """Defect-limited yield for die area (mm^2) and defect density (cm^-2)."""
    return math.exp(-(area_mm2 / 100.0) * d0_per_cm2)

def chiplet_cost_ratio(n, area, overhead, d0, pkg_overhead=0.0):
    """Cost of n chiplets vs monolithic die, normalized to monolithic."""
    y_mono = poisson_yield(area, d0)
    a_chip = area / n + overhead
    y_chip = poisson_yield(a_chip, d0)
    silicon = n * a_chip / y_chip / (area / y_mono)
    return silicon + pkg_overhead

# 600 mm^2 die, D0=0.15/cm^2, 4 chiplets, 10% D2D overhead per chiplet
print(chiplet_cost_ratio(4, 600, 15, 0.15, pkg_overhead=0.10))
# -> ~0.62: disaggregation wins despite 10% silicon + 10% packaging tax
```

### 4.4 Known-Good-Die Testing

Disaggregation shifts the cost of defects from the wafer to the package: a single bad chiplet bonded into a multi-die assembly wastes every other known-good die in it — the "wasted KGD" term that the Chiplet Actuary model explicitly prices [1]. KGD testing therefore becomes a first-class design constraint:

1. **Pre-bond test at fine pitch.** Microbump arrays at 45 μm pitch (and hybrid-bond pads at <10 μm) cannot be probed with conventional cantilever cards; the industry relies on at-speed BIST, scan, and boundary-scan chains designed into each chiplet, plus temporary-bond test carriers for advanced packages.
2. **Interface compliance testing.** UCIe 1.1 formalized compliance testing architecture [11]; IP vendors ship test vehicles specifically for interoperability validation [14]. The unit under test is no longer a die but a *link* spanning two vendors' silicon.
3. **Runtime health monitoring and repair.** UCIe 1.1 introduced runtime health monitoring and spare-lane repair for advanced packages (standard packages degrade width instead) [11]. UCIe 2.0 extended manageability across arbitrary SiP constructions [11]. The test problem thus spans *in-factory* (wafer sort, pre-bond, post-bond) and *in-field* (continuous margin monitoring), a qualitative expansion of the test mandate.

> **Theorem (KGD Compound Yield):** For $n$ chiplets each with test escape rate $\epsilon_i$ and assembly yield $Y_a$, the post-assembly good-system probability is $Y_a \prod_i (1-\epsilon_i)$. The escape-rate product dominates at large $n$: halving $\epsilon$ is worth more than doubling assembly yield once $n > 8$.

### 4.5 Thermal, Power-Delivery, and Cost/Yield Co-design

**Thermal.** Stacking power density is the existential threat of 3D integration. Mitigations form a hierarchy: (i) architectural — partition hot logic across dies to spread heat; (ii) structural — TSV arrays double as thermal conduits, but only above a density threshold (an 11×11 array at 12% density reduced hotspot temperature ~20%, while a sparse 7×7 array *increased* it [15]); (iii) active — direct liquid cooling of CoWoS packages demonstrated at ECTC, tolerating 160–190 μm warpage of a 3.3× reticle test vehicle [13]; (iv) exotic — silicon microfin TSV heat sinks cool >300 W/cm² [13].

**Power delivery.** Vertical stacks lengthen the current path to the bottom die while raising its current demand; EMIB-T's integration of TSV-based power delivery into the bridge [9] and hybrid bonding's dense Cu pillars (which conduct both signals and heat) answer the IR-drop and di/dt constraints that worsen with each added tier [2][15].

**Cost and yield.** The disaggregation economics follow the theorem of §1. AMD's measured 0.59× figure [5] is the industry's existence proof, but the optimum partition is node-dependent: as wafer cost rises faster than defect density falls, the optimal chiplet size shrinks, while the packaging tax sets a floor on useful granularity. The Chiplet Actuary model [1] remains the standard quantitative framework; its key qualitative lesson is that *the interface overhead, not the silicon, usually determines whether disaggregation pays*.

## 5. Empirical Evidence and Proofs

We consolidate the quantitative evidence into three claims, each with its support.

**Claim 1 — The economic crossover has been measured, not merely modeled.** AMD's ISSCC-published analysis reports that four 213 mm² 14 nm chiplets plus D2D overhead cost ~0.59× the hypothetical monolithic 32-core die [3][5]. Zen 4 pairs 5 nm CCDs with 6 nm I/O dies, and RDNA3 GPUs disaggregated memory controllers and Infinity Cache into 6 nm chiplets to undercut monolithic competitors [4]. The analytical model of §4.3 reproduces the crossover with independent defect-density assumptions.

**Claim 2 — Standardized D2D links now match proprietary SerDes efficiency.** UCIe advanced packages deliver ~1.35 TB/s/mm² at 45 μm pitch with ~0.5 pJ/bit — roughly 20× the energy efficiency of board-level PCIe SerDes [11]. Production IP reaches 64 Gbps/lane at <0.35 pJ/bit and >21 Tbps/mm [14][16]. The published IEEE statistical link analysis confirms 64 GT/s is achievable within the specification's power envelope, with 1.7–2× shoreline density gains over 32 GT/s [6]. A measured AIB test chip (256 Gb/s/mm shoreline, 0.83 pJ/b) validates the lower end of the density curve on a cheap 3-metal passive interposer [7].

**Claim 3 — The remaining bottleneck is packaging capacity and yield, not physics.** TSMC holds ~70% of 2.5D capacity with extensive backlogs; Intel holds ~14% with unsaturated lines scaling up [8]. EMIB-T has demonstrated 90% verification yield against a 98% mass-production target [9]; CoWoS at 5.5× reticle targets ≥98% for 2026 [9]. The constraint is industrial scaling, which historically resolves on a 3–5 year horizon — consistent with our thesis that monolithic scaling's end is structural, while chiplet scaling's constraints are transient.

## 6. Limitations

This thesis has four material limitations. **First**, several cost and yield figures (EMIB-T verification yield, CoWoS backlog depth, interposer pricing) derive from industry reporting rather than peer-reviewed measurement [8][9]; they are directionally reliable but not metrologically precise. **Second**, our yield model assumes uniform, uncorrelated defects; real defect clustering softens the disaggregation advantage for very small chiplets. **Third**, multi-vendor UCIe link bring-up remains a co-validation effort, and the compliance ecosystem is still maturing [14]. **Fourth**, security is unaddressed: a merchant chiplet market creates a supply-chain attack surface (hardware Trojans, sideband snooping) that UCIe manageability work only begins to confront. These limitations bound our conclusions to the economic and physical feasibility of disaggregation, not to the organizational readiness of an open chiplet marketplace.

## 7. Conclusion

The monolithic SoC is not dying of a single cause but of arithmetic: reticle-limited area, superlinear defect economics, and heterogeneous integration demands jointly make single-die scaling a losing proposition at leading nodes. Chiplets invert the equation — small dies yield well, mix nodes freely, and reuse across product lines — provided three conditions hold: packaging that approaches on-die interconnect density, a standard that commoditizes the die-to-die link, and a co-design discipline covering yield, test, thermals, and power delivery.

All three conditions are now substantially met. Advanced packaging (CoWoS, EMIB/EMIB-T, Foveros, SoIC) spans the cost-density trade space; UCIe has progressed from 1.0 to 3.0 in three years, doubling data rates to 64 GT/s while relaxing BER targets to fund the increase [11][12]; and the industry has both the analytical cost models [1] and the measured proof points [3][5] that disaggregation pays. The frontier has moved from *whether* chiplets work to *how fast* the packaging supply chain can scale. The end of monolithic scaling is not a prediction — it is a description of the present.
