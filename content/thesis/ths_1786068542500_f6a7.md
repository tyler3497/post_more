# Scalable Training of Mixture-of-Experts Large Language Models: Expert Parallelism Topologies, Capacity Factor Token Dropping Tradeoffs, and Load-Balanced All-to-All Communication Fusion

## Abstract
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) decouples model capacity from per-token compute, enabling trillion-parameter language models at constant FLOPs. Distributed training of MoE, however, reintroduces cost via all-to-all token dispatch and expert load imbalance. This thesis systematizes expert parallelism (EP) topologies, capacity factor token-dropping tradeoffs, and load-balanced communication fusion for Switch-style and GShard-style MoE. We formalize EP as sharding of expert FFNs across devices contrasted with tensor parallelism, analyze capacity factor C = (T/E)*γ and its effect on dropped token rate and memory overprovisioning, and compare token-choice Top-k versus expert-choice routing for perfect balance guarantees. We propose a fused dispatch-compute-combine kernel model that overlaps all-to-all with expert GEMMs and mitigates straggler effects from skewed routing. Empirical synthesis from GShard 600B, Switch Transformer 1.6T, and Mixtral 8x7B deployments shows EP achieves up to 9× throughput over pure tensor parallelism at 128 experts, but requires auxiliary load balancing loss and z-loss regularization to keep imbalance < 3%. We provide theoretical bounds, implementation patterns, and open problems for loss-free balancing and fine-grained expert co-scheduling.

## 1. Introduction

The scaling of dense Transformers is bounded by **quadratic attention and linear FFN cost** per token. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) evades this by conditional computation: for each token `x ∈ R^{d_model}`, a router `R(x)` selects a sparse subset of `k << E` experts from `E` total experts, and only those FFNs are evaluated [1][2].

*Why MoE matters now:* inference cost dominates training cost for deployed LLMs. An MoE with 8 experts and top-2 routing activates ~2× FFN FLOPs but exposes 8× parameters for memorization and specialization. Models like Mixtral 8x7B, DeepSeek-MoE, and Switch-Transformer demonstrate 1.5–7× efficiency gains at matched perplexity compared to dense Chinchilla-optimal baselines.

Scaling MoE to 2048 experts on 1024 accelerators exposes systems wall: weights don't fit, tokens must be routed:

- `dispatch_mask ∈ {0,1}^{G×S×E×C}` from GShard [2]
- `combine_weights ∈ R^{G×S×E×C}` specifying how expert outputs recombine

`C` is **per-expert capacity**, the maximum tokens an expert takes per group. Everything else—parallel topology, communication schedule, load balancing—is organized to manage this sparse `E × G × C` exchange.

> The central thesis of efficient MoE training is simple: keep the router sparse, the experts balanced, and the all-to-all overlapped.

This work focuses on three pillars:

1. **Expert Parallelism Topologies**: data, tensor, expert, and hybrid sharding.
2. **Capacity Factor Token-Dropping Tradeoffs**: static shape versus dynamic routing.
3. **Load-Balanced All-to-All Fusion**: communication, auxiliary loss, and expert-choice determinism.

We synthesize real implementations in TensorRT-LLM [5], GShard TPU training [2][8], and latest Least-Loaded Expert Parallelism [6].

## 2. Background: From Sparsely-Gated MoE to Switch

MoE originated with Shazeer et al. (2017) top-k noisy gating. GShard [2] scaled it to 600B parameters with automatic sharding and group-level routing. Switch Transformer [1] simplified to **top-1 routing** with capacity factor and auxiliary loss.

Classic Switch MoE layer:

- Router: `logits = x * W_router ∈ R^E`, `p = softmax(logits)`
- Selection: `i* = argmax p`, `gate = p_{i*}`
- Expert compute: `y = gate * FFN_{i*}(x)`
- Auxiliary loss: `L_aux = α * E * Σ f_i * P_i` where `f_i` fraction tokens dispatched to expert `i`, `P_i` mean routing probability

This is **token-choice**: tokens pick experts. Alternative: **expert-choice** [3][4] where experts pick tokens, guaranteeing perfect balance without auxiliary loss.

Two failure modes dominate:

- *Expert collapse*: router concentrates on few experts, `f_i` highly skewed, entropy of routing `H(p) → 0`
- *Token dropping*: when tokens routed to expert exceed capacity `C`, overflow tokens skip expert via residual, hurting quality [1]

GShard mitigation: groups `G` allow `S` tokens per group to be routed locally, capacity `C = 2*N/(G*E)` for top-2 [2]. Random second-expert stochastic dropping improves quality.

Switch mitigation: capacity factor `γ` [1] (Equation 3), z-loss to penalize large router logits, and careful initialization.

**Bold insight**: *Load balancing is not auxiliary—it determines hardware efficiency.* Imbalance of 2× in tokens per expert translates to 2× straggler latency in expert-parallel execution because barrier all-to-all forces equal pacing.

## 3. Expert Parallelism Topologies

Expert Parallelism (EP) distributes experts' full weight across devices; Tensor Parallel (TP) shards each expert across devices [5]. Formally:

- EP rank `r` holds experts `E_r ⊂ E`, `|E_r| = E / N_EP`, processes tokens `T_r = { x | router(x) ∈ E_r }`
- TP rank holds slices `W1[:, d/N_TP], W2[d/N_TP, :]` for *all* experts, processes *all* tokens

Thus:

- EP communication: **all-to-all** of hidden states `H ∈ R^{S×d}` (sparse dispatch/combine)
- TP communication: **all-reduce / all-gather** of partial GEMMs (dense)

Hybrid EP×TP aims for balance: each EP group contains TP shards, product `TP*EP = world_size` must match non-MoE parallelism [5].

*Italic tradeoffs:* EP excels when `E` large (64–2048) and `k` small (1–2). TP excels when hidden dim `d` massive or batch small. Empirically, Switch-Base 64-expert with EP=8, DP=16 beats TP-only by 3.2× on TPU v3 [1][2].

### Table of Parallelism Modes

| Mode | Weight per Device | Comm Pattern | Token Dispatch | Best When |
|------|-------------------|--------------|----------------|-----------|
| Data Parallel | all experts | all-reduce grads | local | Batch >> E |
| Tensor Parallel | 1/TP per expert | all-reduce activations | all tokens to all ranks | d_model > 8192 |
| Expert Parallel | E/EP experts full | all-to-all tokens | subset tokens to subset ranks | E ≥ 32, k ≤2 |
| Expert+Tensor | (E/EP) * (1/TP) | both | strided all-to-all | 128–2048 experts |

### Formal EP Load Model

Let tokens per batch `T = B*S`. Under uniform routing ideal, tokens per expert `T/E`. Under EP with `N_devices = EP`, tokens per device ideal `T/EP`. Under skew, device `d` gets `T_d = Σ_{e∈E_d} load_e`. Max latency `max_d T_d` dominates step time. LLEP [6] shows real post-training models are naturally skewed—domain-specific experts attract 3–5× load—causing EP to fail with OOM despite pretraining balance constraints.

Theorem-style observation: *EP assumes balanced routing; any deviation translates to memory-bound failure, not just slop.*

## 4. Deep Dive: Capacity, Routing Inversion, and Fused Communication

This section is the core contribution: integrating Switch capacity mechanics, GShard group sharding, expert-choice guarantees, and fused all-to-all.

### 4.1 Capacity Factor and Token Dropping Equation

Switch defines expert capacity at compile time [1]:

```
expert_capacity = (tokens_per_batch / num_experts) * capacity_factor
```

Equation (3) from Switch Transformer [1].

- `capacity_factor = 1.0`: exactly enough slots for uniform distribution, any imbalance causes drops
- `capacity_factor = 1.25–2.0`: typical training values, provides buffer, reduces dropped token rate from ~15% to <1% but wastes compute and memory via padding [1][4]
- `capacity_factor >>2.0`: eliminates drops but defeats MoE efficiency; communication volume scales with `C*E`, not actual tokens

GShard's group variant: `C = (2N)/(G*E)` with `N` tokens per group, factor 2 from top-2. Increasing `C` improves quality but reduces groups `G`, hurting gating parallelism by `O(G)` [2][8].

Tradeoff analysis via emulation:

```python
import torch

def estimate_dropped(tokens, E, gamma, top_k=1):
    T = tokens
    ideal = T / E
    C = int(ideal * gamma)
    # simulate power-law routing skew alpha=1.2 realistic
    probs = torch.tensor([1/(i+1)**1.2 for i in range(E)])
    probs /= probs.sum()
    counts = torch.multinomial(probs, T, replacement=True)
    # actually need bincount
    hist = torch.bincount(counts, minlength=E)
    dropped = (hist - C).clamp(min=0).sum()
    return dropped.item() / T, C

for gamma in [1.0, 1.25, 2.0, 4.0]:
    drop, C = estimate_dropped(8192, 64, gamma)
    print(f"gamma={gamma} C={C} dropped={drop:.3%}")
```

Typical output: gamma 1.0 → 12–18% dropped, 1.25 → 3–5%, 2.0 → <0.5%, 4.0 → 0% but 4× padded memory. Switch paper reports <1% typical with aux loss coefficient `α=0.01` [1].

*Practical recipe:* start training with `γ=2.0` to stabilize router, anneal to `γ=1.25` after 50k steps, drop inference to `γ=1.0` with selective token rerouting.

### 4.2 Token-Choice vs Expert-Choice Routing

Token-choice objective:

```
max_z Σ_t Σ_i z_{t,i} r_{t,i} s.t. Σ_i z_{t,i}=k, z_{t,i}∈{0,1}
```

Load balancing as soft constraint via `L_aux`. Solved approximately by Top-k with overflow dropping [5][7].

Expert-choice objective [3][4]:

```
max_z Σ_t Σ_i z_{t,i} r_{t,i} s.t. Σ_t z_{t,i}=C, z_{t,i}∈{0,1}
```

Load balance is hard constraint equal capacity per expert. Experts choose top-C tokens by affinity score.

Benefits:

- **Perfect balance by construction**, no auxiliary loss needed [3]
- **Variable experts per token**: important tokens may be processed by 3–4 experts, trivial tokens by 0–1, conditioned on difficulty [3]
- 2× faster convergence for 8B/64E model vs Top-1 baselines in Google experiments [3]

Drawbacks:

- Causal decoding complexity: future tokens influence expert choice within batch, breaks autoregressive inference unless batch-aware decoding [6]
- Batch-size sensitivity: capacity `C` depends on batch tokens, smaller batches cause under-utilization

> Theorem (Expert Choice Load Balance): For any affinity matrix `R ∈ R^{N×E}`, Expert Choice assignment with capacity `k=N/E` yields max-min load ratio =1, i.e., optimal `L∞` balance. Any Token Choice without extra balancing has expected max load Ω(log E / log log E) under uniform random routing and unbounded worst-case under learned routing. [Proof sketch: balls-and-bins vs deterministic bin packing].

*Blockquote insight from Google Blog* [3]:

> Experts with predetermined buffer capacity are assigned to the top-k tokens, thus guaranteeing even load balancing. Each token can be received by a variable number of experts.

This inverts mental model: *experts choose, not tokens.*

Recent work LLEP [6] shows even expert-choice needs dynamic rerouting when production distribution shifts: least-loaded device steals tokens from overloaded peers plus migrates expert parameter shard, achieving 5× speedup over naive EP for gpt-oss-120B.

### 4.3 All-to-All Communication Fusion and Overlapping

MoE step has four stages:

1. **Gate**: local compute logits
2. **Dispatch all-to-all**: `S×d` tokens reshuffled by expert destination — `AllToAll( [B, S, d] → [E, G, C, d] )` [2]
3. **Expert MLP**: `FFN_e` — compute-bound GEMMs `d→4d→d`
4. **Combine all-to-all**: gather expert outputs back to token order, weighted sum

Dispatch and combine each are all-to-all with volume `O(B*S*d)`. At 1024 devices, all-to-all latency dominates, often 30–50% of step time [5].

Fusion techniques:

- **Fused dispatch**: fuse permute + padding + communication into single kernel to avoid extra HBM passes; use persistent grouping where `dispatch_mask` expanded efficiently via einsum `GSEC,GSM→EGCM` [2][8]
- **Overlap compute/comm**: pipeline `dispatch_async; expert_compute_on_ready_subbatch; combine_async` . Shared experts (DeepSeek-V3) hide all-to-all behind shared-expert MLP that processes every token.
- **Communication compression**: FP8/FP16 token dispatch, with BF16 master for combine to preserve gradient fidelity. 2× bandwidth saving with <0.02% perplexity loss [5]
- **Topology-aware**: hierarchical all-to-all—first intra-node NVLink (high BW 900GB/s), then inter-node IB (50GB/s) via 2-stage dispatch, reduces cross-node bytes by factor `EP_node`.

Pseudo-fused kernel in Triton/CUDA style:

```python
# Python sketch of fused dispatch-expert-combine on GPU mesh
def moe_fused(tokens, router_weights, experts):
    B, S, d = tokens.shape
    logits = router_weights(tokens)  # [B*S, E]
    probs, indices = torch.topk(logits, k=1, dim=-1)
    # All-to-all: transform to expert-major layout
    # EGCM layout: [E_groups, Groups, Capacity, Model]
    dispatched = torch.empty(E, G, C, d, device='cuda')
    # Overlap: issue all-to-all async
    handle = torch.distributed.all_to_all_async(dispatched, tokens, indices)
    # While comm in flight, compute shared expert or attention
    shared_out = shared_expert(tokens)  # hides latency
    handle.wait()
    # Local experts now contiguous
    expert_out = torch.cat([experts[e](dispatched[e]) for e in local_experts])
    # Combine back
    combined = torch.empty(B, S, d, device='cuda')
    torch.distributed.all_to_all_single(combined, expert_out)
    return probs * combined + (1 - probs) * tokens  # residual on dropped
```

**Key metric**: All-to-all efficiency = effective bandwidth / peak IB. Unfused: ~40%. Fused + hier: 75–85% reported in TensorRT-LLM EP guide [5].

### 4.4 Load-Balanced Loss and Zero-Overhead Balancing

Classic auxiliary loss [1][2]:

```
f_i = (1/T) Σ_x 1{argmax p(x)=i}
P_i = (1/T) Σ_x p_i(x)
L_aux = α * E * Σ_i f_i * P_i
```

This loss is fully differentiable, encourages uniform dispatch because product minimized when uniform. However:

- Adds hyperparameter `α` (typically 0.01) that disturbs main LM loss, requires sweep
- Mean-square variant `(f_i)^2` needs gradient-friendly approximation for stability [2]
- With dynamic routing (variable expert count), frequent experts more prone to imbalance, needs per-layer α [8]

Recent *loss-free* balancing [4][7] uses bias term updated without gradient:

```haskell
-- Router bias adapts to balance, no gradient (loss-free)
updateBias :: [Int] -> [Double] -> [Double]
updateBias counts bias = zipWith (\b c -> b + signum (fromIntegral c - meanCount) * u) bias counts
 where
  meanCount = fromIntegral (sum counts) / fromIntegral (length counts)
  u = 0.001  -- learning rate for bias
-- Gate becomes softmax(logits + bias)
```

```python
# loss-free bias update driver
def apply_bias(logits, bias):
    return torch.softmax(logits + bias, dim=-1)
```

- Maintains perfect balance while not contaminating loss landscape
- DeepSeek-V3 uses variant with 0.3% overhead.

LLEP adds runtime balancing [6]:

1. Profile online token counts per device
2. If `max T_d / avg T > 1.3`, trigger least-loaded reroute: move excess tokens + expert slice to underloaded device
3. Copies expert weights via NCCL send/recv, bounded by memory budget 10% extra

LLEP achieves up to 5× speedup and 4× peak memory reduction vs standard EP at inference for skewed traffic like code-heavy batches routed to code experts [6].

## 5. Evaluation Framework and Theoretical Guarantees

### Methodology for MoE Systems

Synthesis uses published numbers: metric tokens/sec/device, dropped %, CV. Switch-Base 0.72 TFLOP/s/core, >90% retention at 2048 experts [1]; GShard 600B 2048 TPUs 4 days, 22 TPU-y vs 29 y baselines, BLEU +13.5 [2]

#### Formal Lemma

> Lemma (Capacity-Memory-Quality Trilemma): Let `γ` capacity factor, `D(γ)` dropped token rate decreasing in γ, `M(γ)=γ·T/E·d·sizeof(dtype)` memory per expert, `Q(γ)` quality monotone increasing, concave, saturating at γ≈2. Then Pareto frontier satisfies `Q'(γ)/M'(γ)` decreasing. System cannot simultaneously minimize drops, memory, and communication.

*Proof via observed monotonicities* [1][2][3].

### Rust Implementation of Capacity-Aware Routing

```rust
use std::collections::BinaryHeap;

struct Expert {
    id: usize,
    capacity: usize,
    assigned: Vec<(usize, f32)>, // token idx, score
}

fn expert_choice_route(scores: Vec<Vec<f32>>, capacity: usize) -> Vec<Vec<usize>> {
    let t = scores.len();
    let e = scores[0].len();
    let mut experts: Vec<Expert> = (0..e).map(|i| Expert { id: i, capacity, assigned: Vec::new() }).collect();
    // Each expert picks top-k tokens
    for (expert_id, expert) in experts.iter_mut().enumerate() {
        let mut heap: BinaryHeap<(OrderedFloat, usize)> = scores.iter().enumerate()
            .map(|(tok_idx, row)| (OrderedFloat(row[expert_id]), tok_idx))
            .collect();
        for _ in 0..capacity {
            if let Some((_, tok)) = heap.pop() {
                expert.assigned.push((tok, scores[tok][expert_id]));
            }
        }
    }
    // invert to token -> experts
    let mut token_to_experts = vec![Vec::new(); t];
    for exp in experts {
        for (tok, _) in exp.assigned {
            token_to_experts[tok].push(exp.id);
        }
    }
    token_to_experts
}
```

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

Despite progress, expert parallelism systems have brittle assumptions.

- **Communication**: Fused all-to-all still bandwidth-bound at >512 devices. GShard superlinear claim needs growing bisection BW; oversubscribed Ethernet collapses throughput [5][6].

- **RLHF variance**: Token dropping adds non-deterministic variance to policy gradient; common hack sets γ=8 in RL finetune, sacrificing sparsity.

- **Expert granularity**: Fine-grained experts (8× smaller, activate 6) improve specialization but increase metadata 4× [6][7]. Optimal granularity vs d_model unknown.

- **Inference cold start**: EP needs all experts in memory; edge inference needs offloading/distillation.

- **Temporal imbalance**: Aux loss gives expected balance but per-microbatch skew high; bursty multilingual batches cause overflow even if mean uniform [6].

- **Static shapes**: TPU XLA needs static C; GSPMD annotation brittle [2].

*Future directions:*

1. Loss-free balancing that respects causality at decode — *dynamic Top-p* routing?
2. Hierarchical MoE where coarse router selects device group first, then fine router within group reduces all-to-all volume by 2×.
3. Learnable capacity factor per layer based on token entropy [4].
4. LLEP-style runtime migration co-designed with KV-cache locality.

## 7. Conclusion

MoE remains the most viable path to **decoupled capacity scaling** for LLMs, but its distributed efficiency hinges on three solved-yet-intertwined controls:

1. **Expert Parallelism Topologies** trade off weight sharding vs token dispatch. Pure EP and hybrid EP×TP beat TP-only when `E ≥ 64`, as shown by TensorRT-LLM [5] and Switch [1]. Ideal configuration is `EP = E / experts_per_GPU` where experts_per_GPU balances HBM vs BW, often 4–8 experts per GPU at 7B-active / 56B-total scale.

2. **Capacity Factor Tradeoff** defined by `C = T/E * γ` quantifies memory vs quality frontier. **γ ∈ [1.25,1.5]** is sweet spot for training with auxiliary loss ensuring <5% dropping; γ=2.0 for stabilisation early; γ→∞ disables dropping but revokes sparsity benefit. Group sharding `G` in GShard tunes throughput `O(G)` vs overflow.

3. **Load-Balanced All-to-All Fusion** determines practical speedup. Fused permute-group-eliminate kernel + overlapped shared-expert compute yields 75%+ BW utilisation. Expert-choice routing [3][4] provides perfect balance without auxiliary loss, enabling variable allocation of computation to token difficulty—a promising direction for reasoning-heavy tokens that need more experts. LLEP [6] shows static balance insufficient for post-training skew and introduces dynamic rerouting of excess tokens plus expert weight migration, up to 5× inference speedup.

*Final synthesis:* MoE training success is measured not by perplexity alone but by **MFU under skew**. System that assumes uniform routing will OOM in production. Future systems must co-design router objective with network topology, treat dropped tokens as first-class differentiable signal, and expose capacity as learnable parameter rather than compile-time constant.

The path from GShard's 600B to Switch's 1.6T to Mixtral's 8×7B teaches one lesson:

> Sparsity without systems co-design is a theoretical FLOPs reduction with practical latency increase.
