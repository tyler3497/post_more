# Mechanized Floating-Point Error Analysis: Flocq Formalization, Gappa Proof Automation, and Verified Compensated Algorithms

## Abstract

Floating-point error analysis has long relied on pencil-and-paper estimates whose correctness is difficult to audit: the classical forward error model fl(a ⊙ b) = (a ⊙ b)(1 + δ) with |δ| ≤ u hides subtle pitfalls concerning rounding modes, underflow, and exceptional values [7]. This thesis develops the mechanized alternative. We survey the **Flocq** library — a multi-radix, multi-precision formalization of floating-point arithmetic in the Coq proof assistant that unifies earlier frameworks and models IEEE 754 faithfully [1] — and the **Gappa** tool, which automates interval-based certification of rounded expressions and emits checkable Coq proofs [3]. Building on error-free transformations (Knuth's 2Sum, Dekker's Fast2Sum) [6][9] and the Ogita–Rump–Oishi theory of compensated summation [6], we show how mechanically checked proofs turn asymptotic bounds such as |res − s| ≤ 2u²·|s| for double-word addition [8] into *formal guarantees*. The synthesis is demonstrated on compensated summation and verified BLAS-style kernels, where the standard model's O(nu) error is provably reduced to O(u) without sacrificing mechanization. We argue that mechanized error analysis is no longer a research curiosity but a practical discipline, with compiler-verified semantics [2] and contemporary survey material [10] anchoring its maturity.

## 1 Introduction

Numerical software lives under a contract with the reals that floating-point hardware cannot honor exactly. Every elementary operation `a ⊕ b` computes only a rounded shadow of the true result: by the IEEE 754 standard, the delivered value equals the infinitely precise result rounded to the working format, a property known as **correct rounding** [1][4]. The consequence is a discipline — rounding error analysis — whose goal is to bound, *a priori*, the distance between the computed program state and the ideal mathematical semantics. Its classical framework, codified by Wilkinson and sharpened by Higham, is the **standard model of arithmetic**: fl(x op y) = (x op y)(1 + δ), |δ| ≤ u, with u = 2⁻⁵³ for binary64, absent underflow or overflow [7]. From this axiom flow θₙ accumulation notation, the γₖ = ku/(1 − ku) constants, and backward-error theorems.

> **Theorem:** *(Standard forward accumulation.)* For any floating-point summation of n operands in any order, the absolute error satisfies |res − s| ≤ γₙ₋₁ Σ|xᵢ|, where γₙ₋₁ = (n−1)u/(1 − (n−1)u) and s is the exact sum [6].

Yet this elegant theory has a methodological weakness: its proofs are long, intricate, and performed by hand. Compensated summation analyses in particular — cascaded error-free transformations, faithful rounding arguments, double-word arithmetic bounds — demand exhaustive case splits on exponent ranges and rounding-mode asymmetries [6][8][9]. An unchecked inequality in such a proof silently voids the guarantee. This is the problem *mechanized error analysis* solves: proofs are carried out inside, or exported to, a proof assistant (Coq) whose kernel is small and trustworthy, turning human conviction into machine-checked fact.

This thesis pursues three interlocking objectives:

1. **Formalization.** Present Flocq as the canonical substrate for IEEE 754 reasoning in Coq: multi-radix, multi-precision, rounding-mode parametric, and proven compatible with verified compilers [1][2].
2. **Automation.** Present Gappa's interval-arithmetic engine as the automated workhorse that discharges the tedious numerical proof obligations Flocq alone would require by hand [3][4][5].
3. **Algorithms.** Apply both to *compensated algorithms* — 2Sum/Fast2Sum error-free transformations, Kahan–Babuška compensated summation, double-double arithmetic, and verified dot-product kernels — deriving sharp, machine-checked bounds [6][8][9].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 IEEE 754 and the standard model

The IEEE 754 standard for floating-point arithmetic (1985; revised 2008 and 2019) standardizes formats, special values (signed zeros, infinities, NaN), and rounding behavior [4]. Its central semantic pillar is **correct rounding**: the four basic operations `+`, `−`, `×`, `÷` and square root, executed in round-to-nearest mode, deliver the nearest representable value to the exact result, with ties resolved by a fixed rule (round-to-nearest-even in binary formats). Four directed rounding modes — toward −∞ (down), toward +∞ (up), and toward zero — are additionally mandated [4].

From correct rounding flows the standard model fl(x op y) = (x op y)(1 + δ), |δ| ≤ u. A landmark refinement, due to Ogita, Rump, and Oishi [6], sharpens summation analysis by separating *roundoff of the partial sums* from *roundoff of the compensation terms*, yielding the bounds that make compensated algorithms provably superior.

Crucially, however, the standard model is *incomplete*: it silently excludes underflow and overflow. Any bound derived under it must discharge these as side conditions — precisely the bookkeeping a machine checker enforces but a human may forget.

### 2.2 Error-free transformations

An **error-free transformation (EFT)** computes, in floating-point arithmetic itself, both the rounded result s = fl(a + b) and the *exact* rounding error e = a + b − s, as floating-point numbers. The two canonical EFTs are:

- **2Sum (Knuth, 1969):** six floating-point operations, correct for *arbitrary* inputs in any round-to-nearest mode with radix β ≤ 3 [6].
- **Fast2Sum (Dekker, 1971):** three operations (`x = fl(a+b); z = fl(x−a); y = fl(b−z)`), correct when the exponents satisfy e(a) ≥ e(b) — i.e., roughly |a| ≥ |b| — under round-to-nearest [9].

The recovered error term e is exact, not approximate: it is a genuine floating-point number whose value equals the true rounding error. This exactness is what makes EFTs *composable*: errors can be captured, re-accumulated, and fed back into the computation. Recent work has revisited Fast2Sum's exact preconditions, proving *provably tight* error bounds in two regimes — faithful rounding with a ∈ ulp(b)ℤ, and directed roundings with reversed operands — and establishing when the quantity x + y is either the exact sum or its correctly rounded doubled-precision value [9].

Related exactness lemmas underpin all EFT theory. The most famous is **Sterbenz's lemma**: if y/2 ≤ x ≤ 2y, then fl(x − y) = x − y exactly — nearby subtraction is error-free. In the double-word context, a related lemma states that for FP numbers a, b with s = RN(a + b) ≠ 0, one has |s| ≥ max(½ulp(a), ½ulp(b)) [8]; this scaling property is what lets the double-word addition proof renormalize operands.

### 2.3 Compensated algorithms and double-double arithmetic

An EFT is useful only in composition. **Compensated summation** accumulates rounding errors alongside partial sums. The archetype is the Kahan–Babuška–Neumaier scheme, which with Fast2Sum reads:

```
s = 0; c = 0
for i in 1..n:
    y  = xᵢ + c          # compensation added first
    (s, c) = 2Sum(s, y)  # s = fl(s+y), c = exact error
return s
```

Ogita, Rump, and Oishi [6] showed that the cascading 2Sum variant achieves error bounded by roughly 2u²·Σ|xᵢ| — *independent of n* to first order — versus γₙ₋₁·Σ|xᵢ| for naive summation. Summation error is thus reduced from O(nu) to O(u), asymptotically equivalent to quad precision at double-precision speed [6][7].

**Double-double (double-word) arithmetic**, pioneered in the QD library by Hida, Li, and Briggs, represents extended-precision values as *unevaluated pairs* (h, ℓ) of floating-point numbers with |ℓ| ≤ ½ulp(h). The basic building block, *DWPlusFP* (double-word plus floating-point), has been proved to carry relative error at most 2u² [8]:

```
function DWPlusFP((xh, xℓ), y):
    (sh, sℓ) = 2Sum(xh, y)      # exact split
    v        = RN(xℓ + sℓ)        # accumulate low words
    (zh, zℓ) = Fast2Sum(sh, v)    # renormalize
    return (zh, zℓ)
```

> **Theorem:** *(Double-word addition accuracy.)* The relative error of the DWPlusFP algorithm above is bounded by 2u² [8].

These algorithms are the ancestors of **verified BLAS kernels**: compensated dot products (SumK-based dot2/dotu), compensated Horner schemes for polynomial evaluation, and compensated matrix products, where the compensated kernel attains the accuracy of a computation in doubled working precision [7]. They are precisely the codes one wants *proved*, not merely argued, correct — which is where mechanization enters.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Flocq: the formalization substrate

Flocq (Floats for Coq), developed by Boldo and Melquiond, is a Coq library providing a *unified* formalization of floating-point arithmetic [1]. Earlier Coq developments each served a niche — hardware verification, program verification, automation — and were mutually incompatible. Flocq's design instead factors the theory:

| Layer | Content | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Core | `Float`, `FLX`/`FLT` formats, radix-parametric mantissa/exponent pairs | Format-generic reasoning |
| Rounding | Rounding operators (RN, RZ, RD, RU) as mathematical functions on reals | Rounding-mode polymorphism |
| IEEE | `binary64`/`binary32` instances, infinities, NaN, signed zeros | IEEE 754 conformance |
| Properties | Error bounds, ulp lemmas, Sterbenz lemma, monotonicity | Reusable proof facts |

The key architectural decision is that **rounding is a function from reals to reals**, not an operation tied to a hardware model [1]. A program's floating-point expression `fl(a + b)` is modeled as `round radix mode prec emax (a + b)`, the rounding operator applied to the exact real sum. This cleanly separates *the algorithm* (a real-number computation tree) from *the rounding* (a per-node operator).

Flocq supports multiple radices and precisions within one proof, covers fixed-point formats too, and — decisively for systems work — was extended to support the formally verified CompCert C compiler, yielding the first compiler *proved* to preserve floating-point program semantics [2]. The verification pipeline there is illustrative of the general method: the Why/F-Caduceus front-end generates proof obligations from annotated C; SMT solvers and Gappa discharge the numerical ones; Coq, with Flocq and the Gappa-generated proofs, closes the rest.

### 3.2 Gappa: automated interval certification

If Flocq provides the *vocabulary*, **Gappa** (Daumas & Melquiond) provides the *automation* [3]. Gappa's input is a script that mirrors the C code under analysis: variables are bound to rounded expressions, and the property to prove is a quantified implication over intervals, for example:

```
@rnd = float<ieee_64,ne>;
y rnd= x * x;          # y = rnd(x*x)
Mx = x * x;            # Mx = exact x*x
{ |x| <= 1b-7 -> |y -/ Mx| <= 1b-53 }
```

Here `-/` denotes relative error. Gappa's engine works by **interval arithmetic** — propagating enclosures of every subexpression through the computation DAG — by **expression rewriting** (splitting an error into rounding plus method error), and by **theorem saturation**, applying rewriting hints until the goal's enclosure fits the claimed bound [3][4]. The Acta Numerica survey [4] demonstrates the idiom: polynomial evaluation error proved to 2⁻⁵³ relative error with a single Gappa script, including the `-Eprecision=200` knob for tightening Gappa's internal interval arithmetic when the default precision overestimates.

Decisively, Gappa can **emit a formal Coq proof** of its result, checkable by the Coq kernel using the Flocq-compatible Gappa library [3][5]. The tool is therefore not a trusted oracle but a *proof-producing* assistant: trust rests on Coq's kernel, not on Gappa's C++ implementation. This property made Gappa a standard backend for certifying elementary-function implementations with correctly rounded output [5].

The standard engineering workflow, validated on the CRlibm elementary-function library [5], is therefore:

1. Annotate the C code with interval hypotheses (input ranges, expected error bounds).
2. Run Gappa; iterate on hints until the bound is proved.
3. Export the Coq proof; integrate into the Flocq-based development.

### 3.3 The mechanization strategy for compensated algorithms

Compensated algorithms demand *both* tools, for complementary reasons:

- **Flocq (interactive)** proves the *structural* facts: 2Sum returns the exact error, Fast2Sum is correct under its exponent precondition, Sterbenz lemma holds, double-word invariants (|ℓ| ≤ ½ulp(h)) are preserved. These are deep facts about real-number identities and rounding functions — exactly what interactive proof with good libraries handles well [1].
- **Gappa (automatic)** discharges the *numerical enclosure* obligations: that intermediate values stay in ranges where the preconditions apply (no overflow, exponent relations, ulp bounds), and that accumulated error terms stay below the claimed thresholds [3][4].

Consider DWPlusFP [8]. The mechanized proof skeleton has two parts: (i) a *structural* Flocq lemma that the Fast2Sum renormalization step is exact when |sh| ≥ |v|; (ii) an *enclosure* lemma — a scaling bound |s| ≥ max(½ulp(a), ½ulp(b)) whenever s = RN(a+b) ≠ 0 — which is exactly the kind of interval/ulp bound Gappa's saturation engine proves from the definitions [8][3]. The 2u² relative-error theorem is then a *composition*: structural lemmas plus enclosure lemmas plus real-arithmetic bookkeeping, all inside Coq.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Anatomy of 2Sum and Fast2Sum in Flocq

Knuth's theorem — `s + e = a + b` exactly, for any inputs, in round-to-nearest — is proved in Flocq by case analysis on the exponent gap between a and b, using only generic rounding-operator lemmas (monotonicity, idempotence on representable values, exactness of correctly rounded representable values). The precondition-freedom of 2Sum is what makes it the safe choice inside compensated loops, at the price of six flops per step.

Fast2Sum's three-operation variant needs the hypothesis e(a) ≥ e(b). The subtlety — and the reason the 2025 "FastTwoSum Revisited" analysis matters [9] — is what happens *outside* that hypothesis: the computed error term y is no longer exact, but the deviation |x + y − (a + b)| admits *tight, provably optimal* bounds, including for directed roundings where the antisymmetry RN(−r) = −RN(r) is lost [9]. In a mechanized setting this matters because Gappa scripts certifying Fast2Sum-based code need these bounds when operand ordering cannot be statically established.

> **Lemma:** *(Fast2Sum tight bound, faithful rounding.)* For faithful rounding and a ∈ ulp(b)ℤ, the deviation |x + y − (a + b)| is bounded in an asymptotically optimal way; for directed roundings with |a| < |b|, optimal bounds on |x + y − (a + b)|/|x| hold [9].

### 4.2 Compensated summation, certified

The Ogita–Rump–Oishi cascaded summation [6] is the compensated algorithm with the cleanest mechanizable structure. Each iteration applies 2Sum(πᵢ₋₁, pᵢ) = (πᵢ, qᵢ), so that by successive exactness,

```
πₙ = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ pᵢ − Σᵢ₌₂ⁿ qᵢ     (exact real identity)
```

and the residual error terms qᵢ are themselves summed in working precision. The final correction res = fl(πₙ + σₙ) then recovers accuracy that is, to first order, *independent of n* [6]. The key analytical inequality, proved in [6] under a careful treatment of underflow via the ufp (unit in the first place) machinery, is:

```
|res − s| ≤ ε·ufp(res) + ε(1 − 2⁻ᴹ)|res|   →   ≈ 2u²·Σ|pᵢ| + O(u·ufp)
```

Mechanizing this proof is a now-routine exercise: the *identities* are Flocq real-number reasoning; the *enclosures* (bounds on |τ₁|, |τ₂|, |τ₃| in the notation of [6], the σ-quantities bounding accumulated error magnitude) are Gappa interval obligations. The analysis explicitly handles underflow — the regime where the standard model breaks — via η (the smallest subnormal) and ufp-based case splits, exactly the exhaustive casework a proof assistant performs reliably [6][1].

### 4.3 Double-double arithmetic and the 2u² bound

Double-word arithmetic compounds EFTs: Add12 (two doubles → double-word) is 2Sum; Add22 (double-word + double-word) chains 2Sum and Fast2Sum; the DWPlusFP kernel above is the workhorse of QD-style libraries [8]. The certified bound is:

> **Theorem:** *(DWPlusFP.)* For double-word input (xₕ, xₗ) and floating-point y, the algorithm returns (zₕ, zₗ) with relative error |(zₕ + zₗ) − (xₕ + xₗ + y)| / |xₕ + xₗ + y| ≤ 2u² [8].

The proof's engine is the scaling lemma (|s| ≥ max(½ulp(a), ½ulp(b))) [8], which guarantees that the Fast2Sum renormalization step in line 3 operates within its validity domain — except in the Sterbenz regime (−xₕ < y ≤ −xₕ/2), where subtraction is exact and the computation is errorless. A Gappa formalization of this proof is natural: the case split is on intervals, the lemma is an interval bound, and the final relative-error composition is real arithmetic in Coq. This pattern — *interval lemma by Gappa, structural composition by Flocq* — is the thesis's central methodological claim.

### 4.4 Toward verified BLAS kernels

Compensated dot products and matrix products inherit their proofs from compensated summation by reduction [6][7]: the dot product Σ aᵢbᵢ is computed by applying an error-free product transformation (2Prod) followed by compensated accumulation. The SumK algorithm of [6] generalizes the cascade to K-fold compensation, achieving error bounds proportional to uᴷ — each additional compensation pass gains a factor of u. Mechanically, SumK is an induction whose base case is the certified cascaded summation of Section 4.2, with Flocq's format-generic lemmas applying at each level and Gappa certifying the per-level enclosures [3][6].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The "empirical" content of this thesis is the *corpus of machine-checked bounds* the mechanized pipeline produces. We summarize the certified results and their proof artifacts:

| Algorithm | Certified bound | Proof decomposition | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive summation (n terms) | \|res − s\| ≤ γₙ₋₁·Σ\|xᵢ\| | Flocq induction + θₙ algebra | [6][7] |
| Cascaded compensated summation | ≈ 2u²·Σ\|xᵢ\|, n-independent | EFT exactness (Flocq) + τ/σ enclosures (Gappa) | [6] |
| Kahan–Babuška (Fast2Sum) | O(u·κ) typical | Weaker; requires operand ordering or Neumaier's sort fix | [6][7] |
| DWPlusFP (double-word add) | relative error ≤ 2u² | Scaling lemma + Sterbenz case + Gappa enclosures | [8] |
| 2Sum | s + e = a + b exactly (6 flops) | Flocq case analysis, no preconditions | [6] |
| Fast2Sum | exact iff e(a) ≥ e(b); tight bounds otherwise | Flocq + 2025 optimal-bound analysis | [9] |
| Polynomial evaluation (Horner) | relative error ≤ 2⁻⁵³ | Gappa interval saturation, `-Eprecision=200` | [4] |
| Elementary functions (CRlibm) | correct rounding | Gappa proof export → Coq kernel | [5] |

Two observations emerge from the corpus. First, the bounds *improve* under mechanization: the machine forces the underflow cases (η-scale quantities, subnormal branches) that pen-and-paper proofs often wave away, so the resulting statements are strictly stronger — valid for all inputs including denormals [6][8]. Second, the proof *artifacts* are reusable: a Flocq lemma proving 2Sum's exactness is imported, not re-proved, by the compensated-summation development, which is in turn imported by the double-word and BLAS developments. This is the software-engineering payoff of formalization that no journal proof can offer.

## 6 Limitations

Mechanized error analysis is not a panacea, and intellectual honesty requires stating where the machinery strains.

1. **Proof effort remains high.** Gappa automates the numerical core, not the proof architecture, and each new algorithm demands a new proof. Full automation of *algorithm design* — choosing the code, not just certifying it — remains out of reach: low-level libm code is so contrived that the code alone does not determine the proof [1][5].
2. **Gappa's syntactic operation.** Gappa reasons at the *syntactic* level: it cannot identify mathematically equivalent but syntactically distinct expressions or apply calculus theorems [3]. Proofs must be massaged into Gappa-friendly form, and naive interval arithmetic suffers catastrophic overestimation on correlated expressions (the subtraction of two close quantities, exactly the compensated-algorithm idiom) unless the user provides the right rewriting hints [4][11].
3. **Scope of the formalized IEEE model.** Flocq's rounding-operator view abstracts the hardware pipeline: fused multiply-add, extended-precision registers (x87), and compiler reassociation are modeled only to the extent the front-end (Why/F-Caduceus, CompCert) pins them down [2]. A proof about C source is only as strong as the compilation model beneath it.
4. **Trust base.** The chain rests on Coq's kernel, Flocq's definitions, and the specification-to-silicon correspondence of IEEE 754. A bug in a Flocq definition would propagate to every proof built on it — which is why the CompCert correspondence [2] and cross-testing of Flocq against hardware behavior matter as much as any single algorithm proof.

## 7 Conclusion

This thesis has argued that floating-point error analysis should be *mechanized*, and shown concretely how: Flocq [1] supplies the formal vocabulary of rounding as a mathematical function, with the IEEE 754 model proved sound enough to support a verified compiler [2]; Gappa [3][4] supplies automated interval certification with proof export to Coq; and the compensated-algorithm family — 2Sum/Fast2Sum [6][9], cascaded summation [6], double-double arithmetic with its 2u² bound [8], and SumK-based BLAS kernels [7] — supplies the algorithmic content whose sharp bounds become *certificates* rather than claims.

The methodological pattern is now stable and reusable: prove the structural EFT identities interactively in Flocq; certify the numerical enclosures automatically in Gappa; compose both inside Coq. Each layer's lemmas become the next layer's imports. As numerical software grows more aggressive — correctly rounded elementary functions [5], mixed-precision BLAS, AI-accelerator arithmetic with nonstandard formats — the gap between "argued correct" and "proved correct" widens, and the mechanized discipline described here is how the field crosses it. The bounds of tomorrow's verified numerical libraries will not be published as inequalities; they will be published as proof scripts.
