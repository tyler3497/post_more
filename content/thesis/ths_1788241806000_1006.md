# Measure-Theoretic Denotational Semantics for Probabilistic Programming Languages: Quasi-Borel Structures, Correctness of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, and Compiler Intermediate Representations for Differentiable Inference

## Abstract

Probabilistic programming languages (PPLs) separate model specification from inference, yet the mathematical foundations of this separation remain subtle. This thesis unifies three pillars of modern PPL design: (i) measure-theoretic denotational semantics for higher-order probabilistic programs, centered on the Giry monad and the quasi-Borel spaces that repair the failure of cartesian closure in the category of measurable spaces [1]; (ii) the correctness theory of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) — detailed balance via symplectic geometry and geometric ergodicity via drift and minorization [4][5]; and (iii) compiler intermediate representations that make inference differentiable, as in Stan [6] and Pyro [8], where automatic-differentiation tapes turn a model into a log-density and its gradient. The denotation defines what inference *means*; HMC theory guarantees the sampler *converges* to it; the IR makes this composition *computable*. We present a categorical validation of trace MCMC [3] and an adequacy sketch for a statistical core calculus, and close with open problems: recursive stochastic processes, differentiable control flow, and ergodicity of adaptive samplers such as NUTS [7].

---

## 1 Introduction

The last decade has seen probabilistic programming mature from a research curiosity into an industrial technology. Systems such as Stan [6], Pyro [8], Gen, Turing.jl, and WebPPL allow practitioners to write generative models as ordinary programs, delegating the heavy machinery of posterior inference — Markov chain Monte Carlo, variational optimization, sequential Monte Carlo — to the language runtime. The promise is alluring: *write the model, not the inference*. But a promise is not a theorem, and the gap between the informal intuition that "sampling and scoring define a posterior" and a rigorous guarantee that inference algorithms converge to the mathematically intended distribution is precisely where the foundations of probabilistic programming live.

Three questions structure this investigation. **First**, what does a probabilistic program *mean*? Ordinary programs denote functions; probabilistic programs denote *distributions*, and the measure-theoretic machinery — s-finite kernels, the Giry monad, commutativity of integration — breaks down instructively as soon as the language is higher-order. **Second**, do our inference algorithms actually *realize* that meaning? Hamiltonian Monte Carlo [5] is the workhorse of differentiable PPLs, and its correctness rests on symplectic geometry, reversibility, and Markov chain ergodicity [4]. **Third**, how is this *compiled*? Modern systems lower models through typed intermediate representations exposing the log-density and its gradient as compiled artifacts [6][8], making automatic differentiation the bridge between semantics and numerics.

These questions are not independent. A denotational semantics that cannot justify an inference algorithm is decorative; an inference algorithm whose target is not the program's denotation is simply wrong; and a compiler IR that obscures the measure-theoretic structure harms both analysis and performance. The central contribution of this thesis is a *unified narrative*: quasi-Borel denotational semantics supply the target of inference, HMC ergodicity theory supplies the convergence guarantee, and differentiable compiler IRs supply the computational realization.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Probability as a monad

The classical measure-theoretic view interprets a probabilistic program of type `X` as a probability measure on a measurable space *X*. Moggi's computational-lambda-calculus perspective, adapted to probability by Giry [1], packages this into the **probability monad** *P*: an object *PX* is the space of probability measures on *X*, `return x = δ_x` is the Dirac embedding, and Kleisli composition is integration,

```
(μ >>= f)(A) = ∫ f(x)(A) μ(dx).
```

*Sequencing* random draws becomes Kleisli composition; *scoring* (`score w`) rescales a measure by a weight function. This monadic view is the algebraic structure that makes the commutativity of independent draws provable, via Fubini's theorem.

> **Theorem (Fubini, monadic form):** If `P` is a commutative monad on a suitable category, then for all `μ : PX`, `ν : PY`, and `f : X × Y → PZ`,
> `do { x ← μ; y ← ν; f(x,y) } = do { y ← ν; x ← μ; f(x,y) }`.
> Commutativity of the monad is equivalent to the interchange of integration order.

This single fact justifies a host of program transformations — reordering independent `sample` statements, hoisting observations, Rao–Blackwellization — and is the semantic content of the claim that "evaluation order doesn't matter" for pure probabilistic effects [2][3].

### 2.2 The cartesian closure problem

Trouble arrives with higher-order functions. To interpret a type `X → Y` of probabilistic functions, the semantics needs a *function space object*: the category must be cartesian closed. The category **Meas** of measurable spaces is **not** cartesian closed — there is in general no measurable space `Y^X` classifying measurable maps `X → Y`. Consequently, a measure-theoretic semantics cannot directly interpret programs that sample *functions*, pass distributions as arguments, or return them as results — precisely the programs (Gaussian process priors, Bayesian neural networks, higher-order inference combinators) that modern PPLs must support.

Heunen, Kammar, Staton, and Yang resolved this with **quasi-Borel spaces** [1]: a set *X* with a distinguished collection `M_X ⊆ [ℝ → X]` of *admissible random elements*, closed under constants, precomposition with measurable maps, and countable case-splitting. The category **QBS** is cartesian closed, has all limits and colimits, conservatively extends standard Borel spaces, and carries a commutative probability monad.

### 2.3 Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo [5] is the dominant inference algorithm for continuous, differentiable models. Given a target density `π(q) ∝ exp(−U(q))` on `ℝ^d`, HMC augments the state with a momentum variable `p ∼ N(0, M)`, forming the Hamiltonian `H(q, p) = U(q) + ½pᵀM⁻¹p`. Hamilton's equations `dq/dt = M⁻¹p`, `dp/dt = −∇U(q)` generate a flow preserving the joint density `exp(−H)` (Liouville's theorem) that is exactly reversible. In practice the flow is approximated by the **leapfrog integrator**, a symplectic, volume-preserving, reversible discretization; the Metropolis correction then guarantees the chain's stationary distribution is exactly `π`. The No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) [7] adaptively selects trajectory length by building a binary tree of leapfrog steps until the trajectory begins to double back.

### 2.4 Compiler IRs and automatic differentiation

Stan [6] compiles a model program into an imperative log-density function plus its gradient, computed by reverse-mode automatic differentiation over an expression graph ("tape"). Pyro [8] generalizes this with an *effect-handler IR*: `sample` and `observe` are algebraic effects intercepted by inference handlers. The denotational view clarifies what `Trace_ELBO` *means*: an inference representation whose meaning function is the exact posterior, valid insofar as the gradient estimator is unbiased — following from commutativity of the monad (interchange of differentiation and integration under dominated convergence).

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is that of *programming-languages semantics applied to statistics*: we define a small core calculus, give it a denotational interpretation in quasi-Borel spaces, prove adequacy with respect to an operational trace semantics, and then use the denotation as the correctness target for inference algorithms and compiler transformations.

We work with a statistical core calculus **λₚ**, a call-by-value lambda calculus with ground type `R`, product, sum, and function types, plus two probabilistic primitives:

```haskell
sample : P R        -- draw from a primitive distribution (e.g. Uniform[0,1])
score  : R+ -> P () -- multiply the trace weight by r (soft conditioning)
```

A closed program `⊢ t : P R` denotes, in **QBS**, a morphism `⟦t⟧ : 1 → P⟦R⟧`, i.e. a probability kernel from the terminal object — concretely, an s-finite measure on `ℝ`. Adequacy asserts that the denotation equals the measure induced by the trace semantics — the semantic triangle commutes.

For inference, we follow Ścibior et al. [3]: an inference representation is a pair `(T, m)` of a computational representation `T` and a *meaning function* `m : TX → MX`; an inference step is *valid* when it preserves `m`. This single criterion subsumes the correctness of Metropolis–Hastings, SMC resampling, and trace-MCMC for universal PPLs.

For HMC, our methodology is the standard Markov-chain toolkit [4]: establish `π`-irreducibility and aperiodicity, exhibit a Lyapunov drift function `V` with `PV ≤ λV + b·1_C` for `λ < 1` off a small set `C`, and conclude geometric ergodicity via the Harris theorem.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Quasi-Borel spaces and the probability monad

A **quasi-Borel space** is a pair `(X, M_X)` where `M_X ⊆ X^ℝ` satisfies:

1. every constant map `ℝ → X` is in `M_X`;
2. if `α ∈ M_X` and `φ : ℝ → ℝ` is Borel measurable, then `α ∘ φ ∈ M_X`;
3. if `ℝ = ⨆_n U_n` is a countable Borel partition and `α_n ∈ M_X`, then the glued map `[α_n on U_n]` is in `M_X`.

Morphisms `f : X → Y` are set-functions with `f ∘ α ∈ M_Y` for all `α ∈ M_X`. The monad unit is `η(x) = (1, const_x, δ_*)` and bind is the Kock integral `μ >>= f = ∫ f(x) μ(dx)`, which evaluates to a *measure*, not a scalar. Because **QBS** is cartesian closed, this monad is *commutative*: for random elements on independent sources, integration order is interchangeable, recovering Fubini — the technical heart of [1] and the reason higher-order probabilistic programs admit a compositional measure-theoretic semantics at all.

The semantics of our core calculus is then entirely standard *given* the monad:

| Construct | Denotation |
|---|---|
| `⟦return v⟧` | `η(⟦v⟧)` |
| `⟦do { x ← t; u }⟧` | `⟦t⟧ >>= λx. ⟦u⟧` |
| `⟦sample⟧` | the uniform measure presented as `(ℝ, id, U[0,1])` |
| `⟦score r⟧` | rescaling `r ⊙ η(())` |
| `⟦λx. t⟧` | currying in **QBS** |

Adequacy is proved by a logical-relations argument over the trace operational semantics; the crucial lemma is that the weight accumulated along a trace equals the Radon–Nikodym derivative of the denotation with respect to the proposal measure on traces [3].

### 4.2 Denotational validation of inference: trace MCMC

With the denotation fixed, we can ask what it means for an inference algorithm to be *correct*. Ścibior et al. [3] provide the definitive framework. An **inference representation** is a functor `T` (of "computable representations of distributions") with a natural transformation `m : T ⇒ M` to the semantic measure monad. Examples: `Term` (program syntax trees), `Enum` (explicit enumerations of weighted outcomes), `Samp` (samplers as stateful computations), and `Trace` (pairs of a program skeleton and a distribution over random traces).

An **inference transformation** `t : T ⇒ S` is *valid* when `m_S ∘ t = m_T` — it preserves meaning. Inference algorithms decompose into chains of valid transformations plus *one* step that changes the representation's computational character without changing its meaning.

The tracing transformation is valid by the adequacy theorem; the MH transition kernel `K` on traces satisfies detailed balance with respect to the trace target, hence preserves it; marginalization is a monad algebra. This argument *scales*: the same validity criterion covers single-site MH, particle Gibbs, and delayed-sampling Rao–Blackwellization, each as a meaning-preserving transformation on a suitable representation.

> **Theorem (Validity of trace MH [3]):** Let `K` be the single-site Metropolis–Hastings–Green kernel on program traces with target `π(τ) ∝ w(τ)`. Then `m_Trace ∘ K = m_Trace`, i.e. the kernel is a valid inference transformation. Consequently the chain's stationary distribution projects to the program denotation `⟦t⟧`.

### 4.3 Correctness of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo: reversibility and geometric ergodicity

HMC's correctness has two layers: *stationarity* (the chain targets the right distribution) and *ergodicity* (it converges to it, and how fast).

**Stationarity.** The leapfrog integrator `Φ_ε` is symplectic and reversible under momentum flip `R(q,p) = (q,−p)`; the HMC proposal is therefore a volume-preserving involution, and the Metropolis acceptance probability `min(1, exp(H(q,p) − H(q*,p*)))` makes the chain reversible with respect to `π` [5]. Reversibility implies stationarity.

**Geometric ergodicity.** Under regularity on `U` (roughly: `∇U` grows at most linearly and points inward at infinity), the fixed-integration-time HMC kernel is `π`-irreducible, aperiodic, and all compact sets are small [4]. A Lyapunov drift built from the Hamiltonian yields `‖P^n(x, ·) − π‖_TV ≤ M(x)ρ^n` with `ρ < 1` for exponentially light-tailed targets — e.g. Bayesian logistic regression with Gaussian priors. For heavy-tailed targets HMC provably fails to be geometrically ergodic [4]: a genuine limitation, not a proof artifact.

In infinite dimensions, Glatt-Holtz and Mondaini prove geometric ergodicity of preconditioned HMC on Hilbert space via the weak Harris theorem, under absolute continuity with respect to a Gaussian reference measure.

### 4.4 Compiler IRs for differentiable inference

The semantics and the sampler meet in the compiler. Stan's IR [6] is a *first-order imperative language over an AD tape*: a model block elaborates to a program computing `lp = log p(θ)` with every intermediate recorded on a stack of vari nodes; reverse-mode AD walks the tape accumulating adjoints, yielding `∇_θ lp` at a constant multiple of the forward cost. Constrained parameters are transformed to unconstrained space with Jacobian corrections, so the log-density the sampler sees is exactly the pushforward density the semantics denotes.

Pyro [8] generalizes this with an *effect-handler IR*: `sample` and `observe` are algebraic effects; the model is a Python generator, and inference algorithms are handlers that reinterpret those effects (recording traces, reparameterizing, scoring the ELBO). The ELBO gradient estimator used in stochastic variational inference,

```
∇_φ ELBO = E_{q_φ}[ ∇_φ log p(x, z) − ∇_φ log q_φ(z) ],
```

is computed by Monte Carlo with the reparameterization trick where the IR's AD tape makes `∇_φ` concrete. The denotational view clarifies what Pyro's `Trace_ELBO` *means*: it is an inference representation whose meaning function is the exact posterior, and SVI is valid insofar as the gradient estimator is unbiased — a property that follows from the commutativity of the underlying monad (interchange of differentiation and integration under dominated convergence).

The practical upshot is a design principle: **a PPL compiler IR should be a syntactic presentation of the denotational semantics that simultaneously exposes the log-density and its gradient as compiled artifacts**.

### 4.5 Adequacy sketch for the statistical core calculus

We close the deep dive with the adequacy result that ties operational traces to denotations. Define the trace semantics: a configuration is `(t, τ, w)` with `τ` the remaining randomness and `w` the accumulated weight. The rules:

```
(sample, r::τ, w) → (return r, τ, w)         -- consume one uniform draw
(score s, τ, w)   → (return (), τ, w·s)      -- multiply weight
```

For closed `⊢ t : P R`, let `eval(t)` be the pushforward of `U[0,1]^ℕ ⊗ w` along the evaluation map. Then:

> **Theorem (Adequacy):** `eval(t) = ⟦t⟧` as measures on `ℝ`.

*Proof sketch.* By induction on typing derivations, using the monad laws and change-of-variables. The `sample` case is the definition of the presentation `(ℝ, id, U)`; the `score` case uses that rescaling `r ⊙ μ` corresponds exactly to multiplying the trace weight; the bind case uses disintegration of the trace measure and Fubini/commutativity of `P`. ∎

Adequacy licenses everything downstream: any inference representation whose meaning function agrees with `eval` on traces is sound with respect to the denotation.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

While this thesis is primarily theoretical, the claims above are backed by a substantial empirical and proof-theoretic record in the literature.

**Ergodicity in practice.** The conditions of [4] have been verified on canonical Stan models: for Bayesian logistic regression with Gaussian priors and full-rank design matrix, the log-posterior satisfies the inward-pointing gradient condition and HMC mixes geometrically. Conversely, Neal's funnel,

```python
v ~ Normal(0, 3);  x_i ~ Normal(0, exp(v/2))   # i = 1..n
```

violates the tail conditions: curvature varies over orders of magnitude and fixed-step HMC produces divergent transitions — the empirical signature of a failed drift condition. Reparameterization (the non-centered form) restores the geometry, a transformation justified semantically by change-of-variables on the denotation.

**Denotational validation at scale.** The Ścibior et al. framework [3] was mechanized for the core calculus and its trace-MH instance reproduces the known result of Borgström et al. on the correctness of lightweight MCMC for first-order PPLs. The quasi-Borel semantics of [1] has been used to validate higher-order inference patterns — including distributions over functions (Gaussian process regression written as a higher-order program) — that are *inexpressible* in plain **Meas**.

**Compiler IR benchmarks.** Stan's AD tape [6] computes gradients of a `d`-dimensional log-density at `O(1)` times the density cost (the cheap-gradient principle); on hierarchical benchmarks NUTS in Stan achieves roughly an order of magnitude more effective samples per second than random-walk Metropolis. Pyro's effect-handler IR [8] trades some throughput for compositionality, enabling amortized variational families Stan's static IR cannot express.

**Summary of theoretical results established herein:** (1) commutativity of the quasi-Borel probability monad ⇒ Fubini ⇒ evaluation-order independence of `sample`; (2) validity criterion for inference transformations, with trace-MH meaning-preserving [3]; (3) HMC stationarity from symplectic reversibility and geometric ergodicity under the drift/minorization conditions of [4]; (4) adequacy of the trace operational semantics with respect to the quasi-Borel denotation.

## 6 Limitations

The account given here has significant boundaries, and intellectual honesty requires stating them plainly.

**Discrete and transdimensional structure.** HMC needs gradients, hence continuous parameters. Universal PPLs with stochastic control flow (a random number of `sample` sites, recursion) need transdimensional kernels whose correctness theory — detailed balance across dimensions via the MH–Green ratio — is far less developed in the denotational setting.

**Adaptivity and NUTS.** The ergodicity results [4] apply to HMC with *fixed* integration time. NUTS [7] adapts trajectory length per iteration, breaking the Markov property in subtle ways; its stationary distribution is correct, but quantitative convergence rates for NUTS remain essentially open. Practitioners rely on diagnostics (`R̂`, divergences, E-BFMI), not theorems.

**Differentiable control flow.** Compiler IRs assume a fixed straight-line log-density. Data-dependent branching breaks reverse-mode AD efficiency and changes the density's *support* across branches; the IR must then represent a mixture over program paths. A unified denotational account of differentiable control flow in PPLs does not yet exist.

**Recursion and stochastic processes.** Quasi-Borel *predomains* [2] give semantics for term recursion, but recursive *stochastic processes* with unbounded latent structure still lack a canonical denotational treatment composing with the inference-validation framework.

**Computational cost of rigor.** The quasi-Borel presentation is mathematically clean but computationally inert: no implementation literally manipulates "admissible random elements." The gap between the semantic object and the AD tape is bridged by informal argument; fully mechanized correctness for the whole pipeline — semantics, sampler, compiler — remains a grand challenge.

## 7 Conclusion

We have presented a unified foundations-first account of probabilistic programming across three levels: **meaning**, **convergence**, and **compilation**. Quasi-Borel spaces [1] and their commutative probability monad give higher-order probabilistic programs a compositional measure-theoretic semantics where evaluation order is provably irrelevant. The inference-validation framework [3] turns that semantics into a correctness criterion — meaning preservation — under which trace MCMC, SMC, and variational objectives can each be judged. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo [5] earns its place through symplectic reversibility (stationarity) and drift-and-minorization arguments (geometric ergodicity) [4]. Compiler IRs in the style of Stan [6] and Pyro [8] close the loop, materializing the denotation as a differentiable log-density artifact the sampler can execute.

The through-line is a single slogan: *the denotation is the contract, the sampler is the implementation, and the IR is the interface between them*. When any of the three drifts — a semantics that cannot express the model, a sampler without ergodicity, an IR that silently changes the target — the system fails silently, producing plausible-looking wrong answers. The research program this thesis advocates is therefore conservative in the best sense: extend the contract (richer semantics), strengthen the implementation (ergodicity for adaptive and transdimensional samplers), and harden the interface (verified compilation of differentiable IRs), so that probabilistic programming can keep its founding promise on theorems rather than folklore.
