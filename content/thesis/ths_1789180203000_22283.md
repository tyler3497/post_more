# Distribution-Free Predictive Inference: Split Conformal Prediction, Adaptive Coverage, and the Theory of Valid Prediction Sets

## Abstract

Modern machine learning excels at point prediction but routinely misquantifies uncertainty: neural-network softmax outputs are not calibrated probabilities, and heuristic confidence intervals collapse under misspecification. Conformal prediction, pioneered by Vovk and collaborators, resolves this by constructing **prediction sets** with *finite-sample, distribution-free* coverage guarantees under nothing stronger than exchangeability of the data. This thesis develops the field's full architecture: the conformal framework — nonconformity scores, p-values, and the rank-uniformity argument yielding marginal validity — and **split (inductive) conformal prediction**, whose single-fit design makes it the workhorse of practice, contrasted with full conformal, cross-conformal, and jackknife+. We then treat *adaptivity*: **conformalized quantile regression (CQR)** for heteroscedastic intervals, and **APS/RAPS** scores for classification, which repair the failure of naive scores to reflect local difficulty. Finally we confront the central boundary — the **impossibility of exact conditional coverage** (Barber et al.) — and examine **weighted conformal prediction under covariate shift** and **adaptive conformal inference (ACI)** for non-exchangeable and adversarially shifting data. Proofs, empirical behavior, applications, and limitations complete the treatment.

## 1 Introduction

The central object of *predictive inference* is a set-valued map $\mathcal{C}$ that, given training data $\mathcal{D}_n = \{(X_i, Y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ and a test covariate $X_{n+1}$, returns a subset of the label space $\mathcal{Y}$ believed to contain the unobserved response $Y_{n+1}$. The classical frequentist confidence interval fixes the *parameter* and randomizes over the data; the predictive formulation instead asks for a **coverage guarantee**

$$
\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})\right) \geq 1 - \alpha,
\tag{1}
$$

where $\alpha \in (0,1)$ is a user-chosen miscoverage level. Two features distinguish the modern program from classical interval estimation. First, the guarantee (1) is **finite-sample** — it holds for every $n$, not asymptotically — and **distribution-free**: it must hold for *every* distribution $P$ over $(X, Y)$, with no parametric model, no smoothness, and no correctness assumption about the underlying learning algorithm. Second, the method is a **wrapper**: it bolts onto any black-box predictor $\hat{f}$ — random forests to deep neural networks — without retraining or access to internal representations.

Conformal prediction delivers exactly this. Developed in the late 1990s by Vovk, Gammerman, and Shafer within the *algorithmic randomness* framework [1], it entered mainstream practice around 2019–2022, when the deep learning community recognized that softmax confidences are pathologically miscalibrated [2]. Today conformal methods sit at the center of high-stakes deployments — medical diagnosis, autonomous perception, financial risk — wherever a wrong point prediction with no honest error bar is unacceptable.

> **Theorem (Marginal validity):** Let $(X_1, Y_1), \ldots, (X_{n+1}, Y_{n+1})$ be exchangeable, and let $s$ be any (symmetric) nonconformity score function. Then the conformal prediction set $\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}$ satisfies $\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}(X_{n+1})) \geq 1 - \alpha$ for all $\alpha \in (0, 1)$, and the coverage is also *near-exact*: at most $1 - \alpha + 1/(n+1)$ when scores are almost surely distinct [2].

The qualifier in (1) is **marginal** coverage: the probability averages over both the training data and the test point. A user naturally wants the stronger *conditional* guarantee $\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) \mid X_{n+1} = x) \geq 1 - \alpha$ for the specific individual at hand. The central drama of this field is that the marginal guarantee is achievable and essentially free, while the conditional guarantee is — in a precise and devastating sense — **impossible without assumptions** [5]. Everything else in this thesis is about navigating the space between these two poles: split vs. full conformal for computational tractability, adaptive scores (CQR, APS, RAPS) for approximating conditionality, and weighted and online variants for surviving non-exchangeability.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Exchangeability and the rank argument

All of conformal prediction rests on **exchangeability**: a sequence is exchangeable if its joint distribution is invariant under index permutations. I.i.d. data are exchangeable, but the converse fails (e.g., sampling without replacement). The key fact: for exchangeable, almost surely distinct $Z_1, \ldots, Z_{n+1}$, the rank of any one of them is uniform on $\{1, \ldots, n+1\}$ *regardless of the underlying distribution*. Conformal prediction is an elaborate packaging of this rank-uniformity fact [2].

### 2.2 Nonconformity scores and conformal p-values

A **nonconformity score** $s: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R}$ measures how "strange" a candidate pair $(x, y)$ looks. Canonical examples:

| Task | Score $s(x, y)$ | Resulting set |
|---|---|---|
| Regression | $|y - \hat{\mu}(x)|$ (absolute residual) | Interval $[\hat{\mu}(x) \pm q]$ |
| Regression | $\max(\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x) - y,\, y - \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x))$ | Adaptive interval (CQR) |
| Classification | $1 - \hat{\pi}_y(x)$ (least-ambiguous set value) | Label set |
| Classification | $\sum_{j: \hat{\pi}_j(x) \geq \hat{\pi}_y(x)} \hat{\pi}_j(x)$ | Adaptive label set (APS) |

In **full (transductive) conformal prediction**, for each candidate label $y$ we refit the model on $\mathcal{D}_n \cup \{(X_{n+1}, y)\}$, compute scores $s_1^y, \ldots, s_{n+1}^y$, and include $y$ in the set iff its score is not too large — i.e., the **conformal p-value**

$$
p^y = \frac{1 + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{1}\{s_i^y \geq s_{n+1}^y\}}{n+1}
\tag{2}
$$

exceeds $\alpha$. Exchangeability makes $p^{Y_{n+1}}$ (approximately) uniform on $\{1/(n+1), \ldots, 1\}$, so $\mathbb{P}(p^{Y_{n+1}} > \alpha) \geq 1 - \alpha$, which is exactly marginal coverage [1]. Full conformal is statistically efficient (uses all the data) but requires refitting the model for every candidate $y$ — prohibitive for deep learning.

### 2.3 The marginal vs. conditional distinction

Before building further, we must fix terminology, because the literature's loose language causes real confusion:

- **Marginal coverage:** $\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})) \geq 1-\alpha$, averaging over everything. Achievable distribution-free.
- **Conditional coverage:** $\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) \mid X_{n+1} = x) \geq 1-\alpha$ for *every* $x$. Provably impossible distribution-free [5].
- **Training-conditional coverage:** the guarantee holds with high probability over the training data, rather than in expectation. A stronger *PAC-style* claim; most conformal methods achieve only expectation-level validity unless augmented.
- **Group/label-conditional coverage:** coverage holds conditional on $Y_{n+1} = y$ (class-wise) or on a coarse group $g(X_{n+1})$. Achievable via *Mondrian* conformal predictors, which stratify calibration by group — at the cost of splitting calibration data [2].

This taxonomy organizes every result that follows. Adaptivity — making sets *wider where the problem is harder* — is the pragmatic response to the impossibility of conditional coverage: we cannot guarantee it, but we can *approximate* it, and the better the approximation, the more useful the sets.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Split (inductive) conformal prediction

The practical workhorse is **split** or **inductive** conformal prediction (Papadopoulos, Proedrou, Vovk, Gammerman, ECML 2002; modern treatment in [2]). The data are partitioned once into a *proper training* set $\mathcal{I}_{\text{tr}}$ and a *calibration* set $\mathcal{I}_{\text{cal}}$:

1. **Fit** $\hat{f}$ (any algorithm) on $\mathcal{I}_{\text{tr}}$.
2. **Score** calibration points: $s_i = s(X_i, Y_i)$ for $i \in \mathcal{I}_{\text{cal}}$.
3. **Quantile:** compute $\hat{q} = \text{Quantile}\!\left(\{s_i\}_{i \in \mathcal{I}_{\text{cal}}};\, \frac{\lceil (n_{\text{cal}}+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil}{n_{\text{cal}}}\right)$.
4. **Predict:** $\mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) = \{y : s(X_{n+1}, y) \leq \hat{q}\}$.

Because the model is frozen before scoring, calibration and test scores are exchangeable and the rank argument applies directly; the finite-sample correction is what converts asymptotic validity into exact validity [2]. Train once, calibrate once, predict with a single threshold. The price is data splitting, and the sets are *fixed-width* in score space — the rigidity that adaptive methods repair.

```python
import numpy as np

def split_conformal_sets(X_cal, y_cal, X_test, model, alpha=0.1):
    """Split conformal prediction sets for classification (LAC score)."""
    probs_cal = model.predict_proba(X_cal)          # (n_cal, K)
    scores = 1.0 - probs_cal[np.arange(len(y_cal)), y_cal]
    q = np.quantile(scores, np.ceil((len(scores) + 1) * (1 - alpha)) / len(scores))
    probs_test = model.predict_proba(X_test)        # (n_test, K)
    # include every label whose score is within the calibrated threshold
    return [set(np.where(1.0 - p <= q)[0]) for p in probs_test]
```

### 3.2 Cross-conformal and jackknife+

Two refinements recover data efficiency without full refitting:

- **Cross-conformal (K-fold):** each fold's scores come from a model trained on the other folds; p-values aggregate across folds. Loses the clean exchangeability proof but performs strongly in practice [2].
- **Jackknife+ (Barber, Candès, Ramdas, Tibshirani):** the leave-one-out intervals $\hat{\mu}_{-i}(X_{n+1}) \pm |Y_i - \hat{\mu}_{-i}(X_i)|$ are combined through a min-max p-value, yielding coverage at least $1 - 2\alpha$ — a weaker constant, but with the *stability* property that full conformal lacks when the learning algorithm is erratic [4]. The **CV+** variant generalizes to K folds.

| Method | Fits | Data used for training | Coverage guarantee | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full conformal | one per candidate $y$ | all $n$ | $1-\alpha$ | prohibitive for DL |
| Split conformal | 1 | $n/2$ (typical) | $1-\alpha$ | trivial |
| Cross-conformal ($K$ folds) | $K$ | all $n$ | $\approx 1-\alpha$ (empirical) | moderate |
| Jackknife+ / CV+ | $n$ or $K$ | all $n$ | $1-2\alpha$ (rigorous) | moderate |

### 3.3 The calibration quantile: why the $+1$ matters

The correction $\frac{\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil}{n}$ is the difference between a method that is *valid* and one that is merely *approximately* valid. With the naive level, coverage can dip to $1 - \alpha - \frac{1}{n+1}$; with the correction, the rank argument gives exactly $\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}) \geq 1-\alpha$ for *any* $n$, even $n = 50$. With small calibration sets — common in medical imaging — the $+1$ correction is the entire reason the guarantee can be trusted.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Conformalized quantile regression (CQR)

Standard split conformal with absolute residuals produces intervals of **constant width** $\hat{\mu}(x) \pm \hat{q}$ across the input space. Under heteroscedasticity — the rule, not the exception, in real data — this is wasteful: intervals over-cover easy regions and under-represent hard ones, yielding poor *conditional* behavior even while marginal coverage holds.

**Conformalized quantile regression** [3] fixes this by conformalizing a *quantile* learner rather than a mean learner:

1. On the proper training set, fit quantile regressors $\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x)$ and $\hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x)$ for the conditional quantiles (any algorithm with pinball loss — gradient boosting, quantile random forests, neural nets).
2. On the calibration set, compute the nonconformity score measuring how far $y_i$ falls outside the predicted quantile interval:
   $$
   E_i = \max\{\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(X_i) - Y_i,\; Y_i - \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(X_i)\}.
   $$
3. Let $\hat{Q} = \text{Quantile}(E_1, \ldots, E_{n_{\text{cal}}}; (1-\alpha)(1 + 1/n_{\text{cal}}))$.
4. Output the interval $\mathcal{C}(x) = [\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x) - \hat{Q},\; \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x) + \hat{Q}]$.

The result inherits both parents' strengths: the quantile regression supplies **heteroscedastic adaptivity**, while the conformal step supplies the **finite-sample marginal guarantee**, even if the quantile estimates are arbitrarily wrong [3]. If the quantile models are perfect, $\hat{Q} \approx 0$; if they are poor, $\hat{Q}$ inflates to compensate. On standard benchmarks, CQR intervals are *shorter on average* than residual-based conformal intervals at equal coverage [3].

> **Theorem (CQR validity, [3]):** If $(X_i, Y_i)_{i=1}^{n+1}$ are exchangeable, the CQR interval above satisfies $\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})) \geq 1-\alpha$ for any quantile estimators $\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}, \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}$.

### 4.2 Adaptive prediction sets (APS) and regularized APS for classification

For classification, the naive **LAC** (least-ambiguous set-valued classifier) score $s(x, y) = 1 - \hat{\pi}_y(x)$ — from Sadinle, Lei, and Wasserman — suffers a subtle adaptivity failure: for an easy, high-confidence example it returns the singleton $\{\arg\max \hat{\pi}\}$, but for a genuinely ambiguous example it still tends to return singletons, because only one label needs to beat the global threshold $\hat{q}$. Marginal coverage is maintained by *over-covering* easy points, a misallocation of the coverage budget [4].

The **APS** (adaptive prediction set) score of Romano, Sesia, and Candès [4] replaces the threshold logic with a *cumulative* one. Let the labels be ordered by estimated probability, $\hat{\pi}_{(1)}(x) \geq \hat{\pi}_{(2)}(x) \geq \cdots \geq \hat{\pi}_{(K)}(x)$. Define

$$
s(x, y) = \sum_{j=1}^{r_y} \hat{\pi}_{(j)}(x), \qquad r_y = \text{rank of label } y \text{ in the ordering}.
\tag{3}
$$

That is, the score of a label is the *total probability mass of all labels ranked at least as high*. The prediction set then includes labels greedily in decreasing probability order until their cumulative mass exceeds the calibrated threshold — exactly mimicking the oracle rule that includes labels until reaching $(1-\alpha)$ conditional mass [4]. APS sets therefore *grow* on ambiguous inputs (where probability mass is spread) and *shrink* to singletons on easy ones, approximating conditional coverage far better than LAC.

**RAPS** (regularized APS) adds a penalty $\lambda \cdot \max(0, r_y - k_{\text{reg}})$ to (3), discouraging absurdly large sets on pathological inputs — crucial tail regularization at ImageNet scale [4]. RAPS achieves the smallest average set sizes among valid methods while maintaining the best *worst-slice* conditional coverage [4].

### 4.3 The impossibility of exact conditional coverage

We now arrive at the field's central negative result. One would like

$$
\mathbb{P}\big(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) \,\big|\, X_{n+1} = x\big) \geq 1-\alpha \quad \text{for all } x.
\tag{4}
$$

**Barber, Candès, Ramdas, and Tibshirani [5]** prove that no nontrivial distribution-free procedure can achieve (4): any procedure satisfying exact conditional coverage for all distributions must output essentially the *entire* label space with probability approaching 1 — a vacuous guarantee. The proof is a coupling argument: an adversary concentrates the conditional distribution of $Y_{n+1}$ on whatever region the sets omit at $x$, and with no training data exactly at $x$ (a continuous covariate is never exactly repeated), the procedure cannot defend.

> **Theorem (Limits of conditional predictive inference, [5]):** For nonatomic covariate distributions, any $(1-\alpha)$-conditionally-valid prediction set with nontrivial expected size cannot exist in the distribution-free setting. Exact conditional coverage implies trivial (uninformative) sets.

This is not a counsel of despair but a *compass*. It tells us that adaptivity must be pursued through *relaxations*: Mondrian (group-conditional) coverage, which partitions $\mathcal{X}$ into bins and calibrates within each; *approximate* conditional coverage, which CQR and APS pursue heuristically; and *asymptotic* conditional coverage, which some methods achieve under smoothness assumptions [2, 5].

### 4.4 Weighted conformal prediction under covariate shift

Exchangeability fails the moment the test distribution differs from the training distribution. The most tractable failure mode is **covariate shift**: $P_{\text{train}}(X) \neq P_{\text{test}}(X)$ but $P(Y \mid X)$ is unchanged. **Tibshirani, Barber, Candès, and Ramdas [6]** show that conformal prediction survives this via a *weighted* notion of exchangeability. If the likelihood ratio $w(x) = dP_{\text{test}}(x)/dP_{\text{train}}(x)$ is known (or estimated, e.g., by a classifier distinguishing train from test covariates), then calibration scores receive weights $p_i^w(x) = w(X_i)/(\sum_j w(X_j) + w(x))$, and the prediction set

$$
\mathcal{C}_n^w(x) = \Big\{y : s(x, y) \leq \text{Quantile}\!\big(1-\alpha;\, \sum_i p_i^w(x)\,\delta_{s_i} + p_{n+1}^w(x)\,\delta_{\infty}\big)\Big\}
\tag{5}
$$

satisfies $\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}_n^w(X_{n+1})) \geq 1-\alpha$ under the shifted test distribution [6]. Intuitively, calibration points resembling the test point (high $w$) dominate the quantile; the same machinery extends to label shift, missing data, and causal counterfactuals [6].

```python
def weighted_conformal_threshold(scores, weights, alpha=0.1):
    """Weighted (1-alpha) quantile for covariate-shift calibration."""
    w = np.asarray(weights, dtype=float)
    w = np.append(w / w.sum(), 0.0)          # test point gets an atom at +inf
    s = np.append(np.asarray(scores), np.inf)
    order = np.argsort(s, kind="stable")
    cdf = np.cumsum(w[order])
    return s[order[np.searchsorted(cdf, 1.0 - alpha)]]
```

### 4.5 Online and adversarial conformal: adaptive conformal inference

**Adaptive conformal inference (ACI)** of Gibbs and Candès [7] drops exchangeability entirely, targeting **long-run time-average coverage** under arbitrary temporal shift:

$$
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbf{1}\{Y_t \in \mathcal{C}_t(X_t)\} \;\to\; 1-\alpha.
\tag{6}
$$

ACI maintains a scalar $\alpha_t$ updated by $\alpha_{t+1} = \alpha_t + \gamma(\alpha - \text{err}_t)$, where $\text{err}_t$ is the observed miscoverage; the set at time $t$ is the usual conformal set at level $1-\alpha_t$. This online gradient descent on the coverage error guarantees (6) *for any data-generating process whatsoever* — even adversarial — provided the sets can expand arbitrarily [7]. Multi-rate and quantile-tracking variants ("conformal PID control") handle abrupt changepoints.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The coverage proof, stripped to its bones

All conformal guarantees are corollaries of one lemma.

> **Lemma (Rank uniformity):** If $S_1, \ldots, S_{n+1}$ are exchangeable and almost surely distinct, then $\mathbb{P}(\text{rank of } S_{n+1} \leq k) = k/(n+1)$ for every $k$.

*Proof.* By exchangeability, all $(n+1)!$ orderings are equiprobable, so $S_{n+1}$ is equally likely to be the smallest, second smallest, and so on. ∎

For split conformal, calibration scores and the test score $S_{n+1} = s(X_{n+1}, Y_{n+1})$ are exchangeable (the model is fixed before scoring). The event $\{Y_{n+1} \notin \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})\} = \{S_{n+1} > \hat{q}\}$ means $S_{n+1}$ ranks among the largest scores — by the lemma this has probability at most $\alpha + 1/(n_{\text{cal}}+1)$, tightened to exactly $\alpha$ by the corrected quantile [2].

The same skeleton proves CQR validity (the scores $E_i$ are exchangeable), APS/RAPS validity (any score works), and weighted conformal validity (weighted exchangeability makes the *weighted* rank uniform) [3, 4, 6]. The impossibility result [5] is the converse direction: it shows the lemma's uniformity is *all* one can extract distribution-free, so conditional claims are hopeless.

### 5.2 Representative empirical behavior

The literature's experiments converge on a consistent picture [2, 3, 4]:

1. **Coverage holds at the nominal level** across methods and datasets — 90% intervals empirically cover 89–91% of the time, even with deliberately misspecified models. The guarantee is visible in finite samples, not merely asymptotic.
2. **Adaptivity buys efficiency.** On heteroscedastic regression benchmarks, CQR intervals are 10–30% shorter on average than constant-width conformal intervals at equal coverage [3]. On ImageNet, RAPS sets average 2–3 labels at 90% coverage, vs. 5+ for non-adaptive alternatives, while *worst-class* conditional coverage degrades far less [4].
3. **Weighted conformal recovers coverage under shift** where unweighted conformal under-covers by 5–15 points on synthetic covariate-shift benchmarks; the likelihood-ratio estimate is the binding constraint [6].
4. **ACI tracks nonstationarity.** On stock-volatility and electricity-demand data with regime changes, fixed-$\alpha$ conformal under-covers during volatile regimes, while ACI's time-average coverage converges to $1-\alpha$ with sets that expand and contract [7].

| Setting | Method | Guarantee | Key cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exchangeable, regression | Split conformal | Marginal $1-\alpha$ | Constant-width sets |
| Heteroscedastic regression | CQR [3] | Marginal $1-\alpha$ | Needs quantile learner |
| Exchangeable, classification | APS/RAPS [4] | Marginal $1-\alpha$ | Calibration of $k_{\text{reg}}, \lambda$ |
| Covariate shift | Weighted CP [6] | Marginal $1-\alpha$ under shift | Likelihood-ratio estimation |
| Arbitrary temporal shift | ACI [7] | Long-run average $1-\alpha$ | Sets may grow unboundedly |
| Exact $X$-conditional | — [5] | **Impossible** distribution-free | — |

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating plainly what conformal prediction does *not* do.

1. **Marginality is a weak promise.** A 90% prediction set can cover 100% of easy points and 0% of a hard minority subgroup. In medical or fairness-critical settings this is the central risk; Mondrian stratification mitigates it only for *pre-specified* groups [2, 5].
2. **Exchangeability is load-bearing.** Temporal dependence, feedback loops, and selection bias break it silently — the sets remain well-defined but the guarantee evaporates. Weighted and online variants patch specific violations, not all of them [6, 7].
3. **Efficiency depends on the base model.** The guarantee is free, but *set size* is not: a poor $\hat{f}$ yields valid but uselessly large sets. Conformal prediction quantifies uncertainty honestly; it does not create information.
4. **Conditional coverage is impossible, full stop** — exact and pointwise, without assumptions [5]. Every "adaptive" method is a heuristic whose conditional behavior must be validated slice by slice.
5. **Computational and data costs.** Split conformal halves the training data; full conformal is intractable for deep nets; jackknife+ needs $n$ refits; weighted conformal needs a good density-ratio estimate; ACI needs the freedom to output arbitrarily large sets [1, 4, 6, 7].
6. **The $1-\alpha$ target is arbitrary** and must be set by domain experts; interpreting "90% coverage" as "90% probability this specific set covers the truth" is the classic conditional-coverage fallacy the theory warns against [2, 5].

## 7 Conclusion

Conformal prediction is that rare object in machine learning: a method whose central promise — *finite-sample, distribution-free, assumption-free coverage* — is a theorem rather than a hope. The elementary rank-uniformity argument supports an entire ecosystem: split conformal for cheap validity, full/cross-conformal and jackknife+ for efficiency, CQR and APS/RAPS for adaptivity, weighted conformal for covariate shift, and ACI for adversarial streams.

The field's deepest lesson is its negative one: the impossibility theorem [5] draws a bright line — marginal validity is free, conditional validity is impossible, and everything in between is engineering. That clarity tells practitioners exactly what they buy — honest average-case uncertainty, not individual guarantees — and directs research toward the relaxations that matter: adaptivity, shift-robustness, group-conditional behavior. A framework that *knows what it does not know*, and can prove it, is the responsible default for black-box models in high-stakes settings.
