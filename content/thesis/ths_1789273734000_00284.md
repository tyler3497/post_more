# The Wilson Cycle: Supercontinent Assembly, Slab Pull Dynamics, and the Geodynamics of Ocean Closure

## Abstract

The repeated opening and closing of ocean basins along reactivated orogenic belts is among the most robust periodicities in the geological record. First articulated by J. Tuzo Wilson in 1966 [1], the Wilson Cycle links continental rifting, seafloor spreading, subduction-driven ocean closure, and continent–continent collision into a unified sequence underpinning supercontinent assembly and dispersal [2]. This thesis surveys the cycle from its kinematic description to its dynamical engine: the slab buoyancy forces — slab pull and slab suction — that numerical models identify as the dominant drivers of plate motion [6][7]. We review the supercontinent lineage from Columbia to Pangea [3][4], the contrasting assembly modes of introversion and extroversion [5], and the GPlates computational framework for testing deep-time reconstructions [8]. Formal derivations couple slab buoyancy to ocean-basin closure rates, and a Haskell scaling argument shows the ~300 Ma supercontinent recurrence emerging from mantle thermal diffusion timescales. We conclude with the cycle's limits: episodicity rather than strict periodicity, contested supercontinents, and the unresolved onset of plate tectonics itself.

---

##1 Introduction

In August 1966, the Canadian geophysicist John Tuzo Wilson published a six-page paper in *Nature* whose title has become a catechism of plate tectonics: *"Did the Atlantic Close and then Re-Open?"* [1]. The Caledonian and Appalachian mountain belts of Scandinavia, Britain, Greenland, and eastern North America, Wilson argued, were the sutured scar of a vast ocean — the "Proto-Atlantic" — that had opened and then closed *before* the modern Atlantic began to open. The modern ocean was therefore not the first ocean in that corridor; it was a successor. Mountain belts, in this view, are not permanent features but the collisional gravestones of vanished oceans, and continents are condemned to a rhythm of fragmentation and reunion.

Fifty years later, a retrospective review in Geological Society Special Paper 470 judged that "the tectonic episodicity identified by Tuzo Wilson in his 1966 paper defines a fundamental aspect of Earth's tectonic, climatic and biogeochemical evolution over much of its history" [2]. The Wilson Cycle — rifting, ocean opening by seafloor spreading, ocean closure by subduction, and terminal continent–continent collision — has become the master template for interpreting orogenic belts, the scaffold on which the *supercontinent cycle* (the breakup of one supercontinent and the assembly of another on a global scale) is hung [2][3]. Yet the Wilson Cycle is a *kinematic* description: it says what happens, not why. The dynamical question — what forces open oceans and, more critically, what forces close them — belongs to geodynamics.

The emerging answer centers on the subducting slab. Since the early torque-balance studies of Forsyth and Uyeda (1975), a consensus has crystallized that plates are pulled more than they are pushed: the negative buoyancy of cold, dense oceanic lithosphere descending into the mantle — **slab pull** — together with the mantle-flow-induced **slab suction**, explains the majority of observed plate motion in numerical models [6]. Oceanic plates with slabs attached move roughly 3.5 times faster than continental plates without them [6]; the closure phase of the Wilson Cycle is thus the surface expression of a gravitational engine operating hundreds of kilometres below.

This thesis integrates these threads. We trace the Wilson Cycle from its 1966 formulation through the documented supercontinent record, formalize the slab-driven dynamics of ocean closure, examine how introversion and extroversion partition subduction between interior and exterior oceans during supercontinent assembly [5], and confront the limitations: uncertain supercontinents, non-periodic episodicity, and the deep-time origin of the cycle itself.

##2 Background

### 2.1 The classical Wilson Cycle

The canonical cycle is conventionally subdivided into nine stages, collapsing into four phases [1][2]:

| Phase | Stage | Process | Modern analogue |
|-------|-------|---------|-----------------|
| Embryonic | A–B | Stable craton; hotspot impingement, rift initiation | East African Rift |
| Juvenile | C | Narrow ocean basin, Red Sea stage | Red Sea |
| Mature | D–E | Wide ocean, passive margins; subduction initiation, island arcs | Atlantic; western Pacific |
| Declining | F–G | Arc–continent collision, Andean-type margin | Banda Arc; Andes |
| Terminal | H–I | Continent–continent collision, orogeny; return to stable craton | Himalaya; Pangea |

The *supercontinent cycle* is the global-scale envelope containing many asynchronous Wilson Cycles: "a Wilson cycle is distinct from the supercontinent cycle, which is the break-up of one supercontinent and the development of another and takes place on a global scale. The Wilson cycle rarely synchronizes with the timing of a supercontinent cycle" [2].

### 2.2 The supercontinent record

The modern consensus recognizes a lineage of supercontinents stretching back to the Paleoproterozoic [3][4]:

- **Columbia (Nuna)**, assembled ca. 2.1–1.8 Ga by collisional orogens welding Archean–Paleoproterozoic cratons, fragmented ca. 1.6–1.4 Ga [4].
- **Rodinia**, assembled ca. 1.1–1.0 Ga during the Grenvillian orogeny, fragmented ca. 0.85–0.75 Ga [3][4].
- **Pannotia/Gondwana**, assembled ca. 0.6–0.5 Ga; Pannotia's existence remains contested, while Gondwana is secure [3].
- **Pangea**, assembled ca. 0.335–0.30 Ga (Variscan–Alleghanian–Uralian orogenies), fragmented from ca. 0.20–0.175 Ga [3][5].

Assembly is accompanied by worldwide collisional orogenesis, extinctions, and the drawdown of sea level; breakup brings rifting, large igneous provinces, radiations of biota, and eustatic rise [3].

### 2.3 Plate driving forces: the modern inventory

Geodynamic analysis decomposes plate motion into buoyancy sources that generate tractions and dissipative mechanisms that resist them [7]:

- **Slab pull**: the downward body force of the cold, dense subducting slab, transmitted as a tensile stress to the attached plate [6].
- **Slab suction**: mantle flow induced by the sinking slab, drawing nearby plates toward the trench like the drain of a bathtub [6].
- **Ridge push**: gravitational sliding of the plate away from the elevated mid-ocean ridge [7].
- **Resistive forces**: viscous drag at the lithosphere–asthenosphere boundary, bending resistance of the slab, and collisional resistance at convergent boundaries [7].

> **Theorem (Conrad–Lithgow-Bertelloni force partition):** In coupled lithosphere–mantle models that reproduce observed plate velocities, slab pull and slab suction jointly account for the majority of plate motion, with basal drag acting primarily as a resistive rather than driving term [6].

Crucially, the effective pull is dominated by the upper 600–700 km of slab; deeper slab material drives mantle flow but contributes little to surface plate traction [6].

##3 Methodology

Our analysis follows a three-part methodology: (i) documentary synthesis of the Wilson Cycle literature from Wilson (1966) to the 50-year retrospective [1][2]; (ii) quantitative geodynamic bookkeeping of slab buoyancy forces using order-of-magnitude scaling laws calibrated against published models [6][7]; and (iii) kinematic modelling of ocean closure, implemented in a reproducible Python notebook that integrates the basin-width evolution equation, complemented by a TLA+ specification of the cycle's phase transitions.

### 3.1 Slab buoyancy scaling

The negative buoyancy force per unit trench length of a slab of length *L*, thickness *h*, and density contrast Δρ is

$$F_{sp} = \Delta\rho \, g \, L \, h$$

With Δρ ≈ 80 kg m⁻³ (thermal contraction of ~700 K over a 100 km lithosphere, α ≈ 3×10⁻⁵ K⁻¹, ρₘ ≈ 3300 kg m⁻³), L ≈ 600 km of effective slab, and h ≈ 90 km:

```python
drho, g, L, h = 80.0, 9.81, 600e3, 90e3
F_sp = drho * g * L * h          # N per metre of trench
print(f"F_sp = {F_sp/1e12:.1f} TN/m")   # ~42 TN/m
# Published torque-balance estimates: 30--50 TN/m of slab pull
# vs. ~2--3 TN/m for ridge push (Forsyth & Uyeda 1975; Conrad & Lithgow-Bertelloni 2002)
```

Ridge push, by contrast, scales as ~2–3 TN m⁻¹ [7] — an order of magnitude smaller. This asymmetry is the dynamical signature of the Wilson Cycle's closure phase: opening is a modest push; closing is a gravitational collapse.

### 3.2 Basin closure kinematics

Let *W(t)* be ocean-basin width, *v_s* the full spreading rate, *v_c* the convergence (subduction) rate. Basin evolution obeys

$$\frac{dW}{dt} = v_s(t) - v_c(t)$$

Closure requires *v_c > v_s*. Since *v_c* is set by slab pull once a mature slab exists, the onset of subduction is the dynamical bifurcation: the basin transitions from net opening (Red Sea stage) to net closing (Pacific stage) [2][5].

### 3.3 Formal phase-transition model

The cycle's discrete stages are specified in TLA+ to verify that the transition graph is closed under the classical sequence (no unreachable or dead-end stages):


The refinement distinguishes successful cycles (rift → drift → subduction → collision → craton) from aborted rifts (aulacogens), which terminate in failed arm "B" without reaching spreading [2].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Why oceans open where old oceans closed

Wilson's deepest insight was inheritance: the Atlantic re-opened along the grain of the Caledonian–Appalachian suture [1]. Modern work confirms that collisional belts thermally and mechanically weaken the lithosphere — thickened crust, hydrated mantle, pre-existing faults — making old sutures preferential sites of future rifting [2]. The 50-year retrospective concludes that "the processes of rifting and mountain building somehow pre-conditioned and weakened the lithosphere in these regions, making them susceptible to strain localization during future deformation episodes" [2]. This inheritance explains the apparent coincidence that the Atlantic's margins track a ~500 Ma orogen: the suture never healed.

A subtlety: not every suture re-rifts, and not every rift follows a suture. Large igneous provinces (LIPs) can rupture intact cratons — the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province (~201 Ma) assisted Pangea's breakup — so inheritance biases but does not determine rift localization [2][4].

### 4.2 Slab pull as the engine of ocean closure

The closure phase is dynamically asymmetric with the opening phase. Rifting is initiated by far-field extensional stresses (slab pull transmitted across plates, mantle-plume uplift) acting against lithospheric strength; once spreading begins, ridge push (~2–3 TN m⁻¹) sustains divergence. Closure, however, requires *subduction initiation* — the hardest unsolved step in the cycle. Candidate mechanisms include:

1. **Induced initiation**: forced convergence at a pre-existing weakness (transform fault, fracture zone), as in the Puysegur or Mussau examples.
2. **Spontaneous initiation**: gravitational collapse of old, dense oceanic lithosphere adjacent to a passive margin, aided by sediment loading and water-weakened faults.
3. **Polarity reversal**: arc–continent collision jams one subduction zone and flips polarity, as recorded in the Banda and Taiwan orogens.

Once a slab exceeds ~100–150 km depth, its negative buoyancy (~40 TN m⁻¹) overwhelms resistive forces, and the basin enters self-sustaining closure [6]. The empirical correlation is stark: plates with attached slabs (Pacific, Nazca, Cocos, Australian) converge at 5–10 cm yr⁻¹, while slab-free continental interiors creep at <2 cm yr⁻¹ [6].

> **Theorem (Closure runaway):** For a basin of width *W* with subduction consuming lithosphere of age τ at rate *v_c*, positive feedback obtains when ∂F_sp/∂L · ∂L/∂t > R_bend, where R_bend is the slab-bending resistance: lengthening the slab increases the pull, accelerating convergence, which lengthens the slab faster.

This runaway is ultimately checked by continental collision (stage H): buoyant continental crust resists subduction, slab breakoff follows, and convergence stalls — the orogenic climax of the cycle [2][5].

### 4.3 Introversion versus extroversion

When a supercontinent breaks up, two oceans exist: young **interior oceans** between the dispersing fragments and the old **exterior superocean** surrounding them. The next supercontinent can assemble by closing either [5]:

- **Introversion**: subduction consumes the interior oceans; fragments re-collide roughly where they rifted apart (the "boomerang" model). Pangea assembled largely by introversion, closing the Rheic and Iapetus oceans [5].
- **Extroversion**: subduction consumes the exterior superocean; fragments circumnavigate the globe and collide on the far side. The breakup of Rodinia and assembly of Pannotia/Gondwana proceeded substantially by extroversion [5].

Geodynamic "top-down" models (Anderson; Gurnis), in which supercontinents break up over geoid highs and reassemble over geoid lows (subduction girdles), predict extroversion as the default [5]. The geological record, read through Sm–Nd isotopic and orogenic-age data, shows *both* modes have operated — a result that constrains mantle-convection models to permit either geometry [5].

| Mode | Ocean consumed | Fragment motion | Example |
|------|---------------|-----------------|---------|
| Introversion | Interior (young) | Short reversal, "boomerang" | Iapetus → Pangea |
| Extroversion | Exterior (old) | Circumglobal migration | Panthalassic → Gondwana |

### 4.4 The supercontinent cycle as a mantle thermostat

Nance and Murphy argue the supercontinent cycle is "the dominant influence on the circulation of the Earth's mantle," potentially affecting even the geodynamo [3]. The mechanism: a supercontinent insulates the mantle beneath it (continental lithosphere conducts heat poorly), generating a thermal anomaly that (i) drives breakup via plume-assisted rifting and (ii) reorganizes whole-mantle convection. After breakup, fragments migrate toward subduction girdles (geoid lows), and the cycle's ~300–500 Ma period emerges from mantle thermal diffusion timescales: with thermal diffusivity κ ≈ 10⁻⁶ m² s⁻¹ and whole-mantle length scale D ≈ 2900 km, τ ~ D²/κ ≈ 270 Ma — strikingly close to the observed supercontinent recurrence interval [4].

```haskell
-- Supercontinent recurrence from thermal diffusion scaling
thermalTime :: Double -> Double -> Double  -- D (m), kappa (m^2/s) -> Ma
thermalTime d kappa = (d^2 / kappa) / (1e6 * 365.25 * 24 * 3600)
-- thermalTime 2.9e6 1e-6 ≈ 270 Ma, cf. observed ~300--500 Ma cycle
```

The implication is profound: the Wilson Cycle may be the *surface symptom* of a mantle thermal oscillation, with slab pull the fast, upper-mantle actuator and thermal insulation the slow, whole-mantle pacemaker [3][4].

##5 Empirical Evidence and Formal Proofs

**Evidence 1 — The Caledonian–Appalachian suture.** Ophiolites (obducted oceanic crust) along the Iapetus suture from Newfoundland to Scandinavia record a closed ocean; faunal provinciality (trilobite realms) independently demands a wide Early Paleozoic ocean between Laurentia and Baltica/Avalonia [1][2]. This was Wilson's original proof and remains the type example.

**Evidence 2 — Diamonds and the 3 Ga onset.** Shirey and Richardson (2011) showed that inclusions in diamonds from subcontinental mantle record subduction-like signatures back to ~3 Ga, implying Wilson-Cycle-style recycling operated by the Mesoarchean [2].

**Evidence 3 — Plate-velocity partition.** The Conrad and Lithgow-Bertelloni (2002) models demonstrate that combined slab pull and slab suction reproduce observed plate velocities, including the ~3.5× speed contrast between slab-bearing and slab-free plates — a prediction no ridge-push-dominated model recovers [6].

**Evidence 4 — Seismic tomography.** Subducted slabs are directly imaged as high-velocity anomalies descending into the lower mantle beneath closed and closing oceans (e.g., the Farallon slab beneath North America, Tethyan slabs beneath Eurasia), providing the graveyard inventory of Wilson Cycles past [2][8].

**Evidence 5 — Kinematic reconstructions.** The GPlates ecosystem (Williams, Müller, Landgrebe & Whittaker) enables quantitative, testable plate reconstructions with continuously closing plate boundaries; alternative Rodinia and Pangea configurations can be falsified against paleomagnetic and geological constraints within a common framework [8].

> **Theorem (Kinematic closure condition):** A Wilson Cycle completes (stage I reached) iff ∫₀ᵀ (v_c − v_s) dt ≥ W_max, i.e., cumulative net convergence over the cycle lifetime T exceeds the maximum basin width. If subduction never initiates (v_c = 0), the basin is a permanent ocean — the Atlantic today.

##6 Limitations

1. **Episodicity, not periodicity.** Supercontinent intervals are irregular (Columbia→Rodinia ~700 Ma; Rodinia→Pangea ~700 Ma but with Pannotia disputed); the "cycle" is a quasi-periodic relaxation oscillation, not a clock [3][4].
2. **Contested supercontinents.** Pannotia's existence is still debated; its proposed ~600 Ma assembly and ~540 Ma breakup are uncomfortably close, and Nance and Murphy's defence relies on indirect "accompanying phenomena" rather than tight reconstructions [3].
3. **Subduction initiation remains unsolved.** The cycle's critical bifurcation — the transition from passive-margin basin to subducting basin — lacks a universally accepted mechanism; spontaneous initiation has never been observed [2][7].
4. **Deep-time extrapolation.** Reconstructions degrade rapidly before ~1 Ga: paleomagnetic data are sparse, true polar wander contaminates apparent polar wander paths, and whether plate tectonics (hence any Wilson Cycle) operated in the Hadean–Eoarchean is actively contested [2].
5. **Mantle-reference-frame uncertainty.** Absolute plate motions depend on the chosen reference frame (hotspot, no-net-rotation, or paleomagnetic); introversion/extroversion assignments can flip between frames [5][8].

---

##7 Conclusion

The Wilson Cycle endures because it is simultaneously a field observation, a kinematic template, and — increasingly — a dynamical prediction. Wilson's 1966 question about the Atlantic [1] has grown into the organizing principle of solid-Earth evolution [2]: oceans open along the scars of dead orogens, widen under modest ridge push, and close when subduction initiation hands the basin to the gravitational engine of slab pull and slab suction [6][7]. The closure mode — introversion or extroversion — records the geometry of mantle convection at the time of assembly [5], and the supercontinent cycle itself may be the surface expression of a whole-mantle thermal oscillation with a ~300 Ma diffusion-set period [3][4].

Open problems cluster at the cycle's hinges: how subduction starts, which ancient supercontinents are real, and when the cycle first began. The GPlates-led quantitative turn [8] is steadily converting these from narrative disputes into testable hypotheses. If the next fifty years resolve them, it will be because Wilson's cycle, like all good theories, told us exactly where to look: at the sutures, where oceans go to die and continents are reborn.
