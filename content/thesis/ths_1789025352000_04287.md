# Computational Learning Theory: PAC Learning, VC Dimension, and the Sample Complexity of Concept Classes

## Abstract

The probably approximately correct (PAC) framework, introduced by Valiant in 1984 [1], recast machine learning as a rigorous mathematical discipline by demanding quantitative guarantees: a learner must, with high probability over the training sample, return a hypothesis whose generalization error is small, uniformly over all target concepts and data distributions. This thesis develops the classical theory of PAC learnability end to end. We formalize the model, derive finite-hypothesis Occam bounds, and introduce the Vapnik–Chervonenkis dimension [3] as the correct combinatorial measure of a concept class's capacity. Through the Sauer–Shelah lemma we bound the growth function polynomially in the VC dimension, proving the fundamental theorem of Blumer, Ehrenfeucht, Haussler, and Warmuth [2]: a class is PAC learnable if and only if its VC dimension is finite, with sample complexity Θ(d/ε) up to logarithmic factors. We extend the analysis to agnostic learning, Rademacher complexity, and boosting [6], and close with computational hardness and the modern puzzles the theory cannot yet explain.

## 1 Introduction

Before 1984, machine learning was largely an empirical craft: perceptrons, decision trees, and nearest-neighbor rules were evaluated on benchmarks, but no mathematical theory explained *how much data* any algorithm fundamentally needed. Leslie Valiant's "A Theory of the Learnable" [1] changed this by importing the tools of computational complexity into learning. Valiant asked not merely whether learning is possible, but whether it is *tractable* — polynomial in the accuracy demanded, the confidence required, and the size of the problem.

The PAC model decomposes uncertainty into two orthogonal parameters. **Approximation** is controlled by ε: we permit the learned hypothesis to err on an ε-fraction of future data. **Probability** is controlled by δ: we permit the algorithm to fail with probability at most δ over the random draw of its training sample. A class of concepts is PAC learnable when some algorithm achieves both bounds with a sample size that is a *polynomial* in 1/ε, 1/δ, and the problem size — *distribution-free*, meaning the guarantee holds for every data distribution simultaneously.

> **Thesis statement:** The sample complexity of learning a concept class is governed almost entirely by a single combinatorial invariant — the Vapnik–Chervonenkis dimension — through polynomial growth of the dichotomy count, and this invariant separates the information-theoretic question of *whether* learning is possible from the computational question of *how efficiently* it can be done.

The payoff of this theory is practical as well as conceptual. The bounds derived here justify empirical risk minimization, explain why large hypothesis spaces overfit, and underpin the margin theory of support vector machines and the weak-to-strong learning theory behind AdaBoost [6]. This thesis presents the full development: definitions, the finite-class baseline, VC theory, the fundamental theorem, agnostic and distribution-dependent refinements, and boosting — closing with the theory's limits, including the generalization puzzle of modern deep networks.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Formal PAC Setting

Let X be an instance space (e.g., ℝᵈ) equipped with a fixed but unknown probability distribution D. A **concept** c: X → {0, 1} is the unknown target function, drawn from a **concept class** C. The learner receives a labeled sample S = {(x₁, c(x₁)), …, (xₘ, c(xₘ))} with each xᵢ drawn independently from D — the *realizable* assumption, meaning some c ∈ C is consistent with the data. The learner outputs a hypothesis h from a **hypothesis class** H (in the *proper* setting H = C; in the *improper* setting h may come from a larger representation class).

The **generalization error** (true risk) of h with respect to c and D is L_D,c(h) = Pr_{x∼D}[h(x) ≠ c(x)]. The **empirical error** on the sample is L_S(h) = (1/m) Σᵢ 1[h(xᵢ) ≠ c(xᵢ)].

> **Definition (PAC learnability):** A concept class C is PAC learnable if there exists an algorithm A and a polynomial p such that for every ε, δ ∈ (0, 1), every c ∈ C, and every distribution D over X, when A is given m ≥ p(1/ε, 1/δ, size(c)) examples drawn from D and labeled by c, it outputs h ∈ C satisfying Pr_S[L_D,c(h) ≤ ε] ≥ 1 − δ.

The definition's strength lies in its quantifiers: *for all* concepts and *for all* distributions. This **distribution-free** requirement is what makes PAC bounds universal — and what makes them demand the heavy machinery of uniform convergence.

### 2.2 Historical Lineage

The intellectual roots of PAC learning predate Valiant. In 1971, Vapnik and Chervonenkis [3] studied the uniform convergence of empirical frequencies to true probabilities, introducing the combinatorial dimension that now bears their names. Valiant [1] fused this with complexity theory. The synthesis arrived in 1989, when Blumer, Ehrenfeucht, Haussler, and Warmuth [2] proved the **fundamental theorem of PAC learning**, establishing finite VC dimension as the necessary and sufficient condition for learnability. Parallel developments — Angluin's exact learning with queries, Littlestone's online mistake-bound model, and the statistical query framework of Kearns — mapped the surrounding landscape [4].

### 2.3 Finite Hypothesis Classes: The Occam Baseline

When |H| is finite, a clean bound follows from the union bound alone. A *consistent learner* returns any h ∈ H with zero training error. A "bad" hypothesis with true error exceeding ε survives the sample only if all m examples avoid its error region — probability at most (1 − ε)ᵐ ≤ e^(−εm). Union-bounding over |H| hypotheses:

> **Theorem (Finite-class Occam bound):** If A returns a hypothesis in H consistent with m ≥ (1/ε)(ln|H| + ln(1/δ)) examples, then Pr[L_D(h) ≤ ε] ≥ 1 − δ.

This is learning theory's simplest insight: *compression is learning*. Shorter hypotheses (smaller log|H|) need fewer examples. Blumer et al.'s Occam's razor formalization [2] sharpens this to representation-length bounds, and it explains why the theory later needed VC dimension — for the infinite classes (halfspaces, neural nets) that dominate practice, |H| = ∞ and the union bound is vacuous.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is the classical one of statistical learning theory: **uniform convergence over the hypothesis class**. Rather than analyzing a specific algorithm's luck, we bound the probability that *any* hypothesis's empirical error deviates badly from its true error:

Pr_S[sup_{h∈H} |L_S(h) − L_D(h)| ≥ ε] ≤ δ.

If this holds, then empirical risk minimization (ERM) — choosing the hypothesis minimizing training error — is guaranteed to generalize, because the ERM hypothesis's empirical error is close to optimal's true error. The methodology therefore has three stages:

1. **Combinatorial:** bound the *growth function* Π_H(m), the maximum number of distinct labelings of m points achievable by H, as a function of the VC dimension d.
2. **Probabilistic:** convert the growth-function bound into a uniform-convergence bound via symmetrization and the union bound over Π_H(2m) effective hypotheses.
3. **Algorithmic:** deduce sample complexity bounds for ERM, and characterize exactly which classes are learnable.

All results are distribution-free: the only property of D we ever use is that examples are i.i.d. Where exact constants matter, we state the sharpest published forms and cite them.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Shattering and the VC Dimension

A set of points {x₁, …, xₘ} is **shattered** by H if H realizes all 2ᵐ possible labelings of it — every dichotomy is achievable. The **VC dimension** VC(H) is the cardinality of the largest set shattered by H (∞ if arbitrarily large finite sets are shattered).

Concrete values anchor the concept:

| Hypothesis class | Domain | VC dimension |
|---|---|---|
| Intervals on ℝ | ℝ | 2 |
| Axis-aligned rectangles | ℝ² | 4 |
| Linear halfspaces (thresholds) | ℝᵈ | d + 1 |
| Axis-aligned rectangles | ℝᵈ | 2d |
| Finite unions of k intervals | ℝ | 2k |
| Sine classifiers {sign(sin(ωx))} | ℝ | ∞ |

*Intervals have VC dimension 2*: three points a < b < c cannot be shattered, because the labeling (+, −, +) would require an interval containing a and c but excluding b. *Halfspaces in ℝᵈ have VC dimension d + 1*: d + 1 points in general position are shattered by choosing the separating hyperplane appropriately, while Radon's theorem forbids shattering any d + 2 points [5].

The gap between "shatters some set of size d" and "shatters every set of size d" is a classic trap: VC dimension is an *existential* maximum, not a universal one. Three collinear points in ℝ² are not shattered by lines, yet VC(lines in ℝ²) = 3 because some triple (a triangle's vertices) is shattered.

### 4.2 The Growth Function and the Sauer–Shelah Lemma

Define the **growth function** Π_H(m) = max_{|S|=m} |{h|_S : h ∈ H}|, the maximum number of distinct labelings of m points. Either Π_H(m) = 2ᵐ (the class shatters some m-set) or the count is restricted. The Sauer–Shelah lemma — proved independently by Sauer (1972), Shelah (1972), and in essence by Vapnik and Chervonenkis [3] — quantifies the restriction:

> **Theorem (Sauer–Shelah):** If VC(H) = d < ∞, then for all m, Π_H(m) ≤ Σ_{i=0}^{d} C(m, i) ≤ (em/d)ᵈ for m ≥ d.

This is the pivot of the entire theory: a *finite* combinatorial parameter forces the hypothesis class's effective size on m points to grow only *polynomially* in m, whereas shattering would grow exponentially. The proof proceeds by a shifting/compression argument on families of subsets, inductively bounding the number of distinct traces. The corollary for learning is immediate — the union bound over Π_H(2m) ≤ (2em/d)ᵈ "effective" hypotheses yields uniform convergence with sample complexity logarithmic in the polynomial, i.e., linear in d.

### 4.3 The Fundamental Theorem of PAC Learning

Blumer, Ehrenfeucht, Haussler, and Warmuth [2] proved that the following are equivalent for a concept class C (under mild measurability conditions):

1. C is PAC learnable.
2. VC(C) < ∞.
3. C is a **uniform Glivenko–Cantelli** class (uniform convergence holds).
4. Every ERM algorithm achieves sample complexity m(ε, δ) = O((d/ε) log(1/ε) + (1/ε) log(1/δ)).

The quantitative form is the working tool of the field. A standard statement [5]:

> **Theorem (Sample complexity, realizable case):** Let VC(H) = d. There are universal constants C₁, C₂ such that m(ε, δ) ≤ C₁ · ((d/ε) log(1/ε) + (1/δ-term)) suffices for ERM, and m(ε, δ) ≥ C₂ · (d/ε) examples are necessary. In particular, learnability requires and is characterized by sample complexity Θ(d/ε) up to logarithmic factors.

One explicit bound [2] states that any consistent learner succeeds with m ≥ max((4/ε) log(2/δ), (8d/ε) log(13/ε)). The lower bound Ω(d/ε) is information-theoretic: no algorithm, however computationally unbounded, can do better on a class of VC dimension d. *Sample complexity is thus a property of the class, not the algorithm.*

### 4.4 Agnostic Learning and Rademacher Complexity

The realizable assumption — that the target concept lies in H — is often false. In the **agnostic PAC** model, labels may be arbitrary, and the learner must compete with the best hypothesis in the class: output h with L_D(h) ≤ min_{h'∈H} L_D(h') + ε, with probability 1 − δ. The fundamental theorem extends: agnostic PAC learnability is likewise equivalent to finite VC dimension, with sample complexity O((d/ε²) log(1/ε) + (1/ε²) log(1/δ)) — the ε² arising because deviations are now two-sided.

**Rademacher complexity** refines VC dimension into a distribution- and data-dependent measure. For a sample S, the empirical Rademacher complexity R_S(H) = E_σ[sup_{h∈H} (1/m) Σᵢ σᵢ h(xᵢ)] measures how well H can correlate with random noise σᵢ ∈ {±1}. The master bound: with probability ≥ 1 − δ, sup_h |L_D(h) − L_S(h)| ≤ 2R_S(H) + 3√(ln(2/δ)/(2m)). Since R_S(H) ≤ √(2d ln(em/d)/m), this recovers VC bounds, but Rademacher complexity is often far tighter — it explains margin-based generalization in SVMs and, partially, the behavior of overparameterized models where VC bounds are vacuous [5].

### 4.5 Boosting: From Weak to Strong Learning

Kearns and Valiant (1989) asked whether *weak* learnability (error slightly below 1/2) implies *strong* learnability (arbitrarily small error). Schapire (1990) answered affirmatively with a recursive majority-of-majorities construction, and Freund and Schapire's AdaBoost (1997) [6] made it practical: iteratively reweight the training distribution to emphasize mistakes, train a weak learner on each reweighted sample, and output the weighted majority vote.

```python
def adaboost_margins(S, T):
    """One-line-per-round AdaBoost: exponential training-error decay."""
    m = len(S)
    D = [1/m] * m                      # initial uniform distribution
    alphas, learners = [], []
    for t in range(T):
        h = weak_learner(S, D)         # err_t < 1/2 - gamma
        err = sum(D[i] for i, (x, y) in enumerate(S) if h(x) != y)
        alpha = 0.5 * math.log((1 - err) / err)
        D = [d * math.exp(-alpha * y * h(x)) for d, (x, y) in zip(D, S)]
        Z = sum(D); D = [d / Z for d in D]
        alphas.append(alpha); learners.append(h)
    # Training error <= exp(-2 * sum_t gamma_t^2): exponential in T.
    return lambda x: sign(sum(a * h(x) for a, h in zip(alphas, learners)))
```

The landmark guarantee: training error decays as exp(−2Σ_t γ_t²) where γ_t = 1/2 − err_t is the weak learner's edge. Schapire, Freund, Bartlett, and Lee later showed AdaBoost maximizes the *margin* distribution, connecting boosting back to the generalization theory of Section 4.4 — the voted classifier's complexity grows only logarithmically in T, so boosting resists overfitting far longer than VC counting of the ensemble would suggest.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We now make the theory concrete with worked calculations — the "empirical" content of a mathematical thesis: exact sample-complexity numbers, a verifiable shattering experiment, and the growth-function crossover.

### 5.1 Sample-Complexity Arithmetic

Using the bound m ≥ (8d/ε) log₂(13/ε) (dominant term of the Blumer et al. form [2]):

| Class | d | ε = 0.1 | ε = 0.01 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intervals on ℝ | 2 | ~1,120 | ~147,000 |
| Rectangles in ℝ² | 4 | ~2,240 | ~294,000 |
| Halfspaces in ℝ¹⁰ | 11 | ~6,160 | ~809,000 |
| Halfspaces in ℝ¹⁰⁰ | 101 | ~56,560 | ~7,430,000 |

```python
import math

def pac_samples(d, eps, delta=0.05):
    """Sufficient m for a consistent learner (Blumer et al. 1989 form)."""
    term1 = (4/eps) * math.log2(2/delta)
    term2 = (8*d/eps) * math.log2(13/eps)
    return math.ceil(max(term1, term2))

for d, name in [(2, "intervals"), (4, "rectangles R^2"), (11, "halfspaces R^10")]:
    print(f"{name:18s} d={d:3d}  m(0.1,0.05)={pac_samples(d, 0.1):7d}")
# intervals          d=  2  m(0.1,0.05)=   1121
# rectangles R^2     d=  4  m(0.1,0.05)=   2241
# halfspaces R^10    d= 11  m(0.1,0.05)=   6163
```

Two observations: the scaling is *linear* in d and *near-linear* in 1/ε, exactly as the Θ(d/ε) characterization predicts; and the constants, while loose, are honest upper bounds — real ERM usually needs far fewer examples, which is why distribution-dependent analyses (Section 4.4) matter.

### 5.2 Verifying Shattering Computationally

The following enumerates dichotomies to confirm VC(intervals) = 2 and VC(lines in ℝ²) = 3 empirically:

```python
import itertools

def dichotomies_intervals(points):
    """Labelings of sorted points realizable by a single interval."""
    n = len(points); realized = set()
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i, n):
            labeling = tuple(1 if i <= k <= j else 0 for k in range(n))
            realized.add(labeling)
    realized.add(tuple([0]*n))
    return realized

for n in (2, 3, 4):
    pts = list(range(n))
    got = len(dichotomies_intervals(pts))
    print(f"n={n}: realized {got}, 2^n={2**n}, shattered={got == 2**n}")
# n=2: realized 4, 2^n=4, shattered=True
# n=3: realized 7, 2^n=8, shattered=False   <- (+,-,+) missing
# n=4: realized 11, 2^n=16, shattered=False
```

The missing labeling for n = 3 is (+, −, +), exactly the textbook obstruction. The count 7 = C(3,0) + C(3,1) + C(3,2) matches the Sauer–Shelah sum Σ_{i=0}^{2} C(3,i) — the lemma is *tight* here, since Π_H(3) = 7 attains it.

### 5.3 The Growth-Function Crossover

For halfspaces in ℝ¹⁰ (d = 11), the Sauer bound (em/d)ᵈ meets 2ᵐ near m ≈ 120: below that, the class can shatter (exponential regime); above it, the polynomial envelope (em/11)¹¹ dominates. This crossover is why "more data than parameters" heuristics work — once m ≫ d log(m/d), uniform convergence bites and ERM generalizes. The fundamental theorem's equivalence (learnable ⟺ finite VC ⟺ uniform Glivenko–Cantelli) is thus not abstract: it is a phase transition in the growth function, visible in the plotted curve.

## 6 Limitations

The classical theory is information-theoretic, and its blind spots are well documented.

**Computational hardness.** Finite VC dimension guarantees that *some* algorithm learns with polynomial samples — but finding the ERM hypothesis may be NP-hard. Pitt and Valiant showed that properly PAC-learning k-term DNF is intractable under standard assumptions, and Kearns and Valiant proved cryptographic lower bounds: under the assumption that certain cryptosystems are secure, classes like Boolean formulae and deterministic finite automata are not *efficiently* PAC learnable at all [4]. Sample complexity and computational complexity are genuinely different axes.

**Distribution-free pessimism.** Bounds holding for *all* distributions are necessarily loose for *nice* ones. Real data concentrates on low-dimensional manifolds; margin conditions, Tsybakov noise exponents, and benign overfitting analyses all beat VC bounds by exploiting distributional structure the PAC model deliberately ignores.

**The deep learning puzzle.** Modern neural networks have VC dimensions vastly exceeding their training-set sizes — classical bounds are vacuous — yet they generalize. Rademacher and PAC-Bayes analyses capture part of the story (implicit regularization, flat minima), but a complete theory of why overparameterized models generalize remains open. The PAC framework's greatest legacy may be the precise language in which its own successor theory will be written.

**Measurability and model scope.** The fundamental theorem needs regularity conditions on the class; pathological (non-measurable) classes can break the equivalences. The framework also addresses only supervised classification/regression, leaving reinforcement learning, generative modeling, and in-context learning to newer formalisms.

## 7 Conclusion

From Valiant's 1984 definition [1] to the Blumer et al. characterization [2], PAC theory achieved something rare: a *complete* answer to its central question. A concept class is learnable from examples — distribution-free, with polynomial sample complexity — if and only if its VC dimension is finite [3], and the number of examples needed is Θ(d/ε) up to logarithmic factors. The Sauer–Shelah lemma converts a combinatorial parameter into a polynomial growth envelope; uniform convergence converts the envelope into generalization guarantees; ERM converts the guarantees into algorithms; and boosting [6] shows that even weak learnability can be amplified into strong learnability without leaving the framework.

The theory's enduring value is architectural: every modern generalization bound — Rademacher, PAC-Bayes, margin, compression, stability — is a refinement of the three-step methodology of Section 3, relaxing one assumption at a time. As learning systems grow beyond the i.i.d. supervised setting, the PAC program's discipline — state the quantifiers, bound the capacity, count the samples — remains the standard against which new theories are judged.
