# HotStuff and Chained Byzantine Fault Tolerance: Linear View-Change Communication, Pacemaker Synchronization, Threshold-Signature Quorum Certificates, and Liveness under Partial Synchrony

## Abstract

HotStuff is the first partially synchronous, leader-based Byzantine fault-tolerant (BFT) replication protocol to combine *linear* $O(n)$ communication complexity with *optimistic responsiveness* — the property that a correct leader advances consensus at the pace of the actual network rather than a worst-case delay bound. This thesis presents a rigorous treatment of HotStuff and its chained variant through the four mechanisms behind these properties: **(i)** threshold-signature *quorum certificates* compressing $n-f$ votes into one constant-size authenticator; **(ii)** a three-phase discipline that, pipelined across views, yields a single-message-per-view protocol with a *three-chain commit rule*; **(iii)** a *pacemaker* separating view synchronization from the safety core, making leader failover linear; and **(iv)** a liveness argument in the partially synchronous model guaranteeing progress after GST. We formalize the `safeNode` voting rule, prove the quorum-intersection safety invariants, analyze Cogsworth-style pacemaker bounds, and survey empirical evaluations — the original 100-replica deployment, pipelined HotStuff studies, HotStuff-1's one-phase speculation, and Flow's production pacemaker — closing with limitations including quadratic worst-case failover cascades and threshold-cryptography setup assumptions.

## 1 Introduction

Byzantine fault-tolerant state machine replication has moved from the data center to the open Internet, where blockchains demand *frequent* proposer rotation — for chain quality [2], censorship resistance, and fairness. The inherited classical protocols were designed for long-lived leaders: in PBFT [3], a leader change triggers an all-to-all view-change subprotocol costing $\Theta(n^2)$ messages and $\Theta(n^3)$ authenticators. When views change after *every* block, this dominates the system.

HotStuff [1], introduced by Yin, Malkhi, Reiter, Gueta, and Abraham and published at PODC'19, attacks this bottleneck with two explicit design goals — **linearity** (after GST, any correct leader, *including a replacement*, needs only $O(n)$ communication to commit a block) and **optimistic responsiveness** (a correct leader waits only for the first $n-f$ responses, advancing at actual network speed rather than a worst-case bound $\Delta$). These are achieved by composing threshold-signature quorum certificates, a three-phase pipeline overlapped across views, a pacemaker isolating view synchronization from the safety logic, and the `safeNode` locking discipline. The result is also a *framework* expressing DLS, PBFT, Tendermint, and Casper as instantiations of one generic protocol [1].

This thesis makes the following contributions:

- **(C1)** A precise specification of basic and chained HotStuff — the QC abstraction, the `safeNode` voting rule, and the three-chain commit rule — with executable pseudocode in Python, Rust, Haskell, and TLA+.
- **(C2)** A complexity analysis proving $O(n)$ per-view communication and identifying when worst-case $O(n^2)$ behavior resurfaces.
- **(C3)** A comparative study of pacemaker designs — the abstract HotStuff pacemaker, LibraBFT's timeout certificates, Cogsworth [4], and the 2022 RareSync / Lewis–Pye synchronizers [7].
- **(C4)** A survey of empirical results: the original 100-replica deployment, pipelined HotStuff studies, HotStuff-1 [6], and Flow's production pacemaker.
- **(C5)** A critical discussion of limitations: cascading-failure blowup, threshold-setup trust, the synchrony requirement for liveness, and two-phase descendants' trade-offs.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 System model

We consider a fixed set $\Pi$ of $n = 3f+1$ replicas, at most $f$ of them Byzantine. Communication is point-to-point and authenticated. We work in the *partially synchronous* model: there exists an unknown Global Stabilization Time (GST) and a known bound $\Delta$ such that every message sent after GST is delivered within $\Delta$; before GST, delays are unbounded. Safety must hold in all executions; liveness is required only after GST [1].

A *quorum* is any set of $n-f = 2f+1$ replicas. Any two quorums intersect in at least one correct replica — the entire foundation of BFT safety arguments, which HotStuff's proofs use in exactly the classical way. The novelty lies in how quorums are *represented* and *communicated*.

### 2.2 PBFT and the quadratic view-change barrier

PBFT [3] proceeds in views, each with a designated primary, and three normal-case phases — *pre-prepare*, *prepare*, *commit* — each costing $O(n^2)$ messages. The deeper problem is the *view-change protocol*: each replica sends the next primary a `VIEW-CHANGE` carrying its prepared certificates ($O(n)$ authenticators), and the primary broadcasts a `NEW-VIEW` containing $O(n)$ of them — $\Theta(n^3)$ authenticator complexity worst-case. HotStuff's observation: if a quorum of votes compresses into a single signature, the new leader needs only one constant-size certificate.

### 2.3 Threshold signatures

A $(k, n)$-threshold signature scheme lets any $k$ signature *shares* $\sigma_i$ on a message $m$ be combined into a single signature $\sigma$ verifiable under one public key, while fewer than $k$ shares are useless [8]. Pairing-based (BLS-type) constructions make $\sigma$ the same size as an ordinary signature regardless of $k$. HotStuff instantiates this with $k = n - f$: a *quorum certificate* (QC) is

$$\text{QC} = \langle \text{type},\, \text{viewNumber},\, \text{node},\, \sigma \rangle, \qquad \sigma = \textsf{Combine}(\{\sigma_i\}_{i \in Q}),\; |Q| = n - f.$$

Because shares on *distinct* messages cannot be combined, each QC is bound to a specific $(\text{type}, \text{view}, \text{node})$ triple — a fact the safety proof exploits. A replica stores only its highest QC (`highQC`); a new leader needs only the highest QC among $n-f$ `NEW-VIEW` messages to propose safely.

### 2.4 The HotStuff family tree

HotStuff's framework expresses several protocols as parameter choices: Tendermint (quadratic voting), Casper FFG (two-phase checkpoints), *chained* HotStuff (pipelined phases — the LibraBFT/Diem design), *Streamlet* [5] (simplified voting rule), *Fast-HotStuff* (lower latency, fork resilience), *HotStuff-1* [6] (one-phase speculation), and *HotStuff-2* (two phases).

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Protocol specification method

We specify HotStuff in the *generic* form of [1]: messages are `Msg(type, node, justify)` — a tree node (block) extending its parent, plus the QC certifying the parent branch — and replicas vote with `VoteMsg(type, node, partialSig)`. Three predicates govern behavior: `matchingMsg` (well-formed for phase and view), `matchingQC` (certifies a node for phase and view), and `safeNode` (the voting rule). Basic HotStuff, chained HotStuff, and the classical protocols differ only in how phases map onto views; the safety argument is proved once against the predicates.

> **Definition (safeNode).** Let $b$ be the proposed node, $qc$ the QC justifying it, and $b_{\text{lock}}$ the replica's locked node. Then
>
> $$\textsf{safeNode}(b, qc) \;\triangleq\; (b \text{ extends from } b_{\text{lock}}) \;\lor\; (qc.\text{viewNumber} > b_{\text{lock}}.\text{viewNumber}).$$
>
> Intuition: vote for a proposal on the locked branch, or one justified by a QC *newer* than the lock — a newer QC proves a quorum moved on, so the lock is stale.

### 3.2 Analytical method

Our analysis has three layers. **(i) Complexity accounting:** messages and authenticators per phase, per view change, and per commit, across steady-state, single-fault, and cascading-fault regimes. **(ii) Invariant-based safety proofs:** QC uniqueness from quorum intersection, and the locking argument that a committed node can never be contradicted. **(iii) Liveness sketch under partial synchrony:** after GST, a correct pacemaker aligns $n-f$ correct replicas in a view with a correct leader long enough to complete the phases.

### 3.3 Empirical method

We survey published evaluations: the original 100+-replica deployment against BFT-SMaRt [1]; pipelined HotStuff/Streamlet studies [5]; HotStuff-1's speculation mechanism [6]; and production pacemaker parameters from Flow's consensus implementation (truncated exponential backoff with explicit bounds).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Linear communication via threshold-signature quorum certificates

The decisive break from PBFT is representational: proving "a quorum prepared $m$" needs $2f+1$ signed messages in PBFT but one combined signature in HotStuff. The leader collects $n-f$ votes and runs a local combine (Python sketch below); each phase then costs $n-1$ inbound votes and one broadcast — $O(n)$ messages, $O(1)$ authenticators each.

```python
def form_qc(votes: list[Vote], msg_type: str, view: int, node: Block) -> QC:
    """Leader-side QC formation: n-f shares -> one constant-size certificate."""
    shares = [v.partial_sig for v in votes if v.msg == (msg_type, view, node.digest)]
    assert len(shares) >= N - F, "quorum not reached"
    return QC(type=msg_type, view=view, node=node, sig=tbls_combine(shares))

def verify_qc(qc: QC, pk: PublicKey) -> bool:
    return tbls_verify(pk, (qc.type, qc.view, qc.node.digest), qc.sig)
```

Each phase therefore costs $n-1$ inbound votes and one outbound broadcast: $O(n)$ messages, $O(1)$ authenticators each. The *view-change* path is where savings compound: each replica sends the next leader a `NEW-VIEW` containing only its `highQC`; the leader takes the highest-view QC and proposes a node extending it — $O(n)$ messages of $O(1)$ size versus PBFT's $O(n^2)$ of $O(n)$ size. No replica must prove more than its highest QC, because `safeNode` makes it sufficient: any branch a correct replica locked is either extended by the new proposal or superseded by a newer QC.

The following table quantifies the improvement:

| Protocol | Normal-case msgs | Normal-case auth. complexity | View-change msgs | View-change auth. complexity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT [3] | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n^3)$ |
| BFT-SMaRt | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n^2)$ |
| Tendermint | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n^2)$ |
| **HotStuff** [1] | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ |
| Chained HotStuff | $O(n)$/view | $O(n)$/view | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ |

> **Theorem (QC uniqueness).** If $qc_1$ and $qc_2$ are valid QCs with $qc_1.\text{type} = qc_2.\text{type}$ and $qc_1.\text{viewNumber} = qc_2.\text{viewNumber}$, then $qc_1.\text{node} = qc_2.\text{node}$.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Each QC aggregates shares from $n-f$ replicas on the identical message $(\text{type}, \text{view}, \text{node})$. Two quorums of size $n-f$ out of $3f+1$ intersect in at least one *correct* replica, which signs only one node per $(\text{type}, \text{view})$ pair. Hence both QCs certify the same node. ∎

The caveat: threshold setup needs DKG or a trusted dealer, share verification costs $O(n)$ public-key operations per QC, and pairing-based verification is CPU-intensive — the cryptographic tax on linearity, revisited in Section 6.

### 4.2 Chained HotStuff: pipelining and the three-chain commit rule

Basic HotStuff runs three sequential phases per view. *Chained* HotStuff observes that phase $k$ of view $v$ is structurally identical to phase $k+1$ of view $v-1$, so the phases pipeline: the protocol needs **one message type per view**. The leader of view $v$ proposes a block extending the highest QC it knows; the resulting QC serves simultaneously as the *prepare-QC* of view $v$, the *pre-commit-QC* of view $v-1$, and the *commit-QC* of view $v-2*. Leaders rotate every view, giving per-block fairness.

The commit rule becomes structural — the *three-chain rule*: a block $b$ is committed when $b \prec b' \prec b''$ span three consecutive views, each certified by a QC. The Rust sketch below implements the replica's state machine:

```rust
struct Replica {
    high_qc: QC,      // highest QC seen
    locked_qc: QC,    // QC of locked block
    view: u64,
}

impl Replica {
    /// Chained HotStuff voting rule.
    fn vote_rule(&self, proposal: &Block, justify: &QC) -> bool {
        let extends_locked = proposal.extends_from(&self.locked_qc.node);
        let newer_qc = justify.view > self.locked_qc.view;
        proposal.parent == justify.node && (extends_locked || newer_qc)
    }

    fn on_qc(&mut self, qc: &QC) {
        if qc.view > self.high_qc.view { self.high_qc = qc.clone(); }
        // Three-chain commit: b <- b' <- b'' over three consecutive views.
        if let Some((b, bp, bpp)) = three_chain(qc) {
            if bpp.view == bp.view + 1 && bp.view == b.view + 1 {
                self.locked_qc = grandparent_qc(qc); // lock on b'
                commit(b);                            // b is final
            }
        }
    }
}
```

Forks need no extra communication: a withheld QC just creates a gap, and the three-chain rule waits for three *consecutive* certified views. The locked QC guarantees no conflicting block at the locked height can ever gather a newer QC — the same quorum-intersection argument, applied to the pipeline.

A TLA+ fragment captures the commit rule declaratively:
```tla
------------------------------ MODULE ChainedHotStuff ------------------------------
EXTENDS Integers, Sequences
CONSTANTS Replicas, F, Views
VARIABLES blockTree, qcSet, committed

ThreeChain(b1, b2, b3) ==
    /\ b2 \in Children(b1) /\ b3 \in Children(b2)
    /\ \E q1, q2, q3 \in qcSet :
        /\ q1.node = b1 /\ q2.node = b2 /\ q3.node = b3
        /\ q2.view = q1.view + 1 /\ q3.view = q2.view + 1

CommitRule ==
    \E b1, b2, b3 \in blockTree :
        /\ ThreeChain(b1, b2, b3)
        /\ committed' = committed \cup {b1}
        /\ UNCHANGED <<blockTree, qcSet>>
=====================================================================================
```

Pipelining overlaps phases across blocks: one block per view, three-view latency.

### 4.3 The pacemaker: separating view synchronization from the safety core

Safety in HotStuff never depends on timing — a replica votes only when `safeNode` holds. *Liveness* needs enough correct replicas in the same view with a correct leader for long enough to finish the phases. The **pacemaker** provides this as a separate component with a clean contract [1]: **(P1)** after GST, correct replicas eventually overlap in one view with a correct leader long enough; **(P2)** no correct replica is stranded by unbounded view skipping. Implementations:

- **LibraBFT's pacemaker.** On timeout of view $v$, a replica broadcasts a timeout message; $2f+1$ of them form a *timeout certificate* (TC), the replica advances to $v+1$, and the aggregated TC is forwarded to the next leader, which rebroadcasts it so laggards catch up within $2\Delta$.
- **Cogsworth** [4], the first formal treatment: replicas send `WISH` to the target view's leader, which aggregates $f+1$ wishes into a TC, collects $2f+1$ votes into a QC, and multicasts it. Eventual synchronization holds for view duration $\alpha \ge 4\delta$, with *expected* $O(n)$ communication and $O(\delta)$ latency under honest leaders (consecutive Byzantine leaders $X$ are geometric with $\mathbb{E}[X] \approx 3/2$), but expected $O(n^2)$ once a Byzantine leader appears and $O(t \cdot n^2)$ under a cascade of $t$ faults [4].
- **Successors.** A `WISH`-message field prevents Byzantine traffic amplification (expected $O(n)$ even under attack); **RareSync** and **Lewis–Pye** (2022) achieve optimal worst-case bounds — $O(n^2)$ messages per synchronization with $O(n)$ latency — by bundling views into epochs of $f+1$ with Bracha-style coordination only at epoch boundaries [7].
- **Flow's production pacemaker**: timeout objects on stalled rounds, supermajority TCs, and *truncated exponential backoff* tolerating $k=6$ consecutive faulty leaders, clamped between ~1s and ~60s.

The pacemaker also handles *view skipping*: given a QC or TC for view $v$, a lagging replica jumps to $v+1$ — safe because QCs are self-certifying, verifiable without participating in the skipped views.

```haskell
-- Pacemaker view-advance logic (Haskell sketch)
data ViewState = ViewState { curView :: Int, timeout :: Double, consecFails :: Int }

onTimeout :: ViewState -> [TimeoutMsg] -> (ViewState, [Action])
onTimeout st msgs
  | length msgs >= 2*f + 1 =
      let tc  = aggregateTimeouts msgs
          st' = st { curView = tcView tc + 1
                   , timeout = backoff (consecFails st + 1)
                   , consecFails = consecFails st + 1 }
      in (st', [SendNewView (leader (curView st')) tc, BroadcastTC tc])
  | otherwise = (st, [])
  where backoff k = min maxTimeout (baseTimeout * 2^k)
```

### 4.4 Liveness under partial synchrony and optimistic responsiveness

With the pieces in place, liveness is a composition:

1. **After GST**, delays are bounded by $\Delta$, and pacemaker backoff makes view durations eventually long enough for $n-f$ correct replicas to overlap (Cogsworth: $\alpha \ge 4\delta$ [4]).
2. **Rotation** guarantees a correct leader among any $f+1$ consecutive views, so the protocol reaches a synchronized view with a correct leader.
3. **The correct leader** collects $n-f$ `NEW-VIEW`s, extracts the highest QC, and proposes — waiting only for the *first* $n-f$ responses, so progress runs at network speed $\delta$, including across leader replacement: *optimistic responsiveness*.
4. **The voting rule** admits the proposal at $n-f$ correct replicas (highest QC $\ge$ any correct lock, by quorum intersection), so the phases complete and the commit rule fires.

> **Theorem (Liveness sketch).** After GST, if the pacemaker satisfies (P1)–(P2), chained HotStuff commits new blocks infinitely often.
>
> *Proof sketch.* By (P1), infinitely many intervals align $n-f$ correct replicas in a view $v$ with a correct leader for $\ge 3\delta$ after GST. The leader's proposal carries the highest QC among $n-f$ `NEW-VIEW`s — at least as recent as every correct lock, by quorum intersection — so `safeNode` holds everywhere, all three phases collect $n-f$ votes, and the third QC triggers the commit rule. ∎

No bound is claimed on how long after GST progress resumes — that depends on the pacemaker's backoff schedule and the adversary's leader-corruption pattern.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The original 100-replica deployment

The HotStuff paper [1] reports a deployment on 100+ replicas against BFT-SMaRt: throughput and latency are *comparable*, while leader failover shows a *linear* communication footprint versus BFT-SMaRt's quadratic one.

### 5.2 Pipelining studies and Streamlet

The pipelined HotStuff study [5] confirms chaining preserves safety and liveness. Notably, Streamlet's simpler rule ("vote for the longest notarized chain") matches HotStuff in the common case, but HotStuff's explicit three-phase commit is what buys *responsiveness* — Streamlet advances at a fixed $\Delta$ pace. Fast-HotStuff, in the same study, trades small unhappy-path overhead for lower latency and fork resilience, mapping the design space around the three-chain rule.

### 5.3 HotStuff-1: one-phase speculation

HotStuff-1 [6] shows the three-phase structure is not sacred: with *slotting* and one-phase speculation, replicas execute after a single voting round and commit via a prefix rule, recovering through the standard lock on misspeculation. Its pacemaker integration is explicit — `PACEMAKER.COMPLETEDVIEW()` gates view exit — so the phase count is a tunable parameter, not a correctness requirement.

### 5.4 Production evidence: Flow's pacemaker

Flow's consensus documentation provides rare public production parameters: timeout objects on stalled rounds, supermajority TCs, truncated exponential backoff tolerating $k=6$ consecutive faulty leaders, and hard bounds of roughly 1s to 60s. The theory is faithfully reflected: fast in the common case, bounded in the worst case.

### 5.5 Proof summary

| Result | Statement | Technique |
|---|---|---|
| QC uniqueness | Same (type, view) ⇒ same node | Quorum intersection + unforgeability |
| Lock safety | A committed block is never contradicted | `safeNode` + highest-QC argument |
| Three-chain safety | Committed prefix is consistent across views | Induction over consecutive QCs |
| Pacemaker sync (Cogsworth) | Eventual overlap for $\alpha \ge 4\delta$ | WISH/TC/QC relay with $f+1$ leaders |
| Responsiveness | Progress at speed $\delta$, not $\Delta$ | First-$(n-f)$-response advancement |
| Linearity | $O(n)$ per view incl. failover | Threshold-combined QCs, $O(1)$ NEW-VIEW |

## 6 Limitations

**Quadratic worst case under cascading failures.** Linearity holds per view, but $f$ consecutive Byzantine leaders force $f$ view changes — $O(n^2)$ total, with Cogsworth-style synchronization at $O(t \cdot n^2)$ under a cascade of $t$ faults [4]. RareSync and Lewis–Pye [7] bound but do not eliminate the quadratic term.

**Threshold-cryptography setup.** $(n-f, n)$ keys need DKG or a trusted dealer, plus proactive refresh for long-lived deployments. Pairing-based verification is far slower than symmetric authenticators, shifting the bottleneck from bandwidth to CPU at large $n$.

**Liveness needs synchrony.** Nothing is guaranteed before GST, and GST is unknowable — a deployment cannot distinguish "slow network" from "adversarial network." Pacemaker parameters ($\Delta$ estimates, $k$, bounds) are engineering guesses the theory does not validate.

**Latency vs. robustness.** The three-chain rule commits in three views; HotStuff-2 and Fast-HotStuff cut this to two at the cost of responsiveness subtleties or unhappy-path overhead. Frequent rotation makes the known next leader a predictable DoS target.

**Fairness is not guaranteed.** Linearity and responsiveness say nothing about *whose* transactions are included. A Byzantine leader can censor within its view; chain quality [2] needs extra mechanisms (lotteries, inclusion lists) outside HotStuff proper.

## 7 Conclusion

HotStuff's enduring contribution is architectural: it *decomposed* BFT consensus into a timing-independent safety core — generic messages, threshold-signature quorum certificates, and the `safeNode` locking rule — and a timing-dependent liveness module, the pacemaker. QCs buy linearity in steady state and view change; chaining yields a one-message-per-view pipeline with a clean three-chain commit rule; pacemakers from LibraBFT's TC relay to Cogsworth [4] to RareSync [7] progressively tighten synchronization bounds; optimistic responsiveness lets correct leaders advance at network speed.

Empirically — 100+ replicas with linear failover [1], pipelined variants [5], one-phase speculation [6], tuned production pacemakers — the decomposition was right. Open problems: closing the $O(n)$/$O(n^2)$ gap, removing trusted setup from threshold cryptography, and adding fairness guarantees to match the protocol's efficiency. A decade after PBFT made BFT practical, HotStuff made it *linear*.
