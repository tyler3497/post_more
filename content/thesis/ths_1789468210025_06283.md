# HANK Models and Distributional Macroeconomics: Heterogeneous Agents, Precautionary Savings, and Fiscal Multipliers

## Abstract

Heterogeneous-Agent New Keynesian (HANK) models have replaced the representative-agent benchmark as the workhorse of quantitative macroeconomics. This thesis develops the canonical HANK framework — households facing uninsurable idiosyncratic income risk, borrowing constraints, and multiple assets of differing liquidity — and derives its two central results: first, that precautionary savings generate concave consumption functions with high and heterogeneous marginal propensities to consume, including a large mass of wealthy hand-to-mouth households; second, that monetary and fiscal transmission runs predominantly through indirect general-equilibrium channels — labor income, redistribution, and fiscal reaction — rather than intertemporal substitution. We formalize the redistributive transmission of monetary policy through earnings heterogeneity, the Fisher channel, and interest-rate exposure, and show how deficit-financed fiscal shocks command multipliers above one. Finally, we present the sequence-space Jacobian method, whose fake-news matrix algorithm computes first-order aggregate dynamics of high-dimensional heterogeneous-agent blocks orders of magnitude faster than Krusell–Smith-type solvers, making likelihood-based estimation of HANK models practical.

##1 Introduction

For half a century, the transmission of monetary and fiscal policy was studied through the lens of a single representative agent whose consumption is governed by a smooth Euler equation and whose marginal propensity to consume (MPC) is pinned down by the intertemporal elasticity of substitution. That paradigm produced a sharp, testable prediction: monetary policy works primarily through *intertemporal substitution*, as households defer or advance consumption in response to real interest rate movements, and fiscal deficits are largely neutralized by Ricardian households that internalize the government's intertemporal budget constraint.

Micro data demolished this picture. The distribution of MPCs across households is *high and heterogeneous*: many households, including ones with substantial illiquid wealth, spend a large fraction of any windfall income almost immediately, while the average MPC masks enormous cross-sectional dispersion [6]. These empirical regularities reject the representative-agent model outright and demand a framework in which households differ in income, wealth, and liquidity — and in which those differences feed back onto aggregate demand itself.

Heterogeneous-Agent New Keynesian (HANK) models answer that demand. They combine the incomplete-markets tradition of Bewley, Huggett, and Aiyagari — uninsurable idiosyncratic risk, precautionary savings, borrowing limits — with the nominal rigidities and monetary-policy machinery of the New Keynesian (NK) framework [2]. The synthesis delivers a strikingly different account of policy transmission:

1. **Indirect effects dominate.** In HANK, an interest-rate cut stimulates consumption mostly through general-equilibrium channels — higher labor demand and labor income for constrained households — rather than through substitution [2].
2. **Redistribution is a transmission channel.** Because winners and losers from a monetary shock have systematically different MPCs, who gains and who loses mechanically changes aggregate spending [3].
3. **Fiscal policy is powerful.** Deficit-financed government spending has large multipliers because it targets income at exactly the high-MPC households least able to smooth [4].

This thesis develops these results from first principles, formalizes the redistributive channels of monetary policy, and presents the sequence-space Jacobian machinery that has turned HANK from a computational curiosity into an estimable workhorse [1].

##2 Background

### 2.1 The incomplete-markets foundation

The HANK edifice rests on the heterogeneous-agent incomplete-markets models pioneered by Bewley, Huggett, and Aiyagari. In this tradition, a continuum of households faces idiosyncratic labor-income shocks that cannot be insured away — markets are *incomplete* by assumption — and each household self-insures by accumulating a buffer stock of savings subject to a borrowing limit. Optimal behavior generates *precautionary savings*: households hold extra wealth as a cushion against future bad income realizations, and the stationary distribution of wealth emerges endogenously from the interaction of the savings policy function with the income process.

The key theoretical property, proved by Carroll and Kimball, is that the consumption function is *concave* in wealth: the MPC is highest for poor households near the borrowing constraint and declines as wealth rises. This concavity is the microeconomic engine of everything that follows. It implies that the *distribution* of a given income gain across households — not just its total — determines its aggregate spending effect, a point that the representative-agent model assumes away by construction.

### 2.2 From RANK to HANK

The standard Representative Agent New Keynesian (RANK) model features a single infinitely-lived household, complete markets (or equivalently no heterogeneity), Calvo or Rotemberg price stickiness, and a Taylor-rule central bank. Its transmission mechanism is elegant but empirically fragile: consumption responds to real rates through the Euler equation with a coefficient governed by the intertemporal elasticity of substitution, and forward guidance — promises about future rates — is implausibly powerful, a pathology known as the *forward guidance puzzle* [4].

HANK replaces the representative household with a full cross-sectional distribution over assets and income states, while keeping the NK supply side. The distributional state matters for aggregates because of *aggregation through the MPC distribution*: aggregate consumption equals the integral of individual policy functions against the distribution, and that distribution shifts endogenously with policy. The forward guidance puzzle dissolves naturally — constrained households discount the future heavily, so announcements about distant rates barely move their spending — and the model's MPC distribution can be disciplined directly against micro evidence on spending responses [2][7].

### 2.3 The two-asset refinement and the wealthy hand-to-mouth

A one-asset HANK model struggles to match two facts simultaneously: the *mean* MPC in the data (roughly 0.2–0.5 annually) and the observed concentration of wealth. If the single asset is liquid, too few households are constrained; if illiquid, the model understates the MPC of the wealthy. Kaplan, Moll, and Violante [2] resolve this tension with *two assets*: a liquid asset with low return that can be adjusted frictionlessly, and an illiquid asset with a higher return that can be adjusted only by paying a transaction cost.

The two-asset model generates the empirically crucial category of the *wealthy hand-to-mouth*: households with substantial net worth locked in illiquid assets (typically housing or retirement accounts) but near-zero liquid balances, whose consumption responds to income shocks almost one-for-one. In calibrated versions, roughly one-third of households are hand-to-mouth, and about two-thirds of those are wealthy rather than poor — a fact that one-asset models cannot produce and that proves decisive for the size of fiscal multipliers [2][4].

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 The household problem

We study the canonical discrete-time formulation. Each household is indexed by liquid assets *a*, illiquid assets *b*, and idiosyncratic productivity *z*, solving:

```haskell
-- Recursive formulation of the two-asset household problem
type Assets   = (Liquid, Illiquid)
type State    = (Assets, Productivity)

householdBellman :: Params -> State -> Value

householdBellman p (a, b, z) =
  maximum [ utility c l p + beta * expectedValue p a' b' z'
           | c    <- feasibleConsumption
           , l    <- laborSupply p z
           , a'   <- liquidGrid p, a' >= borrowingLimit p
           , b'   <- illiquidGrid p
           , let budget = (1 + rLiquid p) * a
                        + (1 + rIlliquid p) * b
                        + wage p * z * l - c
                        - adjCost p b b'
           , budget == a' + b' ]
```

Formally, the value function satisfies

> **Theorem:** (Concavity of the consumption function) Under standard regularity conditions — CRRA preferences, a Markov income process with bounded support, and a borrowing limit — the optimal consumption policy *c(a, b, z)* is strictly increasing and strictly concave in liquid wealth *a*. Consequently the MPC out of a transitory income shock, ∂c/∂y, is decreasing in *a* and attains its maximum at the borrowing constraint.

This theorem is the workhorse result. Its proof combines the concavity of the value function (by induction on the Bellman operator) with the envelope theorem; the borrowing constraint binds for a positive-measure set of households in any calibrated stationary equilibrium, producing the mass of hand-to-mouth consumers.

### 3.2 Equilibrium and the distributional state

The aggregate state of a HANK economy is infinite-dimensional: it includes the entire joint distribution μ over (a, b, z). Firms are standard NK price-setters facing Calvo frictions; the government levies taxes, issues debt, and may engage in deficit spending; the central bank follows a Taylor rule. A recursive equilibrium consists of household policy functions, a law of motion for μ induced by those policies, and price sequences such that all markets clear.

Solving such models with aggregate shocks was long considered infeasible — the classic Krusell–Smith approach approximates the distribution by a few moments and iterates on approximate aggregation. The methodological breakthrough of the last decade is the **sequence-space** representation, which we develop in Section 4.5 following [1].

### 3.3 Calibration strategy

Quantitative HANK models are calibrated in two stages. The *steady state* targets micro moments: the wealth distribution (Gini coefficients for liquid and illiquid wealth), the fraction of hand-to-mouth households, the average MPC, and the income process estimated from panel data with leptokurtic (fat-tailed) income changes [2]. The *dynamics* are then computed by linearization around this steady state — the sequence-space Jacobian method computes exact first-order responses of the nonlinear heterogeneous-agent block without any approximate aggregation [1].

| Moment | Data target | One-asset HANK | Two-asset HANK |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mean annual MPC | 0.2 – 0.5 | ≈ 0.05 | ≈ 0.25 |
| Share hand-to-mouth | ≈ 1/3 | ≈ 0.10 | ≈ 0.33 |
| Wealthy hand-to-mouth share of HtM | ≈ 2/3 | 0 | ≈ 0.66 |
| Liquid wealth Gini | ≈ 0.85 | ≈ 0.60 | ≈ 0.85 |

The table summarizes why the two-asset structure is now standard: only it reproduces the joint distribution of MPCs and wealth that the transmission results depend on [2].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Precautionary savings and the geometry of the consumption function

Precautionary savings arise because uninsurable risk makes the marginal utility of future consumption convex in wealth (prudence, u''' > 0). Households therefore save *more* than the certainty-equivalent benchmark, and the strength of the motive is greatest where the constraint threatens — at low wealth. The resulting consumption function is steeply sloped near the borrowing limit and flattens as wealth grows; its curvature is what makes aggregate demand depend on the wealth distribution.

Two consequences follow.

### 4.2 Direct versus indirect transmission of monetary policy

Kaplan, Moll, and Violante [2] decompose the consumption response to an interest-rate cut into *direct* and *indirect* effects.

- **Intertemporal substitution**: lower real rates make current consumption cheaper relative to future consumption (the RANK channel).
- **Income and wealth effects** from changed returns on savings.

Indirect effects operate through general equilibrium:

- **Labor demand**: the rate cut stimulates investment and hiring, raising labor income — which flows disproportionately to high-MPC households.
- **Fiscal response**: the government's debt-service costs fall, and how the resulting fiscal space is used (transfers vs. debt reduction) materially changes the total response because Ricardian equivalence fails [2].
- **Asset prices**: higher equity and house prices raise wealth, but the spending response is muted because the beneficiaries have low MPCs.

The quantitative finding is stark: in the calibrated two-asset HANK model, indirect effects account for the *overwhelming majority* of the consumption response, while intertemporal substitution — the entire transmission mechanism of RANK — is a minor contributor [2].

### 4.3 The redistributive channels: earnings heterogeneity, Fisher, and interest-rate exposure

Auclert [3] refines the transmission decomposition by asking a sharper question: when monetary policy creates winners and losers, how does the *covariance* between gains and MPCs change aggregate spending?

1. **Earnings heterogeneity channel.** An expansion raises aggregate labor income, but the gains are unevenly distributed — low-income workers' earnings respond most elastically to demand. Because these workers have the highest MPCs, the covariance between income gains and MPCs is positive, amplifying the stimulus [3].
2. **Fisher channel.** Unexpected inflation redistributes real wealth from nominal lenders to nominal borrowers. Borrowers are disproportionately constrained with high MPCs; lenders are wealthy with low MPCs. The redistribution therefore raises aggregate consumption, with magnitude governed by the economy's unhedged nominal positions [3].
3. **Interest-rate exposure channel.** A rate change alters the net interest income of households according to their *unhedged interest-rate exposure* (URE) — maturing assets minus maturing liabilities, adjusted for planned consumption. Households with negative URE (net borrowers rolling over debt) gain from a cut and spend a large share of the gain [3].

Using Italian and U.S.

```rust
// Computing unhedged interest-rate exposure across the distribution
struct Household { assets: f64, liabilities: f64, mpc: f64 }

fn aggregate_ure_channel(households: &[Household], dr: f64) -> f64 {
    // URE_i = maturing assets - maturing liabilities - planned consumption
    // dC_i = MPC_i * URE_i * dr  (first-order, Auclert 2019)
    households.iter()
        .map(|h| {
            let ure = h.assets - h.liabilities; // simplified: net nominal position
            h.mpc * ure * dr
        })
        .sum()
}
```

### 4.4 Fiscal multipliers under heterogeneous MPCs

The same MPC heterogeneity that reshapes monetary transmission revolutionizes fiscal analysis.

The survey in [4] reports that deficit-financed fiscal shocks in calibrated HANK models produce cumulative multipliers substantially above one, with the excess over RANK concentrated in the response of hand-to-mouth households.

### 4.5 Sequence-space Jacobians and the fake-news matrix

The computational heart of modern HANK is the sequence-space method of Auclert, Bardóczy, Rognlie, and Straub [1].

```python
import numpy as np

def fake_news_jacobian(policy, dist, T):
    """Compute the sequence-space Jacobian via the fake-news algorithm.
    
    One forward pass with 'fake news' (an anticipated one-period shock at
    date s, revealed to be false) traces the full impulse response;
    shifting gives all columns of the Jacobian in O(T) model solves.
    """
    J = np.zeros((T, T))
    for s in range(T):
        # agents learn at date 0 of a shock at date s; news is fake
        dC = impulse_response_to_fake_news(policy, dist, shock_date=s, T=T)
        J[:, s] = dC  # column s: consumption response to date-s shock
    return J

def general_equilibrium(J_hh, J_nk, shock):
    # Compose heterogeneous-agent and New Keynesian blocks by matrix ops
    H = J_hh @ J_nk          # total derivative through GE feedback
    return np.linalg.solve(np.eye(H.shape[0]) - H, shock)
```

The *fake-news matrix* trick is what makes this fast: because the household problem is linearized around the steady state, the response to a shock at date *s* announced at date 0 can be computed in a single backward-forward pass, and all T columns of the Jacobian follow from T such passes — each roughly as cheap as one steady-state solve [1].

![Sequence-space Jacobian pipeline](/thesis/ths_1789468210025_06283-2.webp)

The method's generality is its strength: any heterogeneous-agent block — one or two assets, with or without aggregate risk — that can be written as a sequence mapping admits the same treatment, and nonlinear perfect-foresight transitions follow by Newton iteration on the stacked sequence equations [1].

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Impulse responses: HANK versus RANK

The signature quantitative experiment [2] subjects the calibrated two-asset HANK model and a comparable RANK model to an unexpected 100-basis-point monetary easing. The results are summarized below (peak responses, percent deviations):

| Variable | RANK | One-asset HANK | Two-asset HANK |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumption (peak, %) | 0.35 | 0.55 | 0.85 |
| Share via indirect channels | < 0.10 | ≈ 0.60 | ≈ 0.80 |
| Output multiplier, deficit-financed fiscal shock | ≈ 0.7 | ≈ 1.1 | ≈ 1.4 |
| Forward-guidance puzzle (5yr-ahead news shock) | Severe | Attenuated | Resolved |

Three facts stand out. First, HANK *amplifies* the consumption response to monetary shocks through the indirect labor-income channel [2]. Second, the fiscal multiplier rises monotonically with the model's MPC heterogeneity, exceeding one only when wealthy hand-to-mouth households are present [4]. Third, forward guidance about the distant future loses its counterfactual power because constrained households effectively discount the future at rates far above the market rate — the puzzle dissolves without any ad hoc discounting [4].

### 5.2 Micro evidence on MPCs and the sufficient-statistic logic

The model's discipline comes from micro data. Lottery winnings, tax rebates, and stimulus payments all reveal the same pattern: spending responses are large on impact and strongly decreasing in liquid wealth, with a fat tail of near-one MPCs among the constrained [6]. Auclert's sufficient-statistic approach [3] goes further, mapping the three redistributive channels to measurable covariances — between earnings cyclicality and MPCs, between net nominal positions and MPCs, and between interest-rate exposures and MPCs — and showing that Italian and U.S. data imply amplification through all three.

> **Theorem:** (Sufficient statistics for redistribution) To first order, the aggregate consumption response to a monetary shock equals the RANK response plus three covariance terms: Cov(MPC, earnings gains) + Cov(MPC, Fisher transfers) + Cov(MPC, URE × rate change). Each term is identified from cross-sectional micro data without solving the full model [3].

This theorem is methodologically liberating: it lets empiricists quantify HANK mechanisms with household survey and administrative data alone, and it gives theorists sharp calibration targets [7].

### 5.3 Estimation and the ZLB frontier

Because the sequence-space linearization is an ordinary linear system, HANK models can now be estimated by full-information likelihood methods — a task that was infeasible under Krusell–Smith simulation [1]. Recent work pushes beyond linearity: Lin and Peruffo [5] combine the sequence-space Jacobian with a two-regime shock structure to solve HANK models with aggregate uncertainty and an occasionally binding zero lower bound, finding that uncertainty amplifies downturns at the ZLB and that household heterogeneity strengthens the amplification — while also making forward guidance *more* powerful when uncertainty is present, a subtle interaction invisible to RANK [5].

A minimal TLA+ sketch of the transmission protocol — useful for model-checking the timing of direct versus indirect effects — illustrates the block structure:

```tla+
---------------------------- MODULE HankTransmission ----------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals
VARIABLES shock, rates, income, consumption, step

Init == /\ shock = 0
        /\ rates = 0
        /\ income = 0
        /\ consumption = 0
        /\ step = "idle"

DirectEffect == /\ step = "idle"
                /\ shock' = 1
                /\ rates' = -1            \* central bank cuts
                /\ consumption' = consumption + 1   \* substitution
                /\ step' = "direct"

IndirectEffect == /\ step = "direct"
                  /\ income' = income + 3   \* labor demand boom
                  /\ consumption' = consumption + 6 \* high-MPC spending
                  /\ step' = "indirect"

Next == DirectEffect \/ IndirectEffect
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<shock, rates, income, consumption, step>>
=============================================================================
```

##6 Limitations

The HANK research program, for all its successes, faces serious limitations that active research is only beginning to address.

- **Linearization around the steady state.** The sequence-space method [1] is exact only to first order. Large shocks, precautionary *re-pricing* of risk, and state-dependent MPCs (the distribution itself moves a lot in deep recessions) all lie outside its scope; fully nonlinear solution with aggregate shocks remains computationally punishing.
- **The two-asset calibration is fragile.** Matching the wealthy hand-to-mouth share requires careful tuning of transaction costs and return wedges; small changes in the illiquid return or the adjustment cost can swing the aggregate MPC substantially [2].
- **Labor-market and firm heterogeneity are thin.** Most HANK models keep a representative firm and a reduced-form earnings process; the interaction of household heterogeneity with firm heterogeneity, endogenous separations, and frictional labor markets is still underdeveloped [4].
- **The ZLB and uncertainty.** Handling occasionally binding constraints with aggregate risk requires approximations (e.g., the two-regime structure of [5]) whose accuracy in severe crises is not fully established.
- **Expectations.** The framework typically assumes rational expectations over aggregates; bounded rationality or learning would change both the direct and indirect channels, and the sufficient-statistic results [3] are first-order only.

##7 Conclusion

HANK models have rewritten the two central propositions of stabilization policy. *Monetary policy* works less through intertemporal substitution than through indirect, general-equilibrium, and redistributive channels — labor income, the Fisher revaluation of nominal positions, and interest-rate exposures interacted with heterogeneous MPCs [2][3]. *Fiscal policy*, freed from Ricardian equivalence by borrowing constraints, commands multipliers above one precisely when it reaches the constrained [4]. And *computation*, via the sequence-space Jacobian and its fake-news algorithm, has caught up with theory: high-dimensional heterogeneous-agent blocks can now be solved, composed, and estimated almost as easily as representative-agent systems [1].

The frontier is clear. Nonlinear methods that preserve the distributional state through large shocks, richer firm and labor-market heterogeneity, and serious estimation confronting both micro and macro data simultaneously [5][7] — these are the steps that will turn HANK from the best available account of demand-side transmission into a genuinely quantitative policy tool. The distribution is no longer a detail to be averaged away; it *is* the transmission mechanism.
