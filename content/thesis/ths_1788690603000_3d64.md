# From Quadratic to Linear: HotStuff's Pipelined BFT Consensus and the Narwhal/Tusk Separation of Dissemination from Ordering

## 1. Introduction

Byzantine fault-tolerant (BFT) state machine replication is the foundation of modern permissioned and permissionless blockchain systems, yet for two decades after the seminal practical Byzantine fault tolerance (PBFT) protocol, the field wrestled with a painful tension between **simplicity, communication complexity, and performance under faults**. PBFT and its descendants achieved consensus in a handful of communication rounds under partial synchrony, but their all-to-all broadcast patterns imposed quadratic message complexity — a cost that materialized most brutally not in the common case, but during *view changes*, precisely the moments when the system is under stress [1].

The 2019 introduction of HotStuff — first as a research paper by Yin, Malkhi, Reiter, Gueta, and Abraham, then as the consensus core of Facebook's Libra/Diem project — reframed the problem. HotStuff demonstrated that a *linear* protocol with *responsiveness* (consensus advancing at the speed of the actual network rather than a conservative timeout) was achievable in the partially synchronous model, and, crucially, that its three-phase structure could be **pipelined**, chaining phases across successive views so that the amortized cost of each consensus decision collapsed toward a single round trip [1].

This thesis argues that HotStuff's true significance is twofold. First, its introduction of *threshold-signature quorum certificates* and a unified *generic-QC* abstraction tamed the infamous complexity of view-change logic, making BFT protocols amenable to engineering at scale. Second — and perhaps more consequentially — HotStuff's clean architecture enabled a radical architectural split that arrived three years later: **Narwhal**, a DAG-based mempool that separates *reliable dissemination* from *ordering*, and **Tusk**, a zero-message-overhead asynchronous consensus protocol that interprets the DAG directly [3]. The composition of Narwhal with consensus protocols (Narwhal-HotStuff, Tusk, Bullshark) delivered order-of-magnitude throughput improvements and now powers production deployments at Aptos and Sui.

We trace this lineage systematically: the PBFT baseline and its quadratic bottleneck, HotStuff's rotating-leader pipelined protocol and threshold-signature mechanics, the responsiveness-versus-synchrony distinction, and the Narwhal/Tusk DAG revolution with its Bullshark refinement — concluding with latency/throughput analysis, production deployments, and open limitations.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Byzantine Model and Partial Synchrony

We consider the classical SMR setting: *n* replicas, at most *f* Byzantine, with *n = 3f + 1* minimal under partial synchrony — an unknown global stabilization time (GST) after which message delays are bounded by Δ. A protocol is *responsive* if, after GST, a correct leader drives consensus at the pace of *actual* message delivery rather than Δ — crucial in practice, since Δ must be provisioned conservatively for WANs.

### 2.2 PBFT: The Two-Phase Baseline and Its Quadratic Core

PBFT (Castro and Liskov, OSDI 1999) organizes consensus around a *stable leader* and a *view-change* sub-protocol for leader replacement. Its steady-state path is elegant: a leader broadcasts a pre-prepare; replicas all-to-all broadcast prepares; then all-to-all broadcast commits. A replica commits upon receiving 2f+1 matching prepares and 2f+1 commits — consensus in two rounds of all-to-all exchange, at O(n²) messages per decision.

The deep structural problem lies in the *view change*. When the leader is suspected faulty, replicas broadcast view-change messages carrying their highest prepared certificates to the new leader, which relays an O(n)-sized new-view message. View-change complexity is therefore **O(n²)** even with authenticator optimization — and it strikes precisely when the system is already degraded.

| Protocol aspect | PBFT | HotStuff (chained) |
|---|---|---|
| Steady-state messages per decision | O(n²) | O(n) |
| View-change communication | O(n²) (authenticators) | O(n) |
| Rounds per decision (steady state) | 3 | 3 (pipelined: 1 amortized) |
| Responsiveness | Yes (steady state) | Yes |
| Leader rotation | Per view, expensive | Per view, cheap (chain) |

> **Theorem:** (Communication lower bound for BFT agreement) Any deterministic partially synchronous BFT agreement protocol with optimal resilience n = 3f + 1 requires Ω(n²) total communication in the worst case across views; however, the *amortized* per-decision cost of the steady state can be made linear. HotStuff achieves O(n) per view and O(n) per decision in the steady state.

The critical insight is that *worst-case* lower bounds do not forbid *steady-state linearity*: a protocol can exchange linearly many messages per consensus instance as long as the cost of recovering from leader failure is itself linear, and leaders rotate cheaply enough that a faulty leader costs only one linear round.

### 2.3 Threshold Signatures and Quorum Certificates

A *quorum certificate* (QC) is evidence that 2f + 1 replicas voted for a proposal. In PBFT, QCs are *aggregates of signatures* — O(n) data structures replicated everywhere. HotStuff's decisive engineering choice is to use **threshold signatures** (notably BLS): a QC is a *single* constant-sized signature that any party can verify, formed by combining 2f + 1 partial signatures. This transforms QCs from heavy payloads into lightweight pointers that can be embedded in proposals, stored in replicas' local state, and relayed during view changes at negligible cost.

Formally, a *(k, n)*-threshold signature scheme yields a combined signature σ on message *m* verifiable against the aggregate public key iff at least *k* distinct signers contributed. With *k = 2f + 1*, two quorums intersect in at least one correct replica — the **quorum intersection** property underpinning all safety arguments.

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is grounded in a comparative-analytic methodology across four dimensions:

1. **Protocol specification analysis.** We reconstruct HotStuff's three-phase protocol and its chained variant from the published specifications [1], formalizing the locking and voting rules in an executable Python sketch.
2. **Asymptotic communication accounting.** We derive message complexity for PBFT, HotStuff, Narwhal-HotStuff, Tusk, and Bullshark in the steady state and under view change [1][3][4][5].
3. **Empirical survey.** We synthesize published WAN evaluation results — throughput, latency, behavior under crash faults — and contextualize them against production deployments at Aptos (AptosBFT, Jolteon, Quorum Store, Raptr) and Sui (Narwhal/Bullshark) [8][9][10].
4. **Formal safety/liveness argument.** We present the canonical locking and QC-intersection argument for HotStuff safety and the pacemaker-driven liveness argument, extending the reasoning to the DAG interpretation layer (Tusk, Bullshark, DAG-Rider) [6].

### 3.1 The HotStuff Core Protocol

HotStuff structures consensus around *views*, each with a designated rotating leader. Every view executes three phases — *prepare*, *pre-commit*, *commit* — each terminating in a QC:

```python
# HotStuff core: replica state and voting rule (simplified)
class Replica:
    def __init__(self):
        self.generic_qc = GENESIS_QC   # highest QC seen
        self.locked_qc = GENESIS_QC    # highest QC locked
        self.last_voted_view = 0

    def on_proposal(self, node, qc):
        # Safety rule: vote iff proposal extends locked QC
        # OR its QC is newer than locked QC (liveness unlock)
        if (node.parent == self.locked_qc.node
                or qc.view > self.locked_qc.view):
            self.generic_qc = max(self.generic_qc, qc)
            self.last_voted_view = node.view
            send_vote(partial_sign(node), view_leader(node.view + 1))
        # Commit rule: three chained QCs => decide
        if three_chain(qc):   # prepareQC -> precommitQC -> commitQC
            execute(node.parent.parent)
```

The three-phase structure exists for one reason: it makes the *view-change* trivial. In HotStuff, every proposal simply carries the leader's *highest known generic QC*; replicas' voting rule — vote if the proposal extends the locked QC or carries a newer QC — guarantees safety *without any per-replica state collection*. The new leader broadcasts one message; replicas reply with votes; the leader aggregates a QC. **View change is linear and structurally identical to the steady state.**

### 3.2 Chaining and Pipelining

Because each phase's output (a QC) is exactly the next phase's input, phases can be *chained* across views: the prepare phase of view *v + 1* simultaneously serves as the pre-commit phase of view *v*'s proposal and the commit phase of view *v − 1*'s. With rotating leaders — a different leader per view — the protocol streams one decision per round trip in the steady state:

```
View v:     Leader_v  broadcasts B_v extending QC(B_{v-1})
View v+1:   Leader_{v+1} broadcasts B_{v+1} extending QC(B_v)   -- precommit(B_v) & prepare(B_{v+1})
View v+2:   Leader_{v+2} broadcasts B_{v+2} extending QC(B_{v+1}) -- commit(B_v) locked; B_{v+1} precommitted
View v+3:   ... B_v becomes executable (decided) upon three-chain
```

The three-chain rule — `prepareQC → precommitQC → commitQC` over consecutive nodes — is the commit condition. Responsiveness is preserved because no phase waits on Δ; each phase advances on receiving 2f + 1 votes, at actual network speed.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Threshold Signatures as the Complexity Lever

The linear complexity claim rests entirely on threshold signatures. Without them, QC verification at every replica remains O(n) per QC. BLS threshold signatures compress a QC to a constant ~96 bytes — not merely an optimization but the *enabling* technology of the generic-QC abstraction: only when QCs are cheap pointers can "the highest QC" be treated as trivially transmissible state [1]. The cost is cryptographic: BLS pairing operations are far slower than EdDSA verification, and distributed key generation adds deployment complexity. Production systems accept this trade, since signature verification parallelizes cleanly while quadratic message amplification does not.

### 4.2 Responsiveness versus Synchrony: The Pacemaker Decoupling

HotStuff's architecture explicitly *decouples safety from liveness*. Safety is a pure function of QCs and the voting rule — it holds regardless of timing. Liveness is delegated to a **pacemaker**: a view-synchronization gadget that advances views on timeouts, ensuring that after GST all correct replicas overlap in the same view with a correct leader long enough to form QCs [1]. The libhotstuff reference implementation makes this decoupling literal: the consensus core exposes a `PaceMaker` interface, and liveness heuristics (even proof-of-work-based leader election for hybrid systems) can be swapped without touching safety logic [12].

This decoupling resolves a deep tension. PBFT's view-change intertwines safety state collection with timing-based leader suspicion, making the code famously bug-prone. HotStuff's pacemaker only needs to *synchronize views*; the safety rule handles the rest.

### 4.3 Narwhal: Separating Dissemination from Ordering

The Narwhal insight (Danezis, Kokoris-Kogias, Sonnino, Spiegelman, 2021/2022) is architectural: *consensus protocols conflate two distinct tasks* — (a) reliably disseminating transactions so every correct replica eventually holds them, and (b) ordering them into a single total order [3]. When the leader must both disseminate bulk transaction data *and* drive ordering, the leader becomes a bandwidth bottleneck: HotStuff's own evaluation showed the leader's egress saturating at ~1,800 tx/s in a WAN deployment, while replicas' ingress sat largely idle.

Narwhal builds a **round-based DAG**: each validator reliably broadcasts one vertex per round, each vertex containing transaction batches plus references to at least 2f + 1 vertices of the previous round (strong edges), forming a *causal history*. Round advancement is driven by a Byzantine fault-tolerant threshold clock. Critically, Narwhal is **asynchronous** — it never blocks on timeouts — and provides *reliable dissemination with storage*: once a vertex is delivered, its causal history is available to all correct validators.

The mempool then exposes this DAG to any consensus layer. **Narwhal-HotStuff** orders *digests of DAG vertices* (tiny), while bulk data rides the DAG — WAN evaluation: **170,000 tx/s at 2.5 s latency** versus 1,800 tx/s for vanilla HotStuff [3]. And because dissemination is leaderless, each validator can run *multiple workers* in parallel, scaling throughput linearly — to **600,000 tx/s** — with *no latency increase* [3].

```
Round r:    V1[r] ──╲
                  ╲──> V2[r+1] ──╲
Round r+1:  V2[r] ──╱              ╲──> V3[r+2]  ...  (each vertex references 2f+1 prior vertices)
            V3[r] ──╲              ╱
Round r+2:  V1[r+1] ╱──> ...
```

### 4.4 Tusk, DAG-Rider, and Bullshark: Ordering Without Messages

Given a Narwhal DAG, *ordering* can be extracted with **zero additional messages**: each validator interprets its local DAG view identically, so if the interpretation rule is deterministic and all correct validators eventually share the same DAG prefix, they commit the same order. This is the lineage from DAG-Rider [6] through Tusk [3] to Bullshark [4][5]:

1. **DAG-Rider** (Keidar et al., 2021): the theoretical foundation — a two-layer protocol (communication via reliable broadcast + ordering via a global perfect coin implemented with threshold signatures) achieving asynchronous Byzantine atomic broadcast. Waves of 4 DAG rounds; leaders elected by the common coin; a leader is committed if 2f + 1 round-*w+1* vertices reference it [6].
2. **Tusk**: the practical refinement — quorum-based (rather than full) reliable broadcast, tighter commit rules for the common case, and *removal of weak links* to enable garbage collection. Tusk achieves **140,000 tx/s at ~4 s latency — 20× the state-of-the-art asynchronous protocol** at the time — and maintains throughput under faults where HotStuff's view-changes inject latency spikes [3].
3. **Bullshark**: the partially synchronous variant. Where Tusk's common-coin election costs rounds of latency, Bullshark uses *pre-defined steady-state leaders* for a 2-round fast path, with fallback leaders preserving liveness under asynchrony. The simplified Bullshark variant (Spiegelman et al., 2022) is what Sui runs in production today [5][8].

### 4.5 Latency–Throughput Analysis

The empirical picture, synthesized from the HotStuff, Narwhal/Tusk, and Bullshark evaluations:

| Configuration (WAN, n≈10–50) | Throughput | Latency | Fault behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT-style (BFT-SMaRt baseline) | ~1–2k tx/s | ~1 s | Degrades sharply; view-change quadratic |
| HotStuff (chained) | ~1.8k tx/s | ~1 s | Linear view change; throughput dips under faults |
| Narwhal-HotStuff | 170k tx/s | 2.5 s | Throughput holds; latency rises on view change |
| Narwhal-HotStuff (multi-worker) | 600k tx/s | 2.5 s | Linear scale-out |
| Tusk | 140k tx/s | ~4 s | Throughput holds under faults |
| Bullshark | >Tusk in steady state | ~2 s (fast path) | Partial-synchrony fast path |

Two structural observations emerge. *First*, throughput is dominated by dissemination, not ordering: any protocol that makes the leader re-broadcast bulk data caps out at leader egress bandwidth. *Second*, latency is dominated by sequential round trips before commit — so the DAG family trades constant-factor latency for two orders of magnitude of throughput, the correct trade for blockchains, where batching amortizes latency across thousands of transactions per commit.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Safety: The Locking Argument

> **Theorem:** (HotStuff Safety) If a correct replica commits node *B* in view *v* (three-chain), then no conflicting node *B′* can ever be committed by any correct replica.

*Proof sketch.* Committing *B* requires a commitQC over the three-chain. Any correct replica's locked QC is at least as recent as the precommitQC of *B*. Suppose a conflicting *B′* were committed with a prepareQC formed in view *w′*. If *w′* precedes the commit view, quorum intersection yields a correct replica that voted for both *B*'s chain and *B′* — impossible under the voting rule. If *w′* is later, the leader's highest-QC proposal must extend *B*'s chain, forcing *B′* to extend *B*. ∎

The argument's elegance is its *locality*: safety depends only on QC freshness, never on collecting replicas' private state.

### 5.2 Liveness: The Pacemaker Argument

> **Theorem:** (HotStuff Liveness) After GST, with a correct pacemaker, consensus decisions are reached continuously.

After GST, the pacemaker keeps all correct replicas overlapped in views with correct leaders. A correct leader proposes extending the highest QC; the *unlock* clause of the voting rule (vote if the proposal's QC is newer than the locked QC) ensures replicas locked on stale state rejoin. Each view adds one chain link; three consecutive correct-leader views commit a decision [1].

### 5.3 DAG Interpretation: Safety of Tusk/Bullshark

The DAG protocols' safety reduces to a single invariant: *all correct validators eventually observe the same DAG*, because every vertex's causal history is delivered by reliable broadcast before interpretation. The ordering function is a deterministic function of the local DAG; therefore all correct validators commit identical sequences. Liveness follows from the common coin (Tusk, DAG-Rider) or the partial-synchrony fast path with fallback leaders (Bullshark): waves advance because rounds advance by threshold clock, and leaders are committed infinitely often [3][4][6].

### 5.4 Production Evidence

- **Aptos.** AptosBFT evolved from DiemBFT (HotStuff-derived); AptosBFTv4 adopted **Jolteon** (a PBFT-style two-phase fast path cutting latency ~50%), and the v1.5 upgrade added **Quorum Store** — Aptos's Narwhal implementation — eliminating the mempool/consensus double-broadcast bottleneck [9]. Current-generation **Raptr/VelociRaptr** introduces *prefix consensus*: validators vote on batch references, and overlapping votes finalize a common prefix immediately [10].
- **Sui.** Sui's consensus stack was built directly on the Narwhal codebase, running Tusk and then the simplified **Bullshark** variant in production; its 2022 open-sourcing included benchmarks showing ~46k tx/s end-to-end at sub-second latency on modest committees [8].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Censorship and fairness.** Rotating leaders and DAG mempools improve *throughput* fairness, but ordering-layer fairness remains weak: a leader can still reorder or delay transactions within its committed prefix. Dedicated order-fairness protocols (Aequitas, Wendy) remain largely undeployed.
2. **Worst-case latency of pipelining.** Chaining amortizes latency in the steady state, but a single faulty leader breaks the chain: the three-chain commit rule means one bad view costs *three* good views before the next commit. Bullshark's fast path mitigates this; Tusk's asynchronous waves accept it.
3. **Threshold-signature trusted setup.** BLS threshold schemes require distributed key generation or a trusted dealer; key resharing under validator-set churn is operationally painful and remains a deployment tax on every HotStuff descendant.
4. **DAG storage and garbage collection.** Narwhal's DAG grows unboundedly; garbage collection requires agreement on which rounds are committed — Tusk's removal of weak links was motivated precisely by making GC tractable, and aggressive GC interacts subtly with slow validators' ability to catch up.
5. **Responsiveness ≠ low latency, and DAG logic is subtle.** Responsiveness guarantees progress at *network speed*, but WAN timeouts and multi-round waves still yield multi-second finality; sub-second finality demands further protocol surgery. Meanwhile, the "zero message overhead" claim covers only the network — Bullshark's wave leaders, vote types, and direct/indirect commit rules are notoriously subtle, and liveness proofs for newer DAG protocols have faced published criticism [7].

## 7. Conclusion

The arc from PBFT to Bullshark is the story of a single bottleneck — quadratic communication — attacked at successively deeper layers. PBFT paid it in view changes. HotStuff eliminated it with threshold-signature QCs, a three-phase structure making view changes linear, and pipelining amortizing each decision to a round trip [1]. Narwhal then observed the leader was still a *bandwidth* bottleneck and split the architecture in two: asynchronous, leaderless dissemination over a DAG, with ordering layered on top [3]. Tusk, DAG-Rider, and Bullshark showed ordering could be extracted from the DAG with no additional messages — hundred-fold throughput at constant-factor latency cost [4][5][6].

Production validates the theory: Aptos's Quorum Store and Sui's Bullshark are Narwhal's direct descendants, sustaining throughput regimes the PBFT generation could not approach. Yet the frontier has moved, not closed: censorship resistance, order fairness, sub-second finality, and verification of intricate DAG ordering rules remain open. The lasting contribution may be methodological — the separation of dissemination from ordering, and of safety from liveness, are design principles that will outlive any single protocol.
