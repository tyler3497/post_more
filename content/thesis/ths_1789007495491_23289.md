# Hindley–Milner Type Inference: Algorithm W, Principal Types, Unification, and the Engineering of Let-Polymorphism

## Abstract

The Hindley–Milner (HM) type system — also called Damas–Milner — is the most influential complete type-inference discipline ever deployed: it recovers the *most general* (principal) type of every typable expression in a polymorphic lambda calculus without requiring a single annotation. This thesis reconstructs HM from first principles. We present the declarative typing rules and the algorithmic heart, **Algorithm W** [1][2], with its mutual recursion between inference and first-order unification; we prove, following Damas, the soundness and completeness theorems that make W *complete*: whenever an expression is typable, W computes a principal type-scheme. We develop unification theory — the Robinson-style solver, the occurs check, termination measures, and almost-linear complexity under structure sharing — and analyze the central design decision of let-polymorphism: the generalization of `let`-bound definitions and its interaction with mutable references, which forced the **value restriction** [4][6]. We then survey the modern frontier where HM breaks: GADTs and type families introduce *local* type assumptions and destroy principality, motivating the constraint-based OutsideIn(X) reformulation [3]; and substitution-based W's traversal-order bias yields the notoriously poor error messages studied by Heeren et al. [5]. Empirical results on complexity bounds, error-localization heuristics, and case studies in OCaml, Haskell, and SML ground the theory.

## 1. Introduction

The central problem of static typing for functional languages is *reconstruction*: given a program with no type information, determine what types its parts must have, and report precisely where they cannot agree. The ML family — Milner's ML, Standard ML, OCaml, Haskell's core — took this harder road. The Hindley–Milner type system infers types completely: every subexpression's type is deduced from how the whole program is used, and when the program is typable, the inferred type is *principal* — the most general type possible [1][2].

This is a remarkable property: the space of possible typings for a program is infinite, yet Algorithm W computes the top of that space in (nearly) linear time on real code. HM achieves this by restricting polymorphism to *prenex* form — type schemes `∀ᾱ.τ` may appear only at `let`-bound definitions — and by generalizing only at `let`. This syntactic discipline, formalized by Hindley, reconstructed by Milner with an efficient algorithm [1], and proved complete by Damas [2], remains the skeleton of nearly every successful type inference engine.

> **Theorem:** (Damas–Milner completeness) If `Γ ⊢ e : σ` is derivable in the HM declarative system, then `W(Γ, e)` succeeds and returns a principal type-scheme `σ₀` such that `σ₀ ≥ σ` — i.e., `σ` is a generic instance of `σ₀`. Conversely, if `W(Γ, e) = (S, τ)`, then `SΓ ⊢ e : close(SΓ, τ)` is derivable. [2]

Yet the elegance of classical HM is fragile. Add mutable references, and naive let-polymorphism is *unsound*: a polymorphic cell can be written at one type and read at another. The **value restriction** — generalizing only syntactic *values* — repairs soundness at the cost of accepting fewer programs [4][6]. Add GADTs or type families, and pattern matching introduces *local* type equalities; principality evaporates, motivating constraint-based reformulations such as OutsideIn(X) [3]. Add the demand for comprehensible diagnostics, and W's left-to-right substitution bias produces the notoriously poor error messages studied by Heeren et al. [5].

This thesis treats HM as a complete engineering artifact: formal foundations, the algorithm and its metatheory, the extensions that stress it, and the empirical reality of production compilers.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The declarative system

The HM language is the simply-typed lambda calculus extended with `let`:

```
e ::= x | λx.e | e₁ e₂ | let x = e₁ in e₂
τ ::= α | τ₁ → τ₂ | C(τ̄)          (monotypes)
σ ::= τ | ∀α.σ                     (type schemes)
```

Types come in two flavors. *Monotypes* `τ` contain no quantifiers. *Type schemes* `σ` bind variables under `∀`. The declarative judgment `Γ ⊢ e : σ` is given by the classic rules — `(Var)`, `(Abs)`, `(App)`, `(Let)` — plus the two rules that make polymorphism work:

- **(Gen)** `Γ ⊢ e : τ , α ∉ ftv(Γ) ⇒ Γ ⊢ e : ∀α.τ`
- **(Inst)** `Γ ⊢ e : ∀α.σ ⇒ Γ ⊢ e : [τ/α]σ`

Polymorphism is *predicative* and *prenex*: instantiation substitutes monotypes for quantified variables, and quantifiers cannot appear under arrows. Generalization is permitted only at `let`-bindings, and the side condition `α ∉ ftv(Γ)` is the crux of soundness: a variable free in the environment must not be generalized, or two uses of `x` under incompatible assumptions would both be admitted [1][2].

### 2.2 Principal types

A *principal type-scheme* for `e` under `Γ` is a `σ₀` such that `Γ ⊢ e : σ₀` and every other derivable `σ` is a *generic instance* of `σ₀` — obtainable by substituting monotypes for its bound variables. Damas proved the celebrated result: *if an expression is typable at all, it has a principal type-scheme, and Algorithm W computes it* [2]. This makes HM a true *reconstruction* discipline rather than mere checking — the algorithm never guesses; it computes the unique most general answer.

### 2.3 Historical arc

The lineage is compact but deep. Curry and Feys (1958) gave the first reconstruction algorithm for the simply typed lambda calculus. Hindley (1969) extended it and proved it infers the most general type. Milner (1978) independently rediscovered the method, recast it as Algorithm W, and embedded it in ML [1]. Damas (1982, 1985 thesis) supplied the completeness proof and extended the system to *polymorphic references* [2][4]. Tofte (1990) then demonstrated that the naive combination is *unsound*, and proposed a modified inference system with store typings that restored soundness and principality [4]. The **value restriction** of SML '97 — a conservative approximation of Tofte's analysis — remains the standard engineering compromise [6].

## 3. Methodology

Our reconstruction follows the standard metatheory [1][2][5]: **(i)** fix the declarative typing rules as ground truth; **(ii)** present Algorithm W as `W(Γ, e) = (S, τ)` together with the first-order unification procedure it depends on, and the constraint-based generate-then-solve reformulation of which W and Algorithm M are deterministic instances; **(iii)** analyze asymptotic behavior, the value restriction's soundness argument [4], the failure of principality under GADTs [3], and the error-reporting literature [5].

Notation: `ftv(·)` denotes free type variables; `S₁∘S₂` substitution composition; `close(Γ, τ)` the generalization operator.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Algorithm W: substitution as the carrier of inference

Algorithm W recurses on the expression, threading a substitution through the computation [1][2]:

```haskell
W :: TypeEnv -> Expr -> (Subst, Type)
W env (Var x) =
  let sigma = lookup x env
  in (emptySubst, instantiate sigma)          -- fresh vars for ∀-bound
W env (Lam x body) =
  let beta  = freshVar
      (s1, t1) = W (extend env x (Mono beta)) body
  in (s1, apply s1 beta :-> t1)
W env (App f a) =
  let (s1, t1) = W env f
      (s2, t2) = W (apply s1 env) a
      beta     = freshVar
      s3       = unify (apply s2 t1) (t2 :-> beta)   -- may fail: type error
  in (s3 `compose` s2 `compose` s1, apply s3 beta)
W env (Let x e1 e2) =
  let (s1, t1) = W env e1
      env1     = apply s1 env
      sigma    = generalize env1 t1              -- close over env-free vars
      (s2, t2) = W (extend env1 x sigma) e2
  in (s2 `compose` s1, t2)
```

Three observations dominate. First, the `(App)` case is where all *equality constraints* are born, and where `unify` may *fail* — that failure point is precisely where a type error is reported, so the error's location is determined by W's fixed left-to-right traversal order, not by any global notion of minimal blame [5]. Second, the `(Let)` case is the *only* place generalization happens: `generalize` quantifies exactly the variables of `t1` not free in the environment, implementing `(Gen)`'s side condition algorithmically. Third, every substitution application `apply s env` is load-bearing: without re-applying the accumulated substitution to the environment before descending, soundness collapses.

**Complexity.** With naive substitution application, W is exponential in the worst case. With the standard union-find representation and path compression, inference is effectively linear on human-written code — yet pathological terms exhibit the true exponential lower bound. Practical compilers (OCaml, GHC) therefore use persistent union-find with *levels* for generalization, due to Rémy, which additionally solves the soundness bug of generalizing variables that "escape" their scope [5].

### 4.2 First-order unification: the occurs check and termination

Unification solves equations `τ₁ ≟ τ₂` between monotypes. The Robinson/Martelli–Montanari rules:

| Rule | Equation | Action |
|------|----------|--------|
| Delete | `τ ≟ τ` | discard |
| Decompose | `C(τ̄) ≟ C(τ̄′)` | push `τᵢ ≟ τᵢ′`; clash on different constructors |
| Orient | `τ ≟ α` (τ not var) | swap to `α ≟ τ` |
| Eliminate | `α ≟ τ`, `α ∉ ftv(τ)` | substitute `[τ/α]` everywhere |
| Occurs check | `α ≟ τ`, `α ∈ ftv(τ)`, `α ≠ τ` | **fail** |

The occurs check is the guardian of *finiteness*: without it, `α ≟ α → β` would unify to the infinite type `μα. α → β` — a rational tree, not a finite HM type [5].

```rust
// Occurs-check guarded elimination (simplified)
fn elim(v: TyVar, t: &Type, s: &mut Subst) -> Result<(), UnifyError> {
    if t.free_vars().contains(&v) && *t != Type::Var(v) {
        return Err(UnifyError::OccursCheck(v)); // reject infinite types
    }
    s.insert(v, t.clone()); Ok(())
}
```

> **Theorem:** (Unification) The algorithm above terminates on all inputs; if it succeeds it returns the *most general unifier*; if it fails, no unifier exists. [2][5]

The most-general-unifier property is the load-bearing joint of the entire metatheory: W's completeness proof proceeds by showing, case by case, that any declarative derivation can be "tracked" by W's substitutions, with unification supplying the MGU at each application node [2].

### 4.3 Let-polymorphism, generalization, and the value restriction

The `(Let)` rule is HM's crown jewel: `let id = λx.x in (id 1, id True)` types because `id` is generalized to `∀α. α → α` and *instantiated afresh at each use* — rank-1, predicative, decidable let-polymorphism. Note the deliberate asymmetry with `(Abs)`: lambda-bound variables are *never* generalized, which is why `λf. (f 1, f True)` is ill-typed in HM — the restriction that keeps inference decidable, since impredicative System F inference is undecidable.

**The unsoundness of naive polymorphic references.** Tofte exhibited the classic counterexample [4]:

```ocaml
let r = ref [] in          (* if generalized: ∀α. α list ref *)
(r := [1];                 (* instantiate α := int *)
 !r @ [true])              (* instantiate α := bool — runtime crash *)
```

Without protection, `r` would receive the scheme `∀α. α list ref`; writing an `int list` and reading it as a `bool list` passes the type checker but corrupts the store. Tofte's diagnosis is precise: the problem is the *capture of free type variables in store typings* — a reference cell's type must remain fixed as the store evolves, so generalizing its type variables is unsound [4]. His modified system tracks store typings explicitly and proves soundness via operational semantics, remaining decidable with principal types [4].

**The value restriction.** The engineering response, adopted by SML '97, is syntactic: *generalize only syntactic values*.

```
close(Γ, τ)  allowed  iff  e₁ is a syntactic value (variable, lambda, constant, constructor application)
```

A syntactic value cannot allocate or mutate, so its type variables cannot be captured by store effects. The restriction is sound but *incomplete* relative to Tofte's system: it rejects programs Tofte would accept. Kiselyov's "Ballad of Value Restriction" [6] shows that even the relaxed value restriction is insufficient for *generating* well-typed polymorphic `let`-expressions in staged metaprogramming, and conjectures a weaker restriction — an active research program. Garrigue's relaxed value restriction (covariant type variables may still be generalized) recovers common cases like `let x = [] in ...` and is implemented in OCaml [5].

| Variant | Rule | Sound? | Accepts `let r = ref []` poly? |
|---------|------|--------|-------------------------------|
| Naive HM | generalize all `let` | **No** [4] | Yes (unsoundly) |
| Tofte 1990 | store typings, imperative vars | Yes | Sometimes |
| Value restriction (SML '97) | generalize only syntactic values | Yes | No |
| Relaxed (Garrigue/OCaml) | generalize covariant vars too | Yes | `[]` yes, `ref []` no |

### 4.4 Extensions: GADTs, type families, and the death of principality

Classical HM rests on a global invariant: *every typable term has a principal type*. Generalized algebraic data types (GADTs) shatter it. A GADT pattern match introduces a *local* type equality:

```haskell
data Term a where
  Lit  :: Int -> Term Int
  Pair :: Term a -> Term b -> Term (a, b)

eval :: Term a -> a
eval (Lit n)      = n            -- here, a ~ Int (local assumption)
eval (Pair x y)   = (eval x, eval y)
```

Inside the `Lit` branch, the equation `a ~ Int` is a *given* that holds only locally. Type inference must therefore solve constraints *under local assumptions* — and different solving orders can yield incomparable types. Vytiniotis, Peyton Jones, Schrijvers, and Sulzmann [3] formalize this in **OutsideIn(X)**: constraints are split into *givens* (top-down, from signatures and GADT matches) and *wanteds* (bottom-up, to be solved), with implication constraints `∀ᾱ. (G ⊃ W)` scoping the local assumptions. Their empirical observation: to retain principality, *local let-generalization must be abandoned* — GHC does not generalize `let`-bindings under local constraints, and their measurements show programmers rarely relied on it anyway [3].

Type families add a second blow: type-level functions make equality *non-structural*, so deciding whether two types are equal requires evaluating the family equations — unification modulo axioms, potentially non-terminating without termination conditions [3]. Johann and Ghiorzi [7] attack from the semantic side, showing that in a Hindley–Milner-style calculus with primitive nested types and GADTs, no functorial interpretation of GADTs via left Kan extensions can remain parametric — a no-go theorem for the naive semantic story.

The modern picture is thus stratified: *pure HM* (principal, complete, fast) sits inside a *constraint-based* framework (OutsideIn(X), HM(X)) that handles local assumptions at the price of principality [3]. Constraint-based presentations also unify W and M as deterministic instances of a single generate-then-solve scheme, yielding their correctness proofs for free [5].

### 4.5 Error reporting and the traversal bias of W

W reports the *first* unification failure encountered in its fixed left-to-right, depth-first traversal. Heeren, Hage, and Swierstra [5] diagnose this as an *algorithmic bias*: the reported location is an artifact of traversal order, not of the program's actual inconsistency. Their remedy is the **constraint-based** reformulation: collect *all* constraints first, then solve under a heuristic, so that (1) multiple messages can be produced from independent inconsistent subsets; (2) solving order can be chosen to maximize information; (3) W and M fall out as deterministic instances, inheriting correctness proofs for free [5].

This line of work directly shaped modern compilers: GHC's constraint solver separates generation from solving, and its error messages are rendered from the *residual* unsolved constraint rather than from the first failure point.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 The metatheoretic core

> **Theorem:** (Soundness of W) If `W(Γ, e) = (S, τ)`, then `SΓ ⊢ e : close(SΓ, τ)` is derivable in the declarative system.

> **Theorem:** (Completeness of W) If `Γ ⊢ e : σ` is derivable, then `W(Γ, e)` succeeds with `(S₀, τ₀)`, and there exists a substitution `R` with `σ` a generic instance of `R(close(S₀Γ, τ₀))` — i.e., W finds a principal type.

The completeness proof is an induction on the declarative derivation, maintaining the invariant that W's current substitution is *more general* than the derivation's implicit one; the MGU property of unification (§4.2) is invoked at each `(App)` node. The constraint-based view [5] re-proves both as corollaries of *solver* soundness/completeness: since W is a deterministic instance of the general scheme, the general theorems specialize for free.### 5.2 Complexity: theory vs. practice

| Measure | Bound | Notes |
|---------|-------|-------|
| Naive W | `O(2^n)` worst case | substitution re-application; McAllester terms |
| Union-find W | near-linear in practice | path compression + levels (Rémy) |
| Unification alone | `O(n · α(n))` | almost-linear (Martelli–Montanari) |
| HM typability | DEXPTIME-complete | Mairson 1990; pathological only |
| HM(X) / OutsideIn(X) | undecidable in general | depends on constraint domain X [3] |

The gap between DEXPTIME-completeness of typability and the observed near-linear behavior is the standard HM miracle: the hard instances are *adversarially constructed* and essentially never arise in human code. Production compilers additionally exploit *levels-based* generalization to avoid quadratic `ftv(Γ)` scans [5].

### 5.3 Case studies in deployed systems

- **OCaml** implements HM with the relaxed value restriction and levels-based generalization; its principal-type guarantee holds for the core language, with objects, polymorphic variants, and GADTs handled by extensions that locally suspend principality.
- **Haskell (GHC)** implements OutsideIn(X) [3]: generate-then-solve with givens/wanteds and implication constraints for GADTs. Local let-generalization is disabled under local assumptions — the principled sacrifice the JFP paper defends with usage data [3].
- **Standard ML** remains closest to classical Damas–Milner with the strict value restriction; its Definition is the reference semantics against which Tofte's soundness proof [4] is calibrated.

### 5.4 A worked inference trace

For `let f = λx.x in (f 1, f True)`: `W` infers `λx.x : β → β`, generalizes to `f : ∀β. β → β`, then instantiates *afresh at each use* — `β := Int` at `f 1`, `β := Bool` at `f True` — yielding `(Int, Bool)`. The occurs check fires on `λx. x x`: inferring `x : β`, the application demands `β ≟ β → γ`, and `β ∈ ftv(β → γ)` — rejection, correctly, since no finite HM type admits self-application.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Rank-1 predicative ceiling.** HM cannot type `λf. (f 1, f True)` or first-class polymorphic arguments. Higher-rank extensions require annotations at precisely the points where inference gives up.
2. **Value restriction over-approximates.** It rejects effect-free programs whose *syntax* is non-value; the relaxed restriction recovers only the covariant fragment [6].
3. **No principality under local assumptions.** GADTs, type families, and local instances force the OutsideIn(X) regime, where the solver must be *guess-free* — and GHC's solution is to silently disable local let-generalization [3].
4. **Error locality is heuristic.** Even constraint-based solvers report *a* minimal inconsistent subset, not *the* programmer's mistake; blame assignment remains underdetermined [5].
5. **Semantic fragility of extensions.** Johann and Ghiorzi's no-go result [7] shows GADTs resist the parametric models that justify HM's metatheory — extensions are justified operationally, and free theorems may fail silently.
6. **Complexity cliffs.** DEXPTIME-hard typability means adversarial inputs can still hang a compiler; production systems rely on the empirical absence of such inputs.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Hindley–Milner type inference is the rare artifact that is simultaneously a *theorem* and an *infrastructure*: Damas's completeness result [2] guarantees that Algorithm W computes principal types whenever they exist, while union-find engineering and levels-based generalization make it fast enough to run on every keystroke in modern IDEs. Its central ideas — unification as constraint solving, generalization quarantined at `let`, the occurs check as finiteness guardian — have survived fifty years of extension pressure. But the extensions that matter — mutable state [4][6], GADTs and type families [3], usable diagnostics [5] — each expose a boundary where classical HM ends and new machinery begins.

The enduring lesson is methodological. HM succeeds not because it is the most expressive system, but because it identifies the *largest decidable, principal, and complete* fragment — and draws the line in exactly the right place. Every modern type checker, from OCaml's to GHC's OutsideIn(X) engine, is best understood as HM at its core with carefully delimited extensions at the edges: a complete kernel plus principled, documented compromises.
