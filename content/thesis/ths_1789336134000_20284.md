# Percolation Theory: Critical Probabilities, the Russo–Seymour–Welsh Theory, and the Conformal Invariance of Critical Interfaces

## Abstract

Percolation theory, introduced by Broadbent and Hammersley in 1957 [1], studies the emergence of macroscopic connectivity in random media. In Bernoulli bond percolation on the square lattice, each edge is open independently with probability p, and the percolation probability theta(p) jumps from zero to positive at the critical probability p_c, which Harris and Kesten proved equals exactly one half [2]. This article surveys the mathematical foundations of the phase transition: the correlation inequalities and Russo's pivotal formula, the Russo–Seymour–Welsh crossing estimates that power the two-dimensional theory [3][4], the scaling relations and critical exponents established by Kesten [5], and the conformal invariance of critical interfaces proved by Smirnov for site percolation on the triangular lattice [7], which identifies the scaling limit of the percolation exploration path as Schramm–Loewner evolution with parameter six [8][9]. We tabulate known and estimated critical probabilities, describe the lace expansion in high dimensions [10], efficient Monte Carlo estimation via the Newman–Ziff algorithm [11], and close with the major open problems that remain.

---

## 1 Intro

Percolation began as a question about gas masks. In the mid-1950s, Simon Broadbent, a statistician at the British Coal Utilisation Research Association, wanted to understand how a fluid diffuses through the random maze of pores in coal. At a 1954 symposium on Monte Carlo methods he posed the problem to John Hammersley, and their 1957 papers *Percolation processes I and II* [1] turned a piece of applied physics into one of the most intensively studied models in probability theory. The abstraction is disarmingly simple: take an infinite graph, keep each edge (or vertex) independently with probability *p*, discard it otherwise, and ask whether the retained structure contains an infinite connected component.

## 2 Background

We work with Bernoulli **bond percolation** on an infinite graph *G = (V, E)*, most often the square lattice *Z²*. Each edge *e* is declared *open* with probability *p* and *closed* with probability *1 − p*, independently of all other edges. The resulting product measure is denoted *P_p*, with expectation *E_p*. An *open cluster* is a connected component of the subgraph of open edges; we write *C(v)* for the open cluster of the vertex *v*, and *v ↔ w* for the event that *v* and *w* lie in the same open cluster.

The central observable is the **percolation probability**

> **Definition.** *theta(p) = P_p(0 ↔ infinity)*, the probability that the origin belongs to an infinite open cluster.

By monotonicity of the underlying coupling, *theta(p)* is nondecreasing in *p*, and the **critical probability** is

*p_c = inf { p : theta(p) > 0 }*.

Kolmogorov's zero-one law implies that the event *{there exists an infinite open cluster}* has *P_p*-probability either *0* or *1*. For *p < p_c* there is almost surely no infinite cluster, and the cluster-size distribution decays exponentially fast — a theorem of Menshikov, and independently of Aizenman and Barsky, whose standard modern account is in Grimmett [12]. For *p > p_c* at least one infinite cluster exists almost surely, it has positive asymptotic density, and by the Burton–Keane argument it is **unique** almost surely on amenable lattices [12]. The phase transition is thus sharp in the sense that infinite connectivity switches on at a single value of *p*.

The two-dimensional theory is anchored by a celebrated exact value. The square lattice is *self-dual*: its planar dual graph is again a square lattice, and an open horizontal crossing of a rectangle is complementary to a closed vertical crossing on the dual. Harris proved in 1960 that *theta(1/2) = 0* [2], establishing *p_c ≥ 1/2* for bond percolation on *Z²*. Twenty years later Kesten proved the matching upper bound, *theta(p) > 0* for *p > 1/2* [2], closing one of the most famous open questions in probability:

> **Theorem (Harris–Kesten).** *For bond percolation on the square lattice Z², the critical probability equals one half: p_c = 1/2.*

## 3 Methodology

The percolation toolkit rests on three pillars: correlation inequalities, derivative formulas, and geometric crossing estimates.

**Correlation inequalities.** The FKG (Fortuin–Kasteleyn–Ginibre) inequality states that *increasing* events — events preserved under opening more edges — are positively correlated: *P_p(A ∩ B) ≥ P_p(A) P_p(B)*. This is the workhorse behind essentially every lower bound in the subject. The BK (van den Berg–Kesten) inequality controls *disjoint* occurrences: the probability that two events occur on disjoint edge sets is at most the product of their probabilities. Together they give analysts control over both the gluing together and the separation of geometric events [12].

**Russo's formula.** For an increasing event *A* depending on finitely many edges, the derivative of its probability with respect to *p* counts pivotal edges:

> **Theorem (Russo's formula, 1978).** *d/dp P_p(A) = sum over edges e of P_p(e is pivotal for A), where e is pivotal for A if flipping the state of e changes the occurrence of A.* [3]

This identity turns analytic questions about sharp thresholds into geometric questions about pivotal edges, and it is the engine behind modern sharp-threshold arguments: if many edges are typically pivotal, the transition must be sharp.

**Simulation: the Newman–Ziff algorithm.** Modern estimates of critical values rely on the union-find algorithm of Newman and Ziff [11], which grows a random permutation of edges one at a time and records, after each addition, whether a spanning cluster has appeared. A single run of length *N* edges yields the spanning observable for every occupation count simultaneously; convolution with the binomial distribution converts the microcanonical data into the canonical curve *Pi(p)*:

```python
import random

class UnionFind:
    def __init__(self, n):
        self.parent = list(range(n))
        self.size = [1] * n
    def find(self, x):
        while self.parent[x] != x:
            self.parent[x] = self.parent[self.parent[x]]
            x = self.parent[x]
        return x
    def union(self, a, b):
        ra, rb = self.find(a), self.find(b)
        if ra == rb:
            return False
        if self.size[ra] < self.size[rb]:
            ra, rb = rb, ra
        self.parent[rb] = ra
        self.size[ra] += self.size[rb]
        return True

def spanning_histogram(n, trials=200):
    N = n * n
    edges = [((x, y), (x + dx, y + dy))
             for x in range(n) for y in range(n)
             for dx, dy in ((1, 0), (0, 1))
             if x + dx < n and y + dy < n]
    m = len(edges)
    hist = [0] * (m + 1)
    for _ in range(trials):
        uf = UnionFind(N)
        order = list(range(m))
        random.shuffle(order)
        spans = [False] * (m + 1)
        for k, e in enumerate(order, start=1):
            (x1, y1), (x2, y2) = edges[e]
            uf.union(y1 * n + x1, y2 * n + x2)
            spans[k] = any(uf.find(r * n) == uf.find(r * n + n - 1)
                           for r in range(n))
        for k in range(m + 1):
            hist[k] += spans[k]
    return [h / trials for h in hist]
```

This is how the landmark value *p_c = 0.59274621(13)* for site percolation on the square lattice was obtained [11] — accuracy no analytic method can currently approach.


**The lace expansion.** In high dimensions a completely different methodology applies: the lace expansion of Hara and Slade [10] proves that percolation on *Z^d* for *d ≥ 19* behaves like percolation on a tree, with mean-field exponents *beta = 1*, *gamma = 1*, *nu = 1/2*. It remains the only method that rigorously determines critical behaviour above the upper critical dimension.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Russo–Seymour–Welsh Theory

The Russo–Seymour–Welsh (RSW) theory [3][4] is the geometric heart of planar percolation. Let *H(R)* denote the event that a rectangle *R* is crossed horizontally by an open path. The RSW lemma asserts that crossing probabilities at criticality are *uniformly* bounded away from *0* and *1* across all scales:

> **Theorem (Russo–Seymour–Welsh).** *There exists a constant c > 0, depending only on the aspect ratio, such that for critical percolation on Z², c ≤ P_{p_c}(H(R)) ≤ 1 − c for every rectangle R of that aspect ratio, at every scale.*

The proof is a gluing argument of striking elegance: a long rectangle is decomposed into overlapping squares; FKG glues crossings of the pieces; and planar duality converts a bound on primal crossings into a bound on dual crossings, which feeds back into the primal estimate. The uniformity in scale is the crucial point — it says critical percolation is *scale invariant* in a qualitative sense, ruling out both the subcritical behaviour (crossing probabilities decaying to zero) and the supercritical behaviour (tending to one).

RSW has three far-reaching consequences. First, it powers Kesten's proof that *p_c = 1/2* [2]: uniform crossing bounds feed the finite-size criterion of Section 3. Second, it yields the *box-crossing property* used to prove that the incipient infinite cluster — the critical cluster conditioned to be infinite — is well defined. Third, and most importantly for what follows, RSW is the a priori estimate on which Smirnov's conformal invariance proof is built [7]: without uniform control of crossings at all scales, no continuum limit could exist.

### 4.2 Scaling Relations and Critical Exponents

Near criticality, percolation observables obey power laws governed by **critical exponents**. The correlation length *xi(p)* — the characteristic scale of finite clusters — diverges as *p* approaches *p_c*, and every other observable is expressed through it. The principal exponents and their exactly known two-dimensional values are:

| Exponent | Observable | Behaviour near *p_c* | 2D value |
|---|---|---|---|
| *beta* | percolation probability *theta(p)* | *(p − p_c)^beta* | 5/36 |
| *nu* | correlation length *xi(p)* | *\|p − p_c\|^(−nu)* | 4/3 |
| *gamma* | mean cluster size *chi(p)* | *\|p − p_c\|^(−gamma)* | 43/18 |
| *eta* | critical connectivity | *P_{p_c}(0 ↔ x) ~ \|x\|^(−(d−2+eta))* | 5/24 |
| *delta* | cluster tail | *P_{p_c}(\|C\| ≥ n) ~ n^(−1/delta)* | 91/5 |
| *tau* | cluster size distribution | *P_{p_c}(\|C\| = n) ~ n^(−tau)* | 187/91 |
| *sigma* | cutoff scale | — | 36/91 |
| *alpha* | singular free energy | *\|p − p_c\|^(2−alpha)* | −2/3 |

These exponents are not independent. Kesten proved in 1987 that, *assuming* a small number of exponents exist, the rest are forced by **scaling relations** [5]:

- *2 − alpha = gamma + 2 beta*
- *gamma = nu (2 − eta)*
- *beta = nu (d − 2 + eta) / 2*
- *delta = (d + 2 − eta) / (d − 2 + eta)*

### 4.3 Smirnov's Theorem: Conformal Invariance and Cardy's Formula

Smirnov's 2001 breakthrough changed everything [7]. Working with critical **site** percolation on the **triangular lattice** (where self-duality pins *p_c = 1/2*), he constructed a discrete observable with a miraculous property. For a domain with three marked boundary points, consider the probabilities that a point *z* is separated from each boundary arc by an open crossing; Smirnov showed these three functions satisfy a discrete version of the Cauchy–Riemann equations — a *preholomorphic* observable — via a combinatorial **colour-swap lemma** that exploits the threefold symmetry of the triangular lattice. Passing to the scaling limit, the observable becomes genuinely holomorphic, and its boundary values are forced to match Cardy's prediction:

> **Theorem (Smirnov, 2001).** *Critical site percolation on the triangular lattice has a conformally invariant scaling limit. In particular, crossing probabilities between boundary arcs converge to Cardy's formula, and the limit is independent of the lattice details.* [7]

### 4.4 Critical Interfaces and Schramm–Loewner Evolution

Smirnov's theorem describes crossing *probabilities*. The complementary question concerns the random *curves* themselves: what does the boundary between an open and a closed cluster look like at criticality? The answer is given by the **Schramm–Loewner evolution** (SLE), introduced by Oded Schramm in 2000 [8].

Chordal *SLE(kappa)* is the random curve generated by Loewner's differential equation driven by a one-dimensional Brownian motion of speed *kappa*: informally, *dW_t = sqrt(kappa) dB_t* feeds the Loewner flow *partial_t g_t = 2 / (g_t − W_t)*.  

Which *kappa* belongs to percolation? The **locality** property of percolation — the interface does not feel the boundary until it hits it — selects *kappa = 6* uniquely. Smirnov's conformal invariance then implies that the percolation exploration path converges to *SLE(6)*, and Lawler, Schramm, and Werner's program converts questions about the discrete model into Itô calculus on the driving Brownian motion [9]. This is how the critical exponents of Section 4.2 became theorems. The conjectural SLE dictionary now reads:

| *kappa* | Lattice model |
|---|---|
| 2 | loop-erased random walk |
| 3 | Ising interface |
| 8/3 | self-avoiding walk (conjectured) |
| 6 | critical percolation |
| 16/3 | FK-Ising interface |
| 8 | uniform spanning tree Peano curve |

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

**Critical values.** 

| Lattice | Bond *p_c* | Site *p_c* |
|---|---|---|
| Square *Z²* | *1/2* (exact, Harris–Kesten [2]) | *0.59274621(13)* (numerical [11]) |
| Triangular | *2 sin(pi/18) ≈ 0.347296* (exact) | *1/2* (exact, self-matching [7]) |
| Honeycomb | *1 − 2 sin(pi/18) ≈ 0.652704* (exact) | *0.6970402* (numerical) |
| Cubic *Z³* | *0.2488126* (numerical) | *0.3116077* (numerical) |

As the dimension grows, *p_c(d) ~ 1/(2d)*, and the lace expansion makes this precise: for *d ≥ 19*, percolation is in the mean-field universality class with exponents *beta = 1*, *gamma = 1*, *nu = 1/2* [10].

**How the proofs work.** Three proof sketches capture the subject's main ideas:

1. *Harris–Kesten, p_c = 1/2.* Self-duality gives *p_c ≥ 1/2* via Harris's argument that *theta(1/2) = 0* [2]. For the reverse inequality, RSW provides uniform lower bounds on rectangle crossings at *p = 1/2* [3][4]; a sharp-threshold argument (Russo's formula plus a differential inequality) boosts the crossing probability of long rectangles arbitrarily close to one for any *p > 1/2*; the finite-size criterion then builds an infinite cluster. Modern presentations streamline this through the Russo–Seymour–Welsh theory and sharp-threshold technology [13].

## 6 Limitations

For all its triumphs, percolation theory has conspicuous gaps — some of the deepest open problems in probability:

1. **No exact square-lattice site value.** Despite fifty years of effort, *p_c* for site percolation on *Z²* is known only numerically (*0.59274621*). No duality or algebraic argument is in sight.
2. **Continuity of theta at p_c.** It is unknown in full generality whether *theta(p_c) = 0* for dimensions between 3 and 18; the two-dimensional case follows from Kesten's work [2] and high dimensions from the lace expansion [10], but the intermediate regime resists.
3. **Conformal invariance on the square lattice.** Smirnov's proof [7] leans essentially on the threefold symmetry of the triangular lattice. Conformal invariance — and hence *SLE(6)* convergence — for bond percolation on *Z²* is expected but unproved.
4. **General graphs.** The critical value on non-amenable graphs, the Benjamini–Schramm conjecture that *p_c < p_u* (non-uniqueness phase), and sharpness of the transition on arbitrary transitive graphs remain active frontiers [13].

These limitations are not blemishes but the field's engine: each open problem above has already generated decades of mathematics.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Percolation theory offers a rare arc in modern mathematics: from a concrete industrial question about coal pores [1], through the exact determination of the square-lattice critical probability [2], the geometric mastery of the Russo–Seymour–Welsh estimates [3][4], the scaling relations of Kesten [5], to the conformal invariance of Smirnov [7] and the Schramm–Loewner evolution [8][9] — a trajectory from applied problem to some of the deepest theorems in probability.  If the past seventy years are any guide, the next breakthrough will again come from taking the geometry of random crossings seriously.
