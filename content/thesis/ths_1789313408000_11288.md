# Randomized Numerical Linear Algebra: Sketching, Concentration, and Single-Pass Low-Rank Approximation

## Abstract

Randomized numerical linear algebra replaces exact deterministic factorizations with stochastic algorithms that first compress a massive matrix into a small *sketch* and then perform the expensive linear algebra on the sketch. This article develops the theory and practice of that program: the randomized range finder and randomized singular value decomposition of Halko, Martinsson, and Tropp; the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma and oblivious subspace embeddings that make sketching rigorous; CUR decompositions built from leverage-score sampling that preserve interpretability; and single-pass, single-view algorithms that construct provably accurate low-rank approximations from one streaming look at the data. The analytical backbone is matrix concentration — matrix Bernstein and Chernoff inequalities — which converts randomized constructions into high-probability relative-error guarantees of the form *sketch error within a factor of (1 + ε) of the optimal rank-k error*. We close with applications to large-scale principal component analysis and kernel methods, where Nyström approximations reduce cubic costs to nearly linear ones.

---

## 1 Introduction

Classical numerical linear algebra is built on deterministic factorizations: the singular value decomposition (SVD), the QR decomposition, and the eigendecomposition. These algorithms are backward stable, exquisitely engineered, and — for matrices with millions of rows — catastrophically slow. Computing a full SVD of an *m*-by-*n* matrix costs on the order of *mn*·min(*m*, *n*) floating-point operations and demands repeated passes over data that may live on disk, on a cluster, or in a stream that can be observed only once. For modern data matrices, where *m* and *n* routinely reach the millions, the classical toolkit is not merely expensive; it is architecturally mismatched to the hardware, because its communication patterns were designed for an era of fast memory and slow processors [1].

*Randomized numerical linear algebra* (RandNLA) inverts the conventional workflow. Rather than manipulating the full matrix, it first *compresses* the matrix with a random linear map into a much smaller object — a sketch — and then runs a classical algorithm on the sketch. The surprising mathematical fact that makes this possible is that a random projection of dimension only slightly larger than the target rank *k* captures essentially all the "action" of the matrix with overwhelming probability. The modular framework of Halko, Martinsson, and Tropp [1] made this idea systematic: (i) use randomness to identify a subspace that captures most of the action of the matrix; (ii) compress the matrix, explicitly or implicitly, to that subspace; (iii) manipulate the reduced matrix deterministically. Their randomized SVD typically beats LAPACK's deterministic routines in speed while matching them in accuracy, and the ideas have since permeated least-squares regression, low-rank approximation, and graph sparsification through the sketching paradigm surveyed by Woodruff [2].

Three mathematical pillars support the edifice. First, *random projections* and the Johnson–Lindenstrauss (JL) lemma: *n* points in Euclidean space embed into dimension *O(ε⁻² log n)* with all pairwise distances distorted by at most a factor of *(1 ± ε)*. Fast and sparse JL transforms [6] make these embeddings cheap to apply. Second, *column and row sampling*: CUR decompositions [4] approximate a matrix by actual columns and rows, chosen with probabilities proportional to *statistical leverage scores*, preserving the interpretability that pure random projections destroy. Third, *single-pass sketching*: algorithms of Tropp, Yurtsever, Udell, and Cevher [5] build low-rank approximations — including structure-preserving ones for positive semidefinite matrices — from a single view of streaming data. Binding all three together is *matrix concentration theory* [3]: noncommutative extensions of the classical Bernstein, Chernoff, and Hoeffding inequalities that deliver the high-probability error bounds distinguishing RandNLA from heuristic dimensionality reduction.

Section 2 fixes notation and the low-rank approximation problem; Section 3 presents the two-stage framework and the sketching model; Section 4 develops randomized SVD, JL embeddings, CUR decompositions, and single-pass algorithms; Section 5 states the key theorems with proof sketches; Section 6 discusses limitations; and Section 7 concludes with applications to large-scale PCA and kernel methods.

## 2 Background

Let *A* be a real *m*-by-*n* matrix with singular values *σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ ⋯*. The *best rank-k approximation* in either the spectral or Frobenius norm is given by the Eckart–Young theorem as the truncated SVD

$$A_k = U_k \Sigma_k V_k^T = \arg\min_{\mathrm{rank}(B) \le k} \|A - B\|,$$

with optimal errors *σ_{k+1}* (spectral) and *(Σ_{j>k} σ_j²)^{1/2}* (Frobenius). The goal of RandNLA is to produce a rank-*k* approximation *Â* satisfying a *relative-error* guarantee

$$\|A - \hat{A}\|_F^2 \le (1 + \varepsilon)\,\|A - A_k\|_F^2 \qquad \text{with high probability}, \tag{1}$$

at cost far below the *O(mnk)* or worse of deterministic methods [2].

A *sketching matrix* *S* is a random *ℓ*-by-*m* matrix (with *ℓ ≪ m*) such that the *sketch* *SA* preserves the geometry of the column space of *A*. The central notion is the *oblivious subspace embedding*: with high probability over the draw of *S*, simultaneously for every *x* in a fixed *k*-dimensional subspace,

$$(1-\varepsilon)\|x\|_2^2 \le \|Sx\|_2^2 \le (1+\varepsilon)\|x\|_2^2. \tag{2}$$

Gaussian matrices with entries drawn independently from a scaled normal distribution satisfy (2) with *ℓ = O(k/ε²)*, and this dimension is optimal up to constants. Sparse alternatives — the CountSketch of Clarkson and Woodruff, and sparse JL constructions — achieve (2) with *ℓ = O(k²/ε²)* or better while applying *S* to *A* in time proportional to the number of nonzeros of *A*, which is essential for sparse data [2].

> **Theorem: (Johnson–Lindenstrauss, 1984).** For every set *X* of *n* points in Euclidean space and every *ε* in the open unit interval, there exists a linear map *f* into dimension *m = Θ(ε⁻² log n)* such that all pairwise squared distances are preserved up to a relative factor of *(1 ± ε)*. Moreover, a random Gaussian matrix succeeds with high probability.

The JL lemma is usually stated for finite point sets, but its proof — concentration of the norm of a random projection of a fixed vector, followed by a union bound over the *O(n²)* pairs — is the template for every subspace embedding argument in the field. The refinements matter enormously in practice: the Fast JL Transform of Ailon and Chazelle [6] computes the embedding in *O(d log d + k log² n)* time using a randomized Hadamard transform followed by a sparse projection, while sparse JL transforms of Kane and Nelson run in time proportional to the sparsity of the input vector. These constructions turn an abstract existence result into algorithms that beat dense matrix multiplication by orders of magnitude.

## 3 Methodology

The Halko–Martinsson–Tropp framework [1] proceeds in two stages. **Stage A (randomized range finding):** draw a random *n*-by-*ℓ* test matrix *Ω* (Gaussian, or a structured alternative such as a subsampled randomized Fourier transform), form the sketch *Y = AΩ*, and orthonormalize its columns to obtain *Q* with *ℓ = k + p* columns, where *p* is a small oversampling parameter (5 or 10 is typical). With high probability, the range of *Q* captures the dominant *k*-dimensional left singular subspace of *A*. **Stage B (deterministic post-processing):** form the small matrix *B = Q*A* (*ℓ*-by-*n*), compute its exact SVD *B = ÛΣV**, and set *U = QÛ*. The product *UΣV** is the rank-*k* randomized SVD, and its error equals the Stage-A projection error *‖A − QQ*A‖* exactly.

When the singular values of *A* decay slowly, Stage A is sharpened by *power iteration*: replace *Y = AΩ* with *Y = (AA*)^q AΩ* for a small integer *q* (1 or 2), implemented with intermediate orthonormalization for numerical stability. Because *(AA*)^q A* has the same singular vectors as *A* but singular values *σ_j^{2q+1}*, the sketch aligns exponentially better with the dominant subspace, at the cost of *2q + 1* passes over *A* [1].

```python
import numpy as np

def randomized_svd(A, k, p=10, q=1, random_state=None):
    """Randomized rank-k SVD via the Halko-Martinsson-Tropp two-stage scheme."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(random_state)
    m, n = A.shape
    ell = k + p
    Omega = rng.standard_normal((n, ell))          # Stage A: random test matrix
    Y = A @ Omega
    for _ in range(q):                            # power iteration sharpens decay
        Y = A @ (A.T @ Y)
        Y, _ = np.linalg.qr(Y)                    # re-orthonormalize for stability
    Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(Y)                        # approximate range basis
    B = Q.T @ A                                   # Stage B: compress to ell x n
    U_hat, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(B, full_matrices=False)
    return (Q @ U_hat)[:, :k], s[:k], Vt[:k, :]    # rank-k factors
```

The complexity is dominated by the *2q + 1* matrix multiplications with *A*, i.e., *O(mnℓ)* for dense *A*, versus *O(mn*·min(*m*,*n*)*)* for a full SVD. When *A* admits fast multiplication (sparse, structured, or available only as an operator), the savings are decisive, and the algorithm parallelizes and vectorizes far better than Krylov methods [1].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Randomized Range Finder and Its Error Bounds

The miracle of Stage A is quantitative. For a Gaussian test matrix with oversampling *p ≥ 2*, Halko, Martinsson, and Tropp prove an expectation bound that has become the workhorse estimate of the field [1]:

> **Theorem: (Expected projection error).** Let *Q* be the orthonormal basis produced by the randomized range finder with target rank *k* and oversampling *p ≥ 2*. Then
>
> $$\mathbb{E}\,\|A - QQ^*A\|_F \le \left(1 + \frac{k}{p-1}\right)^{1/2} \left(\sum_{j>k} \sigma_j^2\right)^{1/2},$$
>
> and with the power scheme of exponent *q*,
>
> $$\mathbb{E}\,\|A - QQ^*A\| \le \left[1 + \sqrt{\frac{k}{p-1}} + \frac{e\sqrt{k+p}}{p}\sqrt{\min(m,n)-k}\right]^{\!1/(2q+1)} \sigma_{k+1}.$$

The Frobenius bound is striking: oversampling by a mere *p = 5* inflates the optimal error by a factor of only *√(1 + k/4)* in expectation, and the spectral bound shows that even *q = 1* or *2* drives the constant toward 1 exponentially fast in *q*. High-probability versions follow from the same Gaussian analysis with slightly larger constants, and *a posteriori* error estimators let practitioners certify accuracy cheaply [1].

A flagship demonstration is the *eigenfaces* computation: a *98,304*-by-*7,254* matrix of face images (5.4 GB uncompressed) whose slowly decaying spectrum defeats the unpowered (*q = 0*) scheme but is captured accurately with *q = 3*, with the largest hundred singular values estimated to high accuracy from a single sketch [1]. The lesson generalizes: randomization is not a heuristic here but a *provable* dimensionality reduction, with the failure probability decaying exponentially in the oversampling.

### 4.2 Johnson–Lindenstrauss Sketching and Oblivious Embeddings

The JL lemma reduces *geometry preservation* to *norm concentration*. For a Gaussian sketch *S* with *ℓ* rows scaled by *1/√ℓ*, the squared norm *‖Sx‖²* is an average of *ℓ* independent chi-squared variables; classical Chernoff bounds give exponential concentration around *‖x‖²*, and a union bound over an *ε*-net of the *k*-dimensional subspace extends the guarantee from one vector to all of them simultaneously [2]. This net argument is the engine behind every subspace embedding theorem.

Structured embeddings trade a modest increase in *ℓ* for a dramatic decrease in application cost:

| Embedding | Sketch dimension *ℓ* | Apply to *x* | Key idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian | *O(k/ε²)* | *O(dℓ)* dense | Optimal dimension; baseline |
| Fast JL (Ailon–Chazelle) [6] | *O(k/ε²)* up to log factors | *O(d log d)* | Randomized Hadamard + sparse projection |
| CountSketch | *O(k²/ε²)* | *O(nnz(x))* | One nonzero per column; streaming-friendly |
| Sparse JL (Kane–Nelson) | *O(k log k/ε²)* | *O(s·nnz(x))* | *s*-sparse columns; near-optimal sparsity |

For low-rank approximation, Woodruff's survey [2] shows that an oblivious embedding with *ℓ = O(k/ε)* rows suffices for a *(1 + ε)* Frobenius relative-error approximation via the *sketch-and-solve* paradigm: compute *SA*, find its best rank-*k* approximation in the sketched space, and lift back. Lower bounds confirm that *Ω(k/ε)* rows are necessary, so the theory is tight up to constants — a rare and satisfying state of affairs in algorithm design.

```julia
# Sketch-and-solve least squares, Julia-style pseudocode
function sketch_solve(A, b, ell)
    S = countsketch(ell, size(A, 1))   # oblivious embedding, O(nnz(A)) time
    SA, Sb = S * A, S * b             # compress the problem
    return (SA \ Sb)                  # tiny dense solve: (1+ε)-optimal w.h.p.
end
```

The same machinery powers *coresets* and *sensitivity sampling* in machine learning [2].

### 4.3 CUR Decompositions and Leverage-Score Sampling

Random projections are *data-oblivious* and therefore destroy interpretability: the sketch *SA* is a dense soup of linear combinations. *CUR decompositions* [4] take the opposite approach, approximating *A ≈ CUR* where *C* consists of actual columns of *A*, *R* of actual rows, and *U* is a small linking matrix (typically *U = W⁺*, the pseudoinverse of the intersection of the chosen rows and columns). When the columns are gene expression profiles or the rows are documents, CUR factors remain directly interpretable — a decisive advantage in scientific applications.

The key algorithmic question is *which* columns and rows to keep. Uniform sampling fails on *coherent* matrices, where a few columns carry most of the mass. The correct importance measure is the *statistical leverage score*: for the top-*k* right singular vectors *V_k*,

$$\ell_j = \|(V_k)_{j,:}\|_2^2, \qquad \sum_j \ell_j = k,$$

the squared row-norms of *V_k*, which quantify how influential each column is in the best rank-*k* fit. Sampling *c = O(k log k / ε²)* columns with probabilities proportional to (overestimates of) the leverage scores yields, with constant probability,

$$\|A - CC^+A\|_F \le (1+\varepsilon)\,\|A - A_k\|_F, \tag{3}$$

and applying the same procedure to the rows of *C* produces a full CUR decomposition with relative error [4]. The proof is a clean application of matrix concentration [3]: the sampled Gram matrix *Σ_j (1/cp_j) v_j v_j^T* is a sum of independent rank-one PSD matrices whose expectation is the identity on the top-*k* subspace, and the matrix Chernoff bound keeps all its eigenvalues near 1.

Computing exact leverage scores requires the very SVD we seek to avoid, but *approximate* leverage scores suffice — they can themselves be estimated with a randomized SVD or a JL sketch, giving a bootstrapping procedure that runs in *o(mn²)* time [4]. This self-referential structure, where sketching accelerates the preprocessing for sampling, is characteristic of mature RandNLA.

### 4.4 Single-Pass Algorithms and the Generalized Nyström Method

The algorithms above need *2q + 1* passes over *A*. But many matrices are *streams*: sensor feeds, simulation time-steps, or datasets too large to revisit. Tropp, Yurtsever, Udell, and Cevher [5] developed *single-view* algorithms that see *A* exactly once, maintaining two sketches as the data flows by:

$$Y = A\Omega \quad \text{(range sketch)}, \qquad W = \Psi A \quad \text{(co-range sketch)},$$

with independent test matrices *Ω* (*n*-by-*k*) and *Ψ* (*ℓ*-by-*m*). From these alone, the *generalized Nyström approximation*

$$\hat{A} = Y\,(\Psi Y)^{\dagger}\,W = (A\Omega)\,(\Psi A\Omega)^{\dagger}\,(\Psi A) \tag{4}$$

reconstructs a rank-*k* approximation whose error nearly matches the two-pass randomized SVD [5]. The formula is algebraically natural: *ΨY = ΨAΩ* is the *core sketch*, and (4) is the oblique projection of *A* onto the range of *Y* along the co-range captured by *Ψ*. For positive semidefinite *A*, taking *Ψ = Ω** yields the classical Nyström approximation *K ≈ K_{nm} K_{mm}^{-1} K_{mn}* of Williams and Seeger [7], which is automatically PSD — a structural guarantee that matters for kernel methods.

The analysis in [5] is notable for its *a priori* parameter selection: the error bounds are informative enough that a user can choose sketch sizes to meet a target accuracy *before* seeing the data, a rarity in randomized algorithms. Fixed-rank variants truncate (4) to exactly rank *k*, and stabilized implementations with pivoted factorizations handle the ill-conditioning of the core sketch in finite precision.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The theoretical centerpiece is a *proof sketch* of the relative-error guarantee, showing how matrix concentration does the heavy lifting. Consider the subspace embedding step: we need the sampled Gram matrix to be an approximate isometry on the top-*k* right singular subspace. Write

$$G = \sum_{t=1}^{c} X_t, \qquad X_t = \frac{1}{c\,p_{j_t}}\, v_{j_t} v_{j_t}^T,$$

where *j_t* is the sampled column index and *v_j^T* is the *j*-th row of *V_k*. Then *E[G] = I_k*, each *X_t* is PSD with *λ_max(X_t) ≤ k/(cβ)* for a quality parameter *β* of the sampling probabilities, and the *matrix Chernoff bound* [3] gives

> **Theorem: (Matrix Chernoff, Tropp).** For independent PSD matrices *X_t* with *λ_max(X_t) ≤ B* almost surely, and *μ_min ≤ λ_min(E[ΣX_t])*,
>
> $$\mathbb{P}\left\{\lambda_{\min}\!\left(\sum_t X_t\right) \le (1-\delta)\mu_{\min}\right\} \le d\left[\frac{e^{-\delta}}{(1-\delta)^{1-\delta}}\right]^{\mu_{\min}/B}, \quad \delta \in (0,1).$$

Choosing *c = O(k log(k/δ)/(βε²))* makes both tails small, so *(1 − ε)I ⪯ G ⪯ (1 + ε)I* with probability at least *1 − δ*. The structural result of [4] then converts this *subspace embedding* into the *relative-error* bound (3): if the sampled columns span a subspace on which *A* acts almost isometrically, the projection *CC⁺A* cannot be much worse than the optimal *A_k*.

For the randomized SVD, the analogous argument uses the *matrix Bernstein inequality* [3]:

> **Theorem: (Matrix Bernstein, Tropp).** For independent zero-mean matrices *X_t* with *‖X_t‖ ≤ B* and variance statistic *σ² = ‖Σ E[X_t X_t^*]‖*,
>
> $$\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\sum_t X_t\right\| \ge t\right\} \le 2d\,\exp\left(\frac{-t^2/2}{\sigma^2 + Bt/3}\right).$$

Empirically, the story is one of consistent over-performance relative to theory. On the SuiteSparse matrix collection and on synthetic matrices with controlled spectral decay, the randomized SVD with *p = 10* and *q = 2* typically attains errors within a few percent of optimal — far better than the pessimistic constants in the theorems suggest [1]. For kernel matrices, Nyström with leverage-score sampling (the *Gittens–Mahoney* analysis [7]) dominates uniform sampling precisely when the spectrum decays slowly, i.e., exactly when the problem is hardest. And in the streaming setting, the single-view Nyström method of [5] tracks the two-pass baseline to within small constant factors on scientific simulation data, validating the single-pass paradigm where re-reading the data is physically impossible.

## 6 Limitations

The guarantees above are real but conditional, and the conditions deserve scrutiny.

**Coherence and adversarial spectra.** All sampling-based bounds degrade when the matrix is *coherent* — when leverage scores concentrate on a few columns — unless the sampling distribution adapts accordingly. Exact leverage scores cost as much as the SVD itself; approximate scores introduce a chicken-and-egg problem that is solvable but delicate [4]. For matrices with flat spectra (*σ_j* nearly constant), no rank-*k* approximation is meaningful, and randomized methods correctly but unhelpfully report large residual error.

**Constants and the theory–practice gap.** The sketch dimensions sufficient for the theorems are routinely several times larger than what works in practice. Practitioners use *p = 5* and *q = 1* as defaults with *a posteriori* estimators [1], but those certify the computed approximation, not optimality.

**Numerical stability.** The power scheme without intermediate orthonormalization squares the condition number at each iteration; the pseudoinverse in the Nyström formula (4) amplifies noise in the core sketch. Stabilized variants exist [5], but they complicate the clean theoretical picture, and mixed-precision or very ill-conditioned inputs can still break the textbook implementations.

**Spectral vs. Frobenius, and beyond.** Relative-error bounds in Frobenius norm are now standard, but *spectral-norm* relative error is strictly harder and requires larger sketches or stronger assumptions. Schatten-*p* norms and robust outlier-tolerant variants each demand their own analysis [2], and sketching lower bounds show some of these goals are genuinely more expensive.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Randomized numerical linear algebra has matured from a collection of tricks into a principled discipline. Its recipe — *sketch randomly, solve deterministically, certify with concentration* — now underpins fast PCA at industrial scale (the randomized SVD is the default solver in major machine-learning libraries), single-pass processing of simulation data that cannot be stored [5], and kernel methods that scale to millions of points via Nyström approximations [7]. The mathematics is unusually honest: the same concentration inequalities that prove the algorithms also delineate their limits, through matching lower bounds on sketch dimension [2].

Two frontiers stand out. First, *structure-aware sketching*: exploiting Kronecker or Toeplitz structure in the sketch itself, so the random map is as cheap as the data allows. Second, *adaptive* algorithms that attain the information-theoretic limits with practical constants, closing the theory–practice gap that still separates the theorems from the defaults.
