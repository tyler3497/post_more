# Solid-State Batteries: Ceramic Electrolytes, Interfacial Resistance, and Dendrite Nucleation

## Abstract

Ceramic solid electrolytes promise lithium-metal anodes with energy densities above 400 watt-hours per kilogram, yet two coupled failure modes constrain deployment: interfacial resistance at solid-solid contacts and lithium dendrite penetration through nominally stiff ceramics. This thesis synthesizes the chemistry, mechanics, and electrochemistry of ceramic electrolytes, spanning sulfide superionic conductors above 10 millisiemens per centimeter, garnet-type LLZO oxides with wide electrochemical windows, and NASICON phosphates. It derives the Monroe-Newman elastic stability criterion, evaluates the Porz electrochemomechanical crack model against measured critical current densities, and decomposes the lithium-electrolyte interfacial resistance into charge-transfer, space-charge, grain-boundary, and interphase terms. Reviewing computed phase-stability diagrams and operando microscopy, we argue that mechanical stiffness alone cannot prevent shorting: interfacial chemical decomposition and defect-driven stress intensification dominate. Design rules for engineered interlayers, controlled stack pressure, and defect-free densification are extracted, with quantitative performance targets and open questions for manufacturing at scale.

## 1 Introduction

The lithium-metal anode, with a theoretical specific capacity of 3860 milliampere-hours per gram and the most negative standard potential of any electrode material, is the clearest route to doubling the energy density of rechargeable batteries [3]. Conventional cells cannot exploit it: liquid carbonate electrolytes react with freshly deposited lithium and fail catastrophically when dendrites bridge the separator. Ceramic solid electrolytes were proposed as the enabling technology that breaks this deadlock, combining ionic conductivities competitive with liquids, unity lithium transference numbers, and shear moduli orders of magnitude above those of polymers [6].

The experimental record, however, tells a more complicated story. Sulfide ceramics such as Li10GeP2S12 (LGPS) reach room-temperature conductivities of 12 millisiemens per centimeter, exceeding many liquid electrolytes [1], and high-power all-solid-state cells built on sulfide conductors demonstrate rate capabilities that rival conventional lithium-ion batteries [8]. Yet these same cells suffer from interfacial resistances of hundreds of ohm square centimeters and short-circuit at current densities far below those tolerated by liquid cells. Garnet-type Li7La3Zr2O12 (LLZO), celebrated for its stability against molten lithium and its stiffness, still grows lithium filaments through its grain boundaries at modest current densities [2]. The community has therefore shifted from asking *which* ceramic conducts best to asking *why* stiff, conductive ceramics fail at their interfaces, a reframing articulated most forcefully by Janek and Zeier in their analysis of interfacial chemistry and chemo-mechanical coupling in solid-state batteries [3].

This thesis provides a unified treatment of the three pillars of the problem: the ceramic electrolytes themselves, the resistance budget of their interfaces, and the nucleation physics of lithium dendrites in solid media. Section 2 surveys the electrolyte families; Section 3 describes the experimental and computational methods by which they are characterized; Section 4 develops the deep theory of transport, interfaces, and fracture; Section 5 compiles the empirical record; Section 6 states the limitations of current understanding; and Section 7 distills design rules.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The taxonomy of inorganic solid electrolytes

Inorganic lithium-ion conductors divide naturally into **sulfides**, **oxides**, and **halide-adjacent** families, each with a distinct compromise between conductivity, stability, and processability [6].

*Sulfide electrolytes* exploit the high polarizability of the sulfide anion, which softens the lattice, lowers migration barriers, and enables the paddle-wheel coupling between anion rotation and lithium hopping [4]. The landmark material is LGPS, reported by Kamaya and co-workers in 2011: a three-dimensional framework of (Ge0.5P0.5)S4 tetrahedra with lithium distributed over LiS4 tetrahedra and interstitial sites, yielding 12 millisiemens per centimeter at room temperature and an all-solid-state LiCoO2/In cell with excellent reversibility [1]. The argyrodite family, typified by Li6PS5Cl, trades some conductivity for dramatically simpler mechanochemical synthesis and has become the workhorse of prototype pouch cells [6]. Sulfides are soft (Young's moduli near 20 gigapascals) and cold-pressable into dense pellets — but they hydrolyze in moist air to release hydrogen sulfide and are thermodynamically unstable against both lithium metal and high-voltage cathodes [6].

*Oxide electrolytes* offer the complementary compromise: lower conductivity but superior chemical and thermal stability. The garnet Li7La3Zr2O12, reported by Murugan, Thangadurai, and Weppner in 2007, exhibits a total (bulk plus grain-boundary) conductivity near 3 times 10 to the power minus 4 siemens per centimeter, stability against molten lithium, and no detectable phase transition between room temperature and 900 degrees Celsius [2]. Its garnet framework of LaO8 dodecahedra and ZrO6 octahedra hosts lithium in tetrahedral and octahedral interstices connected into three-dimensional conduction pathways; the highly conductive cubic polymorph is stabilized by aliovalent dopants such as aluminum that introduce lithium vacancies [2]. NASICON-type phosphates such as Li1.3Al0.3Ti1.7(PO4)3 reach the millisiemens-per-centimeter range but suffer reduction of Ti4+ against lithium metal, while perovskite Li0.33La0.557TiO3 shows excellent bulk conductivity throttled by resistive grain boundaries [6]. Thin-film LiPON, with only microsiemens per centimeter, survives thousands of cycles against lithium because its amorphous network forms a self-limiting passivation layer — a hint that *kinetic* stability can matter more than raw conductivity [6].

### 2.2 What sets the conductivity

Bachman and co-workers' systematic review identified the physical parameters governing ion diffusion in these frameworks: the migrating ion's size and charge, the bottleneck dimensions of the diffusion channel, the polarizability of the anion sublattice, and — remarkably — the frequencies of low-energy optical phonons, which correlate with activation energy across many structural families [4]. Two design principles emerge. First, **frustrated energy landscapes** with many partially occupied, energetically similar lithium sites (as in LGPS and cubic LLZO) flatten the migration barrier. Second, **soft, polarizable anion frameworks** (sulfides over oxides) reduce the electrostatic penalty of the hopping transition state. Grain boundaries then subtract from the bulk value through a brick-layer series resistance that can dominate the total in poorly sintered oxides [2].

## 3 Methodology

Characterizing a ceramic electrolyte demands coupled electrical, structural, and mechanical probes.

1. **Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS).** Pellets are measured from megahertz to millihertz; the high-frequency arc is assigned to bulk transport and the intermediate arc to grain boundaries via the brick-layer model, yielding separate bulk and grain-boundary conductivities and their activation energies from Arrhenius fits.
2. **First-principles computation.** Density functional theory with nudged-elastic-band calculations gives migration barriers along candidate diffusion paths, while grand-potential phase diagrams predict interfacial decomposition products at applied lithium chemical potentials — the computational core of interface-stability screening [9].
3. **Galvanostatic critical-current-density (CCD) testing.** Symmetric lithium/electrolyte/lithium cells are cycled with stepwise increasing current density under controlled uniaxial stack pressure until the voltage collapses, marking dendrite-induced shorting [10].
4. **Operando microscopy and tomography.** Optical cells and X-ray tomography track filament nucleation sites, revealing that penetration initiates at pre-existing surface flaws rather than at atomically flat interfaces [5].
5. **Continuum electrochemomechanical modeling.** Linear stability analysis of a perturbed lithium/electrolyte interface under elastic deformation predicts whether protrusions amplify or decay — the Monroe-Newman framework [7] — while cohesive-zone fracture models couple plating pressure to crack extension [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Ceramic electrolyte families and their trade-offs

No single ceramic dominates all metrics; each family occupies a different point on the conductivity-stability-processability triangle.

| Electrolyte | Family | Room-temp. conductivity | Li-metal stability | Key weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Li10GeP2S12 (LGPS) | Sulfide | ~12 mS/cm [1] | Decomposes (Li2S, Li3P, Li-Ge alloys) | Moisture sensitivity, Ge cost |
| Li6PS5Cl (argyrodite) | Sulfide | mS/cm range [6] | Decomposes, passivating | Interfacial resistance vs. cathodes |
| Li7La3Zr2O12 (LLZO) | Oxide garnet | ~10^-4 S/cm [2] | Stable vs. molten Li [2] | Brittle, high-temp. sintering, air reactivity |
| Li1.3Al0.3Ti1.7(PO4)3 (LATP) | NASICON oxide | ~10^-3 S/cm [6] | Ti4+ reduced by Li | Chemical instability at anode |
| LiPON | Oxynitride glass | ~10^-6 S/cm [6] | Excellent, self-passivating | Only viable as thin film |

The table makes the central tension quantitative: the most conductive ceramics are the least stable against lithium, and the most stable are the least conductive. Sulfides win on power — Kato and colleagues demonstrated high-power all-solid-state cells on sulfide conductors operating from minus 30 to 100 degrees Celsius [8] — while garnets win on the anode interface but demand ceramic processing near 1200 degrees Celsius that is difficult to scale [2].

### 4.2 Ionic transport: hopping, correlation, and the grain-boundary bottleneck

In crystalline ceramics, lithium migrates by **thermally activated hopping** between adjacent sites, with the ionic conductivity following an Arrhenius law whose activation energy reflects the saddle-point energy of the bottleneck. In the best sulfides, correlated multi-ion motion and paddle-wheel anion dynamics lower effective barriers to the 0.2 electron-volt range [4]. In cubic LLZO, the three-dimensional network of partially occupied tetrahedral and octahedral sites gives activation energies near 0.3 electron-volts, while the ordered tetragonal polymorph, with fully occupied low-energy sites, shows barriers nearly twice as large and conductivities lower by two orders of magnitude [2].

A short numerical illustration clarifies how steeply conductivity depends on these barriers:

```python
import math

KB_EV_PER_K = 8.617333e-5  # Boltzmann constant in eV/K

def arrhenius(temp_k, prefactor, barrier_ev):
    """Ionic conductivity from an Arrhenius hopping model."""
    return prefactor * math.exp(-barrier_ev / (KB_EV_PER_K * temp_k))

def stability_ratio(shear_electrolyte_gpa, shear_lithium_gpa=3.0):
    """Monroe-Newman style elastic stability ratio; > ~2 predicts suppression."""
    return shear_electrolyte_gpa / shear_lithium_gpa

materials = (
    ("LGPS-type sulfide", 0.22),
    ("cubic LLZO garnet", 0.30),
    ("tetragonal LLZO", 0.50),
)
for label, barrier in materials:
    sigma = arrhenius(298.0, 1.0e4, barrier)
    print(label, "relative conductivity:", round(sigma, 6))

for label, modulus in (("sulfide", 20.0), ("garnet", 60.0)):
    print(label, "stability ratio:", round(stability_ratio(modulus), 1))
```

Raising the barrier from 0.22 to 0.50 electron-volts collapses the conductivity by roughly four orders of magnitude — the quantitative reason that site disorder and soft lattices are prized [4]. Note also that both model electrolytes satisfy the elastic stability ratio by a wide margin, foreshadowing the puzzle of Section 4.4: *mechanical stiffness, on paper, should already be sufficient.*

Grain boundaries impose the second tax on transport. In polycrystalline oxides, space-charge depletion of lithium vacancies adjacent to the boundary core, segregated impurities, and misaligned conduction channels combine into a boundary resistance that can exceed the bulk by orders of magnitude [6]. Aluminum doping of LLZO is doubly beneficial: it stabilizes the cubic phase through lithium-vacancy creation and can form a lithium-conducting glassy film at the boundaries that partially heals the resistance [2].

### 4.3 Interfacial resistance: charge transfer, space-charge layers, and reactive interphases

The area-specific resistance (ASR) of a lithium/ceramic interface is a series sum of at least four physically distinct terms: (i) the **charge-transfer resistance** of the lithium redox reaction at a solid-solid contact of limited true contact area; (ii) the **space-charge resistance** from lithium depletion or accumulation layers extending nanometers into the electrolyte; (iii) the **grain-boundary resistance** of the near-interface ceramic; and (iv) the resistance of any **interphase** grown by chemical reaction between the electrode and the electrolyte [6].

Term (iv) is frequently decisive. First-principles interface screening by Richards, Miara, Wang, Kim, and Ceder showed that most high-conductivity electrolytes — LGPS included — are thermodynamically unstable against lithium metal, decomposing into mixtures such as lithium sulfide, lithium phosphide, and lithium-germanium alloys [9]. Whether decomposition is fatal depends on product electronic conductivity: insulating products (lithium fluoride, lithium oxide) self-limit into a passivating interphase, while conductive products (lithium-germanium alloys) sustain a **mixed conducting interphase** that grows continuously [9]. This computed taxonomy explains why sulfides, despite their conductivity, develop large and growing interfacial resistances against lithium, whereas LLZO — predicted and observed to be stable — maintains a cleaner interface [2][9].

Cathode-side interfaces face the mirror-image problem: at high potentials, sulfides oxidize and oxide cathodes react with the electrolyte, a degradation pathway that Janek and Zeier identified as the central chemical challenge of the field, alongside the chemo-mechanical fracture driven by electrode volume change [3]. Engineered coatings — lithium niobate or lithium titanate buffers at the cathode, artificial lithium fluoride or lithium nitride layers at the anode — function by inserting a kinetically stable, ion-conducting phase into the grand-potential diagram, narrowing the driving force for decomposition [3][9].

### 4.4 Dendrite nucleation and propagation: from elastic stability to electrochemomechanical fracture

> **Theorem (Monroe-Newman elastic stability).** Consider a lithium/electrolyte interface with a sinusoidal perturbation under elastic deformation. If the shear modulus of the electrolyte exceeds approximately twice the shear modulus of lithium metal (about 3 gigapascals), surface-tension-like elastic forces penalize protrusion growth faster than deposition kinetics can amplify it, and the planar interface is linearly stable [7].

The theorem predicts that any ceramic, with shear moduli in the tens of gigapascals, should be immune to dendrites. Experiment falsifies the naive reading. Porz and colleagues plated lithium against four inorganic electrolytes — amorphous lithium thiophosphate glass, polycrystalline beta-Li3PS4, and polycrystalline and single-crystalline garnet — and observed lithium infiltrating at surface defects above a critical current density *independent of the electrolyte's shear modulus* [5]. Their proposed mechanism replaces elastic instability with **electrochemomechanical fracture**: lithium plating into a pre-existing surface flaw generates a hydrostatic pressure at the flaw tip; when the resulting stress intensity exceeds the ceramic's fracture toughness, the crack extends, fresh lithium plates into the new volume, and the filament advances by repeated crack-plating cycles. Shorting is then controlled not by modulus but by *defect size, defect density, and fracture toughness* [5].

The two pictures reconcile once their domains are distinguished. Monroe-Newman stability governs **nucleation at a pristine interface** — relevant to thin films and single crystals, where dendrite resistance is indeed excellent [5][7]. The Porz mechanism governs **propagation from flaws** — the practically relevant case for polycrystalline pellets and composite cathodes, where grain boundaries, pores, and polishing scratches provide ready-made notches [5]. Supporting evidence includes the observation that single-crystalline garnet resists penetration far better than polycrystalline garnet of identical composition, implicating grain boundaries as both mechanical weak planes and fast diffusion paths [5].

A further subtlety is **void formation during stripping**. When lithium is removed faster than creep and diffusion can replenish it, vacancies coalesce into voids at the interface, constricting the true contact area; on the subsequent plating half-cycle, the entire current funnels through the remaining contact points, locally exceeding the critical current density and nucleating filaments [10]. This stripping-plating asymmetry explains why symmetric-cell CCD values depend on stack pressure, temperature, and prior cycling history rather than being intrinsic material constants.

### 4.5 Mitigation: interlayers, stack pressure, and defect engineering

Four mitigation families follow directly from the diagnosis:

- **Lithiophilic interlayers.** Nanoscale gold, silver, or silicon films alloy with incoming lithium, spreading the nucleation overpotential laterally and converting point nucleation into planar growth; ion-conducting artificial interphases such as lithium nitride or lithium fluoride block electronic leakage that would otherwise feed filament tips [3].
- **Stack pressure in the Goldilocks zone.** Moderate uniaxial pressure (on the order of a few megapascals) maintains intimate solid-solid contact, suppresses void formation during stripping, and measurably raises the critical current density; excessive pressure, however, drives lithium creep into flaws and can fracture brittle pellets — the optimum must be engineered, not maximized [10].
- **Defect elimination by processing.** Hot pressing, field-assisted sintering, and grain-size engineering reduce the flaw population that the Porz mechanism feeds on; dense, large-grained ceramics systematically outperform porous fine-grained ones at identical chemistry [5][6].
- **Chemical passivation by design.** Selecting electrolytes (or coatings) whose decomposition products are electronically insulating converts the reactive interphase into a self-limiting passivation film, as the computational screening of Richards and colleagues prescribes [9].

## 5 Empirical Results

The literature record, compiled across sulfide and oxide systems, supports the coupled chemo-mechanical picture.

| Observation | System | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 12 mS/cm at 25 C, reversible LiCoO2/In cell | LGPS [1] | Sulfide conductivity can exceed liquids |
| High-power ASSB, minus 30 to 100 C operation | Sulfide cells [8] | Power density viable with sulfides |
| CCD independent of shear modulus; flaw-driven infiltration | 4 electrolytes [5] | Fracture, not elastic instability, controls shorting |
| CCD rises with stack pressure to several MPa | Li/SSE/Li symmetric cells [10] | Void suppression dominates at low pressure |
| LGPS vs. Li decomposes to Li2S, Li3P, Li-Ge alloys | Computed interfaces [9] | Mixed conducting interphase grows |
| LLZO stable vs. molten Li; ~10^-4 S/cm total | Garnet [2] | Oxides trade conductivity for anode stability |
| Cubic phase requires dopant-induced Li vacancies | Al-doped LLZO [2] | Site disorder is the conductivity lever |

Two quantitative regularities stand out. First, reported critical current densities for polycrystalline garnets cluster in the tenths of a milliampere per square centimeter at room temperature without stack pressure, rising toward a few milliamperes per square centimeter in hot-pressed or pressure-assisted cells — values that track processing quality more closely than bulk conductivity [5][10]. Second, the interfacial ASR of a pristine lithium/garnet interface is dominated not by charge transfer but by surface contamination: air exposure forms lithium hydroxide and lithium carbonate films that render the surface lithiophobic, and their removal collapses the resistance by orders of magnitude [6]. Both regularities point the same way: *the interface, not the bulk crystal, is the device.*

## 6 Limitations

This synthesis inherits the limitations of its sources. **Computational phase diagrams** are zero-kelvin, equilibrium constructions; they predict the direction of interfacial reactions but not their rates, and sluggish kinetics can kinetically stabilize interfaces that thermodynamics condemns — the LiPON paradox [9]. **Critical current density** remains a protocol-dependent quantity: ramp rate, stack pressure, temperature, electrode area, and surface preparation all shift the measured value, making cross-laboratory comparison treacherous [10]. **Sulfide moisture sensitivity** imposes dry-room processing, and the hydrogen sulfide hazard constrains manufacturing more than any electrochemical metric [6]. **Oxide brittleness** means that the stack pressures beneficial for contact can fracture thin separators, coupling the mechanical design of the cell to the fracture statistics of the ceramic [5]. Finally, most dendrite observations come from model symmetric cells; in full cells with composite cathodes, the cathode-side chemo-mechanics — particle fracture, contact loss, oxidative decomposition — interact with anode-side filament growth in ways no single experiment yet captures [3].

## 7 Conclusion

Three conclusions survive contact with the evidence. **First**, ceramic electrolytes have solved the bulk-conductivity problem: sulfides match or exceed liquid electrolytes, and even oxides reach device-relevant values when grain boundaries are engineered [1][2][4]. **Second**, mechanical stiffness is necessary but not sufficient for dendrite suppression; the Monroe-Newman criterion correctly describes pristine interfaces, but real ceramics fail by electrochemomechanical fracture from defects, a mechanism invisible to modulus-based screening [5][7]. **Third**, interfacial chemistry sets the resistance budget: thermodynamically driven decomposition into mixed conducting interphases, space-charge depletion, and contamination films dominate the impedance that cells actually experience [3][6][9].

The design rules that follow are correspondingly coupled rather than single-variable: pair a conductive sulfide or a stable garnet with an electronically insulating artificial interphase; densify to eliminate the flaw population that feeds crack-driven infiltration; apply stack pressure inside the window where voids close but creep does not begin; and validate full cells, not symmetric models, under realistic pressure and temperature [3][5][9][10]. The quantitative targets are now clear — interfacial ASR below 10 ohm square centimeters, critical current densities above 3 milliamperes per square centimeter at room temperature without extreme pressure, and dry-room-compatible processing. Meeting all three in a manufacturable cell remains the defining challenge of the solid-state battery field.
