# Timely and Differential Dataflow: Naiad's Progress Tracking, Partially Ordered Timestamps, and Iterative Stream Computation

## Abstract

Timely dataflow and differential dataflow jointly reimagine large-scale computation as a single substrate for streaming, batch, and iterative processing. Introduced through the Naiad system at SOSP 2013, timely dataflow equips dataflow graphs with structured cycles and a distributed progress-tracking protocol that lets stateful vertices consume and produce records asynchronously while receiving *notifications* exactly when all inputs for a logical time have arrived [1][2]. Differential dataflow extends this model with a generalized notion of *difference* over partially ordered timestamps, enabling incremental maintenance of arbitrarily nested iterative computations — including continuously updated strongly connected components and recursive Datalog queries [3][6]. This thesis develops the foundations of partially ordered logical time, the pointstamp-based progress protocol, and the arrangement machinery of differential operators; surveys Naiad's empirical results — sub-millisecond iterations, sub-second batch updates, interactive graph queries — contrasts the model with Flink and Spark Structured Streaming, and examines Lean formalizations that mechanically verify stream-correctness of incremental reduce [5]. We close with a candid assessment of limitations: implementation complexity, memory pressure from traces, and the mismatch between mainstream streaming engines and lattice-structured iterative logic.

## 1. Introduction

Most data-processing systems of the late 2000s forced programmers into a false choice. Batch engines such as MapReduce and Dryad offered high throughput at the cost of latency measured in minutes; streaming engines offered millisecond responsiveness but demanded per-record coordination for any operator needing global state, making aggregation and iteration painful [1]. Graph engines such as Pregel introduced bulk-synchronous iteration but treated streaming input as an afterthought, re-running entire computations or resorting to ad hoc delta propagation [2].

Naiad, developed at Microsoft Research and presented at SOSP 2013, argued that these were not three problems but one [1]. Its central insight was that *coordination* — not data movement — is the dominant cost in low-latency distributed computation, and that a sufficiently informative logical clock can tell each vertex, with negligible global communication, when it is safe to act. The resulting model, **timely dataflow**, supports directed dataflow graphs with *structured cycles* (loops with well-defined entry and exit points), stateful vertices that emit records without global synchronization, and a notification mechanism that fires exactly when a vertex has received every record destined for a given logical time.

Built atop timely dataflow, **differential dataflow** generalizes classical incremental computation. Traditional incremental systems maintain differences `δA` between successive versions `A_t` of a collection, requiring versions to form a total order. Differential computation lifts this requirement: versions may be *partially ordered*, so that an update to the input and an advance of an iteration counter yield incomparable differences that can be processed independently and combined exactly when their causal structure demands it [3]. This single generalization unlocks incrementally maintained iteration — recursion inside streaming, Datalog on live graphs, and machine learning over changing data — with asymptotic work proportional to the size of the *change*, not the size of the data.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the intellectual lineage: Dryad's DAGs, BSP-style graph processing, stream processing's tension between latency and coordination, and incremental computation. Section 3 presents the methodology: the timely dataflow execution model, pointstamps, the progress-tracking protocol, and the differential operator suite. Section 4 dives deep into partially ordered time, progress tracking, arrangements, and recursive Datalog. Section 5 surveys empirical results and proof artifacts, including Naiad's measured performance and recent Lean formalizations of differential reduce. Section 6 states limitations honestly. Section 7 concludes with the model's legacy in modern streaming engines.

## 2. Background

### From Batch DAGs to Streaming

Dryad (2007) and its LINQ-based front end DryadLINQ demonstrated that arbitrary acyclic dataflow graphs could be compiled, scheduled, and executed at warehouse scale with a simple vertex/edge/channel abstraction [1]. Naiad inherits this vocabulary directly: computations are graphs of *vertices* connected by *edges*, with records flowing along edges in timestamp order. The decisive break is that Naiad's graphs may contain *cycles*, provided they are structured as nested loop contexts with ingress and egress operators that translate timestamps between nesting levels.

### The Streaming Coordination Problem

Consider a distributed word-count over an unbounded stream. A vertex emitting a count must be *sure* no more records for the current epoch will arrive — certainty that requires coordination: watermarks in Flink, micro-batch barriers in Spark Streaming, or punctuation in earlier systems. Batch engines embrace coordination (barriers between stages); pure streaming engines avoid it but then cannot easily implement blocking operators. The timely dataflow observation is that coordination need not be global or synchronous: it suffices to track, for every point in the graph, which logical times might still produce messages — a *frontier* of outstanding work — and to inform each vertex precisely when its frontier advances past the time it cares about [1][2].

### Incremental Computation and Its Limits

Classical frameworks (self-adjusting computation, incremental view maintenance in databases) assume a *total* order of versions: time marches forward, each version building on the last. Iteration breaks this story. When iteration `i` of a loop consumes input version `t`, the two-dimensional coordinate `(t, i)` has no natural total order respecting both dimensions' causality — a fact differential dataflow turns from an obstacle into its organizing principle.

| System | Execution model | Time structure | Iteration | Incrementality |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MapReduce / Dryad | Batch DAG | Total order (stages) | Driver-side loop | None |
| Pregel / Giraph | BSP supersteps | Total order (steps) | Native (supersteps) | Checkpoint-based |
| Flink | Streaming dataflow | Watermarks (total) | Loop edges + feedback | Savepoints / state |
| Spark Structured Streaming | Micro-batch | Total order (batches) | Limited | Stateful operators |
| **Naiad / Timely** | Timely dataflow | **Partial order (product of coordinates)** | **Structured nested loops** | **Differential (arbitrary)** |
| **Differential DF** | Timely dataflow | **Partial order + differences** | **Incrementally maintained** | **Native, nested** |

## 3. Methodology

### The Timely Dataflow Execution Model

A timely dataflow computation is a directed graph of operators (*vertices*) connected by typed channels (*edges*). Every message carries a **timestamp** drawn from a partially ordered set chosen by the programmer: for a single streaming epoch context it may be `ℕ`; inside a loop nested within epoch `e`, it becomes the product `(e, i) ∈ ℕ × ℕ`, ordered coordinate-wise. Loop ingress operators map outer times to inner times (appending an iteration coordinate initialized to zero); loop egress operators strip the coordinate, so times crossing a loop boundary remain comparable [1][2].

Execution is fully asynchronous and sharded across *workers*. Each worker owns a partition of every vertex and delivers messages and notifications to its vertices. Two primitives define the programming model:

- **Send** `(data, time)`: enqueue a record along an edge. Messages on an edge arrive in timestamp order but otherwise asynchronously.
- **Notify** `(time)`: request a callback when the worker can prove that no future message with timestamp `≤ time` will arrive at this vertex. Notifications support batching: an operator aggregates all records at time `t` and emits exactly once when the frontier passes `t` [2].

> **Theorem (Notification soundness):** If the progress-tracking protocol reports frontier `F` at vertex `v`, then no message with timestamp `t ≤ F` (under the partial order) will ever be delivered to `v` in the future. Hence a notification for time `t` fires only after all records at times `≤ t` have been observed. *(Murray et al. [1], §3.)*

The subtlety is *distributed* progress tracking: each worker must compute a conservative approximation of globally outstanding work from purely local observations plus periodic coordination.

### Differential Dataflow: Differences over Partial Orders

Differential dataflow models every collection as the accumulation of *differences*: for each time `t` in a partial order, `δA_t` is defined so that `A_t = Σ_{s ≤ t} δA_s`, with differences retained rather than folded into the collection as in classical incremental systems [3]. Operators are lifted to act on differences. A stateless operator such as `map` applies pointwise; `join` must consider pairs of differences whose times have a common upper bound; `reduce` recomputes the aggregate exactly at those times where the accumulated input changed — the *join-closure* of input and output times, recently formalized as the `Reached` set in Lean [5].

The implementation substrate is the **arrangement**: a shared, indexed, multiversioned trace of a collection's differences, compacted over time and readable by many operators and many queries simultaneously. Arrangements amortize the cost of indexing across all consumers and are the reason differential dataflow can serve dozens of concurrent iterative queries over one live graph [6].

```rust
// Differential dataflow (Rust, timelydataflow/differential-dataflow [6]):
// incrementally updated reachability over a live edge set.
let edges = input.to_collection(&mut scope);          // Collection<G, (u32,u32)>
let reach = edges.iterate(|reach| {
    edges.enter(&reach.scope())                       // enter iteration scope
         .map(|(s, d)| (d, s))                        // reverse edges
         .join_map(&reach, |(d, s), _| (s, d))        // join on destination
         .concat(&reach)                              // union with prior state
         .distinct()                                  // consolidate differences
});
```

Because timestamps are partially ordered, iteration `(t, i)` and input updates `(t′, 0)` for `t′ > t` are *incomparable*: the system processes them concurrently without serializing one behind the other, and combines them exactly where causality requires. This is the operational content of "nested iteration over streaming input" that earlier systems approximated with checkpoints or driver loops.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Partially Ordered Logical Time

The choice of timestamp lattice is the programmer's principal lever. A single streaming context uses a totally ordered epoch type; each nested loop extends it by product with `ℕ`, yielding coordinates like `(epoch, iter₁, iter₂)` for doubly nested loops [1]. The partial order is coordinate-wise:

```
(t, i) ≤ (t′, i′)   ⟺   t ≤ t′ ∧ i ≤ i′
```

Consequences are profound. Two updates `(t, i)` and `(t′, i′)` with `t < t′` but `i > i′` are *incomparable*: neither causally precedes the other, so no coordination is required between them. Frontiers — downward-closed sets of times summarizing "work possibly outstanding" — are represented as antichains, and the progress protocol manipulates them with lattice operations (meet/join) rather than scalar comparisons. The product construction is what lets iteration and streaming coexist without a global barrier: each loop level advances its own coordinate independently, and the egress operator's timestamp translation preserves exactly the causal relationships the outer context needs [2].

### 4.2 The Distributed Progress Tracking Protocol

Each worker maintains, for every edge, a count of sent versus received messages indexed by *pointstamp* — the pair `(location, timestamp)` identifying where in the graph a message currently sits or is destined [1]. A pointstamp `(ℓ, t)` on an edge from vertex `u` to vertex `v` records that a message at time `t` is in flight toward `v` (or that `u` may still produce one, tracked via *implications* declared by operators: e.g., a loop ingress implies future messages at `(t, 0)` whenever it receives `(t)`).

Workers periodically exchange summaries of their outstanding pointstamps — not the pointstamps themselves, but compacted frontier information — and each computes the set of pointstamps that could still transitively reach each notification request. When no outstanding pointstamp could produce a message at time `≤ t` for vertex `v`, the worker delivers `OnNotify(t)` [1][2].

> **Theorem (Progress liveness):** Under fair message delivery, the frontier reported at every vertex advances monotonically, and every requested notification is eventually delivered. The protocol's coordination cost is proportional to the *graph structure* and the *number of distinct outstanding times*, not to the data volume. *(Murray et al. [1], §4.)*

In practice this means: a streaming word-count with one outstanding epoch pays essentially zero coordination, while a deeply nested iterative graph algorithm pays coordination proportional to its loop structure — precisely the "lightweight coordination when required" of the paper's central claim [1].

### 4.3 Arrangements: Shared Indexed Traces

Differential dataflow's performance rests on **arrangements** — multiversioned, indexed, incrementally maintained traces of collections, shared across operators and even across queries [6]. An arrangement for a keyed collection stores batches of `(key, value, time, diff)` updates in a merge-friendly immutable structure (a relative of LSM trees), supports seeks by key, and exposes *traces* that let a new query attach to a live computation and receive exactly the historical differences it missed, without recomputation.

The operator suite — `map`, `filter`, `join`, `reduce`/`count`/`threshold`, `iterate`, `distinct`, `consolidate` — is implemented against arrangements wherever possible. The notoriously subtle operator is `reduce`: it must determine *which times* to re-evaluate, namely joins of input and output times, and do so incrementally. The 2026 Lean formalization in the differential-dataflow repository proves end-to-end *stream correctness* of the incremental reduce model: every run emits corrections that accumulate to the true reduction at every finalized time, with the single hypothesis `f 0 = 0` [5]. This is a rare instance of a production streaming operator accompanied by a machine-checked correctness argument.

### 4.4 Recursive Queries, Datalog, and Nested Iteration

Differential dataflow's headline application is **incrementally maintained recursion**. Datalog — the canonical language of recursive queries — compiles naturally: each rule becomes a join/map dataflow fragment, and recursion becomes `iterate` [3][6]. Classical Datalog evaluation (semi-naive) assumes a static extensional database; differential evaluation maintains the least fixed point *under updates to the database itself*, with work proportional to the change in the fixed point rather than its size.

The canonical demonstration is strongly connected components on a changing Twitter graph: batch systems recompute SCC from scratch per snapshot, while differential dataflow processes edge insertions and deletions as difference streams through the iterative dataflow, updating only affected components [3]. Nested iteration extends this to queries like "SCCs of the subgraph reachable from each node" — an inner loop per outer-loop element — which the product-order timestamps handle by giving the inner iteration its own coordinate, incomparable across different outer iterations and therefore processed concurrently [1].

```python
# Conceptual sketch: differential SCC as nested fixed points.
# outer time t = input epoch; inner coordinate i = iteration.
def scc_step(edges_t, labels):
    # labels: (node -> component) at (t, i); propagate minima along edges
    propagated = join(edges_t, labels, on="src").map(lambda e,l: (e.dst, l))
    return min_per_key(concat(labels, propagated))   # consolidated at (t, i+1)

# Each epoch t reuses differences: only edges/labels that changed
# at incomparable times are reprocessed; the rest is frontier-advanced.
```

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### Naiad's Measured Performance

The SOSP 2013 evaluation compared Naiad against specialized systems in their home domains and found it competitive or superior across all three [1][2]:

- **Batch updates**: a streaming word-count ingested and reflected batch updates in **under 1 second**, matching dedicated streaming engines.
- **Iterative computation**: PageRank-style iterations completed in **under 1 millisecond per iteration** on a 48-machine cluster [2].
- **Interactive queries**: reachability and shortest-path queries over a live social graph returned in **under 100 ms**, enabling genuinely interactive graph mining [1].

The evaluation's deeper point was architectural: one system, one programming model, and one progress protocol covered batch, streaming, and iterative workloads, with performance within striking distance of purpose-built engines, and near-linear weak scaling attributed to the progress protocol's data-independent coordination cost [1].

### Differential Dataflow's Incremental Wins

The CIDR 2013 paper demonstrated incrementally maintained strongly connected components on Twitter-scale graphs, where updates produced proportionally small recomputation — orders of magnitude less work than snapshot recomputation when changes were small [3]. The modern Rust implementation reports single-update latencies of a few hundred microseconds for interactive queries over tens of millions of edges, and shows that batching concurrent update rounds increases throughput nearly linearly without changing result semantics [6].

### Machine-Checked Correctness

Beyond benchmarks, differential dataflow has recently acquired formal proof artifacts unusual for data systems. The repository's `formal/` directory contains Lean models of the incremental reduce operator, proving stream correctness: for any schedule of rounds, frontiers, and adversarial logical compaction, emitted corrections accumulate to the true reduction at every finalized time [5]. A companion result proves that for *any* operator `f` with `f 0 = 0`, the differential lifting yields a difference trace with updates only at joins of input times — a general keystone covering linear operators, joins, and reductions uniformly [5].

### Comparison with Flink and Spark Structured Streaming

Timely dataflow's partial orders make nested iteration first-class and its progress protocol subsumes watermarks as the special case of a totally ordered timestamp with a single outstanding frontier.

Spark Structured Streaming takes the opposite trade: micro-batch execution gives simple exactly-once semantics and a familiar DataFrame API, but iteration is limited (recursive queries are essentially unsupported; MLlib-style iteration runs driver-side over static data) and latency is bounded below by the batch interval [8]. Differential dataflow occupies the point in the design space both systems avoid: *asynchronous, fine-grained, incrementally maintained iteration*, at the cost of a programming model — lattice timestamps, arrangements, difference traces — that demands substantially more sophistication from its users.

## 6. Limitations

**Cognitive and implementation complexity.** Partially ordered time is a powerful abstraction and a demanding one. Programmers must choose timestamp types, reason about incomparability, and understand when differences cancel versus accumulate. The progress-tracking protocol, while elegant, is notoriously subtle to implement correctly — the Lean formalization effort [5] exists precisely because the gap between the model and a correct high-performance implementation is large.

**Memory pressure from traces.** Differential dataflow retains differences rather than folding them: arrangements accumulate history proportional to the number of *distinct updates*, and logical compaction is heuristic. Long-lived computations over high-churn inputs can see trace sizes grow uncomfortably. Systems with total-order time can aggressively drop state behind the watermark; differential systems must be more conservative, since an incomparable future update may yet need any retained difference.

**Fault tolerance and exactly-once semantics.** Naiad's original fault tolerance was checkpoint-and-restore at the timely level [1]; differential dataflow inherits timely's model, which is weaker in tooling than Flink's mature savepoint ecosystem or Spark's write-ahead-log-based recovery. Exactly-once end-to-end semantics with external sinks remain the operator's responsibility to a greater degree than in mainstream engines.

**Ecosystem and adoption.** Despite its technical influence, differential dataflow's direct adoption is concentrated in research and in Materialize's commercial streaming SQL product. Flink and Spark dominate production streaming, with richer connectors, SQL layers, and operational tooling. The model's generality is also a marketing problem: it is harder to explain "partially ordered incremental iteration" than "exactly-once streaming SQL."

**Datalog fragment and recursion limits.** The supported fragment is essentially positive Datalog with stratified aggregation; non-monotonic recursion (negation or aggregation inside fixed points) remains semantically fraught, since difference-based semantics for non-monotone operators lacks the clean least-fixed-point story of the monotone core [3].

## 7. Conclusion

Timely dataflow and differential dataflow jointly answer a question that batch, streaming, and graph systems each answered partially: how should a distributed system coordinate work whose causal structure is richer than a sequence? Naiad's answer — structured cycles, asynchronous messages, and a progress protocol tracking outstanding pointstamps to deliver exact notifications — showed that low latency and iteration need not be enemies [1]. Differential dataflow's answer — differences over partially ordered timestamps, shared arrangements, and incrementally maintained fixed points — showed that iteration need not be recomputed, even as its inputs stream [3].

The ideas have diffused further than the systems themselves. Watermarks, exactly-once state, and iterative streaming in Flink; continuous processing modes in Spark; the entire "streaming database" category — all inherit, knowingly or not, from the timely dataflow framing of progress as a lattice-valued frontier rather than a scalar clock. And the recent Lean formalizations suggest the model's next chapter may be *verified* data systems, where the subtlety of incremental iteration is discharged by proof rather than testing [5].

The enduring lesson is methodological: choose the right notion of time, and coordination — the fundamental cost of distributed computation — becomes a property of the causal structure rather than a tax on every record. Partial orders were the right notion. The tax, it turned out, was optional.
