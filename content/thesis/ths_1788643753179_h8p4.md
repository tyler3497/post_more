# In-Network Computing with Programmable Data Planes: P4, RMT Match-Action Pipelines, and In-Switch Aggregation for Distributed Training

## Abstract

The convergence of programmable forwarding hardware and the escalating communication demands of distributed machine learning has produced a distinct systems discipline: *in-network computing*. Modern programmable switches — built around the Reconfigurable Match-Action Table (RMT) and Protocol-Independent Switch Architecture (PISA) substrates and programmed in the P4 language — process packets at terabits per second through deterministic, multi-stage match-action pipelines. Rather than merely forwarding traffic, these devices can *compute*: aggregate gradients, detect congestion, and orchestrate collective operations while packets traverse the network fabric. This thesis develops the architecture of in-network computing from first principles, formalizes the RMT/PISA execution model and its hardware constraints (single-pass line-rate processing, stage-local state, limited per-packet ALU budgets), and presents in-switch aggregation as the canonical application. We analyze SwitchML's design for all-reduce acceleration on Intel Tofino hardware, contrast it with scheduling alternatives, and derive the bandwidth and latency bounds of in-network reduction. Empirical results — up to 2.27× training-throughput improvement on 100 Gbps fabrics [4] — are reproduced analytically, and we close with open problems: floating-point data-plane arithmetic, multi-tenant isolation, and dRMT disaggregation.

## 1 Introduction

For three decades, the architectural contract of packet-switching hardware was narrowly defined: parse a fixed set of protocol headers, look up forwarding state, rewrite fields, and transmit. Computation belonged to end hosts; the network was a conduit. This separation, codified in the end-to-end principle, began to fracture with software-defined networking, which moved *control* into software while leaving the *data plane* rigid. The advent of programmable data planes — first demonstrated at scale by the Reconfigurable Match-Action Table (RMT) architecture [2] and exposed to programmers through the P4 language [1] — removed the last pillar of rigidity: the forwarding behavior itself became software-defined, yet still executed at line rate in custom silicon.

This programmability arrives at a moment of acute need. Distributed training of large neural networks is dominated not by floating-point computation but by *communication*: synchronizing gradients across workers via all-reduce consumes the majority of wall-clock time at scale [4]. The switch sits precisely at the chokepoint of this traffic, seeing every gradient from every worker. The natural question — why merely forward packets that the switch could combine? — motivates *in-network aggregation*, the flagship application of in-network computing.

This thesis contributes a unified treatment of this space:

- **Architectural foundations (§2):** the RMT and PISA execution models, the P4 programming abstraction, and the constraints of line-rate processing: single-pass packets, bounded per-stage memory, stage-local registers, and the absence of general arithmetic.
- **Methodology (§3):** how in-network programs are designed within these constraints — packet-format co-design, register-based accumulation, pool/bitmap metadata schemes, and correctness-preserving transport protocols layered over UDP or RDMA.
- **Deep dive (§4):** the P4 language and its architectures (PSA, TNA); the RMT pipeline mechanics (parser, PHV, match-action stages, deparser); the SwitchML system end-to-end; and complementary designs (DAIET, ATP, PANAMA) that extend aggregation to multi-tenant and wide-area settings.
- **Empirical evaluation (§5):** analytical bandwidth models, the 2× traffic-reduction theorem for in-switch aggregation, and published measurements.
- **Limitations and open problems (§6):** floating-point support, stateful scaling, multi-switch trees, security of in-network state, and dRMT-class disaggregated pipelines.

The remainder of this thesis proceeds in this order.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Programmable Forwarding Pipeline

A modern programmable switch ASIC — exemplified by Intel's Tofino family (12.8 Tbps Tofino 2, 25.6 Tbps Tofino 3) — implements the RMT pipeline [2]. Incoming bits enter a *parser* that extracts header fields into a Packet Header Vector (PHV), a wide register bundle (several kilobits) that accompanies the packet through the pipeline. The PHV then traverses a sequence of *match-action stages* — typically 12–20 per pipeline — each of which performs a table lookup (exact match in SRAM, ternary match in TCAM) over a subset of PHV fields and executes the associated action via per-field ALUs. Finally, a *deparser* reassembles the packet. Ingress and egress pipelines are separated by a traffic manager that queues, schedules, and replicates packets.

> Theorem: *Line-rate invariant.* A packet may spend at most one pipeline pass per stage, at a fixed clock budget per stage (~1 GHz, one packet per clock per pipeline). Any computation exceeding the per-stage ALU and memory-access budget cannot be placed in the data plane; it must be recirculated, punted to the control plane, or redesigned.

This invariant is the source of both the power and the difficulty of in-network computing: *arbitrary* programs are permitted, but only those expressible as bounded match-action computations survive.

### 2.2 P4: Programming the Data Plane

P4 (Programming Protocol-Independent Packet Processors) [1] is a domain-specific language for specifying packet-processing behavior independent of the target. A P4 program declares *headers* (user-defined packet formats), a *parser* (a finite-state machine extracting headers), *match-action tables* (lookup key → action), and a *control flow* applying tables in sequence. The P4-16 specification [7] decouples the language from *architectures*: the Portable Switch Architecture (PSA) defines standard externs (meters, counters, registers), while vendor architectures such as the Tofino Native Architecture (TNA) expose richer stateful ALUs, register arrays, and packet generators.

Crucially, P4 provides no loops over packets, no dynamic memory allocation, and no floating point. State persists across packets only through *externs* — principally register arrays accessed with read-modify-write semantics within a single stage. This restriction shapes every in-network computing design.

### 2.3 Distributed Training and the All-Reduce Bottleneck

Data-parallel training replicates the model on *n* workers; each iteration computes local gradients and synchronizes them via *all-reduce*, an operation in which every worker ends with the element-wise sum (then averaged) of all gradients. Classic algorithms — ring all-reduce, tree all-reduce — exchange *O(n)* or *O(log n)* messages per worker, and total fabric traffic scales with model size regardless of worker count. For modern models with tens of billions of parameters, a single all-reduce moves tens of gigabytes; at 100 Gbps, communication dominates compute [4].

The key observation of in-network aggregation is that addition is *associative and commutative*: partial sums can be formed anywhere, in any order. The switch, which observes all workers' gradient packets, can accumulate them in its registers and emit a single aggregated packet per chunk — halving traffic and eliminating the end-host CPU from the reduction loop.

### 2.4 Related In-Network Primitives

Aggregation is one member of a growing family: *in-band network telemetry (INT)* embeds per-hop measurements; *NetCache* serves key-value reads from switch SRAM; sketch-based monitors estimate flow statistics; *ATP* [6] extends aggregation with dynamic quantization and multi-tenant scheduling.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Design Methodology for In-Network Programs

We formalize the design of an in-network computing application as a four-step process:

1. **Reduction identification.** Isolate a per-packet operation that is *associative and commutative* (e.g., integer addition of gradient chunks) so that partial results may be combined in any arrival order.
2. **State placement.** Map accumulator state onto register arrays such that each packet touches *stage-local* state only: a packet's chunk updates exactly one register slot, using one read-modify-write per stage visit. The number of concurrent aggregations (the *pool size*) is bounded by SRAM: with *S* bytes of stage SRAM and chunk size *c*, at most *S/c* slots are available.
3. **Metadata co-design.** Define packet headers carrying the metadata the pipeline needs but cannot compute: a *pool index* identifying which aggregation slot the chunk belongs to, a *bitmap* of contributing workers, and sequence information. The parser extracts these into the PHV at line rate.
4. **Transport co-design.** Because the data plane cannot buffer arbitrarily or retransmit from deep queues, reliability is implemented at the endpoints: workers retransmit unacknowledged chunks; the switch aggregates idempotently or tolerates duplicate-insensitive encodings. SwitchML [4] layers its protocol over UDP (DPDK) or RDMA Unreliable Connected (UC) transports.

### 3.2 Analytical Model

Let *n* workers each hold a gradient vector of *m* elements (each *b* bytes). In ring all-reduce, per-worker traffic is ≈ 2(*n*−1)/*n* · *mb* bytes. With in-switch aggregation on a single switch connecting all workers, each worker sends *mb* bytes and receives *mb* bytes: total per-worker traffic 2*mb*, independent of *n*, and total fabric traffic *nmb* versus 2(*n*−1)*mb* for the ring.

> Theorem: *Traffic reduction bound.* Single-switch in-network aggregation reduces total all-reduce fabric traffic by a factor approaching 2 as *n* grows, relative to ring all-reduce. No endpoint-only protocol can exceed this bound on the same topology, because each gradient byte must enter the fabric at least once and each worker must receive the result at least once.

Latency also improves: the switch performs the reduction *as packets arrive* — sub-RTT — rather than after full vectors are buffered at endpoints. The end-to-end aggregation latency is bounded below by the pipeline latency (hundreds of nanoseconds) plus transmission time, versus milliseconds for endpoint reductions.

### 3.3 Verification Approach

In-network programs are verified at three levels: (i) *functional simulation* in the BMv2 software switch or Tofino model, checking aggregation correctness under packet loss and reordering; (ii) *resource verification* — the P4 compiler reports stage, SRAM/TCAM, ALU, and PHV utilization, and compilation fails if the program exceeds the target; (iii) *end-to-end training* — integration with frameworks (PyTorch via NCCL plugin [4]) confirming convergence parity with baseline all-reduce.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The P4 Language and Target Architectures

P4-16 programs are structured around *architectures* — named collections of programmable blocks and externs. The PSA architecture provides ingress/egress parsers, controls, and deparsers with standard externs:

```p4
// Sketch: register-based accumulator in P4-16 (PSA-style)
register<bit<32>>(POOL_SIZE) grad_accum;

action aggregate(bit<16> pool_idx, bit<32> chunk) {
    bit<32> old;
    grad_accum.read(old, (bit<32>)pool_idx);
    old = old + chunk;              // stateful ALU: single RMW
    grad_accum.write((bit<32>)pool_idx, old);
}

table route_chunk {
    key = { hdr.agg.pool_idx : exact; }
    actions = { aggregate; drop; }
    size = 1024;
}
```

The Tofino Native Architecture (TNA) extends this with wider stateful ALUs (supporting more complex read-modify-write expressions), larger register arrays, and *packet generators* for emitting aggregated results. P4Runtime [1] provides the control-plane API for populating tables and managing the aggregation lifecycle. The compiler maps logical tables onto physical stages subject to the *table dependency graph*: tables with data dependencies must occupy distinct stages, and the total stage count bounds program complexity.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| RMT (abstract) [2] | Programmable | 32 logical | Simple RMW | Registers, meters |
| PSA (P4-16) [7] | Programmable | Target-defined | Basic | Counters, meters, registers |
| TNA (Tofino) | Programmable | 12–20 / pipe | Extended RMW | Packet generator, ghost thread |

### 4.2 RMT Match-Action Pipeline Mechanics

Each RMT stage contains: (i) match memory (TCAM blocks for ternary/longest-prefix matches, SRAM hash tables for exact matches), (ii) action memory (SRAM storing action parameters), and (iii) a Very Long Instruction Word (VLIW) action engine with one ALU per PHV field. A packet's PHV — up to ~4 Kb in RMT [2] — flows through all stages in lock-step; per-packet latency is therefore *deterministic*: stages × clock period, typically under one microsecond.

Three mechanisms allow limited feedback: *recirculation* (re-injecting a packet for a second pass, halving effective throughput for that packet), *mirroring* (cloning to the control plane), and *resubmit*. Aggregation designs avoid recirculation on the fast path: each gradient chunk is parsed, matched on its pool index, accumulated into the register array, and forwarded — all in a single pass.

Memory is the binding constraint. A Tofino-class pipeline offers on the order of tens of megabytes of SRAM per pipeline, shared between match tables and stateful registers. SwitchML's pool of concurrent aggregations is sized so that register state fits alongside forwarding tables; larger models are handled by *chunking* — streaming the gradient vector through the pool in successive waves.

### 4.3 SwitchML: In-Switch All-Reduce at Scale

SwitchML [4] is the canonical realization. Its components:

- **P4 data-plane program (TNA):** parses a custom aggregation header (`pool_idx`, worker bitmap, chunk vector of 32-bit integers), performs the register accumulation, and tracks per-slot arrival bitmaps. When all *n* workers' chunks for a slot have arrived, the switch *multicasts* the aggregated result to all workers and resets the slot.
- **Python controller (BFRuntime):** manages pool allocation, handles timeouts and retransmissions, and coordinates with the ML framework.
- **End-host library:** integrates with frameworks as an NCCL plugin; supports UDP/DPDK and RDMA UC transports. Because switch ALUs lack floating point, gradients are *quantized* to fixed-point integers on the host before transmission and dequantized after aggregation — a transformation shown to preserve convergence for the evaluated models.
- **Correctness under loss:** the protocol numbers chunks and uses cumulative acknowledgments; duplicate packets are detected via the bitmap so retransmissions do not double-count.

Reported results: on a 100 Gbps fabric, SwitchML reduces traffic volume by up to 2.27× and improves training throughput by up to 1.66× on real models (e.g., VGG, BERT-class workloads in the NSDI'21 evaluation), with aggregation occurring at sub-RTT latency inside the switch [4].

### 4.4 Beyond SwitchML: DAIET, ATP, and Multi-Switch Trees

- **DAIET** [3] pioneered key-value aggregation on a software RMT model, establishing the feasibility of in-network reduction but assuming idealized table sizes that physical RMT pipelines cannot provide — a cautionary example of the gap between software models and silicon constraints.
- **ATP** [6] generalizes SwitchML to *multi-tenant* clusters: it introduces dynamic quantization, gradient scheduling across jobs sharing a switch, and loss-tolerant aggregation that degrades gracefully under congestion.
- **Multi-switch aggregation trees** extend the single-switch design hierarchically: leaf switches aggregate racks, spine switches aggregate leaves. This requires *cascaded* pool management and inter-switch aggregation protocols — an area where dRMT-style disaggregation [5], which decouples match-action processing from fixed stage allocation, promises to ease placement of deep aggregation pipelines.

### 4.5 The Arithmetic Gap: Integers, Quantization, and Approximations

The most consequential hardware limitation is arithmetic: RMT ALUs support integer addition, subtraction, bitwise operations, and limited comparisons — *not* floating-point addition, multiplication, or division. Every in-network aggregation system therefore confronts a quantization step. Current practice (fixed-point conversion at endpoints [4][6]) is empirically adequate for SGD-class optimizers, but second-order methods and low-precision training regimes (FP8/FP16) motivate hardware extensions: proposals include *approximate floating-point adders* in stateful ALUs and *log-domain* accumulation. Until such support exists, the integer-only data plane remains the defining constraint of the field.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 Bandwidth Model and the 2× Bound

We formalize §3.2. Consider all-reduce of an *m*-element vector over *n* workers attached to one programmable switch with line rate *R* per port.

- **Ring all-reduce:** each worker sends and receives 2(*n*−1)/*n* · *mb* bytes. Total fabric traffic: 2(*n*−1)*mb*. Time ≥ 2(*n*−1)/*n* · *mb*/*R*.
- **In-switch aggregation:** each worker sends *mb* and receives *mb*. Total fabric traffic: *nmb*. Time ≥ 2*mb*/*R*.

> Theorem: *Asymptotic traffic ratio.* The ratio of ring all-reduce traffic to in-switch aggregation traffic is 2(*n*−1)/*n* → 2 as *n* → ∞. The bound is tight: it is achieved whenever the switch aggregates every chunk exactly once and emits exactly one result copy per worker.

For *n* = 8 workers, *m* = 10⁸ parameters, *b* = 4 bytes (400 MB gradients), *R* = 100 Gbps: ring all-reduce moves 700 MB per worker (56 ms); in-switch aggregation moves 400 MB sent + 400 MB received per worker, with *zero* endpoint reduction CPU and overlapped sub-RTT switch latency. Published SwitchML measurements report end-to-end training speedups up to 2.27× in traffic and 1.66× in throughput on 100 Gbps networks [4], consistent with this model once framework overheads are included.

### 5.2 Latency Analysis

Pipeline latency through a 20-stage RMT pipeline at 1 GHz is ~20 ns per stage plus parser/deparser — well under 1 µs [2]. End-host ring all-reduce latency includes *n*−1 sequential communication rounds plus kernel launch overheads (tens of microseconds each). In-switch aggregation collapses the reduction into the packet flight time itself: the *k*-th chunk's partial sum is complete within microseconds of the last worker's packet arrival, a *sub-RTT* property unattainable by endpoint algorithms.

### 5.3 Resource Scaling

Pool size *P* (concurrent aggregation slots), chunk size *c*, and workers *n* interact: register memory = *P* × *c* × 4 bytes (32-bit accumulators) plus bitmap state *P* × *n* bits. For *P* = 256, *c* = 128 elements: 128 KB of accumulator SRAM — a small fraction of per-pipeline SRAM — while sustaining full line rate. Throughput scales with *P*: the pool must be deep enough to keep the pipeline fed during result multicast, a classic Little's-law sizing argument (bandwidth × RTT / chunk size).

| DAIET [3] | Integer KV | No | Custom | Feasibility (software model) |

## 6 Limitations, Open Problems, and Future Work

1. **Floating-point arithmetic.** The absence of FP ALUs forces endpoint quantization. Hardware support for even *bfloat16* addition in stateful ALUs would remove the quantization round-trip and its (small but nonzero) convergence risk. This is the field's most-requested silicon feature.
2. **Stateful scaling and multi-switch trees.** A single switch's SRAM bounds the pool; hierarchical aggregation across leaf-spine fabrics needs standardized inter-switch protocols and raises questions of fault isolation — a failed aggregation at the spine stalls all racks.
3. **Multi-tenancy and isolation.** Switches are shared infrastructure. ATP [6] makes first steps, but strong performance isolation between training jobs sharing accumulator state, and protection against a malicious tenant corrupting shared registers, remain open.
4. **Disaggregated pipelines.** dRMT [5] proposes decoupling processors from memory in the match-action pipeline, allowing runtime scheduling of stage resources. Applying dRMT to aggregation could dynamically resize pools per job — but requires rethinking the deterministic latency guarantees that make line-rate aggregation predictable.
5. **Transport convergence.** Current systems layer custom protocols over UDP or RDMA UC. Convergence with RoCEv2 congestion control, and with emerging *scheduled fabrics* for AI clusters, is unresolved: in-network aggregation changes traffic matrices in ways congestion controllers do not expect.
6. **Security of in-network state.** Accumulator registers are a new shared-state attack surface: a compromised worker could poison aggregates. Lightweight authentication of gradient chunks at line rate is an open problem.
7. **Generality beyond all-reduce.** Reductions (sum, max, top-k) fit the pipeline; *non-associative* collectives (all-to-all, used in mixture-of-experts) do not decompose as cleanly. Extending in-network computing to these patterns — perhaps via programmable *scheduling* rather than arithmetic — is largely unexplored.

## 7 Conclusion

In-network computing reframes the switch from a forwarding device into a *computational element* of the distributed system. The RMT/PISA pipeline [2], programmed in P4 [1], provides deterministic line-rate execution of match-action programs; its constraints — single-pass processing, stage-local state, integer-only ALUs — are not incidental difficulties but the defining parameters of a new programming discipline. In-switch aggregation for distributed training, realized most completely in SwitchML [4] and extended by ATP [6] and DAIET [3], demonstrates the payoff: traffic halved, latency collapsed to sub-RTT, and training throughput improved by up to 2.27× on production fabrics.

The trajectory is clear. As AI clusters scale to tens of thousands of accelerators and per-port rates reach 400 Gbps and beyond, the network can no longer afford to be a passive conduit for collective communication. Whether through floating-point-capable stateful ALUs, disaggregated dRMT pipelines [5], or deeper co-design with RDMA transports, the data plane will compute — and the systems that embrace this earliest will define the performance envelope of the next decade of distributed learning.
