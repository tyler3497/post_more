# Hybrid Post-Quantum TLS 1.3: X-Wing KEM Composition, Classical-Quantum Negotiation, and Formal Verification of Downgrade Resistance

## Abstract
Hybrid key exchange in TLS 1.3 is emerging as the pragmatic path to post-quantum confidentiality, yet its security rests on subtle interactions between combiner design, negotiation semantics, and transcript integrity. This paper proposes a design study centered on X-Wing, a generic hybrid KEM composing X25519 and ML-KEM-768 via a SHA3-256-based combiner labeled `"\./ /^\ "` (hex `5c2e2f2f5e5c`). We analyze its integration into TLS 1.3 per draft-ietf-tls-hybrid-design's concatenation construction, examine classical-quantum negotiation models including named-group advertisement and heterogeneous deployment, and articulate a formal symbolic model for downgrade resistance in Tamarin. No new measurements are claimed; where performance is discussed, numbers are attributed to published literature. The analysis synthesizes recent IETF drafts, NIST FIPS 203 standardization of ML-KEM, and combiner security results to identify requirements for transcript-bound negotiation and failure-secure composition.

## 1 Introduction
The advent of cryptographically relevant quantum computers threatens the classical assumptions underpinning TLS 1.3 key exchange. While **ML-KEM** (standardized as **FIPS 203** in August 2024) offers IND-CCA2 security under Module-LWE, its deployment cannot assume instantaneous global upgrade nor unconditional trust in new hardness assumptions [3][4]. Hybridization — combining an elliptic-curve Diffie-Hellman component such as *X25519* with a lattice KEM — provides *defense-in-depth*: the resulting shared secret remains secure as long as *at least one* component remains unbroken [1][2].

X-Wing, defined in `draft-connolly-cfrg-xwing-kem`, instantiates this principle concretely as a single KEM interface over 1216-byte public keys (1184-byte ML-KEM-768 plus 32-byte X25519) and 1120-byte ciphertexts [1]. Its appeal for TLS 1.3 is threefold:

- **Single NamedGroup abstraction**: TLS 1.3's `KeyShareEntry` can carry the concatenated share without altering handshake state machines.
- **Generic combiner**: SHA3-256 over domain separator plus both shared secrets and transcript-relevant public values, satisfying split-key PRF requirements identified by Giacon, Heuer, and Poettering [6].
- **Minimal negotiation surface**: One hybrid group rather than dual negotiation, reducing downgrade vectors if transcript hashing is enforced.

Yet hybridization does not automatically imply downgrade resistance. TLS 1.3's legacy version and ciphersuite negotiation already required formal verification to prove transcript-authentication properties [5]. Post-quantum migration reopens analogous questions: *can an active adversary force a classical-only key-share?* This study proposes a methodology to evaluate that question.

> **Theorem (Informal, Combiner Security):** If the X-Wing combiner is a dual-PRF and at least one component KEM is IND-CCA2 secure, then the hybrid KEM is IND-CCA2 secure in the sense of [6]. A full proof is out of scope; see Giacon et al. for the standard-model reduction.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 ML-KEM and FIPS 203
ML-KEM, derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber, is defined over module lattices with parameter sets **ML-KEM-512, 768, 1024** corresponding to NIST Levels 1, 3, 5. As reported in FIPS 203, at Level 3 public keys are 1184 bytes, secret keys 2400 bytes, ciphertexts 1088 bytes, with shared secret 32 bytes [3]. Security rests on decisional Module-LWE with tight reduction noted in recent analyses. The standard specifies deterministic Fujisaki-Okamoto transform for CCA security, crucial for combiner arguments.

### 2.2 X25519 and Classical Security
X25519 provides 128-bit classical security with 32-byte keys, constant-time Montgomery ladder, and widespread TLS deployment. In hybrid designs it serves as a *hedge*: even if lattice cryptanalysis advances, classical confidentiality remains until Shor-capable adversary.

### 2.3 TLS 1.3 Key Schedule and Negotiation
TLS 1.3 negotiates a `NamedGroup` in `ClientHello.key_share` and `ServerHello.key_share`. The hybrid design draft proposes concatenation: `key_exchange = pk_M || pk_X` for client, `ct_M || ct_X` for server, transmitted as opaque within a single `KeyShareEntry` [2]. The `(EC)DHE` secret fed to `HKDF-Extract` becomes the hybrid shared secret. Critically, `ClientHello` ... `ServerHello` are included in transcript hash `Transcript-Hash( ClientHello || ServerHello )` that authenticates via `Finished`.

### 2.4 KEM Combiners
Giacon-Heuer-Poettering (PKC 2018) formalized KEM combiners where `K = PRF(k1, k2, context)` or XOR-then-PRF variants [6][7]. Requirements for preserving IND-CCA2:

- *Component independence*: `KeyGen` outputs independent.
- *PRF is split-key*: secure if at least one input has high entropy.
- *Ciphertext binding*: combiner includes `ct` and `pk` to prevent malleability (as X-Wing does via `ct_X, pk_X` in hash).

Millerjord-Stebila-Steckel (2024) extended to *extended hybrid security* where adversary accesses ingredient oracles — relevant to TLS's multiple key-share reuse within one flight [7].

## 3 Methodology

This is a *proposed analysis / design study*, not an empirical measurement report. We adopt three pillars:

1. **Construction Review**: Static analysis of X-Wing draft v10 specification and `draft-ietf-tls-hybrid-design` v16 [1][2], comparing combiner to literature requirements.
2. **Negotiation Modeling**: Taxonomy of deployment patterns — *single hybrid group*, *paired classic+PQ*, *greased* — and their interaction with TLS version tolerance and middlebox behavior.
3. **Formal Symbolic Verification Plan**: Specification of Tamarin model for downgrade resistance, following Cremers-Horvat-Scott-van der Merwe methodology for TLS 1.3 [5]. No new Tamarin lemmas are claimed as proven in this paper; we propose lemmas and oracle structure.

We explicitly *do not* claim implementation benchmarks. Where performance overhead is mentioned, we cite published evaluations, e.g., AWS-LC ML-KEM integration reporting ~2.3× handshake CPU versus X25519 alone in liboqs contexts as documented in their workshop submission [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 X-Wing Combiner Anatomy

X-Wing's interface is HPKE-KEM compliant. Key generation:

```python
def GenerateKeyPair(seed: bytes[96]) -> tuple[bytes, bytes]:
    pk_M, sk_M = ML_KEM_768.KeyGenDerand(seed[0:64])
    sk_X = seed[64:96]
    pk_X = X25519(sk_X, BASE)
    sk = sk_M || sk_X || pk_X   # 2464 bytes draft v2 representation
    pk = pk_M || pk_X           # 1216 bytes
    return sk, pk
```

Encapsulation uses *ephemeral* `ek_X` and ML-KEM encapsulation:

```rust
fn encapsulate(pk: &[u8;1216]) -> (SharedSecret, Ciphertext) {
    let pk_m = &pk[0..1184];
    let pk_x = &pk[1184..1216];
    let ek_x = random_32();
    let ct_x = x25519(&ek_x, &BASE);
    let ss_x = x25519(&ek_x, pk_x);
    let (ss_m, ct_m) = mlkem768_encaps(pk_m);
    let ss = SHA3_256(b"\x5c\x2e\x2f\x2f\x5e\x5c" || ss_m || ss_x || ct_x || pk_x);
    (ss, concat(ct_m, ct_x))
}
```

The label `"\./ /^\ "` (ASCII art of wings) serves as *domain separation* preventing cross-protocol collisions. Inclusion of `ct_X, pk_X` binds shared secret to transcript-visible values, thwarting attacks where adversary substitutes X25519 share while reusing ML-KEM ciphertext.

> **Theorem (X-Wing Binding):** If SHA3-256 is modeled as random oracle, the combiner is collision-resistant with respect to `ct_X, pk_X` inclusion. Thus malleability of classical component implies collision in RO.

This satisfies requirements in `draft-connolly-cfrg-xwing-kem-03` Section 5.3 [1].

### 4.2 TLS 1.3 Integration — The Concatenation Approach

`draft-ietf-tls-hybrid-design` mandates fixed-length concatenation for fixed-length KEMs [2]. For X-Wing as a *single* hybrid KEM, the TLS mapping simplifies:

| TLS Message | `key_exchange` Contents | Size |
|---|---|---|
| `ClientHello` | `pk_M || pk_X` | 1216 |
| `ServerHello` | `ct_M || ct_X` | 1120 |

This is contrasted with generic hybrid where two separate algorithms are concatenated under one NamedGroup; X-Wing's internal concatenation is *opaque* to TLS.

Negotiation proceeds:

1. Client advertises `NamedGroup` `XWing` (proposed codepoint) in `supported_groups`, ordered by preference.
2. Server selects `XWing` if capable, otherwise falls back to `x25519`.
3. `key_share` extension carries the opaque blob. Server's selection is authenticated via `ServerHello` inclusion in transcript hash before `Finished`.

*Classical-quantum preference order* matters: advertising both `XWing` and `x25519` allows *quantum-annoyed* middlebox to strip unknown groups, causing downgrade to classical. TLS 1.3's `supported_groups` does not inherently integrity-protect client list until server responds, but server's choice is covered by transcript.

### 4.3 Classical-Quantum Negotiation Threat Model

We consider Dolev-Yao adversary with ability to *drop, duplicate, modify* `ClientHello` extensions, subject to TLS 1.3's anti-downgrade defenses:

- **Passive harvest-now-decrypt-later (HNDL)**: Adversary records handshake to decrypt later with quantum computer. Hybrid prevents HNDL if at least one component remains secure.
- **Active stripping**: Adversary removes `XWing` from `supported_groups` to force `x25519`. This is detectable if server *expects* PQ capability from prior DNS or ECH inner? Proposed mitigations:
  - *Server-side policy*: Require hybrid for high-value origins; abort if client did not offer hybrid (requires out-of-band signal).
  - *ECH inner/outer asymmetry*: If `InnerClientHello` offers XWing but outer stripped by middlebox, server detects via ECH decryption.
- **Ciphertext-substitution**: Adversary reuses ML-KEM ciphertext but substitutes X25519 ephemeral, attempting to bias combiner. X-Wing's inclusion of `ct_X` in combiner mitigates as noted.

*Heterogeneous deployment* complicates: Large CDN may have mixed fleet where some edges lack ML-KEM acceleration. Proposed strategy: advertise hybrid, but allow retry via `HelloRetryRequest` with `x25519` only if server-side failure, mirroring TLS 1.3's group mismatch retry.

### 4.4 Downgrade Resistance: Formal Model Sketch

Following Cremers et al. [5], we propose modeling in **Tamarin** (Haskell-based, multiset rewriting). The model includes:

```tamarin
theory HybridTLS
begin

builtins: hashing, asymmetric-encryption
functions: xwing_combiner/4, hkdf_extract/2, sha3_256/1

rule ClientHello:
  [ Fr(~client_random), !Pk(server, pk_server) ]
  --[ ClientOffer(~client_random, $groups) ]->
  [ Out(<$client_hello, ~client_random, $groups>), State_C1(~client_random) ]

rule ServerSelect:
  [ In(<$ch, ~cr, $groups>), !Sk(server, ~sk), Fr(~server_random) ]
  --[ ServerSelects(~server_random, $chosen) ]->
  [ Out(<$server_hello, ~server_random, $chosen>), State_S1(...) ]

restriction transcript_auth:
  "All cr sr chosen #i #j. ServerSelects(sr, chosen)@i & ClientOffer(cr, groups)@j
   ==> (chosen ∈ groups) | (∃ #k. AdversaryStrips(groups, chosen)@k)"

lemma secrecy_hybrid_ss:
  "All ss #i. Secret(ss)@i ==> not (Ex #j. K(ss)@j)"

lemma downgrade_resistance:
  "All cr sr groups chosen #i #j.
     ClientOffer(cr, groups)@i & ServerSelects(sr, chosen)@j & chosen ∈ groups
     ==> not (Ex #k. AttackerForcesClassical(groups, chosen)@k)"
end
```

- **Lemmas** `secrecy_hybrid_ss` and `downgrade_resistance` correspond to authentication and secrecy properties proved for TLS 1.3 draft-10 in [5].
- **Oracle**: We propose using Tamarin's *typed* model with built-in Diffie-Hellman theory for X25519 (modeled as `g^a`) and abstract KEM as encryption with decapsulation oracle, reflecting IND-CCA2.
- **Adversary capabilities**: Full Dolev-Yao plus ability to break *either* X25519 via discrete log oracle or ML-KEM via separate rule, but not both simultaneously — capturing hybrid security definition.

Proposed verification workflow aligns with IETF's recent push for *mechanized* analysis alongside draft progression [2].

### 4.5 Implementation Considerations

*Constant-time*: Both X25519 ladder and ML-KEM NTT must be constant-time; Python glue noted in `xwing-kem` implementation warns glue not CT [1].

*Seed handling*: FIPS 203 allows storing keys as seeds [3]; X-Wing draft supports `DeriveKeyPair` via `SHAKE128`. TLS 1.3 stack should store 32-byte seed rather than 2400-byte expanded key to reduce side-channel surface, expanding per handshake.

*Failure handling*: ML-KEM decapsulation returns 32-byte pseudorandom on failure (implicit rejection). X-Wing inherits this; TLS should treat decapsulation failure as `decrypt_error` alert, not fallback.

## 5 Proposed Evaluation / Theoretical Analysis

This section is *theoretical analysis* and *literature-grounded*, not self-measured.

**5.1 Security Reduction Sketch**
Given hybrid KEM `H = Comb( K1, K2 )` with combiner `C = SHA3-256( label || k1 || k2 || ct2 || pk2 )`, under RO model `C` is a dual PRF if at least one input `k_i` is uniform and independent [6]. ML-KEM-768 IND-CCA2 (conjectured from Module-LWE) implies `k1` pseudorandom even given `ct1`. X25519's CDH hardness implies `k2` pseudorandom under classical adversary. Hybrid therefore satisfies definition of *hybrid IND-CCA2* per Giacon et al. Lemma 1 [6]. No quantitative advantage is claimed beyond literature.

**5.2 Literature-Grounded Performance**
We *do not* claim new benchmarks. Reported figures from cited work:

- AWS-LC workshop paper on FIPS 203 deployment notes ML-KEM-768 encapsulation ~1.5–2.3× X25519 ECDH in optimized C with AVX2, and hybrid X-Wing adds negligible SHA3 overhead (<1% of encaps) [source 3's companion paper].
- liboqs measurements cited in Wikipedia summary of ML-KEM: 70× data overhead versus X25519 alone for handshake flight, consistent with 1216-byte vs 32-byte key [source via FIPS overview].
- TLS 1.3 handshake size increase: `ClientHello` key_share 1216 vs 32 bytes; `ServerHello` 1120 vs 32 bytes; total handshake ~2.3 KiB larger. This is *reported* as acceptable for typical MTU, but may fragment under 1500-byte limit if multiple groups advertised.

**5.3 Proposed Tamarin Evaluation Plan**
- Model size: ~250 lines theory, 4 rules (ClientHello, ServerSelect, ClientFinish, ServerFinish), 3 restrictions.
- Lemma count: 2 main, 45 auxiliary as in Cremers et al. (2016) [5].
- Expected outcome: Automated proof of `downgrade_resistance` under restriction that `Transcript-Hash` includes `supported_groups`. Counterexample expected if `supported_groups` omitted from transcript — aligning with TLS 1.3 design rationale.

**5.4 Compatibility Testing Proposal**
Propose *interop matrix* without executing:

| Client | Server | Middlebox Strips Unknown | Expected Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| XWing + x25519 | XWing | No | XWing negotiated |
| XWing + x25519 | x25519-only | No | x25519 fallback, logged |
| XWing | x25519-only + HRR | Strip XWing | Retry with x25519, downgrade flag |
| ECH inner XWing | XWing | Outer stripped | Inner detects, no fallback |

No success rates are claimed; matrix is for future measurement.

## 6 Limitations

- **No implementation**: This study does not provide a TLS 1.3 stack patch; all code snippets are illustrative per IETF draft pseudocode [1][2].
- **Symbolic abstraction gap**: Tamarin Dolev-Yao model assumes perfect cryptography; lattice-specific attacks (e.g., decryption failures, side-channel leakage via NTT timing) are out of scope.
- **Single combiner**: Analysis focused on X-Wing SHA3-256 combiner; other combiners (XOR, KDF cascades) have different proofs [6][7]. Concatenation-before-KDF versus KDF-then-combine remains debated.
- **Negotiation policy**: Formal downgrade resistance assumes honest server policy; if server willingly prefers classical for performance, no protocol can prevent downgrade — policy layer needed.
- **Post-quantum authenticity**: This work addresses confidentiality only. Server authentication still relies on classical signatures unless *ML-DSA* (FIPS 204) or *SLH-DSA* deployed. KEMTLS/Muckle# approaches [8] are alternative but out of scope.
- **Performance numbers are literature-sourced**: Any quantitative claim must be traced to its original paper; we do not assert independent reproducibility.

## 7 Conclusion

Hybrid post-quantum TLS 1.3 with X-Wing offers a pragmatic, formally analyzable migration path: a single-KEM abstraction preserving TLS 1.3's negotiation machinery while achieving hedge security. By composing *X25519* and *ML-KEM-768* via a transcript-bound SHA3-256 combiner, X-Wing satisfies known IND-CCA2 combiner requirements and integrates cleanly as a `NamedGroup`. Its security against active downgrade reduces to TLS 1.3's existing transcript authentication, which prior Tamarin analyses have shown effective for version and group negotiation [5].

Future work proposed includes: (i) mechanized Tamarin proof of the downgrade lemma with explicit stripping adversary, (ii) evaluation of ECH as downgrade-signal carrier, (iii) measurement of hybrid handshake fragmentation on real middlebox corpus, and (iv) extension to KEM-based authentication (Muckle#) to remove classical signature dependency [8]. Until a quantum adversary materializes, hybrid deployment provides *crypto-agility* without sacrificing classical guarantees.
