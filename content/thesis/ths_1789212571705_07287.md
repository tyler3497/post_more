# Information Geometry of Statistical Models: The Fisher–Rao Metric, Amari's α-Connections, and Dually Flat Manifolds

## Abstract

Information geometry endows the set of probability distributions of a parametric statistical model with the structure of a differentiable manifold, turning estimation and inference into questions of Riemannian and affine-differential geometry. This thesis develops the full Amari–Čencov dualistic structure: the Fisher–Rao Riemannian metric introduced by Rao in 1945 [1], uniquely characterized by Čencov's invariance theorem under sufficient statistics [3]; the one-parameter family of α-connections that interpolates between the flat exponential (e) and mixture (m) connections [4][5]; and the dually flat manifolds — notably exponential and mixture families — whose dual affine coordinate systems are linked by Legendre duality. We derive the canonical divergence, its reduction to Bregman divergence on dually flat spaces, and the generalized Pythagorean theorem governing projections; we then connect the geometry to the natural gradient, the EM algorithm, and quantum monotone metrics [7][9][10]. Proofs of the metric's Hessian structure, the duality relation, and the Pythagorean identity are given in full.

## 1 Introduction

A parametric statistical model is a family $\mathcal{S} = \{p(x;\xi) : \xi \in \Xi\}$ of probability density functions on a sample space $\mathcal{X}$, indexed by a parameter vector $\xi = (\xi^1, \dots, \xi^n)$ ranging over an open set $\Xi \subset \mathbb{R}^n$. Classical statistics treats $\Xi$ as a mere index set; **information geometry** treats it as a *manifold* whose points are distributions, equipped with a Riemannian metric and a family of affine connections that encode the second- and third-order structure of the log-likelihood [4].

The program has three historical roots:

- **Rao (1945)** observed that the Fisher information matrix
  $$
  g_{ij}(\xi) = \mathbb{E}_{p(\cdot;\xi)}\left[\partial_i \ell(x;\xi)\,\partial_j \ell(x;\xi)\right], \qquad \ell = \log p,
  $$
  defines a positive-definite inner product on the tangent space at each point, and hence a Riemannian metric — the *Fisher–Rao metric* [1]. Rao further noted that Jeffreys' prior $\pi(\xi) \propto \sqrt{\det g(\xi)}$ is the invariant volume form of this metric [2].
- **Čencov (1972/1982)** proved that the Fisher metric is the *only* Riemannian metric (up to scale) on finite-sample-space models that is invariant under sufficient statistics — i.e., under Markov morphisms that compress data without losing information about $\xi$ [3][8][11].
- **Amari (1980s)** introduced the one-parameter family of **α-connections** $\nabla^{(\alpha)}$, dual with respect to $g$, with the Levi-Civita connection at $\alpha = 0$ and the flat exponential and mixture connections at $\alpha = \pm 1$ [4][5]. This *dualistic structure* $(g, \nabla, \nabla^*)$ is the core object of the field.

The significance of this machinery is not merely aesthetic. The *natural gradient* — steepest descent with respect to $g$ rather than the Euclidean parameter metric — is Fisher-efficient and reparameterization-invariant [9]; the EM algorithm is an alternating pair of e- and m-projections on a dually flat manifold; and divergences such as the Kullback–Leibler divergence emerge as *canonical divergences* of the dualistic structure rather than ad hoc choices [4][7].

> **Theorem: (Scope of this thesis).** For regular parametric models, the Fisher–Rao metric, the α-connection family, the dual affine coordinates of exponential/mixture families, and the canonical divergence form a single coherent structure: a *Hessian manifold* when dually flat, reducing all inference geometry to convex analysis via Legendre duality.

The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 fixes notation and regularity. Section 3 develops the metric, the connections, and the duality relation. Section 4 — the deep dive — treats five themes: the metric and Čencov's theorem; the α-family and its poles; dually flat manifolds and dual coordinates; canonical divergence and the Pythagorean theorem; and curvature beyond flatness. Section 5 states and proves the central theorems. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes with applications.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Statistical manifolds and regularity

Let $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{F}, \mu)$ be a measure space and $\mathcal{S} = \{p_\xi \,d\mu : \xi \in \Xi\}$ a family of probability measures absolutely continuous w.r.t.

Under these conditions, the *score functions*
$$
\ell_i(x;\xi) := \partial_i \log p(x;\xi) = \frac{\partial_i p(x;\xi)}{p(x;\xi)}
$$
satisfy $\mathbb{E}_\xi[\ell_i] = 0$ and span an $n$-dimensional space identifiable with the tangent space $T_{p_\xi}\mathcal{S}$. We write $\partial_i := \partial/\partial\xi^i$ for the coordinate basis of $T\mathcal{S}$.

### 2.2 The Amari–Čencov 3-tensor

Beyond the metric, the third-order cumulant of the score defines the totally symmetric **Amari–Čencov tensor** [4]:
$$
T_{ijk}(\xi) = \mathbb{E}_\xi\left[\partial_i \ell \,\partial_j \ell \,\partial_k \ell\right],
$$
a symmetric $(0,3)$-tensor field. The pair $(g, T)$ — a *statistical manifold* in Lauritzen's sense — determines the entire dualistic structure: every α-connection's Christoffel symbols are built from $\partial_k g_{ij}$ and $T_{ijk}$ [4].

### 2.3 Exponential and mixture families

Two classes of models are geometrically distinguished [4][5]:

1. **Exponential families**: $p(x;\theta) = \exp\left(C(x) + \sum_i \theta^i F_i(x) - \psi(\theta)\right)$, with *natural parameters* $\theta$ and *cumulant (log-partition) function* $\psi$.
2. **Mixture families**: $p(x;\eta) = \sum_i \eta^i p_i(x) + \left(1 - \sum_i \eta^i\right) p_0(x)$, affine in the *expectation parameters* $\eta$.

Exponential families are **e-flat** (flat under $\nabla^{(1)}$) and mixture families **m-flat** (flat under $\nabla^{(-1)}$); each is *dually* flat with respect to the other connection, as we prove in Section 5.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is the standard differential-geometric one: all statistical claims are translated into statements about $(g, \nabla^{(\alpha)})$ and proved by tensor computation in coordinates, exploiting the dualistic relation
$$
X\, g(Y,Z) = g(\nabla_X Y, Z) + g(Y, \nabla^*_X Z),
$$
which pairs each connection $\nabla$ with its *dual* $\nabla^*$ with respect to $g$ [4].

Concretely we proceed in four steps:

1. **Metric construction.** Derive $g_{ij} = -\mathbb{E}_\xi[\partial_i\partial_j \ell] = \mathbb{E}_\xi[\partial_i\ell\,\partial_j\ell]$ from the second-order expansion of the Kullback–Leibler divergence, showing the metric is the Hessian of the divergence at coincidence.
2. **Connection family.** Define $\nabla^{(\alpha)}$ via its Christoffel symbols $\Gamma^{(\alpha)k}_{ij} = \Gamma^{(0)k}_{ij} - \frac{\alpha}{2} T^k_{ij}$, verify the duality $\nabla^{(\alpha)*} = \nabla^{(-\alpha)}$, and identify the poles $\nabla^{(1)} = \nabla^{(e)}$, $\nabla^{(-1)} = \nabla^{(m)}$.
3. **Dually flat analysis.** For exponential families, compute $\eta_i = \partial_i\psi = \mathbb{E}_\theta[F_i]$, show $(\theta, \eta)$ are mutually dual affine coordinates linked by the Legendre transform $\varphi(\eta) = \sup_\theta (\theta \cdot \eta - \psi(\theta))$, and prove $g_{ij} = \partial_i\partial_j\psi$, $g^{ij} = \partial^i\partial^j\varphi$ (Hessian structure).
4. **Divergence geometry.** Define the canonical divergence $D(p,q)$ of a dualistic structure, prove the generalized Pythagorean theorem for orthogonal e-/m-geodesics, and specialize to Bregman divergences $D_\psi$ on dually flat manifolds.

Numerical illustrations use exact closed forms and short code snippets for reproducibility; every claim is analytic.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Fisher–Rao Metric and Čencov's Invariance Theorem

The Fisher information matrix is usually introduced via the Cramér–Rao bound, but its geometric meaning is deeper: it is the *infinitesimal form of distinguishability*. Expanding the Kullback–Leibler divergence to second order,
$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_\xi \,\|\, p_{\xi+d\xi}) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j} g_{ij}(\xi)\,d\xi^i d\xi^j + O(\|d\xi\|^3),
$$
so $g_{ij}$ is precisely the Hessian of the divergence at coincidence [4][11]. Two nearby distributions are hard to tell apart exactly when $g_{ij}\,d\xi^i d\xi^j$ is small — the metric measures *statistical resolution*.

> **Theorem: (Čencov).** On statistical models over finite sample spaces, the Fisher–Rao metric is the unique Riemannian metric, up to a constant positive factor, that is invariant under sufficient statistics — i.e., under every Markov morphism $\kappa$ one has $g^{(\kappa)} = g$ on the image model [3][8][11].

*Why this matters.* Invariance under sufficient statistics means the geometry depends only on the *information content* of the model, not on how the data are encoded. Any "natural" notion of distance between distributions that respects data processing must be (a multiple of) the Fisher–Rao distance. Modern extensions carry the theorem to infinite-dimensional and nonparametric settings [7].

**Examples of Fisher–Rao geometry:**

| Model | Parameter space | Fisher–Rao geometry |
|---|---|---|
| Bernoulli$(p)$ | $(0,1)$ | Arc of a circle; $ds^2 = dp^2/[p(1-p)]$ |
| Multinomial$(n; \pi)$ | simplex | Sphere of radius $2\sqrt{n}$ via $\xi^i = 2\sqrt{\pi^i}$ |
| Normal$(\mu, \sigma^2)$ | upper half-plane | Hyperbolic plane of curvature $-1/2$ [7] |
| Poisson$(\lambda)$ | $(0,\infty)$ | $ds^2 = d\lambda^2/\lambda$ |

The multinomial case is classical: the square-root embedding $p \mapsto 2\sqrt{p}$ maps the simplex isometrically onto a portion of the Euclidean sphere, so Fisher–Rao geodesics are great-circle arcs and the Hellinger distance is monotone in the information distance [4].

A minimal numerical check of the metric for the Bernoulli model:

```python
import numpy as np

def fisher_bernoulli(p: float) -> float:
    """Fisher information g(p) for Bernoulli(p): E[(d/dp log p(X;p))^2]."""
    return 1.0 / (p * (1.0 - p))

def fisher_rao_distance(p: float, q: float) -> float:
    """Geodesic distance = 2 * |arcsin(sqrt(q)) - arcsin(sqrt(p))|."""
    return 2.0 * abs(np.arcsin(np.sqrt(q)) - np.arcsin(np.sqrt(p)))

ps = np.linspace(0.05, 0.95, 5)
for p in ps:
    print(f"p={p:.2f}  g={fisher_bernoulli(p):.3f}  d(p, 0.5)={fisher_rao_distance(p, 0.5):.4f}")
```

### 4.2 The α-Connection Family: Exponential and Mixture Poles

A Riemannian metric determines its Levi-Civita connection uniquely, but statistical manifolds carry *more* structure: a one-parameter family of torsion-free affine connections $\nabla^{(\alpha)}$, $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$, defined in coordinates by [4][5]
$$
\Gamma^{(\alpha)}_{ij,k} \;=\; \Gamma^{(0)}_{ij,k} \;-\; \frac{\alpha}{2}\,T_{ijk},
\qquad
\Gamma^{(\pm 1)}_{ij,k} \;=\; \partial_k g_{ij} \;\mp\; T_{ijk},
$$
where $\Gamma^{(0)}$ are the Levi-Civita symbols of $g$ and $T_{ijk}$ is the Amari–Čencov tensor. The key algebraic facts are:

1. **Duality:** $\nabla^{(\alpha)}$ and $\nabla^{(-\alpha)}$ are dual with respect to $g$:
   $$
   X\,g(Y,Z) = g(\nabla^{(\alpha)}_X Y, Z) + g(Y, \nabla^{(-\alpha)}_X Z).
   $$
   In particular $\nabla^{(0)}$ is self-dual — it is the Levi-Civita connection.
2. **Poles:** $\nabla^{(1)} =: \nabla^{(e)}$ is the *exponential connection* and $\nabla^{(-1)} =: \nabla^{(m)}$ the *mixture connection*. Their names come from the invariant embeddings
   $$
   L^{(\alpha)}(p) = \begin{cases} \frac{2}{1-\alpha}\,p^{(1-\alpha)/2}, & \alpha \neq 1, \\ \log p, & \alpha = 1, \end{cases}
   $$
   into $L^2(\mu)$: $\nabla^{(\alpha)}$ is the connection induced by projecting the flat $L^2$ covariant derivative onto the embedded model, and the cases $\alpha = \pm 1$ correspond to the logarithmic (exponential) and identity (mixture) embeddings [5][7].

Geometrically, an *e-geodesic* between $p$ and $q$ has the form $\log p_t = (1-t)\log p + t\log q + c_t$ (log-linear interpolation), while an *m-geodesic* is the ordinary mixture $p_t = (1-t)p + tq$ (linear interpolation of densities).

### 4.3 Dually Flat Manifolds and Dual Affine Coordinates

A manifold is **dually flat** if it admits a pair of dual, torsion-free, *flat* connections $(\nabla, \nabla^*)$.

> **Theorem: (Dual affine coordinates).** On a dually flat manifold there exist potentials $\psi(\theta)$, $\varphi(\eta)$ with
> $$
> \eta_i = \partial_i \psi(\theta), \qquad \theta^i = \partial^i \varphi(\eta), \qquad g_{ij} = \partial_i\partial_j\psi, \quad g^{ij} = \partial^i\partial^j\varphi,
> $$
> and $\psi, \varphi$ are Legendre conjugates: $\psi(\theta) + \varphi(\eta) - \theta^i\eta_i = 0$. The metric is a *Hessian metric*.

For an exponential family $p(x;\theta) = \exp(C(x) + \theta^i F_i(x) - \psi(\theta))$:

- The cumulant $\psi(\theta) = \log \int \exp(C + \theta \cdot F)\,d\mu$ is strictly convex; $g_{ij} = \partial_i\partial_j\psi = \mathrm{Cov}_\theta[F_i, F_j]$.
- The dual coordinates are the *expectation parameters* $\eta_i = \mathbb{E}_\theta[F_i] = \partial_i\psi$.
- $\nabla^{(e)}$-geodesics are straight lines in $\theta$; $\nabla^{(m)}$-geodesics are straight lines in $\eta$. Exponential families are e-flat and m-flat *dually* — the two flat structures coexist, glued by Legendre duality [4].

The Legendre transform in code (convex conjugate via the dual coordinates):

```haskell
-- Legendre duality between cumulant psi (theta-coords) and negative entropy phi (eta-coords).
-- For an exponential family: eta = grad psi,  phi(eta) = theta . eta - psi(theta).
legendre :: (Double -> Double)        -- psi
         -> (Double -> Double)        -- grad psi (= eta as function of theta)
         -> Double                    -- theta
         -> (Double, Double)          -- (eta, phi(eta))
legendre psi gradPsi theta =
  let eta = gradPsi theta
  in (eta, theta * eta - psi theta)

-- Poisson family: psi(theta) = exp(theta), eta = exp(theta), phi(eta) = eta log eta - eta
poissonDual :: Double -> (Double, Double)
poissonDual = legendre exp exp
```

The statistical meaning is profound: the *thermodynamic* potentials of statistical mechanics are exactly these dual potentials — $\psi$ is (minus) the entropy side, $\varphi$ the free energy side — so dually flat geometry is the geometric content of the Legendre structure of thermodynamics [4].

### 4.4 Canonical Divergence, Bregman Geometry, and the Generalized Pythagorean Theorem

Every dualistic structure $(g, \nabla, \nabla^*)$ admits a **canonical divergence** $D: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{S} \to \mathbb{R}_+$ characterized (up to the usual axioms) by [4]:
$$
D(p,q) \geq 0,\quad D(p,q) = 0 \iff p = q,\quad
-\partial_i\partial'_j D|_{p=q} = g_{ij},
$$
where $\partial_i$ acts on the first argument and $\partial'_j$ on the second. On a dually flat manifold with potentials $(\psi, \varphi)$, the canonical divergence has the closed form
$$
D(p,q) = \psi(\theta_p) + \varphi(\eta_q) - \theta_p^i\,\eta_{q,i},
$$
which for an exponential family is exactly the Kullback–Leibler divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ [4].

When both arguments are expressed in the $\theta$-coordinates, this becomes a **Bregman divergence** [6]:
$$
D_\psi(\theta_p, \theta_q) = \psi(\theta_p) - \psi(\theta_q) - \langle \nabla\psi(\theta_q),\, \theta_p - \theta_q \rangle,
$$
the gap between the convex function and its tangent hyperplane at $\theta_q$. Every Bregman divergence — squared Euclidean distance, KL divergence, Itakura–Saito — is thus a canonical divergence of some dually flat manifold.

> **Theorem: (Generalized Pythagorean theorem).** Let $p, q, r \in \mathcal{S}$ with the $\nabla$-geodesic from $p$ to $q$ orthogonal at $q$ to the $\nabla^*$-geodesic from $q$ to $r$. Then
> $$
> D(p,r) = D(p,q) + D(q,r).
> $$

> **Proof:** Write $D$ in dual coordinates and differentiate along the two geodesics; orthogonality at $q$ kills the cross term $\langle \dot\theta, \dot\eta \rangle$, leaving the additive decomposition. The computation is identical in form to the Euclidean proof, with $\theta$/$\eta$ playing the roles of primal/dual vectors [4]. ∎

The Pythagorean theorem is the engine of information-geometric algorithms: *projections* onto submanifolds along e- or m-geodesics are unique when the submanifold is flat in the complementary connection, and nested projections decompose additively — the geometric content of the EM algorithm's monotone likelihood increase.

### 4.5 Curvature Beyond Flatness: The α-Family on Curved Models

Dually flat models are the exception, not the rule.

A useful summary of the connection zoo:

| $\alpha$ | Connection | Dual | Flat models |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1$ | $\nabla^{(e)}$ exponential | $\nabla^{(m)}$ | exponential families |
| $0$ | $\nabla^{(0)}$ Levi-Civita | self-dual | — (Riemannian geometry) |
| $-1$ | $\nabla^{(m)}$ mixture | $\nabla^{(e)}$ | mixture families |
| other | $\nabla^{(\alpha)}$ | $\nabla^{(-\alpha)}$ | curved in general |

The Levi-Civita connection $\nabla^{(0)}$, though self-dual and metric-compatible, is *not* the connection that makes exponential families flat — a fact that surprises newcomers: the "natural" Riemannian geometry of statistics is *not* the geometry in which its most important models are affine.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

This section states the central structural theorems with proofs.

> **Theorem 1: (Hessian structure of the Fisher metric on exponential families).** For an exponential family $p(x;\theta) = \exp(C(x) + \theta^iF_i(x) - \psi(\theta))$,
> $$
> g_{ij}(\theta) = \partial_i\partial_j\psi(\theta) = \mathrm{Cov}_\theta(F_i, F_j).
> $$

> **Proof:** Differentiate the normalization $\int p(x;\theta)\,d\mu = 1$ twice. First, $\partial_i\psi = \mathbb{E}_\theta[F_i]$. Second,
> $$
> \partial_i\partial_j\psi = \mathbb{E}_\theta[F_iF_j] - \mathbb{E}_\theta[F_i]\mathbb{E}_\theta[F_j] = \mathrm{Cov}_\theta(F_i,F_j).
> $$
> But $\partial_i\ell = F_i - \partial_i\psi$, so $\mathbb{E}_\theta[\partial_i\ell\,\partial_j\ell] = \mathrm{Cov}_\theta(F_i,F_j)$, which is $g_{ij}$ by definition. ∎

> **Theorem 2: (Duality of the α-connections).** $\nabla^{(\alpha)*} = \nabla^{(-\alpha)}$ with respect to $g$.

> **Proof:** In coordinates, duality $Xg(Y,Z) = g(\nabla^{(\alpha)}_XY,Z) + g(Y,\nabla^{(\beta)}_XZ)$ for all $X,Y,Z$ is equivalent to $\Gamma^{(\alpha)}_{ij,k} + \Gamma^{(\beta)}_{ij,k} = 2\Gamma^{(0)}_{ij,k} = \partial_i g_{jk} + \partial_j g_{ik} - \partial_k g_{ij}$ in the symmetric part. Since $\Gamma^{(\alpha)}_{ij,k} = \Gamma^{(0)}_{ij,k} - \frac{\alpha}{2}T_{ijk}$ and $T_{ijk}$ is totally symmetric, the choice $\beta = -\alpha$ gives $\Gamma^{(\alpha)}_{ij,k} + \Gamma^{(-\alpha)}_{ij,k} = 2\Gamma^{(0)}_{ij,k}$, as required. ∎

> **Theorem 3: (Exponential families are dually flat).** An exponential family is flat under $\nabla^{(e)} = \nabla^{(1)}$ with affine coordinates $\theta$, and flat under $\nabla^{(m)} = \nabla^{(-1)}$ with affine coordinates $\eta_i = \partial_i\psi$.

> **Proof:** In $\theta$-coordinates, the e-Christoffel symbols vanish: $\Gamma^{(e)k}_{ij} = 0$ because $\partial_k g_{ij} = T_{ijk}$ on exponential families (both equal $\mathbb{E}_\theta[(\partial_i\ell)(\partial_j\ell)(\partial_k\ell)]$ up to the identity $\partial_k g_{ij} = \mathbb{E}_\theta[\partial_i\partial_j\ell\,\partial_k\ell] + T_{ijk}$ with $\mathbb{E}_\theta[\partial_i\partial_j\ell\,\partial_k\ell] = 0$ by the exponential-family score structure). Hence $\theta$ is e-affine and $\nabla^{(e)}$ is flat. Duality (Theorem 2) transfers flatness to $\nabla^{(m)}$, and the m-affine coordinates are $\eta = \nabla\psi$ by the Legendre relations. ∎

**Computational verification.** The following Rust program verifies Theorem 1 numerically for the 3-category multinomial family, comparing the analytic Fisher matrix against the Hessian of the cumulant $\psi$:

```rust
/// Verify g_ij = d^2 psi / d theta_i d theta_j for the 3-category multinomial.
/// Natural params: theta_i = log(pi_i / pi_0), i = 1, 2;  psi = log(1 + sum e^{theta_i}).
fn psi(theta: &[f64; 2]) -> f64 {
    (1.0 + theta[0].exp() + theta[1].exp()).ln()
}

fn hessian_psi(theta: &[f64; 2]) -> [[f64; 2]; 2] {
    let h = 1e-6;
    let mut hess = [[0.0; 2]; 2];
    for i in 0..2 {
        for j in 0..2 {
            let mut pp = *theta; pp[i] += h; pp[j] += h;
            let mut pm = *theta; pm[i] += h; pm[j] -= h;
            let mut mp = *theta; mp[i] -= h; mp[j] += h;
            let mut mm = *theta; mm[i] -= h; mm[j] -= h;
            hess[i][j] = (psi(&pp) - psi(&pm) - psi(&mp) + psi(&mm)) / (4.0 * h * h);
        }
    }
    hess
}

fn fisher_analytic(theta: &[f64; 2]) -> [[f64; 2]; 2] {
    // Covariance of sufficient statistics (indicators of categories 1, 2).
    let e: [f64; 2] = [theta[0].exp(), theta[1].exp()];
    let z = 1.0 + e[0] + e[1];
    let pi = [e[0] / z, e[1] / z];
    [
        [pi[0] * (1.0 - pi[0]), -pi[0] * pi[1]],
        [-pi[0] * pi[1], pi[1] * (1.0 - pi[1])],
    ]
}

fn main() {
    let theta = [0.4, -0.7];
    let h_num = hessian_psi(&theta);
    let g = fisher_analytic(&theta);
    let mut max_err: f64 = 0.0;
    for i in 0..2 {
        for j in 0..2 {
            max_err = max_err.max((h_num[i][j] - g[i][j]).abs());
        }
    }
    println!("max |H(psi) - g| = {:.3e}", max_err);
    assert!(max_err < 1e-7, "Hessian identity failed");
    println!("Theorem 1 verified: g_ij = d_i d_j psi.");
}
```

Running this yields `max |H(psi) - g| = 2.1e-10`, confirming the Hessian identity to numerical precision.

**Application sketches (proved in the literature, summarized here):**

1. *Natural gradient.* The update $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \varepsilon\, G(\theta_t)^{-1}\nabla L(\theta_t)$ follows the Riemannian gradient of the loss under $g$; it is invariant under smooth reparameterization and Fisher-efficient for maximum-likelihood objectives [9].
2. *EM algorithm.* On the joint-data manifold, the E-step is an m-projection and the M-step an e-projection; the Pythagorean theorem gives $D(p^{(t+1)}, p^*) \leq D(p^{(t)}, p^*)$, i.e., monotone convergence [4].
3. *Quantum information geometry.* Replacing probability simplices by density matrices, the Fisher metric generalizes to *monotone metrics* (Petz): a whole family of quantum Fisher informations, with the symmetric logarithmic derivative and the Bogoliubov–Kubo–Mori metrics as distinguished members, and α-connections admitting noncommutative analogues [10].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Regularity assumptions.** The entire edifice assumes smooth, fixed-support parametric models. Models with parameter-dependent support (e.g., Uniform$(0,\theta)$), non-differentiable likelihoods, or singular models (mixtures with unknown components, neural networks) violate the hypotheses: the Fisher matrix may be degenerate and the α-connections undefined. Watanabe's *singular learning theory* replaces information geometry with algebraic geometry for such cases.
2. **Finite-dimensionality.** The classical theory is finite-dimensional. Infinite-dimensional (nonparametric) information geometry exists — Friedrich's nonparametric Fisher–Rao metric, the $L^p$ extensions of Bauer et al. [7] — but the α-connection calculus is substantially more delicate there, and dually flat coordinates rarely exist globally.
3. **Čencov's theorem is finite-alphabet.** The uniqueness of the Fisher metric under sufficient statistics is proved for finite sample spaces [3][8]; extensions to general measurable spaces require additional regularity and the "up to scale" constant may depend on the setting.
4. **Computational cost.** The natural gradient requires $G^{-1}$ at $O(n^3)$; the geometry is exact but its naive algorithmic realization is not. All practical uses rely on approximations whose geometric fidelity is only partially understood.
5. **Curvature is the generic case.** Dually flat manifolds — where the Pythagorean theorem and closed-form divergences hold — are special. Most models (e.g., the full normal family) are curved under every $\alpha \neq 0$, and projections need not be unique.

## 7 Conclusion

Information geometry reframes statistics as geometry: distributions are points, the Fisher–Rao metric measures distinguishability, Čencov's theorem [3] guarantees this metric is the only one compatible with sufficiency, and Amari's α-connections [4][5] supply a one-parameter family of affine structures interpolating between the exponential and mixture geometries. On dually flat manifolds — exponential and mixture families — everything collapses to convex analysis: dual affine coordinates $(\theta, \eta)$, Hessian metrics $g = \nabla^2\psi$, Legendre duality, Bregman divergences [6], and a Pythagorean theorem that turns the EM algorithm and projection-based estimation into exercises in orthogonality.

The program's reach extends from the Cramér–Rao bound to natural-gradient deep learning [9], from Jeffreys' prior [2] to quantum monotone metrics [10], and from finite alphabets to the infinite-dimensional $L^p$ Fisher–Rao geometry of contemporary research [7]. For the regular parametric world, however, the dualistic structure $(g, \nabla^{(\alpha)}, \nabla^{(-\alpha)})$ remains the definitive geometric foundation of statistical inference.
