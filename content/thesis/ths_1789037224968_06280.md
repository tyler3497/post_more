# Succinct Zero-Knowledge Arguments for Verifiable Computation: Arithmetization, the Sumcheck Protocol, FRI, and the SNARK–STARK Design Space

## Abstract

Succinct zero-knowledge arguments have matured from theoretical curiosities into deployed infrastructure for verifiable computation, powering private payments, rollups, and verifiable machine learning. This thesis develops a unified account of the modern proving stack: the arithmetization pipeline that reduces programs to polynomial constraint systems — R1CS, quadratic arithmetic programs, Plonkish gates, and algebraic intermediate representations; the sumcheck protocol of Lund, Fortnow, Karloff, and Nisan, the algebraic engine beneath nearly every interactive proof of this generation; polynomial commitment schemes — KZG, the inner-product argument, and FRI — that bind algebraic claims to cryptographic hardness; and the round-by-round assembly of Groth16, Plonk, and STARK systems from these parts. We compare asymptotic and concrete tradeoffs across pairing-based, hash-based, and multilinear designs, summarize empirical prover and verifier costs, and examine open problems in recursion, post-quantum succinctness, and constraint-system safety.

---

## 1 Introduction

A zero-knowledge proof lets a prover convince a verifier that a computation was performed correctly while revealing nothing about private inputs. The modern turn came with *succinctness*: proofs far smaller than the computation they certify, and verification asymptotically faster than re-execution. SNARKs add *non-interactivity* — one message, checked once — and *knowledge soundness*, guaranteeing the prover actually knew a witness.

The deployed landscape divides along two axes. **Pairing-based SNARKs**, from the QAP lineage of Gennaro, Gentry, Parno, and Raykova [6] to Groth's three-element construction [1], achieve constant-size proofs and constant-time verification at the price of a per-circuit trusted setup and non-post-quantum security. **Transparent systems** — STARKs [3] and the sumcheck/FRI family [4][5] — trade larger proofs for setups with no secret trapdoors and security resting only on hashing. Between them, Plonk [2] introduced the universal updatable SRS, Sonic [8] made that architecture practical first, and HyperPlonk [7] moved Plonk onto the hypercube.

This thesis treats all of these as *compilers* sharing one intermediate representation: arithmetic is arithmetized into polynomials, polynomial identities are argued interactively, and commitments plus Fiat–Shamir render the argument non-interactive. The intellectual core is the sumcheck protocol; we develop the material pipeline-first.

---

## 2 Background and Related Work

The prehistory of succinct arguments is the history of interactive proofs. Lund, Fortnow, Karloff, and Nisan introduced the sumcheck protocol to show every language in the polynomial hierarchy has an interactive proof — the result feeding Shamir's **IP = PSPACE** [5]. Goldwasser, Kalai, and Rothblum then gave *bounded-depth* computations interactive proofs with nearly-linear-time verifiers — the GKR protocol — by running sumcheck layer-by-layer down an arithmetic circuit [9]. GKR is the ancestor of every "linear prover, logarithmic verifier" claim in the literature.

The first practical SNARKs took a different route. Quadratic arithmetic programs reduced *n* multiplication gates to polynomials *A(x), B(x), C(x)* with *A(x)·B(x) − C(x)* divisible by a fixed vanishing polynomial [6]. Groth16 [1] minimized this to **three group elements** verified by a **single pairing-product equation**, with quasilinear prover time. The cost: a *circuit-specific* common reference string whose trapdoor could forge proofs.

Two responses reshaped the field. First, the *universal SRS* program: Sonic [8] showed one updatable reference string could serve all circuits up to a size bound; Plonk [2] made it practical via Lagrange-basis evaluations and a grand-product permutation argument. Second, *transparency*: STARKs [3] abandoned pairings, encoding execution traces as algebraic intermediate representations and proving low-degree-ness of Reed–Solomon codewords with FRI [4], committing via Merkle trees. FRI is arguably the most reused primitive of the last decade.

## 3 Methodology

Every system is analyzed against four properties:

1. **Completeness and soundness** — honest provers convince; dishonest ones fail except negligibly. *Knowledge* soundness is tracked separately, since SNARKs are arguments of knowledge with extractors.
2. **Succinctness** — proof size and verifier time sublinear in the computation: ideally *O(λ)* (Groth16 [1], Plonk [2]) or polylogarithmic (STARKs [3]).
3. **Setup transparency** — *per-circuit trusted* (Groth16), *universal updatable* (Plonk, Sonic [8]), or *transparent* (STARKs, FRI-based systems [4]).
4. **Concrete efficiency** — measured prover/verifier costs alongside asymptotics, since constants decide deployments.

Analytically we use the *polynomial IOP* lens: a proving system is the composition

> **arithmetization** → **polynomial interactive oracle proof** → **polynomial commitment scheme** → **Fiat–Shamir**

It explains the field's plug-and-play character: swap KZG for FRI and a Plonk-family system becomes STARK-family; swap univariate sumcheck for multilinear and Plonk becomes HyperPlonk [7].

We further distinguish *univariate* designs (Groth16, Plonk) from *multilinear* designs (HyperPlonk, GKR-style: the Boolean hypercube {0,1}^n). The choice dictates the commitment scheme and the recursion story.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Arithmetization: From Programs to Polynomial Constraints

Arithmetization reduces "this program ran correctly" to "these polynomials satisfy these identities." The dominant constraint systems:

- **R1CS.** Witness vector *w*; each constraint *(A·w) ∘ (B·w) = (C·w)*. Simple, universal, native to Groth16 toolchains.
- **QAP.** R1CS interpolated into polynomials *A(x), B(x), C(x)* over domain *H*; satisfiability becomes *A(x)B(x) − C(x) = H(x)Z_H(x)* with *Z_H* vanishing on *H* [6] — the bridge from combinatorics to algebra that pairing SNARKs exploit.
- **Plonkish.** Each gate enforces *q_M·a·b + q_L·a + q_R·b + q_O·c + q_C = 0*, selectors choosing gate types per row; wiring via a permutation argument over three cosets [2]. Custom gates and lookup tables extend this without changing the protocol skeleton.
- **AIR.** STARKs [3] unroll *time*: the trace is a table of registers × cycles; constraints are polynomials over neighboring rows. Natural for repetitive VM computation, poor for irregular control flow.

The Python sketch shows the R1CS check every pairing-based prover evaluates before committing:

```python
def r1cs_satisfied(A, B, C, w, F):
    """Check (A w) * (B w) == (C w) entrywise over field F."""
    for i in range(len(A)):
        lhs = sum(A[i][j] * w[j] for j in range(len(w))) % F
        mid = sum(B[i][j] * w[j] for j in range(len(w))) % F
        rhs = sum(C[i][j] * w[j] for j in range(len(w))) % F
        if (lhs * mid) % F != rhs % F:
            return False, i
    return True, None

# Prove knowledge of x with x * x = 4, witness w = [1, x, x^2]
F = 2**255 - 19
print(r1cs_satisfied([[0,1,0]], [[0,1,0]], [[0,0,1]], [1,2,4], F))  # (True, None)
```

Arithmetization dominates prover engineering: QAP needs large FFTs per witness polynomial; AIR needs Reed–Solomon trace extension (blowup 4–16×); Plonkish needs coset FFTs. *Constraint count* is the universal currency.

### 4.2 Polynomial Commitment Schemes: KZG, IPA, and FRI

A polynomial commitment binds the prover to *f* with a short digest, then lets her open *f(z) = y* convincingly — the cryptographic glue of every SNARK:

| Scheme | Commitment | Opening proof | Assumption | Transparency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **KZG** [10] | 1 𝔾₁ element | 1 𝔾₁ element | *q*-SDH / pairing | Trusted SRS |
| **Inner-product argument** | 1 group element | *O(log n)* | Discrete log | Transparent |
| **FRI** [4] | Merkle root | *O(log² n)* hashes | Collision-resistant hash | Transparent, post-quantum |

**KZG** [10] commits as *g^{f(τ)}* with SRS trapdoor *τ*; evaluation proofs are single group elements checked by one pairing equation — the reason Plonk's verifier is constant-time. Its price is the trapdoor.

**FRI** commits to *evaluations* of *f* over domain *D* in a Merkle tree, then proves proximity to a low-degree polynomial by *folding*: each round the verifier sends challenge *z* and the prover commits to *f'(y) = (f(x)+f(−x))/2 + z·(f(x)−f(−x))/(2x)* over the halved domain; after *log N* rounds the degree claim is directly checkable. Prover: strictly linear (< *6N* operations); verifier: *O(log N)*; query complexity *2 log N*; constant soundness [4]. It is the engine of STARKs and every hash-based SNARK.

The Rust sketch captures the KZG verification equation at the heart of Plonk's verifier [2][10]:

```rust
// KZG opening check: e(C - g1*y, g2) == e(proof, g2*(tau - z))
fn kzg_verify(
    pairing: &dyn PairingEngine,
    c: G1, proof: G1, y: Fr,
    g1: G1, g2_tau_minus_z: G2, g2: G2,
) -> bool {
    let lhs = pairing.pair(c - g1 * y, g2);         // e(C - [y]1, [1]2)
    let rhs = pairing.pair(proof, g2_tau_minus_z);  // e(pi, [tau - z]2)
    lhs == rhs
}
```

KZG buys tiny proofs at the cost of a ceremony; FRI buys transparency at the cost of proof size. Hybrids — Plonk with FRI, STARKs with KZG — are now routine.

### 4.3 The Sumcheck Protocol and Its Descendants

> **Theorem (Sumcheck [5]).** Let *g: F^n → F* have degree at most *d* per variable, and *H = Σ_{x∈{0,1}^n} g(x)*. There is a public-coin, *n*-round interactive proof where the prover sends one degree-*d* univariate polynomial per round and the verifier, after *n* challenges, checks a single evaluation *g(r₁,…,rₙ)*. Perfect completeness; soundness error ≤ *nd/|F|*.

The mechanism: in round *j* the prover sends *g_j(X_j) = Σ_{b_{j+1}…b_n} g(r₁,…,r_{j−1}, X_j, b_{j+1},…,b_n)*; the verifier checks *g_j(0)+g_j(1)* against the claimed value, samples *r_j*, and recurses. A verifier facing *2^n* terms of work does *O(nd)* plus one oracle evaluation of *g*.

Everything modern is sumcheck with better bookkeeping:

1. **GKR** [9] applies sumcheck per *layer* of an arithmetic circuit, reducing a claim about layer *i* to layer *i+1* via the wiring predicate's multilinear extension. Verifier time *O(d log S)* for depth *d*, size *S*.
2. **HyperPlonk** [7] replaces Plonk's univariate gate and permutation checks with *multivariate* sumchecks over the hypercube — eliminating FFTs and permitting custom gates of *arbitrary* degree, since the prover evaluates the gate polynomial directly on {0,1}^n.
3. **Spartan, Virgo, Orion** push the same idea under different commitments (discrete-log, FRI, linear-time hash-based), showing sumcheck is commitment-agnostic.

The TLA⁺ fragment specifies the *folding invariant* at the heart of FRI's recursive degree reduction [4] — the hash-based analogue of sumcheck's round reduction:

```tla
---- MODULE Folding ----
EXTENDS Naturals
CONSTANTS F, D
VARIABLES f, round

Fold(f, z) == [y \in Halve(D) |->
    (f[Pre1(y)] + f[Pre2(y)]) / 2
      + z * (f[Pre1(y)] - f[Pre2(y)]) / (2 * Pre1(y))]

Next == \E z \in F : /\ f' = Fold(f, z) /\ round' = round + 1
\* Invariant: if f is delta-far from RS[D], then w.h.p. over z,
\* Fold(f, z) is (1 - o(1))*delta-far from RS[Halve(D)].
====
```

Sumcheck reduces *sums* over the hypercube; FRI reduces *degree* claims; GKR reduces *layer* claims — all round-by-round algebraic reductions sharing one soundness skeleton.

### 4.4 End-to-End Constructions: Groth16, Plonk, and STARKs

**Groth16** [1] remains the efficiency ceiling for non-recursive proofs. Setup is per-circuit: sample trapdoor *τ*, publish SRS powers and circuit terms. Proving computes randomized linear combinations of the SRS with the witness polynomials; the three group elements *(A, B, C)* total ~200 bytes. Verification is a *single* equation — *e(A,B) = e(α,β)·e(L,γ)·e(C,δ)*, three effective pairings. Prover time is *O(n log n)* (FFTs plus multiexponentiations). Nothing in production verifies faster, which is why Groth16 anchors on-chain verifiers where pairings are priced in gas.

**Plonk** [2] restructured the same ideas around *evaluations on a subgroup* instead of monomial coefficients. Its five-round protocol: (1) commit to wire polynomials; (2) commit to the permutation grand product *z*; (3) commit to the quotient *t*; (4) open everything at challenge *ζ*; (5) batch openings with one KZG multi-opening proof. The jewel is the permutation argument: wiring becomes *∏ (w_i + β·σ(i) + γ) = ∏ (w_i + β·i + γ)*, checked via the accumulator *z*. Plonk's Lagrange-basis arithmetization cut prover exponentiations roughly 7.5–20× over Sonic [8].

**STARKs** [3] run the same compiler with different parts: AIR arithmetization, trace extended to a Reed–Solomon codeword, constraint composition into one polynomial, FRI [4] proving low-degree-ness, Merkle commitments, Fiat–Shamir. No trapdoor anywhere — "setup" is choosing a hash function. The price: 40–200+ KB proofs and a prover materializing the full extended trace; the reward: post-quantum security and polylogarithmic verification even for very long computations.

| System | Proof size | Verifier | Setup | Post-quantum | Prover |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Groth16** [1] | ~200 B | 3 pairings | Per-circuit trusted | No | *O(n log n)* |
| **Plonk** [2] | ~400 B | *O(λ)* pairings | Universal, updatable | No | *O(n log n)* |
| **zk-STARK** [3] | ~45–200 KB | *O(log² n)* hashes | Transparent | Yes | *O(n log² n)* |
| **HyperPlonk** [7] | *O(log n)* | *O(log n)* | Universal, updatable | Depends on PCS | *O(n)* |

### 4.5 Recursion, Aggregation, and Composition

A SNARK verifying *another SNARK's* verifier circuit yields **recursion**: unbounded computation in bounded proof size via incrementally verifiable computation. The obstacle is arithmetization mismatch — verifier field operations must be expressed in the outer proof's constraint system. Two answers dominate: *cycles of elliptic curves* (each curve's scalar field is the other's base field, cheapening the pairing verifier) and *folding schemes*, which accumulate instances into one running claim with logarithmic per-step verifier work, deferring the expensive check. Halo-style accumulation showed recursion needs no trusted setup when the commitment is discrete-log based (Bowe, Grigg, Hopwood, 2019).

**Aggregation** is the batch sibling: hundreds of proofs compressed into one on-chain verification, amortizing pairing costs across a rollup block. Both rely on the polynomial-IOP modularity of §3 — recursion composes *verifiers as circuits*, aggregation composes *proofs as inputs* — and both are where current engineering concentrates, since proof *generation*, not size, is now the bottleneck.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

Representative measured costs:

- **Groth16** [1]: ~2²⁰ constraints proven in low tens of seconds; verification ~1–3 ms; proof 128–200 bytes. The verifier is essentially optimal.
- **Plonk** [2]: ~1.3–2× slower proving than Groth16 at equal constraint counts (permutation argument, extra FFTs); verification ~4–8 ms; proof ~400 bytes. The universal SRS amortizes setup across all circuits, dominating organizational-scale comparisons.
- **zk-STARK** [3]: 2²⁰ steps proven in seconds to a minute depending on blowup factor and hash choice; proofs 45–200 KB; verification 5–30 ms. The prover's memory footprint — the full Merkle-committed trace — is the binding constraint.
- **HyperPlonk** [7]: no FFT; reported multi-× prover speedups over Plonk on custom-gate-heavy circuits, proof size *O(log n)* under multilinear commitments. Largest wins exactly where Plonk is weakest: high-degree custom gates.

Soundness rests on three pillars: sumcheck [5], where a cheating prover survives round *j* only if *r_j* hits one of ≤ *d* roots (union bound *nd/|F|*); FRI [4], where folding preserves code distance up to *(1−o(1))*, reducing degree-testing to a direct check; and knowledge-soundness reductions — Groth16 [1] and Plonk [2] to *q*-type assumptions, STARKs [3] to FRI plus the random-oracle model.

Engineering subtlety: concrete Fiat–Shamir hashes must bind *every* prior message — public inputs and preprocessed polynomials included — into each challenge; weak-FS instantiations have burned deployed systems.

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

**The prover is the bottleneck.** Even linear-time provers [4][7] make proving 10–1000× costlier than native execution; GPU/FPGA acceleration helps pairing-based provers most.

**Post-quantum succinctness is unresolved.** STARKs [3] are plausibly post-quantum but not succinct in the strong sense; lattice-based SNARKs remain far from practical. A post-quantum system with Groth16-class proof sizes is the field's most wanted primitive.

**Setups are a social problem.** Universal updatable ceremonies [2][8] reduce trust to one-of-many honesty, but thousand-participant ceremonies are operationally heavy, and the toxic-waste narrative still slows adoption.

**Arithmetization is manual and error-prone.** Under-constrained circuits admitting forged witnesses are the dominant real-world vulnerability class — ahead of any cryptographic break. Formal verification of circuits lags deployment.

**Recursion's field-mismatch tax.** Verifying a pairing inside its own constraint system costs tens of thousands of constraints; cycles and folding amortize but don't eliminate it. Native recursion-friendly arithmetization remains open.

**Sumcheck's verifier asymmetry.** GKR-style systems [9] verify in *O(d log S)* but prover bookkeeping is intricate with poor memory profiles; closing the gap — HyperPlonk [7] the current best answer — is active work.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The modern zero-knowledge stack is a compiler: programs become constraint systems, constraints become polynomial identities, identities become interactive arguments via sumcheck and its descendants [5][9], and commitments [4][10] plus Fiat–Shamir make them non-interactive and succinct. Groth16 [1] showed how small proofs could be; Plonk [2] and Sonic [8] showed the setup need not be per-circuit; STARKs [3] and FRI [4] showed it need not exist at all; HyperPlonk [7] showed the hypercube can beat the FFT. The QAP reduction [6] that started the practical lineage is still visible in every system. The frontier is prover cost, post-quantum succinctness, and constraint-system safety.
