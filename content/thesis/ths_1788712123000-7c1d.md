# PostgreSQL Storage Engine Internals: MVCC Snapshots, the Buffer Manager, Write-Ahead Logging, and B-Tree Concurrency under Lehman–Yao

## Abstract

This thesis presents a unified treatment of four subsystems that jointly define the PostgreSQL storage engine: multi-version concurrency control (MVCC) snapshots, the shared buffer manager with its clock-sweep replacement policy, write-ahead logging (WAL) for crash recovery, and the nbtree B-tree implementation rooted in the Lehman–Yao B-link tree algorithm. We reconstruct the tuple visibility predicate over `xmin`/`xmax` header fields, the snapshot acquisition protocol, and the commit-log consult path; the pin/reference-count discipline of `BufferDesc` and its interaction with checkpoint and background writer processes; the physiological WAL record chain, full-page-write protection, and redo-point calculus that bound recovery time; and the latch-coupling, right-link traversal, and node-splitting protocol that allow concurrent readers and writers to share a B-tree without blocking. The synthesis argues that PostgreSQL's durability and concurrency guarantees are not properties of any single mechanism but of a carefully staged composition — versioning for isolation, write-ahead logging for atomicity and durability, buffer discipline for performance, and B-link concurrency for index scalability — whose failure modes and performance trade-offs we analyze in depth.

## 1 Introduction

A database storage engine is a study in controlled contradiction. It must present many transactions with an illusion of serial, private access to shared state while executing those transactions concurrently on physical hardware that can lose power at any instant. PostgreSQL's answer to this contradiction is unusually legible: four cooperating subsystems, each with a sharp responsibility, each designed around a single classical idea. **Concurrency** is governed by *multi-version concurrency control* (MVCC) — readers never block writers and writers never block readers [2]. **Durability** is governed by *write-ahead logging* (WAL), in the ARIES tradition of physiological logging and repeat-history recovery [3]. **Memory performance** is governed by the *shared buffer manager*, a clock-sweep cache with pin semantics that mediates every page access in the system. And **index concurrency** is governed by the *Lehman–Yao B-link tree* protocol [1], implemented in `src/backend/access/nbtree`, where searches descend without holding read latches on upper levels because every page carries a right-link to its sibling.

This thesis reconstructs each of these mechanisms from first principles and from the PostgreSQL source record, shows how they compose — snapshots gate visibility, WAL makes every buffer mutation recoverable, the buffer manager bounds memory pressure, and B-link locking keeps the index hot — and examines where the composition strains: tuple bloat and vacuuming costs under MVCC, checkpoint-induced write storms, buffer replacement pathology under sequential scans, and split-visibility races in the index.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Locking to Versioning

Classical concurrency control serializes access with two-phase locking: readers hold shared locks, writers hold exclusive locks, and conflicts block [5]. PostgreSQL instead keeps *multiple physical versions* of every row. Each heap tuple carries `t_xmin` (the inserting transaction's identifier) and `t_xmax` (the deleting or superseding transaction's identifier, or `0` if still live) in a 23-byte `HeapTupleHeaderData` on most architectures [2]. A transaction determines which versions it may see by consulting a *snapshot* — a logical timestamp capturing the set of transactions that were in progress when the snapshot was taken. The visibility decision is a pure function of the tuple header, the snapshot, and the transaction commit log; no row lock is ever acquired by a reader.

### 2.2 The WAL Contract

Write-ahead logging is the mechanism that lets the buffer manager be lazy and the system be durable. The contract is simple and absolute: *a change must be recorded on stable storage before the changed page is written to stable storage*. ARIES formalized this into physiological logging — log records describe changes at the level of physical pages while carrying logical (operation-level) semantics — and a three-phase recovery of analysis, redo, and undo [3]. PostgreSQL's WAL is a physical/logical hybrid: most records describe physical changes to pages, some describe logical changes, and every record is chained by log sequence number (LSN), allowing replay to be idempotent.

### 2.3 B-Trees under Concurrency

B-trees concentrate concurrency at their roots: every search passes through the root, so naive locking serializes all traffic. The landmark 1981 result of Lehman and Yao [1] broke this bottleneck with *B-link trees*. Two structural additions — a **right-link** pointer from each node to its right sibling and a **high key** bounding each node's key range — let a search that arrives at a node *after* a concurrent split detect the split (the search key exceeds the node's high key) and follow right-links until it reaches the correct node. Readers never block writers at the structural level; latch coupling is confined to a constant number of pages.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is source-code archaeology guided by the primary literature. We take PostgreSQL's documented semantics [2] as the specification, the primary papers — Lehman–Yao [1], ARIES [3], Graefe's B-tree surveys [4][5] — as the theoretical frame, and the codebase's own README files as the engineering bridge between them [8]. Each subsystem is analyzed through the same lens:

1. **State**: the on-disk and in-memory representation (tuple headers, buffer descriptors, WAL records, index page opcodes).
2. **Protocol**: the sequence of operations that preserves the invariant (visibility rules, pin discipline, log-then-write ordering, latch-coupling with right-link repair).
3. **Invariant**: the correctness property maintained (snapshot consistency, recoverability, cache coherence, B-link tree well-formedness).
4. **Cost**: the asymptotic and practical price (bloat, LSN bandwidth, replacement misses, split propagation).

We validate claims against documented behavior and widely reproduced operational experience rather than single-installation benchmarks, and we mark clearly where behavior is version-dependent.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 MVCC: Snapshots, Visibility, and the Commit Log

A PostgreSQL snapshot is a triple: `xmin` (the oldest still-active transaction ID), `xmax` (the next transaction ID to be assigned), and `xip` (the sorted array of transaction IDs in progress between them). Any transaction with ID below `xmin` is known-committed; any at or above `xmax` is known-not-yet-started; IDs in between are checked against `xip`. This representation is compact — typically a handful of integers — yet expressive enough to decide visibility for every tuple.

The visibility predicate, implemented in `HeapTupleSatisfiesMVCC` (`src/backend/utils/time/tqual.c`), proceeds as follows:

1. If `t_xmin` is invalid (aborted inserter) → **invisible**.
2. If `t_xmin` is current transaction's own ID: the tuple is visible iff the inserting command has committed within this transaction (command-ID check), unless deleted by the current transaction.
3. Otherwise `t_xmin` must be *committed before the snapshot*: `TransactionIdPrecedes(t_xmin, snapshot->xmin)` or committed and not in `xip`. If it was still in progress at snapshot time → **invisible**.
4. If `t_xmax` is invalid → **visible**.
5. If `t_xmax` is the current transaction's own → **invisible** (deleted by self).
6. If `t_xmax` committed before the snapshot → **invisible** (deleted before we looked).
7. If `t_xmax` was in progress at snapshot time → **visible** (the delete is not yet real for us).

> **Theorem:** *Snapshot closure.* A snapshot defines a consistent cut over the transaction partial order: if transaction T's snapshot observes tuple version v written by transaction U, then it observes *every* committed effect of every transaction V whose commit precedes U's commit. *Proof sketch.* Snapshot acquisition atomically copies the in-progress set while holding `ProcArrayLock`; visibility depends only on commit order relative to that cut, and PostgreSQL assigns XIDs monotonically at transaction start, so "committed before the cut" is a prefix of commit history. ∎

Consulting the commit log (`pg_xact`, formerly `pg_clog`) for every tuple would be prohibitive, so PostgreSQL caches the verdict in *hint bits* on the tuple header (`HEAP_XMIN_COMMITTED`, `HEAP_XMAX_INVALID`, etc.). The first backend to resolve a tuple's status sets the hint bit; subsequent readers skip the CLOG lookup entirely. This is a classic read-amplification optimization: a cold tuple costs a CLOG page access, a warm tuple costs nothing.

The update path makes the version chain explicit:

```c
/* Simplified heap_update: mark old, insert new, link via t_ctid */
oldtuple->t_data->t_xmax = GetCurrentTransactionId();
newtuple->t_data->t_xmin = GetCurrentTransactionId();
newtuple->t_data->t_xmax = InvalidTransactionId;
ItemPointerSet(&oldtuple->t_data->t_ctid,
               newtuple->t_self.ip_blkid, newtuple->t_self.ip_pos);
```

`t_ctid` chains versions physically: for an updated tuple it points to the successor version, otherwise it points to itself. HOT (heap-only tuple) updates exploit this further — when the indexed columns are unchanged, the new version is placed on the *same page* and no index maintenance occurs, since index entries pointing at the old `ctid` can follow the chain [8].

**Isolation levels** are snapshot disciplines: *Read Committed* acquires a fresh snapshot per statement; *Repeatable Read* and *Serializable* hold one snapshot for the whole transaction. Serializable adds *Serializable Snapshot Isolation* (SSI) — predicate-lock tracking that detects read-write dependency cycles and aborts one participant, converting the snapshot-isolation anomaly (write skew) into a retryable serialization failure rather than silent corruption [2].

| Isolation level | Snapshot scope | Phenomena prevented |
|---|---|---|
| Read Committed | per statement | dirty reads |
| Repeatable Read | per transaction | dirty reads, non-repeatable reads |
| Serializable (SSI) | per transaction + predicate locks | all anomalies, incl. write skew |

### 4.2 The Buffer Manager: Pins, Clock Sweep, and the Writer Processes

Every page access in PostgreSQL passes through the shared buffer pool — a fixed-size array of 8 KiB buffers (default `shared_buffers`, commonly 128 MiB–32 GiB) described by `BufferDesc` entries in shared memory. Each descriptor carries a buffer *tag* (relation, fork, block number), a *pin count* (`refcount`), a *usage count* (`usage_count`), and state flags including dirty status. The discipline is strict:

- **Pin before touch.** A backend must increment the pin count (`PinBuffer`) before reading or writing a buffer's contents. Pins are short-lived — held across a single page operation, not a transaction — and they forbid eviction, never I/O.
- **Dirty before unpin.** Any mutation marks the buffer dirty; the WAL record for the mutation is already in the WAL buffers, preserving the write-ahead invariant.
- **Clock sweep for replacement.** When a free buffer is needed, the clock hand scans descriptors: pinned buffers are skipped, `usage_count` is decremented on each pass, and the first buffer with `usage_count == 0` is chosen as victim. Frequently accessed pages accumulate usage counts and survive; one-shot sequential scans (which PostgreSQL further throttles with a small 256 KiB *scan ring*) do not flush the cache.

```python
# Clock-sweep victim selection, idealized
def clock_sweep(buffers, hand):
    while True:
        desc = buffers[hand]
        hand = (hand + 1) % len(buffers)
        if desc.pin_count > 0:
            continue                      # in use: never evict
        if desc.usage_count > 0:
            desc.usage_count -= 1         # second chance
            continue
        return desc                       # victim
```

Two background processes keep the pool healthy. The **background writer** (`bgwriter`) periodically writes dirty buffers so that checkpoints find less work; the **checkpointer** performs checkpoints: it writes all dirty buffers, emits a checkpoint WAL record containing the redo pointer, and `fsync`s data files. The redo pointer is the recovery bound — crash replay starts there, so checkpoint frequency directly trades *runtime I/O smoothness* against *recovery time* (`checkpoint_timeout`, `max_wal_size`).

Buffer I/O and WAL are coupled by a single rule enforced in `XLogWrite`/`FlushBuffer`: a dirty page may not reach disk until the WAL records describing its changes are durable. Each page header stores the LSN of its latest change (`pd_lsn`); during replay, a record is applied only if its LSN exceeds the page's `pd_lsn`, making redo idempotent even if recovery itself crashes [3].

### 4.3 Write-Ahead Logging: Records, Full Pages, and Recovery

A PostgreSQL WAL record has a fixed header (`XLogRecord`: total length, transaction ID, previous-record pointer `xl_prev`, resource manager ID, info flags) followed by block references and payload data. Records are chained by `xl_prev` per transaction and ordered globally by LSN — a 64-bit byte offset into the WAL stream. Resource managers (heap, btree, gin, xlog, etc.) define their own record types; replay dispatches on the manager ID.

Three mechanisms make this WAL production-grade:

1. **Full-page writes (FPW).** Between checkpoints, the first modification of a page writes the *entire page image* into WAL. This closes the torn-page hole: a crash mid-`write()` can leave a page half old, half new, and without the pre-checkpoint image, redo from an earlier LSN could apply changes onto a torn page. FPW costs bandwidth — it is the dominant WAL volume on write-heavy workloads — but it makes every page reconstructible.
2. **WAL segment management and archiving.** WAL is a stream of 16 MiB segments; completed segments are recycled or archived (`archive_command`), feeding point-in-time recovery and streaming replication. The standby replays the same record stream the primary wrote — replication *is* crash recovery, deferred in time and space.
3. **Bounded recovery.** Restart reads the latest checkpoint record, sets the redo pointer, and replays forward. Because the redo pointer advances with each checkpoint, recovery time is bounded by `max_wal_size`-scale I/O regardless of database size — a property ARIES established as the standard for industrial recovery [3].

```pgsql
-- Observing the WAL/LSN machinery from SQL
SELECT pg_current_wal_lsn();                       -- current insert pointer
SELECT pg_walfile_name(pg_current_wal_lsn());       -- containing segment
CHECKPOINT;                                        -- force redo-point advance
SELECT * FROM pg_stat_bgwriter;                    -- writer/checkpointer stats
```

The WAL also carries *logical* change information used by logical decoding: the same record stream that guarantees durability feeds change-data-capture, a unification that few other engines achieve without a second log.

### 4.4 B-Link Trees: Lehman–Yao Concurrency in nbtree

PostgreSQL's default index, nbtree (`src/backend/access/nbtree`), is a B+-tree variant implementing the Lehman–Yao protocol [1][8]. Every page carries two pieces of concurrency metadata:

- **Right-link** (`btpo_next`): the block number of the right sibling.
- **High key**: the upper bound of keys this page may hold (stored as the first data item on non-rightmost pages).

Search proceeds with *latch coupling* down the tree, holding at most a small constant number of page latches — but the crucial L&Y property is *repair on arrival*: when the search descends to a child via a parent pointer, it checks the key against the child's high key. If the key is greater, a concurrent split has moved the target range right; the search follows `btpo_next` until the high key covers the search key. *No read latch on the parent is needed during this repair*, and upper-level splits can complete without invalidating in-flight searches.

Insertion may split a page: roughly half the items move to a new right sibling, the parent receives a downlink with the separator key, and — critically — the split is *not atomic across pages*. Readers tolerate the transient state because the right-link/high-key structure is always navigable. Deletion follows the Lanin–Shasha simplification: pages are only half-emptied logically, and physical merging was long deferred (modern versions implement bottom-up deletion of empty pages).

> **Theorem:** *L&Y search correctness.* A search that begins at the root and follows downlinks, repairing via right-links whenever the search key exceeds a page's high key, terminates at the unique leaf whose key range contains the search key, regardless of concurrent splits. *Proof sketch.* Splits preserve the invariant that key ranges partition each level left-to-right with right-links chaining the partition in order; each right-link step strictly increases the high key, and high keys are bounded above by +∞, so repair terminates; the leaf reached has `low < key ≤ high`, and L&Y key uniqueness (heap TID as tiebreaker) makes it unique [1][8]. ∎

PostgreSQL adds engineering atop the theory: **suffix truncation** (pivot tuples store only the distinguishing key prefix), **deduplication** of repeated leaf entries, and **index-only scans** via the visibility map. Graefe's surveys [4][5] place these in the broader taxonomy of B-tree techniques — latch/lock separation, key-range locking, prefix compression — confirming nbtree as one of the most complete production realizations of the L&Y design.

## 5 Empirical Evidence

The claims above are not merely theoretical; they are observable in any running cluster and documented across the literature:

- **MVCC concurrency.** Under mixed read-write workloads, PostgreSQL sustains reader throughput independent of writer activity — the defining MVCC signature [2]. The cost appears as *bloat*: dead versions accumulate until `VACUUM` reclaims them, gated by the cluster-wide xmin horizon (the oldest snapshot any backend, slot, or prepared transaction still needs). Long-running transactions therefore cause *cluster-wide* bloat, a failure mode operators monitor via `pg_stat_user_tables.n_dead_tup`.
- **WAL durability.** Crash-recovery correctness is exercised continuously: every unclean shutdown replays from the redo pointer, and the WAL stream simultaneously feeds streaming replicas. The dominant tunable trade-off is FPW volume versus torn-page safety; disabling `full_page_writes` is supported only with filesystem-level atomicity guarantees.
- **Buffer manager behavior.** The clock-sweep algorithm with usage counts approximates LRU at O(1) amortized cost per access, and the scan-ring optimization prevents sequential scans from evicting the working set — a pathology (cache "pollution") that naive LRU exhibits. `pg_buffercache` exposes the pin/usage/dirty state of every buffer for direct inspection.
- **B-link scalability.** L&Y's constant-latch property means index descent scales with core count until the root page latch itself becomes the bottleneck — precisely the contention Graefe's locking survey [4] analyzes, and the reason modern work pursues latch-free and cache-conscious variants [5]. PostgreSQL mitigates root contention in practice through short latch hold times (no I/O while holding index latches) and the repair protocol that keeps searches moving during splits.

## 6 Limitations

No honest treatment omits the costs. **MVCC's versioning** trades write amplification and vacuum load for non-blocking reads; write-heavy workloads on PostgreSQL spend significant I/O on tuple headers, version chains, and vacuum passes that a locking engine avoids. **Snapshot isolation** is not serializability without SSI, and SSI's predicate tracking adds overhead plus abort-retry burden on the application. **WAL's full-page writes** can double log bandwidth on random-update workloads, and checkpoint spikes remain a tuning-sensitive phenomenon despite bgwriter smoothing. **Clock sweep** is a heuristic: adversarial access patterns (large nested-loop joins over cold data) still thrash the pool, and pin-count bugs historically caused some of the subtlest backend crashes. **B-link trees** pay per-page metadata overhead (right-links, high keys) and their split protocol, while non-blocking for readers, still serializes structural modifications through page latches — the root remains a hot spot, and suffix truncation's correctness proofs are delicate [8]. Finally, this thesis treats a single-node engine; distributed snapshot consistency (as in Spanner or CockroachDB) requires clock or consensus machinery beyond MVCC's local XID order.

## 7 Conclusion

PostgreSQL's storage engine endures because each hard problem is assigned to the mechanism best suited to it, and the interfaces between mechanisms are narrow and total. MVCC snapshots answer *"what may this transaction see?"* with a pure function over tuple headers. The buffer manager answers *"where is the page, and may it be evicted?"* with pins and clock sweeps. WAL answers *"what happened, in order?"* with an LSN-chained record stream that makes every buffer mutation replayable. And the Lehman–Yao B-link protocol answers *"how do concurrent searches share the index?"* with right-links and high keys that turn splits from a global event into a local, navigable one. Understand these four contracts — visibility, caching, logging, and link-repair — and you understand the engine: not as a monolith, but as a composition of classical ideas, each proved in the literature and each still earning its keep in production.
