# Width, Degree, and Rank: Unified Lower-Bound Landscapes for Resolution, Cutting Planes, Stabbing Planes, and Polynomial Calculus via Feasible Interpolation and Automatizability Barriers

## Abstract

Propositional proof complexity studies the lengths of proofs in formal systems for tautologies, with implications for automated theorem proving and SAT solving. This thesis unifies the four dominant lower-bound methodologies for weak proof systems: the width–size tradeoff for resolution of Ben-Sasson and Wigderson, which reduces exponential size lower bounds to linear width lower bounds; the Chvátal-rank and feasible-interpolation machinery for cutting planes, extended here to the newer Stabbing Planes system; and the degree-based pseudo-reduction operator framework of Alekhnovich and Razborov for polynomial calculus. We give self-contained expositions of Haken's pigeonhole lower bound, the pebble-game characterization of width, Pudlák's interpolation-based cutting planes bounds, and the non-automatizability of resolution modulo parameterized complexity. We prove a clean formulation of the size–width relation, exhibit the Gaussian-width reduction from resolution width to polynomial calculus degree, and delineate the simulation hierarchy connecting cutting planes, Stabbing Planes, and resolution-augmented polynomial calculus. The thesis closes with open problems on Frege lower bounds and quasi-automatizability.

##1 Intro

The central program of propositional proof complexity, articulated by Cook and Reckhow, is to prove that no proof system whose proofs are efficiently checkable can produce short proofs of all tautologies — a statement whose strongest form would separate P from NP. Although the general program remains far out of reach, the last four decades have produced a remarkably coherent theory of *lower bounds for weak systems*: resolution, cutting planes, and the polynomial calculus. These systems are not chosen arbitrarily. Resolution is the logical skeleton of the DPLL and CDCL algorithms that power modern SAT solvers: the trace of a DPLL run on an unsatisfiable formula is a tree-like resolution refutation, and conflict-driven clause learning searches, in a highly engineered fashion, for general resolution proofs [6]. Cutting planes is the proof-theoretic dual of the branch-and-bound and branch-and-cut methods of integer programming. The polynomial calculus is the proof-theoretic content of the Gröbner basis algorithm.

This thesis presents a unified account of the four most successful lower-bound technologies in this landscape, organized around the three complexity measures that dominate the field — *width*, *degree*, and *rank* — and two meta-technologies that transfer hardness across systems — *feasible interpolation* and *automatizability barriers*. The synthesis itself is the point: the same expansion combinatorics that drives width lower bounds for resolution reappears as Gaussian width in polynomial calculus degree bounds [7], and the same interpolation arguments that defeat cutting planes [6] illuminate the power of the recently introduced Stabbing Planes system [3].

##2 Background

We work with propositional proof systems in the Cook–Reckhow sense: a proof system for a language L is a polynomial-time computable function P whose range is exactly L. One system *p-simulates* another if proofs in the latter can be transformed into proofs in the former with at most polynomial blowup. A *refutation system* proves unsatisfiability of a CNF formula by deriving the empty clause, denoted by the empty set symbol.

**Resolution.** Clauses are disjunctions of literals. From *C ∨ x* and *D ∨ ¬x* the resolution rule derives *C ∨ D*. A refutation of a CNF *F* is a sequence of clauses ending in the empty clause, each from *F* or derived by the rule. The *size* is the number of clauses; the *width* is the maximum number of literals in any clause. Proofs are *tree-like* if every derived clause is used at most once, else DAG-like. Haken proved the first exponential lower bound in 1985: resolution refutations of the pigeonhole principle require exponential size [9].

**Cutting planes.** The system works with linear inequalities over 0–1 variables, with the *Gomory–Chvátal cut rule*: from *Σ aᵢxᵢ ≥ b* with integer coefficients derive *Σ aᵢxᵢ ≥ ⌈b⌉*. From the clause inequalities and 0 ≤ x ≤ 1, a refutation derives 0 ≥ 1. The *rank* counts rounds of cut applications. Cutting planes p-simulates resolution, and Pudlák proved exponential size lower bounds for it via feasible interpolation [9].

**Stabbing planes.** Introduced by Beame, Fleming, Impagliazzo, Kolokolova, Pankratov, Pitassi, and Robere, this system formalizes branch-and-cut with a single rule: subdivide the current polytope by branching on a hyperplane *ax ≥ b* and its *integer negation* *ax ≤ b − 1*, discarding the middle slab *b − 1 < ax < b*, which contains no integer points, and recursing on both branches [3]. Stabbing Planes efficiently simulates cutting planes and is equivalent to a tree-like variant of Krajíček's R(CP) system, yet admits short proofs of Tseitin formulas over the two-element field.

**Polynomial calculus.** Introduced by Clegg, Edmonds, and Impagliazzo as the dynamic analogue of Hilbert's Nullstellensatz, PC encodes each clause as a polynomial equation and adds the Boolean axioms *x² − x = 0*. Its rules: from *f* derive *x·f* for any variable *x*, and from *f, g* derive *af + bg* for field coefficients *a, b*. A refutation derives the constant polynomial 1; the measure is *degree*. PC strictly strengthens Nullstellensatz, and Razborov proved that refuting the pigeonhole principle in PC requires degree at least *n/2 + 1* [6].

**Benchmark principles.** Three formula families recur as lower-bound targets: the *pigeonhole principle* PHP asserting that *m* pigeons fit into *n* holes with *m > n*; *Tseitin formulas* encoding parity constraints on expander graphs; and *random k-CNFs*, which are expanding with high probability and hence hard for both resolution and PC [7].

##3 Methodology

Four methodologies structure everything that follows, and each will be developed rigorously in the deep dive.

1. **The width method.** Prove that every refutation must contain a *wide* clause — one with linearly many literals — via expansion arguments or adversary strategies in pebble games. The Ben-Sasson–Wigderson tradeoff then converts linear width into exponential size [1][5].
2. **Feasible interpolation.** From a refutation of *A(x, y) ∧ B(x, z)* with disjoint blocks *y* and *z*, extract a circuit *I(x)* implied by *A* and contradicting *B*. If the interpolant can be made monotone, monotone circuit lower bounds transfer to proof size. Krajíček formulated the method; Pudlák used it for exponential cutting planes lower bounds [9].
3. **Pseudo-reduction operators.** To prove that no low-degree PC refutation exists, construct a linear operator *R* on polynomials that annihilates every polynomial derivable in low degree from the axioms while satisfying *R(1) = 1*. Such an *R-operator* cannot exist if a low-degree refutation of 1 does. Alekhnovich and Razborov pioneered the *local* construction: for each low-degree monomial *m*, pick a satisfiable subset *S(m)* of the axioms and define *R(m)* by reduction modulo the ideal of *S(m)* [10].
4. **Automatizability reductions.** A system is *automatizable* if proofs of size *s* can be found in time polynomial in the formula size and *s*. Ben-Sasson and Wigderson's dynamic program gives subexponential automatizability for resolution [1]; Alekhnovich and Razborov showed true automatizability would place W[P] inside randomized FPT [2]; Göös et al. proved automating cutting planes is NP-hard [8].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Ben-Sasson–Wigderson Width–Size Tradeoff and Its Consequences

The single most influential structural result in resolution complexity is the relation between size and width. Let *w₀* be the width of the initial formula *F* on *n* variables, and let *S* be the size of the smallest resolution refutation of *F*.

> **Theorem:** (Ben-Sasson–Wigderson [1]) If *F* has a resolution refutation of size *S*, then it has a refutation of width at most *O(√(n · log S) + w₀)*. In contrapositive form: if every refutation of *F* requires width *w*, then every refutation has size at least *exp(Ω((w − w₀)² / n))*.

The proof is a masterpiece of the probabilistic method. A random restriction shrinks wide clauses while preserving unsatisfiability with positive probability; iterating converts size into width. The payoff: proving an exponential size lower bound reduces to proving a *linear* width lower bound, and width is attacked by *adversary arguments* — strategies that keep a partial assignment consistent with all narrow clauses and fail only when a wide clause appears.

Haken's pigeonhole bound is recovered this way: pigeonhole graph expansion forces any refutation to mention many pigeons at once. Tseitin formulas on bounded-degree expanders and random 3-CNFs likewise require linear width with high probability [6].

The tradeoff has profound structural consequences beyond individual lower bounds:

- **Width characterization.** Atserias and Dalmau characterized resolution width via the existential pebble game, and deduced that the minimum *clause space* for refuting a 3-CNF is bounded below by the minimum width minus 3, settling an open problem [5].
- **Complexity of width.** Hertel and Urquhart showed that deciding whether a formula has a refutation of width *k* is EXPTIME-complete, confirming a conjecture of Vardi [4].
- **Tightness.** Bonet and Galesi showed the tradeoff is tight and inapplicable to the weak pigeonhole principle; the dense linear order principle has polynomial-size refutations but large width — width and size are genuinely different resources [5].

### 4.2 Cutting Planes: Rank, Small Coefficients, and Feasible Interpolation

Cutting planes reasons about the integer hull of a polytope. The Gomory–Chvátal cut is geometrically transparent: if the polytope satisfies *Σ aᵢxᵢ ≥ b*, then *Σ aᵢxᵢ ≥ ⌈b⌉* holds for all 0–1 points. The *rank* counts rounds of cut introduction; rank lower bounds are among the strongest known for the system.

Lower bounds come primarily through *feasible interpolation*. From a refutation of *A(x,y) ∧ B(x,z)* one extracts a circuit *I(x)* with *A(x,y) ⊨ I(x)* and *B(x,z) ⊨ ¬I(x)*. For proofs with small coefficients, Pudlák showed the interpolant can be taken *monotone*, so exponential monotone circuit lower bounds — e.g., for clique versus coloring — yield exponential cutting planes size lower bounds [9]. Bonet, Pitassi, and Raz complemented this with lower bounds for small-coefficient proofs, showing the coefficient restriction is itself a genuine weakness.

A crucial structural fact is the *simulation order*: resolution is p-simulated by cutting planes, since a clause *∨ lᵢ* translates to *Σ lᵢ ≥ 1* and the resolution rule is a special case of a cut. Hence every resolution lower bound technique must be re-examined for cutting planes — and indeed, cutting planes has short proofs of some principles that are hard for resolution, while remaining vulnerable to interpolation.

### 4.3 Stabbing Planes: Branch-and-Cut Made Proof-Theoretic

Stabbing Planes (SP) collapses the two rules of cutting planes into one branching rule with a DPLL-like flavor. A refutation is a binary tree: at each node, choose an integer-coefficient hyperplane *ax ≥ b*; the left child continues with *ax ≥ b* adjoined, the right child with *ax ≤ b − 1* adjoined; the middle slab is discarded because it contains no integer points; a branch closes when its polytope is empty, which is checkable in polynomial time [3].

The system's strength profile is surprising and instructive:

- SP *efficiently simulates* cutting planes with only polynomial overhead.
- SP is *equivalent* to a tree-like variant of Krajíček's R(CP) system.
- SP has *quasipolynomial-size* proofs of any unsatisfiable system of linear equations over a finite field, including all Tseitin formulas — principles exponential for cutting planes. Branching on a global linear form counts parity in a way local cuts cannot.
- Yet Beame et al. prove *linear lower bounds on SP rank* by lifting communication complexity lower bounds. Because SP proofs *cannot be balanced*, these are unconditional barriers for balanced proofs, and they yield the first lower bound on the real communication complexity of set disjointness [3].

Stabbing Planes is therefore the sharpest available laboratory for the question of how much branching helps: it dominates cutting planes, defeats the Tseitin barrier, and still succumbs to communication-complexity lifting.

### 4.4 Polynomial Calculus: Degree Lower Bounds via Pseudo-Reduction Operators

In PC, the natural complexity measure is *degree*, and the analogue of the width–size tradeoff is the *size–degree* relation: a PC refutation with *S* lines can be converted to one of degree *O(√(n · log S))* [6]. Hence *linear degree lower bounds imply exponential size lower bounds*, exactly as in resolution.

The engine for degree lower bounds is the *pseudo-reduction operator* (*R-operator*) of Razborov. Let the axioms be polynomials *P*. An R-operator is a field-linear map *R* on polynomials such that *R(q) = 0* for every polynomial *q* derivable from *P* in degree at most *d*, while *R(1) = 1*. If such an operator exists, no degree-*d* derivation of 1 exists — for a genuine derivation of 1 would have to map to both 0 and 1. The art lies in constructing *R*.

Alekhnovich and Razborov's breakthrough was the *local* construction. The intuition: if the axioms were satisfiable, reduction modulo the ideal they generate would be a valid R-operator. Since the axioms are unsatisfiable, build the operator *locally*: for each monomial *m* of degree at most *d*, select a *satisfiable* subset *S(m)* of the axioms (possible when the formula is expanding — small monomials see only a small, satisfiable part of the instance), define *R(m)* as the reduction of *m* modulo the ideal of *S(m)* under an admissible monomial order, and extend linearly. Consistency across overlapping subsets is the technical heart of the argument [10].

This method yields:

- **Razborov's pigeonhole bound:** every PC refutation of the pigeonhole principle needs degree at least *n/2 + 1* [6].
- **Random 3-CNFs are hard for PC:** with high probability, random 3-CNFs require linear PC degree, hence exponential size [7]. The proof passes through *Gaussian width*: the width of the linear system over the rationals equals the resolution width of its CNF encoding, and half the PC refutation degree over fields of characteristic greater than 2 — letting the width machinery do the heavy lifting.
- **The non-binomial case:** Alekhnovich and Razborov extended the bounds to fields of characteristic 2, where the operator combinatorics is substantially more delicate [6].

On the algorithmic side, PC is *not* automatizable in general: Galesi and Lauria showed that automatizability of PC would have unlikely complexity consequences, and de Rezende et al. proved NP-hardness of automating algebraic proof systems — a sharp contrast with resolution's subexponential automatizability.

The three measures are unified by the following comparison.

| Proof system | Primary measure | Flagship lower bound | Dominant technique |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resolution | Width | Exponential size for PHP, Tseitin, random 3-CNF | Width–size tradeoff, expansion, pebble games |
| Cutting planes | Rank | Exponential size via interpolation | Feasible interpolation, monotone circuits |
| Stabbing planes | Rank | Linear rank bounds, unbalanced proofs | Communication lifting, real protocols |
| Polynomial calculus | Degree | Linear degree for PHP, random 3-CNF | R-operators, Gaussian width |

##5 Empirical Results or Proofs

We give the central tradeoff argument, the automatizability barrier, and a program illustrating the width–size computation.

> **Theorem:** Let *F* be an unsatisfiable *k*-CNF on *n* variables with resolution size lower bound derived from width. If the minimum refutation width is *w = Ω(n)*, then the minimum refutation size is *2^{Ω(n)}*.

*Proof sketch.* A refutation of size *S* contains a refutation of width *O(√(n · log S))*: a random partial assignment kills all clauses wider than the target bound while preserving a refutation with positive probability. Contraposing gives *S = 2^{Ω(w²/n)} = 2^{Ω(n)}* when *w = Ω(n)*. Width bounds themselves come from adversary strategies: for Tseitin on an expander, the adversary maintains a partial assignment consistent with all narrow clauses by extending along unused expander edges; only a clause mentioning linearly many vertices forces a contradiction [1][6].

> **Theorem:** (Alekhnovich–Razborov [2]) Neither general nor tree-like resolution is automatizable unless the parameterized class W[P] is solvable by a randomized fixed-parameter tractable algorithm with one-sided error.

*Proof sketch.* The proof reduces *minimum monotone circuit satisfiability* — complete for W[P] under the relevant reductions — to approximating the shortest resolution proof length. An automatizing algorithm would yield a fixed-parameter approximation for this W[P]-complete problem, collapsing the parameterized hierarchy in the randomized sense. The argument uses gap amplification for proof size, embedding the circuit problem into tautologies whose shortest proofs encode optimal satisfying assignments [2].

The following Python program computes the concrete size lower bound implied by the tradeoff for representative parameters, demonstrating how quickly linear width forces exponential size.

```python
import math

def size_lower_bound(n: int, width: int, w0: int = 3) -> float:
    if width <= w0:
        return 1.0
    return math.exp(((width - w0) ** 2) / (8 * n))

for n, w in [(100, 30), (500, 150), (1000, 300), (5000, 1500)]:
    print(f"n={n:5d}  width={w:5d}  size >= 2^{math.log2(size_lower_bound(n, w)):8.1f}")

def cnf_width(clauses: list[list[int]]) -> int:
    return max(len(c) for c in clauses)

php_3_2 = [[1, 2], [3, 4], [5, 6], [-1, -3], [-1, -5], [-3, -5],
           [-2, -4], [-2, -6], [-4, -6]]
print("width of PHP(3,2) encoding:", cnf_width(php_3_2))
```

The Haskell snippet below encodes the two polynomial calculus inference rules as a small algebraic data type, making the degree measure explicit in the type of derivations.

```haskell
type Var  = String
data Poly = Const Rational | V Var | Add Poly Poly | Mul Poly Poly
  deriving (Eq, Show)

data Line = Line { poly :: Poly, degree :: Int } deriving (Show)

multRule :: Var -> Line -> Line
multRule x (Line f d) = Line (Mul (V x) f) (d + 1)

linComb :: Rational -> Line -> Rational -> Line -> Line
linComb a (Line f d1) b (Line g d2) =
  Line (Add (Mul (Const a) f) (Mul (Const b) g)) (max d1 d2)

isRefutation :: [Line] -> Bool
isRefutation ls = any (\(Line p _) -> p == Const 1) ls
```

For cutting planes, the methodology is validated differently: Pudlák's interpolation theorem instantiated on the clique–coloring tautologies produced the first exponential lower bounds for the system [9], and the NP-hardness of automating cutting planes [8] confirms that no efficient proof search can circumvent these bounds in the worst case.

##6 Limitations

- **The Frege barrier.** No nontrivial lower bound is known for the Frege system. All four technologies fail there: width, degree, and rank are meaningless for Frege, and feasible interpolation provably *cannot* yield Frege lower bounds without also proving strong circuit lower bounds.
- **Tightness of the width method.** Bonet and Galesi proved the size–width tradeoff is tight and inapplicable to the weak pigeonhole principle; narrow proofs may be maximally long, so width alone cannot characterize size [6].
- **Interpolation needs disjointness.** Feasible interpolation applies only to tautologies of the split form *A(x,y) ∧ B(x,z)*, and monotone interpolation is unavailable for systems with non-monotone interpolants.
- **R-operators need expansion.** The local construction requires expanding instances so small monomials see satisfiable subsets; dense instances escape the method, and characteristic 2 needed a separate, harder argument.
- **Automatizability gaps.** Resolution is subexponentially automatizable yet not automatizable under W[P] assumptions; tree-like resolution is quasi-automatizable but exactly automating it is NP-hard [2]. These live in different complexity regimes and do not fully explain CDCL's practical success.
- **SP rank versus size.** Known SP lower bounds are on *rank*, and since SP proofs cannot be balanced, they do not automatically lift to size. An exponential *size* lower bound for SP remains open [3].

##7 Conclusion

Width, degree, and rank are three facets of a single phenomenon: the *locality* of a proof system's reasoning. Resolution reasons clause by clause, and wide clauses are its bottleneck; polynomial calculus reasons polynomial by polynomial, and high degree is its bottleneck; cutting planes and Stabbing Planes reason polytope by polytope, and many rounds of cutting or branching are theirs. The size–width tradeoff, the size–degree relation, and communication-complexity lifting are three instances of one meta-principle: *prove that any proof must at some point be non-local, then charge for the non-locality*. Feasible interpolation and automatizability barriers export these limitations into circuit lower bounds and algorithmic impossibility. The outstanding challenge remains Frege, where no exploitable notion of locality is known.
