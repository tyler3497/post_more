# The Microeconomics of Automated Market Makers: Constant-Function Pricing, Concentrated Liquidity, Loss-Versus-Rebalancing, and Maximal Extractable Value

## Abstract

Automated market makers (AMMs) have displaced the limit order book as the dominant trading mechanism on public blockchains, yet their microeconomic foundations remain poorly understood by many practitioners who deploy capital into them. This thesis develops a unified treatment of decentralized exchange economics, spanning the constant-function market maker (CFMM) invariants that generalize the classic constant-product rule, the tick-based concentrated liquidity of Uniswap v3, and the two central costs borne by liquidity providers: *impermanent loss* and the sharper, hedging-adjusted measure of *loss-versus-rebalancing* (LVR). We derive closed-form expressions for adverse-selection costs under geometric Brownian motion, characterize how concentrated positions amplify both fee income and leverage-like IL, and formalize maximal extractable value (MEV) as a tax on traders and LPs arising from public transaction ordering. The analysis covers sandwich attacks, just-in-time liquidity, batch auctions, and proposer-builder separation, and concludes with practical implications for LP strategy, AMM design, and MEV mitigation.

##1 Intro

The rise of decentralized finance has made the *constant-function market maker* the most consequential market microstructure innovation since the electronic limit order book. Rather than matching buyers against sellers, a CFMM holds reserves of two or more assets and quotes prices mechanically: a proposed trade is accepted if and only if it preserves a pre-specified invariant of the reserves [1]. This design removes intermediaries and market makers as discrete agents, replacing them with passive *liquidity providers* (LPs) who earn fees while delegating quoting to a smart contract. The simplicity is deceptive. Behind the invariant hides a rich microeconomics: curvature determines slippage, curvature determines the option-like payoff of LP shares, and the transparency of the mempool turns every trade into a potential target for *maximal extractable value* (MEV) extraction [2].

Four ideas jointly determine the economics of modern decentralized exchange: *constant-function pricing*, which generalizes far beyond the constant-product rule [3]; *concentrated liquidity*, which multiplies capital efficiency while converting passive positions into leveraged exposures [4]; *loss-versus-rebalancing* (LVR), which isolates the pure adverse-selection cost of quoting passively [1]; and *MEV*, which determines who captures the surplus of uninformed order flow [2].

We develop these ideas analytically and empirically, deriving the central closed-form results (impermanent-loss formulas, the LVR rate under geometric Brownian motion, sandwich profitability bounds, and competitive surplus distribution in PBS auctions) and grounding them in published empirical measurements of LP profitability and MEV extraction. The intended reader is a quantitatively sophisticated practitioner or researcher who wants the machinery, not the marketing.

##2 Background

### The CFMM abstraction

A constant-function market maker holds reserves `R = (R1, ..., Rn)` of `n` assets and a *trading function* `phi`. A trader proposing to tender `Delta` and receive `Lambda` succeeds exactly when `phi(R + Delta - Lambda) >= phi(R)`, with equality in the frictionless case. Angeris and coauthors showed that essentially every deployed AMM is a special case of this abstraction, and that the *marginal price* quoted for an infinitesimal trade is the gradient ratio of `phi` [3]. The canonical instance is the constant-product rule `x * y = k` of Uniswap v2: reserves `(x, y)` of tokens X and Y satisfy `x * y = k`, and the marginal price of X in units of Y is `p = y / x`.

Balancer's weighted pools use the *constant-mean* invariant `prod_i x_i^{w_i} = k`, supporting multi-asset baskets and portfolio tilts; Curve's StableSwap blends constant-sum and constant-product behavior for minimal slippage near peg with a no-depletion guarantee far from it. Each invariant is a choice of *curvature* — the primitive economic parameter governing slippage, LP payoff convexity, and the rate of arbitrage extraction.

> Theorem: (Price-consistency of CFMMs) For a differentiable, strictly increasing trading function `phi`, the CFMM quotes a marginal price vector proportional to `grad phi(R)`. A trade strictly improving a trader's utility at external reference prices is accepted iff the CFMM's marginal price deviates from the reference price in the trader's favor; in equilibrium under frictionless arbitrage, the CFMM price tracks the reference price. [3]

This no-arbitrage condition is the heartbeat of AMM economics: whenever the CFMM price goes stale relative to a centralized venue, the first arbitrageur to trade against it earns a risk-free profit — and, because AMM trading is zero-sum between the pool and its counterparties, that profit is a loss to LPs.

### Fees, LPs, and the promise of passive market making

LPs deposit both assets, receive pro-rata pool claims, and earn trading fees (30 bps in Uniswap v2; tiered at 1, 5, 30, and 100 bps in v3). The pitch was *passive* market making — deposit and earn. A widely cited study found 49.5 percent of Uniswap v3 LPs earned negative risk-adjusted returns versus holding, with $199 million in fees on $108.5 billion of volume failing to cover aggregate impermanent loss [5]. Understanding *why* requires the LVR lens of section 4.

### MEV: the ordering tax

Because pending transactions sit in a public mempool and block producers choose ordering, any agent who can reorder, insert, or censor transactions can extract value. Daian et al. named this *maximal extractable value* and documented priority gas auctions in which bots bid up fees for favorable ordering [2]. On AMMs the dominant form is the *sandwich attack*: frontrun the victim's swap, let it execute at a worsened price, then backrun for profit — estimated at over 4,400 attacks per day in 2023–2024 [6].

##3 Methodology

Our approach is analytic microeconomics disciplined by published empirics. We proceed in three layers.

1. **Invariant geometry.** Trading functions, marginal prices, and slippage derived from first principles, compared across designs.
2. **LP payoff analysis.** GBM price model; three benchmarks — buy-and-hold (*impermanent loss*), the *rebalancing strategy* trading at market prices (the LVR reference) [1], and fee income; closed-form LVR rate in the zero-fee continuous-time limit.
3. **MEV game theory.** Sandwich attacks as sequential games among searchers, builders, and proposers; competitive equilibrium of builder-tip bidding under PBS; mitigation survey.

Where closed forms are unavailable we use numerical simulation. The Python sketch below computes impermanent loss and the LVR rate for a constant-product pool under GBM, illustrating the methodology used throughout:

```python
import numpy as np

def impermanent_loss(price_ratio: float) -> float:
    """IL vs. buy-and-hold for a constant-product pool.
    price_ratio = p1 / p0 (end vs. start price of risky asset)."""
    r = price_ratio
    return 2.0 * np.sqrt(r) / (1.0 + r) - 1.0

def lvr_rate(sigma: float, pool_value: float) -> float:
    """Instantaneous LVR per unit time, zero-fee GBM limit [1]."""
    return (sigma ** 2 / 8.0) * pool_value

# Example: 100% annualized vol, $1M pool -> ~$125k/year adverse-selection bleed
print(lvr_rate(1.0, 1e6))

# IL curve: symmetric in log-price, always non-positive
rs = np.exp(np.linspace(-2, 2, 9))
for r in rs:
    print(f"r={r:6.3f}  IL={impermanent_loss(r):+.4f}")
```

A complementary Haskell sketch encodes tick-range arithmetic for concentrated liquidity, showing how virtual reserves are derived from a position's bounds:

```haskell
-- Virtual reserves for a concentrated position with liquidity L
-- between sqrt-prices pa and pb, evaluated at current sqrt-price p.
virtualReserves :: Double -> Double -> Double -> Double -> (Double, Double)
virtualReserves l pa pb p
  | p <= pa   = (l * (1/pa - 1/pb), 0)              -- entirely token X
  | p >= pb   = (0, l * (pb - pa))                  -- entirely token Y
  | otherwise = (l * (1/p - 1/pb), l * (p - pa))    -- mixed inventory
```

All empirical claims (LP profitability, sandwich frequency, fee totals) are taken from peer-reviewed or widely cited published studies rather than our own chain measurements; we cite primary sources throughout [5][6][7].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Constant-Function Market Makers: From `xy = k` to Generalized Means

The constant-product rule is best understood as the simplest member of a parametric family. Consider reserves `(x, y)` and invariant `F(x, y) = k`. The marginal price of X quoted in Y is the ratio of partial derivatives `F_x / F_y` evaluated at the reserves; for `F = x * y` this yields `p = y / x`. A finite trade of `dx` units of X moves reserves to `(x + dx, k / (x + dx))`, so the execution price `dy / dx` degrades with size — this degradation *is* slippage, and its severity is controlled by pool depth relative to trade size.

> Theorem: (Slippage monotonicity) For any strictly concave trading function in log-reserves, the average execution price of a trade is monotone worsening in trade size, and the marginal price impact per unit is inversely proportional to the geometric depth of the pool. Concentrating the same reserves into a narrower effective price range reduces slippage proportionally. [3]

The table below compares the major invariant families on the dimensions that matter economically:

| Design | Invariant | Spot price of X (in Y) | Slippage character | Primary use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Uniswap v2 (constant product) | `x * y = k` | `y / x` | Moderate, uniform curvature | General volatile pairs |
| Balancer (weighted mean) | `prod x_i^{w_i} = k` | `(w_y / w_x)(y / x)` | Tunable via weights | Index-like baskets |
| Curve StableSwap | `A`-blended sum/product | Near 1 near peg | Minimal near peg, steep far | Stablecoin / pegged pairs |
| Uniswap v3 (concentrated) | Virtual `x_v * y_v = L^2` per tick | `y_v / x_v` within range | Very low in-range, infinite out-of-range | Capital-efficient quoting |

Two generalizations matter. The *weighted geometric mean* family lets LPs express portfolio views: an 80/20 pool behaves like a constantly rebalanced index fund, its payoff decomposing into a static-weight portfolio plus convexity costs [8]. *Multi-asset* trades reduce to convex optimization over the feasible reserve set — the basis of modern routers splitting orders across dozens of pools [3][9]. Fees enter as a haircut on the invariant, so the effective `k` *grows* with volume: this growth is LP income, and its race against adverse-selection bleed is the central question of LP profitability.

### 4.2 Concentrated Liquidity and the Tick System of Uniswap v3

Uniswap v3 replaces the single global curve with *concentrated liquidity*: each LP chooses a price interval, and their capital provides depth only inside it. Implementation rests on three interlocking ideas [4]:

- **Ticks.** Price space is discretized at `1.0001^i`; positions start and end on ticks (subject to per-tier spacing). Swaps walk across ticks consuming only active liquidity, applying *liquidityNet* deltas at each initialized crossing.
- **Virtual reserves.** Real reserves `(x, y)` map to virtual reserves `(x + L / sqrt(P_b), y + L sqrt(P_a))` with `x_v * y_v = L^2` for liquidity `L` and bounds `P_a`, `P_b`. Outside the range the position is single-sided.
- **Capital efficiency.** `$25m` concentrated in the 0.99–1.01 band replicates `$5b` of v2 depth; a single 0.10 percent range attains up to 4000x efficiency [4].

The economic consequence is leverage: fee income per dollar scales with concentration, but so does *impermanent loss per dollar*, and out-of-range positions earn nothing while holding depreciated single-asset inventory. Extending IL to leveraged positions distinguishes *minimal IL* from *actual IL*; empirically both exceed fee income for the average v3 LP [5]. Liquidity is never static — LPs enter, exit, and re-range continuously — so static-liquidity analyses are, as recent work stresses, "far from reality" [7].

*Just-in-time* (JIT) liquidity exploits this further: actors mint large concentrated positions in the same block as a known large trade, harvest its fees, and burn immediately after. Existing for a single block, the position faces no price risk — JIT LPs earned roughly $1.27 million with *zero* IL in the studied sample, a pure transfer from passive LPs to latency-advantaged ones [5].

### 4.3 Impermanent Loss, Rebalancing, and Loss-Versus-Rebalancing

*Impermanent loss* compares the LP's terminal portfolio value against simply holding the deposited assets. For a constant-product pool with price ratio `r = p1 / p0`:

```text
IL(r) = 2 sqrt(r) / (1 + r) - 1   <=  0
```

The loss is symmetric in log-price, zero at `r = 1`, and approaches total loss as `r` goes to zero or infinity. It is "impermanent" only in the sense that mean reversion of the price restores it — cold comfort when prices trend.

LVR sharpens this. Buy-and-hold is the wrong benchmark: it leaves the LP exposed to directional market risk unrelated to market making. The right benchmark is the *rebalancing strategy*, which holds exactly the pool's risky inventory at every instant but trades at centralized-exchange prices rather than stale AMM prices [1]. The P&L gap between LP and rebalancer is the *pure cost of quoting passively* — cumulative arbitrageur profit.

> Theorem: (LVR decomposition, Milionis–Moallemi–Roughgarden) In the frictionless continuous-time Black–Scholes setting, an LP's P&L decomposes into (i) the P&L of the rebalancing strategy (a martingale capturing market risk) and (ii) a non-negative, non-decreasing, predictable process — loss-versus-rebalancing — equal to the cumulative arbitrage profit extracted from the pool. For a zero-fee constant-product pool with the risky price following GBM with volatility `sigma` and pool value `V_t`, the instantaneous LVR rate is `sigma^2 V_t / 8`. [1]

The intuition is option-theoretic: providing liquidity equals selling a strip of options — recent work makes this exact via perpetual American continuous-installment options, identifying LVR with time decay (*theta*) [10]. Higher volatility, larger pools, and tighter concentration raise the bleed. Fees and discrete blocks modify the magnitudes — faster chains with lower interblock times materially reduce LP losses [11] — but the message survives: *passive LPs are systematically short volatility and short informed flow*.

This reframes the finding that half of v3 LPs lose money [5]: not bad luck, but the predictable consequence of selling options without pricing them. Fees compensate only when uninformed flow dominates informed flow — the Glosten–Milgrom condition, rediscovered on-chain.

### 4.4 Maximal Extractable Value: Sandwich Attacks and Proposer-Builder Separation

While LVR taxes LPs through stale quotes, MEV taxes *traders* through public ordering. The canonical sandwich attack has three legs [2][6]:

1. **Frontrun.** The searcher spots a victim swap of size `q` in the mempool and submits the same-direction trade at a higher fee, pushing the pool price up. 2. **Victim execution.** The victim trades at the worsened price, incurring excess slippage. 3. **Backrun.** The searcher reverses the frontrun, capturing the price impact as profit.

Profitability requires the victim's price impact to exceed round-trip costs. Under searcher competition, Bertrand-style bidding for builder inclusion drives the tip toward the full surplus minus costs — in equilibrium *builders and proposers capture nearly everything* and searchers earn approximately zero [12].

This dynamic is institutionalized in *proposer-builder separation* (PBS), deployed via MEV-Boost: *builders* construct the highest-value blocks while *proposers* select the highest-bidding header. The supply chain: victims lose surplus `S` to slippage; searchers earn `epsilon ≈ 0` under competition; builders capture `Π − G − p`; proposers capture the block bid `p` [12]. Scale advantages in order flow and simulation concentrate builder share, recreating intermediation rents inside a nominally decentralized system.

Mitigations form an active design frontier:

- **Batch auctions and intent-based execution** (CoW Swap, UniswapX, 1inch Fusion) match or auction flow off-chain before settlement, removing the public mempool signal sandwiches require.
- **Function-maximizing AMMs** batch all trades arriving within a period and execute them at the post-batch marginal price, provably eliminating both LVR and sandwich attacks in the idealized model [13].
- **Private order flow and order-flow auctions** (MEV-Share, MEV-Blocker) return a share of extracted surplus to the victim, converting theft into a rebate.
- **Encrypted mempools and threshold decryption** aim to remove the informational precondition entirely, though liveness and UX trade-offs remain open.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We collect the central quantitative results — proved analytically in the cited works, measured empirically where noted.

**Proof sketch: the LVR rate.** For a constant-product pool of value `V` with the risky price following GBM (`sigma`), the pool-vs-rebalancer P&L gap over `dt` equals the profit of a delta-hedged short-gamma position: `(1/2) |Gamma| S_t^2 sigma^2 dt` by Itô's lemma. With pool value function `V(S) = 2 sqrt(kS)`, `|Gamma| S^2 = V / 4`, giving `dLVR = sigma^2 V / 8 dt` [1]. For general CFMMs the LVR rate is curvature (gamma) of the value function times variance — *curvature is the price of immediacy*.

**Empirics: LP profitability.** Across v3 pools above $10M TVL (~43% of v3 TVL), aggregate IL exceeded aggregate fees for almost every pool; only JIT flash LPs were reliably profitable [5]. Fee income scales with noise volume while adverse-selection cost scales with volatility and TVL — set by unrelated market forces, exactly as LVR predicts.

**Empirics: sandwich extraction.** EigenPhi data (Oct 2022–Sep 2024) show sandwiches averaging over 4,400/day; a worked example (May 10, 2025, block 22450093) has the victim receiving ~10M fewer RATO than the no-frontrun counterfactual, with the builder capturing most of the $128 gross profit via the backrun's fee [6].

**Simulation check.** Monte Carlo of the rebalancing strategy vs. a constant-product pool (GBM, `sigma = 100%`, one year) reproduces the theoretical LVR rate within discretization error; break-even fee income implies a noise-to-arbitrage volume ratio most volatile pairs never reach [1][5].

##6 Limitations

Caveats bound these claims. *First*, the closed-form LVR rate assumes frictionless continuous-time arbitrage, zero fees, and GBM prices; fees and Poisson block arrivals change the quantitative answer [11]. *Second*, concentrated-liquidity analysis assumes costless re-ranging; gas, tick-crossing frictions, and active-management burden make theoretical efficiency unattainable for most. *Third*, the MEV model assumes rational risk-neutral searchers with symmetric information; real competition involves latency races, private order flow, and exclusive builder-searcher integration. *Fourth*, empirical MEV and LP-profitability studies rest on heuristics (trade labeling, price feeds, sample windows); signs are robust, magnitudes less so. *Finally*, mitigations like encrypted mempools and FM-AMMs carry latency, liveness, and UX trade-offs not resolved here.

---

##7 Conclusion

The microeconomics of automated market makers in four sentences: constant-function pricing converts curvature into a complete market-making technology — slippage for takers, convexity for LPs, a no-arbitrage condition keeping pool prices honest. Concentrated liquidity multiplies capital efficiency by selling that convexity exactly where wanted — and multiplies exposure in the same stroke. Loss-versus-rebalancing shows the LP's true cost is not price movement but *adverse selection*: a predictable, volatility-scaled bleed that fees cover only when noise flow dominates. Maximal extractable value shows public ordering taxes everyone else, with competition funneling surplus to builders and proposers under PBS.

For practitioners: treat LP positions as short-volatility derivatives and price them accordingly; concentrate only where edge exceeds amplified LVR; route taker flow through batch auctions or private channels; and judge every new AMM design by one question — *who pays the convexity cost, and are they compensated?*
