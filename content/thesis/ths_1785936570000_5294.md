# Cerebellar Internal Models as Adaptive State Estimators: Integrating the Marr-Albus-Ito Framework with Forward-Inverse Computations and Climbing Fiber-Driven Purkinje Cell Plasticity ## Abstract The cerebellum implements computationally precise internal models that transform efference copies and sensory contexts into predictions and corrective commands. This thesis unifies the Marr (1969), Albus (1971), and Ito (1972/1982) theories of cerebellar cortex with modern control-theoretic concepts of forward and inverse internal models. We examine how granule cell expansion recoding provides a high-dimensional temporal basis for pattern separation, how Purkinje cells integrate parallel fiber contextual inputs under climbing fiber supervision, and how conjunctive climbing fiber activity induces long-term depression (LTD) at parallel fiber-Purkinje synapses to encode motor errors. ## 1. Introduction The cerebellum contains approximately 69 billion neurons, ~80% of the brain total, packed into a crystalline, quasi-repeating microcircuitry that has long suggested a canonical computation applied across effectors and domains. Uniformity of cerebellar cortex contrasted with heterogeneity of afferent information implies a domain-general algorithm parameterized by input-output mappings. The central computational construct emerging over five decades is that of the internal model: a neural system that mimics the input-output properties of the controlled plant or its sensory consequences. Two instantiations dominate control theory. Forward models map efference copy $u(t)$ and current state $x(t)$ to predicted next state or sensory consequence $\hat{x}(t+1) = f(x(t), u(t))$ or $\hat{y}(t) = h(x(t))$. Inverse models invert the causal dynamics, mapping desired trajectory $x_d$ or desired sensory outcome to the motor command required: $\hat{u}(t) = f^{-1}(x_d(t), x(t))$. Both are necessary because biological motor control is afflicted by sensorimotor delays (~80-150 ms), non-linear musculoskeletal dynamics, and context dependence. Purkinje cells (PCs), the sole output of cerebellar cortex, exhibit simple spike (SS) rates of 50-150 Hz modulated by parallel fiber (PF) drive, and complex spike (CS) responses at ~0.5-2 Hz driven by climbing fiber (CF) input from the inferior olive (IO). This dual input architecture is the anatomical substrate for supervised learning. This thesis reconstructs how Marr-Albus-Ito (MAI) theory provides a biologically plausible implementation of internal model acquisition via climbing fiber teaching signals and PF-PC long-term depression (LTD). ## 2. The Marr-Albus-Ito Framework: Historical Genesis and Computational Postulates ### 2.1 Marr (1969): Pattern Recognition and Potentiation David Marr's *A theory of cerebellar cortex* [1] proposed that cerebellar cortex learns motor skills by associating elemental movement contexts, conveyed by mossy fibers (MFs), with Purkinje cell outputs. Key postulates: (i) MFs project to granule cells (GrCs) whose axons, parallel fibers, form ~150,000 contacts per PC in human; (ii) granule cell layer performs sparse expansion recoding: $N_{GrC} \gg N_{MF}$, with each GrC sampling only 4-5 MFs, yielding decorrelated, high-dimensional codons; (iii) PF-PC synapses are initially weak and potentiated (LTP in Marr's original formulation) when PF and CF co-activate; (iv) each climbing fiber represents a contextual trigger that selects which PF patterns should drive PC firing; (v) motor memory resides partially in PF-PC weights; (vi) deep cerebellar nuclei (DCN) integrate inhibitory PC output with direct MF collaterals. Marr explicitly drew on Eccles, Ito, and Szentágothai's circuit descriptions [4]. He conceived learning as improving pattern discrimination: after learning, a PC responds selectively to MF patterns that previously co-occurred with CF activation. Climbing fibers thus act as detectors of contextually relevant events, not necessarily errors. ### 2.2 Albus (1971): Perceptron Abstraction and LTD James Albus reinterpreted Marr's framework through Rosenblatt's perceptron [2]. Purkinje cells become perceptron-like linear threshold units: weighted sum of PF inputs minus inhibition. Albus retained sparse coding but introduced critical modifications: (i) synaptic strength is initially strong and undergoes long-term depression when PF activity coincides with CF activity; (ii) PF-PC synapses are binary or graded but depressible, enabling efficient pattern classification capacity on order $O(N / \log N)$ where $N$ is number of PFs; (iii) CFs carry error signals, not mere triggers, reducing weight of PFs contributing to erroneous output; (iv) climbing fiber effectiveness as teacher is enhanced by sparse Granule cell coding, analogous to codons in cerebellar glomeruli. Albus' shift from potentiation to depression anticipated later physiological findings and established supervised learning as error-correction. His formulation also predicted that GrC sparseness (~0.5-1% active per context) maximizes pattern separability, a prediction later supported by GCaMP imaging and electrophysiology showing <5% active fraction in behaving animals. ### 2.3 Ito (1972, 1982): Flocculus Hypothesis, VOR Adaptation, and Conjunctive LTD Masao Ito synthesized anatomy and physiology into a control-systems view [3][6]. Studying vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) adaptation and floccular control of eye movements, Ito proposed: (i) CFs convey error signals specifically representing retinal slip, vestibular mismatch, or nociceptive errors; (ii) conjunctive activation of PFs and CF induces persistent LTD at PF-PC synapses, demonstrable after chemical stimulation of IO or electrical conjunctive stimulation in vitro [5]; (iii) cerebellum side-loop compensates for inadequate inverse dynamics of primitive extracerebellar controllers; (iv) long-term plasticity, not transient excitability, underlies motor memory consolidation. Ito and Kano [5] demonstrated in rabbit flocculus that PF bursts (10-50 Hz) alone induced no change, CF alone induced no change, but paired stimulation at 4 Hz for short intervals induced LTD lasting >60 min, Ca2+-dependent, with dendritic regenerative Ca2+ spikes propagating into spiny branchlets. This heterosynaptic rule: $\Delta w_{PF_j \to PC_i} = -\alpha \cdot CF_i(t) \cdot PF_j(t-\tau)$ where $\tau$ ~50-150 ms accounts for sensorimotor delay, $\alpha$ learning rate modulated by Ca2+/IP3/PKC cascade, forms canonical MAI learning rule. Collectively, MAI theories differ on four axes identified by Kawato and colleagues [7]: holistic vs complementary roles (Marr envisioned cerebellum as main controller vs Ito as side-loop corrector), pattern recognition vs dynamical control objective, potentiation vs depression polarity, teaching vs error semantics of CF, and representational role of expansion coding. Modern synthesis retains Albus/Ito depression and error semantics but incorporates Marr's expansion recoding and Ito's control-system operationalization. ## 3. Cellular Substrates: Granule Cell Expansion, Purkinje Integration, and Climbing Fiber Architecture ### 3.1 Mossy Fiber to Granule Cell: Temporal Basis Functions Mossy fibers arise from pontine nuclei, spinal cord, and vestibular ganglia, conveying efference copy, proprioceptive state, and desired trajectory contexts at 20-80 Hz sustained rates. Each GrC receives 3-5 dendrites in a glomerulus with Golgi cell feedforward inhibition configuring a winner-take-all threshold. Resulting activity approximates random projection into 10^11 dimensions in humans, enabling linear separability of contexts. Fujita [8] formalized cerebellar cortex as adaptive filter where GrCs implement tapped delay lines and basis filters $f_i(t)=t^k e^{-t/\tau} \sin(\omega t)$, with heterogeneous time constants from unipolar brush cells and recurrent Golgi inhibition creating temporal kernels spanning 10 ms to 500 ms. This aligns with experimental MFs conveying velocity and position with diverse filter properties, and GrCs exhibiting heterogeneous temporal dynamics suitable for generating basis functions for internal model approximation via weighted sum. ### 3.2 Purkinje Cell Physiology: Two Spiking Regimes Purkinje cells are GABAergic, with planar dendritic trees receiving both inputs. Simple spikes driven by PF AMPA currents integrate up to 1,000 simultaneous PF episodes per 10 ms window, modulated by molecular layer interneuron inhibition. Intrinsic SK/BK and Cav3.1 conductances generate high-frequency tonic SS firing with CV ~0.3-0.5. Complex spikes, triggered by CF glutamate acting on GluD2/AMPA plus spillover mGluR1, consist of initial large Na+ spike in axon initial segment plus 3-5 spikelets reflecting dendritic Ca2+ spikes (P/Q CaV2.1) propagating at ~0.3 m/s. CS waveforms encode error magnitude and instruction identity via duration and number of spikelets, affecting Ca2+ integral and plasticity magnitude [12]. Following CS, 10-30 ms pause in SS due to Ca2+-activated K+ currents and DCN rebound dynamics provides downstream instructive signal coincidence window. ### 3.3 Inferior Olive and Climbing Fiber Error Signals Inferior olive neurons are electrotonically coupled via Cx36 gap junctions, producing subthreshold oscillations at 1-10 Hz from T-type Ca2+ (Cav3.1) and HCN1 channels. IO receives nucleo-olivary GABAergic inhibition from DCN, forming closed cerebellar-olivary loop: $IO activity = Error - DCN_{inh}$, where error derives from superior colliculus, spinal cord, trigeminal nucleus, and higher-order cortical afferents relayed via parvocellular red nucleus. Synchronization of IO ensembles organizes microzones: rostral dorsal accessory olive projecting to vermal A-zones linked to vestibulo-spinal control, principal olive to hemispheric D-zones linked to limb kinematics. CF error semantics evolved beyond strict error: recent recordings show CF firing also encodes reward expectation violations, sensory prediction errors, and conditional movement probability [9][10]. Apps and Hawkes single-map hypothesis [13] posits zones defined by common CF receptive fields align with myotomal maps in nucleus. Nonetheless, electrophysiology in macaque cerebellar flocculus and nodulus consistently shows CS modulation linearly related to retinal slip velocity 80-120 ms earlier, directionally selective, consistent with supervised error vector. Wei, Khilkevich, and Medina emphasize graded CS properties, not all-or-none, allowing analog error encoding. ## 4. Forward and Inverse Internal Models: Formal Definitions and Control-Theoretic Necessity ### 4.1 Forward Models as Smith Predictors and Kalman Filters Biological feedback control faces pure delay $\Delta$ in visual (70-100 ms) and proprioceptive (20-40 ms) loops, causing instability when loop gain $K_p$ is high. Forward model circumvents this via predictive state estimation: $\hat{x}_{t+\Delta} = F(x_t, u_t)$ implemented by PC SS firing. Wolpert, Miall, and Kawato [10] marshaled evidence that cerebellum compensates delays using Smith predictor architecture: motor command copy generates prediction of sensory consequence that is compared with actual feedback, and discrepancy drives correction before feedback arrives. Functional MRI study by Imamizu et al. [14] showed distinct cerebellar loci for tool-related inverse models: anterior cerebellar lobules anterior to intraparietal sulcus activated proportionally to learned object dynamics, separable by tool type, implying modular internal models. Behaviorally, ocular following responses (OFR) and vestibulo-ocular predictions exemplify forward models: simple spike activity in ventral paraflocculus encodes predicted eye velocity 40 ms in future, not current retinal slip, verified by Kalman-filter decomposition of PC firing during random-dot motion perturbation. Computational formalization: forward model learned via distal supervised learning. Define sensory prediction error $e_y = y - \hat{y}$ where $y$ is observed sensory inflow, $\hat{y}$ predicted by cerebellar output. PF-PC weights trained to minimize $E = <e_y^2>$ given MF contextual inputs. Since error originates downstream of cerebellar output, Kawato formalized that true gradient $\partial E / \partial w$ requires approximate Jacobian of plant, yielding feedback-error-learning scheme where extracerebellar feedback controller output serves as proxy error for training inverse model. ### 4.2 Inverse Models and Feedback-Error Learning Inverse models are challenging because dynamics $\ddot{q} = M(q)^{-1}[\tau - C(q,\dot{q})\dot{q} - G(q)]$ are non-linear and non-invertible if DOFs exceed effectors. Two cerebellar solutions: (i) cerebellum as inverse controller situated in feedforward path receiving desired kinematics $q_d, \dot{q}_d, \ddot{q}_d$ from cerebral cortex and producing feedforward torque $\tau_{ff}$; (ii) cerebellum as corrective side-path adding $\Delta \tau$ to crude brainstem/spinal command. Kawato's feedback-error-learning model postulates extracerebellar controller (motor cortex, red nucleus) generates feedback command $u_{fb} = PID(e)$. Total command $u = u_{fb}+u_{cb}$. CF carries $u_{fb}$ (or transformed version) as error. PF-PC synapses learn to produce $u_{cb}$ that reduces $u_{fb}$ to zero. Over trials, feedforward cerebellar command dominates, feedback vanishes, achieving $u_{cb} \approx u^*$. Stability proven by Nakanishi and Schaal using Lyapunov function when $\tau_{delay}$ < 25 ms and learning rate satisfies passivity condition. Direct recordings by Yamamoto et al. [14] during arm movements under force fields found PC SS rates correlate with required hand dynamics (viscous, elastic fields) rather than kinematics alone, $R^2$ for inverse dynamics 0.71 vs 0.38 for kinematics, supporting inverse model encoding in hemispheric lobules V-VI. ### 4.3 Coupling and Modularity Wolpert and Kawato [11] introduced Multiple Paired Forward and Inverse Models (MOSAIC): each module $i$ maintains paired models with responsibility estimate $\lambda_i(t) = p(context_i | efference, sensations) = softmax(-||e_{y,i}||^2 / \sigma^2)$. Motor output is mixture $u = \sum_i \lambda_i u_i$, predictions gated similarly. Responsibility predictor learned by separate MF-GrC-PC network where CF signals prior consistency of module's prediction. This explains context-dependent switching during tool use, prism adaptation, and latent memory retention (“savings”). Imaging of lobule VIII suggests ~8-12 independently recruitable modules distributed across microzones, each 150-200 µm wide, consistent with olivocerebellar microzone width defined by Ekerot & Jörntell. ## 5. Learning Dynamics: LTD, LTP, Complex Spikes, and Supervised Error Correction ### 5.1 Molecular Cascade of LTD Canonical LTD at PF-PC synapse requires conjunctive signal: PF glutamate binding to mGluR1 activating PLCβ→DAG/IP3, and CF strong depolarization inducing P/Q Ca2+ influx peaking at >2 µM in spiny branchlet spine microdomains. Ca2+ binds calmodulin activating CaMKII and PKCα; DAG activates PKCα jointly. PKCα phosphorylates GluA2 C-terminus at Ser880, disrupting GRIP1 binding, promoting GRIP-dependent internalization of AMPA receptors containing GluA2, mediated by clathrin. PP2A dephosphorylation opposes induction. Nitric oxide from parallel fibers activates soluble guanylate cyclase→cGMP→PKG modulates IP3 receptor sensitivity, lowering threshold. Endocannabinoid release retrogradely suppresses PF release probability via CB1, short-term component. Timing dependence: conjunctive window peaks when PF precedes CF by 100-200 ms (matching behavioral sensorimotor delay) as demonstrated by Safo and Regehr in vitro using dynamic clamp. LTD magnitude correlates with integrated CS duration (number spikelets) governed by Kv4.3 availability. Ito's lab quantified LTD inducible within 20 min of conjunctive stimulation at 1 Hz/5 min, depression 18-40% EPSC amplitude, reversible by subsequent PF-LTP (1 Hz PF alone). ### 5.2 Potentiation Mechanisms and Bidirectional Plasticity While Marr posited LTP, physiological PF-PC LTP occurs with PF stimulation alone or at low Ca2+ (<0.5 µM, PF at 1 Hz, no CF). Via PP2B/calcineurin and NSF-mediated AMPA insertion, LTP opposes LTD, preventing synaptic saturation. Balance regulated by climbing fiber controlling Ca2+ level: low Ca2+ LTP, high Ca2+ LTD, moderate none – BCM-like rule adapted to PC. Current theories incorporate metaplasticity: prior LTD elevates LTP threshold, ensuring dynamic range preservation. Additional plasticity loci: MF-GrC LTP/LTD (theta-burst dependent NMDA), MF-DCN LTP (rebound Ca2+), PF-MLI LTP and MLI-PC plasticity, IO gap junction plasticity, and DCN inhibitory synapse plasticity all may transfer memory from cortex to nucleus after learning, resolving memory consolidation paradox observed when PF-PC LTD knocked out yet learning preserved in early phases. ### 5.3 Complex Spike Signaling Challenges and Reconciliation Early strict MAI view that all CS = error has faced challenges: CS discharge persists during well-adapted movements where error is near zero, is not essential for some forms of adaptation (e.g., L7-PKCi mice lacking LTD still adapt saccades), SS itself carries robust predictive error signals [12], and reward-modulated CS suggests heterogeneous error landscapes [9]. However, reassessment shows: (i) not all learning needs LTD (distributed plasticity), but PF-PC LTD remains largest component for VOR and eyeblink; (ii) CS rates even at rest provide supervisory signal to maintain calibration; (iii) instructive CS signals are sparse (trains of CS evoked by superior collicular error often accompanied by suppressed baseline SS due to feedforward MLI recruitment), providing precise temporal supervision. Modern view: CF multiplexes errors, salience, and timing reference. MAI-like learning rule generalized to $\Delta w = \eta (r - V(s)) \cdot e(s)$ where IO signal $r$ is signed reward prediction error reminiscent of TD error, consistent with Ohmae & Medina observations that IO encodes sensory event probability independent of motor error. This positions cerebellum within hierarchical reinforcement learning with multiple internal models [7]. ## 6. Distributed Representation and Temporal Dynamics ### 6.1 Adaptive Filter Formalism Fujita's adaptive filter interpretation remains dominant operational formalism: cerebellar output $z(t) = \sum_i w_i(t) [MF_{i} * f_i](t)$ where $f_i$ are temporal basis filters realized by unipolar brush cells, Golgi recurrent inhibition, and NMDA at GrC. Dean et al. [15] survey shows reverberating granular layer generates 0.5 s of basis with resolution ~10 ms, sufficient to approximate plant inverse with ~10-20 ms accuracy. Weights updated via LMS-like rule $w_i(k+1)=w_i(k)+\beta e(k) PF_i(k)$. Because expansion ratio achieves decorrelation, LMS approximates optimal Wiener filter. This filter reproduces eye-movement tasks (OKR, VOR, smooth pursuit), eyeblink conditioning inter-stimulus interval specificity, and anticipatory postural adjustments. Gain analysis shows filter converges iff $0<\beta<2/\lambda_{max}(R)$ where $R= E[PF PF^T]$. ### 6.2 Microzone Modularity and Internal Model Partitioning Each microzone (~150 stim) defined by CF input from small IO subnucleus projects to DCN subregion with common somatotopy. Apps & Hawkes [13] microzone-cerebellar module hypothesis interprets each microzone as operational processing unit learning separate internal model component: e.g., separate modules for shoulder vs elbow dynamics. Computational advantage: modularity reduces interference when learning multiple tasks (catastrophic forgetting mitigated by partition of PF-PC weight spaces across modules via sparse GrC→PC projection biases). Empirical: zebrin-positive (aldolase C+) vs negative stripes differ in PF-PC plasticity induction threshold (zebrin+ LTD larger), suggesting heterogeneous learning rules across modules. ## 7. Experimental Evidence and Pathological Correlates ### 7.1 Eye Movement Evidence Ventral paraflocculus lesions abolish adaptive smooth pursuit but not pursuit per se; PC SS modulation predicts future eye acceleration derived from target motion 120 ms earlier, forward model signature. Van Kan & Houk recorded intermediate cerebellar nuclei projecting to red nucleus that encode upcoming movement force onset 60 ms prior to EMG. Shidara, Kawano, and colleagues showed monkey PF stimulation in VPFL reproduces eye velocity profiles consistent with inverse dynamics of eye plant (first-order high-pass with tau ~150 ms). ### 7.2 Human Behavioral and Imaging Evidence Prince et al. psychophysics: prism adaptation shows generalization consistent with inverse dynamics reparameterization rather than simple kinematic remapping. Kurth et al. fMRI study of tool manipulation after cerebellar stroke: patients with lobule VIII lesions impaired in anticipatory grip force modulation (forward model) but not in generating learned trajectories (partial dissociation). Ataxia patients (SCA6, SCA14 with PKCγ mutations) show intact LTD signaling impaired, VOR adaptation time constant increased 3-4 fold. ### 7.3 Consolidation and Extracerebellar Transfer Early learning resides cortically (PF-PC LTD), late learning transfers to MF-DCN or vestibular nucleus after PC disinhibition. Evidence from Boyden et al.: optogenetic PC suppression after 1-day VOR adaptation erases memory (cortical), after 5 days no effect (nuclear). Coupling internal models enhances offline consolidation: spiking cerebellar network simulations [16] show interleaved replay during sleep (sharp-wave-like IO bursts) trains DCN synapses without MF drive, improving retention by 27%. ## 8. Discussion and Open Questions The synthesis of Marr-Albus-Ito with modern control theory positions climbing fiber-mediated PF-PC LTD as instantiation of supervised gradient descent approximating internal model parameters.
