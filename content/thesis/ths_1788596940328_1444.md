# Neutral-Atom Quantum Processors: Rydberg Blockade Gates, Optical Tweezer Arrays, and Analog Quantum Simulation at Thousand-Qubit Scale

## Abstract

Neutral-atom arrays manipulated by holographic optical tweezers have matured from small proof-of-principle demonstrations into one of the leading platforms for scalable quantum information processing. By encoding qubits in long-lived hyperfine ground states and mediating entanglement through transient excitation to highly excited Rydberg states, the architecture combines the dense, identical qubits of neutral atoms with a conditional interaction — the **Rydberg blockade** — that is simultaneously strong enough for sub-microsecond two-qubit gates and reconfigurable enough to support both digital circuits and analog quantum simulation. This thesis presents a self-contained exposition of the platform: the electrodynamics of the blockade interaction and its scaling with principal quantum number; the optics of defect-free tweezer array assembly via acousto-optic and spatial-light-modulator control; the digital gate set (global Raman single-qubit rotations and blockade-mediated controlled-phase gates, now demonstrated at 99.5% fidelity [2]); the analog mode, in which the many-body Rydberg Hamiltonian directly realizes Ising-like spin models and combinatorial optimization problems; and the zoned, reconfigurable architectures that enabled the first 48-logical-qubit processors with error-corrected operation [3]. We survey state-of-the-art system scales — from QuEra's 256-atom Aquila device [6] to thousand-atom commercial systems — derive the fundamental error budgets limiting gate fidelity, and assess the realistic path toward fault-tolerant neutral-atom computation, concluding that the platform's unique combination of connectivity, scale, and rearrangeability positions it as a frontrunner for both near-term quantum simulation and long-term fault tolerance.

## 1. Introduction

Quantum computing platforms divide roughly into those that prize *fidelity* at small scale — trapped ions, with two-qubit gates exceeding 99.9% but tens of ions — and those that prize *scale* at moderate fidelity — superconducting circuits, with hundreds of qubits and entangling fidelities near 99%. Neutral-atom processors stake a claim on both axes simultaneously: thousands of identical, coherence-preserving qubits held in programmable optical tweezer arrays, entangled on demand through the giant electric dipole moments of Rydberg states [1][5]. The central physical resource is the **Rydberg blockade**: when one atom is promoted to a Rydberg level with principal quantum number $n \sim 50$–$100$, the van der Waals shift of a second atom within the blockade radius $R_b$ is so large that double excitation is energetically forbidden. This conditional dynamics is the atom-optical analogue of the Coulomb blockade in mesoscopic electronics, and it supplies the nonlinearity from which all entangling operations are built.

The last decade has witnessed an extraordinary acceleration. In 2016, independent demonstrations by the Lukin group at Harvard/MIT and the Browaeys group at Institut d'Optique established large-scale programmable Rydberg atom arrays [9]. In 2017, the 51-atom quantum simulator of Bernien *et al.* exhibited a striking dynamical phase transition — the first hint of many-body physics beyond classical simulability. By 2019, Levine *et al.* had implemented parallel two- and three-qubit gates with Bell-state fidelity exceeding 95% and extracted gate fidelity above 97.4% [1][8]. In 2023, Evered *et al.* pushed two-qubit entangling fidelity to **99.5%** across up to 60 atoms in parallel, crossing the surface-code threshold [2]; the same year, Bluvstein *et al.* demonstrated a logical quantum processor with 48 logical qubits on reconfigurable atom arrays, performing error-corrected circuits with transversal gates and atom shuttling [3]. Commercial systems — QuEra's 256-atom *Aquila* on Amazon Braket [6], Pasqal's Fresnel-class machines, Atom Computing's 1,180-qubit Phoenix array, Infleqtion's Sqale platform — have moved neutral atoms from laboratory curiosities to cloud-accessible processors.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the atomic physics: Rydberg states, the blockade interaction, and the qubit encoding. Section 3 describes the experimental methodology: tweezer generation, atom loading and rearrangement, gate implementation, and readout. Section 4 dives deeply into four pillars — blockade gate design, array assembly algorithms, analog Hamiltonian simulation, and error correction on reconfigurable arrays. Section 5 presents the empirical state of the art and the theoretical error budget. Section 6 discusses limitations honestly. Section 7 concludes with an outlook on the thousand-qubit era and beyond.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Rydberg atoms and the blockade interaction

A Rydberg atom is one in which the valence electron is promoted to an orbit with principal quantum number $n \gg 1$ — typically $n = 40$–$100$ in quantum computing experiments. The defining feature of such states is extreme scaling of atomic properties with $n$:

| Property | Scaling | $n = 50$ (order of magnitude) |
|---|---|---|
| Orbital radius | $n^2 a_0$ | $\sim 0.13\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ |
| Electric dipole moment | $n^2 e a_0$ | $\sim 2{,}500\,e a_0$ |
| Polarizability | $n^7$ | $10^{-32}\,\mathrm{C^2 m^2 J^{-1}}$ |
| Radiative lifetime | $n^3$ | $\sim 100\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ |
| van der Waals $C_6$ coefficient | $n^{11}$ | $\sim 2\pi \times 100\,\mathrm{GHz}\,\mu\mathrm{m}^6$ |

The $n^{11}$ scaling of the $C_6$ coefficient is the engine of the platform. Two atoms in Rydberg states $|r\rangle$ at separation $R$ experience a van der Waals shift $V(R) = C_6/R^6$. Consider now two atoms, each driven by a laser coupling its qubit state $|1\rangle$ to $|r\rangle$ with Rabi frequency $\Omega$. If the first atom is excited, the second atom's Rydberg transition is shifted by $V(R)$; when $V(R) \gg \hbar\Omega$, simultaneous excitation is off-resonant and suppressed — the **Rydberg blockade**. The characteristic blockade radius is defined by the resonance condition:

$$V(R_b) = \hbar\Omega \quad \Longrightarrow \quad R_b = \left(\frac{C_6}{\hbar\Omega}\right)^{1/6}.$$

> **Theorem:** For two atoms separated by $R < R_b$ and driven with Rabi frequency $\Omega$ satisfying $\hbar\Omega \ll V(R)$, the doubly excited state $|rr\rangle$ acquires a population bounded by $(\hbar\Omega/V(R))^2 \ll 1$ throughout the gate evolution, so that the joint dynamics is confined to the blockaded subspace $\{|11\rangle, |1r\rangle, |r1\rangle\}$ up to corrections of order $(\hbar\Omega/V)^2$.

In practice $R_b \approx 5$–$10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ for typical parameters, comfortably larger than the $\sim 3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ tweezer spacing and smaller than the $\sim 10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ separation between independent gate sites — a separation of length scales that permits parallel, crosstalk-free gates [3].

### 2.2 Qubit encoding

Rather than encoding information directly in the fragile Rydberg state, modern processors store qubits in two long-lived hyperfine ground states of alkali atoms ($^{87}$Rb, $^{133}$Cs) or nuclear-spin states of alkaline-earth-like atoms ($^{88}$Sr, $^{171}$Yb) [5][9]. The rationale is decisive: in the hyperfine manifold, neighboring atoms are effectively non-interacting — "they don't do anything," as the Harvard team puts it — so that idle qubits experience negligible crosstalk and coherence times measured in *seconds* [9]. Entanglement is performed only when needed: a laser pulse excites $|1\rangle \to |r\rangle$, the blockade mediates conditional dynamics, and the atoms return to the hyperfine manifold "safe and sound," where the entangled state is robust against the trapping light [9]. Single-qubit gates are executed with global or site-resolved Raman transitions between the hyperfine states, and readout is performed by state-selective fluorescence imaging.

### 2.3 The Rydberg many-body Hamiltonian

In the analog mode, the platform does not execute gates at all; instead, the laser drive *is* the computation. The canonical Hamiltonian for an array of atoms driven between ground $|g\rangle$ and Rydberg $|r\rangle$ states is [4]:

$$\hat{H} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\frac{\Omega}{2}\hat{\sigma}_i^x - \delta\,\hat{n}_i\right] + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i \ne j}\frac{V}{(|{\bf r}_i - {\bf r}_j|/a)^6}\,\hat{n}_i\hat{n}_j,$$

where $\hat{\sigma}_i^x = |g_i\rangle\langle r_i| + |r_i\rangle\langle g_i|$, $\hat{n}_i = |r_i\rangle\langle r_i|$, $\Omega$ is the Rabi frequency, $\delta$ the laser detuning, $a$ the lattice spacing, and $V$ parametrizes the interaction strength, re-expressible through the blockade radius via $V/(R_b/a)^6 \equiv \Omega$ [4]. This is a long-range interacting transverse-field Ising model — exactly the class of Hamiltonians whose ground states encode solutions to NP-hard combinatorial problems and whose dynamics exhibits quantum phase transitions, dynamical quantum criticality, and many-body localization.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Array generation and atom assembly

The experimental sequence begins with laser cooling: atoms are collected in a magneto-optical trap (MOT) at microkelvin temperatures [5]. Optical tweezers — tightly focused laser beams that trap atoms through the AC Stark shift — are generated holographically. Two complementary technologies dominate:

- **Spatial light modulators (SLMs)** imprint static phase holograms, producing hundreds of traps in arbitrary 2D geometries with a single device.
- **Acousto-optic deflectors (AODs)**, driven by arbitrary waveform generators (AWGs), generate *mobile* traps that can be steered in real time; 2D AODs provide independent $x$–$y$ control [3].

Loading from the MOT is stochastic — each trap captures an atom with probability $\sim 50$–$60\%$ due to collisional blockade — so defect-free arrays are assembled by **rearrangement**: a reservoir of atoms is imaged, empty target sites are identified, and mobile AOD tweezers transport atoms along collision-free paths in parallel. Modern implementations assemble defect-free arrays of several hundred atoms with filling fidelity above 99% per site and rearrangement times of order 100 ms [3]. The entire objective field of view spans roughly $400\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ [3], accommodating a thousand sites at $4$–$5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ pitch — the geometric basis for the thousand-qubit roadmaps.

### 3.2 Gate implementation

The workhorse entangling operation is the blockade-mediated controlled-phase ($C_Z$) gate. The textbook protocol [5][10] proceeds in three pulses:

1. A $\pi$-pulse on the **control** atom: $|1\rangle_c \to i|r\rangle_c$ (if the control is in $|1\rangle$).
2. A $2\pi$-pulse on the **target** atom: $|1\rangle_t \to -|1\rangle_t$ — *unless* the control occupies $|r\rangle$, in which case the blockade shifts the target out of resonance and the pulse is inert.
3. A final $\pi$-pulse returning the control to $|1\rangle$.

The net effect is the unitary $\mathrm{diag}(1, 1, 1, -1)$: a minus sign accumulates if and only if both atoms were in $|1\rangle$. Modern implementations use shaped, time-optimal pulses designed by optimal control theory rather than square pulses, reducing gate duration to $\sim 0.5\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ and suppressing transient Rydberg population [2]. Crucially, during Rydberg excitation the optical traps are briefly switched off (via TTL), because the tweezer light anti-traps the Rydberg state; the atoms, cooled near the motional ground state, remain ballistically confined for the sub-microsecond gate window [3].

### 3.3 Readout

Measurement is performed by resonant fluorescence imaging: a probe laser scatters photons from atoms in one qubit state while the other remains dark, and the photons are collected on a camera (EMCCD or sCMOS) [5]. Because Rydberg-excited or lost atoms are ejected from the trap, the same image simultaneously reports the qubit state and detects atom loss — enabling **erasure conversion**, in which the dominant error (atom loss) is flagged as a known-location erasure rather than a Pauli error, dramatically improving the efficiency of error correction [3].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Blockade gate design and the fidelity frontier

The infidelity of a Rydberg-mediated two-qubit gate decomposes into a small set of physically transparent contributions. For the optimal-control single-pulse gates of Evered *et al.* [2], the error budget is approximately:

$$\epsilon_{\mathrm{total}} \;\approx\; \underbrace{\epsilon_{\mathrm{decay}}}_{\text{Rydberg spontaneous emission}} \;+\; \underbrace{\epsilon_{\mathrm{Doppler}}}_{\text{thermal motion dephasing}} \;+\; \underbrace{\epsilon_{\mathrm{scatter}}}_{\text{intermediate-state scattering}} \;+\; \underbrace{\epsilon_{\mathrm{laser}}}_{\text{intensity/phase noise}} \;+\; \underbrace{\epsilon_{\mathrm{blockade}}}_{\text{finite } V/\hbar\Omega}.$$

Quantitatively: Rydberg decay contributes $\epsilon_{\mathrm{decay}} \sim t_{\mathrm{gate}}/\tau_r \approx 0.5\,\mu\mathrm{s}/100\,\mu\mathrm{s} \sim 5 \times 10^{-3}$ at leading order, reduced by pulse shaping that minimizes the integrated Rydberg population; Doppler dephasing arises from the atom's thermal velocity $v$ imprinting a random phase $kv\,t_{\mathrm{gate}}$ on the ground–Rydberg transition (with optical wavevector $k$), suppressed by Raman sideband cooling to near the motional ground state; intermediate-state scattering afflicts the conventional two-photon 420-nm/1013-nm excitation scheme (via $6P_{3/2}$ in rubidium), mitigated by atomic dark states that decouple the lossy intermediate level [2].

The trajectory of demonstrated fidelities tells the story of a platform crossing from NISQ curiosity to error-correction relevance:

| Year | Result | Two-qubit fidelity | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2010 | First Rydberg blockade gates (Wisconsin) | $\sim 75\%$ | Isenhower *et al.* |
| 2016 | Single-qubit + entanglement in tweezer arrays | Bell $\sim 73\%$ | Jau *et al.* |
| 2019 | Parallel $C_Z$ and Toffoli gates, 5 pairs | $\ge 97.4\%$ | [1] |
| 2023 | Optimal-control gates, 60 atoms parallel | **99.5\%** | [2] |
| 2023 | Logical processor, 48 logical qubits | below threshold (logical) | [3] |

The 99.5% result of Evered *et al.* [2] is a watershed: it surpasses the $\sim 99\%$ surface-code threshold, meaning that neutral atoms can now, in principle, be error-corrected with constant overhead scaling. Each doubling of fidelity halves the code distance required for a target logical error rate — a super-exponential payoff.

A tantalizing frontier lies in the **ultrafast regime**. Conventional gates operate deeply adiabatically with respect to the available interaction strength ($\pi/\Omega_{\mathrm{CW}} \gg t_J$, where $t_J \sim 1\,$ns is set by the GHz dipole–dipole coupling at micron separations [7]). Recent work on nanosecond energy exchange between Rydberg atoms [7] suggests protocols that exploit the full interaction bandwidth, potentially accelerating gates by two orders of magnitude and proportionally suppressing decay errors — though laser phase noise at nanosecond timescales presents formidable technical challenges.

### 4.2 Defect-free assembly at scale

Rearrangement is an algorithmic problem as much as an optical one: given a stochastic initial loading and a target geometry, find a set of collision-free atom trajectories executable by a limited number of mobile tweezers in minimum time. The standard approach is a greedy parallel sort — e.g., the *parallel compression* algorithm that fills rows independently — with total assembly time scaling as $O(\sqrt{N})$ for $N$ atoms in 2D. Filling fidelity compounds: with per-site success $p = 0.99$ and $N = 256$, the defect-free probability is $p^N \approx 0.076$ — so multiple assembly cycles with reservoir replenishment are standard, and the *Aquila* datasheet accordingly quotes a maximum of 256 filled sites with finite filling rates as the binding constraint [6].

Beyond static assembly, the Bluvstein *et al.* architecture [3] introduced **zoned, reconfigurable arrays**: entangling, storage, and readout zones that can be reprogrammed mid-circuit, with atoms physically shuttled between zones. Shuttling an entangled atom pair apart and re-entangling on arrival enables *non-local* gates without photonic interconnects — the array itself is the interconnect fabric. This capability underlies transversal logical gates: a logical qubit's physical atoms are rearranged so that a transversal operation becomes a set of parallel physical gates, a feat impractical on fixed-connectivity platforms.

### 4.3 Analog quantum simulation: programming with geometry

The analog mode dispenses with gates entirely. Because the interaction term $V_{ij} \propto |{\bf r}_i - {\bf r}_j|^{-6}$ is fixed by geometry, the *positions of the atoms* encode the problem Hamiltonian. The canonical application is the **Maximum Independent Set (MIS)** problem on unit-disk graphs: place one atom per graph vertex; the blockade forbids adjacent vertices from both occupying $|r\rangle$; then adiabatically sweep the detuning $\delta$ from negative (all atoms in $|g\rangle$) to positive (Rydberg excitations energetically favored). The competition between the detuning energy gain and the blockade penalty drives the system toward the ground state — a maximum independent set [4][6].

```python
# Conceptual: encoding Maximum Independent Set on a neutral-atom array
# (Bloqade-style pseudocode, cf. QuEra Aquila SDK [6])
import numpy as np

def unit_disk_mis_program(vertices, blockade_radius_um=5.0):
    """Place atoms at graph vertices; blockade enforces independence."""
    program = []
    program.append(("lattice", {"sites": vertices}))          # atom positions = vertices
    program.append(("rabi",    {"Omega_max": 15.8}))           # rad/us, cf. Aquila datasheet [6]
    # Adiabatic sweep: delta < 0 -> delta > 0 across the phase transition
    program.append(("sweep",   {"delta": (-20.0, +20.0),
                               "duration_us": 4.0}))
    program.append(("measure", {"basis": "rydberg_occupation"}))
    return program

# Readout bitstring: 1 = Rydberg excitation = vertex IN the independent set.
# Blockade guarantees no two adjacent vertices are both 1 (up to defects).
```

This paradigm has produced landmark many-body physics: the observation of a $\mathbb{Z}_2$ symmetry-breaking transition and scarred dynamics in 51-atom chains, the exploration of quantum spin-liquid-like phases on kagome lattices, and demonstrations of quantum advantage in sampling from classically intractable distributions. The *Aquila* system exposes exactly this mode on the cloud — up to 256 atoms, minimum site spacing $4\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, maximum Rabi frequency $15.8\,\mathrm{rad}/\mu\mathrm{s}$, detuning range $|\Delta| \le 125\,\mathrm{rad}/\mu\mathrm{s}$ [6] — and users program it by specifying atom coordinates, the same way one might lay out a printed circuit board.

The mapping extends beyond MIS. With local detuning control (site-resolved light shifts), the platform realizes disordered Ising models for many-body localization studies; with dual-species arrays (e.g., Rb–Cs), it implements spin-1 and fermionic models; and with stroboscopic Floquet driving, it engineers effective Hamiltonians with tunable range and sign. The geometric programmability — a new "chip layout" for every shot, as QuEra describes it [6] — is arguably the platform's deepest advantage over lithographically fixed competitors.

### 4.4 Error correction on reconfigurable arrays

The Bluvstein *et al.* *Nature* paper of December 2023 [3] is the field's fault-tolerance manifesto. Its key innovations, all enabled by reconfigurability:

1. **Zoned architecture.** Separate entangling, storage, and readout zones; atoms are shuttled between zones with AOD tweezers mid-circuit, so that measurement and reset of ancillas do not disturb data qubits.
2. **Transversal logical gates.** By rearranging the atoms of two logical qubits into interleaved pairs, a logical CNOT becomes $d^2$ parallel physical CNOTs — constant-depth, fault-tolerant by construction.
3. **Erasure conversion.** The dominant physical error — atom loss or leakage to non-computational states — is *detected* by the imaging system and converted to an erasure at a known location. Erasures are far easier to correct than depolarizing errors: the surface-code threshold for erasures approaches 50%, versus ~1% for Pauli errors.
4. **High-rate codes.** Follow-up work (January 2026, per industry reporting) demonstrated 96 logical qubits from 448 physical atoms using $\llbracket 16,6,4 \rrbracket$ codes [quantumzeitgeist], exploiting the platform's all-to-all connectivity within the blockade radius to implement LDPC-like codes that would be geometrically impossible on planar superconducting chips.

The demonstration of 48 logical qubits executing complex algorithms — with logical error rates *below* those of the constituent physical qubits, i.e., genuine error suppression — marked neutral atoms' entry into the fault-tolerant era [3].

## 5. Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

We now consolidate the platform's empirical standing and the formal results that bound its performance.

**Result 1 (Blockade suppression).** For $n = 70S_{1/2}$ states of $^{87}$Rb ($C_6 \approx 2\pi \times 860\,\mathrm{GHz}\,\mu\mathrm{m}^6$) driven at $\Omega = 2\pi \times 2\,$MHz, the blockade radius is $R_b = (C_6/\hbar\Omega)^{1/6} \approx 9\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. At a typical gate separation of $3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, the blockade ratio is $V/\hbar\Omega = (R_b/R)^6 \approx 729$, giving double-excitation leakage below $2 \times 10^{-6}$ — negligible compared to decay and dephasing errors. *This justifies treating the blockade as perfect in fidelity models.*

**Result 2 (Coherence hierarchy).** The platform exhibits a clean separation of timescales:

- Hyperfine qubit coherence: $T_2 \sim 1\,$s (seconds-scale, limited by magnetic field noise and differential light shifts) [9].
- Rydberg state lifetime: $\tau_r \sim 100\,\mu\mathrm{s}$.
- Two-qubit gate duration: $t_g \sim 0.5\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ [2].
- Single-qubit Raman gate: $t_{1q} \sim 1\,\mu\mathrm{s}$.

Hence the *coherence budget* permits $\sim 10^6$ single-qubit gates or $\sim 200$ sequential Rydberg gates per coherence time — the latter being the binding constraint that error correction must address, and precisely the regime where erasure conversion pays off [3].

**Result 3 (Scaling law for assembly).** With per-cycle filling probability $p_{\mathrm{site}}$ and $M$ reservoir atoms, the probability of a defect-free $N$-atom array after $k$ cycles is $1 - (1 - p_{\mathrm{site}}^N)^k$ to leading order. For $N = 1000$ and $p_{\mathrm{site}} = 0.99$, roughly $k \approx 10$ cycles suffice for $>99\%$ success — a logarithmic overhead in $N$, confirming that assembly is not the asymptotic bottleneck; laser power for trap generation is [6].

**Result 4 (Surface-code threshold crossing).** Evered *et al.* [2] demonstrated two-qubit gate fidelity $F = 99.5\%$ ($\epsilon = 5 \times 10^{-3}$) in parallel across 60 atoms, with characterized error sources and a roadmap to $99.9\%$ via cavity-enhanced Rydberg excitation and cryogenic operation. Since the surface-code threshold sits at $\epsilon_{\mathrm{th}} \approx 10^{-2}$, neutral atoms have crossed into the regime where adding physical qubits *reduces* the logical error rate — the defining criterion of scalable fault tolerance.

**Result 5 (Commercial scale, 2026).** Publicly reported system sizes: QuEra Aquila, 256 atoms [6]; Atom Computing Phoenix, 1,180 neutral-atom qubits; Pasqal, 324-qubit Fresnel-class systems with a 506-atom defect-free register demonstrated; Infleqtion Sqale, 1,600 physical qubits. These figures, while vendor-reported, are consistent with the optical power and field-of-view scaling analyzed above.

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires a clear-eyed account of what the platform cannot yet do.

**Gate speed versus coherence.** Rydberg gates are slow relative to the Rydberg lifetime: $t_g/\tau_r \sim 5 \times 10^{-3}$ even with optimal control. Every gate spends its entire duration with population in a state that decays in $100\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ — a fundamental tension absent in platforms (ions, superconductors) whose entangling interactions use metastable or virtual states. Cryogenic environments (suppressing blackbody-induced transitions, which dominate $\tau_r$ at room temperature) and higher-$n$ states ($\tau_r \propto n^3$) are the standard mitigations, but both add severe engineering complexity.

**Measurement speed and mid-circuit readout.** Fluorescence imaging requires milliseconds — $10^3$–$10^4$ times longer than a gate. Fast, high-fidelity mid-circuit measurement with atom reuse remains the hardest unsolved systems problem; the zoned architecture [3] sidesteps it by shuttling atoms to dedicated readout zones, but at the cost of millisecond-scale shuttling latency that dominates the logical clock cycle.

**Laser phase noise.** The ground–Rydberg transition is driven by frequency-doubled or sum-frequency lasers whose phase noise directly dephases the gate. Reaching $99.9\%$ fidelity demands phase noise below $\sim -110\,\mathrm{dBc/Hz}$ at MHz offsets — achievable with cavity filtering but not with free-running diode systems, imposing a real cost and complexity floor.

**Analog-mode verification.** Quantum simulators operating beyond classical simulability face an epistemic problem: when the device explores a regime no classical computer can check, how is correctness established? Cross-platform verification, self-verifying protocols, and certification via efficiently checkable observables remain active research areas rather than solved problems.

**Vacuum and background collisions.** Trap lifetimes of $\sim 10$–$100\,$s require ultra-high vacuum ($< 10^{-11}\,$mbar); background-gas collisions cause correlated atom loss that erasure conversion handles gracefully but that still caps circuit depth. Scaling to hour-long computations will require cryogenic vacuum chambers.

## 7. Conclusion

Neutral-atom quantum processors have traversed, in under a decade, the full arc from laboratory demonstration to cloud-accessible, error-corrected logical processors. The physics case is unusually clean: a single interaction — the Rydberg blockade, with its $n^{11}$-scaled van der Waals coefficient — supplies both the digital gate set and the analog Hamiltonian; a single optical technology — holographic tweezers with acousto-optic steering — supplies assembly, connectivity, and shuttling; and a single readout modality — fluorescence imaging — supplies both measurement and erasure detection. The empirical milestones — 99.5% parallel two-qubit gates [2], 48 logical qubits with genuine error suppression [3], 256-atom cloud systems [6], and thousand-atom commercial arrays — are not isolated records but points on a consistent scaling trajectory whose bottlenecks (laser power, vacuum, phase noise) are engineering challenges rather than physical impossibilities.

The most distinctive long-term advantage may be *geometric programmability*: the ability to redefine qubit connectivity shot-by-shot, to implement high-rate LDPC codes forbidden to planar architectures, and to shuttle entanglement across the processor as a primitive operation. If the fault-tolerance roadmaps — QuEra's progression from Aquila through Gemini to the Libra megaquop system targeting 256 logical qubits at $99.9999\%$ fidelity [quera] — are realized on schedule, neutral atoms will contend not merely as one platform among many, but as the architecture that unified analog simulation and digital error correction in a single physical substrate.
