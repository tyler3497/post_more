# Lattice-Based Post-Quantum Cryptography: Ring-LWE, Number-Theoretic Transforms, and the NIST PQC Standards

## Abstract

The advent of large-scale fault-tolerant quantum computers threatens the number-theoretic foundations of RSA, finite-field Diffie–Hellman, and elliptic-curve cryptography, all of which succumb to polynomial-time quantum attacks via Shor's algorithm [1][2]. Lattice-based cryptography has emerged as the dominant family of post-quantum replacements, offering worst-case to average-case security reductions, efficient implementations, and favorable bandwidth profiles. This thesis develops the mathematical and engineering foundations of the lattice-based schemes standardized by NIST: ML-KEM (FIPS 203), derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber, and ML-DSA (FIPS 204), derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium [1][3]. We present the Shortest Vector Problem, the Learning With Errors problem of Regev, and its structured variants Ring-LWE and Module-LWE, together with their reductions to worst-case ideal-lattice problems [6][9]. We give a full exposition of the Number-Theoretic Transform as the enabling arithmetic primitive for fast polynomial multiplication in cyclotomic rings, analyze the Fiat–Shamir with aborts paradigm and uniform rejection sampling behind Dilithium's compact signatures [6][7], the Fujisaki–Okamoto transform conferring IND-CCA2 security on Kyber [3], parameter selection across NIST security levels 1–5, and the side-channel attack surface of real deployments.

## 1 Introduction

Public-key cryptography as deployed today rests on three computational problems: integer factorization, the discrete logarithm in finite fields, and the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm. Peter Shor's 1994 result demonstrates that a sufficiently large quantum computer solves all three in polynomial time [1], collapsing the security of RSA, DSA, Diffie–Hellman, and ECDSA. While Grover's algorithm only quadratically accelerates symmetric-key search — a threat mitigated by doubling key lengths — the asymmetric landscape requires a wholesale replacement. NIST's Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) standardization project, launched in 2016, concluded its third round in 2022 by selecting four algorithms for standardization; the first three standards, FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA), were published on August 13, 2024 [1][2][5].

Two of the three initial standards are lattice-based, reflecting the maturity, performance, and cryptanalytic scrutiny of the lattice family. **ML-KEM** (Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism), originally submitted as **CRYSTALS-Kyber**, provides IND-CCA2-secure key establishment from the Module Learning With Errors (M-LWE) problem [3]. **ML-DSA** (Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm), originally **CRYSTALS-Dilithium**, provides EUF-CMA-secure signatures from the Module-LWE and Module-SIS problems via the Fiat–Shamir with aborts paradigm [6][7]. Together they cover the two fundamental asymmetric primitives — key exchange and authentication — that TLS, SSH, VPNs, and code-signing infrastructures require.

This thesis addresses four central questions: *why lattices* (worst-case to average-case reductions justifying concrete parameters), *what makes them fast* (the NTT in the standards' chosen moduli), *how signatures stay compact without Gaussian sampling* (rejection sampling and Fiat–Shamir with aborts), and *what remains fragile* (side channels, fault injection, and gaps in the security arguments).

We consolidate the definitions, reductions, algorithms, and parameter tables of the two standards into a single self-contained reference; we derive the NTT for the specific moduli $q = 3329$ and $q = 8380417$; we quantify Dilithium's rejection-sampling acceptance probabilities and signature-size accounting; and we survey the post-standardization side-channel literature, including timing attacks on decapsulation and masking countermeasures.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Lattices and hard problems

A **lattice** $\mathcal{L} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is a discrete additive subgroup generated by linearly independent vectors $\mathbf{b}_1, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n$:

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}) = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} z_i \mathbf{b}_i : z_i \in \mathbb{Z} \right\}.$$

The fundamental hard problems are:

- **SVP (Shortest Vector Problem):** given a basis $\mathbf{B}$, find a nonzero $\mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{L}$ minimizing $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert$.
- **SIVP (Shortest Independent Vectors Problem):** find $n$ linearly independent lattice vectors each of length at most $\gamma \cdot \lambda_n(\mathcal{L})$, where $\lambda_n$ is the $n$-th successive minimum.
- **GapSVP$_\gamma$:** decide whether $\lambda_1(\mathcal{L}) \le d$ or $\lambda_1(\mathcal{L}) > \gamma d$.
- **CVP (Closest Vector Problem):** given $\mathbf{t} \notin \mathcal{L}$, find the closest lattice vector.

> **Theorem (Ajtai, 1996):** There exist families of lattices for which solving certain average-case problems (e.g., SIS) is at least as hard as approximating SVP in the *worst case* to within polynomial factors. This worst-case to average-case reduction is the philosophical cornerstone of lattice cryptography: it asserts that random instances are hard *unless* an efficient algorithm exists for all instances of the underlying problem [6].

No quantum algorithm is known that solves approximate SVP to polynomial factors asymptotically faster than classical algorithms; the best known attacks remain lattice-reduction algorithms (LLL, BKZ) whose running time is exponential in the block size $\beta$.

### 2.2 Learning With Errors

Regev (2005) introduced **LWE**: for secret $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n$, distinguish samples $(\mathbf{a}_i, b_i = \langle \mathbf{a}_i, \mathbf{s} \rangle + e_i)$ with $\mathbf{a}_i \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q^n$ uniform and $e_i \leftarrow \chi$ (a narrow error distribution) from uniform samples [9]. Regev proved a quantum reduction from worst-case GapSVP/SIVP to average-case LWE.

**Ring-LWE** (Lyubashevsky, Peikert, Regev, 2010) replaces vectors by polynomials: work in $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^n+1)$ with $n$ a power of two, samples $(a, b = a\cdot s + e)$ where multiplication is in the ring [9]. **Module-LWE** generalizes to modules $R_q^k$ of rank $k$: samples $(\mathbf{a}, b = \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle + e)$ with $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \in R_q^k$ [3][9]. ### 2.3 The NIST PQC timeline

| Year | Milestone |
|------|-----------|
| 1994 | Shor's algorithm published — quantum threat to RSA/ECC identified [1] |
| 2016 | NIST PQC standardization project launched (69 first-round submissions) |
| 2020 | Round 3 finalists announced; Kyber, Dilithium, Falcon, SPHINCS$^+$ selected for analysis |
| 2022 | NIST selects Kyber, Dilithium, Falcon, SPHINCS$^+$ for standardization |
| 2024 | FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) published [1][2][5] |

ML-KEM and ML-DSA are both members of the **CRYSTALS** (Cryptographic Suite for Algebraic Lattices) family [3][6].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology combines *definitional consolidation* (exact parameter sets and algorithms as specified in FIPS 203/204), *algorithmic analysis* (asymptotic and concrete complexity of NTT-based multiplication, rejection sampling, and the Fujisaki–Okamoto transform), and *empirical grounding* (published cycle counts, key/signature sizes, and cryptanalytic cost estimates from the literature) [1][3][5][8].

### 3.1 ML-KEM parameter sets

ML-KEM operates in $R_q$ with $n = 256$, $q = 3329$, and module rank $k \in \{2, 3, 4\}$ [3]:

| Parameter set | $k$ | $\eta_1$ | $\eta_2$ | $(d_u, d_v)$ | pk (B) | sk (B) | ct (B) | NIST level |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-KEM-512 | 2 | 3 | 2 | (10, 4) | 800 | 1632 | 768 | 1 (≈ AES-128) |
| ML-KEM-768 | 3 | 2 | 2 | (10, 4) | 1184 | 2400 | 1088 | 3 (≈ AES-192) |
| ML-KEM-1024 | 4 | 2 | 2 | (11, 5) | 1568 | 3168 | 1568 | 5 (≈ AES-256) |

The binomial error distribution $B_\eta$ samples each coefficient as $\sum_{i=1}^{\eta}(b_i - b'_i)$ with $b_i, b'_i \in \{0,1\}$ uniform — a centered distribution of variance $\eta/2$ that is trivially constant-time to sample, unlike discrete Gaussians [3]. Ciphertext compression drops $d_u, d_v$ low-order bits to shrink bandwidth at the cost of a small, precisely bounded decryption-failure probability ($\delta \le 2^{-128}$ for ML-KEM-512, $\le 2^{-164}$ for ML-KEM-768) [3].

### 3.2 ML-DSA parameter sets

ML-DSA operates in $R_q$ with $n = 256$ and $q = 2^{23} - 2^{13} + 1 = 8380417$ [6][7]:

| Parameter set | $(k, \ell)$ | $\tau$ | pk (B) | sig (B) | NIST level |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-DSA-44 | (4, 4) | 39 | 1312 | 2420 | 2 |
| ML-DSA-65 | (6, 5) | 49 | 1952 | 3309 | 3 |
| ML-DSA-87 | (8, 7) | 60 | 2592 | 4627 | 5 |

All levels share the same ring and modulus; only the module dimensions $(k, \ell)$ and the challenge weight $\tau$ vary, simplifying implementation [7]. The challenge polynomial $c \in R_q$ has exactly $\tau$ coefficients in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and the rest zero, sampled by the `SampleInBall` routine.

### 3.3 The Fujisaki–Okamoto lift and Dilithium's challenge space

We write $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert_\infty$ for the infinity norm, $S_\eta = \{x \in R : \lVert x \rVert_\infty \le \eta\}$ for bounded polynomials, and $B_\tau$ for the set of challenge polynomials with $\tau$ nonzero $\pm 1$ coefficients. All logarithms are base 2. ML-KEM's IND-CCA2 security comes from applying the Fujisaki–Okamoto transform to an IND-CPA core (§4.1); ML-DSA's challenge space $B_\tau$ keeps the challenge entropy at $\binom{256}{\tau} 2^\tau \gg 2^{200}$ for all parameter sets, forcing forgeries through the Module-SIS problem rather than challenge guessing [6][7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 ML-KEM: a CCA-secure KEM from Module-LWE

ML-KEM is built in two layers [3][4]:

1. **K-PKE**, an IND-CPA-secure public-key encryption scheme: key generation samples $\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{e} \leftarrow B_\eta^k$ and $\mathbf{A} \leftarrow R_q^{k \times k}$ (expanded from a seed via SHAKE-128), publishing $\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{e}$. Encryption of $m \in \{0,1\}^{256}$ outputs compressed $(\mathbf{u}, v)$ hiding $m$ under an M-LWE sample.
2. **The Fujisaki–Okamoto (FO) transform** lifts K-PKE to the IND-CCA2-secure KEM: encapsulation re-encrypts the derived message and *verifies* the re-encryption matches the received ciphertext, deriving the shared secret on success and an implicit-rejection value on failure [3][4].

> **Theorem (informal, FO security):** If K-PKE is IND-CPA secure and has negligible decryption-failure probability under the randomness used by the FO transform, then ML-KEM is IND-CCA2 secure in the (quantum) random-oracle model, with tightness loss linear in the number of oracle queries [3].

Implicit rejection — returning a pseudorandom value derived from a secret seed on decapsulation failure rather than an explicit error — is essential: it denies chosen-ciphertext attackers the failure oracle that would otherwise enable key-recovery attacks exploiting the (tiny but nonzero) decryption-failure rate [3].

### 4.2 The Number-Theoretic Transform: fast arithmetic in $R_q$

Naive polynomial multiplication in $R_q$ costs $O(n^2)$ coefficient multiplications. The NTT reduces this to $O(n \log n)$ by evaluating polynomials at roots of unity, multiplying pointwise, and interpolating back:

$$\mathrm{NTT}(f \cdot g) = \mathrm{NTT}(f) \odot \mathrm{NTT}(g),$$

where $\odot$ denotes pointwise multiplication. For the negacyclic convolution $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^n+1)$, one uses roots of $X^n + 1$, i.e., primitive $2n$-th roots of unity $\zeta$ with $\zeta^n \equiv -1 \pmod q$.

The choice of modulus is *deliberate NTT engineering*:

- **ML-KEM:** $q = 3329 = 13 \cdot 2^8 + 1$. Since $2n = 512 \mid (q-1) = 3328 = 13 \cdot 256$... in fact $3328 = 2^8 \cdot 13$, so a primitive 256th root exists but not a 512th root. Kyber therefore uses an *incomplete* NTT: $X^{256}+1$ splits into 128 quadratics $X^2 - \zeta^{2i+1}$, and the transform maps to 128 degree-1 polynomials. Pointwise multiplication becomes multiplication of linear polynomials, costing a few extra modular multiplications [3].
- **ML-DSA:** $q = 8380417 = 2^{23} - 2^{13} + 1$, with $q - 1 = 2^{13} \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 13$... it admits a primitive 512th root ($\zeta = 1753$), so the full NTT splits $X^{256}+1$ into 256 linear factors and pointwise multiplication is a single modular multiply per coefficient [6][7].

```rust
// Incomplete-NTT pointwise multiply (Kyber-style), 128 linear factors
fn basemul(a0: i16, a1: i16, b0: i16, b1: i16, zeta: i16) -> (i16, i16) {
    let c0 = montgomery_reduce(a1 as i32 * b1 as i32) * zeta as i32
           + a0 as i32 * b0 as i32;   // (a0+a1X)(b0+b1X) mod (X^2-zeta)
    let c1 = a0 as i32 * b1 as i32 + a1 as i32 * b0 as i32;
    (montgomery_reduce(c0) as i16, montgomery_reduce(c1) as i16)
}
```

*Complexity accounting.* One NTT of length 256 costs $n \log_2 n = 2048$ butterfly operations. A matrix-vector product $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}$ in ML-KEM-768 needs $k^2 = 9$ forward NTTs (amortized, since $\hat{\mathbf{A}}$ is precomputed and stored in the public key) plus $k$ inverse NTTs — roughly $10^4$ modular multiplications versus $\approx 5.9 \times 10^5$ for schoolbook multiplication: a ~50× speedup that makes lattice schemes competitive with elliptic-curve operations on constrained devices [3][5].

> **Theorem (NTT correctness):** Let $q$ be prime with $2n \mid (q-1)$ (full case) or $n \mid (q-1)$ with suitable quadratic splitting (incomplete case). Then for all $f, g \in R_q$, $\mathrm{INTT}(\mathrm{NTT}(f) \odot \mathrm{NTT}(g)) = f \cdot g \bmod (X^n+1, q)$.

### 4.3 ML-DSA: Fiat–Shamir with aborts and uniform rejection sampling

Dilithium's central innovation is eliminating Gaussian sampling. The signing paradigm of Lyubashevsky (2009, 2012) works as follows [6][7]:

1. Sample masking vector $\mathbf{y} \leftarrow S_{\gamma_1 - 1}^\ell$ *uniformly*.
2. Compute $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}$, split into high and low bits: $\mathbf{w}_1 = \mathrm{HighBits}_q(\mathbf{w}, 2\gamma_2)$.
3. Challenge $c = H(\mu \Vert \mathbf{w}_1) \in B_\tau$ via `SampleInBall`.
4. Response $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{y} + c\mathbf{s}_1$.
5. **Rejection sampling:** if $\lVert \mathbf{z} \rVert_\infty \ge \gamma_1 - \beta$, or $\lVert \mathrm{LowBits}_q(\mathbf{w} - c\mathbf{s}_2, 2\gamma_2) \rVert_\infty \ge \gamma_2 - \beta$, or the hint count exceeds $\omega$ — *abort and restart* with fresh $\mathbf{y}$ [6][7].

Because $\mathbf{y}$ is uniform over a box much larger than the shift $c\mathbf{s}_1$ (whose coefficients are bounded by $\beta = \tau \cdot \eta$), the distribution of accepted $\mathbf{z}$ is statistically independent of the secret $\mathbf{s}_1$. This is the key lemma:

> **Theorem (Rejection-sampling independence, Lyubashevsky):** Let $\mathbf{y} \leftarrow S_{\gamma_1-1}^\ell$ uniform and $\mathbf{v} = c\mathbf{s}_1$ fixed with $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert_\infty \le \beta \ll \gamma_1$. Conditioned on acceptance ($\lVert \mathbf{y} + \mathbf{v} \rVert_\infty < \gamma_1 - \beta$), the distribution of $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{y} + \mathbf{v}$ is uniform over $S_{\gamma_1 - \beta - 1}^\ell$, independent of $\mathbf{v}$. The expected number of repetitions is $\left(\frac{2\gamma_1 - 1}{2(\gamma_1 - \beta) - 1}\right)^{\!256\ell} \approx e^{256\ell\beta/\gamma_1}$.

For Dilithium2 ($\gamma_1 = 2^{17}$, $\beta = 78$, $\ell = 4$), the expected repetition rate is $\approx 4.25$; Dilithium3 $\approx 5.1$; Dilithium5 $\approx 3.9$ [6].

*Why uniform sampling matters.* Discrete Gaussian samplers are notoriously hard to implement in constant time; timing and cache attacks against Gaussian sampling broke several early lattice signatures. Dilithium's uniform-only design was an explicit response: every distribution in the scheme is uniform over a box or ball, making constant-time implementation straightforward [6][7].

### 4.4 Security reductions: from worst-case lattices to the standards

The security claims of ML-KEM and ML-DSA rest on a chain of reductions [3][6][9]:

1. **Worst-case foundation:** approximating SIVP/GapSVP to polynomial factors on $n$-dimensional lattices admits no known subexponential classical or quantum algorithm.
2. **Regev (2005/2009):** quantum reduction from worst-case SIVP/GapSVP to average-case LWE.
3. **Lyubashevsky–Peikert–Regev (2010):** reduction from worst-case problems on *ideal* lattices to Ring-LWE.
4. **Langlois–Stehlé (2015):** reduction from worst-case *module* lattices to Module-LWE.
5. **Concrete cryptanalysis:** the best known attacks on ML-KEM/ML-DSA parameters are primal and dual lattice attacks using BKZ with block size $\beta$; NIST security levels are calibrated so that the estimated cost (in "gates" or core-years under the Core-SVP model) matches or exceeds breaking AES-128/192/256 or SHA3-256 [2][3][5].

*The Core-SVP methodology.* Security estimates count the cost of one SVP oracle call in dimension $\beta$ as $2^{0.292\beta}$ (classical) or $2^{0.265\beta}$ (quantum, via Groverized sieving). For ML-KEM-768, the primal attack requires BKZ block size $\beta \approx 400$–$450$, yielding $\approx 2^{118}$ classical gates — comfortably above the Level 3 target [3]. These estimates are heuristic: they assume sieving scales as predicted and ignore memory costs, polynomial overhead, and the gap between asymptotic models and real implementations. The estimates are deliberately conservative, and NIST's levels incorporate substantial margins [2].

### 4.5 Side-channel hardening and the post-standardization attack surface

Standardization is the beginning, not the end, of security analysis. The deployed attack surface includes [5][8]:

- **Decapsulation timing (KyberSlash, Dec 2023):** non-constant-time Barrett/Montgomery reductions in several Kyber implementations leaked secret-dependent timing through division instructions, enabling key recovery with a few thousand decapsulation queries. The fix — fully constant-time modular arithmetic — is now mandatory guidance [5].
- **Signing leakage in Dilithium:** the rejection-sampling loop has data-dependent iteration counts; implementations must ensure the *abort decision* does not leak secrets through timing or power channels. Masked (secret-shared) implementations split the secrets into shares, at roughly 3–5× cycle-count overhead [8].
- **Fault injection:** skipping the FO re-encryption check in ML-KEM decapsulation converts the KEM into a decryption oracle; skipping a rejection-sampling bound check in ML-DSA signing leaks the secret key directly.

| Countermeasure | Protects against | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Constant-time arithmetic (no secret-dependent branches/division) | Timing attacks (KyberSlash class) | ~5–15% cycles |
| Masking (1st-order, secret sharing) | Power/EM side channels | 3–5× cycles, 2–3× RAM |
| Redundant FO check / infective computation | Fault injection on decaps/verify | ~2× for checked operations |

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: EUF-CMA security of ML-DSA

We sketch the reduction underlying FIPS 204's security claim [6][7]:

1. **Lossiness / zero-knowledge:** By the rejection-sampling theorem (§4.3), accepted transcripts $(\mathbf{w}_1, c, \mathbf{z})$ are simulatable without the secret key: the simulator samples $\mathbf{z}$ uniform from $S_{\gamma_1-\beta-1}^\ell$ and programs the random oracle $H(\mu \Vert \mathbf{w}_1) = c$. Hence signatures leak no information about $(\mathbf{s}_1, \mathbf{s}_2)$ beyond the public key.
2. **Forking:** A forger producing a valid signature $(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{h}, c)$ for a fresh message is rewound (general forking lemma) to obtain two signatures $(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{h}, c)$, $(\mathbf{z}', \mathbf{h}', c')$ with $c \ne c'$ on the same $\mathbf{w}_1$. Subtracting yields $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{z}') = (c - c')\mathbf{t}_1 \cdot 2^d + \text{(small)}$, i.e., a short nonzero vector in the module lattice — a solution to Module-SIS.
3. **Conclusion:** EUF-CMA security reduces to the hardness of Module-LWE (hiding the key) and Module-SIS (unforgeability) in the (quantum) random-oracle model [6].

### 5.2 Measured performance (representative, AVX2 / Cortex-M4)

| Scheme | KeyGen | Sign / Encaps | Verify / Decaps, notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| ML-KEM-768 (AVX2) | ~120k cycles | ~150k / ~180k cycles | Optimized implementations [3][5] |
| ML-DSA-65 (AVX2) | ~300k cycles | ~1.5M cycles (incl. ~5 rejections) | ~400k cycles | Reference benchmarks [8] |

Replacing X25519 with ML-KEM-768 in a chat-encryption scenario increased runtime by ~2.3×, energy by ~2.3×, and bandwidth by ~70× — the bandwidth cost, not computation, is the dominant deployment pain [2][5].

Estimates are heuristic and conservative; they assume idealized sieving and ignore memory and polynomial factors [2][3].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Bandwidth and storage.** ML-KEM-1024 ciphertexts (1568 B) and ML-DSA-87 signatures (4627 B) are 1–2 orders of magnitude larger than ECDH/ECDSA artifacts. Protocols with tight size constraints (DNSSEC, embedded attestations, some blockchain contexts) face real integration costs [5][8].
2. **Decryption failures, however rare.** The FO transform's CCA proof requires failure probability negligible in the security parameter; ML-KEM's $\delta \le 2^{-164}$ satisfies this, but implementers must not weaken error distributions or compression for performance without redoing the analysis [3].
3. **Ideal-lattice structure risk.** Ring- and Module-LWE rely on *structured* lattices (ideals/modules over cyclotomic rings), a strictly stronger assumption than plain LWE. While no attack exploiting the ring structure is known against the standardized parameters, the 2022–2023 cryptanalytic progress against overstretched NTRU-like assumptions is a standing reminder that algebraic structure is a liability as well as an asset.
4. **Side-channel fragility.** The standards specify algorithms, not leakage resilience. Constant-time discipline, masking, and fault-injection countermeasures remain the implementer's burden, and the history (KyberSlash et al.) shows this burden is routinely mishandled [5][8].
5. **Heuristic security estimates.** Core-SVP numbers are extrapolations from small experiments; the true cost of BKZ-450 with sieving is unknown, and quantum resource estimates for lattice sieving are speculative [2].
6. **No forward secrecy from the KEM alone.** ML-KEM provides key establishment; forward secrecy requires ephemeral key usage discipline at the protocol layer (as in TLS 1.3's handshake design).

## 7 Conclusion

Lattice-based cryptography has crossed the threshold from research curiosity to federal standard. ML-KEM and ML-DSA distill two decades of theoretical work — worst-case to average-case reductions, Ring- and Module-LWE, the NTT as a practical arithmetic engine, rejection sampling and Fiat–Shamir with aborts, the Fujisaki–Okamoto transform — into precisely specified, efficiently implementable, conservatively parameterized algorithms [1][3][6]. Their security rests on problems that have resisted both classical and quantum attack for decades, and their performance is practical today [5][8].

The work ahead is engineering and cryptanalytic rather than definitional: hardening implementations against side channels and faults, managing the bandwidth transition across Internet protocols, continuing to stress-test the Core-SVP estimates, and preparing the second wave of standards for signature compactness and algorithm diversity. The mathematical foundations are the strongest in post-quantum cryptography's portfolio; the task now is to deploy them without squandering that strength through implementation error.
