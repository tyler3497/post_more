# Mechanized Game-Based Cryptographic Proofs in EasyCrypt: Probabilistic Relational Hoare Logic, Sequences of Games, Random-Oracle Proofs, and Verified OAEP Security

## Abstract

Mechanized game-based proofs close the gap between the pen-and-paper arguments cryptographers actually write and the rigor that high-assurance cryptography demands. EasyCrypt, a domain-specific proof assistant descended from the CertiCrypt Coq framework, provides a programming-language foundation for machine-checked cryptographic proofs: a module system with abstract adversary interfaces, a *probabilistic relational Hoare logic* (pRHL) for relating pairs of program executions, and a backend that discharges verification conditions to SMT solvers. This thesis presents a self-contained account of how EasyCrypt mechanizes the two dominant pen-and-paper methodologies — Bellare and Rogaway's *code-based game playing* [4][5] and Shoup's *sequences of games* [6] — including the fundamental lemma of game playing, lazy versus eager random-oracle implementations, and coupling arguments via probabilistic lifting. The theory is developed through the emblematic case study of RSA-OAEP, whose IND-CCA security was machine-verified in EasyCrypt [2][7]. We report proof-engineering metrics across verified schemes and assess the logic's limits: the random-oracle idealization, adversary modeling assumptions, and the boundary between computational soundness and absolute assurance.

## 1. Introduction

Modern cryptography derives its confidence from *provable security*: the claim that breaking a scheme is at least as hard as solving a well-studied computational problem. Yet the proofs themselves have historically been written in a semi-formal idiom — pseudocode games, hand-waved reductions, and advantage bounds whose error terms are asserted rather than derived. Two methodological revolutions tamed this complexity on paper. First, Bellare and Rogaway's **code-based game-playing technique** [4][5] recast security definitions as probabilistic programs and reductions as *syntactic transformations* of those programs, organized around the *fundamental lemma of game playing* (the identical-until-bad bound). Second, Shoup's **sequences of games** [6] codified the now-universal discipline of organizing a proof as a chain G₀, G₁, …, Gₙ in which adjacent games differ by a single, precisely justified step, so that the total adversarial advantage *telescopes* into a sum of per-hop differences.

These methodologies are *already* programming-language artifacts: games are programs, hops are program transformations, and advantage bounds are quantitative program properties. This observation is the foundation of **EasyCrypt**, a domain-specific proof assistant that turns game-based proofs into machine-checked objects [1][3]. EasyCrypt descends from **CertiCrypt**, a Coq-embedded framework for certifying cryptographic constructions [8]; EasyCrypt instead stands alone, embedding its program logics *natively* in an ambient higher-order logic and delegating first-order reasoning to SMT solvers. Its core logic is **probabilistic relational Hoare logic (pRHL)**, which reasons about *pairs* of program executions related by probabilistic couplings — exactly the structure of a game hop.

This thesis makes three contributions:

1. A precise, self-contained exposition of pRHL — its judgments, its coupling semantics via *probabilistic lifting*, and its tactic mechanization in EasyCrypt.
2. A systematic account of how the pen-and-paper disciplines of Bellare–Rogaway and Shoup are internalized: hop taxonomies, the mechanized fundamental lemma (`fel` tactic), and lazy/eager random-oracle equivalence (`eager` tactic).
3. An end-to-end treatment of the **verified IND-CCA security of RSA-OAEP** [2][7], the flagship case study, together with proof-engineering metrics and a candid assessment of limitations.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Code-based game playing

In the code-based paradigm, a security experiment is a *probabilistic program* — typically pseudocode with random sampling statements `x <$ D` — executed against an abstract adversary given as an oracle-calling procedure [4][5]. The adversary's *advantage* is the difference between the probability that two games output 1 (in the indistinguishability setting) or the probability that a flag `bad` is set (in the unforgeability setting). A proof is then a *sequence of syntactic refinements*:

- **Bridging steps**: transformations that preserve the joint distribution of the adversary's view up to the point where a flag `bad` is set; justified by the *fundamental lemma*, which bounds the difference in success probabilities by `Pr[bad]`.
- **Reduction steps**: transformations justified by a computational assumption, where a difference in game behavior is converted into an adversary against a hard problem.
- **Information-theoretic steps**: transformations justified by probability theory alone (e.g., one-time pad, uniformity arguments).

> **Theorem (Fundamental Lemma of Game Playing, informal):** If two games G and H are *identical until bad* — i.e., their code differs only after a flag `bad` is set — then for any adversary A, `|Pr[Gᴬ ⇒ 1] − Pr[Hᴬ ⇒ 1]| ≤ Pr[Gᴬ sets bad]` [4][5].

### 2.2 Sequences of games

Shoup's tutorial [6] distilled the discipline: organize the proof as G₀ → G₁ → ⋯ → Gₙ where G₀ is the real security game and Gₙ is information-theoretically secure, and bound each hop `Δᵢ = |Pr[Gᵢ ⇒ 1] − Pr[Gᵢ₊₁ ⇒ 1]|`. By the triangle inequality,

> **Telescoping bound:** `Adv(A) ≤ Σᵢ Δᵢ + Adv_ideal`, where `Adv_ideal` is the (often zero) advantage in the final game.

Each hop must be individually justified as *syntactic* (observationally equivalent code), *statistical* (small distinguishing probability), or *computational* (a reduction). The discipline's power lies in *locality*: the cryptographer reasons about one small change at a time, and the global bound follows mechanically.

### 2.3 From CertiCrypt to EasyCrypt

CertiCrypt [8] embedded this methodology in Coq: games as probabilistic programs in a deeply embedded language, observational equivalence and relational Hoare logic as Coq developments, and program transformations as certified tactics. Its flagship application was the machine-checked IND-CCA security of OAEP [2], a development of tens of thousands of lines.

EasyCrypt [1][3] retains the methodology but re-architects the tool: rather than embedding the language in Coq, it is a *standalone* prover whose ambient logic natively understands probabilistic programs. Program logics — pRHL and a logic for bounding the probability of events — are *axiomatized* rather than derived from a formalized semantics, trading foundational certitude for agility: new logics and a rich module system (abstract adversary types, oracle interfaces) can be experimented with rapidly [1]. Verification conditions that are first-order are discharged by SMT solvers (Alt-Ergo, Z3, CVC), leaving the cryptographer to guide only the relational, game-hopping structure.

### 2.4 The random-oracle model and OAEP

The **random-oracle model (ROM)** idealizes hash functions as perfectly random functions accessible only via oracle queries [7]. OAEP — *Optimal Asymmetric Encryption Padding* — is the canonical ROM success story: a two-round Feistel network that converts any trapdoor one-way permutation `f` into an IND-CCA-secure encryption scheme. With `G : {0,1}^k₀ → {0,1}^(n+k₁)` and `H : {0,1}^(n+k₁) → {0,1}^k₀`:

- **Encrypt:** `s = (m ‖ 0^k₁) ⊕ G(r)`; `t = r ⊕ H(s)`; `c = f(s, t)`.
- **Decrypt:** `(s,t) = f⁻¹(c)`; `r = t ⊕ H(s)`; `M = s ⊕ G(r)`; accept iff the low `k₁` bits of `M` are zero.

Shoup's *OAEP Reconsidered* [7] gave the definitive game-based analysis, establishing plaintext-extractability and a tight reduction to one-wayness. OAEP thus became the natural flagship for mechanization: a proof that is conceptually a long sequence of games with delicate random-oracle bookkeeping — exactly the kind of proof where human error hides.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The EasyCrypt proof workflow

An EasyCrypt development has five ingredients [3]:

1. **Declarations**: types, constants, and operators for the mathematical objects (groups, bitstrings, oracles).
2. **Axioms**: algebraic properties (group laws, XOR algebra) consumed by the SMT backend.
3. **Adversary interfaces**: abstract module types, e.g.

```ec
module type Adv_OAEP = {
  proc choose()    : plaintext * plaintext;
  proc guess(c : ciphertext) : bool;
  proc h(x : bitstring) : bitstring;  (* random oracle H *)
  proc g(x : bitstring) : bitstring;  (* random oracle G *)
};
```

4. **Games**: modules whose `main` procedures encode security experiments.
5. **Proof scripts**: pRHL judgments and tactic invocations discharging each hop.

The adversary is *abstract*: its code is unknown, but its interface is fixed, so all reasoning is relative to an arbitrary efficient adversary — the standard cryptographic quantification.

### 3.2 pRHL judgments

The central judgment form is

```
⊨ c₁ ~ c₂ : Ψ ⇒ Φ
```

read: *if the initial memories of the two programs satisfy the relational pre-condition Ψ, then there exists a coupling of the two output distributions under which the post-condition Φ holds almost surely*. For game hops, the canonical shape is an *equivalence*:

```ec
equiv [ CPA0(A).main ~ CPA1(A).main : ={glob A} ==> ={res} ].
```

meaning: starting from equal adversary state, the two games return equal results with probability 1. Advantage statements are expressed via a probability logic: `Pr[CPA(A).main() @ &m : res]`, the probability that `main` returns true from initial memory `&m`.

### 3.3 The tactic inventory

| Tactic | Purpose |
|---|---|
| `proc` / `wp` / `sp` | Weakest-precondition / strongest-postcondition calculus |
| `sim` | Prove two procedures equivalent by relational invariant |
| `equiv` | Introduce a pRHL goal for two programs |
| `transitivity` | Chain two relational judgments (the *hop combinator*) |
| `fel` | Apply the Fundamental Lemma (identical-until-bad) |
| `eager` | Swap lazy and eager random-oracle implementations |
| `rnd` | Couple two random samplings (e.g., via a bijection) |
| `inline` / `swap` | Semantics-preserving code motion inside a game |
| `smt` | Discharge first-order goals via SMT solvers |

The `transitivity` tactic is the mechanized embodiment of Shoup's telescoping: each hop is an independent `equiv` lemma, and the global bound is assembled by chaining. The `fel` tactic internalizes the fundamental lemma: given a judgment that two games are identical until `bad`, it reduces the advantage difference to a bound on `Pr[bad]` [1][2].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 pRHL: probabilistic lifting and couplings

The semantics of `⊨ c₁ ~ c₂ : Ψ ⇒ Φ` rests on **probabilistic lifting**: a relation `R` on memories lifts to a relation `R♯` on distributions — a *coupling* — where `μ₁ R♯ μ₂` iff there exists a joint distribution `μ` on pairs with marginals `μ₁, μ₂` supported on `R`. A judgment is valid when, for all initial memories satisfying `Ψ`, the output distributions are related by `Φ♯`.

Couplings are constructed *syntactically*. The `rnd` tactic couples two sampling statements `x₁ <$ d₁` and `x₂ <$ d₂` via a bijection `f` on the sample spaces, reducing a probabilistic claim to a deterministic relational verification condition discharged by `smt`. This is the mechanized form of the classic "same coins" argument:

> **Theorem (Coupling via bijection):** If `f : supp(d₁) → supp(d₂)` is a bijection with `Pr[d₁](x) = Pr[d₂](f(x))` for all `x`, then `⊨ x₁ <$ d₁ ~ x₂ <$ d₂ : Ψ ⇒ Ψ ∧ x₂ = f(x₁)`.

More sophisticated couplings — e.g., the "up-to-bad" couplings underlying `fel` — relate executions that agree *conditionally* on a bad event not occurring. The logic thus internalizes both perfect indistinguishability hops and statistical hops in a single judgment form, with the quantitative content carried by the probability logic rather than the relational logic.

### 4.2 Mechanizing sequences of games

Every pen-and-paper hop class has a mechanized counterpart:

| Pen-and-paper hop (Shoup [6]) | EasyCrypt mechanization |
|---|---|
| Syntactic / bridging step | `equiv` + `sim`/`inline`/`swap`; observational equivalence |
| Identical-until-bad | `fel` tactic; reduces to bounding `Pr[bad]` |
| Statistical hop | Coupling with quantitative lifting; union bounds |
| Computational hop | Reduction module `B` built from `A`; `equiv` between game and `B`'s experiment |
| Information-theoretic finale | `Pr` bound of 1/2 or 0 by uniformity arguments |

A typical development proves one lemma per hop — `lemma hop1 : equiv [...]`, `lemma hop2 : ...` — and a final theorem chains them:

```ec
lemma security &m :
  Pr[ G0(A).main() @ &m : res ] <=
  Pr[ G1(A).main() @ &m : res ] + bound1 +
  Pr[ G2(A).main() @ &m : res ] + bound2.
```

The *transitivity* of relational judgments is what makes the telescoping bound a *checked* artifact rather than a hand-waved summation: the proof assistant verifies that the post-condition of hop *i* implies the pre-condition of hop *i+1*, catching the classic pen-and-paper error of mismatched game interfaces.

### 4.3 Random oracles: lazy versus eager sampling

Random oracles admit two *observationally equivalent* implementations whose interchangeability is the workhorse of ROM proofs [2]:

- **Lazy**: maintain a table `T`, initially empty; on query `x`, if `x ∉ dom(T)` sample `T[x] <$ D`, return `T[x]`.
- **Eager**: at initialization, sample the *entire* function table `T[x] <$ D` for all `x` upfront; queries are table lookups.

Eager sampling is infeasible to execute but invaluable for proofs: it makes the oracle's answers *independent of the query order*, enabling arguments like "the value `H(s*)` is uniform and independent of the adversary's view." The `eager` tactic proves the equivalence of the two implementations as a pRHL judgment, so the cryptographer may freely switch representations mid-proof. Puncturing — resampling a single point, the basis of "planting" arguments in OAEP and FDH proofs — is handled by composing `eager` with a coupling that re-randomizes one table entry. The PRP/PRF switching lemma, a staple of the methodology, was among the first results mechanized in this style [2].

> **Theorem (Lazy/eager equivalence, mechanized):** `⊨ LazyRO.main ~ EagerRO.main : ={queries} ⇒ ={responses}` — the observable behavior of the two oracle implementations coincides on all query sequences.

### 4.4 Verified OAEP: IND-CCA security end to end

The EasyCrypt verification of OAEP [2] follows Shoup's game-based analysis [7] as a sequence of games:

1. **G₀**: the IND-CCA experiment with real oracles `G, H` and decryption oracle.
2. **G₁**: eager sampling of `G, H`; the challenge randomness `r*` and values `G(r*)`, `H(s*)` are drawn upfront.
3. **G₂**: the decryption oracle is *simulated without the trapdoor* — invalid ciphertexts are rejected by consulting the oracle tables (plaintext extractability). The hop is justified by identical-until-bad, with `bad` the event that simulation and real decryption disagree.
4. **G₃**: the challenge ciphertext is decoupled from the message bit (one-time-pad style masking via `G(r*)`), making the adversary's advantage zero; the difference is bounded by the one-wayness of `f`.

The final concrete bound has the characteristic shape:

> **Theorem (OAEP IND-CCA, mechanized [2]):** For any IND-CCA adversary `A` making `qG` queries to `G`, `qH` queries to `H`, and `qD` decryption queries, there exists an inverter `I` against the one-wayness of `f` with `Adv^ind-cca_OAEP(A) ≤ Adv^ow_f(I) + (qD·qG + qD·qH + qG·qH) / 2^k₀ + qD/2^k₁`, with `I` running in essentially the time of `A` plus the simulation overhead.

Crucially, every term in this bound is *produced by the proof script*: `fel` applications generate the `bad`-event summands, and the reduction hop generates the `Adv^ow` term together with an explicit inverter module whose runtime is syntactically apparent. The proof is approximately 1,300–1,500 lines of EasyCrypt [2] — large, but each line corresponds to a checkable inference rather than a leap of faith.

### 4.5 Proof automation and engineering discipline

The human effort concentrates where it should: choosing the *sequence of games* and the *couplings*. Everything else — verification-condition generation, algebraic simplification, first-order reasoning — is automated. Two engineering practices proved essential across the EasyCrypt corpus [1][3]:

1. **Modular adversary interfaces.** Because adversaries are abstract modules, a single game definition serves all reductions; only the *wrapper* modules (reductions, simulators) vary.
2. **Lemma-per-hop granularity.** Small, named `equiv` lemmas compose via `transitivity` and are robust to local edits — the proof-engineering analogue of Shoup's locality principle.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation

We summarize representative verified developments from the EasyCrypt/CertiCrypt literature [1][2][3][8]. Figures are approximate as reported; verification times are single-core SMT-backed runs.

| Scheme / result | Security notion | Proof size (LOC) | Check time |
|---|---|---|---|
| ElGamal | IND-CPA | ~300 | < 1 min |
| Hashed ElGamal | IND-CPA (from LCDH) | ~800 | ~2 min |
| RSA-FDH | EUF-CMA (ROM) | ~900 | ~3 min |
| RSA-OAEP | IND-CCA (ROM) | ~1,300–1,500 | ~5–10 min |
| PRP/PRF switching lemma | Statistical bound | ~400 | ~1 min |
| CBC / CTR modes | IND-CPA | ~600–1,000 | ~2–4 min |
| CertiCrypt OAEP (Coq) | IND-CCA (ROM) | tens of thousands | hours |

*Observations.* First, **automation compresses proofs by an order of magnitude**: the Coq-embedded CertiCrypt OAEP proof required tens of thousands of lines, while the EasyCrypt proof of the same theorem is roughly 1,400 lines, because SMT solvers discharge the algebraic and first-order obligations that Coq required humans to script [1][2]. Second, **check times stay interactive** — minutes, not hours — which makes the proof a living artifact that can be re-run after edits. Third, cost scales with *oracle bookkeeping*, not conceptual depth: ROM proofs dominate the table because lazy/eager manipulations and bad-event accounting generate the most verification conditions.

Ablation-style evidence for the value of the methodology: the `fel` tactic alone collapses what is typically a page-long pen-and-paper identical-until-bad argument into a single tactic invocation plus a probability bound, and the `eager` tactic eliminates an entire class of "deferred sampling" errors that historically produced incorrect published ROM proofs.

## 6. Limitations

**The random-oracle idealization.** Mechanization does not dissolve the ROM controversy: a verified ROM proof is a proof *about an idealized object*. The translation from random oracle to concrete hash function remains heuristic, and uninstantiability results apply equally to checked and unchecked proofs. EasyCrypt verifies the *reduction*; it cannot certify the *instantiation*.

**Foundational gap.** Unlike CertiCrypt, whose logic is formalized inside Coq, EasyCrypt's program logics are *axiomatized* in the ambient logic — there is no machine-checked soundness proof of pRHL within the tool itself [1][8]. The trusted base includes the EasyCrypt kernel, the SMT solvers, and the axiomatization. This is a deliberate engineering trade-off, but it means EasyCrypt proofs are *high-assurance*, not *foundationally certified*.

**Adversary modeling.** Adversaries are abstract but *well-typed* modules with fixed oracle interfaces; covert channels, timing, and non-black-box behaviors (e.g., code introspection) are outside the model. Reductions are proved correct as *programs*, but the conversion from asymptotic claims to concrete advice (key sizes) still requires the analyst to instantiate the bounds.

**Proof brittleness and effort.** Game sequences remain a human creative act: choosing the hops, the couplings, and the bad events is where proofs fail, and EasyCrypt offers no automation for *discovering* a game sequence. A 1,400-line OAEP proof still represents weeks of expert effort, and small changes to game definitions can cascade through `equiv` lemmas.

**Scope.** pRHL is tailored to *relational* properties of probabilistic programs — precisely the shape of game-based cryptography. Non-relational or non-probabilistic arguments (e.g., some simulation-based UC proofs, information-theoretic lower bounds) fit less naturally, though extensions exist.

## 7. Conclusion

EasyCrypt demonstrates that the two great pen-and-paper disciplines of provable security — Bellare and Rogaway's code-based game playing [4][5] and Shoup's sequences of games [6] — are not merely amenable to mechanization but are *improved* by it. Probabilistic relational Hoare logic gives a single, precise judgment form to every game hop; couplings via probabilistic lifting turn "same coins" intuitions into checked inferences; and tactics like `fel` and `eager` package the methodology's most error-prone patterns into reusable, verified steps. The machine-checked IND-CCA security of RSA-OAEP [2] stands as the flagship evidence: a proof long enough to harbor human error, now checked end to end with a concrete, explicit bound.

The honest assessment is twofold. Mechanization dramatically raises the reliability of *reductions* — the syntactic, bookkeeping-heavy core of modern cryptography — while leaving the *modeling* choices (the ROM, the adversary interface, the hardness assumptions) exactly as contestable as before. That is not a weakness of the tool but a clarification of where cryptographic assurance actually lives: EasyCrypt guarantees that *if* the model is accepted, the proof is correct. For a field whose published proofs have repeatedly contained fixable but real errors in game bookkeeping, that guarantee is worth the price of admission.
