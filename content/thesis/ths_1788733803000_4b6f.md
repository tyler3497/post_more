# From Neyman–Pearson to Lipschitz: A Unified Theory of Certified Adversarial Robustness

## Abstract

Adversarial examples show that deep networks can be catastrophically misled by imperceptible perturbations, yet most defenses offer only *empirical* robustness — survival against known attacks — rather than provable guarantees. This thesis unifies the two dominant paradigms of certified adversarial robustness. First, randomized smoothing: convolving a base classifier with isotropic Gaussian noise yields a smoothed classifier whose prediction is provably stable inside an ℓ₂ ball whose radius depends only on the noise scale σ and the top-class probability p_A, with optimality established by a Neyman–Pearson likelihood-ratio argument. Second, deterministic Lipschitz guarantees: constraining the network's Lipschitz constant translates the logit margin at a point directly into a certified radius, yielding exact certificates from a single forward pass. We derive both certificate families from first principles, compare their tightness, scaling behavior on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and ImageNet, and analyze the accuracy–robustness tradeoff they share. Certification emerges not as a single technique but as a design discipline: choosing between probabilistic tightness and deterministic rigor depends on the threat model, deployment constraints, and how much accuracy an application can sacrifice for provable safety.

## 1 Introduction

The discovery of adversarial examples exposed a fracture in modern deep learning: classifiers that generalize well can fail with high confidence under invisible perturbations. The first responses — adversarial training, gradient masking, input preprocessing — measured *empirical* robustness against known attacks, a regime plagued by a recurring pathology: each defense fell to a subsequent adaptive attack. Empirical robustness is a lower bound on vulnerability and an upper bound on nothing; absence of a successful attack is not evidence of absence of adversarial examples.

Certified robustness inverts the quantifier. A classifier g is certifiably robust at x with radius ε if g(x + δ) = g(x) for *every* δ with ‖δ‖ ≤ ε. The certification procedure must produce a concrete radius ε(x) per input, backed by a mathematical argument. The fraction of test points both correctly classified and certified at radius ε — the certified accuracy, or verified-robust accuracy (VRA) — is the standard metric.

Two technique families dominate this program. **Randomized smoothing**, in its tight Gaussian form due to Cohen, Rosenfeld, and Kolter [1], converts any base classifier f into a smoothed classifier g(x) = argmax_c ℙ(f(x + ε) = c), ε ∼ 𝒩(0, σ²I), and proves a tight ℓ₂ radius via the Neyman–Pearson lemma. It remains the only certification approach demonstrated at ImageNet scale. **Deterministic Lipschitz certification** instead constrains the network's Lipschitz constant L, so that the margin between the top two logits translates into a certified radius, yielding exact guarantees without sampling [3, 4].

This thesis makes three contributions. First, it derives both families from first principles, exposing them as dual answers to one question: *how fast can a classifier's output change as its input moves?* Second, it compares them on theoretical tightness, computational cost, and certified accuracy across standard benchmarks. Third, it analyzes the accuracy–robustness tradeoff — the empirical and theoretical finding that certified radii cost clean accuracy — and argues that managing this tradeoff is the central design problem of the field.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The threat model

Consider a classifier f: ℝᵈ → {1, …, K} and input x with label y. An adversarial example at budget ε is x′ with ‖x′ − x‖_p ≤ ε and f(x′) ≠ y. For certification purposes the adversary is white-box, since certificates hold against *all* perturbations regardless of attacker knowledge. The ℓ∞ ball captures per-pixel intensity changes; the ℓ₂ ball captures bounded-energy perturbations. Randomized smoothing naturally certifies ℓ₂ robustness (Gaussian noise is rotation-invariant); Lipschitz methods extend to general p, with ℓ∞ requiring bounds on weight matrices' induced ∞-norms [4].

### 2.2 Empirical versus certified robustness

Empirical defenses optimize survival against a specific attack operator, typically projected gradient descent. Certified defenses establish a sound over-approximation of adversarial capability and prove invariance within it. The distinction matters wherever guarantees must hold against *future* attacks — autonomous driving, medical diagnosis, malware detection — because an uncertified robustness claim is unfalsifiable by any finite attack suite.

### 2.3 The taxonomy of certificates

Certification methods split along two axes. By *soundness mechanism*: complete verification (MILP, branch-and-bound) certifies arbitrary networks but scales poorly; incomplete methods (interval bound propagation, convex relaxations) scale better with looser bounds; and the two paradigms here *restructure the classifier itself* so certification becomes cheap. By *determinism*: randomized smoothing yields radii valid with high confidence over Monte Carlo estimation, while Lipschitz certificates are exact statements about the network. Both are certificates in the precise sense that a theorem, not an experiment, backs the radius.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Constructing the smoothed classifier

Randomized smoothing [1] starts from an arbitrary base classifier f and noise ε ∼ 𝒩(0, σ²I), defining the smoothed classifier by majority vote under noise:

```
g(x) = argmax_c P( f(x + ε) = c ),   ε ~ N(0, σ²I)
```

The noise scale σ is the central hyperparameter: larger σ yields larger certified radii but degrades clean accuracy, since f must classify correctly under heavier noise. In practice f is trained with Gaussian augmentation at the certification σ. Certification estimates p_A = ℙ(f(x + ε) = c_A) for the top class and an upper bound p_B on each runner-up probability via Monte Carlo sampling, using Clopper–Pearson intervals to obtain bounds p̲_A, p̄_B valid with confidence 1 − α. The certified radius is then:

```
R = (σ / 2) · ( Φ⁻¹(p̲_A) − Φ⁻¹(p̄_B) )
```

with Φ⁻¹ the inverse standard normal CDF; in the two-class margin form this simplifies to R = σ·Φ⁻¹(p̲_A). A reference implementation:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm, beta

def certified_radius(counts, n, sigma, alpha=0.001):
    """Cohen et al. (2019) radius from n Monte-Carlo samples."""
    top = counts.argmax()
    pA_lo = beta.ppf(alpha, counts[top], n - counts[top] + 1)
    pB_hi = beta.ppf(1 - alpha, counts[top] + 1, n - counts[top])
    if pA_lo <= 0.5:
        return 0.0, False          # abstain: cannot certify
    R = (sigma / 2.0) * (norm.ppf(pA_lo) - norm.ppf(pB_hi))
    return max(R, 0.0), True
```

The procedure abstains whenever p̲_A ≤ 1/2, and abstentions count against certified accuracy.

### 3.2 Bounding the Lipschitz constant

For deterministic certification, networks are trained with known or tightly bounded Lipschitz constants, and certification proceeds via the margin. If f: ℝᵈ → ℝᴷ is L-Lipschitz under ‖·‖_p, the logit margin M(x) = f_y(x) − max_{k≠y} f_k(x) guarantees stability for ‖δ‖_p < M(x)/(2L) (norm-dependent constants) [4]. Modern approaches enforce 1-Lipschitzness architecturally — orthogonal convolutions, spectral normalization, GroupSort activations, distance-to-manifold pooling — so the global constant is 1 by composition [6]. The GloRo construction [3] instead adds an explicit abstain class scoring the *largest achievable runner-up score under perturbation*, f_⊥(x) = max_{k≠y}(f_k(x) + ε√2·K_k), so the network refuses to predict when robustness cannot be certified, and certification costs a single forward pass.

### 3.3 Evaluation protocol

The standard protocol reports certified accuracy as a function of radius: for a grid of radii r, the fraction of test points correctly classified with ε(x) ≥ r. The resulting curve — or its area — summarizes a method. Key axes are dataset (MNIST, CIFAR-10, ImageNet), norm (ℓ₂ vs ℓ∞), the radius at reporting time, and compute budget (samples per input for smoothing; training cost for Lipschitz architectures).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The smoothed classifier and the geometry of the certificate

Randomized smoothing assumes almost nothing: the base classifier f may be any function — a ResNet, a decision tree, a lookup table — and the certificate still holds. Smoothing convolves the decision boundary into a soft object; g(x) is determined by the *mass* of the Gaussian around x in each class region. The radius R = σ·Φ⁻¹(p_A) (binary form) has a clean geometric reading: p_A measures how deeply x sits inside its decision region as seen through the noise, and σ converts that depth into Euclidean distance. A point with p_A = 0.99 under σ = 0.25 certifies radius 0.25 · Φ⁻¹(0.99) ≈ 0.58. The certified-accuracy-versus-radius curve is thus controlled by the single knob σ: choosing σ is choosing a point on the accuracy–robustness frontier.

The Monte Carlo machinery is where the "randomized" actually bites. The true probabilities are unknown; the certificate rests on binomial confidence bounds — Cohen et al. use n = 100,000 samples with α = 0.001 for ImageNet certification, expensive but parallelizable. The confidence statement is precise: with probability at least 1 − α *over the sampling randomness*, the reported radius is valid. Later work has trimmed sample complexity [8], but the fundamental cost — hundreds to thousands of forward passes per input — remains the method's principal operational drawback.

### 4.2 Neyman–Pearson: why the Gaussian certificate is optimal

The theoretical core is a worst-case question: given that 𝒩(x, σ²I) assigns mass p_A to the top class, what is the *smallest* mass the shifted distribution 𝒩(x + δ, σ²I) could assign to it? The adversary chooses the decision region subject to the mass constraint; the certificate must survive the worst choice. This is hypothesis testing in disguise, and its solution is the Neyman–Pearson lemma [1].

> **Theorem (Neyman–Pearson certificate, Cohen et al. 2019):** Let c_A satisfy p_A = ℙ_{ε∼𝒩(0,σ²I)}(f(x + ε) = c_A), and let p_B upper-bound every other class's probability. Then the smoothed classifier g satisfies g(x + δ) = c_A for all δ with ‖δ‖₂ ≤ R, where R = (σ/2)(Φ⁻¹(p_A) − Φ⁻¹(p_B)). Moreover, this radius is *tight*: some base classifier f attains it.

*Proof sketch.* Write X = 𝒩(x, σ²I) and Y = 𝒩(x + δ, σ²I). For any decision region S labeled c_A, the worst case minimizes ℙ(Y ∈ S) subject to ℙ(X ∈ S) = p_A. By Neyman–Pearson, the extremal regions are likelihood-ratio tests S_t = {z : dμ_Y/dμ_X(z) ≤ t}. For Gaussians the likelihood ratio is monotone in the projection of z onto δ, so S_t is a half-space orthogonal to δ, and the worst-case mass reduces to a one-dimensional tail computation: ℙ(Y ∈ S_t) = Φ(Φ⁻¹(p_A) − ‖δ‖₂/σ). The certificate holds while this worst-case mass exceeds the runner-up bound, giving R; the half-space is realizable as a classifier's decision region, proving tightness. ∎

Three consequences follow. First, **optimality**: no analysis using only (p_A, p_B, σ) can improve the bound — gains must come from richer statistics or different noise. Second, **noise generality**: the Neyman–Pearson template extends to other distributions — Laplace noise yields ℓ₁ certificates, and exponential-family noise gives a unified family; the Gaussian is distinguished by the tightest ℓ₂ bound and rotational symmetry matching the ℓ₂ threat. Third, **distributional robustness**: the certificate is really about distributional shift, bounding how class mass degrades as the noise distribution translates — linking smoothing to Wasserstein-robustness frameworks and to the differential-privacy ideas that partly inspired it.

### 4.3 Deterministic certificates via Lipschitz analysis

Lipschitz certification constrains classifier sensitivity at training time rather than smoothing at test time. A map f is L-Lipschitz under ‖·‖_p if ‖f(x) − f(x′)‖ ≤ L‖x − x′‖_p for all x, x′. The certified radius follows from elementary analysis:

> **Theorem (Lipschitz margin certificate):** Let f: ℝᵈ → ℝᴷ be L-Lipschitz under ‖·‖_p, with logit margin M(x) = f_y(x) − max_{k≠y} f_k(x) for the predicted class y. Then g(x) = argmax_k f_k(x) is constant on {x′ : ‖x′ − x‖_p < M(x)/(2L)} (up to norm-dependent constants).

*Proof sketch.* The margin can shrink by at most the Lipschitz variation of the two involved logits: M(x′) ≥ M(x) − 2L‖x − x′‖_p. If ‖x − x′‖_p < M(x)/(2L), then M(x′) > 0 and the argmax is unchanged. ∎

The engineering challenge is a *small, sound* L. Naive product-of-spectral-norms bounds are astronomically loose for deep networks, so the field builds networks that are 1-Lipschitz by construction: orthogonal or skew-orthogonal convolutions with norm-preserving Jacobians, GroupSort activations, ℓ₂-distance pooling [6]. A complementary line bounds *local* Lipschitz constants — sensitivity in a neighborhood of x — which can be dramatically smaller than global bounds and yield larger radii [5]. GloRo nets [3] take a third route: an instrumented abstain output makes training directly optimize verified-robust accuracy, with certification in one forward pass — orders of magnitude cheaper than smoothing's Monte Carlo sampling.

| Method | Norm | Mechanism | Certification cost | Representative VRA |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lipschitz Margin Training [4] | ℓ₂/ℓ∞ | Global Lipschitz + margin loss | 1 forward pass | MNIST ε=1.58: ~45% |
| GloRo Nets [3] | ℓ₂ | Abstain-class construction | 1 forward pass | MNIST ε=0.3: 95.7%; CIFAR-10 ε=0.141: 60.0% |
| 1-Lipschitz CNNs (SOC) [6] | ℓ₂ | Orthogonal layers by design | 1 forward pass | CIFAR-10: +3.8pp VRA over prior SOTA |
| Local Lipschitz bounds [5] | ℓ₂/ℓ∞ | Local sensitivity analysis | 1 pass + bound computation | Beats global-Lipschitz baselines |
| LiResNet recipe [7] | ℓ₂ | Capacity expansion under Lipschitz constraint | 1 forward pass | SOTA deterministic VRA, up to +8.5pp |

### 4.4 The accuracy–robustness tradeoff: theory and evidence

Both paradigms pay for certificates in clean accuracy, and this is no accident. Robust classification demands that decision boundaries keep distance from data points, forcing the classifier to discard fine-grained but fragile features. For smoothing the mechanism is explicit: σ blurs the input, and signal destroyed by noise is lost to classifier and adversary alike. For Lipschitz methods the mechanism is capacity restriction: a 1-Lipschitz network cannot represent rapidly varying logits, and natural classifiers appear to rely on exactly such variation. Hu et al. [7] document that Lipschitz-constrained architectures tend to *underfit*, and recovering performance requires careful capacity engineering plus data augmentation.

The tradeoff also has an information-theoretic basis: learning a robust classifier can require substantially more data than learning a merely accurate one — robustness is a strictly stronger statistical demand. Empirically, CIFAR-10 smoothed classifiers at σ = 0.5 achieve solid certified accuracy at radius 0.5 but lose 10+ points of clean accuracy versus standard training; GloRo nets reach 60% VRA at ε = 0.141 with clean accuracy well below unconstrained models' ~95% [3]. The honest reading: certification converts accuracy into a different currency — guaranteed safety — at an exchange rate set by the data distribution's intrinsic robustness.

### 4.5 Training for certificates: what actually improves the frontier

Since the certificate is only as good as the underlying classifier, both paradigms invest in training. For smoothing, Gaussian augmentation at the certification σ is standard, and adversarial training under noise pushes the frontier outward; denoised smoothing [2] prepends a denoiser to a *pretrained* classifier, certifying off-the-shelf models without retraining. For Lipschitz methods, the objective must balance the margin (which enlarges the radius) against the fixed Lipschitz bound: the GloRo loss directly maximizes the count of points both correct and certifiable. A recurring finding [6, 7] is that *capacity under constraint* is the binding limitation — the frontier moves when architectures grow more expressive at fixed Lipschitz constant, or base classifiers more accurate at fixed noise level, not when the certificate math changes. The Neyman–Pearson bound is already optimal given its inputs; the remaining gains are in the inputs.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

The key reported results: on ImageNet, smoothing is the only demonstrated approach — Cohen et al. [1] report a smoothed ResNet-50 at σ = 0.25 achieving 49% certified top-1 accuracy at ℓ₂ radius 0.5 (43% at radius 1.0 with σ = 0.5), as clean accuracy falls from 76% to roughly 65% and 55%. On CIFAR-10, smoothing at σ = 0.25 certifies 60%+ of test points at radius 0.25 with clean accuracy near 75%; deterministically, GloRo [3] achieves 60.0% VRA at ε = 0.141 with clean accuracy near 77%, and the LiResNet recipe [7] advances deterministic VRA by up to 8.5 percentage points architecturally. On MNIST both paradigms saturate — GloRo reaches 95.7% VRA at ε = 0.3 — leaving little room for the tradeoff to bite.

Beyond numbers, the field's real product is proof. The Neyman–Pearson argument (Section 4.2) is a *tightness* result: it closes the certificate problem for first-order smoothing statistics. The Lipschitz margin argument (Section 4.3) is a *soundness* result: it reduces certification to bounding a constant, which is why effort has flowed into tighter bound propagation and norm-preserving architectures [5, 6]. A third, often overlooked obligation is *statistical*: smoothing's Clopper–Pearson bounds must account for multiplicity across a test set, and the 1 − α confidence is per-input unless corrected. All three proof layers — optimal certificate, sound constant, valid statistics — are jointly necessary; failure at any one voids the guarantee.

## 6 Limitations

**The norm problem.** Certificates live in ℓ_p geometry, but human perceptual similarity is not an ℓ_p ball; a certified ℓ∞ radius of 8/255 says nothing about rotations, translations, or semantic manipulations. The threat model is mathematically precise and perceptually approximate. **Radius scale.** ImageNet certified radii (≈0.5–1.0 in ℓ₂) are small relative to realistic adversarial budgets, and deterministic methods have not scaled to ImageNet at competitive accuracy. **Computational cost.** Smoothing needs 10⁴–10⁵ forward passes per certified input [1] — parallelizable but prohibitive for latency-sensitive deployment — while deterministic single-pass certification [3] currently trades speed for lower certified accuracy on hard datasets. **The abstention loophole.** Both paradigms may decline to certify (smoothing via p̲_A ≤ 1/2, GloRo via its ⊥ class); a deployed system must still act on abstained inputs, and that fallback's safety lies outside the certificate. **Bound looseness.** Product-of-norms Lipschitz bounds remain vacuous for standard architectures, so deterministic certification is effectively restricted to specially designed networks — the certificate shapes the model class, not just the analysis. **Distribution shift.** Certificates are pointwise: they guarantee stability around a given x but say nothing far from the training distribution, where the smoothed vote may be confidently wrong.

## 7 Conclusion

Certified adversarial robustness has matured from a collection of tricks into a discipline with theorems at its center. Randomized smoothing showed that *any* classifier can be certified, at the price of sampling: the Neyman–Pearson lemma converts a probability estimate into an optimal ℓ₂ radius, scaling to ImageNet where nothing else does. Lipschitz analysis showed that *structure* can replace sampling: a bounded-sensitivity network certifies itself in a single forward pass, at the price of architectural constraint and — so far — of ImageNet-scale accuracy. Both pay the same tax, the accuracy–robustness tradeoff, because both demand slow-moving outputs, whether that slowness is enforced by noise or by design.

The open problems are clear. Can Neyman–Pearson tightness be extended with richer statistics — second-order information, adaptive or data-dependent smoothing — without exploding sample complexity? Can deterministic architectures close the ImageNet gap through local Lipschitz analysis [5] or capacity engineering [7]? And can the field move beyond ℓ_p balls toward perceptually meaningful threat models without losing the crispness that makes a certificate a certificate? Whatever the answers, the standard is set: a robustness claim that cannot be proved is, at best, a hypothesis. The future of trustworthy machine learning will be written in certificates — and the probabilistic and the deterministic are its founding documents.
