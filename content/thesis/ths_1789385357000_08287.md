# Peridynamics: A Nonlocal Reformulation of Continuum Mechanics for Fracture

## Abstract

Peridynamics replaces the partial differential equations of classical continuum mechanics with integro-differential equations of motion in which every material point interacts with neighbors inside a finite horizon. Because no spatial derivatives appear, the formulation remains valid across discontinuities, allowing crack initiation and propagation to emerge from the constitutive law itself rather than from separate fracture criteria. This article develops the theory from its bond-based origins to the state-based generalization, examines convergence of nonlocal operators to classical elasticity in the vanishing-horizon limit, and surveys optimization-based coupling strategies that embed nonlocal fracture regions inside efficient local finite element domains. We derive the equations of motion, compare ordinary and non-ordinary state-based constitutive models, formalize damage through critical bond stretch, and discuss numerical discretization, stability, and validation against canonical fracture experiments. Limitations including Poisson ratio constraints, zero-energy modes, surface effects, and computational cost are assessed, positioning peridynamics as a rigorous complement to local theories for problems where discontinuities are the central physics.

## 1 Intro

Classical continuum mechanics rests on the axiom that the internal force density at a material point is the divergence of a stress tensor, a construction that demands the displacement field be smooth enough for spatial derivatives to exist [1]. Fracture violates this axiom at its core: a crack is precisely a surface across which displacement jumps and the deformation gradient ceases to exist. The classical response has been to treat cracks as boundary conditions imposed on an otherwise smooth problem — Griffith's energy balance, cohesive-zone models with traction–separation laws, and enriched finite element methods such as XFEM that track discontinuities with level sets. Each inherits an uncomfortable asymmetry: a theory that cannot represent a crack is asked to predict where one will appear, how fast it will run, and when it will branch, typically by grafting on auxiliary criteria (maximum hoop stress, minimum strain-energy density, phase-field regularization) that live outside the constitutive law itself.

Peridynamics, introduced by Silling in 2000, dissolves this asymmetry by changing the mathematical primitive [1]. Instead of contact forces transmitted through a stress tensor, material points interact at a distance through *bonds*, and the equation of motion is an integral over a neighborhood rather than a differential operator. Because integration does not require continuity, the same equation governs intact material and material traversed by cracks; fracture becomes something the constitutive law *does*, not an exception to it. A bond carries force as a function of the relative displacement of its endpoints; when a bond's stretch exceeds a critical value, the bond breaks irreversibly. Nucleation, propagation, branching, and coalescence then emerge from the dynamics with no crack-tracking algorithm, no enrichment functions, and no remeshing. What follows develops the theory from its bond-based origins through state-based generalizations to its coupling with classical elasticity, with attention to the analysis that makes the framework rigorous rather than merely convenient.

## 2 Background

The peridynamic equation of motion for a body reads

ρ(x) u_tt(x,t) = ∫ f(u(x′,t) − u(x,t), x′ − x) dx′ + b(x,t),

where the integral runs over the *horizon* H_x = { x′ : ‖x′ − x‖ ≤ δ }, δ is the nonlocal length scale, f is the pairwise force density, and b is a prescribed body force [1][3]. The horizon is the theory's single most consequential parameter: it sets the range of interaction, regularizes the otherwise singular crack-tip fields, and provides the length scale against which convergence to classical elasticity is measured.

In the original *bond-based* formulation, f depends only on the deformation of the single bond connecting x and x′. The prototype microelastic brittle (PMB) material posits a pairwise force directed along the deformed bond with magnitude c·s·μ, where s is the bond stretch, c is the micromodulus, and μ is a history-dependent scalar equal to 1 for intact bonds and 0 once s exceeds the critical stretch s_0. Calibration against classical elasticity under homogeneous deformation ties the micromodulus to the elastic moduli — in three dimensions, c = 18K/(πδ^4) with K the bulk modulus — while the critical stretch s_0 = √(5G_0/(9Kδ)) ties bond failure to the critical energy-release rate G_0 [1][7]. Remarkably, this sparse constitutive structure reproduces nucleation and growth of cracks with no additional machinery.

The bond-based theory's elegance carries a famous restriction: because every bond responds independently and centrally, the implied Poisson ratio is fixed — 1/4 in three dimensions and plane strain, 1/3 in plane stress — and plastic incompressibility cannot be represented [1][2]. This is not a numerical artifact but a theorem about pairwise central forces. The *state-based* reformulation of 2007 removes the restriction by introducing peridynamic *states*: mathematical objects that map bonds to quantities such as force vectors, with the force in any single bond allowed to depend on the collective deformation of *all* bonds in the horizon [2]. Bond-based peridynamics is then recovered as the special case in which the force state depends on each bond in isolation. State-based models split into *ordinary* formulations, in which forces remain aligned with deformed bonds, and *non-ordinary* formulations, in which a nonlocal deformation gradient is constructed and classical constitutive laws are imported wholesale through a correspondence principle [2][3].

## 3 Methodology

A peridynamic analysis proceeds through discretization of the body into material points with associated volumes, evaluation of the integral operator by quadrature over each point's family of neighbors within δ, a constitutive update that may irreversibly break bonds, and explicit or implicit time integration. The canonical meshfree discretization assigns each node x_i a volume V_i and approximates the internal force density by a sum over neighbors; the result is a system of ordinary differential equations in time, integrated most commonly with velocity Verlet or central differences, since the method's fracture applications are overwhelmingly dynamic [3][7]. Damage is tracked per bond through the history variable μ, and the scalar damage field φ(x,t) = 1 − (∫ μ dV′)/(∫ dV′) visualizes crack surfaces as regions where φ approaches unity [7].

Constitutive modeling in the state-based setting follows one of two routes. Ordinary state-based models define the force state directly as a nonlocal function of the deformation state — for instance, splitting the extension state into isotropic and deviatoric parts and assigning them separate moduli, which immediately frees the Poisson ratio [2]. Non-ordinary state-based *correspondence* models instead compute a nonlocal shape tensor and a nonlocal deformation gradient F from the deformation state; the first Piola–Kirchhoff stress P from any classical constitutive law then generates the force state. This correspondence is the theory's great practical bridge: it lets decades of calibrated classical material models run inside nonlocal, fracture-capable kinematics [2][3].

Numerically, three concerns dominate. First, the horizon δ and the nodal spacing Δx are not independent: converged results require the ratio m = δ/Δx to be held fixed (typically between 3 and 5) while both tend to zero, a coupled limit sometimes called *m-convergence* [6]. Second, nonlocal boundary conditions cannot be imposed as tractions on a surface; they must be prescribed as *volume constraints* over a collar of thickness δ, which complicates the treatment of free surfaces and motivates coupling to local models [5]. Third, explicit dynamics is conditionally stable with a critical time step scaling with √(ρ/(cδ)) in the bond-based setting, so the horizon directly taxes the allowable step [3][7].

```python
import numpy as np

def internal_force(x, u, neighbors, volumes, c, s0, mu):
    """Bond-based peridynamic internal force with brittle bond failure."""
    f = np.zeros_like(u)
    for i, xi in enumerate(x):
        for j in neighbors[i]:
            xi_vec = x[j] - xi
            eta = u[j] - u[i]
            L0 = np.linalg.norm(xi_vec)
            deformed = xi_vec + eta
            s = (np.linalg.norm(deformed) - L0) / L0
            if s > s0:
                mu[i, j] = 0.0  # irreversible bond rupture
            if mu[i, j] > 0.0:
                direction = deformed / np.linalg.norm(deformed)
                f[i] += c * s * mu[i, j] * direction * volumes[j]
    return f
```

fn build_families(positions: &[Vec3], delta: f64) -> Vec<Vec<usize>> {
    let n = positions.len();
    let mut families: Vec<Vec<usize>> = vec![Vec::new(); n];
    for i in 0..n {
        for j in 0..n {
            if i != j && (positions[j] - positions[i]).norm() <= delta {
                families[i].push(j);
            }
        }
    }

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Bond-Based Formulation and Its Kinematic Restrictions

Bond-based peridynamics is the theory in its most austere form: the force between two points depends only on the stretch of the bond joining them, directed along the deformed bond. The strain-energy density is then a sum over independent pairwise potentials, and the entire constitutive content of the model is a single scalar function of bond stretch [1]. The celebrated consequence is the Poisson-ratio theorem: for an isotropic bond-based solid, ν is locked to 1/4 in 3D (and plane strain) and 1/3 in plane stress, because pairwise central potentials cannot distinguish volumetric from deviatoric deformation. Despite this, bond-based models account for the large majority of peridynamic fracture simulations in the literature: the formulation is simple to implement, unconditionally stable under the usual explicit step restriction, and — crucially — its fracture predictions are excellent, since crack growth in brittle materials is dominated by bond rupture rather than by the precise value of ν [7]. The restriction bites hardest in confined plasticity and in nearly incompressible materials, which is where state-based models take over.

| Feature | Bond-based PD | Ordinary state-based PD | Non-ordinary (correspondence) PD |
|---|---|---|---|
| Force depends on | Single bond only | All bonds in horizon | Nonlocal deformation gradient |
| Poisson ratio | Fixed (1/4 in 3D) | Arbitrary | Arbitrary |
| Classical laws reusable | No | Partially | Yes, directly |
| Zero-energy modes | No | No | Yes, unless stabilized |
| Cost per step | Low | Moderate | High |
| Fracture handling | Native bond rupture | Native bond rupture | Via damage on bonds or states |

### 4.2 State-Based Formulations: Ordinary, Non-Ordinary, and Correspondence Models

The 2007 state-based reformulation introduces the *deformation state* Y, which assigns to each bond ξ the deformed image vector, and the *force state* T, which assigns to each bond the force-density vector acting on it [2]. Balance of linear and angular momentum then constrains the admissible force states: the integral of T over the horizon gives the internal force density, and the integral of Y × T must vanish. In *ordinary* state-based models, T remains parallel to the deformed bond direction, and the constitutive law is written directly on scalar states (extension, dilatation), permitting arbitrary elastic constants while preserving the bond-rupture fracture mechanism [2].

*Non-ordinary* correspondence models go further: they construct a nonlocal deformation gradient F and shape tensor K by weighted least-squares fits over the horizon, then evaluate a classical stress P(F) and map it back to a force state T = ω P K⁻¹ ξ with influence function ω [2][3]. The price of this generality is the well-known *zero-energy mode* pathology: non-uniform deformation states can produce the same F and hence zero force, manifesting as spurious oscillations. Stabilization strategies include the bond-associated deformation gradient, which computes F per bond from the intersection of neighboring horizons rather than from a single nodal average [3].

### 4.3 Fracture as Emergent Bond Rupture

In peridynamics, fracture requires no separate criterion because the failure criterion is already inside the constitutive law. Each bond carries a critical stretch s_0 calibrated to the macroscopic critical energy-release rate G_0; when s exceeds s_0 the bond breaks and never heals [1][7]. A crack is therefore nothing more than a surface across which enough bonds have failed that the damage field φ saturates — and because the equation of motion never differentiates the displacement, it applies identically on and off the crack surface.

This emergence reproduces phenomena that are notoriously hard for local theories:

- **Spontaneous nucleation**: cracks initiate where the stress state first drives bonds past s_0, with no seed flaw or nucleation criterion required.
- **Branching**: dynamic cracks in the PMB model branch when the crack-tip speed approaches a material-dependent fraction of the Rayleigh wave speed, matching the experimentally observed limiting-velocity behavior.
- **Coalescence and fragmentation**: multiple cracks interact through the shared nonlocal force field, merging or shielding without any topological bookkeeping.

The calibration chain from G_0 to s_0 is what makes these predictions quantitative rather than merely qualitative: equating the work to break all bonds across a unit crack area to G_0 fixes s_0 in terms of measurable fracture toughness [1].

### 4.4 The Vanishing-Horizon Limit: Convergence to Classical Elasticity

Peridynamics is a genuine generalization of classical elasticity, not a competing theory, and the precise statement is convergence of the nonlocal operators to the local Navier operator as δ → 0. For homogeneous isotropic media with sufficient regularity, both the bond-based and state-based linear peridynamic operators converge to their classical counterparts — in L∞ for the bond-based operator and in H⁻¹ for the state-based operator [4]. The analysis is more delicate for heterogeneous media: when material properties vary smoothly, convergence persists, but at a sharp material interface the local limit of the peridynamic force *diverges*, because the interface breaks the symmetry of the nonlocal operator and the nonlocal tractions on the two sides mismatch [4].

> **Theorem (Vanishing-horizon convergence).** Let L^δ be the linear peridynamic operator (bond-based or state-based) on a domain with sufficiently regular coefficients. Then L^δ v → N v as δ → 0, where N is the classical linear elasticity operator, in the appropriate function space. At a discontinuity of the material coefficients, the limit diverges unless nonlocal interface conditions are imposed whose local limit recovers the classical traction-continuity conditions [4].

This result has a practical corollary: δ is not a free numerical knob but a material length scale, and simulations that claim mesh independence must demonstrate convergence under the coupled δ → 0, Δx → 0 limit with m = δ/Δx fixed [6].

### 4.5 Coupling Nonlocal Fracture Regions to Classical Elasticity

Nonlocal models are expensive — every point talks to every neighbor in its horizon — while classical finite elements are cheap and mature. The pragmatic resolution is *coupling*: confine peridynamics to the subdomain where fracture may occur and use classical elasticity elsewhere, joining the two across an interface [5]. Several strategies exist:

1. **Blending / Arlequin methods**: the two models coexist in an overlap region with a partition-of-unity weight morphing the energy from local to nonlocal.
2. **Force-based coupling**: peridynamic forces are applied as loads to the local model (or vice versa) across a sharp interface, with ghost-force corrections.
3. **Optimization-based coupling**: the coupling is formulated as a control problem in which the states are the PD and classical solutions, the objective minimizes their mismatch on an overlap of the domains, and the controls are virtual volume constraints and boundary data at the local–nonlocal interface [5].

The optimization approach is attractive because it simultaneously solves the *nonlocal boundary-condition problem*: local traction data prescribed on an outer surface is transmitted into the nonlocal bulk through the optimized volume constraints, and the method demonstrates numerical convergence on realistic three-dimensional geometries [5]. Coupling therefore serves two masters at once — computational economy and the correct imposition of boundary data on nonlocal models.

## 5 Theoretical Guarantees and Empirical Validation

Beyond the convergence theorems, the theory carries well-posedness results for the linear bond-based and state-based equations of motion, and stability analyses of the meshfree discretizations that pin down the conditional stability of explicit integration [3][6]. On the empirical side, peridynamic simulations have been validated against a battery of canonical fracture experiments:

> **Theorem (Stability of explicit bond-based dynamics).** The central-difference discretization of the bond-based peridynamic equation of motion is stable provided the time step satisfies Δt < √(2ρ / (c · Σ V_j)) summed over each family, i.e., the step resolves the stiffest bond in the discretization [3].

| Benchmark | Setup | Peridynamic result | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kalthoff–Winkler impact | Prenotched plate, impact loading | Crack propagates at ~70° to the notch, matching experiment | [7] |
| Mode-I tension | Plate with central crack | Load–displacement and G_0-calibrated growth match Griffith prediction | [1] |
| Dynamic branching | High-rate plate impact | Branching at limiting crack speed, angle consistent with tests | [7] |
| Vanishing-horizon patch test | Smooth manufactured solution | O(δ) convergence to classical elasticity | [4] |
| PD–FEM coupling | 3D notched specimen | < 2% mismatch in overlap region vs. full PD | [5] |

Nodal finite element discretizations of peridynamics — Galerkin projections of the nonlocal weak form onto standard FE spaces — have further closed the gap between peridynamic theory and mainstream computational mechanics, with proven convergence in the joint horizon–mesh limit [6]. These results collectively move peridynamics from a plausible idea to a calibrated predictive tool: the same model that provably recovers classical elasticity away from cracks quantitatively reproduces crack paths, speeds, and branching angles in the laboratory.

## 6 Limitations

The framework's limitations are as instructive as its strengths. **Constitutive restriction**: bond-based models fix the Poisson ratio and exclude plastic incompressibility; state-based models lift this at the cost of complexity [2]. **Zero-energy modes**: correspondence models admit spurious deformation modes that require stabilization, and every stabilization reintroduces a length scale or parameter to calibrate [3]. **Surface effects**: points within δ of a free surface have truncated horizons and hence softer response, producing a boundary layer of artificial compliance unless corrected by surface-adjustment factors or coupling [5]. **Boundary conditions**: tractions cannot be applied on surfaces, only as volume constraints over a δ-collar, which is unnatural for engineering loads and contact [5]. **Calibration ambiguity**: the horizon δ is simultaneously a numerical parameter and a material length scale; treating it as purely numerical invites mesh dependence, while treating it as purely material demands experimental identification that is rarely available. **Cost**: nonlocality makes assembly and force evaluation asymptotically more expensive than local FEM, with neighbor counts growing as m³ in 3D, so large-scale 3D fracture remains computationally demanding despite coupling and parallelization [6][7]. **Ductile fracture**: while brittle bond rupture is mature, ductile tearing with large plastic flow, void nucleation, and triaxiality dependence is still an active research frontier with no consensus formulation.

## 7 Conclusion

Peridynamics reframes fracture not as the breakdown of continuum mechanics but as its natural nonlocal extension. By replacing differential operators with integrals over a finite horizon, the theory keeps a single equation of motion valid across discontinuities; by encoding failure in bond rupture calibrated to fracture toughness, it lets cracks nucleate, propagate, branch, and coalesce as emergent dynamics. The bond-based formulation offers unmatched simplicity for brittle fracture at the price of a fixed Poisson ratio, while state-based and correspondence models recover full constitutive generality — with zero-energy modes and surface effects as the toll. Vanishing-horizon analysis proves the theory collapses onto classical elasticity where it should [4], and optimization-based coupling confines the nonlocal expense to where fracture actually happens while correctly transmitting boundary data [5]. The remaining challenges — ductile failure, horizon calibration, and the cost of three-dimensional nonlocality — define the research frontier. For problems in which discontinuities are not an afterthought but the central physics, peridynamics has earned its place as the rigorous nonlocal complement to local continuum theory.
