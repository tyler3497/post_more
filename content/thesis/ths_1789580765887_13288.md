# Diffeomorphic Density Estimation with Normalizing Flows: From Affine Coupling Layers to Continuous-Time Dynamics

## Abstract

Normalizing flows model complex probability densities by transporting a tractable base distribution through a learnable diffeomorphism, preserving exact likelihood evaluation through the change-of-variables formula. This article develops the theory of diffeomorphic density estimation across three architectural generations: affine coupling layers introduced by NICE and RealNVP, autoregressive flows including masked autoregressive flow and inverse autoregressive flow, and continuous-time flows defined by neural ordinary differential equations. We derive the triangular Jacobian structure that makes each family tractable, analyze the density-evaluation versus sampling asymmetry separating the two autoregressive directions, prove that coupling layers are special cases of autoregressive transformations, and present the instantaneous change-of-variables formula together with Hutchinson's trace estimator that enables free-form architectures in FFJORD. We discuss universality results, topological obstructions inherent to diffeomorphisms, and empirical benchmarks, concluding with open directions in stochastic and manifold-valued flows.

---

##1 Intro

Unsupervised density estimation asks a deceptively simple question: given samples from an unknown distribution, can we learn a model that both *evaluates* the likelihood of new points and *generates* fresh samples from the learned distribution? Most deep generative families answer only half of this question. Variational autoencoders maximize a lower bound on the likelihood rather than the likelihood itself, while generative adversarial networks abandon likelihood evaluation altogether. Normalizing flows occupy the distinctive position of providing *exact* likelihoods together with *exact* sampling, at the cost of architectural constraints that make these two operations simultaneously tractable [8].

The central object is a *diffeomorphism*: a bijective, differentiable map with a differentiable inverse. Starting from a simple base density, typically an isotropic Gaussian, a flow pushes probability mass forward through a parameterized diffeomorphism to sculpt an arbitrarily complex target density. Because the transformation is invertible, the probability density at any data point can be recovered exactly by pulling the point back to the base space and correcting for the local change in volume. This correction is the Jacobian determinant, and the entire field of normalizing flows can be read as a prolonged engineering effort to make that determinant cheap.

This article traces that effort through three architectural generations. First, the *coupling layers* of NICE and RealNVP split the variable into two partitions, transforming one conditioned on the other so that the Jacobian becomes triangular [1][2]. Second, *autoregressive flows* generalize coupling by giving every coordinate its own conditioner over all preceding coordinates, yielding masked autoregressive flow and inverse autoregressive flow, which trade off the cost of density evaluation against the cost of sampling [3][4]. Third, *continuous normalizing flows* replace discrete layers with a neural ordinary differential equation, where the determinant collapses into a trace and Hutchinson's estimator liberates the architecture entirely [6][7]. Along the way we prove the structural relationships between these families, derive their computational profiles, and confront the fundamental limitations that no architectural cleverness can remove.

##2 Background

A diffeomorphism f from a latent space Z to a data space X induces a density on X from any density on Z through the classical change-of-variables theorem:

> **Theorem (change of variables).** Let f : Z → X be a continuously differentiable bijection with differentiable inverse, and let p_Z be a density on Z. Then the pushforward density p_X on X satisfies p_X(x) = p_Z(f^{-1}(x)) · |det Df^{-1}(x)|, where Df^{-1}(x) is the Jacobian of the inverse map at x.

Equivalently, in the generative direction with x = f(z), the density reads p_X(x) = p_Z(z) · |det Df(z)|^{-1}. The absolute Jacobian determinant measures local volume expansion: where the map stretches space, density dilutes; where it compresses, density concentrates. Probability mass is conserved, and the total integrates to one exactly [8].

A normalizing flow composes K such maps, f = f_K ∘ ··· ∘ f_1, and the log-determinants accumulate additively:

log p_X(x) = log p_Z(z) + Σ_k log |det Df_k^{-1}(h_{k-1})|

with h_0 = x and h_K = z. Training is maximum likelihood: minimize the negative log-likelihood over a dataset, backpropagating through the inverse maps and their log-determinants.

The entire difficulty is computational. A generic neural network layer has a dense Jacobian whose determinant costs O(D³) in the dimension D, which is hopeless for images or high-dimensional tabular data. Two escape routes have been discovered:

1. **Structured Jacobians.** Design layers whose Jacobians are triangular (or block-triangular), so the determinant reduces to the product of diagonal entries in O(D) time. Coupling and autoregressive layers both exploit this route.
2. **Continuous-time dynamics.** Replace discrete layers by an ordinary differential equation, where the instantaneous change in log-density depends only on the *trace* of the Jacobian, an O(D) quantity. Hutchinson's stochastic estimator then removes even the trace's residual cost.

A useful terminological anchor: the *forward* (generative) direction maps z → x for sampling, while the *inverse* (inference) direction maps x → z for density evaluation. Architectures differ in which direction is cheap, and that asymmetry shapes everything about how they are trained and deployed.

##3 Methodology

The flow construction recipe has four ingredients, shared across all generations:

- **Base distribution.** Almost always a standard Gaussian or logistic, chosen for trivial sampling and closed-form density. Its role is to supply randomness; all structure is created by the map.
- **Invertible layers.** A sequence of parameterized diffeomorphisms f_1, …, f_K, each with a tractable inverse and tractable log-determinant. Depth compensates for the restricted functional form of each layer.
- **Permutation or mixing.** Because structured layers leave part of the coordinates untouched, successive layers must permute, reverse, or linearly mix coordinates so that every dimension is eventually transformed.
- **Maximum-likelihood training.** The objective −E[log p_X(x)] is optimized by stochastic gradient descent, with gradients flowing through both the inverse maps and the log-determinant terms.

The computational profile of a flow family is summarized by three costs: density evaluation (the inverse pass plus log-determinant), sampling (the forward pass), and the log-determinant itself. The following table organizes the families studied here.

| Family | Density evaluation | Sampling | Jacobian structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| RealNVP [2] | O(D), single pass | O(D), single pass | Block triangular |
| MAF [4] | O(D), single pass | O(D), D sequential steps | Lower triangular |
| IAF [3] | O(D), D sequential steps | O(D), single pass | Lower triangular |
| Glow [5] | O(D), single pass | O(D), single pass | Block triangular |
| CNF / FFJORD [7] | O(D), solver-dependent | O(D), solver-dependent | Free-form (trace) |

Two observations structure the rest of this article. First, *coupling layers* are the unique family in which both directions are parallel and cheap, which explains their dominance in image generation [2][5]. Second, the two *autoregressive* directions are mirror images: MAF is fast at density estimation and slow at sampling, while IAF is fast at sampling and slow at density estimation. This mirror symmetry is not an accident but a theorem about inverting autoregressive structure [3][4].

A practical note on discrete data: flows are defined on continuous spaces, so modeling images or other ordinal data requires *dequantization*, adding uniform noise to discrete values to create a continuous density. The choice of dequantization scheme measurably affects reported likelihoods and must be held fixed when comparing models [5].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Affine Coupling Layers: NICE and RealNVP

The coupling layer partitions the input vector into two parts. The first part passes through unchanged and serves as the *conditioner*; the second part is transformed by an elementwise operation whose parameters are computed from the first. NICE uses a purely additive transformation, y_b = x_b + t(x_a), whose Jacobian is unitriangular with determinant exactly one — the transformation is volume-preserving, which limits expressivity but makes the likelihood trivial [1]. RealNVP generalizes this to an *affine* coupling, y_b = x_b ⊙ exp(s(x_a)) + t(x_a), where s and t are arbitrary neural networks. The scale term allows the layer to expand and contract volume, hence the name *non-volume-preserving* [2].

The Jacobian of an affine coupling layer has a revealing block structure: the identity on the unchanged partition, zeros in the upper-right block (the unchanged part does not depend on the transformed part), and a diagonal matrix diag(exp(s)) on the transformed partition. Triangularity gives the log-determinant immediately:

log |det Df| = Σ_i s_i(x_a)

the sum running over the transformed coordinates only. Inversion is equally cheap: recompute s and t from the unchanged partition, which is available in both directions, then invert the elementwise affine map. A minimal implementation looks like this:

```python
class AffineCoupling(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, hidden, dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.d = d  # size of the unchanged partition
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(d, hidden), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden, hidden), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden, 2 * (dim - d)))  # emits s and t

    def forward(self, x):
        x_a, x_b = x[:, :self.d], x[:, self.d:]
        s, t = self.net(x_a).chunk(2, dim=1)
        y_a = x_a
        y_b = x_b * torch.exp(s) + t
        log_det = s.sum(dim=1)
        return torch.cat([y_a, y_b], dim=1), log_det

    def inverse(self, y):
        y_a, y_b = y[:, :self.d], y[:, self.d:]
        s, t = self.net(y_a).chunk(2, dim=1)  # same net, unchanged input
        x_b = (y_b - t) * torch.exp(-s)
        return torch.cat([y_a, x_b], dim=1)
```

Because each layer transforms only half the coordinates, layers are stacked with alternating masks, channel permutations, or learned mixing so that every dimension is transformed many times. The conditioner networks need not be invertible — only the elementwise coupling operation must be — which is the key design freedom that lets s and t be deep convolutional networks for image data [2].

### 4.2 Autoregressive Flows: MAF and IAF

Autoregressive flows remove the two-partition restriction: every coordinate x_i is transformed conditioned on all preceding coordinates x_{<i}. With an affine elementwise transformation, the masked autoregressive flow (MAF) defines z_i = (x_i − μ_i(x_{<i})) / σ_i(x_{<i}), where the conditioner networks μ_i and σ_i are evaluated for all i in parallel using masked weight matrices in the style of MADE. The Jacobian is lower triangular with diagonal entries 1/σ_i, so the log-determinant is −Σ_i log σ_i(x_{<i}), computable in a single forward pass [4]. Density evaluation is therefore fast, but sampling requires ancestral generation: x_1 must be sampled before μ_2 and σ_2 can be computed, then x_2 before x_3, for D strictly sequential steps.

Inverse autoregressive flow (IAF) runs the same autoregressive structure in the opposite direction: x_i = μ_i(z_{<i}) + σ_i(z_{<i}) · z_i. Now sampling is parallel — given a base sample z, all conditioners evaluate at once — while density evaluation becomes sequential, since recovering z_i requires the preceding x values [3]. This mirror asymmetry is fundamental:

- **MAF**: parallel density evaluation, sequential sampling. Ideal for density estimation benchmarks.
- **IAF**: sequential density evaluation, parallel sampling. Ideal as a flexible posterior in variational autoencoders, where sampling dominates [3].

The two families are more closely related to coupling layers than they first appear:

> **Theorem (coupling as autoregressive).** An affine coupling layer is a special case of both the MAF transformation and the IAF transformation. It is recovered from MAF by setting the shift and log-scale to zero on the unchanged partition and restricting the conditioners of the transformed partition to depend only on the unchanged coordinates; the analogous restriction with conditioning on the base variables recovers it from IAF [4].

In other words, coupling layers trade the full autoregressive dependency for a coarser two-block dependency, gaining single-pass sampling in exchange for reduced flexibility per layer — a deficit repaid by stacking many layers with permuted partitions.

### 4.3 Glow: Invertible 1×1 Convolutions and Multi-Scale Structure

Glow retains the affine coupling layer as its workhorse but replaces the fixed channel permutation with a *learned* mixing operation: an invertible 1×1 convolution, which is simply a matrix multiplication along the channel axis of an image tensor. Parameterized through an LU decomposition W = PLU, the log-determinant of this mixing is Σ log |diag(U)|, computable in O(C) for C channels, while inversion is a single matrix solve [5]. Each Glow step therefore consists of three sub-layers: activation normalization (a data-initialized per-channel affine transform, stabilizing training), the invertible 1×1 convolution, and an affine coupling layer.

A second innovation is the *multi-scale* architecture. After several steps, the tensor is squeezed (trading spatial resolution for channels) and half the channels are factored out as latent variables, with the remainder continuing to deeper scales. This has two benefits: it reduces the computational burden at coarser scales, and it builds a hierarchical latent representation in which early factored-out variables capture fine detail and deeper variables capture global structure. The log-determinant accumulates across scales without any sequential dependency, so both sampling and density evaluation remain fully parallel — the property that made Glow the first flow to generate high-resolution faces competitive with GAN samples while reporting state-of-the-art likelihoods, including 3.35 bits per dimension on CIFAR-10 against RealNVP's 3.49 [2][5].

The triangular log-determinant pattern recurs in a compact form worth isolating:

```rust
/// Log-determinant of any triangular Jacobian: sum of log diagonal magnitudes.
fn log_det_triangular(diag: &[f64]) -> f64 {
    diag.iter().map(|d| d.abs().ln()).sum()
}

/// A Glow-style step accumulates actnorm, 1x1 conv (LU), and coupling terms.
fn step_log_det(actnorm_log_s: f64, lu_diag: &[f64], coupling_s: &[f64]) -> f64 {
    actnorm_log_s + log_det_triangular(lu_diag) + coupling_s.iter().sum::<f64>()
}
```

### 4.4 Continuous Normalizing Flows: Neural ODEs and FFJORD

Continuous normalizing flows abandon discrete layers entirely. A neural network f_θ parameterizes a vector field, and the transformation is defined by integrating the ordinary differential equation dz/dt = f_θ(z(t), t) from t_0 to t_1. Because ODE solutions are invertible by integrating backward in time, the map is a diffeomorphism for any sufficiently regular f_θ, with no partitioning, masking, or triangularity required [6].

The change-of-variables formula takes an instantaneous form. As probability mass flows along the vector field, the log-density evolves according to the divergence of the field:

> **Proposition (instantaneous change of variables).** If z(t) evolves under dz/dt = f(z(t), t), then d/dt log p(z(t)) = −Tr(∂f/∂z), the negative trace of the Jacobian of the dynamics [6].

Integrating from t_0 to t_1 gives log p(z(t_1)) = log p(z(t_0)) − ∫ Tr(∂f/∂z) dt. The determinant has been replaced by a trace — still O(D²) naively, but a single scalar summary rather than a full matrix factorization, and crucially one that imposes *no structural constraint* on f_θ.

FFJORD removes the remaining bottleneck with Hutchinson's trace estimator: Tr(J) = E[εᵀ J ε] for noise vectors ε with zero mean and identity covariance. The vector-Jacobian product εᵀJ is computed by reverse-mode automatic differentiation in O(D) time, yielding an *unbiased* stochastic estimate of the exact log-likelihood with completely unrestricted architectures — the first flow family to combine free-form networks with exact likelihoods and one-pass sampling [7]. Gradients flow through the ODE solver via the adjoint sensitivity method, which solves a second augmented ODE backward in time with O(1) memory instead of backpropagating through solver steps [6].

The price is numerical: adaptive solvers choose their own number of function evaluations, so training cost is data-dependent and can grow as dynamics stiffen; the likelihood is exact only up to solver tolerance; and the Hutchinson estimator, while unbiased, injects variance into the gradients. In practice these costs are manageable, and FFJORD achieved state-of-the-art likelihoods among exact methods on tabular benchmarks at publication [7].

##5 Empirical/Proofs

Empirical validation of flows follows two tracks: low-dimensional toy densities that expose qualitative behavior, and standardized likelihood benchmarks that permit quantitative comparison.

On two-dimensional problems — mixtures of Gaussians, concentric rings, two-moon and checkerboard densities — flows trained by maximum likelihood recover the target geometry faithfully, and the learned diffeomorphism can be visualized directly as a warping of a grid. These visualizations confirm the theory: coupling stacks progressively fold the base Gaussian into multimodal targets, while continuous flows transport particles along smooth learned trajectories [2][7].

The standard tabular benchmark suite (POWER, GAS, HEPMASS, MINIBOONE, BSDS300) reports test log-likelihood in nats. Representative results illustrate the autoregressive advantage in pure density estimation:

| Dataset | MAF [4] | FFJORD [7] |
|---|---|---|
| POWER | 0.24 | 0.46 |
| GAS | 10.08 | 8.59 |
| HEPMASS | −20.07 | −14.92 |
| MINIBOONE | −11.68 | −10.43 |

Higher is better. MAF's fully autoregressive conditioners dominate these benchmarks, while FFJORD's free-form continuous dynamics remain competitive despite using unrestricted architectures — evidence that the trace-based formulation does not sacrifice statistical efficiency for architectural freedom [4][7]. On images, the coupling family leads: RealNVP reached 3.49 bits per dimension on CIFAR-10 [2], improved to 3.35 by Glow's learned 1×1 mixing and multi-scale structure [5].

On the theoretical side, the expressivity of flows rests on universality results. Autoregressive flows with sufficiently flexible one-dimensional conditionals are universal approximators of continuous densities, since each conditional can model an arbitrary univariate distribution given its predecessors [4][8]. For coupling layers, universality requires more care — a single coupling layer cannot mix partitions — but stacks of coupling layers with permutations are universal under mild conditions, a result surveyed alongside the volume-preserving limitations of NICE in the review literature [8]. The proof of the coupling-as-autoregressive theorem in Section 4.2 is constructive: zeroing conditioners on the passive partition and restricting the active partition's dependencies exhibits the coupling layer as a masked special case, which immediately transfers any universality result for autoregressive flows to sufficiently deep coupling stacks.

##6 Limitations

Three classes of limitation constrain normalizing flows, and they differ in how fundamental they are.

1. **Topological obstructions.** A diffeomorphism is a homeomorphism: it preserves the number of connected components, holes, and other topological invariants. A Gaussian base is simply connected with a single component, so a flow cannot assign mass to a genuinely disconnected target without stretching thin bridges of near-zero density between the modes. In practice this manifests as density leaking between well-separated modes and as numerical strain when the target has holes the base lacks. No deeper stack removes this; it is a property of the model class itself [8].
2. **Discrete and manifold data.** Flows live on Euclidean space. Discrete data requires dequantization, which introduces an arbitrary modeling choice that pollutes likelihood comparisons, and the flow wastes capacity modeling the noise distribution [5]. Data on manifolds — rotations, graphs, sets — needs manifold-aware constructions, an active research area reviewed in [8].
3. **Computational trade-offs.** Each generation buys expressivity at a price. Coupling layers need many stacked permutations to mix coordinates fully. Autoregressive flows pay with sequential passes in one direction. Continuous flows pay with adaptive ODE solves whose cost grows with dynamical complexity, plus gradient variance from Hutchinson's estimator and likelihood error from solver tolerance [6][7]. Glow's 1×1 convolution mixes only channels, not spatial positions, leaving spatial mixing to the squeeze operation and coupling conditioners.

Additional practical concerns include training instability when scale networks output large values (the exponential amplifies them), mitigated by careful initialization and activation normalization [5], and the memory cost of the adjoint method's backward solve, which re-integrates the dynamics and can itself become stiff.

---

##7 Conclusion

Normalizing flows derive their power from a single idea pursued with increasing generality: make the Jacobian determinant cheap, and exact likelihood follows. Coupling layers achieved this through triangular structure and the freedom to use non-invertible conditioner networks [1][2]; autoregressive flows extended the triangular idea to full sequential conditioning, exposing a fundamental mirror symmetry between density evaluation and sampling [3][4]; Glow showed that learned mixing and multi-scale factoring could scale the coupling paradigm to high-resolution images [5]; and continuous flows dissolved the structural constraints altogether, replacing the determinant with a trace and the architecture with a vector field [6][7].

The trajectory points toward ever freer parameterizations: stochastic differential equations that generalize the ODE formulation, Riemannian flows for manifold-valued data, and simulation-based methods that sidestep the invertibility requirement while retaining tractable inference. Yet the topological lesson endures — a diffeomorphism can reshape a density arbitrarily within its topological class and not at all outside it. Choosing the base distribution and the geometry of the transformation therefore remains, fundamentally, a modeling decision that no amount of depth can automate away.
