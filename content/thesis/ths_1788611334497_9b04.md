# LSM-Tree Compaction Theory: Leveled, Tiered, and Universal Strategies, Amplification Analysis via the Monkey Cost Model, and Blob Separation at Hundred-Terabyte Scale

## Abstract

The Log-Structured Merge-tree (LSM-tree) is the dominant storage architecture for write-intensive key-value systems, yet its performance is governed almost entirely by one background process: *compaction*. This thesis presents a unified theoretical treatment of LSM-tree compaction strategies — leveled, size-tiered (tiered), universal, FIFO, and the Dostoevsky-derived lazy-leveling hybrid — and derives their write amplification, read amplification, and space amplification bounds under the Monkey cost model of Sarkar et al. [2]. We analyze how RocksDB's dynamic leveled compaction (`level_compaction_dynamic_level_bytes`) collapses the static size-tier target problem, how BlobDB's key-value separation rewrites the amplification equations for large-value workloads, and how space-time tradeoffs behave at 100 TB scale on NVMe, where device bandwidth, write endurance, and tail-latency constraints dominate design decisions. Through the Monkey framework we show that the per-level Bloom filter false-positive allocation forms a geometric series whose sum converges independently of tree depth, yielding lookup cost that is asymptotically independent of the number of levels. We formalize the write-amplification lower bounds for each merge policy, derive the Pareto-optimal frontier between write and read amplification, and validate the theory against production parameters from the RocksDB wiki [3][4] and a 2024 survey of LSM-based storage systems [5].

## 1. Introduction

Few data structures have shaped modern storage engineering as profoundly as the Log-Structured Merge-tree. Introduced by O'Neil, Cheng, Gawlick, and O'Neil in 1996 [1], the LSM-tree was conceived for transaction-processing workloads in which index inserts dominated: the classic analysis showed that B-trees pay roughly two random I/Os per insert on large tables — one to read the target leaf page and one to write it back — because leaf pages are referenced too infrequently to remain buffer-resident. The LSM-tree amortizes these costs by staging inserts in a memory-resident component (*C₀*, the *memtable*) and cascading them through one or more disk-resident components (*C₁ … Cₖ*, the *SSTables*) in large sequential multi-page merge steps [1].

Thirty years later, the LSM-tree underpins RocksDB, LevelDB, Cassandra, ScyllaDB, TiKV, CockroachDB, and Apache AsterixDB [5]. Yet the fundamental tension identified in 1996 persists: every design decision that accelerates writes — batching, deferred merging, tiered policies — imposes costs on reads and space, and vice versa. The process that negotiates this tension is **compaction**: the background merge of immutable SSTable files that reclaims space, drops tombstoned entries, and maintains the level invariants.

This thesis makes four contributions. First, it derives closed-form write-amplification (WA), read-amplification (RA), and space-amplification (SA) bounds for leveled, tiered, universal, and lazy-leveling merge policies as functions of the size ratio *T* and depth *L*. Second, it applies the Monkey cost model [2] to show how non-uniform Bloom filter allocation provably reduces asymptotic worst-case lookup I/O cost relative to state-of-the-art uniform allocation. Third, it analyzes RocksDB's dynamic leveled compaction and BlobDB key-value separation as practical realizations of the theory. Fourth, it quantifies the space-time tradeoffs at 100 TB on NVMe, where 7 GB/s sequential bandwidth, ~600K random 4K IOPS, and finite program-erase (P/E) cycle endurance create a qualitatively different operating regime from the spinning-disk world of 1996.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The LSM-tree cascade

An LSM-tree organizes data into a hierarchy of exponentially growing levels. New writes land in the memtable; when full, it is flushed to L0 as an immutable sorted run. A background compaction process then merges overlapping SSTables from level *Lᵢ* into *Lᵢ₊₁*. Let the *size ratio* between adjacent levels be *T* (conventionally 10 in RocksDB [3]) and let the tree hold *N* entries of total size *S*. The number of levels is then *L ≈ log_T(S/M)*, where *M* is the memtable/flush size [2].

> **Definition 1 (Merge policy).** A *merge policy* determines, for each level *Lᵢ*, how SSTables within that level are merged into *Lᵢ₊₁*. In a **leveled** policy, each level holds exactly one sorted run; compaction merges one *Lᵢ* SSTable with all overlapping *Lᵢ₊₁* files, restoring the single-run invariant. In a **tiered** (size-tiered) policy, each level holds up to *T* overlapping sorted runs; compaction merges all runs at *Lᵢ* into a single new run at *Lᵢ₊₁*.

### 2.2 Amplification metrics

- **Write amplification (WA):** total bytes written to storage per byte of user data written. Every compaction rewrites data, so WA directly determines SSD endurance consumption.
- **Read amplification (RA):** I/Os performed per user read, over and above the ideal single I/O.
- **Space amplification (SA):** ratio of physical storage used to logical user data size, capturing garbage (obsolete versions, tombstones) awaiting compaction.

These three metrics are the axes of the LSM design space, and no policy simultaneously minimizes all three [2][5].

### 2.3 The Monkey insight

Sarkar, Franklin, Chavan, and Idreos [2] showed that LSM-tree key-value stores exhibit an *intrinsic* tradeoff between lookup cost, update cost, and main memory footprint — but that all existing systems expose a *suboptimal* tradeoff because they co-tune the merge policy, buffer size, and Bloom filter false positive rates poorly. The key analytical insight: worst-case lookup cost is proportional to the *sum of Bloom filter false positive rates* across levels, and by allocating filter memory non-uniformly — more bits per entry to *smaller, hotter* levels — this sum forms a geometric series converging to a constant independent of the number of levels. Empirically, Monkey reduced lookup latency by 50–80% on a LevelDB implementation as data volume grew [2].

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in three layers:

1. **Analytical:** we derive WA, RA, and SA bounds for each merge policy using a level-counting argument parameterized by size ratio *T*, tree depth *L*, and per-level run count *Kᵢ*. Proofs use the standard potential-function method: each entry's "merge depth" equals the number of times it participates in a compaction before reaching the bottom level.
2. **Cost-model:** we instantiate the Monkey framework, computing expected point-lookup I/O as *R = Σᵢ fᵢ · cᵢ* where *fᵢ* is the false positive rate of level *i*'s Bloom filter and *cᵢ* the I/O cost of probing it, and we optimize the per-level bits-per-key allocation under a fixed memory budget.
3. **Systems:** we map the theory onto RocksDB's implementation parameters from the official wiki [3][4] — `max_bytes_for_level_base`, `max_bytes_for_level_multiplier`, `level_compaction_dynamic_level_bytes`, `CompactionOptionsUniversal` — and extrapolate to a 100 TB NVMe deployment using published device characteristics.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Leveled compaction: the read-optimized extreme

In leveled compaction, the single-run-per-level invariant means a point lookup probes at most one SSTable per level: RA = *O(L)* SSTable probes in the worst case, reduced to near-*O(1)* with Bloom filters and fence pointers [2][3]. Space amplification is tightly bounded: because each level's size is at most *1/T* of the level below, total garbage is bounded by roughly *1 + 1/T + 1/T² + … ≈ T/(T−1)*, i.e., ~1.11 for *T = 10* [4].

The price is write amplification. Consider an entry inserted at L0: it is rewritten approximately *T/2* times per level crossing on average (each compaction at *Lᵢ* rewrites, on average, half the overlapping *Lᵢ₊₁* data plus the entry itself), yielding:

> **Theorem 1 (Leveled WA).** For size ratio *T* and depth *L*, leveled compaction has write amplification *WA_leveled ≈ 1 + L·T/2*.

*Proof sketch.* An entry participates in one compaction per level. At level *i*, the compaction that moves it down merges one *Lᵢ* SSTable (size *s*) with overlapping *Lᵢ₊₁* files of average total size *(T−1)/2·s* under a uniform key distribution; hence each crossing rewrites *(T+1)/2 ≈ T/2* times the entry's size. Summing over *L* levels and adding the initial flush gives *1 + L·T/2*. ∎

For *T = 10* and *L = 5* (roughly 100 GB with a 256 MB base), WA ≈ 26× — consistent with the RocksDB wiki's reported 10–30× in production [3]. The handbook's comparative table confirms leveled compaction as the read-heavy, space-tight choice for SSDs [6].

RocksDB refines leveled compaction with **compaction picking**: each level receives a score — total level size divided by target size (for L0, file count divided by `level0_file_num_compaction_trigger`, or size over `max_bytes_for_level_base`, whichever is larger) — and the highest-score level compacts first [3]. **Subcompaction** parallelizes a single compaction across key-range partitions using multiple threads [3].

### 4.2 Size-tiered compaction: the write-optimized extreme

Tiered compaction relaxes the single-run invariant: each level accumulates up to *T* overlapping runs, and compaction merges all runs at *Lᵢ* into one run at *Lᵢ₊₁* without touching existing *Lᵢ₊₁* data. Each entry is rewritten only once per level crossing:

> **Theorem 2 (Tiered WA).** *WA_tiered ≈ L* — a factor *T/2* lower than leveled.

The cost moves to reads: a point lookup may probe up to *T* runs per level, so unfiltered RA = *O(T·L)*, and space amplification can reach ~2× because an entire level of obsolete runs may linger before a major compaction [6]. This is Cassandra's STCS default — optimal for write-heavy burst workloads where SSD endurance and ingest throughput dominate [5][6].

### 4.3 Universal compaction: bounded tiering

RocksDB's **universal compaction** is a bounded variant of tiered compaction: runs are merged when their count or total size crosses configurable thresholds (`CompactionOptionsUniversal`), and `max_size_amplification_percent` caps space amplification [4]. Universal achieves tiered-like WA ≈ *O(L)* while bounding SA — the pragmatic middle ground for write-heavy workloads with a space cap [6]. It is also the only compaction style supporting `allow_ingest_behind` for bulk file ingestion [3].

### 4.4 The Monkey cost model and optimal filter allocation

The deepest theoretical result we import is Monkey's filter-allocation theorem. Let level *i* have false positive rate *fᵢ* and probing cost *cᵢ* (I/Os). Expected point-lookup cost is:

```
R = Σᵢ₌₁ᴸ fᵢ · cᵢ
```

State-of-the-art systems assign a fixed number of bits per entry to *all* levels, giving uniform *fᵢ = f*. Monkey instead allocates *mᵢ* bits per entry to level *i*, with *fᵢ ≈ 0.6185^(mᵢ)* for a standard Bloom filter. Minimizing *R* subject to *Σ mᵢ·nᵢ ≤ M_filter* (fixed memory budget, *nᵢ* entries at level *i*) via Lagrange multipliers yields *fᵢ* in geometric progression: *fᵢ₊₁ = fᵢ / T* roughly — smaller (hotter) levels get dramatically lower FPRs [2].

> **Theorem 3 (Monkey, Sarkar et al. [2]).** Under optimal non-uniform filter allocation, worst-case lookup I/O cost is *O(1)* in the number of levels — the geometric series Σ *fᵢ* converges to a constant — whereas uniform allocation yields *O(L)* growth. The optimal allocation simultaneously removes the contention between filter memory and buffer memory, because lookup cost becomes independent of *L*.

Practically, Monkey's LevelDB implementation cut lookup latency 50–80% as data volume grew [2]. The theorem also explains *why* the size ratio *T = 10* is near-optimal: it balances the *T/2* write-amplification penalty of leveling against the geometric convergence rate of the filter series.

A companion line of work — **Dostoevsky** (Dayan & Idreos, 2018) — generalizes merge policies into a continuous design space ("lazy leveling": leveled at the largest level, tiered above), navigable at runtime; it is realized in ScyllaDB's Unified Compaction Strategy and informs RocksDB's dynamic level sizing [6].

### 4.5 RocksDB dynamic leveled compaction

Static leveled compaction has a subtle pathology: on a partially filled database, upper levels sit empty while compactions still push data through all *L* nominal levels, paying full *L·T/2* write amplification for a fraction of the data. RocksDB's `level_compaction_dynamic_level_bytes` (default `true` since v8.4 [3]) sizes levels **bottom-up**: the bottommost level holds the actual data, and each level above targets *1/T* of the level below. Level target sizes thus track the real dataset rather than fixed `max_bytes_for_level_base` multiples:

```
Target(Ln+1) = Target(Ln) × max_bytes_for_level_multiplier   (static)
Target(Ln)   = size(bottommost) / T^(L−n)                      (dynamic)
```

Production reports credit dynamic sizing with 20–40% write-amplification reduction on partially filled databases while bounding space amplification at ≈1.111 [6]. The RocksDB wiki documents the scoring, file picking, and the `CompactRange()` behavior changes that accompany it [3].

### 4.6 BlobDB: key-value separation rewrites the equations

For large values, compaction rewrites the *values* along with the keys — pure waste, since values are never used in merge comparisons. **WiscKey** (Lu et al., 2017) introduced key-value separation: keys and small metadata stay in the LSM-tree; values go to append-only value logs (vLogs), with only pointers compacted [5]. **BlobDB**, RocksDB's production implementation, stores values in blob files and keeps only keys in SSTables.

The amplification analysis transforms. Let average value size be *V* and key+pointer size be *K ≪ V*. Compaction WA now applies only to *K*-sized entries:

> **Theorem 4 (KV-separation WA).** With key-value separation, *WA_total ≈ WA_keys · (K/(K+V)) + 1*, where the *+1* is the single sequential write of the value to the blob log. As *V/K → ∞*, *WA_total → 1*.

For 1 KB values with 32-byte keys, compaction WA on a leveled tree drops from ~26× to ~1.8× [6]. The costs: point lookups pay one extra random read to fetch the value, range scans lose value locality, and a **garbage collector** must reclaim blob space from overwritten/deleted values — reintroducing a form of space amplification governed by GC policy rather than compaction [5][6].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Amplification comparison table

The following table consolidates the derived bounds for *T = 10*, *L = 6* (≈100 TB with a 1 GB memtable budget chain), the standard operating point for the 100 TB analysis in §5.2:

| Policy | Write amp | Read I/O (no filter) | Read I/O (Monkey-optimal) | Space amp | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leveled | *1 + L·T/2* ≈ 31× | *O(L)* = 6 | *O(1)* ≈ 1.2 | ≈1.11 | Read-heavy, space-tight, NVMe |
| Tiered (STCS) | *L* ≈ 6× | *O(T·L)* = 60 | *O(T)* ≈ 12 | ≤2.0 | Write-heavy bursts |
| Universal | *O(L)* ≈ 6–8× | *O(#runs)* | *O(#runs)* | bounded via config | Write-heavy, space-capped |
| Lazy leveling | *O(L+T)* ≈ 16× | *O(T+L)* ≈ 16 | *O(1)*–*O(T)* | ≈1.11 | Mixed / unknown workloads |
| Leveled + BlobDB | ≈1.8× (1 KB vals) | *O(L)* + 1 random | *O(1)* + 1 random | 1.11 + blob GC | Large values (>1 KB) |
| FIFO | ≈1× | *O(#segments)* | n/a | ≈1 | TTL metrics, drop-oldest |

Write amplification figures are validated against RocksDB production guidance (10–30× leveled at 100 GB–1 TB scales [3]) and scale linearly with *L*, matching Theorem 1.

### 5.2 The 100 TB NVMe regime

At *S = 100 TB* with *T = 10* and a 1 GB flush size, *L = log₁₀(10¹⁴/10⁹) = 5* levels below L0 — six total. Consider a representative NVMe device: 7 GB/s sequential write, 600K random 4K read IOPS, and 1 DWPD (drive writes per day) endurance on a 30 TB drive, i.e., 30 TB/day of sustainable writes.

- **Leveled, WA ≈ 31×:** a 1 GB/s user ingest rate becomes 31 GB/s of device writes — exceeding a single device's bandwidth and burning 2.7 PB/day of endurance, ~90× the 1 DWPD budget. Leveled compaction at 100 TB is therefore **bandwidth- and endurance-infeasible** for write-heavy workloads on a small device count; it requires either massive device parallelism or BlobDB separation.
- **Tiered/universal, WA ≈ 6×:** 1 GB/s ingest → 6 GB/s device writes, ~0.5 PB/day — feasible on a handful of devices. This is why write-heavy 100 TB deployments (Cassandra, ScyllaDB STCS/ICS) default to tiered families [5][6].
- **Leveled + BlobDB, WA ≈ 1.8×:** keys compact at 31× but keys are ~3% of bytes; total device writes ≈ 1.9 GB/s per 1 GB/s ingest — the read-optimized layout becomes viable again. The tradeoff is GC: blob GC reclaims space by rewriting live values, and its cost is governed by the *garbage ratio* — at 50% garbage, GC adds ~1× write amp, degrading gracefully [5].
- **Read side:** with Monkey-optimal filters, leveled point-lookup cost is ~1.2 I/Os regardless of *L* (Theorem 3) — at 600K IOPS, a single device sustains ~500K point lookups/s. Tiered without optimal filtering pays up to 60 probes; even at 600K IOPS, tail latency collapses under queueing. **The Monkey allocation is not optional at 100 TB — it is the difference between a viable and a non-viable read path for tiered policies.**

> **Theorem 5 (100 TB feasibility).** For sustained ingest *W* bytes/s on devices with sequential bandwidth *B* and endurance *E* bytes/day, a compaction policy is feasible iff *WA·W ≤ B* and *WA·W·86400 ≤ E*. At *W = 1 GB/s*, *B = 7 GB/s*, *E = 30 TB/day*, leveled (*WA = 31*) violates both; tiered/universal (*WA ≤ 8*) and BlobDB-separated leveled (*WA ≈ 2*) satisfy both.

### 5.3 Proof: geometric convergence of optimal filter FPRs

We sketch Monkey's core proof [2]. Minimize *R = Σ fᵢcᵢ* subject to *Σ mᵢnᵢ = M*, *fᵢ = α^(mᵢ)* with *α ≈ 0.6185*. The Lagrangian *ℒ = Σ α^(mᵢ)cᵢ + λ(Σ mᵢnᵢ − M)* gives first-order condition *α^(mᵢ)·ln(α)·cᵢ + λnᵢ = 0*, i.e., *fᵢ ∝ nᵢ/cᵢ*. Since *nᵢ₊₁ ≈ T·nᵢ* and *cᵢ* is roughly constant across levels, *fᵢ₊₁ ≈ fᵢ/T*. Then *R = f₁·Σᵢ cᵢT^(−(i−1)) ≤ f₁·c_max·T/(T−1) = O(1)* in *L*. ∎

Contrast with uniform *mᵢ = m*: *R = f·Σcᵢ = O(L)*. The gap grows without bound as data (and *L*) grows — exactly the 50–80% latency reduction Monkey measured empirically [2].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Uniform-key-distribution assumption.** Theorems 1–2 assume uniformly distributed keys so that overlapping *Lᵢ₊₁* data averages *(T−1)/2·s*. Skewed workloads (hot key ranges) compact smaller overlaps and see lower realized WA; adversarial sequential inserts can see higher.
2. **Steady-state analysis.** The bounds describe a full tree in steady state. Real deployments oscillate: post-bulk-load, L0 file-count triggers (`level0_file_num_compaction_trigger`, slowdown/stop write triggers [3]) create transient write stalls not captured here.
3. **GC excluded from tiered bounds.** BlobDB/WiscKey WA bounds assume efficient blob GC; pathological update patterns (100% overwrite of large values) can push GC-induced WA above the LSM WA it replaced [5].
4. **NVMe parallelism idealized.** Theorem 5 treats device bandwidth as a scalar; real NVMe performance depends on queue depth, I/O size mix, and internal GC, which compactions perturb via write-clustering effects.
5. **Monkey memory model.** The *O(1)* lookup result assumes the filter memory budget is fixed while data grows; in practice, operators often scale filter memory with data, changing the constants (though not the asymptotics).

## 7. Conclusion

Compaction is the mechanism by which LSM-trees convert sequential write bandwidth into every other performance property, and the choice of merge policy is the choice of *which* amplification to pay. Leveled compaction buys *O(1)*-with-filters read amplification and 1.11× space amplification at the cost of *O(L·T)* write amplification; tiered compaction inverts the trade at *O(L)* writes and *O(T·L)* reads; universal compaction bounds the tiered space blowup; lazy leveling (Dostoevsky) traces the Pareto frontier between them [2][5][6]. The Monkey cost model proves that the read side of this tradeoff is, to first order, a *filter allocation problem* with a closed-form optimal solution whose cost converges independently of tree depth [2]. RocksDB's dynamic leveled compaction and BlobDB show how the theory is operationalized: bottom-up level sizing removes the partially-filled-DB write penalty, and key-value separation removes values from the amplification equations entirely [3][4]. At 100 TB on NVMe, the feasibility inequalities of Theorem 5 are unforgiving — only tiered-family policies or BlobDB-separated leveling survive a 1 GB/s ingest budget — making compaction theory not an academic exercise but the central capacity-planning discipline of modern storage engineering.
