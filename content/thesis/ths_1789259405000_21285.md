# Quantum Error Mitigation in the NISQ Era: Zero-Noise Extrapolation, Probabilistic Error Cancellation, and the Sampling-Overhead Frontier

## Abstract

Quantum error mitigation (QEM) has emerged as the principal strategy for extracting accurate expectation values from noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices without the prohibitive qubit overheads of fault-tolerant quantum error correction. This thesis surveys the theory, algorithms, and fundamental limits of QEM, centering on the two canonical families introduced by Temme, Bravyi, and Gambetta: zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE), which amplifies circuit noise and extrapolates measured expectation values to the zero-noise limit, and probabilistic error cancellation (PEC), which inverts well-characterized noise channels through quasi-probability decompositions over noisy basis operations [1][2]. We examine learning-based protocols such as Clifford data regression that train on classically simulable circuits [3], characterize noise-amplification and inversion protocols within the Pauli–Lindblad framework scaled to hundred-qubit processors [4], and confront the universal exponential sampling-overhead lower bounds that circumscribe every mitigation protocol [5][6]. Finally, we analyze the landmark 127-qubit kicked-Ising experiment that demonstrated quantum utility before fault tolerance [7], and delineate the precise boundary separating mitigation from correction.

---

## 1 Introduction

John Preskill's 2018 framing of the noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era [8] named the regime in which quantum computing currently lives: processors with tens to hundreds of physical qubits, two-qubit gate infidelities in the 10⁻³–10⁻² range, coherence times measured in microseconds, and — crucially — no capacity for fault-tolerant quantum error correction. In this regime, every additional gate layer multiplies the corruption of the prepared state, and the expectation values ⟨O⟩ = Tr[Oρ] that variational algorithms and quantum simulations depend on decay toward uninformative noise floors exponentially fast in the *circuit fault rate* λ, the expected number of faults per circuit execution.

Two philosophies contend for the future of reliable quantum computation. Quantum error *correction* (QEC) encodes logical qubits redundantly and suppresses errors below a fault-tolerance threshold, at the price of enormous qubit overheads that remain out of reach. Quantum error *mitigation* (QEM) takes the opposite bargain: it accepts the noisy hardware as-is and recovers accurate expectation values through modified circuit executions and classical post-processing, with zero additional qubits. The price is paid instead in *sampling overhead* — the multiplicative factor by which the number of circuit executions must grow — and in residual bias that no finite sample budget can fully erase.

This thesis presents a unified account of the field. We develop the noise models and statistical framework (§2–§3), dissect the principal mitigation families — zero-noise extrapolation, probabilistic error cancellation, learning-based regression, and passive techniques (§4) — survey the empirical record including the landmark 127-qubit utility demonstration (§5), and confront the fundamental limits that bound every mitigation protocol, discovered or not (§6).

## 2 Background

### The noise model

A noisy gate is a completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) map ℰ acting on density matrices. For the weakly correlated, approximately Markovian noise of superconducting and trapped-ion platforms, two descriptions dominate. The *Pauli channel* expands the noise in the Pauli basis,

ℰ(ρ) = Σ_{P ∈ {I,X,Y,Z}^{⊗n}} p_P P ρ P,  Σ p_P = 1,

which includes depolarizing noise (p_P uniform over non-identity Paulis) and dephasing as special cases. Continuous-time decoherence is captured by the Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad (GKSL) master equation,

dρ/dt = −i[H, ρ] + Σ_k (L_k ρ L_k† − ½{L_k†L_k, ρ}),

with jump operators L_k encoding amplitude damping (T₁) and pure dephasing (T₂) processes. For a circuit of depth d on n qubits with per-gate error rate p, the expected number of faults λ ≈ ndp/2 (counting only gates in the observable's causal *light cone*) is the single parameter controlling mitigation cost: almost every overhead in this thesis scales as exp(O(λ)).

### The estimation problem

Most near-term algorithms — the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE), the quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA), Trotterized dynamics — reduce to estimating ⟨O⟩ = Tr[O U|0⟩⟨0|U†] for some observable O and unitary circuit U. Hardware returns instead ⟨O⟩_λ, the expectation under the noisy implementation. Two error sources afflict the estimate: *statistical* shot noise, scaling as σ/√N for N shots, and *systematic* bias ⟨O⟩_λ − ⟨O⟩_ideal, which mitigation targets.

Following the framework of Takagi *et al.* [5], a mitigation protocol (1) synthesizes N distorted states {ℰ_n(ψ)} on NISQ hardware, (2) applies a physical process 𝒫 — possibly entangling multiple copies — and (3) outputs a random variable E_A approximating the observable A. Protocol quality is captured by two numbers: the *bias* b_A(ψ) = 𝔼[E_A] − ⟨A⟩ and the *spread* Δe_A, the range of values E_A can take. Spread controls cost: by Hoeffding's inequality, estimating ⟨A⟩ to accuracy ε with confidence 1−δ requires M = O(Δe_A² log(1/δ)/ε²) samples. The sampling overhead is this M normalized by the unmitigated shot count.

> **Theorem** (Bias–variance decomposition): For any estimator Ô of ⟨O⟩, the mean-squared error decomposes as MSE = Bias(Ô)² + Var(Ô). Mitigation reduces the first term at the expense of inflating the second; the art of the field is choosing the exchange rate.

Mitigation and correction differ categorically:

|  | Error correction | Error mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Qubit overhead | Large (10³–10⁴ physical per logical) | None |
| Bias | Arbitrarily suppressible below threshold | Residual; protocol-dependent |
| Cost currency | Qubits and code distance | Samples: exp(O(λ)) |
| Output | Corrected quantum state | Corrected classical expectation |

## 3 Methodology

Every mitigation protocol in this thesis follows a common skeleton: **characterize** the noise, **transform** the experiment (amplify noise, randomize circuits, or gather training data), **execute** an ensemble of modified circuits, and **post-process** the measurement record into a debiased estimate.

**Noise amplification.** ZNE requires evaluating ⟨O⟩ at elevated noise strengths λ > 1. *Unitary folding* replaces each gate layer U by U(U†U)^k, ideally the identity in the noiseless limit, scaling the effective noise by odd integers λ = 2k+1 without changing the ideal circuit [1]. Alternatives include *pulse stretching* (lengthening control pulses to increase decoherence per gate) and *identity insertion* (splicing calibrated noisy identities) [2].

**Quasi-probability inversion.** PEC decomposes the ideal channel as a signed linear combination of implementable noisy operations, 𝒰 = Σ_n q_n ℬ_n, with possibly negative coefficients q_n. Defining γ = Σ_n |q_n| ≥ 1 and sampling ℬ_n with probability |q_n|/γ while multiplying outcomes by sign(q_n)·γ yields an unbiased estimator whose variance is inflated by γ². Per-gate factors multiply: γ_total = Π_i γ_i = exp(O(λ)).

**Learning from simulable circuits.** Instead of modeling noise analytically, learning-based methods execute *training circuits* whose ideal expectation values are classically computable — typically near-Clifford circuits simulable via the Gottesman–Knill theorem — and fit a map from noisy to noiseless outcomes [3].

**Readout correction.** State-preparation-and-measurement (SPAM) errors are handled by calibrating a confusion matrix M with M_{xy} = P(measure x | prepared y) and applying (regularized) inversion to the observed distribution — cheap, but limited by calibration drift and exponential matrix size.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Zero-Noise Extrapolation: Cancelling Noise Order by Order

ZNE is the oldest and most widely deployed mitigation technique, introduced alongside PEC by Temme, Bravyi, and Gambetta [1]. The premise is analytic: under weak noise, the noisy expectation value admits a perturbative expansion in the noise strength,

⟨O⟩(λ) = ⟨O⟩* + a₁λ + a₂λ² + … + a_nλⁿ + R_{n+1}(λ),

where ⟨O⟩* is the zero-noise value. Measuring ⟨O⟩ at scaled noise strengths λ = c₀ < c₁ < … < c_n and applying Richardson's deferred approach to the limit — solving the Vandermonde system for the constant term — cancels the first n orders of the noise perturbation. Temme *et al.* bound the remainder by |R_{n+1}| ≤ ‖𝒜‖ℓ^{n+1}T^{n+1}/(n+1)! and analyze the standard rescaling sequences: exponential (Bulirsch–Stoer, c_j = h^j c₀) and harmonic (c_j = (j+q)c₀) progressions [1].

In practice, polynomial Richardson fits are numerically fragile: the extrapolation coefficients grow combinatorially, amplifying shot noise. Modern practice favors *exponential* fits ⟨O⟩(λ) ≈ A + B e^{−γλ}, motivated by the observation that depolarizing-like noise damps Pauli expectations multiplicatively, or multi-exponential and adaptive ansätze selected by cross-validation. The price of extrapolation is variance: if the estimator is Σ_k b_k ⟨O⟩(c_k), its variance scales as Σ_k b_k² σ²/N_k, and Σ|b_k| grows rapidly with order — the first face of the exponential frontier.

```python
import numpy as np

def zne_richardson(lambdas, means, order):
    """Richardson extrapolation of <O>(lambda) to the zero-noise limit.
    Solves the Vandermonde system for the constant term of the fit."""
    A = np.vander(lambdas, N=order + 1, increasing=True)
    coeffs = np.linalg.solve(A, means)
    return coeffs[0]

# Synthetic data: exponential decay toward a noise floor
lam = np.array([1.0, 3.0, 5.0])
obs = 0.8 * np.exp(-0.35 * lam) + 0.05
print("Richardson estimate:", zne_richardson(lam, obs, 2))
print("Exact zero-noise value:", 0.85)
```

Digital ZNE variants fold at the gate level inside the compiled circuit, and shot budgets are optimally distributed across noise scales by importance sampling proportional to |b_k|.

### 4.2 Probabilistic Error Cancellation: Unbiased at Exponential Price

Where ZNE is biased but cheap, PEC is unbiased but expensive. Given a tomographic model of each noisy gate ℰ_i = 𝒩_i ∘ 𝒰_i, one constructs the formal inverse 𝒩_i^{−1} = Σ_n q^{(i)}_n ℬ^{(i)}_n as a quasi-probability decomposition over noisy basis operations and Monte-Carlo samples the resulting ensemble [1][2]. The estimator is exactly unbiased — Bias = 0 for generic circuits — and its sampling overhead is γ_total² with γ_total = Π_i Σ_n |q^{(i)}_n|.

> **Theorem** (PEC optimality [5]): Among all unbiased single-copy, non-adaptive mitigation protocols for local dephasing noise, probabilistic error cancellation achieves the minimum possible estimator spread. No unbiased protocol in this class can beat PEC's sampling cost.
>
> **Proof sketch:** Takagi *et al.* lower-bound the spread Δe_A for any (Q=1, K=1) protocol with fixed bias via a semidefinite program over feasible estimator ranges; for local dephasing the bound is saturated by the PEC construction, establishing optimality [5].

The historical bottleneck was *learning* the noise model: full process tomography scales exponentially, and naive PEC ignored the correlated, crosstalk-dominated errors of real processors. The breakthrough of van den Berg *et al.* [4] was the *sparse Pauli–Lindblad model*, in which the noise channel is parametrized as ℰ = exp(Σ_{P∈𝒦} λ_P (P·P − ·)) over a sparse set 𝒦 of Pauli generators learned efficiently from cycle-benchmarking data. This captured correlated errors and enabled the first large-scale PEC demonstration on a superconducting processor with crosstalk — a milestone toward bias-free observables at meaningful circuit volumes.

```haskell
-- Quasi-probability sampling for one gate's inverse noise channel:
-- U_ideal = sum_n q_n * B_n,  gamma = sum_n |q_n|
samplePEC :: Double -> [(Double, IO Double)] -> IO Double
samplePEC gamma basis = do
  let probs = map (\(q, _) -> abs q / gamma) basis
  (q, runBasisOp) <- sampleDiscrete probs basis
  outcome <- runBasisOp
  return (signum q * gamma * outcome)  -- unbiased, variance x gamma^2
```

### 4.3 Learning-Based Mitigation: Clifford Data Regression

Clifford Data Regression (CDR), introduced by Czarnik, Arrasmith, Coles, and Cincio [3], sidesteps noise modeling entirely. Given a target circuit U with non-Clifford rotations, CDR generates an ensemble of *training circuits* {W_i} with nearly identical structure but with most non-Clifford gates replaced by Clifford gates drawn near the original angles. Each W_i is executed on hardware (yielding X_i^{noisy}) and simulated classically via Gottesman–Knill (yielding X_i^{exact}). A linear ansatz X^{exact} ≈ a·X^{noisy} + b is fit by least squares (often ridge-regularized), then applied to the target circuit's noisy measurement. On a 16-qubit ground-state energy problem on IBMQ hardware, CDR delivered an order-of-magnitude error reduction, and it scales to 64-qubit noisy simulators [3].

CDR's strength — agnosticism to the noise model — is also its vulnerability: the regression generalizes only insofar as training circuits experience *the same* noise as the target, and the classical simulation cost grows with the number of non-Clifford gates retained. The core idea, however — *learn the noise map from data you can verify* — has become a template for the field's data-driven turn, spawning energy-weighted training selection and multi-observable ansätze.

### 4.4 Passive Companions: Dynamical Decoupling, Readout Mitigation, Symmetry

Not all mitigation is algorithmic post-processing. *Dynamical decoupling* (DD) inserts echo sequences (XY-4, XY-8, CPMG) into idle qubit intervals to refocus low-frequency dephasing — a control-layer technique that composes freely with ZNE and PEC and was essential in the 127-qubit utility experiment [7]. *Readout mitigation* inverts calibrated confusion matrices, often under tensor-product approximations to tame the 2^n scaling. *Symmetry verification* postselects shots on conserved quantities (particle number, magnetization, stabilizer syndromes), trading sample efficiency for bias reduction; *virtual distillation* goes further, using multiple copies to suppress errors exponentially in the copy number at steep sampling cost.

### 4.5 The Sampling-Overhead Frontier

The deepest results in QEM are negative. Takagi, Endo, Minagawa, and Gu [5] derived *ultimate* lower bounds on the estimator spread Δe_A for any mitigation protocol achieving a given bias — bounds no protocol, present or future, can violate. Their framework shows that PEC is optimal among unbiased single-copy protocols for local dephasing, and that the exponential growth of spread with circuit fault rate in layered circuits is an *unavoidable* feature of general mitigation, not a defect of current methods. Takagi, Tajima, and Gu [6] strengthened this to *universal sample lower bounds*: for a wide class of protocols, including those with nonlinear post-processing, the number of noisy-circuit executions needed to mitigate errors must grow exponentially with circuit depth. ZNE is no refuge — its overhead scales exponentially in the number of gates in the observable's light cone.

The operational moral: mitigation converts depth into samples at an exponential exchange rate. It extends the reachable circuit volume by a large constant factor — enough, as §5 shows, to cross into classically intractable territory — but it cannot scale to arbitrarily deep circuits. That is the sampling-overhead frontier, and it is a theorem, not an engineering limitation.

## 5 Empirical and Theoretical Evidence

The decisive empirical test arrived in 2023. Kim *et al.* [7] executed Trotterized dynamics of the kicked transverse-field Ising model on IBM's 127-qubit Eagle processor (*ibm_kyiv*), measuring Pauli observables after up to 60 Trotter steps — circuit volumes far beyond brute-force classical simulation. Their mitigation stack combined ZNE (via gate folding), dynamical decoupling, and readout mitigation. Accuracy was established by comparison against *exactly verifiable circuits* (Clifford parameter points where classical simulation is exact) and, in the strongly entangling regime, the quantum results stood where leading classical approximations — matrix product states and isometric 2D tensor networks — broke down. The authors presented this as evidence for the utility of quantum computing before fault tolerance.

The result catalyzed a productive duel: improved classical tensor-network methods subsequently converged on the same observables with substantially reduced uncertainty [9], sharpening both the quantum experiments and the classical benchmarks. This dialectic — mitigation-enabled experiments probing regimes where classical methods strain, classical methods improving in response — is arguably the healthiest dynamic in near-term quantum computing.

| Method | Mechanism | Residual bias | Sampling overhead | Key requirement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Zero-noise extrapolation | Amplify noise, extrapolate λ → 0 | Fit-model mismatch | exp(O(λ)) via coefficient growth | Tunable noise amplification [1] |
| Probabilistic error cancellation | Quasi-probability channel inversion | Zero (exact noise model) | γ² = exp(O(λ)) | Accurate noise tomography [4] |
| Clifford data regression | Regression on simulable circuits | Training-distribution shift | O(1)–polynomial | Near-Clifford training set [3] |
| Readout mitigation | Confusion-matrix inversion | Calibration drift | Mild (condition number) | Stable SPAM characterization |
| Dynamical decoupling | Echo refocusing sequences | Coherent-error leakage | None (wall-clock only) | Control faster than T₂ [7] |

## 6 Limitations

The limitations of QEM are structural, and honesty about them is what separates the field from hype.

1. **The exponential wall is protocol-independent.** The lower bounds of [5][6] apply to *any* mitigation scheme, including undiscovered ones. Mitigation buys a constant-factor extension of reachable circuit volume — large, but not scalable. Unlike QEC, it has no threshold theorem: it cannot be concatenated to suppress errors arbitrarily.
2. **Characterization is the hidden cost.** PEC's unbiasedness assumes a known noise model, but learning correlated, drifting, non-Markovian noise on hundred-qubit devices is itself a research-scale undertaking; model error reintroduces bias silently [4].
3. **ZNE extrapolation is fragile.** The zero-noise point lies outside the measured data by construction. Wrong ansatz choice (polynomial vs. exponential), overfitting at high order, and coefficient blowup can produce confidently wrong answers — *biased estimates that look converged* are the characteristic failure mode.
4. **Learning methods generalize narrowly.** CDR's regression is only as good as its training distribution's match to the target circuit's noise exposure; drift between training and deployment reintroduces bias [3].
5. **Overhead accounting is subtle.** Published overheads often count only the mitigation multiplier, omitting tomography, calibration, and training circuits — the full experimental budget can be orders of magnitude larger.
6. **Mitigation ≠ correction.** Mitigation yields corrected *expectation values*, not corrected *states*; it cannot prepare high-fidelity states for downstream quantum subroutines, and it offers no composability guarantees.

## 7 Conclusion

Quantum error mitigation is the art of the possible in the NISQ era: a family of techniques that trade the qubit overheads of fault tolerance for sampling overheads governed by exponential — but precisely characterized — laws. Zero-noise extrapolation [1] cancels noise perturbatively through careful amplification and extrapolation; probabilistic error cancellation [2][4] achieves unbiasedness through quasi-probability inversion at a γ² sampling price that is provably optimal [5]; learning-based methods [3] replace noise models with data; and passive techniques compound the gains. The 127-qubit utility experiment [7] proved these tools can push real hardware into regimes where the best classical approximations falter, even as classical methods continue to close the gap [9].

The sampling-overhead frontier [5][6] is the field's defining feature: a theorem-drawn boundary, not a temporary obstacle. The future of mitigation therefore lies not in evading the exponential but in *managing* it — hybridizing with early fault tolerance (mitigation on logical qubits is already emerging), co-designing hardware whose noise is mitigation-friendly, and sharpening learning-based protocols. Until fault tolerance arrives at scale, QEM remains the principal instrument by which noisy quantum processors earn their keep.
