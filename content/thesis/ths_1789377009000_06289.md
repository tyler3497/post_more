# Neural Ordinary Differential Equations: Adjoint Sensitivity, Stiff Solvers, and Continuous Normalizing Flows

## Abstract

Neural ordinary differential equations recast depth as a continuous quantity: instead of stacking discrete layers, the time derivative of the hidden state is parameterized by a neural network, and the model's output is obtained by solving an initial value problem with a black-box numerical integrator. This single move converts network depth into solver tolerance, enables adaptive computation, and reduces the memory cost of backpropagation to a constant independent of solver steps through the adjoint sensitivity method of optimal control theory. The same continuous-time perspective yields continuous normalizing flows, where the change of variables formula collapses to a cheap trace operation, and, with a stochastic trace estimator, to scalable free-form generative models. This thesis develops the full mathematical machinery: the Pontryagin-derived adjoint equations, the stability theory that forces stiff neural dynamics onto implicit solvers, and the instantaneous change-of-variables theorem behind continuous flows. We survey empirical results across classification, density estimation, and time-series modeling, and close with a precise accounting of the framework's limitations, including adjoint drift, estimator variance, topological constraints of ODE flows, and the pathologies of training with adaptive solvers.

## 1 Introduction

The residual connection, h_{t+1} = h_t + f(h_t, theta_t), is an Euler discretization of a continuous transformation. Identifying the layer index with time, the forward pass of a residual network approximates the integral curve of dh(t)/dt = f(h(t), t, theta). Neural ordinary differential equations [1] take this identification literally: depth becomes a real number, the discrete stack of layers is replaced by a single vector field, and the output is produced by a black-box ODE solver applied to dh(t)/dt = f_theta(h(t), t) with h(t_0) = x.

This reformulation buys three things of genuine consequence. First, *adaptive computation*: an adaptive solver automatically spends function evaluations where the dynamics are complicated, so effective depth becomes input-dependent. Second, *memory-efficient training*: gradients are computed by solving a second, augmented ODE backwards in time — the adjoint sensitivity method — with constant memory regardless of solver steps [1]. Third, *new model classes*: continuous-time generative models and latent-variable models for irregularly sampled time series emerge naturally.

The price of the continuous-time formulation is that training inherits the full difficulty of numerical differential equations: the adjoint system is itself an ODE whose backward integration can be unstable; learned vector fields develop stiffness that grinds explicit solvers to a halt; and normalizing flows require re-deriving the change of variables in continuous time. Each problem has a principled resolution — the adjoint equations of optimal control, implicit integrators with IMEX splitting, and Hutchinson's stochastic trace estimator — each with its own subtleties. This thesis examines all three in depth.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From residual networks to continuous depth

A residual network with L blocks computes h_{t+1} = h_t + f(h_t, theta_t) for integer t. Interpreting the update as one Euler step of size one, the hidden states sample the trajectory of dh/dt = f(h(t), t, theta(t)). Chen et al. [1] close the loop: learn the vector field f_theta directly and let the solver handle discretization. The learned parameters theta are now shared across all of continuous time, which explains why Neural ODE models routinely match ResNet accuracy with far fewer parameters.

### 2.2 The adjoint equation in optimal control

The adjoint sensitivity method predates deep learning by decades. In optimal control, Pontryagin's maximum principle associates to every trajectory an adjoint (costate) variable a(t) = dL/dh(t) obeying a linear ODE that runs backwards from the terminal condition, da/dt = -a(t)^T (partial f / partial h), with a(t_1) = partial L / partial h(t_1). The gradient with respect to the parameters follows from a single integral of the adjoint against partial f / partial theta.

### 2.3 Numerical integration: explicit, implicit, and stiff

An ODE solver advances an approximate solution using evaluations of f. Explicit Runge–Kutta methods — Euler, classical RK4, the adaptive Dormand–Prince pair (DOPRI5) — compute each stage from previously computed stages only: cheap per step, but with bounded stability regions. For dy/dt = lambda y with lambda strongly negative, the step must satisfy a stability bound, forcing tiny steps when fast-decaying modes are present. *Stiffness* is this situation: components evolve on widely separated time scales, and step size is dictated by stability rather than accuracy. Implicit methods — backward Euler, Radau IIA collocation, Rosenbrock — solve a system per step, paying a cubic Jacobian factorization but permitting large steps once fast transients have decayed. Chemical kinetics and circuit models are stiff by construction [3][4]; random-projection neural networks offer an alternative that avoids time-stepping entirely, solving stiff benchmarks like ROBER and van der Pol by collocation with Gauss–Newton iteration [6].

### 2.4 Normalizing flows and the Jacobian bottleneck

A normalizing flow models a complex density by pushing a simple base density through an invertible transformation, applying the change of variables formula log p(x) = log p_z(z) - log |det (partial g / partial x)|. The determinant of a D-by-D Jacobian costs O(D^3) in general, so discrete flows restrict architectures — coupling layers, triangular Jacobians — to keep it tractable. Continuous normalizing flows replace the single map with an ODE: if z(t) evolves under dz/dt = f(z(t), t, theta), the density evolves by the instantaneous change of variables d log p(z(t))/dt = -tr(partial f / partial z) [1], a trace costing O(D^2) exactly — still too slow for unrestricted architectures in high dimensions, which motivates stochastic estimation [2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Neural ODE forward and backward pass

A Neural ODE model specifies a vector field f_theta: R^D times R -> R^D, an initial time t_0, a terminal time t_1, and a black-box solver. The forward pass computes h(t_1) = h(t_0) + integral from t_0 to t_1 of f_theta(h(t), t) dt. Training requires dL/dtheta and dL/dh(t_0) for a scalar loss L(h(t_1)). Rather than differentiating through the solver's internal operations — which costs memory linear in the number of steps — the adjoint method solves an augmented system backwards:

- the adjoint a(t) = dL/dh(t), obeying da/dt = -a(t)^T (partial f / partial h),
- the state h(t) reconstructed by integrating dh/dt = f_theta backwards,
- the parameter gradient dL/dtheta = -integral from t_1 to t_0 of a(t)^T (partial f / partial theta) dt,

all in one backward solve with terminal conditions a(t_1) = partial L / partial h(t_1) and dL/dtheta initialized at zero [1]. The vector-Jacobian products a^T (partial f / partial h) are evaluated by automatic differentiation at each step of the backward integration, so the entire gradient computation uses O(1) memory: only the current state of the augmented system is ever stored.

A typical implementation, following the torchdiffeq library released with the original paper, looks like this:

```python
import torch
from torchdiffeq import odeint_adjoint

class ODEFunc(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, hidden_dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = torch.nn.Sequential(
            torch.nn.Linear(hidden_dim + 1, 64),
            torch.nn.Tanh(),
            torch.nn.Linear(64, hidden_dim),
        )
    def forward(self, t, h):
        # time is concatenated so the field may be non-autonomous
        th = torch.cat([h, t.expand(h.shape[0], 1)], dim=1)
        return self.net(th)

func = ODEFunc(hidden_dim=64)
h0 = torch.randn(128, 64)          # batch of initial states
t = torch.tensor([0.0, 1.0])       # integrate over the unit interval
h1 = odeint_adjoint(func, h0, t, method="dopri5", rtol=1e-3, atol=1e-4)[-1]
loss = classifier(h1).sum()        # any downstream loss
loss.backward()                    # gradients flow via the adjoint ODE
```

The `odeint_adjoint` call registers a custom autograd function whose backward pass solves the augmented adjoint system, so memory stays flat even when the adaptive solver takes hundreds of steps [1].

### 3.2 The FFJORD trace estimator

For continuous normalizing flows, exact likelihood requires the trace of partial f / partial z at every solver step: O(D^2) with reverse-mode autodiff. FFJORD [2] replaces the exact trace with Hutchinson's unbiased estimator: for any random vector epsilon with zero mean and identity covariance, E[epsilon^T A epsilon] = tr(A). Choosing Rademacher vectors (entries plus or minus one with equal probability), the log-density dynamics become d log p/dt = -E_epsilon[epsilon^T (partial f / partial z) epsilon], and a single Monte Carlo sample per solver step yields an unbiased estimate of the full integral. Each vector-Jacobian product costs O(D) — the same as one backward pass through f — so the density computation drops from quadratic to linear time in the state dimension, with no architectural restrictions on f whatsoever [2].

### 3.3 Stabilizing stiff neural dynamics

When the learned vector field is stiff, explicit solvers take vanishingly small steps or diverge. Kim et al. [3] developed a training stack for stiff neural ODEs on the ROBER and POLLU chemical-kinetics benchmarks: *IMEX splitting* of the adjoint system so LU factorizations act only on the k-by-k state block; *dense-output quadrature* for the adjoint integral; and *equation and loss scaling*, normalizing states and loss components by their characteristic scales (y_scale = y_max - y_min, t_scale = t_1 - t_0) [3]. Caldana and Hesthaven [4] offer a complementary route for model-order reduction of stiff systems: learn a data-driven reparametrization of time induced by an implicit solver's adaptive stepping, train the neural ODE in the nonstiff reparametrized clock where an explicit integrator is cheap, and map back to physical time with a second network.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The adjoint sensitivity method: derivation and the Pontryagin connection

Consider minimizing L(h(t_1)) subject to dh/dt = f(h(t), t, theta) with h(t_0) = x. Introducing a Lagrange multiplier function a(t) — the adjoint — and integrating by parts, stationarity in the trajectory yields the adjoint ODE da/dt = -a(t)^T (partial f / partial h) with a(t_1) = partial L / partial h(t_1); stationarity in theta then gives the gradient as a quadrature of the adjoint against the parametric sensitivity:

> **Theorem (Adjoint sensitivity).** Let h(t) solve dh/dt = f(h, t, theta) on the interval from t_0 to t_1 and let L be differentiable in h(t_1). Define a(t) by the terminal value problem da/dt = -a^T partial f/partial h, a(t_1) = partial L/partial h(t_1). Then dL/dtheta = -integral from t_1 to t_0 of a(t)^T (partial f/partial theta) dt, and dL/dh(t_0) = a(t_0).

This is a special case of Pontryagin's maximum principle, where the adjoint arises as the costate of an optimal control problem with the neural parameters as controls. The decisive computational fact is that the backward system is solved as a single ODE: memory is O(1) in the number of forward solver steps, versus O(steps) for differentiating through the solver's computational graph [1].

| Quantity | Backprop through solver | Adjoint method |
|---|---|---|
| Memory in solver steps | O(steps) | O(1) |
| Extra forward solves | 0 | 1 (backward reconstruction) |
| Gradient exactness | Exact for the discretized map | Exact for the continuous limit |
| Failure mode | Out-of-memory on long integrations | Drift if backward ODE is unstable |

### 4.2 Stiffness: why explicit solvers collapse and what implicit methods cost

Stiffness is a property of the *equation–solver pair*, not of the equation alone: an IVP is stiff when the step size required for stability of an explicit method is much smaller than the step size required for accuracy. Neural ODE training walks directly into this regime: as optimization sharpens decision boundaries or fits fast transients, the Jacobian partial f/partial h develops eigenvalues with large negative real parts, and DOPRI5's step size collapses — exploding NFE, error-test failures, gradients dominated by the fastest modes.

The remedies form a hierarchy of increasing sophistication and cost:

1. **Adaptive explicit solvers with tight tolerances** — sufficient for benign dynamics; the default in most Neural ODE work [1].
2. **Regularization of the dynamics** — penalizing the kinetic energy integral of ||f||^2 or the Frobenius norm of partial f/partial h keeps trajectories short and Jacobians tame, reducing NFE and stiffness.
3. **Implicit and Rosenbrock solvers with IMEX splitting** — factorizing only the stiff state block, as in [3], makes implicit treatment affordable inside the training loop.
4. **Time reparametrization** — learning the stiff time-warp separately so the neural ODE itself sees a nonstiff problem [4].
5. **Direct collocation / NLP formulations** — discretizing the whole trajectory with high-order implicit collocation (Radau IIA) and optimizing states and parameters jointly, removing the forward-simulation bottleneck at the cost of large linear solves [7].

### 4.3 Continuous normalizing flows and the FFJORD estimator

The instantaneous change of variables is worth deriving, because it is the engine of continuous normalizing flows. For an infinitesimal step, z(t + dt) approx z(t) + f(z(t), t) dt has Jacobian I + (partial f/partial z) dt, whose log-determinant is tr(partial f/partial z) dt + O(dt^2). Chaining infinitesimal steps turns the discrete change-of-variables formula into d log p(z(t))/dt = -tr(partial f/partial z) [1], the minus sign reflecting that we track the density along the forward flow.

FFJORD's contribution [2] is to make this practical at scale. Exact traces cost O(D^2); Hutchinson's estimator reduces each step to a single vector-Jacobian product, O(D). The estimator is unbiased for the log-density *integral*, so maximum-likelihood training with stochastic gradients is exact in expectation. Practical refinements include Rademacher over Gaussian noise (lower variance), ConcatSquash layers for time-dependent dynamics without parameter blowup, and multiscale architectures that factor out dimensions at intermediate times [2].

The payoff is architectural freedom: unlike coupling-layer flows, the vector field can be any neural network — residual blocks, convolutions, attention — because the estimator never needs a structured Jacobian. FFJORD achieved state-of-the-art likelihoods among exact-likelihood models on tabular benchmarks and competitive bits-per-dimension on image data, with one-pass sampling inherited from the ODE formulation [2].

| Family | Likelihood cost per step | Sampling | Architecture |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discrete coupling flow | O(D) determinant | One pass | Restricted (partitioned) |
| Exact-trace CNF [1] | O(D^2) trace | One ODE solve | Mildly restricted |
| FFJORD [2] | O(D) stochastic trace | One ODE solve | Unrestricted |

### 4.4 Augmented, second-order, and regularized variants

ODE flows are homeomorphisms: trajectories cannot cross, and the map z(t_0) -> z(t_1) preserves the topology of the input space. A vanilla Neural ODE therefore cannot represent functions that tear space apart — the canonical example is the reflection map g(x) = -x in one dimension. Augmented Neural ODEs (ANODEs) lift the state into a higher-dimensional space, z = concat(x, a), giving trajectories room to maneuver around each other [5]. Norcliffe et al. [5] push this further, deriving the *second-order* adjoint equations for second-order Neural ODEs — systems of the form d^2x/dt^2 = f(x, dx/dt, t) that model Newtonian mechanics directly — and proving that optimizing the coupled first-order system is equivalent but computationally cheaper than the native second-order adjoint. Their analysis shows ANODEs can learn higher-order dynamics with a minimal number of augmented dimensions, though at some cost to interpretability.

Regularization completes the practical picture. Unregularized Neural ODEs learn needlessly complex vector fields: the number of function evaluations grows during training and the flow becomes stiff. Penalizing trajectory length and the Jacobian norm produces simpler dynamics and fewer solver steps.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: instantaneous change of variables

Let p(z, t) be the density of particles flowing under dz/dt = f(z, t). Conservation of probability gives the continuity equation partial p/partial t + div(p f) = 0. Along a trajectory, dp/dt = -p div f, and since div f = tr(partial f/partial z), we obtain d log p/dt = -tr(partial f/partial z) [1]. The FFJORD extension replaces the trace by its Hutchinson estimator inside the time integral; unbiasedness at each t implies unbiasedness of the integrated log-density [2].

### 5.2 Classification and the parameter-efficiency claim

On MNIST, the original paper reports an ODE-Net matching or slightly beating a six-block ResNet on test error while using substantially fewer parameters, because the vector field's parameters are shared across continuous time rather than replicated per layer [1]. An independent from-scratch reproduction trains three variants against a ResNet-6 baseline and finds the same pattern quantitatively:

| Model | Test accuracy | Parameters | Forward NFE |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neural ODE (Euler) | 97.84% | ~50K | 10 |
| Neural ODE (RK4) | 97.63% | ~50K | 40 |
| ResNet-6 baseline | 97.95% | ~120K | — |

The ODE models reach the ResNet's accuracy band with roughly 58% fewer parameters; the solver's step count (NFE) plays the role that layer count plays in the ResNet [8].

### 5.3 Density estimation with continuous flows

FFJORD's empirical program spans three regimes [2]. On two-dimensional toy densities, continuous flows capture multimodal structure that single-pass invertible maps struggle with. On tabular benchmarks, FFJORD with unrestricted vector fields outperforms masked autoregressive and coupling-based models on held-out likelihood — the first exact-likelihood model to do so without architectural constraints. On MNIST and CIFAR-10, multiscale FFJORD reaches competitive bits per dimension among exact-likelihood models, while sampling remains a single ODE solve. The stochastic trace estimator's variance does not prevent convergence, though it motivates variance-reduction follow-ups.

### 5.4 Time series and stiff scientific dynamics

Latent ODE models — a recognition network mapping irregular observations to an initial latent state, a Neural ODE evolving it, and a decoder emitting observations at arbitrary query times — handle irregular sampling natively, where RNNs require imputation [1]. Caldana and Hesthaven [4] show that time-reparametrized neural ODEs reduce inference cost for stiff model-order-reduction problems while generalizing beyond the training horizon — a property of the continuous-time formulation that discrete surrogates do not share.

## 6 Limitations

The adjoint method's constant memory comes with a numerical catch: the backward pass reconstructs the forward trajectory by integrating in reverse, and for vector fields with large Lipschitz constants — stiff or expansive dynamics — the reconstruction diverges from the true forward states, injecting error into every gradient. Kim et al. [3] demonstrate this on the ROBER benchmark: even imperceptible forward errors produce large reverse-solve errors unless tolerances are pushed below single precision. Checkpointing restores accuracy at the cost of extra memory.

Training dynamics themselves are a second limitation. The number of function evaluations typically *grows* during training as the vector field becomes more complex, so wall-clock time per epoch inflates; regularization of trajectory length and Jacobian norms mitigates this but adds hyperparameters. Stiffness can emerge mid-training even for nonstiff targets, and the implicit solvers that handle it carry cubic factorization costs [3].

Continuous normalizing flows inherit estimator variance: the Hutchinson trace estimate is unbiased but noisy, and its variance grows with the dimension and the Jacobian's spectral spread. Topologically, unaugmented ODE flows are homeomorphisms and cannot represent tearing maps; augmentation fixes expressivity [5] but sacrifices the clean low-dimensional interpretation of the latent space.

Finally, the framework is deterministic and smooth by construction. Nonsmooth vector fields (ReLU networks are only piecewise smooth) technically violate the Lipschitz assumptions the solvers and the adjoint derivation rely on. In practice tanh and softplus activations are preferred, a quiet concession that the theory shapes the engineering.

## 7 Conclusion

Neural ordinary differential equations replace the discrete stack of layers with a learned vector field and a numerical integrator, and the consequences ramify through training, modeling, and inference. The adjoint sensitivity method — Pontryagin's costate equations specialized to neural parameters — makes backpropagation through arbitrary solver trajectories cost constant memory. Stiffness analysis explains when explicit solvers fail and how implicit, IMEX-split, or reparametrized formulations recover tractability. The instantaneous change of variables turns the O(D^3) Jacobian-determinant bottleneck into a trace, and Hutchinson's estimator turns the trace into a linear-time unbiased estimate, yielding free-form generative models with exact likelihoods.

What endures is a design pattern: *differentiable numerical methods as neural network layers*. The solver is not an implementation detail but part of the model, with its tolerances, stability regions, and error estimates participating in learning. The limitations — adjoint drift, training-time stiffness, estimator variance, topological constraints — are not refutations but a research agenda, each one pointing at a place where numerical analysis and deep learning have not yet finished negotiating their interface.
