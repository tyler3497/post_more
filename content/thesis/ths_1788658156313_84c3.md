# Low-Density Parity-Check Codes: Tanner Graphs, Belief-Propagation Decoding, Density Evolution, and the 5G NR Data-Channel Design

## Abstract

Low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes are sparse random linear codes whose near-capacity performance under linear-time iterative decoding reshaped modern channel coding. Introduced by Robert Gallager in his 1962 MIT doctoral dissertation and generalized graphically by Tanner in 1981, LDPC codes languished for three decades before their rediscovery by MacKay and Neal and the theoretical apparatus of irregular degree distributions and density evolution established by Luby, Richardson, and Urbanke. This survey traces the full arc from Gallager's probabilistic decoding to the quasi-cyclic QC-LDPC codes standardized in 3GPP TS 38.212 for the 5G NR data channel. We develop the Tanner-graph representation, the belief-propagation and min-sum decoding recursions with a working reference implementation, density evolution and the concentration theorem that justifies it, EXIT-style threshold analysis, and the quasi-cyclic lifting, puncturing, and rate-matching machinery of the two 5G base graphs. We conclude with empirical threshold tables, error-floor phenomenology, and the limitations that keep LDPC research active at finite blocklengths.

## 1 Introduction

In 1962, Robert G. Gallager submitted a doctoral dissertation at MIT that quietly solved one of the central problems of communication theory: how to construct *explicit, analyzable* codes that approach the Shannon capacity while admitting decoders whose complexity grows only linearly in the blocklength [1]. His answer — linear block codes defined by *sparse* parity-check matrices, decoded by a local, iterative, probabilistic algorithm — was a piece of twenty-first-century engineering that happened to fall in the twentieth. The codes were ignored for thirty-five years. The computing hardware of the era could not simulate them at meaningful blocklengths; the prevailing wisdom favored concatenated Reed–Solomon and convolutional codes; and Gallager's own analysis, though remarkably complete, could not prove the near-capacity operation that experiments now demonstrate routinely.

The resurrection began in the mid-1990s. MacKay and Neal, apparently independently, rediscovered the power of very sparse parity-check matrices [2]; Luby, Mitzenmacher, Shokrollahi, and Spielman showed that *irregular* graphs — where variable and check nodes follow carefully optimized degree distributions — strictly outperform Gallager's regular constructions and can be driven arbitrarily close to capacity [3]. Richardson and Urbanke then supplied the missing analytic foundation: **density evolution**, a deterministic dynamical system that predicts the asymptotic performance of message-passing decoding exactly, together with a **concentration theorem** proving that almost every code in a random ensemble behaves like the ensemble average [4]. Richardson, Shokrollahi, and Urbanke turned these tools into a design discipline, constructing irregular ensembles whose thresholds sit within hundredths of a decibel of the Shannon limit [5].

The practical culmination of this arc arrived with the 3GPP 5G New Radio standard. LDPC codes were selected for the NR *data* channel (with polar codes taking the control channel), in the form of quasi-cyclic codes generated by lifting two carefully engineered base graphs with cyclic shifts — a marriage of random-graph theory and structured VLSI-friendly implementation [6]. Every 5G handset in the world today runs belief propagation on Tanner graphs.

This thesis presents the theory as a unified whole: the graphical representation, the decoding algorithm, the asymptotic analysis that explains *why* the algorithm works, the design methods that optimize the graph, and the standardization that brought it to industry. A working Python reference decoder is included for concreteness.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Linear block codes and sparse parity checks

A binary linear block code of length *n* and dimension *k* is a subspace of the vector space GF(2)^n, conventionally specified by an *m × n* parity-check matrix **H** with *m = n − k* rows: the code is the null space {**x** : **Hx**ᵀ = **0**}. Decoding a general linear code by syndrome search is NP-hard, so the structure of **H** must buy us a tractable algorithm.

Gallager's insight [1] was to make **H** *sparse*: the number of ones grows as *O(n)* rather than *O(n²)*. Sparseness serves two masters simultaneously:

1. **Near-random distance spectra.** A sparse random **H** generates a code whose weight distribution resembles that of a fully random linear code — the "random-like" quality that Shannon's random-coding argument shows is what capacity requires [7].
2. **Local, parallelizable decoding.** Each parity equation involves only a handful of bits, so decoding can proceed by exchanging soft information between neighboring bits and checks, with per-iteration complexity linear in *n*.

A *(d_v, d_c)-regular* LDPC code has exactly *d_v* ones per column and *d_c* per row, giving design rate *R = 1 − d_v/d_c*. Gallager proposed constructions in which the matrix is assembled from randomly permuted submatrices of weight one, and analyzed two decoding algorithms — a hard-decision "flipping" algorithm and a soft *probabilistic* algorithm that is the direct ancestor of modern belief propagation.

### 2.2 Tanner graphs

In 1981, R. Michael Tanner generalized Gallager's construction to *generalized* LDPC codes — replacing single parity checks with arbitrary constituent codes — and introduced the bipartite graphical representation now universally called the **Tanner graph** [8]. For a parity-check matrix **H**, the Tanner graph is a bipartite graph with:

- *variable nodes* *v₁, …, vₙ* on one side, one per code bit (column of **H**);
- *check nodes* *c₁, …, c_m* on the other side, one per parity equation (row of **H**);
- an edge *(vᵢ, cⱼ)* iff *H[j, i] = 1*.

Decoding becomes **message passing** on this graph: each node aggregates incoming messages from its neighbors and forwards refined beliefs onward. The representation is so natural that the modern theory is stated almost entirely in its language — degree distributions, neighborhoods, cycles, stopping sets, and trapping sets are all graph-theoretic notions living on the Tanner graph. Its main pathology is also graph-theoretic: short cycles create dependencies that defeat the independence assumptions behind the analysis, so code construction devotes considerable effort to maximizing *girth*.

### 2.3 The channel models

The canonical settings are the **binary erasure channel (BEC)** with erasure probability *ε* and the **binary-input AWGN channel (BI-AWGNC)** with noise variance *σ²*. On the BEC, messages are scalars in {0, 1, ?} and decoding admits exact closed-form analysis; on the AWGN channel, messages are log-likelihood ratios and the analysis requires tracking probability densities. By the **all-zero codeword assumption**, valid for any symmetric channel and symmetric decoder, analysis conditions on the transmission of the all-zero codeword without loss of generality.

## 3 Methodology

Our method in this survey is threefold: *construct* the code ensembles, *decode* them with message-passing algorithms whose complexity scales linearly, and *analyze* them with density evolution, which becomes exact as *n → ∞* because the computation tree of any node is cycle-free with probability tending to one.

### 3.1 Ensembles and degree distributions

An *irregular* LDPC ensemble is specified by two edge-perspective degree-distribution polynomials

$$\lambda(x) = \sum_i \lambda_i x^{i-1}, \qquad \rho(x) = \sum_i \rho_i x^{i-1},$$

where *λᵢ* (*ρᵢ*) is the fraction of edges incident to a variable (check) node of degree *i*. The design rate is *R = 1 − (Σᵢ ρᵢ/i) / (Σᵢ λᵢ/i)*. A code is drawn by sampling, for each variable node, a degree from *λ*, attaching "sockets," and connecting sockets through a uniform random permutation to check-node sockets. The analysis of [4] shows that the performance of message-passing decoding depends, asymptotically, *only* on *(λ, ρ)* — not on the particular permutation.

### 3.2 Message-passing decoding

The **sum–product (belief-propagation)** algorithm exchanges log-likelihood ratios along the Tanner graph edges. Let *L_ch,i* be the channel LLR at variable node *i*. The variable-to-check and check-to-variable messages at iteration *ℓ* are:

- *Variable-node update:* μ_{vᵢ→cⱼ}^(ℓ) = L_ch,i + Σ_{c ∈ N(i)\{j\}} μ_{c→vᵢ}^(ℓ−1)
- *Check-node update:* μ_{cⱼ→vᵢ}^(ℓ) = 2 · atanh( Π_{v ∈ N(j)\{i\}} tanh(μ_{v→cⱼ}^(ℓ) / 2) )

The celebrated *tanh rule* at the check node is exact on a tree. The **min-sum** approximation replaces the check-node update by a signed minimum:

$$\mu_{c_j \to v_i}^{(\ell)} = \left(\prod_{v \in \mathcal{N}(j)\setminus\{i\}} \mathrm{sgn}(\mu_{v\to c_j}^{(\ell)})\right) \cdot \min_{v \in \mathcal{N}(j)\setminus\{i\}} |\mu_{v\to c_j}^{(\ell)}|,$$

optionally scaled by a *normalizing* factor *α ≈ 0.75–0.9* or corrected by an *offset β* to close the 0.3–0.5 dB gap to sum–product. Min-sum variants dominate hardware because they need only additions and comparisons. We implement both below in §4.3.

### 3.3 Density evolution and thresholds

**Density evolution (DE)** tracks the probability density of messages through iterations under the tree assumption. For the BEC this collapses to a scalar recursion [4]:

> **Theorem (Density evolution on the BEC).** Let *p_ℓ* be the erasure probability of a randomly chosen message at iteration *ℓ*. Then
>
> $$p_{\ell+1} = \varepsilon\,\lambda\!\left(1 - \rho(1 - p_\ell)\right), \qquad p_0 = \varepsilon,$$
>
> and decoding succeeds asymptotically iff the orbit *p_ℓ → 0*. The **threshold** *ε\** is the supremum of *ε* for which convergence holds.

For the BI-AWGNC, the analogous recursion operates on densities (or, under the Gaussian approximation of [9], on a single mean parameter). The threshold separates the noise regimes where the DE fixed point is zero from those where it is not. Justifying the entire edifice is the concentration result:

> **Theorem (Concentration; Richardson–Urbanke).** For a fixed number of iterations, the fraction of incorrect messages of a randomly chosen code concentrates exponentially fast around the density-evolution prediction as the blocklength *n → ∞*.

In other words: DE is not a heuristic — for large *n*, almost every code in the ensemble behaves as DE predicts.

## 4 Deep Dive: Decoding, Analysis, and Structured Construction

### 4.1 Belief propagation as exact inference on trees

On a cycle-free Tanner graph, belief propagation computes *exact* a posteriori marginals: each message summarizes all evidence in the subtree behind it, and the graph's tree structure guarantees the subtrees are conditionally independent. Real LDPC graphs have cycles, but a random Tanner graph is *locally tree-like*: the depth-*ℓ* neighborhood of a typical node is a tree with probability 1 − *O(1/n)*. Concentration converts this local fact into a global performance prediction. The residual dependence caused by cycles is the fundamental source of the **error floor** at high SNR, mediated by small subgraphs called *trapping sets* — for the BEC, the cleaner notion of *stopping sets*: variable-node sets whose neighboring check nodes each connect at least twice into the set, so that iterative erasure decoding can never resolve them.

### 4.2 Density evolution, EXIT charts, and threshold saturation

For general symmetric channels, DE tracks full message densities — computationally expensive but exact. Two simplifications dominate practice:

1. **The Gaussian approximation** [9]: message densities are approximated as consistent Gaussians, reducing DE to a one-dimensional recursion on the mean. Accuracy is excellent for the AWGN channel.
2. **EXIT charts**: plot the *extrinsic information transfer* of variable-node and check-node decoders as mutual-information curves; convergence requires an open tunnel between them. For the BEC, EXIT curves are exact; for the AWGN channel they are a remarkably faithful proxy.

Thresholds for classical ensembles illustrate how far irregularity moved the field:

| Ensemble (rate 1/2) | Channel | Threshold | Shannon limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| (3, 6) regular | BEC | ε\* = 0.4294 | ε = 0.5000 |
| (3, 6) regular | BI-AWGNC | 1.101 dB | 0.187 dB |
| Optimized irregular | BI-AWGNC | 0.92 dB | 0.187 dB |
| Chung–et al. design [10] | BI-AWGNC | 0.0045 dB from limit | — |

Chung, Forney, Richardson, and Urbanke's 2001 design closed the gap to the Shannon limit to *0.0045 dB* [10] — a hair's breadth, though at the cost of very high maximum degrees and long blocklengths. A further breakthrough, **spatial coupling** (convolutional LDPC), saturates the threshold to the MAP threshold of the underlying ensemble, provably achieving capacity on the BEC — but that is a story for another survey.

### 4.3 Reference decoder: belief propagation and min-sum in Python

The following self-contained implementation builds a random regular Tanner graph and decodes with either sum–product or normalized min-sum, using a layered (row-shuffled) schedule. It is pedagogical — production decoders exploit quasi-cyclic structure and fixed-point arithmetic — but it faithfully implements the recursions of §3.2.

```python
import numpy as np

def random_regular_ldpc(n, dv, dc, rng):
    """Return adjacency: var_neighbors[i], check_neighbors[j]."""
    assert n * dv % dc == 0
    m = n * dv // dc
    # configuration model: random permutation of edge stubs
    var_stubs = np.repeat(np.arange(n), dv)
    chk_stubs = np.repeat(np.arange(m), dc)
    rng.shuffle(chk_stubs)
    var_nbrs = [[] for _ in range(n)]
    chk_nbrs = [[] for _ in range(m)]
    for v, c in zip(var_stubs, chk_stubs):
        var_nbrs[v].append(int(c))
        chk_nbrs[c].append(int(v))
    return var_nbrs, chk_nbrs

def decode(var_nbrs, chk_nbrs, llr, max_iter=50, mode="sum_product", alpha=0.8):
    n, m = len(var_nbrs), len(chk_nbrs)
    # v2c[i][k]: message from variable i to its k-th neighboring check
    v2c = [[llr[i]] * len(var_nbrs[i]) for i in range(n)]
    # index map: for variable i, position of check j in its list
    for it in range(max_iter):
        # check-node update (layered: use freshest v2c)
        c2v = [[0.0] * len(chk_nbrs[j]) for j in range(m)]
        for j in range(m):
            msgs = [v2c[v][var_nbrs[v].index(j)] for v in chk_nbrs[j]]
            for k, v in enumerate(chk_nbrs[j]):
                others = msgs[:k] + msgs[k+1:]
                if mode == "sum_product":
                    prod = np.prod([np.tanh(x / 2) for x in others])
                    c2v[j][k] = 2 * np.arctanh(np.clip(prod, -1 + 1e-12, 1 - 1e-12))
                else:  # normalized min-sum
                    s = np.prod(np.sign(others)) if others else 1.0
                    c2v[j][k] = alpha * s * min(abs(x) for x in others)
        # variable-node update
        for i in range(n):
            for k, c in enumerate(var_nbrs[i]):
                s = llr[i]
                for k2, c2 in enumerate(var_nbrs[i]):
                    if c2 != c:
                        s += c2v[c2][chk_nbrs[c2].index(i)]
                v2c[i][k] = s
        # hard decision: check syndrome
        dec = np.zeros(n, dtype=int)
        for i in range(n):
            tot = llr[i] + sum(c2v[c][chk_nbrs[c].index(i)] for c in var_nbrs[i])
            dec[i] = 0 if tot >= 0 else 1
        if all(sum(dec[v] for v in chk_nbrs[j]) % 2 == 0 for j in range(m)):
            return dec, it + 1  # syndrome satisfied
    return dec, max_iter

# Example: (3,6)-regular, n=1008, BI-AWGN at Eb/N0 = 2 dB, rate 1/2
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
var_nbrs, chk_nbrs = random_regular_ldpc(n=1008, dv=3, dc=6, rng=rng)
sigma = np.sqrt(1 / (2 * 0.5 * 10 ** (2 / 10)))
y = 1.0 + sigma * rng.standard_normal(1008)   # all-zero codeword -> +1
llr = 2 * y / sigma ** 2
dec, iters = decode(var_nbrs, chk_nbrs, llr, mode="min_sum")
print("decoded in", iters, "iterations; errors:", int(np.sum(dec)))
```

Two engineering notes deserve emphasis. First, the **layered (horizontal-shuffled) schedule** — updating check rows sequentially and reusing fresh messages within one iteration — converges roughly twice as fast as the flooding schedule above and is standard in silicon. Second, real decoders quantize LLRs to 5–7 bits and rely on the min-sum family's insensitivity to the exact noise variance, sidestepping the SNR estimation that sum–product demands.

### 4.4 Quasi-cyclic lifting and the 5G NR base graphs

Random ensembles are ideal for theory and awkward for hardware: storing a random permutation and routing arbitrary edges costs area and power. The industry answer is **quasi-cyclic (QC) LDPC**: start from a small *base graph* (protograph) with *m_b × n_b* entries, and *lift* it by replacing each nonzero entry with a *Z_c × Z_c* cyclically shifted identity matrix (each zero entry becomes a zero matrix). The lifted parity-check matrix is an array of circulants, so the encoder is a shift-register circuit and the decoder is a regular array of *Z_c*-parallel processors — at the price of a structured, non-random graph whose girth must be managed by choosing shifts carefully.

3GPP TS 38.212 standardizes exactly two base graphs for the NR data channel [6]:

| Parameter | BG1 | BG2 |
|---|---|---|
| Base graph size | 46 × 68 | 42 × 52 |
| Punctured (untransmitted) systematic columns | 2 | 2 |
| Max info bits K | 8448 | 3840 |
| Lifting sizes Z_c | up to 384 | up to 384 |
| Design rates | 1/3 – 8/9 | 1/5 – 2/3 |

Selection follows a simple rule (TS 38.212 §7.2.2): BG2 is used when the transport block is small (*A ≤ 292* bits), or *A ≤ 3824* with rate *R ≤ 0.67*, or *R ≤ 0.25* — otherwise BG1 [6]. Both graphs share a distinctive anatomy: a dense *core* of high-degree parity rows providing strong low-rate protection, a *double-diagonal* extension region enabling linear-time encoding by back-substitution, and degree-1 extension variable nodes that make rate matching by incremental redundancy almost free. The first two systematic columns are always punctured, which both improves the threshold (puncturing state-like nodes is the oldest DE trick) and hides the highest-degree variables from the channel.

Rate matching then punctures or repeats the circular buffer of the mother code to hit any desired transport-block rate, and hybrid ARQ simply transmits further parity segments on retransmission. The whole chain — segmentation, CRC attachment, QC-LDPC encoding, rate matching, interleaving — is specified bit-exactly in [6], which is why a handset and a base station from different vendors interoperate.

## 5 Thresholds, Empirical Evidence, and the Standard

The asymptotic theory makes falsifiable predictions, and six decades of experiment have confirmed them with unusual fidelity. Key validated claims:

1. **Threshold sharpness.** For the (3,6)-regular ensemble on the BEC, DE predicts a threshold *ε\** = 0.4294; simulations at *n* = 10⁵ show the waterfall exactly there, with the concentration theorem explaining the vanishing variance across random draws [4], [7].
2. **Irregularity gains.** Optimized irregular profiles close the AWGN gap to under a decibel, and the Chung–et al. construction reaches 0.0045 dB from the Shannon limit [5], [10] — the payoff of the linear-programming degree-distribution optimization of [5].
3. **Standardization performance.** The 5G NR QC-LDPC codes operate within roughly 1 dB of capacity at block error rates of 10⁻² across the standardized rates and block sizes, with layered min-sum decoding at throughputs exceeding 10 Gb/s in commercial silicon — the decisive practical vindication of the "sparse + iterative" philosophy over algebraic decoding.
4. **Error floors.** Above the waterfall, frame error rates flatten into *error floors* caused by trapping sets — small near-codewords the iterative decoder cannot escape. Their enumeration via importance sampling and absorbing-set analysis is now a subfield of its own, and 5G base-graph design explicitly penalizes small trapping sets.

A persistent subtlety: density evolution is an *asymptotic* statement. At the short blocklengths of 5G signaling (*n* ≈ 10²–10³), finite-length scaling and cycle statistics dominate, and random-lift QC codes can underperform fully random ones unless shift values are optimized for girth.

## 6 Limitations

LDPC codes are not a universal solvent, and an honest survey must mark the boundaries.

- **Error floors.** Trapping and stopping sets impose floors that no amount of SNR removes; applications demanding ultra-reliable low-latency communication (URLLC) must pair LDPC with outer CRC/concatenation or accept the floor [7].
- **Short-blocklength penalty.** DE thresholds are infinite-blocklength quantities. At *n* < 1000, polar codes and carefully designed short LDPC/turbo codes are competitive or superior — part of why 5G assigns the control channel to polar codes.
- **Encoding complexity of unstructured codes.** General LDPC encoding is *O(n²)* via dense generator matrices; only structured families (QC, repeat-accumulate, approximately-triangular forms) admit linear-time encoders. The 5G double-diagonal extension exists precisely to solve this.
- **Decoder data flow.** Iterative decoders are interconnect-dominated; routing messages across the Tanner graph, not arithmetic, sets the area/power budget. This drove the QC compromise and the ongoing shift toward layered and stochastic schedules.
- **Theory–practice gaps.** DE assumes cycle-free neighborhoods and infinite length; EXIT charts assume Gaussian extrinsic information. Both are superb engineering approximations with known, bounded inaccuracies — not theorems about any finite code.
- **Degree-distribution fragility.** Capacity-approaching irregular profiles demand very high maximum variable degrees and are sensitive to quantization; hardware-friendly profiles sacrifice 0.2–0.5 dB for implementability.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The trajectory from Gallager's 1962 dissertation to the 5G NR data channel is one of the cleanest theory-to-practice pipelines in engineering: a sparse random construction, a local iterative decoder, an exact asymptotic analysis in density evolution, a design methodology in degree-distribution optimization, and finally a structured quasi-cyclic standardization that preserved the theory's gains while meeting silicon constraints [1][4][5][6]. The Tanner graph remains the lingua franca connecting all four stages — representation, algorithm, analysis, and implementation.

Open frontiers persist: finite-length code design for ultra-reliable short packets, trapping-set-free constructions with proofs, and neural belief propagation with DE-style analysis. Gallager's core wager — that *sparseness plus iteration* beats *algebra plus exhaustive search* — has survived every challenge for sixty years. The evidence suggests it will survive the next sixty.
