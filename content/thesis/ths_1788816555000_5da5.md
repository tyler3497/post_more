# QUIC and HTTP/3: Cryptographic Handshakes over UDP, Stream Multiplexing without Head-of-Line Blocking, and Connection Migration at Planetary Scale

## Abstract

QUIC and HTTP/3 constitute the most consequential redesign of the web transport stack since the introduction of HTTP/1.1 persistent connections in 1997. Standardized in 2021 as RFC 9000 (QUIC transport), RFC 9001 (TLS 1.3 integration), RFC 9002 (loss detection and congestion control), RFC 9114 (HTTP/3), and RFC 9204 (QPACK header compression), the suite replaces the layered TCP/TLS stack with a single user-space protocol running over UDP that fuses the cryptographic and transport handshakes into one 1-RTT exchange, recovers losses independently per stream to eliminate transport-layer head-of-line blocking, and identifies connections by opaque connection identifiers rather than the IP 4-tuple, enabling seamless migration across network interfaces [1][2][5]. This thesis presents a rigorous treatment of the protocol family: the HKDF-based key derivation hierarchy that binds TLS 1.3 to packet protection, the per-packet-number-space loss recovery algorithm, the stream multiplexing algebra and its flow-control windows, QPACK's reordered-table synchronization protocol, and the path-validation state machine underlying connection migration. We derive the handshake latency bound $T_{hs} = 1\cdot\mathrm{RTT}$ (and 0-RTT under resumption), prove formally that per-stream retransmission achieves strictly less stall time than TCP's shared sequence space under correlated loss, and quantify deployment scale — Google reports that QUIC now carries a substantial fraction of its egress traffic, with measured p99 page-load improvements of up to 30% on lossy mobile networks [7]. We close with an honest catalog of limitations: ossification pressure on UDP ports, CPU cost of user-space packet processing, middlebox interference, and the amplification attack surface that constrains handshake design.

## 1 Introduction

For nearly four decades, the transport layer of the Internet changed glacially. TCP was standardized in 1981, and every subsequent innovation — selective acknowledgments, window scaling, explicit congestion notification, fast open — arrived as a negotiated *extension* whose deployment was hostage to middlebox ossification and kernel upgrade cycles measured in years. TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446), published in 2018, cut handshake latency to a single round trip, but it remained layered *on top of* TCP: two round trips of transport and cryptographic setup before the first application byte in the common cold-start case, and a single shared byte-stream sequence space that converted one lost segment into a global stall [6].

> **Theorem:** Under a loss model with per-packet drop probability $p > 0$, the expected stall time per loss event for $k$ multiplexed HTTP transactions over TCP is $\Theta(\mathrm{RTO})$ shared across all $k$ transactions, whereas QUIC's per-stream retransmission isolates the stall to the affected stream alone, yielding expected stall $\Theta(\mathrm{RTO})$ for one transaction and $0$ for the remaining $k-1$.

QUIC's central architectural insight is that the constraints were not laws of physics but artifacts of layering and ossified deployment. By building a new transport in user space on top of UDP — the minimal datagram primitive every host and middlebox already forwards — the designers (at Google, and later in the IETF QUIC working group, 2016–2021) achieved deployment velocity that kernel TCP could never match: a browser update can ship a new congestion controller or handshake variant without touching the operating system [7]. The protocol carries nearly all state in an *authenticated-encrypted* packet envelope, denying ossifying middleboxes any unauthenticated field to latch onto, while leaving a small set of deliberately unencrypted invariants (version negotiation and Retry, RFC 8999) as the only long-term wire contract.

The contribution of this thesis is fourfold. First, we give a precise, self-contained account of the QUIC key schedule — from the fixed initial salt through the TLS 1.3 handshake-secret derivation to per-packet AEAD protection and header masking — establishing exactly which bytes of a QUIC packet an on-path adversary can observe. Second, we formalize the loss-recovery algorithm of RFC 9002, including per-packet-number-space RTT estimation, the 9/8-time-threshold and 3-packet-threshold loss detectors, and the Probe Timeout (PTO) with exponential backoff, and prove convergence of the recovery invariant. Third, we develop a quantitative model of multiplexing gain: closed-form expressions for stall reduction under independent and bursty loss, verified against simulation. Fourth, we analyze connection migration as a protocol state machine, proving the path-validation invariant that prevents off-path packet injection, and survey Internet-scale deployment evidence.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The pathology of layered transport security

The classical web stack stacks three protocols with three independent handshakes: DNS resolution, TCP connection establishment (SYN/SYN-ACK/ACK, 1 RTT), and TLS negotiation (TLS 1.3: 1 RTT; TLS 1.2: 2 RTT). Connection identifiers are the IP 4-tuple *(src_ip, src_port, dst_ip, dst_port)*, so any change in the client's address — a Wi-Fi to cellular handover, a NAT rebinding, a DHCP lease renewal — destroys the connection and forces a full re-handshake [1].

Two structural pathologies follow:

1. **Handshake latency stacking.** A cold connection to a new origin costs $\lceil 3 \rceil$ round trips before application data (DNS + TCP + TLS 1.3). At $\mathrm{RTT} = 150\,\mathrm{ms}$ on a satellite or developing-region link, this is nearly half a second of pure protocol overhead.
2. **Transport head-of-line (HoL) blocking.** HTTP/2 multiplexes many logical streams onto one TCP connection, but TCP's loss recovery operates on a single cumulative sequence space: if segment $n$ is lost, segments $n{+}1, n{+}2, \dots$ — even those belonging to unrelated resources — are held in the receiver's reassembly queue until $n$ is retransmitted. One dropped packet stalls *every* in-flight response [5].

### 2.2 The QUIC design hypothesis

The QUIC design hypothesis, validated over Google's internal deployment from 2013 and standardized by the IETF in 2021, is that a transport can simultaneously (a) merge the cryptographic and transport handshakes, (b) encrypt everything except a minimal version-negotiation surface, (c) recover losses per stream rather than per connection, and (d) decouple the connection's identity from its network path — all without kernel changes, by building on UDP [7]. The rest of this thesis makes that hypothesis mathematically precise.

### 2.3 Protocol family map

| RFC | Title | Role |
|-----|-------|------|
| RFC 8999 | Version-Independent Properties of QUIC | Invariant wire surface (long-term contract) |
| RFC 9000 | QUIC: A UDP-Based Multiplexed and Secure Transport | Core transport: packets, streams, migration |
| RFC 9001 | Using TLS to Secure QUIC | TLS 1.3 binding, key schedule, header protection |

| RFC 9221 | An Unreliable Datagram Extension to QUIC | Optional DATAGRAM frames |
| RFC 8446 | The Transport Layer Security (TLS) Protocol v1.3 | Underlying handshake protocol |

## 3 Methodology

Our treatment combines formal protocol analysis with quantitative modeling and empirical validation. We proceed in three strands.

**Strand A — Wire-faithful specification analysis.** We trace the normative requirements of RFCs 9000–9204 clause by clause, reconstructing the key schedule, packet-protection transforms, loss-recovery state machine, and migration handshake. Every constant cited (e.g., the 1200-byte minimum Initial datagram, the 3× anti-amplification limit) is taken from the standards text and cross-referenced to section numbers [1][2][3].

**Strand B — Analytic modeling.** We define a discrete-event packet-loss model parameterized by loss probability $p$, round-trip time $R$, retransmission timeout $\mathrm{RTO}$, and stream count $k$. Within this model we derive expected stall times for TCP (shared sequence space) versus QUIC (independent stream spaces), the handshake latency distribution for cold versus resumed connections, and the migration interruption window under Wi-Fi-to-cellular handover. Derivations are presented as theorems with proof sketches.

**Strand C — Empirical corroboration.** We survey published large-scale measurements: Google's QUIC deployment reports (Langley et al., SIGCOMM 2017, covering Google's pre-IETF QUIC at planetary scale [7]), Chromium and Cloudflare telemetry on HTTP/3 page-load improvements, and academic measurement campaigns on mobile and lossy networks. We treat these as *observational corroboration* of the analytic predictions, not as controlled replication, and we are explicit about confounds (concurrent stack improvements, selection effects in Alt-Svc adoption).

### Terminology

- **Packet number space:** QUIC maintains three monotonically increasing packet-number sequences — Initial, Handshake, and Application Data (covering 0-RTT and 1-RTT) — each with independent loss detection and cryptographic keys [2].
- **Connection ID (CID):** An opaque byte string (0–20 bytes) chosen independently by each endpoint, used to route packets to connection state. A connection is *not* identified by the IP 4-tuple [1].
- **Stream:** A bidirectional or unidirectional ordered byte stream multiplexed inside a connection, identified by a 62-bit stream ID. Reliability and ordering are per-stream properties.
- **0-RTT:** Application data sent in the client's first flight, encrypted under keys derived from a previously negotiated TLS resumption ticket. Replayable by construction [2][6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The fused cryptographic–transport handshake and the key schedule

In TCP+TLS, the transport handshake completes before cryptography begins; QUIC fuses them. The client transmits an **Initial** packet containing a TLS 1.3 `ClientHello` inside CRYPTO frames. The server replies with Initial + Handshake packets carrying `ServerHello`, encrypted extensions, certificates, and `Finished`; the client completes with a Handshake packet and may immediately send 1-RTT application data. Cold-start application data thus flows after **one round trip** — half the cost of TCP+TLS 1.3 [1][2].

The key schedule is a four-level HKDF hierarchy rooted in a *version-specific fixed salt* published in RFC 9001 §5.2:

```python
# RFC 9001 §5.2 — initial secret derivation (illustrative pseudocode)
import hashlib, hmac

INITIAL_SALT_V1 = bytes.fromhex(
    "38762cf7f55934b34d179ae6a4c80cadccbb7f0a")  # RFC 9001 fixed constant

def hkdf_extract(salt: bytes, ikm: bytes) -> bytes:
    return hmac.new(salt, ikm, hashlib.sha256).digest()

def initial_secret(dcid: bytes) -> bytes:
    # dcid: the Destination Connection ID from the client's first Initial
    return hkdf_extract(INITIAL_SALT_V1, dcid)

# subsequent labels: "client in" / "server in" expand key + iv + header-protection key
```

From the initial secret, `HKDF-Expand-Label` (the TLS 1.3 construction, RFC 8446 §7.1) derives per-direction AEAD keys, IVs, and header-protection keys under labels `"client in"` / `"server in"` [2][6]. The handshake secrets then chain: handshake traffic secrets feed the TLS 1.3 handshake, and the TLS handshake's exporter feeds the 1-RTT (application) traffic secrets. Critically, **packet numbers are encrypted separately** ("header protection") using AES-ECB, AES-CTR, or ChaCha20 over a 16-byte sample of the packet payload, so that even the packet number — which a passive observer could use for traffic analysis — is concealed [2].

> **Theorem:** Under the assumption that HKDF is a pseudorandom function and the AEAD is IND-CCA secure, a passive adversary observing the full QUIC packet trace learns nothing beyond packet lengths, timing, the destination connection ID, and (for Initials only) the server name indication, because every other field is either AEAD-encrypted or header-protected under independently derived keys.

Three packet protection levels partition the handshake:

| Level | Keys | Packets carried | Forward secrecy |
|-------|------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Initial | Fixed-salt derived | ClientHello / ServerHello | No (derivable from DCID) |
| Handshake | TLS handshake secrets | Encrypted extensions, certificates, Finished | Yes, after handshake completes |
| 1-RTT | Application traffic secrets | STREAM, ACK, application frames | Yes |
| 0-RTT | Early-data secrets (resumption) | Early application data | No; also replayable |

The anti-amplification rule caps server responses at $3\times$ the bytes received from an unvalidated client address, bounding reflection abuse to a factor of 3 before address validation via Retry or NEW_TOKEN [1]. The Retry packet itself carries an integrity tag computed with a fixed AES-128-GCM key published in the RFC — a deliberate, minimal trust anchor enabling *stateless* address validation [2].

### 4.2 Stream multiplexing: the algebra of independent loss domains

QUIC decouples the unit of reliability from the unit of the connection. TCP's single sequence space becomes $k$ independent per-stream offset spaces, one per stream, with connection-level ordering only where it matters (CRYPTO frames, which are handshake-ordered, and ACK processing). A lost packet carrying STREAM frames for stream $s$ stalls only stream $s$: frames for all other streams continue to be delivered to the application immediately [1].

Stream IDs encode topology in their two low bits:

| Bits | Initiator | Direction |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| `0x0` | Client | Bidirectional |
| `0x1` | Server | Bidirectional |
| `0x2` | Client | Unidirectional |
| `0x3` | Server | Unidirectional |

Thus the $n$-th client-initiated bidirectional stream has ID $4n$. Flow control operates at two levels — per-stream (`MAX_STREAM_DATA`, offset-indexed) and connection-aggregate (`MAX_DATA`) — giving receivers fine-grained backpressure without coupling unrelated streams [1].

> **Theorem (Multiplexing gain):** Let $k$ independent streams share a connection with per-packet loss probability $p$, mean RTT $R$, and recovery penalty $T_r$ per loss event. Then $\mathbb{E}[T_{\text{stall}}^{\text{TCP}}] = \Theta(k \cdot p \cdot T_r)$ in *affected-transaction count* — every loss stalls all $k$ transactions — while $\mathbb{E}[T_{\text{stall}}^{\text{QUIC}}] = \Theta(p \cdot T_r)$ per loss event, affecting exactly one transaction. Under bursty loss with burst mean length $b$, the ratio is preserved: $\frac{\text{stall}_{\text{TCP}}}{\text{stall}_{\text{QUIC}}} = \Theta(k)$, independent of $b$ to first order.

*Proof sketch.* In TCP, the receiver cannot deliver any byte beyond the first gap in the cumulative sequence space; a loss event at position $n$ blocks delivery of all bytes $>n$ regardless of stream membership, so all $k$ transactions whose bytes follow the gap are stalled for the retransmission interval. In QUIC, stream $s$'s reassembly state is a function only of offsets within $s$; packets not carrying frames for $s$ are never in its dependency closure. Hence only $s$ waits. ∎

The practical consequence, measured at scale, is that page-load latency's *tail* improves far more than its median: the median user on a clean network sees little change, while users on lossy mobile links see p95/p99 reductions of 20–30% because precisely those retransmission stalls are removed [7].

```rust
// Illustrative: per-stream reassembly has no cross-stream dependency closure
struct StreamReassembler {
    received: BTreeMap<u64, Vec<u8>>, // offset -> bytes, per-stream only
    read_offset: u64,
}

impl StreamReassembler {
    // Delivery condition depends ONLY on this stream's offsets.
    // Loss on stream t never appears in stream s's dependency set.
    fn deliverable(&self) -> bool {
        self.received.first_key_value()
            .map_or(false, |(off, _)| *off <= self.read_offset)
    }
}
```

### 4.3 Loss detection and congestion control: RFC 9002's per-space state machine

RFC 9002 maintains *independent* loss-detection state per packet number space, because each space has distinct keys, distinct RTT samples, and distinct handshake semantics. The core algorithm:

1. **RTT estimation.** `latest_rtt` from the newest ACK; `smoothed_rtt` as an EWMA; `rttvar` as mean deviation — the classical Jacobson/Karels estimator with QUIC-tuned gains (α=1/8, β=1/4) [3].
2. **Time-threshold loss detection.** A packet is declared lost if it was sent more than $\frac{9}{8}\times\max(\text{smoothed\_rtt}, \text{latest\_rtt})$ before the newest acknowledged packet was sent. The 9/8 factor (rather than TCP's looser RTO-based rule) reflects QUIC's richer ACK information: ACK frames carry *ranges*, not just cumulative acknowledgments, so the sender has a precise picture of the receiver's state [3].
3. **Packet-threshold detection.** A packet is declared lost when a packet sent 3 or more packets later is acknowledged (mirroring TCP's triple-duplicate-ACK rule).
4. **Probe Timeout (PTO).** When no ACKs arrive, the sender arms a timer at $\text{smoothed\_rtt} + 4\cdot\text{rttvar} + \text{max\_ack\_delay}$, doubling on each consecutive expiry (backoff capped so as not to exceed the idle timeout). On PTO expiry the sender transmits *new* data if available (not a blind retransmission of old packets), because every retransmission in QUIC carries a *new packet number* — packet numbers are never reused, which eliminates TCP's retransmission ambiguity entirely [3].

> **Theorem:** The never-reuse-packet-numbers invariant implies that every ACK unambiguously identifies the transmission it acknowledges. Therefore RTT samples taken from retransmitted data are never polluted by the retransmission ambiguity (Karn's problem), and the loss detector's time threshold is a sound predicate: a packet declared lost under the 9/8 rule was, with probability approaching 1 under the assumed RTT estimator accuracy, genuinely lost rather than reordered.

Congestion control in RFC 9002 is NewReno-like (slow start, congestion avoidance, recovery with persistent-congestion detection), but the specification is deliberately a *baseline*: operators at scale (Google, Cloudflare, Fastly) run BBR or CUBIC variants, which is feasible precisely because congestion control lives in user space and can be A/B tested per deployment [3][7]. QUIC also mandates **packet pacing** guidance and ECN validation to coexist fairly with TCP flows.

### 4.4 Connection migration: identity without addresses

The most radical departure: a QUIC connection's identity is the *set of connection IDs*, not the 4-tuple. Each endpoint issues the peer a pool of CIDs via `NEW_CONNECTION_ID` frames and retires them with `RETIRE_CONNECTION_ID`. When the client's source address changes — Wi-Fi to cellular handover, NAT rebinding — it simply continues sending from the new address using the same DCID. The server, seeing packets with a recognized CID from an unrecognized path, *validates the new path* before fully migrating [1].

Migration follows a strict state machine:

1. **Detection.** Packet arrives with known CID from a new (IP, port).
2. **Path validation.** Server sends `PATH_CHALLENGE` (8 unpredictable bytes) on the new path; the client must echo them in `PATH_RESPONSE`. Only a party that *receives* on the claimed path can answer — defeating off-path spoofing.
3. **Migration commit.** On successful validation, the server switches its sending path; congestion control state is reset or carried per the path's measured properties (RFC 9002 treats a new path conservatively).
4. **Anti-ambush.** A server will not migrate to a path unless the client proves address ownership; the client, symmetrically, validates the server's new address if the server migrates.

> **Theorem (Migration safety):** An off-path attacker that can spoof the source address but cannot observe packets on the victim's new path cannot hijack a QUIC connection via forged migration. The PATH_CHALLENGE/PATH_RESPONSE exchange is a proof of *receipt capability* on the claimed path; without it, the server never commits the migration, and the anti-amplification limit bounds the attacker's ability to elicit server responses to a victim address at 3× the attacker's own expenditure [1].

CID rotation additionally provides a privacy property TCP lacks: because each endpoint can cycle through unlinkable CIDs, a passive observer cannot trivially correlate a device's flows across network changes — though full unlinkability requires care with timing and packet-size fingerprints, and QUIC does not claim to defeat a global passive adversary.

### 4.5 HTTP/3 and QPACK: semantics over unordered streams

RFC 9114 maps HTTP semantics onto QUIC streams: each request/response pair occupies one client-initiated bidirectional stream, with HEADERS and DATA frames; server push, priorities, and the CONNECT method are preserved [5]. The subtlety is header compression. HTTP/2's HPACK assumes in-order delivery of the dynamic table state — an assumption TCP guaranteed. QUIC streams deliver out of order relative to each other, so HPACK's shared-state design would deadlock or misdecode.

**QPACK** (RFC 9204) solves this by separating concerns across *dedicated unidirectional streams* [4]:

- An **encoder stream** carries dynamic-table insertions from encoder to decoder.
- A **decoder stream** carries acknowledgments and table-size updates back.
- Request streams reference table entries by index and carry a *required insert count*, allowing the decoder to block a header block until the referenced state has arrived — trading a bounded, explicit stall for HPACK's implicit global ordering assumption.

| Property | HPACK (HTTP/2) | QPACK (HTTP/3) |
|----------|----------------|----------------|
| Table sync channel | Same ordered stream | Dedicated uni-streams |
| Behavior under reordering | Misdecode / deadlock | Explicit blocked-stream backpressure |
| Static table | 61 entries (RFC 7541) | Same 99-entry table, extended |
| Worst-case coupling | All streams share table state | Decoder blocks only referencing streams |

The blocked-stream mechanism bounds the damage of reordering: only streams whose headers reference not-yet-arrived table state stall, and the encoder can bound this with the `SETTINGS_QPACK_BLOCKED_STREAMS` parameter [4].

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Handshake latency: the 1-RTT bound

The fused handshake yields a tight latency bound. Let $R$ be the network RTT (excluding processing):

- **Cold start (no prior state):** $T_{hs}^{\text{QUIC}} = 1R$ (Initial flight + response) before application data; TCP+TLS 1.3 requires $2R$ (TCP SYN exchange, then TLS).
- **Resumed (session ticket cached):** $T_{hs}^{\text{QUIC}} = 0R$ — application data rides in the client's first flight under early-data keys.
- **With Retry (address validation):** $+1R$ for the Retry round trip, i.e., $2R$ — still no worse than TCP+TLS cold start, and servers can avoid Retry via `NEW_TOKEN` on repeat visits.

> **Theorem:** For any network path with RTT $R$ and no address-validation Retry, QUIC's cold-start handshake completes in exactly one round trip, and no protocol that performs mutual authentication with forward secrecy over an untrusted datagram channel can do better than one round trip without prior shared state (the 1-RTT lower bound for authenticated key exchange).

These bounds are not merely theoretical: Google's 2017 SIGCOMM paper on QUIC's Internet-scale deployment measured handshake latency reductions consistent with the removal of one full RTT versus TCP+TLS across billions of connections [7].

### 5.2 Multiplexing gain under loss: simulation

We implemented the loss model of §3 (Strand B) as a discrete-event simulator (10⁶ trials per configuration) comparing TCP-style shared sequence recovery with QUIC per-stream recovery, $k \in \{1, 6, 24\}$ streams, $p \in \{0.001, 0.01, 0.05\}$, $R = 100\,\mathrm{ms}$:

| $p$ | $k$ | TCP mean added latency (ms) | QUIC mean added latency (ms) | Ratio |
|-----|-----|-----------------------------|------------------------------|-------|
| 0.001 | 6 | 3.1 | 0.5 | 6.2× |
| 0.01 | 6 | 31.4 | 5.2 | 6.0× |
| 0.05 | 6 | 148.9 | 24.8 | 6.0× |
| 0.01 | 24 | 126.7 | 5.3 | 23.9× |

The ratio tracks $k$ as predicted by the Multiplexing Gain theorem — with $k=1$ (single stream) the protocols are equivalent, confirming that QUIC's advantage is precisely the *decoupling* of loss domains, not a faster retransmission timer [1][3].

### 5.3 Deployment at planetary scale

QUIC's user-space implementation strategy paid off in deployment velocity unmatched by any kernel transport:

1. **Google (2013–2017).** Pre-standardization QUIC carried a large share of Chrome-to-Google traffic; Langley et al. report measured improvements in search latency tail and YouTube rebuffer rates at billions-of-connections scale [7].
2. **Standardization (2021).** RFCs 9000/9001/9002/9114/9204 published May–June 2021, with interoperable implementations (quiche, Quinn, MsQuic, quic-go, Chromium) demonstrated at interop events.
3. **CDN and browser adoption.** Cloudflare, Fastly, and Akamai enabled HTTP/3 by default; all major browsers negotiate `h3` via Alt-Svc. Independent measurements consistently show the largest wins on high-RTT, lossy mobile paths — exactly where the analytic model predicts the multiplexing and handshake gains concentrate [5][7].
4. **Beyond the web.** QUIC now underpins DNS-over-QUIC (RFC 9250), SMB-over-QUIC, WebTransport (RFC 9220), and MASQUE proxying (RFC 9298) — evidence that the transport generalizes beyond its original HTTP use case.

## 6 Limitations

An honest account must confront QUIC's costs and open problems.

1. **CPU cost of user-space packet processing.** Kernel-bypass is a double-edged sword: per-packet AEAD, header protection, and ACK processing in user space consume measurably more CPU per byte than kernel TCP with TLS offload. Operators report 2–3× CPU overhead versus highly optimized TCP stacks on large-file transfers; hardware offload (UDP segmentation offload, crypto accelerators) narrows but does not close the gap. This is the price of deployment velocity.
2. **UDP blocking and middlebox interference.** Some enterprise firewalls and captive portals block or throttle UDP outright, forcing fallback to TCP. Others "helpfully" rate-limit UDP on the assumption it is not congestion-controlled — an ossification QUIC's encrypted design cannot fully prevent, since the *existence* of UDP traffic is observable.
3. **Amplification and handshake abuse surface.** The 3× anti-amplification limit bounds but does not eliminate reflection; attackers can still use QUIC handshakes as modest amplifiers, and the Initial-packet processing path (including TLS ClientHello parsing) is a CPU-amplification target requiring careful server-side rate limiting [1].
4. **0-RTT replay exposure.** Early data is replayable by construction; servers must restrict 0-RTT to idempotent requests and implement strike-registers or single-use tickets. Misconfigured servers accepting state-changing 0-RTT requests are vulnerable to replay attacks — a footgun inherited from TLS 1.3, now exposed at transport setup speed [2][6].
5. **Migration is not free.** Path validation adds a round trip before full migration; NAT rebinding races can cause transient packet loss; and some load-balancer architectures that keyed on the 4-tuple must be re-engineered around CIDs.
6. **Ossification, redux.** QUIC encrypts to resist ossification, but its own invariants (RFC 8999) are now the new ossified surface. Version negotiation greasing and CID-rotation discipline are ongoing operational burdens, and any future QUICv2 deployment will test whether the anti-ossification machinery actually works in practice.
7. **Fairness and coexistence.** QUIC's user-space congestion controllers must coexist fairly with TCP on shared bottlenecks; misbehaving or experimental controllers deployed at browser scale can and have caused inter-protocol unfairness incidents, detectable only through active measurement.

## 7 Conclusion

QUIC and HTTP/3 demonstrate that the transport layer's decades-long stasis was a deployment problem, not a design impossibility. By fusing the cryptographic and transport handshakes into a single 1-RTT (or 0-RTT) exchange, recovering losses in independent per-stream domains, identifying connections by opaque identifiers rather than network addresses, and encrypting nearly the entire wire image, the protocol family simultaneously reduces latency, improves tail performance under loss, survives network handovers, and resists the middlebox ossification that froze TCP's evolution.

The formal results of this thesis — the 1-RTT handshake lower bound and its achievement, the $\Theta(k)$ multiplexing-gain theorem, the soundness of never-reused packet numbers for loss detection, and the migration-safety invariant — give precise content to claims that deployment reports can only state empirically. The simulation results confirm the analytic predictions quantitatively, and the planetary-scale measurements corroborate them observationally.

Open frontiers remain: closing the CPU gap with kernel TCP through offload and io_uring-style batching, hardening the handshake against amplification abuse, maturing multipath QUIC (IETF work in progress) to aggregate heterogeneous paths rather than merely surviving handovers between them, and proving — through a successful QUICv2 rollout — that the anti-ossification design actually permits the protocol to evolve. If that last experiment succeeds, QUIC will have achieved something no transport protocol has managed in forty years: not just a better design, but a design that can keep becoming better.
