# Connectomics: Electron Microscopy Segmentation with Flood-Filling Networks, Agglomeration, and Proofreading

## Abstract

Reconstructing neural circuits at synaptic resolution requires segmenting densely interwoven neurites in teravoxel-scale volume electron microscopy (EM) data. This thesis develops the full computational pipeline of modern connectomics: *flood-filling networks* (FFNs), a recurrent convolutional architecture that grows individual neuron masks by iteratively extending a seeded field of view [1][4]; *agglomeration*, which repairs over-segmentation by greedily or learnedly merging supervoxels on a region adjacency graph [5][2]; and *proofreading*, the human-in-the-loop and increasingly automated process of detecting split and merge errors [8][7]. We formalize the segmentation problem, derive the split–merge decomposition of the Variation of Information metric that structures the entire error economy, and reproduce the landmark empirical results: FFNs achieving a mean error-free neurite path length of 1.1 mm on zebra finch SBEM data [1], and affinity-based pipelines surpassing human accuracy on the SNEMI3D benchmark with a Rand error of 0.0258 versus the human estimate of 0.0600 [2]. We close by analyzing the fundamental limitations — inference cost, domain shift, defect sensitivity, and the irreducible need for topological verification — that keep connectomics a hybrid human–machine science.

---

## 1 Introduction

A connectome is the complete wiring diagram of a nervous system. At the nanometer scale required to resolve the finest axons and spines, the only modality with sufficient resolution and field of view is volume electron microscopy — serial-section transmission EM (ssTEM), serial block-face scanning EM (SBEM), and focused ion beam SEM (FIB-SEM). A cubic millimeter of mammalian cortex at 4 × 4 × 40 nm generates on the order of **1,000 terabytes** [1], and manual tracing of a 100³ µm³ volume was estimated at over **100,000 person-hours** [1].

The reconstruction pipeline has therefore been automated in stages, each addressing a distinct failure mode: **segmentation** (assigning voxels to neurons, yielding an over-segmentation), **agglomeration** (merging fragments while avoiding *merge errors*), and **proofreading** (detecting and correcting residual errors, historically by humans, increasingly by automation [8]).

The asymmetry between the two error types structures everything. A *split error* divides one neuron into fragments; it is visible as a dangling skeleton end and is cheap to fix. A *merge error* fuses two distinct neurons; it corrupts circuit topology silently and is expensive to find. As Plaza, Scheffer, and Chklovskii observed, the economics of proofreading — the error mix a pipeline produces and the human time each error costs — determine whether a reconstruction project is feasible at all [3].

This thesis presents three interlocking contributions. **First**, we give an account of *flood-filling networks*, the recurrent architecture that replaced boundary-prediction pipelines as the state of the art for dense EM segmentation [1][4]. **Second**, we formalize agglomeration as optimization on a region adjacency graph, comparing greedy mean-affinity merging [2] with learned agglomeration classifiers such as GALA [5]. **Third**, we analyze proofreading as an information-theoretic resource allocation problem, from focused proofreading to fully automated skeleton-based correction [8], and reproduce the benchmark evidence that anchors the field.

> The central claim of this work is that connectomic reconstruction is best understood not as a single segmentation problem but as a *staged error-reduction process*, where each stage — flooding, agglomeration, proofreading — is designed around the specific error distribution left by the previous one.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Volume EM imaging

Volume EM assembles a 3D image from thousands of aligned 2D sections. In ssTEM, ultrathin sections are cut with a diamond knife and imaged in a transmission electron microscope; in SBEM, the block face itself is imaged and then shaved away inside the microscope; in FIB-SEM, a focused ion beam mills the surface between imaging passes. Typical resolutions range from 3–5 nm in-plane (sufficient to resolve synaptic vesicles and postsynaptic densities) to 25–70 nm section thickness (which introduces the *anisotropy* that makes naive 3D convolutions inefficient). The resulting volumes are *teravoxel-scale*: a mouse cortical column at 4 × 4 × 40 nm is roughly 10¹⁴ voxels.

### 2.2 The segmentation problem

Let the image be a function *I: Ω → ℝ* on the voxel lattice *Ω ⊂ ℤ³*. A segmentation is a partition *S = {S₁, …, Sₙ}* of *Ω* such that each *Sᵢ* corresponds to one biological object — one neuron, or one glial process, or one cell body. Two classical strategies dominate:

- **Boundary / affinity prediction followed by partitioning.** A convolutional network predicts, for each voxel, the probability that the voxel lies on a cell boundary, or the *affinity* between neighboring voxel pairs (the probability they belong to the same object). An over-segmentation is then produced by a seeded watershed on the boundary map or by thresholding the affinity graph, and supervoxels are agglomerated [2][5].
- **Object-centered recurrent prediction.** A network is seeded at one location and iteratively *floods* outward, deciding at each step which nearby voxels belong to the same object as the seed [1][4].

Both strategies deliberately begin with **over-segmentation**: it is far cheaper to merge fragments that should be one neuron than to split a segment that wrongly fuses two.

### 2.3 Error taxonomy and metrics

The field's standard metrics decompose exactly along the split/merge axis. Let *S* be the predicted segmentation and *T* the ground truth. The **Variation of Information** is

> VI(*S*, *T*) = *H*(*S*|*T*) + *H*(*T*|*S*),

where *H*(*S*|*T*) measures *over*-segmentation (split errors) and *H*(*T*|*S*) measures *under*-segmentation (merge errors) [2]. The **Rand error** — one minus the Rand index computed over voxel pairs — admits the same decomposition into *Rand split* and *Rand merge* scores. Challenge leaderboards such as SNEMI3D report these components separately because they drive different downstream costs [2].

### 2.4 Agglomeration and proofreading in brief

**Agglomeration** operates on the *region adjacency graph* (RAG): nodes are supervoxels, edges connect spatially adjacent supervoxels, and edge weights estimate the probability that the two fragments belong to the same neuron. GALA [5] learns these merge decisions with a classifier trained on region and boundary features, using *active learning* to query the human annotator only on the most uncertain edges — a design that simultaneously improves the classifier and *directs proofreaders to likely areas of error*, yielding 5–16× speedups in reconstruction [5].

**Proofreading** closes the loop. Tools such as webKnossos provide browser-based 3D visualization with supervoxel-graph editing — split and merge operations on segments — and have powered multiple microscale connectomic studies [7]. More recently, automated proofreading systems such as NEURD apply mesh- and skeleton-based heuristics to detect and repair errors without human intervention, making petascale datasets tractable [8].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Flood-filling networks

A flood-filling network [1][4] is a *recurrent* 3D convolutional network that segments one object at a time. The network maintains a mask — the current belief about which voxels belong to the target neuron — and a field of view (FoV) that moves through the volume. At each iteration, the network receives two input channels: the raw EM image within the FoV, and the current mask restricted to the FoV. It outputs an updated mask for the FoV, extending the neuron's reconstruction into newly observed territory. A scheduling queue prioritizes FoV positions near high-uncertainty or newly added voxels, so the flood front propagates along the neurite.

Formally, with FoV *F ⊂ Ω*, image *I*, and mask *m: Ω → [0,1]*, one iteration computes

*m′|_F = σ( f_θ( I|_F , m|_F ) )*,

where *f_θ* is a deep 3D CNN and *σ* a sigmoid. Training uses *soft targets* derived from ground truth: the network is taught not only to flood true neurites but to *stop* at membranes, so that thin processes are neither severed nor merged across synapses. Seeds are sampled densely so that every neurite is flooded at least once; overlapping reconstructions are reconciled by consensus.

```python
# Pseudocode: FFN inference over a volume (after Januszewski et al. [1])
def flood_volume(image, ffn, seed_points, fov=(33, 33, 17)):
    mask = np.zeros(image.shape, dtype=np.float32)   # global belief
    queue = PriorityQueue()
    for s in seed_points:
        queue.push(s, priority=0.0)
    while queue:
        pos = queue.pop()
        fov_img  = crop(image, pos, fov)
        fov_mask = crop(mask,  pos, fov)
        # recurrent step: two-channel input -> updated mask logits
        logits = ffn.predict(np.stack([fov_img, fov_mask], axis=-1))
        update = sigmoid(logits) > 0.5
        newly_added = update & (crop(mask, pos, fov) < 0.5)
        write_back(mask, pos, np.maximum(crop(mask, pos, fov), update))
        # schedule frontier voxels for continued flooding
        for v in frontier_voxels(newly_added, pos):
            queue.push(v, priority=-uncertainty(logits, v))
    return mask
```

The recurrence is the key innovation: unlike feed-forward affinity predictors, the FFN *remembers* the object's shape as it grows, allowing it to bridge brief image defects and to resolve ambiguous membrane crossings using long-range context [1].

### 3.2 Agglomeration on the region adjacency graph

Affinity-based pipelines [2] take a different route. A 3D U-Net predicts *nearest-neighbor affinities* — the probability that adjacent voxels belong to the same neuron — plus *long-range affinities* as an auxiliary task, with training data augmented by simulated defects (missing sections, misalignment, knife marks). Thresholding the affinity graph and running a watershed yields an over-segmentation; then supervoxels are agglomerated greedily by **mean affinity**: repeatedly merge the adjacent pair whose mean boundary affinity is highest, until no edge exceeds a threshold.

GALA [5] replaces the greedy rule with a learned classifier. For each candidate edge, features are extracted — region sizes, boundary moments, intensity statistics, shape descriptors — and a random forest (trained with active learning) predicts *P*(merge | features). The agglomeration then proceeds hierarchically, and the classifier's uncertainty directly prioritizes human review.

| Stage | Input | Output | Dominant error repaired |
|---|---|---|---|
| Affinity / FFN inference | Raw EM volume | Over-segmentation (supervoxels) | Boundary localization |
| Agglomeration | Supervoxels + RAG | Neuron fragments | Split errors |
| Focused proofreading | Fragments + uncertainty | Validated neurons | Residual merges |
| Synapse detection | Validated neurons | Connectome graph | Synaptic assignment |

*Table: the staged reconstruction pipeline. Each stage is optimized for the error distribution of its predecessor.*

### 3.3 Proofreading as resource allocation

Given a segmentation with estimated merge/split probabilities per edge, proofreading asks: *which edges should a human examine to maximize corrected topology per unit time?* Focused proofreading [8] formalizes this as **risk = impact × uncertainty**, reviewing edges in decreasing risk order. Skeleton representations make this efficient: a human verifies kilometers of neurite by inspecting branch points and endpoints rather than every voxel [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The FFN recurrent formulation in detail

The FFN's two-channel input is deceptively simple but carries the entire design philosophy: the image channel provides local evidence while the mask channel provides *object memory*. Applied recurrently, the network implements a learned, shape-aware region growing — a neural generalization of classical flood fill — that bridges brief image defects and resolves ambiguous membrane crossings using long-range context [1].

Three engineering decisions determine performance. **FoV movement policy**: a queue of candidate positions scored by the network's own uncertainty concentrates compute on growth cones, branch points, and ambiguous membranes [1]. **Seed coverage**: seeds are placed on a dense grid with stride smaller than the thinnest processes; redundant seeds are merged by mask-overlap consensus. **Multi-scale training**: scale-jittered training volumes counter the bias toward one structure size across the 50 nm–10 µm range of zebra finch neuropil [1].

The result on the 520 × 520 × 256 test volume: a **mean error-free neurite path length of 1.1 mm** — an order of magnitude better than prior methods on the same data — with only **4 mergers** observed across 97 mm of skeletonized test path [1].

### 4.2 Agglomeration as graph partitioning

Agglomeration is, at its core, a *graph partitioning problem with learned edge weights*. Given the RAG *G = (V, E)* with merge probabilities *w_e ∈ [0,1]*, the greedy mean-affinity algorithm [2] is hierarchical agglomerative clustering with average linkage: fast and simple, but myopic — a single erroneous high-affinity edge triggers a merge only proofreading can undo.

GALA's contribution [5] was to make the edge classifier *data-efficient and uncertainty-aware* via **active learning**: trained on a small labeled set, it asks the human to label only the edges where it is most uncertain. Each answer improves the classifier *and* corrects the segmentation — a cycle that accelerated reconstruction 5–16× in deployment [5]. Boundary *moments* (not just mean intensity) distinguish true membranes from sectioning artifacts, and region *convexity* features penalize implausible merges. A more global alternative, the **multicut** formulation, optimizes over all edges simultaneously, but exact multicut is NP-hard and too slow for graphs with 10⁸+ edges, so greedy and learned-local methods dominate at scale.

### 4.3 Why merges dominate the error budget

The split–merge asymmetry deserves a quantitative statement. From the VI decomposition,

> *H*(*S*|*T*) (splits) and *H*(*T*|*S*) (merges) are both measured in bits, but their *proofreading costs per bit* differ by orders of magnitude.

A split error announces itself: a skeleton ends abruptly mid-neuropil, or a branch dangles. Detection is *local* — the anomaly is visible at the error site — and repair is a single merge operation. A merge error, by contrast, is *topologically silent*: two neurons fused at one voxel look locally plausible everywhere except at the fusion point, which may be a single ambiguous membrane. Detection requires *global* reasoning about which branches belong together, and repair requires splitting a segment — an operation that must correctly reassign every voxel on both sides.

This asymmetry propagates through the whole pipeline design [3][8]: inference is tuned for over-segmentation so the residual budget is split-heavy; proofreading effort is merge-heavy, with focused proofreading ranking candidate *merges* by risk; and metrics are reported per-component, because a single scalar hides the economically relevant merge rate.

### 4.4 Automated proofreading: from skeletons to NEURD

NEURD [8] converts segmentations to meshes and skeletons, then applies morphology-based heuristics — soma detection, axon-versus-dendrite classification, fusion-artifact detection — to propose splits and merges automatically, while extracting per-neuron features for downstream analysis. Synapse detection closes the loop: SyConn2 [6] builds on FFN segmentations [1] with per-voxel synaptic junction, vesicle cloud, and mitochondria predictions to assemble a dense connectivity matrix. The architectural lesson: a merge error fuses two neurons' synapse lists too, so proofreading quality bounds connectome accuracy [6].

### 4.5 Scaling to petascale: systems engineering

Algorithms are necessary but not sufficient. Teravoxel volumes are processed in overlapping chunks (e.g., 482 × 481 × 236 voxels with 20–30 voxel overlap [6]) and stitched by consensus; FFN seeds are partitioned spatially and flooded in parallel with global reconciliation; and sharded multi-resolution pyramids serve both network inference and interactive proofreading. The sobering constant is *throughput*: even with superhuman per-voxel accuracy [2], a mouse brain at 4 × 4 × 40 nm is ~10¹⁴ voxels, and compute and storage remain the binding constraint on whole-brain mammalian connectomics [3].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmark evidence

The SNEMI3D challenge provides the cleanest head-to-head comparison. Lee et al. [2] trained a deeply 3D U-Net (1.5M parameters — an order of magnitude fewer than competitors) on nearest-neighbor plus long-range affinity prediction with defect augmentation, then agglomerated supervoxels by mean affinity. Results on the held-out test set:

| Method | Rand error | Trainable parameters | Test-time augmentation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ours (16 variants) | **0.02576** | 1.5M | 16 |
| Ours (8 variants) | 0.02590 | 1.5M | 8 |
| Ours (mean-affinity aggl., 1 variant) | 0.03332 | 1.5M | 1 |
| *Human estimate* | *0.05998* | — | — |
| DIVE | 0.06015 | 18M × 3 models | 16 × 3 |
| IAL | 0.06561 | 35M | 20 |

*Table: SNEMI3D leaderboard after the submission of Lee et al. [2] — the first automated method to surpass the human accuracy estimate.*

Two facts stand out. First, the automated result beats the human estimate by more than a factor of two — the first demonstration of superhuman dense reconstruction on a public EM benchmark [2]. Second, *mean-affinity agglomeration alone* (no test-time augmentation) already beats the human estimate, showing the agglomeration stage carries much of the accuracy. VI on the AC3 volumes drops from 0.935 (baseline) to 0.500–0.529 with the full pipeline [2].

For FFNs directly [1]: on zebra finch Area X SBEM (10 × 10 × 20 nm), the mean error-free neurite path length reached **1.1 mm** — roughly 10× the previous state of the art on identical data — with just **4 merge errors** in 97 mm of skeletonized test neurite. Since merges are the expensive error class (§4.3), the near-absence of merges is the result that matters most for proofreading economics.

### 5.2 A formal statement

> Theorem: *(Split–merge decomposition of Variation of Information.)* Let *S* and *T* be two partitions of the same finite voxel set *Ω*. Then VI(*S*,*T*) = *H*(*S*|*T*) + *H*(*T*|*S*), where *H*(*S*|*T*) vanishes iff *S* is a coarsening of *T* (no split errors relative to *T*), and *H*(*T*|*S*) vanishes iff *S* is a refinement of *T* (no merge errors relative to *T*). Consequently, any segmentation pipeline can be analyzed as independently minimizing two information-theoretic quantities with *different downstream costs per bit*.

*Proof sketch.* VI is the sum of the two conditional entropies by definition. If *S* coarsens *T* — every *T*-cell is contained in an *S*-cell — then knowing *T* determines *S*, so *H*(*S*|*T*) = 0; this is exactly the absence of splits. The merge case is symmetric. The cost asymmetry (§4.3) then implies that the optimal operating point of a pipeline is *not* the VI minimizer but the minimizer of *c_s·H*(*S*|*T*) + *c_m·H*(*T*|*S*) for proofreading costs *c_s ≪ c_m* — i.e., the pipeline should deliberately trade extra split bits for fewer merge bits. ∎

This is why every successful pipeline over-segments first: the theorem licenses a *biased* objective, and agglomeration plus proofreading are the machinery that spends the cheap split budget to buy down the expensive merge budget.

## 6 Limitations

1. **FFN inference cost.** Flooding is sequential per object: the FoV must traverse each neurite, dense seeding multiplies the work, and the scheduling queue parallelizes poorly. On petascale volumes FFN inference is among the most expensive pipeline steps [1][8].
2. **Defect sensitivity.** Missing sections, knife marks, and staining artifacts break local evidence. Defect augmentation [2] mitigates but does not eliminate the problem; consecutive missing sections still cause splits only specialized merging can repair.
3. **Domain shift.** A network trained on zebra finch SBEM does not transfer to mouse FIB-SEM without retraining. Each new dataset demands new dense ground truth — the bottleneck for entering a new imaging regime [3].
4. **Metric–objective mismatch.** Rand error and VI weight every voxel equally, but a merge on a long-range axon corrupts the connectome far more than a soma boundary wobble. Until metrics incorporate topological importance, benchmark optimization can diverge from scientific value.
5. **The irreducible human.** No pipeline is fully automated at scale. Automated proofreading [8] reduces but does not eliminate review, and the long tail of ambiguous cases is precisely where human judgment is most needed and hardest to replace.

## 7 Conclusion

Connectomic reconstruction has matured from a manual craft into a staged engineering discipline. Flood-filling networks [1][4] showed that recurrent, object-centered prediction could achieve millimeter-scale error-free tracing — an order of magnitude beyond feed-forward pipelines. Affinity prediction with mean-affinity agglomeration [2] showed that a 1.5M-parameter network could surpass human accuracy on SNEMI3D, proving that the agglomeration stage is as decisive as the network itself. Learned agglomeration with active learning [5] and automated proofreading [8] showed that the human-in-the-loop can be made dramatically more efficient — and partially replaced — by directing attention (human or algorithmic) to the highest-risk decisions.

The unifying principle is the *error economy*: splits are cheap, merges are expensive, and every stage should be designed around that asymmetry [3]. The VI decomposition of §5.2 makes it precise — the optimal pipeline minimizes a *cost-weighted* objective, deliberately over-segmenting to buy down merge risk.

Where a 100³ µm³ volume once demanded 100,000 human hours [1], automated pipelines now reconstruct cubic millimeters with proofreading measured in person-weeks [8] — and the gap between imaging throughput and analysis throughput, which defined the field's first crisis, is finally closing.
