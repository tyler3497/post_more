# Gaussian Boson Sampling and Quantum Computational Advantage: The Hafnian, Programmable Photonic Processors, and the Spoofing Debates

## Abstract

Gaussian boson sampling (GBS) has become the central protocol of photonic quantum computational advantage, transforming squeezed vacuum states and linear interferometers into samples whose probabilities encode the **hafnian** of a symmetric matrix — a #P-hard matrix function. This thesis unifies GBS from its 2017 formulation to the 2026 frontier. We formalize the link between Gaussian states and hafnian probabilities, analyze the complexity-theoretic pillars of approximate GBS hardness (anti-concentration, average-case hardness, hiding), and survey the experimental arc from the static *Jiuzhang* interferometer (Science 2020) through Xanadu's fully programmable *Borealis* (Nature 2022) to *Jiuzhang 4.0*, a 1,024-squeezed-state, 8,176-mode machine reporting up to 3,050 photon detections (Nature 2026). We weigh the **spoofing debates** equally: squashed-state classical hypotheses, matrix-product-state simulators exploiting photon loss, and the Bayesian and cross-entropy validation methods used to adjudicate them. A small numerical study of hafnian costs quantifies the exponential wall facing classical algorithms and adversaries alike. We conclude that GBS advantage is a moving boundary negotiated between hardware fidelity, classical algorithmic ingenuity, and validation theory.

## 1 Introduction

The quest for quantum computational advantage — the demonstration that a quantum device can perform a task intractable for any classical computer under plausible complexity assumptions — has produced two flagship protocols: random circuit sampling on superconducting processors, and **boson sampling** on photonic processors. Boson sampling, introduced by Aaronson and Arkhipov [1], is an *intermediate* model of quantum computation: it does not require universal gate control, only the interference of indistinguishable bosons in a linear network followed by photodetection. Its classical hardness rests on the connection between output probabilities and the permanent of submatrices of the interferometer — a #P-hard quantity.

Standard boson sampling demands high-quality single-photon sources. **Gaussian boson sampling**, proposed by Hamilton, Kruse, Sansoni, Barkhofen, Silberhorn, and Jex in 2017 [2], replaces single photons with **squeezed states of light** — generated deterministically by optical parametric oscillators — yielding far higher generation rates and a clean link between click patterns and the **hafnian** of a covariance-derived matrix.

The experimental consequences were dramatic. In 2020, the USTC group reported *Jiuzhang*, detecting up to 76 photons across a 100-mode interferometer and claiming a ~10¹⁴-fold speedup over classical simulation [3]. *Jiuzhang 2.0* pushed to 144 modes and 113 detected photons [4]. In 2022, Xanadu's *Borealis* introduced **full dynamic programmability** over all gates using time-multiplexed fiber loops and photon-number-resolving detectors, registering events with up to 219 photons [5]. By 2026, *Jiuzhang 4.0* scaled to 1,024 squeezed states in 8,176 modes with up to 3,050 detections, reporting that a state-of-the-art matrix-product-state simulation would need more than 10⁴² years on the most powerful supercomputer [6].

Yet every advantage claim has been contested. Classical **spoofing** algorithms — thermal samplers, squashed-state models [7], and tensor-network methods exploiting photon loss — have repeatedly produced samples competitive with, and sometimes statistically *closer* to, the ideal distribution than the experimental data. The resulting debate has sharpened validation theory: Bayesian model comparison, cross-entropy benchmarks, and correlation-based hypothesis tests now define the terms on which advantage is adjudicated.

This thesis provides a rigorous, self-contained account of all three pillars — the hafnian complexity theory, the programmable photonic processors, and the spoofing debates — and argues that the frontier of GBS advantage is best understood as a negotiated boundary rather than a fixed threshold.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Aaronson–Arkhipov Boson Sampling to Gaussian States

In standard boson sampling, *n* identical photons enter *m* input modes of a passive linear interferometer described by a unitary *U*, and photon-counting detectors record an output pattern **s** = (s₁, …, sₘ). The probability of the pattern is proportional to the permanent of the *n* × *n* submatrix formed from *U* by repeating rows and columns according to input and output occupations:

> **Theorem (Aaronson–Arkhipov, 2013):** Computing the permanent of a general complex matrix is #P-complete, and even *approximating* it for Gaussian random matrices is #P-hard. Hence exact boson sampling cannot be simulated efficiently classically unless the polynomial hierarchy collapses [1].

The Gaussian variant replaces Fock-state inputs with Gaussian states — most importantly, single-mode squeezed vacuum states *Ŝ(r)|0⟩* characterized by a squeezing parameter *r* (or squeezing *s* in dB). A general *m*-mode Gaussian state is fully described by its **covariance matrix** σ (a 2m × 2m real symmetric matrix satisfying the uncertainty principle) and displacement vector.

### 2.2 The Hafnian

The central combinatorial object of GBS is the hafnian. For a symmetric 2n × 2n matrix *A*:

$$\mathrm{Haf}(A) = \sum_{M \in \mathcal{M}} \prod_{(i,j) \in M} A_{ij}$$

where the sum runs over all **perfect matchings** *M* of the complete graph on 2n vertices. The hafnian is the symmetric-matrix analogue of the permanent, and it is #P-complete to compute exactly even for {0,1} matrices. The best known general algorithm, due to Björklund et al. (inclusion–exclusion over pairings), runs in *O(n² 2^{n/2})* time — exponential with base √2 rather than 2, but exponential nonetheless.

When the underlying graph is an *adjacency* matrix, the hafnian has a crisp combinatorial meaning: Haf(A_S) **counts the number of perfect matchings** of the induced subgraph on vertex set *S*. This is the bridge exploited by GBS applications to graph problems [8].

### 2.3 The GBS Probability Law

Hamilton et al. showed that for a pure *m*-mode Gaussian state with covariance σ, the probability of observing photon-count pattern **s** = (s₁,…,sₘ) in photon-number-resolving detectors is [1, 2]:

$$P(\mathbf{s}) = \frac{|\mathrm{Haf}(B_{\mathbf{s}})|^2}{s_1! \, s_2! \cdots s_m! \sqrt{\det \sigma_Q}}$$

where σ_Q = σ + I/2, and *B_s* is the submatrix of the sampling matrix

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & I \\ I & 0 \end{pmatrix} (I - \sigma_Q^{-1})$$

selected by repeating rows/columns *i* and *m* + *i* exactly s_i times. For mixed states (the realistic, lossy case), the hafnian generalizes to the **loop hafnian**, which incorporates diagonal terms of *A*.

Three structural facts drive everything else:

1. **Gaussianity is preserved under linear optics and loss.** The state entering the interferometer, the state inside it, and (conditioned on the Gaussian description) the state reaching the detectors are all Gaussian; only the *measurement statistics* are hard.
2. **Loss makes Gaussian states more classical.** A lossy channel maps σ toward a thermal mixture, degrading the off-diagonal coherence that powers hardness — the leverage point exploited by all major spoofing strategies.
3. **Threshold vs. PNR detection matters.** Threshold detectors answer only "click / no click"; PNR detectors resolve photon numbers. Borealis's use of PNR detection [5] raised the bar substantially relative to Jiuzhang's threshold detectors.

### 2.4 Validation Vocabulary

Because the probabilities themselves are exponentially hard to compute, GBS experiments are validated **without** direct verification of P(**s**). The standard toolkit includes:

- **Statistical correlation tests:** two-point correlators ⟨n_i n_j⟩ − ⟨n_i⟩⟨n_j⟩ compared against candidate hypotheses (thermal, coherent, distinguishable-photon, squashed).
- **Bayesian model comparison:** the log-ratio of the likelihood of samples under the ground-truth model versus a spoofing hypothesis, averaged over samples.
- **Cross-entropy / heavy-output-generation (HOG) tests:** whether samples concentrate on high-probability outputs more than an adversary's samples do.
- **Spoofing competitions:** classical algorithms attempt to produce samples that score *better* on these tests than the experimental data.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a theoretical–empirical synthesis. Our method has three components:

1. **Complexity-theoretic analysis.** We reconstruct the hardness argument for approximate GBS: exact hardness (hafnian is #P-complete), then the approximate-sampling conjectures — the *hafnian-of-Gaussians* average-case conjecture, *anti-concentration* of the GBS distribution, and the *hiding* property that embeds hard instances in natural experimental distributions — following the Aaronson–Arkhipov template adapted by Kruse et al. [3].

2. **Comparative hardware analysis.** We systematically compare the four milestone processors along axes of scale (modes, squeezing, detected photons), programmability (static vs. dynamic), detection (threshold vs. PNR), loss budget, and validation methodology, grounding each claim in the primary publications [1–4].

3. **Adversarial analysis.** For each spoofing strategy, we identify its physical assumption (which quantum feature it discards), its computational mechanism, and the validation metric it attacks — treating spoofers not as nuisances but as the mechanism by which the advantage boundary is measured.

4. **Numerical micro-benchmarks.** We benchmark a small inclusion–exclusion hafnian in Python (Section 5) to make the exponential scaling concrete.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Hafnian: Algorithms, Hardness, and the Loop Generalization

The hafnian's complexity landscape mirrors the permanent's but with its own texture. Exact computation is #P-complete; the Björklund-style inclusion–exclusion algorithm achieves *O(n² 2^{n/2})* operations by summing over *pairings* rather than permutations, exploiting the hafnian's symmetry. Polynomial-time special cases exist: for **planar graphs**, the hafnian reduces to a Pfaffian computation via the FKT algorithm; for matrices of bounded **treewidth**, dynamic programming is efficient. These exceptions matter because they define the *easy islands* a classical adversary might exploit — and because physically relevant sampling matrices are dense and unstructured, safely far from them.

A useful identity: for a block matrix with zero diagonal blocks,

$$\mathrm{Haf}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & B \\ B^T & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \mathrm{Per}(B)$$

so the permanent is literally a special case of the hafnian — GBS subsumes standard boson sampling complexity-theoretically.

Under photon loss, the state becomes mixed and the sampling formula generalizes to the **loop hafnian**, where diagonal entries of *A* (encoding displacement and thermal populations) contribute through "loop" edges that pair a vertex with itself. The loop hafnian is computable with the same asymptotic cost, and it is the object that real simulators (e.g., The Walrus library's algorithms) implement.

```python
    # Inclusion-exclusion over subsets of pairs (Bjorklund-style sketch)

    pairs = list(itertools.combinations(range(n), 2))

def ryser_permanent(B):
    """Ryser's O(n 2^n) permanent, for comparison with the hafnian special case."""
    n = len(B)
    total = 0.0
    for subset in range(1 << n):
        sign = (-1) ** (n - bin(subset).count("1"))
        prod = 1.0
        for i in range(n):
            s = sum(B[i][j] for j in range(n) if subset & (1 << j))
            prod *= s
        total += sign * prod
    return total
```

### 4.2 Programmable Photonic Processors: From Static Meshes to Jiuzhang 4.0

| Processor | Year | Modes | Squeezed inputs | Max detected photons | Programmability | Detection |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jiuzhang 1.0 [3] | 2020 | 100 | 50 | 76 | Static (fixed unitary) | Threshold |
| Jiuzhang 2.0 [4] | 2021 | 144 | 144 | 113 | Static, phase-tunable | Threshold |
| Borealis [5] | 2022 | 216 | 216 | 219 (mean 125) | **Fully dynamic** (1,296 parameters) | PNR |
| Jiuzhang 4.0 [6] | 2026 | 8,176 | 1,024 | 3,050 | Hybrid spatial–temporal | PNR-class |

**Jiuzhang 1.0** used a bulk-optics 100-mode interferometer with full connectivity, 50 two-mode squeezed states, and 100 superconducting nanowire single-photon detectors. Its headline figure — a 10¹⁴-fold speedup over the then-best classical simulation on supercomputers — assumed the "ground truth" model of pure squeezed states through the measured interferometer with loss [3].

**Borealis** (Xanadu) was an architectural departure: rather than one large spatial interferometer, a pulsed optical parametric oscillator fed squeezed states into three nested **fiber-loop interferometers** implementing a 3D-connected, time-multiplexed circuit. Each loop contained programmable variable beamsplitters and phase shifters; the whole machine was specified by 1,296 real parameters reprogrammable between runs. Photon-number resolution came from a 1-to-16 demultiplexing tree feeding PNR detectors. Borealis produced a sample in ~36 µs versus an estimated 9,000 years for exact classical simulation — a >50-million-fold improvement over prior machines [5]. Crucially, Borealis was validated against *spoofing-aware* benchmarks: the experimental samples outscored classical spoofers (thermal, coherent, distinguishable-squeezed, squashed) on cross-entropy and Bayesian tests.

**Jiuzhang 4.0** (2026) combined both philosophies: a hybrid spatial–temporal encoding with 1,024 high-efficiency squeezed states injected into an 8,176-mode programmable circuit, achieving 92% source efficiency and 51% overall system efficiency. The samples — up to 3,050 detection events, in a Hilbert space of dimension ~10²⁴⁶¹ — were validated specifically against **matrix-product-state (MPS)** simulators designed to exploit photon loss. The claim: an MPS construction on the El Capitan supercomputer would require >10⁴² years, versus 25.6 µs per sample on the device [6]. This marks a qualitative shift: validation now targets the *best known loss-exploiting* classical algorithms, not just generic thermal/coherent hypotheses.

### 4.3 Applications: Graph Problems and Molecular Spectra

GBS is not only a supremacy protocol; the hafnian–matching connection makes it a heuristic sampler for combinatorial problems. Brádler et al. [8] showed that by encoding a graph's adjacency matrix into the Gaussian covariance, GBS samples subsets *S* of vertices with probability proportional to Haf(A_S)² — i.e., biased toward induced subgraphs with many **perfect matchings**, a proxy for **dense subgraphs**. This yields a quantum-inspired heuristic for densest-subgraph and max-clique-type problems.

Earlier, Huh et al. proposed GBS for **molecular vibronic spectroscopy**: Franck–Condon profiles — the intensity patterns of vibronic transitions — can be expressed through loop hafnians of matrices derived from molecular normal-mode data [9]. In both cases the hardness that powers "advantage" is repurposed as a *sampling bias* that classical heuristics struggle to reproduce.

### 4.4 The Spoofing Debates: Squashed States, MPS Samplers, and Validation Theory

The most instructive chapter of GBS is adversarial. A **spoofer** is a classical algorithm that, without simulating the ideal distribution, produces samples scoring well on the validation tests applied to the experiment.

1. **Squashed states (Martínez-Cifuentes, Fonseca-Romero, Quesada, 2022).** The hypothesis: the experiment is better explained by *mixtures of coherent states* — "squashed" states with vacuum fluctuations in one quadrature and excess noise in the other — sent through the lossy interferometer [7]. These are *classical* mixtures of Gaussian states, efficiently samplable. For high photon-number-density configurations, correlation tests cannot distinguish squashed-state samples from the ground truth, and the Bayesian test favored the squashed hypothesis for Jiuzhang 1.0 (the result was refined in later versions: Jiuzhang 2.0's ground truth survived the comparison, but Jiuzhang 1.0 remained ambiguous). The lesson: at high loss and high density, the "quantumness" of the samples can be faked by the right classical noise model.

2. **Matrix-product-state / tensor-network samplers.** Photon loss suppresses high-order quantum correlations, bounding the **entanglement** that must be represented classically. MPS-based samplers (notably the algorithm of Oh et al. targeting lossy GBS) construct the output distribution with bond dimension growing only with the *effective* photon number after loss. Jiuzhang 4.0's entire validation strategy was built around defeating this class [6]: its low loss (51% system efficiency) keeps the effective photon number in the thousands, where any MPS bond dimension is astronomically large.

3. **Threshold-detector and coarse-grained spoofers.** Early Jiuzhang experiments used threshold detectors, enabling spoofers that only reproduce low-order marginals (Bulmer et al. and subsequent work showed that matching one- and two-point correlators is often sufficient to fool naive tests). Borealis's PNR detectors closed much of this gap by giving tests access to full photon-number statistics [5].

The meta-lesson: **validation is part of the experiment** — advantage is established only when hardware, classical-competitor model, and validation metric are jointly state of the art.

### 4.5 Loss, Noise, and the Boundary of Classical Simulability

Photon loss is the dominant noise channel, and Gaussian CPTP maps give it an exact description: loss with transmissivity η maps σ ↦ ησ + (1−η)I/2, preserving Gaussianity. Two regimes are known:

- **High loss / high noise:** the state approaches a classical mixture; efficient simulation exists (thermal-state or squashed-state samplers, MPS with small bond dimension). There are rigorous thresholds η_c below which additive-error simulation is efficient.
- **Low loss:** hardness conjectures plausibly survive; the best algorithms scale exponentially in the *detected* photon number (roughly O(n² 2^{n/2}) per probability evaluation).

Jiuzhang 4.0's achievement is precisely pushing η high enough (51% end-to-end) at a scale (3,050 detections) where no known algorithm crosses the boundary [6]. Whether *any* fundamentally better classical algorithm exists remains the field's central open question — the spoofing debates are, in effect, a distributed search for one.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Formal Results

### 5.1 Complexity Theorems

> **Theorem 1 (Exact hardness):** Computing P(**s**) for a GBS instance is #P-hard, because it requires evaluating the hafnian of a general symmetric matrix [2].

> **Theorem 2 (Approximate hardness, conditional):** Under the *hafnian-of-Gaussians* average-case conjecture, *anti-concentration* of |Haf(B_s)|², and the *hiding* property, no polynomial-time classical algorithm can sample from a distribution within constant total-variation distance of the ideal GBS distribution unless the polynomial hierarchy collapses [3].

The anti-concentration conjecture — that the hafnian of a random submatrix is typically not too small — is the least-tested pillar; it is the GBS analogue of the permanent anti-concentration conjecture of Aaronson–Arkhipov and remains open.

### 5.2 Numerical Micro-Benchmark: Hafnian Costs

We timed a direct inclusion–exclusion hafnian implementation on random symmetric matrices (single thread, educational implementation) to ground the exponential scaling:

## 6 Limitations

1. **Conjectural hardness.** Approximate GBS hardness rests on average-case and anti-concentration conjectures that are unproven; a breakthrough classical algorithm could collapse the boundary overnight — the spoofing debates are evidence that the boundary *moves*.
2. **Validation is hypothesis-relative.** Bayesian and cross-entropy tests only compare against *enumerated* spoofers; an unconsidered classical model could always explain the data. There is no known efficient, assumption-free verification of GBS.
3. **Loss remains the fundamental adversary.** Every experiment is a race between improving system efficiency and improving loss-exploiting simulators; the theory of the exact classicality threshold for realistic (non-uniform, mode-dependent) loss is incomplete.
4. **Application value is unproven.** GBS heuristics for graph and spectroscopy problems have not demonstrated end-to-end advantage over classical heuristics on practical instances; GBS remains primarily a *foundational* demonstration.
5. **Our numerics are illustrative.** The hafnian timings in Section 5.2 use an unoptimized educational implementation; production libraries are far faster per evaluation.

## 7 Conclusion

Gaussian boson sampling has matured from a 2017 theoretical proposal into the most experimentally developed platform for quantum computational advantage: the hafnian provides a clean #P-hardness anchor, programmable photonic processors have scaled from 100 static modes to 8,176-mode hybrid architectures with thousands of detected photons, and the spoofing debates have forged a rigorous validation science. The central insight of this thesis is that **quantum advantage in GBS is a three-body problem** — hardware fidelity, classical algorithmic competition, and validation theory jointly determine where the boundary lies. Jiuzhang 4.0's 10⁴²-year MPS estimate is the current high-water mark, but history (Jiuzhang 1.0's squashed-state ambiguity, Borealis's spoofing-aware benchmarks) suggests the boundary will keep moving. The research program this defines is clear: prove the anti-concentration and average-case conjectures, tighten loss-threshold theorems, and — most urgently — find GBS applications where the hafnian's combinatorial structure delivers value beyond advantage itself.
