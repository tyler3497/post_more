# Mean Field Games: The Lasry-Lions System, Propagation of Chaos, and the Mathematics of Large Populations

## Abstract

Mean field games model strategic interaction among a continuum of asymptotically negligible agents and supply the rigorous infinite-population limit of large symmetric stochastic differential games. This thesis develops the theory from its dual founding — the partial differential equation program of Lasry and Lions and the McKean–Vlasov program of Huang, Malhamé and Caines — through the forward-backward structure of the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman and Fokker–Planck system, the monotonicity conditions that secure uniqueness, the probabilistic reformulation through forward-backward stochastic differential equations, and Lions' master equation on the Wasserstein space of probability measures. We explain propagation of chaos as the game-theoretic law of large numbers, characterize the possible weak limits of approximate Nash equilibria, and describe the central limit theorem for fluctuations obtained through the master equation. Applications to crowd motion, price formation, systemic risk, and energy systems are surveyed, together with numerical methods and the open problem of closed-loop convergence without monotonicity.

## 1 Intro

A symmetric stochastic differential game with *N* players is, for large *N*, a computational and conceptual disaster. Each player's value function solves a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation posed in the joint state space of all *N* players, and the resulting *Nash system* of *N* coupled equations lives in dimension *N* times the individual state dimension. For *N* in the thousands — a crowd crossing a plaza, a pool of high-frequency traders, a fleet of thermostatically controlled loads — the system is hopeless.

Mean field game theory, introduced independently by Lasry and Lions [1] and by Huang, Malhamé and Caines [2], proposes that the population be replaced by its *distribution*. Because every player is negligible and all players are symmetric, a single player rationally reacts only to the statistical profile of the crowd rather than to the microscopic identity of each opponent. The *N*-body game is thereby reduced to a *two-body* problem: one representative agent facing one probability measure, coupled by a fixed-point consistency condition.

The program unfolds in three layers:

1. **The finite game.** *N* players choose feedback strategies; equilibrium is the Nash equilibrium of the *N*-player game, characterized by the Nash system of PDEs.
2. **The limit game.** As *N* grows without bound, the empirical distribution of states converges to a deterministic flow of measures; the representative agent optimizes against this flow, and the flow is regenerated by the agent's optimal response. This is the *Lasry–Lions system* [1] or, equivalently, the *Nash certainty equivalence* fixed point [2].
3. **The passage between them.** Approximate Nash equilibria of the finite games converge, in a precise weak sense, to solutions of the limit game; conversely every limit solution arises this way. This is propagation of chaos promoted to game theory [5].

The sections that follow develop these three layers in order.

## 2 Background

The mathematical raw material is a family of weakly interacting diffusions. For each player *i* among *N*, the private state evolves as

*dXᵢ(t) = b(Xᵢ(t), μᴺ(t), αᵢ(t)) dt + σ dWᵢ(t)*,

where the *Wᵢ* are independent Brownian motions, *αᵢ* is the chosen control, and *μᴺ(t)* is the **empirical measure**

*μᴺ(t) = (1/N) Σᵢ δ_{Xᵢ(t)}*,

the random probability measure recording the population profile. Player *i* minimizes an expected cost that depends on the trajectory of *μᴺ*, so interaction is *mean field*: only the distribution matters, never individual identities.

Convergence of empirical measures is measured in the **Wasserstein metric**. For probability measures *μ* and *ν* with finite second moment, the 2-Wasserstein distance is the cheapest transport cost over all couplings, and the space of such measures equipped with this metric is the natural geometric arena of the theory [3].

A **Nash equilibrium** of the *N*-player game is a profile of strategies from which no player can profitably deviate unilaterally. Because exact equilibria may fail to exist or be intractable, the limit theory works with **ε-Nash equilibria**: profiles in which no unilateral deviation improves any player's cost by more than *ε*. The fundamental question, resolved in full generality in the doctoral work systematized by Lacker [7], is what happens to *ε_N*-Nash equilibria with *ε_N* tending to zero as *N* tends to infinity.

Formally, the finite game is encoded in the **Nash system**: *N* coupled backward PDEs in the joint state space. The dream of the theory is that this monster collapses, as *N* grows large, onto a low-dimensional system for one representative player and one measure flow. The following Haskell sketch captures the essential object — the empirical measure as a functional on test functions — that survives the limit:

```haskell
-- Empirical measure of an N-particle configuration, as a functional
empirical :: [Double] -> (Double -> Double) -> Double
empirical xs f = sum (map f xs) / fromIntegral (length xs)

-- Pairing a measure against a test function: the mean-field observable
observable :: (Double -> Double) -> [Double] -> Double
observable = flip empirical
```

Classical propagation of chaos makes this collapse rigorous for non-strategic McKean–Vlasov particles; mean field games ask whether it survives when the particles are *rational* [2].

## 3 Methodology

The methodology of mean field games is a **fixed-point methodology**, and it comes in two dialects that say the same thing.

**Analytic dialect.** Freeze a deterministic flow of probability measures *m(t)*. Solve the backward Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation for the representative agent's value *u(t, x)*, obtaining the optimal feedback control as a function of the gradient of *u*. Feed this control into the forward Fokker–Planck (Kolmogorov) equation, which transports the initial distribution *m₀* along the optimally controlled dynamics, producing a new measure flow *Φ(m)*. An equilibrium is a fixed point *m = Φ(m)*. Existence follows from Schauder-type fixed-point theorems applied to the compact mapping *Φ* on a space of measure flows [1].

**Probabilistic dialect.** Freeze the same measure flow and solve the agent's problem by the stochastic maximum principle: the optimal state-adjoint pair satisfies a forward-backward stochastic differential equation of McKean–Vlasov type, in which the law of the forward component appears in the coefficients. Consistency — the law of the optimally controlled state must equal the frozen flow — is exactly the Nash certainty equivalence principle of [2]. The definitive probabilistic treatment, including the differential calculus on Wasserstein space needed to differentiate through the measure argument, is developed at book length in [3].

In practice the two dialects are bridged by iteration, the *fictitious-play* or *fixed-point* loop:

```python
def mfg_fixed_point(m, T, dt, tol=1e-6, relax=0.5):
    # m: discretized population density; T: horizon; dt: time step
    while True:
        u = solve_hjb_backward(m, T, dt)     # backward HJB, frozen m
        m_new = solve_fp_forward(u, m, T, dt) # forward Fokker-Planck
        if sup_norm(m_new - m) < tol:
            return u, m_new                  # fixed point: equilibrium
        m = relax * m_new + (1 - relax) * m  # damped update
```



## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Lasry–Lions System and Monotonicity

The analytic heart of the theory is the **Lasry–Lions system**, a forward-backward pair coupling the representative agent's value *u(t, x)* to the population density *m(t, x)* over a finite horizon *T* [1]:

- *Backward HJB:* −∂t*u* − *ν* Δ*u* + *H(x, ∇u, m)* = 0, with terminal condition *u(T, x) = G(x, m(T))*.
- *Forward Fokker–Planck:* ∂t*m* − *ν* Δ*m* − div(*m* · *D_p H(x, ∇u, m)*) = 0, with initial condition *m(0) = m₀*.

Here *ν ≥ 0* is the diffusion coefficient (*ν = 0* gives *first-order* mean field games, *ν > 0* the *second-order* case), *H* is the Hamiltonian obtained by minimizing the running cost over controls, and the coupling functions *F* (inside *H*) and *G* penalize crowding or reward conformity through the measure argument. The backward equation says: *given the crowd's future, what should I do?* The forward equation says: *given what everyone does, where does the crowd go?* An equilibrium is a pair *(u, m)* satisfying both simultaneously — a consistency that is far from obvious, since one equation runs backward in time and the other forward.

Uniqueness is secured by the celebrated **Lasry–Lions monotonicity condition**: the coupling must satisfy

*∫ (F(x, m₁) − F(x, m₂)) d(m₁ − m₂)(x) ≥ 0*

for all measures *m₁, m₂* (and likewise for *G*). Crowding-aversion costs — agents dislike being where the density is high — are the canonical monotone examples.

> Theorem: (Lasry–Lions uniqueness). Under the monotonicity condition on the coupling functions and standard regularity of the Hamiltonian, the Lasry–Lions system admits at most one classical solution *(u, m)*.

> Proof: Suppose *(u₁, m₁)* and *(u₂, m₂)* both solve the system. Cross-multiplying the HJB equations by the density differences and integrating against the Fokker–Planck equations produces an energy identity whose left-hand side is a sum of nonnegative terms — the monotonicity integrals — while the right-hand side telescopes to zero via the duality between the two equations. Hence each term vanishes, forcing *m₁ = m₂* and then *u₁ = u₂*.

Existence needs no monotonicity: Schauder's theorem applied to *Φ* yields a solution under mild continuity [1] — equilibria are easy to find, hard to promise unique.

### 4.2 The Probabilistic Formulation: McKean–Vlasov FBSDEs

The same equilibrium admits a purely probabilistic description. Freeze the measure flow *m(t)* and consider the representative agent's control problem. By the stochastic maximum principle, optimality is equivalent to solvability of a **forward-backward stochastic differential equation**:

- *Forward:* *dX_t = D_p H(X_t, Y_t, m(t)) dt + σ dW_t*, *X₀* distributed as *m₀*;
- *Backward:* *dY_t = −D_x H(X_t, Y_t, m(t)) dt + Z_t dW_t*, *Y_T = D_x G(X_T, m(T))*.

Consistency demands *Law(X_t) = m(t)* for every *t*: the frozen flow must reproduce itself. The system is then a **McKean–Vlasov FBSDE**, nonlinear in the sense of McKean because the coefficients depend on the law of the solution itself [3].

This is the language of Huang, Malhamé and Caines. Their **Nash certainty equivalence principle** [2] decomposes the game into (i) a single-agent problem parametrized by a mass-effect measure and (ii) a McKean–Vlasov equation determining it, with consistency between the two. The closed-loop character makes the resulting *ε*-Nash theorem genuinely decentralized: each agent needs only its own state and the common measure flow.

The weak formulation of [3], refined in [7], allows randomized strategies and a random measure flow, anticipating Section 4.4. The Rust sketch below shows the computational primitive — an empirical measure tracking its own moments — used by theory and simulation alike:

```rust
/// Empirical distribution of N particles; tracks first two moments
struct EmpiricalMeasure {
    particles: Vec<f64>,
}

impl EmpiricalMeasure {
    fn mean(&self) -> f64 {
        self.particles.iter().sum::<f64>() / self.particles.len() as f64
    }
    fn variance(&self) -> f64 {
        let m = self.mean();
        self.particles.iter().map(|x| (x - m).powi(2)).sum::<f64>()
            / self.particles.len() as f64
    }
}
```

### 4.3 The Master Equation on the Space of Measures

Both previous formulations eliminate the population except through a *fixed* equilibrium flow. Lions' **master equation** removes even that crutch: it describes the value *U(t, x, m)* of the game as a function of time, the agent's position, *and the entire population distribution* [4]. If *U* is known, the value of the game starting from any configuration is known — the master equation is the *mother of all equations* in the theory.

Formally it is a PDE posed on the product of Euclidean space and the Wasserstein space of measures:

*−∂t U − ν Δx U + H(x, Dx U, m) − ∫ Dx U(y, ·) · Dp H(y, Dx U(y, ·), m) m(dy) + cross terms = 0*,

where differentiation in the measure argument is understood through **Lions' derivative** — the lifting of the measure perturbation to a Hilbert space of random variables, the cornerstone of the Wasserstein differential calculus developed in [3].

The master equation earns its keep twice. With **common noise** — a Brownian motion felt by all players — the equilibrium flow becomes genuinely random and the master equation remains the right object, now with an infinite-dimensional noise term [4]. It is also the engine of *convergence*: the Nash system's solutions stay uniformly close to *U* evaluated at the empirical measure, yielding quantitative rates for the mean field limit [4]. The delicate first-order (*ν = 0*) case with common noise was settled by Cardaliaguet and Souganidis [8].

### 4.4 Propagation of Chaos, LLN, and Fluctuations

**Propagation of chaos** is the law of large numbers for interacting systems: as *N* grows, the empirical measure *μᴺ(t)* converges to a deterministic limit *m(t)*, and any fixed finite sub-collection of players becomes asymptotically independent with common law *m(t)*. For non-strategic McKean–Vlasov diffusions this is classical. The game-theoretic question is whether equilibrium *strategies* — which couple all players through everyone's optimization — preserve the collapse [5].

Lacker's characterization gives the definitive answer [5]:

> Theorem: (Mean field limit, Lacker). Approximate Nash equilibria of the *N*-player games, with vanishing approximation error, admit weak limits as *N* tends to infinity, and every limit point is a *weak* mean field game solution. Conversely, every weak MFG solution arises as such a limit.

The surprise is the word *weak*: even *without* common noise, the limiting flow can be **random** — the *N*-player equilibria can correlate through shared history in ways that survive as genuine randomness, so the classical deterministic solution concept is too narrow. This has no analogue in non-strategic propagation of chaos and is the deepest contribution of [5], systematized in [7].

Once the law of large numbers is established, the next order is the **central limit theorem**. Under smoothness assumptions guaranteeing uniqueness, Delarue, Lacker and Ramanan proved that the rescaled fluctuations of the empirical measure around the MFG limit converge to the solution of a *linear stochastic PDE*, with the master equation supplying the test functions that linearize the dynamics [6]. Along the way they obtained a new multidimensional CLT for McKean–Vlasov systems proper — a result of independent probabilistic interest — and verified the whole machinery on an explicitly solvable linear-quadratic example. The message is that the master equation is not merely a descriptive device: it is the *computational* tool that turns the limit theory quantitative.

A minimal TLA+ sketch conveys the discrete-time game whose limit the theorems above describe:

```tla+
---- MODULE MFG ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
CONSTANT N, Horizon
VARIABLES state, measure
Init == state \in [1..N -> Real]
     /\ measure = EmpiricalMeasure(state)
Next == \E i \in 1..N :
          state' = [state EXCEPT ![i] = BestResponse(i, state)]
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<state, measure>>
====
```

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The theory's sharpest statements can be grouped into four results, each with a distinct proof technology.

**Existence via compactness.** The map *Φ* sending a measure flow to the Fokker–Planck evolution under the HJB-optimal feedback is compact on the space of measure flows with the topology of weak convergence; Schauder's theorem yields a fixed point, i.e. a solution of the Lasry–Lions system [1]. No monotonicity is needed — existence is cheap.

**Uniqueness via monotonicity.** The energy identity sketched in Section 4.1 turns the Lasry–Lions monotonicity condition into a uniqueness theorem. The condition is economically natural (aversion to crowding) and mathematically sharp: without it, multiple equilibria genuinely occur.

**Limit characterization via weak convergence.** Lacker's theorem [5] identifies the set of possible limits of approximate Nash equilibria *exactly* with the set of weak MFG solutions, in both directions. The proof couples the *N*-player equilibria to a limiting martingale problem on the space of measure flows, using relaxed controls to keep the strategy sets compact.

**Fluctuations via the master equation.** The CLT of [6] linearizes the empirical-measure dynamics around the limit using derivatives of *U*; the limit is the unique solution of a linear stochastic PDE. In the linear-quadratic case everything is explicit — quadratic values, linear feedback, Gaussian flow governed by Riccati equations — the one regime solvable in closed form.

Numerically, the forward-backward structure invites specialized schemes:

| Method | Target | Regime | Guarantee |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monotone finite differences | Lasry–Lions system | Second order, monotone coupling | Convergent under monotonicity |
| Fixed-point / fictitious play | Lasry–Lions system | Potential games | Convergent for potential MFG |
| Semi-Lagrangian schemes | First-order system | Vanishing diffusion | Convergent with regularity |
| Neural networks (APAC-Net and successors) | HJB–FP / master equation | High dimension | Empirical; analysis emerging [4] |

Applications now span crowd motion and evacuation modeling, price formation in markets with many small traders, systemic risk in interbank lending networks, and demand response in power systems with large fleets of controllable loads [4]. In each case the mean field reduction turns an intractable *N*-agent computation into a tractable PDE or FBSDE problem — the theory's practical dividend.

## 6 Limitations

The theory's power rests on assumptions that are often violated in practice.

- **Monotonicity is restrictive.** Many natural couplings — herding, conformity, network effects — are *anti-monotone*, and there the Lasry–Lions system can admit multiple solutions. Selection among equilibria (by vanishing noise, by learning dynamics, by entropy criteria) remains largely open.
- **Closed-loop convergence is incomplete.** The Nash system describes players who observe one another; the first convergence results in this regime require the same monotonicity that guarantees uniqueness [5]. Without it, whether closed-loop *N*-player equilibria converge at all — and to what — is the theory's central open problem.
- **Common noise demands heavy regularity.** The master-equation machinery with common noise requires derivatives on Wasserstein space whose existence is known only under strong smoothness hypotheses; the first-order case was settled only recently [8].
- **The curse of dimensionality persists.** The master equation lives on an infinite-dimensional space; numerical methods that scale to realistic state dimensions rely on neural approximations whose convergence theory is still being built [4].
- **Calibration is hard.** Mean field models need the distribution of agent preferences and the coupling functions as inputs; estimating these from data, especially in economic applications, is an inverse problem the theory barely addresses.

## 7 Conclusion

Mean field games achieve something rare in applied mathematics: a *dimension reduction* that is also a *conceptual* reduction. The *N*-player Nash system, uncomputable and uninterpretable for large *N*, collapses onto the interaction of one representative agent with one probability measure. The two founding visions — Lasry and Lions' forward-backward PDE system [1] and Huang, Malhamé and Caines' McKean–Vlasov certainty-equivalence principle [2] — turned out to be the same object seen through analytic and probabilistic lenses, unified by the Wasserstein calculus of [3], lifted to universality by the master equation [4], and closed into a complete limit theory by the weak-convergence characterization [5] and the fluctuation analysis [6].

The frontier is clear: equilibrium selection without monotonicity, closed-loop convergence, rigorous high-dimensional numerics for the master equation, and statistical calibration to data. The field has moved from asking *whether* the limit exists to asking *which* limit is selected.
