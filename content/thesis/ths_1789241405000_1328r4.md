# Xenotransplantation and the Engineering of Immune Tolerance: α-Gal Knockout Pigs, Hyperacute Rejection, and the Regulatory Frontier

## Abstract

The chronic scarcity of human organs — more than 103,000 people on the United States waitlist, the overwhelming majority awaiting kidneys [5] — has pushed xenotransplantation from theoretical aspiration to clinical reality. The immunological barrier between *Sus scrofa* and *Homo sapiens* is dominated by preformed human antibodies against the porcine α-Gal trisaccharide, synthesized by α-1,3-galactosyltransferase (*GGTA1*), which precipitate **hyperacute rejection** within minutes of reperfusion. Multiplex CRISPR-Cas9 editing now permits simultaneous ablation of the three dominant xenoantigen genes (*GGTA1*, *CMAH*, *B4GALNT2*), inactivation of porcine endogenous retroviruses, and knock-in of human complement- and coagulation-regulatory transgenes such as *hCD46*, *hCD55*, *hTHBD*, and *hEPCR*. Between January 2022 and late 2024, two pig hearts and four pig kidneys were transplanted into living humans under FDA expanded-access pathways, while decedent studies at NYU Langone demonstrated up to 61 days of rejection-free function of a single-gene GalSafe knockout kidney [2,3]. This thesis synthesizes the molecular immunology of xenograft rejection, the engineering logic of current donor-pig designs, the evidentiary record of first-in-human trials, and the regulatory architecture that will govern the coming multicenter trials in which more than seventy patients are expected to receive pig kidneys [6].

## 1 Introduction

Xenotransplantation is among the oldest dreams of transplant surgery. In 1905, Mathieu Jaboulay attempted to transplant pig kidneys into human patients with end-stage renal disease; the organs failed within hours [7]. For the next century, the field advanced largely through negative results: non-human primate experiments in the 1990s established that porcine organs are destroyed by hyperacute rejection in minutes to hours, driven by preformed human xenoreactive natural antibodies. The arrival of genome editing — first zinc-finger nucleases and TALENs, then multiplex CRISPR-Cas9 — transformed the engineering substrate itself: rather than suppressing the human immune system ever harder, the donor animal could be redesigned.

The modern era crystallized in three stages. First, the *GGTA1* knockout pig, developed in the early 2000s and cleared by the FDA as the **GalSafe** pig in December 2020, demonstrated that eliminating a single carbohydrate epitope could abolish the immediate antibody-mediated catastrophe [3]. Second, decedent (brain-dead recipient) studies from 2021 through 2023 — performed at NYU Langone, the University of Alabama at Birmingham, and elsewhere — showed that single-gene-knockout kidneys could function for one to two months with conventional immunosuppression, with rejection episodes detectable five days before they were clinically visible through blood biomarkers [3,7]. Third, between January 2022 and January 2025, two gene-edited pig hearts and four gene-edited pig kidneys were transplanted into living humans under FDA expanded-access (compassionate use) pathways, with one eGenesis kidney sustaining its recipient for nine months and bridging him to an eventual human kidney [2,6].

> **Theorem (Tolerance by Design):** *A xenograft whose donor genome eliminates the three principal carbohydrate xenoantigens and expresses human complement- and coagulation-regulatory proteins will, under clinical immunosuppression, avoid hyperacute and acute humoral rejection; residual failure modes are therefore dominated by innate cellular recognition, elicited T-cell responses, and latent zoonosis rather than preformed antibody catastrophe.*

This thesis argues that xenotransplantation has crossed the threshold from proof-of-concept to clinical modality, and that the remaining barriers are principally **engineering and regulatory** rather than immunological: optimizing the pig genotype, quantifying long-term zoonotic risk, and constructing trial frameworks that can validate a fundamentally new class of therapeutic.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The organ shortage

More than 103,000 Americans are on the national transplant waitlist, nearly 88,000 of them awaiting a kidney; in 2022, only about 26,000 received one, while nearly 808,000 people in the United States live with end-stage kidney disease [3]. In cardiac transplantation, only 3,500–4,000 human hearts are transplanted annually in the United States against hundreds of thousands of patients with end-stage heart failure [2]. Roughly a dozen waitlist patients die each day. Pigs are physiologically attractive donors: organ size is commensurate with human anatomy, gestation is short, litters are large, and porcine physiology closely parallels human cardiovascular and renal function. Pigs have long contributed to human medicine through heart valves and skin grafts, but whole-organ transplantation was historically blocked by the immune barrier [8].

### 2.2 The xenoantigen triad

Humans, apes, and Old World monkeys lost functional *GGTA1* millions of years ago; consequently, all humans harbor preformed anti-α-Gal antibodies (IgM and IgG) at high titer, comprising roughly 1% of circulating immunoglobulin. Two further carbohydrate antigens were characterized in the 2000s:

| Antigen | Gene | Epitope | Antibody class | Significance |
|---------|------|---------|----------------|--------------|
| α-Gal | *GGTA1* | Galα1-3Galβ1-4GlcNAc | IgM, IgG | Dominant; hyperacute rejection [3] |
| Neu5Gc | *CMAH* | N-glycolylneuraminic acid | IgG, IgM | Humans lack CMAH; dietary uptake complicates biology |
| Sda-like | *B4GALNT2* | GalNAc-containing glycan | IgM, IgG | Non-Gal rejection in GGTA1-KO organs |

Knocking out all three genes — the **triple-knockout (TKO)** pig — removes the targets of the great majority of preformed human xenoreactive antibodies.

### 2.3 Rejection phases

1. **Hyperacute rejection (minutes–hours):** preformed antibody binds graft endothelium, complement cascade activates via the classical pathway, the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) lyses endothelial cells, and the organ infarcts before it can function [2].
2. **Acute humoral xenograft rejection (days–weeks):** elicited antibodies against non-carbohydrate antigens and coagulation dysregulation produce thrombotic microangiopathy [6].
3. **Acute cellular rejection (weeks–months):** human T cells, macrophages, and NK cells infiltrate the graft. The NYU two-month decedent study identified three waves: an innate response at postoperative day 21, a macrophage-driven response at day 33, and a T-cell response at day 45 [7].
4. **Chronic rejection (months–years):** graft vasculopathy and interstitial fibrosis — the failure mode that will ultimately define xenograft durability [6].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis synthesizes primary clinical reports (University of Maryland cardiac xenotransplantation program; Massachusetts General Hospital eGenesis kidney program; NYU Langone decedent studies), regulatory actions (FDA compassionate-use authorizations, December 2020 GalSafe approval), and genomic engineering disclosures from donor-pig developers (Revivicor, eGenesis). The methodological approach is threefold:

- **Comparative genotype analysis:** Revivicor's 10-edit pig (used in the two cardiac cases) is contrasted with eGenesis's 69-edit Yucatan minipig (used for Richard Slayman's March 2024 kidney): three glycan-antigen knockouts, seven human transgenes regulating immunity, coagulation, and complement, and inactivation of porcine endogenous retroviruses [4,5,8].
- **Event-sequence reconstruction:** a milestone timeline from the September 2021 GalSafe decedent kidney through the 2025–2026 multicenter kidney trials is assembled from institutional reports [2,3,4,5,6,7,8].
- **Failure-mode taxonomy:** each reported graft loss is mapped to a rejection phase and a candidate molecular mechanism, following the analytical framework of the NYU multi-omics decedent study [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Ablating the carbohydrate barrier: GGTA1, CMAH, B4GALNT2

The *GGTA1* knockout alone transforms the immunological calculus. In the NYU decedent studies, kidneys from single-gene-knockout GalSafe pigs functioned for 32 to 61 days with standard immunosuppression, avoiding hyperacute rejection entirely — evidence that "just eliminating the gene that triggers a hyperacute rejection may be enough" in kidneys [3]. The eGenesis platform extends this to the triple knockout: removing *CMAH* eliminates Neu5Gc, against which humans mount antibodies partly due to dietary red-meat uptake, and *B4GALNT2* ablation removes the Sda-like antigen responsible for residual non-Gal rejection. The triple knockout is now the consensus baseline genotype for clinical xenotransplantation [4,8].

### 4.2 Humanizing regulation: complement, coagulation, and the macrophage checkpoint

Knocking out antigens is necessary but not sufficient; the graft must actively *regulate* the human innate immune system. Current donor pigs express a cassette of human transgenes:

- **Complement regulators:** *hCD46* (membrane cofactor protein), *hCD55* (decay-accelerating factor), and *hCD59* (protectin) interrupt the complement cascade at the C3-convertase and membrane-attack-complex stages, respectively.
- **Coagulation compatibilizers:** porcine endothelium is profoundly pro-thrombotic in human blood. *hTHBD* (thrombomodulin) and *hEPCR* (endothelial protein C receptor) restore the activated-protein-C anticoagulant pathway, suppressing the thrombotic microangiopathy that characterized early acute humoral failures [6].
- **Phagocytic checkpoint:** *hCD47* engages the human macrophage receptor SIRPα, delivering the "don't eat me" signal that suppresses xenogeneic phagocytosis — mechanistically aligned with the macrophage-driven rejection wave observed at postoperative day 33 in the NYU decedent analysis [7].

The Lisa Pisano case added a further tolerance strategy: transplantation of the pig **thymus gland fused with the kidney**, intended to educate the recipient's T-cell repertoire toward donor tolerance and ultimately reduce immunosuppression requirements [5].

### 4.3 PERV inactivation and the zoonosis calculus

Porcine endogenous retroviruses (PERVs) are integrated in the pig germline at ~25–60 copies and cannot be eliminated by designated-pathogen-free breeding. Using multiplex CRISPR-Cas9, eGenesis inactivated all replication-competent PERV loci in its donor line, accounting for the bulk of its 69 genomic edits [4]. No PERV transmission was detected in any first-in-human recipient, but the statistical power of *n* = 6 living recipients is negligible for a low-probability zoonotic event. Long-term surveillance protocols — analogous to those for xenotransplantation product recipients under FDA guidance — will be the price of clinical translation [6].

### 4.4 The immunosuppression problem

Decedent studies demonstrated that conventional regimens (anti-thymocyte globulin induction, tacrolimus, mycophenolate, steroids) plus complement and co-stimulation blockade can control rejection for two months [3,7]. Yet the David Bennett case illustrated the central dilemma: the balance between suppressing rejection and controlling infection proved fatal, with recurrent infectious episodes preceding multi-organ failure at day 60 [2]. In the second cardiac recipient, Lawrence Faucette, porcine cytomegalovirus was detected in the graft — a failure of donor screening as much as of immunosuppression. The field's open question is whether thymic co-transplantation or mixed chimerism can produce durable tolerance and permit immunosuppression withdrawal [5,6].

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Formal Arguments

The evidentiary record, assembled chronologically:

1. **September 25, 2021:** Robert Montgomery's team at NYU Langone attaches a GalSafe (*GGTA1*-knockout) pig kidney to a brain-dead recipient; the organ produces urine and normalizes creatinine without hyperacute rejection [3].
2. **January 7, 2022:** Bartley Griffith and Muhammad Mohiuddin transplant a 10-edit Revivicor pig heart into David Bennett under FDA compassionate use; the heart functions for 60 days, the first gene-edited animal heart to sustain a living human [2].
3. **Summer 2022:** two further pig-heart transplants into decedents at NYU demonstrate three-day intensive physiological testing [3].
4. **September 2023:** second living pig-heart recipient, Lawrence Faucette, survives six weeks [8].
5. **March 16, 2024:** Leonardo Riella and Tatsuo Kawai's team at Massachusetts General Hospital transplant an eGenesis 69-edit kidney into Richard Slayman — the first living recipient of a CRISPR-edited pig kidney; the organ functions immediately, though Slayman dies two months later of causes the team states were unrelated to the graft [4,8].
6. **April 12, 2024:** Montgomery transplants a thymus-fused GalSafe kidney plus an LVAD into Lisa Pisano, testing the dual strategy of mechanical circulatory support with xenorenal transplantation [5].
7. **November 25, 2024:** Towana Looney receives a pig kidney and is discharged in 11 days with normal function — "the picture of health" [8].
8. **2025–2026:** two multicenter clinical trials of pig kidneys begin, with more than 70 patients expected to receive organs; one eGenesis kidney survived nine months in a living recipient, bridging to a human allograft [6].

> **Theorem (Bridging Sufficiency):** *Even a xenograft with sub-allograft durability is clinically sufficient if its median functional survival exceeds the expected wait for a human organ — the nine-month eGenesis kidney that bridged its recipient to a human kidney establishes xenotransplantation as a bridge therapy independent of destination-therapy durability [6].*

The formal argument for clinical translation now rests on three pillars: (i) hyperacute rejection is an engineering problem that has been solved, with zero occurrences across all genetically edited cases; (ii) acute rejection is detectable days before clinical manifestation via multi-omics biomarkers, enabling preemptive therapy [7]; and (iii) trial-scale evidence (n > 70) will, for the first time, permit powered inference on graft survival distributions [6].

## 6 Limitations

This analysis is constrained by the thinness of the evidentiary base: six living recipients, heterogeneous genotypes, and no randomized controls. Several limitations are structural. **First**, decedent models cannot fully recapitulate living physiology — brain-dead recipients lack normal neurohormonal tone, and, as Mohiuddin cautioned, "it is not clear how closely a deceased body will mimic a live patient's reactions" [3]. **Second**, the zoonosis risk from PERV or porcine cytomegalovirus cannot be quantified from six recipients; a single transmission event could halt the field. **Third**, chronic rejection of xenografts is entirely uncharacterized in humans — no graft has survived long enough to develop it, and the primate data suggest graft vasculopathy will be the durability ceiling [6]. **Fourth**, the regulatory pathway is unprecedented: the FDA must license a *living, evolving* product line whose every new edit iteration arguably requires re-validation, creating tension between innovation and assurance. **Fifth**, equity concerns loom: if pig organs reach the market, allocation must not replicate the disparities of the current waitlist, where end-stage renal disease is 3.8 times more common among Black Americans [8]. Finally, animal-welfare and religious-objection dimensions (porcine tissue is unacceptable to some observant Muslim and Jewish patients) constrain the addressable population and the ethical framing of consent [6].

## 7 Conclusion

Xenotransplantation has, within five years, traveled from the first GalSafe decedent kidney to multicenter clinical trials — a pace comparable to the fastest translational arcs in modern medicine. The immunological core of the problem, hyperacute rejection, has yielded to genome engineering: not a single hyperacutely rejected gene-edited organ has been reported. What remains is an engineering and regulatory program: converging on the minimal sufficient pig genotype, proving long-term zoonotic safety, achieving tolerance that permits immunosuppression minimization, and building trial and surveillance infrastructure commensurate with a product that is simultaneously a drug, a device, and a living animal. The nine-month eGenesis kidney [6] and the discharged, healthy Towana Looney [8] suggest the destination is in view: an on-demand organ supply that could, within five years, serve patients on the kidney and heart waitlists as a matter of routine [6]. The century that began with Jaboulay's hours-long failures may end with the organ shortage itself becoming a historical artifact.
