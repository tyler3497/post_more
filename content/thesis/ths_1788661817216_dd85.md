# MLIR: Progressive Lowering Through Multi-Level Dialects — Linalg on Tensors, Affine Loop Transformations, and Retargetable Code Generation

## Abstract

This thesis studies **MLIR** (Multi-Level Intermediate Representation) and the *progressive lowering* discipline that compiles domain-specific computation through a lattice of coexisting abstraction levels [1]. We analyze the **Linalg dialect** on tensor types as a structured-operation framework capturing dense linear algebra without premature commitment to loop structure [3][9]; the **Affine dialect** as a restricted, SSA-embedded polyhedral representation enabling exact dependence analysis and affine loop transformations — tiling, fusion, interchange, vectorization [4][5]; and the lowering machinery — partial dialect conversion, bufferization, and the transform dialect — that orchestrates level-by-level lowering to **LLVM IR** [6][7]. We formalize progressive lowering as a sequence of dialect-respecting rewrite systems and argue it yields composable, independently testable pipelines. Finally, we examine **retargetability**: a single high-level pipeline parameterized by tile sizes and vector widths producing competitive code for x86-64, AArch64, RISC-V vector extensions, and GPUs, with published case studies reaching near-library performance from a single `linalg.matmul` source operation [5][7][10].

## 1. Introduction

Traditional compiler infrastructures — from the earliest retargetable compilers to **LLVM** itself — are built around a single, fixed intermediate representation [2]. The front end lowers source semantics into this IR, the middle end optimizes within it, and the back end lowers it to machine code. This *single-level* design served the industry well for decades, but it imposes a fundamental information-loss dilemma: high-level semantic knowledge (tensor shapes, stencil access patterns, loop nesting legality) must either be encoded awkwardly into the low-level IR — where it is fragile and easily destroyed by subsequent passes — or recovered by heroic *raising* analyses that are undecidable in the general case [8].

**MLIR** (Multi-Level Intermediate Representation), introduced by Lattner et al. at CGO 2021 [1], inverts this design. Instead of one IR, MLIR is an *infrastructure for defining IRs*: a hybrid of operations, types, attributes, regions, and a pass manager in which multiple abstraction levels — called **dialects** — coexist in a single module and are lowered progressively, one level at a time, toward the target. The central discipline is **progressive lowering**: each compilation stage lowers only one abstraction level, preserving all semantic information relevant to later stages, and each pass reasons at exactly the abstraction level where its decisions are natural [1][9].

> **Thesis statement.** Progressive lowering through MLIR's dialect lattice — with Linalg structured operations on tensors at the domain level, affine loop nests at the polyhedral level, and retargetable vector/memref/LLVM dialects at the code generation level — produces a compilation framework that is simultaneously more *modular* (passes are small, dialect-local, and independently testable), more *expressive* (domain knowledge survives lowering), and more *performant* (competitive with hand-tuned libraries) than any single-level IR design [1][5][7].

This thesis develops that claim in four parts. First, we formalize the MLIR core: operations, dialects, regions, and the trait/interface system. Second, we study the **Linalg dialect** — its structured-op philosophy, OpDSL, and the rationale for data-space tiling over iteration-space tiling [3][9]. Third, we study the **Affine dialect** as an SSA-embedded polyhedral representation, cataloging the affine loop transformations and their legality conditions [4][5]. Fourth, we study the *lowering machinery* — dialect conversion, bufferization, the transform dialect — and the retargetable code generation flows that have achieved competitive performance on CPUs and GPUs [7][10].

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Single-level IRs and the information-loss dilemma

LLVM IR [2] is the dominant example of a single-level design: a typed, SSA-form, RISC-like instruction set that all front ends target and all back ends consume. Optimizations in LLVM — loop unrolling, vectorization, LICM — operate on recovered loop structure. The recovery is the weakness: once a front end flattens a matrix multiplication into scalar IR, the middle end must reconstruct the algebraic structure (via scalar evolution, dependence analysis, loop idiom recognition) before it can optimize it [2]. Polyhedral frameworks such as **GRAPHITE** (GCC) and **Polly** (LLVM) pushed exact dependence analysis into production compilers, but the affine representation was not composable with SSA form, inducing pass-ordering conflicts with canonicalization, LICM, and vectorization [7].

### 2.2 The polyhedral lineage

The polyhedral model represents loop nests as integer polyhedra (iteration domains), access functions as affine maps, and schedules as affine transformations of the iteration space [4][5]. Its strengths are *exactness* (dependence analysis is decidable for affine programs) and *generality* (tiling, fusion, skewing, parallelization are all affine schedule transformations). Its weaknesses, as cataloged by Vasilache et al. [7], are fourfold: (1) the IR grows more complex than affine schedules when representing multi-level tiling and data transfers, requiring isl schedule trees; (2) scheduling relies on exponential algorithms; (3) affine representations are not composable with SSA; and (4) expressiveness limitations around dynamic control flow and irregular indexing persist. MLIR's Affine dialect directly addresses (3) by embedding the polyhedral representation in SSA through attributes and regions [7].

### 2.3 MLIR: scaling compiler infrastructure for domain-specific computation

Lattner et al. [1] present MLIR as a response to the proliferation of domain-specific accelerators and DSLs. The key contributions are:

1. **Dialects** as the unit of IR definition: extensible sets of operations, types, and attributes, defined declaratively in TableGen (ODS) with C++ or Python bindings.
2. **Progressive lowering**: dialects coexist; conversion passes lower one level at a time (`linalg` → `scf`/`affine` → `vector`/`memref` → `llvm`).
3. **Reusable infrastructure**: a common pass manager, verifier, pattern-rewrite engine (DRR), and the FileCheck-based testing discipline.
4. **Traits and interfaces**: `NoSideEffect`, `AffineScope`, `Vectorizable`, and dialect interfaces (e.g., `DestinationStyleOpInterface`, `TilingInterface`) that let generic passes operate over dialect-specific ops [1][9].

MLIR has since been adopted by Flang (Fortran), TensorFlow/XLA-adjacent flows, the CIRCT hardware compiler project, and numerous research compilers [1][6].

### 2.4 Program raising: the dual problem

While lowering is always well-defined, *raising* — recovering high-level dialect ops from low-level IR — is the dual problem and is considerably harder [8]. mlirSynth [8] demonstrates automatic, retargetable raising via program synthesis: given TableGen op definitions, it synthesizes `linalg`-level programs from LLVM-dialect code, enabling C programs to exploit high-performance domain compilers (e.g., XLA on TPUs, with reported 806× speedups in that context). Raising remains an active research frontier; this thesis focuses on the lowering direction.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is *analytical and constructive*: we study the MLIR infrastructure as documented in the primary literature and the living specification [1][3][5][7][9], and we reconstruct the canonical progressive-lowering pipeline for dense linear algebra as a running example. The approach has three components:

1. **Formal characterization.** We define progressive lowering as a rewrite discipline over a dialect lattice and state the legality invariants (type preservation, region well-formedness, trait satisfaction) that the dialect conversion framework enforces [6].
2. **Comparative analysis.** We contrast Linalg's structured-op approach with the Affine dialect's polyhedral approach along five axes: representation of iteration space, dependence analysis requirements, tiling strategy, vectorization interface, and target coverage [3][7][9].
3. **Empirical grounding.** We report published performance results from the MLIR code-generation literature — notably the composable code-generation flow of Vasilache et al. [7] and the affine-centric matmul flow of Bondhugula [5] — to assess whether progressive lowering delivers competitive performance, not merely architectural elegance.

We restrict scope to *dense* tensor computation on CPUs and GPUs, where the linalg/affine/vector pipeline is most mature; sparse tensors (the `sparse_tensor` dialect), while increasingly important, are excluded for space.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The MLIR core: operations, dialects, and progressive lowering

At MLIR's core, every program is a tree of **operations** with SSA operands and results, attributes, and regions containing blocks of further operations. A **dialect** namespaces a coherent set of ops, types, and attributes (e.g., `arith`, `scf`, `linalg`, `affine`, `vector`, `memref`, `gpu`, `llvm`). Dialects are *not* pipeline stages — multiple dialects coexist in one module, and a single function may contain `linalg.matmul` on tensors alongside `arith.addf` on scalars and `llvm.call` intrinsics [1].

> **Theorem 1.** *(Progressive lowering decomposition.)* Any lowering from a domain dialect D₀ to LLVM IR can be factored into a sequence of dialect conversion passes P₁, …, Pₙ such that each Pᵢ lowers operations of exactly one source dialect to a fixed set of target dialects, and the legality invariant — no op outside the declared target set remains — is checkable by the conversion framework after each step [1][6].

The conversion framework's key mechanisms are **type converters** (e.g., tensor → memref via bufferization), **conversion patterns** (1→N op rewrites with benefit ordering), and the *partial conversion* driver that applies patterns until fixpoint or failure, rolling back on illegal states [6]. This discipline is what makes pipelines *composable*: the `linalg → scf` patterns do not know about `vector`, and the `vector → llvm` patterns do not know about `linalg`.

### 4.2 Linalg on tensors: structured operations as the domain level

The **Linalg dialect** embodies a deliberate design philosophy, documented in its rationale [9]: rather than representing loops, represent *structured operations* — `linalg.matmul`, `linalg.conv_2d_nhwc_hwcf`, `linalg.generic` — whose semantics are fully captured by (a) the operand types (tensors or memrefs), (b) *indexing maps* (affine maps from iteration space to data space), and (c) *iterator types* (parallel, reduction, window). A matrix multiplication is a single op:

```mlir
%res = linalg.matmul ins(%A, %B : tensor<1024x1024xf32>, tensor<1024x1024xf32>)
                     outs(%C : tensor<1024x1024xf32>) -> tensor<1024x1024xf32>
```

This is the highest level of the lowering lattice, and its design decisions are informed by hard lessons from polyhedral compilation [9]:

- **Discourage loop skewing.** Skewing, sometimes used to enable parallelization, has surprising negative performance effects and produces complex control flow hostile to GPUs. Linalg's hyper-rectangular iteration domains make skewing unnecessary for ML workloads [9].
- **Declarative, data-space tiling.** Classical polyhedral tiling partitions the *iteration space*, introducing complex control flow and non-affine address expressions under parametric tiling. Linalg tiles the *data space* instead, creating strided `memref` views; access expressions stay predictable and compose cleanly [9].
- **Type polymorphism via OpDSL.** Operations are defined once in a Python-embedded DSL and instantiated over element types and shapes, shifting the API from compiler-IR construction to mathematical expression [7]:

```python
@linalg_structured_op
def matmul(A=TensorDef(T1, S.M, S.K), B=TensorDef(T2, S.K, S.N),
           C=TensorDef(T3, S.M, S.N, output=True)):
    C[D.m, D.n] += cast(T3, A[D.m, D.k]) * cast(T3, B[D.k, D.n])
```

The `DestinationStyleOpInterface` (formerly "linalg on tensors" output semantics) gives every structured op a functional, value-semantic form on immutable tensors, which makes fusion, tiling, and bufferization analyses dramatically simpler than their buffer-mutating counterparts [7][9].

Linalg's transformation vocabulary — *tile*, *fuse*, *vectorize*, *bufferize*, *pad*, *pack* — is exposed both as C++ passes and as composable **transform dialect** scripts, enabling *compilation experts*: parameterized, reusable transformation pipelines such as `Tile(32).then(Vectorize).then(Bufferize).then(LowerToLLVM)` [7]. This is progressive lowering made programmable.

### 4.3 The Affine dialect: polyhedral compilation embedded in SSA

Where Linalg abstracts loops away, the **Affine dialect** represents them explicitly — but with a crucial restriction: loop bounds, conditionals, and memory accesses must be *affine functions* of enclosing induction variables and symbols. This restriction buys decidability: dependence analysis, loop interchange legality, and tiling validity become exact, computable properties rather than heuristics [4][5].

```mlir
affine.for %i = 0 to 1024 {
  affine.for %j = 0 to 1024 {
    affine.for %k = 0 to 1024 {
      %a = affine.load %A[%i, %k] : memref<1024x1024xf32>
      %b = affine.load %B[%k, %j] : memref<1024x1024xf32>
      %c = affine.load %C[%i, %j] : memref<1024x1024xf32>
      %p = arith.mulf %a, %b : f32
      %s = arith.addf %c, %p : f32
      affine.store %s, %C[%i, %j] : memref<1024x1024xf32>
    }
  }
}
```

Bondhugula's end-to-end study [5] demonstrates the affine flow at its most powerful: starting from a single high-level op, a fully automatic sequence of affine transformations — multi-level tiling (cache tiles *M_C*, *K_C*; register tiles *M_R*, *N_R*; unroll factor *K_U*), packing, unroll-and-jam, scalar replacement, memref normalization — generates code competitive with tuned libraries, with all reasoning performed on the affine representation. The key insight is that the affine dialect *maintains the IR in loop form throughout*, embedding polyhedral information as attributes and regions inside SSA, so polyhedral and classical SSA transformations compose without pass-ordering conflicts [7].

The canonical affine transformations and their legality conditions are:

| Transformation | Purpose | Legality condition |
|---|---|---|
| Loop tiling (`-affine-loop-tile`) | Cache locality, register blocking | Always legal (reorders only independent iterations within tiles) |
| Loop interchange | Stride-1 access, vectorization enablement | Dependence direction vectors must remain lexicographically positive |
| Loop fusion | Temporal locality, reduced memory traffic | No fusion-preventing dependences; single-writer or privatizable |
| Unroll-and-jam | Register reuse, ILP exposure | Inner-loop independent or jam-safe; bounded code growth |
| Vectorization | SIMD utilization | No loop-carried dependences on vectorized dimension; unit stride |

Linalg and Affine thus represent *complementary* philosophies: Linalg keeps the program at the structured-op level as long as possible and lowers directly to loops+vectors via tiling/vectorization patterns, while the Affine flow materializes loops early and optimizes them with exact polyhedral reasoning [5][7][9]. Modern pipelines blend both: tile in Linalg, lower to `scf`, convert to affine for dependence-driven fusion, then vectorize [7].

### 4.4 Lowering machinery: bufferization, the transform dialect, and vector code generation

Between tensors and machine code lie three critical mechanisms:

1. **Bufferization** converts value-semantic `tensor` operations into buffer-mutating `memref` operations. One-shot bufferization analyzes the program to reuse buffers where safe (no read-after-write violations on the tensor SSA graph) and inserts copies where necessary — a whole-program, dialect-crossing analysis that is among MLIR's most sophisticated [7].
2. **The transform dialect** reifies transformation *schedules* as IR: matchers find payload ops, and transform ops apply tiling, fusion, or outlining. Schedules are themselves verifiable, composable, and tunable by autotuners — a decisive advance over pass pipelines hard-coded in C++ [7].
3. **Vector dialect lowering** progressively rewrites `vector.transfer` / `vector.contract` through unrolling, extraction, and target-specific patterns down to `llvm` intrinsics, with retargetable hooks for x86 AVX-512, AArch64 SVE, and RISC-V vector dialects [1][7][10].

### 4.5 Retargetable code generation: one pipeline, many targets

Retargetability is progressive lowering's payoff. Because high-level transformations (tiling, fusion, vectorization *strategy*) are target-agnostic, and only the final lowering steps are target-specific, a single pipeline template serves many architectures by varying parameters: tile sizes for cache hierarchies, vector widths for SIMD ISAs, and the terminal dialect (`llvm` with x86 vs. AArch64 vs. RISC-V attributes; `gpu`/`nvvm`/`rocdl` for accelerators) [1][7][10].

| Target | Vector abstraction | Reported outcome (literature) |
|---|---|---|
| x86-64 (AVX-512) | `vector` → `x86vector` → `llvm` | Near-MKL matmul from single `linalg.matmul` [5][7] |
| AArch64 (SVE/Neon) | `vector` (scalable) → `arm_sve` → `llvm` | Portable vector-length-agnostic codegen |
| RISC-V (V ext.) | `vector` → `riscv_vector` → `llvm` | Progressive lowering to custom ISA extensions [10] |
| NVIDIA/AMD GPUs | `linalg` → `gpu` → `nvvm`/`rocdl` → `llvm` | Competitive with cuBLAS on matmul [7] |

The RISC-V case study [10] is particularly instructive: the authors define a full multi-level RISC-V backend *inside* MLIR (dialects for registers, structured and unstructured control flow, ISA extensions), demonstrating that progressive lowering scales down to ISA-level code generation, not just domain-to-LLVM flows.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Theoretical Analysis

### 5.1 Performance evidence

The composable code-generation flow of Vasilache et al. [7] reports single-threaded CPU performance approaching machine peak on key kernels, driven by expert transformation pipelines over Linalg. Bondhugula [5] shows that pure affine flows — tiling with cache/register tile parameters, packing, unroll-and-jam — produce DGEMM-class performance from a one-line source op, with parametric studies over (*M_C*, *K_C*, *M_R*, *N_R*, *K_U*). GPU flows lower `linalg.matmul` through `gpu` to NVVM and achieve performance competitive with cuBLAS on matrix multiplication [7]. mlirSynth [8] demonstrates the converse direction: raising enables 806× speedups by routing C code through XLA on TPUs.

### 5.2 Theoretical analysis: why progressive lowering wins

We can state the advantage information-theoretically. Let *I_k* be the semantic information available at abstraction level *k* (tensor shapes and algebraic properties at the Linalg level; exact dependences at the affine level; vector shapes at the vector level). A single-level compiler must either preserve ∪_k *I_k* in one IR — impossible without ad-hoc metadata that later passes destroy — or recompute it by analysis, which is undecidable in general [2][8]. Progressive lowering instead *consumes* each *I_k* at the level where it is native (tiling decisions at Linalg, dependence checks at Affine) and lowers only the *residual* information the next level needs. The total analysis burden is thus partitioned across levels, each decidable or heuristic-friendly in its own domain [1][7].

> **Theorem 2.** *(Information locality.)* Under progressive lowering, every optimization decision is made at the highest abstraction level at which its legality precondition is exactly checkable; no decision requires recovering information destroyed by an earlier lowering step. *Proof sketch.* By construction of the pass schedule: tiling preconditions (structured-op semantics) hold at Linalg; dependence preconditions (affine accesses) hold at Affine; vectorization preconditions (contraction structure) hold at Vector. Each conversion pass is verified to preserve the preconditions of the next level's decisions [1][6][7].

### 5.3 Cost: compile time and engineering complexity

The price is real: multi-level pipelines run more passes, and TableGen-generated verifiers plus conversion legality checks add compile-time overhead relative to a hand-tuned single-level flow. Engineering complexity also shifts — from writing monolithic passes to designing dialect interfaces and conversion contracts. The literature suggests the trade is favorable: dialect-local passes are smaller, FileCheck tests are more precise (each level has its own checkable IR), and reuse across projects (Flang, CIRCT, IREE) amortizes infrastructure cost [1][6].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Autoscheduling across levels.** Choosing tile sizes, fusion groupings, and vectorization strategies remains expert-driven or autotuned by brute force. A principled *cost model* spanning abstraction levels — predicting final machine performance from Linalg-level decisions — is still missing [7].
2. **Lowering verification.** While the conversion framework checks structural legality, *semantic* preservation (does this tiling preserve the tensor program's meaning?) rests on the correctness of individual patterns. Formal verification of lowering passes is nascent [6].
3. **Phase ordering, revisited.** Progressive lowering reduces but does not eliminate phase-ordering problems: bufferization decisions interact with tiling, and vectorization interacts with unrolling. The transform dialect makes schedules explicit but does not solve the search problem [7].
4. **Beyond dense tensors.** Sparse computation, dynamic shapes, and control-flow-heavy programs stress the structured-op abstraction; the `sparse_tensor` dialect and shape dialect are active areas of development [1].
5. **Raising remains hard.** The lowering direction is well-supported; raising low-level code to high-level dialects requires synthesis or heroic analysis [8].

---

## 7. Conclusion

MLIR's progressive lowering — from **Linalg structured operations on tensors**, through **affine loop nests** with exact polyhedral reasoning, to **retargetable vector and LLVM dialects** — represents the most significant architectural advance in compiler construction since SSA-based optimization became standard [1][2]. By letting multiple abstraction levels coexist and lowering one level at a time, it dissolves the information-loss dilemma of single-level IRs: domain knowledge is consumed where it is native, transformations are dialect-local and composable, and a single pipeline template retargets across x86, Arm, RISC-V, and GPUs with competitive performance [5][7][10]. The open problems — cross-level cost models, verified lowering, autoscheduling — are now well-posed precisely because the abstraction lattice makes them visible. For the compiler researcher, the lesson is architectural: *do not recover what you can preserve; do not flatten what you can lower progressively*.
