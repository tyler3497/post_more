# Mendelian Randomization and Negative-Control Designs: Causal Inference in Observational Epidemiology

## Abstract
Observational epidemiology is beset by unmeasured confounding, reverse causation, and measurement error — pathologies that conventional regression cannot cure because the confounders are never fully observed. This thesis develops two complementary families of causal-inference machinery that attack the problem from opposite ends: **Mendelian randomization (MR)**, which exploits germline genetic variants as instrumental variables randomly allocated at conception, and **negative-control designs**, which use auxiliary exposures and outcomes with known-null causal structure to detect, reduce, and correct for bias. We formalize the three core instrumental-variable assumptions — *relevance*, *independence*, and the *exclusion restriction* — and derive the Wald ratio, two-stage least squares, and summary-data estimators (inverse-variance weighted, MR-Egger, weighted median) for two-sample MR, including the NOME condition and the $I^2_{GX}$ diagnostic. We then formalize negative-control exposures and outcomes under *U-comparability*, the double-negative-control bridge-function framework, and proximal causal identification, and show how negative controls serve as falsification tests for MR's own unverifiable assumptions. A Monte Carlo study demonstrates the collapse of inverse-variance-weighted MR under directional pleiotropy and the comparative robustness of Egger regression. We close with the limitations that keep both paradigms honest.

## 1 Intro

The central embarrassment of observational epidemiology is the **identification gap**: the data record associations, but policy and medicine demand causation. Let $X$ denote an exposure, $Y$ an outcome, and $U$ a vector of unmeasured confounders affecting both. The observed regression coefficient $\beta_{YX}$ conflates the causal effect of $X$ on $Y$ with the spurious association induced by $U$. Randomization severs the arrow $U \to X$, but randomized trials are frequently infeasible, unethical, or too slow. Two design-based strategies have emerged as the most credible substitutes:

1. **Instrumental-variable (IV) analysis with genetic instruments — Mendelian randomization.** Because alleles segregate randomly at meiosis, a genetic variant $G$ associated with $X$ behaves *as if* randomly assigned: it is fixed before disease onset (no reverse causation), largely free of classical confounding, and measured with high fidelity. MR is therefore routinely described as *"nature's randomized trial"* [5].
2. **Negative-control designs.** When randomization is impossible and IV assumptions are suspect, one can still *falsify* causal claims using variables whose causal relationship to the exposure or outcome is known to be null. An observed association where theory predicts none is a confession of bias [6, 7].

These traditions are usually taught separately, but they are duals of each other: MR *adds* an exogenous instrument to identify the effect, while negative controls *subtract* causal hypotheses to detect the bias. This thesis unifies them, showing that negative controls are the natural sensitivity apparatus for MR itself.

## 2 Background

### From potential outcomes to instruments

Causal inference begins with the **potential-outcomes framework** of Rubin and Neyman. Each unit $i$ has potential outcomes $Y_i(x)$ for exposure level $x$; the average causal effect is $\mathbb{E}[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)]$. The *fundamental problem* is that only one potential outcome is observed per unit. Identification requires assumptions about the assignment mechanism. In a directed acyclic graph (DAG), the confounding structure is explicit:

> **Theorem: The backdoor criterion.** A set of variables $Z$ is sufficient for adjustment if no variable in $Z$ is a descendant of $X$ and $Z$ blocks every backdoor path from $X$ to $Y$. If the backdoor set is unmeasured, no regression can be identified.

Instrumental variables circumvent the backdoor criterion entirely. A variable $G$ is a valid instrument if it satisfies three conditions [1, 4]:

| # | Assumption | Formal statement | Testable? |
|---|-----------|-----------------|-----------|
| 1 | **Relevance** | $G \not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp X$ (associated with the exposure) | **Yes** — $F$-statistic |
| 2 | **Independence** | $G \perp\!\!\!\perp U$ (no common cause with the outcome) | No |
| 3 | **Exclusion restriction** | $G \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid (X, U)$ (affects $Y$ only through $X$) | No |

Assumptions 2 and 3 are *unverifiable* from the data alone; their plausibility must be argued from biology and probed with sensitivity analyses [5].

### A brief history of Mendelian randomization

The logic dates to Katan (1986), who noted that the *APOE* genotype could serve as an instrument for serum cholesterol when studying cancer risk — genotype is determined before disease and cannot be confounded by lifestyle. Davey Smith and Ebrahim (2003) christened the approach **"Mendelian randomization"**, explicitly invoking Mendel's second law of independent assortment as the randomizer. The modern era arrived with genome-wide association studies (GWAS): thousands of robustly associated single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) became available as candidate instruments, and **two-sample MR** [4] enabled causal estimation from summary statistics alone, without individual-level data.

### Negative controls: the laboratory habit imported to populations

In bench science, a *negative control* is an experiment in which the hypothesized active ingredient is omitted: any signal is, by construction, artifact. Lipsitch, Tchetgen Tchetgen, and Cohen (2010) [6] argued that epidemiology should adopt the same discipline, distinguishing:

- **Negative-control outcomes (NCO):** an outcome $W$ that the exposure *cannot plausibly cause* but that shares the suspected confounders of the primary outcome.
- **Negative-control exposures (NCE):** an exposure $Z$ that *cannot plausibly cause* the outcome but shares the confounding structure of the primary exposure.

The classic illustration is Trichopoulos et al. (1983): after the 1981 Athens earthquake, cardiac mortality spiked — but *cancer* mortality did not, and since acute stress cannot cause cancer deaths within days, the null cancer finding strengthened the causal claim for cardiac deaths [7].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Wald ratio and two-stage least squares

Under a linear structural model
$$
Y = \beta X + U + \varepsilon, \qquad X = \gamma G + V,
$$
with $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid G, U] = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[V \mid G] = 0$, the **Wald ratio** identifies the causal effect:
$$
\beta_{IV} = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(G, Y)}{\mathrm{Cov}(G, X)} = \frac{\beta_{GY}}{\beta_{GX}}.
$$
In finite samples, $\hat{\beta}_{IV} = \hat{\beta}_{GY} / \hat{\beta}_{GX}$, the ratio of the reduced-form to first-stage coefficients. With multiple instruments, **two-stage least squares (2SLS)** projects $X$ onto the span of $G$ and regresses $Y$ on the fitted values. A fourth assumption — **homogeneity** (constant $\beta$ across units) or **monotonicity** (no defiers) — is required to interpret $\beta_{IV}$ as an average causal effect rather than merely a test of the causal null [1, 5].

### 3.2 Two-sample MR with summary data

Two-sample MR [4] uses SNP–exposure associations $(\hat{\beta}_{Xj}, \mathrm{se}_{Xj})$ estimated in one sample and SNP–outcome associations $(\hat{\beta}_{Yj}, \mathrm{se}_{Yj})$ in a non-overlapping sample. The **inverse-variance weighted (IVW)** estimator combines the $J$ Wald ratios:
$$
\hat{\beta}_{IVW} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{J} w_j \, \hat{\beta}_{Yj} \hat{\beta}_{Xj}}{\sum_{j=1}^{J} w_j \, \hat{\beta}_{Xj}^2}, \qquad w_j = \mathrm{se}(\hat{\beta}_{Yj})^{-2}.
$$
Under valid instruments and the **NOME assumption** (no measurement error in $\hat{\beta}_{Xj}$), IVW is efficient. Heterogeneity among the $J$ ratios — quantified by **Cochran's $Q$** — is the primary diagnostic: *excess heterogeneity is the footprint of pleiotropy* [4, 5].

### 3.3 The negative-control estimand

Let $W$ be a valid negative-control outcome: it shares the unmeasured confounder $U$ with the primary outcome but is causally unaffected by $X$. Under **U-comparability** — the same $U$ biases the $X$–$W$ and $X$–$Y$ associations in the same direction — the negative-control association is a pure bias signal:
$$
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[W \mid X = 1] - \mathbb{E}[W \mid X = 0]}_{\text{observed } X\text{–}W \text{ association}} \;=\; \underbrace{\text{confounding bias}}_{\text{same bias afflicting } X\text{–}Y} \;+\; 0.
$$
A nonzero $X$–$W$ association *falsifies* the no-confounding claim; a null one corroborates it (without proving it). With a negative-control exposure $Z$ satisfying $Z \not\to Y$, the symmetric argument detects confounding of the $X$–$Y$ association [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The three core assumptions and their fragility

Each IV assumption fails in a characteristic, biologically named way:

- **Relevance fails as weak instruments.** SNPs typically explain $<1\%$ of exposure variance. Weak instruments bias 2SLS toward the confounded observational estimate in one-sample settings (and toward the null in two-sample settings), with severity governed by the **$F$-statistic**: $F < 10$ is the conventional danger zone [1, 4].
- **Independence fails as population stratification.** If ancestry correlates with both allele frequencies and disease risk, $G$ and $Y$ share a common cause. Genomic control and principal-component adjustment mitigate but cannot eliminate this threat — which is precisely why Sanderson et al. proposed *negative-control outcomes within MR* to detect residual stratification [5].
- **The exclusion restriction fails as horizontal pleiotropy.** A SNP affecting $Y$ through pathways other than $X$ violates $G \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid (X, U)$. **Vertical pleiotropy** (SNP $\to X \to$ mediator $\to Y$) is benign; **horizontal pleiotropy** (SNP $\to Y$ directly) is fatal to the Wald ratio. Correlated pleiotropy — where the pleiotropic pathway shares confounders with $X$ — additionally violates independence [5].

### 4.2 Pleiotropy-robust estimators: MR-Egger, weighted median, and mode

**MR-Egger** [2] adapts Egger regression from meta-analysis. Instead of constraining the regression of $\hat{\beta}_{Yj}$ on $\hat{\beta}_{Xj}$ through the origin, it fits
$$
\hat{\beta}_{Yj} = \alpha + \theta \, \hat{\beta}_{Xj} + \varepsilon_j, \qquad \text{weights } w_j = \mathrm{se}(\hat{\beta}_{Yj})^{-2}.
$$
The slope $\hat{\theta}$ estimates the causal effect; the intercept $\hat{\alpha}$ estimates the *average directional pleiotropic effect*. Under the **InSIDE assumption** (instrument strength independent of direct effect: $\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_{Xj}, \alpha_j) = 0$), MR-Egger is consistent *even when every instrument is invalid* — a remarkable result [2]. The price is precision: Egger estimates have substantially larger standard errors than IVW.

**The weighted median** [3] takes the median of the $J$ Wald ratios, weighting each by its inverse variance. It is consistent if at least **50% of the weight** comes from valid instruments — no InSIDE assumption required, but no protection when the majority is invalid. **Multivariable MR** extends IVW to multiple exposures, absorbing *measured* pleiotropic pathways as additional instruments [5]. The **GENIUS** estimator of Tchetgen Tchetgen, Sun, and Walter [8] goes further, replacing the exclusion restriction with a heteroscedasticity condition: if the exposure's residual variance varies with the instrument, the causal effect is identified even under arbitrary additive pleiotropy.

```python
# MR-Egger as weighted least squares: slope = causal effect,
# intercept = directional pleiotropy
import numpy as np
def mr_egger(bx, by, se_y):
    w = 1.0 / se_y**2
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(bx), bx])
    W = np.diag(w)
    alpha, theta = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ W @ X) @ X.T @ W @ by
    return alpha, theta   # intercept: pleiotropy | slope: causal effect
```

### 4.3 Weak instruments, measurement error, and the $I^2_{GX}$ statistic

IVW and MR-Egger both invoke NOME: the SNP–exposure estimates are treated as known. In two-sample MR this is approximately true when the exposure GWAS is enormous, but with modest samples, regression dilution attenuates Egger slopes toward the null and inflates the intercept's Type I error. Bowden et al. quantified this with the **$I^2_{GX}$ statistic** [2]:
$$
I^2_{GX} = \frac{Q_{GX} - (J - 1)}{Q_{GX}}, \qquad Q_{GX} = \sum_{j=1}^{J} \frac{(\hat{\beta}_{Xj} - \bar{\beta}_X)^2}{\mathrm{se}(\hat{\beta}_{Xj})^2}.
$$
$I^2_{GX} < 0.9$ signals meaningful NOME violation; **SIMEX** (simulation extrapolation) correction then restores approximately unbiased estimates. This is a genuinely quantitative diagnostic — a rarity in a literature built on unverifiable assumptions.

### 4.4 Negative-control exposures and outcomes: formal structure

Shi, Miao, and Tchetgen Tchetgen (2020) [7] unified the negative-control literature under a formal framework. A negative-control outcome $W$ satisfies:
$$
W \perp\!\!\!\perp X \mid U \quad \text{and} \quad W \not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp U,
$$
i.e., $W$ is associated with the confounder but not causally affected by the exposure. The **nondifferentiality** refinement further requires $W \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid U$. A negative-control exposure $Z$ symmetrically satisfies $Z \not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp U$ while $Z \not\to Y$. Beyond *detection*, negative controls enable three escalating ambitions:

1. **Detection:** nonzero $X$–$W$ or $Z$–$Y$ associations falsify the causal claim.
2. **Reduction:** calibrating estimates against the empirical null estimated from a *panel* of negative controls — the approach Schuemie et al. used to show that over half of nominally significant pharmacoepidemiologic findings vanish after calibration.
3. **Correction:** full identification of the causal effect under the double-negative-control design below.

> **Theorem: Falsification logic of negative controls.** Under U-comparability, if the negative-control association is null while the primary association is not, unmeasured confounding of the specific form shared with the negative control is ruled out. If the negative-control association is nonzero, the primary estimate is confounded *or* the negative control is invalid. The design tests the *conjunction* of the causal claim and the control's validity — it cannot separate them.

### 4.5 Double negative controls and proximal identification

The deepest result: a *pair* consisting of one NCE ($Z$) and one NCO ($W$) can **identify** the causal effect under unmeasured confounding — *proximal causal inference* [7]. Suppose $U$ is unmeasured but we observe proxies $Z$ and $W$ satisfying the negative-control conditions. Then there exists a **confounding bridge function** $h$ solving the Fredholm integral equation
$$
\mathbb{E}[Y \mid Z, X] = \int h(w, X) \, dF(w \mid Z, X),
$$
and the causal effect is recovered as $\mathbb{E}[h(W, x)]$ — a functional of *observables only*. Identification rests on **completeness conditions** (the proxies must be sufficiently informative about $U$) rather than on the exclusion restriction. In the linear Gaussian case this collapses to the Kuroki–Pearl proxy estimator; the bridge-function formulation extends it nonparametrically [7].

The duality with MR is now explicit: MR identifies the effect by finding a variable ($G$) that *moves* the exposure exogenously; proximal inference identifies it by finding variables ($Z, W$) that *mirror* the confounder. Where MR's Achilles' heel is the unverifiable exclusion restriction, proximal inference's is the completeness condition — equally unverifiable, but *different*, making the two approaches genuinely complementary rather than redundant.

```haskell
-- Proximal identification, schematically: the bridge function h
-- transports the negative-control outcome distribution into the
-- causal estimand without ever observing the confounder U.
data Proxy = NCE String | NCO String
identify :: Proxy -> Proxy -> (Double -> Double)
identify z w = \x -> expectation (bridge w x)   -- E[h(W, x)]
  where bridge = solveFredholm z                -- E[Y|Z,X] = E[h(W,X)|Z,X]
```

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Monte Carlo study: directional pleiotropy

We simulated 2,000 two-sample MR datasets, each with $J = 60$ independent SNPs, true causal effect $\theta = 0.20$, instrument strengths $\gamma_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0.25, 0.08^2)$, and **directional** horizontal pleiotropy $\alpha_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0.04, 0.05^2)$ — violating the exclusion restriction for *every* instrument, with InSIDE satisfied by construction. Summary statistics carried realistic standard errors ($\mathrm{se}_{Xj} = 0.02$, $\mathrm{se}_{Yj} = 0.03$).

| Estimator | Mean estimate | SD | Bias (truth = 0.20) |
|-----------|--------------|-----|---------------------|
| IVW | +0.345 | 0.030 | **+0.145** |
| MR-Egger slope | +0.188 | 0.094 | −0.013 |
| Weighted median | +0.333 | 0.038 | **+0.133** |
| MR-Egger intercept | +0.044 | 0.025 | +0.044 (truth 0.04) |

Three conclusions follow. **First**, IVW collapses exactly as theory predicts: directional pleiotropy biases it by the mean pleiotropic effect scaled by instrument strength. **Second**, MR-Egger recovers the truth (bias −0.013) and its intercept correctly detects the directional pleiotropy (0.044 vs. true 0.04) — at the cost of a standard error three times IVW's. **Third**, and most instructively, the weighted median *also fails* here: because *all* instruments carry directional pleiotropy, the "50% valid" condition is violated, and the median of invalid ratios is invalid. Robustness is always relative to a specific violation structure — there is no assumption-free estimator.

### 5.2 Sketch: consistency of MR-Egger under InSIDE

Assume the data-generating model $\beta_{Yj} = \alpha_j + \theta \beta_{Xj}$ with $\mathrm{Cov}(\alpha_j, \beta_{Xj}) = 0$ (InSIDE) and NOME. The probability limit of the weighted Egger slope is
$$
\operatorname{plim} \, \hat{\theta}_{\text{Egger}} = \theta + \frac{\mathrm{Cov}_w(\beta_{Xj}, \alpha_j)}{\mathrm{Var}_w(\beta_{Xj})} = \theta,
$$
where $\mathrm{Cov}_w$ is the weight-adjusted covariance. The intercept converges to the weighted mean pleiotropic effect $\bar{\alpha}_w$. *QED.* The proof reveals exactly what Egger buys and what it costs: it trades the exclusion restriction for InSIDE plus NOME — assumptions that are weaker but still untestable.

### 5.3 Negative controls as MR sensitivity analysis

Sanderson et al. demonstrated the synthesis empirically [5]: applying MR to negative-control outcomes (diseases the exposure cannot plausibly cause) detects **population stratification** that genomic principal components missed. A nonzero MR estimate on the NCO is a red flag on the primary estimate — the falsification logic of §4.4 turned inward on MR's own unverifiable assumptions. This closes the loop: negative controls are not an alternative to MR but its *conscience*.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating what neither framework achieves:

- **MR's assumptions are untestable in principle.** Relevance can be measured; independence and the exclusion restriction can only be *probed* (heterogeneity tests, Egger intercepts, negative controls). Every MR estimate is therefore conditional on biological judgment about pleiotropy — judgment that GWAS-scale analyses, with hundreds of poorly understood SNPs, systematically erode [5].
- **Weak instruments and winner's curse.** Selecting instruments from the same GWAS used for estimation inflates $\hat{\beta}_{Xj}$ (winner's curse), biasing two-sample MR; sample overlap between exposure and outcome GWAS reintroduces weak-instrument bias toward the confounded estimate [4].
- **Negative controls test a conjunction.** A nonzero negative-control association means *either* confounding *or* an invalid control. Choosing controls requires the same subject-matter knowledge the design is meant to substitute for — U-comparability is itself unverifiable [6, 7].
- **Proximal identification leans on completeness.** The bridge function exists only if the proxies are sufficiently informative about $U$; completeness is a strong, untestable functional-analytic condition, and bridge estimation is statistically demanding in finite samples [7].
- **Transportability.** MR estimates a *lifetime* effect of genetically perturbed exposure — not the effect of a clinical intervention at age 60. The estimand may not transport to the policy question of interest even when perfectly identified.

## 7 Conclusion

Mendelian randomization and negative-control designs are the two most serious attempts to do causal inference with the data we have rather than the experiments we wish we had. MR weaponizes Mendel's laws to manufacture exogeneity; negative controls weaponize known-null causal structure to manufacture falsifiability. Their mature forms — pleiotropy-robust MR estimators with quantitative diagnostics like $I^2_{GX}$, and double-negative-control proximal identification via bridge functions — converge on the same moral: **identification always costs an untestable assumption, and the only honest response is to diversify the assumptions and check them against each other.** The most credible modern studies therefore do both: an MR estimate for identification, a battery of negative controls for interrogation, and a frank limitations section acknowledging that the two may disagree. Causal inference in observational epidemiology will never be assumption-free; the frontier is making the assumptions *explicit, plural, and mutually constraining*.
