# Arithmetization of the Ethereum Virtual Machine: Keccak Circuits, Lookup Arguments, Copy Constraints, and Recursive Accumulation in zkEVM Proving Systems

## Abstract

Zero-knowledge Ethereum Virtual Machines (zkEVMs) reduce the correctness of EVM execution to the satisfiability of massive arithmetic constraint systems. This thesis presents a unified treatment of the circuit-design techniques that make this reduction practical: the arithmetization of the Keccak-f[1600] permutation, lookup arguments — Plookup, the Halo2 subset argument, and LogUp — that replace bit-twiddling constraints with table-membership checks, constraint systems for the full EVM opcode set organized around execution-state machines, copy constraints and permutation arguments that enforce cross-circuit consistency, and recursive proof composition via Halo2 accumulation schemes over the Pasta curve cycle. Drawing on the Scroll, Polygon zkEVM, and Taiko architectures [5][6][8][9], we derive the constraint-level mechanisms — grand-product identities, random-linear-combination accumulators, and nested amortization — and analyze their asymptotic and concrete costs. We conclude that lookup arguments and recursive aggregation have shifted the zkEVM bottleneck from opcode logic to hash-heavy primitives, framing the next frontier of circuit design.

## 1. Introduction

The Ethereum Virtual Machine is among the most hostile workloads ever subjected to zero-knowledge proving. It is a 256-bit stack machine with byte-addressed volatile memory, a persistent Merkle-patricia key-value store, dynamic gas metering, and a cryptographic hash function — Keccak-256 — embedded in its hottest paths: contract creation addresses, storage slot derivation, and transaction trie commitments all hash. None of these structures are native to the arithmetic circuits of modern SNARKs, which speak only the language of low-degree polynomial identities over finite fields [4].

A zkEVM must therefore *arithmetize* Ethereum: translate every execution step into polynomial constraints whose satisfiability is equivalent to correct execution. Buterin's taxonomy of zkEVM types [7] frames the design space as a spectrum from Type 1 (fully Ethereum-equivalent, proving the chain as-is) to Type 4 (language-level equivalence), with proof-generation cost falling as fidelity is sacrificed. Taiko pursues the Type-1 extreme; Scroll and Linea sit near Type 2/3; Polygon zkEVM reimplements the state machine in a STARK-native polynomial identity language. Despite their differences, all production zkEVMs converge on five circuit-design pillars:

1. **Keccak arithmetization** — constraining the Keccak-f[1600] permutation, the single most expensive primitive in every zkEVM prover.
2. **Lookup arguments** — Plookup [1], the Halo2 subset argument [3], and LogUp [2] — which convert bitwise and range operations from constraint-heavy decompositions into table-membership checks.
3. **EVM opcode constraint systems** — organizing ~140 opcodes into execution-state machines with per-opcode handlers.
4. **Copy constraints and permutation arguments** — the glue enforcing that values claimed by one subcircuit match those consumed by another.
5. **Recursion via accumulation schemes** — compressing dozens of subcircuit proofs into one L1-verifiable proof, amortizing linear-time verifier work through Halo2's nested amortization [10] or Polygon's STARK recursion pipeline [8].

Our contribution is a single, precise account of how these mechanisms interlock — from the grand-product identity inside a Keccak lookup to the accumulation scheme folding a thousand batch proofs into one SNARK.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 PLONKish arithmetization

Halo2-style proving systems [4] express computation as a *PLONKish* constraint matrix: fixed, advice (witness), and instance (public input) columns, constrained by custom gates — low-degree polynomial identities selected by selector columns — plus a permutation argument enforcing *copy constraints* and lookup arguments enforcing table membership [3]. The prover commits to column polynomials with a polynomial commitment scheme (KZG in Scroll's Halo2 fork, IPA in upstream Halo2) and the verifier checks a handful of identities at random points. Constraint *degree* and *row count* are the two currencies of cost: degree bounds commitments, rows bound prover time and memory.

### 2.2 Why the EVM resists arithmetization

Three properties of the EVM dominate circuit design. First, **256-bit words** over ~254-bit or 64-bit fields force every word into range-checked limbs. Second, **Keccak-256** is bitwise: XOR, AND, NOT, and rotations across a 1600-bit state, none of which is a low-degree polynomial operation. Third, **stateful execution** — stack, memory, storage — demands global consistency of read-write sequences across millions of steps, which is where permutation arguments earn their keep.

### 2.3 The trace-driven methodology

Production zkEVMs do not constrain the EVM *interpreter*; they constrain its *execution trace*. Scroll's `bus-mapping` crate [6] replays transactions in an instrumented Geth client and converts the trace into witness rows for a family of subcircuits: the **EVM circuit** (opcode semantics), the **state circuit** (RW-table consistency), the **bytecode circuit** (code bytes versus code hash), the **Keccak circuit** (hash preimages), and others — finally aggregated by a **super circuit**. Polygon zkEVM instead models the prover itself as a cluster of PIL-described state machines proved with eSTARK/FRI [9].

## 3. Methodology

Our method is *comparative constraint analysis*: for each pillar we state the relation to be proved, derive the enforcing polynomial identities, exhibit the instantiation in Scroll's Halo2 circuits [5][6] or Polygon's PIL machines [8][9], and analyze asymptotic and concrete costs. We treat the EVM Yellow Paper semantics as ground truth and ask, for each mechanism, what polynomial identity makes deviation *unsatisfiable* rather than merely *unlikely*. Random challenges — the Fiat–Shamir-derived $\beta, \gamma$ of permutation and lookup arguments, and the RLC challenges binding byte strings to field elements — are modeled as uniform, with soundness error bounded by Schwartz–Zippel.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Keccak Arithmetization

Keccak-f[1600] operates on a $5 \times 5$ array of 64-bit lanes (1600 bits), iterating 24 rounds of $\theta, \rho, \pi, \chi, \iota$ — compositions of XORs, rotations, and bitwise AND-NOT [5]. Keccak-256 uses rate $r = 1088$ bits (136 bytes) with `pad10*1` padding (`0x01 … 0x80`). A naive bit-decomposition of one permutation costs tens of thousands of constraints; since every `SHA3` opcode, `CREATE` address derivation, and storage slot computation hashes, Keccak dominates zkEVM proving time in every measured deployment.

Scroll's Keccak circuit [5] takes a different route: it constrains the permutation *lane-wise* using lookup-assisted bitwise operations, and amortizes each 24-round permutation over a vertically-packed region. Key layout parameters from the circuit documentation:

| Parameter | Value | Role |
|---|---|---|
| `DEFAULT_KECCAK_ROWS` | 12 | Rows consumed per round region |
| Rounds per permutation | 24 | Keccak-f[1600] round count |
| Rows per permutation | 288 | $24 \times 12$ |
| Rate | 136 bytes | Absorbed per permutation |
| `round_cst` | fixed column | Round constants $RC$ |

A dedicated `CellManager` packs each round's cells *vertically* (unlike the EVM circuit's horizontal packing), aligning 12-row regions per round. Selectors `q_enable`, `q_first`, `q_round`, `q_absorb`, `q_round_last`, `q_padding`, `q_padding_last` gate the absorb, round, and padding phases. Two RLC accumulators bind the witness to the rest of the system: `data_rlc` accumulates input bytes under the `keccak_input_randomness` challenge, and `hash_rlc` accumulates the digest. Other circuits never recompute Keccak — the **bytecode circuit** looks up `(data_rlc, length) → hash_rlc` in the shared **Keccak table** [6], turning hash verification into a single table-membership check.

The $\theta$ step illustrates why lookups matter. In Python-like pseudocode over 64-bit lanes:

```python
# Round constants for the iota step (first 4 of 24 shown)
RC = [0x0000000000000001, 0x0000000000008082,
      0x800000000000808A, 0x8000000080008000]  # ... 24 total

def theta(A):
    # A[x][y]: 5x5 lanes; theta diffuses each column into its neighbors
    C = [A[x][0] ^ A[x][1] ^ A[x][2] ^ A[x][3] ^ A[x][4]
         for x in range(5)]
    D = [C[(x + 4) % 5] ^ rotl64(C[(x + 1) % 5], 1)
         for x in range(5)]
    return [[A[x][y] ^ D[x] for y in range(5)] for x in range(5)]
```

Each 64-bit XOR, one CPU instruction natively, becomes in-circuit eight 8-bit XOR *lookups* or 64 boolean constraints. The circuit therefore decomposes lanes into bytes and performs $\theta, \rho, \pi, \chi$ through fixed 8-bit XOR/AND tables — the lookup argument of §4.2 doing the heavy lifting raw polynomial constraints cannot.

### 4.2 Lookup Arguments: Plookup, the Halo2 Subset Argument, and LogUp

A lookup argument proves that every element of a witness vector $f = (f_1, \dots, f_n)$ appears in a table $t = (t_1, \dots, t_d)$, without constraining the *function* the table tabulates. Showing `c = a XOR b` for bytes then costs one lookup into a $256 \times 256$ table instead of 25–32 bit-decomposition constraints [1].

**Plookup** [1] sorts the multiset union of queries and table entries. Let $s$ be the sorted concatenation of $f$ and $t$. For random challenges $\beta, \gamma$, the prover shows the grand-product identity

$$(1+\beta)^n \prod_{i=1}^{n}(\gamma + f_i) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{n+d}\bigl(\gamma(1+\beta) + s_i + \beta\, s_{i+1}\bigr),$$

enforced row-by-row through an accumulator polynomial $Z$ with $Z(1) = 1$ and

$$Z(gX) \;=\; Z(X)\,\frac{\gamma(1+\beta) + s(X) + \beta\, s(gX)}{(\gamma + f(X))(1+\beta)}.$$

> **Theorem:** *(Plookup soundness [1].)* If the grand-product constraint holds and $\beta, \gamma$ are uniformly random, then $\{f_i\} \subseteq \{t_j\}$ except with negligible probability: any $f_i \notin \{t_j\}$ breaks the multiset-equality that the identity encodes, detectable unless $\beta$ or $\gamma$ hits one of $O(n+d)$ forbidden values.

Multi-column tables such as `(opcode, gas_cost, stack_delta)` are compressed with an extra random challenge, and the *batched range proof* specialization handles thousands of range checks with one table.

**The Halo2 subset argument** [3] avoids the explicit sorted merge. With query column $A$ and table column $S$, the prover supplies permutations $A', S'$ of $A, S$ and the circuit enforces:

- $A'_0 = S'_0$ (anchored by the $l_0$ Lagrange constraint $(A'(X) - S'(X))\,l_0(X) = 0$),
- $(A'_i - S'_i)(A'_i - A'_{i-1}) = 0$ for every row — each permuted query either *repeats* its predecessor or *advances* to the next table entry, which is exactly the sorted-run structure Plookup constructs explicitly,
- a grand product $Z$ with $Z(\omega g)\,(A'(\omega)+\beta)(S'(\omega)+\gamma) = Z(\omega)\,(A(\omega)+\beta)(S(\omega)+\gamma)$ and $Z(1) = 1$ via $(1 - Z(X))\,l_0(X) = 0$.

Multiple tables coexist through a *tag column* merged into one tagged table — the technique Scroll uses to share fixed tables (opcodes, range tables, Keccak I/O) across subcircuits without duplicating commitments [3][6].

**LogUp** [2] replaces grand products with *logarithmic derivatives*. Writing multiplicities $m(x)$ for how often each table entry is used, the identity

$$\sum_{x \in H} \frac{m(x)}{X + t(x)} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{X + f_i}$$

holds as rational functions iff the multisets match. The prover commits only to the multiplicity polynomial $m$ — no sorted merge, no permuted copies — and the identity reduces to sumchecks. For batched multi-column lookups (the common zkEVM case: thousands of byte-XORs against one table), paper-and-pencil operation counts show LogUp's oracle prover **1.5×–4× faster** than the Plookup-style baseline at $2^{12}$-row scale [2].

| Scheme | Prover work | Extra prover oracles | Batching | zkEVM use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plookup [1] | $O((n+d)\log(n+d))$ sort + grand product | sorted merge $s$ | per-table | 8-bit XOR/AND tables, range checks |
| Halo2 subset [3] | $O((n+d)\log(n+d))$, no explicit sort in circuit | permuted $A', S'$ | tag-column multi-table | Scroll/Linea fixed tables |
| LogUp [2] | quasilinear, cheaper oracles | multiplicity $m$ only | natively batched | next-gen batched lookups |

In Halo2's Rust API, a fixed XOR table is declared once and referenced per row [3][4]:

```rust
// Fixed table (a, b, a^b) for all byte pairs, loaded at keygen.
meta.lookup("xor8", |meta| {
    let a = meta.query_advice(xor_a, Rotation::cur());
    let b = meta.query_advice(xor_b, Rotation::cur());
    let c = meta.query_advice(xor_c, Rotation::cur());
    vec![
        (a, table.column(0)),
        (b, table.column(1)),
        (c, table.column(2)),
    ]
});
```

### 4.3 EVM Opcode Constraint Systems

The EVM circuit models execution as a *state machine over steps*. Each step occupies one or more rows keyed by an **execution state** (`BeginTx`, `EndTx`, and one state per opcode). A fixed **state-transition table** constrains which state may follow which — the fetch-decode-execute loop becomes a lookup: `(current_state, opcode) → next_state`. Within a step, per-opcode *handlers* constrain exactly the effects of that opcode, selected by opcode-specific selectors so that the ~140 handlers share one row layout.

Consider `ADD` (pseudocode in a circom-like idiom):

```circom
// ADD handler: pops a, b; pushes (a+b) mod 2^256; costs 3 gas (G_verylow)
template AddHandler() {
    signal input a[8], b[8];      // 32-bit limbs
    signal input carry[8];
    signal output sum[8];
    component lt = LessThan(252);
    for (var i = 0; i < 8; i++) {
        // limb addition with carry, each limb range-checked to 32 bits
        sum[i] <-- (a[i] + b[i] + (i==0 ? 0 : carry[i-1])) % 2**32;
        carry[i] <-- (a[i] + b[i] + (i==0 ? 0 : carry[i-1])) \ 2**32;
        lt.in[i] <== sum[i];      // range check via lookup table
    }
    gas_cost <== 3;
    stack_delta <== -1;           // pop 2, push 1
}
```

Every stateful effect — stack push/pop, memory read/write/expansion, storage `SLOAD`/`SSTORE`, transient storage, logs, call-context changes — emits a row in the **RW table** with fields `(tag, id, address, field_tag, value, value_rlc, rw_counter)`. The EVM circuit does *not* check global RW consistency; it only emits. The **state circuit** then verifies the RW table: `rw_counter` increases monotonically, every read matches the most recent write to the same `(tag, id, address)`, and initial values anchor to the state trie. This *emit-then-verify* split lets opcode handlers stay local while global consistency is proved once, centrally — with copy constraints (§4.4) binding the two views of the same rows.

The **bytecode circuit** [6] closes another loop: for each executed contract it constrains that the witnessed bytecode bytes hash (via the Keccak table lookup) to the claimed `code_hash`, using an internal `push_table` mapping each byte to its `PUSHn` data length so immediates are skipped correctly during disassembly.

### 4.4 Copy Constraints and Permutation Arguments

A *copy constraint* asserts equality between two cells anywhere in the constraint matrix. Collectively, copy constraints form a permutation $\sigma$ over all cells; cells in the same $\sigma$-cycle must hold equal values. The PLONK permutation argument enforces this with a grand product over the wire polynomials $a, b, c$ [4]:

$$Z(gX) = Z(X)\,\prod_{j \in \{a,b,c\}}
\frac{x_j(X) + \beta\,\kappa_j X + \gamma}
     {x_j(X) + \beta\,\sigma_j(X) + \gamma},
\qquad Z(1) = 1,$$

where $\kappa_j$ are coset shifts separating the three wires and $\sigma_j(X)$ encodes the permuted evaluation point. The numerator accumulates each cell's *position*; the denominator accumulates its *image under $\sigma$*. If all cycles are constant, the two multisets coincide and the product telescopes to 1.

> **Theorem:** *(Copy-constraint soundness.)* If the permutation identity holds at a random $\beta, \gamma$ and $Z$ vanishes correctly at the boundaries, then every $\sigma$-cycle is constant except with probability $O(n/|\mathbb{F}|)$: a non-constant cycle contributes a factor $(\beta \cdot \delta)$ mismatch no random challenge can cancel except by hitting a root of a fixed low-degree polynomial.

In zkEVMs, copy constraints do three jobs. **Intra-circuit**, they thread the stack, program counter, and gas counter across steps. **Cross-circuit**, they identify the RW rows emitted by the EVM circuit with the rows verified by the state circuit — Scroll's super circuit imposes these identifications, with byte strings bound across circuits by RLCs under shared challenges (`evm_word` randomness for 256-bit words, `keccak_input_randomness` for hash inputs) [5][6]. **Public I/O** is bound through instance columns: transaction calldata, pre/post state roots, and block hashes are copy-constrained to committed public inputs, so the proof attests to a *specific* state transition, not merely *some* satisfiable trace.

### 4.5 Recursion via Halo2 Accumulation Schemes

A full block's proof fans out into dozens of subcircuit proofs; verifying them all on L1 is infeasible, and each verification costs $O(n)$ group operations under the inner-product argument. Recursion folds them into one proof — but naively verifying a SNARK inside a SNARK reintroduces the linear cost *inside the circuit*.

Halo2's answer is the **accumulation scheme** over the Pasta 2-cycle (Pallas/Vesta), an instance of *nested amortization* [10]. The observation: the IPA verifier's expensive step is a multiscalar multiplication against the SRS vector $G$. Instead of constraining that MSM in-circuit, the recursion circuit takes the purported $G$ *as witness*, checks all the cheap verifier algebra, and *defers* the claim "$G$ is the true SRS combination" into an **accumulator** — a short, homomorphic digest of deferred checks. Accumulators compose: $n$ verifications fold into one accumulator, and a final **decider** (run once, off-circuit or in a tiny circuit) discharges it. Linear work is thus amortized across the recursion tree rather than paid per node.

Scroll instantiates this as an **aggregation circuit**: it verifies the subcircuit proofs, checks cross-circuit copy constraints and public inputs, and emits a single proof cheap enough for an L1 verifier contract. Polygon zkEVM takes the STARK-native path [8][9]: PIL-described state machines are proved with FRI, then a five-stage recursion pipeline — **compression** (`c12a` prover shrinks the polynomial-heavy batch proof so FRI blowup can rise), **recursion**, **aggregation**, **final**, and **SNARK** (fflonk) stages — each stage verifying the previous stage's verifier, culminating in one Groth16-like proof verified on Ethereum L1. Both are the same idea in different arithmetizations: *verify verifiers, defer the expensive part, decide once*.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

**Asymptotics.** Lookup arguments convert per-operation cost from $O(\text{bit-length})$ constraints to $O(1)$ lookups plus amortized table cost: Plookup's prover pays $O((n+d)\log(n+d))$ for the sort-dominated grand product [1]; LogUp removes the sort, keeping quasilinear field work with strictly cheaper oracles and measured 1.5–4× speedups on batched multi-column workloads [2]. The permutation argument adds one grand-product column per copy-constrained wire set — linear prover overhead, constant verifier overhead.

**Concrete layout constants** (documented, not extrapolated): Scroll's Keccak circuit spends 288 rows per permutation (24 rounds × 12 rows) [5]; the Keccak table is committed once and shared by all circuits via lookups. Polygon's pipeline is fixed at five named stages (compression → recursion → aggregation → final → SNARK) [8], each with its own PIL description and FRI parameters tuned so proof size shrinks monotonically along the chain.

**Proof sketches.** *Plookup ⇒ membership:* the grand-product identity equates $\prod(\gamma + f_i)$ with the table-derived product; distinct multisets give distinct polynomials in $\gamma$, so equality at random $\gamma$ implies multiset equality except with probability $\le (n+d)/|\mathbb{F}|$ [1]. *Permutation ⇒ copy constraints:* shown in §4.4. *Accumulation ⇒ recursion soundness:* each folding step preserves the invariant that the accumulator is satisfiable iff all folded instances were; the decider's check therefore attests to the whole tree [10].

**System-level effect.** The cost hierarchy is consistent across deployments: Keccak-heavy blocks dominate proving time, while pure opcode logic — once arithmetized through state machines and lookups — is comparatively cheap. This inversion (cryptographic primitives, not business logic, as the bottleneck) is the direct consequence of the techniques above working as designed.

## 6. Limitations

**Keccak remains the bottleneck.** Every optimization in §4.1–4.2 trims constants, but Keccak-f's 24 rounds of bitwise diffusion are irreducibly expensive in prime-field circuits; hash-heavy blocks still dominate prover time and memory (tens of GB for large circuits, driven substantially by lookup sorting and commitment oracles [3]).

**Table-size tension.** Plookup-style arguments couple prover cost to table size; enormous tables (e.g., 32-bit instruction decodes) cannot be materialized, which is precisely the gap LogUp and Lasso-style multilinear lookups address [2] — at the cost of extension-field arithmetic and newer, less battle-tested security arguments.

**Setup and field tradeoffs.** Scroll's Halo2 fork uses KZG commitments, inheriting a universal-but-trusted setup; Polygon's FRI-based STARKs are transparent but pay larger proofs until the recursion pipeline compresses them [8][9]. EVM's 256-bit words fit no proving field naturally: BN254 demands limb decomposition with range checks on nearly every value, while Goldilocks-native designs (Polygon) pay conversion costs at the EVM boundary instead.

**Semantic gaps.** Gas metering and circuit cost diverge — Keccak is *cheap* in gas but *expensive* in circuit, inverting DoS economics inside the prover. Precompiles (`modexp`, `ecrecover`, `blake2f`) need bespoke circuits or lookup tables each. And accumulation schemes, while elegant, rest on newer security analyses than plain SNARK verification [10]; a flaw in the folding invariant would compromise every aggregated proof at once.

## 7. Conclusion

Arithmetizing the EVM is an exercise in *representation change*: bitwise operations become table memberships via Plookup, the Halo2 subset argument, and LogUp [1][2][3]; global state consistency becomes a permutation grand product; dozens of subcircuit proofs become one through accumulation schemes and recursion pipelines [8][10]. The unifying pattern is that **verification is cheaper than recomputation** — checking membership in a correct table beats re-deriving the function; checking a folded accumulator beats re-verifying each proof. Scroll's shared Keccak table [5], the bytecode circuit's hash-preimage lookups [6], and Polygon's five-stage STARK-to-SNARK pipeline [8] are all instances of this single principle applied at different scales. The frontier is now clear: push lookups toward the *lookup singularity* where arbitrary functions cost one table access [2], and drive recursion until the marginal cost of one more opcode approaches the cost of one more row — at which point proving Ethereum becomes, at last, a routine computation.
