# Thermoelectric Materials: The Figure of Merit ZT, Phonon-Glass Electron-Crystal Design, and Waste-Heat Recovery

## Abstract

Thermoelectric materials convert heat directly into electricity and vice versa, offering a solid-state route to waste-heat recovery and spot cooling without moving parts or working fluids. Performance is governed by the dimensionless figure of merit *ZT* = *S*²σ*T*/κ, where *S* is the Seebeck coefficient, σ the electrical conductivity, *T* the absolute temperature, and κ the thermal conductivity. Because *S*, σ, and the electronic part of κ are interlocked through carrier concentration, maximizing *ZT* is a problem of constrained decoupling: the ideal material conducts electrons like a crystal but heat like a glass. This thesis develops the transport theory behind *ZT*, the Wiedemann–Franz and Kelvin relations that bound its parameters, the phonon-glass electron-crystal (PGEC) design paradigm, and the modern strategies — band engineering, nanostructuring, anharmonic bonding, and rattling guest atoms — that have pushed bulk figures of merit from the historical ceiling near unity to above 2.5 in SnSe. It surveys the principal material families across operating temperatures, and examines device-level realities: module efficiency, contact engineering, and the radioisotope thermoelectric generators that power deep-space missions.

## 1 Intro

Approximately two-thirds of the energy consumed by human industry ends as waste heat, rejected to the atmosphere, cooling water, or exhaust stacks at temperatures ranging from barely warm to incandescent. Recovering even a fraction of this stream would constitute one of the largest untapped energy resources on the planet. Thermoelectric generators (TEGs) address this problem directly: a temperature gradient across a semiconductor junction produces an electric current through the Seebeck effect, discovered by Thomas Johann Seebeck in 1821, and the inverse process — current producing a temperature difference — yields solid-state refrigeration through the Peltier effect [1]. With no moving parts, no working fluid, silent operation, and decades of proven reliability, thermoelectric devices occupy a unique niche that mechanical heat engines cannot easily fill [8].

The central scientific problem is efficiency. A thermoelectric material must simultaneously satisfy three contradictory requirements: a large Seebeck coefficient *S* (voltage per unit temperature difference), high electrical conductivity σ (so that the generated current is not dissipated internally), and low thermal conductivity κ (so that the temperature gradient is not short-circuited by heat conduction). These properties are bundled into the dimensionless figure of merit [1]:

*ZT* = *S*²σ*T* / κ

The numerator *S*²σ is called the *power factor*. The device conversion efficiency is a monotonically increasing function of *ZT*: for a generator operating between hot-side temperature *Tₕ* and cold-side temperature *T꜀*,

η = (*Tₕ* − *T꜀*)/*Tₕ* · (√(1 + *Z T̄*) − 1) / (√(1 + *Z T̄*) + *T꜀*/*Tₕ*)

where *T̄* is the mean operating temperature [7]. This expression reveals why the community fixates on *ZT*: the Carnot factor sets the thermodynamic ceiling, and the bracketed term — rising steeply through *ZT* ≈ 1 and saturating only slowly thereafter — determines how close a real device comes to it. A material with *ZT* = 1 converts roughly 10–15% of a typical industrial heat stream; reaching *ZT* ≈ 3 would roughly double that, crossing the threshold at which many waste-heat applications become economically compelling [5].

For decades the field stagnated at *ZT* ≈ 1, the value of commercial Bi₂Te₃ alloys. Beginning in the 1990s, the phonon-glass electron-crystal (PGEC) concept of Glen Slack reframed the search: an ideal thermoelectric should scatter phonons as effectively as an amorphous glass while conducting electrons as cleanly as a crystalline semiconductor [5][7]. This thesis follows that arc — from the coupled transport equations, through PGEC materials engineering, to the record-breaking chalcogenides of the 2010s and the device systems that put them to work.

## 2 Background

### Coupled transport and the Kelvin relations

Thermoelectric phenomena are linear-response couplings between charge and heat currents. In the presence of an electric field **E** and a temperature gradient ∇*T*, the electric current density **J** and heat current density **Q** obey

**J** = σ(**E** − *S*∇*T*)
**Q** = *S T* **J** − κ∇*T*

The three classical effects are special cases of these coupled equations [1][7]:

- **Seebeck effect** (1821): open-circuit (**J** = 0) voltage per unit gradient, *S* = −Δ*V*/Δ*T*.
- **Peltier effect** (1834): heat carried per unit current at an isothermal junction, the Peltier coefficient Π = *Q*/*J*.
- **Thomson effect** (1851): reversible heating in a current-carrying conductor sustaining a temperature gradient, with Thomson coefficient τ.

Lord Kelvin's thermodynamic analysis linked the three coefficients through relations now bearing his name:

> **Theorem: Kelvin relations.** For a thermoelectric material in local equilibrium, the Peltier and Seebeck coefficients satisfy Π = *S T*, and the Thomson coefficient satisfies τ = *T* d*S*/d*T*. Consequently the full thermoelectric response is determined by a single function, *S*(*T*), plus the conductivities σ and κ [7].

This unification is why the community speaks almost exclusively of the Seebeck coefficient: measuring *S*(*T*) determines the reversible thermal physics, and optimizing a material reduces to optimizing the triad (*S*, σ, κ).

### The Wiedemann–Franz constraint

The thermal conductivity decomposes into electronic and lattice contributions, κ = κₑ + κₗ. The electronic part is tied to σ by the Wiedemann–Franz law:

> **Theorem: Wiedemann–Franz law.** In a degenerate electron gas, κₑ = *L*σ*T*, where the Lorenz number *L* approaches the Sommerfeld value *L₀* = 2.44 × 10⁻⁸ WΩK⁻² for metals and simple degenerate semiconductors. For non-degenerate or multi-band thermoelectrics, *L* deviates substantially and can be extracted from *S* measurements [7].

This relation exposes the cruelest aspect of thermoelectric optimization: raising σ to improve the power factor inevitably raises κₑ. Since κₑ = *L*σ*T*, the electronic part of *ZT* can be rewritten as *S*²/*L* in the limit where κₗ → 0 — meaning that once the lattice contribution is suppressed, the remaining lever is purely the ratio of thermopower to the Lorenz number [7]. Real materials therefore optimize along two orthogonal axes: maximize the power factor through band-structure and doping design, and minimize κₗ through phonon engineering.

### The Mott picture and the quality factor

The Seebeck coefficient follows from the Mott relation for a degenerate conductor:

*S* = (π²*k_B*²*T* / 3*e*) · (d ln σ(*E*) / d*E*) evaluated at the Fermi energy *E_F*

Large *S* demands a strongly energy-dependent conductivity at the Fermi level — a sharp density of states, heavy effective mass, or resonant levels — whereas large σ demands high carrier concentration and mobility. The optimum doping lands in the range of roughly 10¹⁹–10²¹ carriers per cubic centimeter, the classic heavily-doped semiconductor window [1][7].

Snyder and Toberer formalized this trade-off with the *thermoelectric quality factor* *B*, a material parameter combining valley degeneracy *Nᵥ*, band effective mass, mobility, and lattice thermal conductivity; the maximum achievable *ZT* at optimal doping is a function of *B* alone [1][7]. The design imperative follows: seek small-bandgap semiconductors of heavy elements with multiple degenerate carrier pockets, high mobility, and soft, anharmonic lattices.

## 3 Methodology

Because thermoelectric performance is a property of *transport coefficients* rather than of a single static structure, progress relies on a coupled methodology of theory, synthesis, and measurement. This section describes the standard analytical and experimental framework used to evaluate and design materials discussed throughout this thesis.

### 3.1 Transport modeling

First-principles evaluation begins with electronic band structures from density functional theory, feeding the linearized Boltzmann transport equation under the relaxation-time approximation. The Seebeck coefficient, conductivity, and electronic thermal conductivity emerge as Fermi-window integrals of the transport distribution function Σ(*E*):

σ = ∫ Σ(*E*)(−∂*f*/∂*E*) d*E*
*S* = (1/*eT*σ) ∫ (*E* − *E_F*) Σ(*E*)(−∂*f*/∂*E*) d*E*

where *f* is the Fermi–Dirac distribution. For phonons, the lattice thermal conductivity is computed from the phonon Boltzmann equation or the Callaway/Debye model:

κₗ = (1/3) ∫ *C*(ω) *v*(ω)² τ(ω) dω

with mode heat capacity *C*, group velocity *v*, and relaxation time τ(ω), decomposed by Matthiessen's rule into Umklapp, point-defect, boundary, and electron–phonon scattering channels — the basis of the hierarchical "all-scale" phonon scattering strategy [5][7].

### 3.2 The Lorenz number from Seebeck data

Because direct separation of κₑ and κₗ is experimentally difficult, the field standard is to estimate the Lorenz number from the measured Seebeck coefficient using single-parabolic-band or Kane-band models, then subtract κₑ = *L*σ*T* from the total κ [7]. A compact Python implementation of this workflow is:

```python
import numpy as np

L0 = 2.44e-8  # Sommerfeld value, W*ohm/K^2

def lorenz_from_seebeck(S_uv_per_K):
    """Estimate Lorenz number from Seebeck coefficient (SPB model, acoustic phonon scattering)."""
    S = abs(S_uv_per_K) * 1e-6
    # Empirical Kim et al. correlation (APL Materials 3, 041506, 2015)
    L = 1.5 + np.exp(-abs(S) / 116e-6)
    return L * 1e-8

def figure_of_merit(S, sigma, kappa_total, T):
    S, sigma, kappa_total = abs(S), sigma, kappa_total
    L = lorenz_from_seebeck(S * 1e6)
    kappa_e = L * sigma * T
    kappa_l = kappa_total - kappa_e
    ZT = S**2 * sigma * T / kappa_total
    return {"ZT": ZT, "kappa_e": kappa_e, "kappa_l": kappa_l, "L": L}

def device_efficiency(ZTbar, Th, Tc):
    carnot = (Th - Tc) / Th
    r = np.sqrt(1 + ZTbar)
    return carnot * (r - 1) / (r + Tc / Th)

# thermoelectric example parameters
print(figure_of_merit(S=210e-6, sigma=1.1e5, kappa_total=1.5, T=300))
print("eta =", device_efficiency(ZTbar=1.0, Th=500, Tc=300))
```

### 3.3 Experimental characterization

The standard measurement triad is: (i) Seebeck coefficient and electrical conductivity on a bar sample in a commercial ZEM system under helium; (ii) thermal diffusivity by the laser-flash method, converted to κ via measured density and heat capacity; (iii) Hall-effect carrier concentration and mobility. Key pitfalls include cold-finger effects in *S* measurements, radiative losses in laser flash at high temperature, and — as the SnSe controversy demonstrated — sample density and surface oxidation, which can corrupt κ and therefore *ZT* by tens of percent [3][11].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The PGEC paradigm and cage compounds

Slack's phonon-glass electron-crystal proposal observed that the conflict between κₗ and σ can be resolved structurally rather than electronically: build a covalently bonded framework that carries charge efficiently (the "electron crystal"), and embed loosely bound heavy guest atoms in its voids whose independent "rattling" vibrations scatter phonons resonantly (the "phonon glass") [5][7].

Two material families realize this architecture:

- **Skutterudites** (prototype CoSb₃): a body-centered cubic structure with large icosahedral voids accommodating filler atoms such as Yb, Ce, Ba, or La. The fillers donate carriers to tune doping *and* rattle at low frequencies, depressing κₗ by an order of magnitude. Multiple-filled n- and p-type skutterudites reach *ZT* up to 1.7 at 800 K, making them leading candidates for automotive exhaust recovery [6].
- **Clathrates** (e.g., type-I Ba₈Ga₁₆Ge₃₀): silicon/germanium framework cages trapping guest atoms, with *ZT* up to about 1.3 at 800 K [6].

> **Theorem: the rattler criterion.** A filler atom contributes maximally to phonon suppression when its Einstein-like vibrational frequency falls within the acoustic-phonon spectrum of the host framework, hybridizing with and flattening the acoustic branches. Fillers too light or too tightly bound merely dope the framework without disrupting heat transport [5][7].

### 4.2 Anharmonicity and the SnSe breakthrough

An alternative route to glass-like κₗ needs no guest atoms at all: *intrinsic* anharmonicity. In 2014, Zhao and coworkers reported *ZT* = 2.6 ± 0.3 at 923 K in p-type SnSe single crystals measured along the crystallographic *b* axis — the highest value ever reported for a bulk material at the time, achieved without lead or tellurium [2]. The origin is structural: SnSe adopts a distorted rock-salt layered structure with anomalously large Grüneisen parameters, reflecting strongly anharmonic, anisotropic bonding. The lattice thermal conductivity falls to 0.23 ± 0.03 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ at 973 K, below the values of many glasses, while the Seebeck coefficient remains high along the *b* axis [2]. The result was doubly significant: it broke the psychological *ZT* ≈ 2 barrier and demonstrated that ultralow κₗ can be *intrinsic* rather than nanostructured [2].

The story grew more dramatic. Br-doped n-type SnSe crystals later showed *ZT* ≈ 2.8 at 773 K along the *a* axis [3], and Na-doped polycrystalline SnSe with surface oxides removed reached *ZT* ≈ 2.5 at 773 K [11]. But the record ignited controversy: several groups measured much higher κₗ in polycrystalline samples and questioned whether the single-crystal values were intrinsic, arguing that incompletely densified samples had inflated the apparent performance [3]. The dispute was resolved when polycrystalline SnSe *exceeded* single-crystal *ZT* through control of tin-oxide surface layers — the true source of the excess thermal conductivity — underscoring how sensitive *ZT* is to microstructure and measurement fidelity [3].

### 4.3 Band engineering and nanostructuring

Parallel strategies attack the numerator of *ZT*. **Band convergence** — aligning multiple valence or conduction band valleys at the same energy — increases the valley degeneracy *Nᵥ* and hence the density-of-states effective mass without degrading the mobility of each pocket, directly raising the quality factor *B* [1][7]. Resonant impurity levels (e.g., Tl in PbTe) create a sharp distortion in the density of states near *E_F*, boosting *S* through the Mott relation at fixed carrier concentration [1].

**Nanostructuring** targets phonons across length scales. Grain boundaries and nanoinclusions scatter long-wavelength phonons; point defects and alloy disorder scatter short-wavelength ones. The Bi₂Te₃/Sb₂Te₃ superlattice with *ZT* ≈ 2.4 demonstrated the principle, and bulk nanocomposites made it practical: endotaxial SrTe nanocrystals in PbTe, all-scale hierarchical architectures, and dislocation arrays have each pushed lead-chalcogenide *ZT* beyond 2 [5]. The RSC's review of the field traces this evolution from the apparent *ZT* = 1 limit of traditional alloys to the nanocomposite era that broke it [5].

A striking recent variant is the "liquid-like" paradigm: in Cu₂Se, the selenium sublattice forms a rigid framework while copper ions diffuse through it nearly as fast as in a liquid, suppressing transverse phonon modes and yielding glass-like κₗ in a fully dense crystal — PGEC behavior realized through sublattice melting rather than rattling [10].

### 4.4 The temperature-stratified material families

No single material is best at all temperatures, so practical generators are *segmented* or *cascaded* [6][7]. The established families are:

| Material family | Peak *ZT* | Temperature range | Key mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bi₂Te₃ alloys (n/p) | ~1.0 | 250–450 K | Anisotropic layered structure; commercial standard |
| PbTe / PbSe (n/p) | ~1.4–2.0 | 500–900 K | Band convergence; nanostructuring |
| Filled skutterudites | up to 1.7 | 600–850 K | PGEC rattling fillers [6] |
| Half-Heuslers (e.g., ZrNiSn) | >1.0 | 700–1000 K | High power factor; thermal stability [6] |
| SiGe alloys | ~1.0–1.3 | 900–1300 K | Alloy scattering; space heritage |
| SnSe (p/n crystals) | 2.6–2.8 | 700–950 K | Anharmonic ultralow κₗ [2][3] |
| Mg₂Si₁₋ₓSnₓ | >1.0 | 600–800 K | Lightweight, non-toxic mid-temp [6] |

SiGe deserves special mention: despite its modest *ZT*, its extreme thermal stability made it the material of choice for the radioisotope generators of the Voyager, Galileo, and Cassini missions, illustrating that *system-level* selection weighs stability, sublimation, and lifetime as heavily as peak *ZT* [8].

### 4.5 Module physics: from material to device

A thermoelectric module assembles many n–p leg pairs electrically in series and thermally in parallel between ceramic plates. Material *ZT* is necessary but not sufficient; device efficiency is degraded by electrical and thermal contact resistances, interconnect losses, and *compatibility* — the requirement that the relative current density *u* = *J*/(κ∇*T*) be matched along each leg and across segments, without which a high-*ZT* material cannot deliver its theoretical efficiency [7].

The module figure of merit is further reduced by the fill factor and by parasitic heat conduction through insulation and electrodes. For waste-heat recovery, the economic metric is dollars per watt, not peak efficiency: this favors materials that are abundant, non-toxic, mechanically robust, and processable at scale — criteria that have kept Bi₂Te₃ dominant commercially despite its unremarkable *ZT*, and that motivate interest in Mg₂Si, tetrahedrites, and oxide-controlled SnSe [6][11].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The theoretical claims of this thesis rest on a chain of experimental results:

1. **The *ZT* ≈ 1 ceiling was real and then broken.** Commercial Bi₂Te₃ alloys plateaued near unity for decades; the PGEC cage compounds broke through to 1.3–1.7 at 800 K by demonstrating that κₗ could be driven toward glass values without collapsing mobility [5][6].

2. **Intrinsic anharmonicity suffices.** SnSe single crystals achieved *ZT* = 2.6 ± 0.3 at 923 K with lattice thermal conductivity of 0.23 ± 0.03 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ — proof that ultralow κₗ need not come from nanostructure [2]. The anisotropy of the result (2.6 along *b*, 0.8 along *a*) tracks the anisotropy of the bonding, exactly as the anharmonicity model predicts [2].

3. **Reproducibility is a measurement science.** The SnSe controversy — conflicting κₗ values traced to sample density and tin-oxide surface layers, resolved when polycrystalline samples exceeded single-crystal performance — demonstrates that *ZT* above 2 lives close enough to experimental error bars that metrology is part of the physics [3][11].

4. **The PGEC descriptor is quantitative.** Across tens of thousands of experimental data points, high-*ZT* materials cluster at κₗ/κ ≈ 0.5, confirming that the glass-crystal balance is not merely qualitative but a measurable statistical signature of successful thermoelectrics [4].

5. **Deep-space heritage validates lifetime claims.** The Multi-Mission RTG powering the Curiosity and Perseverance rovers uses PbTe/TAGS thermocouples to convert plutonium-238 decay heat into roughly 110 W of electricity, with a 17-year end-of-design-life — a thermoelectric system operating continuously for over a decade in the harshest environment accessible [8][9]. No laboratory *ZT* measurement carries the evidentiary weight of a decade of flight data.

6. **Efficiency follows the *ZT* formula.** Device measurements across material systems track the η(*ZT̄*, *Tₕ*, *T꜀*) expression of Section 1 within the corrections expected from contacts and compatibility, validating the use of *ZT* as the primary optimization target [1][7].

## 6 Limitations

The field's progress must be weighed against persistent limitations:

- **Efficiency remains modest.** Even *ZT* ≈ 2.5 yields only 15–20% conversion for typical waste-heat temperature differences — far below mechanical heat engines. The DOE's long-standing target of *ZT* ≈ 3 for economic viability remains unreached in a stable, manufacturable material [5].
- **Toxicity and scarcity.** The best-performing families rely on lead and tellurium — toxic and geologically scarce respectively. SnSe's appeal is partly that it is free of both, but selenium supply chains and tin-oxide sensitivity are their own constraints [2][11].
- **Thermal stability and sublimation.** Chalcogenides sublime and oxidize at operating temperature; modules require hermetic encapsulation or inert atmospheres, adding cost and failure modes. SiGe survives where chalcogenides cannot, at the price of lower efficiency [8].
- **Mechanical fragility.** Layered crystals like SnSe cleave easily, complicating module fabrication; the brittleness of single crystals is a major reason polycrystalline development matters [3].
- **Contact engineering.** Interdiffusion at hot-side electrodes degrades modules over thousands of hours; diffusion barriers and metallization stacks are as critical as the thermoelectric material itself [6][7].
- **Metrology uncertainty.** As the SnSe dispute showed, κ measurements at high temperature carry uncertainties that can move *ZT* by ±0.3 or more — comparable to the margins by which records are claimed [3].
- **Economic reality.** At current efficiencies, TEG waste-heat systems struggle to compete with falling solar-plus-storage costs, confining them to niches where reliability, silence, or the absence of maintenance dominates: spacecraft, remote sensors, and industrial sites where the heat is genuinely free [8][9].

## 7 Conclusion

The figure of merit *ZT* distills the central tension of thermoelectricity — the coupling of charge and heat transport — into a single number. The Kelvin relations and the Wiedemann–Franz law define what cannot be decoupled; the PGEC concept, band engineering, and nanostructuring define what can. From Slack's rattling fillers in skutterudites to the anharmonic bonding of SnSe, each advance has exploited a distinct physical mechanism to suppress phonon transport while preserving electron transport, lifting bulk *ZT* from the historical ceiling near 1 to values approaching 3 [1][2][5].

Yet the gap between laboratory *ZT* and deployed watts remains wide, governed by contacts, compatibility, stability, and cost rather than by the figure of merit alone. The most instructive systems are not the record-holders but the workhorses: Bi₂Te₃ in every Peltier cooler, PbTe/TAGS in the RTGs still crawling across Mars, SiGe in the Voyagers now in interstellar space [6][8][9]. Their lesson: thermoelectric success is a systems achievement.

The path forward is increasingly computational. Data-driven PGEC descriptors, machine-learned thermal-conductivity models, and high-throughput screening are converting the field's heuristics into quantitative design rules [4]. If these tools deliver a stable, non-toxic, manufacturable material with *ZT* above 2 across a broad temperature range, thermoelectric waste-heat recovery may finally move from the niche it has occupied since Seebeck's compass needle twitched in 1821 into the mainstream of the energy transition.
