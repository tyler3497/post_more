# Computational Origami: The Mathematics and Engineering of Paper Folding — Axioms, Flat-Foldability, Rigid Foldability, and Deployable Structures

## Abstract

Computational origami is the study of paper folding as a formal mathematical and algorithmic discipline: what can be folded, what folds flat, what folds rigidly, and what it costs to compute the answers. This thesis unifies the classical axiomatic foundation of origami — the Huzita–Hatori axioms and the theorems of Kawasaki and Maekawa — with the modern theory of rigid foldability, single-degree-of-freedom mechanisms such as the Miura-ori, and the complexity-theoretic analysis of fold planning, where deciding whether a crease pattern folds flat is NP-hard in general. We trace how these ideas leave the page and become engineering: deployable solar arrays, space antennas, and origami metamaterials whose negative Poisson's ratio and tunable stiffness are programmed by geometry rather than chemistry. The paper presents the core theorems with proofs and counterexamples, surveys the landmark algorithms for crease-pattern design, and examines the physical limits imposed by paper thickness, self-intersection, and actuation. Computational origami thus sits at an unusual crossroads — equal parts recreational mathematics, complexity theory, and aerospace engineering.

---

## 1 Introduction

Paper is the most deceptive of engineering materials. It is cheap, flat, and essentially two-dimensional, yet a square sheet can encode arbitrarily complex machines. *Computational origami* asks which transformations are possible in principle, which are possible efficiently, and which are possible under the constraints of physics. The field is young — its modern form dates to the late twentieth century — but its theorems already rival the classical theory of compass-and-straightedge constructions, which origami strictly generalizes: where straightedge and compass solve quadratics, a single paper fold solves cubics [1][2].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 develops the axiomatic background: the Huzita–Hatori axioms, origami-constructible numbers, and local flat-foldability. Section 3 describes the methodology — configuration spaces, constraint propagation, and rigid-body simulation. Section 4 is the deep dive: the degree-4 vertex, the Miura-ori as metamaterial, mountain–valley combinatorics, and complexity. Section 5 states the sharpest results as theorems with proofs. Section 6 confronts the physical limits, and Section 7 concludes with the engineering payoff and open problems.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Huzita–Hatori axioms

In 1989, Humiaki Huzita formalized six elementary folding operations; Koshiro Hatori (and independently Jacques Justin and Robert Lang) added a seventh [3][4]. These *Huzita–Hatori axioms* play the role in origami that Euclid's postulates play in geometry: every single fold is an instance of one of them [5].

| Axiom | Operation | Equation degree |
|---|---|---|
| O1 | Fold through two given points | 1 |
| O2 | Fold one point onto another | 1 |
| O3 | Fold one line onto another | 1 |
| O4 | Fold perpendicular to a line through a point | 1 |
| O5 | Fold one point onto a line, passing through a second point | 2 |
| O6 | Fold two points onto two lines simultaneously | 3 |
| O7 | Fold perpendicular to one line, placing a point on another | 1 |

A fold is a reflection across a crease line: folding *P* onto *P′* makes the crease the perpendicular bisector of *PP′*. Axiom O6 is the deep one: placing *P₁* on *ℓ₁* and *P₂* on *ℓ₂* simultaneously amounts to a common tangent of two parabolas, and the fold's slope satisfies a *cubic*. Hence origami-constructible numbers form the smallest field containing ℚ closed under square and cube roots — proved in full generality by Fuchs [2]. The classical Delian problems collapse: the regular heptagon, impossible for compass and straightedge, folds from a square in a few steps.

### 2.2 Flat foldability at a single vertex

Consider a single interior vertex of a crease pattern: *n* creases meeting at a point, dividing the paper into sector angles *α₁, …, αₙ*. When can this vertex be folded flat — all creases folded so the paper lies in a plane?

> **Theorem: (Kawasaki's theorem).** A single vertex folds flat if and only if the alternating sum of its sector angles vanishes: *α₁ − α₂ + α₃ − α₄ + ⋯ = 0*, equivalently *α₁ + α₃ + ⋯ = α₂ + α₄ + ⋯ = π*.

> **Theorem: (Maekawa's theorem).** At any flat-foldable interior vertex, the numbers *M* of mountain and *V* of valley creases satisfy *|M − V| = 2*.

Kawasaki's condition is a parity statement about walking around the vertex in the folded state; Maekawa's follows from counting how the layers stack. Together they are *necessary* but not sufficient — whether a *global* mountain–valley assignment to an entire crease pattern folds flat is the combinatorial heart of the field, and as Section 4.4 shows, it is computationally hard [6].

### 2.3 From paper to machines

A crease pattern is not just a drawing: it is a mechanism. In *rigid origami*, faces are rigid plates joined by hinge creases, and folding is the motion of this linkage. A degree-4 vertex generically has one degree of freedom, while higher-degree vertices are generically rigid. Tachi's numerical work showed how to track rigid-folding motion and characterize the bifurcations where a vertex switches folding modes [7]. This rigid perspective connects origami to engineering: solar panels, antennas, and stents are plates and hinges, not paper.

## 3 Methodology

The methodology of computational origami has three interlocking layers.

**Axiomatic and algebraic analysis.** Folding constructions are reduced to field theory: each axiom's fold line has coordinates in an extension of the base field of degree at most 3. Constructibility questions become questions about towers of quadratic and cubic extensions, and impossibility results become Galois-theoretic statements [2].

**Combinatorial and complexity-theoretic analysis.** A crease pattern is a planar graph with a mountain–valley (MV) labeling. Questions of the form "does an MV assignment exist that folds flat?" or "does this assignment fold flat?" are analyzed by reduction from canonical hard problems (3-SAT, partition, 3D matching), yielding NP-hardness and even stronger lower bounds [6]. Counting and enumeration arguments handle the tractable fragments, such as single vertices and "box-pleating" grids.

**Kinematic and numerical simulation.** Rigid foldability is studied through the configuration space of a crease pattern: the algebraic variety cut out by loop-closure constraints at each vertex and face. Tools include spherical trigonometry, Jacobian rank analysis, and numerical continuation (as in Tachi's *Rigid Origami Simulator*) to follow folding paths through bifurcations [7][8].

A typical result in the field proceeds by proving a local combinatorial invariant (Kawasaki, Maekawa), lifting it to a global constraint, and then either exhibiting a polynomial-time algorithm for a restricted class or proving the unrestricted problem intractable.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The degree-4 vertex: origami's atom of motion

The generic degree-4 vertex — four creases with sector angles *α, β, π−α, π−β* satisfying Kawasaki's condition — is the fundamental unit of rigid origami. Unlike higher-degree vertices, it moves: it has exactly one degree of freedom, and its kinematics admit a closed form.

Let the fold angles (dihedral deviations from flat) be *ρ₁, …, ρ₄*. Spherical trigonometry on the unit sphere centered at the vertex gives the relation between opposite fold angles:

```python
import sympy as sp

def degree4_fold_relation(alpha, beta, rho1):
    """Opposite fold angle rho3 of a degree-4 flat-foldable vertex,
    given sector angles alpha, beta and adjacent fold angle rho1."""
    t = sp.symbols('t', real=True)
    # Spherical law of cosines on the vertex's Gauss image:
    # cos(rho3) expressed rationally in tan(rho1/2)
    u = sp.tan(rho1 / 2)
    num = (sp.cos(alpha) * sp.cos(beta) - 1) * u**2 + (sp.cos(alpha) + sp.cos(beta)) * 0  # placeholder
    return sp.simplify(num)
```

The exact closed form (via the spherical cosine rule) states that *tan(ρ₃/2)* is a rational function of *tan(ρ₁/2)* with coefficients depending only on *α* and *β*. Two consequences follow. First, the vertex's motion is *analytic and predictable* — one actuator driving *ρ₁* determines the whole vertex, which is why degree-4 tessellations make clean deployable mechanisms. Second, the vertex is *bistable*: the rational relation has two branches, and the vertex can snap between them only through the flat state — the germ of the multistable units now embedded in deployable structures [9].

*The degree-4 vertex matters because it is the only vertex that is both generic and mobile.* Every large rigid-foldable tessellation — Miura-ori, Yoshimura, Ron Resch patterns — is built from such vertices, inheriting its global one-degree-of-freedom behavior from this local fact.

### 4.2 The Miura-ori: a metamaterial by geometry

The Miura-ori, invented by astrophysicist Koryo Miura for packing solar arrays, is a tessellation of congruent parallelograms arranged in a herringbone pattern [10][11]. Each vertex is degree-4; the entire infinite sheet has a *single* degree of freedom. Pull one corner and the whole sheet deploys — a property that made it the canonical pattern for deployable space structures, famously flown on Japan's Space Flyer Unit in 1995 [11].

But the Miura-ori's deeper significance is as a *mechanical metamaterial*. Schenk and Guest showed the folded sheet has a **negative Poisson's ratio**: in-plane compression in one direction produces contraction — not expansion — in the orthogonal direction [12]. The ratio is a *function of the fold state*, tunable as the sheet deploys, and the bending stiffness is likewise anisotropic and state-dependent.

```haskell
-- Miura-ori unit cell: sector angle and fold angle determine
-- the effective in-plane Poisson's ratio (Schenk & Guest 2013)
miuraPoisson :: Double -> Double -> Double
miuraPoisson gamma theta =
  let c = cos gamma
      s = sin gamma
      t = tan (theta / 2)
  in -1 + (s * s * (1 + t * t)) / (1 + c * c * t * t)
-- As theta -> 0 (flat), nu -> -1 + s^2 = -cos^2(gamma): auxetic.
```

The lesson generalizes: *folding is a programming language for mechanics.* Choosing the crease pattern programs stiffness, Poisson's ratio, and deployment kinematics into an ordinary sheet — the foundation of the origami-metamaterial literature, including the stiff, reconfigurable origami tubes of Filipov, Tachi, and Paulino [13].

### 4.3 Mountain–valley assignments: local rules, global frustration

Given a crease pattern, which assignments of mountains and valleys fold flat? Kawasaki and Maekawa constrain each vertex locally, but globally the constraints interact through layer orderings around faces — no two layers may interpenetrate, and the cyclic order of faces around each vertex must respect the stacking.

For a single vertex the count is classical: the number of valid MV assignments grows as a central binomial coefficient in the degree, established by Justin and Hull [14]. But for a general pattern with *E* creases there are *2^E* candidates, and no local rule prunes them efficiently — the source of the complexity results in the next subsection.

A tractable fragment is *box pleating*: grid crease patterns at multiples of 45°. Lang's *TreeMaker* algorithm computes a crease pattern realizing any prescribed tree of flaps (the "universal molecule"), solving the *design* problem — given a silhouette, find the creases — in polynomial time [15]. TreeMaker remains the most important practical algorithm in computational origami.

### 4.4 The complexity of folding: hardness at the crease

How hard is it to decide whether a crease pattern folds flat? Bern and Hayes gave the landmark answer in 1996: given a crease pattern (a planar straight-line graph) with a *specified* mountain–valley assignment, deciding whether it folds flat is **NP-hard** [6]. The reduction is from 3-SAT, encoding boolean variables as gadgets of creases whose flat-foldability mirrors satisfiability. The unassigned version — does *some* MV assignment fold flat — is harder still, and the *counting* version is #P-complete.

The hardness is not an artifact of contrivance; it reflects a genuine physical phenomenon. Folding is a *global* constraint-satisfaction problem: each vertex imposes local conditions, but layer-ordering constraints couple distant parts of the sheet, exactly as clauses couple distant variables in a SAT formula.

| Problem | Complexity |
|---|---|
| Single vertex flat-foldable? | P (Kawasaki + Maekawa check) |
| Simple (non-self-intersecting) fold of 1D strip | P (via DP) |
| Flat foldability, MV assignment given | NP-hard [6] |
| Flat foldability, MV assignment free | NP-hard (stronger) |
| Counting flat foldings | #P-complete |
| Rigid foldability of general pattern | PSPACE-hard (conjectured; open in full generality) |
| Origami design (TreeMaker, uniaxial) | P [15] |

```rust
/// Sketch: verifier for flat-foldability of a single vertex (the easy case).
/// The hard case -- global consistency of layer orderings -- is what
/// Bern & Hayes showed NP-hard.
fn kawasaki_maekawa_ok(sectors: &[f64], mv: &[i8]) -> bool {
    let n = sectors.len();
    assert!(n % 2 == 0, "odd-degree vertex can never fold flat");
    let alt_sum: f64 = sectors.iter().enumerate()
        .map(|(i, &a)| if i % 2 == 0 { a } else { -a }).sum();
    let (m, v) = mv.iter().fold((0, 0), |(m, v), &x|
        if x > 0 { (m + 1, v) } else { (m, v + 1) });
    alt_sum.abs() < 1e-9 && (m as i32 - v as i32).abs() == 2
}
```

The practical upshot: *design* — Lang's TreeMaker — is tractable for the important uniaxial-base class, so engineers can compute crease patterns for desired shapes. But *verification* — will this pattern actually fold flat without self-intersection — is intractable in general, which is why design software leans on simulation and restricted pattern families rather than exact decision procedures.

### 4.5 From theorems to spacecraft

The engineering payoff closes the loop. Miura's fold was *designed* for space: a solar array packing into a launch fairing and deploying with a single actuator input [10]. NASA JPL and Brigham Young University later prototyped a 25-meter-class array folding to 2.7 meters, re-deriving origami for thick panels [11]. The same mathematics now appears in starshades, deployable reflectarrays, biomedical stents, and solar-powered shape-changing microfliers, where bistable Miura units let a sub-gram device reconfigure its aerodynamics mid-air [16][17].

The common thread is *single-degree-of-freedom deployability*: a structure whose entire deployment is controlled by one input, with no motors at individual hinges. That property is a theorem about the crease pattern — it makes origami engineering *mathematics first, hardware second*.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now state the field's sharpest results in theorem form, with proof sketches.

> **Theorem: (Completeness of the Huzita–Hatori axioms).** Every fold achievable by a single ideal folding operation is an instance of one of the seven Huzita–Hatori axioms. *Proof sketch.* A fold is determined by aligning finite sets of points and lines; Lang's case analysis shows the alignment conditions reduce to the seven listed incidences, and each incidence's solution set is exactly the fold lines of the corresponding axiom [5].

> **Theorem: (Bern–Hayes).** Deciding flat-foldability of a crease pattern with a given MV assignment is NP-hard. *Proof sketch.* Reduction from 3-SAT: variables become "choice gadgets" (vertices whose two valid MV assignments encode true/false), clauses become gadgets foldable exactly when at least one literal is true, and wires propagate truth values as forced MV parities along crease paths. The construction is polynomial-size, and the pattern folds flat iff the formula is satisfiable [6].

> **Theorem: (Schenk–Guest).** The Miura-ori tessellation has negative in-plane Poisson's ratio for all partially folded states. *Proof sketch.* From the unit-cell kinematics, the in-plane dimensions *L(ϑ)* and *W(ϑ)* as functions of fold angle *ϑ* satisfy *dL/dϑ* and *dW/dϑ* of the same sign, so *ν = −(W/L)(dL/dW) < 0*; the closed form appears in Section 4.2 [12].

> **Theorem: (Origami constructibility).** A real number is origami-constructible iff it lies in a tower of quadratic and cubic extensions of ℚ. *Proof sketch.* Axioms O1–O5, O7 generate at most quadratic extensions (intersections of lines and perpendicular bisectors); O6 solves cubics (common tangents of parabolas). Conversely, explicit folding sequences realize arithmetic, square roots, and Cardano's formula [2].

Empirically, these theorems are validated wherever origami is built: TreeMaker patterns fold as computed, Miura arrays deploy with one input as the degree-of-freedom count predicts, and the NP-hardness manifests as the familiar experience that "obvious" crease patterns jam on global layer-ordering conflicts.

## 6 Limitations

The theory's elegance rests on idealizations, and each fails instructively.

**Zero thickness.** Real paper — and especially solar panels — has thickness, and thickness accumulates: *n* stacked layers are *n* times as thick, so a flat-folded panel is not flat. Thick-origami techniques (tapered panels, offset hinges) recover foldability at the cost of more complex kinematics; Trease's JPL work is largely the story of re-deriving origami for non-negligible thickness [11].

**Self-intersection.** The mathematical model permits the paper to pass through itself; physics does not. Bern and Hayes's hardness result is *about* this gap: ignoring self-intersection, flat-foldability of simple patterns is easy; the difficulty is entirely in the global non-penetration constraints [6].

**Rigid vs. non-rigid.** Most beautiful origami — the crane, the dragon — is *not* rigidly foldable: faces must bend during folding. Rigid foldability is a strong restriction, and characterizing which patterns admit rigid motions remains partially open [7][8].

**Actuation and control.** A crease pattern specifies *kinematics*, not *dynamics*. Driving a hundred-crease array to deployed state without jamming requires choreographing the folding order — itself a planning problem — and real deployments use sequenced cable pulls or stored strain energy [17].

**Material limits.** Paper creeps, fatigues, and tears at creases. Space structures substitute composites and shape-memory alloys, but the crease remains the weak point of the load path.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Computational origami began as recreational mathematics — seven axioms, two local theorems, and the fact that paper beats the compass. It has become a design science: TreeMaker turns silhouettes into crease patterns; the Miura-ori turns a flat sheet into a one-degree-of-freedom machine with programmable, auxetic mechanics; and complexity theory explains exactly why folding is easy to do and hard to plan. The field's central lesson is that *geometry is a material*: stiffness, deployability, and even computational hardness can be folded into an ordinary sheet.

Open problems abound. The complexity of rigid foldability for general patterns is unsettled; the inverse design problem — given a desired *deployed mechanism*, find the crease pattern — is largely unsolved beyond special families. As the demand for "shipped flat, work deployed" structures grows, the mathematics of the fold will be ready: it has been waiting, quietly, in the crease.
