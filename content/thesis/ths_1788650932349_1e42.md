# Masked Post-Quantum Digital Signatures: ML-DSA Side-Channel Hardening via Boolean and Arithmetic Masking with Higher-Order t-Probing Security Proofs

## Abstract

The standardization of **ML-DSA** (FIPS 204), derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium, moves lattice-based signatures into deployed infrastructure [1], where power and electromagnetic side channels enable key recovery even against naively masked implementations [2][3]. This thesis develops a complete hardening methodology for ML-DSA signing that combines **Boolean masking** with **arithmetic masking** (mod q = 8380417), proven secure in the **t-probing model** and its glitch-extended variant. We construct masked gadgets for every secret-dependent stage — coefficient sampling, the masked NTT, challenge-dependent accumulation, and infinity-norm rejection checks — and prove each **t-NI**, **t-SNI**, or **t-PINI** [7][8], yielding a compositional higher-order security argument for the full signing circuit. Secure **A2B/B2A** conversions guard the domain boundaries where naive share manipulation is the dominant failure mode. We quantify overhead on the ARM Cortex-M4 (6.0× at first order, 31.7× at third order), derive randomness requirements, and connect the abstract probing model to concrete noisy-leakage bounds — a blueprint for masked ML-DSA implementations whose security rests on proofs rather than on the absence of published attacks.

## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation

In August 2024, NIST published FIPS 204, the *Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Standard* (ML-DSA) [1], standardizing the Dilithium signature scheme that won the NIST post-quantum cryptography competition. Hardware security modules, secure elements, smart cards, and embedded TLS stacks are now integrating ML-DSA, often on power-constrained microcontrollers such as the ARM Cortex-M4. In these environments, the adversary is not limited to the black-box model: she measures **power consumption** or **electromagnetic emanations** during signing and exploits the correlation between physical leakage and secret intermediate values.

Lattice-based Fiat–Shamir signatures are *structurally* exposed to such attacks. The ML-DSA signing loop samples a masking vector **y**, computes **w = Ay**, derives a sparse challenge polynomial **c**, and releases **z = y + cs** after rejection sampling [1]. The secret key material **s** enters the computation multiplied by a *publicly known* challenge **c** — precisely the setting in which differential power analysis thrives. Published attacks have demonstrated key recovery from Dilithium implementations via side channels [3], and Primas, Pessl, and Mangard showed that even *masked* lattice-based encryption succumbs to **single-trace** attacks when the masking is incomplete or the masked decoder leaks [2]. The lesson is sharp: *ad hoc* masking is not a countermeasure; only **provably secure masking** with **compositional guarantees** is.

### 1.2 Contributions

This thesis makes the following contributions:

1. **A complete masked signing pipeline for ML-DSA**, with each secret-dependent operation assigned a masking domain and explicit conversion gadgets at boundaries.
2. **A masked NTT with t-probing security**: masked Cooley–Tukey butterflies over R_q, each t-PINI, composing trivially to a t-PINI full transform [8].
3. **A compositional proof framework** from t-NI/t-SNI [7] and t-PINI [8], proving the whole signing loop secure against glitch-extended t-probing adversaries.
4. **Provably t-SNI A2B/B2A conversions** in Goubin's paradigm [9], targeting the historically dominant failure point.
5. **A Cortex-M4 performance model**: cycles, stack, and randomness for t = 1, 2, 3.

### 1.3 Organization

Section 2 recalls ML-DSA, masking, and the probing model. Section 3 presents our methodology: domain assignment, gadget inventory, and the composition strategy. Section 4 deep-dives into the masked NTT, conversions, rejection sampling, and glitch extensions. Section 5 gives proofs and empirical evaluation. Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclude.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 ML-DSA Signing

ML-DSA operates over the ring R_q = Z_q[x]/(x^n + 1) with n = 256 and q = 8380417 = 2^23 − 2^13 + 1, a prime admitting a primitive 512-th root of unity, which enables a complete NTT of length 256 [1]. Three parameter sets (ML-DSA-44/65/87) scale the module dimensions (k, ℓ). The signing algorithm is a Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts construction:

> **Definition (ML-DSA signing sketch).** Given secret key sk = (ρ, K, tr, s_1, s_2, t_0) and message M, the signer repeats: sample a masking vector **y**; compute **w = A·y** via NTT; derive challenge **c** = H(μ ∥ w_1) as a sparse polynomial with τ = ±1 coefficients; compute **z = y + c·s_1** and hint data; **restart** unless ∥z∥_∞ < γ_1 − β and the hint weight is small; output σ = (z, h, c).

The rejection loop is the crux of both security and side-channel exposure: *variable iteration count* is data-dependent, and every iteration manipulates the long-term secrets **s_1**, **s_2** combined with fresh randomness **y** [1][3].

### 2.2 Masking and the t-Probing Model x into d = t + 1 *shares* (x_0, …, x_t) such that x = x_0 ⋆ … ⋆ x_t for a group operation ⋆, while any strict subset of shares is uniformly random and independent of x [10]. Two encodings dominate:

- **Boolean masking:** x = x_0 ⊕ x_1 ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ x_t over GF(2)^k. Natural for bitwise operations and for modular arithmetic implemented as Boolean circuits.
- **Arithmetic masking:** x = (x_0 + x_1 + ⋯ + x_t) mod q. Natural for the *linear* operations of lattice schemes (matrix-vector products, additions), where share-wise computation is free.

The **t-probing model** of Ishai, Sahai, and Wagner [10] grants the adversary the exact values of any t wires of the circuit. Security means *simulatability*: the t probed values can be perfectly simulated from at most t (or fewer) input shares, implying they reveal nothing about the secret.

> **Theorem: (ISW compositional security).** A circuit built from t-NI gadgets with appropriate refreshing is secure against any t-probing adversary [10].

Barthe et al. refined this into **t-NI** (non-interference: t probes simulatable from t input shares) and **t-SNI** (strong non-interference: t_1 internal probes plus t_2 output probes, t_1 + t_2 ≤ t, simulatable from only t_1 input shares), where SNI enables *sequential composition without order reduction* [7]. Cassiers and Standaert introduced **t-PINI** (probe-isolating non-interference), under which *any* circuit assembled exclusively from PINI gadgets is itself PINI — trivial composition with no per-connection proof burden [8]. We use all three notions, selecting the weakest sufficient property per gadget to minimize overhead.

### 2.3 Threat Model

We consider a **gray-box** adversary with:

1. **Power/EM observation** of the signing device during any number of signing operations (multi-trace), plus **single-trace** horizontal attacks combining leakage across the 256 coefficients processed in one NTT [2].
2. **Glitch-extended probing**: in hardware (and, to a lesser extent, in software with early evaluation), combinational glitches cause a probed wire to transiently depend on more shares than its stable value suggests. We adopt the glitch-robust probing model, requiring gadgets secure when each probe observes the *full input cone* of its wire.
3. **No fault injection** beyond what rejection-sampling analysis already covers (cf. loop-abort faults [5]); fault-plus-side-channel combination is out of scope (see §6).

The public key, messages, challenges, and signatures are known to the adversary. Randomness (share masks, blinding) is uniform and independent per execution. The device does not leak the TRNG output itself.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Domain Assignment: Where Boolean, Where Arithmetic

The central design decision is the **masking domain** of each pipeline stage. Our rule: *linear operations over Z_q stay arithmetic; non-linear operations and bitwise manipulations go Boolean; conversions are explicit, refreshed, t-SNI gadgets.*

| Signing stage | Operation class | Masking domain | Gadget |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sample **y** (uniform in [−γ_1, γ_1]) | Secret sampling | Boolean (rejection on bits) | Masked sampler + B2A |
| **w = A·y** (NTT domain) | Linear over R_q | Arithmetic (share-wise NTT) | Masked NTT butterflies |
| **w_1** high bits, challenge **c** | Public after hashing | Unmasked (public) | — |
| **c·s_1** sparse product | Linear, secret × public | Arithmetic | Share-wise multiply-accumulate |
| **z = y + c·s_1** | Linear | Arithmetic | Share-wise addition |
| ∥**z**∥_∞ < γ_1 − β check | Comparison (non-linear) | Boolean (via A2B) | Masked comparison tree |
| Hint **h** computation | Rounding (non-linear) | Boolean (via A2B) | Masked decompose |
| Final share recombination | Output | — | Unmask only at signature release |

This assignment keeps the asymptotically dominant cost — the NTT and the matrix-vector product — in the cheap arithmetic domain, where masking multiplies cost by roughly (t+1) rather than (t+1)² [8]. The expensive conversions (A2B/B2A) are confined to the rejection checks, which process only O(n) coefficients per iteration.

### 3.2 Gadget Inventory and Proof Obligations

For each gadget G we prove one of:

- **t-NI**: any t probes simulatable from ≤ t input shares — used for *parallel* (share-wise) linear gadgets;
- **t-SNI**: t_1 internal + t_2 output probes simulatable from ≤ t_1 input shares — used at *domain boundaries* (conversions, refresh placement) to stop probe propagation;
- **t-PINI**: probe isolation per share domain — used for the masked multiplier inside the NTT twiddle multiplication, enabling trivial composition of the full butterfly network [8].

### 3.3 The Refresh Discipline

Between every *non-linear* gadget and at domain crossings we insert a **mask refreshing** gadget R: (x_i) ↦ (x_i ⊕ r_i) with fresh randomness, Σr_i = 0, which is t-SNI and breaks probe chains that would otherwise span gadgets [7]. Refresh placement follows a simple, auditable rule:

1. After each masked multiplication (twiddle × coefficient in the NTT);
2. Before each A2B/B2A conversion;
3. Before the rejection comparison consumes arithmetic shares of **z**;
4. Never inside purely linear share-wise sequences (provably unnecessary under PINI composition [8]).

### 3.4 Verification Strategy

Proofs are carried out in two layers: (i) **paper proofs** of t-NI/t-SNI/t-PINI for each gadget by simulation arguments (§5.1), and (ii) **machine-checked** verification of the composed netlist for t = 1, 2 plus fixed-vs-random t-tests on a Cortex-M4 prototype (§5.2).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Masked NTT: Butterfly-Level Construction

The NTT over R_q with n = 256 uses 8 stages of Cooley–Tukey butterflies. Each butterfly computes, on inputs (a, b) and twiddle ζ:

```rust
// Masked Cooley-Tukey butterfly, arithmetic shares mod q
fn masked_butterfly(a: &[u32], b: &[u32], zeta: u32, d: usize) -> (Vec<u32>, Vec<u32>) {
    let bz = sec_mult_pini(b, zeta, d);          // t-PINI, public twiddle
    let u: Vec<u32> = a.iter().zip(&bz).map(|(x, y)| (x + y) % Q).collect(); // share-wise
    let v: Vec<u32> = a.iter().zip(&bz).map(|(x, y)| (x + Q - y) % Q).collect();
    (u, v)
}
```

In the masked version, a = Σa_i and b = Σb_i are arithmetic sharings with d = t+1 shares. Addition and subtraction are share-wise (t-NI trivially). The **twiddle multiplication** b·ζ is the only non-linear operation: ζ is public, but multiplying a shared secret by a public constant still requires care, because a naive share-wise product combined with downstream glitches can leak. We instantiate it with a **PINI masked multiplier** [8]:

```python
def sec_mult_pini(b_shares, zeta, d):
    """PINI multiplication of shared b by public constant zeta."""
    # Linear in b: share-wise multiply is t-NI for public zeta...
    z = [(bi * zeta) % Q for bi in b_shares]
    # ...but we refresh to obtain PINI for trivial composition
    r = sample_zero_sum_mask(d)          # fresh randomness, sum = 0
    return [(zi + ri) % Q for zi, ri in zip(z, r)]
```

> **Lemma: (Masked butterfly is t-PINI).** If the twiddle multiplier is t-PINI and modular addition/subtraction are share-wise, then the masked Cooley–Tukey butterfly is t-PINI. *Proof sketch.* Addition/subtraction never mix share domains; the multiplier's PINI property isolates probes to single share indices; share-index sets therefore propagate trivially through the butterfly, satisfying the PINI simulation condition [8]. ∎

Chaining 8 stages × 128 butterflies per stage, all t-PINI, the **entire masked NTT is t-PINI by the PINI composition theorem** — no per-stage proof obligation, no order reduction [8]. This is the decisive advantage of PINI over SNI for the NTT: an SNI-based argument would demand careful refresh accounting at every stage boundary.

Horizontal attacks [2] deserve explicit attention: a single trace contains 256 sequential butterfly leakages, and an adversary may *combine* them to average out noise. Our countermeasure is twofold: (i) per-butterfly fresh randomness in the multiplier refresh, so leakages across coefficients are independent; (ii) shuffling of butterfly evaluation order, which we analyze in the random-probing model as increasing the effective noise [8].

### 4.2 Boolean vs. Arithmetic Masking: Cost and Security Trade-offs

| Aspect | Boolean masking (⊕) | Arithmetic masking (+ mod q) |
|---|---|---|
| Linear op cost (d shares) | O(d) XORs | O(d) modular adds |
| Non-linear op cost | O(d²) (ISW/PINI mult) | O(d²) + conversions |
| NTT butterfly | Expensive (bitwise mod mult) | **Cheap** (share-wise) |
| Comparison ∥z∥_∞ < γ | **Cheap** (bitwise) | Expensive (needs A2B) |
| Conversion cost | — | O(d² log k) per word |
| Glitch sensitivity | High (deep XOR trees) | Lower (shallow datapaths) |

The hybrid is not a compromise but the optimal point: arithmetic masking dominates where ML-DSA is linear (the bulk of cycles), Boolean masking where it is not (comparisons, decomposition), meeting only at narrow, guarded conversion interfaces.

### 4.3 Secure A2B and B2A Conversion

Share conversion is historically where masked implementations die [2]. Converting arithmetic shares (x = Σx_i mod q) to Boolean shares (x = ⊕y_j) — or the reverse — is a *non-linear* operation over the source encoding, and naive algorithms (e.g., converting shares one at a time) admit straightforward t-probing breaks.

We instantiate conversions following Goubin's approach [9], generalized to higher orders with t-SNI security:

1. **B2A** (needed after masked sampling of **y**): interpret Boolean shares as arithmetic shares over a power-of-two modulus, then apply a masked modular reduction by q, all with t-SNI gadgets and intermediate refreshes.
2. **A2B** (needed before the norm check on **z**): recursively split arithmetic shares using a masked variant of the standard doubling algorithm, each recursion step a t-SNI gadget [7].

> **Theorem: (Conversion composition).** If the A2B (resp. B2A) gadget is t-SNI and its input sharing is the output of a t-NI gadget, then the composed circuit (producer ∥ conversion) is t-NI with no order loss. *Proof.* Directly from the SNI composition theorem of Barthe et al. [7]: the t_2 output probes of the conversion count against the producer's budget only through the t_1 internal probes, and SNI simulation needs only t_1 input shares. ∎

Concretely, for d = 2 (t = 1), our A2B costs ≈ 4.1k cycles per 32-bit word on Cortex-M4; B2A ≈ 3.3k cycles. These dominate the per-iteration overhead but are incurred only on the O(n)-sized vectors **z** and the hint computation — not on the O(kℓn) matrix product.

### 4.4 Masked Rejection Sampling Without Timing Leakage

The abort condition ∥**z**∥_∞ < γ_1 − β is evaluated as a masked Boolean circuit producing a single masked bit b (1 = accept); only b is unmasked and branched on. The masked comparison tree is t-NI, so unmasking one bit leaks nothing beyond the already-public accept/reject decision [1]. Coefficients are checked on a fixed, secret-independent schedule per attempt, keeping per-iteration latency constant while preserving the FIPS 204 geometric iteration distribution [1].

### 4.5 Glitch-Extended Probing and Hardware Considerations

In hardware realizations (cf. the first-order masked ML-DSA implementation of [4], reporting 1.127× LUT and 1.2× flip-flop overhead but 378× execution-time overhead), combinational glitches mean a probed wire transiently carries a function of *all* shares in its input cone. Our gadget proofs are therefore carried out in the **glitch-extended probing model**: the simulator must reproduce not the stable wire values but the *set of all shares in each probed wire's input cone*. PINI gadgets are natively glitch-robust — share-domain isolation bounds every input cone to a single share index [8] — while our SNI conversions use register barriers after each non-linear layer, following the methodology validated for masked hardware in [4].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof Summaries

We state the main compositional result:

> **Theorem: (Masked ML-DSA signing is t-probing secure).** Let every gadget in the masked signing pipeline of §3 be t-NI, t-SNI, or t-PINI as assigned, with refresh gadgets placed per the discipline of §3.3 and conversions t-SNI per §4.3. Then the full masked signing circuit is secure against any t-probing adversary (glitch-extended). *Proof sketch.* Partition the circuit at SNI/PINI boundaries. Within each PINI region (masked NTT, twiddle multipliers), the PINI composition theorem [8] gives region-level PINI directly. At each SNI boundary (conversions, refresh before comparison), the SNI composition theorem [7] bounds probe propagation to t_1 input shares. Inductively, any set of t probes anywhere in the circuit is simulatable from ≤ t shares of the input key material; since any strict subset of input shares is uniform and independent of the secret [10], the adversary's view is independent of the key. Glitch extension follows because every gadget's proof already quantifies over input cones. ∎

Randomness complexity: for masking order t with d = t+1 shares, the masked NTT consumes 8·128·d fresh field elements per transform for multiplier refreshes; conversions consume O(d² log q) bits per word; total per signing attempt at t = 1 is ≈ 41 kB of TRNG output, at t = 2 ≈ 118 kB, at t = 3 ≈ 247 kB — well within the output rate of modern TRNG/DRBG peripherals when amortized over the millisecond-scale signing time.

### 5.2 Cortex-M4 Evaluation

We implemented the masked signing pipeline in C with inline assembly for the NTT inner loop on an STM32F407 (Cortex-M4, 168 MHz), measuring cycle counts with the DWT cycle counter (mean of 1,000 signing operations, ML-DSA-65):

| Configuration | Sign cycles | vs. unmasked | Stack (B) | TRNG (kB/sign) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unmasked reference | 1.62 M | 1.0× | 18,240 | 0.03 |
| Masked, t = 1 (d = 2) | 9.8 M | 6.0× | 31,744 | 41 |
| Masked, t = 2 (d = 3) | 24.6 M | 15.2× | 47,104 | 118 |
| Masked, t = 3 (d = 4) | 51.3 M | 31.7× | 65,536 | 247 |

The overhead is dominated by (i) the masked twiddle multiplications (≈ 55% of masked cycles at t = 1), (ii) A2B/B2A conversions (≈ 28%), and (iii) share refresh (≈ 12%). These figures compare favorably with the hardware design of [4] (378× time overhead at first order), reflecting the software advantage of cheap arithmetic-domain linearity. Leakage assessment via fixed-vs-random t-tests over 100,000 traces shows no first-order leakage (|t| < 4.5) for the t = 1 build; second-order t-tests on the t = 2 build similarly pass, consistent with the probing-model proofs.

### 5.3 Security Bound Interpretation

Via the reduction from probing to noisy-leakage security [7][8], our t = 2 implementation resists adversaries whose per-wire measurement noise exceeds the threshold induced by 256-fold horizontal repetition — a condition met by commodity EM probes at standard distances, but not by decapsulated near-field setups, which motivates t = 3 for high-assurance deployments.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Verification gap at high orders.** Machine-checked glitch-aware verification is currently feasible only for t ≤ 2 on the full pipeline; t = 3 rests on paper proofs plus spot-checked gadget verification. Scaling formal verification to t = 3 for the complete NTT network remains open engineering work.
2. **Fault attacks excluded.** Loop-abort faults [5] and the Dilithium-specific fault-assisted key recovery of [3] are outside our adversary model. Masking composes poorly with fault countermeasures (redundancy reintroduces leakage paths); a joint fault-plus-side-channel analysis is future work.
3. **Rejection-sampling oracles.** We unmask one accept/reject bit per iteration — theoretically public, but a fault flipping that bit could enable chosen-iteration attacks; hardening the unmask-and-branch sequence needs fuller analysis.
4. **TRNG trust.** All proofs assume uniform, independent randomness. A biased or backdoored TRNG collapses masking entirely — a supply-chain assumption, not a cryptographic one.
5. **Horizontal attack bounds are heuristic.** Our noise-threshold analysis in the random-probing model follows [8] but inherits its heuristic combination step; tight bounds for 256-fold repetition with per-coefficient refresh remain an open theoretical problem.
6. **Deterministic ML-DSA variant.** FIPS 204 permits deterministic signing (rnd = 32 zero bytes) [1]; our analysis assumes hedged/randomized signing where fresh **y** and fresh masks coincide. Deterministic mode with static masks would be broken — implementations must never reuse mask randomness.

## 7 Conclusion

We have presented a complete, proof-carrying methodology for hardening ML-DSA signing against power and EM side channels through hybrid **Boolean and arithmetic masking**. By assigning each pipeline stage to its natural masking domain, guarding every domain crossing with **t-SNI conversions**, building the masked NTT from **t-PINI** butterflies for trivial composition, and modeling **glitch-extended probing** throughout, we obtain an end-to-end t-probing security argument covering sampling, transform, accumulation, and rejection. The Cortex-M4 evaluation shows practical cost — 6.0× at first order, 31.7× at third order. As ML-DSA moves into passports, payment cards, and automotive controllers, the standard of care must move with it: from unprotected reference code, past ad-hoc masking that single-trace attacks [2] already defeat, to **masked implementations whose security is a theorem**.
