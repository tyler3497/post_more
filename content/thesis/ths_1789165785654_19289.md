# Hybrid Automata and Reachability Analysis: Hamilton–Jacobi Methods, Barrier Certificates, and the Verification of Cyber-Physical Systems

## Abstract

Cyber-physical systems — autonomous vehicles, surgical robots, aircraft collision-avoidance protocols — couple discrete mode switching with continuous physical dynamics, and their failures can be catastrophic. Testing alone cannot establish safety: the state spaces are infinite and the behaviors adversarial. This thesis develops the foundations and algorithms of *reachability analysis* for cyber-physical systems, grounded in the hybrid automaton formalism of Alur et al. [1]. We survey three complementary verification paradigms: Hamilton–Jacobi–Isaacs partial differential equations solved by convergent level-set methods [3], which compute reachable sets for nonlinear differential games; *barrier certificates* [4], scalar functions whose existence proves forward invariance of a safe region without any reachable-set computation, synthesized tractably via sum-of-squares programming; and *flowpipe* methods based on zonotopes and Taylor models [5][6][7], which scale to hundreds of dimensions. We delineate the decidability boundary — initialized rectangular automata are PSPACE-complete while a single stopwatch renders reachability undecidable [2] — and compare the tool ecosystem (SpaceEx, Flow*, CORA, JuliaReach, DryVR) on benchmarks from autonomous driving and robotics, concluding with open problems for making formal verification routine engineering practice.

---

## 1. Introduction

Modern engineering is dominated by systems in which software makes discrete decisions that steer continuous physical processes: a lane-change controller switching a car's steering law, an aircraft autopilot reconfiguring flaps during landing, an insulin pump reacting to continuous glucose readings. Such *cyber-physical systems* emerge from the interaction of a discrete program with an analog environment [1]. Their safety is non-negotiable, yet their verification is among the hardest problems at the intersection of computer science and control theory.

The fundamental difficulty is *reachability*. Given a set of initial states and a set of unsafe states — a collision configuration, an envelope violation — does there exist an execution driving the system from the former into the latter? For hybrid systems, whose continuous variables range over the uncountable reals, the reachable set is an infinite geometric object that must be characterized analytically or approximated rigorously.

This thesis presents a unified account of how the field answers that question. Our contributions are fourfold:

1. We present the **hybrid automaton** formalism and its operational semantics, the common language of all subsequent techniques.
2. We develop the three dominant algorithmic families — **Hamilton–Jacobi reachability**, **barrier certificates**, and **set-based flowpipe construction** — with their guarantees, trade-offs, and tool realizations.
3. We map the **decidability landscape**, identifying which syntactic restrictions buy tractability and which generalizations destroy it.
4. We ground the theory in **empirical evidence**: tool comparisons, automotive and robotics case studies, and ARCH competition benchmarks.

The intended reader is a graduate student or researcher in formal methods, control, or robotics seeking both the theorems and the engineering reality of verifying cyber-physical systems.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Hybrid Automaton Formalism

A hybrid automaton, in the Alur–Henzinger formulation [1], is a finite directed multigraph whose vertices are *control modes* and whose edges are *control switches*, annotated with continuous dynamics:

> **Definition (Hybrid automaton).** A hybrid automaton $H$ is a tuple $(Loc, Var, Init, Inv, Flow, Trans, Guard, Reset)$ where:
>
> - $Loc$ is a finite set of *locations* (discrete modes);
> - $Var = \\{x_1, \dots, x_n\\}$ is a finite set of real-valued *continuous variables*;
> - $Init(l) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is the set of initial continuous states in location $l$;
> - $Inv(l) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is the *invariant*: flow may continue in $l$ only while $x \in Inv(l)$;
> - $Flow(l)$ is a predicate over $x$ and $\dot{x}$ constraining the continuous dynamics (e.g., $\dot{x} = f_l(x)$);
> - $Trans \subseteq Loc \times Loc$ is the set of discrete transitions;
> - $Guard(e) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ enables transition $e$ when the continuous state satisfies it;
> - $Reset(e) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^n$ relates pre-jump states to post-jump states.

A *state* is a pair $(l, x)$. An *execution* alternates *flows* — continuous evolution under $Flow(l)$ while $x \in Inv(l)$ — and *jumps* — transitions $e = (l, l')$ taken when $x \in Guard(e)$, with the state updated via $Reset(e)$.

The canonical running example is the **thermostat**: two locations, *on* and *off*, with first-order thermal dynamics in each; the guard $x \geq 22$ switches heating off, and $x \leq 18$ switches it on. The same primitives scale to bouncing balls, multi-gear transmissions, and six-mode aircraft autoland models [3].

### 2.2 Safety as Reachability

A *safety property* asserts that "nothing bad ever happens": the system never enters a designated *unsafe set* $\mathcal{X}_u$. Letting $Reach(X_0)$ denote all states reachable from the initial set $X_0$, safety holds iff $Reach(X_0) \cap \mathcal{X}_u = \emptyset$. Verification reduces to computing, or conservatively bounding, the reachable set.

Two directions of analysis are distinguished [3]: **forward reachability** propagates the initial set along the dynamics, while **backward reachability** computes, from a target (unsafe) set, the states that can be driven into it — doubling as the states from which a controller must actively steer away.

Because exact reachable sets are rarely computable, practical methods compute **overapproximations** $\overline{Reach} \supseteq Reach$ (sound for proving safety) or **underapproximations** (sound for falsification). The tension between *precision* and *scalability* organizes the entire field.

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is a *synthetic survey with technical depth*: rather than proving every result from first principles, we present the core theorems with proof sketches that expose the key ideas, instantiate each method on a common running example, and compare the methods along uniform axes. Our comparison methodology evaluates each paradigm on:

1. **Expressiveness** — which dynamics classes it handles (linear, polynomial, general nonlinear, differential games with adversarial inputs);
2. **Guarantee type** — exact, convergent approximation, or sound overapproximation;
3. **Scalability** — the state dimensions demonstrated in the literature and the asymptotic complexity;
4. **Tool maturity** — the state of publicly available implementations and their benchmark performance.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Hamilton–Jacobi Reachability and Level-Set Methods

The Hamilton–Jacobi (HJ) approach, developed principally by Mitchell, Bayen, and Tomlin [3], treats reachability as a *differential game*. Consider a system $\dot{x} = f(x, a, b)$ with control input $a \in A$ (chosen to avoid the target) and disturbance input $b \in B$ (chosen adversarially to force entry into the target). Represent the target set $\mathcal{G}_0$ as the zero sublevel set of a Lipschitz function $g$: $\mathcal{G}_0 = \\{x : g(x) \leq 0\\}$. The *backward reachable set* over horizon $T$ — the states from which the disturbance can force entry into $\mathcal{G}_0$ despite the best control effort — is exactly the sublevel set of the *viscosity solution* $V(x,t)$ of the Hamilton–Jacobi–Isaacs equation:

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \min\\Bigl\\{0, \max_{a \in A}\min_{b \in B}\, \nabla_x V \cdot f(x,a,b)\Bigr\\} = 0, \qquad V(x,0) = g(x).$$

> **Theorem (Mitchell–Bayen–Tomlin [3]).** The zero sublevel set $\\{x : V(x,-T) \leq 0\\}$ of the viscosity solution above equals the backward reachable set of $\mathcal{G}_0$ over horizon $T$. Moreover, the $\min\\{0, \cdot\\}$ term ensures the reachable set grows monotonically with the horizon, so states once classified unsafe can never be reclassified safe.

The viscosity solution — the unique weak solution satisfying a comparison principle — is essential because the value function develops kinks where optimal strategies switch. Numerically, the PDE is solved on a Cartesian grid using *level-set methods*: upwind finite differences, essentially non-oscillatory (ENO) stencils, and Lax–Friedrichs numerical Hamiltonians, as implemented in Mitchell's ToolboxLS. These monotone, consistent schemes converge to the viscosity solution as the grid is refined.

The method's signature strength is **exactness in the limit for fully nonlinear dynamics and adversarial inputs** — no convexity or linearity assumptions are needed. Its signature weakness is the **curse of dimensionality**: a grid with $N$ points per dimension in $\mathbb{R}^n$ requires $N^n$ storage, limiting practical computations to roughly $n \leq 5$–$6$. For hybrid systems, the continuous computation is performed mode-by-mode and coupled across switches via the *reach-avoid operator*, formulated as a constrained HJ variational inequality [3]. The landmark application remains aircraft conflict resolution: Bayen, Crück, and Tomlin computed overapproximations of unsafe zones in a sector of the Oakland Air Traffic Control Center, automatically generating conflict-free maneuvers.

### 4.2 Barrier Certificates: Lyapunov Functions for Safety

Where HJ reachability computes *sets*, barrier certificates [4] compute *proofs*. The insight of Prajna and Jadbabaie — first for hybrid systems in 2004, then extended to worst-case and stochastic settings — is that safety can be certified by a single scalar function, in direct analogy to how Lyapunov functions certify stability:

> **Theorem (Barrier certificate [4]).** Let $\dot{x} = f(x)$, with initial set $\mathcal{X}_0$ and unsafe set $\mathcal{X}_u$. If there exists a continuously differentiable function $B: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ such that
>
> 1. $B(x) > 0$ for all $x \in \mathcal{X}_u$ (positive on the unsafe set),
> 2. $B(x) \leq 0$ for all $x \in \mathcal{X}_0$ (non-positive on the initial set),
> 3. $\nabla B(x) \cdot f(x) < 0$ whenever $B(x) = 0$ (strictly decreasing on the zero level set),
>
> then no trajectory starting in $\mathcal{X}_0$ can enter $\mathcal{X}_u$. The sublevel set $\\{x : B(x) \leq 0\\}$ is a *forward invariant* separating the two.

The geometric picture is vivid: the zero level set of $B$ is a wall between safe and unsafe regions, and the vector field points strictly inward everywhere along the wall, so trajectories can never cross it — a computational form of Nagumo's 1942 invariance theorem.

Crucially, **no reachable-set computation is required** — nonlinearity, uncertainty, and constraints are handled directly. For *polynomial* systems with semialgebraic initial and unsafe sets, the search for $B$ becomes a *sum-of-squares (SOS)* program: writing $B$ and the multipliers witnessing the inequalities as SOS polynomials reduces the problem to semidefinite programming (SDP), solvable in polynomial time for fixed degree. The framework extends to hybrid systems (one barrier per mode, with jump conditions ensuring the wall survives discrete transitions [4]), to stochastic systems (bounding the *probability* of reaching the unsafe set via supermartingale arguments [4]), and to control synthesis, where *control barrier functions* convert the certificate condition into a pointwise constraint on the input, yielding quadratic-program-based safe controllers now standard in robotics.

The price of this elegance is *incompleteness in practice*: the SOS relaxation is sound but not complete — a system may be safe yet admit no polynomial barrier of the chosen degree — and SDP scales poorly beyond a dozen states.

### 4.3 Zonotopic and Taylor-Model Flowpipe Construction

The third paradigm computes *flowpipes*: sequences of sets $\Omega_0, \Omega_1, \dots$ covering all trajectories over successive time intervals. Its workhorse is the **zonotope**, a centrally symmetric polytope $Z = \\{c + \sum_{i=1}^p \beta_i g_i : \beta_i \in [-1,1]\\}$ defined by a center $c$ and generators $g_i$. Zonotopes are closed under linear maps and Minkowski sums — the two operations dominating reachability — and admit efficient order-reduction heuristics that bound the generator count [5].

Girard's 2005 algorithm [5] for uncertain linear systems $\dot{x} = Ax + u$, $\\|u\\|_\infty \leq \mu$, discretizes time and propagates $X_{k+1} = e^{A\delta}X_k \oplus \mathcal{E}$ where $\mathcal{E}$ rigorously bounds the discretization error, producing a guaranteed overapproximation. Two subsequent breakthroughs shaped the modern landscape:

1. **Le Guernic–Girard (2009)** replaced explicit zonotopes with *support functions* $\rho_X(\ell) = \max_{x \in X} \ell^T x$, evaluating sets lazily only in needed directions — enabling systems with *hundreds* of variables. This is the engine inside **SpaceEx** [7], which demonstrated full fixed-point computations beyond 100 dimensions.
2. **Taylor models** (Berz–Makino), adopted by **Flow\***, represent nonlinear reachable sets as Taylor polynomials plus rigorous interval remainders, propagating them through the ODE's Picard operator with validated remainder bounds — extending flowpipes to *nonlinear* dynamics with high accuracy.

Zonotopes are not closed under intersection, so Girard and Le Guernic developed dedicated zonotope–hyperplane intersection algorithms [6], while SpaceEx reduces guard intersection to convex minimization via sandwich algorithms. CORA [8] generalizes the set library — intervals, zonotopes, constrained and polynomial zonotopes, Taylor models, polytopes — with a uniform operator interface; its polynomial zonotopes capture non-convex reachable sets that defeat purely convex methods.

```python
# Sketch: barrier-certificate safety check for a 2D polynomial system
# dot(x) = f(x); candidate barrier B(x) = x1^2 + x2^2 - r^2
import sympy as sp
x1, x2, r = sp.symbols('x1 x2 r', real=True)
f = sp.Matrix([-x1**3 - x2, x1 - x2**3])          # globally asymptotically stable
B = x1**2 + x2**2 - r**2                          # wall: circle of radius r
dB = sp.Matrix([sp.diff(B, x1), sp.diff(B, x2)])
lie_derivative = (dB.T * f)[0]                    # dB/dt along trajectories
sp.factor(lie_derivative)                         # = -2*(x1^4 + x2^4) < 0 off origin
# If B <= 0 on X0 and B > 0 on Xu, safety is certified symbolically.
```

### 4.4 Decidability Boundaries: From Timed Automata to Undecidability

Not all hybrid automata are born equal. Henzinger, Kopke, Puri, and Varaiya [2] drew the sharpest known map of the decidability frontier for reachability:

| Class | Reachability | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Timed automata ($\dot{x}_i = 1$) | **PSPACE-complete** | Region-graph construction (Alur–Dill) |
| Initialized rectangular automata | **PSPACE-complete** | Variables reinitialized whenever rate envelope changes [2] |
| Rectangular automata (uninitialized) | **Undecidable** | Reduction from two-counter machines |
| Timed automata + one stopwatch | **Undecidable** | A single pausable clock suffices [2] |
| Singular automata ($\dot{x} = c$ state-dependent) | **Undecidable** | Even very restricted subclasses |
| O-minimal hybrid systems | **Decidable** | Definable flows admit finite bisimulations |

> **Theorem (Henzinger–Kopke–Puri–Varaiya [2]).** Reachability for *initialized* rectangular automata — where every variable's rate interval may change only if the variable is reinitialized on the transition — is PSPACE-complete, via translation to a timed automaton preserving all reachability information. Dropping initialization, or adding a single stopwatch to a timed automaton, renders reachability undecidable.

The practical moral is stark: the nonlinear systems engineers actually build lie firmly on the undecidable side. Verification therefore cannot mean *deciding* reachability; it means computing sound approximations precise enough to prove the property at hand, refining them (smaller time steps, richer template directions, higher-degree barriers) when they are not. The o-minimal line of work (Lafferriere, Pappas, Sastry) offers a complementary island of decidability — definable flows admit finite bisimulations — but the complexity is forbidding and the tools have not followed.

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 The Tool Landscape

| Tool | Dynamics class | Set representation | Scale demonstrated | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SpaceEx | Piecewise-affine, nondeterministic | Support functions / template polyhedra | 100+ continuous vars [7] | [7] |
| Flow\* | Nonlinear (polynomial, general) | Taylor models | Dozens of vars, tight enclosures | Chen–Ábrahám–Sankaranarayanan, CAV 2013 |
| CORA (MATLAB) | Linear, nonlinear, hybrid, DAE | Zonotopes, polynomial zonotopes, Taylor models, polytopes | Automotive benchmarks; ARCH-COMP participant | [8] |
| JuliaReach | Linear, nonlinear, hybrid | LazySets: zonotopes, hyperrectangles, Taylor models | Decomposition methods for sparse high-dim systems | [9] |
| DryVR | Black-box simulators + white-box guards | Learned discrepancy (sensitivity) functions | Powertrain, AEB, lane-merge, auto-passing [10] | [10] |

The annual **ARCH** competitions subject these tools to common benchmarks, and the results reveal a consistent pattern: *no single representation dominates*. Support functions win on high-dimensional linear systems; Taylor models win on tight nonlinear enclosures; polynomial zonotopes win on non-convex geometry; simulation-driven methods win when only a black-box simulator exists.

### 5.2 Case Studies: Autonomous Driving and Robotics

- **Automatic emergency braking (AEB).** DryVR verified an AEB controller by learning a *discrepancy function* — a bound on how fast trajectories starting from nearby states can diverge, estimated from simulation data — and bloating simulated traces into reachtubes [10]. This *data-driven* paradigm sidesteps the need for white-box dynamics entirely.
- **Autonomous racing and lane merging.** CORA's reachability engine has verified that planned maneuvers keep the ego vehicle's reachable occupancy disjoint from surrounding traffic participants' occupancies (Althoff's dissertation program).
- **Aircraft autolander.** The HJ computation of maximally controlled safe envelopes for a six-mode flap-configuration hybrid model showed that optimal safe behavior switches directly to full flap deflection while maintaining airspeed — a non-obvious control insight extracted from the value function, not from simulation [3].

### 5.3 What the Theorems Actually Promise

Three guarantee-types must not be conflated:

1. **Convergence** (HJ methods): as the grid spacing $h \to 0$, the numerical solution converges to the true viscosity solution [3]. For any *fixed* grid, the result is an approximation with no rigorous error bound — the certificate is asymptotic, not per-computation.
2. **Sound overapproximation** (zonotope/Taylor-model/SpaceEx methods): every computed set rigorously contains the true reachable set, *provided* the implementation uses validated numerics. A disjointness check against the unsafe set is then a genuine proof [5][6][7].
3. **Existence certificates** (barrier methods): exhibiting $B$ satisfying the three inequalities *is* the proof; the SDP solver's numerics are the only trust gap, closed in practice by a posteriori verification of the inequalities with interval arithmetic [4].

## 6. Limitations

1. **The curse of dimensionality is undefeated.** HJ grids stall at 5–6 dimensions; zonotope methods reach hundreds of dimensions only for *linear* dynamics; nonlinear flowpipes degrade as Taylor remainders inflate.
2. **Overapproximation conservatism produces false alarms.** A sound method that reports "possibly unsafe" when the system is in fact safe has proved nothing. Guard intersections, wrapping effects, and template-direction choices all inject spurious volume.
3. **Zeno behavior** — infinitely many discrete transitions in finite time, as in the idealized bouncing ball — breaks fixed-point computations and requires explicit detection and regularization.
4. **Barrier synthesis is incomplete and SDP-limited.** The SOS relaxation may fail to find a certificate that exists at higher degree, and semidefinite programs scale roughly as $O(n^6)$ in the number of monomials, capping out near 10–12 states for dense problems.
5. **The simulation-to-reality gap.** Every method verifies a *model*. CORA's reachset-conformance checking [8] addresses this by testing whether observed system trajectories lie within the model's reachable outputs — but conformance is itself only as good as the test coverage.
6. **Floating-point soundness.** Most tools are implemented in floating point; without validated numerics, a "proof" can be invalidated by rounding. The community's gradual migration toward interval arithmetic and proof-producing pipelines is incomplete.

## 7. Conclusion

Reachability analysis for hybrid systems has traveled, in three decades, from the undecidability theorems of Alur, Henzinger, and their collaborators [1][2] to tools that verify hundred-dimensional linear systems [7], tightly enclose nonlinear flowpipes, synthesize barrier certificates by convex optimization [4], and even verify controllers given only a simulator [10]. The field's central achievement is a *pluralism of guarantees*: exact-in-the-limit PDE solutions, sound geometric overapproximations, and existence proofs by scalar certificates.

The road ahead is defined by the systems now being deployed. Learning-enabled components — neural network controllers, perception stacks — demand verification methods that compose set-based reachability with neural-network bound propagation. Compositional techniques, in the spirit of DryVR's simulation relations [10], must tame the dimensionality of full vehicle stacks. And the engineering workflow must change: verification should be as routine as compilation, integrated into CI pipelines rather than performed by specialists wielding MATLAB toolboxes. Until then, the methods surveyed here remain the strongest available evidence that a cyber-physical system will do what its designers intend — and nothing else.
