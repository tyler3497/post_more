# Oceanic Mixing and the Meridional Overturning Circulation: Internal-Wave-Driven Diapycnal Mixing, Mesoscale Eddy Parameterization, and Abyssal Recipes for the Deep Stratification

## Abstract

The meridional overturning circulation (MOC) couples surface forcing to the abyss through a mechanical energy pathway that remains incompletely quantified. This thesis reconstructs the energetics of the deep overturning from the abyssal recipes of Munk and Wunsch [1] through the internal-wave-driven mixing geography of Kunze [6] to the mesoscale eddy closures of Gent and McWilliams [3]. We derive the advective–diffusive balance that fixes the canonical diapycnal diffusivity near 10⁻⁴ m² s⁻¹, trace the roughly two terawatts of mechanical power through the internal-wave cascade, and show how the residual-mean eddy parameterization [4] controls adiabatic closure in the Southern Ocean. Observational evidence from microstructure surveys [5] and the revised ECCO-based assessment of Wunsch [7] reveals that mixing is spatially concentrated rather than uniform, forcing a reinterpretation of the classical one-dimensional balance. The synthesis closes with quantitative implications for ocean general circulation models and identifies open questions in near-boundary mixing and eddy–mixing interactions.

## 1 Introduction

The ocean is a fluid heated and cooled almost entirely at its surface. A classical result of fluid dynamics, Sandström's theorem, states that a circulation maintained by buoyancy forcing applied at the same geopotential level cannot sustain deep overturning of any vigor. Without a mechanical energy source that mixes dense abyssal water back upward across density surfaces, the deep ocean would, within a few thousand years, lapse into a stagnant reservoir of cold, salty water, ventilated only by slow diffusion and feeble convective adjustment [1]. Yet observations show the opposite: the abyss is stratified, oxygenated, and dynamically connected to the surface through the meridional overturning circulation, an interhemispheric conveyor that transports on the order of 20 Sverdrups (Sv, where one Sverdrup equals one million cubic meters per second) of deep water and carries with it the bulk of the ocean's meridional heat transport in the Atlantic basin.

Reconciling this circulation with the available sources of mechanical energy is the central problem of abyssal oceanography. Walter Munk's 1966 "abyssal recipes" [2] framed the question as a balance between downward advection of the abyssal stratification by upwelling and upward diffusion of buoyancy by turbulent mixing. That framing, refined by Munk and Wunsch into an energetics argument [1], demands roughly two terawatts of mechanical power to maintain the observed density distribution against some 30 Sv of deep-water formation. The tides, with about 3.7 TW of total dissipation, and the wind, with an order of one terawatt of work on the general circulation, are the only reservoirs large enough to supply it.

Two further developments complicate the simple recipe. First, microstructure measurements consistently find pelagic diffusivities an order of magnitude below the canonical value, concentrating the required mixing into "hot spots" near rough topography [5]. Second, the adiabatic closure pathway: wind-driven Ekman suction in the Southern Ocean can lift deep water to the surface along steeply sloping isopycnals with minimal diapycnal mixing, and the compensating eddy fluxes are parameterized in coarse models by the Gent–McWilliams scheme [3][4]. This thesis integrates these strands — the energetics, the geography of internal-wave-driven mixing [6], and the eddy parameterization of the residual circulation — into a single coherent account of how the deep ocean stays mixed and the overturning stays closed.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The advective–diffusive balance

Consider the steady, horizontally averaged balance for a conservative tracer — potential temperature, salinity, or equivalently buoyancy — in the abyssal ocean. Let *w* denote the mean vertical velocity (positive upward) and *κ* the diapycnal diffusivity. Away from boundaries and sources, the tracer equation reduces to the celebrated one-dimensional balance:

```python
# Illustrative advective-diffusive balance (1D abyssal recipe)
# d/dz (w * T - kappa * dT/dz) = 0  ->  w * dT/dz = kappa * d^2T/dz^2
import numpy as np

def abyssal_temperature(z, H=4000.0, kappa=1e-4, w=1e-7, T0=1.0):
    """Exponential profile T(z) = T0 * exp((z + H) * w / kappa) for z in (-H, 0)."""
    return T0 * np.exp((z + H) * w / kappa)
```

With constant coefficients the solution is exponential in depth, with e-folding scale *κ / w*. Munk [2] fitted this form to the observed abyssal temperature profiles and obtained *κ ≈ 10⁻⁴ m² s⁻¹* (one cgs unit) for an upwelling rate consistent with roughly 25 Sv of deep-water formation. Munk and Wunsch [1] later removed the restriction to constant coefficients and, using the Levitus climatology, recovered essentially the same numbers while reinterpreting the diffusivity as a surrogate: the global integral is satisfied not by uniform mixing but by a small number of concentrated mixing hot spots from which water masses — but not turbulence — are exported into the interior.

> Theorem: In a statistically steady abyss with no interior sources of buoyancy, the vertically integrated buoyancy budget requires that the total diapycnal buoyancy flux balance the advective export of buoyancy by the net upwelling. Equivalently, the power required to sustain the stratification against the MOC equals the product of the buoyancy frequency structure and the integrated diffusivity.

### 2.2 The energy budget

Mixing across a stable stratification is work. Raising dense water and lowering light water increases the potential energy of the water column, and turbulence can only do this work if mechanical energy is supplied from outside. The Osborn relation connects the diapycnal diffusivity *K_ρ* to the turbulent kinetic energy dissipation rate *ε*:

*K_ρ = Γ ε / N²*

where *N* is the buoyancy frequency and *Γ* the mixing efficiency, conventionally taken near 0.2 (though this value is itself an active research question). Integrating over the ocean volume, Munk and Wunsch [1] found that about **2.1 TW** of mechanical power must be dissipated in a way that produces buoyancy flux, to maintain the global abyssal density field against 30 Sv of deep-water formation. The astronomically known tidal dissipation of roughly **3.7 TW** (with about 2.5 TW in the *M₂* constituent alone) is the dominant candidate; wind work on near-inertial motions and the geostrophic circulation supplies of order one terawatt more [6].

| Energy source | Estimated power (TW) | Primary pathway |
|---|---:|---|
| Barotropic tide dissipation | ~3.7 | Internal tide generation over topography |
| Wind work on ocean | ~1.0 | Near-inertial waves, Ekman pumping |
| Required for abyssal buoyancy flux | ~2.1 | Turbulent dissipation → diapycnal mixing |
| M₂ tide alone | ~2.5 | Low-mode internal tides radiating from ridges |

### 2.3 Mesoscale eddies and the residual circulation

The MOC is not purely a mixing-driven conveyor. In the Southern Ocean, strong westerly winds drive northward Ekman transport at the surface and, by continuity, upwelling of deep water along steeply tilted isopycnals — an essentially adiabatic process [8]. Mesoscale eddies, through baroclinic instability, flux buoyancy poleward and upward, opposing the wind-driven steepening. In coarse-resolution models that cannot resolve eddies, Gent and McWilliams [3] parameterized this effect as an eddy-induced "bolus" velocity that advects tracers along with the mean flow while relaxing isopycnal slopes. The residual circulation — the sum of the Eulerian mean and the eddy-induced transport — is what actually moves water masses, and it is this residual overturning that observations constrain.

## 3 Methodology

Our approach is diagnostic and theoretical rather than based on new observations. We assemble the argument in four stages:

1. **Energetics audit.** We reproduce the Munk–Wunsch [1] global power accounting from published dissipation inventories, tracking tidal input through internal-tide generation, the internal-wave cascade, and turbulent dissipation to buoyancy flux via the Osborn relation.
2. **Mixing geography.** We use the finescale parameterization literature, synthesized globally by Kunze [6], to map where the internal-wave cascade actually dissipates — distinguishing the weak pelagic background from topographic hot spots [5] — and test whether the geography can close the 2.1 TW budget.
3. **Eddy-closure analysis.** We derive the Gent–McWilliams bolus velocity [3] and its residual-mean formulation [4], and evaluate the adiabatic Southern Ocean closure pathway in which wind-driven upwelling is balanced by eddy fluxes rather than diapycnal mixing.
4. **Model-data confrontation.** We confront the classical one-dimensional recipe with the 26-year ECCO state estimate analyzed by Wunsch [7], which finds the original two stations untypical and the circulation irreducibly three-dimensional, while potential density still obeys an exponential balance.

The internal-wave physics is framed by the Garrett–Munk spectrum and the wave–turbulence transition [8]. For the cascade we adopt the standard sequence: generation (barotropic tide over topography, wind-forced near-inertial waves) → propagation and nonlinear interaction (parametric subharmonic instability, induced diffusion, wave–wave scattering) → shear or convective instability → three-dimensional turbulence → irreversible diapycnal mixing with efficiency *Γ ≈ 0.2*.

A compact Haskell sketch captures the budget structure we evaluate numerically:

```haskell
-- Abyssal energy budget: power in (tides + wind) must cover
-- power required for buoyancy flux + boundary-layer losses
data Budget = Budget { tidalInput   :: Double  -- TW
                     , windInput    :: Double  -- TW
                     , requiredFlux :: Double  -- TW
                     , efficiency   :: Double  -- mixing efficiency Gamma
                     }

budgetClosed :: Budget -> Bool
budgetClosed b = (tidalInput b + windInput b) * efficiency b
                 >= requiredFlux b
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 From barotropic tides to the internal-wave cascade

Approximately one terawatt of the 3.7 TW tidal dissipation is estimated to be converted into internal tides — internal gravity waves at tidal frequencies generated when the barotropic tide flows over rough topography such as mid-ocean ridges, island arcs, and continental slopes [6][8]. These low-mode waves radiate thousands of kilometers, while higher modes dissipate nearer their sources. Near-inertial waves, generated by variable wind stress at the surface, contribute a comparable energy flux into the internal-wave field, concentrated at storm tracks and in the Southern Ocean.

Once generated, internal waves interact nonlinearly. Parametric subharmonic instability transfers energy from a large-scale parent wave to smaller-scale daughter waves at half the frequency; induced diffusion scatters energy across vertical wavenumbers. When wave shear or strain pushes the local Richardson number below the critical threshold near one quarter, Kelvin–Helmholtz or convective instability breaks the waves into turbulence. Microstructure profilers measure this final stage directly as *ε*, and the finescale parameterization infers it from observed shear and strain spectra, enabling the global geography compiled by Kunze [6].

### 4.2 The geography of dissipation: hot spots versus the pelagic background

The central observational tension of the last three decades is the "missing mixing" problem. Munk's recipe calls for a global-mean diffusivity of 10⁻⁴ m² s⁻¹ below the main thermocline, but microstructure surveys and the deliberate tracer-release experiments of Ledwell and colleagues consistently find pelagic values closer to 10⁻⁵ m² s⁻¹ [5][8]. Polzin and co-workers' seminal survey [5] demonstrated that dissipation rates vary by orders of magnitude, elevated by factors of ten to one hundred over rough topography — the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, the Hawaiian Ridge, fracture zones — while the smooth abyssal plains are quiescent.

Kunze's global synthesis [6] resolves the tension quantitatively: integrated over the ocean, the internal-wave-driven dissipation does approach the power required by the recipe, provided one accepts the canonical mixing efficiency and includes the concentrated boundary and topographic contributions. The recipe's diffusivity was therefore never wrong as a global integral constraint — it was wrong as a local field. The modern view is that *κ* is a strongly intermittent function of position, with most of the buoyancy flux accomplished in a small fraction of the ocean volume. Water masses processed in these hot spots are then exported laterally into the interior, exactly the reinterpretation proposed by Munk and Wunsch [1].

### 4.3 Gent–McWilliams and the adiabatic closure

Where diapycnal mixing is weak, the overturning must close along density surfaces. The transformed Eulerian mean formulation separates the tracer equation into advection by the residual velocity *v_res = v̄ + v\** and a residual diffusive flux. Gent and McWilliams [3] closed the eddy buoyancy flux as down-gradient diffusion of isopycnal layer thickness, which is equivalent to an eddy-induced streamfunction:

*Ψ\* = κ_GM S*, 

where *S* is the isopycnal slope vector and *κ_GM* an eddy thickness diffusivity of order 10³ m² s⁻¹. The associated bolus velocity *v\* = ∇ × (Ψ\* ŷ)* flattens sloping isopycnals, releasing mean available potential energy at the rate the wind puts it in. Gent's retrospective [4] documents how this parameterization — originally controversial — eliminated the spurious deep upwelling of the Veronis effect, produced the first non-drifting coupled climate control simulation without flux correction, and remains the standard eddy closure in non-eddy-resolving models.

In the Southern Ocean, the residual overturning is the small difference of two large opposing circulations: the wind-driven Eulerian cell and the eddy-induced cell. Because the isopycnals there connect the deep ocean to the surface mixed layer, deep water can upwell with negligible diapycnal transformation — the "adiabatic" closure. The real ocean uses both pathways: mixing-driven upwelling in the abyss and wind-and-eddy-driven upwelling in the Southern Ocean, with the partition set by the stratification, the wind stress, and the eddy field.

### 4.4 Revisiting the recipe with a state estimate

Wunsch [7] subjected Munk's original calculation to a modern test using a 26-year mean of the ECCO global state estimate. The two hydrographic stations Munk used prove untypical: potential temperature and salinity generally display different vertical scale heights, so no single one-dimensional steady balance describes them. The circulation is fully three-dimensional, with horizontal advection and time dependence entering at leading order. Yet potential density and buoyancy *do* obey a one-dimensional exponential balance quantitatively — the recipe survives, but only for the dynamically relevant variable, and the inferred mixing coefficients must be understood as effective quantities integrating over unresolved three-dimensional structure.

Ocean general circulation models that prescribe a uniform *κ = 10⁻⁴ m² s⁻¹* below the thermocline obtain a plausible MOC for the wrong reason; models with weak interior mixing and GM eddies obtain a plausible MOC through the adiabatic pathway; and the newest generation — with tidal mixing parameterizations that concentrate dissipation over topography [6] — is beginning to get the right answer for closer to the right reason.

### 4.5 A formal statement of the residual-mean tracer budget

To make the eddy–mixing partition precise, consider the thickness-weighted (residual-mean) buoyancy equation in a quasi-geostrophic ocean:

```tla+
---- MODULE ResidualMean ----
EXTENDS Reals
CONSTANTS kappaGM, kappaD, N2
VARIABLES b, vRes
\* Residual buoyancy budget: advection by residual velocity
\* balances diapycnal diffusion; isopycnal eddy fluxes are
\* absorbed into vRes via the Gent-McWilliams streamfunction.
Budget == vRes \cdot Grad(b) = Div(kappaD \cdot Grad(b))
                                /\ kappaD \in 1e-5 .. 1e-3
THEOREM ClosureOK == Budget => UpwellingPartitionDefined
====
```

In words: wherever *κ_d* is at the pelagic background of 10⁻⁵ m² s⁻¹, the residual advection must be nearly along-isopycnal, and any cross-isopycnal mass flux — the diabatic limb of the MOC — must be supplied by the hot-spot mixing. The partition of the overturning into adiabatic and diabatic limbs is therefore a direct observable of the mixing geography, not a free modeling choice.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We now assemble the quantitative case as a sequence of results.

**Result 1 — Power requirement.** Integrating the Osborn relation over the Levitus climatology with *Γ = 0.2* and 30 Sv of deep-water formation yields a required mechanical power of approximately **2.1 TW** to maintain the abyssal density distribution [1]. The calculation is robust to the vertical structure of *κ*: any diffusivity profile whose global integral satisfies the buoyancy budget gives the same power to within tens of percent.

**Result 2 — Available power.** Astronomical constraints fix total tidal dissipation at roughly **3.7 TW**; about one terawatt is converted to internal tides. Wind work adds of order one terawatt, partitioned between near-inertial waves and the large-scale geostrophic circulation. The sum comfortably exceeds the 2.1 TW requirement even at modest conversion efficiencies [1][6].

**Result 3 — Cascade closure.** Finescale-inferred dissipation from tens of thousands of profiles integrates to a global-mean rate consistent with the recipe's requirement [6], but only when topographic hot spots are included: the open-ocean background alone supplies less than half. This is the empirical content of the hot-spot reinterpretation [1][5].

**Result 4 — Eddy closure.** With *κ_GM* of order 10³ m² s⁻¹, the GM parameterization produces an eddy-induced overturning that cancels most of the wind-driven Deacon cell, leaving a residual Southern Ocean upwelling of the observed magnitude with minimal diapycnal mixing [3][4]. Coarse models without GM over-stratify the deep ocean and misplace deep-water formation; with GM they reproduce the observed residual structure.

**Result 5 — Dimensionality.** The ECCO-based test [7] falsifies the one-dimensional steady balance for temperature and salinity individually, while confirming it for potential density — proving that the recipe constrains buoyancy, and that horizontal and temporal terms are leading-order in the full tracer budgets.

> Theorem: The global abyssal buoyancy budget is closed if and only if the sum of (i) the hot-spot diapycnal buoyancy flux and (ii) the adiabatic Southern Ocean residual flux balances the buoyancy export by deep-water formation. Neither term alone suffices in the observed ocean.

What remains uncertain is not *whether* the budget closes but the precise spatial and temporal partition — the frontier addressed in §6.

```rust
// Partition of the MOC into diabatic and adiabatic limbs
// Units: Sverdrups (1 Sv = 1e6 m^3/s)
struct OverturningPartition {
    deep_water_formation: f64, // ~30 Sv total sinking
    diabatic_upwelling: f64,   // hot-spot mixing limb
    adiabatic_upwelling: f64,  // Southern Ocean residual limb
}

impl OverturningPartition {
    fn closes(&self) -> bool {
        let residual = self.deep_water_formation
            - self.diabatic_upwelling
            - self.adiabatic_upwelling;
        residual.abs() < 1.0 // within 1 Sv tolerance
    }
}
```

## 6 Limitations

The synthesis above is honest about its soft points:

- **Mixing efficiency is not a constant.** The value *Γ ≈ 0.2* derives from shear-driven turbulence in specific regimes; convective breaking, double diffusion, and boundary-layer turbulence may have substantially different efficiencies, and recent work suggests efficiency declines at high turbulence intensity. A factor-of-two error in *Γ* propagates directly into the power requirement.
- **The near-boundary frontier.** Most abyssal mixing likely occurs within hundreds of meters of sloping topography — in bottom boundary layers, hydraulic overflows, and breaking internal tides on continental slopes — regions poorly sampled by both microstructure profilers and Argo floats [5][8]. The hot-spot inventory remains incomplete.
- **Eddy–mixing interactions.** The GM parameterization [3][4] treats eddies as adiabatic by construction, but eddies modulate stratification and therefore the internal-wave field; conversely, mixing alters the potential-vorticity gradients that feed baroclinic instability. Coupled closures remain experimental.
- **Temporal variability.** The recipe is a steady-state balance, yet deep-water formation varies decadally, the internal-tide field is modulated by the 18.6-year lunar nodal cycle and mesoscale eddy variability, and the Southern Ocean winds are strengthening. Whether the budget closes instantaneously or only in a long-term mean is unresolved [7].
- **One-dimensionality.** As Wunsch [7] emphasizes, the classical balance fails locally for individual tracers; applying recipe-style reasoning to regional budgets without the full three-dimensional advection terms invites error.

## 7 Conclusion

Sixty years after Munk's abyssal recipes [2], the core insight stands: the deep ocean's stratification is a maintained non-equilibrium state, held up by a terawatt-scale flux of mechanical energy through the internal-wave cascade into diapycnal mixing [1][6]. The refinements — that mixing is concentrated in topographic hot spots rather than spread uniformly [5], that the Southern Ocean offers an adiabatic escape valve regulated by mesoscale eddies [3][4], and that the recipe constrains buoyancy rather than temperature or salinity separately [7] — have not overturned the energetics but have relocated them into a three-dimensional, eddy-active, geographically heterogeneous ocean.

For ocean modeling, the implications are concrete. Uniform background diffusivities are a parameterization of ignorance; the path forward is energetically constrained tidal-mixing schemes that deposit dissipation where internal tides actually break [6], GM-style eddy closures whose coefficients respect the observed eddy kinetic energy [4], and diagnostics that verify the simulated MOC against the integral buoyancy budget rather than against tuned local diffusivities. The abyssal recipes thus remain what they have always been: not a finished theory, but the accounting framework against which every theory of the deep circulation must balance its books.
