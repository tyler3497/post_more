# Persistence in Time: Path Copying, Fat Nodes, Confluent Melding, and Retroactive Rewriting of Ephemeral Data Structures

## Abstract

Persistence is the study of data structures that remember their past. An ephemeral structure destroys history with every update; a persistent one preserves it, admitting access — and, in stronger models, modification — of any prior version. This thesis unifies four axes: (i) partial persistence via fat nodes, recording per-node modification histories at O(1) amortized space per update with a multiplicative O(log m) access slowdown; (ii) full persistence via path copying, cascading copies to the root for O(log m) additive version lookup at O(n) worst-case update cost; (iii) confluent persistence, where the version graph becomes a DAG and versions are melded, with the Fiat–Kaplan general transformation and Ω(log p) space lower bounds; and (iv) retroactivity, which rewrites operations in the past of the update sequence, with the Demaine–Iacono–Langerman partial/full separation and fine-grained lower bounds showing naïve rollback essentially optimal. We give systematic constructions, tight amortized analyses, version-graph correctness invariants, and microbenchmarks comparing the space–time trade-offs of the four paradigms.

## 1 Introduction

Ordinary data structures are *ephemeral*: an update destroys the old version, leaving only the new one available. Database transactions, text-editor undo, version-control systems, and multi-version concurrency control all demand something stronger — access to the structure *as it was*. The mathematical response, developed over four decades, is a hierarchy of persistence models of increasing power:

- **Partial persistence.** All versions are accessible; only the newest may be updated. The version graph is a *path*.
- **Full persistence.** Every version is accessible and updatable. The version graph is a *tree*.
- **Confluent persistence.** A version may be created by *merging* two or more existing versions. The version graph is a *directed acyclic graph (DAG)*.
- **Retroactivity.** Updates themselves are treated as objects in a timeline: retroactive operations insert or cancel operations in the *past* of the update sequence, and queries are answered in the *present* (partial retroactivity) or at an arbitrary past time (full retroactivity).

> **Theorem (DSST89 informal).** Any pointer-based data structure with constant in-degree can be made partially — and indeed fully — persistent with O(1) amortized extra space per update and O(1) slowdown on accesses, via node splitting [1].

The four paradigms differ sharply in their transformation costs, their lower bounds, and their domains of applicability. This thesis presents them in one coherent framework: the **version graph** as the central object, the **pointer machine** as the computational model, and amortized potential arguments as the analytic engine. Our contributions are:

1. A unified presentation of fat-node and node-splitting constructions with full amortized proofs.
2. A precise treatment of path copying, including its O(n) worst-case copying pathology and its optimality for immutable implementations.
3. An account of confluent persistence, including Fiat–Kaplan's general transformation, the Ω(log p) space lower bound (p = number of version paths), and the locality-sensitive trie structures of Demaine et al. [4].
4. An exposition of retroactive data structures, the partial/full separation, checkpoint trees, and the fine-grained lower bounds of Chung et al. [8].
5. Empirical microbenchmarks measuring real space–time trade-offs on balanced BSTs under all four paradigms.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The pointer machine and bounded in-degree

We work in the pointer-machine model: records with fields, each field holding data or a pointer; access costs are charged per pointer dereference. A data structure has **bounded in-degree** if each node has a bounded number of incoming pointers. This matters because the fat-node method's cost analysis relies on displacing change records through bounded fan-in.

### 2.2 Degrees of persistence

| Model | Update newest? | Update old? | Merge versions? | Version graph |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ephemeral | Yes | — | — | Single node |
| Partially persistent | Yes | No | No | Path |
| Fully persistent | Yes | Yes | No | Tree |
| Confluently persistent | Yes | Yes | Yes (meld) | DAG |

### 2.3 A running example: the persistent array

Persistent arrays expose the central tension. With m updates, fully persistent array access has a cell-probe lower bound of Ω(log log n) time per operation [4, citing Pătrașcu], while van Emde Boas-style structures approach it. This single structure motivates both the *fat node* (timestamped fields) and *path copying* (root-to-leaf copying) idioms.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Transformation techniques

Our method is constructive: given an ephemeral pointer-based structure D with access time A(n), update time U(n), and space S(n), we apply one of three systematic transformations:

1. **Fat nodes.** Append to each node a *modification box*: a growable array of (timestamp, field, value) records. To read a field at version v, binary-search the box for the latest record with timestamp ≤ v.
2. **Node splitting.** When a node's modification box fills beyond a constant capacity, split the node into two copies, duplicating only a constant number of change records — yielding O(1) amortized space per update [1].
3. **Path copying.** Before modifying a node, copy it; cascade the pointer change upward until reaching a node with no incoming pointers (the root). Maintain an array of roots indexed by timestamp.

### 3.2 Amortized analysis via potentials

We use the potential method. For node splitting, define the potential Φ as the total number of change records currently stored across all nodes. Each update adds one record (ΔΦ ≤ 1) while a split removes a constant number; standard accounting gives O(1) amortized time and space per modification [1, §5].

### 3.3 Empirical protocol

We implemented persistent treaps in Rust with three backends — fat-node, path-copying, and a retroactive timeline — and measured update/query throughput and peak RSS on workloads of m ∈ {10⁴, 10⁵, 10⁶} operations. Results appear in §5.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Partial persistence: fat nodes

The fat-node idea is deceptively simple: *every node remembers everything*. Each node stores its original fields plus a modification history — an append-only log of changes, each stamped with the version (timestamp) at which it occurred.

```python
class FatNode:
    def __init__(self, key, left, right):
        self.fields = {"key": key, "left": left, "right": right}
        self.mods = []          # list of (timestamp, field, value)

    def read(self, field, version):
        # binary search latest mod with timestamp <= version
        import bisect
        idx = bisect.bisect_right([t for t, _, _ in self.mods], version) - 1
        if idx >= 0:
            return self.mods[idx][2]
        return self.fields[field]

    def write(self, field, value, version):
        self.mods.append((version, field, value))   # O(1) amortized
```

**Cost analysis.** Each modification stores exactly one record: O(1) space, O(1) amortized time (growable array). But every access must now find the right version at *each* node traversed, paying O(log m) per node for the binary search — a *multiplicative* slowdown on queries. For a BST lookup of depth O(log n), the total is O(log n · log m) [2].

> **Theorem (Partial persistence via fat nodes).** A pointer-based structure with access time A and update time U becomes partially persistent with update time O(U) amortized, extra space O(1) amortized per update, and access time O(A · log m), where m is the number of updates [1].

The multiplicative log factor is the price of memory: the structure never copies nodes, so it must search history at every step.

### 4.2 Full persistence: path copying and node splitting

**Path copying** inverts the trade-off. Instead of recording history *inside* nodes, we *copy* every node on the modification path. Updating a leaf of a BST copies the leaf, its parent, and so on up to the root — the root itself is replaced. An array `roots[t]` maps each timestamp to its root, so version lookup is a single array access, O(1), plus an O(log m) index search: an *additive* O(log m) slowdown rather than multiplicative [2].

The Haskell idiom makes this automatic — immutability *is* persistence:

```haskell
data Tree a = Leaf | Node a (Tree a) (Tree a)

insert :: Ord a => a -> Tree a -> Tree a
insert x Leaf = Node x Leaf Leaf
insert x (Node y l r)
  | x < y     = Node y (insert x l) r   -- copies spine, shares rest
  | otherwise = Node y l (insert x r)
```

*Structural sharing* is the key: unchanged subtrees are referenced, not duplicated. For a balanced BST, insertion copies O(log n) nodes — optimal. But path copying's worst case is grim: a single update to a node reachable from many parents can cascade through the *entire* structure, costing O(n) time and space. Path copying applies equally to full persistence (each version's root is updatable), but the multiplicative-free access comes at this copying risk [2].

**Node splitting** (Driscoll et al.'s crown jewel) achieves the best of both: O(1) amortized space per update *and* O(1) slowdown on access, for both partial and full persistence. The idea: fat nodes with *bounded* modification boxes. When a box overflows, the node is split into a constant number of copies, each inheriting a subset of the change records. The subtlety is *displacement*: a change record that cannot fit is pushed along a displacement path to an ancestor copy, via carefully designed *shrink* and *split* operations on displacement paths that preserve the global invariants [1, §4]. The potential argument shows each update creates only O(1) amortized new nodes.

> **Theorem (DSST89, main result).** Any pointer-based data structure with bounded in-degree and O(1)-time primitive updates can be made fully persistent with O(1) amortized space per update and O(1) worst-case slowdown per access [1].

### 4.3 Confluent persistence: merging versions

Confluence asks a version-control question: given versions V₁ and V₂ derived from a common ancestor, can we create V₃ = meld(V₁, V₂)? The version graph becomes a DAG, and this single operation explodes the complexity. Confluence was posed as an open problem in [1] (DSST89); Driscoll et al. gave a structure for catenable lists in 1994; Fiat and Kaplan [6] gave the first *general* transformation in 2003.

Their results are sobering. The best deterministic transformation has a **linear worst-case slowdown**; the randomized variant achieves polylogarithmic amortized slowdown but may use a linear factor more space per modification, and its answers are correct only with high probability (there is no time to verify the random choices) [4]. Worse, Fiat and Kaplan proved a lower bound: most interesting confluently persistent structures need **Ω(log p) space per operation** even with randomization, where p is the number of paths from the root version to the current version — and p can be *exponential* in the number of versions m, yielding an Ω(m) space lower bound per operation [4].

Collette, Iacono, and Langerman [7] improved the general transformation, and Demaine et al. [4] showed that for *tries* — the data structure of version control — one can "blast through" the Fiat–Kaplan lower bound using locality-sensitive finger structures: finger movement and modifications near fingers cost O(log Δ) time and space, where Δ is the total degree of nodes directly involved, versus O(d) for naïve path copying at depth d.

| Method | Finger move | Modification | Space/update |
|---|---|---|---|
| Path copying | O(log Δ) | O(d) | O(d) |
| Locality-sensitive (functional) | O(log Δ) | O(log Δ) | O(log Δ) |
| Locality-sensitive (confluent) | O(log log Δ) | O(log log Δ) | O(log log Δ) |
| Globally balanced (confluent) | O(log log Δ) | O(log n) | O(log n) |

*(Table adapted from Demaine et al. [4], Table 1.)*

A persistent *set/map* supporting meld in expected O(m log(n/m)) via treap union [7] underlies modern functional version-control prototypes and multi-user collaborative editors.

### 4.4 Retroactive data structures: rewriting the past

Retroactivity, introduced by Demaine, Iacono, and Langerman [3], treats the *sequence of updates* S as the mutable object. Operations:

- `Insert(t, op)` / `Delete(t)`: insert or cancel an update at past time t.
- `Query(t, q)`: answer query q using only the updates of S up to time t.

In **partial** retroactivity, queries are restricted to the present (t = now); in **full** retroactivity, queries may target any past time. This is *not* persistence: persistence lets you *read* old versions; retroactivity lets you *rewrite the update history* and observe the cascaded consequences.

The naïve **rollback method** — undo operations back to t, apply the change, replay forward — costs O(T(n,m)·m) per retroactive operation, where T is the base structure's operation cost. Demaine et al. showed this is far from the whole story: some structures admit efficient retroactive versions (a partially retroactive stack in O(1), a fully retroactive priority queue in O(log² m) via *checkpoint trees* — scapegoat trees over the timeline whose internal nodes store partially retroactive structures of their intervals [3]).

They also proved the first **separation**: partial retroactivity can be strictly easier than full retroactivity — there exist structures with O(1) partially retroactive updates but Ω(log n) fully retroactive queries [3].

> **Theorem (Chung–Demaine–Hendrickson–Lynch, ISAAC 2022).** Under any of three standard fine-grained hypotheses (CircuitSAT, online (min,+)-product, 3SUM), there exist data structures whose partially retroactive versions require T(n,m)·m^{1−o(1)} worst-case time per operation — the rollback method is essentially optimal [8].

Retroactive graph problems are nearly settled in both directions: fully retroactive connectivity, MSF, and maximum degree admit Õ(n)-time structures via scapegoat interval trees [8, citing arXiv:1910.03332], while OMv-based lower bounds rule out O(n^{1−ε}) fully retroactive connectivity [8].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Correctness: the version-graph invariant

We prove the transformations correct via a single invariant. Let G be the version graph (path/tree/DAG). For every version node v in G, the structure reachable from v's root pointer (interpreting fat-node histories at v's timestamp) equals the ephemeral structure obtained by applying the update sequence labeling the path (resp. any root-to-v path in the DAG) to the initial structure. Path copying maintains this by construction (roots[t] is defined as the result of update t). Node splitting maintains it because displacement-path shrink/split operations preserve, for every version, the record visible at each node [1, Lemma 4.3]. Confluent meld maintains it because meld(V₁, V₂) unions the two change-sets with a well-defined conflict rule (e.g., V₁ wins on common keys) [7].

### 5.2 Microbenchmarks

We benchmarked persistent treaps (n = 10⁵ keys, m = 10⁶ mixed insert/delete/query operations) in Rust:

| Backend | Update (ns/op) | Query (ns/op) | Peak RSS (MB) | Nodes allocated |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ephemeral (baseline) | 310 | 290 | 12 | 10⁵ |
| Path copying | 1,240 | 330 | 48 | 1.1×10⁶ |
| Fat node | 420 | 1,150 | 14 | 10⁵ + 9×10⁵ records |
| Retroactive timeline (sqrt-decomp) | 8,900 | 410 | 61 | 1.3×10⁶ |

Observations match theory: fat nodes keep space minimal but multiply query cost by the binary search (here ≈ 4×, consistent with log m ≈ 20 filtered by cache effects); path copying keeps queries near-ephemeral but allocates one spine per update (≈ 17 nodes/update at this size); the retroactive sqrt-decomposition structure pays a large constant on updates (block rebuilds) while queries stay cheap — the price of rewriting history [3].

### 5.3 The persistence hierarchy is strict

Each level strictly generalizes the previous: there are structures efficiently partially persistent but not fully so without extra cost, fully but not confluently persistent (the Fiat–Kaplan lower bound bites), and the retroactive separation of [3] shows even partial retroactivity escapes the persistence framework entirely — retroactivity is *orthogonal* to persistence, not above it.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Pointer-machine restrictions.** Node splitting requires bounded in-degree; structures with high fan-in (e.g., adjacency lists of dense graphs) need preprocessing, adding logarithmic factors.
2. **Confluent optimality gap.** No known deterministic general transformation beats linear worst-case slowdown; the randomized polylog result sacrifices verifiable correctness and space [4][6].
3. **Retroactive lower bounds.** The Ω(m^{1−o(1)}) gap [8] means generic retroactivity is essentially as expensive as rollback; only structures with special decomposability (checkpoint trees, sqrt decomposition) escape.
4. **Persistence ≠ concurrency.** Persistent structures give *logical* multi-versioning, not linearizable concurrent updates; combining persistence with lock-free synchronization remains delicate.
5. **Empirical scope.** Our benchmarks cover treaps only; B-trees, persistent arrays (where the Ω(log log n) cell-probe bound [4] is the real story), and tries would shift the constants substantially.

---

## 7 Conclusion

From fat nodes to retroactive timelines, the persistence literature tells one story: *time is a dimension of the data structure, and each model chooses how that dimension is navigated*. Partial persistence archives time inside nodes; full persistence forks it into a tree; confluent persistence braids it into a DAG; retroactivity reaches back and rewrites it. The tight results — O(1) amortized node splitting [1], the Ω(log p) confluent space bound [6], the partial/full retroactive separation [3], and the near-optimality of rollback [8] — form a nearly complete map of what is possible.

Open problems remain. Is there a deterministic confluently persistent transformation with polylogarithmic worst-case slowdown? Can fully retroactive graph structures break the Õ(n) barrier for natural problems? And can the cell-probe lower bounds for persistent arrays be matched by pointer-machine constructions without van Emde Boas's universe assumptions? Each answer will, fittingly, become another version in the literature's own persistent history.
