# Gate Bootstrapping on the Torus: The Mathematics and Engineering of TFHE Fully Homomorphic Encryption

## Abstract

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) enables arbitrary computation on encrypted data without ever exposing the plaintext. Among modern FHE schemes, TFHE (Fast Fully Homomorphic Encryption over the Torus), introduced by Chillotti, Gama, Georgieva, and Izabachène [1], occupies a distinctive niche: it evaluates arbitrary Boolean circuits via *gate bootstrapping*, refreshing the noise of every gate output in under 0.1 seconds [3], and it supports *programmable bootstrapping*, which evaluates arbitrary lookup functions during the refresh itself [5]. This thesis develops the mathematical foundations of TFHE, beginning with torus learning-with-errors (TLWE) and the external product that powers blind rotation. We present the gate-bootstrapping procedure in full detail, analyze its noise propagation and security under standard lattice assumptions, and explain how programmable bootstrapping turns a single refresh into a functional evaluation. We compare TFHE against the BGV, BFV, and CKKS leveled schemes [7], survey empirical timings from the TFHE and TFHE-rs libraries [10], and discuss the scheme's limitations: small message spaces, large key material, and the cost of non-Boolean arithmetic.

## 1. Introduction

Craig Gentry's 2009 construction proved the astonishing: it is possible to compute on encrypted data without decrypting it first [6]. The blueprint — a *somewhat* homomorphic scheme whose decryption circuit is shallow enough to be evaluated homomorphically, yielding *bootstrapping* — transformed a theoretical curiosity into a research program. Yet Gentry's original bootstrapping was catastrophically slow, and for a decade the field pursued *leveled* schemes (BGV, BFV, CKKS) [7] that postpone bootstrapping as long as possible, treating it as a necessary evil.

TFHE inverts this wisdom. Rather than minimizing bootstraps, TFHE bootstraps *every gate*: each Boolean operation is followed by a noise refresh, so noise never accumulates and circuits of arbitrary depth are trivially supported [1]. The key enabler is speed: by working over the real torus $\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ and exploiting the algebraic structure of the external product between TRGSW and TRLWE ciphertexts, TFHE reduced bootstrapping from minutes to roughly 13 milliseconds per gate on commodity hardware [3].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 recalls the lattice foundations: LWE, its torus variant TLWE, and the ring generalizations TRLWE and TRGSW. Section 3 describes the TFHE methodology: parameter selection, the external product, the CMux operator, and the bootstrapping and key-switching pipeline. Section 4 dives deeply into the torus arithmetic, the blind rotation at the heart of bootstrapping, the gate-bootstrapping algorithm itself, and programmable bootstrapping. Section 5 analyzes security, noise growth, and measured performance, including the TFHE-rs implementation [10]. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

The central claim we defend is twofold: (i) the torus formulation is not a cosmetic choice but the source of TFHE's efficiency, because it aligns the message space with the natural geometry of phase rounding; and (ii) programmable bootstrapping converts the refresh operation from a pure noise-management primitive into a *computational* primitive, a shift that redefines what "costly" means in homomorphic circuit design.

## 2. Background

### Learning with errors and its torus variant

The *learning with errors* (LWE) problem, introduced by Regev, asks to distinguish pairs $(\mathbf{a}, \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle + e)$ from uniform randomness, where $\mathbf{s}$ is a secret vector and $e$ is small noise drawn from a discrete Gaussian [1]. LWE is conjectured hard even for quantum computers, and it underpins the security of nearly all modern FHE schemes.

TFHE generalizes LWE to the *real torus* $\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$, the additive group of reals modulo 1. A TLWE sample is a pair $(\mathbf{a}, b) \in \mathbb{T}^n \times \mathbb{T}$ with $b = \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle + e \pmod 1$, where $\mathbf{s} \in \{0,1\}^n$ is a binary secret and $e$ is drawn from a Gaussian over the torus with standard deviation $\sigma$. The *phase* of a TLWE ciphertext,

$$\phi(c) = b - \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle = \mu + e \pmod 1,$$

concentrates around the message $\mu$. Decryption is *rounding*: if $\mu$ encodes a bit as $\mu \in \{0, 1/2\}$, then $\mathrm{round}(2\phi(c))$ recovers the bit provided $|e| < 1/4$. The torus geometry is what makes this clean — messages live at well-separated points of the circle, and noise is an arc length (see figure).

> **Definition (TLWE):** A *TLWE ciphertext* encrypting message $\mu \in \mathbb{T}$ under secret $\mathbf{s}$ with noise parameter $\sigma$ is $(\mathbf{a}, b) \in \mathbb{T}^n \times \mathbb{T}$ where $\mathbf{a} \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{T}^n$ uniformly and $b = \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle + \mu + e$ for $e \leftarrow \mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{T}, \sigma}$.

The ring variants replace vectors over $\mathbb{T}$ with polynomials over the *torus polynomial ring* $\mathbb{T}_N[X] = \mathbb{T}[X]/(X^N + 1)$, where $N$ is a power of two. A TRLWE ciphertext encrypts a *polynomial* message, and a TRGSW ciphertext is a matrix of TRLWE ciphertexts encrypting multiples of a message under a gadget decomposition — the torus analogue of the GSW construction [1]. TRGSW ciphertexts support an *external product* with TRLWE ciphertexts:

$$\square : \mathrm{TRGSW} \times \mathrm{TRLWE} \to \mathrm{TRLWE},$$

which multiplies the underlying messages while adding only a *small, controlled* amount of noise. This operation is the engine of TFHE bootstrapping.

### From FHEW to TFHE

The direct predecessor of TFHE is FHEW ( Ducas–Micciancio, 2015), which introduced the idea of bootstrapping a single NAND gate via *homomorphic accumulator* techniques [2]. FHEW worked over $\mathbb{Z}_q$ with $q$ a power of two and achieved bootstrapping in under a second. TFHE generalized and accelerated the construction by moving to the torus, introducing the external product, and — crucially — adding a fast *key-switching* procedure and *circuit bootstrapping* that converts TLWE ciphertexts back into TRGSW form for leveled composition [4]. The result was a full order-of-magnitude speedup and a scheme expressive enough for arbitrary Boolean circuits [3].

## 3. Methodology

### Parameter sets and the discretization strategy

TFHE parameters are chosen to balance security ($\lambda \geq 128$ bits under the LWE estimator), correctness (decryption failure probability below $2^{-64}$ or better), and speed. A standard gate-bootstrapping parameter set uses:

- LWE dimension $n \approx 630$–$1024$ for TLWE ciphertexts;
- Ring dimension $N = 1024$ or $2048$ for the bootstrapping accumulator;
- Noise standard deviations $\sigma \approx 2^{-15}$ (fresh) growing through operations;
- Gadget decomposition base $B_g = 2^2$–$2^7$ with $\ell = 2$–$4$ digits for TRGSW.

The torus is *discretized* in implementation: elements of $\mathbb{T}$ are represented as 32-bit or 64-bit integers modulo $2^{32}$ or $2^{64}$, so that modular arithmetic is free (it is just integer wraparound). Message $\mu = m/2$ for a bit $m$ maps to the integer $m \cdot 2^{31}$ (for 32-bit torus). This discretization is exact for the operations TFHE needs and costs nothing on modern CPUs.

### The bootstrapping pipeline

A TFHE gate evaluation proceeds in three stages [1]:

1. **Linear combination.** Given input TLWE ciphertexts $c_1, \dots, c_k$ encrypting bits, compute $c = \sum_i \alpha_i c_i + \beta$, a TLWE ciphertext whose phase encodes the desired gate's truth table offset. This step is essentially free (additions on the torus) but grows noise linearly.
2. **Blind rotation (bootstrapping).** Using the bootstrapping key — TRGSW encryptions of the secret-key bits — rotate a *test polynomial* $v \in \mathbb{T}_N[X]$ by the phase of $c$, homomorphically. The result is a TRLWE ciphertext encrypting $v$ rotated by the (noisy) message, i.e., encoding the gate output.
3. **Key switching and sample extraction.** Convert the TRLWE accumulator back to a TLWE ciphertext under the original key with fresh, small noise.

The output is a TLWE ciphertext encrypting the gate result with noise *independent* of the input noise — the refresh property that makes arbitrary-depth circuits possible.

### The CMux operator

A workhorse of TFHE circuits is the *controlled multiplexer*:

$$\mathrm{CMux}(C, d_0, d_1) = C \square (d_1 - d_0) + d_0,$$

where $C$ is a TRGSW encryption of a bit $b$ and $d_0, d_1$ are TRLWE ciphertexts. If $b = 0$ the result encrypts $d_0$'s message; if $b = 1$, $d_1$'s. CMux enables homomorphic table lookups, branching, and — via *circuit bootstrapping* [4] — the conversion of TLWE ciphertexts into TRGSW ciphertexts so that bootstrapped outputs can themselves control multiplexers in deeper circuits.

The following Python-like pseudocode sketches gate bootstrapping at a conceptual level:

```python
def gate_bootstrap(ct_inputs, gate_coeffs, bk, ks):
    # 1. linear combination on the torus
    ct = sum(a * c for a, c in zip(gate_coeffs, ct_inputs))
    ct = ct + gate_offset  # encode truth table
    # 2. blind rotation: ACC = X^{-round(2N * phase)} * test_poly
    acc = blind_rotate(ct, test_polynomial, bk)
    # 3. sample extract + key switch -> fresh TLWE
    return key_switch(sample_extract(acc), ks)
```

The real implementation operates on discretized torus elements and NTT-friendly polynomial rings, but the structure is exactly this.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The torus as a message space

Why the torus rather than $\mathbb{Z}_q$? Three reasons. First, *scale invariance*: the torus has no distinguished modulus, so rescaling arguments and noise analyses are cleaner — noise is always measured relative to the message spacing, which is a fixed fraction of the circle. Second, *rounding is canonical*: decryption is literally rounding a real number, and the failure event $\{|e| \geq 1/4\}$ has a crisp geometric meaning. Third, the polynomial ring $\mathbb{T}_N[X]/(X^N+1)$ supports the *negacyclic* rotations used in blind rotation without any modulus-switching machinery: multiplying by $X$ rotates coefficients, and $X^N = -1$ folds signs automatically.

Concretely, a message bit $m \in \{0, 1\}$ is encoded as $\mu = m/2 \in \mathbb{T}$. After homomorphic operations the phase is $\mu + e$; decryption computes $\lfloor 2(\mu + e) \rceil \bmod 2 = m$ whenever $|e| < 1/4$. The *noise budget* of a ciphertext is thus the slack between the actual noise amplitude and the $1/4$ threshold — a single number that the entire TFHE noise analysis tracks [1].

### 4.2 The external product and blind rotation

The external product $C \square d$ of a TRGSW ciphertext $C$ (encrypting $\mu_C$) and a TRLWE ciphertext $d$ (encrypting $\mu_d$) yields a TRLWE ciphertext encrypting $\mu_C \cdot \mu_d$, with noise roughly

$$\sigma_{\square}^2 \approx \sigma_C^2 + \mu_C^2 \cdot \sigma_d^2 + \text{(decomposition error)},$$

where the decomposition error is controlled by the gadget base $B_g$ and digit count $\ell$: smaller $B_g$ means slower but less noisy products [1]. This product is *asymmetric* — only one operand needs the heavyweight TRGSW form — which is why TFHE keeps data in cheap TLWE/TRLWE form and reserves TRGSW for keys and control bits.

*Blind rotation* is the masterstroke. Given a TLWE ciphertext $c = (\mathbf{a}, b)$ with phase $\phi \approx \mu$, and a test polynomial $v \in \mathbb{T}_N[X]$, blind rotation computes a TRLWE encryption of $X^{-\tilde{\phi}} \cdot v$, where $\tilde{\phi} = \lfloor 2N\phi \rceil$ is the discretized phase. It does so iteratively: starting from an accumulator $\mathrm{ACC} = v$, for each secret bit $s_i$ it updates

$$\mathrm{ACC} \leftarrow \mathrm{CMux}(BK_i, \mathrm{ACC}, X^{-a_i} \cdot \mathrm{ACC}),$$

where $BK_i$ is a TRGSW encryption of $s_i$. After processing all $n$ bits (plus the constant term $b$), the accumulator holds $v$ rotated by exactly the phase — *without ever decrypting it*. Choosing $v$ to encode the gate's truth table (e.g., $v = \sum_{j} g(j/2N) X^j$ for a function $g$) makes the constant term of the rotated polynomial equal the gate output. Sample extraction then pulls out that constant term as a fresh TLWE ciphertext [3].

### 4.3 Gate bootstrapping in full

Putting it together, bootstrapping a gate with truth table $T : \{0,1\}^k \to \{0,1\}$ proceeds as follows [1]:

1. Form $c = \sum_{i=1}^{k} c_i \cdot \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{8} - \frac{T\text{-offset}}{2}$ (for a NAND-like encoding), a TLWE ciphertext whose phase lies in $[0, 1/2)$ iff the gate output is 0, and in $[1/2, 1)$ iff it is 1 — up to the accumulated noise.
2. Set the test polynomial $v = \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} \frac{1}{8} X^j + \sum_{j=N}^{2N-1} \left(-\frac{1}{8}\right) X^{j-N}$ (encoding "output 1 iff phase in upper half"), embedded in $\mathbb{T}_N[X]$ using $X^N = -1$.
3. Blind-rotate: $\mathrm{ACC} = \mathrm{BlindRotate}(c, v, BK)$.
4. $\bar{c} = \mathrm{SampleExtract}(\mathrm{ACC})$: a TLWE ciphertext encrypting $\pm 1/8$ with small noise.
5. Key-switch $\bar{c}$ from the bootstrapping key to the main LWE key: $c' = \mathrm{KeySwitch}(\bar{c}, KS)$.

The result $c'$ encrypts the gate output bit with fresh noise $\sigma_{\text{out}} \approx 2^{-15}$–$2^{-20}$, regardless of how noisy the inputs were. Every gate pays one bootstrapping (~13 ms in the original implementation [3], ~5–10 ms in TFHE-rs [10]), and in exchange noise *never compounds*. Arbitrary Boolean circuits — AES, neural-network inference, private database queries — become straightforward compositions of bootstrapped gates.

### 4.4 Programmable bootstrapping

*Programmable bootstrapping* (also called *functional bootstrapping*) generalizes the test polynomial trick: instead of encoding a single bit's truth table, encode an arbitrary function $f : \mathbb{T}_p \to \mathbb{T}$ as a lookup table in the test polynomial [5]. During the blind rotation, the phase of the input selects the table entry, so the refreshed ciphertext encrypts $f(\mu)$ rather than merely $\mu$. In one bootstrapping operation we therefore get *both* noise refresh *and* a nonlinear function evaluation — for free, in the sense that the cost is identical to a plain bootstrap.

This is transformative for encrypted arithmetic. A small integer $x \in \{0, \dots, p-1\}$ encoded in the phase can be mapped through $f(x) = x^2$, $f(x) = \mathrm{ReLU}(x)$, or $f(x) = 1/x$ (for $x \neq 0$) in a single bootstrap. Multi-precision integers are handled digit by digit with carry propagation via further programmable bootstraps [5]. The *precision* is limited by the noise: the table needs $p$ well-separated slots on the torus, so $p \cdot \sigma \ll 1$ must hold, capping practical precision at roughly 4–8 bits per bootstrap — but chaining and the *circuit bootstrapping* technique [4] extend this to arbitrary precision at higher cost.

> **Theorem (informal, [5]):** Let $c$ be a TLWE ciphertext encrypting $\mu \in \mathbb{T}_p$ with noise amplitude $\eta < 1/(4p)$. Then programmable bootstrapping with test polynomial encoding $f$ outputs a TLWE ciphertext encrypting $f(\mu)$ with fresh noise independent of $\eta$, except with probability bounded by the Gaussian tail beyond the slot margins.

## 5. Empirical Analysis and Theoretical Guarantees

### Security foundations

TFHE's security reduces to the hardness of (ring-)LWE over the torus. The parameters are validated with the standard LWE estimator methodology: the distinguishing advantage against the best known lattice attacks (primal and dual BKZ) is estimated below $2^{-128}$ for the recommended sets [1]. Two caveats deserve emphasis. First, the *circular security* assumption: the bootstrapping key contains TRGSW encryptions of the secret key bits under (a related) key, and key-switching keys contain encryptions of key material as well. TFHE, like all bootstrapped FHE, assumes security holds in the presence of these key-dependent encryptions — a standard but strictly stronger assumption than plain LWE. Second, the implementation must be *constant-time* to resist timing side channels; the reference implementations take care here, but the mathematical specification alone does not guarantee it.

### Noise analysis

The TFHE noise analysis [1] tracks the *variance* of the phase error through each operation, using the independence heuristic (noises are treated as independent Gaussians). For gate bootstrapping, the output noise variance is dominated by three terms: the blind-rotation rounding error (from discretizing the phase to $2N$ slots), the external-product decomposition error, and the key-switching error. With $N = 1024$, $\ell = 3$, $B_g = 2^7$ the analysis gives failure probability below $2^{-64}$ per gate — and because each gate is freshly bootstrapped, failures do not propagate: the per-gate bound *is* the circuit bound. This is the deep advantage over leveled schemes, where noise analysis must budget an entire circuit's multiplicative depth [7].

### Measured performance

The original TFHE library reported gate bootstrapping in about 13 ms on a single core (2016) [3]; the modern TFHE-rs implementation in Rust reports roughly 5–10 ms per programmable bootstrap on commodity CPUs, with AVX-512 and GPU backends pushing further [10]. A useful comparison:

| Scheme | Bootstrap time | Amortized cost | Message space |
|---|---|---|---|
| TFHE (gate) | ~10 ms | ~10 ms/gate | bits / small ints |
| TFHE-rs (PBS) | ~5–8 ms | ~5–8 ms/function | up to ~8-bit LUT |
| BGV/BFV | seconds–minutes | $\mu$s–ms/slot (SIMD) | large integers |
| CKKS | seconds–minutes | $\mu$s–ms/slot (SIMD) | approximate reals |
| FHEW [2] | ~0.6 s (2015) | ~0.6 s/gate | bits |

The table makes the tradeoff explicit: TFHE wins decisively on *latency per nonlinear operation* and on programming simplicity for Boolean circuits, while SIMD-batch schemes (BGV/BFV/CKKS) win on *throughput* for wide vector arithmetic. The schemes are complementary, and hybrid compilers (e.g., via CHIMERA-style scheme switching [9]) increasingly combine them.

## 6. Limitations

TFHE's limitations are real and shape where it is deployed.

- **Small native message space.** The torus phase natively carries bits or small integers (practically $\leq$ 8 bits per programmable bootstrap). Large-integer arithmetic requires digit decompositions and many bootstraps; floating-point is essentially out of reach. CKKS [7] remains superior for approximate numerical workloads.
- **Key material size.** The bootstrapping key (TRGSW encryptions of each secret bit) and key-switching keys total tens to hundreds of megabytes. This is a one-time cost per key, but it dominates bandwidth in client-server deployments.
- **Latency vs. throughput.** Gate bootstrapping is fast per gate but inherently sequential along circuit depth; unlike SIMD schemes it cannot batch thousands of slots into one operation. Wide data-parallel workloads favor BGV/BFV.
- **Circular security.** As noted, bootstrapping keys encrypt secret-key bits. While no attack is known, this is a stronger assumption than standard LWE, and it is not falsifiable in the usual sense.
- **Precision limits of programmable bootstrapping.** The lookup-table precision is bounded by $p \cdot \sigma \ll 1$; pushing beyond ~8 bits per bootstrap requires larger parameters and proportionally slower operations [5].
- **Side channels and correctness.** The $2^{-64}$ failure bound is heuristic (independence assumption); implementations must additionally be constant-time, and few deployments have undergone the side-channel auditing that RSA/ECC implementations receive.

None of these limitations is fatal; each defines a frontier of active research, from *circuit bootstrapping* [4] to GPU-accelerated blind rotation to verified constant-time implementations.

## 7. Conclusion

TFHE showed that the torus is a natural home for fully homomorphic encryption: its geometry makes phase rounding canonical, its polynomial ring makes blind rotation elegant, and the external product makes bootstrapping fast enough to apply at *every gate*. Gate bootstrapping dissolved the leveled/bootstrapped dichotomy by making refresh cheap; programmable bootstrapping then turned refresh into computation, giving homomorphic lookup tables at the price of a single bootstrap. The empirical record — 13 ms bootstraps in 2016 [3], single-digit milliseconds in TFHE-rs [10], and real deployments in private inference and encrypted databases — confirms that aggressive bootstrapping is a viable engineering doctrine, not merely a theoretical convenience.

The broader lesson concerns the design space of FHE itself. For a decade the field optimized *around* bootstrapping; TFHE optimized *through* it. As scheme-switching frameworks [9] mature, the future likely belongs to compilers that route each subcomputation to the scheme that handles it best — torus-based bootstrapping for control flow and nonlinearities, SIMD batching for linear algebra. Understanding the mathematics of the torus, the external product, and blind rotation, as developed in this thesis, is prerequisite to building that future.
