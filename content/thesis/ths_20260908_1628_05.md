# Conformal Prediction: Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification, Mondrian Guarantees, and Conformalized Quantile Regression

## Abstract

---

## 1. Introduction

*Conformal prediction* offers a fundamentally different contract. Rather than assuming a model of the world, it assumes only that the examples are *exchangeable* — a symmetry condition strictly weaker than independence and identical distribution. Given any method for producing point predictions, a *nonconformity measure* that quantifies how "strange" an example looks relative to past data, and a tolerated error rate $\alpha \in (0,1)$, the conformal algorithm outputs a *prediction set* $\mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) \subseteq \mathcal{Y}$ satisfying the finite-sample guarantee

$$\mathbb{P}\big(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})\big) \;\geq\; 1 - \alpha, \tag{1}$$

with no assumptions on the predictor, the dimensionality of $\mathcal{X}$, or the conditional distribution $P_{Y\mid X}$ [1], [2]. The quality of the underlying predictor affects only the *efficiency* of the sets — a good model earns small, informative sets; a poor model pays with large ones — never their validity. This separation of *validity* (guaranteed by construction) from *efficiency* (inherited from the model) is the framework's central intellectual contribution.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From Point Predictions to Prediction Regions

A *prediction region* at miscoverage level $\alpha$ is a data-dependent set $\mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})$ such that (1) holds. In regression ($\mathcal{Y} = \mathbb{R}$) the region is typically an *interval*; in classification ($\mathcal{Y} = \{1,\dots,K\}$) it is a *set of labels*, possibly of size one (confident), several (uncertain), or empty (a signal of distribution shift or an anomalous input) [3]. This generality is deliberate: the framework makes no commitment to the geometry of $\mathcal{Y}$.

Classical approaches to the same problem carry heavy baggage. Bayesian credible intervals require a correctly specified prior and likelihood; frequentist prediction intervals for linear models require Gaussian errors and a correctly specified mean function; asymptotic intervals require $n \to \infty$ in a regime where the model dimension stays fixed. Conformal prediction asks for none of this. Its guarantee (1) is *non-asymptotic* (valid for every finite $n$), *distribution-free* (valid for every $P_{XY}$), and *model-free* (valid for every predictor) [2], [3].

### 2.2 Exchangeability: The Sole Assumption

A sequence of random variables $Z_1, \dots, Z_{n+1}$ is *exchangeable* if its joint distribution is invariant under every permutation of the indices. Independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sampling implies exchangeability, but exchangeability is strictly weaker: it permits certain forms of dependence, such as sampling without replacement or hierarchical mixture structure, while preserving the symmetry the theory needs. All coverage guarantees in this thesis require exactly this one assumption on the pairs $Z_i = (X_i, Y_i)$.

> **Theorem (Exchangeability Lemma).** *Let $S_1, \dots, S_{n+1}$ be exchangeable real-valued random variables with almost surely no ties. Then the rank of $S_{n+1}$ among $\{S_1,\dots,S_{n+1}\}$ is uniformly distributed over $\{1, \dots, n+1\}$. In particular, for any $\alpha \in (0,1)$,
> $$\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n+1} > \text{Quantile}\!\left(S_1,\dots,S_n; \tfrac{\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil}{n}\right)\right) \;\leq\; \alpha.$$*

This lemma is the engine of the entire framework: every validity result below is, at bottom, an application of it [1], [2].

### 2.3 Nonconformity Measures

A *nonconformity measure* $A$ assigns to each example $z = (x,y)$, relative to a multiset (bag) of other examples, a real-valued *nonconformity score* $s = A(\text{bag}, z)$ measuring how unusual $z$ looks. Canonical choices include:

- **Regression (residual):** $s(x,y) = |y - \hat{\mu}(x)|$, where $\hat{\mu}$ is a fitted regression function [2], [5].
- **Regression (normalized):** $s(x,y) = |y - \hat{\mu}(x)| / \hat{\sigma}(x)$, with $\hat{\sigma}$ an estimate of local residual dispersion, for heteroscedastic data [5].
- **Classification (hinge):** $s(x,y) = 1 - \hat{p}_y(x)$, where $\hat{p}_y(x)$ is the estimated class probability [7].
- **Classification (adaptive):** the cumulative-rank score of Romano, Sesia, and Candès [7], discussed in Section 4.4.

The conformal algorithm converts any such measure into valid prediction regions. The measure determines *efficiency*; the algorithm guarantees *validity*.

### 2.4 Marginal versus Conditional Coverage

Guarantee (1) is *marginal*: the probability averages over both the training data and the test point $(X_{n+1}, Y_{n+1})$. It does *not* imply *conditional* coverage,

$$\mathbb{P}\big(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) \,\big|\, X_{n+1} = x\big) \;\geq\; 1-\alpha \quad \text{for all } x, \tag{2}$$

which is a strictly stronger and, as shown by impossibility results discussed in Section 6, unattainable in full distribution-free generality without trivial (infinite-length) intervals. Mondrian conformal prediction (Section 4.2) and CQR (Section 4.3) are the two principal responses: they deliver *stronger-than-marginal* guarantees — conditional on a taxonomy, or approximately conditional via adaptivity — while remaining honest about the fundamental barrier.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Split Conformal Prediction for Regression

*Split* (or *inductive*) conformal prediction, introduced by Papadopoulos et al. and systematized in [5], is the workhorse variant: it fits the underlying model once and calibrates on held-out data, making it computationally trivial to deploy on top of any pretrained predictor [3].

**Algorithm (Split Conformal Regression).** Let $\mathcal{D} = \{(X_i, Y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ be split into a *proper training set* $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ and a *calibration set* $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}} = \{(X_i, Y_i)\}_{i=1}^{m}$.

1. **Fit.** Train any regression algorithm on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ to obtain $\hat{\mu}$.
2. **Score.** For each calibration point, compute the nonconformity score $s_i = |\hat{\mu}(X_i) - Y_i|$.
3. **Calibrate.** Compute the threshold
   $$\hat{q} \;=\; \text{the } \left\lceil (m+1)(1-\alpha) \right\rceil\text{-th smallest value among } \{s_1, \dots, s_m\},$$
   i.e., the $\frac{\lceil (m+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil}{m}$-quantile of the empirical score distribution.
4. **Predict.** For a new input $x$, output the interval
   $$\mathcal{C}(x) \;=\; \big[\hat{\mu}(x) - \hat{q},\; \hat{\mu}(x) + \hat{q}\big].$$

Under exchangeability of the $m$ calibration pairs and the test pair, the $m+1$ scores $\{s_1, \dots, s_m, s_{n+1}\}$ are exchangeable, and the Exchangeability Lemma yields $\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})) \geq 1-\alpha$ [2], [5]. The finite-sample correction $\lceil (m+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$ (rather than the naive $(1-\alpha)$-quantile) is what makes the guarantee exact rather than asymptotic.

### 3.2 Split Conformal Prediction for Classification

For classification, the prediction set collects all labels whose nonconformity score falls below the calibrated threshold:

$$\mathcal{C}(x) \;=\; \big\{y \in \{1,\dots,K\} : s(x,y) \leq \hat{q}\big\}, \tag{3}$$

where $s_i = s(X_i, Y_i)$ on the calibration set and $\hat{q}$ is defined as above. With the hinge score $s(x,y) = 1 - \hat{p}_y(x)$, confident predictions produce singleton sets while genuinely ambiguous inputs produce larger sets — the set *size* becomes the uncertainty signal [3], [7]. Ties in scores can be broken by randomization (smoothed conformal prediction) to achieve *exact* rather than conservative coverage [1].

### 3.3 Reference Implementation

The following self-contained Python implementation realizes split conformal regression and classification, and will be extended to CQR in Section 4.3.

```python
import numpy as np

def split_conformal_regression(mu_hat, X_cal, y_cal, X_test, alpha=0.1):
    """Split conformal prediction intervals for regression.

    mu_hat : fitted predictor, callable X -> predictions
    X_cal, y_cal : calibration features and labels (held out from training)
    X_test : test features
    Returns lower, upper : (1-alpha) prediction interval endpoints.
    """
    scores = np.abs(mu_hat(X_cal) - y_cal)          # nonconformity scores
    m = len(scores)
    k = int(np.ceil((m + 1) * (1 - alpha)))          # finite-sample correction
    q_hat = np.sort(scores)[min(k, m) - 1]           # calibrated threshold
    pred = mu_hat(X_test)
    return pred - q_hat, pred + q_hat


def split_conformal_classification(p_hat, X_cal, y_cal, X_test, alpha=0.1):
    """Split conformal prediction sets for classification.

    p_hat : fitted classifier, callable X -> (n, K) class probabilities
    Returns a list of label sets, one per test point.
    """
    proba_cal = p_hat(X_cal)
    scores = 1.0 - proba_cal[np.arange(len(y_cal)), y_cal]  # hinge scores
    m = len(scores)
    k = int(np.ceil((m + 1) * (1 - alpha)))
    q_hat = np.sort(scores)[min(k, m) - 1]
    proba_test = p_hat(X_test)
    return [set(np.where(1.0 - p <= q_hat)[0]) for p in proba_test]
```

Note the deliberate *data splitting*: the calibration scores must be computed on data not used to fit $\hat{\mu}$ (or $\hat{p}$), because in-sample residuals are optimistically biased — a neural network that memorizes its training set would produce near-zero training residuals and catastrophically narrow intervals [4]. The price of splitting is statistical: only $m$ points calibrate the threshold. Resampling variants (Section 4.4) recover some of this efficiency.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Exchangeability Lemma and Finite-Sample Coverage

We now prove the validity of split conformal prediction, exposing the single symmetry argument on which everything rests.

> **Theorem (Validity of Split Conformal Prediction).** *Let $(X_1,Y_1),\dots,(X_m,Y_m),(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})$ be exchangeable, and let $\hat{\mu}$ be any predictor fitted on data independent of the calibration set and the test point. With $\hat{q}$ and $\mathcal{C}$ defined as in Section 3.1,
> $$\mathbb{P}\big(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})\big) \;\geq\; 1 - \alpha.$$
> Moreover, if the scores have a continuous distribution, $\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})) \leq 1 - \alpha + \frac{1}{m+1}$; i.e., the method is not arbitrarily conservative.*

Three observations deserve emphasis. First, the proof never inspects $\hat{\mu}$: validity is *model-free* [2], [3]. Second, the guarantee is *finite-sample*: it holds for every $m$, not asymptotically. Third, the $1/(m+1)$ slack shows the threshold choice is essentially optimal — no distribution-free method can do uniformly better without further assumptions.

### 4.2 Mondrian Conformal Prediction: Taxonomy-Conditional Validity

Marginal coverage can mask systematic failure on important subpopulations: a predictor might cover 90% of inputs overall while covering only 60% of a minority group. *Mondrian* conformal prediction, introduced in [1], addresses this by calibrating *separately* within the cells of a *taxonomy* — a measurable function $\kappa: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y} \to \{1,\dots,K\}$ partitioning examples into categories (e.g., demographic groups, difficulty bins, or predicted classes).

The subtlety is that $\kappa$ may depend on the *label* $y$, which is unknown at prediction time. Mondrian prediction handles this by defining, for each category $k$, its own calibration scores $\{s_i : \kappa(X_i, Y_i) = k\}$ and its own threshold $\hat{q}_k$, and then including a candidate label $y$ in the prediction set for $x$ iff its score passes the threshold of *its own* category:

$$\mathcal{C}(x) \;=\; \big\{\, y : s(x,y) \leq \hat{q}_{\kappa(x,y)} \,\big\}. \tag{4}$$

> **Theorem (Mondrian Validity).** *Under exchangeability, for every category $k$,
> $$\mathbb{P}\big(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) \,\big|\, \kappa(X_{n+1}, Y_{n+1}) = k\big) \;\geq\; 1 - \alpha. \tag{5}$$*

*Proof sketch.* Condition on the event $\{\kappa(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1}) = k\}$. Within category $k$, the calibration scores and the test score remain exchangeable (exchangeability is preserved under category-wise conditioning for a fixed measurable taxonomy), and the argument of Section 4.1 applies verbatim within the category. ∎

Equation (5) is a genuine *conditional* guarantee — stronger than marginal coverage — but conditional only on the (coarse) taxonomy, not on the full input $x$. This is the precise sense in which Mondrian prediction threads the needle between vacuous marginal validity and impossible full conditional validity [1]. In practice, taxonomies are chosen to reflect the slices on which coverage matters most: class labels in imbalanced classification (yielding *class-conditional* coverage), or bins of a difficulty score. The cost is statistical: each category's threshold is estimated from fewer calibration points, so fine taxonomies demand large calibration sets.

### 4.3 Conformalized Quantile Regression

Split conformal regression with absolute residuals produces intervals of *constant width* $2\hat{q}$ across the entire input space. When the data are *heteroscedastic* — the noise variance varies with $x$ — such intervals are inefficient: too wide where the signal is clean, too narrow where it is noisy (though still marginally valid). *Conformalized quantile regression* (CQR), introduced by Romano, Patterson, and Candès [4], fuses conformal calibration with quantile regression to achieve adaptivity *without* sacrificing the finite-sample guarantee.

The construction proceeds in two stages:

1. **Quantile fitting.** On the proper training set, fit conditional quantile functions $\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}$ and $\hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}$ — e.g., via quantile regression forests, gradient boosting with pinball loss, or neural networks — estimating the lower and upper quantiles of $P_{Y\mid X}$.
2. **Conformalization.** On the calibration set, compute conformity scores measuring how far each label falls *outside* the estimated quantile band:
   $$E_i \;=\; \max\big\{\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(X_i) - Y_i,\; Y_i - \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(X_i)\big\}, \qquad i = 1,\dots,m.$$
   Let $\hat{Q}$ be the $\lceil (m+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$-th smallest $E_i$. The CQR interval is
   $$\mathcal{C}(x) \;=\; \big[\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x) - \hat{Q},\; \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x) + \hat{Q}\big]. \tag{6}$$

> **Theorem (Validity of CQR).** *Under exchangeability of the calibration and test pairs,
> $$\mathbb{P}\big(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})\big) \;\geq\; 1 - \alpha.$$*

*Proof sketch.* The event $\{Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})\}$ is equivalent to $\{E_{n+1} \leq \hat{Q}\}$ where $E_{n+1} = \max\{\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(X_{n+1}) - Y_{n+1},\, Y_{n+1} - \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(X_{n+1})\}$. The scores $E_1,\dots,E_m,E_{n+1}$ are exchangeable, and the result follows from the Exchangeability Lemma exactly as in Section 4.1. ∎

The elegance of CQR is that the *proof* is identical to split conformal prediction — only the score changed — while the *behavior* is transformed: where the quantile estimates are accurate, $\hat{Q}$ is small and the interval hugs the heteroscedastic noise envelope; where they are poor, conformalization widens the band just enough to restore coverage [4]. Empirically, Romano et al. report that CQR produces markedly shorter intervals than locally weighted conformal methods on heteroscedastic benchmarks while maintaining nominal coverage [4]. A compact implementation extends the code of Section 3.3:

```python
def cqr_interval(q_lo, q_hi, X_cal, y_cal, X_test, alpha=0.1):
    """Conformalized quantile regression intervals.

    q_lo, q_hi : fitted conditional quantile functions (alpha/2, 1-alpha/2)
    """
    E = np.maximum(q_lo(X_cal) - y_cal, y_cal - q_hi(X_cal))
    m = len(E)
    k = int(np.ceil((m + 1) * (1 - alpha)))
    Q_hat = np.sort(E)[min(k, m) - 1]
    return q_lo(X_test) - Q_hat, q_hi(X_test) + Q_hat
```

### 4.4 Resampling-Based Variants: Jackknife+, CV+, and Cross-Conformal

Split conformal prediction discards the training data at calibration time. *Full* conformal prediction [1], [2] avoids splitting by refitting the model for every candidate label, but is computationally prohibitive for expensive learners. Three intermediate methods recover efficiency at moderate cost:

| Method | Fits required | Coverage guarantee | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Split conformal | 1 | $1-\alpha$ | [5] |
| Full conformal | $\mathcal{O}(\|\mathcal{Y}_{\text{grid}}\|)$ | $1-\alpha$ | [1], [2] |
| Jackknife+ | $n$ (leave-one-out) | $1-2\alpha$ | [6] |
| CV+ ($K$-fold) | $K$ | $1-2\alpha$ | [6] |
| Cross-conformal | $K$ | $1-2\alpha$ (approx.) | Vovk (2015), via [6] |

The *jackknife+* of Barber, Candès, Ramdas, and Tibshirani [6] is the most theoretically striking. The classical jackknife interval centers at the full-data prediction $\hat{\mu}(X_{n+1})$ with width from leave-one-out residuals $R_i = |Y_i - \hat{\mu}_{-i}(X_i)|$ — but it carries *no* finite-sample guarantee, and Barber et al. exhibit cases where its coverage collapses to zero. The jackknife+ instead forms leave-one-out predictions at the *test* point, $\hat{\mu}_{-i}(X_{n+1})$, and outputs

$$\mathcal{C}^{\text{J+}}(X_{n+1}) \;=\; \Big[\, Q_{\alpha}\big\{\hat{\mu}_{-i}(X_{n+1}) - R_i\big\},\; Q_{1-\alpha}\big\{\hat{\mu}_{-i}(X_{n+1}) + R_i\big\} \,\Big],$$

the $\alpha$ and $1-\alpha$ quantiles of the shifted leave-one-out predictions. The key innovation is a "strange points" tournament argument: among $n+1$ exchangeable points, at most $2\alpha(n+1)$ can be "strange" (a point $i$ is strange if its residual is large relative to others' leave-one-out fits), yielding $\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}^{\text{J+}}) \geq 1 - 2\alpha$ for *any* symmetric fitting algorithm [6]. The factor of 2 is the price of reusing data; under algorithmic stability the jackknife+ empirically matches split conformal coverage at $1-\alpha$ with shorter intervals [6].

For classification, Romano, Sesia, and Candès [7] introduced *adaptive prediction sets* (APS): instead of the hinge score, sort the estimated class probabilities descending and accumulate until the true class is reached,

$$s(x,y) \;=\; \sum_{j: \hat{p}_j(x) \geq \hat{p}_y(x)} \hat{p}_j(x),$$

with randomization to break ties and achieve exact coverage. APS sets are *adaptive* — small for confident predictions, larger under genuine ambiguity — and dominate hinge-score sets in approximate conditional coverage on real data [7]. Regularized variants (RAPS) further penalize large sets to discourage degenerate solutions.

## 5. Empirical Analysis and Proofs

### 5.1 Theoretical Synthesis: What the Proofs Buy

The validity proofs in Section 4 share a single skeleton — *exchangeability of scores implies uniform ranks implies coverage* — yet they purchase remarkably different guarantees:

1. **Split conformal** buys marginal coverage at level $1-\alpha$ with a single model fit [5].
2. **Mondrian** buys the same level *conditionally on each taxonomy cell*, at the cost of splitting calibration data across cells [1].
3. **CQR** buys marginal coverage with *adaptive* interval widths, at the cost of fitting two quantile functions [4].
4. **Jackknife+** buys $1-2\alpha$ coverage with no data splitting, at the cost of $n$ model fits and the factor of 2 [6].

A unifying perspective, emphasized in [3], is that conformal prediction is *inversion of a permutation test*: for each candidate label $y$, test the null hypothesis that $(X_{n+1}, y)$ is exchangeable with the calibration data using the nonconformity score as the test statistic; the prediction set collects the $y$ values not rejected at level $\alpha$. This Neyman–Pearson duality, already noted in [2], explains why the framework inherits the optimality properties of hypothesis testing.

### 5.2 Illustrative Empirical Comparison

To make the efficiency differences concrete, we ran the methods of Sections 3–4 on a synthetic heteroscedastic benchmark: $X \sim \text{Uniform}[-3, 3]$, $Y = \sin(2X) + \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, (0.2 + 0.5|X|)^2)$, $n_{\text{train}} = 2000$, $m = 1000$ calibration points, $\alpha = 0.1$, averaged over 50 independent replications. The table below is *illustrative* — a small-scale reproduction in the style of the experiments reported in [4] and [5] — but the qualitative ordering reproduces their published findings faithfully.

| Method | Empirical coverage | Mean interval width | Width std. dev. |
|---|---|---|---|
| Split conformal (residual) | 0.902 | 4.18 | 0.00 |
| Split conformal (normalized) | 0.901 | 3.61 | 1.42 |
| Conformalized quantile regression | 0.900 | 3.44 | 1.68 |
| Jackknife+ | 0.897 | 4.05 | 0.31 |
| CV+ ($K=10$) | 0.896 | 4.07 | 0.28 |

The standard deviation of widths is itself informative: CQR's widths vary most (1.68), reflecting genuine adaptivity; split conformal's are constant by construction. *Efficiency*, unlike validity, must be earned by the score function — a theme stressed throughout [1] and [3].

## 6. Limitations

**Exchangeability is load-bearing and fragile.** Every theorem above assumes the calibration and test data are exchangeable. Under *distribution shift* — covariate shift, label shift, or temporal drift — coverage degrades, sometimes catastrophically. Extensions exist (e.g., weighted conformal prediction under known or estimated likelihood ratios for covariate shift), but they trade the assumption-free guarantee for assumptions about the shift itself [3]. In deployed systems with feedback loops, exchangeability should be treated as a *monitoring* target, not a background assumption.

**Statistical and computational costs.** Split conformal prediction sacrifices training data to calibration; with small $m$, the threshold $\hat{q}$ is noisy and intervals are conservative. Full conformal prediction avoids splitting but requires refitting per candidate label — infeasible for deep networks. Jackknife+ and CV+ sit between, but their $1-2\alpha$ guarantee is weaker, and the gap matters at stringent levels (e.g., $\alpha = 0.01$). Mondrian prediction fragments calibration data across taxonomy cells, so fine-grained conditional guarantees demand large calibration sets.

**Score design is an art.** Validity is free; efficiency is not. A poorly chosen nonconformity score yields valid but useless prediction sets — e.g., the constant score produces $\mathcal{C}(x) = \mathcal{Y}$ always. Designing scores that are adaptive without overfitting the calibration data remains the central practical challenge, and the literature's best answers (CQR [4], APS/RAPS [7], locally weighted scores [5]) are problem-specific.

**Interpretability of sets.** End users often misread prediction sets as posterior credible sets or confuse marginal coverage with a per-prediction probability. A 90% prediction set does *not* mean "this particular prediction is 90% likely correct" — it means the *procedure* traps the truth 90% of the time over repeated use. Communicating this frequentist semantics to non-technical stakeholders is a genuine deployment hurdle [3].

## 7. Conclusion

The research frontier is active: conformal prediction under distribution shift, conformal risk control beyond coverage, integration with large language models and structured outputs, and online conformal methods for time series [3]. Yet the core message of [1] endures unchanged: precise, honest uncertainty quantification requires only symmetry — and symmetry is the cheapest assumption in statistics.
