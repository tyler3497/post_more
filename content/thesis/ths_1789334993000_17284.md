# Quantum Key Distribution: Decoy-State BB84, Measurement-Device-Independent Protocols, and Satellite-to-Ground Networks

## Abstract

Quantum key distribution (QKD) promises information-theoretic secure key exchange grounded in the laws of quantum mechanics rather than computational hardness assumptions. This thesis develops the modern theory and practice of QKD across three decisive advances beyond the original BB84 protocol: the decoy-state method, which defeats photon-number-splitting attacks on weak coherent pulse sources and restored unconditional security to practical lasers; measurement-device-independent QKD (MDI-QKD), which removes all detector side channels by placing the Bell-state measurement at an untrusted central node; and twin-field QKD, which exploits single-photon interference so that key rates scale with the square root of channel transmittance, beating the Pirandola-Laurenza-Ottaviani-Banchi repeaterless bound. We present the Shor-Preskill security proof via Calderbank-Shor-Steane codes, the entropic uncertainty relation framework for finite-key analysis, and the asymptotic GLLP key-rate formula with decoy-state parameter estimation. We survey experimental milestones including 1000 km twin-field QKD over fiber and the Micius satellite-to-ground network achieving kilohertz key rates over 1200 km with intercontinental trusted-node relay over 7600 km.

## 1 Intro

The central promise of quantum key distribution is radical: two parties, conventionally called Alice and Bob, can distill a shared secret key whose security does not rest on any conjecture about the adversary's computational power, but on the no-cloning theorem and the disturbance induced by quantum measurement. When Bennett and Brassard introduced BB84 in 1984 [1], they reduced cryptography's oldest problem — secure key establishment — to quantum physics. The eavesdropper, Eve, may command arbitrarily advanced technology constrained only by quantum mechanics itself, and still learn essentially nothing about the final key.

This promise took nearly two decades to make rigorous. Early security arguments covered only restricted attacks, and the first unconditional proofs arrived via the entanglement-distillation techniques of Lo and Chau, culminating in the celebrated reduction of Shor and Preskill [2], who showed that prepare-and-measure BB84 inherits the security of an entanglement-purification protocol protected by Calderbank-Shor-Steane (CSS) quantum error-correcting codes. Parallel to these theoretical advances, experimentalists confronted an uncomfortable fact: real devices deviate from the idealized qubit models on which the proofs rest. Attenuated lasers emit *weak coherent pulses* with Poisson photon-number statistics rather than true single photons, opening the devastating photon-number-splitting (PNS) attack; single-photon detectors proved riddled with side channels — time-shift, blinding, efficiency mismatch — that allowed complete key theft without raising the observed error rate [11].

This thesis traces how the field answered each challenge, in three movements:

- **Decoy-state BB84**: by randomly varying pulse intensity, Alice and Bob bound the single-photon yield and error rate, neutralizing PNS attacks and restoring security to off-the-shelf lasers [3].
- **Measurement-device-independent QKD (MDI-QKD)**: by moving the detection apparatus to an untrusted middle node performing a Bell-state measurement, all detector side channels are removed by construction, while doubling the secure distance [4].
- **Twin-field QKD (TF-QKD)**: by interfering phase-locked coherent fields at a central station and conditioning on *single* detection clicks, key rates scale with the square root of transmittance, surpassing the fundamental repeaterless rate-distance limit [5][6] — a regime previously thought to require quantum repeaters.

Finally, we ascend to orbit: the Micius satellite demonstrated decoy-state QKD from space to ground at kilohertz rates over 1200 km, entanglement distribution over the same scale, and intercontinental trusted-node relay across 7600 km [7][8][13], establishing satellite links as the near-term backbone of a global quantum network.

## 2 Background

### The BB84 protocol

In BB84, Alice encodes each raw bit in one of two conjugate bases. Concretely, she prepares qubits in the computational (Z) basis, with eigenstates |0> and |1>, or the diagonal (X) basis, with eigenstates |+> and |->, choosing the basis and the bit value uniformly at random. Bob measures each incoming qubit in a randomly chosen basis. The protocol then proceeds through classical post-processing:

1. **Sifting**: Alice and Bob publicly announce their basis choices (but never the bit values) and discard all events where the bases disagree, keeping roughly half the raw data.
2. **Parameter estimation**: They sacrifice a random subset of the sifted bits to estimate the quantum bit error rate (QBER), aborting if it exceeds a tolerated threshold.
3. **Error correction**: Using an authenticated public channel, they reconcile their strings, leaking a bounded amount of information to Eve.
4. **Privacy amplification**: They compress the reconciled string with a universal-2 hash function, shrinking Eve's residual information to an exponentially small quantity.

Security rests on a simple physical fact: any attempt by Eve to learn the bit value in one basis necessarily disturbs the conjugate basis, and this disturbance is statistically visible in the parameter-estimation step.

### From qubits to weak coherent pulses: the PNS attack

Ideal BB84 demands true single photons. Practical systems instead attenuate a laser to mean photon number mu well below one, producing a Poisson distribution over photon number n. Most pulses are empty, most of the rest contain one photon — but a fraction contain two or more. Eve can exploit this with the photon-number-splitting attack: she performs a quantum nondemolition measurement of photon number, blocks all single-photon pulses, splits one photon off each multi-photon pulse while forwarding the remainder to Bob through a lossless channel, and stores her copies in quantum memory until the basis announcement reveals the correct measurement basis [11]. The result is catastrophic: Eve obtains a perfect copy of a large fraction of the key while the observed QBER stays at zero. For years this cast doubt on whether any weak-coherent-pulse implementation could be unconditionally secure, and it motivated the search for true single-photon sources as well as the decoy-state countermeasure [3].

### Detector side channels

Even with perfect sources, detectors proved the weakest link. The time-shift attack [11] exploited detector efficiency mismatch: by shifting pulse arrival times, Eve steered detections toward the more efficient detector and inferred the bit. The blinding attack was more dramatic still — bright illumination forced avalanche photodiodes into linear mode, after which Eve could fully control Bob's clicks with classical light and learn the entire key without detection. These *quantum hacking* demonstrations made clear that any security claim must account for device imperfections, leading to the device-independent program and, more practically, to MDI-QKD [4][12].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is theoretical and analytic, synthesizing the canonical security-proof techniques of the field and applying them to compare protocol families on equal footing.

**Entanglement-based reductions.** Following Shor and Preskill [2], we analyze prepare-and-measure protocols by reduction to an equivalent entanglement-based protocol. Alice is imagined to prepare EPR pairs and measure her halves; entanglement purification with CSS codes then guarantees that, conditioned on successful purification, Alice and Bob share nearly perfect EPR pairs from which a secret key follows by measurement. Because all of Alice's operations in the reduction commute with the final measurement, they can be pushed into classical post-processing, recovering the original prepare-and-measure protocol with identical security.

**Complementary and entropic arguments.** We present security through *complementarity*: the phase-error rate, which quantifies Eve's information, is estimated from the bit-error rate in the conjugate basis [2]. For the finite-key regime we adopt the entropic uncertainty relation framework, which bounds the smooth min-entropy of Alice's key conditioned on Eve's side information by the max-entropy of the conjugate-basis statistics, combined with the quantum leftover hash lemma to bound the extractable key length [10].

**Composable finite-key security.** A protocol is epsilon-secure if it is epsilon-cor-correct and epsilon-sec-secret, with total epsilon the sum of the two, so that the key can be safely composed with other cryptographic tasks. Statistical fluctuations in parameter estimation are controlled with concentration inequalities such as the Serfling bound applied to random sampling without replacement [10].

**Rate-distance benchmarking.** All protocols are benchmarked against the Pirandola-Laurenza-Ottaviani-Banchi (PLOB) bound [6], the exact secret-key capacity of the pure-loss channel, which sets the ultimate rate-loss tradeoff that no repeaterless point-to-point protocol can exceed. Protocols whose rates scale with the square root of transmittance are evaluated against the single-repeater bound instead.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Shor-Preskill proof and CSS codes

The conceptual breakthrough of Shor and Preskill was to prove BB84 secure *without* requiring Alice and Bob to perform any quantum computation [2]. The argument proceeds in two stages. First, consider an entanglement-based protocol: Alice distributes EPR pairs, both parties randomly measure in Z or X, and they run entanglement purification using a CSS code. CSS codes are built from two classical binary codes, one nested inside the other; the outer code corrects bit-flip (Z) errors and the inner code's dual corrects phase-flip (X) errors *independently*. This separability is the crux: bit errors are handled by ordinary classical error correction on the sifted key, while phase errors — which bound Eve's information — are corrected purely by privacy amplification, i.e., by hashing.

> Theorem: (Shor-Preskill [2]) BB84 with one-way classical post-processing is unconditionally secure against general coherent attacks. The asymptotic secret key rate is positive whenever the QBER lies below approximately 11 percent.

The 11 percent threshold emerges from the key-rate expression R = 1 - 2h(Q), where h is the binary entropy function and Q the QBER: one entropy term pays for error correction, the other for privacy amplification against phase errors. Remarkably, all quantum operations in the purification protocol either commute with the final Z measurements or act only on data that is discarded, so the entire quantum computation evaporates into classical sifting, error correction, and hashing. A second, independent proof route uses complementarity directly: Koashi's approach shows that if Alice could predict Bob's X-basis outcomes well, Eve must be nearly decoupled from the Z-basis key — the same uncertainty tradeoff, expressed without explicit codes. Together these results established the proof template — *reduction to entanglement purification plus complementarity* — on which all subsequent analyses, including decoy-state and MDI-QKD proofs, are built.

### 4.2 The decoy-state method

The decoy-state insight of Hwang, Wang, and Lo-Ma-Chen is disarmingly simple: if Eve cannot distinguish signal pulses from decoy pulses, any attack she mounts must act identically on both, so comparing their detection statistics exposes her [3]. In each round Alice randomly chooses among several intensities — typically a signal mu, a weak decoy nu, and vacuum — and Bob records the *gain* (detection probability) and error rate for each intensity class. Because a Poisson source with mean mu emits n photons with probability e^{-mu} mu^n / n!, the observed gains are linear combinations of the unknown n-photon yields Y_n:

```python
import math

def poisson(n, mu):
    return math.exp(-mu) * mu**n / math.factorial(n)

def gain(mu, yields, cutoff=8):
    # Q_mu = sum_n Y_n * e^{-mu} mu^n / n!
    return sum(yields[n] * poisson(n, mu) for n in range(cutoff))

# vacuum + weak decoy bound on single-photon yield (Lo-Ma-Chen):
def lower_bound_Y1(Q_mu, Q_nu, Q_vac, mu, nu):
    # Y_1 >= mu/(mu*nu - nu^2) * (Q_nu e^nu - Q_mu e^mu nu^2/mu^2
    #        - (mu^2 - nu^2)/mu^2 * Q_vac)
    term = (Q_nu * math.exp(nu) - Q_mu * math.exp(mu) * nu**2 / mu**2
            - (mu**2 - nu**2) / mu**2 * Q_vac)
    return mu / (mu * nu - nu**2) * term
```

From the measured gains one derives rigorous lower bounds on the single-photon yield Y_1 and upper bounds on the single-photon error rate e_1, using only the vacuum and one weak decoy intensity — already nearly tight against the infinite-decoy limit [3]. The secure key is then distilled *only* from the single-photon component, which is provably immune to PNS attack. The resulting GLLP-type rate formula,

**R >= q { -Q_mu f(E_mu) h(E_mu) + Q_1 [1 - h(e_1)] }**,

with q the sifting factor, f the error-correction inefficiency, and Q_1 the single-photon gain, restored unconditional security to practical lasers and, crucially, restored *linear* scaling of the rate with channel transmittance eta — the scaling one expects of an ideal single-photon source. Decoy-state BB84 rapidly became the workhorse of the field, underpinning metropolitan networks and, as we shall see, the first satellite QKD [7].

### 4.3 Measurement-device-independent QKD

MDI-QKD [4] eliminates detector side channels with a conceptual inversion: instead of trusting their detectors, Alice and Bob trust only their *state preparation* and outsource all measurement to an untrusted relay, Charlie, who may be Eve herself. Both parties send BB84-encoded weak coherent pulses (with decoy states) to Charlie, who interferes them on a beam splitter and announces the outcome of a Bell-state measurement. A successful projection onto the singlet or triplet state, heralded by coincident clicks in the two detectors, effectively performs entanglement swapping: conditioned on Charlie's announcement, Alice's and Bob's qubits are projected onto an entangled state even though they never interacted directly.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Proof sketch: the GLLP rate formula

We sketch how the decoy-state rate formula follows from the Shor-Preskill template [2][3]. Partition the sifted key by photon number n. Multi-photon pulses are *tagged*: Eve may know them fully, so they contribute nothing to the secret key. The untagged single-photon fraction has yield lower bound Y_1^L and phase-error upper bound e_1^U from decoy analysis. Applying CSS-code reasoning, bit errors in the single-photon component are corrected at cost Q_1 h(e_1) (with practical inefficiency f), and phase errors are removed by privacy amplification shrinking the key by Q_1 h(e_1). Subtracting the error-correction leakage Q_mu f(E_mu) h(E_mu) on the full sifted block yields the GLLP rate stated in section 4.2. Crucially, every quantity in the formula is either directly measured (gains, error rates) or rigorously bounded (Y_1, e_1) — no assumption about Eve's attack enters.

### Finite-key correction

In practice keys are finite. The Tomamichel-Lim-Gisin-Renner framework [10] gives an explicit, composable bound: for n sifted bits with observed error lambda on a random test sample, the extractable secret length is

**l <= n [1 - h(Q_tol + mu)] - leak_EC - log(2/epsilon_sec)**,

up to small corrections, where mu accounts for statistical fluctuation via the Serfling bound and Q_tol is the abort threshold. The entropic uncertainty relation lower-bounds the smooth min-entropy H_min(X|E) — Eve's uncertainty about the raw key — by the observed conjugate-basis statistics, and the quantum leftover hash lemma converts min-entropy into composable secret bits. Experiments confirmed that of order 10^4 to 10^5 sifted bits suffice for positive finite-key rates at metropolitan distances [10].

## 6 Limitations

- **Trusted nodes.** Both fiber backbones and satellite relays rely on intermediate nodes that hold keys in the clear. A compromised relay breaks end-to-end security; the network is only as trustworthy as its least secure node. True end-to-end security at global scale awaits quantum repeaters or high-rate entanglement-based satellite links [12].
- **Source assumptions remain.** MDI-QKD removes detector assumptions but still trusts state preparation. Source side channels — Trojan-horse attacks, mode dependencies, intensity fluctuations — require optical isolation, monitoring, and loss-tolerant analyses; fully closing them is an active research area [12].
- **Finite-key overhead.** Composable finite-key bounds [10] impose significant penalties at long distance: satellite passes last minutes, yielding limited block sizes, and statistical fluctuation terms can erase the asymptotic rate entirely for short sessions.
- **Phase coherence in TF-QKD.** Maintaining sub-wavelength phase stability between distant independent lasers over hundreds of kilometers of fiber demands dual-band stabilization and precise frequency dissemination — the dominant experimental complexity of twin-field systems [5].
- **Authentication and denial of service.** QKD provides key expansion, not key inception: the classical channel must be authenticated with pre-shared key, and Eve can always deny service by cutting the quantum channel. QKD strengthens, but does not replace, the surrounding cryptographic infrastructure [11].
- **Rate vs. distance tradeoff persists.** Even TF-QKD's square-root scaling eventually succumbs to detector dark counts; the PLOB bound is beaten, not abolished, and only quantum repeaters with memories can restore polynomial scaling indefinitely [6].

## 7 Conclusion

Four decades after Bennett and Brassard, quantum key distribution has matured from a beautiful idea into a layered engineering discipline with rigorous foundations. The Shor-Preskill reduction [2] gave BB84 unconditional security; the decoy-state method [3] made that security compatible with practical lasers; MDI-QKD [4] removed the detector side channels that quantum hackers had so profitably exploited; twin-field QKD [5] broke the repeaterless rate-distance limit [6] that was thought fundamental; and the Micius satellite [7] carried these protocols into orbit, demonstrating kilohertz key exchange over 1200 km and intercontinental trusted-node relay over 7600 km [13]. Finite-key analysis [10] closed the gap between asymptotic theorems and finite experimental runs with composable security definitions.

The open frontier is clear: replace trusted nodes with quantum repeaters, push entanglement-based satellite QKD to useful rates, and integrate QKD into a hybrid cryptographic ecosystem alongside post-quantum algorithms. The trajectory of the field suggests that each "fundamental" limitation — the PNS attack, detector side channels, the PLOB bound, the tyranny of fiber loss — has yielded to a sharper physical insight. The next such insight may finally deliver the global quantum internet.
