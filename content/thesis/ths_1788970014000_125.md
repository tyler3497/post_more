# Soft Robotics: Continuum Mechanics, Pneumatic Actuation, and Model-Based Control of Compliant Manipulators

## Abstract

Soft robotics abandons the rigid-link paradigm in favor of compliant continua whose infinite degrees of freedom arise from the deformation of elastomeric bodies themselves. This thesis unifies the three pillars of the field — continuum mechanics, pneumatic actuation, and model-based control — into a single mathematical framework for compliant manipulators. We derive the piecewise constant curvature kinematics that underpins real-time control, generalize it to the geometrically exact Cosserat rod theory capturing shear, extension, and torsion, and characterize fluidic elastomer actuators that convert chamber pressures into distributed wrench fields. Model-order reduction and feedback architectures are shown to render these infinite-dimensional models tractable for closed-loop control. Empirical evidence from cable-driven octopus-inspired arms, hydraulically actuated deep-sea manipulators, and rehabilitation gloves demonstrates sub-centimeter tracking and dynamic whole-arm manipulation. We close with the open challenges of hysteresis modeling, sensorization, and certified control that separate laboratory prototypes from deployed soft machines.

---

## 1 Introduction

For half a century, robotics was built on an axiom inherited from machine design: precision demands rigidity. Conventional manipulators are modeled as chains of rigid bodies connected by discrete joints, and their control theory — from computed torque to operational-space formulations — rests on finite-dimensional Lagrangian mechanics. Nature, meanwhile, solved manipulation differently. The octopus arm, the elephant trunk, and the human tongue achieve dexterous, whole-body manipulation with no skeleton at all, exploiting distributed compliance rather than suppressing it [1][2].

Soft robotics is the systematic engineering of this principle. Following the now-standard definition, a *soft robot* is a system whose body compliance is intrinsic and functionally exploited: Young's moduli on the order of $10^4$–$10^9$ Pa (comparable to biological tissue) rather than the $10^9$–$10^{12}$ Pa of metals and hard plastics [1]. This compliance confers safety in human contact, robustness to impact, and adaptability to unstructured environments — at the cost of abandoning the finite-degree-of-freedom modeling on which classical control depends.

This thesis addresses three interlocking questions:

1. **Kinematics and dynamics:** How does one model a manipulator with, strictly speaking, infinitely many degrees of freedom? We develop the piecewise constant curvature (PCC) approximation [3] and its geometrically exact generalization, Cosserat rod theory [4][5], showing precisely where each is valid.
2. **Actuation:** How do pneumatic and hydraulic chambers convert pressure into shape? We analyze fluidic elastomer actuators (FEAs), fiber reinforcement, and the pressure-to-wrench map [6][7].
3. **Control:** How does one close the loop on a continuum? We survey model-based architectures — feedforward compensation, reduced-order finite-element control, and state observers — that make real-time control possible [5][8].

The central claim is that soft manipulators are *controllable* in a rigorous sense: with the right reduced coordinates and constitutive laws, continuum mechanics yields models that are both predictive and fast enough for feedback.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Biological inspiration and the compliance spectrum

The field's founding narrative is biological. Trivedi, Rahn, Kier, and Walker surveyed the biomechanics of muscular hydrostats — structures like the octopus arm and elephant trunk that maintain constant volume while generating motion through antagonistic muscle groups [2]. Two lessons proved decisive. First, *embodied compliance* simplifies interaction: a soft body conforms to contact rather than requiring the controller to plan around it. Second, *distributed actuation* enables whole-arm manipulation, where grasping emerges from the arm's shape rather than from an end-effector alone.

Rus and Tolley's landmark 2015 review consolidated the field's design principles: soft materials (silicones, hydrogels, fabric composites), shape-changing mechanisms (pneumatic networks, tendon routing, dielectric elastomers), and the fabrication processes — soft lithography, lost-wax casting, and multi-material 3D printing — that make complex internal chamber geometries manufacturable [1]. Their review remains the canonical entry point.

### 2.2 From rigid kinematics to continua

Classical robot kinematics maps a finite joint vector $\mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ to end-effector pose via the product of exponentials. A soft arm has no joints; its configuration is a curve. Webster and Jones's review established the field's workhorse approximation: under the assumption that each segment bends as a circular arc, the configuration of an $m$-segment arm reduces to $3m$ parameters — arc length $L$, curvature $\kappa$, and bending-plane angle $\phi$ per segment [3]. This *piecewise constant curvature* hypothesis is exact for tendon-driven and multi-backbone designs in the absence of external loads, and it yields closed-form forward kinematics and analytic Jacobians.

Where loads, gravity, or torsion matter, the constant-curvature hypothesis breaks, and the geometrically exact theory of the Cosserat rod takes over [4][5]. Developed for slender structures in the 19th and 20th centuries, Cosserat theory treats the arm as a one-dimensional continuum carrying both position and orientation at every material point — rich enough to model shear, extension, bending, and torsion simultaneously.

### 2.3 Pneumatic actuation and fluidic elastomer actuators

The dominant actuation modality for soft manipulators remains fluidic: pressurized air or liquid inflates internal chambers, and anisotropic wall stiffness converts expansion into bending, twisting, or extension. Marchese, Katzschmann, and Rus provided a reproducible "recipe" for soft fluidic elastomer robots — molding silicone bodies around sacrificial wax cores, then dissolving the cores to form chamber networks — that became the community's standard fabrication pipeline [7]. Fiber reinforcement (inextensible threads wound helically around chambers) suppresses radial ballooning and programs the deformation mode, the principle behind the soft robotic glove for rehabilitation developed by Polygerinos and colleagues, which restores grasp function through precisely routed fiber-reinforced actuators [6].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is mathematical modeling grounded in experimental validation. We proceed in three stages: (i) derive the configuration-space kinematics of a soft segment; (ii) write its dynamics from continuum balance laws; (iii) construct the actuation map from chamber pressures to generalized forces, then design controllers on the resulting model.

### 3.1 Piecewise constant curvature kinematics

Consider a single inextensible segment of rest length $L$. Under the constant-curvature hypothesis, its shape is a circular arc of curvature $\kappa$ lying in a plane rotated by angle $\phi$ about the base $z$-axis. The tip position in the base frame is

$$\mathbf{p}(\kappa,\phi) = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{1-\cos(\kappa L)}{\kappa}\cos\phi \\[6pt] \dfrac{1-\cos(\kappa L)}{\kappa}\sin\phi \\[6pt] \dfrac{\sin(\kappa L)}{\kappa} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \lim_{\kappa\to 0}\mathbf{p} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\L\end{bmatrix}.$$

The configuration of an $m$-segment arm is $\mathbf{q} = (\kappa_1,\phi_1,\dots,\kappa_m,\phi_m)$, and forward kinematics composes the per-segment homogeneous transforms. The Jacobian $\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}) = \partial \mathbf{x}/\partial \mathbf{q}$ follows by differentiation and admits closed form — the property that makes PCC the standard choice for real-time resolved-rate control.

A reference implementation of PCC forward kinematics is:

```python
import numpy as np

def pcc_segment_tip(kappa, phi, L):
    """Tip position of one constant-curvature segment (base frame)."""
    if abs(kappa) < 1e-9:
        return np.array([0.0, 0.0, L])
    r = 1.0 / kappa
    return np.array([
        r * (1.0 - np.cos(kappa * L)) * np.cos(phi),
        r * (1.0 - np.cos(kappa * L)) * np.sin(phi),
        r * np.sin(kappa * L),
    ])

def pcc_segment_transform(kappa, phi, L):
    """Homogeneous transform of one PCC segment (rotation included)."""
    T = np.eye(4)
    R_phi = np.array([[np.cos(phi), -np.sin(phi), 0.0],
                      [np.sin(phi),  np.cos(phi), 0.0],
                      [0.0,          0.0,         1.0]])
    bend = kappa * L
    R_bend = np.array([[np.cos(bend), 0.0, np.sin(bend)],
                       [0.0,          1.0, 0.0         ],
                       [-np.sin(bend),0.0, np.cos(bend)]])
    T[:3, :3] = R_phi @ R_bend @ R_phi.T
    T[:3, 3] = pcc_segment_tip(kappa, phi, L)
    return T

def pcc_forward_kinematics(q, L):
    """q = [k1, p1, k2, p2, ...]; returns list of segment tip transforms."""
    T = np.eye(4)
    out = []
    for i in range(0, len(q), 2):
        T = T @ pcc_segment_transform(q[i], q[i+1], L)
        out.append(T.copy())
    return out
```

Multi-segment arms compose these transforms; velocities follow from the analytic Jacobian, and the standard rigid-robot control toolkit transfers with $\mathbf{q}$ as generalized coordinates.

### 3.2 The pressure-to-wrench map

For a pneumatic segment with $n_c$ chambers, the actuation input is the pressure vector $\mathbf{p} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_c}$. Under quasi-static assumptions, each chamber contributes a generalized force along the configuration coordinates. For three chambers arranged symmetrically at radius $d$ around the backbone with effective piston areas $A_i$, the bending moments about the base frame axes are, to first order,

$$\tau_\phi \;\approx\; d\sum_{i=1}^{3} A_i p_i \sin(\theta_i - \phi), \qquad \tau_\kappa \;\approx\; d\sum_{i=1}^{3} A_i p_i \cos(\theta_i - \phi),$$

with chamber angular positions $\theta_i$. This linear map $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{B}\,\mathbf{p}$ is the actuator Jacobian; its pseudoinverse solves the *control allocation* problem of distributing desired bending wrenches across chambers, and its nullspace parameterizes antagonistic co-contraction — the pneumatic analogue of muscle stiffness modulation.

### 3.3 Constitutive modeling

Elastomers are hyperelastic: stress derives from a strain-energy density $W$. The incompressible Neo-Hookean model $W = \tfrac{\mu}{2}(I_1 - 3) - \mu\ln J + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}(\ln J)^2$ captures moderate strains with a single shear modulus $\mu$, while Mooney–Rivlin and Yeoh forms extend fidelity to large strains. In Cosserat discretizations these laws appear as the stiffness operators $\mathbf{K}_{se}$, $\mathbf{K}_{bt}$ relating strains to internal wrenches. Viscoelasticity (stress relaxation, hysteresis) is the principal unmodeled effect and is treated in Section 6.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Piecewise Constant Curvature: Exactness, Robot-Specific Mappings, and Failure Modes

Webster and Jones distinguished two mappings in PCC modeling [3]: the *robot-independent* map from $(\kappa,\phi,L)$ to task space (universal geometry, given above), and the *robot-specific* map from actuator variables (tendon lengths, chamber pressures) to $(\kappa,\phi,L)$. This separation is the theory's great practical virtue — controllers can be designed in curvature space and retargeted across hardware by swapping the actuator map.

PCC is *exact* under three conditions: inextensibility, absence of shear, and negligible external loads. Tendon-driven and multi-backbone continuum robots satisfy these by design, which is why the hypothesis dominates surgical robotics. It fails gracefully but measurably when violated:

| Violation | Physical cause | Observable symptom |
|---|---|---|
| External tip load | Grasping, contact | S-shaped (non-circular) bending |
| Gravity | Long horizontal arms | Sagging; curvature varies along $s$ |
| Torsion | Twisting loads | Out-of-plane deviation, $\phi$ drift |
| Extensibility | Pneumatic elongation | Arc length $L$ pressure-dependent |
| Shear | Short, stubby segments | Tip position error at large $\kappa$ |

The modern remedy is the *piecewise constant strain* (PCS) extension: each element carries a constant six-dimensional strain twist $(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u})$ rather than scalar curvature, recovering shear, extension, and torsion while preserving closed-form element kinematics via the exponential map on $SE(3)$ [5].

### 4.2 Cosserat Rod Dynamics: The Geometrically Exact Model

The Cosserat rod describes the arm by a material curve $\mathbf{p}(s,t) \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and a director frame $\mathbf{R}(s,t) \in SO(3)$ at each arc-length station $s \in [0,L]$. The strain measures are

$$\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{R}^T \mathbf{p}', \qquad \widehat{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{R}^T \mathbf{R}',$$

where primes denote $\partial/\partial s$ and $\widehat{(\cdot)}$ is the skew-symmetric map. Here $\mathbf{v}$ captures shear and extension ($\mathbf{v} = (0,0,1)^T$ in the reference state) and $\mathbf{u}$ captures bending and torsion. The balance laws read

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{n}' + \mathbf{f} &= \rho A\,\ddot{\mathbf{p}}, \\
\mathbf{m}' + \mathbf{p}' \times \mathbf{n} + \mathbf{l} &= \partial_t(\rho\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}),
\end{aligned}$$

with internal force $\mathbf{n}$, internal couple $\mathbf{m}$, distributed loads $\mathbf{f},\mathbf{l}$, and inertia operator $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}$. Linear constitutive laws close the system: $\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{K}_{se}(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}^\ast)$, $\mathbf{m} = \mathbf{K}_{bt}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^\ast)$.

> **Theorem:** *Passivity of the Cosserat dynamic model.* With the elastic potential $\mathcal{V} = \tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^L [(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}^\ast)^T\mathbf{K}_{se}(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}^\ast) + (\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^\ast)^T\mathbf{K}_{bt}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^\ast)]\,ds$ and kinetic energy $\mathcal{T}$, the time derivative of the total mechanical energy along solutions satisfies $\dot{\mathcal{H}} = \int_0^L (\mathbf{f}^T\dot{\mathbf{p}} + \mathbf{l}^T\boldsymbol{\omega})\,ds + \text{boundary terms}$. Hence the unforced rod is passive with respect to distributed and boundary power ports, and any strictly passive feedback interconnected at these ports yields a stable closed loop.

This passivity property is the theoretical foundation for energy-based control of soft arms: damping injection at the boundary (the base or tip) provably stabilizes the infinite-dimensional plant. Renda and co-workers validated a discrete Cosserat dynamic model against an octopus-inspired cable-driven arm, showing that the model predicts the arm's bending dynamics under cable tensions with accuracy sufficient for feedforward control [4].

Numerically, the rod is discretized by finite differences, finite elements, or strain-parameterized Ritz expansions; the resulting differential-algebraic system is integrated with energy-momentum conserving schemes. The cost is real — hundreds of states versus a handful for PCC — which motivates the reduction techniques of Section 4.4.

### 4.3 Pneumatic Actuation: From Chamber Geometry to Programmable Deformation

A fluidic elastomer actuator converts scalar pressure into programmed motion through *anisotropy*. Three design levers dominate:

1. **Chamber geometry.** PneuNet-style networks of small chambers expand asymmetrically against a strain-limiting layer, producing large bending angles at modest pressures. Chamber aspect ratio sets the pressure–curvature gain.
2. **Fiber reinforcement.** Helically wound inextensible fibers suppress radial strain, channeling volumetric expansion into axial elongation or, with asymmetric fiber angles, into twisting. Polygerinos's rehabilitation glove exploits this: each finger actuator is a fiber-reinforced elastomeric tube whose bending profile is tuned to the biomechanics of the human finger, delivering assistive grasp forces through a wearable form factor [6].
3. **Strain-limiting layers.** Inextensible fabrics or stiffer elastomer plies on one wall convert symmetric inflation into bending — the mechanism in most soft grippers.

The quasi-static pressure–curvature relation for a bending FEA is well captured by a moment balance: the pressure moment $M_p = p \cdot A_c \cdot d$ (chamber pressure times effective area times moment arm) equilibrates the elastic restoring moment $M_e = EI_{\text{eff}}\,\kappa$ of the composite wall, giving $\kappa \approx p A_c d / EI_{\text{eff}}$. Hysteresis enters through the viscoelastic wall material and Coulomb-like friction in fiber windings; rate-dependent Prandtl–Ishlinskii operators model it for feedforward compensation.

Hydraulic variants replace air with liquid, gaining incompressibility (hence bandwidth and force density) at the cost of weight and leakage risk — the trade exploited by the deep-sea soft manipulator, whose hydraulic engine provides proportional control over sixteen channels at depths exceeding 2300 m [8].

### 4.4 Model-Based Control: Reduction, Observation, and Feedback

Controlling a continuum means controlling a PDE. Four architectures have proven viable:

- **(a) PCC feedback linearization.** Treating $\mathbf{q} = (\kappa_i,\phi_i)$ as generalized coordinates yields a finite-dimensional Euler–Lagrange system $\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\dot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q}) + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{p}$, where the elastic term $\mathbf{K}\mathbf{q}$ dominates (soft arms are stiffness-dominated, not inertia-dominated). Computed-torque-style controllers with gravity/elasticity compensation achieve accurate quasi-static tracking.
- **(b) Feedforward Cosserat compensation.** The full dynamic model [4] supplies feedforward wrenches for dynamic tasks — throwing, fast reaching — while a low-gain feedback loop corrects model error. Passivity (Section 4.2) guarantees stability of the interconnection.
- **(c) Reduced-order FEM with state observers.** Goury, Duriez, and colleagues project the full 3D finite-element model onto a low-dimensional POD basis identified offline, then close the loop with a state observer fusing sparse sensor measurements. This was the first demonstration of dynamically closed-loop controlled soft arms using a reduced-order FEM, running in real time by linearizing about the rest shape and updating the reduction online [5].
- **(d) Learning-augmented control.** Gaussian processes and neural networks learn the residual between the physics model and reality (hysteresis, unmodeled contacts), added as a disturbance estimate to (a)–(c).

Proprioception remains the binding constraint: without joint encoders, shape must be inferred from embedded flex sensors, liquid-metal strain gauges, optical fibers, or external vision. The observer in (c) is as important as the controller.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

The models above are not merely descriptive; they predict and enable performance. Representative validated results from the literature:

| System | Model used | Reported outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Octopus-inspired cable-driven arm [4] | Discrete Cosserat dynamics | Simulated bending matches experiment under dynamic cable loading |
| Soft robotic glove [6] | Fiber-reinforced FEA quasi-statics | Restores functional grasp in stroke rehabilitation trials |
| Deep-sea hydraulic manipulator [8] | Kinematic + pressure allocation | Proportional 16-channel control below 2300 m depth |
| Reduced-order FEM arm [5] | POD-reduced FEM + observer | Real-time closed-loop shape control at tens of Hz |
| PneuNet grippers [7] | Chamber moment-balance recipe | Reproducible fabrication; robust grasping across objects |

Three formal claims consolidate these results:

1. **Kinematic sufficiency.** For unloaded, slender, inextensible segments, PCC forward kinematics predicts tip position within a few percent of segment length — sufficient for resolved-rate control without external sensing [3].
2. **Dynamic predictiveness.** The Cosserat model with identified stiffness operators predicts transient bending under time-varying actuation, enabling feedforward control of dynamic maneuvers that quasi-static models cannot [4][5].
3. **Closed-loop stabilizability.** Passivity of the continuum plant plus strictly passive feedback implies asymptotic stability of the regulation error; reduced-order observers make the required state information available in real time [5].

The convergence of these lines is visible in fielded systems: Katzschmann's acoustically controlled soft robotic fish [9] and the deep-sea manipulator [8] operate the same modeling stack — continuum kinematics, pressure allocation, feedback — in the open ocean rather than the laboratory.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating what the framework does *not* yet deliver.

- **Hysteresis and viscoelastic drift.** Elastomers exhibit rate-dependent hysteresis and creep; identified stiffness operators drift with temperature, fatigue, and age. Current controllers compensate locally but lack global guarantees under material aging.
- **The sensing gap.** High-fidelity models demand high-fidelity state, yet embedded soft sensors remain coarser, noisier, and more drift-prone than rigid encoders. Shape estimation, not control law design, is the practical bottleneck.
- **Contact-rich manipulation.** The models above assume known, smooth distributed loads. Grasping and locomotion involve unilateral contact, friction, and large topological shape change — regimes where Cosserat theory needs augmentation by contact mechanics and where PCC fails outright.
- **Computational cost of exact models.** Full 3D FEM and fine Cosserat discretizations remain too slow for high-rate feedback without reduction, and reduction validity is local to the training regime.
- **Force control and safety certification.** Impedance control of soft arms is promising precisely because the hardware is compliant, but certifying force bounds for human interaction — the regulatory prerequisite for clinical and domestic deployment — has no established methodology.
- **Untethered power.** Pneumatic compressors and hydraulic pumps remain bulky; energy-autonomous soft robots are the exception, not the rule [1].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Soft robotics has matured from a materials curiosity into a discipline with predictive theory. The arc of this thesis runs from the geometric simplicity of piecewise constant curvature — three numbers per segment, closed-form kinematics — through the full generality of Cosserat rod dynamics with its passivity guarantees, to the engineering reality of fiber-reinforced fluidic actuators and pressure-allocation control. The empirical record shows that these models are not academic ornaments: they steer rehabilitation gloves, deep-sea manipulators, and dynamic soft arms in closed loop.

The frontier is no longer *whether* soft manipulators can be modeled and controlled, but *how robustly*: against material aging, against contact-rich uncertainty, and against the sensing deficit. Solving those will require the same synthesis this thesis advocates — continuum mechanics for structure, careful actuator physics for the pressure-to-motion map, and control theory that respects infinite dimensionality rather than pretending it away. The octopus does not compute Jacobians; but until we understand embodied intelligence at its level, Jacobians — curvature-parameterized, Cosserat-exact, and pressure-driven — are how we will build its artificial counterparts.
