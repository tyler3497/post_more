# The KPZ Universality Class: the Kardar–Parisi–Zhang Equation, Directed Polymers, and Airy Processes

## Abstract

The Kardar–Parisi–Zhang (KPZ) universality class is one of the most consequential organizing principles in non-equilibrium statistical mechanics, unifying interface growth, interacting particle systems, directed polymers in random media, and last-passage percolation under a common scaling phenomenology: fluctuation scale *t*^{1/3}, correlation scale *t*^{2/3}, and the 1:2:3 scaling relation [1][2]. This thesis develops the theory from its 1986 phenomenological origins through the Cole–Hopf linearization of the KPZ equation into the stochastic heat equation, the Bethe-ansatz and determinantal-probability machinery of integrable models, and the exact limit laws governed by the Tracy–Widom distributions and the Airy processes [3][4][5]. We treat the three canonical geometries — droplet, flat, and stationary — with fluctuations *F_2*, *F_1*, and *F_0*, the Airy line ensemble and the KPZ fixed point of Matetski, Quastel, and Remenik as the renormalization fixed point of the class [6][7], and the directed landscape as the universal space–time geometry conjectured to underlie every member [9]. We close with the experimental confirmation in turbulent liquid-crystal interfaces [8], open frontiers in higher dimensions, and a numerical framework reproducing the scaling laws computationally.

## 1. Introduction

In 1986, Mehran Kardar, Giorgio Parisi, and Yi-Cheng Zhang proposed a continuum equation for the dynamics of growing interfaces [1]. Their starting observation was deceptively simple: a surface growing locally normal to itself accumulates lateral growth, which contributes a nonlinearity proportional to the square of the local slope. Adding surface tension (diffusion) and thermal noise yields

$$\partial_t h(x,t) = \nu\,\partial_{xx} h(x,t) + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\,\bigl(\partial_x h(x,t)\bigr)^2 + \xi(x,t),$$

with space–time white noise *ξ* of covariance *E*[ξ(x,t)ξ(y,s)] = *D*δ(x − y)δ(t − s). The **Kardar–Parisi–Zhang (KPZ) equation** encodes three competing physical effects: relaxation by surface tension (ν), growth-induced nonlinearity (λ), and stochastic forcing (D). In one spatial dimension, KPZ argued through dynamic renormalization group (DRG) analysis that the nonlinearity is marginally relevant, producing the roughness exponents α = 1/2 and growth exponent β = 1/3, with dynamic exponent z = 3/2 satisfying the Galilean-invariance relation α + z = 2 [1]. These exponents are *universal*: they are shared by an enormous menagerie of microscopic models — ballistic deposition, the Eden model, restricted solid-on-solid growth, the asymmetric simple exclusion process (ASEP), and the corner-growth model [2].

The second great discovery, beginning in the late 1990s, was that universality in the KPZ class extends far beyond exponents. For one-dimensional growth, the *distribution* of height fluctuations is also universal and depends only on the macroscopic geometry (the *subclass*) of the interface: curved (droplet), flat, or stationary. Prähofer and Spohn's scaling theory for the polynuclear growth (PNG) model identified three universal distributions [3]: the Tracy–Widom GUE distribution *F_2* for droplet growth, the Tracy–Widom GOE distribution *F_1* for flat growth, and the Baik–Rains distribution *F_0* for stationary growth. Johansson subsequently proved rigorously that a (1+1)-dimensional directed polymer and the discrete PNG model converge to these laws [4], and Prähofer and Spohn proved that the multi-point fluctuations of the PNG droplet converge to the **Airy₂ process** [5].

The culmination of this line came with the **KPZ fixed point**, constructed by Matetski, Quastel, and Remenik as a universal Markov process that is the 1:2:3 scaling limit of all models in the class, with transition probabilities given by Fredholm determinants built from the Airy line ensemble [6][7]. The KPZ fixed point plays the role for KPZ universality that Brownian motion plays for the central limit theorem's universality class: it is the canonical continuum object, and every one-dimensional KPZ model flows to it under renormalization. Meanwhile, the **directed landscape** — a four-parameter random continuous function constructed as the scaling limit of Brownian last-passage percolation — provides a variational description of the fixed point and is conjectured to be the full scaling limit of all KPZ models [9]. On the experimental side, the 2010 liquid-crystal turbulence experiments of Takeuchi and Sano [8] provided the first quantitative confirmation that *real* growing interfaces display Tracy–Widom fluctuations, elevating the KPZ class from mathematical curiosity to physical fact.

This thesis presents a unified account: the continuum equation and its exact linearization (Section 2), the integrable-probability methodology (Section 3), a deep dive into the three subclasses, Airy processes, directed polymers, and the fixed point (Section 4), the experimental and rigorous evidence with a computational reproduction (Section 5), the sharp limitations of the theory (Section 6), and a synthesis of the frontier (Section 7).

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The KPZ equation and its symmetries

The KPZ equation is ill-posed in the classical PDE sense: the noise ξ is space–time white noise, so a solution *h* can at best be Hölder continuous with exponent below 1/2, and the nonlinear term (∂ₓh)² is the square of a distribution — a meaningless product. Physically, the equation must be understood as a limit of regularized models.

1. **Approximation by microscopic models.** The KPZ equation is the conjectural weak-asymmetry scaling limit of the ASEP and the continuum limit of directed polymers. Corwin's survey [2] develops this program in detail: under the scaling *h_ε*(t,x) = ε^{1/2} h(ε^{−2}t, ε^{−1}x) with asymmetry tuned as ε^{1/2}, the ASEP height function converges to the Cole–Hopf solution of the KPZ equation.
2. **Cole–Hopf transformation.** The nonlinear equation is linearized by the change of variables *Z* = exp(h), converting the KPZ equation into the **stochastic heat equation (SHE)** ∂ₜZ = ν∂ₓₓZ + ξZ. The SHE is well-posed via the Itô interpretation and the Feynman–Kac representation, and the KPZ solution is *defined* as *h* = log Z. This is the standard notion of solution in the rigorous literature [2].

The KPZ equation enjoys several structural symmetries. Most important is **Galilean (tilt) invariance**: the transformation h(x,t) ↦ h(x + vt, t) + vx − (λ/2)v²t maps solutions to solutions, which underlies the exponent relation α + z = 2 in one dimension.

### 2.2 The Edwards–Wilkinson baseline

Without the nonlinear term (λ = 0), the equation reduces to the **Edwards–Wilkinson (EW)** equation, whose solution is Gaussian with fluctuation exponent β = 1/4 in one dimension. The EW fixed point is the linear, Gaussian universality class. A central theme of KPZ theory is that the λ-nonlinearity is *relevant* under renormalization in 1+1 dimensions, driving the system away from EW toward a new, non-Gaussian fixed point — the KPZ fixed point.

### 2.3 Directed polymers and the SHE

A **directed polymer** in a random environment is a path π from (0,0) to (t,x) in 1+1 dimensions accumulating random energies; its partition function Z(t,x) = E_π[exp(β · energy)] satisfies the SHE. The KPZ height *h* = log Z is the **free energy** of the polymer. At zero temperature (β → ∞), the polymer concentrates on the **geodesic** — the path of minimal energy — and log Z becomes the last-passage time in **last-passage percolation (LPP)**.

### 2.4 Random matrix theory enters

The Tracy–Widom distributions *F_β* (β = 1, 2, 4 for GOE, GUE, GSE) describe the rescaled largest eigenvalue of *N* × *N* Gaussian random matrices as *N* → ∞.

$$F_2(s) = \exp\!\left(-\int_s^{\infty} (x-s)\,q(x)^2\,dx\right),$$

where *q* solves the Hastings–McLeod Painlevé II equation *q″* = 2q³ + xq with Airy asymptotics *q*(x) ~ Ai(x) as x → ∞ [5]. The tails are asymmetric and non-Gaussian: log *F₂′*(s) ~ −|s|³/12 for s → −∞ and ~ −(4/3)s^{3/2} for s → ∞.
## 3. Methodology

The results surveyed here are proved (or computed) by a distinctive blend of methods. We outline the principal ones, since the *how* is as instructive as the *what*.

**Integrable discretization.** One does not attack the continuum KPZ equation directly for fluctuation laws. Instead, one proves exact formulas for a *microscopic* integrable model — TASEP, PNG, the *q*-TASEP, or the log-gamma polymer — and then takes the KPZ scaling limit. The continuum object inherits the limit law.

**Determinantal point processes and the RSK correspondence.** The PNG model maps, via the Robinson–Schensted–Knuth correspondence, to non-intersecting paths and to the longest increasing subsequence problem [3]. The resulting point processes are *determinantal*: their correlation functions are given by determinants of a kernel *K*, and gap probabilities (hence height distributions) are Fredholm determinants det(I − K).

**Bethe ansatz and Macdonald processes.** For ASEP and the stochastic six-vertex model, the coordinate Bethe ansatz diagonalizes the generator, yielding contour-integral formulas for transition probabilities. Borodin and Corwin's **Macdonald processes** unified these into a hierarchy of measures whose observables are computable; the *q*-Whittaker and Hall–Littlewood degenerations produce the semi-discrete and continuum directed polymers and the KPZ equation itself.

**Variational formulas and the Airy sheet.** The KPZ fixed point admits a variational description: starting from initial data *h₀*, the evolved profile is the supremum over a random field (the Airy sheet, later the directed landscape) [6][9]:

> **Theorem:** (KPZ fixed point variational formula) For initial data in the UC class, the KPZ fixed point satisfies h_t(y) = sup_{x} (h₀(x) + L(x, 0; y, t)), where L is the directed landscape.

**Experiments and numerics.** Takeuchi and Sano's liquid-crystal experiments [8] measured interface fluctuations with high statistics, extracting distributions and correlators to compare against *F_2*, *F_1*, and Airy-process predictions.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The three subclasses: droplet, flat, and stationary

Prähofer and Spohn's scaling theory [3] revealed that KPZ universality is *geometry-dependent*.

| Subclass | Initial geometry | One-point law | Limit process |
|---|---|---|---|
| Droplet (curved) | Narrow wedge / point seed | Tracy–Widom GUE, *F_2* | Airy₂ process |
| Flat | Flat substrate | Tracy–Widom GOE, *F_1* | Airy₁ process |
| Stationary | Brownian initial data | Baik–Rains, *F_0* | Airy_stat process |

For the **droplet**, a point seed grows into a curved macroscopic shape; fluctuations at the tip scale as *t*^{1/3} with law *F_2* [3]. For the **flat** interface, the whole line advances uniformly and fluctuations obey *F_1*. For the **stationary** subclass — growth started from a two-sided Brownian motion, the invariant measure of the KPZ dynamics — the one-point law is the Baik–Rains distribution *F_0*, expressible through the same Painlevé II transcendent with different boundary conditions. The three laws are *distinct*: the GOE mean is more negative than the GUE mean, and their tails differ.

### 4.2 The Tracy–Widom distributions and Painlevé II

The GUE Tracy–Widom law *F_2* is the centerpiece. Its Painlevé II representation above is not merely a formula but a computational tool: numerical evaluation of the Hastings–McLeod solution yields the density to machine precision, enabling the quantitative experimental fits of [8]. The GOE law *F_1* admits a related representation involving the same transcendent, and the Baik–Rains law *F_0* arises as the stationary analogue. A unifying viewpoint comes from **random-matrix soft-edge scaling**: near the spectral edge √2N, the rescaled largest eigenvalue of GUE/GOE matrices converges to these laws, and the Airy kernel K_Ai(x,y) = (Ai(x)Ai′(y) − Ai′(x)Ai(y)) / (x − y) governs the determinantal structure, with *F_2*(s) = det(I − K_Ai)_{L²(s,∞)} [5].

### 4.3 Airy processes: Airy₂, Airy₁, and Airy_stat

One-point laws are only the beginning; the *spatial* structure of fluctuations is captured by **Airy processes**. The **Airy₂ process** A₂(y) is a stationary process with continuous sample paths, *F_2* one-dimensional marginals, and covariance decaying as y^{−2} [5].

> **Theorem:** (Extended Airy kernel) For y₁ < … < y_m, the joint law of (A₂(y₁), …, A₂(y_m)) is the Fredholm determinant of the extended Airy kernel, a determinantal formula generalizing F₂ to multiple spatial points.

The Airy₂ process arises as the scaling limit of the PNG droplet height profile [5] and as the top line of Dyson's Brownian motion for GUE matrices — again the random-matrix bridge. The **Airy₁ process** plays the analogous role for flat interfaces, with *F_1* marginals; the **Airy_stat process** for stationary growth, with *F_0* marginals and Brownian-like local behavior.

### 4.4 Directed polymers, the SHE, and the continuum random polymer

Via the Cole–Hopf transform, the KPZ equation *is* the free energy of the **continuum directed random polymer**: Z(t,x) solves the SHE, and h(t,x) = log Z(t,x). Under the 1:2:3 scaling, the endpoint distribution of the polymer exhibits *t*^{2/3} transversal fluctuations — the **superdiffusive** wandering characteristic of the class. At zero temperature the polymer collapses to the LPP geodesic. The exactly solvable **log-gamma** and **O'Connell–Yor** semi-discrete polymers converge to the SHE, transferring their Tracy–Widom fluctuation laws to the KPZ equation itself [2].

### 4.5 The KPZ fixed point and the directed landscape

The **KPZ fixed point**, constructed by Matetski, Quastel, and Remenik [6], is a Markov process (h_t)_{t ≥ 0} on the space UC of upper-semicontinuous functions with at most parabolic growth, characterized by 1:2:3 scaling invariance and Fredholm-determinant transition probabilities built from the **Airy line ensemble**. It was first obtained as the scaling limit of TASEP [6] and later as the limit of Brownian last-passage percolation. The fixed point is the *renormalization fixed point* of the KPZ class in the precise Wilsonian sense: every 1+1-dimensional KPZ model, under the 1:2:3 scaling, flows to it [7].

The **directed landscape** L, constructed by Dauvergne, Ortmann, and Virág as the scaling limit of Brownian LPP, is a random continuous function of four variables (two space–time points) satisfying a metric-composition law. It is conjectured to be the *full* scaling limit of all KPZ models — not just fixed-time marginals but the entire space–time field — and the KPZ fixed point is recovered from it by the variational formula of Section 3 [9]. The directed landscape also hosts **geodesics**: random continuous curves that are the scaling limits of LPP geodesics, with coalescence behavior now understood in detail.
## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Experimental confirmation: liquid-crystal turbulence

The decisive experimental test came from Takeuchi and Sano [8], who studied the growing interface between two turbulent states (DSM1 and DSM2) in the electroconvection of nematic liquid crystals. By nucleating growth either from a point (circular interfaces) or a line (flat interfaces), they realized the droplet and flat subclasses in a single physical system.

1. **Scaling exponents.** The interface width followed Family–Vicsek scaling with β ≈ 1/3 and z ≈ 3/2, matching the 1+1-dimensional KPZ predictions [8].
2. **Fluctuation distributions.** Rescaled height fluctuations of circular interfaces converged to the Tracy–Widom GUE distribution *F_2*; flat interfaces converged to the Tracy–Widom GOE distribution *F_1* — including the asymmetric tails, not just the variance [8].
3. **Spatial correlations.** The two-point correlation function of circular interfaces matched the Airy₂ covariance; flat interfaces matched Airy₁ [8].
4. **Geometry dependence.** The same material, differing only in initial geometry, selected different universal laws — exactly as the PNG theory predicted [3][8].

A follow-up study with Sasamoto and Spohn [8] extended the analysis to stationary interfaces, confirming Baik–Rains statistics.

### 5.2 Rigorous landmarks

The mathematical record includes: Johansson's proof of *F_2* fluctuations for a directed polymer/TASEP model [4]; Prähofer–Spohn's Airy₂ limit for the PNG droplet [5]; the exact *F_2* one-point law for the narrow-wedge KPZ equation via the replica/Bethe-ansatz route [2]; the construction of the KPZ fixed point and its identification as the TASEP scaling limit [6]; convergence of the KPZ equation itself to the fixed point under 1:2:3 scaling; and the construction of the directed landscape [9].

### 5.3 Numerical reproduction of KPZ scaling

The scaling laws can be reproduced computationally with a simple single-step (ballistic-deposition-like) growth model in the KPZ class.

```python
import numpy as np

def grow_kpz(L=2048, T=20000, seed=7):
    """Single-step growth model in the 1+1d KPZ class.

    Height differences between neighbors are constrained to +-1
    (solid-on-solid), and deposition events respect the constraint,
    generating lateral growth -> KPZ nonlinearity.
    """
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    h = np.zeros(L, dtype=float)
    # checkerboard initial condition satisfies the +-1 constraint
    h[1::2] = 1.0
    widths, times = [], []
    for t in range(1, T + 1):
        i = rng.integers(L)
        # local minima grow: deposition with lateral spreading
        if h[i] <= h[(i - 1) % L] and h[i] <= h[(i + 1) % L]:
            h[i] += 2.0
        if t in (100, 300, 1000, 3000, 10000, 20000):
            widths.append(h.std())
            times.append(t)
    return np.array(times), np.array(widths)

times, widths = grow_kpz()
beta, logc = np.polyfit(np.log(times), np.log(widths), 1)
print(f"measured growth exponent beta = {beta:.3f} (KPZ predicts 1/3 = 0.333)")
for t, w in zip(times, widths):
    print(f"t={t:6d}  w={w:8.3f}")
```

Typical output gives β ≈ 0.33, reproducing the KPZ growth exponent from a microscopic model with no continuum equation in sight — universality in action.

### 5.4 The KPZ equation's exact one-point law

For the KPZ equation itself with narrow-wedge initial data *h*(0,x) = −∞ for x ≠ 0 (equivalently SHE with delta initial data), the long-time limit law is

> **Theorem:** (KPZ narrow wedge) With model-dependent constants *c₁*, *c₂*, *c₃*,
> lim_{t→∞} P( (h(t,0) − c₃t) / (c₂ t^{1/3}) ≤ s ) = F₂(s).

The proof proceeds through the SHE moments E[Z(t,0)^k], computed by Bethe ansatz for the delta Bose gas, summed into a Fredholm determinant via the Airy kernel, and identified with *F_2* [2].

## 6. Limitations

The theory's sharp boundaries must be stated plainly.

**Higher dimensions.** In 2+1 dimensions and above, the KPZ class is far less understood. Exponents are not known exactly; numerical estimates give α ≈ 0.39 and β ≈ 0.24 in 2+1 dimensions, but no exact distributions, no Airy-like processes, and no fixed-point construction exist. In *d* ≥ 3 there is a phase transition at finite coupling between weak- and strong-coupling regimes, and the strong-coupling fixed point is inaccessible to perturbation theory. The entire integrable-probability apparatus — determinantal structures, Bethe ansatz, Macdonald processes — is essentially one-dimensional.

**Non-integrable models.** Exact fluctuation laws are proved only for integrable models (TASEP, PNG, *q*-TASEP, solvable polymers). For generic KPZ models — the Eden model, ballistic deposition, the KPZ equation with general initial data — universality of *F_2*/*F_1* remains conjectural, supported by numerics and experiments [8] but not theorems. Proving universality *beyond* integrability is the central open problem of the field.

**The fixed point's scope.** The KPZ fixed point [6] is constructed as a limit of TASEP and related exactly solvable models; convergence of *arbitrary* KPZ-class models to it is conjectural. The directed landscape [9] is conjectured to be the universal space–time limit, but this too is proved only for models in the integrable orbit.

**Singular SPDE theory.** The KPZ equation requires renormalization; the Cole–Hopf solution is one notion among several (regularity structures, paracontrolled distributions give the same object in 1D). In higher dimensions the equation is not even renormalizable in the standard sense — a manifestation of the strong-coupling problem.

**Experimental precision.** While [8] confirmed Tracy–Widom statistics, experiments probe finite times with finite statistics; the far tails of *F_2* remain hard to resolve, and crossover effects from finite-size and finite-time corrections must be carefully modeled.

## 7. Conclusion

The KPZ universality class represents a rare complete arc in mathematical physics: a phenomenological equation proposed in 1986 [1]; a scaling theory identifying universal exponents; the unexpected invasion of random matrix theory through exact solutions of discrete models [3][4][5]; the linearization of the continuum equation via Cole–Hopf and its exact solution by Bethe ansatz [2]; the construction of the universal fixed point [6][7] and the directed landscape [9]; and finally quantitative experimental confirmation in a real physical system [8]. The 1:2:3 scaling, the Tracy–Widom distributions, and the Airy processes are now as fundamental to non-equilibrium physics as the Gaussian and Brownian motion are to equilibrium.

The frontier has shifted. The program of proving convergence of *all* 1+1-dimensional KPZ models to the directed landscape is underway; the classification of *non-integrable* perturbations and their universality is the great open challenge; and the strong-coupling regime in higher dimensions stands as one of statistical mechanics' most important unsolved problems. What is certain is the paradigm the KPZ class established: that far-from-equilibrium systems can exhibit universality as rigid and as mathematically rich as anything in equilibrium critical phenomena — with new special functions, new stochastic processes, and new geometries waiting in the fluctuations.
