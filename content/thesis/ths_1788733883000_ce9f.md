# Stacked Borrows and Tree Borrows: Formalizing the Rust Borrow Checker with Operational Semantics in Miri

## Abstract

Rust's ownership type system eliminates data races without a garbage collector, yet the language's systems-programming power rests on `unsafe` blocks that bypass the borrow checker's static discipline. The open question is semantic: what aliasing rules must even unsafe code obey for the compiler to soundly apply alias-based optimizations? This thesis gives a rigorous exposition of the two operational aliasing models answering that question — **Stacked Borrows** [1], the POPL 2020 model of Jung, Dang, Kang, and Dreyer, and its successor **Tree Borrows** [2] — and their executable realization in the **Miri** interpreter [3]. We reconstruct retagging, per-location borrow stacks and permission trees, protectors, and the freeze discipline as a small-step operational semantics; connect them to the **RustBelt** semantic foundation [4] and the unsafe-code guidelines [5]; and survey the mechanized Coq proofs licensing LLVM-style reordering optimizations. We report the large-scale validation against the standard library test suite, where Stacked Borrows exposed genuine aliasing violations that were fixed, and analyze the remaining limitations — interior mutability, function-boundary retagging, model selection — that keep aliasing semantics among the hardest open problems in language design.

## 1 Introduction

Rust's *ownership* buys two things at once: memory safety without runtime cost, and *aliasing information* that optimizers can exploit. If the compiler knows `*x` and `*y` cannot alias, it may reorder or eliminate memory accesses across function calls with only intraprocedural reasoning, much as C compilers exploit `restrict`. But real Rust programs are not all checkable: device drivers, lock-free data structures, and foreign-function interfaces must cast references to raw pointers, do pointer arithmetic, or reinterpret bytes. The `unsafe` keyword marks these regions, and with it came a contract whose terms were, for years, unwritten. Programmers knew intuitively which raw-pointer uses were "fine," but the optimizer needed a *specification*, not an intuition. Without one, every alias-based optimization was a gamble.

*Stacked Borrows* [1] proposes that specification as an **operational semantics**: a dynamic model of memory tracking, for every pointer, the borrow it derives from, declaring violators to exhibit *undefined behavior* — freeing the compiler to assume the discipline holds. Implemented in **Miri** [3], an interpreter for Rust's Mid-level Intermediate Representation, any Rust program can be *executed against the specification* and flagged the moment it steps outside the rules. The empirical headline is striking: running large parts of the standard library test suite under the model found only six violations in architecture-independent code, each confirmed as a bug and fixed — evidence the model captures the community's implicit contract rather than inventing one.

Yet Stacked Borrows proved too strict around raw-pointer round-trips, rejecting patterns the community considered legitimate, and its "pop everything above" stack discipline drew criticism as too harsh and too subtle. **Tree Borrows** [2], by Villani and collaborators (PLDI 2025), redesigns the machinery around a *tree* of borrows with explicit permission states, accepting strictly more programs while retaining optimization power. It ships in Miri as the experimental successor via `-Zmiri-tree-borrows`.

## 2 Background

### Ownership, borrowing, and the limits of static checking

Rust's borrow checker enforces a simple invariant: at any program point, a location is reachable through either any number of shared (`&T`) borrows or exactly one mutable (`&mut T`) borrow, never both. Lifetimes approximate borrow regions; the compiler rejects what it cannot prove safe. `unsafe` Rust suspends the checker's judgment but not the optimizer's appetite: LLVM will apply `noalias`-driven reorderings to code with raw-pointer casts unless told otherwise — and historically it was told by nothing. The Rust Reference lists behaviors "considered undefined" [7] but said almost nothing precise about *aliasing* violations, leaving miscompilation reports to be adjudicated by mailing-list consensus.

### RustBelt: the semantic bedrock

The first rigorous account of Rust's *types* came from **RustBelt** [4] (Jung et al., POPL 2018): a semantic model of a core Rust-like language in the Iris concurrent separation logic, mechanized in Coq. RustBelt proves the standard library's central unsafe abstractions — `Cell`, `RefCell`, `Mutex`, `Rc`, channels — *sound*: safe clients cannot observe undefined behavior. A type is a *protocol* governing how ownership may be shared over time, with *lifetime logic* tracking borrow expiry.

RustBelt models *safe* interfaces atop unsafe code; it assumes the unsafe implementation respects an aliasing discipline it does not define. Stacked Borrows fills exactly this gap: where RustBelt says what clients may assume, Stacked Borrows says what implementations may do. The projects share authors, tooling (Iris/Coq), and philosophy — semantics as executable, checkable mathematics.

### Miri: an interpreter as a specification laboratory

**Miri** [3] interprets Rust's MIR with a fully instrumented memory model. Because it executes the compiler's own IR, it observes every allocation, retag, read, and write, and can *enforce* an aliasing model dynamically: the moment a program performs a forbidden access, Miri reports undefined behavior with a diagnostic. This turns a paper semantics into a *test oracle* — `cargo miri test` runs a crate's suite under the aliasing model — and it is the instrument by which both models were validated at scale.

## 3 Methodology

Our investigation follows the primary sources' methodology, unusual in being simultaneously *formal* and *empirical*.

**Operational formalization.** Rather than static axioms, Stacked Borrows defines a *dynamic* monitor: each location carries a *borrow stack* recording borrow history, and every access is checked against — and updates — that stack. Retagging (fresh tag per new reference) makes the model flow-sensitive with no static analysis. The decisive advantage: the specification and the checker are the same implementable artifact.

**Mechanized metatheory.** The POPL 2020 paper proves in Coq that the model is *strong enough to matter*: it validates the alias-based optimizations the authors targeted — reordering reads across unknown code and function calls using only intraprocedural reasoning. The proofs establish the crucial direction: *if* unsafe code respects Stacked Borrows, *then* the compiler's reorderings are sound.

**Ecosystem-scale validation.** Strictness was measured by running the standard library test suite under Miri with Stacked Borrows enforced — calibrating the model against reality rather than authorial taste (§5).

**Comparative analysis.** For Tree Borrows we contrast its permission-tree design against the stack design — retagging, access checking, invalidation, function boundaries — evaluating the claim that it accepts a strict superset of reasonable programs while preserving optimization power, drawing on the reference documentation [6] and Miri implementation.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Tags, retags, and the borrow stack

The central fiction: *every pointer carries a tag* identifying its borrow, and every location carries a *stack* of borrows derived from it.

- On reference creation, Miri performs a **retag**: mint fresh tag *t*, associate the pointer with *t*, and **push** an item for *t* onto the location's stack. Mutable reborrows push `Unique(t)`; shared reborrows push `SharedReadOnly(t)`.
- On a memory access through tag *t*, the model finds *t*'s item, **pops** every item above it, and proceeds. If *t* is absent, the access is *undefined behavior*.

```rust
let mut x = 5;            // stack: [Unique(0)]
let xm = &mut x;          // retag -> tag 1; stack: [Unique(0), Unique(1)]
let raw = xm as *mut i32; // raw ptr gets tag ⊥; pushes SharedRW
*xm = 10;                 // uses tag 1: pops SharedRW
*raw = 20;                // uses ⊥: SharedRW gone -> UB!
```

The write through `xm` pops the raw pointer's `SharedRW` item, so the later write through `raw` finds no valid item. This is the borrow checker's `&mut` discipline, extended *dynamically* past the point where the programmer entered `unsafe`.

> **Theorem (Stack discipline, informal):** *At any point, the tags that may still access a location are precisely the items on its borrow stack; any access through a tag not on the stack is undefined behavior.* [1]

Raw pointers get the untagged marker `⊥` plus a `SharedReadWrite` item: they may read and write but grant no exclusivity and are invalidated first.

### 4.2 Permissions and the freeze discipline

Stack items encode a *permission lattice*:

| Item | Granted by | Permits | Invalidated by |
|---|---|---|---|
| `Unique(t)` | `&mut T` reborrow | read + write through *t* | access through a tag above it |
| `SharedReadOnly(t)` | `&T` reborrow | read through *t* | any *write* through another tag |
| `SharedReadWrite` | raw pointer cast | read + write through `⊥` | access through a stacked borrow |

Creating a shared reference **freezes** the location: while the shared borrow is live, no mutation through an ancestor borrow is allowed — the dynamic mirror of the static `&T`/`&mut T` exclusion. Interior mutability is the principled exception: `UnsafeCell` opts out of the freeze, which is what makes `Cell`, `RefCell`, and atomics expressible. Both models treat `UnsafeCell` as the *only* sanctioned source of aliasing writes under shared borrows.

The freeze is *flow-sensitive*: a shared reborrow freezes only until the shared borrow's last use, after which ancestors may be used again — precision the static checker historically lacked.

### 4.3 Protectors, two-phase borrows, and function boundaries

A purely intraprocedural stack cannot justify LLVM's `noalias` on parameters. The missing link is the **protector**: on function entry, each reference argument receives a protector tied to the *call*; if the body invalidates a protected borrow — e.g., writing through a smuggled raw pointer from another argument — Miri reports UB *at the invalidating access*. The protector encodes "this function will not invalidate its caller's borrows through the back door," exactly what `noalias` asserts to LLVM.

Two mechanisms complete the picture:

1. **Two-phase borrows** — `vec.push(vec.len())` desugars to a mutable borrow whose activation is *deferred* until the call begins. Stacked Borrows pushes the `Unique` item in a not-yet-active state, permitting the intervening shared uses safe Rust allows.
2. **Expiry on return** — protectors expire at return, and caller stacks resume authority, so borrows flow back across the boundary without spurious violations.

These rules validate *interprocedural* optimizations with only *intraprocedural* reasoning — the paper's headline metatheoretic result.

### 4.4 Tree Borrows: from stacks to permission trees

Stacked Borrows' harshest critics pointed at raw-pointer round-trips: converting `&mut T` to `*mut T`, doing arithmetic, converting back — ubiquitous in unsafe code — could be flagged because intermediate raw-pointer accesses *popped* the original borrow. The stack conflates *invalidation by a genuinely conflicting borrow* with *invalidation by re-derivation of the same borrow*.

**Tree Borrows** [2] restructures metadata into a **tree**: each borrow is a node; reborrows become *children*. Instead of popping, each node carries a **permission** evolving through a state machine:

- `Reserved` — fresh mutable borrow, not yet written through;
- `Active` — mutable borrow holding exclusive access;
- `Frozen` — shared borrow (or observed through one);
- `Disabled` — invalidated, never usable again.

The innovation is distinguishing **child accesses** from **foreign accesses**. Access through a borrow *or its descendants* is a child access and always benign — writing through a raw pointer derived from `xm` never disables `xm`. Access through a *sibling* subtree is foreign, and a foreign *write* disables the affected nodes. Protectors, retagging, and `UnsafeCell` exemptions survive; invalidation granularity is now *per-subtree* rather than *everything above*.

```text
        Unique(0): x            <- original borrow
       /            \
  Unique(1): xm   SharedRW: raw <- children; foreign to each other
```

A write through `raw` is foreign to `xm` and disables it (using `xm` afterward is still UB — correctly), but a write through a *child* of `xm` leaves `xm` intact. This eliminates the largest class of Stacked Borrows false positives while, the authors argue, preserving every justified optimization — any reordering sound under the stack discipline stays sound when fewer programs are UB.

### 4.5 What the models license: optimization soundness

The POPL 2020 paper mechanizes proofs that Stacked Borrows validates the two transformations compiler engineers want:

1. **Moving reads across unknown code.** A read through `&T`, followed by unknown code, followed by another read, where the model guarantees no aliasing mutable write intervened — the second read may be eliminated. Classic `noalias` load forwarding, now formally licensed.
2. **Reordering around calls.** Protectors guarantee a callee cannot invalidate caller borrows via aliasing writes, so accesses around a call reorder on purely local alias information.

> **Theorem (Optimization validity, [1]):** *In the Stacked Borrows semantics, the two intraprocedural alias-based reorderings are behavior-preserving for all programs without undefined behavior.*

Tree Borrows aims to be a *drop-in strengthening* — accepting more programs, forbidding nothing the stack model licensed to optimizers — so Miri can eventually flip the default without invalidating accumulated ecosystem compliance.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

**Mechanization.** The metatheory is developed in Coq atop Iris separation logic, in the RustBelt tradition [4]. The formalization covers references, raw pointers, and calls; adequacy connects the operational monitor to the optimizer's view, showing every execution the optimizer assumes possible is one the model deems defined — machine-checked proof, the gold standard here.

**Standard library validation.** The empirical centerpiece: large parts of the std test suite under Miri with Stacked Borrows enforced yielded *six* violations in architecture-independent code — each investigated, confirmed as a genuine bug, and fixed. The model is *compatible* with the most demanding unsafe code ever written, yet *strict enough* to catch errors surviving years of review. Continued ecosystem runs keep surfacing aliasing bugs in widely used crates, establishing dynamic aliasing checking as a practical bug-finding tool.

**Tree Borrows evaluation.** Re-running the methodology [6] gave two headline findings: (i) every suite passing under Stacked Borrows still passes — practical backward compatibility; and (ii) previously-rejected patterns, including raw-pointer round-trips and `UnsafeCell`-adjacent idioms, are now accepted. Miri exposes it via `-Zmiri-tree-borrows`; the trajectory is to gather ecosystem evidence until it can become the default — a migration measured in years, given the stakes.

| Evidence | Stacked Borrows [1] | Tree Borrows [2] |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanized proofs | Coq: optimization validity | In progress; design argues preservation |
| Std test suite | 6 violations found & fixed | Previously-passing suites still pass |
| False positives | Raw-pointer round-trip cases | Largely eliminated by tree structure |
| Miri status | Default (opt out: `-Zmiri-disable-stacked-borrows`) | Experimental (`-Zmiri-tree-borrows`) |

## 6 Limitations

1. **Not yet the Reference.** Neither model is normative text [7]; both live in the guidelines' working documents [5], marked work-in-progress. `unsafe` authors write against a moving target, and model-justified optimizations rest on a social rather than documentary contract.
2. **Strictness debate unresolved.** Stacked Borrows rejects patterns — raw pointers stored in structs, self-referential round-trips — that experienced authors consider legitimate. Tree Borrows addresses the known cases, but each relaxation risks over-permissiveness: a model accepting everything justifies nothing.
3. **Interior mutability is delicate.** The `UnsafeCell` exemption is load-bearing for the ecosystem, yet its interaction with protectors, freezing, and two-phase borrows is among the most intricate parts of both models; subtle soundness questions in generic contexts persist.
4. **Function boundaries and foreign code.** Per-call retagging has real costs in Miri, and protector semantics for *foreign* (non-Rust) functions is necessarily approximate; whole-program C-ABI reasoning is out of scope.
5. **Oracle performance.** Miri with aliasing checks is orders of magnitude slower than native execution; continuous full-ecosystem validation is aspirational, and a faster static approximation of Tree Borrows checking is open engineering work.
6. **No formal link to the borrow checker.** Neither model is connected to the checker's implementation (or Polonius). Proving every statically-accepted program is dynamically accepted — checker soundness w.r.t. the operational semantics — remains future work, arguably the most important missing theorem in Rust's formal story.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Stacked Borrows and Tree Borrows are the most serious attempt yet to write down the *meaning* of Rust's aliasing rules — not as folklore or LLVM annotations, but as an executable operational semantics with machine-checked metatheory and ecosystem-scale validation. Stacked Borrows showed an aliasing model can be simultaneously *formal*, *implementable*, and *compatible with real code*; Tree Borrows shows the first such model need not be the last, and that precision about *which* access invalidates *which* borrow justifies redesigning the data structure.

The road ahead: converge on one model, bless it normatively, connect it to the static borrow checker, and make the oracle fast enough to guard the ecosystem continuously. Until then, every Rust programmer writing `unsafe` should do one thing: run their tests under Miri. The borrow stack — or the borrow tree — is watching.
