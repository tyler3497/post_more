# Liquid Time-Constant Networks and Neural Ordinary Differential Equations: Continuous-Time Dynamics, Adjoint Training, and Control

## Abstract

## 1. Introduction

A decade-long research program has sought to put time back into neural computation. Its pivotal moment came when Chen et al. observed that a residual block, $h_{t+1} = h_t + f(h_t, \theta_t)$, is precisely one Euler step of a continuous transformation [2]. Taking the limit of infinitesimal steps yields the **Neural ODE**:

$$\frac{d\mathbf{h}(t)}{dt} = f(\mathbf{h}(t), t, \theta),$$

whose output is computed by handing the learned vector field to a numerical ODE solver. This reframing buys three things at once: *adaptive computation*, *constant memory cost*, and a native treatment of irregularly sampled data [2, 4].

Yet vanilla Neural ODEs suffer from real weaknesses. Their dynamics are bounded only by the whims of an unconstrained neural vector field; they can be stiff, forcing adaptive solvers to crawl; and the solver itself — its tolerances, its step rejections, its function evaluations — becomes an unpredictable computational bottleneck. **Liquid Time-Constant Networks (LTCs)**, introduced by Hasani et al. [1], attack these weaknesses at their root: instead of declaring dynamics by arbitrary nonlinearities, they construct networks of *linear* first-order systems modulated by nonlinear gates, so that each neuron's effective time constant adapts to the input and hidden state. The result is provably stable, bounded behavior with strictly greater expressivity than standard Neural ODEs, and superior performance on time-series and control tasks.

The story did not stop there. Because LTCs are still trained through numerical solvers, the same authors derived **Closed-form Continuous-time (CfC)** neurons: a tightly bounded analytical approximation of the LTC integral solution that makes time appear explicitly, eliminating the solver and accelerating training and inference by *one to five orders of magnitude* [3].

This thesis presents a unified treatment of this lineage: the LTC dynamics and their stability, the adjoint method's memory efficiency, the CfC approximation and its error bounds, the practical machinery of stiffness and adaptive integration, and the assessment of these models on time-series modeling and robotic control.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Continuous-time recurrent neural networks

Funahashi and Nakamura proved that continuous-time recurrent networks of the form

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = -\frac{x(t)}{\tau} + f(x(t), I(t), t; \theta)$$

are universal approximators of finite-time trajectories of dynamical systems, establishing that first-order neural ODEs can, in principle, model any smooth dynamics [1]. Classical continuous-time RNNs, however, were notoriously hard to train: gradients had to flow through fixed-step integrators, and dynamics could easily diverge.

### 2.2 From residual networks to continuous depth

The bridge between discrete depth and continuous time was built through residual networks: stacking residual blocks is equivalent to Euler-discretizing a continuous transformation, with different architectures corresponding to different numerical schemes [2]. This suggested a radical simplification: *parameterize the derivative itself* and let a solver decide the discretization.

### 2.3 Neural ODEs and the adjoint method

Chen et al. [2] formalized this into Neural ODEs. Given an initial state $h(t_0)$, the output is

$$\mathbf{h}(t_1) = \mathbf{h}(t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t_1} f(\mathbf{h}(t), t, \theta)\, dt = \texttt{ODESolve}(\mathbf{h}(t_0), f, t_0, t_1).$$

The paper's second contribution was arguably the more important: training by the **adjoint sensitivity method**, which solves a second, augmented ODE *backward* in time to obtain gradients with **constant memory cost** [2]. We derive it fully in Section 4.3. The same machinery produced *continuous normalizing flows*, where the instantaneous change-of-variables formula $\frac{d}{dt}\log p(z(t)) = -\mathrm{tr}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right)$ replaces the expensive log-determinant of discrete flows.

### 2.4 Irregular time series: Latent ODEs and ODE-RNNs

Rubanova et al. [4] extended Neural ODEs to the irregularly sampled regime that dominates medicine, finance, and sensor networks. Their **Latent ODE** encodes a time series into a distribution over initial latent states and decodes by forward integration, allowing interpolation and extrapolation at arbitrary query times; **ODE-RNNs** interleave continuous evolution between observations with discrete RNN updates at observation times. Both outperform discrete RNNs on irregular data [4], a result later works have refined with improved batching strategies for the "combined-time" problem of mini-batching heterogeneous timestamps [6].

### 2.5 Liquid time-constant networks

Hasani et al. [1] observed that every model above declares its dynamics through *implicit nonlinearities* — a neural network directly outputs a state derivative with no structural guarantees. Their alternative: couple **linear first-order systems** through nonlinear gates. The LTC neuron obeys

$$\frac{dx(t)}{dt} = -\left[w_\tau + f(x(t), I(t), \theta)\right] x(t) + A\, f(x(t), I(t), \theta), \tag{1}$$

where $w_\tau$ is a positive time-constant parameter, $f$ is a neural network, and $A$ a bias vector. The bracketed term is the neuron's *effective leak rate*: because $f \geq 0$ (under standard activations), the **effective time constant** $\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}(t) = 1/(w_\tau + f(x(t), I(t), \theta))$ varies with state and input — it is *liquid*. Crucially, (1) is a linear ODE in $x$ given $f$, which yields boundedness, stability, and a closed-form integral solution — the properties exploited in Sections 4.1 and 4.2.

### 2.6 Closed-form continuous-time models

The price of LTCs' expressivity is the ODE solver: adaptive integration dominates wall-clock time. Hasani et al. [3] removed it. Noting that (1) admits an integral solution whose only intractable part is a specific integral, they derived a tightly bounded approximation valid for arbitrary inputs, producing **CfC neurons** that evaluate the state at time $t$ *directly*, with time as an explicit argument. The paper reports speedups of one to five orders of magnitude over ODE-based counterparts and superior time-series performance relative to advanced recurrent models [3].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The LTC dynamical system

We study networks of $D$ LTC cells with hidden state $\mathbf{x}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^D$ and exogenous input $\mathbf{I}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^m$:

$$\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = -\left[\mathbf{w}_\tau + f(\mathbf{x}(t), \mathbf{I}(t), \theta)\right] \odot \mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{A} \odot f(\mathbf{x}(t), \mathbf{I}(t), \theta), \tag{2}$$

with $\odot$ the Hadamard product. Because (2) is linear in $\mathbf{x}$ for fixed $f$, its integral solution is

$$\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{x}(0)\, e^{-\int_0^t [\mathbf{w}_\tau + f(s)]\,ds} + \int_0^t e^{-\int_s^t [\mathbf{w}_\tau + f(u)]\,du}\, \mathbf{A} \odot f(s)\, ds. \tag{3}$$

The exponential kernel is the key: it is always decaying (since $\mathbf{w}_\tau + f \geq 0$), which is why LTC states cannot blow up.

### 3.2 The CfC closed form

Equation (3) has no elementary closed form because $f$ is a neural network of the state. The CfC derivation [3] approximates the intractable integral for piecewise-constant inputs, then generalizes to arbitrary inputs with a provable bound, yielding the CfC update:

$$\mathbf{x}(t) \approx \sigma\!\left(-f(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{I}; \theta_f)\, t\right) \odot g(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{I}; \theta_g) + \left[1 - \sigma\!\left(-f(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{I}; \theta_f)\, t\right)\right] \odot h(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{I}; \theta_h), \tag{4}$$

where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid and $f, g, h$ are neural networks sharing a backbone. Time $t$ appears *explicitly*: no solver, no step-size control, no function-evaluation counting. The interpolation between $g$ and $h$ is governed by a time-decaying gate, mirroring the exponential kernel of (3).

### 3.3 Training via the adjoint sensitivity method

For a loss $L(\mathbf{h}(t_1))$, define the **adjoint state** $\mathbf{a}(t) = \partial L / \partial \mathbf{h}(t)$. Differentiating through the ODE solution gives the adjoint ODE:

$$\frac{d\mathbf{a}(t)}{dt} = -\mathbf{a}(t)^\top \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{h}}, \qquad \mathbf{a}(t_1) = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{h}(t_1)}, \tag{5}$$

and the parameter gradients follow from a single backward integral:

$$\frac{dL}{d\theta} = -\int_{t_1}^{t_0} \mathbf{a}(t)^\top \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta}\, dt. \tag{6}$$

All three quantities — $\mathbf{h}(t)$, $\mathbf{a}(t)$, and $dL/d\theta$ — are obtained by one call to the solver on an *augmented* state, in reverse time, with **O(1) memory** [2]. The canonical implementation pattern, as popularized by the `torchdiffeq` library, is:

```python
import torch
from torchdiffeq import odeint_adjoint as odeint

class ODEFunc(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, hidden_dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = torch.nn.Sequential(
            torch.nn.Linear(hidden_dim + 1, 64),  # +1 for time t
            torch.nn.Tanh(),
            torch.nn.Linear(64, hidden_dim),
        )
    def forward(self, t, h):
        tt = torch.ones(h.shape[0], 1, device=h.device) * t
        return self.net(torch.cat([h, tt], dim=1))

func = ODEFunc(hidden_dim=32)
h0 = torch.randn(128, 32)                       # batch of initial states
t = torch.tensor([0.0, 1.0])                    # integrate over [0, 1]
hT = odeint(func, h0, t, method="dopri5",
            rtol=1e-3, atol=1e-4)[-1]            # adjoint-backed, O(1) memory
loss = hT.pow(2).mean()
loss.backward()                                 # triggers the backward adjoint ODE
```

The `method="dopri5"` argument selects the Dormand–Prince 5(4) adaptive Runge–Kutta scheme; `rtol`/`atol` control the local error tolerance and hence the computational budget.

### 3.4 Solvers, tolerances, and stiffness

An ODE system is **stiff** when its dynamics contain widely separated timescales — e.g., a fast transient riding on a slow drift. Explicit solvers (Euler, classical RK4, Dormand–Prince) must then take steps dictated by the *fastest* timescale to remain stable, even when the solution itself is smooth, causing the number of function evaluations (NFE) to explode. Implicit solvers (backward Euler, Radau, Rosenbrock) remain stable at large steps but pay per step with a nonlinear solve. Adaptive controllers estimate local error (e.g., via embedded error estimates in dopri5) and shrink or grow the step to hold it within tolerance; the NFE thus becomes a *data- and parameter-dependent* random variable that typically grows during training as the learned vector field sharpens [2].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The LTC neuron: liquid time constants and provable boundedness

The defining move of (2) is to make the leak rate *a function of the computation itself*. In a classical CTRNN the time constant $\tau$ is fixed; in an LTC the effective constant $\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}(t) = 1/(w_\tau + f(x, I, \theta))$ shortens when the gate $f$ is large (fast tracking of volatile inputs) and lengthens when $f$ is small (long memory of the past).

> **Theorem (Boundedness of LTC dynamics, Hasani et al. [1]).** *If the activation functions in $f$ are bounded and Lipschitz, and the bias $A$ is finite, then the solution $\mathbf{x}(t)$ of (2) remains bounded for all $t \geq 0$ and all bounded inputs $\mathbf{I}(t)$. Moreover, the dynamics are input-to-state stable: bounded inputs produce bounded states with a gain independent of the horizon.*

The proof exploits the exponential kernel of (3): the homogeneous part decays at rate at least $w_\tau > 0$, and the forced part is a convolution of a bounded nonlinearity with that decaying kernel. No such guarantee holds for a generic Neural ODE, whose vector field can (and in practice sometimes does) produce finite-time blowup unless carefully regularized.

Expressivity is measured not by parameter count but by **trajectory length** — the expected arc length of the hidden trajectory, $\mathbb{E}\int \|\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\| dt$, which quantifies how richly the state space is folded. Hasani et al. prove that LTCs achieve strictly greater trajectory length than both Neural ODEs and continuous-time RNNs with the same parameter budget [1]: the liquid time constant acts as a *multiplicative* modulation of the dynamics, and multiplicative interactions are known to expand the effective function class super-linearly.

### 4.2 Closed-form continuous-time neurons: bypassing the solver

The CfC derivation begins from the integral form (3) and isolates the single intractable term: the integral of the kernel against $f(s)$ when $f$ itself depends on the evolving state. For *piecewise-constant* inputs the integral admits an exact closed form; the paper's main theorem shows that replacing the true integral by this form incurs an error that is *tightly bounded* — the bound sharpens as the input's variation over the integration window decreases [3].

The practical consequence is equation (4): a neuron that *looks* like a gated recurrent cell but *is* a continuous-time model, with physical time $t$ as an explicit input. Because no solver runs, the model is:

1. **Fast** — one to five orders of magnitude faster in training and inference than ODE-based counterparts [3];
2. **Deterministic in cost** — compute is fixed per evaluation, which matters enormously for real-time control where adaptive solvers' variable NFE is unacceptable;
3. **Interpretable in time** — the sigmoidal time-gate $\sigma(-f t)$ directly displays how quickly each neuron forgets its initialization and converges to its driven value.

### 4.3 The adjoint sensitivity method: constant-memory training

Backpropagating through an adaptive solver's internal operations is both memory-hungry (every accepted step must be stored) and implementation-hostile (step-size control is non-differentiable branching). The adjoint method sidesteps both. Its derivation is an application of Lagrange multipliers to the ODE constraint $\dot{\mathbf{h}} - f = 0$; the multiplier $\mathbf{a}(t)$ satisfies (5), and the gradient identity (6) follows from integration by parts.

> **Theorem (Adjoint gradient identity, Chen et al. [2]).** *Let $\mathbf{h}(t)$ solve $\dot{\mathbf{h}} = f(\mathbf{h}, t, \theta)$ with $\mathbf{h}(t_0)$ given, and let $L$ depend on $\mathbf{h}(t_1)$. Then $dL/d\theta$ is given by (6), where $\mathbf{a}(t)$ solves (5) backward from $\mathbf{a}(t_1) = \partial L / \partial \mathbf{h}(t_1)$. The memory cost is O(1) in the number of solver steps.*

Two subtleties deserve emphasis. First, the backward pass must *reconstruct* $\mathbf{h}(t)$ by integrating the state ODE in reverse; numerical error in this reconstruction pollutes the gradients, and the "optimize-then-discretize" adjoint can disagree with "discretize-then-optimize" backprop through the solver. Second, the adjoint ODE is itself often *stiffer* than the forward ODE, since it involves the Jacobian $\partial f / \partial \mathbf{h}$; training can therefore be slower than inference even when forward NFE is modest.

### 4.4 Stiffness, adaptive solvers, and the NFE problem

The number of function evaluations is the true currency of Neural ODE training. Chen et al. documented that NFE *increases during training*: as the vector field becomes more complex to fit the data, the adaptive controller takes smaller steps [2].

The community has developed a toolbox:

- **Regularizing the dynamics**: penalizing higher-order derivatives or kinetic energy encourages smooth, non-stiff vector fields and directly reduces NFE.
- **Fixed-step training, adaptive inference** (or vice versa): cheap schemes during training, with validation under the adaptive solver.
- **Architectural stiffness relief**: LTCs and CfCs are relevant here — their dynamics are *structurally* bounded and non-explosive, so the learned vector fields tend to be milder than those of unconstrained Neural ODEs, and CfCs eliminate the solver entirely.

The lesson is that in continuous-time deep learning, the *numerical analyst's* concerns (stability regions, error control, stiffness detection) are first-class modeling concerns, not implementation details.

### 4.5 Applications in robotics and autonomous control

Continuous-time models are a natural fit for embodied systems. A robot's sensors (cameras at 30 Hz, IMUs at 200 Hz, lidars at 10 Hz) produce *irregularly sampled, multi-rate* streams — precisely the regime where discrete RNNs need imputation hacks and Neural ODEs shine [4]. LTC-based policies have demonstrated strong performance on continuous-control benchmarks such as MuJoCo locomotion, where the liquid time constant lets a compact network simultaneously track fast contact transients and slow gait cycles [1].

Three properties make the LTC/CfC lineage especially attractive for autonomy:

1. **Causality and online adaptation.** Because time is explicit, these models handle streaming data without re-binning, and CfCs' fixed compute budget makes worst-case latency certifiable — a prerequisite for deployment on vehicles and drones.
2. **Compactness.** Liquid networks achieve competitive control performance with orders of magnitude fewer neurons than large recurrent or Transformer policies, fitting embedded compute budgets [3].
3. **Physical interpretability.** Time constants in an LTC can be read as the timescales the controller *believes* the world operates on, offering a hook for verification that black-box sequence models lack.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: the adjoint identity

*Proof.* Augment the loss with the ODE constraint via a multiplier $\mathbf{a}(t)$: $\mathcal{L} = L(\mathbf{h}(t_1)) - \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \mathbf{a}(t)^\top (\dot{\mathbf{h}} - f)\, dt$. Varying $\mathbf{h}$ and integrating by parts,

$$\delta \mathcal{L} = \left[\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{h}(t_1)} - \mathbf{a}(t_1)\right]^\top \delta \mathbf{h}(t_1) + \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \left[\dot{\mathbf{a}} + \mathbf{a}^\top \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{h}}\right]^\top \delta \mathbf{h}\, dt - \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \mathbf{a}^\top \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta}\, dt\, \delta\theta.$$

Choosing $\mathbf{a}(t)$ to annihilate the first two terms yields exactly (5) with its terminal condition, leaving $dL/d\theta$ as (6). ∎

### 5.2 Benchmark landscape

The following table summarizes the qualitative standing of the model families discussed, as reported across the cited literature [1, 2, 3, 4]:

| Property | Discrete RNN / LSTM | Transformer | Neural ODE | LTC | CfC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Native irregular sampling | No (needs imputation) | No (needs encodings) | **Yes** | **Yes** | **Yes** |
| Memory cost of training | O(sequence length) | O(sequence length²) | **O(1)** (adjoint) | O(1) (adjoint) | O(1) (direct) |
| Adaptive compute per input | No | No | **Yes** (NFE varies) | **Yes** | No (fixed) |
| Provable state boundedness | No | N/A | No | **Yes** | **Yes** |
| Solver-free evaluation | Yes | Yes | No | No | **Yes** |
| Long-horizon stability | Degrades (gradients) | Strong (attention) | Mixed (stiffness) | **Strong** | **Strong** |

On time-series benchmarks — human activity recognition, traffic and power forecasting, sequential MNIST, and MuJoCo half-cheetah control — the CfC study reports accuracy matching or exceeding LTCs and advanced recurrent baselines, while accelerating training and inference by **one to five orders of magnitude** over ODE-based counterparts [1, 3]. Latent ODE variants remain the reference approach for sparse, irregularly sampled medical and physical time series [4, 5].

> **Theorem (CfC approximation bound, Hasani et al. [3]).** *The closed-form expression (4) approximates the true LTC integral solution (3) with an error that is tightly bounded; the bound vanishes as the input signal's variation over the integration interval tends to zero, and the approximation is exact for piecewise-constant inputs.*

### 5.3 A worked micro-experiment: the damped oscillator

To ground the theory, consider learning $\ddot{x} = -\omega^2 x - \gamma \dot{x}$ from 100 irregularly sampled noisy observations on $t \in [0, 10]$. A discrete GRU fed $(\Delta t_k, x_k)$ pairs cannot query $x(3.7)$ between samples; a Neural ODE or LTC integrates the learned vector field and answers any query time directly. In published replications of this canonical setup, ODE-based models recover the frequency $\omega$ to within a few percent using roughly half the parameters of matched discrete baselines [2], and LTC variants additionally avoid the slow amplitude drift of unconstrained Neural ODEs thanks to their boundedness [1].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Stiffness remains the central practical enemy.** Nothing in the LTC or CfC construction *guarantees* a non-stiff learned vector field; adversarial data or poor initialization can still produce dynamics that make adaptive solvers crawl. CfCs dodge the solver but can still learn effectively stiff input-output maps that are hard to optimize.
2. **Unpredictable latency for adaptive models.** In closed-loop control at kilohertz rates, a solver that occasionally takes 10× more steps is a safety hazard — a genuine argument for CfCs' fixed compute in real-time settings.
3. **Representational constraints.** Neural ODE flows are homeomorphisms: trajectories cannot cross, which limits the functions they can represent without augmentation. LTCs inherit related constraints from their linear-in-state structure; multiplicative gating expands expressivity but does not remove all topological restrictions.
4. **Weakness on static, non-temporal data.** The original LTC study is explicit that these models are *not* designed for static datasets; on i.i.d. classification tasks, Transformers and CNNs dominate, and continuous-time machinery adds cost without benefit [1].
5. **Verification gap.** Boundedness theorems are not safety certificates. For autonomous deployment, the field still lacks scalable verification of learned continuous-time policies against temporal-logic specifications.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The arc from Neural ODEs [2] through Liquid Time-Constant Networks [1] to Closed-form Continuous-time models [3] is the story of a field learning to take *time* seriously and then learning to take the *solver* seriously. Neural ODEs showed that depth could be continuous and memory constant; LTCs showed that structure — linear systems with liquid time constants — buys stability and expressivity that arbitrary vector fields cannot; CfCs showed that the solver bottleneck, long assumed to be the price of continuity, can be approximated away with tight bounds and dramatic speedups.

For time-series modeling of physical and physiological processes, and for robotic control over irregular multi-rate sensors, the continuous-time paradigm is now a mature alternative to discrete recurrence and attention — with provable boundedness, native irregular sampling, and, in the CfC form, fixed, certifiable compute.
