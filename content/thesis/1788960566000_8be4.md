# The eBPF Verifier: Abstract Interpretation of In-Kernel Programs, Symbolic Register-State Exploration, and Provable Safety Guarantees

## Abstract

The eBPF verifier is the static safety gate of the Linux kernel: every untrusted eBPF program must pass its scrutiny before executing with kernel privileges. This thesis presents a rigorous treatment of the verifier as an **abstract interpreter** operating over the control-flow graph of an eBPF program. We formalize the verifier's abstract domain as a product of four interval lattices (unsigned/signed, 32/64-bit) and the *tristate number* (tnum) bitwise domain, and describe its depth-first symbolic exploration of register states, stack frames, and pointer types, pruned by visited-state subsumption. We derive the soundness argument for memory-access checking, reference-lifetime enforcement, and bounded-loop acceptance, and we examine the pipeline's engineering phases: CFG construction, acyclicity checking, register-state exploration, helper-call contracts, and speculative-execution mitigation. Finally, we survey the formal-verification literature around the verifier — the Agni SMT framework, tristate-number theory, Prevail's zone-based re-implementation, and JIT correctness work — and we quantify the verifier's empirical performance, precision, and known unsoundness incidents. The verifier emerges as one of the most ambitious applications of abstract interpretation ever deployed in production systems code.

## 1 Introduction

Extended Berkeley Packet Filter (eBPF) allows unprivileged user space to inject programs into the Linux kernel, where they execute in networking, tracing, and security contexts with full kernel privilege [3]. The safety of this arrangement rests on a single component: the **eBPF verifier**, a static analyzer that either accepts a program (declaring it provably safe) or rejects it before a single instruction executes. No hardware sandbox, no runtime monitor can substitute: the verifier's judgment is the *only* barrier between attacker-controlled bytecode and the kernel [4].

The verifier's task is formidable. It must prove three properties for every program it accepts:

1. **Termination** — the program cannot loop forever or exhaust kernel CPU.
2. **Memory safety** — every load and store addresses memory the program legitimately owns, within bounds, at the moment of access.
3. **Type safety** — pointers are never forged, confused with scalars, or dereferenced outside their sanctioned regions; resources acquired (map references, socket references) are released on all paths.

These properties are undecidable in general. The verifier sidesteps undecidability through *abstraction*: instead of executing the program, it executes an abstract interpretation of it, tracking for each register and stack slot not concrete values but **sets of possible values** — intervals, bit-level masks, and pointer types — that conservatively over-approximate all concrete executions [1][5]. If the abstract execution can find no path to an unsafe operation, the concrete program is safe; the verifier accepts *more* programs than are safe only in the harmless direction of false rejection, never in the catastrophic direction of false acceptance. At least, that is the intended soundness invariant — and as recent research shows, maintaining it across a fast-moving codebase is one of the great open problems of systems verification [2][4].

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews abstract interpretation and eBPF program structure. Section 3 describes the verifier's architecture and its abstract domain. Section 4 deep-dives into the four pillars: the tnum bitwise domain, register-state exploration with path pruning, memory-access checking, and control-flow/structuring rules including bounded loops and speculative-execution hardening. Section 5 covers formal verification efforts and empirical measurements. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Abstract interpretation

*Abstract interpretation*, introduced by Cousot and Cousot, is a framework for sound static analysis: concrete program semantics are replaced by an abstract semantics over a lattice of *abstract values*, related to concrete values by a Galois connection $(\alpha, \gamma)$ between the concrete domain $C$ and abstract domain $A$ [1]. Each concrete operation $f : C \to C$ is replaced by an abstract operator $f^\sharp : A \to A$ satisfying the **soundness condition**

> **Theorem:** $f^\sharp$ is sound iff for all abstract values $a$, $\{ f(c) \mid c \in \gamma(a) \} \subseteq \gamma(f^\sharp(a))$.

That is, the abstract operator must cover every possible concrete outcome. Soundness is a one-directional requirement: $\gamma(f^\sharp(a))$ may be a strict superset of the true outcomes (imprecision), but it must never miss one (unsoundness). This asymmetry is the load-bearing wall of the entire eBPF safety story: imprecision causes rejected-but-safe programs (developer annoyance); unsoundness causes accepted-but-unsafe programs (kernel exploits) [2].

### 2.2 The eBPF machine

An eBPF program is a sequence of fixed-width 64-bit instructions operating on **11 registers** (`R0`–`R10`), where `R10` is a read-only frame pointer into a **512-byte stack**, plus helper-function calls and map accesses [7]. Key structural facts the verifier exploits:

- **Fixed 8-byte encoding and no indirect jumps.** Decoding is total and unambiguous; jump targets are static immediates, so the CFG is computable at load time [3].
- **Bounded program size.** Unprivileged programs are capped at 4,096 instructions; privileged loaders may reach up to one million processed instructions under the complexity limit [3][7].
- **Typed memory regions.** The stack, packet buffers, map values, and context structures are distinct regions; pointer arithmetic across regions is forbidden.

### 2.3 Why static verification instead of sandboxing

Sandboxing (e.g., SFI, WebAssembly-style bounds checks on every access) would impose per-access runtime overhead in the hottest paths of the networking stack. The eBPF design philosophy is the opposite: **verify once, run at native speed**. The verifier's static proof licenses the JIT compiler to emit code with *no* runtime checks on proven-safe accesses — the check has already been done, off the critical path [3].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The verifier as a worklist abstract interpreter

The Linux verifier's algorithm can be stated as an abstract interpretation over the program CFG [2][7]:

1. **CFG construction and acyclicity check** (`check_cfg`). The verifier builds the control-flow graph, verifies every jump target is a valid instruction boundary, and requires the graph to be a DAG — except for *bounded loops*, whose iteration counts are statically bounded (kernel 5.3+, via `check_loop_count`) [3].
2. **Depth-first symbolic exploration** (`do_check`). Starting from entry with all registers in a known initial abstract state (`R1` holding a context pointer for most program types, `R10` the frame pointer, all others "not initialized"), the verifier simulates each instruction's abstract transfer function, forking the abstract state at conditional branches.
3. **Path pruning by subsumption** (`is_state_visited`). When exploration reaches an instruction with a previously recorded state, it compares the current abstract state against the recorded one: if the current state's every register/stack slot is *contained in* (no more general than) the recorded state, the path is pruned — all its futures have already been explored [5][7].
4. **Safety checks at every instruction.** Each instruction's transfer function includes assertions: memory accesses must be provably in-bounds, pointer types must match the operation, helper calls must satisfy argument contracts, and acquired references must be released before exit.

### 3.2 The abstract register state

For each register and stack slot the verifier maintains a `struct bpf_reg_state` comprising [7][8]:

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `type` | Pointer flavor (`SCALAR_VALUE`, `PTR_TO_MAP_VALUE`, `PTR_TO_STACK`, `PTR_TO_CTX`, `PTR_TO_SOCKET`, …) or scalar |
| `off` | Known constant offset added to a pointer |
| `var_off` | A **tnum** capturing the variable (unknown) part of the value |
| `smin_value`, `smax_value` | Signed 64-bit interval bounds |
| `umin_value`, `umax_value` | Unsigned 64-bit interval bounds |
| `s32_min_value`, `s32_max_value`, `u32_min_value`, `u32_max_value` | 32-bit interval bounds |
| `id`, `ref_obj_id` | Identity for pointer-alias and reference tracking |
| `live` | Liveness/precision tracking for pruning |

The four interval domains and the tnum domain form a **reduced product**: after each transfer function, the domains *learn from one another* — e.g., a refined tnum can tighten interval bounds and vice versa [1][5].

### 3.3 Soundness strategy

The verifier's global soundness argument decomposes per instruction: each transfer function is required to be a sound abstract operator, and the exploration must visit every reachable concrete path (modulo sound pruning) [2]. This decomposition is exactly what makes the verifier amenable to *modular* formal verification — and also what makes it fragile, because a single unsound operator in any of hundreds of transfer functions breaks the whole guarantee [2][4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The tnum domain: sound, precise, fast bit-level abstraction

The tristate number (*tnum*) domain represents a set of 64-bit values as a pair `(value, mask)`: bit $k$ is **known** if `mask[k] = 0` (its value given by `value[k]`) and **unknown** ($\mu$) if `mask[k] = 1` [1][8]. Concretely,

$$\gamma(\langle v, m \rangle) = \{\, x \in \mathbb{Z}_{64} \mid x \;\&\; \lnot m \;=\; v \;\&\; \lnot m \,\}.$$

Every bit is in one of three states $\{0, 1, \mu\}$, hence "tristate." The domain supports abstract operators for all ALU operations — addition, subtraction, multiplication, bitwise AND/OR/XOR, shifts — each required to satisfy the soundness condition of Section 2.1. The canonical example is abstract addition [1][8]:

```c
struct tnum tnum_add(struct tnum a, struct tnum b)
{
    u64 sm, sv, sigma, chi, mu;

    sm    = a.mask + b.mask;
    sv    = a.value + b.value;
    sigma = sm + sv;
    chi   = sigma ^ sv;
    mu    = chi | a.mask | b.mask;
    return TNUM(sv & ~mu, mu);
}
```

The ingenuity is that this $O(1)$ bit-trick computes a sound over-approximation of $\{ p+q \mid p \in \gamma(a), q \in \gamma(b) \}$ without enumerating the up to $2^{64}$ concrete pairs [1]. The proof (Vishwanathan et al.) proceeds by showing that carries can only flow *into* bit positions where `mu` is set — the mask `mu` exactly captures carry uncertainty. This paper's soundness and optimality proofs have been partially adopted into the kernel itself [3].

> **Theorem:** For all tnums $a, b$: $\{ p + q \mid p \in \gamma(a), q \in \gamma(b) \} \subseteq \gamma(\text{tnum\_add}(a, b))$. The operator is additionally *optimal*: no strictly smaller sound tnum result exists for addition [1].

The tnum domain is what makes the verifier precise on **bitwise masking idioms** ubiquitous in networking code, e.g., `r1 &= 0xff; r2 = map[r1]` — the interval domain alone would conclude $r1 \in [0, 2^{64})$ after the mask (losing the bound), while tnum knows the top 56 bits are zero, and the *reduced product* lets the interval domain learn $r1 \in [0, 255]$ from the tnum [1][5].

### 4.2 Symbolic exploration and path pruning

The exploration engine is a classic explicit-state model checker over abstract states [5][7]:

1. **State forking.** At a conditional jump `if r1 > 10 goto +5`, the verifier forks: on the taken branch it *refines* `r1`'s abstract value with the constraint $r1 > 10$ (intersection of the abstract value with the condition), and on the fall-through branch with $r1 \le 10$. This is abstract interpretation of the branch condition — precision gained from control flow.
2. **Visited-state subsumption.** `is_state_visited` maintains, per instruction, the list of abstract states already explored there. A new state $s$ is pruned iff there exists a recorded state $s'$ with $s \sqsubseteq s'$ componentwise (every register's abstract value in $s$ is contained in $s'$'s) [7]. Because abstract values only shrink along a path (constraints accumulate), and the lattice has finite height for the domains used, exploration terminates.
3. **Precision tracking.** Since kernel 5.5, registers carry *precision marks*: only registers that feed into memory-access bounds or other safety-critical computations require exact bound tracking on revisit. Imprecise registers may be soundly approximated, dramatically cutting state-space explosion [7]. When a pruned path later needs more precision, `backtrack_insn` walks the recorded instruction history to refine the relevant registers.

This design is deliberately **path-sensitive but merge-free at joins**: unlike a classical dataflow analysis that joins abstract states at merge points (losing path-specific facts), the verifier explores paths independently and prunes by subsumption. The cost is exponential worst-case path counts — bounded in practice by the instruction limits and by aggressive pruning — and the benefit is the precision needed to accept real-world programs without drowning developers in false rejections [3][7].

> **Theorem (exploration soundness, informal):** If every transfer function is sound and pruning only discards states subsumed by previously explored states, then the set of explored abstract paths covers all concrete executions. Any safety property proved over all explored paths holds for all concrete executions [2][5].

### 4.3 Memory-access checking: the heart of the guarantee

Every load/store passes through `check_mem_access`, which enforces [7][8]:

- **Region legitimacy.** The pointer's `type` must permit the access: `PTR_TO_STACK` accesses must land within the 512-byte stack and within the *initialized* portion (tracked per stack slot); `PTR_TO_MAP_VALUE` accesses must satisfy `off + size ≤ map_value_size`; `PTR_TO_CTX` accesses are dispatched to per-program-type validators (`is_valid_access`).
- **No pointer leaks.** Stores of pointer-typed values into maps or visible memory are rejected where they would leak kernel addresses to unprivileged observers.
- **Fixed vs. variable offsets.** If the offset is a known constant (`off` fixed, `var_off` constant), the check is a simple arithmetic comparison. If the offset is variable (a range), the verifier proves `umin + off + size ≤ region_size`; failing that, it attempts **bounds narrowing via `sanitize_ptr_alu`**: rewriting the program to insert a runtime ALU mask (`off &= (size-1)` for power-of-two sizes) that the abstract interpreter can then prove safe [7]. This is the verifier *cooperating with code generation* to make programs provable.
- **Packet access idiom.** For `PTR_TO_PACKET`, the verifier understands the canonical `data`/`data_end` comparison: after `if (data + 14 > data_end) goto drop`, the fall-through state carries the refined bound, making subsequent accesses within 14 bytes provably safe [7].

A subtle and critical rule: **scalar-to-pointer confusion is impossible by construction** because the `type` field is tracked flow-sensitively and every arithmetic operation has a typed transfer function — adding two pointers is rejected; adding a scalar to a pointer yields a pointer with adjusted `off`/`var_off` and recomputed bounds [8].

### 4.4 Control flow, loops, references, and speculative safety

- **Bounded loops.** Since kernel 5.3, back edges are permitted if `check_loop_count` can prove a static bound on total iterations, using the same interval/tnum machinery (the loop counter must be a scalar with provable bounds and a provable exit) [3][7].
- **Subprograms and tail calls.** Function calls are verified with abstract argument states; the 512-byte stack is per-frame; tail calls are bounded (max 33 chained) to preserve termination [3].
- **Reference lifetimes.** Helpers like `bpf_map_lookup_elem` return references that must be released (`bpf_map_release` / `bpf_sk_release`) on *every* path to exit. The verifier tracks `ref_obj_id` flow-sensitively and rejects programs that leak references — a resource-safety property beyond memory safety [7].
- **Helper contracts.** `check_helper_call` validates each helper's argument types against a per-helper prototype table (`check_map_func_compat`), including pointer-versus-scalar, size arguments, and callback signatures [7].
- **Speculative-execution hardening.** Since the Spectre disclosures, the verifier also reasons about *transient* execution: `sanitize_ptr_alu` inserts speculation barriers (`nospec`) and masking sequences so that mispredicted paths cannot transiently read out-of-bounds memory [3]. Verification therefore proves safety under both the architectural and speculative semantics.

## 5 Empirical Results / Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 What has been formally proven

The verifier's scale (~30k lines of intricate C in `kernel/bpf/verifier.c`) long resisted formal treatment, but a research program has made remarkable progress [3][4]:

1. **Tristate numbers (Vishwanathan et al.).** Full soundness and *optimality* proofs for the tnum abstract operators, mechanized reasoning adopted in part by the kernel [1][3]. This is the gold standard: a *proved-optimal* abstract domain in production.
2. **Agni (CAV 2023; Shachnai et al.).** An automated pipeline that extracts the verifier's C range-analysis functions, compiles them to LLVM IR, translates to SMT, and checks soundness conditions with Z3 — verifying the *actual shipped code*, not a model of it [3][5]. Agni's CI re-verifies operators across kernel versions and synthesizes proof-of-concept counterexamples for violations.
3. **Latent unsoundness discovery (SAS 2024).** Applying Agni's specification discipline uncovered previously unknown *unsound* abstract operators in the upstream verifier — real bugs where the abstract operator missed concrete outcomes — with fixes merged into the kernel [2]. This is the nightmare scenario of Section 2.1 made concrete, and the strongest evidence that the soundness decomposition is both the verifier's strength and its attack surface.
4. **Precision via differential synthesis (SAS 2025).** A follow-up study compares the precision of abstract operators across implementations using differential synthesis, giving the first quantitative account of *how much* safety the verifier's precision choices buy versus their complexity cost [2].
5. **Prevail (Gershuni et al.).** A from-scratch re-implementation replacing intervals with the *zone* domain (difference-bound matrices, $x_i - x_j \le c$), proving that a coarser-but-relational abstraction eliminates most false positives on 111 real-world benchmarks while remaining fast enough for load-time use [3].
6. **JIT verification (Nelson et al., OSDI 2020).** The *other* half of the trusted base — the JIT compilers that translate verified bytecode to native code — verified via the Jitterbug/Serval synthesis frameworks, closing the gap between "verified bytecode" and "executed machine code" [3].
7. **Independent audit (Linux Foundation, 2021).** A systematic code review cataloguing verifier internals and attack surface, motivating the formal-verification roadmap [4].
8. **Runtime fuzzing (BRF).** Coverage-guided fuzzing of the eBPF *runtime* (interpreter, JIT, helpers, maps) found multiple CVEs, demonstrating that verification of the static gate must be complemented by testing of the dynamic machinery [6].

### 5.2 Empirical performance

In production, verification typically completes in **milliseconds** for real programs (hundreds of instructions), scaling to seconds only for adversarial near-limit programs; the complexity limit (one million processed instructions for privileged loaders) bounds worst-case load-time CPU [3][7]. Precision is high in practice: the interval–tnum reduced product plus bounds-narrowing rewrites accepts the idioms real systems programs (Cilium, Falco, bcc) actually use [1][3].

### 5.3 The provable guarantee, stated carefully

What the verifier *provably* establishes — modulo the soundness of each transfer function and the speculative-execution model — is:

> **Theorem (verifier safety):** Any eBPF program accepted by the verifier terminates within the complexity bound, performs only memory accesses within provably owned regions, never confuses pointers with scalars, and releases all acquired references on every path to exit [2][3][7].

The "modulo" clause is doing heavy lifting: it is precisely the clause that Agni, the SAS-lineage papers, and the audit literature exist to shrink [2][4][5].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Soundness is empirical, not end-to-end proven.** No complete formal proof connects the C implementation to the safety theorem; the trusted base includes every transfer function, the pruning logic, and the JIT. History (SAS 2024's unsound operators, BRF's CVEs) shows this trust has been misplaced before [2][6].
2. **Incompleteness is a feature with costs.** False rejections force developers to contort code ("verifier gymnastics") — redundant checks, manual unrolling, avoiding 64-bit arithmetic patterns the domains handle poorly. Prevail's results suggest significant headroom remains [3].
3. **The speculative semantics is a moving target.** Transient-execution mitigations model a *particular* microarchitectural threat model; new speculation primitives can obsolete the model faster than the verifier adapts [3].
4. **State explosion is bounded by policy, not by theory.** The complexity limit is a pragmatic circuit breaker; a program can be *safe* yet unverifiable within budget, and the bound's adequacy rests on operational experience rather than proof [7].
5. **Composition is unverified.** Independently verified programs compose through maps and tail calls in ways the verifier reasons about only locally; cross-program invariants (e.g., map-value type discipline across writers) are enforced by convention, not by proof [3][6].

## 7 Conclusion

The eBPF verifier is abstract interpretation at planetary scale: a lattice-theoretic static analyzer standing between untrusted code and the kernel on millions of machines. Its design — a reduced product of interval and tristate-number domains, path-sensitive symbolic exploration with subsumption pruning, cooperative bounds-narrowing rewrites, and speculative-execution modeling — represents the most successful deployment of program-verification theory in systems software to date [1][3][7]. Yet the SAS-2024 discovery of latent unsound operators is a permanent caution: *every operator is load-bearing*, and the durable defense is continuous, automated verification of the verifier itself [2][5]. The trajectory from hand-audited C to SMT-checked operators to proved-optimal domains points toward the field's endgame: a verifier whose safety theorem is not merely asserted but *mechanically checked*, operator by operator, on every kernel release. Until then, the verifier remains what it has always been — the most heavily scrutinized, most formally interesting, and most consequential abstract interpreter ever shipped.
