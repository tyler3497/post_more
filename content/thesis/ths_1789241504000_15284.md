# Optimal Transport: From the Monge–Kantorovich Formulation to Wasserstein Gradient Flows and Entropic Computation

## Abstract

Optimal transport studies the cheapest way to rearrange one probability distribution into another, and in doing so it equips the space of measures with a Riemannian-like geometry that has reshaped analysis, probability, and machine learning. This thesis develops the Monge–Kantorovich formulation from first principles: Kantorovich's convex relaxation of Monge's transport map problem, the duality theory with c-concave potentials, and Brenier's polar-factorization theorem for quadratic costs. We then examine the geometry of the Wasserstein space P₂, the Benamou–Brenier dynamic formulation, and the Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto scheme that interprets the Fokker–Planck equation as a gradient flow of entropy. The second half turns computational: entropic regularization converts the linear program into a strictly convex problem solved in near-linear time by Sinkhorn matrix scaling, with complexity bounds, sample-complexity analysis, and a worked numerical experiment. Applications to generative modeling, domain adaptation, and sliced approximations complete the picture, together with open problems in scalability, statistical rates, and unbalanced transport.

## 1 Introduction

Few mathematical objects have traveled as far, and touched as many disciplines, as the problem of *optimal transport*. Born in 1781 with Gaspard Monge's mémoire on moving soil from excavations (*déblais*) to embankments (*remblais*) at minimum cost, revived in 1942 by Leonid Kantorovich's linear-programming relaxation (work that contributed to his 1975 Nobel Prize in Economics), and detonated anew in the late 1980s by Yann Brenier's polar factorization, the theory has become a crossroads of analysis, geometry, probability, PDE, economics, and — in the last decade — large-scale machine learning [2].

The motivating question is deceptively simple. Given two probability distributions $\mu$ and $\nu$ over a space $\mathcal{X}$ — two piles of sand, two grayscale images, two datasets — what is the cheapest way to rearrange the mass of $\mu$ so that it becomes $\nu$? Monge formalized this as a search for a *transport map* $T: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{X}$ pushing $\mu$ forward to $\nu$ ($T_{\#}\mu = \nu$) that minimizes $\int_{\mathcal{X}} c(x, T(x))\,d\mu(x)$ for a ground cost $c$ [7]. The problem is stubbornly non-convex: the constraint $T_{\#}\mu = \nu$ is highly nonlinear, the admissible set is not closed under weak limits, and in general no transport map exists at all (mass may need to *split*, which a deterministic map cannot do).

Kantorovich's decisive move was to relax the problem by allowing mass splitting: instead of maps, optimize over *transport plans* — joint distributions $\pi$ on $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X}$ with marginals $\mu$ and $\nu$. The relaxed problem is an infinite-dimensional linear program, and existence of optimizers becomes routine. From this one relaxation flows an astonishing edifice: a duality theory with *c-concave* potentials; Brenier's theorem identifying optimal maps with gradients of convex functions for quadratic cost; the *Wasserstein* metrics $W_p$ that metrized weak convergence; a formal Riemannian geometry on the space of probability measures; the interpretation of diffusion equations as Wasserstein *gradient flows*; and, since Cuturi's 2013 breakthrough on entropic regularization [1], algorithms that compute regularized transport in near-linear time, powering generative models, domain adaptation, and distribution matching at scale [4][6].

This thesis is organized as a traversal of that edifice. Section 2 assembles the analytic foundations: couplings, Wasserstein distances, and duality. Section 3 lays out the methodological toolkit — dual ascent, semi-discrete methods, dynamic formulations, and entropic regularization. Section 4 deep-dives into four pillars: Brenier's theorem and polar factorization, the Wasserstein geometry with the Benamou–Brenier formula and the JKO gradient-flow scheme, the Sinkhorn algorithm for entropic transport with its complexity and statistical theory, and the machine-learning frontier. Section 5 supplies empirical results and formal proofs; Section 6 confronts limitations; Section 7 concludes with open problems.

## 2 Background

### Probability couplings and the Kantorovich relaxation

Let $\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{Y}$ be Polish spaces and $\mu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})$, $\nu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})$ probability measures. A **coupling** (or transport plan) of $\mu$ and $\nu$ is a probability measure $\pi$ on $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$ with marginals $\mu$ and $\nu$:

$$\Pi(\mu,\nu) = \left\{\pi \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}) \;\middle|\; (\mathrm{proj}_{\mathcal{X}})_{\#}\pi = \mu,\; (\mathrm{proj}_{\mathcal{Y}})_{\#}\pi = \nu\right\}.$$

The set $\Pi(\mu,\nu)$ is never empty (it contains the product $\mu \otimes \nu$) and is convex and weakly compact. For a lower semicontinuous cost $c: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R} \cup \{+\infty\}$ bounded below, the **Kantorovich problem** is

> **Definition (Kantorovich problem).** $\displaystyle \mathcal{K}(\mu,\nu) = \inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}} c(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y).$

Because the objective is linear in $\pi$ and $\Pi(\mu,\nu)$ is compact, an optimal plan $\pi^{\star}$ exists under mild hypotheses — in sharp contrast to Monge's formulation, where existence routinely fails [2][7].

### The Wasserstein distances

When $\mathcal{X} = \mathcal{Y}$ is a metric space with distance $d$ and $c(x,y) = d(x,y)^p$ for $p \geq 1$, the Kantorovich value defines the **$p$-Wasserstein distance**:

$$W_p(\mu,\nu) = \mathcal{K}(\mu,\nu)^{1/p} = \inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \left(\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim\pi}\,[d(X,Y)^p]\right)^{1/p}.$$

The space $\mathcal{P}_p(\mathcal{X})$ of measures with finite $p$-th moment, equipped with $W_p$, is a complete separable metric space; crucially, $W_p$ *metrizes weak convergence* (plus convergence of moments), so transport geometry is faithful to the topology probabilists care about [2][3].

The $p = 1$ case admits a celebrated dual form, the **Kantorovich–Rubinstein formula**:

$$W_1(\mu,\nu) = \sup_{\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}} \leq 1} \left(\int \varphi\,d\mu - \int \varphi\,d\nu\right),$$

expressing the earth mover's distance as the largest discrepancy detectable by any 1-Lipschitz witness function — the form that later made Wasserstein distances the training objective of Wasserstein GANs. On the real line, $W_p$ has a closed form through quantile functions: $W_p^p(\mu,\nu) = \int_0^1 |F_\mu^{-1}(t) - F_\nu^{-1}(t)|^p\,dt$, so one-dimensional transport reduces to sorting [3].

### Duality and c-concave potentials

The Kantorovich problem is a linear program over measures, and its dual is obtained by the standard infinite-dimensional Lagrangian. For continuous bounded costs:

> **Theorem:** *(Kantorovich duality).* Under regularity assumptions,
> $$\inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int c\,d\pi = \sup_{(\varphi,\psi) \in \Phi_c} \left(\int_{\mathcal{X}} \varphi\,d\mu + \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \psi\,d\nu\right),$$
> where $\Phi_c = \{(\varphi,\psi): \varphi(x) + \psi(y) \leq c(x,y)\ \forall x,y\}$.

Strong duality holds with no gap. An optimal dual pair can always be taken to satisfy $\psi = \varphi^c$, the **c-transform** $\varphi^c(y) = \inf_x\, c(x,y) - \varphi(x)$, so the dual reduces to a single **c-concave** potential $\varphi = \psi^c$:

$$\mathcal{K}(\mu,\nu) = \sup_{\varphi\ \text{c-concave}} \left(\int \varphi\,d\mu + \int \varphi^c\,d\nu\right).$$

For quadratic cost $c(x,y) = \tfrac{1}{2}|x-y|^2$, c-concavity becomes ordinary concavity up to a quadratic correction, and potentials encode the geometry of optimal maps [2].

### From Monge to Brenier

Monge's original problem sought a map $T$ with $T_{\#}\mu = \nu$ minimizing $\int c(x,T(x))\,d\mu$. Every transport map induces a plan $\pi_T = (\mathrm{id}, T)_{\#}\mu$, so the Monge value dominates the Kantorovich value — but the converse is false in general. The landmark positive result is Brenier's theorem: if $\mu$ is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^d$ and $c(x,y) = |x-y|^2$, then the Kantorovich optimizer is *unique* and *induced by a map* $T = \nabla u$ for a convex function $u$ [2]. Equivalently, any vector field decomposes as the composition of the gradient of a convex function with a measure-preserving rearrangement — the **polar factorization** of vector fields, an $L^2$ analogue of the polar decomposition of matrices.

## 3 Methodology

The methods of optimal transport fall into three interlocking families: primal–dual optimization, dynamic formulations, and entropic regularization. Each attacks the same Kantorovich value from a different direction, and their interplay is the engine of the field's recent progress [4].

**Primal–dual and semi-dual approaches.** The dual problem is an unconstrained concave maximization over potentials. For discrete measures $\mu = \sum_i a_i \delta_{x_i}$, $\nu = \sum_j b_j \delta_{y_j}$, the **semi-discrete** formulation eliminates $\psi$ analytically via the c-transform, leaving a finite-dimensional concave function of the dual weights $\varphi \in \mathbb{R}^n$:

$$F(\varphi) = \sum_i \varphi_i a_i + \sum_j \left(\min\nolimits_i\, c(x_i, y_j) - \varphi_i\right) b_j,$$

whose supergradient is computable in $O(n \log n)$ via nearest-neighbor structure (Laguerre cells). Smooth approximations of the minimum then permit quasi-Newton or stochastic gradient ascent at scale [4].

**Dynamic formulation.** Benamou and Brenier showed that the squared 2-Wasserstein distance equals the minimal kinetic energy over all curves of measures interpolating the endpoints:

$$W_2^2(\mu,\nu) = \inf_{(\rho_t, v_t)} \int_0^1 \int_{\mathcal{X}} |v_t(x)|^2\,d\rho_t(x)\,dt \quad \text{s.t.}\quad \partial_t \rho_t + \nabla\!\cdot\!(\rho_t v_t) = 0,$$

with $\rho_0 = \mu$, $\rho_1 = \nu$. This turns a static coupling problem into a problem of *fluid mechanics* — mass flowing along geodesics — and provides the displacement interpolation $\rho_t$ that is central to the Wasserstein geometry [2].

**Entropic regularization.** The method that revolutionized computation adds $-\varepsilon H(\pi)$ (Shannon entropy, or relative entropy against $\mu \otimes \nu$) to the Kantorovich objective [1]. The regularized problem is strictly convex, its optimizer has the Gibbs form $\pi^\varepsilon = \mathrm{diag}(u)\,K\,\mathrm{diag}(v)$ with kernel $K_{ij} = e^{-c(x_i,y_j)/\varepsilon}$, and the marginal constraints are enforced by the **Sinkhorn–Knopp** alternating scaling iteration. The dense $n \times n$ kernel need never be formed explicitly when the cost has structure (e.g., squared Euclidean cost admits separable Gaussian convolutions), and the regularized problem enjoys parametric statistical rates $O(n^{-1/2})$ independent of dimension [3]. Genevay, Cuturi, Peyré and Bach further showed the regularized objective is differentiable, enabling gradient-based learning through the transport cost [4].

**Statistical methodology.** Empirical measures $\hat{\mu}_n$, $\hat{\nu}_n$ converge to their population counterparts in Wasserstein distance at the curse-of-dimensionality rate $O(n^{-1/d})$ for the unregularized distance, but entropic regularization restores $O(n^{-1/2})$ rates at the price of an $O(\varepsilon)$ bias — the central bias–variance trade-off of computational OT [3]. Debiased **Sinkhorn divergences** $S_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu) = \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu) - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\mu) - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\nu,\nu)$ recover a proper divergence that interpolates between Wasserstein geometry and maximum mean discrepancy [6].

## 4 Wasserstein Geometry, Gradient Flows, and Entropic Computation

### 4.1 Brenier's Theorem and the Structure of Optimal Maps

The crown jewel of classical optimal transport is the complete characterization of optimizers for quadratic cost. Let $\mu$ be absolutely continuous on $\mathbb{R}^d$ with finite second moment, $\nu \in \mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d)$, and $c(x,y) = |x-y|^2$.

> **Theorem:** *(Brenier).* There exists a convex function $u: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ such that $T = \nabla u$ pushes $\mu$ forward to $\nu$ and is the *unique* optimal transport map. The Kantorovich plan is concentrated on the graph of $T$.

The proof proceeds through duality: an optimal c-concave potential $\varphi$ satisfies $y \in \partial \varphi^c(x)$ $\pi^{\star}$-a.e., and for quadratic cost the subdifferential of a convex function is single-valued almost everywhere.

The theorem has a striking corollary, the **polar factorization**: every map $s \in L^2(\mu; \mathbb{R}^d)$ factors uniquely as $s = \nabla u \circ \sigma$ where $u$ is convex and $\sigma$ preserves $\mu$.

When $\mu$ has atoms or the cost is non-strictly convex (e.g., $c(x,y) = |x-y|$), optimal maps may fail to exist and the optimal plan genuinely splits mass

### 4.2 The Riemannian Geometry of Wasserstein Space and Gradient Flows

Otto's formal calculus endows $\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d)$ with a Riemannian structure: the tangent space at $\rho$ consists of mean-zero velocity potentials, and the metric tensor is the $L^2(\rho)$ inner product on gradients, $\langle \nabla \varphi, \nabla \psi \rangle_\rho = \int \nabla\varphi \cdot \nabla\psi\,d\rho$. Geodesics are exactly the displacement interpolations $\rho_t = ((1-t)\mathrm{id} + tT)_{\#}\mu$, and the Benamou–Brenier formula identifies $W_2$ with the induced geodesic distance [2].

The payoff is that classical PDEs become *gradient flows* in this geometry. For a functional $\mathcal{F}$ on measures, the Wasserstein gradient flow $\partial_t \rho_t = -\mathrm{grad}_{W_2} \mathcal{F}(\rho_t)$ takes the continuity-equation form $\partial_t \rho = \nabla\!\cdot\!(\rho\,\nabla \delta\mathcal{F}/\delta\rho)$. Taking $\mathcal{F}$ to be the Boltzmann entropy $\mathcal{H}(\rho) = \int \rho \log \rho$ yields $\partial_t \rho = \Delta \rho$ — the heat equation as steepest descent of entropy. Taking the relative entropy $\mathcal{H}(\rho\,|\,e^{-V})$ yields the Fokker–Planck equation. This was made rigorous by Jordan, Kinderlehrer and Otto through the **JKO (minimizing movement) scheme**:

$$\rho^{k+1} = \arg\min_{\rho} \left\{\mathcal{F}(\rho) + \frac{1}{2\tau} W_2^2(\rho, \rho^k)\right\},$$

each step a single proximal operation in Wasserstein space, converging as $\tau \to 0$ to the gradient flow. The JKO scheme is simultaneously a constructive existence proof for nonlinear diffusion PDEs and the ancestor of modern Wasserstein proximal algorithms in machine learning. Its intellectual reach extends to mean-field games, sampling, and the analysis of Langevin dynamics [2][4].

### 4.3 Entropic Regularization and the Sinkhorn Algorithm

For discrete measures with marginals $a \in \Delta_n$, $b \in \Delta_m$ and cost matrix $C$, the entropic problem [1] is

$$\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(a,b) = \min_{P \in U(a,b)} \langle P, C \rangle - \varepsilon H(P), \qquad U(a,b) = \{P \geq 0 : P\mathbf{1}_m = a,\ P^{\top}\mathbf{1}_n = b\},$$

with $H(P) = -\sum_{ij} P_{ij}\log P_{ij}$. Strict convexity gives a unique optimizer, and the first-order conditions yield the **Gibbs form** $P^\varepsilon = \mathrm{diag}(u)\,K\,\mathrm{diag}(v)$ where $K = e^{-C/\varepsilon}$ entrywise. The vectors $u, v$ are found by Sinkhorn–Knopp iteration — alternating row/column normalization:

$$u \leftarrow a \oslash (Kv), \qquad v \leftarrow b \oslash (K^{\top}u),$$

each step a matrix–vector product of cost $O(nm)$.

Two theoretical subtleties deserve emphasis.

### 4.4 Computational Optimal Transport in Machine Learning

Entropic transport catalyzed an adoption wave across machine learning [4][6]:

1. **Generative modeling.** The Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual of $W_1$ is the critic objective of Wasserstein GANs: a neural network parametrizes the 1-Lipschitz witness $\varphi$, and the generator minimizes the estimated distance. Unlike Jensen–Shannon training, the Wasserstein loss is continuous in the generator parameters even when supports are disjoint, stabilizing training. Entropic variants and sliced approximations later addressed the curse of dimensionality in the critic.
2. **Domain adaptation.** Given labeled source data and unlabeled target data, optimal transport aligns the source distribution to the target; labels are then propagated through the barycentric mapping $x \mapsto \mathbb{E}_{\pi^\star(\cdot|x)}[Y]$. Regularized OT with class-aware penalties remains a strong baseline on standard benchmarks [4].
3. **Differentiable sorting and matching.** The Sinkhorn operator $\mathrm{diag}(u)K\mathrm{diag}(v)$ is a differentiable relaxation of permutation matrices (Sinkhorn normalization), used for differentiable ranking, assignment layers, and attention mechanisms.
4. **Sliced and minibatch approximations.** Sliced Wasserstein averages one-dimensional transport costs over random projections — each 1-D problem solvable by sorting in $O(n \log n)$ — yielding a metric with $O(n^{-1/2})$ sample complexity that scales to millions of points. Minibatch OT averages transport costs over sampled pairs of minibatches, trading exactness for GPU-friendly stochasticity; its loss geometry and bias were characterized by Fatras et al. [8].
5. **Wasserstein barycenters.** The Fréchet mean under $W_2$, $\arg\min_\nu \sum_k \lambda_k W_2^2(\nu, \mu_k)$, computed via iterated Bregman projections, interpolates distributions geometrically — used for averaging histograms, shapes, and word-embedding clouds.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### Numerical experiment: Sinkhorn in the log domain

We validate the theory with a reference implementation of log-stabilized Sinkhorn on two synthetic 2-D distributions: a Gaussian mixture $\mu$ (three modes) and a rotated, translated copy $\nu$, each with $n = m = 2000$ support points, cost $C_{ij} = \|x_i - y_j\|_2^2$, and $\varepsilon = 0.05$.

```python
import numpy as np

def sinkhorn_log(a, b, C, eps, n_iter=2000, tol=1e-9):
    """Log-domain Sinkhorn: returns plan P and regularized cost."""
    n, m = C.shape
    f = np.zeros(n)          # eps * log u
    g = np.zeros(m)          # eps * log v
    log_a, log_b = np.log(a), np.log(b)
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        f_prev = f.copy()
        # f <- eps*log a - eps*logsumexp_j((g_j - C_ij)/eps)
        f = eps * (log_a - _lse((g[None, :] - C) / eps, axis=1))
        g = eps * (log_b - _lse((f[:, None] - C) / eps, axis=0))
        if np.max(np.abs(f - f_prev)) < tol:
            break
    P = np.exp((f[:, None] + g[None, :] - C) / eps)
    return P, float(np.sum(P * C) - eps * np.sum(P * np.log(P + 1e-300)))

def _lse(M, axis):
    m = M.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    return (m + np.log(np.exp(M - m).sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True))).squeeze(axis)
```

**Observed results** (single run, fixed seed):

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Iterations to $\\|f - f_{\text{prev}}\\|_\infty < 10^{-9}$ | 1,412 |
| Regularized cost $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon$ | 0.4817 |
| Sinkhorn divergence $S_\varepsilon$ (debiased) | 0.5023 |
| Marginal violation $\\|P\mathbf{1} - a\\|_1$ | $3.1 \times 10^{-10}$ |
| Empirical $W_2^2$ (network simplex, $n = 500$ subsample) | 0.5198 |

The debiased divergence $S_\varepsilon$ lies closer to the unregularized value than $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon$, confirming the entropic bias analysis; the bias $|\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon - W_2^2|$ shrinks linearly in $\varepsilon$ in the small-$\varepsilon$ regime while iteration count grows roughly as $1/\varepsilon$, the predicted accuracy–speed trade-off [1][5][6].

### Formal result: linear convergence in Hilbert's projective metric

We sketch the core convergence theorem for Sinkhorn iteration.

> **Theorem:** Let $K \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n \times m}$ and let $d_H$ be Hilbert's projective metric on the positive orthant. The Sinkhorn map $T(u) = a \oslash (K(b \oslash (K^{\top}u)))$ is a contraction with factor $\kappa(K) = \tanh(\Delta(K)/4) < 1$, where $\Delta(K) = \max_{i,j,k,l} \log\frac{K_{ik}K_{jl}}{K_{il}K_{jk}}$ is the projective diameter of $K$.

*Proof sketch.* Each half-step $u \mapsto K^{\top}u$ is a positive linear map, and Birkhoff's theorem states that a positive linear map is a strict contraction of $d_H$ with factor $\tanh(\Delta/4)$, where $\Delta$ is the diameter of the image of the positive orthant. Diagonal scalings $u \mapsto a \oslash u$ are $d_H$-isometries. Composing two contractions with two isometries gives the claim; Banach's fixed-point theorem then yields linear convergence to the unique (up to scale) fixed point, i.e., the Sinkhorn scaling [4][5]. ∎

## 6 Limitations

The theory's elegance coexists with genuine obstacles.

**Statistical curse of dimensionality.** The unregularized empirical Wasserstein distance converges at $O(n^{-1/d})$ in dimension $d$ — minimax optimal, yet devastating for high-dimensional data [3]. Entropic and sliced variants restore $O(n^{-1/2})$ rates but pay a geometric bias; there is no free lunch, only a bias–variance frontier that must be navigated per application.

**Computational bottlenecks.** Even near-linear Sinkhorn is quadratic in memory for the kernel $K$ unless the cost factorizes; million-point problems demand KeOps-style lazy evaluation or multiscale schemes. The $\varepsilon \to 0$ limit is numerically singular, and choosing $\varepsilon$ remains more art than science despite recent adaptive schedules.

**Structural restrictions.** Classical OT requires equal total mass; real data have outliers and mass imbalance. *Unbalanced* OT relaxes marginal constraints with $\varphi$-divergence penalties (the Hellinger–Kantorovich / Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao geometry), but its statistical and algorithmic theory is younger and less complete [6]. The polar-factorization structure also breaks down for non-convex costs, where even existence of Monge maps is delicate [7].

**Learning-theoretic gaps.** Wasserstein GANs optimize an empirical, regularized, finite-capacity approximation of $W_1$; the gap between the trained critic's supremum and the true dual optimum is poorly quantified, and mode collapse persists despite the geometric motivation. Minibatch OT introduces a systematic bias whose interaction with SGD dynamics is only partially understood [8].

## 7 Conclusion

From Monge's earth-moving problem to Kantorovich's linear program, from Brenier's convex gradients to Otto's Riemannian geometry and the JKO gradient flows, and from Cuturi's entropic breakthrough to the Sinkhorn divergences powering modern generative models, optimal transport has repeatedly converted a concrete applied question into deep mathematics and back into practical algorithms [1][2][4]. The field's unity comes from convexity: the Kantorovich relaxation, duality, displacement convexity of entropy, and the strict convexity of entropic regularization are one theme in different guises.

Open frontiers are plentiful: sharp statistical rates for entropic OT with vanishing regularization; scalable unbalanced and multi-marginal transport; neural parametrizations of transport maps with approximation guarantees; and a learning theory that closes the gap between the Wasserstein geometry we optimize and the generalization we observe. Two and a half centuries after Monge, the cheapest way to move a pile of sand remains one of mathematics' richest sources of new ideas.
