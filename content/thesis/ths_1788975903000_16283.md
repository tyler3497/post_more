# Worst-Case Optimal Joins: The AGM Bound, Leapfrog Triejoin, Generic Join Algorithms, and the Modern Graph Query Engine

## Abstract

For half a century, relational query optimizers decomposed every multiway join into a tree of binary joins, accepting the explosion of intermediate results as an unavoidable fact of life. The work of Atserias, Grohe, and Marx (AGM) in 2008 upended this consensus: the worst-case output size of any full conjunctive query is governed by a fractional edge cover of its hypergraph, and this bound is *tight*. This thesis develops the theory and practice of worst-case optimal join algorithms from first principles: we prove the AGM bound via Shearer's entropy inequality, derive the generic join skeleton of Ngo, Porat, Ré, and Rudra that unifies NPRR and Leapfrog Triejoin, analyze the leapfrog intersection machinery of Veldhuizen's Leapfrog Triejoin, and show how modern graph query engines — exemplified by Stanford's EmptyHeaded — turn this theory into order-of-magnitude performance gains on real graph pattern queries. Along the way we examine why binary plans are provably suboptimal, where the entropy argument breaks down, and what remains open at the intersection of join theory, fine-grained data structure design, and parallel execution.

## 1. Introduction

Joins are the computational heart of relational databases. Given relations $R_1, \ldots, R_m$ over attributes drawn from a universe $\mathcal{V}$, the *natural join* $\bowtie_{i=1}^{m} R_i$ enumerates all tuples consistent across every relation. For decades, commercial systems evaluated multiway joins by selecting a binary join order: join two relations, materialize the intermediate result, and repeat. The optimizer's job was to pick an ordering whose intermediate results were small, using cardinality estimates and dynamic programming over the plan space.

This design encodes a deep assumption: that the *worst case* is governed by the largest intermediate result. In 2008, Atserias, Grohe, and Marx proved this assumption wrong in a precise and devastating way [2]. They showed that for the triangle query

$$Q_\Delta = R(A,B) \bowtie S(B,C) \bowtie T(A,C)$$

with $|R| = |S| = |T| = N$, the output size can never exceed $N^{3/2}$ — yet any binary join plan must, on some instance, produce an intermediate result of size $\Theta(N^2)$. The $N^{2}$-size intermediate is not inherent to the query; it is an artifact of the binary plan shape. Algorithms exist whose running time matches the *output-sensitive* bound $O(N^{3/2})$, and these bounds are tight: there are instances (the complete graph on $\sqrt{N}$ vertices) achieving $\Omega(N^{3/2})$ output.

This observation launched a research program. Ngo, Porat, Ré, and Rudra gave the first algorithm whose worst-case runtime matches the AGM bound [3], and Veldhuizen's Leapfrog Triejoin [4] showed the same guarantee emerges from a strikingly simple variable-at-a-time intersection scheme. The Generic-Join skeleton of Ngo et al. [5] unified both, and systems like EmptyHeaded [6] turned the theory into order-of-magnitude wins on graph workloads.

The thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 fixes the hypergraph model and the binary-plan pathology. Section 3 presents our methodology: the entropy-theoretic proof apparatus and the recursive skeleton from which all worst-case optimal algorithms descend. Section 4 is the deep dive: the AGM inequality and its LP formulation, the Generic-Join algorithm, Leapfrog Triejoin's leapfrog intersection core, and the extension of these ideas beyond pure worst-case analysis via generalized hypertree decompositions. Section 5 reports empirical evidence — from LogicBlox's commercial deployment to EmptyHeaded's order-of-magnitude speedups — alongside the tightness argument. Section 6 catalogs the limitations honestly: memory assumptions, the projection problem, skewed data, and parallelism. We conclude with the open frontier.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The hypergraph model of join queries

A *full conjunctive query* is modeled as a hypergraph $\mathcal{H} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$ where $\mathcal{V}$ is the set of attributes and each hyperedge $F \in \mathcal{E}$ corresponds to a relation $R_F$ over attributes $F$. Let $N_F = |R_F|$. The query answer is $Q = \bowtie_{F \in \mathcal{E}} R_F$. For a subset $I \subseteq \mathcal{V}$, $\pi_I(R)$ denotes projection onto $I$, and $R[t_I]$ denotes the $t_I$-section (the set of extensions of the partial tuple $t_I$ within $R$) [5].

> **Definition (Fractional edge cover).** A *fractional edge cover* of $\mathcal{H}$ is a vector $\mathbf{x} = (x_F)_{F \in \mathcal{E}}$ with $x_F \geq 0$ and $\sum_{F: v \in F} x_F \geq 1$ for every $v \in \mathcal{V}$. The *fractional edge cover number* $\rho^*(\mathcal{H})$ is the minimum of $\sum_F x_F$ over all fractional edge covers.

The all-ones vector is always feasible, so fractional edge covers always exist. The tightest data-dependent bound comes from minimizing $\sum_F (\log N_F) x_F$ over the polytope — a small linear program.

### 2.2 Why binary plans fail: the triangle query

Consider $Q_\Delta$ with $N$-tuple relations. The fractional cover $x_R = x_S = x_T = 1/2$ is feasible (each vertex is covered by two half-weighted edges), giving the AGM bound $|Q_\Delta| \leq N^{3/2}$. But consider the binary plan $(R \bowtie S) \bowtie T$. The relation $R \bowtie S$ on the path query $A-B-C$ can itself contain $\Theta(N^2)$ tuples (e.g., when $R$ pairs every $A$ with a fixed $B_0$ and $S$ pairs $B_0$ with every $C$) — and this blowup is unavoidable for *any* binary plan: every binary plan has a first join, and adversarial instances force it to materialize $\Theta(N^2)$ tuples [6]. The output is at most $N^{3/2}$, so the plan does asymptotically more work than the output requires.

This gap is not a curiosity: triangle counting, $k$-clique enumeration, and subgraph pattern matching are core workloads in social network analysis, fraud detection, and bioinformatics. On cyclic queries generally, binary plans pay for the intermediate blowup rather than the output size, and the gap between the two can be an arbitrary polynomial.

| Query | Best binary-plan intermediate | AGM bound (output) | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| $Q_\Delta$ (triangle) | $\Theta(N^2)$ | $O(N^{3/2})$ | $N^{1/2}$ |
| $K_4$ (4-clique) | $\Theta(N^3)$ | $O(N^2)$ | $N$ |
| $Q_{4\text{-cycle}}$: $R(A,B)\bowtie S(B,C)\bowtie T(C,D)\bowtie U(D,A)$ | $\Theta(N^2)$ | $O(N^2)$ | tight for this cover* |
| $L$-path (acyclic) | $\tilde O(N + Z)$ (Yannakakis) | $\tilde O(N + Z)$ | none |

*For the 4-cycle with the cover $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ the AGM bound is $N^2$, matching the binary plan on worst-case instances — but the *generic* bound over all fractional covers is data-dependent; e.g., with skewed sizes the fractional cover LP can beat every binary plan [5].

### 2.3 The min property and the target

An intersection routine has the *min property* if intersecting sets $A_1, \ldots, A_k$ costs $O(\min_i |A_i| \log(\cdot))$ — bounded by the *smallest* input. EmptyHeaded's survey formulation shows that when the min property holds at every recursion level, a variable-at-a-time join achieves worst-case optimality for *any* query [6] — the bridge from combinatorics to algorithms: the AGM bound sets the target runtime, the min property supplies the mechanism.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is twofold: *analytic*, in that we prove upper bounds via entropy arguments that make no assumption about the algorithm's plan shape; and *constructive*, in that we extract from the inductive proof a recursive algorithm skeleton whose runtime provably matches the analytic bound.

### 3.1 The entropy argument

Let $X = (X_v)_{v \in \mathcal{V}}$ be a uniformly random tuple of the join output. Then $H(X) = \log |Q|$. For each relation $R_F$, the marginal $X_F$ is supported on $R_F$, so $H(X_F) \leq \log N_F$. Shearer's lemma [7] states that for any fractional edge cover $\mathbf{x}$,

$$H(X) \leq \sum_{F \in \mathcal{E}} x_F \, H(X_F) \leq \sum_{F \in \mathcal{E}} x_F \log N_F,$$

and exponentiating yields the AGM inequality [2]:

> **Theorem (AGM):** For any fractional edge cover $\mathbf{x}$ of $\mathcal{H}$,
> $$|Q| \leq \prod_{F \in \mathcal{E}} N_F^{x_F}.$$

Minimizing the right-hand side over the fractional edge cover polytope is a linear program; call its value $\text{AGM}(Q)$.

### 3.2 From proof to algorithm: query decomposition

The inductive proof of the AGM bound due to Ngo et al. [5] rests on a *query decomposition lemma*: for any partition $\mathcal{V} = I \uplus J$,

$$|Q| \leq |\bowtie_{F \in \mathcal{E}_I} \pi_I(R_F)| \cdot \max_{t_I} |\bowtie_{F \in \mathcal{E}_J} \pi_J(R_F \ltimes t_I)|,$$

where $\mathcal{E}_I = \{F : F \cap I \neq \emptyset\}$ and $\ltimes$ is the semijoin. This lemma is the AGM proof's inductive step — and read operationally, it *is* the Generic-Join algorithm. The proof that the algorithm is fast and the induction that proves the bound coincide: the algorithm is the proof, made executable.

### 3.3 Data structures and the RAM model

All analyses assume the RAM model with relations stored in *trie* (multilevel) indices: at variable $v_d$ in the order, each relation's tuples sharing a prefix are grouped, so the algorithm can iterate over $R[t_{<d}]$ projected onto $v_d$ as a sorted set [4][6]. Index construction costs $\tilde O(N)$; the log factors it introduces are the "up to a log factor" in the optimality claims.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The AGM inequality: tightness, LP formulation, and Shearer's lemma

The AGM bound is remarkable not only for its generality but for its *tightness*: for every hypergraph and every cardinality vector, there exists an instance whose output size equals the bound exactly [2][7]. The construction exploits LP duality. The primal LP,

$$\min \sum_F (\log N_F) x_F \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_{F \ni v} x_F \geq 1, \; x_F \geq 0,$$

has dual

$$\max \sum_v w_v \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_{v \in F} w_v \leq \log N_F, \; w_v \geq 0.$$

Given an optimal dual solution $\mathbf{w}$, construct the *product instance*: for each variable $v$ take a domain $D_v$ of size $2^{w_v}$, and let $R_F = \times_{v \in F} D_v$ (the full Cartesian product). Then $|R_F| = 2^{\sum_{v \in F} w_v} \leq N_F$ by dual feasibility, while the join output is the product over all variables, of size $2^{\sum_v w_v}$ — exactly the AGM bound [7]. For the triangle query this recovers the complete graph on $\sqrt{N}$ vertices with $\Theta(N^{3/2})$ triangles.

Shearer's lemma is the *only* nontrivial ingredient: everything else is bookkeeping. It generalizes the subadditivity of entropy to arbitrary fractional covers of the variable set, converting a *combinatorial covering constraint* into an *information-theoretic* one with no reference to any join order. This is why the bound applies to all algorithms equally: it bounds the output itself, not any particular plan.

```python
# Fractional edge cover LP for the triangle query (equal sizes)
import itertools
# minimize  (log N)*(xR + xS + xT)  s.t. xR + xT >= 1 (vertex A),
# xR + xS >= 1 (vertex B), xS + xT >= 1 (vertex C), x >= 0.
# By symmetry the optimum is xR = xS = xT = 1/2, value (3/2) log N.
def agm_triangle(logN):
    x = 0.5
    return 2 ** (3 * x * logN)   # N^{3/2}
```

### 4.2 The Generic-Join skeleton

Ngo, Porat, Ré, and Rudra's Generic-Join [5] is the master algorithm. Given a query $Q$ over variables $\mathcal{V}$ and indexed relations:

```
Generic-Join(Q over V):
  if |V| = 1:
     return ∩_{F ∈ E} R_F          # unary intersection, min property
  pick I ⊂ V, 1 ≤ |I| < |V|        # typically |I| = 1
  L ← Generic-Join(⋈_{F∈E_I} π_I(R_F))
  Q ← ∅
  for each t_I ∈ L:
     Q_{t_I} ← Generic-Join(⋈_{F∈E_J} π_J(R_F ⋉ t_I))
     Q ← Q ∪ ({t_I} × Q_{t_I})
  return Q
```

The base case is a $k$-way intersection of unary relations; with the min property (e.g., via galloping search or leapfrog join) it costs $\tilde O(\min_F |R_F|)$. The recursion mirrors the query decomposition lemma exactly, so the runtime induction and the AGM-bound induction coincide: Generic-Join runs in $\tilde O(\text{AGM}(Q) + N)$ [5]. Both NPRR [3] and Leapfrog Triejoin [4] are instantiations of this skeleton, differing in the choice of $I$ and the intersection primitive.

A subtle and important feature: the algorithm is *instance-adaptive* in a way binary plans are not. When $R$'s section at some $a$ is small, the recursion naturally spends little work there; the min property ensures the intersection at each variable costs no more than the smallest participating section. Skew — the bane of binary plans — is absorbed by the same mechanism that guarantees worst-case optimality.

### 4.3 Leapfrog Triejoin: the leapfrog intersection core

Veldhuizen's Leapfrog Triejoin [4] fixes a variable order $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ and descends a trie of partial assignments in depth-first order. At depth $d$, let $\mathcal{E}_d$ be the relations containing $v_d$; the algorithm computes

$$L_d(t_{<d}) = \bigcap_{F \in \mathcal{E}_d} \pi_{v_d}(R_F[t_{<d}]),$$

the intersection of the sorted value-sets at the current variable, then recurses on each value.

The inner routine, *leapfrog join*, keeps $k$ sorted iterators in a circular list; the iterator at the minimum key gallops (exponential search) to the maximum key, repeating until all keys agree or some iterator exhausts. Total work is $O(\min_i |A_i| \log N_{\max})$: the min property [4]. Crucially, LFTJ is worst-case optimal *for every variable order* [4] — the order affects only constant/log factors and cache behavior, never the asymptotic guarantee. Veldhuizen proved something stronger: LFTJ is optimal for *finer-grained* instance classes defined by constraints on *projection* cardinalities $|\pi_S(R_F)|$, where NPRR is provably not optimal — exhibiting a family on which LFTJ runs in $O(n \log n)$ while NPRR requires $\Theta(n^{1.375})$ [4]. Real graph data is projection-sparse (distinct endpoints far fewer than edges), so this matters in practice.

```rust
// Leapfrog join core: k sorted iterators seek in lockstep.
// Invariant: iters sorted by key; iter[1] seeks to key of iter[k-1].
fn leapfrog_join(iters: &mut [SortedIter]) -> Option<Key> {
    let k = iters.len();
    loop {
        let hi = iters[k - 1].key()?;
        let lo = iters[1].seek(hi)?;      // galloping exponential search
        if lo == hi {
            // all agree: emit, then advance past it
            iters.rotate_left(1);
            return Some(lo);
        }
        iters.rotate_left(1);             // re-sort invariant
    }
}
```

### 4.4 Beyond worst case: GHDs, factorization, and modern engines

Worst-case optimality is a *guarantee*, not a complete performance story. EmptyHeaded [6] showed that on real graph workloads, the AGM bound alone is loose: it ignores the *structure* of the data. Two refinements dominate modern practice.

First, *generalized hypertree decompositions* (GHDs): decompose the query hypergraph into bags, solve each bag with a worst-case optimal join, and combine the bags acyclically with Yannakakis-style semijoin reductions. The resulting *fractional hypertree width* bound is never worse than the AGM bound and strictly better on queries with acyclic substructure — EmptyHeaded reported up to 3000× speedups over pure WCOJ plans on favorable graph pattern queries [6].

Second, *tries as the universal physical layout*: EmptyHeaded stores every relation as a multilevel trie with bitset indices, so the leapfrog machinery operates directly on the storage format with SIMD set intersections [6]. This co-design of algorithm and layout turns the asymptotic guarantee into wall-clock wins: the min property is only as fast as the intersection primitive, and bitset intersections make the constants tiny.

The broader lesson, articulated in the "joins via geometric resolutions" line of work [8], is that the variable-at-a-time paradigm extends beyond natural joins — to theta-joins, inequality predicates, and aggregation — via geometric box-covering arguments. The AGM program thus grew from a single bound into a general methodology for output-sensitive query processing.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The tightness theorem, restated as experiment

The product-instance construction of Section 4.1 is *executable*. On the triangle query with $N = 10^6$, take each relation as the edge set of the complete graph on $10^3$ vertices ($N_F \approx 10^6$); the output holds $\binom{10^3}{3} \approx N^{3/2}/6$ triangles. Any correct algorithm must emit these tuples, so $\Omega(N^{3/2})$ lower-bounds *every* algorithm — while Generic-Join/LFTJ achieve $\tilde O(N^{3/2})$ [3][4]. No binary plan can match this: its first join materializes $\Theta(N^2)$ pairs on this instance.

### 5.2 EmptyHeaded: theory meets graphs

Aberger et al. built EmptyHeaded, a relational engine for graph processing whose core join is worst-case optimal with GHD-based plan selection [6]. Key reported results on real graph datasets (Google+, LiveJournal, Twitter):

- On cyclic pattern queries (triangles, 4-cliques, chordless cycles), EmptyHeaded outperformed specialized graph systems and traditional RDBMS baselines by *orders of magnitude*, with the GHD optimization contributing up to 3000× over naive WCOJ plans on favorable queries [6].
- The trie storage layout with bitset intersections kept the per-intersection constants small enough that the asymptotic advantage translated to wall-clock wins at billion-edge scale.
- Importantly, the same engine remained competitive on ordinary business-intelligence (acyclic) queries, where the GHD plan degenerates to a Yannakakis plan — worst-case optimality costs nothing on easy queries [6].

### 5.3 LogicBlox and the leapfrog lineage

Leapfrog Triejoin was not an academic prototype: it shipped in LogicBlox's commercial Datalog system, where Veldhuizen reported it "compared conspicuously well to the NPRR algorithm in preliminary benchmarks" [4] — the empirical observation that motivated the optimality analysis. The lineage continues in open source: modern Datalog and research engines treat LFTJ as the default multiway join. Its simplicity — implementable on B-trees, with a concise optimality proof — is what carried it from theory into production.

### 5.4 The cost of being worst-case optimal: a balanced ledger

| Dimension | Binary hash-join plans | WCOJ (Generic-Join / LFTJ) |
|---|---|---|
| Worst-case runtime, $Q_\Delta$ | $\Theta(N^2)$ | $\tilde O(N^{3/2})$ |
| Implementation complexity | mature, vectorized | trie indices, leapfrog iterators |
| Index requirement | hash tables per join key | sorted/trie order per variable order |
| Projection/aggregation queries | well supported | requires extensions [8] |
| Parallelism | mature (exchange operators) | active research area |
| Optimizer burden | join ordering (NP-hard) | variable order (any order is optimal [4]) |

One striking row: the optimizer's hardest problem — join ordering — *evaporates*. Since every variable order is worst-case optimal, order selection becomes a constant-factor tuning problem rather than an asymptotic one [4].

## 6. Limitations

**The RAM and index assumptions are load-bearing.** The $\tilde O(\cdot)$ bounds hide trie-index construction ($\tilde O(N)$) and assume uniform-cost memory. On disk-resident or NUMA-remote data, the seek-heavy leapfrog pattern can lose to sequential-scan hash joins; the guarantee is asymptotic, not a wall-clock promise [4][6].

**Projections break the clean story.** The AGM bound governs *full* conjunctive queries (no projections). With projections ($\exists$ variables), the output can be far smaller than any fractional-cover bound on the full query. Veldhuizen extends LFTJ to $\exists_1$ queries [4], but general projection handling requires GHD/factorization machinery or PANDA-style submodular-width algorithms — a strictly harder theory.

**Skew beyond the min property.** The min property bounds intersection cost by the *smallest* input set, but a pathologically skewed relation (one heavy hitter dominating sections) can force repeated revisits of the same heavy values. Degree-based heavy/light partitioning, as in NPRR's power-of-choices analysis [3], mitigates this at the cost of implementation complexity.

**Parallelism and distribution remain open.** The depth-first, seek-driven nature of LFTJ resists the clean partitioning of hash joins; work-stealing over the trie helps on shared memory, but distributed worst-case optimal joins with tight communication bounds are still an active research frontier [8].

**The bound is data-oblivious in one direction.** The AGM bound uses only relation *sizes*, not content statistics. On highly selective instances, a traditional optimizer with good cardinality estimates can beat a WCOJ algorithm by exploiting selectivity the bound cannot see — the bound is tight in the worst case, not representative of the average case [6].

## 7. Conclusion

The arc from AGM's entropy inequality [2] to NPRR's first worst-case optimal algorithm [3], the unifying Generic-Join skeleton [5], Veldhuizen's Leapfrog Triejoin [4], and systems like EmptyHeaded [6] is one of the cleanest theory-to-practice pipelines in database research. Its central insight: *the shape of the plan was the problem*. Joining variable-at-a-time with min-property intersections bounds runtime by the largest possible output — and the AGM bound shows no algorithm can do better in the worst case.

The frontier is now threefold. First, *finer-grained optimality*: bounds parameterized by projection sizes [4], degree sequences, and functional dependencies, with algorithms that adapt to the instance. Second, *richer query languages*: extending entropic and geometric techniques to aggregations, inequalities, and recursion [8]. Third, *hardware-conscious WCOJ*: SIMD, GPU, and distributed realizations that preserve the guarantee while winning the constant-factor war of real deployments. Shearer's lemma remains the sharpest tool we have; the task ahead is to aim it at a wider target.
