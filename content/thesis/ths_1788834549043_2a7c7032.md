# From Pairings to Folding: A Comparative Treatise on zk-SNARKs for Verifiable Computation — Groth16, PLONK's Universal Updatable SRS and Custom Gates, and Halo2-Style Recursion via Accumulation Schemes

## Abstract

Zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (zk-SNARKs) have matured from a theoretical curiosity into the cryptographic substrate of verifiable computation, powering succinct blockchains, privacy-preserving rollups, and outsourced computing with integrity. This thesis presents a unified comparative analysis of three landmark construction families: Groth16's pairing-based proofs, which remain the gold standard for proof size (three group elements) and verifier work (three pairings) at the cost of a per-circuit trusted setup [1]; PLONK's permutation-over-Lagrange-bases framework, which replaces the circuit-specific structured reference string (SRS) with a single universal and updatable SRS while enabling custom gates and lookup arguments that radically compress arithmetization [2]; and Halo 2's recursive composition paradigm, which eliminates trusted setup entirely through inner-product-based accumulation schemes over two-adic curves [3]. We trace the full pipeline from arithmetization — contrasting rank-1 constraint systems with Plonkish tables — through polynomial commitments, trusted-setup ceremonies, and concrete prover/verifier asymptotics, and close with a limitations audit covering toxic-waste ceremonies, quantum vulnerability, and recursion overhead.

## 1 Introduction

*Verifiable computation* lets a weak client delegate an expensive computation to an untrusted server and receive, with the result, a short certificate of correctness. The cryptographic object realizing this ambition, the zk-SNARK, achieves three properties simultaneously that long seemed mutually incompatible: **succinctness** (proofs short and verification fast regardless of the computation's size), **non-interactivity** (a single message from prover to verifier), and **zero-knowledge** (the proof leaks nothing about private inputs) [1].

The field crystallized along two axes. The first is *arithmetization*: translating programs into algebraic constraints. **Rank-1 constraint systems (R1CS)** encode computation as bilinear equations $(\mathbf{A}w)(\mathbf{B}w) = (\mathbf{C}w)$ over a witness vector $w$ [1], underpinning Groth16. **Plonkish arithmetization** instead encodes computation as a *table* of wire assignments with custom gate constraints and copy constraints via permutation polynomials [2] — the native language of PLONK, UltraPLONK, and Halo 2.

The second axis is the *proving system*: the machinery turning a satisfied arithmetization into a succinct proof. The landscape splits into pairing-based systems with structured reference strings (Groth16 [1], PLONK [2]) and systems avoiding trusted setup via discrete-logarithm techniques (Halo [4], Halo 2 [3], Nova [5]) — a spectrum of trade-offs in proof size, prover/verifier time, and trust assumptions, the central subject of this work.

> **Theorem:** (Informal.) No known zk-SNARK simultaneously achieves constant-size proofs, constant-time verification, universal updatable setup, transparent setup, and post-quantum security. Each construction sacrifices at least one; Sections 4–6 formalize the trade-off surface.

This thesis makes the following contributions:

1. **A unified pipeline model** decomposing every construction into five stages — arithmetization, polynomial relation, commitment, challenges, verification — pinpointing where Groth16, PLONK, and Halo 2 differ.
2. **An arithmetization census** quantifying the R1CS/Plonkish gap: custom gates and lookups cut constraint counts by an order of magnitude for bit-heavy circuits, at the price of larger FFT domains.
3. **A literature-grounded cost model** for prover work (group exponentiations), verifier work (pairings), and communication (group elements), validated against reported benchmarks.
4. **A ceremony audit** of powers-of-tau multi-party setups and the updatability contract.
5. **A recursion synthesis** showing how Halo 2 accumulates without pairings and how Nova's folding generalizes it to IVC with minimal overhead.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Interactive proofs and the road to succinctness

Succinct proof systems trace to interactive proofs [6], probabilistically checkable proofs, and Micali's CS proofs [7], which showed any NP computation admits a proof verifiable in polylogarithmic time given a random oracle. Kilian's protocol [8] introduced *knowledge soundness* — the formal notion that a prover producing a valid proof must "know" a witness. The Fiat–Shamir heuristic [9] converts public-coin interactive protocols into non-interactive ones in the random oracle model, a transformation on which every deployed zk-SNARK relies.

### 2.2 Bilinear pairings and the algebraic setting

Pairing-based SNARKs work over a *bilinear group* $(p, \mathbb{G}_1, \mathbb{G}_2, \mathbb{G}_T, e, g_1, g_2)$ where $e: \mathbb{G}_1 \times \mathbb{G}_2 \to \mathbb{G}_T$ is bilinear, non-degenerate, and efficiently computable [10]. Practical instantiations use BN254/alt-bn128 or BLS12-381 curves; recursion demands two-cycles, where one curve's scalar field is the other's base field — the trick exploited by Halo 2's Pallas/Vesta curves [3]. Most pairing-based SNARKs are proven secure in the generic or algebraic group model rather than under falsifiable standard assumptions [1][2].

### 2.3 The quadratic arithmetic program

Groth16's route passes through the **quadratic arithmetic program (QAP)** of Gennaro et al. [11]: R1CS matrices are interpolated into polynomials so the witness is valid iff $A(x)B(x) - C(x) = H(x) \cdot Z(x)$ for target polynomial $Z(x)$. The prover's burden is computing $H(x)$ in $O(m \log m)$ via FFTs plus $O(m)$ group exponentiations from the SRS [1].

### 2.4 Polynomial commitments

A polynomial commitment scheme binds a prover to a polynomial $f$ via a short digest $C = \mathrm{Commit}(f)$, later proving $f(z) = y$ at a verifier-chosen $z$. The **KZG commitment** [12] — the workhorse of Groth16 and PLONK — commits as $C = g^{f(\tau)}$ under SRS $\{g^{\tau^i}\}$, with opening proof $g^{q(\tau)}$ for quotient $q(X) = (f(X)-f(z))/(X-z)$, verified by one pairing check:

$$e(C - g^{f(z)}, g) \stackrel{?}{=} e(\pi, g^{\tau - z}).$$

```python
# KZG opening check, additive notation (cf. [12])
assert pairing(C - f_z * G1, G2) == pairing(pi, tau_G2 - z * G2)
```

KZG yields constant-size commitments and constant-time verification but inherits the SRS's trusted setup [12]. Halo 2 instead uses an **inner-product-argument (IPA)**-based commitment (a descendant of Bulletproofs [13]), needing no trusted setup at the cost of logarithmic-size opening proofs — a cost recursion amortizes [3][4].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis follows a reproducible comparative method with four stages.

**Stage 1 — Pipeline decomposition.** We normalize each construction into the five-stage pipeline (arithmetize → polynomial relation → commit → challenge → verify) and tabulate the algebraic objects, the prover's dominant operations, and the verifier's equations per stage.

**Stage 2 — Cost accounting.** We extract prover complexity in group exponentiations, verifier complexity in pairings, and proof size in group/field elements from the primary sources [1][2][3][4][5], cross-checked against implementation benchmarks (arkworks, bellman, halo2, nova-snark) on comparable curves.

**Stage 3 — Arithmetization census (analytic).** We hand-count constraints for representative sub-circuits (SHA-256 compression, Merkle path authentication) under R1CS and Plonkish encodings to quantify the custom-gate and lookup advantage.

**Stage 4 — Threat-model audit.** We reconstruct powers-of-tau ceremony designs [14] and the updatability formalism, enumerating residual trust assumptions, failure modes, and post-quantum exposure.

Security claims use the cited papers' models (generic/algebraic group model plus random oracle); we flag where constructions lack proofs under standard falsifiable assumptions.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Groth16: the three-element optimum for pairing-based proofs

Groth16 [1] remains the reference point for *concrete* efficiency. From a circuit-specific SRS, the prover computes the QAP witness polynomials, evaluates them at the secret point $\tau$ via multi-exponentiation, and outputs exactly **three group elements** $\pi = (A, B, C) \in \mathbb{G}_1^2 \times \mathbb{G}_2$ — 200 bytes on BN254. Verification is one equation:

$$e(A, B) = e(\alpha, \beta) \cdot e\!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\ell} a_i \cdot \tfrac{\beta u_i(\tau) + \alpha v_i(\tau) + w_i(\tau)}{\gamma}, \gamma\right) \cdot e(C, \delta),$$

requiring **three pairings** plus $\ell$ $\mathbb{G}_1$ scalar multiplications for $\ell$ public inputs [1]. Groth's analysis shows that under the linear-only and knowledge assumptions in play, two $\mathbb{G}_1$ elements and one $\mathbb{G}_2$ element are essentially optimal — no known pairing-based SNARK is smaller [1]. The prover's cost is dominated by three multi-exponentiations of size $O(m)$ plus the QAP-division FFT in $O(m \log m)$ field operations. Circuits of $2^{20}$ constraints prove in tens of seconds on modern multicore hardware; verification takes roughly 1–3 ms — fast enough for on-chain verification, which is why Groth16 dominates deployed rollups [1].

The price is the **circuit-specific trusted setup**: a fresh SRS per circuit, whose toxic waste (the secret $\tau$) must be destroyed. Multi-party ceremonies distribute this trust — security holds if *at least one* participant is honest — but each circuit change invalidates the SRS and demands a new ceremony. For rapidly iterating applications this is operationally punishing, motivating the entire universal-SRS research line.

### 4.2 PLONK: universality, updatability, and the permutation argument

PLONK [2] reframes the problem: instead of R1CS-then-QAP, the circuit becomes *polynomial identities over a multiplicative subgroup* $H = \{1, \omega, \dots, \omega^{n-1}\}$. Wire polynomials $a(X), b(X), c(X)$ interpolate each gate's left, right, and output values; selector polynomials $q_M(X)$, $q_C(X)$ mark multiplication gates and constants. ```rust
// PLONK gate identity evaluated by the verifier at challenge zeta [2]
let gate = q_m * a * b - c + q_c;
assert!(gate == F::ZERO);
```
The gate constraint reads:

$$q_M(X) \cdot a(X) \cdot b(X) - c(X) + q_C(X) = 0 \quad \text{on } H.$$

Copy constraints — "the output of gate $i$ equals the input of gate $j$" — are enforced by the **permutation argument**: the prover commits to an accumulator polynomial $z(X)$ implementing a grand product over the permuted wire values, adapted from Bayer–Groth techniques to Lagrange bases [2]. The verifier checks the permutation identity at a random challenge, reducing all wiring correctness to a handful of polynomial identities.

The crucial structural win is the **universal updatable SRS**. Because the SRS is just monomial powers $\{g^{\tau^i}\}$ independent of any circuit, a single ceremony serves *all* circuits up to a bounded size, and any party can update it with security reducing to the honesty of at least one updater across the entire history [2]. PLONK proofs are larger — roughly 400–500 bytes, verification 2–4 ms — but the operational model (one ceremony, ever) transformed deployment economics and underlies Aztec's and many zkEVM proving stacks.

**Custom gates and lookups.** *Custom gates* are higher-arity polynomial constraints over wire columns (one constraint per Poseidon round or EC addition); *lookup arguments* (Plookup [15], UltraPLONK/PlonkUp) prove wire tuples appear in a precomputed table. SHA-256 compression drops from ~27,000 R1CS constraints to a few thousand Plonkish rows with XOR and range-check tables — an order-of-magnitude win. Halo 2 adds *chip*-level abstractions: reusable gate/table layouts with region-based assignment APIs [3].

### 4.3 Halo 2 and the accumulation-scheme path to recursion

Recursion — proofs verifying other proofs — enables *incrementally verifiable computation* (IVC): a long computation splits into steps, each step's proof attests to the previous proof plus one more step, and the final proof stays succinct [4]. The obstacle for pairing-based SNARKs was the *cycle problem*: verifying a proof in-circuit requires expressing the verifier's curve arithmetic over the proof system's scalar field, efficient only when two curves form a cycle [4].

**Halo** [4] sidestepped pairings, building recursion from the discrete-logarithm-based IPA: proofs are *accumulated* rather than verified in-circuit. An **accumulation scheme** reduces checking two proof instances to checking one *accumulator* — weaker and cheaper than a full proof — so the recursion circuit checks accumulation, not verification. Halo 2 [3] industrialized this: Plonkish arithmetization plus an IPA commitment over the **Pasta curves** (Pallas/Vesta), a high-2-adicity cycle built for recursion, with nested-amortization accumulation.

**Nova** [5] abstracted the idea into **folding schemes**: folding two instances of relaxed R1CS into one, with a *constant-size* recursion circuit dominated by just two group scalar multiplications — the smallest recursion overhead in the literature — and per-step prover work of two multiexponentiations of size $O(|F|)$. Nova needs no trusted setup and no FFTs, and compresses the IVC proof to $O(\log |F|)$ elements with a final Spartan-based SNARK. Together, Halo 2 and Nova show large-scale verifiable computation is *recursive and transparent*, trading Groth16's constant-size proofs for arbitrarily long computations with bounded memory.

### 4.4 Arithmetization: R1CS versus Plonkish — a quantitative census

| Dimension | R1CS (Groth16 route) | Plonkish (PLONK / Halo 2 route) |
|---|---|---|
| Native constraint shape | $(\mathbf{A}w) \cdot (\mathbf{B}w) = (\mathbf{C}w)$ | Custom polynomial identities over wire columns + permutation argument |
| Wiring / copy constraints | Implicit in shared variable indices | Explicit permutation polynomial $z(X)$ (grand product) |
| Bit operations (XOR, AND) | Expensive: bit-decompose, ~1 constraint/bit | Cheap: lookup tables, ~1 row per several bits [15] |
| Hash (SHA-256 compression) | ~27k constraints | ~2–4k rows with lookups |
| Hash (Poseidon, SNARK-friendly) | ~200–300 constraints/round | ~1 custom-gate row per round |
| Prover FFT domain | Size $\approx m$ (constraints) | Size $\approx n$ (rows), often padded for lookup tables |
| Proof size | 3 group elements (~200 B) [1] | 7–13 group elements (~400–600 B) [2][3] |
| Setup | Per-circuit trusted | Universal updatable (PLONK) or transparent (Halo 2) |

The verdict is nuanced. R1CS is unbeatable for *proof size and verifier time per proof* — nothing beats Groth16's three elements. Plonkish wins on *prover ergonomics and constraint efficiency* for bit-heavy circuits and is strictly more expressive (any R1CS embeds in Plonkish, while lookups and custom gates have no compact R1CS analogue). Its hidden cost is prover-side: wider tables mean larger FFT domains and more commitments per proof (Section 5).

### 4.5 Trusted-setup ceremonies: powers of tau and the updatability contract

A structured reference string $\{g^{\tau^i}, h^{\tau^i}\}$ is only as trustworthy as the destruction of $\tau$. The **powers-of-tau** ceremony [14] runs an indefinite multi-party computation: participant $i$ re-randomizes the SRS with fresh randomness $r_i$ and publishes a zero-knowledge proof of correct exponentiation; the final secret $\tau = \prod r_i$ is unrecoverable unless *every* participant colludes. Ethereum's 2023 KZG ceremony scaled this to tens of thousands of contributors.

PLONK's **updatability** strengthens the contract: the circuit-independent SRS can be updated at any time, each update re-randomizing the trapdoor so security depends on at least one honest contributor across the *entire history* [2]. This converts trusted setup from a per-deployment liability into a shared public good. Halo 2 and Nova need no SRS at all (IPA and folding rest on the plain discrete-logarithm assumption), eliminating the ceremony — at the cost of logarithmic proof sizes and heavier verification. The audit conclusion: *updatable universal SRS is the pragmatic sweet spot for deployed systems today; transparent recursion is the research frontier.*

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Analytic cost model

We model prover time as $T_p = c_1 \cdot \mathrm{MSM}_{\mathbb{G}}(n) + c_2 \cdot n \log n$ and verifier time as pairings plus scalar multiplications, consolidating the primary sources [1][2][3][4][5]:

| System | Proof size | Verifier work | Prover work (dominant) | Setup |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Groth16 [1] | 3 G (~200 B) | 3 pairings + O(l) G1 | O(m) MSM + QAP FFT | Per-circuit trusted |
| PLONK [2] | ~9 G + F (~450 B) | ~5 pairings + O(l) | O(n) MSM + permutation FFT | Universal updatable |
| Halo 2 [3] | O(log n) G (~2–5 kB) | O(log n) G ops | O(n) MSM + IPA folding | Transparent |
| Nova [5] | O(|F|) folded; O(log|F|) compressed | O(log|F|) | 2 MSMs of size O(|F|)/step | Transparent |

*G/F = group/field element; $m$ = R1CS constraints, $n$ = Plonkish rows, $l$ = public inputs.*

### 5.2 Empirical validation against reported benchmarks

Benchmarks corroborate the model. Groth16: ~$0.5$–$2$ $\mu$s per constraint proving, $1$–$3$ ms verification [1]. PLONK: roughly $2$–$5\times$ slower per gate (permutation argument plus wider FFT domain), verification under $5$ ms [2]. Halo 2 is another factor slower per row (IPA constants) but recurses beyond single-circuit memory limits, where non-recursive systems cannot compete [3]. Nova's folding step is dominated by exactly two multiexponentiations, as predicted [5].

### 5.3 A succinctness lower-bound sketch

> **Theorem:** (Proof-size barrier, informal.) In the algebraic group model with a $q$-type knowledge assumption, no pairing-based zk-SNARK for circuit satisfiability achieves proofs of fewer than $3$ group elements with a *fully generic* verifier (one whose verification equation is independent of the circuit beyond public inputs). *Proof sketch.* Groth [1] shows the verification equation must simultaneously bind the witness polynomials ($A, B$), enforce QAP divisibility ($C$/$H$), and remain simulatable for zero-knowledge; a degrees-of-freedom counting argument on the generic pairing-product equation forces at least two $\mathbb{G}_1$ and one $\mathbb{G}_2$ element. PLONK's larger proofs are consistent with the bound: its verifier is not fully generic (selector commitments enter the equation), trading size for universality.

### 5.4 Recursion overhead comparison

| Scheme | Recursion circuit size | Per-step prover | Needs cycle of curves | Trusted setup |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Halo (IPA accumulation) [4] | O(log n) constraints | O(n) MSM | No (single curve + IPA) | No |
| Halo 2 [3] | O(log n) + Plonkish overhead | O(n) MSM | Yes (Pasta) | No |
| Nova folding [5] | **Constant** (2 scalar muls) | 2 MSMs of O(|F|) | Yes (curve cycle) | No |

Nova's constant-size recursion circuit makes the marginal step cost essentially the cost of proving $F$ itself [5].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Toxic waste is managed, not solved.** Universal updatable SRS constructions shrink the ceremony to a single shared event, but security requires at least one honest participant *forever* — including future updaters. A compromised SRS retroactively breaks soundness of every proof verified against it. Transparent systems (Halo 2, Nova) eliminate this failure class entirely, at measurable efficiency cost [3][5].

2. **No post-quantum security.** Every construction surveyed rests on discrete-logarithm-type assumptions that Shor's algorithm voids. All Section 5 figures assume ~128-bit classical security; lattice- or hash-based migration would change them by orders of magnitude. zk-STARKs offer the post-quantum alternative today, at ~100 kB proof sizes [13].

3. **Knowledge assumptions are non-falsifiable.** Groth16 and PLONK proofs invoke knowledge-of-exponent-type assumptions in idealized models (GGM/AGM) that cannot be efficiently falsified even in principle, with a history of subtle breaks in adjacent schemes. Conservative deployments should track this as residual risk [1][2].

4. **Arithmetization is the human bottleneck.** Under-constrained circuits — missing constraints admitting malicious witnesses — cause more real-world vulnerabilities than breaks of the underlying cryptography. Formal verification of circuits is nascent [3].

5. **Recursion overhead is real even when asymptotically constant.** Nova's recursion circuit is minimal, but the *folded* witness grows linearly in steps until compressed, and the final compression SNARK adds latency. Halo 2 recursion demands the Pasta cycle's nonstandard curves, complicating BN254/BLS12-381 interoperability [3][5].

6. **Prover scalability walls.** All surveyed provers are quasilinear at best; circuits beyond $2^{26}$–$2^{28}$ constraints strain memory (tens of GB) on commodity hardware. Recursive composition and hardware acceleration mitigate but do not remove this wall.

## 7 Conclusion

The decade from Groth16 to Nova traces a clear arc: from *minimal proofs* (three group elements, three pairings) through *minimal trust ceremonies* (one universal updatable SRS) to *minimal recursion overhead* (constant-size folding circuits with no setup at all) [1][2][5]. No single construction dominates; the engineering choice is a point on the trade-off surface mapped here. When proof size and on-chain verification cost are paramount and circuits are stable, Groth16's per-circuit ceremony remains rational. When circuits iterate rapidly, PLONK's universal SRS with custom gates and lookups is the pragmatic default. When computation is unbounded — blockchains succinct forever, proofs of proofs ad infinitum — the recursive transparent systems, Halo 2's accumulation and Nova's folding, are the only viable path [3][4][5].

Three open problems stand out. First, *post-quantum succinctness*: a transparent, plausibly post-quantum SNARK with sub-10 kB proofs. Second, *verified arithmetization*: toolchains proving circuits correct by construction, eliminating under-constrained bugs. Third, *standard-model succinctness*: SNARKs under falsifiable assumptions without idealized models — a goal resisting a decade of effort. The machinery surveyed here — pairings, permutation arguments, polynomial commitments, folding — is the vocabulary in which those answers will be written.
