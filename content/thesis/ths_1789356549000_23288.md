# Full-Waveform Seismic Inversion: The Adjoint-State Method, Multiscale Strategies, and Imaging Earth's Interior

## Abstract

Full-waveform inversion (FWI) is the most complete seismic imaging method yet devised: rather than extracting a handful of traveltimes or amplitude attributes from seismograms, it fits the entire recorded wavefield by solving a PDE-constrained optimization problem in which the wave equation itself is the constraint. The enabling mathematical technology is the *adjoint-state method*, independently formulated by Lailly [3] and Tarantola [1,2], which computes the gradient of the data misfit with respect to every model parameter at the cost of roughly two wavefield simulations, regardless of how many millions of parameters are inverted. This article develops the adjoint formalism from first principles, explains why the gradient factorizes into a zero-lag cross-correlation of forward and adjoint wavefields, analyzes the central pathology of *cycle skipping* that makes the misfit landscape non-convex, and surveys the multiscale frequency-continuation strategies — from Bunks et al. [3] to extended-source and optimal-transport formulations [6,7] — that restore convergence toward the global minimum. We review empirical landmarks including the Marmousi and BP salt benchmarks, the GPU-accelerated variable-grid implementations [8], and the Southern California crustal adjoint tomography of Tape et al. [4], which consumed 6800 simulations and 0.8 million CPU-hours to reveal basin structures invisible to traveltime tomography. Finally, we confront the method's limits: anisotropy, attenuation, multi-parameter cross-talk, and computational cost.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The forward problem

Seismic wave propagation in an acoustic medium is governed by the constant- or variable-density wave equation. In the variable-density acoustic approximation, the pressure field u(x, t) satisfies

$$ \frac{1}{\kappa(\mathbf{x})} \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} - \nabla \cdot \left( \frac{1}{\rho(\mathbf{x})} \nabla u \right) = s(\mathbf{x}, t), $$

where \kappa is bulk modulus, \rho is density, and s is the source. In exploration practice the density is often held fixed and the model is parameterized by wavespeed c(\mathbf{x}), reducing the equation to

$$ \frac{1}{c(\mathbf{x})^2} \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2 u = s(\mathbf{x}, t). $$

For elastic media — the setting of global seismology — the displacement vector obeys the elastodynamics system with spatially varying Lamé parameters and density, optionally including viscoelastic attenuation [1]. Solving these equations numerically for each source, with absorbing boundary conditions and a free-surface condition, is the *forward problem*: given the model m, compute synthetic data d = F(m) at the receiver positions.

Forward modeling is the beating heart of FWI and its dominant cost. Finite-difference time-domain schemes, spectral-element methods (the engine behind continental-scale tomography [4]), and frequency-domain direct solvers are all in use; GPU acceleration has made 3D time-domain modeling routine [8].

### 2.2 The inverse problem: PDE-constrained optimization

Let m denote the vector of model parameters (wavespeed at every grid node, for example), d_obs the observed seismograms, and F the forward modeling operator. FWI seeks

$$ J(\mathbf{m}) = \frac{1}{2} \, \| \mathbf{d}_{obs} - F(\mathbf{m}) \|^2 \rightarrow \min, $$

subject to the constraint that the wavefield satisfies the wave equation for the given m and source. This is a *PDE-constrained* optimization problem: the misfit is an ordinary function of m, but evaluating it requires solving the wave equation, and the constraint is a partial differential equation in millions of unknowns.

Three properties make this problem ferociously hard. First, it is *highly nonlinear*: the operator F contains multiple scattering, so a small model perturbation can shift waveforms in phase and create entirely new arrivals. Second, it is *ill-posed*: surface data constrain the model unevenly, and infinitely many models can fit band-limited data almost equally well, so regularization is essential. Third, the misfit functional is *non-convex* with an enormous number of local minima, which is why the gradient must be paired with strategies that enlarge its basin of attraction [3,5].

### 2.3 Born approximation and migration: what FWI generalizes

Classical imaging linearizes F around a smooth background model. Under the *Born approximation*, the scattered field is linear in the model perturbation, and the Fréchet derivative (the Jacobian of F) maps a point perturbation to a scattered waveform; its adjoint maps data residuals back to the model — this is *migration* [2]. Least-squares migration iterates this linear machinery. FWI keeps the adjoint machinery but refuses the linearization: the background model is updated at every iteration, so the Fréchet derivative is re-evaluated in the current model and the inversion escapes the Born regime to handle arbitrarily strong scattering [6]. Migration images the short wavelengths (reflectivity); FWI reconstructs the full spectrum, from the long-wavelength background that kinematics demand to the short-wavelength detail that amplitudes supply.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The adjoint-state gradient in one paragraph

The gradient of J with respect to m can be obtained without ever forming the Jacobian. One introduces a Lagrange multiplier field \lambda(\mathbf{x}, t) — the *adjoint field* — and derives from the Lagrangian an *adjoint wave equation* that has exactly the same differential operator as the forward equation but is driven by a source built from the data residuals, injected at the receiver positions and propagated *backwards in time* [1,7]. The gradient is then the zero-lag cross-correlation in time of the forward field and the adjoint field, weighted by the appropriate parameter-dependent coefficients and summed over all sources. Two simulations per source yield the full gradient: one forward, one adjoint. This is the content of the adjoint-state theorem proved in Section 4.1.

### 3.2 The FWI iteration

The practical algorithm, as implemented in virtually every modern code [2,8], proceeds as follows:

1. **Forward modeling.** For each source, solve the wave equation in the current model m_k and store (or recompute) the wavefield u.
2. **Misfit evaluation.** Compute synthetic traces at receivers and form the residual r = d_obs − d_syn, then evaluate J(m_k).
3. **Adjoint modeling.** Back-propagate the residuals as sources in the adjoint wave equation to obtain \lambda.
4. **Gradient assembly.** Cross-correlate u and \lambda at every model point, weighted by 2/(c^3) (for the wavespeed parameterization) or the corresponding Fréchet kernels for density and elastic moduli; sum over sources and time.
5. **Optimization step.** Feed the gradient to a quasi-Newton method — L-BFGS is the workhorse [6,7] — or a nonlinear conjugate-gradient scheme; perform a line search for the step length \alpha.
6. **Model update.** Set m_{k+1} = m_k − \alpha \, H^{−1} \nabla J and iterate until the misfit stagnates or a convergence criterion is met.

Preconditioning and regularization (smoothing, total-variation penalties, prior-model weighting [5]) are applied to the gradient before the update; the Hessian is approximated, rarely computed.

### 3.3 Why the adjoint method wins

The economics are decisive. With N model parameters and S sources, a finite-difference gradient costs roughly N wave simulations; the adjoint gradient costs about 2S, independent of N [7]. When N is in the millions and S in the hundreds or thousands, this is the difference between impossible and routine. Furthermore, because the adjoint equation reuses the same wave-equation machinery (and, in the frequency domain, the same matrix factorization via multiple right-hand sides [5]), the implementation overhead is modest. Automatic differentiation has recently been shown to be mathematically equivalent to the adjoint-state method and provides an alternative implementation route [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The adjoint-state gradient: a rigorous derivation

The adjoint-state method originated in optimal control theory (Lions, 1971) and was imported into geophysics by Lailly and Tarantola [3]. We sketch the derivation for the acoustic wavespeed problem; the elastodynamic case follows the same pattern with more indices [1].

Consider the Lagrangian that couples the misfit to the PDE constraint:

$$ \mathcal{L}(u, \mathbf{m}, \lambda) = J(\mathbf{m}) + \int_0^T \!\!\int_\Omega \lambda(\mathbf{x},t)\, \left[ \frac{1}{c^2} \partial_{tt} u - \nabla^2 u - s \right] d\mathbf{x}\, dt, $$

where \Omega is the spatial domain and (0, T) the recording interval. Because the constraint vanishes for the exact forward field, \mathcal{L} = J along feasible trajectories. Taking variations and integrating by parts twice in time and once in space transfers all differential operators from \delta u onto \lambda, producing boundary and initial terms that vanish under appropriate conditions — in particular, \lambda must satisfy *final* conditions \lambda(T) = \partial_t \lambda(T) = 0, so the adjoint equation is solved *backwards* in time [1].

> **Theorem (Adjoint-state gradient).** Let u be the solution of the forward wave equation with wavespeed c, and let \lambda solve the adjoint equation driven by the data residual r at the receivers, integrated backwards from time T to 0. Then the gradient of the least-squares misfit with respect to the wavespeed field is
>
> $$ \nabla_c J(\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{2}{c(\mathbf{x})^3} \sum_{s=1}^{S} \int_0^T \partial_t u_s(\mathbf{x},t)\, \partial_t \lambda_s(\mathbf{x},t)\, dt, $$
>
> i.e., the gradient at each point is (minus) the zero-lag cross-correlation of the forward and adjoint *particle-velocity* fields, scaled by 2/c^3 and summed over sources. No Jacobian is ever formed.

A complete, rigorous derivation for the visco-elastodynamic case — including the subtle effect of attenuation on the adjoint initial and boundary conditions, and extensions to gradients with respect to boundary parameters and initial conditions — has recently been supplied, closing a long-standing gap in the literature [1]. The theorem's practical reading is a physical one: the forward field carries energy from sources into the medium, the adjoint field carries the residual *information* backwards from the receivers, and where the two coincide in space and time, the model must change. This cross-correlation structure also explains FWI's famous *rabbit-ears* artifacts: spurious correlations generate high-wavenumber noise, which is why gradient smoothing and preconditioning are standard [2].

```python
# Pseudocode: adjoint-state gradient for one source (acoustic, constant density)
def fwi_gradient(model, source, receivers, d_obs, T):
    u = solve_forward(model, source, T)          # forward wavefield, all times
    d_syn = sample_at_receivers(u, receivers)
    residual = d_obs - d_syn                     # adjoint source at receivers
    lam = solve_adjoint(model, residual, receivers, T)  # backwards in time
    grad = zeros_like(model)
    for t in time_steps(T):
        # zero-lag cross-correlation of time derivatives
        grad += time_derivative(u, t) * time_derivative(lam, t)
    return -2.0 / model**3 * grad
```

### 4.2 Cycle skipping: the central pathology

Even with the exact gradient, FWI can fail catastrophically. Suppose the starting model's predicted traveltime for some event differs from the observed traveltime by more than half a period of the dominant frequency. The least-squares misfit then *decreases* by shifting the predicted event toward the *wrong* observed cycle — the update moves the model away from the truth, and the iteration converges to a geologically meaningless local minimum [5]. This is *cycle skipping*, and it is the single most important reason FWI requires good starting models.

Formally, the basin of attraction of the global minimum shrinks as frequency rises: at frequency f, the tolerable traveltime error is approximately 1/(2f). At 5 Hz the tolerance is 100 ms; at 50 Hz it is 10 ms — far below the accuracy of any traveltime tomography used to build starting models [3,5]. Cycle skipping is therefore not a bug in the optimizer but an intrinsic property of the least-squares waveform misfit: the objective oscillates with the phase, and gradient descent follows the nearest valley, not the deepest one.

The phenomenon is vividly demonstrated on the Marmousi benchmark: starting from a heavily smoothed model, single-frequency-band least-squares FWI at exploration frequencies becomes trapped, recovering only shallow structure while the deeper, complex faulted zones are replaced by artifacts [3,5].

### 4.3 Multiscale continuation: climbing down the frequency ladder

The classical remedy, proposed by Bunks et al.

When field data lack usable low frequencies (marine streamers routinely record nothing useful below about 3–5 Hz), the ladder has no bottom rung.

The practical consensus in industry today is a hybrid: begin with traveltime tomography or envelope inversion for the background, continue through escalating frequency bands [3], and finish with elastic, anisotropic least-squares FWI for the final detail [2,8].

### 4.4 High-performance computing: from CPU-centuries to GPUs

FWI is among the most computationally demanding algorithms in all of science, and its history is inseparable from the history of supercomputing.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmarks: Marmousi and the BP salt model

The community validates FWI on shared synthetic benchmarks. The *Marmousi* model — a structurally complex 2D section with growth faults, anticlinal traps, and strong lateral velocity variation — has been the canonical test since the 1990s; modern FWI recovers its fault blocks and stratigraphy from a smooth starting model when multiscale continuation is used, and fails instructively when it is not [6]. The *2004 BP* salt model adds the harder problem of high-contrast salt bodies: conventional least-squares FWI from a sediment-only starting model is trapped in local minima, while extended-source formulations reconstruct the salt geometry correctly, demonstrating the enlarged basin of attraction [6].

### 5.2 Field scale: exploration successes

In hydrocarbon exploration, FWI has moved from research to production. Anisotropic 3D FWI is now routinely applied to wide-azimuth marine surveys, resolving shallow gas anomalies, channels, and fault shadows that traveltime tomography smears beyond recognition [2]. Land FWI, long considered impractical because of elastic complexity and poor signal, has been demonstrated on real datasets. The common thread in every success story is the multiscale recipe of Section 4.3: no production result begins at full bandwidth [2,3].

### 5.3 Continental scale: imaging Southern California

The landmark academic application remains the adjoint tomography of the Southern California crust by Tape, Liu, Maggi, and Tromp [4]. Using 16 iterations of adjoint-based waveform fitting — 6800 spectral-element simulations, 0.8 million CPU-hours — they built a 3D crustal model revealing ±30% wavespeed heterogeneity relative to the traveltime-tomography starting model: sedimentary basins, sharp velocity contrasts across faults, and fragments of subduction-captured oceanic crust, all invisible to ray-based methods. The model demonstrably improves synthetic seismogram fits and has propagated into seismic-hazard assessment for the Los Angeles basin [4]. It remains the clearest proof that the adjoint formalism scales from theory to continent-spanning practice.

## 6 Limitations

Honesty requires stating plainly what FWI cannot yet do.

1. **Anisotropy and attenuation are second-class citizens.** Most production FWI is acoustic and isotropic; elastic, anisotropic, visco-elastic FWI multiplies the parameter count and the cross-talk between parameters (a wavespeed error can masquerade as an attenuation anomaly and vice versa) [1,2].
2. **Multi-parameter cross-talk.** Simultaneously inverting velocity, density, and anisotropy parameters suffers from null spaces and trade-offs; rock-physics constraints and hierarchical strategies mitigate but do not eliminate the problem [3].
3. **The low-frequency gap.** Without low frequencies or an excellent starting model, cycle skipping is unavoidable for band-limited data; envelope and extended-source methods help but add their own tuning parameters [5,6].
4. **Cost.** 3D elastic FWI remains a supercomputer-scale undertaking; the Hessian is essentially never formed, so uncertainty quantification is rudimentary [2,8].
5. **Source and physics errors.** An incorrect source wavelet, unmodeled attenuation, or 3D effects in 2D inversions all map into spurious model structure; the inversion fits the data with whatever degrees of freedom it is given [2].
6. **Non-uniqueness.** Regularization selects one Earth from many that fit the data; different regularizers give different Earths, and the choice is ultimately geological, not mathematical [3].

## 7 Conclusion

Full-waveform inversion is the closest geophysics has come to *seeing* the Earth's interior directly: a mathematically principled, computationally heroic procedure that fits every wiggle of the observed wavefield by solving the wave equation inside an optimization loop. Its engine, the adjoint-state method of Lailly and Tarantola, is one of the great algorithmic ideas of applied mathematics — a gradient for millions of parameters at the price of two simulations, derived from nothing more than Lagrange duality and integration by parts [1,3,7]. Its nemesis, cycle skipping, is equally fundamental: a statement about the oscillatory geometry of the misfit landscape that no optimizer can wish away [5].

The arc of the field's progress runs from recognizing that nemesis to taming it — through Bunks' frequency continuation [3], through envelope and optimal-transport misfits [2,5], through extended-source relaxations [6], and through the GPU and automatic-differentiation revolutions that made the whole enterprise affordable [6,8]. The Southern California crustal model stands as the monument to what the mature method can achieve [4]: a continent's crust, imaged at wavelengths ray theory could never resolve, from nothing but the waveforms themselves.

The frontier now lies in the limitations of Section 6: true multi-parameter elastic inversion with honest uncertainty, learned misfits and learned regularizers, and global adjoint tomography at periods short enough to see the mantle's fine structure. The wave equation is exact; our ability to invert it is still catching up.
