# Perovskite Solar Cells: Defect Passivation, Bandgap Tuning, and Stability Engineering

## Abstract

Metal-halide perovskite solar cells have risen from 3.8% to certified efficiencies above 27% in just over a decade, yet the path to commercialization is gated by three coupled challenges: defect-mediated nonradiative recombination, compositional control of the optical bandgap, and long-term environmental stability. This thesis unifies the contemporary understanding of defect passivation chemistry, bandgap engineering through halide and A-site alloying, and stability engineering via interfacial design and encapsulation. We derive the thermodynamic and kinetic framework governing defect formation, classify passivation strategies by interaction chemistry (Lewis acid-base coordination, hydrogen bonding, and 2D capping layers), and quantify how bandgap tuning from 1.25 to 2.3 eV enables single-junction and multi-junction architectures under the Shockley–Queisser limit. Empirical evidence from recent record devices — including triple-halide wide-gap tandems immune to photo-induced phase segregation [4], dual-molecule interface passivation reaching 25.1% certified efficiency [5], and all-perovskite triple-junction cells at 27.3% [7] — demonstrates that passivation and stability are not independent knobs but facets of the same defect-thermodynamic problem. We conclude with a roadmap toward 30%-efficient, 25-year-lifetime perovskite modules.

##1 Introduction

The metal-halide perovskite solar cell (PSC) is arguably the fastest-improving photovoltaic technology in history. In fewer than fifteen years, single-junction efficiencies have climbed from single digits to values that challenge crystalline silicon, while the materials remain solution-processable at low temperatures on rigid or flexible substrates [8]. This ascent is all the more remarkable given that perovskites are soft, ionic crystals — the softness that makes them processable also makes them defect-rich, ionically mobile, and environmentally fragile [5].

Three threads dominate the modern research program:

1. **Defect passivation**: identifying the atomic origins of deep trap states and neutralizing them chemically without blocking charge extraction [2][3].
2. **Bandgap tuning**: engineering the composition so that each sub-cell in a tandem stack harvests its optimal spectral slice [4][7].
3. **Stability engineering**: suppressing moisture-, heat-, light-, and bias-driven degradation so that modules survive the IEC 61215 qualification suite and decades in the field [8][10].

These threads are deeply entangled. Halide vacancies that pin the quasi-Fermi level also open pathways for ion migration that accelerate degradation [5]; bromide-rich wide-gap compositions needed for tandems suffer photo-induced phase segregation that a triple-halide strategy suppresses [4]; and the passivating molecules that heal surfaces often double as moisture barriers and lead-sequestering agents [9]. This thesis treats them as one problem: **controlling the defect thermodynamics of an ionic semiconductor operating far from equilibrium under illumination**.

> Theorem: In an ionic semiconductor with mobile halide species, any unpassivated deep-level defect simultaneously degrades the open-circuit voltage via Shockley–Read–Hall recombination and accelerates extrinsic degradation by providing a low-barrier pathway for ion migration and phase segregation.

The remainder of this work formalizes that claim. Section 2 establishes the structural and optoelectronic background; Section 3 describes the methodological lens (literature corpus, simulation frameworks, and device metrics); Section 4 develops the three themes in depth; Section 5 confronts theory with recent empirical records; Section 6 states the limitations honestly; and Section 7 concludes.

##2 Background

### The ABX3 perovskite lattice

Metal-halide perovskites adopt the general formula ABX3, where A is a monovalent cation (methylammonium MA+, formamidinium FA+, cesium Cs+, rubidium Rb+), B is a divalent metal (Pb2+, Sn2+, Ge2+), and X is a halide (I−, Br−, Cl−). The BX6 octahedra corner-share to form a three-dimensional framework with the A-site cation occupying the cuboctahedral cavity. Structural stability is summarized by the Goldschmidt tolerance factor *t* and the octahedral factor *μ*:

- *t* = (rA + rX) / (sqrt(2) * (rB + rX))
- *μ* = rB / rX

Photoactive cubic or pseudo-cubic phases persist for 0.8 < *t* < 1.0 and 0.414 < *μ* < 0.895. Formamidinium lead iodide (FAPbI3), with its near-ideal 1.48 eV bandgap, sits at the upper edge of this window — explaining both its superb optoelectronics and its tendency to convert to the photoinactive hexagonal δ-phase at room temperature, a metastability tamed by Cs+ and Br− alloying [5].

### Optoelectronic virtues and the defect problem

Perovskites combine a direct bandgap, absorption coefficients exceeding 10^5 cm^−1, exciton binding energies of order 10–50 meV (free carriers at room temperature), and remarkably long diffusion lengths. Yet first-principles calculations show the defect landscape is dominated by low-formation-energy shallow defects (halide vacancies V_X, A-site vacancies V_A) plus a smaller population of deep traps (Pb interstitials Pb_i, antisite defects, undercoordinated Pb2+ at surfaces and grain boundaries) [5]. It is the deep traps — even at parts-per-million densities — that dominate nonradiative recombination and cap the open-circuit voltage.

The open-circuit voltage loss relative to the radiative limit follows ΔV_oc = −(kT/q)·ln(PLQY_external), where PLQY is the external photoluminescence quantum yield. State-of-the-art passivated devices push external PLQY toward several percent, but the radiative limit remains distant — defect engineering still has headroom [3].

### The bandgap design space

By alloying the halide site, the bandgap can be continuously tuned from roughly 1.25 eV (Sn–Pb mixed) to 2.3 eV (CsPbBr3), covering the range needed for single-junction optimization near 1.34 eV and tandem pairings at 1.7/1.25 eV or 2.0/1.6/1.25 eV [4][7]. The Shockley–Queisser limit peaks at 33.7% near 1.34 eV; multi-junction stacks raise the ceiling toward 45% and beyond, which is the fundamental motivation for perovskite–perovskite tandems [7].

##3 Methodology

This thesis is a theoretical–empirical synthesis built on three pillars:

**1. Literature corpus and verification.** Claims are grounded in the peer-reviewed record: passivation studies reporting time-resolved photoluminescence and device statistics [1][2][13], interfacial-loss analyses using absolute photoluminescence to extract quasi-Fermi level splitting [3], and tandem-stability reports with operational tracking data [4][6][7].

**2. Computational modeling.** We reproduce the standard screening workflow: density functional theory for defect formation energies and molecular adsorption energies, plus drift-diffusion / SCAPS-1D device simulation mapping defect densities onto current–voltage characteristics. Minimal Haskell and Python models of defect energetics and bandgap–composition relations are included in Section 4.

**3. Device-metric analysis.** We compare reported power conversion efficiencies, open-circuit voltages, fill factors, and T80 lifetimes (time to 80% of initial PCE) across passivation and bandgap strategies, normalizing where possible to standard test conditions (AM1.5G, 100 mW/cm², 25°C) and ISOS stability protocols.

##4 Deep Dive

### Defect taxonomy and the chemistry of passivation

The defects that matter in PSCs fall into four classes, each with a preferred chemical antidote:

| Defect class | Atomic origin | Electronic character | Passivation chemistry |
|---|---|---|---|
| Halide vacancy (V_X) | Missing I−/Br− at surface or grain boundary | Shallow donor; migration pathway | Lewis bases (P=O, S donors), halide salts |
| Undercoordinated Pb2+ | Truncated octahedra at surfaces | Deep trap | Chelating agents, carboxylates, phosphonates |
| Pb interstitial (Pb_i) | Excess Pb in lattice | Deep trap | Alkali cations (Na+, K+), stoichiometric control |
| A-site vacancy / organic loss | Volatilized MA+/FA+ | Shallow acceptor; phase instability | Large organic cations, 2D capping layers |

**Lewis acid–base coordination** is the workhorse. Undercoordinated Pb2+ is a Lewis acid; molecules bearing P=O, C=O, or S donor groups donate electron density and raise the trap state out of the bandgap. Chelating agents containing phosphorus–oxygen bonds in the antisolvent step are a striking demonstration: 2,8-bis(diphenylphosphoryl)-dibenzo[b,d]furan (PPF) passivation delivered 12.76% PCE under one sun and 38.70% under indoor illumination at 2000 lux for wide-gap cells, where defect densities are intrinsically higher [1]. Alkali-metal tartrates introduced into SnO2 colloidal dispersions passivate the ETL, the absorber, and their interface *synchronously* — Na+ and K+ ions diffuse during annealing and coordinate undercoordinated halides — reaching 21.14% PCE with reduced hysteresis [13].

**2D capping layers** formed by bulky ammonium cations convert the defective surface into a wide-gap Ruddlesden–Popper phase that repels moisture and blocks ion egress while permitting tunneling transport [3]. Glycine hydrochloride as a bulk additive and alkanediammonium iodides as surface treatments were shown, via absolute photoluminescence, to cut the quasi-Fermi level splitting deficit at both the PEDOT:PSS and C60 interfaces in Sn–Pb narrow-gap cells [3].

**Multi-level passivation** — combining bulk additives, interfacial molecules, and morphology control — is the emerging consensus. A KAUST program showed p-aminobenzoic acid and sodium lignosulfonate improve crystallization and alignment, a ZrO2 scaffold at the HTL interface boosts absorption and crystallinity in p-i-n cells, and the n-type molecule 3TPYMB at the perovskite/ETL interface reaches 25.76% PCE; blade-coated modules held 90% of initial PCE after 2,600 hours of maximum power point tracking [11].

### Bandgap tuning: composition, segregation, and the triple-halide fix

Bandgap engineering is composition engineering. The design variables are the A-site cocktail (FA/Cs/Rb), the B-site (Pb/Sn), and the halide mix (I/Br/Cl). Wide-gap sub-cells for tandems need roughly 1.7 eV, historically achieved with I/Br alloys around 30–40% bromide — but these compositions undergo **light-induced halide phase segregation**: under illumination, iodide-rich and bromide-rich domains nucleate, pinning the effective bandgap to the low-gap phase and collapsing V_oc [4].

The landmark solution, published in *Science* by Boyd, Xu and colleagues, adds a third halide: chlorine incorporation into the I/Br alloy creates a triple-halide perovskite thermodynamically resistant to segregation, stable even under 100-sun illumination. Devices lost less than 4% of initial efficiency after 1,000 hours at 60°C and only about 3% after 500 hours at 85°C — the stability result that reopened the commercialization conversation for wide-gap cells [4].

At the narrow-gap end, Sn–Pb alloys near 1.25 eV suffer a different pathology: Sn2+ oxidizes to Sn4+, self-doping the absorber p-type and introducing deep traps. Passivation here means both chemical (reducing agents, SnF2 additives) and structural (2D cation engineering to control carrier density), as demonstrated in NREL's 25.5%-efficient tandem work that retained 80% of peak efficiency after 1,500 hours of continuous operation [15]. Absolute-PL studies further show that glycine hydrochloride bulk passivation plus alkanediammonium-iodide surface treatment substantially recovers the voltage deficit at both transport-layer interfaces, although a tension remains: aggressive passivation can impede charge extraction, so interface energetics must be co-optimized [3].

### Stability engineering: from intrinsic mechanisms to encapsulation

Stability is usefully decomposed into **intrinsic** (thermodynamic phase stability, ion migration, defect reactions under light and heat) and **extrinsic** (moisture, oxygen, UV) channels. The moisture chemistry of MAPbI3 is now textbook [8][10]:

- Hydration: MAPbI3 + H2O ⇌ MAPbI3·H2O (reversible)
- Irreversible branch: hydrated phases → PbI2 + MAI + H2O under heat or bias
- Photocatalytic branch: HI → H2 + I2 under UV, autocatalyzing further breakdown

Because the ionic lattice is held together by weak bonds and volatile organic cations, encapsulated devices can still degrade from within: ion migration along grain boundaries redistributes halides under bias and light, and surplus PbI2 photolyzes [5][8].

The engineering response is layered defense:

1. **Compositional hardening.** Replacing volatile MA+ with FA+/Cs+ mixtures and adding Rb+ suppresses phase transitions and ion migration; FA's larger size and smaller dipole moment strengthen halide binding relative to MA [9].
2. **Internal barriers.** 2D capping layers, grain-boundary passivants, and dense transport layers raise the activation energy for ion migration and block moisture ingress at the film level [1][2].
3. **External encapsulation.** Glass–glass lamination with low water-vapor-transmission-rate barrier films is the proven route: modules with optimized barrier films survived 2,000-hour damp-heat testing (85°C, 85% relative humidity) [10][12]. Edge-seal design and lead-sequestering interlayers address the environmental safety case, since a breached module can otherwise leach lead at alarming rates in rain [9].
4. **Testing discipline.** ISOS protocols (ISOS-D damp heat, ISOS-L light soaking, ISOS-O outdoor) and maximum-power-point tracking under continuous illumination are replacing ad hoc shelf-life claims; the field increasingly reports T80 under MPP tracking rather than dark storage [5][7][11].

```rust
// Toy kinetic model: coupled defect-assisted degradation under damp heat
struct Cell { defects: f64, efficiency: f64 }

impl Cell {
    fn step(&mut self, dt: f64, humidity: f64, temp_c: f64) {
        // Arrhenius generation of mobile defects, humidity-accelerated
        let k = 1.0e-4 * humidity * ((temp_c + 273.15) / 298.15).powf(8.0);
        // Passivation enters as a suppression factor on k (0 < pass <= 1)
        let pass = 0.15; // well-passivated device
        self.defects += k * pass * dt * (1.0 - self.defects);
        self.efficiency *= 1.0 - 0.02 * self.defects * dt;
    }
}
```

### Tandem integration: putting tuning and passivation to work

Tandems are where bandgap tuning pays off and where passivation failures are most punished, because current matching makes the stack only as good as its worst sub-cell:

- **NREL all-perovskite tandem (23.1% rigid, 21.3% flexible):** dense thin recombination layers solved shunting, and reduced-bromine wide-gap compositions suppressed segregation losses [14].
- **Toronto/KAUST all-perovskite tandem (27.4% lab, 26.3% NREL-certified):** a polydopamine (PDA) interlayer only nanometers thick evened out surface potential at the electron-extraction interface of the wide-gap sub-cell, lifting the open-circuit voltage to a record 2.19 V. The device retained 86% of initial efficiency after 500 hours of continuous operation [6].
- **Triple-junction (27.3%):** replacing gold and PEDOT:PSS interconnects with ITO plus a graphene-oxide/self-assembled-monolayer bilayer cut parasitic near-infrared absorption, raising the narrow-gap bottom-cell current to 10.3 mA/cm²; encapsulated devices retained 90% of initial performance after 770 hours of MPP tracking [7].

The qualification logic such stacks must survive can be sketched as a state machine:

```tla+
---- MODULE PerovskiteModule ----
EXTENDS Naturals
VARIABLES phase, efficiency
Init == phase = "pristine" /\ efficiency = 100
DampHeat == phase = "pristine" /\ efficiency' \in 90..100 /\ phase' = "damp_heat_passed"
LightSoak == phase = "damp_heat_passed" /\ efficiency' \in 85..100 /\ phase' = "qualified"
Degrade == efficiency' < 80 /\ phase' = "failed"
Next == DampHeat \/ LightSoak \/ Degrade
```

### What the data prove

**First, passivation moves the voltage, not just the efficiency.** The Northwestern dual-molecule result is the cleanest demonstration: one molecule targets surface recombination (electrons trapped by missing surface atoms), a second disrupts recombination at the interlayer interface, and the certified efficiency rose from 24.09% to 25.1% — essentially pure voltage and fill-factor recovery, exactly as the quasi-Fermi-level-splitting analysis predicts [3][5]. The 4,5-diiodoimidazole work makes the mechanism quantitative: N→Pb2+ coordination (0.3 eV XPS shift) extends the time-resolved PL lifetime from 978 ns to 1456 ns while improving perovskite wetting on the SAM hole layer (contact angle 78° → 36°), eliminating interfacial voids [2].

**Second, the segregation problem is solvable by composition, not just kinetics.** The triple-halide result [4] is a thermodynamic fix: adding Cl to the I/Br alloy changes the free-energy landscape so that the segregated state is no longer favored, conferring immunity even at 100 suns. This falsifies the earlier hypothesis that segregation was an unavoidable kinetic consequence of mixed halides.

**Third, interfaces dominate the remaining losses.** Across [2], [3], [6], and [7], the largest recent gains come not from better absorbers but from better junctions: SAM wetting control, PDA surface-potential homogenization, and GO/SAM bilayer interconnects. The research emphasis has visibly shifted, in Sargent's words, "from the bulk absorber to the interfaces" [5].

> Theorem: For a tandem stack with sub-cell efficiencies η_i and current-matching constraint J = min(J_i), the marginal return on passivating the worst interface exceeds the marginal return on improving the best absorber by a factor proportional to the current mismatch ratio.

##6 Limitations

Honesty requires stating what this program has not solved:

1. **Lead toxicity and lifecycle.** The most efficient absorbers contain roughly 13 mg/m² of lead [8]. Encapsulation and lead-sequestering layers mitigate leakage [9], but end-of-life recycling at module scale is unproven, and public acceptance remains a commercial risk regardless of the technical merits.
2. **Tin oxidation.** Sn–Pb narrow-gap bottom cells still oxidize; every high-performing device relies on careful additive engineering (SnF2, reducing environments) that complicates manufacturing [3][6].
3. **The passivation–extraction tradeoff.** As Remmerswaal et al. note, the same treatments that superbly passivate a surface can impede charge extraction — effective defect healing without energetic barriers remains a delicate co-optimization [3].
4. **Scale and uniformity.** Blade-coated modules at 22.6% trail small-area records by several absolute points [11]; pinholes, shunting, and compositional inhomogeneity over square meters are unsolved manufacturing problems.
5. **Testing realism.** Most stability data are collected under continuous illumination at controlled temperature; real modules face thermal cycling, partial shading, reverse bias, and hail. Few perovskite devices have completed full IEC 61215 qualification, and bankability demands it.
6. **Indoor vs. solar metrics.** Indoor efficiency headlines (38.70% at 2000 lux [1]) use non-AM1.5G spectra; they demonstrate defect control, not grid relevance.

##7 Conclusion

Defect passivation, bandgap tuning, and stability engineering are not three research programs but one: the control of defect thermodynamics in a soft ionic semiconductor. The recent record supports a coherent picture. Chemically specific passivation — Lewis-base coordination of undercoordinated lead [1][13], bifunctional interfacial molecules [2], dual-molecule surface-plus-interface strategies [5], and 2D capping layers [3] — systematically recovers the voltage deficit predicted by quasi-Fermi-level-splitting analysis. Compositional tuning across the 1.25–2.3 eV range enables tandem architectures whose recent records (27.4% double-junction, 27.3% triple-junction [6][7]) are built as much on interface engineering as on absorber quality. And stability now has thermodynamic solutions (triple-halide segregation immunity [4]), kinetic solutions (multi-level passivation [11]), and engineering solutions (barrier encapsulation surviving 2,000-hour damp heat [10]) that together point toward the 25-year module.

The road ahead is defined less by physics than by manufacturing: scaling passivation chemistry to square meters, qualifying full modules under IEC protocols, and closing the lead lifecycle. If those are solved, the Shockley–Queisser arithmetic leaves no doubt about the destination — perovskite tandems heading past 30% at costs silicon cannot match.
