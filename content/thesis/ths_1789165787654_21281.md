# Coresets for Scalable Machine Learning: Sensitivity Sampling, Mergeable Summaries, and Streaming k-Means with Provable Guarantees

## Abstract

We present a unified treatment of *coresets* — small, weighted summaries of massive data sets that provably preserve the cost of every candidate solution — with emphasis on the sensitivity-sampling framework, mergeable summary composition, and streaming $k$-means. Starting from the geometric constructions of Har-Peled and Mazumdar [1], we develop the Feldman–Langberg unified framework [2], in which each point's *sensitivity* — the worst-case fraction of total cost it can carry — determines an importance-sampling distribution whose total mass governs coreset size. We prove the central bound: sampling $O(\varepsilon^{-2}\,t\log t\cdot \mathrm{VC})$ points with $t$ the total sensitivity yields an $\varepsilon$-coreset [3], explain why composability turns any offline construction into streaming and distributed algorithms via merge-and-reduce [6], and analyze practical relaxations such as lightweight coresets with additive error [4] and the near-optimality of importance sampling [5]. A concrete streaming $k$-means pipeline — bicriteria seeding, sensitivity estimation, sampled coreset, merge-and-reduce tree — is worked through with pseudocode and complexity analysis, followed by empirical evidence, lower bounds, and limitations.

## 1. Introduction

Modern machine learning operates on data sets far too large to scan more than once, too large to store on a single machine, and arriving as streams that must be processed online. The *coreset paradigm* offers a principled escape: compute once, on a tiny weighted subset, a summary that is provably interchangeable with the full data for an entire family of queries [2, 6].

Formally, let $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ be a weighted point set with weight function $w$, and let $\mathcal{Q}$ be a family of candidate solutions (e.g., all $k$-tuples of centers). A weighted set $(S, w')$ is an **$\varepsilon$-coreset** for $P$ with respect to $\mathcal{Q}$ if

$$
\forall\, Q \in \mathcal{Q}:\qquad
\big|\,\mathrm{cost}(P, Q) - \mathrm{cost}_w(S, Q)\,\big| \;\le\; \varepsilon \cdot \mathrm{cost}(P, Q),
$$

where $\mathrm{cost}(P,Q) = \sum_{p\in P} w(p)\,f(p,Q)$ and $\mathrm{cost}_w$ is defined analogously on $S$ [1, 2]. For $k$-means, $f(p,Q) = \min_{q\in Q}\|p-q\|^2$. The definition is *uniform* over all queries: any algorithm run on the coreset inherits a $(1+\varepsilon)$ guarantee on the full data, with no modification. This turns optimization on $n$ points into optimization on $|S| \ll n$ points, and — critically — the guarantee is *composable*, which is what makes streaming and distributed deployment possible [6].

Three developments define the modern landscape:

1. **Geometric coresets** (Har-Peled & Mazumdar, 2004): grid-based constructions of size $O(k\varepsilon^{-d}\log n)$, exponential in $d$ but the first to yield provably polylogarithmic-space streaming $k$-means via merge-and-reduce [1].
2. **The unified sensitivity framework** (Feldman & Langberg, 2011): reduce coreset construction to *importance sampling* where each point is sampled proportional to its *sensitivity* — its maximum relative contribution — giving size $O(kd\log k\,\varepsilon^{-2})$ for $k$-means, with near-optimal dependence on $\varepsilon$ [2, 3, 5].
3. **Practical and streaming variants**: lightweight coresets admitting additive error for one-pass distributed construction [4], coreset trees for efficient $D^2$-sampling in streams [7], and improved analyses showing the sensitivity framework needs only $O(t\log t)$ samples in the total sensitivity $t$ rather than $O(t^2)$ [3].

This thesis unifies these threads: we derive the sensitivity framework from first principles, prove its size bound, show how composability yields streaming algorithms, examine the lightweight relaxation and the empirical picture, and close with lower bounds and open problems.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The $k$-means cost and its query space

Let $X = \{x_1,\dots,x_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$. The $k$-means objective for a center set $C = \{c_1,\dots,c_k\}$ is

$$
\mathrm{cost}(X, C) \;=\; \sum_{x \in X} \min_{c \in C} \|x - c\|^2.
$$

The *query space* $(X, w, \mathcal{Q}_k, f)$ couples the data, a weight function, the family $\mathcal{Q}_k$ of all $k$-subsets of $\mathbb{R}^d$, and the loss $f$. The statistical complexity of the family is captured by its **VC-dimension** (or shattering dimension): for $k$-means in $\mathbb{R}^d$ it is $O(dk\log k)$ [2, 3]. Uniform convergence over $\mathcal{Q}_k$ — the engine that lifts a guarantee for one fixed $C$ to a guarantee for *all* $C$ — costs a factor of this dimension in the sample size.

### 2.2 Sensitivity and total sensitivity

The key quantity is the **sensitivity** of a point, introduced in the universal $\varepsilon$-approximator framework of Langberg and Schulman and imported into clustering by Feldman and Langberg [2]:

> **Definition (Sensitivity).** For a query space $(P, w, \mathcal{Q}, f)$, the sensitivity of $p \in P$ is
> $$
> s(p) \;=\; \sup_{Q \in \mathcal{Q}} \frac{w(p)\, f(p, Q)}{\sum_{q \in P} w(q)\, f(q, Q)},
> $$
> the worst-case fraction of the total cost attributable to $p$. The **total sensitivity** is $t(P) = \sum_{p\in P} s(p)$.

Sensitivity is a data-dependent notion of *importance*: outliers that can dominate the cost of some adversarial query receive high sensitivity and must be sampled with commensurate probability. Exact sensitivities are typically intractable, but *upper bounds* $m(p) \ge s(p)$ that are tight up to constants are efficiently computable from a cheap bicriteria approximation, and only inflate the coreset by a constant factor [2, 3].

### 2.3 Composability: the algebraic heart

Coresets compose. If $S_1$ is an $\varepsilon$-coreset of $P_1$ and $S_2$ an $\varepsilon$-coreset of $P_2$, then $S_1 \cup S_2$ is an $\varepsilon$-coreset of $P_1 \cup P_2$, and a $\delta$-coreset of an $\varepsilon$-coreset is a $((1+\varepsilon)(1+\delta)-1)$-coreset of the original. This monoid-like structure is what lets us build streaming algorithms from static ones [1, 6].

### 2.4 Historical size bounds

The following table summarizes the evolution of coreset size for Euclidean $(k,z)$-clustering ($z=2$ is $k$-means; dependencies on $\log(1/\delta)$ and $2^{O(z)}$ omitted):

| Construction | Size (number of points) | Technique |
|---|---|---|
| Har-Peled & Mazumdar, STOC'04 [1] | $O(k\,\varepsilon^{-d}\log n)$ | Exponential grids, low $d$ only |
| Chen, SICOMP'09 | $O(k^2 d\,\varepsilon^{-2}\log n)$ | Bicriteria + uniform sampling |
| Langberg & Schulman, SODA'10 | $O(k^3 d\,\varepsilon^{-2})$ | Universal approximators |
| Feldman & Langberg, STOC'11 [2] | $O(k d \log k\,\varepsilon^{-2})$ | Sensitivity sampling |
| Braverman et al., SODA'16 | $O(k d\,\varepsilon^{-2})$ | $(\varepsilon,\eta)$-approximations |
| Cohen-Addad et al., STOC'21 [8] | $O(k\log k\,\varepsilon^{-2-\max(2,z)})$ | Grouped importance sampling |

The trajectory is unmistakable: from exponential in $d$ to polynomial, and the constants have continued to fall [5, 8].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The sensitivity-sampling construction

The Feldman–Langberg construction proceeds in three steps [2]:

1. **Bicriteria approximation.** Compute a cheap $O(1)$-approximate solution $C^\star$ with $\beta k$ centers (e.g., via $k$-means++ seeding run $O(\log k)$ times, or a linear-time constant-factor algorithm). This need not be good — it is scaffolding.
2. **Sensitivity upper bounds.** For each $p \in P$, bound $s(p)$ using $C^\star$. For $k$-means the standard bound is
   $$
   m(p) \;=\; \frac{2\alpha\, w(p)\, f(p, C^\star)}{\mathrm{cost}(P, C^\star)} \;+\; \frac{4\alpha}{|C_i(p)|},
   $$
   where $\alpha$ is the bicriteria approximation ratio and $C_i(p)$ is the cluster of $p$ under $C^\star$. The first term charges points by their cost share; the second guarantees small clusters are not starved. Summing gives $t(P) = O(\alpha\beta k)$ — total sensitivity *linear in $k$*, independent of $n$ [2].
3. **Importance sampling.** Sample $m$ points i.i.d. with probability $m(p)/t(P)$, and reweight each sampled point by $t(P)/(m\cdot m(p))$. The reweighting is unbiased: $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{cost}_w(S,Q)] = \mathrm{cost}(P,Q)$ for every fixed $Q$.

Uniform convergence over the query space, via $\varepsilon$-approximation / chaining arguments on the reweighted function class, lifts the fixed-$Q$ concentration to all $Q$ simultaneously [2, 3].

### 3.2 The master theorem

> **Theorem (Sensitivity sampling, Feldman–Langberg 2011; sharpened by Braverman et al. 2021 [3]).** Let the query space $(P, w, \mathcal{Q}, f)$ have VC-dimension $d$, and let $t = \sum_{p} m(p)$ be an upper bound on total sensitivity. Then sampling
> $$
> m \;=\; O\!\left(\frac{t\,d\,\log t \;+\; \log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^2}\right)
> $$
> points by sensitivity-proportional importance sampling, with inverse-probability reweighting, yields an $\varepsilon$-coreset with probability at least $1-\delta$.

The classical Feldman–Langberg analysis required $O(t^2)$ samples; the 2021 refinement of Braverman et al. shows $O(t\log t)$ suffices and that this dependence is essentially optimal as a function of $t$ alone [3]. For $k$-means, $t = O(k)$ and $d = O(dk\log k)$, recovering $O(kd\log k\,\varepsilon^{-2})$ up to the $\log t$ factor.

The proof has two movements. First, *variance control*: because $m(p) \ge s(p)$, each summand $w(p)f(p,Q)/m(p)$ is bounded by $t\cdot \mathrm{cost}(P,Q)$, so Bernstein-type bounds give, for a *fixed* $Q$, deviation $O(\varepsilon)$ with $O(t/\varepsilon^2)$ samples. Second, *uniformization*: the reweighted function class has VC-dimension $O(d)$, and a symmetrization/$\varepsilon$-net argument over the query space upgrades the guarantee to all $Q$ at the price of the $d\log t$ factor [3].

### 3.3 Why $t = O(k)$ for $k$-means: the intuition

The bound $m(p)$ above decomposes into a *cost-proportional* term (summing to $O(\alpha)$) and a *cluster-size* term (summing to $O(\alpha\beta k)$). Intuitively: a point matters either because it carries a large share of the cost (first term), or because it belongs to a tiny cluster whose disappearance would destroy the solution (second term). Both are controlled by $k$, not $n$. This is why coresets can be *independent of $n$*: in the worst case, only $O(k)$ "roles" exist in the data [2, 5].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 From bicriteria seed to sampling distribution: a worked pipeline

In practice, the construction is strikingly concrete. The following Python sketch implements the full offline pipeline

```python
import numpy as np

def kmeans_pp_seed(X, k, rng):
    """O(nk) bicriteria seed; O(log k)-competitive in expectation."""
    n = X.shape[0]
    centers = [X[rng.integers(n)]]
    d2 = np.sum((X - centers[0]) ** 2, axis=1)
    for _ in range(1, k):
        probs = d2 / d2.sum()
        centers.append(X[rng.choice(n, p=probs)])
        d2 = np.minimum(d2, np.sum((X - centers[-1]) ** 2, axis=1))
    return np.array(centers), d2

def sensitivity_coreset(X, k, m, alpha=8.0, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    centers, d2 = kmeans_pp_seed(X, k, rng)
    assign = np.argmin(
        np.sum((X[:, None, :] - centers[None, :, :]) ** 2, axis=2), axis=1)
    total = d2.sum()
    # cluster cardinalities, guard against empty clusters
    counts = np.bincount(assign, minlength=k).astype(float)
    counts[counts == 0] = 1.0
    # Feldman-Langberg style upper bounds [2]
    sens = (2 * alpha * d2 / total) + (4 * alpha / counts[assign])
    t = sens.sum()                       # total sensitivity ~ O(k)
    probs = sens / t
    idx = rng.choice(len(X), size=m, p=probs)
    weights = t / (m * sens[idx])        # inverse-probability reweighting
    return X[idx], weights, t
```

The reweighting is the statistical soul of the method: without it, the sample is biased toward high-cost regions; with it, $\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathrm{cost}}(S,Q)] = \mathrm{cost}(X,Q)$ exactly, and the sensitivity bound caps the variance of every summand [3].

### 4.2 Merge-and-reduce: from offline to streaming

Composability turns any offline coreset construction into a streaming one, via the **merge-and-reduce** paradigm of Har-Peled and Mazumdar [1, 6]:

- Maintain levels $B_0, B_1, \dots, B_h$, each holding at most one coreset (a "bucket").
- Insert points into $B_0$ until it holds $m$ points; then compute an $\varepsilon'$-coreset of $B_0 \cup B_1$'s contents and *promote* it to $B_1$, cascading upward like binary addition.
- At any time, $\bigcup_i B_i$ is an $\varepsilon$-coreset of the stream seen so far, provided the per-level error budget satisfies $\prod_i (1+\varepsilon_i) - 1 \le \varepsilon$ — e.g., $\varepsilon_i = \varepsilon/(2h)$ with $h = O(\log n)$ levels.

> **Lemma (Merge-and-reduce error composition [1, 6]).** If $\mathcal{C}$ is an $\varepsilon$-coreset scheme whose output size is $m(\varepsilon)$, then merge-and-reduce maintains an $\varepsilon$-coreset of an $n$-point stream using $O(m(\varepsilon/\log n)\cdot \log n)$ space and $O(\log n)$ amortized update time per point.

The same tree parallelizes trivially: each machine builds a coreset of its shard, and the coordinator merges $p$ coresets — one communication round, total work $O(nk/p)$ [4, 6]. This is the workhorse behind distributed $k$-means at industrial scale.

The following Rust sketch captures the bucket algebra — the merge operation that makes the tree possible:

```rust
/// A mergeable, weighted summary: the algebraic core of streaming coresets [1, 6].
struct Coreset {
    points: Vec<Vec<f64>>,
    weights: Vec<f64>,
}

impl Coreset {
    /// Union of two summaries (merge step); caller reduces when too large.
    fn merge(mut self, other: Coreset) -> Coreset {
        self.points.extend(other.points);
        self.weights.extend(other.weights);
        self
    }

    /// Reduce: replace a heavy bucket by an eps'-coreset (sensitivity sampling).
    fn reduce(&self, eps_prime: f64, k: usize) -> Coreset {
        let (pts, wts) = sensitivity_sample(&self.points, &self.weights, k, eps_prime);
        Coreset { points: pts, weights: wts }
    }

    fn total_weight(&self) -> f64 { self.weights.iter().sum() }
}
```

And the composition law itself is most crisply expressed as a monoid — here in Haskell, where the type system enforces what the theorems require:

```haskell
-- | A composable summary: merge is union, reduce is a coreset scheme [6].
-- Laws: merge is associative; reduce (reduce s) ≈ reduce s up to (1+eps).
class Mergeable s where
  merge  :: s -> s -> s          -- coreset union: exact, error-free
  reduce :: Double -> s -> s    -- eps-coreset of a summary

-- | Streaming fold: merge-and-reduce as a left fold over buckets.
streamCoreset :: Mergeable s => Double -> [s] -> s
streamCoreset eps = foldr cascade empty
  where
    cascade bucket acc
      | size acc < capacity = merge bucket acc
      | otherwise          = merge (reduce (eps / levels) acc) bucket
    levels   = 64  -- O(log n) for any realistic stream
    capacity = 4096
```

### 4.3 Lightweight coresets: trading exactness for speed

The Feldman–Langberg construction needs a bicriteria pass and $O(nk)$ work — already linear, but the $k$ *sequential* $D^2$-sampling passes of the strongest variants resist distribution [4]. Bachem, Lucic, and Krause introduced **lightweight coresets** [4], which relax the guarantee to admit an *additive* term:

> **Definition (Lightweight coreset [4]).** $(S, w')$ is a lightweight $\varepsilon$-coreset if for all $C$,
> $$
> |\mathrm{cost}(X,C) - \mathrm{cost}_w(S,C)| \;\le\; \varepsilon\,\mathrm{cost}(X,C) \;+\; \varepsilon\,\mathrm{cost}(X, \{\mu(X)\}),
> $$
> where $\mu(X)$ is the data mean. The additive term is the cost of the trivial $1$-means solution.

This small concession buys a great deal: construction needs only **two passes** — one to compute the mean, one to sample from a mixture of uniform and $d^2$-sampling — each trivially parallelizable, with size $\tilde{O}(dk/\varepsilon^2)$. Empirically, lightweight coresets dominate uniform sampling and match full coresets on real benchmarks while running orders of magnitude faster [4].

### 4.4 Near-optimality of importance sampling

How small can a coreset be, in principle? Cohen-Addad, Saulpic, and Schwiegelshohn [5] show that importance sampling is *nearly optimal* among all (possibly randomized) coreset constructions for Euclidean $(k,z)$-clustering: any coreset must have size $\tilde{\Omega}(k\varepsilon^{-2})$, and importance sampling achieves $\tilde{O}(k\varepsilon^{-2})$ up to logarithmic and $z$-dependent factors. The companion STOC'21 framework [8] pushes the upper bound to $O(k\log k\,\varepsilon^{-2-\max(2,z)})$ via *grouped* importance sampling — partitioning points into geometrically regular groups before sampling within each.

The remaining gap is instructive rather than embarrassing: it concentrates in the $\varepsilon$-dependence for $z > 2$ and in lower-order $\log k$ factors, delineating exactly where future work must strike [5, 8].

### 4.5 The streaming $k$-means pipeline, end to end

Putting it together, a production streaming $k$-means system looks like this:

1. **Ingest.** Points arrive; fill level-$0$ buffer of size $m$.
2. **Reduce.** On overflow, compute a sensitivity-sampled $\varepsilon'$-coreset of the merged lower levels (the Rust `reduce` above), promoting upward.
3. **Query.** At any time, run $k$-means++ + Lloyd on the union of $O(\log n)$ buckets (total size $\tilde{O}(k\log n/\varepsilon^2)$) to obtain centers with $(1+\varepsilon)\cdot O(\log k)$ guarantee on the full stream.
4. **Variants.** StreamKM++ [7] replaces generic reduction with a *coreset tree* for $D^2$-sampling directly on the stream, achieving low dimension-dependence and excellent empirical quality against BIRCH and StreamLS.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 What the theory predicts, quantitatively

For $k$-means on $n = 10^7$ points in $d = 50$ with $k = 100$ and $\varepsilon = 0.1$:

| Scheme | Coreset size | Passes | Guarantee |
|---|---|---|---|
| Uniform sample | $\sim 10^5$ (no guarantee) | 1 | none worst-case |
| Har-Peled–Mazumdar [1] | $O(k\varepsilon^{-d}\log n)$ — infeasible at $d=50$ | $O(\log n)$ | $1+\varepsilon$ |
| Feldman–Langberg [2] | $\approx 100 \cdot 50 \cdot \log 100 / 0.01 \approx 2.3 \times 10^6$ (theory; practice $\sim 10^4$) | 2–3 | $1+\varepsilon$ w.h.p. |
| Lightweight [4] | $\tilde{O}(dk/\varepsilon^2) \approx 5 \times 10^5$ (practice $\sim 10^4$) | 2 | $1+\varepsilon$ + additive |
| Lower bound [5] | $\tilde{\Omega}(k\varepsilon^{-2}) = \tilde{\Omega}(10^4)$ | — | — |

Two observations: (i) theoretical constants overestimate practical sizes by 1–2 orders of magnitude — implementations routinely use $m \approx 10k$–$50k$ with excellent fidelity [4, 7]; (ii) the lower bound [5] shows the practical regime is essentially optimal — we are not leaving much on the table.

### 5.2 Proof sketch: the $O(t\log t)$ refinement

We sketch the Braverman et al. improvement [3], the sharpest known analysis of the framework. Fix a query $Q$. Define $g_p(Q) = w(p)f(p,Q)/(m(p)\cdot\mathrm{cost}(P,Q))$; by the sensitivity bound, $0 \le g_p \le 1$ after normalization. The estimator $\hat{Z} = \frac{t}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} g_{p_i}(Q)$ is unbiased for the normalized cost. Since each term is bounded, Bernstein's inequality gives

$$
\Pr\big[|\hat{Z} - Z| > \varepsilon\big] \;\le\; 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{c\,m\,\varepsilon^2}{t}\right),
$$

so $m = O(t\varepsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta))$ suffices *for one $Q$*. The classical analysis then union-bounds over an $\varepsilon$-net of the query space of size $\exp(O(d\log(t/\varepsilon)))$, yielding $O(t^2)$ after a crude step. The refinement replaces the net argument with a *chaining* argument on the reweighted class: because sensitivities already normalize each function by its worst case, the metric entropy at scale $\tau$ grows only as $O(d\log(t/\tau))$, and Dudley's integral converges with total mass $O(t\log t)$ rather than $O(t^2)$ [3]. The $\log t$ factor is the price of uniformity over an infinite query family — and it is tight in general.

### 5.3 Empirical behavior

Across the KDD-cup, Census, and synthetic Gaussian-mixture benchmarks reported in [4, 7]:

- Coresets of size $m = 10^4$ on $n = 10^6$–$10^7$ points preserve $k$-means cost within $1$–$3\%$ for $k \le 100$ — far better than the $\varepsilon = 0.1$ theory would suggest.
- *Relative* to uniform sampling, sensitivity sampling reduces cost error by $2$–$5\times$ at fixed $m$, with the gap widening on skewed, heavy-tailed data where rare clusters dominate [4].
- StreamKM++ [7] matches batch $k$-means++ quality while using $O(m\log n)$ memory, and beats BIRCH/StreamLS on 6 of 7 benchmarks in the original study.
- The dominant practical cost is the bicriteria pass; replacing exact $k$-means++ seeding with a single $D^2$-sampling pass plus lightweight bounds loses almost nothing empirically [4].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Constants and the $\varepsilon^{-2}$ wall.** The $\varepsilon^{-2}$ dependence is information-theoretically near-optimal [5], but the hidden constants (bicriteria ratio $\alpha$, VC-dimension factors) mean the theory only "kicks in" at $m \gtrsim 10^4$; practitioners tune $m$ empirically.
2. **Beyond $k$-means.** Sensitivity bounds are known for $k$-median, $\ell_p$ regression, and subspace approximation [2, 6], but each new loss needs a bespoke sensitivity analysis — there is no fully automatic compiler from loss to $m(p)$. Kernel $k$-means and deep-clustering objectives remain largely open.
3. **Adversarial streams and deletions.** Merge-and-reduce assumes insertions only. Sliding windows need exponential histograms; fully dynamic streams (insertions *and* deletions) need linear sketches with much weaker guarantees [6].
4. **Lightweight coresets' additive term.** On data with huge $1$-means cost (e.g., unnormalized, high-variance features), the additive error $\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{cost}(X,\{\mu\})$ can swamp the signal; normalization is not optional [4].
5. **Dimension dependence.** The $O(dk\log k)$ VC-dimension factor hurts in very high dimensions; dimensionality reduction (Johnson–Lindenstrauss) before coreset construction is standard practice but composes awkwardly with the $1+\varepsilon$ guarantee.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Coresets distill the *mergeable summary* idea to its essence: a weighted subset that answers every query approximately, composes under union, and thereby scales from a laptop to a data center to an infinite stream without changing the algorithm [6]. The sensitivity-sampling framework [2, 3] reduced the problem to computing one number per point — its worst-case importance — and showed the total of those numbers, $O(k)$ for $k$-means, dictates the summary size. Refinements have shaved the analysis to near-optimality [3, 5, 8], and practical relaxations like lightweight coresets [4] and coreset trees [7] have made the theory deployable.

The open frontier is clear: automatic sensitivity bounds for new losses, dynamic streams, and closing the last $\varepsilon$-dependence gaps for $z > 2$ [5, 8].
