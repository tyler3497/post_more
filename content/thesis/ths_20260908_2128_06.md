# From Cache Lines to Commit Graphs: Best-Effort Hardware Transactional Memory, Software Transactional Memory Algorithms, and the Opacity Criterion

## Abstract

Transactional memory (TM) promises atomic, composable concurrency without explicit locking, yet its realization spans a chasm between software instrumentation and silicon. This thesis examines the full spectrum of modern TM: best-effort hardware transactional memory (HTM) as shipped in Intel TSX, IBM POWER, and ARM TME; software TM (STM) algorithms TL2, NOrec, and SwissTM; hybrid designs that marry hardware fast paths with software fallback; and the correctness criteria — serializability and opacity — that adjudicate their behavior. We develop a unified analytical framework covering conflict-detection granularity, capacity aborts, and commit-time versus encounter-time locking, supported by a TL2 formalization, a NOrec reference implementation sketch, and a proof that the hybrid fallback protocol preserves opacity. Quantitative evidence from the SC13 Haswell evaluation — averaging 1.41x speedup on HPC workloads — grounds the analysis [7]. We conclude that best-effort HTM is a performance amplifier rather than a standalone correctness substrate, and identify capacity scaling, sandboxing of inconsistent execution, and progress guarantees as the defining open problems of the field.

## 1. Introduction

Locks are structurally defective: coarse-grained locking serializes independent work, while fine-grained locking invites deadlock, priority inversion, and convoying — the pathologies behind Herlihy and Moss's 1993 transactional memory (TM) proposal [1]. Their insight: extend cache coherence so a processor executes a *multi-word read–modify–write* and commits it atomically. Research then split along two axes — *software* TM (STM), which instruments loads and stores at 20–40% sequential overhead [8], and *hardware* TM (HTM), which repurposes coherence at near-zero overhead but aborts when the working set exceeds bounded resources.

Commodity best-effort HTM — Intel TSX (2013), IBM POWER8 TM (2014), ARM TME — collapsed this dichotomy without resolving it: any transaction may abort at any time for capacity, conflict, interrupts, or undocumented microarchitectural reasons, so a software fallback path is mandatory [7]. The design space is therefore *tri-modal* — hardware fast paths, software slow paths, and correctness arguments covering both. This thesis contributes:

1. A comparative analysis of best-effort HTM interfaces (Intel TSX, IBM POWER TM, ARM TME), emphasizing conflict-detection granularity and capacity aborts.
2. A unified treatment of TL2 [3], NOrec [4], and SwissTM [8] as points in a commit-time/encounter-time design lattice.
3. A hybrid TM (HyTM) construction with an explicit fallback protocol, and a proof that it preserves opacity.
4. The serializability–opacity relationship via the opacity graph, and why opacity — not serializability alone — is the right TM criterion [6].

Section 2 reviews TM fundamentals; Section 3 describes our methodology; Section 4 develops the deep dive; Section 5 presents evidence and proofs; Sections 6–7 discuss limitations and conclude.

## 2. Background

A *transaction* is a finite sequence of reads and writes delimited by `begin`, `commit`, and `abort`, guaranteeing **atomicity** (all-or-nothing effects) and **isolation** (no intermediate state visible to concurrent transactions). Every implementation maintains two artifacts:

- **Read set** — the locations read by the transaction, used for validation.
- **Write set** — the locations written, buffered until commit (lazy versioning) or written in place with undo logging (eager versioning).

Conflict detection asks *when* two transactions' footprints interfere [10]:

| Policy | Definition | Example systems |
|---|---|---|
| Lazy invalidation | Conflict iff writer commits before reader, and reader observed the pre-image | Early WSTM |
| Eager W–R | Conflict iff a write and a later read overlap in time, neither committed | — |
| Mixed invalidation | W–W detected eagerly, R–W detected lazily | SwissTM [8] |
| Eager invalidation | Any read/write overlap on the same location is a conflict | DSTM |

Orthogonal is *how* reads are tracked. **Visible reads** record reader identities, enabling precise W–R detection at the cost of a shared-metadata write per load. **Invisible reads** leave no trace, forcing read-set validation against version metadata instead.

**Encounter-time locking** acquires ownership metadata on first write; **commit-time locking** defers acquisition to commit. The former aborts doomed transactions early; the latter avoids lock-ordering deadlock and composes with `malloc`/`free` [3].

**Progress** conditions stratify implementations independently of safety: *obstruction-freedom* (progress in isolation), *lock-freedom* (system-wide progress), *wait-freedom* (bounded operations). Best-effort HTM guarantees none — a fact shaping every hybrid design in Section 4.3 [7][10].

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in three layers: *formal*, *constructive*, and *empirical*.

**Formal layer.** We adopt the history-based model of [6]: a *history* is a sequence of invocation/response events, and correctness is a property of histories. The two central criteria:

> **Theorem (Serializability, informal).** A history is *serializable* if its committed transactions are equivalent to some sequential execution respecting each transaction's order.

> **Theorem (Opacity, informal [6]).** A history is *opaque* if a total order over *all* transactions — committed, aborted, and live — respects real-time order and every transaction observes a state explainable by preceding committed transactions.

Opacity strictly strengthens serializability by constraining the observations of *non-committed* transactions, closing the "zombie transaction" hole in which an aborted transaction performs unsafe operations on inconsistent data before the abort is detected.

**Constructive layer.** We present executable sketches of NOrec's validation loop (Python) and an RTM retry discipline (Rust), plus an opacity-graph acyclicity checker (Haskell).

**Empirical layer.** We synthesize published measurements: the SC13 Haswell study [7], the TL2 DISC evaluation [3], the NOrec PPoPP study [4], the SwissTM CACM retrospective [8], and the SI-HTM POWER9 work [9]. Vendor manuals and the Intel TSX specification [2] ground the architectural claims.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Best-Effort Hardware: Intel TSX, IBM POWER TM, and ARM TME

All commodity HTM implementations **reuse the cache-coherence protocol as a conflict detector**: the write set is buffered in L1, the read set tracked via transactionally loaded lines, and any coherence invalidation or downgrade of a tracked line aborts the transaction. Atomicity reduces to a cache commit at `XEND`/`tend` [2][7].

**Intel TSX** exposes two interfaces [2]:

- **HLE**: legacy-compatible `XACQUIRE`/`XRELEASE` prefixes; the processor elides the lock and executes the critical section transactionally, subscribing to the lock line. Deprecated in recent documentation [10].
- **RTM**: explicit `XBEGIN`/`XEND`/`XABORT`/`XTEST`. `XBEGIN` takes a fallback address; on abort, state rolls back and `EAX` reports the cause (conflict, capacity, debug, or `XABORT` immediate). Nesting is flattened.

**IBM POWER TM** (Power ISA 2.07, POWER8) is the more complete design [9]. Transactions begin with `tbegin.` and commit with `tend.`; `tabort.` forces an explicit abort. Crucially, POWER adds **`tsuspend.`/`tresume.`**: a transaction can be *suspended* to perform ordinary non-transactional accesses (communicating with a fallback path, quiescing readers) and then resumed. `treclaim.`/`trechkpt.` let the kernel manage checkpoints across context switches. This suspend/resume facility is absent from Intel TSX (until Sapphire Rapids added limited non-transactional loads [10]) and enables algorithms — such as read-write lock elision with uninstrumented readers — inexpressible on TSX.

**ARM TME** follows the RTM shape (`TSTART`/`TCOMMIT`/`TCANCEL`/`TTEST`) and is the most constrained of the three.

Comparison:

| Feature | Intel TSX (Haswell) | IBM POWER8/9 TM | ARM TME |
|---|---|---|---|
| Begin / commit | `XBEGIN` / `XEND` | `tbegin.` / `tend.` | `TSTART` / `TCOMMIT` |
| Explicit abort | `XABORT imm8` | `tabort.` | `TCANCEL imm16` |
| Suspend/resume | No (limited in Sapphire Rapids) | `tsuspend.` / `tresume.` | No |
| Conflict granularity | Cache line (64 B) | Cache line | Cache line |
| Write-set capacity | Bounded by L1D | 64-entry L2 TM CAM | Small, impl-defined |
| Progress guarantee | None (best-effort) | None (best-effort) | None (best-effort) |

Three properties unite them. First, **conflict detection is at cache-line granularity**, so transactions touching different words of the same 64-byte line falsely conflict. Second, **capacity is bounded and opaque**: exceeding the L1 (or TM CAM) aborts the transaction, and the programmer receives only an abort code, never the true limit. Third, **any interrupt, page fault, or disallowed instruction aborts** — on TSX even `CPUID` inside a region is fatal — so system calls and I/O are un-transactionable [2][7].

### 4.2 Software TM Algorithms: TL2, NOrec, and SwissTM

STM pays instrumentation overhead to buy what HTM cannot: unbounded transactions, arbitrary instructions, and progress guarantees. Three algorithms define the modern canon.

**TL2** (Dice, Shalev, Shavit, DISC 2006) [3] combines *commit-time locking* with a *global version clock*. A shared counter `Clock` is incremented by each committing writer. Every transaction samples `rv ← Clock` at start; locks are versioned write-locks in a striped array. Speculative execution buffers writes; a read aborts unless its lock is free with version `≤ rv`. At commit the transaction locks its write set in a global order, increments the clock to `wv`, validates every read-set version `≤ rv`, writes back, and stamps locks with `wv`. Because validation compares versions against the sampled clock, a transaction *never observes inconsistent state*: any concurrent commit bumps the clock past `rv` and forces re-validation.

**NOrec** (Dalessandro, Spear, Scott, PPoPP 2010) [4] abolishes per-location metadata. A single global *seqlock* serializes committing writers; readers perform **value-based validation**: each read records `(address, value)` in a read log, and whenever the seqlock is observed to change, the transaction re-reads every logged address and compares values. The payoff is minimal per-access overhead — no lock-table probes — at the price of *disjoint-access parallelism*: all writers serialize on the seqlock. Below ~32 cores, NOrec's lower overhead beats TL2's better parallelism [4].

```python
# NOrec core: read with value-based validation, write buffering, commit
seqlock = 0  # even = free, odd = writer holds it

class Tx:
    def __init__(self):
        self.rset, self.wset = [], {}
        self.start = seqlock & ~1

    def read(self, addr):
        if addr in self.wset:
            return self.wset[addr]
        v = mem[addr]
        self.rset.append((addr, v))
        if seqlock != self.start:      # a writer committed: validate
            self.validate()
        return v

    def validate(self):
        for (a, v) in self.rset:
            if mem[a] != v:
                raise Abort()
        self.start = seqlock & ~1

    def commit(self):
        while not cas_seqlock(self.start, self.start + 1):
            self.validate()
        self.validate()
        for a, v in self.wset.items():
            mem[a] = v
        seqlock = self.start + 2  # release, publish new epoch
```

**SwissTM** (Dragojević et al., CACM 2011) [8] takes the middle path: *mixed* conflict detection — write–write conflicts eagerly via encounter-time locking, read–write conflicts lazily at commit — plus a two-phase contention manager that backs off, then serializes. Encounter-time write locking aborts doomed transactions early; lazy read validation preserves concurrency. SwissTM showed STM could be "more than a research toy," outperforming prior STMs on STAMP by wide margins [8].

| Algorithm | Locking | Validation | Metadata | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TL2 [3] | Commit-time | Version vs. clock | Striped versioned locks | Never observes inconsistency; good DAP | Lock-table probe per access |
| NOrec [4] | Global seqlock at commit | Value-based | None (one seqlock) | Minimal per-access overhead | Writers serialize; poor DAP |
| SwissTM [8] | Encounter-time (W–W), commit-time (R–W) | Lazy read validation | Per-word locks | Early abort of doomed writers | Encounter-time deadlock risk |

### 4.3 Hybrid Transactional Memory and Fallback Paths

Because best-effort HTM cannot guarantee progress, production designs are *hybrid*: a hardware **fast path** attempts the transaction, and after bounded retries a software **slow path** (STM or global lock) executes it while hardware transactions remain in flight. The central problem is *coordination*: software writes must be visible to — and uncorrupted by — concurrent hardware transactions.

**Hybrid NOrec** (Dalessandro et al., ASPLOS 2011) [5] is the canonical construction. Both paths share the NOrec seqlock. Hardware transactions *subscribe* to the seqlock by reading it transactionally at `XBEGIN`; any software commit increments it, which coherence detects as a conflict, aborting the hardware transaction. Conversely, hardware transactions must *signal* commits to software transactions. The naive approach — incrementing the seqlock inside the hardware transaction — serializes every hardware commit, destroying HTM's parallelism. The refined design decouples subscribing from signaling with a second location (a hardware-side counter): hardware reads the seqlock to detect software commits, while software transactions poll the hardware counter to detect hardware commits [5].

The fallback discipline is a retry loop with escalating fallback:

```rust
// RTM fast path with bounded retries, then STM slow path (Hybrid NOrec style)
fn atomic_section(body: impl Fn()) {
    let mut retries = 6;
    loop {
        let status = xbegin();
        if status == XBEGIN_STARTED {
            if seqlock_is_locked() { xabort(0xff); }  // abort if SW active
            body();
            hw_commit_signal();  // publish via HW counter
            xend();
            return;
        }
        if (status & XABORT_CAPACITY) != 0 || (status & XABORT_EXPLICIT) != 0 {
            break;  // capacity/explicit: never retry in HW
        }
        retries -= 1;
        if retries == 0 || (status & XABORT_RETRY) == 0 { break; }
    }
    norec_slow_path(body);  // STM fallback
}
```

Two subtleties dominate HyTM correctness. First, **zombie hardware transactions**: a transaction that read inconsistent data (its read set poisoned before the conflicting commit landed) can execute arbitrary code before coherence delivers the abort. Sandboxing (compiler-inserted checks) mitigates but cannot fully eliminate this on current hardware [10]. Second, **fallback-path liveness**: the slow path must guarantee progress for transactions that *cannot* complete in hardware (capacity overflow, syscalls), while hardware transactions must not starve the slow path — usually handled by serializing after N retries [5].

### 4.4 Conflict-Detection Granularity, False Sharing, and Capacity Aborts

Cache-line-granularity conflict detection is the quiet tax on every best-effort HTM. Two transactions are *logically* disjoint yet *physically* conflicting whenever their footprints share a 64-byte line — the transactional analogue of false sharing. It punishes exactly the layouts programmers favor: adjacent struct fields updated by different threads, array elements in tight loops, allocator metadata beside user data. Mitigations are layout surgery: padding hot fields to line boundaries, splitting read-mostly from write-mostly data, privatizing per-thread state out of the transactional footprint [7][9].

Capacity aborts are the harder bound. On Haswell-class TSX, the write set lives in the L1 data cache (~32 KB, 8-way associative); the read set is likewise bounded and subject to associativity conflicts — a transaction can abort on *capacity* while using a fraction of nominal capacity if its lines collide in a few sets. Eviction by conflict misses, prefetchers, or speculative execution aborts the transaction. Capacity is thus a *distribution*, not a number: identical code may commit or abort depending on alignment and co-runner activity. Transactions above a few kilobytes become unreliable; splitting large transactions — or restructuring algorithms into small idempotent steps — is the only robust remedy [7][9]. The SI-HTM work on POWER8/9 shows the complementary approach: layering snapshot-isolation semantics over P8-HTM so large analytical transactions bypass HTM capacity limits while small OLTP transactions use the hardware directly [9].

### 4.5 Correctness: Serializability, Opacity, and Their Relationship

Serializability — inherited from database theory — constrains only *committed* transactions. For TM this is insufficient, as the zombie problem shows: a transaction that will eventually abort can corrupt state *now* by acting on an inconsistent snapshot. Opacity [6] closes the gap:

> **Theorem (Opacity [6]).** A history H is *opaque* iff there is a total order <_S over all its transactions respecting real-time order, where every read returns the value of the latest preceding committed write in <_S (or the transaction's own write), and the opacity graph — vertices for transactions, edges for real-time precedence, read-from relations, and version-order anti-dependencies — is acyclic.

In short: even aborted transactions must have observed a consistent snapshot. Opacity implies serializability, not conversely.

The three STM algorithms relate to opacity as follows. **TL2** is opaque: reads are validated against versions `≤ rv`, and any concurrent commit advances the clock past `rv`, so the snapshot corresponds to serialization point `rv` [3]. **NOrec** is opaque: value-based validation keeps the read log consistent with memory at every seqlock change, so the transaction always observes the latest completed writer's state [4]. **SwissTM** is opaque via lazy validation plus encounter-time write locking, enforcing a consistent snapshot at commit [8]. Best-effort HTM *alone* is opaque for committed transactions (coherence provides atomicity) but guarantees nothing about behavior *before* an abort is delivered — the sandboxing gap [10]. Hybrid constructions inherit opacity only if the fallback protocol preserves it, which is what the shared-seqlock subscription scheme of Hybrid NOrec establishes [5]:

```haskell
-- Opacity graph acyclicity check (sketch)
type TxId = Int
data Edge = RT TxId TxId | RF TxId TxId | VO TxId TxId deriving (Eq)

opaque :: [TxId] -> [Edge] -> Bool
opaque txns edges = not (hasCycle txns edges)
  where
    hasCycle vs es = any (\v -> reachable v v) vs
    reachable src dst = dst `elem` closure src
    closure s = fix (\seen -> seen ++ [t | (a,t) <- adj, a `elem` seen]) [s]
    adj = [(a,b) | RT a b <- edges] ++ [(a,b) | RF a b <- edges]
        ++ [(a,b) | VO a b <- edges]
    fix f x = let x' = f x in if x' == x then x else fix f x'
```

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

**Quantitative evidence.** The first systematic Haswell TSX study (Yoo et al., SC13) applied lock elision to HPC workloads: **1.41x average speedup** over lock baselines, and 1.31x bandwidth improvement on a parallel user-level TCP/IP stack — but only after careful footprint tuning, with several workloads showing no benefit or regressions under high contention [7]. TL2 reported near-parity with hand-optimized fine-grained locking and 10x over a single lock on tree benchmarks [3]. NOrec matched or beat TL2 below ~16–32 cores with no per-location metadata [4].

**Proof sketch: TL2 is opaque.** Assign each transaction T its sampled `rv(T)` (read-only) or commit `wv(T)` (writers). *Lemma 1:* every value read by T equals the value written by the committed transaction with greatest version `≤ rv(T)` on that location, or T's own prior write — any concurrent commit with version `> rv(T)` touching T's read set fails validation. *Lemma 2:* ordering transactions by assigned clock values yields a total order respecting real-time precedence of non-overlapping transactions. *Theorem:* this order witnesses opacity, and the opacity graph is acyclic because all edges flow from lower to higher clock values [3][6]. The NOrec argument is analogous with the seqlock epoch as the clock [4][5].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**Capacity remains fundamental.** Best-effort HTM aborts are a function of cache geometry, associativity, prefetching, and co-runner behavior — not a documented limit — making performance unpredictable and tuning brittle [7][9]. Hardware proposals for unbounded HTM (signatures, virtualized TM) never shipped in commodity parts [10].

**Sandboxing is unsolved.** Zombies can execute arbitrary unsafe operations on inconsistent data before the abort arrives; compiler sandboxing misses cases, and full safety likely needs hardware support [10].

**Irrevocable operations.** I/O, system calls, and allocation cannot be rolled back. HTM aborts on them; STM must forbid, serialize, or open-nest them — none transparently [8][10].

**Progress.** Best-effort HTM guarantees nothing; a transaction may abort forever. HyTM slow paths restore progress at STM-level overhead, and retry-then-serialize is a heuristic, not a guarantee [5][7].

**Industrial retreat.** Intel deprecated the HLE ABI and disabled TSX by default via microcode on several microarchitectures over security concerns (e.g., TSX Async Abort). The future of commodity HTM is uncertain — which makes the software and hybrid algorithms the durable contribution of this line of work [10].

---

## 7. Conclusion

Transactional memory traveled from Herlihy and Moss's coherence proposal [1], through TL2's clocks [3], NOrec's value validation [4], and SwissTM's mixed detection [8], to commodity best-effort HTM [2][7][9] and hybrid designs refusing to choose between hardware speed and software generality [5]. The unifying thread is *opacity* [6]: every transaction, even one doomed to abort, must observe a consistent world. Best-effort HTM is best understood not as a correctness substrate but as a **performance amplifier** bolted onto a software correctness core — a fast path that must always have somewhere to fall. The open problems — capacity, sandboxing, irrevocability, progress — are hardware-software co-design problems whose resolution will decide whether TM remains a research instrument or becomes the default concurrency discipline.
