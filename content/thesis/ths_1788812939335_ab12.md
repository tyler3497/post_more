# Hybrid Logical Clocks, TrueTime Uncertainty Windows, and Externally Consistent Commits for Geo-Replicated Event Sourcing

## Abstract

Geo-replicated event sourcing systems face a fundamental tension: they must impose a global, causally faithful order on append-only event streams spanning continents, while the only physical time available is imperfectly synchronized. This thesis develops a unified treatment of three interlocking mechanisms that resolve this tension. First, **Hybrid Logical Clocks (HLCs)** [1], which fuse physical timestamps with Lamport-style logical counters to yield timestamps that track wall time, are monotonically increasing, and preserve causality. Second, **TrueTime-style uncertainty windows** [2], which expose clock error as an explicit interval $[earliest, latest]$, enabling Spanner's *commit wait* to recover **external consistency** — commit-before-begin implies $ts(T_1) < ts(T_2)$ [2][3]. Third, we show how these primitives compose into **externally consistent two-phase commit** and into append semantics for geo-replicated event sourcing, where HLC skew bounds permit single-key linearizability without atomic clocks, while full external consistency is achieved through commit-wait, timestamp ratcheting, or both [4][5][7]. We analyze the formal guarantees, derive latency bounds as a function of the uncertainty interval $\varepsilon$ and the skew bound $\Delta$, and evaluate the empirical tradeoffs measured in Spanner and CockroachDB.

## 1 Introduction

Event sourcing organizes a distributed system around an immutable, append-only log of events: state is a fold over history, and every mutation is a first-class, totally ordered fact. When that log is replicated across datacenters on different continents, the ordering problem becomes acute. Two orders matter, and they are not the same: the *causal order* (what caused what, visible to any correct observer) and the *real-time order* (what happened before what according to a perfect external clock, visible to clients who communicate out of band — over the phone, say, and then issue dependent transactions). A database that scrambles causal order is internally inconsistent; a database that inverts real-time order is *externally inconsistent*, and its users can observe the inversion directly: "I saw Alice's deposit committed, then issued my withdrawal, yet the withdrawal's timestamp precedes the deposit's."

The classical theory of distributed time — Lamport's logical clocks (1978) and Mattern/Fidge vector clocks (1988) — captures causality beautifully while abandoning the physical world: logical timestamps are arbitrary counters, meaningless to operators, queries, and retention policies, and incapable of expressing "this event happened 300 ms ago" [6]. Physical clocks (NTP, PTP) capture the real world but cannot be trusted at the microsecond level across sites, and naive comparison of physical timestamps violates causality whenever clocks drift.

The modern synthesis, which this thesis studies in depth, rests on three pillars:

1. **Hybrid Logical Clocks** [1], which keep the physical component within a bounded distance of wall time while using a logical counter to absorb skew and preserve the clock condition: $e_1 \to e_2 \Rightarrow ts(e_1) < ts(e_2)$, where $\to$ denotes Lamport's happened-before relation.
2. **Uncertainty windows** à la TrueTime [2], which refuse to collapse an uncertain timestamp into a point, instead returning the interval $[earliest, latest]$ guaranteed to contain the absolute time of the call, with half-width $\varepsilon$ engineered below 10 ms by Google's GPS/atomic-clock infrastructure — or left as a configurable NTP-derived bound in systems like CockroachDB [4].
3. **Commit protocols that pay the uncertainty cost explicitly**: Spanner's *commit wait* defers exposing a transaction's commit until its timestamp is certainly in the past, thereby manufacturing external consistency out of uncertain clocks [2][3]; CockroachDB trades weaker guarantees (serializability plus single-key linearizability, not strict serializability) for operation on commodity infrastructure with a configurable `max_clock_offset` of 500 ms and *uncertainty intervals* that trigger timestamp ratcheting on reads [4][5].

Our contribution in this thesis is expository and analytic: we reconstruct the HLC update algorithm with its skew bound, prove the commit-wait correctness argument in the style of the Spanner paper, compare the latency envelopes of the Spanner and CockroachDB designs, and — as a novel framing — cast both as **event-sourcing append protocols**, showing exactly which ordering invariants an event log inherits from each design. Throughout, we ground every claim in the published literature [1][2][3][4][5][7].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Lamport clocks, vector clocks, and the clock condition

Lamport's scalar clock maintains a per-process counter $L$: on any local event or send, $L := L + 1$; on receipt of a message carrying $L'$, $L := \max(L, L') + 1$. The **clock condition** states: if $a \to b$ (happened-before), then $L(a) < L(b)$. A total order is obtained by tie-breaking with process identifiers. The well-known deficiency is interpretability: $L = 1047291$ tells no one *when* anything happened, and the counter can drift arbitrarily far from wall time, so snapshot queries ("state as of 14:00 UTC") are unanswerable [6]. Vector clocks repair concurrency detection ($V_1 \parallel V_2$ iff neither dominates) at $O(n)$ cost per timestamp — prohibitive for a global database with thousands of nodes [1].

### 2.2 Physical clocks and the synchronization wall

NTP over the wide area typically achieves skew of tens of milliseconds but offers no hard guarantees; asymmetric network paths can silently push skew past 100 ms. PTP achieves sub-microsecond precision within a datacenter but does not span continents. Google's TrueTime sidesteps statistical arguments entirely: multiple diverse time references (GPS receivers and atomic clocks), combined via Marzullo's algorithm, advertise the uncertainty $\varepsilon$ that it *knows* it has, generally below 10 ms [2]. The radical move is not the hardware but the API: instead of `now()` returning a fiction, TrueTime returns an interval.

### 2.3 External consistency, strict serializability, linearizability

**External consistency** [2]: if transaction $T_1$ commits before $T_2$ starts in real time, then $ts(T_1) < ts(T_2)$. Equivalently, the serialization order respects real-time order — linearizability lifted from single objects to transactions. **Strict serializability** adds isolation. Spanner provides both; CockroachDB provides serializability plus single-key linearizability but deliberately *not* strict serializability, noting violations require an external low-latency channel between clients [4]. For event sourcing, external consistency is what makes a replicated event log trustworthy: appends acknowledged to clients appear in the log in real-time order.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is analytic reconstruction combined with comparative protocol analysis:

1. **Formal reconstruction.** We re-derive the HLC update rules from Kulkarni et al. [1], including the bounded-skew variant that caps a remote physical component above local wall time by the maximum allowable offset, preventing a Byzantine-fast peer from dragging the local clock forward. We state the two core invariants — monotonicity and bounded drift — and sketch their inductive proofs.
2. **Commit-wait calculus.** Following Corbett et al. [2] and the Google Cloud engineering exposition [3], we formalize TrueTime's interval API, derive the commit-wait rule, and prove that it yields external consistency. We then quantify the *latency cost*: commit wait overlaps Paxos replication, so effective added latency is $\max(0, 2\varepsilon - T_{replication})$.
3. **Comparative protocol anatomy.** We dissect CockroachDB's transaction pipeline (provisional timestamps, write intents, uncertainty-interval reads, parallel commits) from Taft et al. [4] and the multi-region transactions design [5], contrasting its optimistic timestamp management with Spanner's pessimistic lock-then-wait approach.
4. **Event-sourcing mapping.** We translate both designs into append-log semantics: a *log position* is a timestamp; an *append* is a transaction; causal consistency of the log corresponds to the clock condition, and external consistency of the log corresponds to the commit-wait invariant. This mapping is, to our knowledge, presented here for the first time in this form.
5. **Tradeoff evaluation.** We compare designs along four axes — infrastructure requirements, commit latency, read latency, and guarantee strength — with numerical bounds drawn from the cited systems.

All protocols are presented in executable pseudocode (Python for HLC mechanics, TLA⁺-style notation for the commit protocol) so that the reader can verify the claimed invariants by simulation.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The HLC algorithm: physical fidelity with logical safety

An HLC timestamp is a triple $(l, c, j)$: the physical component $l$ (wall time in ms), a logical counter $c$, and a node identifier $j$ used only for tie-breaking. The update rules [1]:

```python
class HLC:
    def __init__(self, node_id, max_skew_ms=500):
        self.l, self.c, self.node = 0, 0, node_id
        self.max_skew_ms = max_skew_ms

    def tick(self, wall_ms):                      # local event / send
        l_prev, c_prev = self.l, self.c
        self.l = max(l_prev, wall_ms)
        if self.l == l_prev:
            self.c = c_prev + 1
        else:                                     # l advanced past old l
            self.c = 0 if self.l == wall_ms else c_prev + 1
        # (simplified; full form: c = 0 if l > l_prev else c_prev + 1)
        return (self.l, self.c, self.node)

    def receive(self, remote, wall_ms):            # message arrival
        l_prev, c_prev = self.l, self.c
        l_remote, c_remote, _ = remote
        # bounded skew: refuse to adopt a physical time too far ahead
        if l_remote - wall_ms > self.max_skew_ms:
            l_remote = wall_ms + self.max_skew_ms
        self.l = max(l_prev, l_remote, wall_ms)
        if self.l == l_prev == l_remote:
            self.c = max(c_prev, c_remote) + 1
        elif self.l == l_prev:
            self.c = c_prev + 1
        elif self.l == l_remote:
            self.c = c_remote + 1
        else:
            self.c = 0
        return (self.l, self.c, self.node)
```

> **Theorem:** (Clock condition [1]) If event $e_1$ happened-before $e_2$ in an execution where every node's HLC obeys the rules above, then $ts(e_1) < ts(e_2)$ lexicographically.

The proof is by induction on the happened-before path: each local tick advances the timestamp, and each receive strictly exceeds both the local and remote timestamps. Moreover, the physical component $l$ satisfies the **bounded-drift property**: $l$ never exceeds $wall + \Delta$ (where $\Delta$ is the maximum skew bound), so HLC timestamps remain interpretable as approximate wall times — operators can read them, TTLs can be computed from them, and event-sourcing consumers can partition the log by approximate time.

### 4.2 TrueTime: uncertainty as a first-class value

The TrueTime API is startlingly small [2]:

```
TT.now()       -> [earliest, latest]     # absolute time t_abs satisfies earliest <= t_abs <= latest
TT.after(t)    -> bool                   # true iff t has certainly passed
TT.before(t)   -> bool                   # true iff t has certainly not arrived
```

The implementation guarantees $latest - earliest = 2\varepsilon$, with $\varepsilon$ typically under 7 ms. Crucially, $\varepsilon$ is *measured*, not assumed: when GPS receivers lose signal, $\varepsilon$ grows, and Spanner "slows down to wait out that uncertainty" rather than silently violating its contract [2][3]. CockroachDB's analog is the **uncertainty interval** [5]: a read at timestamp $t_r$ cannot distinguish real-time order from writes with timestamps in $[t_r, t_r + \Delta]$, where $\Delta$ is `max_clock_offset` (default 500 ms). Any write encountered in that window forces an *uncertainty refresh* — the reading transaction restarts at a higher timestamp.

### 4.3 Commit wait: manufacturing external consistency from uncertain clocks

Spanner's read-write transaction commit, in the simplified single-group case [2][3]:

```
1. Client acquires locks (2PL), buffers writes.
2. Coordinator chooses commit timestamp s such that:
     s > any timestamp used by reads in the transaction,  and
     s >= TT.now().latest                      # s is in the future w.r.t. true time
3. COMMIT WAIT: coordinator waits until TT.after(s) holds,
     i.e., TT.now().earliest > s.
4. Log COMMIT record via Paxos; release locks; notify client.
```

> **Theorem:** (External consistency [2]) If $T_1$ commits before $T_2$ starts (real time), then $s_1 < s_2$.

*Proof sketch.* $T_1$'s coordinator waits until $TT.now().earliest > s_1$ before the commit is visible. Hence $T_1$'s commit instant satisfies $t_{abs}(commit_1) > s_1$ — the timestamp is genuinely in the past at commit time. $T_2$ starts later, so its first $TT.now()$ call yields $earliest_2 > t_{abs}(start_2) > t_{abs}(commit_1) > s_1$... more precisely, $earliest_2 \geq t_{abs}(start_2) > s_1$, and $T_2$'s commit timestamp $s_2 \geq latest_2 \geq earliest_2 > s_1$. ∎

The elegance is that commit wait overlaps Paxos replication: the wait usually completes before quorum acknowledgments return, so the *effective* cost is near zero in the common case [3]. The wait is bounded by $2\varepsilon$ — at $\varepsilon = 7$ ms, roughly 14 ms against 30–100 ms of cross-datacenter replication latency. This is the precise sense in which Spanner **pays the uncertainty cost explicitly and up front**, rather than discovering timestamp ambiguity later and restarting.

### 4.4 CockroachDB's HLC-based transaction pipeline and parallel commits

CockroachDB replaces TrueTime with HLCs over NTP, accepting bounded-but-large skew [4]. Its transaction lifecycle:

1. **Provisional timestamp.** On `BEGIN`, the coordinator assigns $t_{prov}$ from its local HLC — deliberately in the *uncertain present*.
2. **Reads** at $t_{prov}$ from the Raft leaseholder. If a read encounters a provisional or committed write within its uncertainty interval $[t_{prov}, t_{prov} + \Delta]$, it performs an **uncertainty refresh**: restart the transaction at a higher timestamp [5]. Reads therefore never block on uncertainty; they *move*.
3. **Writes** leave **write intents** — provisional MVCC records carrying the transaction's identity. A conflicting reader may *push* the intent's transaction to a higher timestamp rather than waiting [4].
4. **Parallel commits.** The classic two-phase commit is collapsed: the coordinator writes all intents plus a transaction record marked `COMMITTED` with an explicit list of in-flight intents; readers treat those intents as committed without waiting for the second phase to resolve them. This stages the commit decision in a single Raft write [4].

For an event-sourcing log, parallel commits are significant: the *append* becomes visible at the speed of one consensus round, and the HLC timestamp on the intent is already causally ordered, so the log's causal invariant is never violated even while resolution is in flight.

| Property | Spanner [2] | CockroachDB [4][5] |
|---|---|---|
| Clock source | TrueTime (GPS + atomic), $\varepsilon < 10$ ms | HLC over NTP, $\Delta = 500$ ms default |
| Timestamp type | Interval-chosen scalar $s$ | Hybrid logical $(l, c, j)$ |
| Uncertainty handling | **Commit wait**: wait until $earliest > s$ | **Uncertainty refresh**: restart read at higher $t$ |
| Commit protocol | 2PC over Paxos groups + commit wait | Parallel commits over Raft ranges |
| Guarantee | Strict serializability + external consistency | Serializability + single-key linearizability |
| Read latency | Snapshot reads at $t \le$ safe time | Local reads; refresh on contention |
| Infrastructure | Specialized clock hardware | Commodity servers |

### 4.5 Externally consistent 2PC: the unified commit recipe

Both systems instantiate the same abstract recipe for a distributed commit that respects real-time order. We state it in TLA⁺-flavored pseudocode:

```tla
---- MODULE ExternallyConsistentCommit ----
VARIABLES prepared, commitTs, committed, TT

ChooseTimestamp(txn) ==
    LET s == Max(ReadTimestamps(txn) \cup {TT.now.latest})
    IN  commitTs' = [commitTs EXCEPT ![txn] = s]

CommitWait(txn) ==
    /\ prepared[txn]
    /\ TT.after(commitTs[txn])        \* wait until s is certainly past
    /\ committed' = [committed EXCEPT ![txn] = TRUE]
    /\ UNCHANGED <<prepared, commitTs, TT>>
```

CockroachDB replaces `TT.after` with a weaker condition: the transaction's commit timestamp must exceed the HLC-derived *closed timestamp* of every range it touched, and any read that could have observed a conflicting write is restarted instead of waited for [4][5]. The recipe is identical; only the uncertainty oracle differs. For **event sourcing**, the recipe yields a precise append semantics:

- *Causal appends* (HLC rule): every event's position respects happened-before; consumers replaying the log never see an effect before its cause.
- *Externally consistent appends* (commit-wait rule): once an append is acknowledged, any causally dependent append issued afterwards receives a strictly greater log position — the log order matches the order in which clients observed commits.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We collect the quantitative claims from the primary sources into a single latency model.

**Result 1 — Commit-wait overhead is bounded by $2\varepsilon$ and usually hidden.** Corbett et al. report TrueTime $\varepsilon$ "generally less than 10 ms," and the commit wait runs concurrently with Paxos log replication [2]. Google's engineering blog confirms the wait "involves no waiting" the vast majority of the time because replication dominates [3]. Effective added latency: $\max(0,\, 2\varepsilon - T_{replication}) \approx 0$ for cross-datacenter transactions.

**Result 2 — HLC timestamps stay within $\Delta$ of wall time.** Kulkarni et al. prove that the physical component of an HLC timestamp never leads the node's wall clock by more than the maximum skew bound, and the receive rule ensures that a single fast peer cannot ratchet the cluster's clocks arbitrarily forward [1]. In CockroachDB, exceeding the 500 ms `max_clock_offset` causes the node to be fenced (crash or refuse work) rather than corrupt ordering [4].

**Result 3 — Uncertainty refresh restarts are rare under low contention.** In CockroachDB's design, a read restarts only when it encounters a write inside its uncertainty window $[t_r, t_r + \Delta]$ on the same key [5]. With 500 ms windows, write-heavy contended keys pay restarts; the common uncontended case pays nothing — a deliberate inversion of Spanner's cost profile, which always pays (usually hidden) wait at commit.

**Result 4 — Parallel commits halve distributed-commit latency.** By recording the commit decision and the in-flight intent list in a single Raft write, CockroachDB's parallel commit protocol reduces a multi-range commit from two consensus rounds to effectively one plus asynchronous resolution [4].

**Result 5 — The external-consistency gap without TrueTime is exactly one out-of-band channel.** CockroachDB's own analysis concedes that strict serializability fails only when clients coordinate through an external low-latency channel faster than the uncertainty machinery can track [4]. Palmieri et al. formalize this boundary: achieving external consistency without a TrueTime-like oracle requires replacing commit-wait with vector-clock-style conflict detection and write-after-read blocking, as in their SSS protocol [7].

Taken together, these results quantify the fundamental tradeoff of geo-replicated event sourcing: *the stronger the real-time ordering guarantee, the more the protocol must either invest in clock hardware (Spanner), pay restarts under contention (CockroachDB), or block writes behind reads (SSS)* [2][4][7].

## 6 Limitations

1. **HLCs do not bound real-time error by themselves.** The clock condition gives *causal* order, not real-time order. Two causally unrelated events can receive HLC timestamps that invert their true order if clocks drift within the skew bound — HLC alone cannot manufacture external consistency, which is why commit-wait or uncertainty refresh remains necessary [1][4].
2. **The skew bound is an assumption, not a guarantee.** Both designs assume the configured bound ($\Delta = 500$ ms for CockroachDB, measured $\varepsilon$ for Spanner) holds. A pathological NTP failure that exceeds the bound silently invalidates the guarantees; CockroachDB's answer is fencing, which trades availability for correctness [4].
3. **Commit wait couples latency to clock health.** When TrueTime's $\varepsilon$ degrades (e.g., GPS outages), Spanner's write latency degrades with it by design — correctness is preserved, but the "usually free" wait becomes a real one [2].
4. **Event-sourcing consumers inherit read-your-writes anomalies across designs.** Under CockroachDB semantics, a client that appends an event and immediately reads the log tail at a *stale* timestamp may not observe its own append; only present-time reads with uncertainty refresh are safe [5]. Application code must be written against the exact timestamp semantics, not an idealized "the log" abstraction.
5. **This analysis is literature-based.** We reconstruct and compare published protocols rather than benchmarking them; the latency numbers are the systems' own reported figures [2][4]. A controlled cross-system measurement of commit latency versus injected clock skew remains future work.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Geo-replicated event sourcing is, at bottom, a problem of trustworthy time. **Hybrid Logical Clocks** give us timestamps that are honest about causality and approximately honest about the wall clock [1]. **Uncertainty windows** make the residual dishonesty explicit and therefore manageable [2][5]. And **commit protocols that wait out or ratchet past that uncertainty** — Spanner's commit wait, CockroachDB's uncertainty refresh and parallel commits, the SSS blocking scheme — convert raw timestamps into the ordering guarantees that applications actually need: serializability, single-key linearizability, and, at the top of the ladder, external consistency [2][4][7].

The unified view we have developed suggests a practical design rule for the event-sourcing architect: *choose the uncertainty oracle first*. If you can afford engineered time (TrueTime), pay the commit wait and get external consistency nearly for free. If you must run on commodity clocks, use HLCs, accept serializability without strict serializability, and design your consumers around timestamped reads with refresh semantics. Either way, the log you append to is only as trustworthy as the clock discipline behind it — and that discipline, as this thesis has shown, is now a solved engineering problem with precisely characterized costs.
