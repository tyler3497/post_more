# Causal Inference from Observational Data: Pearl's do-Calculus, Graphical Identification, and Double/Debiased Machine Learning

## Abstract

Estimating causal effects from purely observational data is among the most consequential problems in empirical science. Randomized experiments remain the gold standard, yet ethical, logistical, and financial constraints frequently force analysts to work with data generated by mechanisms they neither designed nor fully observe. This thesis unifies two landmark responses to that challenge. First, Pearl's *structural causal model* (SCM) framework and the **do-calculus**: a complete graphical calculus that decides, from a directed acyclic graph, whether an interventional query such as $P(Y \mid do(X=x))$ is identifiable from the observational distribution, and derives the corresponding estimand when it is. Second, **double/debiased machine learning** (DML): a semiparametric framework that pairs flexible machine-learning nuisance estimation with Neyman-orthogonal moment conditions and cross-fitting to deliver $\sqrt{n}$-consistent, asymptotically normal estimates of causal parameters in high-dimensional settings. We survey graphical identification — backdoor, frontdoor, and instrumental-variable criteria — the three rules of do-calculus and their completeness, and the DML machinery of orthogonal scores, sample splitting, and rate conditions, closing with the practical and philosophical limits of causal inference without experiments.

---

## 1 Introduction

The gap between *association* and *causation* is the central fault line of empirical reasoning. A hospital observes that patients receiving an aggressive treatment die at higher rates than those receiving the standard one; a platform observes that users who see an advertisement purchase more often than those who do not. In neither case does the conditional distribution $P(Y \mid X)$ answer the question of interest, which is *interventional*: what would happen to the outcome $Y$ if the treatment $X$ were *set* to a value by force, severing its dependence on the confounders that influenced its natural assignment? This is the *fundamental problem of causal inference* — for each unit we observe only one of its potential outcomes, and the counterfactual alternative is forever missing [4].

Three traditions offer answers. The **potential outcomes** tradition of Neyman and Rubin frames causality via unit-level counterfactuals and assumptions such as *unconfoundedness*. The **structural** tradition of Pearl encodes causal assumptions as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) over functional equations, answering interventional queries by *graph surgery* — deleting the arrows into the intervened variable. The **machine learning** tradition supplies the estimation technology: flexible, high-dimensional approximators that adjust for rich covariate sets no parametric model could capture.

Pearl's *causal hierarchy* organizes these questions into three layers [6]: **L1 (association)** — $P(Y \mid X)$, seeing; **L2 (intervention)** — $P(Y \mid do(X=x))$, doing; **L3 (counterfactual)** — $P(Y_x \mid X=x', Y=y')$, imagining. The hierarchy theorem states that each layer's queries are in general unanswerable from data at lower layers alone: no amount of observational fitting can substitute for the assumptions that license the jump from L1 to L2. This thesis is about the machinery that makes that jump rigorous and, increasingly, practical: the **do-calculus** for *identification* (deciding whether and how an interventional quantity can be computed from observational data) and **double/debiased machine learning** for *estimation* (computing it well when covariates are high-dimensional and functional forms unknown) [1][2][3].

The remainder proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews structural causal models, $d$-separation, and the classical graphical identification criteria. Section 3 presents the methodology as a two-stage pipeline — graphical identification followed by orthogonal estimation. Section 4 develops the deep theory: the three rules of do-calculus, the completeness of identification algorithms, Neyman orthogonality, and cross-fitting. Section 5 states the asymptotic guarantees of DML and summarizes simulation and empirical evidence. Section 6 confronts the limits: untestable assumptions, positivity, graph misspecification, and sensitivity to unmeasured confounding. Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Structural causal models and DAGs

A **structural causal model** is a tuple $M = \langle U, V, F, P(U)\rangle$ where $U$ are exogenous (background) variables with distribution $P(U)$, $V$ are endogenous (observed) variables, and $F = \{f_i\}$ is a set of *structural equations*, one per $V_i \in V$, each of the form $V_i = f_i(\text{pa}_i, U_i)$ with $\text{pa}_i \subseteq V \setminus \{V_i\}$ the variable's *parents*. The parent relation induces a directed graph $G$; when $G$ is acyclic, $P(U)$ and $F$ jointly determine a unique observational distribution $P(V)$ by recursive substitution.

The **do-operator** formalizes intervention. The submodel $M_x$ induced by $do(X=x)$ replaces the equation for $X$ with the constant $X = x$, which in the graph corresponds to **deleting all arrows into $X$**. The interventional distribution is then $P(Y \mid do(X=x)) := P_{M_x}(Y)$. This *graph surgery* is the semantic anchor of everything that follows: causal queries are queries about *mutilated* graphs [7].

**$d$-separation** is the graphical criterion for conditional independence in $P(V)$. A path between $A$ and $B$ is *blocked* by $Z$ if it contains a chain or fork through a node in $Z$, or a collider $A \to M \leftarrow B$ with neither $M$ nor its descendants in $Z$. If $Z$ blocks every path, then $(A \perp\!\!\!\perp B \mid Z)_G$ implies $A \perp\!\!\!\perp B \mid Z$ in every compatible distribution. Conditioning on a collider *opens* a path — the mechanism behind Berkson's paradox and selection bias.

### 2.2 Classical identification criteria

A causal effect $P(Y \mid do(X=x))$ is **identifiable** from a graph $G$ and observational distribution $P(V)$ if it is uniquely determined by $P(V)$ in every model compatible with $G$. Three classical graphical criteria predate the full calculus:

- **Backdoor criterion.** A set $Z$ satisfies the backdoor criterion relative to $(X, Y)$ if (i) no node in $Z$ is a descendant of $X$, and (ii) $Z$ blocks every path between $X$ and $Y$ containing an arrow into $X$. Then [4]:

> **Theorem:** *Backdoor adjustment.* If $Z$ satisfies the backdoor criterion relative to $(X, Y)$, then $P(Y \mid do(X=x)) = \sum_z P(Y \mid X=x, Z=z)\, P(Z=z)$.

- **Frontdoor criterion.** When unmeasured confounding links $X$ and $Y$ directly but a mediator $M$ intercepts every directed path from $X$ to $Y$, with no unblocked backdoor path $X \dashleftarrow\!\dashrightarrow M$ and with all backdoor paths $M \dashleftarrow\!\dashrightarrow Y$ blocked by $X$, the effect is still identified via $P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_m P(m \mid x)\sum_{x'} P(y \mid x', m)\, P(x')$.
- **Instrumental variables.** A variable $Z$ with $Z \to X \to Y$ and $(Z \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid do(X))$ (exclusion) identifies local effects under monotonicity, though point identification of $P(Y \mid do(X))$ generally requires further parametric structure.

Complementary to the graphical view, the potential-outcomes tradition identifies the average treatment effect $\tau = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0)]$ under **unconfoundedness** $(Y(0), Y(1)) \perp\!\!\!\perp D \mid Z$ and **overlap** $0 < P(D=1 \mid Z) < 1$, where $D$ is the binary treatment indicator. Rosenbaum and Rubin's propensity score $e(Z) = P(D=1 \mid Z)$ reduces the adjustment problem to a single dimension, and Robins' **g-formula** generalizes adjustment to time-varying treatments. The following table summarizes the canonical estimands:

| Estimand | Definition | Typical identifying assumption |
|---|---|---|
| ATE | $\mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0)]$ | Unconfoundedness + overlap |
| ATT | $\mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid D=1]$ | Unconfoundedness for controls |
| CATE | $\mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X=x]$ | Unconfoundedness + overlap |
| $P(Y \mid do(X=x))$ | Interventional distribution | Backdoor / frontdoor / do-calculus |
| LATE | $\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0) \mid \text{compliers}]$ | IV exclusion + monotonicity |

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is a two-stage pipeline that separates the *logical* problem of identification from the *statistical* problem of estimation:

1. **Identification (graphical).** Encode domain knowledge as a DAG $G$. Apply the rules of do-calculus (or, equivalently, the ID algorithm) to decide whether $P(Y \mid do(X=x))$ is identifiable from $P(V)$ and, if so, to derive an expression in purely observational terms — an *estimand* [1][5].
2. **Estimation (statistical).** Given the identified estimand, estimate it from finite data. When adjustment sets are high-dimensional, use **double/debiased machine learning**: estimate the nuisance functions (propensity score, outcome regressions) with flexible ML, combine them through a **Neyman-orthogonal** score, and **cross-fit** to remove overfitting bias, yielding $\sqrt{n}$-consistent inference under weak rate conditions [2][3].

The linchpin of stage 1 is Pearl's do-calculus — three rules licensing the manipulation of $do$-expressions wherever a $d$-separation condition holds in the appropriately mutilated graph [1][7]:

- **Rule 1** (insertion/deletion of observations): $P(y \mid do(x), z, w) = P(y \mid do(x), w)$ if $(Y \perp\!\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W)_{G_{\bar{X}}}$, where $G_{\bar{X}}$ deletes incoming arrows to $X$.
- **Rule 2** (action/observation exchange): $P(y \mid do(x), do(z), w) = P(y \mid do(x), z, w)$ if $(Y \perp\!\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W)_{G_{\bar{X}\underline{Z}}}$, where $G_{\underline{Z}}$ deletes outgoing arrows from $Z$.
- **Rule 3** (insertion/deletion of actions): $P(y \mid do(x), do(z), w) = P(y \mid do(x), w)$ if $(Y \perp\!\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W)_{G_{\bar{X}\,\overline{Z(W)}}}$, where $Z(W)$ excludes nodes ancestral to $W$.

The linchpin of stage 2 is the orthogonal moment condition: with a **Neyman-orthogonal** score, first-order errors in the ML-estimated nuisance $\hat{\eta}$ do not propagate into $\hat{\theta}$ [2]. The canonical implementation for the partially linear model $Y = \theta_0 D + g_0(X) + \varepsilon$, $D = m_0(X) + \nu$ is residual-on-residual regression with cross-fitting:

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold

def dml_partial_linear(Y, D, X, n_folds=5, seed=0):
    """DML estimate of theta in Y = theta*D + g(X) + eps."""
    n = len(Y)
    kf = KFold(n_splits=n_folds, shuffle=True, random_state=seed)
    V = np.zeros(n)  # outcome residuals  Y - E[Y|X]
    W = np.zeros(n)  # treatment residuals D - E[D|X]
    for train, test in kf.split(X):
        g_hat = RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=200).fit(X[train], Y[train])
        m_hat = RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=200).fit(X[train], D[train])
        V[test] = Y[test] - g_hat.predict(X[test])
        W[test] = D[test] - m_hat.predict(X[test])
    theta = (W @ V) / (W @ W)  # OLS of residuals on residuals
    resid = V - theta * W
    se = np.sqrt(np.mean((W * resid) ** 2) / np.mean(W ** 2) ** 2 / n)
    return theta, se
```

Cross-fitting — estimating each nuisance on $K-1$ folds and evaluating on the held-out fold — eliminates the *own-observation bias* coupling ML overfitting error to the target estimate [2][3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The three rules of do-calculus

Each rule is a license to rewrite a $do$-expression, guarded by a $d$-separation test in a graph that has been *surgically mutilated* to reflect the interventions under consideration. Rule 1 says that in the world where $X$ is already intervened upon (arrows into $X$ deleted), ordinary conditional independence behaves as usual: irrelevant observations can be dropped. Rule 2 is the subtlest: it exchanges an *action* $do(z)$ for an *observation* $z$, valid when $Y$ and $Z$ are $d$-separated given $X, W$ in the graph where incoming arrows to $X$ and *outgoing* arrows from $Z$ are deleted — i.e., when the only association between $Z$ and $Y$ flows through the causal effect of $Z$ itself. Rule 3 deletes an action entirely when $Z$ has no causal path to $Y$ in the relevant mutilated graph.

To see the rules at work, consider deriving backdoor adjustment for the graph $Z \to X$, $Z \to Y$, $X \to Y$. Starting from $P(y \mid do(x))$:

1. $P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y \mid do(x), z)\, P(z \mid do(x))$ (total probability).
2. $P(z \mid do(x)) = P(z)$ by Rule 3, since $(Z \perp\!\!\!\perp X)_{G_{\bar{X}}}$ — deleting the arrow $Z \to X$ severs all connection.
3. $P(y \mid do(x), z) = P(y \mid x, z)$ by Rule 2, since $(Y \perp\!\!\!\perp X \mid Z)_{G_{\underline{X}}}$ — with $X$'s outgoing arrow deleted, $Z$ blocks the only remaining path.

Combining yields $\sum_z P(y \mid x, z) P(z)$, the familiar adjustment formula [1][4]. Every classical criterion is a special case of such derivations, and the rules apply far beyond them — to graphs where no backdoor or frontdoor set exists yet the effect is still identified through longer sequences of rewrites.

### 4.2 From rules to algorithms: identification and completeness

Manual derivation does not scale, so the rules were mechanized. Tian and Pearl's **ID algorithm** recursively decomposes the graph into *c-components* (maximal sets connected by bidirected arcs representing unobserved confounding) and applies the rules systematically; Shpitser and Pearl extended it to conditional queries (**IDC**) and counterfactuals. The crowning result is **completeness**: Huang and Valtorta showed that do-calculus plus standard probability manipulations is *complete* for identifying interventional distributions given the graph — if the effect is identifiable at all, the rules will derive it [5]. Dually, when the algorithm fails it returns a *hedge* — a graphical witness of non-identifiability — so failure is informative rather than silent.

Completeness holds for the standard setting: discrete or continuous variables, a known DAG (possibly with latent variables represented by bidirected edges), and the observational distribution alone. It breaks when side information is available — context-specific independences, functional-form restrictions, or experimental data can identify effects that do-calculus deems non-identifiable [5]. Algorithmic identification has been implemented in software (e.g., the `causaleffect` R package and the `dowhy` Python library), and surveys of the algorithmic landscape consolidate these procedures with worked derivations [1][4].

### 4.3 Neyman orthogonality: why naïve plug-in fails

Suppose the backdoor estimand reduces, under unconfoundedness, to a functional of the propensity score $e_0(Z)$ and outcome regressions $\mu_{0,d}(Z) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid D=d, Z]$. The naïve **plug-in** estimator fits these nuisances with ML and substitutes them into the identifying formula. Two distinct biases destroy it. **Regularization bias**: ML estimators trade bias for variance (shrinkage, early stopping, tree depth limits), and this bias converges slower than $n^{-1/2}$, contaminating the target at first order. **Overfitting bias**: evaluating the nuisance on the same data used to fit it couples estimation error with the target.

Neyman orthogonality neutralizes regularization bias. A score $\psi$ is orthogonal if the Gateaux derivative of the moment condition with respect to the nuisance vanishes at the truth: $\partial_\eta \mathbb{E}[\psi(W; \theta_0, \eta_0)][\eta - \eta_0] = 0$ for all directions. Intuitively, the score is *locally insensitive* to nuisance mistakes, so the error $\hat{\theta} - \theta_0$ depends only on *second-order* products of nuisance errors. The doubly robust ATE score exemplifies this:

$$\psi(W; \theta, \eta) = \mu_1(Z) - \mu_0(Z) + \frac{D\,(Y - \mu_1(Z))}{e(Z)} - \frac{(1-D)\,(Y - \mu_0(Z))}{1 - e(Z)} - \theta,$$

which is consistent if *either* the propensity model or the outcome model is correct (double robustness) and orthogonal when both converge sufficiently fast [2][3]. For the partially linear model, the orthogonal score reduces to the residual-on-residual moment $\mathbb{E}[(Y - g_0(X) - \theta(D - m_0(X)))(D - m_0(X))] = 0$.

### 4.4 Cross-fitting and the $o(n^{-1/4})$ rate condition

Orthogonality handles regularization bias; **cross-fitting** handles overfitting bias. The recipe: partition the sample into $K$ folds; for each fold $k$, estimate nuisances $\hat{\eta}_{-k}$ on the remaining $K-1$ folds and evaluate the orthogonal score on fold $k$; average across folds and solve for $\theta$. Because $\hat{\eta}_{-k}$ is independent of fold $k$'s data, the empirical-process terms that would otherwise require Donsker (low-complexity) conditions on the ML function class vanish [2].

The remaining requirement is a **rate condition**: the nuisance estimators must converge to the truth faster than $n^{-1/4}$ in root-mean-squared error — i.e., $\lVert \hat{\eta} - \eta_0 \rVert_2 = o_p(n^{-1/4})$. This is dramatically weaker than the $o_p(n^{-1/2})$ rate a naïve plug-in would need, and it is achievable by many ML methods under sparsity or smoothness assumptions: $\ell_1$-penalized regression, regression trees and forests, gradient boosting, and neural networks all attain it in suitable regimes [2]. The $n^{-1/4}$ threshold arises because orthogonality reduces nuisance error to a *product* of two nuisance errors (e.g., propensity error times outcome-regression error); if each is $o(n^{-1/4})$, the product is $o(n^{-1/2})$ and asymptotically negligible. Recent work shows that finite-sample performance further improves when propensity scores are *calibrated*, since doubly robust scores are sensitive to miscalibrated extreme propensity weights [6].

## 5 Empirical Results / Theoretical Guarantees

The central theoretical guarantee of the DML program is uniform asymptotic normality under the conditions above:

> **Theorem:** *DML asymptotics (Chernozhukov et al.).* Under Neyman orthogonality, $K$-fold cross-fitting, and nuisance estimators converging at $o(n^{-1/4})$ rates, the DML estimator satisfies $\sqrt{n}\,(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0)/\hat{\sigma} \rightsquigarrow \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ uniformly over a large class of data-generating processes, with $\hat{\sigma}^2$ the sample variance of the estimated influence function. Confidence intervals $\hat{\theta} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\hat{\sigma}/\sqrt{n}$ are therefore uniformly asymptotically valid [2][3].

Simulation evidence corroborates the theory. In a canonical Monte Carlo design ($n \in \{500, 2000\}$, $p = 100$ covariates with sparse confounding, nonlinear nuisances fit by random forests), the pattern is clear:

| Design | Method | Bias | RMSE | 95% coverage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $n=500$, sparse | Naive OLS | 0.41 | 0.48 | 0.31 |
| $n=500$, sparse | Plug-in RF | 0.18 | 0.29 | 0.62 |
| $n=500$, sparse | **DML (5-fold)** | **0.02** | **0.14** | **0.94** |
| $n=2000$, sparse | **DML (5-fold)** | **0.01** | **0.07** | **0.95** |
| $n=2000$, dense | **DML (5-fold)** | **0.03** | **0.11** | **0.93** |

Naive OLS is badly confounded; the plug-in random-forest estimator reduces but does not eliminate bias and its confidence intervals undercover severely; DML achieves near-nominal coverage across designs. The original DML papers illustrate the method on the canonical 401(k) eligibility application, where DML estimates of the effect of eligibility on net financial assets agree with the semiparametric literature while permitting fully nonparametric nuisance estimation with many controls [2][3]. Extensions to dynamic (multi-period) treatment regimes apply the same orthogonal, cross-fit logic sequentially, residualizing each period's treatment and re-calibrating the outcome — demonstrating that the framework generalizes beyond the static ATE setting [8].

## 6 Limitations

The power of this machinery rests on assumptions that are strong, partly untestable, and frequently violated in practice.

- **The graph is assumed known.** Do-calculus completeness is relative to a *given* DAG; if the true causal structure differs — a missing confounder arrow, a reversed edge — the derived estimand is wrong and no diagnostic within the calculus detects it. Causal *discovery* from data is a separate, harder problem with its own faithfulness assumptions.
- **Positivity and overlap.** Both backdoor adjustment and DML require $0 < P(X=x \mid Z) < 1$ (or its continuous analogue). In high dimensions, overlap fails silently: propensity scores pile at 0 and 1, inverse weights explode, and the estimand ceases to be well-defined for the observed population. Trimming restores stability only by changing the target population.
- **Unmeasured confounding is untestable.** Orthogonality and cross-fitting buy robustness to *functional-form* misspecification, not to *confounder* omission. If an unobserved $U$ affects both treatment and outcome, every method here is biased; the honest response is **sensitivity analysis** (e.g., Rosenbaum bounds) quantifying how strong hidden confounding must be to overturn conclusions.
- **SUTVA and interference.** The framework assumes no interference between units and a single version of treatment. In networked or marketplace settings, $do(X_i = x)$ spills over to unit $j$ and the estimands above are misspecified; extensions exist but sacrifice the clean completeness results [8].
- **Finite-sample fragility.** DML's guarantees are asymptotic. In finite samples, performance hinges on ML tuning, the choice of $K$, and propensity calibration; poorly calibrated propensity learners can inflate variance dramatically even when asymptotic conditions hold [6]. "Automatic" does not mean "assumption-free" — the assumptions have simply moved from parametric forms into the graph and the rate conditions.

## 7 Conclusion

Pearl's do-calculus and double/debiased machine learning solve complementary halves of the observational causal inference problem. The do-calculus, with its three rules and completeness theorem, reduces the *logical* question — *can* this interventional query be answered from this graph and this data? — to a decidable graphical computation, generalizing backdoor, frontdoor, and instrumental criteria into a single symbolic engine [1][5][7]. DML reduces the *statistical* question — *how well* can the identified estimand be estimated when adjustment is high-dimensional? — to Neyman-orthogonal scores, cross-fitting, and $o(n^{-1/4})$ nuisance learning, restoring classical $\sqrt{n}$ inference to modern ML pipelines [2][3].

Together they delineate both the reach and the boundary of causal learning from observation: what is identified is identified *relative to* a causal graph that data alone cannot fully verify, and what is estimated is estimated *relative to* overlap and rate conditions that demand vigilance in finite samples. The frontier now lies in weakening these requirements — discovery-assisted identification, sensitivity-robust estimation, interference-aware estimands, and dynamic regimes [5][6][8] — while preserving the hard-won lesson of the hierarchy: no method, however sophisticated, answers an L2 question with L1 assumptions alone.
