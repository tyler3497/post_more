# Neural Operators for Parametric PDE Surrogates: Fourier Neural Operators with Tucker Factorization, DeepONet Branch–Trunk Encoding, Physics-Informed Residuals, and Zero-Shot Super-Resolution on the Navier–Stokes Equations

## Abstract

We develop a unified treatment of neural operators as parametric partial differential equation (PDE) surrogates, concentrating on three architectural families — Fourier Neural Operators (FNO), DeepONets, and physics-informed neural operators — applied to the two-dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes equations in the turbulent regime. We formalize operator learning as regression between Banach spaces, derive the spectral convolution layer of FNO [1], and show how Tucker factorization compresses the spectral weight tensors to sub-quadratic parameter scaling in the spatial dimension. We contrast this with the branch–trunk factorization of DeepONet [2], grounded in the Chen–Chen universal approximation theorem for operators, and show how physics-informed neural network (PINN) collocation residuals [3] inject governing-law constraints into operator training, following the physics-informed neural operator formulation [4]. A central contribution is the mechanism of zero-shot super-resolution: because learned parameters live in Fourier space rather than on any fixed grid, a model trained on $64\times 64$ vorticity fields evaluates directly on $256\times 256$ grids with no retraining. We derive the discretization-invariance argument, analyze complexity, and discuss failure modes including spectral bias, aliasing, and data-generation cost in turbulent regimes.

## 1. Introduction

Classical numerical methods for partial differential equations — finite elements, finite differences, spectral methods — solve *one instance* of an equation at a time. For design optimization, uncertainty quantification, and real-time control, where thousands of forward evaluations are required, this *solve-from-scratch* paradigm becomes the dominant computational bottleneck.

*Operator learning* reframes the problem: instead of learning a map $\mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^k$ between finite-dimensional vectors, we learn an operator $\mathcal{G}: \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{U}$ between infinite-dimensional function spaces, where $\mathcal{A}$ is a space of parameter functions (initial conditions, coefficients, forcings) and $\mathcal{U}$ is the solution space [1][2]. A trained neural operator approximates the entire solution operator of a parametric PDE family in a *single* offline training phase; inference then reduces to a forward pass, typically two to three orders of magnitude faster than the numerical solver that generated the training data [1].

This article synthesizes three pillars of the modern operator-learning literature. **First**, the Fourier Neural Operator [1], which parameterizes the integral kernel of a kernel-integral layer directly in Fourier space, and its factorized variants [5] that use Tucker-style tensor decompositions [7] to break the curse of dimensionality in higher spatial dimensions. **Second**, DeepONet [2], the branch–trunk architecture whose universality rests on a classical theorem of Chen and Chen, and which remains the most flexible framework for irregular geometries and multi-input operators. **Third**, the physics-informed paradigm [3][4], where PDE residuals evaluated at collocation points via automatic differentiation supply a governing-law loss that complements scarce data. We unify these strands around a single demanding testbed — two-dimensional Navier–Stokes turbulence — and explain the striking *zero-shot super-resolution* property shared by spectrally parameterized operators.

> **Central claim.** Neural operators are not merely fast surrogates: they are *discretization-invariant* approximations of infinite-dimensional maps. Their parameters encode operators, not grids, which is what permits training at low resolution and inference at high resolution without modification.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews operator learning, the Navier–Stokes equations, and PINNs. Section 3 develops the methodology: spectral layers, Tucker factorization, branch–trunk encoding, and physics-informed residuals. Section 4 deep-dives into the four technical mechanisms named in the title. Section 5 presents empirical results and approximation-theoretic guarantees. Section 6 examines limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Operator learning

Let $D \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ be a bounded open domain and $\mathcal{A} = \mathcal{A}(D; \mathbb{R}^{d_a})$, $\mathcal{U} = \mathcal{U}(D; \mathbb{R}^{d_u})$ separable Banach spaces of functions. We seek to approximate a (typically nonlinear) operator $\mathcal{G}^\dagger: \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{U}$, where for each input function $a \in \mathcal{A}$ the output $u = \mathcal{G}^\dagger(a) \in \mathcal{U}$ is the solution of a parametric PDE. Training data consist of $N$ pairs $\{(a_j, u_j)\}_{j=1}^N$ generated by a high-fidelity solver, each observed on a discretization $\{x_1, \dots, x_n\} \subset D$. The empirical risk is

$$
\min_{\theta} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N \lVert \mathcal{G}_\theta(a_j) - u_j \rVert_{\mathcal{U}}^2,
$$

and the crucial question is whether $\mathcal{G}_\theta$ generalizes across *resolutions*: a model trained on $n$-point discretizations should be evaluable on $n'$-point discretizations for arbitrary $n'$. Architectures achieving this are called *discretization-invariant* [1][6].

### 2.2 The Navier–Stokes equations

We focus on the two-dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes equations in vorticity form on the torus $\mathbb{T}^2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_t \omega + u \cdot \nabla \omega &= \nu \Delta \omega + f, \qquad x \in \mathbb{T}^2,\ t \in (0, T], \\
\nabla \cdot u &= 0, \qquad \omega(x, 0) = \omega_0(x),
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\omega = \nabla \times u$ is the scalar vorticity, $\nu$ the kinematic viscosity, and $f$ a forcing term. The Reynolds number $\mathrm{Re} \sim \nu^{-1}$ controls the turbulence intensity. The canonical benchmark [1] uses $\nu = 10^{-3}$ (mild turbulence), $\nu = 10^{-4}$, and $\nu = 10^{-5}$ (strongly turbulent), with the operator mapping initial vorticity $\omega_0$ to the vorticity field at a later time $T$ (or a trajectory of snapshots). At $\nu = 10^{-5}$ the flow develops thin filaments and an energy cascade across scales — a severe test of any surrogate, since the solution operator must capture nonlocal, multiscale interactions.

### 2.3 Physics-informed neural networks

PINNs [3] train a neural network $u_\theta(x, t)$ to satisfy both data and physics by minimizing

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{N_u}\sum_i \lvert u_\theta(x_i, t_i) - u_i \rvert^2}_{\text{data}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{N_f}\sum_j \lvert \mathcal{F}[u_\theta](x_j, t_j) \rvert^2}_{\text{PDE residual}} + \text{BC/IC terms},
$$

where $\mathcal{F}$ is the PDE operator and its derivatives are computed exactly by automatic differentiation at *collocation points* $\{(x_j, t_j)\}$ sampled in the domain — no mesh required. For Navier–Stokes, the residuals are the momentum equations and the continuity equation $\nabla \cdot u = 0$. PINNs are mesh-free and naturally assimilate scattered data, but they solve a *single* PDE instance per training run and scale poorly to turbulent, high-Reynolds regimes [3].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Fourier Neural Operator layer

The FNO [1] constructs the operator as a composition of *kernel integral layers*. Let $v_t: D \to \mathbb{R}^{d_v}$ be the hidden representation at layer $t$. The update is

$$
v_{t+1}(x) = \sigma\!\left( W v_t(x) + \int_D \kappa(x - y)\, v_t(y)\, dy \right),
$$

where $W$ is a local linear map, $\sigma$ a nonlinearity, and $\kappa$ a translation-invariant kernel. By the convolution theorem, the integral becomes pointwise multiplication in Fourier space:

$$
\int_D \kappa(x-y) v_t(y)\, dy = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left( R \cdot \mathcal{F}(v_t) \right)(x),
$$

with $R = \mathcal{F}(\kappa)$ a complex-valued weight tensor. In practice the Fourier series is truncated to $k_{\max}$ modes per dimension, so $R \in \mathbb{C}^{k_{\max}^d \times d_v \times d_v}$. The full network is

$$
\mathcal{G}_\theta = \mathcal{Q} \circ \sigma(W_T + \mathcal{K}_T) \circ \cdots \circ \sigma(W_1 + \mathcal{K}_1) \circ \mathcal{P},
$$

where $\mathcal{P}$ lifts the input to $d_v$ channels and $\mathcal{Q}$ projects back to the output dimension. Each layer costs $O(n \log n)$ via the FFT plus $O(k_{\max}^d d_v^2)$ for the spectral multiplication — quasi-linear in resolution, and crucially the *same* tensor $R$ applies at any resolution, since modes are indexed by wavenumber, not grid index.

### 3.2 DeepONet branch–trunk encoding

DeepONet [2] takes a different route, motivated by the Chen–Chen universal approximation theorem for operators. The branch network encodes the input function $a$ sampled at $m$ fixed sensor locations $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^m$ into coefficients $[b_1, \dots, b_p]$, while the trunk network encodes the query coordinate $y$ into basis functions $[t_1(y), \dots, t_p(y)]$; the output is their inner product:

$$
\mathcal{G}_\theta(a)(y) = \sum_{k=1}^p b_k\!\left(a(x_1), \dots, a(x_m); \theta_b\right)\; t_k(y; \theta_t).
$$

> **Theorem (Chen & Chen, operator universality).** *For any continuous operator $\mathcal{G}$ mapping a compact set of continuous functions to continuous functions, and any $\varepsilon > 0$, there exist sensor points, a branch network, and a trunk network such that $\sup_{a, y} \lvert \mathcal{G}(a)(y) - \sum_k b_k(a) t_k(y) \rvert < \varepsilon$ [2].*

This factorization is a learned, nonlinear generalization of a truncated eigenfunction expansion: the trunk learns a data-adaptive basis, the branch learns the projection coefficients. Unlike FNO, DeepONet queries are pointwise in $y$, so it handles arbitrary geometries and output locations natively.

### 3.3 Physics-informed operator training

The physics-informed neural operator (PINO) [4] augments data-driven operator training with PDE residuals. After the operator predicts $\hat{u} = \mathcal{G}_\theta(a)$, the residual $\mathcal{F}[\hat{u}]$ is evaluated at collocation points using automatic differentiation (or spectral differentiation on the predicted grid), yielding

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{PINO}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{data}} + \lambda_{\text{pde}}\, \frac{1}{N_c}\sum_{j=1}^{N_c} \lVert \mathcal{F}[\mathcal{G}_\theta(a)](x_j, t_j) \rVert^2 + \lambda_{\text{ic/bc}}\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{ic/bc}}.
$$

Because the residual is computed on the *operator's output function* rather than on a single-instance network, one trained PINO amortizes the physics constraint across the entire parameter family: the PDE loss regularizes all future inferences, not just the training instances [4]. For Navier–Stokes, the collocation residuals are evaluated in vorticity–streamfunction form to enforce incompressibility exactly.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Fourier spectral layers and Tucker factorization

The parameter tensor of an FNO layer, $R \in \mathbb{C}^{k_{\max} \times k_{\max} \times d_v \times d_v}$ in 2D, grows as $k_{\max}^2 d_v^2$; in 3D it is $k_{\max}^3 d_v^2$, which quickly becomes prohibitive for turbulent flows demanding many modes. *Factorized* Fourier neural operators [5] attack this with tensor decompositions in the spirit of Tucker [7]: the full $d$-dimensional spectral transform is replaced by a sequence of one-dimensional transforms, and the weight tensor is factorized as

$$
R_{k_1, \dots, k_d} \approx \sum_{r_1=1}^{R_1} \cdots \sum_{r_d=1}^{R_d} \mathcal{C}_{r_1, \dots, r_d}\; \prod_{j=1}^d U^{(j)}_{k_j, r_j},
$$

where $\mathcal{C}$ is a small core tensor and $U^{(j)}$ are factor matrices along each spatial axis. This reduces the parameter count from $O(k_{\max}^d d_v^2)$ to $O(d\, k_{\max} R\, d_v + R^d d_v^2)$ with ranks $R_j \ll k_{\max}$ — near-linear in dimension rather than exponential. Practically, the layer applies a 1D FFT along each axis in turn, multiplies by the factor matrices, contracts the core, and inverts — a separable spectral convolution that preserves discretization invariance while slashing memory. The truncation rank becomes a tunable inductive bias: low rank enforces smooth, large-scale couplings; higher rank recovers fine-scale turbulence interactions.

![Fourier Neural Operator spectral convolution layers with FFT and iFFT blocks](/thesis/ths_1788805749359_4646-0.webp)

A minimal PyTorch-style sketch of the factorized spectral layer:

```python
class SpectralConv2dFactorized(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_ch, out_ch, modes1, modes2, rank):
        super().__init__()
        self.modes1, self.modes2 = modes1, modes2
        # Tucker factors: one complex weight matrix per axis + core
        self.U1 = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(in_ch, out_ch, modes1, rank, dtype=torch.cfloat))
        self.U2 = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(in_ch, out_ch, modes2, rank, dtype=torch.cfloat))
        self.core = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(rank, rank, dtype=torch.cfloat))

    def forward(self, x):
        # x: (B, C_in, H, W) -> FFT along each axis, factorized multiply
        xf = torch.fft.rfft2(x, norm="ortho")
        m1, m2 = self.modes1, self.modes2
        out = torch.zeros(x.shape[0], self.U1.shape[1], x.shape[2],
                          x.shape[3]//2 + 1, dtype=torch.cfloat, device=x.device)
        # separable contraction: axis-1 modes, then axis-2 modes
        tmp = torch.einsum("bixy,iokxr,rs->bokxys", xf[:, :, :m1, :], self.U1, self.core)
        out[:, :, :m1, :m2] = torch.einsum("bokxys,iokys->bokxy",
                                          tmp[:, :, :, :m2, :], self.U2)
        return torch.fft.irfft2(out, s=x.shape[-2:], norm="ortho")
```

### 4.2 Branch–trunk encoding as adaptive basis learning

Where FNO bakes translation invariance into the architecture via the Fourier basis, DeepONet learns its basis from data. The trunk network $t(y; \theta_t)$ can be interpreted as learning $p$ basis functions adapted to the solution manifold, while the branch network learns the (nonlinear) coefficient map from the parameter function. This has two underappreciated consequences:

1. **Geometry agnosticism.** Because the trunk consumes raw coordinates $y$, DeepONet evaluates at arbitrary points — curved boundaries, point clouds, and adaptive meshes are all admissible without regridding [2].
2. **Multi-input operators.** Stacked or parallel branch networks encode multiple input functions (e.g., initial condition *and* forcing *and* viscosity), fusing them before the inner product with the trunk — a natural fit for parametric families with several functional parameters.

The price is the loss of the convolutional inductive bias: without spectral structure, DeepONet must discover spatial correlations from data alone, typically requiring more training samples than FNO on periodic, translation-invariant problems.

### 4.3 Physics-informed residuals via PINN collocation

Data-only operator training inherits the biases of the data generator and fails silently outside the training distribution. Physics-informed residuals [3][4] counter this in three ways:

- **Extrapolation regularization.** The PDE residual penalizes non-physical outputs even where no training data exist, flattening the loss landscape along the constraint manifold.
- **Data efficiency.** PINO [4] attains comparable accuracy with substantially fewer high-fidelity trajectories, because each collocation point contributes a physics constraint essentially for free (no solver call required).
- **Inverse capability.** Making PDE coefficients (e.g., viscosity $\nu$) trainable alongside the operator converts the surrogate into a discovery tool: sparse observations plus the residual loss identify unknown parameters, exactly as in classical PINNs [3] but amortized over the parameter family.

Implementation detail matters: for incompressible flow, computing residuals in *vorticity–streamfunction* form enforces $\nabla \cdot u = 0$ identically, avoiding the pressure–velocity coupling instabilities that plague primitive-variable PINNs. Collocation points are best sampled with a mixture of uniform draws and residual-adaptive refinement — concentrating points where $\lVert \mathcal{F}[\hat{u}] \rVert$ is large.

### 4.4 Zero-shot super-resolution

The most striking property of spectrally parameterized operators is *zero-shot super-resolution* [1]: train on $64 \times 64$ vorticity fields, evaluate on $256 \times 256$ — or any resolution — with no fine-tuning. The mechanism is disarmingly simple. The learnable object $R$ is indexed by *wavenumber* $k$, not by grid index. At inference on a finer grid, the FFT simply produces more Fourier coefficients; the trained modes $k \le k_{\max}$ are multiplied by the learned $R$, while modes beyond $k_{\max}$ are passed through or zero-filled. The network never learned anything grid-specific, so nothing needs to change.

Formally, if $\mathcal{G}_\theta$ is trained on discretizations with mesh size $h$, discretization invariance means the continuum operator limit exists and discrete evaluations converge to it as $h \to 0$ [1][6]. FNO satisfies this because every operation — lifting, Fourier transform, spectral multiplication, inverse transform, projection — is a discretization of a continuum operation. Contrast this with a standard CNN surrogate, whose convolutional filters are tied to pixel neighborhoods and whose fully connected heads fix the output dimension: changing the grid changes the architecture.

Empirically [1], an FNO trained at $64 \times 64$ on Navier–Stokes ($\nu = 10^{-3}$) achieves *lower* relative $L^2$ error when evaluated at $256 \times 256$ than a model trained directly at high resolution in some settings — the low-resolution training acts as a regularizer, while the physics-relevant low modes are shared across scales.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Approximation theory

Two complementary guarantees underwrite neural operators. First, the Chen–Chen theorem gives DeepONet *universality*: any continuous operator on compact function sets is approximable to arbitrary accuracy [2]. Second, Kovachki et al. establish quantitative approximation rates for neural operators, showing that the required network size scales with the complexity (e.g., the Kolmogorov $n$-width) of the solution manifold rather than the ambient discretization dimension [6]. For elliptic and parabolic solution operators with smooth dependence on parameters, the rates are dimension-independent up to logarithmic factors — the theoretical justification for beating the curse of dimensionality on parametric families.

> **Theorem (informal, Kovachki et al.).** *Let $\mathcal{G}^\dagger$ be the solution operator of a parametric PDE whose solution manifold has exponentially decaying Kolmogorov $n$-width. Then there exists a neural operator $\mathcal{G}_\theta$ with $\mathrm{size}(\theta) = O(\mathrm{polylog}(1/\varepsilon))$ achieving $\lVert \mathcal{G}_\theta - \mathcal{G}^\dagger \rVert < \varepsilon$.*

For FNO specifically, the truncation error of the spectral layer is controlled by the smoothness of the kernel: if the true integral kernel is $s$-times differentiable, retaining $k_{\max}$ modes incurs error $O(k_{\max}^{-s})$, mirroring classical spectral-method convergence [1]. This is why FNO excels on smooth solution operators and struggles precisely where spectral methods struggle — shocks and near-discontinuities.

### 5.2 Navier–Stokes benchmarks

The canonical FNO experiments [1] train on 2D Navier–Stokes trajectories ($\nu \in \{10^{-3}, 10^{-4}, 10^{-5}\}$) mapping initial vorticity to the solution at $T = 50$ (in solver time units), evaluated by relative $L^2$ error on held-out initial conditions:

- At $\nu = 10^{-3}$, FNO achieves relative errors around $10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}$, outperforming CNN, ResNet, and U-Net baselines by roughly an order of magnitude, with inference $\sim 1000\times$ faster than the pseudo-spectral solver.
- At $\nu = 10^{-5}$ (turbulent), errors rise for all methods, but FNO maintains the best accuracy, capturing the large-scale vorticity structures while smoothing the finest filaments — a manifestation of the spectral truncation bias discussed in Section 4.1.
- Zero-shot super-resolution [1]: the $64 \times 64$-trained model evaluated at $256 \times 256$ preserves its relative error, confirming discretization invariance in practice.

Physics-informed variants [4] further reduce data requirements: PINO trained with PDE residuals matches data-only FNO accuracy using a fraction of the trajectories, and the residual loss measurably improves generalization to out-of-distribution initial conditions — precisely the regime where pure regression surrogates are least trustworthy.

## 6. Limitations

No honest treatment can omit the failure modes:

1. **Spectral bias and turbulence.** Truncating at $k_{\max}$ modes discards high-wavenumber energy. For $\nu = 10^{-5}$ turbulence with a genuine energy cascade, the surrogate systematically under-resolves fine filaments. Increasing $k_{\max}$ restores accuracy but reintroduces the $O(k_{\max}^d)$ parameter blowup that Tucker factorization [5][7] only partially mitigates.
2. **Aliasing in nonlinear terms.** The pointwise nonlinearity $\sigma$ in physical space generates frequencies beyond $k_{\max}$ that alias back into the resolved modes. Dealiasing (e.g., the 2/3 rule) helps but costs resolution.
3. **Data-generation burden.** Operator learning amortizes *inference*, not *training*: thousands of high-fidelity turbulent trajectories must still be generated by classical solvers, each expensive at high Reynolds number. Physics-informed residuals [4] reduce but do not eliminate this cost.
4. **Geometry restrictions of FNO.** The FFT demands structured, typically periodic grids. Complex domains require extensions (geo-FNO, gFNO) that sacrifice some discretization invariance [1].
5. **PINN optimization pathology.** Physics-informed losses are notoriously hard to optimize: competing gradient magnitudes between data, PDE, and boundary terms, spectral bias toward low frequencies, and sensitivity to collocation sampling [3]. These pathologies transfer to PINO training.
6. **No certified error bounds.** Approximation theorems guarantee *existence* of accurate operators, not that gradient descent finds them, and provide no computable a posteriori error certificate for a trained model — a barrier to safety-critical deployment.

## 7. Conclusion

Neural operators recast PDE simulation as function-space regression, and the three architectures examined here — Fourier Neural Operators with Tucker-factorized spectral layers [1][5][7], DeepONets with branch–trunk encoding [2], and physics-informed operators with PINN collocation residuals [3][4] — collectively demonstrate that a single trained model can approximate an entire parametric family of Navier–Stokes solutions, evaluate orders of magnitude faster than classical solvers, and transfer across resolutions without retraining. The Tucker factorization is the key scalability lever, converting exponential-in-dimension parameter growth into near-linear scaling; the branch–trunk factorization is the key flexibility lever, freeing the operator from structured grids; and the physics-informed residual is the key trust lever, constraining the surrogate to the governing laws where data are absent.

The open frontier is *certifiable, geometry-flexible, turbulence-resolving* operators: architectures that retain FNO's spectral efficiency on arbitrary domains, carry PINO's physics constraints with stable optimization, and come with computable error bounds. Until then, neural operators are best deployed as fast inner-loop surrogates inside workflows — optimization, uncertainty quantification, control — where classical solvers remain the ground-truth oracle for verification.
