# From SIS to Signatures: Lattice-Based Digital Signatures via CRYSTALS-Dilithium and Falcon, the Module-LWE and Module-SIS Hardness Foundations, and the NIST Post-Quantum Standardization

## Abstract

The prospect of cryptographically relevant quantum computers has forced the cryptographic community to redesign digital signature infrastructure on entirely new hardness foundations. This thesis studies lattice-based digital signatures, centered on the two schemes selected by NIST for standardization: CRYSTALS-Dilithium (ML-DSA, FIPS 204) and Falcon (FN-DSA, FIPS 206) [4][7][8]. We trace the intellectual lineage of these schemes from Ajtai's worst-case to average-case reduction for the Short Integer Solution problem [5] through Regev's quantum reduction from approximate shortest vector problems to Learning with Errors [6], and their structured-lattice instantiations as Module-LWE and Module-SIS. We contrast the two dominant design paradigms—Fiat-Shamir with aborts in Dilithium and hash-and-sign via Gaussian preimage sampling in Falcon [7][10]—analyzing their security proofs, parameter sets, implementation characteristics, and vulnerability surface. We examine concrete cryptanalytic cost models (CoreSVP, dual attacks) and side-channel resilience including the Gaussian-sampler attacks that motivated the MITAKA variant [11]. We conclude that module-lattice signatures are the most mature post-quantum signature family, while noting open problems in tight reductions, sampling safety, and migration-scale deployment.

## 1. Introduction

Digital signatures are the trust anchor of the modern internet: they authenticate software updates, bind certificates to identities in the TLS public-key infrastructure, and authorize transactions on blockchains. The dominant constructions—RSA, ECDSA, and EdDSA—derive their security from the presumed intractability of integer factorization and the discrete logarithm problem. Both problems are solvable in polynomial time on a sufficiently large quantum computer by Shor's algorithm, and both have seen accelerating progress toward the "harvest now, decrypt later" threat model: adversaries record encrypted or authenticated traffic today with the expectation of forging or decrypting it once quantum hardware matures. The response has been the most ambitious public standardization effort in cryptographic history: the NIST Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) standardization process, launched in 2016, which attracted 82 candidate algorithms from 25 countries [8].

In July 2022, after three rounds of global cryptanalysis, NIST announced its first selection set: one key-establishment scheme, CRYSTALS-Kyber, and three digital signature schemes—CRYSTALS-Dilithium, Falcon, and SPHINCS+. Two of the three signature schemes are lattice-based. By August 2024, Dilithium was finalized as the Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (ML-DSA) under FIPS 204 [4], designated the primary general-purpose post-quantum signature standard; Falcon was designated for standardization as FN-DSA (FIPS 206), the compact alternative for bandwidth-constrained applications; and SPHINCS+ was standardized as SLH-DSA (FIPS 205) to provide a non-lattice hedge [7][8].

This thesis is structured as follows. Section 2 develops the mathematical background: lattices, the SIS and LWE problems, worst-case to average-case reductions, and the module-lattice instantiations that make these problems efficient. Section 3 describes our methodology—the analytic framework of provable-security reductions, cryptanalytic cost modeling, and comparative implementation analysis used throughout. Section 4 presents the deep dive: the Dilithium/ML-DSA construction based on Fiat-Shamir with aborts, the Falcon/FN-DSA construction based on the Gentry–Peikert–Vaikuntanathan hash-and-sign framework over NTRU lattices, and a head-to-head comparison. Section 5 states and sketches the principal security theorems and summarizes empirical cryptanalytic evidence. Section 6 enumerates the limitations and open problems, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Lattices and hard problems

An $n$-dimensional lattice $\mathcal{L} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is a discrete additive subgroup generated by integer linear combinations of $n$ linearly independent basis vectors $\mathbf{b}_1, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n$. The same lattice admits infinitely many bases, but their *quality* varies dramatically: a basis of short, nearly orthogonal vectors makes lattice problems easy, while a basis of long, skewed vectors hides that structure entirely. This asymmetry—hard to find short vectors from a "bad" basis, easy with a "good" one—is the trapdoor at the heart of lattice cryptography [1].

The two foundational computational problems are:

1. **SIS (Short Integer Solution)** [5]: Given a uniformly random matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^{n \times m}$, find a nonzero vector $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{Z}^m$ with $\lVert \mathbf{z} \rVert \le \beta$ such that $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0} \pmod q$. Ajtai showed that solving random SIS instances is at least as hard as approximating the shortest vector problem ($\mathrm{GapSVP}_\gamma$) in the worst case [5].
2. **LWE (Learning with Errors)** [6]: Given $m$ samples $(\mathbf{a}_i, b_i = \langle \mathbf{a}_i, \mathbf{s} \rangle + e_i)$ with $\mathbf{a}_i \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q^n$ uniform and $e_i$ drawn from a discrete error distribution, distinguish these from uniform randomness, or recover $\mathbf{s}$. Regev proved a *quantum* polynomial-time reduction from worst-case $\mathrm{GapSVP}$ and $\mathrm{SIVP}$ to average-case LWE [6]; Peikert later gave a classical reduction at the cost of an exponential modulus, and Brakerski et al. reduced the modulus back to polynomial [6].

> **Theorem (Regev [2], informal):** For suitable parameters $(n, q, \alpha)$, solving decision-LWE with Gaussian error of width $\alpha q$ in the average case is at least as hard as approximating $\mathrm{SIVP}_\gamma$ or $\mathrm{GapSVP}_\gamma$ to within polynomial factors in the worst case over all $n$-dimensional lattices, via a quantum reduction.

The practical consequence of these reductions is profound and historically novel: breaking a randomly generated LWE instance implies an efficient algorithm for *every* lattice of the same dimension—a far stronger guarantee than the average-case heuristics underpinning RSA. Concrete cryptosystem parameters are, however, typically set more aggressively than what the reductions strictly justify; a reference study shows that instantiating a cryptosystem whose parameters genuinely satisfy Regev's reduction requires substantially larger parameters than those used in practice, highlighting the gap between asymptotic provable security and concrete efficiency [3].

### 2.2 Structured lattices: rings and modules

Plain LWE requires $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$-sized keys, which is prohibitive. The standard remedy is to impose algebraic structure: work in the polynomial ring $R = \mathbb{Z}[X]/(X^n+1)$ for power-of-two $n$, and its quotient $R_q = R/qR$. Replacing vectors by ring elements and matrices by ring matrices yields:

- **Ring-LWE / Ring-SIS**: secrets and errors are polynomials; one ring element encodes $n$ coefficients [6].
- **Module-LWE / Module-SIS**: an intermediate abstraction—modules of rank $k$ over $R_q$, i.e., $k \times \ell$ matrices over $R_q$. This interpolates between unstructured LWE ($R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q$) and Ring-LWE ($k=1$) [7].

Module lattices hit the engineering sweet spot: keys shrink to $\mathcal{O}(kn)$ elements, arithmetic exploits the Number Theoretic Transform (NTT) in $\mathcal{O}(n \log n)$ time, and the extra algebraic structure has not yielded devastating attacks—unlike the multivariate and isogeny families, where Rainbow and SIKE were catastrophically broken during the NIST process [8]. Both Dilithium (Module-LWE/Module-SIS over $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^{256}+1)$ with $q = 2^{23} - 2^{13} + 1 = 8{,}380{,}417$) and Kyber are module-lattice schemes [4][7]; Falcon instead uses NTRU lattices, where the ring is $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^n+1)$ with the small prime $q = 12289$ and $n \in \{512, 1024\}$ [10].

### 2.3 Two signature paradigms

Lattice signature design has converged on two paradigms:

- **Fiat–Shamir with aborts** (Lyubashevsky, 2009/2012): a three-move identification protocol—commit to $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{Ay}$, receive challenge $\mathbf{c}$, respond with $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{y} + \mathbf{c}\mathbf{s}_1$—is made non-interactive by deriving $\mathbf{c}$ from a hash of the commitment and message. A rejection-sampling step discards responses whose distribution would leak the secret, ensuring $\mathbf{z}$ is statistically independent of $\mathbf{s}_1$. This is Dilithium's approach; it needs no trapdoor and no Gaussian sampling [7][4].
- **Hash-and-sign (GPV framework)** (Gentry–Peikert–Vaikuntanathan, 2007): the public key is a "bad" basis $\mathbf{A}$ of a $q$-ary lattice; the secret key is a "good" (short) basis $\mathbf{B}$ of the same lattice. To sign, hash the message to a target point $\mathbf{c}$, then use the trapdoor to sample a lattice vector $\mathbf{v}$ close to $\mathbf{c}$ from a discrete Gaussian (preimage sampling); the signature $\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{c} - \mathbf{v}$ is short and verifies as a lattice vector near $\mathbf{c}$. Falcon instantiates this over NTRU lattices, with the trapdoor sampler accelerated by Fast Fourier sampling [10].

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis follows three complementary methods. First, **provable-security analysis**: we track each scheme's unforgeability claim (EUF-CMA) back through its reduction chain—from the signature scheme, to the underlying identification or trapdoor primitive, to the average-case hardness of Module-SIS/Module-LWE or NTRU-SIS, and ultimately to worst-case lattice problems—recording where reductions are tight, where they are heuristic (random-oracle model), and where they are quantum versus classical. Second, **concrete cryptanalytic cost modeling**: we adopt the community's standard estimators, the primal and dual lattice attacks scored under the CoreSVP model (cost of one BKZ block-$\beta$ SVP oracle call $\approx 2^{0.292\beta}$ classically, $2^{0.265\beta}$ quantumly), to compare the *claimed* NIST security levels against the *estimated* bit-security of deployed parameter sets, including the third-round dual-attack refinements that slightly reduced Dilithium's estimated margins [7]. Third, **comparative implementation analysis**: we tabulate key/signature sizes, cycle counts, sampling requirements, and side-channel attack surface, drawing on the NIST status reports and subsequent implementation literature [7].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 ML-DSA / CRYSTALS-Dilithium: Fiat–Shamir with aborts over module lattices

Dilithium operates over $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^{256}+1)$ with $q = 8{,}380{,}417$. Key generation samples a random $k \times \ell$ matrix $\mathbf{A}$ over $R_q$ (expanded from a seed) and short secret vectors $\mathbf{s}_1 \in S_\eta^\ell$, $\mathbf{s}_2 \in S_\eta^k$, where $S_\eta$ denotes polynomials with coefficients uniform in $[-\eta, \eta]$. The public key is $(\rho, \mathbf{t} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}_1 + \mathbf{s}_2)$, a Module-LWE sample; $\mathbf{t}$ is compressed by dropping low-order bits, with the signer later supplying *hints* to help the verifier reconstruct the high bits [4][7].

Signing proceeds as follows [4]:

1. Sample a masking vector $\mathbf{y}$ with coefficients uniform in $[-\gamma_1+1, \gamma_1-1]$.
2. Compute $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}$ and extract its high-order bits $\mathbf{w}_1$.
3. Derive the challenge $c = H(\mu \parallel \mathbf{w}_1)$, a sparse polynomial with exactly $\tau$ coefficients in $\{-1, 1\}$.
4. Compute $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{y} + c\mathbf{s}_1$.
5. **Rejection sampling**: restart unless $\lVert \mathbf{z} \rVert_\infty < \gamma_1 - \beta$ and the low bits of $\mathbf{w} - c\mathbf{s}_2$ are small and consistent with the hint mechanism. This step is the crux: it guarantees that the distribution of $\mathbf{z}$ is independent of the secret $\mathbf{s}_1$, so signatures leak nothing [7].

Verification recomputes $\mathbf{w}_1$ approximately from $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} - c\mathbf{t}$ using the hints, re-derives $c$, and checks norm bounds.

Three design choices distinguish Dilithium. First, **no Gaussian sampling**: all randomness is uniform over power-of-two ranges or bounded intervals, making constant-time, side-channel-aware implementation dramatically easier than in Falcon [4]. Second, **hedged signing**: FIPS 204 changed the signing randomness $\rho'$ from purely deterministic (derived from secret key and message) to a hedged construction mixing in fresh randomness from an approved RBG, with an optional deterministic mode—defending against fault attacks that exploit deterministic signing [4]. Third, the security proof reduces EUF-CMA security to Module-LWE and Module-SIS via the Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts framework, in both the classical and quantum random-oracle models, with a *tight* reduction for the underlying identification scheme [4].

The standardized parameter sets are ML-DSA-44, ML-DSA-65, and ML-DSA-87, targeting NIST levels 2, 3, and 5 with public keys of 1,312 / 1,952 / 2,592 bytes and signatures of 2,420 / 3,293 (3,309 in FIPS 204) / 4,595 (4,627 in FIPS 204) bytes respectively [4][9].

```python
# Dilithium signing sketch (simplified; omits hint compression)
def dilithium_sign(sk, msg):
    A, s1, s2, t0 = sk.A, sk.s1, sk.s2, sk.t0
    while True:                                   # rejection loop
        y  = sample_uniform(-(GAMMA1-1), GAMMA1-1, L)
        w  = A @ y
        w1 = high_bits(w)
        c  = H(msg || w1)                         # sparse challenge, tau nonzero coeffs
        z  = y + c * s1
        if (norm_inf(z) < GAMMA1 - BETA
                and low_bits_ok(w - c*s2, w1)):
            h = make_hint(w - c*s2, w1)           # help verifier recover high bits
            return (z, h, c)
```

### 4.2 FN-DSA / Falcon: hash-and-sign over NTRU lattices with Fast Fourier sampling

Falcon ("Fast-Fourier Lattice-based Compact Signatures over NTRU") instantiates the GPV framework over NTRU lattices [10]. The secret consists of short polynomials $f, g, F, G \in \mathbb{Z}[X]/(X^n+1)$ satisfying the **NTRU equation**

$$fG - gF = q,$$

with $n \in \{512, 1024\}$ and $q = 12289$. The public key is the single polynomial $h = g \cdot f^{-1} \bmod q$; the secret basis is

$$ \mathbf{B} = \begin{pmatrix} g & -f \\\\ G & -F \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{A}^* = 0 \pmod q, $$

where the public basis $\mathbf{A}$ is equivalent to knowledge of $h$ [10].

To sign a message $m$: sample a random salt $r$, hash to a target $c = H(r \parallel m) \in R_q$, then use the secret basis with the **Fast Fourier Orthogonalization (FFO) sampler** to produce a short pair $(s_1, s_2)$ with $s_1 + s_2 h = c$. Since $s_1$ is determined by $c$ and $s_2$, the signature is just $(r, s_2)$, compressed to 666 bytes (Falcon-512) or 1,280 bytes (Falcon-1024); public keys are 897 / 1,793 bytes [9][10].

The FFO sampler is Falcon's crown jewel and its Achilles' heel. It recursively decomposes the Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization of $\mathbf{B}$ using FFTs, achieving $\mathcal{O}(n \log n)$ Gaussian sampling—orders of magnitude faster than generic Klein/GPV samplers. But it requires extensive **floating-point arithmetic** and a specialized discrete Gaussian sampler, which has three consequences: (i) implementations must manage floating-point precision with extreme care to avoid both correctness failures and distribution skew; (ii) the floating-point unit is a notorious side-channel surface—timing and power analysis of Gaussian samplers have repeatedly leaked secrets in lattice schemes, and Falcon's sampler demands constant-time discipline that is genuinely hard; (iii) platforms without FPUs (many IoT targets) cannot run it at all. These concerns motivated **MITAKA**, a variant replacing FFO sampling with the hybrid sampler of Ducas–Prest, adding online/offline phases, parallelizability, maskability, and even an integer-arithmetic mode [11].

Security rests on the SIS problem over NTRU lattices (equivalently, the NTRU assumption: $h = g f^{-1}$ is pseudorandom), with tight proofs in the ROM and QROM [10]. Falcon-512 targets NIST level 1 ($\approx$ AES-128 classical security) and Falcon-1024 targets level 5.

### 4.3 Head-to-head comparison

| Dimension | ML-DSA (Dilithium) | FN-DSA (Falcon) |
|---|---|---|
| Design paradigm | Fiat–Shamir with aborts | Hash-and-sign (GPV) over NTRU |
| Hardness basis | Module-LWE + Module-SIS | SIS over NTRU lattices |
| Gaussian sampling | None (uniform/bounded) | Required (FFO discrete Gaussian) |
| Floating point | No | Yes (extensive) |
| NIST levels | 2, 3, 5 (ML-DSA-44/65/87) | 1, 5 (Falcon-512/1024) |
| Public key (bytes) | 1,312 / 1,952 / 2,592 | 897 / 1,793 |
| Signature (bytes) | 2,420 / 3,293 / 4,595 | 666 / 1,280 |
| Standardization | FIPS 204 (Aug 2024) | FIPS 206 (draft/forthcoming) |
| Implementation ease | Simple, constant-time friendly | Complex; sampler is delicate |
| Key generation cost | Fast | Slow (NTRU trapdoor + FFO tree) |
| Verification cost | Fast | Fast |

The trade-off is crisp: Dilithium buys simplicity, robust side-channel posture, and stronger cryptanalytic confidence at the cost of large signatures; Falcon buys the smallest bandwidth of any NIST signature finalist at the cost of implementation fragility [7][9].

  t  <- toFFT c
  z  <- ffSampling t basisTree      -- Fast Fourier preimage sampling
  let s2 = fromFFT z               -- short; s1 = c - s2*h implied
  return (r, compress s2)

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

> **Theorem (ML-DSA EUF-CMA [4], informal):** Under the hardness of Module-LWE and Module-SIS for the FIPS 204 parameter sets, ML-DSA is existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack in the (quantum) random-oracle model. The reduction for the underlying identification scheme is tight; Fiat–Shamir with aborts contributes only a small, quantified loss.

> **Theorem (Falcon EUF-CMA [10], informal):** Under the hardness of SIS over NTRU lattices, Falcon is existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack, with tight security proofs in both the ROM and the QROM.

Empirically, security is calibrated by the best known lattice attacks, scored under the CoreSVP methodology:

1. **Primal attack**: reduce the LWE/SIS instance to unique-SVP via Kannan's embedding, then run BKZ with block size $\beta$; cost $\approx 2^{0.292\beta}$ classical operations per SVP oracle call (one of many calls). Dilithium's parameters were chosen so that the required $\beta$ meets the target NIST level [7].
2. **Dual attack**: find short vectors in the dual lattice to distinguish LWE samples from uniform; third-round refinements (MATZOV-style dual attacks with FFT tricks) lowered estimated costs, pushing two Dilithium parameter sets slightly *below* their claimed levels in the RAM model when memory-access costs are ignored—though NIST judged the margins acceptable given conservative modeling [7].
3. **NTRU-specific attacks**: Falcon's NTRU instances face dedicated attacks (subfield attacks, the "overstretched NTRU" line), but at $n \in \{512, 1024\}$, $q = 12289$—the *unstretched* regime—the best attacks remain the generic primal/dual lattice attacks [10].

Performance-wise, both schemes sign and verify in well under a millisecond on modern CPUs; Dilithium key generation is fast while Falcon's is notably slower (NTRU key generation plus FFO tree construction) [9]. Side-channel evaluation is where the record diverges: Dilithium's uniform sampling admits straightforward constant-time code, whereas Falcon's Gaussian sampler has been the subject of multiple key-recovery attacks via timing, power, and cache channels in research implementations—one driver of the MITAKA redesign, which adds provable masking [11].

## 6. Limitations

1. **The structured-lattice assumption gap.** Module-LWE, Module-SIS, and NTRU hardness are *assumptions* supported by cryptanalytic effort, not theorems derived from worst-case lattice problems. A breakthrough exploiting the ring/module structure—as happened to isogeny-based SIKE—would invalidate both schemes simultaneously, which is precisely why NIST standardized the hash-based SLH-DSA as a non-lattice hedge [8][9].
2. **Non-tight concrete reductions.** The worst-case to average-case reductions that justify lattice cryptography require parameters far larger than deployed ones; concrete security therefore rests on cryptanalytic estimation (CoreSVP), a methodology, not a proof [3]. Dual-attack refinements have already eroded estimated margins once [7].
3. **Sampler fragility (Falcon).** Correct, distribution-faithful, constant-time discrete Gaussian sampling with floating-point arithmetic is among the hardest implementation tasks in post-quantum cryptography. Deviations leak secrets; the FPU requirement excludes constrained devices [10][11].
4. **Size and agility costs.** ML-DSA signatures are 30–60$\times$ larger than ECDSA; protocol fields, certificate chains, and embedded slots designed around 64-byte signatures require redesign. Neither scheme offers the 128-byte-class compactness of Falcon-512 at higher levels without moving to Falcon-1024's slower keygen [9].
5. **Quantum random-oracle subtleties.** QROM security proofs for Fiat–Shamir signatures involve non-tight losses and ongoing debate about the right model; hedged signing in FIPS 204 mitigates some fault-attack concerns but the deterministic-vs-randomized signing debate is not fully settled [4].
6. **Migration risk.** The largest near-term risk is not cryptanalytic but operational: hybrid deployments, downgrade attacks during transition, and the long tail of un-upgradable firmware.

## 7. Conclusion

Lattice-based digital signatures have traveled, in under two decades, from Regev's and Ajtai's theoretical reductions to finalized federal standards. ML-DSA (FIPS 204) gives the world a primary post-quantum signature with a simple, side-channel-friendly design rooted in Module-LWE and Module-SIS [4][7]; FN-DSA (FIPS 206) offers the compact alternative, squeezing signatures to 666 bytes through the elegant but delicate machinery of NTRU trapdoors and Fast Fourier Gaussian sampling [10]. Their security rests on the deepest foundation in public-key cryptography—worst-case to average-case reductions for lattice problems [5][6]—tempered by the honest caveat that deployed parameters and structured variants outrun what the theorems strictly cover [3][6]. The open problems are now as much engineering as mathematical: safe samplers, tight QROM proofs, and the decade-long migration of global infrastructure. The quantum threat made new signatures necessary; lattices made them possible.
