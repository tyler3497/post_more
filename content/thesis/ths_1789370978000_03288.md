# Causal Inference: Structural Causal Models, Do-Calculus, and Double/Debiased Machine Learning

## Abstract
Estimating causal effects from observational data requires assumptions that are untestable from the joint distribution alone, yet identification and estimation can be placed on rigorous mathematical footing. This article unifies the two dominant modern paradigms of causal inference: Judea Pearl's *structural* framework, in which causal knowledge is encoded as directed acyclic graphs and manipulated with the do-operator and do-calculus, and the *Neyman-orthogonal* estimation framework of Chernozhukov and colleagues, which marries classical semiparametric theory with arbitrary machine learning methods for nuisance function estimation. We develop structural causal models and d-separation, state the three rules of do-calculus with their completeness guarantees, derive the front-door and back-door identification formulas, and then transition to the potential-outcomes view: propensity scores, doubly robust scores, Neyman orthogonality, and K-fold cross-fitting. A worked empirical study using double machine learning on a simulated treatment-effect design, together with the 401(k) eligibility application, demonstrates that the theory delivers root-n-consistent, asymptotically normal inference even when nuisance functions are estimated by random forests, gradient boosting, or neural networks. Limitations — untestable identification assumptions, positivity violations, and the fragility of graph specification — are confronted directly.

## 1 Introduction

The founding fracture of causal inference is *seeing* versus *doing*: $P(Y \mid X = x)$ describes units who happen to exhibit $x$, while $P(Y \mid do(X = x))$ describes the effect of *forcing* $X = x$. Conflating them produces Simpson's reversal, the birth-weight paradox, and nearly every applied paradox in the literature [1][4].

Two intellectual traditions have attacked this problem. The *structural* tradition, systematized by Pearl [1], treats causality as a property of mechanisms: a structural causal model (SCM) specifies how each variable is generated from its direct causes plus exogenous noise, and interventions are *surgery* on the model. The *potential-outcomes* tradition of Neyman and Rubin treats causality as a missing-data problem: each unit possesses counterfactuals $Y(1)$ and $Y(0)$, only one observed, with inference under exchangeability-type assumptions [4][6].

This article argues that their modern synthesis — graphical identification feeding Neyman-orthogonal machine-learning estimation — is the most complete practical pipeline for causal inference from observational data. We make three contributions:

1. SCMs, d-separation, and do-calculus, including the *completeness* theorems of Shpitser and Pearl [7][8] that make identification algorithmic.
2. DML from first principles: why naive plug-in ML fails for causal parameters, how Neyman orthogonality removes first-order regularization bias, and why cross-fitting removes overfitting bias [2][3].
3. A worked empirical study — simulation plus the canonical 401(k) application — with reference code in Python, Haskell, Rust, and TLA+.

The reader is assumed comfortable with probability, basic graph theory, and elementary asymptotic statistics.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The ladder of causation

Pearl's ladder of causation has three rungs [1]:

1. **Association** — $P(Y \mid X)$: purely observational, answered by the joint distribution.
2. **Intervention** — $P(Y \mid do(X))$: what happens under deliberate manipulation.
3. **Counterfactuals** — $P(Y_{x} \mid X = x', Y = y)$: retrospective reasoning about what *would have* happened.

No rung is answerable from a lower rung without extra assumptions: experiments climb to rung two by physical intervention, while observational studies must earn it through identification theory.

### 2.2 Structural causal models

> **Definition (Structural Causal Model):** An SCM is a tuple $M = \langle U, V, F, P(U) \rangle$ where $U$ is a set of exogenous (background) variables with distribution $P(U)$, $V$ is a set of endogenous (observed) variables, and $F = \{f_i\}$ is a set of structural equations $V_i = f_i(\text{pa}_i, U_i)$ assigning each endogenous variable a deterministic function of its *parents* $\text{pa}_i \subseteq V \setminus \{V_i\}$ and its exogenous noise $U_i$ [1][5].

The model is *Markovian* when the $U_i$ are independent (inducing DAG $G$), *semi-Markovian* when they may be correlated (drawn with bidirected arcs). An intervention $do(X = x)$ replaces $X$'s equation with the constant $x$ — graphically deleting every arrow *into* $X$ (the mutilated graph $G_{\bar{X}}$).

### 2.3 d-Separation and the three canonical structures

d-Separation reads independence off the graph: a path is *blocked* by $Z$ if it contains a non-collider in $Z$, or a collider (or its descendant) outside $Z$ [1]:

- **Chain** $X \to M \to Y$: conditioning on the mediator $M$ blocks the path (explains away the association).
- **Fork** $X \leftarrow Z \to Y$: conditioning on the common cause $Z$ blocks the confounding path.
- **Collider** $X \to C \leftarrow Y$: the path is blocked *unconditionally*, but conditioning on $C$ (or its descendants) **opens** it — the source of selection bias and of Berkson's paradox.

The single most consequential practical corollary: *adjusting for a collider induces spurious association between its causes*. Naive "control for everything" regression is therefore actively harmful [4].

### 2.4 The potential-outcomes view

The Rubin causal model posits potential outcomes $Y(d)$ with observed $Y = Y(D)$ under consistency and SUTVA (no interference, no hidden treatment versions) [4]. The average treatment effect is $\tau = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0)]$. Identification classically requires:

- **Unconfoundedness:** $\{Y(0), Y(1)\} \perp D \mid Z$ for observed covariates $Z$;
- **Positivity (overlap):** the propensity score $e(Z) = P(D = 1 \mid Z)$ satisfies $0 < e(Z) < 1$ almost surely;
- **Consistency:** $Y = Y(D)$.

Rosenbaum and Rubin [6] showed the propensity score is a *balancing score* — $D \perp Z \mid e(Z)$ — so adjusting for the scalar $e(Z)$ removes confounding by all of $Z$.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is a two-stage pipeline: **identification** (reducing the causal query to a statistical estimand) followed by **estimation** (Neyman-orthogonal, cross-fitted ML of that estimand).

### Identification stage

Given a DAG $G$ and query $Q = P(y \mid do(x))$, we apply do-calculus (Section 4.2) or graphical criteria (back-door, front-door) to rewrite $Q$ in terms of the observational distribution $P$. If no rewriting exists, the query is *non-identified* — no estimator can recover it without stronger assumptions — and the completeness theorems [7][8] certify that verdict as definitive.

### Estimation stage

Given an identified estimand such as $\theta_0$ in $Y = D\theta_0 + g_0(Z) + \zeta$, we:

1. Estimate the nuisance functions $\eta_0 = (g_0, m_0)$ with arbitrary ML methods on auxiliary folds.
2. Form Neyman-orthogonal scores that are first-order insensitive to nuisance error.
3. Aggregate across folds (cross-fitting) to kill overfitting bias.
4. Report $\hat{\theta}$ with influence-function standard errors [2][3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Structural Causal Models and the Graphical Machinery

SCMs rest on *modularity*: each equation $f_i$ is a stable mechanism, invariant to interventions elsewhere. This licenses counterfactual reasoning — set $X = x$ in the equations and propagate — and distinguishes an SCM from a mere factorization: two models can share the identical observational distribution $P(V)$ yet disagree on every interventional query [1][5].

d-Separation is *sound and complete*: $X \perp Y \mid Z$ holds in every distribution compatible with $G$ if and only if $Z$ d-separates $X$ and $Y$ in $G$. The "draw your assumptions before your conclusions" workflow [4] is: commit to a graph, check its implied independences against the data, then check identifiability.

| Structure | Path | Conditioning effect | Causal moral |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chain $X \to M \to Y$ | directed | blocks when conditioning on $M$ | mediators explain mechanisms |
| Fork $X \leftarrow Z \to Y$ | back-door | blocks when conditioning on $Z$ | adjust for common causes |
| Collider $X \to C \leftarrow Y$ | blocked | **opens** when conditioning on $C$ | never adjust for colliders |

### 4.2 The Three Rules of Do-Calculus

Let $G_{\bar{X}}$ delete all arrows into $X$, $G_{\underline{Z}}$ delete all arrows out of $Z$, and $Z(W)$ be the nodes of $Z$ not ancestral to $W$ in $G_{\bar{X}}$. Pearl's do-calculus [1] is three rules, valid in every semi-Markovian model:

> **Theorem (Do-calculus):** For disjoint node sets $X, Y, Z, W$ in causal DAG $G$:
>
> **Rule 1** (insertion/deletion of observations): $P(y \mid do(x), z, w) = P(y \mid do(x), w)$ whenever $Y \perp Z \mid X, W$ holds in $G_{\bar{X}}$.
>
> **Rule 2** (action/observation exchange): $P(y \mid do(x), do(z), w) = P(y \mid do(x), z, w)$ whenever $Y \perp Z \mid X, W$ holds in $G_{\bar{X}\underline{Z}}$.
>
> **Rule 3** (insertion/deletion of actions): $P(y \mid do(x), do(z), w) = P(y \mid do(x), w)$ whenever $Y \perp Z \mid X, W$ holds in $G_{\overline{X}, \overline{Z(W)}}$.

Each rule trades a $do(\cdot)$ operator for ordinary conditioning when a d-separation holds in the appropriately mutilated graph; identification is the search for a *sequence* of such rewrites terminating in a do-free expression. The back-door adjustment falls out immediately: in $G_{\bar{X}}$, any set $Z$ satisfying the back-door criterion d-separates $X$ from $Y$, whence Rule 2 yields $P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y \mid x, z)\,P(z)$.

The search is *decidable and complete*:

> **Theorem (Completeness of do-calculus — Shpitser and Pearl, 2006):** A causal effect $P(y \mid do(x))$ is identifiable from the observational distribution in a recursive semi-Markovian model if and only if it can be reduced to a do-free expression by repeated application of the three rules. The ID algorithm decides identifiability in polynomial time and returns the estimand whenever one exists [7].

Huang and Valtorta independently established completeness [7], and Shpitser and Pearl extended it to conditional effects $P(y \mid do(x), z)$ via the IDC algorithm [8]. The ID/IDC algorithms automate the search; back-door and front-door criteria remain the human-usable special cases.

### 4.3 The Front-Door Criterion and Algorithmic Identification

The *front-door criterion* identifies $P(y \mid do(x))$ even with an *unobserved* confounder $U$ on $X$ and $Y$, given a mediator $M$ satisfying three conditions [1]:

1. $M$ intercepts every directed path from $X$ to $Y$;
2. there is no unblocked back-door path from $X$ to $M$;
3. every back-door path from $M$ to $Y$ is blocked by $X$.

The estimand chains two identified pieces — the unconfounded effect of $X$ on $M$ and the effect of $M$ on $Y$ (back-door-adjusted on $X$):

$$P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_m P(m \mid x)\,\sum_{x'} P(y \mid m, x')\,P(x').$$

Remarkably, it extracts a causal effect under unmeasured confounding from observed variables alone, by exploiting mechanistic knowledge of the mediation structure — identification is a property of the *pair* (query, graph), not of the data.

Graph mutilation and Rule 2's exchange condition, encoded in Haskell:

```haskell
-- Causal DAG as adjacency map; mutilation implements do(x)
type Node = String
type DAG  = [(Node, [Node])]          -- node -> children

mutilateIn :: [Node] -> DAG -> DAG    -- G_{\bar{X}}: delete arrows INTO x
mutilateIn xs g = [(v, [c | c <- cs, not (v `elem` xs && False)])
                  | (v, cs) <- g]
  where -- keep children; drop incoming edges by filtering parents below
        incoming x = [v | (v, cs) <- g, x `elem` cs]

-- d-separation check (Bayes-ball reachability), elided for brevity
dSeparated :: DAG -> [Node] -> Node -> Node -> [Node] -> Bool
dSeparated = undefined  -- full Bayes-ball implementation

-- Rule 2: exchange do(z) for observation z when (Y _|_ Z | X) in G_{\bar{X}\underline{Z}}
rule2 :: DAG -> [Node] -> Node -> [Node] -> [Node] -> Bool
rule2 g x y z w =
  dSeparated (mutilateOut z (mutilateIn x g)) x y (z ++ w)
  where mutilateOut zs g' = [(v, [c | c <- cs, not (v `elem` zs)]) | (v, cs) <- g']
```

### 4.4 Neyman Orthogonality and Cross-Fitting

Identification tells us *what* to estimate; modern statistics tells us *how* to estimate it when covariate spaces are high-dimensional. Consider the partially linear model

$$Y = D\,\theta_0 + g_0(Z) + \zeta, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\zeta \mid D, Z] = 0,$$
$$D = m_0(Z) + V, \qquad\qquad\;\; \mathbb{E}[V \mid Z] = 0,$$

where $\theta_0$ is the causal parameter of interest and $\eta_0 = (g_0, m_0)$ are the *nuisance functions*. Naive plug-in ML estimates $\hat{g}, \hat{m}$ and solves the sample analogue of $\mathbb{E}[(Y - D\theta - g_0(Z))D] = 0$ — but regularization bias propagates *first-order* into $\hat{\theta}$, destroying root-n consistency [2].

The remedy has two parts. First, a **Neyman-orthogonal score** $\psi(W; \theta, \eta)$ whose Gateaux derivative in $\eta$ vanishes at the truth:

> **Definition (Neyman orthogonality):** A score $\psi$ is Neyman-orthogonal at $(\theta_0, \eta_0)$ if $\partial_{\eta}\,\mathbb{E}[\psi(W; \theta_0, \eta)]\big|_{\eta=\eta_0} = 0$ for all admissible directions of $\eta$ [2].

The orthogonal score is $\psi(W; \theta, \eta) = \big(Y - D\theta - g(Z)\big)\big(D - m(Z)\big)$ — *residual-on-residual* regression: partial $Z$ out of both $Y$ and $D$ (Frisch–Waugh–Lovell generalized to arbitrary ML), then regress residual on residual. Nuisance error then affects $\hat{\theta}$ only second-order, so $\lVert \hat{g} - g_0 \rVert \cdot \lVert \hat{m} - m_0 \rVert = o_p(N^{-1/2})$ suffices — e.g., each nuisance converging at $o_p(N^{-1/4})$, attainable by many ML methods [2].

Second, **cross-fitting** removes the overfitting bias from evaluating $\hat{\eta}$ and the score on the same data:

```rust
// K-fold cross-fitted DML for the partially linear model
fn dml_partial_linear(y: &[f64], d: &[f64], z: &Matrix, k: usize) -> (f64, f64) {
    let folds = stratified_folds(y.len(), k);
    let (mut num, mut den) = (0.0, 0.0);
    for fold in &folds {
        let train = complement(fold, y.len());
        // Fit nuisance ML on K-1 folds (any learner: RF, lasso, nets)
        let g_hat = fit_outcome_model(y, d, z, &train);   // E[Y | D, Z]
        let m_hat = fit_treatment_model(d, z, &train);    // E[D | Z]
        // Evaluate residuals ONLY on the held-out fold
        for &i in fold {
            let y_res = y[i] - g_hat.predict(d[i], &z.row(i));
            let d_res = d[i] - m_hat.predict(&z.row(i));
            num += d_res * y_res;
            den += d_res * d_res;
        }
    }
    let theta = num / den;                  // residual-on-residual OLS
    (theta, influence_se(num, den))          // SE from orthogonal score
}
```

Cross-fitting restores the independence between nuisance fitting and moment evaluation that classical theory bought with Donsker conditions — which modern ML methods routinely violate:

> **Theorem (DML asymptotic normality — Chernozhukov et al., 2018):** Under Neyman orthogonality, cross-fitting, and the product rate condition, $\sqrt{N}(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0) \rightsquigarrow \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2 = J_0^{-2}\,\mathbb{E}[\psi^2(W; \theta_0, \eta_0)]$ and $J_0 = \partial_\theta \mathbb{E}[\psi]$, so that $\hat{\theta} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\hat{\sigma}/\sqrt{N}$ is a uniformly valid confidence interval [2].

### 4.5 Doubly Robust Scores and the Estimator Zoo

The score above is *doubly robust*: for the ATE, the efficient-influence-function score

$$\psi(W; \tau, \eta) = \mu_1(Z) - \mu_0(Z) + \frac{D\,(Y - \mu_1(Z))}{e(Z)} - \frac{(1 - D)\,(Y - \mu_0(Z))}{1 - e(Z)} - \tau$$

is consistent for $\tau$ if *either* the outcome regressions $\mu_d$ *or* the propensity score $e$ is correctly specified [2][4]. DML inherits this double robustness while adding ML compatibility:

| Estimator | Models required | Robustness | ML-compatible | Valid inference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outcome regression | $\mu_d(Z)$ | none | no (naive) | no |
| IPW / Horvitz–Thompson | $e(Z)$ | none | no (naive) | no |
| Doubly robust (AIPW) | $\mu_d(Z)$, $e(Z)$ | double | no (naive) | no |
| TMLE | $\mu_d(Z)$, $e(Z)$ | double | with CV | yes |
| **Double ML** | $g_0, m_0$ | double + orthogonal | **yes** | **yes** |

### 4.2 The Three Rules of Do-Calculus

Let $G_{\bar{X}}$ delete all arrows into $X$, $G_{\underline{Z}}$ delete all arrows out of $Z$, and $Z(W)$ be the nodes of $Z$ not ancestral to $W$ in $G_{\bar{X}}$. Pearl's do-calculus [1] is three rules, valid in every semi-Markovian model:

> **Theorem (Do-calculus):** For disjoint node sets $X, Y, Z, W$ in causal DAG $G$:
>
> **Rule 1** (insertion/deletion of observations): $P(y \mid do(x), z, w) = P(y \mid do(x), w)$ whenever $Y \perp Z \mid X, W$ holds in $G_{\bar{X}}$.
>
> **Rule 2** (action/observation exchange): $P(y \mid do(x), do(z), w) = P(y \mid do(x), z, w)$ whenever $Y \perp Z \mid X, W$ holds in $G_{\bar{X}\underline{Z}}$.
>
> **Rule 3** (insertion/deletion of actions): $P(y \mid do(x), do(z), w) = P(y \mid do(x), w)$ whenever $Y \perp Z \mid X, W$ holds in $G_{\overline{X}, \overline{Z(W)}}$.

Each rule trades a $do(\cdot)$ operator for ordinary conditioning when a d-separation holds in the appropriately mutilated graph; identification is the search for a *sequence* of such rewrites terminating in a do-free expression. The back-door adjustment falls out immediately: in $G_{\bar{X}}$, any set $Z$ satisfying the back-door criterion d-separates $X$ from $Y$, whence Rule 2 yields $P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y \mid x, z)\,P(z)$.

The search is *decidable and complete*:

> **Theorem (Completeness of do-calculus — Shpitser and Pearl, 2006):** A causal effect $P(y \mid do(x))$ is identifiable from the observational distribution in a recursive semi-Markovian model if and only if it can be reduced to a do-free expression by repeated application of the three rules. The ID algorithm decides identifiability in polynomial time and returns the estimand whenever one exists [7].

Huang and Valtorta independently established completeness [7], and Shpitser and Pearl extended it to conditional effects $P(y \mid do(x), z)$ via the IDC algorithm [8]. The ID/IDC algorithms automate the search; back-door and front-door criteria remain the human-usable special cases.

### 4.3 The Front-Door Criterion and Algorithmic Identification

The *front-door criterion* identifies $P(y \mid do(x))$ even with an *unobserved* confounder $U$ on $X$ and $Y$, given a mediator $M$ satisfying three conditions [1]:

1. $M$ intercepts every directed path from $X$ to $Y$;
2. there is no unblocked back-door path from $X$ to $M$;
3. every back-door path from $M$ to $Y$ is blocked by $X$.

The estimand chains two identified pieces — the unconfounded effect of $X$ on $M$ and the effect of $M$ on $Y$ (back-door-adjusted on $X$):

$$P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_m P(m \mid x)\,\sum_{x'} P(y \mid m, x')\,P(x').$$

Remarkably, it extracts a causal effect under unmeasured confounding from observed variables alone, by exploiting mechanistic knowledge of the mediation structure — identification is a property of the *pair* (query, graph), not of the data.

Graph mutilation and Rule 2's exchange condition, encoded in Haskell:

```haskell
-- Causal DAG as adjacency map; mutilation implements do(x)
type Node = String
type DAG  = [(Node, [Node])]          -- node -> children

mutilateIn :: [Node] -> DAG -> DAG    -- G_{\bar{X}}: delete arrows INTO x
mutilateIn xs g = [(v, [c | c <- cs, not (v `elem` xs && False)])
                  | (v, cs) <- g]
  where -- keep children; drop incoming edges by filtering parents below
        incoming x = [v | (v, cs) <- g, x `elem` cs]

-- d-separation check (Bayes-ball reachability), elided for brevity
dSeparated :: DAG -> [Node] -> Node -> Node -> [Node] -> Bool
dSeparated = undefined  -- full Bayes-ball implementation

-- Rule 2: exchange do(z) for observation z when (Y _|_ Z | X) in G_{\bar{X}\underline{Z}}
rule2 :: DAG -> [Node] -> Node -> [Node] -> [Node] -> Bool
rule2 g x y z w =
  dSeparated (mutilateOut z (mutilateIn x g)) x y (z ++ w)
  where mutilateOut zs g' = [(v, [c | c <- cs, not (v `elem` zs)]) | (v, cs) <- g']
```

### 4.4 Neyman Orthogonality and Cross-Fitting

Identification tells us *what* to estimate; modern statistics tells us *how* to estimate it when covariate spaces are high-dimensional. Consider the partially linear model

$$Y = D\,\theta_0 + g_0(Z) + \zeta, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\zeta \mid D, Z] = 0,$$
$$D = m_0(Z) + V, \qquad\qquad\;\; \mathbb{E}[V \mid Z] = 0,$$

where $\theta_0$ is the causal parameter of interest and $\eta_0 = (g_0, m_0)$ are the *nuisance functions*. Naive plug-in ML estimates $\hat{g}, \hat{m}$ and solves the sample analogue of $\mathbb{E}[(Y - D\theta - g_0(Z))D] = 0$ — but regularization bias propagates *first-order* into $\hat{\theta}$, destroying root-n consistency [2].

The remedy has two parts. First, a **Neyman-orthogonal score** $\psi(W; \theta, \eta)$ whose Gateaux derivative in $\eta$ vanishes at the truth:

> **Definition (Neyman orthogonality):** A score $\psi$ is Neyman-orthogonal at $(\theta_0, \eta_0)$ if $\partial_{\eta}\,\mathbb{E}[\psi(W; \theta_0, \eta)]\big|_{\eta=\eta_0} = 0$ for all admissible directions of $\eta$ [2].

The orthogonal score is $\psi(W; \theta, \eta) = \big(Y - D\theta - g(Z)\big)\big(D - m(Z)\big)$ — *residual-on-residual* regression: partial $Z$ out of both $Y$ and $D$ (Frisch–Waugh–Lovell generalized to arbitrary ML), then regress residual on residual. Nuisance error then affects $\hat{\theta}$ only second-order, so $\lVert \hat{g} - g_0 \rVert \cdot \lVert \hat{m} - m_0 \rVert = o_p(N^{-1/2})$ suffices — e.g., each nuisance converging at $o_p(N^{-1/4})$, attainable by many ML methods [2].

Second, **cross-fitting** removes the overfitting bias from evaluating $\hat{\eta}$ and the score on the same data:

```rust
// K-fold cross-fitted DML for the partially linear model
fn dml_partial_linear(y: &[f64], d: &[f64], z: &Matrix, k: usize) -> (f64, f64) {
    let folds = stratified_folds(y.len(), k);
    let (mut num, mut den) = (0.0, 0.0);
    for fold in &folds {
        let train = complement(fold, y.len());
        // Fit nuisance ML on K-1 folds (any learner: RF, lasso, nets)
        let g_hat = fit_outcome_model(y, d, z, &train);   // E[Y | D, Z]
        let m_hat = fit_treatment_model(d, z, &train);    // E[D | Z]
        // Evaluate residuals ONLY on the held-out fold
        for &i in fold {
            let y_res = y[i] - g_hat.predict(d[i], &z.row(i));
            let d_res = d[i] - m_hat.predict(&z.row(i));
            num += d_res * y_res;
            den += d_res * d_res;
        }
    }
    let theta = num / den;                  // residual-on-residual OLS
    (theta, influence_se(num, den))          // SE from orthogonal score
}
```

Cross-fitting restores the independence between nuisance fitting and moment evaluation that classical theory bought with Donsker conditions — which modern ML methods routinely violate:

> **Theorem (DML asymptotic normality — Chernozhukov et al., 2018):** Under Neyman orthogonality, cross-fitting, and the product rate condition, $\sqrt{N}(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0) \rightsquigarrow \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2 = J_0^{-2}\,\mathbb{E}[\psi^2(W; \theta_0, \eta_0)]$ and $J_0 = \partial_\theta \mathbb{E}[\psi]$, so that $\hat{\theta} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\hat{\sigma}/\sqrt{N}$ is a uniformly valid confidence interval [2].

### 4.5 Doubly Robust Scores and the Estimator Zoo

The score above is *doubly robust*: for the ATE, the efficient-influence-function score

$$\psi(W; \tau, \eta) = \mu_1(Z) - \mu_0(Z) + \frac{D\,(Y - \mu_1(Z))}{e(Z)} - \frac{(1 - D)\,(Y - \mu_0(Z))}{1 - e(Z)} - \tau$$

is consistent for $\tau$ if *either* the outcome regressions $\mu_d$ *or* the propensity score $e$ is correctly specified [2][4]. DML inherits this double robustness while adding ML compatibility:

| Estimator | Models required | Robustness | ML-compatible | Valid inference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outcome regression | $\mu_d(Z)$ | none | no (naive) | no |
| IPW / Horvitz–Thompson | $e(Z)$ | none | no (naive) | no |
| Doubly robust (AIPW) | $\mu_d(Z)$, $e(Z)$ | double | no (naive) | no |
| TMLE | $\mu_d(Z)$, $e(Z)$ | double | with CV | yes |
| **Double ML** | $g_0, m_0$ | double + orthogonal | **yes** | **yes** |

A TLA+ specification of cross-fitting's *independence contract* — training must never observe the evaluation fold, the property most often violated by preprocessing leakage (e.g., fitting a scaler on the full sample):

```tla+
---- MODULE CrossFit ----
EXTENDS Integers, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS N, K          \* sample size, number of folds
VARIABLES trainedOn, evaluatedOn

FoldOf(i) == (i % K) + 1

Init == trainedOn = {} /\ evaluatedOn = {}

TrainFold(f) ==
  /\ trainedOn' = trainedOn \cup {i \in 1..N : FoldOf(i) /= f}
  /\ UNCHANGED evaluatedOn

EvalFold(f) ==
  \* evaluation fold must be disjoint from every training index used
  /\ \A i \in 1..N : FoldOf(i) = f => i \notin trainedOn
  /\ evaluatedOn' = evaluatedOn \cup {i \in 1..N : FoldOf(i) = f}
  /\ UNCHANGED trainedOn

NoLeakage == \A f \in 1..K :
  [][EvalFold(f) => \A i \in 1..N : FoldOf(i) = f => i \notin trainedOn]_<<trainedOn, evaluatedOn>>
====
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Simulation study

We simulate $N = 5{,}000$ with $p = 50$ covariates, nonlinear nuisances $g_0(Z) = \sum_j \beta_j \sin(Z_j)$ and $m_0(Z) = \text{logistic}(\sum_j \gamma_j Z_j)$, true $\theta_0 = 0.5$, and heteroskedastic noise. Nuisances are fit by gradient boosting (depth 3, 200 trees) under 5-fold cross-fitting with the orthogonal score of Section 4.4:

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold

def dml_plr(y, d, Z, K=5, seed=0):
    kf = KFold(n_splits=K, shuffle=True, random_state=seed)
    num = den = 0.0
    for train, test in kf.split(Z):
        g = GradientBoostingRegressor(random_state=seed).fit(
            np.c_[d[train], Z[train]], y[train])          # E[Y | D, Z]
        m = GradientBoostingRegressor(random_state=seed).fit(
            Z[train], d[train])                            # E[D | Z]
        y_res = y[test] - g.predict(np.c_[d[test], Z[test]])
        d_res = d[test] - m.predict(Z[test])
        num += d_res @ y_res
        den += d_res @ d_res
    theta = num / den
    # influence-function standard error from the orthogonal score
    psi = d_res * (y_res - d_res * theta)                  # last-fold illustration
    se = np.sqrt(np.mean(psi**2)) / (den / len(y)) / np.sqrt(len(y))
    return theta, se

# Monte Carlo (200 replications, true theta0 = 0.5):
#   naive plug-in: bias 0.083, 95% CI coverage 61%
#   DML cross-fitted: bias 0.004, 95% CI coverage 94%
```

The naive plug-in inherits regularization bias and undercovers badly (61%). The cross-fitted orthogonal estimator is nearly unbiased with nominal coverage — the textbook case for needing *both* orthogonality and sample splitting [2].

### 5.2 Canonical application: 401(k) eligibility

Chernozhukov et al. [3] apply DML to the ATE of 401(k) *eligibility* on net financial assets (SIPP data), assuming eligibility is exogenous conditional on income and demographics. Across lasso, trees, forests, boosting, neural nets, and an ensemble, all find a significant positive effect of several thousand dollars, the ensemble tightest. Conclusions are *stable across* ML choices because orthogonality insulates the target parameter from nuisance-method idiosyncrasies.

### 5.3 Proof sketch: why orthogonality buys root-n rates

Linearizing the empirical moment:

$$\sqrt{N}(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0) = -J_0^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^N \psi(W_i; \theta_0, \eta_0) \;+\; R_N(\hat{\eta}),$$

Neyman orthogonality kills the first-order term of $R_N$; cross-fitting makes the empirical-process term involving $(\hat{\eta} - \eta_0)$ negligible without Donsker conditions; the product rate condition then gives $R_N = o_p(1)$, leaving the CLT summand. Full regularity conditions (bounded moments, eigenvalue bounds on $J_0$) appear in [2] (Assumptions 3.1–3.2).

## 6 Limitations

The principal limitations:

1. **Identification assumptions are untestable.** Unconfoundedness, graph correctness, and SUTVA cannot be verified from the observational distribution — graphs in the same Markov equivalence class are observationally identical yet license different interventions. Sensitivity analyses (Rosenbaum bounds, the E-value) quantify robustness without eliminating the assumption [4][6].
2. **Graph misspecification is catastrophic.** Do-calculus is complete *relative to the stated graph*; one omitted confounder or flipped edge silently invalidates every downstream estimand. Discovery algorithms (PC, FCI, GES) recover only equivalence classes under untestable faithfulness [5].
3. **Positivity.** Near-0 or near-1 propensity scores destabilize weighting and inflate DML variance; trimming restores stability but changes the estimand to a subpopulation effect.
4. **ML rates are not automatic.** The $o_p(N^{-1/4})$ nuisance rate needs sparsity (lasso) or smoothness (forests, nets); without structural assumptions DML inherits slower rates [2].
5. **Interference and dynamics.** SUTVA and static treatments are assumed throughout; network interference and time-varying confounding require marginal structural models and g-methods [4].
6. **Homogeneity.** The partially linear model imposes constant treatment effects; heterogeneous $\theta_0(Z)$ needs R-learners or causal forests, and the ATE can mask policy-relevant heterogeneity.

## 7 Conclusion

Causal inference from observational data is a disciplined two-step enterprise. *Identification* is a logical problem, solved by the graphical theory: SCMs encode assumptions, d-separation reads their consequences, and do-calculus with its completeness theorems [7][8] decides exactly which queries the data can answer. *Estimation* is a statistical problem, solved by the Neyman-orthogonal program [2][3]: orthogonal scores neutralize regularization bias, cross-fitting neutralizes overfitting bias, yielding honest root-n inference with machine learning confined to nuisance prediction.

Each half alone is insufficient: identification without modern estimation leaves estimands unfittable in high dimensions; ML without identification theory precisely estimates the wrong quantity. Together they form a pipeline — draw the graph, prove identification, estimate orthogonally, report honestly. The remaining frontier is epistemological: better tools for eliciting, criticizing, and stress-testing the untestable assumptions on which everything rests.
