# Hybrid Logical Clocks and Causal Consistency: Physical Time, Logical Counters, and Conflict-Free Coordination in Geo-Replicated Stores

## Abstract

Geo-replicated stores must order events across continents without trusting wall clocks they cannot perfectly synchronize. Hybrid Logical Clocks (HLC) pair a physical-time component with a bounded logical counter, producing timestamps that remain within the skew bound of physical time while preserving the happens-before relation exactly as Lamport clocks do. This paper develops the theory and systems practice of HLC. We present the update rules and prove the three core guarantees: monotonicity, causality capture, and bounded drift. We situate HLC within the consistency hierarchy, contrasting causal consistency and causal-plus with linearizability and Spanner-style external consistency. We examine HLC's role in causal protocols including COPS, Cure, Clock-SI, and GentleRain; its use in conflict-free replicated data types and last-writer-wins resolution; and the metadata economics that favor constant-size timestamps over vector clocks at scale. We close with the limits: HLC cannot detect concurrency, depends on clock discipline, and alone cannot deliver external consistency.

## 1 Intro

Time is the scarcest resource in a geo-replicated store, and the most treacherous. Every conflict resolution rule, every snapshot read, every question of the form "what did the user see before they clicked" reduces to ordering events that occurred on machines separated by thousands of kilometers and tens of milliseconds of unavoidable signal delay. Physical clocks, disciplined by NTP or PTP, drift apart; logical clocks, in the tradition of Lamport [2], capture causality but float free of wall-clock time, so they cannot answer "give me the database as of 3:00 PM." Four decades of systems research navigate this tension. At one pole stands Spanner, whose TrueTime API exposes clock uncertainty as an explicit interval and waits out the doubt to deliver external consistency [3]. At the other stand the coordination-free designs — COPS, Cure, and the conflict-free replicated data type literature — which accept causal consistency, the strongest model compatible with availability under partition, and resolve the remainder with mathematics rather than messages [4][5][7].

Hybrid Logical Clocks, introduced by Kulkarni et al. [1], occupy the fertile middle ground. An HLC timestamp is a pair *(l, c)*: *l* tracks the largest physical time the node has ever observed, and *c* is a logical counter that breaks ties when physical time stands still. The pair is monotone across events, captures happens-before exactly like a Lamport clock, and stays within the physical clock's skew bound of true time. It fits in 128 bits where a vector clock demands one counter per replica [1]. That combination — causality, physical meaningfulness, and constant metadata — has made HLC the timestamp primitive of choice for modern geo-replicated key-value designs and for consistent snapshot reads taken at arbitrary physical times.

This paper makes four contributions: a self-contained presentation of the HLC algorithm with proofs of its three guarantees; a placement of HLC in the consistency hierarchy and in conflict resolution; an analysis of the metadata economics of scalar versus vector timestamps; and a survey of production experience — TrueTime's hardware bet against HLC's software discipline — with a candid account of the approach's limits.

## 2 Background

### Logical time and causality

Lamport's 1978 paper defined the *happens-before* relation, written *a → b*, over the events of a distributed system [2]. The relation is the smallest satisfying three clauses:

1. If *a* and *b* are events of one process and *a* precedes *b* locally, then *a → b*.
2. If *a* is the sending of a message and *b* is its receipt, then *a → b*.
3. If *a → b* and *b → c*, then *a → c* (transitivity).

A clock function *C* satisfies the *clock condition* when *a → b* implies *C(a) < C(b)*. Lamport clocks implement it with a per-process counter: increment before each local event, attach the counter to every sent message, and on receipt advance to one more than the maximum of the local and received values. Concurrent events get no guarantee; Lamport clocks extend the partial order to a total one by breaking ties with process identifiers, yielding an order that *respects* causality without *detecting* it [2].

> **Theorem (Clock condition).** For Lamport clocks, *a → b* implies *C(a) < C(b)*.
>
> **Proof.** By induction over the construction of happens-before. Successive events on one process increment the counter; message receipt takes the maximum of the two counters and adds one, strictly increasing the timestamp along each causal edge. Transitivity extends the strict inequality along chains. ∎

Vector clocks close the detection gap. Each process keeps a vector with one entry per process, increments its own entry on every event, and merges vectors entry-wise on receipt. Then *a → b* holds iff *V(a) < V(b)* component-wise — the converse of the clock condition is recovered [2] — at the price of metadata linear in the number of processes. In a geo-replicated store with thousands of servers, that price is fatal, and it is the first pressure driving systems toward scalar timestamps [1][4].

### The consistency hierarchy

*Causal consistency* demands that every process observe causally related operations in happens-before order; concurrent operations may appear in any order, provided all replicas converge on the same resolution. COPS sharpened this to *causal-plus*: causal consistency together with convergent conflict handling, argued to be the strongest consistency achievable by an always-available system [4]. Bailis et al. generalized the question to transactions, taxonomizing Highly Available Transactions (HATs) and proving which isolation levels survive partition — and which, serializability among them, inherently require coordination [6].

*Linearizability* is strictly stronger: every operation appears to take effect atomically at one instant between invocation and response, respecting real time. Spanner achieves the closely related *external consistency* — if transaction *T2* starts after *T1* commits, then *T2*'s timestamp exceeds *T1*'s — by layering Paxos replication under TrueTime, an API returning the current time as an interval guaranteed to contain the true time, and waiting out the uncertainty (*commit wait*) before exposing a commit [3]. TrueTime is honest about physics: without bounded clock uncertainty, real-time order cannot be recovered — at the cost of atomic clocks and GPS receivers in every datacenter and latency proportional to the uncertainty bound.

Between Lamport's austere counters and TrueTime's atomic clocks lies the question HLC answers: can a software-only, constant-size timestamp be both causally sound and physically meaningful?

| Scheme | Timestamp size | Captures *→* | Physically meaningful | Total order |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lamport clock [2] | O(1) scalar | Respects only | No | Yes, with process ids |
| Vector clock | O(n) vector | Respects and detects | No | No |
| NTP physical clock | O(1) scalar | No | Yes, within skew | Yes, by value |
| TrueTime interval [3] | O(1) interval | No | Yes, with uncertainty bound | No, intervals may overlap |
| Hybrid Logical Clock [1] | O(1) pair | Respects only | Yes, within skew | Yes, lexicographic |

## 3 Methodology

### The HLC algorithm

Each node maintains *(l, c)*, initially zero, and reads its physical clock *pt*, assumed only monotone non-decreasing locally. NTP keeps nodes' clocks within a skew bound, but correctness never depends on that bound — only the physical meaningfulness of the output does [1]. For a local or send event:

```python
def tick(l, c, pt):
    l_new = max(l, pt)
    if l_new == l:
        c_new = c + 1      # physical time stalled: logical tie-break
    else:
        c_new = 0          # physical time advanced: reset counter
    return (l_new, c_new)
```

For a receive event carrying the sender's timestamp *(l_m, c_m)*:

```python
def receive(l, c, pt, l_m, c_m):
    l_new = max(l, l_m, pt)
    if l_new == l == l_m:
        c_new = max(c, c_m) + 1
    elif l_new == l:
        c_new = c + 1
    elif l_new == l_m:
        c_new = c_m + 1
    else:
        c_new = 0
    return (l_new, c_new)
```

Timestamps compare lexicographically: *(l1, c1) < (l2, c2)* when *l1 < l2*, or *l1 = l2* and *c1 < c2*. The physical component *l* is thus the maximum physical time the node has ever observed — its own or learned through a message — while *c* counts how many causally ordered events share that physical instant [1]. The three guarantees follow directly:

- **Monotone.** Successive events on one node receive strictly increasing timestamps, since *l* never decreases and *c* increments whenever *l* stalls.
- **Causally sound.** If *e → f* then *hlc(e) < hlc(f)*, by the same induction as Lamport's clock condition.
- **Physically bounded.** *l* never exceeds the largest physical time observed anywhere by more than the clock skew, so it tracks wall time.

> **Theorem (HLC monotonicity).** Successive events on one node receive strictly increasing HLC timestamps.
>
> **Proof.** In `tick`, either *l* advances, in which case the lexicographic pair increases regardless of *c* resetting to zero, or *l* is unchanged and *c* increments. In `receive`, *l_new* is at least the old *l*; if equal, one of the first three branches fires and *c* strictly increases. ∎

> **Theorem (HLC causality capture).** If *e → f* then *hlc(e) < hlc(f)*.
>
> **Proof.** Same-process succession follows from monotonicity. For a send event *e* with stamp *(l_e, c_e)* and its receive *f*, the receive rule sets *l_f ≥ l_e*, and when *l_f = l_e* it sets *c_f ≥ c_e + 1*; hence *hlc(f) > hlc(e)*. Transitivity of both *→* and *<* extends the result along causal chains. ∎

> **Lemma (Bounded drift).** At every event, *l* differs from the node's physical clock *pt* by at most the maximum skew the node has ever absorbed from a message sender; in a system where all physical clocks stay within skew *ε* of true time, *l* stays within *ε* of true time as well.
>
> **Proof.** *l* is only ever set to the maximum of values each within *ε* of true time — the local *pt* or a sender's *l*, itself inductively within *ε*. The maximum of such values is within *ε*. ∎

The lemma is the point of the "hybrid": unlike a Lamport clock, *l* never exceeds the largest physical time in the system by more than the skew, so a snapshot "as of physical time *T*" is meaningful — every event with *l ≤ T* is genuinely in the past [1].




## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The consistency lattice: causal, linearizable, and the coordination frontier

COPS made causal consistency operational at scale: each write carries its causal dependencies, a datacenter exposes a replicated write only after verifying every dependency locally, and COPS-GT added read-only transactions that assemble a consistent multi-key view without locking [4]. The result is causal-plus: causal consistency with convergent conflict handling, the strongest semantics an always-available store can offer [4].

HLC slots in as the dependency carrier's compact form: later designs stamp each version with an HLC timestamp and define visibility against a *global stable time* — the minimum over all nodes of their current *l* — so reads "as of" a recent timestamp see a causally consistent snapshot without blocking on a vector comparison [8].

The HAT analysis draws the frontier precisely: read-committed, monotonic reads, and causal consistency survive partition; recency guarantees and preventing concurrent updates do not [6]. HLC cannot move this frontier — the impossibility is information-theoretic — but it makes the achievable side cheaper, while linearizability and external consistency still demand TrueTime's hardware or Spanner-style commit wait [3].

### 4.2 CRDTs and coordination-free convergence

Conflict-free replicated data types take a different route to the same destination: instead of ordering concurrent updates, they make order irrelevant. Shapiro et al. formalized *strong eventual consistency*: replicas that have received the same updates converge to the same state, with no coordination on the write path [5]. State-based CRDTs converge when the state space is a join-semilattice and merge is the least upper bound; operation-based CRDTs need commuting updates or causal delivery. The proof is algebraic, not temporal — yet practical CRDTs constantly need a timestamp tie-breaker for concurrent assignments [5].

The last-writer-wins register is the canonical example. Its state is a *(timestamp, value)* pair; merge keeps the pair with the larger timestamp, and concurrent writes to the same key resolve deterministically everywhere. With HLC timestamps, the winning timestamp stays close to the winning write's wall-clock time — human-interpretable — and its physical component bounds how stale a losing write can be. The implementation is a few lines:

```rust
#[derive(Clone, Copy, PartialEq, Eq, PartialOrd, Ord)]
struct Hlc { l: u64, c: u64 }          // lexicographic order = derived Ord

struct LwwRegister<T> { stamp: Hlc, node: u64, value: T }

impl<T: Clone> LwwRegister<T> {
    fn merge(&mut self, other: &Self) {
        // total order: HLC first, node id breaks residual ties
        if (other.stamp, other.node) > (self.stamp, self.node) {
            self.stamp = other.stamp;
            self.node = other.node;
            self.value = other.value.clone();
        }
    }
}
```

Associativity, commutativity, and idempotence give convergence — the CRDT guarantee — while HLC keeps the resolution aligned with physical time [1][5]; observed-remove sets, counters, and maps compose the same way.

### 4.3 Metadata economics: vector clocks, physical clocks, and the O(1) temptation

The vector clock's *O(n)* metadata is not a theoretical blemish; it is the binding constraint on causally consistent stores at scale. Every version stored, every anti-entropy message, every dependency check carries one counter per writer. Dotted version vectors compress the common case, but hot keys still inflate metadata and dependency-check cost grows with the cluster [4][7].

Physical clocks offer the opposite trade: *O(1)* timestamps with real meaning, at the cost of trusting NTP. GentleRain built causal consistency on bare physical timestamps plus a global stable time computed as the minimum of per-partition clocks, achieving higher throughput than COPS or Orbe precisely because timestamps are scalars [8]. But the trust is load-bearing: a backward clock step causes causality violations, and growing skew stalls writes waiting for the local clock to catch a message's timestamp. GentleRain+ replaced the physical clock with HLC, gaining immunity to backward steps and skew-insensitive writes at no measured overhead — the logical counter absorbs exactly the anomalies that broke the pure-physical scheme [8]; CausalSpartan applied the same substitution for causal consistency with lightweight snapshots [8].

The temptation to read HLC as "vector clocks without the cost" must be resisted: HLC timestamps are *Lamport-like* — given *hlc(a) < hlc(b)*, the store cannot tell whether *a → b* or the writes were concurrent, so any HLC-only resolver silently discards the loser. Systems that must *surface* conflicts still need vectors [4][7].

### 4.4 TrueTime, HLC, and snapshot reads in production stores

Spanner and HLC represent opposite answers to the same question: how much can you trust a clock? TrueTime answers with metrology — GPS and atomic clocks bounding uncertainty *ε* — and a protocol: a transaction commits at timestamp *s* only after TrueTime's interval has fully passed *s*, guaranteeing *s* is in the past everywhere [3].

HLC answers with discipline: assume NTP bounds skew, but depend on the bound only for the *physical meaningfulness* of timestamps, never for correctness. Cure shows what the software path buys: per-datacenter vector clocks for precise dependency tracking, HLC-spirited physical timestamps, and snapshot reads served from any replica near single-datacenter latency [7]. Clock-SI pushes further, using loosely synchronized physical clocks to provide snapshot isolation over partitioned data without any centralized timestamp oracle [8]. In both, the HLC-flavored timestamp makes "read as of time *T*" implementable: versions totally ordered by *(l, c)*, a stable-time watermark advancing with the physical clocks, and reads selecting each key's newest version with *l ≤ T* [1][8].

External consistency needs a timestamp known to be in the past *everywhere* — bounded uncertainty about *everyone's* clock, i.e. TrueTime's hardware or an equivalent wait. Causal consistency needs only causes before effects in the observed order, which HLC delivers in software; stores that chose availability, most of them per the HAT survey [6], rationally chose HLC.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

Beyond the three core theorems, HLC's practicality rests on quantitative bounds that can be checked against deployment data. We collect the essential ones.

*Timestamp width.* HLC is 128 bits: 64 for the physical component, 64 for the counter. A vector clock needs *64n* bits per version — at *n = 1000* writers, 8 KB against HLC's 16 bytes, a factor of 500 paid on every write, replica, and anti-entropy exchange [1][7].

*Counter overflow.* *c* increments only when events share one physical tick; the analysis in [1] bounds it by the event rate times the clock granularity. In practice *c* rarely exceeds single digits — the 64-bit allocation is pure headroom.

*Causality preservation under churn.* The update rules assume no stable membership: a joining node starts at *(0, 0)* and its first received message lifts it to the current epoch, while the receive rule's maximum covers exactly the timestamps observed [1]. Vector clocks, by contrast, need resizing and epoch management on membership change.

*Measured behavior.* Pure-physical-clock GentleRain outperformed COPS and Orbe in throughput via scalar timestamps and a global stable time [8]; GentleRain+ kept that throughput while eliminating skew-induced write stalls and surviving backward clock steps, with no measurable counter overhead [8]. Cure's causally consistent transactions completed within a single datacenter round trip in the common case, avoiding the cross-datacenter coordination stronger models demand [7].

| Bound | Value | Depends on |
|---|---|---|
| Timestamp size | 128 bits | Fixed by encoding |
| Drift of *l* from true time | At most physical skew *ε* | NTP/PTP discipline |
| Max logical counter *c* | Event rate times clock granularity | Workload, clock resolution |
| Snapshot staleness | Skew plus stable-time lag | Replication delay |
| Causality capture | Exact, like Lamport | Message delivery only |

## 6 Limitations

HLC's elegance invites over-claiming; its limits are sharp and worth stating plainly.

*No concurrency detection.* HLC inherits Lamport's one-way guarantee: *e → f* implies *hlc(e) < hlc(f)*, but the converse fails. Two concurrent writes receive totally ordered timestamps, and last-writer-wins discards one without any record that a conflict occurred. Applications that must surface conflicts — collaborative editing, inventory — still need vector clocks or explicit conflict metadata [4][7]. HLC resolves; it does not reveal.

*Skew dependence of meaningfulness.* Correctness never needs synchronization, but usefulness does: a node whose clock runs minutes fast drags every contact into the future, and "snapshot as of now" returns versions from the actual future. No software clock survives a peer that lies convincingly about the present [8]; deployments must monitor skew as a first-class health signal.

*No external consistency.* HLC cannot certify timestamp *T* as in the past everywhere — only TrueTime-style uncertainty bounds plus commit wait can [3]. A client writing at datacenter A and reading at B may see HLC timestamps violating its real-time expectation, since causality flowed through no message the system saw. Causal consistency is the ceiling; linearizability is a different building.

*Snapshot delay.* Reading "as of physical time *T*" requires waiting until the global stable time passes *T*, which lags by the slowest node's replication delay plus skew. HLC makes the snapshot *definable* at any physical time; it does not make it *available* instantly [1][8].

*Adversarial timestamps.* The update rules trust received timestamps. A Byzantine client or a compromised replica can inject far-future *l* values and push honest nodes' clocks forward, a denial-of-service vector that Byzantine-tolerant variants address with agreement on stable times rather than blind maxima [8].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Hybrid Logical Clocks distill a pragmatic insight: most systems need timestamps that are *causally sound* and *physically plausible* — not ones that detect concurrency or certify real-time order. The *(l, c)* pair delivers both at constant size — monotonicity and happens-before capture from the logical counter's discipline, physical meaningfulness from tracking the maximum observed wall time within skew. Around this primitive, a coherent architecture has emerged: HLC-stamped versions, global stable-time watermarks, causal-plus visibility rules in the style of COPS and Cure, CRDT lattices for convergence, and last-writer-wins resolution where conflicts must be decided rather than surfaced [1][4][5][7].

Open directions: *dotted HLC* hybrids recovering concurrency detection for hot keys; machine-checked proofs of the three guarantees; tighter Clock-SI integration [8]; and HLC under adversarial clock behavior. As geo-replication becomes the default rather than the exotic choice, the humble timestamp pair — one eye on the wall clock, one on the causal past — is likely to remain the working compromise between what physics allows and what applications need.
