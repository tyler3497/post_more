# The KPZ Universality Class: the Kardar–Parisi–Zhang Equation, Directed Polymers, and Macdonald Processes

## Abstract

Since its introduction in 1986, the Kardar–Parisi–Zhang (KPZ) equation has grown from a phenomenological model of interface growth into the organizing principle of an entire universality class of nonequilibrium stochastic processes [1]. This thesis develops the mathematical theory of KPZ universality in one spatial dimension, where a decade of breakthroughs has converted heuristic scaling arguments into exact theorems. We present the Hopf–Cole linearization of the KPZ equation into the stochastic heat equation, the derivation of the exact one-point distribution for narrow-wedge initial data via the replica Bethe ansatz and via weakly asymmetric exclusion processes [2][3][8], the emergence of Tracy–Widom laws [6] and Airy processes as universal limit objects, and the unification of these results within the framework of Macdonald processes and integrable probability [5]. Numerical and experimental confirmations are surveyed alongside the principal open problems: the higher-dimensional exponents, the complete characterization of the KPZ fixed point, and the passage from integrable models to generic universality.

##1 Introduction

Few equations in statistical physics have traveled as far as the one proposed by Mehran Kardar, Giorgio Parisi, and Yi-Cheng Zhang in 1986 [1]. Their aim was modest: write down the simplest nonlinear stochastic partial differential equation describing the growth of an interface — a flame front, a bacterial colony edge, a surface deposited atom by atom — and compute its scaling exponents by dynamic renormalization group. What they uncovered instead was a *universality class*: an enormous family of models — randomly growing interfaces, asymmetric particle systems, directed polymers in random media, random matrix eigenvalues, even longest increasing subsequences of random permutations — that all share the same large-scale fluctuations.

The KPZ equation in one spatial dimension reads

```
∂t h = ν ∂xx h + (λ/2)(∂x h)² + ξ(x,t)
```

where `h(x,t)` is the interface height, `ν > 0` a surface tension, `λ` the strength of lateral growth, and `ξ` space–time white noise. Each term has a physical reading:

- **Relaxation** `ν ∂xx h`: smooths the interface, as in the Edwards–Wilkinson equation.
- **Lateral growth** `(λ/2)(∂x h)²`: growth normal to the surface generates a slope-dependent excess velocity — the geometric heart of KPZ.
- **Noise** `ξ`: microscopic randomness of deposition or thermal agitation.

> **Thesis statement:** In one dimension, the KPZ equation is *exactly solvable* in a precise probabilistic sense: its one-point statistics converge, under the canonical 1:2:3 scaling, to the Tracy–Widom distributions of random matrix theory, and this solvability extends to a hierarchy of integrable discrete models unified by Macdonald processes.

For twenty-five years the equation resisted rigorous treatment — it is classically ill-posed, since a typical solution is only Hölder-1/2 in space and the square of its gradient is meaningless. Then, between 2010 and 2014, three lines of attack converged: the replica Bethe ansatz applied directly to the continuum equation [2][3], the weakly asymmetric simple exclusion process (WASEP) scaling limit [8], and the algebraic machinery of Macdonald processes [5]. This thesis synthesizes these developments. Section 2 fixes notation and the scaling picture; Section 3 describes the exact methods; Section 4 develops the core theory in depth; Section 5 surveys empirical and experimental confirmation together with the proof landmarks; Section 6 states the limitations honestly; Section 7 concludes with open directions.

##2 Background

### The scaling exponents

KPZ scaling is governed by three exponents — roughness `α`, growth `β`, and dynamic `z` — tied by the relations `α + z = 2` (Galilean invariance) and `z = α/β` (Family–Vicsek scaling). In one dimension the exact values are

| Exponent | Symbol | Value (d=1) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roughness | α | 1/2 | `w(L) ~ L^α` saturated width |
| Growth | β | 1/3 | `w(t) ~ t^β` transient width |
| Dynamic | z | 3/2 | correlation length `ξ(t) ~ t^{1/z}` |

Here the interface width is `w² = ⟨(h − ⟨h⟩)²⟩`. The celebrated **1:2:3 scaling** — space : time : fluctuations as `t^{2/3} : t : t^{1/3}` — says that to see nontrivial limiting fluctuations one must look at spatial windows of order `t^{2/3}` while height fluctuations are of order `t^{1/3}`. This is *not* diffusive scaling, and it is the first signature of the class.

A crucial subtlety, emphasized in the survey [4], is that the limiting *distribution* depends on the initial geometry:

1. **Narrow wedge** (droplet): `h(x,0) ≈ −|x|/δ` — curved growth; limit is Tracy–Widom GUE.
2. **Flat**: `h(x,0) = 0` — limit is Tracy–Widom GOE.
3. **Stationary**: `h(x,0)` a two-sided Brownian motion — limit is the Baik–Rains distribution.

These are genuinely distinct universal laws, not reparametrizations of one another — a hallmark of how rich one-dimensional KPZ is.

### The discrete menagerie

Long before the continuum equation was solved, discrete members of the class were yielding exact formulas. Johansson proved that the shape fluctuations of a certain random growth model converge to the Tracy–Widom GUE law [7], forging the first rigorous link between growth and random matrices. The totally asymmetric simple exclusion process (TASEP) and its partially asymmetric cousin (ASEP) encode interface growth via the height-function representation: particles hopping right become up-steps of an interface. Last-passage percolation and directed polymers in random media (DPRM) provide the zero-temperature and positive-temperature versions of the same optimization geometry. All of these sit inside the 1:2:3 scaling window, and all were eventually recognized as degenerations of a single algebraic structure [5].

##3 Methodology

Four interlocking methods carry the modern theory, and this thesis uses all of them.

**1. The Hopf–Cole transform.** Setting `Z = exp((λ/2ν) h)` (Itô interpretation) linearizes KPZ into the *stochastic heat equation* (SHE)

```
∂t Z = ν ∂xx Z + (λ/2ν) ξ Z ,   Z(x,0) = Z₀(x)
```

formally by Itô's formula. The SHE is linear but driven by *multiplicative* noise; its solution is the partition function of a continuum directed polymer, and `h = (2ν/λ) log Z` is the polymer free energy. Everything hard about KPZ becomes a question about `Z`.

**2. Replica / Bethe ansatz.** The integer moments `⟨Z(t,x)ⁿ⟩` satisfy a closed PDE: the imaginary-time Schrödinger equation for `n` bosons on the line with attractive delta interaction — the Lieb–Liniger or *delta Bose gas*. Its eigenfunctions are known via the Bethe ansatz, and Kardar used them to compute the moment Lyapunov exponents. The moments grow too fast to determine the distribution (severe intermittency), but their generating structure yields, via analytic continuation and Laplace inversion, an exact Fredholm determinant formula for the distribution of `Z` [2].

**3. Weakly asymmetric scaling limits.** Bertini and Giacomin proved that the WASEP height function converges to the Hopf–Cole solution of KPZ [8]. Since ASEP itself admits exact Tracy–Widom-type formulas (Tracy–Widom [6] for ASEP), passing the asymmetry to zero *inside* the exact formula produces the KPZ limit law rigorously — the route of Amir, Corwin, and Quastel [2].

**4. Integrable probability via Macdonald processes.** Macdonald symmetric functions define a family of measures on partitions with exact contour-integral formulas for observables. Degenerating parameters (`q`, `t`) produces a hierarchy — *q*-TASEP, *q*-pushASEP, the log-gamma polymer, the semi-discrete Brownian polymer — each with exact formulas, each converging to the KPZ equation or its relatives [5]. This is the most systematic known source of KPZ-class exact solvability.

> **Theorem (Methodological core):** Exact solvability in the KPZ class always proceeds in two steps — *(i)* an exact, analyzable formula (Fredholm determinant or contour integral) for a finite-time observable, and *(ii)* a steepest-descent asymptotic analysis under 1:2:3 scaling. No step can be skipped: scaling heuristics alone never identify the limit law.

##4 Deep Dive

###4.1 The stochastic heat equation and intermittency

The SHE looks innocent, but its noise is *multiplicative*: peaks of `Z` amplify the noise they feel, producing tall, narrow, widely separated spikes — **intermittency**.

> **Theorem (Kardar's moment formula):** For the SHE with delta initial data, `⟨Z(t,0)ⁿ⟩ = exp(t·γₙ)(1 + o(1))` with `γₙ = (n³ − n)/24` (in standard normalizations).

The cubic growth `γₙ ~ n³/24` is extraordinary: the `n`-th moment is dominated by events of probability roughly `exp(−c n³)`, so moments *cannot* reconstruct the law — the classical moment problem fails catastrophically.

```
E[exp(−ζ Z(t,0))] = det(I − K_ζ)_{L²(0,∞)}
```

for a kernel `K_ζ` built from Airy functions. Inverting this Laplace transform gives the distribution function of `h = log Z`, and the rigorous justification of the inversion is the technical core of [2].

###4.2 Narrow wedge and the Tracy–Widom limit

Take *narrow-wedge* initial data: `Z(x,0) = δ₀(x)`, i.e.

> **Theorem (ACQ 2011 [2]):** As `t → ∞`,
> `(H(t) + t/24) / (t/2)^{1/3}  ⇒  TW_GUE`
> in distribution, where `TW_GUE` is the Tracy–Widom GUE distribution from [6], `F_GUE(s) = det(I − K_Airy)_{L²(s,∞)}`.

The shift `−t/24` is the free-energy density (the law of large numbers), and `(t/2)^{1/3}` is the 1:2:3 fluctuation scale. Sasamoto and Spohn obtained the same limit in parallel via a non-rigorous replica route [3], and their formula matched the rigorous one — a striking confirmation that the Bethe-ansatz analytic continuations, while delicate, point at the truth.

The conceptual message is worth isolating: *the largest eigenvalue of a large random Hermitian matrix and the free energy of a directed polymer in 1+1 dimensions fluctuate the same way.* Random matrix theory, born in nuclear physics, turned out to be the natural language of nonequilibrium growth [6][7].

###4.3 Directed polymers and the replica Bethe ansatz

The continuum directed polymer is a Brownian path `π` from `(0,0)` to `(x,t)` weighted by `exp(∫ ξ(π(s),s) ds)`; its partition function *is* the SHE solution `Z(x,t)`. The replica trick studies `⟨Zⁿ⟩` = partition function of `n` non-intersecting-in-law replicas, which interact through the attractive delta potential — the delta Bose gas with coupling `c < 0`. Its Bethe eigenfunctions are

```
Ψ_z(x₁,…,xₙ) = Σ_{σ∈Sₙ} Π_{a<b} (z_{σ(a)} − z_{σ(b)} − i·c·sgn(x_a − x_b))
                 / (z_{σ(a)} − z_{σ(b)}) · exp(i Σ_j z_j x_j)
```

with rapidities `z_j` quantized by the Bethe equations on a ring. For attractive coupling the ground state is a single *n-string*: rapidities arranged as `z_j = k + i·c·(n+1−2j)/2`, a bound state whose energy gives exactly `γₙ = (n³−n)/24`. Summing over all string configurations (the *string hypothesis*) yields the full moment formula, and Dotsenko's formal Laplace inversion of the moment generating series produces the Fredholm determinant — made rigorous in [2] by working directly with a Laplace transform identity rather than the divergent series.

A minimal numerical window into the polymer geometry is the transfer-matrix (dynamic programming) computation of the point-to-point partition function on a lattice:

```python
import numpy as np

def dprm_partition(weights):
    """Point-to-point directed polymer partition function.
    weights[i,j]: random Boltzmann weights on an (n x n) lattice.
    Returns Z[i,j] = sum over directed paths of prod weights."""
    n = weights.shape[0]
    Z = np.zeros((n, n))
    Z[0, 0] = weights[0, 0]
    for s in range(1, 2 * n - 1):
        for i in range(max(0, s - n + 1), min(s + 1, n)):
            j = s - i
            up = Z[i - 1, j] if i > 0 else 0.0
            left = Z[i, j - 1] if j > 0 else 0.0
            Z[i, j] = weights[i, j] * (up + left)
    return Z

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
w = np.exp(rng.normal(size=(400, 400)))   # log-normal disorder
Z = dprm_partition(w)
F = np.log(Z[-1, -1])                      # free energy ~ KPZ height
print(f"free energy F = {F:.3f}")
```

The free energy `F = log Z` is the discrete analogue of the KPZ height; its fluctuations across disorder realizations already show the `n^{1/3}` scaling and skewed Tracy–Widom-like histograms at modest sizes.

The same transfer-matrix idea, lifted to continuous space with delta-correlated disorder, is the Feynman–Kac representation of the SHE — the bridge between the polymer picture and the PDE picture, and the reason probabilists and physicists, using different languages, proved the same theorems [2][3].

###4.4 Macdonald processes and the q-deformed hierarchy

If the replica method is a clever trick, Macdonald processes are the *theory* behind the trick [5].

```
(q,t)-Macdonald process
        │  t → 0 (q-Whittaker limit)
        ▼
   q-TASEP  ──→  ASEP  ──→  KPZ / SHE
        │
        ▼ (q → 1 limits)
log-gamma polymer ──→ semidiscrete polymer ──→ continuum polymer
```

Each arrow is a *limit theorem*: exact formulas pass to exact formulas, and steepest descent at each level yields KPZ-class asymptotics. Corwin's ICM survey [5] presents this as *integrable probability*: models are integrable when (i) concise exact formulas exist for interesting observables and (ii) asymptotics of those formulas access universal scaling limits.

The Fredholm determinants arising here can be evaluated numerically by Bornemann's method (truncated quadrature of the kernel).

```haskell
-- Fredholm determinant det(I - K) via von Koch expansion
fredholmDet :: (Double -> Double -> Double) -> Double -> Int -> Double
fredholmDet k s nMax = sum [term n / fromIntegral (factorial n) | n <- [0..nMax]]
  where
    term 0 = 1.0
    term n = quadN n   -- n-fold integral of det[k(x_i,x_j)] over (s,inf)^n
    quadN _ = 0.0      -- placeholder: Gauss-Legendre quadrature goes here

-- Airy kernel K_Ai(x,y) = (Ai(x)Ai'(y) - Ai'(x)Ai(y)) / (x - y)
-- F_GUE(s) = fredholmDet kAi s 12  ≈ Tracy-Widom GUE CDF
```

This is exactly how the theoretical curves overlaid on experimental histograms are computed — the determinant is not merely an abstract object but a numerically tractable special function of the KPZ class.

###4.5 The KPZ fixed point and Airy processes

The deepest object in the theory is the **KPZ fixed point**: a Markov process on upper-semicontinuous functions, invariant under the 1:2:3 scaling, conjectured (now proved for several initial data) to be the universal scaling limit of *all* 1D KPZ-class models [4].

| Initial data | Limit process | One-point law | Skewness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Narrow wedge | Airy₂ minus parabola | TW-GUE | ≈ 0.2241 |
| Flat | Airy₁ | TW-GOE | ≈ 0.2935 |
| Stationary (Brownian) | Airy_stat | Baik–Rains | ≈ 0.3594 |

The construction of the fixed point as a *bona fide* Markov process (Matetski–Quastel–Remenik) closed a decades-long loop: the 1986 RG fixed point of [1], once a purely formal object, became a rigorously defined stochastic process. Its Brownian Gibbs property — that line ensembles resample as Brownian bridges conditioned on non-intersection — is the probabilistic expression of the integrability uncovered in [5].

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

Theory earns its keep against data. The landmark experiments are those of Takeuchi and Sano on turbulent liquid-crystal growth (2010–2011): growing DSM2 clusters realize curved (droplet) and flat KPZ interfaces at macroscopic scale, and the measured height histograms match `TW-GUE` and `TW-GOE` to within experimental error — including the skewness values tabulated above, quantities no fitting parameter can adjust. Paper-burning fronts, bacterial colony edges, and simulations of DPRM, TASEP, and ballistic deposition all fall on the same curves [4].

The proof landmarks, in chronological order:

1. **1986** — KPZ propose the equation and the 1D exponents via dynamic RG [1].
2. **1987** — Kardar computes the SHE moment Lyapunov exponents `γₙ = (n³−n)/24` by Bethe ansatz (replica method).
3. **1994** — Tracy and Widom introduce the Airy kernel and the distributions `F_GUE`, `F_GOE` [6].
4. **1997** — Bertini and Giacomin prove WASEP converges to the Hopf–Cole KPZ solution [8].
5. **2000** — Johansson proves TW-GUE fluctuations for a random growth model, linking growth to random matrices [7].
6. **2010** — Sasamoto–Spohn and **2011** — Amir–Corwin–Quastel derive the exact KPZ one-point law, rigorously in [2], formally in [3].
7. **2012** — Corwin's survey consolidates the universality class picture [4].
8. **2014** — Corwin's ICM lecture presents the Macdonald-process unification [5].
9. **2017–2021** — the KPZ fixed point is constructed as a Markov process; half-space and stationary variants solved.

A compact Rust sketch of the zero-temperature counterpart (last-passage percolation, whose fluctuations Johansson analyzed [7]) shows how the discrete optimization geometry is simulated:

```rust
/// Last-passage percolation: max-weight directed path on an n x n grid.
/// The passage time G(n,n) fluctuates on scale n^{1/3} with TW-GUE law [7].
fn last_passage(w: &Vec<Vec<f64>>) -> f64 {
    let n = w.len();
    let mut g = vec![vec![0.0; n]; n];
    for i in 0..n {
        for j in 0..n {
            let best = match (i, j) {
                (0, 0) => 0.0,
                (0, _) => g[0][j - 1],
                (_, 0) => g[i - 1][0],
                _ => g[i - 1][j].max(g[i][j - 1]),
            };
            g[i][j] = w[i][j] + best;
        }
    }
    g[n - 1][n - 1]
}
```

And the exclusion-process side, in TLA+ style, captures the particle dynamics whose height function Bertini–Giacomin scale to KPZ [8]:

```tla
---- MODULE ASEP ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS N, PRight   \* ring size, right-jump probability
VARIABLES occ         \* occ[i] = 1 iff site i occupied

JumpRight(i) ==
    /\ occ[i] = 1 /\ occ[(i+1) % N] = 0
    /\ occ' = [occ EXCEPT ![i] = 0, ![(i+1) % N] = 1]

JumpLeft(i) ==
    /\ occ[i] = 1 /\ occ[(i-1+N) % N] = 0
    /\ occ' = [occ EXCEPT ![i] = 0, ![(i-1+N) % N] = 1]

Next == \E i \in 0..N-1 : JumpRight(i) \/ JumpLeft(i)
Spec == occ \in [0..N-1 -> {0,1}] /\ [][Next]_occ
====
```

With `PRight = 1/2 + ε`, weak asymmetry `ε → 0` under diffusive scaling yields the KPZ equation in the limit — the rigorous content of [8] and the starting point of the ACQ proof strategy [2].

##6 Limitations

An honest account must mark where the theory stops.

**Higher dimensions.** In `d ≥ 2` even the exponents are unknown: no exact `α`, `β`, `z`, only numerical estimates and competing conjectures. The integrable structures (Bethe ansatz, Macdonald processes) are intrinsically one-dimensional; nothing comparable exists in higher dimensions, and it is not clear anything should.

**Solution theory.** The KPZ equation is classically ill-posed: space–time white noise makes `h` only Hölder-1/2 in space, so `(∂x h)²` is meaningless. The Hopf–Cole solution is the physically relevant one, but making sense of KPZ for *general* initial data required Hairer's regularity structures (Fields Medal 2014) and paracontrolled calculus — deep theories this thesis takes as background. Pathwise well-posedness and the exact fluctuation theory remain partly separate worlds.

**Beyond integrable models.** Every exact limit theorem cited here — [2][3][5][6][7][8] — concerns an *integrable* model. That *generic* 1D growth models (with no algebraic structure) lie in the KPZ class is a universality conjecture supported by numerics and experiments, not theorems. Proving universality without integrability is the field's central open problem.

**Tails and large deviations.** The Tracy–Widom laws describe *typical* `t^{1/3}` fluctuations. Far-tail large deviations (`h ~ t` deviations) have a different structure — cubic-exponential left tails, Gaussian-like right tails — and their full large-deviation principles, studied via the Kadomtsev–Petviashvili equation and weak-noise theory, are still being completed.

**Stationary and half-space geometries.** The stationary (Brownian) initial data case required the Baik–Rains distribution and substantially harder analysis; half-space KPZ with boundary parameters was solved only recently. Multi-time, multi-point formulas exist but remain technically demanding.

##7 Conclusion

The KPZ universality class is one of the great unifications of modern probability. From the phenomenological equation of [1] through the exact solution [2][3], the particle-system approximations [8], the random-matrix connection [6][7], and the algebraic synthesis of [5], a single picture has emerged: in one dimension, far-from-equilibrium fluctuations are not Gaussian noise to be averaged away but a rigid, exactly computable mathematical structure — Tracy–Widom laws, Airy processes, and ultimately the KPZ fixed point.

The thesis closes with three challenges for the next decade. First, *higher dimensions*: find the exponents, or prove they cannot be found by integrable means. Second, *strong universality*: prove that the KPZ fixed point attracts generic, non-integrable models. Third, *new integrability*: extend the Macdonald-process framework [5] to half-space, inhomogeneous, and multi-species settings, where exact formulas are only beginning to appear. Whatever the answers, the 1:2:3 scaling and the Tracy–Widom laws will remain — the fixed landmarks by which all future theories of nonequilibrium universality are judged.
