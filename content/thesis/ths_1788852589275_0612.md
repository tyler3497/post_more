# Post-Quantum Digital Signatures for X.509 Public-Key Infrastructure: CRYSTALS-Dilithium and Falcon Lattice Constructions, SPHINCS+ Hash-Based Security, and Standards-Driven Migration Pathways

## Abstract

The standardization of module-lattice and hash-based digital signature algorithms by NIST in 2024–2025 — ML-DSA (FIPS 204) [1], SLH-DSA (FIPS 205), and the forthcoming FN-DSA (draft FIPS 206) [3] — has converted post-quantum migration from a speculative exercise into an urgent infrastructure program. This thesis presents a rigorous treatment of the three surviving signature families with emphasis on their deployment inside X.509 public-key infrastructure. We formalize CRYSTALS-Dilithium's *Fiat–Shamir with aborts* construction over module lattices and its rejection-sampling mechanism, prove-equivalence of its self-target Module Short Integer Solution (MSIS) and Module Learning With Errors (MLWE) reductions, and analyze Falcon's NTRU-lattice trapdoor sampling via the GPV framework with fast Fourier sampling. We then dissect SPHINCS+ as a stateless hash-based scheme built from hypertrees of Merkle trees over WOTS+ and FORS one-time signatures, establishing its minimal security assumptions. The second half of this work turns to migration engineering: we compare composite, dual-certificate, and chameleon certificate encodings under draft-ietf-lamps-pq-composite-sigs [7], quantify handshake-size blowup against Google's 7 KB viability threshold [8], and present a phased migration strategy aligned with NSA CNSA 2.0 deadlines and harvest-now-decrypt-later threat models for authentication.

## 1 Introduction

Public-key infrastructure is the load-bearing wall of digital trust. Every TLS handshake, code-signing ceremony, software update channel, and document-signing workflow depends on the unforgeability of a small number of signature schemes — RSA, ECDSA, and EdDSA — whose hardness rests on integer factorization and discrete logarithms. A cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) executing Shor's algorithm destroys all of these assumptions in polynomial time, collapsing certificate chains that were issued years before the first CRQC is built. Unlike key exchange, where ephemeral keys bound the exposure window, *authentication is retroactively vulnerable*: a CA certificate or signed firmware image captured today can be forged the moment a CRQC exists. This asymmetry makes signature migration strictly more urgent than encryption migration, even as both are driven by the same harvest-now-decrypt-later (HNDL) logic.

NIST's post-quantum cryptography standardization program, concluded in its first phase in August 2024, selected three signature primitives for distinct roles. **ML-DSA** (Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm), derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium and standardized in FIPS 204 [1][2], is the primary general-purpose signature. **SLH-DSA** (Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Algorithm), derived from SPHINCS+ and standardized in FIPS 205, is the conservative backup whose security reduces only to hash-function properties. **FN-DSA** (FFT-over-NTRU-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm), derived from Falcon and expected as FIPS 206, occupies a compactness niche: its Level-1 signatures are only 666 bytes, roughly 3.6× smaller than ML-DSA-44 [8].

> **Theorem (Migration imperative):** For any certification authority whose root certificate has lifetime L years, if a CRQC is projected to arrive within T years with non-negligible probability, then signatures must migrate to post-quantum algorithms before T − L, because certificates valid across the quantum threshold inherit its insecurity. [3]

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the algebraic and hash-based foundations. Section 3 documents our methodology: standards corpus analysis, size/throughput benchmarking methodology, and hybrid-construction formal evaluation criteria. Section 4 provides the technical core: Dilithium's Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts construction, Falcon's GPV trapdoor sampling, SPHINCS+'s hypertree composition, and the X.509 hybrid-certificate encoding standards. Section 5 gives empirical results: measured signature sizes, verification latencies, handshake-blowup analysis, and security proofs sketches. Section 6 discusses limitations, including Falcon's floating-point side channels and SPHINCS+'s signature bloat. Section 7 concludes with an actionable migration roadmap.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Lattice Problems and Structured Variants

The hardness bedrock of ML-DSA and FN-DSA is the **Learning With Errors (LWE)** problem: given pairs (a, ⟨a,s⟩ + e) for random a and small error e, recover s. Its *ring* and *module* variants replace the vector space with the polynomial ring R_q = ℤ_q[x]/(x^n + 1), gaining a factor-n efficiency improvement at the cost of additional algebraic structure. Module lattices, parameterized by a rank k over R_q, interpolate between plain LWE (k = 1 over ℤ_q) and Ring-LWE (k = 1 over R_q), permitting fine-grained security–performance tradeoffs. The **Module Short Integer Solution (MSIS)** problem — find a short vector z with [A | I]·z = 0 mod q — underpins the Fiat–Shamir security of Dilithium [1].

The NTRU lattice underpinning Falcon is defined by a public polynomial h = g·f⁻¹ mod q, with secret short polynomials f, g. Security reduces to the SIS problem over this rank-2 module structure, the *NTRU problem*, which has resisted cryptanalysis since 1996 despite its additional structure.

### 2.2 Hash-Based Signatures

Hash-based signatures are the oldest post-quantum paradigm: Lamport one-time signatures date to 1979, and Merkle trees to 1989. The **Winternitz one-time signature (WOTS+)** generalizes Lamport by trading signature size against the number of hash chains. A Merkle tree authenticates 2^h WOTS+ keys under a single root with O(h) authentication paths. **SPHINCS+** stacks these into a *hypertree*: d layers of Merkle trees, where each tree's root is signed by a WOTS+ key of the tree above, and the bottom layer signs a **FORS** (Forest of Random Subsets) few-time signature of the message. The resulting scheme is *stateless* — no signer-side state tracking is needed, eliminating the catastrophic failure mode of stateful schemes like XMSS where key reuse voids security [2].

### 2.3 The X.509 Encodings Problem

X.509 certificates were designed for compact classical keys: a 256-bit ECDSA public key and 64-byte signature. Post-quantum replacements are 5–50× larger. ML-DSA-65 carries a 1,952-byte public key and 3,309-byte signature [5][6]; SLH-DSA signatures range from ~7.8 KB to ~49 KB. These sizes interact badly with real infrastructure: TLS records fragment at 16 KB, TCP initial windows stall, middleboxes drop large ClientHellos, and Cloudflare measurements show "steep increases in client and middlebox failures" past 10 KB [8]. The IETF LAMPS working group has responded with the **composite signature** drafts, which bundle classical and post-quantum components into single algorithm identifiers so that existing parsers, ASN.1 tooling, and certificate-validation code paths continue to function [7].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis integrates four methodological strands:

1. **Standards corpus analysis.** We track the normative documents: FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) [1][2], the SPHINCS+ standardization (FIPS 205), the draft FIPS 206 for FN-DSA, and the LAMPS drafts `draft-ietf-lamps-pq-composite-sigs` [7] and its companion drafts on composite keys, hybrid CSR attributes, and chameleon certificates. Security claims are checked against the cited reduction proofs in each document.
2. **Parametric size and cost modeling.** Signature, public-key, and secret-key sizes are tabulated per parameter set; handshake-size budgets are computed for full TLS 1.3 chains (leaf + intermediate + root, plus Certificate Transparency SCTs) and compared against empirically observed middlebox thresholds [8].
3. **Hybrid-construction evaluation criteria.** Following the IETF PQUIP hybrid-signature-spectrums taxonomy, we score composite encodings on *non-separability* (weak vs. strong), downgrade resilience, and negotiation agility, and we note where Composite ML-DSA provably fails SUF-CMA against quantum adversaries while still achieving its migration purpose [7].
4. **Cryptanalytic survey.** We review the assumption lineage: the self-target MSIS reduction for Dilithium's Fiat–Shamir with aborts, the GPV framework security proof for Falcon's randomized rounding, and the tight multi-target reductions for SPHINCS+'s tweakable hash functions.

All quantitative claims derive from published standard parameters and cited measurement studies; no new cryptanalysis is claimed.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 CRYSTALS-Dilithium: Fiat–Shamir with Aborts over Module Lattices

Dilithium is the canonical realization of **Fiat–Shamir with aborts** (Lyubashevsky 2009/2012), the lattice analogue of Schnorr signatures with a rejection-sampling firewall. Key generation samples a random module matrix **A** ∈ R_q^{k×ℓ} and short secret vectors s₁, s₂, publishing t = A·s₁ + s₂. Signing proceeds as:

1. Sample a masking vector y uniformly from a bounded range.
2. Compute the commitment w = A·y, then w₁ = HighBits(w).
3. Derive the challenge c = H(μ ∥ w₁) with exactly τ nonzero ±1 coefficients.
4. Compute the response z = y + c·s₁.
5. **Rejection sampling:** if ‖z‖∞ ≥ γ₁ − β or the low bits of w − c·s₂ leak information about s₂, restart from step 1.

The abort step is the security linchpin. Without it, the distribution of z would be a shifted copy of the secret-dependent distribution, leaking s₁ through statistical analysis — precisely the flaw that broke early lattice signatures such as GGH and NTRUSign. With aborts, the output distribution of z is *independent of the secret key*, and the scheme admits a reduction: forging a signature implies solving the **self-target MSIS** problem, which reduces to standard MSIS [1].

Critically, Dilithium samples all randomness from a **centered binomial distribution (CBD)** and uniform bounded ranges — never from discrete Gaussians. This design choice, highlighted in the FIPS 204 security rationale, eliminates the floating-point and high-precision sampling machinery that has historically been the side-channel Achilles' heel of lattice signatures [1][6]. Implementations are constant-time by construction, and the scheme supports a deterministic variant via an internal PRF, removing catastrophic randomness failures.

| Parameter set | NIST level | Public key | Secret key | Signature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-DSA-44 | 2 | 1,312 B | 2,560 B | 2,420 B |
| ML-DSA-65 | 3 | 1,952 B | 4,032 B | 3,309 B |
| ML-DSA-87 | 5 | 2,592 B | 4,896 B | 4,627 B |

The parameter sets correspond to NIST security categories 2, 3, and 5 — roughly the strength of SHA-256/SHA-384/AES-256 against quantum search — and FIPS 204 additionally defines **HashML-DSA**, a domain-separated prehash variant for large or streaming messages [1].

> **Theorem (Dilithium unforgeability):** ML-DSA is strongly existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack (SUF-CMA) assuming the hardness of self-target MSIS and MLWE over the module lattice of rank (k, ℓ), in the (quantum) random-oracle model. [1]

### 4.2 Falcon: GPV Trapdoor Sampling over NTRU Lattices

Falcon takes the opposite design pole from Dilithium: instead of avoiding trapdoor sampling, it *embraces* it, and instead of avoiding Gaussians, it samples from them — but over a lattice with an exceptionally compact representation. The secret key is a pair of short polynomials (f, g) satisfying the NTRU equation f·G − g·F = q for auxiliary short (F, G); the public key is the single polynomial h = g·f⁻¹ mod q.

Signing follows the **Gentry–Peikert–Vaikuntanathan (GPV)** framework: hash the message to a target point c in the lattice's ambient space, then use the trapdoor basis **B** (a 2×2 matrix of polynomials encoding (f, g, F, G)) to sample a lattice vector v close to c via *randomized nearest-plane* with discrete Gaussian widths determined by the Gram–Schmidt norms of **B**. The signature is the short difference s = (s₁, s₂) with s₁ + s₂·h = H(m). Verification checks only the shortness bound and the NTRU relation — no trapdoor needed.

The engineering masterstroke is the **fast Fourier sampling**: because the trapdoor basis has a recursive LDL-tree structure, Gaussian sampling runs in O(n log n) via FFT over complex doubles rather than O(n²), and the entire secret key compresses to the seed that regenerates (f, g). The price is well documented: floating-point Gaussian sampling is "extremely difficult to implement without timing side channels" [8], making Falcon suitable for *infrequent, high-assurance signing* — root and intermediate CAs — rather than high-volume leaf issuance. This is precisely why NIST positioned FN-DSA as a specialized standard (draft FIPS 206) rather than the primary one [3].

At Level 1, Falcon-512 produces **666-byte signatures** with 897-byte public keys — 3.6× smaller than ML-DSA-44 — which materially changes the PKI size calculus: a full Falcon chain adds only 5–8 KB to a handshake versus 17–20 KB for composite ML-DSA hybrids [8].

### 4.3 SPHINCS+: Stateless Hash-Based Security from Minimal Assumptions

SPHINCS+ is architecturally the most conservative of the three: its security reduces *only* to the second-preimage resistance, pseudorandomness, and related properties of its underlying tweakable hash functions (SHA-256, SHAKE-256, or Haraka variants). No lattice, code, or isogeny assumption is required. If SHA-256 survives, SPHINCS+ survives — a hedge against the structural cryptanalytic risk that shadows all structured-lattice schemes.

The construction is a **hypertree** of height h divided into d layers of Merkle trees, each of height h/d. Signing a message M proceeds bottom-up:

1. **FORS** (Forest of Random Subsets): M is hashed and split into k chunks of a bits; each chunk selects one leaf from each of k Merkle trees of height a, revealing k·(a+1) hash values. FORS is a *few-time* signature: each key can sign a handful of messages before security degrades.
2. The FORS public key (the k tree roots) is signed by a **WOTS+** one-time key at the bottom hypertree layer.
3. Each hypertree layer's root is signed by a WOTS+ key of the layer above, chaining d authentication paths to the single top-level root — the SPHINCS+ public key (32–64 bytes).

```python
# Simplified SPHINCS+ hypertree signing (illustrative, not constant-time)
def sphincs_sign(sk_seed, pk_root, msg, d_layers, h_total):
    fors_sig, fors_pk = fors_sign(sk_seed, msg)          # few-time layer
    sig, cur_root = fors_sig, fors_pk
    for layer in range(d_layers):                        # hypertree ascent
        wots_sk = derive_wots_key(sk_seed, layer, idx)
        auth_path = merkle_auth_path(layer, idx)
        sig += wots_sign(wots_sk, cur_root) + auth_path
        cur_root = layer_root(layer, idx)
        idx //= 2 ** (h_total // d_layers)
    assert cur_root == pk_root
    return sig
```

Statelessness is the headline property: unlike XMSS, the signer picks FORS and WOTS+ indices pseudorandomly per message, so no state synchronization is needed across distributed signers The cost is signature size: 7,856 bytes at the smallest (128s) up to ~49 KB (256f), and signing throughput measured in tens of signatures per second rather than thousands.

### 4.4 X.509 Migration: Composite, Dual, and Chameleon Encodings

With algorithms selected, the binding constraint is *deployment*: how do post-quantum signatures enter X.509 without flag-day upgrades? The IETF LAMPS working group has converged on three mechanisms:

**Composite signatures** (`draft-ietf-lamps-pq-composite-sigs` [7]) define new algorithm OIDs whose public key is `SEQUENCE SIZE (2) OF BIT STRING` and whose signature value concatenates both component signatures, e.g. `id-MLDSA65-ECDSA-P256-SHA256`. They are drop-in replacements for the `AlgorithmIdentifier` and `signatureValue` fields of RFC 5280 certificates, PKCS#10 CSRs, and CMS — no parser changes. Security analysis in the draft is candid: Composite ML-DSA is *not* SUF-CMA against quantum adversaries (a quantum attacker breaks the classical component), but it provides **weak non-separability** — an adversary cannot strip one component without leaving evidence — and, within X.509, a practical form of strong non-separability because the signed-over algorithm label still names the composite [7].

**Non-composite (dual-certificate) approaches** issue parallel classical and PQ certificates for the same identity, with the relying party selecting based on capability negotiation. **Chameleon certificates** (draft-sun-lamps-hybrid-scheme) embed the alternative PQ public key and signature as non-critical X.509v3 extensions, so legacy validators see a normal classical certificate while upgraded validators enforce the PQ component. This offers the smoothest incremental rollout at the cost of extension-parsing complexity and larger certificates either way.

A phased migration strategy consistent with CNSA 2.0 timelines and the size data is:

1. **Roots and intermediates first** — long-lived, infrequently signed; Falcon/FN-DSA or ML-DSA-87 with offline ceremonies.
2. **Hybrid issuance for leaves** — composite ML-DSA + ECDSA during the transition decade, accepting 17–20 KB handshake overhead where middlebox tolerance allows.
3. **Negotiation agility** — TLS `signature_schemes` extension and X.509 algorithm-OID agility so endpoints converge on pure-PQ once ecosystem support passes threshold.
4. **SLH-DSA for the crown jewels** — root CA keys and firmware-signing keys where conservative assumptions outweigh size costs.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Size and Performance Landscape

| Scheme | Level | Public key | Signature | Sign ops/s (ref) | Assumption |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ECDSA P-256 | 1 | 64 B | ~71 B | ~20,000 | ECDLP |
| ML-DSA-44 | 2 | 1,312 B | 2,420 B | ~8,000 | MLWE/MSIS |
| ML-DSA-65 | 3 | 1,952 B | 3,309 B | ~5,000 | MLWE/MSIS |
| Falcon-512 | 1 | 897 B | 666 B | ~3,000 | NTRU-SIS |
| SLH-DSA-128s | 1 | 32 B | 7,856 B | ~30 | Hash only |
| SLH-DSA-128f | 1 | 32 B | 17,088 B | ~300 | Hash only |

Verification is fast for all three schemes. Signing is the bottleneck only for SLH-DSA's small (`s`) variants.

### 5.2 Handshake-Blowup Analysis

A full TLS 1.3 chain (leaf + intermediate, plus SCTs) with composite ML-DSA-65/ECDSA certificates adds **17–20 KB** per handshake, exceeding Chrome's viability threshold and triggering Cloudflare-measured middlebox failure cliffs past 10 KB [8]. Mitigations compose: intermediate-certificate suppression via browser preloading saves ~4–5 KB (Firefox already preloads 1,400+ intermediates); Falcon-based chains cut the total to 5–8 KB; and KEMTLS-style designs eliminate handshake signatures entirely at the cost of new CA key-type infrastructure [8]. Google's Merkle-tree certificate (MTC) proposal sidesteps the size problem architecturally rather than algorithmically, underscoring that *no standardized drop-in PQ signature meets the 7 KB chain budget* [8].

### 5.3 Proof Sketches

**Dilithium → self-target MSIS.** The Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts reduction (Lyubashevsky; formalized in FIPS 204's rationale [1]) uses the forking lemma: a forger producing valid (w₁, c, z) pairs for two distinct challenges on the same commitment yields a short nonzero vector in the kernel of [A | I], i.e., an MSIS solution. Rejection sampling ensures the simulated signing oracle is indistinguishable from real signing, so the reduction is tight up to the forking loss.

**Falcon → NTRU-SIS.** The GPV framework proves that randomized nearest-plane sampling with parameter above the smoothing parameter produces signatures statistically independent of the trapdoor; a forger therefore solves SIS on the NTRU lattice. Falcon's concrete instantiation replaces exact Gaussian sampling with FFT-based floating-point sampling, with the deviation bounded and folded into the security estimate — the step cryptanalysts scrutinize most closely.

**SPHINCS+ → hash properties.** The hypertree composes EUF-CMA-secure one-time primitives: WOTS+ reduces to PRF/second-preimage properties of the tweakable hash, Merkle authentication to collision resistance of the tree hash, and FORS to its subset-revealing structure. The multi-target reduction is tight in the number of hash queries, and because each FORS/WOTS+ key is used (pseudorandomly) once, statelessness introduces no key-reuse degradation [2].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Falcon's side-channel surface.** Floating-point Gaussian sampling resists constant-time implementation; known timing and cache attacks recover keys from unprotected implementations. Mitigations (integer-only samplers, masking) cost 3–10× performance. Falcon belongs in offline CAs, not in high-volume online signers [8].
2. **SPHINCS+ signature bloat.** At 8–49 KB per signature, SLH-DSA is unusable for bandwidth-constrained protocols (DNSSEC, embedded TLS) and painful even for code signing at scale. It is a root-key and backup primitive, not a general replacement.
3. **Composite SUF-CMA gap.** As the LAMPS draft proves, Composite ML-DSA cannot be SUF-CMA against quantum adversaries because the classical component falls [7]. Hybrids buy *transition security*, not a stronger primitive.
4. **Negotiation downgrade risk.** Algorithm agility reintroduces downgrade attacks: a network adversary stripping PQ `signature_schemes` can force classical-only negotiation. 
5. **Ecosystem lag.** HSM firmware, CT logs, OCSP responders, and path-validation libraries must all learn new OIDs and size profiles. The long tail of unmaintained validators is the true migration bottleneck — a sociotechnical limitation no proof can close.
6. **Assumption concentration.** ML-DSA and FN-DSA both rest on structured lattices. A single breakthrough in ideal/module-lattice cryptanalysis would wound both primary standards simultaneously; SLH-DSA exists precisely as the uncorrelated hedge, which is why standards bodies recommend dual deployment for high-value roots [3].

## 7 Conclusion

Post-quantum signatures have crossed from research into standardization, and the question is no longer *which mathematics* but *which deployment sequence*. CRYSTALS-Dilithium's Fiat–Shamir with aborts gives the ecosystem a fast, side-channel-clean primary standard in FIPS 204 [1]; Falcon's GPV trapdoor sampling offers unmatched compactness for CA hierarchies at the price of floating-point fragility [8]; and SPHINCS+ provides the conservative hash-based anchor whose security assumptions are minimal and battle-tested [2]. The IETF LAMPS composite-signature framework [7] supplies the encoding machinery to carry all three inside X.509 without breaking the installed base, with honest, formally documented security tradeoffs.

The actionable synthesis: migrate roots and intermediates first using ML-DSA-87 or Falcon with offline ceremonies; issue hybrid composite leaf certificates through the transition decade; invest in negotiation agility and intermediate suppression to survive the handshake-size blowup; and reserve SLH-DSA for the keys whose compromise would be catastrophic. Harvest-now-forge-later is already underway for long-lived signatures — every year of delay extends the window of retroactive forgery. The standards are final; the migration clock is running.
