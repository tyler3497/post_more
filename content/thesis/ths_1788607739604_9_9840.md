# Counterexample-Guided Inductive Synthesis in the Age of Large Language Models: SyGuS Formulations, Enumerative Search, and Verification Oracles

## Abstract

Counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS) has long been the workhorse of formal program synthesis, alternating between a *learner* that proposes candidate programs and a *verifier* that refutes them with concrete counterexamples [1][3]. Large language models (LLMs) have recently emerged as remarkably effective synthesizers, yet they operate almost entirely without formal guarantees: they sample, rank by tests, and hope [5][6]. This thesis develops a unified treatment of LLM-guided program synthesis inside the CEGIS framework. We formalize LLM proposal distributions as stochastic priors over syntax-guided synthesis (SyGuS) grammars, recast sketch completion as hole-filling under grammar constraints, and characterize verification oracles — from SMT decision procedures to differential testing harnesses — as the arbiter converting counterexamples into training signal. Comparing enumerative search with observational equivalence against stochastic and neural-guided search [4], we prove progress and relative-completeness properties for LLM-guided CEGIS. Evidence from AlphaCode [5], CodeARC [8], and counterexample-guided repair [10] supports the central claim: when an LLM's inductive prior is disciplined by a verification oracle, synthesis becomes dramatically more sample-efficient and formally accountable.

## 1. Introduction

Program synthesis — the automatic construction of a program from a specification — is among the oldest ambitions of computer science. The classical formulation asks for a program *P* such that ∀*x*. *σ*(*P*, *x*) holds, where *σ* is a correctness specification in some background theory [1]. The field's central tension is *expressiveness* versus *tractability*: the program space is infinite, undecidable in general, and dense with almost-correct impostors.

Two intellectual traditions grew up around this tension. The *formal* tradition, crystallized in syntax-guided synthesis (SyGuS) [1] and the counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS) loop [3], insists that every candidate be checked against a specification by a sound verifier, and that failures be converted into *counterexamples* — concrete inputs on which the candidate errs — that permanently prune the search space. The *statistical* tradition, from genetic programming through DreamCoder's wake–sleep library learning [4] to today's LLM code generators [5][6], instead learns a *prior* over programs from data and searches in order of decreasing prior probability, accepting empirical rather than logical correctness.

These traditions were long treated as rivals. The formal camp deemed statistical synthesis unsound; the statistical camp deemed formal synthesis brittle and confined to decidable theories. The arrival of large language models changes the calculus. An LLM is, at heart, an extraordinarily strong prior over programs: it assigns high probability to syntactically valid, idiomatic, plausibly-correct code conditioned on a natural-language description [6]. What it lacks is precisely what CEGIS supplies: a *verification oracle* that says not merely "wrong" but *where* it is wrong, and a search discipline that guarantees progress.

This thesis argues that the natural synthesis architecture of the coming decade is neither pure formal search nor pure LLM sampling, but **LLM-guided CEGIS**: the LLM proposes, the oracle disposes, and counterexamples flow back into the LLM's context as the richest possible supervision signal. Our contributions are:

1. A SyGuS formulation of LLM-guided synthesis in which the LLM defines a stochastic prior over grammar derivations and counterexamples become grammar-level constraints.
2. A comparative analysis of enumerative search with observational equivalence, stochastic search (MCMC, evolutionary methods, neural-guided search), and LLM sketch completion, all viewed as different *proposal strategies* inside one CEGIS loop.
3. A taxonomy of verification oracles — SMT-based, test-based, and differential-testing oracles — with their soundness/completeness trade-offs.
4. Progress and relative-completeness theorems for the resulting LLM-guided CEGIS loop, and an empirical survey showing order-of-magnitude sample-efficiency gains when LLMs are placed inside the loop [8][10].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The SyGuS formulation

Syntax-guided synthesis, introduced by Alur et al. [1], standardizes the synthesis problem as a triple (*φ*, *G*, *f*): a semantic specification *φ* in a background theory *T*, a syntactic grammar *G* restricting candidate implementations, and a function symbol *f* to synthesize. A solution is an expression *f*<sub>imp</sub> ∈ *L*(*G*) such that *φ*[*f* ↦ *f*<sub>imp</sub>] is valid in *T*. The annual SyGuS-Comp competition has driven solver development across bit-vector, string, and invariant-synthesis tracks [2][7]. This separation is ideal here: an LLM is, above all, a mechanism for answering the syntactic question well.

### 2.2 CEGIS and the OGIS framework

Counterexample-guided inductive synthesis, originating in sketching work [2], iterates two phases. The *synthesizer* produces a candidate consistent with a finite set of examples; the *verifier* checks the candidate against the full specification, returning either success or a counterexample — an input on which the candidate violates the specification — which is added to the example set and the loop repeats [9]. Jha and Seshia generalized this to *oracle-guided inductive synthesis* (OGIS), a theory of formal synthesis via inductive learning in which arbitrary oracles mediate between learner and specification [3]. LLM-guided synthesis, we argue, is best understood as OGIS with a *neural oracle* for proposals and a *symbolic oracle* for verification.

### 2.3 Program sketching

Solar-Lezama's *sketching* [2] has the programmer write a partial program with *holes* (`??`) plus a correctness harness; the synthesizer fills the holes. It is the direct ancestor of LLM sketch completion, except holes are now filled by a learned prior rather than SAT search.

### 2.4 Enumerative and stochastic search

*Enumerative* solvers expand the grammar bottom-up in order of increasing expression size, discarding candidates that are *observationally equivalent* — indistinguishable on the accumulated examples — to already-seen ones. Equivalence reduction makes bottom-up enumeration complete relative to the grammar [1][7]. *Stochastic* solvers instead sample from a probabilistic grammar, applying MCMC moves or evolutionary operators; they scale to larger spaces but offer only probabilistic convergence guarantees. *Neural-guided* search [4] learns a library of abstractions plus a recognition model *Q*(*ρ*|*x*) proposing programs in posterior order — a learned, amortized proposal distribution. An LLM is the natural endpoint of this trajectory: a proposal distribution trained on the internet's code rather than a single domain.

### 2.5 LLMs as program synthesizers

Codex [6] showed transformers generate correct programs from docstrings; AlphaCode [5] scaled this to competitive programming — millions of candidates, test filtering, clustering — reaching top-54.3% on Codeforces. The recipe is *sample broadly, verify empirically*. Its weakness is cost and silence: a failed candidate yields only a bit of information ("tests failed"), and the model cannot explain or repair its own errors. CEGIS supplies exactly the missing feedback channel.

## 3. Methodology

We study a single unified architecture — **LLM-guided CEGIS** — and instantiate its components systematically:

```
spec φ, grammar G
examples E ← ∅
loop:
    prompt ← render(φ, G, E)            # spec + grammar + counterexamples in context
    P      ← LLM.sample(prompt, G)      # grammar-constrained decoding
    cex    ← Oracle.verify(P, φ)        # SMT / tests / differential testing
    if cex = none:  return P            # ∀x. φ(P, x) verified
    E      ← E ∪ {cex}                 # inductive refinement
```

**Methodological commitments.** First, every LLM proposal is *grammar-constrained*: decoding is restricted to derivations of *G*, so the LLM searches *L*(*G*) rather than all strings; this makes the SyGuS formulation exact rather than metaphorical. Second, counterexamples are *rendered into the prompt* as input–output constraints, so each iteration strictly shrinks the LLM's effective hypothesis space. Third, the oracle is a *parameter*: we evaluate SMT-based verification (sound, expensive), test-based oracles (cheap, unsound), and differential-testing oracles that compare against a reference implementation [8].

Our empirical claims reinterpret published results — AlphaCode [5], CodeARC [8], CEGNS [9], MaxSAT-sketch repair [10], SyGuS-Comp [2][7] — as points in this design space; theorems are proved, numbers cited.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 A SyGuS formulation of LLM-guided synthesis

We extend the SyGuS triple with a *proposal prior* Π<sub>θ</sub> over derivations of *G*. Grammar-constrained decoding makes this precise: each step's token mask admits only terminals keeping some completion inside *L*(*G*), so every sample is a valid derivation.

> **Theorem (Grammar-constrained sampling).** *Let G be a context-free grammar over the target language and let decode be any autoregressive decoder whose per-step token set is restricted to T(G, prefix) = {t : ∃ completion c, prefix·t·c ∈ L(G)}. Then every emitted string belongs to L(G), and the induced distribution Π<sub>θ</sub> is a well-defined distribution over derivations of G.*

Counterexamples enter as *negative constraints*: each *cex* ∈ *E* rules out its equivalence class, so the feasible sets form a strictly decreasing chain *L*(*G*) ⊊ *F*(*E*<sub>1</sub>) ⊊ … — the formal content of learning from mistakes.

### 4.2 Enumerative search: bottom-up enumeration with observational equivalence

Bottom-up enumeration expands expressions by size, testing each against the examples; *observational equivalence* keeps only one representative per class of programs indistinguishable on *E*.

```haskell
-- Bottom-up enumeration with observational equivalence pruning
data Expr = Const Int | Var String | Add Expr Expr | Mul Expr Expr

bySize :: Int -> [Expr]
bySize 0 = [Const 0, Const 1, Var "x", Var "y"]
bySize n = [ op e1 e2 | k <- [0..n-1], e1 <- bySize k, e2 <- bySize (n-1-k)
                     , op <- [Add, Mul] ]

prune :: [Example] -> [Expr] -> [Expr]
prune exs = nubBy (\a b -> all (\e -> eval a e == eval b e) exs)
-- Only one representative per observational-equivalence class survives.
```

Enumerative search is the reference CEGIS synthesizer: sound and complete relative to *G*, but combinatorially explosive. An LLM prior repairs exactly this weakness: enumerate in *prior* order rather than size order, preserving completeness while collapsing expected time-to-solution when the prior is good.

### 4.3 Stochastic search: from MCMC to neural-guided proposals

*Stochastic* synthesis samples *p*(*P*) ∝ exp(−cost(*P*)) — cost penalizing size, rewarding example-consistency — via Metropolis–Hastings moves, annealing, or genetic programming with crossover, mutation, and selection.

Neural-guided search [4] replaces the hand-designed proposal with a learned one. DreamCoder's recognition model *Q*(*ρ*|*x*) amortizes search: after solving tasks in a domain, the network predicts which programs are likely to solve new tasks, and the wake phase enumerates in *Q*-order. The library-learning sleep phase goes further, *changing the grammar itself* by compressing solutions into new abstractions — a learned, growing *G*. The LLM's prior is *frozen* at inference time — which is why CEGIS counterexample feedback is essential: it is the only channel that *corrects* the prior.

| Paradigm | Search bias | Oracle role | Guarantee | Representative |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Enumerative (bottom-up) | Size-ordered grammar expansion | SMT counterexamples prune equivalence classes | Sound; complete w.r.t. *G* | SyGuS-Comp solvers [7] |
| Stochastic (MCMC / evolutionary) | Probabilistic grammar, local moves | Fitness / noisy oracles | Probabilistic convergence | Classical stochastic synthesis |
| Neural-guided | Learned recognition model *Q*(*ρ*\|*x*) | Example-consistency checks | Amortized efficiency, empirical | DreamCoder [4] |
| LLM sampling | Pretrained prior over code | Test-based filtering + clustering | Empirical pass@*k* | AlphaCode [5], Codex [6] |
| **LLM-guided CEGIS** | LLM prior + counterexample prompts | SMT / differential-testing oracles | Sound when oracle is complete | CodeARC [8], CEGNS [9], MaxSAT-sketch repair [10] |

### 4.4 LLM sketch completion: holes, priors, and constrained decoding

Sketching [2] factors synthesis into *structure* (written by the human) and *details* (filled by the solver). LLM sketch completion generalizes this: the "sketch" may be a natural-language intent plus a partial program, and holes are filled by sampling from Π<sub>θ</sub> conditioned on the sketch. Three mechanisms matter:

1. **Hole typing.** Holes carry types (`?? : Int`), enforced by the decoder's token mask as in typed SyGuS grammars; function-typed holes become higher-order subproblems.
2. **Prior shaping via counterexamples.** Each counterexample is appended to the prompt as a *negative example* ("on input *x*, your program returned *y* but the spec requires *z*"). In-context, this re-weights Π<sub>θ</sub> away from the refuted behavior without any gradient update — a form of *test-time* prior correction.
3. **Constrained decoding.** Grammar masks guarantee syntactic validity, but we can go further: *semantic* masks derived from the examples prune partial derivations that already violate an accumulated counterexample, mirroring the conflict-driven learning of modern SyGuS solvers.

### 4.5 Verification oracles: from SMT to differential testing

The oracle is the load-bearing wall of CEGIS. We distinguish three regimes:

- **SMT-based oracles.** Checks ¬*φ*[*f* ↦ *P*] in the background theory; a satisfying assignment *is* the counterexample. Sound and complete for decidable theories, but expensive and inapplicable outside decidable fragments [3][9].
- **Test-based oracles.** A finite suite approximates the spec: cheap and universal but unsound — AlphaCode's filtering stage is essentially this [5].
- **Differential-testing oracles.** Given a *reference implementation*, coverage-guided fuzzing [8] searches for disagreeing inputs — the binary *is* the spec. CodeARC uses two differential-testing tools as its oracle, yet the best model reaches only 52.7%: oracle strength, not just prior strength, bounds performance [8].

```rust
// A bounded verification oracle: refutes the candidate or certifies it
// on the explored input domain.
enum Verdict { Verified, Counterexample(Vec<i64>) }

fn verify(candidate: &dyn Fn(&[i64]) -> i64,
          spec: &dyn Fn(&[i64], i64) -> bool) -> Verdict {
    for inputs in bounded_input_space() {
        let out = candidate(&inputs);
        if !spec(&inputs, out) {
            return Verdict::Counterexample(inputs); // concrete witness
        }
    }
    Verdict::Verified
}
```

This induces a *soundness ladder* — tests, differential, SMT — that LLM-guided CEGIS can climb within a single run: cheap oracles first, expensive ones for finalists.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 What the literature reports

| System | Setting | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| AlphaCode [5] | Competitive programming, LLM sampling + test filtering | Top 54.3% on Codeforces; 34.2% of CodeContests problems solved |
| CodeARC [8] | Inductive synthesis, LLM agents + differential-testing oracle | 1,114 functions; best model (o3-mini) 52.7%; SFT on synthesis traces gives +31% relative on LLaMA-3.1-8B |
| MaxSAT-sketch LLM repair [10] | Program repair, CEGIS + fault localization | 1,431 student programs; all 6 evaluated LLMs repair more programs with smaller patches vs. baselines |
| Counterexample-guided neural synthesis [9] | Invariant synthesis, neural learner + SMT verifier | Neural guidance accelerates CEGIS on loop-invariant benchmarks vs. pure enumeration |
| DreamCoder [4] | Library learning + neural-guided search | Solutions requiring >10<sup>72</sup> years of brute-force search found in <10 minutes |
| SyGuS-Comp [2][7] | Formal synthesis competition | Enumerative + SMT-based solvers dominate decidable tracks; stochastic solvers competitive on PBE |

Two patterns stand out. First, *oracle feedback beats prior scale*: counterexample loops improve sample efficiency by large factors [8]. Second, *the oracle is the bottleneck*: even frontier models stall at 52.7% under strong differential testing [8] — future gains will come as much from better verifiers as from bigger priors.

### 5.2 Theorems: progress and relative completeness

We now state the formal guarantees of LLM-guided CEGIS. Fix a SyGuS problem (*φ*, *G*, *f*) and a sound oracle.

> **Theorem (CEGIS progress).** *In LLM-guided CEGIS with a sound oracle, no candidate program is ever proposed twice up to observational equivalence on the accumulated examples: each iteration's counterexample refutes the current candidate's entire equivalence class, so the sequence of feasible sets is strictly decreasing.*

*Proof sketch.* Let *E*<sub>*t*</sub> be the examples at iteration *t* and *cex*<sub>*t*</sub> the refuting witness. Every program equivalent to *P*<sub>*t*</sub> on *E*<sub>*t*+1</sub> = *E*<sub>*t*</sub> ∪ {*cex*<sub>*t*</sub>} also fails the witness, so *F*(*E*<sub>*t*+1</sub>) ⊊ *F*(*E*<sub>*t*</sub>); the next sample, conditioned on *E*<sub>*t*+1</sub>, cannot lie in the refuted class. ∎

> **Theorem (Relative completeness).** *If the oracle is complete for the background theory T and some f*<sub>imp</sub> ∈ L(G) satisfies φ, then LLM-guided CEGIS with a proposal distribution Π<sub>θ</sub> of full support over derivations of G eventually proposes a correct program.*

*Proof sketch.* Full support plus semantic masking reproduces the classical CEGIS completeness argument [3]; the solution's class is never refuted by a sound oracle, so it survives until proposed. ∎

The practical import is the *rate*: hitting time is governed by Π<sub>θ</sub>(solution | *φ*, *E*), not grammar size — the guarantees of formal synthesis with the efficiency of learned priors.

```python
# The complete LLM-guided CEGIS loop, as studied in this thesis.
def llm_guided_cegis(spec, grammar, llm, oracle, max_iters=64):
    examples = []  # accumulated counterexamples: the training signal
    for t in range(max_iters):
        prompt = render_sygus_prompt(spec, grammar, examples)
        candidate = llm.sample(prompt, grammar=grammar)  # constrained decoding
        cex = oracle.verify(candidate, spec)             # SMT / differential / tests
        if cex is None:
            return candidate  # verified: |= forall x. spec(candidate, x)
        examples.append(cex)  # inductive refinement; strictly shrinks hypothesis space
    raise SynthesisTimeout(f"no solution in {max_iters} iterations")
```

## 6. Limitations

**Specification incompleteness.** LLM-era specifications are often natural-language intents or test suites — both incomplete — and no oracle can verify what was never formalized. Differential testing [8] uses a reference implementation as the spec, but references are not always available.

**Oracle cost.** SMT queries scale poorly and differential testing dominates wall-clock time in systems like CodeARC [8]; the soundness ladder (§4.5) mitigates but does not eliminate this.

**Prior miscalibration.** LLM priors over-weight idiomatic-but-wrong solutions and under-explore unusual-but-correct ones; counterexample feedback corrects the prior only *in context*, never the weights.

**Counterexample quality.** Arbitrary witnesses may yield near-duplicate failures that each prune little; *minimal* or *diverse* counterexamples — active OGIS research [3] — would sharpen the signal.

**Grammar expressiveness vs. decidability.** Constraining the LLM to *L*(*G*) buys guarantees but forfeits the LLM's ability to invent constructs outside the grammar — the very creativity that makes LLMs exciting. Choosing *G* remains an art.

**Non-termination.** Full support and complete oracles are idealizations; in practice temperatures truncate support and oracles time out, so deployments need iteration budgets and fallbacks [9].

---

## 7. Conclusion

We have presented LLM-guided synthesis as CEGIS: the LLM is a stochastic proposal prior over SyGuS derivations, sketch completion is grammar-constrained hole-filling, and verification oracles convert failures into counterexamples that strictly shrink the hypothesis space. Enumeration gives the completeness baseline, neural-guided search [4] the historical bridge, LLMs the prior that makes the loop practical [5][6].

The theorems reassure — strict progress, relative completeness — but the empirical lesson is the point: systems improve most when the *feedback channel* improves. Counterexamples carry strictly more information than scalar fitness, and architectures routing them back into the proposer [8][9][10] dominate sample-and-filter designs. The maxim: *never let a failed candidate die in silence.* The future is neither the LLM nor the solver alone, but the disciplined conversation between them.
