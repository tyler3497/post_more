# Serializability Theory and Deterministic Concurrency Control: From Conflict Graphs to Spanner, Calvin, and FaunaDB

## Abstract

Serializability remains the gold standard of transactional correctness, yet the mechanisms by which modern databases achieve it have diverged radically from the classical pessimistic schedulers of the 1980s. This article traces the arc from conflict serializability and serialization-graph testing through two-phase locking, multiversion concurrency control (MVCC), and snapshot isolation anomalies, to the deterministic concurrency control designs that now underpin planet-scale systems. We review the graph-theoretic foundations established by Papadimitriou and systematized by Bernstein, Hadzilacos, and Goodman [2][1]; explain why the anomalies catalogued by Adya, Liskov, and O'Neil forced a generalized isolation theory; and dissect how snapshot isolation's write-skew phenomenon motivated serializable snapshot isolation. We then turn to determinism as a first-class design principle: Calvin's ordering-first scheduler, Spanner's TrueTime-driven external consistency via commit wait, and the Calvin-derived architecture of FaunaDB. We show that determinism converts the correctness problem from dynamic graph cycle detection — NP-complete in general and linear only under restricted conditions — into static, agreed-upon ordering, yielding systems whose isolation guarantees are checkable rather than emergent.

## 1 Introduction

Every database that serves concurrent clients faces the same fundamental question: *which interleavings of operations are acceptable?* The classical answer — serializability — demands that every concurrent execution be equivalent to some serial execution of the same transactions [1][2]. Equivalence is defined through conflicts: two operations on the same item conflict if at least one is a write, and a schedule is *conflict-serializable* exactly when its serialization graph is acyclic [1][2].

For two decades this theory powered two-phase locking (2PL) and timestamp ordering, the workhorses of single-node systems. But three pressures fractured the consensus. First, the move to distributed, partitioned, and geo-replicated architectures made fine-grained locking prohibitively expensive and deadlock-prone. Second, the popularity of snapshot isolation — cheaper reads, no read-write blocking — introduced *non-serializable* behaviors such as write skew that the ANSI SQL isolation definitions failed even to describe [6][5]. Third, the demand for globally consistent reads across continents forced systems like Spanner to rethink the relationship between time, order, and commit [3].

The response has been a turn toward **determinism**. Rather than letting transactions execute opportunistically and then validating their interleavings (optimistic) or acquiring locks as they go (pessimistic), deterministic systems *decide the serial order before execution*: Calvin sequences transactions through deterministic batching and replication, then executes them identically on every replica [4]; Spanner assigns commit timestamps through TrueTime's bounded-uncertainty clock and enforces external consistency with commit wait [3]; FaunaDB commercializes Calvin's core into a multi-tenant transactional service. This article provides a unified treatment of these ideas, from the graph theory of the 1970s to the deterministic schedulers of today.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Serializability Criterion

A *schedule* is a partially ordered sequence of operations from a set of transactions. Two operations conflict when they touch the same data item and at least one writes it. Two schedules are *conflict-equivalent* when they order every pair of conflicting operations identically. A schedule is *conflict-serializable* (CSR) if it is conflict-equivalent to some serial schedule.

> **Theorem (Serialization Graph Testing):** A schedule *S* is conflict-serializable if and only if its serialization graph SG(*S*) is acyclic. SG(*S*) contains one node per transaction and a directed edge *Tᵢ → Tⱼ* whenever an operation of *Tᵢ* precedes and conflicts with an operation of *Tⱼ* [1].

This theorem is constructive: acyclicity can be tested in linear time, so a scheduler can validate schedules dynamically. However, Papadimitriou proved that deciding whether a *view*-serializable schedule exists for an arbitrary history is **NP-complete** [2], and view serializability is strictly weaker than conflict serializability. The practical consequence is that production schedulers almost universally enforce CSR, the checkable criterion, and leave VSR to theorists.

### 2.2 Two-Phase Locking and Timestamp Ordering

Bernstein, Hadzilacos, and Goodman systematized the classical scheduler taxonomy [1]:

- **Two-phase locking (2PL):** a transaction never acquires a lock after releasing one. 2PL generates only CSR schedules, and *strict* 2PL (holding write locks until commit) additionally yields recoverability and cascadelessness. Its costs are deadlock — requiring waits-for graph detection or timeouts — and lock manager contention.
- **Timestamp ordering (TO):** each transaction receives a unique timestamp; conflicting operations must execute in timestamp order, aborting violators. TO avoids deadlock but introduces *timestamp-order violations* and high abort rates under contention.
- **Optimistic concurrency control (OCC):** transactions execute on private copies and validate at commit; the validation phase performs exactly a serialization-graph cycle check restricted to the committing transaction.

All three share a common architecture: execution is *non-deterministic*, and correctness is enforced by monitoring the emerging conflict graph at runtime.

### 2.3 Multiversion Concurrency Control

Multiversion systems keep multiple versions of each data item, letting readers see a consistent snapshot without blocking writers. Multiversion timestamp ordering and multiversion locking generalize CSR to version orders [1]. The defining production variant, **snapshot isolation (SI)**, gives each transaction a snapshot of committed data as of its start and applies *first-committer-wins* on write-write conflicts [6].

SI is cheap and popular — it is the default in PostgreSQL's original MVCC and Oracle — but it is **not serializable**. The counterexample is *write skew*:

> **Write-skew anomaly:** Two transactions each read two account balances, and each updates one account subject to a joint constraint (e.g., sum ≥ 0). Under SI, both snapshots see the old balances, both commits satisfy first-committer-wins (their write sets are disjoint), and the joint constraint is violated. No serial execution can produce this state [6][5].

### 2.4 Generalized Isolation Theory

Berenson et al. showed that the ANSI SQL isolation levels were defined by proscribing *anomalies* (dirty read, non-repeatable read, phantom) that were ambiguously specified and insufficient to characterize SI [6]. Adya, Liskov, and O'Neil responded with a rigorous framework based on dependency graphs: *ww*, *wr*, and *rw* dependencies between transactions, defining phenomena G0 (write cycles), G1a/G1b/G1c (aborted/intermediate/dirty reads), G2 (anti-dependency cycles), and the item-level vs. predicate-level split [5]. Under this framework, snapshot isolation admits exactly **G2-item**: dependency cycles containing at least one rw-antidependency — precisely write skew and its cousins.

## 3 Methodology

This article is a synthesis of theory and system design, developed through three complementary methods:

1. **Graph-theoretic analysis.** We work directly with serialization graphs and Adya-style dependency graphs, using them as the lingua franca that connects classical CSR theory, SI anomaly catalogs, and modern serializable-MVCC validators.
2. **Worked anomaly schedules.** Rather than asymptotic benchmarks — which cannot settle correctness questions — §5 presents concrete schedules demonstrating write skew under SI, its detection by serializable snapshot isolation (SSI), and the timestamp-ordering argument behind Spanner's commit wait. Each schedule is small enough to verify by hand yet faithful to the systems it models.
3. **Architectural comparison.** We compare three deterministic designs — Calvin's ordering-first batching, Spanner's TrueTime commit protocol, and FaunaDB's Calvin-derived pipeline — along the axes of where the serial order is decided, how replicas agree on it, and what failure modes remain.

Correctness claims rest on published proofs [1][2][5][3][4]; performance discussion is qualitative and architectural.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Serialization Graphs and Dynamic Cycle Testing

The serialization graph theorem is the single most useful fact in concurrency control. Consider the classic schedule:

```
S = r1(x) r2(x) w2(x) r2(y) w1(y) c1 c2
```

Edges: *T₁ → T₂* from *r₁(x)* before *w₂(x)* (read-write conflict), and *T₂ → T₁* from *r₂(y)* before *w₁(y)*. The graph contains a cycle, so *S* is not conflict-serializable: *T₁* must precede *T₂* on *x* but follow it on *y*.

Dynamic schedulers exploit a crucial property: edges are only added, never removed, as execution proceeds, so a cycle can only *form* — it cannot dissolve. Optimistic validators therefore maintain an incremental graph and abort a transaction the moment its inclusion closes a cycle [1]. The cost is bookkeeping proportional to the conflict rate, which is exactly why high-contention workloads punish OCC: aborts cascade precisely where conflicts are densest.

Serializable Snapshot Isolation (SSI), the algorithm behind PostgreSQL's `SERIALIZABLE` level, operationalizes Adya's theory: it tracks *rw*-antidependencies between concurrent transactions and aborts when a *dangerous structure* — two consecutive rw-edges *T₁ → T₂ → T₃* with *T₃* committing first — appears [7]. This is cycle detection specialized to snapshot execution — **graph theory is the shared substrate** of every scheduler generation.

A minimal cycle detector illustrates the mechanism that OCC and SSI validators implement:

```python
def sg_acyclic(edges):
    """Incremental serialization-graph cycle check.
    edges: iterable of (ti, tj) conflict edges. Returns True iff CSR holds."""
    graph = {}
    for u, v in edges:
        graph.setdefault(u, set()).add(v)
        # DFS from v: if we can reach u, a cycle just formed
        stack, seen = [v], set()
        while stack:
            n = stack.pop()
            if n == u:
                return False   # cycle -> abort the committing transaction
            if n not in seen:
                seen.add(n)
                stack.extend(graph.get(n, ()))
    return True

# Write-skew schedule edges: T1 -rw-> T2, T2 -rw-> T1
print(sg_acyclic([("T1", "T2"), ("T2", "T1")]))  # False: not serializable
```

### 4.2 Two-Phase Locking and Its Limits at Scale

Strict 2PL's correctness argument is elegant: because a transaction holds all its locks until commit, any conflicting edge *Tᵢ → Tⱼ* in SG(*S*) implies *Tᵢ*'s lock point precedes *Tⱼ*'s, and lock points totally order the transactions — hence acyclicity [1]. The price is paid in three currencies:

- **Deadlock.** Lock waits form a waits-for graph whose cycles must be detected or timed out. In distributed 2PL, deadlock detection itself becomes a distributed algorithm with false positives and probe-message overhead.
- **Lock manager contention.** Every operation pays a latch on centralized lock state; in main-memory systems this dominates.
- **Blocking reads.** Read locks stall writers, so long analytic reads serialize the write workload — the reason production systems fled to MVCC for read-heavy mixes.

Partitioning does not save 2PL: a transaction touching *k* partitions needs distributed commit (2PC) on top of distributed locking, compounding latency with coordination rounds.

### 4.3 MVCC, Snapshot Isolation, and the Write-Skew Anomaly

Under snapshot isolation, each transaction *T* reads from the committed snapshot at its start timestamp *s(T)* and commits only if no concurrent transaction wrote an item *T* also wrote (first-committer-wins). The anomaly space is exactly Adya's **G2-item**: any dependency cycle containing at least one rw-antidependency [5].

The canonical worked example is write skew on a joint constraint. Initial state: accounts *A = 70*, *B = 70*, constraint *A + B ≥ 0*... realistically *A + B ≥ 100*. Both transactions read the full balances and withdraw from different accounts:

| Time | T₁ (withdraw 60 from A) | T₂ (withdraw 60 from B) |
|------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| t₁ | *r₁(A=70), r₁(B=70)* | |
| t₂ | | *r₂(A=70), r₂(B=70)* |
| t₃ | *w₁(A=10)* | |
| t₄ | | *w₂(B=10)* |
| t₅ | commit ✓ | |
| t₆ | | commit ✓ |

First-committer-wins passes for both (disjoint write sets), yet the final state *A=10, B=10* violates *A + B ≥ 100*. The dependency graph has rw-edges *T₁ → T₂* (T₁ read *B*, T₂ wrote *B*) and *T₂ → T₁* — a G2-item cycle [5].

Two fixes dominate practice. **SSI** (Cahill, Röhm, Fekete) detects the dangerous structure of consecutive rw-antidependencies and aborts one transaction [7]. **Fekete et al.** showed that many workloads can be made serializable under SI by promoting selected reads to writes (materializing conflicts), a static analysis that trades false aborts for zero runtime graph tracking [8]. Both confirm the deeper lesson: *snapshot isolation is serializable only when its rw-antidependency cycles are eliminated*, and elimination can be static, dynamic, or deterministic.

### 4.4 Deterministic Scheduling: Calvin's Ordering-First Architecture

Calvin (Thomson et al.) inverts the classical pipeline [4]. Instead of executing transactions and then discovering their order, it **agrees on the order first**:

1. **Sequencing layer.** Client requests arrive at sequencers, which batch them into epochs and replicate each batch deterministically (every replica sees the same batch in the same order).
2. **Scheduling layer.** A deterministic scheduler maps each transaction in the batch to the partitions holding its read/write sets. Because the batch order is globally agreed, every replica's scheduler makes identical decisions without further coordination.
3. **Execution layer.** Transactions execute against local partitions in batch order; any transaction whose inputs depend on a remote partition's writes waits for the deterministic data flow to deliver them.

Since all replicas execute the same transactions in the same order, they reach identical states — replication *is* serializability. There is no lock manager, no waits-for graph, no deadlock: the conflict graph never needs to be built because the serial order is the input order. Calvin's reported results showed near-linear scaling on partitioned workloads (TPC-C) with throughput an order of magnitude above 2PC/2PL baselines on contended multi-partition transactions [4].

FaunaDB adopts this lineage directly: a Calvin-style deterministic log with temporally versioned data, where log order defines truth and temporal queries read as-of any position — one shared log serializing all tenants without per-tenant lock state.

The deterministic contract has a sharp edge: the read/write set of a transaction must be known (or safely over-approximated) before execution so the scheduler can route it. Calvin handles this with *reconnaissance* — a speculative pre-execution pass — and deterministically aborts transactions whose sets were mispredicted. Determinism thus trades lock overhead for *predictability* overhead.

### 4.5 TrueTime and Externally Consistent Commit in Spanner

Spanner takes the opposite route to the same destination: it keeps non-deterministic execution (2PL on writes, lock-free snapshot reads) but imposes a **globally meaningful order** on commits using TrueTime [3]. TrueTime exposes time as an interval: `TT.now()` returns `[earliest, latest]` with `earliest ≤ t_abs ≤ latest`, where the uncertainty *ε* (typically a few milliseconds) derives from GPS and atomic clocks with Marzullo-style bound tightening.

External consistency requires: *if T₂ starts after T₁ commits (in absolute time), then timestamp(T₂) > timestamp(T₁)*. Spanner's read-write transactions achieve this with two rules and one wait:

- **Start rule:** the coordinator assigns commit timestamp *sᵢ ≥ TT.now().latest* computed after the client's commit request arrives.
- **Commit wait:** the coordinator delays making *Tᵢ*'s writes visible until `TT.after(sᵢ)` — i.e., until *sᵢ* is guaranteed in the past.

> **Theorem (Spanner external consistency):** Let *t_abs(e)* be absolute time of event *e*. If *t_abs(e₁^commit) < t_abs(e₂^start)*, then *s₁ < s₂*. *Proof sketch:* commit wait gives *s₁ < t_abs(e₁^commit)*; causality gives *t_abs(e₁^commit) < t_abs(e₂^start) ≤ t_abs(e₂^server)*; the start rule gives *t_abs(e₂^server) ≤ s₂*. Transitivity yields *s₁ < s₂* [3].

Commit wait costs ~2*ε* of latency per write transaction — the price of external consistency — while read-only transactions at a timestamp *t ≤ t_safe* proceed lock-free across replicas, since multiversion data makes the past immutable [3].

Determinism reappears in Spanner's read path: snapshot reads at a fixed timestamp are deterministic functions of committed state, and Paxos-replicated writes give each tablet a deterministic log. Spanner is therefore a hybrid — deterministic ordering *within* each Paxos group, TrueTime ordering *across* groups.

## 5 Empirical Results

This section presents *worked verifications* — schedules and latency budgets checkable by hand — alongside an architectural trade-off summary.

### 5.1 Anomaly Verification Matrix

The following table classifies representative histories under four isolation regimes, using Adya's phenomena [5]. ✓ means the anomaly is admitted; ✗ means prevented.

| History | Read Committed | Snapshot Isolation | SSI / Serial | Deterministic (Calvin) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| G0: write cycle (lost update) | ✓ | ✗ (first-committer-wins) | ✗ | ✗ |
| G1c: circular information flow | ✓ | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ |
| G2-item: write skew (§4.3) | ✓ | ✓ admitted | ✗ (dangerous-structure abort) | ✗ (batch order is serial) |
| G2-predicate: phantom variant | ✓ | ✓ admitted | ✗ | ✗ |
| G-single: read skew | ✓ | ✗ (snapshot reads) | ✗ | ✗ |

The key row is G2-item: snapshot isolation is the *only* mainstream level that admits write skew, which is why applications on SI databases must either accept the anomaly, promote reads to writes [8], or upgrade to SSI/serializable.

### 5.2 Commit-Wait Latency Budget (Spanner)

With typical TrueTime uncertainty *ε ≈ 2–7 ms*, commit wait adds approximately *2ε* before a write's effects are visible:

| Component | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| `TT.now().latest` sample at coordinator | ~ε uncertainty |
| 2PC prepare across Paxos groups | 1–2 WAN RTTs |
| Commit wait (`TT.after(sᵢ)`) | ~2ε ≈ 4–14 ms |
| Paxos log append per group | 1 WAN RTT |

The striking result of the Spanner paper is that *2ε* dominates only for single-datacenter commits; for geo-distributed transactions, Paxos and 2PC round trips dominate, making external consistency nearly free at planetary scale [3]. This inverted the conventional wisdom that stronger semantics must cost more precisely where scale is largest.

### 5.3 Deterministic Throughput Reasoning (Calvin)

Calvin's batching amortizes sequencing cost: one consensus round orders *thousands* of transactions, so per-transaction coordination tends toward zero as batch size grows. The paper's TPC-C evaluation showed deterministic scheduling sustaining high throughput under contention where 2PL/2PC collapsed into deadlock storms and timeout cascades [4]. The failure mode shifts accordingly: when a batch contains transactions with mispredicted read/write sets, reconnaissance reruns them deterministically — wasted work, but never incorrect state. The system degrades in *throughput*, never in *correctness*, which is the defining reliability property of the deterministic approach.

## 6 Limitations

The deterministic program is powerful but not universal.

- **Read/write set predictability.** Calvin-style scheduling requires knowing data access sets before execution. Workloads with data-dependent access patterns (graph traversals, secondary-index lookups on unindexed predicates) force conservative over-approximation or reconnaissance retries, eroding the coordination savings [4].
- **Single sequencer bottleneck and latency.** Agreeing on a global order adds a sequencing step to the critical path. Deterministic systems optimize throughput-per-coordination-round but can lose on single-transaction latency to a local 2PL system that never leaves the node.
- **TrueTime's trust assumptions.** Spanner's proof assumes bounded clock uncertainty; a faulty clock that violates its advertised *ε* can break external consistency silently. Google's mitigation is hardware redundancy and continuous *ε* monitoring, but the guarantee is ultimately physical, not logical [3].
- **SSI's false aborts.** Dangerous-structure detection is conservative: some aborted transactions were in fact serializable. Under specific contention patterns SSI's abort rate can exceed the anomalies it prevents, pushing practitioners back toward hand-analysis à la Fekete et al. [8].
- **NP-completeness at the boundary.** Papadimitriou's result [2] still bites: no scheduler can efficiently enforce *view* serializability in general, so every practical system — deterministic or not — enforces a stronger, checkable condition and leaves some serializable schedules unexecutable.

## 7 Conclusion

Serializability theory began with a graph and a theorem: a schedule is correct exactly when its serialization graph is acyclic [1][2]. Classical schedulers — 2PL, timestamp ordering, optimistic validation — all monitor that graph at runtime, paying for correctness with locks, aborts, or deadlock detection. Snapshot isolation's popularity exposed the limits of the classical anomaly vocabulary, and Adya's dependency-graph framework [5] gave the field a precise language for what SI admits: G2-item cycles, of which write skew is the canonical instance.

Deterministic concurrency control is the contemporary answer to a fifty-year-old problem. Calvin decides the serial order before execution and replicates the *decision* rather than the *data flow* [4]; Spanner uses TrueTime to stamp a globally consistent order onto non-deterministic execution and waits out clock uncertainty to guarantee external consistency [3]; FaunaDB productizes the deterministic log as a multi-tenant service. In each case, the serialization graph is not detected — it is *prescribed*. The correctness argument moves from "no cycle will form" to "the order is already total," which is checkable by construction rather than by monitoring.

We began by asking which interleavings are acceptable, built elaborate machinery to police them dynamically, and finally realized that agreeing on the order up front is simpler than verifying it afterward. Determinism remains hard — reconnaissance, sequencing latency, and clock trust persist — but it is *accountable*: an explicitly decided order always leaves a log to audit, a batch to replay, and a timestamp to blame.
