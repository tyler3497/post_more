# Quantum LDPC Codes: Tanner Constructions, Expansion, and Single-Shot Decoding Thresholds

## Abstract

Quantum low-density parity-check (qLDPC) codes have moved, in only a few years, from a decades-old open problem to the most credible route toward low-overhead fault-tolerant quantum computation. This thesis develops the modern theory of qLDPC codes from first principles: the Calderbank-Shor-Steane (CSS) framework and its reformulation as a chain complex; the hypergraph product of Tillich and Zemor and the lifted product of Panteleev and Kalachev that finally broke the square-root distance barrier; the expander-based quantum Tanner codes of Leverrier and Zemor built on left-right Cayley complexes; the soundness properties that make single-shot decoding possible despite faulty measurements; and the practical decoder stack anchored by belief propagation with ordered-statistics post-processing. We state the key theorems on asymptotic goodness and linear-time decodability, survey threshold estimates under phenomenological and circuit-level noise, compare encoding overhead against surface codes, and close with the open problems that separate asymptotically good codes from deployable fault tolerance.

## 1 Intro

The central engineering question of quantum error correction is overhead: how many physical qubits must be spent to protect one logical qubit for the duration of a useful computation? The surface code, the workhorse of the field, answers with a quadratic tax. A distance-d patch consumes on the order of d squared physical qubits per logical qubit, so pushing logical error rates toward the levels demanded by factoring-scale circuits drives d into the several tens and the qubit bill into the millions. The community therefore set itself a sharper target: code families with constant encoding rate and distance growing linearly in the block length n, the quantum analogue of classical asymptotically good codes [10].

For more than a decade the best available qLDPC families fell far short. The hypergraph product of Tillich and Zemor gave constant rate but distance only of order square root of n [10], and for years that stood as the benchmark. Whether quantum LDPC codes could achieve constant rate and linear distance simultaneously was widely regarded as one of the central open questions of quantum coding theory.

The dam broke between 2020 and 2022. Panteleev and Kalachev introduced the lifted product, a group-algebra refinement of the hypergraph product, and proved the existence of asymptotically good qLDPC families [3][10]. Within months, Dinur, Hsieh, Lin, and Vidick constructed locally testable codes on the left-right Cayley complex [10], and Leverrier and Zemor translated that geometry into quantum Tanner codes: a CSS construction obtained by placing classical Tanner codes on the two graphs of a two-dimensional expanding complex, with a transparent proof of linear distance [1]. Suddenly the field had three routes to good qLDPC codes, all sharing the same DNA of expansion and local constraints.

Good parameters are necessary but not sufficient. A code is only as useful as its decoder, and the quantum setting is harsher than the classical one: stabilizer measurements are themselves noisy, so syndrome extraction must normally be repeated many times, eroding the advantage of a good code unless the code admits single-shot decoding, correction from a single noisy round of measurements [4][5]. The final piece of the modern picture is therefore algorithmic: soundness, a geometric property of the chain complex that lets a decoder disentangle data errors from measurement errors, and the decoder stack, belief propagation hardened by ordered-statistics post-processing, that turns theory into simulated thresholds [6][9].

## 2 Background

**CSS codes.** A CSS code is defined by two classical binary linear codes, one handling bit flips and one handling phase flips, whose parity-check matrices satisfy an orthogonality constraint. Let H_X be an r_X by n matrix and H_Z an r_Z by n matrix over the binary field. The rows of H_X define X-type stabilizer generators and the rows of H_Z define Z-type generators. For all stabilizers to commute, every X generator must commute with every Z generator, which is exactly the condition that H_X multiplied by the transpose of H_Z vanishes [1][10]. The code is LDPC when both check matrices are sparse: every row and column carries only a bounded number of ones, independently of n.

**Chain complexes.** The orthogonality condition has a clean homological reading. A three-term chain complex is a sequence of vector spaces and linear maps C_2 -> C_1 -> C_0 whose consecutive composition is zero. Identify C_1 with the physical qubits, C_2 with X-type checks, and C_0 with Z-type checks; then the vanishing composition is exactly H_X H_Z^T = 0. Logical Z operators become cycles modulo boundaries, the first homology group, and distance is the systole: the weight of the shortest homologically nontrivial cycle. This dictionary turns code design into complex design [3][4].

**Classical LDPC and expansion.** A classical LDPC code is presented by its Tanner graph: variable nodes for bits, check nodes for parity constraints, edges for incidence. Iterative decoders thrive when the Tanner graph has large girth and good expansion, meaning small sets of variable nodes touch disproportionately many check nodes. Sipser and Spielman made this quantitative: a Delta-regular expander graph, with a small local code of length Delta at each vertex, yields classical Tanner codes that are asymptotically good and efficiently decodable [1]. The quantum constructions lift this template into two dimensions.

**Noise models.** Three levels of realism structure the analysis. In the code-capacity model, syndrome extraction is perfect and only data qubits suffer errors. The phenomenological model adds independent faults to the measured syndrome bits. The circuit-level model injects faults at every gate and measurement of the extraction circuit; it is the model that determines whether a code can run on hardware [6][8]. Thresholds degrade markedly as realism increases, which is why single-shot decoding matters so much.

**What "good" means.** A family of [[n, k, d]] codes is asymptotically good when the rate k/n and the relative distance d/n are both bounded away from zero as n grows, with check weights bounded independently of n. The hypergraph product achieved constant rate with distance of order sqrt(n) [10]; the breakthrough constructions achieved all three requirements at once [1][3][10].

## 3 Methodology

**Hypergraph product.** Begin with two classical codes C_1 = [n_1, k_1, d_1] and C_2 = [n_2, k_2, d_2] with check matrices H_1 and H_2. The hypergraph product is the CSS code defined by

H_X = [H_1 otimes I , I otimes H_2^T],  H_Z = [I otimes H_2 , H_1^T otimes I],

with identity blocks of matching dimension. Qubits occupy the n_1 n_2 positions of the first block together with the r_1 r_2 positions of the second; the tensor structure makes H_X H_Z^T vanish term by term. The parameters are [[n_1 n_2 + r_1 r_2, k_1 k_2, min(d_1, d_2)]], so choosing both classical ingredients to be good LDPC expander codes yields a quantum LDPC family of constant rate and distance Theta(sqrt(n)) [10]. The construction is completely explicit, which explains its long reign as the benchmark:

```python
import numpy as np

def hypergraph_product(H1, H2):
    # CSS check matrices of the Tillich-Zemor hypergraph product.
    r1, n1 = H1.shape
    r2, n2 = H2.shape
    I_n1, I_n2 = np.eye(n1, dtype=int), np.eye(n2, dtype=int)
    I_r1, I_r2 = np.eye(r1, dtype=int), np.eye(r2, dtype=int)
    Hx = np.block([np.kron(H1, I_n2), np.kron(I_r1, H2.T)])
    Hz = np.block([np.kron(I_n1, H2), np.kron(H1.T, I_r2)])
    assert not np.any((Hx @ Hz.T) % 2)  # CSS commutativity
    return Hx % 2, Hz % 2
```

**Lifted product.** Panteleev and Kalachev observed that the Kronecker product is needlessly rigid. Replacing binary matrices by matrices over a group algebra and then lifting through a cyclic group action produces quasi-cyclic codes with dramatically better distance. The lifted product of an expander-based classical LDPC code with a repetition code already reaches distance Omega(N / log N), and the full two-expander version reaches linear distance: the first asymptotically good qLDPC family [3][10]. The codes are quasi-cyclic, a symmetry that also organizes their decoding: Golowich and Guruswami decode them by reduction to decoding classical expander LDPC codes under noisy syndromes, correcting Theta(N / log N) adversarial errors [3].

**Quantum Tanner codes.** Leverrier and Zemor simplified the geometry. Start from the left-right Cayley complex of a group with two symmetric generating sets A and B: vertices are group elements, squares correspond to pairs (a, b), and two Cayley graphs share the same edge set. Place qubits on the squares, define X checks as the Tanner code of the first graph with local code C_A otimes C_B, and Z checks as the Tanner code of the second graph with local code C_A^perp otimes C_B^perp. Local orthogonality implies global commutativity. With Ramanujan Cayley graphs and suitable local codes, the global code has linear rate and linear distance, with cleaner estimates than the original lifted product [1].

**Soundness and metachecks.** A CSS code is (t, f)-sound when every sufficiently light syndrome is explained by a light error [4]. Soundness is enforced by metachecks, linear dependencies among the stabilizers themselves, which let the decoder test the syndrome for internal consistency. A three-dimensional homological product equips a two-dimensional code with an LDPC metacode, and that redundancy is what makes single-shot decoding possible [4][12].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Chain complexes as the native language

The chain-complex viewpoint does more than restate commutativity; it classifies constructions. The surface code is the cellular chain complex of a tiled surface. The hypergraph product is the tensor product of two two-term complexes. The lifted product is a complex of modules over a group ring. Quantum Tanner codes are Tanner sheaves on a square complex, glued by expansion. In every case, *distance is homological*: the X distance is the smallest weight of a cycle that is not a boundary, and the Z distance its cohomological dual. This unity explains why a single decoder idea, flipping small sets to reduce a potential function, ports across families: the geometry that guarantees distance is the same geometry the decoder exploits [1][7].

### 4.2 Products, lifts, and finite-length design

Asymptotics hide a practical problem: the known good families need enormous block lengths before their guarantees engage. Finite-length design therefore studies the Tanner graph directly. For lifted-product and quasi-cyclic generalized hypergraph-product codes, Abiad and Castriota developed a spectral framework for the two-block tensor structure of the checks: a Fourier block-diagonalization reducing cycle counting to small Hermitian blocks, yielding closed-form four-cycle counts, a Sidon-type characterization of girth six, and expander-mixing lower bounds on stopping sets [9]. Complementary work constructs high-girth instances explicitly: Okada and Kasai give checkable conditions for regularity and short-cycle exclusion in square-base hypergraph products, exhibit (3,6)-regular codes of Tanner girth eight, and prove via the voltage-sum criterion that orthogonality-forced eight-cycles cap what circulant lifts can achieve [2]. Their [[28800, 62]] code, distance bounded between 18 and 192, shows that finite-length engineering already produces codes with formidable simulated performance [2].

### 4.3 Expander-based quantum Tanner codes

The engine of the Leverrier-Zemor construction is the interplay between local and global. Locally, the tensor product codes correct a constant fraction of errors on each vertex neighborhood; globally, Ramanujan expansion ensures that any low-weight error has a vertex where its local view is both small and far from the local code, so a greedy flip strictly reduces a potential function. The proof converts local testability, the Dinur et al. breakthrough on the same complex, directly into linear distance [1]. Compared with the Panteleev-Kalachev construction, the argument is shorter and the distance bounds cleaner, which is why the recent linear-time decoder for these codes, alternating sequential and parallel flip phases, could be proved to correct errors up to a constant fraction of the block length [7].

### 4.4 Single-shot decoding and soundness

Single-shot decoding is the requirement that one noisy round of syndrome measurement suffices: the decoder receives a noisy syndrome and must return a correction with small residual error, without the many repeated rounds standard fault tolerance demands. The enabling property is linear soundness: any syndrome of weight below t admits an explaining error of weight at most a constant multiple of the syndrome weight [4][12]. Soundness lets the decoder first clean the syndrome using the metacode, converting a local-stochastic syndrome error into a local-stochastic residual data error; local-stochastic noise covers the short-range correlations of constant-depth circuit noise, and at low enough physical rates errors form small disconnected clusters that a minimum-weight decoder resolves independently [12].

The flagship result is that quantum Tanner codes, which are naturally sound, admit a single-shot threshold: below certain data and measurement error rates (p*, q*), exponentially many rounds of single-shot correction succeed with high probability [4]. An alternative route embeds a two-dimensional code in a three-dimensional homological product to manufacture an LDPC metacode, performs single-shot logical state preparation there, and code-switches back, proving single-shot universality for codes with small-set linear soundness [12].

### 4.5 Decoders: from greedy flips to belief propagation

Three decoder philosophies compete. The small-set-flip decoder is greedy and provable: at each step it flips a small qubit set inside a single stabilizer support so as to reduce syndrome weight, and expansion guarantees progress until the syndrome vanishes; it corrects adversarial errors up to a constant fraction of the distance for hypergraph-product and quantum Tanner codes [7]. Belief propagation is fast and practical but fragile: quantum degeneracy means many errors share one syndrome, and short cycles and trapping sets stall convergence, so plain BP fails precisely where qLDPC codes live [8]. The standard remedy is ordered-statistics post-processing: when BP stalls, OSD selects the most reliable variable subset from BP's soft information, inverts the corresponding submatrix, and enumerates a list of syndrome-consistent candidates, returning the most likely. BP+OSD is the gold standard for accuracy, at the cost of O(n^3) worst-case Gaussian elimination [6][8].

Research attacks that cost from both sides. The ordered Tanner forest replaces matrix inversion with a forest-structured post-processor that avoids the cubic step under circuit-level noise [8]. Best-first OSD reorders the candidate search by decreasing likelihood and invokes OSD after a fixed small number of BP iterations rather than gating on convergence, matching BP+OSD accuracy on bivariate bicycle codes under full circuit-level noise with one hundredth of the query budget [6]. Beam-search decoders guided by BP report a seventeen-fold logical-error reduction over BP+OSD on the [[144, 12, 12]] bivariate bicycle code at physical error rate 10^-3 [11].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

> Theorem: (Asymptotic goodness.) There exist families of CSS LDPC codes with parameters [[n, Theta(n), Theta(n)]] and uniformly bounded check weights. Constructions include the lifted product of Panteleev and Kalachev and the quantum Tanner codes of Leverrier and Zemor [1][3][10].

> Theorem: (Linear-time decodability.) The quantum Tanner code construction admits an efficiently decodable subfamily: a linear-time decoder corrects all errors of weight up to a constant fraction of the block length, hence up to Theta(n) [7].

> Theorem: (Single-shot threshold.) Quantum Tanner codes admit a single-shot threshold: there exist constants (p*, q*) such that for local-stochastic data noise below p* and measurement noise below q*, exponentially many rounds of single-shot quantum error correction succeed with high probability [4].

The numerical record corroborates the theory. The high-girth [[28800, 62]] (3,6)-regular hypergraph-product code of Okada and Kasai, distance proven between 18 and 192, produced zero decoding failures in 2.993 x 10^8 trials at depolarizing probability p = 0.1402 under degeneracy-aware belief propagation with ordered-statistics-lite post-processing, a Wilson 95 percent upper confidence bound of 1.28 x 10^-8 [2]. Breuckmann and Londe's hyperbolic codes show threshold behavior near four percent under phenomenological noise with syndrome faults, decoded single-shot [5].

The overhead comparison is the point of the whole enterprise. A surface-code quantum memory aiming at logical error rates near 10^-12 needs distance in the mid-twenties, hence on the order of a thousand physical qubits per logical qubit. A constant-rate qLDPC code needs only a constant multiple of physical qubits per logical qubit in principle [7][8]. The gap between principle and practice is the decoder: surface-code matching decoders run in near-linear time with thresholds near one percent under circuit noise, while qLDPC decoders with comparable speed and proven thresholds are still being built [6][8].

## 6 Limitations

**Constants and block lengths.** Asymptotic goodness is a statement about infinity. The known constructions engage their guarantees at block lengths far beyond what any near-term device will field; the practical frontier is finite-length codes such as the [[144, 12, 12]] bivariate bicycle family, where thresholds are won by engineering rather than theorems [6][11].

**Thresholds under circuit-level noise.** Code-capacity thresholds flatter every code. Under full circuit-level noise, qLDPC thresholds are substantially lower and far less mapped than surface-code thresholds; several candidate decoders have only recently been benchmarked there at all [6][8].

**Decoder latency.** Fault tolerance is real-time: the decoder must keep pace with syndrome extraction. BP+OSD's O(n^3) worst case is a poor fit for megahertz syndrome rates, and while ordered Tanner forests, parallel BP, and beam search narrow the gap, none yet combines proven accuracy, bounded worst-case latency, and hardware-friendly parallelism [8][11].

**Connectivity.** Good qLDPC codes cannot be geometrically local; bounded-weight checks on an expander graph inherently demand long-range couplers. Any architecture betting on qLDPC must supply non-local connectivity, and the routing cost of that connectivity appears in no threshold plot yet published.

**Measurement overhead of soundness.** Single-shot decoding is not free: metachecks are extra stabilizer measurements, and the redundancy they provide costs ancillas and time. Soundness has been proved for specific families, and extending single-shot guarantees to every good code, with tight constants, remains open [4][12].

**Beyond memory.** Cheap Clifford memory does not by itself yield universal computation. Magic-state distillation, the standard route to non-Clifford gates, carries its own overhead that may dominate the savings from qLDPC memory.

## 7 Conclusion

Quantum LDPC codes have traveled from the Tillich-Zemor square-root barrier to three independent asymptotically good constructions in about two years, and from purely existential results to decoders with proven linear-time guarantees and single-shot thresholds [1][3][4][7][10].

What remains is the translation from theorems to machines:

1. Explicit small good codes with proven, not merely simulated, parameters at block lengths below ten thousand qubits.
2. Decoders that are simultaneously fast enough for real-time operation, accurate under full circuit-level noise, and simple enough to implement in hardware [8][11].
3. Tight threshold estimates for quantum Tanner and lifted-product codes under circuit-level noise [6].
4. Architectures whose connectivity matches the non-locality that good codes provably require.
5. A fault-tolerant universal gate set, including magic-state distillation, whose overhead does not erase the memory savings.

The surface code won its position through a rare combination of simplicity, local layout, and a fast decoder. Quantum LDPC codes now offer something the surface code cannot: constant overhead. Whether that asymptotic promise survives contact with decoders, measurements, and wiring remains the open question.
