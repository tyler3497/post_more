# Lattice-Based Post-Quantum Cryptography: The Geometry of Numbers, Module-LWE, and the ML-KEM Standard

## Abstract

Lattice-based cryptography has emerged as the dominant paradigm for post-quantum public-key cryptography, underpinning the NIST standards ML-KEM (FIPS 203) and ML-DSA (FIPS 204). This thesis develops the mathematical foundations, hardness assumptions, and standardization history of lattice cryptography, from Minkowski's geometry of numbers through worst-case to average-case reductions for Learning With Errors (LWE) and its ring and module variants. We give a detailed account of the CRYSTALS-Kyber KEM selected as ML-KEM: its Module-LWE construction, Fujisaki–Okamoto transform to IND-CCA2 security, and parameter sets targeting NIST levels 1, 3, and 5. In parallel, we trace NTRU — the oldest lattice cryptosystem — from its 1998 ad-hoc ring formulation to NIST finalist status, and analyze the subfield attacks constraining overstretched parameters. Concrete security estimation via BKZ block-size methodology, implementation benchmarks, and the limitations of current reductions, algebraic structure, and side-channel exposure are systematically evaluated. We close with open problems in tight classical reductions and post-quantum migration.

---

## 1 Introduction

Public-key cryptography as deployed today rests on two computational problems: integer factorization and the discrete logarithm problem, in prime fields and on elliptic curves. Shor's polynomial-time quantum algorithm for both [10] implies that a cryptographically relevant quantum computer would collapse RSA, Diffie–Hellman, DSA, and elliptic-curve cryptography simultaneously. Adversaries can *harvest now and decrypt later*, making migration urgent for long-lived data.

In 2016, NIST launched a public standardization process for post-quantum cryptography [12], soliciting key-encapsulation mechanisms and signatures secure against quantum adversaries. After three rounds of cryptanalysis, NIST selected a portfolio dominated by structured lattices: **ML-KEM** (the CRYSTALS-Kyber KEM, FIPS 203 [5]), **ML-DSA** (CRYSTALS-Dilithium, FIPS 204), and **SLH-DSA** (SPHINCS+, FIPS 205), . NTRU [6] — the oldest lattice cryptosystem, dating to 1998 — survived to the final round as the conservative alternative.

Three properties explain why lattices won:

- *Worst-case to average-case reductions.* Breaking random lattice instances is as hard as approximating canonical lattice problems in the worst case [1][3] — a foundation no other PQC family matches.
- *Efficiency through structure.* Ring and module variants exploit the Number Theoretic Transform over cyclotomic rings, achieving performance competitive with elliptic-curve cryptography.
- *Versatility.* Lattices support KEMs, signatures, fully homomorphic encryption, and zero-knowledge proofs from one assumption family [7][9].

What follows: background, methodology, the deep dive, concrete estimates and proofs, limitations, and conclusions.

## 2 Background

A *lattice* L in R^n is the set of all integer linear combinations of linearly independent vectors b_1, ..., b_m, called a *basis*. The integer m is the *rank* (m = n means full-rank). The *determinant* det(L) — the volume of the fundamental parallelepiped — is basis-independent. The *successive minima* lambda_1(L) <= ... <= lambda_n(L) give the smallest radius whose closed ball contains i independent lattice vectors.

The *geometry of numbers* studies lattice points in convex bodies:

> Theorem (Minkowski's First Theorem): For any full-rank lattice L in R^n, lambda_1(L) <= sqrt(n) · det(L)^{1/n}.

This underlies the *Gaussian heuristic*, predicting lambda_1(L) near sqrt(n / (2 pi e)) · det(L)^{1/n} for random lattices.

Cryptography relies on the presumed hardness of these problems, parameterized by an approximation factor gamma >= 1:

| Problem | Informal definition | Cryptographic role |
|---|---|---|
| SVP_gamma | Find a nonzero vector of length at most gamma · lambda_1 | Worst-case foundation |
| CVP_gamma | Find the lattice vector nearest a target, within factor gamma | Decoding; LWE duality |
| SIVP_gamma | Find n independent vectors each of length at most gamma · lambda_n | Regev's reduction target |
| GapSVP_gamma | Decide whether lambda_1 <= 1 or lambda_1 > gamma | Decision variant for reductions |
| BDD_alpha | Decode a target within distance alpha · lambda_1 | Bounded-distance decoding; LWE errors |

Ajtai (1996) gave the first *worst-case to average-case reduction*, from worst-case approximate SVP to random SIS instances. Regev [1] then introduced *Learning With Errors (LWE)*:

> Definition (LWE): Fix integers n, m, q and a noise distribution chi over Z_q. Given samples (a_i, b_i) where each a_i is uniform in Z_q^n and b_i = <a_i, s> + e_i for uniform secret s and noise e_i from chi, distinguish these from uniform pairs — or recover s.

Regev proved average-case LWE is as hard as worst-case SIVP and GapSVP via a *quantum* reduction [1]; Peikert later gave a *classical* reduction [7]. Concretely:

```python
import secrets

def sample_lwe(n, m, q, sigma):
    rng = secrets.SystemRandom()
    s = [rng.randrange(q) for _ in range(n)]
    samples = []
    for _ in range(m):
        a = [rng.randrange(q) for _ in range(n)]
        e = round(rng.gauss(0.0, sigma)) % q
        b = (sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, s)) + e) % q
        samples.append((a, b))
    return s, samples
```

Two algebraic refinements improved efficiency. *Ring-LWE* [2] replaces Z_q^n with R_q = Z_q[x]/(x^n + 1): keys shrink to quasi-linear size and multiplication runs in O(n log n) via the NTT. *Module-LWE* [3] interpolates between the two — secrets are vectors of module rank k over R_q — recovering strong worst-case guarantees (Langlois–Stehlé [3]) with ring-like efficiency. Kyber, and hence ML-KEM, is built on Module-LWE.

Also dating to 1998 is *NTRU* [6], the first practical lattice cryptosystem, whose modern KEM variants (NTRU-HPS, NTRU-HRSS) reached the NIST final round.

Concrete cryptanalysis uses *lattice reduction* — LLL and BKZ [8] — with security expressed through the smallest block size beta defeating a parameter set, converted to bit-operations via sieving cost models.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is an analytical survey: rather than a new construction, it develops a uniform framework for evaluating lattice schemes and applies it to ML-KEM and NTRU. The methodology has four components.

1. *Reduction analysis.* For each scheme we trace the chain from its computational assumption to a worst-case lattice problem, recording whether the reduction is classical or quantum, the approximation factor, and the tightness loss. Tight classical reductions inspire the most confidence [1][7].
2. *Concrete hardness estimation.* Asymptotic reductions do not select parameters. We apply the standard BKZ methodology: model primal and dual lattice attacks, compute the minimal block size beta, and convert beta to classical and quantum bit-security via sieving cost models (roughly 2^{0.292 beta} classical operations).
3. *Constructional anatomy.* We dissect ML-KEM [5]: the Module-LWE encryption core, the Fujisaki–Okamoto (FO) transform lifting IND-CPA encryption to an IND-CCA2 KEM, centered-binomial noise, ciphertext compression, and NTT arithmetic.
4. *Empirical grounding.* We compile published benchmarks (sizes, cycle counts on AVX2 and Cortex-M4) and cryptanalytic milestones (subfield attacks on overstretched NTRU [11]) to test theory against practice.

The FO transform is ML-KEM's linchpin for CCA security. The modular analysis of Hofheinz, Hövelmanns, and Kiltz [13] shows that an IND-CPA encryption scheme with suitable correctness yields an IND-CCA KEM in the (quantum) random oracle model via deterministic re-encryption and implicit rejection. ML-KEM instantiates this pattern directly.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Geometry of Numbers: SVP, CVP, and Minkowski Bounds

Primal lattice attacks on LWE embed the samples into a lattice (Kannan's embedding) of known determinant; BKZ with block size beta succeeds heuristically when it finds vectors near the Gaussian length. This yields the standard pipeline: from LWE parameters compute the minimal beta plausibly recovering the secret [4][5].

These bounds are *existential*, not algorithmic: finding the guaranteed short vectors is the presumed-hard task. Sieving (about 2^{0.292 n}) and enumeration define the concrete cost model of Section 5's parameters. Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization, the engine inside BKZ's block processing, is illustrative:

```haskell
-- Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization of a lattice basis (exact rational arithmetic)
type Vec = [Rational]

dot :: Vec -> Vec -> Rational
dot a b = sum (zipWith (*) a b)

gramSchmidt :: [Vec] -> [Vec]
gramSchmidt = foldl step []
  where
    step orth b =
      let coeffs = [dot b v / dot v v | v <- orth]
          proj   = foldl (\w (c, v) -> zipWith (-) w (map (* c) v)) b (zip coeffs orth)
      in orth ++ [proj]

```

### 4.2 LWE and Its Algebraic Variants: From Regev to Module-LWE

Regev's LWE [1] is deliberately *unstructured*: A is uniform over Z_q^{m × n}, and hardness follows from worst-case lattice problems via a reduction whose only non-classical ingredient is quantum discrete-Gaussian sampling. It supports encryption, fully homomorphic encryption, and more [7].

The price is quadratic key size. *Ring-LWE* [2] quotients by a cyclotomic ring R_q, so one ring element encodes n coefficients and multiplication is an NTT-accelerated convolution. Security now rests on *ideal lattices* — lattices that are also ring ideals — and [2] reduces Ring-LWE to worst-case problems on ideal lattices, a potentially easier class than general lattices. No attack on cyclotomic Ring-LWE is known, but the assumption is strictly stronger.

*Module-LWE* [3] splits the difference. With module rank k over R_q, the secret is a vector of k ring elements; fixing ring degree 256 and varying k tunes security in fine steps while keeping NTT-friendly arithmetic. Langlois and Stehlé's worst-case to average-case reductions for module lattices [3] give Kyber a foundation nearly as strong as plain LWE with efficiency near Ring-LWE — which is why both NIST lattice standards, ML-KEM and ML-DSA, are module-based.

Structure *can* be exploited when overstretched: subfield attacks on NTRU [11] show the ring must be chosen conservatively — power-of-two cyclotomics, as in ML-KEM.

### 4.3 ML-KEM (Kyber): Construction, Parameters, and the FIPS 203 Standard

ML-KEM is the standardized form of CRYSTALS-Kyber [4][5], a Module-LWE KEM layering three ideas: CPA-secure Module-LWE encryption, the Fujisaki–Okamoto transform to CCA security [13], and aggressive engineering (NTT arithmetic, binomial noise, compression).

*Key generation.* Derive random A in R_q^{k × k} from a seed; sample s, e from centered binomials. Public key: (seed, t = A·s + e); secret key: s.

*Encapsulation.* From message m and fresh randomness compute u = A^T·r + e_1 and v = t^T·r + e_2 + encode(m), compress, and derive K by hashing (m, ciphertext).

*Decapsulation.* Recover m with s, *re-encrypt* deterministically, and compare; on mismatch output a pseudorandom key (*implicit rejection*) [5][13].

```rust
// Schematic ML-KEM encapsulation (NTT and compression elided)
struct PublicKey { seed_a: [u8; 32], t: PolyVec }   // t = A·s + e

fn encaps(pk: &PublicKey, rng: &mut impl Rng) -> (Ciphertext, SharedSecret) {
    let m: [u8; 32] = rng.gen();
    let (k_bar, coins) = g(&m, &hash_pk(pk));
    let ct = pke_encrypt(pk, &m, &coins);
    let k = kdf(&k_bar, &hash_ct(&ct));
    (ct, k)
}
```

Parameters are fixed for three levels [4][5]:

| Set | k | n | q | eta_1 | eta_2 | d_u | d_v | pk (B) | sk (B) | ct (B) | Level |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-KEM-512 | 2 | 256 | 3329 | 3 | 2 | 10 | 4 | 800 | 1632 | 768 | 1 |
| ML-KEM-768 | 3 | 256 | 3329 | 2 | 2 | 10 | 4 | 1184 | 2400 | 1088 | 3 |
| ML-KEM-1024 | 4 | 256 | 3329 | 2 | 2 | 11 | 5 | 1568 | 3168 | 1568 | 5 |

The modulus q = 3329 = 13·256 + 1 admits a full NTT over Z_q, so every multiplication runs in quasi-linear time. Centered-binomial noise replaces discrete Gaussians: constant-time sampling aids side-channel resistance at modest tightness cost. NIST cited Kyber's balance of reduction pedigree [3], cryptanalytic scrutiny, and near-ECDH performance.

### 4.4 NTRU: From Ad-hoc Ring Cryptosystem to Standardized KEM

NTRU, proposed by Hoffstein, Pipher, and Silverman in 1998 [6], is the oldest lattice-based public-key cryptosystem and the only pre-NIST design to reach the standardization finals. Work in R = Z[x]/(x^N − 1); choose secret *ternary* polynomials f, g with few nonzero coefficients; publish h = g · f^{−1} mod q; encrypt m as r ⋆ h + m mod q with small random r; decrypt with f. No lattices appear in the 1998 paper — yet the pairs (u, v) with u ⋆ h = v mod q form a 2N-dimensional *NTRU lattice*, and breaking NTRU means finding its unusually short vector (f, g).

The original sets suffered decryption failures and ad-hoc padding; the NIST submissions — NTRU-HPS and NTRU-HRSS — restrict to prime-degree rings (n = 509, 677, 821 for HPS; n = 701 for HRSS), take q as a power of two, and apply provable padding with KEM transforms.

NTRU remains the conservative hedge: 28 years without a break of well-chosen parameters. The cautionary chapter is *overstretched NTRU*: with super-polynomial q, subfield lattice attacks [11] and later fatigue analyses break parameters naive estimates deemed safe — proof that structured-lattice security is parameter-sensitive in ways unstructured LWE is not.

### 4.5 Security Reductions and the Random Oracle Model in Standardization

A security *reduction* proves that breaking a scheme implies solving a presumed-hard problem. Lattice reductions are the field's crown jewel, but standardization exposed their limits.

First, *tightness.* Regev's LWE reduction [1] loses polynomial factors and uses a quantum step; Peikert's classical version [7] loses more. For ML-KEM the chain runs: IND-CCA2 of the KEM reduces via [13] to IND-CPA of the PKE, thence to decisional Module-LWE, thence via [3] to worst-case module-SIVP at approximation factors far beyond efficient algorithms. Yet the composed factor is loose enough that *concrete* security rests on BKZ estimates, not the reduction.

Second, *idealized models.* The FO analysis [13] lives in the random oracle model, with extra losses in the quantum ROM. FIPS 203 instantiates them with SHA3/SHAKE [5].

Third, *what reductions omit.* No reduction rules out a quantum algorithm for approximate SVP; quantum hardness of lattice problems is a *conjecture*, supported only by the failure of quantum speedups for sieving beyond Grover-like factors. Reductions also say nothing about side channels, faults, or misuse — the dominant real-world failure modes.

Reductions are *necessary but not sufficient*: NIST chose parameters by concrete analysis [5][12].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We ground the theory in numbers: security estimates, benchmarks, and proof sketches.

*Concrete security.* The Kyber team's BKZ analysis [4], reproduced by NIST, assigns these core-SVP hardness figures (rounded):

| Set | BKZ beta (approx.) | Classical bits | Quantum bits | NIST level |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-KEM-512 | ~400 | ~118 | ~107 | 1 (AES-128) |
| ML-KEM-768 | ~600 | ~183 | ~174 | 3 (AES-192) |
| ML-KEM-1024 | ~800 | ~256 | ~236 | 5 (AES-256) |

Breaking ML-KEM-768 costs roughly as much as brute-forcing AES-192.

*Scheme comparison* [5][6][12] (approximate sizes):

| Scheme | Assumption | Public key | Ciphertext | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ECDH P-256 | Discrete log | 64 B | 64 B | Broken by Shor [10] |
| RSA-2048 | Factoring | 256 B | 256 B | Broken by Shor [10] |
| ML-KEM-768 | Module-LWE | 1184 B | 1088 B | FIPS 203 standard |
| NTRU-HPS-677 | NTRU lattice | 930 B | 930 B | NIST finalist |
| FrodoKEM-640 | Plain LWE | 9616 B | 9720 B | Conservative, unstructured |

*Benchmarks.* AVX2 code runs all ML-KEM-768 operations in tens of thousands of cycles — near X25519 speeds [4][5].

*Proof sketch: Regev's reduction [1].* The reduction samples discrete Gaussians over lattice cosets via a *quantum* Fourier step, building LWE samples whose secret encodes the lattice — so a distinguisher reveals short vectors, solving GapSVP/SIVP. The quantum sampler is the only non-classical ingredient; Peikert [7] replaced it with classical sampling above the smoothing parameter at the cost of a larger modulus.

*Proof sketch: the FO transform [13].* Transform T derives encryption randomness by hashing the message; transform U derives the session key by hashing (message, ciphertext), with implicit rejection. Hofheinz et al. prove the KEM IND-CCA secure in the ROM [5][13]. KEM correctness itself is a one-line invariant:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE KemCorrectness ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals
CONSTANTS KeySpace
VARIABLES pk, sk, ct, kEnc, kDec

Init == pk \in KeySpace /\ sk \in KeySpace /\ ct = 0 /\ kEnc = 0 /\ kDec = 0

Encapsulate ==
    /\ \E k \in KeySpace, c \in Nat : ct' = c /\ kEnc' = k
    /\ UNCHANGED <<pk, sk, kDec>>

Decapsulate ==
    /\ kDec' = kEnc        \* correctness: decapsulation recovers the key
    /\ UNCHANGED <<pk, sk, ct, kEnc>>

Next == Encapsulate \/ Decapsulate
================================================================================
```

## 6 Limitations

Lattice cryptography's strengths are real, but its limitations must be stated plainly.

- *Quantum hardness is conjectural.* Every reduction terminates at a worst-case lattice problem whose *quantum* hardness is assumed, not proven. Quantum sieving currently offers at most quadratic speedups, but a 'Shor for SVP' would invalidate the whole family at once. Lattice security bets that no such algorithm exists.
- *Reduction looseness.* The composed reductions for ML-KEM reach approximation factors far beyond efficient algorithms, so the reduction gives *qualitative* confidence while parameters come from *concrete* BKZ estimates. The estimates, not the theorems, carry the weight.
- *Algebraic structure risk.* Module and ring variants assume ideal/module lattices are as hard as general ones. Overstretched-NTRU attacks [11] show that structure plus aggressive parameters fails catastrophically; ML-KEM's parameters are conservative (polynomial modulus, power-of-two cyclotomic), but the assumption remains stronger than plain LWE.
- *Implementation security.* The FO transform's re-encryption step is a side-channel magnet: timing and power traces of decapsulation have leaked Kyber secret keys from non-constant-time code. FIPS 203 [5] standardizes only the mathematical layer, leaving hardening to implementers.
- *Migration and agility.* Keys and ciphertexts an order of magnitude larger than ECC strain protocols such as TLS handshakes and embedded systems, while hybrid deployments double complexity. Harvest-now-decrypt-later [10] makes delay costly, but rushed migration risks flaws.

## 7 Conclusion

Lattice-based cryptography earned standardization through a rare combination: deep mathematics, plausible hardness foundations, and genuine engineering efficiency. The geometry of numbers supplies the hard problems; worst-case to average-case reductions [1][2][3] supply the confidence; Module-LWE supplies the performance; and the NIST process [12] supplied the adversarial scrutiny that turned Kyber into ML-KEM, FIPS 203 [5].

NTRU's parallel story [6][11] — an ad-hoc design that survived three decades of cryptanalysis — forced the community to develop the reductions and parameter discipline it lacked. Its finalist status — and the overstretched-parameter attacks bounding its ambitions — taught standardization as much as Kyber's victory.

Open problems remain. Tighter classical reductions would close the theory–practice gap. The *quantum* hardness of approximate SVP needs deeper study as quantum algorithms advance. And the engineering frontier — masked, constant-time, formally verified ML-KEM implementations — will decide whether the mathematics survives deployment. The lattice era has begun; making it deserve its confidence is the work of the next decade.
