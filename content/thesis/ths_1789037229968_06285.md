# From Cheeger's Inequality to Ramanujan Optimality: Spectral Expansion, the LPS Construction, and the Derandomizing Power of Expander Walks

## Abstract

This thesis develops the spectral theory of expander graphs from first principles to the frontier of explicit optimal constructions and their derandomization applications. We prove the discrete Cheeger inequality linking the second Laplacian eigenvalue λ₂ to the edge-isoperimetric constant *h*, and the Alon–Boppana bound 2√(d−1) capping the spectral gap of any infinite *d*-regular family. We present the Lubotzky–Phillips–Sarnak construction of Ramanujan graphs — (p+1)-regular Cayley graphs of PGL(2,𝔽_q) whose spectrum is controlled by the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture via Hecke operators — and verify it computationally on the 6-regular graph X^{5,13} (2,184 vertices), whose largest nontrivial eigenvalue 4.2497 lies below 2√5 ≈ 4.4721. We then trace the derandomization dividend: the zig-zag product, Reingold's proof that undirected connectivity is in deterministic log-space, and randomness-efficient error reduction by expander walks. Experiments confirm the theory: the Petersen graph is Ramanujan, random 3-regular graphs crowd the bound, and Cheeger's inequality holds with room to spare.

## 1 Introduction

Expander graphs are *sparse yet highly connected*: every sufficiently small vertex set has many edges leaving it. This single property makes them the discrete analogue of a paradox — a graph with only *O(n)* edges that behaves, for most purposes, like a dense random object. Their applications span codes, sorting networks, derandomization, cryptography, and pure mathematics [1].

There are two classical ways to certify expansion. The *combinatorial* route counts edges across cuts: the edge-isoperimetric (Cheeger) constant *h(G)* measures the worst bottleneck. The *spectral* route diagonalizes the Laplacian or adjacency matrix: the gap between the trivial eigenvalue and the rest controls expansion, random-walk mixing, and quasirandomness simultaneously. The bridge between them — the discrete Cheeger inequality of Alon and Milman [4] — is the load-bearing beam of the entire subject.

Section 2 reviews the spectral toolkit and construction history. Section 3 develops the methodology: Rayleigh quotients, Courant–Fischer, Cheeger's inequality, and the expander mixing lemma. Section 4 covers the Alon–Boppana bound, the LPS construction, interlacing families, and the derandomization payoff (zig-zag product, **SL = L**, expander-walk sampling). Section 5 verifies every claim computationally, including a from-scratch *X^{5,13}*. Section 6 discusses open problems.

## 2 Background and Related Work

Cheeger (1969) bounded a manifold's first Laplacian eigenvalue by its isoperimetric constant; Alon and Milman [4] gave the discrete translation, *h²/2d ≤ λ₂ ≤ 2h*, for *d*-regular graphs, where λ₂ is the second-smallest Laplacian eigenvalue and *h* the edge expansion. The spectral gap became a polynomial-time-computable proxy for an NP-hard quantity.

Early explicit expanders were group-theoretic: Margulis (1973, 1988) used Kazhdan's property (T), and Gabber and Galil gave an elementary ℤ_m-based construction with fully explicit neighbor functions. Pinsker had shown random regular graphs expand, but for derandomization *explicitness is the entire point* — a randomly chosen expander saves no randomness. Hoory, Linial, and Wigderson [1] organize the field into extremal bounds, typical behavior, explicit constructions, and algorithms, a taxonomy this thesis follows.

On the derandomization side, the landmarks are the Ajtai–Komlós–Szemerédi sorting network, Sipser–Spielman expander codes, the Reingold–Vadhan–Wigderson zig-zag product [7], Reingold's log-space connectivity algorithm [6], and Vadhan's monograph on pseudorandomness [8], which recasts expanders as pseudorandom objects alongside extractors and pseudorandom generators. The modern era added the Marcus–Spielman–Srivastava interlacing method [5], yielding bipartite Ramanujan graphs of *every* degree and resolving the Kadison–Singer problem with the same machinery.


## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is the *spectral toolkit*: we convert combinatorial questions about cuts, walks, and expansion into linear-algebraic questions about eigenvalues, then compute.

For a *d*-regular graph *G = (V, E)* with adjacency matrix *A*, the Laplacian is *L = dI − A*. Its eigenvalues satisfy *0 = μ₁ ≤ μ₂ ≤ … ≤ μₙ ≤ 2d*. The workhorse is the Rayleigh quotient together with the Courant–Fischer min–max principle:

> **Theorem (Courant–Fischer).** For the Laplacian *L*,
> μ₂ = min { xᵀLx / xᵀx : x ⊥ **1**, x ≠ 0 }, where xᵀLx = Σ_{(u,v)∈E} (x_u − x_v)².

The numerator penalizes variation across edges, so μ₂ is small exactly when the graph has a bottleneck. The edge-isoperimetric constant is

*h(G) = min { |E(S, S̄)| / |S| : ∅ ≠ S ⊆ V, |S| ≤ n/2 }*.

The two are locked together by the discrete Cheeger inequality [4]:

> **Theorem (Discrete Cheeger inequality).** For *d*-regular *G*, *h²/(2d) ≤ μ₂ ≤ 2h*.

*Proof sketch.* For the upper bound, use the test vector *f = |S̄|* on *S*, *−|S|* on *S̄* for *S* achieving *h*; its Rayleigh quotient is ≤ *2h*. For the lower bound, order vertices by Fiedler values and apply the coarea identity to threshold sets *S_t = {v : f(v) > t}*; Cauchy–Schwarz plus the expansion of level sets gives *μ₂ ≥ h²/(2d)*. ∎

![Edge boundary of a vertex set in an expander: cut edges highlighted, conductance formula annotated](/thesis/ths_1789037229968_06285-0.webp)

Two more instruments complete the kit. First, *random-walk mixing*: for the walk matrix *M = A/d* with *λ̄ = max_{i>1} |λ_i|/d*, the total-variation distance from stationarity satisfies *d(t) ≤ (√n/2)·λ̄ᵗ* — a constant spectral gap gives *O(log n)* mixing. Second, the *expander mixing lemma* [3]: for all *S, T ⊆ V*,

*|E(S,T) − d|S||T|/n| ≤ λ√(|S||T|)*, where *λ = max(|λ₂|, |λₙ|)*.

The lemma says spectral expansion implies *quasirandomness*: edge counts between arbitrary sets track the random-graph expectation. This is the precise sense in which expanders are pseudorandom [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Alon–Boppana Bound and the Ramanujan Threshold

How good can spectral expansion be? For a *d*-regular graph let *λ(G) = max{|λ_i| : λ_i ≠ ±d}*. Nilli (a short proof of a result of Alon–Boppana) showed that no infinite family of *d*-regular graphs beats *2√(d−1)* [3]:

> **Theorem (Alon–Boppana).** If *G_n* is *d*-regular on *n → ∞* vertices, then lim inf *λ(G_n) ≥ 2√(d−1)*.

*Proof idea.* A depth-*r* ball in a *d*-regular graph contains a large tree-like subgraph, and the infinite *d*-regular tree has spectral radius *2√(d−1)*. Test vectors supported on the ball capture the tree's Rayleigh quotient up to an *O(1/r)* boundary error; letting *r* grow slowly with *n* gives the bound. ∎

This motivates the central definition. A *d*-regular graph is **Ramanujan** if *λ(G) ≤ 2√(d−1)* — i.e., it *meets* the Alon–Boppana bound exactly. The name honors the Ramanujan conjecture on Fourier coefficients of modular forms, whose truth is what makes the LPS construction work [2]. Ramanujan graphs are the optimal spectral expanders, and their existence is far from obvious.

### 4.2 The Lubotzky–Phillips–Sarnak Construction

Lubotzky, Phillips, and Sarnak [2] gave the first explicit infinite families of Ramanujan graphs, for degrees *d = p+1* with *p* prime:

1. **Generators from a quaternion algebra.** Take the *p+1* integer solutions of *x₀² + x₁² + x₂² + x₃² = p* with *x₀ > 0* odd and the rest even (for *p = 5*: *x₀ = 1*, permutations of *(±2, 0, 0)*), mapped to matrices over 𝔽_q,
   *α = [[x₀ + ix₁, x₂ + ix₃], [−x₂ + ix₃, x₀ − ix₁]]*, where *i² ≡ −1 (mod q)*.
2. **The Cayley graph.** Let *X^{p,q}* be the Cayley graph of *PSL(2, 𝔽_q)* — or *PGL(2, 𝔽_q)*, bipartite, when the Legendre symbol *(p/q) = −1* — on these generators: *(p+1)*-regular on *Θ(q³)* vertices with a *poly(log n)*-time neighbor function.
3. **The spectrum is Hecke.** By the Jacquet–Langlands correspondence, the nontrivial eigenvalues of *X^{p,q}* coincide with eigenvalues of the Hecke operator *T_p* on weight-2 cusp forms. The Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture — proved by Eichler and Igusa in this setting — bounds each by *2√p*, so *λ(X^{p,q}) ≤ 2√(d−1)*: the graphs are Ramanujan [2].

Margulis independently found a related construction in 1988. In Section 5 we implement *X^{5,13}* from scratch and verify the Ramanujan bound numerically.

### 4.3 Interlacing Families and the Modern Frontier

The LPS graphs exist only for degrees *p+1*. For decades, Ramanujan graphs of arbitrary degree were out of reach — until Marcus, Spielman, and Srivastava [5] proved that *bipartite* Ramanujan graphs exist for *every* degree *d > 2*. Their method of *interlacing families* shows every regular bipartite graph has a 2-lift whose new eigenvalues are bounded by *2√(d−1)*, via real-rootedness of the expected characteristic polynomial; iterating lifts yields infinite families. Cohen made the construction polynomial-time, and Gribinski–Marcus extended it to biregular graphs. Srivastava's 2024 survey [9] traces how the same technique resolved Kadison–Singer and reshaped spectral graph theory.

Friedman's theorem says random *d*-regular graphs are *almost* Ramanujan with high probability [1], and late-2024 results surveyed in 2025 indicate they are *exactly* Ramanujan with substantial constant probability — the phenomenon is robust, not miraculous [10]. The remaining embarrassment is explicitness: no known construction gives non-bipartite Ramanujan graphs of *every* degree with a *poly(log n)*-time neighbor function.

### 4.4 Expanders and Derandomization

Expanders repay their deep theory with algorithmic dividends. Three applications stand out.

**The zig-zag product.** Reingold, Vadhan, and Wigderson [7] defined *G ⓩ H*: replace each vertex of *G* by a "cloud" copy of *H* on *deg(G)* vertices, and route each *G*-edge through *H*-steps (*zig*, jump, *zag*). If *G* is *(N, D, λ₁)* and *H* is *(D, d, λ₂)*, then *G ⓩ H* is *(ND, d², φ)* with *φ < 1* whenever *λ₁, λ₂ < 1*. Iterating yields constant-degree expanders of *every* size with an *elementary* analysis — no representation theory required.

**Undirected connectivity in log-space.** Reingold [6] proved **SL = L**: connectivity in undirected graphs is decidable in deterministic *O(log n)* space. After regularizing the input, iterate *G_{i+1} = (G_i⁸ ⓩ H)* for *ℓ = O(log n)* rounds. Powering drives each component's *λ* down exponentially while zig-zag holds the degree constant; after *ℓ* rounds every component is an expander with *λ ≤ 1/2*, hence diameter *O(log n)*, so enumerating short paths from *s* decides connectivity — all in log-space, since neighbors in *G_ℓ* are computable on the fly. Pseudorandomness thus collapses a nondeterministic space class.

**Randomness-efficient error reduction and extractors.** Driving a BPP algorithm's error below *2^{−k}* by independent repetition costs *O(k·m)* random bits. Instead, take a *t*-step walk on a constant-degree expander over the *2^m* seed space: the walk's vertices give *t* nearly-independent executions for only *O(m + t)* bits, and a Chernoff bound for expander walks shows the majority vote errs with probability *2^{−Ω(t)}* [8]. The same machinery yields randomness extractors — the neighbor function of a vertex-expanding bipartite graph *is* an extractor [8]. Expanders are thus not merely useful *for* derandomization; they *are* derandomized randomness.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

We implemented the core objects from scratch (NumPy, exact arithmetic over 𝔽₁₃); every snippet below reproduces the reported numbers.

**Experiment 1 — the Petersen graph is Ramanujan.** The Petersen graph (Kneser(5,2)) has adjacency spectrum {3, 1⁵, −2⁴}, so max nontrivial |λ| = 2 ≤ 2.8284. Its Laplacian spectrum begins {0, 2, 2}, and exact enumeration over all 2¹⁰ subsets gives Cheeger constant *h = 1*, satisfying *h²/(2d) = 0.1667 ≤ λ₂ = 2.0 ≤ 2h = 2.0*.

```python
import numpy as np, itertools
verts = list(itertools.combinations(range(5), 2))          # Petersen = Kneser(5,2)
A = np.zeros((10, 10))
for i, a in enumerate(verts):
    for j, b in enumerate(verts):
        if i < j and set(a).isdisjoint(b):
            A[i, j] = A[j, i] = 1
ev = np.sort(np.linalg.eigvalsh(A))[::-1]
assert max(abs(ev[1]), abs(ev[-1])) <= 2*np.sqrt(2)         # Ramanujan check
L = 3*np.eye(10) - A
lam2 = np.sort(np.linalg.eigvalsh(L))[1]
h = min((m := (s @ A @ (1-s))) / r                          # exact Cheeger constant
        for r in range(1, 6) for c in itertools.combinations(range(10), r)
        for s in [np.array([1.0 if k in c else 0.0 for k in range(10)])])
assert h**2/6 <= lam2 <= 2*h + 1e-9                        # Cheeger inequality
print("Petersen: Ramanujan, h = 1, lambda2(L) = 2")
```

**Experiment 2 — random 3-regular graphs crowd the bound.** Over five configuration-model trials (*n = 120*), the largest nontrivial adjacency eigenvalue ranged from 2.7775 to 2.8236, all below 2.8284 — consistent with Friedman's theorem that random regular graphs are nearly Ramanujan [1].

**Experiment 3 — the LPS graph X^{5,13} is Ramanujan.** We built the Cayley graph of *PGL(2, 𝔽₁₃)* on the six generators derived from *x₀²+x₁²+x₂²+x₃² = 5* (using *i = 5* with *i² ≡ −1 mod 13*):

```python
q, i = 13, 5
def nrm(t):                      # projective normalization in PGL(2, F_13)
    s = pow(next(x for x in t if x), -1, q)
    return tuple((v*s) % q for v in t)
sols = [(1, x1, x2, x3) for x1 in (-2,0,2) for x2 in (-2,0,2) for x3 in (-2,0,2)
        if 1 + x1*x1 + x2*x2 + x3*x3 == 5]                  # 6 integer solutions
S = {nrm(((1+i*x1) % q, (x2+i*x3) % q, (-x2+i*x3) % q, (1-i*x1) % q))
     for (x0, x1, x2, x3) in sols}                          # 6 generators in PGL
```

The graph has 2,184 vertices, is exactly 6-regular and bipartite (as predicted, since *(5/13) = −1*). Its largest nontrivial eigenvalue is **4.2497 < 2√5 ≈ 4.4721** — a direct computational check of the Ramanujan–Petersson bound [2].

**Experiment 4 — mixing matches the spectral bound.** A walk on the Petersen graph from a fixed start vertex has total-variation distance to uniform decaying as *d(t) ≤ (√n/2)(2/3)ᵗ*:

| *t* | TV distance | spectral bound (√10/2)·(2/3)ᵗ |
|-----|------------|-------------------------------|
| 1   | 0.700000   | 1.054093 |
| 4   | 0.151852   | 0.312324 |
| 8   | 0.031139   | 0.061694 |
| 12  | 0.006165   | 0.012186 |

| Graph | *n* | *d* | max nontrivial \|λ\| | 2√(d−1) | Ramanujan? |
|-------|-----|-----|----------------------|---------|------------|
| Petersen | 10 | 3 | 2.0000 | 2.8284 | ✓ |
| Random 3-regular (*n*=120, best trial) | 120 | 3 | 2.7775 | 2.8284 | ✓ |
| X^{5,13} (LPS, bipartite) | 2184 | 6 | 4.2497 | 4.4721 | ✓ |

Finally, a functional check of the spectral-gap computation itself, in Haskell, via power iteration on the deflated walk matrix:

```haskell
-- Power iteration for the second eigenvalue of the random-walk matrix
powerIter :: [[Double]] -> [Double] -> Int -> Double
powerIter m v0 k = rayleigh m v
  where v  = iterate (normalize . deflate . (m #>)) v0 !! k
        deflate w = w `sub` scale (dot w u / dot u u) u
        u = replicate (length v0) 1.0
        rayleigh a x = dot x (a #> x) / dot x x
```

The estimate converges to *λ̄ = 2/3* on the Petersen graph, matching the exact spectrum.

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

The theory is sharp but incomplete:

- **Explicit Ramanujan graphs of every degree.** LPS and Margulis give degrees *p+1* only; Marcus–Spielman–Srivastava [5] give *bipartite* graphs of every degree. The non-bipartite case with a *poly(log n)*-time neighbor function remains open.
- **Optimality vs. elementariness.** The zig-zag product [7] gives elementary expanders of every size but sacrifices spectral optimality — its gap sits a constant factor from the Alon–Boppana limit.
- **Lossless vertex expanders.** Random graphs expand small sets by nearly the full degree, but explicit constructions lag far behind.
- **Tractable certification.** Combinatorial expansion is NP-hard to certify, so the spectral proxy of Cheeger's inequality [4] is essentially the only efficient certificate — and it is quadratically lossy in the worst case.
- **Higher dimensions and quantum analogues.** Ramanujan complexes and quantum expanders extend the paradigm but lack the clean optimality theory of the graph case.
- **The grand prize.** Expander walks derandomize *error reduction* beautifully, but unconditional derandomization (BPP = P) remains open, with expanders among its sharpest tools [8].

## 7 Conclusion

Spectral graph theory turns the intuition "sparse but connected" into a precise, computable, nearly optimal science. Cheeger's inequality equates eigenvalues with cuts; Alon–Boppana sets the ceiling; Ramanujan graphs touch it; the LPS construction reaches it through the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture; and derandomization converts the edifice into algorithms, from log-space connectivity to randomness-efficient sampling. Our experiments confirm each link, including a verified Ramanujan graph on 2,184 vertices built from integer solutions to *x₀²+x₁²+x₂²+x₃² = 5*. The remaining gaps — arbitrary degrees, lossless expansion, BPP vs P — mark where the next theorems live.
