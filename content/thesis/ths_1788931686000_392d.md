# Protocols as Resources: Concurrent Abstract Predicates, Rely–Guarantee Interference, and the Iris Framework for Verified Shared-Memory Concurrency

## Abstract

Verifying shared-memory concurrent programs requires reasoning about *interference*: threads mutate state that other threads own, observe, or merely imagine, and any sound program logic must mediate this interference without collapsing into whole-program analysis. This thesis traces the intellectual arc from Reynolds' separation logic through O'Hearn's concurrent separation logic, Jones' rely–guarantee method, and Dinsdale-Young et al.'s concurrent abstract predicates (CAP), culminating in the Iris framework of Jung et al. [1][2][3][7][8][9]. We show how CAP's *fictional separation* encodes abstract protocols over physically shared state, how rely–guarantee interference relations are internalized as ghost-state transitions, and how Iris reduces the menagerie of concurrency logics to two orthogonal ingredients: partial commutative monoids (ghost state) and invariants. We present the authoritative resource algebra, frame-preserving updates, and the Iris invariant protocol in full technical detail, and evaluate Iris against its predecessors on expressiveness, modularity, and mechanization. We close with the framework's limitations — step-indexing overhead, namespace discipline, and the safety/liveness gap — and the open problems they define.

## 1 Introduction

Shared-memory concurrency is the dominant paradigm for exploiting multicore hardware, yet it remains the most failure-prone: data races, atomicity violations, and subtle memory-ordering bugs resist testing because interleavings are astronomically numerous and rarely reproducible. Formal verification offers the only path to *certainty*, but classical Hoare logic [1] was designed for sequential programs, where the state space is local and interference is absent.

Separation logic [2] changed the economics of sequential verification. Its central connective, the *separating conjunction* $P * Q$, asserts that the heap can be split into two *disjoint* parts satisfying $P$ and $Q$ respectively, and the *frame rule*

$$
\frac{\{P\}\; C\; \{Q\}}{\{P * R\}\; C\; \{Q * R\}}
$$

lifts local reasoning to global correctness: a command verified against its footprint remains correct in any larger heap. For concurrency, O'Hearn [3] observed that the same disjointness that enables local sequential reasoning enables *thread-modular* reasoning, via the disjoint concurrency rule:

$$
\frac{\{P_1\}\; C_1\; \{Q_1\} \qquad \{P_2\}\; C_2\; \{Q_2\}}{\{P_1 * P_2\}\; C_1 \parallel C_2\; \{Q_1 * Q_2\}}
$$

Two threads verified against disjoint resources compose soundly — provided they *never* share. But realistic concurrent programs *do* share: locks, queues, and counters are precisely the interesting case. The central question of the last two decades has therefore been: **how do we reason modularly about shared state under interference?**

Three answers define the landscape. *Rely–guarantee* [1] describes interference extensionally, as binary relations over states that a thread tolerates ($R$) and promises ($G$). *Concurrent separation logic* [3] describes sharing intensionally, through resource invariants that threads open briefly at atomic steps. *Concurrent abstract predicates* [5] hide both the sharing and the protocol behind abstract, *fictionally disjoint* predicates, so clients reason as though modules were disjoint even when they are not. Each answer was powerful; each was also bespoke, with its own primitive proof rules and its own baroque soundness argument.

Iris [7][8][9] is the consolidation. Its radical claim — *"monoids and invariants are all you need"* — is that every interference-control mechanism in the prior literature can be *derived* within one small core logic from just two ingredients: partial commutative monoids, which let users construct custom ghost state with custom update rules, and invariants, which tie ghost state to physical state. This thesis explains how that reduction works, why it is sound, and what it costs.

## 2 Background

### From Hoare Triples to Separating Conjunction

Hoare logic reasons about a program $C$ via triples $\{P\}\; C\; \{Q\}$: if $P$ holds initially, $C$ terminates, and $Q$ holds finally. Reynolds [2] extended this to heap-manipulating programs with the points-to assertion $x \mapsto v$ (location $x$ holds value $v$ and *nothing else* is claimed), separating conjunction $*$, and the magic wand $-*$. The innovation is *ownership*: $x \mapsto v$ is not merely a fact about the heap but a *resource* — a permission to access $x$. This resource reading is what makes separation logic a substructural logic, and it is what lets the frame rule scale verification.

### Concurrent Separation Logic and Resource Invariants

O'Hearn's concurrent separation logic (CSL) [3] keeps ownership and adds two rules. First, the disjoint parallel rule above: threads owning disjoint resources cannot interfere, by construction. Second, *resource invariants*: a shared resource $r$ is governed by an invariant $I_r$, a precise assertion describing the shared state's shape. The critical section rule

$$
\frac{\{P * I_r\}\; C\; \{Q * I_r\}}{\{P\}\; \texttt{with}\; r\; \texttt{when}\; B\; \texttt{do}\; C\; \{Q\}}
$$

lets a thread *borrow* the invariant inside an atomic block and requires it to *restore* the invariant on exit. Between critical sections, the thread owns nothing shared, so interference is impossible by construction.

CSL's weakness is granularity: sharing is mediated only by coarse locks and invariants, and there is no way to describe *protocols* — the legal sequences of operations on shared state — or to give clients abstract specifications that hide implementation detail.

### Rely–Guarantee: Interference as Relations

Jones' rely–guarantee method [1] attacks interference directly. A thread is verified under a *rely* relation $R$ describing environment interference it must tolerate, and must itself respect a *guarantee* relation $G$ describing interference it inflicts:

$$
R, G \vdash \{P\}\; C\; \{Q\}
$$

with the parallel rule requiring $G_1 \subseteq R_2$ and $G_2 \subseteq R_1$ (each thread's guarantee is tolerated by the other's rely), plus *stability*: assertions must be preserved under rely steps. Rely–guarantee is expressive — it verifies fine-grained algorithms like Treiber's stack — but notoriously non-modular: rely and guarantee relations are global, whole-state relations, and stability proofs do not compose.

Vafeiadis and Parkinson [4] married the two traditions in *RGSep*: separation logic provides local ownership and framing, while rely–guarantee relations govern only the *shared* portion of state. RGSep showed the two mechanisms compose — but as a bespoke logic with bespoke rules.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is *comparative and reconstructive*. We take three interference-control disciplines — concurrent abstract predicates, rely–guarantee relations, and Iris ghost state with invariants — and show each successive framework *deriving* the previous one's reasoning principles, rather than merely resembling them.

Concretely, we proceed in four stages. First, we formalize CAP [5]: abstract predicates $\mathsf{is\_lock}$, $\mathsf{locked}$, $\mathsf{unlocked}$ with their axioms (e.g., $\mathsf{locked} * \mathsf{locked} \Rightarrow \bot$, duplicability of $\mathsf{is\_lock}$), and show how a lock module's two implementations — spin-lock over test-and-set, ticket lock over fetch-and-add — satisfy identical abstract specifications while hiding different internal protocols. Second, we exhibit rely–guarantee quintuples as *derived* rules: a rely relation $R$ becomes the transition system of a ghost-state protocol, and stability becomes preservation under frame-preserving updates. Third, we develop the Iris core [7][8][9]: cameras (step-indexed resource algebras), the authoritative construction $\mathsf{Auth}$, frame-preserving updates $a \rightsquigarrow b$, and the invariant mechanism $P^{\mathcal{N}}$, culminating in the Iris Proof Mode's view-shift reasoning. Fourth, we evaluate: a feature matrix comparing CSL, RGSep, CAP, TaDA [6], and Iris on abstraction, interference control, higher-order state, and mechanization, grounded in published case studies (concurrent stacks, queues, and the Rust type system [10]).

All logical claims are stated with the precision of the mechanized developments: Iris's metatheory is fully formalized in Coq [7][8][9], and our proof sketches follow the structure of those formalizations.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Concurrent Abstract Predicates and Fictional Separation

The key insight of Dinsdale-Young, Dodds, Gardner, Parkinson, and Vafeiadis [5] is that *disjointness can be fictional*. Consider a lock module. Physically, all clients share one memory cell holding the lock bit; no separating conjunction over the physical heap can divide it. CAP instead introduces *abstract predicates* whose separation is logical rather than physical:

| Predicate | Intuition | Key axiom |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathsf{is\_lock}(l)$ | $l$ is a lock (shareable knowledge) | $\mathsf{is\_lock}(l) \Leftrightarrow \mathsf{is\_lock}(l) * \mathsf{is\_lock}(l)$ (duplicable) |
| $\mathsf{locked}(l)$ | the lock is held (exclusive token) | $\mathsf{locked}(l) * \mathsf{locked}(l) \Rightarrow \bot$ |
| $\mathsf{unlocked}(l)$ | the lock is free (transferable token) | $\mathsf{locked}(l) * \mathsf{unlocked}(l) \Rightarrow \bot$ |

A client holding $\mathsf{unlocked}(l)$ may call $\mathsf{lock}$:

$$
\{\mathsf{is\_lock}(l) * \mathsf{unlocked}(l)\}\; \mathsf{lock}(l)\; \{\mathsf{is\_lock}(l) * \mathsf{locked}(l)\}
$$

and the implementation — whether spin-lock or ticket lock — is verified once, against the *concrete* definitions of these predicates in terms of permissions over the shared cell, using fractional permissions to split ownership fictionally. The client's proof never sees the cell. Crucially, the abstract predicates *behave* like disjoint resources under $*$, so the ordinary frame rule and parallel rule apply unchanged: **fiction recovers modularity**.

> **Theorem:** (CAP abstraction soundness [5]). If a module's operations satisfy their abstract CAP specifications for *some* interpretation of the abstract predicates validating the axioms, then any client verified against the abstract specifications is safe when linked against the module implementation.

The price is the axiomatization burden: each module exports not only predicates but their algebraic laws, and the metatheory must check that concrete interpretations validate them. CAP also handles only *state* abstraction well; *time* abstraction — e.g., a thread performing a multi-step operation that must appear atomic — required the successor logic TaDA [6], with its atomic triples and make-atomic rules.

### 4.2 Rely–Guarantee Reasoning Internalized as Ghost Protocols

Rely–guarantee's quintuple $R, G \vdash \{P\}\; C\; \{Q\}$ can be understood *inside* a separation logic once interference is reified as ghost state. The reconstruction proceeds as follows:

1. **Protocols as ghost state.** The shared state's legal evolution is encoded as a partial commutative monoid (PCM): elements are protocol states, composition is disjoint combination, and the validity predicate $\mathcal{V}$ rules out impossible combinations. A rely relation $R$ becomes the *transition system* of the protocol: the environment may move the ghost state only along $R$-edges.
2. **Stability as update preservation.** An assertion $P$ is *stable* under $R$ iff ghost evolution along $R$-steps preserves $P$ — exactly closure under *frame-preserving updates* $a \rightsquigarrow b$.
3. **Guarantees as owned transitions.** A thread's guarantee $G$ becomes a restriction on which frame-preserving updates *it* may perform on the shared ghost state — enforced because the thread only owns a *fragment* of the ghost resource, and fragments can only evolve in ways compatible with the authoritative whole (see §4.3).

In RGSep [4], this reconstruction was carried out as a primitive logic; in Iris, it becomes a *derived pattern*: users define a camera (resource algebra) whose update relation encodes the protocol, and rely/guarantee-style reasoning falls out of the generic rules for ghost ownership. The quintuple's global relations are thus localized to the ghost resources a thread actually owns — recovering modularity without losing expressiveness.

### 4.3 Iris: Cameras, Authoritative Resources, and Frame-Preserving Updates

Iris's base logic is a higher-order concurrent separation logic whose model is built from *cameras* — step-indexed partial commutative monoids with a validity predicate and a *core* operation $|{-}|$ extracting duplicable knowledge [8][9]. The two derived constructions that carry all the weight are ghost ownership and authoritative resources.

**Ghost ownership.** For any camera $M$ and ghost name $\gamma$, the assertion $\ownGhost{\gamma}{a}$ claims ownership of ghost element $a$. Composition in the camera mirrors separating conjunction: $\ownGhost{\gamma}{a \cdot b} \dashv\vdash \ownGhost{\gamma}{a} * \ownGhost{\gamma}{b}$. Allocation of fresh ghost state is always possible, and ghost state evolves only by *frame-preserving updates*:

$$
a \rightsquigarrow b \;\triangleq\; \forall c.\; \mathcal{V}(a \cdot c) \Rightarrow \mathcal{V}(b \cdot c)
$$

i.e., $a$ may become $b$ only if every frame $c$ compatible with $a$ remains compatible with $b$. This single definition captures permissions (fractional camera), tokens (exclusive camera), histories (append-only camera), and protocols (STS camera) — the "monoids" half of the slogan.

**Authoritative resources.** The construction $\mathsf{Auth}(M)$ adds a distinguished *authoritative* element $\bullet a$ alongside fragments $\circ b$, with composition $\bullet a \cdot \circ b$ valid iff $b \preceq a$ ($b$ is "included in" $a$: $\exists c.\, b \cdot c = a$). This yields the two rules that encode *shared knowledge with local ownership*:

$$
\infer{\ownGhost{\gamma}{\bullet a} * \ownGhost{\gamma}{\circ b} \vdash b \preceq a}{}
\qquad
\infer{\ownGhost{\gamma}{\bullet a} * \ownGhost{\gamma}{\circ b} \Rrightarrow \ownGhost{\gamma}{\bullet a'} * \ownGhost{\gamma}{\circ b'}}{a \cdot b \rightsquigarrow a' \cdot b'}
$$

A thread holding fragment $\circ b$ knows the authoritative state includes $b$ (first rule: agreement), and may update its fragment only in ways the authority can follow (second rule: frame-preserving update of the whole). This is the mechanism behind CAP-style fictional separation *and* rely-style interference control, unified: the authority is the shared protocol state; fragments are threads' local views.

In Coq, the Iris Proof Mode [9] mechanizes this reasoning. A typical ghost update step looks like:

```coq
Lemma lock_acquire_spec γ l :
  {{{ inv_lock γ l ∗ ownGhost γ (◯ Unlocked) }}}
    acquire l
  {{{ RET #(); ownGhost γ (◯ Locked) }}}.
Proof.
  iIntros (Φ) "[#Hinv Htok] HΦ".
  iApply (acquire_spec with "Htok"); iIntros "Htok".
  iMod (ghost_update with "Htok") as "Htok"; (* Unlocked ~~> Locked *)
  iApply "HΦ"; iFrame.
Qed.
```

The `iMod` tactic performs a *view shift* — a logical update of ghost state justified by a frame-preserving update — which the proof mode checks against the camera's validity predicate.

### 4.4 Invariants, View Shifts, and the Iris Proof Mode

Invariants are the "invariants" half of the slogan. An Iris invariant $\knowInv{\mathcal{N}}{P}$ asserts that $P$ holds of *shared* resources under namespace $\mathcal{N}$ [7][9]. Unlike CSL's resource invariants, Iris invariants are *impredicative* (they may mention invariants themselves, via the step-indexed later modality $\triangleright$) and are opened not by syntactic critical sections but by *view shifts* around *physically atomic* steps:

$$
\infer{\knowInv{\mathcal{N}}{P} \vdash \Rrightarrow_{\mathcal{E}\setminus\mathcal{N}}^{\mathcal{E}} \triangleright P * (\triangleright P \Rrightarrow_{\mathcal{E}\setminus\mathcal{N}}^{\mathcal{E}} \mathsf{True})}{}
$$

Reading: to perform an atomic step, a thread may *open* the invariant, gaining $\triangleright P$ (one step's worth of $P$), provided it *closes* it afterward by re-establishing $P$. The masks $\mathcal{E}$ track which invariants are currently open, preventing re-entrant opening and the associated unsoundness. Because opening is tied to physical atomicity rather than lock syntax, Iris verifies *lock-free* algorithms — Treiber's stack, the Michael–Scott queue — where CSL's critical sections do not apply.

The invariant registry itself is ghost state: an authoritative finite map from names to (later-guarded) propositions, using the agreement camera to ensure all threads agree on each invariant's meaning [8]. This is *higher-order ghost state* [9]: propositions stored inside resources, made sound by step-indexing. The Iris Proof Mode's `iInv` tactic automates the open/close discipline, and view shifts compose: $P \Rrightarrow Q$ and $Q \Rrightarrow R$ give $P \Rrightarrow R$, so protocol steps chain into full linearizability arguments.

The payoff is derivability. CAP's abstract predicates become Iris predicates over ghost state; TaDA's atomic triples become logically atomic specifications derived via invariants and view shifts; RGSep's quintuples become Hoare triples over ghost protocols. One core logic, one Coq-checked soundness proof [9], many derived disciplines.

## 5 Empirical Analysis and Proofs

We evaluate the frameworks along five dimensions, grounded in their published metatheory and case studies.

| Dimension | CSL [3] | RGSep [4] | CAP [5] | TaDA [6] | Iris [7][9] |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sharing mechanism | resource invariants | shared region + RG relations | fictional abstract predicates | regions + guards, atomic triples | ghost cameras + invariants |
| Client abstraction | none (concrete invariants leak) | partial | full (predicates hide impl.) | full (abstract atomicity) | full (derived predicates) |
| Interference control | lock discipline | global rely/guarantee | protocol in predicate axioms | guard algebras | frame-preserving updates |
| Higher-order state | no | no | no | no | yes (impredicative invariants) |
| Mechanized metatheory | paper proof | paper proof | paper proof | paper proof | **Coq** |

Three findings emerge. **First, derivability is real.** Jung et al. [7] derive the characteristic rules of CAP, TaDA-style atomicity, and RGSep-style rely–guarantee within Iris, each as a *lemma* rather than a primitive — so a bug in one discipline's rules cannot compromise the core. **Second, mechanization changes the trust equation.** Prior logics rest on paper soundness proofs over bespoke models; Iris's model (cameras, the invariant registry, the adequacy theorem connecting $\vdash \mathsf{wp}$ to operational safety) is checked by Coq [9], and large developments — including a logical-relations model of Rust's type system [10] — are built on it. **Third, expressiveness composes.** A single Iris proof can combine a CAP-style lock abstraction, a rely-style protocol on a shared counter, and linearizability reasoning for a lock-free queue, because ghost state is user-defined rather than fixed by the logic.

> **Theorem:** (Iris adequacy [9]). If $\vdash \mathsf{wp}\; e\; \{\Phi\}$ is derivable in Iris (with no open invariants), then $e$ is safe: it never gets stuck, and if it terminates with value $v$, then $\Phi(v)$ holds.

*Proof sketch.* By the model construction, derivability implies the weakest precondition holds at every step-index $n$ in the step-indexed resource model. The adequacy lemma transfers step-indexed truth to operational behavior: each physical step consumes one index, and validity of the composed ghost/physical resources is preserved by frame-preserving updates and invariant re-establishment. Since the indices are unbounded, safety follows for all finite executions. The full proof is mechanized in Coq [9]. ∎

The cost is also empirical: Iris proofs carry *ghost-state discovery* overhead — the verifier must invent the camera encoding the protocol — and step-indexing forces $\triangleright$-management (later-stripping) that paper logics ignore. Automation remains an active research front.

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

## 7 Conclusion

From Reynolds' separating conjunction to Iris's cameras, the history of concurrent separation logic is the history of a single idea refined: *ownership disciplines interference*. CSL showed disjoint ownership eliminates interference; rely–guarantee showed relational specifications tolerate it; CAP showed fictional ownership abstracts it; and Iris showed that all three — and TaDA, RGSep, and their cousins — are instances of one mechanism: user-defined ghost resources evolving by frame-preserving updates, tied to physical state by invariants [7][8][9].

The practical consequence is a verified-software pipeline that did not exist before: concurrent data structures, and even the core of Rust's type system [10], verified in Coq against machine-checked metatheory, with modular abstractions that survive changes of implementation. The remaining gaps — liveness, automation, weak memory, principled ghost-state design — define the next decade. If the last decade's lesson is that monoids and invariants suffice for safety, the next decade's question is what equally small core suffices for *everything else*.
