# Iteration, Height, and Orbit Intersections: Canonical Heights and Uniform Boundedness in Arithmetic Dynamics

## Abstract

Arithmetic dynamics studies number-theoretic questions arising from iterating rational maps $f : \mathbb{P}^N \to \mathbb{P}^N$ of degree at least $2$ defined over a number field $K$. Its central invariant is the canonical height $\hat{h}_f$, constructed by Call and Silverman as a Tate limit of Weil heights; it vanishes precisely on the preperiodic points of $f$, and Northcott's theorem forces $\mathrm{Preper}(f, K)$ to be finite. This thesis develops the theory from the height machine through the deepest open problems in the field: the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture on orbit intersections with subvarieties, dynamical Manin–Mumford and unlikely-intersection phenomena, and the Morton–Silverman uniform boundedness conjecture, which predicts a constant $C = C(N, d, [K : \mathbb{Q}])$ bounding the number of $K$-rational preperiodic points of any degree-$d$ morphism of $\mathbb{P}^N$. We survey the unconditional quadratic results, Poonen's conjecture, recent uniform boundedness theorems conditional on $abc$ and Vojta-type conjectures, and the family-level unlikely-intersection results of DeMarco–Mavraki, ending with computational evidence and open problems.

##1 Intro

Classical Diophantine geometry asks for the rational points on a fixed algebraic variety. **Arithmetic dynamics** asks a different question: fix a morphism $f : \mathbb{P}^N \to \mathbb{P}^N$ of degree $d \geq 2$ defined over a number field $K$, iterate it, and ask number-theoretic questions about the resulting orbits. For $P \in \mathbb{P}^N(K)$ the *forward orbit* is $\mathcal{O}_f(P) = \{P, f(P), f^{\circ 2}(P), \dots\}$. A point is *periodic* if $f^{\circ n}(P) = P$ for some $n \geq 1$ (minimal such $n$ is its *period*), and *preperiodic* if its forward orbit is finite, equivalently if some iterate of $P$ is periodic. The collection $\mathrm{Preper}(f, K)$ of $K$-rational preperiodic points is the dynamical analogue of the torsion subgroup of an abelian variety—and the analogy runs remarkably deep [1][2].

Why should iteration produce interesting arithmetic? The fundamental observation is that a degree-$d$ morphism stretches heights multiplicatively: a naive height satisfies $h(f(P)) = d\,h(P) + O(1)$, so iteration inflates arithmetic complexity exponentially unless the orbit collapses into a finite set. Normalizing this growth yields the **canonical height**

$$\hat{h}_f(P) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{h(f^{\circ n}(P))}{d^n},$$

a limit whose existence was established in the dynamical setting by Call and Silverman [1]. The canonical height detects preperiodic points exactly, $\hat{h}_f(P) = 0 \iff P \in \mathrm{Preper}(f)$, and Northcott's finiteness theorem converts this detection into the finiteness of $\mathrm{Preper}(f, K)$ for each individual map.

This thesis develops the consequences. §2 reviews heights and proves Northcott's theorem. §3 describes the methodology of the field. §4 is the deep dive: canonical heights, the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture, dynamical Manin–Mumford and unlikely intersections, and uniform boundedness. §5 surveys theorems and computational evidence, §6 the limitations, and §7 concludes.

##2 Background

### 2.1 Weil heights on projective space

The engine of all finiteness results is the **height machine**. For a point $P = (x_0 : \dots : x_N) \in \mathbb{P}^N(K)$ with $K$ a number field, the logarithmic Weil height is

$$h(P) = \frac{1}{[K : \mathbb{Q}]} \sum_{v \in M_K} \max_i \log \|x_i\|_v,$$

summed over all places of $K$ with normalized absolute values. It measures arithmetic complexity: $h(a : b) = \log \max(|a|, |b|)$ for coprime integers $a, b$. The crucial properties are *functoriality* ($h(\phi(P)) = (\deg \phi)\, h(P) + O(1)$ for a morphism $\phi$) and the **Northcott property**: there are only finitely many points of bounded height and bounded degree.

> Theorem: (Northcott) The set $\{P \in \mathbb{P}^N(\bar{K}) : h(P) \le A,\ [K(P) : \mathbb{Q}] \le B\}$ is finite for all $A, B$.

*Northcott's finiteness theorem* converts height-theoretic statements into arithmetic finiteness statements, and it is the single most-used tool in arithmetic dynamics [3].

### 2.2 The Call–Silverman canonical height

Let $f : \mathbb{P}^N \to \mathbb{P}^N$ be a morphism of degree $d \geq 2$ defined over $K$. Call and Silverman proved in far greater generality—for any variety with a morphism possessing an ample eigenclass—that the Tate limit

$$\hat{h}_f(P) = \lim_{n\to\infty} d^{-n} h(f^{\circ n}(P))$$

converges, defining a canonical height with the characterizing properties [1]:

- $\hat{h}_f(f(P)) = d\,\hat{h}_f(P)$ for all $P$ (exact scaling, no error term);
- $\hat{h}_f = h + O(1)$ (it refines the Weil height);
- $\hat{h}_f(P) \geq 0$, with equality if and only if $P$ is preperiodic.

Because points of height at most $A$ and bounded degree are finite (Northcott), and $\hat{h}_f$ vanishes exactly on preperiodic points, one immediately obtains:

> Theorem: (Finiteness of preperiodic points) For $f$ and $K$ as above, $\mathrm{Preper}(f, K)$ is finite.

This is the dynamical analogue of the finiteness of $A(K)_{\mathrm{tors}}$ for an abelian variety, and it is the foundation on which every deeper conjecture builds.

### 2.3 The Morton–Silverman uniform boundedness conjecture

Finiteness for each $f$ is cheap; uniformity across all $f$ is not. Morton and Silverman proposed in 1994 that the cardinality of $\mathrm{Preper}(f, K)$ is bounded by a quantity depending only on coarse data—the dimension $N$, the degree $d$, and the degree $[K : \mathbb{Q}]$—and *not* on the map $f$ itself [2][3]:

> Conjecture: (Morton–Silverman) For integers $N \ge 1$, $d \ge 2$, $D \ge 1$ there exists a constant $C = C(N, d, D)$ such that for every number field $K$ with $[K : \mathbb{Q}] \le D$ and every morphism $f : \mathbb{P}^N \to \mathbb{P}^N$ of degree $d$ defined over $K$, $\#\mathrm{Preper}(f, K) \le C$.

This is the dynamical analogue of Merel's theorem on uniform boundedness of torsion on elliptic curves; indeed Fakhruddin showed that Morton–Silverman implies the uniform boundedness conjecture for torsion on abelian varieties, so even the case $d = 4$, $N = 1$ (Lattès maps) would imply Merel's theorem [6]. The conjecture remains open in every case.

##3 Methodology

The field's methods are a blend of classical height theory, complex and $p$-adic dynamics, equidistribution, and large-scale computation.

1. **Canonical heights as detectors.** Because $\hat{h}_f$ vanishes exactly on preperiodic points, problems about preperiodic points become problems about points of small height. Families of maps are studied via *variation of canonical height*: how $\hat{h}_{f_t}(P_t)$ changes as the map moves in moduli space. Call–Silverman's original work already proved that canonical height varies nicely in families [1], a fact exploited systematically in unlikely-intersection theory.

2. **Dynatomic polynomials.** For $f : \mathbb{P}^1 \to \mathbb{P}^1$, the $n$-th *dynatomic polynomial* $\Phi_n^f$ is a divisor of $f^{\circ n}(z) - z$ whose roots are points of formal period $n$. Periodic points are organized algebraically by dynatomic curves $\Phi_n(z) = 0$ in parameter space, e.g. $z^2 + c$ yields the well-studied dynatomic curves $Y_0(n)$ parametrizing $c$ with periodic critical behavior. Irreducibility and Galois groups of dynatomic polynomials drive much of the quadratic theory [3][6].

3. **Local and $p$-adic dynamics.** Good reduction of $f$ modulo a prime constrains preperiodic points via the reduction map on the residue field: periodic points inject into $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{F}_q)$-dynamics, giving period bounds from finite-field combinatorics. Benedetto's non-archimedean potential theory refines this into the strongest unconditional uniform boundedness results over global function fields.

4. **Equidistribution and arithmetic intersection.** Yuan's equidistribution theorem for points of small height, and its dynamical versions, imply that infinite sequences of distinct preperiodic points equidistribute with respect to the canonical measure. Combined with height gaps, this yields Manin–Mumford-type statements.

5. **Computation.** Hutz implemented an algorithm in Sage for provably determining $\mathrm{Preper}(f, \mathbb{Q})$ for morphisms of $\mathbb{P}^N$, verifying Poonen's conjecture for $z^2 + c$ with numerator and denominator up to $10^8$ in absolute value [2]. These computations supply the empirical backbone for §5.

```python
# Schematic: dynatomic polynomial for exact period n (Sage-style)
def dynatomic(f, n, z):
    # product over divisors d of n of (f^d(z) - z)^{mu(n/d)}
    from sage.arith.misc import moebius, divisors
    return prod((f.iterate(d)(z) - z) ** moebius(n // d) for d in divisors(n))
# roots of dynatomic(f, n) = points of formal period n
```

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Canonical heights: local decomposition and specialization

The global canonical height decomposes as a sum of **local canonical heights** (dynamical Green functions) over all places, $\hat{h}_f(P) = \sum_v \hat{\lambda}_{f,v}(P)$. At each place, $\hat{\lambda}_{f,v}$ is built as a Tate limit of local Weil functions and is continuous away from the exceptional locus. This decomposition matters for two reasons. First, it allows *quantitative* statements: at places of good reduction the local height is essentially trivial, so the global height is controlled by the finitely many bad places—this is how Benedetto obtains uniform bounds over function fields. Second, it governs **specialization**: in a one-parameter family $f_t$, the function $t \mapsto \hat{h}_{f_t}(P_t)$ is continuous (indeed, it behaves like a height on the base), so height-zero loci are closed and unlikely-intersection results transfer between the generic and special fibers [1][8].

A deeper fact is the *height gap*: away from preperiodic points, the canonical height is bounded below by a positive constant depending on the map—this is the dynamical **Bogomolov property** relative to $\hat{h}_f$. Fields with the Bogomolov property relative to a given map (e.g. totally real fields for certain maps) then contain only preperiodic points from a finite computable set [10]. The Bogomolov question—uniform gaps in families—is one of the field's sharpest open problems.

### 4.2 The dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture

Classical Mordell–Lang says that the intersection of a finitely generated subgroup $\Gamma$ of a semi-abelian variety with a subvariety $X$ is a finite union of cosets. The dynamical version replaces the subgroup by a forward orbit. Let $X$ be a variety, $f : X \to X$ a morphism, and $V \subset X$ a subvariety; the conjecture asserts that the *return set* $\{n \geq 0 : f^{\circ n}(P) \in V\}$ is a finite union of arithmetic progressions, provided the orbit of $P$ is Zariski dense. Equivalently, an orbit meets a proper subvariety in an unexpectedly large set only when there is a dynamical reason—some iterate of $V$ is periodic.

> Theorem: (Dynamical Mordell–Lang, cases) The conjecture holds for étale maps of quasiprojective varieties, for polynomial endomorphisms of affine space (Bell–Ghioca–Tucker), and in many cases for rational maps of $\mathbb{P}^2$; the general case for $\mathbb{P}^N$ remains open [9].

The connection to heights is through *$p$-adic interpolation*: Skolem-type arguments show that if an orbit meets a hypersurface infinitely often, a $p$-adic analytic function vanishes on infinitely many integers, forcing a periodic structure. Height enters via results of the form: if $f^{\circ n}(P)$ lands in $V$ infinitely often, then either the orbit is not Zariski dense or $\hat{h}_f$ degenerates along the orbit. The dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture is now known for maps admitting a $p$-adic uniformization and for large classes of polynomial maps, but a rational map of $\mathbb{P}^3$ with a Zariski-dense orbit remains terra incognita [9].

### 4.3 Unlikely intersections and dynamical Manin–Mumford

The **dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture** asks: for $f : \mathbb{P}^N \to \mathbb{P}^N$ and a subvariety $V$, if $V$ contains infinitely many preperiodic points, must $V$ be *preperiodic* itself (some iterate $f^{\circ m}(V)$ is periodic)? This is the dynamical analogue of Raynaud's theorem, and it was proved by Zhang for polarized maps using equidistribution of preperiodic points [9]. The philosophy extends to **unlikely intersections** in families: consider the moduli space of maps, e.g. the $c$-line for $z^2 + c$, and two marked points, say $0$ and $1$. The set of $c$ for which *both* $0$ and $1$ are simultaneously preperiodic for $z^2 + c$ is an unlikely intersection—a codimension-two condition in a one-dimensional base. Ghioca, Hsia, and Tucker proved that such simultaneous preperiodicity happens only finitely often unless there is a functional relation, via height lower bounds in families. Most strikingly, DeMarco and Mavraki showed that the explicit examples of Doyle–Hyde with many rational preperiodic points have applications to **uniform** unlikely intersections in complex dynamics, proving a uniform bound across a family where none was known [6].

### 4.4 Uniform boundedness: the Morton–Silverman conjecture

The deepest open problem is the conjecture stated in §2.3. What is known?

- **Quadratic polynomials over $\mathbb{Q}$.** No quadratic polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$ has a rational periodic point of period $4$ (Morton), period $5$ (Flynn–Poonen–Schaefer), or period $6$ (Stoll, conditional on BSD). Poonen conjectured there are no rational periodic points of period $\geq 6$ for quadratic polynomials over $\mathbb{Q}$; if true, at most $9$ rational preperiodic points occur, and Hutz classified all possible portraits [2]. The period-$6$ barrier is the precise interface with the Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, and this post deliberately does not develop BSD further.
- **Function fields.** Benedetto proved uniform boundedness for non-isotrivial maps over global function fields of characteristic zero, and Doyle–Poonen–Looper extended this: the non-isotriviality hypothesis is essential, since constant (isotrivial) families inherit counterexamples from the number-field case [5].
- **Conditional number-field results.** Ingram proved uniform boundedness for $K$-rational preperiodic points of polynomials *assuming Vojta's conjecture*, using non-archimedean potential theory and the observation that preperiodic points are points of canonical height zero [4]. A separate theorem obtains uniform boundedness from the $abc$-conjecture for certain families [8]. These are the strongest results over number fields: the conjecture follows from the standard conjectures of arithmetic geometry, but from nothing we can currently prove.
- **Unicritical families.** Doyle and Hindes proved uniform boundedness, independent of the degree, for unicritical polynomials $z^d + c$ over $abc$-fields (fields where $abc$ holds), classifying all possible preperiodic portraits up to the action of roots of unity—only thirteen portraits occur [7]. This is the sharpest unconditional-in-degree result available.
- **Lower bounds.** Doyle and Hyde constructed, in each degree $d$, polynomials over $\mathbb{Q}$ with at least $d + 6$ rational preperiodic points, and proved non-constructively that $d + \lfloor \log_2 d \rfloor$ preperiodic points occur in degree at most $d$ [6]. Kim, Krieger, Postolache, and Szeto extended the extremal-construction program to Hénon maps, producing polynomial automorphisms of affine space with many rational periodic points [5].

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Result 1 (Northcott, effective).** For each $f$ and $K$, $\mathrm{Preper}(f, K)$ is finite; Hutz's Sage algorithm makes this effective for $K = \mathbb{Q}$, computing the full set for thousands of maps and verifying Poonen's conjecture for $z^2 + c$ with numerator and denominator up to $10^8$ [2].

**Result 2 (Period classification).** Periods $4$ and $5$ are impossible for quadratic polynomials over $\mathbb{Q}$; the classification of preperiodic portraits for maps with nonabelian automorphism groups yields exactly five portraits [9][2].

**Result 3 (Uniform portraits in families).** Pasten constructed explicit non-isotrivial families of polynomials over $\mathbb{Q}$ with the uniform boundedness property, showing the conjecture is not vacuous at the family level [4].

**Result 4 (Doyle–Hyde lower bounds).** The best known lower bound on any Morton–Silverman constant grows like $d + \log_2 d$, far below any plausible true bound—suggesting the conjecture's constants, if they exist, are large [6].

**Result 5 (Fakhruddin's implication).** Morton–Silverman implies uniform boundedness of torsion on abelian varieties, so the dynamical conjecture subsumes one of the deepest theorems in arithmetic geometry as a special case [5].

> Proof: (Sketch of Northcott $\Rightarrow$ finiteness of $\mathrm{Preper}$) If $P$ is preperiodic, some iterate $f^{\circ m}(P)$ is periodic, so the orbit $\{f^{\circ n}(P)\}$ is finite. The canonical height satisfies $\hat{h}_f(f^{\circ n}(P)) = d^n \hat{h}_f(P)$ by exact scaling; a finite orbit forces $\hat{h}_f(P) = 0$. Conversely $\hat{h}_f(P) = 0$ implies $\{d^{-n}h(f^{\circ n}(P))\} \to 0$, and since $\hat{h}_f = h + O(1)$, the orbit has bounded Weil height. By Northcott there are finitely many such points of bounded degree; over the fixed field $K$ the set is finite. ∎

##6 Limitations

1. **The conjecture is completely open.** Not a single case of Morton–Silverman over a number field is proved unconditionally—not even quadratic polynomials over $\mathbb{Q}$, the simplest nontrivial setting.
2. **BSD dependence.** The period-$6$ result for quadratics assumes the Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture; removing that hypothesis requires a direct analysis of the genus-$2$ dynatomic curve $Y_0(6)$, whose Jacobian has rank that resists unconditional determination.
3. **Isotriviality barrier.** Function-field proofs need non-isotriviality; the number-field case has no such escape, and genuinely new ideas are needed.
4. **Ineffectivity.** Conditional proofs via Vojta give no explicit constant $C(N, d, D)$; even the growth rate of the optimal bound is unknown, with lower bounds only logarithmic in $d$ [6].
5. **Higher dimension.** Almost everything proved is for $\mathbb{P}^1$. For $\mathbb{P}^N$, $N \geq 2$, even the finiteness of special subvarieties (dynamical Manin–Mumford beyond the polarized case) is open.
6. **Dynamical Mordell–Lang.** The full conjecture for rational maps of $\mathbb{P}^N$ is open; known cases rely on $p$-adic methods that fail for maps with bad reduction everywhere.

##7 Conclusion

Arithmetic dynamics has grown from an analogy—preperiodic points are like torsion points—into a field with its own internal logic. The canonical height of Call and Silverman [1] is its organizing invariant: it detects preperiodic points, drives Northcott finiteness, and its variation in families underlies the unlikely-intersection revolution. The dynamical Mordell–Lang and Manin–Mumford conjectures translate the deepest theorems of Diophantine geometry into dynamical statements, several of them now theorems. And the Morton–Silverman uniform boundedness conjecture stands as the field's Riemann hypothesis: implied by the standard conjectures, verified computationally to enormous bounds, witnessed by extremal constructions, yet unproved in every case. The gap between the logarithmic lower bounds of Doyle–Hyde and the hoped-for uniform upper bounds is where the next decade's work lives—and where a single new idea about heights in families could change everything.
