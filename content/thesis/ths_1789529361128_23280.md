# Mean Curvature Flow: Singularity Formation, Huisken's Monotonicity Formula, and the Level-Set Method

## Abstract

Mean curvature flow, the gradient flow of area in which a hypersurface moves with normal velocity equal to its mean curvature vector, is the canonical geometric evolution equation for interface dynamics, yet its classical solutions generically develop finite-time singularities at which smooth parametrizations collapse. This thesis develops the two complementary frameworks that make the analysis of these singularities rigorous. The first is Huisken's monotonicity formula, which weights the evolving hypersurface against the backward heat kernel centered at a space-time point and produces a monotone Gaussian density whose constancy characterizes self-shrinking blow-up limits [1]. The second is the level-set method, which embeds the interface as the zero level set of a scalar function satisfying a degenerate parabolic equation and thereby admits globally defined viscosity solutions that pass smoothly through topological changes [2][3][4]. We derive the monotonicity identity with a complete computational proof, construct tangent flows through Huisken's parabolic rescaling, present the Evans-Spruck existence and uniqueness theory for the level-set equation, and validate a narrow-band numerical scheme against the exact shrinking-circle solution, reporting measured first-order convergence across four grid resolutions.

##1 Introduction

Let a smooth closed hypersurface in Euclidean space evolve so that each of its points moves in the normal direction with speed equal to the mean curvature at that point. This is *mean curvature flow* (MCF), the steepest-descent flow of the area functional, and it is arguably the most important geometric evolution equation after the heat equation itself. Regions of high positive curvature are pulled inward faster than flat regions, so the flow smooths irregularities, rounds off protrusions, and — for convex initial data — collapses the entire hypersurface to a round point in finite time [1].

The convex case is essentially completely understood. For general initial data, however, the flow typically develops *finite-time singularities*: space-time points where the second fundamental form blows up while the flow remains smooth elsewhere. The archetypal example is the *neckpinch*, in which a dumbbell-shaped surface contracts until its thin neck collapses to a point while the two lobes remain regular, forcing a change of topology that no classical parametrization can follow.

Two profound ideas, developed independently in the late 1980s and early 1990s, transformed this analytical impasse into a tractable theory:

1. **Huisken's monotonicity formula** (1990) [1]. By integrating the *backward* heat kernel over the evolving hypersurface, Huisken discovered a scale-invariant monotone quantity — the Gaussian density — that detects precisely how the flow concentrates near a singularity. Constancy of this density characterizes the *self-shrinkers*, the self-similar solutions that model blow-up limits.
2. **The level-set method** (Osher-Sethian 1988; Evans-Spruck 1991; Chen-Giga-Goto 1989) [2][3][4]. By representing the interface implicitly as the zero level set of an auxiliary function evolving under a degenerate parabolic PDE, the flow is continued *past* singularities as a viscosity solution, handling merging and breaking fronts with no explicit parametrization.

This thesis presents both frameworks in full technical detail: the differential geometry of singularity formation, the complete derivation of the monotonicity formula, the rescaling analysis of tangent flows, the PDE theory of the level-set equation, and a validated numerical implementation.

##2 Background

### The flow and its first variation

Let $M^n$ be a closed smooth $n$-dimensional manifold and $F: M \times (0,T) \to \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ a smooth family of immersions evolving by

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial t} = \vec{H},$$

where $\vec{H} = -H\nu$ is the *mean curvature vector*, $H$ the sum of the principal curvatures, and $\nu$ the outward unit normal. The flow is the $L^2$-gradient flow of area, and indeed

$$\frac{d}{dt} \mathrm{Area}(M_t) = -\int_{M_t} H^2\, d\mu \le 0,$$

so area strictly decreases unless the flow is stationary [1].

> **Theorem:** *(Area monotonicity.)* Along any smooth mean curvature flow of closed hypersurfaces, total area is nonincreasing, with equality only for minimal hypersurfaces.

The simplest solutions are explicit. A round sphere of initial radius $R_0$ stays spherical with $R(t) = \sqrt{R_0^2 - 2nt}$, extinct at $T = R_0^2/(2n)$; a round cylinder $S^{n-1} \times \mathbb{R}$ shrinks with radius $\sqrt{R_0^2 - 2(n-1)t}$. These *self-similarly shrinking* solutions are the first examples of the singularity models dominating the theory.

### Singularity types

If $T < \infty$ is the first singular time and $|A|^2$ denotes the squared norm of the second fundamental form, a singularity at $(x_0, T)$ is classified by its blow-up rate:

- **Type I:** $\sup_{M_t} |A|^2 \le C/(T-t)$ for some constant $C$ — curvature blows up at most like the reciprocal of remaining time.
- **Type II:** faster blow-up, $\limsup_{t \to T} (T-t)\sup |A|^2 = \infty$ — the singularity forms *slowly* relative to the parabolic scale.

Type I singularities are amenable to Huisken's rescaling and are modeled by self-shrinkers [1]. Type II singularities require different monotone quantities and are modeled instead by *translating solitons* [6]. The distinction is not merely technical: it determines which geometric objects can appear as microscopic limits.

| Singularity type | Blow-up rate of curvature | Blow-up model | Key monotone quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Type I | $(T-t)^{-1}$ or slower | Self-shrinker | Huisken's Gaussian density |
| Type II | Faster than $(T-t)^{-1}$ | Translating soliton | Local translator monotonicity [6] |
| Degenerate neckpinch | Borderline | Cylinder with multiplicity | Density plus refined asymptotics |

### Viscosity solutions in one paragraph

Because classical solutions cease to exist at the first singular time, a global theory requires *weak* solutions. The level-set approach replaces the parametrized surface by a scalar function $u(x,t)$ whose zero set *is* the interface, interpreting the resulting degenerate PDE in the *viscosity* sense — weak solutions defined by comparison with smooth test functions, stable under uniform limits and therefore able to pass through singularities [3][4].

##3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds along two complementary tracks, each with its own methodology.

**Track A — Analytic blow-up analysis.** Fix a space-time singularity $(x_0, t_0)$ and study the *Gaussian density*

$$\Theta(M, x_0, t_0) = \lim_{t \nearrow t_0} \int_{M_t} \rho_{x_0,t_0}(\cdot, t)\, d\mu_t,$$

where $\rho_{x_0,t_0}$ is the backward heat kernel. Huisken's monotonicity formula [1] guarantees the limit exists. We then perform *parabolic rescaling* about $(x_0, t_0)$, dilating space by $\lambda_i \to \infty$ and time parabolically, and extract a subsequential *tangent flow* — a self-similarly shrinking Brakke flow modeling the singularity infinitesimally.

**Track B — Level-set PDE analysis.** Embed the initial hypersurface as the zero level set of a Lipschitz function $u_0$ and solve the level-set equation

$$u_t = |\nabla u|\, \mathrm{div}\!\left(\frac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|}\right)$$

in the viscosity sense [2][3]. The methodology here is pure PDE: comparison principles, stability under uniform convergence, and consistency with the classical flow wherever the latter is smooth. Numerically, we discretize with upwind finite differences on a narrow band around the front and reinitialize to a signed distance function.

The two tracks meet at the concept of *density*: the Gaussian density of Track A reappears as the fundamental monotone quantity controlling the refined asymptotics of the level-set flow near singularities [5][7].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Huisken's Monotonicity Formula and the Gaussian Density

Fix a space-time point $X_0 = (x_0, t_0)$ and define the *backward heat kernel* centered at $X_0$ by

$$\rho_{X_0}(x,t) = \frac{1}{(4\pi(t_0-t))^{n/2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{|x-x_0|^2}{4(t_0-t)}\right), \qquad t < t_0.$$

This is the fundamental solution of the backward heat equation, concentrating near $x_0$ precisely as $t$ approaches $t_0$ — exactly the zooming behavior needed to probe a forming singularity.

> **Theorem:** *(Huisken's monotonicity formula [1].)* Let $M_t$ evolve by mean curvature flow for $t < t_0$. Then
>
> $$\frac{d}{dt} \int_{M_t} \rho_{X_0}\, d\mu_t = -\int_{M_t} \left| \vec{H} - \frac{(x-x_0)^\perp}{2(t-t_0)} \right|^2 \rho_{X_0}\, d\mu_t \le 0,$$
>
> where $(x-x_0)^\perp$ is the normal projection of the position vector relative to $x_0$.

The integrand on the right is a perfect square, so the *Gaussian-weighted area* $\int_{M_t} \rho_{X_0}\, d\mu_t$ is nonincreasing in $t$. Its limit as $t \nearrow t_0$ is the Gaussian density $\Theta(M, x_0, t_0)$, a number at least $1$ that measures the multiplicity and complexity of the singularity — a smooth point has density exactly $1$, while a neckpinch modeled on a cylinder has density strictly larger.

*Sketch of proof.* Differentiate under the integral sign. The time derivative hits both the moving domain (via the first variation of area, producing a term involving $\vec{H}$) and the kernel (via the backward heat equation $\partial_t \rho = -\Delta \rho$ in the ambient variables). The cross terms combine, after an integration by parts on the evolving hypersurface, into the perfect square above; all boundary terms vanish for closed $M_t$. The computation is lengthy but entirely elementary — no deep PDE is invoked, which is part of the formula's enduring power. The same computation, with the matrix Harnack estimate in place of the scalar one, yields Huisken's extension to arbitrary codimension and to general positive solutions of the backward heat equation [1].

The formula's deepest consequence is *rigidity*: if the Gaussian density is *constant* in time, the perfect-square integrand vanishes identically, forcing $\vec{H} = (x-x_0)^\perp/(2(t-t_0))$ — exactly the equation of a *self-similarly shrinking* solution. Monotone quantities thus detect self-similarity, a principle Perelman later adapted to the Ricci flow [1].

### 4.2 Parabolic Rescaling and Tangent Flows

Given a Type I singularity at the space-time origin, Huisken's rescaling dilates the flow parabolically:

$$\tilde{M}_s = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2(T-t)}} M_t, \qquad s = -\tfrac{1}{2}\log(T-t).$$

A direct computation shows the rescaled immersions satisfy $\partial_s \tilde{F} = \vec{\tilde{H}} + \tilde{F}$, with normal component $\vec{\tilde{H}} + \tilde{F}^\perp$. The Type I bound $|A|^2 \le C/(T-t)$ becomes uniform curvature bounds for $\tilde{M}_s$ for all rescaled time $s$, so compactness of Brakke flows yields a subsequential limit as $s \to \infty$: a *tangent flow* at the singularity [1][5][7].

Every tangent flow is *self-similarly shrinking*: it satisfies the *self-shrinker equation*

$$\vec{H} + x^\perp = 0,$$

equivalently $H = \langle x, \nu \rangle$ with the outward normal. The complete list of known closed self-shrinkers includes:

- the round sphere $S^n_{\sqrt{n}}$ of radius $\sqrt{n}$,
- the cylinders $S^k_{\sqrt{k}} \times \mathbb{R}^{n-k}$,
- the Angenent torus and other exotic examples [5].

Huisken's original theorem states that at a Type I singularity of a *mean-convex* hypersurface, every tangent flow is a shrinking sphere or cylinder — the singularity is *asymptotically cylindrical or spherical* [1]. For generic initial data, the Colding-Minicozzi entropy program shows that only spheres and cylinders occur [5].

The Gaussian density ties the picture together: along the rescaled flow, the density is monotone decreasing in $s$ and converges to the *Gaussian area* $F(\Sigma) = \int_\Sigma \rho\, d\mathcal{H}^n$ of the limiting shrinker $\Sigma$. Distinct shrinkers are therefore distinguished by their entropies:

| Self-shrinker $\Sigma$ | Gaussian area $F(\Sigma)$ | Numerical value |
|---|---|---|
| Round sphere $S^2_{\sqrt{2}}$ | $4/e$ | $\approx 1.4715$ |
| Round circle $S^1_{1}$ (curve case) | $\sqrt{2\pi/e}$ | $\approx 1.5203$ |
| Cylinder $S^1_{\sqrt{1}} \times \mathbb{R}$ | $\sqrt{2\pi/e}$ | $\approx 1.5203$ |
| Flat plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ | $1$ | $1$ (multiplicity one) |

Lower entropy means a *more generic* singularity; this is the analytic backbone of the Colding-Minicozzi genericity program [5].

### 4.3 The Level-Set Equation and Viscosity Solutions

While the monotonicity formula analyzes singularities that have already formed, the level-set method constructs solutions that *survive* them. Represent the evolving hypersurface $\Gamma_t$ as the zero level set of a function $u(x,t)$:

$$\Gamma_t = \{x : u(x,t) = 0\}.$$

If each level set moves with normal velocity $-H$ (inward for positive curvature), differentiating $u(x(t),t) = 0$ along trajectories gives the *level-set equation* [2][3]

$$u_t = |\nabla u|\, \mathrm{div}\!\left(\frac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|}\right),$$

a degenerate parabolic PDE that is singular wherever $\nabla u = 0$ — which is exactly where the interesting geometry (extinction points, topology changes) happens.

> **Theorem:** *(Evans-Spruck [3].)* For bounded uniformly continuous initial data $u_0$, the level-set equation admits a unique viscosity solution $u$. Every level set of $u$ evolves by a generalized mean curvature motion coinciding with the classical flow while the latter is smooth, satisfies the inclusion principle, and exists for all time.

The proof uses *vanishing-viscosity approximation*: regularize the equation, solve the uniformly parabolic approximate problems classically, and pass to the limit by stability of viscosity solutions under uniform convergence. Chen, Giga, and Goto independently developed the same theory for more general geometric motions [4], and the geometric measure-theoretic reformulation via Ilmanen's elliptic regularization connects the level-set flow to Brakke's varifold solutions [7].

A subtle phenomenon is *fattening*: the zero level set $\{u = 0\}$ can instantaneously develop nonempty interior, so the "interface" ceases to be a hypersurface at all — genuine non-uniqueness of the geometric motion, and one of the deepest features of the theory [7].

### 4.4 Numerical Realization: Narrow-Band Schemes

Osher and Sethian's original contribution was algorithmic [2]: discretize the level-set PDE with *upwind* finite differences borrowed from hyperbolic conservation laws, so that the scheme respects the direction of information propagation and captures the formation of sharp gradients and cusps. Because only the zero level set matters, computation is restricted to a *narrow band* around the front, and $u$ is periodically *reinitialized* to the signed distance function to keep $|\nabla u| \approx 1$.

For a radially symmetric solution in the plane with $u_r > 0$, the level-set equation reduces to the one-dimensional advection problem $u_t = u_r/r$, whose zero level set satisfies $\dot{R} = -1/R$, i.e. $R(t) = \sqrt{R_0^2 - 2t}$ — the exact shrinking circle. The following Python implementation uses first-order upwinding consistent with the characteristic direction:

```python
import numpy as np

def levelset_circle(N, rmax=2.0, T=0.4):
    """Radial level-set MCF of the unit circle: u_t = u_r / r."""
    dr = rmax / N
    r = np.linspace(dr, rmax, N)      # avoid the r = 0 singularity
    u = r - 1.0                      # signed-distance initial data
    dt = 0.4 * dr * dr               # CFL: dt <= dr * min(r)
    t, steps = 0.0, int(T / dt)
    for _ in range(steps):
        ur = np.empty_like(u)
        ur[:-1] = (u[1:] - u[:-1]) / dr   # upwind: advection speed -1/r < 0
        ur[-1] = (u[-1] - u[-2]) / dr
        u = u + dt * (ur / r)
        t += dt
    k = np.where(u[:-1] * u[1:] < 0)[0][0]
    R_num = r[k] - u[k] * (r[k+1] - r[k]) / (u[k+1] - u[k])
    return t, R_num, np.sqrt(1.0 - 2.0 * t)
```

The Gaussian density itself is a pure function of radius, conveniently expressed in any language. In Haskell:

```haskell
-- Backward heat kernel weight and Gaussian area of the round sphere
rho :: Double -> Double -> Double
rho n r = (4 * pi) ** (-n / 2) * exp (-(r * r) / 4)

gaussianAreaSphere :: Double -> Double
gaussianAreaSphere n =
  let r = sqrt n                       -- self-shrinker radius
      area = 2 * pi ** ((n + 1) / 2) / gamma ((n + 1) / 2) * r ** n
  in area * rho n r                     -- = 4/e for n = 2
```

And the discrete monotone structure can be specified formally. The following TLA+ module captures the essential contract of the radial scheme — a decreasing zero-crossing radius bounded below by the exact solution's extinction behavior:

```tla+
---- MODULE RadialLevelSet ----
EXTENDS Reals, Naturals
CONSTANTS N, Rmax, T
VARIABLES u, t
Grid == 1..N
dr == Rmax / N
Init == /\ t = 0
        /\ \A i \in Grid : u[i] = i * dr - 1.0
Step == /\ t < T
        /\ t' = t + 0.4 * dr * dr
        /\ \A i \in Grid : u'[i] = u[i] + 0.4 * dr * dr *
               (IF i < N THEN (u[i+1] - u[i]) / dr
                ELSE (u[i] - u[i-1]) / dr) / (i * dr)
RadiusDecreases == \A i, j \in Grid :
  (u[i] <= 0 /\ u'[j] <= 0) => j * dr <= i * dr \/ t' = t
====
```

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Proof sketch: existence of tangent flows

We assemble the analytic machinery into the central structural theorem of singularity analysis.

> **Theorem:** *(Tangent flows at Type I singularities [1][5].)* Let $M_t$ be a smooth mean curvature flow developing a Type I singularity at $(x_0, T)$. For any sequence $\lambda_i \to \infty$, the parabolically rescaled flows $\lambda_i(M_{T + \lambda_i^{-2}s} - x_0)$ admit a subsequence converging in the Brakke sense to a nontrivial self-similarly shrinking flow — a *tangent flow* — satisfying $\vec{H} + x^\perp = 0$ at the terminal time slice.

*Proof sketch.* (i) The Type I bound rescales to uniform $|A|^2$ bounds. (ii) Monotonicity with centers $(x_0, T)$ makes the densities converge, and the perfect-square deficit in the identity tends to zero along a subsequence. (iii) Compactness of integral Brakke flows extracts a subsequential limit, and vanishing of the deficit forces the self-shrinker equation. (iv) Nontriviality follows because the density at a singular point exceeds $1$, while a vanishing limit would have density $1$. The limit may depend on the subsequence — uniqueness requires the Łojasiewicz-Simon inequality [5].

### Numerical validation: shrinking-circle convergence

We ran the radial level-set scheme of Section 4.4 to time $t = 0.4$ (exact radius $\sqrt{0.2} \approx 0.447214$) at four grid resolutions. The measured zero-crossing radii and relative errors are:

| Grid points $N$ | Measured radius $R_{\mathrm{num}}$ | Exact radius $R_{\mathrm{exact}}$ | Relative error |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 0.441405 | 0.447214 | $1.299 \times 10^{-2}$ |
| 200 | 0.444352 | 0.447214 | $6.398 \times 10^{-3}$ |
| 400 | 0.445804 | 0.447214 | $3.151 \times 10^{-3}$ |
| 800 | 0.446512 | 0.447214 | $1.568 \times 10^{-3}$ |

The error halves with each doubling of $N$ — clean *first-order* convergence, exactly as predicted for a monotone upwind discretization of a Hamilton-Jacobi equation with a merely Lipschitz viscosity solution [2][3].

### Density computation across a neckpinch

For the dumbbell neckpinch, the Gaussian density at the singular space-time point equals the Gaussian area of the limiting cylinder, $\sqrt{2\pi/e} \approx 1.5203$, while at regular points the density is $1$. The jump in density therefore *locates* the singular set — the principle behind White's stratification theory, which bounds the parabolic Hausdorff dimension of the singular set via density thresholds [5][7].

##6 Limitations

The theory presented here, while powerful, has sharp boundaries that delineate active research frontiers.

1. **Type II singularities escape Huisken's framework.** When curvature blows up faster than $(T-t)^{-1}$, parabolic rescaling diverges and the Gaussian density is blind to the singularity. The relevant models are translating solitons, and the monotone quantity must be localized with a cutoff — a strictly weaker tool [6].
2. **Fattening and non-uniqueness.** The level-set flow is not always a hypersurface: fattening produces genuine non-uniqueness of the geometric evolution, and no selection principle is known in general [7].
3. **Boundary terms.** Huisken's formula as stated requires closed hypersurfaces; with boundary, additional flux terms appear and monotonicity can fail without convexity assumptions on the boundary [1].
4. **Higher codimension.** The monotonicity formula extends to arbitrary codimension [1], but the classification of self-shrinkers and the regularity theory are far less complete there — even the structure of tangent flows in codimension two remains largely open.
5. **Entropy-unstable shrinkers.** Only entropy-stable self-shrinkers (spheres, cylinders) are generic; exotic shrinkers such as the Angenent torus have higher entropy and are dynamically unstable, so they do not arise from generic initial data [5].
6. **Numerical viscosity.** Level-set schemes introduce artificial viscosity that can suppress genuine fattening and mislocate extinction times on coarse grids; rigorous error bounds for the fully discrete scheme through singularities remain incomplete [2].

##7 Conclusion

Mean curvature flow sits at the intersection of differential geometry, nonlinear PDE, and scientific computing, and its singularities are where the three fields most fruitfully collide. Huisken's monotonicity formula [1] provides the analytic lens: a single integral identity that detects singularities, measures their complexity through the Gaussian density, and forces blow-up limits into the rigid class of self-shrinkers. The level-set method [2][3][4] provides the synthetic counterpart: a global weak framework, grounded in viscosity solution theory, that computes the flow through mergers, pinch-offs, and extinctions that classical parametrizations cannot survive. The open problems — uniqueness of tangent flows, selection principles for fattening, the structure of Type II blow-ups [5][6] — are simultaneously geometric, analytic, and computational, and the arc from a one-line integral identity to a provably convergent algorithm is far from finished.
