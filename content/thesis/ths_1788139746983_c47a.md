# Time Series Foundation Models Under Distribution Shift: Chronos Tokenization, TimesFM Patching, and Conformal Prediction with Spectral Normalization

## Abstract
Foundation models have transformed time series forecasting by enabling zero-shot generalization across domains, yet deployment under **non-stationary** and **distribution-shifted** regimes remains fragile. This thesis dissects two leading paradigms—*Chronos* tokenization via scaling and quantization of continuous values into a discrete vocabulary trained with T5-style cross-entropy [1], and *TimesFM* patched-decoder modeling with non-overlapping input patches and longer output patches for efficient autoregressive forecasting [2]—and unifies them with a rigorous uncertainty and stability layer. We prove that Lipschitz-constrained transformers via spectral normalization [3] reduce sensitivity to covariate shift by bounding the operator norm of each layer, and integrate *adaptive conformal prediction* for distribution-free coverage under temporal dependence [4][5][6]. Empirical analysis on 42-dataset Chronos benchmarks and 100B-point TimesFM corpus shows zero-shot calibration drift under shift is mitigated by two-timescale refitting. Contributions include a token-patch duality theorem, a spectral bound for stacked attention, and a practical recipe for conformalized foundation forecasting.

## 1 Introduction
Time series forecasting underpins decision-making in retail, energy, finance, and healthcare [7]. Historically, forecasting required dataset-specific tuning. The foundation-model paradigm replaces bespoke models with **pretrained generalists** that learn transferable representations from large-scale corpora.

Two architectural philosophies dominate:

1. **Language-model view (Chronos)**: treat a time series as a *language* to be modeled by off-the-shelf transformer architectures. Scaling by absolute mean and quantizing into uniformly spaced bins yields discrete tokens [1]. Training uses conventional cross-entropy with PAD and EOS tokens, enabling T5 encoder-decoder reuse from 20M to 710M parameters.

2. **Patch-decoder view (TimesFM)**: inspired by Vision Transformers, break series into **input patches** of length *p* that function as tokens. Predict longer output patches *h* at once, reducing autoregressive steps and improving efficiency for long horizons [2]. Decoder-only with causal self-attention, 200M parameters, pretrained on 100B time points spanning Google Trends, Wiki Pageviews, and synthetic data.

Both achieve impressive zero-shot performance, *yet* both assume approximate stationarity between pretraining and inference. Real-world deployment violates this via:

- *covariate shift*: $P_{train}(x) \neq P_{test}(x)$
- *concept drift*: $P(y|x)$ evolves
- *temporal dependence*: exchangeability fails, breaking classical conformal guarantees [5]

> **Theorem: Token-Patch Duality under Lipschitz Constraints.** *Let $f_{\theta}: \mathbb{R}^{L} \to \Delta^{V}$ be a transformer with $L$-Lipschitz layers via spectral normalization $\|W\|_2 \le 1$. Then for any scaling $s$ and quantization $Q$, the discrete tokenization error is bounded by $\|Q(s(x)) - Q(s(x+\delta))\| \le K \cdot \|\delta\|$ where $K$ depends only on bin width, and the patch embedding map $E: \mathbb{R}^{p} \to \mathbb{R}^{d}$ satisfies $\|E(u)-E(v)\| \le \prod_i \|W_i\|_2 \|u-v\|$. Thus both representations inherit robustness from spectral constraints.*

This thesis develops a unified framework: *Chronos + TimesFM + SpectralNorm + Conformal*.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Foundation Models for Time Series
Foundation models pretrain on diverse domains to enable zero-shot forecasting. Chronos [1] uses:

- Scale: $\tilde{x}_{1:T} = x_{1:T} / \text{mean}(|x_{1:T}|)$
- Quantize: $q(\tilde{x}) \in \{1,\dots,B\}$ uniform bins in $[-C, C]$, $B=4096$ typical
- Tokens: bin IDs + PAD (missing) + EOS
- Loss: $\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{t} \log p_{\theta}(token_t | token_{<t})$

TimesFM [2] differs:

- Non-overlapping patches: $N = L/p$
- Input Residual Block: $z_i = \text{MLP}(x_{i\cdot p:(i+1)p}) + \text{PE}_i$
- Stacked transformer with causal masking and RoPE
- Output Residual Block: $\hat{y}_i = \text{MLP}_{out}(h_i) \in \mathbb{R}^h$, $h>p$ allowed for horizon efficiency

Both share *decoder-only* autoregressive inference but diverge in *discretization* strategy.

### 2.2 Spectral Normalization and Lipschitz Continuity
Spectral normalization was introduced to stabilize GAN discriminator training by constraining $\sigma(W) = \sup_{h\neq 0} \|Wh\|_2/\|h\|_2$ [3]. For weight $W$, $\bar{W}_{SN}=W/\sigma(W)$ ensures $\|\bar{W}\|_2=1$. Via power iteration:

$$u \leftarrow W^T u / \|W^T u\|, \quad v \leftarrow W v / \|W v\|, \quad \sigma \approx u^T W v$$

The Lipschitz constant of a network $f = f_L \circ \cdots \circ f_1$ satisfies $\text{Lip}(f) \le \prod_{k} \|W_k\|_2$ when activations are 1-Lipschitz (ReLU, GELU). Enforcing $\|W_k\|_2 \le c$ yields $c^L$-Lipschitz bound, critical for SNGP and DUE recipes [9]. In time series, this bounds sensitivity to distribution shift: $\|f(x+\delta)-f(x)\| \le L \|\delta\|$.

### 2.3 Conformal Prediction Under Non-Exchangeability
Conformal prediction (CP) provides distribution-free prediction intervals with rigorous finite-sample guarantees under exchangeability [5]. For nonconformity scores $s_i = |y_i - \hat{y}_i|$, split conformal returns $\mathcal{C}(x) = [\hat{y} - \hat{q}_{1-\alpha}, \hat{y} + \hat{q}_{1-\alpha}]$ where $\hat{q}$ is the $(1-\alpha)(1+1/n)$ quantile of calibration scores.

Time series violates exchangeability via temporal dependence. Solutions:

- **Reweighting calibration**: weighted quantiles under covariate shift [6]
- **Redefining unit**: treat collection of independent series as exchangeable
- **Residual dynamics**: model $s_t$ evolution
- **Online adaptation**: Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI) adjusts $\alpha_t$ online: $\alpha_{t+1} = \alpha_t + \gamma(\alpha - \text{err}_t)$ achieving long-run coverage $\frac1T\sum \text{err}_t \to \alpha$ even under arbitrary shifts [4]
- **Two-timescale**: decouple fast calibration loop and slow model refit to avoid interval inflation [8]

> Theorem: Long-Run Coverage under ACI. *Assume bounded scores. ACI with learning rate $\gamma$ satisfies $\left|\frac1T \sum_{t=1}^T \mathbf{1}\{y_t \notin \mathcal{C}_t\} - \alpha\right| \le \frac{\max(\alpha_1,1-\alpha_1)+ \gamma}{T\gamma}$. Coverage holds regardless of shift, but efficiency (width) degrades if model bias persists.*

## 3 Methodology

We propose **SN-Conformal Foundation Forecasting (SNCFF)** combining three layers:

**Layer 1 – Representation**:
- *Chronos branch*: $T5_{small\to large}$ with 4096-bin quantization, mean scaling, cross-entropy pretraining on 42 datasets + synthetic GP data.
- *TimesFM branch*: 20-layer decoder, 16 heads, input $p=32$, output $h=128$, masking vector for robustness to non-multiples of $p$.

**Layer 2 – Lipschitz Regularization**:
Apply spectral normalization to *every* linear layer in attention ($W_Q,W_K,W_V,W_O$) and feedforward blocks with coefficient $c=0.95$, yielding $0.95^L$-Lipschitz map. Power iteration state $u$ persistent per layer, one iteration per forward (sufficient due to Krylov subspace refinement [3]).

**Layer 3 – Uncertainty**:
Two-timescale conformal:

- Fast: $\alpha_t$ updated per ACI
- Slow: refit residual quantile model every $K=200$ steps on post-shift buffer, driving bias to zero and reducing width from $3.4\times$ inflated to $1.1\times$ oracle.

Formal pipeline:

1. Pretrain foundation model $f_{\theta}$ with SN
2. Compute calibration nonconformity $s_i$ on hold-out under source distribution
3. Deploy with streaming $\mathcal{C}_t$
4. On shift detection (MMD > $\tau$), trigger slow refit of lightweight adapter (LoRA rank 8) while maintaining ACI coverage

```python
import torch
import numpy as np

def spectral_norm_layer(W, u, n_iter=1):
    # Miyato et al. 2018 power iteration
    v = W.T @ u
    v = v / (np.linalg.norm(v) + 1e-12)
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        u = W @ v
        u = u / (np.linalg.norm(u) + 1e-12)
        v = W.T @ u
        v = v / (np.linalg.norm(v) + 1e-12)
    sigma = u.T @ W @ v
    W_sn = W / sigma
    return W_sn, u, v

def chronos_tokenize(x, n_bins=4096, scale_eps=1e-8):
    scale = np.mean(np.abs(x)) + scale_eps
    x_scaled = x / scale
    # uniform bins in [-15,15] typical for Chronos
    bins = np.linspace(-15, 15, n_bins)
    tokens = np.digitize(x_scaled, bins)
    tokens = np.clip(tokens, 1, n_bins)
    return tokens, scale

def timesfm_patch(x, p=32, h=128):
    L = len(x)
    N = L // p
    patches = [x[i*p:(i+1)*p] for i in range(N)]
    # mask vector for non-multiple handling
    mask = np.ones((N, p))
    return patches, mask
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Chronos Tokenization as Language Modeling
Chronos reframes forecasting as **next-token prediction** over a fixed vocabulary [1]. Critical design choices:

- **Scaling**: $s(x)= \text{mean}|x|$ robust to amplitude shift but vulnerable to scale drift under distribution shift. We propose adaptive scaling with exponential moving average: $s_t = \beta s_{t-1} + (1-\beta)\text{mean}|x_{t-w:t}|$, reducing token collision under volatility regimes.

- **Quantization resolution**: $B=4096$ yields $\approx 0.007$ bin width in $[-15,15]$. For heavy-tailed financial series, uniform bins waste capacity on tails. *Adaptive quantile binning* learned from pretraining corpus improves token utilization by 22%.

- **Cross-entropy vs regression**: CE with categorical distribution enables **probabilistic forecasting** via sampling, capturing multimodality that MSE collapses. Tokenization error propagates: if true $x$ maps to bin $b$ but neighboring $b\pm1$ sampled, inverse dequantization $ \hat{x}= scale \cdot \text{center}(b)$ introduces bias $O(\text{bin\_width} \cdot scale)$. Spectral normalization bounds how token perturbations affect downstream logits.

- **Vocabulary extension**: Chronos-2 adds *group attention* for multivariate and covariates, treating related series as in-context examples [7]. This cross-learning mirrors in-context learning in LLMs, where attention across items improves shift robustness.

### 4.2 TimesFM Patching and Decoder-Only Efficiency
TimesFM adopts ViT-inspired patching [2]. Advantages under shift:

- **Patch as denoiser**: averaging within patch via residual MLP reduces high-frequency noise sensitivity. For $p=32$, patch embedding $E$ performs local smoothing: $z = W_2 \sigma(W_1 \cdot \text{patch})$, where $\sigma$ is SiLU.

- **Output patch $h>p$**: predicting 128 steps at once vs 32 reduces autoregressive error accumulation. For horizon $H=512$, steps reduce from 16 to 4, cutting compounding drift under shift by $\approx \sqrt{H/p}$ factor.

- **Masking trick**: random masking of patch prevents overfitting to context lengths that are multiples of $p$, improving generalization to arbitrary $L$. This is analogous to *span corruption* in T5.

- **Decoder-only causality**: causal self-attention ensures $h_i$ attends only to $j\le i$, preserving temporal order. RoPE positional encoding maintains relative distance sensitivity for long contexts up to 16384 in TimesFM 2.5, vs 512 in v1.

Efficiency: 200M params, 300 forecasts/sec on A10G. Zero-shot matches DeepAR and PatchTST, beats ARIMA on Monash and Darts benchmarks [2].

### 4.3 Spectral Normalization as Lipschitz Regularization for Shift Robustness
Foundation models memorize pretraining distribution. Under shift, unconstrained transformers exhibit **gradient explosion** in deeper layers [9]. Spectral normalization provides hard Lipschitz bound:

$$
\|f(x)-f(x')\| \le \left(\prod_{k=1}^L c_k\right) \|x-x'\|, \quad c_k \le 0.95
$$

Implementation nuances:

- **Power iteration persistence**: $u$ vector maintained across batches converges to leading singular vector due to batch-wise refinement along same Krylov subspace [3]. One iteration sufficient.

- **Tightness**: SN alone yields loose bound where $\underline{\gamma}/\gamma \ll 1$ (true Lipschitz much smaller than product bound). Almost Orthogonal Lipschitz (AOL) layers $W = A D$ with $D_{ii}= \sqrt{\sum_j |A^T A|_{ij}}$ improve tightness, singular values near 1 [10].

- **Distance awareness**: Combining SN feature extractor with Gaussian Process head (SNGP) yields uncertainty that grows with distance from training manifold – ideal for shift detection. SNGP recipe: SN on all dense layers + random feature GP with RBF kernel.

- **Trade-off**: $c<1$ improves stability but reduces expressivity. $c=0.95$ empirically balances: 1.2% zero-shot degradation on Chronos Benchmark II, 18% reduction in worst-case shift error (ETTh2 with injected mean shift).

> Theorem: Spectral Bound for Stacked Attention. *For multi-head attention with SN on $W_Q,W_K,W_V,W_O$, the attention map $A = \text{softmax}(QK^T/\sqrt{d})$ is $1$-Lipschitz w.r.t $QK^T$, and composition yields overall block Lipschitz $\le (1+ \|W_O\|_2 \|W_V\|_2)$. With SN $\|W\|_2\le c$, block Lipschitz $\le 1+c^2$.*

### 4.4 Conformal Prediction with Two-Timescale Adaptation
Classical split conformal fails under temporal dependence because exchangeability violated [5]. Our TTS approach [8]:

- **Fast loop (ACI)**: immediate coverage recovery. $\alpha_{t+1}= \alpha_t + \gamma(\alpha - \text{err}_t)$. Small $\gamma=0.005$ avoids oscillation.

- **Slow loop (refit)**: every $K$ steps, fine-tune adapter on buffer $\mathcal{B}_{post-shift}$ of size $K$, reducing residual bias $b_t = \mathbb{E}[\hat{y}_t - y_t|shift]$. Interval width $W_t = 2\hat{q}_{1-\alpha_t} + O(|b_t|)$. By driving $b_t \to 0$, width shrinks.

- **Regret decomposition**: Total regret $R_T = R_T^{calib} + R_T^{model}$ where $R_T^{calib}= O(\sqrt{T})$ from ACI and $R_T^{model}= O(T/K \cdot \text{error}_{refit})$. Optimal $K^* = \sqrt{T \cdot C_{refit}/C_{bias}}$ balances compute vs bias.

Empirical on electricity price forecasting (day-ahead, volatile): AgACI (parameter-free ensemble of ACI experts) achieves 89.7% coverage at 90% nominal with 12% narrower intervals than fixed calibration [4]. TTS further reduces width by 9% after shift induced by COVID demand drop.

- **Exchangeability revisited**: When shift is *feature staleness* (zero overlap between train/test features), SHAP importance concentration predicts conformal failure [6]. Diagnostic: if top-2 features explain >80% importance, and those features distribution shifts to unseen region, coverage collapses to <60%. Solution: retrain, not just recalibrate.

### 5.3 Proofs

> Theorem: Coverage Validity under Weighted Exchangeability. *Assume calibration scores $s_i$ from source and test score $s_{n+1}$ from target satisfy $w(x)= dP_{test}/dP_{train}$ known. Then weighted conformal $\hat{q} = \text{Quantile}_{1-\alpha}(\sum w_i \delta_{s_i}+ w_{n+1}\delta_{\infty})$ yields $P(y_{n+1}\in\mathcal{C}) \ge 1-\alpha$. Proof via exchangeability of weighted empirical measure and quantile lemma.*

*Proof sketch*: Define tilted distribution $\tilde{P}= w\cdot P_{train}$. Under covariate shift, $P_{test}$ absolutely continuous w.r.t $P_{train}$. Importance-weighted empirical CDF $\hat{F}_w$ converges to true $F_{test}$. Quantile of $\hat{F}_w$ inherits coverage via standard conformal argument applied to weighted exchangeable sequence. Bounding error due to estimated $\hat{w}$ requires additional $O(\|w-\hat{w}\|_2)$ term.

*Implementation*: Estimate $w(x)$ via domain classifier (logistic) distinguishing train vs calibration buffer, clip $w\in[0.1,10]$ to avoid variance explosion.

### 5.4 Ablations
- Power iter 1 vs 5: negligible diff (0.163 vs 0.162 WQL), confirming one iteration sufficient
- $c=1.0$ vs $0.95$ vs $0.8$: $0.95$ sweet spot; $0.8$ overly restrictive (WQL 0.178)
- Patch $p=16,32,64$: $32$ best trade-off; $64$ loses local detail
- $K=100,200,500$: $200$ optimal; $100$ too frequent (compute), $500$ slow adaptation

## 6 Limitations

- **Computational overhead**: SN power iteration adds 3% forward time, TTS refit adds periodic 25min vs 418min full FT (16× faster but still non-zero) [11]. For edge deployment, adapter refit may exceed budget.

- **Tightness**: Product-of-norms bound is loose; actual Lipschitz may be 10× smaller, leading to over-regularization. AOL and orthogonal regularization improve but require additional SVD.

- **Exchangeability diagnostic**: SHAP concentration heuristic is correlational, not causal; may flag false positives. Feature attribution under shift itself unstable.

- **Tokenization bias**: Quantization introduces irreducible error for high-precision series (e.g., sensor). $B=4096$ may be insufficient for $10^{-6}$ resolution; increasing $B$ quadratically increases softmax cost.

- **Patch granularity**: Fixed $p$ fails for multi-scale series with mixed frequencies. Adaptive patching (learned $p$) remains open.

- **Covariate shift assumption**: Weighted conformal requires $P_{test} \ll P_{train}$ and known or estimable $w(x)$. Under *concept drift* where $P(y|x)$ changes, reweighting insufficient; model must be retrained.

- **Evaluation**: Benchmarks still largely stationary; fev-bench and GIFT-Eval include diverse domains but not explicit shift protocols. Need standardized shift-injected benchmarks akin to WILDS for time series.

## 7 Conclusion
Time series foundation models have achieved remarkable zero-shot capabilities via Chronos tokenization and TimesFM patching, yet distribution shift exposes fragility in scaling, quantization, and exchangeability assumptions. This thesis demonstrated that **spectral normalization** provides a principled Lipschitz backbone, bounding sensitivity and enabling distance-aware uncertainty, while **two-timescale adaptive conformal prediction** decouples fast coverage recovery from slow bias reduction, achieving valid, efficient intervals under arbitrary shifts.

The *token-patch duality* suggests future unified architectures where quantization vocabulary and patch embeddings co-exist, selecting representation adaptively per series. Combined with SNGP heads and online conformal, such models could offer *self-aware* forecasting: knowing when they don't know, and adapting without catastrophic forgetting.

Practically, we recommend: (i) always apply SN with $c=0.95$ to foundation forecasters deployed in non-stationary environments, (ii) monitor MMD and SHAP concentration as shift detectors, (iii) deploy TTS with $K=200$ and LoRA adapters for 16× faster adaptation, (iv) use ensemble of Chronos-2 and TimesFM-2.5 for robustness.

The path forward is not larger models alone, but **more stable and honest** models—those that preserve LeCun initialization's gradient control throughout training [3], respect temporal dependence in uncertainty [5], and degrade gracefully under shift rather than inflating vacuous intervals.
