# From Naiad to Differential Dataflow: Partially Ordered Time, Signed Differences, and Iterative Incremental Computation in Rust

## Abstract

Differential dataflow is a computational model, and a Rust implementation built atop timely dataflow, that unifies iterative and incremental data-parallel computation by replacing the total order on processing time with a *partial order* of logical timestamps. Collections are lifted to multisets of *differences*: signed, timestamped updates whose consolidation reconstructs state exactly once every timestamp's differences are known. Because timestamps admit incomparable elements—e.g., the product order on `(epoch, iteration)` pairs—work for distinct epochs and iterations executes concurrently and independently, while iteration becomes the fixed-point closure of differences propagated through a cyclic dataflow graph with explicit ingress, egress, and feedback vertices. Indexed shared state, called *arrangements*, lets many operators and even many dataflows reuse one maintained trace, and differential joins exploit these traces together with leapfrog-triejoin-style plans to approach worst-case optimal multi-way joins incrementally. This thesis develops the model's semantics, its difference calculus, the fixed-point interpretation of iteration, arrangements, join planning, implementation architecture, empirical behavior, and its relation to Spark Structured Streaming and Flink, and closes with limitations and open problems.

## 1. Introduction

Data-parallel systems have historically forced a choice. Batch processors offered high throughput but no incrementality; stream processors delivered low latency but could not express iteration; iterative engines descended from Pregel supported fixed-point graph algorithms but recomputed from scratch when inputs changed. The motivating workload of the Naiad project at Microsoft Research Silicon Valley—real-time graph analytics over a changing social graph—required all three properties simultaneously: the throughput of batch, the latency of streaming, and the expressiveness of iteration [1][2].

The breakthrough of McSherry, Isaacs, Isard, and Murray was to generalize *time* in a dataflow [2]. In classical incremental computation, time is totally ordered: input batch 1 precedes batch 2, and state for batch 2 is expressed relative to batch 1. Differential dataflow instead orders time *partially*. A timestamp may be a pair `(epoch, iteration)` under the product order, where `(e₁, i₁) ≤ (e₂, i₂)` only if `e₁ ≤ e₂` **and** `i₁ ≤ i₂`. Thus `(0, 5)` and `(1, 0)` are incomparable: the fifth iteration of epoch 0 and the zeroth iteration of epoch 1 are logically independent and may be computed concurrently. This single generalization collapses the artificial distinction between "batch," "streaming," and "iterative" processing into one model.

The second pillar is the *difference calculus*. A collection `C` at time `t` is the sum of its differences, `C_t = Σ_{s≤t} δC_s`, where each `δC_s` is a multiset of records with signed integer multiplicities (`+1` insertion, `−1` retraction) summed over the downset of `t`. Linear operators—`map`, `filter`, `concat`—distribute over differences directly; stateful operators such as joins and iteration maintain the differences they need and no more. Iteration is repeatedly applying the dataflow's operators to differences until quiescence: a fixed point over multisets, computed incrementally.

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the lineage from MapReduce and Dryad through Naiad's timely dataflow. Section 3 formalizes the methodology: partially ordered timestamps, the difference representation, and the fixed-point semantics. Section 4 develops five deep topics: (4.1) the product order and the *could-result-in* relation, (4.2) the algebra of signed differences, (4.3) iteration as fixed points, (4.4) arrangements as shared indexed state, and (4.5) differential joins and the worst-case optimal join connection. Section 5 presents empirical results and proofs, Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes with a comparison to Spark Structured Streaming and Apache Flink.

## 2. Background

### From batch to timely dataflow

Naiad, presented at SOSP 2013 [1], introduced *timely dataflow*: a distributed data-parallel model in which every message carries a logical timestamp drawn from a partially ordered set, and vertices receive explicit *notifications* when all messages bearing a given timestamp have been delivered. The runtime tracks progress through a distributed protocol over *pointstamps*—(location, timestamp) pairs. This generalizes both barrier synchronization and watermarks: watermarks are the special case of a total order, where completeness of `t` implies completeness of everything before it.

Naiad's graphs are *cyclic*: structured loops contain ingress, egress, and feedback vertices. The ingress vertex extends a timestamp with a new loop counter initialized to zero; the feedback vertex increments the innermost counter; the egress vertex strips it. Timestamps thus take the form `(e, c₁, …, cₖ)` for `k` nested loops, ordered by the product of the natural order on the epoch `e` and the lexicographic order on counter vectors [1]. This structure guarantees that timestamps only advance "forward" through cycles, so notifications remain well-defined even with feedback.

### The CIDR 2013 differential model

The companion CIDR 2013 paper [2] introduced *differential computation* as the programming model atop timely dataflow. Its headline result was practical: algorithms previously considered intractable to maintain incrementally—strongly connected components, for instance—became straightforward compositions of differential operators. The modern incarnation is a from-scratch Rust implementation by Frank McSherry on the timely-dataflow runtime, published as the open-source `differential-dataflow` crate [5]. It is not a port but a re-founding: Rust's ownership discipline makes the aggressive memory reuse of traces and arrangements safe, and the crate exposes differential collections as a library embedded in arbitrary timely dataflows rather than as a closed system.

## 3. Methodology

We study the model through three lenses: (i) the order-theoretic semantics of logical time [6]; (ii) the algebraic treatment of collections as modules over ℤ, yielding the difference calculus; and (iii) the implementation architecture of the Rust crate, documented in its mdbook [5][7]. We validate claims against the primary papers [1][2], the formal semantics [6], the leapfrog triejoin analysis [4], and the implementation's documentation [5][7].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Partially Ordered Logical Timestamps and the Product Order

The primitive move of timely dataflow is to let the timestamp type `T` be any partially ordered set, and to derive from the graph structure a *could-result-in* relation: message `m₁` at time `t₁` could result in message `m₂` at time `t₂` only if `t₁ ≤ t₂`. Progress tracking then reduces to knowing, for each pointstamp, the minimal timestamps of outstanding work that could still produce output at or before a given time [1][6].

For a dataflow with one loop, timestamps are pairs `(e, i) ∈ ℕ × ℕ` under the **product order**: `(e₁, i₁) ≤ (e₂, i₂)` iff `e₁ ≤ e₂` and `i₁ ≤ i₂`. The product order is the least order consistent with the graph's causality: an event in epoch `e` at iteration `i` can influence epoch `e' ≥ e` and iteration `i' ≥ i`, but never an earlier epoch or iteration. The crucial consequence is **incomparability**: `(0, 3)` and `(1, 0)` are unrelated, so the runtime may process iteration 3 of the old epoch concurrently with iteration 0 of the new epoch. In a total order this concurrency would be forbidden; here it is the source of both pipeline parallelism across epochs and the ability to retract an old epoch's iteration while a new one proceeds.

Nesting generalizes naturally: for `k` loops the timestamp is `(e, c₁, …, cₖ)` with the product of ℕ and the lexicographic order on counter sequences [1]. Differential dataflow's Rust API exposes this through `Product<Outer, Inner>` timestamp types, and user code can stack `iterate` scopes arbitrarily.

| System | Time model | Iteration | Incrementality |
|---|---|---|---|
| MapReduce / Spark RDD | total (batch id) | driver-side loop, recompute | none / lineage replay |
| Spark Structured Streaming | total (watermark) | limited (stateful flatMap) | micro-batch deltas |
| Flink DataStream | total (watermark) | `IterativeStream`, bulk/sync | changelog streams |
| Naiad / timely dataflow | **partial** (product order) | nested fixed points, async | pointstamp-tracked |
| Differential dataflow | **partial** (product order) | fixed points over differences | signed multisets |

This is the safety property underlying `NotifyAt`/`OnNotify` in Naiad [1] and `probe`/`frontier` in timely dataflow: progress is a property of the *downset* of `t`, not of a global clock.

### 4.2 Differences as Multisets with Signed Multiplicities

A differential *collection* `C` of records of type `D` is a function from `(D, T)` to ℤ: each record `d` at each time `t` has an integer multiplicity, with contents `C_t(d) = Σ_{s≤t} δC_s(d)` over the downset of `t`. Inserting a record is `+1`; deleting it is `−1`; a retraction exactly cancels a prior insertion, and consolidation annihilates equal `(d, t)` pairs. Because multiplicities are signed, *updates are symmetric*: there is no distinguished "old" and "new" state, only a multiset of changes indexed by partial time.

The power of this representation is that **linear operators commute with differencing**: `map`, `filter`, `concat`, and `negate` apply directly to each difference with no accumulated state. Only *nonlinear* operators—`join`, `group`/`reduce`, `count`, `distinct`, `iterate`—must maintain state, and that state is itself a multiset of differences (a *trace*), indexed by time so the operator can answer: "given the new differences at time `t`, which output differences at times `≥ t` must change?"

Consider maintaining a count. The `count` operator maintains the trace of its *input's* differences and, for each input difference batch at time `t`, recomputes only the affected keys' output differences, emitting `(key, new_count, +1, t)` and `(key, old_count, −1, t)`. Retractions flow through the identical code path as insertions—negation is just multiplication by `−1`—which is why "delete" requires no special logic anywhere in the model.

```rust
// Canonical differential dataflow: out-degree distribution of a graph.
// Each operator acts on differences; only count/join maintain traces.
let out_degree = edges
    .map(|(src, _dst)| src)   // linear: applied to each difference
    .count()                  // non-linear: maintains input trace
    .map(|(_src, deg)| deg)   // linear
    .count();                 // non-linear
```

### 4.3 Iterative Incremental Computation as Fixed Points over Differences

Iteration in differential dataflow is expressed with the `iterate` operator: the least fixed-point of `X = C₀ ∪ F(X)` for a seed collection `C₀` and monotone step `F`, computed *on differences at increasing iteration coordinates*.Concretely, entering an `iterate` scope appends a new inner timestamp coordinate; the ingress vertex maps `(outer)` to `(outer, 0)`; each pass through feedback increments the inner counter; egress strips it. The step function `F` is applied to differences at `(o, i)` to produce differences at `(o, i+1)`, and the loop terminates when an iteration produces the empty difference multiset—detected via timely notifications, since the dataflow knows exactly when time `(o, i)` is complete. Because different `outer` times are incomparable, iterations for distinct epochs interleave freely: a new input batch can begin iterating while the old one still converges, and a retraction in the old batch propagates through *its own* iteration coordinates without disturbing the new one.

> **Theorem (Incremental fixed points).** *Let `F` be a monotone operator on collections and `C, C'` two input collections differing by `Δ = C' − C` (as multisets). The fixed point `lfp(F, C')` can be computed from `lfp(F, C)` by propagating only the differences induced by `Δ` through the iteration coordinates; differences at incomparable times never interact.*

This is the formal content of "differential" iteration: the system never recomputes the fixed point from scratch, and—more subtly—never re-examines iterations unaffected by the change. A single-edge insertion into a graph whose SCC decomposition is maintained typically disturbs only a handful of iteration coordinates; the rest of the trace is reused verbatim. Classical iterative systems (Pregel, GraphX) lack this property because their time model cannot distinguish "iteration 5 of the old graph" from "iteration 5 of the new graph": both are just round 5.

In a reachability query (`edges.iterate(...)` joining a frontier with the edge relation, unioned with seeds, then `distinct`), each iteration's join sees only the *newly reached* nodes as differences; `distinct` suppresses re-derivation. When an edge is retracted, negative differences propagate and `distinct`'s trace determines which nodes lose all derivations—incremental deletion in a recursive query, the operation that defeats most streaming engines.

### 4.4 Arrangements: Indexed Shared State

Every non-linear operator converts its input stream of differences into an indexed representation—a *trace*. Differential dataflow elevates this into a first-class abstraction: the **arrangement** [7], a shared, indexed trace of a collection's differences that can be consumed by many operators and even many dataflows.

```rust
// Arrange once, join many times: the trace is built a single time.
let knows = knows.to_collection(scope).arrange_by_key();
query.join_core(&knows, |x, q, y| Some((*y, (*x, *q))))
     .join_core(&knows, |y, (x, q), z| Some((*q, (*x, *y, *z))));
```

Without arrangements, each `join` would independently index `knows`; with `arrange_by_key`, the index is built once and shared by both joins [7]. The savings compound across dataflows: an arrangement's `trace` handle can be exported from one dataflow and imported into another, so a large, slowly changing relation is indexed once per worker and then probed by an arbitrary number of ad-hoc queries at "almost no additional cost" [7].

Implementation-wise, a trace is a log-structured merge (LSM) structure: batches of differences arrive as immutable, sorted, compacted runs, and merging bounds the number of runs logarithmically. Each batch carries a *frontier*—the lower bound of times it may still be asked about—so the trace can consolidate differences for times no reader can distinguish. This is the physical counterpart of §4.2's claim: times that have become indistinguishable may have their differences summed. The Rust implementation exploits this aggressively: `Trace` implementations are generic over key/value layouts, and timely's ownership model lets batches be shared by reference count across operators without copying.

Arrangements also change the complexity story of iterative algorithms. In the reachability example, the `edges` collection is arranged once; every iteration's join probes the same trace rather than re-indexing the edge set per round. For graph algorithms over static topology with changing queries—or changing topology with static queries—this is the difference between `O(iterations × |E|)` indexing work and `O(|E| log |E|)` once.

### 4.5 Differential Joins and the Worst-Case Optimal Join Connection

The differential `join` is the workhorse non-linear operator, and its design connects directly to the worst-case optimal join literature. A binary equi-join of collections `A` and `B` must, upon receiving differences `δA_t`, produce exactly the output differences implied by `δA_t ⋈ B_{≤t}`—where `B_{≤t}` is `B`'s accumulation over the downset of `t`, served from its trace—and symmetrically for `δB_t`. The operator's work for a batch of differences is proportional to the size of the *change in the output*, not the inputs: the join analogue of incremental view maintenance's delta rules, generalized to partially ordered time.

For multi-way joins, differential dataflow's traces provide precisely the index structure that worst-case optimal join (WCOJ) algorithms need. Leapfrog triejoin [4] evaluates a full conjunctive query by intersecting sorted tries attribute-by-attribute, achieving runtime `O(Q(n) log M(n))` where `Q(n)` is the AGM bound—the maximum output size over instances of bounded input size—rather than the potentially quadratic cost of binary join plans on cyclic queries like triangles (`O(N^{3/2})` instead of `O(N²)`). The trie presentation leapfrog triejoin requires is exactly what an arrangement provides: sorted, indexed access to a relation by successive key attributes, with seeks. Differential dataflow does not ship a full leapfrog-triejoin planner, but its architecture is WCOJ-ready: multi-way differential joins can be implemented as leapfrog intersections over arranged traces, inheriting the AGM-bound runtime *per batch of differences*. The incremental payoff is sharp: maintaining triangle counts under edge updates costs work proportional to the AGM bound of the *delta* query, not the full query.

> **Theorem (Veldhuizen [4], paraphrased).** *Leapfrog triejoin computes a full conjunctive query `Q` in time `O(Q(n) log M(n))`, where `Q(n)` is the largest result size over the instance family and `M(n)` the largest relation size—i.e., it is worst-case optimal up to a log factor.*

The differential reading adds: because differences at incomparable times are independent, the delta queries for concurrent epochs each enjoy this bound separately, and their traces are shared rather than rebuilt.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Naiad's original evaluation** [1] demonstrated the model's practicality: on a 64-node cluster, Naiad outperformed general-purpose batch processors and often beat specialized asynchronous systems offering weaker guarantees. Iterative graph computations achieved sub-millisecond iteration latencies, with median notification latency around 750 µs—numbers that made interactive queries over changing graphs feasible [1].

**Differential iteration benchmarks.** The Rust implementation's `scc-bench` suite reproduces the headline qualitative result: strongly connected components maintained incrementally under edge updates, where per-update latency is typically orders of magnitude below from-scratch recomputation, because only the affected iteration coordinates are re-executed (§4.3).

**Proof sketches.** Three properties carry the model's correctness; full proofs appear in [1][6] and we sketch the arguments:

1. *Notification safety.* Vertices may only emit at times `t' ≥ t` when invoked at `t` [1]; hence the could-result-in relation respects the timestamp order, and the progress tracker's completeness claims are sound. Formalized in [6].
2. *Difference reconstruction.* By induction over the partial order, `C_t = Σ_{s≤t} δC_s` holds for every collection: linear operators distribute, and non-linear operators emit exactly the output differences implied by their input traces' downsets. Consolidation is the physical realization of the sum.
3. *Fixed-point convergence.* Within an `iterate` scope, feedback increments the inner coordinate, so times strictly increase along any cycle; any chain generated by finite differences reaches quiescence when some iteration yields empty differences. Monotonicity of `F` then gives the least fixed point by Kleene's theorem, applied per outer time independently.

**Comparison with Spark Structured Streaming and Flink.** Both systems order time totally (event-time watermarks) and express iteration awkwardly: Structured Streaming supports only limited stateful iteration; Flink's `IterativeStream` implements bulk/synchronous iterations without differential semantics—each iteration reprocesses the full working set, and retractions through iterations are unsupported. Differential dataflow's advantages are therefore (a) *asynchronous* iteration coordinates needing no global barrier, (b) retraction-safe recursion via signed differences, and (c) shared arrangements amortizing index construction. The price is the programming model: users write dataflows against difference semantics, whereas Spark/Flink offer SQL/table APIs with optimizer support that differential dataflow lacks.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**Memory footprint of traces.** Every non-linear operator retains its input's differences for as long as any reader's frontier may need them. For high-churn collections with slow-advancing frontiers, traces grow without bound; the LSM merge discipline bounds the *number* of batches, not their total size. There is no principled spilling to disk in the open-source crate—working sets are assumed to fit in aggregate RAM, exactly Naiad's original assumption [1].

**No cost-based optimizer.** Differential dataflow is a library, not a query engine: join order, arrangement choice, and iteration structure are the programmer's responsibility. A SQL frontend with AGM-aware, delta-aware planning remains an open engineering problem, though the trace APIs were designed to admit it.

**Frontier management burden.** Correct, prompt frontier advancement is manual [7]. A stalled input frontier pins every downstream trace's compaction, causing silent memory growth—a notorious operational footgun that total-order systems avoid via automatic watermark heuristics.

**Expressiveness boundaries.** Non-monotone recursion (negation or aggregation inside iteration) is restricted: the difference calculus handles it only where the semantics remain well-defined over multisets, and the crate offers no stratification analysis. Probabilistic and approximate operators (sampling, sketching) fit poorly with exact signed multiplicities.

**Fault tolerance.** Naiad proposed checkpoint/restore [1]; the Rust timely runtime supports state capture, but exactly-once end-to-end semantics comparable to Flink's checkpointing are not turnkey in the differential crate.

**Open problems.** (1) A formal cost semantics predicting per-delta work from AGM bounds of delta queries; (2) automatic arrangement selection and lifetime analysis; (3) worst-case optimal *incremental* multi-way joins with proven delta bounds; (4) compilation of recursive SQL/Datalog to differential dataflows with optimizer-chosen timestamps.

## 7. Conclusion

Differential dataflow's enduring idea is small enough to state in one sentence—*order time partially, compute with signed differences*—and rich enough to have reorganized how we think about incremental iteration. From Naiad's SOSP 2013 debut [1] through the CIDR differential model [2] to the current Rust implementation [5], the trajectory shows a consistent bet: exposing logical time's true partial order, rather than flattening it into batches or watermarks, unlocks concurrency and incrementality that total-order systems must simulate at far greater cost. Arrangements turned an implementation necessity (indexed traces) into the model's most reusable asset, and the join architecture's affinity with leapfrog triejoin [4] points toward a future where incremental worst-case optimal multi-way joins are routine. Against Spark Structured Streaming and Flink, differential dataflow trades SQL optimizers and managed watermarks for a sharper instrument: exact, retraction-safe, asynchronous fixed points over differences. Its limitations—memory-hungry traces, manual frontiers, no optimizer—are engineering debts against a semantic foundation that has proven unusually durable.
