# Hash-Based Post-Quantum Signatures: Merkle Trees, Winternitz One-Time Signatures, and the SPHINCS+ Stateless Construction

## Abstract

Hash-based digital signatures offer the most conservative answer to the quantum cryptanalysis threat: their unforgeability reduces to nothing more than the second-preimage resistance and pseudorandomness of the underlying hash function, with no reliance on lattices, codes, multivariate equations, or isogenies. This thesis reconstructs the full intellectual arc of the field, from Lamport's 1979 one-time signatures and Winternitz's chain-based compression, through Merkle's tree authentication and Hülsing's WOTS+ chaining function, to the stateful XMSS construction standardized in RFC 8391 and finally the stateless SPHINCS+ scheme standardized by NIST as FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA). We give precise algorithmic descriptions of Winternitz chains, Merkle authentication paths, the FORS few-time signature, and the hypertree composition that binds them together; we derive the parameter sets and the signature-size formula governing the twelve SLH-DSA instances; and we survey the tight security reductions and the empirical performance landscape. We close with an honest appraisal of the costs — multi-kilobyte signatures and heavyweight signing — that position hash-based signatures as a trust anchor rather than a universal replacement.

##1 Intro

The arrival of Shor's polynomial-time quantum algorithm for integer factorization and discrete logarithms placed every widely deployed public-key signature scheme — RSA, DSA, ECDSA, EdDSA — on a cryptanalytic death watch [8]. The NIST post-quantum cryptography standardization process, launched in 2016, concluded its signature track by standardizing ML-DSA (a lattice scheme), and, crucially for this thesis, **SLH-DSA**, a *stateless hash-based* signature scheme derived from SPHINCS+, as FIPS 205 in August 2024 [1]. The inclusion of a hash-based scheme was not an accident of committee politics: among all post-quantum proposals, hash-based signatures rest on the weakest and best-understood hardness assumptions in cryptography.

The core idea is almost fifty years old. In 1979, Leslie Lamport showed that a one-way function alone suffices to build a one-time digital signature [7]; in the same year, Ralph Merkle showed how to compress an arbitrary number of one-time public keys into a single short public key using a binary hash tree. What took four decades was turning these elegant ideas into a *stateless*, standardized, misuse-resistant scheme with acceptable sizes — a journey passing through the Winternitz one-time signature, Hülsing's WOTS+ variant [5], the stateful XMSS scheme of RFC 8391 [3], the original SPHINCS construction [4], and finally SPHINCS+ [2].

##2 Background

### From Lamport to Winternitz

Lamport's one-time signature (OTS) is disarmingly simple. To sign an *n*-bit message, the signer generates *2n* random secret values *x(i, 0)* and *x(i, 1)*, publishes their images *y(i, b) = H(x(i, b))* under a one-way function *H*, and signs message bit *m_i* by revealing *x(i, m_i)* [7]. Verification checks the hash. The scheme is secure as long as *H* is preimage-resistant — but each key signs exactly one message, and keys are enormous: *2n* hash outputs per message.

The Winternitz OTS, also dating to 1979, compresses this dramatically. Instead of one secret per bit, the *n*-bit digest is split into blocks of *lg w* bits for a Winternitz parameter *w*; each block value *b_i*, ranging from 0 to *w − 1*, is signed by iterating a hash chain exactly *b_i* times starting from a secret seed. Because a chain can always be iterated *forward* but never backward, a checksum over the values *w − 1 − b_i* is appended and signed the same way, blocking any forgery that raises a block. The result needs roughly *n / lg w* chain endpoints instead of *2n* secrets.

> **Theorem (informal, Winternitz OTS):** If the hash function is preimage-resistant, the Winternitz OTS is existentially unforgeable under a single chosen-message query, provided each key is used at most once.

### Merkle trees and many-time signatures

Merkle's 1979 insight converts any OTS into a many-time scheme: generate *2^h* OTS key pairs, hash each public key into a leaf, and build a binary tree by hashing pairs of children up to a single root, which becomes the many-time public key [7]. A signature then consists of one OTS signature plus the *h* sibling nodes on the path from the leaf to the root — the *authentication path* — allowing the verifier to recompute the root. This is the direct ancestor of every modern hash-based scheme.

### WOTS+ and XMSS

Hülsing's WOTS+ (2013) strengthened the Winternitz design in two ways that matter enormously for proofs: the chaining function *c^i* applies a keyed, randomized hash at each step, XORing a fresh pseudorandom bitmask before every iteration, and the security reduction targets *second-preimage resistance* of the hash function family rather than the stronger pseudorandomness properties earlier proofs needed [5]. WOTS+ also shrinks signatures, and its parameters — the security parameter *n* and Winternitz parameter *w* chosen from the set containing 4 and 16 — became the standard vocabulary of the field.

XMSS, standardized as RFC 8391 in May 2018, builds a practical many-time scheme on WOTS+ and Merkle trees [3]. It is *stateful*: the private key carries an index into the *2^h* leaves, incremented on every signature, and reusing an index destroys security. XMSS introduced the BDS tree-traversal algorithm for computing authentication paths in logarithmic space, pseudorandom derivation of all WOTS+ keys from a single seed, and bitmasks throughout — the full engineering discipline that SPHINCS+ later inherited. The statefulness, however, is a deployment hazard: virtual-machine snapshots, backups, and cloned signers can silently rewind the index and cause catastrophic key reuse [3].

### SPHINCS and SPHINCS+

The original SPHINCS (Eurocrypt 2015) eliminated state altogether: instead of assigning each message a fresh leaf by index, it selects a leaf *pseudorandomly* from a *hypertree* — a tree of XMSS trees — and signs with a *few-time* signature (HORST) at the bottom, so that occasional leaf collisions degrade security only gracefully [4]. Signatures were large (around 41 kB), but the scheme proved statelessness was practical.

SPHINCS+, submitted to the NIST process, refined every component: FORS replaced HORST as the few-time signature, WOTS+ replaced WOTS throughout, and a tweakable-hash abstraction with rigorous domain separation unified the design [2]. Selected by NIST in 2022 and standardized as FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) in 2024, it is now the conservative, assumption-minimal pillar of the post-quantum signature portfolio [1].

##3 Methodology

All modern hash-based constructions in this thesis are built from a small set of *tweakable* hash functions — hash functions taking an explicit domain-separation tweak alongside their input:

- ***F***: the chaining function used inside WOTS+ hash chains;
- ***H***: the compression function combining two child nodes into a parent;
- ***T_len***: compresses a WOTS+ public key's *len* chain endpoints into one leaf value;
- ***PRF***: derives secret WOTS+ and FORS key material pseudorandomly from the master seed;
- ***PRFmsg*** and ***Hmsg***: randomize and digest the message during signing.

Domain separation is enforced by a 32-byte *address* structure, ADRS, encoding the layer, tree address, key-pair address, chain address, hash address, and tree height/indices for every single hash call. Two hash evaluations anywhere in the scheme — even computing the same logical function — never share an identical input unless they are meant to, which is what makes the multi-target security reductions go through [2].

Key generation samples three *n*-byte seeds: *SK.seed* (derives every secret value in the scheme), *SK.prf* (randomizes message hashing), and the public *PK.seed* (global randomization), then computes the hypertree root *PK.root*. The secret key is *(SK.seed, SK.prf, PK.seed, PK.root)* and the public key is *(PK.seed, PK.root)* — just *2n* bytes, 32 bytes at the 128-bit level [1].

Signing is fully deterministic given an optional randomness input *opt*:

1. Compute *R = PRFmsg(SK.prf, opt, M)*, a fresh *n*-byte randomizer bound to the message.
2. Compute *(md, idx) = Hmsg(R, PK.seed, PK.root, M)*: a *k·a*-bit message digest and a pseudorandom hypertree leaf index.
3. Sign *md* with the FORS key at index *idx*, yielding *SIG_FORS* and the FORS public key *PK_FORS*.
4. Sign *PK_FORS* with the hypertree, yielding *SIG_HT*.
5. Output *(R, SIG_FORS, SIG_HT)*.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Winternitz chains and the WOTS+ chaining function

WOTS+ is parameterized by *n* (bytes per chain element) and *w* (the Winternitz parameter, 4 or 16 in standardized use). The message digest plus checksum is written in base *w*, requiring

- *len_1 = ceil(8n / lg w)* chains for the message,
- *len_2 = floor(lg(len_1 · (w − 1)) / lg w) + 1* chains for the checksum,
- *len = len_1 + len_2* chains total [3].

| *n* (bytes) | *w* | *len_1* | *len_2* | *len* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 16 | 16 | 32 | 3 | 35 |
| 24 | 16 | 48 | 3 | 51 |
| 32 | 16 | 64 | 3 | 67 |

The chaining function is where WOTS+ departs from plain Winternitz. Given a start value *x*, a start index *i*, and a step count *s*, it iterates *c(x, i) = F(PK.seed, ADRS, x ⊕ mask_i)*, where *mask_i* is a fresh pseudorandom *n*-byte bitmask derived from *PK.seed* and the address, and ADRS advances with each step [5]. Key generation sets *pk_i = c^{w−1}(sk_i, 0)*; signing block value *b_i* reveals *sig_i = c^{b_i}(sk_i, 0)*; verification completes the chain *c^{w−1−b_i}(sig_i, b_i)* and compares against *pk_i*. The masks are the crucial proof device: they make each chaining step use an effectively independent function, enabling a tight reduction to second-preimage resistance.

```python
def chain(x: bytes, i: int, s: int, adrs: ADRS, pk_seed: bytes) -> bytes:
    """WOTS+ chaining function c^s(x, i): iterate F s times from step i."""
    for step in range(i, i + s):
        adrs.set_hash_address(step)
        mask = prf(pk_seed, adrs)          # fresh bitmask per step
        x = F(pk_seed, adrs, xor_bytes(x, mask))
    return x

def wots_sign(msg_blocks, checksum_blocks, sk, adrs, pk_seed):
    sig = []
    for idx, b in enumerate(msg_blocks + checksum_blocks):
        adrs.set_chain_address(idx)
        sig.append(chain(sk[idx], 0, b, adrs, pk_seed))
    return sig
```

Increasing *w* shortens signatures (fewer chains) but slows signing (longer chains); *w = 16* is the standard compromise, and *w* has little effect on security [3].

### 4.2 Merkle trees and authentication paths

A Merkle tree over *2^h* WOTS+ leaf values is built bottom-up: leaf *i* is *T_len* applied to the *i*-th WOTS+ public key, and each internal node is *H(PK.seed, ADRS, left || right)* with the address encoding its height and position [2]. The root authenticates all leaves. To prove leaf *i* belongs to the tree, the signer reveals the *h* siblings along the root path; the verifier hashes upward, choosing left/right order from the bits of *i*:

```python
def merkle_root(leaf: bytes, idx: int, auth_path: list[bytes], pk_seed: bytes) -> bytes:
    node, adrs = leaf, ADRS()
    for height, sibling in enumerate(auth_path):
        adrs.set_tree_height(height); adrs.set_tree_index(idx >> 1)
        left, right = (node, sibling) if idx % 2 == 0 else (sibling, node)
        node = H(pk_seed, adrs, left + right)
        idx >>= 1
    return node
```

XMSS signs with leaf *idx* kept as state; SPHINCS+ instead derives *idx* pseudorandomly from the message via *Hmsg*, which is exactly what removes the state [4]. The BDS algorithm from XMSS computes each authentication path in *O(h)* time and space rather than storing the full tree — essential when *h* reaches 63 or 66 and the tree has *2^63* leaves [3].

### 4.3 The FORS few-time signature

FORS — *Forest of Random Subsets* — is SPHINCS+'s few-time signature, replacing the HORST construction of the original SPHINCS [4]. Its parameters are *k* (number of trees) and *t = 2^a* (leaves per tree). The private key is *k·t* pseudorandom *n*-byte values; the public key hashes each tree's leaves into a root and compresses the *k* roots with a tweakable hash into one value [2].

To sign a *k·a*-bit digest, FORS splits it into *k* blocks of *a* bits; block *j* selects leaf *idx_j* in tree *j*, and the signature reveals those *k* leaves plus their authentication paths. Each signature exposes exactly one leaf per tree, so reusing a FORS key degrades security only *gradually* (this is *r-subset resilience*) — which is why selecting the FORS key pseudorandomly per message is safe [6].

| Parameter set | *k* | *a* | Digest bits *k·a* |
|---|---|---|---|
| 128s | 14 | 12 | 168 |
| 128f | 33 | 6 | 198 |
| 192s | 17 | 13 | 221 |
| 256s | 22 | 14 | 308 |

### 4.4 The hypertree: binding FORS to the public key

The hypertree is a *tree of XMSS trees*. Its total height *h* is split into *d* layers, each an XMSS tree of height *h/d*. Layer 0's leaves are WOTS+ keys signing FORS public keys; each higher layer's leaves sign the *roots* of the trees one layer below; the single root of the top layer is *PK.root* [2]. A hypertree signature therefore contains *d* WOTS+ signatures and *d* authentication paths — one per layer — chaining *PK_FORS* all the way up to *PK.root*.

The address ADRS tracks the signer's position through this structure: layer index, tree address within the layer, and key-pair address within the tree. Because every WOTS+ and FORS secret is derived as *PRF(SK.seed, ADRS)*, the entire hypertree — all *2^h* virtual leaves — exists only implicitly; signing materializes just the *d* paths the pseudorandom index *idx* selects [2]. This is the deep trick of statelessness: an exponentially large key space with constant-size secret storage.

> **Lemma:** If FORS is *r*-subset-resilient and WOTS+ is existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack, then the hypertree construction is existentially unforgeable as long as no FORS key is selected more than *r* times — an event the pseudorandom index selection makes negligible [6].

### 4.5 Parameter sets and the size–speed trade-off

FIPS 205 defines twelve parameter sets: two hash families (SHA-2 and SHAKE) times three security levels (128, 192, 256-bit *n* of 16, 24, 32 bytes) times two trade-off profiles — **s** (small signatures, slower signing) and **f** (fast signing, larger signatures) [1].

| Set | *n* | *h* | *d* | *h/d* | *k* | *a* | *w* | Sig. bytes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 128s | 16 | 63 | 7 | 9 | 14 | 12 | 16 | 7,856 |
| 128f | 16 | 66 | 22 | 3 | 33 | 6 | 16 | 17,088 |
| 192s | 24 | 63 | 7 | 9 | 17 | 13 | 16 | 16,224 |
| 256s | 32 | 64 | 8 | 8 | 22 | 14 | 16 | 29,792 |

The signature size follows directly from the construction [9]:

*sig_size = n + k·n·(a + 1) + n·(h + d·len)*,

where the three terms are the randomizer *R*, the FORS signature (*k* leaves plus *k·a* path nodes), and the hypertree signature (*d* WOTS+ signatures of *len* elements plus *h* total path nodes). For 128s: *16 + 14·16·13 + 16·(63 + 7·35) = 7,856* bytes. Public keys are 32 bytes and secret keys 64 bytes at every level — the entire cost of post-quantum conservatism is concentrated in the signature [1].

The **s** variants use tall, thin hypertrees (few layers, tall trees: *d = 7*, height 9 per tree) minimizing signature size at the price of generating large FORS trees and long WOTS+ chains during signing; the **f** variants use many short layers (*d = 22*, height 3) so that signing touches far fewer hashes, roughly doubling or tripling signature size [2].

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### Security reductions

The security argument for SPHINCS+ is a *modular reduction*: each component's unforgeability reduces to a standard property of the tweakable hash functions, and the composition theorems lift component security to the full scheme [6].

> **Theorem (SPHINCS+ EUF-CMA, informal):** If *F* is a pseudorandom function family, *H* and *T_len* are second-preimage resistant, and *PRF*, *PRFmsg*, *Hmsg* are pseudorandom, then SPHINCS+ is existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack in the standard model.

> **Lemma (WOTS+):** WOTS+ is EUF-CMA secure if the chaining function family is second-preimage resistant — a strictly weaker assumption than the pseudorandomness required by earlier Winternitz proofs [5].

> **Proof (sketch):** The tight proof of Hülsing, Rijnswoude, and Song proceeds by games. First, replace the PRF-derived secrets with truly random values (pseudorandomness of *PRF*). Then bound the FORS forger by the *r*-subset-resilience game: a forgery on a fresh message requires either finding a second preimage in some chain or tree node, or reusing revealed FORS leaves to cover a new digest subset. The address-based domain separation guarantees that every hash query in the security game targets a distinct function instance, so the multi-target bounds apply with the standard-model birthday terms. Finally, the hypertree forger is reduced to a WOTS+ forger at the layer where the forged chain diverges [6]. ∎

Notably, the proof needs no random oracle and no number-theoretic assumption — it is valid against quantum adversaries as long as the hash functions resist quantum preimage and collision search, with parameters calibrated so that Grover's quadratic speedup still leaves the target NIST security level intact [1].

### Empirical performance

Because signing must generate full FORS trees (*k·2^a* leaves) and complete WOTS+ chains across *d* layers, SPHINCS+ signing performs *millions* of hash evaluations — typically one to two orders of magnitude slower than lattice schemes such as ML-DSA, with the **s** sets several times slower than their **f** siblings [9]. Verification touches only the revealed paths and completes in well under a millisecond, making SPHINCS+ attractive wherever signatures are produced rarely but checked often, such as firmware signing and software-update channels [9].

Implementation work has steadily narrowed the gap: vectorized and hardware-accelerated SHA-2/SHAKE backends, batched tree computation, and masked implementations for side-channel resistance are all active areas, surveyed in recent optimization literature [9].

##6 Limitations

**Signature size.** At 7.8 kB for the smallest parameter set and nearly 50 kB for the largest, SPHINCS+ signatures dwarf ECDSA's 64 bytes and ML-DSA's ~2.4–4.6 kB. Protocols with strict datagram limits (DNSSEC, some embedded handshakes) cannot carry them without fragmentation [1].

**Signing cost.** Millions of hash evaluations per signature rule out high-throughput or ultra-low-power signers for the **s** variants; only the **f** variants — with their doubled signature sizes — approach interactive speeds [9].

**No advanced features.** The Winternitz/Merkle structure offers no homomorphic properties: there is no aggregation, no threshold signing, and no blind issuance without substantial new machinery. Each signature stands alone.

**Stateful predecessors remain hazardous.** XMSS's catastrophic failure mode on state reuse — cloning a virtual machine or restoring a backup silently rewinds the leaf index — is precisely the operational risk SPHINCS+ was designed to eliminate, and it remains a live footgun for anyone deploying RFC 8391 directly [3].

**Side channels.** Deterministic, hash-heavy signing is a natural target for fault attacks: a single skipped chain iteration during signing can leak secret chain values, and dedicated countermeasures (redundant computation, infective countermeasures) add real overhead, as analyzed in recent fault-attack literature [10].

**Assumption minimalism cuts both ways.** Resting security solely on symmetric primitives means there is no algebraic structure to exploit for efficiency — every gain must come from hashing faster, and Grover's algorithm forces the *n = 16* level to rely on 128-bit classical preimage resistance being "enough" in a post-quantum world, a judgment call rather than a theorem [1].

##7 Conclusion

Hash-based signatures traveled from Lamport's one-bit preimages to a NIST standard without ever changing their foundational bet: that a well-designed hash function is the hardest cryptographic object to break, including for quantum adversaries. SPHINCS+, as standardized in FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA), packages Winternitz chains, Merkle authentication, FORS few-time signatures, and the hypertree into a stateless scheme whose unforgeability reduces tightly to symmetric-key assumptions in the standard model [1][2][6].

The price — kilobyte-scale signatures and hash-intensive signing — confines hash-based signatures to roles where trust outweighs bandwidth: root and firmware signing keys, long-lived certification authorities, and hybrid deployments paired with faster lattice schemes. When every structured assumption is suspect, the scheme that assumes almost nothing is the anchor everything else can be measured against.
