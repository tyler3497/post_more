# Metaprogramming at the Speed of Thought: Tactics, Elaboration, and Machine-Checked Mathematics at Scale in Lean 4 and mathlib

## Abstract

Lean 4 is simultaneously a dependently typed programming language and an interactive theorem prover whose entire metaprogramming stack — parser, elaborator, and tactic framework — is implemented in Lean 4 itself. This thesis presents the architecture of that stack and explains how it enabled mathlib, a community-maintained library of formalized mathematics exceeding one million lines, to become the largest coherent body of machine-checked mathematics ever assembled. We trace the elaboration pipeline from concrete syntax through macro expansion, term elaboration in the `TermElabM` monad, and definitional-equality checking in a small trusted kernel; we analyze the tactic framework built on `TacticM`, including white-box proof search in Aesop and the `simp` rewriting engine; and we quantify the engineering that keeps a million-line development compiling: tabled typeclass resolution, discrimination-tree indexing, parallel elaboration, and continuous integration with proof replay. Case studies — perfectoid spaces, the sphere eversion project, and the Polynomial Freiman–Ruzsa conjecture — illustrate how metaprogramming turns proof automation into a programmable, user-extensible discipline rather than a fixed set of built-in procedures [1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8].

## 1. Introduction

For most of the history of interactive theorem proving, *metaprogramming* — programs that manipulate proofs, terms, and tactics — was a second-class activity. In Edinburgh LCF and its descendants (HOL, Isabelle, Coq), tactics were written in a *metalanguage* (ML, OCaml, Ltac) distinct from the object language of mathematics itself, and extending the prover's automation required stepping outside the logic into an implementation language with its own build system, FFI hazards, and learning curve. The result was a persistent asymmetry: the prover could check mathematics, but programming the prover was an expert craft walled off from ordinary users.

Lean 4 dissolves this asymmetry by a single architectural decision: **the metaprogramming framework is written in Lean 4 itself**. The parser produces `Syntax` objects, the elaborator is a collection of Lean 4 monadic programs, and tactics are ordinary Lean 4 functions in the `TacticM` monad that users can define, compose, debug, and distribute as libraries [2][4]. Because tactics are first-class Lean programs, they enjoy the same tooling — the language server, the package manager Lake, the test framework — as any other code. This thesis argues that this decision, more than any single automation procedure, is what made mathlib possible: a million-line, community-maintained formalization of modern mathematics whose contributors routinely write domain-specific tactics as part of ordinary mathematical work [3].

We proceed as follows. Section 2 reviews the logical and historical background: dependent type theory, the LCF tradition, and the limitations of Lean 3's metaprogramming model that motivated the Lean 4 redesign. Section 3 presents our methodology — an architectural analysis of the elaboration pipeline grounded in the published system descriptions and the Lean 4 source itself. Section 4 is the deep dive: macros and extensible syntax, the elaborator and its monads, the tactic framework, typeclass resolution at scale, and delaboration. Section 5 evaluates the design empirically through mathlib's scale, build infrastructure, and landmark formalizations. Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclude.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Dependent type theory and the trusted kernel

Lean's logic is a version of the Calculus of Inductive Constructions (CIC) with quotient types, an impredicative universe `Prop` of propositions, and definitional proof irrelevance [1][6]. Terms are checked by a small **kernel** implementing type inference, weak-head normalization, and definitional equality (`isDefEq`). The kernel is the *trusted computing base*: everything else — the parser, the elaborator, every tactic — is untrusted code whose outputs are re-checked by the kernel. This is the LCF discipline inherited from Milner's Edinburgh LCF: no matter how baroque the automation, a proof is only accepted when the kernel validates the resulting proof term [1].

The kernel's notion of *definitional* equality is what makes dependent types usable in practice. When a user writes `rfl : 2 + 2 = 4`, no proof search occurs; the kernel simply normalizes both sides and observes they coincide. Elaboration's central job is to bridge the gap between the flexible surface language users write and the fully explicit terms the kernel demands — inserting implicit arguments, resolving overloading, unfolding notation — while deferring hard problems to unification and tactics.

### 2.2 Tactics: from LCF to Ltac to Lean 4

In the LCF tradition, a *tactic* is a program that transforms a goal into subgoals, ultimately validated by composing primitive kernel inferences. Isabelle's Eisbach and Coq's Ltac made tactic *languages* programmable, but they remained separate interpreted languages with limited access to the prover's internals. Lean 3 took a major step forward with a metaprogramming framework in which tactics were written in Lean itself using a `tactic` monad [4]. However, Lean 3's framework was grafted onto a C++ implementation: performance-critical paths lived in C++, the API surface was irregular, and extending the *elaborator* (as opposed to writing tactics) remained essentially impossible for users.

Lean 4 was a ground-up rewrite addressing exactly these constraints [2]. The entire frontend — parsing, elaboration, tactic execution — is implemented in Lean 4 and compiled by Lean 4's own compiler to efficient C code. Users can now write not just tactics but *elaboration extensions*: new command types, new term elaborators, and new syntax with custom macro expansion, all as ordinary libraries.

### 2.3 Why scale matters: the mathlib experiment

mathlib is the proving ground for Lean 4's metaprogramming thesis. Begun in the Lean 3 era and ported to Lean 4, it now contains over a million lines of Lean code covering logic, algebra, topology, analysis, measure theory, number theory, and more, developed by hundreds of contributors with continuous integration checking every pull request [3]. Its scale stresses every component: the elaborator must process enormous terms quickly, typeclass resolution must search instances across a global database of tens of thousands of entries, and `simp` must rewrite with lemma sets numbering in the thousands. Design decisions that are merely elegant at small scale become load-bearing at mathlib scale — which is precisely why the empirical analysis of Section 5 matters.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis is architectural and evidence-driven. We reconstruct the elaboration pipeline from the published system descriptions [1][2], the metaprogramming framework paper [4], Carneiro's formal specification of Lean's type theory [6], and the Lean 4 and mathlib sources. We organize the pipeline into five stages and, for each, identify the key data structures, the monadic interfaces, and the performance mechanisms that make the stage scale:

| Stage | Input → Output | Key mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Parsing | Text → `Syntax` | Extensible parser combinators, custom syntax categories |
| Macro expansion | `Syntax` → `Syntax` | Hygienic macros, `macro_rules` |
| Elaboration | `Syntax` → `Expr` | `TermElabM`/`CommandElabM`, unification, postponed elaboration |
| Tactic execution | Goal state → proof term | `TacticM`, metavariable management |
| Kernel checking | `Expr` → accept/reject | `isDefEq`, weak-head normalization |

Our empirical claims about mathlib — build times, line counts, contributor workflows — are drawn from the library's documented infrastructure and published case studies [3][5][7]. Throughout, we distinguish *trusted* components (the kernel) from *untrusted but validated* ones (everything upstream of it), since this distinction determines which metaprograms can be buggy without endangering soundness.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Macros and extensible syntax: parsing as a library

Lean 4's parser is not a fixed grammar but a **library of parser combinators** that users extend by declaring new syntax categories. A mathematician who wants notation for, say, the $p$-adic valuation writes:

```lean
syntax "padicVal" term : term
macro_rules
  | `(padicVal $p) => `(padicValAux $p 0)
```

The `syntax` command registers new productions in the named category (`term`, `tactic`, `command`, or custom categories); `macro_rules` defines hygienic source-to-source transformations on `Syntax` objects [2][4]. Hygiene — automatic freshening of introduced identifiers — is implemented via macro scopes attached to identifiers, preventing the capture bugs that plague naive macro systems.

This matters for metaprogramming because *tactics themselves have extensible syntax*. The `tactic` category is open: a library can introduce `tactic "my_auto" ...` with its own parser, and the tactic block parser will accept it seamlessly. Notation, tactic syntax, and even new top-level commands are thus all instances of one uniform mechanism, and all of it is programmable by users without touching the compiler.

### 4.2 The elaborator: from syntax to terms in `TermElabM`

Elaboration converts `Syntax` into fully explicit `Expr` terms. Its core is a family of monads — `TermElabM` for terms, `CommandElabM` for top-level commands — layered over `CoreM` (environment, options, diagnostics) and `MetaM` (metavariable context, unification, typeclass cache) [2]. A typical elaboration step:

1. **Pre-elaboration**: macro expansion produces kernel-facing syntax; explicit implicit-argument insertion points are marked.
2. **Bidirectional elaboration**: the elaborator propagates *expected types* inward (checking mode) or synthesizes types outward (inference mode), creating metavariables `?m` for unknown subterms.
3. **Unification**: constraints between metavariables and terms are solved by `isDefEq`, which interleaves unfolding (delta/beta/iota/zeta reduction) with first-order-style assignment, postponing stuck cases.
4. **Postponement**: elaboration units that cannot yet proceed are suspended and retried after more information arrives — the mechanism that makes Lean's famously flexible implicit-argument inference terminate in practice.

The crucial metaprogramming hook is that users can register **custom elaborators** for their own syntax via `@[term_elab]` attributes, receiving the `Syntax` and expected type and returning an `Expr` — arbitrary Lean 4 code running inside elaboration. Whole DSLs (the `aesop` rule configuration language, mathlib's `positivity` extension framework) are built this way.

> **Theorem (Soundness boundary).** Every `Expr` produced by elaboration — whether by built-in rules, user macros, or custom elaborators — is re-checked by the kernel. A bug in any elaborator can therefore cause incompleteness or confusing errors, but never an unsound acceptance. *Proof sketch.* The kernel's `check`/`infer` functions are total over well-formed `Expr`; the environment only admits declarations whose types and values kernel-check [6]. ∎

### 4.3 The tactic framework: `TacticM` and white-box proof search

Tactics in Lean 4 are Lean 4 programs of type `TacticM Unit` operating on a goal state of metavariables. The framework provides combinators (`<;>`, `first | ...`, `repeat`, `all_goals`), backtracking, and — critically — **reflection**: a tactic can inspect the current goal's `Expr`, the local context, and the full environment, then compute. Two workhorses deserve special attention:

- **`simp`**: ordered rewriting with a discrimination-tree-indexed lemma database, conditional rewriting with side-goal discharge, and user-extensible `simprocs` (verified-by-construction simplification procedures). mathlib registers thousands of `@[simp]` lemmas; the engine's indexing keeps per-goal matching sublinear in the lemma count.
- **`aesop`**: white-box best-first proof search [7]. Unlike black-box hammers, Aesop's search tree is inspectable and its rule set is configured by library authors via attributes (`@[aesop]`), with rule priorities learned from the goal shape. Because rules are ordinary Lean tactics, domain experts extend automation without learning a separate tactic language.

The *white-box* character is the philosophical point: Lean 4 automation is not a fixed oracle but a **programmable substrate**. When the sphere eversion formalization needed custom reasoning about corrugations, its authors wrote tactics; when the Polynomial Freiman–Ruzsa project needed entropy-counting automation, they extended `simp` and `aesop` rule sets as part of the mathematical development itself.

### 4.4 Typeclass resolution at scale: tabled search and indexing

Typeclasses — Lean's mechanism for ad-hoc polymorphism and mathematical structure hierarchies — are elaborated by proof search: given a goal `AddCommGroup ?α`, the resolver searches registered instances. mathlib's algebraic hierarchy generates tens of thousands of instances, and naive Prolog-style depth-first search would diverge. Lean 4's resolver, following Selsam et al. [8], uses **tabled resolution**: previously solved (and failed) subgoals are memoized, turning exponential re-exploration into a dynamic-programming table, with loop detection for coinductive cases.

Complementing this, discrimination trees index both `simp` lemmas and typeclass instances by term shape, so candidate retrieval is proportional to the goal's structure rather than the database size. Together these mechanisms keep instance-implicit elaboration — which fires on nearly every mathematical expression — fast enough for interactive use over a million-line library.

### 4.5 Delaboration: pretty printing as metaprogramming

Elaboration has an inverse: **delaboration** converts kernel `Expr`s back into user-facing `Syntax` for display in the infoview. Lean 4 exposes delaborators as user-programmable extensions (`@[delab]`), so libraries can teach the pretty printer to render their abstractions idiomatically — hiding instance-implicit arguments, unfolding domain notation, and generally ensuring that what the user *sees* matches the mathematical vernacular they *wrote*. The round-trip property — delaborate then re-elaborate yields a definitionally equal term — is maintained by construction for the built-in delaborators and is a documented obligation for custom ones.

## 5. Empirical Analysis and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 mathlib at scale: numbers

mathlib's statistics are the strongest evidence for the metaprogramming thesis. The library exceeds one million lines of Lean 4 across thousands of files with a deep import DAG; hundreds of contributors submit pull requests reviewed by maintainers and checked by CI, which rebuilds affected files and replays proofs (`lake build`) before merge [3]. Key engineering facts:

- **Incremental compilation**: Lean 4 compiles files to `.olean` artifacts with fine-grained dependency tracking; editing one file re-elaborates only its reverse dependencies.
- **Proof replay in CI**: every PR is built from scratch on CI runners, so "it works on my machine" cannot smuggle in unreproducible automation.
- **Style and linting**: custom linters — themselves metaprograms running over `Syntax` and `Expr` — enforce naming, documentation, and simp-normal-form conventions across the corpus.

### 5.2 Landmark formalizations

Three projects demonstrate what programmable automation buys:

1. **Perfectoid spaces** (Buzzard, Commelin, Massot): a definition-heavy formalization of Scholze's Fields-medal mathematics, where extending the elaborator with domain-specific notation and tactics was part of the mathematical work [5].
2. **Sphere eversion** (van Doorn and collaborators): a formal proof that the 2-sphere can be turned inside out, requiring bespoke automation for corrugation arguments.
3. **Polynomial Freiman–Ruzsa** (Gowers, Green, Manners, Tao, formalized in Lean): a cutting-edge combinatorics proof whose formalization drove improvements to `simp`/`aesop` rule engineering under real mathematical pressure.

In each case, the formalizers were mathematicians, not prover developers — possible only because metaprogramming speaks Lean, the language they already use.

### 5.3 The soundness guarantee

The theoretical guarantee is crisp and worth restating: **the kernel is the sole trusted component**. All of the machinery described above — macros, elaborators, tactics, typeclass search, delaborators — produces proof terms that the kernel re-checks [6]. The empirical corollary is equally important: mathlib's CI has, over years, caught soundness bugs in untrusted components *as ordinary test failures*, because every component's output must survive kernel scrutiny. Programmability without trust is the design's deepest virtue.

| Guarantee | Enforced by | Failure mode if buggy |
|---|---|---|
| Logical soundness | Kernel re-checking | Impossible (rejected proof) |
| Proof reproducibility | CI proof replay | Build failure, never silent |
| Automation completeness | User-extensible tactics | Unproved goal, never false proof |
| Notation coherence | Hygienic macros | Confusing errors, never capture |

## 6. Limitations

The design is not without costs. First, **performance cliffs**: elaboration is undecidable in general (it embeds higher-order unification), and users periodically encounter goals where automation diverges or slows catastrophically; debugging requires understanding the elaborator's internals, a steep learning curve. Second, **error messages**: when a custom elaborator or macro fails, the reported error often points at generated code rather than user intent, though delaboration improvements are narrowing this gap. Third, **proof brittleness**: heavy automation (`simp`, `aesop`) can break when libraries change lemma sets — mathlib mitigates this with `simp` normal-form conventions and CI, but maintenance burden is real. Fourth, **the kernel, though small, is not verified**: soundness rests on careful implementation and testing of the kernel in Lean/C++, not on a machine-checked proof of the kernel itself (unlike, e.g., CakeML-style verified stacks). Finally, **compile times** for the full library remain measured in hours on CI, a tax on the very scale the system enables.

## 7. Conclusion

Lean 4's bet — that metaprogramming should be ordinary programming in the prover's own language — has paid off at a scale no prior system achieved. By implementing parsing, elaboration, tactics, and pretty printing as user-extensible Lean 4 libraries guarded by a small trusted kernel, it turned proof automation from a fixed vendor-supplied toolkit into a programmable medium that mathematicians themselves extend in the course of doing mathematics. mathlib's million lines, its CI infrastructure, and its landmark formalizations are the empirical record of that bet. The remaining challenges — performance predictability, error reporting, and kernel verification — define the next decade of the research program. If the first decade showed that machine-checked mathematics can be *programmed*, the next must show it can be *engineered* with the predictability that large-scale software demands.
