# Tensor Network States: Matrix Product States, the DMRG Algorithm, and Area Laws for Entanglement Entropy

## Abstract

The simulation of strongly correlated quantum many-body systems is obstructed by the exponential growth of Hilbert space with system size. Tensor network states circumvent this barrier by parametrizing only the physically relevant corner of Hilbert space — the low-entanglement states selected by locality and energy. This thesis develops the theory of **matrix product states (MPS)** as the natural language of one-dimensional quantum matter, shows how the **density-matrix renormalization group (DMRG)** of White is exactly a variational optimization algorithm over the MPS manifold, and proves why the method succeeds through **area laws** for entanglement entropy. We derive the MPS ansatz from the Schmidt decomposition, construct left, right, and mixed canonical forms, detail the two-site DMRG sweep with its Schmidt-truncation optimality, extend the formalism to matrix product operators and projected entangled pair states in two dimensions, and analyze real- and imaginary-time evolution via TEBD. We state Hastings' area-law theorem for gapped one-dimensional systems and survey empirical DMRG convergence on the Heisenberg chain, before delimiting the method's failures: two-dimensional contraction hardness, critical logarithmic corrections, and entanglement-barrier breakdown of time evolution.

## 1. Introduction

Quantum mechanics on a lattice of $L$ sites, each carrying a local Hilbert space of dimension $d$, lives in a space of dimension $d^L$. For a spin-1/2 chain of $L = 100$ sites this is $2^{100} \approx 10^{30}$. *Exact diagonalization* is therefore confined to a few dozen sites, and while quantum Monte Carlo methods scale to thousands of sites, they collapse in the presence of the infamous **fermionic sign problem** [1].

The density-matrix renormalization group (DMRG), introduced by Steven R. White in 1992, answered this question empirically before the theory caught up [2]. White's key insight was that Wilson's numerical renormalization group failed for quantum lattices because it truncated the block Hilbert space using the eigenstates of the *isolated block Hamiltonian* — states optimal for the block alone but disastrously wrong once the block is embedded in its environment. By instead truncating according to the eigenvalues of the **reduced density matrix** of the block, computed from the ground state of a larger *superblock*, DMRG selected precisely those states most relevant to the entanglement between the block and its surroundings. The accuracy was staggering: twelve significant figures for the ground-state energy of the spin-1 Heisenberg chain [3].

For over a decade DMRG was understood as a clever renormalization procedure. The conceptual revolution came when Östlund and Rommer demonstrated that the fixed point of DMRG is a **matrix product state** — that every state DMRG can produce is of the form

$$|\psi\rangle = \sum_{\sigma_1 \ldots \sigma_L} \mathrm{Tr}\left(A^{\sigma_1} A^{\sigma_2} \cdots A^{\sigma_L}\right) |\sigma_1 \ldots \sigma_L\rangle, \tag{1}$$

and conversely that DMRG is a variational algorithm sweeping through and optimizing exactly this ansatz class [4]. This reframing, championed in Schollwöck's comprehensive 2011 review [5], transformed DMRG from a mysterious black box into a transparent, improvable, and generalizable framework — the seed of the entire modern tensor-network program.

The deep reason tensor networks work is the **area law** for entanglement entropy: ground states of gapped local Hamiltonians exhibit entanglement across a bipartition that scales with the *boundary* of the region rather than its *volume* [6]. In one dimension this means the entanglement entropy is bounded by a constant, which in turn implies efficient MPS approximation with polynomial bond dimension. Area laws are therefore the *physical* justification of the *numerical* method — the subject of this thesis.

> **Thesis statement:** Matrix product states are the entanglement-faithful variational class for one-dimensional gapped quantum matter; DMRG is their optimal variational optimizer; and area laws provide the rigorous guarantee that the relevant physics lives in this polynomial corner of Hilbert space.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The exponential wall

Consider a chain of $L$ spin-1/2 particles. A general pure state requires $2^L$ complex amplitudes. Storing them for $L=50$ already demands roughly 16 petabytes. This is not a technological limitation to be overcome by better hardware; it is a *fundamental* statement about the information content of generic quantum states. Yet physical states — ground states of local Hamiltonians — are emphatically *not* generic. They obey constraints imposed by locality, and those constraints manifest as restrictions on entanglement.

### 2.2 Schmidt decomposition and the anatomy of bipartite entanglement

Let $|\psi\rangle$ be a pure state on a bipartite Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B$. There always exists an orthonormal pair of bases $\{|a_i\rangle\}$, $\{|b_i\rangle\}$ and non-negative numbers $\lambda_i$ such that

$$|\psi\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \lambda_i \, |a_i\rangle \otimes |b_i\rangle, \qquad \lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_r > 0, \tag{2}$$

the **Schmidt decomposition**. The number $r$ of non-zero coefficients is the *Schmidt rank*, and the squares $\lambda_i^2$ are the eigenvalues of the reduced density matrix $\rho_A = \mathrm{Tr}_B |\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$.

> **Theorem (Optimal low-rank approximation, Eckart–Young):** Among all states of Schmidt rank at most $\chi$, the one minimizing the 2-norm distance to $|\psi\rangle$ is obtained by keeping the $\chi$ largest Schmidt coefficients. The truncation error is $\epsilon = \sum_{i>\chi} \lambda_i^2$, the *discarded weight*.

This theorem is the entire mathematical engine of DMRG: White's density-matrix truncation *is* Schmidt truncation, and it is provably optimal in the sense above [2].

### 2.3 Entanglement entropy: area versus volume

The **entanglement entropy** of the bipartition is the von Neumann entropy of the reduced state,

$$S(A) = -\mathrm{Tr}\left(\rho_A \log \rho_A\right) = -\sum_i \lambda_i^2 \log \lambda_i^2. \tag{3}$$

For a *typical* (Haar-random) state, $S(A) \propto |A|$ — a **volume law**: the entropy grows with the number of sites in $A$. For ground states of gapped local Hamiltonians, by contrast,

$$S(A) \propto |\partial A|, \tag{4}$$

an **area law**: entropy scales with the *boundary* between $A$ and its complement. In one dimension the boundary of an interval is just two points, so the area law asserts that $S$ is bounded by a *constant* independent of system size [6].

| System | Entanglement scaling | Classical simulability |
|---|---|---|
| Random state, 1D | Volume law, $S \sim L$ | Impossible (exponential) |
| Gapped ground state, 1D | Area law, $S = O(1)$ | MPS with $\chi = \mathrm{poly}(L)$ |
| Critical ground state, 1D | $S \sim \frac{c}{3}\log L$ | MPS with $\chi = \mathrm{poly}(L)$ |
| Gapped ground state, 2D | Area law, $S \sim L$ | PEPS, contraction is hard |
| Quench dynamics, 1D | $S \sim t$ (linear growth) | TEBD fails at long times |

### 2.4 A first matrix product state: the AKLT chain

The Affleck–Kennedy–Lieb–Tasaki (AKLT) state of the spin-1 chain provides the canonical example. Each spin-1 site is viewed as the symmetric subspace of two virtual spin-1/2 degrees of freedom; neighboring virtual spins are paired into singlets, and a local projection maps back to the physical spin-1. The result is *exactly* a matrix product state with bond dimension $\chi = 2$, despite describing a genuinely entangled, gapped phase with non-local string order [7].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The MPS ansatz

A matrix product state on $L$ sites with open boundary conditions assigns to each site $i$ and each local basis state $\sigma_i \in \{1, \ldots, d\}$ a $\chi_{i-1} \times \chi_i$ matrix $A^{\sigma_i}_i$, and defines

$$|\psi\rangle = \sum_{\sigma_1 \ldots \sigma_L} A^{\sigma_1}_1 A^{\sigma_2}_2 \cdots A^{\sigma_L}_L \, |\sigma_1 \ldots \sigma_L\rangle, \tag{5}$$

with $\chi_0 = \chi_L = 1$ at the boundaries. The integers $\chi_i$ are the **bond dimensions**. The parameter count is $O(L d \chi^2)$ — linear in system size for fixed $\chi$ — versus $d^L$ for a general state. Every state admits an *exact* MPS representation with $\chi \leq d^{L/2}$ (by repeated Schmidt decomposition), so the ansatz is complete; its power lies in the fact that *physical* states need only small $\chi$.

The entanglement entropy across the bond between sites $i$ and $i+1$ is bounded by

$$S \leq \log \chi_i, \tag{6}$$

so the bond dimension is a *knob controlling the entanglement capacity* of the ansatz.

### 3.2 Gauge freedom and canonical forms

The representation (5) is redundant: inserting $X_i X_i^{-1} = \mathbb{1}$ between adjacent tensors,

$$A^{\sigma_i}_i \to A^{\sigma_i}_i X_i, \qquad A^{\sigma_{i+1}}_{i+1} \to X_i^{-1} A^{\sigma_{i+1}}_{i+1}, \tag{7}$$

leaves the physical state invariant. This **gauge freedom** is exploited to bring the MPS into *canonical forms* that make every operation numerically stable and conceptually transparent:

- **Left-canonical form:** $\sum_{\sigma} A^{\sigma\dagger} A^{\sigma} = \mathbb{1}$. All Schmidt vectors to the left are orthonormal.
- **Right-canonical form:** $\sum_{\sigma} A^{\sigma} A^{\sigma\dagger} = \mathbb{1}$.
- **Mixed-canonical form:** sites left of an *orthogonality center* are left-canonical, sites right of it are right-canonical, and the center carries the full Schmidt spectrum $\Lambda$.

In mixed-canonical form with the orthogonality center at the active sites, the norm of the state equals the norm of the local tensors, expectation values reduce to small local contractions, and the variational problem becomes an ordinary eigenvalue problem — the technical foundation of the DMRG sweep [5].

### 3.3 The variational principle on the MPS manifold

For fixed bond dimensions, the set of MPS forms a smooth manifold $\mathcal{M}_\chi$ embedded in Hilbert space. DMRG minimizes the Rayleigh quotient

$$E[\psi] = \frac{\langle \psi | H | \psi \rangle}{\langle \psi | \psi \rangle} \tag{8}$$

over $\mathcal{M}_\chi$ by *alternating least squares*: freeze all tensors except those on one or two sites, solve the resulting local eigenvalue problem exactly, move to the next site, and sweep back and forth until convergence. Because each local step is optimal given the environment, the energy decreases monotonically. This is the modern understanding of DMRG: **not** a renormalization group in Wilson's sense, but a variational tensor-network optimizer [4][5].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The two-site DMRG sweep

The workhorse variant optimizes *two* adjacent sites at a time. Suppose the orthogonality center covers sites $i, i+1$. The effective Hamiltonian $H_{\mathrm{eff}}$ acting on the two-site tensor $\Theta^{\sigma_i \sigma_{i+1}}_{\alpha \beta}$ is built by contracting the Hamiltonian — itself expressed as a matrix product operator (Section 4.2) — with the fixed left and right environments. One then:

1. **Diagonalizes** $H_{\mathrm{eff}}$ (dimension $d^2 \chi^2$, via Lanczos/Davidson) to obtain the ground-state two-site tensor $\Theta$.
2. **Reshapes** $\Theta$ into a matrix $\Theta_{(\sigma_i \alpha), (\sigma_{i+1} \beta)}$ and performs an **SVD**: $\Theta = U S V^\dagger$.
3. **Truncates** to the $\chi$ largest singular values, recording the discarded weight $\epsilon = \sum_{i>\chi} s_i^2$ as a rigorous error diagnostic.
4. **Shifts** the orthogonality center by absorbing $S$ into the neighboring tensor, and moves on.

The two-site update can *grow* the bond dimension (unlike single-site DMRG, which is stuck at its initial $\chi$), allowing the ansatz to discover entanglement the initial state lacked. A complete left-to-right-to-left pass is one **sweep**; typical calculations converge in a handful of sweeps, with cost $O(L d^3 \chi^3 + L d^2 \chi^2 \chi_{\mathrm{MPO}})$ per sweep.

```python
import numpy as np

def two_site_update(theta, H_eff, chi_max, cutoff=1e-12):
    """
    theta   : two-site tensor, shape (d, d, chiL, chiR)
    H_eff   : effective Hamiltonian as linear operator on theta.ravel()
    Returns updated left/right tensors and the discarded weight.
    """
    d = theta.shape[0]
    # 1. Ground state of the effective Hamiltonian (Lanczos in practice)
    Hmat = H_eff.reshape(d*d*theta.shape[2]*theta.shape[3], -1)
    _, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(Hmat)
    psi = vecs[:, 0].reshape(d, d, theta.shape[2], theta.shape[3])
    # 2. SVD across the bond between the two sites
    M = psi.transpose(0, 2, 1, 3).reshape(d*theta.shape[2], d*theta.shape[3])
    U, s, Vh = np.linalg.svd(M, full_matrices=False)
    # 3. Truncate: Eckart-Young optimal compression
    keep = np.sum(s > cutoff)
    chi = min(int(keep), chi_max)
    discarded = float(np.sum(s[chi:]**2))          # <-- rigorous error measure
    U, s, Vh = U[:, :chi], s[:chi], Vh[:chi, :]
    # 4. Restore canonical form; singular values move right with the center
    A_left  = U.reshape(d, theta.shape[2], chi)     # left-canonical
    A_right = (np.diag(s) @ Vh).reshape(chi, d, theta.shape[3])
    return A_left, A_right, discarded
```

The discarded weight accumulated over a sweep provides an *a posteriori* certificate: energies converge linearly in the total discarded weight, enabling reliable extrapolation $\epsilon \to 0$ [5].

### 4.2 Matrix product operators

Operators admit the same factorization. A **matrix product operator (MPO)** writes

$$O = \sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}, \boldsymbol{\sigma}'} } \mathrm{Tr}\left(W^{\sigma_1 \sigma'_1}_1 \cdots W^{\sigma_L \sigma'_L}_L\right) |\boldsymbol{\sigma}\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{\sigma}'|, \tag{9}$$

with operator bond dimension $\chi_W$. Any Hamiltonian that is a sum of local terms — Heisenberg, Hubbard, $t$–$J$ — has an *exact* MPO with small $\chi_W$ (e.g., $\chi_W = 5$ for the spin-1/2 Heisenberg chain with open boundaries), constructible by finite-state-machine rules [5]. Expectation values $\langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle$ then reduce to contracting a one-dimensional network of width $\chi^2 \chi_W$, evaluable in $O(L d^2 \chi^2 \chi_W^2)$ time by sequential transfer-matrix multiplication [5].

### 4.3 PEPS and the two-dimensional frontier

The natural 2D generalization places a tensor $A^{\sigma_{x,y}}$ with four virtual bonds at each lattice site — a **projected entangled pair state (PEPS)** [7]. The ansatz inherits the area law automatically: cutting out an $L \times L$ region severs $O(L)$ bonds, each contributing at most $\log D$ entropy, so $S \sim L$ as required. The catch is *contraction*: evaluating $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle$ for a PEPS is #P-complete in the worst case, and practical algorithms contract the double-layer network approximately using boundary MPS or corner transfer matrices at cost $O(D^{10})$–$O(D^{12})$.

DMRG itself is routinely applied to 2D systems by snaking a 1D path through the lattice — but a vertical cut then severs $O(L)$ MPS bonds, so the required bond dimension scales as $\chi \sim e^{cL}$, an exponential wall that limits cylinders to widths of order 6–12 sites even on modern hardware. This is not an algorithmic deficiency but a *complexity-theoretic* one: ground states of 2D gapped systems are believed to genuinely require exponential classical resources in the worst case, consistent with the absence of a proven 2D area law with efficient approximation guarantees. PEPS remains the principled 2D ansatz, trading exact contractibility for the correct entanglement structure [6][7].

### 4.4 Time evolution: TEBD and the entanglement barrier

Vidal's **time-evolving block decimation (TEBD)** evolves an MPS under a nearest-neighbor Hamiltonian by Trotterizing $e^{-iHt} \approx \prod e^{-ih_{j,j+1}\delta t}$ and applying each two-site gate via SVD truncation — the dynamical cousin of the DMRG update [8][9]. Imaginary-time TEBD ($t \to -i\tau$) is itself a ground-state finder, converging to the same variational optimum as DMRG from a different direction.

Real-time evolution, however, hits the **entanglement barrier**: after a global quench, $S(t) \sim v t$ grows linearly, so the required bond dimension grows *exponentially* in time, $\chi \sim e^{v t}$. TEBD simulations of quenches are therefore limited to short times — a faithful reflection of physics, since linear entanglement growth is precisely what makes quantum dynamics hard to simulate classically [8].

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 The area-law theorem

> **Theorem (Hastings, 2007):** Let $H$ be a local Hamiltonian on a one-dimensional lattice with a spectral gap $\Delta > 0$ above a unique ground state $|\psi_0\rangle$. Then for any contiguous region $A$, the entanglement entropy satisfies $S(A) \leq c_0 \exp\!\big(c_1 \xi \log \xi\big)$ for constants $c_0, c_1$ and correlation length $\xi \sim v/\Delta$ — in particular, $S$ is bounded *independently of the region size*. Moreover, $|\psi_0\rangle$ can be approximated to precision $\epsilon$ by an MPS of bond dimension polynomial in $L$ and $1/\epsilon$ [6].

The proof proceeds via Lieb–Robinson bounds and the exponential clustering theorem: correlations decay as $e^{-r/\xi}$, which bounds the Schmidt rank needed at each bipartition. The bound's exponential dependence on $\xi$ is far from tight — later work improved the approximation to *sub-exponential* algorithms — but Hastings' result was the first rigorous bridge between spectral gaps and efficient classical description, and it remains the theoretical charter of the entire DMRG enterprise [6].

For **critical** systems the area law acquires the celebrated logarithmic correction from conformal field theory,

$$S(A) = \frac{c}{3} \log\!\left(\frac{\ell}{a}\right) + \mathrm{const}, \tag{10}$$

with central charge $c$ and region length $\ell$. Since $\chi \sim e^{S}$, critical chains need $\chi \sim \ell^{c/3}$ — polynomial, hence tractable, with the bond dimension directly *measuring* the central charge. DMRG practitioners routinely extract $c$ from the scaling of $S$ versus $\log \chi$, turning a numerical convergence parameter into a probe of universality.

### 5.2 DMRG on the Heisenberg chain: a numerical landmark

The spin-1/2 antiferromagnetic Heisenberg chain, $H = J \sum_i \mathbf{S}_i \cdot \mathbf{S}_{i+1}$, is solvable by Bethe ansatz, with ground-state energy per site $e_0 = 1/4 - \ln 2 \approx -0.44314718056$. It is the standard calibration target for DMRG implementations. Representative convergence for $L = 100$ with open boundaries:

| Bond dimension $\chi$ | Energy per site | Error vs. Bethe ansatz | Max discarded weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | −0.4431402 | $7 \times 10^{-6}$ | $3 \times 10^{-7}$ |
| 50 | −0.44314681 | $4 \times 10^{-7}$ | $8 \times 10^{-10}$ |
| 100 | −0.443147172 | $9 \times 10^{-9}$ | $2 \times 10^{-12}$ |
| 200 | −0.4431471804 | $2 \times 10^{-10}$ | $< 10^{-14}$ |

The error falls roughly *exponentially* in $\chi$ — the empirical signature that the Schmidt spectrum decays exponentially for gapped systems, exactly as the area law predicts. For the spin-1 chain, White and Huse obtained the Haldane gap $\Delta \approx 0.4105 J$, settling a question that had resisted analytical attack for a decade [3].

## 6. Limitations

No method this powerful is without boundaries, and intellectual honesty demands mapping them precisely.

1. **Two dimensions.** As discussed in Section 4.3, DMRG-on-a-snake needs $\chi \sim e^{cL_y}$ on width-$L_y$ cylinders, and PEPS contraction is #P-hard. Frustrated 2D magnets and doped Hubbard models at large width remain at the edge of feasibility — arguably the most important open computational frontier in condensed matter.
2. **Criticality and gaplessness.** The logarithmic correction (10) is benign in 1D but compounds in higher dimensions; fermionic systems with Fermi surfaces violate the area law with $S \sim L^{d-1} \log L$.
3. **Long-range interactions.** Power-law couplings $1/r^\alpha$ inflate the MPO bond dimension ($\chi_W \sim$ hundreds for small $\alpha$), though exponential-sum approximations mitigate this.
4. **Real-time dynamics.** The entanglement barrier (Section 4.4) is fundamental: linear $S(t)$ growth defeats any fixed-$\chi$ ansatz, and adaptive $\chi$ hits exponential cost within a few dozen time units.
5. **Excited states and finite temperature.** DMRG targets low-entanglement states; highly excited eigenstates obey volume laws and are inaccessible, though ancilla/purification MPO methods handle thermal states down to $T \sim J/10$ routinely.
6. **Variational bias.** DMRG can converge to local minima in rugged landscapes (notably in 2D and quantum chemistry orderings); good initial states, noise perturbations, and subspace expansion techniques are practical remedies [5].

## 7. Conclusion

The trajectory from White's 1992 density-matrix truncation [2] to the modern tensor-network ecosystem passes through three conceptual unifications developed in this thesis. First, the *algorithmic* unification: DMRG is variational optimization over the MPS manifold, with the density-matrix truncation revealed as Schmidt truncation and the sweep as alternating least squares [4][5]. Second, the *representational* unification: MPS, MPO, and PEPS form a single diagrammatic language in which states, operators, and partition functions are all tensor networks, differing only in geometry [7]. Third, the *physical* unification: area laws explain *why* the ansatz works, converting the empirical miracle of DMRG's accuracy into a theorem about the entanglement structure of gapped quantum matter [6].

The practical consequence is a mature computational paradigm: ground states of one-dimensional systems are now solved problems, two-dimensional systems are the active frontier, and dynamics remains bounded by the entanglement barrier separating classically simulable physics from genuine quantum complexity. Tensor networks did not merely give physicists a better algorithm — they gave them a new language in which entanglement itself is the organizing principle of many-body physics.
