# Post-Quantum Digital Signatures: Lattice-Based Schemes, Hash-Based SPHINCS+, and the NIST Standardization

## Abstract

The advent of cryptographically relevant quantum computers threatens every deployed digital signature scheme: Shor's algorithm reduces the integer factorization and discrete logarithm problems, on which RSA, ECDSA and EdDSA rest, to polynomial time. In response, NIST's post-quantum standardization program has produced the first federal signature standards — ML-DSA (FIPS 204, from CRYSTALS-Dilithium), SLH-DSA (FIPS 205, from SPHINCS+), and FN-DSA (FIPS 206, from FALCON) — founded on module lattices and hash functions rather than number-theoretic assumptions. This thesis surveys the mathematical foundations, construction, security arguments and measured performance of the three standards. We explain the rejection-sampling and trapdoor-sampling techniques that make lattice signatures practical, describe the hypertree and FORS few-time constructions underlying SPHINCS+, and compare parameter sets, proof strength and side-channel posture across all three. We conclude with open problems in tight security reductions, side-channel hardening and hybrid migration that the cryptographic community must address before these schemes carry the world's authentication infrastructure.

## 1 Introduction

Digital signatures are the trust anchor of the modern Internet. They authenticate software updates, certificates, firmware images, financial transactions and diplomatic communications. Today that trust rests almost entirely on two computational assumptions — the hardness of integer factorization and the hardness of the discrete logarithm problem — which a sufficiently large fault-tolerant quantum computer would destroy through Shor's 1994 algorithm [1]. Harvest-now-decrypt-later adversaries compound the urgency: signatures embedded in long-lived certificates and firmware may need to remain unforgeable decades into the future.

In 2016 the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) launched a public competition for post-quantum cryptographic primitives. From 82 submissions, after three rounds and six years of cryptanalysis, NIST selected on July 5, 2022 its first group of standards: CRYSTALS-Kyber for key encapsulation, and **CRYSTALS-Dilithium, FALCON and SPHINCS+** for digital signatures [2]. The first three standards were finalized on August 13, 2024, under new names:

- **FIPS 203** — ML-KEM (Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism), the primary KEM
- **FIPS 204** — ML-DSA (Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm), the primary signature scheme, based on CRYSTALS-Dilithium
- **FIPS 205** — SLH-DSA (Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Algorithm), the backup signature scheme, based on SPHINCS+

FALCON was renamed **FN-DSA** (FFT over NTRU-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm) and finalized as **FIPS 206**. On March 11, 2025, NIST further selected HQC, a code-based KEM, as a backup for ML-KEM [2].

> **Theorem:** *Security reduction sketch.* ML-DSA is strongly existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack (SUF-CMA) assuming the hardness of Module-LWE, Module-SIS, and the novel SelfTargetMSIS problem, in the quantum random oracle model [3].

This thesis answers three questions: (i) what mathematical structures make each scheme plausibly quantum-resistant, (ii) how the constructions translate abstract hardness into concrete, implementable algorithms, and (iii) how the three standards compare on size, speed and robustness — and where the remaining research gaps lie.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The quantum threat

Classical public-key cryptography assumes certain problems are intractable for both classical and (conjectured) quantum computers. Shor's algorithm, running on a machine with a few million physical qubits and sufficient coherence, solves factorization and discrete logarithms in polynomial time, immediately breaking RSA-2048 and ECDSA-P256. Grover's algorithm gives only a quadratic speedup against symmetric primitives and hash functions, which is countered by doubling key and digest sizes. Consequently, post-quantum cryptography concentrates on problems for which **no** efficient quantum algorithm is known: structured lattices, hash-function preimage resistance, error-correcting codes, and isogenies (the latter largely abandoned after the classical break of SIKE in 2022 [2]).

### 2.2 Lattices and hard problems

A *lattice* is the set of all integer linear combinations of a basis of linearly independent vectors in $R^n$. Cryptographic constructions use structured lattices — ideal and module lattices — arising from rings such as $R_q = Z_q[x]/(x^n + 1)$, which admit fast NTT-based arithmetic while resisting known quantum attacks. The core hardness assumptions are:

- **SIS (Short Integer Solution):** given a random matrix $A$ over $Z_q$, find a short non-zero vector $z$ with $Az = 0$. Module-SIS (MSIS) generalizes this to module lattices.
- **LWE (Learning With Errors):** distinguish samples $(a, \langle a, s \rangle + e)$ from uniform random, where $s$ is secret and $e$ small noise. Module-LWE (MLWE) is the module analogue.
- **NTRU:** find short vectors in the lattice defined by the relation $h = g \cdot f^{-1}$ in $R_q$, where $f, g$ are small polynomials.

These problems reduce from worst-case lattice problems (approximate SVP), so breaking average-case instances implies breaking every instance of a worst-case problem — a stronger foundation than the average-case hardness underlying RSA [3].

### 2.3 Hash-based signatures: a historical note

Hash-based signatures are the oldest class of post-quantum signatures, originating with Lamport one-time signatures (1979) and Merkle trees. They depend only on the security of a hash function — the most conservative assumption available. Their drawback is signature size and, in stateless variants, signing speed, which motivates the hypertree composition of SPHINCS+.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis is primarily *theoretical and comparative*, grounded in the published specifications, security proofs and cryptanalysis literature rather than new experiments. We proceed in four stages:

1. **Specification extraction.** We take the finalized FIPS documents and the NIST IR 8413 third-round status report [5] as ground truth for parameters, API and claimed security categories.
2. **Construction analysis.** For each scheme we trace the path from hardness assumption through the signing construction, identifying the novel technique that makes it practical: rejection sampling for Dilithium, fast Fourier (trapdoor) sampling for FALCON, and the hypertree/FORS composition for SPHINCS+.
3. **Proof inventory.** We catalog the known security reductions — their models (ROM vs. QROM), tightness, and the assumptions they introduce (notably SelfTargetMSIS for Dilithium [3] and multi-target second-preimage notions for SPHINCS+ [6]).
4. **Comparative evaluation.** We tabulate public-key sizes, signature sizes and reported performance at equivalent NIST security categories, and assess implementation maturity and side-channel exposure.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 CRYSTALS-Dilithium and ML-DSA (FIPS 204)

Dilithium, by Ducas et al., is a Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts lattice signature built on MLWE and MSIS [7]. Key generation samples a random matrix $A \in R_q^{k \times l}$ from a seed, a secret $s_1 \in R_q^l$ and $s_2 \in R_q^k$ with small coefficients, and publishes $t = As_1 + s_2$ (compressed). Signing samples a masking vector $y$ with coefficients bounded by $\gamma_1$, computes $w = Ay$, hashes $(w, \mu)$ with the message digest $\mu$ to a sparse challenge polynomial $c$ with exactly $\tau$ nonzero coefficients of magnitude one, and forms $z = y + c s_1$. If $z$ or the low bits of $w - c s_2$ are too large, the attempt is *rejected* and signing restarts — the rejection-sampling step that makes $z$ statistically independent of the secret.

Verification recomputes $w$ from the public key via the identity $Az - c t \approx Ay$, checks the norm bounds, and re-derives the challenge. Because no trapdoor sampling is required, Dilithium admits simple, constant-time, deterministic ("hedged" in FIPS 204) implementations [4].

FIPS 204 defines three parameter sets, mapped to NIST security categories:

| Parameter set | Category | Public key (B) | Signature (B) |
|---|---|---|---|
| ML-DSA-44 | 2 (128-bit) | 1,312 | 2,420 |
| ML-DSA-65 | 3 (192-bit) | 1,952 | 3,293 |
| ML-DSA-87 | 5 (256-bit) | 2,592 | 4,595 |

ML-DSA is NIST's **primary** signature standard: the best-understood, most-implemented, and fastest lattice scheme [2][4].

```python
# Simplified Dilithium signing sketch (hedged randomness, per FIPS 204)
def mldsa_sign(sk, m, rnd):
    A, t, s1, s2 = expand_key(sk)
    mu = hash(tr || m)          # message representative
    while True:
        y = sample_mask(rnd, mu)  # coefficients bounded by gamma1
        w = A @ y
        c = challenge(hash(w_highbits, mu))  # sparse, tau nonzeros
        z = y + c * s1
        if norm(z) < gamma1 - beta and norm(lowbits(w - c*s2)) < gamma2 - beta:
            return (z, hint(w, c, s2), c)
```

### 4.2 FALCON and FN-DSA (FIPS 206)

FALCON (Fouque, Hoffstein, Kirchner, Lyubashevsky, Pornin, Prest, Ricosset, Seiler, Whyte, Zhang) takes the opposite design point: signatures over NTRU lattices via *trapdoor sampling*. The secret key is a short basis $(f, g, F, G)$ of the NTRU lattice satisfying the NTRU equation $fG - gF = q$; the public key is $h = g \cdot f^{-1} \bmod\ q$. Signing hashes the message to a target point $c$ and uses the trapdoor basis to sample a lattice vector close to $c$ — the celebrated GPV framework instantiated with the fast Fourier sampler over the tower of cyclotomic fields [7].

The payoff is dramatic compactness: FALCON-512 offers a 897-byte public key and roughly 666-byte signatures at category 1, and FALCON-1024 roughly 1,793-byte keys with 1,280-byte signatures at category 5 — the smallest of any NIST candidate. The price is implementation complexity: the floating-point FFT sampler is notoriously hard to make constant-time, and FALCON's side-channel posture remains the least mature of the three. NIST nevertheless standardized it as FN-DSA (FIPS 206) for applications where bandwidth dominates, such as DNSSEC and constrained devices [2].

### 4.3 SPHINCS+ and SLH-DSA (FIPS 205)

SPHINCS+ is *stateless* hash-based: its security reduces to properties of the underlying hash function (second-preimage resistance, pseudorandomness) rather than any structured hardness assumption [6]. The construction layers three components:

1. **WOTS+** — a Winternitz one-time signature with chaining function and checksum, used at the leaves.
2. **FORS** — a few-time signature: $k$ trees of height $a$, where the signer reveals one leaf per tree, tolerating a bounded number of reuses.
3. **Hypertree** — a certification tree of Merkle trees of total height $h$ (split into $d$ layers), where each WOTS+ key signs the root of the tree below.

To sign, the message is hashed to select a FORS keypair and hypertree leaf index pseudorandomly; the signature bundles the FORS signature plus the $d$-layer WOTS+ signatures and authentication paths. Because leaf selection is pseudorandom, no signer state is needed — a decisive usability advantage over stateful schemes like XMSS/LMS (standardized separately in SP 800-208).

FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) standardizes 12 parameter sets across security categories 1, 3 and 5, in "small" (s) and "fast" (f) variants. Representative sizes: SLH-DSA-SHA2-128s has a 32-byte public key and 7,856-byte signatures; the 128f variant trades 17,088-byte signatures for much faster signing. Signatures are large and signing is slow, but SLH-DSA is NIST's designated **backup** if lattice assumptions fail [2][6].

```haskell
-- SPHINCS+ hypertree signing, simplified
sphincsSign :: SecretKey -> Message -> Signature
sphincsSign sk msg =
  let (forsIdx, leafIdx) = prfSelect sk msg   -- pseudorandom, stateless
      forsSig  = forsSign (forsKey sk forsIdx) msg
      authPath = hypertreeSign sk leafIdx (forsRoot forsSig)
  in Signature forsSig authPath
```

### 4.4 Comparative parameter landscape

| Scheme | Standard | Assumption | pk (cat. 1/2) | sig (cat. 1/2) | Sign speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-DSA | FIPS 204 | MLWE + MSIS | 1,312 B | 2,420 B | fast |
| FN-DSA (FALCON) | FIPS 206 | NTRU | 897 B | ~666 B | moderate |
| SLH-DSA | FIPS 205 | hash only | 32 B | 7,856 B (s) / 17,088 B (f) | slow |

The table makes the standardization logic transparent: ML-DSA is the balanced default, FN-DSA the compact specialist, SLH-DSA the conservative hedge [2][4][5].

### 4.5 Migration and hybrid considerations

NIST, NSA's CNSA 2.0 suite and industry guidance converge on *hybrid* deployment during transition: X.509 certificates and TLS handshakes carrying both a classical and a post-quantum signature, so security holds if either assumption survives. CNSA 2.0 mandates ML-DSA-87 (category 5) for national-security systems, with LMS/XMSS approved for firmware signing [8]. Protocol work remains: larger signatures strain TLS record limits, DNS UDP packet sizes and embedded stacks, motivating ongoing research into signature compression (e.g., forced pruning for SPHINCS+ variants [9]) and aggregate schemes.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Provable security.** Dilithium's original submission proved EUF-CMA security in the (classical) random oracle model from MLWE and MSIS [7]; the novel SelfTargetMSIS assumption entered with the tight proof attempt, and Kelsey–Miller–Wang subsequently gave the first QROM reduction of SelfTargetMSIS from MLWE, yielding a full post-quantum proof under the ring condition $q = 1 \bmod 2n$ at the cost of larger parameters [3]. For SPHINCS+, Hülsing and Kudinov repaired the tight security proof via the WOTS-TW variant, recovering nearly the claimed bound up to a factor of the Winternitz parameter $w$ [6]; a 2026 analysis further removed heuristic multi-target assumptions, recovering up to 18 bits of classical and 9 bits of quantum security in the concrete bounds for SLH-DSA parameter sets. FALCON inherits the GPV reduction from NTRU-SIS, though its concrete proof is the least formalized of the three.

**Measured performance.** Reference and AVX2-optimized implementations show ML-DSA-44 signing in the low hundreds of thousands of cycles with verification roughly twice as fast; FALCON-512 verification is exceptionally fast (tens of thousands of cycles) while signing is costlier due to the FFT sampler; SLH-DSA-128f signs in tens of millions of cycles — orders of magnitude slower, acceptable only for infrequent operations like code signing [5].

**Cryptanalytic scrutiny.** Six years of the NIST process produced no break of any finalist's core assumption. Notable events sharpened parameters rather than killing schemes: the 2022 observation that SPHINCS+ with SHA-256 fell short of category 5 in the DM-SPR model led to a forgery attack analysis reducing concrete security by about 40 bits for affected sets, and NIST's final SLH-DSA parameters account for it. Fault-injection attacks demonstrated practical universal forgeries against unprotected SPHINCS implementations on microcontrollers, underscoring that side-channel hardening — not the math — is the binding constraint for hash-based deployment [10].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Proof gaps.** No scheme enjoys a tight QROM proof at standardized parameters without extra assumptions: Dilithium needs SelfTargetMSIS, SPHINCS+ needs multi-target idealizations (recently reduced but not eliminated), and FALCON's concrete reduction is the least developed [3][6].
2. **Side channels.** Rejection sampling (Dilithium), floating-point FFT sampling (FALCON) and hash-chain indexing (SPHINCS+) each create timing and fault attack surfaces; practical fault attacks already exist against SPHINCS [10], and constant-time FALCON remains research-grade.
3. **Size and speed costs.** Even the most compact post-quantum signatures are an order of magnitude larger than ECDSA's 64 bytes, with protocol-level consequences for TLS, DNSSEC and IoT that are still being engineered around.
4. **Assumption monoculture.** Two of the three standards rest on module lattices; a single algorithmic breakthrough in lattice cryptanalysis would endanger both ML-DSA and FN-DSA simultaneously — precisely the scenario SLH-DSA hedges against, at steep performance cost.
5. **Migration inertia.** Hybrid certificates double handshake sizes during transition, and long-lived signed artifacts (firmware, legal documents) face a harvest-now-forge-later window that no algorithm agility can retroactively close.

## 7 Conclusion

NIST's signature standardization marks the first generational turnover of public-key cryptography in forty years. ML-DSA brings lattice signatures into the mainstream with a clean Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts design and the strongest implementation story; FN-DSA offers unmatched compactness through NTRU trapdoor sampling for bandwidth-starved settings; SLH-DSA provides a hash-only sanctuary whose security rests on the single most conservative assumption in cryptography. Together they form a layered defense: a fast default, a compact specialist, and a conservative backup. The mathematics is standardized; the remaining work — tight quantum proofs, side-channel-hardened implementations, protocol integration and hybrid migration — is engineering and cryptanalysis at civilization scale. The quantum clock is ticking, but for the first time, the replacement infrastructure exists.
