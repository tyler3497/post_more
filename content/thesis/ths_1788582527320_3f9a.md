# Correctness in Unbounded Time: Event-Time Stream Processing with Watermarks, Trigger Semantics, and Exactly-Once State

## Abstract

Unbounded data streams arrive unordered, incomplete, and unbounded: event-time semantics requires systems to reason about when events *occurred* rather than when they were *observed*. This thesis develops a rigorous account of event-time stream processing centered on watermarks—monotone assertions about temporal completeness—and the mechanisms that reconcile correctness, latency, and cost. We formalize the watermark algebra: per-input minima, min-propagation through operator DAGs, and the invariants relating watermarks to window triggering. We then analyze the two dominant architectural families: MillWheel's per-key checkpointed state with low watermarks and idempotent exactly-once delivery [1], and the Dataflow/Beam model of windows, triggers, and accumulation modes that generalizes windowing into a four-dimensional what/where/when/how framework [2]. We derive the allowed-lateness semantics of Apache Flink—trigger firings, late-element side outputs, and the correctness conditions under which late state re-fires are sound—and prove that watermark advancement plus barrier-aligned snapshots yields effectively-once state under the Chandy–Lamport construction [5,6]. Finally, we compare Flink and Dataflow watermark implementations [4] and survey the empirical results reported in the literature, concluding with the known limitations: idle-partition watermark starvation, heuristic watermarks, and the fundamental impossibility of perfect temporal completeness.

---

## 1. Introduction

Modern data infrastructure is dominated by *unbounded* datasets: sensor telemetry, clickstreams, financial ticks, and log events that never terminate and for which completeness is never attainable. Classical batch analytics assumed *bounded, complete* inputs; streaming systems must instead produce *useful results from incomplete inputs*, revising them as more data arrives. The central tension is threefold [2]:

1. **Correctness**: results must reflect events in *event time*—when things happened in the world—not *processing time*—when the system observed them.
2. **Latency**: speculative early results are often worth more than perfect late ones.
3. **Cost**: every revision, every retained state entry, and every speculative computation consumes resources.

The mathematical object that makes this trilemma tractable is the **watermark**: a time value *t* such that the system asserts no future input will carry an event time *e < t*. Watermarks originated as MillWheel's **low watermark** [1], were generalized into the Dataflow model [2], and are now the correctness backbone of Apache Flink [6,7], Apache Beam [8], and Google Cloud Dataflow.

This thesis contributes a unified formal treatment of event-time processing with watermarks, covering (i) the watermark algebra and its propagation invariants, (ii) the MillWheel execution model with exactly-once semantics via per-key checkpointing, (iii) the Beam/Dataflow decomposition of windowing into windows, triggers, and accumulation modes, (iv) Flink's allowed-lateness machinery and its correctness proof sketch, and (v) out-of-order correction through stateful recomputation, culminating in an empirical survey and a formal analysis of the inherent limits of the approach.

> **Theorem:** *Completeness is undecidable.* No streaming system can distinguish a permanently absent event from an arbitrarily delayed one; watermarks are therefore heuristics with formally characterized error semantics, not oracles.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Time domains

Every stream-processing record carries two distinct timestamps [2]:

| Dimension | Definition | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| **Event time** *e* | When the event occurred in the source domain | Assigned by the producer; may arrive out of order; reflects reality |
| **Processing time** *p* | When the system observes the record | Monotone within a machine; reflects observation, not reality |
| **Ingestion time** *i* | When the record entered the system | Compromise: monotone, source-independent, but reality-blind |

Processing-time windows are trivially implementable but semantically weak: results depend on the arbitrary interleaving of network delays and restarts, making them **non-deterministic**. Event-time windows are deterministic with respect to the data but require a mechanism for judging completeness—enter watermarks.

### 2.2 Windows

A window assignment function maps each element with event time *e* to a set of windows *W(e)*. Common strategies:

- **Fixed (tumbling) windows**: *W(e) = [⌊e/s⌋·s, ⌊e/s⌋·s + s)* for size *s*; each element belongs to exactly one window.
- **Sliding windows**: size *s*, period *p < s*; each element belongs to *s/p* windows.
- **Session windows**: dynamically keyed intervals split by gaps exceeding a timeout *g*; data-driven and unbounded in count.

Windows are the *where* of the Dataflow decomposition [2]; watermarks and triggers are the *when*.

### 2.3 Delivery semantics

Three classical guarantees appear in the literature:

1. *At-most-once*: records may be lost; no duplicates. Cheap but incomplete.
2. *At-least-once*: no loss, but duplicates possible; consumers must be idempotent.
3. *Exactly-once* (or **effectively-once**): each record's *effect* is applied exactly once, even if physical delivery is retried. This is achieved through idempotent updates and transactional state commits, not through physically-once delivery, which is impossible under failure [1,6].

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in four stages:

1. **Formalization.** We define the watermark lattice and propagation rules as a monotone dataflow analysis over the operator DAG, and prove the *min-propagation invariant*.
2. **System reconstruction.** We reconstruct MillWheel's low-watermark mechanism [1] and the Dataflow model's trigger/accumulation algebra [2] in a common notation, making their design decisions commensurable.
3. **Correctness derivation.** We derive Flink's allowed-lateness semantics [6,7] from the formalization, showing precisely which reorderings are sound and which require stateful recomputation.
4. **Empirical synthesis.** We collect reported experimental results—MillWheel's scale-out numbers, Flink's state-backend benchmarks—and evaluate the comparative watermark study of Begoli et al. [4].

All code examples are presented in pseudocode or standard JVM/Python syntax for clarity; they illustrate semantics rather than production APIs.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Watermark Algebra

Define the **watermark** *W* at an input channel as an element of the extended time domain *T ∪ {−∞, +∞}* satisfying:

> **Definition (Watermark):** A watermark *W(t) = τ* is a contract: the producer asserts that all future records on this channel carry event time *e ≥ τ*. The producer may advance *τ* monotonically but never retract it.

*Monotonicity* is the load-bearing invariant. Because watermarks never decrease, downstream operators can permanently *garbage-collect* state for event times below *τ*.

**Propagation rule (min-of-inputs).** An operator with input watermarks *τ₁, …, τₙ* and internal hold *h* (the earliest event time the operator itself still needs, e.g., because of pending timers) emits downstream watermark

```
τ_out = min(τ_1, ..., τ_n, h)
```

This is exactly the *greatest lower bound* in the time lattice and preserves the contract: if no input will deliver anything below *min(τᵢ)*, and the operator holds nothing below *h*, then nothing the operator emits downstream can invalidate *τ_out*.

```python
# Pseudocode: watermark propagation at an operator
def on_watermark(i, new_tau):
    input_wm[i] = max(input_wm[i], new_tau)   # monotone
    hold = min(pending_timer_times(), default=INF)
    emit_watermark(min(min(input_wm), hold))
```

**Idle-partition hazard.** If one input partition goes silent, *min(τᵢ)* stalls and the entire downstream watermark stalls—a single idle Kafka partition can freeze event-time progress globally. Production systems counter this with *idleness detection*: if a partition emits nothing for *δ*, its watermark is extrapolated to the global minimum [7]. This is a deliberate, documented weakening of the contract.

**Heuristic watermarks.** In practice, watermarks are often *bounded-out-of-orderness* estimates: *τ = max_observed_event_time − bound*. These are heuristics—violations (elements with *e < τ*, i.e., **late elements**) are inevitable. The remainder of the thesis is largely about managing this inevitability gracefully.

### 4.2 MillWheel: Low Watermarks and Exactly-Once State

MillWheel [1] (Google, VLDB 2013) processes streams as a directed graph of *computations*; each record is a *(key, value, timestamp)* triple. Its two defining ideas:

1. **Low watermarks** substitute for ordering: instead of requiring in-order delivery, each stage maintains a *low watermark*—a tight bound on the timestamps of all events still in the system. A stage knows that when its low watermark passes *t*, all events with time ≤ *t* have been processed, and can safely emit aggregates and release state.

2. **Per-key fine-grained checkpointing with idempotent delivery.** Every record delivery is *deduplicated* by a persistent per-key journal: a record is applied to per-key state only if its unique ID has not been seen. On failure, a computation restarts from its last checkpoint and replays; duplicates are absorbed by the idempotence check. The result is **exactly-once processing semantics** at internet scale.

```scala
// Pseudocode: MillWheel-style idempotent state update
def process(record: Record, state: KeyedState): Unit = {
  if (deduplicationLog.contains(record.id)) return      // absorb duplicate
  deduplicationLog.add(record.id)
  state.update(record.key, f(state.get(record.key), record.value))
  maybeCheckpoint(state)                                 // fine-grained
}
```

MillWheel also introduced **timers**: callbacks scheduled against either the low watermark or wall-clock time, which generalize periodic roll-ups. Notably, a watermark is just a special timer: *fire when W ≥ t* is the trigger primitive of the whole model.

Empirically, MillWheel demonstrated millions of events per second with low latency and exactly-once semantics in production at Google (the Zeitgeist trending-queries pipeline being the motivating example) [1]—a landmark showing that exactly-once need not imply high latency.

### 4.3 The Dataflow Model: Windows, Triggers, Accumulation

The Dataflow model [2] (Akidau et al., VLDB 2015) decomposes every unbounded computation along four orthogonal axes:

| Axis | Question | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| *What* | Which results are computed | ParDo / GroupByKey transforms |
| *Where* | Over which event-time intervals | Windowing (fixed, sliding, session) |
| *When* | At which processing times results materialize | **Triggers** |
| *How* | How refinements relate to earlier outputs | **Accumulation modes** |

**Triggers** specify *when in processing time* the output for a window is materialized, as predicates over the watermark, processing time, and element counts. Examples:

- `AfterWatermark.pastEndOfWindow()` — fire when the watermark passes the window end (the "on-time" pane).
- `AfterProcessingTime.pastFirstElementInPane().plusDelayOf(d)` — speculative early firing.
- `Repeatedly.forever(AfterCount(1000))` — periodic updates.

**Accumulation modes** specify *how* successive panes combine:

- **Discarding**: each pane contains only new arrivals; downstream sums panes.
- **Accumulating**: each pane is the full window state so far; downstream replaces prior output.
- **Accumulating & retracting**: emits retractions of previously emitted values, enabling correct downstream aggregation under arbitrary revisions.

The combination is remarkably expressive. A pipeline that fires early speculative results every minute, fires the on-time result at the watermark, and refines with accumulating mode for late data is written declaratively:

```java
// Beam-style pseudocode: early/on-time/late trigger composite
PCollection<KV<K, Long>> sums = events
  .apply(Window.into(FixedWindows.of(Duration.standardMinutes(5)))
     .triggering(AfterWatermark.pastEndOfWindow()
        .withEarlyFirings(AfterProcessingTime
            .pastFirstElementInPane().plusDelayOf(Duration.standardMinutes(1)))
        .withLateFirings(AfterCount(1)))
     .withAllowedLateness(Duration.standardHours(2))
     .accumulatingFiredPanes())
  .apply(Sum.longsPerKey());
```

The cost of flexibility is *state retention*: with allowed lateness *L*, per-window state must be kept until *W ≥ window_end + L*, plus buffering for the triggers. The model makes this trade-off explicit and programmable.

### 4.4 Flink: Watermark Propagation, Allowed Lateness, and Snapshots

Apache Flink [6,7] implements event-time processing with a concrete distributed protocol:

1. **Watermark emission.** Sources (e.g., Kafka consumers) emit watermarks based on partition timestamps, typically *max_event_time − bounded_out_of_orderness*. Watermarks flow as *stream elements* through the DAG; operators apply the min-of-inputs rule (§4.1). Multi-input operators (joins, unions) stall on the slowest input, modulo idleness handling.

2. **Triggering on watermarks.** Event-time timers are registered per key and window; when the operator's watermark *τ* reaches a timer time *t*, the timer fires, the window function executes, and results are emitted. Late elements (*e < τ*) arriving afterwards are either dropped, routed to a **side output**, or—if *e ≥ window_end + allowed_lateness* has not yet expired—trigger a **late firing** that recomputes and re-emits the window result.

3. **Allowed lateness and state retention.** `allowedLateness(d)` keeps window state until *τ ≥ window_end + d*. After that, state is purged and later elements are irrecoverably late (or side-output). This bounds the *memory cost of correctness* by a user-chosen constant—the Dataflow correctness/latency/cost trilemma made operational.

4. **Fault tolerance via barrier snapshots.** Flink's checkpointing injects **barrier** markers into the streams; operators snapshot their state (including watermark and timer state) upon barrier arrival, following the Chandy–Lamport distributed-snapshot protocol [5,6]. Sources record offsets; sinks participate in two-phase commit for end-to-end exactly-once. Recovery rewinds to the latest completed checkpoint and replays—duplicates absorbed by state-restore idempotence.

```python
# Pseudocode: Flink-style event-time window operator
class EventTimeWindowOp:
    def on_element(self, e):
        if e.ts < self.watermark:
            if e.ts >= self.window_end + self.allowed_lateness: pass
            elif e.ts >= self.window_end: self.late_fire(e)   # recompute
            else: self.side_output.emit(e)                    # too late
        else:
            self.state.add(e); self.register_timer(self.window_end)
    def on_watermark(self, tau):
        self.watermark = max(self.watermark, tau)
        for t in self.timers_due(tau): self.fire_window(t)
        self.purge_windows_older_than(tau - self.allowed_lateness)
```

**Correctness sketch.** We claim: *for any window W with allowed lateness L, the final emitted pane equals f({e : e ∈ W and e arrived before τ ≥ end(W)+L}), and no element is applied twice.* Proof sketch: (i) state updates are keyed and deterministic functions of the multiset of applied elements; (ii) barrier snapshots capture exactly the state as of a consistent cut, so replay after recovery re-applies precisely the post-cut elements, and elements applied pre-cut are not re-applied because the source offsets are part of the snapshot; (iii) late firings recompute *f* over the full accumulated multiset, and accumulating-mode downstream replaces rather than sums, so re-fires are idempotent at the sink. ∎

### 4.5 Out-of-Order Correction via Stateful Recomputation

When a late element arrives within the allowed-lateness horizon, the system must *correct* already-emitted results. Three strategies exist, in increasing power and cost:

1. **Discard-and-ignore**: late data is dropped. Cheapest; silently wrong for affected windows.
2. **Accumulating re-fire**: recompute the window aggregate over retained state and emit the corrected pane downstream, which replaces the earlier one. Correct if downstream is *replacing*; cost is one recomputation plus retained state.
3. **Retractions**: emit *(old_value, −1)* and *(new_value, +1)* deltas so that even non-replacing downstream (e.g., a global sum) stays correct. This is the most general mechanism and the basis of changelog-based systems.

The key insight of the Dataflow model [2] is that these are not system-level policies but *per-window user choices*: speculation frequency, lateness tolerance, and revision strategy are programmed per computation, because the optimal point on the correctness–latency–cost frontier is workload-dependent.

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Proofs

### 5.1 Reported empirical results

- **MillWheel [1]** reported production deployments processing *millions of events per second* with low-latency exactly-once processing; the paper's evaluation shows near-linear scale-out and checkpoint overhead small enough to sustain sub-second latencies at Google scale.
- **Flink [6]** ("State Management in Apache Flink," VLDB 2017) evaluates state backends (heap vs. RocksDB) on workloads with *hundreds of gigabytes* of keyed state across thousands of nodes, showing that asynchronous incremental snapshots keep checkpoint times in the low seconds with negligible throughput impact.
- **Begoli et al. [4]** ("Watermarks in Stream Processing Systems," VLDB 2021) provide the first systematic comparative semantics of Flink vs. Dataflow watermarks, documenting the observable differences: Flink watermarks are *operator-local* stream elements with min-propagation and idleness timeouts, while Dataflow watermarks are *globally coordinated* with a more conservative advancement policy, trading latency for completeness confidence.

### 5.2 Formal results

> **Theorem:** *(Min-propagation soundness).* If every input channel satisfies its watermark contract and operators emit *τ_out = min(τᵢ, hold)*, then the output contract holds: no future output invalidates *τ_out*.

*Proof.* Suppose some input later delivers *e < τᵢ* for channel *i*—contradicts the input contract. Suppose the operator's own logic emits an output with timestamp *t < hold*—contradicts the definition of *hold* as a lower bound on needed/emitted times. Hence all future outputs carry *t ≥ min(τᵢ, hold) = τ_out*. ∎

> **Theorem:** *(Exactly-once state under barrier snapshots).* Under the Chandy–Lamport barrier protocol with source offsets in the snapshot and deterministic keyed state updates, the recovered state equals the state of a failure-free execution that processed exactly the pre-cut prefix of each input.

*Proof sketch.* Barriers induce a consistent cut: each operator snapshots after processing all pre-barrier and no post-barrier records on every input (alignment). Source offsets recorded at the cut identify the pre-cut prefix. On recovery, operators restore snapshot state and sources replay from recorded offsets; deterministic updates then reproduce exactly the pre-cut state evolution. Elements processed pre-cut but whose effects were snapshotted are not re-applied because their offsets are ≤ the recorded offset. ∎

> **Theorem:** *(Lateness bound completeness).* With allowed lateness *L* and monotone watermarks, every element with *e ≥ end(W)* that arrives before *τ ≥ end(W) + L* is included in some emitted pane of *W*; elements arriving after may be lost.

This is immediate from the purge rule in §4.4 and is the precise sense in which "correctness" is parameterized by *L*.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Watermark starvation from idle or skewed inputs.** The min-of-inputs rule means one stalled partition stalls global event-time progress. Idleness timeouts mitigate this but weaken the contract to a heuristic; choosing the timeout is itself an unprincipled tuning parameter [4,7].
2. **Heuristic watermarks and unprincipled lateness.** Bounded-out-of-orderness watermarks are guesses about network behavior. When the guess is wrong, results are silently incomplete unless allowed lateness covers the gap—and lateness costs state. There is no free lunch: *correctness insurance is paid in memory*.
3. **State retention costs.** Per-key, per-window state retained for *window_end + L* can be enormous for high-cardinality keys with long lateness horizons; RocksDB-backed state spills to disk but at a throughput cost [6].
4. **Non-determinism under speculation.** Early firings make outputs depend on processing-time interleavings; two runs over identical event streams can emit different *sequences* of panes (though the final on-time/late-corrected panes converge). Reproducibility requires fixing the processing-time schedule—impossible in production.
5. **Exactly-once is effectively-once, and only with cooperation.** End-to-end exactly-once requires transactional or idempotent sinks; with a naive sink, Flink/MillWheel provide exactly-once *state* but at-least-once *output*. The literature's "exactly-once" claims must be read with this qualification [6].
6. **Session windows and data-driven explosion.** Sessionization can create unboundedly many windows per key under adversarial input, and watermark-based garbage collection only helps after sessions close—a determined adversary can exhaust state regardless of *L*.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Event-time stream processing with watermarks resolves the central dilemma of unbounded data—how to compute correct results over inputs that are never complete—by replacing the impossible demand for *ordering* with the tractable demand for *temporal completeness assertions*. The watermark algebra (monotone min-propagation) gives these assertions a compositional semantics; MillWheel showed they enable exactly-once processing at internet scale through per-key checkpointed state [1]; the Dataflow model turned them into a programmable four-dimensional design space of windows, triggers, and accumulation modes [2]; and Flink operationalized them with barrier snapshots, allowed lateness, and side outputs for late data [6,7]. The comparative study of Begoli et al. [4] confirms that while implementations differ in advancement policy and coordination, the underlying contract is shared. Yet fundamental limits remain: completeness is undecidable, watermarks are heuristics, and every increment of correctness is purchased with latency or state. The mature engineering position—embodied in all these systems—is not to eliminate the correctness–latency–cost trilemma but to make it *explicit, measurable, and programmable*.
