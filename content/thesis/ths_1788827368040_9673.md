# Physics-Informed Recurrent Learning for Tokamak Disruption Forecasting: Architecture, Stability Priors, and Uncertainty-Quantified Alarms

## Abstract

Plasma disruptions—the abrupt loss of magnetic confinement in tokamaks—are the central operability risk for ITER and future burning-plasma reactors, where a single unmitigated event can halt operation and damage plasma-facing components. This thesis surveys and extends the physics-informed deep-learning program for disruption *prediction*, centered on the Fusion Recurrent Neural Network (FRNN) of Kates-Harbeck *et al.* [1] and its interpretability-augmented successor [2]. We formalize disruption forecasting as sequential binary classification over 0-D scalar signals and 1-D diagnostic profiles, with magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) stability priors—normalized beta $\beta_N$ against the Troyon limit, edge safety factor $q_{95}$ against the Kruskal–Shafranov boundary, internal inductance $l_i$, and radiated power—embedded into engineered 1.5-D features. We derive alarm-threshold selection as a cost-asymmetric decision problem constrained by mitigation-actuation latency budgets, and treat uncertainty quantification through Bayesian and ensemble formulations. Empirically, FRNN transfers prediction skill from DIII-D to JET without retraining [1], event-aware models reach 120 ms median warning on EAST [3], and uncertainty-aware predictors sustain >40 ms warnings on KSTAR [6]. We close with the fundamental limits of extrapolating these predictors to reactor-scale burning plasmas.

---

## 1. Introduction

A *disruption* is the fastest large-scale instability in magnetic-confinement fusion: on millisecond timescales, the plasma's stored thermal energy is lost (*thermal quench*), the plasma current decays (*current quench*), and electromagnetic forces and heat fluxes threaten the vacuum vessel and first wall. For ITER—the multibillion-dollar international burning-plasma experiment—the avoidance and mitigation of disruptions is one of the most pressing challenges in fusion-energy science [1]. Because ITER *cannot afford training disruptions* at full performance, any predictor must be validated on existing machines and transferred across devices with quantified confidence.

Deep learning offers a decisive third path beyond first-principles MHD codes (too slow for real-time) and classical shallow classifiers (which discard spatiotemporal structure): the Fusion Recurrent Neural Network [1], a hybrid convolutional–recurrent architecture that ingests 0-D scalar signals and 1-D diagnostic profiles, evaluates a *disruptivity* score every millisecond, and—critically—transfers from the smaller DIII-D tokamak to the larger JET device. This is not merely an accuracy story; it is a *physics-informed representation-learning* story. The network's inputs carry known MHD meaning: electron temperature and density profiles, toroidal rotation, the safety-factor profile, radiated power. The present thesis reconstructs the FRNN program from first principles, generalizes its physics priors, and formalizes the decision-theoretic machinery—thresholds, latency budgets, uncertainty—that turns a score into a reactor-grade alarm.

> **Thesis statement.** Disruption forecasting is best posed as uncertainty-quantified sequential classification over physics-structured features, where MHD stability limits supply inductive bias, alarm thresholds are set by asymmetric operational costs under hard latency constraints, and cross-machine generalization is the decisive evaluation criterion.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Disruption phenomenology and taxonomy

Disruptions are classified by their *trigger mechanisms* [2][5]:

- **Density-limit disruptions**: as the line-averaged density approaches the Greenwald limit $n_G = I_p/(\pi a^2)$ (in units of $10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$, with $I_p$ in MA and $a$ in m), edge cooling and radiation collapse the current channel, destabilizing tearing modes.
- **Beta-limit disruptions**: as the normalized beta $\beta_N = \beta_t(\%)\,aB/I_p$ approaches the Troyon no-wall limit $\beta_N \lesssim 3.5$, ideal and resistive MHD modes (ballooning, external kinks) grow [7].
- **Impurity-driven disruptions**: accumulation of high-Z impurities (e.g., tungsten) raises core radiated power, hollowing the temperature profile and triggering neoclassical tearing modes; the bolometer signal is a key precursor channel [2].
- **Vertical displacement events (VDEs)**: loss of vertical position control drives the plasma into the wall, with large halo currents.
- **Locked modes**: a slowing and locking of rotating MHD modes to the wall, classically the single strongest 0-D precursor.

Each pathway has a characteristic *precursor timescale*—from hundreds of milliseconds (slow density ramp) to a few milliseconds (fast beta collapse)—which is why a single fixed warning horizon is inadequate, and why sequential models with memory are the natural tool.

### 2.2 MHD stability boundaries as inductive bias

Three dimensionless quantities dominate the operational space:

1. **Normalized beta**, $\beta_N = \beta_t(\%) \cdot aB/I_p$. Ideal MHD stability without a conducting wall requires $\beta_N$ below the Troyon limit ($\approx 3.5$); with an ideal wall the limit rises, but resistive-wall modes reclaim part of that gain [7].
2. **Edge safety factor**, $q_{95}$. The Kruskal–Shafranov criterion demands $q_{95} > 2$ (in practice $q_{95} \gtrsim 3$ for H-mode) to avoid the external kink; low-$q_{95}$ operation is disruption-prone [7].
3. **Internal inductance**, $l_i$, which measures current-profile peakedness. High $l_i$ (peaked current) correlates with tearing-mode instability; $l_i$ collapses during the thermal quench.

Classical predictors hand these to shallow classifiers. The deep-learning program instead feeds them—alongside the full 1-D profiles from which they are derived—into a network and lets representation learning discover the nonlinear precursors, while the stability boundaries remain available as *physics priors* for feature engineering, regularization, and interpretation.

### 2.3 From classical ML to deep sequence models

Pre-2019 disruption predictors were dominated by support-vector machines and random forests on 0-D features, achieving respectable AUCs on single machines but generalizing poorly across devices, because each machine's diagnostic set, sampling rates, and operating regimes differ. The DisruptionBench study [4] later systematized this comparison on a common benchmark, evaluating random forests, hybrid deep-learning (HDL) baselines, causal convolutional networks (CCNN), and even GPT-2-style transformers—finding that modern sequence models extend median warning times dramatically (110–350 ms for the transformer variant versus 40–60 ms for RF/HDL). The lesson: *temporal structure matters*, and architectures with explicit memory or attention capture precursor dynamics that static classifiers miss.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Problem formulation

Let a discharge (shot) be a multivariate time series

$$\mathbf{X}_{1:T} = \{\mathbf{x}_t\}_{t=1}^{T}, \qquad \mathbf{x}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_0} \times \mathcal{P},$$

where $\mathbf{x}_t$ concatenates $d_0$ scalar (0-D) signals and a collection $\mathcal{P}$ of 1-D radial profiles (electron temperature $T_e(\rho)$, density $n_e(\rho)$, toroidal rotation, $q(\rho)$). A binary label $y \in \{0,1\}$ indicates whether the shot disrupts. The predictor is a causal mapping

$$f_\theta : \mathbf{X}_{1:t} \mapsto s_t \in [0,1],$$

producing a *disruptivity* score $s_t$ at every time step (1 ms cadence in FRNN [1]). An alarm is raised at the first time $t_a$ with $s_{t_a} \geq \tau$ for a threshold $\tau$; the *warning time* is $t_{\mathrm{disrupt}} - t_a$. Labels are derived from the disruption time identified in the shot database, with a *prediction horizon* (e.g., the final 30–50 ms labeled positive) that encodes how early a forecast counts as correct.

### 3.2 The FRNN architecture

The Fusion Recurrent Neural Network [1] is a two-stream hybrid:

- **Profile stream (spatial):** the 1-D profiles pass through $N$ layers of 1-D convolution (small kernels, $N_F$ filters) interleaved with max-pooling, compressing each profile into a low-dimensional feature vector that preserves the salient radial structure (e.g., hollow $T_e$ from impurity accumulation).
- **Scalar stream (temporal):** 0-D signals (plasma current $I_p$, stored energy $W_{\mathrm{MHD}}$, locked-mode amplitude, $l_i$, $\beta_N$, $q_{95}$, radiated power $P_{\mathrm{rad}}$, density) are concatenated with the profile features.
- **Recurrent core:** the full feature vector feeds a multi-layer LSTM with $M$ layers, whose hidden state carries the plasma's *dynamical history*—essential because identical instantaneous states can have different fates depending on trajectory.
- **Decision head:** a fully connected layer maps the LSTM output to the disruptivity score $s_t$; crossing $\tau$ triggers the alarm that actuates mitigation (e.g., gas injection).

Training confronts extreme class imbalance (a few percent of shots disrupt) and requires careful sampling; the original work leveraged leadership-class supercomputing to train at scale and to evaluate at the 1 kHz real-time cadence [1].

### 3.3 Physics-informed feature engineering: the 1.5-D layer

Raw diagnostics are 0-D (scalars) or 2-D/3-D (images, Thomson profiles). Between them sits a *1.5-D* layer of physics-derived quantities—dimensionless combinations with stability meaning:

```python
def physics_features(shot: dict) -> dict:
    """Engineer 1.5-D stability features from 0-D/1-D diagnostics."""
    Ip, a, B = shot["Ip_MA"], shot["a_m"], shot["Bt_T"]
    beta_t = shot["beta_toroidal_pct"]          # volume-averaged toroidal beta [%]
    beta_N = beta_t * a * B / Ip                # Troyon normalization
    q95 = shot["q95"]                           # edge safety factor
    li = shot["li"]                             # internal inductance
    n_bar = shot["ne_line_avg"]                 # 1e20 m^-3
    n_G = Ip / (np.pi * a**2)                   # Greenwald limit
    f_GW = n_bar / n_G                          # Greenwald fraction
    f_rad = shot["P_rad"] / shot["P_heat"]      # radiated power fraction
    return {
        "beta_N": beta_N,                       # vs Troyon limit ~3.5
        "q95": q95,                             # vs Kruskal-Shafranov q95 > 2
        "li": li,                               # current-profile peakedness
        "f_GW": f_GW,                           # vs density limit f_GW -> 1
        "f_rad": f_rad,                         # impurity/radiation collapse proxy
        "beta_margin": 1.0 - beta_N / 3.5,      # distance to beta limit
    }
```

These features serve three roles: (i) direct inputs that *anchor* the network to known stability boundaries; (ii) targets for auxiliary losses (multi-task learning: predict $\beta_N$ margin alongside disruptivity); (iii) the vocabulary of *sensitivity scores* for interpretation [2].

### 3.4 Sensitivity scores: from prediction to control

A raw disruptivity score answers *when*; a control system needs *why*. The FRNN extension [2] computes a **sensitivity score** for each physics channel—roughly, how much the alarm depends on that channel—enabling the plasma control system (PCS) to choose actuators: impurity-like sensitivity suggests impurity flushing or heating changes; $q_{95}$/kink sensitivity suggests current-profile control. In a demonstrated DIII-D shot, FRNN predicted the disruption with ~1 s warning while sensitivity analysis correctly attributed it to core radiation and $q_{95}$ [2]. This moves the paradigm from *passive prediction* to *active avoidance*.

### 3.5 Uncertainty quantification

Alarms that cry wolf erode operator trust; missed alarms risk the machine. Bayesian formulations address both. A Bayesian neural network with a temporal-convolutional (TCN) backbone on KSTAR [6] decomposes predictive uncertainty into *aleatoric* (diagnostic noise) and *epistemic* (model ignorance) components, achieving >40 ms warnings before thermal quench while showing that false and missed alarms concentrate at high uncertainty—so thresholding on *uncertainty as well as score* suppresses both failure modes. Ensemble and Monte-Carlo-dropout variants provide cheaper approximations of the same idea.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 How much physics should the network be told?

There is a spectrum. At one end, *physics-agnostic* end-to-end learning from raw profiles; at the other, hard-encoded stability margins. The evidence favors the middle: networks trained on profiles *plus* engineered stability features outperform either alone, and physics-guided feature extraction measurably improves both accuracy and interpretability. The IDP-PGFE predictor on J-TEXT [5] extracts physics-guided features (PGFE) before classification, then uses feature-contribution time traces to *explain* disruptions—e.g., showing that resonant magnetic perturbations (RMPs) delay density-limit disruptions not only by affecting MHD but by modifying the radiation profile. The architectural moral: **let the network learn dynamics, but give it the coordinates in which dynamics are simple**—$\beta_N$, $q_{95}$, $l_i$, $f_{GW}$, $f_{\mathrm{rad}}$ are the normal modes of tokamak operability.

### 4.2 Alarm thresholds as cost-asymmetric decisions

> **Theorem:** *For a calibrated disruptivity score $s_t = P(\mathrm{disrupt}\mid \mathbf{X}_{1:t})$ and costs $C_{FA}$ (false alarm: lost shot time, aborted experiment) and $C_{MD}$ (missed disruption: machine damage risk), the expected-cost-optimal alarm threshold is $\tau^* = C_{FA}/(C_{FA} + C_{MD})$, independent of the base disruption rate.*

*Proof sketch.* Alarm when $s \geq \tau$. Expected cost per time step: $E[C] = C_{FA}\,P(s\geq\tau, y=0) + C_{MD}\,P(s<\tau, y=1)$. For calibrated $s$, the marginal decision at $s=\tau$ alarms iff $\tau C_{FA} \leq (1-\tau)\cdot 0 + \tau\cdot 0$... more precisely, conditioning on $s$: alarm minimizes $C_{FA}(1-s)$ vs. $C_{MD}\,s$; indifference gives $\tau^* = C_{FA}/(C_{FA}+C_{MD})$. ∎

Because $C_{MD} \gg C_{FA}$ for ITER (a damaged first wall dwarfs a wasted shot), optimal thresholds sit *low*—but low thresholds inflate false alarms, which is exactly why uncertainty gating [6] and event-aware modeling [3] matter: they push the ROC curve outward rather than sliding along it. In practice, thresholds are also validated against the *warning-time distribution*: a threshold that maximizes AUC but yields warnings shorter than the mitigation latency is operationally useless.

### 4.3 Mitigation actuation latency budgets

Prediction is only half the system; the other half is the *disruption mitigation system* (DMS). A realistic latency budget decomposes as:

| Stage | Budget (representative) |
|---|---|
| Diagnostic acquisition + equilibrium reconstruction | 1–2 ms |
| Predictor inference (FRNN at 1 kHz cadence) | ~1 ms |
| PCS decision + actuator command | 1–5 ms |
| Actuator physics (e.g., gas/pellet flight to plasma) | 10–30 ms |
| **Total required warning time** | **≳ 30–40 ms** |

This budget explains the field's obsession with *warning time*, not just accuracy: the EAST real-time predictor's 94% success rate at 137 ms mean warning [5, suppl.] "fully meets" ITER-class requirements precisely because it clears this budget with margin. Predictors must therefore be evaluated on the joint distribution of (warning time, false-alarm rate), e.g., median warning at fixed FPR = 0.2 as in [3].

The budget can be modeled as a timed state machine:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE DisruptionAlarm ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, TLC
VARIABLES phase, clock
\* phase \in {"nominal", "alarm", "actuated", "quench"}
Init == phase = "nominal" /\ clock = 0
Tick == clock' = clock + 1 /\ UNCHANGED phase
RaiseAlarm == phase = "nominal" /\ phase' = "alarm" /\ clock' = 0
Actuate == phase = "alarm" /\ clock >= 2 /\ phase' = "actuated" /\ UNCHANGED clock
Quench == phase \in {"alarm", "actuated"} /\ clock >= 40
    /\ phase' = "quench" /\ UNCHANGED clock
Next == Tick \/ RaiseAlarm \/ Actuate \/ Quench
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<phase, clock>>
\* Safety: actuation must precede quench whenever an alarm was raised in time.
=====================================================================================
```

### 4.4 Surrogate MHD stability models

Full MHD stability analysis (e.g., DCON, MISHKA) is too slow for the control loop, but its *outputs*—stability margins like the ideal-wall $\beta_N$ limit or the tearing stability index $\Delta'$—are superb training signals. Two uses dominate:

1. **Multi-task supervision:** train the network to predict both disruptivity and MHD margins; the margin heads regularize the shared representation toward physically meaningful latents.
2. **Synthetic augmentation:** MHD simulations generate disruption trajectories in regimes absent from experimental databases (e.g., ITER-relevant low-torque, high-$q_{95}$ scenarios), and domain-adversarial training aligns simulated and experimental feature distributions.

A surrogate maps $(\beta_N, q_{95}, l_i, p'(\rho), \langle j_\parallel\rangle) \mapsto$ stability margin in microseconds, fast enough to run inside the PCS alongside FRNN—physics and learning in a tight loop rather than in competition.

### 4.5 Cross-machine transfer: the decisive test

ITER cannot provide training disruptions, so *transferability is the metric that matters*. FRNN's headline result is precisely this: trained on DIII-D (and JET) data, it predicts disruptions on the *other* machine [1]—evidence that the learned features capture device-independent MHD physics rather than device-specific diagnostic artifacts. Zhu *et al.* [3] pushed this further: an integrated model trained on DIII-D with manually labeled instability *events* (sawteeth, tearing modes, impurity events) transfers to EAST and Alcator C-Mod, delivering 120 ms median warning on EAST at FPR = 0.2 versus 50 ms for the event-agnostic baseline. The event labels act as an *interlingua*: instability physics is universal even when diagnostics differ. Techniques that help include per-device normalization, domain-adversarial feature alignment, and—most powerfully—anchoring inputs to dimensionless physics quantities ($\beta_N$, $q_{95}$, $f_{GW}$) that are comparable across machines by construction.

## 5. Empirical Results

### 5.1 Headline results across the literature

| Study | Architecture | Train → Test | Key result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kates-Harbeck *et al.* [1] | CNN + LSTM (FRNN) | DIII-D ↔ JET | Cross-machine disruption prediction; 1 ms-cadence disruptivity; long-warning forecasts enabling active control |
| Tang *et al.* [2] | FRNN + sensitivity scores | DIII-D → PCS | ~1 s warning on example DIII-D shot; sensitivity attribution to $P_{\mathrm{rad}}$, $q_{95}$; real-time PCS integration |
| Zhu *et al.* [3] | Integrated DL w/ event labels | DIII-D → EAST / C-Mod | 120 ms median warning on EAST at FPR = 0.2 vs. 50 ms baseline; event information doubles effective warning |
| DisruptionBench [4] | RF / HDL / CCNN / GPT-2 | Benchmark suite | Warning times: GPT-2 110–350 ms, CCNN 90–200 ms, RF/HDL 40–60 ms |
| Shen *et al.* (IDP-PGFE) [5] | Physics-guided features + classifier | J-TEXT | Interpretable predictions; RMP/radiation physics of density-limit disruptions recovered |
| KSTAR BNN [6] | Bayesian TCN | KSTAR | >40 ms warning pre-thermal-quench; uncertainty thresholding suppresses false/missed alarms |

### 5.2 Reading the curves

The canonical evaluation plots are (i) the ROC curve (true-positive rate vs. false-positive rate as $\tau$ varies), summarized by AUC; (ii) the *warning-time histogram* at fixed FPR; and (iii) the *success-rate vs. warning-time* trade-off. Three regularities emerge:

1. **Deep sequence models dominate shallow ones** on both AUC and warning time [4], because precursors are dynamical patterns, not static points.
2. **Event information is worth ~2× warning time** [3]: knowing *which* instability is unfolding (tearing, impurity, density limit) beats knowing only *that* the plasma is "disruptive."
3. **Uncertainty is a second decision axis** [6]: gating alarms on low epistemic uncertainty removes the long tail of false alarms without sacrificing recall.

### 5.3 A worked threshold calculation

Suppose a DIII-D-class device disrupts 5% of high-performance shots. Let a false alarm cost one wasted shot-hour ($C_{FA} = 1$) and a missed disruption risk a week of repairs with probability 0.01 per event ($C_{MD} = 0.01 \times 168 \approx 1.7$ in shot-hour units—deliberately conservative). Then $\tau^* = 1/(1+1.7) \approx 0.37$. For ITER, where $C_{MD}$ is orders of magnitude larger, $\tau^*$ collapses toward zero—*unless* uncertainty gating [6] and event-aware features [3] first drive the false-alarm rate down at fixed recall. Threshold selection is thus inseparable from model quality: better models don't just score higher, they *buy back* operating margin.

A minimal decision-policy sketch in a typed functional style:

```haskell
-- Disruption alarm policy: score, uncertainty, and latency budget.
data Verdict = Nominal | Alarm Double | Veto deriving (Show, Eq)

policy :: Double      -- disruptivity score s_t
       -> Double      -- epistemic uncertainty u_t
       -> Double      -- minimum required warning time (ms)
       -> Double      -- estimated time-to-disruption (ms)
       -> Verdict
policy s u tMin tEst
  | u > uMax        = Veto            -- too uncertain: do not alarm
  | s >= tau && tEst >= tMin = Alarm s -- actionable warning
  | otherwise       = Nominal
  where
    tau  = cFA / (cFA + cMD)          -- cost-optimal threshold
    uMax = 0.35                      -- uncertainty gate (cf. [6])
    (cFA, cMD) = (1.0, 170.0)        -- ITER-class cost asymmetry
```

## 6. Limitations

1. **Extrapolation to burning plasmas.** All training data come from non-burning plasmas; ITER's alpha-particle heating, low external torque, and tungsten wall create regimes with no precedent. Cross-machine transfer [1][3] is encouraging but not a guarantee.
2. **Label ambiguity.** "Disruption time" and the positive-label horizon are human choices; different conventions shift reported warning times by tens of milliseconds, complicating cross-study comparison.
3. **Diagnostic dependence.** Models keyed to specific diagnostic sets (e.g., DIII-D's n1rms channel [2]) degrade when channels are missing or recalibrated; dimensionless physics features mitigate but do not eliminate this.
4. **Causality vs. correlation.** Sensitivity scores [2] and PGFE attributions [5] are associational; intervening on the indicated channel does not provably prevent the disruption without closed-loop experiments.
5. **Real-time compute.** 1 kHz inference with equilibrium reconstruction inside the PCS is a systems achievement [2], not a given; larger models (transformers [4]) must be distilled or accelerated to fit the ~1 ms inference budget.
6. **Rare-event statistics.** Disruptions are rare by design (good operation avoids them), so test sets are small and confidence intervals on AUC and median warning time are wide; benchmark suites [4] help but cannot manufacture data.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Physics-informed deep learning has moved disruption prediction from heuristic 0-D alarms to millisecond-cadence, interpretable, uncertainty-aware forecasting. The FRNN program [1][2] established the architecture (convolutional profile encoding + recurrent memory), the evaluation criterion (cross-machine transfer), and the operational goal (from prediction to PCS-integrated control). Subsequent work added the missing decision-theoretic pieces: event-aware features that double warning times [3], physics-guided interpretability [5], and Bayesian uncertainty gating [6]. The remaining gap is the one no database can close: proving these predictors in a burning plasma. The path forward is clear—tighter coupling of learned predictors with surrogate MHD stability models, closed-loop avoidance experiments on DIII-D and EAST, and threshold policies derived from explicit cost and latency models rather than ROC aesthetics. If that program succeeds, disruptions cease to be an existential risk to fusion energy and become a solved control problem.
