# Programmable Bootstrapping in TFHE: Blind Rotation, the Concrete Compiler, and Encrypted Neural Inference over Torus LWE Ciphertexts

## Abstract

Fully homomorphic encryption over the real torus, instantiated by the TFHE family of schemes, derives its practicality from a single operation: the programmable bootstrapping, which refreshes a noisy ciphertext while simultaneously evaluating an arbitrary univariate function encoded in a test polynomial. This thesis develops a rigorous treatment of programmable bootstrapping: the torus-LWE ciphertext hierarchy (TLWE, TRLWE, TRGSW), the blind-rotation algorithm that powers it, the negacyclic constraint on lookup-table functions, and the engineering reality of the Concrete compiler, which lowers high-level FHE programs through MLIR dialects into circuits of bootstrappings, key switches, and packing operations. We examine how Zama's tfhe-rs library exposes this machinery for encrypted integer arithmetic, and how Concrete ML compiles quantized neural networks — including transfer-learned VGG and ResNet variants — into encrypted inference pipelines that evaluate ReLU, sigmoid, and argmax on the server without decryption. Correctness arguments, noise-growth bounds, parameter trade-offs, and measured benchmarks are presented alongside the scheme's fundamental limitations: the negacyclic function constraint, probabilistic failure rates, and the latency wall of per-gate bootstrapping.

## 1 Intro

The promise of fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) — *compute on data that never decrypts* — was settled in principle by Gentry's 2009 breakthrough, yet remained an engineering fiction for a decade: every homomorphic operation injected noise, and the periodic "refresh" operation called **bootstrapping** was so expensive that no non-trivial workload survived it [1]. The TFHE line of work, culminating in the Chillotti–Gama–Georgieva–Izabachène construction, inverted this economy by making bootstrapping *fast enough to do after every gate* [2]. Where the FHEW scheme of Ducas and Micciancio required 690 ms per gate bootstrap with a 1 GB bootstrapping key, TFHE reduced this to roughly 13 ms per gate with a 16 MB key on a single CPU core, a fifty-fold improvement that moved FHE from leveled batch workloads to gate-by-gate evaluation [2].

The deeper innovation is that TFHE's bootstrapping is **programmable**: while refreshing the noise, the bootstrap evaluates an arbitrary univariate function $f$ — a lookup table — on the encrypted message at nearly zero marginal cost [3]. A ReLU, a sign function, a sigmoid approximation: each becomes a single bootstrapping operation. This thesis analyzes the full stack that makes this possible. We study the torus arithmetic and ciphertext hierarchy of §2, the blind-rotation machinery of §3–4, the Concrete compiler pipeline that turns this cryptography into a usable programming model in §4.4, and the encrypted neural inference applications that justify the complexity in §4.5 and §5. Throughout, we ground the exposition in the parameter regimes and measured latencies reported in the literature and in production tooling [4][5].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The torus and its discretizations

TFHE does not work over integers modulo $q$ like textbook LWE. It works over the **real torus** $\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$, the additive group of real numbers modulo 1. Ciphertexts are noised encodings of points on this torus, and the plaintext space is a discrete subset $\mathbb{T}_p = \{\frac{0}{p}, \frac{1}{p}, \dots, \frac{p-1}{p}\}$ for some modulus $p$. This choice is not aesthetic: multiplication results over the torus ring stay bounded (the product of two torus elements is no larger than the multipliers), which keeps noise analysis tractable and enables the compact bootstrapping at the heart of the scheme [2].

> **Theorem:** *Torus scaling.* Let $m_1, m_2 \in \mathbb{T}$ with representatives in $[-1/2, 1/2)$. Then $|m_1 \cdot m_2| \le \max(|m_1|, |m_2|)/2$, so repeated multiplication over the torus cannot cause the plaintext magnitude explosion that plagues integer-modulus FHE rings.

A TLWE ciphertext encrypting $m \in \mathbb{T}$ under secret $\mathbf{s} \in \{0,1\}^n$ is a pair $(\mathbf{a}, b) \in \mathbb{T}^n \times \mathbb{T}$ where $\mathbf{a}$ is uniform random, $e$ is Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma$, and
$$b = \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle + m + e.$$
Decryption computes the **phase** $\phi = b - \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle = m + e$ and rounds to the nearest element of $\mathbb{T}_p$.

### 2.2 The ciphertext hierarchy

TFHE defines three ciphertext types, each a torus generalization of a classical object [2]:

- **TLWE** (torus LWE): scalar ciphertexts, as above; the workhorse of gate evaluation.
- **TRLWE** (torus RLWE): polynomial ciphertexts over $\mathbb{T}_N[X] = \mathbb{T}[X]/(X^N+1)$, encrypting a polynomial $\mu \in \mathbb{T}_N[X]$ of $N$ packed messages; the accumulator of the blind rotation lives here.
- **TRGSW** (torus GSW): matrix/vector ciphertexts of the same secret bit, enabling the **external product** $\boxdot$ between a TRGSW and a TRLWE ciphertext — the operation whose cost dominates bootstrapping.

The ring is the anticyclic (negacyclic) ring $R = \mathbb{Z}[X]/(X^N+1)$ with $N$ a power of two, so $X^N = -1$ and $X^{N+j} = -X^j$. The library ships a dedicated Fast Fourier Transform for this ring with AVX/AVX2/FMA vectorization, and the default parameter sets target at least 110-bit security under ideal-lattice assumptions [2].

### 2.3 Gate bootstrapping in FHEW and its limits

In FHEW, a bootstrapped gate homomorphically decrypts a noisy LWE ciphertext inside an accumulator and extracts the most significant bit via `msbExtract`. Chillotti et al. collapsed this into external products between TRGSW keys and a TRLWE accumulator — cutting 690 ms to 13 ms [2][9]. The question this thesis pursues: once bootstrapping is cheap, *what else can it compute?*

## 3 Methodology

Our method is a layered reconstruction of the TFHE programmable bootstrapping pipeline, verified against published parameter tables and production implementations:

1. **Algebraic layer.** We formalize blind rotation as an external-product cascade over the negacyclic ring, and derive the exact form of the test polynomial that encodes a lookup table.
2. **Noise layer.** We track noise variance through modulus switching, CMux gates, and sample extraction, identifying where the failure probability $p_{\text{err}}$ concentrates.
3. **Compilation layer.** We describe how the Concrete compiler performs noise analysis, parameter selection, and circuit rewriting (packing, unparsing, key-switch insertion) to map high-level programs onto the bootstrapping budget.
4. **Empirical layer.** We compile the benchmark figures reported by Zama's Concrete ML [4][6], the tfhe-rs release notes [10][11], and independent encrypted-inference studies [3][7] into comparative performance tables.

All code sketches are simplified pedagogical renderings of the algorithms described in [2] and implemented in [10].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Blind rotation: the CMux cascade

Blind rotation is the engine. Given a TLWE ciphertext $(\bar{\mathbf{a}}, \bar{b})$ under secret $\bar{\mathbf{s}}$, we first **modulus-switch** the phase from the torus to $\mathbb{Z}_{2N}$:
$$\bar{b}' = \big\lfloor 2N \bar{b} \rceil, \quad \bar{a}'_i = \big\lfloor 2N \bar{a}_i \rceil,$$
so that the unknown phase $\phi = \bar{b} - \langle \bar{\mathbf{a}}, \bar{\mathbf{s}} \rangle$ becomes an integer $\tilde{\phi} \in \mathbb{Z}_{2N}$ with small rounding error. The trick: instead of decrypting, we rotate a known **test polynomial** $\text{testv} \in \mathbb{T}_N[X]$ by $\tilde{\phi}$ positions *without knowing* $\tilde{\phi}$:
$$\text{ACC} \leftarrow \text{testv} \cdot X^{-\tilde{\phi}} = \text{testv} \cdot X^{-(\bar{b}' - \sum_i \bar{a}'_i \bar{s}_i)}.$$

This is done bit by bit. We initialize $\text{ACC} = X^{-\bar{b}'} \cdot \text{testv}$ (a public rotation), then for each $i$, apply a CMux controlled on the encrypted secret bit $\bar{s}_i$ (provided as a TRGSW bootstrapping key $\text{BK}_i$):
$$\text{ACC} \leftarrow \text{CMux}\big(\text{BK}_i,\; X^{\bar{a}'_i}\cdot \text{ACC},\; \text{ACC}\big) = \text{BK}_i \boxdot (X^{\bar{a}'_i} - 1)\cdot\text{ACC} + \text{ACC}.$$

After $n$ iterations, the constant term of ACC equals $f(m)$ up to noise, where $f$ was encoded in $\text{testv}$. A final **SampleExtract** pulls out the TLWE ciphertext of the constant term [2].

```python
def blind_rotate(lwe, bk, testv, N):
    # modulus switch: torus -> Z_{2N}
    b_bar = round(2*N * lwe.b); a_bar = [round(2*N*a) for a in lwe.a]
    acc = testv * X**(-b_bar)          # public rotation by known -b_bar
    for a_i, bk_i in zip(a_bar, bk):   # one external product per secret bit
        acc = cmux(bk_i, X**(a_i) * acc, acc)
    return sample_extract(acc)          # TLWE of f(m) + refreshed noise
```

```haskell
-- the test polynomial encodes a lookup table f : Z_p -> T
testPoly :: (Int -> Double) -> Int -> Int -> [Double]
testPoly f p n = [ f (j * p `div` (2*n)) | j <- [0 .. n-1] ]
-- negacyclicity: X^{n+j} = -X^j forces f(-x) = -f(x)
```

### 4.2 Programmable bootstrapping and the negacyclic constraint

The standard gate bootstrap uses $\text{testv} = \sum_j \mu_j X^j$ with $\mu_j \in \{\pm 1/8\}$ to extract a sign bit. The **programmable** (functional) bootstrap generalizes this: for any function $f: \mathbb{T}_p \to \mathbb{T}$, set $\mu_j = f(j \cdot p / 2N)$ and the bootstrap outputs an encryption of $f(m)$ while refreshing noise — a lookup-table evaluation *for free* inside the refresh [3].

But the negacyclic ring imposes a hard constraint. Since $X^{N+j} = -X^j$ as polynomials, the rotated test polynomial satisfies
$$X^{-\tilde{\phi}} \cdot \text{testv} \;\; \text{at index } 0 = \begin{cases} \mu_{\tilde{\phi}} & \tilde{\phi} < N \\ -\mu_{\tilde{\phi} - N} & \tilde{\phi} \ge N \end{cases}$$
which is consistent only when the encoded function is **anti-symmetric**: $f(-x) = -f(x)$ (up to the torus representation). Functions violating this — the sigmoid being the canonical example — must be decomposed into anti-symmetric parts, evaluated with multiple bootstraps and linear combinations, doubling or tripling cost. This is why ReLU-like activations ($f(x) = \max(0,x)$ shifted to anti-symmetry) dominate encrypted neural networks, while smooth sigmoids are approximated piecewise or replaced by low-degree polynomials [3][7].

> **Theorem:** *PBS correctness.* Let $(\bar{\mathbf{a}}, \bar{b})$ be a TLWE encryption of $m \in \mathbb{T}_p$ with noise amplitude $< 1/(4p)$, let $\text{testv}$ encode an anti-symmetric $f$, and let the bootstrapping key have noise variance $\sigma_{\text{bk}}^2$. Then blind rotation followed by sample extraction yields a TLWE encryption of $f(m)$ whose noise variance is bounded by $C \cdot n \cdot \sigma_{\text{bk}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{round}}^2$ for an explicit constant $C$ depending only on the decomposition parameters — i.e., the output noise is *independent of the input noise*, which is precisely the bootstrapping property.

### 4.3 The Concrete compiler: from MLIR to key-switched circuits

Hand-writing CMux cascades is not a programming model. Zama's **Concrete** compiler makes TFHE usable by compiling high-level programs — including NumPy-style and scikit-learn-style ML models via Concrete ML — through a hierarchy of MLIR dialects down to concrete FHE circuits [4][6]:

1. **Front end.** The user writes ordinary Python (e.g., a quantized neural net). Concrete ML traces it, producing a computation graph over encrypted integers.
2. **High-level FHE IR.** Table lookups (activations, comparisons) become programmable-bootstrapping nodes; arithmetic becomes leveled LWE/RLWE ops. Crucially, the compiler performs *automatic noise analysis*: it propagates noise estimates through the graph and inserts bootstraps exactly where noise would otherwise exceed the decryption bound.
3. **Parameter selection.** From the noise analysis and a target failure probability (production defaults target $p_{\text{err}} \approx 2^{-40}$ per operation, with tfhe-rs v1.1.0 offering 128-bit PBS at $2^{-128}$ on GPU [11]), the compiler selects LWE dimension $n$, ring dimension $N$, and decomposition bases per circuit region — a multi-objective optimization over latency, key size, and failure rate.
4. **Circuit rewriting and packing.** Independent scalar bootstraps are packed into TRLWE slots; key-switching keys are generated to move between secret keys of different dimensions; the final artifact is a circuit of $\boxdot$ products, key switches, and sample extractions with a matching server key [6].

```rust
// tfhe-rs: a programmable bootstrap evaluating a LUT on encrypted integers
let c: FheUint8 = client_key.encrypt(42u8);
let lut = server_key.generate_lookup_table(|x| x.wrapping_mul(3));
let d: FheUint8 = server_key.apply_lookup_table(&c, &lut);
// d decrypts to 126: one PBS refreshed the noise AND applied the function
```

### 4.4 Encrypted neural inference: quantization, LUT activations, and throughput

Neural inference is the killer workload for PBS because its nonlinearities are exactly univariate functions. The recipe used by Concrete ML [4][6]:

- **Quantization-aware training.** Weights and activations are trained at low precision (typically 2–8 bits), so every value fits the plaintext space $\mathbb{T}_p$ of a single PBS. Overflow-penalty regularization and weight-norm constraints keep accumulator widths within the discrete-torus budget [3].
- **Folding.** BatchNorm parameters are folded into preceding convolutions, eliminating an entire layer of bootstraps; convolutions themselves become leveled multiply-accumulate over packed TRLWE ciphertexts.
- **LUT activations.** ReLU, and quantized variants of sigmoid/tanh, are compiled to single programmable bootstraps. An argmax over 10 logits becomes a small tree of comparison bootstraps.

Measured results: Concrete ML reports FHE inference at roughly **4 minutes per image at 88.7% accuracy**, with quantized VGG11 at **90.2% (CIFAR-10)** and **68.2% (CIFAR-100)** in FHE simulation [4]; independent benchmarks show Concrete-ML XGBoost at ~97.7% accuracy taking 118 s single-core, ~17 s on 8 cores [8]. On the library side, tfhe-rs v1.1.0 added 128-bit programmable bootstrapping to its CUDA backend with failure probability $2^{-128}$, matching the CPU parameter sets [11], while the original TFHE library sustains about 50 bootstrapped binary gates per second per core [2].

```tla+
---- MODULE PBS ----
EXTENDS Naturals
VARIABLES acc, phase
Init == acc \in TestPolys /\ phase \in 0..(2*N-1)
CMuxStep(i) == acc' = IF Bk[i] THEN Rot(acc, a[i]) ELSE acc
Spec == Init /\ [][\E i \in 1..n : CMuxStep(i)]_<<acc,phase>>
THEOREM Spec => []<>(acc = testv * X^(-phase))  \* rotation completes
====
```

### 4.5 Circuit bootstrapping

Circuit bootstrapping converts a TLWE ciphertext into a low-noise TRGSW ciphertext of the same message [2], letting PBS outputs feed leveled arithmetic. Without it, bootstrapped values are stuck in the scalar world; with it, TFHE is fully composable — at roughly 10× the cost of a PBS.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Correctness sketch

Correctness rests on three lemmas standard in [2]: (i) modulus switching adds bounded rounding error independent of the secret; (ii) each CMux encrypts the selected input with noise growing *additively* in the bootstrapping-key noise; (iii) sample extraction pulls the rotated constant term into a TLWE ciphertext inheriting the accumulator's noise. Chained, they yield the Theorem of §4.2: output noise depends only on the *key* noise, hence the refresh property. The failure probability follows from a Gaussian tail: $p_{\text{err}} \approx \text{erfc}(1/(2\sqrt{2}\,p\,\sigma_{\text{out}}))$.

### 5.2 Complexity and measured performance

| Operation | Asymptotic cost | Measured (single core) |
|---|---|---|
| TLWE gate bootstrap (PBS) | $O(n)$ external products, each $O(N \log N)$ FFT | ~13 ms [2] |
| Blind rotation, $n=630$, $N=1024$ | $n$ CMux = $n$ $\boxdot$ | ~10–13 ms PBS [2][9] |
| Key switch $n \to k$ | $O(n \cdot t)$ NTT mults | sub-ms |
| Circuit bootstrap TLWE→TRGSW | ~10× PBS | ~100+ ms [2] |
| Concrete ML encrypted inference (CNN) | hundreds–thousands of PBS | ~4 min/image, 88.7% acc [4] |
| Concrete ML XGBoost (tabular) | PBS-heavy tree eval | 118 s (1 core) → 17 s (8 cores) [8] |

### 5.3 Security argument

Security reduces to torus LWE/RLWE with binary secrets; the blind rotation reveals only the public test polynomial at an unknown offset, and the bootstrapping key encrypts the secret key under a larger key — a standard **circular-security** assumption. Defaults target ≥110-bit security [2], modern tfhe-rs sets 128-bit [10][11], and tfhe-rs v1.1.0's noise-squashing APIs address *noise leakage* across decryption oracles [11].

## 6 Limitations

1. **The negacyclic straitjacket.** Only anti-symmetric univariate functions fit in one PBS; everything else costs multiple bootstraps. Multivariate functions (multiplication of two ciphertexts, attention softmax) cannot be done by PBS at all and require leveled arithmetic or client interaction — which is why production systems increasingly adopt *hybrid* client-server execution, trading round trips for speed [3].
2. **Probabilistic correctness.** Every PBS carries $p_{\text{err}} > 0$; over millions of bootstraps (a deep network on a dataset), the union bound bites. Parameter selection must budget failure probability against the total bootstrap count, pushing $N$ and key sizes up.
3. **Key material and memory.** Bootstrapping keys are tens of MB to GB; the server key for a compiled model can exceed the model itself by orders of magnitude..
4. **Latency wall for interactivity.** ~13 ms per bootstrap and minutes-per-image inference rule out real-time applications; current encrypted inference is viable for batch, offline, or high-value queries only.
5. **Compiler opacity.** Automatic parameter selection is a heuristic multi-objective optimization; pathological programs can compile to parameter sets that are slow, insecure-by-analysis, or fail at runtime, and debugging an FHE circuit offers none of the introspection of plaintext code.

## 7 Conclusion

Programmable bootstrapping is the reason TFHE escaped the laboratory: by fusing noise refresh with function evaluation inside the blind rotation, it turned FHE's most expensive operation into its most *useful* one — and the Concrete compiler turned that primitive into a programming model where data scientists compile sklearn-style models to encrypted circuits without touching a CMux. The measured trajectory is real: 690 ms → 13 ms per bootstrap [2][9], CPU → GPU 128-bit PBS [11], minutes-per-image encrypted CNNs at near-plaintext accuracy [4]. Yet the scheme's boundaries are algebraic, not merely engineering: negacyclicity, probabilistic failure, and the per-bootstrap latency floor are intrinsic. The next decade belongs to compilers clever enough to *spend* bootstraps wisely — packing, folding, quantizing, hybridizing — because in TFHE, the bootstrap is both the currency and the computer.
