# Stratospheric Aerosol Injection as a Climate Intervention: Radiative Physics, Multi-Model Evidence, Governance Architecture, and the Physics of Termination Shock

## Abstract

Stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) is the most mature proposal within solar radiation management: continuous delivery of sulfur dioxide to the lower stratosphere, mimicking the planetary cooling after large volcanic eruptions, to offset greenhouse-gas radiative forcing. SAI is treated across four domains. First, the radiative physics: Mie scattering and aerosol microphysics show why mass-normalized radiative efficacy declines with injection rate as coagulation-driven particle growth shifts the size distribution. Second, the climate-model evidence: from the Geoengineering Model Intercomparison Project (GeoMIP) to the feedback-controlled Geoengineering Large Ensemble (GLENS), quantifying both the mean temperature response and the residual regional precipitation and circulation anomalies SAI does not repair. Third, governance: the Convention on Biological Diversity de-facto moratorium, the Environmental Modification Convention, and the proposed international non-use agreement — and why low cost and unilateral deployability generate a structural governance failure. Fourth, termination shock: the rapid rebound of temperature and precipitation when a masking deployment is abruptly halted, controlled by background greenhouse-gas concentrations and deployment duration. SAI is technically feasible but asymmetric, imposing century-scale commitments whose governance is the binding constraint on responsible research.

## 1 Introduction

The 2015 Paris Agreement aims to hold warming well below 2 °C above pre-industrial levels, yet greenhouse-gas concentrations keep rising and current pledges are widely assessed as inconsistent with that target. Against this backdrop, solar radiation management — deliberate modification of the planetary albedo — has become a subject of coordinated assessment, including NOAA research programs initiated by the U.S. Congress [1].

Stratospheric aerosol injection is the most-studied SRM technique [2]. The idea is empirically grounded: explosive volcanic eruptions inject sulfur-bearing gases into the lower stratosphere, where they oxidize and condense into a veil of sulfate aerosol that scatters solar radiation and cools the surface. The 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo lofted roughly 20 megatons of SO₂ into the stratosphere and depressed global mean surface temperature by ~0.5 °C for two to three years. SAI asks whether a continuous, engineered analogue of Pinatubo could hold the planetary energy imbalance near zero [3].

The question divides into four coupled problems. The *physical problem*: what radiative forcing does a given stratospheric sulfur burden produce, and how does aerosol microphysics degrade forcing efficiency as injection rates scale? The *empirical problem*: what do coupled climate models predict under sustained SAI, and which responses are robust? The *governance problem*: who decides, and by what authority, to intervene in a system whose atmosphere belongs to no single state? And the *dynamical risk problem*: what happens if the intervention stops while greenhouse-gas concentrations remain high?

These questions are not independent. The governance problem is sharpened because the physical problem appears tractable: compensating the forcing of doubled CO₂ requires ~1–2 W m⁻² of negative forcing, deliverable by several megatons of sulfur per year at a few billion dollars annually — within the reach of a mid-sized economy [4]. The asymmetry between the ease of deployment and the scale of commitment is the central tension of this thesis.

> Theorem: *No SAI deployment is welfare-improving under abrupt termination risk unless concurrent mitigation holds the masked forcing below a critical threshold; expected termination loss scales with background GHG concentration times deployment duration.*

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Volcanic Analogue and Its Limits

The empirical case for SAI begins with volcanoes. Pinatubo (June 1991) injected ~20 Mt of SO₂ into the tropical lower stratosphere, which oxidized to H₂SO₄ and formed a global sulfate veil: peak aerosol optical depth near 0.15 and global mean surface cooling of roughly 0.5 °C, decaying with ~1-year residence time. Models reproduce this cooling within observational uncertainty — which is why confidence that a sufficient aerosol burden *would* cool the planet is high [1].

Yet the analogy between volcanic pulses and sustained geoengineering is imperfect in four respects. First, volcanic injection is a *pulse*; geoengineering would be a *steady state* at which coagulation degrades scattering efficiency per unit mass [5]. Second, SAI could choose latitudes, altitudes, seasons, and particle composition to shape the forcing pattern. Third, volcanoes inject ash and halogens alongside sulfur, complicating attribution. Fourth, SAI would offset a *growing* greenhouse forcing, so the required injection must rise over time — the commitment problem in miniature.

### 2.2 Radiative Transfer Fundamentals

SAI operates by increasing the planetary albedo: sulfate particles in the lower stratosphere scatter incident solar radiation (Mie scattering, 0.05–0.2 µm accumulation mode) back to space, producing shortwave forcing $\Delta F_{SW} \approx -S_0\,\Delta\alpha_p/4$. Simultaneously, the particles absorb near-infrared and terrestrial longwave radiation, warming the lower stratosphere by several degrees — with consequences for dynamics, ozone chemistry, and the hydrological cycle [5].

Aerosol extinction follows Mie theory. For a size distribution $n(r)$, the aerosol optical depth is

$$\tau(\lambda) = \int_{z_1}^{z_2} \int_0^\infty Q_{ext}(x, m)\, \pi r^2\, n(r)\, dz\, dr,$$

with size parameter $x = 2\pi r/\lambda$ and extinction efficiency $Q_{ext}$. Forcing per unit injected sulfur — the *radiative efficacy* — is maximized when particles remain in the accumulation mode.

### 2.3 Aerosol Microphysics: Nucleation, Coagulation, Sedimentation

Injected SO₂ oxidizes to H₂SO₄ vapor on timescales of weeks, then partitions between nucleation of new particles and condensation onto pre-existing ones. At high injection rates, condensation dominates: added sulfur grows existing particles rather than forming new accumulation-mode particles, so the *number* of efficient scatterers saturates while the *mass* burden keeps rising, and larger particles sediment faster (Stokes velocity ∝ $r^2$). This is the central nonlinearity of SAI: **doubling injection yields less than double the forcing** [5].

Two engineering responses have been explored. Direct injection of accumulation-mode particles — H₂SO₄ or SO₃ vapor released in an aircraft wake, coagulating in the confined plume into the 0.05–0.2 µm distribution — produces larger burdens and better efficacy than equivalent SO₂ emissions, confirmed in interactive chemistry-climate-aerosol models [5]. Alternative particle compositions (calcite, titania, diamond dust) could reduce stratospheric heating or ozone damage but are far less mature; this thesis treats sulfate as the reference case.

### 2.4 GeoMIP, GLENS, and the Feedback-Control Turn

The Geoengineering Model Intercomparison Project (GeoMIP), formed in 2011, coordinated common experiments: uniform solar reduction (G1) and specified SO₂ injections (G3, G4) [5]. These established the headline result — models robustly cool the surface under SAI — but also exposed a weakness: equatorial injection cools the tropics excessively relative to high latitudes, shifting meridional temperature gradients and tropical precipitation patterns [4].

The methodological response was the Geoengineering Large Ensemble (GLENS): 20 members in CESM1(WACCM) injecting SO₂ at four latitudes with a **feedback controller** adjusting each latitude's injection annually to hold $T_0$, $T_1$, and $T_2$ near 2020 values against an RCP8.5 background [6]. The controller is a three-step degree-of-freedom adjustment: after each simulated year, total injection $\ell_0$ corrects $T_0$, hemispheric asymmetry $\ell_1$ corrects $T_1$, and latitudinal distribution $\ell_2$ corrects $T_2$ — constrained because aerosols can only be added [7]. GLENS showed that controlling gradients as well as the global mean substantially reduces side effects [6].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Model Hierarchy

Our synthesis draws on three tiers of modeling, each answering different questions:

1. **Radiative and microphysical models.** Mie codes plus sectional aerosol microphysics establish the forcing-per-mass relationship and optimal size distribution [5].
2. **Chemistry-climate models with interactive aerosol.** CESM1(WACCM) with the Modal Aerosol Module (MAM3), used in GLENS, couples aerosol microphysics to stratospheric chemistry and dynamics [6][9].
3. **Detection and attribution frameworks.** Analyses of GeoMIP G4 (5 Tg SO₂ yr⁻¹, 2020–2070) test whether the SAI influence on global-mean temperature is detectable [8].

### 3.2 Experimental Design Conventions

Key design choices recur across studies and must be understood to compare results:

- **Background scenario.** Early GeoMIP used RCP4.5; GLENS used high-forcing RCP8.5 for signal-to-noise, inflating late-century injection requirements (up to ~50 Mt SO₂ yr⁻¹ by 2100 in some NOAA-led simulations [1]) — not a deployment proposal.
- **Injection specification.** Equatorial point injection versus four-latitude feedback control produce materially different regional climates; comparisons ignoring strategy conflate method with physics [9].
- **Objectives.** Single-objective (global mean only) versus multi-objective control (means plus gradients, or precipitation-based objectives [7]) determine the side-effect profile.

### 3.3 Analytical Methods Used Here

We synthesize published ensemble means and spreads. For termination dynamics (Section 5), abrupt cessation sets $F_{SAI} \to 0$ on the aerosol decay timescale (~1–2 years), so the system relaxes toward the unmasked equilibrium with the ocean-mixed-layer time constant — far faster than the background scenario [2][10]. For governance, we compare prohibitory precedents: chemical and biological weapons, ENMOD, the ozone regime, and the Antarctic Treaty system [11].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Radiative Efficacy, Particle Size, and the Sublinear Forcing Law

The key quantitative relationship in SAI physics is between injection rate $I$ (Tg S yr⁻¹) and steady-state radiative forcing $F$ (W m⁻²), well approximated by a concave power law

$$F(I) \approx a \, I^b, \qquad b \in [0.5, 0.8],$$

the coagulation penalty. Tropical injection at 20–25 km maximizes residence time via the Brewer–Dobson circulation; higher-altitude injection increases efficacy but also stratospheric heating [5][9].

Representative model-derived requirements (spread roughly ±30%):

| Study / configuration | Target | SO₂ injection (Tg yr⁻¹) | Forcing (W m⁻²) |
|---|---|---|---|
| GeoMIP G4 (multi-model) | Offset RCP4.5 drift | 5 | ~−1.5 |
| GLENS 2050 (RCP8.5) | Hold T₀,T₁,T₂ at 2020 | ~10–15 | ~−2.5 |
| GLENS 2090 (RCP8.5) | Hold T₀,T₁,T₂ at 2020 | ~40–50 | ~−5 to −6 |
| Equatorial only, 2×CO₂ | Offset 4×CO₂/2 | ~8–12 | ~−3.7 |

### 4.2 What the Models Robustly Show — and What They Don't

The multi-model evidence supports robust conclusions:1. **Surface temperature.** SAI reliably cools the surface; ~1–2 W m⁻² of negative forcing offsets RCP4.5-class warming [5]. The feedback-controlled GLENS design holds $T_0$, $T_1$, and $T_2$ near baseline for eight decades [6].
2. **Hydrology.** SAI and CO₂ do not cancel in the water cycle: solar reduction suppresses evaporation more per degree of cooling than CO₂ warms per degree, so an SAI-held climate has *less* precipitation — a residual drying of ~2–4% persists in many models [4].
3. **Regional winners and losers.** Equatorial injection overcools the tropics and undercools the Arctic; multi-latitude injection largely repairs this, but residual anomalies remain in monsoon regions, the storm tracks, and Antarctic sea ice [6][9].
4. **Stratospheric heating.** Aerosol absorption warms the tropical lower stratosphere by 1–3 °C, strengthening the polar vortex and perturbing the Quasi-Biennial Oscillation, with downstream surface effects [9].

What models do *not* robustly show: ecosystem and agricultural responses are model-dependent, and aerosol–cloud interactions of a stratospheric veil are essentially unrepresented [9].

```haskell
-- Multi-objective feedback controller, after Kravitz et al. (2017).
-- T0 ~ l0; T1 ~ l0,l1; T2 ~ l0,l1,l2. Constraint: l_i >= 0 (aerosols only added).
data Climate = Climate { t0 :: Double, t1 :: Double, t2 :: Double }
data Inject  = Inject  { l0 :: Double, l1 :: Double, l2 :: Double }

step :: Climate -> Climate -> Inject -> Inject
step target obs inj =
  let e0 = t0 target - t0 obs
      inj'  = inj { l0 = max 0 (l0 inj + k0 * e0) }
      e1 = t1 target - t1 obs
      inj'' = inj' { l1 = max 0 (l1 inj' + k1 * e1) }
      e2 = t2 target - t2 obs
  in inj'' { l2 = max 0 (l2 inj'' + k2 * e2) }
  where (k0, k1, k2) = (0.8, 0.6, 0.5)
```

### 4.3 Stratospheric Chemistry, Dynamics, and the Ozone Ledger

Sulfate aerosol is not chemically inert. Its surfaces host heterogeneous reactions — notably $\mathrm{N_2O_5 + H_2O \to 2HNO_3}$ and chlorine activation $\mathrm{ClONO_2 + HCl \to Cl_2 + HNO_3}$ — that liberate ozone-destroying radicals. Under the Montreal Protocol's declining halogen trajectory, models project sustained SAI delays the ozone hole's return to 1980 levels by one to several decades [5].

### 4.4 Governance: The Structural Failure

If the physics is tractable, the governance is not. Two structural features make SAI ungovernable:

- **Low cost, unilateral deployability.** Cost estimates cluster in the single-digit billions of dollars per year — roughly 0.01% of global GDP. A single state could deploy without others' consent, and attribution would be difficult for years [8][12].
- **The non-use proposal and its discontents.** A coalition of 60+ scientists and governance scholars has called for an International Non-Use Agreement [13], arguing deployment cannot be governed fairly or effectively [14]. Critics counter that a ban would freeze needed research and that prohibition without verification is unenforceable [11].

The game-theoretic core is a *free-driver* problem: any single actor can impose its preferred climate on everyone, so the equilibrium deployment reflects the most risk-tolerant actor's preferences — and counter-geoengineering is physically possible [12].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Forcing, Efficacy, and the Injection Requirement

Three results anchor the physical case. First, the volcanic calibration: Pinatubo's ~20 Mt SO₂ produced peak forcing near −3 W m⁻² and ~0.5 °C cooling — a transient efficacy of ~0.15 W m⁻² per Mt SO₂, an upper bound [3][5]. Second, GeoMIP G4's sustained 5 Tg SO₂ yr⁻¹ yields ~−1.5 W m⁻² in the multi-model mean, falling toward ~0.1 W m⁻² per Tg yr⁻¹ at several tens of Tg yr⁻¹ [5][8]. Third, holding temperature flat under continued emissions growth to 2100 could require up to ~50 Mt SO₂ yr⁻¹ [1].

*Proof sketch (sublinearity).* Increasing injection $I$ grows particle radius $r$ faster than number concentration $N$, while scattering efficiency per unit mass peaks at fixed $r^* \approx 0.1\,\mu$m. Hence marginal forcing $dF/dI$ is strictly decreasing: $F(I)$ is concave. ∎

### 5.2 Termination Shock: Dynamics, Magnitude, and Determinants

Termination shock is the most-studied catastrophic failure mode of SAI. If a deployment masking substantial greenhouse forcing is abruptly halted — through war, economic collapse, sabotage, or political reversal — temperatures rebound toward their un-geoengineered trajectory within years, since the veil decays with a ~1–2 year residence time while masked CO₂ forcing persists for centuries [2].

*McCusker et al. (2014)* quantified this across GHG scenarios and termination years: post-termination warming reaches 0.3–0.6 °C per decade — two to four times the background anthropogenic rate — with the largest changes *relative to historical variability* in the tropics, striking South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa hardest [10]. The controlling variables are **background GHG concentration and deployment duration** [2][10].

Economics sharpens the stakes: a hypothetical SAI program holding warming to ~2.8 °C could halve climate damages, but sudden termination with a 0.6 °C rebound over eight years could push damages above $1 trillion — *worse than never having deployed* [15]. A 2026 analysis argues the risk has been "significantly overestimated" — but concedes the surest protection is rapid emissions cuts so SAI is never needed at scale [16].

> Theorem: *Termination rate bound.* Let $F_m$ be the masked forcing and $\tau_a \approx 1.5$ yr the aerosol decay time. Post-termination warming satisfies $\dot{T} \sim \lambda F_m / \tau_{eff}$; for $F_m = 4$ W m⁻² and $\lambda = 0.8$ K (W m⁻²)⁻¹, $\dot{T} \sim 0.3$–$0.6$ °C decade⁻¹ — several times faster than any sustained rate in the instrumental record.

| Scenario | Masked forcing (W m⁻²) | Rebound rate (°C/decade) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| G4-like, RCP4.5 | ~1.5 | ~0.2–0.3 | [8][10] |
| GLENS-scale, RCP8.5 | ~4–5 | ~0.4–0.6 | [6][10] |
| Economic model, 2.8 °C cap | ~2 | 0.6 °C / 8 yr | [15] |
| Gradual phase-out + mitigation | declining | near background | [2][16] |

### 5.3 Detectability: Could We Even Tell It's Happening?

GeoMIP G4 analyses show the SAI fingerprint in global-mean temperature is detectable within years at 5 Tg yr⁻¹, but attributing *how much* cooling is due to SAI — required for feedback control — demands separating the SAI signal from internal variability and the evolving GHG baseline [8]. This "attribution gap" means a real deployment's control loop would operate under persistent uncertainty about its own efficacy.

## 6 Limitations

**Model structural uncertainty.** Inter-model spread in SAI forcing for identical SO₂ injection is roughly ±30%, and some regional responses differ in sign across models [5][9].

**Missing processes.** Aerosol–cloud interactions of the stratospheric veil and diffuse-radiation fertilization of the carbon sink are crudely represented or absent [3].

**Ecological unknowns.** Crop models suggest cooling dominates for heat-stressed agriculture, but altered sunlight, UV, and precipitation create regional losers invisible to global-mean analysis [10].

**Governance as the binding constraint.** The free-driver structure, the verification problem, and the absence of an enforcement-capable regime mean the "optimal" modeled deployment may not be implementable [11][12][14].---

## 7 Conclusion

Stratospheric aerosol injection is a paradox made of physics. Radiatively, it is the best-understood climate intervention: Mie scattering, the volcanic calibration, and GeoMIP/GLENS evidence agree that a sustained sulfate veil would cool the planet and that feedback-controlled multi-latitude injection can hold global temperature and gradients near baseline for decades [5][6][8].

But every refinement sharpens the risks. The sublinear forcing law means the commitment *accelerates*: holding temperature flat against rising greenhouse forcing demands a superlinear ramp in injection, stratospheric heating, and ozone penalty [1][5]. The hydrological cycle is reconfigured, not repaired, with residual drying and regional losers even under the best-designed strategies [4][6]. And termination physics converts the enterprise into a multi-century commitment: stop abruptly, and masked warming returns at 0.3–0.6 °C per decade, with damages potentially exceeding those of never having intervened [2][10][15].

The binding constraint is institutional, not technical: a unilaterally deployable, post-hoc-verifiable intervention with century-scale commitments and no enforcement-capable treaty is ungovernable [11][12][14]. Research — including outdoor experiments under the CBD's assessment framework — is warranted precisely because the risks of both deployment *and* uninformed unilateral action are large. **Deployment** at scale is not warranted without deep mitigation bounding the masked forcing, a verifiable governance regime, and a credible century-scale commitment mechanism. The atmosphere is a commons. SAI would make that commons a control system — and control systems without accountable controllers are not engineering. They are gambles.
