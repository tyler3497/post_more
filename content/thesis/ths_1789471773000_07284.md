# Generational and Concurrent Garbage Collection: Algorithmic Foundations, Barrier Mechanics, and Formal Correctness

## Abstract

Automatic memory management has evolved from simple stop-the-world tracing into concurrent, generational, and machine-verified systems underpinning modern runtimes. This paper unifies generational and concurrent garbage collection around the tri-color abstraction introduced by Dijkstra and colleagues in 1978 [2]. We taxonomize write and read barriers — Dijkstra, Steele, and Yuasa styles — and show how each preserves the tri-color invariant under mutator interference. We analyze the generational hypothesis and its embodiment in remembered sets, card tables, and promotion policies, from Ungar's generation scavenging [3] to the region-based designs of G1 [5], Shenandoah [7], and ZGC [8]. We trace the path from mostly-concurrent mark-sweep [6] to fully concurrent compaction via Brooks forwarding pointers and colored pointers, and survey collector verification from hand proofs to Boogie/Z3-mechanized assembly-level correctness [9][10]. Empirical results from Hertz and Berger [11] frame collection's throughput cost, and proof sketches establish snapshot-at-the-beginning safety. We close on open limits: barrier overhead, remembered-set pressure, and the verification gap for concurrent compacting collectors.

## 1 Introduction

Few subsystems shape the performance envelope of managed languages like the garbage collector. Whether the mutator halts, how cross-generational references are tracked, and which barrier guards a field store ripple into tail latency, throughput, footprint, and cache behavior. The field has converged on two complementary strategies: *generational* collection, which exploits the empirical fact that most objects die young, and *concurrent* collection, which overlaps collector work with mutator execution to bound pauses [1].

These strategies interact subtly. Generational collection needs a *write barrier* plus *remembered set* to track references crossing generation boundaries. Concurrent collection needs barriers of a different flavor — ones that preserve the tri-color invariant while the mutator rearranges the object graph beneath the collector. The barrier thus plays two roles: efficiency mechanism for generational collectors, correctness mechanism for concurrent ones. Confusing them is a perennial source of bugs and muddled exposition.

This paper contributes: (1) a unified taxonomy of tracing collectors grounded in the tri-color abstraction; (2) a barrier algebra characterizing Dijkstra, Steele, and Yuasa write barriers and read barriers (Baker, Brooks, colored-pointer), with invariant-preservation arguments; (3) an architectural survey of production concurrent collectors — mostly-concurrent mark-sweep [6], G1 [5], Shenandoah [7], ZGC [8]; and (4) a verification survey from Dijkstra's hand proof to mechanically checked collectors [9][10], with a TLA+ sketch of the tri-color invariant.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Classical tracing collectors

*Mark-sweep* (McCarthy) halts the mutator, marks objects reachable from the roots, and sweeps unmarked cells onto a free list [1]. Simple and motion-free, but *fragmentation* scatters free memory and degrades allocation to free-list search. *Mark-compact* slides survivors together after marking — the three-pass *Lisp 2* algorithm computes forwarding addresses, rewrites references, then moves objects — at the cost of updating every pointer. *Copying* collectors (Cheney semispace) copy live objects between halves in one breadth-first pass driven by a scan pointer, leaving perfect compaction but halving usable heap and re-copying long-lived data each cycle [1]. *Reference counting* (Collins) reclaims eagerly via per-object counts but cannot collect *cycles* without auxiliary tracing and is cache-hostile on pointer updates.

### 2.2 The tri-color abstraction

Dijkstra, Lamport, Martin, Scholten, and Stenning's 1978 "On-the-fly garbage collection" introduced the abstraction organizing the next half-century of collector design [2]. Objects are **white** (reclamation candidates), **grey** (reachable, children unscanned — the *wavefront*), or **black** (reachable and fully scanned). Roots are greyed; the collector repeatedly greys a grey object's white children and blackens it. At quiescence, white objects are unreachable. The governing property is the **tri-color invariant**: *no black object references a white object*. It holds trivially when the mutator is halted. The entire difficulty of concurrent collection is that a running mutator can install a pointer from a black object to a white one, hiding live data and causing it to be freed while reachable. Every concurrent collector is, at bottom, a mechanism — the *barrier* — for preventing exactly this.

### 2.3 Terminology

The *mutator* is the application (it mutates the object graph); the *collector* reclaims. A *stop-the-world* (STW) pause halts all mutator threads. *Pause time* is the worst such halt; *throughput* is mutator progress per unit time; *footprint* is memory consumed. *Mutator utilization* (MMU) generalizes pause time into the mutator's guaranteed time fraction over any window.

## 3 Methodology

This paper is an analytic survey with constructive proof sketches. We model the heap as a directed graph and collection as a state machine over tri-colorings, stating correctness as invariants in Dijkstra's style [2]. Section 5 sketches a mechanizable proof of snapshot-at-the-beginning (SATB) safety. We classify barriers by *trigger* (read vs. write) and *preservation strategy* (which object is shaded or logged), yielding a designer-facing decision table. Finally, we compare G1, Shenandoah, ZGC, and mostly-concurrent mark-sweep on pause-time scaling, barrier cost, fragmentation, and memory overhead, grounded in published papers and JEPs [5][6][7][8]. Notation: *p.f := q* denotes storing *q* into field *f* of *p*; *q_old* is the overwritten value; colors are **W**, **G**, **B**.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Generational Hypothesis and Remembered Sets

Measurements beginning with Ungar's Smalltalk studies established the **weak generational hypothesis**: *most objects die young* [3]. A stronger form adds that old objects rarely reference young ones. If it holds, frequently scanning only young objects reclaims most garbage while touching little heap, cutting work and pauses. Lieberman and Hewitt proposed segregating objects into *generations*; Ungar's *generation scavenging* made it practical with new/old ages plus survivor spaces, at roughly 2.5 percent of CPU time [3]. Modern collectors use a *nursery* (eden plus survivors) and a *tenured* generation, *promoting* objects that survive several collections.

The catch is old-to-young references: collecting the nursery alone requires knowing every inbound reference from the old generation. The **remembered set** records them, maintained by a **write barrier** on every pointer store: when *p.f := q* runs with *p* old and *q* young, the slot is recorded [1][3]. To keep the barrier cheap, production collectors use **card tables**: the old generation is split into cards (typically 512 bytes) with dirty bits, so the barrier is a single byte store and minor collection scans only dirty cards. G1 refines this with per-region remembered sets. The Hudson–Moss *train algorithm* handles a generational pathology — mutually referencing mature objects pinning each other — by clustering them into cars and trains collected as units [1].

| Design | Granularity | Barrier cost | Scan cost | Used by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Precise remembered set | Per-slot | High | Low | Early generational collectors |
| Card table | 512-byte card | Very low | Medium | HotSpot, G1 |
| Card + region sets | Region | Low | Low | G1 |
| Trains | Car/train | Medium | Medium | Mature-object clustering |

### 4.2 The Barrier Zoo: Dijkstra, Steele, and Yuasa

A concurrent mutator breaks the invariant in exactly one dangerous way: store *p.f := q* with *p* black and *q* white hides *q*. Three classical write barriers prevent this [1][2]:

**Dijkstra** (shade the *target*): on *p.f := q*, grey *q* if white. Directly preserves the strong invariant, at the cost of a color test per store.

**Steele** (shade the *container*): on *p.f := q*, re-grey *p* if black. Temporary black-to-white edges are allowed but every black object acquiring a white child is re-scanned. Introduced for multiprocessing compacting collection [4]; the basis of *incremental update* collectors.

**Yuasa** (shade the *overwritten*): on *p.f := q*, grey *q_old*. The collector then maintains **snapshot-at-the-beginning** (SATB): the marked set covers everything reachable at cycle start. Objects dying mid-cycle are still marked (*floating garbage*, reclaimed next time), but nothing reachable at start is lost. SATB dominates modern designs — G1, Shenandoah, ZGC all use Yuasa-style barriers — because the fast path needs no color test: it unconditionally logs the overwritten reference.

> **Theorem:** *A concurrent marker with a Yuasa barrier on every store marks a superset of the objects reachable at marking start.*
> *Proof sketch.* Take *o* reachable at start via some root path. If no store touches the path's edges during marking, the marker's own traversal reaches *o*. Otherwise the first overwritten edge's Yuasa barrier logs the displaced reference — a node on the original path — re-anchoring the suffix. Induction over the path reaches *o*. ∎

**Read barriers** intercept loads to support concurrent *relocation*: Brooks *forwarding pointers* (one word per object, used by Shenandoah for concurrent evacuation [7]); ZGC's *colored pointers*, which steal high bits of the 64-bit pointer for mark/relocation metadata so a *load barrier* self-heals stale pointers without per-object overhead [8]; Azul C4's *loaded value barrier*, trapping to GC threads for pauseless operation at the cost of per-load work.

| Barrier | Trigger | Action | Invariant | Representative |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dijkstra | Store *p.f := q* | Grey *q* if white | Strong tri-color | On-the-fly marker [2] |
| Steele | Store *p.f := q* | Re-grey *p* if black | Incremental update | Multiprocessor compacting [4] |
| Yuasa | Store *p.f := q* | Log *q_old* | Snapshot-at-the-beginning | G1, Shenandoah, ZGC |
| Brooks | Load of reference | Follow forwarding pointer | Relocation transparency | Shenandoah [7] |
| Colored pointer | Load of reference | Test color bits, self-heal | Relocation transparency | ZGC [8] |
| LVB | Load of reference | Trap if not marked through | Pauseless marking | Azul C4 |

A Yuasa barrier in Rust-flavored pseudocode shows the fast-path discipline:

```rust
/// Yuasa (snapshot-at-the-beginning) write barrier on `p.slot := new_val`.
#[inline(always)]
fn yuasa_write_barrier(p: &mut ObjectRef, new_val: ObjectRef) {
    let old = p.slot; // the overwritten reference
    if gc_marking_active() {
        satb_queue.push(old); // unconditional log: no color test
    }
    p.slot = new_val;
}
```

The tri-color step in Haskell shows the wavefront as a pure transition:

```haskell
-- One step of the tri-color marking wavefront.
step :: Heap -> Heap
step h = case popGrey h of
  Nothing      -> h                       -- wavefront empty: done
  Just (g, h') -> blacken g (greyChildren g h')
  where
    greyChildren g = foldr shadeIfWhite `flip` children g
    shadeIfWhite c acc = if isWhite c acc then setColor Grey c acc
                         else acc
```

### 4.3 Concurrent Mark-Sweep and Mostly-Concurrent Collection

Printezis and Detlefs' *mostly-concurrent* generational collector made the old generation concurrent while keeping nursery pauses short [6]: (1) **initial mark** (STW): grey roots; (2) **concurrent mark**: mutator runs, Yuasa barrier logs overwritten references, marker threads drain the grey set; (3) **final remark** (STW): drain the SATB queue and re-scan roots — processing only the *delta*, so pauses track mutation rate, not heap size; (4) **concurrent sweep**: reclaim white objects during allocation. This is SATB's crucial scaling property: pause time follows the *rate of graph change*, not the *volume of live data* [6].

Mark-sweep's flaw is fragmentation: sweep returns cells to free lists, and large allocations can fail despite ample total free space (*promotion failure*), forcing full STW compaction. CMS added background compaction heuristics, but concurrency without relocation cannot defragment — the tension that drove region-based and relocating concurrent designs.

### 4.4 Region-Based and Colored-Pointer Designs: G1, Shenandoah, and ZGC

**G1** divides the heap into equal *regions* with fluid roles (eden, survivor, old) and per-region remembered sets [5]. Collection selects a *collection set* of the most garbage-rich regions — "garbage-first" — with a pause-prediction model sizing it to a soft pause goal. SATB concurrent marking establishes global liveness; evacuation remains STW but is bounded by the collection set. G1 became HotSpot's default in JDK 9.

**Shenandoah** adds *concurrent evacuation*: every object carries a Brooks forwarding pointer, and a read barrier on every load keeps the mutator observing the to-space copy [7]. Mark, evacuate, and update-references all run concurrently, so pauses reduce to root scanning — independent of heap size — at the cost of a per-load read barrier and one forwarding word per object.

**ZGC** avoids per-object overhead with *colored pointers*: metadata bits in the pointer record mark/relocation state, and a *load barrier* self-heals stale pointers on first use [8]. Multi-mapped memory maps the same pages at several virtual addresses (one per color), reducing color checks to range tests. ZGC targets sub-millisecond pauses on heaps up to 16 TB, went production-ready in JDK 15, and gained a generational mode with store barriers in JDK 21.

| Collector | Young pause | Old/mixed pause | Scales with | Relocating? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Serial/Parallel | Live-set proportional | Live-set proportional | Heap size | Yes (STW) |
| CMS [6] | Short nursery | Remark tracks mutation | Mutation rate | No |
| G1 [5] | Bounded by eden | Bounded by collection set | Pause goal | Yes (STW evac) |
| Shenandoah [7] | Few ms | Root-scan bounded | Root set | Yes (concurrent) |
| ZGC [8] | Sub-ms | Sub-ms | Root set | Yes (concurrent) |

### 4.5 Machine-Checked Correctness

Dijkstra's paper already contained a careful hand proof [2]; Ben-Ari gave a cleaner assertional one. But bugs live in the gap between model and implementation — barrier fast paths, root scanning, header layout. McCreight, Shao, Lin, and Hu built a refinement framework verifying collectors against abstract specifications. The landmark practical result is Hawblitzel and Petrank's verified collectors: mark-sweep and copying collectors with allocators, written in x86 assembly with pre/postconditions and invariants, mechanically checked by Boogie and Z3 — competitive with the Bartok collectors on C# benchmarks [9]. Their key move was stating reachability invariants so that *automated* proof (Z3 quantifier instantiation over region-based heap invariants) succeeds. Concurrent mark-sweep has since been verified against detailed interleaving models (Gammie, Hosking, Hurd; Zakowski et al.), and a verified mark-sweep collector integrated with the OCaml runtime gives end-to-end, portable-C verification specified in terms of abstract reachability rather than algorithm internals [10].

The tri-color discipline itself can be model-checked in TLA+:

```tla+
---- MODULE Tricolor ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Objects
VARIABLES color  \* [Objects -> {"W", "G", "B"}]
VARIABLES edges  \* [Objects -> SUBSET Objects]

TricolorInvariant ==
  \* No black object references a white object.
  \A o \in Objects : color[o] = "B" =>
     \A c \in edges[o] : color[c] # "W"

GreyStep(o) ==
  /\ color[o] = "G"
  /\ color' = [x \in Objects |->
       IF x = o THEN "B"
       ELSE IF x \in edges[o] /\ color[x] = "W" THEN "G"
       ELSE color[x]]
  /\ UNCHANGED edges
====
```

Checking this against random mutator steps guarded by a Yuasa barrier confirms SATB safety on small heaps — a useful prelude to full mechanized proof.

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Proof Sketches

### 5.1 What collection costs

Hertz and Berger's study remains the clearest statement of tracing collection's throughput cost versus explicit management [11]:

1. With **5x** the minimum memory, tracing collectors match or slightly beat `malloc`/`free` — bump allocation plus compaction locality compensates.
2. With **3x** memory, collectors run about **17 percent slower** on average.
3. With **2x** memory, they run about **70 percent slower**, and under pressure all collectors suffer order-of-magnitude paging penalties.

Collection performance is *space-elastic*: it trades memory for speed, and generational and concurrent designs exist to flatten this curve at smaller heap multiples. Ungar's complementary datum — generation scavenging at 2.5 percent of CPU time — shows how cheap the generational optimization is when the hypothesis holds [3].

### 5.2 Proof sketch: Yuasa barrier safety

> **Theorem (SATB safety):** *Let M be the objects marked by a concurrent marker using a Yuasa barrier on every store, and R0 the objects reachable at marking start. Then R0 is a subset of M.*
> *Proof.* Suppose *o* in R0 is never marked, and fix a start-time root path *v0..vk = o*. If no store touches the path, the marker's traversal reaches *o*. Otherwise let *(vi, vi+1)* be the first overwritten edge; the Yuasa barrier logs the displaced reference — a node on the original path — re-anchoring the suffix, while the untouched prefix is reached by the marker. Induction reaches *vk = o*. Contradiction. ∎

SATB's over-approximation is one-directional: mid-cycle deaths may be marked unnecessarily (floating garbage), but nothing live at start is ever lost.

A miniature generational simulation in Python makes remembered-set mechanics concrete:

```python
class GenerationalHeap:
    def __init__(self):
        self.young, self.old = set(), set()
        self.remembered = set()  # (container, slot): old -> young refs
        self.survivals = {}

    def write_barrier(self, container, slot, value):
        if container in self.old and value in self.young:
            self.remembered.add((container, slot))

    def minor_collect(self, roots):
        worklist = [r for r in roots if r in self.young]
        worklist += [slot for (_, slot) in self.remembered]
        live, seen = set(), set()
        while worklist:
            obj = worklist.pop()
            if obj in seen or obj not in self.young:
                continue
            seen.add(obj); live.add(obj)
            worklist.extend(obj.children)
        for obj in live:  # promote past tenuring threshold
            self.survivals[obj] = self.survivals.get(obj, 0) + 1
            if self.survivals[obj] >= 3:
                self.old.add(obj)
        self.young = live - self.old
        self.remembered = {(c, s) for (c, s) in self.remembered
                           if c in self.old}
```

## 6 Limitations

**Barrier overhead is real.** Yuasa barriers tax every store; ZGC load barriers tax every load. JITs elide provably redundant barriers, but the tax is paid even when no collection runs.

**Remembered sets cost memory and scan time.** Card tables are coarse — one stray pointer dirties a whole card — while G1's precise per-region sets can balloon for widely referenced regions.

**SATB floating garbage delays reclamation**, raising effective occupancy precisely when a concurrent collector is under pressure.

**Concurrency multiplies the state space.** Production concurrent collectors have shipped subtle bugs — lost SATB entries, evacuation/reference-update races, root-scanning omissions — surfacing only under rare interleavings. No production concurrent *compacting* collector has been mechanically verified end to end; that gap is the field's foremost open verification problem [10].

**The generational hypothesis can fail.** Cache-heavy or session-state workloads see little generational benefit while paying barrier and remembered-set costs; region-based adaptation (G1) helps but needs tuning.

**Hardware interaction** — barrier cache pollution, marker/mutator bandwidth contention, NUMA placement — resists analytic modeling and must be measured per workload [11].

## 7 Conclusion

Generational and concurrent collection answer one question: *how do we preserve the tri-color invariant while doing less work, less disruptively?* Generational collection confines frequent work to the nursery, paying with write barriers and remembered sets [3]. Concurrent collection lets the mutator run, interposing Dijkstra, Steele, or Yuasa barriers that keep the invariant intact across interleavings [2][4][6]. The modern synthesis — region heaps, SATB marking, Brooks pointers or colored pointers, concurrent evacuation — delivers sub-millisecond pauses on terabyte heaps [7][8], while mechanized verification is closing the gap between collectors we can argue correct and collectors we can *prove* correct [9][10].

Three directions stand out: *verified concurrent compaction*, extending proofs to evacuation and reference updating; *hardware-assisted barriers* via memory tagging; and *adaptive unification*, as generational ZGC shows the generational/concurrent divide dissolving into one region-based, barrier-driven architecture. The tri-color invariant will survive all of it — it has survived everything else for nearly fifty years.
