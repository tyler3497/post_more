# Retrieval-Augmented Generation Systems: Dense Passage Retrieval, Hybrid Sparse-Dense Ranking, Fusion-in-Decoder Synthesis, and RAGAS Evaluation

## Abstract

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) addresses a fundamental limitation of parametric language models — knowledge frozen at training time and the propensity to hallucinate — by coupling a non-parametric corpus index with a parametric sequence generator [1]. This thesis presents a unified treatment of the modern RAG pipeline: **dense passage retrieval (DPR)**, where dual-encoder bi-encoders trained with contrastive objectives over in-batch and BM25-mined hard negatives retrieve passages via maximum inner-product search over FAISS indexes [2]; **hybrid sparse–dense ranking**, fusing lexical BM25 signals with dense embeddings through reciprocal rank fusion and cross-encoder reranking; **Fusion-in-Decoder (FiD)** synthesis, concatenating independently encoded passages so the decoder cross-attends over the full retrieved set [7]; and **RAGAS**, an evaluation framework measuring faithfulness, answer relevancy, context precision, and context recall [3]. We formalize RAG-Sequence and RAG-Token marginalization over latent documents, derive the contrastive training objective, analyze indexing structures and chunking strategies, and trace the lineage from DrQA [5] and REALM [6] to the contemporary survey of the field [4], closing with a critical appraisal of retrieval collapse, context-window pressure, and stale indexes.

## 1. Introduction

Modern large language models store knowledge *parametrically*: facts are compressed into billions of weights during pretraining and recalled through next-token prediction. This design yields remarkable fluency but suffers from three structural defects. First, the **knowledge cutoff** — a model trained in month *t* knows nothing of month *t+1* without retraining. Second, **hallucination**: the decoder is a sampler that fabricates plausible-sounding falsehoods when parametric memory is insufficient, because the likelihood objective never penalizes confabulation given inadequate evidence. Third, **opaque provenance**: even correct answers carry no pointers to justifying sources, making verification impossible.

Retrieval-augmented generation, introduced by Lewis et al. [1], attacks all three defects by endowing the generator with *non-parametric memory*: a searchable index over an external corpus that can be queried, refreshed, and inspected independently of model weights. At inference, the system retrieves passages relevant to the query and conditions generation on them, marginalizing over the latent retrieved documents. Corpora update without retraining, and generated claims trace to retrieved evidence.

This thesis dissects the modern RAG stack. §2 traces the lineage from retrieve-and-read pipelines [5] and retrieval-augmented pretraining [6] through dense retrieval [2] to contemporary surveys [4]. §3 formalizes the RAG objective and the RAG-Sequence / RAG-Token distinction. §4 — the technical core — covers DPR training and FAISS indexing (§4.1), hybrid ranking and rerankers (§4.2), Fusion-in-Decoder synthesis [7] (§4.3), and RAGAS evaluation [3] (§4.4). §5 reports empirical results with theoretical analysis; §6 confronts limitations; §7 concludes.

## 2. Background and Related Work

The retrieve-and-read paradigm predates dense retrieval. **DrQA** [5] paired a TF-IDF bag-of-words retriever over Wikipedia with a recurrent Document Reader that predicted answer spans via bilinear question–passage attention. DrQA established the canonical *retriever–reader* decomposition and showed open-domain QA reduces to large-scale retrieval plus reading comprehension. Its limits were instructive: TF-IDF caps recall on paraphrased questions, and span extraction cannot synthesize answers across documents.

**REALM** [6] made retrieval a first-class citizen of *pretraining*. Each masked sentence triggers latent retrieval from Wikipedia via maximum inner-product search, and the model marginalizes over retrieved documents when predicting masked tokens. Because the index drifts as the encoder trains, REALM re-embeds and re-indexes the corpus asynchronously — an early answer to the stale-index problem (§6). *Salient-span masking* (masking entities and dates) forces reliance on retrieved evidence over parametric memory.

The dense-retrieval breakthrough came with **DPR** [2]: a simple dual-encoder trained contrastively substantially outperformed BM25 on open-domain retrieval, replacing lexical overlap with learned semantic similarity. **RAG** [1] closed the loop to generation — a BART-based generator conditions on retrieved passages and marginalizes over them, yielding fluent abstractive answers. **Fusion-in-Decoder** [7] offered a competing synthesis scaling to many passages. Finally, **RAGAS** [3] supplied the missing evaluation science, factorizing quality into retrieval-side and generation-side metrics, while Gao et al. [4] systematized the design space into naive, advanced, and modular RAG.

## 3. Methodology

### The probabilistic model

We consider a query $x$, a corpus $\mathcal{C} = \{d_1, \dots, d_M\}$ of passages (tens of millions of ~100-word Wikipedia chunks), and a target answer $y$. A RAG system comprises:

1. **Retriever** $p_\eta(z \mid x)$ — a distribution over documents $z$, realized as top-$k$ maximum inner-product search between query embedding $E_Q(x)$ and precomputed passage embeddings $E_P(d)$.
2. **Generator** $p_\theta(y \mid x, z)$ — a sequence-to-sequence model conditioning on the query plus retrieved passages.

The modeling crux is how latent documents combine. **RAG-Sequence** [1] treats the document as one latent variable for the entire output:

$$p(y \mid x) = \sum_{z \in \mathrm{top-}k(x)} p_\eta(z \mid x) \prod_{i=1}^{|y|} p_\theta(y_i \mid x, z, y_{<i})$$

**RAG-Token** marginalizes *per generated token*, letting different tokens draw on different documents:

$$p(y \mid x) = \prod_{i=1}^{|y|} \sum_{z \in \mathrm{top-}k(x)} p_\eta(z \mid x)\, p_\theta(y_i \mid x, z, y_{<i})$$

RAG-Sequence yields beam hypotheses each grounded in a single document; RAG-Token enables fine-grained synthesis at the risk of stitching claims from contradictory sources. The retriever distribution is the renormalized top-$k$ softmax:

$$p_\eta(z \mid x) = \frac{\exp(E_Q(x)^\top E_P(z))}{\sum_{z'} \exp(E_Q(x)^\top E_P(z'))}$$

> **Theorem (Latent-variable consistency):** If $p_\eta(\cdot \mid x)$ concentrates on a single document $z^*$, both variants reduce to ordinary conditional generation $p_\theta(y \mid x, z^*)$. If top-$k$ documents contradict one another, RAG-Sequence preserves *per-hypothesis coherence* while RAG-Token can emit *Frankenstein answers* mixing incompatible claims. Granularity of marginalization is thus a bias–variance tradeoff over evidence attribution.

A minimal RAG-Sequence decoder:

```python
def rag_sequence_decode(query, k=5, beams_per_doc=4):
    q = query_encoder.encode(query)
    scores, ids = faiss_index.search(q, k)      # MIPS -> p_eta(z|x)
    doc_probs = softmax(scores)
    hyps = []
    for z, pz in zip(ids, doc_probs):
        ctx = f"question: {query} context: {corpus[z]}"
        for b in generator.beam_search(ctx, beams_per_doc):
            hyps.append((b.text, pz * b.score))  # weight by p(z|x)
    return marginalize_by_string(hyps)           # sum probs of identical strings
```

### Index construction and chunking

The corpus sets the retrieval ceiling: generators cannot use evidence retrievers cannot surface. Standard practice [2] splits Wikipedia into disjoint 100-word passages (~21M units), but chunking is a tunable design variable:

- **Fixed-size windows** with sliding overlap (e.g., 512 tokens, 50-token stride) — simple, but severs semantic units mid-sentence.
- **Semantic chunking** — splits on discourse boundaries or embedding-space topic shifts; coherent but variable-sized.
- **Proposition-level chunking** — atomic factual statements; maximal precision for factoid QA, fragmented narrative context.
- **Hierarchical chunking** — multi-granularity indexes with coarse-to-fine retrieval; trades index size for recall.

DPR [2] uses *separate* question and passage encoders; passage vectors are embedded once and frozen into the FAISS index, while only $E_Q$ runs at query time.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Dense Passage Retrieval: Dual-Encoder Contrastive Learning, Hard Negatives, and FAISS Indexing

DPR [2] replaces lexical matching with learned dense similarity. Two BERT-based encoders — $E_Q$ for questions, $E_P$ for passages — map text into a shared $d$-dimensional space ($d = 768$), with relevance scored by inner product $\mathrm{sim}(q,p) = E_Q(q)^\top E_P(p)$. Training uses a contrastive, InfoNCE-style objective: for each question $q_i$ with positive passage $p_i^+$ and negative pool $\mathcal{N}_i$,

$$\mathcal{L} = -\log \frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(q_i, p_i^+))}{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(q_i, p_i^+)) + \sum_{p^- \in \mathcal{N}_i} \exp(\mathrm{sim}(q_i, p^-))}$$

The composition of $\mathcal{N}_i$ is decisive. DPR combines three negative sources: (i) *random* passages, (ii) *BM25 hard negatives* — top-ranked BM25 passages lacking the answer, forcing the model to surpass lexical matching, and (iii) *in-batch negatives* — every other passage in the mini-batch serves as a negative for every question, yielding $B-1$ negatives per example nearly for free and scaling effective negative count with batch size ($B = 128$ in DPR). Ablations in [2] identify BM25 hard negatives as the largest single contributor to retrieval gains.

At inference, all $M$ passage embeddings are precomputed and indexed with **FAISS**: *IVF-PQ* (inverted file with product quantization) for compressed sub-linear search, or *HNSW* graphs for high-recall, low-latency serving. The index *is* the system's non-parametric memory: updating knowledge means re-embedding changed passages, never retraining weights.

### 4.2 Hybrid Sparse–Dense Retrieval and Cross-Encoder Reranking

Lexical and dense retrieval fail in complementary ways: BM25 excels at rare entities, exact phrases, and out-of-vocabulary terms but misses paraphrase; DPR captures semantics but can overlook precise lexical constraints. **Hybrid retrieval** runs both and fuses rankings. The two dominant fusion schemes are:

1. **Score-level fusion**: $s(d) = \alpha\,\tilde{s}_{\mathrm{BM25}}(d) + (1-\alpha)\,\tilde{s}_{\mathrm{DPR}}(d)$ over normalized scores with tuned $\alpha$ — simple and effective, but sensitive to score calibration.
2. **Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)**: $\mathrm{RRF}(d) = \sum_{r \in \{\mathrm{BM25}, \mathrm{DPR}\}} \frac{1}{\kappa + \mathrm{rank}_r(d)}$ with $\kappa = 60$ conventionally. RRF needs no score normalization, is robust to heterogeneous scorers, and hybrids consistently beat either retriever alone.

A second-stage **reranker** then refines the top-$k$ candidates (typically 100–1000). **Cross-encoders** concatenate query and passage into a single Transformer input and score the `[CLS]` representation — full cross-attention between query and document tokens yields substantially higher precision than bi-encoder dot products, at the cost of one forward pass *per candidate*, which is why reranking is a second stage rather than the retriever. Practical deployments distill cross-encoder scores into the bi-encoder to recover most of the gain at retrieval-time cost, or adopt **late-interaction** models such as ColBERT — precomputed token-level embeddings scored by MaxSim — as a middle ground between efficiency and expressivity.

### 4.3 Fusion-in-Decoder: Joint Attentional Synthesis over Concatenated Passages

**Fusion-in-Decoder** [7] rethinks how retrieved evidence enters the generator. Instead of marginalizing over documents as in RAG [1], FiD encodes each of the top-$k$ passages *independently* — the encoder processes $[x; z_j]$ for each $j$ — then *concatenates* all encoder outputs into one long sequence that the decoder cross-attends over jointly:

$$H = [\,\mathrm{Enc}(x, z_1);\, \mathrm{Enc}(x, z_2);\, \dots;\, \mathrm{Enc}(x, z_k)\,], \qquad y \sim \mathrm{Dec}(y_{<i} \mid H)$$

Because encoding is per-passage, encoder cost grows linearly in $k$ with no quadratic blowup; the decoder's cross-attention fuses evidence *across* all passages, enabling genuine multi-hop synthesis — the model can combine a birth date from passage 3 with a film title from passage 17. Izacard and Grave [7] scale to $k = 100$ passages and report 51.4 exact match on Natural Questions and 67.6 on TriviaQA, surpassing RAG on the same backbone family. The tradeoff: FiD conditions on the *entire* retrieved set, so one adversarial passage can pollute cross-attention (RAG's per-document marginalization is more robust), and training requires hard, diverse passages lest the decoder learn to ignore context. Decoder-only variants — concatenating retrieved documents directly into the prompt ("in-context RAG") — inherit FiD's fusion behavior but pay full self-attention cost over the stuffed context and suffer *lost-in-the-middle* degradation as contexts grow.

### 4.4 Evaluation with RAGAS: Faithfulness, Answer Relevancy, Context Precision, and Context Recall

Evaluating RAG with single-number QA accuracy conflates two failure modes — *retrieval failure* (evidence absent) and *generation failure* (evidence present but misused). **RAGAS** [3] disentangles them with four LLM-judged metrics:

- **Faithfulness**: the answer is decomposed into atomic claims; each claim is verified against the retrieved context via natural-language inference. Faithfulness = supported claims / total claims. It measures hallucination *relative to the context*, not absolute truth.
- **Answer relevancy**: synthetic questions are reverse-engineered from the answer and their embeddings compared (cosine similarity) to the original question embedding. High similarity means the answer addresses what was asked.
- **Context precision@k**: an LLM judge labels each retrieved chunk relevant or not given the ground-truth answer; precision measures ranking quality, penalizing retrieved-but-useless chunks.
- **Context recall**: the ground-truth answer is decomposed into claims; recall is the fraction attributable to retrieved context — the complement of retrieval failure.

Together they *localize* defects: low context recall implicates the retriever or chunking; low faithfulness with high recall implicates the generator. RAGAS [3] validates these metrics against human judgments, though LLM-as-judge introduces biases — verbosity preference, self-enhancement — that practitioners must calibrate. Complementary benchmarks such as KILT and targeted human evaluation remain the gold standard for high-stakes deployment.

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Analysis

### Reported results

On Natural Questions test (exact match), the literature traces a clean progression [1][2][6][7]:

| System | Retriever | Synthesis | NQ test EM |
|---|---|---|---|
| REALM [6] | Learned dense (joint pretraining) | Span extraction | 40.4 |
| DPR + BERT reader [2] | DPR bi-encoder | Extractive reader | 41.5 |
| RAG-Token [1] | DPR (fine-tuned) | Token-level marginalization | 44.1 |
| RAG-Sequence [1] | DPR (fine-tuned) | Document-level marginalization | 44.5 |
| FiD-large [7] | DPR | Fusion-in-decoder | **51.4** |

DPR itself reaches 78.4 top-20 and 85.4 top-100 retrieval accuracy on NQ [2], confirming that retrieval quality *bounds* — but does not determine — end-to-end QA. FiD's leap to 51.4 EM (67.6 on TriviaQA) comes from conditioning jointly on up to 100 passages rather than marginalizing over 5–10 [7].

### Theoretical perspective

Training RAG end-to-end maximizes $\log p(y \mid x)$ with the retriever inside the marginalization — a latent-variable objective optimized through the top-$k$ approximation [1][6]. Its evidence lower bound decomposes into generation and retrieval terms, which explains the *retriever starvation* pathology: if the generator can answer from parametric memory alone, gradients to the retriever vanish and retrieval quality collapses — the joint-training analogue of posterior collapse in VAEs.

> **Theorem (Additive error decomposition):** Let $E$ denote retriever failure — no retrieved document entails the reference answer — with $P(E) \le \epsilon$, and let the generator be $\delta$-faithful, i.e. $P(\mathrm{hallucination} \mid \neg E) \le \delta$. Then end-to-end hallucination probability satisfies $P(H) \le \epsilon + \delta$. *Corollary:* system reliability factorizes into retrieval recall and conditional faithfulness — precisely the factorization RAGAS [3] operationalizes as *context recall* and *faithfulness*. Improving either term in isolation leaves the other as the binding constraint.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

- **Retrieval collapse.** Under weak supervision the query encoder can collapse toward near-constant embeddings; hard negatives (§4.1) are the standard defense. A subtler collapse afflicts joint training: the generator learns to ignore retrieved context, starving the retriever of gradient — mitigated by freezing the retriever, salient-span masking [6], or auxiliary retrieval losses.
- **Context-window pressure.** More passages raise recall but degrade the generator's ability to *use* them: decoder-only "stuffed-context" RAG exhibits *lost-in-the-middle* degradation of mid-context evidence. FiD's cross-attention fusion [7] is more robust yet still pays linear decoder cost in $k$.
- **Stale indexes.** Corpora drift; re-embedding tens of millions of passages is expensive, so production indexes lag the world. REALM's asynchronous re-indexing [6] remains the template, but versioning, incremental updates, and temporal calibration of retrieved evidence are unsolved at scale.
- **Knowledge conflicts.** When retrieved passages contradict parametric priors, models often defer to their weights — the opposite of the intended behavior. Conflict-aware training and explicit source-attribution objectives are active research areas.
- **Cost and latency.** Cross-encoder reranking costs a forward pass per candidate; a raw 21M × 768 index occupies tens of gigabytes (PQ compression trades recall for ~30× shrinkage).

## 7. Conclusion

Retrieval-augmented generation reframes language modeling as *open-book* reasoning: parametric fluency grounded in non-parametric evidence. This thesis has traced the stack from DPR's contrastive dual-encoders and FAISS indexes [2], through hybrid sparse–dense fusion and cross-encoder reranking, to FiD's attentional synthesis over a hundred passages [7] and RAGAS's disentangled evaluation [3] — unified by the latent-variable marginalization at the heart of RAG [1], with roots in DrQA [5] and REALM [6] and a maturing systematization in recent surveys [4].

The frontier is clear: *agentic* RAG interleaves retrieval with reasoning; *differentiable search indexes* collapse retrieval into the parameters; and calibrated abstention — knowing when evidence is insufficient — remains the missing virtue. The additive error decomposition of §5 sets the agenda: drive $\epsilon$ down with better retrieval, $\delta$ down with faithful generation, and measure both, separately, always.
