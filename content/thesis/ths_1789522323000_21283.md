# Replica Symmetry Breaking and the Parisi Formula for Mean-Field Spin Glasses: the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick Model

## Abstract

The Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model—Ising spins with infinite-range Gaussian random couplings—is the mean-field laboratory of spin-glass physics, exhibiting a low-temperature phase with infinitely many pure states organized ultrametrically. This thesis develops the replica method, the failure of the replica-symmetric ansatz (negative entropy, Almeida–Thouless instability), and Parisi's replica-symmetry-breaking scheme: an order parameter that is a function encoding the distribution of overlaps between pure states. We present the Parisi formula for the free energy, its rigorous proof by Guerra's interpolation and Talagrand's theorem, the ultrametricity theorem of Panchenko, and the physical picture of aging and rejuvenation. Applications to combinatorial optimization, neural networks, and high-dimensional inference are surveyed. Limitations include the restriction to mean-field geometries and the open problem of finite-dimensional spin glasses.

## 1 Introduction

Certain alloys—gold with a few percent iron, copper with manganese—display a magnetic phase unlike any other: below a freezing temperature $T_f$, the susceptibility shows a sharp cusp, yet no periodic magnetic order appears. The spins freeze into a disordered configuration, each pointing in a fixed but apparently random direction, with an astronomical number of nearly degenerate ground states. This is the **spin glass**, and its theoretical understanding required one of the most audacious ideas in statistical physics: **replica symmetry breaking** [1][2].

The Sherrington–Kirkpatrick (SK) model captures the essence: $N$ Ising spins $\sigma_i = \pm 1$ with Hamiltonian

$$
H_N(\sigma) = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{1 \le i < j \le N} J_{ij} \sigma_i \sigma_j - h \sum_i \sigma_i,
$$

where the couplings $J_{ij}$ are i.i.d. standard Gaussians, quenched (frozen) disorder. The $1/\sqrt{N}$ scaling keeps the energy extensive. The quantity of interest is the **quenched free energy**

$$
F_N = -\frac{1}{\beta N} \, \mathbb{E} \log Z_N, \qquad Z_N = \sum_{\sigma} e^{-\beta H_N(\sigma)},
$$

where $\mathbb{E}$ averages over the disorder. Computing $\mathbb{E} \log Z$—the average of a logarithm—is the central difficulty; the replica method attacks it via the identity $\mathbb{E} \log Z = \lim_{n \to 0} (\mathbb{E} Z^n - 1)/n$ [1].

> Theorem: (Parisi formula, proved by Talagrand) The thermodynamic limit of the SK free energy is given by a variational problem over nondecreasing functions $x: (0,1) \to (0,1)$: $\lim_{N\to\infty} F_N = \inf_x \mathcal{P}(x)$, where $\mathcal{P}$ is the Parisi functional [2][3].

This thesis proceeds as follows: §2 introduces the replica method and its symmetric solution; §3 explains its failure and Parisi's ansatz; §4 dives into ultrametricity, rigorous proofs, and dynamics; §5 presents applications; §6 discusses limitations.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The replica trick

For integer $n$, $\mathbb{E} Z_N^n$ is the partition function of $n$ **replicas** $\sigma^1, \dots, \sigma^n$ coupled through the disorder average. Averaging over $J_{ij}$ yields an effective replica Hamiltonian depending only on the **overlap matrix**

$$
Q_{ab} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sigma_i^a \sigma_i^b, \qquad a, b = 1, \dots, n,
$$

measuring similarity between replicas $a$ and $b$. By Laplace's method, $\mathbb{E} Z_N^n \approx \exp(-N \min_Q S[Q])$ for an explicit action $S[Q]$. The replica-symmetric (RS) ansatz sets $Q_{ab} = q$ for $a \ne b$ (all replicas equivalent) and $Q_{aa} = 1$, reducing the problem to a single order parameter $q$—the Edwards–Anderson parameter—satisfying the self-consistency equation

$$
q = \mathbb{E}_z \tanh^2\!\left(\beta(\sqrt{q}\, z + h)\right), \qquad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) \quad [1].
$$

At high temperature the only solution is $q = 0$ (paramagnet); below $T_c = 1$ (at $h = 0$) a nonzero solution appears. So far, so good.

### 2.2 Failure of replica symmetry

The RS solution is unphysical at low temperature. Its zero-temperature entropy is $S_{RS}(T=0) = -1/(2\pi) \approx -0.159$—**negative**, impossible for Ising spins. Moreover, de Almeida and Thouless showed the RS saddle point is unstable below the **AT line** $T_{AT}(h)$ in the $(T, h)$ plane: fluctuations breaking replica symmetry (the "replicon" mode) have negative mass squared [4]. The instability signals that the true low-temperature phase breaks the permutation symmetry among replicas.

The physical meaning: the Gibbs measure fragments into many **pure states** $\alpha$, each with weight $w_\alpha$, and two replicas drawn independently may land in different pure states. The overlap $q_{\alpha\gamma} = N^{-1}\sum_i \langle \sigma_i \rangle_\alpha \langle \sigma_i \rangle_\gamma$ between states becomes a *random variable* whose distribution

$$
P(q) = \sum_{\alpha,\gamma} w_\alpha w_\gamma \, \delta(q - q_{\alpha\gamma})
$$

is the fundamental order parameter—no longer a number but a probability distribution [2].

| Regime | Order parameter | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| $T > T_c$ | $q = 0$ | Paramagnetic, RS stable |
| $T_{AT} < T < T_c$ | $q > 0$ | RS stable in field |
| $T < T_{AT}(h)$ | $P(q)$ nontrivial | RSB, many pure states |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Parisi's ansatz

Parisi proposed that the overlap matrix $Q_{ab}$ has a hierarchical block structure: divide $n$ replicas into groups, groups into supergroups, and so on for $k$ levels; $Q_{ab}$ depends only on the coarsest level at which $a$ and $b$ separate. In the limit $n \to 0$—where the combinatorics inverts, groups becoming *larger* than their parents—the discrete hierarchy becomes a continuous nondecreasing function

$$
x: (0,1) \to (0,1), \qquad x(q) = \int_0^q P(q')\, dq',
$$

the cumulative distribution of overlaps. The Parisi functional $\mathcal{P}(x)$ is built from the solution $f(q, y)$ of the **Parisi PDE**

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial q} + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} + \frac{1}{2} x(q) \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)^2 = 0,
$$

with final condition $f(1, y) = \log 2\cosh(\beta y)$, integrated against a Gaussian weight [2]. The free energy is $\inf_x \mathcal{P}(x)$—an infinite-dimensional variational problem whose minimizer $x^*$ encodes $P(q)$.

```python
# Schematic: Parisi PDE solver (backward in q)
import numpy as np
def parisi_functional(x_of_q, beta, nq=2000, ny=400):
    qs = np.linspace(0, 1, nq)
    ys = np.linspace(-8, 8, ny)
    f = np.log(2*np.cosh(beta*ys))          # final condition at q=1
    for q in reversed(qs[:-1]):
        dq = qs[1]-qs[0]
        # explicit step of: f_q + f_yy/2 + x(q)*(f_y)^2/2 = 0
        fy = np.gradient(f, ys); fyy = np.gradient(fy, ys)
        f = f - dq*(0.5*fyy + 0.5*x_of_q(q)*fy**2)
    return f  # integrate against Gaussian weight for P(x)
```

### 3.2 Guerra's interpolation and Talagrand's proof

Making the replica method rigorous took 25 years. Guerra introduced an **interpolation** between the SK Hamiltonian and a decoupled system of independent spins in random fields governed by the Parisi measure; differentiating in the interpolation parameter and using Gaussian integration by parts yields

$$
F_N \le \mathcal{P}(x) \quad \text{for every admissible } x,
$$

i.e., the Parisi functional is always an *upper bound* [5]. The reverse inequality—the hard direction—was proved by Talagrand (2006), using a refined interpolation with an additional perturbation that forces the Ghirlanda–Guerra identities, which in turn imply ultrametricity and identify the limit with $\inf_x \mathcal{P}(x)$ [3]. The Fields-Medal-adjacent achievement closed the central conjecture of spin-glass theory.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Ultrametricity of pure states

The most striking prediction of Parisi's solution: pure states are organized **ultrametrically**. Define the distance $d(\alpha, \gamma) = 1 - q_{\alpha\gamma}$ between pure states. Then with probability one, any three states satisfy

$$
d(\alpha, \gamma) \le \max\{d(\alpha, \beta),\, d(\beta, \gamma)\},
$$

the strong triangle inequality: every triangle is isosceles with the two equal sides longest. States form a hierarchical tree—valleys within valleys within valleys—exactly the structure encoded by $x(q)$ [6].

Panchenko proved ultrametricity rigorously from the Ghirlanda–Guerra identities, which state that the overlap array satisfies a remarkable self-consistency: conditionally on the overlaps among $n$ replicas, the overlap of replica $1$ with a new replica $n+1$ is either an independent copy from $P$ or equals one of the existing overlaps, with explicit probabilities [7]. These identities—consequences of a vanishing variance of the free energy under small random perturbations (stochastic stability)—force the hierarchical structure. It is a rare case where a physicist's boldest conjecture was proved in full.

### 4.2 Dynamics: aging and rejuvenation

Out of equilibrium, spin glasses **age**: the response to a perturbation depends on the waiting time $t_w$ since the quench, not just the measurement time. The system explores the ultrametric tree ever more slowly, getting trapped in deeper valleys. Remarkably, a small temperature drop causes **rejuvenation**—the system appears young again (it must re-equilibrate within sub-valleys)—while reheating restores the memory of the previous aging (**memory effect**) [8].

These phenomena are captured by the Cugliandolo–Kurchan equations for the out-of-equilibrium dynamics of mean-field models, where the fluctuation–dissipation theorem is violated in a structured way: an effective temperature $T_{\mathrm{eff}} > T$ governs the slow modes, directly related to $x(q)$ via $T_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1} \propto x(q)$. Statics and dynamics speak the same language.

### 4.3 The Almeida–Thouless line and chaos

In a magnetic field, the RSB transition occurs along the AT line $T_{AT}(h)$. A deep question—**temperature chaos**—asks whether the pure-state organization reshuffles completely under an infinitesimal temperature change. For the SK model, chaos in temperature was proved: overlaps between states at different temperatures vanish, meaning the energy landscape's valley structure is exquisitely temperature-sensitive [9]. Disorder chaos (sensitivity to the couplings $J_{ij}$) is equally strong. These results delimit what algorithms can hope to achieve: no stable "ground-state valley" persists across parameters.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Result 1 (Parisi formula).** $\lim_{N\to\infty} F_N = \inf_x \mathcal{P}(x)$ (Talagrand 2006), with Guerra's upper bound (2003) as the companion [3][5]. Numerical solution of the Parisi PDE gives ground-state energy density $e_0 \approx -0.76321$, matching Monte Carlo simulations to five digits.

**Result 2 (Ultrametricity).** The overlap array of the SK model is ultrametric in the limit (Panchenko 2013), derived from the Ghirlanda–Guerra identities [7]. This confirms the hierarchical pure-state picture.

**Result 3 (AT line).** The de Almeida–Thouless stability analysis [4] locates the RSB transition; its predictions for the susceptibility cusp and the field dependence match experiments on AuFe and CuMn spin glasses quantitatively.

**Result 4 (Algorithmic hardness).** Approximate message passing (AMP) and related algorithms achieve the information-theoretic threshold for inference problems (e.g., community detection, compressed sensing) exactly when the underlying model is replica-symmetric; RSB phases coincide with **statistical-to-computational gaps**—problems solvable in principle but not efficiently. The Parisi functional thus predicts computational phase transitions [10].

**Applications.** Beyond physics, RSB mathematics now underpins: (i) the theory of random combinatorial optimization (random $k$-SAT, graph coloring thresholds via the cavity method); (ii) the statistical physics of learning (Gardner's capacity calculation for the perceptron, and modern analyses of deep-learning loss landscapes); (iii) high-dimensional Bayesian inference, where AMP algorithms derived from the cavity method are Bayes-optimal in RS phases [10].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Mean-field only.** The SK model has infinite-range interactions; real spin glasses live in three dimensions, where the droplet theory (Fisher–Huse) competes with RSB, and no consensus exists. Proving RSB—or its absence—in finite dimensions is the great open problem.
2. **The $n \to 0$ limit is unrigorous.** The replica method remains a heuristic; only its *predictions* (Parisi formula, ultrametricity) have been proved, by methods that bypass replicas entirely.
3. **Dynamics far from settled.** While statics is rigorous, a complete rigorous theory of aging and the Cugliandolo–Kurchan regime exists only for spherical models, not the SK model itself.
4. **Quantum extensions.** The quantum SK model (transverse field) exhibits a quantum phase transition whose RSB structure is only partially understood; the interplay of quantum fluctuations with ultrametricity is active research.
5. **Algorithmic gaps unexplained.** RSB predicts *where* efficient algorithms fail but not *why* fundamentally—whether the gap reflects a deep complexity barrier or our algorithmic poverty remains open.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Parisi's replica-symmetry-breaking solution is a landmark where physical intuition outran mathematics by decades—and was ultimately vindicated in full. The ideas it introduced—the overlap distribution as order parameter, ultrametric pure states, the Parisi PDE, Guerra's interpolation—have escaped physics to become core tools of probability theory, theoretical computer science, and high-dimensional statistics. The SK model, once a theorist's toy, is now the canonical example of a complex landscape: rugged, hierarchical, and computationally treacherous.

The frontier is clear: carry the RSB picture from mean field to finite dimensions, from statics to dynamics, and from physics to a principled theory of computational hardness. Whatever the outcome, the lesson endures—disorder, treated honestly, generates structures of astonishing mathematical richness.
