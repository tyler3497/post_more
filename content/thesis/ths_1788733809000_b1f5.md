# Exactly-Once Stream Processing in Apache Flink: Distributed Checkpointing, Barrier Alignment, Event-Time Watermarks, and Transactional Sinks

## Abstract

End-to-end exactly-once processing is the strongest correctness guarantee a streaming system can offer, and among the hardest to deliver at scale. This thesis gives a rigorous account of how Apache Flink attains it through three cooperating mechanisms: *Asynchronous Barrier Snapshotting* (ABS), a lightweight distributed-snapshot protocol derived from Chandy–Lamport that captures consistent operator state without pausing the pipeline; a watermark and event-time model that gives deterministic meaning to time over out-of-order, partitioned streams; and a two-phase-commit sink contract that extends exactly-once from internal state to external side effects. We formalize barrier alignment and characterize when it is required, analyze how unaligned checkpoints trade in-flight buffer persistence for bounded checkpoint latency under backpressure, and show how checkpoint barriers double as the prepare phase of a distributed transaction. Cut-consistency and end-to-end atomicity theorems are stated with proof sketches, the empirical cost profile is surveyed, and the boundary conditions — cyclic topologies, backpressure, watermark idleness, sink idempotence — beyond which the guarantee fails are delineated.

## 1 Introduction

A streaming application is a continuous computation over an unbounded dataset, and at warehouse scale failures are routine rather than exceptional. The central question of fault-tolerant stream processing is therefore what the system's observable behavior will be *after* state is lost and recovered. The industry has converged on three candidate answers: *at-most-once* (records may be dropped), *at-least-once* (records may be duplicated), and *exactly-once* (every record's effect is applied precisely one time) [2].

Exactly-once is deceptively difficult. It is not enough to avoid double-processing inside the engine: the engine must also avoid emitting the same externally visible effect twice. A billing pipeline that charges a credit card as a *sink* operation cannot be repaired by replaying its input stream; the money is already gone. Any credible treatment of exactly-once must therefore span the full pipeline — durable replayable sources, consistent snapshots of operator state, deterministic event-time semantics, and transactional or idempotent sinks [4].

Apache Flink is the canonical system that delivers this combination in a single engine. Its design rests on the observation, due to Carbone, Fóra, Ewen, Haridi, and Tzoumas, that the classic Chandy–Lamport distributed snapshot can be adapted to dataflow engines in a way that is both *asynchronous* — it never pauses the computation — and *lightweight* — on acyclic topologies it persists operator state only, with no record of in-flight messages [1]. From this foundation Flink builds barrier alignment for exactly-once checkpointing, an event-time and watermark model for reasoning about time, and a two-phase-commit sink protocol that turns checkpoint completion into transaction commitment. This thesis examines each mechanism, proves the properties they jointly guarantee, measures their cost, and maps their limits.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Delivery semantics

Delivery semantics describe the observable effect of a record on system state, including state held outside the engine. The three levels form a strict hierarchy:

| Guarantee | Duplicates possible | Losses possible | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| At-most-once | No | Yes | Cheapest; no replay, no dedup |
| At-least-once | Yes | No | Replay from durable log; no coordination |
| Exactly-once | No | No | Snapshots + alignment + transactional sinks |

At-least-once is the natural outcome of replay-based recovery: after a failure, the sources re-emit everything after the last committed offset, and any record processed between the snapshot and the failure is processed twice. Exactly-once eliminates these duplicates by ensuring that the state snapshot and the replay position describe the *same* logical cut through the computation, and that side effects are committed only for cuts that complete [2][3].

### 2.2 Distributed snapshots and the Chandy–Lamport construction

A distributed snapshot is a consistent global state assembled from asynchronous local states. Chandy and Lamport showed that consistency — no recorded receive without its matching send — is achievable with marker messages traveling the same FIFO channels as data. Flink's barriers are exactly such markers. The decisive refinement in ABS is that, for *acyclic* dataflows, the snapshot need not capture channel state at all: because the barrier separates pre-snapshot from post-snapshot records on every edge, the operator states alone form a consistent cut [1]. Cyclic dataflows are the exception: on back-edges, ABS logs in-flight records, exactly as the theory demands.

### 2.3 Internal versus external state

Flink distinguishes *internal state* — everything held in its state backends, such as windowed aggregates and keyed values — from *external state* held in systems like Kafka, databases, or files [4]. Internal state is trivially covered by snapshots: the backend persists it, and recovery restores it. External state is the hard case, because the engine cannot roll back a Kafka topic or an HTTP call. This is the gap that transactional sinks close.

### 2.4 Event time, processing time, and watermarks

Stream records carry two notions of time. *Processing time* is the wall clock of the machine that observes the record; *event time* is the timestamp of the event's occurrence in the world. Processing time is cheap but nondeterministic under replay — the same recovery can produce different results. Event time is deterministic but incomplete: the engine can never be certain that all events up to time *t* have arrived. *Watermarks* resolve this tension. A watermark *W(t)* is a control record declaring that event time has reached *t* — that no further records with timestamp *t′ ≤ t* should be expected on that stream [7]. Watermarks flow inline with data, advance each operator's event-time clock, and trigger window evaluation deterministically, so that replay reproduces identical results [7][8].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis proceeds by *mechanism decomposition*: we isolate each of the three pillars — snapshotting, time, and sinks — give a precise operational account of each, and then compose them to obtain the end-to-end guarantee. Our reference model is a directed acyclic graph of operators connected by FIFO streams, executed by parallel sub-tasks, with sources backed by a replayable durable log (e.g., Kafka partitions with offsets) and sinks backed by a transactional store [2][4].

We treat correctness in two stages. First, we prove that ABS yields a consistent cut and that rollback-plus-replay restores a state reachable in some failure-free execution — the *exactly-once state* property. Second, we prove that the two-phase-commit sink protocol makes external effects atomic with respect to checkpoint completion — the *end-to-end* property. Costs are analyzed along three axes emphasized by the literature and the Flink documentation: steady-state overhead of checkpointing, checkpoint *duration* (especially under backpressure), and recovery time [1][5]. Where the theory admits a tunable trade-off — aligned versus unaligned checkpoints, watermark delay versus lateness — we characterize the Pareto frontier rather than prescribing a point on it.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Asynchronous Barrier Snapshotting and barrier alignment

Checkpointing begins at the coordinator, which injects a *checkpoint barrier* — a control record carrying a checkpoint identifier *n* — into every source stream [2][3]. Each source snapshots its own state (for a Kafka source, the per-partition read offsets *Sₖ*) and broadcasts the barrier downstream. The barrier flows with the data, and crucially, it can never overtake records: barriers and records share the same FIFO channels.

An operator with a single input snapshots its state the moment the barrier arrives and forwards the barrier. An operator with *multiple* inputs — a join, or any operator downstream of a repartitioning such as `keyBy` — faces a subtler problem. Barriers may arrive on different channels at different times, and records arriving after the barrier on one channel belong to checkpoint *n+1* while records not yet arrived on another still belong to checkpoint *n*. If the operator kept processing, its snapshot would mix the two epochs and recovery would produce duplicates. The solution is *barrier alignment*: once the barrier for *n* arrives on a channel, the operator buffers subsequent records on that channel until the barrier has arrived on *all* channels. Only then does it snapshot its state and emit the barrier downstream [2].

![Checkpoint barrier injection and alignment across a two-input operator](/thesis/ths_1788733809000_b1f5-0.webp)

*Figure 1. Barriers injected at the sources separate pre-snapshot from post-snapshot records; a multi-input operator aligns barriers across its input channels before snapshotting.*

Alignment is the price of exactly-once, and it is paid only where it is owed. Operators with a single predecessor need no alignment at all, which is why purely embarrassingly parallel pipelines (`map`, `flatMap`, `filter`) deliver exactly-once state even when the job is configured for at-least-once mode — there is simply no channel on which epochs can interleave [2]. The alignment phase typically adds only milliseconds of latency, but under channel skew or backpressure it stretches, and that latency is directly observable in Flink's checkpoint metrics [2][5].

The resulting snapshot for checkpoint *n* contains, per source sub-task, the stream position *Sₖ* at barrier injection, and per operator sub-task, a pointer to its persisted state [3]. Recovery is then a global rollback: the latest completed checkpoint *k* is selected, the entire dataflow is redeployed, each operator is restored to its snapshotted state, and each source resumes from *Sₖ*. Records after *Sₖ* are replayed. Because the snapshot and the replay positions describe the same cut, every record is reflected in the restored state exactly once [3].

```java
StreamExecutionEnvironment env = StreamExecutionEnvironment.getExecutionEnvironment();
env.enableCheckpointing(60_000); // checkpoint every 60s
CheckpointConfig cfg = env.getCheckpointConfig();
cfg.setCheckpointingMode(CheckpointingMode.EXACTLY_ONCE); // barrier alignment on
cfg.setMinPauseBetweenCheckpoints(30_000);
cfg.setCheckpointTimeout(600_000);
cfg.setMaxConcurrentCheckpoints(1);
cfg.setExternalizedCheckpointCleanup(
    CheckpointConfig.ExternalizedCheckpointCleanup.RETAIN_ON_CANCELLATION);
```

### 4.2 Unaligned checkpoints: bounding checkpoint latency under backpressure

Aligned checkpointing has an Achilles' heel: the barrier cannot overtake queued records. Under sustained backpressure, barriers queue behind data in network buffers, alignment stalls, and checkpoints time out — precisely when the job is already struggling [5]. The *unaligned checkpoint* (introduced in Flink 1.11) breaks this coupling. If barriers do not align within a configurable *alignment timeout*, the operator stops waiting and snapshots immediately, persisting not only its state but also the *in-flight* records currently buffered in its input channels [5][6].

On recovery, those persisted buffers are replayed into the operator before processing resumes, restoring exactly the execution state captured at the barrier. The snapshot is slightly larger — it now contains channel state, the very thing ABS avoids on acyclic topologies — but checkpoint *duration* becomes independent of backpressure, which is what operators need for predictable recovery-point objectives [6].

|  | Aligned checkpoints | Unaligned checkpoints |
|---|---|---|
| Snapshot contents | Operator state + source offsets | Plus in-flight channel buffers |
| Checkpoint duration under backpressure | Unbounded (barriers queue behind data) | Bounded (barriers overtake) |
| Steady-state overhead | Minimal | Slightly higher I/O per checkpoint |
| Recovery work | Replay from offsets | Replay from offsets + buffered records |

In practice the two modes compose: the alignment timeout lets a job attempt cheap aligned snapshots and fall back to unaligned ones only when skew or backpressure demands it [6].

### 4.3 Watermarks, event time, and window semantics

Snapshots guarantee that *state* is exactly-once; watermarks guarantee that *time* is meaningful. A watermark *W(t)* asserts that all records with event timestamp ≤ *t* have been observed, allowing the engine to close windows, fire timers, and purge state deterministically [7]. Because sources are partitioned, each partition tracks its own watermark and the source emits the *minimum* across partitions — a single straggler holds back the global event-time clock, which is the price of the no-false-lateness guarantee [8]. The same minimum rule applies at every multi-input operator: the operator's event-time clock is the minimum of its inputs' watermarks, so a join never advances past data its slower input has not yet delivered [8].

Watermark generation is a policy choice. The two canonical strategies are *monotonous timestamps*, for streams known to be ordered, and *bounded out-of-orderness*, which emits *W(t) = max_observed − delay*, tolerating disorder up to the configured bound [7][8]. Streams carry punctuated or periodic watermarks (Flink's default emits every 200 ms), and timestamp assignment extracts event time from the record payload. Two refinements handle pathological cases: *idleness* detection, which stops a silent partition from pinning the minimum, and *watermark alignment* across sources (stabilized in Flink 1.15+), which throttles fast sources to bound skew [8].

![Event-time watermarks advancing over an out-of-order stream and firing tumbling windows](/thesis/ths_1788733809000_b1f5-1.webp)

*Figure 2. Watermarks W(t) advance the event-time clock over out-of-order events; windows fire exactly when the watermark passes their end.*

Late data — records arriving after the watermark has passed their timestamp — is handled by *allowed lateness*, which keeps windows updateable for a grace period, and by *side outputs*, which route irrecoverably late records to a separate stream rather than silently dropping them [7]. Watermarks are checkpointed as part of operator state, so recovery restores the event-time clock to the same cut as the data, and replay reproduces identical window results [7].

### 4.4 Transactional sinks: two-phase commit for end-to-end guarantees

Exactly-once state is necessary but not sufficient: a pipeline writing results to Kafka, a database, or a file system must ensure those *external* effects are also applied exactly once. Flink's answer is to treat the sink as a participant in a distributed transaction coordinated by the checkpointing protocol itself, via the `TwoPhaseCommitSinkFunction` contract [4][9]:

1. **`beginTransaction()`** — opens a new external transaction at the start of each checkpoint epoch.
2. **`invoke()`** — writes each record into the open transaction (invisible to downstream consumers).
3. **`preCommit()`** — triggered by the checkpoint barrier; flushes the transaction and stores its handle in the checkpoint state.
4. **`commit()` / `abort()`** — invoked after the checkpoint completes or fails, across all sink sub-tasks atomically.

The checkpoint barrier is the *prepare* phase; the coordinator's `notifyCheckpointComplete` — sent only after every operator has acknowledged the checkpoint — is the *commit* phase [4][9]. A transaction therefore bundles exactly the writes between two consecutive checkpoints, and it becomes visible if and only if the checkpoint that produced it committed. On failure, open transactions are aborted, never committed, and replay regenerates the same records into fresh transactions. With Kafka, this is realized through Kafka transactions with `read_committed` isolation on the consumer side, so downstream readers never observe aborted writes [4].

![Two-phase commit lifecycle of a transactional sink across checkpoint epochs](/thesis/ths_1788733809000_b1f5-2.webp)

*Figure 3. Each checkpoint epoch opens a transaction; the barrier pre-commits it and notifyCheckpointComplete commits it. Failures abort.*

Where transactions are unavailable, *idempotent* sinks (keyed writes, upserts) offer a cheaper alternative: duplicates are harmless because re-application converges to the same state. The 2PC contract generalizes to any transactional store — Kafka, Pulsar, and XA-capable JDBC sinks all plug into the same barrier-as-coordinator protocol [9].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Correctness

> **Theorem (Cut consistency of ABS).** *For acyclic dataflows, Asynchronous Barrier Snapshotting produces a consistent global cut — no recorded receive without its corresponding recorded send — while persisting only operator state, never channel contents.* [1]

*Proof sketch.* Barriers induce a total order on records per channel: every record before the barrier belongs to epoch *n*, every record after to epoch *n+1*. Single-input operators snapshot at the barrier, so their state reflects exactly the pre-barrier prefix. Multi-input operators align barriers before snapshotting, so their state likewise reflects the pre-barrier prefix of *every* input. Hence the collection of local snapshots corresponds to a cut with no cross-cut messages on acyclic topologies; on cyclic topologies the logged back-edge records close the cut explicitly. ∎

> **Theorem (Exactly-once state).** *On failure, restoring the latest completed checkpoint k and replaying each source from its recorded offset S_k yields operator states reachable in some failure-free execution, with every input record reflected exactly once.* [2][3]

*Proof sketch.* By cut consistency, the snapshot at *k* plus replay of the post-cut suffix is a linearization of a valid execution: records before *S_k* are represented in state, records after are re-applied in order, and barrier alignment guarantees no record straddles the cut. At-least-once mode relaxes alignment, admitting duplicates; exactly-once mode does not. ∎

> **Theorem (End-to-end atomicity).** *With a two-phase-commit sink, a record's external effects become visible if and only if the checkpoint epoch that produced them completes.* [4][9]

*Proof sketch.* Records of epoch *n* are written into transaction *T_n*, invisible until commit. `preCommit` fires on the barrier; `commit` fires only on `notifyCheckpointComplete`, which the coordinator sends only after all operators acknowledge checkpoint *n*. If any operator fails, the checkpoint aborts, *T_n* is aborted, and replay regenerates the records into a new transaction. Visibility and checkpoint completion are therefore logically equivalent. ∎

### 5.2 Cost profile

The ABS paper's evaluation reports that snapshotting imposes no heavy impact on execution: throughput scales linearly with cluster size and frequent snapshots remain cheap, because barriers add only *O(|E|)* marker messages per checkpoint and snapshots are taken asynchronously without pausing the pipeline [1]. In production, alignment latency is typically a few milliseconds, with outliers under skew [2]. The dominant steady-state cost is state I/O, which Flink amortizes with *incremental* checkpoints — RocksDB-based backends persist only the delta since the last checkpoint — and *asynchronous* materialization, which moves serialization off the processing path [5]. Unaligned checkpoints shift cost from latency risk to snapshot size: persisting in-flight buffers grows each checkpoint but bounds duration under backpressure [6].

Recovery cost decomposes into state reload plus replay of the post-cut suffix, which is why the checkpoint interval is the central tuning knob: shorter intervals bound recovery time at the price of higher steady-state overhead, following the classic Young/Daly optimum that balances checkpoint cost against mean time between failures [2][5].

## 6 Limitations

The guarantee is strong but conditional, and the conditions deserve equal billing.

- **Cyclic dataflows.** Iterative computations require ABS to log in-flight records on back-edges, reintroducing the channel-state cost the acyclic fast path avoids [1].
- **Backpressure and barrier latency.** Aligned checkpoints degrade under sustained backpressure; unaligned checkpoints bound the duration but inflate snapshot size. Neither mode eliminates the tension between checkpoint frequency and pipeline saturation [5][6].
- **Watermark idleness and skew.** The per-partition minimum means one stalled partition pins the event-time clock for the entire job. Idleness timeouts and source watermark alignment mitigate this but can mask genuine upstream stalls [8].
- **Sink coverage.** End-to-end exactly-once requires a transactional or idempotent sink. Many real sinks (plain HTTP endpoints, unfenced append-only files) support neither, and the guarantee degrades to exactly-once *state* with at-least-once *effects*. Kafka transactions additionally impose a transaction timeout that must exceed the checkpoint interval [4][9].
- **Latency floor.** Transactional visibility is gated on checkpoint completion, so sub-checkpoint-interval end-to-end latency is unattainable with 2PC sinks [9].
- **Operational scope.** Checkpoints serve fault tolerance, not code upgrades or rescaling — those require savepoints, whose cross-version compatibility is a separate, stricter contract [2].

## 7 Conclusion

Exactly-once stream processing in Apache Flink is not a single feature but a composition of three independently motivated mechanisms: Asynchronous Barrier Snapshotting, which captures consistent distributed state without pausing the pipeline; watermarks, which give event time a deterministic, replay-safe semantics over disordered streams; and the two-phase-commit sink contract, which extends atomicity from internal state to external effects. Barrier alignment is the precise point where the cost is paid, and unaligned checkpoints are where it is renegotiated. The theorems are clean — cut consistency, exactly-once state, end-to-end atomicity — but the engineering lesson is cleaner still: each mechanism degrades gracefully and observably at its boundary, which is why the guarantee survives contact with production.
