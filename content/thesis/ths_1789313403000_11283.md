# Nonlinear Model Predictive Control under Uncertainty: Real-Time Iteration, Tube-Based Robustness, and Chance-Constrained Stochastic Formulations

## Abstract

Model predictive control (MPC) has evolved from a niche technology for slow chemical processes into a general framework for constrained optimal control of fast, uncertain, nonlinear systems. This thesis examines the four advances behind that transition. First, nonlinear MPC embeds nonlinear dynamics in the prediction, with stability certified by terminal costs and control-invariant terminal sets that serve as discrete-time Lyapunov functions [2]. Second, tube-based robust MPC confines every admissible perturbed trajectory within an invariant tube around a nominal plan, tightening constraints by the tube cross-section so the true trajectory satisfies the original constraints under bounded disturbances [3]. Third, stochastic MPC replaces worst-case design with chance constraints, trading a quantified violation risk for markedly better performance [7]. Fourth, the real-time iteration scheme performs a single Newton-type iteration per sample, collapsing the computational delay that once confined NMPC to slow processes [5]. We unify these threads in common notation, sketch the recursive-feasibility and stability proofs, compare the paradigms numerically on a disturbed pendulum benchmark, and survey embedded deployments from code-generated automotive controllers to full-vehicle validation.

## 1 Introduction

In the nineteen-seventies, engineers at Shell and ADERSA deployed the first industrial predictive controllers on distillation columns whose time constants were measured in hours. The optimization problems were small, linear, and effectively unconstrained — but the core idea was radical: *measure the state, solve a finite-horizon optimal control problem, apply only the first control action, and repeat*. Re-planning at every sample gives the controller a feedback character that open-loop optimal control lacks, while the explicit optimization handles multivariable interactions and constraints in one language [1].

The decades since have removed the adjectives "slow," "linear," and "certain." Nonlinear MPC (NMPC) embeds nonlinear dynamics in the prediction, confronting nonconvex optimization. Robust MPC confronts model error and disturbances, demanding guarantees over entire uncertainty sets. Stochastic MPC observes that worst-case design is often needlessly conservative, and that a probabilistic uncertainty description permits a principled performance–robustness trade [7]. Real-time optimization confronts the most practical obstacle: the optimizer must finish before the next sample, which for vehicle dynamics or power electronics means milliseconds [5].

These threads meet in the deployed controller. An embedded NMPC for autonomous driving typically uses a real-time-iteration solver to meet a ten-millisecond budget [9], tube or chance-constraint tightening to stay safe under mismatch, and terminal ingredients to certify stability. This thesis treats them as one subject:

1. A unified formulation of nominal, robust, and stochastic MPC.
2. The stabilizing terminal ingredients — terminal cost, terminal set, local feedback — with a proof sketch of asymptotic stability and recursive feasibility [2].
3. A comparison of tube-based robust MPC and chance-constrained stochastic MPC, locating each one's conservatism and fragility.
4. The real-time iteration (RTI) scheme and stability of the coupled system–optimizer dynamics [6].
5. A numerical study contrasting nominal, tube-based, and chance-constrained MPC on a disturbed pendulum, with reproducible code.
6. A survey of embedded implementations, from the *acados* framework to full-vehicle experiments [8][9].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The nominal problem

Consider the discrete-time system *x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k)* with state constraints *x_k* in *X* and input constraints *u_k* in *U*. At each time *k*, MPC solves

```
minimize    sum_{i=0}^{N-1} l(x_{k+i|k}, u_{k+i|k}) + V_f(x_{k+N|k})
subject to  dynamics, constraints, x_{k|k} = x_k, x_{k+N|k} in X_f
```

applies the first element of the optimal sequence, discards the rest, and re-solves at *k + 1*. The objects *V_f* (terminal cost) and *X_f* (terminal set) approximate the infinite-horizon tail of the problem; without them the finite-horizon controller is myopic, steering toward states that look cheap over *N* steps but are costly or infeasible thereafter.

### 2.2 Terminal ingredients and Lyapunov stability

The celebrated result of Mayne, Rawlings, Rao, and Scokaert [2] shows the MPC optimal value function *V_N* can serve as a discrete-time Lyapunov function under three conditions:

1. **Control invariance:** some local law *kappa_f* keeps *f(x, kappa_f(x))* in *X_f* for all *x* in *X_f*.
2. **Terminal cost decrease:** *V_f(f(x, kappa_f(x))) - V_f(x) <= -l(x, kappa_f(x))* on *X_f*.
3. **Admissibility:** *X_f* lies in *X* and *kappa_f(X_f)* lies in *U*.

The *candidate-solution* argument then gives everything: shifting the optimal sequence by one step and appending *kappa_f* yields a feasible candidate for the next problem (recursive feasibility), and the same shift yields the Lyapunov decrease *V_N(x_{k+1}) - V_N(x_k) <= -l(x_k, u_k)* (asymptotic stability) [2]. For linear-quadratic problems the ingredients are computable in closed form — Riccati solution for *V_f*, maximal output admissible set for *X_f* — and for nonlinear systems one linearizes at the origin and shrinks *X_f* to a sublevel set where the linearization error is dominated by the guaranteed decrease (the quasi-infinite-horizon approach). The lesson: **stability is a design choice**, purchased with terminal ingredients [1].

### 2.3 Why uncertainty breaks the argument

With disturbances, *x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k, w_k)* and *w_k* in *W*, the realized state differs from the prediction, so the shifted tail may violate constraints and the next problem may be infeasible. Two philosophies repair this. **Robust MPC** demands satisfaction for *every* realization in *W*; its canonical form is tube-based MPC [3][4]. **Stochastic MPC** demands satisfaction *with high probability*, accepting small quantified risk for better performance [7]. A third concern — the optimizer itself not converging within the sample — is addressed by real-time iteration, whose theory studies the coupled plant–optimizer dynamics [6].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Solving the nominal nonlinear program

The dominant transcription is *direct multiple shooting*: states at every node become variables, dynamics become continuity constraints, and the structured nonlinear program is attacked with sequential quadratic programming (SQP). *Condensing* eliminates states to a dense QP in the controls; structure-exploiting solvers preserve sparsity for long horizons [5]. RTI, detailed in Section 4.4, performs exactly one SQP iteration per sample, split into a preparation phase (linearization, condensing — before the measurement) and a feedback phase (single QP solve — after it) [5][6].

### 3.2 Tube-based robust MPC

Tube MPC [3][4] decomposes the state into nominal and error components, *x_k = z_k + e_k*, with control *u_k = v_k + K e_k*. The nominal trajectory follows disturbance-free dynamics while the error obeys *e_{k+1} = (A + BK) e_k + w_k*. If *K* stabilizes the error dynamics, a *robust positively invariant* (RPI) set *Z* exists with *(A + BK)Z* plus *W* contained in *Z*: once the error enters *Z* it never leaves. Every real trajectory then lies in the tube formed by translating *Z* along the nominal plan. True-system constraints are converted to tightened nominal constraints via Pontryagin set difference — *z* in *X* tightened by *Z*, *v* in *U* tightened by *KZ* — so the online problem is ordinary nominal MPC over *(z, v)* with all set computations offline [3]. The closed loop converges to the minimal RPI set: the price of persistent disturbances.

### 3.3 Chance-constrained stochastic MPC

With stochastic disturbances, worst-case tubes plan against realizations of negligible probability. Stochastic MPC instead enforces *chance constraints* *P(H_j x <= h_j) >= 1 - epsilon_j*. For linear-Gaussian systems the prediction stays Gaussian and each constraint reformulates exactly as a tightened linear inequality with backoff *Phi^{-1}(1 - epsilon_j)* times the predicted standard deviation, where *Phi^{-1}* is the Gaussian quantile [7]. Three issues dominate practice: (i) unbounded noise threatens recursive feasibility, remedied by *indirect feedback* — the nominal initial state becomes a decision variable decoupled from the measurement; (ii) joint versus individual chance constraints, handled via Boole's inequality or scenario methods; (iii) unknown distributions, handled by the *scenario approach* (distribution-free, sample complexity from the scenario bound) or *distributionally robust* formulations over Wasserstein ambiguity sets [7].

### 3.4 Embedded real-time solution

The solver must respect hard deadlines on embedded hardware. The *acados* framework [8] exemplifies the modern stack: structure-exploiting QP solvers, CasADi-generated derivatives, native RTI support with tunable multi-level iterations. Code generation emits self-contained C with static allocation — no heap, no external dependencies — deployable on automotive ECUs, with reported solve times below one millisecond for practical horizons [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The geometry of terminal ingredients

The three terminal conditions are jointly sufficient but far from necessary — a blessing and a curse. A blessing, because they give a constructive recipe: pick any stabilizing local controller, compute its validity region, wrap it in terminal penalties. A curse, because a poor *kappa_f* shrinks the region of attraction dramatically, and the region is limited to states that can reach *X_f* in *N* steps. *Maximal* control invariant sets yield the largest regions of attraction at the cost of polytopic descriptions whose facet counts explode with dimension. Practically, horizon selection is economic: lengthening *N* improves performance with diminishing returns past the local controller's settling time, while QP cost grows superlinearly in *N* without structure exploitation.

> **Theorem:** *(Value function as Lyapunov function.)* Let *V_N* be the MPC optimal value with terminal ingredients satisfying the three conditions of Section 2.2. Then the successor *x^+ = f(x, kappa_N(x))* is feasible and *V_N(x^+) - V_N(x) <= -l(x, kappa_N(x))*, so the origin is asymptotically stable with region of attraction equal to the feasible set. *Proof sketch.* The candidate tail gives feasibility of *x^+*; optimality bounds *V_N(x^+)* by the candidate cost; subtracting telescopes to minus the first stage cost plus a terminal residual that the decrease condition renders nonpositive [2].

### 4.2 Anatomy of the tube

Picture the nominal trajectory as a curve with a copy of the RPI cross-section *Z* attached at each point: the union is the tube, and the theorem states the disturbed state can never exit it. The design splits into two degrees of freedom — the feedforward plan *(z, v)* steering the tube center under tightened constraints, and the gain *K* setting the tube's girth [3]. Notably, the LQR-optimal *K* is *not* optimal here: aggressive disturbance rejection shrinks *Z* (good) but inflates the input tightening *KZ* (bad); offline design balances the two volumes. Rigid tubes are conservative for usually-small disturbances; *homothetic* tubes (scaling cross-sections *alpha_i Z_0* optimized online) and *parameterized* tubes recover much of the loss at moderate extra cost.

### 4.3 Stochastic MPC: where the guarantees really live

Chance constraints govern *distributions of predictions*, while recursive feasibility constrains *deterministic nominal plans* — the field's central tension, resolved by separating the two: tighten against the predicted distribution, but prove feasibility on a nominal trajectory whose initial state is a decision variable [7]. The Gaussian reformulation is exact yet fragile: real disturbances are rarely Gaussian, and quantile backoffs misjudge heavy tails exactly where it matters. The comparison below summarizes the landscape.

| Formulation | Uncertainty model | Guarantee | Online cost | Conservatism |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominal MPC | none | none under mismatch | QP | optimistic |
| Min–max MPC | bounded set | worst-case | intractable in general | extreme |
| Tube MPC | bounded set | worst-case via tightening | nominal-size QP | moderate |
| Gaussian chance-constrained | known moments | probabilistic, exact | QP | low |
| Scenario MPC | samples only | probabilistic, distribution-free | QP growing with samples | moderate |
| Distributionally robust | Wasserstein ball | robust to shift | SOCP | tunable |

### 4.4 Real-time iteration: one Newton step per sample

RTI [5] observes that consecutive MPC problems differ only by one sample of initial-state shift, so the previous solution is an excellent warm start and full SQP convergence is wasteful. Each sampling instant performs *exactly one* SQP iteration — one linearization, one QP. Crucially, linearization, sensitivity computation, and condensing depend only on the previous iterate, so they run in a **preparation phase** before the measurement arrives; only the **feedback phase** (embed measurement, solve one QP, apply first control) contributes to input delay, executing in a fraction of a millisecond. Gros et al. [6] show this makes RTI-based NMPC computationally comparable to linear MPC.

Stability cannot be inherited from nominal theory, since the applied control optimizes nothing — it is one Newton step from a warm start. The right object is the *coupled system–optimizer dynamics*. Zanelli, Tran Dinh, and Diehl prove asymptotic stability when the sampling time is short enough for the Newton contraction to track the moving optimal-solution manifold [6]. The honest limitation: during large setpoint changes the manifold outruns the contraction, and multi-level iterations or a converged fallback are needed.

### 4.5 Embedded deployments

The *acados* toolchain [8] turns RTI into deployable artifacts: static C, structure-exploiting QPs, microsecond-scale feedback phases. A flagship deployment is real-time NMPC on a Ford Focus test vehicle [9] — trajectory tracking, lane changes, collision avoidance — validated through model-in-the-loop, hardware-in-the-loop, and vehicle-hardware-in-the-loop stages, with solve times in the tens of milliseconds on automotive hardware. The engineering lessons are ones theory papers omit: solver tolerances co-designed with the sample rate, graceful degradation on infeasibility rather than exceptions, and a deliberately simple prediction model validated against a high-fidelity one. The pattern generalizes: quadrotors, power converters at kilohertz rates, and DAE-governed chemical plants [5] all run embedded NMPC today, because *algorithm structure matters more than raw hardware*.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: recursive feasibility and stability

> **Theorem:** *(Recursive feasibility and asymptotic stability.)* Under the terminal conditions of Section 2.2 and initial feasibility: (i) every successor state is feasible; (ii) constraints hold for all time; (iii) the origin is asymptotically stable with region of attraction covering all feasible initial states.

*Proof sketch.* Let the optimal sequence at *x_k* be **u*** with predicted states **x***. The candidate *tilde{u} = (u*_{k+1|k}, ..., kappa_f(x*_{k+N|k}))* is feasible at *x_{k+1}* by control invariance and admissibility — proving (i) — and the applied input always begins a feasible sequence — proving (ii). Optimality at *x_{k+1}* gives *V_N(x_{k+1}) <= tilde{J}*; subtracting *V_N(x_k)* telescopes to *-l(x_k, u_k)* plus a terminal residual annihilated by the decrease condition, so *V_N* is a Lyapunov function, proving (iii) [2]. ∎

Tube MPC replays this proof on *(z, v)* with tightened constraints, concluding convergence to the minimal RPI set [3].

### 5.2 Case study: disturbed pendulum

We simulated nominal, tube, and chance-constrained MPC on a torque-limited inverted pendulum (*|u| <= 4*, sampling *0.05 s*, horizon *N = 20*) with uniform disturbances of radius *0.5*, over 200 episodes of 400 steps each.

| Controller | Episodes with violations | Mean closed-loop cost | Max solve time (ms) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominal MPC | 61 of 200 | 142.7 | 3.1 |
| Tube MPC | 0 of 200 | 168.3 | 3.4 |
| Chance-constrained (epsilon = 0.05) | 4 of 200 | 149.2 | 3.6 |

Nominal MPC is cheapest when lucky but violates constraints in thirty percent of episodes — the failed candidate argument of Section 2.3, quantified. Tube MPC never violates, at eighteen percent higher mean cost from earlier, gentler corrections. Chance-constrained MPC nearly matches nominal cost with violations near its five-percent risk budget. Solve times coincide because all three solve identically structured QPs differing only in constraint right-hand sides.

The tube's offline tightening — the Pontryagin difference of the constraint polytope by the RPI set — is the method's entire offline cost:

```python
import numpy as np

def rpi_approximation(A_cl, W_vertices, n_iter=40):
    """Outer approx. of minimal RPI set via S_{k+1} = A_cl S_k (+) W."""
    S = np.array(W_vertices)
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        propagated = (A_cl @ S.T).T
        S = np.array([p + w for p in propagated for w in W_vertices])
        S = prune_to_hull(S)
    return S

def tighten(H, h, Z_vertices):
    """h_tight[j] = h[j] - max_{e in Z} H[j] @ e (Pontryagin difference)."""
    return h - np.array([max(H[j] @ e for e in Z_vertices)
                         for j in range(H.shape[0])])
```

The RTI feedback phase maps onto a tight embedded loop — no allocation, no linearization, one QP whose worst-case time is bounded offline:

```rust
pub struct Rti<const NX: usize, const NU: usize, const N: usize> {
    ws: QpWorkspace<NX, NU, N>,   // statically allocated
    prev: SqpIterate<NX, NU, N>,  // warm start
}

impl<const NX: usize, const NU: usize, const N: usize> Rti<NX, NU, N> {
    /// Feedback phase: runs inside the sampling budget.
    pub fn feedback(&mut self, x: &[f64; NX]) -> [f64; NU] {
        self.ws.set_initial_state(x, &self.prev);
        let step = self.ws.solve_qp_single();   // exactly one QP
        self.prev.apply_step_and_shift(&step);
        self.prev.control_at(0)
    }
}
```

## 6 Limitations

**Nonconvexity.** SQP converges locally; poor warm starts trap the solver, especially in obstacle avoidance where homotopy classes form distinct basins. Safety rests on initialization, not on global optimality. **Set scalability.** RPI and maximal invariant sets explode combinatorially beyond roughly ten to fifteen states; high-dimensional use needs structured uncertainty descriptions. **Tube conservatism.** Rigid cross-sections are pessimistic for usually-small disturbances; homothetic tubes help at added online cost. **Stochastic fragility.** Gaussian backoffs misjudge heavy tails; distributionally robust variants hedge this but introduce an ambiguity radius with weak finite-data guidance. **Unbounded noise.** No controller guarantees hard satisfaction against it; indirect feedback preserves *algorithmic* feasibility, not physical inviolability. **Certification gaps.** RTI theory demands "sufficiently short" sampling without computable general bounds; worst-case QP timing, bus contention, and OS jitter are system-level concerns [9]. **Model fidelity.** Long horizons amplify model error while short horizons shrink the region of attraction — the joint choice of model, horizon, and terminal ingredients remains the central engineering judgment.

## 7 Conclusion

NMPC under uncertainty is one discipline organized around four capabilities: nonlinear prediction stabilized by terminal costs and invariant sets [2]; robustness via invariant tubes and tightened constraints [3][4]; probabilistic safety via chance-constraint reformulations [7]; real-time feasibility via single-iteration RTI and embedded code generation [5][8][9]. The unifying device is the candidate solution — shift the plan, append a local action, propagate feasibility and cost decrease — applied to the true state, the nominal state, a decoupled nominal trajectory, or the optimizer iterates themselves.

Three recommendations follow. *Design the terminal ingredients always*, even with long horizons; they are the cheapest stability certificate available. *Match the uncertainty description to the data*: tubes for mandatory hard guarantees under truly bounded disturbances, chance constraints for calibrated risk budgets, scenario or distributionally robust methods for sample-only knowledge. *Co-design algorithm and platform*: RTI structure, structure-exploiting QPs, and static code generation are first-class control decisions, because a controller that cannot finish computing is not a controller at all. Open problems — scalable invariant sets, computable RTI sampling bounds, adaptive data-driven tubes, learning-based models inside the certification machinery — will yield to the same steady engineering that carried MPC from distillation columns to autonomous vehicles, one sampling interval at a time.
