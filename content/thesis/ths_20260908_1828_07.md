# QUIC Transport Protocol: Encrypted Handshakes, Stream Multiplexing, Loss Recovery, and Multipath Extensions

## Abstract

QUIC is a UDP-based, multiplexed, and secure transport protocol standardized in RFC 9000 [1], with TLS 1.3 integration in RFC 9001 [2] and loss detection and congestion control in RFC 9002 [3]. This thesis develops a rigorous account of QUIC's architecture: the 1-RTT encrypted handshake fusing cryptographic and transport negotiation, independent stream multiplexing with dual-level flow control, separate packet number spaces per encryption level that eliminate TCP's retransmission ambiguity, and ACK-frame-driven loss recovery built on RACK-style detection and PTO-driven probing. We formalize the protocol's congestion control contract, analyze the per-path congestion state emerging in multipath extensions [5], and evaluate connection migration, address validation, and the MASQUE proxying framework [6] as mechanisms for mobility and ossification resistance. Drawing on measurement literature and the SIGCOMM 2017 deployment study [9], we quantify QUIC's latency and loss-resilience advantages, derive bounds on handshake cost under amplification limits, and identify open problems in multipath scheduling, ECN semantics, and middlebox traversal — a self-contained, PhD-level treatment of the most significant transport-layer redesign since TCP itself.

## 1. Introduction

The Transmission Control Protocol has carried the Internet's reliable byte streams for over four decades, but its architectural constraints — in-kernel deployment, head-of-line blocking across multiplexed HTTP/2 streams, multi-RTT handshakes, and ossification by middleboxes — have proven increasingly expensive [9]. QUIC (originally "Quick UDP Internet Connections") was proposed as a clean-slate transport: a user-space protocol over UDP that is *always encrypted*, negotiates cryptographic and transport parameters in a single handshake, multiplexes independent streams without cross-stream blocking, and survives IP address changes through connection migration [1].

Standardization by the IETF QUIC working group produced a four-document core: RFC 9000 (transport), RFC 9001 (TLS integration), RFC 9002 (loss detection and congestion control), and RFC 9003 (invariants across versions). QUIC version 1 is now deployed at Internet scale by Google, Meta, Cloudflare, and Apple, carrying a large and growing fraction of global web traffic over HTTP/3 [10].

> **Theorem:** *For any QUIC version-1 connection, every packet that is not a Version Negotiation or Retry packet is cryptographically protected, and every stream frame is recoverable independently of the fate of any other stream's packets.* This thesis proves the second claim from the frame-packet decoupling invariant of RFC 9000 [1, §2.3] and surveys the first as a property of the RFC 9001 key schedule [2].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 situates QUIC against TCP/TLS and SCTP. Section 3 describes our methodology: specification-driven analysis grounded in RFC text, formal modeling of the loss-recovery state machine, and simulation-based congestion evaluation. Section 4 provides a deep technical dive across five dimensions. Section 5 presents empirical results and proof sketches. Section 6 states limitations, and Section 7 concludes with open research directions.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 TCP, TLS, and the cost of layering

Classical TCP+TLS requires up to three round trips before application data flows: TCP SYN/SYN-ACK, then the TLS handshake. TCP's byte-stream abstraction couples *all* multiplexed data: a single lost segment stalls every stream sharing the connection — head-of-line blocking that HTTP/2 inherited despite its own framing [10]. Furthermore, TCP's sequence numbers double as the loss-detection namespace, so retransmitted segments are indistinguishable from originals (the *retransmission ambiguity*), forcing estimators like Karn's algorithm to discard RTT samples from retransmissions.

### 2.2 SCTP and the roads not taken

SCTP [RFC 4960] offered multi-streaming and multihoming two decades earlier but failed to deploy widely because middleboxes block or mishandle its IP protocol number, and in-kernel deployment cycles are measured in years. QUIC internalizes these lessons: it runs over UDP (port 443), is encrypted end-to-end so middleboxes cannot ossify on its internals, and lives in user space for rapid iteration [1].

### 2.3 TLS 1.3

TLS 1.3 [4] reduced handshake latency to 1-RTT (0-RTT with resumption), removed static RSA and renegotiation, and made forward secrecy mandatory. QUIC does not merely *use* TLS 1.3; it *embeds* the TLS handshake as CRYPTO frames inside QUIC packets and replaces TLS's record layer entirely with QUIC packet protection [2].

| Property | TCP + TLS 1.2 | TCP + TLS 1.3 | QUIC (RFC 9000/9001) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Handshake RTTs (fresh) | 3 | 2 | 1 |
| 0-RTT resumption | No | Yes | Yes |
| Stream head-of-line blocking | Yes | Yes | No |
| Retransmission ambiguity | Yes | Yes | No (monotonic PNs) |
| Connection migration | No | No | Yes |
| Middlebox-visible transport | Full | Full | Headers only |

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis follows three complementary methods:

1. **Specification-driven formalization.** We extract the normative state machines from RFCs 9000–9002 — the packet number spaces, the ACK frame grammar, the loss-detection algorithm of RFC 9002 Appendix A, and the key-derivation schedule of RFC 9001 — and restate them as executable pseudocode, checking corner cases (e.g., PTO across packet number spaces, key update races) against the RFC text [1][2][3].
2. **Analytic modeling.** We derive bounds on handshake completion under the anti-amplification limit (servers may send at most 3× the bytes received before address validation [1, §8.1]), on ACK-frame encoding efficiency, and on the convergence of the RTT estimator under reordering.
3. **Simulation and measurement synthesis.** We implement the RFC 9002 recovery algorithm in Python to validate loss-detection behavior under controlled reordering, and we synthesize published measurement results on QUIC deployment, handshake latency, and loss resilience [9][10] into a unified empirical picture.

All claims are anchored to primary sources: the RFC series [1][2][3][4], the multipath QUIC draft [5], the MASQUE specifications [6][7], and peer-reviewed measurement studies [9][10].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 TLS 1.3 Integration and the Encrypted Handshake

QUIC's most radical departure is that *cryptography is not a layer above transport but a component of it*. The TLS 1.3 handshake messages are carried in CRYPTO frames on stream-like cryptographic data streams, one per encryption level (Initial, Handshake, Application Data) [2]. Each encryption level has its own packet number space, its own keys, and its own AEAD protection.

Key derivation follows a two-stage schedule. Initial secrets are derived from a version-specific salt and the client's Destination Connection ID via HKDF-Extract, yielding `initial_secret`; from this, client and server Initial keys and IVs are expanded with the labels `"quic key"` and `"quic iv"` [2, §5.2]. Once the TLS handshake completes, 1-RTT keys are derived from the TLS traffic secrets with labels `"quic key"`, `"quic iv"`, and `"quic hp"` (header protection) [2, §5.1]. The use of distinct labels per context guarantees *domain separation*: a key usable for packet protection can never be confused with one for header protection.

Header protection itself is a novel mechanism: a 16-byte sample from the packet payload seeds an AES-ECB (or ChaCha20, or AES-CTR) mask applied to the packet number field and parts of the header [2, §5.4]. Its purpose is not confidentiality of the payload — the AEAD already provides that — but *ossification resistance*: it prevents middleboxes from parsing packet numbers and building dependencies on them.

```
Client                                              Server
  | Initial[CRYPTO: ClientHello]                      |
  |-------------------------------------------------->|
  |                         Initial[CRYPTO: ServerHello],
  |                         Handshake[CRYPTO: EE, CERT, CV, FIN]
  |<--------------------------------------------------|
  | Handshake[CRYPTO: FIN], 1-RTT[STREAM: GET /]       |
  |-------------------------------------------------->|
  |                              1-RTT[STREAM: data...]|
  |<--------------------------------------------------|
        Figure: QUIC 1-RTT handshake (simplified)
```

Because transport parameters (flow-control limits, max streams, idle timeout, stateless-reset tokens) are exchanged inside the TLS handshake as extensions [1, §7.4], a QUIC handshake negotiates *both* security and transport in a single round trip — and the parameters are authenticated, defeating downgrade attacks that plagued earlier designs.

### 4.2 Stream Multiplexing, Flow Control, and Frame Decoupling

QUIC streams are lightweight, ordered byte streams identified by 62-bit stream IDs whose two low bits encode initiator (client/server) and directionality (bidirectional/unidirectional) [1, §2.1]. Stream 0 is reserved; cryptographic data uses dedicated CRYPTO frames rather than streams.

The central invariant is **frame–packet decoupling**: STREAM frames carry an explicit *(stream ID, offset, length)* triple, so a frame is meaningful independent of the packet that carried it [1, §2.3]. Retransmission therefore re-packages *frames*, not *packets*: lost STREAM data is re-sent in a new packet with a *new, monotonically increasing packet number* but the *same stream offset*. This eliminates TCP's retransmission ambiguity at the root — every packet number is sent exactly once, so every ACK unambiguously samples the RTT of a unique transmission [3].

Flow control operates at two levels: per-stream (`MAX_STREAM_DATA`) and connection-wide (`MAX_DATA`), both expressed in absolute byte offsets [1, §4.1]. A receiver advertises windows; a sender blocked by them emits `STREAM_DATA_BLOCKED` / `DATA_BLOCKED` frames so the receiver can distinguish deadlock from idleness. This design prevents a single greedy stream from starving others while keeping the mechanism entirely receiver-driven.

*Head-of-line blocking is eliminated between streams*: loss on stream *A* stalls only stream *A*; stream *B*'s data continues to be delivered to the application. Blocking persists *within* a stream (ordered delivery) and at the connection flow-control level, which is the correct granularity — it reflects genuine resource constraints rather than framing artifacts.

### 4.3 Packet Number Spaces, ACK Frames, and RACK-Style Loss Detection

RFC 9002 defines loss detection over three packet number spaces — Initial, Handshake, and Application Data — each with independent packet number sequences starting at 0 [3, §A.2]. Packet numbers are encoded in 1–4 bytes on the wire; the receiver reconstructs the full 62-bit number from the largest received in that space [1, §17.1].

ACK frames encode *ranges* of received packet numbers plus an ACK delay field, allowing the sender to subtract the peer's processing delay from RTT samples [1, §19.3.2]. ECN counts may ride along in ACK frames, enabling the sender to detect CE-marking without TCP-style flag echo [1, §13.4.2].

Loss detection combines three mechanisms [3]:

- **Packet threshold:** a packet is declared lost when a later packet in the same number space is acknowledged and the gap exceeds `packet_threshold` (default 3) — the direct descendant of TCP's duplicate-ACK rule and of RACK's reordering window.
- **Time threshold:** a packet is declared lost when it was sent more than `9/8 × max(srtt, latest_rtt)` before the newest acknowledged packet — mirroring RACK-TLP's time-based reasoning that any sufficiently older unacknowledged packet has likely been lost rather than reordered.
- **Probe Timeout (PTO):** when no acknowledgment arrives, a timer fires after `srtt + 4×rttvar + max_ack_delay`, prompting the endpoint to send probe data — new data if available, else PING or retransmitted unacknowledged data at the *front* of the queue. Critically, PTO is armed per number space with exponential backoff, and a single PTO event probes *all* spaces with in-flight data [3, §A.8].

> **Theorem:** *Monotone packet numbers plus frame retransmission imply that every RTT sample is unambiguous.* **Proof sketch:** Each packet number is transmitted at most once [1, §12.3]; an ACK for packet number *n* therefore refers to exactly one transmission event with a known send timestamp; the ACK delay correction bounds receiver processing time; hence the sample `now − sent − ack_delay` measures the true round trip of that transmission. ∎

The Python model below reproduces the RFC 9002 smoothed-RTT update:

```python
ALPHA, BETA, K_GRANULARITY = 1/8, 1/4, 1e-3  # seconds

def update_rtt(state, latest_rtt, ack_delay):
    # RFC 9002, Section A.4: adjust for peer processing delay
    adjusted = latest_rtt
    if latest_rtt >= state.min_rtt + ack_delay:
        adjusted = latest_rtt - ack_delay
    if state.srtt is None:  # first sample
        state.srtt, state.rttvar = adjusted, adjusted / 2
    else:
        state.rttvar = (1 - BETA) * state.rttvar + BETA * abs(state.srtt - adjusted)
        state.srtt = (1 - ALPHA) * state.srtt + ALPHA * adjusted
    state.min_rtt = min(state.min_rtt, adjusted)
    state.max_ack_delay = max(state.max_ack_delay, ack_delay)
    return state
```

### 4.4 Congestion Control, Connection Migration, and Address Validation

RFC 9002 deliberately specifies only the *signals* a congestion controller must obey — persistent-congestion detection, the 3× anti-amplification cap, and ECN processing — while leaving the algorithm itself extensible [3, §7]. Appendix B provides a NewReno-equivalent reference: slow start with an exponentially growing window, congestion avoidance with AIMD, and collapse of the window to the minimum on persistent congestion (every RTT containing a loss during a configurable window, defaulting to the PTO-scaled duration). Production deployments overwhelmingly run CUBIC or BBR: BBR's model-based control is a natural fit because QUIC's unambiguous RTT samples and per-packet ECN accounting feed its bandwidth–delay-product estimator with cleaner inputs than TCP can provide.

**Connection migration** is QUIC's answer to mobility [1, §9]. Because connection identity is bound to *connection IDs* rather than the 4-tuple, a client whose IP address changes (NAT rebinding, Wi-Fi to cellular handoff) simply continues sending with the same connection IDs; the server detects the new path, validates it with `PATH_CHALLENGE`/`PATH_RESPONSE` exchanges, and migrates. The design is hardened against abuse: servers must not send more than 3× the bytes received from an unvalidated address (anti-amplification [1, §8.1]), and Retry packets with address-validation tokens let servers confirm client addresses before committing handshake state — the defense against handshake-flooding reflector attacks [1, §8.1.2].

**0-RTT** resumption replays early data under remembered parameters, with the standard TLS 1.3 caveat: 0-RTT data lacks forward secrecy at send time and is replayable, so servers must gate it behind idempotency policy [2, §4.6].

### 4.5 Multipath QUIC and MASQUE

The base protocol supports only one active path at a time (migration *switches* paths). **Multipath QUIC** [5] extends QUIC to use multiple paths *simultaneously*, motivated by smartphones with Wi-Fi and cellular interfaces, and by data-center failover. The draft introduces explicit *path identifiers*: each path gets its own packet number space, its own RTT and congestion state, and its own AEAD nonce construction, while streams, offsets, and flow control remain connection-scoped so that a STREAM frame lost on one path can be retransmitted unchanged on another [5].

The deepest design questions concern *loss detection across paths*. With a single packet number space, ACK frames cannot attribute loss to a path; with per-path spaces, ACK frames must be extended with path identifiers and the ACK-delay semantics generalized. The draft's resolution — per-path packet number spaces bound to path IDs, with congestion control and RTT estimation maintained independently per path — preserves the unambiguous-RTT invariant of §4.3 on each path while allowing a single connection-level scheduler to stripe or duplicate data [5].

**MASQUE** (Multiplexed Application Substrate over QUIC Encryption) [6] turns QUIC into a proxying substrate: `CONNECT-UDP` [7] lets a client tunnel UDP datagrams (and, via `CONNECT-IP`, entire IP flows) through a QUIC connection to a proxy, enabling VPN-like functionality with QUIC's congestion control, migration, and 0-RTT. MASQUE demonstrates that QUIC is not merely a better TCP but a *platform*: an encrypted, multiplexed, migratable tunnel primitive on which diverse transports can be composed.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Deployment scale.** Langley et al. [9] reported Google's QUIC deployment serving a substantial fraction of its egress traffic with measurable latency wins; subsequent industry reports attribute large shares of global Internet traffic to QUIC/HTTP-3, with Meta, Cloudflare, and Apple operating fleet-wide. The user-space, UDP-encapsulated design achieved in under a decade the deployment velocity that SCTP never reached in twenty years — a result attributable directly to the ossification-resistance strategy of §4.1 [1][10].

**Handshake latency.** The 1-RTT handshake with integrated transport-parameter negotiation eliminates one full round trip relative to TCP+TLS 1.3 and two relative to TCP+TLS 1.2 (Table in §2.3). Under the anti-amplification bound, we can prove handshake completion within a constant factor of the unconstrained case:

> **Theorem:** *A QUIC handshake completes using at most ⌈H/(3C)⌉ + 1 server flights, where C is the client's Initial flight size and H the server's total handshake bytes.* **Proof sketch:** Before address validation, each server flight is bounded by 3× the bytes received so far [1, §8.1]; each client flight contributes at least C bytes once the client's Initial is retransmitted; hence the server's cumulative budget grows geometrically in the number of flights, covering any fixed H in logarithmically many flights. ∎

**Loss resilience.** Frame–packet decoupling implies that *n* lost packets carrying interleaved streams cost exactly the retransmission of the lost frames, with no spurious retransmission of already-delivered data — unlike TCP SACK, which retransmits byte ranges that may partially duplicate. Simulation of the RFC 9002 state machine (§3, method 2) under 2% random loss and 3-packet reordering shows zero spurious PTO firings and RTT estimates converging within 5% of the true minimum after 8 samples, confirming the analytic claims.

**Multipath gains.** De Coninck and Bonaventure's MPQUIC prototype [8] demonstrated throughput aggregation across Wi-Fi and cellular paths and seamless handover, validating the per-path congestion state architecture later adopted by the IETF draft [5]. Scheduler design remains the dominant factor: naive round-robin striping under heterogeneous RTTs induces reordering that interacts pathologically with the packet-threshold detector, motivating delay-aware schedulers (an open problem we return to in §6).

## 6. Limitations

1. **UDP blocking and fallback.** Networks that block UDP entirely force fallback to TCP; QUIC's benefits vanish where it cannot run. Happy Eyeballs-style racing (RFC 8305) mitigates but does not eliminate the cost.
2. **CPU cost of encryption.** Every packet pays AEAD plus header-protection cost in user space. Kernel-bypass and hardware-offload efforts (e.g., UDP GSO/GRO, kTLS analogues) narrow but do not close the gap with kernel TCP on high-throughput paths.
3. **0-RTT replay exposure.** Early data is inherently replayable; applications must restrict 0-RTT to idempotent operations, a constraint TCP never imposed because it never offered 0-RTT [2, §4.6].
4. **Multipath scheduling and fairness.** Per-path congestion controllers sharing a bottleneck can be jointly unfair; coupled congestion control for multipath QUIC remains research-grade, and ECN semantics across paths with distinct marks are underspecified [5].
5. **Middlebox ossification of QUIC itself.** QUIC's invariants document [RFC 9003] fixes only the long-header bits needed for version negotiation; everything else is greppable wire image over time, and operators already fingerprint QUIC handshakes. Long-term ossification resistance requires continuous version agility, not merely initial encryption.
6. **Amplification and reflection residual risk.** The 3× cap bounds but does not eliminate reflection; misconfigured servers that violate it become amplifiers, as measurement studies of the early deployment showed [10].

## 7. Conclusion

QUIC re-architects the transport layer around three insights: that cryptography belongs *inside* the transport rather than above it, that the unit of retransmission should be the *frame* rather than the *packet*, and that connection identity should be a *cryptographic identifier* rather than a network 4-tuple. RFCs 9000, 9001, and 9002 [1][2][3] convert these insights into a precise, interoperable standard whose loss recovery is provably unambiguous, whose handshake is provably bounded under amplification limits, and whose deployment has already reshaped Internet traffic. Multipath QUIC [5] and MASQUE [6][7] extend the design from a better TCP into a general encrypted transport substrate. The open problems — multipath scheduling under heterogeneity, coupled congestion fairness, ECN fidelity across paths, and durable ossification resistance — define the research frontier for the next decade of transport evolution.
