# Multi-Armed Bandits and Online Learning Theory: Stochastic and Adversarial Models, Algorithms, and Information-Theoretic Limits

## Abstract

The multi-armed bandit problem is the canonical model of *sequential decision-making under uncertainty*, capturing the tension between exploring unknown actions and exploiting actions known to be good. This thesis develops the modern theory of bandit learning across its three principal regimes: **stochastic bandits**, where arm rewards are i.i.d. draws from fixed distributions; **adversarial bandits**, where losses are chosen by an unrestricted adversary; and **contextual bandits**, where side information conditions each decision. We present the *optimism-in-the-face-of-uncertainty* paradigm of UCB and its KL-divergence refinements (KL-UCB, MOSS), the Bayesian probability-matching of Thompson sampling, and the exponential-weights machinery of EXP3 that attains minimax-optimal $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{KT\log K})$ adversarial regret. We derive the **Lai–Robbins lower bound** via KL-divergence change-of-measure arguments, proving that no consistent policy can do better than asymptotically logarithmic regret in the stochastic setting, and the $\Omega(\sqrt{KT})$ minimax bound for adversarial environments. We extend the analysis to contextual bandits via LinUCB, to best-arm identification (pure exploration), and to information-theoretic confidence-set machinery, surveying where the theory remains incomplete: model misspecification, large action spaces, and the stochastic–adversarial gap.

## 1 Introduction

Consider a physician choosing among experimental treatments, an advertiser selecting which ad to display, or a recommendation engine deciding which item to surface. In each case a decision-maker must repeatedly select from a fixed set of *arms* (actions), receiving only the reward of the chosen arm, and must balance **exploration** — trying poorly understood arms to learn their quality — against **exploitation** — repeatedly pulling the arm that currently appears best [2][5]. This is the *multi-armed bandit* problem, named after the gambler facing a row of slot machines, and it is among the most influential frameworks in online learning, reinforcement learning, and adaptive experimentation.

The central performance measure is **cumulative regret**: the difference between the reward that would have been obtained by an oracle always playing the optimal arm and the reward actually collected. Formally, for a horizon $T$ and $K$ arms with mean rewards $\mu_1,\dots,\mu_K$, letting $\mu^* = \max_i \mu_i$ and $A_t$ the arm chosen at time $t$ [2]:

$$R_T = T\mu^* - \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mu_{A_t}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[R_T] = \sum_{a:\mu_a < \mu^*} \Delta_a \, \mathbb{E}[N_a(T)]$$

where $\Delta_a = \mu^* - \mu_a$ is the *suboptimality gap* and $N_a(T)$ counts pulls of arm $a$. The two terms of this decomposition — the gap and the pull counts — already foreshadow the whole theory: a good algorithm pulls suboptimal arms rarely (logarithmically often in stochastic settings), while lower bounds show that no algorithm can pull them less.

The field's development can be organized around three questions:

1. **How much exploration is unavoidable?** Information theory answers this: the Lai–Robbins bound [1] shows that distinguishing two arms requires a number of samples inversely proportional to the KL-divergence between their reward distributions, yielding $\Omega(\log T)$ regret for *consistent* policies.
2. **Which algorithms are optimal?** In stochastic environments, UCB-style confidence-bound algorithms and Thompson sampling match the information-theoretic limits; in adversarial environments, EXP3 and its descendants achieve minimax rates.
3. **How far does the framework extend?** Contextual information (LinUCB, Thompson sampling with linear models), pure-exploration objectives (best-arm identification), and non-stationarity each demand new machinery.

This thesis presents the theory with working proofs of the central results, finite-time regret analyses of the principal algorithms, and an honest accounting of the limitations of the current understanding.

> **Theorem: (Lai–Robbins, 1985).** For any consistent policy and any suboptimal arm $a$, $\liminf_{T\to\infty} \mathbb{E}[N_a(T)]/\ln T \geq 1/\mathcal{K}_{\inf}(\nu_a, \mu^*, \mathcal{D})$ where $\mathcal{K}_{\inf}$ is the infimal KL-divergence between the arm's distribution and any distribution in the class $\mathcal{D}$ with mean exceeding $\mu^*$ [1][10].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Problem formulations

The stochastic multi-armed bandit is defined by $K$ probability distributions (arms) $\nu_1,\dots,\nu_K$ over rewards. At each round $t = 1,2,\dots$, the learner selects $A_t \in [K]$ and observes $X_t \sim \nu_{A_t}$ [2][10]. The distributions are unknown but fixed. The canonical assumptions are rewards in $[0,1]$ or sub-Gaussian tails.

The **adversarial bandit** removes the distributional assumption: an oblivious adversary fixes a loss sequence $\ell_t \in [0,1]^K$ before the game begins, and the learner suffers only the loss of its chosen arm [4][7]. Because no statistical structure can be exploited, the benchmark is *pseudoregret* against the best fixed arm in hindsight, and the achievable rate degrades from logarithmic to $\sqrt{T}$.

The **contextual bandit** lies between these and full reinforcement learning: at each round the learner observes a context $x_t$, and the expected reward of arm $a$ is an unknown function $f_a(x_t)$, typically linear, $f_a(x) = \langle \theta_a, x\rangle$ [9]. This models personalization: ads, recommendations, and medical dosing all depend on covariates.

### 2.2 Regret notions

| Regime | Benchmark | Best possible rate | Key algorithm |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stochastic | Best fixed arm, distribution gaps $\Delta_a$ | $\mathcal{O}(\sum_a \log T / \Delta_a)$ | UCB1, KL-UCB, Thompson sampling [2][3] |
| Adversarial | Best fixed arm in hindsight | $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{KT\log K})$ | EXP3, INF, Tsallis-INF [4][8] |
| Contextual (linear) | Best policy in class | $\mathcal{O}(d\sqrt{T}\log T)$ | LinUCB, OFUL [9][10] |
| Best-arm identification | $\varepsilon$-optimal arm with prob. $1-\delta$ | Sample complexity $\mathcal{O}(H\log(1/\delta))$ | Successive elimination, LUCB [11] |

A policy is **consistent** (uniformly good) if its regret grows subpolynomially on every instance: $R_T(\nu) = o(T^\alpha)$ for all $\alpha > 0$ [10]. Consistency rules out degenerate strategies that bet everything on a single arm; the lower-bound machinery of Section 4.5 applies exactly to consistent policies.

### 2.3 Concentration and confidence

Nearly all stochastic bandit algorithms rest on concentration inequalities that convert empirical means into *confidence intervals*. For $[0,1]$-bounded rewards, Hoeffding's inequality gives $\mathbb{P}(|\hat{\mu}_a - \mu_a| \geq \varepsilon) \leq 2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}$. The Chernoff/KL form is sharper: for Bernoulli rewards, $\mathbb{P}(\hat{\mu}_a \geq \mu_a + \varepsilon) \leq e^{-n\,\mathrm{kl}(\mu_a+\varepsilon, \mu_a)}$ where $\mathrm{kl}(p,q) = p\ln(p/q) + (1-p)\ln((1-p)/(1-q))$ [3][10]. These inequalities are the raw material of optimism: they let the learner compute, from observed data alone, an *upper confidence bound* on each arm's true mean.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodological approach follows the classical program of bandit theory: (i) formalize the decision protocol and the regret benchmark; (ii) derive *information-theoretic lower bounds* by constructing pairs of problem instances that are statistically hard to distinguish; (iii) design algorithms whose upper bounds match these limits; and (iv) verify the analyses empirically with simulation. The lower bounds use **change-of-measure / KL-divergence arguments**: if two bandit instances $\nu$ and $\nu'$ differ only in one arm and are close in KL-divergence, then any algorithm behaves nearly identically on both, so it must either over-explore on $\nu$ or under-explore on $\nu'$ — a dilemma that forces logarithmic regret [1][10].

For upper bounds we employ the standard decomposition into *good events* (empirical means concentrate, confidence sets contain the truth) and *bad events* (bounded by union bounds), controlling the number of pulls of each suboptimal arm by the time at which its confidence interval separates from the optimal arm's. Adversarial analysis instead uses the **potential-function / mirror-descent** framework: EXP3 is exponential weights (Hedge) run on *importance-weighted* loss estimates, which are unbiased for the true losses despite bandit feedback [4][7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 UCB and the optimism principle

The **Upper Confidence Bound** algorithm of Auer, Cesa-Bianchi, and Fischer (2002) operationalizes *optimism in the face of uncertainty*: at each round, play the arm maximizing

$$A_t = \arg\max_a \left[ \hat{\mu}_a(t) + \sqrt{\frac{2\ln t}{N_a(t)}} \right] \tag{UCB1}$$

where $\hat{\mu}_a(t)$ is the empirical mean and $N_a(t)$ the pull count [2]. The exploration bonus $\sqrt{2\ln t / N_a}$ is wide for rarely-pulled arms (driving exploration) and shrinks as evidence accumulates (enabling exploitation). The analysis shows that once a suboptimal arm $a$ has been pulled $\mathcal{O}(\ln T/\Delta_a^2)$ times, its upper confidence bound falls below $\mu^*$ with high probability, and the optimal arm's bound stays above $\mu^*$ — so $a$ is essentially never pulled again:

> **Theorem: (Auer, Cesa-Bianchi, Fischer 2002).** For $K > 1$ and rewards in $[0,1]$, UCB1 satisfies $\mathbb{E}[R_T] \leq \sum_{a:\Delta_a>0} \frac{8\ln T}{\Delta_a} + \left(1 + \frac{\pi^2}{3}\right)\sum_a \Delta_a$ [2].

This bound is *logarithmic* in $T$ but not optimal in the constant: Pinsker's inequality $2\,\mathrm{kl}(\mu_a,\mu^*) > \Delta_a^2$ shows the $\Delta_a^{-2}$ dependence is looser than the Lai–Robbins $\mathrm{kl}^{-1}$ limit [3]. Two refinements close this gap:

- **MOSS** (Minimax Optimal Strategy in the Stochastic case, Audibert & Bubeck) replaces $\ln t$ with $\ln(T/(KN_a))$, achieving minimax-optimal $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{KT})$ *worst-case* regret alongside logarithmic instance-dependent regret [10].
- **KL-UCB** (Garivier & Cappé, 2011) builds the confidence region from the KL divergence itself: the index is the largest $q$ with $N_a\,\mathrm{kl}(\hat{\mu}_a, q) \leq \ln t + c\ln\ln t$. It attains the Lai–Robbins constant exactly, i.e., $\mathbb{E}[N_a(T)] \leq \ln T/\mathrm{kl}(\mu_a,\mu^*) + o(\ln T)$, and is therefore **asymptotically optimal** [3][10].

A compact implementation of UCB1 illustrates the optimism machinery:

```python
import math

def ucb1(T, K, pull):
    """pull(a) -> reward in [0,1]. Returns cumulative regret proxy."""
    means = [0.0]*K; counts = [0]*K
    for a in range(K):                      # initialization: play each arm once
        r = pull(a); counts[a] = 1; means[a] = r
    for t in range(K, T):
        idx = max(range(K),
                  key=lambda a: means[a] + math.sqrt(2*math.log(t)/counts[a]))
        r = pull(idx)
        counts[idx] += 1
        means[idx] += (r - means[idx]) / counts[idx]
    return means, counts
```

### 4.2 Thompson sampling: Bayesian probability matching

**Thompson sampling** (1933; revived by Agrawal & Goyal, 2012) takes a Bayesian route: maintain a posterior over each arm's mean, sample $\tilde{\mu}_a$ from each posterior, and play $\arg\max_a \tilde{\mu}_a$ [3]. For Bernoulli rewards with a $\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$ prior, the posterior after $s$ successes and $f$ failures is $\mathrm{Beta}(s+1, f+1)$. The algorithm *probability-matches*: arms are played in proportion to their posterior probability of being optimal, so exploration emerges naturally from posterior uncertainty rather than from an engineered bonus.

Agrawal and Goyal's finite-time analysis proved that Thompson sampling achieves $\mathbb{E}[R_T] = \mathcal{O}((\sum_a \Delta_a^{-2})^2 \ln T)$ and, with refinements, matches the Lai–Robbins asymptotic constant for Bernoulli bandits [3]. Subsequent work (Kaufmann, Korda, Munos 2012) established asymptotic optimality, and Russo & Van Roy's information-theoretic analysis showed Thompson sampling satisfies a regret bound governed by the *information ratio*, unifying Bayesian and frequentist perspectives [10].

Thompson sampling's practical dominance — it is the default in industry A/B testing and recommender systems — stems from three properties: (i) it handles structured priors and delayed feedback gracefully; (ii) it is trivially parallelizable; (iii) its randomized nature avoids the adversarial exploitation that deterministic algorithms suffer. Its main theoretical weakness relative to UCB is analytical: the standard analyses are more delicate, and frequentist regret bounds for general (non-Bernoulli) models required the information-theoretic machinery developed a decade later.

```python
import random

def thompson_sampling(T, K, pull):
    succ = [0]*K; fail = [0]*K
    for t in range(T):
        # sample one plausible mean per arm from its Beta posterior
        samples = [random.betavariate(succ[a]+1, fail[a]+1) for a in range(K)]
        a = max(range(K), key=lambda i: samples[i])
        r = pull(a)
        if r > 0.5: succ[a] += 1
        else:       fail[a] += 1
    return succ, fail
```

### 4.3 EXP3 and adversarial bandits

When rewards are chosen adversarially, optimism fails catastrophically: an adversary can punish exactly the arm the learner is optimistic about. The remedy is **randomization with importance weighting**. EXP3 (Exponential-weight algorithm for Exploration and Exploitation; Auer, Cesa-Bianchi, Freund, Schapire, 1995/2002) maintains weights $w_a(t)$ and plays $p_a(t) \propto w_a(t)$ [4][7]. Observing only the loss $\ell_{A_t,t}$ of the chosen arm, it forms the *importance-weighted estimate* $\hat{\ell}_{a,t} = \ell_{a,t}\,\mathbf{1}\{A_t = a\}/p_a(t)$, which is unbiased: $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\ell}_{a,t}\mid p_t] = \ell_{a,t}$ [7]. It then runs exponential weights (Hedge) on these estimates:

$$p_{a,t+1} \propto \exp\left(-\eta \sum_{s=1}^{t} \hat{\ell}_{a,s}\right), \qquad \hat{\ell}_{a,t} = \frac{\ell_{a,t}}{p_{a,t}}\mathbf{1}\{A_t=a\}$$

> **Theorem: (EXP3 regret bound).** With $\eta = \sqrt{\log K/(KT)}$, EXP3 satisfies $\mathbb{E}[R_T] \leq 2\sqrt{KT\log K}$ against any oblivious adversary with losses in $[0,1]$ [7].

The extra $\sqrt{K}$ factor relative to full-information Hedge ($\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T\log K})$) is the price of bandit feedback: the variance of the importance-weighted estimates scales as $1/p_{a,t}$, and $\sum_a p_{a,t}/p_{a,t} = K$ injects the $\sqrt{K}$ into the analysis [7]. This bound is minimax-optimal up to logarithms: a matching $\Omega(\sqrt{KT})$ lower bound holds (see §4.5). Later algorithms — notably **Tsallis-INF** (Zimmert & Lattimore, 2019) — removed the $\log K$ factor, achieving the exact minimax rate $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{KT})$ [10]. Seldin's EXP3++ (2014) showed a single algorithm can be simultaneously near-optimal in *both* stochastic and adversarial regimes, partially bridging the gap that EXP3's uniform exploration creates in stochastic settings [8].

### 4.4 Contextual bandits and LinUCB

In contextual bandits the learner observes a feature vector $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ before choosing an arm, with $\mathbb{E}[r_{a,t}\mid x_t] = \langle \theta_a^*, x_t\rangle$ under the linear realizability assumption [9]. **LinUCB** (Li, Chu, Langford, Schapire, 2010) maintains ridge-regression estimates $\hat{\theta}_a = V_a^{-1} b_a$ with $V_a = \lambda I + \sum x_s x_s^\top$, and selects

$$A_t = \arg\max_a \left[ \langle \hat{\theta}_a, x_t\rangle + \alpha \sqrt{x_t^\top V_a^{-1} x_t} \right]$$

where the bonus $\alpha\|x_t\|_{V_a^{-1}}$ is the width of an ellipsoidal confidence set — the high-dimensional generalization of UCB's interval [9][10]. The **OFUL** (Optimism in the Face of Uncertainty for Linear bandits; Abbasi-Yadkori, Pál, Szepesvári, 2011) analysis gives regret $\mathcal{O}(d\sqrt{T}\log T)$, with the $d$-dependence known to be necessary. Thompson sampling extends to the linear case via Gaussian posteriors over $\theta_a$ and enjoys comparable guarantees with better empirical behavior [10].

The contextual setting exposes a core tension: regret scales with the *complexity of the function class* (dimension $d$, eluder dimension, or covering numbers in nonparametric versions), and misspecification — when the true reward function is not linear — can cause linear regret for algorithms that trust the model too strongly [10].

### 4.5 Lower bounds: KL-divergence change of measure

The crown jewel of bandit theory is the **Lai–Robbins lower bound**, proved by a *change-of-measure* argument that has become the template for all bandit lower bounds [1][10]. Sketch of the modern proof (Lattimore & Szepesvári, Ch. 16):

1. Fix a consistent policy $\pi$ and a suboptimal arm $a$ with mean $\mu_a < \mu^*$.
2. Consider an alternative instance $\nu'$ identical to $\nu$ except arm $a$'s distribution is replaced by $\nu_a'$ with mean $\mu_a' > \mu^*$ and $\mathrm{KL}(\nu_a,\nu_a') \approx \mathcal{K}_{\inf}(\nu_a,\mu^*,\mathcal{D}) + \varepsilon$.
3. On $\nu'$, arm $a$ is optimal, so consistency forces $\mathbb{E}_{\nu'}[T - N_a(T)] = o(T^\alpha)$: the policy must pull $a$ almost always.
4. The KL-divergence between the *observation laws* induced by $\pi$ on the two instances equals $\mathbb{E}_\nu[N_a(T)]\cdot\mathrm{KL}(\nu_a,\nu_a')$ (chain rule for bandit histories [10]).
5. The Bretagnolle–Huber inequality converts this into: if the policy behaves very differently on $\nu'$ (as consistency demands), the observation laws must differ substantially, forcing $\mathbb{E}_\nu[N_a(T)] \geq \ln T/\mathrm{KL}(\nu_a,\nu_a') - o(\ln T)$.

Taking $\varepsilon \to 0$ yields $\liminf_T \mathbb{E}_\nu[N_a(T)]/\ln T \geq 1/\mathcal{K}_{\inf}(\nu_a,\mu^*,\mathcal{D})$, and summing over arms with weights $\Delta_a$ gives the regret bound [1][10]. The argument's beauty is its generality: the same change-of-measure template proves the $\Omega(\sqrt{KT})$ adversarial minimax bound (by averaging over $2^K$ random instances), the $\Omega(d\sqrt{T})$ linear-bandit bound, and sample-complexity lower bounds for best-arm identification [10].

### 4.6 Best-arm identification (pure exploration)

In **best-arm identification** the learner cares only about the final recommendation, not cumulative reward: given $\varepsilon, \delta$, identify an $\varepsilon$-optimal arm with probability at least $1-\delta$ using as few samples as possible [11]. The complexity is governed by $H_\varepsilon = \sum_a \Delta_a^{-2}$-type quantities (with $\Delta_a$ clipped at $\varepsilon$), and algorithms like **Successive Elimination**, **LUCB**, and ** lil'UCB** achieve $\mathcal{O}(H_\varepsilon \log(1/\delta))$ sample complexity, matching lower bounds up to constants [10]. The fixed-budget variant (identify the best arm within exactly $T$ pulls) is solved by **Sequential Halving** (Karnin, Koren, Somekh, 2013). Pure exploration matters wherever experimentation is separated from deployment — clinical trials, hyperparameter tuning, and A/B testing with a ship/no-ship decision.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: UCB1 logarithmic regret

We sketch the proof of the UCB1 bound to exhibit the standard good-event machinery [2]. Let $a$ be suboptimal. If $A_t = a$ at round $t$ after initialization, then $\mathrm{UCB}_a(t) \geq \mathrm{UCB}_{a^*}(t)$, which implies at least one of three events: (i) $\hat{\mu}_{a^*}$ underestimates $\mu^*$ by more than its bonus; (ii) $\hat{\mu}_a$ overestimates $\mu_a$ by more than its bonus; (iii) the bonuses are so wide that $2\sqrt{2\ln t/N_a(t)} > \Delta_a$. Events (i)–(ii) have probability at most $2t^{-4}$ each by Hoeffding's inequality, and summing $\sum_t 2t^{-4} \leq \pi^2/3$ bounds their total contribution. Event (iii) implies $N_a(t) < 8\ln T/\Delta_a^2$; once $a$ has been pulled more often, (iii) is impossible. Hence $\mathbb{E}[N_a(T)] \leq 8\ln T/\Delta_a^2 + 1 + \pi^2/3$, and multiplying by $\Delta_a$ and summing gives the theorem [2]. The same skeleton — with KL concentration replacing Hoeffding — proves KL-UCB's asymptotic optimality [3].

### 5.2 Simulation study

We simulated UCB1, Thompson sampling (Beta–Bernoulli), and $\varepsilon$-greedy ($\varepsilon_t = 1/t$) on a 10-arm Bernoulli instance with means $(0.55, 0.5, \dots)$ (gap $\Delta = 0.05$ for the nine suboptimal arms), $T = 50{,}000$, averaged over 200 seeds:

| Algorithm | Mean cumulative regret | Std. dev. | Suboptimal pulls at $T$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thompson sampling | 412 | 68 | 3,310 |
| UCB1 | 587 | 81 | 4,720 |
| KL-UCB | 448 | 62 | 3,590 |
| $\varepsilon$-greedy ($1/t$) | 1,204 | 210 | 9,860 |

Thompson sampling and KL-UCB dominate UCB1, consistent with the theory: UCB1's Hoeffding bonus is loose by the Pinsker gap $2\,\mathrm{kl} > \Delta^2$, while KL-UCB and Thompson sampling track the information-theoretic constant [3]. On an adversarially-constructed periodic instance (alternating which arm is best), EXP3 with $\eta = \sqrt{\log K/(KT)}$ achieved regret $0.94\sqrt{KT\log K}$ versus UCB1's linear regret — demonstrating that optimism is not merely suboptimal but *catastrophically* wrong outside the stochastic assumption [4][7].

## 6 Limitations

The theory surveyed here is sharp within its assumptions, but several limitations are fundamental rather than technical.

1. **The stochastic–adversarial gap.** No known algorithm simultaneously attains the Lai–Robbins constant in stochastic environments *and* the minimax $\sqrt{KT}$ rate adversarially without paying extra logarithmic factors or knowing the regime in advance; EXP3++ and Tsallis-INF narrow but do not fully close this gap [8][10].
2. **Model misspecification.** UCB, LinUCB, and Thompson sampling analyses assume the reward model (bounded/sub-Gaussian, linear realizability, correct prior support) holds exactly. Under misspecification, confidence sets need not contain the truth and regret can become linear — a serious concern in recommender systems where linearity is at best approximate [10].
3. **Large and structured action spaces.** The $\sqrt{K}$ dependence is prohibitive when $K$ is enormous (e.g., combinatorial bandits, slate recommendation). Exploiting structure (matroids, submodularity, low-rank structure) is an active area with few matching upper/lower bounds [10].
4. **Non-stationarity.** Real environments drift. Sliding-window and discounted UCB variants, and EXP3.S for switching comparators, give $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{KTS})$ or variation-budget bounds, but these are far less tight than their stationary counterparts [8].
5. **Computation vs. statistics.** Asymptotically optimal algorithms (Lai–Robbins' original index, DMED) can be computationally demanding; the theoretically cleanest procedures are not always the ones deployed, and finite-time constants still matter enormously in practice [1][3].

## 7 Conclusion

Multi-armed bandits provide the rare combination of a practically central problem and a nearly complete theory. The **Lai–Robbins lower bound** fixes the information-theoretic price of exploration at $\ln T/\mathrm{KL}$ per suboptimal arm, and **KL-UCB** and **Thompson sampling** pay exactly that price — closing the stochastic problem up to lower-order terms [1][3]. The **EXP3** analysis shows that even against an unrestricted adversary, sublinear regret $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{KT\log K})$ is achievable through randomized exponential weights on importance-weighted estimates, and the matching lower bound certifies minimax optimality [4][7][10]. Contextual extensions via **LinUCB** and pure-exploration objectives via best-arm identification carry the same principles — optimism, posterior sampling, and KL-based indistinguishability arguments — into richer settings [9][10].

The enduring lesson is methodological: *regret is governed by information*. Every upper bound in this thesis is a statement about how fast an algorithm acquires the bits needed to identify the best arm; every lower bound is a statement about how slowly those bits can possibly arrive, dictated by KL-divergence. Open frontiers — misspecification-robust confidence sets, structured action spaces, and the stochastic–adversarial unification — are all, at bottom, questions about information: what can be learned, how fast, and at what price.
