# The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer Conjecture at Analytic Rank Zero and One: Heegner Points, Kolyvagin Systems, and the Gross–Zagier Formula

## Abstract

The Birch and Swinnerton–Dyer conjecture predicts that the order of vanishing of the Hasse–Weil L-function L(E, s) of an elliptic curve E over Q at s = 1 equals the rank of the Mordell–Weil group E(Q), with a refined formula for the leading coefficient involving the regulator, real and Tamagawa periods, torsion, and the order of the Tate–Shafarevich group. This article gives a systematic account of the only substantial unconditional progress toward the conjecture: the theorem of Gross, Zagier, and Kolyvagin establishing the rank part and the finiteness of the Tate–Shafarevich group whenever the analytic rank is at most one. We develop the three pillars of the proof: the modular parametrization of E and the construction of Heegner points via complex multiplication; the Gross–Zagier formula identifying the first derivative of the L-function with the Neron–Tate height of a Heegner point; and Kolyvagin's Euler-system machinery, which converts the nontriviality of that point into a bound on the Selmer group. We survey the Gross–Kohnen–Zagier, Yuan–Zhang–Zhang, and p-converse refinements, and delimit where the argument stops.

##1 Introduction

Let E be an elliptic curve over Q. The set of rational points E(Q) is a finitely generated abelian group, by the theorem of Mordell; its *algebraic rank* r_alg(E) = rank_Z E(Q) is one of the most fundamental and most elusive invariants in arithmetic geometry. On the analytic side, the Hasse–Weil L-function L(E, s), defined initially by the Euler product over primes p of good and bad reduction, extends to an entire function on the complex plane — a consequence of the modularity theorem proved by Wiles, Taylor, and others, which identifies L(E, s) with the L-function of a weight-two newform f of level N equal to the conductor of E [3]. The *analytic rank* r_an(E) = ord_{s=1} L(E, s) is forced by the functional equation relating s to 2 − s, with sign ε(E) = ±1 the root number, to satisfy the parity condition r_an(E) ≡ (1 − ε(E))/2 modulo 2.

The conjecture of Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer [4] asserts two things. First, the *rank part*: r_alg(E) = r_an(E). Second, the *refined formula*: the leading Taylor coefficient L^{(r)}(E, 1)/r! equals

$$
\frac{\Omega_E \cdot R(E) \cdot \prod_\ell c_\ell(E) \cdot |\text{Ш}(E/\mathbf{Q})|}{|E(\mathbf{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}|^2},
$$

where Ω_E is the real period, R(E) the Neron–Tate regulator, c_ℓ(E) the Tamagawa numbers, and Ш(E/Q) the Tate–Shafarevich group measuring the failure of the local-to-global principle.

> Theorem: (Gross–Zagier, Kolyvagin). If r_an(E) ≤ 1, then r_alg(E) = r_an(E) and Ш(E/Q) is finite.

This theorem is the centerpiece of the present article. It is, to date, the *only* general theorem of comparable strength toward BSD over Q. Its proof fuses three deep theories: the modular construction of Heegner points on E, the Gross–Zagier formula relating L-derivatives to heights, and Kolyvagin's Euler-system descent controlling Selmer groups. Our aim is to give a self-contained, graduate-level exposition of this machinery, to state the refined and generalized forms of each ingredient, and to explain the analytic and arithmetic frontiers that remain.

##2 Background

### Elliptic curves, Selmer groups, and the Tate–Shafarevich group

Let E/Q be given by a global minimal Weierstrass model. The Mordell–Weil theorem gives E(Q) ≅ Z^{r} ⊕ T with T finite. The *p-primary Selmer group* Sel_{p^∞}(E/Q) sits in the fundamental exact sequence

$$
0 \to E(\mathbf{Q}) \otimes \mathbf{Q}_p/\mathbf{Z}_p \to \mathrm{Sel}_{p^\infty}(E/\mathbf{Q}) \to \text{Ш}(E/\mathbf{Q})[p^\infty] \to 0,
$$

so the Z_p-corank of Sel_{p^∞}(E/Q) simultaneously encodes the Mordell–Weil rank and the p-part of Ш [5]. A bound on Selmer groups is therefore a bound on both arithmetic invariants at once. The *corank* is the number of copies of Q_p/Z_p in the group; controlling it from above is the province of Euler systems.

The analytic side is governed by L(E, s). Modularity implies that L(E, 1)/Ω_E^+ is rational when the central value does not vanish, and that the functional equation has sign ε(E) = ±1 [1]. The parity conjecture — that r_alg and r_an have the same parity — follows from ε(E) = (−1)^{r_an} and is known in wide generality via the theory of Selmer groups and root numbers, but the full equality of ranks is the content of BSD.

### Modularity and the f-isotypic quotient

By modularity there is a non-constant morphism Φ: X_0(N) → E, the *modular parametrization*, where X_0(N) is the modular curve of level N parametrizing elliptic curves with a cyclic N-isogeny. The Jacobian J_0(N) decomposes up to isogeny into factors attached to Galois orbits of newforms; the factor attached to the newform f corresponding to E is isogenous over Q to E itself. Pulling back points of X_0(N) with special arithmetic meaning — the CM points — yields rational or quadratic points on E. This is the origin of Heegner points.

##3 Methodology

The proof strategy of the rank ≤ 1 theorem proceeds in three stages, each involving a distinct idea.

1. **Construction.** Choose an imaginary quadratic field K satisfying the *Heegner hypothesis*: every prime dividing the conductor N splits in K. From the CM points on X_0(N) attached to K one builds, via the modular parametrization Φ, a point P_K ∈ E(K), the *Heegner point* [1]. Its trace down to Q yields information about E(Q).

2. **Analytic identification.** The Gross–Zagier formula computes the Neron–Tate height ĥ(P_K) in terms of the derivative L'(E/K, 1) of the L-function over K. In particular, when r_an(E) = 1, the Heegner point is provably of infinite order [1]. This converts a statement about L-values into a geometric statement about points.

3. **Euler-system descent.** Kolyvagin uses the full tower of Heegner points over ring class fields of K — not merely P_K — to produce a *system of cohomology classes* {κ_n} in H^1(K, E[p^n]) satisfying norm-compatibility relations. A combinatorial device, the *Kolyvagin derivative operators* D_n, extracts from these a coherent family of classes whose local behavior forces the Selmer group to be small: if P_K has infinite order, then E(K) has rank exactly one and Ш(E/K) is finite [6].

To transfer results from E/K back to E/Q one needs an auxiliary field K such that L(E^K, 1) ≠ 0, where E^K is the quadratic twist; this is supplied by deep *nonvanishing* theorems in analytic number theory [3]. The Gross–Kohnen–Zagier refinement [1] and its generalizations by Yuan, Zhang, and Zhang to Shimura curves [2] extend the method beyond the Heegner hypothesis.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Gross–Zagier formula

Let K be an imaginary quadratic field satisfying the Heegner hypothesis relative to N, and let P_K ∈ E(K) be the Heegner point. The Neron–Tate canonical height ĥ: E(K̄) → R_{≥0} is a positive-definite quadratic form modulo torsion. Gross and Zagier proved [1]:

> Theorem: (Gross–Zagier). Up to an explicit nonzero constant depending only on E and K,
> $$
> L'(E/K, 1) = c(E, K) \cdot \hat{h}(P_K),
> $$
> where the constant c(E,K) involves the degree of the modular parametrization Φ, the Petersson norm of the newform f, and local factors at primes dividing N.

The precise normalized statement identifies ĥ(P_K) with the first derivative of the *Rankin–Selberg* L-function L(f/K, s) at s = 1. The proof is a tour de force of arithmetic geometry: both sides are decomposed into sums of local contributions indexed by places of K, and the two sides are shown to agree place by place. The archimedean contribution involves the Green's function on X_0(N)(C) evaluated at Heegner points; the non-archimedean contributions involve intersection theory on integral models of X_0(N), quaternion algebras, and representation numbers of ternary quadratic forms [1].

The decisive corollary is immediate: **if L'(E/K, 1) ≠ 0 then P_K is of infinite order.** When r_an(E) = 1, one arranges (via the choice of K and a nonvanishing result for the twist) that ord_{s=1} L(E/K, s) = 1, so that L'(E/K,1) ≠ 0, and the Heegner point is nontorsion. This is the bridge from analysis to geometry.

### 4.2 Modular parametrization and the construction of Heegner points

Let H be the complex upper half-plane and Γ_0(N) the usual congruence subgroup. The modular curve X_0(N) = Γ_0(N)\H^* classifies pairs (E, C) with C ⊂ E a cyclic subgroup of order N. A point τ ∈ H is a *CM point* if the lattice Zτ + Z has complex multiplication by an order O in an imaginary quadratic field K; equivalently, τ satisfies a quadratic equation aτ² + bτ + c = 0 with discriminant D = b² − 4ac < 0. The Heegner hypothesis — every prime divisor of N splits in K — guarantees that such CM points descend to *Heegner points* x ∈ X_0(N)(H), where H is the Hilbert class field of K (or a ring class field for non-maximal orders) [1].

The modular parametrization Φ: X_0(N) → E (normalized so Φ(∞) = O) sends a Heegner point x to a point Φ(x) ∈ E(H). Summing over the Galois orbit yields the Heegner point

$$
P_K = \sum_{\sigma \in \mathrm{Gal}(H/K)} \Phi(x)^\sigma \in E(K),
$$

well defined up to torsion and the sign conventions. Varying the order O over orders of K of conductor prime to N produces the infinite *tower* of Heegner points over the ring class fields K[c], satisfying precise norm-compatibility relations: the norm from K[cℓ] to K[c] of the Heegner point at level cℓ equals (a_ℓ − σ_ℓ − σ_ℓ^{−1}) applied to the point at level c, where a_ℓ is the Hecke eigenvalue and σ_ℓ the Frobenius at ℓ [6]. These relations are the seed of the Euler system.

A crucial subtlety: the Heegner hypothesis fails for many pairs (E, K). The generalization of Yuan, Zhang, and Zhang replaces X_0(N) by a *Shimura curve* attached to an indefinite quaternion algebra over Q ramified at the primes dividing N that are inert in K, recovering a Gross–Zagier formula in complete generality [2]. This removes the hypothesis and underlies modern applications such as Tian's work on the congruent number problem [2].

### 4.3 Kolyvagin's Euler system and the derivative classes

Let p be a prime and T = T_p E the p-adic Tate module. For each squarefree integer n composed of primes ℓ inert in K with ℓ ≡ 1 modulo p (the *Kolyvagin primes*), the norm relations among Heegner points produce a cohomology class

$$
\kappa_n \in H^1(K, E[p^{m(n)}])
$$

via the Kummer map applied to a *derived* Heegner point D_n P_{K[n]}, where D_n = ∏_{ℓ|n} D_ℓ is Kolyvagin's derivative operator,

$$
D_\ell = \sum_{i=1}^{\ell} i\, \sigma_\ell^i \in \mathbf{Z}[\mathrm{Gal}(K[\ell]/K)],
$$

with σ_ℓ a chosen generator of the cyclic Galois group Gal(K[ℓ]/K) of order ℓ + 1 [6]. The operator D_ℓ is designed so that D_ℓ P_{K[ℓ]} is fixed modulo p by Gal(K[ℓ]/K) precisely because of the norm relation; the resulting classes κ_n satisfy the *Euler-system relations*: their restrictions and corestrictions at primes dividing n are controlled by the Frobenius eigenvalues a_ℓ.

> Theorem: (Kolyvagin). If the Heegner point P_K is of infinite order, then E(K) has rank one and Ш(E/K) is finite.

The proof is a descent argument of striking originality. Suppose a class s ∈ Sel_{p^m}(E/K) is given. Choosing Kolyvagin primes ℓ with prescribed Frobenius action (possible by the Chebotarev density theorem applied to the division fields), one shows that the localization of s at ℓ is constrained by the class κ_ℓ; a global reciprocity (product formula) argument then forces s to be divisible by p, and iterating bounds the Selmer group. Concretely, Kolyvagin proves an explicit bound: the p-part of Ш(E/K) divides a power of the index [E(K) : Z·P_K] up to controlled local factors [6]. Hence nontorsion P_K implies rank exactly one and finite Ш. The classes κ_n are the prototype of the general theory of *Euler systems* and *Kolyvagin systems* later axiomatized by Mazur, Rubin, and others.

![Figure 3: Kolyvagin derivative system of cohomology classes](ths_1789518745065_20281-2.webp)

The mechanism can be summarized computationally as follows. In practice, for a curve of analytic rank one, one computes the Heegner point numerically to high precision (via complex uniformization and the modular parametrization), recognizes it as an algebraic point, and verifies nontorsion; Kolyvagin's theorem then *certifies* rank one and finite Ш rigorously [3]. The following schematic illustrates the logical flow in a functional style:

```haskell
-- Kolyvagin descent: from a nontorsion Heegner point to a Selmer bound
data EulerSystem = ES { basePoint  :: HeegnerPoint
                      , tower      :: [RingClassField]
                      , primes     :: [KolyvaginPrime] }

derivativeClass :: EulerSystem -> Integer -> CohomologyClass
derivativeClass es n =
  kummer (foldr applyD (heegnerPoint es n) (primeDivisors n))
  where applyD l pt = sum [ i * frobenius l ^ i $ pt | i <- [1..l] ]

selmerBound :: EulerSystem -> Integer -> Maybe Integer
selmerBound es p
  | isTorsion (basePoint es) = Nothing
  | otherwise = Just (pAdicValuation (indexOfHeegnerPoint es) p)
```

### 4.4 The rank zero and rank one theorems over Q

To descend from K to Q, write L(E/K, s) = L(E, s)·L(E^K, s), where E^K is the quadratic twist. Two cases:

- **Analytic rank zero.** Choose K with L(E^K, 1) ≠ 0 (possible by nonvanishing results of Bump–Friedberg–Hoffstein and others [3]). Then L(E/K, 1) ≠ 0, so by a theorem of Coates–Wiles (for CM curves) and its extension via Kato's Euler system of Siegel units [3], E(K) is finite and Ш(E/K) is finite; restricting to Q gives r_alg(E) = 0 and finite Ш(E/Q). Kato's independent Euler system gives a second proof in the rank-zero case [3].

- **Analytic rank one.** Choose K with ε(E^K) = +1 and L(E^K, 1) ≠ 0; then ord_{s=1} L(E/K, s) = 1. The Gross–Zagier formula gives P_K nontorsion; Kolyvagin's theorem gives rank_Z E(K) = 1 and finite Ш(E/K). A parity/descent argument using the action of Gal(K/Q) shows that the rank-one subgroup descends: r_alg(E) = 1 and Ш(E/Q) is finite [3].

The *converse* direction — that algebraic rank ≤ 1 forces analytic rank ≤ 1 — is the subject of the recent *p-converse* theorems. Skinner and Zhang, and subsequently many others, proved that if the p^∞-Selmer corank is r ≤ 1 (under hypotheses on the residual representation and reduction type), then ord_{s=1} L(E, s) = r [5]. The proofs go through Iwasawa theory: the Heegner-point main conjecture (proved via the Euler system) controls the Selmer side, and an explicit reciprocity law for Heegner points connects it to the p-adic L-function, whence to the complex L-value. The rank-zero p-converse for CM curves at arbitrary p was established recently, yielding the first instances of the even-parity Goldfeld conjecture [7].

##5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

The rank ≤ 1 theorem is not merely abstract: it is the engine behind essentially every *verified* instance of BSD. Large-scale computations — the Stein–Watkins database of curves of small conductor, Cremona's tables, and the LMFDB — test the refined BSD formula numerically for millions of curves; for curves of analytic rank at most one, the theorem guarantees that the computed Mordell–Weil rank and the analytic order of Ш are the true values, so the numerical verification of the refined formula is a genuine verification of BSD [8]. A recent landmark makes this completely explicit: for every elliptic curve E/Q of analytic rank at most one, the full BSD formula — including the exact p-part for *every* prime p — is now known, the p-primary part following from the Gross–Zagier–Kolyvagin–Kohnen–Zagier input together with the rationality of L(E,1)/Ω and the nonnegativity of the central value [8].

| Curve (Cremona) | Analytic rank | Heegner field K | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| 11a1 | 0 | Q(√−7) | E(Q) finite, Ш trivial |
| 37a1 | 1 | Q(√−11) | rank 1, Ш trivial |
| 389a1 | 2 | — | outside theorem; rank verified conditionally |
| 5077a1 | 3 | — | minimal rank-3 curve; BSD open |

A numerical sanity check of the analytic input can be performed directly from the Dirichlet series. The partial sums of L(E, s) at s = 1 converge conditionally (accelerated in practice by the functional equation), and the vanishing order is detected by the rate of decay:

```python
# Detecting analytic rank numerically from the Dirichlet series of E
# a_p: Frobenius traces; L(E,s) = prod_p (1 - a_p p^-s + p^(1-2s))^-1
def L_partial(a, s, N):
    from cmath import exp, log
    total = 0j
    for n in range(1, N + 1):
        total += a[n] / (n ** s)
    return total

def analytic_rank_estimate(E, N=200000):
    # functional-equation-accelerated evaluation at s = 1 (Dokchitser)
    L1 = dokchitser_L(E, 1, N)          # ~ 0 if rank >= 1
    dL1 = dokchitser_L_deriv(E, 1, N)   # ~ 0 iff rank >= 2
    if abs(L1) > 1e-6:
        return 0
    if abs(dL1) > 1e-6:
        return 1  # Gross-Zagier + Kolyvagin now certify BSD
    return ">=2 (open)"
```

Whenever this computation returns 0 or 1, the Gross–Zagier–Kolyvagin theorem converts the numerical evidence into a *proof* of the rank part of BSD and the finiteness of Ш [3].

##6 Limitations

The method stops — hard — at analytic rank two. Every step of the argument exploits the vanishing order being at most one:

- The Gross–Zagier formula computes the *first* derivative. For ord_{s=1} L(E/K, s) ≥ 2, the Heegner point P_K is torsion (its height vanishes), and the formula gives no information. Higher derivatives are conjecturally related to heights of *higher Heegner cycles* (Zhang's generalization to Kuga–Sato varieties [2]), but no Kolyvagin-type descent is known there.
- Kolyvagin's bound requires a nontorsion *point*. In rank two and above, the Mordell–Weil group is generated by points invisible to the Heegner construction; the Euler system built from Heegner points cannot see them.
- The refined BSD formula at rank ≤ 1 is known only up to a rational factor at finitely many primes in some treatments; the complete p-part result [8] is very recent and still rests on the rank ≤ 1 input.
- The choice of auxiliary field K relies on nonvanishing theorems that are deep but available; extending the method to totally real fields required Zhang's generalization of both the formula and the Euler system [3].
- Ш(E/Q) is proved *finite*, not computed: the theorem gives finiteness but the exact order comes from the refined formula, which at higher rank is wide open.
- Function-field analogues are better understood (finiteness of Ш is equivalent to BSD on rank there, via the Tate conjecture for elliptic surfaces [3]), but the number-field case of rank ≥ 2 has no unconditional theorem of any kind.

In short: the Gross–Zagier–Kolyvagin theorem settles BSD for a density-one-like (conjecturally 100%, by Goldfeld's conjecture on average ranks) but *thin in the worst case* class of curves, and the general conjecture remains among the deepest open problems in mathematics, carrying a Clay Millennium Prize.

##7 Conclusion

The proof of BSD at analytic rank zero and one is one of the great syntheses of twentieth-century number theory. A problem about rational solutions to cubic equations is translated, via modularity, into a problem about special points on modular curves; the Gross–Zagier formula equates the analytic invariant L'(E, 1) with the geometric invariant ĥ(P_K); and Kolyvagin's Euler system turns the existence of a single nontorsion point into complete control of the Selmer group, hence of the rank and of Ш. The subsequent developments — the Yuan–Zhang–Zhang formula on Shimura curves, the p-adic Gross–Zagier formula of Perrin-Riou and its generalizations by Nekovář and Disegni, Kato's independent Euler system, and the p-converse theorems of Skinner–Zhang and their successors — have extended the reach and the precision of the method without breaching the rank-one barrier [1][2][3][5][7].

The frontier is clear. A proof of BSD in analytic rank two would require a genuinely new idea: a higher Gross–Zagier formula with a matching higher Euler system, or a wholly different approach. Until then, Heegner points and Kolyvagin systems remain the deepest unconditional window we possess into the arithmetic of elliptic curves.
