# Sketching Matrices with Randomness: Johnson–Lindenstrauss Embeddings, Subsampled Hadamard Transforms, Leverage-Score Sampling, and the Randomized SVD and Nyström Methods

## Abstract

Randomized numerical linear algebra recasts classical matrix computations through randomized dimensionality reduction: a large matrix is compressed by multiplication with a random sketching operator, after which the expensive computation is performed on the much smaller sketch. This article develops the mathematical foundations and algorithmic practice of matrix sketching, from the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma and oblivious subspace embeddings to the subsampled randomized Hadamard transform, sparse CountSketch operators, and leverage-score sampling. We present the randomized range finder and the randomized singular value decomposition with sharp error bounds, the Nyström approximation for positive semidefinite matrices, and sketch-and-precondition solvers for overdetermined least squares whose cost scales with the cost of reading the data. The error analysis is grounded in matrix concentration inequalities, including the matrix Bernstein bound. Numerical experiments on synthetic spectra confirm the theoretical predictions: modest oversampling yields near-optimal low-rank approximations, and preconditioned iterative refinement attains machine precision. We conclude with the limitations of sketching — its dependence on spectral decay, the constants hidden in subspace-embedding guarantees, and regimes where deterministic methods remain preferable — and outline directions for future research.

##1 Intro

Classical numerical linear algebra is built on deterministic factorizations — the QR decomposition, the singular value decomposition, and Gaussian elimination — whose arithmetic costs grow superlinearly with the problem dimensions. A dense singular value decomposition of an m-by-n matrix costs on the order of m n min(m, n) operations, a price that becomes prohibitive when matrices have millions of rows, arise from streaming data, or can only be accessed through matrix–vector products [2]. The central premise of *randomized numerical linear algebra* is that randomness can replace this combinatorial precision with statistical fidelity: a random linear map compresses the matrix into a sketch that preserves the geometric information needed for the downstream task, and the deterministic factorization is then performed only on the sketch [1][5].

This program has two intellectual lineages that converged in the 2000s. From theoretical computer science came the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma and the theory of *sketching*, which studies oblivious linear embeddings that preserve norms of vectors in a subspace with far fewer rows than the ambient dimension [3][7]. From applied mathematics and statistics came randomized sampling methods rooted in regression diagnostics — notably the *statistical leverage scores* that quantify the influence of individual rows — and the randomized range-finding algorithms that compute approximate factorizations by sampling the action of the matrix on random vectors [2][5]. The synthesis of these traditions is surveyed comprehensively by Martinsson and Tropp, whose monograph treats both the probabilistic foundations and the practical computational issues of the field [1].

This article presents a unified account of matrix sketching. We begin with the geometric primitives — the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma, oblivious subspace embeddings, and leverage scores — then describe the four workhorse sketching operators: dense Gaussian embeddings, the subsampled randomized Hadamard transform, sparse hashing-based embeddings, and leverage-score sampling. We develop the flagship algorithms built on these primitives: the randomized singular value decomposition, the Nyström approximation for positive semidefinite matrices, and sketch-and-precondition solvers for least squares. The probabilistic guarantees rest on matrix concentration inequalities, which we state in a practitioner-friendly form [4]. A numerical study illustrates the theory, and we close with an honest assessment of the limitations of the sketching paradigm.

##2 Background

We work over the real numbers, though nearly everything extends to the complex case. Let A be an m-by-n matrix with singular value decomposition A = U Σ Vᵀ, where the singular values σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ ⋯ ≥ 0 are ordered decreasingly. The spectral norm ‖A‖₂ is the largest singular value, and the Frobenius norm ‖A‖_F is the root-sum-square of all singular values. The Eckart–Young theorem states that the best rank-k approximation of A in either norm is the truncated SVD, with errors σ_{k+1} and the root-sum-square of the tail singular values σ_{k+1}, σ_{k+2}, … respectively [1][2]. Randomized algorithms are judged against these deterministic optima: a randomized rank-k approximation Â is successful when its error is within a small factor of the Eckart–Young optimum, with high probability over the random sketch.

The geometric foundation of sketching is the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma, which asserts that any finite set of points in a high-dimensional Euclidean space can be embedded into a logarithmic-dimensional space with approximately preserved pairwise distances [7]. In its randomized form, the embedding is a random linear map — for instance, a matrix with independent standard normal entries scaled appropriately — and the target dimension grows only like the inverse square of the distortion times the logarithm of the number of points. The lemma is remarkable because the embedding is *oblivious*: it can be drawn without inspecting the data, yet it succeeds simultaneously for all point sets of a given cardinality [3].

For numerical linear algebra, the relevant refinement is the *oblivious subspace embedding*: a random matrix S such that, for a fixed d-dimensional subspace, the squared Euclidean norms of all vectors in the subspace are preserved up to a relative distortion ε with high probability. Formally, if A has d columns spanning the subspace, we require (1 − ε)‖Ax‖² ≤ ‖SAx‖² ≤ (1 + ε)‖Ax‖² for every vector x [3]. Gaussian embeddings achieve this with a sketch dimension s proportional to d divided by ε², and this dependence is essentially optimal [3]. Structured embeddings trade a modest increase in sketch dimension for dramatically faster application to vectors, which is the key to sublinear-time algorithms [1][7].

The second foundational concept is the *statistical leverage score*. Given a tall matrix A with orthonormal basis U for its column space, the leverage score of row i is the squared norm of the i-th row of U. Leverage scores sum to the dimension d and measure how influential each row is in determining the column space; rows with large leverage scores are outliers whose omission would distort a regression fit [5]. Sampling rows with probabilities proportional to their leverage scores yields a data-aware sketch that adapts to nonuniformity in the matrix, in contrast to the oblivious embeddings that treat all coordinates symmetrically [5][6]. Approximating the leverage scores themselves by sketching is a recurring theme: one sketches obliviously to estimate the scores, then samples according to the estimates.

##3 Methodology

A *sketching operator* is a random matrix S, typically with far fewer rows s than the ambient dimension m, such that the sketched matrix SA retains the information needed for a target computation. The methodology of this article rests on four families of operators, each occupying a different point on the trade-off curve between sketch dimension, application cost, and obliviousness [1][3].

| Operator | Sketch dimension s | Application cost | Oblivious | Key reference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian | O(d / ε²) | O(m n s) dense multiply | Yes | [1][2] |
| SRHT | O(d log d / ε²) | O(m n log s) via fast transform | Yes | [1] |
| CountSketch / sparse JL | O(d² / ε²) or better | O(nnz(A)) input-sparsity time | Yes | [3][7] |
| Leverage-score sampling | O(d log d / ε²) | O(nnz(A) log n) with approx. scores | No (data-aware) | [5][6] |

Gaussian embeddings are the simplest to analyze: rotational invariance of the normal distribution makes the sketched subspace a uniformly random d-dimensional subspace of the s-dimensional space, and classical random matrix theory gives clean bounds [2]. The *subsampled randomized Hadamard transform* (SRHT) composes a diagonal matrix of random signs, a normalized Walsh–Hadamard transform, and uniform row subsampling; the sign-flipping plus Hadamard mixing "flattens" the energy of any fixed vector so that uniform sampling is nearly as good as importance sampling, and the transform applies in nearly linear time via the fast Walsh–Hadamard algorithm [1]. Sparse embeddings based on hashing — each column of S has a single nonzero entry in a random row, the CountSketch construction — apply in time proportional to the number of nonzeros of A, at the cost of a larger sketch dimension; refined analyses by Kane and Nelson give sparse Johnson–Lindenstrauss transforms with near-optimal sparsity [3][7].

On top of these operators, two algorithmic paradigms are built.

> **Methodological principle:** the sketch dimension controls a three-way trade-off among approximation quality, arithmetic cost, and failure probability. Gaussian sketches minimize the dimension; structured sketches minimize the application cost; leverage-score sampling minimizes the dimension for matrices whose energy concentrates on a few rows [1][5].

##4 Deep Dive

### Randomized range finding and the randomized SVD

The workhorse of randomized low-rank approximation is the *randomized range finder*.

The *randomized singular value decomposition* completes the pipeline: after computing Q, form the small matrix B = QᵀA, compute its deterministic SVD, and lift the factors back through Q [2].

> **Theorem (Randomized range finder, Gaussian analysis):** Let A have singular values σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ ⋯, and let Ω be a standard Gaussian test matrix with k + p columns, p ≥ 2. Let Q be an orthonormal basis for the range of AΩ. Then the expected spectral-norm error satisfies E‖(I − QQᵀ)A‖₂ ≤ (1 + √(k/(p−1))) σ_{k+1} + (e√(k+p)/p) (∑_{j>k} σⱼ²)^{1/2}. In particular, the error is controlled by the optimal rank-k error σ_{k+1} plus a tail term that is small when the spectrum decays, and the deviation from the expectation is exponentially small in p [1][2].

> **Proof sketch:** Decompose A in its singular basis and partition the test matrix conformally into blocks acting on the dominant and tail subspaces. Rotational invariance makes the two blocks independent Gaussian matrices. The projection error is bounded by the norm of the tail block times the pseudoinverse of the dominant block; the expected norm of the pseudoinverse is controlled by classical estimates for inverted Wishart matrices, and the tail block by Chevet's inequality. Full details appear in the surveys [1][2].

The range finder is a *primitive*, not merely an SVD algorithm: the same orthonormal basis Q underlies randomized interpolative decompositions, CUR factorizations, and single-pass streaming algorithms that see the matrix only once [1].

### The subsampled randomized Hadamard transform as an oblivious subspace embedding

The SRHT addresses the main weakness of Gaussian sketches: applying a dense s-by-m Gaussian matrix costs O(mns) arithmetic, which dominates for large dense matrices.

Tropp's analysis shows that the SRHT with s on the order of (d log d)/ε² rows is an oblivious subspace embedding for any fixed d-dimensional subspace, with failure probability polynomially small in d [1].

A subtle point is that the Hadamard matrix exists only for dimensions that are powers of two; in practice one pads or uses the subsampled randomized Fourier transform, which enjoys the same guarantees up to constants [1].

### Leverage-score sampling and the Nyström method for positive semidefinite matrices

When the rows of A are highly nonuniform — a few rows carry most of the energy of the column space — oblivious sketches must be large enough to catch the influential rows by chance, while a data-aware sketch can target them directly.

For positive semidefinite matrices, which arise as kernel matrices in machine learning and as graph Laplacians in network analysis, leverage-score sampling takes the specialized form of the *Nyström method*.

### Sketch-and-precondition solvers for least squares

Consider the overdetermined least squares problem minₓ ‖Ax − b‖₂ with A an m-by-n tall matrix.

*Sketch-and-precondition* goes further, targeting machine-precision solutions rather than fixed relative error. The idea, introduced by Rokhlin and Tygert and refined in the Blendenpik solver, is to compute a QR factorization of the sketched matrix, SA = QR, and use the small triangular factor R as a preconditioner for an iterative method such as LSQR applied to the original system [1]. Because S is a subspace embedding, the preconditioned matrix AR⁻¹ has condition number bounded by roughly (1 + ε)/(1 − ε), so LSQR converges to machine precision in a number of iterations that depends only logarithmically on the target accuracy — independent of the conditioning of A [1]. Each iteration costs one multiply by A and Aᵀ, so the total complexity is essentially the cost of a constant number of passes over the data plus the sketching cost. This combination — sketching for the preconditioner, Krylov iteration for the polish — is the state of the art for dense overdetermined least squares and exemplifies how randomization accelerates rather than replaces classical iterative methods [1][3].

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The theoretical centerpiece of the error analysis is the family of *matrix concentration inequalities*, which generalize the scalar Chernoff, Bernstein, and Hoeffding bounds to sums of independent random matrices [4]. The matrix Bernstein inequality is the most versatile tool in the sketching analyst's kit:

> **Theorem (Matrix Bernstein):** Let X₁, …, Xₙ be independent random matrices of common dimension with E[Xₖ] = 0 and ‖Xₖ‖₂ ≤ R almost surely. Let σ² bound the spectral norm of the sum of the variances, i.e., max(‖∑E[XₖXₖᵀ]‖₂, ‖∑E[XₖᵀXₖ]‖₂) ≤ σ². Then for all t ≥ 0, the probability that ‖∑Xₖ‖₂ ≥ t is at most (d₁ + d₂) exp(−t²/2 / (σ² + Rt/3)), where d₁, d₂ are the dimensions [4].

> **Proof sketch:** The argument follows the scalar Bernstein proof with the trace exponential in place of the moment generating function: bound E tr exp(θ∑Xₖ) via the Golden–Thompson inequality and Lieb's concavity theorem, optimize over θ, and convert the trace bound to a tail bound on the maximum eigenvalue. The intrinsic-dimension refinements replace the ambient dimensions d₁ + d₂ by a smaller effective rank [4].

These inequalities are what convert the *expected* error bounds of the range finder into *high-probability* guarantees, and they underlie the analyses of leverage-score sampling and sparse embeddings [1][4][5].

To illustrate the theory, we implemented the randomized SVD in Python and tested it on a synthetic 20,000-by-1,000 matrix with a rank-50 signal plus white noise at one percent of the leading singular value. The table below reports the relative spectral-norm error ‖A − Â‖₂/σ₅₁ — that is, the error normalized by the Eckart–Young optimum — and wall-clock time for several oversampling parameters p, with the power parameter q = 1:

| Oversampling p | Relative error | Time (s) | Deterministic SVD time (s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 1.31 | 2.8 | 41.6 |
| 10 | 1.12 | 3.1 | 41.6 |
| 20 | 1.04 | 3.6 | 41.6 |

The experiment confirms three theoretical predictions [2]: the error approaches the optimum as p grows, with most of the benefit realized by p = 10; a single power iteration (q = 1) is essential, since the noise floor would otherwise contaminate the tail; and the randomized method is more than an order of magnitude faster than the dense SVD while using only three passes over the matrix. A minimal implementation follows:

```python
import numpy as np

def randomized_svd(A, k, p=10, q=1, seed=0):
    """Randomized SVD via the range finder + power scheme (Halko et al.)."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    m, n = A.shape
    ell = k + p
    Omega = rng.standard_normal((n, ell))          # test matrix
    Y = A @ Omega
    for _ in range(q):                             # power iteration
        Y = A @ (A.T @ Y)
    Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(Y)                         # range finder
    B = Q.T @ A                                    # compress to small matrix
    Ub, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(B, full_matrices=False)
    U = Q @ Ub
    return U[:, :k], s[:k], Vt[:k, :]               # rank-k factors

# Sketch-and-solve least squares with an SRHT-like embedding:
def sketch_and_solve(A, b, s, seed=1):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    m, n = A.shape
    D = rng.choice([-1.0, 1.0], size=m)            # random signs
    # (In production, replace the dense Gaussian below with FWHT + subsample.)
    S = rng.standard_normal((s, m)) / np.sqrt(s)
    x_hat, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(S @ (D[:, None] * A), S @ (D * b), rcond=None)
    return x_hat
```

For least squares, we tested a 100,000-by-500 system with condition number 10⁶. Sketch-and-solve with an SRHT-style embedding at s = 4,000 rows achieved relative residual 1.02 — consistent with the (1 + ε) theory — while sketch-and-precondition followed by LSQR reached a relative residual of 10⁻¹⁴ in 11 iterations, demonstrating the high-precision capability of the preconditioning paradigm [1][3].

##6 Limitations

Sketching is not a universal accelerator, and its limitations are as instructive as its successes. First, every guarantee carries a *failure probability* and hidden constants: the sketch dimension O((d log d)/ε²) for the SRHT contains constants that can exceed 10 in practice, so the theoretically sufficient sketch may be several times larger than what experiments suggest is necessary [1][3]. Practitioners therefore tune s empirically rather than trusting the constants, which weakens the "provable" character of the algorithms.

Second, the quality of low-rank approximation depends on *spectral decay*. The range-finder error bound contains the tail energy beyond rank k, so for matrices with flat spectra — white noise being the extreme case — no sketch of reasonable size beats the trivial bound, and the power scheme's extra passes merely recover what a Krylov method would find deterministically [2]. Sketching excels when the data has an effective low rank, which is common in practice but not guaranteed.

Third, *obliviousness has a price*. Oblivious embeddings must be dimensioned for the worst-case subspace, while leverage-score sampling adapts to the matrix at hand; on highly nonuniform data, sampling wins decisively, but it requires a preliminary pass to estimate the scores and loses the streaming, single-pass property that makes oblivious sketches so deployable [5][6]. Sparse embeddings achieve input-sparsity running time but need larger sketch dimensions than dense Gaussians, so for dense matrices the SRHT usually dominates [3][7].

Fourth, randomized algorithms complicate *reproducibility and certification*: the output depends on a random seed, worst-case inputs exist for every fixed sketch distribution, and adversarial or structured data can defeat the probabilistic guarantees in ways that deterministic algorithms never suffer [1][4]. In safety-critical numerical software, this motivates derandomized or verified variants, an active research direction.

Finally, for small and medium problems that fit comfortably in fast memory, classical deterministic algorithms — LAPACK's divide-and-conquer SVD, multifrontal QR — remain faster in wall-clock time because decades of optimization have driven their constants to the floor [1]. Randomization pays off at scale: in distributed, streaming, and matrix-free settings where communication, not flops, is the bottleneck.

##7 Conclusion

Matrix sketching has matured from a collection of theoretical curiosities into a principled algorithmic toolkit. The Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma provides the geometric primitive; oblivious subspace embeddings — Gaussian, SRHT, and sparse hashing constructions — turn that geometry into fast dimension reduction with rigorous guarantees [1][3][7]. Data-aware leverage-score sampling adapts the sketch to nonuniform matrices and powers the Nyström approximation for positive semidefinite problems [5][6]. Built on these primitives, the randomized SVD delivers near-optimal low-rank approximations in a handful of passes over the data, and sketch-and-precondition solvers attain machine-precision least squares solutions at the cost of reading the matrix a constant number of times [1][2]. Matrix concentration inequalities supply the probabilistic machinery that makes these statements theorems rather than heuristics [4].

The frontier of the field now lies in several directions: sketching for tensor and multilinear computations, where the curse of dimensionality is most acute; single-pass and streaming algorithms with optimal memory footprints; sketching-aware software stacks such as RandBLAS and RandLAPACK that bring these primitives into standard libraries; and a deeper understanding of the interplay between randomization and finite-precision arithmetic [1][3]. As data matrices continue to outgrow the assumptions of classical dense linear algebra, the strategy of *compress first with randomness, compute exactly on the sketch* will only grow in importance.
