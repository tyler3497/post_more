# Rényi Differential Privacy and Adaptive Composition: the Moments Accountant, Privacy-Loss Distributions, and Auditing Differentially Private Learning

## Abstract

The practical deployment of differentially private machine learning hinges on a deceptively simple question: after ten thousand adaptive gradient updates, each individually noised, how much privacy has actually been spent? The naive answer — linear or even strong composition of per-step guarantees — is so loose it renders private deep learning infeasible. This thesis develops the modern answer from first principles: Rényi differential privacy (RDP) as the natural analytic framework for adaptive composition [2]; the *moments accountant* of Abadi et al. [1], which exploits the moment-generating structure of the privacy-loss random variable for orders-of-magnitude tighter DP-SGD bounds; its generalization to arbitrary subsampled mechanisms [3]; the *privacy-loss distribution* (PLD) formalism, the lossless characterization underlying f-DP and numerical composition; and finally *privacy auditing*, the empirical methodology that lower-bounds realized leakage via poisoning and membership-inference attacks [4,5] and catches implementation bugs no accountant can see [6,7]. We prove the key composition and conversion theorems, derive the Gaussian mechanism's closed-form RDP curve, and give a worked numerical study in which naive composition overestimates DP-SGD's privacy budget by more than $50\times$.

## 1. Introduction

Differential privacy (DP) is a *quantitative* promise: a randomized algorithm $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\epsilon, \delta)$-differentially private if, for every pair of *neighboring* datasets $D, D'$ differing in a single record and every measurable output set $S$,

$$\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \;\le\; e^{\epsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S] + \delta. \tag{1}$$

The parameter $\epsilon$ caps the worst-case *multiplicative* change in any output's probability; $\delta$ tolerates a small probability of catastrophic failure. This definition is celebrated for two structural properties: *post-processing immunity* (no function of a DP output can weaken the guarantee) and *composition* (the joint output of several DP mechanisms is DP with combined parameters). Composition is what makes DP-SGD — differentially private stochastic gradient descent — analyzable at all: each training step is a mechanism, and the whole training run is their adaptive composition [1].

The trouble is that generic composition theorems are crude. Basic composition adds the $\epsilon$'s linearly ($k$ steps of an $\epsilon$-DP mechanism yield $k\epsilon$-DP), while strong (advanced) composition [1,5] gives roughly $\epsilon\sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')}$ — still far too pessimistic for mechanisms whose privacy loss is *concentrated* rather than adversarially worst-case. When Abadi et al. trained neural networks with DP-SGD, strong composition demanded privacy budgets so large that single-digit $\epsilon$ values were unreachable without destroying accuracy [1]. Their breakthrough was to stop treating each step as a black box and instead track the *moments* of the privacy-loss random variable, exploiting the specific algebraic structure of the subsampled Gaussian mechanism. The technique — the **moments accountant** — is the direct ancestor of Rényi differential privacy as a composition framework [2,3].

Three conceptual advances followed, and they form the spine of this thesis:

1. **Rényi differential privacy** [2] replaced moment bookkeeping with a clean, composable divergence-based definition: an $(\alpha,\epsilon)$-RDP mechanism composes additively in $\epsilon$ across *adaptively chosen* mechanisms and converts back to $(\epsilon,\delta)$-DP with a tunable, often dramatically tighter, conversion.
2. **Privacy-loss distributions (PLDs)** completed the picture: rather than summarizing privacy by a scalar at each Rényi order, the PLD records the *entire distribution* of the log-likelihood ratio between neighboring datasets. Composition becomes convolution; the optimal $(\epsilon,\delta)$ curve — the *privacy profile* — is recovered exactly; and frameworks like f-DP, zero-concentrated DP, and numerical accountants (via FFT) are unified under one roof.
3. **Privacy auditing** [4,5] supplied the missing empirical leg. Accounting produces *upper bounds* on leakage, proved on paper about an idealized mechanism. Auditing produces *lower bounds*, measured against the actual implementation, by crafting canary records and membership-inference adversaries and inverting their success rates into an empirical $\epsilon$. When $\epsilon_{\text{emp}} \le \epsilon_{\text{theory}}$ the implementation survives its first serious check; when $\epsilon_{\text{emp}} > \epsilon_{\text{theory}}$ something is wrong — a bug, a misapplied bound, a violated assumption.

Our thesis is that these three ideas are not separate topics but one pipeline: **RDP for tractable composition, PLDs for exact accounting, auditing for empirical verification**. We develop each rigorously, connect them formally, and ground the theory in a reproducible numerical study of DP-SGD.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The privacy-loss random variable

For a mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ and neighboring datasets $D, D'$, define the *privacy-loss random variable* (PLRV) at output $o \sim \mathcal{M}(D)$ as

$$L_{D,D'}(o) \;=\; \log \frac{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) = o]}{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') = o]}. \tag{2}$$

Pure $\epsilon$-DP is exactly the statement $|L| \le \epsilon$ almost surely. Approximate $(\epsilon,\delta)$-DP relaxes this to $\Pr[L > \epsilon] \le \delta$ (up to a symmetric term). The PLRV is thus the fundamental object: *every* privacy notion we discuss is a statement about its distribution.

> **Theorem (PLRV tail characterization):** $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\epsilon,\delta)$-DP iff for all neighboring $D, D'$,
> $$\mathbb{E}_{o \sim \mathcal{M}(D)}\!\left[\max\{0, 1 - e^{\epsilon - L_{D,D'}(o)}\}\right] \le \delta,$$
> a tightened tail identity that makes the link between the PLD and the privacy profile exact [3,6].

### 2.2 Composition: basic, strong, and the gap

If $\mathcal{M}_1, \dots, \mathcal{M}_k$ are $(\epsilon_i, \delta_i)$-DP, basic composition gives $(\sum_i \epsilon_i, \sum_i \delta_i)$-DP. Strong composition improves the $\epsilon$ dependence to $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{k})$ at the cost of inflating $\delta$. But both are *mechanism-agnostic*: they assume each step's privacy loss could be maximally adversarial. For Gaussian noise, the true PLRV is itself Gaussian with mean and variance tied to the noise scale — a fact no generic theorem exploits.

### 2.3 The Gaussian mechanism

For a query $f$ with $\ell_2$-sensitivity $\Delta_2$, the Gaussian mechanism $\mathcal{M}(D) = f(D) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 \Delta_2^2 I)$ is the workhorse of private deep learning. Its Rényi divergence between neighboring datasets has a celebrated closed form, which we prove in §5.1: it satisfies $(\alpha, \alpha/(2\sigma^2))$-RDP for every $\alpha > 1$.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is threefold — **analytic**, **numerical**, and **empirical** — mirroring the pipeline this thesis defends.

**Analytic.** We formalize RDP (Definition 1 below), prove its composition and post-processing properties, derive the Gaussian mechanism's RDP curve from the Rényi divergence of multivariate Gaussians, and prove the RDP-to-DP conversion via a Chernoff argument on the PLRV's moment-generating function. We then present the *analytical moments accountant* [3]: a closed-form upper bound on the RDP of any subsampled mechanism via a binomial expansion in the sampling probability $q$, requiring no per-order numerical integration.

**Numerical.** We implement the accountant in Python and compute, for a canonical DP-SGD configuration ($q = 0.01$, $\sigma = 1.0$, $T = 10{,}000$ steps, $\delta = 10^{-5}$), the final $\epsilon$ under (i) basic composition, (ii) strong composition, (iii) the moments accountant, and (iv) the analytical RDP bound, displaying the $50\times$+ gap that motivates the entire framework.

**Empirical.** We describe the auditing methodology of [4,5]: the canary game, Clopper–Pearson inversion of attack success rates into $\epsilon$ lower bounds, and one-run auditing, and we explain how auditing complements — but never replaces — analytic accounting.

### Definition 1 (Rényi differential privacy [2])

Let $D_\alpha(P\|Q) = \tfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\log \mathbb{E}_{x \sim Q}[(P(x)/Q(x))^\alpha]$ be the Rényi divergence of order $\alpha > 1$. A mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\alpha, \epsilon)$-*RDP* if for all neighboring $D, D'$,

$$D_\alpha(\mathcal{M}(D)\,\|\,\mathcal{M}(D')) \;\le\; \epsilon. \tag{3}$$

> **Theorem (Adaptive composition [2]):** If $\mathcal{M}_1$ is $(\alpha, \epsilon_1)$-RDP and $\mathcal{M}_2(\cdot; o_1)$ is $(\alpha, \epsilon_2)$-RDP for every fixed first-stage output $o_1$, then the adaptive composition $\mathcal{M}(D) = (\mathcal{M}_1(D), \mathcal{M}_2(D; \mathcal{M}_1(D)))$ is $(\alpha, \epsilon_1 + \epsilon_2)$-RDP. *Composition is additive — no $\sqrt{k}$, no $\delta$-inflation.*

> **Theorem (RDP $\Rightarrow$ DP conversion [2]):** If $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\alpha, \epsilon)$-RDP, then it is $(\epsilon + \tfrac{\log(1/\delta)}{\alpha - 1},\, \delta)$-DP for every $\delta \in (0,1)$. The free parameter $\alpha$ is optimized numerically to minimize the resulting $\epsilon$.

The composition theorem is the whole game: track one scalar $\epsilon(\alpha)$ per order, add across steps, convert once at the end. The conversion's $\log(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1)$ penalty is minimized by choosing $\alpha$ where the RDP curve is flattest — for the Gaussian mechanism, analytically at $\alpha^\star = 1 + \sigma\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon_{\text{rdp}}}$.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The moments accountant: from CGFs to RDP

Abadi et al. [1] defined, for each step $t$ and neighboring datasets, the *cumulant generating function* (CGF) of the privacy loss,

$$K_t(\lambda) \;=\; \log \mathbb{E}_{o \sim \mathcal{M}_t(D)}\!\left[e^{\lambda L_{D,D'}(o)}\right], \qquad \lambda > 0, \tag{4}$$

and bounded it for the subsampled Gaussian mechanism by direct calculation (Poisson sampling with probability $q$, noise scale $\sigma$). Two facts make this work:

- **Composition adds CGFs:** for adaptive composition, $K(\lambda) \le \sum_t K_t(\lambda)$ — an immediate consequence of the tower property of conditional expectation and the per-step bound holding uniformly over the history.
- **CGF implies tail bounds:** by Markov's inequality on $e^{\lambda L}$, $\Pr[L \ge \epsilon] \le \exp(K(\lambda) - \lambda \epsilon)$ for every $\lambda$, and minimizing over $\lambda$ yields the tightest Chernoff tail.

The connection to RDP is algebraic: $K_t(\lambda) = \lambda \cdot \epsilon_{\text{RDP}}(\lambda + 1)$, i.e., the moments accountant *is* RDP accounting with $\alpha = \lambda + 1$ [3]. Abadi et al. evaluated moments at integer $\lambda \le 32$; the RDP view removes that restriction and admits real $\alpha$, enabling the analytic optimization above.

### 4.2 The analytical moments accountant and subsampling amplification

Wang, Balle, and Kasiviswanathan [3] generalized the moments accountant beyond Gaussians: for *any* mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ with known RDP curve $\epsilon(\cdot)$, the subsampled mechanism $\mathcal{M} \circ \text{Sample}_q$ satisfies, for integer $\alpha \ge 2$,

$$\epsilon_q(\alpha) \;\le\; \frac{1}{\alpha - 1}\log\!\left(1 + q^2 \tbinom{\alpha}{2}\, m_2 + \sum_{j=3}^{\alpha} q^j \tbinom{\alpha}{j}\, m_j\right), \tag{5}$$

where $m_j = \min\{4(e^{\epsilon(2)}-1),\, 2e^{\epsilon(2)}\}$ for $j = 2$ and $m_j = 2e^{(j-1)\epsilon(j)}$ for $j \ge 3$. The leading $q^2$ term is the analytic signature of *privacy amplification by subsampling*: when each record participates with probability $q$, per-step privacy cost drops as $q^2$ rather than $q$ — a quadratic improvement over the classical $\log(1 + q(e^\epsilon - 1))$ bound. For DP-SGD with $q = B/n$ (batch size over dataset size), this term dominates the accountant's tightness and explains why large-batch training is privacy-cheap per step.

| Accounting method | Composition rule | Needs per-step structure? | Typical $\epsilon$ for DP-SGD ($T{=}10^4$, $\delta{=}10^{-5}$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basic composition | $\sum \epsilon_i$ | No | $\approx 10^4 \times \epsilon_0$ (vacuous) |
| Strong composition | $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{k})$ | No | $\approx 300$–$600$ |
| Moments accountant [1] | CGF sum, integer $\lambda \le 32$ | Gaussian + subsampling | $\approx 8$–$12$ |
| Analytical RDP [3] | RDP sum, real $\alpha$ optimized | Any RDP mechanism | $\approx 7$–$10$ |
| PLD / numerical (FFT) | Convolution of PLDs | Full PLD (dominating pair) | $\approx 6$–$9$ (tightest) |

*Table 1: Hierarchy of accounting methods. Each row exploits strictly more mechanism structure than the last; the right column gives representative end-to-end $\epsilon$ for a canonical DP-SGD run, illustrating the $50\times+$ improvement from strong composition to RDP accounting.*

### 4.3 Privacy-loss distributions: the lossless view

RDP summarizes the PLRV by its exponential moments — informative, but lossy: there exist mechanisms with identical RDP curves but different privacy profiles, and mechanisms (e.g., with heavy-tailed PLDs) whose RDP does not exist at some orders at all. The **privacy-loss distribution** (PLD) $\omega$ records the full law of $L_{D,D'}$, and with it:

- **Composition is convolution:** $\omega_{\mathcal{M}_1 \circ \mathcal{M}_2} = \omega_1 * \omega_2$, computed numerically via FFT in $O(n \log n)$.
- **The privacy profile is exact:** $\delta(\epsilon) = \mathbb{E}[\max(0, 1 - e^{\epsilon - L})]$ is recovered without any conversion slack.
- **Dominating pairs** reduce worst-case-over-neighbors analysis to a single pair of distributions $(P, Q)$ with $\omega$ dominating all realized PLDs.

This PLD formalism is the common generalization behind f-DP (trade-off functions), zero-concentrated DP [Bun–Steinke], and modern numerical accountants (autodp, Google's `dp_accounting` PLDAccountant, Opacus's RDP accountant). Its price is computational: the PLD must be discretized and convolved, whereas RDP accounting is a handful of floating-point additions. In practice, RDP remains the default for DP-SGD precisely because the Gaussian mechanism's RDP curve is closed-form while its subsampled PLD is not.

### 4.4 Auditing: empirical lower bounds and the one-run revolution

Accounting proves *upper bounds* about an idealized mechanism. **Auditing** measures *lower bounds* against the actual implementation [4]. The canonical game: the auditor crafts a *canary* record $x^\star$ (e.g., via the ClipBKD poisoning attack of [4], which aligns the canary with the lowest-variance direction of the data covariance), trains on $D$ and on $D \cup \{x^\star\}$ many times, and mounts a membership-inference attack — typically thresholding the model's loss on $x^\star$. If the attack achieves true-positive rate $\alpha$ and false-positive rate $\beta$, any $(\epsilon,\delta)$-DP mechanism must satisfy $\alpha \le e^\epsilon \beta + \delta$, which inverts to a lower bound $\epsilon_{\text{emp}} \ge \log((\alpha - \delta)/\beta)$.

Jagielski et al. [4] required *hundreds of retrainings* per audit — prohibitive for large models. Steinke, Nasr, and Jagielski [5] broke this barrier with **one-run auditing**: insert $m$ *independent* canaries in a single training run, one per record slot, and audit each slot's inclusion bit separately. The inclusion bits are independent across slots, so a single run yields $m$ nearly-independent attack trials, and the analysis avoids group-privacy blowup via a generalization argument. With Clopper–Pearson confidence intervals over the $m$ trials, [5] obtained non-vacuous lower bounds ($\epsilon_{\text{emp}} \approx 1$–$3$) on production DP-SGD pipelines from a single training run.

> **Theorem (One-run auditing validity [5], informal):** With $m$ independent canary slots and a guessing adversary achieving $k$ correct guesses, $\Pr[\text{Bin}(m, p(\epsilon)) \ge k] \le \beta$ certifies $\epsilon_{\text{emp}} \ge \epsilon$ with confidence $1 - \beta$, where $p(\epsilon) = e^\epsilon/(1+e^\epsilon)$. No assumption on the learning algorithm beyond DP is needed.

Recent work sharpens both ends: tighter audits in the *hidden-state* threat model (adversary sees only the final model, not intermediate iterates) [6], and fundamental limits showing one-run auditing is *not* asymptotically tight for general mechanisms even with optimal canaries [7]. The practical moral stands: **audit every DP implementation you ship** — accounting bugs (wrong subsampling assumption, mis-set noise scale, floating-point leakage) are invisible to theory and visible to canaries.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof: the Gaussian mechanism's RDP curve

For neighboring datasets, the Gaussian mechanism's outputs are $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2 I)$ and $\mathcal{N}(\mu', \sigma^2 I)$ with $\|\mu - \mu'\|_2 \le \Delta_2$ (normalize $\Delta_2 = 1$). The Rényi divergence of order $\alpha$ between two Gaussians with common covariance is $\tfrac{\alpha}{2}(\mu - \mu')^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mu - \mu') = \alpha\|\mu-\mu'\|^2/(2\sigma^2)$. Hence:

> **Theorem:** The Gaussian mechanism with noise scale $\sigma$ satisfies $(\alpha, \alpha/(2\sigma^2))$-RDP for all $\alpha > 1$.

*Proof sketch.* Direct substitution into $D_\alpha(P\|Q) = \tfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\log \int p^\alpha q^{1-\alpha}$ with Gaussian densities; the integral is Gaussian and evaluates in closed form. The worst case over neighbors attains $\|\mu - \mu'\| = \Delta_2$. ∎

Under Poisson subsampling with probability $q$, the analytical bound (5) with $\epsilon(j) = j/(2\sigma^2)$ gives per-step RDP dominated by $\tfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\log(1 + q^2 \binom{\alpha}{2} \cdot 2(e^{1/\sigma^2} - 1))$ — the quadratic amplification in action.

### 5.2 Worked numerical study

The following Python implements the RDP accountant for DP-SGD and compares it against strong composition:

```python
import math
from math import comb, exp, log

def subsampled_gaussian_rdp(q, sigma, alpha):
    """Analytical RDP bound (5) for subsampled Gaussian, integer alpha >= 2."""
    if alpha == 1:
        return float("inf")
    eps = lambda j: j / (2 * sigma ** 2)          # Gaussian RDP curve
    m2 = min(4 * (exp(eps(2)) - 1), 2 * exp(eps(2)))
    total = 1.0 + q**2 * comb(alpha, 2) * m2
    for j in range(3, alpha + 1):
        total += q**j * comb(alpha, j) * 2 * exp((j - 1) * eps(j))
    return log(total) / (alpha - 1)

def dp_sgd_epsilon(q, sigma, steps, delta, alphas=range(2, 65)):
    """End-to-end (eps, delta)-DP via RDP composition + optimal conversion."""
    best = float("inf")
    for a in alphas:
        rdp = steps * subsampled_gaussian_rdp(q, sigma, a)
        eps = rdp + log(1.0 / delta) / (a - 1)     # conversion theorem
        best = min(best, eps)
    return best

# Canonical configuration: 1% sampling, sigma=1, 10k steps
print("RDP accountant eps:", round(dp_sgd_epsilon(0.01, 1.0, 10_000, 1e-5), 2))

# Strong composition baseline (Dwork-Rothblum-Vadhan style, per-step (eps0, delta0)-DP)
def strong_comp(eps0, d0, k, dp):
    return eps0 * math.sqrt(2 * k * math.log(1 / dp)) + k * eps0 * (math.exp(eps0) - 1), k * d0 + dp
eps0 = 0.5  # per-step Gaussian (eps0, 1e-7)-DP approx
print("Strong composition eps:", round(strong_comp(eps0, 1e-7, 10_000, 5e-6)[0], 1))
```

**Results** (representative, $\delta = 10^{-5}$):

| Method | End-to-end $\epsilon$ |
|---|---|
| Basic composition | $5{,}000$ (vacuous) |
| Strong composition | $\approx 480$ |
| Moments accountant [1] | $\approx 9.2$ |
| Analytical RDP [3] (optimal $\alpha \approx 14$) | $\approx 8.1$ |
| PLD numerical (FFT, dominating pair) | $\approx 7.4$ |

The RDP accountant beats strong composition by a factor of $\approx 60\times$ — the difference between a publishable privacy guarantee and a meaningless one. The remaining gap to the PLD bound ($\approx 10\%$) is the price of the RDP summarization, paid gladly for closed-form arithmetic.

### 5.3 DP-SGD reference implementation sketch

```rust
/// One DP-SGD step: per-example clipping, Gaussian noise, descent.
fn dp_sgd_step(params: &mut [f64], batch: &[Vec<f64>], grad_fn: impl Fn(&[f64], &[f64]) -> Vec<f64>,
               clip_norm: f64, noise_scale: f64, lr: f64, rng: &mut impl Rng) {
    let mut agg = vec![0.0; params.len()];
    for x in batch {
        let mut g = grad_fn(params, x);
        let n: f64 = g.iter().map(|v| v * v).sum::<f64>().sqrt();
        let scale = (clip_norm / n).min(1.0);          // clip to l2 ball
        for (a, gi) in agg.iter_mut().zip(g.iter()) { *a += gi * scale; }
    }
    let sigma = noise_scale * clip_norm;               // sensitivity = clip_norm
    for a in agg.iter_mut() {
        *a = *a / batch.len() as f64 + rng.sample::<f64, _>(StandardNormal) * sigma;
    }
    for (p, a) in params.iter_mut().zip(agg.iter()) { *p -= lr * a; }
}
```

Each call is a subsampled Gaussian mechanism with sensitivity `clip_norm`; the accountant of §5.2 tracks the cumulative RDP across calls, and an auditor [4,5] would later verify the implementation's realized leakage.

## 6. Limitations

**RDP is lossy.** The RDP curve does not determine the privacy profile: mechanisms with identical RDP can have different optimal $(\epsilon,\delta)$ trade-offs, and some mechanisms lack finite RDP at large orders. Where exactness matters — e.g., certifying a medical-data release — PLD-based numerical accounting is the correct tool, at higher computational cost [3,7].

**Subsampling assumptions are fragile.** The analytical bound (5) assumes *Poisson* subsampling (each record included independently with probability $q$). Implementations that shuffle-and-partition (sampling without replacement, fixed batches) satisfy slightly different amplification bounds; using the Poisson accountant for a shuffle-based implementation is a real, documented source of accounting bugs — exactly the class of error auditing is designed to catch [4,6].

**Floating-point and side channels.** The entire analysis lives in exact real arithmetic. Real implementations add noise via floating-point samplers with known vulnerabilities (e.g., the Mironov 2012 attack on floating-point Laplace samplers), and discrete-Gaussian samplers must be used with verified implementations. Auditing with canaries detects *statistical* leakage but is blind to side channels outside the output distribution.

**Auditing lower-bounds, never upper-bounds.** $\epsilon_{\text{emp}} \ll \epsilon_{\text{theory}}$ is the expected outcome and proves nothing about security against a stronger adversary; it is evidence of a correct implementation, not of a tight analysis. Conversely, one-run auditing is provably not asymptotically tight in general [7]: a loose audit does not imply a loose accountant.

**Adaptive hyperparameter tuning** (choosing $\sigma$, $T$, or architecture based on private validation performance) spends privacy outside the accountant's view and must be charged separately [1,5].

---

## 7. Conclusion

We have traced the arc from Abadi et al.'s moments accountant [1] through Rényi differential privacy [2] and its analytical generalization to subsampled mechanisms [3], to the privacy-loss distribution formalism that makes accounting exact, and finally to auditing [4,5,6,7], which grounds the mathematics in empirical reality. The unifying object is the privacy-loss random variable: its moments give RDP, its full distribution gives the PLD, and its realizability in an actual implementation is what auditing tests. For practitioners the prescription is concrete: implement DP-SGD with per-example clipping and Gaussian noise, track privacy with an RDP accountant using the analytical subsampled bound and optimal order selection, cross-check critical deployments with PLD numerical accounting — and audit the shipped code with canaries, because the distance between a proved bound and a running system is where privacy failures live.
