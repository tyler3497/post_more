# Molten Salt Reactor Neutronics: Thorium Fuel Cycles, Online Reprocessing, and Transient Safety Analysis

## Abstract

Molten salt reactors couple liquid fluoride fuel to graphite-moderated cores, producing a neutron economy that is qualitatively different from solid-fuel systems: delayed-neutron precursors drift with the circulating salt, the temperature coefficient of reactivity is dominated by fuel thermal expansion, and fission products can be removed continuously during operation. This thesis develops the neutronics of molten salt reactors in the thorium–uranium-233 fuel cycle, from the Molten Salt Reactor Experiment at Oak Ridge (1965–1969) through contemporary programs including China's TMSR-LF1 and Kairos Power's Hermes. We derive circulating-fuel point kinetics with explicit precursor transport, quantify the reduction in effective delayed-neutron fraction caused by out-of-core decay, analyze the thorium breeding chain with emphasis on protactinium-233 holdup and neutron economy, and review transient safety behavior under unprotected loss-of-heat-sink and loss-of-flow scenarios. Empirical results from the MSRE — over thirteen thousand full-power hours, xenon poisoning reduced by a factor of six through gas stripping, and reactivity coefficients in agreement with prior calculation — anchor the analysis. Limitations including materials compatibility, tritium management, and the absence of a licensed commercial design are assessed.

## 1. Introduction

Among the six reactor families retained by the Generation IV International Forum, the molten salt reactor (MSR) is distinguished by the absence of solid fuel entirely: the fissile and fertile materials are dissolved in a fluoride or chloride carrier salt that serves simultaneously as fuel and coolant [6]. The liquid state is not merely an engineering convenience; it restructures reactor physics. Delayed-neutron precursors, born in fission, are swept out of the core by the flowing salt and decay preferentially in external piping, heat exchangers, and drain tanks. The temperature coefficient of reactivity is governed by the thermal expansion of the liquid, which expels fuel from the high-importance core region. And the chemical form of the fuel — typically tetrafluorides dissolved in a lithium–beryllium fluoride (FLiBe) carrier — permits *online* processing: gaseous fission products can be stripped by helium sparging, noble metals can plate out, and the salt can be adjusted or tapped while the reactor continues to operate [2].

These properties align powerfully with the **thorium–uranium-233 fuel cycle**. Thorium-232 is fertile rather than fissile; neutron capture produces thorium-233, which decays (22-minute half-life) to protactinium-233 and then (27-day half-life) to fissile uranium-233 [1]. The MSRE operated successfully on U-233 derived from thorium, demonstrating the viability of the cycle in 1969 [2]. China has revived the program: the 2 MWt TMSR-LF1 at Wuwei reached first criticality on 11 October 2023 and, in October 2024, completed the world's first thorium addition to an operating molten salt reactor, with protactinium-233 subsequently detected [3][7].

This thesis treats the *neutronics* of thorium-fueled MSRs: circulating-fuel kinetics, the breeding chain, online reprocessing as it feeds back into reactivity, and the transient safety case. Section 2 reviews history and salt physics; Section 3 develops the kinetics methodology; Section 4 treats reprocessing, transients, protactinium, and designs; Sections 5–7 present results, limitations, and conclusions.

> **Thesis statement.** *The distinctive neutronics of the liquid-fueled thorium reactor — precursor drift that suppresses the effective delayed-neutron fraction, expansion-dominated negative temperature feedback, and continuous fission-product removal — together create a safety and fuel-cycle case that is qualitatively stronger than that of solid-fuel thermal reactors, provided that materials, tritium, and reprocessing complexity can be managed at industrial scale.*

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Oak Ridge program: ARE to MSRE

Work on molten salt systems began at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in the 1950s with the Aircraft Reactor Experiment (ARE), a 1–3 MWt circulating-fuel reactor fueled with 93.4% enriched UF4 dissolved in fluoride salt and moderated by beryllium oxide, built to explore nuclear propulsion for aircraft [2]. A circulating liquid fuel expanded on heating and naturally stabilized the reactor, solving the xenon-instability problem that plagued solid-fuel designs [2].

The **Molten Salt Reactor Experiment (MSRE)**, designed from 1960, constructed from 1962, and operated from June 1965 to December 1969, was the decisive civilian demonstration [1][2]. Key parameters and history:

- Graphite-moderated, 7.4–8 MWt (design 10 MWt), cooled with FLiBe-family salt; the reactor vessel, core, fuel pump, and heat exchanger sat inside a containment tank [1][2].
- First criticality with U-235 on 1 June 1965; uranium stripped by fluorination in August 1968; critical on U-233 on 2 October 1968, full power January 1969 [2].
- Operating statistics: 13,172 full-power hours, 9,005 hours on U-235, 4,167 hours on U-233, with 86% availability during the final 15-month reliability period [2].
- The program was terminated in 1973 by the Atomic Energy Commission in favor of the fast-breeder program; the proposed Molten Salt Breeder Reactor (MSBR) was never built [1].

The MSRE's conclusions were broad: the molten-salt fuel concept was viable, fuel salt was immune to radiation damage, graphite was unattacked, Hastelloy-N corrosion was negligible, and measured neutronics — critical loading, reactivity coefficients, dynamics, long-term reactivity changes — agreed with prior calculations [1]. Noble-gas stripping reduced xenon-135 poisoning by a factor of about six; uranium additions during operation were quick and uneventful; uranium recovery by fluorination was efficient [1].

### 2.2 The thorium–uranium-233 breeding chain

Thorium-232 is fertile: it requires a fissile driver (U-235 or plutonium) to supply power and the neutron surplus for breeding [7]:

> **Definition 1 (Thorium breeding chain).** *Th-232 + n → Th-233 (t½ ≈ 22 min) → Pa-233 (t½ ≈ 27 d) → U-233 (fissile).*

The intermediate **protactinium-233** is the crux of thorium neutronics. With a 27-day half-life, Pa-233 that remains in the core absorbs neutrons parasitically (its capture cross-section in the thermal/epithermal spectrum is significant), producing U-234 instead of U-233. This "protactinium effect" imposes a reactivity penalty during startup and degrades the breeding ratio. In a solid-fuel reactor the Pa-233 cannot be removed; in a liquid-fuel reactor it can be extracted continuously from the salt, allowed to decay outside the neutron flux, and the resulting U-233 reintroduced — the decisive fuel-cycle advantage of the MSR [9].

The η of U-233 in a thermal MSR spectrum is ~2.29 with α ≈ 0.09–0.11 — data refined by the MSRE — leaving roughly one surplus neutron per fission for breeding [1].

### 2.3 Fluoride salt chemistry and neutron physics

The carrier salt most studied is **FLiBe** (LiF–BeF2), used in the MSRE and TMSR-LF1 [3]. It must combine low neutron absorption, high actinide-fluoride solubility, radiation stability, and alloy compatibility. Li-7 enrichment (≥99.95% in TMSR-LF1 [3]) is mandatory because Li-6 has an enormous (n,α) cross-section producing tritium — a neutron poison and a radiological contaminant.

Two neutronic facts matter: the salt is neutron-transparent relative to water (graphite does the moderating in thermal designs), and homogeneously dissolved fuel couples thermal expansion directly to reactivity — no pellet-clad gap, no fertile self-shielding advantage.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Circulating-fuel point kinetics with precursor drift

Standard point kinetics assumes delayed-neutron precursors are stationary in the core. In an MSR, precursors of the six (or eight) delayed groups decay with constants λi while being advected through the core with transit time τc and through the external loop (piping, heat exchanger, pump bowl) with transit time τL. Precursors that decay outside the core contribute no delayed neutron to the chain reaction — they represent a *loss* of effective delayed-neutron fraction βeff.

> **Definition 2 (Effective delayed-neutron fraction, circulating fuel).** *For group i with decay constant λi, core transit time τc, and loop transit time τL, the fraction of precursors decaying in the core is fi = (1 − e^(−λiτc)) / (1 − e^(−λi(τc+τL))). The effective delayed fraction is βeff = Σi βi fi, where βi are the physical delayed-neutron fractions.*

The circulating-fuel kinetics equations read:

dP/dt = ((ρ − βeff)/Λ) P + Σi λi Ci
dCi/dt = (βi/Λ) P − (λi + 1/τc) Ci + (1/τc) Ci(t − τc − τL) e^{(−λi τL)}

The essential consequence: βeff is strictly smaller than the static β — for the MSRE roughly half the precursors decayed outside the core — so circulating-fuel reactors respond faster to reactivity insertions and control analysis must use the drifted value [1]. The Python model below implements this:

```python
# Circulating-fuel point kinetics: precursor drift model (illustrative)
import math

# Six-group U-235 delayed data: (beta_i, lambda_i [1/s])
groups = [
    (0.000215, 0.0124), (0.001424, 0.0305), (0.001274, 0.111),
    (0.002568, 0.301),   (0.000748, 1.14),  (0.000273, 3.01),
]
beta_static = sum(b for b, _ in groups)

# MSRE-like loop: 25 s in core, 25 s in external loop
tau_c, tau_L = 25.0, 25.0

def in_core_fraction(lam):
    num = 1.0 - math.exp(-lam * tau_c)
    den = 1.0 - math.exp(-lam * (tau_c + tau_L))
    return num / den

fi = [in_core_fraction(lam) for _, lam in groups]
beta_eff = sum(b * f for (b, _), f in zip(groups, fi))

print(f"static beta      = {beta_static:.5f}")
print(f"circulating beta = {beta_eff:.5f}")
print(f"retained fraction = {beta_eff / beta_static:.3f}")
```

The longest-lived groups retain only ~40–50% of precursors in-core; the weighted βeff falls to roughly half the static value, matching MSRE experience [1].

### 3.2 Temperature-coefficient decomposition

The isothermal temperature coefficient αT decomposes into density, Doppler, and moderator terms. In a liquid-fuel MSR the **density (expansion) term dominates** and is inherently negative: heating the salt reduces fuel number density in the core and pushes fuel into the lower-importance loop region. MSRE measurements confirmed a prompt negative coefficient, and transient tests showed power self-regulation without control-rod motion [1][2].

### 3.3 Neutron-energy-spectrum modeling

Modern analysis couples continuous-energy Monte Carlo (OpenMC, MCNP, Serpent) to depletion solvers, with few-group cross-sections feeding transient codes such as RESTA3D/TMSR-LINK [10]:

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Online reprocessing: fission-product management as neutronics

Because the fuel is a liquid, fission products can be removed *continuously*, and every removal channel is a reactivity effect:

1. **Noble gases (Xe-135, Kr).** Xenon-135 is the dominant neutron poison in thermal reactors (σa ≈ 2.6×10⁶ barns). In the MSRE, helium sparging stripped noble gases from the salt, cutting Xe-135 poisoning by a factor of about six [1]. Since Xe-135 is removed rather than decaying to Cs-135 in-core, the steady-state xenon reactivity penalty is a small fraction of the solid-fuel value, and xenon-override transients after shutdown are greatly attenuated.
2. **Noble metals.** These plate out on graphite and metal surfaces; the MSRE quantified deposition at gas-liquid, graphite, and metal interfaces [1].
3. **Lanthanides and soluble fission products.** These accumulate in the salt and are removed by a chemical side-stream (fluorination/reductive extraction in the MSBR flowsheet); the processing rate trades capital cost against neutron economy [2].
4. **Protactinium-233.** Continuous Pa extraction (e.g., reductive extraction into liquid bismuth in the MSBR flowsheet) is the step that converts the thorium cycle from converter to breeder: Pa-233 decaying outside the flux loses no neutrons to parasitic capture [9].

The net effect is a poison inventory that is a *controlled variable*, not a cumulative one — the reason MSRE long-term reactivity drift matched calculations [1].

### 4.2 Transient safety: loss-of-heat-sink and loss-of-flow

Transient safety in MSRs follows from three passive mechanisms, all demonstrated or analyzed in the Oak Ridge program:

- **Strong negative temperature feedback.** Any temperature excursion expands the fuel and inserts negative reactivity promptly; the reactor is self-regulating and load-following [1][5].
- **Low operating pressure.** Near-atmospheric operation eliminates the stored-energy driving force for dispersal; no high-pressure blowdown and no zirconium–steam hydrogen generation [6].
- **Freeze-plug drain.** A cooled solid-salt plug melts on overheating or power loss, draining fuel by gravity into subcritical, passively cooled tanks with no operator action or external power [5].

In an unprotected loss-of-heat-sink, salt heat-up inserts negative feedback, driving power toward decay-heat levels; the freeze-plug drain provides ultimate passive termination. On pump trip, slowing circulation raises βeff toward the static value — a stabilizing kinetics change — while the temperature rise inserts negative reactivity. Coupled precursor-transport analyses for the TMSR program confirm benign transients within salt temperature limits [10].

> **Theorem (Circulating-fuel self-regulation, informal).** *In a liquid-fuel reactor with negative isothermal temperature coefficient and gravity drain, any unprotected transient that raises fuel temperature inserts negative reactivity faster than precursor-drift kinetics can add positive reactivity, and the drain system bounds the maximum fuel temperature. Hence the transient terminates passively.*

The feedback acts on the prompt heat-transfer timescale, while precursor redistribution acts on the loop-transit timescale (tens of seconds) — the negative feedback wins the race.

### 4.3 Protactinium management and the breeding ratio

The breeding ratio (BR) — fissile atoms produced per fissile atom consumed — is the figure of merit of the thorium cycle. For a thermal thorium MSR with η ≈ 2.29, leakage plus structural absorption of ~0.15–0.20, and Pa-233 capture suppressed by online removal, BR values of 1.02–1.07 are calculated for thermal designs [9]; the MSBR targeted ~1.06 [2]. The TMSR-LF1, with a conversion ratio of only ~0.1 (it is a converter/test reactor, not a breeder), nevertheless demonstrated the key enabling physics: in October 2024 thorium was added to the operating salt and Pa-233 was detected, proving the breeding chain functions in a modern liquid-fuel system [3][7].

At startup, Pa-233 builds toward equilibrium over several half-lives, absorbing neutrons before its U-233 appears; the reactor must carry excess reactivity until equilibrium is approached. Online Pa removal shortens Pa-233 residence in the flux and flattens this transient.

## 5. Empirical Results and Analysis

### 5.1 MSRE operating record

The MSRE data are the empirical anchor of the field [1][2]:

- **Reliability.** Availability in final runs reached 98.6% on U-235 and 99.9% on U-233 operation [2].
- **Neutronics validation.** Critical loading, reactivity coefficients, dynamics, and long-term reactivity changes all agreed with pre-operational calculations [1].
- **Xenon.** Helium sparging stripped noble gases, cutting Xe-135 poisoning by a factor of ~6 [1].
- **Materials.** Hastelloy-N corrosion was negligible, graphite was unattacked, and the UF4/UF3 redox ratio was maintained [2].
- **Fuel handling.** Uranium additions to the circulating salt during operation were uneventful; recovery by fluorination was efficient [1].
- **Nuclear data.** The MSRE improved the measured U-233 capture-to-fission ratio in a representative MSR spectrum, a basic datum for all subsequent thorium breeder calculations [1].

### 5.2 TMSR-LF1: the modern confirmation

The TMSR-LF1 program has, within two years, reproduced the essential MSRE milestones with modern instrumentation [3][7]:

1. First criticality 11 October 2023 (11:08 local) on HALEU (19.75% U-235) driver fuel in FLiBe [3].
2. Full power (2 MWt) 17 June 2024 [3].
3. License to load thorium granted September 2024; world's first thorium addition to an operating molten salt reactor completed October 2024 [7].
4. Ten days of full-power operation on thorium-bearing salt beginning 8 October 2024, with Pa-233 detected — direct observation of the breeding chain in situ [3].


### 5.3 Analysis: what the data imply

Three inferences follow. First, MSRE agreement between measurement and calculation implies the coupled neutronics/thermal-hydraulics of circulating fluoride fuel is *predictable*; the physics contains no hidden instability. Second, xenon-stripping and online-addition results show the chemical plant and reactor operate as one coupled system without neutronic surprises. Third, the TMSR-LF1 Pa-233 detection closes the loop empirically: the thorium breeding chain functions in a modern liquid-fuel core, moving the breeding-ratio debate from "can it work" to "what BR can an optimized design sustain" [9].

## 6. Limitations

**Materials and corrosion.** Fluoride salts at 650–700 °C are corrosive; the MSRE succeeded with Hastelloy-N under careful redox control, (tellurium-induced cracking required a modified Hastelloy-N composition) [1][2]. Sixty-year life extension of graphite and alloy performance under fast fluence (~3×10²² n/cm² limits studied for TMSR graphite [11]) remains an active qualification problem.

**Tritium.** Li-6 (n,α) reactions generate tritium that permeates hot metals; MSRE-like spray-gas removal captures roughly two-thirds, and engineered barriers are needed to hold permeation near ~10% of the source [12].

**Reprocessing complexity.** Online processing presupposes a continuously operating chemical plant — fluorinators, reductive-extraction contactors, Pa decay tanks — under safeguards. The MSBR flowsheet was judged to have "major drawbacks concerning safety and reprocessing" [9], motivating simplified concepts such as the TMSR.

**Proliferation.** The thorium cycle co-produces U-232 (hard-gamma Tl-208 daughter, an intrinsic barrier), but separated Pa-233 decays to pure U-233, so safeguards-by-design of Pa handling is required [8].

**Licensing.** As the IAEA notes, MSR design-safety standards, fuel-salt transport rules, and supply chains do not yet exist; no liquid-fuel MSR has been licensed commercially [13].

**Modeling gaps.** High-fidelity 3-D transport + CFD + moving-precursor simulations with validated turbulence-chemistry coupling are still maturing; RESTA3D/TMSR-LINK is the current state of practice [10].

## 7. Conclusion

The liquid fuel that enables online reprocessing also transports delayed-neutron precursors — halving βeff — and ties reactivity to salt density through thermal expansion, producing a reactor that is prompt-negative in feedback, self-regulating, and drainable to passive safety. The thorium–U-233 cycle exploits the best η of any thermal fissile nuclide, and the protactinium bottleneck is soluble precisely because the fuel is a liquid: Pa-233 can decay outside the flux.

The MSRE's 13,172 full-power hours and agreement between measurement and calculation proved the concept [1][2]; TMSR-LF1 has re-demonstrated the essentials with Pa-233 detected in situ [3][7].

What remains is industrialization, not discovery: materials qualification, tritium control, a simpler chemical plant, and the licensing framework the IAEA identifies as missing [13]. If those are solved, the liquid-fuel thorium reactor offers a thermal breeder with passive safety, continuous fueling, and a waste stream depleted of long-lived transuranics — the realization, at last, of the promise Oak Ridge demonstrated in 1969.
