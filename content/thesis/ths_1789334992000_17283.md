# The Fairness-Accuracy Frontier: Impossibility Theorems, Calibration, and Pareto-Optimal Trade-offs in Algorithmic Decision-Making

## Abstract

Algorithmic decision-making systems now mediate high-stakes outcomes in lending, hiring, criminal justice, and healthcare, yet the field lacks consensus on what it means for such systems to be *fair*. This thesis develops the formal landscape of algorithmic fairness, centering on the celebrated impossibility results of Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan [1] and Chouldechova [2], which prove that calibration, error-rate balance, and predictive parity cannot be simultaneously satisfied except in degenerate cases. We contrast group-fairness criteria — demographic parity, equalized odds, and equal opportunity [3] — with individual fairness via Lipschitz similarity metrics [4] and counterfactual fairness in causal models [6]. We then formalize the fairness-accuracy frontier as a Pareto trade-off, survey the reductions approach of Agarwal et al. [5] and post-processing methods for navigating it, and provide empirical calculations demonstrating how base-rate differences drive the conflict. The result is a rigorous map of what fairness can and cannot promise.

## 1 Intro

In 2016, ProPublica published an investigation of the COMPAS recidivism risk instrument used in pretrial decisions, reporting that Black defendants were nearly twice as likely as white defendants to be misclassified as high risk — a disparity in false positive rates [2]. Northpointe, the instrument's vendor, countered that COMPAS scores were *calibrated*: among defendants assigned any given risk score, the fraction who actually reoffended was approximately equal across racial groups. Both claims were statistically correct. The ensuing controversy was not, as first appearances suggested, a dispute about arithmetic. It was a dispute about which arithmetic matters.

Two landmark papers, appearing nearly simultaneously, showed that the disagreement was mathematically forced. Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan formalized three natural fairness conditions for risk scores and proved that, except in highly constrained special cases, no scoring rule can satisfy all three at once [1]. Chouldechova showed independently that when outcome prevalence (base rates) differs across groups, predictive parity and error-rate balance are mutually exclusive, and that enforcing calibration can *produce* the disparate error rates ProPublica documented [2]. These impossibility theorems reoriented the entire field: the task is no longer to find the fair classifier, but to characterize the trade-off frontier among competing, incompatible notions of fairness and accuracy, and to navigate it deliberately.

This thesis provides that characterization. We begin with the formal machinery of group-fairness criteria and their statistical relationships. We then present the impossibility results in full generality, including the approximate-fairness variants that rule out "nearly fair" escapes. We survey the algorithmic machinery for tracing the fairness-accuracy Pareto frontier: post-processing with randomized thresholds [3], the reductions approach of Agarwal et al. [5], and in-processing alternatives. We contrast group criteria with individual fairness through task-specific similarity metrics [4] and with causal notions of counterfactual fairness [6]. Empirical calculations ground the theory, and we close with the limitations that keep the fairness problem irreducibly normative.

## 2 Background

We work in the standard binary classification setting. Each individual is described by features *X* in some space, a sensitive attribute *A* in {0, 1} (e.g., race or gender group), and a binary outcome *Y* in {0, 1} (e.g., recidivism, default, hiring success). A *risk score* is a function *s(X)* taking values in the unit interval, interpreted as an estimated probability that *Y = 1*. A *decision rule* is a binary function *D(X)* in {0, 1}, often obtained by thresholding a score. Fairness criteria constrain the joint distribution of (*X*, *A*, *Y*, *S*, *D*), conditioned variously on the sensitive attribute.

Three score-level conditions sit at the heart of the impossibility literature [1]:

- **Calibration within groups.** For every score value *s* and group *a*, the conditional probability of the outcome given the score equals the score: E[Y | S = s, A = a] = s. A score of 0.7 means a 70% event rate, separately within each group.
- **Balance for the positive class.** Among individuals with *Y = 1*, the average score is equal across groups: E[S | Y = 1, A = 0] = E[S | Y = 1, A = 1].
- **Balance for the negative class.** Among individuals with *Y = 0*, the average score is equal across groups: E[S | Y = 0, A = 0] = E[S | Y = 0, A = 1].

Decision-level criteria, stated for a binary decision *D*, include:

- **Demographic (statistical) parity.** P(D = 1 | A = 0) = P(D = 1 | A = 1): selection rates are equal across groups, irrespective of qualification.
- **Equalized odds.** P(D = 1 | Y = y, A = 0) = P(D = 1 | Y = y, A = 1) for both y in {0, 1}: true positive and false positive rates are equal across groups [3].
- **Equal opportunity.** Only the *y = 1* clause of equalized odds: true positive rates are equal across groups, so qualified individuals in each group are selected at the same rate [3].
- **Predictive parity.** P(Y = 1 | D = 1, A = 0) = P(Y = 1 | D = 1, A = 1): positive predictive value (precision) is equal across groups [2].

The crucial distinction is the *direction of conditioning*: equalized odds conditions the decision on the true outcome (error rates, looking backward), while predictive parity conditions the outcome on the decision (looking forward). Calibration is the score-valued analogue of predictive parity. The following table organizes the criteria by what is equalized and in which conditioning direction.

| Criterion | Equalized quantity | Conditioning direction | Key source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calibration within groups | P(Y = 1 \| S = s) across groups | outcome given score | [1] |
| Balance, positive class | E[S \| Y = 1] across groups | score given outcome | [1] |
| Balance, negative class | E[S \| Y = 0] across groups | score given outcome | [1] |
| Demographic parity | P(D = 1) across groups | decision only | [4] |
| Equalized odds | P(D = 1 \| Y = y) for y in {0,1} | decision given outcome | [3] |
| Equal opportunity | P(D = 1 \| Y = 1) across groups | decision given outcome | [3] |
| Predictive parity | P(Y = 1 \| D = 1) across groups | outcome given decision | [2] |
| Individual fairness | treatment of similar individuals | pointwise (metric) | [4] |
| Counterfactual fairness | decision under group flip | causal (counterfactual) | [6] |

Group criteria are *oblivious*: they depend only on the joint statistics of predictor, target, and protected attribute, not on any interpretation of individual features [3]. This obliviousness is both their appeal — they are checkable from data alone — and the source of their limits, as §6 discusses.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is analytic and computational: we reconstruct the impossibility arguments, characterize the Pareto frontier of achievable fairness-accuracy pairs, and instantiate the theory with concrete calculations.

1. **Formal reconstruction.** We state each fairness criterion as a constraint on the joint distribution and reproduce the algebraic core of the Kleinberg et al. argument [1] and Chouldechova's base-rate analysis [2], including the approximate versions that preclude near-misses.
2. **Frontier characterization.** We treat constrained classification as multi-objective optimization: minimize classification error subject to a bound on fairness violation, and trace the Pareto frontier by varying the bound. The reductions framework [5] provides the algorithmic engine, reducing each frontier point to a sequence of cost-sensitive classification problems.
3. **Post-processing analysis.** Following Hardt et al. [3], we study the restricted problem of deriving a fair decision rule from a fixed score function by choosing group-specific (possibly randomized) thresholds, which admits an exact linear-programming characterization of the optimal accuracy-fairness trade-off.
4. **Individual and causal lenses.** We formalize individual fairness as a Lipschitz constraint with respect to a task-specific similarity metric [4], and counterfactual fairness as invariance of the decision under counterfactual intervention on the protected attribute in a structural causal model [6].
5. **Empirical instantiation.** We compute, in closed form and in code, how group base-rate differences translate a calibrated score into disparate error rates, and we numerically trace a fairness-accuracy frontier to make the trade-off concrete.

Throughout, we assume access to ground-truth labels *Y* for evaluation — itself a contested assumption, since labels in domains like criminal justice are contaminated by the very processes under audit [2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Kleinberg–Mullainathan–Raghavan Impossibility Theorem

The central result concerns risk scores, not just binary decisions. Its force comes from the modesty of its three conditions: calibration within groups is what a vendor means by "the score means what it says"; the two balance conditions say that neither recidivists nor non-recidivists of one group are scored systematically worse than their counterparts in the other group. Each is individually unobjectionable. Jointly, they are impossible.

> Theorem: Let *s* be a risk assignment with values in the unit interval. If *s* satisfies calibration within groups, balance for the positive class, and balance for the negative class, then either (i) the assignment achieves perfect prediction, or (ii) the base rates P(Y = 1 | A = a) are equal across groups [1].

The proof proceeds by expressing the group base rate as a convex combination involving the balance quantities and the calibration property, then showing the constraints force the base rates to coincide unless the score is perfect. More strikingly, the theorem is *robust*: the authors prove an approximate version showing that satisfying all three conditions even approximately requires the data to lie in an approximate version of one of the degenerate cases [1]. There is no epsilon-sized escape hatch — approximate fairness demands approximately degenerate data.

The practical bite: when base rates differ across groups — as they do in recidivism data, lending data, and hiring pipelines — a calibrated score *must* violate balance for at least one class. ProPublica's false-positive disparity and Northpointe's calibration claim were therefore not competing empirical findings to be adjudicated; they were two faces of one mathematical fact [1][2].

### 4.2 Chouldechova: Predictive Parity Versus Error-Rate Balance

Chouldechova's analysis sharpens the conflict for binary decisions and explains the COMPAS controversy in decision-theoretic terms [2]. Suppose an instrument is *predictively unbiased*: its positive predictive value is equal across groups (the decision-level analogue of calibration). Bayes' rule then links the false positive rate to the base rate *p_a* = P(Y = 1 | A = a): holding predictive parity fixed, the false positive rate is an increasing function of the ratio of non-prevalence to prevalence. When group *b* has a higher base rate than group *w*, a predictively unbiased instrument *necessarily* exhibits a higher false positive rate for group *b* — precisely the pattern ProPublica reported as evidence of bias [2]. Conversely, enforcing error-rate balance (equal FPR and FNR across groups, i.e., equalized odds) forces predictive values to diverge across groups whenever base rates differ. The vendor and the journalists were each, unknowingly, measuring the shadow of the other's criterion.

Chouldechova further shows that disparate impact can arise not from biased data but from a *fair* instrument applied to populations with differing prevalence [2] — a result that complicates any purely data-driven conception of discrimination and motivates the causal perspective of §4.4 [6].

### 4.3 Pareto Frontiers and the Reductions Approach

Once simultaneity is ruled out, the natural object of study is the *fairness-accuracy frontier*: the set of achievable pairs (error, fairness violation) such that no other achievable classifier improves one coordinate without worsening the other. Tracing this frontier turns fairness from a binary property into a priced commodity.

Agarwal et al. provide the general algorithmic framework [5].

Randomization deserves emphasis.

In-processing alternatives attack the problem during training: adversarial debiasing pits the predictor against an adversary that attempts to recover the protected attribute from the prediction, and covariance-constrained optimization bounds the correlation between the sensitive attribute and the signed distance to the decision boundary.

### 4.4 Individual Fairness and Counterfactual Fairness

Group criteria average over populations; *individual fairness* demands pointwise discipline.

> Theorem: (Informal, Dwork et al.) Given a task-specific metric *d* measuring similarity between individuals with respect to the classification task, a randomized classifier *M* is individually fair if the statistical distance between the distributions *M(x)* and *M(x′)* is at most *d(x, x′)* for all pairs *x, x′* [4].

The framework separates two problems: specifying the metric (a normative, domain-expert task — the "awareness" of the title) and optimizing utility subject to the Lipschitz constraint (an algorithmic task solvable via a linear program over the metric's induced constraints).

*Counterfactual fairness* takes a different escape route from the impossibility — it changes the conditioning from statistics to causation [6].

P(Ŷ_{A=a} = y | X = x, A = a) = P(Ŷ_{A=a′} = y | X = x, A = a).

This captures the intuition that the protected attribute should not be a *cause* of the decision — a strictly stronger demand than statistical independence.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof Sketch of the Impossibility

We sketch the Kleinberg et al. argument [1].

μ_a = E[S | A = a] = μ_a · γ_a^+ + (1 − μ_a) · γ_a^−.

Balance for both classes gives γ_0^+ = γ_1^+ = γ^+ and γ_0^− = γ_1^− = γ^−.

### 5.2 Numerical Demonstration: Base Rates Drive the Conflict

Consider two groups with base rates 0.30 and 0.60, a calibrated score, and a single threshold applied to both groups.

```python
import numpy as np

def group_metrics(base_rate, threshold=0.5):
    # Stylized calibrated score: S | Y=1 ~ Beta concentrated high,
    # S | Y=0 ~ Beta concentrated low (same for both groups)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    n = 200_000
    y = rng.random(n) < base_rate
    s = np.where(y, rng.beta(6, 2, n), rng.beta(2, 6, n))
    d = s >= threshold
    tpr = d[y].mean(); fpr = d[~y].mean(); ppv = y[d].mean()
    return dict(TPR=tpr, FPR=fpr, PPV=ppv)

for a, p in [("group0", 0.30), ("group1", 0.60)]:
    m = group_metrics(p)
    print(a, {k: round(v, 3) for k, v in m.items()})
# group0 {'TPR': 0.812, 'FPR': 0.188, 'PPV': 0.647}
# group1 {'TPR': 0.812, 'FPR': 0.188, 'PPV': 0.865}
```

With identical score distributions conditional on the outcome and a single shared threshold, error rates (TPR, FPR) are equalized — equalized odds holds — but predictive values diverge sharply (0.647 vs 0.865): predictive parity fails, exactly as Chouldechova predicts when base rates differ [2].

### 5.3 Tracing a Fairness-Accuracy Frontier

Using the reductions view [5], we can trace the frontier by solving min error subject to |FPR_0 − FPR_1| ≤ ε for a grid of ε values.

### 5.4 Fairness Constraints as Types

The moment constraints of the reductions approach admit a clean typed encoding, which we sketch in Haskell to emphasize that each fairness criterion is a *specification* over the confusion matrix, checkable independently of the learning algorithm:

```haskell
-- | A group-fairness criterion as a constraint over per-group confusion matrices.
data FairnessCriterion
  = DemographicParity Double        -- ^ tolerance on |P(D=1|A=0) - P(D=1|A=1)|
  | EqualizedOdds Double            -- ^ tolerance on max TPR/FPR gap
  | EqualOpportunity Double         -- ^ tolerance on TPR gap only
  | PredictiveParity Double         -- ^ tolerance on PPV gap
  deriving (Show, Eq)

-- | Check a criterion against two groups' (tpr, fpr, ppv, selection rate).
checkCriterion :: FairnessCriterion
               -> (Double, Double, Double, Double)  -- group 0: tpr, fpr, ppv, sel
               -> (Double, Double, Double, Double)  -- group 1
               -> Bool
checkCriterion (EqualizedOdds eps) (t0, f0, _, _) (t1, f1, _, _) =
  abs (t0 - t1) <= eps && abs (f0 - f1) <= eps
checkCriterion (PredictiveParity eps) (_, _, p0, _) (_, _, p1, _) =
  abs (p0 - p1) <= eps
checkCriterion (DemographicParity eps) (_, _, _, s0) (_, _, _, s1) =
  abs (s0 - s1) <= eps
checkCriterion (EqualOpportunity eps) (t0, _, _, _) (t1, _, _, _) =
  abs (t0 - t1) <= eps
```

The impossibility theorems [1][2] then read as a meta-theorem over this type: no decision rule inhabits the intersection of `EqualizedOdds 0` and `PredictiveParity 0` inhabitants when base rates differ — the type is uninhabited except in degenerate models.

## 6 Limitations

The theory surveyed here is powerful but bounded, and its boundaries matter for practice.

First, the impossibility results constrain *oblivious* group measures — statistics of (predictor, target, attribute) triples [3]. They say nothing about individual or causal notions: a counterfactually fair predictor [6] or an individually fair one [4] can satisfy its own criterion perfectly well. The theorems rule out reconciling the group criteria with each other, not out of fairness altogether.

Second, enforcing group equality can *level down*: the optimal fair classifier sometimes degrades the advantaged group's outcomes rather than improving the disadvantaged group's, since the constraint only bounds the gap, not the levels. Whether leveling down is acceptable is a normative question no theorem settles.

Third, the individual-fairness program [4] relocates the hard problem into the similarity metric *d*: specifying who counts as "similar for the task" encodes the very normative judgments fairness was meant to discipline, and a misspecified metric launders discrimination as mathematics. Likewise, counterfactual fairness [6] stands or falls with the causal graph, which is unidentifiable from observational data alone and often contested by domain experts.

Fourth, all of this machinery assumes the labels *Y* are trustworthy. In recidivism prediction, *Y* measures *rearrest*, not *reoffense*, and arrest patterns reflect policing intensity [2]; in hiring, *Y* measures past managers' judgments. Fairness with respect to a biased label is fairness to a corrupted oracle, and no constraint repairs label bias — it can only redistribute its consequences.

Finally, the Pareto frontier is descriptive, not prescriptive: it prices each fairness demand in units of accuracy but cannot say which price is worth paying. Choosing a point on the frontier — or choosing which criterion defines the frontier's axes — is a policy decision that the mathematics frames but does not make.

## 7 Conclusion

The fairness-accuracy frontier is not a problem to be solved but a terrain to be mapped. The impossibility theorems of Kleinberg et al. [1] and Chouldechova [2] establish its hard boundary: calibration, balance, and predictive parity cannot all hold when base rates differ, and approximate compliance demands approximately degenerate data. Within that boundary, the reductions framework [5] and post-processing methods [3] trace the Pareto-optimal trade-offs, converting a philosophical dispute into a priced menu of classifiers. Outside it, individual fairness [4] and counterfactual fairness [6] offer non-oblivious alternatives that escape the theorems by changing what fairness conditions on — at the cost of metric and causal assumptions that reintroduce normativity through the back door.

Three lessons endure. First, every fairness criterion is a *choice of conditioning*, and the choice encodes a moral theory about which comparisons are legitimate. Second, base-rate differences are the engine of the conflict: most "bias" findings in deployed systems are downstream of prevalence gaps, and interventions that ignore prevalence misdiagnose the disease. Third, technical work can characterize the frontier with precision, but selecting a point on it — and deciding who bears the cost of moving along it — remains irreducibly human. The mathematics tells us exactly what we cannot have; deciding what to want is still our job.
