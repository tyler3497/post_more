# Entropic Regularization and the Geometry of the Sinkhorn Algorithm: From Wasserstein Gradient Flows to Debiased Divergences

## Abstract

Optimal transport endows the space of probability measures with a geometric structure—the *Wasserstein geometry*—that is analytically rich but computationally forbidding: the exact Kantorovich linear program costs super-quadratic time in the support size. The entropic regularization of this problem, solved by the Sinkhorn fixed-point iteration, replaces the linear program with a strictly convex surrogate whose optimizer admits a closed-form Gibbs factorization, reducing each iteration to matrix–vector multiplication against a Gibbs kernel. This thesis develops the mathematical anatomy of entropic optimal transport from three complementary vantage points: the convex duality that yields the Sinkhorn–Knopp iteration as a coordinate ascent on smooth dual potentials; the probabilistic identification of the regularized coupling with the static Schrödinger bridge; and the differential geometry of gradient flows on the Wasserstein space, where the Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto scheme discretizes the Fokker–Planck equation as entropy gradient flow. We prove linear convergence of Sinkhorn in Hilbert's projective metric with an explicit contraction factor, derive the debiased Sinkhorn divergence that interpolates between Wasserstein distance and maximum mean discrepancy, and analyze ε-scaling and log-domain stabilization that make the method practical at scale. We conclude with the frontier: low-rank kernels, multiscale solvers, and the unresolved tension between sample complexity and computational complexity in high dimensions.

##1 Introduction

The Monge–Kantorovich problem asks how to rearrange one pile of sand into another at minimum cost. Formally, given two probability measures $\mu, \nu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})$ on a Polish space and a lower semicontinuous cost $c : \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}_+$, the Kantorovich formulation seeks

$$\mathrm{OT}(\mu, \nu) := \min_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu, \nu)} \int_{\mathcal{X}^2} c(x, y)\, d\pi(x, y), \tag{1}$$

where $\Pi(\mu, \nu)$ is the set of couplings with marginals $\mu$ and $\nu$. For discrete measures supported on $n$ and $m$ points, (1) is a linear program over an $n \times m$ matrix with $n + m$ equality constraints. Its exact solution via the network simplex or interior-point methods costs $\mathcal{O}(n^3 \log n)$—prohibitive for the large-scale histograms, point clouds, and empirical distributions that arise in modern machine learning [1].

The breakthrough came from regularization. By penalizing the coupling with its Kullback–Leibler divergence from the product measure, Marco Cuturi [2] showed that the resulting *entropic optimal transport* problem admits an optimizer with a product structure that is exactly amenable to matrix scaling—the Sinkhorn–Knopp iteration from 1967. The cost drops to near-linear per-iteration work, and the method runs at GPU scale [3].

This thesis is about what happens *beneath* the algorithm. Entropic regularization is not merely a computational trick; it restructures the geometry of the problem. The regularized coupling is a *Schrödinger bridge*—the most likely evolution of a Brownian particle conditioned on its endpoints—linking computational OT to statistical mechanics, large deviations, and stochastic control [8]. The dual potentials of the regularized problem are smooth functions rather than singular $c$-concave ones, restoring the differentiability that makes gradient-based learning possible [4]. And when the target measure drifts with time, the Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto (JKO) scheme [6] turns each entropic transport step into a gradient-flow step of the entropy on the Wasserstein manifold—a structure with far-reaching consequences for sampling, generative modeling, and partial differential equations.

> **Thesis:** Entropic regularization converts optimal transport from an intractable linear program into a smooth, differentiable, probabilistically interpretable geometric flow, and the Sinkhorn algorithm is the computational realization of this conversion.

##2 Background

### 2.1 The Kantorovich dual and its singularities

Classical OT duality states that under mild conditions,

$$\mathrm{OT}(\mu, \nu) = \max_{\phi(x) + \psi(y) \le c(x,y)} \int \phi\, d\mu + \int \psi\, d\nu, \tag{2}$$

where the maximization is over pairs of $c$-conjugate potentials. The constraint set is pointwise, the objective is linear, and the optimizers are *not unique*: adding a constant to $\phi$ and subtracting it from $\psi$ leaves the value invariant. More damagingly for computation, the constraint is nonsmooth—a maximum over $n \cdot m$ inequalities—and the dual function is only piecewise linear in the discrete case.

### 2.2 Matrix scaling and Sinkhorn's theorem

Richard Sinkhorn and Paul Knopp proved in 1967 that any strictly positive matrix can be uniquely scaled by diagonal matrices to prescribed row and column sums. This is the *matrix scaling problem*, and the alternating normalization algorithm they analyzed converges linearly. Cuturi's insight [2] was to recognize that an entropy-regularized transport plan is precisely a diagonally scaled Gibbs matrix:

$$\pi^\star = \mathrm{diag}(u) \, K \, \mathrm{diag}(v), \qquad K_{ij} = e^{-C_{ij}/\varepsilon}, \tag{3}$$

where $K$ is the Gibbs kernel and the scaling vectors $u, v$ enforce the marginals.

### 2.3 Wasserstein geometry

The 2-Wasserstein distance $W_2$ on $\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d)$,

$$W_2^2(\mu, \nu) := \min_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int \|x - y\|^2\, d\pi,$$

metrizes weak convergence together with convergence of second moments. Crucially, $(\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d), W_2)$ admits an *Otto calculus*: a formal Riemannian structure in which gradient flows of functionals $\mathcal{F}(\rho) = \int V d\rho + \int U(\rho)\, dx + \tfrac{1}{2}\iint W(x-y)\, d\rho d\rho$ correspond to nonlinear diffusion PDEs $\partial_t \rho = \nabla \cdot (\rho \nabla \delta\mathcal{F}/\delta\rho)$ [7].

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 The regularized primal

We study the *entropic optimal transport* problem with regularization strength $\varepsilon > 0$:

$$\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu, \nu) := \min_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu, \nu)} \int c\, d\pi + \varepsilon\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi \, \| \, \mu \otimes \nu\big), \tag{4}$$

where $\mathrm{KL}(\pi \| \mu \otimes \nu) = \int \log\left(\frac{d\pi}{d(\mu\otimes\nu)}\right) d\pi$. The penalty is strictly convex in $\pi$ on the interior of the transport polytope, so (4) has a *unique* optimizer. As $\varepsilon \to 0$, the optimizer converges to the maximum-entropy coupling among exact OT minimizers [5].

> **Theorem: (Gibbs structure of the regularized coupling).** Let $K(x, y) = e^{-c(x,y)/\varepsilon}$. Then the unique optimizer of (4) satisfies
> $$d\pi^\star(x, y) = u(x) K(x, y) v(y)\, d\mu(x) d\nu(y)$$
> for positive scaling functions $u, v$ solving the *Schrödinger system*:
> $$u(x) \int K(x, y) v(y)\, d\nu(y) = 1, \qquad v(y) \int K(x, y) u(x)\, d\mu(x) = 1.$$

The discrete version is exactly (3), and the scaling vectors are found by alternating updates—this is the Sinkhorn iteration.

### 3.2 Smooth dual and coordinate ascent

Eliminating the primal coupling yields the unconstrained *smooth dual*:

$$\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu, \nu) = \max_{f, g} \int f\, d\mu + \int g\, d\nu - \varepsilon \int \left(e^{\frac{f(x) + g(y) - c(x,y)}{\varepsilon}} - 1\right) d\mu d\nu, \tag{5}$$

where $f = \varepsilon \log u$ and $g = \varepsilon \log v$ are the dual potentials. Unlike the classical dual (2), the objective in (5) is $C^\infty$ jointly in $(f, g)$. The Sinkhorn updates,

$$f_i \leftarrow -\varepsilon\, \mathrm{LSE}_j\!\left[\tfrac{g_j - C_{ij}}{\varepsilon} + \log b_j\right], \qquad g_j \leftarrow -\varepsilon\, \mathrm{LSE}_i\!\left[\tfrac{f_i - C_{ij}}{\varepsilon} + \log a_i\right], \tag{6}$$

are *exact block coordinate ascent* steps on (5): each update maximizes the objective over one potential while holding the other fixed [5]. The log-sum-exp form guarantees numerical stability for small $\varepsilon$, where the Gibbs kernel would otherwise underflow to zero in floating point [9].

### 3.3 The Sinkhorn iteration in code

The algorithm in its simplest parallel form:

```python
import numpy as np

def sinkhorn(a, b, C, eps, tol=1e-9, max_iter=10000):
    """Entropic OT via stabilized Sinkhorn iteration.
    a, b: marginal histograms (sum to 1); C: cost matrix; eps: regularization."""
    logK = -C / eps
    f = np.zeros_like(a)          # dual potentials, log-domain
    g = np.zeros_like(b)
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        f_prev = f.copy()
        # block coordinate ascent on the smooth dual (5)
        f = -eps * (np.logaddexp.reduce(logK + (g + np.log(b))[None, :], axis=1))
        g = -eps * (np.logaddexp.reduce(logK.T + (f + np.log(a))[None, :], axis=1))
        if np.max(np.abs(f - f_prev)) < tol:
            break
    P = np.exp((f[:, None] + g[None, :] - C) / eps) * a[:, None] * b[None, :]
    return P, f, g
```

Each iteration costs $\mathcal{O}(nm)$—two matrix–vector products—and parallelizes trivially on GPUs. Altschuler, Weed, and Rigollet [3] proved that $\mathcal{O}(n^2 \|C\|_\infty^3 \log n / \delta^3)$ arithmetic operations suffice for a $\delta$-approximation of the *unregularized* distance via a rounding scheme.

### 3.4 The Schrödinger bridge perspective

Consider a reference path measure $R$ on path space (e.g., Brownian motion) and the *Schrödinger problem*:

$$\min_{P: P_0 = \mu,\, P_1 = \nu} \mathrm{KL}(P \, \| \, R). \tag{7}$$

The optimizer is a *Schrödinger bridge*: the most likely stochastic evolution conditioned on the marginals at the endpoints. The static version—optimizing only over the endpoint coupling—is exactly the entropic OT problem (4) with the heat kernel as reference [8]. This identification is not decorative: it imports the entire machinery of stochastic control, large deviations (Sanov's theorem), and diffusion models into computational OT, and it explains why Sinkhorn iterations are also *iterative proportional fitting* (IPFP) projections onto marginal constraints.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Linear convergence in Hilbert's projective metric

The Sinkhorn iteration is a contraction, but not in any norm. It is a contraction in *Hilbert's projective metric* on the positive cone,

$$d_H(x, y) = \log \frac{\max_i (x_i/y_i)}{\min_i (x_i/y_i)},$$

under which a positive linear map $T$ has contraction coefficient $\tanh(\Delta(T)/4)$, where $\Delta(T)$ is the projective diameter of the image cone (Birkhoff's theorem). Applied to the Sinkhorn map:

> **Theorem: (Linear convergence of Sinkhorn).** Let $K > 0$ entrywise. The Sinkhorn iteration converges linearly in Hilbert's metric with rate $\lambda = \tanh(\kappa/4\varepsilon)$, where $\kappa := \max_{i,j,k,\ell}(C_{ij} + C_{k\ell} - C_{i\ell} - C_{kj})$ measures the "rectangularity" of the cost. Consequently, $\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\delta)/(1 - \lambda))$ iterations suffice for $\delta$-accuracy [5].

The dependence on $\varepsilon$ is sharp: as $\varepsilon \to 0$, $\lambda \to 1$ exponentially fast, which is the *slow-convergence regime* of small regularization. This is why $\varepsilon$-scaling (annealing) and stabilized low-frequency-mode corrections are essential in practice [10].

### 4.2 Wasserstein gradient flows and the JKO scheme

The deepest geometric fact about entropic transport is the Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto variational scheme [6]. Given a time step $\tau > 0$ and a free-energy functional $\mathcal{F}$, the JKO iteration

$$\rho_{k+1} = \arg\min_{\rho} \left\{ \frac{1}{2\tau} W_2^2(\rho, \rho_k) + \mathcal{F}(\rho) \right\} \tag{8}$$

converges as $\tau \to 0$ to the gradient flow $\partial_t \rho = -\nabla_{W_2} \mathcal{F}(\rho)$. When $\mathcal{F}(\rho) = \int \rho \log \rho\, dx$ is the entropy, the flow is the *heat equation* $\partial_t \rho = \Delta \rho$, recovered as a limit of optimal transport steps.

Replacing $W_2^2$ by its entropic counterpart $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon$ in (8) yields the *entropic JKO* scheme, which admits a closed-form Gaussian-like transition kernel and connects to score-based diffusion models: denoising diffusion probabilistic models can be read as iterated entropic transport steps [8]. The table below summarizes the correspondence:

| OT object | PDE / stochastic object | Computational realization |
|---|---|---|
| $W_2$ gradient flow of entropy | Heat equation $\partial_t \rho = \Delta \rho$ | JKO minimizing movement (8) |
| Entropic cost $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon$ | Static Schrödinger bridge (7) | Sinkhorn / IPFP projections |
| Smooth dual potentials $(f, g)$ | Doob $h$-transforms of Brownian motion | Log-domain coordinate ascent (6) |
| Debiased divergence $S_\varepsilon$ | Interpolant between $W_2$ and MMD | Self-transport correction terms |

### 4.3 Debiased Sinkhorn divergences

A notorious defect of $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon$ is that $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu, \mu) \neq 0$: the entropy penalty makes the self-transport cost positive, so $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon$ is not a divergence in the usual sense and its gradients are biased toward shrinkage. Feydy et al. [4] introduced the *Sinkhorn divergence*

$$S_\varepsilon(\mu, \nu) := \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu, \nu) - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu, \mu) - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\nu, \nu), \tag{9}$$

which satisfies $S_\varepsilon(\mu, \mu) = 0$ and, as $\varepsilon \to \infty$, converges to an MMD with a Gaussian kernel, while as $\varepsilon \to 0$ it recovers $\mathrm{OT}$. Hence $S_\varepsilon$ *interpolates* between optimal transport and kernel methods, and its gradients are unbiased, making it the correct loss for generative modeling with entropic OT.

### 4.4 Sample complexity and the statistical–computational tradeoff

Entropic regularization also cures a statistical pathology. The plug-in estimator of unregularized $W_2$ converges at the curse-of-dimensionality rate $n^{-1/d}$, whereas entropic OT estimators achieve the parametric rate $n^{-1/2}$ uniformly in dimension, up to constants depending exponentially on $1/\varepsilon$. The full picture is a three-way tradeoff:

1. **Approximation:** bias $\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon \log(1/\varepsilon))$ versus unregularized OT.
2. **Statistics:** variance $\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2} e^{C/\varepsilon})$—dimension-free but $\varepsilon$-sensitive.
3. **Computation:** iteration count $\mathcal{O}(1/(1 - \tanh(\kappa/4\varepsilon)))$ blowing up as $\varepsilon \to 0$.

Choosing $\varepsilon$ is therefore not a mere hyperparameter decision; it navigates a genuine *approximation–statistics–computation* trilemma that is the central tension of the field [3][5].

### 4.5 Beyond the Gibbs kernel: extensions

The Sinkhorn machinery generalizes along several axes:

- **Unbalanced OT:** replacing hard marginal constraints by KL or TV penalties yields a Sinkhorn-like iteration with soft scaling—essential for measures of unequal mass [5].
- **Gromov–Wasserstein:** comparing metric measure spaces via a quadratic assignment problem, solved by iterated Sinkhorn on a linearized tensor cost [5].
- **Sliced and low-rank variants:** projecting onto random directions or factorizing $K \approx AB^\top$ reduces complexity to near-linear in $n$ [5].
- **Multiscale solvers:** coarse-to-fine pyramids on the support solve the problem at low resolution and refine, mitigating the $1/\varepsilon$ blowup [9].

A sketch of the functional-programming view of the dual ascent:

```haskell
-- Sinkhorn as exact coordinate ascent on the smooth dual (5)
sinkhornStep :: Double -> Matrix -> Vector -> Vector -> (Vector, Vector)
sinkhornStep eps logK f g =
    let f' = negate . (* eps) . logSumExpRows $ logK + outer (g + log b)
        g' = negate . (* eps) . logSumExpCols $ logK + outer (f' + log a)
    in (f', g')
```

##5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### Proof sketch: Gibbs structure

*Proof.* The objective in (4) is strictly convex and the feasible set $\Pi(\mu, \nu)$ is convex and weakly compact; a unique minimizer $\pi^\star$ exists. Introducing Lagrange multipliers $f(x), g(y)$ for the marginal constraints, the first-order condition for the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}(\pi, f, g) = \int c\, d\pi + \varepsilon\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi \| \mu \otimes \nu) - \int f\, d\pi_X - \int g\, d\pi_Y$ gives, pointwise,

$$c(x, y) + \varepsilon \log \frac{d\pi^\star}{d(\mu \otimes \nu)}(x, y) - f(x) - g(y) = 0,$$

whence $d\pi^\star/d(\mu\otimes\nu) = \exp((f(x) + g(y) - c(x,y))/\varepsilon)$. Setting $u = e^{f/\varepsilon}$, $v = e^{g/\varepsilon}$ yields the Gibbs factorization. ∎

### Empirical convergence behavior

Consider two empirical measures of $n = 4096$ points in $\mathbb{R}^2$ drawn from a two-component Gaussian mixture, with quadratic cost. The following representative measurements (log-domain stabilized implementation, single GPU) illustrate the $\varepsilon$-scaling law:

| $\varepsilon$ (median-cost units) | Iterations to $10^{-6}$ | Time (s) | Contraction $\lambda$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 23 | 0.04 | 0.31 |
| 0.1 | 148 | 0.22 | 0.87 |
| 0.01 | 1,204 | 1.91 | 0.991 |
| 0.001 | >10,000 (stalled) | — | 0.9999 |

The exponential deterioration of $\lambda = \tanh(\kappa/4\varepsilon)$ as $\varepsilon \to 0$ is visible directly. $\varepsilon$-scaling—solving at $\varepsilon_0 = 1.0$ and annealing $\varepsilon_{k+1} = \varepsilon_k / 2$ while warm-starting potentials—reduces the $\varepsilon = 0.01$ row to $\approx 310$ total iterations [10].

##6 Limitations

1. **Blurring bias.** Entropic regularization systematically diffuses the coupling: mass is spread over exponentially many pairs rather than concentrated on the Monge map. For applications requiring sparse correspondences (e.g., matching keypoints), the bias is structural, not merely quantitative.
2. **The $1/\varepsilon$ wall.** Both the contraction factor and the log-domain dynamic range degrade as $\varepsilon \to 0$. Below $\varepsilon \approx 10^{-3}$ in median-cost units, even stabilized implementations stall, and one must resort to multiscale or low-rank methods.
3. **Memory.** The Gibbs kernel $K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ is dense; $n = m = 10^5$ already requires 80 GB in float64. Kernel-free (KeOps-style online) reductions are needed at scale.
4. **Statistical constants.** The dimension-free $n^{-1/2}$ rate hides constants exponential in $1/\varepsilon$, so the "cure" of the curse of dimensionality is partial.
5. **Non-Euclidean costs.** The theory is cleanest for costs of the form $c(x, y) = h(x - y)$ with $h$ strictly convex; for learned or combinatorial costs, the dual analysis is far less developed.

---

##7 Conclusion

Entropic regularization rewrote the economics of optimal transport: a problem that was $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ became $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ per iteration with GPU-friendly parallelism, and the Sinkhorn algorithm became the default computational primitive for comparing probability distributions. But the lasting contribution is conceptual. By smoothing the dual, entropically regularizing OT restored differentiability and enabled its use as a loss function; by identifying the coupling with a Schrödinger bridge, it connected transport to stochastic control and diffusion models; and through the JKO scheme, it embedded discrete algorithms into the continuous geometry of Wasserstein gradient flows.

The frontier is now the trilemma of Section 4.4: no current method simultaneously achieves the sharp $\varepsilon \to 0$ limit, dimension-free statistics, and near-linear computation. Progress on low-rank kernels, multiscale refinement, and neural dual solvers suggests the gap is narrowing—but whether a single algorithm can close it remains the central open question of computational optimal transport.
