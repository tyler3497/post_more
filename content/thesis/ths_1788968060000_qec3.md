# Decoding the Surface Code: Minimum-Weight Perfect Matching, Union-Find, and Belief Propagation in Comparative Perspective

## Abstract

Quantum error correction converts the fragile physical qubit into a robust logical qubit by spreading information across many physical degrees of freedom, and decoding is the classical control loop that makes fault tolerance possible. This thesis presents a unified treatment of the three principal decoder families for the surface code: minimum-weight perfect matching (MWPM), the union–find decoder, and belief-propagation-based decoders. We develop each algorithm from its statistical-inference foundations, analyze its computational complexity and threshold behavior under code-capacity, phenomenological, and circuit-level noise models, and compare them across the dimensions that matter for near-term and large-scale architectures: decoding accuracy, worst-case and average-case runtime, parallelizability, and hardware implementability. MWPM achieves the highest thresholds but at super-linear cost, union–find delivers almost-linear scaling with a modest threshold penalty, and belief propagation with ordered-statistics post-processing bridges the gap when enhanced with degeneracy-aware techniques. We close by analyzing real-time decoding budgets for superconducting processors, where microsecond syndrome rates make latency, not asymptotics, the decisive constraint.

## 1. Introduction

The threshold theorem of fault-tolerant quantum computation [1] guarantees that arbitrarily long quantum computations are possible provided the physical error rate sits below a constant threshold and the classical decoding problem can be solved efficiently. Yet the theorem's promise hinges on a subtle operational detail: every round of syndrome extraction must be *decoded* — the classical processor must convert a stream of parity-check outcomes into a correction that returns the system to the code space — and this decoding must keep pace with the quantum hardware. On a superconducting processor running surface-code cycles at roughly one megahertz, a decoder that requires milliseconds per round is useless no matter how theoretically elegant it is [2].

The surface code, introduced in the topological-code framework of Dennis *et al.* [1] and developed into a practical architecture by Fowler *et al.* [2], is the leading candidate for fault-tolerant quantum memory. Its appeal rests on three pillars: nearest-neighbor interactions on a planar grid, a high threshold under realistic noise, and decoders that reduce to tractable classical problems. Decoding the surface code is a problem of statistical inference on a graphical model: given observed syndrome defects, find the error — or equivalence class of errors — maximizing the posterior probability. Exact maximum-likelihood decoding is #P-complete in general [5], so practical decoding is a study in principled approximation.

Three decoder families dominate the landscape. **Minimum-weight perfect matching** (MWPM) treats each syndrome defect as a vertex and finds the most probable pairing via the Blossom algorithm; it was the first decoder demonstrated at scale and remains the accuracy benchmark [2][6]. The **union–find decoder**, introduced by Delfosse and Nickerson [3], grows clusters around defects and peels them in almost-linear time, trading a small threshold penalty for dramatic speed. **Belief-propagation decoders** propagate marginal probabilities on the Tanner graph and, augmented with ordered-statistics decoding (OSD), approach maximum-likelihood accuracy on codes where degeneracy defeats naive message passing [4].

This thesis unifies these families within a single inference framework, derives their algorithms from first principles, and evaluates them head-to-head. Our contributions are:

- A common statistical formulation of surface-code decoding as inference over error cosets, from which MWPM, union–find, and belief propagation emerge as distinct approximation strategies.
- Self-contained algorithmic expositions with complexity analysis, including Fowler's parallel MWPM construction [6] and the cluster-growth mechanics of union–find [3].
- A controlled numerical study on rotated planar codes of distance 5 through 25 under three noise models, measuring logical error rates, thresholds, and runtime scaling.
- An engineering analysis of real-time decoding budgets for megahertz-rate syndrome extraction, identifying where each decoder family fits in the roadmap to large-scale fault tolerance.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The surface code

The surface code is a topological stabilizer code defined on a two-dimensional lattice. Physical qubits sit on the edges (or, in the rotated layout, the vertices) of the grid, and the stabilizer group is generated by *plaquette* operators $B_p = \prod_{i \in p} Z_i$ and *star* (vertex) operators $A_s = \prod_{i \in s} X_i$, each acting on four neighboring qubits in the bulk and three or two on boundaries [1]. The code space is the simultaneous $+1$ eigenspace of all stabilizers; a code patch of linear size $d$ encodes one logical qubit with distance $d$, meaning any logical operator has weight at least $d$.

Two boundary types complete the construction. *Rough* boundaries terminate $X$-type stabilizers and allow $Z$ logical strings to end; *smooth* boundaries do the reverse [1]. In the rotated surface code, the lattice is tilted 45 degrees so that both boundary types coexist on a compact patch using $d^2$ data qubits and $d^2 - 1$ syndrome qubits, roughly halving the qubit overhead of the unrotated layout [2].

A crucial property is that $X$ errors and $Z$ errors decouple: $X$ errors anticommute only with $Z$-type (plaquette) stabilizers, producing *defects* (syndrome $-1$ outcomes) on plaquettes, while $Z$ errors produce defects on stars. $Y$ errors produce both. Decoding therefore reduces, to first order, to two independent classical problems — one on each syndrome subgraph — each of which is: given a set of defect vertices on a graph, find the most likely set of error chains producing them.

### 2.2 Decoding as statistical inference

Let $E$ be a Pauli error and $s(E)$ its syndrome. The posterior distribution over errors factorizes over independent noise as

$$P(E \mid s) \propto P(s \mid E)\, P(E) = \left[\prod_i p_i^{w_i(E)} (1-p_i)^{1-w_i(E)}\right] \cdot \mathbb{1}[s(E) = s],$$

where $p_i$ is the per-qubit error probability. The **maximum-likelihood decoder** selects the single most probable error consistent with $s$; the **degenerate maximum-likelihood decoder** instead selects the most probable *coset* $E \cdot \mathcal{S}$ (error modulo stabilizers), summing probabilities over all stabilizer-equivalent errors [5]. Degeneracy matters enormously for quantum codes: many distinct error patterns are logically equivalent, and a decoder that ignores this can pick a correction that is individually likely yet belongs to a low-weight logical class.

> **Theorem (Hardness of exact decoding):** Maximum-likelihood decoding of a general stabilizer code is #P-complete, and even approximating it is NP-hard in the worst case [5].

For the surface code, however, the planar graph structure admits efficient algorithms. The syndrome defects always come in pairs (each error chain has two endpoints, possibly on a boundary), and pairing defects by shortest paths on the lattice converts the problem into **minimum-weight perfect matching** on a complete graph — solvable in polynomial time by Edmonds' Blossom algorithm [2].

### 2.3 Noise models

We consider three standard noise models of increasing realism [2][4]:

- **Code-capacity noise:** perfect syndrome measurement; each data qubit suffers depolarizing noise with probability $p$. This isolates decoder quality from measurement faults.
- **Phenomenological noise:** data errors plus independent syndrome-measurement errors, each with probability $p$. Decoding must now operate on a *spacetime* syndrome volume of $d$ rounds, matching defects in three dimensions.
- **Circuit-level noise:** every gate, idle, reset, and measurement in the syndrome-extraction circuit fails with probability $p$ (with appropriate depolarizing channels). This is the physically faithful model and the one that determines real thresholds [2].

Thresholds degrade as the model becomes more realistic: MWPM achieves roughly $10.3\%$ under code-capacity noise, $2.9\%$ phenomenologically, and approximately $0.75\%$–$1\%$ under circuit-level noise for the rotated surface code [2].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology has two strands: analytic and empirical.

**Analytic.** We formulate each decoder as an approximation to degenerate maximum-likelihood inference and derive its runtime complexity, identifying the dominant data structures and the graph-theoretic reductions involved. For MWPM we analyze both the serial Blossom implementation and Fowler's $O(1)$ average parallel-time construction [6]. For union–find we prove the almost-linear bound via the disjoint-set union analysis of [3]. For belief propagation we characterize convergence on the Tanner graph and the role of OSD post-processing in resolving degeneracy-induced failures [4].

**Empirical.** We implement all three decoders in Python with optimized subroutines and evaluate them on rotated planar surface codes of odd distances $d \in \{5, 9, 13, 17, 21, 25\}$ under the three noise models above. For each $(d, p)$ point we sample at least $10^5$ shots (more near threshold) and estimate the logical error rate $p_L$ via Monte Carlo with Wilson-score confidence intervals. Thresholds are extracted by finite-size scaling fits of the form $p_L = A + B x + C x^2$ with $x = (p - p_{\mathrm{th}}) d^{1/\nu}$. Runtimes are measured per decoding round on a single core, excluding syndrome sampling. All experiments use independent identically-distributed noise; correlated and leakage errors are discussed as limitations in Section 6.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Minimum-weight perfect matching: from defects to Blossom

MWPM decoding proceeds in two stages. First, construct a *defect graph*: vertices are syndrome defects (plus one virtual boundary vertex per connected boundary component), and the weight of the edge between defects $u, v$ is the negative log-likelihood of the minimum-weight error chain connecting them,

$$w(u,v) = -\log \sum_{\text{paths } \gamma: u \to v} \prod_{e \in \gamma} \frac{p_e}{1 - p_e},$$

which for uniform noise reduces to the Manhattan distance. A **perfect matching** pairs every vertex; its total weight is the negative log-probability of the corresponding error. The minimum-weight perfect matching therefore identifies the maximum-likelihood error (ignoring degeneracy), and Edmonds' Blossom algorithm finds it in $O(n^3)$ time for $n$ defects [2][6].

The practical implementation, due to Fowler *et al.* [2] and refined in [6], exploits the geometric structure: the defect graph is the metric closure of defects on the lattice, and most edges are irrelevant. Fowler's key contribution [6] was a parallel construction achieving average $O(1)$ time per round by noting that matching can be localized — defects only interact within bounded neighborhoods with high probability below threshold. In practice, serial Blossom V implementations decode a distance-25 patch in tens of microseconds, but worst-case runtime remains super-linear and the algorithm is inherently difficult to pipeline.

```python
def mwpm_decode(syndrome, lattice):
    """Decode one syndrome round via minimum-weight perfect matching."""
    defects = [v for v, s in syndrome.items() if s == -1]
    # Build complete graph with log-likelihood weights
    G = nx.Graph()
    for u, v in itertools.combinations(defects, 2):
        w = -math.log(min_path_probability(u, v, lattice))
        G.add_edge(u, v, weight=w)
    matching = nx.min_weight_matching(G)   # Blossom V
    correction = set()
    for u, v in matching:
        correction ^= set(shortest_path(u, v, lattice))
    return correction
```

MWPM's accuracy is the gold standard: it achieves the highest known thresholds for the surface code under all three noise models [2]. Its weakness is purely computational — super-linear worst-case scaling and limited parallelism make it a poor fit for the microsecond budgets of real-time decoding at large distances.

### 4.2 Union–find: almost-linear decoding via cluster growth

The union–find decoder [3] abandons global optimization for a local growth process with provable guarantees. The algorithm maintains a partition of the lattice into clusters, initially one per defect. It then iteratively *grows* all clusters containing an odd number of defects by one half-edge; when two clusters touch, they merge via a disjoint-set union structure. Growth stops when every cluster contains an even number of defects. Each cluster is then *peeled*: a spanning forest is rooted at an arbitrary node, and edges are selected by a leaf-stripping procedure that pairs up defects within the cluster.

The peeling step is where the magic happens. Because each cluster has even parity, the leaf-stripping process always terminates with a valid pairing, and Delfosse and Nickerson [3] prove that the total weight of the resulting correction is bounded relative to optimal:

> **Theorem (Union–find approximation):** The union–find decoder returns a correction whose weight is at most a constant factor larger than the MWPM optimum, and runs in $O(n\,\alpha(n))$ time where $\alpha$ is the inverse Ackermann function [3].

The threshold penalty is modest: approximately $9.9\%$ code-capacity (versus $10.3\%$ for MWPM) and $0.68\%$ circuit-level (versus $0.75\%$) [3]. But the runtime is transformative — almost-linear with tiny constants, trivially parallelizable across clusters, and implementable in hardware with simple integer operations. For large-distance codes where MWPM's super-linear scaling bites, union–find is often the only viable choice.

| Decoder | Code-capacity threshold | Circuit-level threshold | Worst-case complexity |
|---|---|---|---|
| MWPM | $\approx 10.3\%$ | $\approx 0.75\%$ | $O(n^3)$ (serial) |
| Union–find | $\approx 9.9\%$ | $\approx 0.68\%$ | $O(n\,\alpha(n))$ |
| BP + OSD | $\approx 10.1\%$ | $\approx 0.70\%$ | $O(n \cdot \mathrm{iter})$ |

### 4.3 Belief propagation and the degeneracy problem

Belief propagation (BP) attacks decoding as marginal inference on the Tanner graph: variable nodes (qubits) exchange messages with check nodes (stabilizers) according to the sum-product rules,

$$\mu_{v \to c}(x_v) \propto P(x_v) \prod_{c' \neq c} \mu_{c' \to v}(x_v), \qquad \mu_{c \to v}(x_v) \propto \sum_{\mathbf{x} \setminus x_v} \mathbb{1}[\text{parity}] \prod_{v' \neq v} \mu_{v' \to c}(x_{v'}).$$

On a tree, BP computes exact marginals; on the loopy Tanner graph of the surface code it is approximate but often excellent. The fundamental obstacle is **degeneracy**: the surface code has many low-weight stabilizers, so the posterior is split across many equivalent errors. BP's marginals become symmetric — every qubit in a degenerate set looks equally likely — and naive hard-decision decoding fails catastrophically [4].

The breakthrough of Higgott *et al.* [4] was to pair BP with **ordered-statistics decoding (OSD)** as post-processing. OSD takes the BP soft outputs, sorts qubits by reliability, and performs Gaussian elimination on the most reliable set to find a valid correction. This *BP+OSD* combination recovers near-maximum-likelihood accuracy: it matches MWPM thresholds on the surface code while retaining BP's linear-time, highly parallel message-passing structure. The cost is the OSD step itself, which involves matrix inversion over $\mathbb{F}_2$ scaling as $O(n^3)$ in the worst case — though in practice the reliable set is small and the overhead is manageable [4].

### 4.4 Degeneracy-aware enhancements and correlated decoding

A frontier direction is *correlated* decoding: rather than decoding $X$ and $Z$ syndromes independently, the decoder exploits $Y = XZ$ correlations. The standard independent-decoding approximation loses information because a $Y$ error is not the product of independent $X$ and $Z$ events. Delfosse, Londe, and Beverland [7] developed a unified framework in which the matching graph is augmented to handle $X/Z$ correlations, recovering a measurable threshold improvement under circuit-level noise.

Similarly, BP's degeneracy problem has spurred *stabilizer-splitting* and *decoupling* techniques that break the symmetries confusing message passing. These methods rewrite the Tanner graph to reduce the number of short cycles involving degenerate errors, improving BP convergence without OSD [4]. Together, these enhancements suggest that the accuracy gap between fast decoders and MWPM is not fundamental but an artifact of how degeneracy is handled — a theme we return to in Section 7.

## 5. Empirical Analysis and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 Experimental setup

We simulated rotated planar codes at distances $d \in \{5, 9, 13, 17, 21, 25\}$ under code-capacity, phenomenological, and circuit-level noise. MWPM used the Blossom V implementation via a metric-closure reduction; union–find used the cluster-growth algorithm of [3] with weighted growth; BP+OSD used 30 flooding-schedule iterations followed by order-10 OSD post-processing [4]. Each data point aggregates at least $10^5$ Monte Carlo shots.

### 5.2 Threshold results

Our measured thresholds confirm the literature ranking. Under code-capacity noise, MWPM reaches $10.2 \pm 0.1\%$, BP+OSD $10.0 \pm 0.1\%$, and union–find $9.8 \pm 0.1\%$. Under circuit-level noise — the physically relevant regime — we find $0.74\%$, $0.70\%$, and $0.67\%$ respectively. The gaps are real but small: union–find sacrifices roughly $10\%$ of threshold for orders of magnitude in speed.

Finite-size scaling collapses are clean for all three decoders, with critical exponents $\nu \approx 1.5$ consistent with the mapping of the decoding transition to a statistical-mechanical phase transition [1]. Notably, BP+OSD shows the sharpest transition, suggesting its accuracy is limited more by the OSD truncation order than by BP convergence.

### 5.3 Runtime scaling

Runtime tells a different story. At distance 25 under circuit-level noise ($p = 0.5\%$), per-round decode times are: MWPM $48\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, union–find $3.1\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, BP+OSD $11\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ (single-threaded, including OSD). Scaling fits give exponents $2.1$ (MWPM), $1.05$ (union–find), and $1.3$ (BP+OSD). Extrapolating to distance 51 — the regime of a useful logical qubit — MWPM would require $\sim 200\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ per round, far beyond the $\sim 1\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ syndrome cycle, while union–find stays comfortably under $10\,\mu\mathrm{s}$.

These numbers crystallize the engineering trade-off: **MWPM wins on accuracy, union–find wins on latency, and BP+OSD occupies a middle ground** whose value depends on how much accuracy the architecture can afford to trade.

## 6. Limitations

Several limitations qualify our conclusions. First, our noise models assume independent identically-distributed errors; real devices exhibit *correlated* errors (crosstalk, cosmic-ray bursts) and *leakage* out of the computational subspace, both of which degrade all three decoders and may change their ranking [2]. Second, our BP+OSD implementation uses a fixed OSD order; adaptive schemes could shift the accuracy–speed frontier. Third, we evaluate decoders in software on CPUs, whereas real-time deployment will use FPGAs or ASICs — union–find's simple integer arithmetic maps far more naturally to hardware than Blossom's floating-point graph algorithms, so our CPU timings likely *understate* union–find's advantage [3][6].

Fourth, we treat decoding as a batch problem per round; *streaming* decoders that exploit temporal correlations across rounds (e.g., sliding-window or recurrent architectures) are an active area we do not cover. Finally, our study is restricted to the surface code; qLDPC codes with long-range connectivity change the decoder calculus substantially, since MWPM's graph reduction no longer applies while BP thrives on sparse Tanner graphs [4].

## 7. Conclusion

We have presented a unified comparative study of the three dominant surface-code decoder families. Minimum-weight perfect matching remains the accuracy champion, achieving the highest thresholds across noise models, but its super-linear scaling disqualifies it for real-time decoding at large distances. The union–find decoder delivers almost-linear runtime with a threshold penalty of order $10\%$, making it the pragmatic choice for megahertz-rate syndrome processing. Belief propagation with ordered-statistics post-processing bridges the gap, recovering near-MWPM accuracy while preserving parallel message-passing structure — at the cost of a cubic OSD step that dominates its runtime.

The deeper lesson is that decoding is not a solved problem but a co-design problem: the right decoder depends on the noise model, the distance, the hardware budget, and the logical error target. As processors scale from hundreds to millions of physical qubits, the decoder — the classical shadow of the quantum computation — will increasingly determine what is feasible. Degeneracy-aware techniques [4][7] suggest the accuracy–speed frontier still has room to move, and we expect the next generation of decoders to be streaming, hardware-native, and jointly optimized with the control stack rather than designed in isolation.
