# Adversarial Robustness in Deep Learning: Certified Defenses, Randomized Smoothing, and the Geometry of Decision Boundaries

## Abstract

Deep neural networks achieve superhuman accuracy on clean benchmarks yet remain brittle under *adversarial perturbations*: imperceptible, worst-case modifications of the input that flip the model's prediction with high confidence. This thesis surveys the foundations and the modern theory of adversarial robustness, from the discovery of adversarial examples [1] and the fast gradient sign method [2] to the robust-optimization paradigm built on projected gradient descent [3]. We develop the geometry of decision boundaries that explains why high-dimensional classifiers admit tiny adversarial directions, then present the two principal certified defenses — convex relaxation/interval bound propagation and randomized smoothing — culminating in the tight Gaussian-smoothing certificate [4]. We analyze the provable tension between robustness and standard accuracy [5] and the methodology of reliable evaluation via parameter-free attack ensembles [6]. The exposition is self-contained: every theorem is stated precisely, every empirical claim is tied to a cited result, and every attack and certificate is accompanied by executable reference code.

## 1 Introduction

The vulnerability of deep classifiers to adversarial examples is among the most consequential findings in modern machine learning. In 2014, Szegedy et al. demonstrated that a state-of-the-art image classifier could be induced to misclassify an image by adding a perturbation so small as to be invisible to the human eye — a perturbation found not by accident but by deliberate optimization against the network's own loss surface [1]. Within a year, Goodfellow, Shlens, and Szegedy showed that these perturbations could be generated in closed form, that the phenomenon reflected the essentially *linear* behavior of networks in high dimensions, and that the same adversarial input fooled different architectures trained on different data subsets — the disturbing property of *transferability* [2].

What followed was a decade-long arms race, and two lines of work survived it:

1. **Robust optimization.** Madry et al. recast robustness as a saddle-point (min-max) problem: minimize, over parameters, the *worst-case* loss over a perturbation set, with the inner maximization approximated by projected gradient descent (PGD) from random restarts [3].
2. **Certified defenses.** A certified classifier comes with a *proof*: no perturbation within a specified radius can change its decision. Randomized smoothing [4] made such certificates practical at ImageNet scale for the first time.

Meanwhile, Tsipras et al. established that the price of robustness is real: there exist natural settings where *no* classifier can simultaneously achieve high standard accuracy and high adversarial robustness [5].

This thesis unifies these threads. Section 2 fixes notation and the threat model. Section 3 lays out the methodology of robust optimization and certification. Section 4 is a five-part deep dive into the central theoretical ideas. Section 5 states the key theorems and surveys the empirical state of the art. Section 6 is honest about limitations, and Section 7 concludes with open directions.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Formal setup

Let $\mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ be the input space and $\mathcal{Y} = \{1,\dots,K\}$ the label set. A classifier $f_\theta : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ is typically implemented as $f_\theta(x) = \arg\max_k z_k(x;\theta)$ where $z$ are logits.

An **adversarial example** for a correctly classified input $(x, y)$ is a point $x' = x + \delta$ such that $f_\theta(x') \neq y$ while $\delta$ belongs to an allowed **perturbation set** $\mathcal{S}(x)$, most commonly an $\ell_p$ ball:

$$\mathcal{S}(x) = \{ x + \delta : \|\delta\|_p \le \epsilon \}.$$

The two standard choices are $\ell_\infty$ (bounded per-pixel change, the vision default) and $\ell_2$ (bounded Euclidean energy, the natural norm for certification). Representative threat models used across the literature are:

| Dataset | Norm | $\epsilon$ | Pixel scale |
|---|---|---|---|
| MNIST | $\ell_\infty$ | 0.3 | $[0,1]$ |
| CIFAR-10 | $\ell_\infty$ | $8/255$ | $[0,1]$ |
| CIFAR-10 | $\ell_2$ | 0.5 | $[0,1]$ |
| ImageNet | $\ell_\infty$ | $4/255$ | $[0,1]$ |
| ImageNet | $\ell_2$ | 3.0 (typical eval) | $[0,1]$ |

These radii are chosen to be *imperceptible*. The **robust accuracy** of $f_\theta$ is the fraction of test points for which *no* $\delta \in \mathcal{S}$ flips the prediction — a worst-case, not average-case, quantity.

### 2.2 The attack taxonomy

Attacks are ordered by adversary knowledge and strength:

- **White-box.** Full access to $\theta$ and gradients. Includes L-BFGS [1], FGSM [2], PGD [3], and AutoAttack [6].
- **Black-box / transfer.** The adversary crafts $\delta$ on a surrogate model and transfers it to the target, exploiting the cross-model generalization observed since [1, 2].
- **Adaptive.** Attacks designed *against a specific defense*, with full knowledge of its mechanism. A defense evaluated only against non-adaptive attacks has not been evaluated at all [6].

The history of the field is, to a first approximation, the history of defenses that failed adaptive evaluation: gradient obfuscation creates the *illusion* of robustness while leaving the decision boundary unchanged, and black-box or gradient-free attacks routinely expose it [6].

### 2.3 From L-BFGS to FGSM

Szegedy et al. formulated the attack as box-constrained optimization [1]:

$$\min_r \; c\|r\|_2 + \mathrm{loss}_f(x+r, l) \quad \text{s.t. } x+r \in [0,1]^d,$$

solved with L-BFGS for a target label $l \neq f(x)$. Goodfellow et al. replaced this slow per-example optimization with a single closed-form step [2]:

$$x_{\mathrm{adv}} = x + \epsilon \cdot \mathrm{sign}\big(\nabla_x J(\theta, x, y)\big),$$

the **fast gradient sign method (FGSM)**. Their central theoretical claim — the *linearity hypothesis* — was that vulnerability arises not from pathological nonlinearity but from the approximately linear behavior of networks in high dimension: for a linear model $w^\top x$, the worst-case $\ell_\infty$ perturbation of radius $\epsilon$ shifts the activation by $\epsilon \|w\|_1$, which grows with dimension $d$ even when each coordinate changes imperceptibly. This explained both the existence and the transferability of adversarial examples, and yielded adversarial training as a natural regularizer [2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Robust optimization: the saddle-point formulation

Madry et al. proposed that robustness be treated as a *specification* — a number attached to an explicit perturbation set $\mathcal{S}$ — and formulated training as the saddle-point problem [3]:

$$\min_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[ \max_{\delta \in \mathcal{S}} L(\theta, x+\delta, y) \right].$$

The inner maximization is the attacker's problem; the outer minimization is the defender's:

1. **Inner solver.** PGD with random initialization inside $\mathcal{S}$:
   $$\delta^{(t+1)} = \Pi_{\mathcal{S}}\left( \delta^{(t)} + \alpha \, \mathrm{sign}\big(\nabla_\delta L(\theta, x+\delta^{(t)}, y)\big) \right),$$
   where $\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}$ projects onto the $\epsilon$-ball (and valid pixel range). Multiple random restarts mitigate the non-convexity of the inner landscape; the finding that different restarts reach comparably high-loss points licenses treating PGD as a reliable worst-case estimate [3].
2. **Outer training.** SGD on the adversarial loss, typically 3–10× more expensive than standard training.
3. **Evaluation discipline.** Report robust accuracy under the *strongest known adaptive* attack — today, the AutoAttack ensemble [6] — rather than under the attack used during training.

### 3.2 Certification: from empirical to provable

A **certified defense** returns, for each input $x$, a prediction *and* a radius $R(x)$ with the guarantee that $f(x') = f(x)$ for all $\|x' - x\|_p < R(x)$. Two families dominate:

- **Deterministic methods.** Interval bound propagation (IBP) and its relatives propagate interval bounds $[l, u]$ through each layer, computing a sound over-approximation of the network's reachable outputs over $\mathcal{S}(x)$. If the lower bound of the true logit exceeds the upper bounds of all others, the point is certified. These methods give strong guarantees but historically scale poorly and pay a large clean-accuracy cost.
- **Probabilistic methods: randomized smoothing.** Define the *smoothed* classifier
  $$g(x) = \arg\max_c \; \mathbb{P}_{\delta \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)}[f(x+\delta) = c].$$
  Cohen et al. proved a *tight* $\ell_2$ certificate for $g$ (Theorem 1, Section 5) [4]. It is estimated by Monte Carlo sampling with Clopper–Pearson confidence bounds — the only certified approach demonstrated at full ImageNet scale.

### 3.3 Reliable evaluation methodology

Croce and Hein's AutoAttack [6] standardized evaluation as a *parameter-free ensemble* of four diverse attacks: **APGD-CE** (auto-tuned PGD on cross-entropy), **APGD-DLR** (auto-tuned PGD on the difference-of-logits-ratio loss), **FAB** (fast adaptive boundary attack, minimizing perturbation norm), and **Square Attack** (a query-efficient black-box attack that defeats gradient masking).

Applied to over 50 published defenses, the ensemble reduced reported robust accuracy in all but one case, often by more than ten percentage points [6]. The methodological moral is now consensus: *a defense must be evaluated against adaptive, diverse, parameter-free attacks, and any claim resting on a single attack configuration should be treated as unevaluated.*

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The linearity hypothesis and FGSM

Goodfellow et al.'s linearity hypothesis [2] reframes the entire phenomenon. For a linear model $f(x) = \mathrm{sign}(w^\top x + b)$ under budget $\|\delta\|_\infty \le \epsilon$, the optimal perturbation is $\delta^\star = \epsilon \cdot \mathrm{sign}(w)$, and the induced change in the pre-activation is

$$w^\top \delta^\star = \epsilon \sum_{i=1}^d |w_i| = \epsilon \|w\|_1.$$

If the average weight magnitude is $m$, the activation shifts by $\epsilon m d$ — linear in dimension. For $d = 150{,}528$ (a $224 \times 224 \times 3$ ImageNet input), even $\epsilon = 1/255$ produces an enormous shift while no single pixel changes visibly.

> **Theorem:** *(Linear vulnerability, informal [2]).* For a linear model $f(x) = \mathrm{sign}(w^\top x + b)$ and perturbation budget $\|\delta\|_\infty \le \epsilon$, the adversarial direction $\delta^\star = -\epsilon \cdot \mathrm{sign}(w) \cdot y$ is optimal, and the margin erosion equals $\epsilon\|w\|_1$, growing with input dimension even when $\epsilon$ is imperceptible per-coordinate.

Three consequences follow. First, adversarial examples are *dense*: they occupy large volumes of the $\epsilon$-ball, not isolated pathologies. Second, they *transfer*: different models trained on similar data learn similar linear structure, so $\mathrm{sign}(\nabla_x J)$ computed on a surrogate harms the target [1, 2]. Third, single-step adversarial training under-approximates the true inner maximum; iterated attacks (PGD) find strictly stronger perturbations [3].

### 4.2 PGD and the min-max paradigm

The Madry et al. saddle-point formulation [3] is *robust optimization* imported from operations research: choose parameters minimizing loss under the worst admissible disturbance. Its conceptual contributions were:

- **Specification over aspiration.** Fixing $\mathcal{S}$ explicitly (e.g., $\|\delta\|_\infty \le 8/255$ on CIFAR-10) turns "be robust" into a measurable property.
- **PGD as the canonical first-order adversary.** First-order methods from random starts find maxima of comparable value — making PGD the right *computational* definition of the strongest attack we can mount, and hence the right training signal.
- **Capacity and the robust generalization gap.** Adversarial training demands substantially larger models: the boundary must be correct not just on data points but on entire $\epsilon$-balls around them. Empirically, wide networks (e.g., WideResNet) benefit disproportionately [3].

The paradigm's weakness is equally clear: it certifies nothing. A model trained against 20-step PGD may fall to a stronger attack — which is precisely why AutoAttack's diversity matters [6].

### 4.3 The geometry of decision boundaries

Why should a classifier that is 99% accurate admit adversarial directions at *every* test point? The geometric answer has several layers.

**Margin in high dimension.** For a classifier with decision boundary $\mathcal{B}$, define the *margin* at $x$ as $\rho(x) = \min_{x' \in \mathcal{B}} \|x - x'\|_2$. Data concentrates near low-dimensional manifolds while $\epsilon$-balls are volumetrically enormous; a boundary separating classes on the manifold can pass arbitrarily close to manifold points in ambient space. The relevant quantity is the *minimum* margin over the test set — and deep networks, trained only to classify points correctly, have no incentive to maximize it.

**Piecewise-linear structure.** A ReLU network partitions input space into convex polytopes, on each of which it is affine. The number of such regions grows exponentially with depth, so the boundary is a dense patchwork of hyperplane facets. Near any point, the network *is* the linear model of Section 4.1, and the adversarial direction is simply the facet normal — computable by one backward pass.

**Lipschitz perspective.** If $f$ is $L$-Lipschitz, then $\|f(x+\delta) - f(x)\| \le L\|\delta\|$; a small Lipschitz constant *provably* limits how fast predictions can change. This motivates Lipschitz-constrained architectures (spectral normalization, orthogonal layers), which trade expressivity for a direct analytic handle on robustness — a deterministic counterpart to smoothing's probabilistic certificate.

### 4.4 Randomized smoothing and tight $\ell_2$ certificates

Randomized smoothing [4] is conceptually disarming: take *any* base classifier $f$ that performs well under Gaussian noise, and define the smoothed classifier $g$ by majority vote over noisy samples. The miracle is a *tight, closed-form* robustness radius requiring no assumptions on $f$'s architecture.

> **Theorem:** *(Cohen, Rosenfeld & Kolter [4]).* Let $f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathcal{Y}$ and $\sigma > 0$. Define $g(x) = \arg\max_c \mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)}[f(x+\varepsilon) = c]$. Suppose $g(x) = c_A$, with $\underline{p_A} \le \mathbb{P}[f(x+\varepsilon) = c_A]$ and $\overline{p_B} \ge \max_{c \neq c_A} \mathbb{P}[f(x+\varepsilon) = c]$. Then $g(x+\delta) = c_A$ for all $\|\delta\|_2 < R$, where
> $$R = \frac{\sigma}{2}\left(\Phi^{-1}(\underline{p_A}) - \Phi^{-1}(\overline{p_B})\right),$$
> and $\Phi^{-1}$ is the standard Gaussian inverse CDF. The bound is tight: no larger radius can be certified from $(\underline{p_A}, \overline{p_B})$ alone.

When the top class commands a strict majority ($\underline{p_A} > 1/2$), this simplifies to the memorable $R = \sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(\underline{p_A})$.

Practically, certification selects the putative top class with $n_0$ samples, then lower-bounds $p_A$ with $n$ samples via the Clopper–Pearson interval at confidence $1-\alpha$, abstaining when the bound is too weak. Cohen et al. thereby obtained an ImageNet classifier with 49% certified top-1 accuracy at radius $0.5$ ($=127/255$) — the first certified defense demonstrated at that scale [4]. The costs are real: inference requires hundreds to thousands of forward passes, and $\sigma$ trades clean accuracy against certified radius monotonically.

### 4.5 The robustness–accuracy tradeoff

Tsipras et al. asked whether robustness must cost accuracy, and answered: *sometimes, provably yes* [5]. In a binary classification setting with one robust feature (predictive under perturbation) and many weakly-predictive non-robust features, they proved that any classifier achieving high standard accuracy must exploit the non-robust features and is therefore vulnerable, while any robust classifier must ignore them and accept lower clean accuracy.

The paper's deeper contribution is the *features* viewpoint: standard training latches onto **non-robust features** — patterns highly predictive on clean data but easily flipped by small perturbations — because empirical risk minimization has no reason to prefer robust ones. Robust models therefore learn *different representations*: their input-gradients align with human-salient structure, and their features support image synthesis in ways standard features do not [5]. The tradeoff's severity is dataset- and norm-dependent — mild on MNIST, substantial on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet — reframing adversarial vulnerability as *misaligned feature selection* rather than mere optimization failure.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 The certificate, algorithmically

The tight smoothing certificate of [4] is directly implementable:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm, binomtest

def certify(base_clf, x, sigma, n0=100, n=100_000, alpha=0.001):
    """Return (predicted_class, certified_radius) for input x.
    base_clf maps a batch of noisy inputs to class indices."""
    # Phase 1: guess top class with n0 samples
    counts0 = np.bincount(base_clf(x + sigma * np.random.randn(n0, *x.shape)))
    cA = int(np.argmax(counts0))
    # Phase 2: lower-bound p_A (Clopper-Pearson); abstain if < 0.5
    counts = np.bincount(base_clf(x + sigma * np.random.randn(n, *x.shape)))
    nA = int(counts[cA]) if cA < len(counts) else 0
    pA_low = binomtest(nA, n).proportion_confint(confidence_level=1 - alpha)[0]
    if pA_low < 0.5:
        return cA, 0.0
    radius = sigma * norm.ppf(pA_low)
    return cA, radius
```

The reference attack used throughout the literature is PGD [3]:

```python
def pgd_attack(model, x, y, eps, alpha, steps, norm="inf"):
    """PGD attack; returns adversarial x' within eps-ball."""
    delta = np.zeros_like(x)
    if norm == "inf":
        delta = np.random.uniform(-eps, eps, x.shape)  # random start in S
    for _ in range(steps):
        grad = grad_loss(model, x + delta, y)          # backward pass
        if norm == "inf":
            delta = delta + alpha * np.sign(grad)
            delta = np.clip(delta, -eps, eps)          # project onto S
        else:  # l2 projection
            delta = delta + alpha * grad / (np.linalg.norm(grad) + 1e-12)
            nrm = np.linalg.norm(delta)
            if nrm > eps:
                delta = delta * (eps / nrm)
    return np.clip(x + delta, 0.0, 1.0)
```

### 5.2 What the numbers say

Representative results from the cited literature (robust accuracy = worst-case accuracy under the stated attack):

| Model / method | Dataset ($\epsilon$) | Clean acc. | Robust acc. | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Standard ResNet | CIFAR-10 ($\ell_\infty$, 8/255) | ~95% | ~0% (PGD-20) | [3] |
| PGD adversarial training | CIFAR-10 ($\ell_\infty$, 8/255) | ~87% | ~46% (PGD-20) | [3] |
| PGD adversarial training | MNIST ($\ell_\infty$, 0.3) | ~99% | ~90% (PGD) | [3] |
| Randomized smoothing ($\sigma{=}0.25$) | CIFAR-10 ($\ell_2$) | ~75% | ~50%+ certified at $r{=}0.5$ | [4] |
| Randomized smoothing | ImageNet ($\ell_2$) | ~67% | 49% certified at $r{=}0.5$ | [4] |
| Published defenses (50+ models) | various | — | often 10%+ *below* claimed, under AutoAttack | [6] |

Three patterns stand out:

1. **The accuracy tax is visible everywhere.** PGD training on CIFAR-10 sacrifices roughly 8 points of clean accuracy for ~46 points of robust accuracy [3]; smoothing at $\sigma = 0.25$ pays a similar tax for certificates [4]. This is the empirical face of the tradeoff proved in [5].
2. **Evaluation inflation was systemic.** AutoAttack's re-evaluation of 50+ published defenses found nearly every reported number optimistic, frequently by double digits [6].
3. **Certificates scale, but narrowly.** Smoothing remains the only certified approach demonstrated on full-resolution ImageNet [4]; deterministic methods (IBP and convex relaxations) dominate on small models but have not closed the scalability gap.

### 5.3 Proof sketch: tightness of the smoothing certificate

We sketch why no better radius follows from $(p_A, p_B)$ alone [4]. Fix $x$ and a perturbation $\delta$ with $\|\delta\|_2 = r$. Let $X \sim \mathcal{N}(x, \sigma^2 I)$ and $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(x+\delta, \sigma^2 I)$. For any measurable decision region $S$ with $\mathbb{P}(X \in S) \ge p_A$, the Neyman–Pearson lemma implies $\mathbb{P}(Y \in S)$ is minimized when $S$ is the half-space $\{z : \delta^\top(z - x) \le \beta\}$ with $\beta$ chosen so $\mathbb{P}(X \in S) = p_A$. Direct Gaussian computation gives $\mathbb{P}(Y \in S) = \Phi(\Phi^{-1}(p_A) - r/\sigma)$; stability requires this to exceed $1/2$ (and beat the runner-up), yielding the radius of Theorem 1. Attainment by the half-space classifier proves tightness. ∎

## 6 Limitations

Honesty about the limits of current robustness is a scientific obligation, and the limits are severe:

1. **The $\ell_p$ threat model is a proxy, not reality.** Real adversaries use rotations, translations, lighting changes, patches, and semantic edits — none captured by $\ell_p$ balls. Certified $\ell_2$ radii say nothing about a sticker on a stop sign.
2. **Computational cost.** Adversarial training multiplies training cost several-fold [3]; smoothing multiplies inference cost by the Monte Carlo sample count ($10^2$–$10^5$ forward passes per prediction) [4].
3. **The accuracy tax persists.** It is unclear how much of the observed gap is fundamental versus an artifact of current architectures and training; larger models and more data seem to shrink it, but no general resolution exists [5].
4. **Probabilistic, not absolute, certificates.** Smoothing's guarantee holds with confidence $1-\alpha$, and the procedure may *abstain*; a deployed system needs a policy for abstentions [4].
5. **Adaptive evaluation is necessary but not sufficient.** AutoAttack [6] catches gradient masking and weak attack configurations, but genuinely novel attack classes can still surprise. Robustness claims are always relative to a specified adversary.
6. **Deterministic certification has not scaled.** IBP-style methods give the strongest form of guarantee but remain confined to modest architectures; the gap between what we can certify and what we deploy is the field's central engineering challenge.

## 7 Conclusion

Adversarial robustness has matured from an intriguing pathology [1] into a disciplined subfield with its own optimization paradigm [3], proof techniques [4], impossibility results [5], and evaluation standards [6]. The through-line of this thesis is geometric: high-dimensional linear structure creates adversarial directions [2]; robust optimization reshapes the boundary by training on worst-case neighborhoods [3]; smoothing converts noise-tolerance into a certified radius [4]; and the features a model selects determine which regime it inhabits [5].

Open directions are plentiful. *Beyond $\ell_p$*: certifications for semantic and geometric threat models. *Closing the tax*: architectures and objectives that shrink the robustness–accuracy gap. *Efficient certification*: deterministic guarantees at ImageNet scale, or smoothing with far fewer samples. *Theory of transferability*: a complete account of why adversarial directions align across architectures remains elusive. What is no longer open is the methodology: specify the adversary, attack adaptively, and prove what you claim — or expect AutoAttack to do it for you [6].
