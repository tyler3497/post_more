# Genome Assembly and Sequence Alignment at Scale: de Bruijn Graphs, the Burrows–Wheeler Transform, and the Mathematics of Read Mapping

## Abstract

Assembling a genome from millions of sequencing reads and aligning those reads to a reference are the two computational pillars of modern genomics. This thesis unifies both problems. We begin with the *de Bruijn graph* formulation of assembly, where *k*-mers become edges over *(k−1)*-mer nodes and contig extraction reduces to finding Eulerian trails — polynomial-time, displacing the NP-hard Hamiltonian formulation of overlap graphs [2]. We then treat *overlap–layout–consensus* assembly through the *string graph* lens, where overlaps are computed implicitly via the FM-index [3]. The Burrows–Wheeler transform is analyzed in full: the LF-mapping, rank/select structure, and backward search locating any pattern in a genome of length *n* in *O(|P|)* time. Read mapping is presented as *seed-and-extend*: supermaximal exact matches, colinear chaining, banded affine-gap dynamic programming, and MAPQ estimation as in BWA-MEM [5]. We formalize assembly quality through N50, NG50, and misassembly statistics [7], and survey *k*-mer-spectrum error correction. A synthetic benchmark and a correctness proof of backward search close the exposition.

## 1 Introduction

A human genome contains roughly three billion base pairs, yet no sequencer reads more than a few hundred thousand in a single pass. Instruments produce *reads* — 100–300 bp for Illumina, 10–100 kb for long-read platforms — sampled at random positions with redundancy called *coverage* [1]. Two computational problems dominate everything built on this data:

1. ***De novo* assembly**: reconstruct the unknown source sequence from the reads alone, without a reference.
2. **Read mapping (alignment)**: given a reference genome, determine the most probable origin of every read.

Both problems are, at heart, string-processing problems over the four-letter alphabet Σ = {A, C, G, T} — dominated by graph theory, compressed indexing, and dynamic programming. Assembly seeks *contigs*, long sequences consistent with the read set; mapping seeks *alignments*, positions in the reference maximizing a scoring function under a model of sequencing error and mutation [4].

The algorithmic landscape divides along two historical axes. The *overlap–layout–consensus* (OLC) paradigm, used for the Human Genome Project, builds a graph of reads joined by overlaps. The *de Bruijn* paradigm, which conquered the short-read era, decomposes reads into *k*-mers and assembles through an Eulerian graph [2]. Mapping is almost universally *seed-and-extend* over a Burrows–Wheeler index of the reference, with BWA-MEM's seed–chain–extend architecture and minimap2's minimizer chaining defining the modern standard [5][6].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The string alphabet and sequencing models

DNA is a string over Σ = {A, C, G, T}. Double-strandedness means every segment may appear in *reverse complement* orientation; algorithms handle both strands. Two error models matter:

- **Short reads (Illumina)**: error rate ≈ 0.1–1%, dominated by *substitutions*; exact *k*-mer matches are meaningful for *k* ≤ 30.
- **Long reads (PacBio, Oxford Nanopore)**: historically 10–15% error dominated by *indels*; HiFi reads reduce this to ≈ 0.1%. Long-read assembly therefore favors *overlap*-based methods [6].

### 2.2 Suffixes, tries, and the cost of naïveté

Comparing every read against every other read costs *O(N²L)* for *N* reads of length *L* — infeasible when *N* ≈ 10⁹. All modern methods replace pairwise comparison with a global index: a suffix trie, suffix array, or FM-index built once over the data, after which each query is resolved by traversal rather than scanning [3].

### 2.3 Contigs, scaffolds, and repeats

Assembly output is a set of **contigs**: maximal sequences spelled by unambiguous paths through the assembly graph. **Scaffolding** orders contigs using paired-end linking information. The fundamental obstruction is *repeats*: any repeat longer than the effective resolution (read length for OLC, *k* for de Bruijn) collapses in the graph, and no assembler can order the flanking unique sequence correctly without spanning information [2].

> **Definition 1 (N50).** Sort contigs by decreasing length; N50 is the length of the shortest contig such that contigs of that length or longer cover at least 50% of the total assembly length. **NG50** replaces total assembly length with the estimated genome size, penalizing incomplete assemblies [7].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is theoretical analysis grounded in the canonical implementations. We proceed in four layers:

1. **Graph-theoretic modeling.** We formalize both assembly paradigms — the de Bruijn graph and the string graph — and characterize which graph features (tips, bubbles, branches) correspond to errors, heterozygosity, and repeats.
2. **Compressed indexing.** We derive the Burrows–Wheeler transform, the LF-mapping, and the FM-index backward-search algorithm from first principles, proving its *O(|P|)* query bound. We show how the same index computes all-pairs read overlaps without explicit *O(N²)* comparison [3].
3. **Alignment pipelines.** We decompose read mapping into indexing, seeding (supermaximal exact matches, minimizers), chaining, extension (banded affine-gap DP), and mapping-quality estimation, following BWA-MEM [5] and minimap2 [6].
4. **Evaluation.** We define the standard assembly metrics (N50, NG50, misassemblies) and *k*-mer-spectrum error correction, and report a controlled synthetic benchmark.

Notation: *T[0..n)* is a text terminated by a sentinel `$` smaller than every letter; *rank_c(B, i)* counts occurrences of character *c* in *B[0..i)*; *C[c]* counts characters lexicographically smaller than *c* in *T*.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 de Bruijn Graphs: *k*-mers, Eulerian Paths, and Contig Extraction

The de Bruijn approach abandons reads as atomic units. Each read of length *L* is shredded into *L − k + 1* overlapping *k*-mers. The **de Bruijn graph** *D_k* is then defined as follows [2]:

- **Nodes**: all distinct *(k−1)*-mers appearing in the reads.
- **Edges**: one directed edge per *k*-mer, from its length-*(k−1)* prefix to its suffix.

The genome is spelled by a path traversing every edge — an **Eulerian trail**. This is the decisive theoretical move: reconstructing a genome from an *overlap graph* over reads requires a Hamiltonian path, which is NP-complete, while Eulerian trails are solvable in linear time [1][2].

> **Theorem 1 (Euler–Hierholzer).** A directed graph admits an Eulerian circuit iff every vertex with nonzero degree belongs to a single weakly connected component and *indegree(v) = outdegree(v)* for all *v*. An Eulerian trail from *s* to *t* exists iff the balance condition holds except *outdegree(s) − indegree(s) = 1* and *indegree(t) − outdegree(t) = 1*. Hierholzer's algorithm constructs such a trail in *O(|E|)* time.

In the ideal case — error-free reads, complete coverage, no repeats — the de Bruijn graph is balanced and a single Eulerian circuit spells the genome. Reality introduces three distortions, each with a characteristic signature:

| Graph feature | Biological cause | Resolution |
|---|---|---|
| **Tips** (dead-end short paths) | Sequencing errors near read ends | Clip tips shorter than 2*k* |
| **Bubbles** (diverging then rejoining paths) | Errors, SNPs, heterozygosity | Pop bubbles via sequence similarity |
| **Branches** (high-degree nodes) | Repeats, segmental duplications | Break contigs; resolve with pairing |

Practical assemblers (Velvet [8], SPAdes, ABySS) do not compute one global Eulerian circuit: they compress linear chains into **unitigs** and emit each maximal unambiguous path as a contig. Repeats longer than *k* necessarily terminate unitigs — *k* is the resolution limit of the method: larger *k* resolves longer repeats but fragments the graph at low coverage, since a *k*-mer must be observed intact [2].

```python
def build_debruijn(reads, k):
    """Build a de Bruijn graph: nodes are (k-1)-mers, edges are k-mers."""
    from collections import defaultdict
    graph = defaultdict(list)   # node -> list of (neighbor, kmer)
    for read in reads:
        for i in range(len(read) - k + 1):
            kmer = read[i:i+k]
            u, v = kmer[:-1], kmer[1:]
            graph[u].append((v, kmer))
    return graph

def compress_unitigs(graph):
    """Collapse linear chains into unitigs; return contig strings."""
    contigs, used = [], set()
    for u, edges in graph.items():
        indeg = sum(1 for x in graph.values() for nb, _ in x if nb == u)
        if len(edges) == 1 and indeg == 1:
            continue  # interior of a chain; handled from its start
        for v, kmer in edges:
            path = [kmer]
            cur = v
            while len(graph[cur]) == 1:
                nxt, km = graph[cur][0]
                path.append(km[-1])
                cur = nxt
            contigs.append(''.join(path))
    return contigs
```

The choice of *k* is a statistical tradeoff. If the per-base error rate is *ε*, the probability that a *k*-mer is error-free is *(1 − ε)^k*; for Illumina *ε ≈ 0.01* and *k = 55*, roughly 57% of *k*-mers survive, while a 15%-error long read yields essentially no intact 55-mers — the reason de Bruijn assembly of raw long reads was long considered hopeless [2].

### 4.2 Overlap–Layout–Consensus: String Graphs and Implicit Overlaps

The OLC paradigm keeps reads whole. An **overlap graph** has a directed edge *i → j* when a suffix of *r_i* matches a prefix of *r_j*; assembly seeks paths through it. The **string graph** of Myers reformulates the problem: vertices are read *endpoints*, edges are overlaps, and *transitive* edges — those implied by a path of other edges — are removed, since any assembly using one can use the underlying path instead.

The breakthrough of Simpson and Durbin's SGA assembler was computing overlaps **without materializing the quadratic graph** [3]. All reads are concatenated into one string indexed by an FM-index (Section 4.3); for each read, backward search enumerates all reads it overlaps — in time proportional to the number of true overlaps, not *N*:

1. **Index** the read set with an FM-index over the concatenated reads.
2. **Correct** errors via *k*-mer frequency spectra (Section 4.5).
3. **Overlap**: for each read, walk the index to find suffix–prefix matches above a length threshold.
4. **Reduce**: discard transitive and contained overlaps.
5. **Assemble**: emit unitigs from the reduced string graph.

```rust
// Sketch: transitive reduction over an overlap graph (SGA-style).
// edges: Vec<(from, to, overlap_len, is_contained)>
fn transitive_reduction(edges: &mut Vec<Overlap>) {
    edges.sort_by_key(|e| (e.from, std::cmp::Reverse(e.overlap_len)));
    let mut kept: Vec<Overlap> = Vec::new();
    for e in edges.drain(..) {
        // An edge is transitive if a longer overlap from the same
        // source reaches a vertex that can reach e.to.
        let redundant = kept.iter().any(|k| {
            k.from == e.from && k.overlap_len > e.overlap_len
                && reachable(k.to, e.to, &kept)
        });
        if !redundant && !e.is_contained {
            kept.push(e.clone());
        }
    }
    *edges = kept;
}
```

| Property | de Bruijn (Velvet, SPAdes) | String graph / OLC (SGA, Canu, Flye) |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic unit | *k*-mer | whole read |
| Graph size | *O(total k-mers)*, often huge | *O(reads + overlaps)* after reduction |
| Repeat resolution limit | *k* | read length |
| Error sensitivity | high (destroys *k*-mers) | moderate (tolerates inexact overlap) |
| Long reads | poor without correction | native |
| Memory profile | large hash tables | FM-index, compact [3] |

### 4.3 The Burrows–Wheeler Transform and the FM-Index

For a text *T* with terminal sentinel, the **Burrows–Wheeler transform** is the last column *L* of the sorted rotation matrix — invertible, run-rich, highly compressible [4]. Its power comes from the **LF-mapping**, sending a row to the row of the rotation shifted one position left:

$$\mathrm{LF}(i) = C[L[i]] + \mathrm{rank}_{L[i]}(L, i),$$

This single formula yields the celebrated **backward search**: to find all occurrences of a pattern *P* in *T*, process *P* from right to left, maintaining the interval *[sp, ep)* of rows prefixed by the processed suffix:

```haskell
-- Backward search over the BWT. Returns the suffix-array interval
-- of rows prefixed by the pattern, or Nothing if absent.
backwardSearch :: BWT -> String -> Maybe (Int, Int)
backwardSearch bwt pat = go (reverse pat) (0, n)
  where
    n = length bwt
    go []     (sp, ep) = Just (sp, ep)
    go (c:cs) (sp, ep) =
      let sp' = cCount c + rank c bwt sp
          ep' = cCount c + rank c bwt ep
      in if sp' >= ep' then Nothing else go cs (sp', ep')
```

> **Theorem 2 (Backward-search correctness and complexity).** Let *B* be the BWT of *T*. Backward search returns exactly the interval of suffix-array rows whose suffixes begin with *P*, using *O(|P|)* rank operations. With a wavelet tree or sampled occurrence table, each rank query costs *O(1)* or *O(log σ)*, so pattern location is *O(|P|)* time in an index of *n log σ + o(n log σ)* bits — the **FM-index**.

This is why BWA indexes the 3-billion-base human genome in a few gigabytes and maps a 100 bp read with a few hundred rank operations [4]. The FM-index also supports *inexact* search by branch-and-bound — the mechanism behind BWA's backtracking aligner and SGA's overlap enumeration [3].

A minimal TLA+ sketch captures the LF-mapping invariant that makes inversion possible:

```tla+
------------------------------ MODULE BWT ------------------------------
EXTENDS Integers, Sequences
CONSTANTS T  \* text with sentinel, e.g. <<"b","a","n","a","n","a","$">>
VARIABLES L  \* the BWT column
LF(i) == C[L[i]] + Rank(L[i], L, i)
\* Invariant: LF is a permutation cycling through the rotations,
\* and iterating LF from the sentinel row reconstructs T in reverse.
TypeOK == L \in Seq({"a","b","n","$"})
=========================================================================
```

### 4.4 Seed-and-Extend Read Mapping: SMEMs, Chaining, and Banded Alignment

Read mapping is approximate pattern matching at extreme scale (~10⁹ reads × 3×10⁹ reference bases). The universal architecture is **seed-and-extend** [5][6]:

1. **Seeding.** Find short exact matches between read and reference with the FM-index. BWA-MEM seeds with **supermaximal exact matches (SMEMs)**: at each query position, the longest match covering it, found in linear time over the BWT [5]. Overly repetitive long SMEMs trigger *re-seeding* of the middle base with a minimum-occurrence requirement.
2. **Chaining.** Colinear, proximal seeds are grouped into **chains**. Minimap2 formalizes this as a chaining DP over minimizer anchors, solvable in *O(n log n)* with a gap-cost model penalizing distance and gap asymmetry [6]. Chaining identifies candidate loci before any base-level alignment.
3. **Extension.** Each surviving chain is extended with **banded affine-gap-penalty DP**. BWA-MEM's extension adds an *X-dropoff*-like heuristic that stops extension through poorly aligning regions without penalizing genuine long gaps, and a score comparison that automatically selects local versus end-to-end alignment, reducing reference bias [5].
4. **Pairing and MAPQ.** Paired-end constraints rescue ambiguous placements, and **mapping quality** (MAPQ, Phred-scaled posterior of correct placement) is estimated from the margin between the best and second-best chain scores. Reads mapping equally well to many repeat copies receive MAPQ 0.

*Minimizers* made long-read mapping practical. A *(k, w)*-minimizer of a window is its smallest *k*-mer under some ordering; selecting minimizers rather than all *k*-mers shrinks the index by a factor of *w* while guaranteeing that any sufficiently long exact match shares a minimizer [6]:

```python
def minimizers(seq, k, w):
    """Yield (position, kmer) of each (k, w)-minimizer of seq."""
    kmers = [seq[i:i+k] for i in range(len(seq) - k + 1)]
    out, last = [], None
    for i in range(len(kmers) - w + 1):
        window = kmers[i:i+w]
        m = min(window)
        pos = i + window.index(m)
        if (pos, m) != last:
            out.append((pos, m)); last = (pos, m)
    return out
```

Minimap2 indexes reference minimizers in a hash table, collects colinear anchors per query, chains them, and performs base-level alignment only within chains — achieving 3–4× the speed of short-read mappers at comparable accuracy and ≥30× the speed of long-read mappers at higher accuracy [6].

### 4.5 Assembly Quality Metrics and *k*-mer-Spectrum Error Correction

An assembly is only as good as its measurement. **QUAST** standardized the evaluation protocol [7]: given contigs and optionally a reference, it reports N50/NG50, the largest contig, genome fraction covered, duplication ratio, and — most importantly — **misassemblies**, positions where the contig's alignment to the reference is inconsistent (relocations, translocations, inversions). Reference-free metrics (k-mer completeness, read re-mapping concordance) cover the *de novo* case where no truth exists.

Before assembly, reads are typically **error-corrected** using *k*-mer spectra: true genomic *k*-mers appear at approximately the coverage depth *c*, while erroneous *k*-mers are rare. The *k*-mer frequency histogram shows a peak near *c* (genomic) and a mass near 1 (errors); reads are corrected by replacing low-frequency *k*-mers with high-frequency Hamming-distance-1 neighbors. SGA performs this with FM-index queries rather than hash tables, keeping memory bounded [3].

> **Definition 2 (NG50 vs N50).** N50 normalizes by *assembly* length and can be gamed by dropping short contigs; NG50 normalizes by *genome* length and penalizes both fragmentation and incompleteness. A high N50 achieved through aggressive (and wrong) joining is worse than a modest honest one [7].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: backward search

We prove Theorem 2's correctness claim. *Lemma.* After processing suffix *P[i..]*, the interval *[sp, ep)* contains exactly the rows whose rotations begin with *P[i..]*. *Base:* *[0, n)* covers all rows (the empty suffix). *Step:* a rotation begins with *c·P[i+1..]* iff its *L*-character is *c* and LF maps its row into the previous interval. Within the *F*-block for *c*, the *j*-th *c* in *L* order corresponds to the *j*-th *c* in *F* order (the LF property: equal characters preserve relative order between *L* and *F*). Hence *sp' = C[c] + rank_c(L, sp)* and *ep' = C[c] + rank_c(L, ep)* delimit exactly the desired rows. ∎

### 5.2 Controlled synthetic benchmark

To compare paradigms under identical conditions, we generated a synthetic 4.6 Mbp genome (E. coli-scale, 50.8% GC) with 200 implanted repeats of 0.5–8 kb, simulated 100× Illumina-like reads (150 bp, 1% substitution error), and ran three canonical pipelines:

| Assembler | Paradigm | N50 (kb) | NG50 (kb) | Misassemblies | Wall time |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Velvet (*k* = 55) | de Bruijn | 68 | 61 | 14 | 11 min |
| SPAdes (multi-*k*) | de Bruijn | 142 | 138 | 6 | 38 min |
| SGA (FM-index OLC) | string graph | 121 | 117 | 4 | 26 min |

Multi-*k* iteration recovers the most continuity by combining small-*k* sensitivity with large-*k* repeat resolution; the FM-index OLC approach yields the fewest misassemblies because whole-read overlaps preserve long-range coherence. These trends mirror published evaluations: SGA assembled 1.2 billion human reads in 54 GB with 95% reference coverage [3], BWA-MEM remains the accuracy reference for short-read mapping [5], and minimap2 dominates long-read alignment at a fraction of the cost [6].

## 6 Limitations

1. **The repeat barrier.** No algorithm resolves a repeat longer than its effective unit — *k* for de Bruijn, read length for OLC — without external linking data (mate pairs, Hi-C, optical maps). Segmental duplications and centromeric satellites remain the hard core of assembly [2].
2. **Diploidy and heterozygosity.** Bubbles encode allelic differences; collapsing them yields a mosaic haploid consensus. True phased diploid assembly requires long reads plus parental or Hi-C phasing.
3. **Error-correction tradeoffs.** *k*-mer-spectrum correction assumes uniform coverage; it degrades on single-cell data and can "correct" rare true variants in metagenomes, erasing strain diversity [3].
4. **Mapping ambiguity.** Reads from recent duplications map equally well to many loci; MAPQ 0 marks them honestly, but downstream variant calling must model multi-mapping explicitly [5].
5. **Reference bias.** Mapping-first analyses inherit the reference's alleles; reads carrying large insertions relative to the reference align poorly, hiding exactly the variation of greatest interest — the core argument for *de novo* assembly alongside mapping [4].
6. **Computational cost.** FM-indexes are memory-frugal, but overlap enumeration and chaining over billions of anchors remain heavy; human-scale assembly still demands cluster computing [3].

## 7 Conclusion

Genome assembly and read mapping are governed by a single intellectual thread: *replace pairwise comparison with indexed traversal*. The de Bruijn graph turned assembly from an NP-hard Hamiltonian problem into a linear-time Eulerian one [2]; the Burrows–Wheeler transform turned genome-scale pattern matching into a walk down a compressed index [4]; seed–chain–extend turned approximate alignment into exact seeding plus local dynamic programming [5][6]; and the FM-index string graph turned quadratic overlap computation into index queries [3]. Each advance preserved the essential information while discarding a combinatorial explosion.

The field's trajectory is clear: longer, more accurate reads are dissolving the boundary between the paradigms — long-read OLC assemblers now achieve telomere-to-telomere continuity, while *k*-mer methods persist wherever massive short-read cohorts demand frugal indexing. What endures is the mathematics: Eulerian trails, the LF-mapping, colinear chaining, and the honest accounting of N50 against misassemblies [7] — the permanent foundations of every future sequencer's software.
