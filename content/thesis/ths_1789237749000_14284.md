# The Neuroscience of Navigation: Place Cells, Grid Cells, and the Brain's Cognitive Map

## Abstract

How does the brain know where it is? Half a century of electrophysiological, computational, and human imaging research converges on a single answer: the hippocampal formation constructs an *allocentric cognitive map* of the environment from functionally specialized cell populations — **place cells** that encode the animal's location, **head-direction cells** that encode its orientation, **border cells** that encode environmental boundaries, and **grid cells** that tile space with a periodic hexagonal metric. This thesis traces the arc from O'Keefe and Dostrovsky's 1971 discovery of hippocampal place cells [1] through the Mosers' 2005 discovery of medial entorhinal grid cells [3] to the 2014 Nobel Prize, develops the continuous attractor network theory of path integration [5], proves the optimality of the hexagonal grid as the densest planar packing, and reviews converging human fMRI and intracranial evidence [7][8]. It closes with the consequences for artificial navigation, where grid-cell-like representations now structure deep reinforcement learning and world models.

## 1 Introduction

In 1948, Edward Tolman reported that rats navigating mazes behave as if they possess an internal *cognitive map* — a representation of spatial layout that supports novel shortcuts rather than mere stimulus-response chains [12]. Tolman's claim was behaviorally motivated and neurally ungrounded, and for two decades it remained a tantalizing hypothesis. The neural grounding arrived in 1971, when John O'Keefe and Jonathan Dostrovsky recorded single units in the freely moving rat hippocampus and found cells that fired only when the animal occupied particular locations — *place cells* [1]. The firing of these neurons was neither sensory nor motor: it correlated with the animal's position in an *allocentric* (world-centered) reference frame. From that three-page paper grew the proposition that the hippocampus furnishes "the rest of the brain with a spatial reference map" [1].

Three decades later, May-Britt and Edvard Moser's laboratory, probing one synapse upstream in the medial entorhinal cortex (MEC), discovered a second, stranger spatial code: *grid cells*, each of which fires at the vertices of a periodic hexagonal lattice that tiles the entire environment [3]. Where place cells are metric-poor — a given cell says *here*, but not *how far to the next here* — the grid supplies the missing metric. Between and around these two discoveries, head-direction cells (Taube, Muller, and Ranck, 1990) [10] and border cells (Solstad et al., 2008) [9] filled out a complete positioning system. The 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, awarded to O'Keefe and to the Mosers, ratified the committee's judgment that these cells "constitute a comprehensive positioning system, an inner GPS, in the brain" [8].

This thesis is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews the electrophysiological discoveries and their anatomical organization. Section 3 describes the methods by which these codes are measured and modeled. Section 4 develops the four technical pillars: the place-cell code and remapping, the grid-cell code and its geometry, continuous attractor dynamics, and path integration with error correction. Section 5 marshals the empirical and formal arguments — including the optimality of the hexagonal lattice and exponential resolution scaling — and Section 6 confronts the limitations. Section 7 draws out implications for memory and for artificial navigation.

## 2 Background

### The hippocampal formation

The hippocampal formation comprises the dentate gyrus (DG), the CA fields of hippocampus proper (CA3, CA1), the subicular complex, and the entorhinal cortex. In the rodent it lies dorsally; its human homologue sits in the medial temporal lobe [2]. The dominant cortical input arrives through the *perforant path* from entorhinal cortex layer II to DG and CA3, then via Schaffer collaterals to CA1, then out through the subiculum — the classical trisynaptic circuit. It is this circuit, and especially its entorhinal gateway, that hosts the spatial code.

### Place cells

O'Keefe and Dostrovsky's initial report documented eight hippocampal complex-spike cells with spatially localized firing [1]. O'Keefe's 1976 follow-up established the canonical properties [4]: each place cell fires at high rate within a compact *place field* (typically 20–40 cm in the dorsal hippocampus of a rat, larger ventrally), is silent elsewhere, and collectively the population covers the environment with overlapping fields. Crucially, place fields are *allocentric*: rotating the animal's heading does not move the field, whereas rotating distal landmarks rotates it [4]. When the environment changes sufficiently, the mapping reconfigures — *remapping*: the same cell may develop a new field or go silent, so that each environment gets an orthogonal representation supporting episodic memory [4].

### The entorhinal positioning system

In 2005, Hafting, Fyhn, Molden, Moser, and Moser recorded in rat MEC during foraging in large arenas and found cells with multiple firing fields arranged in a *triangular lattice* covering the whole surface — grid cells [3]. Approximately half of MEC layer II principal cells passed grid criteria. Each grid is parameterized by three quantities: **spacing** (inter-field distance), **orientation** (lattice tilt relative to a reference axis), and **phase** (x–y offset) [3]. Spacing increases systematically along the dorsoventral MEC axis, matching the dorsal-to-ventral place-field enlargement in hippocampus; orientation is shared among co-localized cells while phase is uniformly distributed [3]. Surrounding populations were soon characterized: head-direction cells and conjunctive grid-by-direction cells in deeper MEC layers [13], and border cells firing at geometric boundaries [9], plus speed cells encoding running velocity [8].

## 3 Methodology

**Electrophysiology.** Place and grid cells are recorded in freely moving animals with implanted tetrodes — bundles of four fine wires whose spike waveforms are clustered to isolate single units [1][3]. Rate maps are constructed by binning spikes into spatial pixels and dividing by occupancy. Gridness is quantified by the sixfold rotational symmetry of the spatial autocorrelogram [3].

**Human imaging.** Because single-unit recording is rarely available in humans, Doeller, Barry, and Burgess (2010) exploited a predicted population signature: because all grid cells share a common lattice orientation within an animal, BOLD activity in entorhinal cortex should be higher when a subject moves *along* grid axes than across them, producing a sixfold (60°) symmetric modulation by heading direction [7]. Direct confirmation later came from intracranial recordings in epilepsy patients [8].

**Computational modeling.** Continuous attractor networks (Section 4.3) are simulated as rate-based recurrent networks on a two-dimensional neural sheet with translation-invariant, center-surround connectivity, driven by velocity inputs [5]. Optimality analyses compare lattice geometries by their circle-packing density and by the Fisher information of population decoding [11].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Place-Cell Code: Allocentric Position and Remapping

A place cell's firing rate can be written as a function of position alone, *r*(**x**), to first approximation independent of the animal's heading or speed. The population of *N* overlapping fields implements a distributed code: position is decoded from the vector of firing rates, with resolution set by field width and coverage density. Two computational properties make this code remarkable.

First, *allocentricity*: fields are anchored to distal cues rather than to the animal's body. Rotating a cue card on the wall rotates fields by the same angle; translating the animal does not [4]. Second, *remapping*: across environments the place code reorganizes — *global remapping* changes field locations wholesale, while *rate remapping* preserves locations but changes rates [4]. The dentate gyrus is thought to *pattern-separate* similar environments, CA3 to *pattern-complete* from partial cues, and CA1 to output a stable index suitable for binding "what" to "where" in episodic memory. Remapping thus reconciles the two great theories of hippocampal function — space and memory — by giving each distinct experience a distinct spatial address [8].

> **Observation 4.1.** Place fields persist in darkness, updated by self-motion alone, demonstrating that the map is maintained by *path integration* even without external cues [6].

### 4.2 The Grid-Cell Code: A Hexagonal Metric for Space

Grid cells differ from place cells in two decisive ways: their fields are many and periodic, and the lattice is *rigid* across environments. When hippocampal place cells remap, grid cells *realign* — the lattice translates and rotates as a coherent unit, preserving all inter-cell phase relationships [3]. The grid thus behaves like graph paper: fixed geometry, movable origin.

The hexagonal (equivalently, triangular) arrangement is not incidental. Consider each firing field as a disk of radius *σ* on the plane. For fixed field size, the lattice that maximizes the fraction of space covered per field — and therefore maximizes the code's spatial resolution per neuron — is the one that packs circles most densely:

> **Theorem 4.2 (Optimal lattice).** *Among all planar lattices, the triangular lattice maximizes the circle-packing density, attaining δ = π/(2√3) ≈ 0.9069. Hence, under fixed field size and fixed neuron budget, the hexagonal grid maximizes coverage, and equivalently minimizes the mean decoding error of position.*

*Proof sketch.* The densest packing of equal circles in the plane is achieved by the hexagonal arrangement — the classical result of Thue (1892) and Tóth (1940). A unit cell of the triangular lattice with lattice constant *a* contains one lattice point and has area (√3/2)*a*²; with touching circles of radius *a*/2 the covered fraction is π(*a*/2)² / ((√3/2)*a*²) = π/(2√3). Any square lattice gives π/4 ≈ 0.785 < 0.9069. ∎

Beyond packing, the grid code enjoys an exponential advantage. Mathis, Herz, and Stemmler (2012) showed that a *nested* set of grid modules with geometrically increasing spacings encodes position with resolution that grows *exponentially* in the number of neurons, because each module resolves ambiguity left by the coarser ones — whereas place cells with fixed field size scale only linearly [11]. A few dozen grid cells per module across a handful of modules can thus localize the animal to centimeter precision over meter-scale arenas.

Grid cells also exhibit *theta phase precession*: spikes occur at progressively earlier phases of the 4–10 Hz theta oscillation as the animal traverses a field [13]. Phase precession compresses behavioral-timescale trajectories into theta-timescale spike sequences, a format suitable for spike-timing-dependent plasticity and for "sweeping" future paths — a candidate mechanism for planning.

### 4.3 Continuous Attractor Networks: Dynamics of the Cognitive Map

The leading mechanistic account of grid and head-direction codes is the *continuous attractor network* (CAN). In a CAN, recurrent connectivity is translation-invariant with local excitation and surround inhibition ("Mexican hat"), so that the network sustains a localized *bump* of activity that can sit stably at any position on a continuous manifold — a ring for head direction, a two-dimensional sheet (with twisted-torus topology under periodic boundary conditions) for grids [5].

The dynamics of a rate-based CAN are:

```
τ drᵢ/dt = −rᵢ + φ( Σⱼ Wᵢⱼ rⱼ + Iᵢ(t) )
```

where *W* encodes the center-surround coupling, *I*(*t*) is external drive (velocity, landmarks), and *φ* is a nonlinearity. Symmetric velocity-shifted projections from head-direction or speed-modulated populations translate the bump in proportion to the animal's movement, so that the bump's neural coordinates *integrate* the velocity signal — i.e., the network performs path integration autonomously [5]. Single-cell grid firing is then read out as the bump periodically passes over each neuron's position on the sheet: a single neuron fires whenever the bump crosses its location, producing the hexagonal lattice in *physical* space as a shadow of a bump moving on a *neural* sheet with hexagonal connectivity.

Burak and Fiete (2009) supplied the crucial quantitative result: earlier CAN models accumulated integration error so rapidly that frequent sensory resets were required, casting doubt on the dead-reckoning hypothesis [5]. By analyzing noise-driven drift of the bump, they showed that with plausible parameters and Poisson-like spike variability, a CAN can integrate velocity accurately over **10–100 m and 1–10 minutes** — comfortably spanning a foraging bout — and that both periodic (toroidal) and aperiodic networks achieve this, with network size setting the error rate [5]. The model makes a falsifiable prediction: pairs of grid cells with overlapping fields in one environment must maintain their phase relationship in every environment — exactly the rigidity observed experimentally [3].

A compact numerical sketch of the core mechanism is:

```python
import numpy as np

def mexican_hat(d2, sigma_exc=1.0, sigma_inh=2.0, A=1.0):
    """Center-surround connectivity as a function of squared neural distance."""
    return A * (np.exp(-d2 / (2 * sigma_exc**2))
                - 0.6 * np.exp(-d2 / (2 * sigma_inh**2)))

def step(r, W, I, tau=10e-3, dt=1e-3, phi=np.tanh):
    """One Euler step of the continuous attractor dynamics."""
    return r + (dt / tau) * (-r + phi(W @ r + I))

# Velocity coupling: shift the bump along direction theta at speed v.
def velocity_input(theta, v, gain=0.05, n=64):
    xs = np.arange(n) - n / 2
    shift = gain * v * np.array([np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)])
    # asymmetric drive ~ directional derivative of the bump profile
    return np.outer(np.exp(-(xs - shift[0])**2 / 8), np.ones(n)).ravel()
```

### 4.4 Path Integration, Error Correction, and Sensory Anchoring

Path integration — updating position by integrating velocity over time — is intrinsically fragile: noise accumulates as a random walk, so error grows as √*t* without correction [6]. The hippocampal formation solves this with a hybrid architecture. *Self-motion* signals (vestibular, proprioceptive, optic flow, motor efference copy, encoded by speed and head-direction cells) drive the integrator; *external* signals (visual landmarks, boundaries via border cells, olfactory cues) periodically *re-anchor* it. McNaughton and colleagues formalized this as a coupled system in which the path integrator proposes and sensory input disposes: when landmark evidence is strong, the bump is pulled toward the landmark-consistent location, correcting accumulated drift; when cues are absent, the system free-runs [6].

Remapping is the signature of re-anchoring: when the environment changes enough, the attractor settles into a new, orthogonal configuration rather than stretching the old one [4]. And the grid's multi-module structure provides a built-in error check: because modules have incommensurate spacings, a drift error in one module is inconsistent with the others, enabling the population to detect and correct faults — the same principle as a residue number system [11].

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

**Discovery and replication.** Grid cells have been reported in rats [3], mice, crawling bats (in 3D volumetric lattices; Yartsev et al., 2011), monkeys (Killian et al., 2012), and humans [8] — a phylogenetic breadth arguing for a conserved mammalian positioning system [13].

**Human evidence.** Doeller, Barry, and Burgess (2010) scanned 42 participants navigating a virtual arena and found sixfold-symmetric BOLD modulation in right entorhinal cortex aligned to each participant's putative grid orientation; participants with stronger grid-like signals were significantly better navigators [7]. Jacobs et al. (2013) then recorded grid-like single units directly in human entorhinal cortex and cingulate cortex during virtual navigation, with field spacings of roughly 1–6 m in real-world units [8]. Sixfold symmetry has since been detected for *conceptual* spaces — e.g., morphing bird stimuli — suggesting the grid is a general-purpose code for continuous relational knowledge, not merely physical space [7].

**Formal results.** (i) The hexagonal lattice uniquely maximizes planar packing density (Theorem 4.2), minimizing decoding error per neuron. (ii) Nested grid modules yield exponential resolution scaling in neuron count versus linear scaling for place cells [11]. (iii) Continuous attractor networks integrate velocity with bounded drift over behaviorally relevant distances and times, and predict rigid inter-cell phase relationships, confirmed experimentally [5]. (iv) Non-negativity and translation invariance of the code provably force hexagonal periodicity in trained networks (Sorscher et al., 2023) [13].

**Artificial navigation.** These principles have crossed into machine intelligence. Banino et al. (2018) showed that deep reinforcement-learning agents trained to path-integrate spontaneously develop grid-like units and navigate more effectively; the Tolman–Eichenbaum Machine (Whittington et al., 2018) treats hippocampal–entorhinal circuitry as a general *world model* factorizing structure from sensory content [13]. The brain's inner GPS has become a blueprint for artificial spatial intelligence.

## 6 Limitations

The cognitive-map program faces serious objections. First, the *memory–space debate*: hippocampal lesions impair non-spatial relational memory (as in patient H.M.; Scoville & Milner, 1957), and Eichenbaum and colleagues argue the hippocampus encodes relational organization generally, with space as one instance [8]. Second, causality is thin: grid cells correlate with path integration, but selective grid-cell lesions that spare place cells are technically difficult, and in humans the entorhinal cortex does not appear strictly *necessary* for path integration [13]. Third, the attractor theory's requirement of precise symmetric connectivity is biologically demanding; oscillatory-interference models offer an alternative single-cell mechanism, and the field has not decisively adjudicated between them [5]. Fourth, three-dimensional grid structure in flying bats is less regular than the planar lattice, suggesting the code degrades gracefully rather than perfectly in 3D [13]. Finally, fMRI grid signatures are indirect: sixfold BOLD symmetry is consistent with, but does not prove, single-neuron hexagonal firing [7].

## 7 Conclusion

From Tolman's behavioral conjecture [12] to O'Keefe's place cells [1], from the Mosers' hexagonal grids [3] to Burak and Fiete's attractor dynamics [5], the neuroscience of navigation has converged on a unified picture: the hippocampal formation implements a *metric cognitive map* in which position, direction, boundary, and speed are factorized across specialized cell types and integrated by recurrent dynamics that are provably near-optimal — the hexagon is the densest packing, the nested modules the most efficient code [11]. Human imaging confirms the machinery operates in our own brains and predicts our navigational ability [7][8]. The open frontier is generality: if the entorhinal–hippocampal system is a universal engine for representing continuous relational structure, then the "cognitive map" is not only how brains navigate space, but how minds navigate knowledge itself — and how machines might learn to do the same.
