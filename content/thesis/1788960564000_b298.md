# Neural Radiance Fields: Volumetric Scene Representation, Positional Encoding, and Hierarchical Volume Sampling for Photorealistic View Synthesis

## Abstract

This thesis presents a rigorous treatment of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [1], a class of continuous volumetric scene representations that encode the geometry and view-dependent appearance of real and synthetic scenes within the weights of a multilayer perceptron (MLP). We analyze the three pillars of the method: (i) the five-dimensional neural radiance function $F_{\Theta}: (\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \mapsto (\mathbf{c}, \sigma)$ mapping 3D position and 2D viewing direction to emitted radiance and volume density; (ii) the sinusoidal positional encoding operator that lifts low-dimensional coordinates into a high-frequency feature space, thereby overcoming the spectral bias of MLPs; and (iii) the hierarchical volume sampling procedure that concentrates model capacity on visible scene content via coarse-to-fine importance sampling. We derive the differentiable volume rendering integral and its numerical quadrature, formalize positional encoding as a stationary kernel embedding, and trace the lineage from NeRF through Mip-NeRF [2], Mip-NeRF 360 [3], NeRF in the Wild [4], and tensorial acceleration schemes [6]. Quantitative results, theoretical guarantees, limitations, and open directions complete the analysis.

## 1 Introduction

Novel view synthesis — the problem of rendering unseen viewpoints of a scene from a sparse set of input photographs — has been a central ambition of computer vision for decades. Classical approaches bifurcate into *geometry-first* pipelines, which reconstruct explicit surfaces and reproject textures, and *image-based rendering* methods, which interpolate rays among captured views. Both families suffer from characteristic failure modes: the former from brittle reconstruction in textureless or specular regions, the latter from ghosting and view-dependent inconsistency when the input set is sparse.

Neural Radiance Fields [1] proposed a radical simplification: represent the scene as a *continuous* function parameterized by a neural network, and optimize it end-to-end by differentiating through the classical volume rendering integral. Given only posed RGB images, NeRF learns a 5D radiance field that, once trained, can be queried from arbitrary camera poses to produce photorealistic novel views at resolutions far beyond the capacity of contemporary mesh- or voxel-based neural representations. As surveyed comprehensively in [5], this single formulation catalyzed hundreds of follow-on works spanning dynamic scenes, generative modeling, inverse rendering, and robotics.

Three technical decisions distinguish NeRF from earlier neural scene representations, and each is the subject of deep analysis in this thesis:

1. **The volumetric MLP representation.** Rather than storing features on a discretized grid, NeRF encodes the scene implicitly in the weights of a compact MLP, queried as $F_{\Theta}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) = (\mathbf{c}, \sigma)$, where $\sigma(\mathbf{x})$ is the volume density at position $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d})$ is the emitted RGB radiance in direction $\mathbf{d}$. The crucial subtlety is that density is made *independent of viewing direction* while color is direction-dependent, so that multi-view consistency constrains geometry while specularities and translucency can still vary with the camera.
2. **Positional encoding.** Vanilla MLPs exhibit *spectral bias*: they learn low-frequency functions far more readily than high-frequency ones. NeRF overcomes this by mapping each coordinate through a bank of sinusoids at exponentially increasing frequencies, $\gamma_L(u) = (\sin(2^0\pi u), \cos(2^0\pi u), \ldots, \sin(2^{L-1}\pi u), \cos(2^{L-1}\pi u))$, before the first linear layer [1].
3. **Hierarchical volume sampling.** Evaluating the rendering integral by dense uniform sampling is wasteful: most of the ray traverses empty space. NeRF trains a *coarse* network on stratified samples, converts its predicted densities into a piecewise-constant probability distribution along the ray, and resamples a *fine* network from that distribution — a form of learned importance sampling.

The remainder of this thesis formalizes each component, connects positional encoding to kernel theory, analyzes sampling as variance reduction, and evaluates the method empirically against prior art.

## 2 Background

The intellectual ancestry of NeRF runs through three traditions. The first is the *plenoptic function* — the 7D function $P(x, y, z, \theta, \phi, \lambda, t)$ describing all light rays in space-time — whose 5D static-slice $P(x, y, z, \theta, \phi)$ NeRF approximates with a neural surrogate. The second is *classical volume rendering*, developed in the 1980s and 1990s for scientific visualization: the emission-absorption model expresses pixel color as an integral of attenuated radiance along a ray, precisely the equation NeRF differentiates through [4][5]. The third is *learned 3D representations*: voxel grids, octrees, signed distance functions, and occupancy networks each offered differentiability at the cost of resolution limits, memory scaling, or discretization artifacts.

Early neural view-synthesis systems each committed to one of these representations. Voxel-based approaches such as DeepVoxels stored learned features on a coarse $32^3$ grid and decoded them with 3D convolutions; they produced impressive results for the time but consumed hundreds of megabytes per scene and blurred fine detail [5]. Surface-based methods (e.g., Scene Representation Networks) predicted signed distance fields and rendered via sphere tracing, inheriting the topological rigidity of explicit surfaces and failing on fuzzy or semi-transparent geometry such as foliage, smoke, or hair.

NeRF's volumetric formulation inherits the best of both: like voxel methods it handles *arbitrary topology and translucency* naturally, since density is a soft quantity rather than a hard surface; like implicit methods it is *continuous and memory-efficient*, requiring only the ~5 MB of MLP weights rather than a dense grid. The survey [5] organizes the subsequent explosion of NeRF variants — dynamic, generative, and unbounded-scene extensions — all of which retain this volumetric core.

A second key precursor is the observation, formalized around the same period, that coordinate-based MLPs are biased toward low frequencies. This *spectral bias* meant that feeding raw $(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d})$ coordinates into an MLP yielded reconstructions that were accurate in smooth regions but missing sharp edges and texture — precisely the failure NeRF's positional encoding was designed to cure [1].

## 3 Methodology

### The neural radiance function

NeRF defines a scene as a function

$$F_{\Theta}: (\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \longmapsto (\mathbf{c}, \sigma), \qquad \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3,\ \mathbf{d} \in \mathbb{S}^2,$$

where $\sigma(\mathbf{x}) \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ is the differential probability of a ray terminating at $\mathbf{x}$ (the volume density) and $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \in [0, 1]^3$ is the direction-dependent emitted color. The network is structured so that density depends only on position: the first stage, $\text{MLP}_{\theta_1}$, maps the positionally encoded coordinate $\gamma_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{r}(t))$ to $(\sigma(t), \mathbf{z}(t))$, where $\mathbf{z}(t)$ is a 256-dimensional feature vector; the second stage, $\text{MLP}_{\theta_2}$, maps $(\mathbf{z}(t), \gamma_{\mathbf{d}}(\mathbf{d}))$ to color [4]. ReLU is applied to $\sigma$ and a sigmoid to $\mathbf{c}$.

The concrete architecture used in [1] is an 8-layer MLP with 256 units per layer and ReLU activations, with a skip connection concatenating the encoded input to the fifth layer's activations; the view-dependent branch adds a 128-unit layer. With $L=10$ encoding bands for position (60 dimensions) and $L=4$ for direction (24 dimensions), the input is 84-dimensional.

### Differentiable volume rendering

A pixel's color is the expected radiance along its camera ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}$ for $t \in [t_n, t_f]$:

$$C(\mathbf{r}) = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t)\, \sigma(\mathbf{r}(t))\, \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(t), \mathbf{d})\, dt, \qquad T(t) = \exp\!\left(-\int_{t_n}^{t} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s))\, ds\right),$$

where $T(t)$ is the accumulated transmittance — the probability the ray travels from $t_n$ to $t$ without occlusion [4]. The integral is estimated by stratified sampling: the interval is partitioned into $N$ bins and one sample drawn uniformly per bin, $t_i \sim \mathcal{U}[t_n + \frac{i-1}{N}(t_f - t_n),\, t_n + \frac{i}{N}(t_f - t_n)]$. Setting $\delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i$ and $\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)$, the quadrature is

$$\hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i\, \alpha_i\, \mathbf{c}_i, \qquad T_i = \exp\!\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_j\right).$$

Every operation is differentiable in the network weights, so the photometric loss $\mathcal{L} = \sum_{\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{R}} \lVert C(\mathbf{r}) - \hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) \rVert_2^2$ over a random batch $\mathcal{R}$ of rays (typically 4096 per iteration) is minimized with Adam for 100k–300k iterations [1].

### Hierarchical sampling

The coarse network is first evaluated at $N_c = 64$ stratified samples; its outputs define normalized weights $\hat{w}_i = w_i / \sum_j w_j$ with $w_i = T_i \alpha_i$, interpreted as a piecewise-constant PDF over the ray. $N_f = 128$ additional samples are drawn from this distribution by inverse transform sampling and evaluated with the fine network on the union of all $N_c + N_f$ samples. Both networks are supervised: $\mathcal{L} = \sum_{\mathbf{r}} (\lVert C - \hat{C}_c \rVert_2^2 + \lVert C - \hat{C}_f \rVert_2^2)$, which forces the coarse density to become a useful proposal distribution [1].

```python
import numpy as np

def positional_encoding(x, L):
    """NeRF-style sinusoidal encoding of a scalar coordinate."""
    freqs = 2.0 ** np.arange(L) * np.pi
    return np.concatenate([np.sin(freqs * x), np.cos(freqs * x)])

def render_ray(sigmas, colors, deltas):
    """Volume-rendering quadrature along one ray (Eq. above)."""
    alpha = 1.0 - np.exp(-sigmas * deltas)
    T = np.exp(-np.cumsum(np.concatenate([[0.0], sigmas[:-1] * deltas[:-1]])))
    weights = T * alpha
    return weights @ colors, weights
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Spectral Bias and the Necessity of High-Frequency Features

> **Theorem:** *An MLP with standard initialization and gradient descent learns the eigenfunctions of its Neural Tangent Kernel in order of decreasing eigenvalue; for shift-invariant kernels on the sphere or torus, these eigenvalues decay with frequency, so low-frequency components are fit exponentially faster than high-frequency ones.*

This spectral bias is fatal for naive coordinate MLPs: without encoding, NeRF reconstructions are conspicuously blurry, lacking the texture and edge fidelity that define photorealism. The ablation in [1] demonstrates that removing positional encoding collapses fine detail even while coarse geometry remains plausible. The encoding $\gamma_L$ is therefore not a cosmetic feature but the mechanism that reconditions the optimization landscape — it converts the problem of *learning* high-frequency functions into one of *selecting* among precomputed high-frequency basis functions, a dramatically easier optimization task [1][5].

### 4.2 The Positional Encoding Operator: Kernel Theory and Design Choices

Applying $\gamma_L$ to each coordinate independently yields a feature map whose induced kernel $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \langle \gamma_L(\mathbf{x}), \gamma_L(\mathbf{x}') \rangle$ is *stationary* — it depends only on $\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'$ — and tunable: increasing $L$ widens the kernel's spectral support. The NeRF authors chose $L=10$ for position and $L=4$ for direction after ablation; the direction encoding is deliberately shallower because view-dependent effects (specular lobes, Fresnel terms) vary more smoothly than geometric detail [1].

Mip-NeRF [2] revealed a subtle failure of point-sampled encoding: a single ray per pixel ignores the pixel's footprint, so features sampled at different distances are ambiguous, producing aliasing when zooming. The fix is the *integrated positional encoding* (IPE): each conical frustum along the cone cast from a pixel is approximated by a multivariate Gaussian with mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, and the encoding is replaced by its expectation,

$$\gamma(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} \sin(2^\ell \boldsymbol{\mu}) \exp(-2^{2\ell-1} \mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})) \\ \cos(2^\ell \boldsymbol{\mu}) \exp(-2^{2\ell-1} \mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})) \end{bmatrix} \right\}_{\ell=0}^{L-1},$$

so that high-frequency bands are automatically attenuated inside large frustums — a learned, continuous analogue of mipmapping [2]. This single change lets one *multiscale* MLP replace the coarse/fine pair, cutting model size roughly in half while improving quality [2].

### 4.3 Hierarchical Volume Sampling as Learned Importance Sampling

Hierarchical sampling can be understood rigorously as variance reduction for the Monte Carlo estimator of $C(\mathbf{r})$. The rendering weights $w_i = T_i(1 - e^{-\sigma_i \delta_i})$ form, after normalization, exactly the distribution of *where along the ray the visible surface lies*: they peak at the first high-density region and decay behind it due to transmittance. Sampling proportionally to $\hat{w}_i$ is therefore near-optimal importance sampling for the integrand $T(t)\sigma(t)\mathbf{c}(t)$, concentrating the expensive fine-network evaluations where they reduce estimator variance most [1].

The coarse-to-fine design also regularizes training. Early in optimization the coarse densities are diffuse, so fine samples spread broadly and gradients explore; as the coarse network converges, the proposal sharpens and capacity concentrates on surfaces. This curriculum emerges without explicit scheduling. Mip-NeRF 360 [3] extends the idea with a *proposal MLP* trained by online distillation: a small network predicts the sampling weights, supervised to match the histogram weights of the main NeRF MLP, enabling efficient sampling in unbounded 360° captures where naive stratification would waste nearly all samples on empty sky.

### 4.4 From Bounded Objects to Unbounded Worlds

Vanilla NeRF assumes bounded scenes with cameras on a surrounding hemisphere. Mip-NeRF 360 [3] tackles unbounded outdoor scenes with three innovations: (i) a *scene contraction* mapping $\mathrm{contract}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}$ for $\lVert \mathbf{x} \rVert \le 1$ and $(2 - 1/\lVert \mathbf{x} \rVert)\,\mathbf{x}/\lVert \mathbf{x} \rVert$ otherwise, compressing infinite space into a radius-2 ball so the MLP's capacity is not squandered on distant background; (ii) the proposal-MLP distillation described above; and (iii) a *distortion loss* penalizing "floaters" by encouraging the weight distribution along each ray to be compact. Together these yield clean reconstructions of 360° environments from casually captured photos [3].

Complementary acceleration work attacks the MLP's cost directly. TensoRF [6] factorizes the 4D radiance volume into low-rank tensor components (vector–matrix decompositions), replacing most MLP evaluation with multilinear interpolation and shrinking training from days to minutes with minimal quality loss. Tri-MipRF [7] takes a different route: it stores features on three mipmapped planes and featurizes conical frustums by trilinear area sampling, achieving Mip-NeRF-quality anti-aliasing at a fraction of the compute. NeRF in the Wild [4] addresses photometric inconsistency instead — varying illumination and transient occluders in internet photo collections — by conditioning appearance on per-image latent codes and modeling occluders with an uncertainty-aware transient head.

## 5 Empirical Results / Theoretical Guarantees

On the 8 synthetic Blender scenes of [1] (100 training views each, $800 \times 800$), NeRF achieves a mean PSNR of **31.01 dB**, substantially exceeding contemporary baselines:

| Method | PSNR ↑ (dB) | SSIM ↑ | LPIPS ↓ |
|---|---|---|---|
| SRN | 22.26 | 0.846 | 0.170 |
| Neural Volumes | 26.05 | 0.893 | 0.160 |
| LLFF | 24.88 | 0.911 | 0.114 |
| **NeRF** [1] | **31.01** | **0.947** | **0.081** |

Qualitatively, NeRF resolves sub-pixel texture (the ship's rigging in *Ship*, the drum stand in *Drums*), crisp specular highlights on the metallic *Materials* ball, and correct view-dependent reflections — effects that mesh-based differentiable renderers of the era could not reproduce [1]. On real forward-facing scenes from the LLFF dataset, NeRF similarly dominates, handling the complex occlusions of the *Fern* and *Orchids* scenes where multiplane-image baselines smear.

Theoretical guarantees are comparatively modest, as for most deep rendering methods: the photometric loss is non-convex, and no global convergence result exists. What *can* be shown is that the quadrature estimator is consistent — as $N \to \infty$ with stratified samples, $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) \to C(\mathbf{r})$ for any fixed field — and that the hierarchical scheme is a valid importance sampler whose variance is bounded above by the uniform sampler's whenever the proposal correlates positively with $|T\sigma\mathbf{c}|$. Empirically, the ablations in [1] isolate each component's contribution: removing hierarchical sampling costs ~1.5 dB PSNR; removing positional encoding costs ~5 dB; removing view dependence costs ~2.5 dB on non-Lambertian scenes.

Scale and efficiency have improved by orders of magnitude since: TensoRF [6] reaches comparable PSNR in under 10 minutes versus NeRF's 1–2 days on a single GPU, and Tri-MipRF [7] demonstrates that anti-aliased quality need not cost the cone-tracing overhead of [2].

## 6 Limitations

Despite its impact, the NeRF formulation of [1] carries significant limitations, many catalogued in [5]:

- **Per-scene optimization.** A NeRF overfits one scene; generalizing across scenes requires conditioning or meta-learning, and training takes hours to days with hundreds of posed images.
- **Static scenes only.** The 5D field has no time dimension; dynamic content is smeared or memorized as view-dependent fog. Extensions exist but inherit the sampling cost.
- **Pose dependence.** Accurate camera intrinsics and extrinsics (typically from COLMAP) are prerequisites; joint pose–field optimization is fragile.
- **Sampling inefficiency.** Even with hierarchical sampling, hundreds of MLP evaluations per pixel make real-time rendering impossible without distillation or caching — the motivation for TensoRF [6] and Tri-MipRF [7].
- **No relighting or editing.** The baked radiance entangles illumination, material, and geometry; decomposing them (inverse rendering) remains underdetermined from RGB alone.
- **Failure modes.** Textureless regions admit multiple consistent solutions; the distortion regularizer of [3] mitigates floaters but does not eliminate them; thin structures below the sampling resolution alias despite IPE [2].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Neural Radiance Fields demonstrated that a continuous volumetric function, parameterized by a modest MLP and optimized by differentiating through classical volume rendering, can synthesize photorealistic novel views from ordinary photographs [1]. Its three enduring contributions — the 5D radiance field with direction-independent density, sinusoidal positional encoding as a cure for spectral bias, and hierarchical sampling as learned importance sampling — have each become a subfield: encoding theory now spans integrated, hash-based, and tensorial variants [2][6][7]; sampling theory extends to proposal distillation for unbounded scenes [3]; and the volumetric core underpins work on in-the-wild capture [4] surveyed broadly in [5]. The trajectory from NeRF's day-long optimizations to minute-scale, anti-aliased, unbounded reconstruction suggests the remaining challenges — dynamics, relighting, and real-time performance — are engineering frontiers rather than conceptual barriers.
