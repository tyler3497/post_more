# Atom Interferometry: Light-Pulse Gratings, Mid-Band Gravitational-Wave Detection, and the MAGIS/AION Long-Baseline Program

## Abstract

Light-pulse atom interferometry has matured from the 1991 Kasevich–Chu demonstration of a Raman-pulse matter-wave inertial sensor into a technology platform that may close the last major unobserved window of the gravitational-wave spectrum: the **mid-band between LISA and LIGO**, roughly 30 mHz to 10 Hz. This thesis presents a unified account of that program. We review the Mach–Zehnder light-pulse interferometer and its inertial phase Δφ = **k**_eff·**g**·T², the large-momentum-transfer (LMT) beam splitters that amplify sensitivity linearly with transferred photon recoil, and the *clock-atom interferometry* technique that immunizes differential measurements against laser phase noise. We then analyze the Graham–Hogan–Kasevich–Rajendran single-baseline detection scheme and the staged MAGIS-100 and AION terrestrial programs now under construction at Fermilab and in the United Kingdom, quantifying strain sensitivity, gravity-gradient noise, and systematic wavefront budgets. The emerging conclusion is that kilometer-scale atom gradiometers can reach characteristic strains near 10⁻²⁰/√Hz at 1 Hz, opening the mid-band to intermediate-mass black-hole mergers, early-universe cosmology, and ultralight dark matter searches.

## 1 Introduction

The history of precision measurement is, to a large extent, the history of interferometry: Michelson and Morley split and recombined light; Ramsey split and recombined spin states; Colella, Overhauser, and Werner split and recombined *neutron* wave packets in the field of gravity [1]. The logical successor — splitting and recombining *atom* wave packets with light — was realized in several laboratories in 1991, most consequentially by **Mark Kasevich and Steven Chu**, who used stimulated Raman transitions to build a Mach–Zehnder matter-wave interferometer with laser-cooled sodium atoms [2]. Their device measured gravitational acceleration with a resolution of 3 parts in 10⁸ and demonstrated wave-packet separations of millimeters, establishing the architecture that still dominates the field [2].

Three decades later, the same architecture is being scaled by three orders of magnitude in baseline length, from tabletop instruments to 100-meter vertical shafts and proposed kilometer-scale facilities. The motivation is no longer inertial navigation or geodesy alone, but a fundamentally new observational window: **gravitational waves in the mid-band**, the frequency interval between the peak sensitivities of LISA (~mHz) and LIGO (~100 Hz) where no detector has ever operated. The enabling insight, due to Graham, Hogan, Kasevich, and Rajendran (2013), is that two atom interferometers driven by *shared* laser pulses form a differential sensor whose noise is dominated by atom shot noise rather than laser frequency noise — the curse of conventional long-baseline optical interferometry [3]. This insight underpins the **MAGIS** (Matter-wave Atomic Gradiometer Interferometric Sensor) program in the United States and the **AION** (Atom Interferometer Observatory and Network) program in the United Kingdom [4, 5].

This thesis develops the full picture: the phase-shift physics (§3), large-momentum-transfer and clock-transition atom optics (§4.1–4.2), the long-baseline program (§4.3), the mid-band gravitational-wave response (§4.4), the systematic error budget (§4.5), quantitative performance projections (§5), and the fundamental limitations (§6).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Kasevich–Chu to quantum inertial sensors

The Kasevich–Chu interferometer (1991) works as follows. A cloud of laser-cooled sodium atoms is launched in an atomic fountain. Three counterpropagating Raman pulses, resonant with a two-photon transition between ground-state hyperfine levels, drive a **Mach–Zehnder** sequence [2]:

1. A **π/2 pulse** (beamsplitter) places each atom in a superposition of momentum states differing by 2ℏ**k** (twice the single-photon recoil, from counterpropagating beams).
2. After free evolution for time *T*, a **π pulse** (mirror) swaps the momentum states.
3. After a further time *T*, a second **π/2 pulse** recombines the arms, and the relative phase Δφ is read out as a population imbalance between the hyperfine states.

For atoms in a uniform gravitational field, the phase accumulated between the arms is

> **Theorem (Inertial phase of the Mach–Zehnder atom interferometer):** In a uniform acceleration field **a**, the leading-order phase shift of a π/2–π–π/2 light-pulse interferometer with interrogation time *T* and effective wavevector **k**_eff is Δφ = **k**_eff · **a** T².

This quadratic scaling with *T* is what makes long free-fall baselines so attractive: doubling the interrogation time quadruples the phase. In the Kasevich–Chu experiment, with T ~ 10⁻¹ s and k_eff ~ 2 × 2π/589 nm⁻¹, the sensitivity to *g* reached the 10⁻⁸ level after 1000 s of integration [2].

### 2.2 The state of the art circa 2010–2025

Subsequent decades delivered four enabling advances: **large momentum transfer** (102ℏk in 2011, 141ℏk on the Sr clock line in 2020) multiplying the phase linearly [6]; **single-photon clock interferometry**, immunizing differential measurements against laser phase noise [3]; **long-baseline testbeds** (Stanford 10 m tower, MIGA 150 m, MAGIS-100, AION) [5, 7]; and **gradiometer configurations** that difference two interferometers along one baseline, suppressing common-mode vibration and laser noise.

### 2.3 Why the mid-band matters

The gravitational-wave spectrum spans over 20 decades, yet only three bands have been observed: pulsar timing arrays (nHz), LIGO/Virgo/KAGRA (~10 Hz–kHz), and the LISA band (~mHz, target 2030s). The **mid-band, ~30 mHz–10 Hz**, contains the inspiral phase of stellar- and intermediate-mass black-hole binaries *months before* they enter the LIGO band, white-dwarf binaries, and possible cosmological backgrounds from inflation or phase transitions [8]. Early warning from the mid-band would let electromagnetic telescopes be pointed *before* the event — a long-standing goal of multimessenger astronomy.

## 3 Methodology and Theoretical Framework

### 3.1 Path-integral phase of light-pulse interferometers

We model the interferometer with the Feynman path-integral formalism. The total phase difference between the two arms is the sum of three contributions:

Δφ = Δφ_propagation + Δφ_laser + Δφ_separation

- **Propagation phase** Δφ_prop = (S_cl[upper] − S_cl[lower])/ℏ, the classical action difference along the two spacetime trajectories. In a gravitational potential, this yields the **k**_eff·**g**T² term.
- **Laser phase** Δφ_laser = Σ_i (±k_eff·x_i + φ_i), imprinted at each pulse interaction, where φ_i is the optical phase at the atom's position x_i. A gravitational wave changes the light travel time between the two interferometers, modulating exactly this term [3].
- **Separation phase** Δφ_sep = **p**·Δ**x**/ℏ, from the final wave-packet separation at the output port; it vanishes when the interferometer closes.

For the differential measurement between two interferometers separated by baseline *L*, common laser phase noise cancels:

> **Lemma (Laser-noise immunity):** In a clock-atom interferometer pair driven by the same pulses, the differential phase Δφ_diff = Δφ₁ − Δφ₂ is independent of the absolute laser phase φ_L to leading order; the gravitational-wave signal enters through the *retarded* arrival times of the pulses, which differ by the strain-modified light travel time.

### 3.2 The single-baseline GW detector

Consider two atom clouds separated by distance *L* along the laser axis. A π-pulse emitted from the bottom at time t₀ reaches the top at t₀ + L/c; a gravitational wave modulates the proper distance, hence the arrival times. The differential clock phase over interrogation time *T* is approximately [3]:

Δφ_diff ≈ ω_a · (n L / c) · h

where ω_a is the atomic transition frequency and *n* the LMT order. The strain response therefore scales with the *optical lever arm* ω_a n L / c — the phase of the atomic clock times the fractional length change, amplified by the number of photon recoils.

A reference Python model of the response and shot-noise limit is:

```python
import numpy as np

def gw_phase_response(h, f_gw, L=1e5, T=1.4, n_lmt=1000, omega_a=2*np.pi*429e12):
    """Differential AI phase from a GW of strain h at frequency f_gw.
    L: baseline [m], T: interrogation time [s], n_lmt: LMT order,
    omega_a: Sr clock transition angular frequency [rad/s]."""
    omega_gw = 2*np.pi*f_gw
    # Transfer function: resonant enhancement near f ~ 1/T, low-pass beyond
    H = np.sinc(omega_gw*T/(2*np.pi))**2
    dphi = omega_a * n_lmt * L / 3e8 * h * H
    return dphi

def strain_noise_psd(f, N_atoms=1e8, L=1e5, T=1.4, n_lmt=1000):
    """Atom-shot-noise-limited strain PSD [1/sqrt(Hz)]."""
    omega_a = 2*np.pi*429e12
    dphi_shot = 1/np.sqrt(N_atoms)          # rad per shot
    h_shot = dphi_shot / (omega_a * n_lmt * L / 3e8)
    return h_shot * np.ones_like(f)

f = np.logspace(-2, 1, 200)
h = strain_noise_psd(f)
print(f"km-scale shot-noise floor ~ {h[100]:.2e} /sqrt(Hz) at 1 Hz")
```

With N_atoms ~ 10⁸, n ~ 10³, and L = 1 km, this crude model gives strain floors approaching **10⁻²⁰/√Hz at 1 Hz** — competitive with the gap between LISA and LIGO [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Light-Pulse Mach–Zehnder Interferometry: Phase-Shift Derivation

The core result Δφ = **k**_eff·**g**T² deserves a careful derivation, because every systematic in the long-baseline program is a *correction* to it. Consider a two-level atom with ground state |g⟩ and excited state |e⟩, interacting with counterpropagating Raman fields of wavevectors **k**₁, **k**₂. The effective wavevector **k**_eff = **k**₁ − **k**₂ transfers momentum 2ℏk in the ideal case. The three pulses occur at t = 0, T, 2T. In the atom's freely falling frame, the laser phase seen at each interaction is φ_i = **k**_eff·**x**(t_i) + φ_L(t_i), where **x**(t) = **x**₀ + **v**₀t + ½**g**t². The phase combination relevant to the Mach–Zehnder geometry is:

Δφ_laser = φ(0) − 2φ(T) + φ(2T) = **k**_eff · **g** T² + (laser phase noise terms)

This is the discrete second time derivative of the phase — a *second-difference* filter that rejects constant and linear drifts (including the atom's initial position and velocity) while retaining the quadratic gravitational term. Higher-order corrections — gravity *gradients* (∝ ΓT³), rotations, and finite-speed-of-light delays — are the systematics the long-baseline program must control [3].

> **Theorem (Second-difference filtering):** The Mach–Zehnder pulse sequence implements the discrete operator (1 − 2E_T + E_{2T}) on the laser phase, where E_T is the time-shift operator. Any phase contribution linear in time is exactly cancelled; the detector responds only to *accelerations* — including those induced by gravitational waves.

### 4.2 Large Momentum Transfer and Clock Atom Interferometry

Two innovations transformed the 2ℏk Raman interferometer into a GW-relevant sensor.

**Large momentum transfer.** The phase scales with the momentum splitting: Δφ ∝ n·k_eff, with *n* photon recoils from sequential pulses — Bragg diffraction or Bloch oscillations in an optical lattice — interleaved within the interferometer arms. The 2011 demonstration of 102ℏk used this combination to create macroscopic arm separations [10]; in 2020, Rudolph *et al.* demonstrated **141ℏk LMT on the single-photon 689 nm intercombination line of strontium** with 3 μK atoms — no velocity pre-selection needed, thanks to the broad velocity acceptance of the clock pulses [6]. Single-photon transitions eliminate the differential AC Stark shifts and spontaneous-emission losses that plague high-order two-photon Raman sequences.

**Clock atom interferometry.** In a Raman interferometer the laser phase noise φ_L enters the phase directly; in a *clock* interferometer driven by single-photon pulses on the optical clock transition, *both* interferometers of the gradiometer pair are addressed by the *same* pulses. The laser phase is then common-mode and subtracts out of Δφ_diff, leaving only the differential *propagation time* — the gravitational-wave observable [3, 9]. The heterodyne laser-link scheme (Hogan & Kasevich 2016) extends this to baselines far beyond the Rayleigh range by phase-locking strong local oscillators at each ensemble to the weak reference beam [9]. In short: Raman optics are mature but laser-noise-limited, whereas clock optics trade the need for ultra-narrow-linewidth lasers for laser-noise-immune differential phase, with excited-state decay limits circumvented by keeping atoms in the ground state between pulses [6].

### 4.3 The MAGIS/AION Long-Baseline Program

The terrestrial program proceeds in staged demonstrators, summarized below [4, 5, 7]:

| Program | Baseline | Location | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| MAGIS-10 | 10 m | Stanford | Operating; prototype for MAGIS-100 |
| MAGIS-100 | 100 m | Fermilab (NuMI shaft) | Under construction; world-leading vertical AI |
| AION-10 | 10 m | Oxford | Commissioned under UK QTFP program |
| AION-100 | 100 m | UK (Boulby/Daresbury candidate sites) | Design phase |
| MIGA | 150 m (horizontal) | LSBB, France | Operating; cavity-enhanced |
| MAGIS-1km / AION-1km | 1 km | TBD (mine shafts) | Conceptual design |
| AEDGE | ~40,000 km | Space (MEO) | ESA Voyage 2050 proposal |

**MAGIS-100**, housed in a 100 m vertical shaft at Fermilab, will operate two ⁸⁷Sr interferometers with a shared clock-laser link. Its physics program is deliberately broad: beyond mid-band GW pathfinding, it will search for *ultralight dark matter* (which would modulate atomic transition frequencies) and test the *equivalence principle* with quantum objects — extending the 2020 atom-interferometric EP test that reached the 10⁻¹² level [4, 7]. The vertical orientation is significant: it aligns the baseline with gravity, maximizing the GW response to the dominant polarization components, and places the apparatus deep underground to suppress seismic and anthropogenic noise.

**AION** mirrors MAGIS in the UK with a staged 10 m → 100 m → km roadmap, explicitly designed to operate as a *network* with MAGIS, MIGA, ZAIGA, and ELGAR [4] — not optional, since a single baseline has poor sky localization while two terrestrial baselines provide near-uniform coverage [8].

### 4.4 Mid-Band Gravitational-Wave Response

The strain sensitivity of a long-baseline atom gradiometer can be written as [8]:

h_n(f) ≈ [1 / (ω_a n L / c)] · [1/√(N_atoms · T_int)] · √[1 + (f/f₀)²] + h_GGN(f)

where the terms are, respectively: the optical lever arm, atom shot noise integrated over observation time T_int, the high-frequency roll-off from the finite interrogation time (f₀ ~ 1/T), and **gravity-gradient noise** (GGN) — the dominant low-frequency limit.

![GW strain sensitivity: atom interferometry bridging LISA and LIGO](/thesis/ths_1789295419000_07289-2.webp)

Key results from recent sensitivity studies [8]:

1. **Terrestrial km-scale detectors** reach characteristic strains ~10⁻²⁰/√Hz near 1 Hz — intermediate-mass black-hole mergers to cosmological distances, and stellar-mass binaries tracked months before LIGO-band merger.
2. **Sky localization** is a distinctive strength: sub-degree localization for any detectable source with chirp mass ≲ 50 M☉, enabling genuine early-warning multimessenger astronomy.
3. **Two-baseline networks** provide close-to-uniform sky coverage (SNR varying by less than a factor of two), removing single-shaft blind spots [8].

The mid-band science case thus rests on three pillars: *early warning* (track a binary's inspiral for months), *new populations* (intermediate-mass black holes, white-dwarf binaries, cosmological backgrounds), and *precision localization* (sub-degree pointing for electromagnetic follow-up).

### 4.5 Systematic Effects: Wavefronts, Gravity Gradients, and Noise Budgets

At the target strain sensitivity, every imperfection becomes signal. The systematic budget is dominated by:

- **Laser wavefront aberrations.** The interferometer phase samples the optical phase at the atoms' positions; aberrations in the clock-laser wavefront couple to the thermal expansion and shot-to-shot jitter of the atomic cloud. Mitigation: large-waist beams, spatially filtered mode-cleaner cavities, and *in situ* wavefront characterization. This is the leading systematic in state-of-the-art gravimeters and will dominate the mid-band detector's high-frequency noise.
- **Gravity gradients.** The Earth's static gravity gradient Γ ~ 3×10⁻⁶ s⁻² produces a phase ∝ n k_eff Γ T³ that differs between the two interferometers if their positions or the cloud trajectories differ. For T ~ 1 s and n ~ 1000, this term is enormous — it must be *measured and subtracted* using co-located classical sensors and precise cloud-position metrology.
- **Gravity-gradient noise (GGN).** Time-varying density fluctuations — seismic waves, atmospheric pressure, human activity — produce Newtonian noise that *cannot* be shielded. At low frequencies (≲ 1 Hz), GGN is the fundamental limit for terrestrial detectors, suppressing sensitivity by up to orders of magnitude relative to the atom-shot-noise floor in the noisiest models [8]. Mitigation strategies include deep-underground siting, seismic sensor arrays for coherent subtraction (as planned for the Einstein Telescope), and multi-gradiometer configurations that exploit the spatial correlation structure of GGN.
- **Vibrations and laser phase noise.** Suppressed by the differential gradiometer architecture to leading order; residual coupling remains through imperfect common-mode rejection. Second-order Zeeman shifts and blackbody-radiation Stark shifts of the clock transition must be controlled at the 10⁻¹⁸ fractional level — the standard already achieved by optical lattice clocks, now across a kilometer-scale apparatus.

> **Theorem (GGN dominance):** For a terrestrial single-baseline atom gradiometer with L ≳ 100 m, Newtonian gravity-gradient noise from seismic and atmospheric sources exceeds atom shot noise below ~1 Hz under both standard noise models (NHNM and NLNM); the low-frequency reach of ground-based mid-band detectors is set by the environment, not by quantum metrology [8].

## 5 Empirical Results and Quantitative Proofs

The long-baseline program rests on demonstrated milestones, not projections alone:

1. **1991 — Kasevich & Chu:** First Raman-pulse atom interferometer; g to 3×10⁻⁸; 2.4 mm wave-packet separation [2].
2. **2008–2011 — LMT development:** 24ℏk (Müller *et al.*), then **102ℏk** via Bragg–Bloch sequences [10].
3. **2013 — Graham *et al.*:** Single-baseline clock-AI GW detection scheme; laser-noise immunity proof [3].
4. **2016 — Hogan & Kasevich:** Heterodyne laser links enabling arbitrarily long baselines [9].
5. **2020 — Rudolph *et al.*:** **141ℏk LMT clock interferometry** on the Sr intercombination line; 50× excited-state-lifetime gradiometer durations [6].
6. **2020 — AION proposal:** Staged UK program (JCAP 05, 011) with full dark-matter and GW science case [4].
7. **2021 — MAGIS-100 design:** Full technical design of the 100 m Fermilab detector (Abe *et al.*, Quantum Sci. Technol. 6, 044003) [7].
8. **2024 — AIMforGW:** Fisher-matrix study demonstrating sub-degree sky localization and quantitative mid-band sensitivity for terrestrial km-scale and space-borne detectors [8].

*Quantitative proof of the lever-arm scaling.* Combining the shot-noise phase resolution δφ = 1/√N with the GW response Δφ = ω_a n L h / c gives the strain resolution per shot δh = c/(ω_a n L √N). Inserting Sr clock frequency ω_a/2π = 429 THz, n = 10³, L = 10⁵ m, N = 10⁸: δh ~ 2×10⁻²¹ per shot; with ~1 shot/s over a year (√T_int ~ 5.6×10³), the integrated floor is ~4×10⁻²⁵ in the absence of GGN — illustrating that *quantum metrology is not the limit*; the environment is [8].

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating what the program cannot do:

1. **GGN is irreducible on the ground.** No shielding exists against Newtonian noise; terrestrial detectors will always be GGN-limited below ~0.1–1 Hz. The ultimate mid-band detector is in space (AEDGE), and the terrestrial program is a pathfinder [4, 8].
2. **LMT at the 10³–10⁴ level is unproven.** The largest demonstrated LMT (141ℏk) is an order of magnitude below the n ~ 10³ assumed in sensitivity projections; spontaneous emission, pulse inefficiency, and contrast loss at high n are active research problems [6].
3. **Atom flux and repetition rate.** Projections assume 10⁸ atoms/shot at ~1 Hz with near-unit detection efficiency — beyond current Sr sources by roughly an order of magnitude. Squeezed or entangled sources could help but are far from deployment.
4. **Single-baseline blindness.** A lone vertical shaft measures essentially one strain projection; sky localization and polarization resolution require networks that do not yet exist [8].
5. **Cost and civil engineering.** Kilometer-scale vertical shafts or horizontal tunnels with ultrahigh vacuum and vibration isolation are billion-class facilities; the program's funding trajectory is uncertain.
6. **Competing technologies.** Torsion-bar antennas (TOBA), superconducting gradiometers, and lunar seismometry target overlapping bands with different systematics; atom interferometry is not guaranteed to win [8].

## 7 Conclusion

Light-pulse atom interferometry has traveled from a tabletop demonstration of matter-wave interference in 1991 to the threshold of gravitational-wave astronomy's last unexplored band. The physics is settled: the Mach–Zehnder phase Δφ = **k**_eff·**g**T², amplified by large momentum transfer and immunized against laser noise by clock-transition differencing, converts a pair of free-falling atom clouds into a strain sensor with a response Δφ ~ ω_a n L h / c [2, 3, 6]. The engineering is underway: MAGIS-100 rises in a Fermilab shaft, AION-10 operates in Oxford, and MIGA listens in a French mountain — each a rung on a ladder toward kilometer-scale terrestrial detectors and, eventually, a space-borne observatory [4, 5, 7]. The science case is compelling: months of early warning before stellar-mass mergers, the first census of intermediate-mass black holes, sub-degree sky localization for multimessenger follow-up, and sensitivity to ultralight dark matter and early-universe backgrounds [8].

The principal adversary is not quantum noise but the Earth itself — gravity-gradient noise sets the low-frequency wall, and only deep sites, coherent subtraction, and ultimately space will breach it [8]. Yet even the pathfinder generation will do physics no other instrument can: the first searches for mid-band gravitational waves, and the first dark-matter limits from kilometer-scale quantum sensors. *The atoms are ready; the shafts are being dug.*
