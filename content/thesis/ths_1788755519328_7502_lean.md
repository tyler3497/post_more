# Mechanized Mathematics in Lean 4: Dependent Type Theory, the Elaboration Framework, Metaprogramming, and the Architecture of Mathlib

## Abstract

Lean 4 is a dependently typed interactive theorem prover and programming language whose kernel implements the calculus of inductive constructions with universe polymorphism, proof irrelevance, and an impredicative sort of propositions [1][6]. Around it sits *Mathlib*, a community-maintained library that as of 2025 contains over 210,000 theorems and 100,000 definitions spanning algebra, analysis, topology, number theory, and category theory [2], and which supported the machine-checked verification of the Liquid Tensor Experiment of Clausen and Scholze [8]. This thesis gives a comprehensive account of the Lean 4 mechanization stack: the dependent type theory and its definitional-equality kernel, the multi-stage elaboration pipeline from surface syntax to kernel-checked terms, the monadic metaprogramming and tactics framework that makes large-scale proof engineering tractable, and the architectural and sociotechnical design of Mathlib. We ground the exposition in the primary literature — the Lean 4 system description [1], the Mathlib architecture paper [2], Carneiro's verified typechecker Lean4Lean [4], the metaprogramming reference [5], and the automation literature [3][7] — and close with a quantitative survey of verification case studies and the principal limitations of the approach.

---

## 1. Introduction

Mechanized mathematics is the discipline of expressing mathematical definitions, statements, and proofs in a formal language whose correctness can be checked by machine. The stakes are exactly those that motivated Peter Scholze to issue his *Liquid Tensor Experiment* challenge to the formalization community: contemporary proofs have grown so long and so technically intricate that even expert referees cannot fully audit them, and a computer-checked formalization offers a qualitatively different, and arguably stronger, certificate of correctness [8]. Lean 4, introduced by Leonardo de Moura and Sebastian Ullrich [1], is the proof assistant that has most decisively answered that challenge. It is simultaneously an interactive theorem prover and an efficient programming language: unlike its predecessor Lean 3, Lean 4 compiles itself, generates C code, and exposes its own frontend — parser, elaborator, and tactic framework — as metaprograms written in Lean itself, so that users can extend the system without touching C++ [1].

This thesis is organized as a single, self-contained technical exposition of how Lean 4 mechanizes mathematics. **Section 2** situates Lean in the landscape of dependent type theory and compares it with Coq, Agda, and Isabelle/HOL. **Section 3** describes our methodological approach: a reconstruction of the system's semantics from the primary sources, anchored by the recent *verified* kernel specification of Carneiro [4]. **Section 4**, the deep dive, treats in turn the type theory (universes, inductive families, definitional equality), the elaboration pipeline (parsing, hygienic macros, metavariables, unification, type-class synthesis), the tactics and metaprogramming framework, the Mathlib library architecture, and the landmark verification case studies. **Section 5** presents quantitative evidence of what has been achieved; **Section 6** is candid about limitations; **Section 7** concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Dependent type theory and the Curry–Howard correspondence

The logical foundation of Lean is the *calculus of inductive constructions* (CIC), a dependent type theory in which propositions are types and proofs are terms — the Curry–Howard correspondence made into a working proof discipline [1][6]. A theorem such as `∀ (a b : Nat), a + b = b + a` is a *type*, and a proof is a *program* of that type; the kernel's type checker is therefore the final arbiter of mathematical truth. Because types may depend on values (`Vec α n`, `Fin n`), specifications and implementations live in the same language, which is why Lean 4 can double as a programming language: every proof term is executable code, and every program can carry its specification in its type.

Lean's type theory refines the classical CIC in several distinctive ways. It features an *impredicative* universe `Prop` of propositions with *proof irrelevance* — any two proofs of the same proposition are definitionally equal — together with a predicative hierarchy `Type 0 : Type 1 : Type 2 : …` and *universe polymorphism*, so that definitions like `List.map` can be stated once for all universe levels [1]. Three classical axioms are available but quarantined: propositional extensionality (`propext`), function extensionality (`funext`), and the axiom of choice (which yields `Classical.choice` and hence excluded middle). Lean is thus a *classical* prover built on a constructive core, and every use of these axioms is tracked by the `#print axioms` command [6].

### 2.2 Lean in the proof-assistant landscape

| System | Logic | Kernel style | Proof style | Library scale |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lean 4 | CIC + quotients + choice | Small kernel, elaborator untrusted | Tactics + term mode | Mathlib: >210k theorems [2] |
| Coq | CIC (+ SProp, cumulativity) | Small kernel | Tactics (Ltac2) | Stdlib + MathComp |
| Agda | Martin-Löf type theory | No separate kernel | Term mode | Agda stdlib |
| Isabelle/HOL | Higher-order logic | LCF-style kernel | Isar + tactics | Archive of Formal Proofs |

Lean's decisive differentiators are *metaprogramming* — the elaborator, parser, and tactic engine are ordinary Lean code that users can extend [1][5] — and the *monolithic, classical* Mathlib, which unlike the distributed Coq ecosystem maintains a single coherent library with one canonical definition per concept [2]. The 2025 ACM SIGPLAN Programming Languages Software Award to the Lean developers cites its "significant impact on mathematics, hardware and software verification, and AI" (per the Lean Wikipedia overview).

## 3. Methodology

This thesis reconstructs the Lean 4 mechanization stack from primary sources rather than from secondary summaries. Our method is *triangulation across three layers of evidence*. First, the system papers: de Moura, Kong, Avigad, van Doorn, and von Raumer's description of Lean [6] and de Moura and Ullrich's Lean 4 paper [1], which specify the type theory, the kernel, and the re-engineered frontend. Second, the community reference *Metaprogramming in Lean 4* [5], which documents the monadic architecture (`CoreM`, `MetaM`, `TermElabM`, `TacticM`, `CommandElabM`) and the elaboration APIs from the implementor's perspective. Third, and most unusually for a system of this complexity, an independently *verified* specification: Mario Carneiro's Lean4Lean project [4], which implements a typechecker for Lean 4 *in* Lean 4 and proves it sound with respect to the kernel's definitional-equality relation — providing a machine-checked oracle for the semantics we describe. Quantitative claims about Mathlib's scale and organization are taken from the Mathlib CPP 2020 paper [2] and the repository itself [mathlib4]. Case-study details are drawn from the Liquid Tensor Experiment report [8] and the formalization literature.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Dependent type theory: universes, inductive families, and definitional equality

Lean's type theory is stratified by *universes*. `Prop` (i.e. `Sort 0`) is impredicative: one may form `∀ (p : Prop), p` inside `Prop` itself. Above it, `Type u` (i.e. `Sort (u+1)`) is predicative, and `Type u : Type (u+1)` for every universe level `u`. Universe polymorphism lets a single declaration be instantiated at many levels:

```lean
universe u v
def List.map {α : Type u} {β : Type v} (f : α → β) : List α → List β
  | []      => []
  | x :: xs => f x :: map f xs
```

Unlike Coq, Lean's kernel has *no cumulativity* (`Type u` is not a subtype of `Type (u+1)`); instead, typical ambiguity is resolved by elaboration-time universe constraint solving. This is a deliberate trade-off: it keeps the kernel's conversion check simpler while pushing level management into the elaborator [1][4].

Inductive types are the workhorse of formalization. A declaration such as `inductive Nat | zero | succ (n : Nat)` instructs the kernel to generate not only constructors but a *recursor* (induction principle) and definitional computation rules (*ι-reduction*). Structures, quotient types, and mutually inductive families are all compiled to this primitive [6].

> **Theorem (Soundness of kernel checking).** If the Lean kernel accepts a term `t` as having type `T` in environment `Γ`, then `Γ ⊢ t : T` is derivable in the declarative specification of Lean's type theory, and `t` is strongly normalizing. The Lean4Lean project mechanizes the typechecker half of this claim: its verified checker is proved sound against the kernel's definitional-equality relation [4].

*Definitional equality* — equality "up to computation" — is the engine of the kernel. Two terms are definitionally equal when their normal forms coincide under *β* (function application), *ι* (recursor reduction), *δ* (unfolding of definitions, modulated by reducibility hints), *ζ* (let-expansion), *η* (function and structure eta), and *proof irrelevance* (all proofs of a proposition are equal). The kernel's `isDefEq` does *not* naively normalize: it is a heuristic, backtracking unification procedure that unfolds definitions lazily, preferring to unfold the definition of smaller *definitional height*, and that falls back to η-expansion and structural η when syntactic comparison stalls [4]. Elaborator-level unification queries are written `t =?= u` and may *assign* metavariables to achieve definitional equality [5].

### 4.2 The elaboration pipeline: from surface syntax to kernel-checked terms

Trust in Lean is concentrated in a deliberately small *kernel* (~a few thousand lines): everything else — parsing, macro expansion, elaboration, tactics — is *untrusted* frontend code whose output the kernel re-checks [1]. The pipeline has five stages:

1. **Lexing and parsing.** Lean 4's parser is a hand-written, extensible packrat-style parser built from parser *combinators* in Lean itself. Syntax categories (`term`, `command`, `tactic`, …) are user-extensible, and new notation is declared alongside its parser.
2. **Hygienic macro expansion.** Macros transform `Syntax` trees into other `Syntax` trees before elaboration, with automatic hygiene: macro-introduced identifiers cannot accidentally capture user binders [1]. A macro is declared as:
   ```lean
   macro x:ident ":" t:term " ↦ " y:term : term =>
     `(fun $x : $t => $y)
   ```
   Quotation `` `(…) `` and antiquotation `$x` make syntax construction ergonomic [5].
3. **Elaboration.** The heart of the frontend. The term elaborator (`TermElabM`) walks the syntax tree, generating a kernel `Expr` while solving typing constraints. Unknown subterms become *metavariables* `?m : Γ ⊢ T`; expected types propagate bidirectionally; ambiguous cases are *postponed* and revisited. Overloading is resolved by *type-class synthesis*: the `HAdd.hAdd` behind `a + b` is found by a tabled, Prolog-style resolution procedure with loop detection [7].
4. **Kernel checking.** The elaborated `Expr` is submitted to the kernel, which re-verifies every typing and definitional-equality judgment with the algorithm of §4.1. This is the trust boundary: an elaborator bug can produce confusing errors but never an accepted false proof [1][4].
5. **Compilation.** Accepted definitions are compiled through Lean's intermediate representations to C, enabling the famous self-hosting loop — Lean 4 compiles itself [1].

A custom command illustrates the monadic layering (`CommandElabM` lifting into `TermElabM`):

```lean
elab "#assertType " termStx:term " : " typeStx:term : command => do
  let tp ← liftTermElabM <| elabType typeStx
  discard <| liftTermElabM <| elabTermEnsuringType termStx tp
  logInfo "success"
```

### 4.3 The tactics framework and metaprogramming

*Tactics* are metaprograms that transform proof states. In Lean 4 a tactic is a `TacticM Unit` computation operating on a list of *goals*, each goal a metavariable whose type is the remaining proposition [5]. Users write tactic proofs:

```lean
theorem add_comm' (a b : Nat) : a + b = b + a := by
  induction a with
  | zero      => simp
  | succ a ih => simp [Nat.succ_add, Nat.add_succ, ih]
```

while the library provides industrial-strength automation: `simp` (a conditional rewriting engine driven by discrimination trees and `simp` normal-form lemmas), `omega` (Presburger arithmetic), `aesop` (best-first proof search), `ring`/`linarith`, and `exact?` (lemma search over the Mathlib index) [2][5].

Metaprogramming rests on three pillars. First, the `Expr` datatype reifies kernel terms (`const`, `app`, `lam`, `forallE`, `mvar`, …) for inspection and construction. Second, the monad stack `CoreM → MetaM → TermElabM → TacticM → CommandElabM` threads the environment, metavariable context, and local context through elaboration code. Third, *quoted elaboration* (`elabTerm`, `Term.elabTermEnsuringType`) lets metaprograms invoke the elaborator recursively — this is how `simp` elaborates its rewrite rules and how domain-specific tactic languages like Lean-auto's monomorphization preprocessor are built [3][5]. Because the entire frontend is ordinary Lean code, a tactic is just a library: `import Mathlib.Tactic` brings hundreds of them.

### 4.4 Mathlib: the architecture of a library at scale

Mathlib is the largest coherent formal mathematics library ever assembled: over 210,000 theorems and 100,000 definitions as of May 2025 [2]. Its architecture solves a problem no smaller library faces — *how ten thousand definitions stay mutually consistent across hundreds of contributors*:

| Subsystem | Contents | Scale / notes |
|---|---|---|
| `Mathlib.Algebra` | Groups, rings, fields, modules, order theory | Deepest type-class hierarchy in the library |
| `Mathlib.Analysis` | Topology, metric spaces, measure theory, calculus | Normed-space API shared with LTE [8] |
| `Mathlib.NumberTheory` | Algebraic and analytic number theory | Class groups, L-functions |
| `Mathlib.Geometry` / `Topology` | Manifolds, sheaves, simplicial sets | Condensed-mathematics prerequisites |
| `Mathlib.CategoryTheory` | Categories, functors, limits, abelian categories | Derived categories formalized [hal] |
| `Mathlib.Tactic` | `simp` lemma sets, `norm_num`, linters | Automation co-evolves with the library |
| `Mathlib.Logic` / `SetTheory` | Ordinals, cardinals, model theory fragments | Foundation-adjacent material |

Three design decisions carry the weight. **(i) One canonical definition per concept.** There is exactly one `Group`, one `TopologicalSpace`, one real-number construction in Mathlib; competing definitions are refactored away by maintainers, so lemmas compose without translation layers [2]. **(ii) Type classes as mathematical structure.** The algebraic hierarchy (`Monoid → Group → Ring → Field`, `DistribLattice`, `UniformSpace`, …) is encoded in Lean's type-class system with *bundled* and *semi-bundled* encodings tuned so that instance synthesis — tabled resolution [7] — terminates quickly on real goals. **(iii) Automation as infrastructure.** `simp` normal forms are a library-wide contract: lemmas are stated in `simp`-normal form, and a battery of *linters* runs in CI to reject style violations, missing docstrings, and dangerous instances [2].

The build system is Lake (Lean's Make-like tool), and the import graph is a DAG of thousands of modules with `Mathlib.lean` at the root. Continuous integration compiles the entire library on every pull request — a full build is measured in tens of minutes on CI runners — which makes Mathlib one of the most aggressively tested bodies of formal mathematics in existence [2].

### 4.5 Verification case studies

**The Liquid Tensor Experiment.** In December 2020, Peter Scholze challenged the formalization community to machine-check Theorem 9.1 of his *Lectures on Analytic Geometry* — a result in condensed mathematics whose proof he himself described as forbiddingly technical [8]. A team led by Johan Commelin met the challenge in Lean 3/Mathlib, formalizing the key Theorem 9.4 (May 2021) and the full implication in 2022 [8]. Scholze's published report in *Experimental Mathematics* describes the experience as transformative: the formalization uncovered a subtle gap in a supporting lemma and forced a reorganization of the argument that improved the mathematics itself [8]. The experiment demonstrated that *blueprint-driven* formalization — a LaTeX blueprint with dependency graphs tracking each lemma's formalization status — scales to research-level mathematics.

**Perfectoid spaces.** Buzzard, Commelin, and Massot formalized Scholze's definition of perfectoid spaces in Lean [buzzard2020], a project notable for stress-testing Mathlib's topology and algebra hierarchies: roughly 3,000 lines of the development belonged properly in the library (`for_mathlib`) and had to be upstreamed, establishing the now-standard workflow in which research formalizations *grow* Mathlib [2].

**Derived categories and homological algebra.** Joël Riou's formalization of the derived category of an abelian category — as a localization inverting quasi-isomorphisms — together with the Grothendieck spectral sequence, showed that even highly abstract, definitionally delicate category theory can be carried out in dependent type theory [hal]. These projects, all building on Mathlib, confirm a central thesis of this work: *the elaborator and the library, not the kernel, are the binding constraints on what mathematics can be mechanized* — and both have scaled further than skeptics predicted.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We summarize the quantitative state of Lean 4 mechanization:

| Milestone | Year | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Lean theorem prover (system description) | 2015 | CADE 25 paper [6] |
| Mathlib architecture paper | 2020 | >100k lines then; CPP 2020 [2] |
| Lean 4 system paper; self-hosting compiler | 2021 | CADE 28 paper [1] |
| Liquid Tensor Experiment completed | 2022 | *Experimental Mathematics* report [8] |
| Lean4Lean: verified typechecker | 2024 | arXiv:2403.14064 [4] |
| Lean-auto: ITP→ATP bridge evaluated on Mathlib4 | 2025 | arXiv:2505.14929 [3] |
| Mathlib4 exceeds 210k theorems, 100k definitions | 2025 | Repository statistics [2] |
| ACM SIGPLAN Software Award to Lean developers | 2025 | Cited for impact on mathematics, verification, AI |

Two results deserve emphasis as *proofs about the system itself*. First, Lean4Lean's soundness theorem: a typechecker written in Lean 4 is proved correct against the kernel's definitional-equality specification, shrinking the effective trusted base to the statement of the specification [4]. Second, the *de Bruijn criterion* holds in practice: every Mathlib proof, however heavily automated its construction (`simp`, `aesop`, Lean-auto's monomorphization and SMT translation [3]), is re-checked by the small kernel, so automation bugs manifest as failures, never as false theorems.

## 6. Limitations

**Classical axioms and constructivity.** Mathlib freely uses `propext`, `funext`, and choice; results depending on them cannot be executed or extracted to code, and the library is therefore unsuitable for programs-as-proofs extraction without auditing `#print axioms` output [6].

**No cumulativity.** Lean's kernel lacks universe cumulativity, so universe polymorphism must be managed explicitly; elaboration occasionally produces inscrutable universe-constraint errors, and universe inference remains one of the least predictable parts of the frontend [1][4].

**Elaborator performance and predictability.** Type-class synthesis and higher-order unification are heuristic and can be slow or fail on goals a human finds trivial; Mathlib maintains performance regression tests precisely because a single bad instance can multiply build times [2][7].

**Trust and `sorry`.** The kernel is small but not formally verified end-to-end against a paper specification (Lean4Lean verifies a *model* of the checker [4]); `sorry` introduces unchecked axioms, and large developments must be audited for their absence.

**Automation gaps.** Lean-auto's translation to first-order ATPs/SMT solvers handles only fragments of dependent type theory — dependent arguments are dynamically context-sensitive, and full monomorphization remains incomplete [3]. Proof search still lags human mathematicians on deep, non-computational arguments.

**Sociotechnical fragility.** Mathlib's monolithic model concentrates enormous review load on a small maintainer team; breaking refactors (e.g. the Lean 3 → Lean 4 port of ~500k lines) are heroic efforts that the community cannot repeat casually [2].

---

## 7. Conclusion

Lean 4 demonstrates that dependent type theory, a small trustworthy kernel, an extensible elaboration pipeline, and industrial-strength metaprogramming can jointly sustain the mechanization of mathematics at research scale. The kernel's definitional equality — β, ι, δ, ζ, η plus proof irrelevance, implemented as a lazy heuristic conversion check [4] — is the fixed point of trust; the elaborator's metavariables, unification, and tabled type-class resolution [5][7] are the machinery that makes the type theory usable; and Mathlib's one-definition-per-concept discipline, `simp`-normal-form contracts, and CI-gated monorepo [2] are the social technology that keeps 210,000 theorems coherent. The Liquid Tensor Experiment [8] and its successors show the payoff: proofs too complex for traditional refereeing, checked to the last definitional unfolding. The remaining frontiers — verified kernels, complete automation bridges [3], and the maintenance economics of a library that grows without bound — are now engineering problems with visible paths forward, not open questions of principle.
