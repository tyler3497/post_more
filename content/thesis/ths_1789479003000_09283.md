# Byzantine Fault Tolerance from PBFT to HotStuff: Quorum Intersection, View Changes, and Linear Communication Complexity

## Abstract

Byzantine fault-tolerant state machine replication has been transformed over two decades from the all-to-all communication of Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT) to the linear, responsive protocols exemplified by HotStuff. This article traces that trajectory through three foundational pillars: *quorum intersection*, which grounds safety in the guarantee that any two quorums of (2f + 1) replicas out of (3f + 1) intersect in at least (f + 1) replicas and therefore share an honest member; *view-change protocols*, which preserve liveness across leader failures by transferring locks inside quorum certificates; and *communication complexity*, where leader-centric vote aggregation with threshold signatures collapses quadratic message costs to linear [1][2]. We reconstruct PBFT's pre-prepare, prepare, and commit phases, derive the intersection argument formally, analyze HotStuff's three-phase chained commit rule with rotating leaders, and situate Tendermint and Streamlet within the same design space [4][5]. We close with empirical evidence from deployments exceeding one hundred replicas and identify open problems in asynchronous BFT and adaptive adversaries [6].

##1 Intro

State machine replication — the discipline of keeping a set of deterministic replicas synchronized so that the service survives faulty members — is the substrate of every fault-tolerant distributed system, from replicated databases to modern blockchains [6]. When faults are merely crashes, solutions such as Paxos and Raft suffice. The *Byzantine* setting is harsher: faulty replicas may behave arbitrarily, collude, lie, and adapt their strategy to the protocol's messages. In 1999, Castro and Liskov's **Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT)** demonstrated that Byzantine agreement could be achieved with practical performance in partially synchronous networks, achieving over sixteen thousand null operations per second on contemporary hardware [5][6]. PBFT's normal-case protocol used two all-to-all voting rounds, and its view-change protocol preserved safety when the primary failed.

Yet PBFT carried a structural cost. Each voting round required every replica to broadcast to every other replica: quadratic communication in the number of replicas. Worse, the view-change protocol — the mechanism that replaces a faulty leader — was complex, quadratic, and rarely exercised, a source of notorious implementation bugs [3]. For a decade, PBFT remained the reference design, spawning a family of variants (Q/U, Zyzzyva, Aardvark, BFT-SMaRt) that optimized the happy path while inheriting the quadratic view change.

The blockchain era changed the requirements. Blockchains demand *frequent leader rotation* — proposers must change every block — which turns the view change from a rare recovery path into the steady state. A quadratic leader-replacement protocol is then a quadratic tax on every block. **HotStuff**, presented by Yin, Malkhi, Reiter, Gueta, and Abraham, answered with a protocol that is simultaneously *responsive* (a correct leader drives consensus at the speed of the actual network delay, not the worst-case bound) and *linear* (each view costs communication linear in the number of replicas, including leader replacement), enabled by threshold signatures and a three-phase chained structure [1]. Its unifying framework further showed that PBFT, Tendermint, and Casper are all instances of one generic voting-and-locking discipline [1].

This article reconstructs the intellectual path from PBFT to HotStuff through three pillars. **First, quorum intersection**: the combinatorial fact that any two (2f + 1)-quorums of (3f + 1) replicas intersect in at least (f + 1) replicas, hence in at least one honest replica — the engine of every safety proof in this space [2]. **Second, view changes**: how locks and quorum certificates migrate across leaders without breaking safety, and why HotStuff's formulation is dramatically simpler [3]. **Third, communication complexity**: how vote aggregation collapses quadratic broadcasts into linear leader-centric rounds, and what pipelining buys on top [1][4]. Along the way we compare Tendermint's two-phase locking discipline and Streamlet's simplified voting rule, and we ground the theory in measured deployments [4][5].

##2 Background

### The system model

We consider a fixed set of *n* replicas, at most *f* of which may be Byzantine, with the classical resilience bound **n ≥ 3f + 1** [2][6]. Replicas communicate over point-to-point authenticated channels in a *partially synchronous* network: there exists an unknown global stabilization time (GST) after which message delays are bounded by a known Δ, but before GST the network may behave arbitrarily [6]. This model is the natural home of BFT consensus — the celebrated FLP impossibility result rules out deterministic agreement in fully asynchronous networks with even one crash fault, so some synchrony assumption is unavoidable [6].

Replicas are equipped with standard cryptography: collision-resistant hashes, digital signatures, and message authentication codes. We additionally assume **threshold signatures**: a (k, n)-threshold scheme lets any *k* replicas jointly produce a signature that verifies against a single public key, while fewer than *k* shares reveal nothing [1]. Setting k = (2f + 1) lets a leader compress an entire quorum of votes into one constant-size *quorum certificate (QC)* — the primitive that makes linear communication possible. Threshold setups typically require a trusted dealer or distributed key generation, a limitation we revisit in §6.

### Quorums and the intersection engine

A **quorum** is any set of at least (2f + 1) replicas. Two properties carry the entire safety argument of every protocol in this article [2]:

- **Quorum intersection.** Any two quorums intersect in at least (f + 1) replicas, and therefore in at least one honest replica.
- **Quorum availability.** At most *f* replicas are faulty, so among (3f + 1) replicas there always exists a quorum of (2f + 1) honest replicas, guaranteeing liveness.

> **Lemma 1 (Quorum Intersection).** *Let n = 3f + 1 and let Q₁, Q₂ ⊆ {1, …, n} with |Q₁| = |Q₂| = 2f + 1. Then |Q₁ ∩ Q₂| ≥ f + 1.*

> **Proof.** By inclusion–exclusion, |Q₁ ∩ Q₂| = |Q₁| + |Q₂| − |Q₁ ∪ Q₂| ≥ (2f + 1) + (2f + 1) − (3f + 1) = f + 1. Since at most *f* replicas are Byzantine, any set of (f + 1) replicas contains at least one honest replica. ∎

This five-line argument is the load-bearing wall of BFT safety. Every "no two conflicting values can both be committed" theorem in this article is, at bottom, an application of Lemma 1: a commit requires a quorum, two quorums intersect in an honest replica, and an honest replica never votes for two conflicting values in the same logical step. Flexible BFT generalizes the picture to *flexible quorums* with heterogeneous failure assumptions, recovering the classical (2f + 1)-of-(3f + 1) scheme as a special case [2].

### Safety, liveness, and the failure taxonomy

*Safety* means no two honest replicas disagree on the committed sequence of operations; *liveness* means clients' requests are eventually executed. The protocols below guarantee safety in all executions (even before GST) and liveness after GST, provided the leader is honest and the network has stabilized [1][6].

| Failure class | Behavior | Tolerated bound | Canonical protocol |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crash-stop | Halts silently | f < n | Paxos, Raft |
| Omission | Drops/delays messages | f < n/2 | Viewstamped Replication |
| Byzantine | Arbitrary, colluding | f < n/3 | PBFT, HotStuff |
| Byzantine + asynchrony | Arbitrary + unbounded delay | impossible (FLP) | — |

The f < n/3 bound is tight for partially synchronous Byzantine agreement: no protocol tolerates a third or more Byzantine replicas while guaranteeing both safety and liveness [2][6].

##3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds by *comparative protocol reconstruction*: we extract the voting rule, locking rule, and view-change rule of each protocol into a common vocabulary, then compare them on safety invariants, message complexity, and measured behavior [3][5]. Three methodological choices keep the comparison honest.

**Certificate lifecycle.** Every protocol here revolves around *quorum certificates* — cryptographic evidence that a quorum voted for a value. We track three roles a QC can play: a *prepareQC* records that a quorum accepted a proposal; a *lockedQC* is the newest QC a replica has seen and constrains what it may vote for next; a *highQC* is the freshest QC a new leader collects from a quorum of NEW-VIEW messages and must extend [1]. View-change safety is, in every case, the claim that a committed value's QC survives inside every future leader's highQC.

```python
# Leader-side vote aggregation into a quorum certificate (HotStuff-style)
def collect_qc(leader, phase, view, proposal, n, f):
    shares = {}                      # replica -> partial signature
    leader.broadcast(("VOTE-REQ", phase, view, proposal))
    while len(shares) < n - f:       # one vote message per replica
        r, sig = leader.recv_vote(timeout=delta)
        if verify_partial(r, phase, view, proposal, sig):
            shares[r] = sig
    return threshold_combine(shares) # constant-size QC: O(n) total traffic
```

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 PBFT's Normal-Case Protocol and the Intersection Argument

PBFT processes client requests in numbered *views*, each with a designated primary *p = v mod n*. The normal case has three phases [3][5]:

1. **Pre-prepare.** The primary assigns sequence number *s* to the client request *m* and broadcasts ⟨PRE-PREPARE, v, s, d(m)⟩ with the request digest.
2. **Prepare.** Each backup validates the pre-prepare (view matches, sequence number in the water-mark window, digest correct, no conflicting pre-prepare for (v, s)) and broadcasts ⟨PREPARE, v, s, d⟩ to all replicas. A replica considers the request *prepared* once it holds the pre-prepare plus (2f + 1) matching prepares — a *prepare certificate*.
3. **Commit.** Each replica broadcasts ⟨COMMIT, v, s, d⟩. Upon collecting (2f + 1) matching commits, the replica *commits*: it executes the operation and replies to the client. The client accepts the result after (f + 1) identical replies, guaranteeing at least one honest confirmation.

> **Theorem 1 (PBFT Agreement).** *No two different requests commit with the same sequence number in the same view.*

> **Proof.** Suppose requests *m ≠ m′* both commit at sequence number *s* in view *v*. Each commit required (2f + 1) matching COMMIT messages, i.e., quorums Q and Q′. By Lemma 1, Q ∩ Q′ contains an honest replica *h*. But *h* sends COMMIT for *m* only after observing a prepare certificate for *m*, and it never assembles prepare certificates for two different digests at (v, s) because it broadcasts at most one PREPARE per (v, s). Contradiction. ∎

### 4.2 View Changes: Locks, Progress Certificates, and Safety Transfer

The view-change protocol is where PBFT pays its complexity debt. When backups suspect the primary (via timeouts), replica *i* broadcasts ⟨VIEW-CHANGE, v + 1, C, P, Q⟩ where *C* is its latest stable checkpoint, *P* its prepared certificates, and *Q* its pre-prepared log [3][5]. The new primary for view *v + 1* waits for (2f + 1) VIEW-CHANGE messages, then broadcasts ⟨NEW-VIEW, v + 1, V, O⟩, where *V* bundles the (2f + 1) messages and *O* is a set of re-proposed pre-prepares derived from the highest prepared sequence numbers.

> **Lemma 2 (Safety Transfer).** *If a request m commits at sequence number s in view v, then every valid NEW-VIEW certificate for any view v′ > v re-proposes m at s.*

> **Proof.** The commit of *m* implies a commit-quorum Q of (2f + 1) replicas sent COMMIT for *m*. Any valid NEW-VIEW for view *v′* bundles a quorum Q′ of (2f + 1) VIEW-CHANGE messages. By Lemma 1, some honest replica *h ∈ Q ∩ Q′* both committed *m* and contributed to the NEW-VIEW bundle; *h*'s prepare certificate for *m* is therefore present in the bundle, and the primary's deterministic rule selects the highest prepared sequence number, which includes (v, s, m). No conflicting request can displace it, since displacing it would require a prepare certificate for a different digest at (v, s), contradicting Theorem 1. ∎

### 4.3 HotStuff: Three Phases, Threshold Signatures, and Linear Views

HotStuff reframes BFT consensus as a sequence of *views with rotating leaders* — every view has a fresh leader, so "view change" is the normal path rather than a recovery path — and makes every phase *leader-centric*: replicas vote only to the leader, and the leader aggregates votes into a threshold-signature QC [1].

1. **Prepare.** The leader broadcasts the proposal with its highQC. Replicas vote if the proposal extends their lockedQC (the *safety rule*); the leader forms a prepareQC from (n − f) votes.
2. **Pre-commit.** The leader broadcasts the prepareQC. Replicas store it as their new prepareQC and vote; the leader forms a pre-commitQC.
3. **Commit.** The leader broadcasts the pre-commitQC. Replicas set lockedQC to it and vote; the leader forms a commitQC.
4. **Decide.** The leader broadcasts the commitQC; replicas execute the block and move to the next view.

The phases are then *chained*: the prepare of view *v + 1* doubles as the pre-commit of view *v* and the commit of view *v − 1*, since each proposal carries its parent's QC. A block commits under the **three-chain rule** — three consecutive certified blocks — so the steady state finalizes one block per view at network speed [1][4]. Every phase is leader-centric (one broadcast, (n − f) votes aggregated by threshold signatures into a constant-size QC), giving O(n) normal-case and O(n) view-change communication — the linearity that makes per-view leader rotation affordable [1].

> **Theorem 2 (HotStuff Safety).** *If an honest replica commits block B in view v via the three-chain rule, no conflicting block B′ can ever be committed.*

> **Proof sketch.** Committing *B* requires a commitQC, i.e., (n − f) commit votes in some view *v₂*, which required a pre-commitQC from (n − f) votes in *v₁*, which required a prepareQC from (n − f) votes in *v₀*, with *v₀ < v₁ < v₂* consecutive and each QC extending the last. Replicas that voted in the commit phase set lockedQC to the pre-commitQC for *B*. Any future leader's highQC comes from (n − f) NEW-VIEW messages; by Lemma 1, that quorum intersects the (n − f) commit voters in at least (f + 1) replicas, hence an honest replica whose lockedQC extends *B*. The safety rule (vote only for proposals extending your lock or carrying a higher-view QC) then forces every subsequent proposal to extend *B*'s chain, so no conflicting *B′* can gather even one honest vote, let alone a quorum. ∎

### 4.4 Tendermint, Streamlet, and the Design Space

HotStuff's framework is deliberately general: PBFT, Tendermint, and Casper all emerge as parameterizations of the same voting-and-locking discipline [1]. Two relatives deserve close attention.

**Tendermint** runs *prevote* and *precommit* rounds with round-robin proposers: validators lock after (2f + 1) prevotes and precommit only their locked block, but vote gossip remains all-to-all [5]. **Streamlet** distills the design to a single echo rule — vote for the first proposal extending the longest certified chain — with finalization on three consecutive certified blocks, making the three-chain safety argument nearly self-evident [4]. Fast-HotStuff later showed two phases suffice for responsiveness if failover messages carry extra proofs against forking attacks [7].

| Protocol | Phases per decision | Normal-case msgs | View-change msgs | Responsive | Leader rotation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT | 2 (prepare, commit) | O(n²) | O(n²) | yes | on failure only |
| Tendermint | 2 (prevote, precommit) | O(n²) | O(n²) | yes | every block |
| HotStuff | 3 (chained) | O(n) | O(n) | yes | every view |
| Streamlet | 1 (echo rule) | O(n²) | O(n) | yes | every epoch |
| BFT-SMaRt | 2 | O(n²) | O(n²) | yes | on failure only |

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### Proof consolidation

The safety proofs of §4 share one skeleton, worth stating explicitly:

> **Lemma 3 (Prepare uniqueness).** *In any view v, at most one block can obtain a prepareQC.*

> **Proof.** A prepareQC requires (n − f) prepare votes. Two prepareQCs for distinct blocks in the same view would require quorums Q, Q′ of (n − f) = (2f + 1) voters each; by Lemma 1 they share an honest replica, which votes at most once per view (the voted flag in our TLA⁺ sketch). ∎

> **Theorem 3 (Cross-view safety, generic).** *If block B is committed in view v, every valid proposal in every later view extends B.*

> **Proof.** Commitment implies a quorum Q_c of replicas locked on (a QC extending) *B*. A later view's leader must present a highQC drawn from a quorum Q_n of NEW-VIEW messages. Lemma 1 gives an honest replica in Q_c ∩ Q_n whose lock extends *B*, so the leader's highQC extends *B* or a descendant; the voting rule then permits honest replicas to vote only for proposals extending their locks. Induction over views completes the argument. ∎

Liveness follows from the pacemaker and quorum availability: after GST, views synchronize, an honest leader eventually leads a full view, and (n − f) honest votes always exist to form QCs [1]. Forensic extensions of these protocols further show that when safety *is* violated (necessarily implying more than *f* Byzantine faults), the conflicting QCs themselves identify a culpable set of at least (f + 1) replicas — accountability comes for free from the same intersection structure [3].

### Measured behavior

Theory is vindicated by deployment. The HotStuff evaluation ran over one hundred replicas and found throughput and latency comparable to BFT-SMaRt — the industrial PBFT descendant — while leader failover exhibited a *linear* communication footprint versus BFT-SMaRt's quadratic one [1]. Both the three-phase and two-phase HotStuff variants matched BFT-SMaRt's latency across batch sizes while achieving noticeably higher maximum throughput, with batching amortizing per-decision signature costs [1].

| Deployment | Replicas | Latency vs BFT-SMaRt | Throughput vs BFT-SMaRt | Failover cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| HotStuff (3-phase) | 4–100+ | comparable | higher max | O(n) |
| HotStuff (2-phase) | 4 | comparable | higher max | O(n) |
| Pipelined HotStuff | 100+ | comparable | substantially higher | O(n) |
| PBFT / BFT-SMaRt | 4–100 | baseline | baseline | O(n²) |

##6 Limitations

**Synchrony and setup assumptions.** All protocols here assume partial synchrony; before GST, liveness is not guaranteed, and adversarial network scheduling can delay progress arbitrarily [6]. Threshold signatures — the linchpin of linearity — require either a trusted dealer or a distributed key generation protocol, and post-quantum threshold schemes remain an active research area [1][5].

**Adaptive adversaries and forking.** A rotating Byzantine leader can equivocate strategically, creating forks that waste views. The *hidden lock* problem — where a QC known to few replicas blocks progress indefinitely — forced Fast-HotStuff to add proof-carrying failover messages, trading a small unhappy-path overhead for two-phase latency [7]. Formal analyses confirm that the unhappy path, not the happy path, is where modern BFT protocols win or lose [3][7].

**Censorship and fairness.** Rotating leaders give each replica proposal opportunities, but a Byzantine leader can still censor specific transactions for its view. Fair ordering (e.g., Aequitas-style protocols) and encrypted mempools are layered defenses outside the consensus core surveyed here [5].

**Reconfiguration.** Adding or removing replicas changes *n* and hence every quorum threshold. Dynamic membership with safe reconfiguration — transferring locks across *epochs* with different validator sets — remains delicate, and long-range attacks in proof-of-stake settings compound the problem [5][6].

##7 Conclusion

The arc from PBFT to HotStuff is the arc from quadratic to linear, and its three load-bearing ideas have proven remarkably durable. **Quorum intersection** — the (f + 1)-overlap of any two (2f + 1)-quorums — is the combinatorial invariant from which every safety theorem here descends [2]. **View changes** evolved from PBFT's intricate certificate-bundling recovery path into HotStuff's uniform per-view leader handoff, with locks migrating inside constant-size quorum certificates [1][3]. **Linear communication**, unlocked by threshold signatures and leader-centric aggregation, turned leader rotation from a failure mode into the steady state [1][4].

Open problems cluster around the assumptions we still lean on: fully asynchronous BFT with practical performance, threshold cryptography without trusted setup, adaptive-adversary resilience in the unhappy path, and safe dynamic reconfiguration [5][7]. But the framework itself — vote, lock, certify, rotate — now serves as the common language in which new protocols are expressed, exactly as HotStuff's unifying vision intended [1].
