# Causal Inference: Structural Causal Models, the do-Calculus, and Identification Strategies for Observational Data

## Abstract

Causal inference formalizes the leap from association to causation: given observational data generated by an unknown mechanism, when can we predict the effect of an intervention? This thesis develops the two dominant frameworks—Rubin's potential outcomes and Pearl's structural causal models—and their synthesis. We present the do-calculus, a complete set of graphical rules for determining identifiability of interventional queries from observational distributions; classical identification strategies (back-door adjustment, front-door criterion, instrumental variables, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity); and modern doubly robust and debiased machine-learning estimators with Neyman orthogonality. Empirical results include the LaLonde labor-training reanalysis and the Oregon health-insurance experiment. Limitations cover unmeasured confounding, the faithfulness assumption in causal discovery, and transportability. We conclude with open problems in causal representation learning.

## 1 Introduction

"Correlation does not imply causation" is statistics' most quoted warning—and its least operational. Scientists, policymakers, and physicians need more than warnings: they need to know whether a drug *causes* recovery, whether a job-training program *causes* higher earnings, whether an advertisement *causes* purchases. Randomized controlled trials answer such questions by physical intervention, but experiments are often infeasible, unethical, or too narrow. **Causal inference** is the mathematical discipline that determines when—and how—causal conclusions can be drawn from non-experimental data [1][2].

Two frameworks dominate. The **potential-outcomes** (Rubin) framework posits, for each unit $i$, a pair of potential outcomes $(Y_i(0), Y_i(1))$ under control and treatment; the causal effect is $Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)$, of which only one is ever observed—the *fundamental problem of causal inference* [3]. The **structural causal model** (Pearl) framework encodes mechanisms as functional equations $X_j = f_j(\mathrm{pa}_j, U_j)$ over a directed acyclic graph (DAG), with interventions formalized by the $do$-operator that severs incoming arrows [1].

> Theorem: (Shpitser–Pearl, 2006) The three rules of the do-calculus are *complete* for identification: a causal effect $P(y \mid do(x))$ is identifiable from the observational distribution if and only if it can be reduced to a do-free expression by repeated application of the rules [4].

This thesis is organized as follows: §2 introduces SCMs and potential outcomes; §3 develops the do-calculus and identification theory; §4 dives into classical designs and modern ML estimators; §5 presents landmark empirical studies; §6 discusses limitations; §7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Structural causal models

A **structural causal model** $M = (U, V, F, P(U))$ consists of exogenous variables $U$ with distribution $P(U)$, endogenous variables $V = \{X_1, \dots, X_n\}$, and structural equations $X_j = f_j(\mathrm{pa}_j, U_j)$ where $\mathrm{pa}_j \subset V$ are the parents of $X_j$ in a DAG $G$. The model induces an observational distribution $P(V)$ by pushing $P(U)$ through the equations.

An **intervention** $do(X = x)$ replaces the equation for $X$ with the constant $x$, yielding the mutilated model $M_x$ and the interventional distribution $P(V \mid do(X = x))$. Graphically, all arrows into $X$ are deleted. The **average treatment effect** is then

$$
\tau = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid do(X=1)] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid do(X=0)].
$$

The DAG encodes conditional independencies via **d-separation**: $X$ and $Y$ are d-separated by $Z$ if every path between them is blocked (a chain $A \to M \to B$ or fork $A \leftarrow M \to B$ with $M \in Z$; a collider $A \to M \leftarrow B$ with $M \notin Z$ and no descendant in $Z$). Under the **causal Markov condition**, d-separation implies conditional independence in $P(V)$ [1].

Three canonical structures illustrate why naive conditioning fails:

- **Confounding** ($X \leftarrow Z \to Y$): $Z$ is a common cause; $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]$ mixes causal and spurious association. Adjust for $Z$.
- **Mediation** ($X \to M \to Y$): $M$ is a mechanism; adjusting for $M$ blocks the very effect of interest.
- **Collider bias** ($X \to M \leftarrow Y$): $M$ is a common effect; conditioning on $M$ *creates* spurious association between $X$ and $Y$ (Berkson's paradox).

### 2.2 Potential outcomes and ignorability

In Rubin's framework, treatment assignment $W_i \in \{0, 1\}$ reveals $Y_i = Y_i(W_i)$. The ATE $\tau = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0)]$ is identified under **ignorability** (unconfoundedness), $(Y(0), Y(1)) \perp W \mid X$, plus **overlap** $0 < P(W=1 \mid X) < 1$ and **SUTVA** (no interference) [3]. The frameworks connect: ignorability given $X$ holds iff $X$ blocks all back-door paths in the SCM graph.

| Framework | Primitive | Intervention | Identification condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Potential outcomes | $(Y(0), Y(1))$ | Hypothetical assignment | Ignorability, overlap |
| Structural causal models | Equations + DAG | $do$-operator (graph surgery) | d-separation criteria |
| Graphical (do-calculus) | DAG + $P(V)$ | Rule-based reduction | Completeness theorem |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The do-calculus

Pearl's **do-calculus** comprises three rules licensing the insertion/deletion of observations and interventions, where $G_{\bar X}$ denotes $G$ with arrows into $X$ removed and $G_{\underline Z}$ with arrows out of $Z$ removed [1]:

1. **Insertion/deletion of observations**: $P(y \mid do(x), z, w) = P(y \mid do(x), w)$ if $Y \perp\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W$ in $G_{\bar X}$.
2. **Action/observation exchange**: $P(y \mid do(x), do(z), w) = P(y \mid do(x), z, w)$ if $Y \perp\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W$ in $G_{\bar X \underline Z}$.
3. **Insertion/deletion of actions**: $P(y \mid do(x), do(z), w) = P(y \mid do(x), w)$ if $Y \perp\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W$ in $G_{\overline{XZ(W)}}$, where $Z(W)$ are nodes not ancestors of $W$.

Shpitser and Pearl proved these rules **complete**: any identifiable interventional query reduces to observational quantities, and non-identifiability is certified by the ID algorithm returning failure [4]. The do-calculus thus draws the exact boundary of what observational data can reveal—a rare completeness result in statistics.

```haskell
-- Schematic: the ID algorithm (Shpitser-Pearl)
identify :: DAG -> Query -> Either Fail Expr
identify g (Do x y)
  | null x            = return (marginal g y)
  | noAncestors g y x = return (marginal (delete g x) y)  -- Rule 3
  | otherwise         = case cComponents (ancestors g y) of
      [c] -> factorize g c >>= identify g . Do x
      cs  -> product <$> mapM (identify g . Do (x ∩)) cs
-- Failure <=> provably non-identifiable from P(V)
```

### 3.2 Classical identification strategies

- **Back-door adjustment.** If $Z$ blocks all back-door paths from $X$ to $Y$ (and contains no descendant of $X$), then $P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y \mid x, z) P(z)$—the adjustment formula [1].
- **Front-door criterion.** If $M$ fully mediates $X \to Y$ with no unblocked back-door paths $X \dashrightarrow M$ or $M \dashrightarrow Y$, the effect is identified even with unmeasured $X$–$Y$ confounding, via a two-stage adjustment [1].
- **Instrumental variables.** An instrument $Z$ affecting $X$ but $Y$ only through $X$ identifies local average treatment effects under monotonicity; two-stage least squares is the linear workhorse [5].
- **Difference-in-differences.** With parallel trends, the change in the treated group's outcome minus the change in the control group identifies the effect on the treated—no unconfoundedness needed, at the price of the trends assumption [5].
- **Regression discontinuity.** Treatment assigned by a running variable crossing a threshold identifies effects *at the cutoff* under continuity of potential outcomes—near-experimental credibility from observational data [5].

### 3.3 Doubly robust and debiased machine learning

Modern practice replaces parametric models with ML while preserving valid inference. The **augmented inverse-propensity-weighting (AIPW)** estimator combines an outcome model $\mu_w(X)$ and a propensity model $e(X)$:

$$
\hat\tau = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \left[ \mu_1(X_i) - \mu_0(X_i) + \frac{W_i(Y_i - \mu_1(X_i))}{e(X_i)} - \frac{(1-W_i)(Y_i - \mu_0(X_i))}{1 - e(X_i)} \right],
$$

which is **doubly robust** (consistent if either model is correct) and semiparametrically efficient [6]. **Double/debiased machine learning** adds cross-fitting (sample splitting) and **Neyman orthogonality**—the estimating equation's derivative with respect to nuisance functions vanishes—so that ML nuisance estimators converging at $n^{-1/4}$ rates still yield $\sqrt{n}$-consistent, asymptotically normal causal estimates [7]. This marries the flexibility of modern ML with the rigor of classical semiparametric theory.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The LaLonde reanalysis and the credibility revolution

LaLonde (1986) asked whether non-experimental methods could replicate an experimental benchmark: the National Supported Work Demonstration, a randomized job-training program. Applying leading econometric estimators to observational comparison groups produced estimates wildly off the experimental mark—suggesting observational causal inference was hopeless [8]. Decades of refinement followed: Dehejia and Wahba showed propensity-score methods *could* recover the experimental result, sparking debates about specification searching that ultimately clarified best practice (pre-registration, overlap diagnostics, placebo tests). The episode became the founding parable of the **credibility revolution**: design-based identification with transparent assumptions beats black-box regression [5][8].

### 4.2 The Oregon health-insurance experiment

When Oregon allocated Medicaid slots by lottery in 2008, it created a true randomized instrument for insurance coverage. Finkelstein et al. found that Medicaid *increased* emergency-department use—contradicting the hope that coverage would reduce ER congestion—and improved self-reported health and financial security, with no detectable short-run effect on physical health markers [9]. The study is a masterclass in combining experimental variation with careful causal estimands: the lottery identifies the local average treatment effect for compliers, and the authors are explicit about what it does and does not imply for national policy.

### 4.3 Causal discovery: learning the graph

When the DAG is unknown, **causal discovery** algorithms learn it from data. Constraint-based methods (PC, FCI) use conditional-independence tests to recover the Markov equivalence class; score-based methods (GES) search over graphs; functional methods (LiNGAM) exploit non-Gaussianity to orient edges fully [10]. Under **faithfulness** (independencies in $P$ exactly match d-separation in $G$) and causal sufficiency, these are consistent—but faithfulness is untestable and can fail on measure-zero parameter sets that are dense in practice (near-violations). Discovery remains the fragile frontier: it works best as hypothesis generation, not certification.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Result 1 (Completeness of do-calculus).** Shpitser–Pearl: the ID algorithm decides identifiability of $P(y \mid do(x))$ from $P(V)$ given $G$ [4]. Corollary: the back-door, front-door, and IV criteria are special cases of one unified algorithm.

**Result 2 (Neyman orthogonality).** Chernozhukov et al.: DML estimators with cross-fitting achieve $\sqrt{n}$-consistency and valid confidence intervals even when nuisance functions are estimated by arbitrary ML methods at $o(n^{-1/4})$ rates [7]. This is now the standard pipeline for high-dimensional causal inference.

**Result 3 (Oregon experiment).** IV estimates: Medicaid coverage increased ER visits by about 40 percent and reduced medical debt, with precise nulls on physical health—demonstrating that even gold-standard randomization answers only the estimand it was designed for [9].

**Result 4 (Sensitivity analysis).** Rosenbaum's bounds quantify how strong unmeasured confounding would need to be to overturn a conclusion; modern extensions (Cinelli–Hazlett) report robustness values—the minimal confounding strength that would kill statistical significance—making the identifying assumptions quantitatively debatable rather than binary [6].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Unmeasured confounding.** All observational identification rests on assumptions about what is *not* in the data; they are untestable from the data alone. Sensitivity analysis bounds the damage but cannot eliminate it.
2. **Faithfulness and discovery.** Causal discovery needs faithfulness, which is both untestable and fragile; learned graphs should inform, not conclude.
3. **Transportability.** An effect identified in one population need not transport to another; Pearl and Bareinboim's transport calculus characterizes when it does, but selection diagrams are rarely known [1].
4. **Interference (SUTVA violations).** Network and spillover effects—vaccines, marketplaces, social programs—violate no-interference; interference-aware estimands are an active area.
5. **High-dimensional and unstructured treatments.** Text, images, and dosages as treatments break discrete-treatment machinery; causal representation learning (learning latent causal variables from pixels) is nascent.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Causal inference has matured from philosophical debate into an engineering discipline with completeness theorems, efficient estimators, and a hard-won empirical culture. The do-calculus tells us exactly what can be learned from seeing; the classical designs tell us how to approximate doing; and debiased machine learning lets us do both at scale without sacrificing statistical validity.

The next frontier is ambitious: causal discovery that is robust rather than fragile, transportability that lets evidence travel, and causal representations that lift the theory from tabular variables to the unstructured data of modern AI. If the twentieth century taught us to beware confounding, the twenty-first is teaching us to compute with it—and, where possible, to see through it to the mechanisms beneath.
