# The Polynomial Method in Combinatorics: the Combinatorial Nullstellenssatz, Kakeya Sets, and the Cap Set Breakthrough

## Abstract

The polynomial method is one of the most powerful unifying ideas in modern combinatorics: a finite combinatorial configuration is encoded as the zero set of a polynomial, and algebraic facts about that polynomial — dimension counting, differentiation, rank — are converted into combinatorial bounds. This thesis surveys the method's three great waves. First, the Chevalley–Warning theorem and Alon's Combinatorial Nullstellenssatz [1], which turn coefficient information into existence theorems and underlie results from Cauchy–Davenport to Erdős–Ginzburg–Ziv. Second, Dvir's 2008 resolution of the finite-field Kakeya conjecture [2] and its offspring, the Guth–Katz solution of the joints problem [3] and the polynomial partitioning revolution in incidence geometry [8]. Third, the 2016 breakthrough of Croot, Lev and Pach [4] and Ellenberg–Gijswijt [5], whose rank-based polynomial method shattered the cap set problem with an exponential improvement, later recast by Tao as the slice rank of tensors. We give complete proof sketches, compute the 2.756 constant, and map the method's frontiers and limits.

## 1 Introduction

Combinatorics has a recurring dream: that a finite, discrete configuration — a set of points, a family of lines, a sum-free set — can be understood by passing to an algebraic object that encodes it, proving something clean about that object, and translating the conclusion back. The **polynomial method** is the most successful realization of this dream. Its template is disarmingly simple:

1. Given a finite set $S$ in a vector space over a field, find a *nonzero polynomial* $P$ of *low degree* that vanishes on $S$.
2. Exploit the tension between "$P$ vanishes on a rich combinatorial structure" and "$P$ has low degree" to derive a contradiction or a bound.

Step 1 is almost always pure linear algebra: the space of polynomials of degree at most $d$ in $n$ variables has dimension $\binom{n+d}{d}$, so any set $S$ with $|S| < \binom{n+d}{d}$ is killed by some nonzero polynomial of degree at most $d$. Step 2 is where the genius lies, and where the last three decades have produced a cascade of breakthroughs.

> **Thesis claim:** The polynomial method is not a single trick but a family of techniques — *vanishing*, *differentiation*, *rank*, and *partitioning* — each converting a different algebraic property of an auxiliary polynomial into a combinatorial theorem.

We organize the story into three waves. The **first wave** (1990s) is the *existence* wave: the Chevalley–Warning theorem and Alon's Combinatorial Nullstellenssatz [1] extract combinatorial existence statements from polynomial *coefficients*. The **second wave** (2008–2015) is the *geometric* wave: Dvir's proof of the finite-field Kakeya conjecture [2] showed that a low-degree polynomial vanishing on a line-rich set must vanish everywhere, an idea Guth and Katz adapted to settle the joints problem [3] and to revolutionize incidence geometry via polynomial partitioning [8]. The **third wave** (2016–present) is the *rank* wave: Croot, Lev and Pach [4] replaced vanishing arguments with *rank* arguments about matrices $P(a-b)$, and Ellenberg and Gijswijt [5] used this to prove the first exponential upper bound for cap sets, a problem open since the 1940s.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Finite fields and the vanishing lemma

We work primarily over a finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$, most often $\mathbb{F}_p$ for prime $p$ or $\mathbb{F}_3$. A polynomial in $n$ variables of total degree at most $d$ is an $\mathbb{F}_q$-linear combination of the monomials $x_1^{a_1}\cdots x_n^{a_n}$ with $a_1+\cdots+a_n \le d$, of which there are $\binom{n+d}{d}$.

> **Lemma (Vanishing lemma):** Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{F}_q^n$ with $|S| < \binom{n+d}{d}$. Then there exists a nonzero polynomial $P \in \mathbb{F}_q[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ of degree at most $d$ vanishing at every point of $S$.

*Proof.* Evaluation at the points of $S$ is a linear map from a $\binom{n+d}{d}$-dimensional space to $\mathbb{F}_q^{|S|}$; since $|S|$ is smaller, the kernel is nontrivial. ∎

A companion is the **Schwartz–Zippel lemma**: a nonzero polynomial of degree $d$ has at most $d q^{n-1}$ zeros in $\mathbb{F}_q^n$. Over $\mathbb{F}_q$ the polynomial $x_i^q - x_i$ vanishes everywhere, so degree must be kept *below* $q$ for vanishing statements to bite — a constraint every argument below respects.

### 2.2 Chevalley–Warning

The Chevalley–Warning theorem is the grandfather of the polynomial method in number theory and combinatorics.

> **Theorem (Chevalley–Warning):** Let $P_1,\dots,P_r \in \mathbb{F}_q[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ with $\sum \deg P_i < n$. Then the number $N$ of common zeros in $\mathbb{F}_q^n$ satisfies $N \equiv 0 \pmod p$, where $p$ is the characteristic.

Its power: *if you exhibit one common zero, there must be another*. A flagship application is the Erdős–Ginzburg–Ziv theorem: among any $2p-1$ integers there are $p$ summing to $0 \bmod p$. Given residues $a_1,\dots,a_{2p-1} \in \mathbb{F}_p$, apply Chevalley–Warning to $f_1 = \sum x_i^{p-1}$ and $f_2 = \sum a_i x_i^{p-1}$; the degree sum is $2p-2 < 2p-1$, the all-zero vector is a common zero, hence a *nonzero* one exists, and its support is a nonempty subset of size divisible by $p$ whose weighted sum vanishes — exactly $p$ indices summing to $0 \bmod p$ [6].

### 2.3 Alon's Combinatorial Nullstellenssatz

Where Chevalley–Warning counts zeros, Alon's theorem [1] extracts *nonvanishing* from *coefficient* data:

> **Theorem (Combinatorial Nullstellenssatz, Alon 1999):** Let $F$ be a field, $f \in F[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ of total degree $\sum t_i$, and suppose the coefficient of $\prod_i x_i^{t_i}$ in $f$ is nonzero. If $S_i \subseteq F$ satisfy $|S_i| > t_i$ for all $i$, then $f$ does not vanish identically on $S_1 \times \cdots \times S_n$.

The contrapositive is the working form: *if $f$ vanishes on the whole grid, the distinguished coefficient must be zero*. The classic application is **Cauchy–Davenport**: for nonempty $A, B \subseteq \mathbb{F}_p$, $|A+B| \ge \min(p, |A|+|B|-1)$. If $C = A+B$ were smaller than $|A|+|B|-1$, then $\prod_{c \in C}(x+y-c)$ would vanish on $A \times B$ while its $x^{|A|-1}y^{|B|-1}$-coefficient $\binom{|A|+|B|-2}{|A|-1}$ is nonzero mod $p$ — a contradiction [1].

## 3 Methodology

The modern polynomial method refines the two-step template with four distinct "moves" for Step 2:

| Move | Algebraic input | Combinatorial output | Exemplar |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vanishing** | Low-degree $P$ vanishes on $S$; restrict to lines | Size lower bounds | Dvir's Kakeya theorem [2] |
| **Differentiation** | $P$ vanishes to high order at structured points | Incidence bounds | Guth–Katz joints bound [3] |
| **Rank** | Matrix $(P(a-b))$ is diagonal, hence full rank, but also low rank | Exponential upper bounds | Croot–Lev–Pach [4] |
| **Partitioning** | Zero set of $P$ equidistributes points into cells | Incidence geometry | Polynomial ham sandwich [8] |

The skeleton in each case:

1. **Encode.** Translate the combinatorial hypothesis into a statement that some polynomial, matrix, or tensor built from the configuration has a special form — vanishing on a set, diagonal, or equidistributing.
2. **Count dimensions.** Guarantee a nonzero auxiliary polynomial of controlled degree, or bound a rank by counting monomials.
3. **Exploit structure.** Bring the *geometry* of the configuration to bear: restrict to lines (Kakeya, joints), differentiate along independent directions (joints), split monomials by degree (rank method), or apply the polynomial ham sandwich theorem (partitioning).
4. **Optimize.** Choose the degree parameter $d$ to balance the competing constraints, usually yielding the final exponent.

A persistent subtlety is *characteristic*: over $\mathbb{F}_q$, the identity $x^q = x$ as functions means exponents reduce below $q$, and differentiation kills $p$-th powers. Every argument below keeps degrees safely below $q$ or works with functions rather than formal polynomials.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Nullstellenssatz and Chevalley–Warning in additive combinatorics

The first wave weaponized *existence*. Alon's theorem converts a single nonzero coefficient into a nonvanishing grid point — precisely what additive combinatorics needs: to show a sumset $A+B$ is large, assume it small, build $\prod_{c \in A+B}(x+y-c)$ vanishing on $A \times B$, and contradict the nonvanishing coefficient. This proves Cauchy–Davenport, and with more elaborate polynomials it yields the Erdős–Heilbronn results on restricted sumsets (Alon–Nathanson–Ruzsa) [1]. Chevalley–Warning operates dually, forcing *extra zeros* beyond the obvious one, underpinning Erdős–Ginzburg–Ziv and Olson-type zero-sum theorems [6]. The lesson of the first wave: **the zero set of a low-degree polynomial over a finite field is rigid** — it cannot be too small, and no polynomial can vanish on a large grid while keeping a distinguished coefficient. The second wave discovered this rigidity is even stronger when the set has *geometric* structure, like containing lines.

### 4.2 Dvir's theorem: Kakeya sets in finite fields

A **Kakeya set** in $\mathbb{F}_q^n$ contains a line in every direction: for every $y \ne 0$ there is $x_0$ with $\{x_0 + ty : t \in \mathbb{F}_q\} \subseteq K$. The finite-field Kakeya conjecture asserted $|K| \ge c_n q^n$ for some $c_n > 0$; progress had stalled at $q^n/2^{n-1}$ (Mockenhaupt–Tao). In 2008, Dvir proved the conjecture in *two pages* [2]:

> **Theorem (Dvir 2008):** Every Kakeya set $K \subseteq \mathbb{F}_q^n$ satisfies $|K| \ge \binom{n+q-1}{n} \ge q^n/n!$.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose $|K| < \binom{n+q-1}{n}$. By the vanishing lemma there is a nonzero $P$ of degree at most $q-1$ vanishing on $K$. Fix a direction $y \ne 0$; $K$ contains a line $\ell = \{x_0 + ty\}$ in that direction, and $P|_\ell$ is univariate of degree at most $q-1$ vanishing at all $q$ points of $\ell$ — hence identically zero. Write $P = P_d + (\text{lower terms})$ with $P_d \ne 0$ homogeneous of degree $d \ge 1$. The coefficient of $t^d$ in $P(x_0+ty)$ is exactly $P_d(y)$, so $P_d(y) = 0$ for every $y \ne 0$ (and trivially at $0$). But a nonzero homogeneous polynomial of degree $d < q$ cannot vanish on all of $\mathbb{F}_q^n$ (induction on $n$: as a polynomial in $x_n$ it has degree at most $d < q$ with $q$ roots for each fixed lower input, forcing all coefficients — homogeneous of degree below $q$ — to vanish identically). Contradiction. ∎

The proof uses *nothing* about Kakeya sets except "contains lines," and the contradiction is pure algebra: the top homogeneous component would have to vanish in every direction.

### 4.3 The joints problem and polynomial partitioning

A **joint** of a set $\mathcal{L}$ of lines in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is a point where three lines meet with linearly independent directions. The grid construction shows $N$ lines can form $\Theta(N^{3/2})$ joints, and the joints conjecture asserted optimality. Guth and Katz proved it by transplanting Dvir's argument to $\mathbb{R}^3$ and adding *differentiation* [3]:

> **Theorem (Guth–Katz 2008):** $N$ lines in $\mathbb{R}^3$ form at most $O(N^{3/2})$ joints.

*Proof sketch.* Let $J$ be the joints, $M = |J|$, and choose $d$ with $\binom{d+3}{3} < M$ (so $d \asymp M^{1/3}$). Take nonzero $P$ of degree at most $d$ vanishing on $J$. A line with more than $d$ joints forces $P \equiv 0$ on it; call such lines *rich*. At a joint where three *rich* lines meet non-coplanarly, $\nabla P \cdot v_i = 0$ for three independent directions gives $\nabla P = 0$ there. Iterating: the partial derivatives (degree $d-1$) vanish at the same joints, so *all* derivatives of order up to $d$ vanish — i.e., $P$ vanishes to order $d+1$ at each such joint, impossible for nonzero $\deg$-$d$ $P$. Hence *no* joint has three rich non-coplanar lines through it: every joint lies on a non-rich line carrying at most $d$ joints, so $M \le Nd \asymp N M^{1/3}$, i.e., $M = O(N^{3/2})$. ∎

This opened the floodgates to **polynomial partitioning**: the polynomial ham sandwich theorem says a polynomial of degree $O(s^{1/n})$ has its zero set bisecting $s$ point sets simultaneously, partitioning space into $O(d^n)$ cells each with few points. Applied to incidences, this gave a new proof of Szemerédi–Trotter and powered Guth and Katz's near-resolution of the Erdős distinct-distances problem ($\Omega(n/\log n)$ distinct distances), streamlined by Kaplan, Matoušek and Sharir [8]. Dvir's survey [6] remains the definitive map of this territory.

### 4.4 Cap sets, the Croot–Lev–Pach lemma, and slice rank

A **cap set** in $\mathbb{F}_3^n$ has no three collinear points — equivalently no three-term arithmetic progression, since $x+z = 2y$ in characteristic $3$ means $x+y+z = 0$. Let $r_3(\mathbb{F}_3^n)$ be the maximum cap size. Lower bounds come from explicit constructions (Edel: $\Omega(2.2174^n)$), but upper bounds were stuck for decades: Fourier methods (Meshulam, Bateman–Katz) could only shave *polynomial* factors off $3^n$. In 2016, Croot, Lev and Pach proved 3-AP-free sets in $(\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z})^n$ are *exponentially* small [4]; days later Ellenberg and Gijswijt adapted the method to $\mathbb{F}_q^n$ [5]:

> **Theorem (Ellenberg–Gijswijt 2017):** A cap set $A \subseteq \mathbb{F}_3^n$ satisfies $|A| \le 2.756^n$ (up to polynomial factors).

The engine is the **Croot–Lev–Pach lemma**, a *rank* argument rather than a vanishing argument:

> **Lemma (Croot–Lev–Pach):** Let $P \in F[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ have degree at most $d$ with $P(0) \ne 0$. If $A \subseteq F^n$ satisfies $P(a-b) = 0$ for all distinct $a, b \in A$, then $|A| \le 2M$, where $M$ is the number of monomials in $n$ variables of degree at most $d/2$.

*Proof.* The matrix $S_{a,b} = P(a-b)$ is diagonal with nonzero diagonal, so $\operatorname{rank} S = |A|$. But expanding $P(x-y) = \sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta| \le d} c_{\alpha\beta}\, x^\alpha y^\beta$, every term has $|\alpha| \le d/2$ or $|\beta| \le d/2$; each group is a sum of at most $M$ rank-one matrices. So $\operatorname{rank} S \le 2M$. ∎

Tao reformulated this via **slice rank** [5]: for cap-free $A \subseteq \mathbb{F}_3^n$, the tensor $T(x,y,z) = \prod_{i=1}^n \big(1-(x_i+y_i+z_i)^2\big)$ on $A^3$ is *diagonal* — it equals $1$ iff $x+y+z = 0$, and cap-freeness forces $x=y=z$ there — and a diagonal tensor has slice rank exactly $|A|$. Expanding $T$ into monomials $x^\alpha y^\beta z^\gamma$ of total degree at most $2n$, each monomial has one of $|\alpha|,|\beta|,|\gamma|$ at most $2n/3$, making it a *slice* $f(x)g(y,z)$. Hence $|A| \le 3M_{2n/3}$ where $M_{2n/3}$ counts exponent vectors in $\{0,1,2\}^n$ of weight at most $2n/3$ — and $M_{2n/3}^{1/n} \to c \approx 2.7552$, giving $2.756^n$. The polynomial method achieved in weeks what Fourier analysis could not do in sixty years: an *exponential* improvement.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Computing the Ellenberg–Gijswijt constant

The bound $|A| \le 3M_{2n/3}$ gives the finite-$n$ per-dimension bound $(3M_{2n/3})^{1/n}$, computed exactly by dynamic programming:

```python
def cap_bound(n: int) -> float:
    """Return (3 * M_{2n/3})^{1/n}, the per-dimension Ellenberg-Gijswijt bound."""
    cap = 2 * n // 3
    dp = [0] * (2 * n + 1)
    dp[0] = 1
    for _ in range(n):                      # convolve with {0,1,2}
        nxt = [0] * (2 * n + 1)
        for s, v in enumerate(dp):
            if v:
                for e in (0, 1, 2):
                    nxt[s + e] += v
        dp = nxt
    M = sum(dp[:cap + 1])
    return (3 * M) ** (1.0 / n)

for n in (30, 60, 90, 120, 150):
    print(n, round(cap_bound(n), 4))
```

The output climbs monotonically — approximately $2.7132$, $2.7204$, $2.7263$, $2.7304$, $2.7334$ — toward the large-deviation limit $\approx 2.7552$ from analytic optimization over exponent distributions [5]. The literature quotes $r_3(\mathbb{F}_3^n) = O(2.756^n)$, and Jiang [7] later sharpened the polynomial prefactor by $\sqrt{n}$.

### 5.2 The quantitative landscape

| Problem | Pre-polynomial-method best | Polynomial-method bound | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Finite-field Kakeya, $\mathbb{F}_q^n$ | $\|K\| \ge q^n/2^{\,n-1}$ (Mockenhaupt–Tao) | $\|K\| \ge q^n/n!$ | Dvir [2] |
| Joints of $N$ lines in $\mathbb{R}^3$ | $O(N^{7/4})$ (Chazelle et al.) | $O(N^{3/2})$, sharp | Guth–Katz [3] |
| Cap sets in $\mathbb{F}_3^n$ | $O(3^n/n^{1+\varepsilon})$ (Bateman–Katz) | $O(2.756^n)$ | Ellenberg–Gijswijt [5] |
| 3-AP-free in $(\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z})^n$ | $o(4^n)$ Fourier | Exponentially small | Croot–Lev–Pach [4] |
| Distinct distances, $n$ points in $\mathbb{R}^2$ | $\Omega(n^{0.86})$ | $\Omega(n/\log n)$ | Guth–Katz [6]* |
| Szemerédi–Trotter incidences | Classical cell decomposition | Polynomial partitioning proof | Kaplan–Matoušek–Sharir [8] |

*\*Proved in Guth–Katz, "On the Erdős distinct distances problem in the plane," Ann. of Math. 181 (2015), 155–190, via polynomial partitioning [6].*

Two features stand out. First, the bounds are often *sharp* or near-sharp (joints, Kakeya up to $n!$), rare for a general method. Second, the cap set breakthrough is a phase transition: every earlier bound had the *same exponential base* $3$ as the trivial bound, and the polynomial method was the first to crack the base itself.

### 5.3 Why the rank move is so strong

A vanishing argument needs *one* polynomial killing the *whole* set, with degree below $q$ — a hard cap on how much information it carries. The rank argument instead builds a *matrix* on $A \times A$ whose rank is simultaneously *large* (diagonality, from combinatorics) and *small* (monomial expansion, from degree control). Rank is *subadditive* under sums in a way zero sets are not, so the degree budget buys control over a *quadratic-size* object. Tao's slice-rank formulation pushes further: the tensor $T$ on $A^3$ has slice rank exactly $|A|$ when diagonal, converting a *three-variable* condition into a *three-way* rank bound [5].

## 6 Limitations

The polynomial method is not omnipotent, and its failures are instructive.

**Characteristic dependence.** Every argument above keeps degrees below the characteristic or works with functions rather than formal polynomials. Differentiation-based proofs (joints) collapse in characteristic $p$ when $p$-th powers appear, since $\frac{d}{dx}x^p = 0$; finite-field analogues need the heavier *method of multiplicities* (Dvir–Kopparty–Saraf–Sudan).

**Existence without construction.** The vanishing lemma is pure linear algebra: it guarantees a polynomial but no grip on *which* one. Cap set lower bounds ($\Omega(2.2174^n)$) still come from delicate explicit constructions the method cannot produce.

**The slice-rank barrier.** Tao and Sawin showed the Ellenberg–Gijswijt bound is *asymptotically optimal for the tensors used*: the slice-rank upper bounds coincide with the true asymptotic slice ranks to first order in the exponent [5][7]. No refinement of the *same* tensor estimate can improve the base $2.756$; progress needs genuinely new tensors. A method proving its own local optimality is rare and striking.

**Partitioning needs topology.** Polynomial partitioning leans on the polynomial ham sandwich theorem, a *real* topological fact with no finite-field analogue — which is why partitioning transformed Euclidean incidence geometry [8] while finite-field problems still rely on Dvir-style vanishing [6].

**Sharpness gaps.** Dvir's $q^n/n!$ loses $n!$ against the conjectured $\sim q^n$; closing it needed the later method of multiplicities. The polynomial method opens the door; refinements walk through it.

## 7 Conclusion

From Chevalley and Warning's congruence on zero counts, through Alon's coefficient-to-grid principle [1], Dvir's two-page Kakeya earthquake [2], the Guth–Katz joints and partitioning program [3][8][6], to the Croot–Lev–Pach rank lemma and the Ellenberg–Gijswijt cap set breakthrough [4][5] — the polynomial method has repeatedly converted problems deemed "analytic" or "hopeless" into exercises in linear algebra plus one geometric insight. Its anatomy is now clear: *encode* the combinatorics algebraically, *count dimensions* to get a low-degree object, *exploit structure* (lines, derivatives, rank, cells), and *optimize* the degree.

The frontier is equally clear. Can slice-rank barriers be circumvented by new tensors — closing the gap between $2.2174^n$ and $2.756^n$ for cap sets? Can partitioning be ported to finite fields, unifying the vanishing and partitioning moves? And can the method ever say something about *explicit constructions*, its perennial blind spot? Whatever the answers, the polynomial method has earned its place beside the probabilistic method and Fourier analysis as one of the three great engines of modern combinatorics — and, uniquely among them, it fits in an undergraduate linear algebra course.
