# The Weisfeiler–Leman Hierarchy and the Expressive Limits of Graph Neural Networks

## Abstract

The Weisfeiler–Leman (WL) family of combinatorial refinement procedures, introduced in 1968 for graph isomorphism testing, has become the dominant theoretical yardstick for the expressive power of graph neural networks (GNNs). This thesis develops a rigorous account of the $k$-dimensional WL hierarchy and its precise correspondence with the discriminative power of neural architectures. We prove, following Xu et al., that message-passing GNNs can distinguish two graphs only if the 1-dimensional WL test (1-WL, color refinement) does, and that this bound is tight: the Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN), built on injective multiset aggregation, matches 1-WL. We characterize the canonical failures of 1-WL — the 6-cycle versus two disjoint triangles, regular graphs, substructure counting — and show how higher-order tests $k$-WL stratify into a strict hierarchy with matching neural realizations: $k$-GNNs, invariant graph networks, and provably powerful graph networks (PPGNs) attaining $3$-WL power. We close with complexity trade-offs, the separation–approximation equivalence of Chen et al., and open problems in scalable higher-order architectures.

---

## 1. Introduction

Graphs are the natural data structure wherever relational structure is primary: molecules, social networks, programs. The machine learning problem on graphs is therefore representation learning *modulo relabeling*: graph representations must be permutation-invariant, node representations permutation-equivariant [3].

Two traditions long addressed this problem in isolation. In *kernel methods*, the Weisfeiler–Leman subtree kernel of Shervashidze et al. [4] extracted fixed combinatorial features — histograms of iterated colorings — in $O(hm)$ time with strong benchmark performance. In *neural methods*, message-passing networks (GCN, GraphSAGE) learned representations end-to-end but were evaluated almost entirely empirically. The traditions collided in 2019, when Xu et al. [1] and, independently, Morris et al. [2] proved that standard message-passing GNNs are bounded above by the 1-dimensional WL test — the very color-refinement procedure at the heart of the WL kernel.

This result reframed the research program. If message passing is bounded by 1-WL, then *expressive power* — the capacity to distinguish non-isomorphic graphs — can be studied through the $k$-WL hierarchy, a classical ladder from finite model theory in which each rung $k \geq 2$ colors $k$-tuples of vertices and is strictly stronger than the last. Higher rungs admit neural realizations: $k$-GNNs [2], $k$-order invariant graph networks [3], and the provably powerful graph network (PPGN) achieving $3$-WL power. The central question of this thesis is therefore:

> **What is the precise relationship between the Weisfeiler–Leman hierarchy and the expressive power of graph neural networks, and what are its consequences for architecture design?**

Our contributions are expository and analytic rather than empirical:

1. A unified formal treatment of 1-WL and $k$-WL as color refinement on (tuples of) vertices, with exact complexity bounds.
2. A complete proof sketch of the Xu et al. [1] characterization: message-passing GNNs $\leq$ 1-WL, with equality for GIN via injective multiset aggregation.
3. A catalog of the canonical blind spots of 1-WL — the $C_6$ vs. $2K_3$ pair, regular graphs, and substructure counting — with proofs of indistinguishability.
4. The hierarchy theorems: strictness of $k$-WL, the $k$-GNN correspondence, and the PPGN universality results of Maron et al. [3].
5. A discussion of the separation-versus-approximation equivalence of Chen et al. [5], complexity scalability [6], and open problems.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The 1-WL Test: Color Refinement

Let $G = (V(G), E(G))$ be an undirected graph with $n$ vertices and $m$ edges. The 1-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman test, also called *color refinement*, computes a sequence of colorings $c^{(t)} : V(G) \to \Sigma_t$ as follows. All vertices begin with a common initial color (or, in the labeled case, a color determined by their input label): $c^{(0)}(v) = \ell(v)$. Then, for $t \geq 0$,

$$
c^{(t+1)}(v) \;=\; \mathrm{HASH}\!\left(c^{(t)}(v),\; \big\{\!\!\big\{ c^{(t)}(u) \;:\; u \in N(v) \big\}\!\!\big\}\right),
$$

where $N(v)$ is the neighborhood of $v$, $\big\{\!\!\big\{ \cdot \big\}\!\!\big\}$ denotes a multiset, and $\mathrm{HASH}$ is an injective map assigning a fresh color to each distinct pair. The process stabilizes after at most $n-1$ iterations to the *stable coloring* $c^{(\infty)}$.

Two graphs $G, H$ are deemed non-isomorphic by 1-WL if their color histograms differ at some iteration. If the histograms agree at every iteration, 1-WL *fails to distinguish* the graphs — they may still be non-isomorphic.

> **Theorem (Complexity of 1-WL):** One refinement iteration costs $O(n + m)$; the full procedure costs $O(nm)$ in the worst case, and the WL subtree kernel built on $h$ iterations costs $O(hm)$ per graph [4].

### 2.2 Message-Passing Neural Networks

A message-passing neural network computes node embeddings by iterated neighborhood aggregation [1]:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_v^{(k)} &= \mathrm{AGGREGATE}^{(k)}\!\left(\big\{\!\!\big\{ h_u^{(k-1)} : u \in N(v) \big\}\!\!\big\}\right),\
h_v^{(k)} &= \mathrm{COMBINE}^{(k)}\!\left(h_v^{(k-1)},\, a_v^{(k)}\right),
\end{aligned}
$$

with $h_v^{(0)}$ given by input node features. Popular instantiations differ only in their choice of $\mathrm{AGGREGATE}$ and $\mathrm{COMBINE}$:

| Model | Aggregation | Combine / update |
|---|---|---|
| GCN | normalized mean | linear + ReLU |
| GraphSAGE (mean) | mean | concat + MLP |
| GIN [1] | sum | MLP of $(1+\epsilon)h_v + a_v$ |
| GAT | attention-weighted mean | linear + nonlinearity |

The decisive observation of Xu et al. [1] is that this scheme is structurally identical to color refinement: replace $\mathrm{HASH}$ by a *learned* aggregation function and colors by embedding vectors, and the two procedures coincide in form. Whether they coincide in *power* depends entirely on the injectivity of the learned aggregation — a question we resolve in Section 4.1.

### 2.3 Graph Isomorphism: The Broader Context

Graph isomorphism (GI) is the problem of deciding whether two graphs are structurally identical up to vertex relabeling. Its complexity status is famously unresolved: not known to be in $\mathsf{P}$, not known to be $\mathsf{NP}$-complete. The WL tests are *incomplete* heuristics for GI — polynomial-time procedures that may err by declaring non-isomorphic graphs indistinguishable. Their study as a complexity-theoretic tool long predates their use in machine learning [7], and $k$-WL connects to descriptive complexity (the logic $\mathcal{C}^{k+1}$ with counting quantifiers), Sherali–Adams relaxations, and homomorphism counting [6].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is theoretical — definitions, theorems with proof sketches, and counterexamples — under the following conventions:

- **Separation power.** A model class $\mathcal{F}$ *separates* graphs $G, H$ if some $f \in \mathcal{F}$ satisfies $f(G) \neq f(H)$. A GNN is *bounded by 1-WL* if it separates only pairs that 1-WL separates [1, 2].
- **Colored graphs.** All results extend to labeled graphs by initializing $c^{(0)}$ from input labels; we state the unlabeled case for clarity.
- **Countability.** Following [1], we assume countable feature spaces when invoking universal approximation of injective multiset functions by MLPs.
- **Equivalence of separation and approximation.** We rely on the theorem of Chen et al. [5]: a permutation-invariant model class separates all non-isomorphic graphs in a compact family if and only if it universally approximates all continuous invariant functions on that family. This justifies studying expressiveness through the combinatorial lens of distinguishing non-isomorphic pairs.

We verify all cited theorems against the primary sources [1]–[7] and the official GIN implementation [8]; no claim rests on secondary exposition alone.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The 1-WL Upper Bound: Why Message Passing Cannot Escape Color Refinement

The foundational result of the field is due to Xu et al. [1], refined concurrently by Morris et al. [2].

> **Theorem (Xu et al., 2019):** Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a message-passing GNN with aggregation $\mathrm{AGGREGATE}$ and combine $\mathrm{COMBINE}$. If the 1-WL test assigns identical color histograms to graphs $G$ and $H$, then $\mathcal{A}$ assigns identical graph-level embeddings to $G$ and $H$. In particular, no MPNN can distinguish graphs that 1-WL cannot.

*Proof sketch.* We prove by induction on the layer index $k$ that 1-WL color equality implies embedding equality. Base case: equal initial colors give equal $h^{(0)}$. Inductive step: if $c^{(t)}(v) = c^{(t)}(u)$, then $c^{(t-1)}(v) = c^{(t-1)}(u)$ and the multisets of neighbor colors agree; by the induction hypothesis, neighbor *embeddings* agree as multisets, so the deterministic $\mathrm{AGGREGATE}$ and $\mathrm{COMBINE}$ produce identical outputs. Since the graph-level readout is permutation-invariant, identical color histograms yield identical graph embeddings. ∎

The bound is not merely an upper limit — it is *achievable*. The key is injectivity of the multiset aggregation:

> **Theorem (GIN is 1-WL-complete):** The Graph Isomorphism Network update $h_v^{(k)} = \mathrm{MLP}^{(k)}\!\left((1 + \epsilon^{(k)}) h_v^{(k-1)} + \sum_{u \in N(v)} h_u^{(k-1)}\right)$ with sum aggregation and a sufficiently expressive MLP can realize any injective function on multisets of countable features, and hence matches the distinguishing power of 1-WL [1].

The intuition is a classical fact: the *sum* of elements of a multiset is injective over multisets of one-hot (or suitably encoded countable) features, whereas mean and max are not. Concretely, $\{\!\!\{a, a, b\}\!\!\}$ and $\{\!\!\{a, b, b\}\!\!\}$ have the same mean under a uniform embedding but different sums; mean pooling therefore collapses distinctions that 1-WL's injective hash preserves. This yields a sharp ranking:

| Architecture | Multiset aggregation | Power |
|---|---|---|
| GCN, GraphSAGE-mean | normalized mean | strictly below 1-WL |
| GraphSAGE-max | max | strictly below 1-WL |
| GIN [1] | sum + MLP | equals 1-WL |

The practical corollary is stark: the aggregation function determines the *equivalence class* of a model's expressiveness, not just its accuracy. The official implementation [8] confirms that GIN attains state-of-the-art accuracy on standard graph classification benchmarks precisely because it saturates this bound.

```python
# GIN layer: injective neighborhood aggregation (Xu et al., 2019) [1]
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class GINLayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim, eps=0.0):
        super().__init__()
        self.mlp = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(dim, dim), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(dim, dim))
        self.eps = nn.Parameter(torch.tensor([eps]))

    def forward(self, h, adj):
        agg = torch.matmul(adj, h)  # sum aggregation: injective on multisets
        return self.mlp((1 + self.eps) * h + agg)
```

### 4.2 The Canonical Blind Spots of 1-WL

To understand the limits of all message-passing GNNs, it suffices to study where 1-WL fails. Three classes of counterexamples are canonical.

**Counterexample 1: $C_6$ versus $2K_3$.** Let $G$ be the 6-cycle and $H$ the disjoint union of two triangles. Both graphs are 2-regular on 6 vertices. Under 1-WL, every vertex in both graphs begins with the same color, and every vertex sees the multiset $\{\!\!\{\text{same}, \text{same}\}\!\!\}$ of neighbor colors; refinement stabilizes immediately with identical histograms. Hence 1-WL — and therefore *every* MPNN, including GIN — declares $G$ and $H$ indistinguishable. Yet $G$ is connected and $H$ is not; $G$ has girth 6 and $H$ has girth 3. This single pair defeats the entire message-passing paradigm.

**Counterexample 2: Regular graphs.** More generally, two $d$-regular graphs on the same number of vertices with no input labels are 1-WL-indistinguishable, since refinement sees only uniform degree sequences. Non-isomorphic 3-regular pairs exist on as few as 8 vertices — and since many molecular graphs are nearly regular, this strikes at chemoinformatics applications rather than a pathological corner.

**Counterexample 3: Substructure counting.** Message-passing GNNs bounded by 1-WL cannot count (induced) substructures such as triangles or $k$-cliques in general: color refinement records only *iterated degree sequences*, never the edges *among* a vertex's neighbors. Formally, the homomorphism count of any graph of treewidth $\geq 2$ is not determined by the 1-WL coloring [6].

These failures are not bugs but the *definition* of the 1-WL equivalence class: $G \equiv_{1\text{-WL}} H$ is coarser than graph isomorphism, and every MPNN's output is constant on its equivalence classes.

### 4.3 The $k$-WL Hierarchy: Strictly Increasing Power

For $k \geq 2$, the $k$-dimensional WL test colors $k$-tuples $\mathbf{v} = (v_1, \dots, v_k) \in V(G)^k$ rather than single vertices. The initial coloring $c_k^{(0)}(\mathbf{v})$ is the *atomic type* of the tuple — the isomorphism type of the induced labeled substructure, recording equalities $v_i = v_j$ and adjacencies $(v_i, v_j) \in E$. Refinement aggregates over the $k$ *neighborhoods* obtained by substituting each position:

$$
c_k^{(t+1)}(\mathbf{v}) = \mathrm{HASH}\!\left(c_k^{(t)}(\mathbf{v}),\; \big(\, \big\{\!\!\big\{ c_k^{(t)}(\mathbf{v}[j \!\leftarrow\! w]) : w \in V(G) \big\}\!\!\big\} \,\big)_{j=1}^{k}\right),
$$

where $\mathbf{v}[j \leftarrow w]$ replaces the $j$-th component by $w$. Two graphs are distinguished if their $k$-tuple color histograms differ.

> **Theorem (Strictness of the hierarchy):** For every $k \geq 1$, there exist graphs distinguished by $(k+1)$-WL but not by $k$-WL. The Cai–Fürer–Immerman construction provides explicit separating families [7].

The hierarchy is also *complete in the limit* for bounded $n$: $(n-1)$-WL decides isomorphism. But the price is steep: one iteration of $k$-WL costs $O(k^2 n^{k+1})$ time and $O(n^k)$ space. The $C_6$ vs. $2K_3$ pair is separated already at $k = 2$: atomic types of pairs record adjacency, so 2-WL detects that distance-2 pairs in $C_6$ are non-adjacent while corresponding pairs in $2K_3$ include triangle edges. In logical terms, $k$-WL corresponds to the $(k+1)$-variable fragment of first-order logic with counting quantifiers, $\mathcal{C}^{k+1}$ — a deep bridge between combinatorics, logic, and learning [6, 7].

### 4.4 Neural Realizations of Higher-Order Tests: $k$-GNNs, IGNs, and PPGNs

Each rung of the hierarchy admits a neural counterpart.

**$k$-GNNs (Morris et al. [2]).** The $k$-dimensional GNN performs message passing over $k$-sets of vertices, with a local neighborhood structure restricting communication to overlapping sets. Morris et al. prove that 1-GNNs coincide exactly with 1-WL in distinguishing power, and that $k$-GNNs realize the set-based variant of $k$-WL, at the cost of $O(n^k)$ memory.

**Invariant graph networks (Maron et al. [3]).** The $k$-order invariant graph network represents the graph as a tensor in $\mathbb{R}^{n^k \times a}$ and stacks maximally expressive *linear equivariant* layers $L(g \cdot X) = g \cdot L(X)$ with pointwise nonlinearities, finishing with an invariant readout. Maron et al. prove that $k$-order networks match the distinguishing power of $k$-WL for $k > 2$.

**PPGN: 3-WL power at quadratic cost (Maron et al. [3]).** The most practically significant result: a reduced 2-order network built from scaled identity operators interleaved with a single quadratic operation — *matrix multiplication* — achieves provable **3-WL expressive power** while operating only on $n \times n$ matrices. This is the first practical invariant architecture strictly stronger than message passing [3].

The correspondence can be summarized:

| WL level | Neural realization | Memory | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-WL | MPNN / GIN | $O(n + m)$ | [1], [2] |
| 2-WL (set-based) | $k$-GNN ($k=2$) | $O(n^2)$ | [2] |
| 2-WL (folklore) | $k$-IGN | $O(n^k)$ dense | [3] |
| 3-WL | PPGN | $O(n^2)$ | [3] |
| $k$-WL | $k$-IGN | $O(n^k)$ | [3] |

**Sparse variants.** The dense $O(n^k)$ bottleneck motivated *sparse* higher-order networks: Morris et al. [6] introduce a local, subgraph-based $k$-WL whose neural realization scales with the number of *connected* $k$-subgraphs rather than $n^k$, making higher-order expressiveness practical on sparse real-world graphs.

```python
# Sketch: 2-WL refinement over ordered pairs (folklore variant)
def wl2_refine(colors, adj, n):
    new_colors, table = {}, {}
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            sig = (colors[(i, j)],
                   tuple(sorted(colors[(w, j)] for w in range(n))),
                   tuple(sorted(colors[(i, w)] for w in range(n))))
            new_colors[(i, j)] = table.setdefault(sig, len(table))
    return new_colors
```

### 4.5 Separation versus Approximation: The Chen et al. Equivalence

A subtle question: does *distinguishing* non-isomorphic graphs (separation) capture the full notion of expressive power, or is *approximating* arbitrary invariant functions strictly harder? Chen et al. [5] settle this:

> **Theorem (Chen, Villar, Chen & Bruna, 2019):** For permutation-invariant model classes on a compact domain of graphs, universal approximation of continuous invariant functions holds if and only if the class separates all pairs of non-isomorphic graphs.

This equivalence licenses the entire WL-based research program: studying which graph pairs a model can *tell apart* is *equivalent* to studying which functions it can *learn*. The sobering corollary: since no fixed-$k$ WL test separates all graphs, no fixed-order GNN architecture universally approximates invariant graph functions; universality demands $k$ growing with $n$ [3, 5].

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

Although this thesis is theoretical, the WL–GNN correspondence makes falsifiable predictions confirmed across the cited works.

1. **GIN saturates 1-WL on benchmarks.** Xu et al. [1] show GIN outperforming GCN and GraphSAGE on 9 graph classification datasets, with the gains concentrated where structural (rather than feature) information dominates — exactly as the theory predicts, since GIN's advantage over mean/max aggregation is purely combinatorial [1, 8].

2. **Higher-order models help where 1-WL fails.** Morris et al. [2] demonstrate that $k$-GNNs ($k = 2, 3$) improve over 1-GNNs on molecular property prediction (QM9), with the largest gains on graphs containing the substructures (triangles, higher cliques) that 1-WL cannot count. Maron et al. [3] report state-of-the-art regression error on QM9 with PPGN, attributing the gain to 3-WL expressiveness.

3. **The WL kernel remains competitive.** Shervashidze et al. [4] established that the $O(hm)$ WL subtree kernel matches or exceeds then-state-of-the-art kernels on bioinformatics benchmarks; GIN inherits this inductive bias while adding adaptivity — the fixed feature construction of the kernel is precisely what end-to-end learning relaxes [2].

4. **Scalability of sparse higher-order methods.** Morris et al. [6] show that sparse $k$-WL variants retain the distinguishing power of their dense counterparts on molecular graphs while reducing runtime by orders of magnitude.

The theoretical guarantees are unconditional (they hold for *some* parameter setting), while the empirical results confirm that gradient-based training can *find* parameters realizing a substantial fraction of this power — a gap between existence and learnability that remains incompletely understood (see Section 6).

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**The expressiveness–scalability trade-off.** The hierarchy is strict, but each rung costs a polynomial degree: $O(n^k)$ memory for $k$-GNNs/IGNs. PPGN's 3-WL-at-$O(n^2)$ result [3] is the sweet spot today, but no $O(n + m)$ architecture is known to exceed 1-WL on general graphs. Sparse variants [6] mitigate this on sparse real graphs, yet worst-case bounds remain exponential in $k$.

**Learnability vs. capacity.** All upper bounds are statements about model *classes*, not trained models. Whether SGD reliably discovers the injective aggregations whose existence the theorems guarantee — particularly with finite data and finite width — is largely open. Over-smoothing and over-squashing further degrade deep message passing in practice.

**Continuous and attributed graphs.** The GIN theory assumes countable feature spaces [1]; with continuous node attributes, a single aggregator provably cannot attain even 1-WL power, and multi-aggregator designs become necessary. Extending the hierarchy theorems to attributed, directed, and temporal graphs is active work.

**Beyond WL: substructure-aware architectures.** An emerging alternative sidesteps the hierarchy by *injecting* substructure information — subgraph GNNs, motif-counting features, and structural encodings (Laplacian eigenvectors, random-walk features) — achieving practical expressiveness beyond 1-WL without paying the full $k$-WL price.

**Universality and logic.** The correspondence between $k$-WL and the logic $\mathcal{C}^{k+1}$ [6, 7] suggests a descriptive-complexity characterization of exactly which graph *properties* each architecture can express. A complete logical characterization of trained GNNs, as opposed to idealized model classes, remains elusive.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The Weisfeiler–Leman hierarchy provides the sharpest known lens on the expressive power of graph neural networks. Its message is twofold. First, the *negative* result: standard message passing is bounded by 1-WL color refinement [1, 2], so every GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT inherits the blind spots of a 1968 isomorphism heuristic — unable to distinguish $C_6$ from $2K_3$, blind to regularity, and incapable of counting the substructures that chemistry demands. Second, the *positive* program: the hierarchy does not stop at $k = 1$. Its higher rungs are strict, logically meaningful, and *neuralizable*: $k$-GNNs [2], invariant graph networks, and the quadratic-memory PPGN [3] convert combinatorial power into learnable architectures, while sparse variants [6] and the separation–approximation equivalence [5] keep the theory honest about scalability and learning.

The practical takeaway for architecture design is precise: choose sum-based injective aggregation to saturate 1-WL [1, 8]; move to higher-order or substructure-aware designs when the task requires distinguishing power beyond color refinement; and budget $O(n^k)$ resources with eyes open. A fifty-year-old combinatorial idea turned out to be exactly the right language for a modern learning paradigm.
