This thesis unifies the two dominant near-term paradigms for quantum combinatorial optimization: quantum annealing (QA) on D-Wave processors and the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) on gate-based hardware. We develop the transverse-field Ising dynamics and adiabatic theorem underlying QA, analyze minor-embedding onto the Pegasus topology including chain-break physics and 4-clique embeddings, and present QAOA together with its alternating-operator generalization. A central result is the dual role of structure in variational landscapes: parameter concentration makes QAOA angles transferable across instances, while barren plateaus threaten trainability at scale. We prove concentration via lightcone locality, diagnose gradient-variance scaling numerically, and survey mitigation strategies from layerwise initialization to warm-starting. Empirical benchmarks span statevector QAOA simulations, Advantage-class embedding overheads, reverse-annealing refinements, and the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick infinite-size analysis where depth-11 QAOA surpasses semidefinite programming. We close with honest limitations: embedding overhead, noise-induced plateaus, topological barriers, and the open question of practical quantum advantage.

# Neural Ordinary Differential Equations and Physics-Informed Neural Networks for Scientific Computing: Adjoint Sensitivity, Neural SDEs, and Adaptive Solver Interplay

## 1. Introduction

Scientific computing has long been dominated by the twin pillars of numerical analysis and partial differential equation (PDE) theory: finite differences, finite elements, spectral methods, and adaptive Runge–Kutta integrators. Over the last decade, deep learning has emerged as a third paradigm, not as a replacement for classical methods but as a complementary language in which unknown constitutive laws, sub-grid closures, and even the solution operators of PDEs can be learned from data. Two landmark frameworks anchor this synthesis. The first is the **neural ordinary differential equation (neural ODE)** of Chen et al. [1], which replaces discrete layer stacking with a continuous-time vector field parameterized by a neural network and computed with black-box ODE solvers. The second is the **physics-informed neural network (PINN)** of Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis [2], which embeds the residual of a PDE directly into the training loss via automatic differentiation, yielding mesh-free, differentiable surrogate solvers.

What unifies these frameworks is a shared reliance on **differential structure as an inductive bias**. In neural ODEs, depth becomes a continuous variable and gradient computation is outsourced to the *adjoint sensitivity method*, which solves a second ODE backward in time with *constant memory cost* regardless of the number of solver steps [1]. In PINNs, the PDE operator itself becomes part of the loss landscape, so that physical conservation laws regularize an otherwise purely data-driven regression. Yet both frameworks expose a deep tension: the **numerical solver is no longer a neutral subroutine** but an active participant in the learning dynamics. The choice of adaptive integrator, error tolerance, and stiffness-handling strategy materially changes convergence, memory footprint, and even the function class the model can represent.

This thesis develops a unified treatment of these ideas. We derive the adjoint sensitivity method from the Pontryagin maximum principle, analyze its interplay with adaptive solvers such as Dormand–Prince, extend the discussion to **neural stochastic differential equations (neural SDEs)** trained as Wasserstein GANs [3], and survey the efficient gradient machinery of reversible solvers and Brownian intervals [4]. We connect these to continuous normalizing flows and the FFJORD estimator [5], and to the torchdiffeq software stack that industrialized the field [6]. Throughout, we ask a central question: *how does the numerical discretization of time interact with the statistical discretization of learning?*

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From Residual Networks to Continuous Depth

A residual network defines a sequence of hidden states

$$h_{t+1} = h_t + f(h_t, \theta_t)$$

which is recognizably an explicit Euler discretization with unit step size of the ODE $\dot{h} = f(h, t, \theta)$. Taking the limit as the number of layers grows and the step size shrinks, one arrives at the neural ODE hypothesis: hidden-state dynamics should be parameterized in continuous time [1]. This move buys three properties that discrete networks lack. First, **adaptive computation**: an error-controlled solver automatically allocates more function evaluations (NFEs) to difficult regions of the trajectory. Second, **constant-memory backpropagation** through the adjoint method. Third, **continuous-time modeling** of irregularly sampled time series, where observations arrive at arbitrary timestamps that need not align with any fixed layer grid.

### 2.2 Optimal Control and the Adjoint Method

The adjoint sensitivity method predates deep learning by decades and is the workhorse of inverse problems in geophysics, weather forecasting (four-dimensional variational data assimilation), and aerospace trajectory optimization. Its origin lies in Pontryagin's maximum principle: to compute the gradient of a scalar loss with respect to parameters of a dynamical system, one introduces a *costate* (adjoint) variable $\lambda(t)$ that propagates sensitivity information backward in time. The celebrated property is that $\lambda(t)$ satisfies its own linear ODE, so gradients require only the solution of an additional initial-value problem, never storage of the forward trajectory.

### 2.3 Physics-Informed Learning

Classical PDE solvers discretize space (meshes, spectral bases) and advance in time. PINNs invert this philosophy: a neural network $u(t, x; \theta)$ approximates the *solution itself*, and its partial derivatives — obtained exactly by automatic differentiation — are substituted into the PDE operator $\mathcal{N}$ to form a residual $f(t,x;\theta) = \partial_t u + \mathcal{N}[u]$. Training minimizes the residual at a set of collocation points alongside boundary, initial, and observational data terms [2]. The result is a mesh-free, fully differentiable surrogate that unifies the forward problem (solve the PDE) and the inverse problem (identify unknown coefficients from data).

### 2.4 Stochastic Dynamics and Generative Modeling

Deterministic ODEs cannot model intrinsic noise. Neural SDEs parameterize drift $\mu_\theta$ and diffusion $\sigma_\phi$ networks in

$$dX_t = \mu_\theta(t, X_t)\,dt + \sigma_\phi(t, X_t) \circ dW_t,$$

and learn them from path-valued data. Kidger et al. showed that classical SDE calibration is a special case of Wasserstein GAN training, with Brownian motion as latent noise and a neural controlled differential equation (CDE) as discriminator, yielding neural SDEs as *infinite-dimensional GANs* [3]. Meanwhile, continuous normalizing flows [1, 5] use ODE-defined transformations to make the change-of-variables determinant collapse to a cheap Jacobian trace, enabling scalable exact-likelihood generative models.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Neural ODE Formulation

Let the hidden state evolve according to

$$\frac{dh(t)}{dt} = f(h(t), t, \theta), \qquad h(t_0) = h_0,$$

where $f$ is a neural network. The output $h(t_1)$ is produced by any black-box ODE solver:

$$h(t_1) = h(t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t_1} f(h(t), t, \theta)\,dt.$$

The torchdiffeq library [6] implements this as a PyTorch `autograd.Function` (`odeint`, `odeint_adjoint`) supporting solvers such as fixed-step Euler/midpoint/RK4 and adaptive Dormand–Prince (`dopri5`), Bogacki–Shampine, and adaptive Heun. A typical model wraps the integral between input and output projections; time $t$ is often concatenated to the state so that $f$ can be non-autonomous.

### 3.2 The Adjoint Sensitivity Method

Consider a scalar loss $L$ depending on $h(t_1)$ and possibly on the trajectory. Define the adjoint $a(t) = \partial L / \partial h(t)$. The gradient with respect to $\theta$ is obtained by solving the *augmented ODE* backward in time [1]:

> **Theorem (Adjoint sensitivity):** Let $a(t)$ satisfy
>
> $$\frac{da(t)}{dt} = -a(t)^\top \frac{\partial f}{\partial h}(h(t), t, \theta), \qquad a(t_1) = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h(t_1)}.$$
>
> Then
>
> $$\frac{dL}{d\theta} = -\int_{t_1}^{t_0} a(t)^\top \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta}(h(t), t, \theta)\,dt,$$
>
> computed by integrating the augmented system $(h, a, dL/d\theta)$ jointly backward from $t_1$ to $t_0$.

Memory cost is *independent of the number of solver steps*, in contrast to backpropagating through the unrolled integrator, which stores every intermediate state. This is the method's central economic argument and the reason neural ODEs could be trained at all on high-dimensional problems.

### 3.3 PINN Formulation

In the continuous-time PINN [2], the solution network $u(t,x;\theta)$ induces the physics residual

$$f(t,x;\theta) = \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \mathcal{N}[u](t,x;\theta),$$

where $\mathcal{N}$ is a possibly nonlinear spatial differential operator. All derivatives are exact automatic-differentiation gradients of the network output with respect to its inputs. Training minimizes the composite loss

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \lambda_d \mathcal{L}_{\text{data}} + \lambda_f \mathcal{L}_{\text{PDE}} + \lambda_b \mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}} + \lambda_i \mathcal{L}_{\text{IC}},$$

with collocation points sampled in the domain interior, on boundaries, and at the initial time. For inverse problems, unknown PDE coefficients are appended to $\theta$ and identified jointly. A discrete-time variant uses implicit Runge–Kutta stages with a large number of stages to take very large time steps while remaining A-stable.

### 3.4 Neural SDE Formulation

A neural SDE is defined by drift and diffusion networks $\mu_\theta, \sigma_\phi$ in Itô or Stratonovich form. Training as a GAN [3] optimizes the Wasserstein objective

$$\min_\theta \max_\phi \left( \mathbb{E}[F_\phi(Y)] - \mathbb{E}[F_\phi(Y_{\text{true}})] \right),$$

where $Y$ are generated paths, $Y_{\text{true}}$ are data paths, and the discriminator $F_\phi$ is a neural CDE:

$$F_\phi(Y) = m_\phi \cdot H_T, \quad H_0 = \xi_\phi(Y_0), \quad dH_t = f_\phi(t,H_t)\,dt + g_\phi(t,H_t) \circ dY_t.$$

Gradients through the SDE solve use a *stochastic adjoint* — a backward-in-time SDE — but naive implementations suffer severe speed and accuracy problems. Kidger et al. [4] resolve these with the **reversible Heun method** (algebraically reversible, halving NFEs), the **Brownian interval** (exact, memory-efficient Brownian reconstruction giving up to $10.6\times$ speedups), and careful discriminator regularization replacing gradient penalties.

### 3.5 Continuous Normalizing Flows and FFJORD

If $z(t)$ follows $\dot{z} = f(z, t)$ with $z(t_0) \sim p_{z_0}$, the instantaneous change-of-variables theorem [1] gives

$$\frac{d \log p(z(t))}{dt} = -\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right),$$

so the log-likelihood requires only the Jacobian *trace* — $O(D)$ with Hutchinson's stochastic estimator — rather than the $O(D^3)$ determinant of discrete flows. FFJORD [5] exploits this to train free-form architectures (no partitioning or rank-one constraints) as scalable reversible generative models, achieving state-of-the-art exact-likelihood density estimation on tabular and image data.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Adjoint Dynamics as Optimal Control

The adjoint equation is not a deep-learning invention: it is the continuous-time limit of reverse-mode automatic differentiation applied to an ODE discretization, and equivalently the costate equation of Pontryagin's principle for the optimal-control problem $\min_\theta L(h(t_1))$ subject to $\dot{h} = f(h,t,\theta)$. This viewpoint clarifies *when the adjoint is exact*. The adjoint ODE is derived under the assumption that the forward and backward solves use the same continuous dynamics; in practice both are numerical approximations, and the mismatch introduces gradient error that grows when the solver tolerance is loose or the dynamics are stiff [1, 4]. Crucially, the augmented backward solve needs only $(h, a, dL/d\theta)$ at the current time — never the full forward trajectory — which is the source of the $O(1)$ memory guarantee.

### 4.2 The Adaptive Solver as a Learned-Depth Controller

Adaptive solvers such as Dormand–Prince (`dopri5`) choose step sizes to keep the local error below tolerances `rtol`/`atol`, effectively making the number of function evaluations (NFE) a *data- and parameter-dependent quantity*. Chen et al. [1] observed that NFE grows during training as the learned vector field becomes more complex — the solver "notices" that the dynamics have stiffened. This creates a feedback loop with three practical consequences:

1. **Compute–accuracy coupling.** Tightening tolerances improves gradient fidelity but increases wall-clock cost super-linearly; loosening them can destabilize training through adjoint mismatch.
2. **Stiffness blowup.** Learned vector fields frequently become stiff, forcing explicit adaptive solvers into tiny steps. Implicit solvers are expensive per step; a common remedy is regularizing the Jacobian norm or kinetic energy of the flow.
3. **NFE as diagnostic.** Forward and backward NFE counts serve as a proxy for model complexity — a cheap, solver-native analogue of effective depth in discrete networks.

The table below summarizes the solver options in torchdiffeq [6] and their roles.

| Solver | Type | Evals/step | Use case |
|---|---|---|---|
| `euler` | Fixed-step | 1 | Fast prototyping, rough dynamics |
| `midpoint` | Fixed-step | 2 | Better accuracy per step |
| `rk4` | Fixed-step | 4 | Smooth non-stiff problems |
| `dopri5` | Adaptive | 6 | Default; error-controlled |
| `dopri8` | Adaptive | 13 | High-precision solves |
| `bosh3` | Adaptive | 4 | Cheap adaptive alternative |
| `adams` | Multistep adaptive | 1–2 | Smooth, cheap vector fields |

### 4.3 FFJORD: Unbiased Density Estimation in Continuous Time

FFJORD [5] marries the instantaneous change-of-variables formula with Hutchinson's trace estimator,

$$\operatorname{tr}(A) = \mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}[\epsilon^\top A \epsilon], \qquad \epsilon \sim \text{Rademacher},$$

so that each log-density evaluation costs a single vector–Jacobian product. The estimator is unbiased, and the resulting model is a continuous-time generative model with one-pass sampling (integrate forward) and exact likelihood (integrate the augmented trace ODE). This removed the architectural straitjacket of discrete flows — coupling layers, dimension partitioning, rank-one convolutions — and transferred the burden of tractability onto the *solver*, which must now integrate a $(D+1)$-dimensional augmented system. On MNIST and CIFAR-10, multiscale FFJORD achieved the best exact-likelihood bits-per-dim among models with efficient sampling, demonstrating that solver-based likelihoods can scale to tens of thousands of dimensions.

### 4.4 Neural SDEs: From Deterministic Flows to Path-Space GANs

Extending neural ODEs to Itô SDEs introduces two new difficulties. First, the Brownian path $W_t$ must be sampled *and reconstructed* on the backward pass; storing it costs memory linear in the number of steps, while resampling it breaks gradient correctness. The Brownian interval [4] solves this with an exact, tree-structured reconstruction giving logarithmic memory. Second, discretize-then-optimize SDE solvers are not algebraically reversible, so the backward solve accumulates truncation error; the reversible Heun method eliminates it by construction. With these innovations, SDE-GANs [3] match classical calibration theory — arbitrary drift and diffusion are identifiable in the infinite-data limit because the Wasserstein loss has a unique global minimizer — while remaining fully differentiable, memory-efficient generative models of time series.

### 4.5 PINNs Meet Neural ODEs: Two Views of Time

PINNs and neural ODEs treat time in complementary ways. A neural ODE *integrates* a learned vector field: time is the integration variable and the physics (if any) lives in the parameterization of $f$. A continuous-time PINN *regresses* a solution function $u(t,x)$: time is an input coordinate and the physics lives in the residual loss. This yields a natural hybrid strategy for scientific computing:

- Use a **neural ODE / neural SDE** as a *latent dynamical prior* for partially observed systems (e.g., inferring unobserved state trajectories from sparse sensor data, as in the latent-ODE time-series models of [1]).
- Use a **PINN** as a *differentiable surrogate* for the known PDE operator, providing cheap forward solves and adjoints for outer-loop optimization (design, control, uncertainty quantification).
- Couple them: parameterize unknown PDE coefficients or closure terms with neural ODEs and train end-to-end through the PINN residual — physics provides the equations, the ODE provides the unknown constitutive law.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Reported Empirical Results

Chen et al. [1] trained neural ODEs on MNIST and found test error comparable to a residual network with the *same number of parameters*, but with constant memory and adaptive NFE — the ODE-Net matched the ResNet baseline while the solver allocated more evaluations to harder digits. Their continuous normalizing flow matched discrete-flow density-estimation benchmarks at a fraction of the Jacobian cost, and their latent-ODE model substantially outperformed RNN baselines on irregularly sampled time-series interpolation and extrapolation.

Raissi et al. [2] demonstrated PINNs on the 1D viscous Burgers equation with a shock-forming solution, the Schrödinger equation, and the Allen–Cahn equation, achieving relative $L_2$ errors on the order of $10^{-3}$–$10^{-4}$ with only a few hundred data points supplemented by tens of thousands of collocation points — a data efficiency unattainable for pure regression. Their discrete-time PINNs took single time steps orders of magnitude larger than explicit schemes permit, by using hundreds of implicit Runge–Kutta stages.

Kidger et al. [3] trained SDE-GANs on stocks, air-quality, and ICU time series, evaluating with classification, prediction, and maximum-mean-discrepancy (MMD) metrics via path signatures; the neural SDE generator consistently matched or beat latent-ODE and classical baselines. The follow-up [4] showed the reversible Heun plus Brownian interval stack delivering up to $1.98\times$ and $10.6\times$ speedups respectively, with strictly smaller gradient error.

### 5.2 Proof Sketches

> **Theorem (Instantaneous change of variables [1]):** If $z(t)$ satisfies $\dot{z} = f(z,t)$ and $p(z,t)$ is its density, then $\frac{\partial \log p(z(t),t)}{\partial t} = -\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right)$.

*Sketch.* Consider an infinitesimal volume element transported by the flow. Its log-volume changes at the rate given by the divergence of the vector field — the trace of the Jacobian — by Liouville's theorem. Integrating from $t_0$ to $t_1$ and applying the change-of-variables formula at the endpoints yields the exact log-density of the pushforward distribution. The neural ODE flow is a diffeomorphism provided $f$ is Lipschitz, so the argument applies directly. ∎

> **Theorem (Stochastic adjoint [4]):** Gradients of an expected loss over neural SDE paths can be computed by a backward Stratonovich SDE whose drift involves vector–Jacobian products of the forward drift and diffusion, requiring only the reconstructed Brownian motion, not the stored forward trajectory.

*Sketch.* Apply the Itô formula to the composition of the loss with the stochastic flow and use the duality between forward and backward SDEs (the nonlinear Feynman–Kac correspondence). Algebraic reversibility of the solver guarantees the discrete backward SDE inverts the discrete forward SDE exactly, removing the truncation error that plagues discretize-then-optimize approaches. ∎

A minimal torchdiffeq [6] implementation illustrates the full pipeline:

```python
import torch
from torchdiffeq import odeint_adjoint as odeint

class ODEFunc(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = torch.nn.Sequential(
            torch.nn.Linear(dim + 1, 64), torch.nn.Tanh(),
            torch.nn.Linear(64, 64), torch.nn.Tanh(),
            torch.nn.Linear(64, dim),
        )
    def forward(self, t, h):
        # concatenate time so the vector field may be non-autonomous
        return self.net(torch.cat([h, t.expand(h.shape[0], 1)], dim=1))

func = ODEFunc(dim=16)
h0 = torch.randn(128, 16, requires_grad=True)
t = torch.tensor([0.0, 1.0])
ht = odeint(func, h0, t, method="dopri5", rtol=1e-5, atol=1e-7)[-1]
loss = ht.pow(2).mean()
loss.backward()  # gradients via the adjoint ODE; O(1) memory
```

## 6. Limitations

**Topological constraints.** A neural ODE defines a homeomorphism of the state space: trajectories cannot cross. Dupont and co-workers showed this prevents representing certain functions (e.g., the reflection map $x \mapsto -x$ in one dimension), motivating *augmented* neural ODEs that lift the state into a higher-dimensional space. The same concern applies to continuous normalizing flows, which cannot change the topology of the base distribution's support.

**Stiffness and solver cost.** Learned vector fields routinely become stiff during training, and explicit adaptive solvers respond with exploding NFE counts. Stiff neural ODEs may require implicit solvers whose per-step linear solves are costly, or Jacobian regularization that biases the learned dynamics. In the worst case, the "adaptive computation" advantage inverts into a compute trap.

**Adjoint gradient fidelity.** The adjoint method's $O(1)$ memory comes at the price of recomputing the forward trajectory backward in time. Numerical error in this reconstruction — especially with loose tolerances or chaotic dynamics — produces gradient estimates that can disagree with true gradients, occasionally destabilizing optimization. Checkpointed or interpolated adjoints trade memory back for accuracy.

**PINN training pathologies.** PINNs suffer from spectral bias (difficulty learning high-frequency solutions), gradient imbalance between the PDE residual and boundary/data terms, and the need for careful loss weighting and collocation sampling. For high-dimensional or strongly nonlinear PDEs, classical solvers remain far more reliable; PINNs excel where data are sparse, the domain is complex, or inverse problems are the goal.

**Neural SDE complexity.** SDE-GAN training inherits GAN instability (the discriminator is now a neural CDE over path space), and the stochastic adjoint machinery — Brownian intervals, reversible solvers — is substantially more intricate than its deterministic counterpart. Likelihood-based neural SDE training avoids adversarial dynamics but requires Girsanov-based estimators with their own variance challenges.

## 7. Conclusion

Neural ODEs, PINNs, and neural SDEs are not three separate inventions but three facets of a single idea: *differentiable dynamics as a modeling primitive*. The adjoint sensitivity method turned ODE solvers into trainable layers with constant memory [1, 6]; continuous normalizing flows and FFJORD turned the Jacobian trace into a scalable likelihood [5]; PINNs turned PDE residuals into loss functions [2]; and neural SDEs, with reversible solvers and Brownian intervals, extended the entire apparatus to path space [3, 4].

The deepest lesson of this literature is that the solver is part of the model. Adaptive integrators do not merely compute trajectories — they allocate computation, regularize dynamics, and mediate the fidelity of gradients. Future progress in scientific machine learning will likely come not from larger vector-field networks but from tighter co-design of integrators and optimizers: structure-preserving solvers, stiffness-aware architectures, and hybrid schemes in which classical numerical analysis and deep learning each do what they do best. The continuous-depth revolution began by observing that a residual network is a discretized ODE; its mature form will be a numerical analysis that is itself differentiable.
