## Abstract

View synchronization — the mechanism by which replicas agree on the current leader and round — is the least celebrated yet most failure-prone component of Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus. While safety arguments rest on quorum intersection, liveness in the partially synchronous model reduces to a single question: after the global stabilization time, can correct replicas converge on the same view long enough for one correct leader to drive a decision? This article dissects the evolution of that mechanism from PBFT's quadratic view-change protocol, through HotStuff's linear, responsive pacemaker and its pipelined three-chain commit rule, to DAG-based designs — Narwhal, Tusk, and Bullshark — that eliminate explicit views entirely by interpreting causal histories as votes. We compare communication complexity, commit latency under synchrony and attack, and the timeout engineering that Tendermint's liveness bug showed to be non-trivial, and we close with proof sketches, measured throughput figures, and the open problems of fairness under garbage collection.


##1 Intro

Consensus in the presence of Byzantine faults is conventionally narrated as a story about *safety*: no two correct replicas decide differently, no committed value is ever rolled back. The literature is accordingly dense with quorum systems, certificates, and commit rules. Yet the history of deployed Byzantine systems tells a different story. PBFT's view-change protocol, the most intricate part of the original design, was also its most bug-prone; Tendermint's original specification contained a genuine liveness violation that survived review until formal scrutiny exposed it; and the modern DAG-based protocols are celebrated less for novel safety arguments than for the radical way they *dissolve the view abstraction altogether*. The thesis of this article is that **liveness in Byzantine consensus is fundamentally a view-synchronization problem**, and that the last three decades of protocol design can be read as a progressive refinement — and eventual elimination — of the view-change primitive.

To make this precise, recall the standard setting: a set of *n* replicas, at most *f* of which may be Byzantine, with the classical bound *n >= 3f + 1* for agreement under partial synchrony. Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson proved that no deterministic protocol guarantees consensus in a fully asynchronous system [3], so practical protocols assume *partial synchrony*: there exists an unknown global stabilization time (GST) after which message delays are bounded by an unknown Δ [4]. Safety must hold even before GST; liveness is only required after it. This asymmetry is the source of nearly all complexity. Before GST, the adversary controls the network and can stall, reorder, and duplicate messages arbitrarily. After GST, correct replicas must somehow reconverge — elect a leader, agree on which round they are in, and then execute the steady-state protocol fast enough to commit before the next failure.

That reconvergence is *view synchronization*: in leader-based protocols, progress requires all correct replicas to overlap in the same view long enough for a correct leader to collect a quorum. The pacemaker abstraction [2] separates this concern from the consensus core, guaranteeing *synchronization periods* after GST in which correct replicas share a view with a correct leader.


##2 Background

### The Byzantine agreement problem and its bounds

The Byzantine generals formulation asks *n* processes to agree on a value despite up to *f* behaving arbitrarily — sending conflicting messages, colluding, or remaining silent [4]. In the partially synchronous model, agreement with *n = 3f + 1* is achievable and the bound is tight for the standard validity conditions. The quorum argument is elementary: any two quorums of *2f + 1* intersect in at least one correct replica, so a value *locked* by one quorum cannot be contradicted by another.

The FLP impossibility result [3] forces the entire field into the partial-synchrony compromise: deterministic safety *and* liveness cannot both hold in asynchrony, so protocols guarantee safety always and liveness only after GST. This is not a theoretical nicety — it is the load-bearing wall of every production deployment. Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer formalized partial synchrony and showed consensus solvable there [4], but their protocols were not practical; PBFT was the first to make Byzantine agreement performant enough for real services [1].

### PBFT: three phases and the view-change bottleneck

PBFT's normal case is a three-phase dance. A client sends a request to the *primary*; the primary multicasts a `PRE-PREPARE`; backups that accept it multicast `PREPARE`; upon collecting *2f + 1* matching prepares (the *prepared* certificate), each replica multicasts `COMMIT`; upon *2f + 1* commits, the request executes and the client accepts *f + 1* matching replies [1]. The protocol is *responsive* in the normal case, and the authors demonstrated a Byzantine-tolerant NFS service only 3% slower than its unreplicated counterpart.

The view-change sub-protocol is where the trouble lives. If backups suspect the primary (via timeouts), each multicasts a `VIEW-CHANGE` message containing its prepared certificates to the next primary, which collects *2f + 1* of them and multicasts a `NEW-VIEW`. This is an all-to-all exchange: *O(n^2)* messages in the worst case, and worse, it is *non-responsive* — replicas wait for the maximum delay bound rather than the actual delay. Every subsequent protocol in this article can be read as an attempt to tame, linearize, or abolish it.

### Responsiveness, linearity, and the pacemaker idea

Two properties became the yardsticks of the modern era [2]:

- **Optimistic responsiveness**: after GST, a correct leader drives consensus at the speed of the actual network delay δ, not the worst-case bound Δ.
- **Linearity**: communication complexity linear in *n* per decision, including during leader replacement.

HotStuff was the first partially synchronous protocol to achieve both simultaneously [2]. Its key organizational insight was the *pacemaker*: a separate module responsible for view synchronization, so the consensus core (propose–vote–commit) never reasons about timeouts directly. The pacemaker guarantees that after GST, correct replicas enter views roughly together and stay long enough for progress; the consensus layer only assumes that *eventually* such a synchronized view with a correct leader occurs. ---


##3 Methodology

This article is a comparative-analytic study, not an implementation report. Our method has three strands:

1. **Formal reconstruction.** We restate each protocol's view-synchronization mechanism in a common vocabulary — views, leaders, timeouts, quorum certificates — following the unifying framework of the HotStuff paper [2]. 2. **Complexity and latency accounting.** For each design we tabulate per-view communication complexity, worst-case view-synchronization cost, and commit latency measured in message delays under (a) synchrony with a correct leader, (b) synchrony with a faulty leader, and (c) asynchrony, where applicable.
3. **Empirical grounding.** Where published measurements exist — PBFT's NFS evaluation [1], HotStuff's 100-replica deployment [2], Narwhal-HotStuff and Tusk WAN benchmarks [5], Bullshark's throughput figures [6] — we report them and note the experimental conditions, distinguishing measured fact from asymptotic claim.

> **Theorem (View-synchronization reduction, informal):** In the partially synchronous model with *n = 3f + 1*, any leader-based BFT protocol whose steady state commits within a bounded number of message delays of a correct leader's proposal achieves liveness if and only if its view-synchronization sub-protocol guarantees infinitely many post-GST views in which all correct replicas overlap for a sufficient duration with a correct leader.

The "only if" direction is the FLP-flavored observation that without reconvergence, an asynchronous adversary stalls the protocol forever; the "if" direction is the standard GST argument. Everything that follows is commentary on how hard the "if" is to engineer.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The pacemaker: separating synchronization from agreement

The pacemaker abstraction deserves first-class treatment because it reorganized how the field thinks. Before it, each protocol embedded its own timeout logic into the consensus state machine — PBFT's view-change messages carry protocol state (prepared certificates) and are inseparable from the agreement logic [1]. The pacemaker instead exposes a minimal interface:

```python
class Pacemaker:
    def __init__(self, replica_id, n, f):
        self.view = 0
        self.timeout = T0          # initial timeout, e.g. 2 * estimated_delta
        self.n, self.f = n, f

    def on_timeout(self):
        # broadcast signed TIMEOUT(view) to all replicas
        broadcast(("TIMEOUT", self.view, sign(self.view)))
        self.view += 1
        self.timeout *= 2          # exponential backoff per view

    def on_quorum_timeout(self, view):
        # 2f+1 TIMEOUT(view) messages => advance together
        self.view = view + 1
        self.timeout = T0
        enter_view(self.view, leader(self.view))
```

The doubling timeout is the classical DLS-style mechanism: since Δ is unknown, replicas escalate until the timeout exceeds the true bound, after which all correct replicas' timeouts overlap and views advance in lockstep [4]. The devil is in the bookkeeping — *when* to reset the timer and *which* messages count toward the *2f + 1* threshold. Tendermint's original gossip-based voting interacted pathologically with its timeout and locking rules, producing executions in which correct validators never converged: a liveness violation found only through formal analysis, and proof that timeout engineering is protocol design, not plumbing [2][4].

A TLA+ sketch of the core safety invariant that every pacemaker must preserve illustrates why mechanized verification has become standard for these components:

```tla+
---- MODULE ViewSync ----

(* Any two correct replicas in the same view agree on the leader *)

    \A r1, r2 \in CorrectReplicas :
        view[r1] = view[r2] => Leader(view[r1]) = Leader(view[r2])

(* After GST, timeouts eventually exceed the true delay bound *)

====
```

### 4.2 HotStuff: linear view change and the three-chain rule

HotStuff's breakthrough was achieving linearity *and* responsiveness together, solving what its authors describe as a decades-long open problem [2]. The protocol replaces PBFT's all-to-all phases with a star topology: the leader collects votes and aggregates them into a *quorum certificate* (QC) using threshold signatures, so each phase costs *O(n)* messages. View change becomes almost trivial — the new leader simply collects *2f + 1* `NEW-VIEW` messages, each carrying the sender's highest QC, and proposes on top of the highest one. No prepared-certificate log transfer, no quadratic blowup.

The safety argument rests on the *three-chain commit rule*: a block *B* proposed in view *v* commits when three QCs form a chain *B ← QC(v) ← QC(v+1) ← QC(v+2)*, i.e., two consecutive views extend *B* and one further view extends that. The middle QC acts as a *lock*: any future leader's proposal must extend the highest QC, and quorum intersection guarantees that at least one correct replica holding the lock will force the new leader to respect it.

> **Theorem (HotStuff safety, three-chain):** If a correct replica commits block *B* in view *v* via the three-chain rule, then every valid QC formed in any view greater than *v* extends a chain containing *B*. Hence no conflicting block can ever commit [2].

*Proof sketch.* Committing requires QCs in views *v*, *v+1*, *v+2*. The *2f + 1* replicas voting for the QC in *v+1* include at least *f + 1* correct ones, each of which sets its `lockedQC` to the QC of *v* and thereafter votes only for proposals extending it. Any QC in a higher view needs *2f + 1* votes, hence at least one vote from those locked correct replicas, so it extends the locked chain containing *B*. ∎

### 4.3 From view changes to viewless progress: Narwhal, Tusk, and zero-message ordering

The DAG-based protocols attack the problem from the opposite direction: instead of making view changes cheaper, they *remove the leader from the critical path of data dissemination*. Narwhal is a mempool protocol, not a consensus protocol: every validator reliably broadcasts batches of transactions, each batch referencing hashes of previously seen batches, forming a growing directed acyclic graph of *causal histories* [5]. Narwhal guarantees that all correct validators eventually observe the same DAG prefix (via a Byzantine consistent broadcast per vertex) and tolerates asynchrony throughout — it never needs timeouts at all.

Consensus is then *interpreted* from the DAG rather than executed over the network. In Tusk, the asynchronous ordering layer, leaders (anchors) are committed when referenced by enough subsequent vertices, and the causal history of each committed anchor is ordered deterministically. Different validators may momentarily see slightly different DAGs, but the commit rule is designed so that all correct validators commit the *same* anchors in the *same* order — with **zero additional messages** beyond DAG construction [5][8].

This is the deepest conceptual shift in the article. The classical view-change exists to answer "what did the previous leader do?" after a failure. In the DAG, that question is answered *by construction*: every vertex embeds its causal past, so a new leader's proposal automatically carries the evidence of everything that preceded it. There is no `NEW-VIEW` message because there is no view to enter; there is only the next round of the DAG, which every correct validator advances at its own pace.

| Protocol | Model | Per-decision messages | View-change cost | Commit latency (good case) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT [1] | partial sync | *O(n²)* | *O(n²)*, non-responsive | 3 delays (responsive steady state) |
| HotStuff [2] | partial sync | *O(n)* | *O(n)*, responsive | 3 delays, pipelined |
| Narwhal–HotStuff [5] | partial sync ordering, async mempool | *O(n)* + DAG broadcast | *O(n)* | ~2 s (throughput-dominated) |
| Tusk [5] | asynchronous | 0 (DAG only) | n/a (viewless) | ~3 s expected |
| Bullshark async [6] | asynchronous | 0 (DAG only) | n/a (viewless) | 6 delays expected |
| Bullshark partial-sync [7] | partial sync | 0 (DAG only) | embedded timeouts | 2 delays (fast path) |

### 4.4 Embedding timeouts in the DAG: Bullshark's symmetric partial synchrony

Pure DAG protocols optimize for the asynchronous worst case and therefore leave performance on the table during the synchronous periods that dominate real deployments [6]. Bullshark closes this gap with a fast path: during synchrony it commits in just **2 round trips** (a 75% latency improvement over DAG-Rider), while retaining 6-round-trip expected latency under asynchrony [6].

The technically delicate contribution is the *eventually synchronous* (partially synchronous) variant of Bullshark — described by its authors as the first partially synchronous consensus protocol completely embedded into a DAG [7]. Bullshark's variant is *symmetric*: it needs no view change after a faulty leader at all. Instead, timeouts are embedded directly into DAG construction — a validator advances rounds not merely upon receiving *2f + 1* vertices (the DAG-Rider rule, which an adversary can exploit by reordering messages within the synchrony bound to skip the leader's vertex) but upon receiving *2f + 1* vertices **or** a timeout, with explicit waits for the predefined leader's vertex [6].

This is the pacemaker idea reincarnated inside the DAG: the "view" is a DAG round, the "leader" is the round's predefined anchor, and "view synchronization" is the timeout-gated round-advance rule. The result commits 125,000 TPS at 2-second latency across 50 honest parties, degrading gracefully under faults because the leader never sits on the data path [6][7]. It also resolved a genuine open problem: Bullshark garbage-collects old DAG rounds *while* preserving fairness (timely inclusion of every validator's transactions) during synchronous periods, where prior designs forced a choice between the two [6].

The lesson generalizes: **every BFT liveness mechanism is a view-synchronization mechanism in disguise**. Whether the artifact is called a view change, a pacemaker, a wave, or a DAG round, the irreducible content is the same — after the network heals, correct replicas must find a shared coordination point with a correct leader before the steady state can proceed.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### Measured performance

- **PBFT (1999):** A Byzantine-tolerant NFS service built on PBFT was measured only 3% slower than the unreplicated baseline — the founding evidence that BFT could be practical [1].
- **HotStuff (2019):** Deployment over 100+ replicas achieved throughput and latency comparable to BFT-SMaRt, with linear (vs. quadratic/cubic) communication during leader failover [2]. The chained form sustained pipelined proposals with one block per view.
- **Narwhal–HotStuff / Tusk (2022):** On a WAN, Narwhal–HotStuff exceeded 130,000 tx/sec at sub-2-second latency (vs. 1,800 tx/sec for HotStuff alone), scaling linearly to 600,000 tx/sec with added workers and no latency increase. Tusk reached 160,000 tx/sec at ~3 s latency with zero message overhead for ordering [5].
- **Bullshark (2022):** 125,000 TPS at 2 s latency with 50 honest parties for the partially synchronous variant; the async fast path halved DAG-Rider's round trips from 8 to 2 during synchrony [6][7].

### Proof sketch: why quorum intersection is not enough

Safety proofs in this literature are quorum-intersection arguments; liveness proofs are pacemaker arguments. We sketch the latter for a generic linear pacemaker:

> **Theorem (Pacemaker liveness):** Let timeouts double per view from a base *T₀*, and suppose after GST every message between correct replicas arrives within Δ. Then there exists a view *v\** after GST in which all correct replicas overlap for at least *T_commit* (the time a correct leader needs to drive a decision), and views thereafter recur with correct leaders infinitely often under round-robin rotation.

*Proof sketch.* After GST, let *v₀* be the first view entered after GST. Timeouts grow exponentially, so some view *v\* ≥ v₀* has timeout exceeding *2Δ + T_commit*. All correct replicas enter *v\** within Δ of each other (each advances on *2f + 1* timeout messages, which the *2f + 1* correct replicas all eventually send), so their sojourns in *v\** overlap for at least *T_commit*. With round-robin leaders, every *(f + 1)*-st such synchronized view has a correct leader. ∎

The sketch exposes where implementations fail: the timeout-message bookkeeping that Tendermint's bug got wrong — making the pacemaker the highest-value verification target in the system.

A Haskell-flavored rendering of the three-chain commit predicate shows how little state the modern core actually needs:

```haskell
-- A quorum certificate for the block proposed in a view
data QC = QC { qcView :: View, qcBlock :: Block, qcSig :: ThresholdSig }

-- Commit when two consecutive QCs extend the block's QC, plus one more
committed :: Block -> [QC] -> Bool
committed b qcs =
  let chain = filter (\(QC v _ _) -> True) qcs
      extends q q' = qcView q' == qcView q + 1
                 && parentBlock (qcBlock q') == qcBlock q
  in any (\(q1,q2,q3) -> qcBlock q1 == b && extends q1 q2 && extends q2 q3)
         (triples (sortOn qcView qcs))
```

##6 Limitations

**The synchrony assumption is load-bearing and untestable.** Every liveness claim in §§4.1–4.2 and 4.4 holds only after an *unknown* GST with an *unknown* bound Δ. Operators cannot verify these premises; adaptive timeout tuning is heuristic engineering atop a theorem that assumes the problem away.

**Cryptographic assumptions are stronger than stated.** Linearity depends on threshold signatures, which require a trusted dealer or distributed key generation ceremony; post-quantum security further restricts the signature menu (Bullshark's claim to post-quantum safety rests on using only hash-based primitives in its ordering path [6]).

**DAG protocols trade latency for throughput and memory for fairness.** Tusk's expected latency (~3 s) exceeds HotStuff's good-case latency by an order of magnitude in message delays; the DAG must be retained long enough for slow validators' vertices to be ordered, and garbage collection interacts adversarially with fairness — a validator whose vertices are collected before inclusion is effectively censored. Bullshark resolves this only for synchronous periods [6]; the fully asynchronous fairness–GC tradeoff remains open.

##7 Conclusion

View synchronization began as PBFT's most dreaded sub-protocol — quadratic, non-responsive, and intricate enough to harbor the system's worst bugs [1]. The pacemaker abstraction tamed it by separation of concerns; HotStuff's linear, responsive design made synchronization cheap enough to perform every view rather than only on failure [2]; and the DAG-based protocols completed the arc by making the question the view-change answered — *what happened while we were partitioned?* — answerable from the causal structure of dissemination itself, with zero extra messages [5][8].

Three lessons endure. First, **liveness is a synchronization problem, not an agreement problem** — every safety proof in this literature is a quorum argument, and every liveness proof is a pacemaker argument. Second, **the leader should leave the data path**: the order-of-magnitude throughput gains of Narwhal come not from faster agreement but from refusing to route data through the consensus bottleneck. Third, **simplicity is a liveness property**: the protocols that survive contact with production are those whose synchronization logic fits in one auditable module — or vanishes into the DAG entirely.

The open frontier is the asynchronous endgame: fairness with bounded memory, measured resilience against pacemaker-targeting adversaries, and mechanized proofs of the timeout machinery that remains, stubbornly, the place where correct-looking protocols go to stall.
