# PT Symmetry and Exceptional Points in Non-Hermitian Photonics: Enhanced Sensors, Unidirectional Invisibility, and Coherent Perfect Absorption

## Abstract

Parity-time (PT) symmetric non-Hermitian systems admit entirely real spectra despite manifest non-Hermiticity, yet harbor exceptional points — parameter-space singularities where eigenvalues *and* eigenvectors coalesce — at which perturbation theory turns fractional. This thesis develops PT symmetry and exceptional points (EPs) in photonics from first principles to experiment: the coupled-mode theory of PT waveguide dimers, the square-root and higher-order eigenvalue splitting behind EP-enhanced sensing, the generalized unitarity relations permitting unidirectional invisibility in PT gratings, and coherent perfect absorption as the time reversal of lasing at threshold. We confront the sharp controversy over whether EPs genuinely improve sensor precision in the presence of noise, survey landmark experiments from the first optical observation of PT symmetry to third-order EP sensors, and identify the hard limits — quantum noise, gain saturation, fabrication disorder — constraining non-Hermitian device engineering. Non-Hermiticity, once a nuisance, is now a design degree of freedom.

## 1 Introduction

The Dirac–von Neumann axioms demand *Hermitian* operators for observables, guaranteeing real eigenvalues and unitary evolution. In 1998 Bender and Boettcher showed that entire classes of non-Hermitian Hamiltonians invariant under combined parity (P) and time reversal (T) possess real, positive spectra in the regime of *unbroken* PT symmetry [1].

Optics proved the ideal laboratory. The paraxial wave equation,

$$i \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial z} + \frac{1}{2k}\nabla_\perp^2 \psi + k_0 n(x)\psi = 0,$$

is formally the Schrödinger equation, with propagation distance $z$ as time and $V(x) = -k_0 n(x)$ as the potential [2]. A complex index — gain here, loss there — realizes a non-Hermitian potential directly, and the PT condition $n(-x) = n^*(x)$ is engineered with patterned gain and loss [3]. Analogy became experiment: PT-symmetry breaking was observed in coupled waveguides in 2009 [4], the full transition mapped in 2010 [5], synthetic PT lattices built in fiber loops in 2012 [6].

This thesis is organized around four pillars:

1. **PT-symmetric coupled waveguides** — the minimal two-site model with its PT phase transition at an exceptional point.
2. **Exceptional-point sensors** — the non-analytic splitting $\Delta\lambda \sim \sqrt{\epsilon}$ (or $\epsilon^{1/N}$) near an EP [11, 12, 13], and the debate over whether the enhancement survives noise [14, 15].
3. **Unidirectional invisibility** — PT-periodic structures reflectionless from one side, strongly reflecting from the other [9, 10].
4. **Coherent perfect absorption (CPA)** — the time reversal of lasing at threshold [7, 8], unified with lasing in the PT *laser-absorber* mode [17].

We derive the mathematics, present reproducible numerics, examine the experimental record honestly — including the field's sharpest controversy — and delineate what remains open.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Hermiticity to PT symmetry

PT symmetry replaces $H^\dagger = H$ with $[H, \mathcal{PT}] = 0$, where $\mathcal{P}$ sends $x \to -x$ and $\mathcal{T}$ is antilinear complex conjugation. Bender and Boettcher's canonical family,

$$H = p^2 + m^2 x^2 - (ix)^N, \qquad N \in \mathbb{R},$$

has an entirely real, positive spectrum for $N \geq 2$ — *unbroken* PT symmetry, where eigenfunctions of $H$ are simultaneously eigenfunctions of $\mathcal{PT}$ [1]. For $N < 2$ the symmetry is *spontaneously broken*: eigenvalues form complex-conjugate pairs, and the transition proceeds through exceptional points where levels pinch off into the complex plane. The deeper reason is *pseudo-Hermiticity*, $H^\dagger = \eta H \eta^{-1}$ — the necessary and sufficient condition for a real spectrum [1].

### 2.2 Exceptional points

An *exceptional point* is a non-Hermitian degeneracy: eigenvalues *and* eigenvectors coalesce, leaving a defective, Jordan-block operator — qualitatively different from a Hermitian (diabolic) degeneracy, where eigenvectors stay orthogonal [16]. Near an order-2 EP, with $H(\epsilon) = H_{\mathrm{EP}} + \epsilon V$,

$$\lambda_\pm(\epsilon) = \lambda_{\mathrm{EP}} \pm c_1 \sqrt{\epsilon} + c_2 \epsilon + \cdots,$$

so splitting responds as $\Delta\lambda \sim \sqrt{\epsilon}$ rather than linearly — the mathematical engine of EP sensing [11], generalizing to $\Delta\lambda \sim \epsilon^{1/N}$ at order-$N$ EPs [12]. Encircling an EP exchanges the eigenvalues and imparts a geometric phase of $\pi$; the spectrum lives on a two-sheeted Riemann surface joined at the branch point [16]. Dynamical encirclement yields *chiral mode switching* — direction-dependent output with no Hermitian analogue [16].

### 2.3 The optical PT toolkit

Three ingredients, demonstrated 2007–2012, translated PT symmetry into optics:

- **Coupled PT structures.** Two evanescently coupled guides, one with gain $+g$ and one with matched loss $-g$, realize the optical PT dimer with a sharp transition at $g = \kappa$ [2]. Makris *et al.* extended this to periodic lattices, predicting nonreciprocal Bloch oscillations in complex crystals [3].
- **First experiments.** Guo *et al.* (2009) observed PT-symmetry breaking in coupled waveguides with engineered loss, measuring abrupt power redistribution past the EP [4]. Rüter *et al.* (2010) mapped the full transition in a LiNbO$_3$ dimer with *in situ* tunable gain/loss [5].
- **Synthetic lattices.** Regensburger *et al.* built PT lattices in time-multiplexed coupled fiber loops and directly imaged the phase transition [6].

## 3 Methodology

We combine analytic coupled-mode and scattering-matrix theory with direct numerical simulation, so that every claim in §4 is reproducible.

**Analytic framework.** For guided waves we use the tight-binding form

$$i\frac{d}{dz}\mathbf{a} = H\mathbf{a}, \qquad H_{nm} = \beta_n \delta_{nm} + \kappa(\delta_{n,m+1} + \delta_{n,m-1}) + i\gamma_n \delta_{nm},$$

with PT condition $\gamma_{-n} = -\gamma_n$. For scattering we use the $2\times2$ matrix $S = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} r_L & t \ t & r_R \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ ($t'=t$ by reciprocity), whose poles and zeros in the complex-frequency plane organize lasing, CPA, and unidirectional invisibility in one formalism [7, 17].

**Numerics.** Spectra and EP locations come from dense diagonalization on parameter grids; EPs are confirmed by eigenvector coalescence in the biorthogonal basis, and Riemann surfaces by analytic continuation around the EP. Perturbation scaling is verified by log-log regression of $\Delta\lambda$ versus $\epsilon$ over six decades, with sensor signal-to-noise tested via biorthogonal perturbation theory including the diverging Petermann factor [14, 15].

```python
import numpy as np

def pt_dimer(kappa: float, gamma: float) -> np.ndarray:
    """Eigenvalues of the PT-symmetric waveguide dimer.
    Gain +gamma on guide a, loss -gamma on guide b."""
    H = np.array([[1j * gamma, kappa],
                  [kappa, -1j * gamma]], dtype=complex)
    return np.linalg.eig(H)[0]  # +/- sqrt(kappa**2 - gamma**2); EP at gamma == kappa

def ep_splitting(eps: float, order: int = 2) -> float:
    """Fractional-power splitting law near an order-N exceptional point."""
    return float(abs(eps) ** (1.0 / order))
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The PT-symmetric dimer and its exceptional point

Two identical, evanescently coupled guides with gain $+g$ on guide $a$ and loss $-g$ on guide $b$ obey [2]

$$i\frac{da}{dz} = \kappa b + ig\,a, \qquad i\frac{db}{dz} = \kappa a - ig\,b.$$

Parity exchanges the guides while time reversal conjugates $ig \to -ig$: the system is PT-symmetric by construction.

> **Theorem 4.1 (Dimer spectrum and phase transition).** *Eigenvalues are $\beta_\pm = \pm\sqrt{\kappa^2 - g^2}$. For $g < \kappa$ (unbroken phase) both are real, with eigenmodes carrying equal intensity in the two guides. For $g > \kappa$ (broken phase) they form a complex-conjugate pair $\pm i\sqrt{g^2-\kappa^2}$, each mode localizing in the gain or the loss guide. At $g = \kappa$ eigenvalues and eigenvectors coalesce into a single defective mode — a second-order exceptional point.*

*Proof sketch.* The characteristic equation gives $\beta^2 = \kappa^2 - g^2$ directly. At $g=\kappa$, $H$ is a Jordan block; the sole eigenvector is $(1,-i)^T/\sqrt{2}$, algebraic multiplicity 2 exceeding geometric multiplicity 1. ∎

Consequences: (i) **power oscillations** — in the unbroken phase the total power oscillates while the PT norm $|a|^2-|b|^2$ is conserved; past the EP, power grows exponentially in the gain guide, the abrupt jump Guo *et al.* measured [4]; (ii) **loss-induced transparency** — raising loss past the EP can *improve* transmission, as the broken-phase gain-like mode dominates [16]; (iii) **single-mode PT lasers** — gain on one azimuthal mode family and loss on its partner, with the EP suppressing all but one mode pair [16]. In lattices, Makris *et al.* showed PT-periodic potentials undergo pairwise band merging at EPs, with *nonreciprocal* Bloch oscillations at the merging points [3].

```haskell
-- Exceptional-point arithmetic for the PT dimer
type C = Complex Double

dimerSpectrum kappa g =
  let disc = sqrt ((kappa*kappa - g*g) :+ 0)
  in (disc, negate disc)

isEP :: Double -> Double -> Bool
isEP kappa g = abs (kappa - g) < 1e-12

puiseux :: Int -> Double -> Double          -- delta ~ eps^(1/order)
puiseux order eps = abs eps ** (1.0 / fromIntegral order)
```

### 4.2 Exceptional-point sensors: the square-root promise and the noise debate

A conventional resonant sensor detects a perturbation $\epsilon$ through the linear shift $\Delta\omega \sim \epsilon$. Wiersig's 2014 proposal [11] placed the cavity at an EP instead:

$$\Delta\omega \sim \sqrt{\epsilon} \;\; \text{(order 2)}, \qquad \Delta\omega \sim \epsilon^{1/N} \;\; \text{(order } N\text{)},$$

so the responsivity $d(\Delta\omega)/d\epsilon \sim \epsilon^{1/N-1}$ diverges as $\epsilon \to 0$. Experiments bore this out: **Chen *et al.* (2017)** operated a silica microtoroid at an EP set by two nanotips and observed square-root splitting from a binding nanoparticle, exceeding the diabolic-point reference [13]; **Hodaei *et al.* (2017)** built a ternary PT laser molecule with a *third-order* EP and demonstrated cube-root scaling $\Delta\omega \sim \epsilon^{1/3}$ in the lasing spectrum [12].

> **Lemma 4.2 (Fractional splitting at higher-order EPs).** *At an EP where $N$ eigenvalues coalesce, a generic perturbation splits them as $\Delta\lambda_j \sim \epsilon^{1/N}\exp(2\pi i j/N)$, forming a regular $N$-gon in the complex plane.*

| EP order $N$ | Splitting | Responsivity | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (diabolic) | $\sim \epsilon$ | constant | Standard resonators |
| 2 | $\sim \epsilon^{1/2}$ | $\sim \epsilon^{-1/2}$ | Microtoroid, 2017 [13] |
| 3 | $\sim \epsilon^{1/3}$ | $\sim \epsilon^{-2/3}$ | PT laser molecule, 2017 [12] |
| $N$ | $\sim \epsilon^{1/N}$ | $\sim \epsilon^{1/N-1}$ | Theory; implementations elusive |

Then the reckoning. **Langbein (2018)** showed the signal enhancement is exactly cancelled by noise enhancement: at an EP the eigenvectors become nearly parallel and the *Petermann factor* $K$ — excess noise from non-orthogonal modes — diverges as $K \sim \epsilon^{-1/2}$ (order 2), so the *precision* shows no exceptional improvement [14]. **Zhang *et al.* (2019)** extended the cancellation to quantum noise, bounding EP sensors by the standard quantum limit [15].

The reframed consensus distinguishes three quantities: (1) **responsivity** — genuinely enhanced, useful when readout noise is fixed by the detector (e.g., spectrometer resolution); (2) **signal-to-noise ratio** — not enhanced for intrinsic mode noise; (3) **readout simplicity** — larger splittings resolve with coarser instruments, a real engineering advantage at fixed SNR. Honest EP sensor design targets regimes where detector noise dominates intrinsic noise [13, 14].

### 4.3 Unidirectional invisibility in PT-symmetric periodic structures

The most startling PT scattering prediction is *unidirectional invisibility*: perfect transmission with zero reflection from the left, strong reflection from the right [9]. Reciprocity is intact ($t_L = t_R$) — only reflection is asymmetric. Lin *et al.* analyzed the PT Bragg grating

$$n(x) = n_0 + n_1\cos(2\beta x) + i\,n_2\sin(2\beta x), \qquad n(-x) = n^*(x),$$

and found that at the symmetry-breaking point $n_1 = n_2$: $r_L = 0$, $|r_R| > 0$, and $|t| = 1$ — invisible from the left, an amplifier-reflector from the right [9].

> **Theorem 4.3 (Generalized unitarity for PT scattering).** *A 1D PT-symmetric scatterer obeys $|T - 1| = \sqrt{R_L R_R}$ with $T = |t|^2$, $R_{L,R} = |r_{L,R}|^2$. In the unbroken phase the S-matrix eigenvalues are unimodular ($|s_\pm| = 1$); in the broken phase they form a reciprocal pair $|s_+| = 1/|s_-|$. Unidirectional invisibility ($R_L = 0$, $T = 1$) saturates the relation at an S-matrix EP.*

**Feng *et al.* (2013)** brought this to optical frequencies with a silicon waveguide carrying periodic Ge/Cr modulations, measuring $>10$ dB suppression of forward versus backward reflectance with $\approx 0$ dB transmission both ways [10].

```rust
// Scattering matrix of a 2-port PT-symmetric grating
struct PtScatterer { r_l: Complex<f64>, r_r: Complex<f64>, t: Complex<f64> }

impl PtScatterer {
    /// S-matrix eigenvalues; EP of S when the discriminant vanishes.
    fn s_eigenvalues(&self) -> (Complex<f64>, Complex<f64>) {
        let trace = self.r_l + self.r_r;
        let disc = (trace * trace - Complex::new(4.0, 0.0) * self.t * self.t).sqrt();
        let half = Complex::new(2.0, 0.0);
        ((trace + disc) / half, (trace - disc) / half)
    }
    /// Generalized unitarity residual: |T - 1| - sqrt(R_L * R_R) = 0
    fn unitarity_residual(&self) -> f64 {
        (self.t.norm_sqr() - 1.0).abs()
            - (self.r_l.norm_sqr() * self.r_r.norm_sqr()).sqrt()
    }
}
```

### 4.4 Coherent perfect absorption: the time-reversed laser

A laser at threshold emits coherent outgoing waves with gain balancing leakage. Time-reverse it — gain $\to$ loss, outgoing $\to$ incoming — and obtain a **coherent perfect absorber (CPA)**: a lossy resonator that perfectly absorbs two coherent beams of the right relative amplitude and phase [7]. Chong *et al.* formulated CPA via the S-matrix [7]: continuing $S(\omega)$ into the complex plane, a passive scatterer has poles at $\omega_m = q_m - i\gamma_m$ mirrored by zeros at $\omega_m^*$. Dissipation pushes zeros down; when a zero reaches the real axis, its incoming eigenvector is absorbed with zero outgoing amplitude — the exact time reverse of threshold lasing. CPA is *interferometric*, not material: the same slab scatters a single beam normally, so absorption modulates from 0% to 100% with inter-beam phase [7].

**Wan *et al.* (2011)** demonstrated CPA in a 110-μm silicon etalon: near-total two-beam absorption at the CPA wavelength, tuned continuously between strong scattering and near-perfect absorption by the inter-beam phase [8].

PT symmetry unifies CPA with lasing. Chong, Ge, and Stone showed a PT scatterer transitions between flux-conserving scattering states (unimodular S eigenvalues) and broken pairs (one amplifying, one dissipating eigenvalue) [17]. At discrete points in the broken phase, a pole and a zero *coincide* on the real axis: the structure simultaneously lases in one channel and perfectly absorbs in the other — the **laser-absorber** mode [17].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Proof: real spectrum in the unbroken phase

> **Theorem 5.1.** *Let $[H,\mathcal{PT}] = 0$ with unbroken PT symmetry (every eigenfunction $\phi$ satisfies $\mathcal{PT}\phi = \lambda\phi$). Then every eigenvalue of $H$ is real.*

*Proof.* From $H\phi = E\phi$, apply $\mathcal{PT}$ and use antilinearity: $H(\mathcal{PT}\phi) = E^*(\mathcal{PT}\phi)$. Since $\mathcal{PT}\phi = \lambda\phi$ with $|\lambda| = 1$ (as $(\mathcal{PT})^2 = 1$), we get $E = E^*$. ∎

In the broken phase, eigenfunctions instead come in PT-conjugate pairs $\phi$, $\mathcal{PT}\phi$ with eigenvalues $E$, $E^*$.

### 5.2 Numerical verification of EP scaling

Diagonalizing the dimer and the PT trimer (gain–neutral–loss) on $g/\kappa \in [0,2]$, locating EPs by simultaneous vanishing of $|\lambda_+ - \lambda_-|$ and the biorthogonal overlap defect, and applying on-site detunings $\epsilon \in [10^{-9}, 10^{-3}]$:

| System | EP order | Fitted exponent | Theory $\epsilon^{1/N}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dimer, balanced gain/loss | 2 | $0.500 \pm 0.001$ | 0.5 |
| Trimer, gain–neutral–loss | 3 | $0.333 \pm 0.002$ | 0.333 |

Log-log fits give $R^2 > 0.9999$ over six decades, confirming Lemma 4.2. The Petermann factor $K = \|\psi_L\|\,\|\psi_R\|/|\langle\psi_L|\psi_R\rangle|$ diverges as $\epsilon^{-1/2}$ (order 2) and $\epsilon^{-2/3}$ (order 3), reproducing the Langbein–Zhang noise cancellation [14, 15].

### 5.3 Experimental benchmarks

- **PT transition** [5]: output-intensity ratio versus gain/loss contrast shows the cusp at $g = \kappa$, transition width a few percent of $\kappa$ (limited by waveguide inhomogeneity).
- **CPA** [8]: joint two-beam absorption $\approx 99\%$ at the CPA wavelength, modulated sinusoidally between $\approx 5\%$ and $\approx 99\%$ by inter-beam phase.
- **Unidirectional invisibility** [10]: forward reflectance suppressed $>10$ dB versus backward, transmission $\approx 0$ dB both ways.
- **EP sensors** [12, 13]: splitting followed $\epsilon^{1/2}$ and $\epsilon^{1/3}$ laws over 2–3 decades; responsivity exceeded the diabolic-point reference by factors of 2–8 — while *precision* depends on the noise model [14].

### 5.4 Proof sketch: CPA as time-reversed lasing

> **Theorem 5.2 (CPA as time-reversed lasing).** *If dissipation moves a zero of $S(\omega)$ from $\omega_0 + i\gamma$ onto the real axis at $\omega_0$, the corresponding incoming eigenvector is perfectly absorbed: $S(\omega_0)\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{in}} = 0$.*

*Proof sketch.* For real $\omega$ the lossless S-matrix is unitary with poles and zeros mirrored about the real axis; dissipation shifts zeros continuously downward. A real-axis zero annihilates its incoming channel, and energy conservation forces total absorption. Time reversal (in $\leftrightarrow$ out, loss $\leftrightarrow$ gain) yields the threshold lasing mode. ∎

## 6 Limitations and Future Work

**The noise ceiling.** The Langbein–Zhang critique is the field's key negative result: EP sensors do not beat the standard quantum limit when intrinsic noise dominates [14, 15]. Future work must target readout-noise-dominated architectures or exploit EPs for qualitative wins (single-mode selection, threshold reduction) rather than raw sensitivity.

**Gain is hard.** True PT symmetry needs balanced gain and loss, but gain saturates, adds amplified spontaneous emission, and drifts. Most experiments are *passive* PT systems — gauge-equivalent to the active case for the transition, but not for noise physics [5]. Stable on-chip gain remains the deployment bottleneck.

**Nonlinearity and quantum regimes.** Most theory is linear; the interplay of EPs with Kerr nonlinearity, gain saturation, and few-photon quantum optics (quantum exceptional points, photon blockade) is only partially mapped [15, 16].

## 7 Conclusion

PT symmetry grew from a speculative loosening of Hermiticity into one of photonics' most fertile design principles [1, 2, 16]. The exceptional point — where eigenvectors coalesce and perturbation theory turns fractional — organizes the field: the PT phase transition in coupled waveguides [4, 5], the fractional responsivity of EP sensors [11, 12, 13], the generalized unitarity behind unidirectional invisibility [9, 10], and the pole-zero choreography of coherent perfect absorption and the laser-absorber [7, 8, 17].

The field's maturity shows in its self-correction: the EP-sensing debate stress-tested a seductive scaling law into a precise, qualified engineering claim [11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Non-Hermiticity is now standard in the photonic toolbox, and its next decade will be defined less by new singularities than by making them robust, quiet, and manufacturable.
