# Processing-in-Memory Architectures: UPMEM DPUs, HBM-PIM Bank-Level Parallelism, and Near-Data Analytics at Scale

## Abstract

Processing-in-memory (PIM) resurrects a fifty-year-old architectural idea — compute where the data already lives — with, for the first time, commercially fabricated silicon. This thesis analyzes the two dominant contemporary realizations of the paradigm: **UPMEM's** DRAM processing units (DPUs), general-purpose in-order cores embedded beside DDR4 banks, and **Samsung's HBM-PIM (Aquabolt-XL)**, a bank-level programmable computing unit integrated into HBM2 stacks. We dissect the *memory wall* and von Neumann bottleneck that motivate PIM, the scratchpad-centric programming model of the UPMEM SDK (24 tasklets per DPU, 64 KB WRAM, 64 MB MRAM, explicit `mram_read`/`mram_write` DMA), and the 1.2 TFLOPS-per-stack bank-parallel AI engine of HBM-PIM with its drop-in JEDEC-compatible interface. We evaluate near-data analytics kernels — scans, hash joins, aggregations — through the lens of the **PrIM** benchmark suite's 16 workloads, reporting published speedups over CPUs and GPUs, and we confront the remaining hard problems: coherence, virtual memory, inter-DPU communication, and workload partitioning. PIM is not a replacement for accelerators; it is a *complement* that collapses the dominant cost of modern workloads — data movement — at the bank itself.

## 1. Introduction

For six decades, the von Neumann architecture has separated processor from memory and paid a growing toll at the interface between them. DRAM bandwidth has improved roughly 20× since the 1990s while per-core compute throughput has improved by orders of magnitude more [1]; the result is the *memory wall*: workloads stall not on arithmetic but on the wires between the memory array and the socket. Data movement now dominates both execution time and energy — moving a 64-bit word from off-chip DRAM costs on the order of 100× the energy of a 32-bit floating-point multiply on the same process node [2].

Processing-in-memory attacks the bottleneck at its root by placing computation *inside* the memory hierarchy, near or within the DRAM banks themselves. The idea dates to the 1960s (Stone, Kautz, and later the IRAM and DIVA projects of the 1990s), but technology constraints — the incompatibility of logic and DRAM processes, yield, thermals — prevented commercialization for over fifty years [2]. That changed in 2019 when **UPMEM** shipped the first publicly available PIM product: standard DDR4 DIMMs in which each DRAM bank is paired with an in-order, multithreaded DRAM Processing Unit (DPU) [3]. In 2021, **Samsung** followed with the industry's first HBM-PIM (Aquabolt-XL), embedding a programmable AI engine into each bank of an HBM2 stack and demonstrating nearly 2.5× system performance and 60%+ energy reduction on a commercial accelerator [5][6].

> **Theorem:** For any workload whose arithmetic intensity \(I\) satisfies \(I < B_{\text{bus}}/P_{\text{peak}}\), execution time is dominated by host↔memory transfers; moving computation to the bank reduces the transfer volume by the output-to-input ratio \(R\), yielding a theoretical speedup bounded by \(\min(R, B_{\text{bank}}/B_{\text{bus}})\) where \(B_{\text{bank}}\) is the aggregate internal bank bandwidth.

This thesis presents a unified treatment of modern PIM: the physics of the memory wall, the UPMEM architecture and its unusual *scratchpad-centric* programming model, the bank-level parallelism of HBM-PIM, near-data analytics for databases, the unsolved systems problems (coherence, virtual memory, inter-PIM communication), and empirical evidence from the PrIM benchmark suite. The central claim is that PIM is best understood not as a processor replacement but as a **memory-side coprocessor tier** that converts memory-bound phases of heterogeneous workloads into embarrassingly parallel, bandwidth-abundant in-situ computation.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The memory wall and the von Neumann bottleneck

The von Neumann bottleneck is the latency and bandwidth of the processor↔memory channel; the *memory wall* (Wulf and McKee, 1995) is the observation that processor speed grows ~55%/year while DRAM latency improves ~7%/year, so the performance gap widens exponentially over time. Three decades of mitigation — deeper cache hierarchies, prefetching, out-of-order execution, HBM — have treated symptoms. Caches now consume more die area than cores on many server chips, yet memory-bound workloads (streaming analytics, graph traversal, sparse ML inference) remain stranded at a few percent of peak FLOPS.

PIM inverts the mitigation strategy. A single DRAM bank can sustain enormous internal bandwidth — tens of GB/s per bank — of which the narrow off-chip bus exposes only a small fraction. Aggregate internal bandwidth across thousands of banks exceeds the pin-limited bus by one to two orders of magnitude. PIM harvests this internal bandwidth by computing at the source.

### 2.2 A taxonomy of PIM

Modern PIM proposals fall into three families:

1. **Near-memory / near-data processing (NDP):** general-purpose or domain-specific logic on the memory die or module (UPMEM DPUs, Samsung AXDIMM).
2. **In-situ / true PIM:** computation using the memory cells' own physics — e.g., *RowClone* (bulk copy via row activation), *Ambit* (bulk bitwise AND/OR/NOT via triple-row activation), compute-in-SRAM/RRAM crossbars for analog MAC.
3. **Bank-level accelerators:** programmable engines replicated per bank or per subarray (Samsung HBM-PIM, SK hynix AiM/GDDR6-PIM).

UPMEM and HBM-PIM, the subjects of this work, belong to families 1 and 3 respectively: both are **digital, programmable, and commercially fabricated**, which makes them the right subjects for empirical analysis [1].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is an analytical and empirical survey grounded in published measurement studies, vendor documentation, and open benchmark infrastructure:

- **Architectural analysis** follows the comprehensive UPMEM characterization of Gómez-Luna et al. (the *PrIM* work), including their microbenchmark methodology and the uPIMulator simulation framework [1][2][3].
- **Programming-model analysis** is drawn from the UPMEM SDK and its runtime library (`mram_read`/`mram_write`, tasklet threading, mutex/barrier primitives), cross-checked against ETH Zurich's laboratory curriculum for real PIM hardware [4].
- **HBM-PIM analysis** uses Samsung's ISSCC and Hot Chips disclosures, Samsung Newsroom integration results on the Xilinx Virtex UltraScale+ (Alveo) accelerator, and EE Times reporting on the PIM roadmap (AXDIMM, HBM3-PIM, LPDDR-PIM) [5][6][7].
- **Evaluation evidence** is taken from the PrIM suite: 16 memory-bound workloads with strong- and weak-scaling studies and comparisons against state-of-the-art CPUs and GPUs [3].

Where quantitative claims are vendor-reported, they are labeled as such; where independently measured, the methodology is described in §5.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The UPMEM DRAM Processing Unit: architecture and programming model

**Organization.** A UPMEM DIMM contains 8 or 16 PIM-enabled DDR4 chips arranged in one or two ranks. Each chip holds **8 DRAM banks of 64 MB** (the *Main RAM*, MRAM) and **8 DRAM Processing Units**, one per bank — 64 DPUs per rank. A fully populated 20-DIMM server can host up to **2,560 DPUs**, each an independent in-order core, representing a genuine massively parallel machine built from memory chips [2][3].

**DPU microarchitecture.** Each DPU is a 32-bit in-order, fine-grained multithreaded RISC core (DPU-specific ISA, LLVM toolchain) clocked at a few hundred MHz, with:

- **24 KB IRAM** — instruction memory,
- **64 KB WRAM** — scratchpad data memory,
- **64 MB MRAM** — its private DRAM bank,
- a DMA engine mediating MRAM↔WRAM transfers,
- 24 hardware thread contexts ("tasklets") sharing the pipeline.

The pipeline is deliberately simple: launching **at least 11 tasklets** is recommended to hide pipeline latency, a figure derived from microbenchmarks of the 14-stage pipeline [2].

**The scratchpad-centric programming model.** UPMEM's defining quirk, emphasized by Oliveira and Mutlu [1], is that DPU threads can load/store *only* from WRAM. Data in MRAM is not directly addressable: the programmer must stage every working set through the scratchpad with explicit DMA calls:

```c
/* DPU kernel: vector add, UPMEM SDK style */
__mram_noinit uint32_t mram_A[N], mram_B[N], mram_C[N];

int main() {
    uint32_t tid = me();                       /* tasklet id, 0..23 */
    uint32_t *wA = mem_alloc(BLOCK * sizeof(uint32_t));
    /* ... partition rows across tasklets ... */
    for (uint32_t i = start; i < end; i += BLOCK) {
        mram_read(&mram_A[i], wA, BLOCK * sizeof(uint32_t));  /* DMA MRAM->WRAM */
        mram_read(&mram_B[i], wB, BLOCK * sizeof(uint32_t));
        for (uint32_t j = 0; j < BLOCK; j++)
            wC[j] = wA[j] + wB[j];                            /* WRAM-only ALU */
        mram_write(wC, &mram_C[i], BLOCK * sizeof(uint32_t)); /* DMA WRAM->MRAM */
    }
    return 0;
}
```

This is *CUDA-like* in its explicit data movement (`mram_read` ≈ `cudaMemcpy`) but stricter: CUDA threads may address both shared memory and global DRAM, while UPMEM threads address only the scratchpad [1]. Tasklets within one DPU share MRAM/WRAM and synchronize via mutexes, barriers, and semaphores in an atomic region; **tasklets in different DPUs share nothing and cannot communicate or synchronize directly** — the host CPU mediates all inter-DPU exchange via CPU↔DPU transfers [2]. The host API supports *serial*, *parallel*, and *broadcast* transfers between main memory and MRAM banks [4].

Four programming recommendations, validated experimentally by the PrIM authors, govern efficient UPMEM code [2]:

1. **Execute long code phases on the DPUs**, avoiding frequent host interaction — CPU↔DPU transfers traverse the narrow memory bus and reintroduce the bottleneck PIM promises to remove.
2. **Split the workload into independent data blocks** that DPUs process concurrently — maximizing parallelism and eliminating inter-DPU synchronization.
3. **Use as many working DPUs as possible** while the workload stays busy.
4. **Launch ≥11 tasklets per DPU** to fill the fine-grained multithreaded pipeline.

### 4.2 Samsung HBM-PIM: bank-level parallelism and the Aquabolt-XL

Samsung's approach attacks the same bottleneck from a different angle: rather than a general-purpose core per bank, HBM-PIM integrates a **DRAM-optimized AI engine inside each memory bank** of an HBM2 stack [5]. The design, presented at ISSCC, is built on 20 nm HBM2 ("Aquabolt-XL") and delivers a **1.2 TFLOPS programmable computing unit per stack using bank-level parallelism** for machine-learning workloads — each bank computes on its resident data concurrently, so aggregate throughput scales with bank count rather than pin count.

Three properties made Aquabolt-XL commercially notable [5][6][7]:

- **Drop-in compatibility.** It is architected as a replacement for HBM2 and is fully compatible with JEDEC-compliant HBM2 memory controllers; Samsung demonstrated a commercial processor running TensorFlow-based code with **no application source changes** [7].
- **Measured system gains.** Integrated into the Xilinx Virtex UltraScale+ (Alveo) AI accelerator, HBM-PIM delivered **~2.5× system performance and >60% energy reduction** on memory-bound AI workloads (Samsung's launch figures cite >2× performance and >70% energy reduction) [5][6].
- **A roadmap beyond HBM.** Samsung extended PIM to the **AXDIMM** — an AI engine in the DRAM module's buffer chip that parallelizes across multiple ranks instead of one rank at a time — and announced plans for HBM3-PIM and LPDDR-PIM for on-device AI [7].

> **Design contrast:** UPMEM is *core-per-bank* (flexible, low clock, huge core count); HBM-PIM is *engine-per-bank* (domain-targeted, high internal bandwidth, transparent to software). The former maximizes programmability and core count; the latter maximizes compatibility and per-bank MAC throughput for AI. Together they bracket the PIM design space.

The bank-level parallelism model can be expressed as an extension of the roofline: PIM raises the memory-bandwidth ceiling from the pin-limited \(B_{\text{pin}}\) to the aggregate internal \(B_{\text{int}} = n_{\text{banks}} \cdot b_{\text{bank}}\), rotating the memory-bound slope of the roofline upward by the ratio \(B_{\text{int}}/B_{\text{pin}}\). Workloads with arithmetic intensity between the old and new knees — precisely where LLM decoding, recommendation, and graph analytics live — move from memory-bound to compute-bound without any change in DRAM technology.

### 4.3 Near-data analytics: scans, joins, and PIM-enabled databases

Database analytics is PIM's natural proving ground: scans, filters, hash joins, and aggregations are streaming, embarrassingly parallel, and limited almost entirely by memory bandwidth.

**Scans and selections.** A column scan maps trivially onto UPMEM: partition the column across DPUs, have each tasklet stream 8-byte-aligned blocks MRAM→WRAM, apply the predicate in WRAM, and write back a compacted bitvector or result list. Because each DPU operates on its private bank, aggregate scan bandwidth scales with DPU count. The PrIM suite includes such kernels and reports near-linear strong scaling for data-parallel scans on hundreds of DPUs [3].

**Hash joins.** The classic PIM database kernel is the partitioned hash join. The build phase distributes the smaller relation's hash table across DPUs (partitioned by hash key, since DPUs cannot share memory); the probe phase streams the larger relation through each DPU, probing the local hash partition and emitting matches. No inter-DPU communication is needed if partitioning is key-consistent — a direct embodiment of recommendation #2 in §4.1. The host then merges per-DPU outputs, an \(O(\text{output})\) cost that is small relative to the avoided input scan [2].

**Aggregations.** Group-by aggregation follows the same pattern: local partial aggregates per DPU, host-side final reduction. The communication volume is proportional to the number of groups, not the input size, which is exactly the reduction ratio \(R\) from the theorem in §1.

```python
# Host-side orchestration sketch (UPMEM SDK, Python bindings style)
import upmem  # conceptual
def pim_hash_join(R, S, key):
    R_parts = partition_by_hash(R, key, n_dpus)   # key-consistent partitioning
    dpu.upload("mram_R", R_parts)                 # parallel host->MRAM transfer
    dpu.upload("mram_S", partition_roundrobin(S, n_dpus))
    dpu.launch(kernel="hash_join", tasklets=16)   # SPMD, >=11 tasklets
    partials = dpu.download("mram_out")            # small: only matches
    return merge(partials)                        # host-side final merge
```

PIM-enabled databases (research prototypes building on this pattern) report that join and scan phases, which dominate TPC-H-style analytical queries, see order-of-magnitude bandwidth amplification versus socket execution, with the host retaining responsibility only for coordination and result merging.

### 4.4 Coherence, virtual memory, and system-integration challenges

If PIM hardware is the good news, the system stack is the hard problem. Five challenges dominate [1][2]:

1. **Cache coherence.** DPUs operate on DRAM directly, outside the CPU's coherence domain. Any host-cached copy of a PIM-operated region must be flushed/invalidated explicitly; there is no MESI-style protocol spanning the memory bus for PIM cores. UPMEM sidesteps this by treating PIM memory as a separate, explicitly managed address space — correct but programmer-visible.
2. **Virtual memory.** DPUs use physical addressing into their MRAM bank; there is no MMU/TLB on the DPU. Address translation, page faults, and protection are host responsibilities, which complicates pointer-rich data structures and multi-tenant sharing.
3. **Inter-PIM communication.** The absence of a DPU↔DPU channel makes any workload with cross-partition dependencies (graph traversals with frontier exchange, iterative solvers) pay host-mediated round trips. Efficient *PIM-to-PIM* interconnects — perhaps via the memory bus itself or a dedicated network — are an active research direction [1].
4. **Workload partitioning and load balance.** Skewed key distributions unbalance hash-partitioned joins; the host must repartition dynamically, and the cost model for "what runs where" (host vs. DPU vs. bank engine) is still immature. A compiler or runtime that automates the §4.1 recommendations remains an open problem.
5. **Programming-model friction.** The scratchpad-centric model is powerful but unforgiving: every byte must be staged through 64 KB of WRAM with 8-byte-aligned DMA, and debugging in-situ cores is primitive relative to CPU/GPU tooling. Higher-level abstractions (PIM-aware dataframe libraries, SQL pushdown) are needed before application programmers adopt PIM at scale.

### 4.5 The PrIM benchmark suite and evaluation methodology

The **PrIM (Processing-In-Memory) benchmark suite** of Gómez-Luna et al. is the standard empirical instrument for UPMEM-class hardware [3]: 16 workloads chosen specifically because they are *memory-bound on conventional processor-centric systems*, spanning dense/sparse linear algebra, graph processing, databases, data analytics, bioinformatics, and ML. The suite is open-source [3] and ships with both CPU/GPU baselines and scaling harnesses (strong scaling: fixed problem, growing DPU count; weak scaling: problem ∝ DPU count).

Methodologically, PrIM established the template every subsequent PIM study follows: (i) microbenchmark the primitives (DMA bandwidth MRAM↔WRAM, tasklet scaling, synchronization cost); (ii) validate the programming recommendations (§4.1) by controlled violation; (iii) benchmark end-to-end against tuned CPU (multicore, SIMD) and GPU baselines on identical datasets; (iv) report both time and energy where instrumentation allows. This discipline is what separates PIM evaluation from marketing: the gains claimed must survive comparison against a well-utilized baseline, not a strawman.

## 5. Empirical Results and Benchmarks

The published record, led by the PrIM characterization [2][3] and Samsung's integration studies [5][6], supports five empirical conclusions:

| Workload class | PIM approach | Reported result | Baseline |
|---|---|---|---|
| 16 memory-bound kernels (PrIM) | UPMEM, 2,560 DPUs | Competitive with / exceeding CPU & GPU on bandwidth-bound kernels; near-linear strong scaling on data-parallel scans | Multicore CPU, high-end GPU [2] |
| Vector/streaming kernels | UPMEM DPUs | DPUs saturate aggregate internal bank bandwidth; host↔DPU transfer dominates if phases are short (validates recommendation #1) | CPU STREAM [2] |
| AI inference (HBM-PIM) | Aquabolt-XL in Xilinx Alveo | **~2.5× system performance, >60% energy reduction** (Samsung: >2× perf, >70% energy) | HBM2 without PIM [5][6] |
| Database scans/joins | UPMEM partitioned kernels | Order-of-magnitude bandwidth amplification on scan-heavy phases; join limited by partitioning skew | CPU analytical engine [2] |
| Microbenchmarks | DPU pipeline/DMA | ≥11 tasklets needed to fill pipeline; DMA bandwidth MRAM↔WRAM is the per-DPU limiter, not ALU | — [1][2] |

Three patterns recur across studies. **First**, PIM wins exactly where the roofline predicts: low arithmetic intensity, streaming access, large working sets. **Second**, the host↔PIM transfer is the new bottleneck — workloads that bounce small phases between host and DPUs lose their gains to the same narrow bus they sought to escape, which is why recommendation #1 (long DPU phases) is the single most important programming rule [2]. **Third**, energy follows bandwidth: eliminating off-chip transfers removes the most expensive operation in the system (the DRAM access), so performance-per-watt improvements routinely exceed raw speedups [5][6].

The uPIMulator work [1] adds a forward-looking result: simulating scaled-up PIM organizations (more DPUs, wider DMA, inter-DPU links) suggests that the *scratchpad-centric* model remains viable at larger scale provided the DMA engine, not the core, is the scaling focus — a concrete hint for next-generation PIM architects.

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating what PIM does not solve:

- **PIM is not a general-purpose accelerator.** Compute-bound workloads (dense GEMM at high arithmetic intensity, well-cached kernels) gain nothing; the DPU's few-hundred-MHz in-order core is far weaker than a host core per thread. PIM accelerates *data movement*, not *computation*.
- **The programming model is expert-only.** Explicit DMA staging, 8-byte alignment, 64 KB scratchpad budgets, no inter-DPU communication, physical addressing — this is closer to 1980s vector programming than to modern parallel frameworks. Adoption awaits compilers and libraries.
- **Capacity and thermal ceilings.** Logic in the DRAM process is area- and power-constrained; DPUs are weak *by design*. HBM-PIM's engine is domain-specific *by design*. Neither will host an operating system or a full application.
- **Measurement gaps.** Most published PIM numbers come from a single vendor's hardware (UPMEM) or vendor-reported integration studies (Samsung); independent, multi-vendor, end-to-end application studies remain scarce, and energy measurements on real PIM DIMMs are still methodologically young.
- **Economic uncertainty.** UPMEM-class DIMMs compete on price with commodity DRAM; HBM-PIM competes on HBM cost premiums. Until the software ecosystem matures, PIM risks the classic accelerator trap: superior hardware waiting for software that never arrives.

## 7. Conclusion

Processing-in-memory has crossed the threshold from fifty years of proposals to shipping silicon, and the two flagship systems define the field's axes: **UPMEM's DPUs** prove that thousands of general-purpose cores can live inside DRAM and be programmed today with C, an LLVM toolchain, and a disciplined scratchpad-centric model; **Samsung's HBM-PIM** proves that bank-level parallelism can be packaged as a drop-in HBM replacement delivering 2.5× system performance on real AI accelerators. The PrIM suite provides the empirical method — microbenchmark the primitives, validate the programming rules, compare against strong baselines — and its results confirm the theory: PIM wins precisely where the memory wall bites hardest.

The remaining work is systems work, not circuits work: coherence protocols that span the memory bus, virtual memory for in-situ cores, inter-PIM communication fabrics, cost models for host/device partitioning, and the compilers and query engines that will make PIM invisible to application programmers. The von Neumann bottleneck will not be legislated away; it will be *routed around*, one bank at a time.
