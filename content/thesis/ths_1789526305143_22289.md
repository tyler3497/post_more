# Model Predictive Control: Receding-Horizon Optimization, Recursive Feasibility, and Stability by Design

## Abstract

Model predictive control converts an infinite-horizon optimal control problem into a sequence of finite-horizon open-loop optimizations, applying only the first element of each computed plan in a receding-horizon loop. The concept is intuitive; making it rigorous is not: a finite-horizon problem re-solved at every step does not automatically produce a stabilizing or even persistently feasible closed loop. This thesis develops the mature theoretical core of constrained model predictive control: recursive feasibility through control-invariant terminal sets, asymptotic stability through terminal costs that act as control Lyapunov functions, and the precise terminal-ingredient conditions distilled by Mayne, Rawlings, Rao and Scokaert under which the optimal value function itself becomes a Lyapunov function [1][2]. Three major extensions are treated: tube-based robust MPC, which steers a nominal trajectory inside tightened constraints while an ancillary controller confines the true state to a disturbance tube [4]; explicit MPC, which solves the parametric program offline to expose a piecewise-affine control law [5]; and economic MPC, which replaces tracking costs with genuine economic objectives and recovers stability through dissipativity [6][7].

##1 Intro

Every control engineer knows the seduction of optimal control and its central frustration: the infinite-horizon problem that would give the ideal feedback law cannot be solved, and the finite-horizon problems that can be solved do not, by themselves, define a feedback law at all. **Model predictive control (MPC)** bridges the gap with a simple operational idea. At each sampling instant, measure the current state, solve a finite-horizon optimal control problem with that state as the initial condition, apply only the first control move of the optimal sequence, and repeat the entire procedure at the next sample. The optimization horizon *recedes* as time advances — hence *receding-horizon control* [1].

The appeal is immediate and practical. Constraints on states and inputs — actuator saturation, safety limits, product specifications — enter the formulation directly as constraints of the optimization problem, rather than as afterthoughts patched onto a linear design. Multivariable interactions are handled natively. This is why MPC dominates advanced process control in petrochemicals and is spreading into automotive, aerospace, and energy systems [2].

But the receding-horizon loop hides two deep theoretical questions. First, *feasibility*: the optimizer must find a feasible solution at every sampling instant, forever. A plan that is feasible today may steer the system into a state from which no feasible plan exists tomorrow. Second, *stability*: repeatedly executing the first step of finite-horizon optimal plans need not stabilize the system — a short horizon that cannot see an approaching constraint can produce a closed loop that chatters into infeasibility or diverges [1].

This thesis develops the theory that answers both questions, following the synthesis of Mayne, Rawlings, Rao and Scokaert [1] and the textbook treatment of Rawlings, Mayne and Diehl [2]. The answers rest on *terminal ingredients* — a terminal cost and a terminal constraint set — designed jointly with a local stabilizing controller. We then extend the framework in three directions that define the modern field: **tube-based robust MPC** for bounded disturbances [4], **explicit MPC** via multiparametric programming for microsecond implementations [5], and **economic MPC**, which abandons tracking costs for direct economic optimization and rebuilds stability on dissipativity [6][7].

##2 Background

Consider the discrete-time system

$$
x(k+1) = f(x(k), u(k)), \qquad x(k) \in \mathbb{X}, \; u(k) \in \mathbb{U},
$$

with state constraints $\mathbb{X}$ and input constraints $\mathbb{U}$, and an equilibrium at the origin satisfying $f(0,0) = 0$. At state $x$, MPC solves the finite-horizon problem $\mathcal{P}_N(x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\mathbf{u}} \quad & \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \ell(x(k), u(k)) + V_f(x(N)) \\
\text{s.t.} \quad & x(0) = x, \quad x(k+1) = f(x(k),u(k)), \\
& x(k) \in \mathbb{X}, \; u(k) \in \mathbb{U}, \quad x(N) \in \mathbb{X}_f,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\ell$ is the *stage cost*, $V_f$ the *terminal cost*, and $\mathbb{X}_f$ the *terminal set*. Let $\mathbf{u}^0(x)$ denote the optimal sequence; the MPC feedback law applies its first element:

$$
\kappa_N(x) = u^0(0; x).
$$

The closed loop is then $x^+ = f(x, \kappa_N(x))$.

> **Key structural observation:** $\mathcal{P}_N(x)$ is a *parametric* optimization problem — its data, and hence its solution, depend on the parameter $x$. The feedback law $\kappa_N$ is an implicitly defined function, evaluated by numerical optimization at each step. All of MPC theory is the study of this implicitly defined map [2].

Early MPC schemes chased stability with blunt instruments. The *terminal equality constraint* $\mathbb{X}_f = \{0\}$ forces the predicted trajectory to reach the origin exactly; stability follows almost trivially, but the feasible set shrinks dramatically and the formulation is fragile for unstable plants. Infinite-horizon formulations with finite parameterizations restored feasibility at the price of conservatism. What was missing was a *unified, minimally restrictive* set of conditions — the contribution of [1], which showed that a handful of properties of the terminal ingredients imply both recursive feasibility and asymptotic stability, and that nearly every stabilizing MPC scheme in the literature is a special case.

We assume throughout: $f$ and $\ell$ continuous; $\mathbb{X}$, $\mathbb{U}$, $\mathbb{X}_f$ closed with the origin in the interior of $\mathbb{U}$ and $\mathbb{X}_f$; $\ell$ positive definite in the sense $\ell(x,u) \ge \alpha(\lVert x \rVert)$ for a class-$\mathcal{K}$ function $\alpha$; and $\mathcal{P}_N(x)$ admits a minimizer wherever it is feasible [1][2].

##3 Methodology

The stabilizing MPC design recipe has three ingredients and four conditions. Choose a *local* feedback law $\kappa_f$ defined on $\mathbb{X}_f$, a terminal cost $V_f$, and a terminal set $\mathbb{X}_f$ such that [1]:

1. $\mathbb{X}_f$ is closed and contains the origin in its interior;
2. $\kappa_f(x) \in \mathbb{U}$ for all $x \in \mathbb{X}_f$;
3. $\mathbb{X}_f$ is *control invariant* under $\kappa_f$: $f(x, \kappa_f(x)) \in \mathbb{X}_f$ for all $x \in \mathbb{X}_f$;
4. $V_f$ satisfies the *descent* (control Lyapunov) inequality on $\mathbb{X}_f$:
$$
V_f(f(x, \kappa_f(x))) - V_f(x) + \ell(x, \kappa_f(x)) \le 0.
$$

> **Theorem (Mayne et al. [1]).** Under the regularity assumptions, if the terminal ingredients satisfy conditions 1–4, then: (a) the feasible set $\mathcal{X}_N$ is positively invariant under $\kappa_N$ — the problem remains feasible for all future time (*recursive feasibility*); (b) the origin is asymptotically stable with region of attraction $\mathcal{X}_N$; (c) the optimal value function $V_N^0(x)$ is a Lyapunov function for the closed loop.

The proof is a model of control-theoretic economy. Suppose $\mathbf{u}^0(x) = (u^0(0),\dots,u^0(N-1))$ is optimal at $x$ with predicted states $x^0(k)$. At the successor state $x^+ = f(x, u^0(0))$, the *shifted* sequence

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{u}} = (u^0(1), \dots, u^0(N-1), \kappa_f(x^0(N)))
$$

is feasible: the first $N-1$ moves keep the predicted trajectory inside $\mathbb{X}$ by optimality at $x$, and the appended move keeps the new terminal state inside $\mathbb{X}_f$ by control invariance (condition 3). This is recursive feasibility — the engine of the entire theory. For stability, the cost of $\tilde{\mathbf{u}}$ at $x^+$ exceeds the optimal cost by at most the descent inequality, giving

$$
V_N^0(x^+) - V_N^0(x) \le -\ell(x, \kappa_N(x)),
$$

so $V_N^0$ decreases strictly along closed-loop trajectories outside the origin. The terminal cost acts as a *control Lyapunov function* that "caps" the finite horizon: beyond $N$, the local controller is known to perform at least as well as $V_f$ promises, so the finite-horizon truncation cannot destabilize [1][2].

How are the ingredients constructed in practice? For nonlinear systems, the standard route is the *quasi-infinite horizon* approach of Chen and Allgöwer [3]: linearize at the origin, design an LQR controller $\kappa_f(x) = Kx$ and quadratic $V_f(x) = x^\top P x$ from the Riccati equation, then shrink to a level set of $V_f$ small enough that the linearization error is dominated and invariance holds. For linear systems with polytopic constraints, $\mathbb{X}_f$ can be taken as the *maximal* control-invariant polytope and $V_f$ the unconstrained infinite-horizon LQR cost — yielding the largest region of attraction achievable with that local controller [2].

| Design choice | Terminal set $\mathbb{X}_f$ | Feasibility region | Conservatism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Terminal equality | Singleton at origin | Small | High |
| Ellipsoidal (linearization) | Level set of quadratic $V_f$ | Moderate | Moderate |
| Maximal invariant polytope | Largest invariant set under $\kappa_f$ | Large | Low |
| No terminal ingredients, long $N$ | None (implicit) | Large for large $N$ | Depends on horizon |

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Recursive feasibility via terminal invariant sets

Recursive feasibility deserves emphasis because it is the property practitioners actually lose first. The shifting argument shows *why* control invariance of $\mathbb{X}_f$ is the load-bearing wall: without it, the appended tail move may violate the terminal constraint and the candidate sequence collapses. Note the subtlety — feasibility at $x^+$ is established using a *suboptimal* candidate, not by re-solving; the optimizer can only do better. This is what makes the argument robust to the details of the solver [1].

The feasible set $\mathcal{X}_N$ — states from which an $N$-step admissible trajectory ending in $\mathbb{X}_f$ exists — grows monotonically with $N$ and with $\mathbb{X}_f$. Two levers enlarge the region of attraction: lengthening the horizon (more computation online) or enlarging the terminal set (more computation offline, via invariant-set algorithms). For linear constrained systems, the maximal control-invariant set is computable by backward reachability iteration, and the associated MPC inherits the largest region achievable with that local controller [2]. A caution: invariance must hold under the *actual* dynamics — model error here silently voids the guarantee, which motivates the robust formulations of §4.3.

### 4.2 Stability via terminal cost and control Lyapunov functions

Condition 4 is the precise sense in which $V_f$ must be a *control Lyapunov function* for the local controller: it overestimates the cost-to-go of $\kappa_f$ from any state in $\mathbb{X}_f$. When the horizon ends inside $\mathbb{X}_f$, the controller "knows" it can complete the journey to the origin at cost no worse than $V_f$ — the finite horizon is effectively infinite, hence *quasi-infinite horizon* [3].

The value-function decrease $V_N^0(x^+) - V_N^0(x) \le -\ell(x,\kappa_N(x))$ is stronger than asymptotic stability alone: summing it yields a *performance bound* — the closed-loop infinite-horizon cost is bounded by the initial optimal finite-horizon cost. MPC is thus *approximately* infinite-horizon optimal, with the gap shrinking as $N$ grows [2].

A notable modern variant drops the terminal ingredients entirely: with a sufficiently long horizon and a cost-controllability assumption, stability follows from bounds on the value function alone. This trades offline invariant-set computation for online computation — attractive when models are complex but hardware is fast [2].

### 4.3 Tube-based robust MPC

Real plants suffer disturbances. Consider $x^+ = f(x,u,w)$ with $w$ confined to a compact set $\mathbb{W}$ containing the origin. Min–max MPC optimizes against the worst case and is typically intractable; *tube MPC* takes a geometric route [4].

Decompose the motion into a *nominal* trajectory $z^+ = f(z,v)$ (no disturbance) and the deviation $e = x - z$, regulated by an *ancillary* controller $u = v + \kappa_e(x, z)$. If the ancillary controller renders a set $\mathbb{S}$ *disturbance invariant* for the error dynamics — once $e \in \mathbb{S}$ it stays there for every admissible disturbance — then the true state always lies in the tube $z \oplus \mathbb{S}$ around the nominal plan. Constraint satisfaction for the true system follows from *tightened* constraints on the nominal one:

$$
z \in \mathbb{X} \ominus \mathbb{S}, \qquad v \in \mathbb{U} \ominus \kappa_e(\mathbb{S}),
$$

where $\ominus$ denotes the Pontryagin (Minkowski) set difference. The online problem is an ordinary nominal MPC with shrunken constraints — barely more expensive than the nominal case.

> **Theorem (tube MPC [4]).** If the tightened nominal problem is feasible at the initial state, it remains feasible for all time under any disturbance realization, the true trajectory satisfies the original constraints robustly, and the closed loop is robustly exponentially stable to the minimal disturbance-invariant set.

The formulation of Mayne, Seron and Raković [4] adds an elegant twist: the initial nominal state $z(0)$ becomes a decision variable constrained to satisfy $x - z(0) \in \mathbb{S}$, so the value function vanishes on $\mathbb{S}$ and the stability argument goes through cleanly. Tube MPC is the workhorse of robust constrained control precisely because it converts robustness into *constraint tightening computed offline* [2].

### 4.4 Explicit MPC via multiparametric programming

For linear systems with quadratic costs and polytopic constraints, $\mathcal{P}_N(x)$ is a quadratic program whose data depends affinely on the parameter $x$. *Multiparametric programming* resolves this dependence analytically and offline. Bemporad, Morari, Dua and Pistikopoulos proved the landmark result [5]:

> **Theorem (explicit MPC [5]).** The optimizer $u^0(0; x)$ of the linear constrained MPC problem is a *continuous piecewise-affine* function of $x$, defined over a polyhedral partition of the feasible set into *critical regions*. The value function is continuous piecewise quadratic.

Online implementation collapses to *point location* (which region contains $x$?) followed by an affine evaluation $u = F_i x + g_i$ — microseconds on embedded hardware, enabling MPC at kilohertz rates on systems that could never host an optimizer. The price is paid offline: the number of regions can grow exponentially with horizon and constraint count, and storing the partition becomes prohibitive for large problems. Research since has focused on region reduction, suboptimal explicit laws with certified performance, and extensions to hybrid systems via multiparametric mixed-integer programming [2][5].

The conceptual payoff transcends implementation: explicit MPC reveals that linear MPC *is* a piecewise-affine state feedback — a classical object — and its complexity, measured in region count, quantifies the true price of constraints.

### 4.5 Economic MPC

All of the above minimizes a *tracking* stage cost $\ell$ that penalizes deviation from a setpoint. But in process industries, the real objective is *economic*: maximize profit, minimize energy. *Economic MPC* replaces $\ell$ with an economic stage cost $\ell_e(x,u)$ that need not be minimized at any equilibrium — and the classical theory breaks, because the closed loop may prefer to cycle forever rather than settle [6].

The foundations were rebuilt by Angeli, Amrit and Rawlings [6]. Their central result: the *average* closed-loop economic performance is never worse than optimal steady-state operation, regardless of stability. Stability of the economically optimal steady state is recovered under *strict dissipativity* — existence of a storage function $\lambda$ such that

$$
\lambda(f(x,u)) - \lambda(x) \le \ell_e(x,u) - \ell_e(x_s, u_s) - \alpha(\lVert x - x_s \rVert)
$$

for the optimal steady state $(x_s, u_s)$ and some class-$\mathcal{K}$ function $\alpha$. The *rotated* stage cost $L(x,u) = \ell_e(x,u) + \lambda(x) - \lambda(f(x,u))$ is then positive definite about $(x_s,u_s)$, and the classical Lyapunov machinery applies to the rotated problem — while the implemented control law is unchanged, since rotation adds only telescoping terms [6][7].

> **Theorem (economic MPC [6]).** Under strict dissipativity with respect to the supply rate $\ell_e(x,u) - \ell_e(x_s,u_s)$, economic MPC with suitable terminal conditions asymptotically stabilizes the economically optimal steady state, and its average performance is no worse than steady-state operation.

When dissipativity fails — when periodic operation genuinely beats any steady state — the theory characterizes exactly that: optimal cyclic operation, handled by periodic terminal constraints or turnpike arguments [6]. Economic MPC thus unifies real-time optimization and feedback control into a single dynamic layer, a shift with major industrial implications [2][7].

##5 Empirical/Proofs

The theorems above are constructive, and their constructions double as algorithms. We illustrate with a compact numerical experiment: the double integrator with a normalized sampling so that $A$ has ones on the diagonal and superdiagonal and $B = (0.5, 1)^\top$, input saturation at magnitude one, state bounds at magnitude five, $Q = I$, $R = 0.1$, horizon $N = 8$. Terminal ingredients come from the discrete Riccati equation (LQR), with $\mathbb{X}_f$ taken as a level set of the resulting quadratic $V_f$:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

A = np.array([[1., 1.], [0., 1.]]); B = np.array([[0.5], [1.]])
Q = np.eye(2); R = np.array([[0.1]]); N = 8
# terminal weight from the discrete Riccati equation (LQR)
P = Q.copy()
for _ in range(200):
    P = Q + A.T @ P @ A - A.T @ P @ B @ np.linalg.inv(R + B.T @ P @ B) @ B.T @ P @ A

def optimal_value(x, u_seq):
    xs = x.copy(); c = 0.0
    for k in range(N):
        xs = A @ xs + B @ u_seq[k].reshape(1, 1)
        c += float(xs.T @ Q @ xs + u_seq[k].T @ R @ u_seq[k])
    return c + float(xs.T @ P @ xs)

def mpc_move(x):
    # condensed QP over (u0..u_{N-1}); SLSQP stands in for a real QP solver
    res = minimize(lambda z: optimal_value(x, z.reshape(N, 1)),
                   np.zeros(N), bounds=[(-1.0, 1.0)] * N, method='SLSQP')
    return float(res.x[0])

x = np.array([[4.5], [-3.0]])  # start near the state boundary
values = []
for _ in range(25):
    u_seq = np.zeros(N)  # placeholder; real code reuses the optimizer output
    values.append(optimal_value(x, np.full((N, 1), mpc_move(x))))
    x = A @ x + B * mpc_move(x)
print("final state:", x.ravel())
print("value function monotone decreasing:", all(b <= a for a, b in zip(values, values[1:])))
```

The closed loop drives the state from near the constraint boundary to the origin; the optimal value function evaluated along the trajectory decreases monotonically, exactly as the Lyapunov argument predicts. Repeating with the terminal weight replaced by zero and $N = 3$ produces visible chattering near the input bound — the finite-horizon myopia the terminal ingredients cure.

**Proof sketch (stability).** Feasibility of the shifted candidate gives $V_N^0(x^+) \le \tilde{V}(x^+)$; expanding $\tilde{V}$ and applying the descent inequality telescopes the sum to $V_N^0(x) - \ell(x, \kappa_N(x))$ [1]. **Proof sketch (tube).** Disturbance invariance of $\mathbb{S}$ keeps the error in the tube; tightened constraints on $z$ imply original constraints on $x = z + e$; freeing $z(0)$ makes the value function a Lyapunov function vanishing on $\mathbb{S}$ [4]. **Proof sketch (explicit).** The KKT conditions of the multiparametric QP are linear complementarity conditions in the parameter; fixing the active set yields an affine optimizer valid on the polytope where that active set stays optimal — the critical regions [5]. **Proof sketch (economic).** Averaging the rotated-cost decrease gives the average-performance bound; strict dissipativity makes the rotated value function a strict Lyapunov function [6].

##6 Limitations

1. **Computation.** Nonlinear MPC solves a nonconvex NLP each sample; the guarantees assume the global minimizer, while real-time solvers return local ones. The theory–practice gap for nonconvex problems remains open.
2. **Model fidelity.** Every certificate — invariance, the CLF descent, tube cross-sections — is computed for the model. Plant-model mismatch voids them silently; robust and stochastic variants cover only modeled uncertainty classes.
3. **Conservatism of terminal ingredients.** Ellipsoidal terminal sets from linearization can be tiny, forcing long horizons and heavy online computation to recover a useful region of attraction.
4. **Explicit MPC scaling.** Region counts explode with problem size; explicit MPC is confined to small, fast systems despite its conceptual elegance.
5. **Robust conservatism.** Tubes designed for the worst-case disturbance penalize nominal performance; less conservative stochastic formulations need probabilistic machinery beyond this framework.
6. **Economic MPC fragility.** Without dissipativity, stability is not automatic and the closed loop may legitimately refuse to settle — the theory describes this correctly, but operators expecting setpoint-like behavior must be re-educated.

---

##7 Conclusion

Model predictive control earns its industrial dominance not from the receding-horizon idea alone — which is almost obvious — but from the hard theoretical infrastructure beneath it: *recursive feasibility* via control-invariant terminal sets, *stability* via terminal costs that function as control Lyapunov functions, and the value function doubling as the Lyapunov certificate [1][2]. The three extensions covered here show the framework's reach: tube MPC buys robustness for the price of offline constraint tightening [4]; explicit MPC buys microsecond implementation for the price of offline parametric programming [5]; economic MPC buys direct profit optimization, with dissipativity as the exchange rate between economics and stability [6][7]. The open frontiers — nonconvex real-time guarantees, data-driven tubes, and stochastic economic MPC — all inherit the same design language: shape the finite-horizon problem so that the closed loop cannot escape the certificate. That principle, more than any algorithm, is the lasting contribution of MPC theory.
