# Adaptive Deep Brain Stimulation for Parkinson's Disease: Beta-Band Biomarkers, Closed-Loop Control Algorithms, and Network-Level Targets

## Abstract

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) of the subthalamic nucleus (STN) is an established therapy for medication-refractory Parkinson's disease (PD), yet conventional open-loop stimulation delivers fixed parameters regardless of fluctuating clinical state, risking side effects and inefficient energy use. Exaggerated beta-band (13-30 Hz) oscillations in STN local field potentials constitute a validated electrophysiological biomarker of the parkinsonian state, attenuated by both dopaminergic medication and therapeutic DBS [1]. This thesis examines the transition from open-loop to closed-loop neuromodulation: adaptive DBS (aDBS) systems that modulate stimulation amplitude or duty cycle from real-time beta biomarkers. We formalize threshold and proportional-integral control policies, analyze beta-burst temporal dynamics as the physiologically relevant control variable, and review clinical evidence from peri-operative studies, chronic sensing neurostimulators, and the ADAPT-PD trial that supported the 2025 FDA approval of Medtronic BrainSense adaptive DBS [10]. We further situate aDBS within a network framework - sweet-spot mapping, directional leads, hyperdirect pathway connectivity [7][8] - and discuss limitations and future multi-biomarker controllers.

---

## 1 Introduction

Conventional deep brain stimulation for Parkinson's disease delivers high-frequency electrical pulses (typically 130-180 Hz) to the subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus internus (GPi) through chronically implanted electrodes. While continuous open-loop DBS markedly improves bradykinesia, rigidity, and tremor, its parameters are fixed: the device neither senses the patient's neural state nor adapts to the medication cycle, sleep-wake transitions, or moment-to-moment fluctuations in motor disability. The consequences are threefold. First, stimulation delivered when not needed wastes battery and invites stimulation-induced side effects such as dysarthria, gait freezing, and dyskinesia. Second, fixed dosing cannot respond to levodopa-on dyskinesia, where stimulation should ideally be reduced. Third, programming remains an empirical, clinician-intensive process.

The discovery that exaggerated beta-band oscillations in basal ganglia local field potentials (LFPs) track the parkinsonian state - suppressed by dopaminergic therapy and by effective DBS - supplied the missing feedback signal [1][2]. Because beta power can be recorded chronically from the very electrodes used for stimulation (via sensing neurostimulators such as the Medtronic Percept PC), it became possible to close the loop: a controller estimates the patient's clinical state from beta dynamics and adapts stimulation in real time [1][10]. This thesis develops that program systematically. We begin with the neurophysiology of beta oscillations and burst dynamics, formalize the control-theoretic core of adaptive DBS, survey the clinical evidence through the ADAPT-PD trial, and embed the technology in a network view of DBS in which *where* to stimulate is as important as *when* [7].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Beta Oscillopathy of Parkinson's Disease

Recordings from the STN of PD patients reveal pathologically synchronized oscillations in the beta band, conventionally defined as 13-30 Hz (some authors extend the range to 11-35 Hz) [1][4]. Beta power correlates with contralateral bradykinesia and rigidity severity; it rises in the medication-off state and falls with levodopa and with therapeutic high-frequency DBS [1][2]. Crucially, beta-band stimulation of the STN *worsens* bradykinesia and boosts cortical beta lateralization, supporting a *causal* - not merely epiphenomenal - role of beta in motor slowing [4].

Yet mean beta power is a coarse statistic. High-temporal-resolution analysis shows beta arrives in **bursts** of varying duration and amplitude. Short bursts (<200 ms) appear physiological; *prolonged* bursts (greater than ~400 ms) correlate with motor impairment, gait disturbance, and freezing [2][3]. Beta amplitude grows in proportion to burst duration, consistent with progressively increasing synchronization of the STN population [2]. This burst structure matters for control design: a controller that responds to long bursts specifically will differ mechanistically from one that merely averages power.

### 2.2 From Open-Loop to Closed-Loop Stimulation

Conventional DBS (cDBS) applies continuous pulses regardless of state. Adaptive DBS (aDBS) instead implements the feedback loop

> **Definition (Closed-loop DBS).** A neurostimulation system is *closed-loop* if a measured neural or behavioral signal $x(t)$ is mapped through a feature extractor $\Phi$ and a control policy $\pi$ to stimulation parameters $u(t) = \pi(\Phi(x(t)))$, delivered back to the brain in real time.

The canonical instantiation uses $x(t)$ = STN LFP, $\Phi$ = beta-band power (or burst duration), and $u(t)$ = stimulation amplitude [1][5][9]. Early proof-of-concept studies externalized the leads in the peri-operative period and showed that beta-triggered aDBS could match cDBS on bradykinesia while delivering substantially less stimulation energy [1][2]. Fully implanted bidirectional brain-computer interfaces (e.g., Medtronic Activa PC+S) then demonstrated chronic feasibility, and in 2025 the FDA approved BrainSense adaptive DBS - the first adaptive system for PD - on the basis of the ADAPT-PD trial [10].

### 2.3 Anatomical and Network Context

STN, GPi, and the ventral intermediate nucleus of the thalamus (VIM, for tremor) are the canonical targets. Modern DBS is increasingly understood as a *network therapy*: efficacy depends on modulating specific circuits, particularly hyperdirect cortico-subthalamic fibers and the dentato-rubro-thalamic tract for tremor [7][8]. Sweet-spot mapping - voxelwise regression of modeled volumes of tissue activated (VTA) against clinical improvement - identifies optimal stimulation zones, while directional leads steer the field toward therapeutic fibers and away from the internal capsule [7]. Adaptive control (the *temporal* dimension) and network targeting (the *spatial* dimension) are complementary: the ideal future device steers both where and when.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis synthesizes computational modeling, signal processing, control theory, and clinical trial evidence. Our methodological contributions are: (i) a formal taxonomy of aDBS control policies; (ii) a reference implementation of beta-burst detection; (iii) integration of VTA-based spatial targeting with closed-loop temporal control.

### 3.1 Biomarker Estimation Pipeline

A typical aDBS signal chain is: bipolar LFP recording from non-stimulating contacts -> artifact rejection (template subtraction or blanking) -> band-pass filtering (13-30 Hz) -> rectification and smoothing (e.g., 200-400 ms moving average) -> thresholding or burst segmentation. Smoothing-window choice trades responsiveness against noise: a 200 ms window tracks fast beta dynamics, though behavioral benefit over 400 ms smoothing is not guaranteed [1].

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import butter, filtfilt

def beta_envelope(lfp, fs, band=(13, 30), smooth_ms=400):
    """Estimate smoothed beta-band amplitude from STN LFP."""
    b, a = butter(4, [band[0]/(fs/2), band[1]/(fs/2)], btype='band')
    beta = filtfilt(b, a, lfp)
    rect = np.abs(beta)
    win = int(fs * smooth_ms / 1000)
    kernel = np.ones(win) / win
    return np.convolve(rect, kernel, mode='same')

def detect_bursts(envelope, fs, thr_percentile=75, min_gap_ms=50):
    """Segment beta bursts exceeding a percentile threshold."""
    thr = np.percentile(envelope, thr_percentile)
    above = envelope > thr
    # label contiguous segments; merge gaps shorter than min_gap_ms
    idx = np.flatnonzero(above)
    bursts, start, prev = [], idx[0], idx[0]
    for i in idx[1:]:
        if (i - prev) / fs * 1000 > min_gap_ms:
            bursts.append((start / fs, prev / fs))
            start = i
        prev = i
    bursts.append((start / fs, prev / fs))
    durations = [b - a for a, b in bursts]
    return bursts, durations
```

Burst-duration histograms then separate pathological long bursts (>400 ms) from physiological short ones [2][3][5].

### 3.2 Control Policies: A Taxonomy

| Policy | Law | Biomarker | Timescale | Clinical evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single-threshold (bang-bang) | $u = u_{\max}$ if $\beta > \theta$, else $u_{\min}$ | Smoothed beta power | ~400 ms | Superior to cDBS for progressive bradykinesia in 60-min chronic tests [1] |
| Dual-threshold (proportional band) | $u$ ramps linearly between $\theta_{lo}$, $\theta_{hi}$ | Beta power | minutes | ADAPT-PD mode; tracks medication fluctuations [10] |
| Proportional-integral (PI) | $u = K_p e + K_i \int e\,dt$, $e=\beta-\beta^*$ | Beta power error | seconds | Simulated; suppresses pathological beta with stability guarantees [5] |
| Burst-duration triggered | stimulate while burst $> \tau_{crit}$ | Burst duration | ~100 ms | Benchtop + pilot human data; targets specifically pathological bursts [3] |
| Amplitude-modulated aDBS | continuous $u(t) \propto \beta(t)$ | Instantaneous beta | real-time | Model: restores beta to normal range with less total current than cDBS [9] |

> **Theorem (Bounded adaptive regulation).** *Let $e(t) = \beta(t) - \beta^*$ be the beta-tracking error and let $u(t) \in [u_{\min}, u_{\max}]$ be generated by a threshold policy with hysteresis $h > 0$ and slew-rate limit $|\dot u| \le \rho$. Then (i) the stimulation envelope remains bounded for all $t$; (ii) chattering across the threshold is impossible, since re-triggering requires $\beta$ to traverse the full hysteresis band; (iii) if stimulation monotonically suppresses $\beta$ with gain $\gamma > 0$, the mean error satisfies $|\bar e| \le h/\gamma + O(\rho^{-1})$.*

*Proof sketch.* Boundedness follows from saturation. Hysteresis prevents limit cycles of arbitrarily high frequency because each switch dissipates the band $h$ of signal variation; with finite slew rate, switching frequency is bounded by $\rho/(u_{\max}-u_{\min})$. The mean-error bound follows from standard averaging of the relay-with-hysteresis characteristic against the monotone plant $\beta(u)$. $\square$

### 3.3 Spatial Targeting Methodology

The temporal controller assumes a well-placed lead. VTA models - finite-element estimates of the tissue volume exceeding the neural activation threshold - are combined with diffusion-tractography connectivity to define sweet spots: stimulation of dorsolateral STN and adjacent hyperdirect fibers predicts rigidity/bradykinesia improvement, while fiber-filtering approaches identify the specific white-matter tracts whose modulation explains outcome variance across cohorts [7][8]. Directional leads (segmented contacts) then sculpt the VTA to intersect the sweet spot while sparing the internal capsule [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Beta Bursts as the Mechanistic Control Variable

The decisive refinement of the beta hypothesis is temporal: aDBS and cDBS both suppress mean beta power, but they do so differently. Effective aDBS **truncates long beta bursts**, shifting the burst-duration distribution toward short, low-amplitude events; cDBS globally suppresses beta without changing the duration distribution [2]. Because long bursts - but not short ones - correlate positively with motor impairment, burst truncation explains why aDBS can outperform cDBS on progressive bradykinesia while delivering less energy [1][2]. This motivates burst-duration-triggered controllers that stimulate only while a burst exceeds a critical duration $\tau_{crit}$, evaluated on benchtop playback systems and in pilot human studies [3]. Computational models reproduce the phenomenology: parkinsonian beta emerges as a network resonance of the cortico-basal ganglia-thalamic loop, and high-frequency stimulation desynchronizes it through nonlinear interaction with the pulse train [5][6].

### 4.2 Adaptive Algorithms: From Thresholds to Model-Based Control

The first-generation clinical policy was a single linear discriminator: stimulate at high amplitude when beta power exceeds one threshold [1]. The ADAPT-PD trial evaluated two refinements in 68 patients with STN or GPi leads: a **single-threshold** mode that rapidly suppresses beta bursts, and a **dual-threshold** mode that holds beta within a therapeutic window, adapting gradually to levodopa effects [10]. Single-threshold suits narrow therapeutic windows and stimulation-induced side effects; dual-threshold suits fluctuating medication responses.

Simulation studies extend the design space to continuous controllers. Proportional-integral schemes modulate amplitude from the beta error signal and, in silico, suppress pathological oscillations while retaining thalamic relay reliability - the network-level readout of motor fidelity [5][6]. Amplitude-modulated aDBS of the STN restores beta to the normal range with notably less total injected current than cDBS [9]. The trajectory is clear: from on/off relays toward smooth, model-informed regulators, with formal stability analysis (Section 3.2) replacing heuristic tuning.

### 4.3 Network Targets: Sweet Spots, Directionality, and Hyperdirect Pathways

Temporal adaptation cannot rescue a misplaced lead. Voxelwise VTA-outcome mapping in STN-DBS identifies a dorsolateral sweet spot whose stimulation - and whose hyperdirect connectivity fingerprint to primary motor and premotor cortex - predicts motor improvement [8]. Fiber-filtering across large cohorts shows that modulating specific tracts (hyperdirect pathway, pallidosubthalamic fibers) explains outcome variance better than contact coordinates alone [7]. Directional leads exploit this: steering current toward the sweet spot and away from the internal capsule widens the therapeutic window, reducing dysarthria and capsular side effects [7]. For tremor, the effective network shifts to the dentato-rubro-thalamic tract in VIM/posterior subthalamic area. The emerging synthesis is a *spatiotemporal* prescription: stimulate the right network (spatial) only when the biomarker demands it (temporal).

### 4.4 Beyond Beta: The Expanding Biomarker Landscape

Beta is necessary but not sufficient. Cortical **phase-amplitude coupling** - STN beta phase modulating broadband gamma amplitude - indexes pathological synchrony across the basal ganglia-thalamocortical circuit and informs targeting and closed-loop design [1]. Finely tuned **gamma** oscillations (~60-90 Hz) are prokinetic and are better preserved by aDBS than by cDBS during movement, a mechanistic advantage of delivering less stimulation during voluntary action [1]. Tremor, notably, is less beta-driven and responds less completely to beta-triggered aDBS than to cDBS, motivating additional biomarkers for tremor-dominant phenotypes [1]. Future controllers will likely fuse beta bursts, gamma, and kinematic decoding into multi-input policies - at the cost of greater validation burden.

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Formal Arguments

**Clinical evidence.** (i) In peri-operative externalized-lead studies, beta-triggered aDBS matched or exceeded cDBS on bradykinesia and rigidity with roughly half the stimulation energy, and truncated pathological long bursts selectively [1][2]. (ii) With chronically implanted bidirectional devices, 60 minutes of closed-loop STN DBS was superior to clinically optimized open-loop DBS for progressive bradykinesia [1]. (iii) Beta-band (not high-frequency) STN stimulation causally slows movement and boosts cortical beta, confirming the biomarker's mechanistic status [4]. (iv) The ADAPT-PD randomized crossover trial (n=68, STN or GPi, 10 centers) established safety and effectiveness of chronic single- and dual-threshold aDBS versus cDBS in clinical and home settings, underpinning the 2025 FDA approval of BrainSense adaptive DBS [10].

**Computational evidence.** Cortico-basal ganglia-thalamic network models show amplitude-modulated aDBS suppresses parkinsonian beta while preserving thalamic relay reliability - the formal requirement for faithful motor command transmission - using less current than cDBS [6][9]. A quantitative STN population model predicts stimulation protocols from fitted LFP spectra, offering a patient-specific design tool for adaptive paradigms [5].

**Formal argument for adaptivity.** Consider total injected charge $Q = \int u(t)\,dt$. For any fixed $u_{c}$ achieving mean symptom control, the adaptive policy $u(t) = \pi(\beta(t))$ with $u_{\min} < u_c$ achieves $Q_{aDBS} < Q_{cDBS}$ whenever the biomarker spends nonzero time below threshold - which holds because beta fluctuates with medication and behavior. Since side-effect probability and battery drain are monotone in $Q$, adaptivity Pareto-dominates fixed stimulation *provided* the biomarker faithfully tracks state - the central empirical premise validated above [1][2][10].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Biomarker heterogeneity.** Not all patients exhibit a clean beta peak; akinetic-rigid phenotypes show stronger beta signatures than tremor-dominant ones, and beta desynchronizes physiologically during movement - precisely when a naive controller would withdraw stimulation [1].
2. **Artifact and sensing constraints.** Stimulation artifact contaminates LFP recordings; current systems mitigate this with blanking, template subtraction, and sensing from non-stimulating contacts, at the cost of spatial resolution.
3. **Control latency vs. physiology.** Burst dynamics evolve over hundreds of milliseconds; controllers must act within this window without inducing oscillations in the loop itself [3][5].
4. **Tremor gap.** Beta-triggered aDBS improves tremor over no stimulation but underperforms cDBS for tremor, indicating missing biomarkers [1].
5. **Long-term plasticity unknown.** Whether chronic adaptive stimulation induces compensatory network changes over years - as opposed to hours-to-weeks trial windows - remains unstudied.
6. **Computational and regulatory burden.** Multi-biomarker, model-based controllers increase verification complexity; current approvals cover threshold policies only [10].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Adaptive deep brain stimulation marks the transition of neuromodulation from open-loop dosing to feedback control. The beta-band biomarker - refined from mean power to burst-duration statistics - provides a validated, chronically recordable state estimate of the parkinsonian motor circuit [1][2][3]. Threshold and proportional control policies translate that estimate into stimulation with formal boundedness guarantees, reduced energy delivery, and, in the ADAPT-PD trial, clinical effectiveness sufficient for FDA approval [5][9][10]. Embedded in a network framework of sweet spots, directional leads, and hyperdirect connectivity [7][8], closed-loop DBS becomes a spatiotemporal therapy: the right circuit, stimulated only when needed. The remaining challenges - tremor biomarkers, artifact-robust sensing, long-term plasticity, and multi-input control - define the next decade of neuromodulation engineering.
