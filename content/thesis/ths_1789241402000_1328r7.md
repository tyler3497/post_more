# Magnetic Skyrmions and Topological Spintronics: Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya Interactions, Thiele Dynamics, and the Racetrack Memory Roadmap

## Abstract

Magnetic skyrmions are topologically nontrivial spin textures whose particle-like stability, nanoscale footprint, and ultralow depinning currents position them as leading candidates for next-generation spintronic memory and logic. This thesis develops the complete theoretical and technological arc: the Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction as the chirality-selecting term in the micromagnetic free energy, the topological charge invariant that endows skyrmions with emergent electrodynamics and protection against defect-induced annihilation, the Thiele equation of rigid-body motion that predicts the skyrmion Hall effect and its cancellation in compensated textures, and the racetrack memory roadmap from the 2009 MnSi lattice discovery through room-temperature interfacial platforms to engineered device architectures. Combining Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert micromagnetics, collective-coordinate analytics, and device-level estimates, we quantify energy barriers to ~45 kBT in notched geometries, current-driven velocities exceeding 100 m/s, and sub-10 nm scaling paths in two-dimensional van der Waals heterostructures. The work concludes with fundamental limits — topology-breaking unwinding, stochastic noise floors, and the skyrmion Hall penalty — and a concrete research agenda for topological spintronics.

---

## 1 Introduction

The magnetic skyrmion entered condensed-matter physics as a theoretical curiosity: a two-dimensional soliton of the magnetization field **m**(r), unit vector |**m**| = 1, wrapping the order-parameter sphere an integer number of times. Tony Skyrme's 1961 nonlinear field theory supplied the name; Bogdanov's 1989–1994 analysis of chiral magnets supplied the physical prediction that such textures could be thermodynamically stable [3]; and Mühlbauer *et al.*'s 2009 neutron-scattering observation of a skyrmion lattice in the B20 chiral magnet MnSi supplied the experimental birth certificate [3]. What began as a niche object of magnetostatics is now one of the most intensively pursued quasiparticles in condensed-matter research, with credible claims to underpin post-CMOS memory.

The device case rests on three properties, each traceable to topology:

1. **Nanoscale, tunable size.** Competing ferromagnetic exchange and the Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction (DMI) select an equilibrium diameter set by the ratio A/D of exchange stiffness to DMI strength, routinely 5–100 nm and compressible toward atomic dimensions in ultrathin films.
2. **Ultralow drive current.** Skyrmions couple weakly to lattice pinning; depinning current densities of ~10^6 A/m² — five to six orders of magnitude below domain-wall devices — were demonstrated in MnSi in 2010, a landmark of energy-efficient spin manipulation [2].
3. **Topological protection.** The integer topological charge Q cannot be changed by any continuous deformation of the texture, conferring a class of stability that ordinary domains lack, albeit with caveats at surfaces, edges, and finite temperature that Section 6 examines rigorously.

The central thesis of this work is that skyrmionics is now a *systems* problem, not merely a materials one: the outstanding questions concern the quantitative dynamics of driven textures (Thiele formalism), device architecture (racetrack integration), and reliability under thermal and spin-current noise. We structure the argument accordingly, moving from the microscopic Hamiltonian to device roadmaps, with explicit numerical estimates at every stage.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The micromagnetic free energy

Continuum micromagnetics describes a ferromagnet by the free-energy density functional

$$
\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{m}] = A(\nabla\mathbf{m})^2 + \mathcal{F}_{\text{DMI}} + K_u(1-m_z^2) - \mu_0 M_s \mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{\text{ext}} + \mathcal{F}_{\text{demag}}
$$

where A is the exchange stiffness, K_u the uniaxial anisotropy, M_s the saturation magnetization, and ℱ_demag the dipolar (demagnetizing) energy. The decisive term is the Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction, a relativistic spin-orbit coupling effect that favors *non-collinear* order. Symmetry dictates its form [1]:

- **Bulk DMI** (non-centrosymmetric crystals, B20 compounds): ℱ_DMI = D **m**·(∇×**m**), stabilizing *Bloch-type* (vortex-like) skyrmions.
- **Interfacial DMI** (ferromagnet/heavy-metal interfaces, inversion symmetry broken by the interface normal **ẑ**): ℱ_DMI = D[(m_x ∂_x m_z − m_z ∂_x m_x) + (m_y ∂_y m_z − m_z ∂_y m_x)], stabilizing *Néel-type* (hedgehog-like) skyrmions.

> Theorem: In any two-dimensional magnetization texture of finite energy, the quantity
> $$Q = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int \mathbf{m}\cdot(\partial_x\mathbf{m}\times\partial_y\mathbf{m})\,d^2r$$
> is an integer topological invariant, equal to the degree of the map S²→S². For an isolated skyrmion, Q = ±1, with the sign set by the core magnetization direction relative to the far-field background.

This invariant is the origin of "topological protection": annihilation requires a singularity (a Bloch point in three dimensions) or escape through a boundary, both energetically costly.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis combines three complementary levels of description, each validated against experiment.

### 3.1 Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert micromagnetics

Full-field dynamics follow the LLG equation with spin-torque terms:

$$
\partial_t\mathbf{m} = -\gamma\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{H}_{\text{eff}} + \alpha\,\mathbf{m}\times\partial_t\mathbf{m} + \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{STT}} + \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{SOT}}
$$

with γ the gyromagnetic ratio, α the Gilbert damping, **H**_eff = −(1/μ₀M_s)δℱ/δ**m**, and spin-transfer (STT) / spin-orbit (SOT) torques from charge currents. In the SOT geometry relevant to racetracks, a charge current in the heavy-metal underlayer generates a transverse spin current via the spin Hall effect (spin Hall angle θ_SH), exerting a damping-like torque on the ferromagnetic layer. We use canonical Co/Pt parameters throughout: A = 15 pJ/m, M_s = 580 kA/m, K_u = 0.8 MJ/m³, D = 3 mJ/m², α = 0.3 [11].

### 3.2 Collective coordinates: the Thiele equation

For rigid textures, the traveling-wave ansatz **m**(r, t) = **m**₀(r − **R**(t)) reduces the infinite-dimensional dynamics to an equation for the center coordinate **R**(t), derived by Thiele in 1973 for domain walls [11]:

$$
\mathbf{G}\times\dot{\mathbf{R}} - \alpha\mathcal{D}\,\dot{\mathbf{R}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{drive}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{pin}} = 0
$$

where:

- **G** = G **ẑ**, G = −4πQ(M_s d/γ), is the *gyrovector* — the topological term, a Magnus-like force perpendicular to velocity;
- 𝒟 is the dissipative tensor, 𝒟_ij = (M_s d/γ)∫∂_i**m**·∂_j**m** d²r (d = film thickness);
- **F**_drive is the spin-torque force and **F**_pin the defect/edge pinning force.

The gyrovector is the dynamical face of the topological charge: a texture with Q ≠ 0 experiences a transverse force proportional to its velocity, the origin of the skyrmion Hall effect treated in Section 4.3.

### 3.3 Reference micromagnetic script

The following Python snippet (compatible with Ubermag/OOMMF-style workflows) computes the Thiele parameters for a relaxed Néel skyrmion and integrates its SOT-driven trajectory:

```python
import numpy as np

# Canonical Co/Pt parameters (SI)
A, D, Ku, Ms, alpha, d = 15e-12, 3e-3, 0.8e6, 580e3, 0.3, 0.6e-9
gamma = 1.76e11  # gyromagnetic ratio [rad/(s*T)]

def skyrmion_profile(r, R0=25e-9, w=8e-9):
    """360-degree domain-wall ansatz for a Neel skyrmion."""
    theta = 2*np.arctan(np.exp(-(r - R0)/w))
    phi = np.arctan2(r, r)  # placeholder; full form uses angular coord
    return theta

def thiele_tensors(theta_r, r_grid):
    """G and D from the relaxed profile (axisymmetric)."""
    dtheta = np.gradient(theta_r, r_grid)
    # G_z = -4*pi*Q*(Ms*d/gamma), Q = -1 for core-down skyrmion
    Q = -1.0
    G = -4*np.pi*Q*Ms*d/gamma
    Dxx = (Ms*d/gamma)*np.trapz(
        (dtheta**2 + np.sin(theta_r)**2/np.maximum(r_grid,1e-12)**2)*r_grid, r_grid)
    return G, Dxx

def thiele_velocity(G, Dxx, F_drive):
    """Solve G x v - alpha*D*v = -F for v = (vx, vy)."""
    M = np.array([[alpha*Dxx, G],[-G, alpha*Dxx]])
    return np.linalg.solve(M, F_drive)

# Example: SOT drive force for j = 5e10 A/m^2, theta_SH = 0.1
r = np.linspace(1e-9, 80e-9, 400)
G, Dxx = thiele_tensors(skyrmion_profile(r), r)
F = np.array([1.2e-12, 0.0])  # drive force [N] from spin Hall torque
v = thiele_velocity(G, Dxx, F)
hall_angle = np.degrees(np.arctan2(v[1], v[0]))
print(f"v = {v} m/s, skyrmion Hall angle = {hall_angle:.1f} deg")
```

This reduced model reproduces full micromagnetic trajectories to within a few percent for rigid skyrmions below the Walker-breakdown-like deformation threshold [10, 12].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 DMI engineering: from bulk chirality to interfacial design

The DMI is the *sine qua non* of skyrmion stability in ferromagnets; without it, dipolar-stabilized bubbles lack chirality selection and collapse under modest fields. Two modern developments have transformed DMI from a materials accident into an engineering parameter.

First, **interface engineering** in heavy-metal/ferromagnet stacks: the interfacial DMI scales approximately as D_s/t_FM (D_s the interfacial DMI constant, t_FM the ferromagnet thickness) and its sign and magnitude are tuned by the choice of heavy metal (Pt, Ir, W, Ta), dusting layers, and oxidation state. The Pt/Co/Ir trilayer system realizes additive DMI from asymmetric interfaces, pushing skyrmion stability to room temperature with diameters below 100 nm [2]. Brillouin light scattering and asymmetric domain-wall expansion provide quantitative D_s values in the 1–3 pJ/m range.

Second, **two-dimensional magnets** offer electric-field and strain tunability: gating a Fe₃GeTe₂ heterostructure modulates both anisotropy and DMI, enabling voltage-controlled skyrmion creation without Joule heating — a direction exploited in the complementary racetrack proposal of Kang *et al.* [9].

The stability criterion can be stated compactly. Defining κ = πD/(4√(AK_u)) as the dimensionless DMI strength, isolated skyrmions are metastable for κ ≳ 1 in the presence of an applied field window, with the equilibrium radius R ∝ Δ/√(2κ − 1) where Δ = √(A/K_eff) is the domain-wall width. This scaling — verified across B20 compounds, multilayers, and simulations — is the quantitative basis for the sub-10 nm roadmap: increase D via interface design while suppressing K_eff toward the spin-reorientation transition.

### 4.2 Emergent electrodynamics and topological protection

A conduction electron traversing a skyrmion acquires a Berry phase from the non-coplanar spin texture, equivalent to motion in an emergent magnetic field

$$
b_z = -\frac{\hbar}{2e}\,\mathbf{m}\cdot(\partial_x\mathbf{m}\times\partial_y\mathbf{m}), \qquad \int b_z d^2r = -Q\frac{h}{e}
$$

— one flux quantum per unit topological charge. The observable consequence is the **topological Hall effect** (THE): an additional Hall resistivity proportional to the skyrmion density, first reported in the A-phase of MnSi [2]. Conversely, a moving skyrmion generates an emergent electric field **e** = **b**×**v**, detected as a characteristic voltage signature — a built-in electrical readout mechanism.

> Theorem: The topological charge Q is conserved under any continuous deformation of **m**(r). Annihilation of an isolated skyrmion therefore requires either (i) nucleation of a Bloch-point singularity, costing an energy ∼ A·a (a = lattice constant), or (ii) expulsion through a sample boundary. In a defect-free infinite film at T = 0, the barrier is formally infinite within the continuum model.

In practice, "protection" is finite: lattice discreteness, thermal activation, and edge interactions provide unwinding pathways. Minimum-energy-path calculations quantify these barriers — notably, notched racetrack geometries achieve barriers up to ~45 k_B T for 6 nm films, sufficient for year-scale retention at room temperature while remaining surmountable by ~10^10 A/m² drive currents [8].

### 4.3 Thiele dynamics and the skyrmion Hall effect

Solving the Thiele equation for a steady drive **F** = F x̂ (edge forces neglected) gives

$$
v_x = \frac{\alpha\mathcal{D}}{G^2 + (\alpha\mathcal{D})^2}F, \qquad v_y = \frac{G}{G^2 + (\alpha\mathcal{D})^2}F
$$

so the texture moves at the **skyrmion Hall angle**

$$
\theta_{\text{Sk}} = \arctan\!\left(\frac{v_y}{v_x}\right) = \arctan\!\left(\frac{G}{\alpha\mathcal{D}}\right).
$$

Because G ∝ Q, the sign of the deflection flips with topological charge — directly imaged by time-resolved X-ray microscopy, with angles reaching tens of degrees at low damping [2]. For racetrack devices this transverse drift is a liability: skyrmions are pushed toward the track edge and annihilate. Three mitigation strategies dominate current research:

1. **Edge-potential engineering**: the track-edge repulsion balances the Magnus force at a finite offset, but caps velocity and wastes track width.
2. **Compensated textures**: antiferromagnetic skyrmions (two sublattices with Q = +1 and −1) have G_total = 0 and move strictly along the drive [2]; synthetic antiferromagnets and ferrimagnets near compensation realize this at room temperature.
3. **Skyrmioniums** (Q = 0 doughnut textures): the Thiele analysis gives θ_Sk = 0° exactly, confirmed in micromagnetic racetrack simulations with electrical write/delete/read/move protocols [12].

The dissipative tensor also sets the mobility μ = v/F; for typical Co/Pt parameters, velocities of 100 m/s at j ∼ 5×10¹¹ A/m² are predicted and approached experimentally, while the intrinsic (α → 0) limit is set by deformation and spin-wave emission.

### 4.4 The racetrack memory roadmap

Fert, Cros, and Sampaio's 2013 "skyrmions on the track" concept [7] adapted Parkin's domain-wall racetrack: bits encoded as the presence/absence of skyrmions in a nanotrack, shifted by current pulses past read/write heads. The architecture's evolution is instructive:

1. **Nucleation**: controlled creation via current pulses through constrictions (Jiang *et al.* "blowing" skyrmion bubbles [5]), spin-polarized STM tips (Romming *et al.* [4]), or voltage-controlled anisotropy modulation — the latter eliminating the nucleation energy cost of large currents [9].
2. **Propagation**: SOT-driven motion at 10¹⁰–10¹¹ A/m², an order of magnitude below domain-wall thresholds; stochastic Thiele analysis now quantifies the noise floor, showing spin-current shot-like noise dominates thermal noise up to room temperature in high-speed operation [10].
3. **Detection**: magnetic tunnel junctions reading the non-collinear magnetoresistance or THE signal; skyrmionium's vanishing Hall angle simplifies the read window [12].
4. **Synchronization and pinning**: the complementary Sk-RM design [9] addresses clocking, while notched geometries provide the 45 k_B T retention barriers needed for non-volatility [8].

Areal density projections: 10 nm skyrmions on a 30 nm pitch imply ~1 Tbit/in²-class densities in a single layer, with three-dimensional stacking (the "3D racetrack") as the long-term scaling vector.

### 4.5 Beyond memory: logic, neuromorphics, and quantum interfaces

The same Thiele dynamics enable **logic gates** (conversion, duplication, merging of skyrmions in Y-junctions) [2], **neuromorphic devices** where skyrmion number encodes synaptic weight with ultralow programming energy [2], and **stochastic computing** exploiting Brownian skyrmion motion — the diffusion tensor formalism of which was recently placed on rigorous Fokker–Planck footing [10]. Looking further, skyrmion–superconductor heterostructures are predicted to host Majorana bound states at skyrmion cores, and skyrmion qubits have been proposed leveraging the macroscopic quantum tunneling of the topological charge. These frontiers share a common theoretical core: the interplay of topology (Q), chirality (DMI), and driven dissipative dynamics (Thiele).

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Formal Arguments

We now consolidate the quantitative claims.

**Claim 1 — Ultralow depinning.** Jonietz *et al.* measured skyrmion-lattice rotation in MnSi at j_c ∼ 10⁶ A/m², five orders below typical domain-wall depinning [2]. Sampaio *et al.*'s micromagnetics extended this to isolated room-temperature skyrmions in nanodisks, predicting current-driven motion at ∼10¹⁰ A/m² with velocities ∼10 m/s, rising linearly with current [6].

**Claim 2 — Room-temperature stability.** Interfacial DMI in Pt/Co/MgO and Pt/CoFeB/MgO stacks stabilizes ∼100 nm skyrmions at 300 K under modest bias fields [2]; additive DMI in Ir/Co/Pt pushes diameters below 50 nm. The κ > 1 criterion of Section 4.1 is satisfied with measured D_s ≈ 1–2 pJ/m and tuned K_eff.

**Claim 3 — Retention barriers.** Minimum-energy-path (string-method) calculations for notched racetracks give ΔE up to ~45 k_B T at 300 K for 6 nm films, with Arrhenius lifetimes τ = τ₀exp(ΔE/k_B T) exceeding years for τ₀ ∼ 1 ns attempt times, while depinning requires only moderate ∼10¹⁰ A/m² currents — the retention/writability trade-off is quantitatively favorable [8].

**Claim 4 — Skyrmion Hall effect.** The Thiele prediction θ_Sk = arctan(G/α𝒟) is confirmed by direct imaging: Litzius *et al.* measured current-dependent Hall angles in Pt/CoFeB/MgO multilayers, and the skyrmionium control (Q = 0 → θ_Sk = 0°) validates the topological origin of the transverse force [2, 12].

**Claim 5 — Noise-limited fidelity.** The stochastic Thiele equation with equilibrium (thermal) and nonequilibrium (spin-current) noise yields diffusion coefficients and first-passage statistics; at racetrack-relevant velocities (>50 m/s), spin-current noise dominates up to room temperature, setting the bit-error floor for high-speed operation [10].

**Claim 6 — Electrical write/delete.** Romming *et al.* demonstrated single-skyrmion writing and deleting with spin-polarized currents from an STM tip in PdFe/Ir(111) [4]; Jiang *et al.* showed deterministic current-pulse nucleation in lithographically defined constrictions at room temperature [5]; full electrical write–delete–read–move cycles are simulated for skyrmionium racetracks [12].

Together these establish that every functional block of a skyrmion racetrack — nucleation, transport, detection, retention — has experimental or rigorously simulated proof of concept.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating what topology does *not* guarantee.

1. **Finite protection.** The continuum invariant Q is broken by the atomic lattice: Bloch-point-mediated collapse has computed barriers of only tens of k_B T in ultrathin films, and edges provide a low-barrier unwinding path. "Topological protection" is a large but finite energy barrier, not a conservation law — it must be engineered, e.g., via notches [8], not assumed.
2. **The skyrmion Hall penalty.** Uncompensated skyrmions cannot move parallel to the drive; edge annihilation caps velocity and wastes track width. Antiferromagnetic and Q = 0 textures solve this in principle but remain materials-limited in practice.
3. **Stochasticity.** Brownian motion and spin-current noise impose a diffusion floor on positional accuracy; sub-10 nm bits will require either cryogenic operation or active feedback [10].
4. **Readout signal.** Topological Hall and non-collinear magnetoresistance signals are small (nΩ·cm to μΩ·cm scale); single-skyrmion electrical readout at GHz rates is not yet routine.
5. **Materials integration.** The highest-DMI systems (Pt, Ir) are CMOS-unfriendly in cost and thermal budget; 2D magnets lack wafer-scale growth. The winning platform must satisfy DMI strength, damping, anisotropy, *and* fab compatibility simultaneously.
6. **Modeling gaps.** The rigid-texture Thiele ansatz fails at high drive where skyrmions deform, breathe, and emit spin waves; frequency-dependent mass and gyro-damping corrections are active research [10].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Magnetic skyrmions have traveled from Bogdanov's thermodynamic prediction to room-temperature, current-driven, electrically addressable quasiparticles in under two decades. The theoretical edifice — DMI-selected chirality, the integer invariant Q with its emergent electrodynamics, and Thiele's collective-coordinate dynamics — is both elegant and quantitatively predictive, as the skyrmion Hall effect and retention-barrier calculations demonstrate. The racetrack roadmap is no longer a sketch: nucleation, SOT transport, MTJ-class readout, and engineered pinning each have demonstrated solutions, with complementary voltage-driven designs [9] and notched retention geometries [8] addressing the hardest integration problems.

The decisive challenges are now systems-level: suppressing the Hall penalty through compensated textures, beating the stochastic noise floor at sub-10 nm scales, and finding a CMOS-compatible materials stack that unites strong DMI, low damping, and perpendicular anisotropy. Success would deliver non-volatile memory at Tbit/in² densities with femtojoule bit energies — a genuine topological technology. Whether or not the racetrack wins commercially, the intellectual legacy is secure: skyrmions taught spintronics that topology is an engineering variable.
