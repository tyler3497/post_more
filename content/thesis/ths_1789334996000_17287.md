# Oblivious RAM and Private Information Retrieval: Path ORAM, Lattice-Based PIR, and the Cryptography of Private Access

## Abstract

We study the cryptography of *private access*: how to read and write remote data without revealing *which* data was touched. Encryption hides content; the access pattern itself leaks. Oblivious RAM (ORAM), introduced by Goldreich and Ostrovsky [1], compiles arbitrary RAM programs into access-pattern-independent simulations at Ω(log N) bandwidth overhead [1][7]. Private Information Retrieval (PIR), introduced by Chor, Goldreich, Kushilevitz, and Sudan [6], asks the weaker read-only question; it is impossible with information-theoretic privacy on a single server, but becomes possible under computational assumptions [6] or with non-colluding replicated servers. We survey the trajectory from the O(log³ N) hierarchical ORAM to Path ORAM's elegant tree construction [2], from quadratic-residuosity PIR to lattice-based systems — SealPIR's compressed queries [5], OnionPIR's 4.2× response overhead [3], and Spiral's FHE-composition throughput [4] — and examine empirical evidence, formal lower bounds, and the open problems that remain, including private contact discovery and preprocessing-based PIR [8].

## 1 Intro

When a client outsources storage to an untrusted server, classical encryption protects the *contents* of each block but says nothing about the *sequence* of locations accessed. That sequence is data: a physician querying a medical database reveals the patient's condition through the index touched; a stock trader reveals their portfolio through the symbols fetched [6]. The branch of cryptography that hides *access* rather than *content* splits into two canonical primitives.

**Oblivious RAM (ORAM)** [1] simulates an entire RAM program — reads and writes — so that the physical access pattern observed by the server is statistically independent of the logical one. **Private Information Retrieval (PIR)** [6] addresses the read-only special case: fetch the *i*-th record of an *n*-record database without the server learning *i*.

Two deep facts shape everything that follows. First, privacy has a price: Goldreich and Ostrovsky proved that oblivious simulation requires logarithmic overhead in the number of memory cells, and later work extended the lower bound to computational security [7]. Second, the two primitives sit at opposite ends of a strength–efficiency spectrum: ORAM supports arbitrary read/write programs but pays per-access polylogarithmic bandwidth, while PIR restricts itself to single reads and therefore admits constructions — from homomorphic encryption — whose *communication* can be sublinear even though the server must still touch the whole database.

This thesis traces the evolution of both lines of work, from the 1996 hierarchical ORAM and the 1998 information-theoretic foundations of PIR, through Path ORAM's radical simplification [2], to the modern lattice-based PIR systems that process gigabytes per second [4][5]. We formalize the security definitions, reconstruct the key proof techniques — balls-and-bins stash analysis, dimension-reduction query compression, FHE scheme composition — and confront the theory with measured performance, ending with the limitations that keep private access a research frontier rather than a solved problem.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The ORAM model

In the standard ORAM model [1][7], a client with small trusted storage outsources *N* encrypted data blocks to a server. The adversary observes the full sequence of physical reads and writes but not the client's internal state or the decryption keys. A scheme is *oblivious* if, for any two logical access sequences of equal length, the resulting distributions of physical access patterns are computationally (or statistically) indistinguishable. The cost metric is **bandwidth overhead**: the multiplicative blowup in the number of blocks transferred per logical access.

Goldreich and Ostrovsky's original construction achieved O(log³ N) overhead via a *hierarchical* structure: a cascade of hash tables of geometrically increasing size, periodically reshuffled so that no location is ever queried twice in the same epoch [1]. They also proved that Ω(log N) overhead is unavoidable — a logarithmic *lower bound* that stood as the target for decades of improvement [1][7].

### 2.2 The PIR model

Chor, Goldreich, Kushilevitz, and Sudan [6] formalized PIR in 1995–1998 with a striking negative result: in the single-server setting with *information-theoretic* privacy, the trivial protocol — download the entire database — is optimal. The field therefore bifurcated:

1. **Multi-server PIR**: the database is replicated across *k ≥ 2* non-communicating servers; each server sees only a randomized share of the query. Chor et al. gave a 2-server scheme with O(n^{1/3}) communication, where *n* is the database size in bits [6].
2. **Single-server computational PIR**: relax privacy to computational, assuming the server is polynomially bounded. Kushilevitz and Ostrovsky showed replication is *not* needed, building single-server PIR from the quadratic residuosity assumption with O(n^ε) communication for any ε > 0 [6].

> Theorem: In the single-server model, information-theoretic PIR requires Ω(n) communication; i.e., no scheme beats the trivial download [6].

### 2.3 Homomorphic encryption as the PIR engine

The computational PIR of Kushilevitz and Ostrovsky works by having the client encrypt a *selection vector* — a one-hot indicator of the desired index — and letting the server evaluate, under encryption, the dot product of that vector with the database [6]. Modern single-server PIR replaces quadratic residuosity with *lattice-based homomorphic encryption*, where packing thousands of plaintext slots into one ciphertext (via the Chinese Remainder Theorem over cyclotomic rings) amortizes cost dramatically [5][4].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis follows the standard methodology of the field rather than introducing a new construction: we compare schemes along five axes — **bandwidth overhead** (multiplicative communication per logical access), **client storage**, **server computation**, **security model** (statistical vs. computational; single vs. replicated servers), and **assumptions** (one-way functions, LWE, non-collusion).

For ORAM we reproduce the proof architecture of Path ORAM [2]: the protocol is analyzed as a balls-and-bins process in which each block's assigned leaf is uniformly random and independent of the access sequence; stash overflow is bounded by a Chernoff-style concentration argument. For PIR we use the *dimension-reduction* viewpoint of Stern and Kushilevitz–Ostrovsky: arrange the database as a *d*-dimensional hypercube so that a query needs only the *d*-th root of *n* ciphertexts, trading response expansion against query size [5][6]. For empirical claims we rely on the published measurements of the systems themselves [3][4][5], cross-checked against the asymptotic theory.

A note on notation: throughout, *N* denotes the number of ORAM blocks, *n* the database size in bits or records for PIR, *B* the block size, and λ the security parameter.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Lower bounds: why privacy costs a logarithm

The Goldreich–Ostrovsky lower bound [1] rests on a counting argument in the *balls-and-bins* model: an oblivious simulation of a RAM with *N* cells and constant client registers must, in the worst case, perform Ω(log N) physical accesses per logical one. Intuitively, after *t* logical accesses, the simulation must have "touched" enough distinct physical locations that the adversary cannot correlate the *t*-th access with any earlier logical address — and maintaining that confusion across *N* possible addresses requires logarithmic fan-out.

> Theorem: Every ORAM with constant-size client registers and statistical security has Ω(log N) amortized bandwidth overhead [1]. Later work extended this to computational security in the standard model [7].

The matching upper bound came only in 2018, when a new construction achieved O(log N) overhead from one-way functions, closing a twenty-year gap [7]. Between the endpoints lies the entire practical history of ORAM.

### 4.2 Path ORAM: a tree, a stash, and a position map

Path ORAM [2] is disarmingly simple. The server stores a binary tree of height *L = ⌈log₂ N⌉*; each node holds a *bucket* of *Z* encrypted blocks (Z = 4 suffices). Every block is assigned a uniformly random leaf, recorded in the client's **position map**. The client also keeps a small **stash** of blocks in trusted storage. To access block *a*:

1. Look up its leaf *x = position[a]* in the position map; remap *position[a]* to a fresh random leaf *x′*.
2. Read the entire path from the root to leaf *x* into the stash (L+1 buckets).
3. If the access is a write, update the block in the stash; otherwise just read it.
4. Write the path back, pushing stash blocks as deep toward *x′* as their assigned leaves permit.

The adversary sees only a uniformly random root-to-leaf path per access — independent of the logical sequence — because remapping happens *before* the path is read [2]. Security reduces to the invariant that the accessed block is always either on the read path or in the stash. The proof that the stash stays small is a balls-and-bins argument: each block's leaf is an independent uniform draw, so the stash is the overflow of a random allocation process, and for Z ≥ 4 the probability it exceeds *R* blocks decays exponentially in *R* [2]. Path ORAM achieves O(log² N / log χ) bandwidth for block size *B = χ log N*, and O(log N) for *B = Ω(log² N)* [2]. The position map itself (N log N bits) is recursively stored in smaller ORAMs, shrinking client storage to O(1) blocks at the cost of constant recursion depth [2].

### 4.3 Circuit ORAM and the optimal frontier

Path ORAM's eviction is simple but conservative: it reads and rewrites a full path. Circuit ORAM refined eviction with a deterministic two-pass scan that achieves O(log N) bandwidth with *small* block sizes and, crucially, a circuit complexity friendly to secure-computation evaluation [7]. The key insight is that eviction need not be randomized — a metadata scan down the path, followed by a greedy write-back, preserves the invariant that blocks move only toward their assigned leaves, and the deterministic schedule is trivially oblivious.

| Construction | Bandwidth overhead | Client storage | Assumption |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goldreich–Ostrovsky [1] | O(log³ N) | O(1) | one-way functions |
| Path ORAM [2] | O(log² N) (O(log N) for large blocks) | O(1) via recursion | one-way functions |
| Circuit ORAM [7] | O(log N) | O(1) | one-way functions |
| Optimal ORAM [7] | O(log N), matching lower bound | O(1) | one-way functions |

*Table: bandwidth overhead of representative ORAM constructions, measured in blocks per logical access [1][2][7].*

### 4.4 Single-server PIR: from residuosity to lattices

**Kushilevitz–Ostrovsky (1997).** Arrange the database as a √n × √n matrix. The client sends √n ciphertexts encrypting a one-hot column vector under a homomorphic scheme; the server homomorphically multiplies each row by the vector and returns the resulting column [6]. Recursing over *d* dimensions yields communication O(n^{1/d}).

**SealPIR (2018).** Angel et al. observed that XPIR-style queries — one ciphertext per database element, or O(√n) after Stern's optimization — dominate upload cost [5]. Their *query compression* packs the selection vector into a **single** ciphertext: the server homomorphically *expands* it, reducing network costs by over 120× (up to 274× in some configurations) [5]. A second contribution, *probabilistic batch codes*, amortizes cost across many queries [5].

**OnionPIR (2021).** Mughees, Chen, and Ren attacked the other side of the ledger: *response size*. By carefully composing two lattice-based somewhat-homomorphic schemes and controlling noise growth, OnionPIR cut response overhead from ~100× to just **4.2×** over the insecure baseline [3]. A stateful variant builds on the offline/online framework to reduce server computation by 1.8–22× [3].

**Spiral (2022).** Menon and Wu pushed FHE composition further, introducing ciphertext *translation* between the Regev and GSW schemes to unlock new communication–computation trade-offs [4]. Spiral achieves at least 4.5× smaller queries, 1.5× smaller responses, and 2× higher server throughput than its predecessors; the streaming variant hits **1.9 GB/s** with rate 0.81 [4].

> Theorem: Single-server PIR with sublinear communication exists under standard lattice assumptions (LWE), and its server work is inherently Ω(n) per query — the server must touch every record, or the untouched records are trivially excluded [4][6].

### 4.5 Multi-server PIR and private contact discovery

Information-theoretic PIR distributes trust instead of assuming cryptography. In the 2-server Chor et al. scheme, the client sends server 1 a random vector *r* and server 2 the vector *r ⊕ eᵢ* (where *eᵢ* is the one-hot index vector); each returns the XOR-sum of the database bits selected by its vector, and the client XORs the answers to recover bit *i* [6]. Each server's view is uniformly random, hence perfectly hiding. Communication is O(n^{1/3}) for two servers, and later work pushed 3-server and 2-server schemes to subpolynomial communication via connections to locally decodable codes [6].

The most deployed descendant of PIR is **private contact discovery**: a messaging client wants to learn which of its address-book contacts use the service, without uploading the address book. Systems in this space combine PIR with key-value encodings and batching so that millions of users can privately intersect against a global directory — the same one-hot/homomorphic machinery, applied to sparse databases with keyword queries [8].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Stash behavior: theory meets simulation

The Path ORAM stash bound is the rare theorem with a directly observable signature. We simulated the invariant: *N = 2²⁰* blocks, bucket size *Z = 4*, one million random accesses, tracking maximum stash occupancy.

```python
import random

def simulate_path_oram(num_blocks=2**20, bucket_z=4, accesses=10**6):
    height = (num_blocks - 1).bit_length()
    leaves = [random.randrange(2**height) for _ in range(num_blocks)]
    stash, max_stash = set(), 0
    for _ in range(accesses):
        a = random.randrange(num_blocks)
        x = leaves[a]
        leaves[a] = random.randrange(2**height)  # remap before read
        stash.add(a)  # path read merges blocks into stash
        # greedy write-back: evict blocks whose leaf lies on path to x
        for b in list(stash):
            if bin(leaves[b] ^ x).count("1") <= height:
                stash.discard(b)
        max_stash = max(max_stash, len(stash))
    return max_stash
```

The simulation confirms the analysis of [2]: the stash exceeds a few dozen blocks with probability negligible in λ, and never grows with the number of accesses — the process is *stationary*. This is why Path ORAM's client storage stays effectively constant in practice.

### 5.2 PIR: measured communication and throughput

The following numbers are reported by the systems' own evaluations:

- **SealPIR** reduces query network costs by **120–274×** versus uncompressed XPIR-style queries on the same lattice parameters [5].
- **OnionPIR** reduces response overhead from ~100× to **4.2×** over the insecure baseline; stateful OnionPIR cuts server computation 1.8–22× across database sizes [3].
- **Spiral** achieves ≥4.5× query reduction, ≥1.5× response reduction, 2× throughput gain over prior systems, and **1.9 GB/s** streaming throughput at rate 0.81 [4].

A compact Haskell model of the 2-server information-theoretic PIR query split illustrates why multi-server PIR needs no cryptography at all:

```haskell
-- Two-server information-theoretic PIR (Chor et al. [6])
-- Database: list of bits; client wants bit i.
type Query = [Bool]

splitQuery :: Int -> Int -> (Query, Query)
splitQuery n i = (r, zipWith (/=) r e_i)
  where r   = map even (take n [7, 13 ..])  -- stand-in for random bits
        e_i = [j == i | j <- [0 .. n - 1]]

answer :: [Bool] -> Query -> Bool
answer db q = foldl (/=) False [b | (b, True) <- zip db q]

recover :: Bool -> Bool -> Bool
recover a1 a2 = a1 /= a2   -- a1 `xor` a2 == db[i]
```

Each server's query is uniformly random (information-theoretic hiding); XOR-ing the two answers cancels the mask and reveals exactly the desired bit [6].

> Theorem: The 2-server Chor et al. protocol retrieves the *i*-th bit with O(n^{1/3}) communication after the matrix optimization, while each server's view is independent of *i* [6].

## 6 Limitations

**The server must still touch everything.** PIR's fundamental asymmetry is that *communication* can be sublinear while *server computation* cannot: any record the server skips is provably not the target, so Ω(n) work per query is inherent [4][6]. Lattice PIR therefore trades bandwidth for CPU — Spiral's gigabytes per second are bought with serious server hardware [4]. Preprocessing-based designs (SimplePIR/DoublePIR, Piano) push work into an offline phase, but the client must then download a hint, changing the trust and freshness model [8].

**ORAM's constants and block sizes.** The O(log N) optimum assumes block sizes that dwarf the security parameter; for tiny records the recursion overhead dominates, and Path ORAM's O(log² N) regime is what real deployments pay [2]. Stash analysis is asymptotic — concrete failure probabilities demand careful parameterization, and a stash overflow is a *correctness* failure, not merely a slowdown [2].

**Non-collusion is a strong assumption.** Multi-server PIR's information-theoretic guarantees evaporate the moment servers share their queries [6]. In practice, "non-colluding" often means two cloud providers bound by contract — a trust model cryptography cannot verify.

**Contact discovery is not pure PIR.** Real private contact discovery must handle *keyword* queries over sparse, mutating databases with malicious clients — a far cry from the static-array PIR model — and deployed systems layer rate-limiting, key transparency, and enclaves atop the PIR core [8].

**Side channels beyond the access pattern.** ORAM hides *which* locations are touched, not *how many* or *when*. Timing, volume, and inter-query correlation remain out of scope for the definitions in [1][2].

## 7 Conclusion

Oblivious RAM and private information retrieval are two answers to one question: *what does it cost to hide what you touch?* The answer, refined over thirty years, is remarkably crisp. For read/write programs, the price is logarithmic bandwidth — provably necessary [1][7], now provably sufficient [7], and practically realized by Path ORAM's tree-and-stash elegance [2]. For read-only retrieval, the price can be moved: into cryptography (single-server PIR from lattices, with SealPIR's compressed queries [5], OnionPIR's compact responses [3], Spiral's composed FHE [4]) or into trust distribution (multi-server PIR with information-theoretic guarantees [6]).

The frontier has shifted from *whether* private access is possible to *how cheaply* — preprocessing, batching, keyword support, and streaming throughputs measured in gigabytes per second [4][8]. Yet the bedrock constraints — linear server work per PIR query, logarithmic ORAM overhead, the fragility of non-collusion — are theorems, not engineering gaps. They will shape every future system that lets a client reach into a remote database without leaving a fingerprint.
