# Mechanistic Interpretability of Transformers: Induction Heads, Superposition, and Sparse Autoencoders

## Abstract

Mechanistic interpretability treats trained neural networks as compiled programs whose source code can be recovered. This thesis presents the central arc of that programme for the transformer architecture across three interlocking discoveries. First, *induction heads* — two-head circuits in attention-only transformers that implement the `[A][B]...[A] -> [B]` prefix-matching and copying algorithm — provide a mechanistic account of in-context learning, validated by causal ablation experiments. Second, the *superposition hypothesis* explains the ubiquity of polysemantic neurons: networks represent more features than they have dimensions by encoding features as nearly-orthogonal directions, a compression whose phase structure is characterised in toy models. Third, *sparse autoencoders* (SAEs) trained with dictionary learning decompose polysemantic activations back into monosemantic features, scaling to millions of interpretable units in production models and enabling feature steering. We formalise the residual-stream computational model, derive the QK/OV circuit decomposition, quantify superposition geometry, analyse SAE objectives and pathologies, and survey empirical evidence from small attention-only models through frontier-scale deployments. The thesis concludes by arguing that causal validation — ablation, activation patching, and intervention — remains the non-negotiable standard distinguishing genuine mechanistic explanation from compelling but ungrounded story-telling.

## 1 Introduction

The transformer dominates contemporary machine learning, yet the models we deploy remain, in a precise sense, illegible to their creators. We optimise them with stochastic gradient descent on next-token prediction, and out of that optimisation emerge capabilities — in-context learning, chain-of-thought reasoning, factual recall — whose implementation in weights and activations we did not design and do not directly observe. *Mechanistic interpretability* is the research programme that refuses to accept this opacity as fundamental. Its central claim is that neural networks are *reverse-engineerable*: that the apparently inscrutable high-dimensional computation can be decomposed into discrete, human-understandable mechanisms called **circuits**, and that these mechanisms can be validated causally rather than merely correlated observationally [1].

Three discoveries structure the modern form of this programme, and they form the spine of this thesis:

1. **Induction heads.** Specific attention heads implement a concrete algorithm — search the context for a previous occurrence of the current token, then copy what followed it — and this algorithm accounts for a substantial fraction of in-context learning, the phenomenon whereby loss decreases as context position grows [2].
2. **Superposition.** Networks represent *more features than they have dimensions* by encoding features as directions in activation space that are nearly but not exactly orthogonal. Polysemanticity — neurons responding to unrelated concepts — is not a bug but the expected signature of a network under representational pressure [3].
3. **Sparse autoencoders.** Dictionary learning with sparsity penalties can *un-pack* superposition, decomposing polysemantic activations into monosemantic features that admit human interpretation and causal steering [4].

These three ideas are not independent. Superposition explains why naive inspection of neurons fails; induction heads are what successful circuit-level analysis looks like despite that failure; and sparse autoencoders are the methodological bridge that makes circuit analysis tractable at scale. Understanding their interrelation is, arguably, understanding the current state of the art in mechanistic interpretability itself.

> **Thesis claim:** The residual-stream view of the transformer — as a shared communication channel on which attention heads and MLP layers perform sequential read and write operations over linear feature directions — is the correct level of abstraction for mechanistic explanation; induction heads validate this abstraction causally; superposition describes its principal obstruction; and sparse autoencoders constitute the most promising instrument for overcoming that obstruction at frontier scale.

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews the mathematical framework for transformer circuits: residual streams, the QK/OV decomposition, and virtual attention heads. Section 3 describes the methodology of mechanistic research — hypothesis formation from weights and activations, and causal validation through ablation and activation patching. Section 4 develops the three core phenomena in depth. Section 5 surveys the empirical evidence, from attention-only toy models to SAEs trained on production-scale language models. Section 6 examines limitations and open problems, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The transformer as a residual-stream computer

The foundational move of the circuits programme is to view the transformer not as a stack of opaque layers but as a **residual-stream computer** [1]. Let the input sequence be embedded into an initial residual stream $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{model} \times n}$, where $n$ is the context length. Each layer then *reads* from this stream and *writes* additive updates back into it:

$$x_{\ell+1} = x_\ell + \mathrm{Attn}_\ell(x_\ell) + \mathrm{MLP}_\ell(x_\ell + \mathrm{Attn}_\ell(x_\ell))$$

Because all communication between layers passes through the residual stream, every component must express its computation as *movement of information into and out of a shared linear space*. This single observation is enormously constraining and therefore enormously useful: it means that features correspond (approximately) to *directions* in the residual stream, and that understanding a component means understanding which directions it reads and which it writes [1].

### 2.2 The QK/OV decomposition of attention heads

An attention head at position $i$ computes a weighted average of value vectors from earlier positions $j \le i$ (in a causal model), where the weights are determined by query-key compatibility. Elhage et al. show that the head factorises cleanly into two circuits [1]:

- The **QK circuit** determines *where* the head attends: the attention pattern $A_{ij} = \mathrm{softmax}(q_i^\top k_j / \sqrt{d_{head}})$ is a bilinear form in the residual stream, $x_i^\top W_Q^\top W_K x_j$, which can be read directly from the weights.
- The **OV circuit** determines *what* the head writes: given attention to position $j$, the head adds $W_O W_V x_j$ to the residual stream at position $i$.

Crucially, the OV circuit is *linear*, while the QK circuit contains the softmax nonlinearity. The two circuits often admit separate mechanistic hypotheses: one can characterise "this head attends to previous tokens whose embedding matches the current token" (QK) independently of "and copies the following token's embedding forward" (OV) [1][2].

### 2.3 Virtual heads and composition

In multi-layer models, heads compose. A head in layer $\ell$ can attend *based on information written by* a head in layer $\ell - 1$; the composition $W_Q^\top W_K^{(\ell)} W_O W_V^{(\ell-1)}$ forms a **virtual attention head** spanning layers, with its own effective QK and OV circuits [1]. This composition is the mechanism by which two-layer attention-only models implement induction: a *previous-token head* in layer 0 shifts information about token $j-1$ to position $j$, and an *induction head* in layer 1 uses QK-composition through that shifted information to match the current token against earlier tokens, then OV-copies what followed them [2].

### 2.4 From toy circuits to polysemantic reality

The circuits framework was developed on attention-only transformers, often with zero, one, or two layers and no MLPs, precisely because the QK/OV factorisation is then *exact* and fully readable from weights [1]. Real models interpose MLPs — themselves decomposable into key-value memories over directions — and exhibit **polysemanticity**: single neurons and heads that respond to mixtures of unrelated features. The superposition hypothesis [3] was introduced to explain this, and sparse autoencoders [4] to circumvent it. We now turn to methodology before developing each phenomenon in full.

## 3 Methodology

Mechanistic interpretability is distinguished from neighbouring fields (probing, saliency, behavioural evaluation) by its epistemic standard: a proposed mechanism must be **causally validated**, not merely correlated with behaviour. The standard workflow has four stages.

### 3.1 Hypothesis formation

Researchers form hypotheses from two complementary sources:

1. **Weight analysis.** Because the QK and OV circuits are bilinear or linear forms readable from parameters, one can compute what a head *should* do in closed form — e.g., that a head's QK circuit implements previous-token lookup — before ever running data through the model [1].
2. **Activation analysis.** Tools such as the *logit lens* (projecting intermediate residual states through the unembedding matrix) reveal which token predictions are being promoted or suppressed at each layer, suggesting where in the network a computation occurs.

### 3.2 Causal validation

A hypothesis is tested by *intervention*:

- **Ablation.** Zeroing or mean-replacing a head's output and measuring the behavioural deficit. If induction heads mediate in-context learning, ablating them should destroy the model's ability to exploit repeated context while sparing other capabilities [2].
- **Activation patching.** Running the model on two inputs (a "clean" and a "corrupted" one) and splicing activations from one run into the other at specific components, thereby localising which components carry the causally relevant information. *Path patching* refines this to individual edges between components.
- **Feature steering.** With SAE-derived features, one can clamp a feature's activation to high or low values during the forward pass and observe behavioural changes — the strongest form of validation, since it demonstrates both necessity and sufficiency [4][6].

### 3.3 Toy models as laboratories

Because frontier models are expensive to intervene upon and their training data uncontrolled, much foundational work uses **attention-only models trained on synthetic data** — for instance, repeated random token sequences designed so that induction is the only learnable strategy for reducing loss [2], or tiny ReLU networks trained on sparse synthetic features to study superposition in isolation [3]. The methodology is deliberately reductionist: establish the phenomenon and its mathematical characterisation in the cleanest possible setting, then verify that the same signatures appear in large models.

### 3.4 Dictionary learning as measurement

Sparse autoencoders invert the usual direction of analysis. Rather than hypothesising a circuit and testing it, one trains an auxiliary model — an encoder $f(x) = \sigma(W_{enc} x + b_{enc})$ with a sparsity-inducing penalty and a decoder $\hat{x} = W_{dec} f(x)$ — to decompose activations into a large dictionary of candidate features, then interprets and steers the discovered units [4]. Recent work extends this methodology to vision-language models [6] and studies architectural variants such as expander-structured decoders [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Induction heads: the circuit behind in-context learning

**The algorithm.** An induction head implements the following procedure at each position $i$ [2]:

1. *Prefix matching (QK):* attend to positions $j$ where the token at $j$ matches the token at $i$ — more precisely, where position $j$ was *preceded by* a token matching the current one, using information shifted by a previous-token head.
2. *Copying (OV):* write the embedding of the token *following* $j$ into the residual stream at $i$, thereby boosting the logit of the token that previously followed the current token.



| Line of evidence | Finding |
|---|---|
| Training dynamics | Induction heads form at precisely the training step where a sharp "bump" in loss marks a sudden increase in in-context learning |
| Ablation | Ablating induction heads in small models destroys most of the in-context learning advantage |
| Argument by construction | Two-layer attention-only models can be hand-constructed to implement induction |
| Scaling analysis | The phase change is visible across model scales |
| Attention patterns | Heads in large models exhibit the diagnostic prefix-matching pattern |
| Synthetic tasks | Models trained on repeated sequences rely on induction measurably |



```python
# Idealised induction-head attention (illustrative)
# scores[i, j] high when token[j-1] == token[i]
def induction_scores(tokens, shift_info):
    n = len(tokens)
    scores = [[0.0] * n for _ in range(n)]
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(1, i + 1):
            # shift_info[j] carries the identity of token j-1
            if shift_info[j] == tokens[i]:
                scores[i][j] = 1.0  # attend; OV then copies token j+1
    return scores
```

> **Theorem (informal, after [1][2]):** In a two-layer attention-only transformer, QK-composition of a previous-token head in layer 0 with a matching head in layer 1 yields a virtual head whose attention implements prefix matching and whose OV circuit implements copying — i.e., induction. No single-layer attention-only head can implement induction, because the query at position $i$ cannot access token $j-1$'s identity without the layer-0 shift.

### 4.2 Superposition: why neurons are polysemantic




- **Polysemanticity is expected**, not pathological: with $m > d$, a neuron (a fixed linear readout $w^\top x$) necessarily responds to multiple features whose directions have positive inner product with $w$.
- **Sparsity enables superposition**: the sparser the features, the more can be packed per dimension, since interference only matters among co-active features.
- **Privileged bases matter**: superposition is a statement about the *feature* basis, which need not align with the neuron basis — explaining why individual neurons look meaningless while directions in activation space are interpretable.
- **Phase changes**: as the sparsity or the feature-to-dimension ratio varies, toy models exhibit sharp transitions between dedicated (one-neuron-per-feature) and superposed regimes, with intermediate "partial superposition" geometries [3].


```haskell
-- Superposition as sparse coding (conceptual)
-- m features, d dimensions, m > d
type Feature = Vector Double   -- direction v_k in R^d
dictionary :: [Feature]        -- the m nearly-orthogonal directions
encode :: [Double] -> Vector Double
dictionary `encode` coeffs = foldr add zero [c *. v | (c, v) <- zip coeffs dictionary]
-- A neuron w reads out: w <.> x ~= sum of c_k * (w <.> v_k)
-- polysemanticity <=> w has large inner product with several v_k
```


### 4.3 Sparse autoencoders: unpacking superposition with dictionary learning





- Features firing selectively for *DNA sequences*, *legal language*, *HTTP requests*, *Hebrew text*, *code comments*;
- Features tracking abstract properties such as *the presence of apostrophes* or *numbers in a particular range*;
- Features whose ablation or clamping causally altered model behaviour in the predicted direction — e.g., suppressing a "French language" feature degraded French text completion.



| Problem | Description | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Feature splitting | One true feature fragments across multiple dictionary atoms | Matryoshka/hierarchical SAEs learn nested dictionaries [7] |
| Feature absorption | A general feature's cases get absorbed by specialised latents | Improved sparsity schedules; diagnostic benchmarks |
| Dead features | Dictionary atoms that never activate waste capacity | Resampling / aux-loss revival |
| Amortisation gap | The encoder underperforms iterative sparse coding | Iterative or matching-pursuit encoders |
| Parameter cost | Decoder has $m \times n$ parameters | *Expander SAEs* use fixed sparse masks, reducing learned parameters while preserving a storage-fidelity frontier [7] |



```rust
// TopK sparse autoencoder forward pass (illustrative)
fn sae_forward(x: &Vector, w_enc: &Matrix, w_dec: &Matrix, k: usize) -> (Vector, Vector) {
    let pre: Vector = w_enc.mul_vec(x);          // pre-activations
    let f: Vector = top_k(&pre, k);              // hard sparsity: keep k largest
    let x_hat: Vector = w_dec.mul_vec(&f);       // reconstruction
    (f, x_hat) // f = monosemantic feature activations
}
```

### 4.4 Synthesis: circuits, compression, and control


1. **Circuits** ([1][2]) show that reverse engineering is *possible*: attention-only models implement legible algorithms, and the residual-stream plus QK/OV vocabulary is the right language for describing them.
2. **Superposition** ([3]) shows why reverse engineering is *hard*: the legible features are not aligned with neurons, and naive inspection drowns in polysemanticity.
3. **Sparse autoencoders** ([4][7]) show that the hardness is *surmountable*: dictionary learning recovers the feature basis in which the network's ontology becomes legible, and the recovered features support causal steering.

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

### 5.1 Induction heads: causal evidence


1. *Construction.* Two-layer attention-only models can be explicitly wired to implement induction, proving the architecture *can* express the algorithm.
2. *Emergence.* Models trained on repeated-token synthetic data develop induction heads at a sharp phase transition, coincident with a discontinuous drop in loss attributable to in-context learning — the "bump" in the training curve.
3. *Ablation.* Removing induction heads from small trained models eliminates most of the in-context learning advantage while leaving other capabilities intact — the causal test.
4. *Correlational scaling.* In large models with MLPs, heads exhibiting the diagnostic prefix-matching attention pattern are ubiquitous, and their prevalence correlates with in-context learning strength — weaker evidence, honestly presented as such [2].

### 5.3 Sparse autoencoders: from toy models to Claude 3 Sonnet


- **One-layer transformer (2023).** SAEs with a few thousand features recover interpretable, monosemantic units — DNA sequences, legal text, code — from a small model's MLP activations, with causal steering demonstrated by clamping [4].
- **Production LLM (2024).** Scaling to Claude 3 Sonnet yields millions of features spanning concrete entities, abstract concepts (deception, sycophancy), and multilingual phenomena; feature clamping steers open-ended behaviour, and the features enable safety-relevant auditing (e.g., detecting when the model internally represents deceptive reasoning) [5].
- **Vision-language models.** SAEs trained on CLIP vision encoders and LLaVA-style models learn monosemantic visual-concept features that support steering of generated descriptions — e.g., amplifying a "panda" feature causes the model to describe panda-like content — demonstrating that the dictionary-learning methodology generalises across modalities [6].
- **Architectural scaling.** Expander SAEs and related variants show that dictionary quality can be preserved while drastically reducing parameter count, and formalise the trade-off as a storage-fidelity frontier [7].

## 6 Limitations

**Completeness.** No published circuit analysis claims to explain *all* of a model's behaviour on a task; typical analyses account for a substantial fraction of the relevant logit difference and leave a long tail of small effects unattributed. Whether the residual is itself composed of many small circuits or reflects a fundamentally distributed computation is unknown [1][2].

**Correlational drift.** As models scale and MLPs enter the picture, evidence quality degrades from causal (ablation in toy models) to correlational (pattern-matching in large models). Feature steering with SAEs partially restores causal grounding, but steering a feature is not the same as proving the model *uses* that feature in its natural computation [4][6].

**SAE validity.** Sparse autoencoders find *a* sparse dictionary that reconstructs activations — not necessarily *the* dictionary the model uses. Feature splitting and absorption show the learned atoms can misalign with the model's true ontology, and reconstruction error on the long tail of rare features remains poorly characterised [7]. Evaluation of SAE quality is itself an active research area, with synthetic benchmarks (e.g., SynthSAEBench) only recently emerging.

**The monosemanticity debate.** It is not settled whether monosemanticity is a property to be *encouraged* in models or merely *measured*. Interventions that increase feature decorrelation show alignment benefits in some settings [5], but the relationship to robustness and capability is contested, and optimising for interpretability could in principle trade against performance.

**Scope.** The circuits programme has its cleanest results in attention-only models and its most impressive demonstrations in English-language, text-only transformers. Multimodal extensions exist [6] but are young, and the methodology for recurrent or state-space architectures is far less developed.

## 7 Conclusion

Mechanistic interpretability has, in half a decade, moved from a speculative aspiration to a research programme with landmark results. Induction heads [2] proved that a core capability of large models — in-context learning — admits a complete, causal, circuit-level explanation. The superposition hypothesis [3] explained why that explanation was hard to find by naive inspection, reframing polysemanticity as optimal compression under representational pressure. Sparse autoencoders [4] turned that reframing into an instrument, recovering monosemantic features at scales from toy models to Claude 3 Sonnet and enabling causal steering of model behaviour [5][6][7].

The through-line is a distinctive epistemic standard: hypothesise mechanisms from weights and activations, then *validate by intervention* — ablation, patching, steering. Where that standard is met, as with induction heads in small models, the results are genuinely explanatory; where it is relaxed, as with correlational feature attributions in large models, the results are suggestive but provisional. The field's central bet is that the gap can be closed — that feature-level circuit analysis, grounded in ever-better dictionary learning, will eventually deliver causal explanations of frontier-model behaviour complete enough to audit safety-critical properties. Whether that bet pays off will determine whether interpretability becomes a true engineering discipline or remains, in the sceptic's phrase, neuroscience envy. The evidence surveyed here suggests the bet is worth taking seriously.
