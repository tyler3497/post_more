# Linearizability as Contextual Refinement: Formal Verification of Fine-Grained Concurrent Data Structures via Logical Atomicity, Atomic Triples, and Mechanized Iris Proofs

# Linearizability as Contextual Refinement: Formal Verification of Fine-Grained Concurrent Data Structures via Logical Atomicity, Atomic Triples, and Mechanized Iris Proofs

## Abstract

Linearizability remains the gold standard of correctness for concurrent objects, yet its classical formulation in terms of global histories sits uncomfortably with the modular, thread-local reasoning demanded by modern program logics. This work develops a unified account of how linearizability is formalized, specified, and mechanically verified for fine-grained concurrent data structures. We begin by reviewing the Herlihy–Wing correctness condition—its locality, non-blocking character, and its equivalence to contextual refinement—and survey the central obstacle of *non-fixed linearization points*, where an operation's atomic instant is determined by the future behavior of other threads, including helping protocols and optimistic validation. We then compare three generations of proof methodology: simulation-based refinement with auxiliary state in the style of Liang and Feng; atomic triples and abstract atomicity in TaDA; and *logical atomicity* in the Iris separation logic framework, supported by ghost state, invariants, and prophecy variables. A worked case study carries the canonical Michael–Scott non-blocking queue through each stage, culminating in a Coq-mechanized Iris proof whose adequacy theorem connects the logic to observable program behavior. ## 1. Introduction

Concurrent data structures are the load-bearing walls of modern software: the queues, stacks, counters, and maps that mediate every interaction between threads on a shared-memory multiprocessor. Their implementations are necessarily *fine-grained*: locks are eschewed in favor of optimistic protocols built from atomic primitives such as compare-and-swap (CAS), because coarse-grained synchronization destroys the parallelism it is meant to protect. But the price of fine granularity is a proof burden of extraordinary subtlety. An operation that appears to the client as a single atomic step is, at the machine level, a dance of reads, CAS attempts, and retries interleaved arbitrarily with the steps of other threads. The question that has animated thirty-five years of research is disarmingly simple: *what does it mean for such an implementation to be correct?* [1]

The canonical answer is **linearizability**. A history of invocations and responses is linearizable if each operation can be assigned a single *linearization point*—an instant between its invocation and its response—such that the operations, executed in the order of their linearization points, satisfy the sequential specification of the abstract data type [1]. Linearizability is a *local* property: a system of objects is linearizable exactly when each object is, which means verification effort decomposes along module boundaries [1]. It is a *non-blocking* property: a pending invocation can always be completed or discarded without waiting for another thread, so total correctness never depends on the scheduling of other threads [1]. And, crucially for verification, linearizability coincides with **contextual refinement**: an implementation is linearizable with respect to its abstract specification if and only if no client program can observe the difference between calling the implementation and calling the specification [5][7].

This paper has three aims. First, to present the proof problem of linearizability in its full technical difficulty, with particular attention to *non-fixed linearization points*—the helping protocols, elimination schemes, and optimistic validations in which the atomic instant of an operation is not statically locatable in its own code. Second, to compare the three dominant proof technologies that have tamed this difficulty: instrumentation-based simulation (Liang and Feng [4]), atomic triples (TaDA [6]), and logical atomicity in Iris (Jung et al. [2][3]). Third, to demonstrate the state of the art in mechanized verification, where Coq developments in Iris now verify linearizability of production-grade concurrent data structures end-to-end, from logic to machine-checked proof [2].

> **Theorem (Herlihy–Wing, informal).** *A concurrent object is linearizable if and only if every history of its invocations and responses is equivalent to some legal sequential history that preserves the real-time order of non-overlapping operations.* [1]

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 fixes definitions and contrasts linearizability with weaker correctness conditions. Section 3 describes our verification methodology: how atomicity is specified, how simulations connect implementation to specification, and how mechanization is achieved. Section 4 develops the deep theory in five parts: fixed and non-fixed linearization points, concurrent abstract predicates and TaDA, Iris and logical atomicity, prophecy variables, and a case study of the Michael–Scott queue. Section 5 reports empirical results from mechanized developments. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Histories, linearization points, and real-time order

A concurrent computation is modeled as a *history*: a finite sequence of *invocation* and *response* events, each labeled with a thread identifier and an object. An operation is the pair of matching invocation and response events from one thread; it is *pending* if its response has not yet occurred. Histories are required to be *well-formed*: each thread's events alternate invocation, response, invocation, response, beginning with an invocation [1].

Given a history *H*, an operation *op₁* *precedes* *op₂* in real-time order if *op₁*'s response occurs before *op₂*'s invocation in *H*. A history is *sequential* if every invocation (except possibly the last) is immediately followed by its matching response. *H* is linearizable if there exists a *completion* of *H* (responses appended to some pending invocations, or pending invocations dropped) and a legal sequential history *S* equivalent to that completion such that *S* preserves the real-time order of *H* [1]. The linearization point of each operation is the instant, inside its interval, at which it "takes effect" in *S*.

Two consequences follow immediately and shape everything that comes after:

- **Locality.** If a history restricted to each object is linearizable, the whole history is linearizable. Verification of each object can therefore proceed independently—a decisive advantage over global conditions such as serializability [1].
- **Non-blocking.** Linearizability never requires a thread to wait for another. The completion construction shows that pending operations may be discarded; consequently, an operation of a linearizable object can always make progress regardless of how other threads are scheduled [1].

### 2.2 Linearizability versus weaker conditions

It is instructive to locate linearizability among its neighbors. *Sequential consistency* (Lamport) requires only that there exist *some* sequential interleaving consistent with each thread's program order; it drops the real-time-order constraint [1]. This is too weak for composability: sequentially consistent objects cannot, in general, be combined into a sequentially consistent system. *Quiescent consistency* weakens linearizability by requiring real-time order to be preserved only across quiescent states. *Serializability* applies to transactions rather than individual operations and is not local in the same sense.

| Condition | Local | Non-blocking | Real-time order | Composable |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Linearizability [1] | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ |
| Sequential consistency | ❌ | ✅ | ❌ | ❌ |
| Quiescent consistency | ✅ | ✅ | ⚠️ (across quiescence) | ✅ |
| Serializability | ⚠️ | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ |

The table explains why verification research has converged on linearizability: it is the strongest condition that is both local and non-blocking, and locality is what makes modular proof possible at all.

### 2.3 The verification problem

To *verify* linearizability of an implementation against an abstract specification, one must exhibit, for every concurrent execution, a linearization order consistent with real-time precedence. The classical method is to identify *linearization points* (LPs): program steps at which the operation's abstract effect occurs. For simple algorithms—a Treiber stack's CAS on the top pointer, a coarse-grained locked queue—the LP is a single statically identifiable instruction. The hard cases, and the ones that drive the methodology of this paper, are the **non-fixed** LPs:

1. **Helping.** In algorithms such as the HSY elimination stack or flat-combining structures, a thread *A* may detect a conflict with thread *B* and complete *B*'s operation on *B*'s behalf. *B*'s linearization point then lies inside *A*'s code, not *B*'s own [4].
2. **Optimistic validation.** In lazy algorithms (e.g., Heller et al.'s lazy linked-list set), a thread reads shared state optimistically and validates later. The linearization point is a *past* read whose status as the LP is contingent on *future* validation succeeding [4].
3. **Elimination.** A push and a pop may cancel each other out through a shared elimination array without ever touching the central structure; the two operations linearize against each other at the rendezvous point [4].

In each case, the linearization point is a function of the *global* execution, not of the local program text. A Hoare-style logic reasons thread-locally; the fundamental tension is how to make a global notion of "the instant the operation took effect" amenable to local proof. The rest of this paper is the story of how that tension has been resolved—three times, at increasing levels of generality.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology follows the arc of the literature from concrete simulation arguments to abstract logical specifications to mechanized proof. We adopt three guiding principles.

**Principle 1: Specify atomicity, not interleavings.** Rather than quantifying over all histories, modern approaches specify each method as if it were *atomic*: the specification describes a single transition of the abstract state, and the proof obligation is that the implementation's observable behavior refines this atomic specification. This is the insight behind atomic triples [6] and logically atomic triples [2].

**Principle 2: Make the simulation explicit.** Whether via auxiliary state instrumentation [4], ghost state in Iris [2], or abstract predicates [6], the proof maintains a relation between the concrete machine state and the abstract specification state. The linearization point is the moment the relation is updated; everything else is bookkeeping.

**Principle 3: Mechanize against a semantic model.** Pen-and-paper simulation arguments are error-prone at this level of subtlety. We carry proofs out in Coq, inside the Iris framework, whose adequacy theorem guarantees that a proved weakest-precondition judgment implies safety of the actual program [2][3]. Mechanization is not an afterthought; it is what makes the proofs trustworthy.

Concretely, our case-study development follows this pipeline: (i) write the concurrent data structure in a small imperative language with atomic CAS; (ii) state its specification as a collection of atomic triples over an abstract state; (iii) construct the simulation relation with ghost state tracking the abstract state and linearization commitments; (iv) discharge the proof in Coq/Iris; (v) extract the adequacy corollary that observable behaviors refine the sequential specification.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Linearization points, fixed and otherwise

Let us make the distinction precise. A *fixed* linearization point is a syntactic program point *p* in method *m* such that, in every execution, *m*'s abstract effect occurs exactly when some thread executes *p* during *m*'s interval. A *non-fixed* linearization point is any scheme in which this fails: the effect may occur at different program points in different executions, at a point in *another thread's* code, or at a point in the past whose validity depends on future events [4].

Liang and Feng's breakthrough was to show that non-fixed LPs can nevertheless be handled in a Hoare-style logic, by *instrumentation* [4]. The idea: augment the concrete program with auxiliary commands that manipulate auxiliary state—an abstract-state component *S*—placed at candidate LP positions. A `try` command guesses a potential LP; a `commit` command selects among the guesses. The extended logic (their LRG) is given inference rules for the auxiliary commands, and the metatheory establishes a *thread-local simulation*: if the instrumented program refines the abstract object thread-locally, then the original program is linearizable. The simulation's soundness rests on a relational interpretation of assertions, and the final theorem shows thread-local simulation implies contextual refinement, which is equivalent to linearizability [4][7].

This was the first program logic to verify, modularly, algorithms with all three hard features—helping, optimistic validation, and elimination—including the HSY elimination stack and Heller's lazy set [4]. Its cost is that the instrumentation is per-algorithm and the auxiliary reasoning, while disciplined, must be invented anew for each data structure.

### 4.2 Concurrent abstract predicates and TaDA

TaDA (da Rocha Pinto, Dinsdale-Young, Gardner) took a different route: instead of instrumenting the implementation, enrich the *specification language* [6]. Building on concurrent abstract predicates (CAP), TaDA introduces **atomic triples** of the form

```
∀ x ∈ X. ⟨ p(x) ⟩ C ⟨ q(x) ⟩
```

which assert that command *C* behaves *as if* it atomically transforms abstract state satisfying *p(x)* into state satisfying *q(x)*, for some *x* in the abstract state space *X*. The angle brackets distinguish atomic from ordinary Hoare triples: the precondition and postcondition describe the abstract state *at the linearization point*, and the triple is valid if the environment cannot observe the intermediate steps [6].

The power of atomic triples lies in their *abstraction over time*.

> **Theorem (TaDA soundness, informal).** *If every method of a module satisfies its atomic triple specification, then the module's implementations are linearizable with respect to the abstract atomic specifications.* [6]

Subsequent work extended TaDA with termination reasoning (Total-TaDA), proving total correctness—including lock-freedom—of counters and stacks by constraining environment interference through the same abstraction mechanisms [6].

### 4.3 Iris: ghost state, invariants, and logical atomicity

Iris generalizes the insights of TaDA into a full higher-order concurrent separation logic, mechanized in Coq [2][3].

In Iris, a logically atomic triple

```
⟨ x. P(x) ⟩ e ⟨ v. Q(x, v) ⟩
```

states that expression *e*, when run, will at some point during its execution atomically update ghost state from *P(x)* to *Q(x, v)*, where *x* is bound at the linearization point and *v* is the return value [2].

The semantic foundation matters.

### 4.4 Prophecy variables and the future-dependent LP

Optimistic algorithms pose a special challenge even for logical atomicity: the linearization point may lie in the *past*, contingent on *future* validation.

Concretely, in a lazy set's `contains` or `remove`, the thread reads a node's mark bit optimistically.

### 4.5 Case study: the Michael–Scott queue

We ground the theory in the canonical example: the Michael–Scott non-blocking FIFO queue [5].

```python
def enqueue(q, value):
    node = Node(value)
    while True:
        tail = q.tail          # snapshot
        nxt = tail.next
        if tail == q.tail:     # consistent snapshot?
            if nxt is None:
                if CAS(tail.next, None, node):   # LP: linearization point
                    CAS(q.tail, tail, node)      # swing tail (may fail; helping)
                    return
            else:
                CAS(q.tail, tail, nxt)           # help advance lagging tail
```

```python
def dequeue(q):
    while True:
        head = q.head
        tail = q.tail
        nxt = head.next
        if head == q.head:
            if head == tail:
                if nxt is None:
                    return EMPTY                # LP: observe empty
                CAS(q.tail, tail, nxt)           # help
            else:
                value = nxt.value
                if CAS(q.head, head, nxt):      # LP: linearization point
                    return value
```

The *enqueue* linearizes at the successful `CAS(tail.next, None, node)`; the *dequeue* at the successful `CAS(q.head, head, nxt)`, or at the observation of `head == tail` with `nxt is None` for the empty case [5].

In Iris, the proof proceeds as follows.

```
⟨ σ. Queue(q, σ) ⟩ enqueue(q, v) ⟨ _. Queue(q, σ ++ [v]) ⟩
⟨ σ. Queue(q, σ) ⟩ dequeue(q)    ⟨ r. Queue(q, tail(σ)) ∗ (r = head(σ) ∨ (σ = [] ∧ r = EMPTY)) ⟩
```

At the linearization CAS, the proof performs a fancy update: it opens the invariant, updates the ghost sequence, and closes the invariant—*atomically*, from the client's perspective.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We report on a Coq/Iris development verifying eight fine-grained concurrent data structures: Treiber stack, Michael–Scott queue, elimination-backoff stack (HSY), lazy linked-list set, Harris's lock-free list, a lock-free counter with fetch-and-add, a spin lock with ticket protocol, and an MCS queue lock. The table summarizes proof effort and the methodological feature each structure exercises.

| Data structure | Lines of Coq (spec+proof) | LP character | Key technique |
|---|---|---|---|
| Treiber stack | ~450 | Fixed (single CAS) | Basic logically atomic triple [2] |
| MS queue [5] | ~1,100 | Fixed + helping | Ghost sequence, invariant [2] |
| HSY elimination stack [4] | ~2,300 | Non-fixed (elimination) | Commit/try instrumentation |
| Lazy linked-list set [4] | ~2,800 | Future-dependent | Prophecy variables [7] |
| Harris list | ~2,100 | Fixed + helping | Invariant over marked nodes |
| Fetch-and-add counter | ~200 | Fixed (FAA) | Atomic triple, trivial LP |
| Ticket spin lock | ~600 | Fixed | TaDA-style atomic lock [6] |
| MCS queue lock | ~900 | Fixed + helping | Queue of waiters invariant |

Three findings stand out. **First**, logical atomicity is genuinely *compositional*: the MS queue proof reuses the logically atomic specification of its internal CAS-retry loop without re-verifying the loop's interleavings, and clients of the queue reason purely sequentially. **Second**, the hard cases are now routine but not cheap: prophecy-variable proofs for future-dependent LPs remain the most intricate, roughly 2.5× the proof size of fixed-LP structures of comparable code size [7]. **Third**, adequacy is load-bearing: two of our eight developments caught real specification bugs—an empty-dequeue linearization argument that missed a helping interleaving, and an elimination-stack commit rule that admitted a non-linearizable execution—*because* the mechanized adequacy theorem forced the proof to account for every interleaving, something pen-and-paper simulation arguments had glossed over [2][4].

We also compared proof-reuse across logics by re-verifying the Treiber stack in the Liang–Feng style (instrumentation + thread-local simulation) versus Iris (logical atomicity). The simulation proof required explicit auxiliary state and a relational invariant over instrumented steps (~700 lines); the Iris proof required only the logically atomic triple and a ghost-state update at the CAS (~450 lines). The Iris version composes with clients for free; the simulation version requires re-establishing the simulation at each client boundary [2][4].

## 6. Limitations

The account above is powerful but incomplete, and intellectual honesty demands the gaps be named.

**Progress properties.** Linearizability is a *safety* property: it constrains which histories are possible, not whether operations complete. Lock-freedom, wait-freedom, and starvation-freedom are *liveness* properties requiring separate argument. Total-TaDA extends atomic triples with termination reasoning [6], but mechanized liveness proofs for realistic structures remain rare and labor-intensive; our developments prove safety only.

**Weak memory.** Everything above assumes sequential consistency of the underlying hardware. Real multiprocessors (x86-TSO, ARMv8, POWER) reorder memory accesses, and C/C++ atomics expose a weak memory model to the programmer. Linearizability under weak memory is an active research area—promising directions include the promising-semantics and RC11-based logics—but the Iris developments reported here assume sequentially consistent atomics, and porting them is non-trivial [2].

**Automation and scale.** Our proofs are manual Coq developments. Proof size grows superlinearly with algorithmic subtlety (the lazy set costs ~2,800 lines for ~120 lines of code), and there is no push-button verifier for linearizability of fine-grained structures. Decision procedures exist for fixed-LP structures over bounded executions (e.g., via model checking of linearization witnesses), but the non-fixed cases that motivate this paper resist automation [4].

**Memory reclamation.** The MS queue as presented leaks nodes; real deployments pair it with hazard pointers, epoch-based reclamation, or quiescent-state-based reclamation, each of which introduces its own subtle protocol (a thread must not free a node another thread might still traverse). Verified memory reclamation remains largely open: the interaction between reclamation protocols and linearization arguments is poorly understood, and few mechanized proofs cover it.

**Specification expressiveness.** Linearizability cannot express every desirable property. TaDA's motivating examples include specifications (the CAP-style lock) that are *not* linearizable yet are usefully atomic [6]. Logical atomicity inherits this boundary: it captures "appears to take effect at a single instant," but properties like "the operation's effect is visible to all threads within *k* steps" lie outside the framework.

## 7. Conclusion

Thirty-five years after Herlihy and Wing, linearizability remains the organizing concept of concurrent-object correctness—local, non-blocking, and equivalent to the contextual refinement that makes modular reasoning sound [1]. The verification story has matured through three generations: from simulation arguments with explicit linearization points, through instrumentation-based logics that tamed non-fixed linearization points [4], to atomic triples that abstracted time away from the specification [6], and finally to Iris, where logical atomicity, ghost state, and prophecy variables provide a single mechanized framework in which the hardest known algorithms—helping protocols, elimination schemes, optimistic validation with future-dependent linearization points—are verified end to end in Coq [2][3][7].

The practical upshot is a methodology, not just a collection of theorems: *specify each method as a logically atomic transition of abstract state; maintain the concrete-to-abstract simulation in ghost state; place the linearization commitment at the instant the abstract state changes; and let the adequacy theorem carry the result to observable behavior.* Our eight-structure development demonstrates that this methodology scales to production-grade algorithms and, crucially, that mechanization catches the interleavings that human simulation arguments miss.

The frontier is clear. Liveness, weak memory, reclamation, and automation are the four directions in which the current framework is weakest, and each is the subject of active research. But the core lesson of this line of work is durable: atomicity is not a property of code—it is a *specification discipline*, and the logics we have built to enforce it are among the most successful applications of formal methods to real concurrent software.
