# Quantum Optimal Control for High-Fidelity State and Gate Engineering: GRAPE, Krotov, Counterdiabatic Driving, and Quantum Speed Limits

## Abstract

Quantum optimal control (QOC) seeks time-dependent external fields that steer a quantum system's unitary or open dynamics toward a desired target while respecting experimental constraints. This article gives a doctoral-level treatment of the dominant algorithmic frameworks: the gradient-based GRAPE (Gradient Ascent Pulse Engineering) method, the monotonically convergent Krotov sequential update, and analytic counterdiabatic (transitionless) driving for shortcuts to adiabaticity, all bounded by fundamental quantum speed limits. We derive the exact analytic gradient of the fidelity functional for piecewise-constant controls, explain the second-order Krotov correction that guarantees monotonic convergence even for nonlinear state dependence, and show how counterdiabatic terms cancel nonadiabatic transitions exactly at the cost of auxiliary drives. A unified comparison table, a theorem on the Mandelstam-Tamm and Margolus-Levitin bounds, and a numerical case study of a two-qubit entangling gate illustrate convergence behavior, pulse complexity trade-offs, and the practical limits imposed by hardware bandwidth and decoherence.

## 1 Intro

The ability to steer quantum dynamics with shaped electromagnetic pulses underpins nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, electron spin resonance, superconducting-qubit processors, trapped-ion systems, and quantum-dot architectures. In each case, the central question is the same: given a drift Hamiltonian $H_0$ and a set of control Hamiltonians $\{H_k\}$ coupled to adjustable amplitudes $u_k(t)$, find control trajectories $u_k(t)$ over a fixed interval that maximize a figure of merit such as state-transfer fidelity or gate fidelity, subject to amplitude and bandwidth limits.

Quantum optimal control theory (QOCT) formalizes this as a variational problem over the space of admissible control functions. Two historically distinct schools emerged. The first, rooted in magnetic resonance, discretizes the control into piecewise-constant segments and applies exact gradient ascent; the GRAPE algorithm of Khaneja, Reiss, Kehlet, Schulte-Herbrüggen and Glaser (2005) is the canonical example [1]. The second, rooted in chemical physics and mathematical control theory, applies Krotov's global method, whose sequential update is provably monotonically convergent; Sklarz and Tannor (2002) and Reich, Koch and Ndong (2012) extended it to nonlinear Schrödinger dynamics and arbitrary functionals [2]. A third, analytic strand designs pulses by exact cancellation of nonadiabatic transitions, known as counterdiabatic driving or transitionless quantum driving. Fundamental quantum speed limits, the Mandelstam-Tamm and Margolus-Levitin bounds, delineate what any algorithm can in principle achieve.

This article unifies these strands. We derive the GRAPE gradient from first principles, state the Krotov iteration with its monotonicity guarantee, derive the counterdiabatic correction, and prove the speed-limit bounds that constrain all three.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The control problem

Consider a closed quantum system with Hilbert space dimension $d$, governed by the Schrödinger equation

$$ i\hbar \, \partial_t |\psi(t)\rangle = \hat{H}(u(t)) \, |\psi(t)\rangle, \qquad |\psi(0)\rangle = |\psi_0\rangle, $$

with the Hamiltonian linear in the controls,

$$ \hat{H}(u(t)) = \hat{H}_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{p} u_k(t) \, \hat{H}_k. $$

The time-ordered propagator $U(T)$ generated over duration $T$ is compared to a target unitary $U_\mathrm{target}$ (gate synthesis) or a propagated state $|\psi(T)\rangle$ to a target state $|\phi\rangle$ (state transfer). The standard final-time figure of merit is the squared overlap,

$$ \Phi[u] = \frac{1}{d^2} \left| \mathrm{Tr}\left( U_\mathrm{target}^{\dagger} U(T) \right) \right|^2, $$

or for states $\Phi[u] = |\langle \phi | \psi(T) \rangle|^2$. A full cost functional augments this with running costs that penalize fluence, constrain bandwidth, or encode state-dependent constraints:

$$ J[u] = J_T[|\psi(T)\rangle] + \int_0^T g\bigl[u(t), |\psi(t)\rangle, t\bigr] \, dt. $$

The *quantum control landscape* is the map $u \mapsto \Phi[u]$. A celebrated result of Rabitz and co-workers states that for controllable systems the landscape is generically trap-free: critical points of the fidelity are global maxima or saddle points, never suboptimal local maxima, provided no significant constraints are imposed. Constraints, however, reintroduce traps, which is why monotonicity guarantees and good initial guesses matter in practice.

### 2.2 Piecewise-constant discretization

GRAPE assumes the controls are piecewise constant over $N$ equal time slices of duration $\Delta t = T/N$:

$$ u_k(t) = u_k^{(j)}, \qquad t \in ((j-1)\Delta t, j\Delta t]. $$

The propagator factorizes as $U(T) = U_N \cdots U_1$ with $U_j = \exp\left(-i\Delta t \, H^{(j)}/\hbar\right)$ and $H^{(j)} = H_0 + \sum_k u_k^{(j)} H_k$. The discretized fidelity becomes a smooth function of $p \times N$ real variables, amenable to gradient ascent. Forward and backward propagated operators are defined as $P_j = U_j \cdots U_1$ and $Q_j = U_N \cdots U_{j+1}$, so that $U(T) = Q_j U_j P_{j-1}$. This factorization is what makes the GRAPE gradient exact and computable in $O(N)$ propagations per iteration [1].

### 2.3 Optimality conditions

For the continuous-time problem, Pontryagin's maximum principle introduces the adjoint state $|\chi(t)\rangle$, which satisfies the same Schrödinger equation with a final-time boundary condition derived from $\partial J_T / \partial \langle\psi(T)|$. A control is optimal when, at each instant, it extremizes the Pontryagin Hamiltonian. Both GRAPE and Krotov can be viewed as constructive numerical routes to satisfying these conditions; they differ in whether the update is concurrent (all time slices at once) or sequential (each time slice updated with the freshest available state).

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 GRAPE: exact gradient ascent

Define the overlap $c = \mathrm{Tr}(U_\mathrm{target}^{\dagger} U_N \cdots U_1)/d$ so that $\Phi = |c|^2$. The partial derivative with respect to a single control amplitude follows from the chain rule and the exact derivative of a matrix exponential:

$$ \frac{\partial U_j}{\partial u_k^{(j)}} = -\frac{i\Delta t}{\hbar} \int_0^1 e^{-i(1-s)\Delta t H^{(j)}/\hbar} H_k \, e^{-is\Delta t H^{(j)}/\hbar} \, ds. $$

In the limit of short $\Delta t$ (or by the first-order approximation used in the original GRAPE implementation), this reduces to

$$ \frac{\partial U_j}{\partial u_k^{(j)}} \approx -\frac{i\Delta t}{\hbar} \, U_j \, H_k, $$

and the fidelity gradient becomes

$$ \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial u_k^{(j)}} = \frac{2\Delta t}{\hbar} \, \mathrm{Im}\, \mathrm{Tr}\left[ \left( c^{\ast} \, U_\mathrm{target}^{\dagger} Q_j U_j \right) \left( H_k P_{j-1} \right)^{\dagger} \right] / d^2. $$

The GRAPE iteration updates all $pN$ amplitudes simultaneously,

$$ u_k^{(j)} \leftarrow u_k^{(j)} + \varepsilon \, \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial u_k^{(j)}}, $$

with a step size $\varepsilon$ chosen by line search or a quasi-Newton accelerator (L-BFGS). A representative Python skeleton is:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import expm

def grape_update(u, H0, Hk_list, dt, U_target, eps):
    p, N = u.shape
    d = H0.shape[0]
    # forward propagation
    P = [np.eye(d)]; U_list = []
    for j in range(N):
        Hj = H0 + sum(u[k, j] * Hk_list[k] for k in range(p))
        Uj = expm(-1j * dt * Hj)
        U_list.append(Uj); P.append(Uj @ P[-1])
    # backward propagation of the adjoint
    Q = np.eye(d)
    grad = np.zeros_like(u)
    for j in reversed(range(N)):
        # exact slice derivative via augmented exponential (first-order form shown)
        Uj = U_list[j]
        c = np.trace(U_target.conj().T @ P[-1]) / d
        for k in range(p):
            grad[k, j] = (2 * dt * np.imag(
                c.conj() * np.trace(U_target.conj().T @ Q @ Uj
                                   @ (-1j * Hk_list[k]) @ P[j])) / d**2).real
        Q = Q @ Uj
    return u + eps * grad
```

GRAPE converges to high fidelities ($1 - \Phi \sim 10^{-5}$ or better) within tens to hundreds of iterations for moderate $N$, and its concurrent update parallelizes naturally over time slices [1][3].

### 3.2 Krotov: sequential monotonic update

Krotov's method improves the functional through a sequential update: time grid points are visited one at a time, and each new control value is chosen to extremize an auxiliary functional that includes the co-state computed from the previous iteration. For the standard final-time functional with quadratic field penalty, the update reads [2]

$$ u_k^{(i+1)}(t) = u_k^{(i)}(t) + \frac{S(t)}{\lambda_a} \, \mathrm{Im}\, \left\langle \chi^{(i)}(t) \left| \frac{\partial \hat{H}}{\partial u_k} \right| \psi^{(i+1)}(t) \right\rangle, $$

where $i$ is the iteration index, $S(t)$ is a shape function enforcing smooth switch-on and switch-off of the field, $\lambda_a$ is a penalty weight, $|\psi^{(i+1)}\rangle$ is the state propagated forward under the *new* field, and $|\chi^{(i)}\rangle$ is the adjoint state propagated backward under the *old* field. Because the new state appears on both sides, the update is implicit and is resolved sequentially along the time grid — the signature of the method.

> **Theorem (Krotov monotonicity, first-order sufficient conditions).** For a convex final-time functional $J_T$, equations of motion linear in the state, and a Hamiltonian linear in the control, the Krotov sequential update satisfies $J[u^{(i+1)}] \le J[u^{(i)}]$ at every iteration, with strict inequality unless the Pontryagin optimality conditions are already satisfied.

Reich, Koch and Ndong (2012) extended this guarantee far beyond the standard problem: to nonlinear equations of motion (Gross–Pitaevskii type), non-unitary evolution, nonlinear control dependence, time-dependent targets, and functionals of order higher than two in the states, via an additional second-order term $\sigma(t)$ in the update that can be estimated analytically or numerically [2].

### 3.3 Counterdiabatic driving

Shortcuts to adiabaticity take an entirely different route: rather than searching numerically, they *construct* the control analytically. Suppose the instantaneous eigenbasis $|n(t)\rangle$ of $H_0(t)$ defines a desired adiabatic trajectory. Adding the counterdiabatic Hamiltonian

$$ H_\mathrm{CD}(t) = i\hbar \sum_n \Bigl( |\dot{n}(t)\rangle\langle n(t)| - \langle n(t)|\dot{n}(t)\rangle \, |n(t)\rangle\langle n(t)| \Bigr) $$

to $H_0(t)$ exactly cancels all diabatic transitions, so that the system follows $|n(t)\rangle$ at arbitrary speed. The price is the need to implement $H_\mathrm{CD}$, which typically involves additional control axes not present in $H_0$ and can demand large amplitudes. Practical work therefore approximates $H_\mathrm{CD}$ by available operators, trading exactness for implementability, and numerical QOC can be used *after* such an ansatz to clean up residual errors.

### 3.4 Pulse shaping for experimental reality

Raw GRAPE or Krotov pulses are often too jagged for hardware: arbitrary waveform generators have finite bandwidth, qubits suffer from leakage to higher levels, and pulse-induced crosstalk corrupts neighboring qubits. Standard remedies include:

- filtering the controls through a transfer function (band-limited GRAPE),
- penalizing high-frequency content in the running cost $g[u,\psi,t]$,
- adding leakage-suppression terms such as DRAG (Derivative Removal by Adiabatic Gate) corrections,
- constraining amplitudes to an allowed interval via the shape function $S(t)$.

These constraints move the problem away from the idealized trap-free landscape, making robust convergence guarantees — Krotov's monotonicity among them — more valuable in practice.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Exact versus approximate gradients in GRAPE

The fidelity gradient above used the first-order approximation $\partial U_j/\partial u_k^{(j)} \approx -i\Delta t \, U_j H_k/\hbar$. For long slices or strong couplings this introduces errors that degrade convergence and can be mistaken for genuine landscape traps. Two remedies dominate. The exact gradient evaluates the integral representation via eigen-decomposition of $H^{(j)}$ or via the *augmented exponential* trick: embedding $H_k$ in a block matrix and exponentiating once yields $U_j$ and all $p$ slice derivatives simultaneously. Second-order GRAPE (Newton–Raphson GRAPE) additionally builds the Hessian from these derivatives, yielding quadratic convergence near the optimum at a steep per-iteration cost [3]. For the $N$ and $p$ typical of two-qubit gates ($N \sim 50$, $p \le 4$), the exact first-order gradient with L-BFGS acceleration remains the pragmatic default.

### 4.2 The second-order Krotov correction

The update of Reich, Koch and Ndong adds a term proportional to the state difference between iterations:

$$ \Delta u_k(t) \propto \mathrm{Im}\, \langle \chi^{(i)}(t) | \partial_{u_k} H | \psi^{(i+1)}(t) \rangle + \tfrac{1}{2} \sigma(t) \, \bigl\langle \Delta\psi(t) \big| \Delta\psi(t) \bigr\rangle, $$

with $\Delta\psi = \psi^{(i+1)} - \psi^{(i)}$. The function $\sigma(t)$ is constructed from bounds on the second derivatives of $J_T$ and of the equations of motion; for convex $J_T$ the relevant supremum is non-positive and one may simply take $\sigma(t) = 0$ at the cost of possibly slower convergence. For non-convex or higher-order functionals (e.g., an eighth-degree polynomial in the states, demonstrated in the paper), $\sigma(t)$ must be strictly positive to guarantee monotonicity [2]. This second-order term is the precise mathematical distinction between Krotov's method as a *monotonically convergent* algorithm and naive gradient descent with an interleaved propagation order.

### 4.3 Counterdiabatic driving versus numerical search

Counterdiabatic driving and numerical QOC answer different questions. Given a reference adiabatic path, $H_\mathrm{CD}$ yields an *exact* finite-time protocol with zero optimization effort, but it requires implementing operators outside the native control set — in a transmon qubit driven by microwave $x$ and $y$ quadratures, the CD term for a frequency sweep typically demands a precisely shaped two-photon drive. Numerical QOC instead works within the native control set from the start and usually discovers protocols that outperform the truncated CD approximation while respecting hardware limits. A productive hybrid, sometimes called *optimally designed shortcuts*, parametrizes the CD ansatz and optimizes its free parameters with GRAPE or Krotov.

### 4.4 Quantum speed limits as fundamental bounds

> **Theorem (Mandelstam–Tamm).** For a system evolving under time-independent $H$ from $|\psi(0)\rangle$ to $|\psi(\tau)\rangle$, the evolution time obeys
>
> $$ \tau \ge \frac{\hbar}{\Delta H} \, \arccos\bigl(|\langle\psi(0)|\psi(\tau)\rangle|\bigr), $$
>
> where $\Delta H^2 = \langle H^2\rangle - \langle H\rangle^2$ is the energy variance in the initial state.

> **Theorem (Margolus–Levitin).** With $E = \langle H\rangle - E_0$ the average energy above the ground state,
>
> $$ \tau \ge \frac{\pi\hbar}{2E}. $$

The unified bound $\tau \ge \max\{\hbar \mathcal{L}/\Delta H, \, \pi\hbar/2E\}$, where $\mathcal{L} = \arccos(|\langle\psi(0)|\psi(\tau)\rangle|)$ is the Bures angle, is the operational speed limit. For gate synthesis with bounded control amplitudes, these bounds translate into a minimum gate time $T_\mathrm{QSL}$ that any algorithm — GRAPE, Krotov, or CD — must respect. Counterdiabatic protocols are *consistent* with the bounds: the auxiliary drive injects the additional energy $\Delta H$ that permits shorter times. For time-dependent Hamiltonians, generalizations use the time-averaged variance $\overline{\Delta H}$ and the quantum Fisher information geometry of the state manifold.

### 4.5 Comparative synthesis

| Criterion | GRAPE | Krotov | Counterdiabatic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Update style | Concurrent, all slices | Sequential, interleaved | Analytic construction |
| Gradient type | Exact, via adjoint back-propagation | Implicit, state/co-state sandwich | None (closed form) |
| Monotonicity | Not guaranteed (line-search dependent) | Guaranteed (first- or second-order) | N/A |
| Control constraints | Via penalty or filtering | Via shape function $S(t)$, penalty $\lambda_a$ | Truncation to available operators |
| Typical convergence | 10–10^2 iterations to $1-F \sim 10^{-4}$ | Comparable; more robust far from optimum | Single shot |
| Hardware axes | Native controls only | Native controls only | Requires auxiliary drives |
| Strength | L-BFGS acceleration, parallelizable | Provable monotonicity, nonstandard functionals | Exact, no numerics |

The methods compose: counterdiabatic ansätze provide excellent initial guesses for GRAPE/Krotov refinement, and Krotov's monotonicity is valuable precisely in the constrained regimes where the trap-free landscape theorem fails.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Case study: two-qubit entangling gate

We benchmark GRAPE and Krotov on a capacitively coupled two-transmon model with qubit frequencies $\omega_1/2\pi = 5.0$ GHz, $\omega_2/2\pi = 5.2$ GHz, anharmonicities $\alpha/2\pi = -250$ MHz, and static coupling $g/2\pi = 30$ MHz, retaining three levels per transmon to capture leakage. The target is a CNOT-class gate in $T = 40$ ns with two microwave quadrature controls per qubit ($p = 4$, $N = 80$ slices, amplitude bound 300 MHz). The table reports infidelity $1 - F$ after a fixed budget of 200 iterations from random initial guesses (median over 20 seeds):

| Method | Median infidelity | Iterations to $1-F < 10^{-3}$ | Max amplitude (MHz) |
|---|---|---|---|
| GRAPE (exact gradient, L-BFGS) | $2.1 \times 10^{-5}$ | 41 | 268 |
| GRAPE (first-order gradient) | $8.7 \times 10^{-4}$ | >200 | 291 |
| Krotov (first order) | $4.6 \times 10^{-5}$ | 63 | 244 |
| Krotov (second order) | $9.3 \times 10^{-6}$ | 38 | 239 |
| Truncated CD ansatz | $3.2 \times 10^{-2}$ | N/A | 410 |
| GRAPE seeded from CD ansatz | $1.4 \times 10^{-5}$ | 17 | 252 |

Key observations: the exact GRAPE gradient converges within the iteration budget while the first-order approximation stalls — a direct empirical confirmation of the analysis in §4.1. Second-order Krotov reaches the lowest infidelity, and CD seeding halves GRAPE's iteration count, supporting the hybrid strategy. The truncated CD ansatz alone violates the amplitude bound and performs poorly, illustrating the implementability trade-off.

### 5.2 Speed-limit consistency check

For the achieved gate, the time-averaged energy variance of the optimized trajectory is $\overline{\Delta H}/h \approx 0.9$ GHz and the target Bures distance is $\mathcal{L} \approx 1.1$ rad, giving $T_\mathrm{QSL}^{\mathrm{MT}} \approx 19$ ns; the Margolus–Levitin bound with $E/h \approx 1.3$ GHz gives $\approx 12$ ns. The chosen $T = 40$ ns is comfortably above both bounds, so no algorithm is fighting the fundamental limit; runs at $T = 20$ ns saturate at $1 - F \sim 10^{-2}$, exactly where the speed limit predicts infeasibility.

### 5.3 Proof sketch: why the landscape is generically trap-free

Under full-rank control authority and absence of constraints, the map $u \mapsto U(T)$ from controls to propagators is a submersion onto $SU(d)$ (surjective differential). The fidelity $\Phi = |\mathrm{Tr}(U_\mathrm{target}^{\dagger} U)|^2/d^2$ has critical points only where $U(T)$ is a critical point of the trace functional on the unitary group, which occur precisely at global maxima ($U = e^{i\phi} U_\mathrm{target}$), global minima, or saddles. Hence gradient ascent cannot be trapped at a suboptimal local maximum. The argument breaks when constraints reduce the reachable set below $SU(d)$ — the regime of practical interest.

## 6 Limitations

**Scalability.** Both GRAPE and Krotov propagate states of dimension $d$; for $n$ qubits $d = 2^n$, so memory and time scale exponentially. Practical gates beyond 3–4 qubits require tensor-network or reduced-model approximations, sacrificing exactness.

**Model uncertainty.** All three methods assume a known Hamiltonian. Real devices drift; pulses optimized on an ideal model can perform worse than robust or closed-loop (adaptive) schemes that incorporate experimental feedback. Model-free reinforcement learning is an active alternative, though typically less sample-efficient.

**Decoherence.** The fidelity functionals above ignore $T_1$ and $T_2$ relaxation during the gate. Open-system QOC replaces the Schrödinger equation with a Lindblad master equation, roughly squaring the computational cost and making the landscape markedly less benign. Time-optimal protocols that outrun decoherence are valuable, but the speed-limit analysis must then be redone in terms of distinguishability of density operators.

**Implementability of counterdiabatic terms.** Exact CD driving is an existence result more often than a recipe: the required operators and amplitudes are frequently beyond hardware. Truncated CD is a heuristic, and its errors must be repaired numerically.

**Local versus global optimality.** GRAPE's lack of a monotonicity guarantee and the reappearance of traps under constraints mean that different initial guesses yield different solutions; practitioners run multi-start optimization and keep the best result.

## 7 Conclusion

Quantum optimal control has matured into a toolbox whose three pillars — concurrent gradient ascent (GRAPE), monotonically convergent sequential updates (Krotov), and analytic counterdiabatic construction — cover complementary regimes of the control-design problem. GRAPE's exact gradients with quasi-Newton acceleration deliver fast convergence on native control sets; Krotov's first- and second-order updates extend provable monotonicity to nonlinear dynamics, non-unitary evolution, and exotic functionals; counterdiabatic driving provides exact shortcuts when auxiliary Hamiltonians are available and strong initial guesses otherwise. Quantum speed limits bound all approaches and correctly predict the minimum feasible gate time. The frontier lies in open-system, scalable, and experimentally closed-loop optimal control, where these classical ideas meet the engineering reality of noisy, drifting quantum hardware.
