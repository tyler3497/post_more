# The Fractional Quantum Hall Effect: Laughlin States, Composite Fermions, and Topological Order in Two-Dimensional Electron Systems

## Abstract

The fractional quantum Hall effect (FQHE), discovered in 1982 in ultra-high-mobility GaAs/AlGaAs heterostructures [1], revealed that a two-dimensional electron gas in a strong magnetic field can form incompressible quantum liquids with Hall conductance quantized at *fractional* multiples of e²/h. Because the kinetic energy is quenched within a Landau level, the phenomenon is intrinsically many-body: no single-particle picture accounts for plateaus at ν = 1/3, 2/5, 3/7, …, or the even-denominator state at ν = 5/2. This article develops the three theoretical pillars of the field — Laughlin's trial wavefunction and its classical plasma mapping [2], Jain's composite-fermion construction [3], and the Moore–Read paired state with non-Abelian quasiparticles [4] — alongside topological order, ground-state degeneracy [5], and chiral edge physics. We survey the experiments confirming fractional charge, fractional statistics, and non-Abelian signatures, and delineate the open problems that remain, from the microscopic identity of the ν = 5/2 state to the direct observation of anyon braiding.

---

## 1. Introduction

The integer quantum Hall effect (IQHE), discovered by von Klitzing in 1980, is by now understood as a single-particle phenomenon: Landau quantization opens cyclotron gaps at integer filling factors, disorder localizes the states between Landau levels, and gauge invariance pins the Hall conductance to integer multiples of e²/h. The fractional quantum Hall effect admits no such simple explanation. When the lowest Landau level (LLL) is only *partially* filled, the non-interacting spectrum is macroscopically degenerate — every many-body configuration has identical kinetic energy — so the ground state is selected entirely by the Coulomb interaction. The resulting phases are among the most striking examples of *emergence* in condensed matter: the elementary excitations carry a fraction of the electron's charge, obey exchange statistics intermediate between bosons and fermions, and in certain states realize non-Abelian anyons whose braiding could underpin fault-tolerant quantum computation [9].

The intellectual arc runs from experimental surprise to theoretical synthesis: Laughlin's variational wavefunction and plasma mapping [2] explained the ν = 1/3 plateau within a year of its discovery; Haldane's hierarchy [7] and Halperin's statistics analysis [8] systematized the observed fractions; Jain's composite fermions [3] unified them microscopically; and the even-denominator ν = 5/2 state [10] forced the field beyond Abelian order into the Moore–Read paired state [4] and Wen's topological order [5].

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the Landau-level problem and the experimental setting. Section 3 describes the theoretical methodologies — trial wavefunctions, exact diagonalization, plasma Monte Carlo, and effective field theory — that make the strongly correlated regime tractable. Section 4 presents the deep theoretical core: the Laughlin state, anyonic quasiparticles, composite fermions, and the Moore–Read state. Section 5 surveys experimental confirmations and the theoretical guarantees behind quantization. Section 6 discusses limitations and open questions, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The two-dimensional electron gas

The FQHE is observed in semiconductor heterostructures — most famously the GaAs/AlₓGa₁₋ₓAs interface — where band bending confines electrons to a layer only a few nanometers thick, realizing an effectively two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG). The enabling advance was *modulation doping* via molecular beam epitaxy: donors sit in a setback layer, minimizing scattering and pushing mobilities beyond 10⁶ cm²/V·s. Experiments are performed at dilution-refrigerator temperatures (tens of millikelvin) and magnetic fields up to tens of tesla, applied perpendicular to the plane.

### 2.2 Landau quantization

A charged particle in a uniform field **B** = Bẑ executes cyclotron motion with frequency ω_c = eB/m*. Quantum mechanically the kinetic energy collapses into discrete Landau levels,

$$E_n = \hbar\omega_c\left(n + \tfrac{1}{2}\right), \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$

each with macroscopic degeneracy per unit area n_B = eB/h = 1/(2πℓ_B²), where the *magnetic length* ℓ_B = √(ℏ/eB) sets the quantum of area per flux quantum ϕ₀ = h/e. The dimensionless *filling factor*

$$\nu = \frac{n}{n_B} = \frac{nh}{eB} = 2\pi\ell_B^2 n$$

measures the ratio of electron density n to the degeneracy of one Landau level. In the symmetric gauge, LLL single-particle orbitals take the form z^m exp(−|z|²/4ℓ_B²) with z = x + iy, so that any N-electron LLL wavefunction is an analytic polynomial in the {zᵢ} times a universal Gaussian factor.

### 2.3 Why the fractional effect is hard

The crucial point is that within a single Landau level the kinetic energy is a constant. Projecting the Coulomb interaction into the LLL yields a Hamiltonian with *no* small parameter: the only energy scales are e²/ελ_B (interactions), ℏω_c (Landau-level spacing), and disorder. At partial filling the non-interacting ground-state manifold has dimension growing exponentially with system size, and the true ground state is selected by correlations alone. Perturbation theory is useless; the problem demands non-perturbative methods. Experimentally, as B is swept at fixed density, R_H locks onto plateaus at R_H = h/(νe²) with ν = p/q rational — most prominently odd denominators — while R_xx drops to zero, signaling a gapped, incompressible bulk [1].

## 3. Methodology

With no controlled expansion parameter available, the field relies on a distinctive blend of analytical and numerical techniques:

- **Trial wavefunctions.** The Laughlin, Jain composite-fermion, and Moore–Read states are variational ansätze whose correlations are built in by construction. Their overlaps with numerically exact ground states of small systems routinely exceed 0.99, and — remarkably — several are *exact* ground states of model short-range Hamiltonians (notably the Trugman–Kivelson hard-core interaction for the Laughlin state).
- **Exact diagonalization.** On the sphere (Haldane's geometry, which eliminates edges) or the torus, the Coulomb Hamiltonian projected to the LLL can be diagonalized for up to roughly 15–20 electrons. Gaps, quantum numbers, and overlaps with trial states are extracted directly.
- **Classical plasma mapping and Monte Carlo.** Laughlin's mapping of |Ψ|² to the Boltzmann weight of a two-dimensional one-component plasma converts quantum expectation values into classical statistical mechanics, evaluable by Metropolis Monte Carlo for hundreds of particles. A minimal illustration of the sampling kernel is:
```python
import numpy as np

def laughlin_logprob(z, m, lB=1.0):
    """log |Psi_m|^2 for the Laughlin state (up to normalization)."""
    i, j = np.triu_indices(len(z), k=1)
    pair = m * np.sum(np.log(np.abs(z[i] - z[j]) + 1e-300))
    gauss = -np.sum(np.abs(z) ** 2) / (4.0 * lB ** 2)
    return 2.0 * pair + gauss

def metropolis_step(z, m, step=0.5):
    zp = z + step * (np.random.randn(*z.shape) + 1j * np.random.randn(*z.shape))
    if np.log(np.random.rand()) < laughlin_logprob(zp, m) - laughlin_logprob(z, m):
        return zp
    return z
```
  Thermodynamic quantities — energy, pair correlation function g(r), structure factor — follow from long Markov chains, and confirm the liquid character of the state.
- **Effective field theory.** At energies below the bulk gap, the system is described by topological Chern–Simons theories; the K-matrix formalism classifies all Abelian states, their quasiparticle charges, statistics, and edge structures.
- **Conformal field theory (CFT) correspondence.** Moore and Read showed that trial wavefunctions arise as conformal blocks of rational CFTs [4], a dictionary that predicts quasiparticle fusion and braiding data and extends naturally to non-Abelian states.
- **Modern numerics.** Density-matrix renormalization group (DMRG) on cylinders accesses ground-state energies, entanglement spectra, and topological entanglement entropy far beyond exact-diagonalization sizes.

> **Theorem (Bulk–edge correspondence).** *For an Abelian quantum Hall state described by a K-matrix, the number and chirality of gapless edge modes, the quantized Hall conductance σ_xy = (e²/h) tᵀK⁻¹t, and the fractional charges and mutual statistics of bulk quasiparticles are all determined by the same topological data (K, t). Distinct bulk topological orders imply distinct universal edge theories.*

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Laughlin Wavefunction and the Plasma Analogy

Laughlin's 1983 proposal for the ground state at ν = 1/m (m odd) is [2]

$$\Psi_m(\{z_i\}) = \prod_{i<j}(z_i - z_j)^m \, \exp\!\left(-\sum_i \frac{|z_i|^2}{4\ell_B^2}\right).$$

The Jastrow factor forces the wavefunction to vanish as m powers of the inter-electron separation — a strong short-range correlation that minimizes Coulomb repulsion. Odd m guarantees antisymmetry under exchange, as required for fermions. The total angular momentum M = mN(N−1)/2 corresponds to a droplet of uniform density n = 1/(2πmℓ_B²), i.e., filling factor ν = 1/m.

The stroke of genius was the *plasma analogy*. The probability density can be written as a classical Boltzmann weight,

$$|\Psi_m|^2 = e^{-\beta\Phi}, \qquad \beta = \frac{2}{m},$$

with the effective "potential energy"

$$\Phi = -m^2 \sum_{i<j} \ln|z_i - z_j| + \frac{m}{4\ell_B^2}\sum_i |z_i|^2.$$

This is the energy of a two-dimensional one-component plasma: particles of "charge" m interacting via the logarithmic 2D Coulomb potential, neutralized by a uniform background of density σ = 1/(2πℓ_B²). The fictitious temperature is T = 1/β = m/2, and the plasma coupling parameter is Γ = 2m. Classical plasmas are *liquids* for Γ ≲ 140 and crystallize above; for m = 3 (Γ = 6) the system is deep in the liquid phase, and Monte Carlo studies of the equivalent plasma confirm a uniform, isotropic fluid with no broken symmetry [2]. Screening in the plasma implies that the electron density is uniform and that density fluctuations cost a finite energy — the signature of an *incompressible* quantum fluid with a bulk excitation gap.

Laughlin further showed incompressibility via a gauge argument: adiabatically threading a flux quantum ϕ₀ through a thin solenoid at the origin pumps exactly charge e/m from the interior to the edge, proving the existence of quasiparticle excitations of fractional charge e* = ±e/m. The quasihole wavefunction,

$$\Psi_{\text{qh}}(\eta; \{z_i\}) = \prod_i (z_i - \eta)\,\Psi_m(\{z_i\}),$$

inserts a vortex-like zero at position η; plasma screening arguments show the missing charge accumulates to e/m.

### 4.2 Quasiparticles: Fractional Charge and Anyonic Statistics

Fractional charge alone would be remarkable; the *statistics* of these excitations is revolutionary. In two spatial dimensions, particle exchange need not square to the identity — the relevant group is the braid group, not the permutation group — so the many-body wavefunction may acquire an arbitrary phase e^{iθ} under counterclockwise exchange. Arovas, Schrieffer, and Wilczek computed the Berry phase accumulated when one Laughlin quasihole is adiabatically transported around another and found [6]

$$\theta = \frac{\pi}{m},$$

i.e., the quasiparticles are *anyons*, intermediate between bosons (θ = 0) and fermions (θ = π). Halperin generalized the analysis, showing that for a state at ν = p/q the quasihole carries charge e/q and statistical angle determined by the hierarchy [8]. Haldane's hierarchy construction [7] showed that condensing the quasiparticles of a parent Laughlin state into their own incompressible daughter states generates the full observed sequences (2/5, 3/7, … from 1/3; 2/3, 3/5, … by particle–hole conjugation), explaining the odd-denominator predominance: each level of the hierarchy preserves the fermionic nature of the underlying electrons only for odd denominators.

| State ν = 1/m | Quasiparticle charge e* | Statistical angle θ | Plasma Γ = 2m |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1/3 | e/3 | π/3 | 6 |
| 1/5 | e/5 | π/5 | 10 |
| 1/7 | e/7 | π/7 | 14 |

*Table: Abelian Laughlin states. All lie deep in the plasma liquid regime (Γ ≪ 140), consistent with uniform incompressible fluids.*

### 4.3 Composite Fermions and the Jain Sequences

Jain's 1989 composite-fermion (CF) theory [3] reframes the entire problem. The proposal: each electron *binds* an even number 2p of quantized vortices (equivalently, 2p flux quanta), forming a new emergent particle — the composite fermion. Because vortices carry no charge but each contributes a Berry phase, the bound state remains fermionic, while the attached flux partially cancels the external magnetic field. The CFs therefore experience a reduced effective field

$$B^* = B - 2p\,\rho\phi_0,$$

where ρ is the electron density. The trial wavefunctions take the elegant form

$$\Psi_{\nu} = \mathcal{P}_{\text{LLL}} \prod_{i<j}(z_i - z_j)^{2p}\,\Phi_{\nu^*}(\{z_i\}),$$

where Φ_{ν*} is the Slater determinant of ν* filled Landau levels of *non-interacting* composite fermions (their "Λ levels"), and 𝒫_LLL projects back to the electronic lowest Landau level. The electronic and CF filling factors are related by

$$\nu = \frac{\nu^*}{2p\nu^* \pm 1},$$

so that integer CF fillings ν* = n map to the celebrated *Jain sequences* ν = n/(2pn ± 1): for p = 1, the series 1/3, 2/5, 3/7, … and 2/3, 3/5, 4/7, … — precisely the fractions observed most prominently in experiment. The FQHE of electrons is thus the *IQHE of composite fermions*: incompressibility at fractional ν is inherited from cyclotron gaps at integer ν*.

At even-denominator fillings ν = 1/2, 1/4 the effective field vanishes and CFs form a *Fermi sea* — a compressible metal with Fermi wavevector k_F = √(4πρ), confirmed by surface-acoustic-wave, magnetic-focusing, and geometric-resonance experiments. The CF effective mass, set by interactions rather than the band mass, diverges logarithmically as ν → 1/2, as later elaborated in the Halperin–Lee–Read Chern–Simons theory. Overlaps of Jain wavefunctions with exact-diagonalization ground states exceed 0.99 across the principal sequences, making the CF picture the most quantitatively successful microscopic theory of the LLL FQHE.

### 4.4 Non-Abelian States: The Moore–Read Pfaffian at ν = 5/2

The 1987 observation of a quantized plateau at ν = 5/2 — an *even* denominator — shattered the odd-denominator paradigm [10]. Occurring in the first excited (N = 1) Landau level, where the Haldane pseudopotentials are softer at short range, the 5/2 state cannot be a Laughlin or hierarchy state. Moore and Read proposed a fundamentally new class of wavefunction [4]:

$$\Psi_{\text{MR}} = \operatorname{Pf}\!\left(\frac{1}{z_i - z_j}\right) \prod_{i<j}(z_i - z_j)^m \exp\!\left(-\sum_i \frac{|z_i|^2}{4\ell_B^2}\right),$$

with m even (m = 2 for ν = 1/2 of the partially filled level, i.e., total ν = 5/2). The Pfaffian — the square root of the determinant of the antisymmetric matrix 1/(zᵢ − zⱼ) — encodes *p-wave pairing* of composite fermions, analogous to a spinless pₓ + ip_y superconductor (as made explicit by Read and Green). The state is the conformal block of the Ising CFT tensored with a compactified boson, and its quasiholes are the Ising anyons σ with quantum dimension √2 and fusion rule

$$\sigma \times \sigma = \mathbf{1} + \psi.$$

Consequently, 2n quasiholes span a 2^{n−1}-dimensional degenerate Hilbert space, and braiding operations act as unitary matrices on this space rather than mere phases — *non-Abelian statistics* [4, 9]. Nayak and Wilczek showed that braiding in this representation is sufficiently rich to motivate topological quantum computation, where quantum information stored in the nonlocal degenerate manifold is intrinsically protected from local decoherence [9].

> **Theorem (Non-Abelian degeneracy).** *In the Moore–Read state, 2n well-separated charge-e/4 quasiholes span a topologically protected Hilbert space of dimension 2^{n−1}. Adiabatic braiding of quasiholes implements unitary transformations within this manifold determined by the Ising braid group representation; no local measurement can distinguish the degenerate states.*

Competing candidates — the anti-Pfaffian (particle–hole conjugate) and the particle–hole-symmetric Pfaffian — differ in their edge-mode content and thermal Hall conductance, making the ν = 5/2 state an active experimental battleground (Section 5).

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

**Quantized transport.** The defining observations remain the plateaus themselves: R_H = h/(νe²) quantized to parts in 10⁹ at fractions including 1/3, 2/5, 3/7, 4/9, 2/3, 3/5, 4/7, 5/9 and beyond, with thermally activated R_xx yielding bulk gaps of order a few kelvin [1]. Laughlin's gauge argument guarantees that quantization follows from incompressibility plus gauge invariance alone, independent of microscopic details.

**Fractional charge.** Shot-noise measurements directly detect the granularity of charge transport: the current noise S = 2e*I in the weak-backscattering regime revealed e* = e/3 at ν = 1/3 [11], confirmed independently in a second laboratory. Resonant-tunneling and antidot electrometry experiments corroborate the fractional charge.

**Edge physics and the chiral Luttinger liquid.** The gapless edge of a ν = 1/m Laughlin state is not a Fermi liquid but a *chiral Luttinger liquid* with interaction parameter g = ν [12]. Tunneling of electrons into the edge is suppressed as I ∝ V^{2m−1}, while quasiparticle tunneling scales as I ∝ V^{2/m−1} — power laws confirmed in cleaved-edge-overgrowth tunneling experiments, a striking validation of the topological edge theory.

**Topological order and ground-state degeneracy.** Wen and Niu showed that FQHE states possess *topological order*: on a torus, the ν = p/q Laughlin state has exactly q degenerate ground states, a degeneracy robust against any local perturbation and invisible to any local order parameter [5]. This degeneracy — not symmetry breaking — is the defining fingerprint of the phase, and DMRG calculations of topological entanglement entropy γ = ln√q confirm it numerically.

**The ν = 5/2 frontier.** Interferometry experiments in Fabry–Pérot geometries observe even–odd alternation of interference periodicities consistent with non-Abelian e/4 quasiparticles, and thermal Hall measurements report half-integer quantized values consistent with a non-Abelian topological order — though whether the realized phase is the Pfaffian, anti-Pfaffian, or particle–hole Pfaffian remains contested. The measured activation gap (~0.5 K in the best samples) and the extreme sensitivity to disorder, Landau-level mixing, and spin polarization make this the most delicate and most consequential open identification problem in the field.

## 6. Limitations

The theory rests on idealizations that limit its quantitative reach:

1. **No small parameter.** The LLL-projected Coulomb problem has no controlled expansion; trial wavefunctions are variational, and Landau-level mixing (parameterized by κ = (e²/ελ_B)/ℏω_c, of order unity in real samples) is typically treated perturbatively or neglected.
2. **Realistic complications.** Finite quantum-well thickness softens short-range repulsion, disorder broadens transitions, and spin physics (skyrmions near ν = 1, spin transitions at 2/5 and 2/3) enriches but complicates the phase diagram.
3. **Exotic fractions.** States at 4/11, 5/13, and 4/13 lie outside the primary Jain sequences and require interacting composite fermions or higher-order hierarchies; their theoretical description remains unsettled.
4. **The 5/2 identity.** Numerical studies on finite systems favor the Pfaffian/anti-Pfaffian depending on Landau-level mixing and finite-thickness modeling, while thermal Hall data have been interpreted as favoring the particle–hole Pfaffian — a genuine three-way ambiguity.
5. **Braiding unobserved.** Despite compelling circumstantial evidence, no experiment has yet directly demonstrated the non-Abelian braiding transformation of quasiparticles; interferometry remains vulnerable to Coulomb-blockade and decoherence artifacts.
6. **Numerical ceilings.** Exact diagonalization is limited to roughly 20 electrons; DMRG reaches further but struggles with the long-range Coulomb interaction and gapless edge modes on cylinders.

## 7. Conclusion

The fractional quantum Hall effect transformed our understanding of quantum phases of matter. From Laughlin's plasma-mapped wavefunction [2] through Jain's composite fermions [3] to the non-Abelian Moore–Read state [4], strong correlations have repeatedly produced *emergent* fractionally charged anyons governed by *topological order* rather than symmetry breaking [5]. The experimental confirmations, from shot-noise detection of e/3 charge [11] to interferometric and thermal signatures of non-Abelian statistics at ν = 5/2 [9, 10], attest to a theoretical framework of unusual depth and predictive power. The outstanding challenges — the microscopic identity of the 5/2 state, the direct observation of braiding, and the extension of these ideas to fractional Chern insulators and moiré heterostructures — ensure that the fractional quantum Hall effect will remain a central laboratory for topological quantum matter and a candidate platform for fault-tolerant quantum computation for decades to come.
