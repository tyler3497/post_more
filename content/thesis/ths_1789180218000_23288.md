# Generalized Consensus and the EPaxos Family: Fast Paths, Dependency Graphs, and Commutativity-Based Ordering

## Abstract

Classical state-machine replication (SMR) derives total order from a distinguished leader: Multi-Paxos assigns ecommand a log position through a single sequencer, an architecture that concentrates failure risk and wide-area latency at one site. Generalized consensus weakens this requirement — only *non-commuting* commands must be ordered — and Egalitarian Paxos (EPaxos) turned that insight into the first practical leaderless SMR protocol: ereplica may propose, interference is recorded in an explicit dependency graph, and commands commit on a *fast path* in two message delays when quorums agree and concurrent commands commute. This treatise reconstructs the EPaxos family from first principles: Lamport's generalized consensus and its fast-ballot lower bounds, the quorum arithmetic of fast and slow paths, dependency-graph construction with strongly-connected-component execution, and the recobugs identified by Sutra and repaired by the provably correct EPaxos* variant. It surveys empirical results from geo-replicated deployments, the protocol's descendants (Janus, Caesar, BPaxos), and the recently established optimal e-fast/f-resilient tradeoff n ≥ max{2e + f − 1, 2f + 1}.

## 1 Introduction

Replicated state machines must apply the same commands in a linearizable order. Classical Paxos [5] funnels eproposal through a leader that assigns sequence numbers — at once the protocol's performance engine and its Achilles' heel. In geo-replicated deployments, a Sydney client proposing to a Virginia leader pays a full trans-Pacific round trip before dissemination begins [1]; when the leader fails, the service stalls through a view change.

*Generalized consensus* observes that total order is stronger than necessary. If two commands commute — reads on disjoint keys, increments of distinct counters, idempotent no-ops — their relative order is unobservable to any client, and forcing them through a single ordering bottleneck is pure overhead. Lamport's generalized Paxos [7] formalized this: consensus need only agree on a *partial* order consistent with a commutativity relation, collapsing to classic Paxos when every pair of commands interferes.

Egalitarian Paxos (EPaxos), introduced by Moraru, Andersen, and Kaminsky [1], is the landmark protocol that made generalized consensus *leaderless* and *fast* simultaneously. Its three design pillars are:

1. **Egalitarianism.** Every replica is simultaneously proposer, acceptor, and replica. There is no leader, no view change, and no single process whose failure halts progress: the protocol sustains non-zero throughput with up to f crashes among n = 2f + 1 processes [2].
2. **The fast path.** A command proposed when at most e = ⌈(f+1)/2⌉ other processes have failed, and when all concurrently submitted commands commute with it, commits in *two message delays* (one round trip) — optimal wide-area latency for the common case [1, 5].
3. **Dependency graphs.** Instead of a log, each replica maintains a directed graph whose edges record interference between commands; execution linearizes the graph by its strongly connected components (SCCs), ordering only what must be ordered [4].

This architecture proved enormously influential — and enormously subtle. Sutra [3] identified a correctness bug in EPaxos's ballot-recovery logic that could break linearizability; a decade of follow-ups (Caesar, Janus [6], BPaxos [6]) inherited both the ideas and, in some cases, the bugs. Only recently has EPaxos* [2] supplied a provably correct, pedagogically clean variant that is optimal across the full (e, f) tradeoff spectrum. This treatise presents the family as a coherent whole: the mathematics that make it work, the experiments that justify it, and the failure modes that nearly broke it.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Paxos to Generalized Paxos

Single-decree Paxos proceeds in two phases: *prepare* (ballot b, phase 1) and *accept* (phase 2), with safety resting on the invariant that any two quorums intersect. Multi-Paxos amortizes phase 1 across a stable leader, who then drives a growing sequence of phase-2 instances — a *log*. Generalized Paxos [7] retains the ballot structure but replaces the log with a partially ordered set of commands: acceptors vote for *sequences*, and two sequences accepted by quorums must be *compatible*, meaning one extends the other up to commutation. When all commands commute, every ballot is "fast": proposals go straight to acceptors with no leader arbitration.

Lamport's fast-ballot analysis [5] established the fundamental price of speed. A *fast* protocol — one in which some process can decide in two message delays — requires larger quorums than classic Paxos, because recovery must distinguish a fast decision from a collision. The classical bound is n ≥ max{2e + f + 1, 2f + 1}, where e is the number of failures tolerated on the fast path [2, 5]. Recent work [5] sharpens this to n ≥ max{2e + f − 1, 2f + 1} under a pragmatic definition of fastness (only the *proxy* replica that received the client request must decide fast), and shows the bound is tight.

### 2.2 Mencius: Rotating Leadership Without Generalization

Mencius [8] partitions Multi-Paxos log instances round-robin among replicas, eliminating the single leader but *not* the total order: an entry cannot execute until all preceding entries are decided, so one slow replica stalls the prefix. Mencius is multi-leader but *not* generalized — commutativity buys it nothing, and its all-to-all traffic limits scalability [6]. EPaxos is its generalized successor: where Mencius distributes *log positions*, EPaxos distributes *ordering decisions*, and commuting commands execute without waiting on slow owners.

### 2.3 Fast Paxos and the Collision Problem

Fast Paxos lets proposers bypass the leader for two-delay commits absent *collisions* (concurrent proposals), which force slow recovery. EPaxos inherits this optimism but redefines collisions via commutativity: concurrent proposals that commute are not collisions at all. Replacing *temporal* concurrency with *semantic* interference as the slow-path trigger is what lets EPaxos stay fast under realistic few-percent conflict rates [6].

### 2.4 The Leaderless Landscape

EPaxos sits at the intersection of the two taxonomies formalized in [4] — *leaderless* and *generalized* — with descendants exploring the space around it:

| Protocol | Leaderless | Generalized | Fast path | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Multi-Paxos | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ | Stable leader, total log |
| Mencius [8] | ✓ (rotating) | ✗ | ✗ | Log still totally ordered |
| Fast Paxos | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ | Leader recovers collisions |
| Generalized Paxos [7] | ✗ | ✓ | ✓ | Leader arbitrates conflicts |
| **EPaxos** [1] | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | Dependency graphs, SCC execution |
| Caesar | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | Timestamps bias fast-path odds |
| BPaxos [6] | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ | Modular; 8 delays, simpler |
| Janus | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | EPaxos adapted to transactions |

## 3 Methodology

This treatise is a *reconstructive survey*: we re-derive the protocol's mechanisms from the primary sources [1, 2, 3, 4, 7] and verify each quantitative claim against published measurements or specifications.

- **Specification analysis.** We trace the EPaxos ballot protocol through its TLA+ specification, paying special attention to the recovery path where Sutra [3] located the linearizability bug, and to the simplified EPaxos* recovery [2] that repairs it.
- **Quorum arithmetic.** Fast-quorum and slow-quorum sizes are derived from first principles and checked against the optimal (e, f) tradeoff theorem of [2, 5].
- **Empirical grounding.** Latency and throughput figures are taken from the SOSP'13 evaluation [1], the BPaxos head-to-head comparison [6], and the Janus transaction-layer study — all geo-replicated, all with conflict-rate sweeps.
- **Executable models.** The dependency-graph execution algorithm (§4.2) is presented as executable Python pseudocode, and the core ballot invariant as a TLA+ fragment, so that the reader can test the claims directly.

All citations reference peer-reviewed papers, technical reports, or arXiv preprints with stable identifiers; no claim rests on an unverifiable source.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Fast Path: Quorum Arithmetic and Two-Delay Commit

In EPaxos every command *c* is proposed by its *command leader* — typically the nearest replica — which broadcasts `PreAccept(c)` to a fast quorum Q_fast. Each recipient replies with the commands it has seen that *interfere* with c; the union becomes dep(c). If all replies agree on dep(c), the leader broadcasts `Accept(c, dep(c))` to a classic quorum and, upon a majority of acknowledgements, c is *committed*: one round trip to the fast quorum plus one to the accept quorum — four message delays, two round trips — versus Multi-Paxos's forwarded request plus two leader-driven phases [1].

Let n = 2f + 1. A classic quorum is any f + 1 processes. A fast quorum must satisfy two constraints: (i) any two fast quorums intersect in a correct process despite e failures, so conflicting fast decisions cannot both complete; and (ii) a fast quorum and a recovery quorum intersect in enough processes for recovery to reconstruct a possibly-committed fast decision. The EPaxos choice is:

> **Theorem (Fast-quorum sizing [1, 2]):** With n = 2f + 1 and fast-path resilience e = ⌈(f+1)/2⌉, a fast quorum of size q_fast = f + ⌊(f+1)/2⌋ + 1 intersects every classic quorum of size f + 1 in at least ⌊(f+1)/2⌋ + 1 processes, which suffices for recovery to detect any fast-path decision. No smaller fast quorum admits e-fast recovery.

Concretely: for f = 1 (n = 3), q_fast = 3 — *all* replicas must agree, which is why Janus [6] conservatively used full fast quorums. For f = 2 (n = 5), q_fast = 4. The slow path, taken when fast-quorum replies disagree on dep(c) (a genuine interference collision) or when too many replicas are unreachable, runs a classic Paxos ballot to agree on a single dependency set, adding one round trip [1].

The subtlety that broke the original protocol lives here. During recovery, a new ballot leader must choose a dependency set from phase-1b replies; Sutra [3] showed EPaxos's merge rule could *drop* an edge a fast quorum had committed, letting replicas execute interfering commands in different orders — a linearizability violation. The bug is in the *handoff* between fast and slow paths. EPaxos* [2] replaces the rule with a simpler invariant — *propose the union of all dependency sets reported by a recovery quorum, then close under interference* — proved correct for thrifty and non-thrifty variants.

```tla+
---- MODULE EFastPath ----
EXTENDS Integers, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Replicas, Commands, Quorums
VARIABLES deps,      \* deps[c] : dependency set of command c
          ballot,    \* ballot[c] : highest ballot seen for c
          committed  \* set of committed commands

\* Safety invariant: committed interfering commands agree on order
TypeOK == /\ deps \in [Commands -> SUBSET Commands]
          /\ committed \subseteq Commands

\* EPaxos* recovery invariant: recovery proposes the quorum-closure
RecoverySafe(Q, c) ==
  LET reported == UNION {depsSeen[p][c] : p \in Q} IN
  deps[c] = ClosureUnderInterference(reported)
=============================
```

### 4.2 Dependency Graphs and SCC-Based Execution

Each replica maintains a *dependency graph*: vertices are command instances, with a directed edge c → d whenever d ∈ dep(c) — the quorum observed that c interferes with the earlier command d. Edges point newer → older, making the graph a faithful record of the agreed ordering constraints.

Cycles arise naturally: if c and d are proposed concurrently and interfere, c's quorum may see d while d's quorum sees c, producing edges both ways. EPaxos therefore executes *strongly connected components* in reverse topological order of the condensation DAG, applying commands within each SCC in deterministic instance-number order:

```python
def execute(dep_graph):
    """dep_graph: dict cmd -> set of interfering predecessors."""
    sccs = strongly_connected_components(dep_graph)  # Tarjan's algorithm
    order = topological_sort(condensation_dag(dep_graph, sccs))
    for scc in reversed(order):          # dependencies first
        for cmd in sorted(scc, key=instance_id):  # deterministic tie-break
            apply_to_state_machine(cmd)
```

> **Theorem (Execution consistency [1, 4]):** If all replicas eventually agree on dep(c) for every committed command c, then every replica computes the same condensation DAG and applies interfering commands in the same relative order; commuting commands may be applied in any order without observable difference.

This is the sense in which EPaxos is *generalized*: the agreed object is a partial order. Two consequences follow. First, *thriftiness*: replicas contact only fast quorums and lazily disseminate the rest, cutting message complexity from O(n²) toward O(n) [1, 2]. Second, *graceful degradation*: as interference rises, only the conflicting subgraph serializes — the commuting majority still takes the fast path, unlike Mencius where one slow owner stalls everything [8, 6].

### 4.3 Commutativity: The Semantic Core

Everything hinges on the interference relation c ≁ d, supplied by the application and treated as an oracle. Typical instantiations:

- **Key-value store:** `get(k)` commutes with everything except `set(k)` on the same key; two `set`s on the same key interfere [6].
- **Counters / CRDTs:** increments on the same counter commute (the operation is the state update, not the read-modify-write), so counter workloads are almost entirely fast-path [7].
- **Transactions (Janus):** two transactions interfere iff their read/write sets intersect on at least one key; Janus layers this over EPaxos's per-shard graphs to get one-round-trip commits in the best case [6].

The relation need not be statically decidable: it may be conservative (declare interference when unsure), since over-approximation costs only slow-path detours, never safety. What matters is that it is *deterministic and replica-consistent* — every replica must compute the same dep(c) from the same replies, or the SCC execution theorem fails. This is also why EPaxos* keeps the failure-free path byte-identical to the original [2]: the fast path was never the broken part.

*EPaxos is what Fast Paxos becomes when "collision" is redefined semantically rather than temporally.* Fast Paxos treats any two concurrent proposals as conflicting; EPaxos asks whether they interfere. Under the low conflict rates typical in practice [6], this converts almost every concurrent proposal from a collision into a fast commit.

### 4.4 Recovery, Ballots, and the EPaxos* Repair

When the fast path fails — disagreeing dep sets, unreachable replicas, a dead command leader — any replica may start a classic ballot. Safety demands the Paxos invariant lifted to dependency sets: *if any quorum may have committed c with dep set D, recovery must propose a compatible superset D' ⊇ D.* The original recovery *merged* reported dependency sets via intricate ballot-number case analysis; Sutra's counterexample [3] shows the merge dropping an edge a fast quorum had committed, after which replicas execute the two endpoint commands in opposite orders.

EPaxos* [2] replaces the case analysis with one rule — union all reported dependency sets and close under interference — and proves the Paxos invariant holds. The key lemma: any fast-committed dep set D is contained in the reported union of *every* recovery quorum, since fast and recovery quorums intersect in enough correct processes (§4.1). Beyond the bug fix, EPaxos* generalizes the protocol to the full optimal tradeoff curve:

> **Theorem (Optimal e-fast/f-resilient SMR [2, 5]):** For n processes, an SMR protocol that is f-resilient (makes progress despite f crashes) and e-fast (commits in two message delays despite e failures on the fast path) exists *iff* n ≥ max{2e + f − 1, 2f + 1}, and EPaxos* attains the bound.

The classical EPaxos operating point — f = ⌊(n−1)/2⌋, e = ⌈(f+1)/2⌉ — is one point on this curve; the theorem reveals the whole frontier, letting operators trade total resilience f against fast-path resilience e at fixed n. This strictly improves on Lamport's earlier bound n ≥ max{2e + f + 1, 2f + 1} [2] by adopting the pragmatic definition of fastness from [5].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 Geo-Replicated Performance (SOSP'13)

Moraru et al. [1] evaluated EPaxos across five EC2 regions (Virginia, California, Ireland, Singapore, Tokyo) against Multi-Paxos and Mencius. The headline results:

- **Wide-area latency:** with the leader in Virginia, a Tokyo client's Multi-Paxos commit pays ~2× the intercontinental RTT; EPaxos's nearest-replica fast path commits in a single regional round trip, cutting 99th-percentile latency by up to 50% under low conflict rates [1].
- **Throughput:** because every replica proposes concurrently, EPaxos saturates aggregate wide-area bandwidth rather than the leader's NIC, outperforming Multi-Paxos by 1.5–2× at scale [1].
- **Failure resilience:** killing the "leader" region halts Multi-Paxos until re-election; EPaxos throughput dips only by the lost region's share and recovers without reconfiguration [1].
- **Conflict sensitivity:** at 0–2% conflict rates (typical of the evaluated key-value workloads), >95% of commands take the fast path; at 100% conflict the protocol degenerates to slow-path throughput comparable to Multi-Paxos — the price of generality [1, 6].

### 5.2 Head-to-Head: BPaxos

BPaxos [6] reimplemented EPaxos in Scala and compared it against its own modular redesign: EPaxos's fast path achieves *four* network delays best-case versus BPaxos's eight (modularity costs round trips through extra layers), but EPaxos's tight coupling — every node simultaneously leader, dependency node, proposer, acceptor, and replica — concentrates load on bottleneck nodes and resists disaggregation [6].

### 5.3 The Transaction Layer: Janus

Janus [6] ports EPaxos's dependency graphs to general cross-shard transactions: each shard runs an EPaxos-like commit, and cross-shard transactions union their per-shard dependency sets — committing in *one* wide-area round trip in the best case, two under conflicts. Its TPC-C evaluation shows ~2× the throughput of TAPIR-like baselines at low contention, demonstrating that commutativity-based ordering composes beyond single-key operations [6].

### 5.4 Proof Sketches

*Safety (agreement on interfering order).* By the quorum-intersection argument, any two committed dep sets for interfering commands are comparable; SCC execution then yields identical orders at all replicas (Theorem, §4.2) [1, 2].

*Liveness (f-resilience).* With at most f crashes, some classic quorum of f + 1 correct processes remains; any command proposed to it eventually completes a ballot, because EPaxos ballots satisfy the standard Paxos termination argument under partial synchrony [2, 4].

*Fast-path optimality.* The lower bound n ≥ max{2e + f − 1, 2f + 1} [5] is matched by EPaxos*'s quorum construction (§4.4), closing a gap open since Lamport's original fast-Paxos analysis.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Conflict-rate cliff.** EPaxos's advantage evaporates as interference rises: at high conflict rates the slow path dominates, dependency sets bloat, and execution serializes through large SCCs. Workloads with inherently sequential hot keys (e.g., a single global counter incremented transactionally) see no benefit over Multi-Paxos [1, 6].
2. **Dependency-set size.** dep(c) can grow linearly with concurrency, inflating PreAccept replies and the committed metadata. Thriftiness mitigates but does not eliminate this; it is the fundamental space cost of recording a partial order explicitly.
3. **Execution stalls on missing predecessors.** A replica cannot execute c until dep(c) is committed *and* received. A slow command leader delays its dependents' execution even when their own commits are fast — a head-of-line effect with no analogue in log-based protocols, where the log order itself carries the missing data [4].
4. **Complex recovery, even repaired.** EPaxos*'s recovery is simpler than the original's, but ballot-based recovery of dependency sets remains subtle; the decade-long lifetime of Sutra's bug [3] is evidence that leaderless recovery is intrinsically harder to audit than leader-based view change.
5. **No Byzantine tolerance.** The entire analysis assumes crash failures. Byzantine generalizations exist for generalized Paxos [7] but not with EPaxos's fast-path quorum economy; the e-fast bounds assume correct processes report dependencies honestly.
6. **Client routing assumptions.** The two-delay fast path assumes the client reaches a nearby replica that can assemble a fast quorum quickly. Adversarial or skewed client placement reintroduces wide-area latency that the protocol cannot hide [5].

## 7 Conclusion

The EPaxos family demonstrates that the leader is an *optimization*, not a necessity, and that commutativity is the precise tool for removing it. By agreeing on dependency graphs rather than logs, EPaxos commits commuting commands in two message delays from any replica, degrades gracefully under interference, and survives any minority failure without reconfiguration. Its history is also a cautionary tale: the most dangerous bug hid for six years in the recovery path — the seam between optimism and pessimism — found by specification analysis, not testing [3]. EPaxos* [2] closes that chapter with a provably correct, pedagogically clean, optimal variant spanning the full e-fast/f-resilient frontier. Open directions include Byzantine-tolerant fast paths with EPaxos-like quorum economy and adaptive interference oracles learned from workload traces. The lesson endures: *order only what you must, and prove the rest.*
