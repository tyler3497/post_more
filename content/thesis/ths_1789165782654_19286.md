# The Wait-Free Consensus Hierarchy: Herlihy's Consensus Numbers, Universal Constructions, and the Limits of Shared-Memory Synchronization

## Abstract

In 1991, Maurice Herlihy's "Wait-free synchronization" classified shared-memory primitives by a single number — the *consensus number*: the largest *n* for which objects of a given type, together with atomic registers, solve *n*-process consensus. This classification yields an infinite hierarchy: read/write registers sit at level 1, test-and-set, fetch-and-add, swap, queues and stacks at level 2, and compare-and-swap and load-linked/store-conditional at level ∞. Herlihy further proved the *universality of consensus*: an unbounded supply of objects with consensus number *n* implements *any* object wait-free in an *n*-process system. This thesis reconstructs the hierarchy from first principles: the shared-memory model and progress guarantees, the bivalence machinery of Fischer–Lynch–Paterson and Loui–Abu-Amara that caps what registers can do, the exact consensus numbers of the classical primitives, Herlihy's universal construction via helping, the robustness debate around combining techniques, and the widening gap between the theory's clean infinite ladder and the realities of modern multicore hardware.

---
## 1. Introduction

Shared-memory multiprocessors promised that programming concurrent systems could be as simple as reading and writing variables. The catch, discovered over three decades of research, is that *atomic* reading and writing is nearly useless for fault-tolerant coordination. Consider the simplest conceivable synchronization problem: *n* asynchronous processes, each with a private input bit, must agree on a single output bit despite up to *n − 1* of them crashing at arbitrary moments. This is the **consensus problem**, and it is the yardstick against which all synchronization is measured.

The stakes are not abstract. Every lock-free queue, every non-blocking stack, every "scalable" counter in a production system ultimately reduces to getting asynchronous processes to agree despite failures. In 1985, Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson [2] proved that no deterministic algorithm solves consensus in an *asynchronous* message-passing system with even a single crash failure — the celebrated FLP impossibility. Six years later, Herlihy [1] transplanted this question into shared memory and discovered something far richer than a yes/no answer: a graded *hierarchy* of synchronization power, indexed by a single integer — or infinity.

The **consensus number** of an object type *T* is defined as the maximum *n* such that consensus among *n* processes can be solved wait-free using objects of type *T* together with atomic read/write registers; if no such maximum exists, the consensus number is ∞. This single number determines implementability completely: an object type with consensus number *n* cannot implement, wait-free, any object whose consensus number exceeds *n* — in a system with more than *n* processes.

> **Theorem:** *(Herlihy's universality of consensus [1].)* An object type is universal in an *n*-process system — i.e., an unbounded collection of objects of that type plus registers wait-free implements *every* object in that system — if and only if its consensus number is at least *n*.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The asynchronous shared-memory model

We work in the standard asynchronous shared-memory model [9]. A system comprises *n* deterministic processes (threads), *p₁ … pₙ*, communicating through shared **base objects** that support atomic operations. Processes run at arbitrary relative speeds — a process may be delayed between any two steps for an unbounded time — and up to *n − 1* processes may **crash** (halt permanently) at any point. There is no bound on step times and no failure detector.

Base objects are classified by their operations.

### 2.2 Progress conditions



- **Blocking** (lock-based): a delayed process holding a lock can block all others indefinitely.
- **Obstruction-free**: a process running in isolation (without contention) eventually completes its operation.
- **Lock-free**: *some* process always makes progress; the system as a whole never stalls, though individual processes may starve.
- **Wait-free**: *every* non-faulty process completes its operation in a bounded number of its own steps, regardless of the behavior — delays or crashes — of others [1].



### 2.3 The consensus problem





### 2.4 Consensus numbers and the hierarchy



> **Definition:** *cn(T)* is the largest *n* such that *n*-process wait-free consensus is solvable from objects of type *T* and atomic registers. If arbitrarily large *n* are solvable, *cn(T) = ∞*.

The definition yields an infinite ladder.

### 2.5 The impossibility lineage: FLP and Loui–Abu-Amara

Two impossibility results form the bedrock beneath the hierarchy. **Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson (1985)** proved that no deterministic protocol solves consensus in an asynchronous *message-passing* system with one faulty process [2]. Their proof technique — the **bivalence argument** — analyzes *configurations* (global states) as *0-valent* (only decision 0 reachable), *1-valent*, or *bivalent* (both decisions reachable), shows a bivalent initial configuration must exist, and then shows the adversary can keep the system bivalent forever by delaying the "decisive" step [6].

**Loui and Abu-Amara (1987)** transferred impossibility into shared memory, establishing the memory requirements for agreement among unreliable asynchronous processes [3]. Their work — together with Herlihy's register-only impossibility proof — shows that atomic read/write registers are too weak to solve even *2*-process wait-free consensus: no protocol using only registers can break the symmetry between two contending processes in a way that survives a crash at the critical moment. Registers thus sit at consensus number 1, and everything interesting in the hierarchy lives above them.

## 3. Methodology



**Upper bounds: constructive protocols.** To show *cn(T) ≥ n*, one exhibits an explicit wait-free *n*-process consensus protocol using objects of type *T* and registers. The canonical 2-process test-and-set protocol illustrates the pattern [1]: each process writes its input to its own register, then performs `test-and-set` on a shared bit initialized to 0. The process that observes 0 (the "winner") decides its own input; the other (the "loser") reads the winner's register and decides that value. Agreement follows because exactly one process sees 0; validity and wait-free termination are immediate. Similar constructive protocols exist for fetch-and-add, swap, queues, and stacks at level 2, and trivially for CAS at every level [1][5].

**Lower bounds: bivalence and covering arguments.** To show *cn(T) ≤ n*, one proves that *(n+1)*-process consensus is impossible from type-*T* objects. The standard tool is a valency argument in the style of FLP [2][6]: assume a protocol exists, find a **critical configuration** — a bivalent configuration in which every applicable step leads to a univalent configuration — and derive a contradiction from the object's limited "decisiveness." For test-and-set, the argument shows that among three processes, two can be scheduled so that neither's test-and-set reveals the other's presence, letting both believe they won — a contradiction with agreement. More generally, for any RMW operation that returns only bounded information, a *covering* argument constructs executions in which the decisive operation is "hidden" from the deciding process [1][7].

**The implementability yardstick.** These two techniques combine into the hierarchy's main methodological payoff, Theorem 1.1 of the distributed-computing column literature [8]: if *cn(A) = n* and *cn(B) = m < n*, then *A* cannot be implemented wait-free from *B* (plus registers) in any system with more than *m* processes. Proof sketch: such an implementation, composed with the *n*-process consensus protocol from *A*, would yield *(m+1)*-process consensus from *B* — contradicting *cn(B) = m*. Consensus is therefore a complete *yardstick* for relative synchronization power.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The consensus numbers of the classical primitives



| Level | Object types | Representative operations |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Read/write registers | `read`, `write` |
| 2 | Test-and-set, swap, fetch-and-add, queue, stack, priority queue | `TAS`, `swap`, `fetch&add`, `enqueue`/`dequeue`, `push`/`pop` |
| 2*n*−2 | *n*-register assignment | atomic write to *n* registers |
| ∞ | Memory-to-memory move and swap, augmented queue, **compare-and-swap**, fetch-and-cons, sticky byte | `CAS`, `LL/SC` |

Several features deserve emphasis.

**Registers at level 1** is the hierarchy's foundational impossibility: *1*-process "consensus" needs no interaction at all, so every type has consensus number at least 1, but registers can do no better [1][3]. Intuitively, a register write is *invisible* in the critical sense: if process *p* writes and then crashes before anyone reads, the system cannot distinguish this from *p* never having acted — and an adversary exploits exactly this to keep configurations bivalent.

**Level 2 is crowded.** Test-and-set, swap, fetch-and-add, and — remarkably — *queues and stacks* all sit at level 2 [1]. The queue protocol is a gem: to solve 2-process consensus, each process enqueues its input into a shared queue initialized with a single "winner" token; the process that dequeues the token decides its own value, the other dequeues the winner's enqueued input and decides that. Conversely, none of these can solve 3-process consensus — the covering arguments generalize the test-and-set case. An immediate corollary with teeth: **fetch-and-add cannot implement compare-and-swap** in a system of 3 or more processes. This disproved a conjecture of Gottlieb et al. [11] underlying the NYU Ultracomputer project, which had bet its combining-network architecture on fetch-and-add as a universal primitive [1].

**The 2*n*−2 rung** is exotic but instructive: an atomic assignment to *n* registers has consensus number exactly 2*n*−2, showing the hierarchy is genuinely infinite and not merely {1, 2, ∞} [1].

**Level ∞: the universal operations.** Compare-and-swap, load-linked/store-conditional, memory-to-memory move, and the "sticky byte" (a write-once register) all have *unbounded* consensus number [1]. The CAS-based *n*-process consensus protocol is almost embarrassingly simple:

```python
# Shared: cell = CAS register, initially None
# Each process p_i proposes value v_i
def propose(v_i):
    if cell.CAS(None, v_i):   # atomic: if cell is None, set to v_i
        return v_i            # I won: decide my own value
    else:
        return cell.read()    # someone won: decide their value
```

Exactly one CAS succeeds, so all processes agree on the winner's value; validity and wait-freedom are immediate. Hardware designers took note: after 1991, no serious shared-memory architecture shipped without CAS or LL/SC [7].

### 4.2 Universality of consensus and the universal construction

The hierarchy's crown jewel is the **universality theorem**: consensus is *universal* — from consensus objects plus registers, *any* object can be implemented wait-free [1].

The construction is a masterclass in the **helping** technique. To implement an arbitrary sequential object *O* for *n* processes:

1. Maintain an infinite sequence of *n*-process consensus objects *C₁, C₂, …*, where *Cₖ* will decide the *k*-th operation applied to *O*.
2. Each process *pᵢ* owns an **announce** register; to invoke an operation, *pᵢ* writes a descriptor (operation type, arguments, a per-process sequence number) into `announce[i]`.
3. Process *pᵢ* then iterates *k = 1, 2, …*: it scans the announce array for the lowest-indexed process with a pending (not yet applied) operation — *helping* others before itself — proposes that operation's descriptor to *Cₖ*, and applies the decided descriptor to its local copy of the object's state. When *pᵢ*'s own descriptor is decided by some *Cₖ*, its operation is complete.

```python
# Herlihy's universal construction (simplified) [1]
announce = [None] * n          # announce[i]: p_i's pending operation
decide_log = []                # C_1, C_2, ... consensus objects

def invoke(i, op):
    announce[i] = (op, seq[i]); seq[i] += 1
    k = len(decide_log)
    while True:
        # helping: pick lowest-indexed pending operation
        j = min(j for j in range(n) if pending(announce[j]))
        chosen = consensus[k].propose(announce[j])
        apply(chosen)          # update local replica of object state
        if chosen.owner == i:
            return chosen.result
        k += 1
```

Linearizability follows because the decided sequence of operations is a single total order consistent with real time; wait-freedom follows because each iteration decides *some* pending operation, so after at most *n* rounds *pᵢ*'s own operation is the lowest pending one and must be chosen [1][9]. This construction is the ancestor of every modern non-blocking "announce and help" algorithm, from lock-free memory reclamation schemes to software transactional memory.

### 4.3 Impossibility machinery: bivalence and critical configurations

The lower bounds rest on FLP-style valency theory [2][6], worth presenting precisely because the same machinery recurs throughout distributed computing.

> **Theorem:** *(Bivalence lemma.)* Every consensus protocol has a bivalent initial configuration.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose all initial configurations are univalent. Adjacent initial configurations — differing in one process's input — must include both 0-valent and 1-valent ones (consider the all-0 and all-1 inputs). But an execution that crashes the differing process before it takes a step is indistinguishable to all other processes in both configurations, forcing the same decision — contradiction. ∎

> **Theorem:** *(FLP [2].)* No deterministic protocol solves consensus in an asynchronous system with one crash failure.

*Proof sketch.* From a bivalent initial configuration, the protocol must eventually reach a **critical configuration**: bivalent, but every single step leads to a univalent configuration. A commuting-steps argument shows that at a critical configuration, all decisive steps belong to the *same* process *p*. Crash *p* at that moment: the remaining processes face a configuration indistinguishable from the critical one but can never resolve the bivalence — contradiction. ∎

Loui and Abu-Amara's contribution [3] was to show that even the *shared-memory* model with atomic registers inherits a version of this impossibility: registers provide no operation whose outcome *depends on contention* in the way consensus requires. This is exactly what separates level 1 from level 2 (test-and-set reveals-and-sets) and level 2 from level ∞ (CAS reveals, compares, and conditionally sets — the comparison against an expected value being the extra power that defeats all covering arguments).

### 4.4 Combining, and the question of robustness

A subtle crack in the hierarchy appeared early: is the classification *robust*? Suppose we have unboundedly many objects of type *A* (consensus number *n*) *and* unboundedly many of type *B* (also consensus number *n*). Can the *combination* solve (*n*+1)-process consensus? For the plain hierarchy the answer can be yes — the hierarchy is not obviously compositional [5]. Jayanti [4] therefore proposed the refined hierarchy *hₘʳ*: a type *T* is at level *n* of *hₘʳ* if unboundedly many *T*-objects plus registers solve *n*-process consensus but not (*n*+1)-process consensus. Whether *hₘʳ* is robust for arbitrary deterministic objects remains open, but Ruppert showed it is robust for RMW registers and objects with state-returning reads, and Borowsky, Gafni, and Afek sketched a topological proof for symmetric deterministic objects [5]. For the well-behaved objects of practice, consensus number is a genuine classification: no pile of registers, fetch-and-adds, test-and-sets, and queues — however large — can build a CAS.

**Combining** is the practitioner's answer to the contention that universal primitives create. The NYU Ultracomputer's fetch-and-add was backed by *combining networks* that merged concurrent RMW requests in hardware [1]. In software, *combining funnels* (Shavit and Zemach) and especially **flat combining** (Hendler, Incze, Shavit, and Tzafrir) batch concurrent operations: one thread becomes the *combiner*, applies everyone's operations sequentially, and publishes results. These techniques do not change consensus numbers — they change the *constant factors* and *contention profiles* that the hierarchy ignores.

### 4.5 The theory–practice gap

Here the thesis turns critical, because the hierarchy's elegance conceals real limitations:

1. **Hardware made the hierarchy moot — superficially.** Every modern multicore (x86, ARM, RISC-V) ships CAS or LL/SC, i.e., level-∞ primitives. By the universality theorem, arbitrary wait-free synchronization is therefore *possible* everywhere. Yet possibility is not practicality: Herlihy's universal construction applies operations one consensus at a time — *O(n)* consensus objects per operation, each potentially contended — and no production system is built that way.

2. **Consensus number ignores step complexity and contention.** A CAS-based consensus protocol is wait-free but, under contention, *n* processes hammering one cache line serialize through the coherence protocol; throughput collapses. Real lock-free structures (the Michael–Scott queue, Harris's linked list) use CAS sparingly, accept lock-freedom rather than wait-freedom, and engineer for the common uncontended case. The hierarchy says nothing about *remote memory references* (RMRs), the metric that actually predicts multicore performance.

3. **Helping is rare in practice.** The universal construction's helping mechanism — doing other threads' work — destroys cache locality and is widely regarded as impractical. Production non-blocking algorithms overwhelmingly prefer *lock-free* (some thread progresses) over *wait-free* (every thread progresses), precisely because wait-freedom's helping overhead is too high. The hierarchy classifies by *possibility*, not *cost*.

4. **Randomization escapes the hierarchy.** FLP and the register impossibility are *deterministic* results. Randomized consensus (Ben-Or, Rabin) and randomized wait-free constructions sidestep them — at the cost of expected rather than worst-case bounds. The hierarchy as stated applies to deterministic objects; the randomized landscape is far less charted.

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees



- **Space and step lower bounds.** Jayanti, Tan, and Toueg established fundamental time/space trade-offs for wait-free implementations: any wait-free implementation of certain objects from registers requires Ω(*n*) space or super-constant time. These bounds explain *why* the universal construction is expensive — the cost is inherent, not an artifact of the construction.

- **Consensus number is decidable for RMW.** Ruppert [5] gave an algorithm to *compute* the consensus number of a read-modify-write object from its sequential specification, turning classification from an art into a mechanical procedure for the most common primitive class.

- **Hardware validation.** The prediction that architectures would converge on level-∞ primitives was borne out: post-1991 designs standardized on CAS (x86 `CMPXCHG`, SPARC) or LL/SC (MIPS, ARM, PowerPC, RISC-V `lr`/`sc`). The Dijkstra Prize in Distributed Computing (2003) recognized the paper's impact [7].

- **Measured contention behavior.** Empirical studies of CAS-based structures consistently show a "contention cliff": throughput scales near-linearly at low thread counts and collapses under high contention on a single location — the practical phenomenon that combining techniques (Section 4.4) address. This validates the hierarchy's silence on performance as a *limitation* (Section 6), not a refutation: the hierarchy answers "can it be done at all?" while systems work answers "how fast?"

## 6. Limitations

The consensus hierarchy, for all its beauty, has boundaries its admirers should state plainly:

1. **Determinism only.** Every impossibility result cited — FLP [2], Loui–Abu-Amara [3], the level-1/level-2 caps [1] — assumes deterministic processes. Randomized algorithms break through: there *are* randomized wait-free consensus protocols from registers. The hierarchy does not classify randomized power.

2. **The robustness question is open.** Jayanti's *hₘʳ* hierarchy [4] was designed to be compositional, but whether it is robust for *arbitrary* deterministic objects remains unresolved after three decades. The clean statement "level *n* objects cannot build level *m > n*" holds for well-behaved objects (symmetric, deterministic, with reads [5]) but is not a theorem in full generality.

3. **No cost model.** Consensus number is a purely *computability* classification. It does not bound step complexity, RMR complexity, space, or contention — the quantities that decide whether an algorithm survives contact with a 128-core NUMA machine. Two objects at the same level can differ astronomically in practical efficiency.

4. **Crash failures only.** The model assumes halting crashes, not Byzantine behavior. The Byzantine consensus hierarchy is a different, harsher landscape, and the shared-memory results do not transfer.

5. **Universality ≠ usability.** The universal construction proves *existence* of wait-free implementations, not their practicality. Real systems use bespoke lock-free algorithms, fine-grained locking, or hybrid schemes — the universal construction itself is a proof device, not a design pattern.

6. **Finite-level subtleties.** Objects like *n*-register assignment (level 2*n*−2) show the hierarchy's fine structure, but few such objects appear in real instruction sets, limiting the intermediate rungs' practical relevance.

## 7. Conclusion

Herlihy's 1991 paper did for shared-memory synchronization what FLP did for distributed agreement: it replaced folklore with a theorem. The **consensus number** compresses the synchronization power of an object type into a single integer — 1 for registers, 2 for test-and-set, fetch-and-add, and queues, 2*n*−2 for *n*-register assignment, ∞ for compare-and-swap — and the **universality of consensus** shows that this number is all that matters for *possibility*: level *n* plus registers builds anything, for *n* processes, wait-free [1].

The surrounding results deepen the picture without disturbing it. FLP [2] and Loui–Abu-Amara [3] explain *why* the bottom of the hierarchy is barren; the universal construction's announce-and-help protocol shows how consensus bootstraps into arbitrary objects; Jayanti's robustness program [4][5] probes whether the ladder survives composition; and combining techniques address the contention the theory ignores.

The honest assessment is two-sided. As *computability* theory, the hierarchy is essentially complete and its architectural prediction — that hardware would standardize on universal primitives — came true. As *engineering* guidance, it is deliberately silent on cost, and the decades since 1991 have been spent filling that silence: with RMR complexity, contention-aware design, flat combining, and the pragmatic retreat from wait-freedom to lock-freedom. The hierarchy tells us what synchronization *can* be; the multicore era taught us what it *costs*. Both lessons were necessary, and the first made the second possible.
