# Asymptotically Optimal Motion Planning: PRM*, RRT*, and Kinodynamic Planning in High-Dimensional Configuration Spaces

## Abstract

Sampling-based motion planning has become the dominant paradigm for robots with many degrees of freedom, where deterministic discretization of the configuration space is computationally intractable. The probabilistic roadmap (PRM) and the rapidly-exploring random tree (RRT) achieve *probabilistic completeness* but return solutions with no quality guarantees. This thesis develops the theory and practice of **asymptotically optimal** sampling-based planning: PRM*, RRT*, and their kinodynamic extensions. We analyze why naive variants converge almost surely to *suboptimal* costs, derive the connection-radius scaling laws that guarantee optimality through random geometric graph theory, and detail the rewiring primitives that separate optimal from merely feasible planners. We then examine kinodynamic planning under differential constraints, where two-point boundary-value steering replaces straight-line connections, and survey convergence acceleration: informed ellipsoidal subsets, lazy dynamic programming in FMT*, and batch heuristic search in BIT*. A proof sketch of asymptotic optimality is given alongside empirical benchmarks in high-dimensional narrow-passage domains. We close with open limitations: slow convergence rates, narrow-passage pathology, non-additive costs, and the frontier of learned samplers and certified optimality.

##1 Introduction

Motion planning asks a deceptively simple question: given a robot, a start configuration, a goal configuration, and a set of obstacles, find a *collision-free* path between them [1]. The difficulty explodes with dimension. A manipulator with seven revolute joints lives in a seven-dimensional configuration space \(\mathcal{C}\); a humanoid may live in thirty or more. Classical exact algorithms — cell decomposition, retraction, potential fields with navigation functions — scale exponentially in the dimension of \(\mathcal{C}\) and are unusable beyond a handful of degrees of freedom.

Two randomized algorithms broke this curse in the 1990s. The **probabilistic roadmap** (PRM) of Kavraki, \v{S}vestka, Latombe, and Overmars builds a graph whose vertices are random collision-free configurations and whose edges are local collision-free connections, then answers queries by graph search [2]. The **rapidly-exploring random tree** (RRT) of LaValle grows a tree incrementally toward random samples, exhibiting a strong *Voronoi bias* that pulls exploration into unvisited regions [3]. Both are *probabilistically complete*: if a solution exists, the probability of finding it tends to one as samples accumulate. Both are blind to cost: they return the first feasible path, not the best one.

> **Theorem: (Informal).** PRM and RRT find *some* collision-free path with probability approaching one. They offer no guarantee that the returned path cost approaches the optimum — and in fact it generally does not.

This thesis is concerned with the fix. In 2011, Karaman and Frazzoli introduced PRM*, RRG, and RRT*, proving that sampling-based planners *can* be asymptotically optimal: the cost of the returned solution converges almost surely to the optimum as the number of samples tends to infinity, at computational cost within a constant factor of their non-optimal counterparts [1]. The 2010 incremental precursor paper established the key negative result — plain RRT converges almost surely to a *non*-optimal value — and introduced the rapidly-exploring random graph (RRG) [5]. This thesis traces that arc from feasibility to optimality, then into the two harder frontiers: **kinodynamic planning**, where dynamics constrain which curves are admissible, and **high-dimensional acceleration**, where naive optimal planners converge too slowly to be useful.

We present a unified derivation of the radius scaling laws, the rewiring machinery, proof sketches, kinodynamic steering, and a survey of accelerators — informed sampling [7], FMT* [6], and BIT* [8].

##2 Background

### 2.1 Configuration space and problem statement

Let \(\mathcal{C}\) be the configuration space of a robot, \(\mathcal{C}_{obs}\) the obstacle region, and \(\mathcal{C}_{free} = \mathcal{C} \setminus \mathcal{C}_{obs}\) the free space. Given \(q_{init}, q_{goal} \in \mathcal{C}_{free}\) and a cost functional \(c\) (typically path length), the *geometric* planning problem seeks a continuous curve \(\sigma\) in \(\mathcal{C}_{free}\) minimizing \(c(\sigma)\). The *kinodynamic* problem seeks a trajectory \((x(t), u(t))\) satisfying \(\dot{x} = f(x, u)\), control bounds, and obstacle avoidance, minimizing a time or energy cost.

Sampling-based planners approximate \(\mathcal{C}_{free}\) with a graph:

- **Sample**: draw \(q_{rand} \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(\mathcal{C}_{free})\).
- **Connect**: attempt edges to nearby existing vertices via a local planner (usually straight-line interpolation with collision checking).
- **Search**: run Dijkstra or A* on the resulting graph.


### 2.2 PRM: multi-query roadmaps

PRM proceeds in two phases [2]. In the *learning phase*, \(n\) configurations are sampled and each is connected to its \(k\) nearest neighbors (or all neighbors within radius \(r\)) through the local planner. In the *query phase*, start and goal are attached to the roadmap and the shortest graph path is extracted. Because the roadmap is reusable across queries in a static environment, PRM is a *multi-query* planner.

### 2.3 RRT: single-query trees and Voronoi bias

RRT grows a tree rooted at \(q_{init}\) [3]. Each iteration samples \(q_{rand}\), finds the nearest tree vertex \(q_{near}\), and extends a bounded step \(\eta\) toward the sample:

```python
def rrt_extend(tree, q_rand, eta):
    q_near = nearest(tree, q_rand)
    q_new = steer(q_near, q_rand, eta)   # bounded step toward sample
    if collision_free(q_near, q_new):
        tree.add_vertex(q_new)
        tree.add_edge(q_near, q_new)
    return q_new
```

Because the nearest-neighbor rule selects the vertex of the largest Voronoi cell with probability proportional to its volume, growth is biased toward unexplored space — the *Voronoi bias* [3]. RRT-Connect grows two trees, one from start and one from goal, greedily attempting to connect them; it remains the fastest known single-query *feasible* planner for many problems [4].

### 2.4 Why naive samplers are not optimal

Karaman and Frazzoli proved a striking negative result [1][5]: under mild technical conditions, the cost of the best path in an RRT (and in PRM with fixed connection radius) converges almost surely to a value *strictly greater* than the optimum. Optimality demands that the *connection radius shrink with sample density* at exactly the right rate, and that every new sample be considered as a *potential parent* of its neighbors — the rewiring step.

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 PRM*: the radius-scaled roadmap

PRM* differs from PRM in one decisive parameter: the connection radius

$$r_n = \gamma_{PRM*} \left( \frac{\log n}{n} \right)^{1/d},$$

where \(d = \dim \mathcal{C}\) and \(\gamma_{PRM*}\) exceeds a problem-dependent threshold derived from random geometric graph percolation [1]. As \(n\) grows, the radius shrinks, but slowly enough that the implicit random geometric graph stays connected along any robustly feasible path. Every pair of samples within \(r_n\) is a candidate edge, so the graph densifies toward a continuum approximation.

### 3.2 RRG and RRT*: incremental optimality

The rapidly-exploring random graph (RRG) applies the same radius law to incremental tree growth: each new sample connects to *all* near neighbors within \(r_n\), forming a graph [5]. RRT* preserves the tree structure while inheriting RRG's optimality through two operations on the near set \(Q_{near}\):

1. **ChooseParent**: select the neighbor minimizing cost-to-come plus edge cost.
2. **Rewire**: for each neighbor, check whether routing through the new sample lowers its cost-to-come; if so, reparent it.

```haskell
-- RRT* core: choose parent, then rewire the neighborhood
rrtStarStep :: Tree -> Config -> Double -> Tree
rrtStarStep t qNew r =
  let qNear  = nearSet t qNew r
      parent = argmin (\q -> costToCome t q + edgeCost q qNew) qNear
      t1     = insertWithParent t qNew parent
  in foldl (rewireThrough qNew) t1 qNear

rewireThrough :: Config -> Tree -> Config -> Tree
rewireThrough qNew t q
  | costToCome t qNew + edgeCost qNew q < costToCome t q = reparent t q qNew
  | otherwise = t
```

Rewiring is the soul of RRT*. It converts a static growth process into a dynamic programming relaxation: each new sample triggers a local Bellman update over its neighborhood, and the accumulation of these updates over all samples yields global optimality in the limit [1].

### 3.3 The connection-radius threshold

The constant \(\gamma\) must satisfy

$$\gamma_{RRT*} > \left( 2 \left(1 + \frac{1}{d}\right) \frac{\mu(\mathcal{C}_{free})}{\zeta_d} \right)^{1/d},$$

where \(\mu\) is Lebesgue measure and \(\zeta_d\) the volume of the unit \(d\)-ball. Above the threshold, the expected degree grows as \(\Theta(\log n)\), keeping total work at \(O(n \log n)\) — the same complexity class as RRT up to constants [1].

> **Theorem: (Asymptotic optimality of RRT*, informal).** Let \(c^*\) be the optimal path cost and \(c_n\) the cost of the best path in the RRT* tree after \(n\) samples. Under the radius law above and standard regularity assumptions, \(c_n \to c^*\) almost surely as \(n \to \infty\).

### 3.4 Complexity accounting

| Planner | Per-iteration work | Guarantee |
|---|---|---|
| RRT | \(O(\log n)\) nearest neighbor | Probabilistic completeness |
| PRM (fixed \(k\)) | \(O(k \log n)\) | Probabilistic completeness |
| PRM* | \(O(\log n)\) expected degree | Asymptotic optimality [1] |
| RRT* | \(O(\log n)\) near set + rewire | Asymptotic optimality [1] |
| RRT-Connect | \(O(\log n)\), greedy connect | Fastest feasible, no optimality [4] |

The table's message is the paper's headline: optimality costs at most a constant factor over feasibility [1].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Random geometric graphs: the hidden engine

The analysis of [1] hinges on a novel connection between sampling-based planning and **random geometric graphs** (RGGs): graphs formed by connecting random points whose pairwise distance falls below a radius. Classical RGG theory studies the connectivity threshold — the critical radius at which a giant component emerges. Karaman and Frazzoli adapt these tools to *graphs embedded in \(\mathcal{C}_{free}\) with collision-checked edges*, showing that if the connection radius decays as \((\log n / n)^{1/d}\) with a large enough constant, then with high probability the graph contains a path approximating *every* robustly feasible path, including the optimal one, to arbitrary precision [1]. This is why the \(\log n\) factor is non-negotiable: without it, the graph's minimum degree collapses and narrow corridors of the optimal path's homotopy class vanish from the approximation.

The proof strategy tiles a near-optimal path with overlapping balls of shrinking radius, shows each ball contains samples with high probability (a covering argument), shows consecutive balls are linked by edges (a percolation argument), and chains these into a graph path whose cost converges to the optimum. Rewiring then guarantees the *tree* cost tracks the *graph* cost [1][5].

### 4.2 Kinodynamic planning: steering under differential constraints

Geometric planners assume any two nearby configurations can be joined by a straight line. For systems with dynamics — a car that cannot move sideways, a quadrotor with bounded thrust — the admissible curves are those satisfying \(\dot{x} = f(x,u)\) with \(u\) in a control set. Connecting two states now requires solving a **two-point boundary value problem** (TPBVP): find a control driving the system from \(x_a\) to \(x_b\). Closed-form TPBVP solutions exist for a few systems (Dubins and Reeds–Shepp cars, double integrators, LTI systems), which kinodynamic RRT* exploits directly [1].

For general nonlinear systems, the field resorts to *forward propagation*: sample a random control and duration, integrate the dynamics, and add the resulting state — the approach of kinodynamic RRT and its probabilistically complete variants. Asymptotic optimality in this setting was lifted to the **state-cost space** \(\mathcal{X} \times \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\), where the extra coordinate tracks accumulated cost and the goal becomes a *goal region in cost-augmented space*; AO-RRT and its refinements prove optimality for systems with drift by planning in this augmented space and pruning dominated states. The price is steep: forward propagation explores the reachable set blindly, so convergence in kinodynamic domains is far slower than in the geometric case, and the steering-function requirement remains the fundamental bottleneck — without an exact or near-exact TPBVP solver, the rewiring step that powers RRT* cannot be evaluated [1].

```rust
// Kinodynamic RRT*: connect via optimal steering (closed-form TPBVP)
fn kino_rrt_star_step(tree: &mut Tree, x_rand: State, sys: &dyn Dynamics) {
    let x_near = nearest(tree, x_rand);
    // Steer solves the two-point boundary value problem exactly
    if let Some(traj) = sys.steer(&x_near, &x_rand) {
        if collision_free(&traj) {
            let x_new = traj.end_state();
            // choose parent minimizing cost-to-come through near set
            let parent = best_parent(tree, &x_new, sys);
            tree.insert(x_new, parent);
            rewire(tree, &x_new, sys); // rewire needs steer(x_new, q) too
        }
    }
}
```

A subtle consequence: kinodynamic RRT* is *asymmetric*. Steering from \(x_a\) to \(x_b\) may be feasible while the reverse is not (think of a car facing a wall), so near sets must be defined with directed reachability and rewiring must respect the direction of time. The symmetric radius law survives, but the constant now depends on the system's local controllability — small-time local controllability (STLC) is the standing assumption that makes the covering argument go through [1].

### 4.3 Accelerating convergence: informed sets, FMT*, and BIT*

Asymptotic optimality is a cold comfort if convergence takes longer than the mission. Three ideas dominate the acceleration literature.

**Informed RRT*** [7]. After a first solution of cost \(c_{best}\) is found, only states that can possibly improve it need sampling. For path-length costs, this *informed subset* is exactly a prolate hyperspheroid with foci at start and goal and transverse diameter \(c_{best}\). Gammell, Srinivasa, and Barfoot give an exact direct-sampling procedure for this ellipsoid and prove that Informed RRT* retains RRT*'s completeness and optimality while converging dramatically faster in high dimensions — because the probability that a uniform sample lands in the improving set decays exponentially in \(d\), whereas direct informed sampling keeps it at one [7]. As \(c_{best}\) decreases, the ellipsoid shrinks, focusing effort ever more tightly.

**FMT*** [6]. The Fast Marching Tree abandons incremental growth for *lazy dynamic programming*: draw all \(n\) samples upfront, then expand a tree outward in cost-to-come order, connecting each unvisited sample to its cheapest neighbor in the wavefront and collision-checking edges lazily (only when they would be used). Analyzed under convergence *in probability* rather than almost surely, FMT* admits the first convergence-*rate* bounds in the field and empirically dominates PRM* and RRT* per unit computation time, especially in high dimensions [6]. Its tree structure also extends naturally to differential constraints.

**BIT*** [8]. Batch Informed Trees unify the two: search a sequence of increasingly dense *implicit* random geometric graphs with A*-style heuristics, reusing information across batches like lifelong planning algorithms. BIT* processes samples in batches, ordering edge expansions by \(f = g + h\) and pruning with the informed set, which focuses computation on the homotopy classes that matter [8].

```tla+
---- MODULE InformedSampling ----
EXTENDS Reals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Start, Goal, Dim
VARIABLES cBest, SampleSet

InformedSubset == { x \in StateSpace :
    Dist(Start, x) + Dist(x, Goal) <= cBest }

Next == /\ cBest' \in { c \in Reals : c <= cBest }
        /\ SampleSet' = SampleSet \cup DirectEllipsoidSample(cBest')
        /\ UNCHANGED << >>
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<cBest, SampleSet>>
(* Invariant: every sample lies in the admissible improving set. *)
====
```

### 4.4 Narrow passages and the sampling bottleneck

All radius-scaled planners inherit PRM's nemesis: **narrow passages**. If the optimal path threads a passage of width \(\epsilon\), the expected sample count scales as \(\epsilon^{-d}\) — exponential in dimension. Mitigations include:

- **Bridge and Gaussian samplers** that bias sampling toward obstacle boundaries.
- **Lazy collision checking** (as in FMT* and lazy PRM*), deferring the dominant cost — edge validation — until edges are needed [6].
- **Deterministic low-dispersion sequences** (Halton, Sukharev grids) with better covering radius.
- **Learned samplers** predicting promising regions from experience, trading guarantees for speed.

None remove the exponential dependence; they improve the constants. The narrow-passage problem is, in a precise sense, the *information-theoretic* core of sampling-based planning: finding a passage of width \(\epsilon\) in dimension \(d\) requires resolving volume \(\epsilon^d\).

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: asymptotic optimality of PRM*

We sketch the argument of [1], which proceeds in four stages.

> **Theorem: (PRM* asymptotic optimality).** Let \(\sigma^*\) be an optimal robustly feasible path with cost \(c^*\), and let \(c_n\) be the shortest-path cost in the PRM* graph on \(n\) samples. Then \(\mathbb{P}(\lim_{n\to\infty} c_n = c^*) = 1\).

*Proof sketch.* (1) **Approximation.** For any \(\epsilon > 0\), there exists a path \(\sigma_\epsilon\) with *strong \(\delta\)-clearance* (a tube of radius \(\delta\) around it lies in \(\mathcal{C}_{free}\)) and cost at most \((1+\epsilon)c^*\). (2) **Covering.** Tile \(\sigma_\epsilon\) with balls of radius shrinking as \(r_n\); a Chernoff-plus-union-bound argument shows that for large \(n\), every ball contains at least one sample almost surely, provided \(\gamma\) exceeds the percolation threshold. (3) **Connection.** Consecutive balls overlap enough that their resident samples lie within \(r_n\) of each other, so PRM* adds the connecting edges (collision-free by the clearance tube). (4) **Chaining.** The resulting graph path has cost at most \((1+\epsilon)c^* + o(1)\); letting \(\epsilon \to 0\) along a sequence gives \(\limsup c_n \le c^*\) a.s., while \(c_n \ge c^*\) always. ∎

RRT* inherits this via RRG: the rewiring step ensures the tree's best cost equals the graph's best cost asymptotically [5][1].

### 5.2 Benchmarks in high-dimensional domains

Representative published results (geometric path-length cost, single thread):

| Domain | Dim \(d\) | Planner | Samples to 5% of opt | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cluttered SE(2) | 3 | RRT* | ~2,000 | Rewiring-heavy |
| Cluttered SE(2) | 3 | Informed RRT* | ~800 | Informed subset [7] |
| 7-DOF arm | 7 | RRT* | ~20,000 | Slow tail |
| 7-DOF arm | 7 | FMT* | ~5,000 | Lazy DP [6] |
| 14-DOF dual arm | 14 | BIT* | ~30,000 | Batch heuristics [8] |
| Narrow passage | 6 | PRM* | ~100,000 | Passage-dominated |

The pattern is consistent across studies [6][7][8]: per *iteration*, RRT* and PRM* behave similarly; per *second*, lazy and heuristic-guided variants (FMT*, BIT*, Informed RRT*) reach a given suboptimality bound several times faster, and the gap widens with dimension. For kinodynamic systems the absolute sample counts are one to two orders of magnitude larger, reflecting the cost of TPBVP steering or forward propagation.

### 5.3 Convergence-rate perspective

FMT*'s analysis yields explicit bounds: the probability that the returned cost exceeds \((1+\epsilon)c^*\) decays with \(n\) at a rate depending on \(\epsilon\), \(d\), and the clearance of the near-optimal path [6]. Practically, planners are run as *anytime* algorithms with branch-and-bound pruning of the tree outside the informed set [7].

##6 Limitations

1. **Slow asymptotic tails.** In dimensions above ten, RRT* may need millions of samples to approach the optimum; the guarantee is real but operationally thin without acceleration [7][8].
2. **Narrow passages.** The \(\epsilon^{-d}\) sampling bottleneck is information-theoretic. Radius scaling only ensures that *if* samples land in the passage, they connect correctly.
3. **Steering bottleneck in kinodynamic planning.** Exact TPBVP solvers exist for few systems. Forward-propagation variants recover probabilistic completeness but sacrifice the rewiring machinery — current kinodynamic optimality results require state-cost-space augmentation and strong controllability assumptions [1].
4. **Cost structure.** The theory assumes additive, Lipschitz costs (path length, time). Non-additive objectives — risk, information gain, multi-objective Pareto fronts — break the dynamic-programming structure that rewiring exploits.
5. **Metric sensitivity.** In kinodynamic spaces the Euclidean metric misleads: states close in space may be far in cost (a car facing away from its target), degrading both exploration and rewiring efficiency.
6. **Determinism and certification.** Randomized planners cannot certify *non-existence* of a solution, and their outputs vary run to run — problematic for safety-critical certification.

---

##7 Conclusion

The arc from PRM and RRT to PRM* and RRT* is one of the cleanest stories in robotics: a negative result (naive samplers converge to suboptimal costs [5]), a diagnosis (frozen connection topology), and a cure (radius scaling plus rewiring) that costs only a constant factor [1]. Random geometric graph theory provided the analytical lens; the rewiring primitive provided the algorithmic mechanism. The subsequent decade layered acceleration on top — informed ellipsoids [7], lazy dynamic programming [6], batch heuristic search [8] — and pushed the framework into kinodynamic domains via steering functions and state-cost spaces.

Open frontiers remain. *Learned samplers* promise to break the narrow-passage bottleneck using experience, but marrying them with optimality guarantees is unsolved. *Certified planning* demands deterministic, verifiable variants of these inherently randomized algorithms. The enduring lesson of the asymptotically optimal program is methodological: in sampling-based planning, the right question was never "does it find a path?" but "what does the implicit graph converge to?" — and the answer, with the right radius law, is the optimum.
