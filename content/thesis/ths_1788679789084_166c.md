# Concurrent Compaction Without Pause: Colored Pointers, Load Barriers, and Brooks Forwarding Pointers in ZGC and Shenandoah

## Abstract

Modern latency-critical Java services demand garbage-collection pauses measured in microseconds, not seconds, even on multi-terabyte heaps. This thesis presents a comparative, implementation-grounded analysis of the two production concurrent compacting collectors in OpenJDK: **ZGC**, which builds on *colored pointers* and a load-value barrier (LVB), and **Shenandoah**, which builds on *Brooks forwarding pointers* attached to every object plus read/write barriers. We reconstruct the collectors' core mechanisms — ZGC's multi-mapped virtual address views, generational ZGC's low-bit color encoding with explicit barriers, Shenandoah's to-space invariant and snapshot-at-the-beginning (SATB) marking — and formalize the correctness argument for concurrent evacuation as an invariant-maintenance problem rather than a stop-the-world synchronization problem. A methodology section specifies how barrier correctness can be validated with formal models (TLA+) and fault-injection harnesses. Quantitative evidence is drawn from published benchmark studies comparing ZGC, Shenandoah, and G1: ZGC achieves sub-millisecond pauses (mean ~0.07–0.12 ms, max typically under 15 ms) while Shenandoah averages 1–4 ms with occasional 200–600 ms spikes under extreme pressure [6][7].

## 1. Introduction

The central ambition of garbage-collector research since the mid-1990s has been to decouple pause time from heap size and live-set size. Classical collectors such as Parallel GC scale their stop-the-world (STW) pauses roughly with the live data they must scan; a 60 GB heap with gigabytes of live objects can induce pauses measured in seconds. The G1 collector (default since JDK 9) improved the situation with region-based incremental evacuation, yet published measurements show its worst-case pauses floor at roughly 20–25 ms even in favorable conditions [6]. For latency-sensitive domains — trading systems, stream processors, interactive services — a 25 ms pause is a *tail-latency event* that directly inflates the 99th and 99.99th percentile response times.

Two OpenJDK collectors attack the problem head-on, both *concurrent, region-based, compacting* collectors with STW phases limited to root scanning: **ZGC** (JEP 333, JDK 11 [1]; generational ZGC in JEP 439, JDK 21 [2]), built on **colored pointers** plus a JIT-injected **load barrier**; and **Shenandoah** (JDK 12 [8]), built on **Brooks forwarding pointers** in every object header plus read/write/SATB/CAS barriers.

This thesis makes three contributions. First, it reconstructs both designs from primary sources [1][2][3][4][5]: ZGC's multi-mapped views, self-healing barriers, and the generational redesign; Shenandoah's JDK 13 forwarding-word elimination [4]; and the *to-space invariant* behind concurrent evacuation. Second, it formalizes barrier correctness as invariant-preservation theorems with a TLA+ validation sketch. Third, it compares measured pause-time behavior and the degenerated/full-GC failure modes that bound the low-latency regime.

> **Thesis statement:** Concurrent evacuation does not require stopping the world; it requires a single, checkable indirection layer — either *in the pointer* (ZGC's colors) or *in the object* (Shenandoah's Brooks word) — plus a barrier discipline whose correctness reduces to maintaining one invariant under all interleavings of mutator and collector threads.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The concurrent compaction problem

A compacting collector reclaims fragmented free space by *relocating* live objects: copying them from one region to another and updating all references. Doing this concurrently with mutators is hazardous because a mutator may hold a stale pointer to the old (from-space) copy while the collector has already installed the new (to-space) copy. The classical solutions are:

- **Stop the world** during relocation (G1's evacuation pauses; Parallel GC's full collections).
- **Read/write barriers** that intercept mutator accesses and redirect them to the canonical copy.
- **Self-healing pointers**, where the barrier repairs stale pointers lazily.

Both ZGC and Shenandoah choose the barrier route but place the indirection in different locations, which determines almost everything else about their designs.

### 2.2 Tri-color marking and its concurrent variants

Both collectors mark concurrently with the tri-color abstraction (white/gray/black). They differ in how they close the *lost-update* window when mutators mutate the graph mid-mark: Shenandoah uses a **snapshot-at-the-beginning (SATB)** write barrier that logs overwritten references [3], while ZGC uses **incremental update driven by the load barrier** — loading a white reference during concurrent mark triggers marking, maintaining the strong tri-color invariant (no black→white edges) [1].

### 2.3 Regions, evacuation sets, and remembered sets

Both collectors divide the heap into equal-sized regions and evacuate a *collection set* of the sparsest regions first. References pointing into the collection set from outside are tracked via generational ZGC's double-buffered remembered sets [2] or Shenandoah's concurrent update-references phase.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Primary-source reconstruction

The design reconstruction in Section 4 is grounded in primary sources: JEP 333 (ZGC [1]), JEP 439 (Generational ZGC [2]), the OpenJDK Shenandoah wiki (barrier flags, heuristics, performance analysis guidance [3]), Aleksey Shipilёv's Shenandoah slide decks [5], and Per Lidén's ZGC presentations [4]. Benchmark numbers are taken from published studies [6][7] with methodology caveats noted.

### 3.2 Formal validation strategy

Barrier correctness is argued as an invariant-preservation theorem (Section 5). To move beyond prose proof, we specify a validation methodology:

1. **TLA+ model** of the barrier protocol: mutator operations (read, write, CAS) and collector operations (mark, evacuate, update-refs) as atomic actions; state the to-space or good-color invariant; model-check all interleavings on a bounded heap. A sketch appears in Section 4.4.
2. **Stress harness:** run a synthetic mutator workload under `-XX:+StressLCM`/`-XX:+StressGCM`-style compiler randomization with `gc+phases` logging, asserting at each safepoint-free boundary that every reachable reference satisfies the invariant.
3. **Barrier ablation:** Shenandoah's *passive* heuristics allow selectively disabling individual barriers (`-XX:-ShenandoahSATBBarrier`, `-XX:-ShenandoahReadBarrier`, etc. [3]); differential testing between full and ablated configurations on deterministic workloads isolates the correctness contribution of each barrier.

### 3.3 Benchmark interpretation protocol

Pause-time numbers in Section 5 are *reported*, not newly measured. We apply a consistent interpretation protocol: distinguish *GC pause time* (STW duration) from *application latency* (end-to-end, including barrier overhead and pacing stalls); report mean, p95/p99.99, and max; and note the workload regime (allocation pressure, heap size), because both collectors degenerate under extreme pressure.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 ZGC: colored pointers and the load-value barrier

**Colored pointers.** In non-generational ZGC, a 64-bit object pointer carries metadata in its high bits [1][4]:

| Bits | 63–46 (4 bits: Marked1, Marked0, Remapped, Finalizable) | 45–42 | 41–0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Color | — | Object address (44 bits → 16 TB address space) |

The four color bits encode mark/remap/finalizer state for the current cycle, riding free in otherwise-unused high bits — at the cost of no 32-bit or compressed-oops support and a 16 TB heap ceiling.

**Multi-mapped memory.** The ingenious trick that makes color *checking* cheap is *multi-mapping*: the same physical heap is mapped into three (later more) virtual address ranges, one per "view" of the colors. The load barrier's fast path is then a single `test` instruction against a "bad mask" in thread-local storage [4]:

```c
// ZGC load barrier, fast path (x86-64), from Lidén's ZGC-JFokus-2018 [4]
mov  0x20(%rax), %rbx      // Object o = obj.fieldA  (load reference)
test %rbx, (%r15)          // bad color?  (r15-relative bad mask)
jnz  slow_path            // yes -> mark/relocate/remap, heal pointer
```

In the common case (good color), the barrier costs a load, a test, and a correctly-predicted not-taken branch — comparable to compressed-oops decoding. On the slow path, the barrier *heals* the pointer: it marks the object if unmarked, relocates it if it lives in the relocation set, or remaps its fields, and writes the corrected (good-color) pointer back into the field — a **self-healing** barrier, so each stale pointer pays the slow path at most once per cycle [1].

**Phases.** A ZGC cycle alternates three O(roots) pauses (*Mark Start*, *Mark End*, *Relocate Start*) with concurrent mark/remap, prepare-for-relocate, and relocate phases; pause time is independent of heap and live-set size [1].

**Generational ZGC (JEP 439).** The redesign [2] drops multi-mapping (no more tripled `ps` heap reporting; colors need not live in the addressable range, so heaps can exceed 16 TB), keeps references *colored in the heap but colorless on the stack* with colors in the low-order bits (one x64 shift both tests and strips metadata), and adds optimized barriers, double-buffered remembered sets, dense regions, and minor/major cycles to recover the ≤15% throughput gap vs. G1 [1].

### 4.2 Shenandoah: Brooks forwarding pointers and the to-space invariant

**The Brooks pointer.** Every object carries an extra header word — the *forwarding pointer* — which at all times points to the object's canonical (to-space) copy [5]. The fundamental protocol rule, the **to-space invariant**, is:

> **Theorem (To-space invariant):** *At every point in the collection cycle, all mutator reads observe, and all mutator writes land in, the to-space copy of each object, as resolved through the Brooks forwarding pointer.*

Evacuation proceeds as follows: the GC selects collection-set regions, and for each live object allocates a to-space copy, copies the payload, and atomically installs the forwarding pointer (CAS) from the from-space object to the copy. Any mutator access that arrives *before* evacuation of that object proceeds on the from-space copy (which is still valid); any access *after* is redirected through the forwarding pointer. All accesses go through the indirection, so the collector never stops the world to move an object [5].

**Barrier suite.** Shenandoah's barriers are more numerous than ZGC's, because the indirection lives in the object rather than the pointer [3]:

| Barrier | Trigger | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| SATB write barrier | Reference store overwrites old value | Log old value for snapshot-at-the-beginning marking |
| Read barrier | Heap read (reference *and*, historically, primitive) | Resolve forwarding pointer → to-space copy |
| Write (to-space) barrier | Reference store | Ensure the store targets the to-space copy |
| CAS / acmp / clone barriers | Atomic ops, `==`, cloning | Forwarding-pointer-aware atomicity and identity |

A key evolution: in JDK 13, Shenandoah **eliminated the dedicated forwarding-pointer word** by overlaying it on the mark word (using the illegal lock-bit pattern `0b11` to distinguish a forwarding pointer from a true mark word) and, more importantly, by introducing **load-reference barriers** so that *primitive* reads no longer need barriers at all — only reference loads do [4]. This cut the steady-state barrier overhead substantially and reduced per-object memory overhead from 5–10% toward the mark-word-overlay cost.

**Phases.** Shenandoah cycles through STW *Init/Final Mark* and *Update References* pauses around *Concurrent Mark*, *Concurrent Evacuation*, and *Concurrent Update References* phases. Under extreme allocation pressure, pacing fails and it falls back to *Degenerated* or *Full GC* — the brittleness seen in benchmarks [6].

### 4.3 Load barrier vs. Brooks pointer: the fundamental trade-off

The two designs can be compared along four axes:

| Dimension | ZGC (colored pointers + LVB) | Shenandoah (Brooks + barriers) |
|---|---|---|
| Indirection location | In the *pointer* (metadata bits) | In the *object* (header word) |
| Barrier trigger | Reference *loads* only | Reads, writes, CAS, acmp, clone |
| Steady-state cost | Test+branch per reference load; reads dominate | Per-object header word (memory); barrier on stores/CAS |
| Healing model | Self-healing: slow path repairs pointer once per cycle | Forwarding pointer always current; no healing needed |

The deep reason for the difference: **reads vastly outnumber writes** in typical Java programs, so ZGC pays its (cheap) barrier on the hot path — every reference load — while Shenandoah pays a heavier but less frequent cost on writes plus a constant per-object memory tax. Conversely, ZGC's scheme requires 64-bit pointers with spare bits (no compressed oops in the original design) and multi-mapped memory (or explicit low-bit encoding in generational ZGC), whereas Shenandoah works with ordinary pointers and no virtual-memory tricks [1][2][5].


### 4.4 Concurrent marking and relocation in detail

Both collectors' concurrent marking must solve the *lost update* problem. Consider the interleaving: the collector has scanned object A (black); the mutator then stores a reference to white object W into A. Without a barrier, W is never marked and is wrongly reclaimed — a soundness violation (dangling reference), the worst possible GC bug.

- *ZGC:* no store barrier is needed — a later *load* of the field sees the bad (unmarked) color and marks W; the *Pause Mark End* phase closes the residual window [1].
- *Shenandoah:* the SATB write barrier logs the overwritten reference at store time and the final-mark pause drains the buffers; objects allocated during marking are born live [3][5].

Relocation correctness then rests on the respective invariant: ZGC's *good-color* invariant (every pointer the mutator uses has been healed to the current cycle's good color before use) and Shenandoah's *to-space* invariant. In both cases the collector may reclaim a from-space region **before all pointers into it are fixed** — ZGC because stale colors are healed on next load; Shenandoah because the forwarding pointer survives the region's reuse discipline (the from-space copy is not overwritten until update-references completes). This "reclaim before remap" property removes the need for a full-heap mark-compact fallback and keeps heap overhead low [1][5].

A minimal TLA+ sketch of Shenandoah evacuation illustrates the formal approach:

```tla
----------------------------- MODULE BrooksEvac -----------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Objects, Regions
VARIABLES fwd,       \* obj -> canonical copy (Brooks pointer)
          loc,       \* obj -> current region
          cset       \* set of regions being evacuated

ToSpaceInvariant ==
    \A o \in Objects : loc[fwd[o]] \notin cset
    \* every canonical copy lives outside the collection set

Evacuate(o) ==
    /\ loc[o] \in cset
    /\ LET c == NewCopy(o) IN
       /\ loc' = [loc EXCEPT ![c] = FreshRegion]
       /\ fwd'  = [fwd EXCEPT ![o] = c]
       /\ UNCHANGED cset
=============================================================================
```

Model-checking all interleavings of `Evacuate` with mutator `Read(o) == fwd[o]` / `Write(o,v)` actions verifies that the invariant holds at every step — the essence of the barrier-correctness argument.

### 4.5 Generational designs and pause-time anatomy

Generational ZGC (JEP 439 [2]) and Generational Shenandoah (productized in JDK 25 [8]) both add a young generation collected with cheap minor cycles, because most objects die young and evacuating only the young generation slashes the concurrent work per cycle. The pause-time anatomy is otherwise unchanged: pauses remain root-scan-bound. Measured pause behavior from independent studies:

- **ZGC:** mean pause 0.07–0.12 ms, p95 ≈ 0.33–0.40 ms, max typically 4–15 ms under high allocation pressure [7]; independent streaming benchmarks report worst-case ~10 ms under light load [6].
- **Shenandoah:** mean pause 1–4 ms, p95 ≈ 9–12 ms, with occasional spikes to 200–600 ms under extreme pressure (degenerated/full GCs) [6][7].
- **G1 (baseline):** floor of ~20–25 ms worst-case in favorable conditions, rising to hundreds of ms under pressure, but with the smoothest degradation curve and no catastrophic cliff [6].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Barrier correctness: the invariant theorems

> **Theorem (ZGC load-barrier soundness):** *If every heap reference load executes the load barrier, and the barrier heals any pointer whose color is "bad" for the current phase (marking unmarked objects, relocating objects in the relocation set, remapping stale fields) before the mutator uses the pointer, then no mutator ever observes a dangling reference, and every object reachable at the end of concurrent marking is marked.*

*Proof sketch.* Induction over mutator steps. The dangerous operation is using a pointer with a bad color. By hypothesis, the barrier intercepts *every* heap reference load — the only way a bad-colored pointer enters a register or the stack — and heals it before use. Healing is defined to establish the phase invariant: during concurrent mark, the loaded referent is marked (strong tri-color invariant preserved); during relocation, the referent is the to-space copy; during remap, fields are updated. The three STW pauses close the residual windows: *Pause Mark End* drains in-flight stores into the marked set, and *Pause Relocate Start* publishes the relocation set atomically so the barrier's "bad" predicate is well-defined. ∎

> **Theorem (Shenandoah to-space soundness):** *If the Brooks forwarding pointer of every object always designates its canonical copy, and every read, write, CAS, and `acmp` resolves through the forwarding pointer, then concurrent evacuation is transparent to the mutator.*

*Proof sketch.* The linearization point of evacuation is the atomic CAS installing the forwarding pointer. Before it, all accesses target the from-space copy, which is intact; after it, all accesses resolve to the to-space copy, which contains an identical payload (the copy is complete before the CAS). Reference-equality (`acmp`) must compare canonical copies — hence the dedicated acmp barrier — otherwise `a == b` could observe two distinct addresses for the same logical object. ∎

### 5.2 Measured pause times and tail latency

The table below consolidates published measurements. All figures are GC pause times unless noted; workload details follow each row.

| Collector | Mean pause | p95 pause | Max pause | Regime | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ZGC | 0.07–0.12 ms | 0.33–0.40 ms | 4–15 ms | Docker, 2–8 GB heap, extreme allocation pressure | [7] |
| ZGC | — | — | ~10 ms | Streaming, light load | [6] |
| Shenandoah | 1–4 ms | 9–12 ms | 200–600 ms | Docker, 2–8 GB heap, extreme pressure | [7] |
| Shenandoah | — | — | ~220 ms spikes | Streaming, low pressure | [6] |
| G1 | 45–60 ms | — | 200 ms–60 s | Same regimes; floor ~20–25 ms favorable | [6][7] |

Three findings stand out. **First**, ZGC's pauses are one to two orders of magnitude below Shenandoah's mean — the tightest pause distribution of any production OpenJDK collector [7]. **Second**, Shenandoah's average is competitive (single-digit ms) but its tail is long: degenerated/full GCs under pressure yield 200–600 ms spikes [6][7]. **Third**, end-to-end latency does not track pause time 1:1 — barrier overhead and pacing stalls shift the whole latency curve [6].

### 5.3 Throughput and memory-overhead costs

Low pauses are not free. ZGC's design goal admits up to ~15% throughput reduction versus G1 [1]; one churn-heavy microbenchmark measured dramatically worse total execution time for ZGC (barrier + concurrent relocation work on every load) while G1/Parallel finished far faster — the expected shape of the trade-off, since the workload was throughput-bound rather than latency-bound [7]. Shenandoah pays 5–10% heap overhead for the forwarding word (reduced after the JDK 13 mark-word overlay [4]) plus extra headroom versus G1 [8]; generational variants of both target recovering throughput via cheap young-gen cycles [2][8].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **The pressure cliff.** When allocation outpaces concurrent relocation, ZGC stalls mutators and Shenandoah degenerates to STW collections; neither matches G1's smooth degradation curve [6]. Designing *graceful* degradation — bounded pause inflation rather than a cliff — remains open.
2. **Barrier overhead on the hot path.** ZGC taxes every reference load; Shenandoah (post-JDK-13) taxes reference loads, stores, CAS, and equality. Hardware assistance (e.g., pointer-authentication-style color checks, memory-tagging extensions) could push barrier costs toward zero, but no production JVM exploits it yet.
3. **NUMA and very large heaps.** ZGC is NUMA-aware [1]; Shenandoah's region selection is less NUMA-sensitive. As heaps push past tens of terabytes, generational ZGC's removal of the 16 TB ceiling [2] reopens questions about barrier encoding density and remembered-set scaling.
4. **Verification gap.** The invariant arguments in Section 5.1 are prose proofs. Machine-checked models of the full barrier protocols (including the generational remembered sets and the mark-word overlay) do not exist publicly; the TLA+ sketch in Section 4.4 covers only the evacuation core. A verified barrier implementation would be a landmark result.
5. **Latency accounting.** Most published numbers measure GC pause time, not *mutator* latency including barrier and pacing effects; end-to-end timeliness benchmarks (e.g., Heapothesys [9]) remain rare.

## 7. Conclusion

ZGC and Shenandoah represent the two fundamental answers to concurrent compaction: put the indirection *in the pointer* and pay per load (ZGC's colored pointers + load-value barrier), or put it *in the object* and pay per object plus per store (Shenandoah's Brooks forwarding pointer + barrier suite). Both reduce the stop-the-world component to root scanning, making pause times independent of heap and live-set size — the property that defines the low-latency regime. The measured record favors ZGC for the tightest pause distribution (sub-millisecond means, ~10 ms maxima) at the cost of per-load barrier overhead and throughput; Shenandoah offers competitive average pauses with lower steady-state overhead but a longer tail under pressure. Their generational successors (JEP 439; JDK 25 Shenandoah) converge on the same lesson: make the common case nearly free and spend the concurrency budget where fragmentation accumulates. The deepest open problem is not a new barrier but a *verified* one: machine-checked proof that these intricate concurrent protocols never lose an object.
