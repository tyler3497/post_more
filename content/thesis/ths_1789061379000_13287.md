## 1 Introduction

The discovery that deep neural networks are vulnerable to *adversarial examples* — imperceptibly perturbed inputs that induce confident misclassification — ranks among the most consequential findings in modern machine learning safety research. For over a decade, the community's response followed a familiar pattern: propose an empirical defense, watch it be broken by a stronger attack, and repeat. Gradient masking, defensive distillation, and a host of input transformations each claimed robustness, and each was subsequently reduced to obscurity by adaptive adversaries. This cycle revealed a methodological crisis: *robustness against known attacks is not robustness*. What safety-critical deployment demands is a **proof** — a certificate that, for a given input and perturbation budget, *no* adversarial example exists.

**Certified robustness** formalizes this demand. Given a classifier $f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathcal{Y}$ and a threat model $\mathcal{S}(x) = \{x' : \|x' - x\|_p \le \epsilon\}$, we say $f$ is *certified robust* at $x$ if

$$\forall x' \in \mathcal{S}(x),\quad f(x') = f(x).$$

The fraction of test points satisfying this property — the **certified accuracy** — is the gold-standard metric of certified defense research. Unlike empirical *robust accuracy* measured against a particular attack, certified accuracy is a *lower bound* on true worst-case robustness that no future, undiscovered attack can refute.

Three families of techniques dominate this landscape, and this thesis is devoted to their rigorous unification:

1. **Randomized smoothing** [1][5]: a *probabilistic* certificate. By voting under Gaussian noise, any base classifier — however deep, however nonlinear — becomes provably robust within an ℓ₂ ball whose radius is computed in closed form from the vote margins.
2. **Interval bound propagation** [3]: an *algebraic* certificate. Propagating interval arithmetic through the network computes sound bounds on all reachable activations at roughly twice the cost of a forward pass, and — crucially — the bounds are differentiable, enabling training that directly optimizes certifiability.
3. **Convex relaxations** [2][4][6]: an *optimization* certificate. Replacing each nonlinear activation with a convex envelope turns verification into a convex program, yielding the tightest bounds among scalable methods at the price of per-sample optimization.

Our central thesis is that these are not competing alternatives but points on a single **tightness–computability frontier**, and that understanding the frontier's shape — where each method sits, and what is provably impossible beyond it — is the key to deploying provable defense in practice.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The verification problem

For a feed-forward network $f(x) = z^{(K)}$ with layers $z^{(k)} = \sigma(W^{(k)} z^{(k-1)} + b^{(k)})$, the local robustness question at input $x_0$ with true class $y$ reduces to the **minimum-margin problem**:

$$\min_{\delta \in \mathcal{S}(x_0)} \left( z^{(K)}_y(x_0 + \delta) - \max_{y' \neq y}\, z^{(K)}_{y'}(x_0 + \delta) \right).$$

If the minimum is positive, the prediction is certifiably stable; if negative, an adversarial example exists. This optimization is **NP-hard** for ReLU networks even for modest depth — a fact that forces every practical method into one of two escape routes:

- **Sound but incomplete** methods (IBP, convex relaxations, Fast-Lin): compute a *lower bound* on the margin. A positive bound certifies; a negative bound is inconclusive — it may indicate either a true adversarial example or mere looseness of the relaxation.
- **Probabilistic** methods (randomized smoothing): certify a *high-probability* guarantee via sampling, trading determinism for scalability to ImageNet-scale models.

### 2.2 A taxonomy of certificates

| Family | Certificate type | Threat norm | Completeness | Scalability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Randomized smoothing [1] | Probabilistic, tight ℓ₂ | ℓ₂ (ℓ₁, ℓ∞ variants exist) | Exact certificate via Neyman–Pearson | ImageNet-scale; Monte Carlo cost |
| Interval bound propagation [3] | Deterministic, sound | ℓ∞ (ℓ₂ variants) | Incomplete; loose for deep nets | ~2× forward pass; trainable |
| Convex outer polytope [2] | Deterministic, sound | ℓ∞ | Incomplete; LP dual as backprop | Small–medium networks |
| CROWN / Fast-Lin [4][7] | Deterministic, sound | ℓ∞/ℓ₂ | Incomplete; tighter than IBP | Linear bounds per neuron |
| Complete verification (MILP) | Deterministic, complete | ℓ∞ | Complete (exact) | Tiny networks only |

### 2.3 Key notation

Throughout, $\Phi$ denotes the standard Gaussian CDF and $\Phi^{-1}$ its inverse. $\underline{z}^{(k)}, \overline{z}^{(k)}$ denote sound lower and upper bounds on layer-$k$ activations under the threat model. The **adversarial polytope** $\mathcal{Z}(x_0, \epsilon)$ is the set of all final-layer activations reachable by admissible perturbations — the object every relaxation method seeks to outer-approximate.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis proceeds by *derivation and comparison* rather than by new experiments: we reconstruct the governing certificates of each family from first principles, prove their soundness, quantify their tightness, and locate them on the relaxation hierarchy. Where empirical claims are made, we report published benchmark results [1][2][3][5], whose procedures we describe precisely enough for independent replication.

**Method 1 — Smoothing analysis.** We define the smoothed classifier, state the Neyman–Pearson lemma for Gaussian location families, derive the certified radius, and present the Monte Carlo certification protocol with Clopper–Pearson confidence intervals as implemented in the reference implementation of [1].

**Method 2 — IBP analysis.** We formalize interval arithmetic through affine layers and monotone activations, derive the certified training objective with ε-annealing and κ-mixing [3], and prove soundness by induction over layers.

**Method 3 — Relaxation analysis.** We present the convex outer adversarial polytope of [2] and its LP dual — which takes the form of a modified backpropagation pass — then describe how CROWN [4] generalizes this to arbitrary activation functions via adaptive linear and quadratic envelopes. We unify these with the **auto_LiRPA** viewpoint [6], in which every relaxation is an instance of linear bound propagation with different per-neuron envelope choices.

**Method 4 — Frontier analysis.** We compare certificates on three axes — *tightness* (how close the certified bound is to the true worst-case margin), *computability* (cost and differentiability), and *generality* (norms, activations, architectures) — and derive the impossibility arguments that bound what any tractable certificate can achieve.

The following Python sketch implements the core certification primitives analyzed in this thesis, following the reference protocols of [1] and [3]:

```python
import math
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

def certified_radius(pA: float, pB: float, sigma: float) -> float:
    """Cohen et al. (2019) Neyman-Pearson radius [1].
    pA: lower bound on top-class vote probability.
    pB: upper bound on runner-up vote probability."""
    return sigma * (norm.ppf(pA) - norm.ppf(pB)) / 2.0

def clopper_pearson_lower(count: int, n: int, alpha: float = 0.001) -> float:
    """One-sided lower confidence bound for a binomial proportion."""
    from scipy.stats import beta
    return beta.ppf(alpha, count, n - count + 1)

def certify(f, x: np.ndarray, sigma: float, n0: int = 100,
            n: int = 100_000, alpha: float = 0.001):
    """Monte Carlo certification of the smoothed classifier g [1]."""
    counts = np.bincount([f(x + np.random.normal(0, sigma, x.shape))
                          for _ in range(n0)])
    cA = int(np.argmax(counts))
    nA = sum(1 for _ in range(n) if f(x + np.random.normal(0, sigma, x.shape)) == cA)
    pA = clopper_pearson_lower(nA, n, alpha)
    if pA < 0.5:
        return cA, 0.0, False          # abstain: cannot certify
    pB = 1.0 - pA                      # worst-case runner-up bound
    R = certified_radius(pA, pB, sigma)
    return cA, R, True

def ibp_affine(z_lo, z_hi, W, b):
    """Sound interval propagation through an affine layer [3]."""
    Wp, Wn = np.clip(W, 0, None), np.clip(W, None, 0)
    return Wp @ z_lo + Wn @ z_hi + b, Wp @ z_hi + Wn @ z_lo + b

def ibp_relu(z_lo, z_hi):
    """Monotone activations propagate coordinate-wise [3]."""
    return np.clip(z_lo, 0, None), np.clip(z_hi, 0, None)

def ibp_certified(model, x, eps, y):
    """Sound margin lower bound via interval bound propagation [3]."""
    lo, hi = x - eps, x + eps
    for W, b in model.layers:
        lo, hi = ibp_affine(lo, hi, W, b)
        lo, hi = ibp_relu(lo, hi)
    margin = lo[y] - max(hi[j] for j in range(len(hi)) if j != y)
    return margin > 0, margin
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Neyman–Pearson Certificate and Its Tightness

Randomized smoothing begins with a disarmingly simple construction [1]. Given any base classifier $f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathcal{Y}$ and noise scale $\sigma > 0$, define the **smoothed classifier**

$$g(x) = \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{Y}}\; \mathbb{P}_{\delta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)}\big(f(x + \delta) = c\big).$$

Prediction and certification are performed by Monte Carlo: draw $n_0$ samples to identify the top class $c_A$, then $n$ samples to lower-bound $p_A = \mathbb{P}(f(x+\delta) = c_A)$ via the Clopper–Pearson interval, and upper-bound the runner-up probability $p_B = \max_{c \neq c_A}\mathbb{P}(f(x+\delta) = c)$.

> **Theorem (Cohen–Rosenfeld–Kolter, 2019 [1]):** Suppose $p_A, p_B \in [0,1]$ satisfy $\mathbb{P}(f(x+\delta)=c_A) \ge p_A \ge p_B \ge \max_{c\ne c_A}\mathbb{P}(f(x+\delta)=c)$ for $\delta \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)$. Then $g(x+\delta') = c_A$ for all $\|\delta'\|_2 < R$, where
> $$R = \frac{\sigma}{2}\Big(\Phi^{-1}(p_A) - \Phi^{-1}(p_B)\Big).$$

The proof applies the **Neyman–Pearson lemma** to the hypothesis test distinguishing $\mathcal{N}(x, \sigma^2 I)$ from $\mathcal{N}(x+\delta', \sigma^2 I)$: among all measurable classifiers, the likelihood-ratio test maximizes the probability of class $c_A$ under the shifted distribution, and the worst-case adversarial $f$ can therefore be assumed to be exactly such a threshold test. The monotone likelihood ratio of the Gaussian location family then yields the closed form above. Crucially, this certificate is **tight**: no ℓ₂ radius larger than $R$ can be certified from $(p_A, p_B)$ alone, because the threshold-test adversary attains it. Earlier DP-based smoothing bounds [1] were strictly looser; the Neyman–Pearson analysis is the first optimal one.

Two properties make smoothing remarkable:

- **Model agnosticism.** The certificate makes *zero* assumptions about $f$ — it applies to any classifier, including non-differentiable ensembles and decision trees. This is why smoothing was the first certified defense demonstrated on **ImageNet**: a ResNet-50 base classifier yields 49% certified top-1 accuracy at ℓ₂ radius $0.5 = 127/255$ [1].
- **Adaptive training.** Salman et al. showed that adversarially training the *smoothed* classifier — attacking the vote distribution rather than the base classifier — substantially improves certified accuracy, establishing the state of the art for provable ℓ₂ defenses on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet [5].

Yet tightness of the *certificate* does not imply tightness of the *defense*: the radius depends on the base classifier's noise robustness, and training base classifiers to be accurate under large Gaussian noise incurs a steep accuracy–robustness trade-off.

### 4.2 Interval Bound Propagation: Soundness at Twice the Cost of a Forward Pass

IBP takes the opposite approach: rather than randomizing the input, it *deterministically* tracks the set of all possible activations as intervals [3]. Starting from $\underline{z}^{(0)} = x - \epsilon$, $\overline{z}^{(0)} = x + \epsilon$ for an ℓ∞ ball, each layer propagates bounds analytically:

- **Affine layers** use the positive/negative decomposition of the weight matrix:
$$\underline{z}^{(k)} = W^{(k)}_+ \underline{z}^{(k-1)} + W^{(k)}_- \overline{z}^{(k-1)} + b^{(k)},\qquad \overline{z}^{(k)} = W^{(k)}_+ \overline{z}^{(k-1)} + W^{(k)}_- \underline{z}^{(k-1)} + b^{(k)},$$
- **Monotone activations** (ReLU, sigmoid, tanh) propagate coordinate-wise: $\underline{z}^{(k)} = \sigma(\underline{z}^{(k-1)})$, $\overline{z}^{(k)} = \sigma(\overline{z}^{(k-1)})$.

> **Proposition (Soundness of IBP [3]):** By induction on layers, $z^{(k)}(x+\delta) \in [\underline{z}^{(k)}, \overline{z}^{(k)}]$ for every $\|\delta\|_\infty \le \epsilon$. If the *worst-case margin* $\underline{z}^{(K)}_y - \max_{y'\ne y}\overline{z}^{(K)}_{y'} > 0$, the network is certifiably robust at $x$.

The cost is roughly **two forward passes** — orders of magnitude cheaper than solving an LP per sample — and, decisive for practice, every operation is **differentiable**. Gowal et al. exploit this to train *for certifiability*: the loss mixes clean logits $z^{(K)}$ with worst-case logits $\hat{z}^{(K)}$ (upper bounds for wrong classes, lower bound for the true class),

$$\mathcal{L} = \kappa\,\ell(z^{(K)}, y) + (1-\kappa)\,\ell(\hat{z}^{(K)}, y),$$

with both the perturbation radius $\epsilon$ and the mixing weight $\kappa$ annealed during training [3]. This "robust natural" training was the first to produce networks with genuinely tight IBP bounds at scale.

IBP's weakness is structural: intervals are *axis-aligned boxes*, the crudest convex outer approximation. The wrapping effect — each layer's box over-approximating the true reachable set — compounds with depth, so bounds can explode in deep networks unless training explicitly regularizes them. IBP is thus simultaneously the cheapest sound method and the loosest in the relaxation hierarchy.

### 4.3 Convex Relaxations: The Outer Adversarial Polytope and CROWN

Wong and Kolter's **convex outer adversarial polytope** [2] was the first framework to *train* provably robust ReLU networks by convex relaxation. For each ReLU with pre-activation bounds $[l, u]$, the exact graph $\{(z, \max(0,z))\}$ is replaced by its convex hull — a triangle of linear constraints:

$$\hat{z} \ge 0,\quad \hat{z} \ge z,\quad \hat{z} \le \frac{u}{u-l}(z - l)\quad \text{when } l < 0 < u.$$

Minimizing the worst-case loss over this outer polytope is a **linear program**. The breakthrough of [2] is that the LP *dual* admits a closed form — a "dual network" resembling backpropagation through modified activations — so the certified robust loss can be optimized with essentially a few extra forward/backward passes. On MNIST they produced a convolutional classifier with provably under **5.8% test error** against *any* ℓ∞ attack with $\epsilon = 0.1$ — the largest verified network of its time [2].

**CROWN** [4][7] generalizes this idea to arbitrary activation functions. Where Fast-Lin-style methods commit to a fixed ReLU envelope, CROWN *adaptively* selects, per neuron, between linear and quadratic bounds on the activation, and propagates linear lower/upper bounds on the network output backward through the layers. The result: certified lower bounds on the minimum adversarial distortion up to **28% tighter** than Fast-Lin at comparable computational cost, and applicability to tanh, sigmoid, and arctan networks where ReLU-specific methods fail [7].

The modern unification is the **auto_LiRPA** view [6]: IBP, Fast-Lin, CROWN, and the Wong–Kolter dual are all instances of *linear bound propagation* differing only in the tightness of their per-neuron envelopes. This yields the **CROWN-IBP** training scheme, which combines CROWN's tight bounds with IBP's cheap differentiable forward bounds — inheriting the tightness of the former and the scalability of the latter [6].

### 4.4 The Tightness–Computability Frontier

We can now state the frontier precisely. Let $m^\star(x)$ be the true worst-case margin and $\underline{m}_{\mathcal{M}}(x)$ the certified lower bound from method $\mathcal{M}$. The **relaxation gap** $\Delta_{\mathcal{M}} = m^\star - \underline{m}_{\mathcal{M}} \ge 0$ measures tightness loss. Across methods:

| Method | Per-sample cost | Typical gap | Differentiable |
|---|---|---|---|
| IBP [3] | $O(\text{forward pass})$ | Large (wrapping effect) | Yes |
| Fast-Lin / CROWN [4][7] | $O(\text{layers} \times \text{neurons})$ | Moderate | Partially (CROWN-IBP [6]) |
| Wong–Kolter dual LP [2] | $O(\text{forward+backward})$ | Moderate | Yes (dual network) |
| Complete MILP verification | Exponential | Zero | No |
| Randomized smoothing [1] | $O(n)$ samples | Zero (tight certificate) | Via noise augmentation [5] |

Three *impossibility* observations define the frontier's boundary:

1. **Exactness is intractable.** Complete verification of ReLU networks is NP-complete; any polynomial-time certificate must sometimes abstain or be loose. There is no free exactness.
2. **Tightness trades against generality.** The Neyman–Pearson certificate is tight but probabilistic and ℓ₂-native; deterministic convex relaxations are general but incomplete. No known method is simultaneously deterministic, tight, and scalable.
3. **Depth amplifies looseness.** For any fixed relaxation family, the gap $\Delta$ grows with network depth unless training explicitly controls the Lipschitz behavior of layers — which is precisely what IBP-style and adversarial training accomplish, at the cost of clean accuracy.

These are not transient engineering gaps but structural facts about approximating a nonconvex reachable set with tractable convex objects.

## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Published benchmark results

The following certified accuracies are reported in the cited works (threat model and architecture as specified):

| Method | Dataset | Threat model | Certified accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Randomized smoothing [1] | ImageNet | ℓ₂, $r = 0.5$ | **49%** top-1 |
| Randomized smoothing [1] | CIFAR-10 | ℓ₂, $r = 0.25$ | **61%** (typical, $\sigma=0.25$) |
| SmoothAdv (adv.-trained smoothing) [5] | ImageNet | ℓ₂, $r = 0.5$ | **SOTA**, large margin over [1] |
| Convex outer polytope [2] | MNIST | ℓ∞, $\epsilon = 0.1$ | **>94.2%** (error provably <5.8%) |
| IBP-trained model [3] | MNIST | ℓ∞, $\epsilon = 0.3$ | Competitive certified accuracy at low cost |
| CROWN [4] | Various ReLU nets | ℓ∞/ℓ₂ | Up to **28%** tighter certified lower bounds than Fast-Lin |

The pattern is unmistakable: smoothing dominates ℓ₂ benchmarks and scales to ImageNet; IBP dominates the cost axis; convex relaxations dominate tightness among deterministic methods on small networks.

### 5.2 Proof sketch: soundness of the IBP margin

*Claim.* Let $[\underline{z}^{(k)}, \overline{z}^{(k)}]$ be computed by the IBP recurrences of §4.2. Then for every $\|\delta\|_\infty \le \epsilon$ and every layer $k$, $z^{(k)}(x+\delta) \in [\underline{z}^{(k)}, \overline{z}^{(k)}]$.

*Proof.* By induction on $k$. Base case $k=0$ holds by construction of the ℓ∞ input box. Inductive step: assume the claim for layer $k-1$. For an affine layer, $\underline{z}^{(k)}_i = \min_{\underline{z}^{(k-1)} \le z \le \overline{z}^{(k-1)}} e_i^\top (W^{(k)}z + b^{(k)})$ because the linear objective attains its minimum at a vertex of the box — exactly the positive/negative decomposition. For monotone $\sigma$, $\min_{[l,u]} \sigma = \sigma(l)$ and $\max = \sigma(u)$. ∎

*Corollary.* A positive worst-case margin $\underline{z}^{(K)}_y - \max_{y'\ne y}\overline{z}^{(K)}_{y'} > 0$ implies $f(x+\delta) = y$ for all admissible $\delta$ — a sound certificate. The bound is, however, *incomplete*: a negative margin proves nothing, since the true reachable set may be strictly inside the box.

## 6 Limitations

- **Norm-bounded threat models are narrow.** ℓ₂ and ℓ∞ balls poorly model real-world corruptions — rotations, lighting changes, semantic edits. Certified accuracy under one norm says nothing about another; the entire literature's guarantees are *norm-relative*.
- **The accuracy–robustness trade-off persists.** Certified training (IBP annealing, noise augmentation, SmoothAdv) consistently reduces clean accuracy. Provable safety is purchased with standard performance, and the exchange rate is steep at large radii.
- **Probabilistic certificates abstain.** Smoothing certification returns "abstain" when $\underline{p_A} < 1/2$; abstention rates grow with $\sigma$ and dataset difficulty. A guarantee that frequently declines to answer is of limited operational value.
- **Deterministic relaxations degrade with scale.** LP-based certificates remain impractical for the largest vision and language models; the wrapping effect of IBP compounds in very deep networks; complete verification is confined to toy architectures.
- **Tightness is certificate-relative.** The Neyman–Pearson radius is tight *given the vote probabilities* — but the votes themselves come from a base classifier whose noise robustness we cannot verify. Tightness of the certificate must not be confused with tightness of the defense.
- **Specification, not just robustness.** Certified robustness proves the model's prediction is *stable* under perturbation — not that it is *correct*. A certifiably robust classifier can be certifiably wrong; provable defense addresses output stability, leaving specification correctness untouched.

## 7 Conclusion

Certified adversarial robustness has matured from a mathematical curiosity into a rigorous engineering discipline with three complementary pillars. **Randomized smoothing** [1][5] proved that provable ℓ₂ defense scales to ImageNet, with a tight certificate derived from the Neyman–Pearson lemma that no vote-based argument can improve upon. **Interval bound propagation** [3] proved that sound certificates can cost little more than a forward pass and — through annealed certified training — be made tight enough for practical ℓ∞ defense. **Convex relaxations** [2][4][6] proved that the adversarial polytope can be outer-approximated with increasing fidelity, culminating in adaptive frameworks like CROWN that handle general activations and in CROWN-IBP hybrids that merge tightness with trainability.

The deeper lesson is the **frontier**: exactness is NP-hard, tightness trades against generality and computability, and depth amplifies every relaxation's looseness. These limits are not bugs to be patched but theorems to be respected — they delineate what provable defense can ever promise. Future progress lies not in escaping the frontier but in *moving along it intelligently*: certificates for semantic and perceptual threat models, tighter envelopes for modern architectures, and verification-aware training that makes tightness a first-class objective. The arms race is over; the era of proofs has begun, and its boundaries are now, at last, mapped.
