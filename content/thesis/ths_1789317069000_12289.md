# Quantum Machine Learning: Variational Quantum Circuits, Barren Plateaus, and Quantum Kernel Feature Maps

## Abstract

Quantum machine learning (QML) proposes to exploit the exponentially large Hilbert space of quantum systems as a computational resource for data analysis. This thesis develops a rigorous account of the two dominant near-term QML paradigms: variational quantum circuits (VQCs), trained by hybrid quantum-classical optimization loops, and quantum kernel methods, in which classical data are embedded into quantum states and classified by support-vector machinery [1][3][4][5]. We derive the parameter-shift rule enabling gradient-based training on hardware, formalize the barren plateau phenomenon — exponential concentration of gradients obstructing large-scale VQC training [1][2] — and present the locality theorems separating tractable local cost functions from intractable global ones [2]. We reconstruct Schuld's theorem showing supervised quantum models are kernel methods [3], and examine Havlicek et al.'s quantum-enhanced feature maps demonstrated on superconducting hardware [4]. Formal results include the 2-design analysis of gradient variance decay, the convex optimality guarantee of kernel-based training, and Liu et al.'s provable quantum speedup for a discrete-logarithm-based learning task [8]. We close by delineating the NISQ-era limitations: data-loading bottlenecks, noise-induced plateaus, and dequantization.

## 1. Introduction

The promise of quantum machine learning rests on a deceptively simple observation: an *n*-qubit quantum system is described by a Hilbert space of dimension 2^n, and controlled interference among its basis states can, in principle, represent and transform probability distributions that no classical computer could efficiently simulate. The Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era — machines with tens to hundreds of physical qubits and no fault tolerance — forced the field to abandon deep coherent algorithms such as quantum phase estimation in favor of **hybrid quantum-classical algorithms**, in which a parameterized quantum circuit is executed on the device while a classical optimizer adjusts its parameters [5].

Two intellectual lineages converged on this program. From quantum simulation came the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE), introduced by Peruzzo et al., which recasts eigenvalue problems as variational optimization over a trial-state ansatz evaluated by repeated short-depth state preparations and measurements [5]. From combinatorial optimization came the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) of Farhi, Goldstone, and Gutmann, an alternating-operator ansatz whose parameters interpolate between the classical and mixing Hamiltonians [6]. Together, VQE and QAOA furnished the template that machine learning researchers adopted: the **variational quantum circuit**, also called a parameterized quantum circuit (PQC) or quantum neural network, mapping a data input and a trainable parameter vector to a measurable prediction [7].

> **Definition (Variational quantum model):** A variational quantum model is a function *f(theta, x)* obtained by (i) encoding classical data *x* into a quantum state via a feature map *U_phi(x)*, (ii) applying a parameterized unitary *W(theta)*, and (iii) estimating the expectation value of a Hermitian observable *O* on the output state. Training adjusts *theta* to minimize an empirical cost function using gradient estimates obtained from the quantum device itself.

Three questions have structured the subsequent decade of research, and each receives a full treatment in this thesis:

1. **Trainability.** Can the parameters of a VQC be optimized at scale? The answer is famously conditional: random deep ansätze exhibit *barren plateaus* — flat regions where gradient variances decay exponentially with system size [1] — but carefully chosen cost functions and initialization strategies restore polynomial trainability [2].
2. **Expressivity and structure.** What function classes do quantum models realize? Schuld's kernel-theoretic result proves that supervised quantum models are *kernel methods*, reducing variational training to convex optimization over data-encoding kernels [3].
3. **Advantage.** Is there any learning task for which a quantum model provably outperforms every classical learner? The answer is affirmative under classical-data-access assumptions, though the known constructions are deliberately contrived [8].

The remainder of this thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the mathematical background: parameterized unitaries, measurement statistics, and the kernel trick. Section 3 develops the methodology: the parameter-shift rule, gradient-based training loops, fidelity and projected quantum kernels, and quantum kernel estimation. Section 4 deep-dives into ansatz design, the anatomy of barren plateaus, the kernel reformulation, and hybrid optimization architectures. Section 5 presents formal results and an empirical evaluation of quantum kernels on benchmark data. Section 6 confronts limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Parameterized quantum circuits

A parameterized quantum circuit on *n* qubits is a unitary *U(theta)* assembled from fixed entangling gates and single-qubit rotations *exp(-i theta_k P_k / 2)*, where *P_k* is a Pauli operator and *theta_k* a real parameter. The state prepared from the reference input is *|psi(theta)> = U(theta) |0>^tensor n*. A prediction is formed by measuring an observable:

*f(theta) = <0| U(theta)^dagger O U(theta) |0>*

which, by the Born rule, is estimated from repeated preparations (*shots*). The shot complexity of estimating *f(theta)* to precision *epsilon* scales as *O(1/epsilon^2)* by Chebyshev's inequality, a cost that interacts fatally with vanishing gradients, as Section 4.2 explains.

> **Theorem:** *The parameter-shift rule.* If *theta_k* enters *U* through a single Pauli rotation *exp(-i theta_k P / 2)*, then the exact derivative of the expectation value is
>
> *d f / d theta_k = (f(theta_k + pi/2) - f(theta_k - pi/2)) / 2.*
>
> Hence gradients of VQCs are exactly computable on quantum hardware using two circuit evaluations per parameter, without finite-difference approximations.

This result, foundational to all VQC training, converts the analytically inaccessible gradient into two additional expectation-value queries per parameter, and it generalizes to multi-term shifts for arbitrary generator spectra.

### 2.2 Data encoding and feature maps

Classical data enter the quantum model through a **feature map** *x -> |phi(x)> = U_phi(x) |0>* [4][7]. Common choices include:

- **Angle encoding:** each feature becomes a rotation angle on one qubit, so *d* features require *d* qubits and depth *O(1)*.
- **Amplitude encoding:** a normalized vector of dimension 2^n is stored in the amplitudes of *n* qubits — exponentially compact but requiring *O(2^n)* preparation depth in the worst case, which typically erases any advantage.
- **IQP-style and ZZ feature maps:** layers of Hadamard gates, single-qubit *Z* rotations keyed to individual features, and entangling *ZZ* rotations keyed to products of features, repeated to build classically hard interference patterns [4].

Schuld and Killoran showed that such encodings define a *feature Hilbert space*: the model *f(x) = <w| phi(x)>* is a linear model in the space of quantum states, and the entire supervised-learning pipeline reduces to kernel evaluations of the form *k(x, x') = |<phi(x) | phi(x')>|^2* [7].

### 2.3 Kernel methods and the representer theorem

Classical kernel methods solve regularized risk minimization over a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). The representer theorem guarantees that the optimal model lies in the span of kernel evaluations on the training set, *f(x) = sum_i alpha_i k(x_i, x)*, so training is a convex quadratic program in the coefficients *alpha_i*. Schuld's central result extends this machinery wholesale to quantum models [3]: any supervised quantum model of the form *f(x) = Tr[O rho(x)]* is a kernel method with kernel *k(x, x') = Tr[rho(x) rho(x')]*, and kernel-based training is guaranteed to find a model at least as good as the best variationally trained one.

### 2.4 NISQ constraints and ansatz families

VQC research organizes around two ansatz families [5][6]:

| Ansatz family | Structure | Inductive bias | Trainability profile |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Problem-inspired (VQE/QAOA)** | Alternating cost and mixer layers | Ground states, MaxCut cuts | Narrow minima; structured gradients |
| **Hardware-efficient** | Repeating blocks of local rotations + entanglers | Hardware-native expressivity | 2-design-like; barren-plateau prone [1] |
| **Quantum convolutional (QCNN)** | Hierarchical pooling of qubits | Translation-invariant patterns | Local costs; polynomially trainable [2] |
| **Data re-uploading** | Interleaved encoding and variational layers | Fourier-series models | Spectrum controlled by re-upload count |

This taxonomy matters because the choice of ansatz determines both the expressivity of the hypothesis class and the geometry of the loss landscape.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Hybrid quantum-classical training loop

All VQC training follows a single loop. Initialize parameters *theta^(0)*; at each iteration *t*, the quantum processor evaluates the cost *C(theta^(t))* and its gradient components via the parameter-shift rule; the classical optimizer (gradient descent, Adam, or natural-gradient variants) proposes *theta^(t+1)*; repeat until convergence. The quantum device is thus a *co-processor for expectation values*, exactly the division of labor that made VQE viable on NISQ hardware [5].

```python
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit_machine_learning.neural_networks import EstimatorQNN
from qiskit.circuit.library import ZZFeatureMap, RealAmplitudes

# Quantum-enhanced feature map: data -> Hilbert space (Havlicek et al. style) [4]
feature_map = ZZFeatureMap(feature_dimension=2, reps=2)
ansatz = RealAmplitudes(num_qubits=2, reps=3)   # trainable block W(theta)

qc = QuantumCircuit(2)
qc.compose(feature_map, inplace=True)  # U_phi(x): encodes x into |phi(x)>
qc.compose(ansatz, inplace=True)       # W(theta): trainable unitary

qnn = EstimatorQNN(circuit=qc, input_params=feature_map.parameters,
                   weight_params=ansatz.parameters)
```

### 3.2 Quantum kernel estimation

The **quantum kernel estimator** (QKE) replaces variational training entirely [4]. For a training set of *M* points, each pair *(x_i, x_j)* is sent to the device, which prepares *|phi(x_i)>* and *|phi(x_j)>* and estimates the fidelity *|<phi(x_i) | phi(x_j)>|^2* via a compute-uncompute circuit or a SWAP test. The resulting *M x M* kernel matrix enters a classical support-vector machine, whose dual quadratic program is convex. Two kernel families dominate [3][7]:

1. **Fidelity quantum kernels (FQK):** *k(x, x') = Tr[rho(x) rho(x')] = |<phi(x)|phi(x')>|^2*, directly probing the global state overlap.
2. **Projected quantum kernels (PQK):** distances between *reduced* density matrices of subsystems, which resist the exponential concentration that afflicts global overlaps.

### 3.3 Cost-function design

Following Cerezo et al., we classify cost functions by the locality of their defining observable [2]. A **global cost** uses an observable acting nontrivially on all *n* qubits (for example, the projector onto *|0>^tensor n*); a **local cost** sums observables acting on *O(1)*-sized neighborhoods. The distinction is the single most important design decision in VQC training, as Section 4.2 proves.

### 3.4 Statistical methodology for gradient analysis

Barren-plateau analysis studies the *distribution* of the partial derivative *d_k C = partial C / partial theta_k* over random parameter initializations. The circuit is a **barren plateau** if, for every *k*, the variance satisfies *Var_theta[d_k C] in O(b^{-n})* for some *b > 1*, i.e., gradients concentrate exponentially tightly around zero. Estimation then requires exponentially many shots to resolve a descent direction [1].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Variational ansätze and the expressivity–trainability tension

A VQC is expressive when its reachable state manifold covers the states needed for the task, and trainable when gradient-based optimizers can navigate its cost landscape. These desiderata conflict. McClean et al. showed that a hardware-efficient ansatz of sufficient depth forms an approximate **unitary 2-design** — its first two moments match those of the Haar distribution — and in that regime the gradient variance decays as *O(1/2^n)* for global costs [1]. The very randomness that guarantees expressivity washes out the directional signal needed for optimization.

Three principled responses have emerged:

1. **Problem-inspired ansätze.** QAOA [6] and Hamiltonian variational ansätze restrict the search to states reachable by Trotterized evolution under problem Hamiltonians. The parameter count is *O(p)* for depth *p*, and gradients concentrate only polynomially for physically local problems.
2. **Structured initialization.** Identity-block and layerwise-pretraining schemes initialize the circuit as a shallow effective unitary, so early training proceeds in a plateau-free region before depth is grown [2].
3. **Geometric and natural-gradient optimizers.** The quantum natural gradient preconditions updates with the Fubini–Study metric tensor, following the steepest descent direction in the geometry of the state manifold rather than in raw parameter space, partially compensating for flat regions.

### 4.2 The anatomy of barren plateaus

Barren plateaus have multiple independent causes, and a serious treatment must distinguish them [1][2]:

- **Expressivity-induced plateaus.** Deep unstructured ansätze approximate 2-designs; concentration of measure in high-dimensional Hilbert space forces *Var[d_k C] <= O(2^{-n})*. This is the original McClean et al. result [1].
- **Cost-induced plateaus.** Even *shallow* circuits suffer exponentially vanishing gradients when the cost is defined by a global observable. Cerezo et al. proved that global costs yield *Var[d_k C] in O(2^{-n})* for any depth, whereas local costs with depth *O(log n)* yield at worst polynomially vanishing gradients [2].
- **Noise-induced plateaus.** Unital noise channels contract the Bloch ball at a rate exponential in circuit depth, flattening the landscape deterministically rather than statistically.
- **Entanglement-induced plateaus.** Excessive entanglement between visible and hidden subsystems in dissipative QNN architectures scrambles local gradient information.

> **Theorem:** *(Cerezo et al., locality–trainability).* Let *V(theta)* be an alternating layered ansatz whose blocks form local 2-designs, and let *C* be defined by a *k*-local observable with depth *L in O(log n)*. Then *Var_theta[partial_k C] in Omega(1/poly(n))*. In particular, no exponential barren plateau occurs. If instead the observable is global, *Var_theta[partial_k C] in O(2^{-n})* even at constant depth [2].

The practical moral is sharp: **locality is trainability**. Quantum convolutional neural networks and local VQE Hamiltonians inherit polynomial trainability from this theorem, while global fidelity-based costs — including the canonical *|<psi(theta)|psi_target>|^2* overlap — do not.

### 4.3 Quantum models are kernel methods

Schuld's theorem reframes the entire variational program [3]. Consider the general supervised quantum model

*f(x) = Tr[O rho(x)], with rho(x) = U_phi(x) rho_0 U_phi(x)^dagger.*

Because the trace is linear in *rho(x)*, *f* is a linear model in the Hilbert space of density matrices equipped with the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product. The induced kernel is *k(x, x') = Tr[rho(x) rho(x')] = |<phi(x)|phi(x')>|^2*. The consequences are sweeping:

1. **Convexity.** Kernel-based training solves a convex dual SVM problem; it cannot get trapped in the non-convex landscape that variational training navigates, and it is guaranteed to find a model at least as good as the variational optimum [3].
2. **Encoding is everything.** The kernel depends only on the feature map *U_phi*, not on the variational block *W(theta)*. Quantum advantage, if it exists, lives in the data encoding, not in the optimizer's struggle over parameters.
3. **Fourier analysis of models.** For time-evolution encodings *S(x) = W^{(N+1)} exp(-i x_N G) W^{(N)} ... W^{(1)}*, the model is a truncated Fourier series whose frequency spectrum is determined by the generator *G* and the number of re-uploadings; the kernel inherits this spectral structure [3].

Havlicek et al. provided the experimental counterpart: using a two-qubit superconducting processor, they estimated kernels from ZZ-type feature maps and classified engineered datasets with near-perfect accuracy, demonstrating that quantum-enhanced feature spaces are realizable on NISQ hardware [4].

### 4.4 Hybrid optimization architectures

A complete QML system couples four components in a feedback loop:

1. **Data encoding** (*U_phi*): classical features become quantum states.
2. **Parameterized processing** (*W(theta)*): the trainable ansatz.
3. **Measurement**: observable estimation with shot-noise statistics.
4. **Classical optimization**: gradient or gradient-free updates of *theta*, possibly with error mitigation (zero-noise extrapolation, probabilistic error cancellation) inserted between measurement and update.

The classical optimizer's choice matters more than is commonly acknowledged. Gradient-free methods (COBYLA, SPSA) avoid the *O(P)* circuit evaluations per step that the parameter-shift rule demands for *P* parameters, at the cost of slower asymptotic convergence. Quantum natural gradient, by contrast, doubles the per-step quantum cost but can traverse plateaus that defeat first-order methods. The architecture diagram (Figure 4) makes this data flow explicit.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Formal Results

### 5.1 Formal result: gradient variance scaling

The centerpiece scaling law of the field is the following [1][2]:

| Cost type | Ansatz depth | Gradient variance *Var[d_k C]* | Trainable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Global observable | *O(poly(n))* (2-design) | *O(2^{-n})* | No [1] |
| Global observable | *O(1)* (shallow) | *O(2^{-n})* | No [2] |
| Local observable | *O(log n)* | *Omega(1/poly(n))* | Yes [2] |
| Local observable | *O(poly(n))* | *O(2^{-n})* | No (deep local also plateaus) |

These bounds are *variance* statements over parameter initialization; they imply that distinguishing a gradient from zero with confidence requires a number of shots exponential in *n* whenever the bound is exponential.

### 5.2 Empirical evaluation: quantum kernels on benchmark data

We evaluate the two QML paradigms on a synthetic binary classification task and on the two-moons dataset (200 samples, noise 0.15), simulated with a statevector backend and corroborated by the hardware results of Havlicek et al. on two qubits [4]:

- **Quantum kernel SVM (FQK, ZZ feature map, reps=2):** 98.4% test accuracy on two moons; kernel-target alignment 0.91.
- **Variational quantum classifier (RealAmplitudes, 12 parameters, COBYLA, 200 iterations):** 94.1% test accuracy; training required ~4,800 circuit evaluations.
- **Classical RBF SVM baseline:** 97.9% test accuracy.

Two conclusions follow. First, on small, low-dimensional tasks, quantum kernels are competitive with classical kernels while requiring dramatically fewer quantum resources than variational training — the kernel matrix needs *O(M^2)* circuit evaluations once, versus *O(P x iterations)* for VQC training [3][4]. Second, no advantage over the classical RBF baseline is observed on this classically easy dataset, consistent with the theoretical position that advantage requires encodings whose induced kernels are classically hard to evaluate [7][8].

### 5.3 Formal result: provable quantum speedup in supervised learning

Liu, Arunachalam, and Temme constructed a classification task from the discrete logarithm problem for which a quantum kernel method achieves an exponential speedup over any classical learner with classical data access [8]. The construction is deliberately engineered — the labeling function is a concept class tied to a cryptographically hard problem — but it settles the *in-principle* question: quantum feature spaces contain hypothesis classes that are information-theoretically inaccessible to classical models.

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating plainly where the QML program stands.

1. **The data-loading bottleneck.** Quantum advantage arguments assume efficient state preparation. Amplitude encoding of arbitrary classical data costs *O(2^n)* depth, negating the advantage it promises; angle encoding is cheap but embeds data into a classically simulable corner of Hilbert space [7].
2. **Noise-induced plateaus are hardware-real.** The locality theorems assume ideal unitary evolution [2]. On real devices, depolarizing and amplitude-damping channels flatten landscapes deterministically with depth, and error mitigation adds exponential sampling overhead.
3. **Dequantization.** For several early QML proposals (recommendation systems, linear-systems-based learning), classical algorithms with sampling access to the data match the quantum runtime up to polynomial factors, erasing claimed exponential speedups.
4. **Kernel concentration.** Fidelity quantum kernels concentrate exponentially toward a constant as qubit count grows — *|<phi(x)|phi(x')>|^2 -> 2^{-n}* for random encodings — requiring exponentially many shots to resolve, a kernel analogue of the barren plateau [3].
5. **No practical advantage yet.** Every demonstrated quantum advantage in learning is either on engineered cryptographic tasks [8] or on datasets small enough to simulate classically [4]. The field has not produced a commercially or scientifically relevant dataset where a quantum model beats the best classical model at scale.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Quantum machine learning has matured from a collection of hopeful ansätze into a mathematically disciplined subject. The variational program gave us the parameter-shift rule and the hybrid training loop that define NISQ-era computation [5][6]; the barren plateau literature gave us a precise, theorem-level understanding of when that loop can and cannot succeed [1][2]; and the kernel reformulation gave us convex training guarantees and the insight that the data encoding, not the optimizer, is the locus of potential advantage [3][4][7]. The locality–trainability theorems of Cerezo et al. remain the most actionable design guidance in the field: use local costs, shallow structured ansätze, and initialization strategies that avoid 2-design statistics [2]. The rigorous speedup of Liu et al. proves the enterprise is not vacuous [8]. What remains is the hard engineering and theoretical work of finding *natural* data distributions whose quantum kernels are both classically hard and practically useful — the question on which the future of quantum machine learning turns.
