# Two-Phase Responsiveness and DAG-Based Dissemination: From HotStuff-2's Optimal View Regime to Narwhal–Bullshark Consensus in Byzantine State Machine Replication

## Abstract

This thesis examines two consequential developments in Byzantine Fault Tolerant (BFT) state machine replication: the *phase reduction* pioneered by HotStuff-2 and the *dissemination–ordering separation* of the Narwhal mempool with Tusk and Bullshark consensus. HotStuff-2 [3] proves that a two-phase, optimistically linear, optimistically responsive protocol with optimal quadratic worst-case communication is achievable in partial synchrony — *two phases are enough*. Jolteon and Ditto [4] attack the remaining weak point, view synchronization under asynchrony, replacing it with a network-adaptive fallback that guarantees progress with probability one at no extra asymptotic cost. Orthogonally, Narwhal [5] reframes consensus as reliable dissemination of causal histories through a round-based directed acyclic graph (DAG), reducing ordering to a zero-message local *interpretation*; Bullshark [6] adds a two-round partially synchronous fast path atop the same substrate. Through unified formal analysis, simulation, and synthesis of published wide-area measurements, we show the modern design space is a single continuum parameterized by phases per decision, view-change complexity, and dissemination locus, and we derive concrete guidance for protocol selection under production constraints.

## 1. Introduction

Byzantine Fault Tolerant state machine replication asks how *n* replicas, up to *f* of them arbitrarily malicious, can agree on a single growing log despite an adversarial network [8]. The classical answer, PBFT [1], established a template — three phases, quadratic all-to-all exchanges, an intricate view-change — and with it a persistent belief: one must choose between *efficiency* on the happy path and *robustness* when the network or leader misbehaves.

Recent work has dismantled this belief from two directions. The first is *phase reduction within the leader-based paradigm*. HotStuff [2] replaced PBFT's heterogeneous phases with a uniform propose–vote pattern, threshold-signature quorum certificates (QCs), and a three-chain commit rule, achieving linear view change and optimistic responsiveness — progress at the actual network speed *δ* rather than the bound *Δ* [8]. HotStuff-2 [3] then showed the third phase was not load-bearing: a *two-phase* view regime built around a *double certificate* (a certificate over a certificate) simultaneously achieves optimal *O(n²)* worst-case communication, optimistically linear communication, and optimistic responsiveness.

The second direction is *architectural*. Jolteon and Ditto [4] attack timer-driven view synchronization, the fragility of partially synchronous protocols under asynchrony; Ditto falls back to an asynchronous mode instead of performing view changes, guaranteeing progress with probability one. Narwhal [5] goes further, separating the mempool from consensus entirely: validators build a DAG of causally-ordered, reliably-broadcast batches, and consensus becomes *zero-message-overhead* local interpretation of that DAG [7]. Tusk instantiates asynchronous consensus on this substrate; Bullshark adds a two-round fast path exploiting synchronous periods [6].

This thesis contributes a **unified formal framework** for these protocol families with quorum-theoretic safety proofs; a **comparative methodology** parameterizing protocols by phases-per-decision, view-change complexity, and dissemination locus; an **empirical synthesis** of published wide-area measurements; and a **decision calculus** for practitioners choosing between streamlined two-phase protocols and DAG-based architectures.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The classical model

We consider *n = 3f + 1* replicas with at most *f* Byzantine, and a *quorum* of *n − f = 2f + 1*. The network is *partially synchronous* [8]: after an unknown Global Stabilization Time (GST), delays are bounded by *Δ*. Safety must hold always; liveness after GST.

> **Theorem (Quorum Intersection):** Any two quorums of size *2f + 1* in a *3f + 1* system intersect in at least *f + 1* replicas — hence at least one *honest* replica.

*Proof.* |Q₁ ∩ Q₂| = |Q₁| + |Q₂| − |Q₁ ∪ Q₂| ≥ (2f+1) + (2f+1) − (3f+1) = f + 1; at most *f* replicas are faulty. ∎

This single combinatorial fact is the load-bearing wall of every protocol in this thesis: quorum-formed certificates *cannot* contradict each other without an honest replica witnessing it.

### 2.2 From PBFT to HotStuff

PBFT [1] uses three phases — *pre-prepare*, *prepare*, *commit* — each all-to-all, giving *O(n²)* cost per decision and a heavy view-change. HotStuff [2] made three decisive simplifications:

1. **Uniform propose–vote phases.** Every phase is identical: leader proposes, replicas vote, leader aggregates *2f + 1* votes into a constant-size threshold-signature *quorum certificate* (QC).
2. **Chained pipelining.** Blocks in consecutive views form a chain; a QC doubles as vote-aggregation for one phase and proposal-carrier for the next. The **three-chain commit rule** commits *B* when three QCs form a contiguous chain, the first two links in consecutive views.
3. **Linear view change.** A new leader needs only the highest QC observed — *O(n)* instead of *O(n²)*.

HotStuff also introduced **optimistic responsiveness** (decisions at actual delay *δ*, not bound *Δ*) and a linear-overhead *Pacemaker* for view synchronization. Its price was an extra phase per view — three voting phases (prepare, pre-commit, commit) plus decide. The community long believed this phase was the cost of combining responsiveness with linear view change. HotStuff-2 proved otherwise.

### 2.3 Timing models and the robustness gap

HotStuff's liveness machinery — timeout-driven view synchronization — assumes eventual synchrony. Under sustained asynchrony or targeted denial-of-service against leaders, partially synchronous protocols stall, while fully asynchronous protocols historically paid quadratic communication *even on the happy path*. Jolteon/Ditto [4] and Narwhal/Tusk/Bullshark [5,6] attack this robustness–efficiency trade-off — the former within the leader-based paradigm, the latter by changing the paradigm.

## 3. Methodology

**Track A — Formal modeling.** We model each protocol as a transition system over *(view, lockedQC, preparedBlock)* and prove safety via one invariant schema: *no two conflicting blocks can both obtain certificates at or above any honest replica's lock*. For HotStuff-2 we formalize the double-certificate rule; for Tusk/Bullshark, commit rules as predicates over a replica's local DAG view, following [7].

**Track B — Simulation.** A discrete-event simulator models *n ∈ {4, 10, 31, 100}* replicas with configurable delays, crash and equivocation faults, measuring commit latency in round-trips and messages per decision under identical conditions.

**Track C — Empirical synthesis.** We aggregate published WAN measurements from [2,3,4,5,6], normalized to 512-byte transactions, cross-checking internal consistency and treating vendor figures as upper bounds.

The Python sketch below implements the certificate logic shared by the HotStuff lineage:

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass, field

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class QC:
    view: int
    block_hash: str
    signers: frozenset  # threshold-signature shares

@dataclass
class Replica:
    rid: int
    n: int; f: int
    locked_qc: QC | None = None
    pending_votes: dict = field(default_factory=dict)

    def vote(self, block_hash: str, qc: QC | None, view: int) -> bool:
        """HotStuff-2 safe-node predicate: vote iff the proposal extends
        the locked QC, or carries a QC strictly newer than the lock."""
        if self.locked_qc is not None and qc is not None:
            if not (extends(block_hash, self.locked_qc.block_hash)
                    or qc.view > self.locked_qc.view):
                return False
        return True  # emit signature share to leader

    def on_prepare_qc(self, qc: QC):
        """Lock on the prepare certificate; a second certificate over
        this QC (the commit certificate) authorizes commit."""
        if self.locked_qc is None or qc.view > self.locked_qc.view:
            self.locked_qc = qc
```

The Haskell fragment expresses the three-chain versus two-chain commit rules as pure predicates, showing exactly what HotStuff-2 eliminates:

```haskell
data CertBlock = CB { cbView :: Int, cbHash :: Hash, cbQC :: QC }

-- HotStuff (v1): three-chain commit rule.
committedHS1 :: [CertBlock] -> Maybe Hash
committedHS1 (b1:b2:b3:_)
  | extends b2 b1 && extends b3 b2
  , cbView b2 == cbView b1 + 1   -- first two links consecutive
  = Just (cbHash b1)
committedHS1 _ = Nothing

-- HotStuff-2 / Jolteon: two-chain (double-certificate) commit rule.
committedHS2 :: [CertBlock] -> Maybe Hash
committedHS2 (b1:b2:_)
  | extends b2 b1                -- QC-of-QC: commit cert over prepare cert
  = Just (cbHash b1)
committedHS2 _ = Nothing
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 HotStuff-2: two phases are enough

HotStuff's third voting phase exists for liveness, not safety. Safety rests on one invariant: *if an honest replica locks on QC(B) at view v, no conflicting block obtains a QC at view ≥ v*. The *pre-commit* phase let replicas vote immediately (preserving responsiveness) while ensuring a view change surfaces the highest locked QC. Malkhi and Nayak [3] showed two phases suffice by restructuring what the second phase certifies:

1. **Propose.** Leader *L_v* broadcasts a block extending its highest QC.
2. **Vote (phase 1).** Replicas check the safe-node predicate and return shares; *L_v* aggregates *2f + 1* into a *prepare certificate* *C_v(B)* and broadcasts it.
3. **Vote (phase 2).** Replicas receiving *C_v(B)* *lock* on it and return shares to the *next* leader *L_{v+1}*, which forms the *commit certificate* *C_v(C_v(B))* — a **certificate over a certificate** — and disseminates it. Replicas observing it commit *B*.

> **Theorem (HotStuff-2 Safety):** If an honest replica commits *B* at view *v*, no conflicting *B′ ≠ B* obtains a prepare certificate at any view *v′ ≥ v*.

*Proof sketch.* Committing needs a commit certificate: *2f + 1* replicas locked on *C_v(B)*. By quorum intersection, any prepare QC at *v′ ≥ v* shares an honest replica *h* with that quorum; *h*'s predicate only votes for proposals extending its lock (or carrying a strictly newer QC, which inductively extends *B*). No conflicting *B′* gathers *2f + 1* votes. ∎

| Property | HotStuff | HotStuff-2 |
|---|---|---|
| Phases per view | 3 | **2** (+½ for commit-cert dissemination) |
| Optimistic communication | *O(n)* | *O(n)* |
| Worst-case communication | *O(n²)* | *O(n²)* (optimal) |
| Optimistic responsiveness | Yes | Yes |
| View-change complexity | Linear | Linear |

The "two-and-a-half phases" accounting reflects that the second phase's QC is formed by the *next* leader; steady-state cost is two linear rounds per decision — optimal for responsive BFT, which needs at least propose and vote rounds.

### 4.2 Jolteon and Ditto: network-adaptive consensus

Jolteon [4], built for Diem and deployed at Aptos [9], independently discovered the two-chain idea with a different trade: it keeps **quadratic view-change** communication in exchange for shedding a full phase of commit latency. Reported measurements show Jolteon's commit latency beating HotStuff by **200–300 ms** across system sizes — significant for sub-second-finality payment networks.

Ditto targets the deeper failure mode: *adversarial asynchrony*. Partially synchronous protocols rely on doubling timeouts across failed views, which an adversary exploits to stall progress indefinitely. Ditto's insight is that robustness need only be paid for when it is needed:

- **Happy path:** run Jolteon (linear, two-chain commits).
- **Fallback trigger:** on stalled views, replicas switch to a randomized *asynchronous mode* — leaderless agreement — instead of timer-driven view changes.
- **Recovery:** once the fallback decides, replicas re-synchronize onto the linear happy path.

> **Theorem (Ditto Liveness, informal [4]):** Ditto guarantees progress with probability one under full asynchrony, while matching Jolteon's linear communication whenever the network is synchronous and the leader honest.

This breaks the classical trade-off: earlier robust protocols paid quadratic cost even when nothing was wrong. Ditto instantiates "pay for what you use" — a principle the DAG designs push further.

### 4.3 Narwhal: separating dissemination from ordering

Leader-based protocols couple two distinct tasks: **disseminating** transaction data and **ordering** it. The leader becomes a bandwidth bottleneck, and view changes interrupt data flow on healthy networks. Narwhal [5] severs the coupling:

1. **The mempool as a DAG.** Validators advance through numbered *rounds*; each validator reliably broadcasts a *vertex* per round containing transaction batches and references to ≥ *2f + 1* prior-round vertices. *2f + 1* acknowledgments *certify* a vertex. The DAG encodes the causal history of all disseminated data.
2. **Validity and availability.** Every certified vertex references a quorum of prior vertices, so honest validators' DAGs converge on identical causal histories — a consistent broadcast medium progressing under *full asynchrony*.
3. **Scale-out workers.** Batching is sharded across worker processes per validator, so throughput scales linearly with provisioned bandwidth, independent of consensus.

```rust
/// A certified Narwhal vertex: transaction batches plus causal
/// references to the previous round. Certificates are (2f+1)-signed.
pub struct Vertex {
    pub author: ValidatorId,
    pub round: u64,
    pub batch_digests: Vec<Digest>,      // disseminated by workers
    pub parents: Vec<CertificateDigest>, // >= 2f+1 of round - 1
}

pub struct Certificate {
    pub vertex: Vertex,
    pub signatures: AggregateSignature,  // BLS quorum aggregate
}

impl Certificate {
    /// Valid iff 2f+1 distinct valid signatures cover the vertex
    /// digest — verified once; the DAG edge is then trusted forever.
    pub fn is_valid(&self, committee: &Committee) -> bool {
        self.signatures.verify_quorum(&self.vertex.digest(), committee)
    }
}
```

Critically, *no consensus happens inside Narwhal*. Consensus is what validators *do with* the DAG.

### 4.4 Tusk and Bullshark: consensus as interpretation

**Tusk** [5] is the purest DAG protocol: *asynchronous* consensus with **zero message overhead** beyond DAG construction. Rounds are grouped into *waves* of three; a shared coin elects a *leader vertex* per wave, and each validator locally commits the leader if ≥ *2f + 1* second-round vertices reference it, then recursively commits its causal history in deterministic order. Honest validators eventually see the same DAG, so they compute identical commits — agreement by *interpretation*, not communication.

**Bullshark** [6] adds a **partially synchronous fast path**: each round designates *two* leaders — a pre-determined *steady-state* leader and a randomly elected *fallback*. In synchronous periods the steady-state leader's vertex commits after just **two DAG rounds**; under asynchrony the protocol degrades to the DAG-Rider-style fallback, preserving liveness with probability one. The fast path adds *no messages* — a second interpretation rule over the same DAG.

> **Theorem (Bullshark fast-path latency [6]):** During synchrony with an honest steady-state leader, Bullshark commits within two DAG rounds of dissemination, with zero consensus messages beyond Narwhal certificate traffic.

The implementation is famously minimal — ~**200 lines** atop Narwhal [6] — because the DAG absorbs reliable broadcast, view change, and quorum bookkeeping. The deep lesson: *most of what BFT protocols communicate about is data availability; once availability is factored out, ordering is nearly free*.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Formal verification status

The safety arguments above follow the quorum-intersection schema mechanically; HotStuff-family protocols have been partially machine-checked (TLA+, Agda/Coq), and Tusk/Bullshark commit rules admit clean formalizations as pure functions over finite DAG prefixes. Our simulator reproduces the predicted round complexities exactly: HotStuff commits in 4 message delays (pipelined), HotStuff-2 and Jolteon in 3, Bullshark's fast path in 2 DAG rounds.

### 5.2 Published wide-area measurements

The table synthesizes WAN results from [4,5,6] for 512-byte transactions (as-reported upper bounds):

| System | Throughput (tx/s) | Latency | Committee | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline HotStuff | ~1,800 | ~1 s | 20 | Traditional mempool |
| Narwhal–HotStuff | ~170,000 | ~2.5 s | 20 | Same consensus, DAG mempool |
| Narwhal–HotStuff (scaled) | ~600,000 | ~2.5 s | 20 | Extra workers, linear scale-out |
| Tusk | ~140,000 | ~4 s | 20 | Async, zero consensus overhead |
| Bullshark | ~125,000 | ~2 s | 50 | Partially-sync fast path |
| Jolteon vs HotStuff | — | −200 to −300 ms | 4–100 | Two-chain latency saving |

Three conclusions stand out. **First**, swapping only the mempool (Narwhal–HotStuff vs. baseline) improves throughput ~**100×** — dissemination, not ordering, was the binding constraint. **Second**, Tusk achieves ~20× the throughput of prior asynchronous protocols at comparable latency, validating zero-overhead interpretation. **Third**, Bullshark's fast path recovers the latency that asynchrony-robustness costs: 125k tx/s at 2 s latency across 50 nodes, ~50% lower latency than async-optimized competitors.

### 5.3 Asymptotic comparison

|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT [1] | *O(n²)* | *O(n²)* | Partial sync | 3 |

*Narwhal's per-round certificate traffic is O(n²) but carries data amortized over large batches — the relevant metric is bytes per transaction, which shrinks as batches grow, unlike per-decision control overhead in leader-based protocols.*

## 6. Limitations

**Adversarial throughput degradation.** Narwhal's headline figures assume honest validators fill batches; Byzantine validators may emit empty (but valid) vertices, and while Bullshark's fairness bounds the damage [6,7], effective throughput under a maximally adversarial *f* falls well short of honest-case numbers.

**Latency floor of DAG waves.** Tusk's three-round waves and Bullshark's two-round fast path impose a structural floor: decisions need multiple DAG rounds of quorum references regardless of network speed. For sub-500 ms finality (payments, trading), streamlined two-phase protocols committing in three message delays remain superior — the DAG line trades latency for throughput and robustness.

**Conserved complexity.** Bullshark's "200 lines" counts only the interpretation layer; beneath it lies Narwhal's reliable broadcast, certificate management, round garbage collection, and worker coordination — a large, subtle system with correspondingly higher operational burden than a monolithic HotStuff-2 deployment.

**Cryptographic assumptions.** All protocols here depend on threshold/aggregate (typically BLS) signatures and, for Tusk/Bullshark, a shared random coin via distributed key generation. Post-quantum safety claims [6] assume primitive replacement not yet standard practice.

**Evaluation gaps.** Published figures come from authors' own testbeds; independent 100+ node WAN reproduction is sparse, and Ditto's fallback behavior under real DDoS conditions deserves third-party measurement.

## 7. Conclusion

A decade of BFT research reads as the progressive elimination of unnecessary work. HotStuff removed PBFT's phase heterogeneity and quadratic view changes. HotStuff-2 removed the third phase, proving two-phase responsive BFT with optimal worst-case communication is not just possible but *simple* — dispelling the belief that the desirable properties could not coexist [3]. Jolteon and Ditto removed the robustness penalty, making asynchrony-tolerance free on the happy path [4]. Narwhal, Tusk, and Bullshark removed the deepest coupling of all — between moving data and agreeing on order — reducing consensus to local interpretation of a shared causal DAG [5,6,7].

The practical upshot is a clean decision rule. When **latency dominates** under reasonable synchrony — payments, settlement — deploy the HotStuff-2/Jolteon lineage: three message delays to finality, linear communication, minimal code. When **throughput or adversarial robustness dominates** — high-volume ledgers, hostile networks — deploy the DAG stack: Narwhal dissemination with Bullshark interpretation, accepting a multi-round latency floor for two orders of magnitude more throughput and liveness no network adversary can revoke.

Open problems remain. Can the DAG line recover sub-second latency without reintroducing a leader bottleneck? Can Ditto-style adaptive fallback compose with DAG interpretation for the best of all three worlds? Can production DAG-BFT complexity be tamed to HotStuff-2's conceptual economy? The field's trajectory suggests these, too, will yield to the strategy behind every advance surveyed here: find work the protocol need not do, and stop doing it.
