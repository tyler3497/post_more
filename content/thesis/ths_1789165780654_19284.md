# WebAuthn and the FIDO2 Ceremony: Attestation, CTAP2 Authenticator Protocols, and Phishing-Resistant Authentication at Internet Scale

## Abstract

Passwords and phishable multi-factor authentication remain the dominant vector for account takeover: credential theft succeeds because secrets are either replayable or can be socially engineered from the user. This thesis presents a rigorous treatment of **WebAuthn** (W3C Web Authentication) and the **FIDO2** protocol suite as a principled, phishing-resistant replacement for passwords. We formalize the two core *ceremonies* — registration and authentication — as origin-bound challenge–response protocols, dissect the **CTAP2** (Client-to-Authenticator Protocol) command set (`authenticatorMakeCredential`, `authenticatorGetAssertion`, `authenticatorGetInfo`), and analyze attestation formats (`packed`, `tpm`, `android-key`, `apple`, `fido-u2f`, `none`) as a trust-establishment mechanism. We survey discoverable credentials ("passkeys"), WebAuthn Level 3 extensions (PRF, `largeBlob`, related origins, conditional mediation), and the cryptographic core (ECDSA P-256, EdDSA, signature counters, token binding semantics). Empirical evidence — Google's zero successful phishings after security-key rollout, 1+ billion passkey authentications across 400+ million Google accounts, Microsoft's 3×/8× speedup data — demonstrates that origin-bound public-key authentication converts phishing from a probabilistic human-factors problem into a cryptographic impossibility. We close with remaining attack surface: local adversaries, authenticator cloning, account recovery, and the sync-vs-device-bound credential tension.

---

## 1. Introduction

Authentication on the web has been in a decades-long stalemate. Passwords are *reusable*, *replayable*, and *phishable*: a user who enters credentials into `examp1e.com` has no reliable defense, because nothing in the password protocol binds the secret to the identity of the party receiving it. Multi-factor authentication (MFA) raised the bar but did not change the game. One-time codes delivered over SMS or TOTP are phishable in real time — an adversary relaying a phishing page to the legitimate site simply forwards the code, and push-approval MFA degrades into "MFA fatigue," where conditioned users approve attacker-initiated prompts [1]. The security community thus faced a precise technical requirement: design an authentication primitive in which the credential is **cryptographically bound to the relying party's origin**, so that a signature produced for `attacker.com` is mathematically useless at `victim.com`.

The answer emerged from the FIDO (Fast IDentity Online) Alliance's **U2F** protocol and was generalized into **FIDO2**, whose two components are the **CTAP2** protocol — the wire protocol between client and authenticator — and the **WebAuthn** web API — the JavaScript/browser interface that web applications use to invoke authenticators [3][5]. Together they implement *public-key credential* ceremonies: the authenticator generates a key pair per relying party (RP), returns only the public key, and later signs RP-supplied challenges with the private key after verifying user presence or verification. Because the signed payload includes the RP's origin, a phishing site cannot relay the assertion to the real RP. This origin binding is the load-bearing idea of the entire edifice.

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the lineage from U2F to WebAuthn Level 2 and Level 3, and the terminology (relying party, authenticator, client, ceremonies). Section 3 describes our methodology: protocol-level analysis of the specifications, cryptographic verification of the ceremonies' binding properties, and synthesis of deployment data and formal studies. Section 4 dives into registration (§4.1), authentication (§4.2), CTAP2 (§4.3), attestation and the trust model (§4.4), and passkeys, Level 3, and deployment (§4.5). Section 5 consolidates empirical results and theoretical guarantees; Section 6 enumerates limitations and residual risk; Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From U2F to FIDO2

In 2012, Google and Yubico began work on Universal 2nd Factor (U2F), standardized in the FIDO Alliance in 2014: a hardware token that performs an ECDSA signature over a challenge bound to the site's origin (via the "appId") and to TLS channel parameters [4]. U2F's limitations were structural: it required a password alongside the token (so password leaks remained dangerous), and it performed no *user authentication* — possession of the token sufficed, with no PIN or biometric gate. FIDO2, developed from 2015, addressed both: passwordless operation and user verification (UV) integrated into the authenticator.

**WebAuthn Level 1** became a W3C Recommendation in March 2019; **Level 2** followed on 8 April 2021 [5]. **Level 3** progressed through Candidate Recommendation snapshots in 2025–2026 and was proposed for advancement to full Recommendation in July 2026 [6], expanding capability — key derivation, related origins, conditional mediation — while Level 2 had already frozen the phishing-resistance core [7].

### 2.2 The actors

The protocol involves three parties:

- **Relying Party (RP):** the web application (e.g., `example.com`), consisting of a front-end JavaScript component and a server that generates challenges, stores public keys and credential IDs, and verifies assertions.
- **Client:** typically the web browser (the "user agent"), which mediates access, enforces origin policy, computes the `clientDataJSON` hash, and relays CBOR-encoded commands to the authenticator.
- **Authenticator:** the device holding private keys — a roaming hardware security key (USB/NFC/BLE) or a platform authenticator (Windows Hello, Touch ID, Android device lock).

A **public key credential** comprises a credential ID, an RP ID, a key pair, and metadata (sign counter, user handle), and may be *non-discoverable* (opaque credential ID stored server-side) or *discoverable* (resident keys; the marketing term is **passkeys**).

> **Theorem (informal, origin binding):** Let an authenticator sign the tuple `(rpId, clientDataHash, ...)` where `clientDataHash = SHA-256(clientDataJSON)` and `clientDataJSON` includes the origin `O` asserted by the trusted client. Then a signature obtained by a phishing site at origin `O' ≠ O` cannot authenticate at `O`, because verification at `O` recomputes the expected hash with origin `O`, which will not match.

The theorem's soundness depends on the client being trustworthy — the client supplies `rpIdHash = SHA-256(rpId)` to the authenticator and reports the true origin. Browser-extension and XSS adversaries are therefore the interesting residual class [2].

### 2.3 Cryptographic primitives

FIDO2 credentials use **ECDSA over P-256 (secp256r1)** with SHA-256 as the dominant algorithm (`alg: -7`, COSE); **EdDSA over Ed25519** (`alg: -8`) is supported by newer authenticators. Signatures are computed over the concatenation `authenticatorData || SHA-256(clientDataJSON)`. The authenticator data includes a flags byte (UP: user presence, UV: user verification, BE/BS: backup eligibility/state, AT: attested credential data present, ED: extension data present) and a monotonically increasing **signature counter** used for clone detection.

| Property | Mechanism | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Origin binding | `clientDataJSON.origin`, hashed into signature | Phishing resistance |
| Replay resistance | RP-supplied random challenge (≥16 bytes recommended) | Freshness per ceremony |
| Clone detection | Monotonic `signCount` per credential | Detect duplicated private keys |
| Trust in device | Attestation statement + AAGUID | RP policy on authenticator model |
| User consent | UP bit (touch); UV bit (PIN/biometric) | Authorization evidence |

## 3. Methodology

This thesis combines three methods. First, **specification analysis**: we trace the normative algorithms in W3C WebAuthn Level 2 (REC 2021-04-08) [5] and the Level 3 draft [7], and the FIDO CTAP2 specification (v2.0/v2.1) [3], reconstructing each ceremony as an explicit message sequence and verifying the binding claims step by step. Second, **cryptographic verification**: we check that the signed payload in each ceremony contains all security-relevant context (origin, challenge, RP ID, token binding ID where applicable), and we construct the adversarial argument showing what a relay attacker would need to break. Third, **empirical and literature synthesis**: we integrate deployment results (Google's internal rollout [4][8], Google's public passkey statistics [9], Microsoft's authentication telemetry [10]), usability studies (Farke et al.'s small-company FIDO2 deployment [11]), and formal/adversarial analyses (the NDSS 2024 systematization of local attacks against FIDO2 [2], Barbosa et al.-style ceremony analyses, and the FIDO2 position paper [1]).

Where specifications leave behavior to implementers (e.g., counter strategies, enterprise attestation privacy), we note the variance explicitly. All factual claims about deployment scale are drawn from vendor announcements and cited accordingly.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The registration ceremony

Registration creates a new public key credential bound to an RP and (optionally) a user account. The RP generates a random challenge and `PublicKeyCredentialCreationOptions` — `rp` (id, name), `user` (id, name, displayName), `pubKeyCredParams` (acceptable algorithms), `excludeCredentials` (to prevent duplicate registration), `authenticatorSelection` (resident key requirement, UV policy, authenticator attachment), `attestation` preference, and timeout. The browser calls `navigator.credentials.create({publicKey})`, then:

1. The **client** builds `clientDataJSON` = `{type: "webauthn.create", challenge, origin, ...}`, hashes it to `clientDataHash`, and issues CTAP2 `authenticatorMakeCredential` (0x01) with the hash, rp entity, user entity, and options.
2. The **authenticator** checks `excludeList`, obtains user consent (and UV if required), generates a fresh key pair, constructs **attested credential data** (AAGUID, credential ID, COSE public key), and produces an **attestation statement** signing `authenticatorData || clientDataHash` with its attestation private key.
3. The client returns the `AuthenticatorAttestationResponse` (attestation object + clientDataJSON) to the RP server.
4. The **RP verifies**: challenge matches and is fresh; origin matches its own; `rpIdHash` matches SHA-256 of its RP ID; the attestation signature verifies against a trusted attestation root; the credential public key is stored alongside the credential ID and sign counter.

The subtle design point is that *attestation* and *authentication* keys are distinct: attestation keys are batch-shared across many devices (to preserve privacy), while the credential key pair is unique per (authenticator, RP, account). Privacy is thus engineered into the trust model, not bolted on.

### 4.2 The authentication ceremony

Authentication produces an *assertion*: proof of possession of the private key for a previously registered credential, bound to a fresh challenge.

1. The RP sends `PublicKeyCredentialRequestOptions` — random challenge, `rpId`, `allowCredentials` (credential ID list; empty for discoverable-credential flows), `userVerification` policy, timeout.
2. The client builds `clientDataJSON` with `type: "webauthn.get"`, hashes it, and issues CTAP2 `authenticatorGetAssertion` (0x02), which locates the credential (by allow list or resident-key scan), requires user presence/verification, increments the sign counter, and signs `authenticatorData || clientDataHash`.
3. The RP verifies: origin, challenge freshness, `rpIdHash`, signature over the reconstructed payload with the stored public key, UV/UP flags against policy, and the sign counter strictly increasing (clone detection).

```python
# RP-side assertion verification (simplified, per WebAuthn §7.2)
import hashlib
from ecdsa import VerifyingKey, NIST256p, util

def verify_assertion(auth_data, client_data_json, signature, stored_pubkey,
                     expected_rp_id, expected_challenge, expected_origin):
    # 1. Recompute the signed payload exactly as the authenticator did
    client_hash = hashlib.sha256(client_data_json).digest()
    signed = auth_data + client_hash
    # 2. Check RP ID binding
    assert auth_data[0:32] == hashlib.sha256(expected_rp_id.encode()).digest()
    # 3. Check origin binding inside clientDataJSON (parsed elsewhere)
    assert parse_origin(client_data_json) == expected_origin
    # 4. Verify ECDSA signature with the registered public key
    vk = VerifyingKey.from_string(stored_pubkey, curve=NIST256p)
    assert vk.verify(signature, signed, hashfunc=hashlib.sha256,
                    sigdecode=util.sigdecode_der)
    # 5. Flags and counter
    flags = auth_data[32]
    assert flags & 0x01  # UP: user present
    return True
```

The critical property: the signature covers both the challenge (freshness) and the origin (anti-phishing). An attacker relaying the ceremony in real time — the classic attack that defeats TOTP and push MFA — receives a signature over *their own* origin, which the RP rejects [1].

### 4.3 CTAP2: the client-to-authenticator protocol

CTAP2 is the lower layer that WebAuthn clients speak to authenticators, transported over CTAPHID (USB), BLE, or NFC, with messages encoded in **canonical CBOR**. The command set (command byte in parentheses):

| Command | Code | Role |
|---|---|---|
| `authenticatorMakeCredential` | 0x01 | Generate credential + attestation |
| `authenticatorGetAssertion` | 0x02 | Sign challenge for assertion |
| `authenticatorGetNextAssertion` | 0x03 | Iterate multiple matching credentials |
| `authenticatorGetInfo` | 0x04 | Capability discovery (versions, options, extensions, AAGUID) |
| `authenticatorClientPIN` | 0x06 | PIN set/change/verify, `pinToken` issuance via ECDH + AES-256-CBC |
| `authenticatorReset` | 0x07 | Factory reset (user-presence gated) |
| `authenticatorCredentialManagement` | 0x0A | Enumerate/delete resident credentials |

`authenticatorGetInfo` is the negotiation primitive: the client learns whether the authenticator supports CTAP 2.0 vs 2.1 (`FIDO_2_0`/`FIDO_2_1`), resident keys (`rk`), user verification (`uv`), client PIN, `credMgmt`, `largeBlobs`, and extensions (`hmac-secret`, `credProps`, `prf`). The PIN protocol itself is a small cryptographic ceremony: ECDH (P-256) key agreement establishes a shared secret, the PIN is verified as `LEFT(SHA-256(PIN), 16)` encrypted under AES-256-CBC, and a `pinToken` authorizes subsequent operations — with an 8-attempt retry counter providing brute-force protection [3].

> **Theorem (PIN brute-force bound):** With retry counter `R = 8` and a minimum PIN length of 4 digits, an adversary with physical possession has at most 8 guesses per authenticator lifetime-epoch before the device locks or resets; the residual success probability is bounded by `8 / 10^4 = 0.08%` for numeric PINs, independent of computational power.

CTAP 2.1 refined several behaviors, notably allowing `makeCredUvNotRqd` so non-discoverable credentials can be created without user verification — a deliberate usability/security trade-off for second-factor-style flows.

### 4.4 Attestation formats and the trust model

Attestation answers the RP's question: *what kind of authenticator created this credential?* The attestation object carries a `fmt` identifier, the attestation statement, and the authenticator data. The registered formats:

- **`packed`**: the workhorse; the authenticator signs with a batch attestation key (X.509 certificate chain) or with the credential key itself (self-attestation).
- **`tpm`**: for TPM-backed platform authenticators; the statement includes TPM attestation structures.
- **`android-key`**: Android Keystore attestation.
- **`android-safetynet`**: deprecated in Level 3 (SafetyNet retired in favor of Play Integrity).
- **`fido-u2f`**: backwards compatibility with U2F authenticators.
- **`apple`**: Apple's anonymous attestation for platform authenticators.
- **`none`**: the RP requested no attestation (privacy-preserving default; the AAGUID is zeroed in Level 2, preserved in Level 3 [7]).
- **`enterprise`**: uniquely identifying attestation, only for enterprise-managed deployments where the RP is explicitly trusted.

The trust model is deliberately *reputational*: the RP maintains a metadata service (FIDO MDS) mapping AAGUIDs to authenticator security properties, and sets policy ("accept any packed attestation with a valid chain," or "require certified L2 authenticators"). Privacy is protected because attestation certificates are shared across batches of ≥100,000 devices — individual devices are not uniquely identifiable, except under `enterprise` attestation where the deployer has consented to identifiability.

### 4.5 Discoverable credentials, passkeys, and Level 3

**Discoverable credentials** (resident keys) store the user handle and credential on the authenticator, enabling *usernameless* flows: the RP sends an empty allow list, the authenticator enumerates its credentials for the RP ID, the user selects an account, and the assertion carries the user handle back. **Passkeys** are the industry term for discoverable credentials that are *synced* across a user's devices via a credential provider (iCloud Keychain, Google Password Manager) — trading the "key never leaves hardware" property for recovery and multi-device usability. The authenticator data flags `BE` (backup eligible) and `BS` (backup state) let the RP observe this: a credential with `BS=1` exists outside the original hardware, and high-security RPs may step up authentication accordingly.

WebAuthn **Level 3** adds the capabilities that turn passkeys from a sign-in primitive into a broader cryptographic identity layer:

1. **PRF extension** (`prf`): derive symmetric keys from the credential, enabling end-to-end encryption use cases (e.g., encrypting vaults with a key that only the authenticator can reproduce).
2. **Related origins**: a `.well-known/webauthn` mechanism allowing one credential to serve a brand's family of domains — carefully scoped to preserve the origin-binding guarantee.
3. **Conditional mediation**: `mediation: "conditional"` integrates credential *creation* and *request* into the browser's autofill UI, removing the modal-dialog friction that hurt enrollment.
4. **Signal methods** (`signalUnknownCredential`, `signalAllAcceptedCredentials`, `signalCurrentUserDetails`): keep the provider's credential list synchronized with the RP's server state, fixing the "deleted account still offers a passkey" desynchronization.
5. **`getClientCapabilities()`** and JSON serialization methods: cleaner feature detection and server-driven flows.

**Deployment at scale** validates the design. Google reported zero successful phishing attacks against employees after mandating U2F security keys [4]. By May 2024, passkeys had authenticated users over **1 billion times across 400+ million Google accounts**, exceeding legacy 2FA methods combined, with sign-in 50% faster than passwords [9]. Amazon created 175 million passkeys; Google later enabled them for over 800 million accounts; Microsoft measured passkey sign-in as **3× faster than passwords and 8× faster than password+MFA** [10].

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

We consolidate the evidence along two axes: what the cryptography *guarantees* and what deployments *measured*.

**Theoretical guarantees.** (i) *Phishing resistance*: under the assumption of a trustworthy client, no adversary controlling only a lookalike origin can obtain an assertion valid at the target RP, because the signed origin is fixed by the client at ceremony time. This defeats real-time relay attacks that bypass TOTP/push MFA [1]. (ii) *No server-side secrets*: RPs store public keys only; a database breach yields no authenticatable material — the analogue of password hashing, but without any brute-forceable secret. (iii) *Replay resistance*: challenges are single-use and random; signatures cannot be replayed across sessions. (iv) *Clone detection*: the monotonic counter bounds the window in which a cloned authenticator can operate undetected — the first counter regression flags the compromise.

**Empirical results.** Google's two-year Security Key deployment study measured reduced sign-in times and reduced support burden [4]; the subsequent passkey rollout's billion-authentication milestone with 50%-faster sign-in demonstrates usability *improving* alongside security — a rare combination [9]. Microsoft's telemetry (4,000+ password attacks per second in 2023, up 3,378% since 2015) quantifies the threat being neutralized [10]. Usability research tempers the optimism: Farke et al.'s field deployment found users still reaching for passwords out of habit, and the NDSS 2024 systematization demonstrated seven practical attacks from *local* adversaries (malicious extensions, XSS, physical access), showing that the ceremony's guarantees hold at the protocol layer while endpoints remain the battleground [2][11].

> **Theorem (composition):** Phishing resistance of the ceremony composes with, but does not substitute for, endpoint integrity. Formally, if the client is compromised, the adversary can substitute `origin` before hashing; all origin-binding guarantees are then void. WebAuthn therefore *reduces* phishing to endpoint compromise — a strictly harder problem for remote attackers — rather than eliminating it absolutely.

## 6. Limitations

1. **The trusted-client assumption.** The entire origin-binding argument assumes the browser truthfully reports the origin and the RP ID. Malicious browser extensions, XSS-injected scripts on the RP page, and compromised clients can subvert the ceremony [2]. Mitigations (extension permission models, CSP, site isolation) are outside WebAuthn's scope.
2. **Account recovery — the weakest link.** A phishing-resistant credential is useless if recovery falls back to email-based reset, which reintroduces phishability. RPs must design recovery (multiple registered authenticators, delayed recovery, identity proofing) with the same rigor as authentication.
3. **Sync vs. device-bound tension.** Synced passkeys (`BS=1`) move private key material through cloud providers; the security argument shifts from "hardware-bound" to "provider-account-bound." High-assurance RPs should inspect the `BE`/`BS` flags and apply step-up policy.
4. **Attestation privacy and policy complexity.** Meaningful attestation policy requires maintaining trust anchors (FIDO MDS), and strict policies can exclude legitimate authenticators. Most consumer RPs accept `none` attestation, forfeiting device assurance entirely.
5. **Ecosystem fragmentation.** Cross-ecosystem portability (moving a passkey from iCloud Keychain to Google Password Manager) historically required per-credential re-registration; credential exchange formats are still maturing.
6. **User mental models.** Users habituated to passwords misunderstand passkeys ("where is my password?"), and device loss without synced backup locks users out — the usability studies consistently flag enrollment and recovery as the friction points [11].
7. **Biometric/PIN verification is local.** UV proves presence to the *authenticator*, not liveness to the RP; the RP sees only a flag bit. A coerced or spoofed local biometric is outside the protocol's threat model.

## 7. Conclusion

WebAuthn and FIDO2 represent the first authentication architecture to make phishing *cryptographically* infeasible rather than merely *behaviorally* discouraged. The mechanism is conceptually small — sign the origin along with the challenge — but its consequences are large: relay attacks fail by construction, server breaches yield no secrets, and the ceremony composes cleanly with user verification for passwordless operation. CTAP2 provides the authenticator-side machinery (credential lifecycle, PIN protocols, capability negotiation), attestation provides the trust-establishment layer, and WebAuthn Level 3 extends the primitive toward key derivation, related origins, and provider synchronization.

The deployment record — zero Google employee phishings post-U2F, a billion passkey authentications, multi-vendor commitment across billions of devices — suggests the industry has crossed the adoption chasm. The remaining work is not cryptographic but systemic: recovery ceremonies as rigorous as authentication, honest signaling of sync state via `BE`/`BS`, endpoint integrity against local adversaries, and user education that matches the protocol's strength. Passwords persisted for sixty years because they were *deployable*; passkeys may finally displace them because they are deployable *and* phishing-resistant — the rare security technology that is both stronger and faster.
