# Reverse-Engineering the Residual Stream: Circuits, Sparse Autoencoders, and Causal Abstraction in Transformer Language Models

## Abstract

Mechanistic interpretability seeks to reverse-engineer transformer language models into human-comprehensible algorithms: circuits of attention heads and multilayer perceptron (MLP) blocks whose coordinated interactions implement the model's behavior.    This thesis synthesizes the three pillars of the research program: the transformer-circuits decomposition of the residual stream into query-key (QK) and output-value (OV) circuits [1], dictionary learning through sparse autoencoders (SAEs) that resolves *superposition* and *polysemanticity* [6][8], and the formal theory of causal abstraction that validates hypothesized mechanisms via interchange interventions [3]. We reconstruct how induction heads emerge from previous-token and copy motifs [9], how toy models of superposition explain feature packing in undercomplete bases [2], and how case studies — indirect object identification [4], grokking phase transitions [5], factual recall localization [11], and automated circuit discovery [7] — validate the program. We prove sparse-coding identifiability of latent features under incoherence conditions, report scaling behavior from production SAE deployments [10][12], and catalogue limitations including feature-splitting, the causal faithfulness gap, and the illusion-of-interpretability problem.

## 1 Introduction

The transformer architecture underlies virtually every frontier language model, yet its internal computations remain poorly understood in algorithmic terms.   *Behavioral* evaluation — benchmarks, red-teaming, probes — tells us *what* a model does; it cannot tell us *how* it does it, nor what it will do under distributional shifts for which behavioral coverage is inevitably incomplete.

Three intellectual threads have converged into the modern program.   *First*, the **transformer circuits** thread (Anthropic, 2020–2022) developed the mathematical decomposition of attention into QK and OV circuits operating on a shared residual stream, discovered induction heads as the engine of in-context learning [9], and produced the first complete circuit for a nontrivial behavior, indirect object identification in GPT-2 small [4].

> **Thesis.** The residual stream of a transformer language model can be decomposed into a sparse, causal, human-legible computation: attention and MLP components implement QK/OV circuits; the feature basis of the residual stream is recovered by sparse dictionary learning; and hypothesized mechanisms are certified by causal abstraction. The remainder of this thesis reconstructs, proves, and empirically grounds each claim.

This thesis is organized as follows.     Section 2 reviews the transformer architecture with the notational machinery of the residual stream.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The transformer as a residual-stream machine

A decoder-only transformer processes a sequence of tokens $t_1, \dots, t_n$ by maintaining a *residual stream* — a sequence of vectors $x^{(0)}, \dots, x^{(L)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ across $L$ layers.

$$x_i^{(\ell)} = x_i^{(\ell-1)} + \mathrm{Attn}^{(\ell)}(x^{(\ell-1)})_i + \mathrm{MLP}^{(\ell)}\big(x_i^{(\ell-1)} + \mathrm{Attn}^{(\ell)}(x^{(\ell-1)})_i\big).$$

Here $\mathrm{Attn}^{(\ell)}$ is multi-head self-attention and $\mathrm{MLP}^{(\ell)}$ is a positionwise two-layer network.

### 2.2 Attention decomposed: QK and OV circuits

Single-head attention computes, per position $i$, a weighted sum of value vectors with weights from query–key dot products.

- **QK circuit**: $W_{QK}^h = W_Q^{h\top} W_K^h \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. The attention score from destination position $i$ to source position $j$ is $x_i^\top W_{QK}^h x_j$ (pre-softmax). The QK circuit determines *where the head attends* — which information moves.
- **OV circuit**: $W_{OV}^h = W_V^h W_O^h \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. Given attention weights $A_{ij}$, head $h$ adds $\sum_j A_{ij} W_{OV}^h x_j$ to the stream. The OV circuit determines *what the head writes* when it attends.

This factorization is exact (up to LayerNorm rescaling, which can be folded into the matrices) and conceptually clean: *QK circuits route, OV circuits compute*.

### 2.3 Polysemanticity and superposition

*Neurons* are the wrong unit of analysis.   Individual neurons in trained networks are typically **polysemantic**: they fire for multiple, semantically unrelated features [6].

## 3 Methodology

The mechanistic program combines three methodological families, each addressing a distinct question.

1. **Circuit analysis** (questions: *what are the components, and how do they compose?*). The workflow is: (a) decompose the model into components (heads, MLP layers, or SAE features); (b) hypothesize each component's input–output function by correlational study of activations and attention patterns; (c) test causal necessity and sufficiency by *interventions* — activation patching, ablation, path patching — measuring changes in a task metric [4][7]; (d) assemble components into an algorithm and validate it end-to-end, ideally by knocking out the hypothesized circuit and observing task performance collapse while preserving the rest of the model.

2. **Sparse dictionary learning** (questions: *what are the features?*). Train a sparse autoencoder on model activations $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$:
   $$\mathcal{L}(x) = \|x - D(\sigma(E(x)))\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\sigma(E(x))\|_1,$$
   with encoder $E: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^m$ ($m \gg d$, e.g. $m = 8d$ to $32d$), decoder $D$, and nonlinearity $\sigma$. The $L_1$ penalty forces the latent code $z = \sigma(E(x))$ to be sparse, so reconstruction must route through a small number of *dictionary atoms* — the columns of $D$. Empirically, these atoms are far more interpretable (monosemantic) than neurons [6][8][10].

3. **Causal abstraction** (questions: *is the interpretation faithful?*). Given a high-level causal model $\mathcal{C}$ with variables $V$ and a low-level neural network $\mathcal{N}$, an **alignment** $\tau$ maps each $V_i$ to a (possibly distributed) set of neural activations. An **interchange intervention** replaces the activations $\tau(V_i)$ on a base input with the values they take on a source input, then checks whether $\mathcal{N}$'s output matches $\mathcal{C}$'s counterfactual output. The fraction of matches is the **interchange intervention accuracy (IIA)**; high IIA certifies $\mathcal{C}$ as a faithful abstraction of $\mathcal{N}$ [3]. *Interchange intervention training* (IIT) additionally trains networks to satisfy a target causal model [3].

```python
# Minimal sparse autoencoder (conceptual): dictionary learning on activations
import torch, torch.nn as nn

class SAE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_model: int, dict_mult: int = 8, l1: float = 1e-3):
        super().__init__()
        self.d_lat = d_model * dict_mult
        self.enc = nn.Linear(d_model, self.d_lat, bias=True)
        self.dec = nn.Linear(self.d_lat, d_model, bias=False)
        self.l1, self.b_dec = l1, nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(d_model))
    def forward(self, x):
        z = torch.relu(self.enc(x - self.b_dec))   # sparse code
        x_hat = self.dec(z) + self.b_dec            # dictionary reconstruction
        recon = ((x - x_hat) ** 2).mean()
        sparsity = self.l1 * z.abs().sum() / z.shape[0]
        return x_hat, recon + sparsity
```

```rust
// Interchange intervention, sketched in Rust: swap aligned activations
// between a base run and a source run, then read the counterfactual output.
fn interchange_intervene(
    net: &mut Network, base: &Input, source: &Input,
    align: &dyn Fn(&Activations) -> Vec<usize>,
) -> Output {
    let src = net.forward_collect(source);      // source activations
    let mut b = net.forward_collect(base);      // base activations
    for &idx in &align(&b) {                    // aligned neural variables
        b.units[idx] = src.units[idx];          // interchange intervention
    }
    net.forward_from(b)                         // counterfactual output
}
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Residual Stream as a Shared Communication Channel

The power of the circuits viewpoint comes from a single structural observation: everything in a transformer is *addition to a shared vector*.

Formally, with LayerNorm folded into adjacent weights (an exact operation for the per-token affine rescaling when LayerNorm is applied immediately before a linear read), the logits at position $i$ decompose as:

$$\mathrm{logits}_i = W_U \left( x_i^{(0)} + \sum_{\ell, h} \mathrm{head}_{\ell,h}(x^{(\ell-1)})_i + \sum_{\ell} \mathrm{MLP}^{(\ell)}(\cdot)_i \right),$$

where each head's contribution further factorizes through its QK and OV circuits.

A subtlety: *virtual attention heads*.

| Composition type | Data path | Typical role |
|---|---|---|
| Q-composition | $h_1 \to$ Q-side of $h_2$ | Query refinement, conditional attending |
| K-composition | $h_1 \to$ K-side of $h_2$ | Key-side matching (induction mechanism) |
| V-composition | $h_1 \to$ V-side of $h_2$ | Value enrichment, chained copying |

### 4.2 Induction Heads and the Two-Head Motif

**Induction heads** are the flagship discovery of the circuits program [9]. An induction head at position $i$ attends to position $j$ when the token at $j$ was *preceded by the same token as $i$* — i.e., it completes the pattern $[A][B] \dots [A] \to [B]$. Induction heads are argued to be the primary mechanism of *in-context learning*: few-shot demonstrations are just long-range repeated patterns, and induction implements "do what was done before."

The mechanism is a **two-head composition**:

1. A **previous-token head** (K-composition source) attends from each position to the immediately preceding token, copying the embedding of $t_{i-1}$ into position $i$'s stream.
2. The **induction head** proper uses K-composition: its keys read the previous-token head's output, so key at position $j$ encodes "$t_j$ was preceded by $t_{j-1}$"; its queries, reading the current stream, encode the current token $t_i$. The QK match $x_i^\top W_{QK} x_j$ fires when $t_{i} = t_{j-1}$, and the OV circuit copies $t_j$'s information forward [9].

> **Theorem (Induction mechanism, informal).** *In a two-layer attention-only transformer, let head $p$ be a previous-token head with OV circuit $W_{OV}^p \approx I$ on the embedding subspace, and let head $q$ compose with $p$ via K-composition. Then $q$ implements induction: its attention from $i$ to $j$ is monotone in $\langle e(t_i), e(t_{j-1}) \rangle$, i.e., it attends to the token following the previous occurrence of the current token.*

### 4.3 Superposition, Polysemanticity, and Toy Models

Why are neurons polysemantic?

$$f(x) = \mathrm{ReLU}(W^\top W x + b), \qquad \mathcal{L} = \sum_i I_i (x_i - f_i(x))^2,$$

where feature $i$ is active with probability $1 - S$ (sparsity $S$) and importance $I_i$ weights the loss.

Key quantitative findings from TMS:

- **Features per dimension**, $\|W\|_F^2 / n$, measures superposition: values $> 1$ indicate packing beyond the ambient dimension.
- Geometry undergoes *phase transitions* as sparsity varies: features arrange into elegant symmetric configurations (digons, triangles, tetrahedra) that balance interference against reconstruction.
- *Privileged bases*: superposition is a property of the *feature* basis, not neurons — rotating to a random basis destroys the structure, which is why SAEs must learn the dictionary rather than reading off neurons [2][8].

This is the theoretical foundation of the entire dictionary-learning enterprise: if models compress $n$ features into $m$ dimensions, then an overcomplete sparse code with $m' \gg m$ latents can *unpack* them — provided the features are sparse enough that the dictionary is identifiable (see §5.3).

### 4.4 Sparse Autoencoders and Dictionary Learning at Scale

The SAE program operationalizes the TMS insight on real models [6][8].

- **Loss variants.** The original $L_1$ penalty [6] was largely superseded by **$k$-sparse** autoencoders (keep only the top-$k$ activations) and **gated SAEs**, which separate the *detection* of a feature (a gating network) from its *magnitude estimation*, mitigating the shrinkage bias of $L_1$ [12].
- **Scale.** Anthropic's "Scaling Monosemanticity" trained SAEs with up to **34 million latents** on Claude 3 Sonnet's residual stream, finding features for concepts as specific as the Golden Gate Bridge, code vulnerabilities, and sycophantic praise — and demonstrating *feature steering*: clamping a feature's activation up or down causally modulates model behavior on that concept [10]. OpenAI's concurrent effort scaled to **16 million latents** on GPT-4, with systematic evaluation of reconstruction fidelity versus interpretability [12].
- **Evaluation.** Monosemanticity is measured by **auto-interpretability**: a language model writes a natural-language description of a feature from its top-activating examples, and a second model predicts held-out activations from the description [6][10]. Sparse probing (does a single latent linearly predict a concept?) and steering (does clamping change behavior?) provide causal cross-checks.

The SAE lens reframes the neuron as a historical accident: *features* are the natural kind; neurons are arbitrary linear mixtures of them.

### 4.5 Causal Abstraction and Interchange Interventions

Observational interpretability — probes, saliency, SAE latents — suffers a fundamental gap: correlation is not causation.

> **Definition (Causal abstraction, after Geiger et al.).** *Let $\mathcal{N}$ be a neural network and $\mathcal{C}$ a high-level causal (structural-equation) model. An alignment $\tau$ maps each variable $V_i \in \mathcal{C}$ to a set of neural activations. $\mathcal{C}$ is a causal abstraction of $\mathcal{N}$ under $\tau$ iff, for base/source input pairs, interchange interventions on $\tau(V_i)$ in $\mathcal{N}$ produce the same outputs as the corresponding interventions on $V_i$ in $\mathcal{C}$. The **interchange intervention accuracy (IIA)** is the fraction of input pairs for which they agree.*

Geiger et al.

The frontier is *distributed* alignment: high-level variables rarely sit in single neurons.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 End-to-end circuits: the indirect object identification case study

Wang et al. reverse-engineered GPT-2 small on **indirect object identification** (IOI): given *"When Mary and John went to the store, John gave a drink to ___"*, predict *Mary*. Using path patching, they identified a complete circuit [4]:

1. **Duplicate-token heads** detect that *John* appears twice.
2. **S-inhibition heads** use that signal to *suppress* attention to the duplicated name.
3. **Name-mover heads** then attend to the remaining name (*Mary*) and copy it to the output.

Knocking out any component degrades IOI performance; knocking out the full circuit reduces the logit difference to near zero while leaving the rest of the model intact — a *causal* completeness claim, not merely correlational [4]. This remains the gold standard for what a finished mechanistic explanation looks like.

### 5.2 Phase transitions: grokking and circuit formation

Nanda et al.    studied **grokking** — delayed generalization on modular arithmetic — through a mechanistic lens [5].

### 5.3 Localizing factual recall: ROME

Meng et al.'s **ROME** (Rank-One Model Editing) localized factual associations (*"The Eiffel Tower is in ___"*) to mid-layer MLP blocks, treating them as key–value memories [11].

### 5.5 Identifiability of sparse features: a theorem

We now give a theoretical guarantee for the SAE program in a simplified setting.

> **Theorem (Sparse identifiability under incoherence).** *Let the mutual coherence $\mu(D^*) = \max_{i \ne j} |\langle d_i^*, d_j^* \rangle|$ satisfy $\mu < 1/(2k-1)$, and let each $k$-sparse $z$ have nonzero entries bounded away from zero. Then, in the noiseless limit, any global minimizer $(D, \hat{z})$ of the $L_1$-penalized reconstruction objective recovers $D^*$ up to permutation and sign, and the support of $\hat{z}$ equals the support of $z$.*

*Proof sketch.* Under the coherence bound, the $k$-sparse representation of any $x$ in the column span is unique (a standard compressed-sensing result: if $D^* z_1 = D^* z_2$ with $\|z_i\|_0 \le k$, then $z_1 - z_2$ is $2k$-sparse in the nullspace, contradicting $\mu < 1/(2k-1)$ via the Gershgorin argument on the Gram matrix).   Hence the $L_0$ problem has a unique solution, and $L_1$ minimization recovers it under the same incoherence condition (the exact-recovery theorem of Donoho–Elad–Temlyakov).

The theorem's assumptions are strong — real features are only *approximately* sparse, dictionaries are learned jointly with codes, and superposition *violates* incoherence by design (feature directions are deliberately non-orthogonal [2]).

## 6 Limitations

The program's successes notwithstanding, serious limitations remain.

1. **SAE pathologies.** Dictionary learning is underspecified: *feature splitting* (one concept fragmented across latents), *feature absorption* (a general latent "absorbs" a specific one), dead latents, and shard effects where the learned dictionary depends on training-data mixtures [10][12]. Reconstruction fidelity does not imply that the latents carve the model's computation at its joints.

2. **The causal gap.** Most SAE features are validated observationally (autointerp, probing) rather than interventionally. Steering experiments [10] are a step toward causal validation, but systematic IIA-style certification of feature circuits [3] at frontier scale remains aspirational.

3. **Illusion of interpretability.** Human-legible descriptions can be *misleadingly* compelling: a feature that fires on "code vulnerabilities" in autointerp may actually implement a syntactic heuristic. Without adversarial evaluation of explanations, interpretability risks becoming a storytelling exercise.

4. **Completeness and faithfulness.** Circuit analyses typically explain *most* of a task metric, not all; residual unexplained variance may hide the most safety-relevant computations. Automated methods [7] trade recall for precision, and there is no accepted *completeness certificate* for a circuit claim.

5. **Scale and cost.** Training production SAEs costs millions of GPU-hours equivalent [10][12]; interpretability remains far more expensive than training, inverting the economics needed for routine auditing.

## 7 Conclusion

Mechanistic interpretability has moved, in half a decade, from a speculative proposal to an empirical science with validated case studies. The residual stream admits an exact decomposition into QK and OV circuits [1]; induction heads demonstrate that these circuits implement legible algorithms behind in-context learning [9]; toy models of superposition explain why the natural units are features, not neurons [2]; sparse autoencoders recover those features at the scale of tens of millions of latents [10][12]; and causal abstraction supplies the formal criterion — interchange intervention accuracy — by which any mechanistic claim must ultimately be judged [3].

The path forward is clear.    *First*, unify the threads: run automated circuit discovery over SAE-feature graphs and certify the resulting circuits with interchange interventions, closing the loop from observation to causal proof.
