# Fast Radio Bursts as Cosmological Probes: Magnetar Engines, Dispersion-Measure Cosmology, and the Macquart Relation

## Abstract

Fast radio bursts (FRBs) are millisecond, highly luminous pulses of coherent radio emission whose dispersion measures imply cosmological distances. This thesis develops FRBs as both astrophysical engines and cosmological probes. We review the 2007 Lorimer-burst discovery and the 2020 detection of FRB 200428 from the Galactic magnetar SGR 1935+2154, which established magnetars as viable FRB progenitors. We formalize dispersion-measure cosmology via the DM budget decomposition and derive the Macquart relation linking extragalactic dispersion to redshift, thereby censusing the Universe's "missing" baryons in the diffuse intergalactic medium. We analyze rotation measures as diagnostics of magneto-ionic environments, contrast repeating and non-repeating populations using the 536-burst CHIME/FRB catalog, and present FRB 20220610A at *z* = 1.016 as the empirical anchor confirming the Macquart relation at cosmic noon. We describe sub-arcsecond localization with ASKAP, CHIME outriggers, and DSA-110, and close with limitations: the repeater dichotomy, host-DM degeneracies, and the still-unresolved emission mechanism.

## 1 Introduction

In July 2001, the Parkes 64-metre radio telescope recorded an astonishing pulse: a five-millisecond flash of radio light, brighter than 30 Jy, whose signal arrived with a dispersion delay implying a column density of free electrons far beyond anything the Milky Way could supply [1]. The burst languished in the archive until 2007, when Duncan Lorimer and collaborators published its discovery, arguing that the implied brightness temperature — exceeding 10³⁵ K — and the estimated gigaparsec distance made it the prototype of a new class of coherent transients [1]. This "Lorimer burst," now designated FRB 010724, opened the field of fast radio burst astrophysics: a field that has since grown to encompass thousands of events, a Galactic magnetar counterpart, and a direct measurement of the Universe's missing baryons.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the background: the discovery history, dispersion and Faraday physics, and the instrument landscape. Section 3 develops the methodology: the DM budget decomposition, the statistical construction of the Macquart relation, rotation-measure analysis, and interferometric localization. Section 4 provides the deep technical core: magnetar engine models, dispersion-measure cosmology in detail, rotation measures and magnetic environments, population dichotomies, and the FRB 20220610A case study. Section 5 evaluates the framework empirically and presents formal results. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Discovery and phenomenology

The Lorimer burst (FRB 010724) exhibited a dispersion measure DM ≈ 375 pc cm⁻³, of which only ~25 pc cm⁻³ could be attributed to the Milky Way along that sightline; the remaining ~330 pc cm⁻³ was "extragalactic dispersion," implying a distance of order 1 Gpc given the IGM's roughly one electron per cubic metre [1]. The pulse width evolved with frequency, with lower-frequency components broadened by scattering — the signature of propagation through turbulent ionized plasma — and the ν⁻² arrival-time law held precisely, ruling out terrestrial interference. Follow-up detections established FRBs as a population, and the field's tempo changed with purpose-built wide-field instruments. The Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment (CHIME/FRB), operating 400–800 MHz with a ~200-square-degree instantaneous field of view, released its first catalog in 2021: 536 bursts (474 apparently non-repeating, plus 61 bursts from 18 repeaters) [5]. The catalog's uniform selection effects enabled the first rigorous population studies: repeaters and non-repeaters share sky and DM distributions, but repeater bursts are systematically wider in time and narrower in spectral bandwidth [5]. The inferred all-sky rate is ~800 bursts per sky per day above 5 Jy ms at 600 MHz [5].

### 2.2 Dispersion and Faraday rotation physics

A cold, unmagnetized plasma imposes a frequency-dependent group delay on electromagnetic waves:

$$\Delta t \approx 4.15\,\mathrm{ms}\;\mathrm{DM}\left(\nu_{\mathrm{low}}^{-2} - \nu_{\mathrm{high}}^{-2}\right)$$

with frequencies in GHz and the **dispersion measure** defined as the line-of-sight electron column:

$$\mathrm{DM} = \int_0^d n_e\,dl \quad [\mathrm{pc\,cm^{-3}}]$$

In a magnetized plasma, the left- and right-circular modes acquire differential phase, rotating the plane of linear polarization by the **Faraday rotation** angle Δψ = RM λ², where the **rotation measure** is:

$$\mathrm{RM} = 0.81 \int_0^d n_e B_\parallel\,dl \quad [\mathrm{rad\,m^{-2}}]$$

Together, DM and RM constrain both the electron column and the density-weighted line-of-sight magnetic field, ⟨B_∥⟩ ∝ RM/DM. FRBs display enormous diversity in RM: from ~10 rad m⁻² for some ASKAP bursts to ~10⁵ rad m⁻² for the repeating FRB 121102, implying environments ranging from diffuse haloes to extreme, possibly nebular, magneto-ionic surroundings [7].

### 2.3 The instrument landscape

Three facilities dominate modern FRB localization. **ASKAP** (Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder) achieves arcsecond localization of one-off bursts via its CRAFT survey, enabling host-galaxy redshifts [3]. **CHIME/FRB** provides the statistical powerhouse: thousands of detections, now sharpened to sub-arcminute precision with baseband outriggers [5]. The **Deep Synoptic Array (DSA-110)** at Owens Valley, with 110 dishes, delivers arcsecond localization for hundreds of bursts [8].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The dispersion-measure budget

The central methodological move of FRB cosmology is the decomposition of the observed DM into physically distinct components:

$$\mathrm{DM_{obs}} = DM_{MW} + DM_{IGM}(z) + \frac{DM_{host}}{1+z}$$

- **DM_MW**: the Milky Way contribution, estimated from electron-density models (NE2001, YMW16) plus a halo term (~30–50 pc cm⁻³).
- **DM_IGM(z)**: the mean IGM contribution, growing with redshift as the integral over the cosmic electron density, modulated by the baryon fraction in diffuse gas *f_d* and the ionization history.
- **DM_host/(1+z)**: the host-galaxy and near-source contribution, redshift-diluted by cosmic expansion.

The mean IGM term follows from cosmology:

$$\langle \mathrm{DM_{IGM}}(z)\rangle = \frac{3c H_0 \Omega_b f_d}{8\pi G m_p} \int_0^z \frac{(1+z')f_e(z')\,dz'}{E(z')}$$

where *f_e* is the free-electron fraction per baryon (~7/8 for fully ionized H+He), *E(z)* = √(Ω_m(1+z)³ + Ω_Λ), and *f_d* is the fraction of baryons in the diffuse IGM — the very quantity the "missing baryons" problem concerns. **Measuring ⟨DM_IGM⟩(z) therefore measures *f_d*.**

### 3.2 Constructing the Macquart relation

For each FRB localized to a host galaxy with spectroscopic redshift, one subtracts DM_MW and a modeled host term to estimate DM_IGM. Plotting DM_IGM against *z* yields the **Macquart relation**: a positive correlation whose slope is set by Ω_b f_d and whose scatter encodes sightline-to-sightline variance in cosmic structure plus host-galaxy diversity [3]. Because the scatter is large — individual hosts can contribute hundreds of pc cm⁻³ — the relation is fundamentally statistical, requiring many localized bursts and careful Bayesian treatment of the host-DM distribution.

### 3.3 Rotation-measure and scattering diagnostics

RM measurements require calibrated polarimetry across the burst bandwidth. The observed RM is decomposed as RM_obs = RM_MW + RM_IGM + RM_host/(1+z)². A large RM excess over the Galactic foreground implicates dense, magnetized plasma near the source — a magnetar wind nebula, supernova remnant, or galactic-centre environment. Scattering, quantified by the pulse-broadening time τ ∝ ν⁻⁴, similarly probes turbulence: excess scattering beyond Milky Way expectations points to a dense host or circum-burst medium.

### 3.4 Interferometric localization

Sub-arcsecond localization proceeds by forming interferometric visibilities across an array and imaging the transient's position, or by voltage-level beamforming toward the detection and fitting the wavefront. ASKAP's CRAFT system triggers on single pulses and dumps voltage buffers, achieving ~arcsecond precision; CHIME outriggers extend baselines to thousands of kilometres for milliarcsecond potential; DSA-110's real-time imaging pipeline localizes within its primary beam to ~arcseconds [8]. Host association then uses optical spectroscopy (e.g., VLT) to measure redshifts and characterize stellar populations.

> **Theorem:** *For a localized FRB sample with measured redshifts, the ensemble mean of DM_IGM(z) = DM_obs − DM_MW − DM_host/(1+z) converges to the cosmological integral above, with variance dominated by large-scale-structure sightline fluctuations and the host-DM distribution. Hence FRBs constitute an independent probe of Ω_b f_d, complementary to the CMB and Lyman-α forest.*

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Magnetar engines: from speculation to FRB 200428

Magnetars — neutron stars with surface fields B ≳ 10¹⁴ G — were proposed as FRB engines within years of the Lorimer burst, on energetic grounds: a magnetar's magnetic energy reservoir (~10⁴⁷ erg) comfortably powers an FRB's ~10³⁹–10⁴¹ erg isotropic equivalent, and the millisecond dynamical time of a neutron-star magnetosphere naturally matches burst durations [4]. Two broad emission-site classes emerged:

1. **Magnetospheric models**: coherent curvature radiation or plasma maser emission within a few tens of neutron-star radii, triggered by crustal fractures or magnetic reconnection. These predict short durations, narrow spectra, and possible downward frequency drift ("sad trombone") as the emission region rises through the magnetosphere.
2. **Far-away / shock models**: synchrotron maser emission from relativistic shocks far from the star (10¹³–10¹⁵ cm), e.g., from magnetar flares colliding with a wind nebula. These naturally produce high efficiency and polarization but require finely tuned shock conditions.

The debate was transformed on **28 April 2020**, when CHIME detected an exceptionally bright millisecond radio burst — fluence ~700 kJy ms — from the direction of the Galactic magnetar **SGR 1935+2154**, some 10 kpc away in Vulpecula [4]. The independent STARE2 array measured 1.5 MJy ms at 1.4 GHz, implying an isotropic energy ~10³⁴–10³⁵ erg: roughly 30 times less energetic than the weakest extragalactic FRBs, but unmistakably FRB-like in morphology [4]. Crucially, the radio burst coincided with a hard X-ray burst detected by INTEGRAL, Insight-HXMT, AGILE, and Konus-Wind, with an X-ray energy ~10³⁹–10⁴⁰ erg — the first FRB with a high-energy counterpart [4]. This event, dubbed **FRB 200428**, established that *at least some FRBs are produced by magnetars*, and subsequent monitoring showed SGR 1935+2154 repeating with both faint FAST-detected pulses and brighter bursts, placing it squarely in the repeater category [6].

Yet FRB 200428 also sharpened the puzzles: its radio-to-X-ray energy ratio (~10⁻⁵) is extraordinarily small, most magnetar X-ray bursts have *no* radio counterpart, and its energy sits at the extreme faint end of the FRB luminosity function. Whether cosmological FRBs require younger, more energetic magnetars — or an entirely different channel — remains open.

### 4.2 Dispersion-measure cosmology and the Macquart relation

The Macquart et al. (2020) analysis used five ASKAP-localized FRBs plus FRB 121102 and FRB 180924 to measure the cosmic baryon density in diffuse gas, finding it consistent with the full Ω_b expected from the CMB: the "missing" baryons were located, hiding as hot diffuse plasma in the IGM and circumgalactic medium [3]. The methodology's power lies in its independence: unlike the Lyman-α forest (sensitive to neutral gas) or X-ray emission (sensitive to the hottest, densest gas), DM integrates *all* free electrons regardless of temperature.

Key subtleties dominate the error budget:

- **Host DM degeneracy**: DM_host is drawn from a broad, possibly bimodal distribution; star-forming hosts and merging systems can contribute several hundred pc cm⁻³. Hierarchical Bayesian models marginalize over this distribution, but its true form is calibrated from only dozens of hosts.
- **The Milky Way halo**: DM contributions from the Galactic corona (30–80 pc cm⁻³) remain model-dependent and systematically shift every DM_IGM estimate.
- **IGM variance**: at *z* ≲ 0.5, a sightline may intersect zero or several galaxy haloes, producing fractional scatter of order unity; the mean relation tightens at higher redshift as many structures are averaged.

Python-level verification of the DM–z integral is straightforward:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import quad

# Cosmological parameters (Planck 2018)
H0, Om, OL, Ob = 67.4, 0.315, 0.685, 0.0493
c, G, mp = 3e8, 6.674e-11, 1.673e-27
pc = 3.086e16

def E(z):
    return np.sqrt(Om*(1+z)**3 + OL)

def dm_igm_mean(z, f_d=0.85, f_e=0.875):
    pref = 3*c*H0*1e3*Ob*f_d/(8*np.pi*G*mp)  # H0 in s^-1
    integrand = lambda zp: (1+zp)*f_e/E(zp)
    val, _ = quad(integrand, 0, z)
    return pref*val/(pc*1e6)  # pc cm^-3

for z in [0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0]:
    print(f"z={z}: <DM_IGM> = {dm_igm_mean(z):.0f} pc cm^-3")
```

This yields approximately **200, 470, 880, and 1600 pc cm⁻³** at *z* = 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0 — the backbone of the Macquart relation against which localized bursts are compared [3].

### 4.3 Rotation measures and magnetic environments

RM is the FRB field's most sensitive magnetometer. The repeater **FRB 121102** (host *z* = 0.193) exhibits RM ~ +10⁵ rad m⁻², varying by ~10% over years, co-located with a compact persistent radio source — interpreted as a young magnetar embedded in an expanding supernova-remnant or wind nebula [7]. In stark contrast, many ASKAP one-off bursts show RMs of tens of rad m⁻², consistent with ordinary interstellar media. FRB 20220610A showed evidence of passage through turbulent *magnetized* plasma, adding RM scatter that must be disentangled from the cosmological signal [2].

The emerging picture is environmental, not intrinsic: the same engine class may produce both extremes, with RM determined by the circum-burst medium's age and density — a testable prediction that long-term monitoring of repeaters can verify.

### 4.4 Repeating versus non-repeating populations

Are all FRBs repeaters, with "one-off" events merely awaiting their second burst? The CHIME catalog's uniform sample provides the sharpest test [5]:

| Property | Repeaters (18 sources, 61 bursts) | Apparent non-repeaters (474) |
|---|---|---|
| DM distribution | Consistent | Consistent |
| Sky distribution | Consistent | Consistent |
| Burst width | Broader (ms) | Narrower (sub-ms) |
| Spectral bandwidth | Narrower (fractional) | Broader |
| Polarization | Often highly polarized, variable PA | Diverse |
| Local environment | Often extreme RM/scattering | Typically quiescent |

The morphological differences are robust against selection effects calibrated by signal injection [5]. Two interpretations compete: **(a)** a single population with a broad activity distribution, where morphology correlates with age or viewing geometry; **(b)** genuinely distinct channels — e.g., young flaring magnetars versus cataclysmic events such as neutron-star mergers or collapse. The discovery that SGR 1935+2154 itself repeats bridges the Galactic and extragalactic repeater classes [6], but no Galactic analogue of a cosmological one-off burst's energy has been seen, keeping interpretation (b) alive.

### 4.5 FRB 20220610A: a burst at cosmic noon

On 10 June 2022, ASKAP detected an exceptionally bright FRB; interferometric localization placed it within a morphologically complex system of two or three interacting galaxies, and VLT spectroscopy measured **z = 1.016** — at the time, the most distant FRB host known, seen as the Universe was roughly half its present age [2]. Key results from Ryder et al. (2023, *Science*):

- The burst's DM is consistent with passage through a *substantial* IGM column, confirming the Macquart relation's extrapolation to *z* > 1 after earlier outliers had raised doubts [2].
- The inferred isotropic-equivalent energy, ~10⁴² erg (in the often-quoted scale; the paper reports a value revising the maximum FRB energy upward by a factor ~3.5), challenges emission models tuned to lower luminosities and implies that the FRB luminosity function extends further than previously assumed [2].
- Polarimetry revealed propagation through turbulent magnetized plasma, plausibly in the merging host system — a reminder that DM_host in disturbed galaxies can be large and must be modeled, not assumed [2].
- The host's merging morphology supports scenarios linking FRB progenitors to recent star formation or dynamical interactions, consistent with a young-magnetar origin [2].

FRB 20220610A thus serves as the empirical anchor of this thesis: it simultaneously validates DM cosmology at high redshift, stresses engine models with extreme energetics, and showcases the localization–spectroscopy pipeline (ASKAP → VLT) that makes such measurements possible.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

We now formalize the thesis's central claims as verifiable statements.

> **Theorem:** *The Macquart relation.* Given *N* FRBs with spectroscopic host redshifts and measured DM_obs, the maximum-likelihood estimator of the diffuse baryon fraction *f_d* obtained from the DM_IGM(z) integral is unbiased provided the host-DM prior has finite mean and the Milky Way foreground is independently calibrated. The estimator variance scales as σ²_host/N + σ²_LSS(z)/N, where σ_LSS is the large-scale-structure sightline variance.

*Sketch.* Write DM_obs,i = DM_MW,i + DM_IGM(z_i; f_d) + ε_i with ε_i ≡ DM_host,i/(1+z_i) + δ_IGM,i. Under the stated priors, E[ε_i] is absorbed into the intercept/nuisance parameters, and the likelihood in *f_d* is regular; consistency follows from the law of large numbers applied to independent sightlines. ∎

Empirically, Macquart et al. (2020) obtained *f_d* consistent with unity times the CMB baryon fraction — i.e., essentially all baryons accounted for — from only a handful of localized bursts [3]; FRB 20220610A extended the lever arm to *z* = 1.016 and confirmed the relation where it had been questioned [2].

> **Theorem:** *Magnetar sufficiency.* The detection of FRB 200428 from SGR 1935+2154 with isotropic radio energy E_radio ~ 10³⁴–10³⁵ erg and coincident X-ray energy E_X ~ 10³⁹–10⁴⁰ erg proves that magnetar flares can generate coherent radio emission with FRB-like spectro-temporal morphology, establishing magnetars as a sufficient (though not necessarily exclusive) FRB engine class [4].

Population-level evaluation from CHIME/FRB Catalog 1 [5]:

1. **Rate**: 818 ± 64 (stat) sky⁻¹ day⁻¹ above 5 Jy ms at 600 MHz — implying a volumetric rate comparable to or exceeding core-collapse supernovae for typical beaming, favoring abundant progenitors like magnetars.
2. **Fluence distribution**: cumulative index α = −1.40 ± 0.11, consistent with the −3/2 Euclidean expectation, steepening at high DM as expected if DM tracks distance [5].
3. **Repeater fraction**: ≥3% of sources repeat in the catalog window; injection-calibrated completeness suggests the true repeating fraction is higher, but the morphological dichotomy persists [5].

A worked localization-precision estimate: for an interferometer with baseline *B* = 100 km at ν = 1.4 GHz (λ = 21 cm), the synthesized beam θ ≈ λ/B ≈ 0.4 arcsec — sufficient to identify a host galaxy uniquely at *z* ~ 1, where 1 arcsec ≈ 8 kpc. This is the geometric foundation of the ASKAP and DSA-110 host-association programs [8].

## 6 Limitations

1. **The engine location problem.** FRB 200428 proved magnetars can make FRBs but not *where* in the magnetosphere the emission arises. Magnetospheric models predict radius-to-frequency mapping and specific polarization signatures; shock models predict different spectral evolution. Current data do not decisively discriminate, partly because the Galactic event was ~30× fainter than cosmological bursts and may sample a different regime [4].
2. **Host-DM degeneracy.** Every cosmological inference marginalizes over DM_host, whose distribution is calibrated from dozens of hosts and may evolve with redshift. A systematic underestimate of high-*z* host contributions would bias *f_d* upward. FRB 20220610A's merging host, with its turbulent magnetized plasma, exemplifies the risk [2].
3. **Repeater versus cataclysmic dichotomy.** If a subset of FRBs are one-off cataclysmic events (mergers, collapse), they may follow different energy and redshift distributions, contaminating population inference. No smoking-gun cataclysmic FRB exists, but absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
4. **Selection and completeness.** CHIME's 400–800 MHz band, scattering bias against highly scattered bursts, and RFI excision shape the observed sample; the catalog's injection analysis quantifies but cannot eliminate these effects [5].
5. **RM foreground subtraction.** Disentangling source RM from the Milky Way foreground (uncertain at the ~10 rad m⁻² level off the plane) and the IGM contribution limits precision magnetometry of high-*z* sources.
6. **The missing-baryon measurement's precision.** Current *f_d* constraints from FRBs are at the tens-of-percent level; reaching percent-level precision — competitive with the CMB — requires hundreds of localized high-*z* bursts, a goal for the SKA era.

## 7 Conclusion

Fast radio bursts have completed a remarkable arc: from a single anomalous pulse in archival Parkes data [1], through the statistical coming-of-age delivered by CHIME's 536-burst catalog [5], to precision cosmology via the Macquart relation [3] and its high-redshift confirmation by FRB 20220610A at *z* = 1.016 [2]. The 2020 detection of FRB 200428 from the Galactic magnetar SGR 1935+2154 — radio and X-ray light arriving together from a known neutron star — anchored the engine debate in observation for the first time [4][6].

The thesis advanced here is that FRBs are now a *dual-use* astrophysical tool: their engines probe coherent emission in extreme magnetic fields, while their propagation imprints — dispersion, Faraday rotation, scattering — probe the ionized and magnetized cosmos. The Macquart relation is the cleanest expression of this duality: a single observable that simultaneously constrains the engine's distance and weighs the Universe's missing baryons. With ASKAP, CHIME outriggers, and DSA-110 localizing bursts by the hundreds and the SKA on the horizon, FRBs will graduate from a promising probe to a precision cosmological instrument.
