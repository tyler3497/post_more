# Channel Coding at the Shannon Limit: LDPC Ensembles, Polar Code Construction, and the Engineering of 5G NR

## Abstract

Claude Shannon's 1948 channel coding theorem established the capacity of a noisy channel as the supremum of achievable rates with arbitrarily small error probability, yet the theorem was non-constructive: it proved the existence of capacity-approaching codes without exhibiting a single practical one. Two code families closed that six-decade gap through opposite routes. Low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes, invented by Robert Gallager in 1963 and resurrected in the mid-1990s, approach capacity through *random-like sparse graph ensembles* decoded by iterative belief propagation, with Richardson and Urbanke's density evolution (2001) optimizing irregular degree distributions to within 0.0045 dB of the Shannon limit. Polar codes, introduced by Erdal Arıkan in 2009, achieve capacity through *explicit algebraic construction*: channel polarization synthesizes virtual channels tending to extremes of perfect or useless, with successive cancellation decoding achieving block error below 2^(−N^β) at O(N log N) complexity. This thesis unifies these developments — from Shannon's random coding argument to 5G NR's dual-coder architecture, where quasi-cyclic LDPC base graphs carry data channels and CRC-aided polar codes carry control channels — evaluating their guarantees, decoders, and trade-offs.


## 1 Introduction

In 1948, Shannon announced that every discrete memoryless channel possesses a number C, the *capacity*, such that communication at any rate below C is possible with vanishing probability of error, while no code can transmit reliably above it [1]. The argument was an existence proof: random codes, decoded with maximum likelihood, achieve the bound, but neither the codebook nor the decoder could be implemented. For nearly fifty years, the gap between the theorem's promise and engineering practice narrowed only gradually — through convolutional codes, concatenated Reed–Solomon systems, and Viterbi decoding — until the discovery of turbo codes in 1993 and the subsequent rediscovery of LDPC codes demonstrated that practical, polynomial-complexity schemes could operate within a fraction of a decibel of the limit [2].

The modern theory crystallized around two great ideas that appear contradictory. The first is the *probabilistic method applied to sparse graphs*: consider the ensemble of bipartite Tanner graphs with specified edge-degree distributions λ(x), ρ(x); decode by iterative message passing; and analyze average behavior via density evolution. Surprisingly, randomness plus sparsity yields ensembles whose thresholds provably converge to the Shannon limit, with no algebraic structure whatsoever. The second is *channel polarization*, a deterministic and explicitly constructive phenomenon: starting from N identical copies of a binary-input channel W and applying the polarization kernel G₂ = [[1,0],[1,1]], the synthesized channels W_N^(i) split into two populations — those with symmetric capacity near 1 and those with capacity near 0 — with the fraction of good channels approaching I(W). Polar codes were the first family proved to achieve the symmetric capacity of any binary-input discrete memoryless channel with an explicit construction and O(N log N) complexity [3].

> **Theorem (Shannon's channel coding theorem, 1948).** For a discrete memoryless channel with capacity C, and for any rate R < C, there exists a sequence of block codes of rate R whose block error probability tends to zero as block length tends to infinity. Conversely, no sequence of codes with rate above C can drive the error probability to zero.

The 3rd Generation Partnership Project's 5G New Radio (NR) standard is the first cellular generation to internalize both revolutions simultaneously: quasi-cyclic LDPC codes derived from two standardized base graphs protect the data channels, while CRC-aided polar codes under successive cancellation list decoding protect the control channels [4]. This thesis examines the mathematics that made this architecture inevitable.


## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Shannon to Gallager

Shannon's random coding argument treats the codebook as drawn uniformly at random and the decoder as maximum-likelihood — both exponential in block length. Gallager's 1961 doctoral thesis at MIT, published in *IRE Transactions on Information Theory* (1962) and as the monograph *Low-Density Parity-Check Codes* (MIT Press, 1963), proposed something that looked like the opposite of good engineering: parity-check matrices H that are extremely sparse — each column containing a small constant number of ones rather than a number growing with block length — so that a *probabilistic* iterative decoder could operate in linear time [5]. The codes were forgotten for three decades, until MacKay and Neal (1996) independently rediscovered them and demonstrated near-capacity performance, triggering the modern era.

### 2.2 The Tanner Graph Representation

Every binary linear block code C = {x : xH^T = 0} admits a graphical description introduced by Tanner (1981): a bipartite graph with n *variable nodes* (one per codeword bit) on one side and n−k *check nodes* (one per parity equation) on the other, with an edge wherever H_{ji} = 1. For LDPC codes this graph is sparse — the number of edges is O(n) rather than O(n²) — which is precisely what permits iterative decoding. The code is called *(d_v, d_c)-regular* when each variable node has degree d_v and each check node degree d_c; the famous (3,6)-regular ensemble has rate 1/2. *Irregular* ensembles generalize this to edge-perspective degree distributions:

- λ(x) = Σᵢ λᵢ x^{i−1}, where λᵢ is the fraction of *edges* incident on variable nodes of degree i;
- ρ(x) = Σᵢ ρᵢ x^{i−1}, the analogous distribution for check nodes.

The design rate follows from these distributions: R = 1 − (∫₀¹ ρ)/(∫₀¹ λ). Irregularity is not a curiosity but the decisive innovation — high-degree variable nodes anchor reliable bits early, low-degree nodes are cheap to protect — and optimized irregular ensembles achieve thresholds provably within 0.0045 dB of capacity [6].

### 2.3 Channel Polarization

Arıkan's 2009 construction begins with the smallest possible transform. Given two independent copies of a binary-input channel W, the kernel

```
G₂ = | 1  0 |
     | 1  1 |
```

maps input bits (u₁, u₂) to (x₁, x₂) = (u₁ ⊕ u₂, u₂). The two resulting *synthesized channels* satisfy I(W₂^(1)) < I(W₂^(2)) with I(W₂^(1)) + I(W₂^(2)) = 2I(W): one virtual channel is degraded, the other upgraded. Iterating this splitting/combining n times over N = 2ⁿ channel uses produces N synthesized channels W_N^(i). Arıkan's polarization theorem states that as N → ∞, the fraction of indices i with I(W_N^(i)) near 1 approaches I(W), while the fraction near 0 approaches 1 − I(W) [3]. The polar code transmits K information bits on the K most reliable synthesized channels and freezes the remainder to known values (typically zero), achieving rate R < I(W) under a decoder of complexity O(N log N).


## 3 Methodology

This thesis proceeds by analytic comparison rather than simulation. We assemble the two theories side by side using the following method:

1. **Ensemble specification.** LDPC codes are studied as *random graph ensembles* parameterized by (λ, ρ); polar codes as *deterministic constructions* parameterized by the information set I and frozen set F. This contrast — average-case versus instance-specific analysis — structures every comparison that follows.
2. **Threshold analysis via density evolution.** For LDPC ensembles, we track the probability density of messages passed along edges of the computation tree as decoding iterations grow; the threshold is the supremum channel parameter for which the density concentrates on certainty. For polar codes, the analogous object is the evolution of the Bhattacharyya parameters Z(W_N^(i)) under polarization recursion, which contracts doubly-exponentially fast on good channels.
3. **Decoder complexity accounting.** We compare asymptotic complexity (O(N) per iteration for belief propagation versus O(N log N) total for successive cancellation), finite-length constants, list-size scaling for CRC-aided SCL decoding, and hardware amenability (parallelism, memory access patterns).
4. **Standardization evidence.** We examine the 5G NR specifications' base graphs BG1 (46×68) and BG2 (42×52) and the polar reliability-sequence construction, treating the standard as a revealed-preference experiment: which code won, for which channel, and why [4].

Throughout, inline citations [1]–[8] map to the References. Mathematical claims follow the original sources; where we present simulation-grade numbers (thresholds, waterfall curves), they are quoted from published designs rather than computed here.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 LDPC Ensembles and the Density Evolution Machinery

The central analytic instrument of the LDPC theory is *density evolution*, introduced by Richardson and Urbanke [2]. Consider belief propagation on the infinite computation tree rooted at a variable node, with the all-zero codeword transmitted (valid by channel symmetry and linearity). In iteration ℓ, each directed edge carries a log-likelihood-ratio message whose distribution evolves deterministically: variable-node updates sum incoming densities (convolution), check-node updates apply the "tanh rule"

```
tanh(m_out / 2) = ∏_{j} tanh(m_j / 2),
```

implemented as a dual-domain (Fourier) transform of densities. Density evolution tracks the full message density p_ℓ; the ensemble's *threshold* σ* is the worst channel for which p_ℓ → δ_∞ (certainty) as ℓ → ∞. Below threshold, a random code from the ensemble decodes successfully with probability approaching one exponentially fast in block length; above it, the density converges to a non-trivial fixed point and decoding fails.

Two landmark designs demonstrate the method's power. Richardson, Shokrollahi, and Urbanke (2001) designed irregular ensembles whose thresholds approach capacity arbitrarily closely [6], and Chung, Forney, Richardson, and Urbanke (2001) exhibited a rate-1/2 ensemble with threshold within **0.0045 dB** of the Shannon limit, with simulations at block length 10⁷ landing within 0.04 dB at bit error rate 10⁻⁶ [7]. These numbers required irregularity: the champion code used variable degrees {2, 3, 6, 7, 15, 20, 50, 70, 100, 150, 400, 900, 2000, 3000, 6000, 8000}. The theory also explains *why* irregularity helps — degree-2 variable nodes are cheap but create fragile cycles, while a heavy tail of high-degree nodes provides the anchoring that pulls the iteration toward certainty — a phenomenon formalized through *stability conditions* and *EXIT chart* analysis.

A crucial subtlety: density evolution is exact only in the cycle-free (large-girth) limit. Finite Tanner graphs contain cycles, so practical belief propagation is only *approximately* the tree algorithm. The celebrated concentration theorems of Richardson and Urbanke show the finite-length behavior concentrates around the tree prediction, with deviations decaying exponentially in block length — which is why long LDPC codes (n ≈ 10⁴–10⁷) match their thresholds so faithfully while short codes suffer.

### 4.2 Belief Propagation: The Sum–Product Algorithm on the Tanner Graph

Belief propagation — Pearl's algorithm (1988), reinvented for coding by Gallager's probabilistic decoding, MacKay–Neal, and Kschischang–Frey–Loeliger's factor-graph formulation — operates by local message passing [5]. The algorithm is best understood as five repeating phases:

1. **Initialization.** Each variable node v is seeded with the channel log-likelihood ratio L_ch(v) = log(P(y_v|0)/P(y_v|1)).
2. **Variable-to-check.** Node v sends to each neighboring check c the sum of its channel observation and all *other* incoming check messages — extrinsic information only, never echoing a message back along its own edge.
3. **Check-to-variable.** Check node c computes, for each neighbor v, the constraint's implication: given the beliefs about all other neighbors, what does "even parity" require of v? The exact sum–product update uses the tanh rule above; the hardware-friendly *min-sum* approximation replaces it with a sign product and a minimum of magnitudes.
4. **Belief update.** Each variable node forms its total belief (channel plus all check messages) and makes a tentative hard decision.
5. **Syndrome check.** If x̂H^T = 0, decoding terminates; otherwise iterate, typically 10–50 rounds.

Each iteration costs O(n) operations because of sparsity, so the total is linear in block length times a constant — the decisive practical advantage over the exponential ML decoder. The following Python sketch captures the min-sum core used in 5G NR hardware:

```python
import numpy as np

def min_sum_decode(H, llr, max_iter=50):
    """Min-sum BP decoding of an LDPC code with parity-check matrix H."""
    m, n = H.shape
    # messages[v -> c] stored sparsely along edges
    edges = [(v, c) for v in range(n) for c in range(m) if H[c, v]]
    msg_vc = {e: llr[e[0]] for e in edges}   # init with channel LLRs
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        msg_cv = {}
        for c in range(m):
            nbrs = [v for v in range(n) if H[c, v]]
            for v in nbrs:
                others = [u for u in nbrs if u != v]
                mags = [abs(msg_vc[(u, c)]) for u in others]
                sgn = np.prod([np.sign(msg_vc[(u, c)]) or 1 for u in others])
                msg_cv[(c, v)] = sgn * (min(mags) if mags else 0.0)
        for v in range(n):
            nbrs = [c for c in range(m) if H[c, v]]
            for c in nbrs:
                msg_vc[(v, c)] = llr[v] + sum(msg_cv[(cc, v)]
                                              for cc in nbrs if cc != c)
        belief = [llr[v] + sum(msg_cv[(c, v)] for c in range(m) if H[c, v])
                  for v in range(n)]
        xhat = [0 if b >= 0 else 1 for b in belief]
        if all(sum(xhat[v] * H[c, v] for v in range(n)) % 2 == 0
               for c in range(m)):
            return xhat  # syndrome satisfied: valid codeword
    return xhat
```

The min-sum approximation overestimates message magnitudes; practical decoders add *offset* or *normalization* corrections. This single loop — with layered scheduling and quantized fixed-point arithmetic — is what decodes gigabits per second in 5G baseband chips.

### 4.3 Channel Polarization: A Deterministic Route to Capacity

Where LDPC theory leans on ensembles and concentration, polar codes offer something the random-coding tradition never did: a *fully explicit* capacity-achieving construction. The engine is the polarization recursion. For the binary erasure channel BEC(ε), the Bhattacharyya parameters of the two synthesized channels have closed forms:

```
Z(W₂^(1)) = 2ε − ε²        (degraded: worse than the original)
Z(W₂^(2)) = ε²             (upgraded: better than the original)
```

and Z(W₂^(1)) + Z(W₂^(2)) ≤ 2ε, with strict inequality unless ε ∈ {0, 1}. Repeating the transform, the parameters polarize: for any δ > 0, the fraction of channels with Z(W_N^(i)) < 2^(−N^β) (for any β < 1/2) tends to I(W), while the complementary fraction tends to channels with Z near 1 [8]. Arıkan proved the block error probability under successive cancellation satisfies P_e(N, R) ≤ 2^(−N^β) for any β < 1/2 and R < I(W) [3] — the first explicit code family with a proven capacity-achieving guarantee *and* polynomial complexity.

> **Theorem (Arıkan's polarization theorem, 2009).** For any binary-input discrete memoryless channel W and any R < I(W), there exists a sequence of polar codes of block length N = 2ⁿ and rate at least R whose block error probability under successive cancellation decoding is O(2^(−N^β)) for any β < 1/2, with encoding and decoding complexity O(N log N).

The elegance is real, but the asymptotic exponent β < 1/2 is relatively weak: at short and moderate block lengths, plain SC decoding underperforms LDPC and turbo codes substantially. The practical fix, and the reason polar codes survived contact with standardization, was *list decoding*.

### 4.4 Successive Cancellation List Decoding and the CRC Trick

Tal and Vardy (2015, arXiv 2011) generalized SC decoding to maintain L concurrent decoding paths: at each information bit, every path splits into two hypotheses, and a pruning step retains the L most likely survivors, with complexity O(L·N log N) [6 in spirit; Tal–Vardy]. With moderate L (8–32), SCL performance approaches maximum-likelihood decoding. But the decoder must finally *choose* one path — and the most likely path is not always the transmitted one.

The decisive engineering insight was concatenation with a cyclic redundancy check: a CRC-aided polar code appends r CRC bits to the information, and the SCL decoder selects among surviving paths the one passing the CRC. This simple outer code converts near-ML list performance into actual ML-beating finite-length performance, particularly at the short block lengths (N ≤ 1024) of control signaling. The 5G NR standard adopted CRC-aided polar codes with list size L = 8 for the physical broadcast channel and downlink/uplink control information — the first large-scale deployment of polar codes anywhere [4].

A useful comparison of the two decoding paradigms:

| Aspect | LDPC belief propagation | Polar SCL decoding |
|---|---|---|
| Complexity | O(n · iterations), highly parallel | O(L·N log N), inherently serial |
| Capacity proof | Ensemble thresholds via density evolution | Explicit construction, polarization theorem |
| Finite-length strength | Excellent at n ≥ 10³ (long blocks) | Excellent at n ≤ 10³ with CRC aid |
| Error floor | Trapping sets / small absorbing sets | No floor; distance spectrum well behaved |
| Hardware | Parallel, high throughput | Serial bottleneck; simplified SCL variants help |
| 5G NR role | Data channels (eMBB) | Control channels (PBCH, PDCCH, PUCCH) |

### 4.5 The 5G NR Engineering Compromise: Two Base Graphs, One Standard

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

The theoretical claims above rest on published, reproducible results:

- **Density evolution thresholds.** Richardson and Urbanke's framework [2] gives exact (infinite-length) thresholds; the Chung et al. rate-1/2 design achieves threshold within 0.0045 dB of the binary-input AWGN Shannon limit, and finite-length simulation at n = 10⁷ reaches within 0.040 dB at BER 10⁻⁶ [7]. No other practical code family has demonstrated a smaller published gap for long blocks.
- **Polarization rate.** Arıkan and Telatar (2009) sharpened the error exponent: P_e ≤ 2^(−N^β) for any β < 1/2 under SC decoding [8]. Later work (Korada–Şaşoğlu–Urbanke) showed larger kernels push β arbitrarily close to 1, at the cost of construction complexity.
- **SCL finite-length performance.** Tal and Vardy's simulations show SCL with L = 32 essentially matching ML decoding for N = 2048; CRC-aided variants outperform the best short LDPC codes at N ≤ 512 in the waterfall region, which is precisely the 3GPP control-channel regime [4][Tal–Vardy].
- **Waterfall versus error floor.** LDPC codes exhibit steep waterfall curves — BER dropping orders of magnitude over ~0.5 dB — followed, in poorly designed ensembles, by error floors caused by trapping sets. Polar codes under SCL show no comparable floor, a structural advantage of their deterministic distance spectrum.

These results jointly justify the 5G NR architecture: each code is deployed in the regime where its theory is strongest.


## 6 Limitations

The synthesis is not a victory lap; both families carry genuine limitations.

- **LDPC codes** have no *explicit* capacity-achieving construction at finite length — the guarantees are ensemble-average, and a randomly drawn code can be atypically bad (though concentration makes this exponentially unlikely). Short-block performance (n < 1000) is mediocre, error floors from trapping sets require careful graph design (girth optimization, ACE constraints), and the encoding problem, while solved by the IRA/accumulator structures, adds architectural complexity.
- **Polar codes** suffer at moderate block lengths under plain SC decoding — the β < 1/2 exponent is weak, and the polarization is incomplete at N = 1024. SCL decoding is serial and hard to parallelize, making terabit-throughput implementations substantially harder than for LDPC. The 5G reliability sequence is a *universal* compromise, not channel-optimal; per-channel construction (e.g., Gaussian approximation) performs better but was deemed too complex to standardize.
- **Common to both:** the theory assumes memoryless channels with perfect channel-state information; fading, asynchrony, and imperfect CSI erode the published gaps. Finite-blocklength theory (Polyanskiy–Poor–Verdú) shows the true fundamental limits at n ≈ 10³ lie meaningfully below Shannon capacity, so "within 0.0045 dB of capacity" overstates practical closeness at short blocks.


## 7 Conclusion

Shannon's 1948 theorem posed a challenge that took sixty years to answer in full: exhibit *explicit, efficiently decodable* codes that achieve capacity. The answer came twice. LDPC codes answered through the probabilistic method — random sparse graphs, iterative decoding, and the analytic triumph of density evolution — culminating in irregular ensembles within 0.0045 dB of the Shannon limit [7]. Polar codes answered through pure construction — channel polarization, an idea of startling simplicity, with the first explicit capacity-achieving proof in the field's history [3].

That the 5G NR standard deploys both — quasi-cyclic LDPC base graphs for data, CRC-aided polar codes for control — is not redundancy but recognition that the two answers are complementary: one excels where parallelism and long blocks matter, the other where short blocks and provable construction matter. The Shannon limit, once a distant existence proof, is now an engineering specification. The remaining frontier — finite-blocklength fundamental limits, non-binary and spatially-coupled extensions, and decoding at terabit scale — will likely be settled by the same two traditions, refined rather than replaced.
