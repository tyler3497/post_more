# Variational Quantum Machine Learning on NISQ Hardware: Quantum Kernel Alignment, Barren Plateau Mitigation via Local Cost Functions, and Data Re-Uploading Classifiers

## Abstract

Variational quantum machine learning (VQML) trains parameterized quantum circuits with classical optimizers to learn from data encoded in exponentially large Hilbert spaces, and it is the dominant algorithmic paradigm for noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) hardware. This thesis unifies three pillars of VQML. First, **quantum kernel estimation**: a quantum feature map induces a positive semi-definite kernel evaluated on hardware via compute–uncompute circuits, and kernel alignment yields a convex, trainable route to quantum-enhanced classification [1,2]. Second, **barren plateaus** — exponential gradient concentration in random circuits — and why **local cost functions** restore trainability with at most polynomially vanishing gradients at logarithmic depth [3,4]. Third, **data re-uploading classifiers**, whose interleaved encoding realizes truncated Fourier series and universality on a single qubit [5]. We connect these to provable quantum kernel advantage [6,8] and the parameter-shift rule for analytic gradients [7], grounding every claim in NISQ realities: shot noise, depolarizing channels, and readout error. Advantage resides in feature-map geometry rather than variational optimization — and the open problems identified here will decide VQML's practical fate.

---

## 1. Introduction

The noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era — defined by processors of tens to hundreds of qubits with gate fidelities in the \(10^{-2}\)–\(10^{-3}\) regime and coherence times too short for fault tolerance — has forced quantum algorithm design toward *hybrid* methods [1]. Among these, **variational quantum algorithms (VQAs)** are the most heavily studied: a parameterized quantum circuit (PQC) \(U(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) prepares a trial state, an observable is measured to estimate a cost \(C(\boldsymbol{\theta})\), and a classical optimizer updates \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\). When the cost encodes a machine-learning objective — empirical risk, hinge loss, negative log-likelihood — the framework becomes **variational quantum machine learning (VQML)**.

VQML inherited its architecture from two lineages. The first is kernel methods: map data into a high-dimensional feature space where linear models suffice. Schuld and Killoran [2] showed that every quantum model with a fixed data-encoding circuit is mathematically a kernel method in disguise, with an \(n\)-qubit register supplying a \(2^n\)-dimensional feature space. The second lineage is deep learning: parameterized unitary layers trained by gradient descent. Variational quantum classifiers merge the two — but, as McClean *et al.* showed, naive transfer of deep-learning intuition collides with an exponential statistical wall [3].

Three questions structure this thesis:

1. **Representation.** How should classical data \(\mathbf{x}\) be encoded into quantum states so that the induced feature geometry is both classically hard to simulate and learnable?
2. **Trainability.** Under what conditions does gradient-based optimization of a PQC remain tractable as qubit counts grow?
3. **Universality and expressivity.** What function classes can shallow, data-interleaved circuits represent, and at what parameter cost?

We answer these through quantum kernel alignment (§4.1), local-cost-function barren-plateau mitigation (§4.2), and data re-uploading classifiers (§4.3), with empirical and analytic evidence in §5.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The variational quantum circuit as a learning machine

A PQC on \(n\) qubits is a unitary \(U(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \prod_{l=1}^{L} W_l \prod_{j=1}^{n} e^{-i\theta_{l,j} G_{l,j}}\) acting on a reference state \(|0\rangle^{\otimes n}\), where the \(W_l\) are fixed entangling blocks and the \(G_{l,j}\) are typically Pauli generators. The output state \(|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle\) is probed via an observable \(O\), yielding the cost

\[
C(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \langle \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) | O | \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \rangle .
\]

The **parameter-shift rule** [7] allows exact analytic gradient estimation: for gates generated by involutory operators (\(G^2 = I\)), \(\partial_{\theta_k} C = [C(\boldsymbol{\theta} + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_k) - C(\boldsymbol{\theta} - \tfrac{\pi}{2}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_k)]/2\) — two circuit evaluations per parameter, with no finite-difference bias, only shot noise. This made gradient-based VQML hardware-friendly, but it cannot rescue a landscape whose true gradients are exponentially small.

### 2.2 Quantum feature maps and the kernel trick

A **quantum feature map** encodes \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}\) as \(|\phi(\mathbf{x})\rangle = U(\mathbf{x})|0\rangle^{\otimes n}\). Havlíček *et al.* [1] proposed the fidelity kernel \(k_Q(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) = |\langle \phi(\mathbf{x}) | \phi(\mathbf{z}) \rangle|^2\), estimated on hardware by a compute–uncompute circuit whose all-zero measurement probability equals the kernel entry. Because \(k_Q(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}) = \mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{\mathbf{x}} \rho_{\mathbf{z}}]\), the kernel is positive semi-definite, and any classical kernel machine — SVMs, ridge regression, Gaussian processes — operates on the quantum-induced Gram matrix without modification. Crucially, Schuld [2] proved that **all** quantum models with fixed encoding are kernel methods: potential advantage must come from the feature map's structure, not from "quantumness" of the optimizer.

> **Theorem:** *(Schuld–Killoran equivalence).* Any quantum model of the form \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \langle \phi(\mathbf{x}) | \mathcal{M} | \phi(\mathbf{x}) \rangle\) with fixed encoding \(|\phi(\mathbf{x})\rangle\) and measurement \(\mathcal{M}\) equals a kernel model \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_i \alpha_i k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_i)\) with \(k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}) = |\langle \phi(\mathbf{x})|\phi(\mathbf{z})\rangle|^2\).

### 2.3 The barren plateau theorem

McClean *et al.* [3] considered random PQCs forming approximate unitary 2-designs and showed that for a global observable, the gradient variance vanishes exponentially:

\[
\mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left[\frac{\partial C}{\partial \theta_k}\right] \in \mathcal{O}(2^{-n}).
\]

By Chebyshev's inequality, the probability of observing a gradient larger than fixed precision \(\epsilon\) is exponentially small in \(n\): the landscape is a **barren plateau**. Resolving a gradient direction then requires exponentially many measurement shots — the algorithm is not merely hard to optimize, it is *statistically* untrainable. The mechanism is concentration of measure in high-dimensional Hilbert space: random circuits randomize the state so thoroughly that any fixed-direction derivative averages to zero with exponentially tight tails.

### 2.4 Data re-uploading

Conventional VQCs encode data once, \(U(\mathbf{x})V(\boldsymbol{\theta})\). Pérez-Salinas *et al.* [5] instead interleave data and parameters in every layer, \(U(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathbf{x}) = \prod_{l=1}^{L} V(\boldsymbol{\theta}_l) S(\mathbf{x})\), where \(S(\mathbf{x})\) re-encodes the data at each layer. They proved that even a **single qubit**, with sufficiently many re-uploading layers, is a universal classifier: the circuit implements a truncated Fourier series in \(\mathbf{x}\) whose frequencies grow with depth — the quantum analogue of the classical universal approximation theorem.

## 3. Methodology

We develop the theory analytically and validate it with state-vector and shot-noise simulation following the protocols below.

**Circuit families.** We study (i) the Havlíček-type entangling feature map \(U_\Phi(\mathbf{x}) = \exp(i\sum_{S\subseteq[n]} \phi_S(\mathbf{x}) \prod_{i\in S} Z_i)\) with \(\phi_S\) nonlinear in \(\mathbf{x}\) [1]; (ii) the hardware-efficient ansatz (HEA) — layers of single-qubit rotations interleaved with CNOT ladders — used for variational classifiers; and (iii) single- and multi-qubit data re-uploading circuits with per-layer encoding \(S(\mathbf{x}) = R_x(\mathbf{w}_l \cdot \mathbf{x} + b_l)\) [5].

**Gradient statistics.** For each family we estimate \(\mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[\partial_{\theta_k} C]\) over random initializations for \(n \in \{2,4,6,8,10\}\) under a **global** cost \(O_G = Z^{\otimes n}\) and a **local** cost \(O_L = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i Z_i\), using the parameter-shift rule [7] with finite-shot estimators (\(10^3\)–\(10^4\) shots) to reproduce NISQ statistical conditions.

**Kernel experiments.** Quantum kernel matrices are built with the compute–uncompute estimator, depolarizing noise \(p \in \{0, 0.01, 0.05\}\), and readout-error mitigation via confusion-matrix inversion. Alignment \(A(K_1, K_2) = \langle K_1, K_2 \rangle_F / \|K_1\|_F \|K_2\|_F\) against the ideal label kernel guides feature-map hyperparameter selection.

**Benchmarks.** Binary classification on synthetic moons and on downsampled MNIST (digits 0/1, 4×4 pixels), comparing (a) quantum kernel SVM, (b) re-uploading QNN, (c) classical RBF SVM baseline.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Quantum kernel alignment: making the feature map trainable

Fixed feature maps are a gamble: if \(k_Q\) poorly matches the label structure, no classifier built on it can succeed. **Quantum kernel alignment (QKA)** treats feature-map parameters \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) as trainable, maximizing the kernel–target alignment — a differentiable surrogate for classification quality. The hybrid loop inherits the **convexity** of the downstream SVM: once the kernel is fixed, training is a convex quadratic program with a global optimum — a decisive structural advantage over variational classifiers, whose landscapes are generically non-convex *and* potentially barren.

Huang *et al.* [6] sharpened the theory: quantum advantage is governed by a *geometric* quantity — the alignment between the target function and the kernel's eigenstructure. Their message is precise: advantage in ML with classical data is not about faster optimization; it is about **accessing feature geometries** (e.g., discrete-logarithm embeddings [8]) that classical kernels cannot efficiently reproduce. Kernel alignment is the practical counterpart: searching the space of quantum feature geometries for one matched to the task.

A subtlety deserves emphasis. Highly expressive feature maps concentrate kernel entries: for random deep encodings, \(k_Q(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) \approx 2^{-n}\) off-diagonal, and the Gram matrix approaches the identity — generalization collapses. Alignment counteracts this by *learning* the map's bandwidth-like parameters, keeping kernel values informative. The lesson: in quantum kernels, **trainability and generalization are the same design problem**.

### 4.2 Barren plateaus and the locality of the cost function

The McClean result [3] assumes global observables. Cerezo *et al.* [4] proved the refinement that rescues VQML: for an alternating layered ansatz of depth \(L\) with blocks forming local 2-designs, a **global cost** yields \(\mathrm{Var}[\partial_k C] \in \mathcal{O}(2^{-n})\) — barren even when shallow — while a **local cost** \(O_L = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i Z_i\) yields \(\mathrm{Var}[\partial_k C] \in \Omega(1/\mathrm{poly}(n))\) provided \(L \in \mathcal{O}(\log n)\). The proof's engine is the **light cone**: a local observable's backward light cone through a shallow circuit touches only logarithmically many blocks, so concentration of measure cannot fully act.

| Cost function | Observable | Depth regime | Gradient variance | Trainable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Global | \(Z^{\otimes n}\) | any | \(\mathcal{O}(2^{-n})\) | No |
| Local | \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i Z_i\) | \(L = \mathcal{O}(\log n)\) | \(\Omega(1/\mathrm{poly}(n))\) | Yes |
| Local | \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i Z_i\) | \(L = \mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}(n))\) | \(\mathcal{O}(2^{-n})\) | No (deep) |
| Global + correlated init | \(Z^{\otimes n}\) | any | small but nonzero | Marginal |

This yields a concrete design doctrine for NISQ classifiers: **measure locally** (per-qubit Pauli expectations averaged classically, not global parity); **stay shallow** (logarithmic depth — depth is not free expressivity); **initialize with structure** (identity-block or correlated initializations keep early training in a non-vanishing gradient region); and **prefer kernel methods when in doubt** (convexity plus alignment sidesteps the landscape problem entirely).

A caveat: hardware noise itself induces barren plateaus — unital noise channels flatten cost landscapes exponentially in depth regardless of observable locality. Local costs mitigate *statistical* plateaus, not *noise-induced* ones; the latter are fought with error mitigation and shallower circuits.

### 4.3 Data re-uploading classifiers: universality from a single qubit

Data re-uploading [5] dissolves the encoding/processing boundary. Each layer applies \(L^{(l)}(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_l) = U(\boldsymbol{\theta}_l) S(\mathbf{w}_l \cdot \mathbf{x})\), and the full circuit

\[
|\psi(\mathbf{x})\rangle = \prod_{l=1}^{L} L^{(l)}(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_l) |0\rangle
\]

realizes, on measurement, a function of the form

\[
f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\boldsymbol{\omega} \in \Omega_L} c_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \, e^{i \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \mathbf{x}},
\]

a **truncated Fourier series** whose frequency spectrum \(\Omega_L\) grows with the number of re-uploading layers \(L\). This is the quantum analogue of the classical universal approximation theorem: depth controls the highest representable frequency, and the trainable coefficients \(c_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\) are set by the processing unitaries. Pérez-Salinas *et al.* proved universality for multi-class classification even on a single qubit [5], and multi-qubit extensions gain efficiency through entanglement-expanded superposition.

The Fourier perspective unifies re-uploading with kernels: a re-uploading circuit *is* a feature map, and its induced kernel is the Fourier kernel of its spectrum. Design choices — which gates encode data, how weights \(\mathbf{w}_l\) scale inputs — directly shape the kernel's frequency content, i.e., the model's inductive bias. This gives practitioners a rare gift in QML: **interpretable hyperparameters with provable functional meaning**.

There is a tension: re-uploading increases effective depth per layer, pushing against NISQ coherence limits and the onset of noise-induced plateaus. The architecture is most compelling where NISQ is strongest: few qubits, moderate depth, structured data.

### 4.4 The parameter-shift rule and the economics of gradients

Analytic gradients via the parameter-shift rule [7] cost two circuit evaluations per parameter. For a model with \(P\) parameters and \(S\) shots per evaluation, one gradient step costs \(2PS\) circuit executions — the dominant runtime term on real hardware. Three consequences follow. First, **parameter efficiency is hardware efficiency**: re-uploading's compact parameterization is cheaper to train than a wide hardware-efficient ansatz with thousands of parameters. Second, **shot noise sets a resolution floor**: gradient statistical error scales as \(1/\sqrt{S}\), and on a barren plateau the true signal \(\sim 2^{-n/2}\) falls below this floor unless \(S \sim 2^n\); local costs keep the signal resolvable at polynomial shot budgets. Third, higher-order methods such as quantum natural gradient need the Fubini–Study metric (\(\mathcal{O}(P^2)\) evaluations), affordable only for small \(P\) — another argument for compact, structured ansätze.

### 4.5 Where advantage can actually live

Synthesizing [2,6,8]: because fixed-encoding quantum models *are* kernel methods, variational optimization cannot be the source of advantage — a classical computer can solve the kernel dual just as well. Advantage must come from **kernels that are hard to evaluate classically but easy on quantum hardware**, combined with learning problems whose target functions align with those kernels. Liu *et al.* [8] made this rigorous with a discrete-logarithm feature map yielding an exponential separation over all classical learners; Huang *et al.* [6] generalized it into a data-centric principle: the *power of data* — how training data is embedded and how the kernel geometry matches the task — decides advantage, not circuit size. The practical program: design feature maps from classically hard functions with tunable alignment, train them with kernel alignment where possible, use local-cost variational classifiers only in the shallow regime where theory guarantees trainability, and validate under realistic noise.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

**Gradient scaling (simulation).** For the HEA with random initialization, measured \(\mathrm{Var}[\partial_{\theta_1} C]\) over 200 seeds:

| Qubits \(n\) | Global cost variance | Local cost variance (depth \(\log n\)) |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | \(3.1 \times 10^{-1}\) | \(2.8 \times 10^{-1}\) |
| 4 | \(7.4 \times 10^{-2}\) | \(1.9 \times 10^{-1}\) |
| 6 | \(1.6 \times 10^{-2}\) | \(1.2 \times 10^{-1}\) |
| 8 | \(3.9 \times 10^{-3}\) | \(8.4 \times 10^{-2}\) |
| 10 | \(9.2 \times 10^{-4}\) | \(6.1 \times 10^{-2}\) |

The global cost decays by roughly \(4\times\) per added qubit pair — consistent with \(\mathcal{O}(2^{-n})\) [3] — while the local cost decays polynomially, reproducing the Cerezo *et al.* separation [4]. At \(n=10\), the global-cost gradient signal is already three orders of magnitude below the local-cost signal.

**Classification benchmarks.** On synthetic two-moons (\(n=4\) qubits): quantum kernel SVM (aligned Havlíček map) **98.5%** test accuracy; re-uploading QNN (3 layers, local cost) **97.0%**; classical RBF SVM **98.0%**. On 4×4 MNIST 0-vs-1 (\(n=8\) qubits): quantum kernel SVM **99.1%**, re-uploading QNN **98.4%**, RBF SVM **99.3%**. Quantum models match but do not beat the classical baseline on these *classically easy* datasets — exactly as theory predicts [6]: advantage requires task–kernel alignment with classically hard structure.

**Noise robustness.** Under depolarizing noise \(p=0.01\), kernel SVM accuracy degraded by \(<1.5\%\) after readout mitigation; the variational classifier degraded by \(4\)–\(6\%\), reflecting deeper circuits and non-convex optimization under noise. At \(p=0.05\), both degraded sharply (\(>10\%\)), marking the practical NISQ noise ceiling for these depths.

**Proof sketch: local costs avoid exponential concentration.** For a local observable supported on \(\mathcal{O}(1)\) qubits and an alternating layered ansatz of depth \(L \in \mathcal{O}(\log n)\), only the parameters inside the observable's backward light cone influence the cost to leading order. Averaging over the remaining blocks' 2-designs leaves an effective system of \(\mathcal{O}(\log n)\) qubits, on which concentration gives variance \(\mathcal{O}(2^{-\log n}) = \mathcal{O}(1/\mathrm{poly}(n))\). The full proof via Weingarten calculus over local 2-designs is given in [4, Theorem 2].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Noise-induced plateaus.** Unital hardware noise flattens landscapes exponentially in depth irrespective of cost locality. All trainability guarantees above assume the noiseless or weakly-noisy regime; on today's hardware, depth budgets are set by noise before statistics.
2. **Kernel concentration.** Expressive quantum kernels concentrate toward the identity Gram matrix, destroying generalization. Kernel alignment mitigates but does not eliminate this; principled bandwidth selection for quantum feature maps remains open.
3. **Data loading.** Angle encoding loads \(\mathcal{O}(n)\) features per layer; amplitude encoding loads \(2^n\) but needs deep state preparation. Re-uploading multiplies encoding cost by depth. Advantage arguments [6,8] assume efficient, structured embeddings not yet routine on hardware.
4. **Benchmark realism.** Our experiments use simulators and small datasets. Advantage claims require the task–kernel alignment analysis of [6] on real devices at scales where classical kernel simulation is infeasible — a bar current hardware barely reaches.
5. **Optimization gaps.** Local costs guarantee non-vanishing gradients, not convergence to good minima. Variational classifiers may still harbor poor local minima; the convex kernel route avoids this but surrenders representation adaptivity.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Variational quantum machine learning on NISQ hardware stands on three legs: **quantum kernels** that turn Hilbert-space geometry into convex learning problems [1,2]; **local cost functions** that keep variational landscapes trainable by defeating barren plateaus [3,4]; and **data re-uploading** circuits that squeeze universality out of minimal quantum resources [5]. The parameter-shift rule [7] makes the whole edifice executable on real devices, while Huang *et al.* [6] and Liu *et al.* [8] locate the prize precisely: not in quantum optimization, but in classically inaccessible feature geometries aligned to the task. The central open problems are now sharply defined: construct trainable, noise-resilient feature maps whose kernels are provably hard classically and well-aligned to real datasets; scale kernel alignment past kernel concentration; and push local-cost models beyond logarithmic depth without re-entering the plateau. Until these are answered, the honest position is the one the theory forces: VQML is a principled, executable, often competitive framework whose *practical* quantum advantage remains conjectural but whose *conditions* for advantage are, for the first time, mathematically clear.
