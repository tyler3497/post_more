# Mechanistic Interpretability of Transformers: Circuits, Superposition, and Causal Dissection of Learned Computation

![Induction head circuit](/thesis/ths_1788690604000_4e55-0.webp)

## 1. Introduction

Most analyses of large language models treat the trained network as a black box whose behavior is characterized statistically: benchmarks are run and failure modes are cataloged from the outside. *Mechanistic interpretability* inverts this posture. It asks whether the artifacts produced by gradient descent can be **reverse-engineered** — decomposed into human-comprehensible algorithms implemented by identifiable components (attention heads, MLP neurons, and directions in the residual stream), in the same way an engineer might decompile a binary [1].

This program is ambitious because the object of study is genuinely alien. A transformer was not *designed* to implement induction or modular arithmetic; it *learned* to do these things under the pressure of a loss function, and the learned implementation need bear no resemblance to a human-engineered solution. The central methodological bet is that these implementations are nevertheless **legible** — that gradient descent converges to solutions built from a modest vocabulary of reusable circuit motifs [2, 3].

This thesis surveys the state of that research program. We begin with the mathematical foundations that make attention circuits legible at all: the decomposition of attention heads into *QK circuits* (which determine where information moves) and *OV circuits* (which determine what information moves), and the interpretation of the transformer as a network of paths through the residual stream [1]. We then examine four pillars of empirical progress. **First**, induction heads — the two-layer circuit identified by Olsson et al. as the mechanistic substrate of in-context learning [2]. **Second**, superposition — the geometric hypothesis explaining why neurons are *polysemantic*, representing more features than the network has dimensions [3]. **Third**, sparse autoencoders (SAEs), the dictionary-learning technique that decomposes superposed activations into interpretable, monosemantic features, from one-layer toys to production models with 34 million features [5, 6, 8]. **Fourth**, the causal intervention toolkit — activation patching, path patching, and attribution methods — that separates what information *exists* in a representation from what the model actually *computes* [4, 9]. As a capstone case study, we examine grokking: the reverse-engineering of delayed generalization into a Fourier-multiplication algorithm, yielding continuous progress measures for an apparently discontinuous phase change [7]. We close with an honest accounting of limits — faithfulness, completeness, and the scaling wall facing frontier reasoning models.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Residual Stream and Path Expansion

The modern transformer is usefully understood through the **residual stream** perspective [1]. Each token position maintains a vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$, the *residual stream*, which every layer reads from and writes to additively:

$$x_{\ell+1} = x_\ell + \sum_{h} \text{Attn}_h(x_\ell) + \text{MLP}(x_\ell)$$

Because all communication between components passes through this shared bus, and because attention heads and MLPs write to it additively, the network's computation can be expanded as a sum over **paths** — sequences of components through which information flows from input embedding to output logits. This path-expansion view is what makes *path patching* [4] well-defined: one can isolate the causal contribution of a specific path $h_1 \to h_2 \to \text{logits}$ by swapping the activations along that path with activations from a counterfactual run.

### 2.2 QK and OV Circuits

Elhage et al. [1] showed that in an attention-only transformer (no MLPs), each head can be decomposed exactly into two bilinear circuits. The **QK circuit** $W_Q W_K^\top$ determines the *attention pattern* — which source tokens the head attends to — while the **OV circuit** $W_O W_V$ determines the *output* — what information is written to the residual stream given the attended tokens. Concretely, for a head at destination position $d$ attending to source position $s$:

$$A_{d,s} \propto x_d^\top W_Q W_K^\top x_s \qquad \text{(QK: where)}$$
$$\text{out}_d = \sum_s A_{d,s}\, W_O W_V x_s \qquad \text{(OV: what)}$$

This decomposition is the field's Rosetta Stone. It converts an opaque $d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{model}}$ parameter block into two interpretable questions: *which pairs of tokens does this head compare?* and *what does it copy when it finds a match?* When MLPs are present the analysis is more involved — MLPs act as key-value memories and complicate clean decomposition [1] — but the residual-stream and circuit vocabulary remains the lingua franca.

> **Theorem (Circuit Completeness, informal):** In an attention-only transformer, the full input-output map factorizes into a sum over virtual attention heads — compositions of QK and OV circuits across layers — each of which can be studied independently as a bilinear operator on pairs of residual-stream states [1].

### 2.3 Polysemanticity: The Problem Statement

Empirically, individual neurons in trained networks rarely correspond to single clean concepts. A neuron in an early vision model may fire for both *cat faces* and *car fronts*; a neuron in a language model may fire for both *Python code* and *French poetry* [3]. This **polysemanticity** is the central obstacle to a naive "one neuron, one concept" interpretability program. The superposition hypothesis [3] explains *why* it arises and, crucially, predicts the conditions under which it is optimal: when features are sparse and more numerous than dimensions, representing them in **superposition** — as almost-orthogonal directions in a compressed space — is the loss-optimal strategy. SAEs [5, 6] are the constructive answer: learn an overcomplete dictionary that *undoes* superposition.

## 3. Methodology

Mechanistic interpretability is defined as much by its **methods** as by its results. The field's toolkit has converged on a layered protocol that moves from correlational observation to causal confirmation [4, 9].

### 3.1 Observation: Probes, Logit Lens, and Direct Attribution

- **Linear probes** train classifiers on activations to test whether information (e.g., part-of-speech, board state) is *linearly decodable* from a representation. They establish an upper bound on available information — never what the model uses.
- The **logit lens** applies the final layer norm and unembedding matrix $W_U$ to intermediate residual states, projecting them into vocabulary space: $\text{LogitLens}(h) = \text{LayerNorm}(h) W_U$. It reveals *when* during the forward pass a prediction crystallizes.
- **Direct logit attribution** decomposes the final logits into additive contributions from each head and MLP, identifying components with large direct effects.

### 3.2 Causal Intervention: Activation and Path Patching

The decisive move is **intervention**. In *activation patching* [4, 9], the model is run twice: once on a *clean* prompt (where it exhibits the behavior of interest) and once on a *corrupted* prompt (designed to break it). Activations from the corrupted run are then spliced into the clean run at a targeted site, and the degradation of a behavioral metric — typically the **logit difference** $\text{LD} = \text{logit}(\text{correct}) - \text{logit}(\text{incorrect})$ — measures that site's causal importance. *Path patching* refines this to isolate specific computational paths $h \to R$ between a head $h$ and downstream components $R$, distinguishing a head's direct effect on logits from effects mediated through other heads [4]. Best practices demand careful choice of corruption distribution, because patching with out-of-distribution activations can produce misleading attributions [9].

### 3.3 Dictionary Learning: Sparse Autoencoders

Where patching *tests* hypotheses about components, SAEs *discover* the units of analysis. An SAE is trained on activations $x$ from a target site (residual stream, MLP output) with an overcomplete dictionary $d_{\text{dict}} \gg d_{\text{model}}$ and a sparsity penalty:

$$\mathcal{L}(x) = \|x - \hat{x}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|f\|_1, \qquad f = \text{ReLU}(W_{\text{enc}}x + b_{\text{enc}}), \quad \hat{x} = W_{\text{dec}} f + b_{\text{dec}}$$

with decoder columns constrained to unit norm after each step [5]. The learned features $f_i$ tend to be **monosemantic** — each firing for one interpretable concept — and their decoder directions can be used for causal steering (adding $\alpha \, d_i$ to the residual stream) to verify that the interpretation is behaviorally real [6].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Induction Heads and the Mechanism of In-Context Learning

In-context learning (ICL) — the ability of a model to infer a task from examples in its prompt without weight updates — is arguably the most consequential emergent capability of transformers. Olsson et al. [2] proposed a concrete mechanistic account: **induction heads**. An induction head implements the pattern-completion operation $[A][B]\ldots[A] \to [B]$: given a sequence where token $A$ was previously followed by $B$, and $A$ appears again, the head predicts $B$.

The circuit requires two layers. A **previous-token head** in layer 1 copies information about token $t-1$ into the residual stream at position $t$ (its OV circuit shifts content backward). The **induction head** in layer 2 then uses its QK circuit to match the current token $A$ against positions whose *shifted* content equals $A$ — i.e., positions *following* an earlier $A$ — and its OV circuit copies the token at the matched position (which is $B$). Formally, with the layer-1 shift operator $S$:

$$A^{\text{(ind)}}_{d,s} \propto x_d^\top W_Q^{\text{ind}} {W_K^{\text{ind}}}^\top (W_O^{\text{prev}} W_V^{\text{prev}} x_{s-1}),$$

so the head attends to positions $s$ where the token *preceding* $s$ matches the current token — attending to the $B$ that followed the earlier $A$.

![Superposition geometry](/thesis/ths_1788690604000_4e55-1.webp)

The evidence is unusually strong for this field [2]: (i) a discrete **phase change** in training loss coincides with the formation of induction heads; (ii) an *in-context learning score* jumps at the same point; (iii) ablating induction heads degrades ICL specifically; and (iv) a "smeared key" modification letting single-layer models form induction heads causes the phase change to appear where it otherwise never does.

### 4.2 Superposition and Polysemanticity

If induction heads show what circuits look like when we can see them, **superposition** explains why we so often cannot [3]. Elhage et al. studied toy models — a linear encoder/decoder with a ReLU bottleneck trained to reconstruct sparse inputs $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ through $m < n$ dimensions: $h = Wx$, $x' = \text{ReLU}(W^\top h + b)$. The striking finding is a **phase transition** in feature geometry as input sparsity increases. At low sparsity, the model represents only the most important features with dedicated (orthogonal) directions. Past a critical sparsity, it represents *all* features in superposition: feature directions arrange into symmetric configurations (e.g., a pentagon for 5 features in 2 dimensions) that maximize pairwise angles, tolerating small interference because features rarely co-occur.

```python
# Toy model of superposition (Elhage et al. 2022, simplified)
import torch, torch.nn as nn

class ToySuperposition(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_features=5, n_hidden=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.W = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(n_hidden, n_features) * 0.5)
        self.b = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(n_features))
    def forward(self, x):                      # x: sparse, shape (batch, n_features)
        h = x @ self.W.T                       # compress into bottleneck
        return torch.relu(h @ self.W + self.b) # decode with tied weights + ReLU filter
```

The ReLU is load-bearing: it implements *nonlinear filtering* that suppresses the small interference terms superposition introduces. The theory predicts, and experiments confirm, that superposition is the optimal compression strategy precisely when features are **sparse** — which natural data features (specific entities, syntactic constructions, rare concepts) overwhelmingly are. The implication is sobering: in frontier models, the "true" features almost certainly outnumber the dimensions by orders of magnitude, so neuron-level interpretability is structurally doomed. Any faithful account must operate at the level of **directions**, not neurons [3, 5].

### 4.3 Sparse Autoencoders for Feature Decomposition

SAEs turn the superposition diagnosis into a treatment [5]. By training an overcomplete sparse dictionary on real model activations, we ask gradient descent to *invert* the superposition the model itself learned. Bricken et al. [5] demonstrated the recipe on a one-layer transformer's MLP activations ($d_{\text{model}} = 512$, dictionary sizes 4,096 and 131,072): the recovered features were dramatically more interpretable than neurons, including features for Arabic script, DNA sequences, and specific code patterns. Key engineering details proved essential: **decoder-column unit-norm constraints** (preventing the dictionary from gaming the L1 penalty by shrinking activations and growing weights), **dead-feature resampling** (reinitializing features that never fire), and large-scale activation harvesting.

![Sparse autoencoder architecture](/thesis/ths_1788690604000_4e55-2.webp)

Templeton et al. [6] then answered the field's central open question — *does this scale?* — by training SAEs with up to **34 million features** on the middle-layer residual stream of Claude 3 Sonnet, a production-scale model. The results were striking: features for the Golden Gate Bridge, for brain sciences, for *sycophancy* and *deception* — abstract, safety-relevant concepts, not surface patterns. Crucially, these features proved **causally steerable**: clamping the Golden Gate Bridge feature high caused the model to obsess over the bridge unprompted; clamping a sycophancy feature altered the model's social behavior [6]. Multilingual and even multimodal features emerged despite text-only training, suggesting SAEs recover genuinely conceptual directions. Independent replications on GPT-2 and Pythia confirmed the recipe's generality [8].

| SAE variant | Sparsity mechanism | Notable property |
|---|---|---|
| ReLU + L1 (Bricken et al. 2023) [5] | L1 penalty on activations | Baseline; shrinkage bias |
| Gated SAE | Separate gating/magnitude paths | Reduces shrinkage |
| JumpReLU / TopK | Hard sparsity constraint | Exact L0 control; popular at scale |
| BatchTopK | Top-k across batch | Prevents feature collapse |

### 4.4 Grokking and Progress Measures

The most complete reverse-engineering triumph to date concerns not a component but a **training dynamic**: grokking, the phenomenon where a small transformer trained on modular addition memorizes the training set, plateaus, then suddenly generalizes long after [7]. Nanda et al. fully reverse-engineered the learned algorithm: the model embeds tokens via discrete Fourier modes and uses trigonometric identities to convert addition into rotation about a circle,

$$\text{logit}(c) \;\propto\; \sum_{k \in K} \cos\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi k}{p}(a + b - c)\right),$$

constructively interfering at the correct answer $c^* = a+b \bmod p$. Four lines of evidence — periodicity in weights, Fourier-sparse neuron-logit maps, degree-2 polynomial structure of individual neurons, and ablation of key frequencies reducing performance to chance — confirm the mechanism. Armed with the true algorithm, the authors defined **progress measures** (restricted and excluded loss) that vary *smoothly* through training, revealing three phases — memorization, circuit formation, cleanup — and showing that the "sudden" generalization is merely the visible surface of gradual circuit amplification followed by the removal of memorizing components [7]. Grokking is thus the proof of concept for the field's deepest promise: *emergence is not magic; it is compressed mechanism, and mechanism can be uncompressed.*

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

The methodological gold standard is the **end-to-end circuit**: a behavior explained, component by component, with each link causally validated. The canonical example is Wang et al.'s circuit for **indirect object identification (IOI)** in GPT-2 small [4]. Given "When Mary and John went to the store, John gave a bottle of milk to __", the model must output *Mary* (the indirect object), not the repeated *John*. Path patching revealed a three-stage algorithm mirroring the human solution: **duplicate-token heads** detect the repeated name, **S-inhibition heads** suppress attention to the duplicated subject, and **name-mover heads** copy the remaining name to the output. The identified circuit — 26 heads across the 144-head model — recovers the large majority of the logit difference, and targeted ablations of each head class produce exactly the predicted behavioral deficits [4].

Quantitative validation protocols have since matured [9]:

1. **Logit-difference recovery**: a proposed circuit is sufficient if patching *only* its components from clean into corrupted runs restores task performance; it is necessary if ablating it destroys performance.
2. **Faithfulness vs. completeness**: a circuit is *faithful* if it captures the model's true computation on the task distribution, and *complete* if no behaviorally relevant component is omitted. Most published circuits are faithful-but-incomplete — a critical caveat [9].
3. **SAE steering as causal proof**: a feature's interpretation is validated by clamping its activation and observing the predicted behavioral shift, as in the Golden Gate Bridge demonstrations [6].

![Activation patching flowchart](/thesis/ths_1788690604000_4e55-3.webp)

> **Theorem (Patching identifies direct effects):** Under the path-expansion of the residual stream, path patching the edge set $h \to R$ measures the *total* causal effect of head $h$ on components $R$ along direct paths, excluding effects mediated through other attention heads — provided the corruption distribution preserves the marginal statistics of non-patched paths [4, 9].

## 6. Limitations

The field's honesty about its limits is one of its strengths, and the limits are severe.

**Faithfulness and completeness.** There is no general certificate that a published circuit is the computation the model *actually* performs rather than a computation *consistent* with the observations. Correlational methods (probes, logit lens) systematically over-attribute; even causal methods depend on the choice of corruption distribution, and out-of-distribution patching can fabricate effects [9]. Completeness — the claim that *nothing important was missed* — is essentially never established for large models.

**The scale gap.** The most rigorous results (induction heads, IOI, grokking) concern small models or narrow tasks. SAEs have scaled to frontier models [6], but they decompose *activations*, not *computations*: knowing that a "deception" direction exists does not reveal the circuit that uses it. Automated circuit discovery (ACDC, attribution patching) finds candidate subgraphs at scale, but many fail causal validation — the precision-recall tradeoff is unresolved.

**Superposition strikes back.** SAEs assume features are sparse linear directions. If the model's true ontology includes nonlinear features, features in superposition *across* the SAE's own dictionary, or distributed representations with no sparse basis, dictionary learning will return a plausible-but-wrong factorization. Recent work on the geometry of SAE features [6] shows significant feature splitting and absorption artifacts.

**Reasoning models and beyond.** Frontier systems increasingly rely on long chains of thought, tool use, and test-time search — computational structures for which the circuit vocabulary (heads, residual paths) is at best a partial description. The 2025 circuit-tracing work on Claude represents the deepest attempt yet to trace multi-step reasoning, but it remains labor-intensive and incomplete.

## 7. Conclusion

Mechanistic interpretability has, in five years, traveled from a mathematical framework for attention-only toys [1] to the extraction of millions of steerable, human-labeled features from production language models [6]. Its core achievements are real and cumulative: a precise circuit vocabulary (QK/OV decomposition, path expansion); a causal account of in-context learning via induction heads [2]; a geometric theory of why neurons are polysemantic [3]; a scalable dictionary-learning technology for undoing superposition [5, 6, 8]; a causal intervention toolkit that separates computation from correlation [4, 9]; and a demonstration — grokking — that even emergent phase changes yield to reverse engineering [7].

The open question is whether this toolkit *composes* into understanding of frontier systems that reason, plan, and act. The pessimistic view holds that interpretability faces a fundamental scaling wall: as models grow, the number of circuits grows, superposition deepens, and human verification bandwidth stays fixed. The optimistic view holds that the field is still in its "microscope" era — that SAEs, automated circuit discovery, and AI-assisted interpretation will compound, and that today's labor-intensive case studies are the training data for tomorrow's automated science of learned computation.
