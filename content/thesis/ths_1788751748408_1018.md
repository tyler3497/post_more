# Verified Cryptography with HACL* and EverCrypt: Low* to C Extraction, Vale Assembly Proofs, and Agile Cryptographic APIs with Side-Channel Resistance

## Abstract

Formal verification of cryptographic software has matured from research artifact to industrial practice. This thesis investigates the HACL* and EverCrypt projects — large-scale efforts built atop the F* dependently-typed programming language — that produce verified, high-performance cryptographic libraries through Low*-to-C extraction and Vale assembly verification. We present the full verification pipeline: functional specifications of modern primitives (ChaCha20, Poly1305, Curve25519, AES-GCM), memory-safety and functional-correctness proofs in F*, side-channel resistance under a constant-time model enforced by the secret independence type discipline, extraction of idiomatic C via KreMLin, and hand-optimized assembly proofs in Vale. We further analyze EverCrypt's multiplexing and algorithmic agility, its compositional proof architecture, and its deployment footprint in Firefox, WireGuard, and TLS stacks. Empirical results demonstrate performance parity with the best unverified implementations (e.g., OpenSSL, libsodium) at 2.7M Merkle-tree insertions/sec and line-rate TLS. Limitations — proof engineering costs, compiler trust, and microarchitectural side channels — are discussed with candor.

## 1 Introduction

Cryptographic libraries are the *linchpin* of internet security and, simultaneously, a perennial source of catastrophic vulnerabilities. Heartbleed, BERserk, and a litany of timing attacks on AES and ECDSA implementations have demonstrated that testing alone cannot secure code paths that are both mathematically subtle and adversarially scrutinized [5]. This thesis argues — and demonstrates — that the alternative of **formal verification**, once dismissed as too laborious for performance-critical code, is now viable at industrial scale. The evidence is the **EverCrypt** provider and its **HACL*** core: over 124,000 verified lines of specifications, code, and proofs producing over 29,000 lines of C and 14,000 lines of assembly, already shipping in Mozilla Firefox, the WireGuard VPN, the Linux kernel's Zinc successor effort, and the Tezos blockchain [2][3].

The technical spine of this achievement is the **F\*** language, a dependently-typed functional programming language and proof assistant, together with two domain-specific languages embedded in it: **Low\***, a first-order subset of F\* that compiles to idiomatic C via the **KreMLin** toolchain, and **Vale**, a verified assembly language embedded in F\*. The architecture establishes a single trusted specification per algorithm, against which both the C implementation and the hand-tuned assembly implementation are verified, enabling *multiplexing* — selecting per-platform the fastest verified implementation — and *algorithmic agility* — selecting per-connection the algorithm with the best security/performance trade-off — without duplicating proofs [2][4].

This thesis makes the following contributions:

1. A systematic exposition of the F\*-Low\*-Vale verification stack, including the memory model, the effect system, and the KreMLin extraction pipeline.
2. A treatment of *secret independence* — the type-level discipline by which HACL* enforces constant-time, side-channel-resistant coding — with its semantic justification and empirical validation.
3. An analysis of EverCrypt's API design: how abstraction and zero-cost generic programming make multiplexing and agility provably safe for verified clients.
4. A critical evaluation of empirical results: proof-engineering cost, performance relative to OpenSSL and libsodium, and adoption in production.
5. A candid account of limitations: the trusted computing base, microarchitectural channels beyond the threat model, and the economics of proof maintenance.

The remainder of this work proceeds as follows. Section 2 surveys the background: F\*, KreMLin, Vale, and the constant-time model. Section 3 details our methodology: how one proves a cipher correct, memory-safe, and side-channel resistant. Section 4 deep-dives into extraction semantics, Vale's assembly proofs, the agility/multiplexing API, and secret-independence enforcement. Section 5 reports empirical results and proof statistics. Section 6 enumerates limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The F\* Programming Language and Proof Assistant

F\* (pronounced "F star") is a general-purpose functional programming language with dependent types, effects, and an SMT-backed program verifier [6]. Unlike Coq or Isabelle/HOL, which separate the programming language from the proof language, F\* unifies them: programs carry refinements, preconditions, and postconditions in their types, and the verifier discharges proof obligations through a combination of SMT solving (typically Z3) and tactic-based manual proof. The design philosophy is *proof by typing*: whenever the SMT solver can discharge a goal automatically, no manual proof script is required; manual intervention is reserved for obligations the solver cannot handle alone [1].

F\*'s effect system is central to cryptographic verification. Pure computations live in the `Tot` effect; computations that manipulate mutable state live in the `ST` (state) effect, indexed by a precondition and postcondition over the heap; computations with failure live in other effects. This discipline makes the *frame condition* explicit: a function type records precisely which memory regions it may modify, enabling modular reasoning about buffers — the bread and butter of cryptographic code.

### 2.2 Low\* and KreMLin: From Verified Code to C

**Low\*** is a first-order subset of F\* restricted to what can be compiled to C: no closures, no higher-order functions, no garbage-collected allocation — only stack frames, heap buffers, and machine integers of fixed width [1]. A Low\* program is an ordinary F\* program whose types certify *memory safety* (no out-of-bounds access, no use-after-free, no double-free) and *functional correctness* (the C-level loop implements the high-level mathematical specification). **KreMLin** (now succeeded by KaRaMeL) then extracts the Low\* program into idiomatic, human-readable C99: F\* machine integers become `uint32_t`/`uint64_t`, buffers become pointer+length pairs, and the extracted code is annotated with the original specification as comments [1][2].

The extraction is *type-preserving in spirit* if not in proof: the guarantees are established at the F\*/Low\* level, and extraction must faithfully preserve semantics. The extraction pipeline is part of the trusted computing base — a point we revisit in Section 6.

### 2.3 Vale: Verified Assembly

Some primitives cannot reach peak performance in portable C. AES-NI, PCLMULQDQ, AVX2 vectorized Curve25519 field arithmetic, and carryless-multiplication-based GHASH demand hand-written assembly. **Vale** is a domain-specific language for *verifying* such assembly: the programmer writes assembly-like procedures annotated with pre/postconditions in F\*, and Vale generates both the assembly text and a proof, in F\*, that the assembly satisfies its specification [4][7]. Vale procedures carry *taint labels* tracking which registers and memory hold secrets, and the verifier enforces that control flow and memory-access patterns are independent of secret taints — a machine-code-level enforcement of constant-time discipline.

Crucially, Vale and Low\* share F\* as their verification infrastructure, so a Vale assembly routine and a Low\* C routine can be proved against the *same* functional specification. This shared specification is the linchpin of EverCrypt's multiplexing: the provider can dispatch to either implementation with no change to the client's proof obligations [2][7].

### 2.4 The Constant-Time Model of Side-Channel Resistance

HACL* and EverCrypt prove resistance against a *timing and cache side-channel adversary* formalized as follows: the adversary observes the sequence of memory addresses accessed and the sequence of branches taken, but not the *values* of secret data. A program is **secret independent** (constant-time) if, for any two executions that agree on all public inputs, the observable trace — branches and addresses — is identical [8]. This model captures remote timing attacks and local cache-timing attacks of the FLUSH+RELOAD and PRIME+PROBE families insofar as they exploit secret-dependent control flow or addressing; it does *not* capture power analysis, electromagnetic emanation, or speculative-execution channels (Section 6).

> Theorem: (Secret independence implies trace equivalence.) Let *P* be a Low\* program well-typed under the secret-independence discipline with public inputs *p* and secret inputs *s₁, s₂*. Then the execution traces of *P(p, s₁)* and *P(p, s₂)* are equal: same branch outcomes, same addresses accessed.

The proof, mechanized in F\*, proceeds by a logical relation over the Low\* operational semantics: well-typedness implies the program's control flow and addressing depend only on public data [1][8].

| Primitive | Family | Verified property | Implementation language |
|---|---|---|---|
| ChaCha20 | Stream cipher | Functional correctness, memory safety, secret independence | Low\* → C |
| Poly1305 | MAC | Functional correctness, memory safety, secret independence | Low\* → C / Vale ASM |
| Curve25519 | ECDH | Functional correctness, memory safety, secret independence | Low\* → C / Vale ASM |
| AES-GCM | AEAD | Functional correctness, memory safety, secret independence | Vale ASM (AES-NI/PCLMUL) |
| SHA-2 / SHA-3 | Hash | Functional correctness, memory safety | Low\* → C |
| Ed25519 | Signatures | Functional correctness, memory safety, secret independence | Low\* → C |

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology follows the *specification-driven* workflow pioneered by the Project Everest consortium [1][2][3]:

1. **Mathematical specification.** Write a pure, obviously-correct functional specification of the primitive in F\* — e.g., ChaCha20 as a function over mathematical sequences of words, the quarter-round as a pure function, the full cipher as 20 rounds of a state transformation. The specification is executable and is tested against RFC test vectors *before* any proof effort begins.
2. **Low\*-level implementation.** Write the performance-oriented implementation using mutable buffers and loops, with a loop invariant relating the imperative state to the functional specification at each step.
3. **Proof of memory safety and correctness.** Type the implementation in Low\*; discharge spatial obligations (buffer disjointness, liveness, bounds) via the effect system and functional-correctness obligations (loop invariants imply the specification) via SMT, with manual lemmas where the solver stalls.
4. **Secret-independence audit.** Ensure all secret data carries the secret label; the type system rejects secret-dependent branches and secret-dependent indexing. Conditional selects on secrets are permitted (constant-time select idiom).
5. **Extraction and differential testing.** Extract with KreMLin; run the extracted C against RFC test vectors and against reference implementations (e.g., libsodium, OpenSSL) on randomized inputs.
6. **Vale acceleration (where needed).** For hot primitives, write the Vale procedure, prove it against the *same* specification, and register both implementations under EverCrypt's multiplexing interface.
7. **Agile API integration.** Expose the primitive through EverCrypt's indexed API, proving that dispatch (by algorithm and by implementation) preserves the specification for all choices [2].

A methodological principle worth emphasizing: *specifications are written once, implementations many times*. The functional specification of Curve25519 scalar multiplication is shared by the portable C implementation, the 64-bit optimized C implementation, and the Vale AVX2 implementation; each is proved against the same spec, and multiplexing correctness follows from this sharing rather than from pairwise equivalence proofs [2].

```fstar
(* Low* sketch: ChaCha20 quarter round on a state buffer *)
let quarter_round (st: buffer UInt32.t) (a b c d: UInt32.t)
  : Stack unit
  (requires fun h -> live h st /\ length st = 16)
  (ensures fun h0 _ h1 -> modifies (loc st) h0 h1 /\
    as_seq h1 st == Spec.chacha_quarter_round (as_seq h0 st) a b c d)
=
  let x_a = st.(a) in
  let x_b = st.(b) in
  ...
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Low\* to C Extraction: Semantics Preservation and the Memory Model

Low\*'s memory model is a *hyper-stack*: a region-based discipline in which stack frames are pushed and popped in LIFO order and heap regions are explicitly allocated and freed. Every buffer carries a *liveness* precondition — `live h b` asserts buffer `b` is allocated in heap `h` — and every update carries a *modifies* clause naming the exact footprint. KreMLin's extraction maps this model onto C99 in a remarkably direct fashion [1]:

- `buffer UInt32.t` becomes `uint32_t*` with a companion length where statically known;
- `push_frame`/`pop_frame` become C block scopes with stack-allocated arrays;
- machine integers `UInt32.t` become `uint32_t`, with F\*'s wrapping arithmetic matching C's unsigned semantics bit-for-bit;
- top-level Low\* functions become C functions with identical signatures modulo type erasure.

Semantics preservation is argued *informally but carefully*: KreMLin is a syntactic translation over a restricted subset, and its output is validated by compiling and running the full RFC test-vector suite plus differential fuzzing against OpenSSL. The deeper guarantee is architectural: because Low\* forbids the features that make C semantics treacherous (pointer arithmetic beyond buffer bounds is untypeable; aliasing is controlled by disjointness preconditions), the extracted C lies in a *safe subset* of C99 where the compiler's latitude for miscompilation is minimized [1][6].

One subtlety deserves emphasis: **the C compiler is in the TCB**. Verified C source compiled with an unverified compiler yields unverified machine code in the strictest sense. In practice, the project validates with CompCert-style reasoning for critical paths and with extensive testing otherwise; Vale closes the gap entirely for assembly routines by verifying the final machine code (Section 4.2).

### 4.2 Vale Assembly Proofs: Taint Tracking at Machine Level

Vale's innovation is to treat assembly as a *verified* artifact rather than a trusted one [4][7]. A Vale procedure is written in a structured assembly DSL:

```fstar
(* Vale sketch: AES-NI round, with taint-annotated operands *)
procedure aesenc_round (state: vaesenc_reg, key: secret vaesenc_reg)
  requires old(state) == Spec.aes_state /\ taint(key) = Secret
  ensures  state == Spec.aesenc old(state) old(key)
{
  vaesenc state, key;   // hardware AES round; constant-time by construction
}
```

The Vale verifier checks three things: (i) the register/memory effects match the declared footprint; (ii) the functional postcondition holds, via weakest-precondition reasoning discharged in F\*; (iii) **no secret-tainted value influences control flow or addressing** — branches, jumps, and memory operands must be public-tainted. Vale's second incarnation (POPL 2019) reimplemented the language *inside* F\* rather than on Dafny, enabling the deep C/ASM integration that EverCrypt exploits: a Vale procedure's specification is an F\* predicate, directly comparable — indeed, *identical* — to the Low\* function's specification [7].

The payoff is concrete. Vale-verified AES-GCM using AES-NI and PCLMULQDQ, and Vale-verified Curve25519 using AVX2, match or beat OpenSSL's hand-tuned assembly while carrying machine-checked proofs of correctness, memory safety, and side-channel resistance [2][7].

### 4.3 Agile Cryptographic APIs: Multiplexing Without Proof Duplication

EverCrypt's central design problem: a *cryptographic provider* must offer agility (multiple algorithms: e.g., AES-GCM *or* ChaCha20-Poly1305 for AEAD) and *multiplexing* (multiple implementations per algorithm: e.g., portable C *or* Vale AES-NI for AES-GCM, chosen at runtime by CPUID detection) — while presenting clients a single, simple, verified interface [2].

The solution is **zero-cost generic programming with abstraction**:

1. Each implementation is verified against a shared *algorithm specification* indexed by a configuration parameter.
2. The provider API is *abstract*: clients see `aead_encrypt : key -> nonce -> plaintext -> ciphertext` with a specification quantifying over the configuration, never naming the implementation.
3. Dispatch is proved correct *once*: a multiplexing lemma shows that for every configuration, the dispatched implementation refines the shared spec. Adding a new implementation requires proving it against the shared spec — the client's proof is untouched.

Because F\*'s type abstraction hides the configuration index from verified clients, agility is *free for clients*: a TLS implementation verified against the abstract AEAD interface remains verified regardless of which algorithm or implementation is selected at runtime [2]. This is a genuine advance over the state of the art, where agility historically meant `#ifdef` forests with per-configuration testing and no shared correctness argument.

### 4.4 Secret Independence: A Type Discipline for Constant-Time Code

HACL*'s most far-reaching methodological contribution may be its treatment of side channels as a *typing* problem [1][8]. Secret integers have a distinct abstract type; the API offers no operation that branches on them or uses them as indices. The programmer who needs a secret-dependent choice uses the constant-time select:

```fstar
val select : b:bool{sec} -> x:uint64 -> y:uint64 -> Tot uint64
(* implemented as branch-free bitwise masking; b secret, timing public *)
```

The discipline is *permissive where safe, restrictive where dangerous*: arithmetic on secrets is unrestricted (ALU timing is data-independent on all supported platforms), while control flow and addressing on secrets are rejected at type-checking time. The semantic theorem (Section 2.4) connects typing to trace equivalence, and the project validates the claim empirically with **dudect**-style statistical timing analysis on the compiled binaries [8].

Notably, the discipline extends through extraction: KreMLin maps secret integers to ordinary C integers, so the *C compiler* could in principle introduce secret-dependent branches (e.g., via clever optimizations of masking idioms). The project mitigates this by (a) inspecting generated assembly for hot routines, (b) running timing measurements on final binaries, and (c) preferring Vale for the most timing-sensitive code, where the verified artifact *is* the machine code [4][7][8].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Scale of verification.** EverCrypt comprises over 124,000 lines of verified F\*, Low\*, and Vale code (specifications, implementations, and proofs), producing over 29,000 lines of C and 14,000 lines of assembly [2]. HACL* alone covers the modern primitive suite — NaCl-style AEADs, Curve25519/Ed25519, SHA-2/SHA-3, HMAC, HKDF — each with machine-checked proofs of memory safety, functional correctness, and secret independence [1].

**Performance.** The decisive empirical question — *does verification cost performance?* — is answered in the negative:

| Primitive | EverCrypt/HACL* | Best unverified baseline | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| ChaCha20-Poly1305 | 2.87 cycles/byte | libsodium 2.91 cpb | 0.99× |
| Curve25519 (64-bit) | ~159k cycles | OpenSSL ~170k cycles | 0.94× |
| AES-GCM (AES-NI) | 0.63 cpb | OpenSSL 0.62 cpb | 1.02× |
| SHA-256 | 7.4 cpb | OpenSSL 7.1 cpb | 1.04× |

(Figures representative of the S&P 2020 evaluation on x86-64; exact numbers vary by microarchitecture [2].)

**Case studies.** Two verified clients validate the API design at scale: a record-layer implementation for a secure network protocol achieving *line-rate* throughput, and a Merkle-tree library deployed in the Tezos blockchain sustaining **2.7 million insertions per second** [2]. Mozilla Firefox ships HACL* for its Curve25519 and ChaCha20-Poly1305 implementations; WireGuard uses HACL*-verified primitives; the miTLS verified TLS stack builds directly on EverCrypt [2][3].

**Proof effort.** The project reports roughly *person-decades* of proof engineering across the consortium — a sobering datum. However, the marginal cost of adding a primitive has fallen sharply as libraries of verified lemmas (bignum arithmetic, field arithmetic, block-cipher modes) accumulated; HACLxN's generic SIMD framework, for instance, verifies vectorized code *once* and instantiates it per platform [9].

**Side-channel validation.** Beyond the type discipline, compiled artifacts were subjected to statistical timing analysis (dudect methodology): no secret-dependent timing was detected across millions of measurements for the AEAD and ECDH paths [8]. This closes the loop between the formal model and the shipped binary.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires enumerating what the proofs do *not* cover:

1. **Trusted computing base.** F\* itself, the Z3 SMT solver, KreMLin/KaRaMeL extraction, the C compiler, the assembler, and the hardware are all trusted. A soundness bug in Z3 or a miscompilation by GCC could invalidate guarantees — the Vale path narrows but does not eliminate this, since the Vale *verifier* and assembler remain trusted [6][7].
2. **Microarchitectural channels.** The threat model covers timing and cache behavior at the architectural level. It excludes power/EM analysis, and — critically — *speculative execution* channels of the Spectre/Meltdown families, which postdate the original HACL* threat model and can leak secrets through transient execution invisible to the trace model [8].
3. **Specification fidelity.** Proofs show the code implements *the specification*; they cannot show the specification captures the *intent*. A bug in the RFC-derived spec (e.g., a misread of the Poly1305 clamping requirements) would be faithfully, verifiably wrong. Test vectors mitigate but do not eliminate this risk [1].
4. **Denial of service and liveness.** The proofs establish safety, not liveness or resource bounds; algorithmic-complexity attacks are out of scope.
5. **Proof economics.** Person-decades of effort and ongoing maintenance as F\* and its libraries evolve. Verification debt is real: every upstream change to F\* or Z3 can break proofs, and the project maintains continuous integration over the full proof corpus to detect such breakage [2][9].
6. **Agility hazards.** EverCrypt proves each algorithm correct, but *protocol-level* agility (downgrade attacks, cross-protocol confusion) remains the client's responsibility — verified primitives do not make a verified protocol [2].

## 7 Conclusion

HACL* and EverCrypt demonstrate that *verified, high-performance, side-channel-resistant cryptography at industrial scale is achievable today*. The recipe — a dependently-typed host language (F\*), a C-targeting subset (Low\*) with faithful extraction (KreMLin), a verified assembly language (Vale) sharing the same specifications, a type discipline for secret independence, and an API architecture that makes multiplexing and agility zero-cost for verified clients — has produced a provider that matches the fastest unverified code, ships in major products, and rules out entire classes of vulnerabilities by construction [1][2][3][4].

The deeper lesson is methodological: verification scales when *specifications are shared assets* — written once, reused across C and assembly implementations, across platforms via generic SIMD frameworks, and across clients via abstract agile APIs. The remaining frontiers — speculative-execution-aware models, verified compilers in the loop (à la CompCert integration), and reducing proof cost through better automation — define the next decade of verified cryptography. The trajectory from HACL* (CCS 2017) through EverCrypt (S&P 2020) to HACLxN (CCS 2020) suggests that trajectory is steep and accelerating [1][2][9].
