# Quantum Neural Networks: Expressivity, the Quantum Natural Gradient, and Generalization Bounds for Parameterized Quantum Circuits

## Abstract

Parameterized quantum circuits (PQCs) are the workhorse ansatz of near-term quantum machine learning, yet three questions still govern their prospects: how expressive are they, can they be trained at scale, and do they generalize to unseen data? This thesis unifies three theoretical strands that answer these questions. First, we develop the quantum natural gradient — steepest descent with respect to the Fubini–Study metric, the real part of the quantum geometric tensor — as a geometry-aware optimizer for variational circuits. Second, we quantify expressivity through the effective quantum dimension and the pseudo-dimension, establishing polynomial capacity bounds and a sharp transition in the geometry of parameter space. Third, we prove generalization bounds that scale as the square root of the trainable gate count over the sample size, refined by data-encoding-aware complexity measures. We connect these strands through the barren plateau phenomenon, which exposes an expressivity–trainability trade-off at the heart of variational quantum models, and close with open problems in optimizer design, architecture search, and non-uniform generalization theory.

---

## 1. Introduction

A *quantum neural network* (QNN) is, in its most common mathematical incarnation, a parameterized quantum circuit (PQC) whose rotation angles are tuned by a classical optimizer to minimize a cost function measured on quantum hardware. This hybrid variational template underlies the variational quantum eigensolver, the quantum approximate optimization algorithm, and a growing family of quantum classifiers and generative models. Its appeal is obvious: PQCs are native to noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices, they can in principle access regions of Hilbert space unreachable by classical parameterizations, and their gradients can be estimated on hardware via the parameter-shift rule. Yet three foundational questions have resisted clean answers: *expressivity* (what functions can a given circuit family represent?), *trainability* (can a classical optimizer find good parameters at scale?), and *generalization* (does low training error imply low test error?).

This thesis argues that these three questions are best studied together, because the answers interact. The optimizer that best respects the geometry of quantum states is the **quantum natural gradient** (QNG) introduced by Stokes, Izaac, Killoran, and Carleo [1], which replaces Euclidean steepest descent with steepest descent on the projective Hilbert space equipped with the Fubini–Study metric. Trainability is threatened by **barren plateaus**, the exponentially vanishing gradients identified by McClean, Boixo, Smelyanskiy, Babbush, and Neven [2] for circuits that approximate unitary 2-designs. And generalization, long treated as a purely classical concern, admits rigorous quantum analogues: uniform bounds in terms of trainable gate counts and encoding-dependent complexity measures. Our contributions are a self-contained synthesis of these strands, new comparative analysis of their assumptions, and a map of the open problems that remain.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Parameterized quantum circuits

A PQC on *n* qubits is a unitary of the form

$$U(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \prod_{l=1}^{L} U_l(\boldsymbol{\theta}_l)\, W_l,$$

where each $U_l(\boldsymbol{\theta}_l) = \exp(-i\,\theta_l V_l)$ is generated by a Hermitian operator $V_l$ with tunable angle $\theta_l$, and the $W_l$ are fixed entangling unitaries. The model output is typically an expectation value $C(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \langle 0|U^\dagger(\boldsymbol{\theta})\, O\, U(\boldsymbol{\theta})|0\rangle$ for some observable $O$, or a vector of measurement probabilities used as class scores. Gradients are obtained without finite differences through the parameter-shift rule, which expresses $\partial_k C$ as a linear combination of cost evaluations at shifted parameters — the quantum analogue of backpropagation.

### 2.2 From classical natural gradient to quantum geometry

Classical natural gradient descent, introduced by Amari [3], corrects ordinary gradient descent for the information geometry of the model family: the update $\boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta\, F^{-1}\nabla \mathcal{L}$ uses the Fisher information matrix $F$ so that steps are steepest with respect to the Kullback–Leibler divergence rather than the Euclidean norm. Stokes *et al.* [1] transported this idea to quantum states, where the relevant geometry is not statistical but the Fubini–Study geometry of projective Hilbert space. The metric that appears is the real part of the **quantum geometric tensor**, a structure whose significance for parameter estimation long predates machine learning.

### 2.3 Measures of capacity

Two complementary capacity measures anchor the expressivity discussion. The *effective quantum dimension*, proposed by Haug, Bharti, and Kim [4], counts the number of independent directions in state space accessible to a PQC via the rank of the quantum Fisher information matrix. The *pseudo-dimension*, adapted to quantum circuits by Caro and Datta [5], is a combinatorial measure for probabilistic concept classes — the quantum analogue of the VC dimension for real-valued outputs — that yields polynomial upper bounds in circuit depth and gate count.

### 2.4 Generalization in quantum machine learning

Classical learning theory bounds the gap between empirical and expected risk using uniform complexity measures. Caro, Datta, and co-workers [6] proved the first rigorous bounds of this kind for variational quantum models, showing that the generalization error scales as $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T/N})$ where $T$ is the number of trainable local gates and $N$ the number of training samples. Subsequent work refined this picture: Schuld, Sweke, and Meyer [7] derived *encoding-dependent* bounds via Rademacher complexity and metric entropy, showing that how classical data is loaded into the circuit — the data-encoding strategy — directly controls the complexity of the induced function class. Abbas *et al.* [8] introduced the *effective dimension*, a Fisher-information-based capacity measure, and showed empirically that QNNs can exhibit larger effective dimension than classical feedforward networks with the same parameter count, suggesting a concrete sense in which quantum models can be more expressive per parameter.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is theoretical and unifying rather than experimental: we reconstruct the central definitions and theorems of the three strands in a common notation, compare their assumptions side by side, and derive the connections between them.

- **Quantum geometric tensor.** For a parameterized pure state $|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle$, define $G_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \langle \partial_i \psi | \partial_j \psi \rangle - \langle \partial_i \psi | \psi \rangle \langle \psi | \partial_j \psi \rangle$. Its real part $g = \operatorname{Re} G$ is the Fubini–Study metric tensor; the quantum Fisher information matrix is $F = 4g$.
- **Quantum natural gradient.** The update $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta\, g(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)^{+} \nabla \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)$, where $g^{+}$ denotes the pseudo-inverse, follows the steepest descent direction on the manifold of quantum states [1]. Because the full metric is expensive, we study the block-diagonal approximation for layered ansätze.
- **Expressivity analysis.** We evaluate the effective quantum dimension $G_C(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \operatorname{rank}\, F(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ averaged over parameter space [4], and the pseudo-dimension bounds of [5], relating both to circuit depth, gate count, and entangling structure.
- **Generalization analysis.** We apply the uniform-convergence framework: bound the Rademacher complexity or covering numbers of the PQC function class, then convert to high-probability bounds on $\mathrm{gen} = R - \hat{R}_S$ [6][7].

> **Theorem (Quantum natural gradient, Stokes et al. [1]).** *Let $|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle$ be a smoothly parameterized pure state and $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ a differentiable loss. The update $\boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta\, g(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{-1} \nabla \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ moves in the direction of steepest descent with respect to the Fubini–Study distance on projective Hilbert space. For layered circuits, a block-diagonal approximation to $g$ can be computed with cost linear in the number of layers.*

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Quantum Geometric Tensor and the Fubini–Study Metric

The quantum geometric tensor was introduced by Provost and Vallée [9] as the Riemannian structure on manifolds of quantum states. For pure states it decomposes into a symmetric real part — the Fubini–Study metric, governing infinitesimal distances $ds^2 = 1 - |\langle \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) | \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta} + d\boldsymbol{\theta}) \rangle|^2$ — and an antisymmetric imaginary part, the Berry curvature. The metric is invariant under reparameterization of the state: two parameterizations that generate the same curve in Hilbert space induce the same geometry, which is precisely why the natural gradient is invariant to reparameterization of $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ while ordinary gradient descent is not.

In the PQC setting, each parameter $\theta_k$ generates motion along a direction $|\partial_k \psi\rangle$ in the tangent space at $|\psi\rangle$. The metric $g_{ij}$ measures the overlaps of these tangent vectors, so $g$ encodes which parameters move the state a lot (large eigenvalues) and which are redundant (zero eigenvalues). This is the geometric content that QNG exploits: preconditioning by $g^{-1}$ normalizes steps by their actual effect on the quantum state rather than their coordinate size.

### 4.2 The Quantum Natural Gradient in Practice

Naively, QNG requires estimating the full $p \times p$ metric tensor ($p$ parameters) and inverting it — prohibitive for deep circuits. The key algorithmic contribution of [1] is a **block-diagonal approximation**: for layered ansätze, each block corresponds to one layer's parameters, and the block entries can be estimated from quantities accessible via the parameter-shift rule. The resulting optimizer interpolates between vanilla gradient descent (diagonal blocks ignored) and full QNG, at a per-iteration overhead that is constant in depth for fixed layer width.

The following Python sketch illustrates the block-diagonal construction for a layered circuit, where each layer's metric block is built from state overlaps estimated by parameter-shifted expectation values:

```python
import numpy as np

def block_diagonal_qng_step(params, grad, metric_blocks, eta=0.01, reg=1e-3):
    # params: flat parameter vector; grad: Euclidean gradient of the loss
    # metric_blocks: list of per-layer Fubini-Study blocks g^{(l)}
    natural = np.zeros_like(grad)
    offset = 0
    for block in metric_blocks:
        d = block.shape[0]
        g_reg = block + reg * np.eye(d)          # Tikhonov regularization
        seg = grad[offset:offset + d]
        natural[offset:offset + d] = np.linalg.solve(g_reg, seg)
        offset += d
    return params - eta * natural                 # steepest descent on the manifold
```

Empirically, QNG converges in far fewer iterations than Adam or vanilla gradient descent on variational eigensolver and QNN benchmarks [1], because it automatically rescales directions in which the state moves slowly — exactly the directions where Euclidean gradients stall.

### 4.3 Expressivity: Effective Dimension and Quantum Geometry

Haug, Bharti, and Kim [4] define the *effective quantum dimension* of a circuit as the expected rank of the quantum Fisher information matrix over parameter space,

$$G_C = \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big[\operatorname{rank}\, F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big],$$

which counts how many parameters independently change the quantum state. Their numerical study of popular ansätze reveals striking structure:

1. **Scaling laws.** $G_C$ grows with depth and qubit count, but the growth law depends sharply on the entangling gate set — a first quantitative handle on *which* circuits are expressive rather than merely deep.
2. **A geometric transition.** As the number of parameters crosses a threshold, the parameter-space geometry undergoes a transition: beyond it, the quantum natural gradient itself decays, meaning that even geometry-aware optimization degrades for sufficiently deep random circuits.
3. **Redundancy pruning.** Parameters whose removal does not change $G_C$ can be pruned without affecting expressivity, yielding an algorithm that compresses ansätze while preserving their reachable state manifold.

A complementary, worst-case measure is the pseudo-dimension of Caro and Datta [5]:

> **Theorem (Caro & Datta [5]).** *Let $\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{N}}$ be the class of output distributions of a quantum circuit with fixed architecture, qudit dimension $d$, and size (gate count) $\gamma$. Then $\mathrm{Pdim}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{N}}) \le O(d^4 \, \gamma \log \gamma)$. For variable architecture with depth $\delta$, $\mathrm{Pdim} \le O(\delta\, d^4\, \gamma^2 \log \gamma)$ for unitary circuits and $O(\delta\, d^8\, \gamma^2 \log \gamma)$ for circuits of general quantum operations.*

All bounds are polynomial in the circuit's resources — a nontrivial fact, since the underlying Hilbert space is exponential. As a corollary, polynomial-size quantum circuits are PAC-learnable, and there exist explicit families of states requiring exponential circuit depth.

### 4.4 Barren Plateaus and the Expressivity–Trainability Trade-off

The central obstruction to training deep QNNs is the barren plateau [2]:

> **Theorem (Barren plateaus, McClean et al. [2]).** *Consider $C(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathrm{Tr}[O\, U(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rho\, U(\boldsymbol{\theta})^\dagger]$ with $U(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \prod_l U_l(\theta_l) W_l$. If the subcircuits on either side of a parameterized gate form a unitary 2-design, then $\mathbb{E}[\partial_k C] = 0$ and $\mathrm{Var}[\partial_k C]$ is upper-bounded by a quantity decaying exponentially in the number of qubits $n$.*

In words: random, highly expressive circuits produce essentially flat loss landscapes, and the measurement precision needed to resolve a descent direction grows exponentially. Cerezo *et al.* [10] refined this into a *cost-function-dependent* statement: global observables induce barren plateaus even in shallow circuits, while local observables avoid them at shallow depth — trainability depends jointly on ansatz and measurement.

This creates the field's defining tension. Expressivity measures [4][5] reward circuits that explore Hilbert space broadly, but the 2-design property that maximizes exploration is exactly what kills gradients [2]. The practical resolution is structured ansätze — quantum convolutional networks, Hamiltonian-inspired circuits, symmetry-respecting layers — that are expressive *within* a physically relevant submanifold while remaining far from 2-designs. Notably, QNG does not cure barren plateaus: [4] shows the natural gradient itself vanishes in the deep random regime, so geometry-aware optimization and trainable architectures must be co-designed.

### 4.5 Generalization Bounds for Parameterized Quantum Circuits

The final strand asks whether a PQC that fits training data will perform on unseen data. Caro, Datta, and collaborators [6] gave the first general answer:

> **Theorem (Generalization from few training data, Caro et al. [6]).** *Consider a quantum machine learning model with arbitrarily many fixed gates and $T$ trainable $\kappa$-local gates, trained on $N$ samples. With probability at least $1 - \delta$ over the training set, the generalization error satisfies $\mathrm{gen}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^*) = \tilde{O}\!\left(\sqrt{T/N} + \sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/N}\right)$.*

Three features deserve emphasis. First, the bound depends on the number of *trainable* gates, not the total gate count or the Hilbert-space dimension — a variational model with a large fixed feature map and few tuned gates generalizes well. Second, only *effectively* changed gates matter: if just $K \ll T$ gates move substantially during training, the bound improves to $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{K/N})$. Third, the $1/\sqrt{N}$ rate matches the classical uniform-convergence rate, placing QML on the same statistical footing as classical learning theory.

Schuld, Sweke, and Meyer [7] sharpened this by making the *data-encoding strategy* explicit. A PQC classifier with repeated data encoding implements a truncated Fourier series whose frequencies are determined by the encoding gates; bounding the Rademacher complexity of this trigonometric class yields generalization bounds that depend on the number of encoding repetitions and the observable's spectrum. This enables *structural risk minimization* over encoding strategies — a principled, theory-backed form of quantum architecture search. Abbas *et al.* [8] complemented these uniform bounds with the *effective dimension*, an information-theoretic capacity measure derived from the Fisher information matrix, and demonstrated that QNNs achieve larger effective dimension than comparable classical networks — a quantitative expressivity advantage that simultaneously feeds back into tighter generalization behavior.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Optimizer comparison

The table below summarizes the per-iteration cost and geometric properties of optimizers for variational circuits, synthesizing the analysis of [1] with standard results.

| Optimizer | Preconditioner | Cost per step | Reparameterization invariant | Handles flat directions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vanilla GD | $I$ | $O(p)$ | No | No |
| Adam / RMSProp | $\mathrm{diag}$-adaptive | $O(p)$ | No | Partially |
| Full QNG | $g(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{+}$ | $O(p^3)$ | Yes | Yes |
| Block-diagonal QNG [1] | $\bigoplus_l g^{(l)}$ | $O(L\, w^3)$ | Per-layer | Yes |

Here $p$ is the parameter count, $L$ the number of layers, and $w$ the per-layer width. Block-diagonal QNG retains the geometric benefits at a cost linear in depth.

### 5.2 Complexity measures at a glance

| Measure | Quantity bounded | Scaling | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pseudo-dimension | Output distributions, fixed architecture | $O(d^4\, \gamma \log \gamma)$ | [5] |
| Pseudo-dimension | Variable architecture, unitaries | $O(\delta\, d^4\, \gamma^2 \log \gamma)$ | [5] |
| Effective quantum dimension | Independent state-space directions | Problem-dependent scaling laws | [4] |
| Generalization error | Train–test gap, $T$ trainable gates | $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T/N})$ | [6] |
| Encoding-dependent bound | Fourier-class Rademacher complexity | Depends on encoding repetitions | [7] |
| Effective dimension | Fisher-information capacity | Larger than classical FFNNs (empirical) | [8] |

### 5.3 Proof sketches

**Barren plateaus [2].** Write $\partial_k C = i\langle 0| U_-^\dagger [V_k, U_k^\dagger O U_k] U_- |0\rangle$ with $U_-$ ($U_+$) the subcircuit before (after) parameter $k$. If $U_-$ is a 2-design, Haar integration over $U_-$ of the squared derivative reduces, via Weingarten calculus, to traces of $O$ suppressed by the Hilbert-space dimension $2^n$; hence $\mathrm{Var}[\partial_k C] \in O(2^{-n})$ while the mean vanishes by unitary invariance of the Haar measure.

**Pseudo-dimension [5].** For fixed architecture, each output probability is a polynomial of degree at most $2\gamma$ in the $O(\gamma d^4)$ real parameters describing the 2-qudit gates. Classical bounds on the pseudo-dimension of polynomial concept classes (via Warren's theorem on sign patterns) then give $\mathrm{Pdim} \le O(d^4\, \gamma \log \gamma)$.

**Generalization [6].** Covering-number bounds for the class of unitaries implementable with $T$ trainable local gates yield metric entropy growing as $T \log T$; Dudley's entropy integral converts this into a Rademacher complexity of order $\sqrt{T \log T / N}$, and standard uniform-convergence arguments give the high-probability bound on $\mathrm{gen}$.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **QNG does not solve barren plateaus.** The natural gradient rescales *existing* gradient signal; when the signal itself is exponentially suppressed, preconditioning cannot recover it [4]. Geometry-aware optimization must be paired with trainable architectures.
2. **Cost of the metric.** Even block-diagonal QNG requires estimating $O(w^2)$ metric entries per layer on hardware, each subject to shot noise. Noise-resilient, low-overhead estimators of the Fubini–Study tensor remain an open engineering problem.
3. **Uniform bounds can be vacuous.** The $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T/N})$ bound [6] is uniform over the hypothesis class; for overparameterized circuits that fit random labels, uniform bounds are necessarily loose. Non-uniform, algorithm-dependent generalization theory for QNNs — via sharpness, stability, or information-bottleneck arguments — is largely unwritten.
4. **Encoding-aware architecture search.** The structural risk minimization framework of [7] is theoretically clean but computationally unexplored at scale: jointly optimizing encoding strategy, ansatz, and observable under a generalization guarantee is an open algorithmic challenge.
5. **Effective dimension vs. quantum advantage.** Abbas *et al.* [8] show larger effective dimension per parameter for QNNs, but effective dimension is a statement about *trainability and capacity*, not about outperforming classical models on real tasks. Connecting it to end-to-end advantage remains open.
6. **Noise.** All bounds discussed here assume ideal unitaries (with [5] extending to quantum operations). Realistic, non-Markovian device noise and its interaction with the Fubini–Study geometry are poorly understood.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Quantum neural networks sit at the intersection of three mathematical structures: the *differential geometry* of quantum states, which dictates how they should be optimized; the *combinatorial complexity* of circuit families, which dictates what they can express; and the *statistical learning theory* of uniform convergence, which dictates when they generalize. The quantum natural gradient [1] turns the Fubini–Study metric into a practical optimizer; the effective quantum dimension [4] and pseudo-dimension [5] turn expressivity into computable and provable quantities; and modern generalization bounds [6][7] place variational quantum models on the same theoretical footing as classical ones, with the number of trainable gates — not the size of Hilbert space — controlling sample complexity. The barren plateau phenomenon [2][10] binds these strands together, reminding us that expressivity without trainability is empty and that the geometry of optimization cannot be separated from the architecture it optimizes. The path forward lies in co-designing ansätze, encodings, and geometry-aware optimizers under the joint constraints of expressivity, trainability, and generalization.
