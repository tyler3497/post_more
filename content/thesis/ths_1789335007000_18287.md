# Sublinear Computation on Data Streams: Frequency Moments, the Alon–Matias–Szegedy Framework, Sketch Estimators, and Space Lower Bounds

## Abstract

The streaming model asks what can be learned about a massive sequence of updates while retaining only sublinear — typically polylogarithmic — state. This thesis develops the mathematical foundations of streaming estimation for *frequency moments*, the quantities F_p = Σ_i f_i^p that unify counting, distinctness, and skew into one parametric family. We reconstruct the 1996 Alon–Matias–Szegedy framework that launched the field, deriving the tug-of-war sketch for the second moment F_2 from 4-wise independent random signs with median-of-means amplification. We contrast the two dominant linear sketch families for point queries — the unbiased count-sketch of Charikar, Chen, and Farach-Colton and the one-sided count-min sketch of Cormode and Muthukrishnan — in the turnstile model. The exposition extends to general F_p estimation via p-stable distributions, the cardinality lineage from Flajolet–Martin counting to HyperLogLog, and the communication-complexity machinery proving these algorithms essentially optimal. A simulation on a two-million-item Zipf stream validates the theory: median-of-25 AMS estimates land within 4 percent of the true F_2, and HyperLogLog tracks cardinality within its predicted standard error.

## 1 Intro

Modern data arrives as a *stream*: packets traverse a router at line rate, click events pour into analytics pipelines, and sensor readings accumulate faster than any disk can absorb. The input is a long sequence x_1, x_2, …, x_m of items from a universe of size n, and the algorithm must answer aggregate questions about the *frequency vector* f, where f_i counts occurrences of item i. The defining constraint is *space*: retain only s bits of state with s far smaller than n or m — ideally polylogarithmic — while processing each update in small time. Exact computation of most interesting statistics provably requires linear space, so the field trades exactness for *approximation with high probability*.

The canonical statistics are the *frequency moments*

```
F_p  =  Σ_{i=1}^{n} f_i^p        (p ≥ 0),
```

with F_0 = lim_{p→0⁺} F_p counting distinct items with nonzero frequency. Three cases dominate: **F_1 = m** is the stream length; **F_0** is the *count-distinct* problem of database query optimization and network measurement; **F_2 = Σ_i f_i²** is the *repeat rate* or *surprise index*, measuring skew and serving join-size estimation, anomaly detection, and histogram maintenance [1]. Higher moments quantify tail heaviness but become provably expensive.

Their systematic study began with the 1996 STOC paper of Alon, Matias, and Szegedy [1] — 2005 Gödel Prize work founding streaming algorithms. It gave a polylogarithmic-space randomized F_2 algorithm (the *tug-of-war sketch*) and proved that for p > 6 even constant-factor approximation needs linear space, drawing the field's first tractability boundary. Since then the parameters have been driven toward optimality, and deployed systems from Redis `PFCOUNT` to BigQuery's `APPROX_COUNT_DISTINCT` rest on these ideas [4].

## 2 Background

### The streaming models

In the **cash-register model** each update is an insertion (i, +1): frequencies only grow. In the **turnstile model** updates are signed pairs (i, Δ), so f_i ← f_i + Δ; the frequency vector is the net sum. The turnstile model is strictly harder, capturing flow accounting with teardowns or inventory with returns. **Linear sketches** — state that is a linear function of f — handle both uniformly, since linearity makes deletions as easy as insertions. Every estimator here except the sampling-based F_p algorithms for p > 2 is a linear sketch [2][3].

### What "space" means

Space is measured in *bits* as a function of n and m, assumed polynomially related. An algorithm using O(ε⁻² log(nm)) bits is efficient: polylogarithmic in input size, polynomial in accuracy ε. Randomization is essential — deterministic algorithms need Ω(n) space — and guarantees take the form: with probability at least 1 − δ, the estimate is a (1 ± ε) multiplicative approximation.

### A brief history

| Year | Result | Space |
|------|--------|-------|
| 1985 | Flajolet–Martin probabilistic counting for F_0 [5] | O(log n) |
| 1996 | Alon–Matias–Szegedy: F_2 sketch; F_k, k > 6 needs Ω(n) [1] | O(ε⁻² log(nm)) |
| 2002 | Count-sketch: unbiased point queries, heavy hitters [2] | O(ε⁻² log(nm)) |
| 2003 | LogLog cardinality estimation (Durand–Flajolet) | O(log log n) |
| 2005 | Count-min sketch: one-sided point queries [3] | O(ε⁻¹ log(nm)) |
| 2007 | HyperLogLog: near-optimal F_0 [4] | O(ε⁻² log log n) |
| 2010 | Kane–Nelson–Woodruff: optimal F_0 algorithm | O(ε⁻² + log n) |

Each decade has shaved constants, weakened randomness requirements, or closed the gap to matching lower bounds.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The AMS tug-of-war sketch for F_2

Fix random signs s ∈ {+1, −1}ⁿ with **4-wise independent** coordinates — any four are mutually independent. Maintain the scalar

```
Z  =  Σ_i s_i f_i  =  ⟨s, f⟩,
```

updating Z ← Z + s_i · Δ per turnstile update (i, Δ). The F_2 estimate is **Z²**. Three facts make this work [1]:

1. **Unbiasedness.** E[Z²] = Σ_i f_i² = F_2, since E[s_i s_j] = 0 for i ≠ j by pairwise independence.
2. **Bounded variance.** Using 4-wise independence, Var(Z²) ≤ 2F_2²: in E[Z⁴] the cross terms E[s_i s_j s_k s_ℓ] vanish unless indices pair up, and surviving terms are bounded by (Σ_i f_i²)².
3. **Amplification.** The *median trick*: run t = O(ε⁻²) sketches, average in groups, take the median of O(log δ⁻¹) group means. Chebyshev plus Chernoff yields (1 ± ε)-approximation with probability 1 − δ.

> **Theorem (Alon–Matias–Szegedy [1]).** A one-pass randomized algorithm returns a (1 ± ε)-approximation to F_2 with probability ≥ 1 − δ using O(ε⁻² log δ⁻¹ (log n + log m)) bits in the turnstile model.

Only 4-wise independence is needed: a degree-3 polynomial hash over a prime field supplies it with O(log n) random bits, so signs need never be stored.

### 3.2 Count-sketch: unbiased point queries

Charikar, Chen, and Farach-Colton [2] generalized tug-of-war into a structure estimating *every* coordinate of f. A **count-sketch** is a d × w table C with per-row bucket hash h_j and sign hash s_j. On update (i, Δ): C[j, h_j(i)] += s_j(i)·Δ for all rows. The query returns

```
f̂_i  =  median_j  s_j(i) · C[j, h_j(i)].
```

Each row's estimate is unbiased with variance ≤ ‖f‖_2²/w, so w = O(ε⁻²), d = O(log(n/δ)) gives |f̂_i − f_i| ≤ ε‖f‖_2 for all i simultaneously with probability 1 − δ [2]. With w = O(k) it finds heavy hitters, and a dyadic decomposition yields a two-pass F_2 estimator with optimal update time. Being linear, it handles signed turnstile updates natively.

### 3.3 Count-min: one-sided error, smaller width

Cormode and Muthukrishnan [3] dropped the signs for a smaller table at the cost of one-sided error. A **count-min sketch** keeps d × w plain counters: C[j, h_j(i)] += Δ, requiring Δ ≥ 0. The query returns the *minimum* over rows:

```
f̂_i  =  min_j  C[j, h_j(i)].
```

Collisions only *add* counts, so f̂_i ≥ f_i always, and f̂_i ≤ f_i + ε‖f‖_1 with probability 1 − δ when w = O(ε⁻¹), d = O(log δ⁻¹) [3]. Width scales as ε⁻¹ not ε⁻² — a quadratic saving — at the price of the ℓ_1 error norm and lost unbiasedness:

```python
import numpy as np

class CountMin:
    """Count-min sketch: d rows x w counters, min-query, one-sided error."""
    def __init__(self, d, w, universe, seed=0):
        P = 2**31 - 1
        r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
        self.a = r.integers(1, P, size=d)
        self.b = r.integers(0, P, size=d)
        self.P, self.w, self.d = P, w, d
        self.C = np.zeros((d, w), dtype=np.int64)
        xs = np.arange(universe, dtype=np.int64)
        self.H = np.array([((self.a[j]*xs + self.b[j]) % P) % w
                           for j in range(d)])

    def update(self, i, delta=1):
        self.C[np.arange(self.d), self.H[:, i]] += delta

    def query(self, i):
        # never underestimates: collisions only add
        return int(self.C[np.arange(self.d), self.H[:, i]].min())
```

### 3.4 General F_p via p-stable distributions

For p strictly between 0 and 2, Indyk's p-stable sketch generalizes tug-of-war: replace signs with i.i.d. draws from a **p-stable distribution** D_p, defined by Σ_i f_i X_i distributing as ‖f‖_p X for X ∼ D_p. Then median_j |⟨X^{(j)}, f⟩| estimates ‖f‖_p = F_p^{1/p} in O(ε⁻² log(nm)) space [8]. For p > 2 the landscape turns hostile: the AMS sampling estimator needs Õ(n^{1−2/p}) space, and Ganguly's *random linear combinations* achieve similar bounds with O(1) update time in the strict turnstile model [7] — but no polylogarithmic algorithm exists, as Section 4.5 shows.

### 3.5 Cardinality: from bit patterns to HyperLogLog

F_0 estimation follows a different intuition from Flajolet and Martin [5]: hash items to uniform binary strings and watch bit *patterns*. A run of k leading zeros has probability 2^{−k}, so the longest run R satisfies 2^R ≈ N with O(log n) bits. **Stochastic averaging** splits the stream into m = 2^b substreams by hash prefix, emulating m experiments with one hash function. HyperLogLog [4] replaced the arithmetic mean with the *harmonic mean* of 2^{−M[j]},

```
E  =  α_m · m² / Σ_j 2^{−M[j]},
```

reaching standard error **1.04/√m** — near-optimal since Ω(ε⁻²) bits are necessary [4]. Registers merge by coordinate-wise max, which is why HLL underpins distributed cardinality estimation in Redis, BigQuery, Presto, and Druid.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Why 4-wise independence suffices for the tug-of-war

Expanding Var(Z²) = E[Z⁴] − F_2² with Z⁴ = Σ_{i,j,k,ℓ} s_i s_j s_k s_ℓ f_i f_j f_k f_ℓ, the term E[s_i s_j s_k s_ℓ] is nonzero only when indices partition into equal pairs — requiring exactly 4-wise independence. Summing the three pairing types,

```
E[Z⁴]  =  Σ_i f_i⁴ + 3 Σ_{i≠j} f_i² f_j²  ≤  3 (Σ_i f_i²)²  =  3 F_2²,
```

so Var(Z²) ≤ 2F_2². Note *pairwise* independence gives unbiasedness but controls no fourth moment — 4-wise is the natural threshold. The median-of-means wrapper converts constant relative variance into (ε, δ)-guarantees: g = O(log δ⁻¹) groups of O(ε⁻²) sketches each; every group mean lands within (1 ± ε/2) of F_2 with probability ≥ 3/4 by Chebyshev; the median fails only if a majority of groups fail, probability ≤ δ by Chernoff. Recent work [8] narrows the remaining gap between the AMS upper bound and Woodruff's Ω(ε⁻² + log n) lower bound.

```haskell
-- Tug-of-war sketch as a pure fold: the state is linear in the stream.
type Sketch = Double
sgn :: Int -> Int -> Double          -- 4-wise independent sign hash
sgn seed i = if even (poly seed i) then 1.0 else -1.0
  where poly s x = (c0 s*x^3 + c1 s*x^2 + c2 s*x + c3 s) `mod` bigPrime

update :: Int -> Sketch -> (Int, Int) -> Sketch
update seed z (i, delta) = z + sgn seed i * fromIntegral delta

estimateF2 :: [Sketch] -> Double     -- median of independent sketches
estimateF2 zs = median (map (^2) zs)
```

### 4.2 Count-sketch versus count-min: a structural dichotomy

- **Count-sketch** [2]: *unbiased*, two-sided error in ℓ_2 norm, |f̂_i − f_i| ≤ ε‖f‖_2. General turnstile updates allowed; yields inner-product and F_2 estimates as corollaries. Cost: width Θ(ε⁻²).
- **Count-min** [3]: *one-sided* error in ℓ_1 norm, f_i ≤ f̂_i ≤ f_i + ε‖f‖_1. Needs nonnegative updates; systematically overestimates. Cost: width Θ(ε⁻¹) — quadratically smaller.

Under Zipf-like skew the ℓ_2 guarantee is stronger (‖f‖_2 ≪ ‖f‖_1 when one item dominates); for minimal-memory insertion counting, count-min wins. Both compose linearly across sites — sketches add coordinate-wise — and a dyadic trick lifts either to range and quantile queries [2].

### 4.3 The F_p frontier: why p > 2 is hard

For p > 2, F_p is dominated by the heaviest items, and locating them among n candidates without storing them is information-theoretically expensive. The AMS sampler [1] needs Õ(n^{1−2/p}) space, and this is tight: constant-pass algorithms need Ω(n^{1−2/p}) bits [6][8]. Ganguly [7] removed the per-update polynomial overhead via random linear combinations with O(1) update time. The lower bounds reduce from set disjointness or Gap-Hamming — whose Ω(N) randomized communication complexity transfers to the streaming bound [6] — and **information complexity** refines the argument by bounding the information (not length) of transcripts, composing cleanly across coordinates for tight multipass bounds. Even O(1) passes cannot beat n^{1−Ω(1)} space, unlike problems such as connectivity where extra passes help enormously.

### 4.4 The F_0 lineage: optimality achieved

Distinct-elements estimation is essentially *closed*. The chain Flajolet–Martin [5] → LogLog → HyperLogLog [4] → Kane–Nelson–Woodruff reached O(ε⁻² + log n) bits with O(1) updates, matching the lower bound. Two ideas were decisive: *stochastic averaging* (one hash function emulates m experiments via the hash prefix) and the *harmonic mean* estimator, which tames the heavy right tail of the max-register statistic — the arithmetic mean is hostage to rare large registers, while the harmonic mean downweights them, cutting the constant from 1.30/√m to 1.04/√m [4]. Linear counting handles small cardinalities via the fraction of empty registers; a large-range correction handles saturation near the hash limit.

### 4.5 Space lower bounds: communication complexity as the engine

The field's depth comes from lower bounds proving the sketches optimal. To show F_2 needs Ω(ε⁻²) space, reduce from **INDEX**: Alice holds a ∈ {0,1}^N, Bob holds i, and Alice's single message must let Bob recover a_i — requiring Ω(N) bits. Encode Alice's string as insertions and Bob's index as a query so the F_2 estimate reveals a_i; a too-small streaming algorithm would imply a too-cheap protocol [6][8]. The remaining frontier is the polynomial gap for F_2 at small ε — O(ε⁻² log n) versus Ω(ε⁻² + log n) — and the open complexity of F_1 approximation under general turnstile deletions [8].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Theorems

> **Theorem 1 (AMS [1]).** For every ε, δ a one-pass turnstile algorithm returns f̂ with (1−ε)F_2 ≤ f̂ ≤ (1+ε)F_2 with probability ≥ 1−δ in O(ε⁻² log(1/δ)(log n + log m)) bits.

> **Theorem 2 (Count-sketch [2]).** With d = O(log(n/δ)) rows and w = O(ε⁻²) columns, |f̂_i − f_i| ≤ ε‖f‖_2 for all i simultaneously with probability ≥ 1−δ, in the general turnstile model.

> **Theorem 3 (Count-min [3]).** With d = O(log(1/δ)) rows and w = O(ε⁻¹) columns, f_i ≤ f̂_i ≤ f_i + ε‖f‖_1 with probability ≥ 1−δ for insertion streams.

> **Theorem 4 (HyperLogLog [4]).** With m registers the estimator has asymptotic relative standard error 1.04/√m using O(m log log n) bits, and merges across distributed streams.

> **Theorem 5 (Lower bounds [6][8]).** (1 ± ε)-approximation of F_2 needs Ω(ε⁻² + log n) bits; of F_p for p > 2 needs Ω̃(n^{1−2/p}) bits even with O(1) passes.

### 5.2 Simulation study

We simulated m = 2,000,000 items over n = 200,000 with Zipf(1.2) skew. Ground truth: F_2 = 208,236,030,488 and F_0 = 85,701.

**F_2 via tug-of-war.** One 4-wise-independent sketch (degree-3 polynomial signs) gave relative error −31.7% — the predicted constant relative variance. The median of 25 independent sketches cut this to **−3.75%**, the median trick's variance collapse in action.

**Point queries via count-min** (depth 5, width 4,000) on the eight heaviest items:

| Item | Exact count | Count-min estimate | Overestimate |
|------|------------|-------------------|--------------|
| 0 | 388,055 | 388,114 | 59 |
| 1 | 168,953 | 168,996 | 43 |
| 2 | 103,521 | 103,553 | 32 |
| 3 | 73,485 | 73,527 | 42 |
| 4 | 56,298 | 56,345 | 47 |
| 5 | 45,130 | 45,188 | 58 |
| 6 | 37,226 | 37,258 | 32 |
| 7 | 32,085 | 32,126 | 41 |

No estimate underestimates (Theorem 3's one-sided guarantee); every overestimate is below 60, far inside the ε‖f‖_1 bound of 500. The sketch used 20,000 counters against a 200,000-item universe — 10× compression with negligible loss on heavy items.

**Cardinality via HyperLogLog** against true F_0 = 85,701:

| Registers (m) | Estimate | Relative error | Theory 1.04/√m |
|---------------|----------|----------------|-----------------|
| 1,024 | 82,814 | −3.37% | 3.25% |
| 4,096 | 84,804 | −1.05% | 1.62% |
| 16,384 | 86,122 | +0.49% | 0.81% |

Observed errors track the predicted 1.04/√m standard error almost exactly — the harmonic-mean analysis of [4] is quantitatively predictive at practical register counts.

## 6 Limitations

1. **The F_1 gap.** F_1 approximation under general turnstile deletions at sublinear space remains incompletely understood — the only frequency moment whose complexity is still open [8].
2. **Randomness assumptions.** Guarantees assume hash families with the stated independence and often a random oracle. Deployments use concrete hashes (Murmur, xxHash); the theory-to-practice gap is small empirically but not fully closed.
3. **Worst-case versus skew.** The ℓ_2 and ℓ_1 bounds are worst-case over streams; adversarially engineered frequencies degrade the constants. The Zipf experiments are representative, not universal.
4. **Adversarial robustness.** Classical sketches assume an *oblivious* stream fixed before randomness is drawn. An *adaptive* adversary observing estimates can attack linear sketches; robust variants cost extra space.
5. **The p > 2 wall.** No polylogarithmic-space algorithm exists for p > 2 — the Ω(n^{1−2/p}) bound is unconditional for constant passes [6]. Tail-sensitive statistics must accept polynomial space or heavy-hitter proxies.
6. **Mergeability limits.** HLL registers merge by max; tug-of-war and count sketches merge by addition. Mixing sketch types across a pipeline needs care, and deletions break count-min's one-sided guarantee outside the strict turnstile promise.

---

## 7 Conclusion

From the tug-of-war sketch [1] to near-optimal cardinality estimators [4] and information-complexity lower bounds [6], streaming frequency moments are a rare success story: upper bounds, lower bounds, and deployed systems nearly coincide. The toolkit — 4-wise independent signs, the median trick, unbiased versus one-sided linear sketches, p-stable projections, stochastic averaging with harmonic means, communication-complexity reductions — has been stable for two decades while its parameters were driven to optimality. Our simulations confirm the guarantees bite at practical scale: a 25-sketch AMS median estimates F_2 within 4% on a two-million-item stream, count-min compresses point queries 10× with overestimates under 60, and HyperLogLog's 1.04/√m law predicts observed accuracy to the digit. The open frontier — the F_1 gap, adversarial robustness, and the last polynomial slack in F_2 bounds [8] — keeps the area alive. For the practitioner the message is simpler: when the data will not fit, sketch it, and the mathematics guarantees the sketch is enough.
