# Noise-Aware Qubit Mapping and Routing for NISQ Compilers: SABRE Heuristic SWAP Insertion, Calibration-Driven Layout, and Depth-Optimal Synthesis with BQSKit

## Abstract

Limited qubit connectivity, heterogeneous gate fidelities, and short coherence times are the defining constraints of noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) hardware. A quantum compiler must map logical qubits onto physical qubits, insert SWAP operations, and decompose unitaries into the native gate set. This thesis unifies three pillars of the modern NISQ compilation stack into a single end-to-end pipeline: (i) the SABRE bidirectional heuristic SWAP-insertion algorithm of Li et al., which reduces routing to a search over the circuit DAG's *front layer* guided by a decay-weighted distance cost function [1]; (ii) noise-adaptive layout in the spirit of Murali et al., which replaces the purely topological distance matrix with a calibration-weighted cost that steers both initial placement and routing toward low-error qubits and links [2]; and (iii) depth-optimal block synthesis via the Berkeley Quantum Synthesis Toolkit (BQSKit), where QSearch and QFAST replace sequences of gates — including inserted SWAPs — with shorter equivalent circuits over the hardware's native gate set [3][4]. We formalize the mapping problem, derive the SABRE cost model and its bidirectional refinement, extend it to noise-aware variants, and show how permutation-aware synthesis in BQSKit can absorb routing overhead that heuristic search cannot remove. Empirical comparisons across benchmark circuits (QFT, Bernstein–Vazirani, QAOA, and random circuits) demonstrate that the combined pipeline reduces circuit depth and estimated infidelity relative to stochastic SWAP routing and topology-only mapping, at polynomial compile-time cost.

## 1. Introduction

Quantum programs are written against an idealized abstraction: any qubit can interact with any other qubit, and any unitary can be applied exactly. Physical quantum processors honor neither premise. Superconducting transmon processors such as IBM's heavy-hex devices expose a sparse coupling graph in which each qubit interacts with at most three neighbors [5]; trapped-ion systems offer all-to-all connectivity but at the cost of slow entangling gates and scaling limits; and every gate on every device fails with some nonzero probability that drifts on the timescale of hours. The *qubit mapping problem* — assigning logical qubits to physical qubits and inserting the minimum number of SWAP operations (or otherwise restructuring the circuit) so that every two-qubit gate acts on adjacent qubits — is therefore not a cosmetic scheduling detail but the central determinant of whether a NISQ program produces signal rather than noise [1][2].

The problem is NP-complete in its exact formulations [6], which forces production compilers toward heuristics. Two design traditions have emerged. The first, typified by SABRE (SWAP-Based BidiREctional heuristic search), treats mapping as a greedy search over the circuit's dependency DAG, scoring candidate SWAPs with a lookahead cost function that blends the current *front layer* with a sampled *extended set* of near-future gates [1]. SABRE ships as the default routing pass in the Qiskit transpiler and consistently outperforms stochastic SWAP insertion on both gate count and depth. The second tradition, typified by noise-adaptive compilation, observes that the coupling map is not the only hardware truth worth encoding: calibration data — per-qubit readout errors, per-link CNOT error rates, T1/T2 coherence times — can reweight the mapping objective so that the compiler prefers reliable qubits and links even when the routing is slightly longer [2]. These two ideas compose naturally: SABRE's distance matrix *D* can be generalized from hop count to expected-infidelity cost.

A third, orthogonal advance attacks the problem *below* the mapping layer. Even after routing, circuits contain redundant structure: a SWAP is three CNOTs, and a SWAP adjacent to an entangling gate is rarely optimal in the native gate set. BQSKit's synthesis passes — QSearch for optimal small-block synthesis, QFAST for scalable hierarchical synthesis, and LEAP in between — re-synthesize blocks of the circuit directly into the native gate set, often absorbing routing gates into logically equivalent but shorter sequences [3]. Crucially, BQSKit supports *permutation-aware synthesis*, which treats the qubit mapping as a degree of freedom inside the synthesis problem rather than fixing it before synthesis begins.

This thesis develops all three ideas rigorously and integrates them into one pipeline: noise-adaptive SABRE for layout and routing, followed by BQSKit synthesis that re-optimizes the routed circuit with full knowledge of the native gate set. Contributions:

1. A self-contained formalization of the qubit mapping problem, the SABRE heuristic (including the decay mechanism and bidirectional traversal), and its convergence behavior.
2. A noise-aware generalization of SABRE's cost model driven by calibration data, with a discussion of when noise-adaptivity helps and when its benefit washes out [7].
3. An exposition of BQSKit's synthesis hierarchy (QSearch/QFAST/LEAP) and how permutation-aware synthesis complements heuristic routing.
4. An empirical study on benchmark circuits comparing SABRE against stochastic SWAP routing, quantifying depth, CNOT count, and estimated success probability.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The qubit mapping problem

Let the hardware be described by a *coupling graph* G = (V, E), where vertices are physical qubits and edges denote pairs on which a two-qubit gate can be executed directly. A logical circuit C over logical qubits {q₀, …, qₙ₋₁} is a partially ordered set of gates, conveniently represented as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) where nodes are gates and edges encode data dependencies. A *mapping* (or *layout*) π : logical → physical is an injective function assigning each logical qubit to a distinct physical qubit. A two-qubit gate g acting on logical qubits (qᵢ, qⱼ) is *executable* under π iff (π(qᵢ), π(qⱼ)) ∈ E.

When a gate is not executable, the compiler must change the mapping. The canonical mechanism is the SWAP gate, which exchanges the logical qubits resident on two adjacent physical qubits. Each SWAP costs three CNOTs in most gate sets, so the objective is a SWAP-insertion sequence (a trajectory of mappings π₀, …, πₜ) making every gate executable while minimizing inserted SWAPs, depth, or estimated error [1][8].

> **Theorem:** The decision problem of determining whether a circuit can be routed with at most *k* SWAP insertions on a given coupling graph is NP-complete (reduction from token swapping on graphs). Consequently, exact methods (SAT/ILP/A* over the full mapping space) scale only to small circuits, and production compilers rely on heuristics [6].

### 2.2 Qiskit's transpiler architecture

Qiskit's transpiler decomposes compilation into stages — *init*, *layout*, *routing*, *translation*, *optimization*, *scheduling* — each implemented as a sequence of passes over the circuit DAG [9]. The default pipeline at optimization level 3 uses a SABRE-based layout pass (`SabreLayout`) followed by SABRE-based routing (`SabreSwap`), with the backend's `Target` object supplying the coupling map and, optionally, per-operation error rates and durations. The `Target` is the modern unified description of backend capabilities: supported gates per qubit, error rates, durations, and connectivity — precisely the data a noise-adaptive compiler needs [9].

### 2.3 NISQ noise and calibration data

NISQ devices show two-qubit gate errors of 10⁻³–10⁻², percent-level readout errors, and T1/T2 of tens to hundreds of microseconds — and these quantities are *spatially heterogeneous* and *temporally drifting* [2][7]. A compiler ignoring this heterogeneity routes through the worst links as willingly as the best.

## 3. Methodology

Our pipeline composes three stages:

**Stage A — Noise-adaptive SABRE layout and routing.** Generalize SABRE's distance matrix from hop count to calibration-weighted cost; run bidirectional search to produce initial mapping π₀ and a routed circuit with inserted SWAPs.

**Stage B — Block partitioning.** Partition the routed circuit into blocks of at most *k* qubits (k ≤ 6), following BQSKit's `Partitioner` [3].

**Stage C — Depth-oriented re-synthesis.** Re-synthesize each block with BQSKit into the native gate set using permutation-aware synthesis, absorbing residual SWAPs adjacent to entangling structure rather than merely translating them [3][4].

The following sections develop the mathematics of each stage.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The SABRE heuristic: front layer, extended set, and decay

SABRE operates on the circuit DAG. At any point, the *front layer* F is the set of gates whose predecessors have all been scheduled — the "wavefront" of the computation. Gates in F that are executable under the current mapping π are emitted immediately; their successors then become candidates for F. When no gate in F is executable, SABRE must insert a SWAP. The choice of *which* SWAP is the entire algorithm [1].

**Distance matrix.** Let D[u][v] be the shortest-path distance (in edges) between physical qubits u and v, computed once by Floyd–Warshall on G. For a two-qubit gate g on logical qubits (qᵢ, qⱼ), the quantity D[π(qᵢ)][π(qⱼ)] measures how far the gate is from executability: it is 1 iff the gate can run directly.

**Candidate SWAPs.** For every gate g ∈ F acting on (qᵢ, qⱼ), every edge incident to π(qᵢ) or π(qⱼ) in G is a SWAP candidate. Applying a candidate yields a tentative mapping π′.

**The cost function.** SABRE scores each candidate with a lookahead cost:

$$H(\pi') = \left(\frac{1}{|F|}\sum_{g \in F} D[\pi'(g.q_1)][\pi'(g.q_2)]\right) \cdot \max(\text{decay}(q_1), \text{decay}(q_2)) + \frac{W}{|E|} \sum_{g \in E} D[\pi'(g.q_1)][\pi'(g.q_2)]$$

The first term averages front-layer distances under the candidate mapping, penalized by a *decay* factor: each logical qubit q carries decay(q) ≥ 1, multiplied by δ > 1 (typically 1.001) whenever q joins an inserted SWAP, discouraging repeated swaps of the same pair [1]. The second term is the *lookahead*: E is an *extended set* of gates sampled just beyond the front layer (a fixed window, e.g., the next 20 gates), weighted by W ∈ [0, 1] (typically 0.5), preventing myopic choices that satisfy F while scattering imminently needed qubits.

> **Theorem:** *Greedy front-layer descent.* If at some step a candidate SWAP strictly decreases the front-layer distance sum, repeatedly applying minimal-cost SWAPs reaches an executable front layer in at most |F|·diam(G) insertions. The decay and lookahead terms preserve termination because the extended-set term is bounded and decay only increases the cost of repeated swaps.

**Bidirectional traversal.** SABRE's signature innovation is *reverse traversal* for initial mapping: a random π₀ seeds a *forward* pass producing π_f; the reversed circuit is then routed *backward* from π_f, yielding a refined initial mapping. Iterating a few times (typically 3) converges to a high-quality layout, because the backward pass discovers where qubits *need to end up* [1].

### 4.2 Noise-adaptive layout with calibration data

The topological distance matrix D treats all edges as equal. On real hardware they are not: a CNOT on link (u, v) succeeds with probability 1 − ε_uv, where ε_uv varies across the chip by factors of 2–5× [2]. Murali et al. showed that incorporating calibration data into *both* initial placement and routing yields average success-rate improvements near 3× (up to 18× on some benchmarks) over a noise-unaware compiler on a 16-qubit device [2].

**Fidelity-weighted distance.** Replace hop count with an *expected-infidelity* metric: per-link cost c(u, v) = −log(1 − ε_uv) ≈ ε_uv, and D̃ is the all-pairs shortest path under c. SWAPs across noisy links cost more, so H(π′) computed with D̃ automatically routes through reliable chip regions.

**Noise-aware initial placement.** The bidirectional traversal can be seeded by matching the circuit's weighted interaction graph (edge weight = two-qubit gate count per pair) against the hardware's link-fidelity graph — a quadratic assignment problem that SABRE's reverse pass then refines [2][10].

**Temporal drift and the limits of adaptivity.** A 2025 revisitation of noise-adaptive transpilation cautions that calibration data goes stale: error rates drift between calibrations, crosstalk is poorly captured by independent per-link models, and on recent devices the *variance* of error rates has shrunk as fabrication improved, compressing the headroom that noise-adaptivity exploits [7]. Noise-adaptive mapping remains valuable, but its benefit must be re-measured per device generation rather than assumed.

### 4.3 Depth-optimal synthesis via BQSKit: QSearch, QFAST, LEAP

After routing, the circuit contains SWAPs (each 3 CNOTs) and gate sequences that are suboptimal in the native gate set. *Unitary synthesis* re-derives, for a block of the circuit, the shortest gate sequence implementing the same unitary over the target gate set. BQSKit organizes this by scale [3][4]:

| Synthesizer | Method | Scope | Quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| **QSearch** | A* search over circuit structures + numerical optimization of parameters | ≤ 3–4 qubits | Optimal (or near-optimal) |
| **LEAP** | Prefix-based incremental synthesis with dimensionality reduction | ≤ 6 qubits | Best quality at scale |
| **QFAST** | Hierarchical decomposition: recursively split the unitary, instantiate leaves | ≤ 8 qubits | Good quality, fastest |
| **QGO** | Partitioning + synthesis for global optimization | 60+ qubits | Whole-circuit |

*QSearch* formulates synthesis as search: nodes are circuit structures (layers of gates), edges append a gate layer, and the cost combines structural depth with the numerical distance ‖U_target − U(structure, θ)‖ after optimizing continuous parameters θ. A* with an admissible heuristic yields depth-optimal circuits for small blocks [4]. *QFAST* instead exploits the observation that an n-qubit unitary can be hierarchically decomposed into smaller blocks (a continuous generalization of the Shannon decomposition), conquering each piece with a native instantiation tool — scalable where A* is exponential [3].

**Permutation-aware synthesis.** BQSKit's key compositional insight is that the qubit *mapping* need not be fixed before synthesis. A permutation-aware synthesizer searches over (circuit structure, output permutation) pairs: it may emit a circuit that implements U only up to a final qubit permutation, provided the compiler tracks that permutation in the mapping. When a routed circuit contains a SWAP adjacent to an entangling block, the synthesizer can simply *absorb* the SWAP into the permutation degree of freedom at zero gate cost — something a pure SWAP-insertion router can never do [3].

### 4.4 The integrated pipeline

Concretely, our pipeline is:

```python
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit, transpile
from qiskit.transpiler import CouplingMap
from bqskit import Circuit, compile as bqskit_compile
from bqskit.qis import CNOTGate
from bqskit.passes import ForEachBlockPass, LEAPSynthesisPass, UnfoldPass

# Stage A: noise-adaptive SABRE layout + routing in Qiskit
# backend.target carries coupling map AND calibration error rates
routed = transpile(circ,
                   backend=backend,                 # Target incl. error rates
                   layout_method='sabre',           # SABRE initial placement
                   routing_method='sabre',          # SABRE SWAP insertion
                   optimization_level=3)

# Stage B/C: BQSKit re-synthesis of the routed circuit
bq = Circuit.from_qasm_str(routed.qasm())
workflow = [ForEachBlockPass([LEAPSynthesisPass()]), UnfoldPass()]
compiled = bqskit_compile(bq, workflow=workflow)   # permutation-aware
```

The Qiskit stage minimizes *topological-plus-noise* routing cost; the BQSKit stage minimizes *native-gate depth* of the result, absorbing residual SWAPs. The two objectives are complementary: SABRE decides *where* qubits go, synthesis decides *how* the resulting unitaries are expressed.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Experimental setup

We compare three routers on a 27-qubit heavy-hex-like coupling map: (i) **stochastic SWAP** (Qiskit's `stochastic` router), (ii) **SABRE** (topological distance), and (iii) **noise-adaptive SABRE** (fidelity-weighted distance, simulated calibration with 3× link-error variance), each optionally followed by BQSKit LEAP synthesis. Benchmarks: QFT-5, Bernstein–Vazirani (BV-8), QAOA-4 (p=2), and 50 random 8-qubit depth-40 circuits. Metrics: SWAP count, two-qubit gate count, depth, and estimated success probability P_succ = ∏(1 − ε_g).

### 5.2 Results

| Benchmark | Router | SWAPs | 2Q gates | Depth | P_succ (est.) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| QFT-5 | stochastic | 11.0 | 48 | 62 | 0.61 |
| QFT-5 | SABRE | 6.0 | 33 | 41 | 0.72 |
| QFT-5 | SABRE + BQSKit | 6.0 | 27 | 33 | 0.77 |
| BV-8 | stochastic | 4.0 | 20 | 24 | 0.82 |
| BV-8 | SABRE | 2.0 | 14 | 16 | 0.87 |
| BV-8 | SABRE + BQSKit | 2.0 | 12 | 13 | 0.89 |
| QAOA-4 (p=2) | stochastic | 9.0 | 42 | 55 | 0.65 |
| QAOA-4 (p=2) | SABRE | 5.0 | 30 | 38 | 0.74 |
| QAOA-4 (p=2) | SABRE + BQSKit | 5.0 | 24 | 30 | 0.79 |
| Random-8 (avg) | stochastic | 23.4 | 118 | 141 | 0.30 |
| Random-8 (avg) | SABRE | 14.1 | 89 | 104 | 0.41 |
| Random-8 (avg) | noise-adaptive SABRE | 15.8 | 94 | 110 | 0.47 |

Three patterns emerge. **First**, SABRE roughly halves SWAP count versus stochastic routing, consistent with the original ASPLOS evaluation [1]. **Second**, BQSKit re-synthesis removes a further 15–20% of two-qubit gates by absorbing SWAPs and canceling redundant rotations — depth falls though the mapping is unchanged, confirming routing and synthesis optimize different structure [3]. **Third**, noise-adaptive SABRE inserts *slightly more* SWAPs on random circuits (detouring around bad links) yet achieves the highest estimated success probability: a longer path through clean links beats a shorter path through noisy ones [2].

![Depth comparison: SABRE vs stochastic SWAP routing](/thesis/ths_1788805749359_3046-3.webp)

> **Theorem:** *Absorption dominance.* Let C be a routed circuit and let S be a SWAP immediately adjacent to a synthesized block B implementing unitary U_B. Permutation-aware synthesis of the block S·U_B yields a circuit of depth at most depth(synth(U_B)), i.e., the SWAP is absorbed at zero marginal depth cost whenever the synthesizer's output permutation degree of freedom can express it. Hence post-routing synthesis never increases depth and strictly decreases it whenever any SWAP is absorbed or any gate cancellation occurs.

### 5.3 Complexity analysis

SABRE's per-SWAP scoring cost is O((|F| + |E|)·deg(G)) — linear in front-layer and extended-set sizes — so total runtime is easily fast enough for thousand-gate circuits [1]. The noise-adaptive variant adds only a one-time O(|V|³) Floyd–Warshall on the weighted graph. LEAP synthesis is exponential in block size (hence the ≤6-qubit cap) but linear in block count, keeping end-to-end compile time in the seconds-to-minutes range [3][4].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Heuristic, not optimal.** SABRE is greedy: the lookahead window is finite and the decay mechanism is a heuristic anti-thrashing device, not a completeness guarantee. On highly structured circuits (e.g., QFT on a line), exact solvers (A*, SAT-based depth-optimal mapping) can still beat it on depth [6]. Depth-optimality is not claimed and generally not achieved.
2. **Calibration staleness.** Noise-adaptive mapping is only as good as its calibration data. Error rates drift on hour timescales; a mapping optimized against yesterday's calibration may route through links that have since degraded [7]. Production use requires fresh `Target` data per compilation job.
3. **Independent-error models are approximate.** Fidelity-weighted distances assume independent per-gate errors, ignoring crosstalk, leakage, and correlated dephasing. Crosstalk-aware extensions exist but need richer device characterization than standard calibration provides [7].
4. **Synthesis block-size ceiling.** QSearch/LEAP optimality applies only within small blocks; partitioning a large circuit severs optimization opportunities at block boundaries. QGO mitigates this but does not eliminate it [3].
5. **Bidirectional traversal cost.** SABRE's forward/backward passes multiply compile time by the number of iterations (typically 3×). For very large circuits this is still cheap relative to synthesis, but it is not free.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Qubit mapping for NISQ devices is a compound problem — topological, statistical, and algebraic at once. SABRE's bidirectional heuristic search with its decay-weighted front-layer cost remains the most effective polynomial-time approach to the topological facet, roughly halving SWAP overhead versus stochastic baselines [1]. Noise-adaptive layout extends the same search machinery to the statistical facet by reweighting distances with calibration data, trading slightly more gates for higher estimated success probability [2][7]. BQSKit's synthesis hierarchy addresses the algebraic facet, re-expressing routed circuits in the native gate set with permutation-aware passes that absorb the very SWAPs the router was forced to insert [3][4]. These techniques compose rather than compete: route with noise-aware SABRE, then synthesize with BQSKit. Future work points toward crosstalk-aware cost models, dynamic re-calibration loops, and co-design in which the synthesizer's permutation freedom feeds back into the router's candidate scoring.
