# io_uring and the Proactor Turn: Submission/Completion Queues, Fixed Buffers, and Zero-Copy I/O on Linux

## Abstract

The Linux `io_uring` interface, merged in kernel 5.1 by Jens Axboe, replaces the syscall-per-operation contract between applications and the kernel with two lock-free ring buffers in shared memory: a submission queue (SQ) that carries I/O request descriptors and a completion queue (CQ) that carries results. This paper gives a systems-level account of `io_uring` as the first mainstream realization of the *proactor* pattern on Linux — programs submit work orders and later harvest completions, rather than reacting to readiness notifications. We formalize the ring protocol and its memory-ordering obligations, derive a cost model that explains the interface's advantage over `epoll` and `libaio`, and dissect the three mechanisms that compound it: registered (fixed) buffers and file tables that amortize setup, kernel-side submission polling (SQPOLL) that removes syscalls from the hot path altogether, and zero-copy transmission (`IORING_OP_SEND_ZC`) paired with kernel-selected buffer rings on the receive path. We survey empirical evidence from storage-engine benchmarks, prove an amortization bound on per-operation submission cost, and close with the interface's sharp edges: the CPU price of polling, pinned-memory economics, sandboxing gaps, and strict Linux specificity.

##1 Intro

For most of its history, Linux asynchronous I/O has been a story of *readiness*: the kernel tells the application that a descriptor can be read or written without blocking, and the application then performs the operation with an ordinary syscall. `select`, `poll`, and `epoll` are all variations on this reactor theme, and they carried the industry through the C10K era into the C10M era [9]. The limitation is structural. Every operation still costs at least one syscall on submission and one wakeup on completion; the kernel and the application rendezvous twice per unit of work, and each rendezvous crosses the user-kernel boundary with its attendant context-switch and speculation-barrier costs.

The proactor pattern inverts the relationship. Instead of asking the kernel *whether* I/O is possible, the application tells the kernel *what* I/O to perform and is notified when it is *done*. Linux never had a first-class proactor equivalent: POSIX AIO was specified but starved of implementations, and `libaio` supported only `O_DIRECT` file I/O, never sockets, pipes, or buffered files [8].

`io_uring`, merged in Linux 5.1, is the correction. Its central move is to replace syscalls with shared memory: the application and the kernel communicate through a pair of ring buffers, and once the rings are established, submitting and reaping I/O requires no privilege transition at all [1]. This paper argues that `io_uring`'s importance is architectural rather than merely incremental. The rings are not a faster `epoll`; they are a different contract — one that makes batching natural, cancellation explicit, buffer management a first-class kernel concern, and the elimination of data copies an interface property rather than an application trick.

Our contributions are:

1. A precise description of the SQ/CQ ring protocol, including the memory-ordering discipline that makes it sound.
2. A cost model decomposing per-operation latency into syscall, setup, copy, and wakeup terms, with an amortization theorem for batched submission.
3. An analysis of the three compounding mechanisms — fixed resources, SQPOLL, and zero-copy networking — with their exact preconditions and costs.
4. A survey of empirical results and a frank account of the interface's limitations.

##2 Background

### 2.1 A short taxonomy of Linux async I/O

Linux has accumulated four generations of asynchronous I/O, each removing one bottleneck while exposing the next:

- **POSIX AIO (`aio_read`/`aio_write`).** Specified by POSIX, implemented in glibc as a userspace thread pool. True kernel async never materialized on Linux; the interface is widely regarded as a cautionary tale.
- **`libaio` (`io_submit`).** A genuine kernel interface, but restricted to `O_DIRECT` file I/O. It cannot express sockets, pipes, `accept`, or buffered reads, which excludes the entire networking domain [8].
- **`epoll`.** The mature reactor: `epoll_wait` reports readiness, and the application issues the I/O itself. Batching is limited to the readiness side; each operation still costs its own syscall, and edge-triggered mode pushes substantial state-machine complexity into userspace [9].
- **`io_uring`.** The proactor: operations are described once, submitted in batches, and completed asynchronously, with sockets, files, pipes, and even syscalls like `openat` and `connect` expressible as ring operations [1].

The progression is visible in a single metric: the number of user-kernel transitions per completed operation. POSIX AIO and `libaio` need at least two; `epoll`-driven nonblocking I/O needs two to three; `io_uring` needs *zero* in the steady state, amortized over a batch.

### 2.2 Ring buffers and memory ordering

The SQ and CQ are single-producer/single-consumer rings: the application owns the SQ tail and the CQ head; the kernel owns the SQ head and the CQ tail. Indices advance modulo the ring size, which is a power of two. Correctness rests on a single invariant with teeth:

> **Theorem:** If the producer publishes the tail index with release semantics *after* writing the entry, and the consumer reads the entry *before* acquiring the tail, then every consumed entry is fully written. No locks are required.

This is the classic release/acquire protocol, and it is also the interface's most notorious footgun: `io_uring` exposes the raw rings, so applications (or their libraries) must emit the right barriers on every architecture [7]. The `liburing` helper library exists largely to encapsulate this discipline behind `io_uring_get_sqe`, `io_uring_submit`, and `io_uring_wait_cqe` [7].

### 2.3 The proactor precedent: IOCP

Windows I/O Completion Ports showed long ago that completion-based I/O scales: threads dequeue completion packets from a port, and the kernel balances concurrency. `io_uring` is Linux's belated answer, but it goes further than IOCP in one respect — the submission path itself is a shared-memory data structure rather than a syscall, which is what makes the zero-syscall steady state possible.

##3 Methodology

We proceed in three movements. First, an *analytical* cost model decomposes per-operation latency into additive terms — syscall transitions, per-operation setup (file lookup, buffer mapping), data copies, and wakeup latency — and shows how each `io_uring` mechanism deletes or amortizes one term. Second, a *formal* treatment specifies the ring protocol in TLA+ and states the safety invariants a correct implementation must preserve. Third, an *empirical* survey reports published benchmark results comparing `io_uring` against `libaio` and `epoll`-driven designs.

A compact statement of the cost model, in Python, fixes notation for the rest of the paper:

```python
def per_op_cost(n_batch, c_syscall, c_setup, c_copy, c_wakeup, amortized_setup=True):
    # n_batch: operations submitted per io_uring_enter
    # With io_uring batching, one syscall and one wakeup serve the whole batch.
    submit = c_syscall / n_batch
    setup = 0.0 if amortized_setup else c_setup   # fixed buffers/files kill this term
    complete = c_wakeup / n_batch
    return submit + setup + c_copy + complete

# epoll reactor: every op pays its own syscall; setup and copy remain.
def epoll_cost(c_syscall, c_setup, c_copy, c_wakeup):
    return 2 * c_syscall + c_setup + c_copy + c_wakeup
```

The model is deliberately simple — it ignores queueing, NUMA, and interrupt effects — but it correctly predicts *where* the wins come from, which is what we need to evaluate each mechanism on its own terms.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Ring Pair: Submission and Completion Queues

An `io_uring` instance is created by `io_uring_setup`, which returns a file descriptor and fills in `io_uring_params` with the offsets at which the SQ ring, CQ ring, and SQE array must be `mmap`ed [1]. Three shared regions result:

| Region | Producer | Consumer | Contents |
|---|---|---|---|
| SQ ring | application | kernel | indices into the SQE array, plus head/tail |
| SQE array | application | kernel | fixed 64-byte submission queue entries |
| CQ ring | kernel | application | completion queue events (result + `user_data`) |

Submission is a pure userspace act: fill an SQE (opcode, file descriptor, buffer address, length, offset, and an opaque 64-bit `user_data` tag), advance the SQ tail with release ordering, and — in the default mode — call `io_uring_enter` to hand the batch to the kernel [1]. The kernel consumes SQEs, executes them (possibly via worker threads for operations that may block), and posts one CQE per completion carrying a signed result and the echoed `user_data`. Completions may arrive out of order, so `user_data` is the application's only reliable correlation key.

Two flags on submission shape execution. `IOSQE_IO_LINK` chains SQEs so that a later entry executes only if the earlier one succeeds — `openat`, `read`, `close` can be issued as one atomic chain [1]. `IOSQE_ASYNC` forces offload to a worker thread.

The core safety invariant fits in a TLA+ sketch:

```tla
---- MODULE IoUringRing ----
EXTENDS Naturals
CONSTANT N  \* ring size, power of two
VARIABLES sqHead, sqTail, sqEntries

TypeOK == sqHead \in 0..N /\ sqTail \in 0..N
Safety == (sqTail - sqHead) % N <= N   \* never overwrite unconsumed entries
Submit == sqTail' = (sqTail + 1) % N   \* producer: write entry, then release tail
Consume == sqHead' = (sqHead + 1) % N  \* consumer: acquire tail, read entry, advance
====
```

Liveness — that every submitted entry is eventually consumed — is the kernel's obligation and the application's assumption; the spec above deliberately states only what shared memory alone can guarantee.

### 4.2 Registered Resources: Fixed Buffers and Fixed Files

Every I/O operation carries setup costs: resolving a file descriptor through the file table, and mapping a userspace buffer into kernel address space (page pinning and DMA mapping). For small operations these fixed costs dominate. `io_uring` attacks them with *registration*: resources declared once, referenced by index forever after.

`IORING_REGISTER_BUFFERS` pins a vector of userspace buffers and records their kernel mappings in a per-ring table; subsequent SQEs name a buffer by index instead of by address, and the kernel skips the entire mapping path [1]. `IORING_REGISTER_FILES` installs a dense file table so that SQEs carry small integer indices rather than file descriptors, eliminating per-operation file-table lookup and reference counting [2]. The Haskell sketch below models the asymptotic effect:

```haskell
-- Per-operation setup cost with and without registration.
-- Without: O(map) per op, where map includes page pinning and fd lookup.
-- With:    O(1) table index per op; the O(map) cost is paid once at register.
data Mode = Direct | Registered

setupCost :: Mode -> Int -> Double
setupCost Direct n    = fromIntegral n * pinCost
setupCost Registered n = pinCost + fromIntegral n * indexCost
  where pinCost   = 1.0   -- normalized page-pinning cost
        indexCost = 0.01  -- array indexing
```

Registration is not free. Pinned pages cannot be reclaimed, so a ring holding gigabytes of fixed buffers exerts real memory pressure; and the registered set is immutable without re-registration, which pushes buffer-lifecycle complexity back into the application.

### 4.3 Kernel-Side Polling: SQPOLL and the io-wq Backend

In the default mode, `io_uring_enter` is still a syscall per batch — cheap when amortized, but a syscall nonetheless. `IORING_SETUP_SQPOLL` removes even that: the kernel spawns a polling thread that spins on the SQ tail, consuming submissions the moment they appear [2]. The application then performs I/O with *zero* syscalls in the steady state — submission is a memory write, completion is a memory read — provided it checks the `IORING_SQ_NEED_WAKEUP` flag and re-arms the poller with `io_uring_enter` when the thread has gone idle [1][2].

The price is a dedicated CPU core burned in a busy loop, which is why the man page counsels case-by-case evaluation rather than treating SQPOLL as an automatic speedup [2]. The poll thread can be pinned with `IORING_SETUP_SQ_AFF`, and since kernel 5.11 it no longer requires registered files or root privileges (earlier kernels demanded both, with the capability requirement relaxing across 5.11 to 5.13) [2].

Behind the rings, actual execution is handled by `io-wq`, a bespoke workqueue subsystem created for `io_uring` when the generic workqueue proved too rigid about where and when work runs [4]. Each ring gets an `io_wq` bound to the creator's memory context, and operations that cannot complete inline — buffered reads that miss the page cache, for instance — are offloaded to its worker threads.

A Rust-flavored sketch of the SQPOLL steady state shows how little the application does:

```rust
// Steady state with SQPOLL: no syscalls on the hot path.
loop {
    while let Some(sqe) = next_request() {
        sqe.prep_send(fd_idx, buf_idx, len);   // fixed file + fixed buffer
        sqe.set_user_data(token);
        sq_tail.fetch_add(1, Release);          // visible to the poll thread
    }
    while let Some(cqe) = cq_peek() {          // pure shared-memory read
        dispatch(cqe.user_data(), cqe.result());
        cq_head.fetch_add(1, Release);
    }
    if sq_flags.load(Acquire) & NEED_WAKEUP != 0 {
        io_uring_enter(fd, 0, 0, ENTER_SQ_WAKEUP); // rare: re-arm the poller
    }
}
```

### 4.4 Zero-Copy Networking: SEND_ZC and Provided Buffer Rings

Networking is where `io_uring`'s proactor design pays most visibly, because the classic `epoll` plus `recvmsg` loop interleaves readiness syscalls with data syscalls and copies on every receive. Two mechanisms address the two halves.

On the *transmit* path, `IORING_OP_SEND_ZC` performs a zero-copy send: the kernel hands the userspace pages directly to the NIC driver for DMA instead of copying them into socket buffers. The catch is lifetime — the application must not reuse or free the buffer until the kernel signals it is done. `io_uring` solves this with a *notification* CQE: the send completes (data accepted by the stack) with one CQE, and a second CQE with `IORING_CQE_F_NOTIF` fires only when the pages may be recycled [6].

On the *receive* path, the problem is inverted: the application cannot know which connection will produce data next, so pre-assigning one buffer per queued `recv` wastes memory — exactly the flexibility that `poll`-style readiness preserved [3]. The answer is *provided buffer rings*: the application registers a pool of buffers with `IORING_OP_PROVIDE_BUFFERS`, the kernel selects one per incoming datagram, and the CQE reports which buffer id was chosen in its flags. Multishot `recv` then lets a single SQE generate one CQE per datagram indefinitely, so a connection's entire receive lifetime costs one submission [3]. Buffer selection thus recovers the deferred-allocation property of readiness notification without reintroducing its syscalls.

### 4.5 Programming the Proactor: liburing and Completion Discipline

Raw rings are an expert interface — memory barriers, ring arithmetic, and feature probing across kernel versions — so in practice nearly everyone programs through `liburing`, the userspace library maintained alongside the kernel subsystem [7]. Its vocabulary is small and worth learning in full:

1. `io_uring_queue_init` / `io_uring_queue_exit` — create and tear down the ring.
2. `io_uring_get_sqe` — claim the next SQE slot.
3. `io_uring_prep_readv`, `io_uring_prep_send_zc`, `io_uring_prep_accept_direct`, and friends — fill the opcode-specific fields.
4. `io_uring_sqe_set_data` — attach the `user_data` correlation token.
5. `io_uring_submit` — publish the batch (a no-op memory write under SQPOLL; an `io_uring_enter` otherwise).
6. `io_uring_wait_cqe` / `io_uring_peek_cqe`, `io_uring_cqe_get_data`, `io_uring_cqe_seen` — harvest completions and advance the CQ head [7].

The discipline this imposes is the proactor discipline: *every* operation is a state machine keyed by `user_data`, completions arrive out of order, short reads and restart-style retries are ordinary events rather than edge cases, and cancellation (`IORING_OP_ASYNC_CANCEL`) is addressed to tokens, not threads.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 The amortization argument

The cost model of Section 3 yields a clean statement about batching:

> **Theorem:** Let `c_enter` be the cost of one `io_uring_enter` syscall and `c_op` the per-operation kernel execution cost. Submitting `n` operations in one batch gives per-operation submission cost `c_enter / n + c_op`, which tends to `c_op` as `n` grows. Under SQPOLL, `c_enter` is paid only on poller wakeup, so the steady-state submission cost is `c_op` exactly.

*Proof sketch.* `io_uring_enter` consumes all newly published SQEs in a single traversal of the ring; its cost is independent of the batch size. Dividing by `n` and taking the limit gives the result. With SQPOLL the kernel thread performs the traversal, and userspace pays nothing per batch unless the `NEED_WAKEUP` flag forces re-arming. ∎

The practical reading: batching converts a per-operation tax into a per-batch tax, registration converts per-operation setup into per-ring setup, and SQPOLL converts the per-batch tax into a fixed CPU cost. Each step moves cost from the variable column to the fixed column, which is why `io_uring`'s advantage *grows* with load.

### 5.2 What the benchmarks say

The most careful public comparison comes from the YDB team's storage-engine study, which benchmarked `libaio` against `io_uring` across kernels 5.4 through 6.8 on NVMe hardware with `O_DIRECT`, sweeping queue depth, block size, and read/write mix [8]. Their headline result: `io_uring` consistently outperforms `libaio` on throughput and tail latency, with the gap widening at high queue depths where syscall amortization dominates — precisely what the theorem above predicts. They also document a cautionary finding: IOMMU translation overhead can silently dominate both interfaces on some platforms, a reminder that the kernel-userspace boundary is not the only boundary that matters [8].

On the networking side, the `epoll` comparison is best understood structurally rather than as a single number. A reactor loop pays, per message, at minimum one readiness notification slice plus one data syscall; `io_uring` with multishot receive and provided buffers pays a fraction of one submission syscall per message plus the data path, with no copies on transmit when zero-copy send applies [9][3].

| Interface | Syscalls per op (steady state) | Setup per op | Copies (network TX) | Sockets |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `epoll` + nonblocking | 2–3 | full | 1 | yes |
| `libaio` | 2 | full | 1 | no |
| `io_uring` (batched) | 1/n | indexed (registered) | 1 | yes |
| `io_uring` (SQPOLL + ZC) | 0 | indexed (registered) | 0 | yes |

The table's last row is the asymptote, not the default: it requires SQPOLL's dedicated core, registered buffers, and DMA-capable hardware. Real deployments land somewhere in the middle, which is exactly why the per-mechanism accounting of Section 4 matters more than any single benchmark number.

##6 Limitations

**Polling burns cores.** SQPOLL's zero-syscall steady state is purchased with a kernel thread spinning on shared memory. For latency-sensitive or saturated workloads the trade is excellent; for bursty or low-rate workloads it is pure waste, and the man page explicitly warns against treating it as a universal speedup [2].

**Pinned memory is not free.** Registered buffers are locked into RAM for the ring's lifetime. A large fixed-buffer pool reduces paging flexibility system-wide, and on IOMMU-enabled platforms the translation layer can add overhead that rivals the savings [8]. Zero-copy is a contract with the memory subsystem, not just the NIC.

**The sandboxing gap.** Because `io_uring` operations are analogous to syscalls but invisible to `seccomp`, a seccomp-confined process with ring access can bypass its filter entirely; several sandboxing frameworks simply disable `io_uring` [5]. Restriction mechanisms (operation allow-lists and task-level controls) exist but shift the burden to the application to declare its permitted operations up front [5].

**Memory-ordering discipline.** The rings expose raw release/acquire protocols to userspace. A missing barrier is not a performance bug but a correctness bug — torn reads of SQEs, lost wakeups — and it will manifest only on weakly ordered architectures or under specific interleavings [7]. `liburing` encapsulates the discipline, but anyone bypassing it assumes the full burden.

**Portability and surface churn.** `io_uring` is Linux-only, and the opcode surface is still growing — `bind` and `listen` arrived as recently as 2024, multishot variants appear regularly — so applications must probe feature flags per kernel and carry fallback paths [6][2].

---

##7 Conclusion

`io_uring` is best understood not as a faster system-call interface but as a change of contract: from readiness notification to completion notification, from per-operation syscalls to shared-memory rings, from application-managed buffers to kernel-selected buffer pools, from copied transmits to DMA with honest lifetime signaling. Each mechanism in the stack — the SQ/CQ pair, fixed resources, SQPOLL, zero-copy send — moves one cost from the per-operation column to the fixed column, and the composition is what makes the interface dominant under load rather than merely competitive.

What is no longer defensible is treating `io_uring` as an exotic optimization. It is the interface around which the next decade of Linux systems software is being written — in runtimes, databases, and network stacks alike — and fluency in its ring protocol, its resource model, and its cost structure is rapidly becoming table stakes for systems programming on Linux.
