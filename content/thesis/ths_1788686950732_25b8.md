# From FLP Impossibility to Stake-Weighted Finality: A Comparative Analysis of PBFT, HotStuff, and Tendermint Byzantine Consensus

## Abstract

Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus lets mutually distrustful processes agree on a single value even when up to *f* of them behave arbitrarily. This thesis gives a unified comparative analysis of the three protocols defining the modern design space: Castro and Liskov's PBFT (1999), Yin et al.'s HotStuff (2019), and Buchman's Tendermint (2016). Starting from the Fischer–Lynch–Paterson (FLP) impossibility of deterministic consensus in pure asynchrony [1] and the partial-synchrony model of Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer [4], we derive the *n ≥ 3f+1* lower bound and the *2f+1* quorum-intersection rule underlying all three designs. We dissect PBFT's three-phase protocol and cubic view-change, HotStuff's pipelined phases achieving *O(n)* communication via BLS threshold-signature aggregation [5], and Tendermint's round-based proposer rotation with ABCI separation powering proof-of-stake finality [6]. Empirical 20-node benchmarks [7] and analytical complexity tables [8] locate each protocol on the throughput–latency–scalability frontier, explaining why Byzantine consensus costs what it costs and what remains open.

## 1. Introduction

Consensus is the load-bearing abstraction of distributed computing: a collection of processes, each proposing a value, must collectively *decide* on exactly one of them. The problem is deceptively simple to state and notoriously hard to solve once failures enter the picture. In the *crash* failure model — the domain of Paxos and Raft — a faulty process simply stops; *n ≥ 2f+1* replicas and majority quorums suffice. In the *Byzantine* failure model, named after Lamport, Shostak, and Pease's formulation of the Byzantine Generals Problem, a faulty process may lie, equivocate, collude, or behave in arbitrarily adversarial ways [8]. The price of this stronger adversary is steep and exact: no protocol can tolerate *f* Byzantine faults with fewer than *3f+1* replicas, and every safety argument in the field rests on quorum intersection over majorities of size *2f+1*.

**PBFT** (Castro and Liskov, OSDI 1999) was the first *practical* Byzantine state machine replication protocol, with performance comparable to unreplicated services on benign workloads [2]. **HotStuff** (Yin et al., PODC 2019) recast BFT in chained phases and proved *linearity* — *O(n)* communication per decision — achievable without sacrificing responsiveness, by aggregating *2f+1* votes into one threshold signature [3]. **Tendermint** (Buchman, 2016) adapted the PBFT lineage to permissionless stake-weighted validator sets with rotating proposers and the ABCI application interface, becoming the consensus engine of Cosmos [6].

This thesis makes the following contributions:

- A self-contained derivation of the *3f+1* lower bound and the quorum-intersection argument, grounded in the FLP impossibility result [1] and the partial-synchrony model [4] that circumvents it.
- A phase-by-phase comparative anatomy of PBFT, HotStuff, and Tendermint, with precise message and authenticator complexity accounting.
- An analysis of the cryptographic layer — particularly BLS threshold signatures [5] — that converts HotStuff's quadratic vote collection into linear communication.
- An treatment of *economic* finality: how Tendermint-style proof-of-stake binds safety violations to slashable, attributable misbehavior.
- A synthesis of empirical results [7][8] locating each protocol on the throughput–latency–scalability frontier.

> **Theorem (informal):** Under partial synchrony, deterministic Byzantine consensus with optimal resilience *f < n/3* requires *n ≥ 3f+1* replicas; PBFT attains this bound with *O(n²)* normal-case communication, HotStuff attains it with *O(n)*, and Tendermint attains it with *O(n²)* plus accountable, stake-weighted validator sets.

The remainder of this thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 develops the background: failure models, timing models, the FLP result, partial synchrony, and quorum theory. Section 3 defines the comparative methodology and metrics. Section 4 is the deep dive: four subsections on PBFT's normal case, PBFT's view change, HotStuff's linearization, and Tendermint/ABCI plus proof-of-stake finality. Section 5 presents proofs and empirical results. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The consensus problem and the Byzantine adversary

A consensus protocol must satisfy three properties:

1. **Agreement (safety):** no two correct processes decide differently.
2. **Validity:** the decided value was proposed by some process (in state machine replication: the log is a prefix-consistent sequence of client commands).
3. **Termination (liveness):** every correct process eventually decides.

The Byzantine adversary controls up to *f* processes completely: they may send different messages to different recipients (*equivocation*), withhold messages, forge nothing (we assume unforgeable signatures or message authentication codes), and coordinate arbitrarily. The correct processes must guarantee agreement and validity *unconditionally*, and termination under stated timing assumptions.

### 2.2 The FLP impossibility result

In 1985, Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson proved one of the most consequential negative results in computer science: **no deterministic protocol can guarantee consensus in a fully asynchronous system with even one crash failure** [1]. The proof's engine is the *bivalency argument*: some initial configuration is bivalent (both outcomes reachable), and from any bivalent configuration the adversary — controlling message scheduling — can reach another bivalent configuration by delaying exactly the message whose delivery would force a decision. Because asynchrony provides no bound on message delay, no process can distinguish a crashed peer from a slow one, and the adversary can defer decision forever. FLP does not say consensus is impossible in practice; it says deterministic consensus requires *either* timing assumptions *or* randomization. Every practical BFT protocol takes the first exit.

### 2.3 Partial synchrony and the DLS model

Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer formalized the middle ground in 1988: the **partially synchronous** model [4]. In the *eventually synchronous* variant, there exists an unknown Global Stabilization Time (GST) and an unknown bound Δ such that every message sent after GST is delivered within Δ. Before GST, the network is fully asynchronous and the adversary reigns; after GST, timeouts become meaningful. Safety must hold *always* — even during asynchronous periods — while liveness is required only after GST. This matches reality: networks partition and recover; protocols must never fork, but may stall until the partition heals.

Partial synchrony also yields the resilience bound. Suppose *n ≤ 3f*. Partition the replicas into three groups *A, B, C* of size at most *f*. In an asynchronous window, group *C* is silent; *A* and *B* cannot distinguish "C is crashed" from "C is partitioned away." If *A* and *C* are the correct ones, *B*'s silence looks like a crash and the protocol must decide with *A ∪ C*... but symmetrically with *B ∪ C*. A Byzantine group can then play both sides, driving the two partitions to decide differently — a safety violation. Hence **n ≥ 3f+1** is necessary for Byzantine agreement under partial synchrony, and it is sufficient, as the protocols below show.

### 2.4 Quorums and the intersection argument

With *n = 3f+1*, define a **quorum** as any set of *2f+1* replicas. Two quorums *Q₁, Q₂* intersect in at least

$$|Q_1 \cap Q_2| \geq 2(2f+1) - (3f+1) = f+1$$

replicas — and since at most *f* are faulty, **every quorum intersection contains at least one correct replica**. This is the load-bearing lemma of the entire field: a correct replica that voted in *Q₁* can be relied upon to report that vote when *Q₂* is assembled, so no two quorums can certify contradictory decisions. Every safety proof in Sections 4.1–4.5 is an elaboration of this single counting argument.

## 3. Methodology

Our comparison uses a uniform analytical framework applied to all three protocols.

**System model.** *n = 3f+1* replicas, at most *f* Byzantine, partial synchrony with unknown GST and Δ, reliable links, unforgeable signatures. Clients submit commands; correct replicas execute them in identical total order.

**Metrics.** Message and authenticator complexity per committed command; latency in communication rounds; view-change cost; **responsiveness** (commit latency depends only on actual delay δ, not worst-case Δ); **accountability** (safety violations identify culprits).

**Evidence base.** Safety claims reduce to the quorum-intersection lemma; complexity figures follow the BFT literature's standard accounting [8]; performance draws on controlled AWS benchmarks of all three protocols on identical 20-node LAN/WAN deployments [7].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 PBFT: the three-phase normal case

PBFT replicates a deterministic service across *n = 3f+1* replicas with a designated **primary** *p = v mod n* for view *v*. A client sends its request to the primary, which drives the three-phase protocol [2]:

- **Pre-prepare.** The primary assigns a sequence number *s* and multicasts `⟨PRE-PREPARE, v, s, d(m)⟩`, where *d(m)* is the request digest. This is the *only* phase in which a single replica speaks for the group.
- **Prepare.** Backups validate and multicast `⟨PREPARE, v, s, d(m), i⟩`. A request is **prepared** when a replica holds the pre-prepare plus *2f* matching prepares — a *2f+1* quorum certifying agreement on ordering.
- **Commit.** Replicas multicast `⟨COMMIT, v, s, d(m), i⟩`. A request is **committed-local** when a replica holds *2f+1* matching commits; it executes after all lower sequence numbers. The client accepts after *f+1* matching replies.

*Safety argument.* Two prepare quorums for different digests at the same *(v, s)* intersect in a correct replica (Section 2.4), which would have had to prepare both — impossible. The two levels matter: prepare certifies a quorum *agrees on order*; commit certifies a quorum *knows* a quorum agrees — the second level is what survives view changes.

*Complexity.* Pre-prepare is *O(n)*; prepare and commit are all-to-all, *O(n²)* messages each. Total: **O(n²) messages per request** (amortized over batching). Latency is 3 message delays plus execution — and crucially, PBFT is *responsive*: phases advance on quorum receipt, never waiting for Δ.

*Authentication.* PBFT's original design used pairwise MACs for replica-to-replica messages (fast, but *O(n)* authenticators per multicast) and public-key signatures only for view-change and checkpoint messages, a pragmatic split between the hot path and the rare path.

### 4.2 View change, checkpoints, and liveness in PBFT

If the primary is faulty or the network is slow, backups trigger a **view change** on timeout. Each replica *i* multicasts `⟨VIEW-CHANGE, v+1, C, P, i⟩`, where *C* is the latest stable checkpoint proof and *P* is the set of requests prepared-but-not-committed since *C*. The new primary *p' = (v+1) mod n* waits for *2f+1* view-change messages, then multicasts `⟨NEW-VIEW, v+1, V, O⟩` where *V* is the collected set and *O* re-proposes the prepared requests at their original sequence numbers.

*Why safety survives.* A request committed-local at some correct replica had *2f+1* commits, hence *2f+1* replicas prepared it. Any *2f+1* view-change quorum intersects that prepare set in a correct replica, whose *P* set carries the prepared request into the new view. The new primary is therefore *forced* to re-propose it — the honest overlap of Section 2.4, weaponized. This is the deepest idea in PBFT: liveness mechanisms (leader replacement) are constrained by safety certificates so that progress can never rewrite history.

*Cost.* View change is the protocol's Achilles' heel: *O(n²)* view-change messages each carrying *O(n)*-sized *P* sets, and an *O(n²)* new-view message — **O(n³) authenticator complexity** in the worst case. Checkpoints bound this: every *K* sequence numbers, *2f+1* matching checkpoint messages form a stable checkpoint that garbage-collects the log.

*Liveness.* After GST, timeouts grow exponentially until they exceed Δ; an honest primary eventually holds the view and pending requests complete. PBFT is live under partial synchrony but not responsive across view changes.

### 4.3 HotStuff: pipelining, linearity, and threshold signatures

HotStuff's insight is that PBFT's all-to-all phases exist only to *disseminate* votes, and dissemination can be centralized at the leader without weakening the quorum argument [3]. Each view has a leader; a view consists of three voting phases — **prepare**, **pre-commit**, **commit** — plus a final **decide** broadcast:

1. The leader proposes *m* extending the highest quorum certificate (QC) it knows.
2. Replicas send their vote *to the leader only*.
3. The leader aggregates *2f+1* votes into a **QC** — a single threshold signature — and broadcasts it as the next phase's proposal.

A QC is transferable proof that *2f+1* replicas voted: with BLS threshold signatures [5], *2f+1* partial signatures combine into one constant-sized aggregate verifiable against a single group public key. Vote collection is *O(n)* messages to the leader; the QC broadcast is *O(n)*. Per phase: **O(n)**, versus PBFT's *O(n²)*.

**Chained HotStuff** pipelines the phases: the QC for phase *k* of block *B* simultaneously serves as the proposal for phase *k+1*, so a single message stream carries three interleaved block pipelines. A block is committed when three chained QCs form — the *three-chain rule* — mirroring PBFT's prepare-then-commit two-level certification (HotStuff needs three levels because phases are leader-mediated rather than all-to-all).

*Pacemaker and responsiveness.* Leaders rotate every view; replicas advance on timeout or on the leader's message, so replacing a leader costs *O(n)* — no cubic blowup. HotStuff is **responsive**: after GST, decisions complete in *O(δ)* actual network time.

*The cryptographic linchpin.* None of this works without signature aggregation. Naive multisignatures would require tracking *which* replicas signed (a bitmask) and verifying *O(n)* keys; BLS threshold signatures [5] give constant-size QCs with a single verification. The trade-off is a distributed key generation (DKG) setup and pairing-friendly curve arithmetic — heavier per-operation than Ed25519, but asymptotically decisive.

### 4.4 Tendermint: round-based BFT with ABCI and rotating proposers

Tendermint adapts the PBFT lineage to blockchains with two structural changes [6]:

1. **Rotating proposers and rounds.** Time is divided into *heights* (block positions), each with numbered *rounds*. The proposer of round *r* is determined deterministically (weighted round-robin over the validator set). Each round runs three all-to-all gossip phases — **propose**, **prevote**, **precommit** — closely analogous to PBFT's pre-prepare/prepare/commit but with an explicit locking rule: a validator that precommits for block *B* at *(h, r)* becomes *locked* on *B* and will only prevote for *B* (or for a block backed by a polka — *2f+1* prevotes — from a higher round). This lock is the Tendermint analogue of PBFT's *P*-set: it is the certificate that a future proposer must respect, enforced by the validators themselves rather than by a new-view message.
2. **ABCI: the application blockchain interface.** Consensus is separated from execution by a socket protocol: the consensus engine calls `CheckTx`, `DeliverTx`/`FinalizeBlock`, and `Commit` on the application. This made Tendermint the first *general-purpose* BFT engine — the same consensus core drives currencies, exchanges, and name services — and the architectural ancestor of the Cosmos SDK.

*Gossip and complexity.* Tendermint validators gossip votes rather than multicasting directly, which preserves *O(n²)* worst-case message complexity but improves robustness on unreliable networks. Notably, Tendermint phases include a **timeout-driven wait** (the `timeoutPrevote`/`timeoutPrecommit` steps): unlike PBFT and HotStuff, Tendermint is *not* fully responsive — each phase waits for either a quorum *or* a timeout, adding δ-scale latency that benchmarks confirm as the highest among the three protocols [7].

*Validator sets and stake.* Validators are weighted by bonded stake; quorums are counted in *voting power*, not heads. This is a profound shift: the *2f+1*-of-*3f+1* counting argument is preserved, but the "replicas" are economic actors whose misbehavior can be *punished*.

### 4.5 Proof-of-stake finality and the economic layer

In proof-of-stake BFT, safety violations are not merely *prevented* — they are *attributed and punished*. The mechanism is **slashing**: validators lock stake as a bond, and protocol-defined evidence of misbehavior (double-signing two blocks at the same height, or surrounding votes in Casper FFG) triggers automatic confiscation. Because every vote is a signature, a safety violation leaves cryptographic evidence identifying at least *f+1* culprits — the quorum-intersection lemma again, now with economic teeth. Systems like Ethereum's Gasper (Casper FFG finality gadget over LMD-GHOST) finalize checkpoints when *2/3* of stake attests, giving *economic finality*: reverting a finalized block requires burning at least one-third of total stake [6].

This introduces problems absent from classical BFT: **costless equivocation** (validators voting on multiple forks for free, countered by slashing conditions), **long-range attacks** (adversaries forging alternate histories from old keys, countered by *weak subjectivity* — new nodes bootstrap from a recent trusted checkpoint), and **liveness vs. inactivity** (offline validators halt finality; *inactivity leaks* drain non-participants' stake until the online set regains a *2/3* supermajority). The fault threshold thus shifts from a hardware assumption to an incentive assumption — weaker against irrational attackers, stronger against rational ones.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The 3f+1 lower bound (proof sketch)

We prove necessity of *n ≥ 3f+1* for Byzantine agreement under partial synchrony [4]. Assume *n ≤ 3f* and partition replicas into three groups *A, B, C*, each of size ≤ *f*. Consider two scenarios. *Scenario 1:* group *C* is Byzantine and silent toward *B* but behaves correctly toward *A*; groups *A* and *C* are correct and must decide *v₁*. *Scenario 2:* group *C* is Byzantine and silent toward *A*; groups *B* and *C* are correct and must decide *v₂ ≠ v₁*. During the asynchronous period before GST, group *A* cannot distinguish Scenario 1 (where *B* is the faulty group, silent) from a scenario where *B* is merely partitioned away — so *A* must decide *v₁* hearing only from *A ∪ C*. Symmetrically, *B* decides *v₂* hearing only from *B ∪ C*. After GST the truth emerges, but two correct groups have decided differently: safety violated. Therefore *n ≥ 3f+1*. Quorum systems with *2f+1* majorities achieve it, so the bound is tight.

### 5.2 Complexity comparison

| Protocol | Normal-case messages | Authenticators | Latency (delays) | View-change cost | Responsive |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT [2] | *O(n²)* | *O(n²)* MACs | 3 | *O(n³)* | Yes (normal case) |
| HotStuff [3] | *O(n)* | *O(n)* threshold sigs | 4 (pipelined) | *O(n)* | Yes |
| Tendermint [6] | *O(n²)* (gossip) | *O(n²)* signatures | 3 + timeouts | *O(n²)* (next round) | No (timeout-gated) |

The literature's full accounting confirms the ordering: HotStuff-family view changes cost *O(fn)* versus PBFT's *O(f(n³+2n²+n))* for *f* successive faulty leaders [8].

### 5.3 Measured performance

Controlled AWS benchmarks on identical 20-node LAN and WAN deployments with 90 clients [7] report: **throughput** — HotStuff highest in LAN, near Paxos (crash-tolerant) in WAN, via responsive leader rotation and 4-stage pipelining; PBFT and Tendermint degrade faster as *n* grows, consistent with *O(n²)* all-to-all phases. **Latency** — Tendermint highest due to per-phase timeout waits; PBFT's latency grows more steeply with *n* than HotStuff's. **Scalability** — per-decision message count is the first-order throughput determinant: quadratic protocols saturate the network before the CPU.

### 5.4 Responsiveness, formally

A protocol is *responsive* if, after GST, its commit latency depends only on the actual network delay δ, never on the worst-case bound Δ. PBFT's normal case and HotStuff (via its pacemaker) are responsive; Tendermint's timeout-gated phases are not, paying Δ-scale penalties per phase even on fast networks. Responsiveness matters because Δ must be provisioned conservatively (seconds to minutes in WAN deployments), while δ is typically milliseconds — a responsive protocol converts that gap directly into latency wins [3].

## 6. Limitations

**PBFT's view-change bottleneck.** The *O(n³)* worst case dominates recovery under *f* consecutive faulty primaries, and the original paper specified no reconfiguration protocol for dynamic replica sets [2].

**HotStuff's leader centralization and cryptography.** Linear communication concentrates *O(n)* signature verifications on the leader per phase; a Byzantine leader can censor for a view (rotation bounds the damage). BLS threshold signatures [5] need distributed key generation and costly pairing operations — and post-quantum migration would forfeit aggregation entirely.

**Tendermint's liveness and gossip costs.** Timeout-gated phases sacrifice responsiveness; gossip multiplies bandwidth by fan-out; deterministic proposer selection is predictable and DDoS-able without sentry architectures.

**Economic finality's assumptions.** Slashing punishes only attributable faults; an adversary with ≥1/3 of stake breaks safety regardless of cryptography; weak subjectivity concedes that new nodes cannot bootstrap trust purely from the protocol.

**Common gaps.** None of the three addresses *fair ordering* (the MEV problem), reconfiguration is bolted on, and under sustained asynchrony liveness is lost by FLP-necessity [1].

## 7. Conclusion

From FLP's bivalency argument to slashable stake, the history of Byzantine consensus is the history of a single question: *what must correct processes assume, and what must they pay, to agree despite arbitrary malice?* The answers form a clean progression. FLP [1] proved timing assumptions unavoidable; DLS [4] supplied the partial-synchrony model that makes them useful; the *3f+1* bound and quorum-intersection lemma fixed the price of the adversary. PBFT [2] showed the price could be paid in practice with three phases and certificates that survive leader replacement. HotStuff [3] showed the quadratic tax was optional — threshold cryptography [5] linearizes vote collection while pipelining hides latency. Tendermint [6] showed the same machinery could secure open, stake-weighted validator sets, converting safety violations from undetectable faults into attributable, punishable offenses.

The Pareto frontier is now well mapped: linearity and responsiveness (HotStuff), deployment-proven simplicity with economic accountability (Tendermint), and the pedagogical and historical foundation (PBFT). The open frontiers — fair ordering, post-quantum aggregation, asynchronous BFT with practical latency, and reconfiguration without trusted operators — are where the next decade's impossibility results and protocols will be written. The *n ≥ 3f+1* inequality will survive them all; it is the one theorem in this thesis that no engineering can repeal.
