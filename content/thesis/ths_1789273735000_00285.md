# Algorithmic Information Theory: Kolmogorov Complexity, Solomonoff Induction, and the Minimum Description Length Principle

## Abstract

This thesis develops the mathematical theory that grounds information content in the individual object rather than the ensemble. We formalize Kolmogorov complexity K(x), the length of the shortest program for a universal Turing machine that outputs the finite string x, and prove the invariance theorem that renders it machine-independent up to an additive constant [1][2]. We then construct Solomonoff's universal prior M(x), a semimeasure dominating every computable predictive distribution, and show how Bayesian conditioning on it yields an optimal inductive procedure whose cumulative prediction error is bounded by K of the true data-generating measure [3][4]. We contrast this incomputable ideal with its computable shadow, Rissanen's Minimum Description Length (MDL) principle, developing crude two-part codes, the refined normalized-maximum-likelihood (NML) code, and the parametric complexity that quantifies a model class's expressive power [5][6]. Finally we extend universal induction to sequential decision making through Hutter's AIXI agent, proving its self-optimizing behavior and mapping the frontier between uncomputable optimality and the practical approximations — NCD, NML, prequential codes — that bring algorithmic information theory into statistics and machine learning [7][8].

##1 Introduction

Shannon's information theory assigns entropy to *distributions*: the bit `0110010110` drawn from a fair coin carries ten bits because the ensemble does, not because of anything intrinsic to the string itself. Algorithmic information theory, inaugurated independently by Solomonoff, Kolmogorov, and Chaitin in the 1960s, reverses the locus of information. The question it asks is not "how surprised should a decoder knowing only the source distribution be?" but rather "what is the shortest program that generates this exact object?" [1]. The answer — the *Kolmogorov complexity* K(x) — measures the intrinsic information content of an individual string, with no probabilistic ensemble required.

This shift is profound for inductive inference. Science itself is the business of compressing observations into short laws: Newton's *F = ma* compresses astronomical tables into a program of a few dozen symbols plus initial conditions. Solomonoff saw that Occam's razor could be *formalized*: the shortest program consistent with the data is, in a precise Bayesian sense, the most probable explanation, and weighting *all* consistent programs by an exponential penalty on their length yields a predictor that dominates every computable alternative [3][4]. Kolmogorov, approaching from probability theory, asked when a finite string deserves to be called "random" and gave the algorithmic definition that now underwrites the theory of randomness. Chaitin gave the cleanest formulation — prefix complexity — and discovered the halting probability Ω, a real number whose bits are algorithmically random.

Yet the theory has a notorious blemish: K is uncomputable. No algorithm can, given x, return its true complexity. This does not render the theory idle — the *incompressibility method* turns uncomputability into a proof technique, and the universal prior's convergence theorems set the gold standard against which computable learners are judged [2]. Moreover, Rissanen's Minimum Description Length principle translates the algorithmic ideal into statistical practice: restrict the description language to a model class, and the "best" model is the one minimizing the two-part code length *L(H) + L(D|H)*, the length of the hypothesis plus the length of the data encoded with its help [5]. Refined MDL sharpens this to a single one-part universal code, the normalized maximum likelihood distribution, whose parametric complexity is computable and minimax-optimal [6]. At the summit stands AIXI, Hutter's universally optimal reinforcement learner, which merges Solomonoff induction with Bellman-optimal sequential decisions and is Pareto-optimal in a strong sense [7].

This thesis reconstructs that entire arc: foundations (§2), method (§3), deep dives into prefix complexity, algorithmic probability, refined MDL, and AIXI (§4), formal theorems and empirical evidence (§5), limitations (§6), and a conclusion (§7).

##2 Background

### The three 1960s origin stories

*Solomonoff (1960–1964).* Ray Solomonoff's "A Formal Theory of Inductive Inference" (Parts I and II, 1964) proposed that prediction be reduced to Bayesian inference over *programs*: enumerate all prefix Turing machines, assign prior probability 2^−ℓ(p) to program p, and predict by posterior-weighted mixture [3][4]. Solomonoff explicitly synthesized Epicurus' principle of multiple explanations with Occam's razor and Bayes' rule — the mixture never commits to a single hypothesis, yet concentrates exponentially fast on the true one when it is simple.

*Kolmogorov (1965).* Andrei Kolmogorov's "Three approaches to the quantitative definition of information" defined the combinatorial, probabilistic, and *algorithmic* approaches to information, proposing K(x) as the length of the shortest program generating x on a fixed universal machine, and proved the invariance theorem: changing the universal machine perturbs K by at most an additive constant [1]. Kolmogorov then defined random strings as those with K(x) ≈ |x|, connecting randomness to incompressibility.

*Chaitin (1966–1975).* Gregory Chaitin simplified the framework by restricting attention to *prefix* (self-delimiting) machines, whose programs form a prefix-free code satisfying the Kraft inequality. This yields a clean theory: prefix complexity K, algorithmic probability, and the halting probability Ω = Σ_{p halts} 2^−ℓ(p), whose binary expansion is a paradigm of a random, uncomputable real [2].

### Shannon versus Kolmogorov

The relationship is captured by a coding theorem: for a computable distribution P, the expected prefix complexity satisfies E_P[K(x)] = H(P) + O(1), i.e. average algorithmic complexity recovers Shannon entropy up to a constant [2]. But K goes further: it assigns content to *individual* strings, and conditional complexity K(x|y) supports an algorithmic mutual information I(x:y) = K(x) + K(y) − K(x,y), mirroring Shannon's but without ensembles.

> **Theorem:** *Invariance.* For any two universal prefix machines U, V there exists a constant c_{U,V} such that |K_U(x) − K_V(x)| ≤ c_{U,V} for all x. Hence K(x) is well-defined up to an O(1) term independent of x.

The proof is a one-line compiler argument: let c_{V→U} be a program that simulates V on U; prepend it to V-programs. This constant swallows all reference-machine arbitrariness, and asymptotic statements about K are machine-free.

### Computability landscape

K is *upper semicomputable*: we can enumerate ever-shorter programs for x and converge on K(x) from above, but no algorithm halts with the exact value — determining K would solve the halting problem. Its time-bounded cousin K^t (Levin's Kt) is computable and grounds practical approximations. The structure function and sophistication refine the crude notion of "shortest program" by separating meaningful structure from accidental noise, anticipating MDL's model-versus-residual split [6].

##3 Methodology

Our methodology is analytic and comparative. We (i) reconstruct definitions and principal theorems from primary sources [1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8]; (ii) prove the key results — invariance, the coding theorem, Solomonoff's convergence bound, MDL consistency, AIXI self-optimizingness — in compressed but rigorous form; (iii) compare the three inductive frameworks (Solomonoff induction, crude MDL, refined MDL) along the axes of universality, computability, and statistical consistency; (iv) survey empirical evidence for the computable surrogates (NCD clustering, NML model selection, prequential neural codes); and (v) delimit the theory's failures: uncomputability, the constant problem, dependence on the reference machine at small scales, and the gap between AIXI's optimality and any implementable agent.

Notation: {0,1}* is the set of finite binary strings; ℓ(x) its length; U a fixed universal *prefix* machine; K(x) = min{ℓ(p) : U(p) = x}; K(x|y) conditional complexity; M(x) = Σ_{p: U(p) starts with x} 2^−ℓ(p) the universal prior; `+=` means equality up to an additive constant; expectations are in bits, logs base 2.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Prefix Complexity and the Coding Theorem

Prefix complexity earns its central role through two properties. First, the Kraft inequality Σ_x 2^−K(x) ≤ 1 turns K into a *code*: the lengths K(x) are the codeword lengths of an effective prefix code (the shortest-program code). Second, the **coding theorem** (Levin): −log M(x) += K(x), i.e. algorithmic probability and prefix complexity are two faces of the same object — the shortest description dominates the universal mixture up to a constant factor [2].

```haskell
-- Prefix complexity as shortest self-delimiting program
kolmogorov :: UniversalMachine -> String -> Int
kolmogorov u x = minimum [ length p | p <- allPrograms, haltsOn u p x ]
-- Invariance: for any universal U, V, |k_U x - k_V x| <= c
```

The proof sketch is instructive. Enumerate programs by dovetailing; the shortest-program code assigns length K(x) to x, and since it is a prefix code, Σ 2^−K(x) ≤ 1. But M dominates every enumerable semimeasure, including 2^−K(x): hence M(x) ≥ 2^−K(x)−c. Conversely, Shannon–Fano coding of M gives a prefix code with lengths ⌈−log M(x)⌉+1, which by universality of K cannot beat the shortest program: K(x) ≤ −log M(x) + O(1). Equality up to a constant follows.

Three structural theorems deserve emphasis. **Symmetry of information**: K(x,y) += K(x) + K(y|x*) += K(y) + K(x|y*), where x* is a shortest program for x — additivity of complexity holds for *individual* objects, not merely on average [2]. **The incompressibility lemma**: for each n, at least a (1 − 2^−c) fraction of strings of length n satisfy K(x) ≥ n − c; random strings are the overwhelming majority. **Uncomputability**: no total computable function lower-bounds nontrivially and no partial computable function coincides with K — otherwise the Berry paradox ("the smallest integer not nameable in fewer than n symbols") becomes an algorithm.

### 4.2 Solomonoff Induction and Algorithmic Probability

Solomonoff's construction begins with the universal prior over *infinite* sequences, built from a universal monotone machine: M(x) = Σ_{p: output begins with x} 2^−ℓ(p). This M is a *semimeasure* (it sums to at most 1) that **dominates** every enumerable semimeasure ν: there exists c_ν > 0 with M(x) ≥ c_ν·ν(x) for all x. In particular, for every computable measure μ, M ≥ 2^−K(μ)·μ: the universal prior's only prejudice is a complexity penalty on the environment itself [3][4].

Prediction proceeds by Bayes' rule: M(a|x_{<t}) = M(x_{<t}a)/M(x_{<t}). The centerpiece is Solomonoff's convergence theorem, sharpened by Hutter: if data are drawn from a computable measure μ, the total squared prediction error of the universal predictor is bounded by

> **Theorem:** *Solomonoff–Hutter bound.* Σ_{t≥1} E_μ[(M(x_t|x_{<t}) − μ(x_t|x_{<t}))^2] ≤ K(μ)·ln 2 + O(1) [4].

*Interpretation:* the universal predictor makes only finitely many mistakes, bounded by the complexity of the truth — it converges to informed prediction for every computable sequence, without knowing μ. This is the strongest possible justification of Occam's razor: among all computable predictors, Solomonoff's is *Pareto-optimal*, and no predictor can do uniformly better, since for any predictor there exists a simple μ on which it incurs more error than M does [3].

```python
def solomonoff_predict(history, programs, horizon=1):
    # M(a | history) = sum_{p: p -> history+a} 2^-len(p) / sum_{p: p -> history} 2^-len(p)
    num = sum(2.0 ** -len(p) for p in programs if p.generates(history + [1]))
    den = sum(2.0 ** -len(p) for p in programs if p.generates(history))
    return num / den if den > 0 else 0.5
```

The price: M is uncomputable (only lower-semicomputable), and even its approximations are catastrophically expensive. The *speed prior* (Schmidhuber) and Levin's Kt-weighted variants trade optimality for computability, yielding predictors that are optimal among time-bounded agents — the pragmatic compromise the field actually deploys [2].

### 4.3 Minimum Description Length: Crude and Refined

Rissanen's MDL principle (1978) is Solomonoff induction made statistical: choose the model minimizing *L(H) + L(D|H)*, the description length of the hypothesis plus the data encoded with its help [5]. Crude two-part MDL works beautifully as model selection: for polynomial regression, L(H) ≈ (k/2) log n encodes k parameters to 1/√n precision and L(D|H) = −log P(D|θ̂) is the fit; the sum balances underfitting against overfitting without any held-out data.

But crude MDL is arbitrary — the code for H is a design choice, and a "lucky" code can make any hypothesis win. **Refined MDL** (Rissanen 1996; Grünwald 2007) eliminates the arbitrariness by assigning each model class ℳ a single *universal code*, the normalized maximum likelihood (NML) distribution [6]:

> **Definition:** *NML.* P̄_NML(x^n | ℳ) = max_{θ∈Θ} P(x^n|θ) / Σ_{y^n} max_{θ∈Θ} P(y^n|θ).

The denominator's logarithm is the **parametric complexity** COMP_n(ℳ), measuring the class's capacity to fit arbitrary data. Refined MDL selects the class minimizing −log P̄_NML(D|ℳ) = −log P(D|θ̂) + COMP_n(ℳ): goodness-of-fit plus an intrinsic, code-independent complexity penalty.

```tla+
---- MODULE RefinedMDL ----
EXTENDS Reals
CONSTANTS Data, ModelClasses
VARIABLES bestClass
Score(M) == -LogLikelihood(Data, MLE(Data, M)) + ParametricComplexity(M)
\* Invariant: the selected class minimizes the one-part codelength
Select == bestClass' = CHOOSE M \in ModelClasses : \A N \in ModelClasses : Score(M) <= Score(N)
====
```

The four universal codes — two-part, Bayes mixtures, prequential (predictive-sequential), and NML — are asymptotically equivalent yet have distinct finite-sample behavior; NML is minimax-optimal for the regret, while prequential coding ("predict each point with the model fit on the past") needs no quantization and extends naturally to deep learning [6]. MDL's asymptotic theory is strong: under mild conditions it is consistent, achieves minimax-optimal rates, and its redundancy is (k/2) log n + O(1) for k-dimensional parametric families — the celebrated (k/2) log n penalty rederived from first principles.

| Framework | Universality | Computability | Model cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solomonoff M | All computable μ | Lower-semicomputable only | K(μ) penalty |
| Crude MDL | Restricted to ℳ | Computable | Ad hoc L(H) |
| Refined MDL (NML) | Restricted to ℳ | Computable (often) | Parametric complexity |
| Bayes mixtures | Prior-dependent | Often tractable | −log prior |

### 4.4 AIXI: Universal Intelligence as Optimal Sequential Decisions

Hutter's AIXI (2000; book 2005) lifts Solomonoff induction from passive prediction to *active* agency. The environment is an unknown computable chronological measure ν; the agent selects actions by expectimax over all programs weighted by the universal prior ξ, the action-conditional analogue of M [7]:

a*_t = argmax_{a_t} Σ_{o_t r_t} … max_{a_m} Σ_{o_m r_m} [r_t + … + r_m] · ξ(o_{1:m} r_{1:m} | a_{1:m}),

the Bellman-optimal policy with the true environment replaced by the universal mixture. AIXI is *self-optimizing*: in any environment where some policy is asymptotically optimal and learnable, AIXI attains the optimal average reward — it cannot be beaten in a strong Pareto sense, and its "intelligence order relation" formalizes what it means for one agent to dominate another [7].

```rust
// AIXI expectimax skeleton: mixture over all computable environments
fn aixi_action(history: &History, horizon: usize, prior: &UniversalMixture) -> Action {
    // argmax_a E_{nu ~ xi}[ sum of future rewards | a, optimal continuation ]
    best_action(prior.posterior(history), horizon) // Bellman-optimal in the mixture
}
```

AIXI also exposes the theory's central tension: the optimal agent is uncomputable, and even Levin-style computable variants (AIXI-tl) have astronomical constants. Practical descendants — Monte Carlo AIXI, Thompson-sampling approximations, and modern Bayesian RL — inherit the architecture (mixture + planning) while sacrificing the optimality certificate [7]. Legg and Hutter's definition of *universal intelligence* as expected reward across all computable environments weighted by 2^−K(μ) remains the cleanest formalization of "general intelligence" the field possesses [7].

##5 Empirical Evidence and Formal Proofs

**Formal core.** The results proven or sketched above — invariance [1], coding theorem and symmetry of information [2], the Solomonoff convergence bound [3][4], MDL's (k/2) log n redundancy and consistency [5][6], AIXI's self-optimizingness [7] — constitute a chain from definition to optimal agency. The Berry-paradox proof of K's uncomputability and Martin-Löf's demonstration that K-randomness coincides with his measure-theoretic randomness (K(x_{1:n}) ≥ n − O(1) iff x passes all effective statistical tests) close the loop between algorithmic and classical randomness [2].

**Empirical evidence for the computable surrogates.**

1. *Clustering by compression.* Cilibrasi and Vitányi's normalized compression distance NCD(x,y) = (C(xy) − min(C(x),C(y))) / max(C(x),C(y)), with C a real compressor, approximates the uncomputable normalized information distance. Applied blindly — no features, no domain knowledge — it reconstructed mammalian phylogeny from mitochondrial genomes (supporting Marsupionta over Theria), and clustered languages, music, and literary texts correctly [8].
2. *NML in practice.* Refined MDL with NML codes is the state of the art in histogram construction, decision-tree pruning, and AR-order selection, consistently matching or beating BIC/AIC with finite-sample guarantees neither possesses [6].
3. *Prequential deep learning.* Online (prequential) coding with neural predictors yields description lengths competitive with Bayesian marginal likelihoods for architecture comparison, showing MDL scales to modern model classes [6].
4. *AIXI approximations.* Monte Carlo AIXI solves small POMDPs and bandit problems optimally in the limit, and its regret bounds transfer to the practical Thompson-sampling agents used in industry [7].

**A worked miniature.** Consider the string s = (01)^500, a thousand alternating bits. Its Shannon entropy under the empirical distribution is 1000 bits; its Kolmogorov complexity is K(s) ≤ ℓ("print '01' 500 times") + O(1) ≈ 40 bits. Solomonoff's prior assigns M(s) ≥ 2^−40 — enormously larger than the 2^−1000 a fair-coin hypothesis gives — so after observing a few dozen repetitions, the posterior concentrates on the periodic program and predicts `01` with near certainty. MDL with the model class "periodic strings of period ≤ k" selects period 2 with total length ≈ log k + O(1) bits versus 1000 for the null model: compression *is* learning, made quantitative.

##6 Limitations

The theory's limitations are as instructive as its theorems.

1. **Uncomputability is fundamental, not incidental.** K, M, and AIXI cannot be computed; only bounded from above (K) or below (M). Every application relies on a surrogate — real compressors, NML, prequential codes, Monte Carlo planning — and the surrogate's behavior need not inherit the ideal's theorems. NCD, for instance, is not a metric for arbitrary compressors and can be skewed by window sizes [8].
2. **The constant problem.** All equalities hold up to O(1) terms that can dominate at the short string lengths where applications live. The invariance theorem is vacuous for strings shorter than the compiler constant; practical claims about "the Kolmogorov complexity of this genome" smuggle in a choice of compressor that the theory does not license.
3. **Reference-machine dependence.** Prefix versus plain complexity, the choice of universal machine, and the enumeration order all shift results by constants or logarithmic terms. Asymptotic statements are robust; finite-sample ones are not.
4. **MDL's model-class relativity.** MDL selects the best model *within* the entertained class; if the truth lies outside ℳ, consistency guarantees are void, and the two-part code's arbitrariness never fully disappears — refined MDL fixes the code given the class but not the class itself.
5. **AIXI's impracticality and wireheading.** AIXI-tl is computable only in a technical sense; its constants exceed any physical budget. Worse, the reward-maximization formalism admits degenerate "wireheading" solutions the theory does not rule out — optimality is relative to the reward channel, not to human intent [7].
6. **No free lunch for induction.** Solomonoff's bound is relative to *computable* environments; against an adversarial, uncomputable nature the guarantees evaporate, and the universal prior's convergence can be arbitrarily slow for high-K truths.

##7 Conclusion

Algorithmic information theory answers a question Shannon left unasked: how much information does an *individual* object contain? Kolmogorov's K(x) — the shortest program generating x — turns out to be the keystone of a complete inductive edifice. Solomonoff showed that weighting all programs by 2^−ℓ(p) yields a universal prior whose Bayesian predictions converge to any computable truth with total error bounded by the truth's own complexity, formalizing Occam's razor as a theorem rather than a slogan [3][4]. Rissanen translated the ideal into statistics: MDL's two-part and refined one-part codes give model selection a principled, minimax-grounded foundation in which complexity penalties like (k/2) log n emerge from coding arguments rather than being postulated [5][6]. Hutter closed the arc by embedding universal induction in Bellman-optimal agency, producing AIXI — provably self-optimizing, provably unimplementable, and the clearest existing formalization of general intelligence [7].

The enduring lesson is methodological: *compression and understanding are the same phenomenon viewed from different angles.* Every successful learning algorithm, from gzip-based clustering to prequential neural codes, is an approximation to the same uncomputable ideal [8][6]. The ideal cannot be reached, but knowing exactly where it lies — and exactly how far each approximation falls short — is what makes the theory, six decades on, still the deepest account of induction we have.
