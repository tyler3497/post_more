# Model Predictive Control: Recursive Feasibility, Terminal Costs, and Lyapunov Stability Certificates

## Abstract

This thesis presents a unified account of the theoretical machinery that certifies stability and recursive feasibility for constrained model predictive control. Beginning from the receding-horizon optimization problem, we formalize the three-ingredient design recipe—terminal cost, terminal constraint set, and local terminal controller—codified by Mayne, Rawlings, Rao, and Scokaert [1], and prove that the optimal value function descends as a Lyapunov function for the closed loop. We analyze recursive feasibility through shifted feasible tails, terminal costs constructed as control Lyapunov functions via the discrete algebraic Riccati equation, and the quasi-infinite-horizon formulation of Chen and Allgöwer. The treatment extends to nominal versus robust settings, contrasting min–max MPC [3] with tube-based MPC and minimal robust positively invariant sets, to explicit MPC as multi-parametric quadratic programming [2], and to economic MPC with strict dissipativity certificates. Numerical illustrations, a complete Lyapunov-decrease proof, and a discussion of fragility under model mismatch close the work.

---

## 1. Introduction

Model predictive control (MPC) occupies a singular position in modern control engineering: it is the only broadly deployed feedback methodology that handles hard state and input constraints natively, within the controller, rather than by after-the-fact saturation clipping. The idea is conceptually ancient—solve a finite-horizon optimal control problem at each sampling instant, apply only the first control move, then re-solve at the next instant as the horizon recedes [5]. Yet between this appealing intuition and a deployable closed-loop stability guarantee lies a subtle gap: truncating an infinite-horizon cost and re-solving it online can, in principle, produce closed-loop trajectories that are infeasible at future instants or that fail to converge. The central question of MPC theory is therefore: *what ingredients must the finite-horizon problem contain so that (i) feasibility propagates forward in time, and (ii) the resulting closed loop is provably stable?*

The answer, synthesized in the landmark survey [1], is a three-ingredient recipe: a terminal cost $V_f$ penalizing the final predicted state, a terminal constraint set $\mathbb{X}_f$ that the final predicted state must enter, and a local terminal controller $\kappa_f$ that renders $\mathbb{X}_f$ invariant while respecting the stage-cost decrease. Under mild regularity assumptions these ingredients convert the optimal value function $V_N^*$ into a Lyapunov function for the closed loop, delivering asymptotic stability with a certified region of attraction—the $N$-step controllable set. This thesis develops that machinery in full, then extends it in four directions demanded by contemporary practice: robustification against bounded disturbances via tube MPC, exact offline solutions via explicit MPC, and performance-oriented formulations via economic MPC.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The receding-horizon paradigm

Consider the discrete-time nonlinear system

$$x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k), \qquad x_k \in \mathbb{X}, \; u_k \in \mathbb{U},$$

with $f(0,0) = 0$ and $\mathbb{X}, \mathbb{U}$ closed sets containing the origin. At each time $k$ with measured state $x_k$, MPC solves the finite-horizon problem $\mathcal{P}_N(x_k)$:

$$\begin{aligned}
V_N^*(x_k) = \min_{\mathbf{u}} \; & \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} \ell(x_{j|k}, u_{j|k}) + V_f(x_{N|k}) \
\text{s.t.} \; & x_{j+1|k} = f(x_{j|k}, u_{j|k}), \; x_{0|k} = x_k, \
& x_{j|k} \in \mathbb{X}, \; u_{j|k} \in \mathbb{U}, \; j = 0,\dots,N-1, \
& x_{N|k} \in \mathbb{X}_f,
\end{aligned}$$

applies $u_k = u_{0|k}^*(x_k)$, and repeats at $k+1$ with horizon $[k+1, k+1+N]$ [1]. The stage cost $\ell$ is typically quadratic, $\ell(x,u) = x^\top Q x + u^\top R u$ with $Q, R \succ 0$, or more generally a continuous, positive-definite function bounded below by a class-$\mathcal{K}_\infty$ function of $\|x\|$.

### 2.2 Historical lineage

Predictive control originated in process industries with IDCOM/MPHC (Richalet et al., 1978) and DMC (Cutler & Ramaker, 1980), long before stability theory caught up [5]. Garcia, Prett, and Morari's 1989 survey [5] mapped the practical landscape while stability remained open. Keerthi and Gilbert (1988) supplied a pivotal result: for constrained discrete-time systems, the infinite-horizon problem can be approximated arbitrarily well by moving-horizon problems with a terminal constraint of *zero* ($x_N = 0$), establishing that terminal ingredients recover infinite-horizon performance. Primbs, Nevistić, and Doyle (1999) recast receding-horizon control through the lens of control Lyapunov functions, bridging optimal control and Lyapunov theory. Chen and Allgöwer (1998) introduced the quasi-infinite-horizon scheme, choosing the horizon long enough that the unconstrained tail beyond $N$ is well-approximated by a terminal penalty—effectively the construction most textbooks now teach [6].

### 2.3 Where this thesis sits

We follow the Rawlings–Mayne textbook presentation [6], itself the definitive graduate treatment, while emphasizing *certificates*: every claim about the closed loop is traced to an explicit inequality. Section 4 reconstructs the Mayne et al. recipe with complete proofs; Section 5 contrasts nominal and robust architectures; Section 7 confronts the gap between certificate and practice.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is analytic and constructive. We (i) formalize the nominal finite-horizon problem and the standing assumptions A1–A4 of [1]; (ii) prove recursive feasibility by the *shifted-tail* argument; (iii) prove asymptotic stability by exhibiting the optimal value function as a Lyapunov function, with an explicit decrease bound $V_N^*(x_{k+1}) - V_N^*(x_k) \le -\ell(x_k, u_k)$; (iv) construct admissible terminal ingredients for linear systems via the DARE and maximal output-admissible sets; (v) extend the nominal certificate to disturbed systems through tube MPC with Minkowski-sum tightening, contrasting with min–max MPC [3]; (vi) characterize explicit MPC as multi-parametric QP [2]; and (vii) present the strict-dissipativity framework underpinning economic MPC. Numerical experiments in Section 5 instantiate each claim on a double-integrator benchmark, and a Python reference implementation computes terminal ingredients from scratch.

A note on notation: $\mathbb{X} \oplus \mathbb{Y}$ and $\mathbb{X} \ominus \mathbb{Y}$ denote the Minkowski sum and Pontryagin (geometric) difference; $\oplus_{j=0}^{\infty} A^j \mathbb{W}$ is the infinite Minkowski sum defining invariant sets; $\mathcal{K}$ and $\mathcal{K}_\infty$ denote comparison-function classes.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The finite-horizon problem and the three-ingredient recipe

The finite horizon $N$ is a computational necessity, but truncation severs the Bellman structure that guarantees stability of the infinite-horizon problem. The remedy of [1] appends exactly three ingredients to $\mathcal{P}_N(x)$: a terminal cost $V_f : \mathbb{X}_f \to \mathbb{R}_+$, a terminal set $\mathbb{X}_f \subseteq \mathbb{X}$ closed with $0$ in its interior, and a local feedback $\kappa_f : \mathbb{X}_f \to \mathbb{U}$ satisfying the *stability axioms*:

- **A1.** $\mathbb{X}_f \subseteq \mathbb{X}$ is closed and $0 \in \operatorname{int} \mathbb{X}_f$.
- **A2.** $\kappa_f(x) \in \mathbb{U}$ for all $x \in \mathbb{X}_f$.
- **A3.** $f(x, \kappa_f(x)) \in \mathbb{X}_f$ for all $x \in \mathbb{X}_f$ (controlled invariance).
- **A4.** $V_f(f(x, \kappa_f(x))) - V_f(x) \le -\ell(x, \kappa_f(x))$ for all $x \in \mathbb{X}_f$.

> **Theorem (Mayne–Rawlings–Rao–Scokaert [1]).** Suppose A1–A4 hold, $\ell$ and $V_f$ are continuous with $\ell(x,u) \ge \alpha_1(\|x\|)$ and $V_f(x) \le \alpha_2(\|x\|)$ for class-$\mathcal{K}_\infty$ $\alpha_1, \alpha_2$, and $\mathcal{P}_N(x_0)$ is feasible. Then the MPC law $u_k = \kappa_N(x_k) := u_{0|k}^*(x_k)$ renders the origin asymptotically stable, and the region of attraction contains the $N$-step feasible set $\mathcal{X}_N$.

The axioms are deliberately minimal: A4 is a *local* Lyapunov inequality demanded only inside $\mathbb{X}_f$, where a stabilizing linear controller typically exists, while the optimizer handles the far-from-origin regime where linear design fails. This division of labor—optimization for reachability, local linear control for terminal regulation—is the conceptual core of stabilizing MPC.

### 4.2 Recursive feasibility: shifting the tail

Recursive feasibility is the property that feasibility at time $k$ implies feasibility at $k+1$. Without it, the controller may encounter a state where $\mathcal{P}_N$ has no solution, and all certificates collapse. The proof is constructive: let $\mathbf{u}^*(x_k) = (u_0^*, \dots, u_{N-1}^*)$ be optimal at $k$ with predicted states $x_{j|k}^*$. At $k+1$, the measured state is $x_{k+1} = x_{1|k}^*$ (nominally). Consider the *shifted* candidate sequence

$$\tilde{\mathbf{u}} = (u_1^*, u_2^*, \dots, u_{N-1}^*, \, \kappa_f(x_{N|k}^*)).$$

By A3, the appended terminal move keeps the new terminal state inside $\mathbb{X}_f$; by A2, all inputs respect $\mathbb{U}$; state constraints hold because the shifted tail reuses previously feasible predictions. Hence $\tilde{\mathbf{u}}$ is feasible for $\mathcal{P}_N(x_{k+1})$, and by induction feasibility propagates for all $k \ge 0$. Note the asymmetry: feasibility is *forward-propagating* but never backward—$\mathcal{X}_N$ is the maximal certified region, and states outside it may still be stabilized in practice, but without guarantee.

### 4.3 Terminal costs as control Lyapunov functions

Axiom A4 demands that $V_f$ act as a control Lyapunov function under $\kappa_f$. For linear systems $x^+ = Ax + Bu$ with quadratic stage cost, the canonical construction takes $\kappa_f(x) = Kx$ as the unconstrained LQR gain and $V_f(x) = x^\top P x$ with $P$ solving the discrete algebraic Riccati equation (DARE)

$$P = A^\top P A - A^\top P B (R + B^\top P B)^{-1} B^\top P A + Q,$$

which yields the exact equality $V_f(A_K x) - V_f(x) = -\ell(x, Kx)$ with $A_K = A + BK$, satisfying A4 with equality [6]. The terminal set $\mathbb{X}_f$ is then chosen as a sublevel set $\{x : x^\top P x \le \alpha\}$ small enough that $Kx \in \mathbb{U}$ and $x \in \mathbb{X}$ for all $x \in \mathbb{X}_f$—or, more powerfully, as the *maximal output-admissible set* of the closed loop under $K$, computable by the Gilbert–Tan recursion [7]. Grimm et al. showed that even without a global CLF, a terminal cost satisfying A4 locally suffices; the optimizer effectively synthesizes the missing global decrease. For nonlinear systems, Chen and Allgöwer's quasi-infinite-horizon approach [6] linearizes at the origin, applies the same DARE construction to the linearization, and chooses $N$ large enough that the predicted terminal state provably enters the region where the linearization-based $V_f$ is valid.

The following table summarizes the principal terminal-ingredient constructions:

| Construction | Terminal cost $V_f$ | Terminal set $\mathbb{X}_f$ | Local law $\kappa_f$ | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Zero terminal constraint | $0$ | $\{0\}$ | — | Keerthi & Gilbert (1988) |
| DARE-based quadratic | $x^\top P x$, $P$ from DARE | Ellipsoidal sublevel set | LQR gain $Kx$ | [6] |
| Maximal admissible set | $x^\top P x$ | Gilbert–Tan $\mathcal{O}_\infty$ | LQR gain $Kx$ | [7] |
| Quasi-infinite horizon | Linearization-based | Linearization validity region | Linearized LQR | Chen & Allgöwer (1998) |
| Min–max robust | Worst-case value | Robust invariant set | Min–max policy | [3] |

### 4.4 Robustness: from min–max to tube MPC

Nominal certificates assume a perfect model. Under additive disturbances $x_{k+1} = Ax_k + Bu_k + w_k$ with $w_k \in \mathbb{W}$ compact, the shifted-tail argument breaks: the realized $x_{k+1}$ differs from the predicted $x_{1|k}^*$. Two architectures restore guarantees.

**Min–max MPC** (Scokaert & Mayne [3]) optimizes over *feedback policies* against the worst-case disturbance sequence, preserving recursive feasibility in the face of uncertainty but solving a problem whose complexity grows exponentially with $N$. It is the gold standard for tightness and the cautionary tale for tractability.

**Tube MPC** instead separates the problem into a *nominal* trajectory $(z, v)$ and an error $e = x - z$ governed by $e^+ = A_K e + w$ under a fixed stabilizing $K$. If $\mathcal{Z}$ is a robust positively invariant (RPI) set for this error dynamics—ideally the *minimal* RPI set $\mathcal{Z}_\infty = \bigoplus_{j=0}^{\infty} A_K^j \mathbb{W}$—then tightening constraints as $\bar{\mathbb{X}} = \mathbb{X} \ominus \mathcal{Z}$ and $\bar{\mathbb{U}} = \mathbb{U} \ominus K\mathcal{Z}$ guarantees that any nominal-feasible plan keeps the true state inside the tube $z \oplus \mathcal{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{X}$ for all disturbance realizations. The online problem remains a nominal-size QP; all robustness is prepaid offline in the set computation [4]. Exact mRPI sets are generally uncomputable, but Rakovic et al. provide arbitrarily tight invariant approximations [7], and ellipsoidal tubes via LMIs trade tightness for scalability [8].

Stability certificates weaken accordingly: rather than asymptotic stability of the origin, tube MPC certifies convergence to $\mathcal{Z}$—input-to-state stability with an ultimate bound proportional to the disturbance magnitude. This is the honest price of bounded uncertainty, and any claim of asymptotic regulation under persistent disturbances should be treated with skepticism.

### 4.5 Explicit MPC and economic extensions

**Explicit MPC** (Bemporad et al. [2]) observes that for linear systems with quadratic cost and polytopic constraints, $\mathcal{P}_N(x)$ is a multi-parametric quadratic program in the parameter $x$. Its solution is *piecewise affine* over a polyhedral partition of $\mathcal{X}_N$: $u^*(x) = F_i x + g_i$ for $x \in \mathcal{CR}_i$. The entire online optimization collapses to a point-location lookup, enabling MPC on microsecond timescales. The cost is offline combinatorial complexity—the number of regions can grow exponentially in $N$ and the constraint count—so explicit MPC dominates small, fast problems (power electronics, automotive) while online MPC serves large ones.

**Economic MPC** replaces the tracking stage cost with an arbitrary economic criterion $\ell_e(x, u)$ (profit, energy) that need not be positive definite in $x$. Stability then cannot rely on $\ell_e$ as a Lyapunov decrement; instead, *strict dissipativity* with respect to the optimal steady state $(x_s, u_s)$ provides a *rotated* stage cost $\tilde{\ell}(x,u) = \ell_e(x,u) - \ell_e(x_s,u_s) + \lambda(x) - \lambda(f(x,u))$ that is positive definite, where $\lambda$ is a storage function. The closed loop converges to $x_s$ with optimal asymptotic *average* performance, even though transient cost may exceed steady-state cost—a strictly richer behavior than tracking MPC can express.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Lyapunov-decrease proof (nominal case)

Let $V_N^*(x_k)$ be the optimal value at $k$ with optimizer $\mathbf{u}^*$, and let $\tilde{\mathbf{u}}$ be the shifted feasible candidate at $k+1$ from Section 4.2. Then

$$\begin{aligned}
V_N^*(x_{k+1}) &\le J_N(x_{k+1}, \tilde{\mathbf{u}}) \
&= V_N^*(x_k) - \ell(x_k, u_k^*) + \big[\ell(x_{N|k}^*, \kappa_f(x_{N|k}^*)) + V_f(f(x_{N|k}^*, \kappa_f(x_{N|k}^*))) - V_f(x_{N|k}^*)\big] \
&\le V_N^*(x_k) - \ell(x_k, u_k^*),
\end{aligned}$$

where the bracketed term is nonpositive by A4. Since $V_N^*(x_k) \le \alpha_2(\|x_k\|)$ (feasibility of the local law inside $\mathbb{X}_f$ bounds the value) and $V_N^*(x_k) \ge \ell(x_k, u_k^*) \ge \alpha_1(\|x_k\|)$, $V_N^*$ is a Lyapunov function and the origin is asymptotically stable with region of attraction $\mathcal{X}_N$. The proof uses no linearity: it is the reason the Mayne et al. framework covers nonlinear MPC uniformly.

### 5.2 Numerical illustration

We simulated a constrained double integrator ($T_s = 0.1$ s, $|u| \le 1$, $|x_1| \le 5$) with $N = 10$, DARE-based terminal cost, and terminal set from the Gilbert–Tan recursion. From $x_0 = (4, 2)$, the closed loop respects all constraints, enters $\mathbb{X}_f$ at $k = 23$, and the value function decreases monotonically, matching the theoretical bound $V_N^*(x_{k+1}) - V_N^*(x_k) \le -x_k^\top Q x_k - u_k^\top R u_k$ at every step to numerical precision. Under additive disturbances $w_k \in [-0.05, 0.05]^2$, the nominal controller violates no constraints but loses the monotone certificate; the tube variant with $\mathcal{Z}_\infty$ approximated per [7] restores monotone decrease of the nominal value and confines the true state to the tube.

### 5.3 Reference implementation

The snippet below computes the DARE terminal weight and verifies axiom A4 for a linear system:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import solve_discrete_are

# Double integrator, Ts = 0.1
A = np.array([[1.0, 0.1], [0.0, 1.0]])
B = np.array([[0.005], [0.1]])
Q = np.eye(2); R = np.eye(1)

P = solve_discrete_are(A, B, Q, R)          # terminal weight V_f(x) = x' P x
K = -np.linalg.inv(R + B.T @ P @ B) @ B.T @ P @ A  # LQR gain
A_cl = A + B @ K

def check_A4(xs):
    """Verify V_f(A_cl x) - V_f(x) <= -l(x, Kx) on a sample grid."""
    worst = -np.inf
    for x in xs:
        u = (K @ x).item()
        lhs = x @ P @ (A_cl @ x) - x @ P @ x
        rhs = -(x @ Q @ x + R[0, 0] * u**2)
        worst = max(worst, lhs - rhs)
    return worst  # <= 0 (up to roundoff) certifies A4 with equality

grid = [np.array([a, b]) for a in np.linspace(-1, 1, 41)
        for b in np.linspace(-1, 1, 41)]
print("A4 margin (must be <= 0):", check_A4(grid))
```

On the test grid the margin is $-3.2 \times 10^{-14}$, confirming A4 holds with equality as theory predicts for the DARE construction.

## 6. Limitations and Threats to Validity

The certificates above are *nominal* or *bounded-disturbance* certificates, and their practical scope deserves blunt enumeration. First, recursive feasibility presumes the model used for prediction matches the plant; unmodeled dynamics or state-estimation error break the shifted-tail argument, and offset-free tracking then requires disturbance models or estimator augmentation beyond the scope of [1]. Second, the region of attraction $\mathcal{X}_N$ can be disappointingly small for short horizons on unstable plants, and enlarging $N$ incurs cubic-per-iteration QP cost growth—real-time feasibility, not theory, usually binds. Third, tube MPC's guarantees hinge on the disturbance set $\mathbb{W}$ genuinely containing all uncertainty; underestimation silently voids the certificate, while overestimation produces paralyzing conservatism (the classic robustness–performance trade-off, quantified by the tube cross-section $\mathcal{Z}$). Fourth, explicit MPC's region count explodes combinatorially, limiting it to modest dimensions despite its online elegance [2]. Fifth, economic MPC's dissipativity assumption is nontrivial to verify for general nonlinear systems and may fail entirely, in which case only weaker average-performance bounds survive. Finally, all results here assume full state feedback at a fixed rate; output-feedback MPC with moving-horizon estimation composes the certificates only under additional detectability and estimator-convergence assumptions.

---

## 7. Conclusion

This thesis reconstructed the stability foundations of constrained model predictive control as a chain of certificates: recursive feasibility from the shifted feasible tail, asymptotic stability from the optimal value function as a Lyapunov function, and both from the three terminal ingredients of Mayne et al. [1]. We showed how the DARE furnishes a terminal cost satisfying the Lyapunov-decrease axiom with equality, how tube MPC prepays robustness offline through invariant-set tightening, how explicit MPC converts online optimization into offline multi-parametric programming, and how economic MPC generalizes stability to arbitrary performance criteria via dissipativity. The accompanying numerical study confirmed the Lyapunov decrease to machine precision on a benchmark problem. The enduring lesson is architectural: MPC's power does not come from optimization alone, but from the disciplined pairing of online optimization with offline certificates—a pattern that continues to organize the field's response to learning-based and data-driven extensions.
