# Lattice Quantum Chromodynamics: The Wilson Action, Fermion Doubling, and the Sign Problem at Finite Density

## Abstract

Lattice quantum chromodynamics is the only known nonperturbative, systematically improvable regulator of the strong interaction. Its foundation is Kenneth Wilson's 1974 formulation of gauge theory on a discrete Euclidean lattice [1], which trades the continuum path integral for a finite-dimensional integral over SU(3) link variables amenable to Monte Carlo sampling. This thesis develops the subject's three pillars: the Wilson gauge action and its continuum limit, the fermion doubling problem with the Nielsen–Ninomiya no-go theorem and the chiral constructions that evade it, and the sign problem obstructing lattice QCD at finite baryon density. We derive the Wilson plaquette action and its continuum expansion, demonstrate the sixteenfold doubling of the naive Dirac operator in momentum space, and analyze the resolutions — Wilson, staggered, domain-wall, and overlap fermions — through the Ginsparg–Wilson relation [3]. We formulate Hybrid Monte Carlo as the exact algorithm for dynamical fermions [4][5], explain why chemical potential destroys importance sampling, and survey complex Langevin, Lefschetz thimbles, and related cures [6][7][8], grounding the discussion in four decades of numerical evidence [9].

## 1 Intro

Quantum chromodynamics — the SU(3) Yang–Mills theory of quarks and gluons — is *asymptotically free* but strongly coupled in the infrared, and perturbation theory fails precisely where the most interesting phenomena live: confinement, chiral symmetry breaking, the hadron spectrum, and the phase diagram of nuclear matter. A regulator is therefore needed that (i) preserves gauge invariance exactly, (ii) renders the path integral finite-dimensional and hence computable, and (iii) possesses a well-defined continuum limit. Wilson's lattice provides exactly this [1]: replace continuous Minkowski spacetime by a finite hypercubic grid of spacing *a*, replace the gauge potential *Aμ(x)* by link variables *Uμ(x)* in the gauge group, and replace the Yang–Mills action by a sum over elementary plaquettes. The resulting partition function is an ordinary (if enormous) integral over a compact manifold, and importance sampling turns it into a computational problem.

The intellectual arc of this thesis follows three crises, each resolved by deepening the theory. *First*, the naive discretization of the continuum action must reproduce continuum QCD as *a → 0*; this demands asymptotic freedom, universality, and control of lattice artifacts through Symanzik improvement. *Second*, naive discretization of the Dirac operator multiplies fermion species by sixteen — the **fermion doubling problem** — and the Nielsen–Ninomiya theorem (1981) proves that no local, chirally symmetric bilinear lattice Hamiltonian can host a single Weyl fermion [2]. The escape routes — Wilson's explicit chiral-symmetry breaking, staggered fermions with their four tastes, and the Ginsparg–Wilson fermions that realize an exact lattice chiral symmetry — define the modern zoo of lattice Dirac operators [3]. *Third*, introducing a baryon chemical potential *μ* makes the fermion determinant complex, and the Monte Carlo method collapses: the **sign problem**. Recent years have produced a flourishing of candidate cures — complex Langevin dynamics, Lefschetz-thimble integration, contour deformations, and tensor networks [6][7][8] — but a controlled solution for cold dense QCD remains open.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Euclidean path integral

Lattice QCD works in Euclidean spacetime, where the partition function

$$Z = \int \mathcal{D}A\,\mathcal{D}\bar\psi\,\mathcal{D}\psi\; e^{-S_E[A,\bar\psi,\psi]}$$

is formally analogous to a statistical-mechanics partition function with Boltzmann weight *e^(−S_E)*. Wick rotation converts Minkowski amplitudes into Euclidean correlation functions, from which masses follow from exponential decay:

> **Theorem:** *For a gauge-invariant interpolating operator O with the quantum numbers of a hadron H, the Euclidean two-point function admits the spectral representation ⟨O(t)O†(0)⟩ = Σₙ |⟨0|O|n⟩|² e^(−Eₙt), so the ground-state energy E₀ = m_H is extracted from the large-t exponential falloff.*

Monte Carlo evaluation therefore reduces spectroscopy to measuring exponential decays on a finite ensemble of gauge configurations — provided the measure *e^(−S)* is positive.

### 2.2 Link variables and gauge invariance

On a lattice Λ with spacing *a*, the fundamental gauge degrees of freedom are **link variables**

$$U_\mu(x) = \mathcal{P}\exp\left(ig\int_x^{x+a\hat\mu} A\cdot dx\right) \in \mathrm{SU}(3),$$

living on the oriented edges from site *x* to *x + aμ̂*. Under a gauge transformation Ω(x) ∈ SU(3),

$$U_\mu(x) \to \Omega(x)\,U_\mu(x)\,\Omega^\dagger(x+a\hat\mu),$$

so the trace of any closed loop of links — a **Wilson loop** — is gauge invariant. The smallest such loop, the 1×1 **plaquette**

$$U_{\mu\nu}(x) = U_\mu(x)U_\nu(x+a\hat\mu)U_\mu^\dagger(x+a\hat\nu)U_\nu^\dagger(x),$$

is the building block of the gauge action. Expanding *Uμν = exp(iga²Fμν + O(a³))* shows that the plaquette measures the discretized field-strength tensor, and confinement will later be diagnosed through the area law of large Wilson loops [1][9].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Wilson gauge action

Wilson's gauge action is the simplest gauge-invariant lattice functional with the correct naive continuum limit [1]:

$$S_W[U] = \beta \sum_{x}\sum_{\mu<\nu}\left(1 - \frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,U_{\mu\nu}(x)\right), \qquad \beta = \frac{2N}{g_0^2},$$

with *N = 3* for QCD (so *β = 6/g₀²*). Expanding the plaquette,

$$\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,U_{\mu\nu} = 1 - \frac{g_0^2 a^4}{4N}\mathrm{Tr}\,F_{\mu\nu}^2 + O(a^6),$$

recovers the continuum Yang–Mills action *S = (1/4)∫ d⁴x Tr F²* up to *O(a²)* discretization errors. The partition function

$$Z = \int \prod_{x,\mu} dU_\mu(x)\; e^{-S_W[U]}$$

integrates each link against the normalized Haar measure of SU(3); compactness of the group makes the integral convergent without gauge fixing — a decisive advantage over continuum perturbation theory.

### 3.2 Importance sampling and the quenched approximation

Expectation values ⟨O⟩ = Z⁻¹∫𝒟U O[U] e^(−S) are estimated by generating *N_cfg* configurations distributed as *e^(−S)/Z* and averaging. The original 1980 simulations by Creutz studied pure SU(2) gauge theory on tiny lattices, establishing confinement numerically and verifying asymptotic freedom through the scaling of the string tension [1][9]. Integrating out the Grassmann quark fields analytically,

$$\int \mathcal{D}\bar\psi\,\mathcal{D}\psi\; e^{-\bar\psi (D[U]+m)\psi} = \det(D[U]+m),$$

leaves an effective gauge action *S_eff = S_W − Σ_f ln det(D + m_f)*. Setting the determinant to unity — the **quenched approximation** — was the workhorse of the 1980s and 1990s; modern simulations include *N_f = 2+1* (and increasingly *2+1+1*) dynamical flavors via the algorithms of §4.4 [4].

| Quantity | Lattice definition | Continuum meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Link *Uμ(x)* | Path-ordered exponential of *Aμ* | Parallel transporter |
| Plaquette *Uμν* | 1×1 Wilson loop | *exp(iga²Fμν)* |
| Wilson loop *W(C)* | Tr ∏_{C} U | *exp(−σ·Area)* if confined |
| Polyakov loop *L(x⃗)* | Tr ∏_t U₄(x⃗,t) | Order parameter for deconfinement |
| Topological charge *Q* | Σ_x q(x), *q ∼ Tr F F̃* | Winding of gauge field |

### 3.3 Setting the scale

The lattice spacing *a* is not an input but is determined *a posteriori*: one computes a dimensionful quantity (e.g. the Ω-baryon mass, the pion decay constant *f_π*, or the gradient-flow scale *w₀*) in lattice units and matches to experiment, yielding *a* in femtometers. The continuum limit is then taken at fixed physics by sending *β → ∞* (hence *g₀ → 0*) along a line of constant physics — possible only because of asymptotic freedom, which guarantees that the continuum limit sits at vanishing bare coupling.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Wilson action, confinement, and the continuum limit

The Wilson action's triumph is that it makes confinement *computable*. The static quark–antiquark potential *V(r)* is extracted from rectangular *R × T* Wilson loops via *⟨W(R,T)⟩ ∼ exp(−V(R)T)*, and an **area law** *⟨W⟩ ∼ exp(−σA)* implies a linearly rising potential *V(r) ∼ σr* with string tension *σ ≈ (440 MeV)²*. Creutz ratios,

$$\chi(R,T) = -\ln\frac{W(R,T)W(R-1,T-1)}{W(R,T-1)W(R-1,T)},$$

cancel perimeter divergences and converge to *σa²*, providing a clean lattice estimator of the running coupling [9]. The absence of any phase transition separating strong and weak coupling in SU(3) — established numerically in the early 1980s [1] — means the confining strong-coupling regime is analytically connected to the asymptotically free weak-coupling regime where the continuum limit is taken. Universality then asserts that any lattice action in the same basin of attraction (Wilson, Symanzik-improved, Iwasaki, DBW2) yields identical continuum physics, differing only in the rate at which *O(a²)* artifacts vanish.

### 4.2 Fermion doubling and the Nielsen–Ninomiya theorem

Discretizing the free Dirac operator with the symmetric difference,

$$D_{\mathrm{naive}} = \sum_\mu \gamma_\mu \frac{\nabla_\mu + \nabla_\mu^*}{2} + m,$$

gives, in momentum space,

$$\tilde D_{\mathrm{naive}}(p) = i\sum_\mu \gamma_\mu \frac{\sin(p_\mu a)}{a} + m.$$

The propagator *D̃⁻¹(p)* has poles wherever *sin(pμa) = 0* — that is, at *pμ ∈ {0, π/a}* for each of the four directions, producing **2⁴ = 16** degenerate fermion species in the continuum limit instead of one. This is *fermion doubling*, and it is not an artifact of a clumsy discretization but a topological inevitability:

> **Theorem (Nielsen–Ninomiya, 1981):** *No local, translation-invariant, bilinear, hermitian lattice Hamiltonian can describe a single Weyl fermion with an exactly conserved, quantized chiral charge. Any such theory contains equal numbers of left- and right-handed species in each complex representation of the symmetry group [2].*

The proof is a counting argument on the Brillouin zone: Weyl nodes are Berry-curvature monopoles, and because the Brillouin torus is compact, their total chirality must vanish [2]. Evading the theorem requires sacrificing at least one hypothesis — locality, exact chiral symmetry, or bilinearity — and each sacrifice defines a fermion formulation.

### 4.3 The fermion zoo: Wilson, staggered, domain-wall, and overlap

- **Wilson fermions (1974).** Add the irrelevant Wilson term *−(ar/2)ψ̄□ψ*, a momentum-dependent mass that vanishes as *O(a)* at *p ≈ 0* but contributes *O(1/a)* at the doubler momenta, lifting fifteen species out of the spectrum. The price is explicit breaking of chiral symmetry: *O(a)* discretization errors, additive mass renormalization, and the need to tune *κ → κ_c* to reach the chiral limit. *Clover (Sheikholeslami–Wohlert) improvement* removes *O(a)* errors.
- **Staggered (Kogut–Susskind) fermions.** Spin-diagonalization reduces sixteen species to four **tastes** with a remnant U(1) chiral symmetry and *O(a²)* errors, making staggered fermions the cheapest formulation — the basis of the MILC program's precision results. Simulating *N_f ≠ 4* flavors requires the **fourth-root trick**, *det¹ᐟ⁴*, whose locality and universality were fiercely debated but are now supported by extensive numerical evidence.
- **Ginsparg–Wilson fermions.** In 1982 Ginsparg and Wilson derived the lattice remnant of chiral symmetry,

$$\{\gamma_5, D\} = a\,D\gamma_5 D,$$

which evades Nielsen–Ninomiya because *D* does not anticommute with *γ₅* [3]. Two explicit solutions emerged in the 1990s: **domain-wall fermions** (Kaplan 1992; Shamir), which localize a chiral zero mode on a defect in a fictitious fifth dimension, and the **overlap operator** (Neuberger 1997–98),

$$D_{\mathrm{ov}} = \frac{1}{a}\left(1 + \gamma_5\,\mathrm{sgn}\,H_W\right), \qquad H_W = \gamma_5 D_W,$$

which satisfies the relation exactly and reproduces the correct axial anomaly via the index theorem [3]. These formulations carry exact chiral symmetry at finite *a* at roughly two orders of magnitude greater cost than Wilson fermions [5].

```python
# Naive vs. Wilson Dirac operator spectra on a free 1D lattice
import numpy as np

def naive_dispersion(p, a=1.0):
    """E(p) = sin(pa)/a: extra zero at the Brillouin zone edge p = pi/a."""
    return np.sin(p * a) / a

def wilson_dispersion(p, a=1.0, r=1.0):
    """Wilson term (r/a)(1 - cos(pa)) lifts the doubler to mass ~ 2r/a."""
    return np.sqrt((np.sin(p * a) / a) ** 2 + (r * (1 - np.cos(p * a)) / a) ** 2)

p = np.linspace(0, np.pi, 9)
for pi in p:
    print(f"p={pi:5.3f}  naive E={naive_dispersion(pi):7.4f}  Wilson E={wilson_dispersion(pi):7.4f}")
# At p = pi the naive operator has a spurious zero (a doubler);
# the Wilson operator gives it a cutoff-scale mass instead.
```

### 4.4 Hybrid Monte Carlo: simulating dynamical fermions

Including *det(D†D)* in the measure requires sampling gauge fields with a nonlocal fermionic weight — the central algorithmic problem of lattice QCD [4]. The **Hybrid Monte Carlo (HMC)** algorithm (Duane, Kennedy, Pendleton, Roweth, 1987) solves it by introducing pseudofermion fields,

$$\det(D^\dagger D) = \int \mathcal{D}\phi^\dagger\mathcal{D}\phi\;
\exp\!\left(-\phi^\dagger (D^\dagger D)^{-1}\phi\right),$$

conjugate momenta *πμ(x)*, and the Hamiltonian *H = ½π² + S_g + φ†(D†D)⁻¹φ*. A trajectory integrates Hamilton's equations with a reversible, area-preserving integrator (leapfrog), and a Metropolis accept/reject step with probability *min(1, e^(−ΔH))* renders the algorithm *exact* despite integration error [4]. Variants — Hasenbusch mass preconditioning, RHMC for fractional powers (staggered rooting), and domain-decomposed DD-HMC — differ in how they factorize the determinant to tame the fermion force, and their interplay with the integrator determines performance [4]. First dynamical overlap simulations showed the expected physics at roughly a hundred times the cost of Wilson-fermion runs [5]. The empirical acceptance law *P_acc = erfc(√⟨ΔH⟩/2)* and the identity *⟨e^(−ΔH)⟩ = 1* provide sharp diagnostics of algorithmic correctness.

### 4.5 The sign problem at finite density

At nonzero quark chemical potential *μ*, the Dirac operator loses *γ₅*-hermiticity:

$$\gamma_5\, D(\mu)\, \gamma_5 = D(-\mu)^\dagger,$$

so *det D(μ)* is generically **complex** and *e^(−S_eff)* is no longer a probability measure [6]. Reweighting from the *μ = 0* ensemble,

$$\langle O\rangle_\mu = \frac{\langle O\, e^{i\theta}\rangle_0}{\langle e^{i\theta}\rangle_0},$$

fails because the average phase *⟨e^{iθ}⟩₀ ∼ e^{−VΔf}* vanishes exponentially in the volume — the overlap problem. The consequences are physical, not merely technical: the QCD phase diagram in the *(T, μ_B)* plane — the crossover at *T_c ≈ 155 MeV*, the conjectured critical endpoint, color-superconducting phases at high density — cannot be computed by brute force [6].

The modern response complexifies the integration domain itself [6][7][8]:

1. **Complex Langevin.** Stochastic quantization with complexified fields, *U ∈ SL(3,ℂ)*, needs no probabilistic interpretation of the weight. Early wrong-convergence failures were tamed by gauge cooling, enabling the first full-QCD simulations at high *μ* [7].
2. **Lefschetz thimbles.** Picard–Lefschetz theory decomposes *Z = Σ_σ n_σ ∫_{J_σ} e^{−S}* over steepest-descent manifolds on which *Im S* is constant, removing oscillations; a residual sign problem from thimble curvature remains mild [7][8].
3. **Contour deformations / holomorphic flow** and **tensor renormalization group** methods, which sidestep sampling entirely, plus machine-learned contours — all reviewed in the recent sign-problem literature [6].

None is yet a controlled solution for cold, dense QCD, but together they map the frontier.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

Four decades of numerical experiment anchor the theory. Creutz's 1980 Monte Carlo study of SU(2) on a handful of lattice sites demonstrated confinement and asymptotic freedom numerically, launching the field [1]. The string tension extracted from Creutz ratios follows two-loop asymptotic scaling, confirming that the continuum limit lies at *β → ∞* [9]. The hadron spectrum computed with *2+1* dynamical flavors now reproduces experimental masses at the percent level, and the crossover temperature *T_c = 156.5 ± 1.5 MeV* at *μ_B = 0* is determined from the chiral susceptibility peak. Chiral perturbation theory fits to Ginsparg–Wilson fermion data confirm the Gell-Mann–Oakes–Renner relation with controlled systematics [3].

For the sign problem, the evidence is constructive rather than conclusive: complex Langevin with gauge cooling reproduces the Silver Blaze phenomenon and the expected *μ*-dependence in HDQCD and full QCD at high temperature [7]; Lefschetz-thimble simulations of the relativistic Bose gas and the Thirring model agree with dual-variable results where both exist [8]; and the average-phase scaling *⟨e^{iθ}⟩ ∼ e^{−cV}* has been measured directly, quantifying exactly why reweighting dies [6]. The *proofs* in this domain are the no-go results — Nielsen–Ninomiya [2], and the complexity-theoretic argument that the generic sign problem is NP-hard — which explain why a universal cure is not expected.

```haskell
-- Sketch: Nielsen-Ninomiya counting on a 1D Brillouin circle
-- A smooth periodic dispersion E(p) must cross zero upward
-- as many times as it crosses downward: doublers are topological.
zeros :: (Double -> Double) -> [Double] -> [(Double, Double)]
zeros e ps = [(p, sign (e (p + dp) - e (p - dp))) | p <- ps, abs (e p) < 1e-9]
  where dp = 1e-6
-- For e(p) = sin(p): (0, +1) and (pi, -1): one right-mover, one left-mover.
```

## 6 Limitations

- **Continuum and infinite-volume extrapolation.** Every lattice result carries *O(aⁿ)* discretization and *e^{−mL}* finite-volume systematics; controlled extrapolations demand multiple lattice spacings and volumes, multiplying cost.
- **Chiral fermions remain expensive.** Overlap and domain-wall formulations cost 10–100× Wilson fermions [5], so the most chiral formulations are used sparingly, and rooting systematics for staggered fermions require care.
- **Critical slowing down and topology freezing.** As *a → 0*, HMC autocorrelation times diverge (topological charge freezes), biasing sampling; open boundary conditions and metadynamics are partial remedies [4].
- **The sign problem is unsolved for cold dense QCD.** Complex Langevin can converge to wrong limits without verifiable criteria; thimble methods face multi-thimble and residual-phase costs; all current methods are validated only in models or in QCD at small-to-moderate *μ* [6][7][8].
- **Scale setting and renormalization.** Bare lattice parameters must be matched to continuum schemes (RI-MOM, Schrödinger functional), introducing perturbative truncation errors in quantities like quark masses.

## 7 Conclusion

Lattice QCD's power flows from a single idea — Wilson's gauge-invariant discretization [1] — disciplined by three hard-won lessons. The Nielsen–Ninomiya theorem taught us that chiral symmetry and locality cannot both survive discretization, and the Ginsparg–Wilson relation showed us the precise sense in which chirality can be recovered [2][3]. Hybrid Monte Carlo taught us how to sample the fermion determinant exactly, turning dynamical quarks from a dream into an industry [4][5]. The sign problem now teaches us the limits of importance sampling itself, and the complexified methods rising to meet it — Langevin, thimbles, tensor networks [6][7][8] — may yet complete the phase diagram that reweighting could never reach. The lattice remains, as ever, both a regulator and a computer: the only framework in which the nonperturbative predictions of QCD can be derived, with errors that shrink as computers grow.
