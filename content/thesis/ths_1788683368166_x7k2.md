# Mechanistic Interpretability of Transformers via Sparse Autoencoders and Circuit Analysis: The Superposition Hypothesis and Beyond

## Abstract

Modern large language models execute billions of floating-point operations per forward pass, yet the computational structures they learn remain largely opaque. Mechanistic interpretability seeks to reverse-engineer these models into *circuits* — small, causally-verified subgraphs of components whose interaction explains specific behaviors. This thesis presents a unified treatment of the three pillars that define the contemporary research program: (i) the **superposition hypothesis**, which explains why individual neurons are *polysemantic* through the packing of more sparse features than representational dimensions; (ii) **sparse autoencoders** (SAEs), a dictionary-learning methodology for decomposing dense activations into overcomplete sets of *monosemantic* features via L1- or TopK-constrained reconstruction; and (iii) **circuit analysis**, the causal-intervention framework — activation patching, path patching, and attribution patching — that maps discovered features and heads onto algorithmic roles such as induction and indirect-object identification. We derive the toy models of superposition from first principles, characterize the SAE optimization landscape including L0-sparsity trade-offs and shrinkage pathologies, and review landmark circuits: induction heads, the IOI circuit, and Claude-scale feature hierarchies.

---

## 1. Introduction

The dominant paradigm in modern machine learning trains overparameterized neural networks by gradient descent on next-token prediction, producing artifacts — transformer language models — whose behavioral competence far outstrips our scientific understanding of their internal computation. A model with 10^11 parameters is, in a meaningful sense, an alien artifact: we possess its complete source code (the weights) and can instrument every floating-point value, yet we lack a *theory* of what algorithm it implements. **Mechanistic interpretability** is the research program that attempts to supply this theory by reverse-engineering trained networks into human-comprehensible mechanisms — circuits, features, and the causal dependencies between them [2][6].

The program is motivated by both scientific curiosity and concrete engineering needs. If we can decompose a model's behavior into verified circuits, we can *audit* it (does a safety-relevant behavior depend on a particular subcomputation?), *edit* it (can we remove or strengthen a specific capability by intervening on identified components?), and *predict* its generalization (does a test-time behavior rely on a robust mechanism or a brittle heuristic?). None of this is possible at the level of raw weights; interpretability must therefore identify the right *units of analysis* — the atoms from which mechanistic explanations are built.

> **Theorem (informal):** A mechanistic explanation of a neural network behavior is a causal graph over representational units, validated by intervention, that accounts for the behavior's dependence on inputs while remaining minimal and faithful. [7]

This thesis argues that the correct units of analysis are *features* — directions in activation space — rather than *neurons*, and that three ideas interlock to make this program tractable:

1. **Superposition** explains *why* the neuron basis is wrong: networks compress more features than dimensions by storing sparse features as nearly-orthogonal directions, producing polysemantic neurons [1].
2. **Sparse autoencoders** provide the *inverse operation*: dictionary learning that recovers the underlying monosemantic feature set from superposed activations [3][4][5].
3. **Circuit analysis** supplies the *validation machinery*: causal interventions (patching, ablation) that test whether discovered features and heads genuinely implement the hypothesized algorithms [6][7][10].

The remainder of this thesis develops each pillar in depth and concludes with a critical assessment of the program's limitations.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Transformers as Programs over the Residual Stream

Elhage et al. [2] introduced the foundational formalism: a transformer is a program that reads from and writes to a shared **residual stream** — the vector of dimension `d_model` carried by each token position through the layers. Each attention head and MLP block reads the stream, computes an update, and adds it back. Crucially, attention can be factored into a **QK circuit** (which tokens a head attends to) and an **OV circuit** (what content it writes), which often admit independent mechanistic hypotheses. This decomposition — the *mathematical framework for transformer circuits* — underlies all subsequent circuit work and provides the vocabulary in which hypotheses about computation are stated and tested.

### 2.2 From Neurons to Features

Early interpretability work examined individual neurons and found some to be interpretable — e.g., sentiment neurons, curve detectors — but most neurons in large models are **polysemantic**: they fire for multiple, seemingly unrelated concepts [1]. This is not a measurement artifact. If a network represents *n* features in *d < n* dimensions, no orthonormal basis of neurons can align with all feature directions, and *any* neuron basis will conflate features. The feature — a direction in activation space, `f = x · d̂` — is therefore the natural unit of analysis, and the research problem becomes: *given activations in which features are superposed, how do we recover the true feature directions?*

### 2.3 Dictionary Learning and Sparse Coding

Sparse autoencoders are an instance of **dictionary learning** (Olshausen & Field, 1996): learn an overcomplete dictionary `D ∈ ℝ^{d×m}` with `m ≫ d` such that activations `x` admit sparse decompositions `x ≈ D f` with `‖f‖₀` small. The same mathematics that explained Gabor-like receptive fields in visual cortex promises to *undo* superposition by finding the overcomplete basis the network is "really" using [4].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology integrates three investigative stages, each addressing a distinct inferential gap.

**Stage 1 — Decomposition.** Train a sparse autoencoder on activations sampled from a target model and layer. The encoder maps `x ∈ ℝ^d` to a sparse latent `f ∈ ℝ^m` (`m ≫ d`); the decoder reconstructs `x̂ = D f`. The standard objective is:

```
L(x) = ‖x − D f(x)‖₂² + λ ‖f(x)‖₁
```

with `f(x) = ReLU(W_enc x + b_enc)` in the classical formulation, or `f(x) = TopK(W_enc x, k)` in **k-sparse autoencoders** [8], which replace the soft L1 penalty with a hard top-k nonlinearity and eliminate the separate sparsity penalty entirely. Training is performed on tens of billions of activation tokens; the learned dictionary columns are the candidate features.

**Stage 2 — Interpretation.** Each feature is characterized *correlationally*: maximally-activating dataset examples, logit-lens projections, and automated interpretability scores (LLM judges rating feature explanations against held-out activations) [5]. Features form *abstraction hierarchies*: low-level token features, mid-level syntactic features, and high-level semantic or planning features [4].

**Stage 3 — Causal validation.** Correlational interpretation is insufficient — a feature that *looks* interpretable may be epiphenomenal. Validation uses **activation patching**, **path patching** (isolating a specific causal path between components) [7], and **attribution patching** (first-order approximations enabling circuit discovery at scale) [10]. A feature or head earns its mechanistic role only when interventions confirm it.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Superposition and Polysemanticity: The Toy Model

The toy models of superposition [1] study the minimal setting in which the phenomenon appears: an autoencoder-like model `x̂ = ReLU(WᵀWx + b)` trained to reconstruct sparse, non-negative features. Each of *n* ground-truth features is present independently with probability `1 − S` (sparsity) and magnitude uniform on `[0, 1]`. The model has only *m < n* hidden dimensions — it *must* superpose.

The analysis reveals a **phase change** in the learned geometry. When features are dense, the model learns the top-*m* features as orthogonal basis vectors and ignores the rest. As sparsity increases, the optimal strategy shifts: the model stores *more* features than dimensions by arranging feature embedding vectors `W_i` as **nearly orthogonal directions**, tolerating interference that is rare precisely because features are sparse. The geometry is striking — for `n = 5, m = 2`, the learned vectors form a **regular pentagon**; for `n = 6, m = 2`, a hexagon — the uniform polytopes that maximize the minimum angle between vectors, minimizing worst-case interference [1].

> **Theorem (Superposition regime, informal):** In the sparse regime, the reconstruction-optimal solution embeds `n > m` features as a maximally-separated set of directions in `ℝ^m`. Expected reconstruction error decomposes into a *dimensionality tax* (information lost to the bottleneck) and an *interference tax* (error from feature collisions), the latter controlled by feature sparsity. [1]

The toy model also exhibits **privileged bases** and a connection to adversarial examples: interference between superposed features creates directions in which small perturbations flip which feature the model "sees." Most importantly for the field, the toy model establishes superposition as a *rational* strategy — not a pathology — whenever (a) the environment contains more features than the model has dimensions, and (b) features are sparse. Both conditions plausibly hold for natural language, where the number of distinct concepts vastly exceeds `d_model`.

*Polysemanticity* is the observable consequence: when `n ≫ m`, each hidden neuron `h_j = Σ_i W_{ji} x_i` mixes many features, responding to unrelated concepts. The neuron basis is simply the wrong basis — the features live in *directions*, not coordinates.

### 4.2 SAE Training and Dictionary Learning

Given superposed activations `x`, the SAE seeks the inverse mapping: recover sparse feature activations `f` and a dictionary `D` such that `x ≈ Df`. The classical objective combines reconstruction fidelity with an L1 sparsity penalty:

```python
def sae_loss(x, W_enc, b_enc, D, b_dec, lam):
    f = torch.relu(x @ W_enc.T + b_enc)      # sparse latent, dim m >> d
    x_hat = f @ D.T + b_dec                  # linear decoder: features compose additively
    recon = ((x - x_hat) ** 2).mean()
    sparsity = lam * f.abs().sum()
    return recon + sparsity
```

Several design decisions dominate the literature and each carries a distinct inductive bias:

| Variant | Sparsity mechanism | Key property | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vanilla SAE | L1 penalty on activations | Simple; suffers **shrinkage** (L1 biases magnitudes toward zero) | [3] |
| k-sparse / TopK SAE | Hard top-k per input | No penalty term; best L0–MSE Pareto frontier; clean scaling laws | [8] |
| Gated SAE | Separate gate & magnitude paths | Decouples feature *detection* from *magnitude estimation*, fixing shrinkage | Rajamanoharan et al., 2024 |
| JumpReLU SAE | Thresholded activation | Learnable per-feature thresholds via straight-through estimators | [8] |

The **L0–MSE Pareto frontier** is the central evaluation object: for a given number of active latents (L0, the count of nonzero features per token), how well can the SAE reconstruct? Gao et al. [8] demonstrate clean power-law scaling of this frontier with dictionary width and training compute, and show TopK dominates ReLU-based variants. However, reconstruction fidelity is not interpretability: an SAE can achieve low MSE by learning *dense*, uninterpretable dictionaries. Monosemanticity must be measured separately — e.g., via automated interpretability probes or sparse probing of ground-truth concepts [5].

Two pathologies deserve emphasis. **Feature absorption** occurs when a general feature (e.g., "starts with letter A") is absorbed into a more specific one, leaving a "hole" in the general feature's activations — the dictionary fragments concepts non-canonically. **Feature splitting** is the converse: one ground-truth concept is split across multiple latents. Both arise because the SAE objective is *underdetermined*: many dictionaries achieve similar reconstruction loss, and sparsity alone does not select the "true" one. This is the field's deepest methodological wound: we lack ground truth for features in real models, so we cannot directly measure recovery quality [10].

### 4.3 Circuit Discovery and Attribution Patching

Once features (or heads) are hypothesized, circuit analysis tests *what computes what*. The workhorse is **activation patching**: run the model on a clean prompt `p_clean` and a corrupted prompt `p_corr`, cache activations, then re-run on `p_clean` with one component's activation replaced by its `p_corr` value. If a behavioral metric (e.g., logit difference between correct and incorrect completions) collapses, that component is causally implicated.

**Path patching** [7] refines this by isolating individual causal *paths*: patch the output of component A, but only allow the effect to flow through component B, freezing all other downstream effects at their clean values. This distinguishes "A matters" from "A matters *through* B" — the difference between a component list and a circuit.

Naive patching is O(components × prompts) and infeasible at scale. **Attribution patching** (AtP) linearizes: approximate the patching effect by the first-order Taylor term `∇_a m · (a_corr − a_clean)`, computable with a single backward pass. Integrated-gradients variants improve the approximation [10]. AtP enables *automated circuit discovery* (e.g., ACDC) over full models, and **sparse feature circuits** [10] extend the method from heads to SAE features, producing causal graphs whose nodes are interpretable features rather than polysemantic neurons.

> **Theorem (Faithfulness criterion, informal):** A proposed circuit `C` is *faithful* to behavior `B` if ablating all components outside `C` preserves `B`, and ablating any component inside `C` degrades `B` beyond a completeness threshold. Faithfulness, completeness, and minimality jointly adjudicate circuit hypotheses. [7]

### 4.4 Landmark Circuits: Induction Heads and the IOI Circuit

In-context learning (ICL) is the ability to adapt to novel associations from context alone, without weight updates. Olsson et al. [6] argue that induction heads — heads implementing the match-and-copy algorithm `[A][B]...[A] → [B]` — constitute the primary mechanism of ICL: induction formation coincides with a sharp phase change in training loss, and induction-head ablation selectively destroys ICL while sparing other capabilities.

**The IOI circuit** [7] is the most complete end-to-end reverse-engineering of a natural behavior "in the wild." On indirect-object identification prompts (*"When John and Mary went to the store, John gave a drink to ___"* → *Mary*), Wang et al. identified **26 attention heads in 7 functional classes**: duplicate-token heads detect the repeated subject; S-inhibition heads suppress attention to the subject; name-mover heads copy the indirect object to the output; plus induction heads, previous-token heads, and backup name-movers. The circuit was validated against faithfulness, completeness, and minimality criteria via systematic ablation — a methodological template the field still follows. Marks et al. [10] later re-derived the same circuit at *feature* granularity using SAEs, demonstrating that feature-level circuits recover the head-level roles while exposing finer structure.

**Scaling to production models.** Bricken et al. [3] first showed that dictionary learning recovers interpretable, monosemantic features from a one-layer transformer; Templeton et al. [4] scaled the approach to Claude 3 Sonnet, extracting millions of features and demonstrating **feature steering**: clamping a feature's activation causally alters model behavior. Lieberum et al.'s Gemma Scope [9] released open SAE suites across Gemma 2 layers, establishing the shared benchmark infrastructure the field now uses.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We summarize the quantitative backbone of the program, distinguishing *mathematical results* (toy models), *scaling results* (SAEs), and *causal results* (circuits).

**Toy-model results [1].** The phase diagram in `(n, m, S)` space shows sharp transitions between dedicated-dimension regimes (orthogonal features) and superposition regimes (uniform-polytope geometries). The pentagon/hexagon solutions are not anecdotes: they are the *kissing-number-optimal* arrangements, provably minimizing maximum pairwise interference for given `(n, m)`. Adversarial vulnerability scales with superposition: the same interference that enables capacity also creates exploitable directions.

**SAE scaling results [8][4].** Across dictionary widths from 2^13 to 2^17 latents, the L0–MSE frontier follows a power law in both width and training tokens; TopK SAEs Pareto-dominate ReLU SAEs at matched L0. Automated interpretability scores (LLM-judge agreement with feature explanations) improve with scale but saturate, suggesting that *reconstruction* and *interpretability* are distinct objectives — a crucial caveat for the "scale will solve it" narrative. Templeton et al. [4] report feature densities spanning six orders of magnitude and demonstrate that steering high-level features (e.g., "unsafe code patterns") produces predictable behavioral shifts, the strongest available evidence that SAE features are causally efficacious rather than correlational.

**Circuit causal results [6][7].** Induction-head ablation selectively destroys in-context learning while sparing other capabilities; the loss-bump timing of induction formation is replicable across seeds and scales. The IOI circuit achieves high faithfulness: the 26-head circuit recovers the full model's logit difference on the task distribution, and no smaller head subset does. Sparse feature circuits [10] reproduce the IOI head classes at feature granularity with comparable faithfulness, validating SAEs as a substrate for circuit discovery.

```python
# Attribution patching: first-order estimate of a component's causal effect
def attribution_patch(model, clean, corrupted, component, metric):
    acts_clean = cache_activations(model, clean, component)
    acts_corr  = cache_activations(model, corrupted, component)
    grad = torch.autograd.grad(metric(model(clean)), acts_clean)[0]
    effect = (grad * (acts_corr - acts_clean)).sum()  # linearized patching effect
    return effect
```

## 6. Limitations

The program faces serious, acknowledged limitations that constrain how strongly its conclusions can be stated.

**1. The ground-truth problem.** In real models we never observe the true features, so SAE evaluations are indirect: reconstruction loss, sparsity, LLM-judge scores, and steering effects. Feature absorption and splitting demonstrate that the SAE objective does not uniquely identify ground truth even in toy settings where it exists [10]. Without a metric for *recovery*, claims of monosemanticity rest on shaky foundations.

**2. Correlation vs. causation.** Most SAE feature interpretations are correlational (max-activating examples, probes). Causal validation via steering or patching is expensive and rarely applied comprehensively; the vast majority of the millions of features in production SAEs have never been causally tested. A feature dashboard is a hypothesis generator, not a verified explanation.

**3. Scalability of circuit methods.** Exhaustive patching is infeasible for frontier models; attribution patching's linear approximation degrades for large interventions and interacting components. Automated circuit discovery still requires careful human curation of task distributions — the "corrupted" prompt design is itself a substantive interpretability judgment.

**4. Completeness and the long tail.** The IOI circuit covers 26 heads, but GPT-2 small has 144; the remaining heads' contributions are hand-waved as "backup" or "noise." For complex, open-ended behaviors (reasoning, deception), no complete circuit exists, and it is unclear whether the circuit concept *scales* to behaviors that are genuinely distributed.

**5. Ontological risk.** The entire program assumes that models decompose into discrete features and circuits. If frontier models instead rely on fundamentally distributed, holographic representations — or if "features" are observer-relative projections — then SAEs may be imposing structure rather than discovering it. The superposition hypothesis itself predicts that the true feature set may be unrecoverable in principle when features are dense.

## 7. Conclusion

Mechanistic interpretability has, in under five years, progressed from a speculative research direction to a disciplined empirical science with its own theoretical foundations, measurement instruments, and validation criteria. The superposition hypothesis [1] explains *why* naive inspection fails; sparse autoencoders [3][4][5][8] provide the decomposition instrument; circuit analysis [2][6][7][10] provides the causal verification. Together they have produced genuine scientific artifacts: the induction mechanism of in-context learning, the complete IOI circuit, and million-feature decompositions of production models with demonstrated steering control.

Yet the field remains pre-paradigmatic in the precise sense that its central object — the feature — lacks a ground-truth definition in the systems under study. Until we can *measure* feature recovery rather than *proxy* it, and until causal validation scales to the full feature inventory, mechanistic interpretability will remain a powerful hypothesis-generating enterprise rather than a settled reverse-engineering discipline. The most important open problem is therefore methodological: developing evaluation protocols — toy models with known ground truth, synthetic models with planted circuits, and scalable causal metrics — that can adjudicate between competing decompositions. If that problem is solved, the path from correlational feature dashboards to verified mechanistic theories of frontier models is, at last, visible.
