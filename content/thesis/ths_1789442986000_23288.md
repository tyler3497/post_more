# From Three-Phase Voting to DAG Consensus: Byzantine Fault Tolerance from PBFT through HotStuff to Narwhal and Tusk

## Abstract

Byzantine fault tolerance studies agreement among replicas when a bounded fraction may behave arbitrarily. This thesis traces the arc from Castro and Liskov's Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance, with its three-phase normal case and quadratic view change, through HotStuff's linear, responsive, quorum-certificate-chained design, to the DAG-based separation of dissemination and ordering in Narwhal and Tusk. We formalize the Dwork-Lynch-Stockmeyer partial-synchrony model, derive the quorum intersection invariant underpinning every safe protocol in the n equals 3f plus 1 regime, and sketch the Dolev-Reischuk quadratic lower bound on message complexity. We analyze HotStuff's three-chain commit rule and linear view change, contrast rotating leaders with stable primaries, and show how Narwhal's round-structured DAG absorbs transaction dissemination while Tusk extracts total order from the DAG with zero extra messages. Reported WAN figures reach 170,000 transactions per second for HotStuff over Narwhal and 140,000 for Tusk, twentyfold above prior asynchronous protocols.

## 1 Introduction

The Byzantine Generals Problem asks how generals, some of whom may be traitors, agree on a common plan through unreliable messengers [1]. In modern terms it is *state machine replication* under arbitrary faults: replicas must execute the same deterministic command sequence despite up to f compromised participants. Two properties pull in opposite directions. **Safety** demands that no two honest replicas commit conflicting commands at the same log position. **Liveness** demands continued progress, eventually executing every honest client's command.

The central tension is that both properties cannot hold in a fully asynchronous network with even one faulty process [4]. The resolution, due to Dwork, Lynch and Stockmeyer, is *partial synchrony*: the network may behave arbitrarily for an unbounded period, but after an unknown Global Stabilization Time (GST) messages arrive within a bounded delay [5]. Safety must hold always; liveness is required only after GST. Nearly every practical Byzantine protocol lives in this model.

Three generations occupy it. **PBFT** (1999) made Byzantine replication practical with a three-phase normal case and checkpoint-driven view change, demonstrating a replicated NFS only 3 percent slower than unreplicated [1]. **HotStuff** (2019) replaced all-to-all phases with leader-collected threshold-signature quorum certificates, achieving linear communication and a responsive view change, then chained phases into a pipeline where each block carries its parent's certificate [2]. **Narwhal and Tusk** (2021) separated the two jobs consensus had always fused: transaction dissemination moved into an asynchronous DAG mempool, with ordering layered on top either by a partially synchronous protocol or by Tusk's zero-message-overhead asynchronous interpretation of the DAG [3].

This thesis compares these designs under one lens: the quorum intersection invariant, the message-complexity lower bound, and the responsiveness criterion. The aim is to show not only *how* each protocol works, but *why* each successor was forced by its predecessor's limitation.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 System and fault model

We consider n replicas, at most f *Byzantine*: they may deviate arbitrarily, collude, or equivocate. The rest are *honest*. Communication is point-to-point and authenticated; the adversary controls scheduling before GST. The standard bound is n equal to 3f plus 1, justified by quorum intersection. A *quorum* is any set of 2f plus 1 replicas, and any two quorums drawn from 3f plus 1 replicas intersect in at least f plus 1 replicas, hence at least one honest replica:

> Theorem (Quorum intersection): In a system of n equal to 3f plus 1 replicas with at most f Byzantine, any two quorums of size 2f plus 1 share at least one honest replica.

This single honest witness carries safety across phases, views, and leaders. Every protocol here is a different engineering of that invariant. A **quorum certificate** (QC) compresses a quorum's assent: 2f plus 1 threshold signature shares combine into one constant-size signature over a proposal, forwardable across views [2].

### 2.2 Partial synchrony and the FLP boundary

Fischer, Lynch and Paterson proved no deterministic protocol guarantees both safety and liveness in full asynchrony with one faulty process [4]. The Dwork-Lynch-Stockmeyer model weakens asynchrony just enough: after unknown GST, every message arrives within unknown bound Delta [5]. Protocols may not know GST or Delta. *Responsive* protocols go further, advancing at the actual network delay delta rather than the pessimistic bound:

> Definition (Responsiveness): A protocol is responsive if, after GST, its progress depends only on the actual delay delta and never on the a priori bound Delta.

Responsiveness matters because Delta in wide-area deployments is a pessimistic overestimate that a Delta-paced protocol pays on every decision.

### 2.3 The message-complexity lower bound

Dolev and Reischuk showed deterministic Byzantine agreement costs a quadratic number of messages in the worst case [9]:

> Theorem (Dolev-Reischuk): Any deterministic protocol solving Byzantine agreement with f faults exchanges a number of messages in Omega of n squared in some execution.

*Intuition.* With fewer messages, some honest replica contacts too few destinations; the adversary partitions the network so its silence is indistinguishable from a Byzantine crash elsewhere, forcing contradictory decisions under validity and agreement. The bound constrains how HotStuff's linearity claim must be read: HotStuff achieves O of n messages *per decision in the common case*, but no deterministic protocol escapes the quadratic floor in the worst case. Authenticator complexity, counting signature operations, is the finer metric [2].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is an analytical and comparative study with three components.

1. **Invariant-first reconstruction.** For each protocol we isolate the safety invariant, usually a quorum-intersection or locked-certificate statement, and show how phases and view-change rules preserve it.
2. **Complexity accounting.** We compare message complexity, authenticator complexity, and round latency, separating the failure-free normal case from the view-change path and responsive from Delta-paced behavior.
3. **Literature-grounded evaluation.** Empirical claims come from the protocols' own evaluations: PBFT's replicated NFS [1] and the WAN experiments for HotStuff over Narwhal and Tusk [3], supplemented by Tendermint [6], Streamlet [7], and Fast-HotStuff [8]. No new experiments were conducted; all figures are attributed.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 PBFT: three phases and the heavy view change

PBFT replicates a deterministic service across 3f plus 1 replicas with one *primary* per *view* [1]. The client sends its request to the primary, which drives three phases:

1. **Pre-prepare.** The primary multicasts a pre-prepare binding the request to view v and sequence number s.
2. **Prepare.** Backups validate and multicast prepares. A replica is *prepared* on (v, s) with the pre-prepare plus 2f matching prepares: one quorum of evidence.
3. **Commit.** Prepared replicas multicast commits; a replica is *committed-local* with 2f plus 1 matching commits and then executes.

The client waits for f plus 1 matching replies, guaranteeing an honest executor. Safety applies quorum intersection twice: prepared quorums intersect in an honest replica that blocks equivocation within a view, and commit-quorum members carry the decision across views.

The *view change* replaces a faulty primary. Replicas time out, multicast view-change messages with their latest stable checkpoint and prepared certificates, and the new primary collects 2f plus 1 of them, reconstructing every possibly-committed request from quorum overlap and re-proposing it. The cost is quadratic, every replica shipping its prepared state to the new leader, and the process is not responsive: timeouts double per failed view. Yet the design proved practical, a Byzantine-tolerant NFS within 3 percent of unreplicated latency [1]. Tendermint later kept the same phase skeleton but replaced PBFT's stable primary with round-robin proposers and gossip dissemination [6].

### 4.2 HotStuff: linearity, responsiveness, and the chained pipeline

HotStuff keeps PBFT's quorum logic but switches all-to-all phases to *leader-collected* ones [2]. Each phase, the leader gathers 2f plus 1 votes, combines threshold shares into one QC, and broadcasts it: O of n messages per phase with constant-size certificates. An added phase, giving prepare, pre-commit, commit, and decide, buys the key property: **linear, responsive view change**.

The mechanism is the *locked* QC. A replica locks on the pre-commit QC it voted for and rejects conflicting branches unless shown a newer QC. Since QCs are forwardable, a new leader collects the highest QC from 2f plus 1 constant-size new-view messages and proposes extending it; quorum intersection guarantees the collection includes an honest replica whose lock reflects any possibly-committed value. No bulk history transfer is needed.

The *chained* variant rotates the leader every view and pipelines phases: the QC formed in one view rides inside the next view's block, so one block is simultaneously a prepare message for its own height and commit evidence for its grandparent. Safety becomes the *three-chain rule*: a block commits when three consecutive certified blocks extend it:

```haskell
-- | Three-chain commit rule: b commits if three adjacent
-- | certified blocks extend it in a single chain.
isCommitted :: [Block] -> Block -> Bool
isCommitted chain b =
  any threeAdjacent (tails chain)
  where
    threeAdjacent (x:y:z:_) =
      extends y x && extends z y && extends x b
    threeAdjacent _ = False
    extends child parent =
      qcView (parentQC child) > qcView (parentQC parent)
```

Responsiveness follows because each phase completes on 2f plus 1 votes without waiting for Delta. Fast-HotStuff showed a *two-chain* rule suffices with adjusted voting, saving a round [8]; Streamlet recast the structure as a minimal textbook protocol where notarized chains of length three finalize blocks [7]. Linearity's price is leader bottleneck and denial-of-service exposure, which per-view rotation bounds and distributes [2].

The TLA+ fragment below formalizes the shared lock discipline: a replica votes when the proposal extends its locked branch *or* carries a newer justification QC. Locks advance only via forwardable certificates, never bulk history, which is exactly why the view change stays linear:

```tla+
---- MODULE HotStuffLock ----
EXTENDS Integers
VARIABLES lockedQC, currentView

Init ==
  /\ lockedQC = [view |-> 0, hash |-> "genesis"]
  /\ currentView = 0

VoteForBlock(b) ==
  /\ b.view > currentView
  /\ \/ ExtendsBranch(b, lockedQC)
     \/ b.justifyQC.view > lockedQC.view
  /\ lockedQC' = IF b.justifyQC.view > lockedQC.view
                  THEN b.justifyQC ELSE lockedQC
  /\ currentView' = b.view

Next == \E b \in Blocks : VoteForBlock(b)
====
```

### 4.3 Narwhal: the mempool as a DAG

Narwhal observes that consensus fuses two tasks with different profiles: *disseminating* transactions (bandwidth-heavy, parallelizable) and *ordering* them (latency-sensitive, sequential) [3]. It moves dissemination into a standalone mempool structured as a directed acyclic graph of blocks, built under full asynchrony.

Each validator runs a *primary* plus several *workers*. Workers batch and disseminate transactions; the primary proposes DAG blocks holding batch digests and references to at least 2f plus 1 previous-round blocks. A round advances when the primary collects 2f plus 1 certified blocks of the prior round. Quorum intersection again does the work: any two such quorums share an honest validator, so at least one honest replica holds the full data behind every certified block, giving *availability*. Certificates of availability, 2f plus 1 signatures on a digest, let anyone fetch missing data from that honest holder.

The DAG thus yields a growing causal history whose certified blocks are always retrievable, with no timing assumptions. Ordering reduces to sequencing DAG vertices. Paired with HotStuff, Narwhal reached 170,000 transactions per second at 2.5 seconds latency on a WAN, against 1,800 for standalone HotStuff, and extra workers scaled throughput linearly to 600,000 with no latency increase [3]. The mempool, not the consensus core, had been the bottleneck.

### 4.4 Tusk: consensus with zero message overhead

Tusk asks whether the DAG needs an ordering protocol at all: the DAG *is* the protocol under a shared random coin [3]. It structures the DAG into *waves* of three rounds. Each wave, a common coin retrospectively elects a leader, the author of one first-round block. A validator commits the leader's block, and its full causal history, when at least f plus 1 second-round blocks reference it. Quorum intersection guarantees agreement: second-round quorums of two validators overlap in an honest block referencing a unique leader.

The striking property is cost: Tusk adds **zero messages** beyond DAG construction; ordering is pure local interpretation. Fully asynchronous, it needs no GST for liveness and is immune to the view-change latency spikes that hit partially synchronous protocols under attack. Reported performance is 140,000 transactions per second at 4 seconds latency, about twenty times the best prior asynchronous protocol [3].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Safety of the chained commit rule

> Theorem (HotStuff safety [2]): If an honest replica commits block b at height h, no honest replica commits a conflicting block at height h.

*Proof sketch.* Commitment needs a three-chain of QCs extending b, each QC holding 2f plus 1 votes; any two QCs intersect in an honest replica. Suppose a conflicting block at height h also gains a three-chain. Take the first view where a QC extends the conflicting branch while some honest replica is locked on a QC extending b. That replica voted for the conflicting QC only on a justification QC newer than its lock, which by chaining must extend b's branch, contradicting the conflicting chain's construction. Hence no conflicting three-chain forms. This is the chained analogue of PBFT's prepared-predicate argument, with locks as prepared certificates and QCs as view-change evidence [1][2].

### 5.2 Reported performance

The table below summarizes published measurements. Testbeds differ, so figures are indicative rather than strictly comparable, but the orders of magnitude are instructive.

| Protocol | Setting | Throughput | Latency | Fault behavior |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT [1] | LAN, BFT NFS | within 3 percent of unreplicated | milliseconds | view change stalls, quadratic |
| HotStuff [2] | LAN/WAN | tens of thousands tx/s | seconds | responsive linear view change |
| HotStuff over Narwhal [3] | WAN | 170,000 tx/s | 2.5 s | throughput preserved under faults |
| Narwhal, scaled workers [3] | WAN | 600,000 tx/s | 2.5 s | linear scale-out |
| Tusk [3] | WAN | 140,000 tx/s | 4 s | no view change; degrades gracefully |
| Prior best asynchronous [3] | WAN | approx. 7,000 tx/s | higher | baseline Tusk beats twentyfold |

Two conclusions stand out. First, separating dissemination from ordering removed the throughput ceiling: 170,000 against 1,800 on the same consensus core is nearly two orders of magnitude [3]. Second, asynchrony need not mean low throughput; Tusk outperforms the prior asynchronous state of the art twentyfold while sending no consensus messages [3].

### 5.3 Complexity comparison

| Dimension | PBFT [1] | HotStuff [2] | Narwhal/Tusk [3] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal-case messages per decision | O of n squared | O of n | O of n (DAG) plus ordering |
| View-change communication | O of n squared | O of n | none (Tusk); linear (HotStuff over Narwhal) |
| Responsiveness | normal case only | full | Tusk: n/a (asynchronous) |
| Commit rule | 2 phases plus checkpoint | 3-chain | wave plus f plus 1 rule |
| Leader | stable, replaced on fault | rotating every view | none for ordering (Tusk) |
| Timing assumption | partial synchrony | partial synchrony | asynchrony for DAG; partial synchrony if paired with HotStuff |

## 6 Limitations

1. **Latency under asynchrony.** Tusk's throughput does not extend to latency: 4 seconds reflects the three-round wave plus coin-reveal delay. Asynchronous protocols cannot be responsive in the DLS sense, since responsiveness is defined relative to a GST that may never arrive. Sub-second finality still requires partial synchrony and its view-change risk.
2. **Storage and causal-history growth.** The DAG's total recall is a storage burden: each committed leader drags its ancestry into the log. Garbage collection needs agreement on stable frontiers, reintroducing coordination the DAG avoided; deployments bound history with checkpoints and snapshot state transfer.
3. **Threshold setup trust.** Threshold-signature QCs need distributed key generation or a trusted dealer. A compromised setup undermines every QC at once, a correlated failure PBFT's per-replica authenticators did not share; multi-signature alternatives shift cost to signer vectors and batched verification [2].
4. **The quadratic floor.** Dolev-Reischuk still binds worst-case deterministic behavior [9]. HotStuff's linearity is a common-case property; adversarial schedules can force quadratic work, and randomized protocols like Tusk escape only via probabilistic liveness.
5. **Fairness and censorship.** Rotating leaders bound censorship to one view, but a Byzantine leader still excludes transactions during its tenure. Narwhal mitigates this since transactions enter through any honest validator's workers, yet the ordering leader keeps proposal power over which certified history commits first.
6. **Evaluation comparability.** Headline figures span different testbeds, transaction sizes, and fault scenarios [1][3]. The field lacks a standardized BFT benchmark under realistic wide-area faults, so cross-paper comparisons remain indicative.

## 7 Conclusion

Three ideas, each answering the previous generation's bottleneck, organize modern Byzantine consensus. PBFT made arbitrary faults tolerable at practical cost, paying with quadratic view changes [1]. HotStuff linearized every phase including the view change with threshold-signature quorum certificates, and chaining them produced a pipelined, responsive, rotating-leader protocol [2]. Narwhal and Tusk showed the hardest job, disseminating transactions, need not be consensus at all: an asynchronous DAG mempool absorbs it, and ordering becomes a thin partially synchronous layer or a zero-message local interpretation driven by a common coin [3].

The through-line is quorum intersection. In PBFT it appears as prepared and committed predicates; in HotStuff as locked QCs carried by 2f plus 1 new-view messages; in Narwhal as availability of certified blocks; in Tusk as the f plus 1 second-round references pinning a wave's leader. Each protocol re-answers one question: *what is the cheapest evidence that an honest quorum has seen a value?* PBFT answered with replicated state, HotStuff with a forwardable certificate, Tusk with the DAG itself.

Open problems remain where theory meets deployment: bounding DAG storage without reintroducing coordination, standardizing evaluation under adversarial wide-area conditions, and closing the latency gap between asynchronous safety and responsive finality. The lower bounds guarantee these problems are hard; the last two decades guarantee they are worth solving.
