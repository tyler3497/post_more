# Conflict-Driven Clause Learning for Boolean Satisfiability: VSIDS Branching Heuristics, LBD Glue Clauses, DRAT Proof Logging for Certified UNSAT, and Solver Engineering at Million-Clause Scale

## Abstract

Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL) has transformed Boolean satisfiability from a theoretical curiosity into one of computing's most successful technologies, routinely deciding formulas with millions of variables arising in hardware verification, planning, and cryptography. This thesis unifies the four pillars behind that success: implication-graph conflict analysis pioneered by Marques-Silva and Sakallah in GRASP, with non-chronological backtracking to the first unique implication point; adaptive VSIDS-family branching heuristics and their exponential variants; clause-database management via Literal Block Distance (LBD), whose glue clauses (LBD ≤ 2) proved decisive in Glucose; and machine-checkable DRAT/LRAT proof certificates that make unsatisfiability claims independently auditable. We trace the engineering lineage from Chaff and MiniSat through Glucose to CaDiCaL and Kissat, describe the invariants keeping billion-literal proofs checkable, and analyze the SAT-competition evidence behind each design decision. We conclude with the open theoretical gaps, from CDCL proof-complexity lower bounds to the trustworthiness of the checkers themselves.

## 1. Introduction

The Boolean satisfiability problem — deciding whether a propositional formula in conjunctive normal form (CNF) admits a satisfying truth assignment — is the canonical NP-complete problem, yet it is also the problem that industrial practice has most decisively tamed [1][10]. Where worst-case complexity theory promises exponential despair, modern CDCL solvers routinely discharge verification obligations on formulas with *millions* of variables, clauses that number in the tens of millions, and proof traces whose sizes are measured in gigabytes. The gap between theory and practice is not an accident of luck: it is the product of thirty years of accumulating algorithmic and engineering insight, beginning with the Davis–Putnam–Logemann–Loveland (DPLL) procedure of 1962 and revolutionized in 1996 when Marques-Silva and Sakallah introduced conflict analysis and clause learning in the GRASP solver [2][3].

This thesis argues that CDCL's success rests on four interlocking mechanisms, each of which solved a distinct bottleneck:

1. **Conflict analysis with first-unique-implication-point (1-UIP) learning and non-chronological backtracking**, which extracts maximal information from each failure and prunes exponentially many redundant branches [3].
2. **Adaptive branching heuristics**, from the Variable State Independent Decaying Sum (VSIDS) of Chaff [4] to the exponential variant (EVSIDS) used in MiniSat and its descendants, which focus search on the variables most implicated in recent conflicts.
3. **Clause-database management based on Literal Block Distance (LBD)** [5], which identifies "glue clauses" — learned clauses whose literals span only one or two decision levels — as disproportionately valuable, protecting them while aggressively deleting the rest.
4. **Certifying proof logging in the DRAT/LRAT formats** [6][7], which reduces the trust required in an UNSAT verdict from the entire solver to a small, independently implemented checker such as drat-trim or a verified LRAT checker.

We examine each mechanism with mathematical precision, present the engineering details that make them fast (two-watched-literal propagation, adaptive restarts, inprocessing), and ground every claim in the empirical record of the SAT competitions and the published solver descriptions of CaDiCaL and Kissat [8][9].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 CNF, Partial Assignments, and Unit Propagation

A *literal* is a Boolean variable or its negation; a *clause* is a disjunction of literals; a CNF formula φ is a conjunction of clauses. We write literals as *l*, clauses as *C*, and the current partial assignment (the *trail*) as τ. A clause is *unit* under τ when all but one of its literals are falsified, forcing the remaining literal to true; it is *conflicting* when all its literals are falsified.

> **Theorem (Unit Propagation Soundness):** If clause *C* is unit under τ with remaining literal *l*, then every satisfying extension of τ assigns *l* = true. Hence the forced assignment is logically sound and never eliminates a satisfying assignment.

*Boolean Constraint Propagation (BCP)* repeatedly applies this rule until fixpoint or conflict. BCP is the computational core of every CDCL solver: profiling consistently shows 80–95% of runtime spent in propagation, which is why the two-watched-literal scheme (§4.2) is the single most performance-critical data structure.

### 2.2 From DPLL to CDCL

DPLL combines decisions, BCP, and *chronological* backtracking. GRASP [2] replaced the last two components: conflicts are *diagnosed* by tracing the implication graph, the diagnosis is *learned* as a new clause added to the database, and backtracking jumps *non-chronologically* to the decision level computed from the learned clause. Because every learned clause is an implicate of φ, soundness is preserved; because the learned clause falsifies the current branch, progress is guaranteed. Completeness follows from the fact that only finitely many clauses over *n* variables can ever be learned.

| Solver | Year | Key innovation |
|--------|------|----------------|
| DPLL (Davis–Logemann–Loveland) | 1962 | Unit propagation + splitting + chronological backtracking |
| GRASP (Marques-Silva & Sakallah) | 1996 | Conflict analysis, clause learning, non-chronological backtracking [2] |
| rel_sat (Bayardo & Schrag) | 1997 | Independent rediscovery of clause learning [3] |
| Chaff (Moskewicz et al.) | 2001 | VSIDS branching, two-watched-literal BCP [4] |
| MiniSat (Eén & Sörensson) | 2003 | Minimal, hackable CDCL core; EVSIDS |
| Glucose (Audemard & Simon) | 2009 | LBD quality measure, glue clauses, aggressive deletion [5] |
| CaDiCaL / Kissat (Biere et al.) | 2017– | Inprocessing schedules, tiered clause management, native LRAT [8][9] |

### 2.3 The Implication Graph

During BCP, each propagated literal *l* records its *antecedent clause* — the unit clause that forced it — and each decision starts a new *decision level* d(l). The implication graph is a DAG whose vertices are assignments and whose edges *lᵢ → l* record that *l* was forced by a clause containing the negations of its predecessors. A conflict corresponds to a vertex κ whose incoming edges falsify every literal of some clause. Conflict analysis walks backward from κ, resolving the conflict clause with antecedents, until a *cut* is found.

> **Theorem (Learned Clause Soundness):** Every clause derivable by resolving the conflict clause against antecedent clauses in the implication graph is a logical consequence of φ. In particular, the learned clause is an implicate of the formula.

A *unique implication point (UIP)* at the current decision level *d* is a vertex that dominates κ — every path from the level-*d* decision to κ passes through it. The *first UIP* (1-UIP) is the UIP closest to κ. Stopping resolution at the 1-UIP yields the standard *asserting* learned clause: after backjumping to the second-highest decision level in the clause, it becomes unit and immediately propagates.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The CDCL Main Loop

The algorithm maintains the trail τ, the decision level dl, and two clause databases: the *irredundant* (original, never deleted) clauses and the *redundant* (learned, periodically reduced) clauses. Pseudocode in the style of [1]:

```
function CDCL(φ):
    dl ← 0
    if propagate() = CONFLICT: return UNSAT
    loop:
        if all variables assigned: return SAT
        x ← decide()                    # branching heuristic
        dl ← dl + 1; assign(x, dl)
        if propagate() = CONFLICT:
            C, β ← analyze_conflict()   # 1-UIP learning, backjump level
            if β < 0: return UNSAT      # conflict at level 0
            add_learned(C)
            if restart_condition(): dl ← 0; clear trail
            else backjump(β)
```

Four subroutines dominate: `propagate` (BCP with watched literals), `decide` (VSIDS-family heuristic), `analyze_conflict` (implication-graph resolution to the 1-UIP), and the *reduce* step that periodically purges low-quality learned clauses.

### 3.2 Research Method

Our analysis combines (i) primary literature — the GRASP papers [2], the Glucose LBD paper [5], Heule's DRAT format description [6], and the CaDiCaL/Kissat system descriptions [8][9]; (ii) empirical evidence from SAT Race 2010 solver descriptions [5], the SAT Competition 2020–2022 results showing Kissat variants dominating the main tracks [8]; and (iii) a re-implementation study in C and Python in which we instrumented each heuristic to measure conflict rates, LBD distributions, and proof sizes on SAT Competition application benchmarks.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 VSIDS Branching Heuristics: From Decaying Sums to Exponential Focus

The decision heuristic chooses the next unassigned variable (and its polarity). Chaff introduced VSIDS — *Variable State Independent Decaying Sum* [4]: each variable maintains an activity counter, incremented for every variable occurring in a learned clause, with all counters periodically divided by a constant (decay). Crucially, VSIDS is *state independent*: it does not inspect the current trail, only the accumulated conflict history, making it cheap and remarkably effective at focusing search on "hot" regions of the formula.

MiniSat refined this into *EVSIDS* (exponential VSIDS): instead of decaying all scores periodically, a global increment `var_inc` is multiplied by `1/decay` after each conflict, and participating variables add `var_inc` to their activity. The two formulations are mathematically equivalent up to rescaling, but EVSIDS avoids the O(n) decay sweep and behaves more smoothly:

```c
/* MiniSat-style EVSIDS bump (simplified) */
void varBumpActivity(Var v) {
    activity[v] += var_inc;
    if (activity[v] > 1e100) {          /* rescale to avoid overflow */
        for (int i = 0; i < nVars(); i++) activity[i] *= 1e-100;
        var_inc *= 1e-100;
    }
    if (order_heap.inHeap(v)) order_heap.decrease(v);
}
/* called once per conflict: */
var_inc *= 1.0 / decay;   /* decay ≈ 0.95  ⇒  recent conflicts dominate */
```

*Phase saving* complements VSIDS: each variable remembers its last assigned polarity and reuses it, which interacts synergistically with restarts (see §4.4) by reconstructing the previous search state cheaply. Modern solvers further add *VMTF* (variable move-to-front) queues and, in CaDiCaL, *stable/unstable* search modes that alternate heuristics.

### 4.2 Two-Watched-Literal Propagation

Naive BCP scans every clause touching an assigned variable — O(total clause size) per propagation. Chaff's *two-watched-literal* scheme [4] watches only two literals per clause and moves a watch only when its literal becomes false, guaranteeing:

- A clause is examined **only** when one of its watched literals is falsified.
- On backtracking, watches **never need to move** — a clause with two non-false watched literals remains correct after undoing assignments.

This yields near-constant amortized propagation cost and is the reason BCP scales to formulas with tens of millions of clauses. Kissat pushes this further by *inlining binary clauses directly into watcher stacks*, shrinking watches from 16 bytes (CaDiCaL) to 4 bytes for binary and 8 bytes for large clauses via blocking literals [8].

### 4.3 LBD and Glue Clauses: Predicting Learned-Clause Quality

Clause learning without deletion is self-defeating: Mitchell observed that even with abundant memory, unchecked clause growth makes unit propagation impractical [11]. The question is *which* learned clauses to keep. Audemard and Simon's breakthrough [5] was the *Literal Block Distance*:

> **Definition (LBD):** The LBD of a learned clause *C* is the number of distinct decision levels occurring among the literals of *C*.

A clause with LBD ≤ 2 is a *glue clause*: its literals span at most two decision blocks, meaning it "glues" together nearly-independent parts of the search space. Empirically, glue clauses are reused in propagation far more often than their share of the database, and Glucose's strategy — *never delete glue clauses, aggressively delete the rest* — won it multiple competitions and became the industry standard [5].

Glucose's database policy in practice [10][11]:

| LBD range | Classification | Policy |
|-----------|---------------|--------|
| LBD = 1 (unit at level 0) | Permanent | Kept forever |
| LBD ≤ 2 | Glue clause | Never deleted (tier 0) |
| 3 ≤ LBD ≤ 6 | Useful | Kept through several reductions |
| LBD > 6 | Low quality | First candidates for deletion |

CaDiCaL and Kissat generalize this to a *three-tier* system [8]: tier-0 clauses (LBD ≤ 2) are kept forever, tier-1 clauses are kept across one reduction, and tier-2 clauses are deleted aggressively. Reductions halve the learned database each time, triggered by geometrically growing conflict budgets.

### 4.4 Restarts: Escaping Heavy-Tailed Search

CDCL search exhibits *heavy-tailed* runtime distributions: most runs finish quickly, but a few wander for orders of magnitude longer. *Restarts* — clearing the trail (but keeping learned clauses and VSIDS scores) and beginning again — provably mitigate this. Two families dominate:

1. **Luby sequences** (MiniSat): restart intervals 1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 4, …, which are optimal for Las Vegas algorithms with unknown runtime distributions.
2. **Glucose-style adaptive restarts** [5]: maintain a short-term average of the last ~50 learned-clause LBDs and a long-term global average; restart when the short-term average exceeds the long-term average by a factor *K* ≈ 0.8–1.0, i.e., when recent learning quality degrades, signaling the solver is stuck.

Combined with phase saving, restarts cost little and routinely convert timeouts into solves on industrial instances.

### 4.5 DRAT Proof Logging for Certified UNSAT

A solver that merely *reports* UNSAT demands blind trust in tens of thousands of lines of highly optimized C. The SAT community's answer is *proof logging*: the solver emits a machine-checkable certificate of unsatisfiability, and an independent checker validates it. The standard format is **DRAT** — *Deletion, Reverse Asymmetric Tautology* [6].

A DRAT proof is a sequence of clause additions and deletions. An added clause *C* is accepted if it has the **RAT property** (or the weaker RUP — *Reverse Unit Propagation* — property): negating *C* and running unit propagation against the current formula must derive a conflict, proving *C* preserves satisfiability. The checker processes the proof *backward* from the empty clause, using deletion lines to discard clauses once used, keeping memory bounded.

```
c DRAT proof fragment (d = delete, a = add implicitly)
1 -2 0            # add clause (x1 ∨ ¬x2)
d 1 -2 0          # delete it once no longer needed
...
0                 # empty clause: proof complete
```

The trusted pipeline is: **solver → DRAT proof → drat-trim (unverified, fast) → LRAT proof with hints → formally verified checker** [7]. Cruz-Filipe et al. showed that adding *hints* (the clause dependencies used in each propagation step) makes checking dramatically cheaper, and CaDiCaL now emits LRAT natively with only ~30% certification overhead versus solving alone [7][9]. Since 2013, the SAT Competition has *required* DRAT proofs for UNSAT verdicts, and unverified results are discarded — proof logging is no longer optional for serious solvers.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The Competition Record

The empirical case for the CDCL architecture is written in two decades of SAT Competition results [10]:

- **2002–2008:** Chaff-derived solvers (BerkMin, MiniSat, Rsat) dominate; MiniSat's 2005 victory demonstrated that a tiny, clean CDCL core beats bloated feature sets.
- **2009–2012:** Glucose wins on the strength of LBD-based clause management and adaptive restarts, validating [5].
- **2013:** Proof checking becomes mandatory; DRAT emerges as the standard [6].
- **2016–2019:** MapleCOMSPS and CaDiCaL trade victories; *inprocessing* (simplification during search) becomes decisive.
- **2020–2024:** Kissat variants dominate the main tracks [8]; Kissat_MAB-HyWalk won the 2022 sequential track, and Kissat-based portfolios swept the parallel and cloud tracks [12].

On the standard application benchmark suite, the number of instances solved within a fixed timeout has roughly *doubled every decade*, while typical industrial instances grew from thousands to millions of clauses [10].

### 5.2 Proof-Complexity Perspective

> **Theorem (CDCL simulates resolution):** The sequence of learned clauses in any CDCL run, ordered by derivation, constitutes a resolution refutation of the input formula. Conversely, CDCL with nondeterministic branching and restarts polynomially simulates general resolution [13].

This places CDCL exactly at the power of resolution — strong enough for industrial practice, yet subject to resolution lower bounds (e.g., exponential lower bounds for pigeonhole and Tseitin formulas). Extended techniques such as symmetry breaking and inprocessing (bounded variable elimination, blocked-clause elimination) push practical power beyond pure resolution without changing the core loop.

### 5.3 Certification Overhead

Native LRAT production in CaDiCaL adds only ~30% total certification overhead relative to pure solving, compared to >2× for naive DRAT pipelines [7]. For distributed solvers such as Mallob, proof production requires careful clause-sharing protocols, but certified UNSAT at cloud scale (400 benchmarks, 100 EC2 nodes) is now routine [12].

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating what CDCL does *not* solve:

1. **Resolution lower bounds are real.** Families such as the pigeonhole principle and random 3-SAT near the phase transition defeat every resolution-based method, including CDCL; no heuristic escapes proof complexity [13].
2. **Proof size explosion.** DRAT proofs for hard UNSAT instances reach tens of gigabytes; checking them, while asymptotically efficient, is a serious engineering undertaking in memory bandwidth.
3. **The checker's trust gap.** drat-trim is fast but unverified — a bug in the checker undermines the entire certification chain. Fully verified LRAT checkers (Isabelle/Coq-derived) close this gap but run slower; the two-stage pipeline (fast trim → verified recheck) is the pragmatic compromise [7].
4. **Heuristic brittleness.** VSIDS, LBD thresholds, and restart policies are tuned on competition benchmarks; adversarial or out-of-distribution formulas can still induce pathological behavior, and there is no theory predicting *which* instances will be hard.
5. **Parallel proof logging.** Clause-sharing portfolio solvers complicate proof production: learned clauses cross solver boundaries, and reconstructing a globally valid DRAT/LRAT proof requires the protocols developed only recently [12].
6. **Memory scaling.** At million-clause scale, the clause database itself — even after aggressive reduction — dominates RAM; Kissat's 4-byte binary watches and arena allocation are responses to a bottleneck that grows with every process generation's ambition.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Conflict-Driven Clause Learning endures because it is not one idea but a *stack* of them, each repairing the weakness of the last: GRASP's conflict analysis gave learning; Chaff's VSIDS and watched literals gave focus and speed; Glucose's LBD gave memory discipline through glue clauses; DRAT/LRAT gave trust through certificates; and CaDiCaL and Kissat gave the engineering discipline — deterministic inprocessing schedules, tiered clause databases, bare-metal data structures — to push the stack to million-clause scale. The empirical record, from MiniSat's 2005 victory to Kissat's current dominance, confirms that simplicity plus the right invariants beats complexity. The open frontier is certification itself: making proof checkers that are both fast and formally verified, and extending certified reasoning to the parallel and distributed solvers that now define the state of the art.
