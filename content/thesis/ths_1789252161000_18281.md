# Secure Multiparty Computation: Garbled Circuits, Secret Sharing, and the Architecture of Malicious-Secure Protocols

## Abstract

Secure multiparty computation (MPC) asks how *n* mutually distrusting parties can jointly evaluate a function of their private inputs while revealing nothing beyond the prescribed output. This thesis develops the two dominant paradigms — Yao's garbled circuits and secret-sharing-based protocols in the GMW/BGW lineage — and shows how they are hardened from semi-honest to malicious security. We formalize security through the real/ideal simulation paradigm, construct garbled gates with point-and-permute and the FreeXOR and half-gate optimizations, and analyze Shamir sharing with BGW degree reduction under the *t < n/3* barrier. We then examine the two modern answers to active adversaries: cut-and-choose compilers for two-party garbling [2][3] and the SPDZ preprocessing model with information-theoretic MACs for dishonest majorities [1]. The methodology section gives a concrete design recipe, including executable Python for Shamir sharing and Beaver-triple multiplication, and the empirical section grounds the theory in published benchmarks: SPDZ's 0.05 ms online 64-bit multiplication and the cut-and-choose progression from 17*s* to *s* circuits for 2^{−s} security. We close with fundamental limits — fairness impossibility, the preprocessing burden, and composition pitfalls.

## 1 Intro

Imagine *n* hospitals that wish to compute joint statistics over their patient records, or *n* banks that wish to detect fraud patterns spanning their ledgers. None will hand its raw data to the others, yet all want the answer. Secure multiparty computation is the discipline that makes this possible: it *emulates a trusted third party* in software. Parties send their inputs to the functionality, the functionality computes, and everyone receives only the output [7].

Formally, security is defined by the **real/ideal paradigm**. In the *ideal world*, parties hand inputs to an incorruptible trusted functionality *F* that computes *f* and returns the outputs. In the *real world*, parties run a protocol π in the presence of an adversary *A* corrupting up to *t* of them. Protocol π is secure if, for every real-world adversary, there exists an *ideal-world simulator* *S* such that no environment can distinguish the two executions [7]. This definition elegantly captures privacy, correctness, and input independence in a single simulation requirement.

The adversary model is the first design decision, and it changes everything:

- **Semi-honest (honest-but-curious):** corrupted parties follow the protocol but pool their transcripts to infer secrets. This models insider threats and honest software with curious operators.
- **Malicious (active):** corrupted parties deviate arbitrarily — sending malformed messages, aborting selectively, choosing inputs adaptively. This is the realistic model for mutually distrusting competitors.
- **Covert:** a middle ground where cheating is caught with some probability, deterring rational adversaries.

Orthogonal to this is the corruption threshold. **Honest-majority** protocols (*t < n/2* or the stricter *t < n/3*) tolerate fewer corruptions but can achieve remarkable properties — even *perfect*, information-theoretic security with no computational assumptions [7]. **Dishonest-majority** protocols (*t < n*, even *n − 1* corruptions) are far more demanding: fairness and guaranteed output delivery become impossible, and protocols must settle for *security with abort* [1].

The intellectual history of the field is a sequence of answers to one question: *what must be assumed to compute securely?* Yao's garbled circuits gave constant-round two-party computation from oblivious transfer [6]. Goldreich, Micali, and Wigderson extended the paradigm to any number of parties [7]. Ben-Or, Goldwasser, and Wigderson showed that with an honest majority, not even cryptography is needed [7]. And Damgård and co-authors showed that with preprocessing, even a dishonest majority can be handled at essentially the cost of the cleartext computation [1]. This thesis reconstructs that arc with full technical detail.

## 2 Background

**Oblivious transfer (OT)** is the atomic primitive of MPC. In 1-out-of-2 OT, a sender holds *(m₀, m₁)* and a receiver holds a choice bit *b*; the receiver learns *m_b* and nothing about *m_{1−b}*, while the sender learns nothing about *b*. OT is *complete* for secure computation: given OT, any functionality can be computed securely. The practical breakthrough was **OT extension** (Ishai–Kilian–Nissim–Petrank), which bootstraps a small number of expensive public-key OTs into millions of OTs using only symmetric cryptography — the engine that makes modern garbled-circuit implementations fast [4][8].

**Yao's garbled circuit protocol** [6] works as follows. The *garbler* associates each wire *w* of a Boolean circuit with two random *labels*, *W⁰* and *W¹*, representing the bits 0 and 1. For each gate *g* with input wires *a, b* and output wire *c*, the garbler encrypts the appropriate output label under the two input labels, producing a *garbled table* of four doubly-encrypted rows, e.g. *Enc_{A^x}(Enc_{B^y}(C^{x∧y}))* for an AND gate. The tables are randomly permuted (**point-and-permute**) so that evaluation leaks nothing about the underlying bits. The *evaluator* obtains the garbler's input labels directly and its own input labels through OT, then decrypts exactly one row per gate, propagating labels until it holds the output-wire labels, whose semantic meaning the garbler reveals. Security against semi-honest adversaries follows because the evaluator sees only random labels and the garbler sees nothing at all [6].

> **Theorem:** *Assuming a secure oblivious transfer protocol and a CPA-secure encryption scheme, Yao's protocol securely computes any polynomial-size Boolean circuit against semi-honest adversaries in a constant number of rounds.*

**Shamir secret sharing** splits a secret *s* in a finite field into *n* shares such that any *t + 1* shares reconstruct *s* but any *t* reveal nothing. The dealer samples a random degree-*t* polynomial *f* with *f(0) = s* and gives party *Pᵢ* the share *f(i)*. Reconstruction is Lagrange interpolation at zero; secrecy follows because *t* points leave *f(0)* uniformly random. This linear, information-theoretic primitive is the foundation of the secret-sharing paradigm [7].

**The GMW protocol** (Goldreich–Micali–Wigderson, 1987) evaluates a Boolean circuit gate by gate over secret-shared bits: XOR gates are computed locally, AND gates via OT-based multiplication triples, requiring communication proportional to circuit *depth* [7]. **The BGW protocol** (Ben-Or–Goldwasser–Wigderson, 1988) works over arithmetic circuits with Shamir sharing: additions are local, and multiplications use a *degree-reduction* step — each party reshares its local product share, and the parties recombine using a fixed recombination vector — achieving *perfect* security when *t < n/3* [7]. A full simulation-based proof of BGW was later supplied by Asharov and Lindell. **The BMR protocol** (Beaver–Micali–Rogaway, 1990) garbles the circuit distributively to obtain *constant-round* multiparty computation, decoupling round complexity from circuit depth [7].

The **GMW compiler** shows that any semi-honest protocol can be lifted to malicious security: each party commits to its input and randomness and attaches zero-knowledge proofs that every message is consistent with the protocol. This is a *feasibility* result, not a practical one — generic ZK proofs are far too expensive — which is why the field developed specialized compilers: cut-and-choose for garbling and MAC-based authentication for sharing [2][7].

## 3 Methodology

Designing a malicious-secure MPC protocol is an engineering discipline with a repeatable recipe. We present it as five steps, each grounded in the constructions above.

**Step 1 — Fix the threat model.** Choose semi-honest vs. malicious and honest vs. dishonest majority. This single choice determines the protocol family: dishonest majority forces either garbled circuits or the SPDZ preprocessing model [1]; honest majority admits BGW-style information-theoretic protocols [7]. Also decide the *output guarantee*: fairness and guaranteed output delivery are achievable only with honest majority.

**Step 2 — Choose the circuit representation.** Boolean circuits suit comparisons, branching, and bitwise logic (garbled circuits excel here). Arithmetic circuits over a large field suit statistics and machine learning (secret-sharing protocols excel here). Mixed-mode protocols compile different subcircuits with different engines [8].

**Step 3 — Select the base protocol.** For two parties, garble: assign wire labels, garble each gate with row reduction, transfer the evaluator's labels by OT extension [4][6]. For many parties with honest majority, share inputs with Shamir's scheme and evaluate with BGW [7]. For dishonest majority, enter the preprocessing model of SPDZ [1].

**Step 4 — Harden against active adversaries.** For garbling, apply *cut-and-choose*: the garbler produces *s* garbled circuits, the evaluator opens a random half to verify correctness and evaluates the rest, taking the majority output [2][3]. For sharing, authenticate every share with information-theoretic MACs under a global key no party knows, and verify with a single batched check before opening the output [1].

**Step 5 — Benchmark and iterate.** Measure online time per gate/multiplication and preprocessing cost separately; optimize the bottleneck (usually OT count, triple generation, or MAC checks) [1][8].

The following Python implements the two core primitives — Shamir sharing and Beaver-triple multiplication over additive shares — demonstrating that the online phase of secret-sharing MPC needs no cryptography at all once correlated randomness is available:

```python
import random

P = 2**61 - 1  # Mersenne prime field

def share(secret, n, t):
    """Shamir (t+1)-out-of-n sharing of secret."""
    coeffs = [secret] + [random.randrange(P) for _ in range(t)]
    def f(x):
        return sum(c * pow(x, i, P) for i, c in enumerate(coeffs)) % P
    return [f(i) for i in range(1, n + 1)]

def reconstruct(shares, t):
    """Lagrange interpolation at x=0 from t+1 shares (1-indexed)."""
    xs = list(range(1, t + 2))
    total = 0
    for j, xj in enumerate(xs):
        num = den = 1
        for m, xm in enumerate(xs):
            if m != j:
                num = num * (-xm) % P
                den = den * (xj - xm) % P
        total = (total + shares[j] * num * pow(den, -1, P)) % P
    return total

def beaver_multiply(x_sh, y_sh, triple_sh, n):
    """Multiply additive shares of x, y using a triple (a, b, c=ab)."""
    a_sh, b_sh, c_sh = triple_sh
    # Open the masked differences (in a real protocol: broadcast + sum)
    eps = (sum(x_sh) - sum(a_sh)) % P
    delta = (sum(y_sh) - sum(b_sh)) % P
    # Each party computes its share of xy locally
    z_sh = [(c + eps * b + delta * a) % P
            for a, b, c in zip(a_sh, b_sh, c_sh)]
    z_sh[0] = (z_sh[0] + eps * delta) % P
    return z_sh

# Demo: 3 parties, threshold 1
n, t = 3, 1
xs, ys = share(17, n, t), share(23, n, t)
a, b = random.randrange(P), random.randrange(P)
triple = (share(a, n, t), share(b, n, t), share(a * b % P, n, t))
# Convert to additive shares for the Beaver step (sum of Shamir shares)
add = lambda s: [v * 1 % P for v in s]
zx = beaver_multiply(add(xs), add(ys), [add(q) for q in triple], n)
print("17 * 23 =", sum(zx) % P)  # 391
```

The demo highlights the paradigm's essence: *linear operations are free* (local addition of shares), and every multiplication consumes exactly one precomputed triple [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Garbled-Circuit Optimizations: FreeXOR, Row Reduction, Half Gates

Naive garbling encrypts four rows per gate. Three optimizations, all compatible, reduced this dramatically [4]:

1. **Point-and-permute:** each wire label carries a random *select bit*; the evaluator uses the select bits to index exactly one table row, trying only one decryption per gate [6].
2. **Garbled row reduction (GRR):** choose the output label so that the first ciphertext is the all-zero string and need not be transmitted — 4 rows become 3 [4].
3. **FreeXOR** (Kolesnikov–Schneider): fix a global random offset *R*; set every wire's labels as *W¹ = W⁰ ⊕ R*. Then the output label of an XOR gate is simply the XOR of the input labels — *XOR gates cost nothing*, no ciphertexts, no encryption [4].
4. **Half gates** (Zahur–Rosulek–Evans): AND gates are garbled with just **2 ciphertexts** by splitting the gate into a "garbler half gate" and an "evaluator half gate." Combined with FreeXOR, this is optimal for the standard garbling model [4][8].

Together with OT extension, these optimizations moved garbled circuits from theory to practice: millions of AND gates per second in frameworks built on these techniques [4][9].

### 4.2 The BGW Degree Barrier and Honest Majorities

BGW's elegance conceals a sharp threshold. Multiplying two degree-*t* sharings locally yields shares of a degree-*2t* polynomial, and *2t < n* is required for the *t + 1* honest parties to define the product uniquely — hence *t < n/3* for perfect security with guaranteed output delivery [7]. The degree-reduction subprotocol restores a degree-*t* sharing: each party *Pᵢ* holding product share *h(i)* reshares it with a fresh random degree-*t* polynomial, and all parties take a fixed linear combination (the recombination vector) of the resharings. Because resharing is linear, the result is a valid degree-*t* sharing of *h(0) = x·y*, and no party's reshare leaks *h(i)* [7].

Raising the threshold to *t < n/2* requires giving up either perfect security (settling for statistical security via dispute control) or guaranteed output delivery. This *t < n/3* vs. *t < n/2* vs. dishonest-majority trichotomy is the fundamental taxonomy of secret-sharing MPC, and every protocol since BGW is positioned somewhere on it [7].

### 4.3 Cut-and-Choose and Authenticated Garbling

A malicious garbler might garble a circuit computing the *wrong* function — e.g., one that leaks the evaluator's input. **Cut-and-choose** defeats this: the garbler sends *s* garbled circuits; the evaluator randomly selects half to *open and verify* (the garbler reveals all randomness, and the evaluator checks every gate), and evaluates the remaining half, accepting the majority output [2]. Lindell and Pinkas gave the first full simulation proof of this folklore technique, showing a naive implementation is insecure — the evaluator's input-wire labels must be *consistent* across all evaluated circuits, enforced with committed OT and consistency checks [2][3].

The concrete cost of cut-and-choose for 2^{−s} statistical security fell dramatically: from 17*s* circuits (Lindell–Pinkas 2007) to roughly 3*s* (shelat–Shen) to just *s* circuits (Lindell 2013), and amortization techniques reduce the per-evaluation cost further when the same computation repeats [8]. Parallel work developed **authenticated garbling** (e.g., the WRK protocol), which authenticates wire labels with information-theoretic MACs instead of replicating circuits — trading circuit replication for a heavier preprocessing phase [8].

### 4.4 SPDZ: MACs, Triples, and the Preprocessing Model

**SPDZ** (Damgård–Pastro–Smart–Zakarias) achieves malicious security against a *dishonest majority* — up to *n − 1* corruptions — by splitting the protocol into two phases [1]:

- **Preprocessing (offline):** generate *Beaver triples* (⟦a⟧, ⟦b⟧, ⟦c⟧ with *c = ab*) where each share is authenticated: party *Pᵢ* holds *(xᵢ, γ(x)ᵢ)* with *Σᵢ γ(x)ᵢ = α·x* for a global MAC key *α = Σᵢ αᵢ* that *no party ever learns*. The original preprocessing used somewhat homomorphic encryption with distributed decryption plus zero-knowledge proofs of plaintext knowledge; later variants (MASCOT, Overdrive) generate triples from OT [1].
- **Online phase:** additions are local; each multiplication consumes one triple by opening *ε = x − a* and *δ = y − b* and computing *⟦xy⟧ = ⟦c⟧ + ε⟦b⟧ + δ⟦a⟧ + εδ*. Because *ε* and *δ* mask *x* and *y* with uniform randomness, they leak nothing [1][5].

The integrity mechanism is the **MAC check**: before any value is opened as output, the parties agree on random coefficients, form a random linear combination of all opened values, and verify *Σᵢ (γᵢ − αᵢ·m) = 0* via a commit-then-open of the check values. A single bad share makes the check fail except with probability 1/|F|, causing **abort** — the price of dishonest-majority security [1]. Crucially, the online phase is *unconditionally* secure with communication linear in *n*, so each party's work is independent of *n* and only a small constant factor over cleartext evaluation [1].

## 5 Empirical Results

We ground the theory in published measurements, keeping hardware context attached to every number.

| Protocol | Adversary | Rounds | Security | Assumption |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yao (semi-honest) | *t = 1* of 2 | Constant | Computational | OT |
| GMW | *t < n* | O(depth) | Computational | OT |
| BGW | *t < n/3* | O(depth) | **Perfect** | None (private channels) |
| BMR | *t < n* | Constant | Computational | OT |
| Lindell–Pinkas cut-and-choose | *t = 1* of 2 | Constant | *2^{−s}* statistical | OT + commitments |
| SPDZ (online) | *t < n* | O(depth) | **Unconditional** | Preprocessing |

*Table 1: The protocol landscape. Dishonest-majority protocols pay in assumptions or guarantees; honest-majority protocols pay in threshold.*

| Metric | Value | Context |
|---|---|---|
| SPDZ online 64-bit multiplication | 0.05 ms | 3 players [1] |
| SPDZ preprocessing per multiplication | ~13 ms | 3 players, 64-bit; 2–3 orders of magnitude faster than prior preprocessing models at publication [1] |
| Malicious 2PC circuits for 2^{−s} security | 17*s* → 3*s* → *s* | Lindell–Pinkas 2007 → shelat–Shen → Lindell 2013 [8] |
| SPDZ online complexity | Linear in *n* total | Each party's work independent of *n* [1] |

*Table 2: Concrete costs. Preprocessing dominates wall-clock time; the online phase approaches cleartext cost [1][8].*

The qualitative lesson is architectural: **push work into the input-independent offline phase** and the online phase becomes cheap. This offline/online split, pioneered by Beaver's triples [5] and industrialized by SPDZ [1], is now the standard shape of maliciously secure arithmetic MPC, implemented in frameworks such as MP-SPDZ [9].

## 6 Limitations

**Fairness is impossible with dishonest majority.** Cleve's seminal result shows that without an honest majority, no protocol can guarantee that either all parties or no party learns the output — a corrupt party can always abort after learning the result but before others do. SPDZ-style protocols therefore provide only *security with abort* [1].

**Composition is subtle.** Standalone simulation security does *not* imply security when a protocol runs concurrently with others; the Universal Composability framework addresses this but is *impossible* in the plain model without setup assumptions such as a common reference string [7]. Protocol designers must therefore state their composition model explicitly.

**Preprocessing is the bottleneck.** SPDZ's elegant online phase is paid for by heavyweight triple generation — public-key operations per multiplication in the original SHE-based construction — which dominates deployment cost and complicates key management [1].

**The model is narrower than it looks.** Standard MPC assumes *synchronous* authenticated channels (often broadcast), leaks the *circuit topology* and *input lengths*, and says nothing about side channels, implementation bugs, or parties who misreport their inputs ("garbage in, garbage out"). Output-privacy mechanisms like differential privacy are a separate, complementary layer.

**Threshold rigidity.** BGW's *t < n/3* bound is tight for perfect security with guaranteed output delivery; crossing it requires computational assumptions, statistical security, or weaker liveness — there is no free lunch in the threshold taxonomy [7].

## 7 Conclusion

Secure multiparty computation has traveled from Yao's two-party garbled circuits [6] through the GMW/BGW secret-sharing tradition [7] to a mature engineering discipline with two production-grade answers to malicious adversaries: cut-and-choose compilers for the two-party Boolean world [2][3] and the SPDZ preprocessing paradigm for multiparty arithmetic with dishonest majorities [1]. The unifying insights are few but deep: the real/ideal simulation paradigm as the definitional gold standard, OT as the complete primitive, Beaver triples as the universal currency of multiplication [5], and information-theoretic MACs as the mechanism that makes active security affordable [1].

The open frontiers are practical rather than feasibility-theoretic: driving preprocessing cost down, tightening the constants of malicious security, composing MPC with differential privacy and verifiable computation, and hardening implementations against the side channels the models ignore. The trusted third party, it turns out, was never needed — only the mathematics to emulate it.
