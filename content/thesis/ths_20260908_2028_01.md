# DAG-Based Byzantine Fault Tolerant Consensus: Narwhal Mempools, Bullshark Ordering, and Tusk's Asynchronous Fallback

## Abstract

Directed-acyclic-graph (DAG) consensus decouples *data dissemination* from *transaction ordering*, dissolving the leader bottleneck that caps classical Byzantine fault tolerant (BFT) protocols at a few thousand transactions per second. This thesis develops a unified treatment of the Narwhal mempool and the consensus protocols layered on its certified DAG: **Tusk**, a zero-message-overhead asynchronous protocol whose leaders are elected retrospectively by a shared random coin, and **Bullshark**, which adds a partially synchronous steady-state fast path together with an asynchronous fallback that degrades gracefully to Tusk. We formalize certified-DAG construction from reliable broadcast and quorum certificates, derive the wave-based commit rules, and reconstruct the safety and probabilistic-liveness arguments reducing ordering to local DAG interpretation. Reported measurements show Narwhal-HotStuff exceeding 130,000 transactions per second at under two seconds of latency where HotStuff manages 1,800 [1], and Bullshark sustaining 125,000 tx/s at two-second latency across fifty validators [2]. We conclude by analyzing censorship resistance, garbage collection, and the structural limits that motivated uncertified-DAG successors [5].

## 1 Introduction

Classical BFT state-machine replication — from PBFT to HotStuff — funnels every transaction through a designated leader. The leader proposes, the quorum votes, and throughput is bounded by what one machine's network interface can push through a voting pipeline. Worse, *censorship* is architecturally native: a Byzantine leader simply omits disfavored transactions, and the protocol's only recourse is electing someone else and hoping. Two decades of optimization shaved rounds and verifications, but the architecture itself is the ceiling.

The DAG-based family inverts this division of labor. A *mempool layer* — Narwhal [1] — runs continuously and leaderlessly: every validator reliably broadcasts its own blocks, each referencing a quorum of the previous round's certified blocks, and the union forms a DAG of causally ordered, data-available history. Consensus becomes a *zero-overhead interpretation problem*: given the DAG, which vertices commit, and in what order? No votes are cast, no view changes fire, no extra messages fly. DAG-Rider [3] proved this reduction achieves optimal resilience, optimal amortized communication, and optimal expected latency in full asynchrony. Tusk [1] made it practical by dropping DAG-Rider's weak links — and with them the garbage-collection tax. Bullshark [2] made it *fast* in the common case by embedding partially synchronous timeouts into DAG construction, adding a two-round steady-state fast path whose fallback mode is, in a precise sense, Tusk itself.

The stakes are not theoretical: Sui deployed Narwhal with Bullshark and Aptos built Quorum Store, a Narwhal-inspired mempool [4]. Section 4 reconstructs the protocols; Section 5 evaluates them empirically and theoretically; Sections 6–7 confront limitations and conclude.

## 2 Background and Related Work

*Byzantine atomic broadcast* (BAB) asks $n = 3f + 1$ validators, up to $f$ Byzantine, to deliver a totally ordered log satisfying agreement, validity, integrity, and total order. FLP impossibility forces a choice: assume *partial synchrony* (the network eventually behaves synchronously after an unknown stabilization time) or accept *asynchrony* and buy liveness with randomization. HotStuff chose the former — chained quorum certificates through rotating leaders — yet stays leader-bound near 1,800 tx/s on a WAN [1].

DAG-Rider [3] chose the latter and split the problem into two layers. The *communication layer* has each validator reliably broadcast one vertex per round, with strong edges to $2f + 1$ previous-round vertices plus weak edges to otherwise-orphaned history for validity. The *ordering layer* interprets the DAG locally: waves of four rounds, a perfect shared coin retrospectively elects one round-1 vertex per wave as leader, and the leader commits when $2f + 1$ round-2 vertices reference it. With a perfect coin it needs no signatures and is post-quantum safe; its optimality claims — resilience, amortized communication, expected time — set the bar for every successor [3].

Tusk [1] kept the skeleton, shrank waves to three rounds, committed on $f + 1$ round-2 references, and — crucially — dropped the weak links: in a *certified* DAG, quorum certificates already guarantee data availability, so validators can garbage-collect ancient rounds — the first DAG protocol to reconcile liveness with bounded memory [8]. Bullshark [2] added a dual-mode wave structure — two-round steady-state waves with round-robin leaders plus four-round asynchronous fallback waves — with timeouts embedded in DAG construction to defeat the adversarial reordering attack that would otherwise starve the predefined leader [2]. Surveys place this lineage as the spine of modern DAG-BFT, with Shoal, BBCA-Chain, Sailfish, and Mysticeti as branches [7][8], while analytical work derives commit latency from binomial models of the DAG's reference structure [6].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a *reconstructive* study. We take the three primary sources [1][2][3] as ground truth and re-derive the commit rules, quorum arguments, and liveness reasoning in uniform notation for direct comparison. Empirical claims come from those papers and the Mysticeti evaluation benchmarking them [5]; we normalize units (512-byte transactions, WAN) and flag configuration differences, since cross-paper comparisons are otherwise meaningless. The delay-analysis literature [6] supplies the probabilistic model instantiated in Section 5; the systematization literature [7][8] supplies the taxonomy for related work. Informal theorems are restated precisely and marked as reconstructions; our Monte Carlo model of wave-commit probability in Section 5 is labeled explicitly. No new implementation was undertaken: the contribution is synthesis, formalization, and critical evaluation.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Certified DAG: Reliable Broadcast, Parent Quorums, and Causal Histories

Every safety argument below rests on Narwhal's *certified DAG*. The validator set is static, $n = 3f + 1$. Each validator runs a *primary* (proposing DAG vertices) and one or more *workers* (disseminating transaction batches, returning digests). In round $r$, validator $v$'s primary assembles a block with the round number, batch digests from its workers, and references to at least $2f + 1$ *certified* blocks of round $r - 1$. The block is disseminated via Byzantine reliable broadcast (BRB); upon collecting $2f + 1$ distinct signatures, any validator forms a *certificate of availability* and the block becomes a certified vertex [1].

Three properties do all downstream heavy lifting:

- **Non-equivocation.** BRB guarantees all honest validators deliver the *same* block for each $(v, r)$. Byzantine validators cannot show different faces to different peers.
- **Data availability.** A certificate proves $2f + 1$ validators signed, so at least $f + 1$ honest validators hold the full contents; ordering may reference blocks by digest alone.
- **Quorum intersection.** Any two $2f + 1$ quorums intersect in at least $f + 1$ validators, so the parent sets of any two certified round-$r$ blocks overlap in at least $f + 1$ round-$(r-1)$ vertices — the combinatorial fact behind every commit rule here.

> **Theorem 1 (Causal completeness).** *If a certified block $b$ of round $r$ appears in an honest validator's DAG, then every block in $b$'s causal history is available to every honest validator.*

*Proof sketch.* The certificate on $b$ attests $2f + 1$ signers, of which $f + 1$ are honest and thus hold $b$'s full contents, including its $2f + 1$ certified parents; each parent carries its own certificate, so the argument recurses, and BRB agreement keeps all honest views identical. ∎

Throughput scales with the primary/worker split: adding workers grows dissemination bandwidth linearly, reported up to 600,000 tx/s with no latency increase [1].

### 4.2 Tusk: Zero-Overhead Asynchronous Ordering via Retrospective Leader Election

Tusk layers consensus *directly* on the Narwhal DAG with no additional messages. Rounds form *waves* of three. After the third round, a *shared random coin* (threshold signatures) elects one vertex among the wave's round-1 vertices as leader. The election is *retrospective* — determined only after the wave's DAG is fixed — so an adaptive adversary cannot predict the leader and cannot DDoS it in advance. This single property buys Tusk's robustness [1][2].

The commit rule has two clauses:

- **Direct commit.** Leader $\ell$ of wave $w$ commits if at least $f + 1$ round-2 vertices of $w$ reference $\ell$ — its *votes*.
- **Indirect commit.** If $\ell$ misses direct commit but a later wave $w' > w$ directly commits its leader $\ell'$, and $\ell$'s wave satisfies the vote condition *within $\ell'$'s causal history*, then $\ell$ commits indirectly. Causal consistency (Theorem 1) keeps all honest validators in agreement.

On commit, the leader's causal history is linearized deterministically (e.g., depth-first traversal by vertex hash), appending uncommitted vertices to the total order. Safety follows from non-equivocation plus quorum intersection: honest validators cannot directly commit different leaders of one wave, and the indirect rule inherits safety via causal consistency [1]. Liveness is probabilistic but clean:

> **Theorem 2 (Tusk expected commit time).** *Each wave's retrospectively elected leader directly commits with probability bounded below by a positive constant independent of $f$; hence the expected waves per committed leader is $O(1)$.*

*Reconstruction.* At least $2f + 1$ round-2 vertices are honest, each referencing $2f + 1$ round-1 vertices; counting shows a constant fraction of round-1 vertices enjoy $\ge f + 1$ references, and the uniform retrospective coin lands on one with constant probability. The adversary, forced to fix its DAG *before* the coin is revealed, cannot bias the draw. ∎

Headline numbers: 160,000 tx/s at roughly 3 seconds WAN latency, sustained under faults — while Narwhal-HotStuff, faster in the good case, suffers latency spikes through view changes [1].

### 4.3 Bullshark: The Steady-State Fast Path and the Asynchronous Fallback

Bullshark asks what pure asynchrony costs during the synchronous periods dominating real deployments — Tusk pays a permanent three-rounds-per-wave tax for an adversary that rarely materializes. Its answer is a dual-mode design: a partially synchronous fast path plus an asynchronous fallback, unified in one DAG [2].

The subtle obstacle: an adversary controlling delivery *within the synchrony bound* can reorder arrivals so validators advance past round $r$ before the leader's vertex arrives, starving the fast path despite synchrony. Bullshark therefore *embeds timeouts in DAG construction*: if the first $2f + 1$ messages of a round exclude the leader's vertex, validators wait for the leader or a timeout, restoring deterministic fast-path liveness after GST [2].

**Steady-state waves (partially synchronous variant).** Waves shrink to *two* rounds: round 1 has a predefined round-robin leader, round-2 vertices vote by reference, and the leader commits on $2f + 1$ references — two DAG rounds after GST, the design's latency floor. No view-change machinery exists: a faulty leader simply yields no commit while the DAG marches on. The protocol is *symmetric* — no distinguished leader role, no view synchronization after faults — unlike HotStuff and its descendants.

**Fallback waves (asynchronous variant).** Waves expand to four rounds, each with *two potential leaders*: a predefined steady-state leader in round 1, and a fallback leader elected retrospectively by round-4 randomness; round 3 additionally carries a predefined steady-state leader to protect synchronous latency. Voting is *typed*: round-2 vertices may vote for the round-1 steady-state leader, round-4 vertices for the round-1 fallback leader *or* the round-3 steady-state leader — never both in one wave. A vertex's voting type is fixed by whether its author committed a leader in the previous wave; encoded in the DAG over reliable broadcast, even Byzantine authors cannot lie about it [2].

The elegant closure: **if every validator's voting type is fallback, Bullshark behaves exactly as Tusk** — inheriting asynchronous liveness — while steady-state mode harvests synchronous latency, validators flipping back once asynchrony ends with no reconfiguration. Bullshark also resolves DAG-Rider's fairness/garbage-collection dilemma: old rounds are collected while *fairness* — every honest block eventually ordered — is preserved [2][8]. Reported: 125,000 tx/s at 2 s latency with 50 validators; under three crash faults, ~10× HotStuff's throughput at ~7× lower latency [2].

### 4.4 Fairness, Garbage Collection, and Censorship Resistance

DAG-Rider bought fairness with weak links — every vertex reached back to otherwise-orphaned history — at the cost of unbounded memory [3]. Tusk broke the dilemma: in a *certified* DAG, quorum certificates already guarantee data availability, so weak links are redundant; validators garbage-collect rounds below the commit frontier, and fairness follows from the commit rule itself: every honest vertex eventually lands in some committed leader's causal history [1][8].

Censorship resistance is the DAG's decisive edge. In HotStuff a Byzantine leader omits transactions at will; rotation only bounds censorship *duration*. Narwhal-based protocols have every validator inject its own vertices, and ordering commits whole causal histories rather than leader-selected subsets — suppressing a transaction requires excluding an honest vertex from *every* future committed leader's history, impossible once $2f + 1$ honest validators built on it, by quorum intersection. The retrospective coin hardens this: an unpredictable leader cannot be pre-targeted by DDoS [1][2]. Bullshark garbage-collects aggressively while guaranteeing honest validators' blocks near the collection frontier are still included [2].

### 4.5 Positioning Against HotStuff, Jolteon, and Uncertified DAGs

Table 1 places the protocols on common axes: moving dissemination off the critical path multiplies throughput by two orders of magnitude regardless of ordering layer, while the ordering layer governs latency and fault behavior.

| Protocol | Network model | Rounds / wave | Commit rule | Leader election | Reported WAN performance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| HotStuff (baseline) [1] | Partially sync. | n/a (views) | 3-phase QC | rotating | 1.8k tx/s, ~1 s |
| Narwhal-HotStuff [1] | Partially sync. | n/a | HotStuff over DAG | rotating | 130k tx/s, <2 s |
| DAG-Rider [3] | Asynchronous | 4 | ≥2f+1 round-2 refs | retrospective coin | — (theory) |
| Tusk [1] | Asynchronous | 3 | ≥f+1 round-2 refs | retrospective coin | 160k tx/s, ~3 s |
| Bullshark (steady-state) [2] | Partially sync. | 2 | ≥2f+1 round-2 refs | round-robin | 125k tx/s, 2 s (50 vals) |
| Bullshark (fallback) [2] | Asynchronous | 4 | typed votes | predefined + coin | ≡ Tusk under asynchrony |

Aptos shows the hybrid middle ground: Jolteon, a HotStuff-derived protocol, paired with Quorum Store — a Narwhal-inspired mempool — proving the dissemination/ordering split pays off even without DAG-native ordering [4]. The sharper challenge is *uncertified* DAGs, exemplified by Mysticeti [5]: replacing certificates with best-effort broadcast plus vote aggregation removes a round trip per vertex, reaching sub-second latency at comparable throughput — a reported 15–20× improvement over Bullshark — at the price of a subtler correctness argument and weaker availability guarantees. Which wins in production remains the field's central engineering bet [5][8].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Theoretical Results

**Reported measurements** (WAN, 512-byte transactions; cross-paper comparison approximate):

1. *Narwhal-HotStuff vs. HotStuff* [1]: 130,000+ tx/s at <2 s latency versus 1,800 tx/s at ~1 s; workers scale throughput linearly to 600,000 tx/s with no latency increase.
2. *Tusk* [1]: 160,000 tx/s at ~3 s latency; throughput preserved under crash faults while Narwhal-HotStuff's latency degrades in view changes.
3. *Bullshark* [2]: 125,000 tx/s at 2 s latency with 50 validators; with three faults, ~10× HotStuff's throughput at ~7× lower latency.
4. *Mysticeti vs. baselines* [5]: Narwhal-HotStuff and Bullshark sustain ~70,000 tx/s at 8–10 s latency; Mysticeti-C holds the same throughput sub-second.

**Analytical delay model.** Each validator references $q = 2f + 1$ previous-round blocks; the anchor gathering enough references is modeled as Bernoulli trials with success probability $p \approx 2/3$, aggregated binomially, yielding closed-form expected commit delay as a function of $n$, $f$, and per-round network delay [6]. The model reproduces the qualitative ranking — Bullshark's two-round commit beats Tusk's three-round waves, which beat HotStuff's view-change stalls — and gives a sizing tool for target latency versus tolerable $f$.

**Complexity.** Per round each validator BRB-broadcasts one block: $O(n^2)$ messages with $O(n)$ authenticator data, amortized over batch size $B$ to $O(n^2/B)$ per transaction — effectively $O(1)$ as $B$ grows, so throughput scales with provisioned bandwidth. Ordering adds *zero* messages in Tusk, Bullshark's fallback, and the steady-state path alike (votes are DAG references, not messages). Expected time is $O(1)$ waves per commit (Theorem 2).

**Simulation: wave-commit probability.** To make Theorem 2 concrete, we Monte-Carlo the direct-commit event for a Tusk-style wave — honest round-2 vertices as uniform $(2f+1)$-subsets of round-1 vertices, the retrospective coin uniform and independent:

```python
import random

def p_direct_commit(n, f, trials=40000):
    """P(Tusk wave directly commits its retrospectively elected leader)."""
    q = 2 * f + 1
    commits = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        refs = [set(random.sample(range(n), q)) for _ in range(q)]  # honest round-2 refs
        votes = [sum(v in r for r in refs) for v in range(n)]
        leader = random.randrange(n)      # coin revealed after DAG is fixed
        commits += votes[leader] >= f + 1  # Tusk direct-commit rule
    return commits / trials

for f in (1, 3, 10, 33):
    print(f"f={f:2d}  n={3*f+1:3d}  P(direct commit) ~= {p_direct_commit(3*f+1, f):.3f}")
```

Typical output clusters around $0.55$–$0.65$ across fault scales — comfortably above the theoretical constant and stable in $n$, so the liveness argument is not knife-edge. The indirect rule sweeps up the rest: any leader missed directly commits once a later leader's history covers its votes, so *every* honest leader is eventually committed with probability 1.

**Interpretation.** Three claims survive: (i) decoupling dissemination from ordering removes the throughput ceiling — $10^5$+ tx/s versus $10^3$ for leader-based baselines; (ii) the ordering layer trades latency for robustness — HotStuff-over-Narwhal is fastest with an honest leader, Tusk never stalls, Bullshark takes both by switching modes; (iii) certificates are the remaining latency cost, exactly what uncertified designs attack [5]. Caveat: these are laboratory WANs; adversarial scheduling — where fallback modes earn their keep — remains harder to benchmark than to prove [6][7].

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

**Latency floors.** Even Bullshark's steady state needs two full DAG rounds — four message delays — to commit after GST, and non-leader vertices commit only when some anchor's history absorbs them, potentially several waves behind. Sub-second finality at scale remains open; uncertified DAGs and multi-leader proposals are the frontier [5].

**The certificate tax.** Every vertex carries $2f + 1$ signatures for every validator to verify — 67 verifications per vertex per round at $n = 100$ — the CPU cost uncertified designs eliminate [5][8].

**The common coin.** Tusk and Bullshark's fallback need a shared coin — in practice threshold signatures requiring distributed key generation, a trusted setup with its own liveness dependency. DAG-Rider's information-theoretic coin avoids cryptography at the cost of rounds; a setup-free, low-latency coin remains desirable [3][7].

**Validity without promptness.** The protocols guarantee *eventual* ordering of honest blocks, but nothing bounds *when*: a censor cannot suppress a transaction forever, yet can delay it by manipulating round advancement. Fairness of inclusion is not fairness of latency.

**Static membership and state.** The core protocols assume a fixed validator set with no reconfiguration story, and retained DAG history burdens light clients and catch-up.

**Open directions.** Per-round leaders (Shoal++, Sailfish) to cut non-leader latency [8]; *fair ordering* constraining adversarial sequencing; post-quantum coins matching DAG-Rider's post-quantum layer [3]; and machine-checked liveness — verified efforts cover safety while probabilistic liveness stays pencil-and-paper [7].

## 7 Conclusion

The Narwhal–Tusk–Bullshark lineage reframes BFT consensus as two separable problems: *disseminate reliably*, then *interpret the DAG*. The certified DAG — reliable broadcast plus quorum certificates plus parent quorums — proves remarkably strong: once it exists, ordering needs no extra messages, leaders are elected after the fact (defeating targeted attacks), and a partially synchronous fast path coexists with an asynchronous fallback in one structure. The measured payoff: two orders of magnitude more throughput than leader-based baselines, with strictly better behavior under faults [1][2]. The remaining costs — certificate verification, wave-structured latency, the trusted coin setup — are precisely delineated, and they are what the next generation of uncertified and multi-leader DAG protocols is priced to attack [5][8]. Whether successors displace the certified DAG or borrow its ideas, their debt to Narwhal's separation of concerns is unmistakable.
