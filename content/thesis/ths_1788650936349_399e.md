# Distributed Graph Coloring in the LOCAL Model: Network Decompositions, Shattering, and Deterministic (Δ+1)-Coloring

## Abstract

The problem of computing a proper *(Δ+1)*-coloring of an *n*-vertex graph of maximum degree *Δ* is the central symmetry-breaking task of the theory of locality in distributed computing. In the **LOCAL** model, where processors communicate with unbounded messages in synchronous rounds, the gap between randomized and deterministic round complexity of this problem was one of the deepest open questions of the field for three decades. This thesis presents a unified treatment of the modern resolution of that question: *network decompositions*, introduced by Awerbuch, Goldberg, Luby, and Plotkin [1]; the *graph shattering* technique of Barenboim, Elkin, Pettie, and Schneider [4] and its culmination in the randomized algorithm of Chang, Li, and Pettie [7]; and the derandomization breakthrough of Rozhoň and Ghaffari [8], which yields polylogarithmic-time deterministic algorithms via distributed *conditional expectations*. We prove Linial's celebrated *Ω(log\* n)* lower bound through neighborhood graphs [2], develop the deterministic *(deg+1)*-list-coloring pipeline in the **CONGEST** model following Bamberger, Kuhn, and Maus [9], and extend the analysis to *list coloring* and graphs of bounded *arboricity*.

---

## 1 Introduction

Consider a communication network modeled as an undirected graph *G = (V, E)* with *|V| = n* and maximum degree *Δ*. Each vertex hosts a processor with a unique identifier, and computation proceeds in synchronous rounds of message exchange. A *proper vertex coloring* assigns each vertex a color such that adjacent vertices receive distinct colors; the classical greedy bound states that *Δ+1* colors always suffice. The distributed coloring problem asks how many rounds of communication are necessary and sufficient to compute such a coloring.

This question is foundational for three reasons. First, coloring is the *archetypal symmetry-breaking problem*: every deterministic solution must break the symmetry of initially indistinguishable processors, and the difficulty of doing so locally governs the complexity of maximal independent set, maximal matching, and edge coloring. Second, the problem exhibits the sharpest known *randomization gap* among classical distributed tasks. Third, the techniques developed for coloring — *network decomposition*, *graph shattering*, and *distributed derandomization* — have become the central machinery of the entire area.

> **Theorem:** (Central thesis.) In the LOCAL model, the deterministic complexity of *(Δ+1)*-coloring is *poly(log n)*, the randomized complexity is *O(log\* n + Det(poly log n))*, and every randomized improvement reduces to improving the deterministic algorithm on *polylogarithmic-size* instances [7, 8].

The history of this problem divides naturally into three eras. The *classical era* (1987–1992) established Linial's *Ω(log\* n)* lower bound and *O(Δ²)*-coloring upper bound [2], alongside the network-decomposition framework of Awerbuch et al. [1] and its *2^O(√log n)*-time refinement by Panconesi and Srinivasan. The *shattering era* (2012–2018) saw randomized algorithms descend from *O(log n)* to *poly(log log n)* rounds via the shattering technique [4, 7]. The *derandomization era* (2017–present) began with Ghaffari, Kuhn, and Maus's distributed method of conditional expectations and culminated in Rozhoň and Ghaffari's polylogarithmic-time deterministic network decomposition [8], collapsing the deterministic complexity from *2^O(√log n)* to *poly(log n)*.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 develops the LOCAL and CONGEST models and proves Linial's lower bound via neighborhood graphs. Section 3 presents our methodology: the three-phase architecture of modern coloring algorithms. Section 4 contains the deep technical development — network decompositions, shattering, derandomization, and extensions to list coloring and arboricity. Section 5 states the main theorems with complexity tables. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes with open problems.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The LOCAL and CONGEST Models

The **LOCAL** model, formalized by Linial [2], is defined as follows: in each synchronous round, every node may send an *arbitrarily large* message to each neighbor, receive messages from all neighbors, and perform arbitrary local computation. Initially, each node *v* knows its unique identifier *ID(v)*, the network size *n*, and the maximum degree *Δ*.

The **CONGEST** model restricts each message to *O(log n)* bits. Because identifiers themselves require *Ω(log n)* bits, CONGEST forbids the transmission of full neighborhoods in a single round. Any algorithm that works in CONGEST also works in LOCAL, but not conversely: gathering a *k*-hop neighborhood in LOCAL takes *k* rounds, whereas in CONGEST it may take far longer if the neighborhood is dense.

The LOCAL model "captures the locality of graph problems in a mathematical sense" [6]: the output of a *t*-round algorithm at node *v* is a function of the *t*-hop neighborhood *B_t(v)*.

### 2.2 Linial's Lower Bound and Neighborhood Graphs

The first and still strongest general lower bound for distributed coloring is due to Linial [2]:

> **Theorem:** (Linial, 1987.) Any deterministic distributed algorithm computing an *O(1)*-coloring of the *n*-node ring requires *Ω(log\* n)* rounds. The bound extends to randomized algorithms (Naor, 1991) [2, 6].

*Proof sketch.* For integers *r ≥ 1*, define the *r-round neighborhood graph* *N_r*: its vertices are all possible *r*-hop views (labeled paths of length *2r* with distinct identifiers), and two views are adjacent if they can occur at adjacent nodes in some ring. Any *r*-round *c*-coloring algorithm induces a proper *c*-coloring of *N_r*. Linial showed that *χ(N_r) = Ω(log^(2r) n)*, where *log^(k)* is the *k*-fold iterated logarithm. Setting this equal to a constant *c* forces *2r ≥ log\* n − O(1)*, i.e., *r = Ω(log\* n)*. ∎

The cleanest modern presentation proves the *3*-coloring lower bound on directed paths: any deterministic algorithm needs at least *(log\* n)/2 − 2* rounds [6]. The *log\** function is defined by *log\* x = 0* for *x ≤ 1* and *log\* x = 1 + log\*(log x)* otherwise; it grows so slowly that *log\* n ≤ 5* for all *n ≤ 2^65536*.

### 2.3 Classical Upper Bounds

Linial also gave the matching-direction upper bound: an *O(Δ²)*-coloring in *O(log\* n)* rounds, by iteratively reducing an initial *n^c*-coloring (from identifiers) via a *cover-free* family argument [2]. The celebrated *Cole–Vishkin* algorithm 3-colors an *n*-node directed path or rooted tree in *O(log\* n)* rounds, which is optimal by the lower bound above. For *(Δ+1)*-coloring, the classical randomized algorithms of Luby and of Johansson achieve *O(log n)* rounds [7].

| Problem | Deterministic (pre-2020) | Randomized (pre-2020) | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| *O(Δ²)*-coloring | *O(log\* n)* | *O(log\* n)* | Linial [2] |
| *(Δ+1)*-coloring | *2^O(√log n)* | *O(log Δ) + 2^O(√log log n)* | Panconesi–Srinivasan; BEPS [4] |
| *(Δ+1)*-list coloring | *2^O(√log n)* | *O(log\* n + Det(poly log n))* | Chang–Li–Pettie [7] |
| *O(a)*-coloring, arboricity *a* | *O(log n)* | *O(log n)* | Barenboim–Elkin [4] |

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology follows the now-standard *three-phase architecture* shared by all state-of-the-art deterministic coloring algorithms. Each phase is a self-contained distributed module with a precise interface, and the composition of the three yields the final result.

1. **Decomposition phase.** Compute a *(D, χ)*-network decomposition: a partition of *V* into clusters of (weak or strong) diameter at most *D* such that the *cluster graph* — obtained by contracting each cluster — is *χ*-colorable. Modern parameters achieve *D = χ = poly(log n)* in *poly(log n)* deterministic rounds [8].

2. **Shattering phase (randomized pre-processing).** Run a fast randomized partial-coloring procedure (the *random trial* algorithm of Chang, Li, and Pettie [7]) for *O(log\* Δ)* rounds. With high probability, the subgraph induced by uncolored vertices shatters into connected components of size at most *poly(log n)*.

3. **Derandomized completion phase.** On each small component, simulate a deterministic algorithm using the network decomposition restricted to that component. Randomness is eliminated by the *method of conditional expectations*, implemented distributively: cluster leaders gather bounded-radius topology (feasible because clusters have small diameter) and fix random bits one by one, always choosing the value that does not increase the expected number of "bad events" [8, 9].

The key analytical tools are the *shattering lemma*, the *neighborhood-graph* framework for lower bounds, and the *ball-carving* process for constructing strong-diameter decompositions. We verify technical accuracy of every complexity claim against the primary sources [1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 9].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Network Decompositions: From Awerbuch to Rozhoň–Ghaffari

A *(D, χ)*-network decomposition of *G = (V, E)* is a partition *𝒫 = {C₁, …, C_q}* of *V* into clusters such that each cluster has diameter at most *D* (in the *strong* sense — distance measured inside the induced subgraph *G[C_i]* — or the *weak* sense, measured in *G*), and the *supergraph* *𝒢(𝒫)* obtained by contracting each cluster is properly *χ*-colorable [1, 5]. An ordinary proper *χ*-coloring is the special case *(D, χ) = (0, χ)*.

The power of the construct lies in the *standard template*: given a *(D, χ)*-decomposition with a *χ*-coloring of the cluster graph, any locally checkable problem can be solved by processing the *χ* color classes sequentially — solving the problem independently inside each cluster of the current class in *O(D)* rounds, then deleting solved vertices and continuing. Total cost: *O(D · χ)* rounds plus the cost of computing the decomposition.

The history of decomposition algorithms is a thirty-year march toward *poly(log n)* parameters:

- **Awerbuch–Goldberg–Luby–Plotkin (1989)** [1]: deterministic strong-diameter decomposition in *2^O(√(log n log log n))* rounds.
- **Panconesi–Srinivasan (1992)**: improved all parameters to *2^O(√log n)*, unchallenged for 27 years.
- **Linial–Saks (1993)**: randomized *weak*-diameter *(O(log n), O(log n))*-decomposition in *O(log² n)* rounds.
- **Elkin–Neiman (2016)** [5]: randomized *strong*-diameter *(O(log n), O(log n))*-decomposition in *O(log² n)* CONGEST rounds, via the Miller–Peng–Xu exponential-start-time clustering.
- **Rozhoň–Ghaffari (STOC 2020)** [8]: *deterministic* weak-diameter decomposition with *D = χ = poly(log n)* in *poly(log n)* LOCAL rounds — the breakthrough.

The Rozhoň–Ghaffari construction proceeds in *O(log n)* phases; in each phase, every node becomes a cluster center with probability roughly *1/(d log n)* where *d* is a degree threshold, clusters grow by *ball carving* (expanding until the boundary-to-volume ratio drops below a threshold, guaranteeing small weak diameter while discarding few vertices), and vertices that would cause large-diameter clusters are *deferred* to later phases. Each phase clusters a constant fraction of the remaining vertices, and the deferred vertices form a graph of geometrically decreasing maximum degree. The entire procedure is then *derandomized* by conditional expectations — the subject of Section 4.3.

```python
# Ball carving: grow a cluster until boundary/volume ratio is small.
# Guarantees weak diameter O(log n) while discarding few vertices.
def ball_carve(center, remaining, n):
    r = 0
    while True:
        ball = nodes_within(center, r, remaining)
        boundary = nodes_within(center, r + 1, remaining) - ball
        if len(boundary) <= len(ball) / 2 or r >= log2(n):
            return ball          # cluster; discard `boundary`
        r += 1
```

### 4.2 The Shattering Technique

The *graph shattering* technique, pioneered by Beck in the context of the constructive Lovász Local Lemma and imported into distributed computing by Barenboim, Elkin, Pettie, and Schneider (PODC 2012) [4], is the engine of all fast randomized symmetry-breaking algorithms. The idea: run a *fast but imperfect* randomized procedure that colors most vertices, leaving an uncolored residual graph whose connected components are *small* — at most *poly(log n)* vertices. The small components are then finished with a slower deterministic algorithm, whose cost now depends on *log log n* rather than *log n*.

> **Lemma:** (Shattering lemma, informal [4, 7].) Suppose a randomized *O(1)*-round procedure leaves each vertex *v* uncolored with probability at most *Δ^{-c}*, independently of randomness outside *O(1)* hops from *v*, for a large constant *c*. Then with high probability, every connected component of the uncolored subgraph has size at most *poly(Δ · log n)*.

The proof is a union bound over connected subgraphs: there are at most *n · (eΔ)^s* connected *s*-vertex subsets containing a fixed root, and each is fully uncolored with probability at most *Δ^{-cs}*; choosing *c* large makes *s = Ω(log n)* subgraphs vanishingly unlikely. For coloring, the *random trial* algorithm of Chang, Li, and Pettie [7] achieves the required per-vertex failure probability after *O(log\* Δ)* rounds of palette-shrinking trials.

Chang, Kopelowitz, and Pettie proved that shattering is not merely sufficient but *inherent*: the randomized complexity of any locally checkable problem on *n*-vertex graphs is at least its deterministic complexity on instances of size *√log n* [7]. Hence every randomized coloring algorithm has the form *Rand + Det(poly log n)*, and improving the randomized bound *requires* improving the deterministic bound as a function of *n*.

### 4.3 Derandomization via Conditional Expectations

The classical *method of conditional expectations* converts a randomized algorithm with expected bad-event count *μ < 1* into a deterministic one by fixing random bits one at a time, always choosing the value that minimizes the conditional expectation. Ghaffari, Kuhn, and Maus (STOC 2017) showed how to implement this *distributively*: the obstruction is that computing conditional expectations requires global knowledge, but inside a *small-diameter cluster* a leader can gather the entire relevant topology and perform the fixing locally.

Combined with the Rozhoň–Ghaffari network decomposition, this yields the general derandomization theorem [8]:

> **Theorem:** (Distributed derandomization [8].) **P-RLOCAL = P-LOCAL**: for any distributed problem whose solutions can be *verified* in *poly(log n)* rounds, every *poly(log n)*-round randomized LOCAL algorithm can be derandomized to a *poly(log n)*-round deterministic LOCAL algorithm.

The proof pipeline is: (i) amplify the randomized algorithm so the failure probability is *n^{-c}* for large *c*; (ii) compute a *poly(log n)*-parameter network decomposition; (iii) process cluster color classes sequentially, and inside each cluster, fix the random bits of its vertices by conditional expectations — the leader gathers the cluster's *O(poly log n)*-hop neighborhood, evaluates both settings of each bit, and keeps the better one. Because clusters of the same color class are independent, all clusters in a class are processed in parallel. The total round complexity is *poly(log n)*.

A sharper instantiation for coloring is due to Ghaffari and Kuhn, who derandomized a *fractional* coloring via rounding to obtain deterministic *(Δ+1)*-coloring in *O(log² Δ · log n)* rounds [3]. Bamberger, Kuhn, and Maus [9] adapted the framework to the CONGEST model, giving the first *poly(log n)*-round deterministic *(deg+1)*-list-coloring algorithm with *O(log n)*-bit messages.

### 4.4 List Coloring, deg+1 Coloring, and Arboricity

The strongest form of the coloring result is *list coloring*: each vertex *v* is given a palette *L(v)* of allowed colors, and must select a color from its own palette such that adjacent vertices differ. The *(deg+1)*-list-coloring problem — where *|L(v)| = deg(v)+1* — is strictly harder than *(Δ+1)*-coloring, yet all modern algorithms solve it at no asymptotic extra cost [7, 9]. The reason is that the shattering and derandomization arguments only ever use the *slack* between palette size and degree: a vertex with *s* uncolored neighbors and a palette of size *s+1* always has a free color.

*Arboricity* *a(G)* — the minimum number of forests partitioning *E(G)* — measures sparse graphs; forests have *a = 1*, planar graphs *a ≤ 3*. Barenboim and Elkin showed that graphs of arboricity *a* admit *O(a)*-colorings in *O(log n)* deterministic rounds, exponentially fewer colors than the *Δ+1* bound when *a ≪ Δ* [4]. The algorithm orients edges acyclically with out-degree *O(a)* (via Nash–Williams orientations computed by local peeling) and then colors greedily along the orientation. Notably, *Ω(log n / log a)*-round lower bounds show this is near-optimal. Arboricity thus interpolates between the trivial *(Δ+1)* bound and the much stronger bounds available for structured sparse graphs.

```haskell
-- Palette invariant for (deg+1)-list coloring under partial progress:
-- a vertex with u uncolored neighbors always retains >= u+1 colors.
shrinkPalette :: [Color] -> [Vertex] -> [Color]
shrinkPalette palette coloredNeighbors =
  palette \\ map colorOf coloredNeighbors  -- length >= uncoloredDegree + 1
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now state the consolidated complexity landscape. The table below gives the best known round complexities for *(Δ+1)*-coloring and its list variant; *Det_d(n')* denotes the deterministic complexity of *(deg+1)*-list coloring on *n'*-vertex graphs.

| Algorithm | Model | Deterministic | Randomized | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Panconesi–Srinivasan network decomposition + template | LOCAL | *2^O(√log n)* | — | [1]-lineage |
| Barenboim–Elkin–Pettie–Schneider shattering | LOCAL | — | *O(log Δ) + 2^O(√log log n)* | [4] |
| Chang–Li–Pettie | LOCAL | — | *O(log\* n + Det_d(poly log n))* | [7] |
| Rozhoň–Ghaffari derandomization | LOCAL | *poly(log n)* | — | [8] |
| Ghaffari–Kuhn rounding | LOCAL | *O(log² Δ · log n)* | — | [3] |
| Bamberger–Kuhn–Maus | CONGEST | *poly(log n)* | — | [9] |

> **Theorem:** (Deterministic *(Δ+1)*-coloring [8, 9].) There is a deterministic LOCAL algorithm computing a *(Δ+1)*-coloring — indeed a *(deg+1)*-list coloring — in *poly(log n)* rounds. In CONGEST, the same holds with *O(log n)*-bit messages [9].

> **Theorem:** (Randomized *(Δ+1)*-list coloring [7].) There is a randomized LOCAL algorithm for *(Δ+1)*-list coloring running in *O(log\* n + Det_d(poly log n))* rounds, where *Det_d* is the deterministic complexity of *(deg+1)*-list coloring. With current deterministic bounds this is *2^O(√log log n)*.

*Proof sketch of the randomized bound.* Phase 1 runs *O(log\* Δ)* rounds of the CLP trial procedure: each uncolored vertex *v* proposes a uniform random color from its current palette *L(v)*; it becomes permanently colored if no neighbor proposes the same color, and palettes shrink otherwise. Phase 2 applies the shattering lemma: the residual components have size *poly(log n)* w.h.p. Phase 3 runs the best deterministic *(deg+1)*-list-coloring algorithm on each component — the palette invariant of Section 4.4 guarantees the subproblem is a valid *(deg+1)*-list-coloring instance on the residual graph. ∎

The lower-bound side remains anchored at Linial's *Ω(log\* n)* [2, 6], which applies to randomized algorithms via Naor's extension. The CKP lower bound [7] additionally shows that the *Det(poly log n)* term is *unavoidable* in form. The outstanding gap is therefore entirely in the *deterministic* complexity as a function of *n*: between the *Ω(log\* n)* lower bound and the *poly(log n)* upper bound lies an exponential chasm.

## 6 Limitations

Despite the dramatic progress, several fundamental limitations persist.

1. **The exponential gap in *n*.** The deterministic upper bound *poly(log n)* and the *Ω(log\* n)* lower bound differ exponentially. Closing this gap — either by a faster deterministic algorithm or a stronger lower bound — is the central open problem of the area. The neighborhood-graph technique that yields *Ω(log\* n)* seems inherently limited to iterated-logarithm bounds, while all known deterministic algorithms pay at least a *log*-factor per decomposition phase.

2. **LOCAL vs. CONGEST asymmetry.** The Rozhoň–Ghaffari derandomization leans heavily on unbounded messages: cluster leaders gather entire topologies. The CONGEST adaptation [9] is substantially more delicate and yields worse polynomials. Whether deterministic *(Δ+1)*-coloring admits an *O(log n)*-round CONGEST algorithm remains open.

3. **Shattering is LOCAL-native.** The shattering lemma's *poly(Δ · log n)* component bounds degrade when *Δ = n^{Ω(1)}*; for dense graphs the "small" components are not small at all. The CLP analysis handles this by a preliminary degree-reduction, but the resulting bounds are non-uniform in *Δ*.

4. **List-coloring barrier at *deg*.** The *(deg+1)* bound is tight for the palette-slack method: with palettes of size exactly *deg(v)*, even the sequential greedy algorithm can fail (odd cliques), and distributedly the problem becomes as hard as *Δ*-coloring, which requires *Ω(log n)* rounds on trees. Our techniques do not extend below the *deg+1* threshold.

5. **Verification cost of derandomization.** The **P-RLOCAL = P-LOCAL** theorem [8] applies only to problems verifiable in *poly(log n)* rounds; for problems with expensive verification, the conditional-expectations computation cannot be localized.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Thirty years after Linial launched the theory of locality with an *Ω(log\* n)* lower bound [2], the distributed *(Δ+1)*-coloring problem has been transformed from a landscape of exponential gaps into one of the best-understood problems in distributed computing. Network decompositions [1, 8] provide the universal reduction from global to local; shattering [4, 7] reduces randomized complexity to deterministic complexity on *polylogarithmic* instances; and distributed conditional expectations [8, 9] eliminate randomness at polylogarithmic cost.

The frontier is now sharply delineated: the *Ω(log\* n)* lower bound against the *poly(log n)* upper bound, the LOCAL/CONGEST asymmetry, and the *deg+1* palette barrier. The most tantalizing conjecture, implicit in Chang, Li, and Pettie [7] and strengthened by Rozhoň and Ghaffari [8], is that the randomized and deterministic complexities of *(Δ+1)*-coloring *coincide* asymptotically. Resolving it will likely require either a fundamentally new lower-bound technique beyond neighborhood graphs, or a deterministic algorithm running in *O(log\* n)*-like time.
