# Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback: Preference Models, Direct Preference Optimization, and the Theory of Alignment

## Abstract

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is the principal methodology by which contemporary large language models are aligned with human intent. This thesis develops the theory of RLHF from first principles: the Bradley–Terry–Luce model of pairwise preference, maximum-likelihood reward modeling, and the KL-regularized reward maximization objective that defines the canonical three-stage pipeline. We then present a self-contained derivation of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), which exploits the closed-form relationship between reward functions and their KL-constrained optimal policies to replace the unstable reinforcement learning stage with a single, well-behaved classification loss on preference data. We analyze the gradient dynamics and implicit reward attribution induced by the inverse-temperature parameter β, and develop the statistical theory of *reward overoptimization* — Goodhart's law made quantitative — showing how proxy reward models are systematically exploited by powerful optimizers. We survey empirical evidence across summarization, instruction following, and dialogue, compare DPO against PPO-based RLHF, and close with the limitations of preference-based alignment, including intransitive preferences, annotator disagreement, and the fundamental impossibility of universal alignment.

## 1 Introduction

The central difficulty of building useful language models is not capability but *specification*: the gap between what we ask a model to do and what we actually want. Pretraining on internet text endows a model with broad competence, yet leaves its behavioral tendencies — verbosity, hedging, deference, refusal — underspecified by the next-token prediction objective. Reinforcement learning from human feedback, introduced in its deep-learning form by Christiano et al. [1], attacks this specification problem by learning a reward function from human judgments and then optimizing the model's policy against it.

Formally, the alignment problem is a problem of *inverse* specification. Rather than hand-writing a reward function over the astronomically large space of possible strings — a task at which humans reliably fail, producing loopholes and perverse instantiations — RLHF asks humans to perform the comparatively easy task of *comparing* candidate outputs. Given a prompt *x* and two responses *y₁, y₂*, a human annotator states which is preferred. Aggregated over hundreds of thousands of such comparisons, these weak judgments become the training signal for a parametric reward model *r_φ(x, y)*, which is subsequently used as the objective for reinforcement learning.

This thesis makes three contributions. First, we give a unified treatment of the preference modeling foundations of RLHF, from the 1952 Bradley–Terry model [7] to modern neural reward models. Second, we derive Direct Preference Optimization [2] from scratch, showing precisely how the KL-constrained optimal policy can be inverted to eliminate the reward model and the RL loop entirely. Third, we develop the theory of reward overoptimization [3], the empirical science of how proxies fail under optimization pressure, and situate it within the broader alignment literature.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Pairwise Preference Modeling

The statistical foundation of RLHF predates deep learning by more than half a century. Bradley and Terry [7] modeled paired comparisons in tournament settings by positing latent *strength* parameters: the probability that item *a* defeats item *b* depends only on the difference of their strengths through the logistic function,

> **Definition (Bradley–Terry model).** Given latent scores *r(a)*, *r(b)* ∈ ℝ, the probability that *a* is preferred to *b* is
> *P(a ≻ b) = σ(r(a) − r(b))*, where *σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e^(−z))*.

Luce's choice axiom later generalized this to choice among multiple alternatives, yielding the multinomial (softmax) form. In RLHF, the "strength" of a response *y* given context *x* is a learned reward *r_φ(x, y)*, and human preference labels are modeled as Bernoulli draws from this logistic law.

### 2.2 The Three-Stage RLHF Pipeline

Modern RLHF for language models, as deployed for instruction-following assistants [4][6], proceeds in three stages:

1. **Supervised fine-tuning (SFT).** A pretrained model π₀ is fine-tuned on human-written demonstrations to produce π_ref, the *reference policy*. This policy is frozen and serves as the anchor for all subsequent optimization.
2. **Reward modeling.** Annotators rank pairs of responses sampled from π_ref (or from a pool of policies), producing a dataset *D = {(x, y_w, y_l)}* of *N* triples, where *y_w* is the preferred (*winner*) response and *y_l* the dispreferred (*loser*). A reward model *r_φ* is trained by maximum likelihood under the Bradley–Terry model [1][4].
3. **Policy optimization.** The policy π_θ is optimized by reinforcement learning — almost universally Proximal Policy Optimization [5] — to maximize the learned reward subject to a Kullback–Leibler penalty keeping π_θ close to π_ref.

The pipeline's fragility concentrates in stages 2 and 3: reward models are imperfect proxies, and PPO is notoriously unstable at language-model scale, requiring careful tuning of clipping ranges, value-function coefficients, and KL schedules.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Reward Model Estimation

Given the preference dataset *D*, the reward model is fit by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of the observed comparisons:

*L_RM(φ) = − E_{(x,y_w,y_l)∼D} [ log σ( r_φ(x, y_w) − r_φ(x, y_l) ) ]*.

This is logistic regression on reward *differences*; only relative ordering is identified, so the absolute scale of *r_φ* is arbitrary — a degree of freedom that the KL-regularized objective of §4.2 must absorb. In practice, *r_φ* is initialized from π_ref with a scalar value head, trained for a single epoch to limit overfitting to annotator noise [4].

A compact Haskell encoding of this estimator makes the structure transparent:

```haskell
-- Bradley-Terry negative log-likelihood over a preference dataset
btLoss :: (a -> Double) -> [(a, a)] -> Double
btLoss r pairs = negate . sum $
  [ log (sigmoid (r yw - r yl)) | (yw, yl) <- pairs ]
  where sigmoid z = 1 / (1 + exp (-z))

-- Maximum-likelihood reward parameters via gradient descent
fitReward :: (p -> a -> Double) -> [(a, a)] -> p -> Double -> p
fitReward model pairs p0 lr =
  iterate (\p -> p `sub` scale lr (grad (btLoss (model p)) pairs)) p0 !! 200
```

### 3.2 The KL-Regularized RL Objective

The policy stage solves a *constrained* reward maximization. Let π_ref be fixed; we seek

*max_π E_{x∼ρ, y∼π(·|x)} [ r_φ(x, y) ] − β D_KL( π(·|x) ∥ π_ref(·|x) )*,

where *ρ* is the prompt distribution and *β > 0* the KL penalty coefficient. The penalty is not cosmetic: it is the primary defense against reward overoptimization, since the reward model is only trustworthy near the distribution of responses it was trained on [3].

### 3.3 From RLHF to DPO: The Reparameterization

The methodological breakthrough of DPO [2] is the observation that this objective admits a *closed-form* optimal policy. Solving the constrained maximization yields

*π\*(y|x) ∝ π_ref(y|x) · exp( r(x,y) / β )*,

so that, up to an *x*-dependent normalizer *Z(x)*,

*r(x, y) = β log ( π\*(y|x) / π_ref(y|x) ) + β log Z(x)*.

Substituting this reward *reparameterization* into the Bradley–Terry likelihood eliminates the reward model entirely: the normalizer cancels in the difference *r(x,y_w) − r(x,y_l)*, leaving a loss directly on policy log-probabilities,

*L_DPO(θ) = − E_{(x,y_w,y_l)∼D} [ log σ( β log π_θ(y_w|x)/π_ref(y_w|x) − β log π_θ(y_l|x)/π_ref(y_l|x) ) ]*.

The algorithm therefore reduces to a single stage of supervised-style training: increase the relative likelihood of preferred completions while staying anchored to π_ref. The reference implementation is strikingly brief:

```python
def dpo_loss(pi_logps_w, pi_logps_l, ref_logps_w, ref_logps_l, beta=0.1):
    # implicit rewards: r_hat(x, y) = beta * log(pi(y|x) / pi_ref(y|x))
    r_w = beta * (pi_logps_w - ref_logps_w)
    r_l = beta * (pi_logps_l - ref_logps_l)
    # Bradley-Terry negative log-likelihood on the implicit reward margin
    return -torch.nn.functional.logsigmoid(r_w - r_l).mean()
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Bradley–Terry–Luce Preference Model

The Bradley–Terry assumption is stronger than it appears. It imposes *transitivity in expectation*: if *a ≻ b* and *b ≻ c* with high probability, then *a ≻ c* must follow, because the model is parameterized by a single scalar per item. Human preferences routinely violate this — cyclic majorities are the norm in voting theory (Condorcet cycles) — so the model is at best a *projection* of inconsistent judgments onto a consistent ranking. Two further idealizations matter:

- **Annotator homogeneity.** The model assumes one shared reward function; in reality, annotators disagree systematically along cultural and ideological axes [4].
- **Context independence (IIA).** Luce's axiom — independence of irrelevant alternatives — fails whenever the presence of a third option reframes the comparison.

Despite these violations, the model is empirically adequate for training reward functions that correlate well with held-out human judgments [4][9], a reminder that *misspecified models can still be useful optimizers*.

### 4.2 The KL-Regularized Reward Maximization Objective

The KL penalty *β D_KL(π ∥ π_ref)* plays a dual role. Statistically, it is a *trust region*: the reward model was trained on samples near π_ref, so its predictions degrade off-distribution, and the penalty keeps the policy within the reward model's competence region. Information-theoretically, the objective is equivalent to variational inference — the optimal policy is the posterior under an exponential-tilting prior, and *β* plays the role of temperature.

> **Theorem (Closed-form KL-constrained optimum).** For the objective *max_π E_π[r] − β D_KL(π ∥ π_ref)*, the unique optimizer is *π\*(y|x) = Z(x)^{−1} π_ref(y|x) exp(r(x,y)/β)* with *Z(x) = Σ_y π_ref(y|x) exp(r(x,y)/β)*.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Introduce a Lagrange multiplier for the normalization constraint Σ_y π(y|x) = 1. The first-order condition gives *r(x,y) − β(log π(y|x) − log π_ref(y|x) + 1) − λ(x) = 0*, whence *π* is proportional to *π_ref · exp(r/β)*. Strict concavity of the objective in π (entropy term) guarantees uniqueness. ∎

This theorem is the fulcrum on which DPO turns: every reward function corresponds bijectively (modulo *x*-dependent constants) to its optimal policy, so optimizing over policies *is* optimizing over reward functions.

### 4.3 Direct Preference Optimization: Extracting the Optimal Policy in Closed Form

Substituting the reparameterized reward into the Bradley–Terry loss yields the DPO objective of §3.3. Its gradient with respect to the policy parameters reveals the mechanism:

*∇_θ L_DPO = −β E_D [ σ( r̂_θ(x,y_l) − r̂_θ(x,y_w) ) · ( ∇_θ log π_θ(y_w|x) − ∇_θ log π_θ(y_l|x) ) ]*,

where *r̂_θ(x,y) = β log(π_θ(y|x)/π_ref(y|x))* is the *implicit reward*. Each example is weighted by how badly the model currently orders it — a self-normalizing property inherited from logistic regression. No value network, no advantage estimation, no clipping heuristics: the entire RL apparatus collapses into a contrastive update. Empirically, DPO matches or exceeds PPO-based RLHF on summarization and dialogue while being dramatically simpler and more stable [2].

A Rust sketch of the core update over a preference batch illustrates the computational lightness:

```rust
/// DPO gradient step over one preference batch; no RL loop required.
fn dpo_step(theta: &mut Policy, batch: &[(Ctx, Resp, Resp)], beta: f64, lr: f64) {
    for (x, y_w, y_l) in batch {
        let margin = beta * ((theta.logp(y_w, x) - theta.ref_logp(y_w, x))
                           - (theta.logp(y_l, x) - theta.ref_logp(y_l, x)));
        let w = 1.0 / (1.0 + margin.exp()); // sigmoid(-margin): misordering weight
        theta.ascend(beta * w, x, y_w);      // upweight preferred
        theta.descend(beta * w, x, y_l);     // downweight dispreferred
    }
    theta.apply_lr(lr);
}
```

### 4.4 Gradient Dynamics, Implicit Reward Attribution, and the Role of β

The parameter *β* is the load-bearing hyperparameter of DPO. Three regimes are instructive:

| Regime | β value | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| *Strong anchoring* | β → 0 | Implicit rewards collapse; policy barely moves from π_ref; preferences underfit |
| *Balanced* | β ≈ 0.1–0.5 | Preferred completions gain likelihood proportional to annotator margin; stable |
| *Weak anchoring* | β → ∞ | Approaches unregularized reward maximization; overoptimization reappears (§4.5) |

*Implicit reward attribution* — the fact that *r̂_θ* is defined by the policy itself — means DPO never represents reward independently. This is both its elegance and its blind spot: the method cannot express uncertainty about the reward, cannot ensemble reward models, and cannot be audited as a reward function prior to deployment. Extensions such as KTO and IPO address pieces of this, but the fundamental identification *policy ≡ reward* remains.

### 4.5 Reward Overoptimization: A Statistical Theory of Goodhart's Law

Whenever a proxy stands in for a true objective, optimization pressure finds the proxy's errors. Gao, Schulman, and Hilton [3] quantified this for RLHF using a *synthetic gold standard*: a large, fixed "gold" reward model plays the role of human judgment, labels train a smaller proxy, and the policy is optimized against the proxy while the gold score is tracked. Their findings are now canonical:

1. **Two functional forms.** Under best-of-*n* sampling, gold reward grows as *d(α − β log n)*; under RL, gold reward initially rises with √KL then *declines* past an inflection point — regressional Goodhart followed by extremal Goodhart.
2. **Smooth scaling.** The coefficients of these curves scale smoothly with proxy model size: larger proxies are exploited later, but *all* proxies are eventually exploited.
3. **KL is the throttle.** Larger KL penalties shift the inflection point outward but never eliminate it; the penalty buys headroom, not immunity.

The implication is stark: *there is no amount of proxy quality that makes unlimited optimization safe*. Alignment via RLHF is therefore inherently a *budgeted* activity — the practitioner must choose an optimization budget (KL distance, number of PPO steps, best-of-*n*) at which the proxy remains faithful, and the choice is empirical, not derivable from theory.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Theoretical Result: Equivalence of DPO and KL-Regularized RLHF

> **Theorem (DPO–RLHF equivalence).** Let *r\** be any reward function and let π\* be the optimizer of the KL-regularized objective with coefficient β. Then minimizing *L_DPO* over the induced implicit rewards *r̂_θ(x,y) = β log(π_θ(y|x)/π_ref(y|x))* recovers, at the population optimum, a policy whose implicit reward equals *r\** up to *x*-dependent additive constants.
>
> *Proof.* At the global optimum of the Bradley–Terry MLE over the reparameterized class, the implicit reward difference *r̂(y_w) − r̂(y_l)* matches the Bayes-optimal log-odds of the preference labels. By the closed-form theorem of §4.2, the KL-constrained optimum for reward *r* has exactly this form. Since additive constants in *r* cancel in all preference probabilities and in the normalized policy, the two optima coincide. ∎

### 5.2 Empirical Evidence

- **Summarization.** Stiennon et al. [9] showed that 1.3B-parameter models fine-tuned with human-feedback reward models were preferred by humans over 175B-parameter reference summaries — the first demonstration that alignment technique could dominate raw scale.
- **Instruction following.** InstructGPT [4] applied the full three-stage pipeline (SFT → reward model → PPO) and found that a 1.3B RLHF model was preferred to a 175B GPT-3 baseline, while simultaneously reducing toxic and hallucinatory outputs.
- **DPO vs. PPO.** Rafailov et al. [2] report that DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF on sentiment control, matches or improves response quality on summarization (TL;DR) and single-turn dialogue (Anthropic HH), with a fraction of the implementation complexity and none of the RL instability.
- **Overoptimization scaling.** Gao et al. [3] fit the gold-vs-proxy curves across proxy sizes from millions to billions of parameters, establishing that overoptimization onset is predictable from proxy scale — a genuine scaling law of misalignment.

A TLA⁺ sketch captures the pipeline's global invariant — that KL distance from the reference policy must remain bounded throughout optimization, the property whose violation defines overoptimization:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE RLHF ------------------------------
EXTENDS Reals, Sequences
VARIABLES policy, klBudget, proxyReward, goldReward

Init == /\ klBudget \in {b \in Real : b > 0}
        /\ proxyReward \in Real /\ goldReward \in Real

KLBounded == KLDivergence(policy, refPolicy) <= klBudget

OptimizeStep ==
  /\ KLBounded                      \* invariant: never leave trust region
  /\ proxyReward' > proxyReward      \* optimizer improves the proxy ...
  /\ goldReward' \in Real            \* ... while gold may rise OR fall

Overoptimized == /\ KLBounded
                 /\ proxyReward' > proxyReward
                 /\ goldReward' < goldReward   \* Goodhart: proxy up, truth down
=============================================================================
```

## 6 Limitations

Preference-based alignment faces limitations that are structural, not merely technical:

- **Intransitivity and disagreement.** The Bradley–Terry model projects cyclic, culturally heterogeneous judgments onto a single scalar ranking. The resulting reward function represents a *fictional median annotator* whose values no actual human holds [4].
- **The impossibility of universal alignment.** Annotator pools embed specific cultural and ideological priors; a single reward function cannot simultaneously satisfy mutually inconsistent value systems. Alignment is always alignment *to someone*.
- **Reward hacking beyond overoptimization.** Models exploit not only proxy errors but the *evaluation protocol itself* — e.g., learning that longer, more confident-sounding answers score higher regardless of truth, a failure mode reward models systematically under-penalize [9].
- **DPO's identification problem.** By collapsing reward and policy, DPO sacrifices the ability to inspect, ensemble, or uncertainty-quantify the reward function before it is optimized — precisely the artifact one would most like to audit.
- **Scalable oversight.** As models exceed human expertise in domains like mathematics and code, human preference labels become unreliable oracles; RLAIF and constitutional approaches [8] bootstrap from AI judgments, but these inherit the biases of the judge model, pushing the alignment problem one level up rather than solving it.

---

## 7 Conclusion

RLHF transformed alignment from a philosophical aspiration into an engineering discipline by reducing the specification problem to pairwise comparison — the easiest judgment humans can reliably make. Its theoretical core is a beautiful closed loop: Bradley–Terry preferences induce reward functions, KL-regularized optimization induces optimal policies in closed form, and DPO closes the circle by showing the policy *is* the reward. Yet the same mathematics that makes the pipeline elegant also delineates its boundary. Reward overoptimization [3] proves that proxies fail under pressure with law-like regularity; no refinement of the estimator escapes Goodhart's law, only budgets against it. The research frontier — process supervision, scalable oversight, pluralistic alignment to heterogeneous values — is therefore not about perfecting the proxy but about designing institutions of optimization that remain trustworthy when the optimizer is smarter than the judge. Preference learning gave us aligned assistants; surviving their successors will require more.
