# QUIC and Multipath QUIC Internals: Reimagining Transport from Handshake to Scheduling

## Abstract

QUIC (RFC 9000) [1] collapses the classical TCP/TLS stack into a single user-space, UDP-based transport that fuses the TLS 1.3 handshake, stream multiplexing, loss recovery, and congestion control into one cryptographic frame of reference. This thesis dissects QUIC across four axes: (i) the integrated TLS 1.3 handshake (RFC 9001) [2] — the latency economics of 1-RTT establishment and 0-RTT resumption, and the principled containment of replay risk; (ii) connection migration via connection IDs and PATH_CHALLENGE path validation, decoupling connection state from the network 4-tuple; (iii) the three packet number spaces and per-space loss detection (RFC 9002) [4], whose monotonic, never-reused numbering dissolves TCP's retransmission ambiguity; and (iv) pluggable congestion control — CUBIC, BBR, and the NewReno baseline — generalized by the Multipath QUIC draft [5] to per-path packet number spaces, MP_ACK frames, and independent validation. Triangulated against production measurements [6][7][8], we formalize the core correctness theorems and argue that multipath's scheduling-agnostic architecture is its pivotal design decision.

## 1. Introduction

Transport protocols ossify: TCP's middleboxes learned to read, depend upon, and freeze its wire image — sequence numbers in-band and unencrypted, flags set exactly so, deviations discarded by appliances deployed in 2004. QUIC's founding claim is that the only reliable defense is *encrypting everything but the minimum needed for routing* [1]: almost the entire QUIC header — packet numbers, acknowledgment state, control signaling — is cryptographically protected, leaving the network only a connection identifier (CID) and a handful of bits.

This thesis treats QUIC not as a faster TCP but as a re-imagining of what a transport is. Four design decisions carry most of the weight:

1. **The handshake is the TLS handshake.** QUIC does not run *over* TLS; it *embeds* TLS 1.3 (RFC 9001) [2], carrying TLS records inside CRYPTO frames at negotiated encryption levels. A cold connection reaches first byte in one round trip; a resumed connection can send application data in zero.
2. **Connections are named by CIDs, not 4-tuples.** Endpoints identify a connection by a connection ID chosen by the peer, so the connection survives IP address and port changes — the technical substrate of connection migration and the Multipath QUIC extension [5].
3. **Packet numbers are monotonic and never reused.** Lost data is retransmitted in *new* packets with fresh numbers, eliminating the retransmission ambiguity that forces TCP to lean on Karn's algorithm heuristics [4].
4. **Congestion control is pluggable and per-path.** RFC 9002 specifies only a NewReno baseline [4]; production stacks run CUBIC or BBR, and the multipath extension generalizes the per-space state machine to per-path machines with independent RTT estimators, windows, and packet number spaces [5].

The remainder: Section 2 surveys background; Section 3 states the methodology — a specification-driven analysis cross-checked against measurements; Section 4 dives into the handshake (4.1), migration (4.2), packet number spaces (4.3), congestion control (4.4), and Multipath QUIC (4.5); Section 5 presents evidence and formal results; Sections 6–7 state limitations and conclude.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The TCP/TLS cost stack

A classical HTTPS connection pays a compounding latency tax: TCP three-way handshake (1 RTT), TLS 1.2 handshake (2 RTTs), and, on packet loss, transport-level head-of-line (HoL) blocking, because TCP presents a single ordered byte stream. HTTP/2 multiplexed streams over that single byte stream and thereby made HoL *worse*: one lost TCP segment stalls every multiplexed stream. TLS 1.3 reduced the cryptographic handshake to 1-RTT (or 0-RTT with resumption), but the transport and cryptographic handshakes remained sequential.

### 2.2 QUIC's lineage

Google's gQUIC (deployed in Chrome from 2013) [7] proved the viability of a UDP-based, encrypted, multiplexed transport at planetary scale: by 2020 Facebook reported 75% of its traffic on QUIC, and Google's mobile applications (YouTube, Gmail) had migrated entirely [7]. IETF standardization produced the RFC 9000 suite in May 2021: RFC 9000 (transport) [1], RFC 9001 (TLS integration) [2], RFC 9002 (loss detection and congestion control) [4], and RFC 9114 (HTTP/3). This thesis takes the RFC series as ground truth and the multipath extension draft [5] as the active frontier.

### 2.3 Related empirical work

Yu and Benson's *Dissecting Performance of Production QUIC* [6] built a harness around production endpoints (Google, Facebook) rather than unoptimized open-source stacks, finding that implementation choices dominate protocol effects — a caution this thesis takes seriously. A quiche-based re-evaluation of the earlier "Taking a Long Look at QUIC" claims [7] found QUIC unfair to TCP depending on start order, benefits concentrated at high bandwidth and low loss, and degradation under high jitter. The LiteSpeed BBR-over-QUIC experiments [8] isolated the congestion-control variable: under 2% loss, CUBIC took 14.3 s to transfer 10 MB while BBR took 4.7 s. The Demystifying QUIC specification survey [3] provides the clearest modern statement of the loss-detection mechanics used here.

## 3. Methodology

This is a *specification-driven analysis with empirical triangulation*. Primary sources are the normative RFCs and the multipath draft [1][2][4][5], read as executable specifications: every claim about state machines, timers, and frame semantics is traceable to a numbered section. Empirical claims are drawn from peer-reviewed and industrially published measurements [6][7][8], never from synthetic benchmarks authored for this thesis. Where the thesis formalizes a theorem, the proof sketch references the mechanism that enforces the invariant rather than asserting it. Numerical parameters (thresholds, timers) are quoted at their RFC-recommended defaults.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Integrated Handshake: TLS 1.3 Inside QUIC, and the Price of 0-RTT

QUIC replaces sequential TCP+TLS establishment with a single *encryption-level-factored* handshake: TLS 1.3 messages ride in CRYPTO frames at four encryption levels — Initial, Early Data (0-RTT), Handshake, Application Data (1-RTT) — with packet protection keys derived per level via the TLS 1.3 key schedule [2]. A cold handshake completes in one round trip: the client sends an Initial with the TLS ClientHello (plus transport parameters); the server replies with Initial and Handshake flights; both derive 1-RTT keys. This is not two handshakes in parallel — transport parameters and cryptographic negotiation are the same flight.

The more interesting case is **0-RTT resumption**: a client holding a resumption ticket (PSK) sends application data in its very first flight, protected with early-data keys [2]. The latency economics are irresistible — first byte with zero round trips — but 0-RTT data has **no forward secrecy** and **no replay protection**: an attacker capturing a 0-RTT flight can replay it, and the server, lacking scalable per-ticket single-use enforcement, may process the same data twice.

RFC 9001's response is architectural rather than cryptographic: it *contains* the blast radius [2].

> **Theorem (0-RTT Containment).** If an application restricts 0-RTT payloads to idempotent operations, then arbitrary replay of 0-RTT packets preserves application-level correctness.

*Proof sketch.* Replay produces duplicate delivery of the same frames. Duplicate delivery of an idempotent operation has the same net effect as single delivery. Non-frame state changes (connection attempts) cannot complete a handshake from replays, since the handshake requires fresh key shares. ∎

Concretely, RFC 9001 [2] declares STREAM, RESET_STREAM, STOP_SENDING, and CONNECTION_CLOSE frames potentially unsafe in 0-RTT, forbids the server from sending 0-RTT packets, forbids the client from decrypting received 0-RTT packets, and requires the application protocol to define a 0-RTT profile. The client must stop sending 0-RTT on rejection and never send it unless the application explicitly requested it — *latency and security are traded at the application boundary, not hidden in the transport*.

| Handshake mode | Round trips to first byte | Forward secrecy | Replay protection |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cold 1-RTT | 1 | Yes | n/a (fresh handshake) |
| Resumed 0-RTT | 0 | No (early data) | **No** — application must restrict to idempotent ops |
| TLS 1.2 over TCP (legacy) | 3 | Yes | n/a |

### 4.2 Connection Migration and Path Validation: Surviving the 4-Tuple

In TCP, the connection *is* the 4-tuple (source/destination IP and port). Change any element — walk from Wi-Fi to cellular, traverse a NAT rebinding — and the connection dies. QUIC names connections by connection IDs: each endpoint advertises the CIDs it will accept via NEW_CONNECTION_ID frames, and packets carry the destination CID [1]. The 4-tuple becomes mere routing metadata. When a client's address changes, the server observes packets arriving from a new source address bearing a known CID and *migrates* the connection rather than terminating it.

Migration is not free, because it opens a reflection/amplification attack surface: an attacker could spoof a migration to redirect a server's packet flood at a victim. RFC 9000 therefore requires **path validation** before an endpoint sends significant data to a new address [1]. The mechanism is a challenge-response: the migrating endpoint sends PATH_CHALLENGE frames carrying unpredictable 8-byte payloads; the peer must return them in PATH_RESPONSE. Additionally, the anti-amplification rule caps a server to sending at most **3× the bytes received** from an unvalidated address, bounding the abuse potential even if validation is in flight. Preferred-address migration (the server advertising an alternate address in its transport parameters) and deliberate client migration follow the same validated pattern, with `retire_prior_to` sequencing CID retirement so that stale identifiers cannot be weaponized.

> **Theorem (Migration Liveness with Bounded Amplification).** Path validation completes in one RTT on a bidirectional path, and a server never emits more than 3× received bytes toward an unvalidated address.

*Proof sketch.* PATH_CHALLENGE/PATH_RESPONSE is a single round trip by construction. The 3× cap is enforced per remote address in the sender's accounting [1]. ∎

This machinery is the substrate for the multipath extension: Multipath QUIC generalizes "migrate from path A to path B" into "use paths A and B simultaneously."

### 4.3 Packet Number Spaces and Loss Detection: Dissolving Retransmission Ambiguity

TCP's deepest measurement wound is *retransmission ambiguity*: an ACK arriving after a retransmission cannot be attributed to the original or the copy, corrupting RTT estimates (hence Karn's algorithm). QUIC removes the wound: **packet numbers increase monotonically within a space and are never reused**, so retransmitted *frames* travel in *new packets* and ACKs always unambiguously identify what they acknowledge [1][4].

The numbering is factored into three **packet number spaces**, each with independent loss state [1]:

- **Initial** — Initial packets, Initial keys;
- **Handshake** — Handshake packets, Handshake keys;
- **Application Data** — both 0-RTT and 1-RTT packets (deliberately sharing a space so recovery logic is uniform across the encryption-level upgrade).

Each space numbers from 0 over 0..2^62−1, truncated to 1–4 bytes on the wire [1]; cryptographic separation follows for free, since a packet can only be acknowledged within its own space and encryption level.

Loss detection runs **per space**, with three complementary mechanisms [4][3]:

1. **Packet threshold**: a packet is declared lost when a later-numbered packet in the same space is acknowledged and ≥3 (`kPacketThreshold`) packets were sent after it.
2. **Time threshold**: a packet is declared lost if unacknowledged for `9/8 × max(smoothed_rtt, latest_rtt)` (plus 1 ms granularity), tolerating reordering up to that bound.
3. **Probe timeout (PTO)**: one connection-wide timer armed from the earliest-expiring space, firing at `smoothed_rtt + max_ack_delay + 4 × rttvar` (floor 1 ms). On expiry it sends probe packets — new packets carrying unacknowledged frames — with exponential backoff; PTO never declares loss, it provokes the ACKs that let the thresholds decide.

The pseudocode below captures the per-space detection core (adapted from the RFC 9002 Appendix A pseudocode):

```python
K_PACKET_THRESHOLD = 3
K_TIME_THRESHOLD = 9 / 8  # RTT multiplier
K_GRANULARITY = 0.001     # 1 ms

def detect_lost(space, largest_acked, now, srtt, latest_rtt):
    lost = []
    time_cutoff = max(K_TIME_THRESHOLD * max(srtt, latest_rtt),
                      K_GRANULARITY)
    for pkt in space.sent_packets:
        if pkt.number > largest_acked:
            continue
        if pkt.acked or pkt.declared_lost:
            continue
        packet_reordered = (largest_acked - pkt.number) >= K_PACKET_THRESHOLD
        time_expired = (now - pkt.time_sent) >= time_cutoff
        if packet_reordered or time_expired:
            lost.append(pkt)
            pkt.declared_lost = True
    return lost

def pto_duration(srtt, rttvar, max_ack_delay):
    # RFC 9002 Section 6.2.1: single PTO per connection,
    # armed from the earliest space deadline
    return max(srtt + max_ack_delay + 4 * rttvar, K_GRANULARITY)
```

RTT estimation is per-space-aware: peer-reported ACK Delay is folded into `smoothed_rtt` only for the Application Data space (Initial/Handshake ACKs may be handshake-delayed), and `min_rtt` is a floor ACK Delay can never push below [4][3].

> **Theorem (Unambiguous RTT).** Every RTT sample in QUIC is attributable to exactly one transmission, because packet numbers are never reused.

*Proof sketch.* An ACK frame references packet numbers; each number was transmitted exactly once, so the sample `now − time_sent[number]` is unambiguous. No Karn-style exclusion is needed. ∎

### 4.4 Congestion Control over QUIC: NewReno, CUBIC, BBR

RFC 9002 deliberately specifies only a **NewReno baseline** (Appendix B) [4]: slow start, congestion avoidance, halving of the congestion window on loss, and a persistent-congestion collapse that resets the window after sustained loss across the PTO-count threshold. The specification is a floor, not a ceiling — congestion control is a *pluggable* sender-side algorithm, signaled by nothing on the wire. Production stacks run CUBIC or BBR:

- **CUBIC** (loss-based) grows the window as a cubic function of time since last loss. It is fair to TCP CUBIC flows but reads every loss as congestion. On a 2%-loss link, the LiteSpeed measurements show CUBIC collapsing to 14.3 s for a 10 MB transfer [8] — the classic loss-based pathology on wireless and buffer-managed paths.
- **BBR** (model-based) estimates the bottleneck bandwidth (BtlBw) and round-trip propagation time (RTprop) and paces to `BtlBw × RTprop`, ignoring loss as a congestion signal. The same 2%-loss experiment completed in 4.7 s [8]; BBR additionally avoids bufferbloat, so competing latency-sensitive flows (the small-file test) complete in ~1 RTT rather than 1–2 s [8].

QUIC's architecture *helps* BBR: precise, unambiguous RTT samples (Section 4.3) feed the RTprop estimator, and ACK-frequency control plus rich ACK ranges give the bandwidth sampler better data than TCP's cumulative ACKs. The quiche evaluation [7] sounds the cautionary note — QUIC-vs-TCP fairness depends on start order and the CCA pairing, and under high jitter QUIC can underperform — reminding us that the CCA, not the wire format, often dominates outcomes [6].

```python
# BBR-style control sketch over QUIC's precise signals
def bbr_update(state, acked_bytes, rtt_sample):
    state.btlbw = max(state.btlbw * 0.99, acked_bytes / rtt_sample)  # max-filter
    state.rtprop = min(state.rtprop, rtt_sample)                     # min-filter
    bdp = state.btlbw * state.rtprop
    state.pacing_rate = state.btlbw * state.pacing_gain              # cycle gains
    state.cwnd = bdp * state.cwnd_gain                               # cap inflight
```

### 4.5 Multipath QUIC: Generalizing Every Invariant to N Paths

The Multipath QUIC extension (draft-ietf-quic-multipath, Liu et al., currently draft-21) [5] asks: if a connection can *migrate* between paths, why not *use* them simultaneously — Wi-Fi plus cellular on a handset, dual-homed servers, aggregated datacenter links? The draft's central architectural decision is to **generalize every per-connection invariant to per-path state** while leaving scheduling policy unspecified:

- **Negotiation**: an `enable_multipath` transport parameter opts the connection in; without it, all multipath frames are protocol violations [5].
- **Per-path packet number spaces.** This is the deep change. In single-path QUIC, packet numbers identify transmissions connection-wide; with multiple paths, a single space would conflate loss on a lossy path with loss on a clean one. The draft therefore assigns each path its own packet number space, requiring non-zero CIDs to demultiplex path and space, and folding the path identifier into the AEAD nonce calculation [5]. Loss detection, RTT estimation, and congestion control then run **independently per path** — a loss burst on cellular never shrinks the Wi-Fi congestion window.
- **New frame types**: `MP_NEW_PATH`/`MP_RETIRE_PATH` (path lifecycle), `MP_ACK`/`MP_ACK_ECN` (per-path acknowledgments; codepoints 0x15228c02/03), alongside the standard PATH_CHALLENGE/PATH_RESPONSE validation, which every new path must pass before carrying non-probe traffic [5].
- **Scheduling deliberately unspecified.** The draft provides no scheduling algorithm — round-robin, lowest-RTT-first, weighted fair, redundant (for latency), and failover schedulers are all conformant; only implementation guidance appears [5]. Open-source stacks already ship the classic policies. This is the right layering: scheduling is application policy, and the transport's job is to make every policy safe.

> **Theorem (Per-Path Isolation).** With per-path packet number spaces and per-path congestion state, a loss event on path *p* cannot alter the congestion window, RTT estimate, or loss-detection state of any path *q ≠ p*.

*Proof sketch.* All recovery inputs (ACKs via MP_ACK, timers, cwnd) are keyed by path ID; the AEAD and CID demultiplexing guarantee attribution. No cross-path state is read by any recovery algorithm. ∎

Path validation per new path reuses Section 4.2's machinery, and the amplification cap applies per unvalidated remote address, so multipath does not multiply the reflection surface [5][1].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Handshake latency.** The 1-RTT cold handshake and 0-RTT resumption are structural, not empirical — they follow from folding TLS 1.3 into the transport's first flight [2]. The security cost is measured in replay exposure, contained by the application-profile discipline of Section 4.1.

**Head-of-line blocking.** HTTP/3 maps each request/response to an independent QUIC stream; loss on one stream's packets delays only that stream [1][7]. Production analysis reports ~30% latency reduction for mobile users on HTTP/3 versus HTTP/2, attributed to HoL elimination and migration [7]; the same literature cautions that on high-bandwidth fixed links, UDP processing and CPU overhead can erase the gain [7]. The honest statement is conditional: *QUIC eliminates transport HoL; whether that dominates page-load time depends on the bottleneck*.

**Loss detection precision.** The per-space, never-reused numbering yields RTT estimates without Karn exclusions and ACK ranges with SACK semantics by default [4][3]. The single connection-wide PTO (armed from the earliest space deadline) recovers tail losses that pure threshold detection would miss [4].

**Congestion control.** The LiteSpeed BBR/CUBIC-over-QUIC experiments [8] are the cleanest isolation of the CCA variable:

| Loss | CUBIC (s) | BBR (s) | Transfer |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0% | 4.5 | 4.5 | 10 MB |
| 1% | 8.6 | 4.6 | 10 MB |
| 2% | 14.3 | 4.7 | 10 MB |

Loss-based control interprets random loss as congestion; model-based control does not. The fairness caveat from the quiche study [7] stands: BBR can starve CUBIC flows sharing a bottleneck, and QUIC-vs-TCP fairness is start-order dependent.

**Multipath.** The draft [5] is pre-RFC; empirical claims rest on implementations (Go, Rust, and research stacks) demonstrating aggregated throughput and seamless failover. The formal contribution is the isolation theorem of Section 4.5: multipath QUIC is *safe by construction* for any scheduler, because the recovery invariants are per-path.

## 6. Limitations

1. **UDP blocking and fallback.** Networks that block UDP/443 force fallback to TCP; QUIC's benefits are conditional on UDP reachability, and fallback must not silently downgrade security expectations [2].
2. **CPU cost.** User-space packet processing and per-packet cryptography cost CPU; at high throughput on fixed links this can make QUIC slower than kernel-optimized TCP [7][6].
3. **0-RTT remains opt-in and subtle.** The containment theorem assumes correct application profiles; a misconfigured application that sends non-idempotent operations in 0-RTT is vulnerable by design [2].
4. **Multipath scheduling is unsolved in the standard.** Leaving scheduling to implementations invites fragmentation and makes performance non-portable across stacks [5].
5. **Middlebox ossification, round two.** Encrypting the wire image defends against ossification of *fields*, but UDP throttling and QUIC-aware blocking are policy ossification of a different kind.
6. **Fairness.** BBR-over-QUIC's aggression toward loss-based flows, and QUIC-vs-TCP start-order unfairness [7], remain active research problems; BBRv2's loss/ECN-aware modeling is the proposed remedy [8].

## 7. Conclusion

QUIC reimagines transport along four axes — handshake integration, CID-based identity, unambiguous numbering, and pluggable, per-path congestion control — and each axis is a response to a specific TCP scar: sequential handshakes, 4-tuple fragility, retransmission ambiguity, and one-size-fits-all recovery. The 0-RTT design shows the project's philosophical signature most clearly: rather than pretending replay risk away, the specification *prices* it and hands the decision to the application with a formal containment argument. Multipath QUIC extends the same signature to scheduling: the transport guarantees per-path isolation and validated, amplification-bounded path setup, and refuses to legislate policy. If the extension standardizes, the transport layer will have completed its arc from "a reliable byte stream between two addresses" to "a cryptographic, multipath, application-scheduled packet fabric." The theorems in this thesis — containment, migration liveness, unambiguous RTT, per-path isolation — are the load-bearing walls of that fabric.
