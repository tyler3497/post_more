# Algebraic Effects and Effect Handlers: Row Polymorphism, the Koka and Eff Language Designs, Handler Fusion, and Compilation to Native Code

## Abstract

Algebraic effects and their handlers recast computational effects — state, exceptions, nondeterminism, concurrency, input/output — as operations of an algebraic theory together with handlers that supply the theory's models, generalizing exception handlers to arbitrary effects. This thesis develops the design space from categorical foundations to industrial compilation. We present the Plotkin–Pretnar formulation of handlers as the homomorphisms induced by the universal property of free models, then analyze row-polymorphic effect systems as realized in Daan Leijen's Koka, where effect rows with duplicate labels and Hindley–Milner-style inference give precise, modular effect tracking. We contrast Koka with Bauer and Pretnar's Eff, the first language built from the ground up around handlers and instance-indexed effects. We give a rigorous account of handler fusion — the Wu–Schrijvers fusion law collapsing nested handlers into a single fold over the free monad — and of modern compilation: evidence-passing translations, scoped resumptions, tail-resumptive in-place execution, and multi-prompt delimited-control runtimes that compile handlers to portable C and native machine code without runtime handler search.

## 1 Introduction

For most of programming-language history, computational effects were second-class citizens. Moggi's monadic semantics gave them a categorical home, but monads fix the interpretation of an effect once and for all: composing two monads demands monad transformers, whose stacking order changes semantics in surprising ways. The algebraic approach starts elsewhere: an effect is a *signature of operations* — `get`/`set` for state, `raise` for exceptions, `choose` for nondeterminism — subject to equations, generating a free model that induces the monad. The *handler* is then a first-class construct interpreting each operation with access to the *continuation*, generalizing `try/catch` to arbitrary effects [1][2].

Handlers now organize several language designs. **Eff** (Bauer and Pretnar) was the first language built from the ground up around handlers, with effect instances and a type-and-effect system tracking the "dirt" a computation may incur [2]. **Koka** (Leijen) pairs handlers with *row-polymorphic effect types*: rows like `⟨exn | μ⟩` compose modularly, with HM-style inference computing them automatically [3]. **Frank** (Lindley, McBride, McLaughlin) makes handlers the primary abstraction, generalizing functional abstraction via call-by-push-value and an "ambient ability" [8].

Handlers long suffered a performance reputation problem: the rule `handle h E[perform op v] ⟶ f v k` needs a *linear search* for the innermost handler, and capturing `k` is expensive. This thesis argues the problem is now substantially solved through **handler fusion** (nested handlers collapse into single passes [7]), **evidence-passing translations** (constant-offset indexing into evidence vectors; tail-resumptive operations run in place [5][6]), and **multi-prompt delimited-control runtimes** compiling to portable C and native code [6].

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 From monads to algebraic operations

Moggi modeled a notion of computation as a strong monad. Plotkin and Power observed that many such monads arise uniformly from *algebraic theories*: a signature `Σ` of operations plus equations, whose *generic effects* correspond bijectively to *algebraic operations*. Effects so presented are *algebraic*; except for continuations, all standard effects — exceptions, state, nondeterminism, I/O — are algebraic [1]. Combining state and exceptions is then the disjoint union of their signatures, with no order-dependence, unlike monad transformers.

### 2.2 Handlers: the Plotkin–Pretnar construction

Plotkin and Pretnar treated exception handling — long resistant to algebraic treatment — as the special case of a general construct [1]. A *handler* gives, for each operation `op`, a clause `op x k ↦ M` with the captured continuation `k`, plus a return clause `return x ↦ N`; `handle h M` denotes the homomorphism induced by the universal property of the free model.

> **Theorem (Adequacy of handling, Plotkin–Pretnar 2013).** The denotation of `handle h M` equals the unique `Σ`-homomorphism from the free model into the model defined by `h`'s clauses [1].

This one construct subsumes exception handlers, backtracking search, coroutines, stream redirection, timeout and rollback, and CCS relabelling/restriction [1].

### 2.3 Eff: effects as a language-defining feature

Bauer and Pretnar's **Eff** was the first language designed from the ground up around effects and handlers [2]:

1. *Effect instances.* An effect declaration creates a type of *instances* — dynamically created resources with their own operations. State is not global: an instance `r : ref` has `lookup`/`update`, so two references are two instances of one effect type.
2. *Dirt.* The type-and-effect system tracks the operations a computation may perform. Handlers discharge dirt; empty dirt means pure.

Eff proved algebraic effects could be a language's *primary* abstraction, and its instances influenced later capability-passing designs.

### 2.4 The row-polymorphic turn: Koka

Koka (Leijen, 2014) attacked the modularity problem of effect systems [3]. Classical systems assign each function a fixed effect set, demanding subtyping or annotation. Koka uses *row polymorphism*: rows are effect labels ending in a tail variable `μ`, e.g. `⟨exn | μ⟩`. Function types carry rows — `div : (int, int) → ⟨exn | μ⟩ int` — and HM-style inference with row unification computes principal types annotation-free. Koka's device is **duplicate labels**: handling is typed as label *elimination*, and duplicates preserve principality without subtyping. Leijen's POPL 2017 paper gives the type-directed account plus a coherence proof [4].

### 2.5 Frank, and the implementation lineage

Frank (Lindley, McBride, McLaughlin, POPL 2017) makes handlers the *only* abstraction mechanism [8]: built on call-by-push-value, it generalizes functional abstraction — a function is a special case of a handler — with commands handled by an implicitly propagated *ambient ability*. Beyond the core languages, Eff and Links proved the semantics viable; Multicore OCaml retrofitted handlers onto a production compiler with one-shot continuations as heap-allocated stack segments [5]; Koka (§4.4) targets portable C via `libmprompt`/`libmpeff` [6]; and the Haskell ecosystem (extensible effects, freer monads, `fused-effects`) explored handlers as libraries, where fusion originated as an optimization [7].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is synthetic: it reconstructs the design space from primary sources and contributes (a) a comparison of Koka-style rows versus Eff-style dirt, (b) a self-contained exposition of the fusion law with a worked example, (c) an operational account of evidence-passing compilation, and (d) a benchmark methodology with complexity analysis. Anchors: Plotkin–Pretnar [1] for the free-model account; Leijen [3][4] for rows; Wu–Schrijvers [7] for fusion; Xie–Leijen [5][6] for compilation. Code is illustrative pseudocode; theorems carry proof sketches with full proofs cited; empirical claims rest on published reports plus derived complexity arguments.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Signatures, free models, and handlers as homomorphisms

An *effect signature* `Σ` is a set of operation symbols with arities: `op : A_op → B_op` takes an argument, each outcome selecting a continuation branch. A *computation* over `Σ` returning `A` is an element of the free model `F_Σ(A)` — a tree with `return v` leaves and operation nodes over subtrees:

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Callable

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Return:
    value: object

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Op:
    name: str                        # operation symbol
    arg: object                      # argument
    cont: Callable[[object], "Comp"]  # continuation

Comp = Return | Op                   # the free model F_Sigma(A)

def handle(gen, clauses, comp):
    match comp:
        case Return(v):
            return gen(v)
        case Op(name, arg, cont):
            k = lambda b: handle(gen, clauses, cont(b))
            return clauses[name](arg, k)
```

> **Theorem (Handlers as homomorphisms).** `handle(gen, clauses, ·)` is the unique `Σ`-homomorphism from the free model `F_Σ(A)` to the model defined by the clauses. Handler composition is homomorphism composition [1].

This theorem is the field's workhorse: it justifies equational reasoning — fusion in §4.3 is a corollary — and explains why handling is semantically total. *Example:* nondeterminism's `choose : 1 → Bool` is handled by `gen v = [v]`, `choose () k = k True ++ k False` — depth-first search as a homomorphism.

### 4.2 Row polymorphism: the Koka discipline

Koka's type system rests on four ideas [3][4]:

1. **Effect rows.** Closed `⟨l₁, …, lₙ⟩`, open `⟨l₁, …, lₙ | μ⟩`, or bare `μ`; `⟨⟩` is *total* (pure).
2. **Row unification.** `⟨exn | μ₁⟩` with `⟨div | μ₂⟩` gives `⟨exn, div | μ₃⟩` — Rémy rows, scoped labels.
3. **Duplicate labels.** Handling eliminates one occurrence: `catch : (() → ⟨exn | μ⟩ α, exception → ⟨μ⟩ α) → ⟨μ⟩ α`, principal without subtyping.
4. **Inference.** Algorithm W plus row variables; annotations never required.

```haskell
map   : ((a -> e b), list<a>) -> e list<b>          -- any row e
catch : (() -> <exn|e> a, exception -> e a) -> e a  -- discharges one exn
risky : (int, int) -> <exn, div | e> int            -- rows concatenate
```

Contrast Eff's *dirt* [2]: a computation's type records the *instances'* operations it may perform. Dirt distinguishes *which resource* is touched, but composes by set union and generalizes less smoothly than row tails. The tradeoff:

| Dimension | Koka (rows) [3][4] | Eff (dirt) [2] | Frank (ambient ability) [8] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Effect identity | Labels in a row | Instance-indexed operations | Commands on interfaces |
| Polymorphism | Row tails `μ`, inferred | Dirt variables, less automatic | Implicit ambient ability |
| Handler typing | Label elimination, duplicate labels | Dirt discharge by instance | Ability extension |
| Composition | Row concatenation/unification | Set union of dirt | Ability concatenation |
| Inference | Principal (Alg. W + rows) | HM + dirt | Bidirectional, CBPV-based |
| Target | Compiled to C via evidence [6] | Prototype implementation | Research interpreter |

Koka's labels are lexically scoped by handlers; Eff's instances are dynamically created resources, partially recovered via *named handlers* (Xie, Cong, Leijen, HOPE 2021) [6].

### 4.3 Handler fusion: collapsing the tower of interpreters

Naive semantics builds *intermediate trees*: `handle h₂ (handle h₁ m)` reifies `m` handled by `h₁`, then folds. Handlers *are* folds, and folds *fuse* [7]:

> **Theorem (Fusion for free, Wu–Schrijvers 2015).** If `h₁ = fold gen₁ alg₁` and `h₂ = fold gen₂ alg₂` satisfy the homomorphism condition — each handler's clauses commute with the other's fold — then `h₂ ∘ h₁ = fold gen alg`: nested handlers fuse into a *single* fold with no intermediate tree [7].

The condition holds exactly for *algebraic* handlers — folds with algebras natural in the result type: state, reader, writer, accumulation. Fusing a state handler with a logging handler turns two tree traversals into one state-passing fold:

```rust
// Fused state + writer handler: a single fold, no intermediate tree.
fn fused_run<S, W, A>(init: S, comp: &Comp<A>) -> (A, S, Vec<W>) {
    fn go<S, W, A>(s: S, log: &mut Vec<W>, c: &Comp<A>) -> (A, S) {
        match c {
            Comp::Return(v) => (v.clone(), s),
            Comp::Op("get", _, k) => go(s.clone(), log, &k(Box::new(s.clone()))),
            Comp::Op("put", arg, k) => {
                let (v, _) = go(arg.clone(), log, &k(Box::new(())));
                (v, arg.clone())
            }
            Comp::Op("tell", w, k) => {
                log.push(w.clone());
                go(s, log, &k(Box::new(())))
            }
            _ => unreachable!("fused signature is closed"),
        }
    }
    let mut log = Vec::new();
    let (v, s) = go(init, &mut log, comp);
    (v, s, log)
}
```

Handlers inspecting the continuation non-homomorphically — counting invocations, pruning global search state — resist fusion. Fusion is also *deforestation* for effectful programs: the `F_Σ` tree is the effectful analogue of an intermediate list, and handler fusion the analogue of foldr/build fusion — a correspondence Koka's simplifier exploits [6][7].

### 4.4 Compiling handlers to native code: evidence passing and multi-prompt runtimes

The rule `handle h E[perform op v] ⟶ f v k` has two costs: *finding* `h` means searching `E` (linear in depth), and *building* `k` means capturing the continuation. The Xie–Leijen programme eliminates both [5][6]. **(1) Scoped resumptions:** ICFP 2020 restricts handlers so continuations cannot escape their handler's extent — true of all standard effects — buying a sound, *coherent* translation [5]. **(2) Evidence passing:** each handler is passed as *evidence* (a record of its clauses) directly to every operation. A function `τ₁ → ⟨ε⟩ τ₂` compiles to one taking an *evidence vector* `w` per label in `ε`; performing an operation indexes `w` at a constant offset computed statically from the row. No search, ever:

> **Theorem (Soundness and coherence, Xie et al. 2020).** The evidence translation is type-preserving and operationally sound, and any two elaborations of the same program are contextually equivalent [5].

**(3) Tail resumption in place:** when a clause ends in a tail call to the continuation, the translation never captures it — the clause runs in place. State `get`/`put`, reader `ask`, writer `tell` are all tail-resumptive, so the hottest handlers become tight loops [5]. **(4) Multi-prompt generalization:** ICFP 2021 refines the semantics in stages — multi-prompt delimited control, evidence-passing semantics, *yield bubbling* (yields bubble through the context one frame at a time, after Pretnar [2]), and a monadic translation via a multiprompt monad into plain lambda calculus [6]. The result compiles to C, LLVM, WASM, or JavaScript with no special runtime; Koka's backend uses `libmprompt`/`libmpeff`, portable C for multi-prompt delimited continuations, plus Perceus reference counting.

Multicore OCaml made `continue` invalidate `k`, with explicit cloning for multi-shot [5]; Koka copies the evidence-delimited segment only for multi-shot resumptions [6].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof obligations and their status

Three published results underpin the pipeline:

1. **Coherence of type-directed compilation (Leijen, POPL 2017).** Elaboration is type-preserving; different elaborations are contextually equivalent, via a step-indexed logical relation [4].
2. **Soundness and coherence of evidence translation (Xie et al., ICFP 2020).** Under scoped resumptions, operational correspondence holds; constant-offset indexing always finds the innermost handler [5].
3. **Correctness of the multiprompt translation (Xie & Leijen, ICFP 2021).** Each refinement stage is correct as a bisimulation or contextual equivalence [6].

### 5.2 Benchmark methodology

Absolute timings depend on hardware; the portable claims are *complexity* claims, each derived above. The Evidently papers report Koka's translated handlers within a small constant of hand-written code on tail-resumptive workloads, with deep-stack pathologies eliminated [5][6]; Wu and Schrijvers show large speedups from fusion by removing intermediate trees [7]. These *relative* claims transfer across hardware where absolute numbers do not.

| Benchmark | Effect pattern | What it stresses | Complexity target |
|---|---|---|---|
| `state-loop` (10⁷ ops) | Tail-resumptive state | Capture vs. in-place | O(1) per op; ≈ hand-written loop |
| `reader-deep` (10³ handlers) | Deep handler stack | Search vs. evidence indexing | O(1) dispatch at any depth |
| `nqueens` | Multi-shot continuations | Resumption cloning | O(segment size) per clone |
| `except-exn` | Exception-like ops | vs. native exceptions | Small constant of native |
| `ping-pong` | Mutual resumption | Prompt switching | O(1) switch, no allocation |
| `parse-log` fused | State + writer | Fusion (§4.3) | 1 pass vs. 2; no tree alloc |

The portable claims are *complexity* claims: (1) **Dispatch is O(1)** — constant-offset indexing replaces linear handler search, with offsets static from the row [5]. (2) **Tail-resumptive operations allocate nothing** — the continuation is never reified [5]. (3) **Fusion removes a multiplicative factor** — `n` nested algebraic handlers become one fold, eliminating intermediate tree allocation [7]. (4) **Multi-shot resumption is O(s)** for segment size `s`, versus O(1) one-shot — the Multicore OCaml rationale [5].

### 5.3 Worked verification: fusion correctness on state

We sketch fusion for a state handler `hₛ = fold genₛ algₛ` with a writer handler `h_w = fold gen_w alg_w`. The homomorphism condition needs `h_w`'s clauses to commute with `hₛ`'s fold: `tell` appends to the log and resumes without inspecting its continuation, and state operations never touch the log, so the naturality square commutes. By the universal property of the free model, the folds agree on all computations — the fused handler is *provably* equivalent to the nested pair [7]. Fusion is "for free": only the homomorphism check per handler pair, discharged once.

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

- **Scoped resumptions.** Evidence translation needs resumptions not to escape their handler's extent [5]. Exotic control operators are rejected; tracking escape in the row is open.
- **Multi-shot costs.** Cloning is O(s); naive backtracking over deep resumptions can go quadratic beneath "O(1) dispatch". A cost semantics in types is future work.
- **Rows versus instances.** Koka's labels are lexical; two state cells need two labels or named handlers. Eff's instances distinguish resources dynamically but complicate inference [2][3]. A unified theory with principal inference is missing; "accidental handling" persists in pure row systems.
- **Quantitative reasoning.** Rows track *which* operations may occur, not how many, in which order, or at what cost. Protocols, quantitative bounds, and effect-dependent types have no consensus design.
- **Tooling.** Handlers reify control flow that debuggers do not understand; evidence vectors and fused handlers distance the running program from its source. Effect-aware debugging and profiling lag the theory.
- **Linearity.** Combining linear resources with row polymorphism and multi-shot resumptions — where a resource might be resumed twice — is unsolved in full generality, though scoped resumptions bound duplication to the handler's extent.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Algebraic effects have traveled, in fifteen years, from a categorical observation — effects are operations, handlers are models — to a compilation technology emitting competitive native code. Plotkin and Pretnar [1] gave handlers meaning; Eff [2] made them a language-defining feature; Koka [3][4] made them modular; Frank [8] showed they could subsume functional abstraction; Wu and Schrijvers [7] showed nested handlers fuse into single folds; and the Xie–Leijen programme [5][6] showed handlers need neither runtime search nor special hardware, compiling to portable C. Where monads asked the programmer to choose an interpretation up front and live with transformer stacks, handlers defer interpretation to the use site, compose by signature union, fuse by homomorphism laws, and compile by evidence passing. Effects, at last, are just operations — and handling them is just programming.
