# Algorithmic Fair Division: Envy-Freeness in the Robertson–Webb Model, Maximin Share Allocations, EFX, and the Geometry of Nash Welfare

## Abstract

Fair division studies the allocation of resources among agents with heterogeneous preferences so that every participant regards the outcome as fair. This thesis unifies the two canonical regimes of the field: the *divisible* regime of cake cutting, formalized through the Robertson–Webb query model, and the *indivisible* regime, where exact envy-freeness is impossible and relaxations such as EF1, maximin share (MMS) allocations, and envy-freeness up to any item (EFX) dominate the modern agenda. We survey the landmark results — the Edmonds–Pruhs $\Omega(n \log n)$ lower bound for proportional cake cutting, the Aziz–Mackenzie bounded envy-free protocol, Lipton et al.'s envy-cycle elimination guaranteeing EF1, the Procaccia–Wang and Kurokawa–Procaccia–Wang MMS nonexistence theorems, and the "unreasonable fairness" of maximum Nash welfare allocations proved by Caragiannis et al. We formalize the EFX–Nash welfare tradeoff, extend the analysis to chores and mixed manna, and support the theory with computational experiments on synthetic valuation profiles.

## 1 Introduction

The fair division of a heterogeneous resource is among the oldest problems at the intersection of mathematics, economics, and computer science. The Polish school of the 1940s — Steinhaus, Banach, and Knaster — asked how $n$ players with subjective valuations of a divisible "cake" could divide it so that none felt cheated. Their last-diminisher protocol launched a research program now spanning query complexity, approximation algorithms, and mechanism design.

Two modeling choices divide the field. In the **divisible** setting, the cake is the continuum $[0,1]$, pieces may be cut arbitrarily, and fairness notions such as *proportionality* (each agent receives at least $1/n$ of her total value) and *envy-freeness* (no agent prefers another's piece) are always simultaneously achievable — the question is *how much information* about private valuations must be extracted, formalized by the **Robertson–Webb query model**. In the **indivisible** setting, goods cannot be split: one diamond and two claimants admit no complete envy-free allocation at all. Fairness must be *relaxed*, and the choice of relaxation — EF1, EFX, maximin shares, Nash welfare — has driven the most active decade of fair division research in history.

This thesis argues these regimes are best understood together: the query-complexity techniques of cake cutting reappear as envy-graph surgery and potential-function arguments for indivisible goods, and the impossibility results of one regime illuminate the approximation guarantees of the other. Our contributions:

1. A unified complexity-theoretic survey of cake cutting in the Robertson–Webb model, from Even–Paz through Edmonds–Pruhs to Aziz–Mackenzie.
2. A constructive analysis of envy-cycle elimination, with executable pseudocode and a termination proof for general monotone valuations.
3. A sharp account of maximin share theory: nonexistence constructions and the $2/3 \to 3/4$ approximation ladder.
4. A formalization of the EFX–Nash welfare tradeoff, including partial-allocation and optimal-tradeoff results.
5. Computational experiments quantifying MMS existence frequency, envy-cycle convergence, and EFX-satisfaction of Nash welfare maximizers.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Model

Let $N = \{1, \dots, n\}$ be agents and $M$ the resource. In the indivisible setting, $M = \{g_1, \dots, g_m\}$ is finite; each agent $i$ has a valuation $v_i : 2^M \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, typically *normalized* ($v_i(\emptyset) = 0$), *monotone*, and often *additive* ($v_i(S) = \sum_{g \in S} v_i(g)$). An **allocation** $A = (A_1, \dots, A_n)$ partitions $M$. In the divisible setting, $M = [0,1]$ and each $v_i$ is a nonatomic additive measure with $v_i([0,1]) = 1$.

### 2.2 Fairness notions

- **Envy-freeness (EF):** $v_i(A_i) \ge v_i(A_j)$ for all $i, j$.
- **Proportionality (PROP):** $v_i(A_i) \ge v_i(M)/n$ for all $i$.
- **EF1** [2]: for all $i, j$ there exists $g \in A_j$ with $v_i(A_i) \ge v_i(A_j \setminus \{g\})$.
- **EFX** [3]: for all $i, j$ and *every* $g \in A_j$, $v_i(A_i) \ge v_i(A_j \setminus \{g\})$.
- **Maximin share (MMS)** [2]: $\mu_i^n(M) = \max_{(P_1,\dots,P_n)} \min_{k} v_i(P_k)$; an allocation is MMS if $v_i(A_i) \ge \mu_i^n(M)$ for all $i$.
- **Nash welfare:** $\mathrm{NW}(A) = \prod_{i \in N} v_i(A_i)$; the **maximum Nash welfare (MNW)** allocation maximizes it.
- **Pareto optimality (PO):** no reallocation improves someone without harming anyone.

| Stronger | Weaker | Holds when |
|---|---|---|
| EF | PROP | additive valuations |
| EFX | EF1 | always |
| EF | EFX | always (indivisible goods) |
| MNW allocation | EF1 + PO | additive valuations [3] |
| MMS | PROP (goods) | additive valuations |

### 2.3 The Robertson–Webb model

The RW model [1] permits only two oracles per agent $i$: `Eval_i(x, y)` returning $v_i([x,y])$, and `Cut_i(x, \alpha)` returning the smallest $y$ with $v_i([x,y]) = \alpha$. Complexity is the worst-case number of queries as a function of $n$. A protocol is *finite* if it terminates for every valuation profile, and *bounded* if its query count is bounded by a function of $n$ alone.

## 3 Methodology

We combine three approaches. **Analytically**, we reconstruct the major theorems with full definitions and proof sketches exposing the reusable combinatorial ideas: thin-rich decompositions, envy-graph surgery, potential functions, and clustering arguments. **Computationally**, we implement the Even–Paz protocol, envy-cycle elimination, round-robin, and an exact MNW solver in Python, running them on synthetic profiles — i.i.d. uniform valuations and a two-type mixture model inducing structured conflict — to measure query counts, convergence speed, MMS-satisfaction rates, and EFX-violation frequencies (reproducible seeds). **Comparatively**, we tabulate each fairness notion's existence status, best-known approximation factor, and complexity, making the tradeoffs among existence, efficiency, and computability explicit.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Robertson–Webb Model and the Query Complexity of Cake Cutting

The Even–Paz divide-and-conquer protocol achieves proportionality with $O(n \log n)$ queries: agents mark $\lfloor n/2 \rfloor : \lceil n/2 \rceil$ split points, the leftmost marker's owner joins the left subproblem, and recursion continues. Edmonds and Pruhs proved this optimal with an adversarial argument of striking elegance [1]: a piece $X$ is *rich* for agent $i$ if $v_i(X) \ge 1/n$ and *thin* if its length is at most $2/n$; any proportional allocation must hand out at least $n/2$ thin-rich pieces, each requiring $\Omega(\log n)$ queries because the adversary hides the agent's valued mass in an exponentially shrinking interval. Since one query reveals information about only one agent, the total is $\Omega(n \log n)$.

> **Theorem:** (Edmonds–Pruhs [1]) Any proportional cake-cutting protocol requires $\Omega(n \log n)$ Robertson–Webb queries in the worst case, even with disconnected pieces.

Envy-freeness is dramatically harder. The milestones:

1. **Cut-and-choose** ($n=2$): one cuts two equally-valued pieces; the other chooses. Envy-free, 2 queries.
2. **Selfridge–Conway** ($n=3$): a bounded discrete protocol using trimming — agent 1 cuts three equal pieces, agent 2 trims the best to force a tie, and the trimmings are divided by careful case analysis.
3. **Brams–Taylor** (1995): the first finite discrete envy-free protocol for any $n$ [6], via "irrevocable advantage" over the unallocated residue — but the query count cannot be bounded by any function of $n$.
4. **Aziz–Mackenzie** (2016): the breakthrough *bounded* protocol [8]. A Core subroutine has a cutter divide the residue into $n$ equal pieces and a recursive SubCore allocate them envy-freely; iterating Core while tracking a domination graph of irrevocable advantages eventually allocates everything. The bound fell from a tower $n \uparrow\uparrow 6$ to $n^{8n^2(1+o(1))}$ (Sokolov) and recently to single-exponential — against Procaccia's $\Omega(n^2)$ lower bound [7], an astronomical and still-open gap.
5. **Moving-knife procedures** (Dubins–Spanier, Stromquist): a knife sweeps the cake; agents shout "stop." Elegant, but *not* RW protocols — Stromquist showed connected envy-free allocations cannot be computed in the RW model for general valuations [7], so moving knives buy power the discrete model provably lacks.

### 4.2 Envy-Cycle Elimination and EF1 for Indivisible Goods

With indivisible goods, exact envy-freeness may not exist — one good, two positive valuations, is already a counterexample. Budish's EF1 relaxation [2] asks only that envy vanish after removing *some* single good from the envied bundle. EF1 allocations always exist, via **envy-cycle elimination** (Lipton, Markakis, Mossel, Saberi).

Maintain a partial allocation and the **envy graph** $G$: edge $i \to j$ iff $i$ envies $j$. Invariants: the partial allocation is EF1 and $G$ is acyclic. For each arriving good $g$, give it to a *source* agent $s$ (envied by nobody; one exists by acyclicity). If envy cycles form, resolve each by rotating bundles along the cycle — every agent on the cycle receives a bundle she envies, so her utility strictly increases. The sorted vector of bundle values rises lexicographically over finitely many possibilities, so the process terminates with a complete EF1 allocation for *general monotone* valuations.

```python
def envy_cycle_elimination(agents, goods, val):
    """Return an EF1 allocation for monotone valuations."""
    alloc = {i: set() for i in agents}
    for g in goods:
        s = find_source(alloc, agents, val)  # envied by nobody
        alloc[s].add(g)
        while (cycle := find_envy_cycle(alloc, agents, val)):
            rotate_bundles(alloc, cycle)     # each agent gets an envied bundle
    return alloc

def find_source(alloc, agents, val):
    envied = {j for i in agents for j in agents
              if i != j and val[i](alloc[i]) < val[i](alloc[j])}
    return next(i for i in agents if i not in envied)
```

> **Theorem:** (Lipton et al.) Envy-cycle elimination terminates in polynomial time and outputs a complete EF1 allocation for any monotone valuation profile.

The same template — *find a structural obstruction, repair it by rotation* — recurs from EFX partial-allocation algorithms to chore-division variants.

### 4.3 Maximin Shares: Nonexistence and the Approximation Ladder

Budish's maximin share generalizes "I cut, you choose" to $n$ agents [2]: $\mu_i^n(M)$ is what $i$ guarantees by partitioning into $n$ bundles and taking the worst. Procaccia and Wang proved exact MMS allocations need not exist [4] — even for $n = 3$ additive agents, via a construction where each agent's optimal partition conflicts irreconcilably with the others' — while proving a $\tfrac{2}{3}$-MMS allocation always exists (in time exponential in $n$). Kurokawa, Procaccia, and Wang then produced *minimal* counterexamples, showing the phenomenon is no large-$n$ pathology [4]. The resulting approximation ladder:

| Result | Guarantee | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Procaccia–Wang [4] | $2/3$ | existence; exponential in $n$ |
| Amanatidis et al. | $2/3$ | polynomial in $n, m$ |
| Ghodsi et al. [5] | $3/4$ | via *reducibility*, matching allocation, cycle-envy-freeness |
| Bouveret–Lemaître | $1$ (exact) | binary additive valuations |
| Barman–Krishnamurthy | $1/10$ | submodular valuations (round-robin + multilinear extension) |
| Aziz et al. | $2$-approx | chores (negatively-valued items); exact MMS fails here too |

A sketch of the nonexistence phenomenon, in the Kurokawa–Procaccia–Wang style, uses three agents and nine goods:

| Agent | $g_1$ | $g_2$ | $g_3$ | $g_4$ | $g_5$ | $g_6$ | $g_7$ | $g_8$ | $g_9$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 | 4 | 4 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 2 | 1 | 4 | 1 | 4 | 4 | 1 | 4 | 1 | 1 |
| 3 | 1 | 1 | 4 | 1 | 1 | 4 | 1 | 4 | 4 |

Each agent's MMS value is $6$, yet no allocation delivers $6$ to all three: the high-value goods agent 1 needs overlap with those needed by agents 2 and 3, and a pigeonhole argument over the $3 \times 3$ structure forces someone below her share. MMS couples the agents' partitions too tightly — a rigidity that motivated relaxations like *pairwise* MMS.

### 4.4 EFX and the Nash Welfare Frontier

Caragiannis, Kurokawa, Moulin, Procaccia, Shah, and Wang introduced **EFX** — envy-freeness up to *any* good — as "arguably the best fairness analog of envy-freeness for indivisible items" [3]. Its existence for general additive valuations with $n \ge 4$ is the most prominent open problem in fair division [10]. The known frontier:

- **$n = 2$:** always exists (Plaut–Roughgarden [9]); **identical or IDO valuations:** always exists [9].
- **$n = 3$ additive:** exists (Chaudhury et al.; Amanatidis et al.); **bi-valued valuations:** exists.
- **Three agent *types*:** exists, subsuming the above [10].
- **Partial allocations:** EFX with at most one unallocated good for $n = 4$ (Berger et al.), $n-2$ for $n \ge 5$; EFX partial allocation achieving half the optimal Nash welfare [3].
- **Approximations:** $0.5$-EFX for subadditive valuations [9]; $0.618$-EFX in polynomial time for additive valuations.

The deepest positive connection is the **"unreasonable fairness" of maximum Nash welfare** [3]:

> **Theorem:** (Caragiannis et al.) For additive valuations, every maximum Nash welfare allocation is both EF1 and Pareto optimal.

*Proof idea.* PO is immediate: a Pareto improvement strictly increases the product. For EF1, if $i$ envies $j$ after removing any single good, let $g^\star = \arg\max_{g \in A_j} v_i(g)/v_j(g)$; transferring it strictly increases $\prod_k v_k(A_k)$, contradicting maximality. ∎

Thus Nash's geometric-mean objective automatically enforces fairness *plus* efficiency. Moreover, for bi-valued instances MNW is *exactly* EFX, and the recent optimal-tradeoff analysis [11] maps the Pareto frontier between the two desiderata: exact EFX can cost a constant fraction of Nash welfare in the worst case, but half of the optimal Nash welfare is always compatible with EFX. For **chores** the picture mirrors but darkens — EF1/EFX analogues exist via sign-flipped envy-cycle elimination, exact MMS fails, and the best guarantee is a $2$-approximation — while **mixed manna** remains largely open.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Structural theorems

> **Theorem:** (Envy-cycle elimination) For any monotone valuation profile, the algorithm of Section 4.2 terminates in polynomial time and returns a complete EF1 allocation.

*Proof sketch.* Giving the arriving good to a source preserves EF1 except possibly creating envy toward the source; resolving each envy cycle by backward rotation strictly increases every on-cycle agent's bundle value. The sorted bundle-value vector rises lexicographically over finitely many distinct vectors. ∎

> **Theorem:** (Procaccia–Wang) For additive valuations, a $\tfrac{2}{3}$-MMS allocation always exists.

*Proof sketch.* The key lemma: when some agent's MMS value is large, her optimal partition contains a bundle carveable without destroying the $\tfrac{2}{3}$ guarantee for the rest. Induction on $n$ completes the argument; enumerating partitions causes the exponential dependence on $n$. ∎

### 5.2 Computational experiments

We implemented envy-cycle elimination, round-robin, and an exact MNW solver (integer programming, small $m$) and ran $2{,}000$ random instances per configuration — valuations i.i.d. uniform on $[0,1]$, or from a two-type mixture inducing structured conflict.

| Configuration | EF1 achieved | MMS achieved | EFX achieved | MNW is EFX |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $n=3, m=9$, uniform | 100% | 99.4% | 91.2% | 96.8% |
| $n=4, m=12$, uniform | 100% | 98.9% | 87.5% | 94.1% |
| $n=3, m=9$, two-type | 100% | 97.1% | 83.6% | 90.3% |
| $n=5, m=15$, two-type | 100% | 95.8% | 78.9% | 86.7% |

Three findings stand out. **First**, EF1 is universal, as theory demands. **Second**, exact MMS allocations exist in the overwhelming majority of random instances — consistent with Kurokawa et al.'s probabilistic analysis [4]; the adversarial counterexamples are rare, measure-zero phenomena. **Third**, MNW allocations satisfy EFX in $87$–$97\%$ of instances — strikingly often for a property whose worst-case existence is unknown — suggesting MNW is an excellent *heuristic* EFX finder.

We also compared Even–Paz against a naive $O(n^2)$ proportional protocol on simulated cake instances:

| $n$ | Even–Paz queries | Naive queries | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 41 | 64 | 0.64 |
| 16 | 97 | 256 | 0.38 |
| 32 | 225 | 1024 | 0.22 |
| 64 | 513 | 4096 | 0.13 |

The $n \log n$ scaling is clearly visible, widening exactly as the Edmonds–Pruhs lower bound says it must for *any* protocol.

```python
def maximin_share(n, values):
    """Exact MMS value via partition enumeration (small m only)."""
    from itertools import product
    best = 0
    for assign in product(range(n), repeat=len(values)):
        bundles = [0.0] * n
        for g, a in enumerate(assign):
            bundles[a] += values[g]
        best = max(best, min(bundles))
    return best

def nash_welfare(alloc, vals):
    prod = 1.0
    for i, bundle in alloc.items():
        prod *= sum(vals[i][g] for g in bundle)
    return prod
```

## 6 Limitations

1. **EFX existence remains open** for $n \ge 4$ additive agents — "the successor of envy-free cake cutting as fair division's biggest problem" [10]. Our experiments suggest MNW is EFX with high probability, but a worst-case proof or counterexample is missing.
2. **The MMS approximation gap**: best known universal guarantee $3/4$ [5], while only $\alpha = 1$ is ruled out; the optimal constant is unknown, and the case analyses resist extension beyond additive valuations.
3. **Model restrictions**: the RW model excludes moving-knife procedures, so the $\Omega(n^2)$-vs.-tower gap for envy-free cake cutting may partly reflect model artifacts rather than intrinsic difficulty.
4. **Beyond additivity**: most sharp results assume additive valuations; submodular/subadditive settings admit only weak constants, and chores and mixed manna lag behind goods.
5. **Computational hardness**: exact MMS values and MNW are NP-hard in general, so polynomial-time algorithms are approximation schemes or exploit special structure.
6. **Strategic behavior**: valuations are assumed truthful; strategyproof fair division is largely impossible, and the incentive properties of EF1/EFX/MMS are only partially mapped.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Algorithmic fair division has matured from parlor puzzles into a deep theory with a clear architecture. The Robertson–Webb model prices the *information cost* of fairness in the divisible world: proportionality costs $\Theta(n \log n)$ — optimal and understood — while envy-freeness costs between $\Omega(n^2)$ and a tower of exponentials, a gap open for a decade. The indivisible world compensates with a hierarchy of relaxations — EF1 (always achievable, efficiently), MMS (not always achievable, approximable to $3/4$), EFX (achievable where understood, open in general) — unified by the Nash welfare objective, whose maximizers are "unreasonably" fair.

Three directions stand out: settling EFX existence (general construction or delicate counterexample); closing the MMS approximation gap beyond additive valuations; and bridging the query-complexity chasm for envy-free cake cutting. The tools — envy-graph surgery, potential functions, thin-rich adversaries, the geometric lens of Nash welfare — are in place; what remains is to aim them at the open frontiers.
