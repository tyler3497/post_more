# Mechanical Certainty for Concurrency: The Temporal Logic of Actions, Iris Separation Logic, and Model Checking of Concurrent Systems

## Abstract

Concurrent systems dominate modern computing yet resist conventional testing: interleavings explode combinatorially, and races manifest only under rare schedules. This thesis synthesizes three rigorous traditions for taming concurrency — Leslie Lamport's Temporal Logic of Actions (TLA), modern concurrent separation logics culminating in Iris, and fully automatic model checking founded by Clarke and Emerson. We formalize action-based specifications with stuttering invariance and refinement, derive the frame rule and ghost-state reasoning for heap-manipulating threads, and present the fixpoint algorithms underlying temporal-logic model checking with complexity analysis. Empirical evidence from Amazon Web Services, where TLA+ and the TLC model checker exposed seven deep design bugs missed by testing, review, and fault injection, demonstrates industrial viability [6]. We compare deductive verification (sound, compositional, labor-intensive) against model checking (automatic, bounded, state-explosion-prone), prove key meta-theorems including the refinement-safety theorem and linearizability locality, and articulate the open frontiers: weak-memory mechanization, quantitative verification of liveness, and unified logics spanning specification and proof.

## 1 Introduction

Few propositions in computer science are simultaneously as banal and as terrifying as this one: *the program you tested on Monday may fail on Tuesday*, not because the code changed, but because the scheduler chose a different interleaving. Dijkstra's 1965 solution to the mutual exclusion problem [2] founded concurrent programming; more than half a century later, concurrency remains the single largest source of catastrophic software failure, from cloud control planes to kernel lock-free data structures.

The root difficulty is epistemic rather than algorithmic. Sequential programs admit a total order of events; the programmer can reason by induction over execution steps. Concurrent programs admit only a *partial order*: the happens-before relation of Lamport, whose famous observation was that physical simultaneity across distributed clocks is meaningless and only causal structure survives. When processes communicate through shared memory, the number of interleavings grows as the multinomial coefficient of per-thread step counts, and the bug that testing misses is precisely the interleaving that only emerges under production load.

Three mathematical traditions have risen to this challenge, and this thesis treats them in unified fashion:

1. **Specification and model checking in the Temporal Logic of Actions (TLA).** TLA, introduced by Leslie Lamport [1], treats a program as a formula describing a set of *behaviors* — infinite sequences of states. Actions are predicates over a pair of states; fairness is first-class; and refinement between specifications is implication between formulas. The TLA+ language and the TLC model checker turned this theory into an industrial practice [6].

2. **Deductive proof with concurrent separation logic.** Reynolds' separation logic [4] solved the frame problem for heaps through the separating conjunction `*`; O'Hearn's concurrent extension split ownership across threads; and Iris [2][3] unified two decades of concurrent program logic into a single higher-order framework mechanized in Coq, with partial commutative monoids and invariants as orthogonal primitives.

3. **Automatic model checking of temporal specifications.** Clarke and Emerson's 1981 algorithm [7] for CTL model checking over Kripke structures established that temporal verification can be *decided* — pushed the verification problem from proof search to graph algorithms, eventually earning the 2007 Turing Award shared with Sifakis.

> Theorem: (Thesis statement). Every correct concurrent design admits a mechanical certificate of correctness along one of two axes: a *finite* reachable-state certificate checkable by temporal model checking, or a *compositional* deductive certificate checkable by a separation-logic proof assistant. The two axes are complementary in cost, scope, and automation, and neither subsumes the other.

The remainder of this thesis formalizes both axes, weighs their evidence, states their limits precisely, and points to the hybrid frontier.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Concurrency, Histories, and Correctness Conditions

A concurrent system is conventionally modeled as a set of sequential processes communicating through shared objects. Herlihy and Wing's *linearizability* [5] gives the canonical correctness condition for concurrent objects: each operation appears to take effect *instantaneously* at some point — the *linearization point* — between its invocation and its response. Formally, a history `H` is linearizable if there exists a legal sequential history `S` such that (i) `H` is equivalent to `S` (same per-process projection), and (ii) the real-time order of `H` is preserved in `S`. Linearizability is both *local* — a system is linearizable iff each object is — and *nonblocking*, properties that together justify its dominance as the contract between lock-free library authors and their clients [5].

Weaker conditions — sequential consistency, causal consistency, eventual convergence — trade real-time guarantees for availability, but for the verification problems of this thesis linearizability is the gold standard: it lets us reduce concurrent object correctness to sequential specification, and it composes.

### 2.2 Why Testing Fails

Empirical methodology founders on a counting argument. With `n` threads each executing `k` instructions, the number of serial interleavings is `(n·k)! / (k!)ⁿ`; for `n = 4, k = 100` this exceeds `10²³⁰`. Worse, the scheduler's choices are not uniformly random — they correlate with hardware timing, cache behavior, and system load, so the failing interleaving is systematically the least likely to be sampled. This is the argument that moves verification from *sampling* executions to *reasoning about* all executions, whether by proof or by exhaustive symbolic exploration.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis follows a *comparative formal methods* methodology in three stages.

1. **Axiomatize.** Each tradition is presented from first principles: the syntax and semantics of TLA, the proof rules of separation logic, and the fixpoint characterization of CTL satisfaction. Definitions are chosen to be standard (following [1], [4], and [7]) so that results transfer to the primary literature.

2. **Exemplify.** A single running example — a *concurrent FIFO queue* with `enqueue` and `dequeue` — is carried through all three traditions: specified in TLA+, verified linearly with Iris-style ghost state, and model-checked with TLC/CTL. Running one example through every machinery exposes precisely where each shines and where each strains.

3. **Evaluate empirically.** Theoretical claims are grounded in the largest published industrial evidence base: the AWS formal-methods deployment reported by Newcombe et al. [6], plus the mechanized proof corpus of the Iris project [3]. Cost, bug yield, and coverage are compared on equal footing.

All citations are to peer-reviewed venues or archival technical reports with persistent identifiers; no claim rests on a single informal source.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Temporal Logic of Actions

TLA begins with a radical simplification: *a system is a formula, and a property is a formula, and correctness is implication*. Let a state assign values to variables; a *behavior* is an infinite sequence of states `σ = s₀, s₁, …`. An *action* `A` is a predicate on a pair of states, written with primed variables denoting next-state values: `x' = x + 1` holds of `(s, t)` iff `t(x) = s(x) + 1`.

The crucial TLA operator is `□[A]_v`, read "always `A` or stutter on `v`":

```tla
Next == \E p \in Procs : Enq(p) \/ Deq(p)
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_vars /\ WF_vars(Next)
```

Here `[Next]_vars` means every step either satisfies `Next` or leaves `vars` unchanged — a *stuttering step*. Stuttering invariance is the masterstroke: it makes refinement a simple implication between formulas. If `Spec₁ ⇒ Spec₂` is valid, then every behavior of implementation 1 is a behavior of (refines) specification 2, because the implementation may take fine-grained steps that the specification views as stuttering. No other temporal framework makes step-refinement this clean [1].

Safety is expressed as `□P` (an invariant over states, via the standard `[]` always operator on `[Next]_v` next-state formulas), and liveness as *fairness*: `WF_v(A)` (weak fairness) asserts that if `A` is continuously enabled it is eventually taken; `SF_v(A)` (strong fairness) asserts that if `A` is infinitely often enabled it is infinitely often taken. Lamport proved the fundamental *refinement theorem*: if `Init₁ ⇒ Init₂`, `[N₁]_v ⇒ [N₂]_v`, and fairness conditions align, then `Spec₁ ⇒ Spec₂`.

> Theorem: (Stuttering invariance). If behaviors `σ` and `τ` differ only by finite stuttering on `v`, then `σ ⊨ F` iff `τ ⊨ F` for every TLA formula `F` with `v` as its tuple. Hence adding or removing stuttering steps preserves all TLA-definable properties, including fairness.

This theorem underwrites the standard TLA+ practice of proving a message-passing *system* spec refines an *algorithmic* spec by pure implication — the pattern behind every AWS case study [6].

### 4.2 Model Checking: From CTL to TLC

Clarke and Emerson [7] observed that for *finite* Kripke structures, temporal satisfaction is decidable by a labeling algorithm. Given structure `M = (S, R, L)` and CTL formula `f`, the algorithm computes, for each subformula `g`, the set `Sat(g) ⊆ S` of states satisfying `g`, bottom-up:

| Subformula | Fixpoint characterization |
|---|---|
| `EG f` | `νZ. Sat(f) ∩ pre(Z)` — greatest fixpoint |
| `E[f₁ U f₂]` | `μZ. Sat(f₂) ∪ (Sat(f₁) ∩ pre(Z))` — least fixpoint |
| `AX f` | states whose all successors satisfy `f` |

where `pre(Z)` is the set of states with a successor in `Z`. The algorithm runs in time `O(|f| · (|S| + |R|))` — *linear* in both model and formula size [7]. The fixpoint structure is no accident: Emerson's precursor work mapped CTL modalities into the modal μ-calculus, and Tarski's fixpoint theorem guarantees convergence.

TLC, the TLA+ model checker, is an *explicit-state* checker for untyped ZF set theory: it enumerates reachable states by BFS/DFS over the `Next` relation, checking invariants and, with bounded depth or fairness-aware exploration, liveness [6]. Its strength is generality — any TLA+ operator is executable in principle — and its weakness is the state explosion it inherits: the reachable set of a faithful model of DynamoDB replication is far beyond enumeration, so engineers *abstract*, replacing payloads with symbolic constants and bounding queue lengths. The AWS discipline is explicit about this: the model is not the system, and the engineer's judgment selects the abstraction level. The empirical record (Section 5) shows that even aggressive abstraction catches the bugs that matter.

Symbolic techniques — BDDs, SAT-based bounded model checking, and partial-order reduction — represent state *sets* rather than states, but TLA+'s untyped set theory resists symbolic encoding, which is why TLC remains explicit-state by design.

### 4.3 Separation Logic: Local Reasoning about Heaps

Hoare logic foundered on aliasing: `{P} c {Q}` in classical Hoare logic says nothing about the heap cells `c` does not mention. Reynolds' separation logic [4] repairs this with two innovations:

- The *separating conjunction* `P ∗ Q`: `P` and `Q` hold of *disjoint* heap portions. The points-to assertion `ℓ ↦ v` claims ownership of exactly one cell.
- The *frame rule*, the engine of local reasoning:

```
{P} c {Q}
──────────────── (FRAME)
{P ∗ R} c {Q ∗ R}
```

If `c` is verified against its small footprint `P`, the frame rule lifts the proof to any larger heap: *ownership* is the permission to touch, and unowned cells are automatically preserved.

O'Hearn extended the discipline to concurrency with the *disjoint parallelism* rule: threads verified against disjoint resources compose without interference. To handle *shared* resources, concurrent separation logic introduces *invariants* `I`: assertions that must hold whenever no thread is inside the critical section, with the lock rule transferring ownership of `I`'s footprint into and out of the atomic region.

### 4.4 Iris: Monoids and Invariants as Orthogonal Primitives

Two decades of concurrent logics accumulated baroque primitive notions — rely/guarantee relations, subjective auxiliary state, logically atomic triples, prophecy variables — each handling a feature the others could not. The Iris project [2][3] asked the reductionist question: *what is the minimal basis from which all of these derive?* The answer, validated by a full Coq mechanization, is two orthogonal concepts:

1. **Partial commutative monoids (PCMs)** for ghost state. A PCM `(M, ·, ε)` models *shareable knowledge*: the authoritative/exclusive construction `Auth(M)` lets many threads hold *fragments* `◯a` while one ghost location holds the authoritative element `●a`, with the agreement law `●a ∗ ◯b ⊢ b ⪯ a` connecting them. Fractional permissions, counting permissions, and monotonically evolving ghost state are all instances.

2. **Invariants** `P^N`: persistent, duplicable assertions about shared state, opened only around *physically atomic* steps. The invariant rule

```
    atomic(e)    {P ∗ ▷I} e {v. Q ∗ ▷I}^E\N
─────────────────────────────────────────────
         I^N ⊢ {P} e {v. Q}^E
```

lets a thread borrow the invariant's resources for one atomic step, provided it restores `▷I` (the *later* modality, guarding step-indexed soundness). The later modality `▷` is the price of higher-order ghost state — impredicative invariants require guarded recursion — and Jung et al. proved it is *essential*: removing it makes the logic inconsistent [3].

From these two primitives, Iris *derives* as theorems: Hoare triples, the frame rule, logically atomic triples (for linearizability-style specifications with non-fixed linearization points), prophecy variables, and even Iris's own program-logic layer — "Iris from the ground up" [3] shows the program logic is merely a derived form in a small base logic, a striking conceptual simplification.

Concretely, a lock-free queue's linearizability proof in Iris proceeds by: (i) allocating ghost state tracking the abstract queue contents via an authoritative PCM; (ii) establishing the invariant tying the concrete linked structure to the ghost abstraction; (iii) proving each operation's triple by opening the invariant at the *linearization point* — the single atomic step (e.g., the successful CAS swinging the tail pointer) where the abstract effect becomes visible. The proof is machine-checked in Coq; the trusted base is Coq's kernel plus the operational semantics.

## 5 Empirical Evidence / Proofs

### 5.1 Industrial Evidence: TLA+ at Amazon Web Services

The strongest empirical case for specification-level verification comes from Newcombe et al.'s account of TLA+ adoption at AWS [6]. Beginning with DynamoDB and S3 in 2011, engineers wrote TLA+ models of system designs — described as "exhaustively testable pseudo-code" — and model-checked them with TLC before implementation. The reported results are striking:

- **Seven serious, subtle bugs** were found across ten systems by TLC, including one requiring a **35-step trace** — bugs missed by design review, conventional testing, static analysis, stress testing, and fault injection.
- Engineers **learned TLA+ from scratch** and integrated it into normal development; the paper reports high perceived value and spreading adoption.
- The methodology enabled **aggressive optimization** of complex replication and consensus algorithms, because the model checker certified that the optimized design preserved the original's safety properties.

The authors are candid about the central caveat: formal methods verify *models*, not systems — "all models are wrong, some are useful" — so the value depends on the engineer's judgment in choosing what to model [6]. This is not a weakness of the method so much as a statement of its contract: model checking shifts the epistemic burden from *finding interleavings* (which machines do exhaustively) to *choosing abstractions* (which remains human).

### 5.2 Mechanized Evidence: The Iris Corpus

On the deductive side, the Iris project has accumulated one of the largest machine-checked concurrent-proof corpora in existence: verified examples include lock-free data structures, a concurrent garbage collector, Rust's type system soundness (RustBelt [3]), and weak-memory program logics (the 2024 AxSL work extends Iris to Arm-A [3]). Every proof is checked by Coq's kernel — a few thousand lines of trusted code — so the *proof-checking* trust base is small even though the *proof-construction* effort is large.

### 5.3 Two Meta-Theorems

> Theorem: (Refinement preserves safety). If `Spec₁ ⇒ Spec₂` is valid in TLA and `P` is a safety property with `Spec₂ ⇒ □P`, then `Spec₁ ⇒ □P`. *Proof.* By transitivity of implication; stuttering invariance guarantees the implication respects the observable/unobservable variable distinction. ∎

> Theorem: (Linearizability locality [5]). A concurrent system is linearizable iff each of its objects is linearizable. *Proof sketch.* Given per-object linearizations, Herlihy and Wing show they can be merged preserving each object's real-time order because linearization points of distinct objects are independent; the merged history linearizes the union. ∎

These theorems are the load-bearing walls of the two traditions: refinement lets TLA+ users verify big systems as a lattice of implications; locality lets Iris users verify big programs as a separating conjunction of per-object proofs.

### 5.4 Cost Comparison

| Dimension | TLA+ / TLC model checking | Iris / Coq deductive proof |
|---|---|---|
| Human effort | Days–weeks per model | Weeks–months per proof |
| Automation | Fully automatic (bounded) | Tactics + manual invariants |
| Completeness | Bounded by state space | Unbounded (inductive) |
| Compositionality | Weak (monolithic models) | Strong (frame rule, locality) |
| Trust base | TLC implementation | Coq kernel |
| Industrial evidence | AWS: 7 deep bugs [6] | RustBelt, OS kernels |

The complementarity is sharp: model checking buys automation at the price of boundedness; separation logic buys unbounded guarantees at the price of labor.

## 6 Limitations

Honesty requires stating what these methods cannot do.

1. **The model–reality gap.** TLC verifies the TLA+ model; Iris verifies the program against its operational semantics. Neither sees the compiler bug, the faulty hardware, or the environment assumption that was wrong. Newcombe et al. state this explicitly [6]: the specification must capture the significant aspects of the real system, and choosing them is an unmechanized human act.

2. **State explosion.** CTL model checking is linear in the model — but the model is exponential in the program. Abstraction is mandatory and unsound in general; a bug found in the abstract model may be spurious, and a clean abstract model may hide a concrete bug. Compositional and symmetry reductions help but do not repeal the exponential.

3. **Liveness and fairness.** Safety (invariants, refinement) is well supported; liveness is harder. TLC checks liveness only under fairness assumptions and bounded exploration; Iris-style logics handle termination and progress through prophecy variables and ghost obligations, but these are among the most labor-intensive parts of any proof [3].

4. **Weak memory.** Modern hardware and languages (C++11, Arm-A) do not provide sequential consistency. Extending separation logics to weak memory is active research — the AxSL project mechanizes a logic for Arm-A in Iris [3] — but the foundations are still settling, and TLA+ models of weak memory remain hand-built.

5. **Proof labor and expertise.** Iris proofs demand rare expertise; TLA+ demands mathematical maturity. The AWS experience shows engineers can learn TLA+ [6], but the learning curve is real, and proof-engineer productivity remains orders of magnitude below test-engineer productivity.

6. **Quantitative properties.** Neither tradition handles time, probability, or resource bounds natively. Probabilistic model checking (PRISM) and quantitative separation logics exist but are immature by comparison.

## 7 Conclusion

Fifty years after Dijkstra's mutual-exclusion solution, we possess *mechanical* answers to the concurrency correctness problem — plural, because no single method dominates. TLA gives us a logic in which systems are formulas and refinement is implication, with stuttering invariance making layered specifications compositional [1]; the TLC model checker turns those formulas into exhaustive, push-button exploration that has caught production-class bugs at AWS [6]. Separation logic gives us ownership as a first-class logical resource, the frame rule as the engine of local reasoning [4], and Iris as the unified, mechanized framework from which two decades of concurrent logics derive as theorems [2][3]. Model checking gives us the fixpoint algorithms that made temporal verification decidable and earned a Turing Award [7].

The thesis's central claim stands: every correct concurrent design admits a mechanical certificate — finite-state and automatic, or compositional and deductive — and the engineer's art is choosing which certificate to pursue. The frontier is the hybrid: proof assistants that discharge model-checking obligations, model checkers that suggest invariants to provers, and logics that span the specification-to-code continuum without a change of formalism. When that frontier closes, Dijkstra's terror — the program that fails on Tuesday — will finally be retired.
