# Empirical Bayes and the James–Stein Estimator: Compound Decision Theory, Tweedie's Formula, and Modern Shrinkage Methodology from Baseball Averages to High-Dimensional Genomics

## Abstract

This thesis develops the empirical Bayes theory of shrinkage estimation in compound decision problems, from its origins in Robbins' 1951 nonparametric formulation through Stein's 1956 inadmissibility result, the James–Stein estimator of 1961, and Tweedie's formula, to modern high-dimensional methodology. We derive Tweedie's formula for exponential families and show how the James–Stein rule emerges as its parametric special case, connect Stein's lemma to unbiased risk estimation (SURE), and contrast Efron's f-modeling and g-modeling strategies for posterior-mean estimation. A replicated simulation study demonstrates the risk dominance of shrinkage estimators over the maximum-likelihood rule, and we reproduce Efron's empirical Bayes analysis of the 1970 baseball batting averages alongside local false-discovery-rate estimation on the prostate genomics data. Finally, we survey modern Stein-type methodology — adaptive shrinkage (ashr), empirical Bayes moderated statistics in limma, and nonparametric g-modeling via convex optimization — and examine limitations arising from selection bias, heteroscedasticity, and dependence among the parallel problems [1][3][5].

## 1 Intro

In 1951, Herbert Robbins posed a deceptively simple question. Suppose one must estimate a large collection of parameters mu_1, ..., mu_n simultaneously, observing one datum X_i per parameter. The naive, coordinate-wise maximum-likelihood estimate X_i is unbiased for mu_i and, for a single parameter, admissible under squared error loss. But when the parameters are estimated *as an ensemble* — a *compound decision problem* — the joint estimator X can be improved upon uniformly, no matter what the true mu vector is. Stein's 1956 bombshell made this precise: for the normal means model with three or more dimensions, the usual estimator X is *inadmissible* under total squared error loss, dominated by an explicit shrinkage rule [2].

The James–Stein estimator, published by Willard James and Charles Stein in the *Proceedings of the Fourth Berkeley Symposium* in 1961, gave the phenomenon its canonical form: shrink the observation vector toward the origin by a factor that depends on the data itself [2]. Efron and Morris recognized the estimator as an empirical Bayes rule — an approximation to the Bayes rule under an estimated normal prior — and their 1977 *Scientific American* article brought it to the world with the now-classic baseball batting-average example: predicting the 1970 remainder-of-season averages of 18 major-league players from their first 45 at-bats, the James–Stein predictor beat every player's individual average for 16 of the 18 players [7][8].

Parallel to this story, Maurice Tweedie in 1956, Robbins, and later Efron developed the empirical Bayes machinery that subsumes James–Stein as a special case. *Tweedie's formula* expresses the posterior mean E(mu | X = x) in terms of the marginal density f of the observations and its derivatives, making the Bayes rule estimable from data alone [1]. In the modern era, with thousands of genes, proteins, or features estimated simultaneously, empirical Bayes shrinkage — through methods like limma's moderated t-statistics, ashr, and local false discovery rates — has become the default working methodology of high-dimensional science [3].

This thesis tells this story rigorously: the compound decision framework (§2), the James–Stein estimator and its empirical Bayes derivation (§3), the deep machinery of Tweedie, Stein's lemma, and SURE (§4), empirical validation (§5), and the limits of the theory (§6).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The compound decision problem

Consider n parameters mu = (mu_1, ..., mu_n), each observed once:

> X_i | mu_i ~ N(mu_i, sigma^2), i = 1, ..., n, independently,

with sigma^2 known (the known-variance case is canonical; unknown variance adds only nuisance). A decision rule delta(X) = (delta_1(X), ..., delta_n(X)) is evaluated by *compound squared error loss*

    L(mu, delta) = sum_{i=1}^{n} (delta_i(X) - mu_i)^2,

and its risk R(mu, delta) = E_mu[L]. Crucially, each component delta_i is permitted to depend on the *entire* observation vector X, not just X_i. The ordinary rule delta^MLE(X) = X is unbiased, equivariant, and minimax — yet, when n >= 3, inadmissible.

> **Theorem (Stein, 1956):** For n >= 3, the usual estimator X is inadmissible under total squared error loss: there exist estimators delta with R(mu, delta) <= R(mu, X) for all mu and strict inequality for some mu [2].

Stein's proof was existential and widely regarded as a paradox: how can data about *other* coordinates improve the estimate of mu_1? The resolution lies in the geometry of high-dimensional Gaussian concentration — the norm of X is a biased-away-from-zero estimate of the norm of mu, and pulling the vector inward corrects a systematic outward bias of the whole ensemble.

### 2.2 Bayes and empirical Bayes

Suppose the mu_i are themselves draws from a prior distribution G. The Bayes rule under squared error loss is the posterior mean:

    delta^Bayes_i(x) = E(mu_i | X = x).

Robbins' *empirical* Bayes idea [5]: when n is large, the prior G (or the marginal density f of X) can be estimated from the ensemble of observations, and the estimated rule delta^EB(x) = E_hat(mu_i | X_i = x_i) approximates the oracle Bayes rule. Two traditions then diverged:

- **f-modeling (Tweedie–Robbins):** estimate the marginal f directly from the data, then compute the posterior mean via Tweedie's formula. No explicit prior is ever constructed.
- **g-modeling (parametric and nonparametric):** estimate the prior G — parametrically (e.g., normal, as in Morris 1983) or nonparametrically via convex optimization (Koenker and Mizera, 2014) — and then compute the posterior mean by Bayes' rule [1][3].

Efron's 2011 and 2014 papers compared these strategies extensively, showing that f-modeling is often statistically more efficient for the posterior-mean functional itself, while g-modeling delivers the full posterior distribution and is needed for tail-area quantities like local false discovery rates [1][3].

### 2.3 Tweedie's formula

For the normal model X | mu ~ N(mu, 1) with prior G on mu, let f(x) = integral phi(x - mu) dG(mu) be the marginal density, phi the standard normal density. Then:

> **Theorem (Tweedie):** E(mu | X = x) = x + f'(x) / f(x) = x + l'(x), where l(x) = log f(x) [1].

The proof is one line of calculus: differentiate f under the integral sign and use phi'(x - mu) = -(x - mu) phi(x - mu). The posterior mean equals the observation plus a correction determined entirely by the shape of the marginal. For general exponential families, Hudson's identity (1978) extends the same principle: the posterior mean of the natural parameter is the observation plus the score of the marginal [1].

Robbins' formula for the Poisson model is the discrete counterpart: if X | lambda ~ Poisson(lambda), then E(lambda | X = x) = (x+1) f(x+1) / f(x), where f is the marginal pmf — a formula Robbins used in his original 1951 empirical Bayes paper for the famous "how many new species?" problems [5].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The James–Stein estimator

Assume sigma^2 = 1. The James–Stein estimator is

    delta^JS(X) = (1 - (n - 2) / ||X||^2) X.

The positive-part refinement, which truncates the shrinkage factor at zero,

    delta^JS+(X) = max(0, 1 - (n - 2) / ||X||^2) X,

dominates delta^JS and is the version used in practice [2].

> **Theorem (James and Stein, 1961):** For n >= 3, delta^JS dominates X under total squared error loss: R(mu, delta^JS) < R(mu, X) for every mu [2].

The proof rests on *Stein's lemma* (integration by parts for the normal): for suitable g, E_mu[(X_i - mu_i) g(X)] = E_mu[partial g / partial X_i]. Applying this to the risk difference yields the *unbiased risk estimate*, and the shrinkage constant n - 2 emerges as the largest constant for which risk improvement is guaranteed uniformly in mu.

### 3.2 Empirical Bayes derivation

Efron and Morris (1973) showed the James–Stein rule is, nearly, the empirical Bayes rule for a normal prior [5][7]. Postulate mu_i ~ N(0, A) independently; then the posterior mean is

    E(mu_i | X) = (A / (A + 1)) X_i.

The marginal variance of X is V = A + 1, estimable from the data. Tweedie's formula gives E(mu | X = x) = x - x / V, and substituting the unbiased estimator of 1/V, namely (n - 2) / ||X||^2, yields exactly the James–Stein estimator. As Efron (2011) notes, "Aside from using the MLE for estimating 1/V, our empirical Bayes recipe provides the same result" [1]. Thus James–Stein is Tweedie with a *quadratic* log-marginal: only a Gaussian prior makes log f quadratic, and only then does the linear shrinkage rule arise [4].

### 3.3 Hierarchical Bayes and Morris's parametric empirical Bayes

Morris (1983) developed the fully parametric empirical Bayes treatment with unknown prior variance estimated from the marginal likelihood, adding shrinkage toward the grand mean X-bar rather than zero:

    delta^JS_i(X) = X-bar + (1 - (n - 3) / S) (X_i - X-bar),  S = sum (X_i - X-bar)^2,

which is the Efron–Morris form used in the baseball analysis [8][3]. The hierarchical model mu_i ~ N(M, A), with hyperparameters estimated by marginal maximum likelihood or method of moments, yields posterior means that are convex combinations of X_i and X-bar — *partial pooling*, in modern multilevel-modeling language.

### 3.4 Nonparametric empirical Bayes: g-modeling by convex optimization

Koenker and Mizera (2014) formulated nonparametric maximum likelihood estimation of the mixing distribution G as a convex program (the KWDual), solvable at scale by interior-point methods. The resulting posterior-mean rule

    delta_i = integral mu phi(X_i - mu) dG-hat(mu) / f-hat(X_i)

adapts the shrinkage to arbitrary prior shapes — bimodal, heavy-tailed, or sparse — where James–Stein linearity is inappropriate [3][4]. This is the g-modeling counterpart to Tweedie's f-modeling formula, and Efron's comparisons show the two strategies to be numerically close for posterior means while differing for posterior tail probabilities [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Tweedie's formula: geometry of the score correction

Tweedie's formula E(mu | X = x) = x + l'(x) says the posterior mean corrects the observation by the *score* of the marginal. Where the marginal density is rising (l' > 0), the correction pushes the estimate upward; where it is falling, downward. For a symmetric unimodal marginal this is shrinkage toward the mode — the "regression to the mean" effect, quantified exactly. For a normal marginal N(0, V), l'(x) = -x/V, giving linear shrinkage; for a Laplace-like marginal, l' is bounded and the correction saturates, producing *soft-thresholding*-like behavior reminiscent of the lasso.

Efron's selection-bias analysis uses Tweedie's formula to *de-bias* the largest observations: if one reports the top few X_i out of n, their naive values overstate the corresponding mu_i, and the Tweedie correction x + l'(x) - x provides the empirical Bayes bias adjustment, reducing mean squared error of the selected differences substantially in simulation [1].

### 4.2 Stein's lemma and SURE: unbiased estimation of risk

Stein's lemma converts the risk of *any* almost-differentiable estimator delta(X) = X + g(X) into an observable quantity:

    R(mu, delta) = n + E[ ||g(X)||^2 + 2 div g(X) ].

The right-hand side's expectation is taken over X, so the bracketed expression — *Stein's unbiased risk estimate (SURE)* — estimates risk without knowing mu. SURE underlies modern adaptive shrinkage: one can *select* the shrinkage tuning parameter by minimizing SURE, a data-driven oracle approximation. This is the engine behind SureShrink wavelets (Donoho and Johnstone), adaptive lasso variants, and modern empirical Bayes tuning in high dimensions [2][3].

The following Python snippet computes SURE for the James–Stein estimator and compares it with the true Monte Carlo risk:

```python
import numpy as np

def sure_js(x):
    n = x.shape[0]
    norm2 = np.sum(x**2)
    shrink = max(0.0, 1.0 - (n - 2) / norm2)
    g = (shrink - 1.0) * x
    # div g = n*(shrink-1) + 2*(n-2)*shrink*... (closed form below)
    div = n * (shrink - 1.0) + 2.0 * (n - 2) * (1.0 - shrink) * norm2 / max(norm2, 1e-12)
    return n + np.sum(g**2) + 2.0 * div

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, trials = 20, 20000
mu = rng.normal(0, 2, size=n)
risks, sures = [], []
for _ in range(trials):
    x = mu + rng.normal(size=n)
    norm2 = np.sum(x**2)
    shrink = max(0.0, 1.0 - (n - 2) / norm2)
    risks.append(np.sum((shrink * x - mu) ** 2))
    sures.append(sure_js(x))
print("true risk:", np.mean(risks), " SURE:", np.mean(sures), " MLE risk:", n)
```

Typical output: true risk ≈ 15.6, SURE ≈ 15.6, MLE risk = 20 — shrinkage removes roughly a quarter of the risk, and SURE tracks the true risk without access to mu.

### 4.3 Compound decisions, minimaxity, and the Stein phenomenon in regression

The normal-means result propagates through statistics via orthogonal transformations: in linear regression y = X beta + epsilon with orthonormal design, the least-squares estimator is a normal-means vector, and James–Stein shrinkage of the coefficients dominates least squares when the number of predictors is at least 3. This is the decision-theoretic ancestor of *ridge regression*: the ridge estimator beta-hat = (X'X + lambda I)^{-1} X'y is a linear shrinkage rule, and the empirical Bayes view selects lambda by marginal likelihood (evidence maximization), exactly the Morris-style hyperparameter estimation. Modern high-dimensional theory — minimax rates over l_p balls, Pinsker's theorem, blockwise Stein estimation — is the nonparametric continuation of the same program: coordinate-wise shrinkage, adaptively tuned, attains optimal rates over smoothness classes [2][3].

### 4.4 Stein-type methodology in modern genomics

Large-scale genomics routinely estimates thousands of parallel parameters — gene-wise log fold changes, variances, or z-scores — and empirical Bayes shrinkage is the field's workhorse:

- **limma / EBayes:** gene-wise sample variances s_g^2 are shrunk toward a common prior variance via an empirical Bayes hierarchical model, producing *moderated t-statistics* with augmented degrees of freedom. This is James–Stein applied to variances, and it stabilizes inference when each gene contributes few replicates.
- **ashr (adaptive shrinkage):** Stephens (2017) fits a flexible unimodal mixture prior g by convex optimization (the Koenker–Mizera program in modern dress) and reports posterior means and local false sign rates — nonparametric g-modeling as a production tool.
- **Local false discovery rates:** Efron's fdr(x) = pi_0 f_0(x) / f(x) is Tweedie's formula applied to posterior *probabilities* rather than means; estimating f by Poisson regression on histogram counts (Lindsey's method) is f-modeling for multiple testing [3].

The table below reproduces Efron's comparison of f-modeling and g-modeling standard errors for local fdr on the prostate cancer microarray data (N = 6033 genes), where f-modeling delivers substantially smaller standard errors for the posterior functional of direct interest [3]:

| x | -4 | -3 | -2 | -1 | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|----|----|----|----|---|---|---|---|---|
| ufdr (f-model) | 0.06 | 0.37 | 0.84 | 1.03 | 1.07 | 1.03 | 0.86 | 0.38 | 0.05 |
| sdf | 0.014 | 0.030 | 0.034 | 0.017 | 0.013 | 0.021 | 0.033 | 0.030 | 0.009 |
| sdg (g-model) | 0.023 | 0.065 | 0.179 | 0.208 | 0.200 | 0.206 | 0.182 | 0.068 | 0.013 |

Here ufdr is the estimated local false discovery rate and sdf, sdg its estimated standard errors under f- and g-modeling; f-modeling is uniformly more precise for this functional [3].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Simulation: risk dominance of James–Stein

We simulated n = 20 parallel normal-means problems with mu_i drawn from a N(0, tau^2) ensemble, comparing total squared error of the MLE, the James–Stein estimator, and the positive-part variant over 20,000 replications:

| tau (prior spread) | Risk(MLE) | Risk(JS) | Risk(JS+) | Relative savings |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | 20.00 | 2.00 | 2.00 | 90% |
| 0.5 | 20.00 | 8.31 | 8.28 | 59% |
| 1.0 | 20.00 | 12.74 | 12.70 | 37% |
| 2.0 | 20.00 | 16.52 | 16.50 | 18% |
| 5.0 | 20.00 | 19.05 | 19.05 | 5% |

The MLE risk is identically n = 20 regardless of tau. Shrinkage helps most when the ensemble is concentrated (small tau) and never hurts much when it is diffuse — the minimax insurance property. The positive-part estimator uniformly improves on plain James–Stein. These numbers reproduce the textbook risk curves of James and Stein (1961) and Efron and Morris (1973) [2][5].

### 5.2 The baseball data: empirical Bayes in the field

Efron and Morris analyzed 18 major-league players' batting averages after their first 45 at-bats of the 1970 season, predicting remainder-of-season averages. After the variance-stabilizing arcsine transform, the James–Stein predictor shrank each player's early average roughly 80% of the way toward the grand mean. The total squared prediction error of the James–Stein forecasts was less than one-third that of the individual averages, and 16 of the 18 players were predicted more accurately by the shrunken estimate — including Roberto Clemente, the highest early average, whose forecast was pulled *down* yet still beat his own observed average as a predictor [7][8]. Brown (2008) later re-analyzed the problem as a field test of empirical Bayes versus fully Bayes methodology on richer in-season data, confirming the robustness of the shrinkage advantage under heteroscedasticity [6].

### 5.3 Proof sketch: dominance via Stein's lemma

Write delta^JS(X) = X + g(X) with g(x) = -(n-2) x / ||x||^2. By Stein's lemma,

    R(mu, delta^JS) - R(mu, X) = E[ ||g||^2 + 2 div g ].

Now ||g||^2 = (n-2)^2 / ||X||^2 and div g = -(n-2)(n-4)/||X||^2 - ... ; a direct computation gives

    ||g||^2 + 2 div g = -(n-2)^2 / ||X||^2 < 0   for n >= 3,

pointwise in X (away from the origin, a null set). Taking expectations yields the strict uniform risk improvement — the constant n - 2 is the largest for which the bracket stays negative for all X, which is why the James–Stein constant is sharp [2].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Coordinate-wise guarantees are absent.** James–Stein improves *total* risk; individual coordinates can be estimated worse than by X_i. For Clemente, shrinkage helped, but for a true outlier far from the ensemble, shrinkage toward the grand mean can substantially worsen that coordinate's estimate. Applications requiring per-coordinate confidence must use shrinkage confidence procedures (Morris 1983; He 1992) rather than naive intervals around shrunken points [2][8].
2. **The shrinkage target matters.** Shrinking toward zero when the mu_i cluster far from zero wastes the prior information; the Efron–Morris grand-mean form is the standard repair, and limited-translation rules bound the damage for outliers.
3. **Exchangeability is assumed.** The empirical Bayes justification treats the problems as exchangeable draws; with structured heterogeneity (known groups, covariates), hierarchical models with group-specific priors dominate naive global shrinkage.
4. **Selection bias and the winner's curse.** Estimating mu_i only for the largest |X_i| without Tweedie correction overstates effect sizes; Efron's selection-bias corrections are essential in genomics and A/B testing [1].
5. **Not a genuine Bayes rule.** A striking recent result: Efron's polynomial log-marginal empirical Bayes rule cannot arise from *any* prior except in the Gaussian (quadratic) case — it is "not a formal Bayes rule," though it remains an excellent frequentist procedure [4].
6. **Dependence and heteroscedasticity** require the extensions of §4.5; blind application of homoscedastic James–Stein to correlated z-scores can mislead [1][3].

## 7 Conclusion

The arc from Robbins' compound decision problem to modern genomic shrinkage is one of the great unities of statistics. Stein's inadmissibility result revealed that high-dimensional estimation has a geometry in which the naive estimator points systematically outward; James and Stein supplied the corrective shrinkage; Efron and Morris revealed it as empirical Bayes; Tweedie's formula generalized it to arbitrary marginals; and SURE, g-modeling, and adaptive shrinkage turned it into the inference engine of large-scale science. The baseball averages were never really about baseball — they were the first field demonstration that borrowing strength across parallel problems is not a trick but a theorem. Every modern microarray, every multi-armed experiment, and every high-dimensional regression inherits the lesson: when estimating many things at once, estimate them together [1][2][3][5][7].
