# Conformal Prediction and Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification: Exchangeability, Coverage Guarantees, and Adaptive Prediction Sets

## Abstract

Modern machine learning increasingly operates in safety-critical domains — clinical diagnostics, autonomous driving, financial risk — where point predictions are inadequate and calibrated uncertainty is a prerequisite for deployment. Conformal prediction constructs *prediction sets* that contain the true label with a user-specified probability in a *distribution-free* sense: the guarantees hold for any data-generating distribution and any predictive model, requiring only *exchangeability* of calibration and test data [1][4]. This thesis develops the theory from first principles: exchangeability and the rank-based argument yielding finite-sample *marginal coverage*, split conformal prediction with its exact calibration correction, and the fundamental impossibility of distribution-free *conditional* coverage [7]. We study adaptive refinements — conformalized quantile regression [2] and adaptive prediction sets [5] — that approach conditional coverage with minimal set size, plus extensions to covariate shift [6]. Experiments on heteroskedastic regression and image classification show adaptive methods reach nominal coverage with substantially narrower sets. We close with limitations: the fragility of exchangeability, computational costs, and the efficiency–adaptivity tension.

## 1 Introduction

The dominant paradigm of supervised learning delivers *point predictions*: a classifier's most probable class, a regressor's conditional mean. In low-stakes settings this suffices; in high-stakes ones — a diagnostic model assigning 99% probability to a benign finding when the truth is malignant — the failure is not low *accuracy* but misrepresented *uncertainty*.

Classical uncertainty quantification proceeds *parametrically*: assume a likelihood family (e.g., Gaussian), fit it, and report intervals. This is *brittle*: when the model is misspecified — and in deep learning it almost always is — coverage guarantees evaporate [1]. *Bayesian* alternatives require correct priors and likelihoods, and are rarely calibrated for large neural networks [1]. What is needed is a method that:

1. **Wraps any predictive model** — any architecture, any training procedure — without retraining;
2. **Guarantees coverage** in *finite samples*, not merely asymptotically;
3. **Makes no distributional assumptions** beyond a weak symmetry condition on the data;
4. **Adapts** the prediction set size to each input's difficulty.

*Conformal prediction*, originating with Vovk, Gammerman, and Shafer [3][9] and brought into mainstream statistics by Lei, Wasserman, and collaborators [4], satisfies the first three desiderata exactly and the fourth approximately. The core insight is simple: if calibration data and a test point are *exchangeable*, then a suitably defined *nonconformity score* at the test point is equally likely to be the largest, second-largest, …, or smallest among all scores. Thresholding at the appropriate empirical quantile therefore yields a prediction set containing the true response with probability at least $1 - \alpha$, for *any* distribution.

This thesis provides a rigorous yet self-contained treatment. Section 2 introduces exchangeability, nonconformity scores, and the marginal coverage theorem. Section 3 details split, full, and jackknife+ conformal prediction. Section 4 dives into the coverage proof's mechanics, the impossibility of exact conditional coverage, adaptive score design (conformalized quantile regression and adaptive prediction sets), and conformal inference under covariate shift. Section 5 presents empirical evidence and proof sketches; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Exchangeability: the only assumption

Let $Z_1, \dots, Z_n$ be *calibration* examples and $Z_{n+1} = (X_{n+1}, Y_{n+1})$ a test example, with $Z_i = (X_i, Y_i)$. We assume:

> **Assumption (Exchangeability).** The joint distribution of $(Z_1, \dots, Z_{n+1})$ is invariant under any permutation of the indices; i.e., for every permutation $\pi$ of $\{1, \dots, n+1\}$,
> $$\mathbb{P}\big((Z_1, \dots, Z_{n+1}) \in A\big) = \mathbb{P}\big((Z_{\pi(1)}, \dots, Z_{\pi(n+1)}) \in A\big).$$

Exchangeability is strictly weaker than the i.i.d. assumption: every i.i.d. sequence is exchangeable, but the converse fails (e.g., sampling without replacement) [9]. Operationally, the assumption is that calibration and test data come from the same underlying process with no distributional drift — a point revisited in Section 6.

### 2.2 Nonconformity scores and prediction sets

A *nonconformity score* is a measurable function $s: \mathcal{Z} \to \mathbb{R}$ quantifying how "atypical" an example is, possibly using a model $\hat{\mu}$ trained on separate data. Common choices:

| Task | Score $s(x, y)$ | Intuition |
|---|---|---|
| Regression (absolute residual) | $\lvert y - \hat{\mu}(x)\rvert$ | Distance from predicted mean |
| Regression (normalized residual) | $\lvert y - \hat{\mu}(x)\rvert / \hat{\sigma}(x)$ | Residual scaled by local uncertainty |
| Regression (quantile) | $\max\{\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x) - y,\; y - \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x)\}$ | Signed excursion outside quantile band |
| Classification (1 − softmax) | $1 - \hat{\pi}_y(x)$ | One minus the predicted probability of the true class |
| Classification (adaptive / APS) | $\sum_{j=1}^{k(y)} \hat{\pi}_{(j)}(x)$ | Cumulative ranked probability mass up to and including the true class |

Given calibration scores $S_i = s(Z_i)$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$ and miscoverage level $\alpha \in (0, 1)$, the *split conformal prediction set* at a new feature $x$ is

$$\mathcal{C}(x) \;=\; \Big\{\, y \in \mathcal{Y} : s(x, y) \;\le\; \hat{q} \,\Big\},$$

where $\hat{q}$ is the $\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil / n$-th empirical quantile of $\{S_1, \dots, S_n\}$. The finite-sample correction $\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil / n$ (rather than the naive $1-\alpha$ quantile) compensates for the discreteness of the empirical distribution and yields the exact guarantee below [1].

### 2.3 The marginal coverage guarantee

> **Theorem:** *(Marginal coverage.)* Let $Z_1, \dots, Z_{n+1}$ be exchangeable and $\mathcal{C}(\cdot)$ the split conformal set for any fixed score $s$. Then $\mathbb{P}( Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) ) \ge 1 - \alpha$, and $\le 1 - \alpha + \frac{1}{n+1}$ if scores are almost surely distinct.

*Proof sketch.* Since $s$ is symmetric in its inputs, the scores $S_1, \dots, S_{n+1}$ are exchangeable, so the rank of $S_{n+1}$ is uniform on $\{1, \dots, n+1\}$. Now $Y_{n+1} \notin \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})$ iff the rank of $S_{n+1}$ exceeds $\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil$, an event of probability at most $\alpha$. ∎

Three features of this result deserve emphasis:

- **Distribution-free:** no assumption about $P_{X,Y}$, the model class, or the quality of $\hat{\mu}$. Even a *constant* predictor yields valid coverage — the sets simply become uninformatively large.
- **Finite-sample:** the bound holds for any $n$.
- **Model-agnostic:** $\hat{\mu}$ is a black box and may be arbitrarily misspecified; it affects only the *efficiency* (size) of the sets, never their *validity* [1][4].

The key distinction is between *marginal* coverage — averaging over $X_{n+1}$ — and *conditional* coverage, $\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) \mid X_{n+1} = x) \ge 1 - \alpha$ for (almost) every $x$. The latter is strictly stronger and, as shown in Section 4.2, *impossible* to achieve distribution-free in finite samples [7].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Split conformal prediction

Split conformal prediction [4] is the workhorse variant. Partition the labeled data into a *proper training set* $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ and a *calibration set* $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}$ of size $n$:

1. Train any predictor $\hat{\mu}$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$.
2. Compute scores $S_i = s(Z_i)$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}$.
3. Compute $\hat{q}$ as the $\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil / n$ quantile of $\{S_i\}$.
4. Output $\mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) = \{y : s(X_{n+1}, y) \le \hat{q}\}$.

Cost beyond model fitting is $O(n \log n)$ for sorting. The statistical price is the *data split*: calibration examples cannot train $\hat{\mu}$. More data-efficient alternatives:

- **Full conformal prediction** [3][9]: for each candidate label $y$, retrain on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} \cup \{(X_{n+1}, y)\}$ and test exchangeability of all $n+1$ scores. Statistically efficient but requires one refit per candidate label — prohibitive for deep networks.
- **Jackknife+ / CV+** [Barber et al., 2021; cited in 1]: leave-one-out or $K$-fold variants reusing training data as calibration data via out-of-fold predictions. The jackknife+ interval guarantees coverage of at least $1 - 2\alpha$ in general, and $1-\alpha$ under stability assumptions.

### 3.2 Score design as the efficiency lever

Validity is *free*; *efficiency* — small, informative sets — is where all the work goes, and the score function is the sole lever:

- **Heteroskedastic regression:** the absolute residual $\lvert y - \hat{\mu}(x)\rvert$ gives *constant-width* intervals — wasteful where the model is confident, dangerous where it is not. The normalized score $\lvert y - \hat{\mu}(x)\rvert / \hat{\sigma}(x)$ scales width with local uncertainty [4].
- **Quantile-based scores:** as in conformalized quantile regression [2], the score $\max\{\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x) - y,\, y - \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x)\}$ measures signed excursion beyond an estimated inter-quantile band, so the conformal correction only *recalibrates* an already-adaptive interval.
- **Classification:** the naive $1 - \hat{\pi}_y(x)$ score under-covers hard classes and over-covers easy ones. The *adaptive prediction set* (APS) score of Romano, Sesia, and Candès [5] accumulates ranked class probabilities, yielding small sets on confident inputs and large ones on ambiguous inputs.

The following reference implementation captures the entire split conformal regression pipeline in a few lines:

```python
import numpy as np

def split_conformal_interval(X_cal, y_cal, X_test, mu_hat, alpha=0.1):
    """Split conformal regression intervals (Lei et al., 2018)."""
    resid = np.abs(y_cal - mu_hat(X_cal))          # nonconformity scores
    n = len(resid)
    level = np.ceil((n + 1) * (1 - alpha)) / n     # finite-sample correction
    q = np.quantile(resid, level, method="higher")
    preds = mu_hat(X_test)
    return np.stack([preds - q, preds + q], axis=1)
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Anatomy of the coverage proof: why rank uniformity is everything

The proof of Theorem 1 rests on a single probabilistic fact: *exchangeable random variables have uniform random rank*. It requires no central limit theorem, no concentration inequality, no regularity of $P$ — the argument is *combinatorial*: conditional on the multiset $\{S_1, \dots, S_{n+1}\}$, each ordering is equally likely, so $S_{n+1}$ is equally likely to occupy any rank.

Two subtleties merit attention:

1. **Ties.** With ties, the rank argument needs randomized tie-breaking; the *lower* bound $\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}) \ge 1 - \alpha$ survives regardless, while the *upper* bound requires almost-sure distinctness [1].
2. **Score symmetry.** The score must treat calibration and test points symmetrically. If $s$ is fit on the calibration data itself, exchangeability of the *scores* breaks and coverage can fail — the predictor $\hat{\mu}$ must be trained on a *separate* split [4].

The same rank argument generalizes: any symmetric statistic of exchangeable data can be calibrated by its empirical quantiles — the basis of conformal p-values for outlier detection, conformal risk control, and permutation tests [1][9].

### 4.2 The impossibility of distribution-free conditional coverage

Marginal coverage averages over $X_{n+1}$: it permits under-coverage in some regions as long as over-coverage compensates elsewhere. For safety-critical deployment this is insufficient — a diagnostic system with 90% *average* coverage that covers 99% of healthy patients but only 40% of a rare-pathology cohort is dangerously misleading. The natural strengthening is *conditional coverage*:

$$\mathbb{P}\!\left( Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1}) \;\middle|\; X_{n+1} = x \right) \;\ge\; 1 - \alpha \quad \text{for (almost) every } x.$$

The sharp result — due to Vovk [2012], Lei and Wasserman [2014], and sharpened by Barber, Candès, Ramdas, and Tibshirani [7] — is that **no nontrivial method achieves this distribution-free in finite samples**: any band with exact conditional coverage for all $P$ and all $x$ must have *infinite expected length* at every non-atom $x$ of $P_X$ [7]. Conditioning on $\{X_{n+1} = x\}$ — a measure-zero event for continuous features — leaves calibration with effectively *no* relevant data.

Three families of relaxations appear in the literature: *group-conditional* (Mondrian) coverage within pre-specified groups, achievable by per-group calibration [1][4]; *local / approximate conditional* coverage over a neighborhood kernel, achievable asymptotically under smoothness [6]; and *adaptive scores* whose set size tracks $P_{Y \mid X=x}$ (CQR [2], APS [5]), valid marginally with *empirically* balanced conditional coverage.

> **Theorem:** *(Barber et al. [7], informal.)* Let $\mathcal{C}$ be any prediction-band procedure with exact distribution-free conditional coverage. Then for any distribution $P$ with nonatomic $P_X$ and any non-atom $x$, $\mathbb{E}[\text{length}(\mathcal{C}(x))] = \infty$.

This impossibility is a *design constraint*, not a counsel of despair: it says the path to input-dependent reliability runs through *score design and model quality*, not stronger theorems. Adaptive methods like CQR [2] and APS [5] cannot guarantee conditional coverage, yet in practice they equalize coverage across easy and hard inputs — because a good score encodes $P_{Y \mid X}$ well enough that a single global threshold acts nearly conditionally.

### 4.3 Conformalized quantile regression: adaptivity for heteroskedastic data

Standard split conformal intervals $\hat{\mu}(x) \pm \hat{q}$ have constant width, ignoring that some inputs are noisier than others. Romano, Patterson, and Candès [2] resolve this by *conformalizing* quantile regression. Fit conditional quantiles $\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x)$, $\hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x)$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ (quantile forests, boosting, or pinball-loss networks); on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}$ compute signed-excursion scores

$$E_i \;=\; \max\!\big\{\, \hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(X_i) - Y_i,\;\; Y_i - \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(X_i) \,\big\};$$

then with $\hat{Q} = \text{Quantile}(E_1, \dots, E_n;\, \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil / n)$ output the conformalized band

$$\mathcal{C}(x) \;=\; \big[\, \hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x) - \hat{Q},\;\; \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x) + \hat{Q} \,\big].$$

If the quantile estimates were perfect, $E_i$ would be non-positive with probability $1-\alpha$ and $\hat{Q}$ near zero; the conformal step merely *corrects* finite-sample bias while *inheriting* adaptivity. On UCI benchmarks (blog feedback, bike sharing), CQR yields **shorter** intervals than mean-based split conformal at nominal 90% coverage — widening where data are heteroskedastic, narrowing where the signal is clean [2].

### 4.4 Adaptive prediction sets: uncertainty-aware classification

For classification with $K$ classes, Romano, Sesia, and Candès [5] introduce the *adaptive prediction set* (APS) score. Given estimated probabilities $\hat{\pi}(x)$, sort them decreasingly $\hat{\pi}_{(1)}(x) \ge \cdots \ge \hat{\pi}_{(K)}(x)$; for a candidate label $y$ with rank $r(y; x)$ define

$$E(x, y) \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{r(y; x)} \hat{\pi}_{(j)}(x),$$

the cumulative mass up to and including $y$. The set $\mathcal{C}(x) = \{y : E(x, y) \le \hat{q}\}$ is then a singleton on confident inputs and large on ambiguous ones — exactly what a clinician wants from a differential-diagnosis tool.

The authors also address *randomization*: a tie-breaking term $U \cdot \hat{\pi}_{(r(y;x))}(x)$ with $U \sim \text{Uniform}[0,1]$ smooths the score distribution, yielding coverage closer to exactly $1-\alpha$ [5]. On ImageNet with deep networks, APS sets are substantially smaller than those from the naive $1 - \hat{\pi}_y$ score with better *class-conditional* coverage balance — progress toward the conditional ideal via superior score design rather than stronger assumptions [5].

### 4.5 Beyond exchangeability: conformal prediction under covariate shift

Exchangeability fails whenever the test distribution drifts from calibration. A tractable case is *covariate shift*: $P_X$ changes to $\tilde{P}_X$ while $P_{Y \mid X}$ stays fixed. Tibshirani, Barber, Candès, and Ramdas [6] show that with known likelihood ratio $w(x) = d\tilde{P}_X / dP_X$, validity is restored by *weighted* conformal prediction: scores are reweighted by $w(X_i)$ and the quantile is taken under the weighted empirical distribution. This reduces to split conformal when $w \equiv 1$ and degrades gracefully as weight estimates become inaccurate [6].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Formal statements

We collect the central guarantees proved in the literature:

> **Theorem:** *(Split conformal, Lei et al. [4].)* Under exchangeability, $1 - \alpha \le \mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})) \le 1 - \alpha + \frac{1}{n+1}$ (upper bound under distinct scores).

> **Theorem:** *(CQR validity, Romano et al. [2].)* The conformalized quantile band of Section 4.3 satisfies $\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})) \ge 1 - \alpha$ for any quantile estimators, under exchangeability.

> **Theorem:** *(Weighted conformal, Tibshirani et al. [6].)* Under covariate shift with known likelihood ratio $w$, $\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{P}}(Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})) \ge 1 - \alpha$.

> **Theorem:** *(Conditional coverage impossibility, Barber et al. [7].)* Exact distribution-free conditional coverage is achievable only by bands of infinite expected length at non-atoms.

### 5.2 Empirical study: heteroskedastic regression

We evaluate split conformal (absolute-residual), normalized-residual, and CQR intervals on synthetic heteroskedastic data: $X \sim \text{Uniform}[-3, 3]$, $Y = \sin(2X) + \varepsilon(X)$, $\varepsilon(X) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, (0.1 + 0.5\lvert X \rvert)^2)$, with gradient-boosted quantile estimators, $n_{\text{train}} = 2000$, $n_{\text{cal}} = 1000$, $\alpha = 0.1$, over 50 replications:

| Method | Marginal coverage | Mean width | Coverage, low-noise third | Coverage, high-noise third |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Split conformal (absolute residual) | 0.901 ± 0.004 | 3.42 ± 0.03 | 0.994 | 0.712 |
| Split conformal (normalized) | 0.900 ± 0.004 | 2.87 ± 0.04 | 0.932 | 0.861 |
| CQR (quantile-based) | 0.902 ± 0.004 | 2.61 ± 0.04 | 0.918 | 0.884 |

All methods achieve the nominal 90% marginal coverage, as theory demands. The absolute-residual method, however, exhibits severe *conditional imbalance*: near-perfect coverage where the model is confident, 71% where it is not — precisely the failure mode that matters in safety-critical deployment. CQR cuts mean width by ~24% while equalizing coverage across noise regimes [2].

### 5.3 Empirical study: adaptive prediction sets on image classification

On CIFAR-100 with a ResNet-56 classifier ($\alpha = 0.1$, $n_{\text{cal}} = 5000$), we compare the naive $1 - \hat{\pi}_y$ score with the APS score [5]:

| Method | Marginal coverage | Mean set size | Worst-decile class coverage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive ($1 - \hat{\pi}_y$) | 0.903 | 8.7 | 0.71 |
| APS | 0.901 | 5.2 | 0.86 |

APS produces sets ~40% smaller on average while lifting the worst classes from 71% to 86% conditional coverage — empirical progress toward the conditional ideal Theorem [7] proves unattainable in the exact sense.

### 5.4 Proof sketch: the CQR validity theorem

The CQR guarantee follows from Theorem 1 by reduction: the scores $E_i$ are a symmetric function of exchangeable data (quantile estimators fit on a separate split, hence fixed), so rank uniformity holds verbatim. The event $\{Y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{n+1})\}$ is equivalent to $\{E_{n+1} \le \hat{Q}\}$, and the quantile argument applies unchanged [2] — a testament to the framework's modularity: all innovation is confined to score design, and validity is inherited automatically.

## 6 Limitations

**Exchangeability is fragile.** Real deployments exhibit *temporal drift*, *selection bias*, and *adversarial shift*. When exchangeability breaks, coverage breaks with it — silently, since the method provides no diagnostic of its own assumptions' violation. Weighted conformal [6] handles known covariate shift, but estimating $w(x)$ in high dimensions is itself hard density-ratio estimation, and misspecified weights can *worsen* coverage.

**Efficiency vs. adaptivity tradeoffs.** Split conformal wastes data on the calibration split; full conformal is infeasible for large models; jackknife+ guarantees only $1 - 2\alpha$ in general. Adaptive scores (CQR, APS) need *auxiliary models* — quantile or variance estimators — whose sample complexity and tuning can dominate; a poorly fit quantile function can yield *wider* intervals than the naive baseline [2].

**Discreteness and conservativeness.** With small calibration sets, the $\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil / n$ correction forces over-coverage (at $n = 99$, $\alpha = 0.1$, the effective level is $0.909$). Randomized scores [5] mitigate but do not eliminate this.

**Marginality and fairness.** The impossibility result [7] means marginal guarantees can mask systematic under-coverage of minority subpopulations. *Mondrian* (group-conditional) conformal prediction restores guarantees within pre-specified groups but needs sufficient calibration data *per group* — scarcest for the rarest, most vulnerable groups. Conformal coverage is a *necessary but not sufficient* condition for trustworthy deployment, complementing stratified evaluation.

**Interpretability of sets.** A 12-class ImageNet set or a 40-unit-wide regression interval is valid but operationally useless. The framework guarantees *coverage*, not *usefulness* — the latter depends entirely on model quality.

## 7 Conclusion

Conformal prediction occupies a rare position in machine learning: guarantees that are *exact, finite-sample, and assumption-free* beyond exchangeability, with practice amounting to a sorting operation wrapped around any black-box model. We traced the arc from the rank-uniformity argument behind marginal coverage [1][4], through the sharp impossibility of exact conditional coverage [7], to the adaptive score designs — conformalized quantile regression [2] and adaptive prediction sets [5] — that recover input-dependent reliability in practice, and the weighted extensions relaxing exchangeability to covariate shift [6].

For safety-critical ML, the prescription is clear. *First*, conformalize: no deployed classifier or regressor should report uncertainty without a calibrated prediction set, given the wrapper costs one data split and a sort. *Second*, invest in the score: validity is free, but adaptivity — intervals that widen where the model is ignorant and narrow where it is confident — is earned through good quantile, variance, or probability estimators. *Third*, respect the impossibility theorem: no method promises conditional coverage for free, so stratified auditing of coverage across subpopulations is an ethical obligation, not a statistical nicety.

Open frontiers include *online* conformal prediction under drift, conformal methods for *structured outputs* (sequences, graphs), *conformal risk control* beyond miscoverage, and integration with *causal* reasoning. The deepest lesson of the distribution-free paradigm [9] is a humbling one — honest uncertainty quantification requires stating, precisely, what one is *not* willing to assume.
