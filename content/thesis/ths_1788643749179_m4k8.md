# Full-Waveform Inversion in Exploration Seismology: Adjoint-State Gradients, Multiscale Frequency Continuation, and Exascale Elastic Imaging

## Abstract

Full-waveform inversion (FWI) reconstructs subsurface material properties by minimizing the misfit between recorded seismic data and synthetic wavefields computed from a numerical model of the full wave equation. This thesis develops the mathematical foundations of modern FWI for exploration seismology, centered on the **adjoint-state method** for computing the data-misfit gradient at the cost of only two wave simulations per source [1][3], and on **multiscale frequency-continuation strategies** that mitigate the non-convexity and cycle skipping that plague naive least-squares formulations [4][5]. We derive the adjoint gradient for acoustic and isotropic elastic media, analyze how the choice of misfit functional — *L2* waveform, correlation-based, and traveltime-driven objectives [2][6][7] — shapes the basin of attraction of the global minimum, and quantify the wavenumber illumination delivered by successive frequency bands. We further address the exascale computational burden of 3D elastic FWI, comparing time-domain and frequency-domain solver architectures, memory-versus-recomputation trade-offs, and the emergence of matrix-free, GPU-accelerated frameworks. Benchmarks on the Marmousi model and field-data case studies establish both the resolving power and the limits of current practice.

## 1 Introduction

For most of the twentieth century, seismic imaging proceeded by *reduction*: raw traces were muted, deconvolved, stacked, and migrated, each processing step discarding some fraction of the recorded wavefield in exchange for a tractable linear inverse problem. Traveltime tomography used only arrival times; migration used only single-scattered reflection amplitudes. Full-waveform inversion (FWI) rejects this philosophy. It asserts that *all* of the recorded wavefield — direct arrivals, reflections, refractions, multiples, diffractions, converted phases — carries information about the medium, and that the proper way to extract it is to solve the nonlinear least-squares problem

$$\min_{m} \mathcal{J}(m) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=1}^{N_s} \big\lVert P\,u_s(m) - d_s \big\rVert_2^2 + \mathcal{R}(m),$$

where $m$ is the discretized model, $u_s(m)$ the wavefield of shot $s$ satisfying a discrete wave equation $A(m)u_s = f_s$, $P$ the receiver sampling operator, $d_s$ the observed data, and $\mathcal{R}$ a regularization term.

The intellectual lineage is compact but deep. **Tarantola (1984)** [1] formulated seismic inversion as a generalized nonlinear least-squares problem, deriving the Fréchet derivative of the acoustic wave operator via the *adjoint-state method*. **Lailly (1983)** [9] independently constructed the same machinery from prestack migration, showing that the first FWI iteration is formally a reverse-time migration. The method then stalled for a decade: repeated 3D wave simulations were prohibitive, and *cycle skipping* defeated naive applications. The renaissance began with **Pratt (1990, 1999)** [2], who recast FWI in the frequency domain, and **Bunks et al. (1995)** [4], who showed that inverting low frequencies first keeps the optimizer inside the basin of attraction. From roughly 2008, 3D field-data FWI — Valhall, the Gulf of Mexico — proved industrial viability [10].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the forward problem and the failure modes of linearized inversion. Section 3 derives the adjoint-state gradient and Hessian structure. Section 4 is the deep dive: the adjoint-state machine, cycle skipping, multiscale continuation, elastic multiparameter inversion, and exascale solver design. Section 5 presents empirical evidence and formal results; Section 6 confronts limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The forward problem

In the frequency domain, the acoustic wave equation with $m(x) = 1/c^2(x)$ (squared slowness) reads

$$A(m,\omega)\,u = -\omega^2 m\,u - \nabla^2 u = f,$$

with absorbing boundaries implemented by perfectly matched layers (PML). For elastic media, the first-order velocity–stress system is preferred:

$$\rho\,\partial_t v = \nabla \cdot \sigma + f, \qquad \partial_t \sigma = C(m) : \varepsilon(v),$$

with particle velocity $v$, stress $\sigma$, density $\rho$, stiffness tensor $C$, and strain rate $\varepsilon$. Forward solvers fall into four families (Table 1).

| Solver family | Domain | Complexity per shot | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Explicit finite difference (FD) | Time | $\mathcal{O}(N_x N_t)$, memory-light with checkpointing | Simple, massively parallel, elastic-friendly | CFL-limited, numerical dispersion, boundary storage |
| Pseudospectral / spectral element | Time | $\mathcal{O}(N_x \log N_x \cdot N_t)$ | Near-exact dispersion, complex topography | Costly per step, meshing complexity |
| Direct sparse LU (multifrontal) | Frequency | $\mathcal{O}(N_x^{3/2})$ 2D / $\mathcal{O}(N_x^2)$ 3D factor | Exact, trivial multisource solves | Memory-prohibitive in 3D; one factorization per $\omega$ |
| Iterative (Krylov + preconditioner) | Frequency | $\mathcal{O}(N_x \cdot N_{\text{iter}})$ | Multiscale-friendly, low memory | Preconditioning Helmholtz at high $k$ is hard |

> **Theorem (Virieux & Operto, 2009):** A scattering event at frequency $\omega$ and angle $\theta$ illuminates model wavenumber $k = 2(\omega/c)\sin(\theta/2)$ [3]. Diving waves ($\theta \to 0$) recover long wavelengths; reflections ($\theta \to \pi$) recover short wavelengths — the *tomographic vs. migration* dichotomy.

### 2.2 Why linearization fails

Born (single-scattering) linearization replaces $u(m_0 + \delta m) \approx u(m_0) + (\partial u/\partial m)\,\delta m$, valid only when the accumulated phase error is small compared to the dominant period. In exploration geometries with 10–20 km offsets, a 2% velocity error accumulates many wavelengths of phase shift, and the linearized update points *away* from the true model. FWI is therefore a genuinely nonlinear optimization requiring dozens of iterations, each demanding forward and adjoint wave simulations.

### 2.3 Optimization landscape

FWI is attacked with *gradient-based* local optimizers:

1. **Steepest descent** — robust but slow given the Hessian's extreme eigenvalue spread.
2. **Nonlinear conjugate gradient (NLCG)** — modest improvement, no Hessian storage.
3. **L-BFGS** — the industry workhorse; builds a low-rank inverse-Hessian approximation from gradient history [11].
4. **Gauss–Newton / truncated Newton** — fewer iterations, but each needs Hessian–vector products (an extra forward+adjoint pair per CG step).

All four converge to the *nearest* stationary point. Without a good starting model, that point is a cycle-skipped local minimum.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The adjoint-state gradient

Consider the Lagrangian of the constrained problem $\min_{m,u} \mathcal{J}(u,m)$ subject to $A(m)u = f$:

$$\mathcal{L}(m, u, v) = \frac{1}{2}\lVert Pu - d \rVert^2 + \langle v,\, A(m)u - f \rangle.$$

Stationarity in $u$ gives the **adjoint equation** $A(m)^T v = -P^T(Pu - d)$: the data residual, injected at the receivers, is propagated *backward* through the transpose forward operator [1][12]. Stationarity in $m$ yields the gradient for one forward plus one adjoint simulation:

$$\nabla_m \mathcal{J} \;=\; \Re \sum_{\omega} \omega^2 \sum_{s=1}^{N_s} u_s(x,\omega)\,\overline{v_s(x,\omega)},$$

the zero-lag cross-correlation of forward wavefield $u_s$ with adjoint wavefield $v_s$. **This is the central computational fact of FWI**: the gradient with respect to millions of parameters costs no more than two wave simulations per shot — the same miracle that makes backpropagation cheap in deep learning [13].

### 3.2 The Hessian and its approximations

The full Hessian $\nabla^2 \mathcal{J} = J^TJ + \sum_i r_i \nabla^2 r_i$ has a positive-semidefinite Gauss–Newton term $J^TJ$ plus a residual-weighted second-order term. Dropping the latter gives Gauss–Newton; approximating its inverse from gradient history gives L-BFGS. A cheaper preconditioner is the *pseudo-Hessian* — the diagonal of $J^TJ$ approximated by squared forward-wavefield energy, correcting geometric spreading with depth [14].

### 3.3 Reference implementation (acoustic, 2D)

The following Python sketch captures the complete algorithm: finite-difference forward modeling, adjoint backpropagation of residuals, cross-correlation imaging condition, gradient descent.

```python
import numpy as np

def forward(m, f_src, dx, dt, nt, damp):
    """2nd-order acoustic FD: A(m) u = f."""
    nz, nx = m.shape
    u = np.zeros((nz, nx)); um = np.zeros((nz, nx))
    c2 = 1.0 / m
    snap = np.zeros((nt, nz, nx))
    for n in range(nt):
        lap = (np.roll(u, 1, 0) + np.roll(u, -1, 0)
             + np.roll(u, 1, 1) + np.roll(u, -1, 1) - 4*u) / dx**2
        up = (2*u - um + dt**2 * c2 * (lap + f_src[n])) * damp
        um, u = u, up
        snap[n] = u
    return snap

def fwi_gradient(m, dobs, src_pos, rec_pos, dx, dt, nt, damp):
    """Adjoint-state gradient: one forward + one adjoint solve."""
    u = forward(m, inject(ricker(nt, dt), src_pos), dx, dt, nt, damp)
    resid = sample(u, rec_pos) - dobs
    v = forward(m, inject(resid[::-1], rec_pos), dx, dt, nt, damp)  # backpropagate
    grad = np.zeros_like(m)
    for n in range(nt):
        grad += u[n] * v[nt-1-n]      # zero-lag cross-correlation
    return grad * dt**2
```

Each call to `fwi_gradient` performs two wave simulations regardless of model size — the adjoint-state dividend.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Adjoint-State Machine: Gradient Computation at the Cost of Two Simulations

Everything else in FWI is scaffolding around the adjoint-state identity. Direct gradient computation would require the Fréchet derivative $\partial u_s/\partial m_j$ for every model parameter — one additional wave simulation *per parameter*, or $\sim 10^7$ simulations for a modest 3D model. The adjoint method collapses this to two [12].

Physically, the adjoint wavefield is a *time-reversed experiment*: residuals recorded at the receivers are re-emitted into the medium and propagate backward. Where the forward and adjoint wavefields coincide with high amplitude, the model must change to explain the residual. The cross-correlation imaging condition is therefore not arbitrary — it is the exact gradient of the *L2* misfit via the Lagrangian argument of Section 3.

Two subtleties deserve emphasis:

- **Source-wavelet dependence.** The gradient scales with the source spectrum; wavelet errors map into update amplitude errors. Joint wavelet–model inversion is the active remedy [15].
- **Gradient versus update direction.** The raw gradient is dominated by high-wavenumber components because reflections dominate the residual. Approximate-inverse-Hessian preconditioning rebalances the tomographic (low-wavenumber) components that fix cycle skipping [14].

> **Theorem (Plessix, 2006):** For any differentiable misfit functional $\mathcal{J}(u(m))$, the gradient $\nabla_m \mathcal{J}$ can be obtained from one forward and one adjoint solve, where the adjoint source is $\partial \mathcal{J}/\partial u$ evaluated at the current model [12]. The cost is independent of the dimension of $m$.

### 4.2 Cycle Skipping and the Local-Minimum Landscape

Cycle skipping is the defining pathology of FWI. If predicted and observed waveforms differ by more than half a period ($|\Delta t| > T/2$), the *L2* misfit decreases when the prediction is shifted *away* from the truth by a full cycle. The basin of attraction of the global minimum thus has radius on the order of half the dominant wavelength — demanding a starting model within a few percent of the true long-wavelength velocity [4].

Three classes of remedy exist:

1. **Start low, go slow (multiscale).** Invert low frequencies first; their longer wavelengths widen the basin of attraction (Section 4.3).
2. **Change the misfit.** Replace sample-by-sample *L2* comparison with *kinematic* functionals:
   - *Traveltime / cross-correlation* misfits [6] compare phase shifts rather than amplitudes.
   - *Adaptive waveform inversion* (AWI; Warner & Guasch, 2016) [7] applies a Wiener filter matching predicted to observed traces and penalizes its deviation from identity — convex over a far wider range than *L2*.
   - *Optimal-transport* (Wasserstein) misfits measure the "work" to morph one seismogram into another and are provably convex with respect to time shifts [16].
3. **Change the physics.** *Wave-equation migration velocity analysis* (WEMVA) and *reflection FWI* exploit the tomographic component directly [17].

The sobering truth: no misfit in routine use eliminates cycle skipping entirely for reflection-dominated data without low frequencies. AWI and optimal transport enlarge the basin substantially, but the starting-model problem remains the central practical bottleneck [7][16].

### 4.3 Multiscale Frequency Continuation: From Time Windows to Octave Strategies

The multiscale principle — *solve a coarse problem first, then refine* — is implemented differently in the two domains:

- **Time domain (Bunks et al., 1995)** [4]: successively invert low-pass-filtered data with increasing cutoffs, each stage initializing the next.
- **Frequency domain (Pratt, 1999; Sirgue & Pratt, 2004)** [2][5]: invert a few discrete frequencies, doubling (one per octave) from low to high. Sirgue and Pratt showed a *single* well-chosen frequency per octave preserves the convergence path while slashing cost, since adjacent frequencies carry redundant wavenumber information.

The wavenumber argument makes this quantitative. At frequency $\omega$, velocity $c$, and maximum scattering angle $\theta_{\max}$, inversion recovers wavenumbers up to $k_{\max} = 2(\omega/c)\sin(\theta_{\max}/2)$ [3]. Low frequencies constrain only the smooth background; each octave adds an annulus of higher wavenumbers. *Cycle skipping is avoided iff the background recovered at stage $k$ predicts stage $k+1$'s data to within half a period.*

Practical schedules also employ **offset continuation** (near offsets before far) and **time windowing**: early-arrival (diving-wave) tomography first, since transmitted waves are far less cycle-skipped than reflections [18].

### 4.4 Elastic Inversion, Parameter Crosstalk, and Radiation Patterns

The Earth is elastic, and acoustic FWI applied to elastic data leaves artifacts: P-to-S conversions are treated as noise, and the inverted "velocity" is a mongrel of $V_p$, $V_s$, and density effects. Elastic FWI inverts the velocity–stress system for multiple parameter classes — typically $(V_p, V_s, \rho)$ or $(\lambda, \mu, \rho)$ — but immediately confronts **crosstalk**: the gradient for one parameter class leaks into another because their radiation patterns overlap [19].

| Parameter | Radiation pattern | Coupling risk |
|---|---|---|
| $V_p$ | Isotropic, monopole-like | Low — best resolved |
| $V_s$ | Dipole / quadrupole | High with density at short offsets |
| $\rho$ | Wide-angle backscattering | Severe — often fixed or scaled |

Mitigation strategies include hierarchical inversion ($V_p$ first from diving waves, then $V_s$, then $\rho$), radiation-pattern-based parameter scaling, and reparameterization into better-decoupled classes such as impedance and Poisson's ratio [19][20]. With multicomponent data and careful preconditioning, elastic FWI recovers $V_s$ structure invisible to acoustic inversion — at roughly an order of magnitude above acoustic cost [20].

> **Theorem (Tarantola, 1986):** In multiparameter elastic inversion, the Hessian's off-diagonal blocks encode inter-parameter coupling; neglecting them (as diagonal preconditioners do) is justified only when radiation patterns are orthogonal over the acquisition aperture — a condition rarely met in surface seismic geometries [19].

### 4.5 Exascale Solvers: Time vs. Frequency Domain at Industrial Scale

A production 3D elastic FWI is among the most demanding computations in applied science: a 20 × 20 × 8 km survey at 25 m discretization gives $\sim 2 \times 10^8$ grid points, $\sim 10^4$ shots, $\sim 10^4$ time steps — roughly $10^{16}$–$10^{17}$ FLOPs per iteration. The adjoint method *requires* the forward wavefield at every time step during backpropagation, naively demanding petabytes of storage [3].

The engineering responses define the modern solver landscape:

1. **Checkpointing (optimal revolve).** Store the forward field at sparse time steps; recompute segments during backpropagation — logarithmic extra cost for orders-of-magnitude memory savings [21].
2. **Boundary reconstruction.** Store only boundary values and re-simulate the interior backward; exact for lossless media [22].
3. **Source encoding.** Replace $N_s$ shots by a few *super-shots* with random phase encoding — cost nearly shot-count independent, at the price of crosstalk noise [23].
4. **Iterative Helmholtz solvers.** One solve per frequency per iteration with block Krylov multisource efficiency — but robust 3D Helmholtz preconditioners at exploration frequencies remain elusive [24].
5. **Matrix-free GPU frameworks.** Compilers like Devito generate optimized stencils from symbolic PDEs, reaching near-roofline GPU performance [25].

The time domain has largely won at industrial scale: explicit FD maps perfectly onto GPUs, checkpointing solves the storage problem, and source encoding tames the shot count. The frequency domain retains its niche in 2D and in multiscale workflows where a handful of low frequencies suffice [3][5].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 The Marmousi benchmark

The Marmousi synthetic (Versteeg, 1994) — a 9.2 × 3 km structurally complex model with growth faults and a high-velocity salt wedge — remains the canonical FWI test. Starting from a 1D gradient model:

- **Single-scale L2 FWI** converges to a cycle-skipped local minimum; the salt body is mispositioned by hundreds of meters [4].
- **Multiscale FWI** (Bunks schedule: 5 → 10 → 20 Hz) recovers velocity to within 2–3% RMS error, with fault resolution approaching the $\lambda/2$ diffraction limit [3][4].
- **Octave strategy** (Sirgue & Pratt: 3, 6, 12 Hz) matches that accuracy with roughly one-third the wave simulations of dense time-domain filtering [5].

### 5.2 Field-data milestones

- **Valhall (North Sea, 2008–2009):** 3D FWI on wide-azimuth ocean-bottom data imaged shallow gas channels invisible to traveltime tomography — the first unambiguous industrial demonstration [10].
- **Gulf of Mexico subsalt:** Elastic/anisotropic (TTI) FWI now routinely images beneath salt where ray-based methods fail, directly impacting drilling decisions [8].

### 5.3 A formal convergence sketch

> **Theorem (informal, after Bunks et al. 1995; Sirgue & Pratt 2004):** If the starting model predicts traveltimes to within $1/(2f_1)$ at the lowest frequency $f_1$, and the schedule satisfies $\omega_{k+1} \le 2\omega_k$ with each stage's minimizer initializing the next, gradient descent converges to a neighborhood of the true model whose radius shrinks as $1/\omega_{\max}$.

The argument rests on two facts: (i) at low frequency the misfit is locally convex in a neighborhood whose radius grows with wavelength; (ii) the octave condition keeps each stage's minimizer inside the next stage's basin of attraction [4][5]. The constants are pessimistic, but the theorem captures exactly why continuation works.

## 6 Limitations, Open Problems, and Future Work

Honesty requires stating what FWI cannot yet do:

- **The low-frequency gap.** Marine sources deliver little usable energy below 3–5 Hz — precisely the frequencies the convergence theory demands. Broadband acquisition and DAS are narrowing the gap, but physics, not algorithms, is the binding constraint [8].
- **Elastic anisotropy at scale.** Real sediments are VTI/TTI anisotropic; even 5-parameter TTI FWI has severe null spaces. Current practice inverts a subset and fixes the rest from well data [20].
- **Attenuation (Q).** $Q$ and velocity trade off almost perfectly; joint inversion needs independent constraints rarely available [3].
- **Uncertainty quantification.** FWI delivers a single "best" model with no error bars. Bayesian FWI via MCMC is intractable at scale; variational and ensemble methods are promising but immature [27].
- **Learned components.** Physics-informed neural surrogates accelerate pieces of the pipeline (e.g., [13]), but no learned method yet replaces the adjoint-state core — the wave equation remains the best prior we have.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Full-waveform inversion has matured from a 1980s theoretical curiosity into the highest-resolution imaging technology in exploration seismology. Its three pillars — the **adjoint-state gradient**, making million-parameter optimization cost two simulations per shot [1][12]; **multiscale frequency continuation**, taming cycle skipping one octave at a time [4][5]; and **exascale elastic solvers** [20][25] — constitute one of the most successful inverse-problem campaigns in applied mathematics.

The frontier is clear: close the low-frequency gap, tame multiparameter crosstalk in anisotropic elastic media, and attach honest uncertainty to every image. The adjoint-state machine will remain at the center — because when the physics is known, the cheapest way to differentiate through it is still to run it backward.
