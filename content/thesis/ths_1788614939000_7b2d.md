# Progressive Lowering in MLIR: Dialect Architecture, the Operation–Attribute–Type System, Declarative Rewrites with PDL, and Polyhedral Optimization via the Affine Dialect

## Abstract

MLIR reimagines compiler intermediate representations as a *family* of interoperable dialects rather than a single monolithic IR, enabling **progressive lowering**: the stepwise decomposition of high-level abstractions into progressively more concrete ones within a single extensible infrastructure. This thesis gives a rigorous account of MLIR's core mechanisms and their interplay. We formalize the operation/attribute/type system — the SSA-based building blocks of every dialect — and the Operation Definition Specification (ODS) that generates dialects from declarative TableGen descriptions. We analyze progressive lowering as a discipline of legality-constrained graph rewriting through the `ConversionTarget` and `ConversionPattern` machinery, contrasting monolithic lowering with incremental dialect mixing. We present declarative rewrite-rule technology from DAG-to-DAG Declarative Rewrite Rules (DRR) through the first-class Pattern Description Language (PDL) dialect and its surface language PDLL, including the PDL bytecode interpreter. Finally, we develop the affine dialect as MLIR's polyhedral substrate — affine maps, integer sets, static-control parts, dependence analysis, tiling and interchange — and trace a complete lowering trajectory from `linalg.matmul` through tiling, vectorization, and the affine dialect to the LLVM dialect.

## 1. Introduction

For decades, mainstream compilers converged on a single, fixed intermediate representation. LLVM IR, for instance, proved enormously successful as a target-independent substrate for analysis and optimization, yet its very fixedness became a liability: every domain — machine learning accelerators, FPGAs, heterogeneous GPUs, quantum co-processors — must raise its high-level constructs *into* LLVM IR and then laboriously recover the lost structure before it can be optimized [1]. This structure-recovery step, often called *raising*, is fragile, incomplete, and a perpetual source of missed optimization.

MLIR (Multi-Level Intermediate Representation), introduced by Lattner et al. in 2020–2021 and developed inside the LLVM project, attacks this problem at its root [1]. Rather than one IR, MLIR provides an *infrastructure for defining families of IRs* — **dialects** — that coexist in a single module, intermix freely, and lower progressively toward machine code. The key intellectual move is that **lowering is decomposition, not translation**: a high-level `linalg.matmul` operation does not get *translated* into something foreign; it is *decomposed* into structured loops (`scf.for`), then polyhedral loops (`affine.for`), then vector contractions (`vector.contract`), then memory operations (`memref`, `llvm`), each step a small, verifiable, reusable rewrite [5].

This thesis examines four pillars of that design:

1. **The operation/attribute/type system** — the SSA core shared by all dialects, and its declarative specification mechanism (ODS).
2. **Dialects and progressive lowering** — how legality-driven dialect conversion replaces monolithic lowering pipelines.
3. **Declarative rewrite rules** — DRR in TableGen, the PDL dialect, and PDLL, with the PDL bytecode interpreter.
4. **The affine dialect** — MLIR's simplified polyhedral representation for loop-nest optimization.

Each pillar is presented with formal precision: definitions, theorems, worked code, and measured outcomes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From single IRs to IR families

Traditional compilers pipeline a single IR through fixed phases: front-end parsing, mid-end optimization, back-end lowering. MLIR instead treats the IR itself as a *tower of dialects*. A **dialect** is a namespace of operations, types, and attributes sharing a common semantic level. Crucially, operations from *different* dialects may appear in the same function — a module may contain `linalg.matmul` on tensors next to `scf.for` loops next to `arith` arithmetic [1]. This mixing is what makes progressive lowering practical: a pass need only convert the ops it understands, leaving the rest untouched.

### 2.2 SSA, regions, and blocks

Every MLIR program is in SSA form. An **operation** produces zero or more **values**, each typed; a **value** has exactly one definition (an op result or a block argument). Control flow is organized into **regions** — the bodies of ops like functions and loops — which contain **blocks** of ops ending in terminators [1]. This graph-region hybrid distinguishes MLIR from pure CFG IRs: structured ops (`scf.for`, `affine.for`, `gpu.launch`) carry their control flow *as data* in nested regions, so analyses see loops as single nodes rather than scattered branches.

> **Theorem: (SSA dominance invariant).** In well-formed MLIR, every use of an SSA value is dominated by its definition within the region hierarchy, where a region entry dominates its interior. All dialect-level rewrites that preserve this invariant preserve program well-formedness. ∎

### 2.3 The polyhedral model

The **polyhedral model** treats static-control loop nests as integer polyhedra: iteration domains are sets defined by affine inequalities, and data dependences are affine relations between iterations. Classical polyhedral compilers (GRAPHITE for GCC, Polly for LLVM) recover this structure from low-level IR — a lossy *raising* step. MLIR's affine dialect instead *preserves* polyhedral structure from the start: `affine.for` bounds and `affine.load`/`affine.store` indices are affine maps of enclosing iterators by construction [6].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is constructive and evidence-driven:

1. **Primary-source reconstruction.** All formal claims about the op/attribute/type system derive from the MLIR ODS specification and the canonical MLIR paper [1]; claims about PDL/PDLL derive from the PDL dialect documentation [2], the PDLL reference [3], and the declarative-rewrites (DRR) documentation [4].
2. **Working artifacts.** Every major mechanism is illustrated with runnable MLIR text or TableGen/PDLL code drawn from the upstream documentation.
3. **Empirical anchoring.** Performance claims cite published measurements: the composable code-generation flow [5], the mlirSynth raising study (Polly 1.9×, Polygeist+Pluto on Affine IR 3.2× on `doitgen` from Polybench) [7], and Bondhugula's affine-based GEMM code-generation study [8].
4. **Complexity analysis.** Where algorithms are discussed (dependence analysis, schedule search), we state asymptotic costs in terms of iteration-domain dimensionality and constraint count.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Operation/Attribute/Type System and ODS

At the bottom of MLIR's tower sits a tiny, fixed core: **operations, attributes, types, values, regions, and blocks**. Everything else — every dialect — is defined *in terms of* this core. An operation has a name (`"affine.for"`), operands, results, attributes, successors, and regions [1]. A **type** classifies values (e.g., `tensor<4x8xf32>`, `index`, `memref<64xf64>`); an **attribute** is compile-time-known constant data attached to an op (e.g., the affine map on an `affine.for`, the indexing maps on `vector.contract`).

Dialect authors rarely write C++ by hand. Instead they write **ODS (Operation Definition Specification)** in TableGen, and `mlir-tblgen` generates the C++ classes, parsers, printers, and verifiers:

```tablegen
def MyMatmulOp : Op<MyDialect, "matmul"> {
  let summary = "blocked matrix multiply";
  let arguments = (ins
    TensorOf<[F32]>:$lhs, TensorOf<[F32]>:$rhs,
    I32Attr:$blockM, I32Attr:$blockN);
  let results = (outs TensorOf<[F32]>:$result);
  let hasVerifier = 1;
}
```

The ODS layer is where MLIR's "specification as data" philosophy begins: op definitions carry traits (`NoSideEffect`, `SameOperandsAndResultType`), interfaces, and declarative verifiers. Because every dialect — built-in or user-defined — is specified through ODS, tooling (documentation, bindings, pattern matchers) is uniformly derivable. This uniformity is what lets the pattern-matching machinery of §4.3 reason about *any* dialect generically.

| Concept | C++ entity | ODS/TableGen entity | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operation | `mlir::Operation` | `Op<Dialect, mnemonic>` | Atomic unit of computation |
| Attribute | `mlir::Attribute` | `AttrDef` / `AttrConstraint` | Compile-time constant data |
| Type | `mlir::Type` | `TypeDef` / `TypeConstraint` | Classifies SSA values |
| Dialect | `mlir::Dialect` | `Dialect` | Namespace + registration |
| Pattern | `RewritePattern` | `Pat<>` / `Pattern<>` | Source DAG → result DAG |

### 4.2 Dialects and Progressive Lowering

A dialect conversion is not a monolithic "lower everything" pass. It is a *legality problem*: given a set of `ConversionPattern`s and a `ConversionTarget` declaring which ops, types, and dialects are legal, the conversion driver rewrites the IR until a fixed point where everything remaining is legal [1]. The driver handles type conversion (`TypeConverter`), signature materialization, and rollback on failure.

The canonical lowering trajectory for a tensor kernel looks like this:

```mlir
// Level 1: named tensor op (linalg dialect)
%C = linalg.matmul ins(%A, %B : tensor<64x64xf32>, tensor<64x64xf32>)
                     outs(%C0 : tensor<64x64xf32>) -> tensor<64x64xf32>

// Level 2: tiled into structured loops (scf + tensor dialects)
scf.for %i = %c0 to %c64 step %c16 {
  %a = tensor.extract_slice %A[%i, 0] [16, 64] [1, 1]
      : tensor<64x64xf32> to tensor<16x64xf32>
  ...
}

// Level 3: vectorized contraction (vector dialect)
%v = vector.contract {indexing_maps = [#mapA, #mapB, #mapC],
                      iterator_types = ["parallel", "parallel", "reduction"],
                      kind = #vector.kind<add>} %va, %vb, %vc
    : vector<4x8xf32>, vector<8x16xf32> into vector<4x16xf32>

// Level 4: machine-level (llvm dialect)
%ptr = llvm.getelementptr %base[%i] : (!llvm.ptr, i64) -> !llvm.ptr
```

Three design principles make this work. **(i) Intermixing:** intermediate stages freely mix dialects, so each conversion pass is small and total. **(ii) Optionality:** dialects provide lowering passes one level down as reusable components; a compiler selects the subset it needs. **(iii) Transformation-oriented design:** instead of legality analyses on low-level IR, the flow relies on progressive decomposition of carefully designed abstractions [5]. Phase-ordering problems are alleviated by *pass fusion* — composing declarative patterns so that tiling, fusion, and vectorization decisions are made jointly rather than in brittle sequence.

> **Theorem: (Lowering confluence under legality).** Let *T* be a conversion target and *P* a set of conversion patterns such that every illegal op has at least one applicable pattern and pattern application is measure-decreasing in the number of illegal ops. Then the dialect-conversion driver terminates with all ops legal w.r.t. *T* or fails explicitly with rollback; it never produces a partially-converted module silently. ∎

### 4.3 Declarative Rewrite Rules: DRR, PDL, and PDLL

MLIR's transformation engine is pattern rewriting, and MLIR offers it at three levels of declarativeness.

**Level 1 — DRR (Declarative Rewrite Rules) in TableGen.** The oldest mechanism: a `Pattern`/`Pat` record gives a source DAG matched against the IR and result DAGs to generate, with `NativeCodeCall` hooks for constraints not expressible structurally [4]:

```tablegen
def FoldReshapeReshape : Pat<
  (toy_reshape (toy_reshape $arg)),
  (toy_reshape $arg), [], (addBenefit 10)>;
```

`mlir-tblgen --gen-rewriters` compiles these into `RewritePattern` subclasses. DRR is powerful but compile-time bound: changing a rule means recompiling the compiler.

**Level 2 — the PDL dialect.** PDL (Pattern Description Language) represents *patterns as MLIR IR itself* [2]. A `pdl.pattern` op holds a matcher region and terminates in `pdl.rewrite`; PDL values are *handles* (`!pdl.operation`, `!pdl.value`, `!pdl.attribute`, `!pdl.type`) to the payload IR, not payload values themselves — the single most important conceptual distinction for newcomers:

```mlir
pdl.pattern : benefit(1) {
  %resultType = pdl.type
  %inputOperand = pdl.operand
  %root = pdl.operation "toy.reshape"(%inputOperand) -> %resultType
  pdl.rewrite %root {
    pdl.replace %root with (%inputOperand)
  }
}
```

Because patterns are IR, the *entire MLIR infrastructure applies to the infrastructure itself*: patterns can be verified, transformed, and optimized with ordinary passes [2]. The `pdl_interp` dialect plus the **PDL bytecode interpreter** executes patterns loaded at runtime — no recompilation, enabling dynamically extensible rewriting (e.g., hardware drivers registering patterns at load time [1]).

**Level 3 — PDLL.** PDLL is a purpose-built surface language compiled by `mlir-pdll` to PDL IR (or to C++ headers), with a richer type system, named rewrite functions, and match/rewrite section separation [3]:

```pdll
Pattern ReshapeReshapeOptPattern with benefit(10) {
  let root = op<toy.reshape>(op<toy.reshape>(arg: Value));
  replace root with op<toy.reshape>(arg);
}
```

ODS metadata makes PDLL `op<>` expressions strongly typed: argument names resolve against the op's ODS definition. The three levels form a deliberate ladder — *DRR for compile-time codegen, PDL for runtime-loaded IR patterns, PDLL for human-authored declarative rules* — unified by a single rewrite driver with benefit-ordered greedy application.

> **Theorem: (Benefit-ordered termination).** For a finite pattern set with bounded benefits and strictly measure-decreasing rewrites, the greedy rewrite driver applies at most *O(B · n)* pattern instances (B = max benefit chain, n = initial op count) before reaching a normal form. Non-decreasing patterns (e.g., canonicalization loops) require explicit fixed-point guards. ∎

### 4.4 The Affine Dialect and Polyhedral Optimization via `affine`

The **affine dialect** is MLIR's simplified polyhedral representation, and arguably its deepest idea. Classical polyhedral compilers infer affine structure *from* low-level IR; MLIR instead *preserves* it: `affine.for` bounds are affine maps of loop-invariant values, `affine.if` conditions are integer sets, and `affine.load`/`affine.store` indices are affine functions of enclosing iterators [1][6]. The rationale document is explicit about the tradeoff being optimized: full polyhedral generality (arbitrary Presburger sets, isl schedule trees) buys expressiveness at the cost of exponential scheduling algorithms and IR complexity; the affine dialect deliberately restricts to the **static control part (SCoP)** closure where dependence analysis is exact and cheap [6].

Formally, an affine map is a function *f: ℤᵈ × ℤˢ → ℤʳ* with *f(d, s) = M·[d; s] + c* for integer matrix *M* and vector *c*. The iteration domain of an `affine.for` nest is a parametric polytope; each `affine.load`/`store` defines an access relation — an affine map from iteration vectors to memref index vectors. Dependence analysis then reduces to integer linear programming over the difference of access relations, which MLIR implements with a Flatland/isl-style solver restricted to the affine fragment [1].

This buys the classical polyhedral transformation suite as *dialect-local rewrites*:

- **Loop tiling/strip-mining**: `affine.for %i = 0 to 1024` → two-level nest with tile size *T*, emitted by `-affine-loop-tile=tile-sizes=32,32`. Tiling is expressed as a pure rewrite of the iteration polytope, no dependence re-analysis needed beyond legality checks.
- **Loop interchange/permutation**: reordering dimensions of the schedule, valid iff the permuted dependence distance vectors remain lexicographically positive.
- **Loop fusion and distribution**: merging or splitting loop nests with compatible domains — the affine dialect's structural loops make this a region-level splice rather than CFG surgery.
- **Unroll-and-jam, scalar replacement, memref normalization**: all operate on affine ops directly [8].

A canonical example — the polynomial-multiplication / GEMM-style kernel:

```mlir
#mapA = affine_map<(d0, d1, d2) -> (d0, d2)>
#mapB = affine_map<(d0, d1, d2) -> (d2, d1)>
#mapC = affine_map<(d0, d1, d2) -> (d0, d1)>
func.func @gemm(%A: memref<64x64xf32>, %B: memref<64x64xf32>,
               %C: memref<64x64xf32>) {
  affine.for %i = 0 to 64 {
    affine.for %j = 0 to 64 {
      affine.for %k = 0 to 64 {
        %a = affine.load %A[%i, %k] : memref<64x64xf32>
        %b = affine.load %B[%k, %j] : memref<64x64xf32>
        %c = affine.load %C[%i, %j] : memref<64x64xf32>
        %p = arith.mulf %a, %b : f32
        %s = arith.addf %c, %p : f32
        affine.store %s, %C[%i, %j] : memref<64x64xf32>
      }
    }
  }
  return
}
```

Bondhugula's study shows that starting from a single high-level op and applying this affine transformation suite — tiling, packing, unroll-and-jam, vectorization — generates code competitive with hand-tuned libraries, because every stage stays within the affine fragment and each transformation is a small, verifiable rewrite [8]. The mlirSynth work independently confirms the value of the affine level: raising C to Affine IR via Polygeist and applying the Pluto scheduler yields a 3.2× speedup on Polybench `doitgen`, versus 1.9× for Polly on LLVM IR — the preserved structure is worth roughly 1.7× over recovered structure [7].

Crucially, the affine dialect *embeds the polyhedral representation in SSA*: constraints live in attributes, loops in region-carrying ops. This resolves the classic composability complaint against polyhedral compilers — that affine representations fight SSA-based passes like LICM and induction-variable canonicalization [5]. In MLIR, `affine.for` is just another op; SSA passes and polyhedral passes interleave freely.

### 4.5 End-to-End: One Kernel, Four Levels

Putting it together, a complete compilation of `linalg.matmul` on an AVX-512 CPU proceeds as:

1. **Tile** (`linalg` → `linalg`+`scf`+`tensor`): 64³ problem → 32³ tiles via declarative tile patterns.
2. **Bufferize** (`tensor` → `memref`): one-shot bufferization resolves tensor SSA into in-place memref updates.
3. **Vectorize** (`scf` → `vector`): inner loops become `vector.contract` with explicit indexing maps.
4. **Affine scheduling** (`scf` → `affine`): loops converted to `affine.for`; tiling, interchange, and unroll-and-jam applied under exact dependence analysis.
5. **Lower** (`affine`+`vector`+`memref` → `llvm`): affine maps materialize as arithmetic, vector ops become LLVM vector intrinsics, memrefs become pointer arithmetic.

Each arrow is a small conversion pass; the whole pipeline is assembled from reusable, upstream-maintained components. This is the composable, modular code generation of [5]: *transformation-oriented IR design* where legality analyses are replaced by progressive decomposition.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Lowering-correctness proof sketch

We sketch the central metatheorem: *progressive lowering preserves program semantics*.

> **Theorem: (Progressive lowering soundness).** Let *L₀ → L₁ → ⋯ → Lₙ* be a lowering pipeline where each stage is either (a) a conversion pass whose patterns are individually semantics-preserving under the dialect's operational semantics, or (b) a transformation pass (tiling, fusion, interchange) whose legality precondition is exact dependence analysis over the affine fragment. Then the composed pipeline is semantics-preserving. ∎
>
> *Proof sketch.* By induction on stages. Case (a): conversion patterns replace matched DAGs with result DAGs that are observationally equivalent by the pattern's contract; the driver applies them atomically with rollback, so intermediate states are invisible. Case (b): tiling/interchange/fusion are valid exactly when permuted dependence distance vectors stay lexicographically positive — checked by the affine dependence analysis, which is exact (not conservative) on SCoPs because all bounds and accesses are affine by construction [6]. Composition of semantics-preserving stages is semantics-preserving. ∎

The practical force of this theorem is *auditability*: a miscompile can be bisected to the single small pass that introduced it, because each pass's contract is local.

### 5.2 Measured performance

| Study | Setup | Result |
|---|---|---|
| mlirSynth raising [7] | Polybench `doitgen`, Intel i7-8700K | Polly on LLVM IR: **1.9×** over -O3; Polygeist→Affine IR + Pluto: **3.2×** |
| Composable codegen [5] | Single-threaded CPU tensor kernels | Strong performance vs. hand-tuned baselines without systematic autotuning |
| Affine GEMM study [8] | DGEMM via affine tiling/packing/unroll-and-jam | Competitive with vendor libraries from a single `hop.matmul` op |
| Katel et al. (via [1]-era reports) | GPU matmul vs. cuBLAS | MLIR-generated GPU code competitive with cuBLAS |

The consistent lesson: *preserved* high-level structure (affine dialect, linalg named ops) outperforms *recovered* structure by a wide margin, because dependence information that is exact by construction never has to be re-derived through alias analysis [7].

### 5.3 Pattern-system expressiveness

The PDL interpreter executes patterns as bytecode (`pdl_interp` dialect), making pattern application *O(m · n)* where *m* is the number of patterns and *n* the IR size in the common case, with benefit ordering providing deterministic conflict resolution [2]. PDLL's compilation to PDL preserves semantics by construction — it is a desugaring, not a reimplementation [3].

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating what MLIR does *not* solve:

1. **Affine is not fully polyhedral.** The affine dialect deliberately excludes non-affine bounds, dynamic control flow, and parametric integer sets beyond the SCoP fragment. Programs outside this fragment must stay in `scf` or lower further, losing exact dependence analysis [6]. General polyhedral scheduling (isl schedule trees, exponential search) remains out of scope by design.
2. **Phase ordering is alleviated, not eliminated.** Pass fusion and declarative pattern composition reduce phase-ordering pain [5], but benefit values, pattern ordering, and pass-pipeline selection still require expert tuning; there is no automatic pipeline synthesizer in upstream MLIR.
3. **PDL expressiveness ceiling.** Structural matching cannot express semantic constraints (e.g., "this loop is parallel because a dependence test says so") without `NativeCodeCall` escape hatches to C++ [2][4]. The declarative ladder is leaky at the top.
4. **Dialect proliferation cost.** Every new dialect is a new semantic island: mixing dialects requires conversion patterns for each pair, an *O(d²)* integration surface in the worst case. The ecosystem mitigates this with shared "hub" dialects (`arith`, `func`, `memref`), but the cost is real.
5. **Verification gaps.** ODS generates structural verifiers, not semantic ones. A mis-specified affine map or an unsound hand-written pattern compiles and runs — incorrectly. The soundness theorem of §5.1 assumes pattern contracts that are checked by convention, not by proof.
6. **Compile time.** Benefit-ordered greedy rewriting over large pattern sets is a known compile-time sink; production pipelines rely on pattern freezing, folding caches, and the PDL interpreter's bytecode dispatch to stay tractable.

## 7. Conclusion

MLIR's contribution is not a new optimization but a new *organization* of compilation. The operation/attribute/type core plus ODS makes dialects cheap to define; progressive lowering makes them cheap to compose; DRR/PDL/PDLL make transformations declarative and reusable; and the affine dialect shows that a *simplified* polyhedral form — exact where it applies, honest about where it does not — can outperform fully general polyhedral recovery [1][5][6][7][8].

The deeper thesis is architectural: **compilers should be structured as towers of small, verifiable, reusable decompositions, not as pipelines of monolithic phases.** Every lowering step in MLIR is a local, auditable rewrite with an explicit legality contract. That discipline — more than any single dialect — is what lets one infrastructure serve TensorFlow graphs, Fortran front ends, GPU kernels, and hardware synthesis alike. The open problems (automatic pipeline synthesis, verified pattern contracts, bridging the affine/SCoP gap) are now well-posed *because* the tower exists. Future work that closes them will do so one small, declarative rewrite at a time.
