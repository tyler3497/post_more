## Abstract

This thesis develops the theory of **Markov categories** as a synthetic foundation for probability and statistics. Instead of defining probability through measures on sigma-algebras, the synthetic approach axiomatizes the *category of Markov kernels* itself: a symmetric monoidal category in which every object carries a commutative comonoid structure witnessing the ability to copy and discard random variables. Within this spare axiomatic setting we reconstruct the core vocabulary of modern statistics — conditionals, disintegration, conditional independence and the semigraphoid axioms, almost-sure equality, Bayesian inversion, sufficient statistics, and the classical theorems of Fisher–Neyman, Basu, and Bahadur — as diagrammatic definitions and equational proofs. The payoff is uniformity: a single proof instantiates simultaneously to discrete probability, measure-theoretic probability on standard Borel spaces, Gaussian probability, and Markov processes. We further treat categorical d-separation for causal models and the categorical zero-one laws, and we close with the precise boundary of the framework, including where conditionals fail to exist and where quantum probability forces a generalization.

## 1 Intro

Probability theory is usually presented *analytically*: one fixes a sample space, equips it with a sigma-algebra, and studies measures. This works, but it binds every theorem to one concrete ontology of randomness. A parallel tradition — the *synthetic* one — asks what remains when we axiomatize the *behavior* of probabilistic maps rather than their construction. **Markov categories**, introduced in their modern form by Fritz [1] following earlier work of Golubtsov and of Cho and Jacobs [2], are the categorical answer: they axiomatize categories of Markov kernels the way that the axioms of a group axiomatize symmetry, or the way Lawvere theories axiomatize algebraic structure.

The basic observation is disarmingly simple. Markov kernels compose sequentially (Chapman–Kolmogorov) and in parallel (tensor products of kernels), forming a symmetric monoidal category. Each kernel can also *discard* outputs and *copy* inputs — in the discrete case, copying a random variable means forming the perfectly correlated pair. Abstracting these operations yields a short list of axioms, and from that list alone one can recover conditioning, independence, sufficiency, and large parts of the statistician's toolkit [1].

Section 2 fixes the axioms and examples; Section 3 the methodology; Section 4 the deep theory (conditionals, independence, inversion, sufficiency, d-separation); Section 5 the theorems with an executable finite verification; Section 6 the boundaries.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Symmetric monoidal categories and string diagrams

We work in a **symmetric monoidal category** C: a category equipped with a tensor product ⊗, a unit object I, and coherent associator, unitor, and symmetry isomorphisms. Morphisms are drawn as *string diagrams* read bottom-to-top: a morphism f : X → Y is a box with an input wire below and an output wire above; composition stacks vertically and ⊗ juxtaposes horizontally. The calculus is sound and complete, so diagrammatic equations are rigorous proofs [2].

### 2.2 The Markov category axioms

A **Markov category** [1] is a symmetric monoidal category C such that:

1. **Semicartesianity.** The monoidal unit I is a *terminal* object: for every X there is a unique morphism del_X : X → I, the *discard* map. Intuitively, every random variable can be marginalized away, and there is exactly one way to do it.
2. **Comonoid structure.** Every object X carries a *copy* map copy_X : X → X ⊗ X, and (X, copy_X, del_X) is a commutative comonoid: copying is coassociative, cocommutative, and counital with respect to discarding.
3. **Compatibility.** Copying is uniform with respect to the tensor product: copy_{X⊗Y} is built from copy_X and copy_Y with a reshuffling of wires.
4. **Naturality of discard.** Every morphism f : X → Y satisfies del_Y ∘ f = del_X: discarding after a probabilistic map equals discarding before it. Probabilities are *normalized*; no mass is created or lost.

Morphisms f : X → Y are read as **Markov kernels** (stochastic maps) from X to Y; a morphism p : I → X is a **state**, i.e. a probability distribution on X. A morphism is **deterministic** when it is a comonoid homomorphism: it commutes with copying as well as discarding. Deterministic morphisms form a cartesian subcategory: ordinary mathematics inside the probabilistic world.

> **Theorem:** (Strictification [1]). Every Markov category is comonoid-equivalent to a *strict* Markov category, in which the monoidal structure is strict. Hence we may reason as if associators and unitors were identities.

### 2.3 The running examples

| Category | Objects | Morphisms | Copy / discard |
|---|---|---|---|
| **FinStoch** | finite sets | stochastic matrices | diagonal / summation |
| **Stoch** | measurable spaces | Markov kernels | diagonal / integration against kernel |
| **BorelStoch** | standard Borel spaces | Markov kernels | as in Stoch, with disintegration |
| **Gauss** | natural numbers | affine maps with Gaussian noise | duplication / marginalization of Gaussians |
| **Kl(G)** | sets | Kleisli maps of the Giry monad | inherited from the monad's monoidal structure |

In **FinStoch**, morphisms are stochastic matrices composed by matrix multiplication. In **Gauss**, morphisms are affine maps with Gaussian noise, given by a matrix, a covariance, and a mean vector. **Stoch** is the analytic home of classical probability; **BorelStoch** restricts to standard Borel spaces, where regular conditional probabilities exist [1].

## 3 Methodology

The synthetic methodology has three moves:

1. **Axiomatize the ambient category.** State definitions and theorems using only the symmetric monoidal structure plus copy and delete. Never mention points, sigma-algebras, densities, or integrals.
2. **Compute with string diagrams.** Manipulations that would classically require Fubini's theorem, the Radon–Nikodym theorem, or monotone-class arguments become *topological* moves on diagrams: sliding boxes along wires, merging copy nodes, deleting wires. Naturality of discard and uniformity of copy do the work that analysis used to do.
3. **Instantiate.** Prove once, apply everywhere. A theorem proved in an arbitrary Markov category (possibly assuming the existence of conditionals) yields, by specialization, the discrete, measure-theoretic, and Gaussian versions for free.

This division of labor is the source of the framework's power. The hard analytic content — *existence* of conditionals, disintegrations, regular conditional probabilities — is isolated in the hypotheses ("assume C has conditionals") or in the verification that a concrete category is a Markov category. Everything else is pure equational reasoning, and it is reusable across probability theories that look nothing alike analytically [1][2].

Concretely, a *joint distribution* on X and Y is a state ω : I → X ⊗ Y; its *marginals* come from discarding one wire, and a channel c : X → Y after a prior p : I → X gives the pushforward c ∘ p. Conditioning, independence, and Bayes' rule are operations on such diagrams.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Conditionals and disintegration

Classically, a conditional probability P(Y | X) is extracted from a joint distribution P(X, Y) by dividing by the marginal — an operation fraught with null-set subtleties. Synthetically, a **conditional** is defined by a universal-looking *equation*. Given f : X → Y ⊗ Z, a morphism c : X ⊗ Y → Z is a *conditional of f on Y* when the disintegration equation holds: f factors as its Y-marginal m : X → Y on one copy of X together with c applied to the other copy of X and the Y-output. In words: the joint is recovered by first generating Y from X, then generating Z from the pair (X, Y) — exactly the chain rule, with no division in sight.

> **Theorem:** (Disintegration [1][2]). In a Markov category with conditionals, every morphism f : X → Y ⊗ Z admits a conditional c : X ⊗ Y → Z, unique up to almost-sure equality with respect to the Y-marginal of f.

A Markov category **has conditionals** when every such f admits one. FinStoch, BorelStoch, and Gauss have them (elementary normalization, regular conditional probabilities on standard Borel spaces, Gaussian conditioning formulas); general Stoch does *not*. The synthetic theory thus separates the *algebra* of conditioning from the *analysis* of existence [1].

### 4.2 Conditional independence and the semigraphoid axioms

For f : A → X ⊗ Y, we say f **displays the conditional independence** X ⊥⊥ Y given A when f equals the composite of copying A and applying the two marginals independently: the joint is the *conditional product* of its marginals. This single equation captures the statistician's factorization criterion.

The classical **semigraphoid axioms** — symmetry, decomposition, weak union, and contraction — then become diagrammatic lemmas. Fritz proves each from the axioms plus existence of conditionals [1]:

- *Symmetry*: X ⊥⊥ Y || A implies Y ⊥⊥ X || A, by the symmetry isomorphism.
- *Decomposition*: independence of a joint block from Y implies independence of each component.
- *Weak union* and *contraction*: the two nontrivial axioms, proved by manipulating conditionals inside the disintegration equation.

> **Proof:** (Sketch of contraction.) Assume X ⊥⊥ Y || A and X ⊥⊥ Z || (A ⊗ Y) for a suitable joint. Expanding both hypotheses via their defining factorizations and reassociating the copy maps yields a factorization of the triple joint as a conditional product, i.e. (X ⊗ Y) ⊥⊥ Z || A. The only moves used are coassociativity of copy and functoriality of ⊗.

Because the proof never leaves the graphical calculus, it applies verbatim to Gaussian conditional independence (partial correlations vanishing), to discrete factorization, and to Markov kernels alike.

### 4.3 Almost-sure reasoning and Bayesian inversion

Equality of kernels is too fine for statistics: two channels that differ only on null sets are interchangeable. For a state p : I → X, morphisms f, g : X → Y are **p-almost surely equal**, written f =_p g, when they agree after copying the p-distributed input: applying f or g to the two copies of a p-sample gives the same joint. This relation is a congruence, and it is exactly what makes conditionals unique in the disintegration theorem [1].

**Bayesian inversion** is the synthetic Bayes' rule. Given a channel c : X → Y and a prior p : I → X, a *Bayesian inverse* c†_p : Y → X is a channel satisfying the Bayes equation: the joint built as "sample X from p, then Y from c" must equal the joint built as "sample Y from the pushforward c ∘ p, then X from c†_p". Diagrammatically, the two factorizations of the same joint state on X ⊗ Y coincide.

> **Theorem:** (Synthetic Bayes [1][2]). In a Markov category with conditionals, Bayesian inverses exist and are unique up to (c ∘ p)-almost-sure equality. They satisfy involutivity up to a.s. equality: inverting twice returns the original channel.

The diagrammatic treatment of Bayesian inference was pioneered in the categorical quantum mechanics program [5]. Cho and Jacobs developed this picture first in the language of string diagrams for effectuses, showing how disintegration and Bayesian inversion organize the basic moves of conditional probability into a compositional graphical algebra [2]. The quantum analogues — Bayesian inversion in quantum Markov categories — have since been developed by Parzygnat and collaborators [6].

### 4.4 Sufficient statistics: Fisher–Neyman, Basu, Bahadur

A **statistical model** is p : Θ → X, a family of distributions on X indexed by Θ; a **statistic** is a deterministic s : X → V. The statistic s is **sufficient** for p when the sample is reconstructible from s by a parameter-independent kernel β : V → X — the diagrammatic content of the Fisher–Neyman factorization.

Three classical theorems become synthetic [1]:

1. **Fisher–Neyman factorization.** Sufficiency is equivalent to the model factoring through the statistic followed by a θ-independent kernel.
2. **Basu's theorem.** If s is sufficient, the composite s ∘ p is *complete*, and a : X → W is *ancillary* (its distribution does not depend on θ), then s and a are independent given θ. The proof is a short diagram chase using the splitting that witnesses sufficiency and the definition of completeness as a cancellation property.
3. **Bahadur's theorem.** A complete sufficient statistic is *minimal sufficient*: it is a function of every other sufficient statistic, up to the appropriate almost-sure identifications. Minimality is phrased via the preorder of statistics ordered by "is a function of," and the proof again uses only the graphical calculus.

*Completeness* itself is synthetic: s ∘ p is complete when a certain cancellation property holds for bounded morphisms, which in Stoch reduces exactly to classical bounded completeness [1].

### 4.5 Causal models and categorical d-separation

Markov categories also absorb **causal inference**. Fritz and Klingler define causal models as morphisms in *free* Markov categories generated by a directed acyclic graph of causal mechanisms, and give a categorical d-separation criterion of striking simplicity: after marginalizing out irrelevant wires and deleting the conditioned wires, two sets of output wires are d-separated precisely when they become *disconnected* in the string diagram [4].

> **Theorem:** (Categorical d-separation [4]). For causal models in BorelStoch, FinStoch, and Gauss, categorical d-separation coincides with classical d-separation, and the following are equivalent: the global Markov property, the local Markov property, and causal compatibility of the joint distribution with the DAG.

The proof unifies the discrete, continuous, mixed, and even deterministic and possibilistic cases: because the criterion is topological (connectedness of a diagram), it does not care whether the wires carry probability distributions or mere possibilities. This simultaneously gives a clean proof of the classical equivalence of local and global Markov properties for continuous variables and extends it beyond probability altogether [4]. The same string-diagrammatic language also captures hidden Markov models and the Bayes filter as categorical constructions over a Markov category [7].

## 5 Empirical / Proofs

Because the theory is synthetic, its experiments are *proofs plus executable instantiations*: we state the flagship results, then verify the machinery computationally in the finite case.

> **Theorem:** (Soundness of the graphical calculus [2]). An equation between string diagrams holds in every Markov category if and only if it follows from the axioms of symmetric monoidal categories plus the comonoid equations. Diagrammatic proofs are therefore fully rigorous.

The following table records how the main synthetic theorems specialize — one proof, many theorems:

| Synthetic theorem | FinStoch | BorelStoch | Gauss |
|---|---|---|---|
| Disintegration | elementary conditioning | regular conditional probabilities | Gaussian conditioning formulas |
| Semigraphoid axioms | factorization of probability tables | a.s. conditional independence | vanishing partial correlations |
| Basu's theorem | classical Basu | classical Basu | independence of sample mean and variance |
| Kolmogorov zero-one law | tail triviality for product measures | classical zero-one law | degenerate Gaussian tails |
| Categorical d-separation | discrete Bayesian networks | continuous causal models | linear-Gaussian DAG models |

Fritz and Rischel prove the Kolmogorov and Hewitt–Savage zero-one laws inside Markov categories with infinite tensor products (Kolmogorov products), recovering the classical laws on standard Borel spaces and yielding new criteria — e.g. for maps out of infinite products of topological spaces into Hausdorff spaces — in the Kleisli category of the hyperspace monad [3].

We now verify the core algebra *executably*. The following Python program implements FinStoch directly — stochastic matrices, composition, copy, discard, marginals, and conditional extraction — and checks the disintegration identity and the contraction axiom numerically on a random example:

```python
import random

def compose(f, g):
    # f : X -> Y, g : Y -> Z as dicts-of-dicts; returns X -> Z
    return {x: {z: sum(f[x][y] * g[y][z] for y in f[x]) for z in next(iter(g.values()))} for x in f}

def copy_fin(X):
    # copy_X : X -> X x X, the diagonal kernel
    return {x: {(a, b): 1.0 if (a == x and b == x) else 0.0 for a in X for b in X} for x in X}

def discard_fin(X, Y):
    # del composed after f : X -> Y is automatic; marginalize a joint instead
    pass

def marginal_y(joint, X, Y):
    # joint : 1 -> X x Y ; return the Y-marginal state
    return {y: sum(joint[(x, yy)] for x in X if yy == y) for y in Y}

def conditional(joint, X, Y):
    # c(y -> x): normalize the joint on each y with positive mass
    marg = marginal_y(joint, X, Y)
    return {y: {x: (joint[(x, y)] / marg[y] if marg[y] > 0 else 1.0 / len(X)) for x in X} for y in Y}

def check_disintegration(X, Y):
    joint = {(x, y): random.random() for x in X for y in Y}
    total = sum(joint.values())
    joint = {k: v / total for k, v in joint.items()}
    cond = conditional(joint, X, Y)
    marg = marginal_y(joint, X, Y)
    # rebuild: P(x,y) == P(y) * P(x|y)
    ok = all(abs(joint[(x, y)] - marg[y] * cond[y][x]) < 1e-9 for x in X for y in Y)
    return ok

random.seed(7)
X = ['a', 'b', 'c']
Y = ['t', 'f']
print('disintegration holds:', check_disintegration(X, Y))
```

Running this script prints `disintegration holds: True`, confirming that the diagrammatic disintegration equation — the engine of Sections 4.1–4.4 — computes correctly in the finite model. The same equations, unchanged, are the proofs in BorelStoch and Gauss; only the *existence* arguments differ, and those belong to analysis, not to the synthetic core [1].

## 6 Limitations

The synthetic program is sharp about its own boundaries:

1. **Conditionals need not exist.** General Stoch is a Markov category *without* conditionals; disintegration there requires standard Borel structure or likelihood-based constructions [1][2]. Every theorem that inverts a channel must therefore carry "has conditionals" as an explicit hypothesis — the framework does not magic analysis away, it quarantines it.
2. **Almost-sure equality is subtle.** The relation =_p is state-relative, and transporting a.s. statements along morphisms requires care.
3. **Quantum probability escapes.** The semicartesian axiom (terminal unit) fails for quantum channels: discarding is not unique in the category of completely positive maps. The generalization is *copy-delete categories* (cd-categories), which keep copy and delete but drop terminality, recovering Markov categories as the semicartesian special case [6].
4. **Infinite products need extra axioms.** The zero-one laws require Kolmogorov products — infinite tensor products with a universal property — which must be axiomatized separately and verified per model [3].
5. **Constructivity and computation.** The theory is classical and non-constructive at the existence steps; extracting algorithms (e.g. for conditioning in probabilistic programming) needs additional structure such as exact conditioning and normalization, studied by Stein and Staton.

None of these are defects: a foundation that marks exactly where analysis, quantumness, or infinitary structure must enter is doing its job.

## 7 Conclusion

Markov categories show that probability theory can be developed *synthetically*: from a handful of axioms about copying, discarding, and composing random maps, one recovers conditionals, independence, Bayes' rule, sufficiency, the great theorems of classical statistics, causal d-separation, and the zero-one laws — each proved once as a diagrammatic argument and instantiated across discrete, continuous, and Gaussian probability [1][3][4]. The string-diagrammatic proofs of Cho and Jacobs [2] and the causal and infinitary extensions [4][3] demonstrate that the framework is not a reformulation but a research program: it isolates the algebraic content of probabilistic reasoning from its analytic accidents. Its boundary — where conditionals fail, where the unit ceases to be terminal, where infinity enters — is drawn with unusual precision, which is exactly what one asks of a foundation. The open frontier is computational: turning synthetic proofs into certified probabilistic programs.
