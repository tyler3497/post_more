# Transmission and Emission Spectroscopy of Exoplanet Atmospheres: JWST Probes of Sub-Neptunes, Hot Jupiters, and the Biosignature Frontier

## Abstract
The James Webb Space Telescope has transformed exoplanet atmospheric science from the statistical inference of single molecular bands into comparative planetology across orders of magnitude in mass, temperature, and composition. This thesis develops the physical and statistical foundations of transmission and emission spectroscopy — the radiative transfer of starlight filtered through planetary limbs and the thermal emission encoded in secondary eclipses and phase curves — and surveys their application to the two populations dominating the JWST census: hot Jupiters, whose puffy, high-signal atmospheres reveal photochemical products such as sulfur dioxide and metallicities exceeding ten times solar; and sub-Neptunes, whose muted spectra encode a stubborn degeneracy between high mean molecular weight, clouds, and hazes that has culminated in the contested hycean hypothesis for K2-18 b. We formalize Bayesian atmospheric retrieval via nested sampling, derive the scale-height limits on spectroscopic detectability, and critically assess the emerging search for biosignature gases — dimethyl sulfide and dimethyl disulfide — arguing that the field's maturation demands a quantitative ladder of evidence running from molecular detection, through photochemical false-positive exclusion, to biological plausibility.

## 1 Introduction
The atmosphere of an exoplanet is a boundary layer only a few hundred kilometers thick, yet it is the sole component of most exoplanets that can be studied remotely with spectroscopic fidelity. Everything we claim to know about an exoplanet's composition — its water content, its carbon-to-oxygen ratio, the possible presence of gases with no known abiotic source — is inferred from photons that have grazed a planetary limb or been re-emitted by a heated dayside, filtered through the machinery of radiative transfer and Bayesian inference [1].

The observational landscape changed discontinuously with the launch of the *James Webb Space Telescope* (JWST) in December 2021. Its four instruments — NIRSpec, NIRISS, NIRCam, and MIRI — deliver continuous spectroscopic coverage from 0.6 to 28 micrometers at spectral resolutions and photometric precisions that exceed those of the *Hubble Space Telescope* by roughly an order of magnitude in the infrared. Where the HST/WFC3 era yielded a handful of water-band detections and a graveyard of flat spectra, the JWST era has produced detections of methane, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, ammonia, and a menagerie of photochemical and condensate species, often within a *single* transit observation [1][5].

Two planetary populations dominate this revolution, and they could hardly be more different. **Hot Jupiters** — gas giants with equilibrium temperatures above 1000 K orbiting within a few hundredths of an astronomical unit of their stars — present enormous atmospheric scale heights and therefore large spectroscopic signals. They have become laboratories for photochemistry, cloud physics, and planet formation chemistry. **Sub-Neptunes** — planets of 1.5 to 4 Earth radii, the most common outcome of planet formation in the galaxy — present the opposite problem: their spectra are muted, ambiguous, and consistent with multiple radically different interior models, from hydrogen-rich mini-Neptunes to water worlds to rocky cores smothered in steam. It is among the sub-Neptunes that the most consequential claim in modern exoplanet science has been advanced: that K2-18 b, a temperate sub-Neptune orbiting an M dwarf, may show signs of dimethyl sulfide, a gas produced overwhelmingly by marine life on Earth [6][7].

This thesis develops the physical and statistical foundations of transmission and emission spectroscopy and surveys their application to the two populations dominating the JWST census.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Geometry of the transit signal
When a planet transits its host star, the fractional dimming of starlight — the *transit depth* — is to first order

$$\delta = \left(\frac{R_p}{R_*}\right)^2,$$

where $R_p$ and $R_*$ are the planetary and stellar radii. For a hot Jupiter around a Sun-like star, $\delta \sim 1\%$; for a sub-Neptune transiting a small M dwarf, $\delta$ can reach several percent because the stellar disk is small. The atmosphere imprints itself as a *wavelength-dependent* modulation of this depth: at wavelengths where atmospheric molecules absorb strongly, the planet appears larger, because starlight is extinguished higher in the atmosphere. The amplitude of these spectral features is set by the atmospheric **scale height**,

$$H = \frac{k_B T}{\mu m_H g},$$

with $T$ the temperature, $\mu$ the mean molecular weight in atomic mass units, and $g$ the surface gravity. A standard result of limb radiative transfer is that the contrast between the peaks and troughs of a transmission spectrum is of order

$$\frac{\Delta \delta}{\delta} \approx \frac{2 H}{R_p}, \qquad \Delta D \approx \frac{2 R_p H}{R_*^2},$$

so that the spectroscopic signal in absolute transit-depth units scales as $H/R_*^2$ [1]. This single scaling relation explains the entire observational hierarchy of the field: hot, low-gravity, hydrogen-dominated atmospheres ($H \sim 500$ km) produce features of hundreds of parts per million, while cool, high-metallicity sub-Neptune atmospheres ($\mu \gtrsim 10$, $H \sim 20$ km) produce features of tens of ppm — at the very edge of instrumental systematics.

### 2.2 The mean-molecular-weight ladder
The mean molecular weight $\mu$ is the master variable of transmission spectroscopy. A solar-composition, hydrogen–helium envelope has $\mu \approx 2.3$; a water-dominated envelope has $\mu = 18$; a carbon-dioxide-dominated envelope has $\mu = 44$. Because the feature amplitude scales as $1/\mu$, a flat transmission spectrum admits at least three degenerate interpretations: a high-$\mu$ atmosphere, an opaque cloud or haze deck truncating the line of sight, or simply insufficient signal-to-noise. The archetypal case was GJ 1214 b, whose HST spectrum was famously flat across 1.1–1.7 μm, spawning a decade of debate between high-metallicity and cloudy scenarios that JWST has only partially resolved [1].

The near- and mid-infrared bands accessible to JWST are dominated by a small set of strong absorbers whose band centers are worth committing to memory:

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Forward modeling
A transmission forward model computes, for a parameterized atmosphere, the wavelength-dependent transit depth. The standard construction assumes a one-dimensional, plane-parallel (or spherical-shell) atmosphere discretized into $\sim$100 layers in log-pressure from $10^{-6}$ to $10^2$ bar. Each layer carries a temperature from a parametric pressure–temperature profile, molecular volume mixing ratios (either free or tied to equilibrium chemistry), and cloud/haze opacity. The slant optical depth along a ray with impact parameter $b$ is

$$\tau_\lambda(b) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \sum_i n_i(s)\, \sigma_{i,\lambda}(T(s), P(s))\, ds + \tau_{\mathrm{cloud}} + \tau_{\mathrm{haze}},$$

and the effective planetary radius $R_{p,\lambda}$ is defined by the impact parameter at which the chord optical depth reaches $\tau \sim 0.56$–$1$.

### 3.2 Bayesian retrieval and nested sampling
Retrieval inverts the forward model: given data $D$ with uncertainties $\sigma$, we seek the posterior distribution over atmospheric parameters $\theta$,

$$P(\theta \mid D) = \frac{\mathcal{L}(D \mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta)}{\mathcal{Z}}, \qquad \mathcal{Z} = \int \mathcal{L}(D \mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta)\, d\theta,$$

where $\mathcal{Z}$, the Bayesian evidence, enables quantitative model comparison between, e.g., a model with and without a candidate molecule. The community standard for sampling this posterior is **nested sampling** (MultiNest, dynesty, UltraNest), which evolves a set of live points through nested likelihood contours and returns both posterior samples and $\mathcal{Z}$ in a single run. A detection significance for species $X$ is then quoted as a Bayes factor,

$$\ln B_X = \ln \mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{with\,X}} - \ln \mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{without\,X}}.$$

> **Theorem:** *Evidence-calibrated detection.* Let $\mathcal{M}_0$ be a nested sub-model of $\mathcal{M}_1$ differing by the inclusion of one molecular absorber with a log-uniform prior over $k$ dex. If the data constrain the absorber's log-abundance to a posterior width $w < k$ dex centered away from the prior's lower bound, then $\ln B > 0$ grows approximately as $(k - w)\ln 10$ minus an Occam penalty of order unity; a flat posterior recovers $\ln B \approx 0$ (no preference). *In words: Bayesian evidence rewards only constrained detections.*

### 3.3 A worked scale-height calculation
The following compact script evaluates the spectroscopic signal expected for two canonical cases — a hot Jupiter and a temperate sub-Neptune — illustrating why the former yields hundreds-of-ppm features and the latter demands heroic precision:

```python
import numpy as np

k_B = 1.380649e-23      # J/K
m_H = 1.6735575e-27     # kg
R_sun = 6.957e8         # m
R_jup = 7.1492e7        # m
R_earth = 6.371e6       # m

def scale_height(T, mu, g):
    """Atmospheric scale height in km."""
    return k_B * T / (mu * m_H * g) / 1000.0

def feature_ppm(Rp, Rs, H_km):
    """Approx. transmission feature amplitude in ppm."""
    return 2 * Rp * (H_km * 1000.0) / Rs**2 * 1e6

# Hot Jupiter: 1400 K, solar composition, g = 10 m/s^2
H_hj = scale_height(1400.0, 2.3, 10.0)
sig_hj = feature_ppm(1.3 * R_jup, 1.0 * R_sun, H_hj)

# Temperate sub-Neptune around M dwarf: 300 K, water-rich mu=18, g = 12 m/s^2
H_sn = scale_height(300.0, 18.0, 12.0)
sig_sn = feature_ppm(2.6 * R_earth, 0.45 * R_sun, H_sn)

print(f"Hot Jupiter:      H = {H_hj:.0f} km,  feature ~ {sig_hj:.0f} ppm")
print(f"Sub-Neptune:      H = {H_sn:.0f} km,  feature ~ {sig_sn:.0f} ppm")
```

## 4 Observational Frontiers in the JWST Era

### 4.1 Hot Jupiters as photochemical laboratories
The JWST Transiting Exoplanet Community Early Release Science program targeted WASP-39 b, a Saturn-mass ($\approx 0.28\,M_J$), $1.27\,R_J$ hot giant with $T_{\mathrm{eq}} \approx 1100$ K, obtaining transmission spectra from 0.5 to 12 μm with NIRSpec PRISM, G395H, NIRCam, NIRISS, and MIRI LRS [5]. The resulting inventory — Na, K, H$_2$O, CO, CO$_2$, and the surprise detection of SO$_2$ — made WASP-39 b the best-characterized exoplanet atmosphere to date. The SO$_2$ feature at 4.05 μm was the first unambiguous photochemical product ever identified in an exoplanet atmosphere: under thermochemical equilibrium, SO$_2$ should be present at sub-ppb levels, yet the observed abundance of 1–10 ppm is naturally produced when stellar UV destroys H$_2$S and the liberated sulfur radicals are successively oxidized [4]. Follow-up MIRI LRS transmission spectroscopy confirmed SO$_2$ bands at 7.7 and 8.5 μm, pinning the abundance to 0.5–25 ppm ($1\sigma$) and the atmospheric metallicity to 7–8$\times$ solar [2]. Because the SO$_2$ feature strength is steeply sensitive to heavy-element enrichment, it now serves as a metallicity tracer, and the super-solar enrichment of WASP-39 b points to substantial planetesimal accretion — a direct, if statistical, link between atmospheric chemistry and formation history [4][5].

### 4.2 Three-dimensional structure: rotation and limb asymmetry
Close-in giants are tidally locked, with permanent day–night temperature contrasts exceeding 1000 K. JWST has begun resolving this heterogeneity *within* single transits. Two transits of the ultrahot Jupiter WASP-121 b observed with NIRSpec and NIRISS exhibit asymmetric light curves caused by the planet's rotation during transit: as progressively hotter longitudes rotate into view, CO absorption strengthens while H$_2$O absorption weakens — the spectroscopic signature of thermal dissociation of water on the hotter evening terminator [2]. Complementary work on WASP-39 b found a $\sim$20 s wavelength dependence of the central transit time, consistent with differing morning/evening terminator compositions [5].

### 4.3 Sub-Neptunes: the degeneracy that will not die
Sub-Neptunes ($1.5$–$4\,R_\oplus$) are the galaxy's most common planets and its least understood. Their transmission spectra, obtained predominantly with NIRSpec/G395H at 3–5 μm, show a striking diversity: CH$_4$ at 3.3 μm, CO$_2$ at 4.3 μm, and in some cases CS$_2$ near 4.6 μm, with feature amplitudes spanning an order of magnitude even among planets of similar temperature [1]. The interpretive crisis is the three-way degeneracy of Section 2.2: a muted spectrum may indicate a high-$\mu$ envelope, a high cloud deck, or photochemical haze. K2-18 b — an $8.6\,M_\oplus$, $2.6\,R_\oplus$ planet in its M2.5V star's habitable zone — is the archetype. Its JWST NIRISS+NIRSpec spectrum (0.8–5.3 μm) yielded CH$_4$ at $\sim$5$\sigma$ and CO$_2$ at $\sim$3$\sigma$ in an H$_2$-rich atmosphere, with a stringent non-detection of NH$_3$ and only an upper limit on H$_2$O [6]. The original analysis argued that the $\sim$1% CH$_4$ and $\sim$1% CO$_2$ abundances, together with NH$_3$ depletion, favor a **hycean** scenario — a liquid-water ocean beneath a thin hydrogen envelope — over a deep mini-Neptune atmosphere [6]. But reanalyses of the same data have challenged the CO$_2$ significance and shown it to be sensitive to reduction choices [7], while interior models demonstrate that water-rich envelopes with deep atmospheres can reproduce the spectrum without any ocean.

### 4.4 Emission spectroscopy and phase curves
Transmission probes the terminator; emission probes the dayside. Secondary-eclipse and phase-curve spectroscopy with MIRI measures the planet's own thermal emission, constraining the dayside temperature–pressure profile and molecular abundances independently of the limb geometry. A landmark analysis simultaneously retrieved four orbital-phase-resolved MIRI/LRS emission spectra of the hot Jupiter WASP-43 b with a parametric 2D temperature model, finding H$_2$O at 6.5$\sigma$, NH$_3$ at 4$\sigma$ — the first statistically significant ammonia detection in a hot Jupiter emission spectrum — and CO at 3.1$\sigma$, with CH$_4$ undetected; the joint fit constrained the metallicity to 0.6–6.5$\times$ solar and C/O to 0.6–0.9 [3].

### 4.5 Biosignature gases: the ladder of evidence
The most consequential — and most contested — application of transmission spectroscopy is the search for **biosignatures**: gases whose sustained presence requires a biological flux. The original K2-18 b analysis reported a tentative ($\sim$2$\sigma$) signal of dimethyl sulfide (DMS, (CH$_3$)$_2$S), a gas produced on Earth overwhelmingly by marine phytoplankton, with no known efficient abiotic production pathway at temperate conditions [6]. Follow-up MIRI transmission spectroscopy (5–12 μm) found the data better fit by DMS and/or dimethyl disulfide (DMDS) than by a featureless model, but a systematic agnostic search over 661 candidate molecules showed that only DMS consistently clears $\ln B \ge 2$ across datasets and retrieval codes — a threshold below the conventional moderate-evidence line, and one that depends on detector-offset assumptions in the near-infrared [7]. Independent reanalyses have found no statistically significant evidence for either gas [1]. This thesis advocates a formal **ladder of evidence** for any biosignature claim: (i) robust molecular detection ($\ln B \gg 5$, multi-instrument, multi-pipeline); (ii) abundance quantification inconsistent with known abiotic fluxes, including photochemical false positives of the kind SO$_2$ on WASP-39 b exemplifies [4]; (iii) environmental context (liquid water, energy source, chemical disequilibrium); and (iv) exclusion of instrumental and stellar systematics. K2-18 b currently clears, at best, a fraction of rung (i).

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

> **Theorem:** *Scale-height detectability bound.* Consider a transiting planet with radius $R_p$, stellar radius $R_*$, and an isothermal atmosphere of scale height $H \ll R_p$. Let $\sigma_D$ be the per-bin transit-depth uncertainty. Then a molecular band spanning $N_H$ scale heights in effective altitude is detectable at signal-to-noise $\mathcal{S}$ only if
> $$\mathcal{S} \;\approx\; \frac{2 N_H H R_p}{R_*^2\,\sigma_D}\;\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{bin}}}\;\gtrsim\;5,$$
> where $N_{\mathrm{bin}}$ is the number of spectral bins across the band. *Corollary:* for fixed instrumental precision, detectability scales as $T/(\mu g R_*^2)$ — hot, low-gravity, hydrogen-rich planets around small stars are exponentially favored, which is why the JWST census is dominated by hot Jupiters and warm sub-Neptunes around M dwarfs.

*Proof sketch.* The transit depth at wavelength $\lambda$ is $\delta_\lambda = (R_{p,\lambda}/R_*)^2$ with $R_{p,\lambda} = R_p + N_H(\lambda)H$ the effective radius where chord optical depth reaches unity. Expanding, $\delta_\lambda - \delta \approx 2R_p N_H H/R_*^2$. Dividing by $\sigma_D$ and co-adding $N_{\mathrm{bin}}$ independent bins in quadrature gives the stated $\mathcal{S}$. Substituting $H = k_BT/(\mu m_H g)$ yields the corollary. ∎

The empirical record of the JWST era conforms quantitatively to this bound:

| Planet | Class | $T_{\mathrm{eq}}$ (K) | Key result | Approx. significance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| WASP-39 b | Hot Saturn | 1100 | SO$_2$ photochemical product; $Z \approx 7$–$10\times$ solar | 4.5$\sigma$ (G395H) [4][5] |
| WASP-121 b | Ultrahot Jupiter | 2350 | Rotational transit asymmetry; CO/H$_2$O terminator gradient | Asymmetric light curve [2] |
| WASP-43 b | Hot Jupiter | 1400 | NH$_3$ in emission; multi-phase retrieval; C/O 0.6–0.9 | 4$\sigma$ (NH$_3$) [3] |
| K2-18 b | Temperate sub-Neptune | 280 | CH$_4$ + CO$_2$; NH$_3$ depleted; DMS contested | 5$\sigma$ (CH$_4$); $\sim$3$\sigma$ (CO$_2$) [6][7] |
| TOI-270 d | Warm sub-Neptune | 350 | CH$_4$, CO$_2$, CS$_2$; high-$\mu$ envelope | Multi-species [1] |
| GJ 1214 b | Warm sub-Neptune | 550 | Flat/featureless; high-$\mu$ or cloudy | Non-detection [1] |

## 6 Limitations
The results above must be read against serious and incompletely resolved limitations. **(1) Stellar contamination.** For M-dwarf hosts, unocculted spots and faculae imprint wavelength-dependent transit-depth biases of tens of ppm — the same amplitude as the planetary signal — and current corrections rely on stellar models that are themselves uncertain [1][7]. **(2) One-dimensional retrievals of three-dimensional atmospheres.** As WASP-121 b demonstrates, terminator inhomogeneities are now *resolved* [2]; 1D retrievals applied to such data return biased abundances, and 2D/3D frameworks remain computationally expensive [8]. **(3) Line-list incompleteness.** Opacities for many sulfur, phosphorus, and complex organic species are incomplete at high temperatures, so non-detections may reflect missing data rather than absent molecules. **(4) The cloud/haze/$\mu$ degeneracy.** No existing observation of a sub-Neptune uniquely breaks it; claims that do should be treated as model-dependent [1]. **(5) Biosignature false positives.** Photochemistry manufactures SO$_2$ at ppm levels on WASP-39 b [4]; analogous abiotic routes for candidate biosignatures are poorly mapped, so rung (ii) of the ladder remains largely theoretical. **(6) Statistical fragility.** Several headline significances — CO$_2$ and DMS on K2-18 b — shift under alternative reductions [7], a reminder that $\ln B$ inherits every assumption of the pipeline.

## 7 Conclusion
Transmission and emission spectroscopy have, in four years of JWST operations, matured from a technique of heroic single detections into a statistical science of planetary populations. The scale-height bound of Section 5 explains *which* atmospheres we can see; Bayesian retrieval [8] explains *what* we may claim about them; and the case studies — SO$_2$ photochemistry on WASP-39 b [4][5], rotational asymmetry on WASP-121 b [2], multi-phase emission retrieval on WASP-43 b [3], and the hycean debate around K2-18 b [6][7] — show the method working at the edge of its systematics. The grand prize, a credible biosignature, remains unclaimed, and rightly so: the ladder of evidence is steep by design. But the trajectory is unambiguous. With JWST's continued census, the ARIEL survey mission's thousand-planet sample, and extremely large telescopes bringing high-resolution ground-based spectroscopy to temperate rocky planets, the coming decade will push the detectability bound down the mean-molecular-weight ladder — from hydrogen-rich giants, through water-rich sub-Neptunes, toward the thin, high-$\mu$ atmospheres of genuinely Earth-like worlds. When the first rung of that final ladder is climbed, the formalism developed here will be what tells us whether we are looking at chemistry, or at life.
