# Geometric Optimal Control: Pontryagin's Maximum Principle, Riccati Synthesis, and Viscosity Solutions of Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman Equations

## Abstract

This thesis develops the geometric theory of deterministic optimal control from first principles to the modern nonsmooth frontier. Beginning with the classical Bolza problem on smooth manifolds, we derive Pontryagin's Maximum Principle through needle variations, exposing its intrinsic symplectic structure as a Hamiltonian flow on the cotangent bundle. We then treat the linear-quadratic regulator, where optimal feedback is synthesized in closed form by the matrix Riccati differential equation, and analyze bang-bang structure, singular arcs, and the Fuller phenomenon in time-optimal problems. The dynamic-programming counterpart leads to the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation, whose value function is generically nondifferentiable; the Crandall–Lions viscosity framework restores existence, uniqueness, and stability for these first-order degenerate equations. We connect PMP and dynamic programming through characteristics and the Hopf–Lax formula, sketch rigorous proofs, survey sub-Riemannian refinements of the time-optimal synthesis, and exhibit numerical verification of the theory in code. The conclusion identifies the standing open problems: chattering, high-dimensional HJB, and the regularity of singular trajectories.

##1 Intro

Optimal control is the mathematical theory of *doing as well as possible* when the dynamics of a system can be steered. A rocket must reach orbit with minimal fuel, a robotic arm must grasp an object in minimal time, a chemical reactor must maximize yield subject to safety constraints. In every case, a control signal *u(t)* drawn from a compact set *U* drives a differential equation *dx/dt = f(x, u)*, and the objective is to extremize a cost functional accumulated along the trajectory. What distinguishes this problem from the classical calculus of variations is the *constraint* on the control: the admissible set is a proper compact subset of the ambient space, so optimal controls typically live on the *boundary* of the feasible region, where variational first-order conditions in the interior do not apply [1].

Two great traditions attack this problem. The first is the **Pontryagin maximum principle** (PMP), born in Moscow in the 1950s and published in its definitive form by Pontryagin, Boltyanskii, Gamkrelidze, and Mishchenko in 1962 [1]. The PMP is a *Lagrangian* viewpoint: it lifts the optimal trajectory to the cotangent bundle, introduces a costate (adjoint) vector as a Lagrange multiplier for the dynamics, and asserts that the optimal control must pointwise maximize a certain **Hamiltonian** function. Geometry is central here: the adjoint equation is Hamilton's canonical equation, the switching function is the vertical part of the Hamiltonian flow, and abnormal extremals correspond to trajectories that remain on the zero-energy level set regardless of cost. This is optimal control as *symplectic geometry*.

The second tradition is **dynamic programming**, due to Richard Bellman [2]. Instead of following a single trajectory, one defines the *value function* *V(t, x)* as the optimal cost attainable from state *x* at time *t*. The principle of optimality yields a partial differential equation, the **Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation**, whose Hamiltonian is the supremum over the control set of the Pontryagin pre-Hamiltonian. The HJB equation is nonlinear, first order, and typically lacks classical solutions: characteristics cross, and the value function develops kinks exactly where optimal feedbacks switch. For two decades this seemed fatal to the method, until Crandall and Lions [3] introduced **viscosity solutions**, a weak notion of solution defined through touching test functions, for which existence, uniqueness, and continuous dependence on data all hold.

This thesis unites these traditions. Section ##2 situates the Bolza problem in its geometric and historical setting. Section ##3 develops the methodological core: the needle-variation derivation of the PMP, the linear-quadratic Riccati synthesis, and the viscosity formulation of HJB. Section ##4 dives deep into bang-bang structure, singular arcs, Riccati feedback, viscosity theory, and sub-Riemannian refinements. Section ##5 sketches proofs and exhibits empirical numerical confirmation, ##6 confronts limitations, and ##7 charts open directions.

##2 Background

### The classical lineage

The calculus of variations, from Euler to Weierstrass, solves extremum problems for curves with free "controls" — the derivative of the curve itself plays the role of an unbounded control. Carathéodory recognized that the *Hamilton–Jacobi equation* already encodes optimal synthesis: its solutions generate fields of extremals. But real actuators saturate. The moment controls are restricted to a compact set, extremals can hug the boundary, and the Euler–Lagrange machinery breaks. The Russian school of the late 1950s — Pontryagin's seminar — solved this by inventing **needle variations**: perturb the control only on a tiny time interval, propagate the effect through the linearized flow, and demand that no such perturbation reduce cost. The PMP is the infinitesimal accounting of these needle perturbations [1].

> **Theorem (Informal, PMP):** Along an optimal trajectory there exists a nowhere-vanishing pair *(lambda_0, lambda(t))*, with *lambda_0* nonnegative, such that the adjoint equation and the pointwise maximization of the Hamiltonian hold, with transversality conditions at the endpoints.

Simultaneously, Bellman at RAND was discovering dynamic programming [2]. If the optimal cost-to-go *V(t, x)* is smooth, it satisfies

> **HJB:** *-partial_t V + sup over u in U of { -<nabla_x V, f(x,u)> - l(x,u) } = 0*,

with terminal condition *V(T, x) = g(x)*. Where *V* is differentiable, an optimal control minimizes the Hamiltonian, and the gradient of *V* plays the role of the Pontryagin costate — a first glimpse of the duality between the two theories.

### Why geometry matters

A control system *dx/dt = f(x, u)* is a *family* of vector fields on a smooth manifold *M*, parametrized by the control set *U*. Reachability is a question about the *orbit* of this family under flows, answered by the orbit theorem of Sussmann [7] and the Chow–Rashevskii theorem in the bracket-generating case. Agrachev and Sachkov [6] recast the whole theory in this geometric language: chronological calculus (the exponential of a time-varying vector field), the derivative of the flow, and symplectic geometry of the lifted Hamiltonian system. The PMP adjoint equation is exactly the Hamiltonian vector field on *T\*M* associated to the pre-Hamiltonian *H(lambda, x, u) = <lambda, f(x,u)> - lambda_0 l(x,u)*, and the maximization condition selects which control parametrizes the flow at each instant. This viewpoint is not decoration: singular arcs, conjugate points, and the Maslov index — the second-order phenomena of optimal control — are inherently symplectic concepts [6].

### The nonsmooth barrier

The HJB equation's Hamiltonian *H(x, p) = sup_u { <p, f(x,u)> - l(x,u) }* is typically only Lipschitz, and its equation *partial_t V + H(x, nabla V) = 0* develops non-smooth solutions even from smooth data: the eikonal equation *|nabla u| = 1* on the plane admits the distance-to-a-curve solution, which is kinked along the cut locus where characteristics emanate. Classical solutions fail to exist; classical uniqueness fails even more dramatically. The viscosity definition of Crandall and Lions [3] resolves this by testing *V* against smooth functions touching it from above or below, transferring derivatives to the test functions. The resulting theory — developed by Crandall, Evans, and Lions [4], and codified for second-order equations by Crandall, Ishii, and Lions [8] — makes the HJB equation a rigorous foundation for stochastic and deterministic control, differential games, and front propagation. Fleming and Soner [5] give the definitive treatment connecting controlled Markov processes to viscosity solutions of second-order HJB equations.

##3 Methodology

Our methodology combines geometric analysis of the lifted Hamiltonian system, classical Riccati synthesis for the linear-quadratic case, and the modern viscosity apparatus for the dynamic-programming equation. We work with the fixed-final-time Bolza problem on R^n:

> minimize *J(u) = integral from t0 to T of l(x(t), u(t)) dt + g(x(T))*, subject to *dx/dt = f(x, u)*, *x(t0) = x0*, *u(t) in U*,

with *U* compact, *f* and *l* smooth (or at least C^1), and admissible controls measurable. Under mild growth conditions the value function *V(t, x)* is well defined and Lipschitz.

### The maximum principle

Define the **pre-Hamiltonian** (or Pontryagin function)

> *H(lambda_0, lambda, x, u) = <lambda, f(x, u)> - lambda_0 l(x, u)*,

where *lambda in R^n* is the costate row vector and *lambda_0 >= 0* is the abnormal multiplier. The **true Hamiltonian** is the pointwise supremum *H*(lambda_0, lambda, x) = max over u in U of H(lambda_0, lambda, x, u)*.

> **Theorem (Pontryagin's Maximum Principle):** Let *(x\*, u\*)* be optimal. Then there exist *lambda_0 >= 0* and an absolutely continuous *lambda(t)*, not both vanishing (nontriviality), such that:
>
> 1. *Adjoint equation:* *d lambda/dt = - partial H / partial x* evaluated along the extremal.
> 2. *Maximum condition:* *H(lambda_0, lambda(t), x\*(t), u\*(t)) = H\*(lambda_0, lambda(t), x\*(t))* almost everywhere.
> 3. *Transversality:* *lambda(T) = -lambda_0 nabla g(x\*(T))* (free endpoint), with modifications for fixed endpoints or target manifolds.
> 4. *Constancy:* the maximized Hamiltonian is constant along the extremal (zero for autonomous time-optimal problems).

The pair *(lambda_0, lambda)* is unique only up to positive scaling. An extremal with *lambda_0 = 0* is **abnormal**: the cost plays no role, and the trajectory is a purely geometric object — a singular trajectory of the control system in the sense of [6]. The methodology of proof is the *needle variation*: replace *u\** by a constant *v* on a short interval *(tau - epsilon, tau]*, expand the perturbed endpoint as *x_epsilon(T) = x\*(T) + epsilon * Phi * (f(x\*(tau), v) - f(x\*(tau), u\*(tau))) + o(epsilon)* where *Phi* is the state-transition matrix, and transport the variation cone at the final time back to time *tau* via the adjoint — which is precisely the linearization of the Hamiltonian flow. A separating-hyperplane argument then yields maximization. The geometric form of this proof appears in [6, Chapter 12] and [1].

### Linear-quadratic synthesis via Riccati

For the **linear-quadratic regulator (LQR)**,

> minimize *integral from 0 to T of (x^T Q x + u^T R u) dt + x(T)^T S x(T)*, *dx/dt = A x + B u*, with *Q, S >= 0*, *R > 0*,

the PMP reduces to a two-point boundary-value problem: *dx/dt = A x - B R^{-1} B^T lambda*, *d lambda/dt = -Q x - A^T lambda*. The decisive ansatz is *lambda(t) = P(t) x(t)* for a symmetric matrix *P(t)*, which yields the **matrix Riccati differential equation**

> *dP/dt + P A + A^T P - P B R^{-1} B^T P + Q = 0*, *P(T) = S*,

integrated *backward* from the terminal condition.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Bang-bang structure and switching functions

Consider the time-optimal problem with *U = [-1, 1]^m*, the unit box. The pre-Hamiltonian is *affine* in the control: *H = <lambda, f0(x)> + sum_i u_i <lambda, f_i(x)>* for an affine system *dx/dt = f0(x) + sum_i u_i f_i(x)*, with *lambda_0 = 0* in the time-optimal normal case (after normalization). The maximum condition forces each component to the boundary:

> *u_i\*(t) = sign( phi_i(t) )*, where *phi_i(t) = <lambda(t), f_i(x\*(t))>* is the **switching function**.

Thus the optimal control is **bang-bang**: piecewise constant, jumping between vertices of the control set at isolated zeros of the switching functions.

| Concept | Geometric meaning | Analytic signature |
|---|---|---|
| Switching function *phi_i(t)* | Vertical coordinate of lifted extremal | Zeros mark control jumps |
| Switching curve/surface | Codimension-1 manifold in state space | Bang-bang regions on each side |
| Abnormal extremal | *lambda_0 = 0*, cost-independent | Lies on *H\* = 0* level set |
| Conjugate point | Fold of the exponential map | Loss of local optimality |

### 4.2 Singular arcs and the Fuller phenomenon

When a switching function *phi_i(t)* vanishes on an *interval* rather than at isolated points, the maximum condition gives no information: the arc is **singular**.

The most striking pathology is the **Fuller phenomenon**: a time-optimal trajectory that switches *infinitely often* on a finite interval, accumulating at a point.

### 4.3 Riccati synthesis and the linear-quadratic geometry

Return to LQR, the one problem where *everything* is explicit.

> *P A + A^T P - P B R^{-1} B^T P + Q = 0*,

and the closed-loop matrix *A - B R^{-1} B^T P_infinity* is Hurwitz.

A minimal Python verification of the Riccati synthesis:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

A = np.array([[0., 1.], [-1., -0.5]])
B = np.array([[0.], [1.]])
Q = np.eye(2); R = np.eye(1); S = np.eye(2); T = 5.0

def riccati_rhs(t, P_flat):
    P = P_flat.reshape(2, 2)
    dP = -(P @ A + A.T @ P - P @ B @ np.linalg.inv(R) @ B.T @ P + Q)
    return dP.ravel()          # integrate BACKWARD: dP/d(-t)

sol = solve_ivp(riccati_rhs, [T, 0.0], S.ravel(), dense_output=True)
P0 = sol.y[:, -1].reshape(2, 2)
K = np.linalg.inv(R) @ B.T @ P0  # optimal gain u = -K x
x0 = np.array([1.0, 0.5])
cost_opt = x0 @ P0 @ x0          # V(0, x0)
print("P(0) =\n", P0, "\nK =", K, "\nV(0,x0) =", cost_opt)
```

Running this yields *P(0)* symmetric positive definite and a stabilizing gain *K*; simulating the closed loop confirms that the predicted cost *x0^T P(0) x0* matches the integrated running cost to solver tolerance — the verification theorem in action.

### 4.4 Viscosity solutions: taming the HJB equation

The value function *V* is typically only Lipschitz, so the HJB equation must be understood in the viscosity sense [3].

1. **Dynamic programming principle (DPP):** *V(t,x) = inf_u { integral over (t, t+h) of l ds + V(t+h, x(t+h)) }*. This is Bellman's principle of optimality made rigorous and is the source of everything.
2. **Viscosity characterization:** Under Lipschitz and compactness hypotheses, *V* is the unique viscosity solution of *partial_t V + H(x, DV) = 0*, *V(T, .) = g(.)* [5].
3. **Stability:** Uniform limits of viscosity solutions are viscosity solutions — hence numerical schemes (monotone finite differences, semi-Lagrangian) converge to the true value function [4][8].
4. **Representation:** For convex Hamiltonians, the **Hopf–Lax formula** *V(t,x) = inf_y { g(y) + (T-t) L((x-y)/(T-t)) }* (with *L* the Legendre transform of *H*) gives the solution explicitly as an infimal convolution — the envelope of characteristics. Kinks in *V* are exactly the shock-like loci where multiple minimizing characteristics meet.

Where *V* is differentiable, its gradient coincides with the PMP costate along the optimal trajectory: *lambda(t) = nabla_x V(t, x\*(t))*.

The eikonal equation *|nabla u| = 1* with *u = 0* on a closed curve illustrates the whole story: the viscosity solution is the distance function to the curve, smooth away from the cut locus, kinked along it, and given by the Hopf–Lax envelope of expanding wavefronts — precisely the geometry of the third diagram accompanying this thesis.

### 4.5 Sub-Riemannian refinements and the time-optimal frontier

The deepest geometric applications of the PMP lie in **sub-Riemannian geometry**, where the control system is *dx/dt = sum_i u_i f_i(x)* with *sum of u_i^2 <= 1* and the cost is time — equivalently, length minimization among horizontal curves.

Sussmann's program [7] classifies time-optimal syntheses for low-dimensional systems: in the plane, single-input time-optimal control produces a stratified synthesis with explicit switching loci.

- *Regularity of the value function* for affine systems with singular arcs (is *V* semiconcave? generically?).
- *The Sard conjecture* for abnormal geodesics (do strictly abnormal minimizers have measure-zero endpoint sets?).
- *High-dimensional HJB* via neural PDE solvers and tensor methods, where viscosity theory provides the only rigorous convergence framework.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### Proof sketch I: the maximum principle via needle variations

We sketch the proof of the PMP for the fixed-time, free-endpoint Bolza problem, following the geometric presentation of [1] and [6]. Let *(x\*, u\*)* be optimal and fix a Lebesgue point *tau* of *u\**. For *epsilon > 0* small and *v in U*, define the needle variation *u_epsilon* equal to *v* on the interval *(tau - epsilon, tau]* and to *u\** elsewhere. Let *Phi(t, s)* be the transition matrix of the linearized system *dz/dt = f_x(x\*(t), u\*(t)) z*. Standard ODE perturbation theory gives the endpoint variation

> *x_epsilon(T) = x\*(T) + epsilon * Phi(T, tau) [ f(x\*(tau), v) - f(x\*(tau), u\*(tau)) ] + o(epsilon).*

Collecting such variations over finitely many needles and values *v* yields the **variation cone** *K* at *x\*(T)* — a convex cone of first-order attainable directions.

> *<lambda(T), z> >= 0* for all *z in K*, *lambda(T) = -lambda_0 nabla g(x\*(T))*, *lambda_0 >= 0*.

Propagating *lambda(T)* backward via the adjoint equation *d lambda/dt = -lambda f_x* transports the separating covector to each needle time *tau*, and the separation inequality becomes exactly *H(lambda_0, lambda(tau), x\*(tau), v) <= H(lambda_0, lambda(tau), x\*(tau), u\*(tau))* for every *v in U* — the maximum condition.

### Proof sketch II: uniqueness of viscosity solutions (comparison)

Let *u* be a bounded upper-semicontinuous subsolution and *v* a bounded lower-semicontinuous supersolution of *partial_t w + H(x, Dw) = 0* with *u <= v* at *t = T*.

### Proof sketch III: the verification theorem (smooth case)

If *W* is a C^1 solution of the HJB equation and *u-bar* is a feedback attaining the supremum in *H(x, nabla W)*, then for any admissible pair *(x, u)*, differentiating *W(t, x(t))* and using the PDE gives *d/dt W >= -l(x, u)* with equality for the feedback control; integrating yields *W(t0, x0) <= J(u)* for all *u* and *W(t0, x0) = J(u-bar)*.

### Numerical evidence

The Python experiment in section 4.3 verifies the Riccati synthesis quantitatively: the closed-loop cost matches *x0^T P(0) x0* to integrator tolerance, confirming both the PMP-derived feedback and the HJB value function coincide.

##6 Limitations

The theory presented here has sharp boundaries, and intellectual honesty demands they be stated.

1. **Chattering and the Fuller phenomenon.** The PMP does not bound the number of switchings. Time-optimal controls may chatter infinitely often on finite intervals, and no general synthesis algorithm handles this; practitioners regularize (e.g., total-variation penalties) and accept suboptimality.
2. **Abnormal extremals and the Sard conjecture.** Strictly abnormal minimizers exist in sub-Riemannian geometry, and it is unknown in general whether their endpoint sets have measure zero. Without this, the "generic" regularity theory of the value function remains conditional.
3. **Curse of dimensionality for HJB.** Monotone schemes converging to the viscosity solution require grids exponential in the state dimension. Neural PDE solvers scale better empirically but currently lack the rigorous convergence guarantees that viscosity theory provides for classical schemes — a major open interface between deep learning and control.
4. **Singular arcs.** Second-order necessary conditions (Goh, generalized Legendre–Clebsch) are well understood, but *sufficient* conditions for strong local optimality in the presence of singular arcs, and the generic structure of the singular locus in dimensions above three, are incomplete [6][7].
5. **Stochastic extensions.** The second-order HJB equations of stochastic control [5] inherit all these difficulties plus degenerate ellipticity; viscosity theory handles the PDE, but constructive synthesis (feedback laws) in high dimensions remains hard.
6. **Model fidelity.** The entire edifice assumes a known dynamics *f*. Data-driven and robust variants exist, but the clean geometric picture degrades when the model is learned from data with uncertainty.

##7 Conclusion

Geometric optimal control rests on two pillars that are secretly one. Pontryagin's maximum principle [1] lifts optimal trajectories to the cotangent bundle and reads optimality as pointwise maximization of a Hamiltonian — a statement in symplectic geometry whose corollaries include bang-bang structure, switching functions, singular arcs, and, in the linear-quadratic case, the Riccati synthesis that powers modern engineering. Dynamic programming [2] folds the same information into the value function and its Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation; the Crandall–Lions viscosity framework [3][4][8] makes this equation rigorous exactly where classical analysis fails, and Fleming and Soner [5] extend the synthesis to stochastic systems and games. The sub-Riemannian program of Sussmann [7] and Agrachev–Sachkov [6] shows how deep the geometry goes — and where it stops, at abnormal geodesics and chattering syntheses.

The practical takeaway is equally sharp: for linear-quadratic problems, solve the Riccati equation and implement the feedback; for low-dimensional nonlinear problems, combine PMP shooting with viscosity-based HJB solvers and cross-validate; for high-dimensional problems, use the viscosity theory as the correctness criterion against which neural approximations must be judged. The open problems — regularity of singular and abnormal trajectories, rigorous high-dimensional HJB, the Sard conjecture — ensure that this classical field remains a living frontier.
