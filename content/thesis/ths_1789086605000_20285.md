# Linear Logic and Session Types: Resource-Sensitive Typing for Concurrent Protocols

## Abstract

Session types discipline concurrent programs by assigning protocols, rather than mere data shapes, to channels. Their deepest foundation is *linear logic*: Girard's resource-sensitive refinement of classical logic [1], in which hypotheses are consumed exactly once unless explicitly marked reusable. This thesis develops the arc from linear logic to modern session-type theory. We reconstruct the sequent-calculus machinery — multiplicative and additive connectives, the exponentials `!` and `?`, involutive duality — and show how it yields a Curry–Howard correspondence for concurrency: Caires and Pfenning's intuitionistic interpretation of propositions as sessions, with processes as proofs and communication as cut elimination [2], refined by Wadler's classical formulation [3]. We trace binary session types from Honda's original dyadic theory [4] through structured communication calculi [5], then generalize to multiparty protocols via Honda, Yoshida, and Carbone's global types with endpoint projection [6]. We prove the central metatheoretic properties — session fidelity, communication safety, progress, and deadlock freedom — explain why linearity governs deadlock, survey mechanization and practical embeddings, and identify open frontiers: asynchronous subtyping, failure handling, and the limits of logic as specification medium [7].

## 1. Introduction

Concurrent programs fail in ways sequential programs do not. A misbehaving channel endpoint can deadlock a whole system, corrupt a protocol state machine, or leave a peer blocked forever on a message that never arrives. The *session type* discipline addresses this by lifting types from descriptions of data to descriptions of *conversations*. A channel of type

```
!Int . ?Bool . end
```

is a contract obliging its owner to send an integer, then receive a boolean, then close — and obliging the peer to perform the dual actions in lockstep.

But why should linearity — the resource discipline of linear logic — be the right foundation for such contracts? The answer is that a protocol step is inherently *ephemeral*: sending a message consumes the obligation to send it, and what remains is the continuation. This is precisely the logical structure of linear implication. Girard's linear logic [1] refined classical logic by making the structural rules of weakening and contraction — the silent duplication and discarding of assumptions — unavailable except under explicit modalities. Propositions become resources: used once, then gone. Channels in a session-typed process behave exactly this way, and this is no coincidence.

The decisive technical step was a genuine Curry–Howard correspondence for concurrency: Caires and Pfenning [2] showed that intuitionistic linear propositions *are* session types, proofs *are* processes, and cut elimination *is* communication. Wadler [3] recast the correspondence in classical linear logic, unifying it with the binary session types inaugurated by Honda [4] and developed for structured communication by Honda, Vasconcelos, and Kubo [5]. Finally, Honda, Yoshida, and Carbone [6] generalized the binary theory to *multiparty* sessions, where a *global type* describes the whole conversation and is *projected* onto each participant's local view.

Section 2 reconstructs the background; Section 3 the logical methodology; Section 4 the theory; Section 5 the metatheoretic results and empirical embeddings; Section 6 the limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Linear logic: resources, not truths

Classical logic is a logic of *truth*: from `A` we may freely derive `A ∧ A` (contraction) or ignore `A` entirely (weakening). Girard observed that this freedom erases a computational distinction that matters: whether a resource is used once, many times, or never [1]. In linear logic, the judgment `Γ ⊢ C` treats `Γ` as a multiset of *resources*, each consumed exactly once by the proof. The connectives split into multiplicative and additive families:

| Family | Connective | Reading | Session intuition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Multiplicative | `A ⊗ B` | both, split resources | send a channel, continue |
| Multiplicative | `A ⊸ B` | consume `A` to get `B` | receive a channel, continue |
| Multiplicative | `1` | empty resource | terminated session |
| Additive | `A & B` | choose *by the environment* | external choice (branch) |
| Additive | `A ⊕ B` | choose *by the proof* | internal choice (select) |
| Additive | `⊤` / `0` | vacuous / impossible | — |
| Exponential | `!A` | reusable `A` | replicated server |
| Exponential | `?A` | consumable-any-number | client pool |

Linear negation `(·)⊥` is involutive (`A⊥⊥ = A`) with De Morgan dualities across the multiplicative/additive divide: `(A ⊗ B)⊥ = A⊥ ⅋ B⊥`, `(A & B)⊥ = A⊥ ⊕ B⊥`, and `(!A)⊥ = ?A⊥`.

The structural rules are the crux: exchange is unrestricted, but weakening and contraction apply *only* to formulas of the form `!A` (or dually `?A`). Everything else is strictly linear.

Proof nets, Girard's parallel syntax for proofs [1], represent derivations as graphs whose correctness criteria guarantee sequentializability — exposing the *parallel* structure latent in a proof, a preview of the idea that a typed process network is itself a proof.

### 2.2 The π-calculus and binary sessions

Honda's seminal insight [4] was that the *sequence* of interactions along a single channel — a *session* — deserves its own type. The structured communication calculus [5] showed that session types integrate with realistic language primitives: session initiation, labeled branching and selection, and delegation (sending a live channel over another channel).

Binary session types have the following grammar:

```
S ::= !T . S        (send a value of type T, continue as S)
    | ?T . S        (receive a value of type T, continue as S)
    | ⊕{li : Si}    (select label li, continue as Si)
    | &{li : Si}    (offer branches li, continue as Si)
    | μX . S        (recursive session)
    | end           (terminated)
```

*Duality* is the central relation: the dual `S̄` swaps send with receive and selection with branching. Two endpoints of the same channel must carry dual types — the type-level expression of the physical fact that one party's output is another's input. The *session fidelity* theorem states that reduction preserves the session typing of the remaining protocol.

### 2.3 From Abramsky to Caires–Pfenning

Abramsky's "proofs as processes" program interpreted linear proofs as π-calculus processes, but through an encoding that did not preserve cut elimination as communication. Caires and Pfenning [2] aligned the logical and operational structures exactly, using *intuitionistic* linear logic with a judgment

```
Γ ; Δ ⊢ P :: x : A
```

meaning: process `P`, using shared channels `Γ` and linear channels `Δ`, *provides* session `A` along channel `x`. The Curry–Howard reading is literal: `A ⊸ B` on the right is input, `A ⊗ B` on the right is (bound) output, `1` is inaction, `!A` is replication, `A & B` is external choice, `A ⊕ B` is internal choice. Wadler's reformulation [3] used classical linear logic with one-sided sequents, where duality alone determines communication direction.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is *logical reconstruction*: derive the typing rules from the sequent calculus and inherit the metatheory from proof theory:

1. **Fix a logic.** Intuitionistic linear logic (Caires–Pfenning) or classical linear logic (Wadler). The choice determines whether communication direction is read from sequent position or from duality.
2. **Assign process terms to proofs.** Each inference rule becomes a process constructor. The right rule for `⊗` becomes bound output `(νy)x⟨y⟩.(P | Q)`, splitting the linear context `Δ` into `Δ1, Δ2` — mirroring the multiplicative splitting of resources.
3. **Interpret cut as parallel composition with restriction.** The cut rule composes a process providing `x : A` with one using `x : A`, hiding `x`. Cut elimination then *computes*: principal cuts reduce to communication steps.
4. **Read off the metatheory.** Subject reduction is cut admissibility in motion; progress and deadlock freedom follow from the property that a closed, well-typed process corresponds to a proof of `· ; · ⊢ P :: x : 1`, which can only reduce to inaction.

This methodology is falsifiable in a strong sense: any proposed extension (asynchronous communication, polymorphism, recursion) must correspond to a coherent logical extension, or the correspondence breaks and the metatheory must be re-established by hand. The multiparty theory [6] is the interesting boundary case: it has no single canonical logic, and its metatheory is proved directly on the type system — a fact we return to in Section 6.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Linear logic as a logic of protocol state

Consider the session type `!Int . ?Bool . end`: each communication consumes the head constructor and exposes the continuation — precisely the behavior of a linear hypothesis decomposed by left rules. The *ephemerality* of protocol state is linearity made operational.

The exponentials explain the one place where sessions admit reuse: *shared* channels. A channel of type `!A` in the unrestricted context `Γ` denotes a *replicated server*, each invocation spawning a fresh linear session of type `A`. The promotion rule

```
Γ ; · ⊢ P :: y : A
─────────────────── (!R)
Γ ; · ⊢ !x(y).P :: x : !A
```

requires the body to depend on *no* linear resources — a server cannot capture a one-shot channel, because that channel could not be duplicated across invocations.

> **Theorem:** (Server linearity). If `Γ ; · ⊢ !x(y).P :: x : !A` then every linear channel used by `P` is bound within the body of the replicated input. No free linear channel of the server can be aliased across sessions.

Duality is the logical content of *compatibility*. In classical linear logic, `⊢ P :: x : A` and `⊢ Q :: x : A⊥` compose by cut; in the intuitionistic reading, left and right rules conspire to the same effect. Duality is not an ad hoc relation on protocol syntax but the involutive negation of the underlying logic — which is why it enjoys the algebraic properties that make session-type metatheory tractable.

### 4.2 Binary session types: fidelity and safety

The classical binary theory [2, 3] is summarized by three theorems.

> **Theorem:** (Session fidelity). If `Γ ⊢ P ▷ Δ` and `P → Q`, then `Γ ⊢ Q ▷ Δ'` where `Δ'` advances the session types of the channels that communicated. Typing is preserved *and* the residual types describe exactly the remaining protocol.

> **Theorem:** (Communication safety). A well-typed process never exhibits a communication mismatch: no endpoint sends a value where a label is expected, no selection chooses an unoffered label, and arities always agree.

> **Theorem:** (Progress / deadlock freedom, binary case). A well-typed closed process is either inactive or can reduce. No well-typed process gets stuck waiting on a partner that will never speak.

The proofs are standard: fidelity by induction on reduction with a substitution lemma for delegation; safety as a corollary via canonical forms; progress because a stuck process would contain a cyclic dependency among linear channels, which linearity forbids in the binary case. *Deadlock freedom is a theorem of linearity*, not an extra check — the moment channels become shareable or sessions interleave across channels without discipline, the theorem can fail.

Subtyping extends the binary theory in the expected direction: `S <: T` when a channel of type `S` can safely be used where `T` is expected — the usual co/contravariant discipline. The logical view explains *why* subtyping is coherent: it corresponds to a notion of proof transformation, systematized in the survey of Hüttel et al. [7].

### 4.3 Propositions as sessions: πDILL and CP

We now give the correspondence itself. In the Caires–Pfenning system πDILL, the typing judgment `Γ ; Δ ⊢ P :: x : A` is read as a *sequent*, and the process constructors are proof terms: `A ⊸ B` on the right is input, `A ⊗ B` on the right is (bound) output, `1` is inaction, `!A` is replication, `A & B` is external choice, `A ⊕ B` is internal choice.

The operational semantics is *cut reduction*. The principal cut between an output and an input

```
(νx)( (νy)x⟨y⟩.(P | Q)  |  x(z).R )  →  (νx)(νy)( P | Q | R{y/z} )
```

is exactly the multiplicative cut-elimination step of linear logic, replayed as communication. The commuting conversions of the sequent calculus become the structural rearrangements of processes (scope extrusion, parallel associativity). This is the sense in which the correspondence is *tight*: not an encoding of proofs into processes, but an identity between proof normalization and process execution.

Wadler's CP [3] simplifies the picture with classical linear logic and one-sided sequents `⊢ P :: Δ`, where duality `A⊥` does all the directional work. The payoff is a direct, type-preserving translation from Gay and Vasconcelos's functional session language GV into CP — connecting the "standard" presentation of session types used by programmers with the logical presentation used by proof theorists. GV programs translate to CP processes that are *race-free and deadlock-free by construction*, because CP inherits cut elimination from classical linear logic.

The two systems differ instructively. Caires–Pfenning's intuitionistic reading enforces *locality* of shared channels: a received channel cannot be used for replicated input. Wadler's classical reading is more permissive — and therefore admits processes the intuitionistic system rejects.

### 4.4 Multiparty sessions: global types and projection

Binary sessions describe conversations between *two* parties. Real protocols — a buyer, a seller, and a shipper negotiating a purchase — involve *n* participants. Honda, Yoshida, and Carbone [6] generalized session types to the multiparty, asynchronous setting: describe the *whole* conversation once, as a *global type*, and *project* it onto each participant.

```
G ::= p → q : { li(Ti) . Gi }     -- p sends to q one of labels li with payload Ti
    | μX . G | X | end
```

A global type `Buyer → Seller : { quote(Int) . Seller → Buyer : { price(Int) . end } }` projects onto `Buyer` as `!quote(Int) . ?price(Int) . end` and onto `Seller` as the dual. Projection is defined by cases on whether the participant is the sender, the receiver, or a third party — in which case all branches must agree on its behavior, the *mergeability* condition.

> **Theorem:** (Multiparty session fidelity). If a multiparty process is well-typed against the projections of `G` and reduces, the reduct is well-typed against the projections of the residual global type.

> **Theorem:** (Deadlock freedom, multiparty). A well-typed multiparty session either reduces or all participants have terminated. Asynchronous communication is modeled by per-participant queues, and the type system guarantees queued messages are consumed in an order consistent with `G`.

These results earned the paper the ACM SIGPLAN Most Influential POPL Paper award for 2018 — recognition that multiparty session types had become the canonical typed foundation for structured multiparty communication, spawning the Scribble protocol description language and runtime monitoring frameworks.

In practice (as in the Rumpsteak line of work), duality becomes a type-level computation via associated types, while *linearity* — use-exactly-once — is approximated by affine move semantics, since safe Rust cannot express "must use". This affine/linear gap recurs in every practical embedding.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The metatheoretic core, summarized

| Result | Binary (sync) | πDILL / CP | Multiparty (async) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Session fidelity | Direct [2, 3] | Cut admissibility [4, 5] | Direct, via global types [6] |
| Communication safety | Corollary | Corollary | Corollary |
| Deadlock freedom | Direct | Logical (progress) [4, 5] | Direct, queue-based [6] |
| Liveness / termination | Not guaranteed | Not guaranteed | Not guaranteed |
| Subtyping soundness | Direct | Proof-theoretic | Active research [7] |

Two observations emerge. First, the logical systems *compress* the metatheory: fidelity, safety, and deadlock freedom become consequences of cut elimination plus a progress lemma. Second, the multiparty theory resists full logical reconstruction — its projection and mergeability conditions have no standard proof-theoretic counterpart. This asymmetry is the honest state of the field, surveyed by Hüttel et al. [7].

### 5.2 A worked example: the three-party purchase

Consider a buyer `B`, seller `S`, and shipper `H`:

```
G = B → S : { order(Item).
      S → B : { price(Int).
      B → S : { accept(). S → H : { ship(Addr). end },
                decline(). end } } }
```

Projection yields `B: !order(Item) . ?price(Int) . ⊕{accept(). end, decline(). end}`, `S` the dual with `!ship(Addr)` after `accept`, and `H: ?ship(Addr) . end`.

Note that `H`'s projection *skips* the choice it does not participate in — but only because both branches agree on `H`'s behavior (here, `decline` leaves `H` with nothing to do). If the branches disagreed about `H`, projection would be *undefined* and the global type rejected: the type system refuses protocols in which an uninvolved participant could detect the outcome of someone else's choice. This is causality made static.

Now consider the failure mode the theory prevents: the seller's implementation, after `accept`, forgets to notify the shipper — a *partial deadlock* where buyer and seller terminate and the shipper waits forever. In the typed setting, the seller's endpoint has residual type `!ship(Addr) . end`, a linear obligation dischargeable only by sending. The process is ill-typed if it drops the endpoint, and if well-typed it *must* eventually send. The bug is not caught by testing; it is *inexpressible*.

### 5.3 Mechanization and adoption

The logical correspondence has been mechanized in proof assistants, with cut elimination proved once and for all rather than re-proved per language. Practically, session types appear as libraries and language extensions — Haskell's `simple-sessions`, Rust's `session-types` and Rumpsteak (multiparty, with code generation from Scribble), Go and Java protocol frameworks, and the Scribble toolchain. The empirical pattern is consistent: binary embeddings are routine; multiparty embeddings require code generation from global types.

## 6. Limitations

**Asynchrony vs. the logical reading.** The propositions-as-sessions correspondence is proved for *synchronous* communication. Real systems are asynchronous: sends enqueue, receives dequeue. Asynchronous session theory and the multiparty queue semantics [6] recover the metatheory, but the tight Curry–Howard story becomes looser, and the "right" logic for asynchronous sessions remains open.

**Subtyping and polymorphism.** Precise subtyping for *asynchronous multiparty* sessions was only recently settled, and the algorithms are intricate. Polymorphism over session types exists, but its interaction with duality and linearity is delicate [7].

**Failure, timeouts, and partial participation.** Session types describe the happy path. Affine sessions (drop allowed, as in most Rust embeddings) model *cancellation* but not *failure detection*; exception-aware session calculi complicate duality, because a thrown exception is not the dual of anything. There is no consensus logical account of failure.

**The expressiveness ceiling of global types.** Projection requires mergeability: uninvolved participants must behave uniformly across branches. Some natural protocols — where a third party's behavior legitimately depends on a choice it did not make — are unprojectable. Extensions (parametric roles, dependent payloads) enlarge the class but re-open the metatheory.

**Logic is not a methodology for everything.** The logical reconstruction derives binary session discipline from proof theory, but multiparty projection, asynchronous subtyping, and failure handling are proved directly on operational semantics. The correspondence is a powerful organizing principle, not a universal one.

## 7. Conclusion

Linear logic taught us that assumptions are resources; session types taught us that protocols are resources too. From Girard [1] through Honda's dyadic sessions [4] and structured communication [5], to the Curry–Howard correspondence of Caires and Pfenning [2] and Wadler [3], and finally to the multiparty global types of Honda, Yoshida, and Carbone [6], a single idea has been refined for four decades: *a type can describe not what a channel carries, but what a conversation means* — and if the description is linear, the conversation cannot go wrong in the ways that matter.

The practical payoff is real: deadlock freedom, communication safety, and session fidelity as theorems rather than test suites; embeddings in Haskell, Rust, and Go; the Scribble toolchain generating verified multiparty code. The open problems mark the boundary of what resource-sensitive typing currently guarantees [7].

The deepest lesson is methodological. The propositions-as-sessions correspondence did not merely *justify* session types; it *explained* them: duality is negation, communication is cut elimination, and deadlock freedom is a corollary of linearity. When a type discipline aligns with a logic, its theorems come for free and its extensions are guided.
