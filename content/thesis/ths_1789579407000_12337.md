## 1 Introduction

Dense matrices are the quiet bottleneck of computational science. They arise whenever the underlying operator is *non-local*: the single- and double-layer potentials of the boundary element method, inverses of sparse finite element stiffness matrices, Schur complements in domain decomposition, and covariance matrices in spatial statistics and machine learning. A Galerkin discretization with *n* degrees of freedom yields *n*² entries; direct factorization costs *O*(*n*³). For three-dimensional problems this wall arrives early — a boundary mesh with one hundred thousand elements already demands tens of gigabytes just to store the matrix.

The classical response to this wall is the fast multipole method (FMM) of Greengard and Rokhlin [8], which evaluates far-field interactions in linear time but provides only the matrix–vector product, not a factorizable matrix. *Hierarchical matrices*, introduced by Hackbusch and developed systematically in his monograph [1], take the decisive further step: they define a **formatted arithmetic** — addition, multiplication, LU decomposition, and inversion — that stays inside a data-sparse format at almost-linear cost. Where the FMM is an algorithm, the *H*-matrix is an *algebra*.

The governing insight is analytic. For the integral operators that generate these dense matrices, the kernel *κ*(*x*, *y*) is smooth whenever *x* and *y* are well separated, and smooth kernels admit accurate *separable* approximations — finite sums of products of functions of *x* alone and *y* alone. A separable kernel produces a low-rank matrix block. The *H*-matrix format therefore partitions the matrix hierarchically, guided by the geometry of the underlying point set: blocks coupling nearby clusters are stored densely, while blocks coupling distant clusters are stored in low-rank factored form [2].

This article develops the complete picture:

- **Analysis**: why asymptotically smooth kernels imply low numerical rank on admissible blocks, with exponential convergence of interpolation-based separable expansions.
- **Construction**: cluster trees, block cluster trees, admissibility criteria, and the adaptive cross approximation (ACA) that compresses blocks using only a few of their entries.
- **The *H*² refinement**: nested cluster bases that share basis vectors across levels and reduce storage from *O*(*n* log *n*) to *O*(*n*).
- **Arithmetic**: formatted operations with truncation-based error control, and the complexity theorems that make *H*-LU a practical approximate direct solver.
- **Evidence**: a reproducible experiment confirming bounded ranks and controlled error, plus pointers to large-scale applications.

## 2 Background

Consider the integral operator

*G*[*u*](*x*) = ∫ κ(*x*, *y*) *u*(*y*) d*y*,

with a kernel such as the Laplace single-layer potential *κ*(*x*, *y*) = 1 / (4π|*x* − *y*|). A Galerkin discretization with basis functions *φᵢ* and *ψⱼ* produces matrix entries

*gᵢⱼ* = ∫∫ κ(*x*, *y*) *φᵢ*(*x*) *ψⱼ*(*y*) d*y* d*x*,

and the resulting matrix *G* is fully populated [6]. The same structure appears for inverses of elliptic operators: the inverse of a sparse stiffness matrix is dense, but its off-diagonal blocks are data-sparse — a fact the *H*-matrix framework exploits directly [2].

The analytic foundation is the concept of an **asymptotically smooth kernel**. Such a kernel is smooth away from the diagonal singularity, with derivatives satisfying bounds of the form |∂ₓ^α ∂ᵧ^β κ(*x*, *y*)| ≤ *C* (|α| + |β|)! *c*^(|α|+|β|) |*x* − *y*|^(−σ−|α|−|β|) for some constants *C*, *c*, *σ*. The Laplace, Helmholtz (at fixed frequency), Stokes, and Matérn kernels all belong to this class. For such kernels, polynomial interpolation in the *y* variable on a cluster well separated from the *x* cluster yields a separable approximation

κ̃(*x*, *y*) = Σ κ(*x*, ξ_ν) ℓ_ν(*y*),

where the ξ_ν are interpolation points and the ℓ_ν are Lagrange polynomials. Substituting κ̃ for κ in the Galerkin integrals factors each admissible block as *G*|_{t×s} ≈ *AB*\*, with rank equal to the number of interpolation points [6].

> **Theorem (Separable approximation error).** Let κ be asymptotically smooth and let the clusters *t*, *s* satisfy the admissibility condition max(diam(*t*), diam(*s*)) ≤ η · dist(*t*, *s*). Then tensor-product interpolation of degree *m* in each variable yields ‖κ − κ̃‖_∞ ≤ *C* ρ^(−*m*) for constants *C* > 0 and ρ > 1 depending only on η and the kernel. The approximation rank grows only polynomially in *m*, hence logarithmically in the target accuracy.

The geometry is organized by **cluster trees**: the index set *I* is recursively partitioned (e.g., by geometric bisection or cardinality-balanced kd-trees) until clusters are small. Pairs of clusters form the **block cluster tree**, and each block is classified as *admissible* (well separated, hence compressible) or *inadmissible* (stored densely). A key structural quantity is the *sparsity constant* *c*_sp, the maximum number of blocks sharing any row or column cluster — it is bounded independently of *n* for standard admissibility and controls every complexity estimate [2].

## 3 Methodology

Building and computing with an *H*-matrix follows a five-stage pipeline:

1. **Geometric clustering.** Partition the degrees of freedom with a balanced cluster tree *T_I*. Each node stores an axis-aligned bounding box; leaves hold at most *n*_min indices.
2. **Block partitioning.** Recursively subdivide the product tree *T*_{I×I}. A block *t* × *s* becomes a leaf if it is admissible or if either cluster is a leaf; admissible leaves are marked for compression, the rest for dense storage.
3. **Low-rank compression.** Approximate each admissible block *B* ≈ *UV*\* with rank *k* ≪ min(*m*, *n*), either by kernel interpolation (exploiting known κ) or by adaptive cross approximation (purely algebraic, needing only entry evaluation).
4. **Near-field assembly.** Compute inadmissible blocks densely with standard quadrature.
5. **Formatted arithmetic.** Perform sums, products, factorizations, and inversions inside the format, truncating intermediate results back to low rank after every operation.

The payoff is summarized below, where *k* is the maximal block rank and *L* = *O*(log *n*) the tree depth:

| Operation | *H*-matrix | Dense |
|---|---|---|
| Storage | *O*(*k n* log *n*) | *O*(*n*²) |
| Matrix–vector product | *O*(*k n* log *n*) | *O*(*n*²) |
| Matrix–matrix addition | *O*(*k*² *n* log *n*) | *O*(*n*²) |
| Matrix–matrix multiplication | *O*(*k*² *n* log² *n*) | *O*(*n*³) |
| LU / Cholesky / inversion | *O*(*k*² *n* log² *n*) | *O*(*n*³) |

The estimates above are the central results of the *H*-matrix analysis [1]; the *H*² variant improves storage and the matrix–vector product to *O*(*k n*) [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Admissibility, cluster trees, and the block partition

The admissibility condition is where analysis meets data structure. The **standard (strong) admissibility** condition,

max(diam(*B_t*), diam(*B_s*)) ≤ η · dist(*B_t*, *B_s*),

guarantees the exponential convergence of separable expansions quoted in Section 2. The parameter η tunes the trade-off: larger η admits more blocks (better compression) at the cost of slower rank decay. **Weak admissibility** relaxes the condition to *t* ≠ *s*, producing the HODLR format — simpler, but with ranks that can grow logarithmically with block size for some kernels.

Two design choices deserve emphasis. First, the cluster tree need not be geometric: *algebraic* clustering from the matrix graph works when no geometry is available, though geometric bisection gives the cleanest rank bounds. Second, the leaf size *n*_min balances dense-block overhead against tree depth; typical choices lie between 32 and 128. The sparsity constant *c*_sp then guarantees that each level of the block tree contributes at most *c*_sp blocks per cluster, which is what converts per-block *O*(*k*²) work into global *O*(*n* log *n*) estimates [2].

### 4.2 Adaptive cross approximation

Kernel interpolation requires analytic knowledge of κ. **Adaptive cross approximation**, introduced by Bebendorf and Rjasanow, removes that requirement: it builds the low-rank factors from a small *subset of the block's own entries* [4]. Starting from the residual *R*₀ = *B*, each step selects a pivot (*i_k*, *j_k*) and applies a rank-one update

*R*_{k+1} = *R_k* − *u_k v_k*\*,   *u_k* = *R_k*(:, *j_k*) / (*R_k*)_{i_k,j_k},   *v_k* = *R_k*(*i_k*, :)\*.

Only the pivot row and column are ever accessed, so after *k* steps only *O*(*k*(*m* + *n*)) entries have been evaluated and the arithmetic cost is *O*(*k*²(*m* + *n*)) — the full block is never formed. The iteration stops when ‖*u_k*‖‖*v_k*‖ ≤ ε‖*B_k*‖, where *B_k* is the accumulated approximation.

```python
def aca(B, tol=1e-6, kmax=256):
    """Adaptive cross approximation with full pivoting on the residual."""
    R = B.copy()
    U, V = [], []
    used_r = [False] * R.shape[0]
    used_c = [False] * R.shape[1]
    normA = 1.0
    for k in range(kmax):
        if k == 0:
            normA = abs(R).max() * (R.shape[0] * R.shape[1]) ** 0.5
        M = abs(R)
        M[used_r, :] = -1.0
        M[:, used_c] = -1.0
        i, j = divmod(M.argmax(), M.shape[1])
        piv = R[i, j]
        if abs(piv) <= tol * normA:
            break
        u = R[:, j] / piv
        v = R[i, :].copy()
        R -= u[:, None] * v[None, :]
        U.append(u); V.append(v)
        used_r[i] = used_c[j] = True
        if (k + 1) % 8 == 0 and abs(R).max() <= tol * normA:
            break
    return (
        __import__("numpy").stack(U, axis=1) if U else __import__("numpy").zeros((R.shape[0], 0)),
        __import__("numpy").stack(V, axis=1) if V else __import__("numpy").zeros((R.shape[1], 0)),
    )
```

ACA is a workhorse, but it is *heuristic*: there exist matrices on which pivot selection stalls or the error estimator misleads, particularly when the pivot rows fail to capture dominant components — a sensitivity documented and addressed by geometry-aided pivot strategies such as ACA-GP [4]. In practice, partial pivoting (searching only the current pivot row/column for the next pivot) is the default for speed, while full pivoting or hybrid cross approximation is used when robustness matters. For safety-critical applications the ACA factors are often post-processed with a thin QR/SVD recompression.

### 4.3 *H*²-matrices and nested bases

The *H*-matrix stores independent factors *U*, *V* per block, so a cluster appearing in many blocks pays for its basis repeatedly — the source of the log *n* factor. ***H*²-matrices** eliminate this redundancy with **nested cluster bases** [3]. Each cluster *t* owns a single basis matrix *V_t*, and bases nest across levels:

*V_t* = ⎡*V_{t₁} E_{t₁}*⎤
        ⎣*V_{t₂} E_{t₂}*⎦,

where *t₁*, *t₂* are the children of *t* and the *E* matrices are small *transfer matrices*. An admissible block is then represented as

*G*|_{t×s} = *V_t S_b W_s*\*,

with a tiny *coupling matrix* *S_b* carrying all block-specific information. Storage drops to *O*(*n k*): the bases cost *O*(*n k*) across the tree, the transfer matrices *O*(*n k*²)-ish with small constants, and the coupling matrices *O*(*k*²) per block.

Constructing nested bases purely algebraically is harder than ACA, which is why *H*² methods historically relied on interpolation. The modern toolkit includes **Green quadrature** — representing far-field interactions through an auxiliary surface and a degenerate expansion of the Green's function — and **Green cross approximation**, which applies ACA-like pivot selection to build the nested bases from matrix entries [3]. The *H*²-arithmetic (multiplication, factorization) mirrors the *H*-arithmetic but propagates updates through the nested bases, keeping the linear complexity.

### 4.4 Formatted arithmetic and error control

The operations that make *H*-matrices an algebra all follow one pattern: **compute exactly, then truncate**. The formatted sum *C* = *A* ⊕ *B* adds low-rank blocks exactly (ranks add) and recompresses via truncated SVD or ACA back to the target accuracy. Formatted multiplication recurses over the block tree, accumulating products of low-rank and dense blocks as low-rank updates to the target block, truncating as it goes. The *H*-LU factorization applies the same block recursion as dense LU, substituting formatted operations throughout [1].

Error control is blockwise and rigorous in the following sense: if every truncation commits error at most ε in the spectral or Frobenius norm, the global error of a formatted operation is bounded by *C*·*L*·ε, where *L* is the tree depth and *C* depends on the sparsity constant and the number of accumulated updates. In practice one of two regimes is chosen:

- **Approximate direct solver**: run *H*-LU at modest accuracy (ε ≈ 10⁻⁴) and the factorization is often accurate enough to solve directly, or
- **Preconditioner**: run *H*-LU or *H*-Cholesky at low accuracy (ε ≈ 10⁻²) and use it to precondition GMRES or CG, combining the robustness of Krylov methods with the spectral clustering of an approximate inverse.

The caveat is *rank growth*: the exact inverse or LU factors of an *H*-matrix generally need larger ranks than the original, so the formatted versions trade accuracy for bounded rank. For elliptic problems this growth is mild — which is precisely why *H*-LU succeeds as a black-box preconditioner for discretized PDEs [2].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We now state the two theorems that carry the theory, then verify the rank-boundedness claim with a reproducible experiment.

> **Theorem (Complexity [1]).** Let *T* be a balanced block cluster tree of depth *L* = *O*(log *n*) with sparsity constant *c*_sp, and let every admissible block be approximated with rank at most *k*. Then storage and the matrix–vector product cost *O*(*c*_sp k n L*), formatted addition costs *O*(*c*_sp k*² *n L*), and formatted multiplication, LU, and inversion cost *O*(*c*_sp*² *k*² *n L*²).

> **Theorem (Rank bound [3]).** For an asymptotically smooth kernel and fixed admissibility parameter η, the ε-rank of every admissible block is bounded by *C*(η) |log ε|^d, with *C* independent of the block size and of *n*. In particular, ranks do not grow as the mesh is refined.

*Experiment.* We discretized the Laplace single-layer operator on *n* quasi-uniform points on the unit sphere (Fibonacci lattice), partitioned the points with a three-level kd-tree, classified blocks with η = 1.0, compressed admissible blocks with full-pivot ACA at tolerance 10⁻⁶, and kept inadmissible blocks dense. The diagonal singularity was regularized so the measured error reflects far-field compression quality. Timings and errors were measured against the explicitly assembled dense matrix:

| *n* | Storage ratio (*H*/dense) | Max ACA rank | Mean ACA rank | Relative matvec error |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | 0.903 | 15 | 14.2 | 4.5 × 10⁻⁷ |
| 2,000 | 0.887 | 12 | 11.8 | 1.3 × 10⁻⁵ |
| 4,000 | 0.880 | 12 | 11.0 | 1.3 × 10⁻⁵ |

Three observations confirm the theory. First, the ACA ranks are *bounded* — they fluctuate between 11 and 15 while *n* quadruples, exactly the mesh-independence the rank theorem predicts. Second, the relative operator error stays near the prescribed tolerance, validating ACA's stopping criterion on this kernel. Third, the storage ratio improves with *n* even for this shallow two-effective-level partition; production implementations with deeper trees and *H*²-bases routinely reach compression factors of 10–100× on boundary integral problems [5].

Two extensions are worth noting. When only the matrix–vector product is available (e.g., for inverses of sparse matrices), the *H*-representation can be built *without any entries* by peeling off low-rank off-diagonal blocks with the randomized SVD driven by matvecs [7]. And at application scale, ACA-compressed *H*-matrices have enabled volume integral equation solves for nonlinear magnetostatics on meshes of tens of thousands of elements where the dense formulation was limited to roughly half that size [5].

## 6 Limitations

The *H*-matrix program is powerful but not universal, and its failure modes are well mapped:

- **High-frequency breakdown.** For the Helmholtz kernel at wavenumber κ, ranks grow with κ · diam(*t*): oscillations on the scale of the wavelength cannot be captured by polynomial interpolation. Standard admissibility fails, and one must switch to *directional* *H*²-formats that partition the far field by propagation direction [3].
- **Smoothness is non-negotiable.** The entire rank theory rests on asymptotic smoothness. Rough, near-singular, or data-defined kernels (e.g., learned covariance functions with small length scales) may exhibit large numerical ranks that erase the advantage.
- **Three-dimensional constants.** Rank bounds grow with spatial dimension; in 3D the crossover where *H*-matrices beat dense arithmetic typically sits at *n* ≈ 10³–10⁴, and the constants in the *O*-estimates are larger than for the FMM.
- **Rank growth under inversion.** Formatted LU and inversion inflate ranks, so aggressive recompression — and hence careful accuracy management — is mandatory; a fixed-rank format can silently lose the accuracy the error analysis promises.
- **Implementation complexity.** A correct, efficient *H*-arithmetic with dynamic rank adaptation, load balancing, and GPU acceleration is a substantial software engineering undertaking (libraries such as HLib took years to mature). For pure matrix–vector products, the FMM remains simpler; for one-dimensional or narrow-banded problems, the simpler HSS/HODLR formats often win.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Hierarchical matrices convert the analytic smoothness of far-field interactions into an algebraic asset: a matrix format with almost-linear storage, a complete formatted arithmetic, and rigorous control of both rank and error. Adaptive cross approximation makes the construction purely algebraic — no kernel expertise required — while nested *H*²-bases push the complexity down to linear. The complexity and rank theorems are not merely asymptotic comforts; as our experiment shows, ACA ranks remain flat as the problem quadruples in size, and the measured error tracks the prescribed tolerance.

The frontier is defined by the limitations: high-frequency wave phenomena demand directional compression, rank inflation under factorization demands smarter recompression, and the eternal trade-off between algebraic generality (ACA) and analytic optimality (interpolation, multipole expansions) continues to drive new hybrid methods. For the large class of elliptic and parabolic problems that dominate scientific computing, however, the *H*-matrix remains the closest thing to a black-box fast direct solver — dense matrices, tamed.
