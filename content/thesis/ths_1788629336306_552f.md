This thesis provides a comprehensive technical analysis of the QUIC transport protocol's internals as standardized in RFC 9000, RFC 9001, and RFC 9002. We dissect the three packet number spaces (Initial, Handshake, Application Data) and prove that the AEAD nonce construction — IV XOR left-padded packet number — guarantees per-space, per-epoch nonce uniqueness, eliminating catastrophic nonce reuse. We analyze the RACK-inspired loss detector with its 9/8 time threshold and 3-packet reordering threshold, the probe timeout that recovers tail loss without collapsing the congestion window, and the persistent-congestion detector that reserves window collapse for genuinely congested paths. We examine QUIC's pluggable congestion control interface, contrasting the exemplary NewReno-like controller with CUBIC and BBRv2 deployments. We formalize connection migration via PATH_CHALLENGE/PATH_RESPONSE with anti-amplification bounds and the indistinguishable Stateless Reset with unguessable 128-bit tokens. Finally, we prove transport-level replay safety of 0-RTT while delineating its application-layer replay exposure and the 1-RTT handshake's forward-secrecy guarantees.

# QUIC Transport Protocol Internals: Packet Number Spaces and AEAD Nonce Construction, Loss Detection with PTO, Pluggable Congestion Control, Connection Migration with Stateless Reset, and 0-RTT/1-RTT Handshake Security

## Abstract

This thesis presents a comprehensive analysis of the QUIC transport protocol's internals as standardized in RFC 9000, RFC 9001, and RFC 9002. Full details are provided in the accompanying abstract field.

---

## 1 Introduction

QUIC is the most significant redesign of Internet transport since TCP congestion control was standardized in the 1980s. Developed inside Google as *gQUIC* and deployed at planetary scale years before its IETF standardization in May 2021 [4], QUIC reimagines reliable transport as a user-space, UDP-encapsulated, always-encrypted protocol that fuses the TLS 1.3 handshake, multiplexed streams, and a modern loss-recovery framework into one coherent design.

Three architectural decisions motivate this thesis: **cryptographic binding of transport state** (each packet's AEAD nonce derives from its packet number), **per-space loss detection** (packet numbers are never reused, so TCP's retransmission ambiguity vanishes), and **identity decoupled from address** (opaque connection IDs enable migration and stateless reset).

Section 2 surveys background; Section 3 describes methodology; Section 4 provides the five-part deep dive; Section 5 presents empirical evidence and formal arguments; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

---

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The three companion RFCs

QUIC version 1 is specified across three documents with a deliberate separation of concerns:

| Document | Authors | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| RFC 9000 [1] | Iyengar & Thomson | Core transport: packets, frames, streams, migration, reset |
| RFC 9001 [2] | Thomson & Turner | TLS 1.3 integration: key schedule, packet protection, handshake mapping |
| RFC 9002 [3] | Iyengar & Swett | Loss detection and an exemplary NewReno-like congestion controller |

The wire format, cryptographic envelope, and recovery algorithm are specified independently — an implementer may swap the congestion controller without touching packet parsing.

### 2.2 Why TCP could not simply be patched

Four structural defects of TCP motivated a clean-slate design [4]:

- **Head-of-line blocking.** One lost TCP segment stalls every logical stream sharing the connection, defeating application-layer multiplexing.
- **Ossified middleboxes.** TCP's visible wire image freezes fields; QUIC encrypts almost everything to make ossification cryptographically impossible.
- **Handshake latency.** TCP + TLS 1.3 costs 2–3 round trips; QUIC folds both handshakes into one: 1-RTT by default, 0-RTT on resumption.
- **Identity bound to the 4-tuple.** Roaming clients must rebuild TCP connections; QUIC's connection IDs make migration a transport primitive.

### 2.3 Deployment context

QUIC is not a laboratory curiosity. Google's services, Meta's mobile applications (~75% of Meta's Internet traffic by 2020), Cloudflare's edge, and Microsoft's SMB-over-QUIC stack all run production QUIC. By mid-2026 roughly 21% of requests at Cloudflare's edge arrived over HTTP/3 — a plateau reflecting saturation of the *negotiable* web rather than failure [9]; active measurements show QUIC used for subsequent fetches by ~70% of measured users. The protocol's internals carry direct operational consequences at Internet scale.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis uses a *specification-driven methodology* with three strands:

1. **Normative trace analysis.** Each mechanism is traced to its RFC 9000–9002 requirements language, separating MUST-level guarantees from SHOULD/MAY-level guidance.
2. **Comparative analysis.** Each mechanism is contrasted with its TCP/TLS analogue — RACK-TLP [10], TLS 1.3 early data, TCP RTO — to isolate necessary changes from cosmetic ones.
3. **Empirical grounding.** Claims are cross-checked against deployment studies [4][5][9] and implementation evidence from *quiche*, *msquic*, *mvfst*, and *quinn*.

The security analysis follows the RFC 9001 threat model: an on-path and off-path network adversary with full observation and injection capabilities, against which we evaluate replay resistance, forward secrecy, and key-update agility.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Packet Number Spaces and AEAD Nonce Construction

#### 4.1.1 Three spaces, one invariant

RFC 9000 Section 12.3 partitions every connection's packets into exactly three *packet number spaces*:

| Space | Encryption level | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| Initial | Initial keys | Initial packets (ClientHello / ServerHello flight) |
| Handshake | Handshake keys | Handshake packets (TLS handshake completion) |
| Application Data | 0-RTT / 1-RTT keys | 0-RTT and 1-RTT packets |

Each space maintains an **independent** packet number sequence starting at zero; ACK frames acknowledge only within the space of the carrying packet; and loss detection state — RTT estimates, PTO timers, sent-packet maps — is maintained per space (RFC 9002, Section 6.1).

Notably, *0-RTT and 1-RTT share the Application Data space*: packet numbers continue monotonically across the 0-RTT→1-RTT boundary, so frames retransmitted under 1-RTT keys after 0-RTT rejection need no duplicated recovery state [1]. The Initial and Handshake spaces are *ephemeral*: once handshake keys are confirmed, their keys and recovery state are discarded (RFC 9001, Section 4.9.1).

Packet numbers are 62-bit integers, but only the low 1–4 bytes appear on the wire. The receiver reconstructs the full number from the largest received number in the space (RFC 9000, Appendix A):

```c
/* Simplified from RFC 9000 Appendix A */
uint64_t decode_packet_number(uint64_t largest_pn, uint32_t truncated_pn,
                              unsigned pn_nbits) {
    uint64_t expected_pn = largest_pn + 1;
    uint64_t pn_win = (uint64_t)1 << pn_nbits;
    uint64_t pn_hwin = pn_win / 2, pn_mask = pn_win - 1;
    uint64_t candidate_pn = (expected_pn & ~pn_mask) | truncated_pn;
    if (candidate_pn <= expected_pn - pn_hwin &&
        candidate_pn < (UINT64_C(1) << 62) - pn_win)
        return candidate_pn + pn_win;
    if (candidate_pn > expected_pn + pn_hwin && candidate_pn >= pn_win)
        return candidate_pn - pn_win;
    return candidate_pn;
}
```

Reconstruction succeeds provided fewer than half a window's packets vanish consecutively — a condition the PTO machinery enforces.

#### 4.1.2 The nonce: binding cryptography to the packet number

RFC 9001 Section 5.3 defines the AEAD nonce `N`: the 62 bits of the reconstructed packet number in network byte order, left-padded with zeros to the IV size, XORed with the IV:

```python
def quic_aead_nonce(iv: bytes, packet_number: int) -> bytes:
    # RFC 9001 Section 5.3: N = IV XOR leftpad(pn)
    pn_bytes = packet_number.to_bytes(8, "big")          # 62 bits, network order
    padded   = b"\x00" * (len(iv) - len(pn_bytes)) + pn_bytes
    return bytes(a ^ b for a, b in zip(iv, padded))
```

This yields the protocol's central cryptographic invariant:

> **Theorem:** *Within a given packet number space and key epoch, the AEAD nonce is unique for every packet an endpoint sends.*
> *Proof sketch.* Nonces are `IV ⊕ pad(pn)`. The IV is fixed per key epoch; packet numbers strictly increase within a space [1]. The map `pn ↦ pad(pn)` is injective over 62-bit values, and XOR with a constant preserves injectivity. Distinct spaces use cryptographically separated keys (HKDF labels), and key updates advance the epoch (Section 4.5), so cross-space or cross-epoch reuse is impossible. ∎

The invariant is load-bearing: GCM and ChaCha20-Poly1305 fail catastrophically under nonce reuse, and QUIC eliminates "random nonce collision" errors by deriving nonces deterministically from an existing counter. The associated data `A` is the header through the *unprotected* packet number, and the 16-byte tag provides integrity.

#### 4.1.3 Key derivation and header protection

Initial secrets derive from a fixed version-1 salt via HKDF-Extract keyed by the client's first Destination Connection ID, then HKDF-Expand-Label (`"client in"`/`"server in"`); traffic keys, IVs, and header-protection keys expand under `"quic key"`, `"quic iv"`, `"quic hp"` [2]. Since the salt and first DCID are visible on the wire, Initial protection is deliberately weak — deterring casual observation, not authenticating.

After AEAD protection, QUIC applies *header protection*: a mask from a 16-byte ciphertext sample — `AES-ECB(hp_key, sample)` for AES AEADs, raw ChaCha20 for ChaCha20-Poly1305 [2] — concealing the packet number and part of the first header byte to deny middleboxes ossifiable fields, while connection IDs stay visible for routing.

### 4.2 Loss Detection and the Probe Timeout

#### 4.2.1 No retransmission ambiguity — ever

TCP's classic difficulty is *retransmission ambiguity*: an ACK cannot identify whether it covers the original or the retransmission, poisoning RTT estimates (Karn's algorithm). QUIC abolishes it structurally — **packet numbers are never reused** — so ACKs unambiguously identify transmissions and RTT samples are clean by construction.

#### 4.2.2 RACK-style loss detection

RFC 9002 Section 6.1 specifies a detector modeled on RACK-TLP for TCP [10], run independently per packet number space. A packet is declared lost when either threshold fires:

- **Packet (reordering) threshold:** an earlier-sent packet is lost once a packet numbered ≥3 higher is acknowledged in the same space — mirroring TCP's duplicate-ACK threshold.
- **Time threshold:** loss is declared if the packet was sent more than `max(9/8 × max(smoothed_rtt, latest_rtt), 1ms)` before a later-sent ack-eliciting packet is acknowledged.

Per-space detection lets the handshake complete robustly under loss that would stall TLS-over-TCP's single recovery context.

#### 4.2.3 The Probe Timeout

When acknowledgments stop entirely — tail loss, or a vanished flight — QUIC arms a *probe timeout* (RFC 9002, Section 6.2):

```
PTO = smoothed_rtt + max(4 × rttvar, kGranularity) + max_ack_delay
```

The formula mirrors TCP's RTO but adds the peer's `max_ack_delay` transport parameter instead of a fixed minimum. On expiry the sender transmits probes — new data if the window allows, else retransmittable data — doubling the PTO per consecutive expiry.

Decisively, **PTO expiry does not collapse the congestion window**. Like RACK-TLP, QUIC holds that one tail loss is not persistent congestion; only the *persistent congestion* detector — ack-eliciting packets sent over `3 × PTO` all declared lost — collapses the window to the 2-packet minimum (RFC 9002, Sections 6.2, 7.6), avoiding spurious collapses and obviating hacks like Forward RTO-Recovery.

#### 4.2.4 Clean RTT estimation

QUIC's ACK frames carry an *ACK Delay* field that the sender subtracts from measured RTT. With unambiguous packet numbers, the EWMA `smoothed_rtt`/`rttvar` estimates rest on cleaner foundations than TCP's.

### 4.3 Congestion Control Pluggability: NewReno, CUBIC, and BBRv2

#### 4.3.1 The exemplary controller and the generic signal interface

RFC 9002 Section 7 specifies a sender-side controller "similar to TCP NewReno" — but explicitly *exemplary, not mandatory*:

> *"The signals QUIC provides for congestion control are generic and are designed to support different sender-side algorithms. A sender can unilaterally choose a different algorithm to use, such as CUBIC [6]."*

This unilateral pluggability is a deployability win: the user-space controller needs no peer cooperation and can be A/B-tested per connection, constrained only by RFC 8085 Section 3.1. The generic signals are per-packet ACKs with ECN counts, accurate RTT, persistent-congestion declaration, and per-path state.

The exemplary controller's phases:

| Phase | Behavior |
|---|---|
| Slow start | `cwnd` grows per bytes ACKed; initial window 12000 bytes |
| Congestion avoidance | `cwnd += max_datagram² / cwnd` per ACK |
| Recovery (loss/ECN-CE) | `cwnd = max(cwnd/2, 2 × max_datagram)`; one cut per loss epoch |
| Persistent congestion | `cwnd = 2 × max_datagram_size` |

Two details are distinctly QUIC: the **minimum window is two packets**, not TCP's one (Section 4.8) — losing a lone packet would otherwise force a full PTO stall — and **handshake packets are not special** (Section 4.9), unlike TCP's treatment of SYN loss as persistent congestion.

#### 4.3.2 CUBIC and BBRv2 over QUIC signals

**CUBIC** [6] ports with minimal friction and ships in *mvfst*, *quiche*, and *msquic*; its growth curve is clocked by QUIC's clean RTT rather than TCP's Karn-polluted estimates, tightening it around the true bandwidth-delay product.

**BBRv2** [7] replaces loss-driven probing with an explicit path model — bottleneck bandwidth × minimum RTT bounding inflight. QUIC's cleaner RTT/ACK-delay inputs reduce the model noise behind BBRv1's unfairness episodes, its loss/ECN-tolerant `inflight_hi`/`inflight_lo` bounds suit lossy wireless paths, and QUIC's ECN path validation (RFC 9000, Section 13.4.2.1) closes a cheating vector BBRv2 would otherwise inherit.

A stack can thus run BBRv2 for video, CUBIC for bulk transfer, and the RFC 9002 controller for short flows — simultaneously, per connection, with no negotiation.

### 4.4 Connection Migration with Stateless Reset

#### 4.4.1 Identity without address

A QUIC connection is identified by *connection IDs* — opaque strings each endpoint chooses for its peer — not by the IP-port 4-tuple, so a client roaming from Wi-Fi to cellular keeps its connection alive. The migration protocol (RFC 9000, Section 9) balances agility against abuse:

- **Client-initiated only in v1.** Servers never migrate toward new client addresses unprompted; `preferred_address` lets a server steer the client to a new server address post-handshake.
- **No migration before handshake confirmation** (RFC 9001, Section 4.1.2), blocking hijack of half-open connections.
- **`disable_active_migration`.** A server may forbid migration; violators' packets are dropped *without* a Stateless Reset, which would otherwise hand attackers a connection-killing oracle.
- **NAT rebinding handled uniformly.** Any observed address change — deliberate or middlebox-caused — triggers *path validation*.

#### 4.4.2 Path validation and anti-amplification

A new path is proven by challenge-response (RFC 9000, Section 8.2): PATH_CHALLENGE carries 8 unpredictable bytes echoed in PATH_RESPONSE. Until validation, the **anti-amplification limit** caps the server at 3× received bytes on that address (Section 8.1), blocking DDoS reflection. Congestion state resets for the new path — fresh window, no inherited RTT samples.

#### 4.4.3 Stateless Reset

Servers crash and lose state. QUIC's answer (RFC 9000, Section 10.3) is a UDP datagram crafted to be indistinguishable from an ordinary short-header packet:

```
Stateless Reset {
  Fixed Bits (2) = 1,
  Unpredictable Bits (38..),
  Stateless Reset Token (128),
}
```

Constraints: minimum 21 bytes with padding toward length-indistinguishability; the **token occupies the last 16 bytes**, detected by constant-time comparison; tokens are unguessable — typically `trunc_128(HMAC(static_key, connection_id))`, recomputable without per-connection storage — and single-use. A reset MUST NOT be 3× larger than the triggering packet and SHOULD be *smaller*, preventing reset ping-pong between stateless endpoints. Tokens arrive via the `stateless_reset_token` transport parameter and NEW_CONNECTION_ID frames; on recognition the endpoint enters the draining period and goes silent.

> **Theorem:** *An off-path attacker cannot forge a Stateless Reset for a connection whose token it has not observed.*
> *Proof sketch.* The 128-bit token is a PRF output under a server secret. Forgery means guessing 128 unpredictable bits with only an accept/reject oracle, and endpoints rate-limit resets per remote address: success probability 2⁻¹²⁸ per attempt — negligible. ∎

### 4.5 0-RTT and 1-RTT Handshake Security

#### 4.5.1 The compressed handshake

QUIC fuses TLS 1.3 into its packet exchange: the client's Initial carries the ClientHello in a CRYPTO frame; the server answers with Initial plus Handshake packets — **one round trip** from cold start. Transport parameters travel as an authenticated TLS extension [2]. Key updates need no TLS message: either endpoint flips the *Key Phase* bit and derives the next epoch via `HKDF-Expand-Label(secret_N, "quic ku", "", Hash.length)`.

#### 4.5.2 0-RTT: speed with a replay asterisk

A returning client with a session ticket sends application data in its *first flight*, coalesced with its Initial packet under keys from `client_early_traffic_secret` — and may even open streams, which TLS 1.3's raw early data cannot do. Constraints are strict: CRYPTO frames in 0-RTT packets are a `PROTOCOL_VIOLATION`; `EndOfEarlyData` does not exist; stream limits come from *remembered* transport parameters.

The cost is replay. RFC 9001 Section 9.2: 0-RTT inherits TLS 1.3's replay exposure, and *"application protocols MUST NOT use 0-RTT data for any operation that is not safe to retry."* But **the transport itself is not vulnerable** — frame processing is idempotent and QUIC state produces no effects beyond the connection lifetime. The exposure lives entirely in the *application data*: HTTP/3 mandates RFC 8470 anti-replay mitigations [8], and 0-RTT lacks forward secrecy.

> **Theorem:** *Replay of 0-RTT packets cannot corrupt QUIC connection state.*
> *Proof sketch.* No transport-affecting frame type is non-idempotent under duplication, and connection state is discarded at close. Replayed 0-RTT packets therefore induce no invalid state; application-layer effects are the application's responsibility. ∎

#### 4.5.3 What 1-RTT guarantees

The 1-RTT handshake carries the full TLS 1.3 profile: always-on server authentication, optional client authentication, forward secrecy via (EC)DHE, and transcript-bound downgrade protection. Address validation tokens (Retry, NEW_TOKEN) add proof-of-address-ownership before the server commits resources — the server can demand a validated source address at the cost of one round trip, exactly when under load, blunting handshake-flooding DoS.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Deployment evidence

Three measurement programs corroborate the design. The **SIGCOMM 2017 experience paper** [4] reported QUIC cutting latency on lossy mobile networks with user-space iteration outpacing kernel TCP. The **IMC 2019 study** [5] fingerprinted QUIC stacks Internet-wide, finding migration and 0-RTT exercised in the wild. **Operator traffic shares** [9] show HTTP/3 near 21% of edge requests — the *negotiable* web, saturated.

### 5.2 Formal properties

Beyond the three inline theorems, two further results follow:

- **Recovery soundness.** Packet numbers are never reused and ACKs never reneged, so the per-space detector cannot declare lost a packet the receiver acknowledged.
- **Migration safety.** Path validation plus the 3× anti-amplification bound gives SYN-cookie-grade protection with zero per-connection server state.

### 5.3 Performance characteristics

The mechanisms compose into QUIC's profile: 1-RTT/0-RTT establishment wins on high-latency paths; per-space RACK-style recovery with non-collapsing PTO wins under tail loss; clean RTT signals let BBRv2 and CUBIC operate on measurements TCP cannot supply. Akamai's 2025 measurements attribute ~30% mobile HTTP/3 latency reductions versus HTTP/2 to these mechanisms.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Version 1 only.** QUIC v2 and the multipath extension (which folds a path identifier into the nonce) are out of scope.
2. **Implementation divergence.** PTO granularity, ACK tuning, BBRv2 parameters, and reset rate limits differ across *quiche*, *msquic*, *mvfst*, and *quinn*.
3. **Middlebox reality.** Encryption defeats inspection, but UDP blocking and throttling persist.
4. **0-RTT caution.** Application-layer replay remains the primary operational risk; many operators disable 0-RTT.
5. **Evaluation gap.** Public BBRv2-vs-CUBIC-over-QUIC benchmarks across controlled loss/latency matrices remain scarce.

---

## 7 Conclusion

QUIC's internals cohere around one principle: *bind every transport decision to cryptographic identity and measure everything cleanly*. Packet number spaces give recovery and cryptography a shared coordinate system; the nonce construction yields a provable uniqueness guarantee; per-space RACK-style detection with non-collapsing PTO recovers without TCP's ambiguity; the signal interface makes congestion control a user-space plugin; connection IDs make migration and stateless reset first-class; the TLS 1.3 handshake delivers 1-RTT or replay-careful 0-RTT.

Deployment validates the architecture: these mechanisms carry a fifth of the web's requests and most of several operators' mobile traffic. Future work — multipath nonces, v2 migration semantics, BBRv3-over-QUIC — will test whether the invariants survive the protocol's second decade.
