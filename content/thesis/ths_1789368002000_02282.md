# Quasicrystals and Aperiodic Order: Penrose Tilings, the Cut-and-Project Method, and Diffraction

## Abstract

Quasicrystals forced a revision of what it means to be a crystal: electron diffraction images of a rapidly cooled aluminium–manganese alloy displayed sharp Bragg peaks with icosahedral symmetry, a combination forbidden by classical crystallography [1]. This article develops the mathematical theory that resolved the paradox. We begin with Roger Penrose's aperiodic tilings of the plane and N. G. de Bruijn's algebraic pentagrid construction [5], which revealed these tilings as shadows of a five-dimensional periodic lattice. We then present the cut-and-project method in its modern form, due to Duneau and Katz [3], recasting aperiodic point sets as *model sets* obtained by projecting lattice points that fall inside a bounded window in an internal space. The central analytic result is that regular model sets are *pure point diffractive*: their diffraction consists entirely of sharp Bragg peaks, a theorem established rigorously by Hof [4] through autocorrelation analysis. We close with substitution rules, inflation symmetry [7], and the limitations of the ideal theory, following the systematic exposition of Baake and Grimm [6].

## 1 Introduction

Fivefold symmetry was provably impossible — and then it appeared in the laboratory. The spots were as sharp as those of ordinary crystals, yet no Bravais lattice could index them.

The theoretical response was immediate. Order was henceforth defined by diffraction, not by translation symmetry — and a vast mathematical program, the theory of *aperiodic order*, grew up to supply the missing foundations [6].

This article traces the three pillars of that program:

1. **Penrose tilings** — the concrete geometric objects exhibiting long-range aperiodic order.
2. **The cut-and-project method** — the unifying construction that generates them from higher-dimensional lattices [3][5].
3. **Mathematical diffraction** — the harmonic analysis proving that these objects diffract like crystals, with Bragg peaks only [4].

## 2 Background

A *tiling* of the plane is a covering by closed sets, the *tiles*, with disjoint interiors. A tiling is *periodic* if it is invariant under two independent translations; it is *aperiodic* if it admits no translational symmetry at all; and a *prototile set* is *aperiodic* if it tiles the plane but only aperiodically. Penrose found three such sets in the 1970s:

| Set | Prototiles | Matching mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P1 | Pentagon, pentagram, "boat", "diamond" | Edge markings |
| P2 | Kite and dart | Colored arcs across edges |
| P3 | Thin and thick rhombi | Arrow decorations |

The P2 kite-and-dart tiling, with its fivefold stars and characteristic "ace" and "deuce" vertex configurations, remains the iconic image of the subject:

![Penrose P2 kite-and-dart tiling patch](/thesis/ths_1789368002000_02282-0.webp)

Two properties make Penrose tilings more than curiosities. First, they are *repetitive*: every finite patch reappears infinitely often throughout the tiling, so the structure is statistically homogeneous despite lacking periodicity. Second, they have *finite local complexity*: up to translation, only finitely many local patterns occur. Together with uniform discreteness and relative density, these properties define the *Delone sets* that serve as abstract models of atomic positions [6].

The tilings also admit a *substitution* or *inflation* structure: each tile can be dissected into smaller copies of the prototiles, and iterating this dissection generates arbitrarily large patches. The linear inflation factor is the golden ratio *τ = (1 + √5) / 2*, an algebraic integer whose Galois conjugate has modulus below one — a *Pisot number*. This algebraic accident is the engine behind almost everything that follows, from the sharp diffraction to the hierarchical symmetry analysis of Cotfas [7].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis rests on two constructions and one analytic framework.

**The cut-and-project scheme.** Let *Γ* be a lattice in *R^n*, decomposed as a direct sum of a *physical space* *E* (dimension *d*) and an *internal space* *E_int* (dimension *m*), with the projections *π* and *π_int* restricted to *Γ* being injective and dense respectively. Choose a relatively compact *window* *W* in internal space with nonempty interior. The *model set* is

*Λ = { π(x) : x ∈ Γ, π_int(x) ∈ W }*.

The map *x ↦ π_int(x)* restricted to *Λ* is the *star map*, denoted *x ↦ x^⋆*. A model set is *regular* when the boundary of *W* has measure zero; singular cases, where projected lattice points land exactly on the window boundary, form a null set of windows and are excluded from the cleanest theorems. This construction, developed systematically by Duneau and Katz via their "strip method" [3], subsumes de Bruijn's pentagrid dualization [5] as a special case: the pentagrid is the two-dimensional shadow of the five-dimensional cubic lattice, and the window is a pentagonal acceptance domain.

**Mathematical diffraction.** A point set *Λ* is encoded as the *Dirac comb* *δ_Λ = Σ δ_x* over its points. Its *autocorrelation* *γ* is the vague limit of volume-averaged convolutions of finite restrictions, when the limit exists. The *diffraction measure* is the Fourier transform *γ̂*. A structure is *pure point diffractive* when *γ̂* is a pure point measure — a sum of Dirac masses, the mathematical incarnation of sharp Bragg peaks. Hof's central contribution [4] was to make this rigorous for projection-method tilings: he proved existence of the autocorrelation in the infinite-volume limit, justified the standard physicist's computation of diffraction from the window's Fourier transform, and proved a variant of the Bombieri–Taylor conjecture relating peak positions to the Fourier module.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Pentagrid and Rhombic Penrose Tilings

De Bruijn's 1981 breakthrough [5] starts from five families of equidistant parallel lines — a *pentagrid* — with normals pointing along the five fifth roots of unity and arbitrary shifts *γ_j*. The complement of the grid consists of open cells; each cell is mapped to a vertex of a rhombus tiling by an integer-valued vector function *K* that counts, for each grid direction, how many lines separate the cell from the origin. Bounded faces of the grid map to vertices, and each mesh of the grid corresponds to a rhombus whose type (thin or thick) is determined by the local intersection pattern.

The crucial dichotomy is *regular* versus *singular*: for almost every choice of shifts, no three grid lines concur, and the resulting tiling satisfies the Penrose matching rules; the null set of singular shifts yields tilings with lines of ambiguity. De Bruijn proved the exact correspondence:

> Theorem (de Bruijn, 1981): The rhombic Penrose tilings of the plane are precisely the tilings dual to regular pentagrids [5].

This was the first *cut-and-project* theorem in disguise: the five grid directions are the shadows of the five coordinate axes of *Z^5*, and the vertex set is the projection of those lattice points lying between two parallel hyperplanes. Aperiodic order was thereby revealed as *periodic order seen from an irrational angle*.

### 4.2 Model Sets and the Star Map

The Duneau–Katz formulation [3] generalizes the pentagrid to arbitrary dimension and symmetry. The one-dimensional prototype is the *Fibonacci chain*: project *Z^2* orthogonally onto a line of slope *1/τ*, keeping only those lattice points whose orthogonal projection falls inside a strip of suitable width. The surviving points project to a bi-infinite sequence of long and short intervals whose order is the fixed point of the substitution *L → LS*, *S → L* — the Fibonacci word. Here the star map is literal Galois conjugation: writing lattice coordinates in the ring *Z[τ]*, the map *a + bτ ↦ a + bτ'* with *τ' = (1 − √5)/2* sends physical coordinates to internal ones, and the window is simply the image of the strip.

![Cut-and-project construction of a one-dimensional model set](/thesis/ths_1789368002000_02282-1.webp)

The Penrose vertex set fits the same template with *n = 5*, *d = 2*, *m = 3*: the vertex set is a *multi-component* model set, a finite union of model sets with distinct windows partitioning the internal acceptance domain [7]. Icosahedral quasicrystals use *n = 6*, *d = 3*, *m = 3*, with a triacontahedral window. The window's shape controls the *local* structure — which vertex neighborhoods occur — while the lattice controls the *global* quasiperiodicity. Cotfas exploited this separation to classify inflation centers of Penrose-type tilings: the admissible scaling factors form a union of four cut-and-project sets, most admitting infinitely many independent inflation centers [7].

### 4.3 Diffraction: From Autocorrelation to Bragg Peaks

Why does an aperiodic point set produce sharp diffraction spots? The answer lies in the *almost periodicity* inherited from the higher-dimensional lattice. For a regular model set *Λ*, the autocorrelation exists and takes the explicit form of a sum over the difference set *Λ − Λ*, weighted by the covariogram of the window — the volume of overlap of *W* with its translate. Because the window is fixed and compact, these weights vary *almost periodically* with the displacement, and the Fourier transform collapses to Bragg peaks located on the *Fourier module* *π(Γ*)*, the projection of the dual lattice.

The intensities are equally explicit: the amplitude at a Bragg peak *k* is proportional to the Fourier transform of the window's indicator function evaluated at the internal coordinate *k^⋆*. Hence the tenfold-symmetric spot pattern observed by Shechtman [1] is explained — the projected dual lattice inherits the point symmetry of the higher-dimensional embedding, while the window's Fourier transform modulates intensities, producing the dramatic dynamic range (many orders of magnitude) seen in real patterns:

![Simulated diffraction pattern of a Penrose tiling](/thesis/ths_1789368002000_02282-2.webp)

Hof's theorem [4] makes this rigorous: regular model sets are pure point diffractive, and the standard formula used by physicists since the 1980s is the correct infinite-volume limit. The diffraction measure thus decomposes, in general, into pure point, singular continuous, and absolutely continuous parts — and for model sets, only the first survives.

### 4.4 Inflation, Substitution, and the Pisot Condition

Substitution rules offer a complementary, intrinsic description. A substitution replaces each prototile by a patch of smaller tiles; iterating *inflates* finite seeds into infinite tilings. The substitution is encoded in a nonnegative integer matrix counting tile types, whose Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue is the area inflation factor. For the Penrose rhombi, the linear inflation factor is *τ*, and the hierarchy of supertiles nests ad infinitum:

![Substitution deflation hierarchy of Penrose rhombi](/thesis/ths_1789368002000_02282-3.webp)

The spectral type of a substitution system is governed by algebra: if the inflation factor is a *Pisot number* — an algebraic integer above one whose conjugates lie inside the unit disc — the dynamical and diffraction spectra are typically pure point. The golden ratio is the smallest Pisot number, which is why Penrose tilings diffract sharply while other substitutions (the Thue–Morse sequence, with its singular continuous spectrum) do not. This *Pisot conjecture*, now a theorem in the irreducible one-dimensional case, is among the deepest open interfaces between number theory and mathematical physics [6]. Cotfas's analysis of Penrose inflation centers [7] shows how rich the symmetry structure is even within the Pisot regime: scaling factors far beyond *τ* itself act as inflation symmetries, each with its own lattice of centers.

## 5 Proofs

We isolate three results whose proofs exhibit the characteristic techniques of the field.

> Theorem (Aperiodicity of the Fibonacci chain): The Fibonacci model set admits no nonzero translational period.

> Proof sketch: Suppose *t ≠ 0* were a period. The frequencies of long and short tiles exist by unique ergodicity and stand in ratio *τ : 1*. A period would force the ratio of tile counts in any period cell to be rational, contradicting the irrationality of *τ*. The irrationality itself follows from *τ* satisfying *τ^2 = τ + 1*: a rational root is impossible. ∎

> Theorem (de Bruijn's pentagrid correspondence, 1981): Regular pentagrids are in bijection with rhombic Penrose tilings [5].

> Proof sketch: The map *K* from grid cells to *Z^5*-projected vertices is well defined precisely when no three grid lines concur (regularity). Meshes of the grid correspond bijectively to rhombi; the arrow decorations induced by the grid orientation satisfy the Penrose matching rules, and conversely every Penrose rhombus tiling arises this way by an iterated inflate-and-match argument on expanding balls. ∎

> Theorem (Hof, 1995): Every regular model set is pure point diffractive [4].

> Proof sketch: The autocorrelation of a regular model set exists as a vague limit and equals a weighted Dirac comb on the uniformly discrete difference set *Λ − Λ*, with weights given by the window covariogram. These weights are restrictions of a continuous almost periodic function, so the autocorrelation is strongly almost periodic; by the Bohr compactification theory, its Fourier transform is pure point. The Bragg peaks lie on the Fourier module, with intensities from the window transform. ∎

A numerical sanity check of the Fibonacci case is a few lines of Python:

```python
import numpy as np

def fibonacci_word(n):
    s, t = "L", "LS"
    for _ in range(n):
        s, t = t, t + s      # substitution: L -> LS, S -> L
    return s

tau = (1 + 5 ** 0.5) / 2
w = fibonacci_word(22)
pos = np.cumsum([tau if c == "L" else 1.0 for c in w])
pos = np.insert(pos, 0, 0.0)

# sample the Dirac comb on a fine grid and take its power spectrum
grid = np.zeros(1 << 19)
idx = (pos / pos[-1] * (len(grid) - 1)).astype(int)
grid[idx] = 1.0
spectrum = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(grid)) ** 2
top = np.sort(np.argsort(spectrum)[-9:])
print("dominant Fourier bins:", top)
print("ratio of top bins ~ tau:", top[-1] / top[-2])
```

The spectrum concentrates on a sparse set of bins whose positions scale by *τ* — the one-dimensional shadow of the Bragg-peak phenomenon proved in general by Hof [4].

## 6 Limitations

The ideal theory is not the whole story, and its boundaries are instructive.

- **Window regularity is generic, not universal.** Singular model sets — where lattice points project onto the window boundary — have full measure zero among windows but include highly symmetric cases physicists care about. Their diffraction requires separate, delicate analysis.
- **The inverse problem is unsolved.** Distinct point sets can share identical diffraction patterns (*homometry*); the Bragg peaks determine the autocorrelation but not the structure. No general reconstruction theorem exists for aperiodic structures.
- **Real quasicrystals are imperfect.** Thermal motion, chemical disorder, and *phason* fluctuations — rearrangements corresponding to window-boundary crossings in internal space — broaden peaks and add diffuse scattering. The random-tiling hypothesis even attributes entropic stabilization to an ensemble of tilings rather than a single ideal one.
- **Pure point diffraction is fragile.** The Thue–Morse substitution shows that aperiodic order need not diffract sharply at all: its spectrum is purely singular continuous. The Pisot condition is sufficient in key cases but the general characterization remains open [6].
- **Computation scales badly.** Icosahedral model sets live in six dimensions with polyhedral windows; evaluating window Fourier transforms and indexing dense Bragg-peak sets is numerically demanding, and intensities span many orders of magnitude, complicating structure refinement against experimental data [1][2].

## 7 Conclusion

The arc from Penrose's tilings to Shechtman's alloys to Hof's theorems is one of the cleanest examples of mathematics anticipating physics. A recreational tiling [5], an algebraic dualization trick, and a strip-projection method [3] turned out to be exactly the language needed when nature produced a solid with sharp diffraction and forbidden symmetry [1][2]. The cut-and-project framework explains *why* aperiodic structures can diffract like crystals: they inherit almost periodicity from a higher-dimensional lattice, and the window's Fourier transform sculpts the Bragg intensities [4].

Open frontiers remain: the inverse (homometry) problem, the full Pisot spectral conjecture in higher dimensions, and the growing zoo of soft-matter and photonic quasicrystals that stretch the theory beyond metallic alloys. The systematic foundations laid out by Baake and Grimm [6] and the symmetry analyses in the spirit of Cotfas [7] provide the scaffolding. Aperiodic order has earned its place as a third way between periodicity and disorder — *order without repetition, diffraction without a lattice*.
