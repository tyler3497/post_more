# Spin Foam Models of Quantum Gravity: Discretized BF Theory, the EPRL Amplitude, and the Semiclassical Limit

## Abstract

Spin foam models formulate quantum gravity as a sum over histories of spin networks, realizing Feynman's path integral on a background-independent, discrete spacetime. The canonical route passes through topological *BF* theory, whose quantization yields a spin foam amplitude labeled by unitary irreducible representations of the gauge group, and reaches general relativity only after imposing the *simplicity constraints* that break the topological symmetry and restore local degrees of freedom. This thesis develops the full arc: from the Ponzano–Regge quantization of three-dimensional gravity and its exact asymptotic relation to the Regge action, through the Barrett–Crane model and its well-documented shortcomings — degenerate-sector dominance, frozen intertwiner degrees of freedom, and an incorrect graviton propagator — to the Engle–Pereira–Rovelli–Livine (EPRL) and Freidel–Krasnov (FK) models, in which coherent-state intertwiners and the linear simplicity constraints yield a vertex amplitude whose large-spin stationary-phase asymptotics reproduce the Regge action with calculable measure factors. We treat the group field theory completion, in which the sum over 2-complexes becomes the Feynman expansion of a nonlocal field theory on the group

## 1 Intro

The central obstacle of quantum gravity is conceptual before it is computational: general relativity teaches that spacetime is dynamical, so the quantum theory must define its observables and its path integral without a fixed background metric on which perturbation theory could lean [1]. Loop quantum gravity (LQG) answers this by quantizing the *kinematical* phase space of connection variables, obtaining a Hilbert space spanned by spin network states — graphs whose edges carry irreducible representations of *SU(2)* and whose vertices carry intertwiners — on which geometric operators such as area and volume acquire discrete spectra [1]. But a Hilbert space is not a dynamics. The missing piece is the transition amplitude between spin network states, the physical inner product that selects solutions of the Hamiltonian constraint.

Spin foam models supply precisely this piece. A spin foam is a two-complex — a collection of vertices, edges, and faces — decorated with group-theoretic data: irreducible representations labeling faces, intertwiners labeling edges. It is the two-dimensional history of a spin network, in exactly the sense that a worldsheet is the history of a string. The partition function is a sum over such foams,

$$\mathcal{Z} = \sum_{\text{foams}} \sum_{j_f, \iota_e} \prod_f A_f \prod_e A_e \prod_v A_v ,$$

where the face, edge, and vertex amplitudes $A_f$, $A_e$, $A_v$ define the model. What makes this program powerful — and delicate — is that the most natural starting point is not general relativity itself but the *topological* theory known as *BF* theory, whose path integral can be rigorously discretized into a spin foam. Gravity emerges only after *simplicity constraints* are imposed, restricting the topological configurations to geometric ones [2][3].

> Theorem: *Discretized BF theory.* Let $M$ be a compact oriented four-manifold triangulated by $\Delta$, with dual 2-complex $\Delta^*$. The partition function of $SO(4)$ (or $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$) BF theory on $\Delta^*$ reduces exactly to a spin foam state sum with face amplitude $\dim j_f$, edge amplitude from the Haar projector, and vertex amplitude the *15j* symbol. This model is topological: it is invariant under refinements of the triangulation [2][3].

The history of spin foam gravity is the history of successive attempts to constrain this topological amplitude correctly. The Barrett–Crane model (1998) imposed the simplicity constraints at the *quantum* level and produced a vertex amplitude — the *10j* symbol — that was beautiful but ultimately defective [2]. The Engle–Pereira–Rovelli–Livine (EPRL) and Freidel–Krasnov (FK) models (2007–2008) imposed a *linearized* version of the constraints using $SU(2)$ coherent states, yielding a vertex amplitude that, in the large-spin limit, reproduces the Regge action of discretized general relativity with the correct phase [4][5]. This thesis reconstructs this development in full, derives the semiclassical asymptotics via stationary-phase analysis, presents the group field theory (GFT) completion in which the sum over complexes becomes perturbative quantum field theory, and assesses the numerical and analytical evidence for the semiclassical limit alongside the limitations that remain: the flatness problem, divergence structure, and the still-open continuum limit [4][5][6][7][8].

## 2 Background

### BF theory and its discretization

*BF* theory is the gauge theory with action

$$S_{BF}[B,A] = \int_M \mathrm{Tr}\,(B \wedge F(A)),$$

where $A$ is a connection one-form and $B$ a Lie-algebra-valued $(d-2)$-form. The equations of motion are $F(A) = 0$ and $d_A B = 0$: every solution is flat, so the theory has *no local degrees of freedom* — it is topological [3]. Upon discretization on a 2-complex, the curvature becomes the holonomy around each face, and integrating out the $B$ field enforces flatness as a product of delta functions, $\prod_f \delta(g_{f})$ with $g_f$ the face holonomy. Expanding the delta function in the Peter–Weyl basis,

$$\delta(g) = \sum_j \dim(j)\,\chi_j(g),$$

converts the flatness condition into a sum over representations — the spin foam [2][3]. For $SU(2)$ in three dimensions this yields the Ponzano–Regge model, with 6j symbols at each vertex of the dual complex; for $SO(4)$ in four dimensions it yields the *15j* amplitude of BF theory.

General relativity in the Plebanski formulation is BF theory *plus constraints*: writing the two-form $B$ in terms of a tetrad $e$ as $B = {\star}(e \wedge e)$, the constraint

$$\epsilon_{IJKL}\, B^{IJ} \wedge B^{KL} \propto \epsilon\,\epsilon_{IJKL}\, e^I \wedge e^J \wedge e^K \wedge e^L$$

restricts $B$ to be *simple*, i.e. decomposable as a wedge of one-forms [2]. Quantizing the constraints is the hard step: imposed naively, they are second-class and do not commute, so the Dirac procedure and the Gupta–Bleuler weak-imposition strategy give different answers — a distinction that separates the old Barrett–Crane model from the modern EPRL construction.

### The Ponzano–Regge lesson

Three-dimensional gravity is topological and therefore exactly solvable. The Ponzano–Regge model assigns to each tetrahedron of a triangulation the *6j* symbol of $SU(2)$,

$$\mathcal{Z}_{PR} = \sum_{\{j_e\}} \prod_e (-1)^{2j_e}(2j_e+1) \prod_{\text{tet}} \begin{Bmatrix} j_1 & j_2 & j_3 \ j_4 & j_5 & j_6 \end{Bmatrix},$$

and its large-spin asymptotics were shown by Roberts to satisfy the *Ponzano–Regge conjecture*:

> Theorem: *Ponzano–Regge asymptotics.* For spins $j_e = \lambda k_e$ with $\lambda \to \infty$, the 6j symbol satisfies
> $$\begin{Bmatrix} \lambda k_1 & \cdots \ \cdots & \lambda k_6 \end{Bmatrix} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{12\pi V}}\cos\left(S_{\mathrm{Regge}} + \frac{\pi}{4}\right),$$
> where $V$ is the volume of the Euclidean tetrahedron with edge lengths $l_e = k_e + 1/2$ and $S_{\mathrm{Regge}} = \sum_e l_e \theta_e$ is the Regge action with dihedral angles $\theta_e$.

This is the template for the entire semiclassical program in four dimensions: if a model's vertex amplitude oscillates with the *Regge action* at large spins, its low-energy limit plausibly contains general relativity [2]. The Regge action is the discrete Einstein–Hilbert functional, $S_R = \sum_h A_h \epsilon_h$, summing over hinges (triangles in 4D) with area $A_h$ and deficit angle $\epsilon_h$.

## 3 Methodology

### The Barrett–Crane construction and its failure modes

Barrett and Crane quantized the simplicity constraints strongly: a representation $(j^+, j^-)$ of $Spin(4) \simeq SU(2) \times SU(2)$ is *simple* when the second Casimir vanishes, i.e. $j^+ = j^-$ (in the Riemannian signature), and the intertwiner at each tetrahedron must be the *unique* Barrett–Crane intertwiner, the projector onto simple representations [2]. The resulting vertex amplitude is the *10j* symbol. Barrett and Williams computed its large-spin asymptotics by stationary phase over the five normals $x_i \in S^3$ parametrizing the five tetrahedra of a 4-simplex:

$$A_v(j_{ik}) \sim \sum_\sigma P(\sigma)\cos\left(S_{\mathrm{Regge}}(\sigma) + \kappa \tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) + D,$$

with the sum over geometric 4-simplices $\sigma$ whose triangle areas are set by the spins [2]. The phase is the Regge action — encouraging. But Baez, Christensen, and Egan showed that the *degenerate* term $D$, coming from configurations where tetrahedron normals coincide, actually *dominates* the asymptotics [2]. Three further defects accumulated: (i) the intertwiner space is one-dimensional, so the model cannot capture the full boundary Hilbert space of LQG, where intertwiners carry genuine degrees of freedom; (ii) the graviton two-point function computed from the model does not reproduce the free graviton propagator at leading order [5]; (iii) the constraints are imposed too strongly, eliminating degrees of freedom that the classical theory retains.

### The EPRL/FK construction

The new models of 2007–2008 reformulate the simplicity constraints *linearly* using $SU(2)$ coherent states. The key object is the *Y-map* (EPRL embedding),

$$Y_\gamma : V_j \longrightarrow V_{(j^+, j^-)}, \qquad j^\pm = \frac{1 \pm \gamma}{2}\, j,$$

which embeds the spin-$j$ representation of the diagonal $SU(2)$ (the one of canonical LQG) into the lowest-weight subspace of the $Spin(4)$ representation selected by the Immirzi parameter $\gamma$; in the Lorentzian theory the embedding targets the principal-series representations of $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ with Casimir labels $(\rho = \gamma j, k = j)$ [5]. The vertex amplitude is then the contraction of coherent intertwiners:

$$A_v^{\mathrm{EPRL}} = \sum_{i_1 \ldots i_5} \prod_a d_{i_a}\, \left\{15j\right\}_{Spin(4)}\!(j^\pm_{ab}, i_a)\; \langle \iota_{i_a} | Y_\gamma^{\otimes 4} | n_a \rangle,$$

where $|n_a\rangle$ are Livine–Speziale coherent intertwiners labeled by the normals of the tetrahedron, and the fusion coefficients implement the simplicity constraints weakly [5]. The FK model reaches an equivalent amplitude from the coherent-state path integral with a slightly different constraint imposition; the two agree for the parameter ranges of physical interest [5].

The following table summarizes the model landscape:

| Model | Constraint imposition | Intertwiner space | Semiclassical phase |
|---|---|---|---|
| BF (Ooguri) | none (topological) | full | topological invariant |
| Barrett–Crane | strong, quadratic | 1-dimensional | Regge action + dominant degenerate sector [2] |
| EPRL | weak, linear (coherent states) | full $SU(2)$ intertwiner space | Regge action, correct boundary states [4][5] |
| FK | coherent-state path integral | full | agrees with EPRL in physical regime [5] |
| q-deformed EPRL | weak + quantum group | full, cut off at $j_{\max}$ | Regge action with $\Lambda \neq 0$ [6] |

### Numerical methodology

Because the Lorentzian EPRL vertex is a non-compact oscillatory integral, direct evaluation is extremely costly. Two complementary numerical strategies exist. Christensen's algorithm for the Riemannian models evaluates vertex amplitudes by contracting $SU(2)$ 15j symbols with fusion coefficients, extended to factored boundary states to test wave packet propagation and the graviton propagator [5]. For the Lorentzian theory, Dona and Speziale decomposed $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ Clebsch–Gordan coefficients into $SU(2)$ ones, enabling the first direct numerical evaluations of the Lorentzian EPRL vertex and a quantitative comparison with the Barrett–et al. saddle-point asymptotics — power-law decay plus Regge-phase oscillations [4]. Monte Carlo methods complement this: the Riemannian Barrett–Crane amplitude is *nonnegative* for closed foams, permitting Metropolis sampling of expectation values [1].

A minimal Python sketch of the stationary-phase logic used in the asymptotics:

```python
import numpy as np

def regge_action_4simplex(areas, dihedral_angles):
    """S_Regge = sum over the ten triangles of A_ik * theta_ik."""
    return float(np.sum(areas * dihedral_angles))

def vertex_asymptotics(areas, thetas, n_stationary=2):
    """Large-spin template: sum over geometric 4-simplices of
    measure * cos(S_Regge + kappa*pi/4), cf. Barrett-Williams [2]."""
    S = regge_action_4simplex(areas, thetas)
    measure = 1.0 / np.sqrt(np.prod(areas) + 1e-300)  # schematic
    return measure * sum(np.cos(S + k * np.pi / 4) for k in range(n_stationary))
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Discretized BF theory as a topological state sum

The exact solvability of BF theory is the foundation everything else deforms. In the path integral, integrating the $B$ field on each face of the dual 2-complex produces $\delta(g_f)$, and Peter–Weyl expansion converts geometry into representation theory: faces carry spins, edges carry the Haar projector $\mathcal{P} = \int dg\, D^{j_1}(g) \otimes \cdots \otimes D^{j_n}(g)$ onto the invariant subspace, and vertices carry the *15j* contraction. Because flatness is enforced exactly, the amplitude depends only on the topology of the complex — Pachner moves leave $\mathcal{Z}$ invariant. This is both the triumph and the limitation: any theory of gravity must *break* this topological invariance by constraining which representations and intertwiners contribute, and the constraint must reduce, in the semiclassical limit, to the statement that the discrete $B$ field is the wedge product of a discrete tetrad [2][3].

A useful formalization of the constraint problem in a dependently-typed idiom makes the distinction between strong and weak imposition explicit:

```haskell
-- Simplicity as a predicate on bivectors, à la Plebanski
data Simplicity (b : Bivector) : Type where
  Simple :: (e1 e2 : OneForm) -> b = wedge e1 e2 -> Simplicity b

-- Strong imposition: project the representation space
strongImpose :: Rep Spin4 -> Rep Spin4
strongImpose (jPlus, jMinus) =
  if jPlus == jMinus then (jPlus, jMinus) else zero  -- Barrett-Crane

-- Weak imposition: constrain expectation values on coherent states
weakImpose :: CoherentState -> Expectation -> Bool
weakImpose psi obs = abs (expect psi (casimir2) - gamma * expect psi (casimir1)) < eps
```

### 4.2 The EPRL vertex: coherent intertwiners and the Y-map

The EPRL innovation is to parametrize the boundary tetrahedron by a *coherent intertwiner* $|\iota(n_1,\ldots,n_4)\rangle = \int_{SU(2)} dg\, \bigotimes_a g|j_a, n_a\rangle$, peaked on the classical geometry with face normals $n_a$ and areas proportional to $j_a$ [5]. The Y-map embeds each $SU(2)$ leg into the simple sector of $Spin(4)$ (or the $\gamma$-simple sector of $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$), so that the vertex amplitude is the evaluation of a $Spin(4)$ spin network on the boundary data:

$$A_v(j_{ab}, n_{ab}) = \int_{Spin(4)^5} \prod_a dg_a \prod_{a<b} \langle j_{ab}, n_{ab} | Y_\gamma^\dagger g_a^{-1} g_b Y_\gamma | j_{ab}, n_{ba} \rangle.$$

For boundary data corresponding to a genuine Lorentzian 4-simplex, stationary-phase analysis (Barrett et al.) shows the amplitude is dominated by critical points where the $g_a$ reconstruct the 4-simplex geometry, and the phase of each contribution is $\pm S_{\mathrm{Regge}}$ [4]. This is the precise sense in which the EPRL model passes the test the Barrett–Crane model failed: the Regge phase emerges *without* degenerate-sector dominance, and the boundary states span the full LQG intertwiner space.

### 4.3 Large-spin asymptotics and the semiclassical limit

The semiclassical limit is the double scaling $\hbar \to 0$, $j \to \infty$ with the physical area $A = \gamma \ell_P^2 \sqrt{j(j+1)}$ held fixed. At leading order the EPRL vertex behaves as

$$A_v \sim \sum_{\text{crit}} N(j)\, e^{\pm i S_{\mathrm{Regge}}/\hbar} (1 + O(j^{-1})),$$

with the measure factor $N(j)$ now understood geometrically — Kaminski and Steinhaus gave the first geometric interpretation of the measure for the two-simplex (4-sphere) amplitude in the Barrett–Crane case, showing it encodes the gluing of simplices through shared tetrahedra [2]. The Lorentzian numerics of Dona and Speziale confirm the analytic prediction: for Regge boundary data the amplitude exhibits the predicted power-law decay in the spins with superimposed oscillations at the Regge frequency, and truncation of the $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ decomposition to the first term already reproduces the simplified EPRL model qualitatively [4].

In Rust-flavored pseudocode, the asymptotic evaluation pipeline is:

```rust
/// Evaluate the semiclassical vertex for one geometric 4-simplex.
fn eprl_vertex_asymptotic(spins: &[f64; 10], thetas: &[f64; 10]) -> Complex {
    let s_regge: f64 = spins.iter().zip(thetas).map(|(j, t)| area(*j) * t).sum();
    let measure = geometric_measure(spins); // Kaminski-Steinhaus style [2]
    // sum over the two orientation sectors
    measure * (Complex::cis(s_regge) + Complex::cis(-s_regge))
}
```

### 4.4 Group field theory: second quantization of spin foams

Group field theory completes the picture by generating the *sum over complexes* itself. A GFT is a field theory on $d$ copies of the gauge group, e.g. $\varphi: SU(2)^4 \to \mathbb{C}$ for 4D Riemannian gravity, with action

$$S[\varphi] = \frac{1}{2}\int [dg]^4\, \varphi\, \mathcal{K}\, \varphi + \frac{\lambda}{5!}\int [dg]^{10}\, \varphi^5\, \mathcal{V},$$

where the quintic interaction glues five tetrahedra into a 4-simplex.

### 4.5 The cosmological constant and quantum deformation

With $\Lambda \neq 0$, the gauge group deforms to the quantum group $SL_q(2,\mathbb{C})$ with $q$ a root of unity set by $\Lambda \ell_P^2$, and the representation sum truncates at a maximum spin — rendering all amplitudes finite [6].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

What counts as "empirical" in a background-independent quantum gravity program is necessarily indirect: exact mathematical results about the amplitudes, numerical evaluation, and recovery of known low-energy physics.

1. **Analytic asymptotics.** The Barrett–Williams computation for the Barrett–Crane vertex and its EPRL generalization by Barrett, Dowdall, Fairbairn, Hellmann, and Pereira prove that the vertex phase is the Regge action for non-degenerate boundary data [2][4]. The Ponzano–Regge theorem in 3D (proved by Roberts) is the exact prototype [2].

2. **Numerical confirmation.** Dona and Speziale's direct evaluation of the Lorentzian EPRL vertex matches the saddle-point asymptotics — power-law decay and Regge oscillations — for both vector and Regge boundary geometries [4]. Christensen's Riemannian numerics confirmed the Barrett–Crane asymptotics including the dominant degenerate sector, and showed that the new models' wave packet propagation does not match semiclassical expectations under unfrozen-spin conditions, a cautionary result [5].

3. **Positivity and Monte Carlo.** The Riemannian Barrett–Crane amplitude is nonnegative on closed foams, so Metropolis sampling can compute expectation values of geometric observables — one of the few cases where a 4D spin foam model admits genuine statistical computation [1].

4. **Graviton propagator.** The EPRL/FK two-point function, computed by Bianchi, Modesto, Rovelli and collaborators by expanding around a semiclassical boundary state, reproduces the free graviton propagator at leading order in the large-distance limit — the sharpest available check that the model contains linearized general relativity [5].

## 6 Limitations

The program's open problems are substantial and should be stated plainly.

- **The flatness problem.** Recent analyses of multi-vertex amplitudes (effective spin foams, and the EPRL model on complexes with bulk faces) show that in the semiclassical limit the amplitude is dominated by *flat* configurations: curvature degrees of freedom are exponentially suppressed when several 4-simplices are glued, because the face amplitude's phase structure favors vanishing deficit angles. Whether the physical EPRL model truly suffers this suppression at all orders, or whether it is an artifact of approximations, remains contested [4][5].
- **Divergences.** The sum over spins diverges for the most natural face amplitudes; the degree of divergence grows with the complex. The q-deformation regulates this at the cost of introducing $\Lambda$, and GFT renormalization is still in its infancy for 4D gravity models [6][7].
- **Continuum limit.** No spin foam model has a controlled continuum limit. Diffeomorphism symmetry is broken by the discretization, and its restoration — the analogue of the renormalization-group fixed point — is unproven. Refinement must be accompanied by a coarse-graining flow of the amplitudes, a program only begun [8].
- **Lorentzian signature.** Most rigorous results are Riemannian. The Lorentzian EPRL amplitude involves non-compact integrals over $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$; its finiteness, its measure, and the precise form of its asymptotics for arbitrary complexes are less developed than the Euclidean case [4].
- **Matter coupling.** Coupling matter fields to spin foams is largely unexplored; the restricted models that exist (e.g. on hypercubic 2-complexes) are proof-of-concept [8].
- **Canonical–covariant matching.** The precise relation between the EPRL transition amplitude and the physical Hilbert space of canonical LQG — i.e. whether the spin foam sum implements the Hamiltonian constraint — is still conjectural [1].

## 7 Conclusion

Spin foam models convert the deepest question of quantum gravity — what is the quantum dynamics of spacetime geometry? — into a concrete computational program: discretize BF theory exactly, impose the simplicity constraints that turn topology into geometry, and verify that the large-spin limit returns the Regge action. The Barrett–Crane model showed that strong imposition of the constraints produces the right phase but the wrong physics, dominated by degenerate configurations and starved of boundary degrees of freedom [2]. The EPRL and Freidel–Krasnov models, by imposing the linearized constraints weakly on coherent states, restored the full intertwiner space and recovered the Regge phase with the correct boundary data, confirmed numerically in the Lorentzian regime [4][5]. Group field theory elevates the construction to a genuine quantum field theory, generating the sum over complexes as its Feynman expansion and opening the renormalization-group perspective [7][8], while quantum deformation with a cosmological constant renders the amplitudes finite and predicts deformed Poincaré phenomenology [6].

The road ahead is well-marked: control the flatness problem, establish divergence structure and the continuum limit, extend rigorous results to Lorentzian signature, and couple matter. But the core achievement stands — a background-independent, nonperturbative path integral for quantum gravity whose semiclassical limit is, term by term, discretized general relativity.
