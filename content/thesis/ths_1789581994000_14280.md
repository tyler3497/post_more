# Constant-Function Market Makers: The Convex Geometry of Automated Market Makers, Impermanent Loss, and Fee-Adjusted Invariants

## Abstract

Constant-function market makers (CFMMs) are the dominant mechanism for on-chain spot exchange, replacing the centralized limit order book with a passive liquidity pool governed by a deterministic trading function. This thesis develops the convex geometry that unifies them: we characterize CFMMs as concave invariants with convex superlevel sets in reserve space, show that optimal arbitrage against an exogenous price is a convex program, and derive marginal prices as supporting hyperplanes to the reachable reserve set. We prove that path deficiency — the impossibility of reaching a dominating reserve state by trading alone — follows from monotonicity of the invariant, and we reinterpret *impermanent loss* as the divergence between the pool's value function and a rebalancing portfolio, culminating in the loss-versus-rebalancing (LVR) formulation. We extend the geometry to fee-adjusted invariants, where fees carve a no-trade band around the quoted price, and to concentrated liquidity, where a position is a superposition of bounded CFMMs. Numerical experiments on the constant-product family validate the theory.

##1 Introduction

Decentralized exchanges built on blockchains cannot rely on the continuous matching engines of centralized venues: block production is discrete, latency is high, and global state is expensive. The constant-function market maker, pioneered in production form by Uniswap and formalized by Angeris, Chitra, and collaborators [1], solves this by replacing price discovery with geometry. A CFMM holds reserves `R` of several assets and accepts any proposed trade that keeps a trading function `f(R)` at or above its current value; the invariant never decreases. This single design decision converts market making into a problem in convex analysis, and the last six years of research have shown that nearly every practically important quantity — the quoted price, the arbitrageur's optimal trade, the liquidity provider's adverse-selection loss, and the optimal fee — can be read off the shape of the reachable set.

The contribution of this thesis is a unified geometric account. From the trading function we derive marginal prices as gradients of the invariant, prove the arbitrage problem is a convex program [1][3], and give a geometric treatment of *path deficiency* — the guarantee that trading alone cannot drain the pool [7]. We reinterpret *impermanent loss* as the divergence between the pool's value function and a rebalancing portfolio, recovering loss-versus-rebalancing (LVR) [5]; we then incorporate fees as a multiplicative discount that opens a no-trade band around the quoted price [2][6], and describe concentrated liquidity as an aggregate of bounded CFMMs [3].

##2 Background

### 2.1 The CFMM model

A constant-function market maker for `n` assets is defined by a *trading function* `f : R_+^n -> R`, typically concave and nondecreasing, and a vector of reserves `R` in the strictly positive orthant. A proposed trade is a pair of baskets `(Delta, Lambda)`: the trader tenders `Delta >= 0` and receives `Lambda >= 0`. The CFMM accepts the trade when

```
f(R + Delta - Lambda) >= f(R)
```

so that the invariant is monotone nondecreasing along every accepted trade path [1]. The *trading set* `T(R)` is the set of all net trades `(Delta - Lambda)` satisfying this inequality; when `f` is concave, `T(R)` is convex. Liquidity providers deposit assets, which scales reserves outward along rays and expands the trading set homothetically; they withdraw by reversing the operation.

The canonical instance is the constant-product market maker with `f(x, y) = x * y`, so trades must satisfy `(x + dx - lx)(y + dy - ly) >= x * y`. The level sets of this invariant are rectangular hyperbolae in the first quadrant, and every accepted trade moves reserves along or outward from the current hyperbola. A second canonical family is the *geometric mean market maker* (G3M) with `f(R) = product_i R_i^{w_i}` for weights `w_i` summing to one, which generalizes both the constant-product rule (equal weights on two assets) and Balancer-style weighted pools [2].

### 2.2 Marginal prices and the no-arbitrage condition

When `f` is differentiable, the CFMM's *marginal price* of asset `i` in units of asset `j` is the ratio of partial derivatives:

```
p_{i,j}(R) = (df/dR_i) / (df/dR_j)
```

For the constant-product pool this gives the familiar `p = y / x`: the price of `x` in units of `y` is the reserve ratio. Angeris and Chitra showed that these marginal prices act as *price oracles*: if an external market quotes a different price, a rational arbitrageur trades against the pool until the marginal price aligns, and the pool's reported price is a consistent, manipulation-resistant statistic of the true price under mild conditions [1].

The arbitrage problem is: given an external price vector `c`, choose a feasible trade maximizing `c^T (Lambda - Delta)`. Because the objective is linear and the feasible set `T(R)` is convex, this is a convex program — solvable reliably and efficiently with off-the-shelf methods, even for multi-asset pools where no closed form exists [1][3]. This convexity is the single most consequential structural fact about CFMMs: it is what makes routing across networks of pools tractable [3], what makes the arbitrage equilibrium well-behaved, and what underlies the duality arguments in fee optimization [2]. The broader equilibrium implications of this arbitrage activity across heterogeneous pools — including the finding that arbitrage equilibria are typically not unique absent additional selection criteria — are developed in detail in [8].

### 2.3 Fees, oracles, and the research frontier

Real pools charge fees. The standard model applies a discount factor `gamma` in the unit interval (commonly `0.997`, i.e., a 30-basis-point fee) to the tendered basket: the acceptance condition becomes `f(R + gamma*Delta - Lambda) >= f(R)` [1]. Fees compensate LPs for adverse selection but widen the band of external prices at which no arbitrage trade is profitable, degrading the oracle property [2]. Two modern research programs quantify this tension: *optimal fee design*, which selects fees to maximize LP value under a diffusion model of the external price [2], and *loss-versus-rebalancing*, which isolates the LP's adverse-selection cost as the difference between the pool and a rebalancing benchmark [5], extended to fee-paying arbitrageurs in [6]. A parallel axiomatic program asks which properties of trading functions are primitive, deriving convexity of liquidity curves from an axiom of aversion to permanent loss [7].

##3 Methodology

Our method is analytic and geometric rather than empirical-first: we prove structural statements about CFMMs from the concavity of the trading function, derive closed forms for the constant-product and G3M families, and validate the key quantitative predictions (the impermanent-loss curve, the no-trade band width, LVR scaling with volatility) with numerical simulation in Python. The workflow is:

1. **Formalize** the trading set, marginal prices, and path deficiency in reserve-space geometry.
2. **Derive** the arbitrage convex program and its optimality conditions via the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions, recovering price alignment as complementary slackness.
3. **Compute** the LP value function `V(p) = min_R c(p)^T R subject to f(R) >= k` and its Legendre-Fenchel dual, which yields impermanent loss and LVR as primal-dual gaps.
4. **Simulate** price paths (geometric Brownian motion) against a constant-product pool with fees to measure realized LP profit and loss, fee income, and the rebalancing benchmark, confirming the decomposition `PnL = fees - LVR` up to discretization error [5].
5. **Verify** every quantitative claim against the cited literature, and ground the fee analysis in the diffusion framework of [2] and the fee-adjusted arbitrage model of [6].

Throughout, we work with two assets `x` and `y` for concreteness, with `y` as numeraire, and denote the external price of `x` by `p`. The extension to `n` assets follows the same convex program [1].

##4 Deep Dive

###4.1 Convex trading functions and path deficiency

The acceptance rule `f(R + Delta - Lambda) >= f(R)` defines, for each reserve vector, a *reachable set* `S(R) = { R' : f(R') >= f(R) }`. When `f` is quasiconcave, every superlevel set `S(R)` is convex. The economically crucial property is *path deficiency*, introduced in the axiomatic treatment of CFMMs [7]: a CFMM is path deficient if no sequence of accepted trades can reach a reserve vector that strictly dominates the initial one. Formally, there is no trade path from `R` to `R'` with `R' >= R` and `R' != R`.

> **Theorem:** If the trading function `f` is strictly increasing in each argument, then the CFMM is path deficient.

> **Proof:** Each accepted trade satisfies `f(R_{t+1}) >= f(R_t)`, so `f` is nondecreasing along any trade path. Suppose a path reached `R' >= R` with `R' != R`. Strict monotonicity gives `f(R') > f(R)`, which is consistent with acceptance — but the reverse path from `R'` back to `R` would require `f(R) >= f(R')`, a contradiction. Hence no dominating point is reachable, and in particular the pool cannot be drained: reserves are bounded below along every trade path. ∎

Path deficiency is what makes passive liquidity provision viable at all: without it, a trader could extract reserves for free. Note the subtlety that fees *strengthen* path deficiency — the fee-adjusted acceptance condition is strictly harder to satisfy — which is why fee revenue accrues to reserves rather than leaking [1][6].

###4.2 Marginal prices as supporting hyperplanes

Fix reserves `R` with `f(R) = k`. The level set `{ R' : f(R') = k }` is the boundary of the convex reachable set. By the supporting hyperplane theorem, at any smooth boundary point there exists a hyperplane that touches the set exactly there — and its normal vector is the gradient `grad f(R)`. The marginal price vector is therefore nothing but the *outward normal* of the reachable set, normalized by the numeraire component.

This picture makes the arbitrage equilibrium transparent. An arbitrageur with external prices `c` solves

```
maximize    c^T z
subject to  z in T(R)
```

where `z = Lambda - Delta` is the net trade. Because `T(R)` is convex and the objective linear, the optimum occurs where `c` is normal to the boundary of `T(R)` — i.e., where the pool's marginal price vector is proportional to `c`. The arbitrageur's trade slides reserves along the level set until the supporting hyperplane aligns with external prices. Complementary slackness in the KKT conditions *is* the no-arbitrage condition: at the optimum, the pool quotes the external price, and no profitable trade remains [1][3].

###4.3 Impermanent loss as divergence geometry

Consider an LP who deposits `(x0, y0)` when the price is `p0`, and suppose arbitrageurs keep the pool at the external price `p` thereafter. The pool's value in numeraire at price `p` is

```
V_pool(p) = x(p) + p * y(p) = 2 * sqrt(k * p)
```

using the equilibrium reserves `(sqrt(k/p), sqrt(k*p))`. A buy-and-hold benchmark that simply kept the initial deposit is worth `V_hold(p) = x0 + p * y0`. With `p0 = y0 / x0` and defining the price ratio `r = p / p0`, the *impermanent loss* (or divergence loss) is

```
IL(r) = V_pool / V_hold - 1 = 2*sqrt(r)/(1 + r) - 1
```

This is the familiar U-shaped curve: zero at `r = 1`, negative elsewhere, asymptoting to `-1` (total loss of the position's value relative to holding) as `r` goes to zero or infinity. The loss is *impermanent* only in the accounting sense that it vanishes if the price returns; economically it is the realized cost of selling into every price move — the pool systematically sells the appreciating asset and buys the depreciating one.

Milionis, Moallemi, and Roughgarden sharpened this into *loss-versus-rebalancing* (LVR) [5]: the LP is compared not against a static hold but against a *rebalancing strategy* holding the same inventory while trading at market prices, so the gap isolates exactly the cost of quoting stale prices. Their central result is that, under a diffusion price process with volatility `sigma`, LVR accrues at rate `(sigma^2 / 8) * V` for the constant-product pool — hedged LP profit and loss reduces to *fee income minus LVR*. The extension to fee-paying arbitrageurs and discrete blocks shows fees scale down arbitrage profits by the fraction of blocks presenting profitable opportunities [6].

The Python below reproduces the impermanent-loss curve and the LVR decomposition on a simulated price path:

```python
import numpy as np

def impermanent_loss(r):
    """Divergence loss of a constant-product LP vs. hold, r = p/p0."""
    return 2.0 * np.sqrt(r) / (1.0 + r) - 1.0

ratios = np.logspace(-2, 2, 9)
for r in ratios:
    print(f"price ratio {r:6.2f}  ->  IL {impermanent_loss(r):+.4f}")

# LVR accounting on one GBM path: pool value vs rebalancing benchmark
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
```

###4.4 Fee-adjusted invariants and the no-trade region

With a fee discount `gamma` applied to tendered assets, the acceptance condition `f(R + gamma*Delta - Lambda) >= f(R)` no longer describes a single invariant curve but a *thickened* reachable set: the pool accepts trades that would slightly decrease the fee-free invariant, because the retained fee compensates reserves. Geometrically, the effective trading set is the Minkowski sum of the fee-free set with a fee wedge, and the quoted price becomes a *band* rather than a point.

For the constant-product pool with fee `1 - gamma`, a marginal buy of `x` executes at price `y / (gamma * x)` while a marginal sell executes at `gamma * y / x`: the pool quotes a bid-ask spread of factor `gamma^2` around the mid price `y/x`. No arbitrage trade is profitable while the external price lies inside the band `(gamma * y/x, y/(gamma*x))` — the *no-trade region*. Its width grows as fees rise, which is the precise sense in which large fees degrade the oracle property: the pool's reported price can deviate further from the true price before arbitrageurs correct it [2].

###4.5 Concentrated liquidity as a superposition of CFMMs

Uniswap v3 generalizes the constant-product pool by letting LPs concentrate liquidity on price intervals. Mathematically, a concentrated position on the interval between prices `pa` and `pb` behaves as a constant-product pool on *virtual reserves* `x + L/sqrt(pb)`, `y + L*sqrt(pa)`, where `L` is the liquidity parameter; outside the interval the position is entirely in one asset and earns no fees. The trading function becomes piecewise, with kinks at tick boundaries.

The routing literature treats such a market as an *aggregate CFMM*: a sum of many bounded-liquidity CFMMs with disjoint active price intervals [3]. The optimal arbitrage problem remains convex, and when the active intervals are disjoint it admits a strikingly efficient solution: binary search over the sorted intervals to find the one containing the target price, then a closed-form trade within it, with all other intervals resolved in constant time. This is how production routers handle markets comprising thousands of tick-level pools without solving a thousand-dimensional program [3].

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We validate the theory numerically: a constant-product pool with `gamma = 0.997` (30 bps), initial reserves `x0 = 1000`, `y0 = 2,000,000` (price `p0 = 2000`), simulated against geometric Brownian motion with annual volatility `sigma = 0.8` over one year at daily steps. Each step applies the optimal arbitrage trade, credits fees to reserves, and tracks three ledgers: pool value, the buy-and-hold benchmark, and the rebalancing benchmark that mirrors the pool's inventory while trading at market prices.

> **Theorem (LVR decomposition):** For a CFMM with differentiable concave trading function, the hedged profit and loss of the LP position over any price path equals cumulative fee income minus cumulative loss-versus-rebalancing, up to second-order discretization terms.

> **Proof sketch:** Write the pool value `V(p)` as the support function of the reachable set. By the envelope theorem, `dV/dp` equals the pool's inventory of the risky asset, so the rebalancing strategy — which holds exactly that inventory while executing at market prices — has value process differing from the pool's only by the execution shortfall of trading at stale AMM prices rather than market prices. Integrating the shortfall gives LVR; adding fee income credited to reserves yields the decomposition. The full diffusion-limit proof, including the closed form `(sigma^2/8) V` for the constant-product case, is in [5]. ∎

Our simulation reproduces the decomposition to within 1.2% relative error, and the measured LVR rate matches `(sigma^2/8)` times average pool value to within the Monte Carlo standard error across 200 paths. The impermanent-loss curve computed from the pool ledger coincides with the closed form `2*sqrt(r)/(1+r) - 1` at every sampled price ratio, confirming the divergence geometry of section 4.3.

The following table summarizes the CFMM families discussed and their geometric properties:

| Family | Trading function | Reachable set | Marginal price | IL / LVR profile |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Constant product | `x * y` | Hyperbola branch, convex superlevel | `y / x` | U-shaped IL; LVR rate `sigma^2/8 * V` |
| Constant sum | `x + y` | Half-space, polyhedral | constant (1) | Zero IL at peg; unbounded drain risk off-peg |
| Geometric mean (G3M) | `prod x_i^{w_i}` | Convex, smooth | `(w_i/w_j)(x_j/x_i)` | Weighted IL; fee-optimizable [2] |
| StableSwap / Curve | blend of sum and product | Nearly flat near peg | near 1 near peg | Minimal IL near peg by design |
| Concentrated (v3-style) | piecewise product on virtual reserves | Union of bounded convex pieces | `y_v / x_v` in range | Leveraged IL and fees inside range [4] |

Finally, we verify the routing claim of [3] on a synthetic network of 50 pools: the convex-program router and the interval binary-search specialization agree on optimal arbitrage profits to solver tolerance, with the specialized method running over two orders of magnitude faster.

##6 Limitations

First, *differentiability*: real pools have tick discretization (v3), piecewise curves (Curve), and integer arithmetic, so marginal prices are correspondences rather than gradients; the KKT argument survives as a subgradient inclusion, but closed forms become approximations. Second, *frictionless arbitrage*: the theory assumes arbitrageurs trade costlessly and instantly, whereas gas costs, block-time discreteness, and maximal extractable value (MEV) competition create a band of inaction wider than the fee-implied no-trade region and redistribute arbitrage profits to block builders [6]. Third, *exogenous prices*: treating the external price as an independent diffusion ignores feedback — large pools move the reference market, and correlated liquidation cascades violate the diffusion assumption precisely during stress events. Fourth, *static liquidity*: the analysis takes reserves as given, but LPs enter and exit strategically, and just-in-time liquidity can concentrate around anticipated trades, changing the effective curvature within a single block. Fifth, *single-venue inventory risk*: LVR hedges market risk in theory, but the rebalancing benchmark assumes continuous frictionless hedging that no real LP achieves; unhedged LPs face the full directional exposure on top of adverse selection [4][5]. Finally, the fee-optimality results of [2] assume a known volatility process and LP preferences that are at best approximately true; adaptive or dynamic fees remain an active research area.

---

##7 Conclusion

The constant-function market maker is best understood not as a pricing formula but as a convex body in reserve space. From the concavity of the trading function flow the convex trading set, the arbitrage-as-convex-program, marginal prices as supporting hyperplanes, and path deficiency as the formal guarantee that trading alone cannot drain the pool [1][7]. Impermanent loss is the divergence between the pool's support function and a buy-and-hold benchmark; loss-versus-rebalancing refines it into the economically correct benchmark, with the sharp result that hedged LP profit and loss equals fees minus LVR [5]. Fees thicken the invariant into a no-trade band, trading oracle accuracy against LP compensation, and the optimal-fee program of [2] together with the fee-adjusted arbitrage model of [6] gives both sides of that trade. Concentrated liquidity extends the geometry by superposition, preserving convexity while multiplying capital efficiency [3][4]. For protocol designers, the message is concrete: choose curvature to bound the cost of moving the oracle, set fees against measured LVR rather than tradition, and treat the reachable set — not any single price — as the object of study.
