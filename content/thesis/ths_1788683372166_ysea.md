# Algebraic Effects and Handlers: Evidence Translation, Multishot Delimited Continuations, and Compilation in Koka and Eff

## Abstract

This thesis develops the theory and compilation of algebraic effects and their handlers, from the algebraic foundations of Plotkin and Pretnar to the modern evidence-passing compiler of the Koka language. We present the central semantic insight that effect handlers are folds over operation trees: a handled computation is a term in the free model of an equational theory, and a handler is the unique homomorphism extending a value interpretation. We contrast the dynamically scoped, untracked design of Eff with Koka's row-polymorphic type-and-effect inference, then give a full exposition of evidence-passing translation — the pipeline that lowers handlers through multi-prompt delimited control, generalized evidence vectors, yield bubbling, and a monadic translation into efficient C. We analyze multishot resumptions, the provably correct tail-resumptive optimization, deep versus shallow handlers, and selective non-CPS compilation, closing with metatheoretic results, benchmark evidence, and the field's open problems.

## 1. Introduction

Computational effects — mutable state, exceptions, nondeterminism, input/output, concurrency — have historically been tamed by two opposing forces. The *monadic* approach of Moggi encapsulates each effect in a bespoke datatype and composes effects with monad transformers, a design whose notorious non-compositionality is folklore. The *algebraic* approach of Plotkin and Power instead observes that impure behavior always arises from a small set of *operations* — `get`/`set` for state, `raise` for exceptions, `read`/`print` for I/O, `choose` for nondeterminism — and that these operations satisfy equational laws which determine the computation monad *uniformly* [1, 4].

Effect handlers, introduced by Plotkin and Pretnar in their 2009 ESOP paper and developed in the 2013 journal version [2, 3], complete the picture: where an operation is an *abstract* interaction, a handler is a *concrete interpretation* of those interactions, with privileged access to the delimited continuation of the operation call. Handling generalizes exception handling to arbitrary effects and, crucially, permits resuming the captured continuation **zero, one, or many times** — the multishot capability that strictly separates handlers from `try`/`catch` (which cannot resume) and `async`/`await` (which resumes exactly once).

Two designs anchor the practical story. **Eff** (Bauer and Pretnar [5]) keeps the theory's spirit: first-class, dynamically scoped, untracked effects and handlers — maximum expressiveness, minimal ceremony. **Koka** (Leijen [6]) bets on a Hindley–Milner-style *type-and-effect* inference engine with row polymorphism and full effect safety (a term lacking `exn` provably never throws). Its compiler lowers handlers not through whole-program CPS but through **evidence passing** — functions receive an evidence vector naming their dynamic handlers — culminating in Xie and Leijen's *generalized evidence passing* [8, 9], which emits efficient C.

The thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the algebraic theory of effects and the Plotkin–Pretnar handling calculus. Section 3 describes our methodology: a denotational reading of handlers as folds, instantiated in Eff and Koka code, then traced through the evidence-passing compilation pipeline. Section 4 is the deep dive, covering folds, the formal calculus, evidence translation, and multishot continuations. Section 5 collects the metatheoretic theorems (adequacy, type safety, contextual equivalence of the tail-resumptive optimization) and the empirical compilation results. Section 6 states limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Algebraic theories and free models

An *effect signature* Σ is a set of operations, each with a parameter type and an arity (result) type, written `op : A → B`. Operations are subject to equations *E*; together (Σ, *E*) is an *algebraic theory*. The fundamental correspondence of Plotkin and Power [4] states that giving a generic effect is equivalent to giving an algebraic operation, and that the monad of computations is the **free-model monad** of the theory: terms are operation trees whose leaves are return values and whose interior nodes are operation invocations; the monad laws hold modulo the equations.

A canonical example is global state. The signature has `get : 1 → S` and `set : S → 1` with equations `get; set = skip`, `set s; get = s`, `set s; set t = set t`, and so on. The free model is the state monad `S → (A × S)`; the equations are exactly the familiar state laws. Exceptions give `raise : exc → 0` over the theory with no equations beyond the unit, whose free model is `A + exc`. Nondeterminism gives `choose : 1 → Bool` with idempotence, commutativity, and associativity, whose free model is the powerset (list) monad.

### 2.2 From operations to handlers

Plotkin and Pretnar's 2009 breakthrough [2] was to treat the *handler* — previously only a special case for exceptions — as a first-class construct over arbitrary algebraic theories. A handler provides, for each operation `op`, a clause `op p k ↦ N` binding the parameter `p` and the **continuation** `k`, plus a return clause `return x ↦ M`. The handling construct

```
handle M with { return x ↦ Mᵣ } ⊎ { opᵢ p k ↦ Mᵢ }ᵢ
```

applies the handler *H* to a computation *M*. Operationally, evaluation of *M* proceeds until it either returns a value (handled by the return clause) or performs an operation `opᵢ(v)`, at which point the continuation up to the nearest enclosing handler is captured as `k : Bᵢ → C`, and the clause body `Mᵢ[v/p, k/k]` runs [2, 3, 5].

### 2.3 The landscape of implementations

The idea has since been realized across many systems, each making distinct trade-offs:

| System | Effect tracking | Handler scope | Multishot | Continuation strategy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Eff (Bauer–Pretnar) [5] | none (dynamic) | dynamic | yes | delimited control (custom runtime) |
| Koka (Leijen) [6, 8] | row-polymorphic inference | dynamic | yes | evidence passing → C |
| Effekt (Brachthäuser et al.) [12] | capability-based | **lexical** | yes | capability-passing CPS |
| Multicore OCaml [13] | none | dynamic | one-shot default | stack segments |
| Links (Hillerström–Lindley) [10] | row-polymorphic | dynamic | yes | CEK machine |
| Frank (Lindley et al.) [11] | ambient abilities | dynamic | shallow-oriented | — |

Eff elects *not* to track effects in the type system, following the ML tradition that the effect discipline should not burden the programmer; Koka elects *full* tracking, proving that a row-polymorphic type-and-effect system integrates smoothly with Hindley–Milner inference and even recovers Haskell's `runST`-style state encapsulation [6]. Effekt takes a third path — *lexical* handlers with capability-passing, where capabilities are second-class values that cannot leak [12].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is threefold. **First**, the *denotational* account: handlers as unique homomorphisms from the free model — folds over operation trees — yielding adequacy, compositionality, and the equations optimized implementations must respect. **Second**, concrete Eff and Koka code demonstrating deep and shallow handlers, multishot backtracking, and state rollback. **Third**, the *compilation* story: how evidence-passing translation [8] eliminates handlers at compile time, replacing dynamic lookup with evidence-vector indexing, multi-prompt delimited control with yield bubbling, and whole-program CPS with a selective translation that keeps effect-free code in direct style.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Effect Signatures and Handlers as Folds

The single most clarifying idea in the theory is due to the original paper [2]: *a handler is a fold*. View a computation as a tree

```
        op₁
       /   \
      v₁    op₂
           /   \
          v₂   return v₃
```

where interior nodes are operation invocations `op(v; y. c)` — the parameter `v`, and the continuation `y. c` — and leaves are returned values. A handler *H* with clauses `opᵢ p k ↦ Mᵢ` and `return x ↦ Mᵣ` defines an **algebra** (a model of the theory) on the result type: each operation symbol is interpreted by its clause body, with `k` standing for the recursive fold of the subtree. The handling construct `handle M with H` is then precisely the unique homomorphism from the free model (the computation tree) to this algebra that extends the return-clause map on leaves. This is the *universal property of the free model*, and it is why handler equations look like fold fusion laws.

> **Theorem (Handler as homomorphism [2, §2]).** Fix a return clause `in₁ : A → M` and clause bodies interpreting each `opᵢ`. Then `h(M) = handle M with H` is the unique homomorphism from the free computation monad to the model *M* extending `in₁`. In particular, `handle (return V) with H = Mᵣ[V/x]` and handling commutes with the algebraic operations modulo the equations of the theory.

Consider nondeterministic choice in Eff [5]:

```
effect choose {
  operation decide : unit -> bool
}

let all_results m =
  handle m with
  | val v      -> [v]
  | #decide () k -> k true @ k false
```

The clause for `decide` invokes the continuation `k` **twice** — a multishot use no exception mechanism can express. The fold reading is literal: the list model interprets `decide` as concatenation of the two subfolds, and `all_results` is the induced homomorphism into lists.

State with rollback shows how handlers compose *interpretations* rather than *effects*:

```koka
effect state<s>
  ctl get() : s
  ctl set(x : s) : ()

fun snapshot() : state<int> int
  val x = get()
  set(x + 10)
  x

val (r, s) = with handler
  var st := 0
  return x     -> (x, st)
  ctl get()    -> resume(st)
  ctl set(x)   -> { st := x; resume(()) }
  snapshot()
```

If an exception effect is handled *outside* this state handler, the local variable `st` is discarded on unwinding, giving transactional rollback for free — behavior the monadic world buys only with a `StateT`-over-`Either` stack and lifting boilerplate [5, 6].

### 4.2 The Plotkin–Pretnar Calculus

The formal core is a fine-grain call-by-value calculus. Values *V* and computations *M* are separated; operation calls have the form `op(V; y. M)`; the handling construct is

```
handle M with H        H ::= { return x ↦ Mᵣ } ⊎ { opᵢ p k ↦ Mᵢ }ᵢ
```

with the two characteristic β-rules [3]:

1. **(return)** `handle (return V) with H ↦ Mᵣ[V/x]`
2. **(op)** `handle E[opᵢ(V; y. M)] with H ↦ Mᵢ[V/p, {λy. handle E[return y] with H}/k]`

where *E* is an evaluation context with no intervening handler for `opᵢ`. Rule (op) is the whole operational story in one line: capture the continuation *up to the handler*, rebind it as `k`, and run the clause. The re-wrapping `handle E[return y] with H` inside `k` is exactly what makes the handler **deep** — resuming re-installs the handler around the remaining computation.

The 2013 journal version [3] adds full proofs — type safety, a free-model denotational semantics, and adequacy — while Pretnar's tutorial [7] shows handlers subsuming exceptions, generators, coroutines, backtracking search, and cooperative multithreading as instances of the same two rules.

A crucial design axis, already visible in the calculus, is **deep vs. shallow** handlers. In a deep handler, `k : Bᵢ →ᴱ′ C` — the continuation returns into the *handled* computation, so further operations are handled recursively. In a shallow handler, `k : Bᵢ →ᴱ A` — the continuation escapes the handler, and the handler processes only the *first* operation. Hillerström and Lindley showed that shallow handlers are macro-expressible via deep ones and vice versa only with restrictions, and connected shallow handlers to `control₀`/`prompt₀` delimited control [10]; Koka defaults to deep handlers but supports shallow ones explicitly.

### 4.3 Evidence-Passing Translation in Koka

Koka's headline contribution is not the handler semantics — it is making handlers *compile efficiently* without giving up type-and-effect inference. The problem: a naive implementation searches the dynamic handler stack at every `perform`, and a naive CPS transform rewrites the entire program. The solution, developed across Xie–Leijen 2020 [8] and generalized in 2021 [9], is **evidence-passing translation (EPT)**, refined into **generalized evidence passing (EPS)**.

The pipeline has four stages:

```
Koka source (handle / perform)
   │ ① elaboration with evidence vectors
   ▼
λ-calculus with multi-prompt delimited control + evidence
   │ ② yield bubbling (Yield/Pure monad)
   ▼
monadic λ-calculus (no control operators)
   │ ③ selective translation, join-point sharing, bind inlining
   ▼
plain C (libmprompt runtime / direct evidence dispatch)
```

**Stage ①.** Every effectful function takes an extra *evidence vector* `w`, a runtime list of handler records for its dynamic extent. An operation `perform ask ()` compiles to a lookup: with a fully known effect row `⟨read⟩`, Koka uses *canonical evidence vectors* and the lookup is a static offset `w[0]`; with a polymorphic row `⟨read | μ⟩` it degrades to a dynamic `find_ev(w, tag_read)` search [9]. A handler clause compiles to a record of function pointers installed into the vector.

**Stage ②.** Multi-prompt delimited control is defunctionalized into *yield bubbling*: every effectful step returns `Pure v` or `Yield(marker, op, k)`, and binds extend `k` as the yield bubbles to its prompt — the `Mp.Eff` monadic library [9], a Haskell implementation proving the translation is not C-specific.

**Stage ③.** The masterstroke is the **tail-resumptive optimization**: the overwhelmingly common pattern is a clause of the form `op p -> { ...; resume(v) }` where `resume` is invoked in tail position. Rather than capturing the continuation, yielding, and resuming — an expensive cycle — the translation evaluates the operation *in place*, splicing the clause body directly into the call site. Xie and Leijen prove this optimization contextually equivalent to the unoptimized semantics, a subtle result because it must remain correct in the presence of *unscoped* resumptions (continuations that escape their handler's dynamic extent) [9, §2.5, §2.9].

The generated C is startlingly direct. From [9], `perform ask () + perform ask ()` under a `read` handler becomes essentially:

```c
int expr(context_t* ctx) {
  int x = perform_ask(ctx->w[0], ctx);
  if (ctx->is_yielding) { yield_extend(&join2, ctx); return_0; }
  int y = perform_ask(ctx->w[0], ctx);
  if (ctx->is_yielding) { yield_extend(alloc_closure_join1(x,ctx), ctx); return_0; }
  return (x + y);
}
```

Effect-free code pays only a well-predicted branch on `is_yielding`; effect operations dispatch through one indirection. No heap-allocated continuations appear on the tail-resumptive fast path at all.

Koka's type system makes the static-offset case the common one: rows like `⟨exn, div | μ⟩` with duplicate labels and row polymorphism propagate precise effect information without annotations [6], and scoped labels let differently-named instances of one effect coexist without the accidental-capture bugs of dynamic scoping.

### 4.4 Multishot Delimited Continuations and One-Shot Optimization

Handlers are a *structured interface to delimited continuations* [10]: `perform` is `shift` (capture to the prompt), `handle` installs the prompt, and `resume` invokes the captured continuation. The continuation is **delimited** (it extends only to the handler, not to the top of the program) and, in the general semantics, **multishot** — it may be invoked any number of times, as the nondeterminism handler does.

Multishot resumptions are expensive. Copying a continuation means copying its stack segment; naive implementations pay O(depth) per resume. Two engineering responses dominate:

- **One-shot by default** (Multicore OCaml [13]): the runtime *moves* rather than copies the stack segment, giving O(1) resume, and raises a runtime error on a second invocation. Multishot is available via explicit cloning. The trade-off is dynamic: linearity of resumptions is unchecked statically.
- **Copy-on-resume with sharing** (Koka [9]): evidence passing represents the continuation as a closure chain (`yield_extend`), and multishot resume shares structure; the tail-resumptive fast path avoids materializing continuations entirely for the common case.

> **Theorem (Correctness of tail-resumptive optimization [9, §3.2]).** An operation clause whose resumption occurs in tail position evaluates in place to a term contextually equivalent to the yield-and-resume semantics, including in the presence of unscoped resumptions.

The proof proceeds by a logical relation over the evidence-passing semantics, showing that the in-place evaluation and the yield-bubble-and-resume evaluation are indistinguishable to all well-typed contexts. This is the result that lets Koka claim handler overhead "in the noise" for idiomatic code: the abstraction compiles away exactly where it is used most.

Finally, **selective CPS**: unlike Effekt's capability-passing translation [12], which CPS-converts the whole program, Koka translates only effectful functions to the monadic/Yield form and leaves total functions in direct style. The residual cost for straight-line code is one predictable `is_yielding` branch per effectful call — nearly free on modern processors [9, §2.8].

## 5. Empirical Results and Metatheoretic Proofs

The metatheory of handlers rests on three pillars, each proved in the primary literature:

1. **Type safety and adequacy** [3]. The Plotkin–Pretnar calculus enjoys progress and preservation; the denotational semantics via free models is *adequate* — denotational equality implies contextual equivalence — so the fold reading of Section 4.1 is not a metaphor but the actual semantics.
2. **Expressive power** [14]. Forster, Kammar, Lindley, and Pretnar show that effect handlers, monadic reflection, and delimited control are mutually macro-expressible (up to termination), pinning handlers' exact place in the hierarchy of control abstractions.
3. **Compilation correctness** [8, 9]. Evidence-passing translation is proved correct with respect to the operational semantics of handlers; the generalized evidence-passing semantics (EPS) lifts the scoped-resumption restriction of the original EPT, and the tail-resumptive optimization is proved contextually equivalent.

Empirically, Xie and Leijen [9] benchmark Koka-to-C on effect-heavy workloads — state threading, exception-heavy parsers, generator pipelines, backtracking search — finding evidence-vector dispatch adds negligible overhead over hand-written direct-style code, the tail-resumptive optimization keeps effectful loops at constant stack depth, and join-point sharing plus bind inlining remove the closure-allocation tax of naive monadic translations. The `Ev.Eff`/`Mp.Eff` Haskell libraries [8, 9] confirm evidence passing is a portable strategy, not a C-specific hack.

## 6. Limitations

The theory, for all its elegance, has sharp boundaries. **Equations are assumed away**: Plotkin and Pretnar's programming-oriented account drops the equational theory that justified the free-model semantics in the first place, and most implementations follow suit [2, 10]. Recovering equational reasoning — e.g., proving that a handler validates the state laws — remains manual.

**Effect inference is undecidable in general**: Koka infers precise rows, but exact effect typing is undecidable for Turing-complete languages (a loop may or may not raise), so inference is necessarily approximate; programmers occasionally supply annotations [6].

**Multishot remains second-class in production**: OCaml 5 restricts continuations to one-shot by default [13] precisely because multishot stack copying interacts badly with resources (file handles, locks) acquired inside the captured region — the *linearity* problem. Static linearity checking for resumptions is an open research area.

**Lexical vs. dynamic scoping is unresolved**: Eff and Koka use dynamic handler binding, which admits accidental handling (an inner handler catching an outer effect's operations); Effekt's lexical capabilities [12] fix this but complicate higher-order code. First-class *named* handlers in Koka [8] partially address it via generative effect instances.

Finally, **shallow handlers lack a mature optimization story**: the tail-resumptive optimization targets deep handlers; shallow handlers, which correspond to `control₀`/`prompt₀` [10], still pay the full capture cost in most implementations.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Algebraic effects and handlers have traveled, in a dozen years, from a semanticist's generalization of exception handling to a compilation target that emits competitive C. The through-line is the fold: a handler interprets an operation tree by the universal property of the free model, and every serious implementation is, at bottom, an efficient evaluator of that fold. Eff [5] showed the abstraction could be a *language*; Koka [6, 8, 9] showed it could be a *fast* language, via row-polymorphic inference that recovers static handler identity and an evidence-passing translation that turns dynamic dispatch into vector indexing and yield bubbling into straight-line code. The remaining frontiers — linear resumptions, equational reasoning about handlers, and the lexical/dynamic tension — will decide the next decade. What is no longer in doubt is the central claim of [2]: handling is the uniform, modular, and now efficient way to give meaning to computational effects.
