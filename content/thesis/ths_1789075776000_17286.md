# Verifiable Random Functions: From the Micali–Rabin–Vadhan Formalism Through ECVRF Standardization to Cryptographic Sortition, NSEC5, and the Post-Quantum Frontier

![Figure 1: The VRF API. KeyGen produces (sk, vk); Prove(sk, x) outputs a unique y and proof π; Verify(vk, x, y, π) accepts or rejects. Only the secret-key holder can evaluate; anyone holding the public key can verify.](ths_1789075776000_17286-0.webp)

## Abstract

Verifiable random functions (VRFs) are the public-key analogue of keyed cryptographic hashes: only the holder of a secret key can evaluate the function, yet anyone possessing the corresponding public verification key can confirm that a claimed output was computed correctly, learning nothing else [1]. Introduced by Micali, Rabin, and Vadhan in 1999, VRFs are characterized by three properties — uniqueness, pseudorandomness, and verifiability — and have matured from an inefficient RSA-based construction into standardized, widely deployed primitives. This thesis reconstructs the definitional foundations, then develops the principal constructions: the Dodis–Yampolskiy VRF from bilinear maps under the q-decisional bilinear Diffie–Hellman inversion assumption [2], and the elliptic-curve VRF ciphersuites standardized in RFC 9381 [3]. We examine two flagship deployments — Algorand's cryptographic sortition for Byzantine-agreement committee selection [5] and NSEC5's privacy-preserving DNSSEC denial of existence [4] — disentangle VRFs from verifiable delay functions [6, 7], analyze tightness and trusted-setup considerations [8], and chart post-quantum directions.

## 1 Introduction

Randomness is infrastructure. Leader election, proof-of-stake lotteries, protocol challenges, and privacy-preserving data structures all need values *unpredictable* before reveal and *unforgeable* after claim. Classical PRFs solve the unpredictability half — but only among key holders. In open, permissionless systems like public blockchains or the DNS, there is no shared secret: any participant must *verify* that a claimed random value was produced honestly, without being able to compute or bias it.

Verifiable random functions close this gap. A VRF behaves like a PRF keyed by *sk*, except the evaluator also emits a proof π, and anyone with the public verification key *vk* can check that *y* is the *unique* correct evaluation on *x*. The output is unpredictable to everyone but the key holder, yet its correctness is publicly checkable — the engine of *private* randomness, selecting committees, proposers, or lottery winners without revealing in advance who was selected.

This thesis follows the primitive from theory to standardization to deployment: the Micali–Rabin–Vadhan definition and its three properties; the Dodis–Yampolskiy and ECVRF constructions; two deployments exercising opposite facets — Algorand's *cryptographic sortition* (unpredictability for private committee sampling) and NSEC5 (verifiability for DNSSEC denial of existence without zone leakage); the VRF-vs-VDF distinction; and the trusted-setup and post-quantum questions shaping the primitive's future.

## 2 Background

The VRF sits at the intersection of several classical lines of work, and each contributes one ingredient of the modern understanding.

*Pseudorandom functions.* The Goldreich–Goldwasser–Micali construction showed that pseudorandom functions exist assuming one-way functions, giving keyed functions indistinguishable from random ones to anyone without the key. A VRF is, informally, "a PRF with proofs": the key holder can additionally *convince* others of correct evaluation [1].

*Unique signatures and verifiable unpredictable functions (VUFs).* A signature scheme is *unique* if each message admits exactly one valid signature per public key. Micali, Rabin, and Vadhan reached VRFs via VUFs — unpredictable but not necessarily pseudorandom functions — then applied a Goldreich–Levin hard-core bit to obtain pseudorandomness. The surviving lesson: *uniqueness of the output under the public key* is what makes verification meaningful, ruling out equivocation between two "correct" values.

*The original RSA-based VUF.* The 1999 construction works in ℤ*_m* for an RSA modulus *m = pq*: public key *PK = (m, r, Q, coins)*, secret key adding φ(*m*). A *prime sequence generator* maps each input *x* to a prime *p_x*, and the function outputs *r^{1/p_x} mod m* — computable only with φ(*m*) — under a variant of the RSA assumption. It is correct and secure but heavy: per-input prime generation, large keys, and the extra hard-core-bit step to reach pseudorandomness.

*Elliptic curves and pairings.* The Dodis–Yampolskiy construction needs *bilinear groups* with a pairing *e: G × G → G_T*; ECVRF needs only a prime-order curve with hash-to-curve. Both are analyzed in the *random oracle model*; RFC 9381 instantiates hashes with SHA-256/SHA-512 and explicit domain separation.

*Notation.* λ is the security parameter, *G* a group of prime order *q* with generator *B*, *x ←$ S* uniform sampling. VRF algorithms form the triple *(Gen, Prove, Verify)* with keys *(sk, vk)*, input *α*, output *β*, proof *π*.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Micali–Rabin–Vadhan Definition: Uniqueness, Pseudorandomness, Verifiability

A verifiable random function scheme is a triple of efficient algorithms [1]:

- **Gen(1^λ)** → *(sk, vk)*: randomized key generation producing a secret key and a public verification key.
- **Prove(sk, x)** → *(y, π)*: on input *x*, outputs the function value *y* and a proof *π* of correctness.
- **Verify(vk, x, y, π)** → *{0, 1}*: deterministic verification; outputs 1 iff *y* is the correct evaluation on *x*.

Three properties define security:

1. **Correctness.** For all *(sk, vk)* from Gen and all *x*, *(y, π) ← Prove(sk, x)* implies *Verify(vk, x, y, π) = 1*.
2. **Uniqueness.** No adversary given *vk* — even unbounded — can produce *x, y₁ ≠ y₂, π₁, π₂* with both proofs verifying: at most one acceptable output per input.
3. **Pseudorandomness.** An adversary with oracle access to *Prove(sk, ·)* chooses an unqueried challenge *x\** and receives either the real *y\** or a uniform string, *along with the proof π\**; its distinguishing advantage is negligible. The proof must leak nothing about *y\** beyond correctness.

> **Theorem (Uniqueness implies non-equivocation).** In any VRF, a malicious key holder cannot present two distinct outputs for the same input that both verify under its published verification key. Hence VRF outputs function as *commitments*: publishing *vk* commits the holder to exactly one value per input, before any input is chosen.

The pseudorandomness experiment is where VRFs differ most sharply from signatures: a signature is *supposed* to be public, while a VRF proof must be *zero-knowledge-like* with respect to the output. This is why the original construction needed the Goldreich–Levin step — the raw VUF output is unpredictable, but only a hard-core bit of it is provably pseudorandom given the proof.

### 4.2 The Dodis–Yampolskiy Construction and the q-DBDHI Assumption

In 2005, Dodis and Yampolskiy gave the first *practical* VRF, bypassing the expensive unique-signature-to-VRF transformation entirely [2]. Let *e: G × G → G_T* be a bilinear map with generator *g* of *G*:

- **Gen:** *sk ←$ ℤ_p*, *vk = g^sk*.
- **Prove(sk, x):** *y = e(g, g)^{1/(x+sk)}*, *π = g^{1/(x+sk)}*.
- **Verify(vk, x, y, π):** accept iff *e(g^x · vk, π) = y*.

Correctness is one pairing equation: *e(g^x · g^sk, g^{1/(x+sk)}) = e(g, g) = y*. Proofs are a single group element and keys constant-size — versus linear-size proofs before — and an *admissible hash* extends the small-domain scheme to arbitrary inputs.

Security rests on the **q-decisional bilinear Diffie–Hellman inversion (q-DBDHI)** assumption: given *(g, g^sk, …, g^{sk^q})*, no efficient adversary distinguishes *e(g, g)^{1/sk}* from random; the reduction maps the adversary's *q* oracle queries to the *q* powers and the challenge to the inversion target. The paper won the **PKC 2005 Best Paper Award** and the **2021 PKC Test-of-Time Award** [2].

On *tightness*: reductions for VRFs from non-interactive assumptions inherently lose a factor *Q* in the number of queries — Niehues proved this loss *unavoidable* via meta-reduction, then gave the first VRF whose q-DBDHI reduction achieves the optimal bound [8]. Parameters must absorb this linear loss, e.g., groups roughly log *Q* bits above the target level.

### 4.3 ECVRF over Elliptic Curves and RFC 9381

ECVRF, developed by Goldberg, Reyzin, Papadopoulos, and Včelák in the CFRG and published as **RFC 9381** in August 2023, carried VRFs into Internet standards [3]. It needs no pairings — only a prime-order curve and hash-to-curve — with security proved in the random oracle model under DDH, including concrete analysis that fixed bugs in prior treatments.

Four ciphersuites are defined — *ECVRF-P256-SHA256-TAI*, *-SSWU*, *ECVRF-EDWARDS25519-SHA512-TAI*, and *-ELL2* — differing in curve, hash, and hash-to-curve method (try-and-increment vs. SWU/Elligator2). The core algorithm is uniform; with base point *B*, secret scalar *x*, public key *Y = x·B*:

1. *H = ECVRF_hash_to_curve(suite, Y, α)* — map the input to a curve point;
2. *Γ = x·H* — the secret scalar multiplication that only the key holder can perform;
3. choose *k ←$ [1, q−1]*; *U = k·B*; *V = k·H*;
4. *c = ECVRF_hash_points(H, Γ, U, V)* — Fiat–Shamir challenge;
5. *s = (k + c·x) mod q*; *π = encode(Γ, c, s)*; output *β = ECVRF_proof_to_hash(Γ)*.

Verification recomputes *U′ = s·B − c·Y*, *V′ = s·H − c·Γ*, re-derives the challenge, and accepts on match; *β* is then derived from Γ. The proof is a Schnorr-style proof of knowledge of *x*, *simultaneously* bound to the statement "*Γ = x·H*" — which is what makes the output unique and verifiable.

![Figure 2: The ECVRF-EDWARDS25519-SHA512-ELL2 construction. The input α is hashed to a curve point H; the secret scalar x produces Γ = x·H; a Schnorr-like proof (c, s) over random nonce k binds Γ to the public key Y; the VRF output β is derived from Γ.](ths_1789075776000_17286-1.webp)

```rust
// ECVRF_verify (RFC 9381), simplified pseudocode
fn ecvrf_verify(vk: &PublicKey, pi: &[u8; 80], alpha: &[u8]) -> Option<[u8; 64]> {
    let (gamma, c, s) = decode_proof(pi)?;              // 32 + 16 + 32 bytes
    let h = hash_to_curve(SUITE_ID, vk.as_bytes(), alpha)?;
    let u = &B * &s - vk.as_point() * &c;               // U = s*B - c*Y
    let v = &h * &s - &gamma * &c;                      // V = s*H - c*Gamma
    let c_prime = hash_points(&[h, gamma, u, v])?;      // Fiat-Shamir challenge
    (c == c_prime).then(|| proof_to_hash(&gamma))       // beta output
}
```

Deployment predates the RFC: Algorand ships a libsodium fork implementing an early draft of ECVRF-EDWARDS25519-SHA512-ELL2, Google's Key Transparency uses a P-256 variant, and the NSEC5 project produced the first implementations [3]. Per-ciphersuite test vectors — fixed *(sk, α, π, β)* tuples — make implementations interoperable and independently checkable.

### 4.4 VRFs vs. VDFs: Disentangling Two Primitives

Few confusions in applied cryptography are as consequential as conflating *verifiable random functions* with *verifiable delay functions*: their security properties are nearly orthogonal, and choosing the wrong one breaks protocols.

A **VDF**, formalized by Boneh, Bonneau, Bünz, and Fisch and efficiently instantiated by Wesolowski [6] and Pietrzak [7], is a function whose evaluation requires *T sequential steps* — parallelism does not help — while verification takes *polylog(T)* time. Wesolowski's construction, in a group of unknown order, has the prover compute *y = x^{2^T}* and a single-element proof *π = x^{⌊2^T/ℓ⌋}* for a verifier-chosen prime ℓ, checked via *π^ℓ · x^{2^T mod ℓ} = y* [6]. Pietrzak's recursive halving protocol publishes *log T* intermediates for *O(log T)* verification at *O(√T)* proving cost [7]. Both rest on the *repeated-squaring* assumption in groups of unknown order (RSA groups or class groups).

| Property | VRF | VDF |
|---|---|---|
| Who evaluates | secret-key holder only | anyone (public parameters) |
| What is enforced | unpredictability + uniqueness | *sequential* delay |
| Output on input *x* | unique *y* bound to *vk* | deterministic *y = x^{2^T}* |
| Proof convinces of | correct evaluation under *vk* | correct *T*-step computation |
| Trust anchor | key secrecy | group of unknown order |
| Canonical use | private leader/committee election | public randomness beacons |

Operationally: a VDF's output is *publicly computable* — its value lies in *when* it becomes known; a VRF's output is *privately computable* — its value lies in *who* may compute it. VDFs build unbiased public randomness beacons (nobody computes the output early); VRFs build private selection (nobody predicts *your* output without your key).

**Algorand's cryptographic sortition** [5] makes the distinction vivid. Each round, every user *i* locally computes *(yᵢ, πᵢ) = VRF_{skᵢ}(seed ‖ role)* with public round seed, then interprets *hash(yᵢ)* as a binomial draw — parameterized by its stake *wᵢ*, total stake *W*, and target committee size *τ* — deciding how many stake units it contributes to the committee. Membership stays *private* until a selected user reveals its proof, so adversaries cannot target committee members with denial-of-service before they act — a property a VDF (public outputs) cannot provide, and exactly what Algorand's Byzantine agreement needs to replace Sybil-vulnerable voting with stake-weighted private sampling.

### 4.5 Trust, Deployment, and the Post-Quantum Frontier

*Trusted setup.* ECVRF needs no trusted setup beyond a standard curve — no trapdoor exists whose knowledge breaks the scheme, and nothing-up-my-sleeve parameters are publicly auditable [3]. RSA-FDH-VRF needs an *honestly generated* modulus (whoever knows φ(*N*) forges proofs), requiring a trusted dealer or MPC ceremony; Dodis–Yampolskiy needs pairing-friendly curves with verifiable generation. For VDFs the bar is higher: RSA-based VDFs need a modulus whose factorization is *unknown to everyone*, while class-group VDFs offer transparent setup with less mature cryptanalysis [6, 7].

*NSEC5: VRFs against zone enumeration.* Classical NSEC chains zone names in cleartext (*N* queries reveal *N* names); NSEC3 hashes them, but offline dictionary attacks still enumerate typical zones. Goldberg, Naor, Papadopoulos, and Reyzin proved a lower bound: preventing enumeration with soundness requires *one online public-key operation per negative response* [4]. NSEC5 meets it with a VRF: the secondary holds *SKS* and, for a query *q* on a nonexistent name, returns an *NSEC5PROOF* record with *π = S(q)* (an RSA-FDH-VRF evaluation of *h₁(q)*) plus a signed *NSEC5* record covering the interval containing *h₂(π)*. The resolver checks *π^{e_S} ≡ h₁(q) (mod N_S)* and interval containment. Soundness holds *even against compromised secondaries* — uniqueness prevents fabricating denials for existing names — while privacy holds because only the online key holder can evaluate the keyed function. Later NSEC5 drafts migrated from RSA to ECVRF ciphersuites [3].

![Figure 4: Denial of existence in DNSSEC. NSEC3's offline hashes admit zone enumeration by dictionary attack; NSEC5 replaces them with an online VRF evaluation, so only the key-holding nameserver can map queries to proof values, preserving privacy with strong soundness.](ths_1789075776000_17286-3.webp)

*Post-quantum directions.* Every standardized VRF rests on discrete logarithms or factoring and falls to Shor's algorithm. The frontier includes lattice-based VRFs from LWE (where *uniqueness* under a lattice trapdoor is the central difficulty), hash-based constructions with large proofs, isogeny-based VUFs, and aggregate VRFs compressing many evaluations into one proof. Open problems: a post-quantum VRF with short proofs and no trusted setup; formally verified RFC 9381 implementations; beacons needing *both* unbiasability and private verifiability.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation

Concrete costs decide deployments. Indicative figures for the main constructions (commodity 64-bit hardware):

| Construction | Prove | Verify | Proof size | Assumption |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ECVRF-EDWARDS25519-SHA512-ELL2 | ~2 scalar mults (≈0.1–0.3 ms) | ~4 scalar mults (≈0.2–0.5 ms) | 80 B | DDH (ROM) |
| ECVRF-P256-SHA256-SSWU | similar, slower h2c | similar | 81 B | DDH (ROM) |
| RSA-FDH-VRF-2048 | 1 RSA private op (≈1–3 ms) | 1 RSA public op | 256 B | RSA (ROM) |
| Dodis–Yampolskiy (BN curve) | 1 exp in *G* + hashing | 2 pairings (≈1–2 ms) | 1 group element | q-DBDHI |

ECVRF on Edwards25519 is the clear efficiency winner: sub-millisecond prove and verify, 80-byte proofs, and no trusted setup, which explains its selection by Algorand and its prominence in RFC 9381 [3]. The Dodis–Yampolskiy construction pays for its standard-model-friendly assumption with pairing costs, while RSA-FDH-VRF pays in proof size and setup ceremony.

*Algorand at scale.* The SOSP'17 evaluation reports experiments with tens of thousands of simulated users and confirmation latency on the order of a minute — far below Bitcoin's hour-scale confirmation — with safety preserved under network partitions [5]. Sortition costs one VRF evaluation per user per role (sub-millisecond on ed25519); scalability comes from running Byzantine agreement only among the privately selected committee (target size *τ*). The Python sketch below is the per-node sortition counting logic:

```python
import hashlib, math

def sortition_count(vrf_output: bytes, w: int, W: int, tau: int) -> int:
    # Algorand-style sortition: sub-users selected for this node.
    # vrf_output: VRF hash; w/W: node's/total stake; tau: expected committee size.
    h = int.from_bytes(hashlib.sha256(vrf_output).digest(), "big") / 2**256
    p = tau / W
    j, cumsum = 0, 0.0
    while j <= w:  # invert the binomial CDF at point h
        cumsum += math.comb(w, j) * (p ** j) * ((1 - p) ** (w - j))
        if h < cumsum:
            return j
        j += 1
    return w
```

*NSEC5 overhead.* The price of NSEC5's privacy is exactly the lower bound: one online private-key operation per negative response, plus one RSA verification at the resolver [4]. This is practical for authoritative servers (thousands of RSA signs per second per core), and *NSEC5PROOF* values can be precomputed for static zones; resolver cost is comparable to NSEC3 validation. The residual concern is availability: the online key is a denial-of-service target — a trade-off shared with NSEC3 "white lies" online signing.

## 6 Limitations

**Random-oracle reliance**: ECVRF's proof models hashes as random oracles, and real instantiations have contained bugs caught only by careful concrete analysis [3]. **Tightness**: standard-model proofs lose a factor *Q* in queries — optimal per [8], but still forcing conservative parameters. **Setup**: RSA variants need honestly generated moduli; a compromised ceremony silently destroys unforgeability. **NSEC5's online key**: placing the private key on secondaries enlarges the theft/DoS surface, though soundness survives compromise [4]. **VRFs are not beacons**: outputs are unpredictable only to parties lacking key or proof; adversaries choosing inputs after seeing others' outputs can grind, so protocols like Algorand must chain seeds carefully [5]. **Quantum vulnerability**: all deployed VRFs rely on discrete logarithms or factoring.

## 7 Conclusion

In a quarter century, VRFs traveled from an inefficient RSA-based theoretical object to an Internet standard with test vectors and production deployments securing staked assets and DNS denial-of-existence. The through-line is the Micali–Rabin–Vadhan definition: *uniqueness* makes outputs committal, *pseudorandomness* makes them unpredictable, *verifiability* makes them checkable — and each application exercises a different facet. Algorand's sortition leans on unpredictability for private committees; NSEC5 leans on uniqueness and verifiability for sound denials against compromised servers. Dodis–Yampolskiy showed elegance — one pairing equation — could replace machinery; ECVRF showed standardization could replace machinery with consensus. The open frontier is post-quantum: rebuilding on Shor-resistant assumptions without sacrificing the short proofs and transparent setup that made VRFs deployable.
