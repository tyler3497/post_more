# Beyond the Kernel Datapath: A Formal Treatment of Poll-Mode Drivers, io_uring Rings, XDP Programs, and Userspace TCP at Hyperscale

## Abstract

The commodity Linux networking stack imposes per-packet costs — interrupt dispatch, context switches, system calls, skb allocation, and multi-core synchronization — that bound datacenter servers well below NIC line rates for small messages. This thesis develops a unified framework for the four principal mechanisms that restructure or eliminate the kernel datapath: DPDK poll-mode drivers, which trade interrupt-driven reception for run-to-completion polling over userspace NIC queues; `io_uring`, which collapses the syscall boundary into shared submission/completion rings; XDP and AF_XDP, which relocate programmable packet processing into the earliest driver hook while preserving kernel isolation; and userspace TCP stacks such as mTCP, Seastar, and SlimTCP, which reimplement connection semantics per-core with lock-free structures. We formalize a per-packet cost model, derive an interrupt-amortization bound, and compare mechanisms against published measurements: 24 Mpps single-core XDP drop, 3–25× mTCP transaction-rate gains, and order-of-magnitude wins for the IX protected dataplane — closing with the residual costs no approach removes.

## 1 Introduction

Modern datacenter workloads are dominated by *small messages at high packet rates*: RPC fan-out, key-value lookups, and 5G user-plane traffic routinely demand tens of millions of packets per second per server [1][7]. Commodity operating systems were architected around assumptions that no longer hold — few cores, slow NICs with long inter-packet gaps, and throughput utilization prioritized over per-packet latency [3].

The cost anatomy of the classical Linux receive path is well documented. Each packet arrival raises a hardware interrupt, which the driver handles under NAPI by scheduling a softirq; the softirq budget processes a bounded batch before returning control. Packets traverse `sk_buff` allocation, netfilter hooks, routing decisions, and socket-queue insertion, after which the application must cross the kernel–userspace boundary via system calls to retrieve them. For a 64-byte packet at 10 Gb/s, the inter-arrival time is approximately 67 ns — leaving, on typical hardware, a few hundred CPU cycles of budget per packet once interrupt overhead, cache misses, and lock contention are accounted for [7].

Two broad responses have emerged. The first is *kernel bypass proper*: frameworks such as DPDK, netmap, and PF_RING DNA move packet I/O entirely into userspace, dedicating cores to polling NIC descriptor rings [4][5]. The second is *kernel reorganization*: `io_uring` replaces the syscall stream with shared-memory rings [6], while XDP injects verifier-checked programs at the earliest driver hook, optionally redirecting frames into userspace UMEM [2]. A third axis is *protocol processing*: userspace TCP stacks (mTCP [1], Seastar, SlimTCP [7]) or protected dataplane kernels (IX [3]) reimplement connection management with per-core resources and lock-free structures.

> **Theorem (Datapath Cost Decomposition):** Let the per-packet cost on the receive path be $C = C_{int} + C_{sys} + C_{cpy} + C_{sync} + C_{proto}$, where $C_{int}$ is interrupt/softirq dispatch, $C_{sys}$ is system-call boundary crossing, $C_{cpy}$ is data copying, $C_{sync}$ is multicore synchronization, and $C_{proto}$ is protocol processing. Then maximum packet rate $R_{max} \le f_{cpu}/C$, where $f_{cpu}$ is core frequency. Every mechanism surveyed here provably reduces at least two of these five terms; none reduces all five simultaneously.

This thesis contributes: (i) a taxonomy of kernel-bypass and kernel-restructuring mechanisms along the axes of isolation, API compatibility, and resource dedication; (ii) a formal cost model with a derived batch-amortization bound; (iii) deep architectural treatments of DPDK, `io_uring`, XDP/AF_XDP, and userspace TCP; (iv) an empirical synthesis of published results with an honest accounting of residual limitations.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The interrupt-driven receive path and its pathologies

In the classical Linux design, packet arrival triggers a hardware interrupt; to avoid livelock under flood, drivers use NAPI, which disables the interrupt source and polls a bounded batch (default budget 64) inside a softirq context. While NAPI amortizes *interrupt* cost over a batch, it does not remove the remaining per-packet expenses:

- **Allocation churn.** Each packet requires an `sk_buff` header and page fragments; allocation and cache-line initialization dominate at small packet sizes.
- **Crossing costs.** `recvmsg`-style syscalls cost on the order of 100+ ns each even before work begins; event notification via `epoll` adds a second trip through the kernel [6].
- **Synchronization.** The socket receive queue, netfilter connection tracking, and the routing cache are shared across cores, incurring cache-line bouncing and lock contention.
- **Wake-up latency.** Interrupt-driven reception couples latency to scheduler decisions: an idle core must be woken via inter-processor interrupt, adding microseconds of tail latency precisely when the system is lightly loaded.

### 2.2 First-generation bypass: netmap, PF_RING, and the polling turn

Before DPDK consolidated the ecosystem, several frameworks attacked individual cost terms. **PF_RING** (and DNA) reduced copy time by polling NIC rings into userspace via NAPI — paying a double-polling penalty, or offloading the copy to the NIC network processor at the cost of exclusive ring ownership [4]. **netmap** introduced a shared-memory ring API with batching and zero-copy, proving the kernel–userspace boundary could be crossed at amortized cost. These systems established the design vocabulary — rings, batches, zero-copy, core pinning — that DPDK industrialized.

### 2.3 Measurement methodology for this thesis

Our empirical section synthesizes peer-reviewed and technically vetted published measurements [1][2][3][4][5][6][7]. Absolute numbers are reported exactly as published; hardware generation and workload are noted per case, since bypass performance is notoriously sensitive to both.

## 3 Methodology

We analyze each mechanism along five dimensions:

1. **Boundary crossed.** Which of the five cost terms ($C_{int}, C_{sys}, C_{cpy}, C_{sync}, C_{proto}$) does the mechanism eliminate or amortize?
2. **Isolation model.** Does it preserve kernel-mediated protection between mutually untrusting applications, or does it require trusted, dedicated resources?
3. **API compatibility.** Can existing socket applications run unmodified, or is a rewrite required?
4. **Resource dedication.** Does it demand pinned cores, hugepages, or exclusive NIC queues — resources with real opportunity cost?
5. **Deployability.** Does it coexist with standard tooling (routing tables, firewalls, observability), or does it fork the operational stack?

Our formal model treats packet processing as a pipeline of stages with per-stage costs. For polling-based systems, we derive the following bound.

> **Theorem (Batch-Amortization Bound):** Consider a poll-mode receiver that checks an empty descriptor ring at cost $c_{poll}$ per check and processes an arriving burst of size $b$ at cost $c_{pkt}$ per packet, with average batch occupancy $\bar{b}$. The effective per-packet cost is
>
> $$C_{eff} = c_{pkt} + \frac{c_{poll}}{\bar{b}}.$$
>
> As load $\rho \to 1$, $\bar{b}$ approaches the descriptor-ring batch limit and $C_{eff} \to c_{pkt}$; as $\rho \to 0$, $\bar{b} \to 1$ and polling wastes $c_{poll}$ per empty check. Hence polling maximizes throughput at high load but maximizes *energy and core waste* at low load — the fundamental tension that motivates hybrid interrupt/poll schemes such as AF_XDP's `need_wakeup` flag [4].

*Proof sketch.* Each receive iteration performs one ring check; with $\bar{b}$ packets found on average, the check cost is shared across them, while $c_{pkt}$ is batch-independent in the run-to-completion model. The limits follow directly. ∎

This theorem explains why real deployments pair polling with adaptive sleep, busy-poll budgets, and wake-up signaling rather than naive spin loops.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 DPDK poll-mode drivers: run-to-completion over userspace queues

The Data Plane Development Kit (DPDK) is the reference implementation of full kernel bypass on commodity NICs [4][5]. Its architecture rests on four pillars:

**Poll-mode drivers (PMDs).** Each NIC queue is serviced by a userspace driver that continuously polls descriptor rings. Reception is *run-to-completion*: a logical core (`lcore`) executes the full pipeline — dequeue descriptors, parse headers, apply forwarding logic, enqueue transmit descriptors — without preemption. This eliminates $C_{int}$ (no interrupts), $C_{sys}$ (no syscalls on the data path), and much of $C_{sync}$ (per-core resources, no shared socket queues). The cost is *core dedication*: a polling core burns 100% CPU regardless of traffic, formalized by the Batch-Amortization Bound above.

**Lock-free rings and mbuf pools.** DPDK's `rte_ring` supports single- and multi-producer/consumer models via compare-and-swap, providing the pipeline's inter-stage queues. Packet buffers (`mbuf`s) come from per-core mempools backed by 2 MB or 1 GB hugepages — reducing TLB misses and guaranteeing physically contiguous DMA regions [4].

**Thread affinity and NUMA awareness.** DPDK pins lcores to physical cores and allocates mempools on the NUMA node local to the NIC's PCIe root complex. Reported results exceed 80 Mpps on a single processor in optimized forwarding configurations [4].

**Operational consequences.** Bypassing the kernel means bypassing its *services*: no routing table, no netfilter, no TCP — applications must reimplement everything above L2/L3, the gap userspace TCP stacks (§4.4) fill. Deployment requires hugepages, IOMMU/VFIO for safe DMA, and exclusive NIC queues, complicating multi-tenant servers. The AF_XDP PMD offers a middle path: a DPDK application drives AF_XDP sockets on kernel-managed queues, retaining kernel tooling while avoiding per-packet interrupts [4].

```c
/* Canonical DPDK run-to-completion receive loop (simplified) */
while (!force_quit) {
    uint16_t n = rte_eth_rx_burst(port, queue, mbufs, BURST_SIZE);
    if (n == 0)
        continue;               /* empty poll: pure c_poll cost */
    for (uint16_t i = 0; i < n; i++)
        process_packet(mbufs[i]);
    rte_eth_tx_burst(port ^ 1, queue, mbufs, n);
}
```

### 4.2 io_uring: collapsing the syscall boundary without leaving the kernel

`io_uring`, introduced in Linux 5.1 by Jens Axboe, attacks $C_{sys}$ — the system-call cost — while deliberately *remaining inside the kernel* [6]. Its design centers on two shared-memory rings between application and kernel:

| Structure | Writer | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Submission Queue (SQ) | application | Submission Queue Entries (SQEs) describing operations |
| Completion Queue (CQ) | kernel | Completion Queue Entries (CQEs) with results |
| SQ poll thread (optional) | kernel | `IORING_SETUP_SQPOLL`: kernel thread consumes SQEs with **zero syscalls** |

Key mechanisms:

1. **Batching and amortization.** An application fills many SQEs and issues one `io_uring_enter`; with `SQPOLL`, even that syscall vanishes as a kernel thread polls the SQ — the same polling tradeoff as DPDK, relocated kernel-side [6].
2. **Registered resources.** `IORING_REGISTER_BUFFERS`/`FILES` pre-register memory and descriptors, removing per-operation lookup, pinning, and reference-counting.
3. **Linked operations.** SQEs link (`IOSQE_IO_LINK`) so dependent chains (accept → read → write) execute without userspace round-trips.
4. **Network support without bypass.** `IORING_OP_RECV`/`SEND`/`ACCEPT` submit socket operations fully asynchronously. The kernel TCP/IP stack still processes packets — $C_{proto}$ and $C_{int}$ remain — but the userspace interaction cost $C_{sys}$ is driven toward zero.

> **Theorem (Syscall Amortization):** If an application submits batches of $k$ SQEs per `io_uring_enter` with per-syscall cost $s$ and per-operation kernel work $w$, the per-operation boundary cost is $s/k + w$. With `SQPOLL`, the boundary cost becomes $w + c_{poll}/k'$ where $k'$ is the SQ drain batch size — structurally identical to the DPDK bound, with the poll cost now paid by a kernel thread rather than the application core.

The security dimension is non-trivial: `io_uring`'s asynchronous cross-boundary operation chains have produced a sustained stream of privilege-escalation vulnerabilities, prompting seccomp-style opcode restrictions (`IORING_REGISTER_RESTRICTIONS`) in recent kernels. Interfaces that blur the user/kernel boundary widen the attack surface; this is a first-class design cost.

### 4.3 XDP and AF_XDP: programmable early dispatch with kernel isolation

The eXpress Data Path (XDP) answers a different question: *can we get bypass-class packet rates without abandoning the kernel's protection and tooling?* Høiland-Jørgensen et al. demonstrated 24 Mpps single-core drop rates by executing eBPF programs at the earliest point in the driver — before `sk_buff` allocation — with five verdicts: `XDP_DROP`, `XDP_PASS`, `XDP_TX`, `XDP_REDIRECT`, and `XDP_ABORTED` [2].

**Safety by construction.** XDP programs are statically verified: the eBPF verifier proves termination, memory safety, and resource limits before JIT-compiling to native code. This makes in-driver programmability deployable — the kernel retains isolation while ceding per-packet decisions to verified code, unlike DPDK where a userspace driver bug can corrupt DMA memory system-wide.

**AF_XDP: zero-copy userspace redirection.** `XDP_REDIRECT` steers frames into an AF_XDP socket's UMEM — shared memory mapped into userspace — achieving zero-copy delivery without traversing the stack. Four rings coordinate the transfer (RX, TX, Fill, Completion), and the `need_wakeup` flag lets the application sleep when idle, addressing low-load polling waste [4]. This yields *selective bypass*: only matched traffic leaves the kernel path; everything else receives normal routing and firewalling.

The three XDP deployment modes trade performance against driver support: **generic XDP** in the stack path (works everywhere, lowest gain), **native XDP** in the driver before skb allocation (the 24 Mpps regime), and **offloaded XDP** on SmartNIC hardware.

### 4.4 Userspace TCP stacks: reimplementing the transport per core

Bypass frameworks deliver packets; applications need *connections*. The kernel TCP stack's costs — per-socket locks, shared timers, cache-line bouncing on connection tables — motivated clean-slate userspace reimplementations [1][7]:

**mTCP (NSDI '14).** Jeong et al. built a per-core TCP stack over DPDK/PSIO: (i) per-core resource localization — flow tables, TCB pools, socket buffers are core-private, RSS distributing flows; (ii) lock-free inter-core communication via SPSC queues; (iii) batched event processing amortizing application/stack context switches. Result: 3×–25× small-message transaction rates vs. Linux TCP, and 33%–320% on unmodified applications (lighttpd, SSLShader) [1].

**Seastar and F-Stack.** Seastar (the framework behind ScyllaDB) uses shard-per-core reactors — each core runs an independent TCP stack, allocator, and scheduler with no locks by construction. F-Stack ports the FreeBSD TCP/IP stack to userspace over DPDK for POSIX-compatible legacy applications.

**SlimTCP (2026).** A clean-room userspace TCP over DPDK that deliberately disables TSO/LRO/RSS to isolate the stack's intrinsic cost, using lock-free SPSC rings between a protocol worker core and the application core and XXH32-hashed flow tables [7].

**The IX counterpoint.** Belay et al. argued bypass alone is insufficient: it removes context switches but not the throughput/latency tradeoff, and sacrifices protection [3]. IX uses hardware virtualization to split the *control plane* (Linux: configuration, coarse scheduling) from the *dataplane* (dedicated cores running a protected, zero-copy, run-to-completion stack with adaptive batching). IX beat Linux by an order of magnitude on microbenchmarks, improved memcached throughput 3.6×, and more than halved tail latency — protection and performance coexisting by OS redesign rather than evasion [3].

```python
# Analytical model: cycles-per-packet budget vs. line rate
def max_pps(core_hz: float, cycles_per_packet: float) -> float:
    return core_hz / cycles_per_packet

# 64B packets at 100 Gb/s need ~148.8 Mpps; at 3 GHz the budget is
#   3e9 / 148.8e6 = 20 cycles/packet  -> impossible in software.
# 1500B packets at 100 Gb/s need ~8.1 Mpps:
#   3e9 / 8.1e6 = 370 cycles/packet   -> feasible for DPDK/XDP.
for mtu, rate_gbps in [(64, 100), (1500, 100), (64, 10)]:
    pps = rate_gbps * 1e9 / ((mtu + 20) * 8)   # incl. framing overhead
    print(f"MTU {mtu}, {rate_gbps}Gb/s -> {pps/1e6:.1f} Mpps, "
          f"budget {3e9/pps:.0f} cycles/pkt @3GHz")
```

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Formal Results

### 5.1 Published measurements, normalized

| System | Venue | Reported result | Normalized reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| XDP drop [2] | CoNEXT '18 | 24 Mpps, single core | $C_{int}+C_{sys}+C_{cpy}$ eliminated; pre-skb hook |
| DPDK forwarding [4] | Survey of NFV acceleration | >80 Mpps per processor | Full run-to-completion; dedicated cores |
| IX dataplane [3] | OSDI '14 (best paper) | 10× microbench vs Linux; 3.6× memcached; >2× tail-latency cut | Protected bypass via virt. separation |
| mTCP [1] | NSDI '14 | 3–25× small-msg txn rate; +33–320% app throughput | Per-core TCP over DPDK/PSIO |
| io_uring (storage) [6] | Kernel reports | 2.58 M IOPS/core; amortized syscall | Boundary collapse, in-kernel stack retained |

Two patterns emerge. First, *every* order-of-magnitude gain comes from eliminating at least two cost terms simultaneously — no single-term optimization (e.g., zero-copy alone) breaks the 10× barrier [4]. Second, gains concentrate on small packets and short connections: at 1500 B MTU the kernel is rarely the bottleneck, since DMA and wire time dominate; at 64 B the software path is everything.

### 5.2 A unified cost accounting

We can now complete the decomposition from §1 for each mechanism:

- **DPDK PMD:** $C_{int}, C_{sys}, C_{cpy}\approx 0$; $C_{sync}$ minimized per-core; $C_{proto}$ reimplemented. Residual: dedicated cores, lost kernel services.
- **io_uring:** $C_{sys}\to w$ (amortized); $C_{int}, C_{cpy}, C_{proto}$ unchanged. Residual: full kernel stack still per-packet; new attack surface.
- **XDP (native):** early drop cuts $C_{int}$; pre-skb $C_{cpy}\approx 0$; $C_{sys}=0$ for drop/TX verdicts. Residual: verifier expressiveness; driver support.
- **AF_XDP:** $C_{sys}, C_{cpy}$ gone for redirected flows; `need_wakeup` avoids $C_{int}$. Residual: UMEM management; per-queue binding.
- **Userspace TCP:** $C_{sync}, C_{sys}$ minimized; $C_{proto}$ re-engineered per core. Residual: duplicated TCP state machines; timer/congestion-control risk.
- **IX:** all five terms addressed with protection via virtualization. Residual: hypervisor dependency; split-plane complexity.

> **Theorem (No-Free-Lunch for Datapaths):** Any packet-processing architecture that (a) sustains >10 Mpps per core on 64 B packets, (b) preserves mutual isolation between untrusting applications, and (c) requires no application rewrite must dedicate at least one of: exclusive cores, specialized hardware, or a verified-execution substrate. *Justification:* (a) forces eliminating at least two cost terms (§5.1); eliminating $C_{int}$ or $C_{sys}$ without application changes needs kernel cooperation, which at line rate requires polling (dedicated cores), offload (hardware), or verified in-kernel programs (XDP/eBPF) — the three disjuncts. ∎

## 6 Limitations

**Measurement comparability.** The numbers in §5.1 span a decade of hardware and incomparable workloads; treating "24 Mpps (2018)" and "80 Mpps (survey)" as points on one curve is suggestive, not rigorous. Bypass performance is exquisitely sensitive to PCIe topology, DDIO behavior, and descriptor-ring sizing.

**The polling energy tax.** Our bound shows polling is optimal only near saturation, yet datacenter servers spend most of their lives at 10–30% utilization, where a dedicated polling core burns full power for near-zero work. AF_XDP's `need_wakeup`, DPDK power-management PMDs, and interrupt/poll hybrids mitigate but do not eliminate this; energy-proportional bypass remains open.

**Reimplemented-protocol risk.** Userspace TCP stacks reimplement congestion control, SACK processing, and timer management — historically the buggiest parts of any stack. mTCP and SlimTCP are research artifacts; production userspace stacks (Seastar, F-Stack, Google's Snap) carry enormous testing burdens because they fork from the kernel's decades-hardened state machine.

**io_uring's security debt.** The asynchronous cross-boundary operation chains that make `io_uring` fast also make it dangerous; the steady cadence of io_uring CVEs has led distributions to restrict it by default, limiting deployability of the fastest configurations.

**XDP's expressiveness ceiling.** The eBPF verifier's guarantees (termination, bounded memory) are exactly what forbid stateful L7 processing in the fast path; anything beyond header inspection and table lookup must punt to userspace or the stack, reintroducing the boundary XDP sought to avoid.

**Operational fragmentation.** DPDK forks the operational stack (separate drivers, no nftables, custom observability); XDP/AF_XDP preserve it but only for cooperating drivers; io_uring preserves everything but fixes only the boundary. No single mechanism dominates all five methodology dimensions of §3.

## 7 Conclusion

Kernel-bypass networking is not one technique but a design space parameterized by *which costs you pay, where you pay them, and what you give up*. DPDK pays dedicated cores to erase interrupts and syscalls; `io_uring` pays kernel complexity to amortize the syscall boundary in place; XDP pays verifier-constrained expressiveness to keep isolation while reaching driver-level rates; userspace TCP pays reimplementation risk to fix per-core protocol scaling; IX pays hypervisor machinery to have protection and performance at once.

The formal contribution of this thesis is the cost-decomposition framework and its two bounds — batch amortization and the no-free-lunch theorem — which explain *why* the field converged on this menagerie: each mechanism is a different Pareto-optimal answer to the same five-term equation, and the residual limitations of §6 are the conserved quantities of the tradeoff. For practitioners the decision is mechanical: >10 Mpps/core with unmodified applications and strong isolation means budgeting exclusive cores or SmartNICs; to keep kernel tooling, XDP/AF_XDP is the narrow waist; if the bottleneck is the syscall stream rather than the wire, `io_uring` suffices and bypass is overkill.

The next frontier is convergence: AF_XDP already blends DPDK-style polling with kernel integration, and eBPF is migrating up the stack. The long-run equilibrium is likely not "kernel vs. bypass" but a *verified programmable datapath* spanning driver, kernel, and userspace under one safety story — the trajectory XDP began and io_uring continues.
