# Bayesian Persuasion and Information Design: Concavification, Bayes Plausibility, and the Geometry of Optimal Signaling

## Abstract

When can an agent change another's behavior merely by designing what they learn — even when everyone is rational, shares a common prior, and understands the data-generating process? Kamenica and Gentzkow's Bayesian persuasion framework answers through a sender who commits ex ante to a signal observed by a receiver who updates by Bayes' rule and acts to maximize her own payoff [1]. The sender's problem reduces to choosing a Bayes-plausible distribution over posteriors, and its value equals the concavification of the sender's indirect value function at the prior: persuasion strictly helps exactly when the prior lies below its concave envelope. This article develops the geometry of optimal signaling — concavification, belief splitting, the revelation-principle reduction to action recommendations — surveys applications from courtrooms to school grading and bank stress tests, extends the analysis to multi-receiver design and multi-sender competition, and traces the deep duality between information design and mechanism design as well as the boundary with cheap talk [2][5][6].

---

## 1. Introduction

Consider a prosecutor who wants a conviction, a lobbyist who wants a favorable vote, a salesperson who wants a purchase, or a school that wants its graduates hired. In each case, one party — the *sender* — cannot directly choose the outcome; instead, another party — the *receiver* — takes an action that determines both players' welfare. The sender's only instrument is *information*: she can design what the receiver learns before acting. The striking insight of Kamenica and Gentzkow [1] is that, with the power to *commit* to a disclosure rule before learning the state, the sender can systematically manipulate a fully rational receiver's beliefs to her own advantage — even when their preferences are diametrically opposed, and even when the receiver knows exactly what the sender is doing.

This is the **Bayesian persuasion** model, and it has become the canonical foundation of the broader field of **information design** surveyed by Bergemann and Morris [2] and Kamenica [7]. The model's analytical core is geometric. Define the sender's *indirect value function* $V(\mu)$ as her expected payoff when the receiver holds belief $\mu$ and acts optimally. Because the sender can randomize over which beliefs the receiver ends up holding — subject only to the constraint that beliefs average back to the prior, a condition known as **Bayes plausibility** — her problem is to maximize the expectation of $V$ over all mean-preserving distributions of posteriors. The solution is the **concave envelope** (concavification) of $V$ evaluated at the prior. The entire design problem thereby collapses to a single picture: a possibly non-concave value function, its smallest concave majorant, and the supporting hyperplane at the prior that reveals the optimal splitting of beliefs.

> **Theorem (Concavification, Kamenica–Gentzkow 2011):** Let $V(\mu)$ be the sender's expected payoff when the receiver's belief is $\mu$ and she best-responds. Let $\bar{V}$ denote the smallest concave function dominating $V$. Then the sender's optimal expected payoff from any signal equals $\bar{V}(\mu_0)$, and a signal strictly benefits the sender relative to no disclosure if and only if $V(\mu_0) < \bar{V}(\mu_0)$ [1].

Three features make this result remarkable. First, it is *necessary and sufficient*: it tells the sender exactly when persuasion is worth attempting. Second, it is *constructive*: the geometry of the envelope dictates the optimal signal. Third, it is *robust*: the same concavification logic extends to multi-receiver settings through Bayes correlated equilibrium [2], admits a price-theoretic dual interpretation [3][4], and degrades gracefully into cheap talk when commitment is removed [5].

## 2. Background

The intellectual lineage of Bayesian persuasion runs through the economics of information. **Blackwell's** comparison of experiments established that one information structure dominates another when it induces a mean-preserving spread of posteriors — more informative signals split beliefs further apart. **Aumann's** correlated equilibrium showed how a mediator's recommendations can coordinate play beyond Nash outcomes. The direct predecessor, however, is **Crawford and Sobel's** model of strategic information transmission, universally known as *cheap talk* [5]. There, the sender is privately informed about a state, sends a costless message, and the receiver acts — but the sender *cannot commit* to a messaging rule. Equilibrium communication then takes a strikingly coarse form: the sender partitions the state space into intervals and reveals only which interval contains the state, with the number of partition elements shrinking as the players' preference bias grows. Full revelation is impossible whenever preferences conflict.

Bayesian persuasion flips the commitment assumption and, with it, the conclusions. The sender moves *before* observing the state, publicly committing to a signal — a stochastic mapping $\pi: \Omega \to \Delta(S)$ from states to distributions over signal realizations. Because the receiver observes the realized signal and updates by Bayes' rule, and because the sender's commitment is credible, the sender effectively chooses the *distribution of the receiver's posterior beliefs*, constrained only by Bayes plausibility. The contrast is sharp:

| Dimension | Cheap Talk (Crawford–Sobel) [5] | Bayesian Persuasion (Kamenica–Gentzkow) [1] | Information Design (Bergemann–Morris) [2] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who designs information | Informed sender, no commitment | Uninformed sender, full commitment | Designer commits to information structure |
| Timing | Sender learns state, then speaks | Sender commits, then state realized | Designer commits before play |
| Equilibrium concept | Partition Bayesian-Nash equilibria | Sender-optimal signal; receiver best-responds | Bayes correlated equilibrium |
| Key condition | Bias small enough for fine partitions | Prior below concave envelope | Obedience constraints |
| Informativeness | Decreasing in preference conflict | Can be high even under total conflict | Designer-optimal over all structures |

Kamenica's survey [7] organizes the subsequent explosion of applications: a school improves job placement by issuing only *coarse grades*; a platform like Google reduces congestion by giving drivers *noisy* traffic information; a social planner raises welfare by providing only *partial* information about bank solvency. In every case, garbling information — deliberately withholding or coarsening it — benefits the designer, and the concavification theorem explains exactly how.

## 3. Methodology

We formalize the canonical model [1]. Let $\Omega$ be a finite set of states with common prior $\mu_0 \in \Delta(\Omega)$. The receiver chooses an action $a \in A$ (finite) after observing a signal realization; utilities are $u_R(a,\omega)$ for the receiver and $u_S(a,\omega)$ for the sender. The sender commits to a **signal** (experiment) $\pi$, where $\pi(\cdot \mid \omega) \in \Delta(S)$ for each state $\omega$. Upon realization $s$, the receiver forms the posterior $\mu_s \in \Delta(\Omega)$ via Bayes' rule and selects an action from her best-response correspondence $\hat{a}(\mu_s) = \arg\max_a \mathbb{E}_{\mu_s}[u_R(a,\omega)]$.

The methodological breakthrough is a **revelation-principle-like reduction** that eliminates the signal space $S$ entirely. Because the receiver's behavior depends on a realization $s$ only through the posterior $\mu_s$ it induces, the sender's problem can be rewritten as a direct choice over *distributions of posteriors* $\tau \in \Delta(\Delta(\Omega))$. Not every $\tau$ is attainable: by the law of iterated expectations, any feasible $\tau$ must satisfy **Bayes plausibility**,

> **Definition (Bayes Plausibility):** A distribution $\tau$ over posteriors is *Bayes-plausible* given prior $\mu_0$ if $\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[\mu] = \mu_0$ — that is, posteriors average back to the prior.

Kamenica and Gentzkow show this condition is also sufficient: every Bayes-plausible $\tau$ is induced by some signal [1]. This is the workhorse lemma of the field.

Next, define the sender's **indirect value function** $V: \Delta(\Omega) \to \mathbb{R}$ by

$$V(\mu) = \mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[u_S(\hat{a}(\mu), \omega)\right],$$

where ties in the receiver's best response are broken in the sender's favor (an innocuous genericity assumption). The sender's design problem is then the clean geometric program

$$\max_{\tau : \mathbb{E}_{\tau}[\mu] = \mu_0} \mathbb{E}_{\tau}[V(\mu)].$$

This is a linear objective over a convex, compact set of distributions — and its value is exactly the concave envelope of $V$ at $\mu_0$. Geometrically: the sender wants to "split" the prior into posteriors where $V$ is high, but Bayes plausibility forces the split to average back to $\mu_0$; the best she can do is ride the smallest concave majorant of $V$. An optimal signal exists, and by Carathéodory-type arguments its support can be taken to be small — with binary states, two posteriors suffice [1][4].

Dworczak and Martini [3] provide a striking alternative methodology: a **price-theoretic duality**. They recast the sender as a consumer who *purchases* posterior beliefs at linear prices, using the prior as her endowment, while a firm with garbling technology supplies them. Walrasian equilibrium prices then certify optimality — the welfare theorems become a verification tool for persuasion schemes. This is especially tractable when the sender's utility depends only on the *expected state* (posterior mean), where optimal signals take monotone-partition forms. Dworczak and Kolotilin [4] subsequently developed this into a general **persuasion duality**, giving dual certificates for arbitrary persuasion problems and unifying the concavification approach with linear programming formulations.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Concavification Theorem and the Value of Persuasion

The concave envelope $\bar{V}$ is the pointwise-smallest concave function dominating $V$ on the simplex $\Delta(\Omega)$. The theorem's proof is a two-line duality argument: any Bayes-plausible $\tau$ yields $\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[V(\mu)] \le \mathbb{E}_{\tau}[\bar{V}(\mu)] \le \bar{V}(\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[\mu]) = \bar{V}(\mu_0)$ by Jensen's inequality, so no signal beats the envelope; conversely, $\bar{V}(\mu_0)$ is by definition a convex combination of values $V(\mu_i)$ at posteriors $\mu_i$ averaging to $\mu_0$, and Bayes plausibility converts that combination into a real signal [1].

The *strict benefit* condition $V(\mu_0) < \bar{V}(\mu_0)$ has an intuitive reading: persuasion helps exactly when the sender's value function is *locally non-concave* at the prior — when there exist beliefs on either side of the prior whose average value exceeds the value at the prior itself. If $V$ is concave, information can only hurt (on average); if $V$ is convex, full revelation is optimal. The interesting cases live in between, where the optimal signal *partially* reveals: it splits the prior into a mix of posteriors, some inducing favorable actions, others unfavorable, calibrated so the receiver's obedience is preserved.

### 4.2 Bayes Plausibility and the Geometry of Splitting

Bayes plausibility is best understood geometrically on the belief simplex. Each signal realization maps to a posterior point; the signal is a lottery over these points whose barycenter is the prior. *Splitting* the prior means replacing the point mass at $\mu_0$ with a mean-preserving spread — pushing probability mass outward toward posteriors where $V$ is high, while keeping the center of mass fixed. Blackwell's ordering reappears: a more informative signal is precisely a mean-preserving spread of a less informative one.

The supporting-hyperplane picture completes the geometry. At the optimum, there exists an affine function $L$ on the simplex such that $L \ge V$ everywhere and $L(\mu_0) = \bar{V}(\mu_0)$; every posterior in the optimal signal's support lies where $V$ touches this hyperplane. Dworczak and Martini's price theory [3] gives this hyperplane an economic interpretation: it is the *price vector* for beliefs in the persuasion economy, and the touching points are the beliefs the sender "buys." The duality of Dworczak and Kolotilin [4] generalizes this certificate to arbitrary sender preferences, showing that optimal persuasion schemes are always supported by such linear price functionals.

### 4.3 The Revelation-Principle Argument: Signals as Action Recommendations

A further simplification mirrors the revelation principle of mechanism design. Because only the receiver's *action* matters for payoffs, any signal can be transformed into a **direct signal** whose realizations are action recommendations, with **obedience constraints** requiring that the receiver weakly prefers to follow each recommendation given the posterior it induces. Formally, replace each realization $s$ with the recommended action $\hat{a}(\mu_s)$; group realizations by recommendation; the resulting signal recommends actions, and obedience holds by construction [1][2].

This reduction is what connects Bayesian persuasion to **mechanism design**. Bergemann and Morris [2] show that the single-receiver persuasion problem is the special case of *Bayes correlated equilibrium* (BCE) with one player: the designer chooses an information structure (equivalently, a BCE decision rule) to maximize her objective subject to obedience. The duality runs deep — mechanism design fixes the information structure and optimizes over game forms subject to incentive compatibility; information design fixes the game form and optimizes over information structures subject to obedience. The two are, in a precise sense, dual problems [2][4].

### 4.4 Worked Example: The Prosecutor and the Judge

The canonical illustration [1] makes the geometry concrete. The state is $\Omega = \{\text{guilty}, \text{innocent}\}$ with prior $\mu_0 = 0.3$ on guilt. The judge (receiver) convicts if and only if her posterior probability of guilt is at least $0.5$; the prosecutor (sender) gets payoff $1$ from conviction and $0$ otherwise. The indirect value function is a step: $V(\mu) = 1$ if $\mu \ge 0.5$, else $0$. Its concave envelope is the piecewise-linear function $\bar{V}(\mu) = \min(2\mu, 1)$, so $\bar{V}(0.3) = 0.6$: the prosecutor can raise the conviction probability from $0$ (no disclosure, since $0.3 < 0.5$) to $0.6$.

The optimal signal splits the prior into posteriors $\mu = 0.5$ (with probability $0.6$) and $\mu = 0$ (with probability $0.4$). In signal terms: if the defendant is guilty, the prosecutor always announces "guilty"; if innocent, she announces "guilty" with probability $3/7$ and "innocent" with probability $4/7$. Hearing "guilty," the judge's posterior is exactly $0.5$ and she convicts (tie broken for the sender); hearing "innocent," she acquits. Bayes plausibility is satisfied: $0.6 \times 0.5 + 0.4 \times 0 = 0.3$. The sender *lies by selective emphasis* — never fabricating guilt from nothing, but strategically pooling some innocent states with guilty ones. The following Python snippet verifies the arithmetic:

```python
import numpy as np

mu0 = 0.3          # prior P(guilty)
threshold = 0.5    # judge convicts iff posterior >= threshold

# Concave envelope of step function V(mu) = 1[mu >= threshold]
def cav_V(mu):
    return min(mu / threshold, 1.0)

# Optimal split: posteriors mu_high (= threshold) and mu_low (= 0)
p_high = mu0 / threshold            # Bayes plausibility: p_high*0.5 = 0.3
print("conviction prob:", p_high)   # 0.6
print("envelope value :", cav_V(mu0))  # 0.6

# Signal: P("guilty" | innocent) chosen so posterior after "guilty" is 0.5
x = (mu0 / threshold - mu0) / (1 - mu0)
print("P(guilty-signal | innocent):", round(x, 4))  # 0.4286 = 3/7
```

### 4.5 Multi-Receiver Design and Competition Among Senders

With **multiple receivers**, the designer chooses between *public* signals (one realization observed by all) and *private* signals (personalized realizations). Bergemann and Morris [2] show the BCE framework absorbs this distinction: the designer's problem is to choose a joint distribution over states, signal profiles, and action profiles maximizing her objective subject to each player's obedience constraints. Private signals are weakly more powerful — they allow the designer to correlate recommendations across players arbitrarily — while public signals are often more realistic (a stress-test announcement, a product rating). The single-sender concavification theorem generalizes: with multiple receivers and no externalities, the problem remains a linear program over Bayes-plausible distributions, now over higher-dimensional belief spaces.

With **multiple senders**, commitment meets competition. Gentzkow and Kamenica [6] study senders who simultaneously commit to signals before a common receiver acts. When signal spaces are *Blackwell-connected* (rich enough that senders can replicate each other's information), equilibrium outcomes are characterized by *unimprovability*: no sender can deviate to a signal that strictly improves her payoff. A central finding is that competition tends to produce *more informative* outcomes — senders with opposed preferences unravel each other's garbling — though senders themselves Pareto-prefer minimally informative equilibria. In the leading partitions environment with simple persuasion preferences, equilibrium is fully revealing: competition destroys the commitment rents that a monopolistic sender enjoys [6]. This provides the persuasion analogue of the classic insight that competition disciplines market power — here, the "market power" is over beliefs.

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

The theory comes with unusually strong *guarantees*. Existence of an optimal signal follows from compactness of the Bayes-plausible set and continuity of the objective [1]. **Cardinality bounds** keep optimal signals simple: with $|\Omega|$ states, an optimal distribution over posteriors can be supported on at most $|\Omega|$ points (a Carathéodory argument refined in the persuasion duality [4]); with binary states, two posteriors always suffice, as the prosecutor example shows. The dual certificates of Dworczak and Kolotilin [4] provide *verifiable* optimality conditions: a candidate signal is optimal if and only if a supporting price functional exists — a practical tool for applied work in grading, ratings, and disclosure design.

On the computational side, Dughmi and Xu [8] initiated the **algorithmic** study of Bayesian persuasion, giving polynomial-time algorithms for computing optimal signaling schemes in canonical settings and mapping the complexity frontier for richer environments. This matters because real applications — recommender systems choosing what to reveal, platforms designing information feeds — require actually computing the concave envelope in high dimensions, where naive discretization of the simplex is infeasible.

Empirically, the model's sharpest prediction — that commitment power has *first-order* value even against fully rational receivers — has been tested in laboratory experiments on communication games, which broadly confirm that senders exploit commitment to shift receiver behavior beyond what cheap-talk equilibria permit, and that receiver welfare responds to signal informativeness as the theory predicts. Kamenica's survey [7] documents the accompanying field evidence: coarse grading schemes that improve hiring outcomes, stress-test disclosures calibrated to avoid bank runs, and advertising strategies that reveal just enough to induce purchase. In each domain, the estimated gains from optimized disclosure are economically large — precisely because the concave envelope can sit far above the value function at the prior when preferences are misaligned.

## 6. Limitations

The framework's power rests on assumptions that deserve scrutiny. **Commitment** is the load-bearing pillar: the sender must be able to bind herself to a disclosure rule before learning the state. Remove it, and the model collapses to cheap talk [5], where misaligned preferences destroy informativeness. Real institutions approximate commitment through reputation, regulation, or technology (audited grading algorithms, certified stress tests), but the approximation is imperfect, and the literature on *limited commitment* remains active.

Second, the **common prior** assumption rules out disagreement about fundamentals; with heterogeneous priors, "persuasion" and "agreeing to disagree" become entangled. Third, receivers are **perfect Bayesians** — no inattention, no motivated reasoning, no bounded rationality — though recent work on rational inattention and quantal response begins to relax this. Fourth, signals are **costless** to design and transmit; introducing lying costs or verification costs (as in evidence models) changes the geometry substantially. Fifth, the baseline model is **single-receiver**; multi-receiver design with payoff externalities can make even the existence of simple optimal signals delicate [2]. Finally, the sender's objective is assumed known and the receiver's best-response correspondence well-behaved; with multiple optimal actions, tie-breaking in the sender's favor is a real (if generic) assumption. None of these limitations overturns the core geometry — concavification, Bayes plausibility, and obedience remain the organizing concepts — but each marks a frontier where the elegant picture frays.

## 7. Conclusion

Bayesian persuasion distills the strategic design of information to a single geometric operation: **concavification**. A sender who can commit to a signal chooses not messages but *distributions over her audience's beliefs*, constrained only by the requirement that beliefs average back to the prior. Her optimal payoff is the concave envelope of her indirect value function at the prior; she benefits exactly when the prior sits in a non-concave region of that function; and the supporting hyperplane at the optimum reveals both the optimal splitting of beliefs and — through the price-theoretic duality [3][4] — the shadow prices of information itself.

The framework's reach extends well beyond its sender-receiver origins. Through Bayes correlated equilibrium it becomes the multi-player theory of information design [2]; through multi-sender competition it explains when rivalry unravels manipulative disclosure [6]; through its duality with mechanism design it completes a symmetric picture of economic design — choosing the game given the information, and choosing the information given the game [2][4]. And its boundary with cheap talk [5] teaches the deepest lesson: information is power, but only *committed* information is design. Everything else is just talk.
