# Stochastic Volatility: From the Black–Scholes PDE to Dupire's Formula, Heston Calibration, and Rough Volatility

## Abstract
From the 1973 Black–Scholes–Merton partial differential equation to the non-Markovian rough volatility models of the 2010s, the quantitative theory of volatility has traced a single arc: each new empirical fact — the implied smile, the exploding short-dated skew, the jagged paths of realized volatility — has forced a richer stochastic description of volatility itself. This thesis unifies four landmarks: the Black–Scholes PDE and its delta-hedging argument; Dupire's 1994 dual formula, which recovers a unique local volatility surface from market call prices; the Heston 1993 square-root stochastic volatility model, its affine characteristic function, and the practical inverse problem of calibrating its five parameters; and the rough volatility paradigm of Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum, in which log-volatility follows a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst exponent near 0.1. We derive each construction, connect them through Gyöngy's mimicking theorem, compare their explanatory power for the short-dated skew, and argue that roughness is the signature the market has always carried.

##1 Intro

The Black–Scholes–Merton model is the most successful failed model in the history of finance. It gave the first closed-form price for a European option under no-arbitrage assumptions [1], and became the industry's quotation language: traders no longer quoted option prices at all, but quoted the single number — implied volatility — that makes the Black–Scholes formula match the market price. Yet this very quotation mechanism exposed the model's central fiction. If volatility were truly the constant parameter of the Black–Scholes world, implied volatility would be identical across strikes and maturities. Instead, equity index markets display a pronounced *smile* (or *skew*): out-of-the-money puts trade at substantially higher implied volatilities than at-the-money options, and the slope of this skew explodes as maturity shrinks toward zero. 

Three successive generations of theory have answered this challenge, each by enriching the dynamics of volatility itself. The first, *local volatility*, keeps the one-dimensional Markov diffusion of Black–Scholes but allows the diffusion coefficient to depend on time and spot. Dupire's 1994 result [3] is the capstone: given the full surface of European call prices, there is a *unique* local volatility function consistent with it, and it can be written in closed form from the derivatives of the price surface. The second, *stochastic volatility*, grants volatility its own source of randomness. Heston's 1993 model [2] became the industry standard because it combines an economically sensible variance process — mean-reverting with the leverage effect — with a closed-form characteristic function that makes Fourier pricing and hence calibration fast. The third, *rough volatility*, abandons the Markov assumption altogether. The empirical finding of Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum [5] that log-volatility behaves like a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst exponent $H \approx 0.1$ — far rougher than Brownian motion — resolved a puzzle the earlier models could not: why the at-the-money implied skew decays as a power law $T^{H-1/2}$ rather than flattening as every classical stochastic volatility model predicts.

This thesis argues that these are not competing models but layers of a single response to model inadequacy, connected by precise mathematics. Dupire's formula is the *static* inversion of the market; Gyöngy's mimicking theorem shows it is also the *Markovian projection* of any stochastic volatility model, so that local and stochastic volatility are dual descriptions of the same marginal distributions. Heston calibration is the *dynamic* inversion — fitting a parsimonious parametric engine to the same surface Dupire fits nonparametrically. Rough volatility is what the data demand when we stop requiring volatility to be a semimartingale.

##2 Background

We work on a filtered probability space carrying a risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$, under which discounted asset prices are martingales — the content of the fundamental theorem of asset pricing. The risk-neutral price of a European contingent claim with payoff $h(S_T)$ is the discounted expectation

$$V(t, S_t) = e^{-r(T-t)} \, \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[h(S_T) \,\middle|\, S_t\right],$$

where $r$ is the risk-free rate. The derivation assumes geometric Brownian motion with constant volatility, frictionless continuous trading, constant rates, no dividends, and European exercise [1].

> **Theorem: Feynman–Kac.** Let $X$ solve $dX_t = \mu(t,X_t)\,dt + \sigma(t,X_t)\,dW_t$ and let $V$ solve $\partial_t V + \mu\,\partial_x V + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\,\partial_{xx} V - rV = 0$ with terminal condition $V(T,x) = h(x)$. Then $V(t,x) = e^{-r(T-t)}\mathbb{E}[h(X_T)\mid X_t = x]$. The PDE and the expectation are two faces of the same object.

The Black–Scholes formula itself,

$$C = S\,N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2), \qquad d_{1,2} = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r \pm \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}},$$

is the special case for a call payoff under geometric Brownian motion. Because the price is strictly increasing in $\sigma$, the formula can be inverted: the *implied volatility* $\sigma_{\text{imp}}(K,T)$ is the number that equates model and market price. For equity indices it is downward-sloping in strike (the *skew*) and its at-the-money slope grows without apparent bound as $T \to 0$ — a fact no classical diffusion-based stochastic volatility model can reproduce [8].

The forward Kolmogorov (Fokker–Planck) equation governs the transition densities of a diffusion, and it is the duality between the backward pricing PDE and the forward density equation that makes Dupire's inversion possible. ---

##3 Methodology

This thesis treats volatility modeling as a sequence of *inverse problems*: given observed call prices $C^{\text{mkt}}(K,T)$, recover the volatility dynamics the market implies. Three distinct inversions are developed, each with its own regularization philosophy.

**Dupire inversion (nonparametric).** From a smooth call-price surface, the local volatility $\sigma_{\text{loc}}(T,K)$ is recovered by differentiating the surface twice in strike and once in maturity. In practice the surface is interpolated under static no-arbitrage constraints with Tikhonov regularization to tame the second strike derivative [4].

**Heston calibration (parametric).** The parameter vector $\Theta = (\kappa, \theta, \xi, \rho, v_0)$ is chosen to minimize a weighted misfit, typically

$$\min_{\Theta} \sum_{m=1}^{M} w_m \left(\sigma^{\text{model}}_{\text{imp}}(K_m, T_m; \Theta) - \sigma^{\text{mkt}}_{\text{imp}}(K_m, T_m)\right)^2,$$

subject to positivity and the Feller constraint. Each objective evaluation requires pricing the full option panel, which is done by Fourier inversion of the model's closed-form characteristic function — the affine structure of Heston is what makes this calibration industrially feasible [6]. Optimization uses Levenberg–Marquardt with analytically differentiated gradients, often after a global pre-search.

**Roughness estimation (statistical).** The Hurst exponent is estimated from high-frequency data via the scaling of power variations, $m(q,\Delta) = \mathbb{E}|\log\sigma_{t+\Delta} - \log\sigma_t|^q \propto \Delta^{qH}$. Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum applied this across equity indices, FX, and commodities and found $H \approx 0.1$ with striking universality [5]. The methodology deliberately avoids any pricing model: it is a statement about the historical measure, which the rough volatility program then transports into pricing.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Black–Scholes PDE: Hedging, No-Arbitrage, and Feynman–Kac

Consider a self-financing portfolio $\Pi = V - \Delta S$ that is long one option and short $\Delta$ shares. By Itô's lemma,

$$d\Pi = \left(\partial_t V + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \partial_{SS} V\right)dt + \left(\partial_S V - \Delta\right)dS.$$

Choosing $\Delta = \partial_S V$ eliminates all $dS$ risk; no-arbitrage ($d\Pi = r\Pi\,dt$) then yields the Black–Scholes PDE:

$$\partial_t V + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \,\partial_{SS} V + rS\,\partial_S V - rV = 0,$$

with terminal condition $V(T,S) = h(S)$. The Feynman–Kac theorem quoted above then converts the PDE into the risk-neutral expectation, completing the circle between the trader's hedging portfolio and the probabilist's martingale measure [1].

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

def bs_call(S, K, T, r, sigma):
    """Black-Scholes European call price."""
    d1 = (np.log(S / K) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * T) / (sigma * np.sqrt(T))
    d2 = d1 - sigma * np.sqrt(T)
    return S * norm.cdf(d1) - K * np.exp(-r * T) * norm.cdf(d2)

def implied_vol(price, S, K, T, r, tol=1e-10):
    """Invert the BS formula by bisection: market price -> implied vol."""
    lo, hi = 1e-6, 5.0
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if bs_call(S, K, T, r, mid) > price:
            hi = mid
        else:
            lo = mid
        if hi - lo < tol:
            break
    return 0.5 * (lo + hi)
```

### 4.2 Dupire's Formula and the Local Volatility Surface

Fix today's spot $S_0$ and view the call price as a function of maturity and strike, $C(T,K)$. The forward Kolmogorov equation for the risk-neutral density yields Dupire's dual PDE:

$$\partial_T C = \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{\text{loc}}^2(T,K)\,K^2\,\partial_{KK} C - (r-q)K\,\partial_K C - qC.$$

Solving for the diffusion coefficient gives **Dupire's formula** [3]:

$$\sigma_{\text{loc}}^2(T,K) = \frac{2\left(\partial_T C + (r-q)K\,\partial_K C + qC\right)}{K^2\,\partial_{KK} C}.$$

> **Theorem: Gyöngy's mimicking theorem.** Let $dS_t = \alpha_t\,dt + \beta_t\,dW_t$ be an Itô process with bounded coefficients. Then there exists a Markovian diffusion $d\tilde{S}_t = a(t,\tilde{S}_t)\,dt + b(t,\tilde{S}_t)\,d\tilde{W}_t$ with the *same one-dimensional marginal distributions* at every time $t$, where $b^2(t,x) = \mathbb{E}[\beta_t^2 \mid S_t = x]$ [9].

The mimicking theorem is the deep justification of local volatility: for *any* stochastic volatility model, the Dupire local volatility is exactly the conditional expectation of the instantaneous variance given the spot level,

$$\sigma_{\text{loc}}^2(T,K) = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[v_T \,\middle|\, S_T = K\right],$$

Extensions of the formula to stochastic rates and stochastic local volatility preserve this structure while complicating the inversion [4].

| Model class | Objects matched | Dynamics specified? | Exotic pricing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Black–Scholes (constant $\sigma$) | One ATM option | Yes (GBM) | Closed form, wrong |
| Dupire local volatility | All vanillas exactly | Yes (Markov diffusion) | PDE, forward-smile bias |
| Heston stochastic volatility | All vanillas approx. | Yes (2-factor SDE) | Fourier/MC, consistent |
| Rough volatility | All vanillas + skew term structure | Non-Markovian | Simulation-heavy |

### 4.3 The Heston Model: Affine Structure and Fourier Pricing

Heston's 1993 model [2] postulates a mean-reverting square-root process for the instantaneous variance $v_t$:

$$\begin{aligned}
dS_t &= rS_t\,dt + \sqrt{v_t}\,S_t\,dW^{(1)}_t, \\
dv_t &= \kappa(\theta - v_t)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW^{(2)}_t, \qquad d\langle W^{(1)}, W^{(2)}\rangle_t = \rho\,dt,
\end{aligned}$$

with mean reversion speed $\kappa$, long-run variance $\theta$, vol-of-vol $\xi$, spot–variance correlation $\rho$, and initial variance $v_0$.

The model's decisive advantage is *affine structure*: the characteristic function of log-spot is exponential-affine in $v_0$,

$$\varphi_\tau(u) = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[e^{iu\ln S_\tau}\right] = \exp\left(C_\tau(u) + D_\tau(u)\,v_0 + iu\ln S_0\right),$$

where $C_\tau, D_\tau$ solve Riccati ODEs in closed form. The numerically stable representation (Albrecher et al.) is

$$\begin{aligned}
d(u) &= \sqrt{(\kappa - i\rho\xi u)^2 + \xi^2(iu + u^2)}, \\
g(u) &= \frac{\kappa - i\rho\xi u - d(u)}{\kappa - i\rho\xi u + d(u)}, \\
D_\tau(u) &= \frac{\kappa - i\rho\xi u - d(u)}{\xi^2}\,\frac{1 - e^{-d(u)\tau}}{1 - g(u)e^{-d(u)\tau}}, \\
C_\tau(u) &= iru\tau + \frac{\kappa\theta}{\xi^2}\left[(\kappa - i\rho\xi u - d(u))\tau - 2\ln\frac{1 - g(u)e^{-d(u)\tau}}{1 - g(u)}\right].
\end{aligned}$$

Heston's original pricing formula writes the call as $C = S_0 P_1 - Ke^{-r\tau}P_2$ with

$$P_j = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{\infty} \operatorname{Re}\!\left[\frac{e^{-iu\ln K} f_j(u)}{iu}\right] du,$$

reducing option pricing to a single numerical integral. Fourier methods such as Carr–Madan FFT [10] and the COS method price whole strike panels fast enough for calibration loops [6]. A compact implementation of the characteristic function:

```haskell
-- Heston characteristic function (stable Albrecher et al. form)
data HestonParams = HP { kappa :: Double, theta :: Double
                       , xi :: Double, rho :: Double, v0 :: Double }

charFunc :: HestonParams -> Double -> Double -> Double -> Complex Double
charFunc (HP k th x r v) tau u s0 = exp (cTau + dTau * (v :+ 0) + (0 :+ u) * logS0)
  where
    iu    = 0 :+ u
    dval  = sqrt ((k :+ 0 - iu * (r*x :+ 0))^2 + (x^2 :+ 0) * (iu + (u^2 :+ 0)))
    gval  = ((k :+ 0) - iu*(r*x :+ 0) - dval) / ((k :+ 0) - iu*(r*x :+ 0) + dval)
    edtau = exp (-dval * (tau :+ 0))
    dTau  = ((k :+ 0) - iu*(r*x :+ 0) - dval) / (x^2 :+ 0) * (1 - edtau) / (1 - gval*edtau)
    cTau  = iu*(r*tau :+ 0) + ((k*th/(x^2)) :+ 0) *
              (((k :+ 0) - iu*(r*x :+ 0) - dval)*(tau :+ 0)
               - 2*log ((1 - gval*edtau)/(1 - gval)))
    logS0 = log (s0 :+ 0)
```

### 4.4 Calibration Practice: Objectives, Constraints, and the Feller Condition

Industrial Heston calibration minimizes vega-weighted implied-volatility errors over the quoted panel, subject to $\kappa, \theta, \xi, v_0 > 0$ and $\rho$ strictly inside the open interval $(-1, 1)$. Three practical realities dominate:

1. **Identifiability is weak.** The parameters trade off against each other along nearly flat valleys of the objective: raising $\xi$ (vol-of-vol) and lowering $|\rho|$ can produce nearly identical smiles. 
2. **The Feller condition is routinely violated.** Market-calibrated $\xi$ is typically large enough that $2\kappa\theta \le \xi^2$, meaning the variance process can hit zero. 
3. **The short end resists.** Even a perfectly calibrated Heston model generates an at-the-money skew that stays bounded as $T \to 0$, while the market skew diverges. No choice of the five parameters repairs this: it is a structural property of the Brownian-driven variance diffusion, and it is precisely the anomaly that motivates rough volatility [8].

The SWIFT wavelet method and related Fourier techniques have pushed single-panel calibration times into the sub-second regime [6][7].

### 4.5 Rough Volatility: Fractional Brownian Motion and the Short-End Skew

A fractional Brownian motion $W^H$ with Hurst exponent $H$ strictly between $0$ and $1$ is the centered Gaussian process with covariance $\mathbb{E}[W^H_t W^H_s] = \tfrac{1}{2}(t^{2H} + s^{2H} - |t-s|^{2H})$. The Rough Fractional Stochastic Volatility (RFSV) model of Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum sets

$$d\log \sigma_t = \nu\, dW^H_t \quad (\text{plus mean reversion at longer scales}),$$

and their power-variation estimation across asset classes finds $H \approx 0.1$ — volatility is far rougher than a Brownian motion and far from the long-memory regime above one half [5].

For pricing, the workhorse is the *rough Bergomi* model of Bayer, Friz and Gatheral [11]:

$$\sigma_t = \xi_0(t)\,\exp\!\left(\nu\sqrt{2H}\int_0^t (t-s)^{H-1/2}\,dW_s - \tfrac{1}{2}\nu^2 t^{2H}\right),$$

a lognormal volatility driven by a Riemann–Liouville fractional kernel, with the forward variance curve $\xi_0(t)$ calibrated exactly to the market. The rough Heston model replaces the variance SDE's Brownian driver with the fractional kernel $K(t) = t^{H-1/2}/\Gamma(H+1/2)$, turning the Riccati ODEs into *fractional* Riccati equations [7].

> **Theorem: Short-time skew asymptotics (Fukasawa; Alòs–León–Vives).** In a stochastic volatility model where volatility is driven by fractional Brownian motion with Hurst exponent $H$ below one half, the at-the-money implied volatility skew satisfies $\left.\partial_k \sigma_{\text{imp}}(k,T)\right|_{k=0} \sim C\,T^{H-1/2}$ as $T \to 0$. Classical Brownian-driven models ($H = 1/2$) produce bounded skew; only $H < 1/2$ reproduces the market's power-law explosion [8][12].

This is the empirical trump card. With just three parameters plus the forward variance curve, rough Bergomi fits the *entire* implied volatility surface, including the short-dated skew Heston cannot generate. The estimated $H$ from option prices coincides with the $H$ estimated from high-frequency volatility time series: the historical and pricing measures agree on roughness, a consistency no classical model achieves [5][8].

##5 Empirical Evidence / Proofs

The evidence for each layer is now quantitative. For Dupire, the evidence is constructive: nonparametric local volatility surfaces calibrated to listed equity options reproduce every vanilla quote to within bid–ask, and the mimicking identity is verified numerically inside calibrated Heston models [4][9].

For Heston, the evidence is the industry's revealed preference: two decades of equity, FX, and rates desks calibrate it daily, and Fourier-based methods (SWIFT, COS, Carr–Madan FFT) deliver the speed this requires [6][7]. It also fails empirically: calibrated sets violate the Feller condition, and its ATM skew term structure decays exponentially where the market's is power-law.

For roughness, the evidence is twofold and mutually reinforcing. Under the historical measure, scaling regressions of $m(q,\Delta)$ against $\Delta$ on Oxford-Man realized-volatility data yield $H$ near $0.1$ across indices, single stocks, FX, and commodities [5]. Under the pricing measure, log–log regressions of ATM skew against maturity yield the exponent $H - 1/2$ with the same $H$ — the pricing measure confirms the historical measure's roughness [8].

##6 Limitations

**Dupire inversion is ill-posed**: differentiating noisy data twice in strike amplifies noise, wings are extrapolated rather than identified, and the flat forward smile corrupts cliquet and forward-start pricing. **Heston is over-rigid**: five parameters cannot independently control the smile's level, slope, curvature, and term structure; the Feller violation is not a technicality but a symptom that the market wants more vol-of-vol than a square-root diffusion can safely provide; and its short-end skew is structurally bounded. **Rough models pay for realism with tractability**: the volatility is not a semimartingale, so no PDE exists; simulation requires the hybrid scheme or exact Cholesky methods with their heavy cost; calibration of rough Heston demands solving fractional Riccati equations inside every Fourier integral [7]; and a lively debate continues over whether jumps, rather than roughness, explain the short-dated skew.

##7 Conclusion

The arc from Black–Scholes to rough volatility is the arc of an inverse problem becoming honest about what it can identify. The Black–Scholes PDE [1] showed that volatility, whatever it is, is the only unhedgeable input to an option price. Dupire's formula [3] showed that the market's vanilla prices determine a unique Markovian volatility surface — and Gyöngy's theorem [9] revealed that surface as the projection of any richer dynamics. Heston [2] showed that a parsimonious two-factor diffusion with an affine characteristic function can fit the smile fast enough to calibrate every morning. Rough volatility [5] showed that the data — both the jagged paths of realized volatility and the power-law explosion of the short-dated skew — demand $H \approx 0.1$, and that three rough parameters explain what five classical ones cannot.

Open frontiers include multivariate roughness, joint SPX–VIX calibration, and machine-learning surrogates that make rough calibration as routine as Heston's [6][7].
