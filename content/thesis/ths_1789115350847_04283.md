# Differential Dataflow: Naiad's Timely Execution, Partially Ordered Timestamps, and DBSP's Incremental Circuit Semantics

## Abstract

This thesis presents a unified treatment of three interlocking advances in data-parallel incremental computation: Naiad's *timely dataflow* execution model, the differential-dataflow abstraction of computations over partially ordered timestamps, and DBSP's reformulation of incremental view maintenance as a circuit semantics over streams of $\mathbb{Z}$-sets. We trace how a single coordination problem — knowing when an operator has observed every message that could ever arrive at a given logical time — is resolved by *pointstamps* and *frontiers* in a distributed progress-tracking protocol [1], generalized by differential dataflow to arbitrary partially ordered times so that iteration, streaming, and batch updates compose within one engine [2], and finally re-derived from first principles in DBSP as differentiation and integration of streams over an abelian group [3]. We develop the formal semantics of each layer, prove the core correctness invariants of progress tracking, analyze the asymptotic cost of incremental operators, and survey empirical results from the Naiad, differential-dataflow, and DBSP implementations. We close by mapping where incremental maintenance is asymptotically free and where fundamental limits remain.

## 1. Introduction

Data-parallel systems have historically been partitioned into three tribes. **Batch processors** achieve high throughput at latencies of minutes and reprocess entire datasets when inputs change. **Stream processors** achieve millisecond latency but struggle with operations requiring global coordination, such as aggregation over unbounded windows or iterative graph algorithms. **Graph and iterative systems** handle iteration efficiently but treat the input graph as static, forcing full recomputation when edges arrive or depart. Yet these regimes are not fundamentally distinct: a batch update is a *stream* of changes applied to a collection; an iterative algorithm is a *nested* computation whose inner loop can itself be incremental; a streaming aggregation is an iterative computation over a growing input. What has been missing is a *coordination substrate* expressing all three within one execution model, and an *incremental semantics* letting every operator react to changes rather than to states.

Naiad [1] supplies the substrate. Its contribution, *timely dataflow*, is a computational model in which every message carries a **timestamp** drawn from a partially ordered set, and a lightweight distributed protocol tracks, for every edge of the dataflow graph, the set of timestamps that might still produce messages. Operators learn when a timestamp is *complete* — when no further messages bearing it (or any lesser timestamp) will ever arrive — and can therefore safely emit results, fire aggregations, and retire state. Differential dataflow [2] is the programming model this substrate enables: collections indexed by partially ordered times, operators lifted to act on *differences* with signed multiplicities, iteration as just another operator over a product order. DBSP [3] then re-derives the same ideas from streams, $\mathbb{Z}$-sets, differentiation $\mathcal{D}$, integration $\mathcal{I}$, and delay $z^{-1}$ — yielding a mechanical incrementalization algorithm for arbitrary queries with clean correctness proofs.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Classical incremental computation

Incremental computation predates distributed dataflow by decades. The *delta-rule* literature studied how to maintain a query result $Q$ under input changes: given $\Delta R$, compute $\Delta Q$ with $Q(R \oplus \Delta R) = Q(R) \oplus \Delta Q$, where $\oplus$ is multiset union with signed multiplicities. For the relational algebra, delta rules are mechanical — selection and projection distribute over $\oplus$ — and joins obey bilinearity:

$$\Delta(R \bowtie S) = (\Delta R \bowtie S) \oplus (R \bowtie \Delta S) \oplus (\Delta R \bowtie \Delta S).$$

Two facts shape everything that follows. First, *deletions require multiplicities*: a result tuple supported by two derivations must survive the deletion of one, so collections must be multisets — or elements of an abelian group with negative multiplicities as retractions. Second, *aggregation is the hard case*: $\Delta(\text{COUNT})$ is easy, but $\Delta(\text{MIN})$ under deletions may require re-scanning the group. Differential dataflow's answer is to retain, per group, the full multiset of contributing values (an *arrangement*), trading memory for incremental correctness [2].

### 2.2 Streaming systems and the coordination problem

Stream processors face a coordination problem batch systems avoid: with unbounded input, the system must decide *when it is safe to act* on partial information. The classical answers are **watermarks** (a single scalar clock per stream; one slow partition stalls everything, iteration unsupported), **barriers/snapshots** (periodic global coordination with overhead proportional to pipeline depth), and **punctuation** (ad-hoc control messages with no compositional theory).

Timely dataflow generalizes all three. A *pointstamp* $(e, t)$ — an edge $e$ and timestamp $t$ — is the unit of "what might still arrive." The system's *clock* is the set of undischarged pointstamps; its complement is knowledge. Watermarks are the special case of a total timestamp order with a scalar frontier; barriers are the special case of lockstep frontier advance. The generalization to *partial* orders is what lets iteration and streaming coexist: timestamps $(epoch, iteration)$ under the product order make $(5,1)$ and $(4,3)$ *incomparable*, so epoch 5's first iteration proceeds while epoch 4 still iterates — inexpressible with any scalar watermark [1][7].

### 2.3 From Naiad to differential dataflow to DBSP

The lineage is unusually direct. Naiad (SOSP 2013, best paper [1]) introduced timely dataflow as a *systems* contribution: a distributed runtime with a progress-tracking protocol. Differential dataflow (CIDR 2013 [2]) introduced the *programming model*: with partially ordered timestamps, iteration, incremental updates, and streaming become the same operator applied at different nesting depths. The Rust reimplementation [4] became the foundation of Materialize's streaming SQL engine. DBSP (arXiv 2022, VLDB 2023 [3][5]) then derived the whole edifice from simpler mathematics — streams, $\mathbb{Z}$-sets, $\mathcal{D}$, $\mathcal{I}$, $z^{-1}$ — yielding cleaner proofs and a systematic incrementalization algorithm for *arbitrary* circuits. A TLA$^+$ mechanization of the progress protocol [7] closes the loop with a machine-checked soundness proof.

## 3. Methodology

We formalize each layer, prove its central invariant, and exhibit how the layers compose. Three formal objects suffice.

**Timely dataflow graphs.** A computation is a directed graph $G = (V, E)$ of *operators* connected by *edges* carrying timestamped messages, with timestamps in a poset $(T, \le)$. Operators send messages only at times $\ge$ those received, and may request *notifications* — callbacks fired when the protocol guarantees no further messages at certain timestamps will arrive.

**Pointstamps, clocks, frontiers.** The pointstamp $(e,t)$ denotes the possibility of a future message on edge $e$ with time $\le t$. The *clock* $\text{Clock} \subseteq E \times T$ holds undischarged pointstamps; a pointstamp leaves the clock when the operator at the head of $e$ proves it will never again emit at times $\le t$ on $e$. The *frontier* of an edge is the antichain of minimal clocked timestamps — the horizon of outstanding work. An operator notified at time $t$ once $t$ is not dominated by any frontier element has, provably, seen every message it will ever receive at times $\le t$.

**Differential collections and DBSP streams.** A *differential collection* is a multiset of records evolving over partially ordered times, stored as *differences* $(r, t, \Delta)$, $\Delta \in \mathbb{Z}$; operators map difference streams to difference streams, and the value at time $t$ accumulates differences over the down-set of $t$. In DBSP, the same idea is a *stream* $s : \mathbb{N} \to A$ over an abelian group $A$ (typically a $\mathbb{Z}$-set), with **differentiation** $\mathcal{D}(s)[t] = s[t]-s[t-1]$, **integration** $\mathcal{I}(s)[t] = \sum_{i\le t} s[i]$, and **delay** $z^{-1}(s)[t] = s[t-1]$ breaking feedback cycles. Every DBSP circuit is built from these plus lifted stateless operators — and every such circuit incrementalizes by a fixed syntactic transformation.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Partially ordered timestamps and nested iteration

The most consequential decision in timely dataflow is that timestamps need only be *partially* ordered. Iterative computation demands that iteration $k$ observe the complete output of iteration $k-1$; under a total order, iteration $k$ of epoch $e$ must additionally wait for *all* work at lesser times, including unrelated epochs and loops. Under the product order $(e,i) \le (e',i') \iff e \le e' \land i \le i'$, independent coordinates are incomparable, the frontier (an antichain) advances along independent dimensions independently, and nested scopes simply append timestamp coordinates — tracked by the same protocol with no new machinery [1].

Hence the unification: *batch* is one epoch with no iteration; *streaming* is many epochs with no iteration; *iteration* is one epoch with many loop coordinates; *incremental iterative* computation (e.g., connected components over a changing graph) is many epochs $\times$ many iterations — handled with no special cases, each $(\text{epoch}, \text{iteration})$ point advancing independently.

> **Theorem (Frontier completeness).** Let $F_e$ be the frontier antichain of edge $e$. If timestamp $t$ is not $\ge$ any element of $F_e$, no message with timestamp $\le t$ will ever arrive on $e$; dually, every future arrival has timestamp $\ge$ some element of $F_e$. *Proof sketch.* A pointstamp $(e,t)$ remains clocked exactly while some upstream operator might still emit at times $\le t$; operators discharge pointstamps only when input frontiers and internal state rule out future emissions. The frontier of minimal undischarged pointstamps therefore dominates all future arrivals [1][7].

### 4.2 The timely progress-tracking protocol

Each worker maintains, per local edge, the multiset of pointstamps representing outstanding capabilities ("I may yet send messages at these times") and periodically exchanges *pointstamp summaries* across worker boundaries. Senders report held pointstamps; receivers merge them into the local clock. Because pointstamp multisets support *consolidation* (adding multiplicities on duplicate reports, canceling on dropped capabilities), the protocol converges to the true global clock with no worker holding global state. Traffic is proportional to *capability churn*, not data volume — steady-state streaming with a fixed frontier generates no protocol traffic — and workers never block awaiting frontier information; stragglers delay only frontiers for which they actually hold capabilities [1].

The mechanized analysis in [7] models workers, edges, and the clock as a TLA$^+$ state machine and proves the safety property: once $(e,t) \notin \text{Clock}$, messages on $e$ never have times $\le t$. The proof requires inputs to never *gain* capabilities over time — enforced in practice by the `advance_to` discipline: an input may move its frontier forward, never backward.

The Rust surface is remarkably small [4]:

```rust
use timely::dataflow::operators::{Input, Inspect, Map};

fn main() {
    timely::execute_from_args(std::env::args(), |worker| {
        // Open a dataflow; timestamps here are u64 epochs.
        let mut input = worker.dataflow::<u64, _, _>(|scope| {
            let (handle, stream) = scope.new_input();
            stream
                .map(|x: u64| x * x)
                .inspect(|x| println!("worker saw {:?}", x));
            handle // returned so the driver can send/advance
        });
        // Drive ten epochs, advancing the input frontier each round.
        for round in 0..10u64 {
            input.send(round);
            input.advance_to(round + 1); // retract capability for time `round`
            worker.step();               // run until no more local work
        }
    }).unwrap();
}
```

`advance_to` is the programmer-visible face of pointstamp discharge: it retracts the capability to send at times $\le$ `round`, letting downstream frontiers advance and notifications fire.

### 4.3 Differential dataflow: differences, arrangements, and consolidation

A differential **collection** is stored as a stream of **differences** $(r, t, \Delta)$, $\Delta \in \mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}$; every operator maps difference streams to difference streams, and the value at time $t$ accumulates differences over its down-set. Because accumulation respects the partial order, differences at incomparable times — different epochs, different iterations — coexist without interference, so one code path handles batch updates, streaming updates, and iterative fixpoints [2].

Two engineering ideas make the abstraction fast:

- **Arrangements.** A maintained, indexed, multiversion representation of a collection keyed by $(\text{key}, \text{time})$, shared by all downstream operators. Building it once amortizes indexing across the dataflow; because arrangements index *differences*, they update incrementally for free. Joins against arrangements become indexed lookups rather than re-scans.
- **Consolidation.** Difference streams accumulate redundancy ($+1$ and $-1$ for the same record cancel). Periodic consolidation merges the stream, canceling opposite multiplicities — the differential analogue of garbage collection — keeping memory proportional to the *current* collection rather than total update history.

The operator suite is closed under differentiation: linear operators (map, filter, concat) distribute over $\oplus$; the bilinear join uses the delta rule of §2.1 with each term an arrangement lookup; `iterate` scopes timestamps into a product order and applies the loop body to differences, so each iteration processes only newly derived facts — semi-naive evaluation recovered as a special case [2].

| Operator | Incremental rule | Cost per update batch |
|---|---|---|
| `map` / `filter` | distribute over $\oplus$ | $O(\|\Delta\|)$ |
| `concat` | union of difference streams | $O(\|\Delta\|)$ |
| `join` (arranged) | bilinear delta rule, 3 terms | $O(\|\Delta R\|\bar{d}_S + \|\Delta S\|\bar{d}_R)$ |
| `reduce` / `group` | per-key multiset maintenance | $O(\|\Delta\| \log n)$ amortized |
| `distinct` | multiplicity threshold at zero | $O(\|\Delta\| \log n)$ amortized |
| `iterate` (fixpoint) | semi-naive over product timestamps | $O(\text{newly derived facts})$ |

No operator's incremental cost depends on full input size except through index lookups — the defining property of a genuinely incremental engine.

### 4.4 DBSP: streams, $\mathbb{Z}$-sets, and the incremental circuit semantics

DBSP re-derives differential dataflow from a minimal core. Fix an abelian group $(A,+ ,0,-)$; a **stream** $s : \mathbb{N} \to A$ assigns each discrete step a value (a $\mathbb{Z}$-set stream models an evolving database: $\mathcal{DB}[t]$, the snapshot after $t$ transactions). Three operators suffice: **differentiation** $\mathcal{D}(s)[t] = s[t]-s[t-1]$, **integration** $\mathcal{I}(s)[t] = \sum_{i\le t} s[i]$ with $\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{D}(s)) = s$, and **delay** $z^{-1}(s)[t] = s[t-1]$ ensuring causal feedback. A **DBSP circuit** wires streams through lifted stateless operators plus $\mathcal{D}$, $\mathcal{I}$, $z^{-1}$; its **incremental version** $Q^\Delta$ replaces each lifted $f$ with $f^\Delta = \mathcal{D} \circ f \circ \mathcal{I}$ and cancels adjacent $\mathcal{I}\circ\mathcal{D}$ pairs [3].

> **Theorem (DBSP incrementalization [3]).** For every DBSP circuit $Q$ and input stream $x$, $\mathcal{I}(Q^\Delta(\mathcal{D}(x))) = Q(x)$. If each operator's incremental version costs proportional to its input change, $Q^\Delta$'s total work is proportional to $\sum_t \|\mathcal{D}(x)[t]\|$ — cost follows the *delta*, not the state.

This yields a *compiler*: any query expressible as a circuit — relational algebra, grouping, aggregation, nested relations, flatmap, monotone and non-monotone recursion — incrementalizes automatically, with no hand-derived delta rules. Classical delta rules are the special case of $f^\Delta$ applied to relational operators. Recursion is a feedback edge with $z^{-1}$ delay; under incrementalization the loop propagates only newly derived differences (semi-naive evaluation, derived rather than special-cased), and $\mathbb{Z}$-sets give non-monotone operators a well-defined action on retractions [3][5].

### 4.5 Putting it together: incremental joins and nested recursion at scale

The layers compose into a complete story for the hardest workloads — *incremental, iterative* queries like maintaining connected components over a changing graph. Timely dataflow's protocol tracks frontiers over $(\text{epoch}, \text{iteration})$ product timestamps, so the system always knows exactly which pairs are complete. Differential dataflow's `iterate` expresses the fixpoint as a nested-scope dataflow, with joins and aggregations sharing arrangements across iterations. DBSP's circuit semantics explains *why* it is correct: the dataflow *is* a circuit, its incrementalization is the $\mathcal{D}$/$\mathcal{I}$ transformation, and the nested loop is a delayed feedback edge.

For incremental strongly connected components — the flagship of [2] — each epoch's fixpoint reuses the previous epoch's work: iterations propagate only differences, and consolidation cancels shared derivations.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Core theorems

> **Theorem (Timely progress safety [1][7]).** If pointstamp $(e,t)$ has been discharged from the global clock, no message with timestamp $\le t$ will subsequently be sent on $e$. An operator notified at time $t$ on frontier information has therefore observed every message it will ever receive at times $\le t$.

> **Theorem (Differential correctness [2]).** For a differential dataflow $Q$, the accumulation of output differences at times $\le t$ equals the non-incremental query applied to accumulated inputs at times $\le t$. Iteration scopes compute least fixpoints of their bodies.

> **Theorem (DBSP incrementalization [3]).** For every DBSP circuit $Q$, $\mathcal{I}(Q^\Delta(\mathcal{D}(x))) = Q(x)$ for all input streams $x$, and $Q^\Delta$ is itself a DBSP circuit. Incremental view maintenance for *any* DBSP-expressible query reduces to circuit construction.

End to end: timely dataflow tells operators *when* to act, differential dataflow tells them *what* the incremental result denotes, and DBSP guarantees the incremental program *exists* and is derivable by a fixed algorithm.

### 5.2 Empirical evidence

**Naiad (SOSP 2013).** On a 48-machine cluster, one engine matches or beats specialized systems in iterative machine learning, streaming analytics, and interactive graph queries [1]. Reported per-iteration latencies fall below one millisecond and interactive queries below 100 ms — against minutes per job for batch systems — with the progress protocol's overhead negligible next to data movement: coordination is *not* the bottleneck [8].

**Differential dataflow (CIDR 2013).** Incrementally maintained strongly connected components over a web-scale graph (tens of millions of vertices, billions of edges) at *interactive* timescales — seconds versus minutes for from-scratch recomputation — absorbing edge updates continuously [2][6]. Differences at incomparable timestamps are independent, so iterations of different epochs overlap freely, a parallelism bulk-synchronous systems cannot express.

**Timely (Rust) and Materialize.** The Rust reimplementation [4] turned the prototype into production infrastructure: Materialize's streaming SQL engine is built directly on timely and differential dataflow, maintaining SQL views — joins, aggregations, recursive queries — over changing inputs at millisecond-scale update latency. A deployed system whose daily correctness rests on the theorems of §5.1.

**DBSP (VLDB 2023).** The paper and its reference implementation [5] evaluate circuit incrementalization across the full relational algebra plus grouping, aggregation, nested relations, unnest, and monotone/non-monotone recursion: per-transaction work scales with the *change*, and feature combinations that previously needed bespoke delta rules (e.g., aggregation inside recursion) incrementalize correctly out of the box [3].

| System | Workload | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| Naiad [1] | Iterative ML, streaming, graph queries (48 nodes) | Beats specialized systems per-domain; sub-ms iterations |
| Differential dataflow [2] | Incremental SCC, web-scale graph | Interactive latencies vs. minutes for recomputation |
| Timely (Rust) [4] / Materialize | Streaming SQL views in production | Ms-scale view maintenance; deployed correctness |
| DBSP [3][5] | Full relational + recursion IVM | Work proportional to change; automatic incrementalization |

## 6. Limitations

**Coordination is cheap but not free.** Protocol traffic scales with *capability churn*, not data volume — but workloads with rapidly oscillating frontiers (fine-grained out-of-order event time with frequent retractions) can generate pointstamp traffic rivaling the data. A single operator holding a capability at time $t$ pins every downstream frontier at $\le t$, and identifying the straggler in a large deployment remains operationally painful [1].

**Partial orders complicate the programming model.** Product timestamps are powerful but unintuitive; reasoning about visibility across (epoch, iteration) pairs and notification firing under nested scopes has a steep learning curve. Timestamp-discipline bugs — e.g., emitting below the current frontier — manifest as hangs (the frontier never advances) rather than clean errors [4].

**Arrangements trade memory for incrementality.** Headline results depend on arrangements: indexed multiversion state proportional to input size, retained indefinitely. Dense joins (near-cross-products) can exceed any memory budget, and consolidation's scheduling interacts badly with latency SLOs in the worst case [2].

**DBSP's group requirement excludes some operators.** The $\mathcal{D}$/$\mathcal{I}$ duality needs an abelian group. Top-$k$ with arbitrary tie-breaking, approximate sketches with non-invertible merges, and non-commutative model updates embed awkwardly or not at all. Non-monotone recursion is *expressible* but retractions cascading through negation or min-aggregation can degrade to recomputation: the theory guarantees correctness, not efficiency [3].

**Fault tolerance is out of scope.** Neither Naiad's paper nor DBSP's core theory addresses checkpointing, recovery, or exactly-once delivery; production systems layer these on via snapshots and replay, but the interaction of frontier-based progress with snapshot coordination remains under-formalized relative to the $\mathcal{D}$/$\mathcal{I}$ algebra.

## 7. Conclusion

Timely dataflow, differential dataflow, and DBSP are three views of one idea: *computation over partially ordered time, incrementalized by construction*. Naiad showed a distributed system can track, with minimal coordination, exactly when each logical time is complete — even over product orders spanning epochs and nested iterations [1]. Differential dataflow showed that programming against differences over such times unifies batch, streaming, and iterative computation in one operator suite with state-of-the-art incremental performance [2]. DBSP showed the whole construction follows from streams over abelian groups plus differentiation, integration, and delay — yielding a mechanical incrementalization algorithm and the cleanest proofs in the lineage [3].

The open problems are now sharply posed: cost-aware progress tracking, memory-bounded arrangements, circuit semantics beyond abelian groups for ML-augmented queries, and an algebraic treatment of fault tolerance that makes exactly-once streaming a theorem rather than an engineering discipline.
