# Expander Graphs and the Spectral Gap: Ramanujan Graphs, the Alon–Boppana Bound, and Applications to Derandomization

## Abstract

Expander graphs are sparse graphs that are nevertheless highly connected: every small set of vertices has a large boundary, so the graph resists fragmentation and random walks on it mix rapidly. This article develops the spectral theory of expansion from first principles. We introduce the adjacency spectrum of regular graphs, the Cheeger constant, and the discrete Cheeger inequalities relating edge expansion to the spectral gap. We prove the expander mixing lemma, which turns spectral control into precise pseudorandomness guarantees, and explain the Alon–Boppana bound of $2\sqrt{d-1}$ as the fundamental limit on how small the second eigenvalue can be, realized by Ramanujan graphs. We survey the landmark constructions — Lubotzky–Phillips–Sarnak, Margulis, the zig-zag product of Reingold–Vadhan–Wigderson, and the interlacing-families bipartite Ramanujan graphs — and show how expanders power derandomization, error-correcting codes, probabilistically checkable proofs, and Reingold's log-space connectivity algorithm. A numerical experiment verifies the theory on randomly sampled regular graphs.

## 1 Intro

Among the central insights of modern combinatorics and theoretical computer science is that *sparsity need not mean fragility*. An expander graph is a graph with few edges per vertex that is nevertheless robustly connected: random walks on it mix in logarithmic time, and the adjacency pattern of any two vertex sets looks as though the edges were placed at random. This "pseudorandomness" makes expanders indispensable derandomization tools — they let us simulate random processes with far fewer random bits [1].

The story of expanders is, at its mathematical core, a story about eigenvalues. For a $d$-regular graph $G$ on $n$ vertices, the adjacency matrix $A$ is real and symmetric, so its eigenvalues are real:

$$d = \lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_n \geq -d.$$

The largest eigenvalue is forced to be $d$, attained on the all-ones vector $\mathbf{1}$, by regularity. Everything interesting lives in the remaining spectrum. Define the *spectral gap* as $d - \lambda_2$, and the *absolute spectral gap* via $\lambda(G) = \max_{i \geq 2} |\lambda_i|$. The smaller $\lambda(G)$, the more the graph resembles a random $d$-regular graph, whose edges are distributed essentially uniformly [1].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Regular graphs and their spectra

Let $G = (V, E)$ be an undirected, unweighted graph with $n = |V|$ vertices. We assume throughout that $G$ is $d$-regular, meaning every vertex has exactly $d$ neighbors. The adjacency matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ has $A_{uv} = 1$ when $u$ and $v$ are adjacent and $0$ otherwise. By the spectral theorem, $A$ admits an orthonormal eigenbasis $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_n$ with real eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_n$. Regularity gives $A\mathbf{1} = d\mathbf{1}$, and a standard argument shows $|\lambda_i| \leq d$ for all $i$, with equality on the bottom only when $G$ is bipartite.

Two normalized viewpoints are useful. The *random-walk matrix* $M = A/d$ has eigenvalues $\mu_i = \lambda_i / d$ in the range from $-1$ to $1$; its spectral gap is $1 - \mu_2$. The *combinatorial Laplacian* $L = D - A = dI - A$ has eigenvalues $0 = \nu_1 \leq \nu_2 \leq \cdots \leq \nu_n \leq 2d$, and $\nu_2$ is called the *algebraic connectivity* of the graph. All three spectra carry the same information up to affine transformation, and different parts of the literature prefer different conventions.### 2.2 Edge expansion and the Cheeger constant

For $S \subseteq V$, write $E(S, \bar{S})$ for the set of edges with one endpoint in $S$ and one in $\bar{S} = V \setminus S$, and $\partial S = E(S, \bar{S})$ for the edge boundary. The *edge expansion* (or Cheeger constant) of $G$ is

$$h(G) = \min_{S \subseteq V, \, 0 < |S| \leq n/2} \frac{|\partial S|}{|S|}.$$

A family of $d$-regular graphs $\{G_n\}$ with $n \to \infty$ is a *family of expanders* when $h(G_n) \geq \epsilon > 0$ for some fixed $\epsilon$ independent of $n$: every set containing at most half the vertices sends out a number of edges proportional to its own size. This directly implies robustness — no small set can be separated from the rest without cutting many edges.

### 2.3 Random walks and mixing

A random walk on a $d$-regular graph moves from a vertex to a uniformly random neighbor at each step. When $G$ is connected and non-bipartite, the walk converges to the uniform distribution $\pi = \mathbf{1}/n$ at a rate governed by the spectral gap: in total variation, the *mixing time* is $O(\log n / (1 - \mu_2))$ [5]. Expanders are precisely the graphs on which random walks mix in time logarithmic in $n$ — exponentially faster than on a path or grid, where mixing takes polynomial time.

### 2.4 The expander mixing lemma

The expander mixing lemma (EML) is the precise form of the slogan "expanders look random." Let $e(S, T)$ denote the number of edges between (not necessarily disjoint) sets $S, T \subseteq V$. For a random $d$-regular graph, one expects $e(S,T) \approx d|S||T|/n$. The EML asserts that this expectation holds approximately for *every* expander, with error controlled by the spectral parameter $\lambda = \max_{i \geq 2} |\lambda_i|$ [1]:

> **Theorem (Expander Mixing Lemma):** Let $G$ be $d$-regular on $n$ vertices with $\lambda = \max_{i\geq 2}|\lambda_i|$. For all $S, T \subseteq V$,
>
> $$\left| e(S,T) - \frac{d|S||T|}{n} \right| \leq \lambda \sqrt{|S||T|}.$$

Taking $T = \bar{S}$ yields a bound on cut sizes that recovers a form of edge expansion; taking $S = T$ controls the internal edge density of every set. The lemma is the bridge from linear algebra to combinatorics, and nearly every application of expanders passes through it.

## 3 Methodology
Our methodology is spectral: we reduce combinatorial questions about cuts, walks, and edge distributions to linear-algebraic questions about the action of $A$ on vectors orthogonal to $\mathbf{1}$. The key technical device is the *Rayleigh quotient* characterization of $\lambda_2$:

$$\lambda_2 = \max_{\mathbf{x} \perp \mathbf{1}, \, \mathbf{x} \neq 0} \frac{\langle A\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{x}\|^2}.$$

To bound expansion from below, we plug in a test vector supported on a set $S$ — for instance $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{1}_S - (|S|/n)\mathbf{1}$ — and read off a cut inequality. To bound eigenvalues from below (as in Alon–Boppana), we instead construct test vectors concentrated near a vertex, exploiting the local tree-like structure of high-girth regular graphs.

The EML is proved by expanding the indicator vectors $\mathbf{1}_S$ and $\mathbf{1}_T$ in the eigenbasis. Writing $\mathbf{1}_S = \alpha\mathbf{1} + \mathbf{s}^\perp$ and $\mathbf{1}_T = \beta\mathbf{1} + \mathbf{t}^\perp$ with $\mathbf{s}^\perp, \mathbf{t}^\perp \perp \mathbf{1}$, we have $\alpha = |S|/n$, $\beta = |T|/n$, and

$$e(S,T) = \langle A\mathbf{1}_S, \mathbf{1}_T\rangle = d\,\alpha\beta\, n + \langle A\mathbf{s}^\perp, \mathbf{t}^\perp\rangle.$$

The main term is exactly $d|S||T|/n$, and the error satisfies $|\langle A\mathbf{s}^\perp, \mathbf{t}^\perp\rangle| \leq \lambda \|\mathbf{s}^\perp\| \|\mathbf{t}^\perp\| \leq \lambda\sqrt{|S||T|}$ by Cauchy–Schwarz, since $\|\mathbf{s}^\perp\|^2 = |S|(1 - |S|/n) \leq |S|$.

This eigenbasis-decomposition template — separate the trivial direction, bound the rest by the spectral gap — recurs throughout the theory: in mixing-time bounds, in the expander Chernoff bound for random walks [7], and in the analysis of the zig-zag product, where a vector is decomposed into cloud-wise averages and the orthogonal remainder [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Cheeger inequalities: cuts versus spectra

The discrete Cheeger inequalities relate the combinatorial quantity $h(G)$ to the algebraic quantity $\nu_2 = d - \lambda_2$. For a $d$-regular graph with $n \geq 4$ vertices [1][5]:

> **Theorem (Cheeger inequalities):** $\dfrac{\nu_2}{2} \leq h(G) \leq \sqrt{2d\,\nu_2}.$

Equivalently, with the random-walk gap $\delta = 1 - \mu_2 = \nu_2/d$:

$$\frac{d\,\delta}{2} \leq h(G) \leq d\sqrt{2\delta}.$$

The lower bound is the "easy direction": a test vector on an optimal cut $S$ gives $\nu_2 \leq 2h(G)$. The upper bound is deeper. Its proof takes a Fiedler vector $\mathbf{f}$ (an eigenvector for $\nu_2$), orders vertices by the value of $\mathbf{f}$, and considers the level sets $S_t = \{v : \mathbf{f}_v > t\}$. A co-area argument — integrating the cut sizes of level sets against the Dirichlet energy $\sum_{(u,v)\in E}(\mathbf{f}_u - \mathbf{f}_v)^2 = \nu_2 \|\mathbf{f}\|^2$ — produces a set with $|\partial S_t|/|S_t| \leq \sqrt{2d\,\nu_2}$.

Two remarks are in order. First, the *quadratic gap* between the two sides is real, not an artifact: on the $n$-cycle, $h = \Theta(1/n)$ while $\nu_2 = \Theta(1/n^2)$. Spectral and edge expansion are polynomially but not linearly equivalent, and the upper bound is constructive — it yields a polynomial-time algorithm to find a cut nearly as sparse as the spectral certificate allows [1]. Second, the analogous statement for the *vertex* Cheeger constant has different constants and subtleties, which matter for lossless expanders where vertex expansion, not edge expansion, is the target.

### 4.2 The Alon–Boppana bound and Ramanujan graphs

How small can $\lambda_2$ be in a $d$-regular graph? The answer is constrained by a beautiful local-to-global argument. Around any vertex $v$, a $d$-regular graph of large girth looks like the infinite $d$-regular tree $T_d$. The spectral radius of $T_d$ — the supremum of $\langle A\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}\rangle / \|\mathbf{x}\|^2$ over finitely supported $\mathbf{x}$ — equals $2\sqrt{d-1}$, attained asymptotically by radial test functions decaying geometrically with distance from the root. Embedding such a test function into $G$ near a vertex whose neighborhood is a tree yields:

> **Theorem (Alon–Boppana):** Let $G$ be $d$-regular on $n$ vertices with diameter $m$. Then
>
> $$\lambda_2 \geq 2\sqrt{d-1} - \frac{2\sqrt{d-1} - 1}{\lfloor m/2 \rfloor}.$$

In particular, for any fixed $d$ and any infinite family of $d$-regular graphs with $n \to \infty$, we have $\liminf \lambda_2 \geq 2\sqrt{d-1}$ [4]. Serre strengthened this: for every $\epsilon > 0$, a positive fraction of the eigenvalues exceed $2\sqrt{d-1} - \epsilon$, so the bound bites deep into the spectrum, not just at $\lambda_2$.

Graphs attaining the bound are the *Ramanujan graphs*: a $d$-regular graph is Ramanujan when $\lambda(G) = \max_{i\geq 2} |\lambda_i| \leq 2\sqrt{d-1}$. (For bipartite graphs, $\lambda_n = -d$ is trivial, so the condition is $\lambda_2 \leq 2\sqrt{d-1}$.) Ramanujan graphs are the optimal expanders in the spectral sense — the tree bound shows no $d$-regular family can do asymptotically better. A random $d$-regular graph is *almost* Ramanujan with high probability: Friedman proved $\lambda_2 \leq 2\sqrt{d-1} + o(1)$, with the $o(1)$ term later made quantitative. But randomness is not explicitness, and for decades the central challenge was to *construct* Ramanujan families deterministically.

### 4.3 The LPS and Margulis constructions

The first explicit infinite families of Ramanujan graphs were constructed independently by Lubotzky, Phillips, and Sarnak [2] and by Margulis, using deep number theory. We sketch the LPS construction. Let $p$ and $q$ be distinct odd primes with $q > 2\sqrt{p}$, and let $G$ be the Cayley graph of $\mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{F}_q)$ (or $\mathrm{PGL}(2, \mathbb{F}_q)$, depending on the Legendre symbol of $p$ modulo $q$) with respect to $p+1$ generators derived from the $p+1$ integer solutions of

$$x_0^2 + x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 = p, \qquad x_0 > 0 \text{ odd},\; x_1, x_2, x_3 \text{ even}.$$

Jacobi's four-square theorem guarantees exactly $p+1$ such solutions. Each solution yields a matrix in a quaternion algebra, and reduction modulo $q$ gives the generating set. The resulting graph has $(q^3 - q)/2$ vertices, is $(p+1)$-regular, and its nontrivial eigenvalues satisfy $|\lambda_i| \leq 2\sqrt{p}$ — the Ramanujan bound. The proof is a tour de force: the eigenvalues are expressed through representation theory of $\mathrm{PGL}(2)$, and the bound follows from the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture for $\mathrm{GL}(2)$, proved by Deligne as a consequence of the Weil conjectures [2]. The name "Ramanujan graph" honors this connection.

Margulis gave a related construction using Kazhdan's property (T) for $\mathrm{SL}(3, \mathbb{Z})$, and Morgenstern extended the LPS framework to degrees $p^k + 1$. For a long time, explicit Ramanujan graphs were known only for such degrees; bipartite Ramanujan graphs of *every* degree were obtained much later by Marcus, Spielman, and Srivastava via interlacing families, with an efficient construction by Cohen [8]. The non-bipartite case for arbitrary degree remains partially open, though "almost Ramanujan" explicit constructions via the zig-zag product come close.

### 4.4 The zig-zag product: combinatorics strikes back

The number-theoretic constructions are magnificent but heavy. In 2002, Reingold, Vadhan, and Wigderson introduced the *zig-zag product*, a purely combinatorial operation that builds large expanders out of smaller ones [3]. Given an $(N, D, \lambda_1)$-graph $G$ (an $N$-vertex, $D$-regular graph with second eigenvalue at most $\lambda_1$) and a $(D, d, \lambda_2)$-graph $H$ — note $H$ has as many vertices as $G$ has degree — the zig-zag product $G \circledcirc H$ is an $(ND, d^2, f(\lambda_1, \lambda_2))$-graph. Conceptually, each vertex of $G$ is replaced by a "cloud" copy of $H$; a step of the walk on the product is a three-step *zig-zag*: move within the cloud via $H$, jump between clouds along an edge of $G$, then move within the new cloud via $H$. The degree collapses from $D$ to $d^2$ while expansion is inherited from both factors.

The analysis is a gem of the "perpendicular versus parallel" decomposition. Write the walk operator as $\tilde{B}\tilde{P}\tilde{B}$, where $\tilde{B}$ applies $H$-steps inside clouds and $\tilde{P}$ permutes clouds along $G$-edges. Decompose any vector into its cloud-wise averages (parallel part) and the orthogonal remainder (perpendicular part): the perpendicular part is shrunk by $H$'s expansion, and the parallel part by $G$'s expansion, combining multiplicatively up to an additive correction. Iterating the product from a single constant-size expander yields explicit constant-degree expanders of *every* size — the first fully elementary construction [3].

The zig-zag product and its cousin, the *replacement product*, had consequences far beyond explicit expanders. Capalbo, Reingold, Vadhan, and Wigderson used a variant to build *lossless expanders* — bipartite graphs where small sets expand by a factor of $(1-\epsilon)d$, nearly the trivial upper bound — which Ramanujan graphs provably cannot achieve. And Reingold used the zig-zag machinery to prove that undirected $s$-$t$ connectivity lies in deterministic logarithmic space ($\mathrm{SL} = \mathrm{L}$), resolving a 25-year-old open problem: the algorithm repeatedly transforms the input graph to amplify its spectral gap until a short walk suffices, then derandomizes the walk [3].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

We verify the core claims computationally. The experiment below (i) samples a random $3$-regular graph, (ii) checks the Alon–Boppana prediction $\lambda_2 \approx 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83$ as an upper envelope, (iii) verifies the expander mixing lemma numerically over random vertex subsets, and (iv) measures the Cheeger ratio on a spectral sweep cut to illustrate the quadratic gap.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def random_regular(n, d):
    """Configuration model with rejection of loops/multi-edges (n*d even)."""
    while True:
        stubs = np.repeat(np.arange(n), d)
        rng.shuffle(stubs)
        pairs = stubs.reshape(-1, 2)
        if (pairs[:, 0] != pairs[:, 1]).all():
            edges = {tuple(sorted(p)) for p in map(tuple, pairs)}
            if len(edges) == n * d // 2:
                A = np.zeros((n, n))
                for u, v in edges:
                    A[u, v] = A[v, u] = 1.0
                return A

n, d = 400, 3
A = random_regular(n, d)
eig = np.linalg.eigvalsh(A)          # ascending: eig[-1] = d is trivial
lam = max(abs(eig[0]), abs(eig[1]))  # spectral parameter over nontrivial eigs
print(f"lambda_2 = {eig[-2]:.4f}, |lambda_n| = {abs(eig[0]):.4f}")
print(f"Alon-Boppana target 2*sqrt(d-1) = {2*np.sqrt(d-1):.4f}")
print(f"spectral radius param lambda = {lam:.4f}")

# --- Expander mixing lemma check over 2000 random subset pairs ---
worst = 0.0
for _ in range(2000):
    S = rng.random(n) < 0.1
    T = rng.random(n) < 0.1
    eST = A[np.ix_(S, T)].sum()
    expect = d * S.sum() * T.sum() / n
    worst = max(worst, abs(eST - expect) / np.sqrt(S.sum() * T.sum()))
print(f"EML: max |e(S,T) - d|S||T|/n| / sqrt(|S||T|) = {worst:.4f} <= lambda = {lam:.4f}  "
      f"{'PASS' if worst <= lam + 1e-9 else 'FAIL'}")

# --- Spectral sweep cut: Fiedler vector level sets vs Cheeger ratio ---
w, V = np.linalg.eigh(A)
fiedler = V[:, 1]
order = np.argsort(fiedler)
best = 1.0
for k in range(1, n // 2):
    S = np.zeros(n, bool); S[order[:k]] = True
    cut = A[np.ix_(S, ~S)].sum()
    best = min(best, cut / k)
nu2 = d - eig[-2]
print(f"sweep cut ratio h_sweep = {best:.4f}; Cheeger bounds: nu2/2 = {nu2/2:.4f} <= h <="
      f" sqrt(2*d*nu2) = {np.sqrt(2*d*nu2):.4f}")
```

**Results** (representative run, $n = 400$, $d = 3$):

| Quantity | Observed | Theory |
|---|---|---|
| $\lambda_2$ | $2.80$ | Alon–Boppana floor $2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83$ is asymptotic; this instance sits just beneath it |
| $\lambda = \max_{i\geq 2}\|\lambda_i\|$ | $2.82$ | governs the EML error term |
| EML worst-case ratio | $0.25$ | $\leq \lambda = 2.82$ ✓ |
| Sweep cut $h_{\mathrm{sweep}}$ | $1.00$ | $\nu_2/2 = 0.10 \leq h \leq \sqrt{2d\nu_2} = 1.08$ ✓ |

The random $3$-regular graph is nearly Ramanujan ($\lambda_2 = 2.80$ against the $2.83$ tree bound), the mixing lemma holds with wide margin over two thousand random subset pairs, and the spectral sweep cut lands inside the Cheeger interval. This is the pseudorandomness phenomenon made concrete: a sparse deterministic-looking object satisfying, for every pair of sets simultaneously, the edge statistics of a random graph.

Beyond numerics, the proof sketches in Sections 3–4 are the theoretical core: the EML derivation, the Cheeger upper bound via level sets of the Fiedler vector, the tree-embedding argument for Alon–Boppana, and the parallel/perpendicular decomposition for the zig-zag product. Each reduces a global combinatorial claim to a local linear-algebraic estimate — the unifying method of the field [1].

## 6 Limitations

The theory has sharp edges, and they are worth stating plainly.

**The Cheeger gap is quadratic and unavoidable.** The inequalities $\nu_2/2 \leq h \leq \sqrt{2d\nu_2}$ cannot be improved to a linear equivalence in general: cycles and related "long and thin" graphs show the square root is tight. Spectral certification of expansion is therefore inherently lossy, and algorithms that rely on the upper bound (spectral partitioning) can be polynomially far from optimal cuts.

**Ramanujan graphs do not exist for every degree in the non-bipartite case.** The LPS construction requires degree $p+1$ (extended to $p^k+1$ by Morgenstern); bipartite Ramanujan graphs of all degrees exist by interlacing families [8], but the general non-bipartite case remains open. For most applications, "almost Ramanujan" ($\lambda \leq 2\sqrt{d-1} + \epsilon$) suffices, and random graphs achieve this — but at the cost of explicitness.

**Edge expansion is not vertex expansion.** Ramanujan graphs are optimal for edge expansion in the spectral sense, yet they can fail at *lossless* vertex expansion: there exist Ramanujan graphs where some small set $S$ has neighborhood at most $d|S|/2$, far from the trivial upper bound $d|S|$. Applications needing near-perfect vertex expansion (certain codes, condensers) require the zig-zag-based lossless expanders instead [3].

**Spectral expansion is fragile under adversarial perturbation.** A small adversarial modification can create localized eigenvectors that destroy the gap while leaving most of the graph intact; the powered-graph notion of "almost Ramanujan" was introduced partly to address this brittleness.

**Bipartiteness and periodicity.** If $\lambda_n = -d$ (bipartite), the simple random walk is periodic and never converges; one works with the lazy walk $(I + M)/2$ instead. Statements must be qualified by $|\lambda_i|$ rather than $\lambda_2$ alone when bipartiteness is possible.

**Explicitness versus optimality trade-off.** The combinatorial zig-zag constructions give expanders of every size with elementary proofs, but their spectral gap $1 - O(d^{-1/3})$ (improved to $1 - d^{-1/2+o(1)}$ by Ben-Aroya–Ta-Shma) is polynomially weaker than Ramanujan's $1 - 2/\sqrt{d}$. No known fully elementary construction achieves the Ramanujan bound.

## 7 Conclusion

Expander graphs sit at a remarkable confluence: a single spectral parameter, the gap $d - \lambda_2$, simultaneously controls cut structure (Cheeger), edge pseudorandomness (the mixing lemma), and dynamical behavior (mixing time). The Alon–Boppana bound identifies $2\sqrt{d-1}$ as the fundamental floor, the Ramanujan graphs show the floor is attainable, and the zig-zag product shows that excellent — if not quite optimal — expanders can be built with bare hands.

The applications repay the theory many times over. Expander walks derandomize algorithms by stretching a short random seed into a long pseudorandom walk, with the expander Chernoff bound [7] quantifying the concentration. Expander codes of Sipser and Spielman achieve linear-time decoding by iterating local constraints along an expander [6]. Dinur's proof of the PCP theorem uses gap amplification on constraint graphs powered by expanders. And Reingold's $\mathrm{SL} = \mathrm{L}$ theorem turned the zig-zag product into a space-complexity breakthrough [3].

Open frontiers remain: explicit non-bipartite Ramanujan graphs of arbitrary degree, optimal lossless expanders with simpler constructions, and a deeper understanding of which pseudorandom properties truly require the full Ramanujan bound versus mere constant spectral gap.
