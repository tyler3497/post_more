# The Geometry of Deep Learning Loss Landscapes: Mode Connectivity, the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis, and Sharpness-Aware Minimization

## Abstract

Why do overparameterized neural networks, optimized on highly non-convex objectives with millions of parameters, generalize at all? This thesis advances the view that the answer lies in the *geometry* of the loss landscape rather than in classical capacity control. We synthesize four complementary strands of evidence: (i) **mode connectivity**, the discovery that independently trained minima are joined by simple low-loss curves and even high-dimensional volumes; (ii) the **lottery ticket hypothesis**, which reframes trainability as a property of initialization within the landscape; (iii) **sharpness-aware minimization**, an optimizer that explicitly seeks flat basins with neighborhood-uniformly low loss; and (iv) **Hessian spectral analysis**, which quantifies local curvature through the eigenvalue bulk and outliers of second-order structure. We derive the sharpness-based generalization bound that motivates SAM, analyze the curve-finding procedures that expose connected valleys, formalize the rewinding dynamics behind winning tickets, and show how the Hessian spectrum unifies flatness with mode geometry. Together, these ideas replace the classical picture of isolated pits in the loss surface with a connected, navigable, and exploitable manifold of solutions.

## 1 Introduction

Modern deep networks routinely contain millions of parameters and are trained by stochastic gradient descent (SGD) on non-convex empirical risk objectives. Classical statistical learning theory predicts that such models should overfit catastrophically; in practice they generalize remarkably well. The resolution of this tension, increasingly, is being sought not in parameter counts but in the *shape* of the loss surface that optimization traverses [7].

The naive geometric picture is familiar: a rugged high-dimensional terrain scattered with isolated local minima, narrow valleys, and saddle points whose number may grow exponentially with depth and width. This picture suggests fragility: different random initializations should land in unrelated pits, and interpolation between solutions should cross high-loss barriers. Over the past decade, a series of results has systematically dismantled this view. Garipov et al. demonstrated that independently trained modes are connected by simple low-loss curves [3]. Frankle and Carbin showed that sparse subnetworks, identifiable only through a full training run, are trainable from special initializations inside the same landscape [2]. Foret et al. turned the *sharpness* of a minimum — how quickly loss rises under perturbation — into an optimization objective with a rigorous generalization bound [1]. And spectral analyses of the Hessian revealed that local curvature decomposes into an uninformative bulk plus a handful of directions that actually matter.

This thesis unifies these threads into a single geometric narrative. We argue that the low-loss region of a deep network's parameter space is best understood as a **connected, structured manifold**: modes sit on a continuous low-loss volume [6], winning tickets occupy basins reachable by rewinding along that volume's history [4], flat minima are its wide, stable neighborhoods [1], and the Hessian spectrum is the local coordinate system in which these claims become quantitative. The practical consequences are real: fast geometric ensembling along connecting curves [3], trainability of sparse networks from rewound initializations [4], and improved generalization from explicitly flat solutions [1].

Our contributions are organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the optimization-theoretic vocabulary. Section 3 presents the three analytical instruments we use — curve finding, iterative magnitude pruning, and sharpness-aware updates with spectral diagnostics — and a reproducible visualization protocol. Section 4 is the deep dive: from mode tunnels to loss-surface simplexes. Section 5 collects empirical results and formal statements. Section 6 confronts the limitations, and Section 7 concludes with open directions.

## 2 Background

We consider supervised learning with training set $\mathcal{S} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^m$ drawn from a distribution $\mathcal{D}$ over $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$. A network $f(\cdot; w)$ parameterized by $w \in \mathbb{R}^d$ defines the empirical loss $L_S(w) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \ell(f(x_i; w), y_i)$ and the population loss $L_{\mathcal{D}}(w) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(f(x;w), y)]$. Training minimizes $L_S$; the quantity of interest is $L_{\mathcal{D}}$.

**Local geometry.** Where $L_S$ is twice differentiable, the local shape around $w$ is captured by the Hessian $H(w) = \nabla^2 L_S(w)$. The second-order Taylor expansion,

$$L_S(w + \Delta w) \approx L_S(w) + \nabla L_S(w)^\top \Delta w + \tfrac{1}{2} \Delta w^\top H(w) \, \Delta w,$$

shows that at a stationary point ($\nabla L_S(w) = 0$), curvature alone governs loss growth. The eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_d$ of $H(w)$ therefore encode *sharpness*: a minimum dominated by large positive eigenvalues is sharp, while one whose spectrum concentrates near zero is flat [7].

**Sharpness and generalization.** Keskar-style $\epsilon$-sharpness measures $\max_{\|\epsilon\|\le\rho} L_S(w+\epsilon) - L_S(w)$, the worst-case loss increase within a Euclidean ball of radius $\rho$. The intuition — broad minima are robust to the train/test mismatch between $L_S$ and $L_{\mathcal{D}}$ — has been remarkably predictive, and SAM converts it into a PAC-style bound [1] rather than a heuristic.

**Key terminology** used throughout this work:

- **Mode**: a local minimizer $w^*$ found by a training run (usually distinct across random seeds).
- **Mode connectivity**: the property that modes $w_1, w_2$ are joined by a path $\phi(t)$, $t \in [0,1]$, with $\phi(0)=w_1$, $\phi(1)=w_2$, along which the loss stays nearly constant [3].
- **Winning ticket**: a sparse subnetwork (mask $m$, initialization $w_0 \odot m$) that trains in isolation to accuracy matching the dense network [2].
- **Barrier**: the maximum loss along the linear interpolation $(1-\alpha) w_1 + \alpha w_2$, minus the endpoint losses; connectivity means barriers are small along *some* (possibly nonlinear) path.

> **Theorem:** (Informal, Foret et al. [1]) For any $\rho > 0$, with high probability over training sets drawn from $\mathcal{D}$,
$$L_{\mathcal{D}}(w) \le \max_{\|\epsilon\|_2 \le \rho} L_S(w+\epsilon) + h\left(\tfrac{\|w\|_2^2}{\rho^2}\right),$$
> where $h$ is strictly increasing. The right-hand side separates into the sharpness term, the training loss, and a norm regularizer.

This bound is the theoretical backbone of Section 3's methodology and Section 5's formal results.

## 3 Methodology

We organize our investigation around three complementary instruments, each probing the landscape at a different scale, plus a spectral diagnostic that bridges them.

**3.1 Curve finding and manifold discovery.** Given two independently trained modes $w_1, w_2$, we search for a connecting curve $\phi_\theta(t)$ parameterized as a polygonal chain with one bend: $\phi(t) = (1-t)^2 w_1 + 2(1-t)t \, \theta + t^2 w_2$ (a quadratic Bézier with learned bend point $\theta$). The bend is trained to minimize the expected loss along the curve, $\mathbb{E}_{t \sim U[0,1]}[L_S(\phi_\theta(t))]$. When the resulting path keeps loss uniformly low, mode connectivity is established [3]. We extend this to *simplicial* volumes: optimize a simplex whose vertices are several modes, verifying that every point in the convex hull maintains low loss [6].

**3.2 Iterative magnitude pruning with rewinding.** Following Frankle and Carbin [2], we (i) train a dense network to convergence, (ii) prune a fraction (typically 20%) of smallest-magnitude weights, (iii) rewind surviving weights to their values at iteration $k$ (the *rewinding point*), and (iv) retrain. Repeating this loop yields a sequence of sparse networks; those matching dense accuracy are winning tickets. The crucial analytical move of [4] is *instability analysis*: training the same initialization twice and measuring whether the two runs converge to linearly connected solutions. Winning tickets, remarkably, are linearly mode-connected across runs while randomly reinitialized sparse networks are not.

**3.3 Sharpness-aware minimization (SAM).** Instead of minimizing $L_S(w)$ directly, SAM minimizes $\max_{\|\epsilon\|_2 \le \rho} L_S(w+\epsilon) + \lambda \|w\|_2^2$, the worst-case loss in a $\rho$-neighborhood [1]. The inner maximization is approximated by a first-order step $\epsilon^* \approx \rho \nabla L_S(w)/\|\nabla L_S(w)\|_2$, yielding a two-step update: ascend to the sharp neighborhood point, then descend using the gradient *there*. This effectively penalizes directions of high curvature and steers optimization toward flat basins.

**3.4 Hessian spectral diagnostics.** We compute the top eigenvalues of $H(w)$ at candidate minima via Lanczos iteration (matrix-vector products from Hessian-vector products, avoiding the full $d \times d$ matrix). Following the spectral analysis program of [7] and related work, we interpret the bulk of the spectrum (many near-zero eigenvalues) as evidence of flat directions and the outlier eigenvalues as the effective dimensionality of sharpness. We correlate $\lambda_{\max}$ with generalization gaps across SAM and SGD solutions.

The following Python snippet implements the standard filter-normalized 1D interpolation test used to detect barriers between modes, a building block of every connectivity experiment in Section 4:

```python
import numpy as np
import torch

def interpolate_and_evaluate(model_a, model_b, dataloader, n_points=21):
    """Linearly interpolate between two modes, evaluating loss along the segment."""
    params_a = torch.nn.utils.parameters_to_vector(model_a.parameters())
    params_b = torch.nn.utils.parameters_to_vector(model_b.parameters())
    losses = []
    for t in np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, n_points):
        w = (1.0 - t) * params_a + t * params_b
        torch.nn.utils.vector_to_parameters(w, model_a.parameters())
        losses.append(eval_loss(model_a, dataloader))
    barrier = max(losses) - max(losses[0], losses[-1])
    return np.linspace(0, 1, n_points), losses, barrier

def filter_normalized_direction(model):
    """Sample a random direction with the same per-filter norm as the weights,
    the protocol that makes landscape visualizations scale-invariant."""
    direction = []
    for p in model.parameters():
        if p.dim() <= 1:
            direction.append(torch.zeros_like(p))
        else:
            d = torch.randn_like(p)
            d.mul_(p.norm() / (d.norm() + 1e-10))
            direction.append(d)
    return torch.nn.utils.parameters_to_vector(direction)
```

Filter normalization is essential: without it, apparent sharpness can be an artifact of arbitrary scale, since rescaling a ReLU network's layers changes curvature without changing the function [7].

## 4 From Tunnels to Volumes: The Connected Geometry of Low-Loss Regions

### 4.1 Mode Connectivity and the One-Bend Curve

The classical experiment is disarmingly simple: train the same architecture twice from different seeds, obtaining modes $w_1$ and $w_2$, and evaluate the loss on the straight segment between them. The loss typically spikes — the *barrier* is large — suggesting the modes are isolated [3]. Yet when Garipov et al. optimized a single bend point $\theta$ to minimize loss along the resulting quadratic curve, the barrier collapsed: a polygonal chain with one bend connected the modes with nearly constant training and test accuracy on CIFAR and ImageNet models [3].

The implications are profound. First, the number of *distinct* basins may be far smaller than the number of *distinct functions*: endpoints differ in prediction space even while their connecting curve stays low-loss, so connectivity does not imply redundancy [7]. Second, connectivity is algorithmically exploitable: **Fast Geometric Ensembling (FGE)** samples checkpoints along a cyclic schedule that wanders through the connecting region, producing an ensemble in the time of a single training run and beating snapshot ensembles on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet [3].

### 4.2 Linear Mode Connectivity and the Stability of Tickets

Linear mode connectivity is the stricter demand that even the *straight* segment between two solutions stays low-loss — no bend allowed. Frankle et al. discovered that this property characterizes the solutions of lottery tickets [4]. Concretely: train a winning ticket twice from its rewound initialization; the two resulting solutions are linearly connected. Train the same sparse mask from a random reinitialization; the solutions are separated by a barrier.

This yields **instability analysis**, a diagnostic for when a subnetwork is "stable" enough to be a ticket [4]. The key parameter is the rewinding iteration $k$: rewinding to $k=0$ works for small networks, but for large-scale models like ResNet-50 on ImageNet, matching tickets require rewinding a few epochs in — *late resetting* — recovering accuracy within a percentage point of dense performance with up to 79% of parameters removed [2]. The geometric reading: early training moves weights into a linearly connected basin, and only then does the sparse mask inherit the connectivity structure of the dense landscape.

| Regime | Rewinding point | Linear connectivity? | Ticket quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small nets (LeNet, small Conv) | $k = 0$ (initialization) | Yes | Winning tickets at 10–20% size [2] |
| ResNet-50 / ImageNet | $k \approx$ a few epochs | Yes after late resetting | Matching at 79% sparsity [2] |
| Random reinitialization | n/a | No (barrier) | Fails to train [4] |
| Dense, two independent runs | n/a | No (needs bend) | Connected nonlinearly [3] |

### 4.3 Loss Surface Simplexes: Multi-Dimensional Volumes

Benton et al. pushed the geometry further: not just curves between pairs of modes, but entire **simplicial complexes of low loss** containing arbitrarily many independently trained modes [6]. Optimizing a $k$-simplex (vertices = modes, interior = convex combinations) on CIFAR and ImageNet shows that the low-loss region has genuine *volume* — it is a high-dimensional manifold, not a web of tunnels. This revises the mental model a third time: from isolated pits, to connected tunnels, to an extended low-loss continent on which SGD's independent runs are merely scattered settlements.

### 4.4 Ensembling Along the Manifold

Connectivity is not just descriptive; it changes how we build ensembles. Deep ensembles — independent runs averaged at prediction time — are the gold standard for uncertainty, and Fort, Hu, and Lakshminarayanan showed that ensemble diversity corresponds to *functionally distinct* points within the connected low-loss region: identical loss does not imply identical functions [7]. FGE [3] and the simplex sampling of [6] collect such diverse points cheaply by traversing the manifold rather than restarting training. The geometry thus converts an expensive procedure (multiple full runs) into a cheap one (a walk through the valley).

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

We now assemble the quantitative record, pairing experimental findings with the formal statements that explain them.

**Result 1 — Lottery tickets exist and are sparse.** Frankle and Carbin consistently found winning tickets at 10–20% of the original network size on MNIST and CIFAR-10 architectures, tickets that trained faster and reached *higher* test accuracy than the dense network; random reinitialization of the same masks destroyed performance, proving the initialization — not merely the architecture — carries the trainability [2].

**Result 2 — SAM improves generalization across benchmarks.** Foret et al. reported consistent gains from SAM over SGD/Adam baselines on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet, with the largest improvements on label-noise benchmarks where flat minima are most protective [1]. The JMLR analysis of SAM's dynamics shows the optimizer "bounces across ravines": on quadratic models it provably descends along the leading Hessian eigenvector direction, aligning with flat directions in a precise, non-asymptotic sense.

> **Theorem:** (SAM generalization bound, Foret et al. [1]) Let the loss be bounded and let $\rho > 0$. Then with probability at least $1-\delta$ over $\mathcal{S} \sim \mathcal{D}^m$,
$$L_{\mathcal{D}}(w) \le \underbrace{\max_{\|\epsilon\|_2\le\rho} L_S(w+\epsilon) - L_S(w)}_{\text{sharpness}} + L_S(w) + h\!\left(\tfrac{\|w\|_2^2}{\rho^2}\right).$$
> The bound certifies that controlling neighborhood-wise loss — exactly what SAM minimizes — controls population risk, replacing the minimum's *point value* with its *local geometry* as the object of optimization.

**Result 3 — Connectivity barriers vanish along learned curves.** Across VGG and ResNet families, one-bend curves reduce the linear-interpolation barrier to near zero on both train and test loss [3]; simplicial volumes extend this to three or more modes simultaneously [6]. Function-space measurements confirm the endpoints remain diverse classifiers — ensembling them helps — even though a low-loss path joins them [7].

**Result 4 — Hessian spectra: bulk plus outliers.** Spectral analyses of trained networks show the Hessian eigenvalue density splitting into a large bulk concentrated near zero (flat directions, the majority of parameter space) and a small number of large positive outliers (sharp directions that dominate the generalization gap) [7]. SAM solutions systematically shift mass from the outliers into the bulk relative to SGD solutions at the same training loss — the spectral signature of the flat minima that the bound in [1] predicts will generalize.

**Result 5 — The linear theory of tickets.** For linear models, iterative magnitude pruning provably identifies trainable sparse subnetworks, and the dynamics can be analyzed in closed form [8]; the nonlinear lottery ticket phenomenon inherits this structure but adds the rewinding requirement, whose geometric correlate is exactly the onset of linear mode connectivity [4].

Taken together, these results support a unified claim: *the low-loss region is a connected, mostly-flat manifold; tickets are its stable sparse coordinates; SAM is its principled navigation rule; and the Hessian spectrum is its local chart.*

## 6 Limitations

Several caveats temper this synthesis. First, sharpness is **scale-sensitive**: naive $\epsilon$-sharpness can be manipulated by rescaling layers of ReLU networks without changing the function, so filter normalization or scale-invariant definitions are mandatory [7]; SAM's empirical success partly reflects its interaction with normalization layers rather than pure geometry. Second, SAM **doubles the gradient cost** (ascent plus descent step), and its first-order approximation of the inner maximum can be inaccurate when the landscape curves sharply within radius $\rho$ — explicit probing variants attempt to fix this at further cost. Third, the lottery ticket pipeline is **prohibitively expensive** as a discovery procedure: finding a ticket requires full dense training plus multiple prune-retrain cycles, so tickets explain trainability more than they yet deliver efficiency [2][4]. Fourth, mode connectivity results are concentrated on **image classification** with convolutional and residual architectures; whether transformers and language-model landscapes share the same connected-volume structure is far less settled. Finally, Hessian spectral claims rest on **Lanczos approximations** of an intractably large matrix, and the bulk-outlier picture can shift with batch size, batch normalization, and the choice of loss.

## 7 Conclusion

The loss landscapes of deep networks are far more hospitable than their non-convexity suggests. Modes that appear isolated are joined by low-loss curves [3] and even volumetric simplices [6]; sparse winning tickets inherit linear connectivity once rewound past the instability of initialization [4]; sharpness-aware minimization converts flatness from an observed correlate of generalization into an optimized objective with a rigorous bound [1]; and the Hessian spectrum provides the local coordinates — bulk and outliers — in which flatness becomes measurable [7].

The practical program this enables is clear: navigate the manifold rather than restart from scratch. Ensembles can be harvested along connecting curves in single-run time [3][7]; sparse trainable networks can be found by exploiting the basin structure that rewinding reveals [2][4]; and optimizers can be designed to target neighborhoods, not points [1]. The open frontier is to extend this geometric understanding beyond vision models — to transformers, to reinforcement learning, and to the landscapes of fine-tuning — and to develop scale-invariant, computationally cheap diagnostics that make the manifold's structure a routine engineering tool rather than a research discovery.
