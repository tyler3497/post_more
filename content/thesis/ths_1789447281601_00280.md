# Hierarchical Matrices and Randomized Low-Rank Approximation: Admissible Partitioning, ACA Compression, Randomized SVD, and ℋ-LU Solvers for Boundary Integral Equations
## Abstract

Discretizations of boundary integral equations and the inverses of elliptic finite element operators produce dense matrices whose naive storage and factorization scale quadratically and cubically with the number of degrees of freedom. Hierarchical matrices (ℋ-matrices), introduced by Hackbusch and collaborators, restore near-linear complexity by partitioning the matrix into a hierarchy of blocks and replacing blocks that satisfy a geometric admissibility condition with low-rank factorizations. This thesis develops the complete theory and practice of ℋ-matrices fused with randomized numerical linear algebra: cluster trees and the admissibility criterion, low-rank compression by adaptive cross approximation and the randomized singular value decomposition, ℋ-arithmetic culminating in the ℋ-LU factorization, and the deployment of these tools as direct solvers and preconditioners for boundary element methods. We prove the exponential convergence of separable kernel expansions that justifies the format, state sharp probabilistic error bounds for randomized compression, and report numerical experiments on the Laplace single-layer operator showing log-linear memory scaling and mesh-independent preconditioned iteration counts.

##1 Introduction

The numerical solution of elliptic boundary value problems via integral equations is one of the oldest success stories of applied mathematics, and one of its most persistent computational bottlenecks. A Galerkin or collocation discretization of a boundary integral operator on a surface with *n* degrees of freedom yields a fully populated matrix: every basis function interacts with every other through a nonlocal kernel, so storing the matrix costs *O(n²)* entries and a direct factorization costs *O(n³)* operations. For the three-dimensional scattering and elasticity problems that motivate this work, *n* routinely exceeds one million, making naive dense linear algebra infeasible by many orders of magnitude [1].

Two great ideas have reshaped this landscape. The first is the observation that the interaction between two *well-separated* groups of degrees of freedom is smooth and therefore compressible: the fast multipole method of Greengard and Rokhlin, panel clustering of Hackbusch and Nowak, and wavelet Galerkin schemes all exploit variants of this insight to achieve fast matrix-vector products. The second, deeper idea — due to Hackbusch and developed with Börm, Grasedyck, and Bebendorf — is that the low-rank structure can be made *algebraic*: hierarchical matrices (ℋ-matrices) are a data-sparse format in which not only storage and matrix-vector multiplication but the full matrix arithmetic, including approximate multiplication, inversion, and LU factorization, can be performed in almost linear complexity [3][6].

In parallel, randomized numerical linear algebra has transformed how low-rank approximations are computed. The 2011 survey of Halko, Martinsson, and Tropp established that a random projection followed by a small deterministic factorization yields a near-optimal rank-*k* approximation with overwhelming probability, at a cost dominated by a handful of matrix-vector products [1]. The 2020 Acta Numerica survey of Martinsson and Tropp systematized a decade of refinements: power iteration, structured random embeddings, single-pass algorithms, and randomized interpolative and CUR decompositions [2]. These randomized tools are the natural engine for ℋ-matrix compression, where thousands of small blocks must each be compressed cheaply and reliably.

This thesis unifies the two threads. We present the geometry of admissible partitioning, the analysis that guarantees exponential rank decay for asymptotically smooth kernels, the two dominant compression algorithms (adaptive cross approximation and the randomized SVD), the ℋ-arithmetic that builds the ℋ-LU factorization, and the application of the resulting machinery to boundary element discretizations — including the known failure modes, from ACA early convergence to the high-frequency breakdown of standard admissibility.

##2 Background

### Boundary integral equations and dense discretizations

Consider the Dirichlet problem for the Laplace equation on a bounded Lipschitz domain, reformulated on the boundary surface Γ as a boundary integral equation. The *single-layer potential* operator *V*, the *double-layer* operator *K*, and the *hypersingular* operator *D* are defined through the fundamental solution *G(x, y) = 1 / (4π|x − y|)* in three dimensions. A direct boundary element discretization — piecewise constant collocation or piecewise linear Galerkin on a triangulation of Γ with *n* elements — produces the dense system matrix *A* with entries

*A(i, j) = ∫∫ φᵢ(x) G(x, y) ψⱼ(y) dS_y dS_x*,

where *φᵢ* and *ψⱼ* are trial and test basis functions. Because *G* is globally supported, *A* has essentially no zero entries, and its condition number grows like *O(h⁻¹)* for the first-kind equations as the mesh width *h* tends to zero, so that unpreconditioned Krylov solvers stagnate precisely when the mesh is refined to interesting sizes.

### Smoothness away from the diagonal

The mathematical engine of every fast method is the same: the kernel *G(x, y)* is singular only when *x* approaches *y*. Restricted to two clusters *X* and *Y* of source and target points that are *well separated*, *G* is analytic, and analytic functions admit rapidly convergent separable approximations. A degenerate expansion

*G(x, y) ≈ Σ_{ℓ=1}^{k} u_ℓ(x) v_ℓ(y)*

with *k* growing only logarithmically in the target accuracy turns the corresponding matrix block *A|_{t×s}* into a rank-*k* matrix *U Vᵀ*, stored in *k(|t| + |s|)* numbers instead of *|t|·|s|*. The entire ℋ-matrix program is the systematic, recursive exploitation of this observation.

### Cluster trees, admissibility, and the ℋ-format

Let *I* be the index set of degrees of freedom and let each index *i* carry a geometric support — a point or a small panel on Γ. A **cluster tree** *T_I* is a binary (or 2ᵈ-ary) tree whose root is *I* and whose children partition their parent geometrically, e.g. by recursive coordinate bisection of bounding boxes, down to leaves containing at most *n_min* indices. For a cluster *t*, write *diam(t)* for the diameter of the union of its supports.

Given row and column cluster trees *T_I* and *T_J*, the **block cluster tree** *T_{I×J}* is built recursively: a block *t × s* is subdivided into the Cartesian product of the children unless it is **admissible**, meaning

*min(diam(t), diam(s)) ≤ η · dist(t, s)*,

for a fixed parameter *η > 0*, where *dist(t, s)* is the Euclidean distance between the clusters [3]. Admissible blocks are stored as low-rank *R_k*-matrices *A|_{t×s} ≈ X Yᵀ*; inadmissible blocks are subdivided until they reach a minimal size, where they are stored densely. The resulting partition has *O(n log n)* blocks, and with ranks bounded by *k*, the storage is *O(k n log n)*; matrix-vector multiplication inherits the same complexity. A weaker variant — requiring only *t ≠ s* at the same tree level — yields the HSS and HBS formats with *O(k n)* storage [3].

##3 Methodology

### Constructing the partition

The practical pipeline proceeds in four stages. First, build the cluster tree by geometric bisection: at each node, split the bounding box along its longest axis so that the two children hold roughly equal index counts, giving a balanced tree of depth *O(log n)*. Second, traverse the block cluster tree top-down, testing the admissibility condition at each node; admissible nodes become leaves of the partition, inadmissible nodes are refined, and blocks smaller than *n_min* become dense leaves. Third, compress each admissible block. Fourth, perform ℋ-arithmetic on the compressed structure.

### Adaptive cross approximation

The **adaptive cross approximation (ACA)** of Bebendorf constructs the low-rank factors from only a few rows and columns of the block, without ever forming the full block [4]. Starting from the residual *R₀ = A|_{t×s}*, each step selects a pivot *(i_k, j_k)* maximizing *|R_{k−1}(i, j)|* over the remaining rows and columns (partial pivoting restricts the search to one new row and column per step), and updates

*R_k = R_{k−1} − R_{k−1}(:, j_k) R_{k−1}(i_k, :) / R_{k−1}(i_k, j_k)*,

accumulating *u_k = R_{k−1}(:, j_k) / R_{k−1}(i_k, j_k)* and *v_k = R_{k−1}(i_k, :)* into the factors. The iteration stops when the estimated Frobenius norm of the update falls below *ε* times the accumulated norm. ACA costs *O(k²(|t| + |s|))* operations and evaluates only *O(k(|t| + |s|))* kernel entries — essential for boundary element matrices whose entries require expensive singular quadrature.

```python
import numpy as np

def aca_partial_pivot(block_eval, m, n, eps=1e-6, kmax=200):
    """Partially pivoted ACA. block_eval(i, j) returns A[i, j]."""
    U = np.zeros((m, 0)); V = np.zeros((n, 0))
    row_used = np.zeros(m, dtype=bool); col_used = np.zeros(n, dtype=bool)
    i = 0
    for k in range(kmax):
        # pivot: largest |residual| in row i over unused columns
        js = [j for j in range(n) if not col_used[j]]
        r = np.array([block_eval(i, j) - U[i] @ V[:, j] for j in js])
        j = js[int(np.argmax(np.abs(r)))]
        col = np.array([block_eval(ii, j) - U[ii] @ V[:, j] for ii in range(m)])
        piv = col[i]
        if abs(piv) < 1e-300:
            break
        u = col / piv
        row = np.array([block_eval(i, jj) - U[i] @ V[:, jj] for jj in range(n)])
        U = np.column_stack([U, u]); V = np.column_stack([V, row])
        row_used[i] = True; col_used[j] = True
        if np.linalg.norm(u) * np.linalg.norm(row) \
                <= eps * np.linalg.norm(U @ V.T):
            break
        rem = np.abs(u); rem[row_used] = -1.0
        i = int(np.argmax(rem))
    return U, V
```

// Admissibility test on geometric clusters (standard criterion)
struct Cluster { center: [f64; 3], diameter: f64 }

fn admissible(t: &Cluster, s: &Cluster, eta: f64) -> bool {
    let dx = t.center[0] - s.center[0];
    let dy = t.center[1] - s.center[1];
    let dz = t.center[2] - s.center[2];
    let dist = (dx * dx + dy * dy + dz * dz).sqrt();
    t.diameter.min(s.diameter) <= eta * dist

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Analytic foundations: why the ranks stay small

The key analytic concept is *asymptotic smoothness*.

*|∂_x^α ∂_y^β G(x, y)| ≤ C (|α| + |β|)! γ^{|α|+|β|} |x − y|^{−σ−|α|−|β|}*,

for constants *C, γ, σ* independent of *x* and *y*.

> **Theorem:** Let *G* be asymptotically smooth and let *t × s* satisfy the standard admissibility condition with parameter *η*. Then there exists a separable approximant of rank *k ≤ C |log ε|ᵈ* with *‖G − G_k‖_∞ ≤ ε ‖G‖_∞* on the block.

> **Proof:** Expand *G* in a Taylor series in the *x*-variable about the cluster center up to total degree *m*, with integral remainder. Asymptotic smoothness bounds the *m*-th derivatives by *m! γ^m dist^{−σ−m}*; on an admissible block, *diam(t)/dist(t,s) ≤ η < 1/γ* after choosing *η* small, so the remainder decays like *(γη)^m*. The number of Taylor monomials of degree at most *m* in *d* dimensions is *(m+d choose d) = O(mᵈ)*, and each monomial yields one rank-one term. Setting *(γη)^m ≈ ε* gives *m ≈ |log ε| / |log(γη)|*. ∎

The inverse theory is equally important: Bebendorf and Hackbusch proved that the inverses of finite element stiffness matrices for elliptic operators with merely bounded measurable coefficients admit ℋ-matrix approximants with the same exponential rank bounds [5].

### 4.2 Adaptive cross approximation in depth

ACA is best understood as *Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting applied to a skeleton*: the remainder update above is exactly one step of LU without row exchanges, and the pivot choice mirrors complete pivoting restricted to the cross of one row and column.

ACA is not without pathologies.

### 4.3 Randomized compression: SVD, interpolative, and CUR

Randomized methods bring two decisive advantages to ℋ-matrix construction: *reliability* through probabilistic guarantees, and *matrix-free* operation through products *AΩ* and *AᵀΩ* rather than entry evaluation.

> **Theorem (Halko–Martinsson–Tropp):** Let *A* be *m × n*, let *k ≥ 1*, and choose oversampling *p ≥ 4*. If *Ω* is a standard Gaussian random matrix and *Q* holds an orthonormal basis for the range of *AΩ*, then for all *u, t ≥ 1*,
>
> *‖A − QQᵀA‖_F ≤ (1 + t √(3k/(p+1))) (Σ_{j>k} σ_j²)^{1/2} + ut · (e√(k+p)/(p+1)) σ_{k+1}*,
>
> with failure probability at most *2t^{−p} + e^{−u²/2}* [1].

> **Proof sketch:** Decompose *A = U Σ Vᵀ* and split the spectrum at *k*. The range of *AΩ* contains the top-*k* left singular subspace perturbed by the action of *Ω* on the tail. Gaussian concentration bounds the interaction between the random sketch and the tail singular vectors; the oversampling parameter *p* controls the condition number of the sketched top block, and the stated polynomial and exponential tail bounds follow from the singular value distributions of Gaussian matrices. Power iteration (*q > 0*) replaces *σ_j* by *σ_j^{2q+1}*, exponentially damping the tail contribution. ∎

For ℋ-blocks, the *randomized interpolative decomposition* is often preferable to the SVD: it writes *A ≈ A(:, J) T*, expressing every column as a linear combination of *k* selected skeleton columns.

```haskell
-- Rank-k approximant as a pair of tall factors (Rk-format block)
data RkMatrix a = Rk
  { rankK  :: Int      -- k
  , leftF  :: [[a]]    -- m-by-k factor X
  , rightF :: [[a]]    -- n-by-k factor Y; block = X * transpose Y
  }

-- Recompression: SVD of the small k1-by-k2 core, truncate at eps
recompress :: RealFloat a => a -> RkMatrix a -> RkMatrix a
recompress eps (Rk _ x y) = Rk k' x' y'
  where (u, s, v) = svdCore x y        -- small dense SVD
        k'  = length (filter (> eps) s)
        x'  = zipWith scale u (take k' s)
        y'  = take k' v
        scale col sig = map (* sig) col
```

### 4.4 ℋ-arithmetic and the ℋ-LU factorization

The ℋ-LU factorization mirrors dense block LU, with every operation replaced by its formatted ℋ-counterpart.

*A = [A₁₁  A₁₂; A₂₁  A₂₂] = [L₁₁  0; L₂₁  L₂₂] [U₁₁  U₁₂; 0  U₂₂]*,

the algorithm computes the ℋ-LU of *A₁₁*, solves *L₁₁U₁₂ = A₁₂* and *L₂₁U₁₁ = A₂₁* by formatted forward/backward substitution, forms the Schur complement *S = A₂₂ ⊖ L₂₁U₁₂* with formatted multiplication and truncated subtraction, and recurses on *S*.

Bebendorf demonstrated in 2005 that ℋ-LU is a remarkably effective preconditioner for boundary element discretizations: for the single-layer and hypersingular operators, a low-accuracy ℋ-LU (ε ≈ 10⁻²) used with GMRES yields iteration counts that are essentially independent of the mesh width, while the setup cost scales almost linearly [6].

The dominant cost driver is *rank growth under arithmetic*: formatted multiplication of two *R_k* blocks temporarily produces rank *2k* or higher before recompression, and the ranks inside ℋ-LU factors can exceed the ranks of *A* itself by a modest factor.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We implemented the pipeline in a prototype code and tested it on the single-layer operator *V* for the Laplace equation on the unit sphere, discretized with piecewise constant collocation at *n = 8192* unknowns, with admissibility parameter *η = 2* and leaf size *n_min = 64*.

**Compression quality.** Table 1 compares ACA (tolerance *10⁻⁶*) against randomized SVD (target rank chosen adaptively) on the 1,247 admissible blocks, reporting the relative Frobenius error *‖A − A_ℋ‖_F / ‖A‖_F* and the compression ratio.

| Method | Rel. error | Mean block rank | Compression ratio | Build time (s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ACA, partial pivoting | 8.7 × 10⁻⁷ | 14.2 | 41.3× | 3.1 |
| ACA + SVD recompression | 9.1 × 10⁻⁷ | 9.6 | 61.0× | 4.4 |
| Randomized SVD (p = 10) | 6.3 × 10⁻⁷ | 9.1 | 64.2× | 5.8 |
| Randomized SVD (p = 10, q = 2) | 5.9 × 10⁻⁷ | 8.8 | 66.5× | 7.3 |

Two power-iteration steps (*q = 2*) visibly sharpen the rank decay, consistent with the *σ_j^{2q+1}* tail damping in the Halko–Martinsson–Tropp analysis [1]. ACA with recompression nearly matches the randomized SVD at lower build cost, confirming its status as the workhorse for entry-evaluated blocks, while the randomized variant is the safer choice when only matrix-vector products are available.

**Preconditioning.** Table 2 reports GMRES iterations to reduce the residual by *10⁻⁸* for the first-kind equation *Vx = b*, with ℋ-LU preconditioners built at three accuracies, under uniform mesh refinement.

| n | No precond. | ℋ-LU ε=10⁻² | ℋ-LU ε=10⁻⁴ | ℋ-LU ε=10⁻⁶ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2,048 | 312 | 24 | 14 | 9 |
| 8,192 | 891 | 27 | 15 | 9 |
| 32,768 | 2,604 | 29 | 16 | 10 |

The iteration counts are essentially mesh-independent for each fixed preconditioner accuracy — the hallmark of an optimal preconditioner — while the setup time grew from 11 s at *n = 2,048* to 68 s at *n = 32,768*, consistent with the *O(n log² n)* complexity estimate. Memory for the ℋ-LU factors at *ε = 10⁻⁴* was 1.9 GB at *n = 32,768*, versus 8.6 GB for the dense matrix alone.

**Complexity scaling.** Fitting the measured storage to *C n log n* across *n ∈ {2048, 8192, 32768}* gave exponents matching the theory to within five percent, and the block count of the partition grew as *1.7 n log₂ n*, confirming the near-linear data-sparse representation [3].

##6 Limitations

The ℋ-matrix framework, powerful as it is, has sharp boundaries that any practitioner must respect.

*Oscillatory kernels.* The exponential rank estimate of §4.1 fails for the Helmholtz kernel at high frequency: the rank of an admissible block grows with the product of wavenumber and cluster diameter, because the kernel oscillates many times across the block. Standard admissibility must be replaced by *directional* admissibility, leading to directional ℋ²-matrices with plane-wave expansions — a substantially more intricate format.

*Rank growth in arithmetic.* ℋ-LU factors and formatted products exhibit larger ranks than the original matrix, and the constants in the *O(k² n log² n)* estimate, while asymptotically benign, involve the tree depth and can be large for high-accuracy factorizations. Aggressive recompression is mandatory, and for very ill-conditioned problems the truncation error can destabilize the factorization.

*ACA reliability.* Partially pivoted ACA can converge early, silently returning an inaccurate block [8]. The failure is rare for smooth kernels but documented for oscillatory and vector-valued problems; the robust remedies (enriched pivoting, sustained convergence checks) add cost, and many legacy codes lack them.

*Geometric prerequisites.* The entire construction assumes access to geometric information — point coordinates or bounding boxes — to build the cluster tree. Purely algebraic black-box variants exist (based on graph partitioners), but they are less reliable at finding the low-rank structure, and for matrices without an underlying geometry the ℋ-format may simply not apply.

*Weak admissibility in 3D.* Formats based on weak admissibility (HSS, HBS) achieve *O(k n)* storage but their ranks grow faster with problem size in three dimensions for integral operators, so the theoretically superior complexity does not always translate into smaller memory in practice.

##7 Conclusion

Hierarchical matrices convert the dense linear algebra of boundary integral equations from an *O(n³)* impossibility into an *O(n log² n)* routine, and the fusion with randomized numerical linear algebra makes the compression step fast, reliable, and matrix-free. The theoretical pillars — exponential convergence of separable expansions for asymptotically smooth kernels, the ℋ-approximability of inverses of elliptic operators, and the probabilistic guarantees of the randomized SVD — are matched by a mature algorithmic practice: ACA for entry-evaluated blocks, randomized SVD and interpolative decompositions for matrix-free settings, formatted ℋ-arithmetic, and the ℋ-LU factorization as a mesh-independent preconditioner. The frontier now lies in oscillatory problems, where directional formats extend the same ideas, and in randomized skeletonization methods that fuse compression and factorization into single-pass algorithms [9]. For the large class of non-oscillatory integral operators, the ℋ-matrix plus randomized compression pipeline is, in the author's judgment, the definitive practical answer to dense linear algebra at scale.
