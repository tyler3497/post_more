# Modern Garbage Collection: Concurrent Marking, Colored Pointers, and Region-Based Heaps

## Abstract

Managed-language runtimes now serve latency-sensitive workloads on multi-terabyte heaps where stop-the-world pauses of even tens of milliseconds violate service-level objectives. This thesis surveys and formalizes the four architectural families that dominate modern automatic memory management: *G1's* region-based concurrent marking with snapshot-at-the-beginning write barriers and remembered sets, *ZGC's* colored pointers with self-healing load barriers, *Shenandoah's* Brooks-pointer concurrent evacuation, and *Immix's* mark-region line-granular collection. We derive the tri-color invariants underlying each barrier design, prove termination and soundness of snapshot-at-the-beginning marking, and analyze pause-time complexity — showing how the field converged from O(heap)-pause evacuation to O(root-set) pause bounds. Empirical results from the SPECjbb2015 and Renaissance suites demonstrate sub-millisecond maximum pauses on 16 TB heaps for ZGC and single-digit-millisecond pauses for Shenandoah, while region-based generational evacuation in G1 sustains high throughput with configurable soft real-time targets. We close with open problems in barrier amortization, NUMA-aware placement, and formally verified collectors.

## 1 Introduction

Automatic memory management has transitioned, over five decades, from a throughput-oriented batch concern into a *latency-engineering* discipline. Early tracing collectors [4] accepted stop-the-world (STW) pauses linear in heap size because heaps measured in megabytes made such pauses tolerable. Contemporary heaps, by contrast, span terabytes: a single STW full-heap traversal at even optimistic memory bandwidths (50–100 GB/s) costs tens of seconds — several orders of magnitude beyond acceptable pause budgets for trading systems, web tiers, and interactive applications, which commonly demand 99.99th-percentile pauses under 10 ms [1,7].

Three intertwined innovations made modern collectors possible. *First*, **concurrent marking** decouples the mark phase from mutator execution, shrinking STW work to root scanning and phase-boundary synchronization. *Second*, **barrier technology** — software instrumentation of heap reads and writes — preserves collector invariants while mutators run: pre-write barriers implement Yuasa's snapshot-at-the-beginning (SATB) discipline [3], post-write barriers implement Dijkstra's incremental-update discipline [2], and load barriers implement self-healing forwarding for concurrent relocation [7,8]. *Third*, **region-based heap organization** [1] replaces monolithic generations with hundreds of uniformly sized regions, enabling collection-set selection driven by a garbage-first heuristic: evacuate the regions containing the most garbage per unit of copy cost.

This thesis unifies these threads. We formalize the invariants (Section 3), dissect four production systems — G1 (Section 4.1), ZGC (Section 4.2), Shenandoah (Section 4.3), and Immix (Section 4.4) — evaluate empirical results and proof sketches (Section 5), enumerate limitations (Section 6), and conclude with research directions (Section 7).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Tri-Color Abstraction

Dijkstra et al. [2] introduced the tri-color abstraction that underpins virtually all concurrent tracing collectors. Objects are *white* (unvisited, presumed dead), *grey* (visited but with unscanned outgoing references), or *black* (visited and fully scanned). Collection proceeds by greying roots and repeatedly greying the white successors of grey objects until no grey objects remain; all remaining white objects are unreachable and may be reclaimed. Correctness rests on a single invariant:

> **Theorem (Weak Tri-Color Invariant):** *At no point during concurrent marking may a mutator create a reference from a black object to a white object without the white object being (transitively) reachable from some grey object. Equivalently, every path from a black object to a white object passes through a grey object.*

If a mutator violates the invariant — storing a white object's address into an already-scanned black object — the object can be "hidden" from the marker and freed while live. Barriers exist precisely to prevent this [2,3].

### 2.2 Snapshot-at-the-Beginning vs. Incremental Update

Two classical disciplines enforce the invariant under mutation. *Snapshot-at-the-beginning* (SATB), due to Yuasa [3], treats the logical heap as frozen at mark-start: a **pre-write barrier** logs the *overwritten* referent before each reference store, so any object reachable at cycle start is marked even if subsequently unlinked. SATB over-marks (floating garbage) but never under-marks. *Incremental update* (IU), from Dijkstra et al. [2], uses a **post-write barrier** that greys the *newly stored* referent after each store; combined with a final root rescan, every new black→white edge created during marking is caught. Table 1 contrasts the two.

| Property | SATB (pre-write) | Incremental Update (post-write) |
|---|---|---|
| Barrier fires on | Reference overwrite, *before* | Reference store, *after* |
| Logged value | Old (overwritten) referent | New referent |
| Correctness shape | Marks superset of start-reachable set | Marks superset of end-reachable set |
| Floating garbage | Yes (objects dying mid-cycle) | Less; new objects born marked |
| Cost center | Barrier on every ref-store + log buffers | Barrier on every ref-store + rescans |
| Used by | G1, Shenandoah (default mode) | CMS (historical), Shenandoah (IU mode) |

### 2.3 Generational Hypothesis and Region Heaps

Appel [12] and the generational collectors that followed exploit the *weak generational hypothesis*: most objects die young, so concentrating effort on a small young generation is efficient. Classical designs partition the heap into contiguous eden/survivor/old spaces. Region-based designs [1] generalize this: the heap is divided into *N* fixed-size regions (1–32 MB in G1), each tagged with a *role* — eden, survivor, old, or humongous — that changes over time. Collection then operates on a *collection set* (CSet): a subset of regions evacuated during a pause. This decouples pause time from heap size and enables the garbage-first policy: rank regions by expected reclamation yield and evacuate the most profitable first.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis follows a comparative-analytic methodology:

1. **Invariant-first analysis.** For each collector we state the preserved invariant, identify the barrier(s) that enforce it, and sketch the soundness argument (no live object is ever reclaimed; Section 5).
2. **Complexity accounting.** We derive asymptotic pause bounds in terms of heap size *H*, live set *L*, root set *R*, and collection-set size *C*, following the cost models of [1] and [7].
3. **Empirical grounding.** We report published measurements from SPECjbb2015 (max-jOPS/critical-jOPS), Renaissance, and vendor technical evaluations for G1, ZGC, and Shenandoah, and cite the original systems papers for Immix [5], G1 [1], and Shenandoah [6].
4. **Source discipline.** Every factual claim about a production system is traceable to a primary source: the original papers [1,2,3,4,5,6], the OpenJDK JEPs [7,8,9], or HotSpot engineering documentation [10,11].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 G1: Concurrent SATB Marking and Remembered Sets

The Garbage-First collector [1], production default in OpenJDK since Java 9, is the canonical *region-based, generational, concurrent-marking* collector. Its heap is a grid of regions; a concurrent marking cycle interleaves with mutator execution across seven observable phases: initial-mark (piggybacked on a young evacuation pause), root-region scanning, concurrent marking, remark (STW), cleanup (partially STW), and concurrent cleanup [11].

#### SATB concurrent marking

G1's marker implements SATB via a **pre-write barrier** compiled into every reference store:

```python
def satb_write_barrier(field, new_value, thread):
    old_value = field.load()          # read the value about to be overwritten
    if marking_active and old_value is not None:
        thread.satb_queue.enqueue(old_value)   # log the overwritten referent
        if thread.satb_queue.is_full():
            flush_to_global_satb_set(thread.satb_queue)
    field.store(new_value)
```

The barrier logs only non-null overwritten values; initializing stores to null fields are filtered cheaply, which matters because most stores in practice are initializations [1]. Marking threads drain the global SATB buffer set while traversing the heap with per-task local mark stacks and work stealing; a global "finger" plus overflow-restart logic guarantees termination [11]. The *remark* pause drains residual buffers and rescans thread stacks, finalizing a marking that is provably a superset of the objects reachable at cycle start.

> **Theorem (SATB Soundness):** *If every reference overwrite during concurrent marking logs its previous referent and the marker transitively marks all logged objects, then every object reachable at mark-start is marked at mark-end.*

*Proof sketch.* Consider any object *o* reachable at mark-start via path *r → … → o*. If no edge on the path is overwritten during marking, the marker's traversal reaches *o*. Otherwise, let *e = (u, v)* be the first overwritten edge on the path, overwritten at time *t*. The pre-write barrier at *t* logs *v*; the marker transitively marks *v* and, by induction on the suffix, all of *o*'s predecessors downstream of *v*… ∎ (Full treatment in [3]; G1's variant with TAMS — top-at-mark-start — region watermarks is detailed in [11].)

#### Remembered sets

To evacuate a single old region without scanning the whole heap, G1 must know all *cross-region references* into it — the region's **remembered set** (RSet). A **post-write barrier** dirties cards (512-byte granules) on reference stores; concurrent refinement threads process dirty cards and record precise `(region, card)` entries into per-region RSets stored as sparse/coarse/fine hash structures [1]. During evacuation pauses, *Update RS* drains pending refinement and *Scan RS* treats RSet entries as additional roots [10]. RSet maintenance is frequently the dominant pause cost, which is why region sizing (`-XX:G1HeapRegionSize`) and the `G1RSetUpdatingPauseTimePercent` throttle exist [10].

Collection-set selection then applies the garbage-first policy: after marking, regions are ranked by liveness; young regions plus the highest-yield old regions form the CSet, bounded by the soft pause target (`-XX:MaxGCPauseMillis`, default 200 ms). G1 thus converts the classic generational design into a *scheduling* problem — which regions to evacuate *now* — rather than a fixed spatial policy.

### 4.2 ZGC: Colored Pointers and Load Barriers

ZGC [7] is a *concurrent, region-based, compacting* collector targeting sub-millisecond maximum pauses on heaps from 8 MB to 16 TB, production-ready since JDK 15 [7] and generational since JDK 21 [8]. Its two defining mechanisms are **colored pointers** and **load barriers**.

#### Colored pointer encoding

On 64-bit platforms, ZGC steals high pointer bits for GC metadata. On Linux/x86_64, bits 42–45 carry four colors — `Marked0`, `Marked1`, `Remapped`, `Finalizable` — while bits 0–41 address 4 TB of heap (the 16 TB ceiling is addressed through multi-mapped virtual reservations) [7]:

```rust
// ZGC colored pointer bit layout (Linux/x86_64), illustrative
const ADDR_MASK: u64   = 0x0000_03FF_FFFF_FFFF; // bits  0..=41: object address
const MARKED0:  u64    = 1 << 42;               // color bit: marked in phase 0
const MARKED1:  u64    = 1 << 43;               // color bit: marked in phase 1
const REMAPPED: u64    = 1 << 44;               // color bit: forwarding complete
const FINALIZABLE: u64 = 1 << 45;               // referent needs finalization

fn strip_color(ptr: u64) -> u64 { ptr & ADDR_MASK }
fn color_of(ptr: u64) -> u64    { ptr & !ADDR_MASK }
```

The *current global phase* defines the "good color"; any pointer whose color disagrees is *bad* — it denotes an object not yet processed in this phase [7].

#### The load barrier and self-healing

Every heap reference *load* passes through a JIT-injected **load barrier**:

```python
def zgc_load_barrier(field) -> object:
    ptr = field.load_raw()                 # colored pointer
    if color_of(ptr) == GOOD_COLOR:        # fast path: already processed
        return deref(strip_color(ptr))
    return slow_path(field, ptr)           # mark / relocate / remap, then heal
```

The slow path's behavior depends on phase: during concurrent mark it marks the referent (setting the good color); during concurrent relocate it checks the *relocation set*, forwards the object if needed, and installs the remapped color [7]. Crucially, the slow path then **self-heals**: it writes the corrected colored pointer back into the field (`field.store_raw(healed)`), so subsequent loads hit the fast path — an *amortized* barrier cost that decays as the heap converges to the good color. The barrier must be *total*: it applies to loads from the heap, and root scanning plus *colored* stack-slot handling cover the rest; a missed barrier would dereference a stale address after relocation.

ZGC's cycle — *Pause Mark Start → Concurrent Mark → Pause Mark End → Concurrent Prepare for Relocation → Pause Relocate Start → Concurrent Relocate* — keeps every STW pause proportional to root scanning and phase bookkeeping only, i.e. **O(R)** and independent of *H* and *L* [7]. Generational ZGC [8] refines this with a young generation (collected by minor cycles using remembered sets between generations), cutting allocation-path and marking overhead while preserving the pause bound.

### 4.3 Shenandoah: Brooks Pointers and Concurrent Evacuation

Shenandoah [6,9] pursues the same sub-millisecond goal via a different relocation primitive: the **Brooks forwarding pointer** [6]. Every object carries an extra header word pointing to its current location. Evacuation copies objects concurrently; the mutator always dereferences through the forwarding pointer, installed atomically via CAS, so *there is no instant at which a mutator can observe a stale copy*.

> **Theorem (Brooks Indirection Correctness):** *If every mutator access resolves the Brooks pointer before touching object contents, and the forwarding pointer is updated atomically from from-space to to-space copy, then concurrent copying is observationally equivalent to atomic relocation.*

Shenandoah's barriers are correspondingly read-heavy: a **read barrier** resolves the Brooks pointer on loads (maintaining the *to-space invariant* that mutators only ever see to-space copies), while **write barriers** implement SATB or incremental-update marking [6]. Because evacuation is fully concurrent, Shenandoah's pauses are also O(R): brief pauses for initial-mark, final-mark, and region-state flips. The price is *per-access* indirection — one extra dependent load per field access — plus the header word per object. Shenandoah 2.0 heuristics improved pacing and ergonomics, and Generational Shenandoah (JEP 521) adds a young/old split to reduce the concurrent marking load [9].

The ZGC-vs-Shenandoah contrast is instructive: ZGC pays barrier cost *lazily* (only bad-colored pointers take the slow path, then heal), while Shenandoah pays a *uniform* small cost per access. ZGC needs no per-object header word; Shenandoah needs no pointer-bit stealing (a decisive advantage on 32-bit or pointer-authentication platforms).

### 4.4 Immix: Mark-Region Collection

Immix [5] occupies a different point in the design space: it targets *throughput and space efficiency* rather than minimal pauses, and its ideas — line-granular marking with opportunistic copying — influenced later region designs. Immix divides the heap into 32 KB *blocks*, each subdivided into 128-byte *lines*. Allocation is contiguous bump-pointer within free lines (fast, like a nursery); collection is mark-region: instead of marking objects, the collector marks the *lines* containing live objects and reclaims whole unmarked lines. A block with fragmented free lines can be *defragmented* by evacuating its live objects to a fresh block — copying only where fragmentation actually hurts, unlike full-heap compaction [5].

Immix demonstrates a deep principle: *mark granularity is a tunable knob*. Object-granular marks maximize precision; line-granular marks trade a bounded waste factor (at most one line per live object cluster) for dramatically cheaper metadata and cache-friendly sweeps. Its defragmentation policy — copy from the most fragmented blocks first — is the direct ancestor of G1's garbage-first region ranking [1,5].

### 4.5 Generational vs. Region-Based Design Tradeoffs

The field has converged on region-based heaps, but the generational question persists in refined form:

- **Pure generational (Parallel GC, CMS-era):** contiguous spaces, simple promotion, but old-generation collections are monolithic STW events with O(H) pauses — unacceptable at scale.
- **Region-based generational (G1):** regions carry generation roles; young collections evacuate eden/survivor regions while mixed collections add selected old regions. Pause time becomes a *policy choice* (CSet size), but cross-region remembered sets impose a standing tax on every reference store.
- **Region-based non-generational (ZGC pre-21, Shenandoah):** no ages, no remembered sets between generations; instead, load/read barriers plus concurrent relocation bound pauses by O(R). Barrier cost is paid on loads rather than stores, and concurrent marking must trace the whole live set each cycle — more GC CPU, but no generational bookkeeping.
- **Region-based generational low-pause (Generational ZGC [8], Generational Shenandoah):** the emerging synthesis — young-generation minor cycles for the common case (most objects die young), concurrent full cycles for the old generation, barriers on both paths.

The unifying lesson: *region granularity decouples collection scheduling from heap layout*, and once scheduling is free, the remaining design variables are barrier placement (load vs. store), evacuation concurrency (STW, concurrent, or self-healing), and how aggressively the young generation is segregated.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketches

We have sketched SATB soundness (Section 4.1) and Brooks-pointer correctness (Section 4.3). Two further results anchor the field:

> **Theorem (Concurrent Marking Termination):** *G1-style concurrent marking with a global finger, per-task local mark stacks, SATB buffer draining, and overflow-triggered finger reset terminates with all start-reachable objects marked.* The argument: each overflow restart re-greys all marked objects, so progress is monotone in the marked set; the finger advances over a finite heap; SATB buffers are finite [11].

> **Theorem (Baker Real-Time Bound):** *An incremental collector with a bounded mutator allocation rate and a collector that reclaims at least as fast as allocation keeps pauses below a constant determined by the increment quantum* [4]. ZGC and Shenandoah are the modern realizations: their STW increments are root scans bounded by *R*.

### 5.2 Measured pause behavior

| Collector | Max pause (reported) | Heap range | Barrier hot path | Key source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | ~200 ms soft target (configurable) | GB–TB | Pre-write SATB + post-write card | [1,10] |
| ZGC | < 1 ms | 8 MB–16 TB | Load barrier (self-healing) | [7] |
| Generational ZGC | < 1 ms | 8 MB–16 TB | Load + generational store barriers | [8] |
| Shenandoah | single-digit ms | GB–TB | Read (Brooks) + SATB write | [6,9] |
| Immix | STW, throughput-oriented | MB–GB | None (STW mark-region) | [5] |

On SPECjbb2015, ZGC holds *critical-jOPS* (latency-constrained throughput) nearly flat as heap scales, because pauses never enter the latency budget; G1 achieves higher *max-jOPS* (raw throughput) on moderate heaps but its critical-jOPS degrades once mixed-collection pauses exceed the response-time SLA [7]. Shenandoah's published results show < 10 ms pauses on multi-GB heaps with throughput within ~10% of Parallel GC on the Renaissance suite [6,9]. Immix, in [5], outperforms both mark-sweep and semi-space collectors on SPECjvm98/2006-derived workloads while using less memory than copying collection — validating mark-region as a throughput design [5].

### 5.3 Barrier cost accounting

Barrier overhead is the central quantitative question. Measurements across HotSpot show SATB pre-write barriers costing single-digit percent throughput on allocation-heavy workloads, dominated by queue-full flushes rather than the inline check [1,11]. ZGC's load barrier, being self-healing, concentrates cost at phase transitions: SPECjbb2015 runs show steady-state overhead in the low single digits, spiking briefly during relocate phases [7]. Shenandoah's Brooks read barrier is steadier but never zero — the dependent load defeats some compiler optimizations, a cost accepted for its portability (no pointer-bit requirements) [6].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Barrier coverage is total-or-nothing.** A single unbarriered heap access in ZGC or Shenandoah is a soundness bug, not a performance bug. This forces deep JIT integration and complicates Unsafe/native interoperation — a recurring source of real-world defects [7,9].
2. **Concurrent collectors trade throughput for latency.** The GC threads, barrier checks, and floating garbage of concurrent designs cost 5–15% throughput versus Parallel GC on batch workloads [7,10]. There is no free lunch: pause bounds are purchased with CPU.
3. **NUMA and huge heaps remain hard.** ZGC is NUMA-aware in allocation, but cross-node relocation traffic and page-level effects at 16 TB are active engineering concerns [7]. Region placement policies are heuristic, not optimal.
4. **Formal verification lags practice.** The proof sketches in Section 5 are hand arguments. Mechanized verification of a production concurrent collector with weak-memory semantics remains an open grand challenge; existing verified collectors are orders of magnitude simpler than ZGC or Shenandoah.
5. **Generational low-pause designs reintroduce remembered sets.** Generational ZGC [8] pays intergenerational barrier and RSet costs that the original non-generational design avoided — the field is rediscovering, in region form, the bookkeeping it once shed.
6. **Immix-style opportunistic copying has pause spikes.** Defragmentation pauses in Immix are proportional to the evacuated volume; it offers no hard pause bound, limiting its role to throughput-sensitive deployments [5].

## 7 Conclusion

The trajectory from stop-the-world mark-sweep to sub-millisecond concurrent collection is, at its core, a story about *invariants and who enforces them*. Dijkstra's tri-color invariant [2] moved enforcement into write barriers; Yuasa's SATB [3] made those barriers practical for real mutators; region heaps [1] turned collection into a scheduling problem; and colored pointers [7] plus Brooks indirection [6] moved enforcement onto the read path, finally decoupling pause times from heap size entirely.

The emerging consensus — generational, region-based, concurrent, barrier-mediated — is visible in Generational ZGC [8] and Generational Shenandoah: segregate the young for efficiency, relocate the old concurrently for latency, and pay for it all with carefully amortized barriers. Open frontiers include hardware-assisted barriers (memory-protection keys, pointer authentication), verified concurrent collectors, and adaptive policies that learn collection-set schedules online. As heaps continue to grow and latency budgets continue to shrink, the invariant-first methodology of this thesis — state the invariant, identify the barrier, prove the bound — will remain the essential tool for designing the next generation of collectors.
