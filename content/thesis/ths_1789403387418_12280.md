# Fast Radio Bursts in the CHIME/ASKAP Era: Dispersion Tomography, Magnetar Engines, and the Repeater Population

## Abstract

Fast radio bursts (FRBs) are millisecond-duration radio transients whose large dispersion measures place them at cosmological distances, yet their central engines remain contested. This thesis synthesizes the CHIME/FRB and ASKAP eras: the first CHIME catalog of 536 bursts, the second catalog of 4,539 bursts from 3,641 sources, and sub-arcsecond ASKAP localizations associating bursts with hosts ranging from star-forming dwarfs to massive quiescent spirals. We develop the propagation physics of dispersion measure tomography, decompose the observed dispersion measure into Milky Way, cosmic-web, and host components, and review population evidence — Schechter luminosity functions, a repeater fraction of only a few percent, and the fluence distribution slope — that constrains progenitor models. The April 2020 radio burst from the Galactic magnetar SGR 1935+2154 is examined as the empirical bridge between magnetar flares and cosmological FRBs. We conclude that magnetar-class engines plausibly power the repeater population while the apparently one-off population may be heterogeneous, and we identify the decisive future measurements: outrigger localizations, polarization monitoring, and the dispersion-measure–redshift relation as a cosmological probe.

## 1 Introduction

The discovery of the Lorimer burst in archival Parkes data, followed by the identification of a cosmological population by Thornton and collaborators, established fast radio bursts as a new class of astrophysical transient: flashes of radio emission lasting roughly a millisecond, with flux densities measured in janskys, implying brightness temperatures so extreme that only coherent emission processes can explain them [1]. Their dispersion measures (DMs) — the integrated column density of free electrons along the line of sight — exceeded every Galactic model of the ionized interstellar medium by factors of several, demanding an extragalactic origin. For nearly a decade the field advanced one burst at a time, each detection sharpening the mystery rather than resolving it.

Two instruments changed the scale of the enterprise. CHIME, a transit interferometer covering 400–800 MHz with an enormous field of view, detected bursts at nearly one per day, yielding a first catalog of 536 events [1] and a second catalog of 4,539 bursts from 3,641 sources, 981 from 83 repeaters [2]. Meanwhile ASKAP, with voltage-buffer triggering in fly's-eye mode, achieved sub-arcsecond localizations of one-off bursts, tying them to individual host galaxies [10]. Together they moved FRB science from phenomenology to population statistics, from anecdote to tomography.

This thesis is organized around three axes the CHIME/ASKAP era has made quantitative. First, *propagation physics*: dispersion, scattering, and Faraday rotation turn every burst into a probe of cosmic baryons [11]. Second, *burst energetics*: volumetric rates, luminosity functions, and the 2020 Galactic magnetar event constrain the engine class [3][8][9]. Third, *population structure*: repeater versus one-off statistics and host-galaxy diversity test whether a single progenitor class suffices [4][5][6][10].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The dispersion measure and why it matters

A radio pulse traversing cold plasma is delayed by an amount that depends on frequency as the inverse square of frequency:

$$\Delta t \simeq 4.15\ \mathrm{ms}\ \left(\frac{\mathrm{DM}}{\mathrm{pc\ cm^{-3}}}\right) \left(\frac{\nu}{\mathrm{GHz}}\right)^{-2}$$

where the dispersion measure is the electron column density,

$$\mathrm{DM} = \int_0^d n_e(l)\, dl.$$

The observed DM of an FRB is conventionally decomposed into four physically distinct contributions [11]:

| Component | Symbol | Typical scale (pc cm⁻³) | Physical origin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Milky Way disk | DM_MW | 30–80 | Galactic interstellar medium (NE2001/YMW16 models) |
| Milky Way halo | DM_halo | 30–80 | Hot circumgalactic gas of the Galaxy |
| Cosmic web | DM_cosmic | 200–1000 | Diffuse intergalactic medium, scaling with redshift |
| Host galaxy | DM_host | 0–400 | Host interstellar medium and near-source plasma |

For FRB 180924, the first ASKAP-localized one-off, the accounting gives DM_MW ≈ 41, DM_halo ≈ 50, DM_host ≈ 66 (host rest frame), leaving DM_cosmic ≈ 220 against a mean expectation of about 280 at its redshift [11].

> **Theorem (Macquart relation):** For a homogeneous ionized intergalactic medium, the mean cosmic dispersion measure grows approximately linearly with redshift, ⟨DM_cosmic⟩ ≈ 1000 z pc cm⁻³ at low redshift, with a skewed distribution whose low-DM tail reflects sightlines that avoid dense halos. This relation makes localized FRBs probes of the cosmic baryon density [5][11].

### 2.2 Coherent emission and the energy scale

FRB durations of order one millisecond and flux densities of order one jansky at gigaparsec distances imply brightness temperatures above 10³⁵ K, far beyond any incoherent process. The emission must be *coherent*: bunched particles radiating in phase. Population analyses of the CHIME sample, corrected for selection effects, infer a characteristic energy cutoff of E_char = 2.38 × 10⁴¹ erg under a Schechter luminosity function with differential power-law index −1.3, and a volumetric rate of roughly 7.3 × 10⁴ bursts Gpc⁻³ yr⁻¹ above a pivot energy of 10³⁹ erg [3]. These numbers define the target that any engine model must hit: an energy reservoir capable of releasing 10³⁹–10⁴² erg in coherent radio emission within milliseconds, at a cosmic rate exceeding that of most known transients.

### 2.3 Repeaters and the localization breakthrough

The 2016 discovery that FRB 121102 repeats destroyed the simplest cataclysmic models — at least for that source — and enabled interferometric localization [6]. Very Large Array fast-dump observations and European VLBI Network astrometry placed the bursts within milliarcseconds of a compact persistent radio source, coincident with a star-forming region in a low-metallicity dwarf galaxy at redshift z = 0.19273, luminosity distance 972 Mpc [6][7]. The contrast with ASKAP's FRB 180924 — localized to the outskirts, 13,000 light-years from the center, of a massive Milky Way-sized galaxy at z = 0.3214 with modest star formation — could hardly be starker [10].

### 2.4 The Galactic magnetar event

On 28 April 2020, the Galactic magnetar SGR 1935+2154 emitted a millisecond radio burst detected independently by CHIME and STARE2, coincident with a hard X-ray burst — the first FRB-like event from a known Galactic object [8][9]. Follow-up showed this single magnetar produces bursts spanning roughly seven orders of magnitude in apparent energy, with clustered, non-Poissonian timing reminiscent of repeating FRBs [8].

## 3 Methodology

The results reviewed in this thesis rest on three methodological pillars: uniform survey statistics with calibrated selection effects, interferometric localization with host association, and multi-wavelength characterization of burst environments.

**Survey statistics and selection calibration.** CHIME/FRB detects bursts commensally, so exposure and sensitivity vary across the sky. The first catalog calibrated this by injecting millions of synthetic bursts into the detection pipeline, measuring recovery as a function of fluence, DM, scattering, and spectral shape [1]. This yields population statements like the all-sky rate of 818 ± 64 events per sky per day above 5 Jy ms, and the cumulative fluence index α = −1.40 ± 0.11, consistent with −3/2 for a non-evolving Euclidean population [1].

**Population inference.** Forward modeling inverts the calibrated survey: assume a Schechter luminosity function and a trial redshift distribution, propagate through the instrument response, and fit the observed DM–fluence plane [3]. Treating the host DM as log-normal with a total Galactic contribution of 80 pc cm⁻³, the data prefer a median DM_host of 84 pc cm⁻³ [3].

**Localization and host association.** ASKAP's Commensal Real-time ASKAP Fast Transients (CRAFT) survey freezes voltage buffers on detection, enabling coherent interferometric localization to sub-arcsecond precision — the "city block from the Moon" capability [10]. Optical follow-up with Keck, Gemini South, and the Very Large Telescope then identifies the host and measures its redshift; the chance-coincidence probability quantifies the association (for FRB 121102 it was below 3 × 10⁻⁴) [6].

A minimal Python implementation of the dispersion-delay calculation used throughout FRB analysis illustrates the core observable:

```python
def dm_delay_ms(dm: float, freq_ghz: float, ref_ghz: float = 1.4) -> float:
    """Dispersive delay of frequency freq_ghz relative to ref_ghz, in ms."""
    K = 4.148808e3  # MHz^2 pc^-1 cm^3 s
    return K * dm * (freq_ghz**-2 - ref_ghz**-2) / 1e3

def dm_budget(dm_obs, dm_mw=41.0, dm_halo=50.0, dm_host=66.0, z=0.3214):
    """Decompose an observed DM (cf. FRB 180924 accounting [11])."""
    dm_cosmic = dm_obs - dm_mw - dm_halo - dm_host / (1 + z)
    return {"mw": dm_mw, "halo": dm_halo,
            "host_rest": dm_host, "cosmic": round(dm_cosmic, 1)}

print(dm_budget(362.16))  # -> cosmic ~ 220 pc cm^-3, cf. mean ~280 [11]
```

The forward-modeling step expressed functionally in Haskell:

```haskell
-- Schechter luminosity function, differential in energy
schechter :: Double -> Double -> Double -> Double
schechter eStar gamma e = (e / eStar) ** gamma * exp (-e / eStar)

-- Expected detections: integrate luminosity function x selection x volume
expectedCounts :: (Double -> Double) -> [Double] -> Double
expectedCounts selection energies =
    sum [ schechter 2.38e41 (-1.3) e * selection e | e <- energies ]
-- E_char = 2.38e41 erg, gamma = -1.3 from CHIME population fits [3]
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Dispersion Measure Tomography and the Macquart Relation

The most powerful idea in FRB cosmology is also the simplest: the cosmic DM contribution grows with path length through the ionized intergalactic medium, so FRBs with measured redshifts map the universe's baryon content [5][11].

Cross-correlation of CHIME sources with galaxy catalogs provides an independent handle on where the electrons live. A statistically significant correlation (p ∼ 10⁻⁴) between CHIME FRBs and galaxies at redshift 0.3 to 0.5, in three independent photometric surveys, is consistent with an order-one fraction of CHIME bursts residing in the same dark-matter halos as survey galaxies [5]. The same analysis finds evidence for a subpopulation with large host DM, around 400 pc cm⁻³, plausibly arising from FRBs near the centers of massive (∼10¹⁴ solar mass) halos [5].

The practical difficulty is degeneracy: only the sum is measured, and only DM_cosmic carries clean cosmological information. Progress therefore depends on *localized* samples where redshift is known and host properties constrain DM_host — exactly what ASKAP, CHIME outriggers, and DSA-2000 are building [2][10].

### 4.2 Magnetar Engines: From Giant Flares to Coherent Radio Emission

No Galactic magnetar had ever produced an FRB-scale radio burst before April 2020, and this absence was the sharpest observational argument against magnetar models — the giant flare of SGR 1806−20, for instance, showed no detectable radio counterpart even in the side lobes of Parkes [9]. The SGR 1935+2154 event inverted the argument overnight.

Two broad classes of magnetar emission models survive this test, and they differ in *where* the radio is generated:

1. **Magnetospheric models** place the coherent emission inside the magnetosphere, analogous to pulsar radio emission but powered by crustal quakes or magnetic reconnection that launches Alfvén waves into the charge-starved magnetosphere. These naturally explain high polarization and rapid variability.
2. **Relativistic shock models** place the emission far from the star, where an ultra-relativistic flare outflow collides with the surrounding medium and a synchrotron maser instability generates coherent radio waves. These naturally explain the association with X-ray flares and the large inferred energy budgets.

> **Theorem (energy continuity):** SGR 1935+2154 demonstrates that a single magnetar produces bursts whose isotropic-equivalent radio energies span roughly seven orders of magnitude, with comparable rates across that range [8]. If this continuum extends upward by one more order of magnitude, the weakest cosmological FRBs are energetically reachable by magnetar-class engines without invoking new physics.

The April 2020 X-ray/radio fluence ratio matches shock-maser expectations and strains simple magnetospheric pictures — though beaming can rescue the latter [9]. There is no single "magnetar model"; the discovery supports the *class* while placing specific variants in tension [9].

### 4.3 Repeaters versus Apparent One-Offs: A Population Divide

CHIME's second catalog transformed the repeater question from anecdote to statistics: 83 confirmed repeating sources among 3,641, with 981 repeater bursts, and a dedicated analysis bringing the total repeater sample to 80 sources with uniform population statistics [2][4]. Only 2.4 ± 0.4 percent of CHIME sources have been observed to repeat, a fraction that shows no significant evolution over the survey duration [4].

Repeater bursts differ morphologically from one-off bursts: they are intrinsically wider in time and narrower in bandwidth, with characteristic downward-drifting time-frequency structure [1]. Yet population analysis finds *no* significant bimodality in burst-rate distributions: upper limits from one-off sources lie entirely within the observed repeater range, and both samples are equally well fit by a power-law repeat-rate distribution under which 50 to 100 percent of all sources repeat [4].

| Property | Repeating sources | Apparent one-offs |
|---|---|---|
| Observed fraction (CHIME) | 2.4 ± 0.4% of sources [4] | Remainder (upper limits consistent with repeaters) [4] |
| Temporal width | Intrinsically wider [1] | Narrower [1] |
| Spectral bandwidth | Narrower, downward-drifting [1] | Broader [1] |
| DM variability | Secular trends in some [4] | Not measurable (single epoch) |
| Host environments | Dwarf star-forming (121102); others diverse | Massive spirals, ellipticals, dwarfs [10] |

### 4.4 Host Galaxies as Laboratories of the Progenitor Environment

If the engine debate is about *what*, hosts are about *where* — and where constrains what. The early sample suggested a dichotomy: FRB 121102 in a low-metallicity, star-forming dwarf galaxy of stellar mass only a few × 10⁷ solar masses, coincident with a star-forming knot and accompanied by a compact persistent radio source of luminosity ∼10³⁹ erg s⁻¹ [6][7]; versus FRB 180924 in the outskirts of a massive, Milky Way-sized galaxy with modest star formation, 13,000 light-years from its center, with no persistent counterpart [10].

The growing localized sample complicates that picture: FRBs now appear in massive spirals, quiescent ellipticals, and even a globular cluster, spanning the full range of stellar mass, metallicity, and star-formation rate. This diversity admits two readings. The *heterogeneous* reading invokes multiple progenitor channels — young magnetars in dwarfs, aged magnetars or mergers in quiescent hosts. The *unified* reading notes that magnetars form through core collapse, accretion-induced collapse, and mergers, and so naturally inhabit all these environments. Cross-correlation evidence that an order-one fraction of CHIME FRBs occupy group-scale halos adds another axis: some bursts may trace intra-group gas rather than host interstellar medium [5].

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

The case for the picture developed above rests on a chain of quantitative results, each independently calibrated:

1. **The population exists at cosmological distances and in large numbers.** The first CHIME catalog's 536 bursts, with DMs consistent with a single sky and DM distribution for repeaters and non-repeaters alike, established the sample; the second catalog's 4,539 bursts from 3,641 sources, every event reprocessed under a uniform framework, confirmed that earlier inferences survive improved analysis [1][2]. The all-sky rate of 818 events per sky per day above 5 Jy ms makes FRBs among the most common extragalactic transients [1].

2. **The luminosity function has a physical cutoff.** Selection-corrected forward modeling prefers a Schechter form with E_char = 2.38 × 10⁴¹ erg, index −1.3, and a volumetric rate of 7.3 × 10⁴ Gpc⁻³ yr⁻¹ above 10³⁹ erg — numbers any progenitor model must reproduce [3].

3. **The repeater fraction is small but the rate distribution is continuous.** With 80 uniformly analyzed repeaters, 2.4 ± 0.4 percent of sources repeat, yet the absence of bimodality in repeat-rate distributions means the data are equally consistent with most sources repeating at rates below current sensitivity [4]. Secular DM variations in four repeaters prove evolving circumsource plasma [4].

4. **Magnetars demonstrably produce FRB-like emission.** SGR 1935+2154's April 2020 burst, coincident with a hard X-ray flare, plus two further bright bursts and a faint FAST detection, establish a single object spanning seven orders of magnitude in burst energy with clustered, non-Poissonian timing — the empirical existence proof for the magnetar hypothesis [8][9].

5. **Hosts are localized and diverse.** FRB 121102's dwarf host at z = 0.19273 with a persistent counterpart [6][7], FRB 180924's massive spiral host at z = 0.3214 [10], and CHIME's statistical association with structure at redshift 0.3 to 0.5 [5] prove FRB engines operate across the galaxy population — or that more than one engine exists.

6. **The DM budget closes.** For the best-studied sightlines, the decomposition into Milky Way, halo, cosmic, and host terms accounts for the observed DM within the expected scatter of the Macquart relation, validating the use of FRBs as baryon probes [11].

## 6 Limitations

Candor requires stating what the current data cannot do.

- **DM degeneracy.** The observed DM is a sum of four terms, and only their total is measured. Host and foreground-halo contributions are modeled, not measured, for most bursts; a burst with DM_host of several hundred pc cm⁻³ can masquerade as a high-redshift event [3][5][11].
- **Beaming and the true energy scale.** All energetics assume isotropic emission. If FRB emission is beamed into a small solid angle, true energies fall proportionally and volumetric rates rise — the entire luminosity function shifts [3][9].
- **Selection effects at the faint and scattered end.** The catalogs are demonstrably incomplete for bursts with scattering times above 10 ms at 600 MHz and for narrow-band events at low fluence; the inferred population may miss a large scattered subpopulation [1].
- **The one-off ambiguity.** Non-detection of repetition is an upper limit, not a measurement. Whether apparently one-off bursts are cataclysmic events or simply infrequent repeaters remains unconstrained by current exposure [4].
- **Missing multi-wavelength counterparts.** Apart from SGR 1935+2154, no FRB has a confirmed counterpart at other wavelengths; the persistent radio source of FRB 121102 remains exceptional rather than typical [6][8].
- **Sky coverage bias.** CHIME observes the northern sky in transit; southern-hemisphere and low-declination populations are underrepresented until SKA-era surveys mature [2].
- **Scattering versus intrinsic width.** Measured temporal widths conflate intrinsic emission timescales with multipath scattering in host and intervening plasma, blurring the morphological repeater/one-off distinction [1].

## 7 Conclusion

The CHIME/ASKAP era has converted fast radio bursts from a curiosity into a quantitative science. Dispersion measure tomography is now an operational technique: with calibrated DM budgets and growing samples of localized hosts, FRBs are beginning to weigh the ionized baryons of the cosmic web [5][11]. The magnetar hypothesis has graduated from theoretical preference to empirically demonstrated mechanism, at least for events resembling the Galactic prototype — though the gap between magnetospheric and shock-maser variants, and between the Galactic prototype and the most luminous cosmological bursts, remains open [8][9]. The repeater population is characterized with percent-level precision, and the continuity of burst-rate distributions keeps alive the hypothesis that most FRBs repeat [4].

What will decide the remaining questions is localization at scale. CHIME/FRB outriggers, ASKAP's CRAFT survey, DSA-2000, and the Square Kilometre Array will deliver thousands of sub-arcsecond positions, turning the DM–redshift relation into a precision cosmological tool and the host sample into a demographic census [2][10]. If the magnetar-unified picture holds, that census will show magnetar formation channels written across host types; if not, it will reveal the second engine. Either outcome is a discovery — and the bursts keep arriving, each one carrying, in its sweep from high to low frequency, a measurement of everything it passed through on its way to us.
