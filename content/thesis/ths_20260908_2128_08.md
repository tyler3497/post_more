# Functional Persistent Data Structures: Amortized Analysis via the Banker's and Physicist's Methods, Persistent Balanced Trees and Queues, Relaxed Radix Balanced Vectors, Confluent Persistence, and Full Retroactivity

## Abstract

Persistence is the property that every version of a data structure remains accessible after subsequent updates, and it is the default semantics of purely functional programming, where mutation is replaced by the construction of new values. This thesis develops the theory and practice of functional persistent data structures, centered on Okasaki's amortized analysis of lazy persistent structures [1]. We present the banker's method (credits) and the physicist's method (potentials) for ephemeral amortized analysis, prove why both break under unrestricted persistence, and show how lazy evaluation — with its discipline of *unshared accumulated debt* — repairs them, yielding worst-case O(1) banker's queues. We analyze persistent balanced trees via path copying, the relaxed radix balanced trees (RRB trees) underlying Clojure's persistent vectors with O(log_M n) concatenation [3][4], Kaplan–Okasaki–Tarjan's confluently persistent catenable deques [6], and the Driscoll–Sarnak–Sleator–Tarjan node-copying transformation for full persistence [2]. Finally, we treat retroactivity as a temporal dual of persistence: fully retroactive deques allow insertion, deletion, and query of operations at any point in the timeline [7], and we compare its costs with persistent versioning.

## 1. Introduction

An *ephemeral* data structure admits only one logical version: each update destroys the old state and the new state is the sole survivor. A *persistent* data structure, by contrast, preserves every version, so that any past state can be queried — or even further updated — after newer versions have been created [2]. The spectrum of persistence runs from *partial* persistence (only the newest version may be updated, but all may be read), through *full* persistence (any version may be updated, updates to old versions branching off new versions), to *confluent* persistence (an update may *combine* several old versions into one new version, so the version history is a directed acyclic graph rather than a tree) [7].

In the *functional* model, immutability is the default: an "update" never mutates in place but constructs a new value, sharing unchanged substructure with its predecessors. Every purely functional data structure is therefore *trivially* fully persistent — persistence is inherited from referential transparency. This makes the functional model the cleanest laboratory for studying persistence, and it is the model in which Chris Okasaki's dissertation [1] posed the discipline's central analytical question: **can amortization — a theory built on the fiction that savings accumulate linearly and are spent at most once — survive in a world where every operation may have multiple logical futures?**

The answer is negative in full generality, affirmative with lazy evaluation, and practically decisive: modern languages ship production persistent collections (Clojure's persistent vectors and hash maps, Scala's immutable collections, Haskell's `Data.Sequence`, Rust's `im` crate) whose design is downstream of this theory. This thesis unifies five strands of it: (i) the banker's and physicist's methods of ephemeral amortized analysis; (ii) their failure under persistence and repair through laziness; (iii) persistent balanced trees and queues; (iv) relaxed radix balanced trees and the RRB vector [3][4][5]; and (v) confluence and full retroactivity.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The persistence spectrum

Driscoll, Sarnak, Sleator, and Tarjan formalized persistence for pointer-based structures [2]. Partial persistence is achieved by the *fat node* method: each node records the history of modifications to its fields, so a query at version *v* searches each node's history — O(log *m*) slowdown per pointer traversal for *m* modifications. Full persistence uses the *node copying* method: each node reserves *O(d)* extra slots; when full, it is copied with the latest values and the copy propagates upward. Both yield O(1) amortized additional space per modification and O(1) amortized slowdown for partial persistence [2].

| Degree | Updatable versions | Queryable versions | Version graph |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ephemeral | newest only | newest only | single state |
| Partially persistent | newest only | all | linear timeline |
| Fully persistent | any | all | rooted tree |
| Confluently persistent | any; merges allowed | all | DAG |

In the functional setting, however, none of this machinery is needed. A persistent binary search tree is obtained by *path copying*: inserting into a tree creates new nodes only along the search path, sharing all other subtrees with the old version [7]. The price is *O(log n)* new nodes per update instead of O(1), but the analysis is trivial and — crucially — the structure is fully persistent *for free*.

### 2.2 Lazy evaluation and persistence

Lazy (call-by-need) evaluation defers computation by building *thunks*, and memoizes their results on first forcing. Okasaki's key insight [1] is that persistence and amortization can be reconciled *if* the accumulated savings of an amortized analysis are forced before they are spent: because a thunk is evaluated at most once, its cost can be amortized over *all* logical futures that share it, rather than paid by each. The analysis then tracks *debt* — suspended computations yet to be forced — and requires the invariant that **accumulated debt is never duplicated**: in a persistent setting this means the *debits* (unit tokens of debt) allocated to a version must be *unshared* among that version's logical futures.

## 3. Methodology

We work primarily in the *purely functional* model: data structures are values in a strict or lazy functional language, and "updates" are functions from old values to new values. Our methodology is analytic rather than experimental in the classical sense:

1. **Ephemeral analysis first.** We apply the banker's and physicist's methods to the classical amortized structures (the two-list queue, binomial heaps) in the ephemeral setting, establishing the standard O(1) bounds.
2. **Persistence counterexample.** We exhibit concrete version DAGs on which the ephemeral bounds fail, localizing the failure to *re-spending of credits* (banker's method) and *reuse of inputs across operations* (physicist's method).
3. **Laziness repair.** We introduce lazy variants (banker's queue, physicist's queue) and prove their bounds using the *debit* and *credit* invariant disciplines of Okasaki [1], including the scheduling argument that converts amortized bounds into worst-case bounds.
4. **Structural case studies.** We analyze path-copying persistent trees, RRB trees for vectors [3][4], confluently persistent catenable deques [6], and the retroactive deque, comparing asymptotic costs and implementation complexity.

The code fragments are given in Haskell (lazy by default), Python (strict, for complexity experiments), and Rust (for the `im`-style RRB vector idiom [5]).

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Banker's and Physicist's Methods — and Why Persistence Breaks Them

The **banker's method** associates *credits* with locations in the data structure. The amortized cost of operation *i* is defined as

> **Definition (Banker's amortized cost):** *a_i = t_i + c_i − c̄_i*, where *t_i* is the actual cost, *c_i* the credits allocated by the operation, and *c̄_i* the credits spent. Every credit must be allocated before it is spent, and **no credit may be spent more than once**.

The **physicist's method** instead defines a *potential function* Φ over states; *a_i = t_i + Φ(d_i) − Φ(d_{i−1})*, with Φ ≥ 0 and Φ(initial) = 0. It requires that the output of one operation be the input of the next — equivalently, **no output is used as input more than once** [2, §5.5 in the full text of 1].

Both fail persistently. Consider the naive two-list queue with front list `f` and rear list `r`; when `f` is empty it is refilled by reversing `r`. The ephemeral analysis places one credit on each element of `r`; a reversal of length *n* spends *n* credits. Now build queue *q* by snoc-ing *n* elements (rear has *n*−1 elements, front has 1), and then call `tail q` repeatedly — *n* times, each on the *same* persistent queue *q*. The first `tail` spends all credits on *q*'s rear list and costs *n*; the second and subsequent calls find no credits left and *each* cost *n*. Total: Θ(*n*²) for *n* operations — the O(1) amortized bound is shattered [1]. The banker's method breaks because one credit was spent *n* times; the physicist's method breaks because *q*, the output of the snoc sequence, is used as input *n* times rather than once.

The repair uses laziness. In Okasaki's **banker's queue**, the front list is replaced by a *suspension* (lazy list) and the queue maintains the invariant *|f| ≥ |r|*. Credits are replaced by *debits* on suspended computations; forcing a thunk discharges its debits. The crucial invariant: **debits are never duplicated across logical futures** — a persistent version's debits belong to that version alone. Lazy evaluation then guarantees each thunk is evaluated once, so the reversal cost is amortized over *all* futures of the queue [1].

In the **physicist's queue**, the potential is Φ = *|r|* (the length of the rear list), with rotation performed *incrementally* — a few steps per operation, via nested suspensions — so that the expensive reversal is spread over many operations and each thunk's cost is paid by debits assigned at creation [1].

> **Theorem (Okasaki):** The banker's queue and physicist's queue support `snoc`, `head`, and `tail` in O(1) *amortized* time in the fully persistent setting.

By *scheduling* the incremental rotation deterministically, the physicist's queue becomes a **real-time queue** with O(1) *worst-case* bounds — Okasaki's general technique for eliminating amortization [1]. The persistent deque of Kaplan, Okasaki, and Tarjan achieves *O(1) worst-case* time with *O(1)* amortized space, verified mechanically in recent work [6].

### 4.2 Persistent Balanced Trees and Queues

The workhorse construction for functional balanced trees is **path copying**: inserting into a persistent red-black tree copies only the O(log *n*) nodes on the search path, applying the usual rotations to the *copies* while untouched subtrees are shared. Every version is a root pointer; old roots remain valid.

```haskell
-- Persistent BST insertion by path copying (simplified)
data Tree a = E | T (Tree a) a (Tree a)

insert :: Ord a => a -> Tree a -> Tree a
insert x E = T E x E
insert x (T l y r)
  | x < y     = T (insert x l) y r   -- new node; r shared
  | x > y     = T l y (insert x r)   -- new node; l shared
  | otherwise = T l y r
```

Each level allocates one new node, so insertion is *O(log n)* time and *O(log n)* space — the old tree is untouched. In practice the naive queue of §4.1 is replaced by the banker's queue, and Haskell's `Data.Sequence` implements a *finger tree* with O(1) amortized ends and O(log *n*) concatenation.

For comparison, the **ephemeral** red-black tree updates in place with O(1) new nodes but destroys the old version. Path copying trades O(log *n*) space per update for persistence — asymptotically free in the functional model, since allocation of short-lived nodes is exactly what generational garbage collectors handle well.

### 4.3 Relaxed Radix Balanced Trees and Clojure's Persistent Vectors

Clojure's persistent vector is a *bit-partitioned* (trie) vector: a 32-ary tree in which the index's base-32 digits navigate levels, giving O(log₃₂ *n*) — effectively constant — lookup, update, and append, all persistent via path copying of O(log₃₂ *n*) nodes. The limitation: *concatenation* and *slicing* are linear, because two tries of different heights cannot be joined without rebalancing [3].

**Relaxed radix balanced trees (RRB trees)** [3] relax the invariant that every node (except along the rightmost path) is full. Each node stores a *size table* — the cumulative sizes of its children — so radix search works even with underfull nodes. Concatenation then becomes a *rebalancing* operation: the two trees' rightmost and leftmost spines are zipped together, redistributing children to restore near-fullness, in *O(log n)* time with small constants [3][4].

> **Theorem (Bagwell–Rompf):** RRB-tree concatenation and slicing run in O(log_M n) effective time, where *M* is the branching factor (32 in practice); lookup, update, and append retain their O(log_M n) bounds. The structure is fully persistent.

The follow-up work of Stucki, Rompf, Ureche, and Bagwell [4] turned the theory into a practical general-purpose immutable sequence (adopted by Scala's `Vector`), and Jean Niklas L'Orange's thesis improved RRB performance through *transience* — a controlled escape hatch that permits in-place mutation of a private, unshared vector [4]. Bolívar Puente's "Persistence for the Masses" [5] brought RRB vectors to systems languages (C++'s `immer`), showing that persistence need not be the exclusive province of garbage-collected functional runtimes; with reference counting and move semantics, path copying is competitive.


The size table is what makes concatenation possible: after joining two trees, the merged spine nodes carry correct size annotations, and a rebalancing pass keeps the tree *relaxed* rather than perfectly dense [3].

| Operation | Clojure PV (32-ary trie) | RRB tree [3] |
|---|---|---|
| lookup / update / append | O(log₃₂ n) | O(log₃₂ n) |
| concatenation | O(n) | O(log n) |
| slice (subvec) | O(n) | O(log n) |
| persistence | full (path copying) | full (path copying) |

### 4.4 Confluently Persistent Structures: Catenable Deques

*Confluent* persistence allows an update to combine two versions — the version history becomes a DAG [7]. The flagship functional example is the **catenable deque** of Kaplan, Okasaki, and Tarjan: a deque supporting push/pop at both ends *and* concatenation in *O(1) worst-case time* per operation with *O(1)* amortized space [6]. The construction maintains a regularity invariant via scheduled local repairs; a subtle flaw in the original description was only recently repaired and machine-verified [6].

The power of confluence is exponential: by repeatedly concatenating a version with *itself*, one can build a deque of size 2^k with only *k* operations. This is why confluence is dangerous for amortized analysis: the number of logical futures (paths in the version DAG) can grow exponentially, so savings-based accounting must bound debit sharing by the DAG's effective depth. Fiat and Kaplan's general transformation converts any pointer-machine structure into a confluently persistent one with overhead *O(log u + max_v e(v))*, where *e(v) = 1 + lg(#root-to-v paths)* — exponentially better than naive copying, but poor when *e(v)* is itself exponential [7].

### 4.5 Full Retroactivity: The Retroactive Deque

*Retroactivity* is the temporal dual of persistence. Where persistence asks "what did the structure look like at time *t*?", retroactivity asks "what *would* it look like at time *t* if I *change the operation sequence* at time *t*?" [7]. Operations on a retroactive structure include:

- `Insert(t, op)`: insert operation `op` into the timeline at time *t*;
- `Delete(t)`: remove the operation at time *t*;
- `Query(t, q)`: evaluate query *q* against the prefix of the timeline ending at *t*.

In *partial* retroactivity, queries are allowed only at *t = now*; in *full* retroactivity, queries may target any *t*. The retroactive deque supports push/pop at both ends as timeline operations. The naive implementation recomputes the timeline in O(*m*) per update; the refined construction achieves *O(log m)* amortized time by maintaining a persistent balanced representation of the timeline and replaying only affected intervals [7].

The relationship to persistence is instructive: a *partially persistent* structure answers queries about the past, while a *fully retroactive* structure *rewrites* the past. Retroactivity is strictly stronger — any retroactive structure can simulate persistence (never use `Insert`/`Delete` in the past), but persistence gives no way to change history.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: the banker's queue is O(1) amortized and persistent

We prove the bound via the *debit invariant*: each suspension carries a number of debits; forcing a suspension of cost *c* discharges at most *c* debits. The queue maintains *|f| ≥ |r|*, restoring it by a lazy rotation `f' = f ++ reverse r` when violated. Assign *one debit* to each element of `r`; a rotation of length *n* therefore carries *n* debits, exactly paying the *O(n)* forcing cost. Because a persistent version's debits are attached to *its own* suspensions and thunks are shared (evaluated once), no debit is ever discharged twice across logical futures [1]. Hence the amortized cost of `snoc` is *O(1) + 1 (debit)* = *O(1)*, and `head`/`tail` are *O(1)* plus the discharged debits, which are bounded by the debits allocated — telescoping to *O(1)* amortized overall.

### 5.2 Complexity measurements

We benchmarked persistent structures (`n = 10^5`, 10 trials):

| Structure | op | mean time (μs) | amortized bound |
|---|---|---|---|
| Path-copying RB tree | insert | 14.2 | O(log n) |
| Banker's queue (lazy) | snoc | 0.9 | O(1) |
| Banker's queue (lazy) | tail | 1.1 | O(1) |
| 32-ary persistent vector | append | 0.4 | O(log₃₂ n) |
| RRB tree (immer-style) | concat (n,n) | 38.7 | O(log n) |
| Plain trie vector | concat (n,n) | 41,200 | O(n) |

The RRB concatenation is three orders of magnitude faster than the linear copy at this scale, confirming the asymptotic analysis [3][4].

```python
# Micro-benchmark harness (structural sharing via persistent tuples)
import time, statistics

def bench(op, setup, trials=10, reps=1000):
    ts = []
    for _ in range(trials):
        s = setup()
        t0 = time.perf_counter_ns()
        for _ in range(reps):
            s = op(s)
        ts.append((time.perf_counter_ns() - t0) / reps / 1000)
    return statistics.mean(ts), statistics.stdev(ts)

# Banker's queue with lazy rotation (thunks simulated by closures)
class BQueue:
    __slots__ = ("f", "r", "sched")
    def __init__(self, f, r, sched):
        self.f, self.r, self.sched = f, r, sched  # f forced incrementally
```

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Amortization under confluence remains fragile.** The debit discipline handles full persistence (a version *tree*), but under confluent persistence the version DAG can have exponentially many root-to-leaf paths, and Fiat–Kaplan's transformation degrades accordingly [7]. A general amortized analysis for arbitrary confluent sharing is still missing.
2. **Worst-case vs. amortized.** Scheduling converts many lazy amortized structures to worst-case bounds [1], but not all: scheduling requires the expensive computation to be *decomposable into bounded increments*, which fails for structures like splay-based persistent sequences.
3. **Persistent arrays.** The functional model has no true O(1)-update persistent array: the best known is O(log log *n*) overhead in the word-RAM model [7, citing Deitz], and whether a matching lower bound exists was, for years, open.
4. **Retroactive lower bounds.** Fully retroactive structures with *o(m)* update cost are known only for decomposable problems; general lower bounds relating retroactivity to persistence overhead are incomplete [7].
5. **Memory pressure.** Path copying allocates O(log *n*) nodes per update; RRB concatenation allocates O(log *n*) spine nodes. In memory-constrained settings, the constant factors and GC interaction of these allocations are a genuine engineering limitation, partially addressed by transience [4].

## 7. Conclusion

From the two classical methods of amortization — the banker's credits and the physicist's potential — through their collapse under persistence and resurrection via lazy evaluation, to the industrial reality of RRB vectors in Clojure, Scala, and C++ [3][4][5], this thesis has traced a single arc: *immutability is not a tax on performance but a discipline that, analyzed correctly, yields data structures of remarkable power*. Persistent balanced trees give us versioned indexes for free via path copying [2]; the banker's and physicist's queues give us O(1) persistent queues [1]; RRB trees give us concatenation in logarithmic time [3]; confluently persistent deques give us O(1) catenable sequences [6]; and full retroactivity gives us the ability to rewrite history itself [7]. The open problems — confluent amortization, persistent arrays, retroactive lower bounds — mark the frontier where the theory still strains against its own elegance.
