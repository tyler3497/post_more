# Mechanistic Interpretability of Transformers: Grokking Dynamics, Sparse Autoencoders, and Circuit Discovery in Attention Heads

## Abstract

Mechanistic interpretability seeks to reverse-engineer the learned algorithms inside transformer networks, moving beyond behavioral probes toward a causal, weight-level account of model computation. This thesis unifies three pillars of that program: (i) **grokking dynamics**, the delayed phase transition from memorization to generalization on algorithmic tasks, dissected via Fourier-circuit analysis of modular arithmetic [1,2]; (ii) **sparse autoencoders (SAEs)**, which decompose superposed activations into interpretable, monosemantic feature dictionaries and have become the standard vocabulary for inspecting large models [5,6]; and (iii) **circuit discovery** in attention heads, exemplified by induction heads that implement match-and-copy algorithms and constitute a mechanistic substrate for in-context learning [3,4]. We develop the shared mathematical language of the residual stream, derive the training objective of SAE dictionary learning, formalize activation patching as causal intervention, and present a quantitative account of the grokking delay law grounded in norm separation under regularized optimization [1]. Empirical vignettes — progress measures in modular addition, ablation of key Fourier frequencies, SAE feature probes, and induction-circuit compositionality — illustrate how these methods compose into a coherent research paradigm. We close with limitations: faithfulness gaps, scalability costs of SAEs, and the unresolved generality of toy-circuit findings to frontier-scale reasoning.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models exhibit capabilities — in-context learning, chain-of-thought reasoning, zero-shot instruction following — that were never explicitly programmed. The dominant explanatory strategy for these capabilities has been *behavioral*: vary inputs, measure outputs, and fit scaling laws. Yet behavioral description leaves a fundamental question unanswered: **what algorithm does the network actually implement?** Mechanistic interpretability is the attempt to answer that question at the level of weights, activations, and causal pathways [2].

The central difficulty is that neural networks store their computations in a form hostile to inspection. A single neuron is typically *polysemantic*, responding to many unrelated inputs [3], because the model represents more features than it has dimensions — a compression strategy known as **superposition**. Reverse-engineering must therefore proceed through structured methods rather than naive neuron inspection:

1. **Circuit discovery** — identifying small subnetworks (e.g., induction heads) that implement recognizable algorithms and composing them into end-to-end explanations [3,4].
2. **Grokking analysis** — exploiting the delayed-generalization phenomenon, where a model first memorizes and only later discovers a structured, generalizable circuit, as a natural experiment in circuit formation [1,2].
3. **Sparse autoencoders** — learning overcomplete sparse dictionaries that disentangle superposed activations into individually interpretable features [5,6].

This thesis develops each pillar in depth, shows how they interlock, and reports the empirical and formal results that justify their prominence. Our running example is the one-layer and two-layer transformer trained on modular addition $a + b \pmod{p}$, the task in which grokking was first characterized and for which the learned circuit is now essentially fully reverse-engineered [1,2].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Transformer as a Residual Stream

Elhage et al. introduced the **residual stream** formalism: each token's representation is a vector $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ that persists across layers, while attention heads and MLPs read from and write into it [2]. Writing attention-head $h$ at layer $\ell$ with output matrix $W_O^{(h)}$ and value matrix $W_V^{(h)}$, its contribution is:

$$\text{Attn}^{(h)}(x) = \sum_j \alpha_{ij}^{(h)}\, W_O^{(h)} W_V^{(h)} x_j, \qquad \alpha_{ij}^{(h)} = \text{softmax}_j\!\left(\frac{x_i^\top W_Q^{(h)\top} W_K^{(h)} x_j}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right).$$

The **QK circuit** $W_Q^\top W_K$ determines *where* information moves, and the **OV circuit** $W_O W_V$ determines *what* information is moved. Any computational story about an attention head decomposes along these two circuits [2].

### 2.2 Superposition and Polysemanticity

A transformer of width $d$ routinely encodes far more than $d$ task-relevant features. Superposition is optimal under compression: if features are sparse (rarely co-active), they can share dimensions nearly without interference. The cost is that individual neurons activate for multiple unrelated concepts, defeating simple inspection [3]. This motivates dictionary-learning approaches (§4.4).

### 2.3 Induction Heads

An **induction head** implements the copying rule $[A][B]\dots[A] \rightarrow [B]$: upon seeing token $A$ repeated, it predicts the token that followed $A$ previously. It requires *two* heads composed across layers: a **previous-token head** in layer 0 copies each token's embedding into the next position's residual stream, and an **induction head** in layer 1 matches the current token against previous tokens via its QK circuit and copies the *successor* token via its OV circuit [2]. Olsson et al. showed that induction heads emerge at precisely the point in training where in-context learning ability sharply increases, making them the leading mechanistic candidate for the majority of few-shot learning in large models [3,4].

### 2.4 Grokking

Power et al. trained small networks on modular arithmetic with a fraction of the operation table and observed a striking pattern: training accuracy reaches 100% (memorization) while test accuracy lingers near chance — then, after hundreds of thousands of additional steps, test accuracy abruptly jumps to near 100% [4]. Weight decay is the key driver: the generalizing circuit has smaller norm than the memorizing one, so regularized training eventually prefers it. The delay follows a quantitative **Norm-Separation Delay Law** $T_{\text{grok}} - T_{\text{mem}} = \Theta(\gamma_{\text{eff}}^{-1}\log(\lVert\theta_{\text{mem}}\rVert^2 / \lVert\theta_{\text{post}}\rVert^2))$ [1].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Activation Patching as Causal Intervention

Interpretability claims are cheap when purely correlational. **Activation patching** elevates them to causal claims: run the model on a clean input $x$ and a corrupted input $x'$ (e.g., swapping the operand in $a+b \pmod p$), then patch an activation from the corrupted run into the clean run at a chosen component and measure the change in output logits [3,6]. The standard metrics:

| Metric | Definition | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Direct logit difference | $\Delta = \text{logit}_{\text{patch}}(y^*) - \text{logit}_{\text{clean}}(y^*)$ | Causal effect on the correct answer |
| Probability change | $P_{\text{patch}}(y^*) - P_{\text{clean}}(y^*)$ | Effect on normalized output |
| KL divergence | $D_{\text{KL}}(p_{\text{patch}} \,\Vert\, p_{\text{clean}})$ | Total behavioral shift |

Path patching extends this to *edges*: it isolates whether a component affects the output only through a particular downstream path, enabling full circuit graphs.

### 3.2 Sparse Autoencoder Training Objective

Given activations $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ sampled from a layer of interest, an SAE learns an overcomplete dictionary $D \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times m}$ ($m \gg d$) with sparse codes $z \in \mathbb{R}^m$ [3]:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{SAE}} = \lVert x - Dz \rVert_2^2 + \lambda \lVert z \rVert_1 + \gamma\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}}.$$

Modern variants replace the $\ell_1$ penalty with exact-$k$ sparsity (TopK) or JumpReLU gating for better feature utilization, and sweep widths up to $10^7$ latents on frontier models [5,6]. The decoder columns $D_{:j}$ are the *feature directions*: steering the model means adding $D_{:j}$ into the residual stream.

### 3.3 Progress Measures for Circuit Formation

Nanda et al. introduced **restricted loss** and **excluded loss** for the modular-addition circuit: project out (or retain only) the key Fourier frequencies the reverse-engineered circuit uses, then track loss on the remaining subspace [5]. These metrics improve continuously *before* grokking occurs, revealing that the generalizing circuit forms gradually and the sharp test-accuracy jump is merely the moment weight decay finishes cleaning up the memorization circuit — a three-phase picture of **memorization → circuit formation → cleanup** [5].

> **Theorem:** (Norm-Separation Delay Law, informal [1]). Under regularized first-order optimization with effective contraction rate $\gamma_{\text{eff}}$, the grokking delay between memorization onset $T_{\text{mem}}$ and generalization $T_{\text{grok}}$ satisfies $T_{\text{grok}} - T_{\text{mem}} = \Theta(\gamma_{\text{eff}}^{-1}\log(\lVert\theta_{\text{mem}}\rVert^2/\lVert\theta_{\text{post}}\rVert^2))$. Empirically validated across 293 runs: inverse scaling with weight decay ($R^2 = 0.97$) and logarithmic dependence on the norm ratio ($r = 0.91$).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Residual Stream as a Shared Blackboard

Treating layers as additive writers to a persistent stream $x \leftarrow x + f(x)$ reframes interpretability as *accounting*: every logit must be explainable as a sum of contributions from identified components. The residual-stream view is what makes circuit decomposition tractable — each head's OV output is a vector in the same space as the logits' unembedding, so one can read a head's "vote" directly by projecting through $W_U$ [2]. This linearity is the workhorse behind direct-logit attribution and explains why attention-only models were reverse-engineered first: MLPs add nonlinear writes that are harder to attribute, though key-value memory interpretations of MLP layers narrow the gap [6].

```python
# Direct logit attribution: how much does head h's output at position i
# vote for the correct next token y*?
def direct_logit_attribution(model, head, layer, x, y_star):
    with trace(model) as tracer:
        out = tracer.get(f"blocks.{layer}.attn.heads.{head}.output")
        # project through the unembedding: votes in logit space
        votes = out @ model.W_U
    return votes[:, y_star].sum().item()
```

### 4.2 Induction Heads and the Copy Mechanism

The induction circuit is the cleanest example of a discovered algorithm. Concretely, for a two-layer attention-only model [2]:

1. **Layer 0 — previous-token head:** QK circuit is a fixed positional pattern; $x_{i+1} \mathrel{+}= W_{OV}^{\text{prev}} x_i$. The residual stream at position $i+1$ now carries *both* $x_{i+1}$ and a copy of $x_i$.
2. **Layer 1 — induction head:** its query reads the current token $A$; its key reads the previous-token head's write, i.e., attends to positions $j$ where the token *before* $j$ matches $A$; its value copies the token *at* $j$ (which is $B$ if position $j$ followed $A$). Output: predict $B$.

Six lines of evidence — including ablation that destroys few-shot performance while sparing other capabilities — support the claim that induction heads implement the bulk of in-context learning [2]. Subsequent theory shows their emergence follows tight dynamical bounds: on a minimal ICL task, gradient flow is confined to a 19-dimensional subspace, only 3 dimensions govern emergence, and time-to-emergence scales quadratically in context length [7].

### 4.3 Grokking and Fourier Circuits in Modular Arithmetic

The most complete circuit reverse-engineering to date is Nanda et al.'s account of a one-layer transformer trained on $a + b \pmod{113}$ [5]. The network discovers the trigonometric identity

$$\cos\!\big(\omega_k(a+b-c)\big) = \cos(\omega_k a)\cos(\omega_k b)\cos(\omega_k c) + \cdots,$$

where $\omega_k = 2\pi k / 113$ are key frequencies. The evidence is strikingly direct [5]:

- The embedding and unembedding matrices are well approximated by sums of sinusoids at a handful of key frequencies.
- MLP neurons are well approximated by degree-2 polynomials of those sinusoids — the model literally computes products of cosines.
- Ablating the key frequencies destroys performance; ablating *all other* frequencies slightly improves it.

Crucially, **restricted loss** (ablating non-key frequencies) and **excluded loss** (ablating key frequencies) improve continuously throughout the "memorization plateau," proving the generalizing circuit is being built long before test accuracy moves. The grokking jump is the *cleanup* phase, where weight decay eliminates the high-norm memorization circuit [2,8]. This reframes grokking from mystery to mechanism: a predictable consequence of norm separation between competing interpolating solutions under regularized training [1].

### 4.4 Sparse Autoencoders: Disentangling Superposed Features

SAEs attack the superposition problem directly: learn the overcomplete dictionary in which activations are sparse, and interpret its columns. Applied to frontier models, SAEs recover monosemantic features for entities, syntax, and abstract concepts (e.g., "Golden Gate Bridge" features that, when steered, make the model obsess over the bridge) [3]. The modern pipeline:

1. Sample $\sim 10^9$ activations from a target layer/site (residual stream, MLP output, or attention output) [5,6].
2. Train a JumpReLU or BatchTopK SAE with width $16\text{k}$–$10\text{M}$, tuning sparsity to the knee of the reconstruction-vs-sparsity Pareto frontier.
3. Auto-interpret features: show a strong LM the top-activating contexts and have it label the feature; score labels by simulation accuracy.
4. Validate causally: steer or ablate features and measure behavioral change on benchmarks (sparse probing, targeted perturbation) [3].

Notably, recent comparisons find that transformer MLP key-value memories are *nearly* as interpretable as SAE features on modern benchmarks — SAEs win marginally on some axes but vanilla feed-forward parameters are a strong baseline, raising the question of how much SAEs add beyond faithful reading of the model's own parameters [6].

### 4.5 From Toy Circuits to a General Paradigm

The three pillars interlock. Grokking supplies the *dynamics*: how circuits form under optimization pressure. Induction heads supply the *compositional unit*: a two-head circuit with a complete algorithmic story. SAEs supply the *vocabulary*: the feature directions in which all circuits should ultimately be expressed. Recent theory closes loops between them — e.g., the norm-separation delay law explains *when* circuits finish forming [1], while cross-layer SAEs and residualized variants handle the redundancy that naive single-layer dictionaries miss [3]. The research bet is that frontier-model behaviors decompose into a large but finite catalog of such circuits, each discoverable by the same toolkit.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Formal Results

We consolidate the key quantitative findings from the cited program:

| Finding | Method | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Induction heads ⇒ ICL onset | Ablation + phase-change timing | Few-shot accuracy collapses when induction heads are ablated; heads form exactly at the ICL loss bump [2] |
| Fourier circuit in modular addition | Weight/activation projection | Neuron-logit map ≈ sum of sinusoids at key frequencies; ablation of key frequencies → chance [5] |
| Three-phase grokking | Restricted/excluded loss | Circuit forms continuously; sharp test jump occurs during cleanup, after generalization mechanism exists [5] |
| Delay law | 293 runs, mod-add/mult, parity | $T_{\text{grok}}-T_{\text{mem}} \propto \gamma_{\text{eff}}^{-1}\log(\text{norm ratio})$; $R^2 = 0.97$ vs weight decay [1] |
| SGD fails to grok | Optimizer comparison | SGD fails where AdamW groks at identical hyperparameters — decoupling memorization from contraction is required [1] |
| SAE interpretability | CE-Bench, auto-interp | Middle layers (L10–L15) most interpretable; residual vs attention vs MLP sites comparable [3] |
| KV memories vs SAEs | Head-to-head benchmark | FF key-value vectors nearly as interpretable as SAE latents; SAEs marginally better on some axes [6] |

A minimal reproduction of the grokking experiment fits in a few dozen lines [1,2]:

```python
import torch, torch.nn as nn

P, FRAC = 113, 0.3  # modulus and training fraction
pairs = [(a, b) for a in range(P) for b in range(P)]
train = pairs[: int(FRAC * len(pairs))]

model = nn.TransformerEncoder(...)          # 1-layer, d_model=128, 4 heads
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3, weight_decay=1.0)

for step in range(200_000):
    a, b = train[torch.randint(len(train), (512,))]
    logits = model(a, b)                    # predict (a+b) % P
    loss = nn.functional.cross_entropy(logits, (a + b) % P)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    # ~step 1k: train acc -> 100% (memorization); ~step 50k+: test acc -> 100% (grok)
```

The Haskell-flavored specification of the discovered circuit, as trigonometric identities over the key frequencies, is equally compact:

```haskell
-- Reverse-engineered modular-addition circuit (Nanda et al.)
omega :: Int -> Double
omega k = 2 * pi * fromIntegral k / 113

logit :: Int -> Int -> Int -> Double
logit a b c = sum [ cos (omega k * fromIntegral (a + b - c)) | k <- keyFreqs ]
-- keyFreqs: the ~5 frequencies the model actually uses; all others ablated
```

## 6 Limitations

1. **Toy-task generality.** The complete circuits (induction, Fourier) were reverse-engineered in 1–2-layer models on synthetic tasks. Whether frontier reasoning decomposes into the same *kinds* of circuits — or qualitatively different ones — remains open [4,6].
2. **SAE faithfulness.** SAEs are proxy modules trained to reconstruct activations, not to mirror the model's own ontology. Reconstruction error, feature splitting, and shrinkage introduce artifacts; auto-interp scores are model-judged and can be gamed [5,6].
3. **Scalability.** Training SAEs across all layers of frontier models costs orders of magnitude more compute than the base model run, and cross-layer redundancy (features duplicated across residual streams) degrades intervention quality [3].
4. **Causal completeness.** Ablation studies establish necessity, rarely sufficiency; a circuit that survives ablation may still miss components (the "hydra effect," where backup heads compensate) [2].
5. **The generality of the delay law.** The norm-separation theory is validated on algorithmic tasks; its extension to natural language, where no clean memorization/generalization dichotomy exists, is speculative [1].

## 7 Conclusion

Mechanistic interpretability has moved from aspiration to a productive engineering discipline. We now possess a causal toolkit — patching, progress measures, dictionary learning — a catalog of fully reverse-engineered circuits, and quantitative laws governing when circuits form. The induction head explains a large share of in-context learning [2]; the Fourier circuit explains grokking on modular arithmetic [5]; sparse autoencoders provide the feature vocabulary in which such explanations can be written at scale [3]; and the norm-separation delay law predicts the *timescale* of circuit emergence [1]. The central open bet is compositionality at scale: that frontier-model cognition is a large but finite library of such circuits, discoverable by the same methods. If that bet holds, interpretability graduates from case studies to cartography — a complete map of machine thought.
