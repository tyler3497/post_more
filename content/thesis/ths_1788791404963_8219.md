# From Counterexamples to Oracles: A Unified Theory of Counterexample-Guided, Syntax-Guided, Sketch-Based, Deductive, and Neuro-Symbolic Program Synthesis

## Abstract

Program synthesis — the automatic construction of programs from specifications — has fragmented into a family of paradigms that differ less in their goals than in how they tame an astronomically large search space. This thesis develops a unified account of five major traditions: **counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS)** [1,4], **syntax-guided synthesis (SyGuS)** [2,3], **sketch-based synthesis** [1], **deductive synthesis**, and **LLM-guided / neuro-symbolic synthesis with verified oracles** [7]. We show that every paradigm can be cast as an instance of *oracle-guided inductive synthesis (OGIS)* [6]: a learner proposes candidates inside a hypothesis space while an oracle answers a restricted vocabulary of queries (correctness, witness, membership, distinguishing inputs). The paradigms differ precisely in (i) the shape of the hypothesis space, (ii) the query interface, and (iii) the direction in which information flows — top-down from a proof or sketch, bottom-up from enumeration, or sideways from a learned prior. We prove-position the known decidability frontiers: general synthesis is $\Sigma^0_3$-complete [5], CEGIS-style generalization is uncomputable [5], and minimum-query inductive synthesis is NP-hard [6], while syntactic restrictions (SyGuS grammars, sketches, templates) buy back decidability at the cost of *relative* completeness. Finally, we analyze how large language models act as powerful but unsound proposal oracles, and how verified oracles (SMT solvers, proof checkers) restore soundness — at a price measured in verification calls.

## 1 Introduction

The dream of *automatic programming* is nearly as old as computing itself: given a description of *what* a program should do, derive *how* it does it, mechanically and correctly. Modern program synthesis realizes this dream only by conceding a central tension — the space of candidate programs is infinite, the specification is almost always incomplete, and the mapping between the two is computationally brutal. Every successful synthesis architecture is therefore, at its core, a **search strategy plus a pruning discipline**: a way of proposing candidates and a way of rejecting wrong ones cheaply.

Five schools have dominated the last two decades:

1. **CEGIS** (Solar-Lezama et al., 2006) [1,4]: iterate between an inductive synthesizer that fits a finite set of examples and a verifier that refutes candidates with counterexamples.
2. **SyGuS** (Alur et al., 2013) [2]: constrain the search syntactically with a grammar $G$ and semantically with a logical formula $\varphi$, and let solver competitions drive progress.
3. **Sketching** (Solar-Lezama, 2008) [1]: let the programmer supply the high-level algorithmic skeleton with *holes* for low-level details, reducing synthesis to constraint solving.
4. **Deductive synthesis**: derive the program constructively from a proof of the specification's satisfiability, in the tradition of Manna and Waldinger's deductive tableaux.
5. **Neuro-symbolic synthesis** [7]: use learned models — most recently large language models — to propose candidates, while *verified oracles* (SMT solvers, type checkers, proof assistants) retain the final word on correctness.

The central claim of this thesis is that these are not five different fields but five parameter settings of one machine. The **oracle-guided inductive synthesis (OGIS)** framework of Jha and Seshia [6] provides the common substrate: a learner $\mathcal{L}$ and an oracle $\mathcal{O}$ interact through queries. Once we see CEGIS as OGIS with a *correctness* oracle, SyGuS as OGIS with a *grammar-restricted* concept class, sketching as OGIS with a *hole-instantiation* oracle backed by SAT, deductive synthesis as OGIS where the oracle *is* a proof procedure, and LLM-guided synthesis as OGIS where the proposal oracle is *learned and unsound*, the entire landscape snaps into focus — including where each paradigm hits its decidability wall.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The formal synthesis problem

Fix a (possibly infinite) input domain $\mathcal{D}$, a concept class $\mathcal{C}$ of programs, and a specification $\Phi$ — a predicate over program behaviors. The *formal inductive synthesis* problem is the tuple $\langle \mathcal{C}, \mathcal{D}, \Phi, \mathcal{O} \rangle$ [6]: find $c \in \mathcal{C}$ such that $\Phi(c)$ holds, using only queries from the oracle interface $\mathcal{O}$. The interface typically contains some subset of:

| Query | Oracle returns |
|---|---|
| $q_{\text{corr}}(c)$ | YES, or a counterexample $x \in \mathcal{D}$ where $c$ violates $\Phi$ |
| $q_{\text{wit}}^+$ | A positive witness: an input-output pair consistent with $\Phi$ |
| $q_{\text{mem}}(x)$ | YES/NO: does the (unknown) target behave correctly on $x$? |
| $q_{\text{diff}}(c_1, c_2)$ | An input distinguishing two candidates |

CEGIS is the special case $\mathcal{O} = \{q_{\text{corr}}\}$ with an adversarial verifier: the synthesizer solves the constraint problem on a finite example set $d \subset \mathcal{D}$, the verifier either certifies the candidate or returns $x \in \mathcal{D}$ refuting it, and $x$ is added to $d$ [4].

### 2.2 Enumerative search and version spaces

Before oracles, there was enumeration. The classical inductive approach enumerates $\mathcal{C}$ in order of increasing program size and tests each candidate against examples — *enumerative search*. Its modern refinement is the **version space algebra**: rather than materializing candidates, maintain a compact symbolic representation of *all* programs consistent with the examples seen so far, and intersect version spaces as new examples arrive [7]. FlashMeta (Polozov & Gulwani, 2015) showed that inverse semantics can decompose examples through DSL operators, making version spaces practical for real string-transformation tasks. Enumeration is complete *relative to $\mathcal{C}$* but blind: without guidance, the number of programs of size $n$ grows exponentially, and solver-backed pruning is what makes it viable.

### 2.3 A note on specifications

Specifications come in three flavors, and the flavor determines the oracle:

- **Logical**: $\forall \vec{x}.\, \varphi(f, \vec{x})$ in a background theory $T$ (SMT-LIB). The verifier is an SMT solver [3].
- **Examples**: input-output pairs, possibly with noise. The oracle is a membership test [7].
- **Reference implementations / tests**: behavioral equivalence against a golden model. The oracle is differential testing [1].

Each paradigm commits to one or two of these; none handles all three uniformly — a first hint of the fragmentation this thesis resolves.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is *comparative formalization*: we fix the OGIS vocabulary and re-derive each paradigm as a restriction of it, then read off complexity, completeness, and pruning properties from the restriction. Concretely, for each paradigm we identify:

1. **Concept class $\mathcal{C}$**: all programs? grammar-bounded? sketch instances? proof inhabitants?
2. **Oracle interface $\mathcal{O}$**: which queries exist, and are they decidable?
3. **Information flow**: does knowledge flow *downward* (specification $\to$ program, deductive), *upward* (examples $\to$ generalization, inductive), or *laterally* (corpus/prior $\to$ bias, neuro-symbolic)?

We then survey the decidability frontiers established in the literature — the $\Sigma^0_3$-completeness of general synthesis [5], the uncomputability of generalization [5], the NP-hardness of minimum-query synthesis [6] — and locate each paradigm on the tractability map. Empirical grounding comes from the SyGuS competition series [2], enumerative/neuro-symbolic solver evaluations, and library-learning results [7]. Where the literature is silent (e.g., precise query complexity of LLM-oracle loops), we derive bounds from the OGIS reduction rather than inventing experiments.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 CEGIS and the oracle-guided view

CEGIS is deceptively simple. Let $E$ be a finite set of examples (initially possibly empty):

```python
def cegis(synthesize, verify, spec):
    E = set()                      # distinguishing examples so far
    while True:
        cand = synthesize(spec, E)  # inductive step: fit E
        ce = verify(spec, cand)     # deductive step: refute or certify
        if ce is None:
            return cand            # verified: done
        E.add(ce)                  # learn from the counterexample
```

The brilliance is the division of labor: the *synthesizer* solves a finite, hence typically decidable, constraint problem, while the *verifier* shoulders the unbounded universal quantification $\forall x.\, \text{spec}(\text{cand}, x)$ [4]. Termination is not guaranteed in general — the loop may enumerate infinitely many candidates — and this is not an implementation defect but a complexity-theoretic necessity:

> Theorem: There is no computable generalization procedure that maps an arbitrary solution correct on a finite example set to a solution correct on the full infinite domain. Generalization is $\Sigma^0_2$-complete, and CEGIS as a generalization algorithm is therefore suboptimal in hardness terms, however effective in practice [5].

The OGIS lens sharpens this: CEGIS uses only $q_{\text{corr}}$, and Jha & Seshia show that even *minimizing the number of queries* to solve $\langle \mathcal{C}, \mathcal{D}, \Phi, \mathcal{O} \rangle$ is NP-hard, via reduction from minimum set cover [6]. Practical CEGIS therefore lives or dies by **solver-backed pruning** — the synthesizer is usually a SAT/SMT solver, and each counterexample becomes a learned clause constraining all future candidates. Singh and Solar-Lezama [4] further observe that CEGIS performance is volatile: the sequence of CSPs solved across iterations can hit SAT phase transitions, where instances are constrained enough to be hard but not enough to prune.

Modern CEGIS extends the loop in two directions. *Relational synthesis* lifts counterexamples to relational ground formulas and builds version spaces as hierarchical tree automata [2]. *Template CEGIS* for invariant inference parameterizes a template (e.g., piecewise-linear $T(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$) and learns linear lemmas from each counterexample state, converging on inductive invariants after a handful of lemmas [9].

### 4.2 SyGuS: syntax as a decidability instrument

Syntax-Guided Synthesis [2] makes the concept class *explicit and first-class*: find $f$ such that (i) $\varphi(f)$ holds in background theory $T$, and (ii) $f$ is derivable from a grammar $G$. In SyGuS-IF (built on SMT-LIB) [3]:

```lisp
(set-logic LIA)
(synth-fun f ((x Int) (y Int)) Int
  ((i Int) (c Int) (b Bool))
  ((i Int (c x y (+ i i) (ite b i i)))
   (c Int (0 1))
   (b Bool ((<= i i)))))
(declare-var x Int) (declare-var y Int)
(constraint (>= (f x y) x))
(constraint (>= (f x y) y))
(constraint (or (= (f x y) x) (= (f x y) y)))
(check-synth)
;; a compliant solver returns: (define-fun f ((x Int) (y Int)) Int (ite (<= x y) y x))
```

The grammar does double duty: it encodes *programmer intent* (only consider "reasonable" programs) and it is a *decidability instrument*. For restricted fragments — e.g., single-invocation properties in linear integer arithmetic — synthesis becomes decidable, and solvers like CVC4/CVC5 exploit this with termination guarantees [2]. The annual **SyGuS-Comp** competition [2] turned this into an empirical science: tracks for LIA, invariants (INV), programming-by-example (PBE), and general grammars; solvers combining enumeration with unification (EUSolver), stochastic search, and Sketch-derived CEGIS engines (Sketch-ac). The competition's lesson is quantitative: on grammar-conformant problems, modern solvers (notably CVC5's SyGuS mode) are fast and consistent, solving dozens of benchmarks in under a second — but performance collapses the moment the target function leaves the pre-defined grammar [8].

Enumerative SyGuS solvers deserve special attention because they expose the pruning problem in pure form. Naive enumeration drowns in the exponential blowup of grammar derivations; practical solvers add **observational equivalence** (discard programs indistinguishable on the example set), **symmetry breaking**, and **unification/decision-tree learning** over enumerated terms [2]. These are all instances of one principle: *the verifier's answers must prune whole equivalence classes of candidates, not single programs*.

### 4.3 Sketches, templates, and deductive synthesis

*Sketching* [1] attacks the search problem from the opposite end: instead of restricting the grammar globally, the programmer writes a **partial program** — a sketch — that fixes the high-level control structure and leaves *holes* (`??`) for unknown constants, operators, and reorderings:

> Theorem: A sketch with $k$ holes over finite domains reduces synthesis to a finite constraint-satisfaction problem solvable by a SAT/SMT solver, with the CEGIS loop providing soundness against the full specification beyond the test suite [1].

```haskell
-- Sketch: reorder three values with unknown comparisons (holes)
sketch :: Int -> Int -> Int -> (Int, Int, Int)
sketch x y z =
  if ??          -- hole 1: which comparison?
    then (x, y, z)
    else if ??
      then (y, x, z)   -- hole 2
      else (x, z, y)   -- ...
```

Solar-Lezama's thesis [1] shows this is not merely a convenience: the sketch makes the hypothesis space *finite* (bounded holes over finite domains), so the inductive synthesis step is a single SAT query, and CEGIS lifts finite-example correctness to full correctness via the verifier. The programmer's insight and the solver's exhaustiveness meet exactly at the holes.

**Deductive synthesis** inverts the flow entirely. In the Manna–Waldinger tradition, one proves $\forall \vec{x}.\, \exists \vec{y}.\, \text{spec}(\vec{x}, \vec{y})$ constructively, and the *proof itself* is the program (Curry–Howard in spirit, deductive tableaux in practice). Modern heirs include Leon/Stainless-style systems that interleave verification and synthesis: failed proof attempts generate *counterexamples*, which refine the program — a deductive/inductive hybrid where the oracle is a theorem prover. Deductive synthesis offers the strongest guarantees (correctness by construction) at the highest price (it needs a specification complete enough to prove from, and proof search is itself undecidable in general). In OGIS terms: the "learner" proposes proof skeletons, the oracle answers with proof obligations, and counterexamples are unprovable subgoals.

The three approaches form a spectrum of *who supplies the structure*:

| Paradigm | Structure source | Search burden | Guarantee |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deductive | Proof of spec | Proof search (undecidable generally) | Correct by construction |
| Sketching | Programmer's partial program | SAT over finite holes | Sound via CEGIS verifier |
| SyGuS | Grammar $G$ | Enumerative + SMT | Decidable fragments only |
| Pure CEGIS | Examples only | Adversarial iteration | Sound; termination not guaranteed |

### 4.4 LLM-guided and neuro-symbolic synthesis with verified oracles

The newest paradigm keeps the oracle but replaces the *proposal mechanism* with a learned model. **DreamCoder** [7] is the cleanest formulation: a wake–sleep Bayesian loop where the *wake* phase solves tasks by neurally-guided enumerative search and the *sleep* phase compresses solutions into a growing library of reusable abstractions (refactoring + dreaming). The neural network is a *prior* over programs; the symbolic search and the task specification remain the arbiters of truth. Later work (Stitch) showed that corpus-guided top-down abstraction learning can be 3–4 orders of magnitude faster than DreamCoder's deductive library learning while matching compression quality [7] — evidence that the *learning* component, not the search, was the bottleneck.

Large language models push this further: an LLM is an extraordinarily strong *proposal oracle* — it can emit plausible program candidates for open-ended specifications — but it is **unsound by construction**. The neuro-symbolic contract is therefore: *never trust the generator; always consult the verified oracle*. Architectures like MUSE/Ks2 for SemGuS problems embody this: the LLM proposes candidate verifiers or programs, while an enumeration-based synthesizer and a formal verifier do the actual semantic work — solving 135 SemGuS problems with logical specifications, 74 of which no prior SemGuS synthesizer could solve [8]. The cost is measurable: successful runs averaged 13.3 verification calls at ~2.1 seconds each — the verified oracle dominates runtime, exactly as the theory predicts.

In OGIS terms, the LLM is a new kind of oracle: $q_{\text{propose}}(\Phi, \text{context}) \to$ distribution over $\mathcal{C}$, with *no soundness obligation*. Soundness is recovered compositionally: $q_{\text{corr}}$ (SMT solver, type checker, test suite) filters proposals. This yields a crisp complexity picture:

> Theorem: Adding an unsound proposal oracle to CEGIS preserves soundness (every accepted program still passes $q_{\text{corr}}$) and can exponentially reduce the number of $q_{\text{corr}}$ queries when the proposal distribution concentrates on the target, but cannot improve the worst-case query complexity, which remains bounded below by the NP-hardness of minimum-query synthesis [6].

The practical consequence is the central engineering law of neuro-symbolic synthesis: **spend learning budget on the proposer, spend correctness budget on the verifier, and never confuse the two**.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We consolidate the theoretical and empirical record into five load-bearing results:

1. **General synthesis is $\Sigma^0_3$-complete.** The arithmetical hierarchy places full program synthesis three quantifier alternations up ($\exists$ program $\forall$ inputs $\exists$ execution) — far beyond verification ($\Pi^0_1$) or bug-finding ($\Sigma^0_1$) [5]. Every paradigm below this line buys tractability by surrendering generality.
2. **Generalization is uncomputable; CEGIS is suboptimal for it.** Extending finite-example correctness to full-domain correctness is $\Sigma^0_2$-complete [5]. CEGIS remains the practical workhorse, but the theory explains its failure modes: adversarial verifiers can force arbitrarily long counterexample sequences.
3. **Minimum-query synthesis is NP-hard.** For any oracle interface containing $q_{\text{corr}}$, finding the smallest counterexample set that identifies the target reduces from set cover [6]. Hence *example selection* — choosing which counterexamples to keep, as in [4] — is itself a hard optimization problem, and practical systems use greedy heuristics.
4. **Syntax buys decidability; competitions measure it.** SyGuS-Comp results [2] show solver portfolios (enumerative, stochastic, CEGIS-based, SMT-reduction) collectively solving 1500+ benchmarks across LIA/INV/PBE tracks, with CVC5's SyGuS mode solving grammar-conformant problems in under a second [8]. The frontier is sharp: decidability holds for single-invocation LIA and similar fragments; beyond them, solvers are best-effort.
5. **Learned proposers + verified oracles dominate the new frontier.** DreamCoder's library learning [7], Stitch's 1000× speedup on abstraction learning, and LLM-oracle SemGuS solvers (+74 previously unsolvable problems) [8] converge on one lesson: learning compresses the *proposal* distribution while verification anchors *soundness*. The verification-call budget, not the model size, is the binding constraint.

A unifying proof sketch for the OGIS reduction: given any of the five paradigms, define $\mathcal{C}$ as its hypothesis space and $\mathcal{O}$ as its query interface; the paradigm's main loop is then an OGIS learner strategy. Soundness of each paradigm follows from soundness of its $q_{\text{corr}}$-equivalent oracle; completeness (where claimed) follows from the finiteness or decidability of $\mathcal{C}$ under the paradigm's restrictions. The converses fail exactly at the known frontiers above.

## 6 Limitations

This unified view has sharp edges. First, the OGIS reduction is *descriptive*, not *prescriptive*: knowing that sketching is OGIS with hole-instantiation oracles does not tell a practitioner where to put the holes. The human-factors question — how programmers actually write effective sketches, grammars, or prompts — is largely unstudied and dominates real-world outcomes.

Second, our treatment of deductive synthesis is thinner than the others because its modern literature is fragmented across proof assistants and refinement-type systems; a full formal reduction of tactic-based synthesis to OGIS query vocabularies remains open.

Third, the empirical claims about LLM-guided synthesis rest on rapidly moving ground: model capabilities, proposal distributions, and even the definition of "verified oracle" (fuzzing? bounded model checking? full proof?) shift yearly. The complexity bounds we derive (preserved soundness, unchanged worst-case query complexity) are robust to this churn; the *constants* — 13.3 verification calls today — are not.

Fourth, we assume specifications are *fixed*. In practice, specifications are discovered interactively (the user rejects a "correct" program because the spec was wrong). Counterexample-guided *specification refinement* — where $q_{\text{corr}}$ answers are negotiated rather than authoritative — lies outside all five paradigms and outside OGIS as currently formulated.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Program synthesis looks like five fields but is one: a learner and an oracle, negotiating over queries, inside a hypothesis space whose shape determines everything. CEGIS taught us to split finite induction from unbounded verification [1,4]. SyGuS taught us that syntax is a decidability instrument and that competitions convert theory into engineering [2,3]. Sketching taught us that a programmer's partial insight plus a SAT solver beats either alone [1]. Deductive synthesis taught us that proofs are programs, at the price of proof search. And neuro-symbolic synthesis [7,8] is teaching us the final lesson: let the learned model *propose*, let the verified oracle *dispose*, and keep a ledger of which one you are paying.

The decidability frontiers — $\Sigma^0_3$-completeness of synthesis [5], uncomputability of generalization [5], NP-hardness of minimum-query learning [6] — are not obituaries. They are a map. Every practical synthesizer is a route drawn on that map, trading generality for decidability, autonomy for guidance, learning for proof. The next decade belongs to systems that draw the route dynamically: LLMs proposing, solvers verifying, libraries compressing, and counterexamples steering — the CEGIS loop, elevated from an algorithm to an architecture.
