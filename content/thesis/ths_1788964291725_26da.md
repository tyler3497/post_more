# Metagenomic Assembly and Strain-Resolved Binning: Long-Read Overlap Graphs, Compositional Signatures, and MAG Quality Assessment

## Abstract

Metagenomic sequencing recovers genomes directly from complex communities without cultivation, but reconstruction quality is governed by the interplay of assembly graph resolution, strain-level variation, and binning fidelity. This thesis unifies the three pillars of genome recovery: **assembly** (de Bruijn and overlap paradigms), **binning** (compositional and coverage signals), and **quality assessment** (marker-gene standards and taxonomic placement). We contrast short-read de Bruijn assemblers — *metaSPAdes* [1] and *MEGAHIT* [2] — with long-read overlap-layout-consensus approaches — *hifiasm-meta* [3] and *metaFlye* [4] — showing how read length transforms repeat resolution from an information-theoretic impossibility into a tractable problem. We formalize binning as clustering in a joint composition–coverage embedding, survey *MetaBAT2* [5], *CONCOCT* [6], and *VAMB* [7], and derive lower bounds on strain deconvolution. Finally, we develop the *CheckM* [8]/*CheckM2* [9] single-copy-marker framework, the *MIMAG* quality tiers [11], and *GTDB* taxonomic classification [10], closing with a candid account of what remains unsolved: microdiversity, mobile elements, and eukaryotic dark matter.

## 1 Introduction

A gram of soil or a milliliter of seawater contains thousands of co-occurring microbial genomes. *Shotgun metagenomics* sequences this mixture indiscriminately, yielding a bag of reads drawn from an unknown number of unknown genomes at unknown relative abundances. The computational problem is twofold: **assembly** — stitching reads into contiguous sequences (contigs, then scaffolds); and **binning** — partitioning contigs into groups that approximate single genomes, yielding metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs).

For a decade, the workhorse was short-read assembly via de Bruijn graphs (dBGs), exemplified by metaSPAdes [1] and MEGAHIT [2]. These tools reconstruct consensus sequences efficiently, but repeats longer than the read length (or the k-mer size) collapse into tangled graph regions, and closely related *strains* — genomes differing by a handful of single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) per kilobase — merge into chimeric consensus paths. The arrival of accurate long reads (PacBio HiFi) and overlap-layout-consensus (OLC) metagenome assemblers such as metaFlye [4] and hifiasm-meta [3] has pushed the field from consensus contigs toward **strain-resolved** assemblies, where individual haplotypes are separately reconstructed.

Parallel to assembly, binning matured from compositional signatures (GC content, tetranucleotide frequencies) into principled probabilistic models that fuse *composition* with *differential coverage* across samples: CONCOCT [6], MetaBAT2 [5], and the deep-learning VAMB [7]. Bin quality is policed by single-copy marker-gene estimators (CheckM [8], CheckM2 [9]), standardized by the MIMAG checklist [11], and contextualized phylogenetically by GTDB-Tk [10].

This thesis argues that assembly, binning, and quality control are not sequential pipeline stages but coupled inference problems over the same underlying data: the assembly graph. We develop that view rigorously, with theorems, algorithms, and a working code sketch.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The de Bruijn paradigm

In dBG assembly, each read is decomposed into overlapping k-mers; k-mers are nodes (or edges, depending on convention) and the genome corresponds to an Eulerian path through the graph. Repeats induce *branches* and *bubbles*: a repeat of length ≥ k with distinct flanking sequences creates a collapsed node with multiple in/out edges — the classic *repeat-resolution problem*. In metagenomes, repeats are both intra-genomic (rRNA operons, transposons, CRISPR arrays) and *inter-genomic* (conserved regions shared across community members), so graph tangles are dense. MetaSPAdes [1] addresses this with iterative multi-k assembly, strain-aware bubble collapsing, and metagenomic-specific coverage normalization; MEGAHIT [2] trades some sensitivity for memory efficiency via succinct dBGs, enabling assembly of terabase-scale datasets on commodity hardware.

### 2.2 Overlap-layout-consensus

OLC assembly instead builds an *overlap graph* between reads, then lays out paths and derives consensus. Historically too expensive for short reads, OLC became the natural choice for long reads: a read spanning a repeat unambiguously anchors both flanks. MetaFlye [4] adapts the repeat-graph framework of Flye to metagenomes with uneven coverage; hifiasm-meta [3] extends the hifiasm string graph to separate haplotypes using the low error rate of HiFi reads, achieving near-complete circular bacterial chromosomes directly from communities.

### 2.3 Binning signals

Binning exploits two approximately genome-invariant signals:

- **Compositional signatures.** Oligonucleotide usage (most commonly tetranucleotide frequencies, TNF) is conserved within a genome but varies between taxa, acting as a genomic fingerprint [6].
- **Coverage profiles.** Contigs from the same genome share abundance; across multiple samples, their *differential coverage* vectors are highly correlated [5].

Binning is therefore a clustering problem in a joint feature space, subject to biological constraints (e.g., contigs containing the same single-copy marker gene should rarely co-occur in one bin).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 De Bruijn vs. overlap graphs under strain heterogeneity

Consider a community containing two strains of one species diverging at *d* SNVs per kilobase. In a dBG with k-mer size *k*, each SNV creates a bubble of length roughly *k*; at *d* ≈ 5/kb and *k* = 55, bubbles overlap and merge into an unresolvable tangle — the assembler must choose between *collapsing* strains into a mosaic consensus (losing strain identity) or *fragmenting* contigs at every bubble (losing contiguity). MetaSPAdes [1] navigates this with "strain contigs" that preserve dominant paths while reporting variants; MEGAHIT [2] collapses more aggressively, favoring contiguity.

Overlap graphs behave qualitatively differently. With reads of length *L* ≫ inter-SNV distance, each read samples a *haplotype segment*; overlaps between reads from different strains carry the SNV differences, so the graph naturally separates into strain-specific paths wherever read length and divergence jointly permit. The phase transition occurs at *L* · *d* ≳ 1: when a typical read captures at least one strain-distinguishing variant, phasing becomes possible [3]. This is why HiFi reads (L ≈ 10–15 kb, error < 0.5%) are transformative: for typical bacterial strains (*d* ≈ 1–50/kb), *L·d* ≫ 1 holds comfortably, and hifiasm-meta [3] recovers dozens of circular, strain-resolved chromosomes per sample.

> **Theorem:** (Repeat-resolution lower bound). *Let G be a de Bruijn graph of order k over a metagenome containing a repeat R of length |R| ≥ k whose copies are flanked by distinct unique sequences. No assembler observing only k-mer adjacency can uniquely determine the correct traversal through R; the number of candidate traversals grows as ∏ᵢ mᵢ, where mᵢ is the copy multiplicity at tangle i. Resolution requires auxiliary information — reads spanning the repeat, paired-end links, or long-range (Hi-C) contacts — whose span exceeds |R|.*

*Proof sketch.* The collapsed repeat node has in-degree and out-degree equal to the number of distinct flanking contexts. Any Eulerian traversal pairing in-edges to out-edges is locally consistent with all k-mer evidence; choosing among them demands information beyond k-mer adjacency, i.e., sequences longer than the repeat. ∎

The practical corollary: short-read assemblers *cannot* resolve repeats longer than the insert span, full stop — they can only guess (scaffolding heuristics) or stop (contig breaks). Long reads convert the bound into an engineering target: sequence reads longer than the longest repeat class of interest.

### 4.2 Compositional signatures and the coverage embedding

Why do tetranucleotide frequencies identify genomes? Oligonucleotide usage reflects genome-wide mutational and selective pressures (codon bias, restriction-modification avoidance, replication strand asymmetry), which are approximately stationary within a genome [6]. The 256-dimensional TNF vector (4⁴, often reduced to 136 by reverse-complement collapsing) is therefore a stable fingerprint — but a weak one: related species overlap, and short contigs give noisy estimates (variance ∝ 1/length).

Coverage rescues composition. In a single sample, contigs from one genome share mean depth (modulo GC bias and stochasticity); across *S* samples, each contig maps to a point in ℝˢ, and contigs of one genome form a tight cluster under the shared abundance vector [5]. Binning is thus *clustering in a concatenated feature space* ℝ^{TNF} × ℝ^{coverage}, and modern binners differ mainly in how they model that space's geometry.

The following sketch computes TNF profiles and performs a minimal coverage-composition binning, illustrating the core signal before the probabilistic machinery of [5, 6, 7]:

```python
import numpy as np
from itertools import product
from collections import Counter

KMERS = [''.join(p) for p in product('ACGT', repeat=4)]
KIDX = {k: i for i, k in enumerate(KMERS)}
RC = str.maketrans('ACGT', 'TGCA')

def canonical(kmer):
    rc = kmer.translate(RC)[::-1]
    return min(kmer, rc)

# collapse to 136 reverse-complement pairs
CANON = sorted({canonical(k) for k in KMERS})
CIDX = {k: i for i, k in enumerate(CANON)}

def tetranucleotide_freqs(seq):
    seq = seq.upper()
    counts = Counter()
    n = 0
    for i in range(len(seq) - 3):
        k = seq[i:i+4]
        if set(k) <= set('ACGT'):
            counts[CIDX[canonical(k)]] += 1
            n += 1
    v = np.zeros(len(CANON))
    for i, c in counts.items():
        v[i] = c
    return v / n if n else v  # 136-dim compositional fingerprint

def bin_contigs(contigs, n_bins=5, iters=50, seed=0):
    """Toy binning: k-means over [TNF | log-coverage] features."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    X = np.array([np.concatenate([tetranucleotide_freqs(c['seq']),
                                  [np.log1p(c['cov'])]]) for c in contigs])
    X = (X - X.mean(0)) / (X.std(0) + 1e-9)
    cent = X[rng.choice(len(X), n_bins, replace=False)]
    for _ in range(iters):
        d = ((X[:, None, :] - cent[None]) ** 2).sum(-1)
        lab = d.argmin(1)
        new = np.array([X[lab == b].mean(0) if (lab == b).any() else cent[b]
                        for b in range(n_bins)])
        if np.allclose(new, cent):
            break
        cent = new
    return lab  # bin assignment per contig

# contigs = [{'seq': 'ACGT...', 'cov': 42.0}, ...]
# labels = bin_contigs(contigs)
```

Production binners replace this sketch with calibrated models: CONCOCT's variational Gaussian mixtures with automatic relevance determination [6], MetaBAT2's medoid refinement constrained by single-copy genes and paired-end graph topology [5], and VAMB's autoencoder that learns a nonlinear latent embedding where Euclidean distance separates genomes [7]. The sketch is honest about the shared foundation: *geometry in composition–coverage space*.

### 4.3 Strain-level resolution and SNV graphs

Strain resolution asks for more than binning: given a species bin, separate its constituent haplotypes and call the SNVs distinguishing them. Two regimes exist. In **read-backed phasing**, SNVs are linked when co-observed on single reads; the problem reduces to clustering reads by haplotype — tractable when *L·d* ≳ 1, as in §4.1. In **assembly-graph strain decomposition**, bubbles in the dBG/overlap graph are retained rather than collapsed, and strain paths are extracted as alternative traversals. MetaFlye [4] implements a version of this by keeping strain variants in its repeat graph; hifiasm-meta [3] phases at the string-graph level using HiFi accuracy to distinguish true variants from errors.

A subtle failure mode is *cross-strain chimerism*: when two strains share a long identical region, the graph merges there, and paths exiting the shared segment can be misassigned. Formally, this is the same repeat-resolution bound as the Theorem above, with "repeat" replaced by "shared haplotype block." The only cure is reads (or links) spanning the shared block — hence the empirical rule that strain resolution quality tracks *read length relative to the strain divergence landscape*, not raw coverage.

### 4.4 MAG quality assessment and the MIMAG proposition

A bin is a hypothesis; *CheckM* tests it with lineage-specific single-copy marker genes [8]. **Completeness** = fraction of expected markers observed; **contamination** = fraction observed in multiple copies (indicating merged genomes). CheckM2 [9] replaces lineage placement with a machine-learning predictor trained across the tree of life, improving estimates for novel lineages. Taxonomic assignment then proceeds via GTDB-Tk [10], which places MAGs into the Genome Taxonomy Database reference tree using concatenated marker alignments and relative evolutionary divergence.

The community standard for reporting is MIMAG [11]:

> **Theorem:** (MIMAG quality proposition). *A MAG satisfying completeness ≥ 90% and contamination ≤ 5% (CheckM/CheckM2 estimates) qualifies as **high-quality**; additionally requiring the presence of 23S, 16S, and 5S rRNA genes and ≥ 18 tRNAs elevates it to **high-quality draft** approaching finished-genome status. Medium-quality requires ≥ 50% completeness and < 10% contamination. These tiers are necessary but not sufficient: marker-based estimates are blind to strain chimerism, mobile-element misbinning, and assembly errors in marker-free regions.*

*Justification.* Single-copy markers sample the genome sparsely (~1 per 50–100 kb); a chimeric bin fusing two strains of one species carries one copy of most markers and passes contamination filters undetected. MIMAG tiers therefore bound *gene-content* quality, not *haplotype* fidelity — the gap motivating strain-resolved assembly [3, 4]. ∎

In practice, the pipeline is: bin → CheckM2 completeness/contamination → filter to MIMAG tiers → GTDB-Tk classification → dereplicate at 95% ANI into species clusters. Only then do biological conclusions follow.

## 5 Empirical / Proofs

**Benchmark behavior (CAMI and mock communities).** On the CAMI strain-madness challenge, long-read assemblers recovered an order of magnitude more strain-resolved circular contigs than short-read dBG tools at matched depth, consistent with the §4.1 phase transition [3, 4]. For binning, VAMB [7] and MetaBAT2 [5] consistently top precision–recall curves on multi-sample datasets, with VAMB's advantage largest on closely related species where linear TNF geometry under-separates. CONCOCT [6] remains competitive when the number of clusters is well-estimated, but degrades under misspecified K.

**Empirical validation of the repeat bound.** Across 200+ human-gut HiFi assemblies, contig N50 plateaus once median read length exceeds the 95th percentile of repeat lengths (~7 kb for insertion sequences and rRNA operons) — exactly the span predicted by the Theorem: each additional kilobase of read length resolves a further slice of the repeat-length distribution, with diminishing returns beyond it [3].

**Worked proof of the MIMAG proposition's insufficiency.** Construct a bin merging two strains at 99.5% ANI. Marker genes: each strain contributes one copy; the merged bin shows ~100% completeness, ~0–2% contamination (only strain-divergent markers duplicate). GTDB-Tk [10] assigns one species. Yet the bin is a mosaic: SNV analysis reveals two haplotypes. The marker framework *provably* cannot detect this — it is outside the estimand. Strain-resolved assembly [3] is the only remedy, confirming the proposition's "necessary but not sufficient" clause.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Microdiversity below the resolution limit.** Strains diverging at *d* with *L·d* < 1 remain unphasable; most soil and ocean communities live in this regime.
2. **Mobile elements.** Plasmids and phages have coverage decoupled from their hosts and compositional signatures of their own; they are systematically misbinned or lost, distorting the mobilome.
3. **Eukaryotes and viruses.** Marker-gene frameworks [8, 9] are bacterial/archaeal; eukaryotic MAGs lack standardized quality tiers, and viral binning is a separate discipline.
4. **Chimeric contigs.** Misassemblies propagate silently into bins; no current quality estimator detects chimeric *contigs*, only chimeric *bins*.
5. **Reference bias.** GTDB-Tk [10] placement degrades for deep-branching novel lineages; "unclassified" MAGs accumulate without phylogenetic homes.

## 7 Conclusion

Metagenomic genome recovery has crossed a threshold: from fragmented consensus contigs to circular, strain-resolved chromosomes, enabled by long reads that defeat the repeat-resolution bound rather than surrendering to it [3, 4]. Binning has matured into geometric inference in composition–coverage space [5, 6, 7], policed by marker-gene statistics [8, 9] and standardized by MIMAG [11] and GTDB [10]. Yet the field's central tension persists — *completeness versus correctness*: every method trades one against the other, and marker-based quality control cannot see the haplotype-level errors that matter most. The next decade belongs to methods that unify assembly and binning into joint inference over the assembly graph, where a bin is not a bag of contigs but a *path*, and quality is measured not by marker counts but by the probability that the reconstructed genome is the true one.
