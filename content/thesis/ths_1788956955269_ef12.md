# QUIC and the Modern Transport Layer: 0-RTT Handshakes, Connection Migration, Loss Detection, and BBR Congestion Control at Scale

## Abstract

The Transmission Control Protocol served as the Internet's workhorse for four decades, yet its kernel residency, handshake latency, and ossified wire image have become structural liabilities. This thesis provides a rigorous treatment of QUIC, the UDP-encapsulated transport that relocates congestion control, loss detection, stream multiplexing, and cryptographic state into user space while binding the TLS 1.3 handshake directly into packet protection. We analyze four interlocking subsystems: 0-RTT session resumption and its replay-safety envelope, connection migration via connection-ID routing and path validation, the RFC 9002 loss-detection machinery of packet thresholds and probe timeouts, and BBR, the model-based congestion controller that replaces loss signals with explicit bandwidth-delay product estimation. Drawing on the original SIGCOMM'17 deployment study, the RFC 8999/9000/9001/9002 standards corpus, and the BBR literature, we evaluate empirical results at Internet scale, present formal correctness arguments, and delineate the protocol's remaining limitations: ossification pressure, middlebox interference, 0-RTT replay exposure, and fairness open questions.

## 1 Introduction

Transport-layer innovation on the public Internet has historically been *slow, painful, and frequently impossible*. TCP, standardized in the early 1980s, resides in operating-system kernels whose upgrade cycles are measured in years, and its wire image — sequence numbers, flags, options — is parsed, rewritten, and occasionally mangled by a vast population of middleboxes. Empirical measurement has shown that middleboxes block or corrupt previously unseen TCP extensions at rates that make protocol evolution a multi-year negotiation with the installed base [1]. This phenomenon, termed **protocol ossification**, is the central motivating problem of the modern transport layer.

QUIC (originally "Quick UDP Internet Connections") was conceived at Google beginning in 2012 as a radical response: implement the entire transport above UDP in user space, encrypt nearly everything on the wire, and integrate the cryptographic handshake with the transport handshake so that connection establishment costs a single round trip — or zero, on resumption [1]. By 2017, Google's deployment carried approximately 7% of all Internet traffic, with measured reductions of 3.6–8% in Search latency and 15–18% in YouTube rebuffering [1]. The Internet Engineering Task Force chartered a working group in 2016 to standardize the design, culminating in May 2021 in a four-RFC suite: RFC 8999 (version-independent invariants) [5], RFC 9000 (the core transport) [2], RFC 9001 (TLS integration) [3], and RFC 9002 (loss detection and congestion control) [4].

This thesis examines four pillars of the QUIC architecture that jointly redefine what a transport protocol can do:

1. **0-RTT handshakes**, in which a returning client transmits application data in its very first flight, reusing resumption secrets derived from a prior TLS 1.3 session.
2. **Connection migration**, in which a connection survives changes of the client's IP address and port — across NAT rebinding, Wi-Fi to cellular handoff — by routing on *connection IDs* rather than the classic 4-tuple.
3. **Loss detection**, a redesigned recovery subsystem with per-packet-number spaces, packet-number thresholds, time thresholds, and the Probe Timeout (PTO) mechanism that subsumes TCP's retransmission timer.
4. **BBR congestion control**, a model-based controller that estimates bottleneck bandwidth and round-trip propagation time to operate at Kleinrock's optimal operating point, replacing the loss-driven control laws that have governed TCP since 1988 [6][7].

We proceed as follows. Section 2 surveys the background: TCP ossification, the gQUIC-to-IETF-QUIC evolution, and the invariants that make QUIC version-negotiable. Section 3 describes our methodology — a combination of standards analysis, formal reasoning about protocol state machines, and synthesis of published measurement studies. Section 4 develops the deep technical dive across the four pillars. Section 5 presents empirical results and formal guarantees. Section 6 enumerates limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The ossification crisis

By the mid-2010s, deploying a new TCP option or a new transport protocol number on the open Internet had become an exercise in futility. Middleboxes — firewalls, NATs, load balancers, intrusion-detection systems — make *assumptions* about the protocols they carry, and packets that violate those assumptions are dropped, "normalized," or misrouted [1]. The result is that the wire image of TCP is effectively frozen: any field a middlebox can see becomes a field that cannot change. QUIC's answer is twofold. First, run over UDP (protocol 17), whose wire image middleboxes largely leave alone. Second, encrypt as much of the QUIC header as possible — packet numbers are *header-protected* with a dedicated cipher, and nearly all transport parameters travel inside the TLS-encrypted handshake [3]. The small set of fields that must remain visible for version negotiation and routing is codified as the **version-independent invariants** of RFC 8999, a deliberately minimal contract with the network [5].

### 2.2 From gQUIC to IETF QUIC

Google's original design (gQUIC) used a bespoke cryptographic handshake, QUIC-Crypto, which itself pioneered 0-RTT resumption and later influenced TLS 1.3 [1]. The IETF working group made three decisive changes. First, it replaced QUIC-Crypto with **TLS 1.3 carried in CRYPTO frames**, so the handshake messages are ordinary TLS records fragmented across QUIC frames, while QUIC performs packet protection itself using keys the TLS stack derives [3]. Second, it cleanly separated the transport layer from the HTTP mapping (which became HTTP/3, RFC 9114). Third, it redesigned the wire format, introducing long and short headers, variable-length connection IDs, and a formal packet-number space separation [2]. Independent implementation studies confirm that the resulting specification, while complex, attracted an unprecedented number of interoperating implementations during its development [8].

### 2.3 Architectural overview

A QUIC connection is identified not by the 4-tuple but by a set of **connection IDs** (CIDs), opaque byte strings up to 20 bytes long chosen by each endpoint [2][5]. Packets are organized into three **packet number spaces** — Initial, Handshake, and Application Data — each with its own monotonically increasing packet numbers and its own cryptographic keys; crucially, packet numbers are never reused across spaces, which eliminates the retransmission ambiguity that has plagued TCP since its inception [4]. Streams provide ordered, reliable byte-stream delivery multiplexed over the connection, so the loss of one stream's packets no longer blocks the others — the head-of-line blocking elimination that motivated the protocol [1][2]. Flow control operates at both stream and connection granularity, and congestion control is pluggable, with RFC 9002 specifying a NewReno-compatible baseline while explicitly permitting BBR-style model-based controllers [4][6].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds on three complementary tracks. **First, standards analysis**: we trace normative requirements through RFCs 8999–9002, with attention to the *reasons* behind design decisions — why three packet number spaces exist, why the PTO fires per packet-number space, why migration requires explicit path validation. **Second, formal reasoning**: we express key protocol mechanisms as state machines and invariants, including a TLA+-style sketch of the handshake key schedule and a proof sketch of PTO liveness. **Third, empirical synthesis**: we aggregate published measurement results — Google's Internet-scale deployment data [1], independent QUIC implementation studies [8], and the BBR evaluation literature including reproducible measurement campaigns [6][7][9][10] — to ground the design in observed behavior rather than specification optimism. We additionally implemented small executable models (Section 4.4) to validate our understanding of BBR's gain-cycling dynamics numerically.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 0-RTT Handshakes and the TLS 1.3 Key Schedule

The classical TCP+TLS 1.2 establishment costs three round trips before application data flows: TCP SYN/SYN-ACK/ACK, then the TLS handshake. QUIC collapses this by fusing the transport and cryptographic handshakes. A fresh connection completes in **1-RTT**: the client's Initial flight carries a TLS ClientHello in CRYPTO frames; the server's response carries ServerHello through Finished; and by the time the client's second flight arrives, 1-RTT keys are active in both directions [3].

The more radical case is **0-RTT resumption**. A client that has previously completed a handshake with a server possesses a *resumption ticket* (a TLS PSK) and the server's transport parameters. It may immediately transmit application data — STREAM frames carrying, e.g., an HTTP request — in its very first UDP datagram, encrypted under **0-RTT keys** derived from the resumption secret [3]. The TLS 1.3 key schedule, which QUIC imports wholesale, derives a hierarchy of secrets:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE QuicKeySchedule ------------------------------
\* Sketch of the RFC 9001 key schedule as a state machine over encryption levels.
EXTENDS Naturals, Sequences

VARIABLES level,       \* "initial" | "early" | "handshake" | "application"
          trafficSecret,
          packetNumber

Init ==
  /\ level = "initial"
  /\ trafficSecret = HKDF_Extract(initial_salt, client_destination_cid)
  /\ packetNumber = 0

Next ==
  \/ /\ level = "initial"       \* server Hello received: Handshake keys
     /\ level' = "handshake"
     /\ trafficSecret' = DeriveSecret(handshake_secret, "quic hs traffic")
  \/ /\ level = "initial"       \* resumption ticket present: 0-RTT keys
     /\ level' = "early"
     /\ trafficSecret' = DeriveSecret(resumption_master_secret, "quic early data")
  \/ /\ level \in {"handshake", "early"}
     /\ level' = "application"  \* TLS Finished: 1-RTT keys
     /\ trafficSecret' = DeriveSecret(master_secret, "quic application traffic")

Liveness == <> (level = "application")
=============================================================================
```

Several subtleties make 0-RTT safe *enough* for deployment while bounding its risk:

- **No forward secrecy for early data.** The 0-RTT keys derive from a long-lived resumption secret; compromise of that secret retroactively exposes early data. RFC 9001 therefore restricts 0-RTT to data the application deems replay-safe [3].
- **Replay attacks are inherent.** An attacker can capture a client's first flight and replay it. TLS 1.3 mitigates this with single-use tickets and server-side strike registers, but the fundamental guarantee is weaker than 1-RTT: applications must ensure 0-RTT requests are *idempotent* (safe to execute twice) [3].
- **Transport parameter memory.** The client remembers the server's transport parameters (flow-control limits, max datagram sizes) from the prior connection and applies them to 0-RTT packets; the server may *reduce* but never increase these limits relative to what it will advertise in the new handshake, preventing the client from exploiting stale, generous parameters [3].
- **0-RTT rejection.** If the server declines early data (e.g., ticket expired), it simply ignores the 0-RTT flight and the handshake falls back to 1-RTT; the client's 0-RTT STREAM data is retransmitted under 1-RTT keys without application involvement [3].

> **Theorem (0-RTT key separation):** Early-data keys, handshake keys, and 1-RTT keys are derived via distinct HKDF labels from distinct input secrets. Consequently, compromise of any one key epoch reveals nothing about the plaintext of any other epoch, except that the resumption secret links the early-data epoch to the prior session.

### 4.2 Connection Migration and Connection-ID Routing

In TCP, a connection *is* its 4-tuple (source IP, source port, destination IP, destination port). When a mobile client moves from Wi-Fi to cellular, its IP address changes, and every TCP connection breaks. QUIC severs this binding: the connection is identified by **connection IDs**, and endpoints route incoming packets by the Destination Connection ID in the header [2][5]. A client whose address changes simply continues sending from the new address; the server, recognizing the CID, associates the packets with the existing connection state.

Unrestricted migration would, however, be a security catastrophe — it would let an off-path attacker hijack connections by spoofing source addresses. RFC 9000 therefore imposes a disciplined migration protocol [2]:

1. **Server-side CIDs are issued, not chosen.** The server provides the client with multiple CIDs via `NEW_CONNECTION_ID` frames; the client uses a fresh, previously unused CID when probing a new path, so observers cannot trivially link the old and new paths.
2. **Path validation precedes use.** Before an endpoint sends substantial data on a new path, it must validate the path by exchanging `PATH_CHALLENGE` and `PATH_RESPONSE` frames containing unpredictable 64-bit payloads. This proves the peer is reachable at the claimed address and defeats address-spoofing attacks.
3. **Anti-amplification limits.** A server must not send more than 3× the bytes it has received from an unvalidated address, bounding the protocol's utility as a DDoS amplifier [2].
4. **Preferred addresses and NAT rebinding.** Servers may advertise a `preferred_address` transport parameter to steer clients onto a better path after the handshake, and ordinary NAT rebinding (same logical path, new port mapping) is handled transparently since the CID, not the port, is the connection key.

The following Rust sketch captures the essence of server-side migration handling — CID-keyed routing with a validation gate before the new path is trusted:

```rust
/// Minimal model of CID-routed connection lookup with path validation (RFC 9000 §9).
use std::collections::HashMap;

struct Connection {
    cids: Vec<ConnectionId>,
    validated_paths: Vec<SocketAddr>,
    pending_challenge: Option<u64>,
}

struct Endpoint {
    table: HashMap<ConnectionId, Connection>,
}

impl Endpoint {
    fn on_datagram(&mut self, dcid: &ConnectionId, src: SocketAddr, payload: &[u8]) {
        let conn = match self.table.get_mut(dcid) {
            Some(c) => c,
            None => return, // unknown CID: drop or stateless reset
        };
        if !conn.validated_paths.contains(&src) {
            // New path: must validate before trusting it (RFC 9000 §9.2).
            let challenge: u64 = rand::random();
            conn.pending_challenge = Some(challenge);
            self.send_path_challenge(src, challenge);
            return;
        }
        self.deliver(conn, payload);
    }

    fn on_path_response(&mut self, dcid: &ConnectionId, src: SocketAddr, echo: u64) {
        if let Some(conn) = self.table.get_mut(dcid) {
            if conn.pending_challenge == Some(echo) {
                conn.validated_paths.push(src); // path proven; migration complete
                conn.pending_challenge = None;
            }
        }
    }
}
```
## 5 Empirical Analysis and Formal Guarantees

The empirical record for QUIC is unusually strong for a protocol of its youth, because Google deployed it at scale *before* standardization and published the results [1]:

- **Latency.** Google Search over QUIC showed mean latency reductions of ~3.6% on desktop and ~8% on mobile relative to TCP+TLS, attributable primarily to the 1-RTT handshake and the elimination of head-of-line blocking [1].
- **Rebuffering.** YouTube sessions over QUIC experienced 15–18% fewer rebuffering events, a direct consequence of faster loss recovery and improved congestion response on variable mobile paths [1].
- **Scale.** By the time of the SIGCOMM'17 publication, QUIC carried roughly 7% of global Internet traffic — the largest deployment of a new transport protocol in history [1]. Subsequent independent studies document dozens of interoperating implementations and accelerating IETF-QUIC adoption post-RFC [8].

For BBR, the deployment evidence is equally striking: BBR on Google's B4 backbone improved throughput by *orders of magnitude* over CUBIC on high-BDP paths, and YouTube/ google.com serving with BBR reduced latency across five continents, most dramatically in developing regions with shallow buffers and lossy links [6]. Controlled reproductions corroborate the mechanism-level claims — fairness convergence among BBR flows, RTT-probe synchronization, and robustness to random loss — while also documenting the known failure modes: BBRv1's aggressiveness against CUBIC and RTT unfairness among heterogeneous flows [10]. BBRv2/v3 measurements show improved coexistence at some cost in absolute throughput, an explicit design trade [7][9].

Formally, the protocol's guarantees compose cleanly:

1. **Handshake authentication** reduces to TLS 1.3's: QUIC adds no new cryptographic assumptions beyond the key-derivation labels of RFC 9001 [3].
2. **Loss-detection correctness** follows from packet-number uniqueness: every ACK unambiguously identifies delivered packets, so the packet/time thresholds and PTO operate on exact information, unlike TCP's heuristic-laden recovery [4].
3. **Migration safety** follows from the path-validation theorem of Section 4.2 plus CID unpredictability: an attacker who cannot observe the path cannot forge validation or guess CIDs [2].
4. **Congestion-control stability** for BBR rests on the max/min filter estimation theory: with *BtlBw* as a 10-RTT max filter and *RTprop* as a 10-second min filter, the controller converges to a neighborhood of Kleinrock's operating point under stationary conditions, with ProbeBW cycling providing persistent excitation for tracking nonstationarity [6][7].

## 6 Limitations

No honest treatment of QUIC can omit its costs and open problems.

**Computational cost.** QUIC encrypts every packet and protects every packet-number header; per-packet AEAD operations in user space impose measurable CPU overhead relative to kernel TCP, particularly at tens of gigabits per second. Hardware offload (for UDP segmentation, receive-side coalescing, and eventually crypto) narrows but does not eliminate the gap, and small-packet workloads remain the worst case.

**Ossification, deferred.** Encrypting the wire image defeats *content*-based ossification, but UDP itself can be blocked or throttled: several enterprise and national networks rate-limit or deny UDP, and QUIC's fallback in such environments is necessarily TCP-based (HTTP/2 or HTTP/3-over-TCP proposals remain nonstandard). Moreover, the invariants of RFC 8999 [5] deliberately freeze a minimal wire image — a new, subtler ossification surface: middleboxes will inevitably begin parsing long headers, version fields, and CID lengths, and future versions must then negotiate around those expectations.

**0-RTT replay exposure.** The replay-safety envelope of Section 4.1 is a pragmatic compromise, not a solution. Applications that mistakenly mark non-idempotent operations as replay-safe inherit double-execution vulnerabilities, and server-side strike registers are operationally demanding at scale [3].

**Migration and privacy.** Connection IDs are long-lived identifiers; a passive observer who can link CIDs across paths can track users across network changes. RFC 9000 mandates CID rotation precisely for this reason, but the tension between routability (load balancers need stable CIDs, cf. QUIC-LB) and unlinkability is unresolved [2].

**Congestion-control fairness.** BBR's coexistence with loss-based controllers remains contested. BBRv1 demonstrably starves CUBIC on shared bottlenecks; BBRv2/v3 improve fairness but sacrifice some of BBRv1's throughput advantage and introduce more parameters whose interactions are incompletely understood [7][9][10]. The broader question — whether the Internet should converge on model-based control or retain loss-based norms — is as much economic and political as technical.

**Middlebox cooperation.** QUIC's encrypted transport headers blind the very middleboxes that perform legitimate functions: performance-enhancing proxies on satellite links, enterprise traffic classifiers, and lawful-interception infrastructure. The spin bit (RFC 9000 §17.4) exposes a minimal RTT signal as a concession, but the architectural conflict between end-to-end encryption and in-network optimization persists [2][8].

**Implementation complexity.** The specification suite spans four RFCs plus extensions (QPACK, datagrams, multipath drafts); independent implementation surveys document substantial behavioral diversity and lingering interoperability hazards despite the protocol's maturity [8].

## 7 Conclusion

QUIC represents the most significant rethinking of the Internet transport layer since TCP's congestion-control algorithms were introduced: user-space deployment defeating ossification, cryptographic and transport handshakes fused into 0-RTT/1-RTT establishment, connections decoupled from network addresses via connection IDs, loss detection rebuilt on unambiguous packet-number semantics, and congestion control liberated from the loss signal by BBR's explicit path model. The empirical record — single-digit latency wins at Google scale [1], order-of-magnitude throughput gains on high-BDP paths [6], and a thriving multi-implementation ecosystem [8] — validates the architecture. Yet the protocol also concentrates new responsibilities at the endpoints: CPU-intensive per-packet cryptography, replay-aware application design, privacy-conscious CID management, and congestion-control citizenship in a mixed BBR/CUBIC Internet. The transport layer's next decade will be defined less by whether QUIC succeeds — it already has — than by how these tensions resolve: in hardware offload, in BBR's standardization, in multipath extensions, and in the ongoing negotiation between encryption and the middle. The narrow waist held for forty years; QUIC is the first credible proposal for what replaces it.
