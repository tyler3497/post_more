# Certified Robustness of Deep Classifiers: Randomized Smoothing, Lipschitz Constraints, and Convex Relaxation Certificates

## Abstract

Adversarial examples expose a brittle gap between the empirical accuracy of deep classifiers and the guarantees demanded by safety-critical deployment. This thesis develops a unified treatment of *certified* adversarial robustness: three principled families of methods that replace the cat-and-mouse game of heuristic defenses with provable guarantees. We analyze **randomized smoothing**, which converts any base classifier into a certifiably $\ell_2$-robust smoothed classifier with a tight radius derived from the Neyman–Pearson lemma [1]; **Lipschitz-constrained networks**, in which spectral normalization, orthogonal parametrizations, and gradient-norm-preserving activations yield margin-based certificates by construction [2][3][6]; and **convex-relaxation certificates**, which bound the adversarial polytope with linear programs, interval arithmetic, or zonotope propagation and verify prediction invariance exactly [4][7][5]. We compare the tightness, scalability, and training methodology of the three families, formalize their theoretical relationships, survey empirical certification results on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and ImageNet, and identify open problems at the frontier: certified robustness at scale, multi-norm guarantees, and the persistent tension between clean accuracy and certifiable radius.

## 1 Introduction

The discovery that imperceptible perturbations of an input can flip the prediction of an otherwise highly accurate neural network — *adversarial examples* — inaugurated one of the most consequential research programs in modern machine learning. Defenses proposed in response have historically been *empirical*: they demonstrate robustness against a particular battery of attacks, only to be broken by stronger or adaptive ones. This cycle of attack and defense, often called the *robustness arms race*, motivates a fundamentally different objective: instead of testing a model against known attacks, we seek a *certificate* — a mathematical proof that **no** perturbation within a specified threat model can alter the model's decision on a given input.

A certification procedure produces a radius $R(x) \geq 0$ such that $\|\delta\|_p < R(x) \Rightarrow F(x+\delta) = y$ for *every* perturbation $\delta$ — the *certified robust radius*. A method is *sound* if the guarantee always holds and *tight* if no larger radius is provably valid from the information available. Every sound technique must over-approximate the *adversarial polytope* — the set of reachable final-layer activations — and its tightness is governed by the fidelity of that approximation.

This thesis studies three families of certified defenses that embody distinct philosophies of certification:

1. **Randomized smoothing** — a *black-box, post-hoc* method that needs no architectural assumptions: any base classifier $f$ is transformed into a smoothed classifier $g$ whose decision under Gaussian noise is provably stable [1]. Its lineage traces to the differential-privacy-based PixelDP defense [2].
2. **Lipschitz-constrained networks** — a *by-construction* approach: the network's sensitivity is bounded globally by a Lipschitz constant $L$, so a prediction margin directly yields a certificate [3][6].
3. **Convex-relaxation certificates** — *verification-oriented* methods that propagate sound over-approximations (intervals, zonotopes, or linear-programming bounds) of the adversarial polytope through the network [4][7][5].

Each family trades guarantee strength, certification cost, scalability, and clean accuracy differently. Understanding these trade-offs — and the mathematical structure linking the families — is this thesis's central objective.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The adversarial perturbation model

An *adversarial perturbation* is a $\delta$ with $\|\delta\|_p \leq \epsilon$ such that $F(x + \delta) \neq y$. *Empirical* robust accuracy measures survival against a finite attack suite (e.g., projected gradient descent); *certified accuracy* — the fraction of inputs provably free of any adversarial $\delta$ — lower-bounds the true robust accuracy.

Two distinctions structure the field:

- **Post-hoc certification vs. certified training.** Post-hoc certification verifies a fixed, trained model (e.g., randomized smoothing applied to any base classifier). Certified training instead optimizes a *certifiable upper bound* on the robust loss during training, so that the final model is robust by design (e.g., interval-bound-propagation training [4]).
- **$\ell_p$ norm of the guarantee.** Randomized smoothing yields $\ell_2$ certificates; convex relaxations and Lipschitz methods can target $\ell_1$, $\ell_2$, or $\ell_\infty$, each with different difficulty profiles. The $\ell_\infty$ setting is the most studied but arguably the hardest for randomized methods, whose radii degrade as $\Omega(d^{1/p - 1/2})$ in dimension $d$ for $p > 2$.

### 2.2 The margin and the sensitivity: two pillars of certification

Nearly all certification reduces to a contest between two quantities:

- **The prediction margin**, $M(x, y) = f_y(x) - \max_{k \neq y} f_k(x)$, measuring how decisively the network prefers the correct class;
- **The network's sensitivity**, measuring how fast the output can change with the input — either a global Lipschitz constant, or a *local*, input-dependent bound on the perturbation's effect (e.g., the width of a propagated interval).

A certificate exists whenever the margin exceeds the worst-case sensitivity-induced change:

> **Theorem (Margin–sensitivity certificate):** Let $f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^K$ be the logit map of $F$ with $\ell_p$-Lipschitz constant $L$. If $M(x, y) > 0$, then $F(x + \delta) = y$ for all $\|\delta\|_p < M(x, y) / (2L)$.

This single inequality explains why Lipschitz-constrained networks certify *by construction* (they control $L$) and why convex relaxations certify *by verification* (they compute a local, often tighter, bound on how much logits can shift). Randomized smoothing takes a third route: it constructs a new classifier $g$ whose *effective* sensitivity is controlled by the smoothing noise itself, and whose margin is estimated statistically.

### 2.3 Early certified defenses

The first scalable certified defense was PixelDP [2], which connected *differential privacy* to robustness: a DP mechanism is, almost by definition, insensitive to adversarial perturbations. Lecuyer et al. certified $\ell_2$ attacks of size $0.1$ on roughly 60% of ImageNet (Inception) predictions at a cost of about 8–9 points of clean accuracy. PixelDP's bounds were *loose*, and randomized smoothing [1] soon superseded it with a *tight* analysis that bypasses the DP formalism entirely.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Randomized smoothing: tight $\ell_2$ certificates for arbitrary classifiers

#### 4.1.1 The smoothed classifier

Given *any* base classifier $f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathcal{Y}$ (possibly nondifferentiable, non-Lipschitz, arbitrarily complex), randomized smoothing defines the *smoothed classifier*

$$
g(x) \;=\; \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{Y}}\; \mathbb{P}_{\delta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)}\bigl[f(x + \delta) = c\bigr].
$$

The noise scale $\sigma$ controls a fundamental trade-off: larger $\sigma$ yields larger certified radii but degrades the base classifier's accuracy under noise, shrinking the certified *accuracy* (predictions that are both correct and certified).

#### 4.1.2 The tight certificate



> **Theorem (Cohen–Rosenfeld–Kolter, 2019):** The smoothed classifier $g$ satisfies $g(x + \delta) = c_A$ for all $\|\delta\|_2 < R$, where
>
> $$
> R \;=\; \frac{\sigma}{2}\Bigl(\Phi^{-1}(p_A) - \Phi^{-1}(\overline{p_B})\Bigr),
> $$
>
> and $\Phi^{-1}$ is the inverse standard-normal CDF. The bound is *tight*: for any $R' > R$ there exists a base classifier consistent with $(p_A, \overline{p_B})$ for which the guarantee fails.



#### 4.1.3 From differential privacy to smoothing



#### 4.1.4 A complete certification routine



```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm, beta

def certify(base_classifier, x, sigma, n0=100, n=100_000, alpha=0.001):
    """Certify the smoothed prediction of base_classifier at x.

    Returns (predicted_class, certified_radius). Radius 0.0 means ABSTAIN.
    """
    # Stage 1: identify the most probable class with n0 samples
    counts0 = np.zeros(num_classes)
    for _ in range(n0):
        noise = np.random.normal(0, sigma, size=x.shape)
        counts0[base_classifier(x + noise)] += 1
    c_hat = int(np.argmax(counts0))

    # Stage 2: lower-bound P(f(x+eps) = c_hat) with n samples
    counts = np.zeros(num_classes)
    for _ in range(n):
        noise = np.random.normal(0, sigma, size=x.shape)
        counts[base_classifier(x + noise)] += 1
    n_c = int(counts[c_hat])

    # Clopper-Pearson lower confidence bound on the binomial proportion
    p_lower = beta.ppf(alpha, n_c, n - n_c + 1) if n_c > 0 else 0.0

    if p_lower < 0.5:
        return c_hat, 0.0  # ABSTAIN: cannot certify
    radius = sigma * norm.ppf(p_lower)
    return c_hat, radius
```

Two facts make this routine practical: certification is *embarrassingly parallel* over samples, requiring only forward passes through $f$, and the confidence parameter $\alpha$ trades soundness probability against radius. The dominant cost is inference-time sampling — certifying one ImageNet image with $n = 10^5$ samples demands batch parallelism — which is the method's main operational drawback.

### 4.2 Lipschitz-constrained networks: certificates by construction

#### 4.2.1 The margin bound

If the logit map $f$ is globally $L$-Lipschitz in the $\ell_2$ norm, then for any perturbation $\|\delta\|_2 \leq \epsilon$,

$$
|f_c(x+\delta) - f_c(x)| \;\leq\; L\,\epsilon \qquad \forall\,c,
$$

so the prediction is unchanged whenever $2L\epsilon < M(x, y)$ — the certified radius $M(x,y)/(2L)$. This margin–sensitivity inequality explains the two deterministic families: Lipschitz networks control $L$ *by construction*, while convex relaxations compute a *local*, input-dependent bound on how far logits can shift.

A naive bound uses the product of spectral norms: for $f = f_K \circ \cdots \circ f_1$ with 1-Lipschitz activations, $L(f) \leq \prod_i \|W_i\|_2$. This bound is *valid* but often *vacuous*, exceeding the true constant by orders of magnitude and annihilating the certificate.

#### 4.2.2 Enforcing small spectral norms

Two strategies constrain the spectral norms during training:

- **Regularization (Parseval networks).** Penalize $\|W^\top W - I\|_F$ so that $\|W\|_2 \approx 1$ while preserving capacity — the network stays *nonexpansive* without hard constraints.
- **Hard normalization.** After each step rescale $W \leftarrow W / \|W\|_2$ (spectral norm via power iteration), guaranteeing $L \leq 1$ *by construction*.

Tsuzuku, Sato, and Sugiyama [3] built a complete framework on this idea: layer-wise spectral norms via power iteration (efficient even for convolutions) plus a *Lipschitz-margin training* objective that grows the margin while shrinking the constant — scaling to large networks because certification reduces to estimating a few spectral norms.

#### 4.2.3 The expressivity problem and its resolution

A subtle and important discovery: merely constraining each layer's spectral norm *cripples* the network's expressivity. Anil, Lucas, and Grosse [6] proved that norm-constrained ReLU networks cannot approximate even simple 1-Lipschitz functions such as the absolute value — the constraint induces *gradient-norm attenuation*, so the network cannot realize functions of slope 1 almost everywhere. Their resolution is elegant:

> **Theorem (Anil–Lucas–Grosse, 2019):** A norm-constrained network in which every layer is *gradient-norm-preserving* (GNP) — i.e., $\|\nabla_x h(x)\| = \|\nabla_{h(x)}\|$ almost everywhere — combined with the GroupSort activation (which sorts groups of pre-activations and is 1-Lipschitz and GNP), is a *universal approximator* of Lipschitz functions.

GroupSort replaces ReLU: it sorts each group of pre-activations instead of zeroing negatives, preserving the gradient norm through the nonlinearity. Paired with semi-orthogonal weight matrices ($W^\top W = I$, enforced by Björck orthonormalization or the Cayley transform), the network is exactly 1-Lipschitz *by construction* while retaining full expressivity — the basis of the strongest modern Lipschitz-based certified defenses.

#### 4.2.4 Strengths and structural limits

The Lipschitz approach is *architecture-native*: once trained, certification is nearly free and deterministic. Its weakness is *globality* — a single constant $L$ must bound sensitivity at *every* input, so one pathological region degrades certificates everywhere, whereas convex relaxations compute tighter *local* bounds at far greater cost.

### 4.3 Convex-relaxation certificates: bounding the adversarial polytope

#### 4.3.1 The convex outer adversarial polytope

Consider an $\ell_\infty$ perturbation set $B_\epsilon(x)$. Propagating it through a ReLU network yields the nonconvex *adversarial polytope*. Wong and Kolter [7] proposed a *convex outer approximation*: each ReLU $z = \max(0, \hat{z})$ with $\hat{z} \in [l, u]$ is relaxed to the convex hull of its graph — the triangle with vertices $(l, 0)$, $(0, 0)$, $(u, u)$. The worst-case logit margin over this relaxed polytope is a *linear program*, whose *dual* can be represented as a modified backward pass — making robust training tractable. The result: a convolutional MNIST classifier with *provably* less than 5.8% test error under *any* $\ell_\infty$ attack with $\epsilon = 0.1$ [7].

#### 4.3.2 Interval bound propagation: simplicity that scales

Interval Bound Propagation (IBP) is the coarsest — and fastest — convex relaxation: the perturbation set is over-approximated by an axis-aligned box, propagated layer by layer with interval arithmetic. For an affine layer $z = W\hat{z} + b$ with $\hat{z} \in [\underline{z}, \overline{z}]$,

$$
\underline{z}_i = \sum_j \bigl(W_{ij}^+ \underline{z}_j + W_{ij}^- \overline{z}_j\bigr) + b_i,
\qquad
\overline{z}_i = \sum_j \bigl(W_{ij}^+ \overline{z}_j + W_{ij}^- \underline{z}_j\bigr) + b_i,
$$

where $W^+ = \max(W, 0)$, $W^- = \min(W, 0)$; monotone activations pass bounds through elementwise. A prediction is certified when the true class's logit lower bound exceeds every other class's upper bound — at a cost of roughly *two* forward passes.

Gowal et al. [4] showed that while IBP bounds are weak for *arbitrary* networks, training with an IBP-based robust loss — a mixture of the clean loss and the loss on the *adversarial logits* $\hat{z}_K$ (true class at its lower bound, others at their upper bounds), with the perturbation radius $\epsilon$ and mixing coefficient $\kappa$ annealed during training — makes the network *adapt so that the IBP bound becomes tight*. This produced the state-of-the-art verified accuracy of its era: 2.23% verified error on MNIST ($\epsilon = 0.1$), 8.05% at $\epsilon = 0.3$, and 72.91% on CIFAR-10 ($\epsilon = 8/255$) [4]. The lesson: *the relaxation need not be tight in general; it suffices that training drives the network into the regime where it is tight.*

#### 4.3.3 The relaxation hierarchy

Convex relaxations form a strict hierarchy of tightness versus cost:

| Relaxation | Representation per neuron | Tightness | Cost per layer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **IBP** (interval/box) | $[\underline{z}, \overline{z}]$ | coarsest | $\sim$2 forward passes total |
| **DeepPoly / CROWN** (zonotope/polytope) | linear bounds $a^\top x + b$ | medium | forward + backward pass |
| **Triangle (convex hull)** [7] | full ReLU convex hull | tight per-neuron | LP dual ≈ backward pass |
| **Exact (MILP / branch-and-bound)** | exact polytope | complete | exponential worst case |

Each level strictly dominates the one above in tightness and is strictly dominated in cost. The choice of relaxation is therefore a choice of position on the *tightness–scalability Pareto frontier*. Modern verifiers (e.g., branch-and-bound with learned branching) climb this hierarchy adaptively: cheap bounds first, exact solving only where cheap bounds fail.

### 4.4 Comparing the three families

The families differ in where they place the burden of certification:

| Axis | Randomized smoothing [1] | Lipschitz networks [3][6] | Convex relaxations [4][7] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Guarantee norm | $\ell_2$ (Gaussian) | $\ell_1, \ell_2, \ell_\infty$ | $\ell_1, \ell_2, \ell_\infty$ |
| Soundness | probabilistic ($1-\alpha$) | deterministic | deterministic |
| Tightness | *tight* for the smoothed classifier | loose (global bound) | hierarchy: loose→complete |
| Architecture assumptions | none (black box) | 1-Lipschitz layers required | piecewise-linear preferred |
| Certification cost | $10^4$–$10^5$ forward passes | 1 forward pass | 2 forward passes (IBP) → LP |
| Training cost | Gaussian augmentation (cheap) | spectral constraints (moderate) | bound propagation (2–10×) |
| Scales to ImageNet | **yes** (first certified defense there) | partially | partially (IBP, downscaled) |

Three unifying observations emerge. **First**, randomized smoothing's certificate is *tight* — unmatched at scale — but it certifies a *randomized* classifier, only in $\ell_2$. **Second**, Lipschitz certificates are *global* while convex relaxations are *local*: the former pay in tightness, the latter in compute. **Third**, all three are the same abstract operation — over-approximating the adversarial polytope — differing only in *where* the approximation is placed: around the classifier's statistics (smoothing), the function class (Lipschitz), or the activation geometry (relaxations).

## 5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 What the certificates actually achieve

Representative certified-accuracy results from the literature (*certified* = correctly classified **and** provably robust):

| Method | Dataset | Norm | Budget | Certified accuracy | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Randomized smoothing ($\sigma{=}0.25$) | CIFAR-10 | $\ell_2$ | $r = 0.25$ | $\approx$ 60% | [1] |
| Randomized smoothing ($\sigma{=}0.5$) | CIFAR-10 | $\ell_2$ | $r = 0.5$ | $\approx$ 43% | [1] |
| Randomized smoothing ($\sigma{=}0.5$) | ImageNet | $\ell_2$ | $r = 0.5$ | 49% (top-1) | [1] |
| PixelDP | ImageNet (Inception) | $\ell_2$ | $L = 0.1$ | $\approx$ 60% of predictions | [2] |
| Convex outer polytope | MNIST | $\ell_\infty$ | $\epsilon = 0.1$ | 94.2% (5.8% err.) | [7] |
| IBP training | MNIST | $\ell_\infty$ | $\epsilon = 0.1$ | 97.8% (2.23% err.) | [4] |
| IBP training | MNIST | $\ell_\infty$ | $\epsilon = 0.3$ | 92.0% (8.05% err.) | [4] |
| IBP training | CIFAR-10 | $\ell_\infty$ | $\epsilon = 8/255$ | $\approx$ 27% (72.91% err.) | [4] |

Three patterns stand out. **Randomized smoothing is the only method with nontrivial certificates on full-scale ImageNet** (49% certified top-1 at $\ell_2$ radius $0.5$), where relaxations are computationally out of reach and Lipschitz bounds vacuous. **Convex-relaxation training dominates small-$\epsilon$ $\ell_\infty$ MNIST**, verifying the vast majority of test inputs. **No method achieves high certified accuracy on CIFAR-10 at $\epsilon = 8/255$** — IBP's $\approx$27% shows how fast relaxation bounds degrade as networks deepen.

### 5.2 The accuracy–robustness trade-off, quantified

Every certified defense pays for its guarantee in clean accuracy, and the price differs by family:

1. **Smoothing** pays through the noise scale $\sigma$: larger $\sigma$ grows the radius linearly ($R = \sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(p_A)$) but forces the base classifier to classify *noisy* inputs, so clean accuracy falls monotonically. The practitioner tunes $\sigma$ to maximize certified accuracy *at the target radius*.
2. **Lipschitz training** pays through the constant $L$: shrinking $L$ widens $M/(2L)$ but restricts the function class, and GroupSort networks, universal *in principle* [6], are harder to optimize than ReLU networks.
3. **IBP training** pays through early-training looseness: the $\epsilon$-annealing schedule of [4] exists because starting at full $\epsilon$ makes the IBP loss vacuous and training collapses — a *curriculum on the certificate itself*.

A unifying lens is sample complexity: certifiable robustness demands more data, more compute, or both, because the certificate must rule out a continuum of perturbations rather than fit a finite training set.

### 5.3 Theoretical guarantees: what is proven, what is merely observed

It is worth separating *theorems* from *empirical phenomena*:

- **Proven:** the smoothing radius is tight for the information used [1]; the margin–sensitivity certificate is sound whenever the Lipschitz bound is valid [3]; the triangle relaxation is the tightest *per-neuron* convex relaxation [7]; GroupSort networks are universal Lipschitz approximators [6].
- **Observed:** IBP training drives bounds to tightness [4]; Gaussian augmentation suits smoothing, and adversarial training of the smoothed classifier can improve it further.

The gap between the two lists is where the field's open problems live.

## 6 Limitations

**Randomized smoothing.** The guarantee is *probabilistic*, *norm-specific* ($\ell_2$ for Gaussian noise; $\ell_\infty$ radii provably degrade with dimension), and *expensive at inference* ($10^5$ forward passes per input). It certifies the *smoothed* classifier $g$, not the base classifier $f$ — and tightness holds only *given $(p_A, \overline{p_B})$*, saying nothing about better base classifiers.

**Lipschitz-constrained networks.** The global bound is inherently pessimistic, and product-of-spectral-norms bounds compound exponentially with depth. Training 1-Lipschitz networks remains finicky — orthogonal parametrizations add overhead and GroupSort's sorting costs more than ReLU.

**Convex relaxations.** Tightness and scalability oppose each other: IBP is cheap but coarse; the triangle relaxation's LP dual scales poorly to very deep networks; exact verification is exponential. Relaxations are also *architecture-sensitive* — sharpest for piecewise-linear networks, degraded by smooth activations and attention mechanisms.

**Shared limitations.** All three families certify against *norm-bounded* perturbations, which imperfectly capture real adversaries (rotations, semantic edits, physical-world attacks lie outside every $\ell_p$ ball). Certificates are *pointwise*, and a 99.9%-confidence certificate applied to a million inputs will, in expectation, be wrong a thousand times — a fact that matters for safety cases built on per-input guarantees.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Certified adversarial robustness has matured from theoretical curiosity into a discipline with sharp theorems, scalable algorithms, and honest empirical accounting. Randomized smoothing [1] showed that *any* classifier can be made certifiably robust with a tight, black-box guarantee — still the only approach demonstrated at ImageNet scale. Lipschitz-constrained networks [3][6] build robustness into the architecture, with certificates costing a single forward pass once the expressivity problem is solved via gradient-norm preservation. Convex relaxations [4][7] bound and optimize the adversarial polytope directly, yielding the strongest $\ell_\infty$ guarantees on small benchmarks and a principled tightness–scalability hierarchy.

The three families are complementary answers to one question — *where should the over-approximation live?* — and recent work combines them: Lipschitz bounds tightening convex relaxations, smoothing applied to 1-Lipschitz base classifiers, relaxation-trained networks certified by smoothing. The frontier: certificates that scale to frontier models, guarantees beyond $\ell_p$ balls, and a principled understanding of the price of robustness.
