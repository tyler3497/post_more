# Sieve Methods in Analytic Number Theory: the Selberg Sieve, the Bombieri–Vinogradov Theorem, and Bounded Gaps Between Primes

## Abstract

Sieve methods convert the multiplicative problem of detecting primes into a variational problem over auxiliary weights, and no episode displays this transformation more dramatically than the bounded gaps story. This article traces the arc from Selberg's 1947 quadratic-form sieve — whose exact diagonalization yields the sharpest general upper bounds in the subject — through the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem, which grants the primes an average level of distribution of one half over moduli, to the weighted sieves of Goldston, Pintz and Yıldırım. We explain Zhang's 2013 breakthrough, which pushed past the one-half barrier for smooth moduli and proved that gaps below seventy million recur infinitely often, and the multidimensional Maynard–Tao weights, which eliminated the need for any distribution beyond one half. The Polymath8b numerical optimization then drove the unconditional bound to 246, with 6 under the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture. Along the way we develop the Selberg diagonalization in full, the parity barrier that limits every sieve, and the deep analytic inputs — Kloosterman sums and Deligne's Riemann hypothesis over finite fields — that power the modern results.

##1 Intro

The prime number theorem tells us that the *average* gap between consecutive primes near *x* is about log *x*, yet it says almost nothing about the *individual* gaps that actually occur. Are there infinitely many pairs of primes separated by exactly 2 — the twin prime conjecture? By at most 100? By at most some fixed bound *H*? Until 2013, even the last and weakest of these questions was completely open: it was conceivable, for all anyone could prove, that prime gaps tend to infinity, with liminf *(p_{n+1} − p_n) = ∞*.

> **Conjecture:** *(Prime k-tuple conjecture, Hardy–Littlewood).* Let *H = {h_1, …, h_k}* be an *admissible* set of integers — meaning that for every prime *p*, the residues *h_i* mod *p* do not cover all classes mod *p*. Then there are infinitely many integers *n* such that *n + h_1, …, n + h_k* are simultaneously prime, and indeed the count of such *n ≤ x* is asymptotic to *𝔖(H) x / (log x)^k*, where *𝔖(H)* is the singular series.

The twin prime conjecture is the case *H = {0, 2}*. This conjecture is far beyond current methods, so analytic number theory adopted a humbler strategy: rather than producing *k* simultaneous primes, detect *some* pair of primes inside a long admissible tuple. If a tuple of diameter *H* contains two primes infinitely often, then prime gaps of size at most *H* recur infinitely often. This reduction — from a pointwise conjecture to a *statistical* one — is what made the problem tractable, and it is entirely a creation of sieve theory.

This article develops that machinery: §2 recalls Brun's sieve, the sifting function, levels of distribution and Bombieri–Vinogradov; §3 constructs the Selberg sieve and the Goldston–Pintz–Yıldırım weights; §4 gives four deep dives — the Selberg diagonalization, Zhang's smooth-moduli breach of one half, why one-dimensional weights stalled, and the Maynard–Tao multidimensional sieve; §5 covers the numerics, §6 the limitations led by the *parity barrier*, and §7 the road ahead.

##2 Background

The oldest sieve is Eratosthenes': cross out multiples of each prime *p ≤ √x*. Legendre made this an *identity* via inclusion–exclusion, but with *2^{π(z)}* terms it is useless as an estimate. Brun's 1919 breakthrough was to *truncate* inclusion–exclusion to terms with few prime factors: the truncated sums bound the sifting function above and below. He used this to prove the sum of reciprocals of twin primes converges [3], and — more importantly — to give the first *upper bounds of the correct order of magnitude* for prime patterns.

The modern setup is as follows. Let *A* be a finite set of integers to be sifted, *P* a set of sifting primes, and *z* the sifting level. The *sifting function*

*S(A; P, z) = #{a ∈ A : (a, P(z)) = 1},   P(z) = ∏_{p < z, p ∈ P} p*

counts survivors. For each squarefree *d* composed of sifting primes, write *A_d = {a ∈ A : d | a}* and assume a decomposition

*|A_d| = X · ω(d)/d + r_d*,

where *X* approximates *|A|*, *ω* is a multiplicative *density* with *0 ≤ ω(p) < p* — e.g. *ω(p) = 1* for primes in an interval, *ω(p) = 2* for twin primes at odd *p* — and *r_d* is a remainder. The *dimension* *κ*, defined by *∑_{p ≤ z} ω(p) log p / p = κ log z + O(1)*, measures sifting aggressiveness; prime-detecting sieves have *κ = 1*.

Everything hinges on the remainders *r_d*. Pointwise bounds are hopeless; instead one asks for *average* control over moduli *d ≤ x^θ*. This is the *level of distribution*:

> **Definition:** The primes have *level of distribution θ* if, for every *A > 0*,
> *∑_{q ≤ x^θ} max_{(a,q)=1} |π(x; q, a) − π(x)/φ(q)| ≪_A x / (log x)^A*,
> where *π(x; q, a)* counts primes *p ≤ x* with *p ≡ a* mod *q*.

The foundational result is:

> **Theorem:** *(Bombieri–Vinogradov [2]).* The primes have level of distribution *θ* for every *θ < 1/2*. In words, the primes are as well distributed as the Riemann hypothesis would predict, *on average* over moduli, up to the square root of *x*.

The great open conjecture extends this to the full range:

> **Conjecture:** *(Elliott–Halberstam).* The primes have level of distribution *θ* for every *θ < 1*.

The number *1/2* in Bombieri–Vinogradov is the great watershed of the subject: essentially every sieve-theoretic result on prime patterns is either provable at level *1/2* or requires punching through it. The bounded gaps story is the story of that number.

Finally, we need the combinatorics of tuples. A set *H = {h_1, …, h_k}* is *admissible* if it misses at least one residue class modulo every prime *p*; otherwise *n + H* always contains a multiple of *p* and simultaneous primality is impossible. Let *ν_p(H)* be the number of occupied classes mod *p*. The *singular series*

*𝔖(H) = ∏_p (1 − ν_p(H)/p) · (1 − 1/p)^{−k}*

is the Hardy–Littlewood correction factor: the naive Cramér-model prediction *x/(log x)^k* multiplied by the local adjustments at each prime. For twins, *𝔖({0,2}) ≈ 1.32032*; we compute such constants numerically in §5.

| Sieve | Idea | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eratosthenes–Legendre | Exact inclusion–exclusion | Identity, hence sharp | *2^{π(z)}* terms — useless |
| Brun (1919) | Truncated inclusion–exclusion | First upper bounds of right order; twin reciprocal sum converges | Combinatorial explosion; weak constants |
| Selberg (1947) [1] | Quadratic weights, optimized | Best general upper bounds; Brun–Titchmarsh with constant 2 | Upper bounds only; parity barrier |
| Large sieve | Fourier duality over moduli | Bombieri–Vinogradov; average distribution | Capped at level *1/2* |
| GPY / Maynard–Tao | Weighted detection of prime pairs in tuples | Bounded gaps: 70M → 600 → 246 | Needs *θ > 1/2* (GPY) or heavy optimization (M–T) |

##3 Methodology

### The Selberg sieve

Selberg's idea [1] starts from the *fundamental inequality*: for any reals *λ_d* with *λ_1 = 1*, the survival indicator is majorized by a square — the inner sum equals *1* on survivors and the square is nonnegative regardless — so

*S(A; P, z) ≤ ∑_{a ∈ A} (∑_{d | a, d | P(z)} λ_d)².*

Set *λ_d = 0* for *d ≥ z* and *choose the remaining λ_d to minimize the right-hand side*. Expanding the square,

*∑_{d_1, d_2 < z} λ_{d_1} λ_{d_2} |A_{lcm(d_1, d_2)}| = X · Q(λ) + O(∑_{d < z², d | P(z)} 3^{ω(d)} |r_d|),*

where *Q(λ)* is the quadratic form

*Q(λ) = ∑_{d_1, d_2 < z} λ_{d_1} λ_{d_2} ω(l) / l,   l = lcm(d_1, d_2).*

The error term is controlled exactly when the remainders are small on average up to *z²* — this is where the level of distribution enters, capping *z ≤ x^{θ/2}*.

### The Goldston–Pintz–Yıldırım weights

To detect *pairs* of primes, Goldston, Pintz and Yıldırım [4] sift the *tuple* rather than the integers. Fix an admissible *H = {h_1, …, h_k}* and put *Q(n) = ∏_{i=1}^k (n + h_i)*. Their weight is a Selberg-style square,

*w(n) = (∑_{d | Q(n), d < R} μ(d) (log(R/d) / log R)^{k+ℓ})²,*

with parameters *R = N^{θ/2 − ε}* and an integer *ℓ ≥ 0* to be optimized. Form the sums over a dyadic range,

*S_1 = ∑_{N < n ≤ 2N} w(n),   S_2^{(m)} = ∑_{N < n ≤ 2N, n + h_m prime} w(n),   S_2 = ∑_{m=1}^k S_2^{(m)}.*

The *detection criterion* is a pigeonhole argument: *w(n) ≥ 0* always, so if every *n* contributed at most one prime among the *n + h_i*, we would have *S_2 ≤ S_1*. Hence

*S_2 / S_1 > 1  (for infinitely many N)  ⟹  some n + H contains two primes infinitely often,*

and the diameter of *H* then bounds the prime gap. The entire method is the asymptotic evaluation of the ratio *S_2/S_1*: *S_1* is a pure sieve main term, while each *S_2^{(m)}* needs primes in arithmetic progressions — i.e., a level of distribution *θ* — to evaluate its main term as *𝔖(H)* times an integral. GPY showed the ratio tends to a constant *M_k(θ)* exceeding *1* for large *k* whenever *θ > 1/2*, and deduced bounded gaps under any such *θ* [4]. Unconditionally, at *θ = 1/2*, they could only prove *liminf (p_{n+1} − p_n) / log p_n = 0*: gaps smaller than any fixed fraction of the average, but not bounded.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Diagonalization of the Selberg quadratic form

The miracle of Selberg's method is that *Q(λ)* can be *diagonalized exactly*. Writing the least common multiple via Möbius inversion and collecting terms, one finds multiplicative functions *f* and *g* with *f(p) = (p − ω(p))/ω(p)* such that

*Q(λ) = ∑_{d < z, d | P(z)} (1/f(d)) · y_d²,   y_d = ∑_{m < z, d | m} λ_m / g(m/d) · (Möbius factors),*

a sum of *independent* squares. Minimizing subject to the linear constraint *λ_1 = 1* is then a one-line Cauchy–Schwarz argument, giving the minimum value *1/J* with

*J = ∑_{d < z, d | P(z)} μ²(d) / f(d),*

attained at the *optimal weights*

*λ_d^opt = μ(d) · J_d / J,   J_d = ∑_{m < z, d | m, m | P(z)} μ²(m) / f(m).*

> **Theorem:** *(Selberg sieve [1][9]).* With notation as above,
> *S(A; P, z) ≤ X / J + ∑_{d < z², d | P(z)} 3^{ω(d)} |r_d|.*
> For the prime-detecting case *ω(p) = 1* one has *J* on the order of *log z*, recovering upper bounds of the correct magnitude with an explicit, optimized constant.

Conceptually, the Selberg weights are the *smoothest majorant* of the sifting indicator — a continuous optimization where Brun used combinatorial truncation — and their most famous corollary is the Brun–Titchmarsh theorem:

> **Theorem:** *(Brun–Titchmarsh).* For *1 ≤ q < x* and *(a, q) = 1*,
> *π(x; q, a) < 2x / (φ(q) · log(x/q)).*

The constant *2* is best possible by sieve methods alone — a first shadow of the parity barrier. Note the error sum runs over *d < z²*, so average control of remainders up to *x^θ* forces *z ≤ x^{θ/2}*; the Bombieri–Vinogradov value *θ = 1/2* is what makes the theorem nontrivial.

### 4.2 Breaching one half: Zhang's smooth moduli

For thirty years the GPY program was stuck: it provably needed *θ > 1/2*, and nobody could improve on Bombieri–Vinogradov for *all* moduli. Zhang's 2013 breakthrough [5] sidestepped the obstacle rather than removing it. The key was an observation of Motohashi and Pintz: in the GPY sieve, one may restrict the weight support to *smooth* moduli *d* — integers all of whose prime factors are small, below *N^δ* — at negligible cost to the main term.

The proof is a tour de force of the dispersion method: after Cauchy–Schwarz, the error becomes bilinear forms in Kloosterman-type sums — the *Type I, II, III* estimates — with the critical Type III case handled by hyper-Kloosterman correlation bounds from Deligne's Riemann hypothesis over finite fields.

> **Theorem:** *(Zhang [5]).* *liminf_{n → ∞} (p_{n+1} − p_n) < 7 × 10^7.* There are infinitely many pairs of consecutive primes less than seventy million apart.

Polymath8a [7] then optimized every component — narrow-tuple searches, a Bessel-function sieve cutoff (Farkas–Pintz–Révész), densely divisible moduli, sharper exponential sums — reaching level *1/2 + 7/300* and gap bound *4680*.

### 4.3 Why one-dimensional weights could never cross one half

A natural question: was Zhang's heavy analytic machinery *necessary*, or could a cleverer choice of the GPY polynomial *P* have squeezed bounded gaps out of *θ = 1/2* alone?

The intuition is geometric: the one-dimensional weight sees only the *product* *d = d_1 ··· d_k*, collapsing *k* degrees of freedom into one.

### 4.4 The Maynard–Tao multidimensional sieve and Polymath8b

In late 2013, Maynard [6] — and independently Tao — replaced the one-dimensional cutoff with a *multivariate* one:

*w(n) = (∑_{d_1 | n+h_1} ··· ∑_{d_k | n+h_k} μ(d_1···d_k) · F(log d_1/log R, …, log d_k/log R))²,*

where *F* is smooth and supported on the simplex *{t_i ≥ 0, ∑ t_i ≤ 1}*.

> **Theorem:** *(Maynard [6]).* *liminf_n (p_{n+1} − p_n) ≤ 600.* Moreover *liminf_n (p_{n+m} − p_n) < ∞* for every fixed *m*, and under the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture the bound improves to *12*.

The multidimensional weights were a qualitative leap, delivering three things at once:

- *arbitrarily many* primes in tuples, for any fixed count — not just pairs;
- *no distribution beyond θ = 1/2*: the classical Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem suffices;
- a *variational problem* for *F* solvable by numerical optimization.

The Polymath8b project [8] then industrialized the method — optimizing *F* over symmetric polynomials numerically and deploying the narrowest known admissible tuples from exhaustive computer search — to obtain, with *k = 50*:

> **Theorem:** *(Polymath8b [8]).* Unconditionally, *liminf_n (p_{n+1} − p_n) ≤ 246*; under the generalized Elliott–Halberstam conjecture the bound is *6*.

Very recently, Stadlmann [10] combined the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem with Zhang-type smooth-moduli estimates to shave the unconditional record to *240*, showing the story is not yet over.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### The detection criterion, proved

The logical core converting sieve asymptotics into prime gaps is a pigeonhole argument. Suppose for some admissible *H* of diameter *D* we have *S_2 > S_1* for infinitely many *N*. If every *n* in *(N, 2N]* had *at most one* prime among the *n + h_i*, each *n* would contribute to at most one *S_2^{(m)}*, giving *S_2 ≤ ∑_n w(n) = S_1* — contradiction. Hence some *n* has *two* of the *n + h_i* prime, at distance at most *D*, infinitely often. That the weights are *squares* — hence nonnegative — is what makes this step legal, and the quiet reason every successful approach uses Selberg-style quadratic weights.

### The numerical pipeline

Turning *M_k > 2* into an explicit *H* needs three computational ingredients:

1. **Admissible tuples.** Finding the narrowest admissible *k*-tuple is a combinatorial optimization — a covering-system problem attacked by greedy construction plus local search (notably Engelsma's tables, which Polymath8b mined for the diameter-*246* 50-tuple).
2. **The cutoff *F*.** The variational problem for *M_k* is solved numerically over symmetric-polynomial bases; Polymath8b's optimized *F* is a piecewise polynomial whose coefficients were tuned by large-scale computation.
3. **The sieve ratio.** With *θ = 1/2* fixed, one verifies *M_k > m + 1* to force *m + 1* primes in the tuple.

### Computing the singular series

The Hardy–Littlewood constants that govern tuple frequencies are infinite Euler products, but they converge rapidly enough to compute directly:

```python
def singular_series(H, primes, limit=100000):
    """Partial product for S(H) = prod_p (1 - nu_p(H)/p) * (1 - 1/p)^(-k)."""
    k = len(H)
    s = 1.0
    for p in primes:
        if p > limit:
            break
        nu = len({h % p for h in H})          # occupied residue classes
        s *= (1 - nu / p) * (1 - 1 / p) ** (-k)
    return s

# twins (0,2) -> 1.3203247... ; triplets (0,2,6) -> 2.8582555...
# prime quadruplets (0,2,6,8) -> 4.1512008...
for H in [(0, 2), (0, 2, 6), (0, 2, 6, 8)]:
    print(H, singular_series(H, primes_upto(100000)))
```

The partial product up to *10^5* gives *1.3203247* for twins — the twin prime constant — and *2.8582555* and *4.1512008* for the triplet and quadruplet constellations.

For contrast, the *additive* sieve that started it all is a one-liner in a lazy language:

```haskell
-- The sieve of Eratosthenes, as an infinite lazy stream.
primes :: [Integer]
primes = sieve [2..]
  where
    sieve (p:xs) = p : sieve [x | x <- xs, x `mod` p /= 0]
```

Elegant — and a reminder of why analytic sieves exist: this program *enumerates* primes, but proving anything about the *gaps* in its output requires the machinery above.

##6 Limitations

**The parity barrier.** The deepest limitation was identified by Selberg himself: every sieve weight here is a *square* — hence nonnegative — and therefore *blind to the parity of Ω(n)*, the number of prime factors of *n*. A sieve cannot distinguish a prime (*Ω = 1*) from a product of two primes (*Ω = 2*), so any lower bound it produces for primes is matched by an at-least-as-large count of almost primes. Sieves *cannot* prove the twin prime or Goldbach conjectures — not even under Elliott–Halberstam. Chen's theorem (every large even number is a prime plus a product of at most two primes) remains the closest they have come, precisely because it *accommodates* the barrier.

**The one-half wall.** The Bombieri–Vinogradov level *1/2* is the natural limit of the large sieve, and even GRH would not improve the *averaged* estimate sieves need. Zhang's breach works only for smooth moduli and at enormous cost — a *3.5-million*-entry tuple for a bound of *70 million* — because the exponential-sum estimates sit barely past the square-root cancellation threshold.

**Effectivity.** Maynard–Tao proves *existence* of bounded gaps but not where they occur; the parity barrier suggests *2* needs genuinely new ideas, likely fusing sieves with automorphic forms. The complementary *large* gaps problem fell in the same era (Ford–Green–Konyagin–Maynard–Tao), but small gaps remain the harder direction.

##7 Conclusion

In a century, sieve theory traveled from Brun's truncated inclusion–exclusion to a variational calculus over weight functions. Selberg taught us to *optimize* rather than truncate; Bombieri–Vinogradov to ask for distribution *on average*; GPY to *weigh* tuples toward prime pairs; Zhang that the one-half barrier has a smooth-moduli door; Maynard and Tao that *multidimensional* weights need no door at all; Polymath that the final constant is partly computational engineering.

The bound *246* is a waystation, not the end. The parity barrier stands as the central mystery — a proof that sieve weights, as currently conceived, *cannot* see primes alone. Whether twins fall to a parity-breaking sieve, a hybrid with automorphic methods, or something unforeseen, the arc from Brun to bounded gaps suggests the next breakthrough will again come from reimagining what a sieve is *for*.
