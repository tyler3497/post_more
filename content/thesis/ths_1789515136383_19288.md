# Streaming Algorithms: Frequency Moments, AMS Sketches, and Lower Bounds via Communication Complexity

## Abstract

Modern data systems routinely process streams far too large to store: network telemetry, click logs, and sensor feeds arrive at rates measured in millions of updates per second, forcing algorithms to operate in a single pass with memory that is polylogarithmic in the stream length. This thesis develops the theory of streaming algorithms for frequency-moment estimation, the canonical testbed of the field. We present the Alon–Matias–Szegedy sketch for the second frequency moment F2, whose analysis under 4-wise independence remains the paradigm for unbiased low-variance estimation; the Count-Min and Count sketches for point queries and heavy hitters; and the lineage of distinct-element estimators from Flajolet–Martin through HyperLogLog. We then turn to the Indyk–Woodruff optimal Fp estimators and the matching lower bounds showing these algorithms are essentially space-optimal, with hardness derived from one-way communication complexity via reductions from the Index and Gap-Hamming problems. Finally, we extend the picture to sliding windows through the smooth-histogram framework and survey exact-computation tradeoffs, delivering a unified account of what can and cannot be computed from a stream.

## 1 Introduction

Few computational settings are as unforgiving as the *data stream model*. A stream `a_1, a_2, ..., a_m` of items arrives one element at a time; the algorithm may examine each element once, in order, while holding only a tiny working memory — typically polylogarithmic in the stream length `m` and the universe size `n`. Storing the stream itself is forbidden. This restriction is not an academic contrivance: it models network routers that must summarize terabits of traffic per second, search engines that must count distinct queries over exabytes of logs, and database systems that must maintain statistics over relations that never stop growing.

The central objects of study in this model are the *frequency moments*. Given a frequency vector `f` in which `f_i` records how many times item `i` has appeared, the `k`-th frequency moment is

> **Definition:** The `k`-th frequency moment of a stream is `F_k = Σ_i f_i^k`, where `F_0` counts the number of distinct elements (with the convention `0^0 = 0`) and `F_∞ = max_i f_i` is the maximum frequency.

`F_1` is trivial — it is the stream length. `F_0`, `F_2`, and the higher moments are not. The breakthrough result of Alon, Matias, and Szegedy showed that `F_0` and `F_2` admit randomized one-pass approximations in polylogarithmic space, while every `F_k` with `k > 2` requires polynomial space — a sharp phase transition that earned the work the 2005 Gödel Prize [1]. Understanding *why* this transition occurs, *how* the upper bounds are achieved, and *what* the matching lower bounds look like is the central project of streaming algorithms.

This thesis develops that project in full. We begin with the model and its two principal variants — the *cash-register* model of insert-only streams and the *turnstile* model permitting deletions. We then present the core algorithmic machinery: the AMS sketch for `F_2` with its celebrated 4-wise-independence analysis, the Count-Min and Count sketches for point queries and heavy hitters, and the lineage of distinct-element estimators from Flajolet–Martin [2] through HyperLogLog [4]. We continue to the modern culmination of the upper-bound story, the near-optimal `F_p` estimators of Indyk and Woodruff [5], and then turn the tables: we prove that these algorithms are essentially the best possible, deriving space lower bounds from one-way communication complexity via reductions from the *Index* and *Gap-Hamming* problems [6][7]. We close the technical core by extending estimation to the *sliding-window* model, where only the most recent `W` items matter, through the smooth-histogram framework [9] and exact-computation tradeoffs [10].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The stream model and its variants

A data stream is a sequence of updates to an underlying frequency vector `f ∈ ℤ^n`, initially zero. In the **cash-register model** each update is an insertion `(i, +1)`; in the **turnstile model** each update is `(i, Δ)` with `Δ ∈ ℤ`, permitting both insertions and deletions, and in the *strict* turnstile model the vector never goes negative. A streaming algorithm processes updates sequentially, maintaining a state of `s` bits, and at the end (or at query time) produces an estimate of some function of `f`.

An algorithm `(ε, δ)`-approximates a quantity `Q` if it outputs `Q̃` satisfying

> **Definition:** `Pr[ (1 − ε)·Q ≤ Q̃ ≤ (1 + ε)·Q ] ≥ 1 − δ.`

Space is measured in bits as a function of `n`, `m`, `ε`, and `δ`. The gold standard is `poly(log n, log m, 1/ε, log 1/δ)` — sublinear, and ideally nearly independent of the stream size.

### 2.2 Linear sketches

A *linear sketch* is a (randomized) linear map `L` from the frequency vector to a small summary: `L(f) = S·f` for a random matrix `S`. Linearity is the property that makes sketches compatible with the turnstile model: an update `(i, Δ)` is handled by adding `Δ` times column `i` of `S`, so deletions are processed exactly as naturally as insertions. Every estimator in this thesis — AMS, Count-Min, Count, and the `p`-stable sketches of Indyk and Woodruff — is a linear sketch, and the lower-bound story of Section 5 can be read as the statement that linear sketches are, up to logarithmic factors, the *only* efficient one-pass algorithms.

### 2.3 Communication complexity

Lower bounds in streaming are proved by reduction from communication complexity. In the one-way model, Alice holds input `x`, Bob holds input `y`, Alice sends a single message of `s` bits to Bob, and Bob must compute a function of `(x, y)`. If a streaming algorithm uses `s` bits of memory, Alice can run it on her portion of a stream, send the memory state to Bob, and Bob can continue the execution on his portion — so `s` must be at least the one-way communication complexity of the induced problem. The two workhorse problems are:

1. **Index:** Alice holds a bitstring `x ∈ {0,1}^n`; Bob holds an index `i`; Bob must output `x_i`. One-way complexity: `Ω(n)` bits.
2. **Gap-Hamming-Distance:** Alice and Bob hold `x, y ∈ {0,1}^n` with the promise that the Hamming distance `Δ(x,y)` is either at most `n/2 − g` or at least `n/2 + g`; they must decide which. One-way complexity: `Ω(n)` bits for constant gap, and the scaled gap version yields `Ω(1/ε²)` lower bounds for approximation problems [7].

### 2.4 Hashing with limited independence

Full independence among hash values is expensive to store. Streaming algorithms therefore use *k-wise independent* hash families: a family `H` of functions `h : U → R` is k-wise independent if for any `k` distinct inputs, their images are uniformly and independently distributed over `R`. A degree-`(k−1)` polynomial over a finite field gives such a family with `O(k log n)` bits of storage. The AMS analysis needs only 4-wise independence — a striking economy that we exploit in Section 4.1.

## 3 Methodology

The methodology of this thesis is the methodology of the field itself, and it has three pillars.

**Pillar 1: Unbiased estimators with controlled variance.** Virtually every streaming estimator follows one template: construct a random variable `Z` with `E[Z] = Q` (the target quantity) and `Var(Z) ≤ C·Q²` for a modest constant `C`; average `s_1 = O(C/ε²)` independent copies to drive the relative standard deviation below `ε` (Chebyshev's inequality); then take the median of `s_2 = O(log 1/δ)` such averages to boost the success probability to `1 − δ` (Chernoff bounds). This *median-of-means* (or median-of-averages) construction converts any constant-factor estimator into an `(ε, δ)`-approximation with multiplicative overhead `O((1/ε²)·log(1/δ))`.

**Pillar 2: Linearity for the turnstile model.** All our sketches are linear in the frequency vector, so every update — insertion or deletion — is a rank-one modification of the sketch state. This single design decision is what lifts cash-register algorithms to the fully dynamic setting.

**Pillar 3: Reductions for lower bounds.** To prove a space lower bound of `Ω(g(ε, n))`, we embed a hard communication problem into a stream: Alice's input becomes the first half of the stream, Bob's the second half, and the streaming algorithm's memory state becomes the one-way message. Solving the streaming problem then solves the communication problem, so the streaming space is at least the communication complexity. Choosing Index gives `Ω(log n)`-style bounds; choosing Gap-Hamming with a carefully tuned gap gives the `Ω(1/ε²)` bounds that match the upper bounds of Pillar 1.

**Evaluation methodology.** Where the original literature offers theorems rather than benchmarks, we follow it: correctness is established by the variance and concentration calculations, optimality by the reductions. Section 5 additionally reports a small computational experiment confirming the AMS estimator's convergence behavior on synthetic streams.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The AMS Sketch: estimating F₂ with 4-wise independence

The AMS estimator for the second frequency moment is arguably the most influential single idea in streaming algorithms [1]. Let `h : U → {−1, +1}` be drawn from a 4-wise independent family, and maintain the single counter

```
Z = Σ_i f_i · h(i)
```

updated on stream element `j` by `Z ← Z + h(j)` (so deletions subtract). Then:

> **Theorem (AMS):** `E[Z²] = F_2` and `Var(Z²) ≤ 2·F_2²`.

*Proof sketch.* Expanding, `E[Z²] = Σ_{i,j} f_i f_j E[h(i)h(j)]`. For `i = j` the term is `f_i²`; for `i ≠ j`, pairwise independence gives `E[h(i)h(j)] = E[h(i)]·E[h(j)] = 0`. For the variance, `Var(Z²) = E[Z⁴] − E[Z²]²`, and expanding `Z⁴` produces terms `f_i f_j f_k f_l E[h(i)h(j)h(k)h(l)]`. By 4-wise independence, every term with an odd multiplicity vanishes, and the surviving terms are bounded by `2·(Σ_i f_i²)² = 2·F_2²`. ∎

Only *four*-wise independence is ever used — higher moments of the hash family never enter. This is why the sketch needs just `O(log n)` bits of randomness. Applying the median-of-means template with `s_1 = O(1/ε²)` counters per group and `s_2 = O(log 1/δ)` groups yields an `(ε, δ)`-approximation of `F_2` in `O((1/ε²)·log(1/δ)·log(mn))` bits of space. Since `Z` is linear in `f`, the algorithm works unchanged in the turnstile model.

A quarter-century later, the bound is essentially tight: Braverman and Zamir proved an `Ω(log(nε²)/ε²)` lower bound for `F_2` estimation for all `ε = Ω(1/√n)`, matching the classical AMS upper bound for `ε > n^{−1/2+c}` and supplying a refined algorithm matching the bound below that threshold [8].

### 4.2 Point queries and heavy hitters: Count-Min and Count sketches

Where AMS estimates a global norm, the **Count-Min sketch** answers *point queries* — estimating an individual frequency `f_i` [3]. It maintains a `d × w` array of counters with `d` independent hash functions `h_r : U → {1, ..., w}`. Update `(i, Δ)` increments counter `(r, h_r(i))` by `Δ` for every row `r`; the estimate is `f̃_i = min_r C[r, h_r(i)]`.

> **Theorem (Count-Min):** With `w = 2/ε` and `d = log(1/δ)`, with probability at least `1 − δ`: `f_i ≤ f̃_i ≤ f_i + ε·‖f‖_1`.

The estimate never undercounts (in the cash-register model) because collisions only add mass; the minimum over `d` rows suppresses the collision noise exponentially. The **Count sketch** (Charikar, Chen, Farach-Colton) trades the one-sided guarantee for unbiasedness: it multiplies by random signs and takes the *median* over rows, yielding `|f̃_i − f_i| ≤ ε·√F_2` with high probability — an `ℓ_2` guarantee that is much stronger for skewed distributions.

| Sketch | Guarantee | Space (counters) | Deletions |
|---|---|---|---|
| AMS | `F_2` within `1 ± ε` | `O(ε^{−2} log 1/δ)` | yes (linear) |
| Count-Min | `f_i` within `ε‖f‖_1`, one-sided | `O(ε^{−1} log 1/δ)` | yes |
| Count | `f_i` within `ε√F_2`, unbiased | `O(ε^{−2} log 1/δ)` | yes |

Heavy hitters — items with `f_i ≥ φ·‖f‖_1` — fall out immediately: enumerate candidate items and verify each with a point query.

### 4.3 Distinct elements: from Flajolet–Martin to HyperLogLog

Estimating `F_0`, the number of distinct elements, is the oldest streaming problem. Flajolet and Martin's 1985 algorithm hashes each distinct item into the unit interval and tracks the *minimum* hash value `m̂`; since `E[m̂] ≈ 1/(F_0 + 1)`, the reciprocal estimates the cardinality [2]. The single minimum has enormous variance, so the modern lineage replaces it with *stochastic averaging* over `m` registers:

1. **Flajolet–Martin (1985):** minimum hash; needs median-of-means over many instances.
2. **LogLog (Durand–Flajolet, 2003):** for each item compute `ρ`, the number of leading zeros of its hash; keep the maximum `ρ` per register; combine with a geometric mean. Standard error `1.30/√m`.
3. **HyperLogLog (2007):** same registers, but combined with the *harmonic* mean `m²/(Σ_j 2^{−M[j]})`, which dampens the influence of outlier registers. Standard error `1.04/√m` — provably near-optimal [4].

With `m = 2^p` registers of about 5 bits each, HyperLogLog estimates cardinalities beyond a billion to within 2% in roughly 1.5 kilobytes — the data structure behind production cardinality estimation at Google, Redis, and Meta.

### 4.4 Optimal F_p estimation and the Indyk–Woodruff program

For general `p`, Indyk and Woodruff gave the definitive upper bounds [5]: using sketches based on *p-stable distributions* — distributions closed under linear combination, so that `Σ_i f_i X_i` (with i.i.d.

The conceptual punchline is a trichotomy:

- **`0 < p ≤ 2`:** polylogarithmic space suffices, and the dependence on `ε` is `Θ(1/ε²)` — optimal [5][8].
- **`p > 2`:** any constant-pass algorithm needs `Ω(n^{1−2/p})` space [1] — the sketch must be nearly as large as the universe.
- **`p = 0`:** polylogarithmic space suffices, but the road to optimality ran through communication complexity, as Section 5 explains.

### 4.5 Sliding windows and smooth histograms

Real streams expire: a router cares about the last hour, not all of history.

For *smooth* functions — those whose value on a window cannot change much when a small suffix is dropped — Braverman and Ostrovsky's **smooth-histogram** technique maintains `O((1/β)·log W)` checkpoints, each running a standard whole-stream estimator on a suffix, and answers queries from the checkpoint whose suffix best approximates the window [9].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 The F₀ lower bound via Gap-Hamming

We now prove the flagship lower bound: any one-pass `(ε, δ)`-approximation of `F_0` needs `Ω(1/ε² + log m)` bits [6]. The `Ω(1/ε²)` term comes from a reduction from Gap-Hamming-Distance.

> **Theorem (Indyk–Woodruff):** For constant `δ`, any one-pass streaming algorithm that `(ε, δ)`-approximates `F_0` uses `Ω(min{1/ε², m})` bits of space, for `ε = Ω(m^{−1/(9+k)})` and any fixed `k > 0`.

*Reduction sketch.* Alice holds `x ∈ {0,1}^n`, Bob holds `y ∈ {0,1}^n`. Alice inserts item `i` into the stream for every position where `x_i = 1`; she then sends her memory state (the "message") to Bob, who inserts item `i` for every position where `y_i = 1` and queries the `F_0` estimate. The number of distinct elements equals the size of the union of the two supports, which encodes the Hamming distance: `|supp(x) ∪ supp(y)| = (‖x‖_1 + ‖y‖_1 + Δ(x,y))/2`. Distinguishing the two Gap-Hamming cases therefore reduces to approximating `F_0` to relative error `ε = Θ(g/n)`, where `g` is the gap. Since Gap-Hamming-Distance has one-way communication complexity `Ω(n)` — optimally characterized by Chakrabarti and Regev [7] — the streaming space must be `Ω(n)`, i.e., `Ω(1/ε²)` after reparametrization. ∎

The matching `Ω(log m)` term is elementary: the algorithm must at least count up to `m`. Together with the upper bounds, the landscape is now completely mapped:

| Problem | Upper bound (bits) | Lower bound (bits) | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| `F_0`, `(ε,δ)`-approx | `O(ε^{−2} log n log 1/δ)` [4][5] | `Ω(ε^{−2} + log m)` [6][7] | tight |
| `F_2`, `(ε,δ)`-approx | `O(ε^{−2} log(mn) log 1/δ)` [1] | `Ω(log(nε²)/ε²)` [8] | tight |
| `F_p`, `0<p<2` | `O(ε^{−2} log n log 1/δ)` [5] | `Ω(ε^{−2} + log n)` | tight |
| `F_k`, `k>2` | `O(n^{1−2/k} poly(1/ε, log n))` [1] | `Ω(n^{1−2/k})` [1] | tight |

### 5.2 Computational experiment: AMS convergence

To ground the theory, we simulated the AMS estimator on a synthetic stream of `m = 10^6` items over `n = 10^5` distinct keys with a Zipf(1.2) frequency distribution, using 4-wise independent sign hashes:

```python
import random

def ams_estimate(stream, n_groups=32, per_group=64):
    # 4-wise independent signs via degree-3 polynomial over a large prime
    P = 2**61 - 1
    def make_hash(a, b, c, d):
        return lambda x: 1 if (a*x**3 + b*x**2 + c*x + d) % P % 2 == 0 else -1
    group_means = []
    for _ in range(n_groups):
        vals = []
        for _ in range(per_group):
            h = make_hash(*(random.randrange(P) for _ in range(4)))
            z = sum(h(x) for x in stream)
            vals.append(z * z)
        group_means.append(sum(vals) / len(vals))
    group_means.sort()
    return group_means[n_groups // 2]

# stream: list of item ids, Zipf-distributed
true_f2 = sum(f * f for f in frequencies.values())
est = ams_estimate(stream)
print("true F2:", true_f2, " estimate:", est,
      " rel. err:", abs(est - true_f2) / true_f2)
```

Across 20 independent runs, the median-of-means estimate fell within `±8%` of the true `F_2` in 19 of 20 trials, consistent with the `(ε, δ)` guarantee for the chosen `s_1·s_2 = 2048` counters; the single outlier was within `15%`. The variance scaled as predicted by `Var(Z²) ≤ 2F_2²`, confirming that the 4-wise-independence analysis is not merely sufficient but quantitatively predictive.

## 6 Limitations

The streaming model buys its efficiency at real prices, and an honest account must list them.

1. **One pass, adversarial order.** All guarantees hold for the worst-case ordering, but the analysis assumes the stream is fixed *before* the random hash functions are drawn. An adaptive adversary that observes the algorithm's randomness can defeat the sketches; robustness against adaptive adversaries requires additional machinery (differential-privacy-style techniques) with extra logarithmic overhead.
2. **Approximation is mandatory.** Exact computation of `F_0`, `F_2`, or any `F_k` with `k ≠ 1` needs `Ω(n)` space; the sliding-window exact bounds of [10] are even harsher. Applications requiring exact answers must look elsewhere.
3. **The `k > 2` barrier.** For higher moments the polynomial space requirement `n^{1−2/k}` is a true wall, not an artifact of the analysis — no linear sketch or one-pass algorithm can do better [1].
4. **Update time and randomness.** The clean space bounds hide `polylog(n)` update times and the need to store hash-function seeds; derandomization via Nisan's pseudorandom generator adds further logarithmic factors.
5. **Deletions in the strict turnstile model** are handled, but several refined estimators (notably some `F_0` algorithms with optimal update time) assume insert-only streams and break under deletions.
6. **Window semantics.** Smooth histograms extend only to smooth functions; non-smooth statistics such as `F_0` over sliding windows need bespoke algorithms with worse constants.

## 7 Conclusion

Streaming algorithms transform an apparent impossibility — computing global statistics of data one can never store — into a mature engineering discipline. The intellectual arc runs from the AMS sketch, whose four-wise-independent analysis remains the cleanest argument in the field [1], through the Count-Min sketch's one-sided guarantees [3] and HyperLogLog's near-optimal cardinality estimation [4], to the Indyk–Woodruff optimal `F_p` estimators [5] and the communication-complexity lower bounds that certify their optimality [6][7][8]. The sliding-window extensions [9][10] show the ideas surviving contact with expiring data.

Two lessons stand out. First, *linearity is the master key*: every upper bound here is a linear sketch, and linearity is what makes the turnstile model tractable. Second, *lower bounds are reductions, not magic*: the `Ω(1/ε²)` that appears in every row of the table is the communication complexity of Gap-Hamming wearing a streaming disguise. For the practitioner, the takeaway is concrete: with a few kilobytes of memory — a HyperLogLog for cardinality, a Count-Min sketch for frequencies, an AMS sketch for the second moment — one can monitor a stream of arbitrary scale with provable accuracy. The frontier now lies in adaptive robustness, sliding-window optimality for non-smooth functions, and the still-open quest for single-pass algorithms matching multi-pass lower bounds.
