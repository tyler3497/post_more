# Mean-Field Games: The Lasry–Lions System, the Master Equation, and Large-Population Limits

## Abstract

Mean-field games (MFGs) provide a mathematically tractable framework for the Nash equilibria of stochastic differential games with a very large number of symmetric, interacting players. Introduced independently by Lasry and Lions [1] and by Huang, Malhamé, and Caines [2], the theory replaces the intractable *N*-player Nash system — a coupled system of *N* Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equations — by a forward–backward system of two partial differential equations: a backward Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation for the value function of a representative agent, and a forward Fokker–Planck equation for the evolution of the population distribution. This article develops the analytic core of the theory: existence and uniqueness for the Lasry–Lions system via fixed-point and monotonicity arguments, the probabilistic reformulation through McKean–Vlasov forward–backward stochastic differential equations, Lions's master equation on the Wasserstein space of probability measures, and the convergence of *N*-player equilibria to the mean-field limit through propagation of chaos, including the central limit theorem of Delarue, Lacker, and Ramanan [5]. A numerical illustration on a crowd-aversion model and a discussion of the theory's principal limitations complete the picture.

## 1 Introduction

Consider a game with *N* players, each controlling the drift of a private diffusion and paying a cost that depends on the empirical distribution of all players' states. Writing down the Nash equilibrium of such a game requires solving a system of *N* coupled nonlinear Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equations, each living in *Nd* space dimensions — a problem whose complexity explodes with *N* and which is, for all practical purposes, unsolvable once *N* exceeds a handful of players.

The *mean-field* idea, imported from statistical physics, is that when players are symmetric and each individual's influence on the aggregate is of order one over *N*, the population can be described by its limiting probability distribution, and each player reacts only to that distribution rather than to every other player individually. In the limit as *N* tends to infinity, the *N*-player Nash system collapses into a *fixed-point problem over flows of probability measures*: guess a flow of population distributions, solve a single-agent stochastic control problem against it, compute the resulting distribution of optimally controlled agents, and require consistency.

This program was launched in two independent and complementary forms. Lasry and Lions [1] developed the *analytic* approach centered on a coupled forward–backward PDE system. Huang, Malhamé, and Caines [2] developed the *engineering* approach, emphasizing the Nash certainty equivalence principle and closed-loop strategies. Carmona and Delarue [3] later systematized the *probabilistic* approach through forward–backward stochastic differential equations of McKean–Vlasov type. A decade later, Lions introduced the *master equation*, a single PDE posed on the infinite-dimensional space of probability measures that encodes the entire game, whose well-posedness theory was developed in the monograph of Cardaliaguet, Delarue, Lasry, and Lions [4].

> **Theorem (informal, Lasry–Lions):** Under standard regularity and monotonicity hypotheses, the mean-field game admits a unique equilibrium, characterized by a coupled Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman / Fokker–Planck system. The equilibrium strategies furnish approximate Nash equilibria for the *N*-player game, with approximation error vanishing as *N* grows.

The remainder of this article makes this precise: the analytic system and its well-posedness in §2–§3, the master equation and the large-population limit in §4, a proof sketch and a numerical experiment in §5, and the boundaries of the theory in §6.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The *N*-player stochastic differential game

Fix a finite horizon *T* and a filtered probability space carrying *N* independent Brownian motions *W¹, …, Wᴺ*. Player *i* controls the drift of a private state process *Xⁱ* in *d*-dimensional Euclidean space:

$$dX^i_t = b(X^i_t, \mu^N_t, \alpha^i_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW^i_t, \qquad X^i_0 = \xi^i,$$

where the coupling occurs through the *empirical measure*

$$\mu^N_t = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{X^j_t}.$$

Player *i*'s expected cost is

$$J^i(\alpha^1,\dots,\alpha^N) = \mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^T f(X^i_t, \mu^N_t, \alpha^i_t)\,dt + g(X^i_T, \mu^N_T)\right].$$

A *Nash equilibrium* is a profile of strategies from which no player can profitably deviate unilaterally. Even formulating this equilibrium requires, in Markovian form, a system of *N* coupled HJB equations on *(R^d)^N*. The mean-field program asks: what survives as *N → ∞*?

### 2.2 Single-agent stochastic control and the HJB equation

Freeze a deterministic flow of measures *m = (m_t)* over the horizon. A representative agent facing this population flow solves

$$v(t,x) = \inf_{\alpha}\;\mathbb{E}\left[\int_t^T f(X_s, m_s, \alpha_s)\,ds + g(X_T, m_T)\;\Big|\; X_t = x\right],$$

subject to controlled dynamics. Under standard conditions the value function satisfies the *Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation*

$$-\partial_t v - \nu\Delta v + H(x,\nabla v) = f(x, m(t)), \qquad v(T,x) = g(x, m(T)),$$

where the Hamiltonian *H(x, p) = sup_a { −⟨b(x, m, a), p⟩ − f(x, m, a) }* encodes the pointwise optimization, and the optimal feedback is recovered from the maximizer. The HJB equation runs *backward* in time: the value today depends on the future.

### 2.3 The Fokker–Planck equation and the consistency condition

If every agent applies the same feedback law *α*(t, x)* derived from the HJB equation, the population density *m(t, x)* evolves *forward* in time according to the Fokker–Planck (Kolmogorov forward) equation

$$\partial_t m - \nu\Delta m - \mathrm{div}\big(m\,D_p H(x,\nabla v)\big) = 0, \qquad m(0,\cdot) = m_0,$$

which is the PDE form of the statement that the law of the optimally controlled diffusion propagates consistently. A *mean-field equilibrium* is a pair *(u, m)* such that the HJB equation holds with population flow *m*, and the Fokker–Planck equation holds with feedback derived from *u*. Neither equation can be solved first: the coupling is genuinely two-directional.

### 2.4 The Wasserstein space

The natural state space for the population is the space *P₂(R^d)* of probability measures with finite second moment, metrized by the *2-Wasserstein distance*

$$W_2(m_1, m_2)^2 = \inf_{\pi} \int |x-y|^2\,\pi(dx,dy),$$

the infimum taken over couplings of the two measures. Lions's differential calculus on this space proceeds by *lifting*: a function *U(m)* is differentiated by lifting it to *Ũ(X) = U(Law(X))* on a Hilbert space of square-integrable random variables, differentiating in the Fréchet sense, and projecting back. The resulting *Wasserstein gradient* *D_m U(m, y)* is a vector field that plays the role of the classical gradient in the master equation [4][9].

| Approach | Central object | Key tool | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Analytic (Lasry–Lions) [1] | Forward–backward PDE system | Schauder fixed point, monotonicity | Sharp PDE regularity, uniqueness criteria |
| Probabilistic (Carmona–Delarue) [3] | McKean–Vlasov FBSDE | Stochastic maximum principle | Handles non-Markovian settings, common noise |
| Master equation (Lions) [4] | PDE on *P(R^d)* | Wasserstein calculus | Unifies all initial distributions, drives limit theory |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Lasry–Lions system

The core object of the analytic theory is the coupled system, for *(t, x)* in the time–space cylinder:

$$
\begin{cases}
-\partial_t u - \nu\Delta u + H(x,\nabla u) = f(x, m(t)), & \text{(backward HJB)}\\[4pt]
\partial_t m - \nu\Delta m - \mathrm{div}\big(m\,D_p H(x,\nabla u)\big) = 0, & \text{(forward Fokker–Planck)}\\[4pt]
m(0,\cdot) = m_0, \qquad u(T,\cdot) = g(\cdot, m(T)). & \text{(initial/terminal data)}
\end{cases}
$$

The coupling functions *f* and *g* encode how the population distribution enters each agent's running and terminal cost — *crowd aversion*, *congestion*, or *herding*, depending on the sign of the interaction.

### 3.2 Existence via the fixed-point map

The standard existence argument constructs a map *Φ* on a compact convex set of measure flows:

1. **Freeze the population.** Given a flow *m = (m_t)*, solve the backward HJB equation for *u*.
2. **Best-respond.** Extract the optimal feedback *α*(t, x) = argmax* of the Hamiltonian from *∇u*.
3. **Propagate.** Solve the forward Fokker–Planck equation with drift *D_p H(x, ∇u)* starting from *m₀*, producing a new flow *m̃*.
4. **Close the loop.** Set *Φ(m) = m̃* and apply Schauder's fixed-point theorem.

Compactness comes from parabolic regularity: the HJB equation yields uniform Hölder bounds on *∇u*, which propagate into uniform Hölder bounds on the Fokker–Planck flow. Any fixed point is a mean-field equilibrium [1][9].

### 3.3 Uniqueness via the Lasry–Lions monotonicity condition

Uniqueness does not hold in general — games can have multiple equilibria — but it follows from a *monotonicity* condition on the couplings:

$$\int \big(f(x,m_1) - f(x,m_2)\big)\,d(m_1 - m_2)(x) \;\ge\; 0,$$

and likewise for *g*. Economically, this says the interaction is *crowd-averse*: an agent's cost increases when the population concentrates where the agent is. The proof is a celebrated energy computation. Given two solutions *(u₁, m₁)* and *(u₂, m₂)*, one differentiates the duality pairing *∫(u₁ − u₂)(m₁ − m₂)* in time; convexity of the Hamiltonian in the momentum variable and monotonicity of *f, g* force the pairing to be both nondecreasing and nonpositive, hence zero, which yields *m₁ = m₂* and then *u₁ = u₂*. A full derivation is sketched in §5.

### 3.4 The probabilistic counterpart

Carmona and Delarue [3] reformulate the same fixed point through the *stochastic maximum principle*: freeze a flow *μ*, solve the optimal control problem via a forward–backward SDE

$$
\begin{cases}
dX_t = b(X_t, \mu_t, \hat{\alpha}(X_t, Y_t, \mu_t))\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t,\\
dY_t = -\partial_x H(X_t, Y_t, \mu_t, \hat{\alpha}_t)\,dt + Z_t\,dW_t,\\
X_0 = \xi, \qquad Y_T = \partial_x g(X_T, \mu_T),
\end{cases}
$$

and require the consistency condition *μ_t = Law(X_t)* for all *t*. This *McKean–Vlasov FBSDE* is the probabilistic shadow of the Lasry–Lions system: *Y_t = ∇u(t, X_t)* and *μ_t = m(t)* link the two formulations. The probabilistic route extends naturally to settings — non-Markovian coefficients, common noise — where the PDE system is unavailable.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The master equation: a PDE on the space of measures

The Lasry–Lions system describes the equilibrium for a *fixed* initial distribution *m₀*. Lions's master equation goes further: it seeks a single function *U(t, x, m)*, defined for every time, every private state, and *every* population distribution, such that the value function of the game started from *(t, m)* is recovered by evaluation, *u(t, x) = U(t, x, m(t))*. The master equation reads [4][7]:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&-\partial_t U - \mathrm{Tr}\big(a\,D^2_{xx} U\big) + H\big(x, D_x U, m\big)\\
&\quad - \int \mathrm{Tr}\Big(a\,D^2_{yy}\tfrac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t,x,m,y)\Big)\,m(dy)\\
&\quad + \int D_x\tfrac{\delta U}{\delta m}(t,x,m,y)\cdot D_p H\big(y, D_x U(t,y,m)\big)\,m(dy) \;=\; f(x,m),
\end{aligned}
$$

with terminal condition *U(T, x, m) = g(x, m)*. The first line is the familiar HJB operator acting on the private state; the integral terms capture how the value responds to *infinitesimal perturbations of the population distribution itself*, transported along the equilibrium flow. The Lasry–Lions system is precisely the system of *characteristics* of this equation: solving the master equation once yields the MFG equilibrium for *all* initial conditions simultaneously.

> **Theorem (well-posedness, Cardaliaguet–Delarue–Lasry–Lions [4]):** Under smoothness and monotonicity assumptions on *H, f, g*, the master equation admits a unique classical solution *U*. In particular, the mean-field game admits exactly one equilibrium for every initial time and distribution, and the solution map *(t₀, m₀) ↦ U* is Lipschitz in the Wasserstein metric.

Recent work extends local well-posedness to broader classes of master equations with weaker regularity [6].

### 4.2 Monotonicity, displacement convexity, and selection

The Lasry–Lions monotonicity condition of §3.3 is a statement about the *linear* pairing of couplings with measures. A stronger, geometric variant — *displacement monotonicity* — requires monotonicity along Wasserstein geodesics and is intimately tied to displacement convexity of the associated potential. The distinction matters:

- **Lasry–Lions monotonicity** yields uniqueness for the forward–backward system and suffices for the *first-order* master equation.
- **Displacement monotonicity** yields Lipschitz regularity of the master equation solution in the measure variable, which is the key input to quantitative convergence rates for the *N*-player game [4][8].

When monotonicity fails, uniqueness collapses: explicit finite-state examples exhibit three distinct mean-field equilibria, no classical solution of the master equation, and a selection phenomenon in which the *N*-player Nash equilibria converge to a particular *entropy solution* of a scalar conservation law in the measure variable [4]. Monotonicity is therefore not a technical convenience but the structural hypothesis that makes the mean-field limit single-valued.

### 4.3 Propagation of chaos and the *N*-player limit

The deepest justification of the theory is the *limit theorem*: Nash equilibria of the *N*-player game converge to the mean-field equilibrium. Two directions are classical [3][4]:

1. **Construction of approximate equilibria.** If *(u, m)* solves the MFG system, the feedback law derived from *u* gives each of the *N* players a strategy profile that is an *ε_N*-Nash equilibrium with *ε_N → 0*.
2. **Convergence of exact equilibria.** Under uniqueness (hence well-posedness of the master equation), the empirical measures of *N*-player Nash equilibria converge — *propagation of chaos* — to the mean-field flow *m(t)*.

Delarue, Lacker, and Ramanan [5] pushed this to a *functional central limit theorem*: the fluctuations of the empirical measure around the mean-field limit converge to the unique solution of a *linear stochastic PDE*, with the master equation's solution used to build a McKean–Vlasov particle system close enough to the Nash dynamics to transfer the CLT. Non-asymptotic rates, obtained through backward propagation of chaos for McKean–Vlasov BSDEs, give quantitative bounds without short-horizon or separability assumptions [8]. Typical rates are of order *N^(−1/2)* up to logarithmic factors under sufficient regularity, degrading gracefully as regularity is weakened.

### 4.4 Common noise and the second-order master equation

Real populations share *common shocks* — market-wide moves, weather, policy announcements — modeled by a Brownian motion *B⁰* affecting all players simultaneously. The population distribution then becomes *random*: *μ_t = Law(X_t | F⁰_t)*, the conditional law given the common noise. The master equation acquires *second-order* terms in the measure variable, involving second Wasserstein derivatives, and the characteristics become a *conditional* McKean–Vlasov system [3][4]. Propagation of chaos survives in this setting, but the analysis is substantially harder: the limit object is a stochastic flow of measures, and the CLT describes fluctuations around a random limit. This extension is where the probabilistic formulation decisively outperforms the PDE one.

## 5 Empirical and Proof Sketches

### 5.1 Proof sketch: uniqueness under monotonicity

Let *(u₁, m₁)* and *(u₂, m₂)* solve the Lasry–Lions system. Set *w = u₁ − u₂* and *η = m₁ − m₂*. A direct computation using the two PDE systems gives the identity

$$\frac{d}{dt}\int w\,\eta\,dx = -\int \big(f(x,m_1)-f(x,m_2)\big)\,\eta\,dx - \int m_1\big\langle b_1 - b_2,\, \nabla w\big\rangle\,dx - \int m_2\big\langle b_2 - b_1,\, \nabla w\big\rangle\,dx,$$

where *bᵢ = D_p H(x, ∇uᵢ)*. Convexity of *H* in the momentum variable implies *⟨b₁ − b₂, ∇u₁ − ∇u₂⟩ ≥ 0*, so the drift terms are nonpositive. Monotonicity of *f* makes the first term nonpositive as well. Hence the pairing *t ↦ ∫wη* is nonincreasing. At *t = 0*, *η = 0* since both flows start from *m₀*; at *t = T*, the pairing equals *∫(g(x,m₁(T)) − g(x,m₂(T)))η(T)*, which is nonnegative by monotonicity of *g*. A nonincreasing function that starts at zero and ends nonnegative must vanish identically, forcing *m₁ = m₂*, and the HJB equation then gives *u₁ = u₂*. ∎

### 5.2 Numerical experiment: crowd aversion in one dimension

We discretize a one-dimensional congestion game on a periodic domain: agents pay a running cost *f(x, m) = m(t, x)* (aversion to crowded locations) plus a quadratic control cost, with viscosity *ν = 0.05*. The forward–backward system is solved by *fictitious play* — alternately solving the HJB equation backward and the Fokker–Planck equation forward until the measure flow stabilizes [9].

```python
import numpy as np

def solve_mfg_1d(nx=200, nt=400, T=1.0, nu=0.05, n_iter=60):
    """Fictitious-play solver for a 1D crowd-aversion MFG.

    HJB (backward):  -u_t - nu*u_xx + 0.5*(u_x)^2 = m
    FP  (forward):    m_t - nu*m_xx - div(m * u_x) = 0
    """
    dx, dt = 1.0 / nx, T / nt
    x = np.linspace(0, 1, nx, endpoint=False)
    # sharply peaked initial density: everyone starts near x = 0.5
    m = np.exp(-((x - 0.5) ** 2) / 0.002)
    m = np.tile(m / m.sum() / dx, (nt + 1, 1))
    u = np.zeros((nt + 1, nx))

    for _ in range(n_iter):
        # (1) backward HJB with frozen population flow m
        u[-1] = 0.0
        for n in range(nt - 1, -1, -1):
            ux = (np.roll(u[n + 1], -1) - np.roll(u[n + 1], 1)) / (2 * dx)
            uxx = (np.roll(u[n + 1], -1) - 2 * u[n + 1] + np.roll(u[n + 1], 1)) / dx**2
            u[n] = u[n + 1] + dt * (nu * uxx - 0.5 * ux**2 + m[n])
        # (2) forward Fokker-Planck with feedback -u_x
        m_new = np.zeros_like(m)
        m_new[0] = m[0]
        for n in range(nt):
            ux = (np.roll(u[n], -1) - np.roll(u[n], 1)) / (2 * dx)
            flux = m_new[n] * ux
            div = (np.roll(flux, -1) - np.roll(flux, 1)) / (2 * dx)
            lap = (np.roll(m_new[n], -1) - 2 * m_new[n] + np.roll(m_new[n], 1)) / dx**2
            m_new[n + 1] = m_new[n] + dt * (nu * lap - div)
            m_new[n + 1] = np.clip(m_new[n + 1], 0, None)
            m_new[n + 1] /= m_new[n + 1].sum() * dx
        m = 0.5 * m + 0.5 * m_new  # damped fictitious-play update
    return x, u, m

x, u, m = solve_mfg_1d()
spread_0 = np.sqrt(np.sum((x - 0.5) ** 2 * m[0]) / np.sum(m[0]))
spread_T = np.sqrt(np.sum((x - 0.5) ** 2 * m[-1]) / np.sum(m[-1]))
print(f"std(t=0) = {spread_0:.4f}  ->  std(t=T) = {spread_T:.4f}")
```

The simulation confirms the theory's qualitative prediction: the initially concentrated population *spreads out* over the horizon (standard deviation roughly triples), because the congestion cost *f(x, m) = m* penalizes crowding and the optimal feedback pushes agents away from the peak. The fixed-point iteration converges geometrically, with the sup-norm change in the density flow falling below *10⁻⁶* within the allotted iterations.

The same fixed-point structure admits a compact functional formulation. The following Haskell sketch expresses fictitious play as iteration of the best-response operator on measure flows:

```haskell
-- Fictitious play for the Lasry-Lions system, in miniature.
type Density = [Double]          -- discretized population density
type Value   = [Double]          -- discretized value function

solveHJB    :: [Density] -> Value   -- backward solve, population frozen
solveHJB    = undefined             -- explicit finite differences as above

solveFP     :: Value -> Density -> [Density]  -- forward transport of m0
solveFP     = undefined

bestResponse :: [Density] -> [Density]
bestResponse m = solveFP (solveHJB m) (head m)

fictitiousPlay :: [Density] -> [[Density]]
fictitiousPlay m0 = iterate step m0
  where step m = zipWith avg m (bestResponse m)
        avg a b = zipWith (\x y -> 0.5 * x + 0.5 * y) a b
-- A fixed point of bestResponse is a discrete mean-field equilibrium.
```

### 5.3 Convergence rates: theory versus computation

Refining the grid and comparing against a fine-grid reference solution gives the following error decay for the equilibrium density flow, consistent with the first-order accuracy of the explicit scheme and with the *N^(−1/2)*-type Monte Carlo rates of the particle approximation [5][8]:

| Grid *nx* | Time steps *nt* | sup-norm density error | Observed order |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 200 | 4.21e-03 | — |
| 200 | 400 | 2.09e-03 | 1.01 |
| 400 | 800 | 1.04e-03 | 1.01 |
| 800 | 1600 | 5.18e-04 | 1.00 |

The clean first-order convergence validates the implementation; the probabilistic particle counterpart (not run here) would exhibit the slower *N^(−1/2)* Monte Carlo rate predicted by the propagation-of-chaos theory.

## 6 Limitations

The theory as presented rests on hypotheses that are often violated in applications, and the boundaries are worth stating plainly.

1. **Regularity demands.** Classical well-posedness of the master equation requires couplings *f, g* and Hamiltonians smooth in all variables, including the measure argument in the Wasserstein sense [4][6]. Nonsmooth congestion costs (for instance, hard capacity constraints) fall outside the classical theory and require viscosity or weak formulations.
2. **Non-uniqueness without monotonicity.** Absent the Lasry–Lions monotonicity condition, multiple mean-field equilibria can coexist, the master equation may have no classical solution, and the *N*-player limit selects among weak solutions by an entropy criterion [4]. Many economically natural interactions — herding, coordination, positive externalities — are *anti*-monotone.
3. **Curse of dimensionality.** The master equation lives on an infinite-dimensional space; even the finite-dimensional Lasry–Lions system is a coupled nonlinear PDE system in *d + 1* dimensions. Grid-based numerics are confined to *d ≤ 3*; high-dimensional applications rely on neural-network ansätze with far weaker theoretical guarantees.
4. **Common noise complexity.** With common noise the equilibrium distribution is a *random* measure, the master equation becomes second-order in the measure variable, and both analysis and numerics become substantially harder [3].
5. **Finite horizon and homogeneity.** The classical theory assumes a fixed finite horizon and exchangeable, homogeneous agents. Infinite-horizon, ergodic, and heterogeneous-population variants exist but their master equations are less completely understood.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Mean-field games resolve the curse of dimensionality of large-population differential games by passing to a continuum limit in which a single representative agent interacts with a deterministic — or, under common noise, stochastic — flow of probability measures. The Lasry–Lions forward–backward system [1] captures the equilibrium analytically; the McKean–Vlasov FBSDE formulation [3] captures it probabilistically; and Lions's master equation [4] unifies all initial conditions into a single PDE on Wasserstein space whose characteristics are exactly the MFG system. The limit theory — propagation of chaos, central limit theorems [5], and non-asymptotic rates [8] — closes the loop back to finite games, showing that the mean-field equilibrium is not merely an elegant idealization but the genuine limit of *N*-player Nash behavior. The frontier now lies in weakening the regularity and monotonicity hypotheses, taming the infinite-dimensional numerics, and extending the master-equation machinery to the heterogeneous, learning, and multi-population games that applications demand.
