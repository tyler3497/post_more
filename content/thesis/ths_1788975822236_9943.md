# Adversarial Robustness in Deep Learning: Certified Defenses, Randomized Smoothing, and the Geometry of Decision Boundaries

## Abstract

Adversarial vulnerability — the susceptibility of high-accuracy classifiers to imperceptible perturbations — remains one of the most consequential unsolved problems in machine learning security. This thesis develops a unified treatment of adversarial robustness spanning *threat models* defined by $\ell_p$ balls, *empirical defenses* built on adversarial training as a saddle-point problem, and *certified defenses* that produce provable robustness guarantees. We derive the randomized smoothing certificate from first principles via the Neyman–Pearson lemma, showing how isotropic Gaussian noise converts any base classifier into a smoothed classifier with a tight $\ell_2$ certified radius $R = \tfrac{\sigma}{2}(\Phi^{-1}(p_A) - \Phi^{-1}(p_B))$. We then examine deterministic certification through convex bound propagation (IBP, CROWN, and the dual polytope view) and the geometry of decision boundaries, including margin theory, robust/non-robust features, and frequency-bias phenomena. Finally, we analyze the provable tension between standard and robust accuracy, the curse of dimensionality afflicting $\ell_\infty$ certificates in high-dimensional inputs, and the frontier of scalable certification. Our exposition is supported by seven authoritative sources from the adversarial machine learning literature.

## 1. Introduction

The discovery that imperceptible perturbations can flip the predictions of state-of-the-art neural networks — with perturbations measured in $\ell_\infty$ norms as small as $8/255$ on CIFAR-10 — exposed a deep mismatch between statistical learning and geometric stability [1][6]. Adversarial examples transfer across architectures, survive preprocessing, and persist despite years of defensive effort, motivating a scientific program whose goals are now sharply divided into two camps:

1. **Empirical robustness**: harden models against known attacks (e.g., projected gradient descent, PGD) via *adversarial training*, a minimax optimization that replays the strongest first-order attack inside the training loop [5].
2. **Certified robustness**: produce classifiers whose robustness can be *mathematically proven* — either probabilistically, via randomized smoothing [6], or deterministically, via convex relaxations of the network [7][8].

The stakes are practical (autonomous perception, malware detection, biometric authentication) and theoretical: adversarial robustness exposes fundamental limits of empirical risk minimization, reveals that the features networks exploit are often *non-robust yet predictive* [2], and forces a re-examination of the robustness–accuracy tradeoff [3][4]. This thesis treats certified defenses as the central object of study, developing the geometry, probability, and optimization that underlie modern guarantees, and it situates empirical methods as a necessary complement.

> **Definition 1.1 (Adversarial example).** Given a classifier $f : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ and a labeled input $(x, y)$, an *adversarial perturbation* is any $\delta$ such that $f(x+\delta) \neq y$ and $\|\delta\|_p \leq \varepsilon$, where $\|\cdot\|_p$ is an $\ell_p$ norm and $\varepsilon$ the perturbation budget. The classifier's *robust accuracy* is $\Pr_{(x,y)}[f(x+\delta) = y \ \forall\, \|\delta\|_p \leq \varepsilon]$.

The $\ell_\infty$ threat model is the community standard for vision: it bounds the maximum per-pixel change. The $\ell_2$ model is more natural for certified methods, since Gaussian smoothing yields clean closed-form $\ell_2$ certificates.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The minimax formulation of adversarial training

Madry et al. [5] reformulated robustness as a *saddle-point* (minimax) problem:

$$
\min_\theta\ \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \max_{\|\delta\|_\infty \leq \varepsilon} \ \ell(f_\theta(x+\delta), y) \right]
$$

The inner maximization — the attack — searches the $\varepsilon$-ball for the worst-case input; the outer minimization — the defense — fits parameters against it. The key practical insight of [5] is that *projected gradient descent* (PGD), a first-order method with random restarts, reliably approximates the global maximizer of the inner problem for neural networks, and Danskin's theorem then justifies differentiating through it. Adversarial training is thus:

1. Sample a minibatch $\{(x_i, y_i)\}$.
2. For each example, compute $\delta_i^* \approx \arg\max_{\|\delta\|_\infty \leq \varepsilon} \ell(f_\theta(x_i+\delta), y_i)$ via multi-step PGD with projection $\Pi_{B_\varepsilon}$.
3. Take a gradient step on $\theta$ using $\ell(f_\theta(x_i+\delta_i^*), y_i)$.

This recipe lifted CIFAR-10 robust accuracy against $\ell_\infty$ PGD ($\varepsilon = 8/255$) from near zero to roughly $45\%$, at the cost of standard accuracy dropping from $\sim 95\%$ to $\sim 87\%$ [5] — the first concrete observation of the tradeoff that later work made theoretically precise [3][4].

### 2.2 The robustness–accuracy tradeoff: features perspective

Tsipras et al. [3] constructed a simple synthetic distribution in which the Bayes-optimal standard classifier necessarily has *zero* robust accuracy, while only a degraded classifier can be robust — proving the tradeoff can be *inherent to the task*, not merely an artifact of optimization. Ilyas et al. [2] gave this a mechanistic explanation: datasets contain two kinds of predictive signal, *robust features* (correlated with the label and stable under perturbation) and *non-robust features* (correlated with the label but brittle). Standard training harvests both; adversarial training is forced to discard non-robust features, sacrificing accuracy on them. Strikingly, [2] showed that a dataset stripped of all robust features — retaining only the non-robust signal — still trains models that generalize to the *original* test set, proving non-robust features are real, generalizing signal rather than noise.

### 2.3 Decision-boundary geometry and frequency bias

Three geometric perspectives illuminate *why* models are vulnerable:

- **Margin geometry.** For a linear classifier, robustness to $\ell_p$ perturbations is exactly the $\ell_p$ margin; adversarial training pushes the boundary away from data points [4]. For deep networks, the boundary is highly curved, and curvature correlates with vulnerability.
- **Manifold geometry.** If data concentrate near a low-dimensional manifold, then any test error forces most correctly classified points to lie adjacent to misclassified ones, guaranteeing the existence of adversarial examples under any nontrivial perturbation [2].
- **Frequency bias.** Neural networks exhibit a bias toward *high-frequency* components that are predictive but invisible to humans — spectral non-robust features. Low-pass filtering inputs disproportionately removes the brittle signal, at the cost of accuracy, mirroring the robust-feature distillation of [2].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis synthesizes three methodological pillars — empirical adversarial training, probabilistic certification via randomized smoothing, and deterministic certification via bound propagation — and analyzes them against the feature-geometry lens of Section 2.

**Adversarial training as a baseline empirical defense.** We treat PGD-based minimax training [5] as the gold-standard *empirical* defense: no proof is produced, but the defense survives all known adaptive attacks when evaluated with sufficient capacity and correct hyperparameters. All certified methods below are compared against this baseline.

**Randomized smoothing as a probabilistic certificate.** Given a base classifier $f$, define the *smoothed classifier*

$$
g(x) = \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{Y}}\ \mathbb{P}_{\delta \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)}[f(x+\delta) = c].
$$

Cohen et al. [6] proved a *tight* $\ell_2$ robustness guarantee for $g$: if $g$ classifies the Gaussian cloud around $x$ as class $c_A$ with probability lower bound $\underline{p_A}$ and every other class with upper bound $\overline{p_B}$, then $g(x+\delta) = c_A$ for all $\|\delta\|_2 < R$ where

$$
R = \frac{\sigma}{2}\left(\Phi^{-1}(\underline{p_A}) - \Phi^{-1}(\overline{p_B})\right),
$$

$\Phi$ being the standard Gaussian CDF. In practice $p_A, p_B$ are estimated by Monte Carlo sampling with Clopper–Pearson confidence intervals, so the certificate holds with probability $1-\alpha$ over the sampling. The noise level $\sigma$ directly trades accuracy against certified radius [6].

**Bound propagation as a deterministic certificate.** Interval Bound Propagation (IBP) [7] propagates the box $[x-\varepsilon, x+\varepsilon]$ layer by layer, computing worst-case logit bounds in two forward passes; a sample is certified if the lower bound of the true-class logit exceeds the upper bounds of all others. CROWN [8] generalizes IBP with linear (rather than interval) bounds per layer, yielding strictly tighter certificates at higher cost. Both integrate into training objectives (CROWN-IBP) that minimize the certified upper bound on robust loss.

**Evaluation protocol.** We report (i) *certified accuracy*: fraction of test points both correctly classified and certified at radius $\varepsilon$; (ii) *empirical robust accuracy*: accuracy under strong adaptive PGD; and (iii) *standard accuracy*, to quantify the tradeoff frontier.

## 4. Deep Technical Dive

### 4.1 The Neyman–Pearson proof of the smoothing certificate

> **Theorem (Cohen, Rosenfeld & Kolter, 2019 [6]):** Let $f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathcal{Y}$ be arbitrary, $\delta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$, and $g$ the smoothed classifier. Suppose $\mathbb{P}(f(x+\varepsilon) = c_A) \geq \underline{p_A}$ and $\max_{c \neq c_A}\mathbb{P}(f(x+\varepsilon) = c) \leq \overline{p_B}$ for $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)$. Then $g(x+\delta) = c_A$ for all $\|\delta\|_2 < \tfrac{\sigma}{2}(\Phi^{-1}(\underline{p_A}) - \Phi^{-1}(\overline{p_B}))$.

*Proof sketch.* Fix $\delta$ and ask: among all base classifiers $f$ consistent with the observed probabilities at $x$, which one *minimizes* the probability of classifying $\mathcal{N}(x+\delta, \sigma^2 I)$ as $c_A$? By a Neyman–Pearson-type argument [6], the worst-case $f^*$ is a *linear halfspace classifier* with decision boundary normal to $\delta$:

$$
f^*(x') = \begin{cases} c_A & \text{if } \delta^\top (x' - x) \leq \sigma\|\delta\|_2\,\Phi^{-1}(\underline{p_A}) \\ c_B & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

This worst case shifts the Gaussian mass by $\|\delta\|_2/\sigma$ standard deviations, so $\mathbb{P}(f^*(x+\delta+\varepsilon) = c_A) = \Phi(\Phi^{-1}(\underline{p_A}) - \|\delta\|_2/\sigma)$. Requiring this to exceed $\overline{p_B}$ (and hence $1/2$ in the simplified statement) yields exactly the radius formula. Because the argument ranges over *all* measurable $f$, the certificate is tight: no smoothing-based proof can do better for the given $(p_A, p_B, \sigma)$.

The result is remarkable in that it makes *no assumptions whatsoever* about $f$ — it applies equally to ResNets, transformers, and lookup tables — and it certifies $\ell_2$ robustness where adversarial training traditionally certifies $\ell_\infty$.

### 4.2 The $\ell_\infty$ barrier and the curse of dimensionality

Randomized smoothing's $\ell_2$ success masks a sobering limitation. Blum et al. showed that any *isotropic* smoothing distribution certifies $\ell_\infty$ radius at best $\sigma/\sqrt{d}$ — the certified $\ell_\infty$ ball shrinks as dimension grows [5 of sources: 2002.03517]. For ImageNet ($d \approx 150{,}000$), an $\ell_\infty$ certificate of $2/255$ would demand an astronomically large noise level $\sigma$, destroying standard accuracy. Non-isotropic and heavy-tailed noise (Laplace for $\ell_1$, generalized Gaussians for fractional norms) recover certificates in other $\ell_p$ geometries, but the $\sqrt{d}$ gap between $\ell_2$ and $\ell_\infty$ is a *fundamental* price of isotropy, not a loose bound. This dimensional penalty is why certified $\ell_\infty$ defenses on ImageNet remain far behind empirical ones.

### 4.3 IBP, CROWN, and the convex relaxation barrier

Deterministic certification treats verification as constrained optimization: is the logit margin positive over the entire $\varepsilon$-ball? The exact problem is NP-hard (it generalizes neural-network verification), so tractable methods solve a *convex relaxation*:

- **IBP** propagates intervals: each layer maps input intervals to output intervals using the monotonicity of affine maps and elementwise activations [7]. Cheap ($2\times$ a forward pass) but loose; interval widths grow exponentially with depth unless the network is trained to keep them small — hence IBP *training* with an $\varepsilon$-schedule that ramps the perturbation budget from $0$ to the target.
- **CROWN** propagates *linear* bounds $A^L x + b^L \leq z^{(k)} \leq A^U x + b^U$ backward through the network, choosing per-neuron ReLU relaxations (lower envelope adaptively selected from $\{0, \alpha z, \text{the upper line}\}$) [8]. Strictly tighter than IBP at roughly $O(n)$ backward passes.
- **Dual / LP view.** Wong & Kolter [8] showed the convex outer adversarial polytope has a tractable dual; minimizing the dual during training yields verifiably robust networks on MNIST ($\varepsilon = 0.1$) with certified accuracy above $94\%$.

Salman et al. unified these relaxations in one framework and proved a *convex relaxation barrier*: LP-based verifiers cannot certify perturbations beyond a certain radius regardless of tightness, because the relaxation gap is inherent to the polytope family. IBP escapes the barrier only empirically — its training-time regularization reshapes the network so that even loose bounds are meaningful.

### 4.4 Minimax optimization: why adversarial training works

The empirical success of adversarial training rests on two properties of the loss landscape identified in [5]:

1. **The inner max is well-approximated by PGD.** Across random restarts, PGD finds maxima with tightly concentrated loss values — the saddle-point problem is "easy" because the adversarial loss concentrates near its global maximum even in high dimensions. This justifies Danskin's theorem: the gradient at the PGD maximizer is a valid descent direction for the robust objective.
2. **Capacity matters.** Robust classification is a strictly harder function class to fit than standard classification [3]: increasing network width/depth monotonically improves the robust–standard frontier, and under-capacity models cannot simultaneously fit the $\varepsilon$-balls around training points.

Modern variants refine the outer objective rather than the inner attack: **TRADES** [4] decomposes robust error as $\mathcal{R}_{\text{rob}}(f) = \mathcal{R}_{\text{nat}}(f) + \mathcal{R}_{\text{bdy}}(f)$ and minimizes natural error plus a boundary-regularization term $\max_{x' \in B_\varepsilon(x)} \mathcal{L}(f(x), f(x'))/\lambda$, trading off robustness against accuracy through a single hyperparameter $\lambda$ with a theoretically calibrated surrogate. **Friendly adversarial training** selects adversarial examples just across the decision boundary rather than maximally distant ones, preserving natural accuracy [survey: 2008.02076].

### 4.5 Robust features, margins, and the geometry of the boundary

The feature view of [2] yields a geometric prediction: adversarial training *straightens and reorients* the decision boundary so that its normal aligns with *robust* (human-meaningful) feature directions. Evidence includes:

- Adversarial perturbations of robust models, when visualized, resemble the target class (they "paint" semantic features), whereas perturbations of standard models look like unstructured noise — the boundary's gradient points at meaning, not noise [2][3].
- Robust training acts as a *spectral regularizer*: it suppresses reliance on high-frequency features, flattening the Fourier spectrum of input gradients — the frequency-bias perspective.
- Margins concentrate: the distribution of $\ell_2$ distances to the boundary becomes heavier near $\varepsilon$ for robust models, matching the TRADES boundary term [4].

This geometry also explains *transferability*: different standard models all exploit the same non-robust features of the data distribution, so one model's adversarial direction fools them all.

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

| Defense | Certificate type | Threat model | CIFAR-10 result (reported in literature) | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PGD adversarial training [5] | Empirical (no proof) | $\ell_\infty$, $\varepsilon=8/255$ | $\approx 46\%$ robust accuracy, $\approx 87\%$ standard | $7$–$10\times$ training cost |
| TRADES [4] | Empirical (calibrated) | $\ell_\infty$, $\varepsilon=8/255$ | $\approx 53\%$ robust accuracy at $\lambda=6$ | Similar to PGD-AT |
| Randomized smoothing [6] | Probabilistic, $1-\alpha$ | $\ell_2$, $R=0.5$ | $\approx 49\%$ certified accuracy ($\sigma=0.5$) | $10^4$–$10^5$ forward passes per sample |
| CROWN-IBP [8] | Deterministic | $\ell_\infty$, $\varepsilon=8/255$ | $\approx 30\%$+ certified accuracy | $2\times$ forward pass per batch |
| IBP training [7] | Deterministic | $\ell_\infty$, $\varepsilon=8/255$ | $\approx 34\%$ certified accuracy | $2\times$ forward pass per batch |

Key quantitative lessons:

1. **The certified–empirical gap.** On CIFAR-10, the best *certified* $\ell_\infty$ defenses trail the best *empirical* ones by roughly $15$–$20$ percentage points. The gap is the price of provability — and of the convex relaxation barrier.
2. **Radius–accuracy curves.** For randomized smoothing, certified accuracy decays roughly as $\Phi(\Phi^{-1}(p_A) - R/\sigma)$: doubling the certified radius demands an exponential increase in $\sigma$, collapsing standard accuracy. The operating point is chosen on this Pareto frontier, never off it.
3. **Sample complexity.** Robust generalization requires more data: learning a robust classifier can demand polynomially more samples than a standard one [3], and unlabeled data with robust self-training measurably closes the gap.
4. **Theoretical guarantees in closed form.** The smoothing certificate [6] is tight for its inputs; TRADES [4] upper-bounds robust error by a calibrated surrogate with a provable decomposition; IBP [7] soundness follows from interval arithmetic. These are *proofs about the deployed artifact*, not about the training procedure — the distinguishing mark of certification.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

def certified_radius(p_A: float, p_B: float, sigma: float) -> float:
    """Cohen et al. (2019) tight l2 radius for a smoothed classifier.

    p_A: lower bound on top-class probability under N(x, sigma^2 I)
    p_B: upper bound on runner-up probability
    """
    assert 0.5 < p_B <= p_A <= 1.0
    return 0.5 * sigma * (norm.ppf(p_A) - norm.ppf(p_B))

# Example: sigma=0.5, p_A=0.9, p_B=0.1 -> R = 0.5*0.5*(1.2816-(-1.2816)) = 0.6408
print(f"R = {certified_radius(0.9, 0.1, 0.5):.4f}")

def clopper_pearson_lower(counts: int, n: int, alpha: float = 0.001) -> float:
    """One-sided lower confidence bound on a binomial probability."""
    from scipy.stats import beta
    return beta.ppf(alpha, counts, n - counts + 1)

# Monte-Carlo certification: 100k samples, 80k votes for top class
p_A_hat = clopper_pearson_lower(80_000, 100_000)
print(f"p_A lower bound = {p_A_hat:.4f}, "
      f"certified R = {certified_radius(p_A_hat, 1 - p_A_hat, 0.5):.4f}")
```

## 6. Limitations

1. **Threat-model myopia.** All certificates discussed are $\ell_p$-ball certificates. Real adversaries use rotations, translations, patches, and semantic edits; an $\ell_\infty$-certified model has no guarantees against a $3^\circ$ rotation. Certification against *semantic* threat models remains largely open.
2. **The dimensionality curse for $\ell_\infty$.** Isotropic randomized smoothing cannot certify meaningful $\ell_\infty$ radii in high dimensions [5 of sources], and deterministic LP relaxations hit the convex barrier. ImageNet-scale $\ell_\infty$ certification is still impractical.
3. **Computational cost.** Monte-Carlo certification needs $\sim 10^5$ forward passes *per test point*; IBP doubles training cost and requires delicate $\varepsilon$-scheduling; CROWN-style verification is too slow for training very deep networks.
4. **The tradeoff is real (sometimes).** Where Tsipras et al. [3] prove an inherent tradeoff for specific distributions, subsequent work (e.g., the $r$-separation analysis in "A Closer Look at Accuracy vs. Robustness") shows well-separated data admit both accuracy *and* robustness — the tradeoff is a property of the data distribution, not of learning per se.
5. **Adaptive attackers vs. certificates.** Empirical defenses must be evaluated against *adaptive* attacks designed with full knowledge of the defense; gradient obfuscation has repeatedly produced false robustness claims. Certificates are immune to this failure mode by construction — but only within their stated threat model.

## 7. Conclusion

Adversarial robustness has matured from an empirical curiosity into a discipline with theorems. The minimax formulation [5] gave us the strongest empirical defense; the robustness–accuracy tradeoff [3][4] and the robust-features framework [2] explained *why* defenses cost accuracy; and certified methods — randomized smoothing [6] with its Neyman–Pearson certificate, and bound propagation [7][8] with its convex-relaxation machinery — gave us guarantees we can check rather than attacks we hope are strong enough.

Three frontiers remain. First, *semantic certification*: extending provable robustness beyond $\ell_p$ balls to the transformations adversaries actually use. Second, *scaling deterministic certification* to ImageNet-scale architectures without surrendering tightness. Third, *closing the certified–empirical gap*: every percentage point between PGD robust accuracy and certified accuracy is a theorem waiting to be proved. The geometry of decision boundaries — margins, curvature, spectral bias, and feature alignment — will be the language in which those theorems are written.
