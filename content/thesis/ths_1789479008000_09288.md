# Magnetic Confinement Fusion and MHD Stability: Tokamak Scaling Laws, Ballooning Modes, and the ITER Burning-Plasma Program

## Abstract

Magnetic confinement fusion aims to reproduce stellar energy release by suspending a hundred-million-degree plasma in nested magnetic flux surfaces long enough for deuterium–tritium reactions to outpace energy losses. This thesis develops the magnetohydrodynamic foundations of the tokamak: axisymmetric equilibrium through the Grad–Shafranov equation, the pressure limits encoded in Troyon normalized-beta scaling, and the hierarchy of ideal instabilities — kinks, ballooning modes, and peeling modes — that bound operational space. We derive the normalized-beta formalism and its shaping dependence, survey the peeling–ballooning model of edge-localized modes and the kinetic extensions that reshape pedestal predictions, and examine the ITER burning-plasma program, whose fifteen-mega-ampere baseline scenario targets five hundred megawatts of fusion power at a scientific gain of ten while navigating precisely the stability boundaries derived here. Empirical confinement scalings, the Lawson criterion, and modern gyrokinetic scenario predictions are synthesized to assess what burning plasmas will teach us — and which fundamental limits they cannot evade.

##1 Intro

In 1957, John Lawson published an inequality of disarming simplicity: for a fusion plasma to ignite, the product of density and energy confinement time must exceed a threshold set by the fusion reactivity [8]. Nearly seventy years later, that criterion remains the zeroth-order scorecard of the field, and the tokamak — a toroidal device in which a strong externally imposed toroidal field is twisted by a plasma-driven poloidal field — remains the leading magnetic geometry for satisfying it. The Soviet T-3 results announced at Novosibirsk in 1968, confirming electron temperatures near one kiloelectron-volt in a tokamak, redirected the world's fusion effort into this single line [2].

Yet the tokamak's promise has always been shadowed by a paradox. The same magnetic topology that confines the plasma also supports a rich spectrum of magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) instabilities capable of destroying confinement in microseconds. Performance is therefore not set by heating power or magnetic field strength alone, but by *stability boundaries* — mathematical surfaces in parameter space beyond which equilibria cease to exist or become violently unstable. The central intellectual achievement of tokamak theory has been to map these boundaries: the Kruskal–Shafranov current limit, the Troyon beta limit, the Greenwald density limit, and the peeling–ballooning edge stability diagram [9][2][4].

This thesis presents that map as a unified whole. We begin with equilibrium — the Grad–Shafranov equation and the flux-surface geometry it produces — then derive the scaling laws that quantify how close a plasma may approach its pressure limits. We dissect the two great instability families that enforce those limits: pressure-driven ballooning modes and current-driven kink and peeling modes. Finally, we examine the ITER experiment, the first device designed to access the burning-plasma regime, as a case study in designing *around* every boundary this thesis derives [3][7].

##2 Background

Fusion of deuterium and tritium releases 17.6 MeV per reaction, carried mostly by a 14.1 MeV neutron and a 3.5 MeV alpha particle. The reaction rate per unit volume scales as the square of the density times the velocity-averaged cross section, which peaks at ion temperatures of several tens of kiloelectron-volts. Lawson's analysis balances this fusion power against conduction and radiation losses to derive the ignition condition: for deuterium–tritium fuel, the product of density and energy confinement time must exceed roughly two times ten to the twentieth per cubic meter-second, with the more precise triple-product threshold reaching a minimum of 2.9 × 10²¹ keV m⁻³ s at about 14 keV for complete ignition [8]. The triple product *nTτE* is the superior figure of merit for magnetic confinement because it is proportional to the square of the plasma pressure — and pressure, not density or temperature alone, is what MHD stability limits [8].

Magnetic confinement exploits the Lorentz force to bind charged particles to field lines. In a straight solenoid, particles stream freely out the ends; bending the solenoid into a torus closes the field lines but introduces curvature and gradient drifts that charge-separate the plasma and drive it outward. The tokamak solution superimposes a poloidal field — generated by a toroidal plasma current — onto the dominant toroidal field produced by external coils. The resulting helical field lines possess *rotational transform*: a field line winds poloidally as it travels toroidally, short-circuiting the charge separation and yielding, in the ideal limit, a set of nested toroidal flux surfaces on which pressure is constant.

The economic figure of merit is the plasma beta, the ratio of kinetic pressure to magnetic pressure, β = 2μ₀⟨p⟩/B², typically a few percent in conventional tokamaks [2]. Fusion power density scales as pressure squared, so a reactor must operate at the highest beta its magnets and stability boundaries permit — which is why the Troyon beta limit became, in the words of the literature, simply *the* beta limit of tokamaks [2].

##3 Methodology

Our analysis rests on the ideal MHD model: the plasma is treated as a single, perfectly conducting fluid governed by mass continuity, momentum balance with the Lorentz force, an adiabatic equation of state, and Faraday's law with vanishing resistivity. The equilibrium condition reduces to force balance,

**∇p = J × B**,

together with Ampère's law and ∇ · **B** = 0. Assuming axisymmetry — no dependence on the toroidal angle — this system collapses to a single nonlinear elliptic partial differential equation for the poloidal flux function ψ(R, Z), the Grad–Shafranov equation, which we derive and solve in Section 4.1 [1].

Stability is assessed with the MHD *energy principle*: a static equilibrium is stable to ideal perturbations if and only if the potential energy change δW associated with every admissible plasma displacement is positive. Here δW splits into fluid, vacuum, and surface contributions, and minimizing it over trial displacements yields both analytic criteria (Suydam, Mercier) and the numerical eigenvalue problems solved by stability codes [9].

Our third methodological pillar is *dimensional scaling analysis*. Because the governing equations contain only a handful of dimensionless groups — normalized beta βN, safety factor q, normalized gyroradius ρ*, collisionality ν*, and the Greenwald fraction — experimental databases spanning orders of magnitude in machine size collapse onto power-law scalings. The most consequential is the ITER98(y,2) thermal confinement scaling, a log-linear regression over multi-machine H-mode data:

τE,th = 0.0562 · I^0.93 · B^0.15 · P^−0.69 · n^0.41 · M^0.19 · R^1.97 · ε^0.58 · κ^0.78

with current in mega-amperes, field in tesla, power in megawatts, density in 10¹⁹ m⁻³, and size in meters. This scaling underwrites the ITER performance projections examined in Section 4.4 [7].

> **Lemma:** In axisymmetric ideal MHD equilibrium, both the pressure p and the toroidal flux function F = RBφ are constant on flux surfaces, i.e., p = p(ψ) and F = F(ψ).
> **Proof:** Dotting ∇p = **J** × **B** with **B** gives **B** · ∇p = 0, so p is constant along field lines; axisymmetry extends this to entire flux surfaces. The toroidal component of the equilibrium then forces F to be likewise a flux function. ∎

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Grad–Shafranov Equilibria and Flux-Surface Geometry

Writing the magnetic field in axisymmetric cylindrical coordinates (R, φ, Z) through the poloidal flux ψ,

**B** = ∇φ × ∇ψ + F(ψ)∇φ,

and inserting this representation into force balance yields the Grad–Shafranov equation [1]:

Δ\*ψ = −μ₀R² p′(ψ) − F(ψ)F′(ψ),

where the elliptic operator Δ\*ψ = R ∂/∂R(∂ψ/∂R / R) + ∂²ψ/∂Z² and primes denote d/dψ. The right-hand side encodes the toroidal current density, Jφ = Rp′ + FF′/(μ₀R): the first term is the diamagnetic and Pfirsch–Schlüter current driven by the pressure gradient, the second the Ohmic and bootstrap contributions [1].

Solving this equation requires prescribing the two free flux functions p(ψ) and F(ψ) — or, equivalently, the pressure and current-density profiles — plus boundary conditions at the plasma edge. Analytic progress is possible for linear source functions (the Solov'ev equilibria), but realistic reconstructions solve the nonlinear problem iteratively, constrained by magnetics, Thomson scattering, and motional Stark effect measurements [1]. Every quantity of operational interest descends from ψ: the safety factor q(ψ), the magnetic shear, the Shafranov shift of the magnetic axis, and the elongation and triangularity of the boundary.

A hallmark prediction is the *Shafranov shift*: finite pressure displaces flux surfaces outward in major radius, with the axis shift scaling approximately as Δ ∝ (a²/R)(βp + li/2), where βp is the poloidal beta and li the internal inductance. This outward shift deepens the magnetic well on the inboard side and modifies ballooning stability — a first hint that equilibrium and stability cannot be separated.

```python
import numpy as np

MU0 = 4*np.pi*1e-7

def shafranov_shift(a, R, beta_p, l_i):
    """Outward shift of the magnetic axis (m), large-aspect-ratio estimate."""
    return (a**2 / R) * (beta_p + l_i/2)

for label, a, R, bp, li in [("JET-like", 1.0, 3.0, 0.8, 0.9),
                            ("ITER baseline", 2.0, 6.2, 0.7, 0.8)]:
    print(f"{label:14s} Delta = {shafranov_shift(a, R, bp, li)*100:.1f} cm")
```

### 4.2 The Troyon Beta Limit and Normalized-Beta Scaling

Because fusion power scales with pressure squared while magnet cost scales with field energy, the achievable beta is the decisive economic parameter. In 1984, Francis Troyon and collaborators performed a numerical survey of ideal MHD stability over tokamak equilibria and found that the maximum stable beta collapses onto a single scaling law [2]:

βmax = βN · I / (aB₀),

where I is the plasma current in mega-amperes, a the minor radius in meters, B₀ the toroidal field in tesla, and the *normalized beta* βN — expressed in the conventional units of percent·meter·tesla per mega-ampere — takes the approximately constant value 2.8. A companion study by Wesson and Sykes reached the same conclusion, and the result matched operating experience so well that it became known simply as the Troyon limit [2].

The physics behind the scaling is a competition between two instability families. At modest pressure, long-wavelength external kink modes set the boundary; at higher pressure with optimized profiles, short-wavelength ballooning modes take over. Both drives scale with the current-normalized pressure gradient, which is why βN — beta divided by I/(aB) — is the natural dimensionless measure of "distance to the limit."

Crucially, βN is not a universal constant: strong plasma shaping raises it substantially. Elongated, triangular cross-sections access βN ≈ 3.5–4, and dedicated experiments have reached absolute betas near twelve percent in agreement with Troyon scaling [6]. The high-poloidal-beta scenario developed for steady-state reactors exploits exactly this: since the bootstrap current fraction scales as fbs ∝ ε^0.5 βp ∝ ε^0.5 βN q95, raising βN simultaneously improves both fusion power density and the self-driven current fraction that a reactor needs for steady-state operation [6].

| Device / scenario | I (MA) | a (m) | B (T) | βN | βmax (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| JET-class conventional | 4.0 | 1.0 | 3.4 | 2.8 | 3.3 |
| DIII-D highly shaped | 1.6 | 0.67 | 2.1 | 3.5 | 4.0 |
| ITER baseline | 15.0 | 2.0 | 5.3 | 2.8 | 4.0 |
| Spherical tokamak | 1.0 | 0.6 | 0.5 | ~10 | ~33 |

The table illustrates the scaling's predictive power — and the spherical tokamak's dramatic departure from it, a consequence of very low aspect ratio rather than a violation of MHD [2].

```python
def troyon_beta_max(beta_N, I_MA, a_m, B_T):
    """Troyon scaling; beta_N in %-m-T/MA returns beta as a fraction."""
    return beta_N * I_MA / (a_m * B_T) / 100.0

cases = [("JET-class", 2.8, 4.0, 1.0, 3.4),
         ("DIII-D shaped", 3.5, 1.6, 0.67, 2.1),
         ("ITER baseline", 2.8, 15.0, 2.0, 5.3)]
for name, bN, I, a, B in cases:
    print(f"{name:14s} beta_max = {troyon_beta_max(bN, I, a, B)*100:.2f} %")
```

### 4.3 Ballooning Modes, Peeling Modes, and Edge Stability

Where the Troyon limit describes global pressure boundaries, the *ballooning mode* governs local ones. Consider a flux tube on the outboard midplane, where field-line curvature points in the same direction as the pressure gradient — the "bad curvature" region. An interchange-like perturbation that swaps adjacent flux tubes releases pressure energy faster than field-line bending can restore it, and because the mode can localize where curvature is worst while extending along the field line where it is favorable, it evades the stabilizing line-bending that suppresses pure interchanges. The result is a high toroidal-mode-number instability driven by the local pressure gradient, analyzed through the ballooning equation in the *s–α* diagram: magnetic shear s versus normalized pressure gradient α. Moderate shear stabilizes, but at high α the plasma can enter the *second stability* regime, where access to steeper gradients reopens [5].

At the plasma edge, a second drive appears. The H-mode transport barrier sustains enormous pressure gradients over a narrow pedestal, and the associated bootstrap current peaks at the edge, flattening the local shear. Reduced shear destabilizes ballooning modes, while the edge current itself drives *peeling modes* — low-to-intermediate-n external kinks fed by current-density gradients. Snyder and colleagues showed that the coupled *peeling–ballooning* mode, typically at toroidal mode numbers between three and twenty, sets the pedestal limit and triggers edge-localized modes (ELMs), the quasi-periodic eruptions that expel pedestal energy to the divertor [4]. Their model, implemented in the ELITE stability code and the EPED pedestal predictor, quantitatively reproduces pedestal heights across machines — a landmark success of ideal MHD [4].

Kinetic refinements matter. The ideal ballooning threshold used in EPED is increasingly replaced by the *kinetic ballooning mode* (KBM) constraint, and recent gyro-fluid calculations show that high-n global ballooning modes can limit access to second stability, providing a transport mechanism that clamps pedestal width evolution with pedestal poloidal beta [5]. For ITER, which must survive ELM heat loads on a tungsten divertor, whether the pedestal sits in first or second stability is a first-order design question [5][7].

### 4.4 The ITER Burning-Plasma Mission and Scenario Design

ITER is the first magnetic confinement device designed to produce a *burning plasma* — one in which alpha-particle self-heating dominates the power balance. Its principal parameters are fixed by the scaling laws of the preceding sections [3]:

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Major / minor radius R / a | 6.2 m / 2.0 m |
| Toroidal field | 5.3 T |
| Plasma current | 15 MA |
| Elongation κ / triangularity δ | 1.85 / 0.49 |
| Fusion power at Q = 10 | 500 MW |
| Burn duration | ~400 s |
| Installed heating & current drive | 73 MW |

The baseline scenario operates at fifteen mega-amperes with an edge safety factor q95 near three — safely above the Kruskal–Shafranov kink boundary — and a normalized beta near 1.8, deliberately below the Troyon limit to preserve disruption margin [7]. Fifty megawatts of auxiliary heating (33 MW neutral beams, 20 MW electron cyclotron) drive five hundred megawatts of fusion power; the one hundred megawatts of alpha power then exceeds the external input, satisfying the conventional definition of a burning plasma [3].

First-principles gyrokinetic predictions of this scenario, coupling global electromagnetic turbulence simulations to transport solvers, find temperature and density profiles consistent with the Q = 10 target and a pedestal pressure near 130 kPa — squarely in the peeling–ballooning-limited regime analyzed above [7]. The remaining uncertainties are precisely the subjects of Sections 4.2–4.3: whether the pedestal accesses second stability, how large unmitigated ELMs will be, and whether alpha-particle-driven Alfvén eigenmodes redistribute the fast-ion population before it thermalizes [5][7].

##5 Empirical/Proofs

The theoretical boundaries derived above are not merely computational; several are theorems, and all have survived confrontation with experiment.

> **Theorem (Kruskal–Shafranov):** A cylindrical plasma column is ideally stable to the m = 1 external kink only if the edge safety factor exceeds unity; toroidal tokamak operation in practice requires qa above two.
> **Proof sketch:** Minimizing δW over helical displacements of a screw pinch yields the stability condition q(a) > 1, with toroidal and shaped-geometry corrections raising the practical bound. Experimental disruption databases confirm operation below this boundary is inaccessible [9]. ∎

> **Theorem (Suydam criterion):** A localized interchange near a rational surface is stable if (rBz²/8π)(q′/q)² + 2p′ > 0 — magnetic shear stabilizes, pressure gradient destabilizes.
> **Proof sketch:** Expansion of the energy integral δW for radially localized trial functions gives a necessary condition on the competition between shear and pressure drive; violation guarantees instability [9]. ∎

Empirically, the Troyon scaling has been confirmed across dozens of devices: maximum stable beta follows βN·I/(aB) with βN between 2.8 and 4 depending on shaping, and the predicted limiting instabilities — long-wavelength kinks and short-wavelength ballooning modes — have been observed with the expected mode structures [2][6]. The density boundary follows the Greenwald scaling, nG = Ip/(πa²) in units of 10²⁰ m⁻³ with current in mega-amperes, giving 1.2 × 10²⁰ m⁻³ for the ITER baseline — a limit set by edge cooling and MHD destabilization rather than ideal theory, yet obeyed with remarkable universality. Confinement itself follows the ITER98(y,2) scaling quoted in Section 3, whose strong size dependence (τE ∝ R^1.97) is the fundamental justification for building at ITER scale [7].

##6 Limitations

Ideal MHD, the framework of this thesis, is a powerful but deliberately coarse lens. Five limitations deserve emphasis.

1. **Resistivity and reconnection.** Ideal MHD forbids magnetic reconnection; real plasmas tear. Neoclassical tearing modes — magnetic islands seeded at rational surfaces and sustained by the missing bootstrap current within the island — degrade confinement below ideal limits and require active stabilization by electron cyclotron current drive [6].
2. **Kinetic effects.** The ideal ballooning threshold is only an approximation to the kinetic ballooning mode that actually regulates gradients; quantitative pedestal prediction demands gyrokinetic treatment, and recent work shows ideal-MHD second-stability access can be closed by kinetic and global effects [5].
3. **Disruptions.** When stability boundaries are crossed, the plasma can terminate in a disruption: thermal quench in milliseconds, current quench driving halo currents and runaway electron beams carrying a large fraction of the fifteen-mega-ampere current into the wall. ITER's disruption mitigation system exists because no scaling law repeals this risk [3].
4. **Steady state.** The baseline scenario is inductively driven and pulsed (~400 s). True steady-state operation needs the bootstrap fractions of the high-βp scenario, which trades disruption margin for current-drive efficiency — a tension ideal MHD quantifies but cannot resolve [6].
5. **Beyond physics.** Tritium breeding, neutron-resistant materials, and divertor heat-flux handling are engineering limits as hard as any beta limit, and they lie outside MHD entirely [3].

##7 Conclusion

The tokamak's operational space is a room whose walls are theorems. The Grad–Shafranov equation fixes the shape of the room; the Kruskal–Shafranov, Troyon, and Greenwald scalings locate its walls; ballooning and peeling modes patrol the edges, particularly the pedestal where H-mode performance is won or lost. ITER is an experiment designed, bolt by bolt, to inhabit the largest stable volume this theory permits: fifteen mega-amperes, five hundred megawatts, a burning plasma held just inside every boundary derived here [3][7].

What remains is to test whether the map is complete. Burning plasmas will probe alpha-particle physics no present device can access, kinetic modifications to ideal boundaries will be measured against gyrokinetic predictions, and the pedestal's stability regime will be settled by data rather than debate [5][7]. If the scaling laws hold at reactor scale, the path from ITER to a demonstration power plant is an engineering program. If they break, the breakage itself will be the most interesting physics result of the century. Either way, MHD stability theory — from a 1957 inequality to the peeling–ballooning diagram — will have earned its place as the grammar in which the answer is written [8][4].
