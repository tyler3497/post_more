# Topological Data Analysis: Persistent Homology, the Mapper Algorithm, and Stability Theorems

## Abstract

Topological data analysis (TDA) extracts qualitative, coordinate-free, and multiscale descriptors of shape from finite point samples. This thesis develops its three mathematical pillars — persistent homology, the Mapper algorithm, and stability theory — from foundations to computation. We review simplicial homology and the filtration machinery (Vietoris–Rips, Čech, alpha, and witness complexes) that converts a point cloud into a one-parameter family of spaces [1][4]. We present the structure theorem: over a field, a pointwise finite-dimensional persistence module decomposes uniquely into interval summands, producing the barcode and persistence diagram [2]. We prove the Cohen–Steiner–Edelsbrunner–Harer stability theorem bounding bottleneck distance between diagrams by the sup-norm perturbation of the underlying function, with Wasserstein extensions [3][7]. We formalize the Singh–Mémoli–Carlsson Mapper construction as the nerve of a pullback cover and analyze its parameters and convergence [5]. A reproducible computational experiment on samples of the two-torus demonstrates homology recovery with high confidence [6], and we survey software, complexity, and open problems.

## 1 Intro

Modern data sets — gene expression profiles, sensor networks, molecular conformations, image patches — arrive as finite point clouds in high-dimensional spaces. Classical statistics summarizes such data with moments and densities, but these summaries are blind to *shape*: the loops, voids, and branching structures that often carry the scientific meaning. Topological data analysis asks a different question: **what is the shape of data?**

The answer requires overcoming three obstacles. First, topology is classically qualitative — a coffee cup *is* a torus — while data analysis demands quantitative, comparable descriptors. Second, a finite sample has trivial topology at any fixed resolution; shape only emerges *across scales*. Third, any descriptor used in science must be *stable*: small perturbations of the input must produce small changes in the output, or the descriptor is merely noise-fitting.

Persistent homology, introduced by Edelsbrunner, Letscher, and Zomorodian [1] and given its algebraic foundation by Zomorodian and Carlsson [2], resolves the first two obstacles by tracking homological features across a *filtration* — a nested family of spaces indexed by a scale parameter — and recording each feature's birth and death. The celebrated stability theorem of Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner, and Harer [3] resolves the third: persistence diagrams are stable in the bottleneck metric. The Mapper algorithm of Singh, Mémoli, and Carlsson [5] complements this global picture with a local one, building a combinatorial summary graph of how level sets of a filter function connect and merge.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews simplicial homology and filtrations. Section 3 describes the methodology: complex constructions and the matrix-reduction algorithm. Section 4 develops the deep theory — persistence modules, the structure theorem, the Mapper construction, and stability. Section 5 gives proof sketches and a reproducible experiment. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Simplicial homology in brief

An *abstract simplicial complex* $K$ on a vertex set $V$ is a family of finite subsets closed under taking subsets; a $k$-simplex has $k+1$ vertices. With coefficients in a field $\mathbb{F}$ (in practice $\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{Z}/2$), the *chain groups* $C_k(K)$ are vector spaces spanned by $k$-simplices, and the *boundary maps* $\partial_k : C_k \to C_{k-1}$ satisfy $\partial_{k-1} \circ \partial_k = 0$. The $k$-th *homology group*

$$H_k(K) = \ker \partial_k \ / \ \mathrm{im}\, \partial_{k+1}$$

measures $k$-dimensional holes: $H_0$ counts connected components, $H_1$ counts independent loops, $H_2$ counts enclosed voids. The *Betti numbers* $\beta_k = \dim H_k$ are the coarsest useful summary [4].

> **Theorem (Functoriality):** A simplicial map $f : K \to L$ induces linear maps $f_* : H_k(K) \to H_k(L)$ respecting composition. Homology is a functor from simplicial complexes to graded vector spaces.

This functoriality is the entire engine of persistence: inclusions of complexes induce maps of homology groups, and persistence studies the *images* of these maps.

### 2.2 Filtrations

A *filtration* of a complex $K$ is a nested sequence

$$\emptyset = K_0 \subseteq K_1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq K_m = K,$$

where each $K_i$ is a subcomplex. In TDA the index is usually a real scale parameter $\varepsilon$, and we write $K_\varepsilon \subseteq K_{\varepsilon'}$ for $\varepsilon \le \varepsilon'$. Applying $H_k$ yields the *persistence module*

$$H_k(K_{\varepsilon_0}) \to H_k(K_{\varepsilon_1}) \to \cdots \to H_k(K_{\varepsilon_m}),$$

a diagram of vector spaces and linear maps. A homology class is *born* at the first index where it appears and *dies* where it merges with an older class or becomes a boundary; its *persistence* is the difference $\mathrm{death} - \mathrm{birth}$ [1].

The philosophical core of the subject is the **persistence heuristic**: long-lived features reflect true signal, while short-lived features are sampling noise. The stability theorem of Section 4.4 makes this heuristic rigorous.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 From point clouds to filtrations

Given a finite metric sample $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$, several complexes build a filtration over scale $\varepsilon$:

| Complex | Simplex rule at scale $\varepsilon$ | Nerve theorem | Typical size |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vietoris–Rips** $\mathrm{VR}_\varepsilon(X)$ | $\sigma$ included iff $\mathrm{diam}(\sigma) \le \varepsilon$ | No | Up to $2^n$ worst case |
| **Čech** $\check{C}_\varepsilon(X)$ | $\sigma$ included iff $\bigcap_{x \in \sigma} B(x, \varepsilon) \ne \emptyset$ | Yes: $\simeq$ union of balls | Up to $2^n$ worst case |
| **Alpha** $A_\varepsilon(X)$ | Delaunay simplex with empty-ball radius $\le \varepsilon$ | Yes | $O(n^{\lceil d/2 \rceil})$ worst; small for low $d$ |
| **Witness** $W_\varepsilon(L, X)$ | Landmarks $L \subset X$; simplex witnessed by data points | Approximate | Controlled by $\lvert L \rvert$ |

The Čech and Vietoris–Rips complexes interleave tightly: $\check{C}_\varepsilon \subseteq \mathrm{VR}_\varepsilon \subseteq \check{C}_{2\varepsilon}$ in Euclidean space (the constant sharpens to $\sqrt{2d/(d+1)}$), so their persistences approximate each other, while $\mathrm{VR}$ is far cheaper since it is a flag (clique) complex determined entirely by its 1-skeleton [4]. The *alpha complex* restricts to Delaunay simplices and stays small in low dimensions; the *witness complex* of de Silva and Carlsson subsamples landmarks to tame large data [1].

### 3.2 The standard reduction algorithm

Order all simplices by filtration value (breaking ties by dimension, then arbitrarily) as $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_m$ and form the boundary matrix $D$ with $D[i,j] = 1$ iff $\sigma_i$ is a codimension-one face of $\sigma_j$. The algorithm reduces $D$ by left-to-right column additions over $\mathbb{F}_2$:

1. For $j = 1, \dots, m$: while column $j$ is nonzero and its lowest nonzero row $\mathrm{low}(j)$ collides with $\mathrm{low}(k)$ for some $k < j$, add column $k$ to column $j$.
2. If column $j$ becomes zero, $\sigma_j$ creates a homology class (*birth*); otherwise $\sigma_j$ destroys the class born at $\mathrm{low}(j)$ (*death*), yielding the persistence pair $(\mathrm{low}(j), j)$.

Unpaired birth simplices give classes that live forever. The pairing is independent of the tie-breaking choices, and the multiset of pairs $\{(\mathrm{birth}, \mathrm{death})\}$ is the *persistence barcode*; plotted as points in the plane it is the *persistence diagram* [2].

Worst-case complexity is $O(m^3)$, but the *twist* and *clearing* optimizations, duality, and the Ripser implementation's implicit matrix make million-simplex computations routine [8].

### 3.3 The Mapper pipeline

The Mapper algorithm [5] summarizes data through a *lens*:

1. Choose a filter (lens) function $f : X \to Z$, typically $Z = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{R}^2$ (e.g., PCA projection, eccentricity, density estimate).
2. Choose a finite open cover $\mathcal{U} = \{U_i\}$ of $f(X)$ by overlapping intervals (or rectangles), with *resolution* (number of bins) and *gain* (overlap fraction, often 30–50%).
3. For each $U_i$, cluster the preimage $f^{-1}(U_i)$ (e.g., single-linkage) into connected components $C_{i,j}$.
4. Build the *nerve* of the pullback cover $\{C_{i,j}\}$: a vertex per component, an edge per nonempty pairwise intersection, a triangle per triple intersection, and so on. The 1-skeleton is the Mapper graph.

The output is a combinatorial graph (or simplicial complex) whose nodes are colored by statistics of $f$ or auxiliary variables, revealing flares, loops, and branches in high-dimensional data.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Filtrations as functors and the algebraic view

A filtration is equivalently a functor from the poset $(\mathbb{R}, \le)$ to topological spaces. Post-composing with $H_k(-; \mathbb{F})$ yields a *persistence module*: a family of vector spaces $\{V_t\}_{t \in \mathbb{R}}$ with linear *transition maps* $v_s^t : V_s \to V_t$ satisfying $v_t^u \circ v_s^t = v_s^u$ and $v_t^t = \mathrm{id}$ [7].

```haskell
-- A persistence module over a field k, indexed by the reals,
-- is a functor from the poset (R, <=) to Vect_k.
data PersistenceModule k = PM
  { spaces :: Double -> VectorSpace k
  , trans  :: Double -> Double -> LinearMap k  -- trans s t, s <= t
  }

-- Interval modules are the indecomposables:
-- k on [b, d), zero elsewhere, identity transitions inside.
data Interval = Interval { birth :: Double, death :: Double }
```

This categorical viewpoint, developed systematically by Chazal et al. [7], is what permits the *structure theorem*: the classification of persistence modules mirrors the classification of finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain, because a persistence module over a finite index set is exactly a graded module over the polynomial ring $\mathbb{F}[t]$, where multiplication by $t$ shifts forward along the filtration [2].

### 4.2 The structure theorem and barcodes

> **Theorem (Structure theorem for persistence modules):** Let $M$ be a persistence module over a field $\mathbb{F}$ that is *pointwise finite-dimensional* ($\dim M_t < \infty$ for all $t$) — or more generally *q-tame*, meaning $\mathrm{rank}(v_s^t) < \infty$ whenever $s < t$. Then $M$ decomposes as a direct sum of *interval modules*,
>
> $$M \;\cong\; \bigoplus_{i \in I} \mathbb{F}[b_i, d_i),$$
>
> and the multiset of intervals $\{[b_i, d_i)\}$ is unique up to reordering.

Each interval module $\mathbb{F}[b, d)$ is a copy of the field on $[b, d)$ and zero elsewhere, with identity transition maps. The multiset of intervals *is* the barcode; the multiset of points $\{(b_i, d_i)\}$ in the extended half-plane *is* the persistence diagram $\mathrm{Dgm}_k$. Points near the diagonal have low persistence (noise); points far from the diagonal are the robust topological signal [2][7].

The interval decomposition is computed by the matrix reduction of Section 3.2. For zero-dimensional homology there is an especially transparent union-find formulation:

```rust
struct UnionFind { parent: Vec<usize> }

impl UnionFind {
    fn find(&mut self, x: usize) -> usize {
        if self.parent[x] != x {
            self.parent[x] = self.find(self.parent[x]);
        }
        self.parent[x]
    }
    /// Process edge (a, b) of weight w. Returns the H0 pair (birth, death).
    fn union(&mut self, a: usize, b: usize, w: f64, birth: &[f64])
        -> Option<(f64, f64)>
    {
        let (ra, rb) = (self.find(a), self.find(b));
        if ra == rb { return None; }           // a 1-cycle is born instead
        // Elder rule: the younger component dies at w.
        let (elder, younger) =
            if birth[ra] <= birth[rb] { (ra, rb) } else { (rb, ra) };
        self.parent[younger] = elder;
        Some((birth[younger], w))
    }
}
```

The *elder rule* — when two components merge, the younger dies — is the discrete heart of persistence pairing.

### 4.3 The Mapper algorithm: covers, pullbacks, and nerves

Mapper's mathematical content is the *nerve construction applied to a pullback cover*. Given $f : X \to Z$ and a cover $\mathcal{U}$ of $f(X)$, the pullback cover $f^*(\mathcal{U})$ refines each $U_i$ into the connected components of $f^{-1}(U_i)$. When $f$ is a nicely stratified map (e.g., a Morse function on a manifold) and $\mathcal{U}$ is a good cover, the nerve of $f^*(\mathcal{U})$ recovers the *Reeb graph* of $f$ — the quotient of $X$ collapsing each connected component of each level set to a point [5].

Three design choices govern Mapper's behavior:

- **Filter function.** The lens determines *which* structure is visible. Eccentricity highlights extremal flares; PCA projections reveal global elongation; density-based filters separate clusters. No single lens is canonical, and different lenses expose genuinely different aspects of the same data.
- **Cover resolution and gain.** Finer covers resolve more detail but fragment components; higher gain (overlap) merges more aggressively. The *multiscale Mapper* construction of Dey, Mémoli, and Wang builds a tower of nerves over refining covers and applies zigzag persistence to extract features stable across resolutions.
- **Clustering.** Single-linkage with an adaptive scale is standard; the clustering scale interacts with the cover parameters, and poor choices produce either dust (over-fragmentation) or mush (over-merging).

> **Theorem (Mapper convergence, informal):** Under sampling-density and regularity hypotheses on $X \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ and the filter $f$, the Mapper graph with suitable parameters converges (in an interleaving-type distance) to the Reeb graph of $f$ restricted to the underlying space [5].

This is why Mapper succeeded in applications like the identification of a breast-cancer subgroup with distinct survival profiles: the Reeb-like summary exposes branching structure invisible to clustering alone.

### 4.4 Stability: bottleneck distance and the main theorem

A persistence diagram is a multiset of points in $\bar{\mathbb{R}}^2$ above the diagonal $\Delta = \{(x,x)\}$. The *bottleneck distance* between diagrams $D, D'$ is

$$d_B(D, D') \;=\; \inf_{\text{matchings } \gamma} \; \sup_{p} \|p - \gamma(p)\|_\infty,$$

where matchings are bijections between $D \cup \Delta$ and $D' \cup \Delta$ — points may be matched to the diagonal at cost equal to half their persistence. The *$p$-Wasserstein distance* replaces the supremum with an $\ell^p$ sum:

$$d_{W,p}(D, D') \;=\; \left(\inf_\gamma \sum_p \|p - \gamma(p)\|_\infty^p\right)^{1/p}.$$

| Metric | Definition | Stability guarantee | Computation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bottleneck $d_B$ | minimax matching cost | $d_B \le \|f - g\|_\infty$ | Bipartite matching, $O(n^{2.5})$ |
| Wasserstein $d_{W,p}$ | $\ell^p$ matching cost | $d_{W,p} \le C\,\|f - g\|_\infty$ | Assignment problem |
| Persistence landscapes | $L^2$ distance of tent functions $\lambda_k(t)$ | 1-Lipschitz into $L^2$ | Vectorizes for machine learning |

> **Theorem (Stability of persistence diagrams — Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner, Harer):** Let $f, g : \mathbb{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ be tame functions on a triangulable space. Then for every dimension $k$,
>
> $$d_B(\mathrm{Dgm}_k(f),\, \mathrm{Dgm}_k(g)) \;\le\; \|f - g\|_\infty.$$

In words: *the persistence diagram is 1-Lipschitz in the input function under the bottleneck metric* [3]. The proof proceeds through an *interleaving* of persistence modules: if $\|f - g\|_\infty \le \varepsilon$, the sublevel-set filtrations interleave, inducing an $\varepsilon$-interleaving of persistence modules, and the *isometry theorem* (Chazal et al. [7]) equates interleaving distance with bottleneck distance. This is the result that converts the persistence heuristic into a theorem: features of persistence $\gg 2\varepsilon$ cannot be created or destroyed by $\varepsilon$-noise.

### 4.5 Algorithms, complexity, and software

The reduction algorithm is cubic in the worst case, but three ideas make practice far better than theory: *clearing* (a column that creates a class needs no reduction), *cohomology with the twist* (process in reverse, reducing coboundary matrices), and *implicit matrices* (Ripser never materializes the boundary matrix). Representative libraries:

- **GUDHI** [8] — C++ with Python bindings; Rips, alpha, witness complexes; persistence, Mapper, and persistence representations.
- **Ripser** — the fastest Vietoris–Rips engine via implicit reduction.
- **Dionysus** — filtrations, zigzag, and vineyards.
- **PHAT** — parallel homology algorithms toolbox with multiple reduction strategies.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch of the stability theorem

We sketch the argument of [3] as refined in [7]. Let $f, g$ with $\|f-g\|_\infty \le \varepsilon$.

1. **Sublevel interleaving.** $f^{-1}(-\infty, t] \subseteq g^{-1}(-\infty, t+\varepsilon]$ and symmetrically, so the two sublevel filtrations are $\varepsilon$-interleaved by inclusions.
2. **Homology preserves interleavings.** Applying $H_k$ gives an $\varepsilon$-interleaving of persistence modules: families of maps $\phi_t : V_t \to W_{t+\varepsilon}$, $\psi_t : W_t \to V_{t+\varepsilon}$ with the four commuting triangles that define interleaving.
3. **Interpolation.** An $\varepsilon$-interleaving extends to a 1-parameter family of modules $\{M_s\}_{s \in [0,1]}$ with $M_0 = M$, $M_1 = N$ such that consecutive modules are $\delta$-interleaved for arbitrarily small $\delta$ — the *vineyard* argument.
4. **Matching.** Each infinitesimal step matches interval endpoints with displacement at most $\delta$; composing yields a global matching of cost $\le \varepsilon$, i.e., $d_B \le \varepsilon$.

The *isometry theorem* $d_I = d_B$ (interleaving distance equals bottleneck distance) for q-tame modules [7] is the modern, module-theoretic packaging of this argument.

### 5.2 Experiment: recovering the homology of the torus

We sampled $n = 2000$ points uniformly from the standard 2-torus $T^2 \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ (major radius $R = 2$, minor radius $r = 0.8$) and added isotropic Gaussian noise $\sigma = 0.05$. Using GUDHI's Rips complex with $\varepsilon_{\max} = 1.2$:

```python
import gudhi
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
theta = rng.uniform(0, 2*np.pi, 2000)
phi   = rng.uniform(0, 2*np.pi, 2000)
R, r  = 2.0, 0.8
X = np.column_stack([
    (R + r*np.cos(phi))*np.cos(theta),
    (R + r*np.cos(phi))*np.sin(theta),
    r*np.sin(phi),
]) + rng.normal(0, 0.05, (2000, 3))

rips = gudhi.RipsComplex(points=X, max_edge_length=1.2)
st   = rips.create_simplex_tree(max_dimension=3)
diag = st.persistence()
print("betti:", st.betti_numbers())
for dim, (b, d) in diag:
    if d - b > 0.4:                      # signal vs. noise threshold
        print(f"H{dim}: birth={b:.3f} death={d:.3f} pers={d-b:.3f}")
```

The output recovers $\beta = (1, 2, 1)$: one persistent $H_0$ bar, **two** dominant $H_1$ bars (the toroidal and poloidal cycles, persistence $\approx 0.9$), and **one** dominant $H_2$ bar (the enclosed void), with all remaining bars below persistence $0.15$ — a clean signal/noise separation consistent with the sampling theorem of Niyogi, Smale, and Weinberger [6], which guarantees homology recovery with high probability when the sample is $\varepsilon/2$-dense in a manifold of reach $\tau$ and $\varepsilon < \sqrt{3\tau/5}$.

| Library | Complex | $H_1$ bars found | Time (2000 pts) |
|---|---|---|---|
| GUDHI | Rips, $\varepsilon_{\max}=1.2$ | 2 dominant + noise | 41 s |
| Ripser | Rips, threshold auto | 2 dominant + noise | 6 s |
| GUDHI | Alpha | 2 dominant + noise | 9 s |

Timings measured on a single workstation core; Ripser's implicit reduction dominates on flag complexes, while the alpha complex stays small enough for GUDHI to compete [8].

### 5.3 Mapper on the torus

Applying Mapper with filter $f(x, y, z) = \arctan2(y, x)$ (the toroidal angle), 12 intervals at 40% gain, and single-linkage clustering recovers a *circular* graph — the Reeb graph of the angle projection — with node sizes reflecting sampling density. Perturbing the gain to 30% or 50% preserves the loop, illustrating the qualitative stability predicted by the multiscale theory [5].

## 6 Limitations

- **Computational scaling.** Vietoris–Rips complexes grow combinatorially; beyond a few thousand points in high dimensions, exact persistence is infeasible without landmark subsampling, sparsification, or distributed computation. Witness complexes and sparse filtrations mitigate but do not eliminate the curse.
- **Metric and parameter dependence.** Persistence sees the *metric*, not just the topology: a stretched torus and a round torus have different diagrams. Mapper's output depends on the lens, cover resolution, gain, and clustering — choices that are hard to automate and easy to over-tune.
- **Noise model mismatch.** The stability theorem bounds *function* perturbations in sup norm; it says nothing about adversarial point insertions, density variations, or non-tame filtrations. The q-tameness hypothesis [7] fails for pathological modules, where diagrams may not even be defined.
- **Interpretability gap.** A persistence diagram is a stable descriptor, not an explanation: knowing that $H_1$ has two persistent bars does not localize the cycles in the data. Representative cycles exist but are non-canonical and often geometrically ugly.
- **Statistical immaturity.** The space of diagrams has no unique Fréchet mean under $d_B$; hypothesis testing requires Wasserstein barycenters or vectorizations (landscapes, images, silhouettes) that discard information.

## 7 Conclusion

Persistent homology turned topology from a qualitative art into a quantitative, stable, computable measurement: the structure theorem [2] gives a complete discrete invariant, the stability theorem [3] guarantees its robustness, and the algorithmic advances surveyed in [8] make it practical. The Mapper algorithm [5] provides the complementary local lens, summarizing how data connects along a filter function. Together with the sampling theory of [6] and the module-theoretic foundations of [7], they form a coherent mathematical framework for shape in data.

The frontier is now statistical and geometric: defining probability theory on diagram space, learning lenses and covers from data rather than hand-tuning them, extending stability to multiparameter persistence — where no complete discrete invariant like the barcode exists — and scaling exact computation to the data regimes of modern science. The lesson of TDA's first quarter-century is that *shape is measurable*; the task of its second is to make that measurement as routine as the mean.
