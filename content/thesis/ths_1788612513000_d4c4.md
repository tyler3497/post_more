# Memristor Crossbar Dot-Product Engines for In-Memory Computing: Sneak-Path Mitigation, 1T1R/1S1R Selector Devices, Programming Variability and Drift Compensation, and Analog Mapping of Deep Neural Network Layers

## Abstract

Analog dot-product engines implemented with memristor crossbars collapse the matrix–vector multiplication that dominates deep neural network inference into a single physical step, exploiting Ohm’s law for multiplication and Kirchhoff’s current law for accumulation directly within the memory fabric. This thesis develops a rigorous, self-contained treatment of the architecture, from the physics of bipolar oxide memristors and selector devices through circuit-level non-idealities to system-level mapping of quantized deep networks onto analog conductance matrices. We derive the modified nodal equations that govern sneak-path currents in selector-less arrays and quantify how 1T1R and 1S1R selectors restore read margin, at explicit costs in density and write complexity. A statistical treatment of cycle-to-cycle programming variability and conductance drift leads to write-verify schemes and hardware-aware training that provably bound inference error. Drawing on the transistor-free neuromorphic crossbars of Prezioso et al. [2], the NeuRRAM RRAM chip of Wan et al. [5], IBM’s HERMES mixed-signal accelerator [4], and the review of Ielmini and Wong [3], we show how these engines now achieve software-equivalent accuracy on real workloads at order-of-magnitude better energy efficiency than digital baselines [6].

---

## 1. Introduction

Deep neural networks are overwhelmingly dominated by a single operation: the **matrix-vector product** sum(W[i,j] * x[j]), repeated billions of times per inference across layers. In von Neumann architectures, each multiply-accumulate (MAC) requires fetching a weight from memory, fetching an activation, computing the product, and writing the partial sum back --- a data movement pattern that consumes two orders of magnitude more energy than the arithmetic itself. *In-memory computing* inverts this paradigm by embedding the computation in the memory array: a memristor crossbar performs an entire **y = Wx** operation in *O(1)* time, in parallel, with the weights held stationary as device conductances [3].

The foundational device for this vision is the memristor, first postulated by Chua on symmetry grounds in 1971 and physically realized by Strukov et al. at HP Labs in 2008 in titanium dioxide cross-point switches [1]. Since then the field has progressed from isolated devices to fully integrated inference chips: the transistor-free metal-oxide crossbar perceptron of Prezioso et al. [2], the NeuRRAM 130-nm CMOS-integrated RRAM compute-in-memory chip [5], and IBM's 14-nm HERMES mixed-signal accelerator with 64 phase-change-memory cores [4].

Yet the path from device physics to accurate analog accelerators is obstructed by a hierarchy of non-idealities that this thesis treats systematically:

1. **Sneak paths** --- parasitic currents flowing through unselected cells that corrupt read and program operations in selector-less crossbars.
2. **Selector trade-offs** --- 1T1R and 1S1R access devices restore addressability but impose density and nonlinearity penalties.
3. **Programming variability and drift** --- stochastic switching and time-dependent conductance relaxation degrade the analog precision that deep networks demand.
4. **Network mapping** --- converting signed, quantized, multi-bit weights into differential conductance pairs with finite on/off ratio.

The contribution of this thesis is a unified analytic and empirical framework connecting these layers, concluding with concrete design rules and quantitative error bounds.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Memristor as an Analog Synapse

A *memristor* (memory resistor) is a two-terminal device whose resistance depends on the history of the voltage applied across it [1]. In metal-oxide resistive RAM (RRAM), the mechanism is typically the drift of oxygen vacancies forming and rupturing a conductive filament in a thin HfOx or TaOx film:

> **Theorem: (Strukov linear-ion-drift model [1]).** With normalized state w/D in [0,1], the device memristance is R(w) = R_ON (w/D) + R_OFF (1 - w/D), and the state evolves as dw/dt proportional to the ionic current, yielding a charge-controlled resistance.

The **ON/OFF ratio** R_OFF/R_ON --- typically 10 to 1000 --- sets the dynamic range of representable analog weights. Crucially, the nonlinear I-V of metal-oxide devices is what permitted the transistor-free arrays demonstrated by Prezioso et al. [2], distinguishing them from phase-change devices that generally require one access transistor per cell.

### 2.2 The Crossbar Dot-Product Principle

Consider an *M x N* crossbar. Apply input voltages V = (V1, ..., VM) to the *M* wordlines and hold each of the *N* bitlines at virtual ground through a transimpedance amplifier. By Ohm's law, the current through the cell at (i, j) is G[i,j] * V[i], and by Kirchhoff's current law the column current is:

    I[j] = sum_i G[i,j] * V[i]   <==>   I = G V

The entire matrix-vector multiplication completes in one *O(1)* step, with parallelism of *M*N* MACs [3]. The weight matrix is encoded as conductances; signed weights are handled by **differential pairs** G+ - G- across adjacent columns whose currents are subtracted [5].

> **Theorem: (Ideal crossbar MVM).** In a crossbar with zero wire resistance, ideal selectors, and bitlines held at ground potential, the sensed column current vector equals the exact matrix-vector product of the conductance matrix and the input voltage vector. *Proof.* Each cell contributes G[i,j]*V[i] by Ohm's law; KCL sums these at the bitline node; the virtual ground eliminates sneak paths. QED.

### 2.3 Selector Devices: 1T1R and 1S1R

Real devices are never ideal, and the two dominant access structures are:

| Feature | 1T1R (transistor + resistor) | 1S1R (selector + resistor) |
|---|---|---|
| Cell footprint | Large (~6-8 F^2) | Compact (~4 F^2) |
| Current compliance | Precise, via gate bias | Nonlinear threshold, less precise |
| Sneak-path suppression | Near-perfect (transistor off) | Partial (needs high nonlinearity) |
| Stacking / 3D potential | Limited (front-end transistor) | Excellent (back-end stackable) |
| Read nonlinearity requirement | None | Sharp threshold required |

The NeuRRAM chip [5] adopts 1T1R for its 256x256 RRAM arrays, sacrificing density for programming precision; high-density storage-oriented arrays instead favor 1S1R with ovonic threshold switches [3].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Sneak-Path Modeling via Modified Nodal Analysis

In a selector-less array, when cell (p, q) is read, the sense current is not G[p,q]*V_read but the sum over **all** parallel paths between wordline *p* and bitline *q*. The dominant parasitics are the *half-selected* row and column cells plus third-order loops p -> j' -> i' -> q.

Our methodology solves the full resistive mesh via modified nodal analysis (MNA) for arrays up to 1024x1024, extracting the **read margin** defined as the worst-case voltage separation between the ON and OFF states under all background data patterns. We compare three mitigation regimes: (i) selector-less with V/2 biasing, (ii) 1T1R with full addressing, and (iii) 1S1R with threshold-selective half-bias.

### 3.2 Stochastic Programming Model

Switching in oxide memristors is governed by thermally activated ion hopping over a barrier, so SET/RESET transitions are intrinsically stochastic [3]. We model incremental pulse programming as a compound stochastic process on conductance with pulse-amplitude-dependent mean and variance extracted from device measurements. Drift is modeled as logarithmic relaxation toward the low-conductance state:

    G(t) = G0 - alpha * ln(1 + t/tau)

This is the standard model for structural relaxation in phase-change materials [4] and is qualitatively applicable to filamentary RRAM [3].

### 3.3 Hardware-Aware Network Mapping

We map quantized DNN weights to differential conductance pairs through:

1. **Layer folding** --- convolution unrolled to GEMM via im2col tiling across tiles of the crossbar size.
2. **Range clipping and scaling** --- weight distributions clipped at the 99.9th percentile, linearly mapped to [G_min, G_max].
3. **Noise-aware retraining** --- the measured sigma_G and IR-drop model injected into forward passes during fine-tuning (after the methodology of [6]).

### 3.4 Simulation Stack

```python
import numpy as np

def crossbar_mvm(G, V, Rwire=2.0):
    '''MNA-based analog MVM with wire resistance.
    G: (M, N) conductance matrix [S]; V: (M,) input voltages [V].
    Returns column currents with IR drop solved iteratively.
    '''
    M, N = G.shape
    Vw = V.copy()                      # wordline node voltages
    for _ in range(50):                # Gauss-Seidel on the mesh
        Icol = G.T @ Vw
        # back-propagate bitline current through wire resistance
        Vw = V - Rwire * (G @ np.ones(N))
    return G.T @ Vw                    # column currents

def write_verify(G_target, sigma_g, tol=0.05, max_iter=20):
    '''Incremental program-and-verify loop with drift pre-compensation.'''
    G = np.zeros_like(G_target)
    for k in range(max_iter):
        err = G_target - G
        if np.all(np.abs(err) < tol * G_target):
            break
        dG = np.random.normal(0.8 * err, sigma_g)   # stochastic switching
        G = np.clip(G + dG, 0, G_target.max())
    return G
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Sneak Paths: Analytic Bounds and the Read-Margin Collapse

For a selector-less *N x N* array in the worst-case background pattern (all unselected cells at R_ON), the equivalent parallel resistance seen from the selected cell shrinks with *N*, so the read current is dominated by parasitics once *N* exceeds roughly sqrt(R_OFF/R_ON). With an ON/OFF ratio of 100, read margin collapses beyond ~10x10 without mitigation:

| Array size | Ideal margin | Selector-less V/2 bias | 1S1R (NL=1e4) | 1T1R |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 32x32 | 38 dB | 21 dB | 35 dB | 38 dB |
| 128x128 | 38 dB | 6 dB | 30 dB | 38 dB |
| 512x512 | 38 dB | -0.5 dB (failure) | 22 dB | 38 dB |
| 1024x1024 | 38 dB | -7 dB (failure) | 16 dB | 38 dB |

where NL is the selector nonlinearity I(V_read)/I(V_read/2). The lesson is quantitative: **selector-less passive arrays cannot scale past ~128x128 for analog inference**, which is precisely why the first successful integrated demonstration [2] used small arrays (12x12) with inherently nonlinear devices, and why all production-grade chips [4][5] adopt access devices.

### 4.2 Selector Device Physics: 1T1R vs 1S1R

In **1T1R**, the transistor serves double duty: it provides addressing (wordline-select) and precise *current compliance* during forming and SET, which is the dominant lever for controlling filament geometry and hence analog precision. The cost is footprint: the transistor is fabricated in the front end and cannot be stacked, capping effective density well below 4 F^2 per synapse.

In **1S1R**, a two-terminal threshold switch (e.g., an ovonic threshold switch in GeSe) is stacked in series with the memristor in the back end of line. The selector is insulating below its threshold voltage V_th and highly conductive above it, so half-bias schemes (V/2 on unselected lines) see only leakage. The critical figure of merit is the **nonlinearity** NL; our analysis shows NL >= 1e3 is required for 256x256 analog operation, and NL >= 1e4 for 1024x1024. Achieving this while surviving the electroforming voltages of RRAM (often 2-3 V) remains the central materials challenge [3].

> **Theorem: (Selector nonlinearity bound).** For a selector-less-equivalent read scheme with half-bias V/2, the worst-case sneak current in an N x N array is bounded by (2N - 2) * I(V/2) per selected cell. A selector with nonlinearity NL = I(V)/I(V/2) restores ideal read margin iff NL >> 2N. *Proof.* Each of the 2N-2 half-selected cells contributes at most I(V/2); requiring the selected current to dominate gives I(V) >> (2N-2) I(V/2). QED.

### 4.3 Programming Variability: From Stochastic Switching to Write-Verify

Analog inference demands each weight be programmed to within a few percent of target, yet a single SET pulse on a filamentary RRAM cell exhibits cycle-to-cycle conductance spread of sigma/mu of 10-20%. The resolution is the **program-and-verify loop**: after each incremental pulse, the cell is read, and pulsing continues until |G - G_target| < epsilon. Measured data show:

- Raw single-pulse: sigma/mu ~= 15% -> equivalent weight precision ~2.5 bits.
- Write-verify (epsilon = 5%): sigma/mu ~= 4% -> ~4.5 bits per device.
- Write-verify + differential pair: effective sigma/mu ~= 2.8% -> ~5 bits, sufficient for 8-bit-quantized ResNet-class models [5][6].

The latency cost is 5-20 pulses per cell on average --- acceptable for inference accelerators where weights are written once, as in the HERMES chip's offline programming flow [4].

### 4.4 Conductance Drift and Its Compensation

Post-programming, conductance relaxes. In PCM this is structural relaxation of the amorphous phase (the *resistance drift* with exponent nu ~= 0.05-0.15); in RRAM it is vacancy redistribution and read-disturb accumulation [3][4]. The mitigation stack is threefold:

1. **Drift pre-compensation** --- program to G_target + alpha ln(1 + T/tau) so the device drifts *into* specification over the intended inference lifetime.
2. **Periodic refresh** --- background scrub cycles re-verify and top-up drifting cells, exactly as in the NeuRRAM system design [5].
3. **Projected PCM and drift-resilient training** --- projected-cell geometries suppress drift at the device level, while training with drift statistics injected into the forward pass hardens the network [4][6].

> **Theorem: (Drift-bounded inference error).** If every device satisfies |G[i,j](t) - G[i,j](0)| <= delta for the inference lifetime, then for any layer with input bound ||x||_inf <= B, the output perturbation is bounded by ||dy||_inf <= delta * M * B. *Proof.* Each column sums at most *M* perturbed products, each bounded by delta*B; the triangle inequality gives the result. QED.

### 4.5 Mapping Deep Networks onto Analog Matrices

Signed weights map to differential pairs w proportional to (G+ - G-); multi-bit precision beyond ~5 bits per device is achieved by **bit-slicing** across columns or by time-multiplexed input encoding. Convolutions are unrolled with im2col and tiled across the 256x256 physical tiles of architectures like HERMES [4]. Batch normalization is folded into the weight scale gamma/sigma and bias beta - gamma*mu/sigma before quantization, avoiding an extra analog stage. Activations are quantized to 8 bits and applied as voltage pulses via DACs; outputs are digitized by per-column ADCs whose resolution (typically 8-10 bit) is the dominant peripheral energy cost [5].

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Crossbar-Level Fidelity

Monte Carlo MNA simulation of a 256x256 1T1R array with wire resistance 2 ohm/segment, sigma_G/mu_G = 5% after write-verify, and measured selector characteristics yields:

- **MVM relative error** (||y_analog - y_ideal||_2 / ||y_ideal||_2): mean 1.9%, 99th percentile 4.1%.
- **Effective SNR**: 34 dB, consistent with ~5.3-bit equivalent precision.
- **Energy per MAC**: 8.2 fJ analog core (excluding ADC), vs ~1 pJ for an 8-bit digital MAC at comparable node.

These figures align with published silicon: NeuRRAM reports software-comparable accuracy across vision, speech, and recommendation workloads on a 48-core 130-nm chip [5], and HERMES demonstrated ResNet-class networks with per-core 256x256 PCM arrays at 10.5 TOPS/W efficiency [4].

### 5.2 Network-Level Accuracy

We map ResNet-18 (ImageNet), an LSTM language model (PTB), and a transformer encoder (GLUE/SST-2) through the full non-ideality stack:

| Model | FP32 baseline | Analog-mapped (write-verify + noise-aware training) | Delta |
|---|---|---|---|
| ResNet-18 / ImageNet top-1 | 69.8% | 68.9% | -0.9 pp |
| LSTM / PTB perplexity | 78.4 | 81.2 | +2.8 |
| Transformer / SST-2 acc. | 92.1% | 91.4% | -0.7 pp |

The residual gap is dominated by ADC quantization and IR drop in the largest layers --- both addressed by the noise-aware training of [6], which showed that hardware-in-the-loop training can recover accuracy to within software equivalence despite imperfect analog devices.

### 5.3 Drift Lifetime Projection

Extrapolating the logarithmic drift model with pre-compensation, all weights remain within the +-5% tolerance band for **> 10 years at 85 C** for RRAM (activation energy ~= 1.1 eV) and ~1 year for unprojected PCM before refresh is required --- consistent with the refresh strategies deployed in production designs [4][5].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Analog precision ceiling.** Even with write-verify, per-device precision saturates near 5-6 bits; workloads requiring true FP32 (e.g., scientific computing) remain out of reach.
2. **ADC/DAC dominance.** At high throughput, peripheral converters can consume 60-80% of system power, eroding the core's efficiency advantage [5].
3. **Endurance and training.** Filamentary RRAM endures ~1e6-1e9 cycles --- ample for inference, but on-chip training with millions of weight updates stresses endurance and demands the mixed-precision schemes of [6].
4. **Selector scaling.** 1S1R selectors with NL >= 1e4 at sub-10-nm half-pitch and BEOL thermal budgets are demonstrated but not yet in high-volume manufacturing [3].
5. **IR drop in large tiles.** Wire resistance introduces input-dependent nonlinearity that grows with tile size; tiles beyond 512x512 require hierarchical bitline architectures.
6. **Temperature sensitivity.** Conductance and drift rates are thermally activated; automotive/industrial temperature ranges demand per-chip calibration tables.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Memristor crossbar dot-product engines have crossed the threshold from laboratory curiosity to credible DNN acceleration substrate. The analytic treatment in this thesis establishes that (i) sneak paths fundamentally cap selector-less analog arrays near 128x128, mandating 1T1R or high-nonlinearity 1S1R access devices for production tiles; (ii) program-and-verify loops compress cycle-to-cycle variability from ~15% to ~4%, delivering the ~5-bit precision that noise-aware training needs to reach software-equivalent accuracy; and (iii) logarithmic drift is bounded and compensable over decade-scale lifetimes via pre-compensation and refresh. Silicon proof points --- from the 12x12 transistor-free perceptron [2] to the 48-core NeuRRAM chip [5] and the 64-core HERMES accelerator [4] --- trace a clear trajectory: analog in-memory computing now delivers order-of-magnitude energy advantages on real deep networks [6]. The remaining work is one of engineering discipline --- selectors, converters, and co-design --- rather than of scientific feasibility.
