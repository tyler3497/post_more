# Superoptimization by Equality Saturation: e-Graph Rewriting, Verified Lifting, and Stochastic Search in the STOKE Tradition

## Abstract

Superoptimization — the search for the *optimal* instruction sequence for a given computation — was once dismissed as a curiosity for tiny, loop-free kernels. Two lineages have made it practical. The first, inaugurated by STOKE, treats superoptimization as a stochastic search problem: Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling over x86-64 programs, with correctness and performance encoded as terms in a cost function. The second, rooted in equality saturation over e-graphs and industrialized by the *egg* library, represents exponentially many equivalent programs compactly and explores rewrite sequences exhaustively until saturation. *Verified lifting* generalizes the agenda further: it synthesizes equivalent programs in a high-level target language and proves equivalence with an SMT solver. This thesis unifies these traditions. We present the e-graph formalism, egg's innovations of rebuilding and e-class analyses, STOKE's cost-function and verification architecture, and verified-lifting pipelines exemplified by TASO and modern LLM-assisted lifters — arguing that equality saturation is *superoptimization with a memory*, and that the convergence of stochastic, symbolic, and learned search is the defining compiler research program of the decade.

## 1 Introduction

A traditional optimizing compiler applies a fixed pipeline of passes — constant folding, dead-code elimination, register allocation — each of which makes locally greedy decisions. The result is good code, but it is not *optimal* code, and no finite pipeline of such passes can be: instruction selection, register allocation, and scheduling interact in ways that greedy phase ordering systematically misses. Superoptimization rejects this architecture entirely. Given a reference program *P*, a superoptimizer searches for the program *P′* that is semantically equivalent to *P* and minimal under some cost model — the fastest, the shortest, or the lowest-energy realization of the same computation [1].

The field began with Massalin's 1987 superoptimizer, which enumerated instruction sequences exhaustively [1]. Exhaustive search is complete but scales only to a handful of instructions. Two ideas broke that ceiling. First, Schkufza, Sharma, and Aiken's **STOKE** (ASPLOS 2013) reformulated the loop-free binary superoptimization task as a *stochastic search problem*: a Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampler performs a random walk over the space of all x86-64 programs, guided by a cost function that jointly encodes correctness and performance [1]. Beginning from `llvm -O0` binaries, STOKE produced code that matched or outperformed `gcc -O3` and `icc -O3`, and in some cases rivaled expert hand-written assembly [1][2].

Second, equality saturation took the opposite approach: instead of sampling, *represent everything*. An **e-graph** compactly represents a congruence relation over many expressions simultaneously; applying rewrite rules to the e-graph adds equivalent forms without deleting the originals, so the structure explores the space of all rewrite sequences non-destructively until a fixpoint — *saturation* — is reached. Modern equality saturation, embodied by the *egg* library of Willsey et al. (POPL 2021), specializes e-graphs to this workload with amortized invariant restoration (*rebuilding*) and domain-specific reasoning (*e-class analyses*) [3].

These two traditions — stochastic sampling and exhaustive rewriting — are often presented as alternatives. This thesis argues they are better understood as points on a spectrum of *search strategies for semantic equivalence classes*, and that **verified lifting** — synthesizing a target-language program equivalent to a source program, then verifying equivalence with a theorem prover — is the natural generalization of both [5]. The remainder of this work develops this unified view, from the congruence-closure foundations of e-graphs through STOKE's cost function to the substitution-generation machinery of TASO [4].

## 2 Background

**Rewriting and congruence.** Term rewriting has been the compiler writer's workhorse since the earliest peephole optimizers: rules of the form *l → r* replace instances of a pattern with an equivalent term. The classical difficulty is *ordering* — applying rules in the wrong order, or the right rules in the wrong places, misses optimizations or diverges. Equality saturation sidesteps ordering by *never committing*: every rewrite is recorded as an equivalence in a persistent data structure, and all rules apply to all matching subterms simultaneously.

The data structure that makes this possible is the **e-graph**. E-graphs were developed in the late 1970s for use in automated theorem provers implementing congruence closure [3]. An e-graph represents a set of terms and an equivalence relation over them. Terms are stored in *e-nodes*; equivalent e-nodes are grouped into *e-classes*; e-classes are linked by a union-find structure with a *congruence invariant*: if two e-nodes have the same operator and their children are in pairwise-equal e-classes, the e-nodes are in the same e-class. Because equivalent terms share structure, an e-graph can represent an *exponential* number of programs in polynomial space — for example, *n* applications of a binary rewrite to a chain produce 2ⁿ distinct terms in a structure of size *O(n)*.

**From theorem proving to optimization.** Tatlock and Wang's insight was to repurpose this structure for program optimization: *equality saturation* grows an e-graph with semantics-preserving rewrite rules, then *extracts* the cheapest term from each e-class according to a cost model. Unlike a greedy pass pipeline, extraction sees the entire equivalence class at once and picks the globally cheapest representative — no phase-ordering problem arises because no destructive choice was ever made [3].

**Stochastic superoptimization.** STOKE takes the complementary route [1]. Where equality saturation is exhaustive within its rewrite set, STOKE is *complete in ambition but stochastic in method*: its MCMC sampler proposes random program transformations — instruction swaps, opcode mutations, operand changes, deletions, insertions — and accepts them according to a Metropolis criterion on the cost function. Correctness is enforced in two stages: fast filtering on a generated test suite, then formal equivalence checking with an SMT solver. This two-stage design is the ancestor of the search–verify–generate loop that verified lifting inherits [5].

**Lifting.** Verified lifting starts from a low-level program and *synthesizes* a high-level program in a target DSL, then proves the two equivalent [5]. TASO (SOSP 2019) is the emblematic instantiation for deep learning: given operator specifications, it automatically *generates* graph substitutions — rewrites of tensor computation graphs — verifies them formally, and jointly optimizes substitution and data layout [4]. Recent work extends verified lifting with LLM-guided synthesis, replacing hand-tuned search with neural proposals while keeping the verifier as the ground-truth arbiter [5].

## 3 Methodology

We now describe the shared methodological skeleton underlying all three traditions: **search**, **equivalence management**, and **extraction/verification**.

**Equality saturation loop.** The core algorithm maintains an e-graph *E* over a term language and a set of rewrite rules *R*:

```
saturate(E, R):
    repeat until E unchanged or resource limit reached:
        for each rule (l -> r) in R:
            for each match σ of l in E:
                union(eclass(l[σ]), eclass(r[σ]))
        rebuild(E)          // restore congruence invariant
    return extract(E)
```

Matching is *e-matching*: patterns match modulo the equivalence relation, so a rule fires once per e-class rather than once per syntactic occurrence — this is what makes saturation scale. `rebuild` restores the congruence invariant after a batch of unions; egg's key performance contribution is making rebuilding *amortized*, so the cost of invariant restoration is spread across iterations rather than paid per union [3].

**Extraction.** Given a cost function *c* over e-nodes (e.g., AST size, estimated latency, operator cost), extraction computes, for each e-class, the minimum-cost term:

*c(e-class) = min over e-nodes n in class of [ c_op(n) + Σ c(child classes) ]*

Because the e-graph is a DAG with sharing, this is a well-defined dynamic program provided costs are monotone; egg computes it with a fixed-point iteration, and extraction can be re-run under different cost models without re-saturating [3].

**STOKE's cost function.** STOKE's sampler optimizes:

*cost(p; T) = ω_eq · eq(p, T) + ω_perf · perf(p)*

where *eq* measures behavioral distance from the target *T* (via test-case Hamming distance and, ultimately, SMT equivalence) and *perf* estimates runtime (instruction latency sums, with a learned model in later work). A proposed rewrite *p → p′* is accepted with probability proportional to *exp(−β(cost(p′) − cost(p)))*, the Metropolis–Hastings criterion; the temperature schedule is annealed so the walk first explores broadly, then converges [1][2].

**Verified lifting pipeline.** Lifting decomposes into three phases [5]:

1. **Search** — find a program summary *Pₛ* in the target IR equivalent to source *S* (via enumeration, stochastic search, e-graph rewriting, or neural synthesis);
2. **Verification** — prove *S ≡ Pₛ* for all inputs with an SMT solver or theorem prover;
3. **Code generation** — translate *Pₛ* into concrete target syntax by pattern matching.

TASO instantiates this for tensor graphs: it *enumerates* small computation graphs, groups them by *fingerprint* (a hash of outputs on fixed inputs, computed over integers to avoid floating-point error), tests same-fingerprint pairs for equivalence, and formally verifies the survivors before admitting them as substitutions [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 e-Graphs, Congruence Closure, and Non-Destructive Rewriting

The e-graph's power derives from a single invariant — **congruence closure** — maintained by a union-find structure over e-classes. Consider the term *(a × b) + (a × b)*. A naive AST stores the multiplication twice; an e-graph stores one e-node `×(a, b)` whose e-class is referenced twice by the `+` e-node. Now apply distributivity-like rewrites or strength reductions: each fires *within* e-classes, adding e-nodes without removing old ones — e-classes acting as containers for equivalent e-nodes, with child pointers into shared subterms and union-find merges recording discovered equalities.

> **Theorem:** *An e-graph with the congruence invariant maintained represents exactly the congruence closure of its initial terms under the applied equalities. Two terms are in the same e-class if and only if they are provably equal from the initial terms and asserted rewrites.*

This is why equality saturation is *superoptimization with a memory*: a traditional rewrite engine forgets alternatives the moment it fires a rule; the e-graph remembers all of them. The price is that the structure can explode — saturation may not terminate, and e-matching can produce exponentially many matches. In practice, engines bound iterations or e-graph size, and extraction still yields the best term found so far. Recent work on *optimistic* analyses and cycle deduplication addresses the cyclic-program case that inductive e-class analyses handle imprecisely [7].

### 4.2 egg: Rebuilding and E-Class Analyses

The *egg* library (Willsey et al., POPL 2021) is the modern reference implementation, and its two technical contributions are worth understanding precisely [3].

**Rebuilding.** Classical e-graph implementations restore the congruence invariant *eagerly* after every union — each merge triggers a cascade of parent re-canonicalization. Equality saturation workloads, however, perform *many* unions per iteration (one per rewrite match), making eager restoration asymptotically wasteful. Egg *defers* invariant restoration: unions accumulate in a worklist, and `rebuild` processes the worklist once per saturation iteration, deduplicating and batching the congruence repair. The authors prove this is asymptotically faster in practice on saturation workloads — the same total work, but with the constant factors of hash-consing amortized across batches [3].

**E-class analyses.** Purely syntactic rewrites cannot express transformations that depend on *semantic* facts: constant folding needs to know a subterm is constant; bounds analysis needs ranges. Prior systems bolted such reasoning on ad hoc. Egg introduces **e-class analyses** as a first-class mechanism: user-defined lattice data attached to e-classes, computed bottom-up over the e-graph and maintained across merges. An analysis is a tuple *(make, join, modify)* where `make` computes data for an e-node from its children's data, `join` merges data when e-classes union, and `modify` optionally adds e-nodes implied by the analysis (e.g., constant folding inserting the computed constant). Because analyses participate in the same fixpoint as rewriting, semantic and syntactic reasoning interleave soundly [3].

The case studies in the egg paper — a floating-point accuracy improver, a linear-algebra simplifier, and a CAD geometry kernel — demonstrate that one library now serves domains that previously required bespoke e-graph implementations [3].

### 4.3 Stochastic Search in the STOKE Tradition

STOKE's enduring contribution is not any particular optimized binary but its **cost-function formulation** of superoptimization [1]. By encoding correctness as a *soft* term during search and a *hard* gate at the end, STOKE decouples exploration from verification:

1. **Proposal.** Random transformations of the current candidate: replace an opcode, swap operands, insert/delete instructions, exchange registers.
2. **Fast filter.** Evaluate the candidate on a generated test suite; Hamming distance on outputs gives the *eq* term. This is orders of magnitude cheaper than SMT and prunes the vast majority of incorrect proposals.
3. **Metropolis acceptance.** Accept or reject based on the cost delta, with annealing.
4. **Formal verification.** Periodically, the best candidates are checked for full equivalence with Z3/CVC4-backed equivalence checking [1].

The results were startling: from `llvm -O0` binaries, STOKE routinely matched or beat `gcc -O3` and `icc -O3`, discovering non-obvious sequences such as a 17-instruction population count matching the hardware `popcnt`, multiply-based bit tricks, and LEA chains no human would write [2]. The lineage continued through conditionally-correct superoptimization (OOPSLA 2015), floating-point stochastic optimization with tunable precision (PLDI 2014), and the complete x86-64 formal semantics of PLDI 2019 [2].

The STOKE tradition's lesson for equality saturation is *cost-function thinking*: the two-stage filter (cheap tests, expensive proof) is directly mirrored in TASO's fingerprint-then-verify pipeline [4], and modern equality-saturation compilers like *Denali* and *Isaria* incorporate stochastic and oracle-guided rule discovery to escape the limits of hand-written rewrite sets. Stochasticity and saturation are not rivals — saturation *is* search, with perfect recall.

### 4.4 Verified Lifting and Substitution Generation: the TASO Lineage

Verified lifting generalizes superoptimization along the language axis: instead of finding the best *x* in the same language, find the best *y* in a *better* language and prove *x ≡ y* [5]. TASO is the canonical realization for deep-learning graphs [4]:

- **Generation.** TASO enumerates small tensor graphs (up to a threshold size) built from operator specifications, discarding graphs with duplicated computation. Each graph's *fingerprint* — a two-stage hash of its outputs on integer inputs — is computed; graphs sharing a fingerprint are candidate-equivalent.
- **Verification.** Candidate pairs are tested on randomized inputs, then formally verified; verified pairs become *graph substitutions* — exactly the rewrite rules an equality-saturation engine would consume.
- **Joint optimization.** TASO then optimizes substitution application *jointly with data layout*, a coupling that sequential frameworks miss, achieving significant speedups on real models [4].

The verified-lifting formulation of LLMLift shows the next step: the *search* phase need not be symbolic at all. An LLM proposes candidate target programs; the theorem prover still decides truth [5]. This preserves the methodology's soundness invariant — **the verifier is the only component that must be trusted** — while allowing arbitrarily clever, even unsound, proposal mechanisms. STOKE's MCMC sampler, egg's e-matching, and an LLM's next-token distribution are all just *proposal distributions* feeding the same verify-then-extract pipeline. That unification is the central claim of this thesis.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

The three traditions are evaluated differently, but their results compose into a single story about the maturity of superoptimization.

| System | Search strategy | Verification | Reported result |
|---|---|---|---|
| STOKE (ASPLOS 2013) | MCMC over x86-64 programs | Test cases + SMT (Z3/CVC4) | Matches/beats `gcc -O3`, `icc -O3`; some expert assembly [1][2] |
| egg (POPL 2021) | Equality saturation + e-class analyses | Rewrite rules proved sound by construction | Asymptotic speedups on saturation workloads; 3 domain case studies [3] |
| TASO (SOSP 2019) | Enumerative substitution generation | Fingerprint + random tests + formal verification | Auto-generated substitutions; joint layout optimization beats frameworks [4] |
| LLMLift (2024) | LLM-guided synthesis | Theorem-prover equivalence | Outperforms symbolic lifters on 4 DSLs in benchmarks and time [5] |
| Auto-Code-Opt w/ e-graphs (2021) | Equality saturation on IR | Sound rewrite extraction | Automated optimization competitive with hand passes [8] |

Two correctness properties anchor the theory. First, extraction soundness for equality saturation:

> **Theorem:** *Let E be an e-graph whose e-classes partition terms into provably-equal sets, and let extract(E, c) return a minimum-cost representative per class under a monotone cost function c. Then for every e-class, the extracted term is semantically equal to every term in the class, and no equal term has lower cost.*

Second, the verified-lifting soundness invariant:

> **Theorem:** *In a lifting pipeline (search, verify, generate), if the verifier is sound — i.e., it accepts (S, Pₛ) only when S ≡ Pₛ for all inputs — then the generated target program is semantically equivalent to the source regardless of the soundness of the search procedure.*

The second theorem is the deep justification for the modern synthesis: it licenses STOKE-style stochasticity, LLM-style neural proposals, and enumerative TASO-style generation alike, because *unsound search cannot produce unsound results* — it can only waste time. The verifier is the narrow waist of the architecture.

Empirically, the trend is unambiguous: STOKE demonstrated that stochastic search over raw machine code beats industrial compilers [1]; egg demonstrated that equality saturation can be made fast and extensible enough to serve as infrastructure [3]; TASO demonstrated that substitution *discovery* itself can be automated and verified [4]. Each removed one more piece of human expertise from the optimization loop.

## 6 Limitations

The unified picture has sharp edges, and intellectual honesty requires stating them.

**Scalability of saturation.** Equality saturation's memory is also its curse: the e-graph can grow exponentially, e-matching can produce a combinatorial explosion of matches, and saturation frequently fails to terminate within practical budgets. Extraction then returns the best of an incomplete search — with no characterization of *how* incomplete. Unlike STOKE, which is anytime by construction, saturation gives little signal about marginal returns on further iterations.

**STOKE's scope.** STOKE operates on loop-free x86-64 binaries; control flow, memory aliasing, and floating-point subtleties (partially addressed in follow-up work [2]) remain hard. Its cost model's performance term is an estimate, and SMT verification of long instruction sequences is expensive — the two-stage filter helps but does not eliminate the bottleneck. The project is no longer actively maintained.

**Rewrite-set dependence.** Equality saturation is only as good as its rewrites. Hand-written rule sets are incomplete and sometimes unsound; automatically *discovered* rules (in the TASO spirit) must be verified, and verification of rules over rich theories is itself undecidable in general. Oracle-guided and stochastic rule discovery remain research frontiers [7].

**Lifting fragility.** Verified lifting requires a precise formal semantics for both source and target; where semantics are murky (undefined behavior in C, floating-point reassociation), the verifier's guarantees weaken to the fidelity of the model. LLM-guided lifters inherit the model's unreliability in proposal — mitigated, but not eliminated, by the verifier.

**Cost-model fidelity.** All extraction is only as good as its cost function. A minimum-cost term under a flawed model is a flawed optimum — the classic gap between static cost estimates and silicon behavior persists across every tradition surveyed here.

## 7 Conclusion

Superoptimization has traveled from Massalin's exhaustive enumerator to a mature engineering discipline with three interoperable pillars. **Equality saturation** provides the memory: e-graphs that hold every equivalent program the rewrites can reach, with *egg*'s rebuilding and e-class analyses making the structure fast and extensible [3]. **Stochastic search** in the STOKE tradition provides the courage: MCMC exploration of program spaces no rewrite set covers, disciplined by a cost function that trades correctness against performance [1][2]. **Verified lifting** provides the ambition: synthesizing programs in better languages and proving them equivalent, with the verifier as the single trusted component that lets search be as wild as we dare [4][5].

The convergence is already underway. Substitution discovery (TASO) feeds rewrite sets to saturation engines; stochastic and neural methods propose candidates that verifiers certify; e-graphs serve as the shared representation in which all these searches deposit their findings. The defining compiler research program of the coming decade is not any one of these techniques — it is their composition: *proposal distributions of increasing cleverness, equivalence representations of increasing completeness, and verifiers of increasing power, arranged in a loop that never stops searching*. The optimal program is out there; we finally have the machinery to remember every candidate we have ever seen.
