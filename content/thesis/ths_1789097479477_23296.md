# Dataflow Architectures and Systolic Arrays: From the Connection Machine to TPU Matrix Units

## Abstract

The dataflow principle—scheduling computation by the availability of operands rather than by the advance of a program counter—has resurfaced in nearly every era of computer architecture, each time wearing the costume of the dominant technology. This thesis traces a continuous intellectual lineage from Jack Dennis's static dataflow machines of the 1970s, through Arvind and Nikhil's MIT tagged-token architecture, through W. Daniel Hillis's Connection Machine with its 65,536 one-bit processors arranged on a hypercube, to the weight-stationary systolic arrays at the heart of Google's Tensor Processing Units. We formalize the systolic execution model as a space-time transformation of nested-loop computations, prove its asymptotic and bandwidth advantages for dense matrix algebra, analyze why general-purpose dataflow machines failed commercially while their spatial descendants succeeded, and quantify the measured gains of the modern incarnations: 15–30× performance and 30–80× performance-per-watt for the TPU over contemporary CPUs and GPUs [1], and 1.4–2.5× energy improvements from row-stationary dataflows in Eyeriss [2]. The central argument is that dataflow never died; it was merely waiting for a workload—deep learning—whose regularity matched its discipline.

## 1 Introduction

Every computer architect eventually collides with the same wall. In the von Neumann model, the processor fetches instructions in sequence, fetches their operands from memory, executes, and writes results back, and the bandwidth of the path between memory and processor—Backus's famous bottleneck [3]—caps everything. Two distinct rebellions against this order were born in the late 1970s. The *dataflow* rebellion, pioneered by Jack Dennis at MIT and pursued at Manchester and MIT through the 1980s, abolished the program counter entirely: an operation fires the moment its operands arrive, like a neuron firing when its inputs are sufficient. The *systolic* rebellion, formalized by H. T. Kung and Charles Leiserson, kept the regularity of the array but abolished global communication: data pulses rhythmically through a grid of simple processing elements, each performing a small operation before passing results to its neighbor, in analogy to blood pumped by the heart [4].

Both movements were declared dead by the mid-1990s. General-purpose dataflow machines were defeated by the *token-matching problem*—the overhead of dynamically pairing operands at scale—and by the unstoppable economics of RISC microprocessors. The Connection Machine, the most ambitious massively parallel machine of its era, bankrupted its maker [5].

And yet the ideas returned, triumphant, in the 2010s. Google's Tensor Processing Unit (TPU), announced in 2016, is at its core a 256×256 systolic array of multiply-accumulate cells [1]. Every modern deep-learning accelerator—NVIDIA's tensor cores, Graphcore's IPUs, the MIT Eyeriss chip with its row-stationary dataflow [2]—is an exercise in spatial dataflow. This thesis asks three questions: *what exactly is the dataflow/systolic execution model, formally?*; *why did the first incarnation fail and the second succeed?*; and *what are the provable limits of the model?*

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The von Neumann bottleneck and the three escapes

Three broad strategies have been proposed for escaping sequential instruction execution. *Instruction-level parallelism* (pipelining, superscalar, out-of-order execution) keeps the von Neumann contract and extracts parallelism within a sequential instruction stream; it is bounded by the available ILP, empirically a factor of two to four in general code. *Control-driven parallelism* (vector processors, SIMD, GPUs) issues the same operation to many data items under explicit control. *Data-driven parallelism*—the subject of this thesis—makes data availability itself the scheduling event.

### 2.2 Static and dynamic dataflow

In a dataflow machine, a program is a directed graph: nodes are operations, edges carry *tokens* (data values). A node is *enabled* when a token has arrived on every input edge; it then fires, consumes its input tokens, computes, and emits result tokens on its output edges. There is no program counter, no instruction fetch in the conventional sense, and synchronization is implicit—an operation cannot fire before its data is ready, so races are impossible by construction.

The field split into two camps. **Static dataflow** (Dennis, 1974) allows only one token per edge at a time, which serializes loop iterations but is simple to build. **Dynamic dataflow** permits multiple tokens per edge, distinguished by *tags* identifying the loop iteration. The Manchester Dataflow Machine (Gurd, Watson) and the MIT Tagged-Token Dataflow Architecture (Arvind, Nikhil, 1990) [6] are the canonical dynamic machines: a token is a triple *(tag, value, destination)*, and the **matching store** pairs each arriving token with its partner operand (same tag, same destination) before releasing the pair to an execution unit. The elegance is undeniable; the cost, as we shall see in §6, was fatal in the 1980s.

### 2.3 Systolic arrays: Kung and Leiserson

In 1978, Kung and Leiserson published "Systolic Arrays (for VLSI)," proposing a radical simplification of parallel hardware [4]. A systolic system is a network of processing elements (PEs), each capable of a simple operation, in which information flows between cells in a pipelined, rhythmic fashion, with communication to the outside world occurring *only at the boundary cells*. The canonical example is matrix multiplication: in a hex-connected or square array, each cell repeatedly performs the inner-product step *c ← c + a × b*, with matrices *A* and *B* streaming through the array in lockstep.

Kung later distilled the argument in "Why Systolic Architectures?" (1982) [7]: systolic designs achieve high throughput with modest I/O bandwidth because **each input datum is used multiple times** as it passes through the array—*O(n³)* operations on *O(n²)* data items—the same *arithmetic intensity* argument that would justify the TPU thirty-five years later.

### 2.4 The Connection Machine

The Connection Machine, conceived by W. Daniel Hillis as his MIT doctoral dissertation (1985) and commercialized by Thinking Machines Corporation, took a different path to the same destination: instead of rhythmically pumping data through a fixed pipeline, it placed *the data itself* in a vast sea of processors [8]. The CM-1 and CM-2 connected up to 65,536 one-bit processors in a 12-dimensional boolean hypercube (plus a NEWS grid for nearest-neighbor communication), each with its own memory, all executing the same broadcast instruction—SIMD at an unprecedented scale.

The programming model, embodied in *Lisp (*Lisp) and later C*, treated the machine as a *data-parallel* computer: operations applied to entire *xectors* (parallel variables) at once. In retrospect, the Connection Machine was a spatial dataflow machine in which the "tokens" were single bits resident in processor memory and the "firing rule" was the global SIMD clock. Its failure was economic and programmatic: at several million dollars per installation, it competed against the exponentially improving commodity microprocessor, and its SIMD discipline punished irregular, branchy code—the dominant workloads of the 1990s.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a work of architectural analysis. Our method has three components:

1. **Formal modeling.** We model systolic execution as a *space-time transformation* of affine loop nests, which lets us derive exact latency, throughput, and I/O complexity for systolic matrix multiplication.
2. **Taxonomy.** We classify spatial dataflows by *stationarity*—which operand remains resident in the PE while the others stream past (WS/OS/RS)—following Chen, Emer, and Sze [2].
3. **Historical case analysis.** We reconstruct the design decisions and measured results of four landmark systems—the MIT tagged-token machine [6], the Connection Machine CM-2 [5], Google TPU v1 [1], and Eyeriss [2]—and extract the variables that separated success from failure.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The mathematics of systolic matrix multiplication

Consider the canonical computation *C = A × B* for *n × n* matrices, i.e. *c_{ij} = Σ_k a_{ik} b_{kj}*. The naive loop nest has three indices *(i, j, k)*. A systolic mapping is a *space-time transformation*: we choose a projection that maps two of the three indices to spatial PE coordinates and the remaining index (plus a skewing function) to time.

The classical Kung–Leiserson mapping assigns PE *(i, j)* to compute *c_{ij}*. Element *a_{ik}* enters the west edge of row *i* at time *t = i + k*, and *b_{kj}* enters the north edge of column *j* at time *t = j + k*; both propagate one PE per cycle, so they arrive at PE *(i, j)* simultaneously at time *t = i + j + k*. Each PE executes:

```
on every clock tick:
    c ← c + a · b      # MAC: multiply-accumulate
    pass a east, pass b south
```

The input streams must be **skewed**—row *i* of *A* is delayed by *i* leading zeros, column *j* of *B* by *j*—so that the wavefronts align. The invariant is: *a_{ik}* and *b_{kj}* meet at PE *(i,j)* at tick *i + j + k*, so after *3n − 2* ticks every accumulator holds *Σ_k a_{ik}b_{kj} = c_{ij}* exactly.

> **Theorem (Systolic GEMM complexity):** An *n × n* systolic array of MAC cells multiplies two *n × n* matrices in *3n − 2 = Θ(n)* clock cycles using *Θ(n²)* processing elements, performing *Θ(n³)* arithmetic operations on *Θ(n²)* input words. The computation-to-I/O ratio is therefore *Θ(n)*, versus *Θ(1)* for the naive von Neumann loop nest.

*Proof sketch.* There are *n²* PEs, each performing one MAC per tick for *n* "real" ticks: *n³* multiplications and *n³* additions total. The boundary injects *2n²* words (the two matrices) and extracts *n²* words (the result), i.e. *Θ(n²)* I/O. Time is dominated by the pipeline depth: the last result emerges after *(n−1) + (n−1) + n = 3n − 2* ticks. ∎

This is the fundamental result of the field: **systolic execution converts quadratic I/O into cubic computation**. Any architecture whose bottleneck is memory bandwidth—and in the deep-learning era, that is every architecture—must achieve exactly this conversion or lose.

### 4.2 The tagged-token machine: dataflow in its purest form

Where systolic arrays fix the *schedule* in hardware, the MIT tagged-token architecture [6] made scheduling fully dynamic. Programs compiled to dataflow graphs; each graph node became an *instruction template* stored in the machine's *activity store*; execution state flowed as tokens around a pipelined ring of processing elements. The two hard problems were **matching** (pairing the two operand tokens of a dyadic operator, distinguished by tag) and **token storage** (buffering unmatched tokens without deadlock).

In TLA+ one can specify the matching-store invariant that the entire machine's correctness rested on:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE TokenMatch ------------------------------
EXTENDS Integers, FiniteSets
VARIABLES waiting, enabled
TypeOK == waiting \subseteq [tag : Nat, dest : Nat, val : Nat]
Fire(t) == \E w \in waiting :
             /\ w.tag = t.tag /\ w.dest = t.dest /\ w /= t
             /\ waiting' = waiting \ {w}
             /\ enabled' = enabled \cup {[dest |-> t.dest,
                                          op1 |-> w.val, op2 |-> t.val]}
Match(t) == IF \E w \in waiting : w.tag = t.tag /\ w.dest = t.dest
            THEN Fire(t)
            ELSE waiting' = waiting \cup {t}
=============================================================================
```

Determinacy is dataflow's great semantic gift: parallelism without races, synchronization without locks. But the matching store is also its great *hardware* curse: every dyadic operation requires an associative lookup keyed by *(tag, destination)* against a potentially enormous waiting set. Arvind's group built working prototypes (the Monsoon machine at Motorola [9]), yet no general-purpose dataflow machine ever shipped commercially.

### 4.3 The Connection Machine: SIMD dataflow at hypercube scale

Hillis's insight was that one could dodge the matching problem by *inverting* it: instead of routing data tokens to instructions, keep the data stationary in tiny processors and broadcast the instructions [8]. The front-end sequencer broadcast nano-instructions; each PE executed or ignored them based on a local *context flag*—predicated execution, decades before GPUs made it standard.

The CM-2's peak of roughly 10 GFLOPS (32-bit) in the early 1990s was extraordinary, and its programming model—*data-parallel* operations over distributed arrays—directly anticipates both CUDA thread blocks and the *xla*/*pjit* sharding of modern ML compilers. Its failure was not architectural but economic and programmatic: at several million dollars per installation, it competed against the exponentially improving commodity microprocessor, and its SIMD discipline punished irregular, branchy, pointer-chasing code—the dominant workloads of the 1990s. The lesson, which Google would internalize, is that **spatial architectures must be matched to workloads whose control flow is trivial and whose dataflow is regular**.

### 4.4 The TPU Matrix Multiply Unit: systolic arrays at datacenter scale

Google's TPU v1, described by Jouppi et al. at ISCA 2017 [1], is the vindication of Kung and Leiserson. Its heart is the **Matrix Multiply Unit (MXU)**: a *256 × 256 systolic array* of 8-bit integer MAC cells—65,536 multipliers—fed by a 24 MiB unified buffer and draining into 4 MiB of 32-bit accumulators. The dataflow is **weight-stationary**: the weight matrix is pre-loaded into the array (one weight per PE, held in a local register), activations stream in from the left, and partial sums accumulate downward and drain from the bottom. At 700 MHz, the MXU delivers *92 × 10¹²* multiply-accumulate operations per second (counted as 2 ops per MAC).

The measured results are the empirical core of this thesis [1]: on production MLP and CNN inference, the TPU delivered 15–30× the throughput of a contemporary Haswell CPU or K80 GPU, and 30–80× the performance per watt, with the 256×256 MXU occupying roughly 100 mm² of a 331 mm² 28 nm die running INT8 multiply with INT32 accumulation.

Two design decisions deserve emphasis. First, **deterministic single-threaded execution**: the TPU has no caches, no branch prediction, no out-of-order logic, no speculation—features that consume the majority of a CPU's area and power but contribute nothing to dense matrix math. The systolic schedule is fixed at design time; there is nothing to predict. Second, **software-managed memory**: the 24 MiB unified buffer is explicitly tiled by the compiler (XLA), eliminating cache misses by construction.

### 4.5 A taxonomy of stationary dataflows

"Stationary" names the operand that stays put. In **weight-stationary (WS)** (TPU), weights are pinned in PEs while activations stream; this minimizes weight movement, ideal when weights are reused across many inputs (inference with large batches). In **output-stationary (OS)** (e.g., ShiDianNao), each PE owns one output pixel and accumulates it locally; partial sums never leave the PE, minimizing psum traffic, at the cost of streaming both inputs. In **row-stationary (RS)** (Eyeriss [2]), each PE holds one row of filter weights *and* one row of activations in local scratchpads and performs 1-D convolution, maximizing reuse of *all three* data types for convolutional layers with small filters.

| Dataflow | Resident operand | Minimizes movement of | Exemplar |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weight-stationary | Filter weights | Weights | Google TPU MXU [1] |
| Output-stationary | Partial sums | Partial sums | ShiDianNao |
| Input-stationary | Activations | Activations | (various) |
| Row-stationary | Filter row + ifmap row | All three, jointly | Eyeriss [2] |

Chen, Emer, and Sze's energy analysis [2] showed RS to be 1.4–2.5× more energy-efficient than prior dataflows on AlexNet convolutional layers and ≥1.3× on fully-connected layers (batch > 16), precisely because DRAM access energy dominates and RS minimizes *total* DRAM traffic rather than one component (see the comprehensive dataflow taxonomy in [10]). The deeper point: **there is no universally optimal dataflow; optimality is a function of the reuse structure of the workload**.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now consolidate the formal and empirical claims.

> **Theorem (I/O lower bound for GEMM):** Any architecture that multiplies *n × n* matrices must transfer *Ω(n²)* words between slow and fast storage (the matrices must enter and the result must leave). The systolic array achieves *Θ(n²)* I/O with *Θ(n)* time on *Θ(n²)* PEs, and is therefore asymptotically I/O-optimal for the *n²*-PE complexity class.

> **Theorem (Control-overhead elimination):** In a systolic MAC array, the control state per PE is *O(1)* bits—the operation is identical every cycle—versus *Ω(log M)* bits per cycle for a von Neumann core fetching from an *M*-word instruction memory.

These theorems explain the measured numbers. The TPU's 30–80× performance-per-watt advantage [1] decomposes into roughly three factors: (a) 8-bit quantized arithmetic vs. 32-bit float (~4–8× energy per op), (b) elimination of control overhead—no speculation, no caches, deterministic scheduling (~4×), and (c) the systolic data-reuse factor itself, which keeps 65,536 MACs fed from a modest 24 MiB buffer.

A compact Rust model captures the PE microarchitecture that all these machines share:

```rust
/// One processing element of a weight-stationary systolic array.
struct Pe {
    weight: i8,   // stationary operand, loaded once
    psum: i32,    // partial sum flowing south
}

impl Pe {
    #[inline(always)]
    fn tick(&mut self, act_in: i8) -> (i8, i32) {
        // MAC, then forward activation east and psum south
        self.psum += self.weight as i32 * act_in as i32;
        (act_in, self.psum)
    }
}
```

Finally, the historical negative result is itself empirical: the Manchester machine, the MIT tagged-token prototypes, and Monsoon all demonstrated *correct* execution of real programs with speedups on parallel workloads, yet none achieved commercial viability [6][9]. The failure was not in the semantics but in the *economics of irregularity*, to which we now turn.

## 6 Limitations

**The matching-store bottleneck.** Dynamic dataflow's central structure, the matching store, must perform an associative lookup per dyadic operation. For fine-grained graphs this means the control hardware rivals the datapath in area and power—the very overhead dataflow was meant to abolish. Static scheduling (systolic arrays, TPU) avoids matching entirely by fixing the schedule at compile time, but only for *regular* loop nests.

**Irregularity intolerance.** Systolic arrays assume affine, statically analyzable data movement. Sparse matrices, data-dependent control flow, and dynamic shapes collapse utilization: a 256×256 array computing a 32×32 tile wastes ~98% of its MACs. Structured sparsity support and reconfigurable interconnect are active research, not solved problems.

**The utilization wall.** All spatial arrays suffer *dimensional mismatch*: real tensor shapes rarely equal the array dimensions, and per-layer utilization for small-batch inference can fall below 20%.

**Programmability and the compiler burden.** Static dataflow pushes complexity into the compiler: tiling, double-buffering, skewing, and stationary assignment must all be decided ahead of time (XLA for TPUs, the Eyeriss mapper [2]). A missed tiling decision silently converts a 90-TOPS machine into a 9-TOPS machine.

**Historical contingency.** The Connection Machine and the dataflow machines of the 1980s faced an economic headwind no architecture could fix: the commodity microprocessor was improving ~60% per year. The TPU succeeded partly because Dennard scaling had ended—CPUs could no longer outrun specialization.

## 7 Conclusion

Dataflow architectures and systolic arrays are one idea seen from two ends of a telescope. From the near end—Kung and Leiserson's 1978 paper [4]—we see a concrete VLSI methodology: pump data rhythmically through simple cells, reuse each word many times, abolish control. From the far end—Dennis's graphs, Arvind's tokens [6], Hillis's hypercube [5]—we see a semantics of computation in which data availability, not a program counter, is the fundamental scheduling event. The TPU [1] and Eyeriss [2] are the synthesis: statically scheduled spatial dataflow, with the compiler doing the token routing that dynamic hardware once attempted, and systolic pumping doing the operand reuse that caches once attempted.

The governing inequality is now clear. Let *R* be the reuse factor (operations per word transferred), *C* the control cost per operation, and *U* the achievable utilization given workload regularity. A spatial dataflow fabric wins when *R/C × U* exceeds the von Neumann baseline by more than the cost of specialization. Deep neural networks—dense, regular, reuse-rich, control-trivial—are the first workloads in computing history to satisfy this inequality by orders of magnitude.
