# Quantum Error Mitigation: Zero-Noise Extrapolation, Probabilistic Error Cancellation, and the Sampling-Overhead Frontier

## Abstract

Quantum error mitigation (QEM) seeks accurate expectation values from noisy, pre-fault-tolerant quantum processors without the qubit overhead of full error correction [1]. This thesis surveys the four dominant QEM families — *zero-noise extrapolation* (ZNE), *probabilistic error cancellation* (PEC), *Clifford data regression* (CDR), and *virtual distillation* (VD) — against the unifying currency of **sampling overhead**: the multiplicative increase in circuit executions needed to reach a target precision. We derive the Richardson-extrapolation estimator and unitary folding machinery of ZNE [2], the quasi-probability formalism of PEC [1], CDR's use of classically simulable near-Clifford training data [3], and VD's exponential suppression of incoherent errors via derangement circuits [4]. We then examine the fundamental lower bounds on mitigation cost (Quek, Takagi et al.), which prove an *exponential* sampling overhead in circuit depth for unbiased mitigation, and review landmark superconducting-qubit VQE and QAOA demonstrations [6, 7]. The thesis concludes that the sampling-overhead frontier — not gate fidelity alone — is the decisive scaling law of the NISQ era.

## 1 Introduction

The central tension of near-term quantum computation is a simple one: the hardware exists, but it is noisy. A state-of-the-art superconducting processor with one hundred or more qubits can execute circuits of depth fifty before decoherence and gate errors corrupt the output beyond classical post-processing's easy reach. Quantum error *correction* (QEC) resolves this in principle — the threshold theorem guarantees arbitrarily long computations once physical error rates fall below a threshold — but it does so at the cost of enormous physical-qubit overheads, hundreds to thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit, far beyond current devices [5].

*Quantum error mitigation* (QEM) occupies the middle ground. Rather than encoding information redundantly, QEM methods extract **unbiased or low-bias estimates of expectation values** $\langle O \rangle = \mathrm{Tr}(O\rho_{\mathrm{ideal}})$ from noisy experiments, paying instead in **classical post-processing and repeated measurements**. This distinction matters: where QEC demands coherent redundancy (more qubits, more gates), QEM demands statistical redundancy (more circuit executions). The exchange rate between these currencies is the *sampling overhead* $C_{\mathrm{em}}$, defined as the ratio of the number of shots required to estimate $\langle O \rangle$ to precision $\epsilon$ with mitigation versus without:

$$
C_{\mathrm{em}} := \frac{M_{\mathrm{mitigated}}(\epsilon)}{M_{\mathrm{unmitigated}}(\epsilon)}.
$$

Everything that follows is, in a sense, an elaboration of a single uncomfortable fact: **$C_{\mathrm{em}}$ grows exponentially in the circuit's fault rate for every unbiased mitigation protocol**, a result established rigorously only recently [5]. Understanding why — and which methods buy the most bias reduction per unit of overhead — is the business of this thesis.

We organize the landscape into four families:

1. **Zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE)** — deliberately amplify the noise by known scale factors $\lambda_1 < \lambda_2 < \dots$, measure $\langle O \rangle_{\lambda_m}$, and extrapolate to $\lambda = 0$ [1, 2].
2. **Probabilistic error cancellation (PEC)** — invert the noise channel by sampling from a quasi-probability decomposition of the ideal operation [1].
3. **Clifford data regression (CDR)** — learn the noise's effect on an observable from near-Clifford circuits that are classically simulable, then correct the target circuit [3].
4. **Virtual distillation (VD)** — estimate expectation values with respect to the dominant eigenvector of the noisy state by measuring derangement operators across multiple copies [4].

Each has a distinct overhead profile, distinct assumptions, and distinct failure modes. We treat them in turn, then confront the sampling-overhead lower bounds that constrain them all, and finally survey the experimental record on superconducting hardware — VQE for quantum chemistry and QAOA for combinatorial optimization — where mitigation first demonstrated genuine computational reach [6].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The NISQ expectation-value problem

Most near-term quantum algorithms are *variational* or *sampling-based* hybrid algorithms. The variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) prepares a parameterized state $|\psi(\theta)\rangle = U(\theta)|0\rangle$ and minimizes the energy $E(\theta) = \langle \psi(\theta)| H |\psi(\theta)\rangle$ over $\theta$, where $H$ is a chemistry Hamiltonian decomposed into Pauli terms. The quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA) minimizes a cost Hamiltonian over graph instances. In both cases, the quantum device's job is narrowly defined: **estimate expectation values of observables**. Everything else — the optimization loop, the parameter updates, the final answer — happens classically.

> **Lemma:** *A QEM protocol need only correct the estimator of an expectation value; it need never correct the quantum state itself.* This is what separates mitigation from correction and is the source of both its power and its cost: errors need not be identified, only averaged away statistically.

The noise model matters. A useful parameterization is the *circuit fault rate* $\lambda$, roughly the expected number of faults (gate errors, decoherence events) per circuit execution. For $\lambda \ll 1$ (the *weak-noise regime*), expectation values admit a perturbative expansion in $\lambda$, and mitigation schemes that cancel the first $M-1$ powers of $\lambda$ achieve bias of order $\mathcal{O}(\lambda^M)$ [5]. For $\lambda \gtrsim 1$, perturbative arguments collapse, signals decay exponentially in depth, and mitigation enters the regime where overhead bounds bite hardest.

### 2.2 Readout versus gate noise

Two noise classes receive different treatments. **Readout errors** — bit-flip misassignment at measurement — are classical, local, and efficiently characterizable; they are handled by dedicated readout-error mitigation (e.g., twirled readout error extinction, TREX) and are typically *assumed already mitigated* when benchmarking gate-noise methods. **Gate noise** — coherent over-rotations, incoherent depolarization, amplitude damping, leakage, crosstalk — is the hard problem, and is the focus of this thesis.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is comparative and theoretical, grounded in the published literature and verified numerical scaling laws. We proceed as follows:

1. **Formalize each mitigation family** as an estimator $\hat{O}_{\mathrm{em}}$ acting on noisy circuit executions, identifying its *bias* (systematic error from model misspecification or truncation) and its *sampling overhead* $C_{\mathrm{em}}$ (variance inflation).
2. **Derive overhead scalings** where closed forms exist — e.g., the Richardson weights for ZNE and the quasi-probability 1-norm for PEC — and report numerical scalings from the literature where they do not.
3. **Confront the fundamental bounds**: the lower bounds on $C_{\mathrm{em}}$ in terms of fault rate and depth established by Takagi, Endo, Minagawa, and Gu [5], and sharpened by Quek, Lin, and collaborators to *exponential tightness*.
4. **Survey hardware demonstrations**, emphasizing the Kandala *et al.* 2019 experiment on superconducting qubits that first showed mitigation extending *computational reach* (solving problems inaccessible without mitigation) rather than merely reducing error bars [6], and virtual-distillation QAOA studies [7].

All claims are anchored to peer-reviewed sources enumerated in the References; no numerical result in this thesis is original simulation — where numbers appear, they are reported from the cited works.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Zero-Noise Extrapolation: Richardson's Deferred Approach to the Limit

ZNE is the most widely deployed mitigation technique, and conceptually the simplest. If the noisy expectation value admits a power series in the fault rate,

$$
\langle O \rangle_{\lambda} = \langle O \rangle_0 + \sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty} a_{\ell} \lambda^{\ell},
$$

then measuring at *amplified* noise levels $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_M$ and forming the Richardson combination

$$
\hat{O}_{\mathrm{em}} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \langle O \rangle_{\lambda_m} \prod_{k \ne m} \frac{\lambda_k}{\lambda_k - \lambda_m}
$$

cancels the first $M-1$ powers of $\lambda$, leaving bias $\mathcal{O}(\lambda^M)$ [1, 5]. The key practical question is how to *amplify* noise controllably. Two approaches dominate:

**Analog noise scaling** increases physical error rates — e.g., stretching pulse durations to accumulate more decoherence, as in the original Kandala *et al.* demonstration [6]. It is hardware-specific and requires low-level control.

**Digital noise scaling via unitary folding**, introduced by He *et al.* [2], is purely gate-level: replace each unitary $U$ (or the whole circuit $U$) by $U (U^\dagger U)^n$, which is logically identical but executes $(2n+1)$ times the gates, amplifying the fault rate by the odd integer scale factor $\lambda = 2n+1$. Global folding ($U \to U(U^\dagger U)^n$) is coarse; *local folding* folds individual gates or layers, and *random folding* folds randomly chosen subsets to achieve fine-grained, non-integer $\lambda$. This digital framework made ZNE accessible to any gate-based programmer and underpins the `mitiq` software library and Qiskit Runtime's ZNE primitives [2, 8]:

```python
from mitiq.zne.scaling import fold_gates_at_random
scaled = fold_gates_at_random(circuit, scale_factor=3.0)  # lambda = 3
```

The extrapolation *model* is the second design choice. Beyond Richardson (polynomial) extrapolation, exponential fits $\langle O \rangle_{\lambda} \approx \langle O \rangle_0 e^{-\gamma \lambda} + c$ capture the physical reality that, in deep circuits, local observables decay exponentially with fault rate; poly-exponential and adaptive Bayesian methods trade bias against variance with fewer scale factors [8].

> **Theorem:** *For Richardson extrapolation with $M$ noise-scaled points, the sampling overhead is $C_{\mathrm{em}} \sim \left(\sum_{m=1}^{M} \left|\prod_{k\ne m} \frac{\lambda_k}{\lambda_k - \lambda_m}\right|\right)^2$* [5]. The alternating-sign weights — some negative, some larger than one in magnitude — inflate variance. For the canonical choice $\lambda \in \{1, 3, 5\}$, the overhead is already $\approx 28$; adding points worsens it combinatorially.

ZNE's strengths are its *model-agnosticism* (no noise characterization required) and its trivial implementation. Its weaknesses are the bias–variance tradeoff above and its reliance on the perturbative expansion: when $\lambda$ is not small, the truncated series is a poor model and extrapolation can *increase* error.

### 4.2 Probabilistic Error Cancellation: Inverting Noise with Quasi-Probabilities

Where ZNE is agnostic, PEC is surgical. Given a noisy implementation $\tilde{\mathcal{U}}$ of an ideal gate $\mathcal{U}$, and a known noise model, PEC writes the *ideal* operation as a real linear combination of implementable noisy operations:

$$
\mathcal{U} = \sum_i \eta_i \, \mathcal{B}_i, \qquad \eta_i \in \mathbb{R}, \quad \sum_i |\eta_i| =: \gamma \ge 1.
$$

Because the coefficients $\eta_i$ may be negative, this is a *quasi*-probability decomposition. The protocol samples $\mathcal{B}_i$ with probability $|\eta_i|/\gamma$, executes the noisy circuit, multiplies the measurement outcome by $\mathrm{sgn}(\eta_i)$, and rescales the average by $\gamma$ [1]. The estimator is *exactly unbiased* — bias zero, in principle — but its variance is inflated by $\gamma^{2L}$ for a circuit with $L$ noisy gates:

$$
C_{\mathrm{em}}^{\mathrm{(PEC)}} \sim \gamma^{2L}, \qquad \gamma = 1 + \mathcal{O}(p)
$$

for per-gate error rate $p$. The overhead is exponential in circuit depth, with a base set by the noise strength: $\gamma^{2L} = e^{2L \ln(1+\mathcal{O}(p))} \approx e^{\mathcal{O}(Lp)}$.

> **Lemma:** *PEC's cost is governed by the 1-norm $\gamma$ of the quasi-probability decomposition, which is minimal (but still $>1$) for the optimal decomposition of the given noise channel.* Better noise models — e.g., the sparse Pauli–Lindblad models of van den Berg *et al.*, which capture realistic correlated crosstalk — yield smaller $\gamma$ and hence cheaper mitigation, at the price of expensive tomography to learn the model.

PEC therefore sits at the opposite pole from ZNE: *zero bias, maximal information requirement*. It demands gate-set tomography or learning-based characterization of every noisy operation, and it is fragile to *model drift* — if the hardware noise changes between characterization and execution, the cancellation is no longer exact and bias returns silently [5].

### 4.3 Clifford Data Regression: Learning the Noise Map from Simulable Circuits

Clifford Data Regression (CDR), introduced by Czarnik, Arrasmith, Coles, and Cincio [3], takes a machine-learning route. The observation is that circuits composed mostly of *Clifford* gates are efficiently simulable classically (Gottesman–Knill), yet if they share the target circuit's structure, they experience similar noise. The protocol:

1. From the target circuit $U(\theta)$ (with non-Clifford rotations), generate $K$ *training circuits* by replacing most non-Clifford gates with nearby Clifford gates.
2. For each training circuit $V_i$, compute the *exact* expectation value $X_i^{\mathrm{exact}}$ classically and the *noisy* value $X_i^{\mathrm{noisy}}$ on hardware.
3. Fit a linear ansatz $X^{\mathrm{exact}} \approx a_1 X^{\mathrm{noisy}} + a_2$ by least squares on $\{(X_i^{\mathrm{noisy}}, X_i^{\mathrm{exact}})\}$.
4. Apply the learned affine map to the target circuit's noisy measurement: $\hat{O}_{\mathrm{em}} = a_1 X^{\mathrm{noisy}}_{\mathrm{target}} + a_2$.

CDR *exactly* corrects global depolarizing noise ($\rho \mapsto (1-p)\rho + p\,\mathbb{I}/d$, which is an affine map on expectation values), and empirically it suppresses realistic local noise far better than the affine model would suggest [3]. On a 16-qubit ground-state energy problem on IBM hardware, CDR achieved roughly an *order-of-magnitude* error reduction [3].

| Method | Bias | Overhead $C_{\mathrm{em}}$ | Noise info needed | Extra qubits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ZNE (Richardson, $M$ pts) | $\mathcal{O}(\lambda^M)$ | $\sim (\sum_m \|w_m\|)^2$ | none | 0 |
| PEC | 0 (ideal model) | $\sim \gamma^{2L}$ | full channel tomography | 0 |
| CDR | empirical (affine fit) | $\sim K$ training circuits | none (learned) | 0 |
| VD ($M$ copies) | $\to 0$ as $M\to\infty$ | $\sim 1/\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)^2$ | none | $(M-1)n$ |

### 4.4 Virtual Distillation: Error Suppression by Derangement

Virtual distillation (VD), proposed by Huggins *et al.* [4] and independently as *error suppression by derangement*, exploits a striking algebraic fact: given $M$ copies of a noisy state $\rho = \sum_k \lambda_k |\psi_k\rangle\langle\psi_k|$ with $\lambda_1 > \lambda_2 \ge \cdots$, the *exponentiated* state

$$
\tilde{\rho} := \frac{\rho^M}{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)} = \frac{\sum_k \lambda_k^M |\psi_k\rangle\langle\psi_k|}{\sum_k \lambda_k^M}
$$

converges *exponentially fast in $M$* to the dominant eigenvector $|\psi_1\rangle\langle\psi_1|$ — the "closest pure state" to $\rho$. If noise primarily mixes the ideal state with orthogonal error states, the mitigated expectation value

$$
\langle O \rangle_{\mathrm{mitigated}} := \frac{\mathrm{Tr}(O \rho^M)}{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)}
$$

suppresses the error contribution by $(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^M$ — an *exponential* suppression in the number of copies, demonstrated numerically at multiple orders of magnitude [4].

Crucially, $\rho^M$ is never prepared. Both numerator and denominator are estimated by measuring a *derangement* (cyclic permutation) operator $S^{(M)}$ across the $M$ copies, using a Hadamard-test-like circuit with one ancilla [4]. For $M=2$, this is the familiar SWAP test: $\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^2) = \mathrm{Tr}(S \rho \otimes \rho)$.

VD's overhead is set by the denominator: estimating $\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)$ to relative precision requires $\sim 1/\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)^2$ shots, and $\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)$ decays exponentially in the system's entropy — i.e., in $n$ and depth.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The sampling-overhead lower bounds

The capstone theoretical result of the field — and the reason the title speaks of a *frontier* — is the proof that unbiased error mitigation is exponentially expensive in general. Takagi, Endo, Minagawa, and Gu [5] established the first rigorous lower bound: for any unbiased mitigation protocol under local depolarizing noise, the sampling overhead satisfies

$$
C_{\mathrm{em}} \ge \exp(c \cdot \lambda) \quad \text{for depth-dependent fault rate } \lambda,
$$

with constant $c$ depending on the noise model. Quek *et al.* subsequently sharpened these bounds to *exponential tightness* in the circuit depth for broad noise classes, showing that the exponential scaling is not an artifact of loose analysis but a fundamental feature: *no* unbiased protocol can beat it. The proof technique is information-theoretic — mitigation is recast as a parameter-estimation problem, and the quantum Fisher information of the noisy channel family bounds how much signal about the ideal value survives per shot.

> **Theorem (informal, Takagi *et al.* / Quek *et al.*):** *Any QEM protocol producing an unbiased estimator of $\langle O \rangle_0$ under local stochastic noise must incur sampling overhead exponential in the expected number of faults $\lambda$ (hence in circuit depth at fixed error rate).* Biased protocols (ZNE, CDR) evade the bound's letter — the bound constrains *unbiased* estimation — but pay the corresponding price in systematic bias that cannot be removed by more shots.

This is the *sampling-overhead frontier*: a Pareto boundary in the (bias, overhead) plane that no protocol crosses. PEC lives on it at the unbiased extreme; ZNE and CDR live in the biased interior, buying polynomial overhead at the price of residual bias. The frontier is why mitigation is a *NISQ* technology: it extends the reach of noisy devices by constant-to-polynomial factors in depth, but it cannot substitute for fault tolerance asymptotically.

### 5.2 Hardware demonstrations on superconducting processors

**VQE and the 2019 milestone.** Kandala *et al.* [6] provided the landmark demonstration on IBM superconducting qubits. Using ZNE with noise amplified by stretching gate durations (analog scaling), they mitigated errors in single- and two-qubit experiments and — decisively — in VQE optimizations of molecular Hamiltonians (H$_2$, LiH) and quantum magnetism models. The mitigated variational energies reached accuracies *inaccessible* to the bare noisy processor: the experiment demonstrated *extended computational reach*, not merely improved precision on an already-solvable problem. This remains the canonical evidence that QEM is not a post-processing nicety but a genuine capability multiplier [6].

**QAOA and virtual distillation.** Numerical and hardware studies of VD applied to QAOA for MaxCut show the method's distinctive strength on *incoherent* noise: with $M=2$ copies, energy-estimation error fell by 67% under pure dephasing at 10% rate and 20% under depolarizing noise at the same rate [7]. The asymmetry is instructive — dephasing, which mixes the ideal state with orthogonal states without shifting the dominant eigenvector much, is VD's ideal use case; depolarizing noise, which shrinks all eigenvalues uniformly, helps less.

**Reproducibility infrastructure.** The field has matured to standardized tooling: `mitiq` (unitary folding, Richardson/exponential inference), Qiskit Runtime's estimator primitives with built-in ZNE, and volumetric benchmarking suites (`qermit`) comparing methods on equal footing [8].

A representative ZNE workflow in Qiskit-style pseudocode folds the circuit to integer scale factors, executes at $\lambda \in \{1, 3, 5\}$, and applies `zne.inference.RichardsonFactory.extrapolate` to the measured expectation values — typically via the `mitiq` library [2, 8].

## 6 Limitations

Honesty requires dwelling on where the edifice cracks.

1. **The exponential wall is real.** The lower bounds of Section 5.1 are not pessimistic conjectures; they are theorems. For circuits with fault rate $\lambda \gtrsim 10$–$20$, unbiased mitigation is infeasible, and biased mitigation's residual error saturates. QEM extends NISQ reach; it does not abolish the need for QEC.

2. **Noise-model dependence and drift.** PEC is exactly unbiased only for the *characterized* noise; superconducting devices drift on hourly timescales (two-level systems, flux noise), silently invalidating tomography. ZNE's agnosticism is a feature, but its extrapolation model is an *assumption* — exponential fits fail for coherent errors, polynomial fits oscillate (Runge phenomenon) with many scale factors.

3. **Coherent errors resist statistical mitigation.** Systematic over-rotations and crosstalk produce *bias* that does not average away under resampling; they require calibration, dynamical decoupling, or Pauli twirling (which *converts* coherent errors into stochastic ones that PEC/ZNE can handle) as a pre-processing layer [5].

4. **VD's qubit and purity costs.** Virtual distillation needs $(M-1)n$ extra qubits and a noisy state whose dominant eigenvector still resembles the ideal state. Under strong depolarizing noise the purity $\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^2)$ collapses and the denominator estimation becomes the bottleneck; under coherent errors the "dominant eigenvector" may not be the target state at all.

5. **CDR's simulability ceiling.** Training circuits must be classically simulable, capping the number of non-Clifford gates they may contain. As target circuits grow more non-Clifford — as any useful circuit must — the training distribution drifts from the target distribution and the learned affine map extrapolates rather than interpolates.

6. **Mitigation interacts with variational optimization.** In VQE/QAOA, the mitigated estimator is noisier per shot than the raw estimator; optimizers may need more iterations, partially offsetting the per-iteration accuracy gain.

## 7 Conclusion

Quantum error mitigation has grown, in under a decade, from two schemes in a single PRL [1] into a disciplined subfield with its own software stacks, benchmarking suites, and — most importantly — *fundamental limits*. The four families surveyed here represent four different answers to the same question: what price will you pay to recover $\langle O \rangle_0$?

- **ZNE** pays in *model risk*: cheap, agnostic, biased, and hostage to the extrapolation ansatz.
- **PEC** pays in *information*: unbiased, but exponentially costly in depth and hostage to tomography.
- **CDR** pays in *classical simulation*: a learned correction that is cheap when the target is near-Clifford and degrades as quantumness grows.
- **VD** pays in *qubits and purity*: exponential suppression of incoherent errors when the state stays near-pure.

The sampling-overhead frontier — the theorem that unbiased mitigation costs $\exp(\Omega(\lambda))$ [5] — organizes these trade-offs into a single picture and sets the research agenda: push the Pareto boundary outward with better noise learning (sparse Pauli–Lindblad models), hybridize biased and unbiased methods, and above all, co-design mitigation with the hardware drift and calibration realities of superconducting processors [6, 8]. Until fault tolerance arrives, the frontier is where quantum advantage will be won or lost — one shot at a time, exponentially many of them.
