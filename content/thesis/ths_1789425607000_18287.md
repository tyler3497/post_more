# Mechanistic Interpretability of Transformers: Induction Heads, Circuits, and the Mathematics of In-Context Learning

## Abstract

Mechanistic interpretability reverse-engineers neural networks by decomposing trained weights into human-understandable algorithms [1]. This thesis develops the mathematical theory of transformer circuits with induction heads as its central case study: two-head compositions that complete "A B ... A" with "B" and plausibly account for most in-context learning in large language models [2]. We derive the QK/OV circuit decomposition of self-attention, formalize the previous-token-plus-induction-head motif as a bilinear match-and-copy operation over residual-stream subspaces, and survey the evidence that induction heads emerge in a sharp phase change coincident with the onset of in-context learning [6]. We then present the fully reverse-engineered Fourier circuit that explains grokking on modular addition [3], the indirect-object-identification circuit in GPT-2 small [4], and recent work distinguishing induction heads from function-vector heads [8]. We close by confronting the limitations of circuit-level explanations — superposition, incomplete coverage, and scalability — and argue that induction heads are the first existence proof of a complete mechanistic account of an emergent neural capability.

---

## 1 The Reverse-Engineering Program: From Weights to Algorithms

The dominant paradigm of deep learning treats trained models as black boxes, evaluated by input-output behavior alone. Mechanistic interpretability rejects this agnosticism [1]. Its goal is to *reverse-engineer* the network: factor high-dimensional weight tensors into functional components ("features"), trace their interaction through the computation graph ("circuits"), and state the learned algorithm in language a human can audit, perturb, and predict.

The transformer is the natural proving ground. Its computation is largely *linear algebra with a few well-placed nonlinearities*: embeddings, residual-stream additions, per-head attention patterns, and MLP nonlinearities [1]. Elhage et al. showed that much of a one- or two-layer attention-only transformer can be understood exactly by decomposing each attention head into a *QK circuit* (which decides where the head attends) and an *OV circuit* (which decides what it writes) [1]. This factorization is the mathematical substrate of everything that follows.

The crown jewel of the program is the *induction head*: an attention head that, given "... A B ... A", increases the logit of "B" [2]. Olsson et al. presented six lines of evidence that induction heads constitute the mechanism behind the majority of *in-context learning* — the ability of language models to decrease loss as context grows, adapting to novel patterns without weight updates [2]. If correct, induction heads are the first emergent neural capability to receive a complete mechanistic explanation, and the template by which all subsequent explanations will be judged.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The transformer as a residual-stream machine

A transformer processes tokens through stacked layers, each *reading from* and *writing to* a shared residual stream — the running sum of embeddings and layer outputs at each position [1]:

```
x_i = W_E[t_i] + W_pos[i]                  # initial residual stream
x_i = x_i + attention_layer(x)             # attention adds its result
x_i = x_i + MLP(x_i)                       # MLP adds its result
logits = W_U @ x_i                         # final readout
```

Because every layer's output is merely added to the stream, each component's contribution can be analyzed *independently*, read through the lens of the unembedding matrix W_U [1].

### 2.2 Attention heads as QK/OV pairs

Elhage et al. decomposed each head's behavior into two circuits [1]:

- **QK circuit**: W_Q^T W_K maps the query stream at position i and the key stream at position j into an attention score. It answers *where* information flows.
- **OV circuit**: W_O W_V maps the value stream at the attended position j into an output written at position i. It answers *what* information flows.

Both are *bilinear*, and both can be studied over token-pair space by sandwiching them between unembedding and embedding matrices: W_U W_OV W_E describes a head's direct token-to-token copying, while W_E^T W_QK W_E describes which token pairs it matches.

### 2.3 In-context learning

*In-context learning* (ICL) is the ability to improve predictions as a function of context length, learning novel associations from the prompt alone with no gradient updates [2]. The canonical quantitative signature is the difference between the loss at the 500th and the 50th token of a context: an ICL-capable model has lower loss later in the sequence because it has absorbed the document's statistics. Since adaptation is a *forward-pass computation*, it must be implemented by some circuit inside the frozen weights.

### 2.4 Grokking and phase changes

Small transformers on algorithmic tasks exhibit *phase changes*: long plateaus of memorization followed by sudden generalization [3]. Nanda et al. showed that grokking of modular addition is fully explicable: the network gradually builds a Fourier multiplication circuit in its weights, and sudden generalization marks the moment the generalizing circuit overtakes the memorizing one [3]. The induction-head story has the same shape — a sharp loss drop at the precise moment induction heads form [2][6] — and both share a common signature: simple circuits composed into structured, generalizing algorithms.

## 3 Methodology

Mechanistic interpretability has converged on a reusable experimental toolkit.

**Circuit decomposition.** Factor weight matrices into per-head QK and OV circuits [1], then study *head composition*: how a layer-0 head's output enters the Q, K, or V of a layer-1 head, forming "virtual heads" whose effective circuit is a product of matrices. This is the lens through which induction heads were discovered: the composition of a *previous-token head* with an *induction head* yields a virtual head implementing match-and-copy.

**Induction scores.** Heads are scored by their behavior on repeated random sequences: the induction score sums the attention weight a head places on the successor of each token's previous occurrence, normalized to the unit interval [8]. A head with score near 1 is a textbook induction head.

**Ablation and causal intervention.** The gold standard is to *ablate* the candidate circuit — zeroing its residual-stream contribution — and check that behavior degrades as predicted [2][3][4]. Olsson et al. ablated induction heads and found the ICL metric collapsed; Nanda et al. ablated key Fourier frequencies and reduced grokking to chance [3].

**Fourier and spectral analysis.** Projecting weights and activations into the discrete Fourier basis reveals the sparse spectral structure of learned algorithms [3] — the route by which the modular-addition circuit's "trigonometric identities" were read off the network.

**Path patching.** For full-scale models, Wang et al. selectively substituted activations from counterfactual inputs along specific computational paths to isolate the indirect-object-identification circuit in GPT-2 small [4].

> **Theorem (informal, Elhage et al.):** In an attention-only transformer, the entire forward pass decomposes into a sum of QK circuits, OV circuits, and compositions thereof. Every behavioral effect on token distributions is attributable to some sub-sum of these circuit terms [1].

The limitation of this methodology, taken up in section 6, is *coverage*: these analyses explain the heads we can find, not necessarily all the computation the model performs.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The QK/OV Decomposition: A Calculus of Attention

The decomposition's power lies in linearity. A head's output at position i is a convex combination of value vectors:

output_i = sum_j A_ij * (W_O W_V x_j)

where A_ij is the attention pattern and x_j the residual stream at position j. The fixed linear map **W_O W_V** — the OV circuit — decides what is written [1]; the bilinear form x_i^T W_Q^T W_K x_j — the QK circuit — decides the attention weights. This yields two clean analytical objects:

1. **The OV eigenvalue spectrum.** Sandwiching the OV circuit between unembedding and embedding gives a vocab-by-vocab matrix. Heads with large positive real eigenvalues are *copying heads*: they move tokens forward through the residual stream, amplifying the attended token's logit [1].
2. **The QK token-matching matrix.** W_E^T W_Q^T W_K W_E reveals which *token pairs* a head matches, independent of position; positional behavior is captured separately through W_pos.

| Circuit | Governs | Diagnostic | Typical role |
|---|---|---|---|
| OV circuit | What is written | W_U W_OV W_E spectrum | Copying, suppression |
| QK circuit | Where attention flows | Token-pair matching matrix | Position routing, pattern match |
| Q/K composition | Cross-layer matching | Virtual-head QK | Induction, prefix matching |

The critical move is *composition*: head h0's output in layer 0 becomes part of the residual stream that head h1 reads through its Q, K, or V. The composition of h1's Q with h0's output is a new bilinear form — a *virtual attention head* — matching patterns no single head could [1]. Induction heads are precisely such a composition.

### 4.2 The Induction Head: A Two-Layer Copy Machine

An **induction head** implements the rule "... A B ... A" → "B", realized by a two-layer motif [2][6]:

1. **Layer 0: the previous-token head.** Its QK circuit routes attention from each position to its immediate predecessor, and its OV circuit copies that predecessor's token identity into the current position's residual stream. After layer 0, the stream at position i represents "the token that came before position i".
2. **Layer 1: the induction head proper.** Its *query* reads the current token ("A"); its *key* reads the previous-token head's output, which at position j represents "the token before j". The QK composition matches current "A" against positions j whose *predecessor* was "A" — i.e., it attends to the position *after* the previous "A", which holds "B". The OV circuit copies "B" forward into the logits.

Formally: the induction head attends where query(x_i) ≈ key(p_j), with p the previous-token head's output, so attention concentrates on position j with token_{j-1} = token_i, and the OV circuit writes token_j. The motif is a *match-and-copy* operation implementable by the bilinear algebra of attention — no MLP required [2].

The training dynamics are as interesting as the forward pass. Neither component is useful alone: a previous-token head without an induction head merely shuffles tokens backwards, and an induction head without one has no key signal to match. This *co-dependence* explains the sharp phase change at formation — gradient descent must discover both together, producing a cooperative jump in ICL ability [2][6]. Recent theory makes this precise: in minimal models, training dynamics are confined to a 19-dimensional subspace with only 3 dimensions accounting for emergence, and emergence time obeys a bound quadratic in context length [5]; optimization-theoretic work characterizes the abrupt transition from lazy n-gram behavior to the rich induction-head mechanism [7].

### 4.3 In-Context Learning as Iterated Copy-Based Completion

Why would a copying primitive explain *general* in-context learning? Olsson et al. offered six complementary lines of evidence [2]:

1. **Co-emergence.** Induction heads form at exactly the training step where ICL ability — loss(500th token) minus loss(50th token) — sharply increases.
2. **Causal ablation.** Removing induction heads from small attention-only models destroys the ICL metric while leaving early-token loss intact.
3. **Argument by construction.** Two-layer attention-only models *with* induction circuits show the ICL signature; matched models where they cannot form do not.
4. **Scaling.** The ICL benefit of induction heads grows with model and data scale consistently with large-model behavior.
5. **Mechanistic generality.** The same previous-token-plus-induction motif appears across model sizes and training runs.
6. **Correlational evidence in MLP models.** In larger models with MLPs, induction-head prevalence correlates with ICL strength.

The conceptual bridge is that natural text is *repetitive at the right scale*: names, technical terms, syntactic frames, and stylistic tics recur within documents, and a head completing "A B ... A" with "B" can exploit n-gram statistics, few-shot label mappings, and translation pairs alike. Few-shot prompting is, from this perspective, induction over longer, semantically structured bigrams.

Recent comparative work sharpens this picture by contrasting induction heads with **function-vector (FV) heads** [8]. The finding is nuanced: induction heads dominate token-level, copy-flavored ICL, while FV heads carry more abstract task-vector behavior. The two mechanisms coexist, with their relative balance shifting by model scale and task type [8]. This refines rather than refutes the hypothesis: in-context learning is a *portfolio* of circuits, and induction heads are its largest holding.

### 4.4 The Fourier Circuit: Grokking Reverse-Engineered

The most complete reverse-engineering result in the literature is Nanda et al.'s analysis of grokking on modular addition [3]. A one-layer transformer trained to compute (a + b) mod p first memorizes, then — long after training accuracy saturates — suddenly generalizes. The explanation, read off the weights:

1. **Embeddings are sinusoidal.** The embedding maps each token a to sines and cosines at a sparse set of key frequencies w_k: a → (cos(w_k a), sin(w_k a)).
2. **Attention and MLP compose rotations.** Via trigonometric identities — cos(w(a+b)) = cos(wa)cos(wb) − sin(wa)sin(wb) — the network computes sin and cos of w_k(a+b).
3. **Logit readout by inverse rotation.** The unembedding "reads off" candidates c by rotating backwards, giving logits proportional to cos(w_k(a+b−c)), maximized exactly when a+b ≡ c (mod p).

```python
# Idealized Fourier circuit for (a + b) mod p  (Nanda et al. 2023)
import numpy as np

def embed(a, freqs, p):
    # token a -> sinusoids at key frequencies
    return np.concatenate([np.cos(2*np.pi*f*a/p) for f in freqs] +
                          [np.sin(2*np.pi*f*a/p) for f in freqs])

def logits(a, b, freqs, p):
    # compose rotations via trig identities, then inverse-rotate readout
    scores = np.zeros(p)
    for c in range(p):
        scores[c] = sum(np.cos(2*np.pi*f*(a + b - c)/p) for f in freqs)
    return scores  # argmax == (a+b) mod p
```

Four lines of evidence confirm the circuit: periodic structure in weights and activations, sinusoidal neuron-logit maps, degree-2 polynomial fits in Fourier space, and ablation showing that removing key frequencies destroys performance while removing the other 95 percent of frequencies slightly *improves* it [3]. Training splits into three continuous phases — *memorization*, *circuit formation*, and *cleanup* — with grokking the visible moment the structured circuit overtakes the memorizing one: the induction-head phase change, replayed in miniature and fully solved.

```haskell
-- Addition as rotation composition, functionally
type Freq = Double
embed :: Int -> [Freq] -> Int -> [(Double, Double)]
embed a freqs p = [ (cos (2*pi*f*fromIntegral a/fromIntegral p)
                   , sin (2*pi*f*fromIntegral a/fromIntegral p))
                 | f <- freqs ]

-- cos(w(a+b-c)) peaks exactly when a+b == c (mod p)
score :: Int -> Int -> Int -> [Freq] -> Int -> Double
score a b c freqs p =
  sum [ cos (2*pi*f*fromIntegral (a+b-c)/fromIntegral p) | f <- freqs ]
```

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

**Induction heads and the ICL phase change.** In two-layer attention-only transformers, Olsson et al. documented a sharp training-loss drop at the step where induction heads form, with the ICL metric improving discontinuously at the same point [2]. Subcircuit analyses show this phase change is *data-dependent*: timing and sharpness depend on which subcircuits of the induction motif must "go right", and partial formation yields partial ICL gains [6].

**Causal necessity in small models.** Ablating induction heads removes the ICL advantage while preserving early-position performance — the signature of a *specific* rather than general degradation [2]. This is the strongest evidence mechanistic interpretability can offer: the behavior is not merely correlated with the circuit; it is *implemented by* it.

**Predictive theory of emergence.** Musat et al. proved for a minimal ICL task that dynamics are confined to a 19-dimensional subspace, with only 3 dimensions accounting for emergence, and derived a tight quadratic-in-context-length bound on emergence time [5]. Optimization-theoretic work characterizes the abrupt lazy-to-rich transition [7]. These are the beginnings of a *predictive* theory: not just "induction heads exist" but "they must emerge by step T under conditions C".

**Grokking, fully solved.** Nanda et al.'s Fourier circuit is the field's existence proof that a nontrivial learned algorithm can be recovered *exactly* from weights, verified by four independent lines of evidence, with progress measures rendering the "sudden" generalization continuous [3].

**Circuits in the wild.** Wang et al. reverse-engineered the indirect-object-identification (IOI) circuit in GPT-2 small — duplicate-token heads, S-inhibition heads, and name-mover heads composed into a multi-step algorithm [4]. The IOI circuit requires inhibition as well as copying, showing the methodology scales beyond toy models.

| Result | Model scale | Method | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Induction heads explain ICL | 1–3 layer attention-only | Ablation + co-emergence | Causal [2] |
| Induction-head phase change | Small transformers | Subcircuit analysis | Mechanistic [6] |
| Emergence-time bounds | Minimal ICL task | Dynamical-systems proof | Theoretical [5] |
| Lazy-to-rich transition | Small transformers | Optimization analysis | Theoretical [7] |
| Grokking Fourier circuit | 1-layer transformer | Fourier ablation | Fully solved [3] |
| IOI circuit | GPT-2 small (117M) | Path patching | Mechanistic [4] |
| Induction vs. FV heads | Modern LLMs | Comparative scoring | Correlational [8] |

## 6 Limitations

**Superposition.** The cleanest results concern settings where features align with neurons or heads. In large models, features are routinely represented in *superposition*: more features than dimensions, entangled across the same parameters [1]. Any claim that induction heads "explain ICL" must be weighted by how much residual-stream variance they account for — a quantity rarely measured.

**Incomplete coverage.** Analyses explain the circuits we find, not the computation we miss. The IOI circuit explains a large *fraction* of GPT-2 small's behavior on its task distribution [4]; for induction heads in large models with MLPs, the evidence remains correlational [2], and FV-head results show alternative ICL mechanisms carry real weight [8]. A complete account of ICL in frontier models does not yet exist.

**Proof vs. evidence.** Ablation shows necessity but rarely sufficiency; co-emergence shows correlation across training time; "the attention pattern looks like induction" is suggestive but not dispositive. The strongest results pair multiple independent methods — as Nanda et al. did with four lines of evidence [3] — but few large-model claims meet that bar.

**Scalability of method.** Path patching, manual circuit tracing, and Fourier analysis are labor-intensive and model-specific. Automated circuit discovery remains nascent, and the gap between two-layer attention-only proofs and hundred-billion-parameter claims is the field's central open problem.

**Fragility of definitions.** The induction score, the ICL metric, and even "in-context learning" are operational choices [2][8]. Different metrics rank heads differently, and the induction-versus-FV comparison shows conclusions are sensitive to scoring [8].

These limitations calibrate rather than refute the program. Induction heads remain the best-understood emergent computation in deep learning, and the items above are a research agenda, not a refutation.

## 7 Conclusion

Mechanistic interpretability has moved from aspiration to demonstration. The QK/OV decomposition gave us a calculus for attention [1]; the induction head gave us a complete, causal, mathematically precise account of how a network learns to learn in context [2]; the Fourier circuit gave us a fully solved instance of emergent generalization [3]; and the IOI circuit showed the methods working in a real language model [4]. Recent theory is beginning to *predict* when these circuits form [5][7], and comparative work is mapping the full portfolio of ICL mechanisms [8].

> **Theorem (the field's central bet):** Every emergent capability of a neural network is implemented by some circuit in its weights; with sufficient analytical effort, that circuit can be identified, verified by intervention, and stated as an algorithm.

Whether this bet pays off at frontier scale is unknown, and the limitations — superposition, incomplete coverage, the labor of manual analysis — are formidable. But the induction head stands as an existence proof that the bet is not empty: a capability as striking as in-context learning, once mysterious, now has a mechanism with a name, an equation, and an ablation curve. The mathematics of in-context learning turned out to be, at its core, the mathematics of matching and copying — composed, iterated, and amplified by gradient descent into something that looks, from the outside, like understanding.
