# Threshold Signatures for Cryptocurrency Custody: FROST, GG20 TSS, Distributed Key Generation, and Nonce-Misuse Resistance in Multi-Party Wallets

## Abstract

The custody of digital assets has converged on a single architectural primitive: the *t-of-n* threshold signature, in which a quorum of share-holders jointly authorizes transactions while no single party ever holds the complete signing key. This thesis treats the two dominant protocol families in production multi-party wallets — the Flexible Round-Optimized Schnorr Threshold (FROST) protocol, standardized by the IRTF as RFC 9591 [1][2], and the Gennaro–Goldfeder GG20 threshold ECDSA construction with identifiable abort [3] — together with the distributed key generation ceremonies that provision their shares and the nonce-misuse defenses without which any threshold deployment leaks key material. We formalize the security models, derive the share-aggregation equations, analyze the multiplicative-to-additive (MtA) conversion at the heart of GG20's offline phase, and prove that FROST's binding-factor mechanism neutralizes the concurrent-session forgeries that broke earlier Schnorr threshold designs. Benchmarks from reference implementations quantify the gap: FROST signs in two rounds with *O(t)* curve operations, while GG20's dishonest-majority ECDSA presigning needs six to eight rounds dominated by Paillier operations. The result is a decision framework for matching protocol selection to custody threat models.

## 1 Introduction

Cryptocurrency custody is, at its core, a key-management problem. The single point of failure — one private key, one device, one operator — accounts for the overwhelming majority of large-scale asset losses, from exchange hot-wallet compromises to funds stranded when sole key holders become unavailable. Two naive responses dominate practice: *multisignature* scripts, which move quorum logic on-chain at the cost of larger transactions, privacy leakage, and chain-specific scripting; and naive *key splitting*, which offers none of the verifiability or accountability of proper threshold cryptography.

*Threshold signatures* occupy the middle ground. A group of *n* participants jointly holds a signing key split into shares such that any *t* of them can produce a signature indistinguishable from a single signer's, while any *t − 1* learn nothing about the key. The on-chain footprint is one ordinary signature — verifiers cannot distinguish threshold-signed transactions from single-key ones, yielding both privacy and maximal chain compatibility.

Two signature families dominate production:

1. **Schnorr-based threshold signatures**, culminating in FROST [2], specified in RFC 9591 [1]. Schnorr's linear structure — the response is a linear function of the secret — makes thresholdization comparatively straightforward, requiring no general-purpose MPC machinery.
2. **Threshold ECDSA** (TSS-ECDSA), of which GG20 [3] is the most widely deployed dishonest-majority construction. ECDSA's nonlinear structure (the signature involves the modular inverse of the nonce) forces the use of heavy machinery: Paillier homomorphic encryption, multiplicative-to-additive share conversion, and range proofs.

This thesis makes four contributions. **First**, a unified algebraic presentation of FROST and GG20, exposing exactly where each protocol spends its complexity budget. **Second**, an analysis of distributed key generation — the Pedersen-style verifiable secret sharing ceremony provisioning shares without a trusted dealer — and its security subtleties. **Third**, a formalization of nonce-misuse resistance, showing why deterministic nonce derivation is catastrophically insecure in the multi-party setting and how FROST's binding factors provide domain separation across concurrent sessions. **Fourth**, empirical performance data and custody-architecture guidance for practitioners building multi-party wallets.

> **Thesis statement:** *For Schnorr-compatible chains, FROST with Pedersen DKG and randomized session nonces is the strictly dominant custody primitive; for ECDSA-only chains such as Bitcoin and Ethereum, GG20 (or its CGGMP21 successor) remains the sound choice, provided the presigning phase is amortized offline and identifiable-abort mechanisms are used to attribute failures in adversarial deployments.*

## 2 Background and Related Work

### Threshold cryptography and secret sharing

Shamir's *(t, n)*-threshold secret sharing [9] underlies nearly all deployed threshold signature schemes: a secret *s* is encoded as the constant term of a random degree-*(t − 1)* polynomial *f(x)*, and party *i* receives *sᵢ = f(i)*. Any *t* shares reconstruct *s* via Lagrange interpolation:

$$s = \sum_{i \in S} \lambda_{i,S} \cdot s_i, \quad \lambda_{i,S} = \prod_{j \in S, j \ne i} \frac{j}{j - i}$$

Feldman's verifiable secret sharing (VSS) [7] adds public commitments *Cⱼ = g^{aⱼ}* to each polynomial coefficient, allowing shareholders to verify that their received share is consistent with the dealer's committed polynomial — the foundation of dealerless distributed key generation.

### The ECDSA threshold lineage

Threshold ECDSA has been studied since the 1990s, but the modern dishonest-majority line begins with the Lindell–Nof–Ranellucci fast multiparty ECDSA construction with practical DKG [9] and the Gennaro–Goldfeder line: GG18 ("Fast Multiparty Threshold ECDSA with Fast Trustless Setup") and its successor GG20, "One Round Threshold ECDSA with Identifiable Abort" [3], which contributed (i) a non-interactive online signing phase after offline presigning, and (ii) a mechanism to *identify* the misbehaving party when the protocol aborts — a critical property in distributed custody, where undetectable denial-of-service by any single participant is otherwise free. The line was subsequently strengthened by CGGMP21 ("UC Non-Interactive, Proactive, Threshold ECDSA with Identifiable Aborts") [4][10], which adds proactive share refresh and a UC-security proof, and by DKLS23, a three-round construction with lighter assumptions now common in open-source MPC wallet libraries [5].

### The Schnorr threshold lineage

Schnorr threshold signatures are inherently simpler because of linearity, but early designs were vulnerable to *concurrent-session* attacks: an adversary controlling the message scheduling across parallel signing sessions could extract key shares (the classic attack of Benhamouda et al. against naive two-round Schnorr thresholdization). FROST, introduced by Komlo and Goldberg [2] and standardized in RFC 9591 [1], defeated this attack class with a simple and elegant mechanism: a *binding factor* ρᵢ that cryptographically binds each participant's nonce contribution to the full set of commitments, the message, and the group public key. Robust asynchronous wrappers further provide *robustness* — guaranteed output delivery against Byzantine signers — by wrapping FROST in a coordinator-driven retry loop over different signer subsets.

### Custody practice

In production, threshold ECDSA underpins the wallet architectures of major custodians and the open-source Vultisig MPC wallet stack [8], while FROST has been adopted in the Zcash and Stacks ecosystems and in emerging Bitcoin custody tooling via Taproot/Schnorr. A published survey of ECDSA threshold signing protocols [9] provides a useful taxonomy of the round/computation trade-offs.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is *protocol-theoretic with empirical grounding*: we derive each protocol from its algebraic core, state the exact security model under which it was proven secure, and then anchor the analysis in measurements from publicly available reference implementations (the RFC 9591 test vectors [1], the `go-mpc` FROST implementation [8], and published GG20 benchmarks [3]).

**Threat model.** We work in the *dishonest-majority, static-corruption* model standard for custody: up to *t − 1* of *n* participants may be fully malicious (Byzantine), colluding, and adaptive within a session, while the network is synchronous with authenticated broadcast for DKG and point-to-point authenticated channels for signing. We assume the discrete logarithm and computational Diffie–Hellman problems are hard in the chosen group, and — for ECDSA constructions — the semantic security of Paillier encryption and the strong-RSA assumption underlying its range proofs.

**Evaluation criteria.** Each protocol is scored on:

- *Round complexity* of signing (latency-dominant in geo-distributed custody);
- *Computational profile* (elliptic-curve ops vs. Paillier/bignum ops);
- *Accountability* (identifiable abort vs. anonymous abort);
- *Keygen trust assumptions* (trusted dealer vs. dealerless DKG);
- *Nonce hygiene* (resistance to reuse and bias attacks);
- *On-chain compatibility* (secp256k1 ECDSA for Bitcoin/Ethereum vs. Schnorr for Taproot/Ed25519 chains).

## 4 Threshold Signature Protocols for Custody

### 4.1 FROST: Two-Round Schnorr Threshold Signatures

FROST operates over a prime-order group 𝔾 of order *q* with generator *g*. Each participant *i* holds a Shamir share *sᵢ* of the group secret *s*, with group public key *Y = gˢ*. Signing proceeds in exactly two rounds:

**Round 1 — Nonce commitment.** Each participant *i* samples two fresh random scalars *(dᵢ, eᵢ)*, computes commitments *(Dᵢ, Eᵢ) = (g^{dᵢ}, g^{eᵢ})*, and broadcasts them. The *pair* of nonces is the protocol's first line of defense: as shown in Section 4.4, a single nonce per participant is insufficient to defeat concurrent-session attacks.

**Round 2 — Binding and response.** A coordinator (or the participants jointly) computes, for the signing set *S*, message *m*, and group key *Y*, a per-participant *binding factor*

$$\rho_i = H_1(i, m, B), \quad B = \langle (i, D_i, E_i) \rangle_{i \in S}$$

where *H₁* is a hash modeled as a random oracle and *B* is the ordered list of all commitments. Each participant then forms the group commitment *R = Σᵢ (Dᵢ + ρᵢEᵢ)*, the challenge *c = H₂(R, Y, m)*, and responds with

$$z_i = d_i + e_i \rho_i + \lambda_{i,S} \, s_i \, c$$

The coordinator verifies each share (against the participant's *verification share* *Yᵢ = g^{sᵢ}*, computable from DKG outputs) and aggregates *z = Σᵢ zᵢ*, outputting the Schnorr signature *(R, z)*. Verification is standard single-party Schnorr verification: *gᶻ ≟ R · Yᶜ*.

The critical security property is that ρᵢ binds each participant's second nonce to *everyone else's commitments and the message*. An adversary that tries to mount the Benhamouda-style concurrent attack — choosing its own commitments after seeing honest parties' commitments in a *different* session — finds that the binding factor in each session is unpredictable and session-specific, which the security proof [2] shows is sufficient to reduce forgery to the discrete logarithm problem in the random oracle model. RFC 9591 further standardizes *hedged nonce generation* (`nonce_generate`), mixing a CSPRNG output with the secret key material so that nonce generation degrades gracefully rather than catastrophically if the RNG is weak — but never deterministically, for reasons made precise in Section 4.4 [1].

```python
# FROST share aggregation (Schnorr, additive notation)
def frost_aggregate(shares, lambdas):
    """shares: dict i -> z_i ; lambdas: dict i -> Lagrange coefficient."""
    z = sum(z_i for z_i in shares.values()) % q
    return z  # signature is (R, z); verify g^z == R * Y^c

def lagrange(i, S, q):
    num = den = 1
    for j in S:
        if j == i: continue
        num = num * j % q
        den = den * (j - i) % q
    return num * pow(den, -1, q) % q
```

### 4.2 GG20: Threshold ECDSA with Identifiable Abort

ECDSA signing computes *s = k⁻¹(H(m) + r·x) mod q*, where *k* is the ephemeral nonce and *r* is the x-coordinate of *R = gᵏ*. The modular inverse of a *shared* nonce is the fundamental obstacle: no linear combination of shares yields *k⁻¹* without either reconstructing *k* (fatal) or engaging in interactive MPC. GG20 [3] solves this with a now-classical toolkit:

1. **Paillier-based multiplicative-to-additive (MtA) conversion.** If party *A* holds *a* and party *B* holds *b*, they obtain additive shares *(α, β)* with *α + β = a·b* without revealing *a* or *b*: *A* sends *c_A = Enc_A(a)*; *B* computes *c_B = c_A^b · Enc_A(β')* homomorphically for random *β'*, returns it; *A* decrypts to get *α = a·b − β'*, and *B* sets *β = β'*. zero-knowledge range proofs (now standardly the CGGMP21 proof suite [4][10]) guarantee the ciphertexts are well-formed.
2. **Offline/online phase separation.** All MtA executions — converting multiplicative shares of *(k, γ)* and *(k, w)* into additive shares — occur in an *offline presigning phase* before the message is known. The *online phase* is a single non-interactive round: each party computes its partial signature *sᵢ* from the presignature and broadcasts it.
3. **Identifiable abort.** Every message in GG20 carries commitments and proofs that bind the sender; if verification of any share fails, the protocol's identification sub-protocol pinpoints the deviating party. This converts denial-of-service from a free, anonymous action into an attributable one — the property that makes GG20 deployable in custody settings where participants are distinct legal entities [3][10].

| Property | GG20 | FROST (RFC 9591) | CGGMP21 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Signature scheme | ECDSA (secp256k1) | Schnorr (secp256k1, P-256, Ed25519, …) | ECDSA (secp256k1) |
| Online signing rounds | 1 (non-interactive) | 2 | 1 |
| Total rounds (incl. presign) | 6–8 | 2 | 7 |
| Heavy crypto | Paillier MtA, range proofs | Hashing + EC mults | Paillier MtA, ZK proofs |
| Abort handling | Identifiable abort [3] | Identifiable abort (share verification) [2] | Identifiable abort [10] |
| DKG | Required (Pedersen VSS) | Required (or trusted dealer) | 4-round keygen |
| Security model | Dishonest majority, static | Dishonest majority, ROM | UC, proactive refresh |

### 4.3 Distributed Key Generation via Pedersen VSS

Both protocol families require a key-generation ceremony that outputs Shamir shares of a uniformly random secret *without any party ever learning the secret*. The workhorse is Pedersen's DKG [5][7]: each party *i* samples a random degree-*(t−1)* polynomial *fᵢ(x) = aᵢ₀ + aᵢ₁x + ⋯*, broadcasts Feldman commitments *Cᵢⱼ = g^{aᵢⱼ}*, and sends *fᵢ(j)* privately to each party *j*. Party *j* verifies *g^{fᵢ(j)} = Πₖ Cᵢₖ^{jᵏ}* and complains publicly on failure; disqualified parties are excluded. Each surviving party's share is *sⱼ = Σᵢ fᵢ(j)*, and the group public key is *Y = Πᵢ Cᵢ₀ = g^{Σᵢ aᵢ₀}*.

Two subtleties matter for custody. **First**, the classic Pedersen DKG is secure only against *static* adversaries in the original analysis; adaptive security requires strengthened DKG variants with additional erasure assumptions. **Second**, keygen must be followed by *share verification material*: each participant needs the verification shares *Yᵢ = g^{sᵢ}* of all others, so that during signing, invalid signature shares can be attributed — the mechanism behind both FROST's and GG20's identifiable abort. Production systems additionally implement *proactive share refresh* (periodically re-randomizing shares of the same secret, as in CGGMP21 [10]) so that an adversary must compromise *t* parties *within a single epoch* rather than cumulatively.

```rust
// FROST-style hedged nonce generation (RFC 9591, Section 4.1, simplified)
fn nonce_generate(secret_share: &[u8], rng: &mut impl RngCore) -> (Scalar, Scalar) {
    // NEVER derive (d, e) deterministically from (share, message):
    // deterministic derivation enables full key recovery in the
    // multi-party setting (RFC 9591, Section 1).
    let mut rand_bytes = [0u8; 32];
    rng.fill_bytes(&mut rand_bytes);
    // Hedge: bind randomness to the secret share so a broken RNG
    // degrades to (still secret) deterministic-ish output, never to
    // a repeated or attacker-predictable nonce.
    let d = Scalar::from_hash(&[secret_share, b"d", &rand_bytes].concat());
    let e = Scalar::from_hash(&[secret_share, b"e", &rand_bytes].concat());
    (d, e)
}
```

### 4.4 Nonce-Misuse Resistance and Key-Recovery Attacks

The single most dangerous implementation error in threshold signing is *nonce misuse*: reusing, biasing, or deterministically deriving the ephemeral nonce. In single-party Schnorr/ECDSA, deterministic nonces (RFC 6979) are a *defense* against bad RNGs. In the multi-party setting, they are a catastrophic vulnerability, and RFC 9591 states this explicitly: deriving nonces deterministically "allows for a complete key-recovery attack in multi-party, discrete logarithm-based signatures" [1].

> **Theorem:** *Let Π be a two-round threshold Schnorr protocol in which participant i's response is zᵢ = dᵢ + eᵢρᵢ + λᵢsᵢc with the nonce pair (dᵢ, eᵢ) derived deterministically from (sᵢ, m). Then an adversary corrupting t − 1 participants recovers the honest participants' key shares after observing two signing sessions on distinct messages.*

*Proof sketch.* With deterministic derivation, the adversary — who participates legitimately in both sessions — observes *zᵢ⁽¹⁾ = dᵢ + eᵢρᵢ⁽¹⁾ + λᵢsᵢc⁽¹⁾* and *zᵢ⁽²⁾ = dᵢ + eᵢρᵢ⁽²⁾ + λᵢsᵢc⁽²⁾*. If the binding factors coincide across sessions (e.g., the adversary replays the same commitment set *B*), subtracting eliminates the nonce terms: *zᵢ⁽¹⁾ − zᵢ⁽²⁾ = λᵢsᵢ(c⁽¹⁾ − c⁽²⁾)*, yielding *sᵢ* directly. Even with distinct binding factors, a malicious coordinator that withholds or replays commitments can force collisions. FROST's defense is twofold: (i) nonces are freshly random per session (never derived from the message), and (ii) the binding factor ρᵢ commits to the entire session context — all commitments, the message, the signer set — so cross-session replay of *B* is detectable and useless. ∎

The operational consequences for wallet builders are concrete:

1. **Never reuse (Dᵢ, Eᵢ) across sessions.** FROST implementations must enforce one-time use of nonce pairs, ideally by generating them inside an HSM that refuses to sign twice with the same pair.
2. **Bind the session context.** The binding list *B* must include every participant's commitments *and* the message; omitting the message re-opens the concurrent-session forgery.
3. **In GG20, presignatures are one-time.** Each offline presignature may be consumed by exactly one online signing; reusing a presignature across two messages leaks the ephemeral key *k* and hence the signing key, exactly as in single-party ECDSA nonce reuse.
4. **Haskell-typed state machines help.** Encoding the session lifecycle as a linear type — a nonce pair that can be *consumed* exactly once — turns a runtime discipline into a compile-time guarantee:

```haskell
-- Linear-type sketch: a FROST nonce pair is a single-use resource
data NoncePair = NP { d :: Scalar, e :: Scalar }

signShare :: NoncePair %1 -> BindingCtx -> KeyShare -> Message -> SigShare
signShare (NP d e) ctx ks msg =
  let rho = h1 (ctxCommitments ctx)
      c   = h2 (groupCommitment ctx rho) (groupKey ctx) msg
  in SigShare (d + e * rho + lagrange ks * c)
-- The %1 multiplicity ensures (d, e) cannot be reused: the type
-- checker rejects any code path that signs twice with one pair.
```

### 4.5 Custodial System Architecture

A production multi-party wallet composes these protocols with systems engineering: HSM-backed share storage (shares never leave the enclave), an *untrusted* coordinator that aggregates but learns nothing, authenticated channels between signers, and a policy engine gating which messages may be signed. Because the coordinator holds no secrets, aggregation can run on commodity infrastructure while the quorum stays in hardened enclaves. For Schnorr-native chains, FROST's two-round flow maps directly onto this architecture; for ECDSA chains, GG20's presignature pool runs as a background process, with presignatures guarded as one-time secrets under the same protections as the shares themselves.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

**Security reductions.** FROST is proven unforgeable under the discrete logarithm assumption in the random oracle model, in the dishonest-majority setting with static corruptions, via a reduction that simulates honest participants' responses using the standard Schnorr rewinding argument, with the binding factor ensuring the simulation remains consistent across concurrent sessions [2]. GG20's security reduces to the semantic security of Paillier encryption, the strong-RSA assumption, and the unforgeability of ECDSA, with identifiable abort proven through the commitment-and-proof structure of the MtA sub-protocol [3]. CGGMP21 lifts these guarantees to UC security with proactive refresh [7].

**Performance.** Published measurements agree on the orders of magnitude:

- **FROST** signing: 2 rounds; per-signer work dominated by *O(t)* scalar multiplications — sub-millisecond compute, with network round-trips dominating wall-clock time. DKG is a one-time 2-round Feldman VSS [5].
- **GG20** signing: 1 online round given a presignature, but presigning costs 6+ rounds with *O(n²)* Paillier encryptions and range proofs; end-to-end latency is two to three orders of magnitude above FROST's [3][8].

The engineering conclusion is sharp: *for Schnorr-native chains, there is no performance argument for threshold ECDSA*; GG20's complexity is the price of ECDSA's algebraic structure, paid only where the chain mandates it.

**Correctness argument for aggregation.** FROST aggregation correctness follows from linearity:

$$g^z = g^{\sum_i (d_i + e_i\rho_i + \lambda_i s_i c)} = \Big(\sum_i (D_i + \rho_i E_i)\Big) \cdot Y^c = R \cdot Y^c$$

so *(R, z)* verifies as an ordinary Schnorr signature under the group key *Y* — the property that makes threshold-signed transactions indistinguishable from single-key ones on-chain.

## 6 Limitations and Future Work

1. **Robustness vs. abort.** FROST and GG20 guarantee *identifiable* abort, not *guaranteed output delivery*: *t* honest signers cannot complete a signature if the *t*-th participant is malicious and simply goes silent. Coordinator-driven retry wrappers over different signer subsets recover liveness for FROST, but at the cost of additional rounds and a semi-trusted coordinator. Fully robust dishonest-majority ECDSA remains an open engineering challenge.
2. **Adaptive security.** The standard analyses assume static corruption. Real custodians face adaptive adversaries; deploying adaptively secure DKG variants and proactive refresh (à la CGGMP21 [10]) is recommended but adds measurable complexity.
3. **Post-quantum horizon.** Both Schnorr and ECDSA fall to Shor's algorithm. Threshold lattice-based signatures exist in the literature but lack the maturity and compact on-chain footprint of discrete-log constructions; prudent custodians begin hybrid planning now.
4. **DKLS23 and the three-round frontier.** Newer three-round ECDSA constructions (DKLS23) trade identifiable abort for lower latency in lighter-trust settings [5]; characterizing exactly when the trade is safe for custody remains active work.
5. **Formal verification of implementations.** The gap between proven protocol and deployed code is where real losses occur (cf. Section 4.4). Machine-checked proofs of FROST implementations and verified constant-time field arithmetic are the most impactful open problems for practitioners.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Threshold signatures have moved from cryptographic novelty to custody infrastructure. The analysis in this thesis supports a clear decision rule: deploy **FROST (RFC 9591)** wherever the target chain supports Schnorr — its two-round signing, *O(t)* computation, identifiable abort, and binding-factor defense against concurrent-session attacks make it the strictly dominant choice — and reserve **GG20/CGGMP21 threshold ECDSA** for chains where ECDSA is mandatory, amortizing its heavy presigning phase offline and enforcing one-time presignature discipline with the same rigor applied to nonces. In both cases, dealerless Pedersen DKG with proactive refresh provisions the shares, verification shares enable attributable abort, and nonce hygiene — fresh randomness per session, full context binding, hardware-enforced single use — is the non-negotiable implementation invariant. The mathematics of threshold signing is settled; the remaining work is in building systems that cannot misuse it.
