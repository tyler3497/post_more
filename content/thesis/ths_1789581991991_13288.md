# Algebraic Effects and Handlers: Operational Semantics, Effect Typing Disciplines, and Compilation Strategies

## Abstract

Algebraic effects and handlers, introduced by Plotkin and Pretnar [1][2], generalize exception handlers to arbitrary computational effects presented by equational theories: state, nondeterminism, input/output, concurrency, and backtracking. Where monads fix a semantic domain and derive operations, the algebraic approach fixes a signature of operations and interprets programs in the free model of the associated theory; a handler then denotes a homomorphism from that free algebra, given by a return clause and per-operation clauses with access to the resumption. This thesis unifies three pillars of the field. First, an operational semantics for a core calculus with deep handlers, in which operations bubble through the evaluation context to the nearest handler, which captures the delimited continuation as a resumption. Second, a comparison of effect typing disciplines — monadic encodings, row-polymorphic effect types [5][7], and capability passing [6] — covering inference and effect polymorphism. Third, a survey of compilation strategies, from type-directed selective CPS translation to evidence passing and fiber runtimes, with the soundness proofs and published performance evidence. We close with open problems in scoped operations, linear resumptions, and dependent effect typing.

##1 Introduction

*Monads* gave semanticists a uniform vocabulary for effects and gave Haskell programmers a disciplined sequencing interface. Yet monads compose poorly: combining two monads demands a transformer tower whose order is significant and whose lifting boilerplate grows with tower height. *Algebraic effects* attack from the opposite direction. Fix a set of *operations* with arities, axiomatized by equations, and interpret programs in the *free model* of that theory [1][2]. Effects combine by coproduct of signatures — modular and order-independent — and the monad emerges as the free-model functor.

The missing piece was handling. Plotkin and Pretnar's ESOP 2009 paper [1] and its journal expansion [2] gave handlers algebraic dignity: a handler for a theory is a *model* of that theory, and the handling construct denotes the unique homomorphism out of the free model determined by the handler's clauses. A handler supplies a *return clause* for pure values and an *operation clause* per operation — with access to the *resumption*, the delimited continuation from the operation site up to the handler, invokable zero, one, or many times. One mechanism thus subsumes exceptions, backtracking, coroutines, generators, cooperative concurrency, and rollback [3].

The last decade concentrated on engineering: the precise reduction behavior of handlers, static tracking of effects in the type system, and compilation of what are essentially delimited continuations to efficient code. This thesis addresses all three — a core calculus with small-step semantics and a soundness proof, a taxonomy of typing disciplines, and a survey of compilation techniques centered on Leijen's selective CPS translation [5] and evidence-passing runtimes.

##2 Background

### From monads to algebraic theories

Moggi modeled notions of computation as strong monads T: a program of type A denotes a morphism into T A. Plotkin and Power observed that many such monads are *algebraic* — the free-model monad of an equational theory of operations [2]. Mutable state is presented by `get` and `put` with the global-state equations; nondeterminism by binary `choose` with semilattice equations; exceptions by nullary `raise_e` with no equations. A computation returning A is an element of the free model F A: a tree of operations with return-value leaves, quotiented by the equations.

The algebraic view buys *modularity*: combining effects is the coproduct of theories, computed uniformly. It also yields a *generic-effects* perspective: an operation with parameter P and result A is a morphism P → T A, recovering the monadic view as a special case [2].

### Handlers as homomorphisms

Plotkin and Pretnar [1][2] treat each handler as a model of the theory: interpretations for each operation plus the return case extend uniquely, by the universal property of the free model, to a homomorphism applied by the handling construct. Operationally, handling is *delimited control*. When evaluation reaches `do op v` in context E, and the nearest enclosing handler H has a clause for op, reduction reifies the context between the operation site and H as a first-class continuation k bound in the clause. Handlers whose resumptions are re-wrapped in H are *deep* (folds over the computation tree, true homomorphisms); those that are not are *shallow* (single-step case analysis) [3]. The Eff language of Bauer and Pretnar [3] first demonstrated the breadth of the construct — backtracking, breadth-first search, selection functionals, cooperative multi-threading — and Pretnar's tutorial [4] remains the standard introduction.

##3 Methodology

We fix a fine-grain call-by-value calculus λ_eff separating *values* v from *computations* c:

```
v ::= x | () | (v, v) | fun x -> c
c ::= return v | let x = c1 in c2 | v1 v2
    | do op v
    | handle h with c
h ::= { return x -> cr } ∪ { op x k -> cop | op ∈ Σh }
```

Computation types have the form A ! ε, where ε is an *effect row* of operation labels. The handler rule is the crux: if each clause `op x k -> cop` types with resumption k : A_op → B ! ε', and the return clause yields B ! ε', then `handle h with c : B ! ε'` whenever c : A ! (ε_h ∪ ε') — the handled operations ε_h are discharged and the remainder ε' forwarded.

Reduction uses evaluation contexts E ::= [] | let x = E in c2 | handle h with E:

```
let x = return v in c2          ⟼  c2[v/x]
handle h with (return v)        ⟼  cr[v/x]
handle h with E[do op v]        ⟼  cop[v/x][λy. handle h with E[return y] / k]   (op ∈ dom(h))
handle h with E[do op v]        ⟼  E'[do op v]                                     (op ∉ dom(h), forwarded outward)
```

The third rule is the heart of the semantics: the operation *bubbles up* until it finds a matching clause, and the intervening context is captured as the resumption — re-wrapped in the handler, hence *deep* by construction. Unhandled operations propagate outward unchanged.

> **Theorem 1 (Type Soundness).** If · ⊢ c : A ! ε and c ⟼* c′, then either c′ reduces further, c′ = return v with · ⊢ v : A, or c′ = E[do op v] with op ∈ ε. A closed well-typed computation never gets stuck on an operation outside its declared row.
>
> **Proof.** Progress and preservation. Preservation's delicate case is the operation-clause rule: the resumption λy. handle h with E[return y] has exactly the type A_op → B ! ε' the clause demands, via deep re-wrapping. Progress: a closed, well-typed, non-value computation is a redex or E[do op v]; handler typing guarantees op is handled by the nearest enclosing handler or forwarded, never stuck. ∎

The calculus omits polymorphism, recursion, and operation equations, but exhibits every phenomenon we need: bubbling, capture, deep re-wrapping, and forwarding. All claims about published systems are grounded in [1][2][3][4][5][6][7]; performance discussion reports published measurements.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Operational semantics: bubbling, capture, and resumption disciplines

Three orthogonal choices shape every implementation:

* **Bubbling vs. dynamic binding.** Our semantics propagates operations through the *evaluation context*. An alternative resolves operations by dynamically binding handler frames to names. The two coincide for deep handlers but diverge for shallow handlers and memoized handler lookup. Koka's semantics [5] formalizes the context-based view, with `E[perform op]` searching outward for the innermost handler.
* **Resumption cardinality.** A *one-shot* resumption is invoked at most once; a *multi-shot* resumption zero, one, or many times. Multi-shot resumptions make backtracking and probabilistic programming elegant — a choice handler invokes k twice, once per branch — but demand *copying* the captured stack (or persistent stacks), since resuming twice must not corrupt the first resumption's state. Koka compiles only one-shot resumptions efficiently [5]; Multicore OCaml makes one-shot the default. The trade-off is fundamental: multi-shot recovers full delimited-continuation power, one-shot admits plain stack manipulation.
* **Deep vs. shallow handlers.** Deep handlers re-wrap the resumption and denote folds; shallow handlers denote single-step case analysis. Shallow can simulate deep by explicit recursion, but not vice versa without encoding overhead. Surface languages (Eff [3], Koka [5], OCaml 5) default to deep handlers, matching the homomorphism semantics of [2].

```ocaml
effect Choose : bool

let all_choices (p : unit -> 'a) : 'a list =
  try p () with
  | effect Choose k -> continue k true @ continue k false  (* multi-shot *)
  | x -> [x]                                              (* return clause *)
```

The clause invokes the captured continuation twice — the hallmark of multi-shot use, rejected by one-shot-only runtimes. Note also *forwarding*: if `p` performs I/O, the `Choose` handler lets those operations bubble past it via the fourth reduction rule.

### 4.2 Effect typing disciplines

| Discipline | Effect representation | Inference | Representative systems |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monadic encoding | Type constructor T; transformer stacks | Standard HM; manual lifting | Haskell (mtl) |
| Row-polymorphic effects | Effect rows ⟨l₁,…,lₙ \| μ⟩ | Row unification; principal | Koka [5], Links [7], Eff [3] |
| Capability passing | Handler values as capabilities | Lexical scoping; none needed | Effect Handlers for the Masses [6] |

*Monadic encodings* are battle-tested but non-modular: transformer order is observable and lifting is manual. *Row-polymorphic effect types* dominate the handlers literature. A type A ! ⟨exn, st⟨h⟩ | μ⟩ returns A, may raise exceptions and use heap h, and may have further effects μ. The row tail μ gives effect polymorphism for free: `map : (a -> <e> b) -> list<a> -> <e> list<b>` needs no annotations. Koka's inference [5] extends Hindley–Milner with *row unification* over *scoped labels*: `st⟨h⟩` carries the heap variable, so state on different heaps never accidentally unifies — the basis of type-safe effect encapsulation in the style of `runST`.

The subtle point is *generalization*. Naively generalizing effect rows at `let` is unsound with mutable state — the value-restriction problem in effect clothing. Koka's solution [5] observes that effect rows do not affect runtime representation, so generalization stays sound provided duplicate-elimination and open/closed-row machinery prevents leaking scoped labels — yielding principal inference without a value restriction.

*Capability-passing* designs [6] suit object-oriented languages: effects are interfaces, handlers are implementing objects, and performing an operation is a method call on a passed capability. Lexically scoped handlers need no inference at all; the cost is verbosity and lost effect polymorphism.

```haskell
-- Koka-style row-polymorphic signature (illustrative)
effect state<h>
  fun get() : <state<h>> h
  fun set(x : h) : <state<h>> ()

fun increment() : <state<h> | mu> int
  val x = get(); set(x + 1); x
```

`increment` infers the open row `<state<h> | mu>` and works under any ambient effects; a handler for `state<h>` discharges exactly that label, leaving `mu` to be forwarded — the static counterpart of operational forwarding.

### 4.3 Handlers as delimited control

Every handler desugars into delimited continuations. The clause `op x k -> cop` binds k to the continuation *delimited by the handler*; `do op v` is `shift`-like (capturing up to the nearest handler), the handler a `reset`-like prompt. Three differences make handlers a *programming* construct rather than a raw control operator: (1) **named prompts** — clauses dispatch on operation names, not one anonymous prompt; (2) **folding semantics** — deep handlers re-install themselves automatically; (3) **typed effects** — rows statically rule out unhandled operations.

The correspondence cuts both ways. Implementors learn that any runtime with one-shot delimited continuations (segmented stacks, as in Multicore OCaml) supports one-shot handlers with modest effort. Semanticists learn that CPS-transform correctness results transfer to handlers — exactly Leijen's route in [5].

### 4.4 Compilation strategies

Compiling handlers efficiently means compiling delimited continuations without taxing code that never uses them. Three strategies dominate:

**Selective CPS (Koka).** Leijen [5] gives a *type-directed, selective* CPS translation: only functions with non-total effect rows become CPS; total functions stay direct-style. Guided by inferred effect types, a handler-free program compiles to code identical to a plain functional compiler's output — zero cost for pure code. Handlers compile to dispatchers over CPS computations. The translation is proved type- and semantics-preserving, and the JavaScript backend performs competitively, with handler-heavy code within a small constant factor of hand-written state passing.

**Evidence passing.** Later Koka backends thread an *evidence vector* — the active handler frames — as an extra parameter through effectful functions. Performing an operation becomes a nearest-evidence lookup plus a direct clause call: no CPS transform, direct-style code throughout. The vector is a small stack-allocated structure, and one-shot resumptions reduce to stack capture — the key to Koka's competitive C backend.

**Fibers / segmented stacks (OCaml 5).** Multicore OCaml compiles handlers to *fibers*: heap-allocated stack segments. Performing an effect captures the segment up to the handler's fiber; continuing splices it back. One-shot capture is O(1) pointer manipulation; multi-shot needs segment copying — the semantic cost of §4.1 made concrete. The price is runtime co-design and GC interaction.

| Strategy | Pure-code overhead | Multi-shot | Runtime requirements |
|---|---|---|---|
| Selective CPS [5] | None (direct style) | Via copying | Closures only |
| Evidence passing | Small (extra parameter) | Via copying | Stack capture/copy |
| Fibers / segmented stacks | Small (segment mgmt) | Via segment copy | Custom runtime + GC |

Brachthäuser et al. [6] add a fourth point: restricting handlers to lexically scoped, capability-passing form compiles them to ordinary object dispatch with no control operators at all — handlers as a type-checked design pattern, at the price of multi-shot resumptions and deep folding as primitives.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Proof outline: soundness of the selective CPS translation

The deepest verification result in the area is Leijen's correctness proof for the selective CPS translation [5], in three stages. First, a *direct operational semantics* for the source language with evaluation contexts and handler frames, as in §3. Second, the type-directed translation ⟦·⟧ to a target with explicit continuations, where function types translate to direct-style or CPS arrows based on their effect rows. Third, a *logical relation* between source and target configurations, indexed by types, relating source values to target values and source continuations to target continuations. Selectivity is crucial to tractability: pure code is never CPS-transformed, so the relation needs no continuation case for total types. The full proof in [5] is the gold standard for handler compilation correctness.

A second proof-theoretic line concerns the *equational theory* of handlers. Plotkin and Pretnar [1][2] characterize handlers denotationally as homomorphisms; the Eff metatheory [3] and Pretnar's tutorial [4] establish *adequacy* between denotational and operational semantics: the small-step behavior of `handle h with c` coincides with the homomorphic image of c's denotation. Programmers may thus reason operationally (bubbling and capture) or denotationally (homomorphisms), with a theorem guaranteeing agreement.

### Empirical performance landscape

Published measurements give a consistent picture:

* **Koka (selective CPS).** Leijen [5] reports the selective translation eliminates CPS overhead for pure code entirely, and handler-intensive programs (state, exceptions, generators via handlers) run within a small constant factor of hand-optimized direct-style equivalents. The later evidence-passing backend improved on this, with state-heavy benchmarks competitive with hand-written C — evidence lookup costing roughly a virtual dispatch.
* **Links (row-typed handlers).** Hillerström and Lindley [7] compile handlers via elaboration into the Links runtime; their report emphasizes expressiveness — web-server concurrency via cooperative threads, previously inexpressible — with acceptable rather than record-setting performance.
* **Eff.** Bauer and Pretnar [3] present Eff as an interpreter-based prototype prioritizing semantic clarity; it makes no performance claims, honestly reflecting its role as a research vehicle.
* **Multicore OCaml (fibers).** One-shot capture is O(1) pointer manipulation; the cost is the segmented-stack machinery, which OCaml 5 pays for concurrency regardless.

The qualitative takeaway: *one-shot* handlers compile on par with existing control constructs, while *multi-shot* handlers are expensive everywhere, because continuation copying is inherent. Language designers respond by defaulting to one-shot (Koka, OCaml 5) with multi-shot opt-in.

##6 Limitations

**Scoped operations.** Our development treats operations as strictly algebraic: `do op` commutes with continuation binding per the equations. Real effects are often *scoped* — once the scope ends, the continuation must not escape (exception handlers themselves, `catch`-like constructs). Scoped operations break simple bubbling, and their metatheory (inference, compilation) is far less settled than what we surveyed.

**Linearity.** Multi-shot resumptions duplicate the continuation and everything it closes over — including linear resources like file handles. Invoking k twice may *use a linear resource twice*. Linear/affine handler disciplines restricting resumptions when linear resources are in scope are active research (cf. qualified effect types), but no mainstream language enforces them.

**Runtime integration.** JavaScript's lack of stack capture forces CPS or trampolining; the JVM's continuations do not directly expose the named-prompt structure handlers need; the fiber approach demands runtime co-design, as OCaml 5 undertook.

**Handler composition.** Effects compose modularly at the signature level, but *handlers* do not: nesting order is observable (state inside vs. outside a nondeterminism handler gives local vs. global state), and no general theory predicts when two handlers commute. The type system offers no help.

**Dependent and quantitative types.** Tracking value-dependent effects ("performs exactly n operations") through handler clauses with resumptions is supported by no mature inference algorithm; marrying handlers with dependent or quantitative types remains frontier work.

---

##7 Conclusion

Algebraic effects and handlers have traveled, in under two decades, from a semanticist's generalization of exception handlers [1][2] to a practical feature with principled operational semantics, mature effect type systems, and competitive compilation [5][6][7]. The core is stable: operations by signature, computations as free-model trees, handlers as homomorphisms realized by bubbling and delimited-continuation capture. Around it grew an engineering discipline — row-polymorphic typing with scoped labels for principal inference, selective CPS and evidence passing for zero-cost pure code, fiber runtimes for one-shot handlers.

For language designers, the guidance is concrete. *Default to one-shot resumptions and deep handlers*: they cover state, exceptions, generators, async/await, and concurrency, and compile efficiently everywhere. *Track effects with rows* when modular combination matters; use *capability passing* when embedding in OO type systems without HM inference. And *prove compilation correct* against a direct operational semantics — the logical-relations technique of [5] is reusable.

The open problems — scoped operations, resumption linearity, handler commutativity, dependent effect typing — are the expected problems of a construct that unifies control and effects. Handlers succeeded by making control flow programmable; the next decade is about making it *disciplined*.
