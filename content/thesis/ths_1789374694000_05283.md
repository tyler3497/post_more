# Compositional Design of Genetic Circuits: Toggle Switches, Repressilators, Retroactivity, and the Engineering of Predictable Cellular Computation

## Abstract

The engineering of living cells as programmable substrates demands the same compositionality that underwrites modern electronics: devices whose behavior is preserved when wired together. This article develops the mathematical and experimental foundations of compositional genetic-circuit design, beginning with the two canonical circuits that inaugurated synthetic biology — the Gardner–Collins toggle switch and the Elowitz–Leibler repressilator [1][2]. We derive their ordinary-differential-equation models from mass-action kinetics, show how Hill-function nonlinearities generate bistability and oscillation through saddle-node and Hopf bifurcations, and then confront the central obstacle to composition: interconnection changes behavior. Following Del Vecchio, Ninfa, and Sontag [3], we formalize retroactivity as the biological analogue of impedance loading and analyze insulation devices that attenuate it. We treat resource competition through the isocost-line framework [4] and burden-driven feedback control [5], survey the library-based route to scale-up via mined orthogonal repressors [6], CRISPR interference gates [7], and the Cello design-automation pipeline [8], and contrast in vivo assembly with DNA strand-displacement computation [9]. The result is a coherent account of why cellular computation is predictable only when modules, interfaces, and shared resources are modeled together.

## 1 Intro

Engineering disciplines progress by *composition*: a resistor behaves the same whether it sits alone on a bench or inside a radio. Biology has historically resisted this discipline. A gene placed downstream of a promoter does not merely *receive* a signal; it sequesters the transcription factors that produce it, consumes the ribosomes that translate it, and slows the growth of the host that carries it. Every wire in a genetic circuit is also a load, and every load feeds back.

Two experiments, published back-to-back in *Nature* in January 2000, demonstrated that rational circuit design in living cells is possible. Gardner, Cantor, and Collins built a **toggle switch** — two mutually repressing promoters forming a bistable memory element flipped by transient induction [1]. Elowitz and Leibler built the **repressilator** — three repressors in a ring producing sustained, if noisy, oscillations [2]. These circuits are the transistor and the oscillator of synthetic biology: proof that multistability and periodic dynamics can be implemented in *E. coli* from non-specialized parts.

The quarter century since has converted these demonstrations into a design discipline — and exposed exactly where naive compositionality breaks. This article argues that predictable cellular computation rests on three pillars: **device models** relating part characteristics to circuit behavior, **interface theory** accounting for how interconnection perturbs modules, and **resource theory** budgeting the shared pools of polymerases, ribosomes, and energy every circuit draws. Sections 2 and 3 establish the modeling language; Section 4 develops the core results; Section 5 confronts theory with experiment; Section 6 catalogues the limitations; Section 7 sketches the road ahead.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The central dogma as a signal chain

Gene expression is a signal chain: a promoter recruits RNA polymerase, polymerase synthesizes messenger RNA, ribosomes translate mRNA into protein, and proteins eventually degrade or dilute by cell division. A transcription factor modulates this chain by binding operator sites near a promoter, either recruiting polymerase (activation) or sterically blocking it (repression).

### 2.2 From mass action to Hill functions

The quantitative link between input and output begins with elementary binding chemistry. If a repressor $R$ binds a promoter $P$ as an $n$-mer with dissociation constant $K$, and binding equilibrates rapidly, the normalized transcription rate is the **Hill function**

$$f(R) = \frac{1}{1 + (R/K)^n}$$

with Hill coefficient $n$ measuring cooperativity [1]. Activation is described by the complementary form, possibly with basal leak. The crucial property is *ultrasensitivity*: for $n > 1$ the response is sigmoidal, switching sharply over a narrow input range — the molecular substrate of digital behavior, bistability, and oscillation alike.

### 2.3 Deterministic and stochastic descriptions

When molecule counts are large, mass action yields ordinary differential equations for concentrations. For a protein $x$ produced at rate $p(x_{\mathrm{in}})$ and degraded or diluted at rate $\gamma x$,

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = p(x_{\mathrm{in}}) - \gamma x$$

with $p$ typically a Hill function of the upstream regulator. At low copy number the same chemistry is a continuous-time Markov chain, and the repressilator's noisiness is a signature of this regime [2]. We work primarily in the deterministic limit, where bifurcation theory applies cleanly, and treat noise as a limitation in Section 6.

### 2.4 Timescale separation and quasi-steady states

Transcription-factor binding equilibrates in seconds, transcription and translation proceed over minutes, and protein dilution tracks the cell-doubling time of tens of minutes. This separation of timescales justifies the **quasi-steady-state approximation**: fast binding reactions are replaced by their equilibrium relations (the Hill functions), leaving slow ODEs for protein concentrations — a singular-perturbation logic that reappears in Section 4.3, where it quantifies retroactivity.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Model construction

Our models follow a uniform recipe. *First*, enumerate the slow species (mRNAs and proteins, or proteins alone when mRNA dynamics are fast). *Second*, write production terms as Hill functions of regulator concentrations, with parameters for maximal rate $\alpha$, half-maximal concentration $K$, cooperativity $n$, and basal leak $\alpha_0$. *Third*, include linear degradation and dilution. *Fourth*, nondimensionalize, collapsing the parameter space to dimensionless groups such as $\alpha / (\gamma K)$ that control qualitative behavior.

### 3.2 Bifurcation analysis

Circuit function is read off from **nullclines** — curves where one time derivative vanishes — and their intersections, the steady states. Stability follows from linearization: negative real parts of the Jacobian's eigenvalues mark attractors. As a parameter varies, steady states are created and destroyed in **bifurcations**: saddle-node bifurcations generate bistability, Hopf bifurcations generate oscillation. Design is thus an inverse bifurcation problem: choose parameters so the desired bifurcation structure exists and is robust to variation.

### 3.3 The design–build–test–learn cycle

Models guide construction, but construction exposes model error. The field therefore operates a **design–build–test–learn** (DBTL) cycle, compressed by two technologies:

1. **Cell-free transcription–translation** (TX-TL) systems execute genetic circuits in extract or reconstituted mixtures, enabling same-day prototyping without cloning [9].
2. **Design automation** tools such as Cello compile high-level logic specifications into DNA by searching libraries of characterized gates [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The toggle switch: bistability by mutual repression

The toggle comprises two repressors, $u$ and $v$, each repressing the other's promoter. With Hill kinetics and linear degradation, Gardner's dimensionless model is [1]:

$$\frac{du}{dt} = \frac{\alpha_1}{1 + v^{\beta}} - u, \qquad \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{\alpha_2}{1 + u^{\gamma}} - v$$

The $u$-nullcline $u = \alpha_1/(1+v^{\beta})$ is a decreasing sigmoid; the $v$-nullcline is its mirror image. For cooperativities near unity the nullclines cross once, but as $\beta$ and $\gamma$ grow, the sigmoids steepen and three intersections appear: two stable nodes flanking an unstable saddle.

> **Theorem (Bistability of the mutual-repression motif).** For the symmetric toggle ($\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \alpha$, $\beta = \gamma$), bistability obtains when $\beta > 1$ and $\alpha$ exceeds a critical value $\alpha_c(\beta)$ that decreases with $\beta$. The symmetric steady state loses stability in a pitchfork bifurcation, and the asymmetric branches are created in saddle-node bifurcations.

The nullcline intersections are readily located numerically:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import fsolve

alpha, beta = 12.0, 2.0

def nullcline_u(v):
    return alpha / (1.0 + v ** beta)

def nullcline_v(u):
    return alpha / (1.0 + u ** beta)

def equations(state):
    u, v = state
    return [u - nullcline_u(v), v - nullcline_v(u)]

roots = set()
for guess in [(a, b) for a in (0.1, 3.0, 11.0) for b in (0.1, 3.0, 11.0)]:
    u, v = fsolve(equations, guess, full_output=False)
    roots.add((round(u, 3), round(v, 3)))
print(sorted(roots))
```

Two consequences matter for design. *First*, cooperativity is non-negotiable: linear repression admits only monostability. Multimerization of repressors, or tandem operator sites, supplies the nonlinearity. *Second*, bistability requires **balance**: if $\alpha_1$ greatly exceeds $\alpha_2$, one stable state dominates and the device cannot be flipped. Gardner balanced the arms with matched promoter–repressor pairs (Plac/lacI induced by IPTG, and the temperature-sensitive $\lambda$ $P_L$/cI pair), then flipped the switch with transient pulses that tilt the nullclines past the saddle-node boundary [1] — a one-bit cellular memory with a nearly ideal switching threshold.

### 4.2 The repressilator: oscillation in a ring of repressors

Replace mutual repression with cyclic repression among three genes — lacI represses tetR, tetR represses cI, cI represses lacI — and add a GFP reporter on one promoter. Elowitz and Leibler's model tracks mRNAs $m_i$ and proteins $p_i$ for $i$ in the cyclic set $\{1,2,3\}$ [2]:

$$\frac{dm_i}{dt} = -m_i + \frac{\alpha}{1 + p_j^n} + \alpha_0, \qquad \frac{dp_i}{dt} = -\beta\,(p_i - m_i)$$

where $j$ is the predecessor of $i$ in the ring and $\beta$ is the ratio of protein to mRNA decay rates. The symmetric steady state always exists; linearizing shows oscillations emerging through a Hopf bifurcation when the three repression gains are large enough and the delay — encoded in the mRNA–protein cascade and $\beta$ — is sufficient.

> **Theorem:** Oscillation in odd repressor rings. A ring of $N$ identical repressors with monotone decreasing Hill kinetics has a unique symmetric steady state. For odd $N \geq 3$, it destabilizes via Hopf bifurcation at large $\alpha$ and cooperativity $n > 1$, yielding a stable limit cycle; for even $N$, it gives way to multiple stable asymmetric states. Delay in the transcription–translation cascade is necessary: without it, negative feedback stabilizes rather than oscillates.

A minimal Rust integration confirms the basin structure:

```rust
fn step(u: f64, v: f64, alpha: f64, beta: f64, dt: f64) -> (f64, f64) {
    let du = alpha / (1.0 + v.powf(beta)) - u;
    let dv = alpha / (1.0 + u.powf(beta)) - v;
    (u + dt * du, v + dt * dv)
}

fn main() {
    let (alpha, beta, dt) = (12.0, 2.0, 0.01);
    for (u0, v0) in [(11.0, 0.5), (0.5, 11.0), (3.0, 3.0)] {
        let (mut u, mut v) = (u0, v0);
        for _ in 0..20000 {
            let (un, vn) = step(u, v, alpha, beta, dt);
            u = un;
            v = vn;
        }
        println!("start=({u0:.1},{v0:.1}) -> end=({u:.3},{v:.3})");
    }
}
```

Three design lessons follow. *First*, **oddness is topological**: an even ring is a cascade of toggles, not an oscillator. *Second*, the loop needs *gain with delay* — steep repression plus slow protein turnover. *Third*, the period is set by the slowest timescale (protein degradation, on the order of the cell cycle), so the original circuit oscillated with periods of hours, slower than cell division, with state inherited across generations [2]. Later work confirmed the parity prediction: five-gene rings oscillate with longer periods while four-gene rings settle to steady states.

### 4.3 Retroactivity and insulation: the impedance problem of biology

The toggle and the repressilator are characterized in isolation, yet real circuits are *interconnected*: the repressilator's output drives a reporter, a toggle's state feeds a downstream gate. Del Vecchio, Ninfa, and Sontag showed that biomolecular modules have no idealization akin to zero output impedance, and they gave the perturbation a name: **retroactivity** [3].

Consider an upstream module producing transcription factor $X$ at concentration $x(t)$, with isolated dynamics $\dot{x} = f(x)$. Connecting $N$ downstream promoter sites sequesters $X$ into bound complexes $C$, so total protein $x_t = x + C$ obeys $\dot{x}_t = f(x)$ while free $x$ follows

$$\dot{x} = f(x) - \dot{C}.$$

The extra term is the **retroactivity to the output**: flux into downstream complexes drains the upstream signal. Singular perturbation theory — binding is fast, protein dynamics slow — yields a retroactivity measure interpretable as the sensitivity of the slow manifold to downstream load. Retroactivity is large when the transcription factor is scarce relative to its binding sites or when affinities are high: precisely when signals are weak and specific, the regime circuits inhabit.

> **Theorem:** Insulation by high-gain feedback. For an upstream module modified to $\dot{x} = Gz - Kx + s$, where $z$ is the desired input, $G$ a large production gain, $K$ a large degradation gain, and $s$ the retroactivity flux, the steady-state output is $x = (Gz + s)/(G + K)$; as $G$ grows with fixed ratio $G/K$, dependence on $s$ vanishes and the output tracks $z/K$ regardless of downstream load.

This is the biomolecular op-amp: large input gain plus large negative feedback attenuates retroactivity to the output, while the device presents low retroactivity to its own input. Two biological realizations were proposed [3]: a strong non-leaky promoter (gain) with targeted proteolysis (feedback), and a phosphorylation–dephosphorylation cycle, whose fast enzymatics furnish an intrinsic insulator — validated by a phosphorylation-based **load driver** preserving upstream dynamics under load. Compositionality is not a property of parts but of *interfaces*: it must be engineered, device by device.

### 4.4 Resource competition, burden, and isocost lines

Retroactivity captures *specific* coupling through binding; a second, cruder coupling acts through *shared resources*. Every heterologous gene consumes RNA polymerase, ribosomes, tRNAs, and energy. György and Del Vecchio quantified this economy with two fluorescent reporters on one plasmid and no regulatory link between them [4]. The attainable protein pairs fall on a straight line — an **isocost line** borrowed from microeconomics. Inducing one reporter slides expression along the line, depressing the other by up to sixty percent; the line rotates with ribosome-binding-site strength and shifts with plasmid copy number, exactly as a resource-allocation model predicts [4].

Cells *feel* this coupling as **burden**, a growth-rate penalty that selects for mutants and destabilizes circuits over evolutionary time. Ceroni and colleagues identified native *E. coli* promoters that activate under heterologous-expression stress and used them to drive a dCas9-based controller that throttles the synthetic construct when burden rises, restoring growth while maintaining function [5]. Complementary **capacity monitors** — constitutive reporters whose fluorescence drop reports residual expression capacity — turn burden into a measurable design variable. The lesson is quantitative: a gate library characterized at one expression level does not transfer to another, because the resource budget is a global variable every module perturbs.

### 4.5 From repressors to RNA-guided gates: CRISPRi logic and design automation

Scaling beyond hand-built circuits requires parts that are *orthogonal* (no crosstalk), *composable* (uniform signal carriers), and *numerous*. Stanton and colleagues mined 73 TetR homologs from prokaryotic genomes, characterized 11 as orthogonal repressors, and assembled them into NOT and NOR gates, demonstrating layered logic in single cells [6]. Orthogonality, however, is bounded by the repertoire of natural transcription factors — which motivated a pivot to **RNA-guided regulation**, where specificity is encoded in a short guide sequence rather than a protein fold.

Gander and colleagues built digital NOR gates in *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* from catalytically dead Cas9 fused to the Mxi1 repressor domain [7]. Each gate converts guide-RNA inputs into a guide-RNA output, so gates wire together through a uniform molecular signal. Because dCas9–Mxi1 recruitment remodels chromatin, leak is minimal and responses are digital; cascades of up to seven layers functioned with little signal degradation, the largest eukaryotic circuits of their time [7].

The culmination of the library-based program is **Cello**, a pipeline that compiles Verilog specifications into DNA [8]. Gates are characterized by Hill-fitted response functions in relative promoter units (RPU); simulated annealing assigns gates to logic positions to maximize noise margins. Of 60 circuits designed, 45 functioned correctly on the first attempt — a hit rate unthinkable in the hand-tuning era. Cello's abstraction is powerful but leaky: response functions are measured in specific contexts, and retroactivity and resource competition — Sections 4.3 and 4.4 — are precisely the context effects no static gate characterization can fully capture.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

Theory earns its keep by surviving contact with cells. The record is mixed in instructive ways.**Bistability and memory.** The toggle behaved as designed: transient IPTG or thermal pulses flipped the population between states with a sharp switching threshold, confirming that Hill-type ultrasensitivity plus balanced synthesis rates suffices for a cellular memory element [1].

**Oscillation.** The repressilator oscillated in single cells with periods of roughly 150 minutes, but noisily: only about 40 percent of cells showed sustained oscillations [2]. The noise is not experimental sloppiness but intrinsic stochasticity at low molecule number — a limitation that motivated quorum-sensing-coupled designs to synchronize populations.

**Retroactivity and insulation.** The load-driver device — a phosphorylation cycle inserted between an upstream oscillator and downstream promoters — restored the upstream dynamics that downstream load had distorted, confirming the insulation theorem's central prediction in living cells [3]: the closest biology has come to a modular interface standard.

**Resource coupling.** The isocost experiments showed that inducing an unrelated reporter depresses a constitutive reporter by the amount a shared-resource model predicts, and the effect disappears when the competing gene is removed [4]. Burden-driven feedback maintained host growth rate under sudden overexpression where uncontrolled constructs collapsed it [5].

**Scale-up.** Cello's 45-of-60 first-pass success rate [8], seven-layer CRISPRi cascades with minimal degradation [7], and orthogonal repressor libraries enabling multi-gate programs [6] demonstrate that compositional design works when interfaces are respected. Outside the cell, Qian and Winfree's DNA strand-displacement cascades — with thresholding and catalytic amplification implementing signal restoration at every gate — scaled to a 130-strand four-bit square-root circuit with linear delay scaling [9].

The pattern across these results is consistent: *models predict qualitative structure* (bistability, oscillation, attenuation, linear coupling) reliably, while *quantitative prediction* (periods, thresholds, margins) requires iterative calibration.

## 6 Limitations

**Stochasticity.** Deterministic ODEs describe population means, but single cells at low copy number are dominated by bursty transcription and partitioning noise. The repressilator's irregularity is intrinsic [2]; digital abstraction leaks wherever molecule counts are small. Stochastic simulation and moment-closure extend the theory at the cost of analytic tractability.

**Evolutionary instability.** A circuit that burdens its host is a selection pressure for its own destruction: inactivating mutations sweep populations on timescales of days. Burden feedback [5] and genomic integration mitigate but do not eliminate this; no design method guarantees evolutionary half-lives commensurate with industrial deployment.

**Context dependence.** Promoter behavior depends on supercoiling, neighboring sequences, growth rate, and host strain. Cello's response functions are measured in one context and applied in others [8]; retroactivity [3] and resource competition [4] formalize two context effects, but ribosome queueing, transcriptional interference, and metabolic state remain partially modeled.

**Measurement and parameter identifiability.** Fitting Hill parameters from fluorescence data confounds promoter strength, plasmid copy number, and reporter maturation. Many published parameter sets are sloppy: predictions are robust along some directions and fragile along others, and the fragile directions are rarely reported.

**Growth-rate coupling.** Dilution sets the slowest timescale, so circuit dynamics and host physiology are bidirectionally coupled. Models treating growth rate as fixed miss feedbacks in which burden slows growth, which slows dilution, which raises protein concentrations — a positive loop that can flip qualitative behavior.

**Insulation costs.** Insulators consume the very resources they protect: phosphorylation cycles burn ATP, high-gain promoters consume polymerase. Compositionality bought with insulation is paid for in burden, and the optimal tradeoff remains an open design question.

## 7 Conclusion

The arc from the toggle switch and the repressilator to Cello-compiled CRISPRi logic is the arc of a field learning what *engineering* means in a substrate not designed to be engineered. The founding circuits proved that computation — memory and oscillation — can be implemented in cells from characterized parts [1][2]. The decades since supplied the theory of composition: retroactivity names the price of interconnection, insulation devices pay it, isocost lines budget shared resources, and burden controllers keep the host viable [3][4][5].

Three frontiers stand out. *First*, **closed-loop design**: coupling models, automated assembly, and high-throughput measurement into DBTL cycles that run without human intervention, so context dependence is learned rather than assumed away. *Second*, **evolutionary robustness as a design objective**: circuits whose function is a stable fixed point of the mutation–selection dynamics, not merely of the ODEs. *Third*, **multi-scale composition**: extending the module–interface–resource framework to signaling, metabolism, and multicellular consortia, where new retroactivities — metabolic, mechanical, ecological — await formalization.

The deepest lesson is methodological: biology will never offer the clean interfaces of silicon, so predictable computation is achieved by *modeling the impurity* — quantifying how interconnection perturbs modules and designing interfaces that absorb the perturbation. Compositionality in living matter is not found. It is built.
