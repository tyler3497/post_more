# Beyond Message Passing: The Weisfeiler-Leman Hierarchy, Graph Isomorphism Networks, and the Expressive Limits of Graph Neural Networks

## Abstract

Message passing neural networks (MPNNs) have become the dominant paradigm for representation learning on graphs, yet their expressive power remained poorly understood until a series of landmark results connected them to the classical Weisfeiler–Leman (WL) graph isomorphism test [1]. This thesis develops a unified account of the expressive hierarchy of graph neural networks, beginning with color refinement (the 1-WL test) and proving that no message passing architecture can distinguish graphs that 1-WL cannot distinguish. We then present the Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN) of Xu et al. [2], whose injective multiset aggregation provably attains the maximal expressivity available to the message passing class. Climbing further, we formalize the *k*-WL hierarchy and show that *k*-order invariant graph networks reach *k*-WL expressivity at *O(nᵏ)* cost, while the folklore 2-WL variant yields practical architectures with 3-WL power [3]. We connect the WL hierarchy to counting logic, homomorphism counts, and universality theorems for invariant and equivariant networks [4], and close with practical tradeoffs: the tension between expressivity, generalization, and computational tractability that governs real-world architecture design.

## 1 Introduction

Graph-structured data — molecules, social networks, knowledge graphs, program syntax trees — demands learning architectures that respect **permutation invariance**: relabeling the vertices of a graph must not change the model's output. The message passing paradigm, formalized by Gilmer et al. and refined through Graph Convolutional Networks, GraphSAGE, and Graph Attention Networks, encodes this invariance by construction: each node iteratively aggregates transformed features of its neighbors. But this convenience conceals a fundamental question: *which pairs of graphs can a message passing network distinguish, and which are invisible to it?*

The answer, discovered in parallel by Xu et al. [2] and Morris et al. [3] in 2019, is striking in its precision: **the discriminative power of any message passing GNN is upper-bounded by the 1-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman test**, a 1968 heuristic for the graph isomorphism problem [1]. This bound is not merely approximate — under precise injectivity conditions it is *tight*, and the architecture that attains it, the Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN), is among the simplest conceivable. The result reframes GNN design from an empirical dark art into a problem of approximation theory: *a GNN is a neural re-implementation of a combinatorial algorithm, and its ceiling is the algorithm's ceiling.*

Yet 1-WL is a low ceiling. It cannot count triangles in general, cannot distinguish regular graphs of the same degree, and fails on strongly regular graphs that arise in molecular and social domains. This thesis therefore climbs the hierarchy: the *k*-WL family of higher-order tests, indexed by the tuple size *k*, is **strictly more powerful with increasing *k***, and each level admits neural counterparts. We trace this correspondence through three intellectual threads:

1. **Combinatorial**: color refinement, its higher-order refinements, and the Cai–Fürer–Immerman lower bounds showing that no fixed *k* suffices for all graphs.
2. **Algebraic**: invariant and equivariant linear layers, multisymmetric polynomials, and universality results that make sufficiently powerful networks dense in the space of continuous invariant functions [4].
3. **Statistical/practical**: why practitioners routinely choose *less* expressive models — the bias–variance tradeoff, overfitting of high-order features, and the computational cliff of *O(nᵏ)* complexity.

> **Thesis claim.** The WL hierarchy is not merely a diagnostic instrument but a design language: every GNN architecture implicitly commits to a level of a graded expressivity lattice, and responsible architecture choice requires matching that level to the structural demands of the data.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Graphs, isomorphism, and permutation invariance

A graph *G = (V, E)* consists of a vertex set *V* with *n = |V|* elements and an edge set *E ⊆ V × V*. Two graphs *G₁, G₂* are **isomorphic**, written *G₁ ≅ G₂*, if there exists a bijection *π: V₁ → V₂* preserving adjacency. The **graph isomorphism problem** — deciding whether *G₁ ≅ G₂* — occupies a singular position in complexity theory: neither known to be in P nor NP-complete, with Babai's quasipolynomial-time algorithm [5] representing the current frontier. Any graph-level neural network *f* must satisfy *f(G₁) = f(G₂)* whenever *G₁ ≅ G₂*, i.e., it must be permutation-invariant. Node-level networks must be **equivariant**: permuting the input permutes the output correspondingly.

### 2.2 Message passing neural networks

A message passing neural network computes node representations through *K* layers of neighborhood aggregation:

```python
# Canonical message passing layer (Gilmer et al.)
def mpnn_layer(h, adj, k):
    messages = aggregate([mlp_msg(h[u]) for u in neighbors(v, adj)])
    h_v = mlp_update(concat(h[v], messages))
    return h_v
```

Formally, at layer *k*:

$$h_v^{(k)} = \phi^{(k)}\!\left(h_v^{(k-1)},\, f^{(k)}\!\left(\left\\{h_u^{(k-1)} : u \in \mathcal{N}(v)\right\\}\right)\right)$$

where *f⁽ᵏ⁾* operates on **multisets** (order is irrelevant) and *φ⁽ᵏ⁾* combines the node's own representation with the aggregated neighborhood. Popular variants differ only in their choices of *f*: mean pooling (GCN), max pooling (GraphSAGE), or attention-weighted sums (GAT).

### 2.3 The 1-WL test: color refinement

The 1-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman test, also called **color refinement**, iteratively recolors vertices [1]:

```python
def wl_1_color_refinement(G, max_iter):
    color = {v: hash(degree(v)) for v in G.nodes}   # initial coloring
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        new_color = {}
        for v in G.nodes:
            # multiset of neighbor colors, hashed with own color
            signature = (color[v], sorted(color[u] for u in G.neighbors(v)))
            new_color[v] = hash(signature)
        if multiset(new_color.values()) == multiset(color.values()):
            break
        color = new_color
    return multiset(color.values())   # graph invariant
```

Two graphs are declared non-isomorphic if their final color multisets differ. Color refinement runs in *O(n + m)* per iteration, converges in at most *n* rounds, and succeeds on almost all graphs — yet fails systematically on **regular graphs** of equal degree (every vertex receives identical colors forever) and on strongly regular families like the Cai–Fürer–Immerman graphs [6], which require dimension growing with *n*.

> **Theorem (upper bound).** *Any MPNN of the form above cannot distinguish graphs that the 1-WL test cannot distinguish [2, 3].*
>
> *Proof sketch.* Induct on layers: if two nodes receive identical 1-WL colors at iteration *k*, their *k*-hop unfolded subtree patterns are identical, and every MPNN layer computes a function of exactly those multisets. The readout, being a function of the final multiset of node embeddings, likewise cannot separate 1-WL-indistinguishable color multisets. ∎

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds by **expressivity correspondence**: we pair each neural architecture with the combinatorial algorithm it simulates, then exploit known separations between algorithms to derive separations between architectures. The methodology has four components.

### 3.1 Multiset function characterization

The core technical device is the characterization of functions on multisets. A function *f* on multisets is **injective** if distinct multisets map to distinct outputs. The key lemma, due to Xu et al. [2]:

> **Lemma.** *For countable feature spaces, there exist functions φ and ρ such that the sum-decomposition f(X) = ρ(Σ_{x ∈ X} φ(x)) is injective on multisets X of bounded size.*
>
> With continuous features, sum-decomposition with MLPs is *not* universally injective — but it can approximate any continuous multiset function, and with injective φ it separates multisets exactly.

This yields the GIN update rule:

```python
def gin_layer(h, adj, mlp, eps):
    # eps: learnable or fixed scalar; eps=0 gives sum over closed neighborhood
    agg = h[v] * (1 + eps) + sum(h[u] for u in neighbors(v, adj))
    return mlp(agg)
```

The MLP after summation is what makes GIN special: a single linear layer followed by ReLU cannot represent arbitrary injective multiset maps, but a sufficiently wide MLP can — this is the precise sense in which GIN is "as powerful as 1-WL" [2].

### 3.2 The *k*-WL hierarchy

For *k ≥ 2*, the *k*-WL test colors *k*-tuples of vertices rather than single vertices [7]:

| Level | Object colored | Complexity | Separates |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-WL | vertices | *O(n + m)* | trees, almost all graphs |
| 2-WL (folklore) | ordered pairs | *O(n³)* | many regular graphs, some CFI pairs |
| 3-WL | triples | *O(n⁴)* | strictly more than 2-WL |
| *k*-WL | *k*-tuples | *O(n^{k+1})* | hierarchy is strict: *k*-WL ⊊ (*k*+1)-WL |

A crucial subtlety: the **folklore** 2-WL (2-FWL) is equivalent to 3-WL in power, while the "oblivious" 2-WL equals 1-WL. This is why Morris et al.'s practical architecture [3] — a 2-order network with matrix multiplication — achieves **3-WL expressivity** with only *O(n²)* memory.

### 3.3 Logical characterization

The *k*-WL test is equivalent in distinguishing power to the **counting logic** *C^{k+1}*: first-order logic with counting quantifiers (∃^{≥m}x) restricted to *k+1* variables [7]. This yields a clean yardstick: *a GNN can express exactly those graph properties definable in the corresponding fragment of counting logic.* For instance, "the graph contains a triangle" is expressible in *C³* and hence detectable by 2-FWL-powerful networks, but **not** by any 1-WL-bounded MPNN on unattributed graphs — a fact with direct consequences for molecular property prediction, where rings and cycles carry the signal.

### 3.4 Homomorphism counting perspective

Recent work reframes expressivity through **homomorphism counts** [8]: the vector *(hom(F, G))_{F ∈ ℱ}* of homomorphism counts from a pattern family ℱ determines which graphs a model distinguishes. The 1-WL test corresponds to homomorphism counts of *trees*; *k*-WL corresponds to graphs of treewidth at most *k*. This perspective is constructive: architectures that explicitly inject homomorphism counts of small patterns (e.g., cycles up to length 6) provably exceed 1-WL on the structures that matter for chemistry, while remaining tractable.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 GIN dissected: why injectivity is everything

Consider the aggregation landscape. Mean pooling collapses *{a, a}* and *{a}* to the same value; max pooling collapses *{a, b}* and *{a, a, b}*. Only **sum** preserves multiset cardinality, and only an injective map after the sum preserves identity. Xu et al. prove two complementary facts [2]:

1. **Necessity.** If either the aggregation *f* or the combine step *φ* fails to be injective, there exist non-isomorphic graphs (distinguishable by 1-WL) that the network conflates. Concretely, a GCN with mean aggregation cannot distinguish a 6-cycle *C₆* from two disjoint triangles *2C₃* — both are 2-regular, so every node sees the identical neighborhood multiset forever.
2. **Sufficiency.** With injective *f, φ* and injective readout, the network's node embeddings are in bijection with 1-WL colors at every layer, so the network distinguishes *exactly* the graphs 1-WL distinguishes.

The practical lesson is quantitative, not just qualitative. On the TU datasets (MUTAG, PTC, PROTEINS), GIN with sum aggregation and a 2-layer MLP update consistently outperforms GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT variants precisely on datasets where structural discrimination matters [2]. The *ε* parameter — weighting the node's own feature against its neighbors — is learnable; setting *ε = 0* recovers a particularly clean form whose theory is tightest.

```haskell
-- GIN update as a pure function on multisets (pseudocode)
ginUpdate :: MLP -> Double -> Multiset Vec -> Vec
ginUpdate mlp eps neighborhood =
    mlp ((1 + eps) `scale` selfFeature + foldr (+) zero neighborhood)
-- injectivity of (mlp . sum) is the entire content of the GIN theorem
```

### 4.2 Climbing the hierarchy: *k*-GNNs and invariant graph networks

To surpass 1-WL, architectures must break the single-node message passing mold. Two families dominate:

**Higher-order message passing (*k*-GNNs).** Morris et al. [3] generalize message passing to *k*-sets of vertices: nodes of the *k*-GNN are *k*-element subsets, and messages flow between subsets differing in one element. A 2-GNN operates on ordered pairs and provably matches 2-WL power, but already costs *O(n²)* memory and *O(n³)* time — feasible for molecules (*n ≈ 30*) but prohibitive for social graphs.

**Invariant Graph Networks (k-IGNs).** Maron et al. build networks from linear layers equivariant to the full symmetric group *Sₙ* acting on order-*k* tensors [3]. The space of such linear layers has Bell-number dimension (15 for *k = 2*), each learnable. A *k*-IGN with pointwise nonlinearities matches *k*-WL expressivity. The crown jewel is the **reduced 2-order network**: 15 equivariant linear maps collapse to a scaled identity plus a single quadratic operation — matrix multiplication of the feature matrix with itself — and the resulting model is provably 3-WL-powerful while storing only *n × n* tensors [3].

> **Theorem (Maron et al. [3]).** *The k-order invariant graph network is as expressive as the k-WL test. In particular, the reduced 2-order model with matrix multiplication distinguishes all graphs distinguishable by 3-WL (equivalently, 2-FWL).*

The **complexity wall** is fundamental, not incidental: Cai, Fürer, and Immerman [6] constructed, for every *k*, pairs of non-isomorphic graphs indistinguishable by *k*-WL, with size polynomial in *k*. No fixed-order architecture — however wide or deep — escapes this. Universality requires the order to grow with the graph.

### 4.3 Counting logic and the descriptive complexity of GNNs

The correspondence between *k*-WL and the counting logic *C^{k+1}* [7] converts expressivity questions into logic questions, which are often easier to settle. Examples:

- *Triangle detection* — "∃x∃y∃z (E(x,y) ∧ E(y,z) ∧ E(z,x))" — needs 3 variables, hence 2-WL power. **No MPNN can count triangles** on unattributed graphs; any GNN that succeeds must use node identifiers, port numbering, or higher-order structure.
- *4-cycle detection* is *C³*-expressible but the **number** of 4-cycles incident to a node requires homomorphism counts beyond what 2-FWL captures in full generality.
- *Graph diameter, girth, and planarity* sit at various levels of the counting hierarchy, giving a principled map of which architectures suit which tasks.

This logical lens also explains a subtle phenomenon: adding **random node features** or unique identifiers makes MPNNs universal in a probabilistic sense (they can simulate higher-order tests by breaking symmetry), but at the cost of destroying the permutation-invariance inductive bias and harming generalization — a tradeoff formalized in recent work on the generalization–expressivity frontier.

### 4.4 Universality: Stone–Weierstrass for graphs

Expressivity (distinguishing non-isomorphic graphs) is weaker than **universality** (approximating any continuous invariant function). Keriven and Peyré [4] proved that invariant graph networks with a single hidden layer — sums of channels formed by equivariant linear operators, pointwise nonlinearities, and invariant pooling — are universal, via an equivariant Stone–Weierstrass theorem. The result holds for graphs of *varying* size with a *fixed* parameter set, a nontrivial extension of classical universality.

The catch, again, is order: universality proofs require tensor order growing with the approximation target. In practice, the relevant question is not "is my architecture universal?" but "does its WL level cover the structural statistics my task needs?" For molecular property prediction, 1-WL plus explicit cycle features often suffices; for combinatorial optimization (e.g., learning to branch in MILP solvers), higher-order structure appears genuinely necessary.

### 4.5 The practical frontier: subgraph GNNs and local enhancements

Between 1-WL and full *k*-WL lies a fertile middle ground of **subgraph-based** architectures: for each node, extract its *k*-hop ego-graph (or mark the root specially, as in ID-GNN / NGNN), run an MPNN on each subgraph, and pool. These models exceed 1-WL — they can count cycles up to bounded length — while costing only a constant factor over standard MPNNs. The "Weisfeiler and Leman go loopy" framework [9] pushes this further: *r*-loopy WL collects messages along paths between neighbors, counting cycles up to length *r + 2* with linear overhead in the neighborhood size, and its neural counterpart *r*-loopy GIN is competitive on molecular benchmarks while remaining scalable. This is where theory currently meets practice: **local higher-order structure at near-linear cost.**

## 5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

### Benchmark evidence

| Model | WL level | MUTAG | PTC | PROTEINS | NCI1 | Complexity/layer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GCN | ≤ 1-WL | 85.6 | 64.2 | 76.0 | 76.7 | *O(m)* |
| GraphSAGE | ≤ 1-WL | 85.1 | 63.9 | 75.9 | 77.7 | *O(m)* |
| GIN | = 1-WL | **89.4** | **64.6** | **76.2** | **82.7** | *O(m)* |
| 2-GNN | = 2-WL | 88.7 | 63.0 | 75.8 | 80.9 | *O(n³)* |
| PPGN (3-WL) | = 3-WL | 90.2 | 66.2 | 77.2 | 83.2 | *O(n³)* |

*(Representative figures from [2, 3]; exact numbers vary with splits and tuning.)* The pattern is consistent: attaining the 1-WL ceiling (GIN) yields the largest single jump; climbing to 2-WL/3-WL helps modestly on structure-sensitive datasets but at steep computational cost.

### Theoretical guarantees, summarized

> **Theorem (Xu et al. [2]).** *A message passing GNN with injective aggregation, injective combine, and injective readout distinguishes exactly the pairs of graphs distinguished by the 1-WL test.*

> **Theorem (Maron et al. [3]).** *The k-IGN architecture matches k-WL distinguishing power; the reduced 2-order variant with matrix multiplication matches 3-WL.*

> **Theorem (Keriven–Peyré [4]).** *Single-hidden-layer invariant/equivariant graph networks are universal approximators of continuous invariant/equivariant functions on graphs of bounded size.*

> **Theorem (Cai–Fürer–Immerman [6]).** *For every fixed k, there exist non-isomorphic graphs of size polynomial in k that k-WL cannot distinguish. The hierarchy is strict and no fixed level decides isomorphism.*

Together these draw a complete map: MPNNs live at level 1, GIN sits exactly on the level-1 ceiling, *k*-order networks climb the ladder rung by rung, universality is achievable but only with unbounded order — and no finite rung reaches the top.

## 6 Limitations

**Expressivity ≠ generalization.** The WL framework measures *distinguishing power*, a worst-case combinatorial notion. A maximally expressive model can memorize training graphs while failing to generalize; indeed, highly expressive architectures often exhibit *worse* test performance on small molecular datasets because they fit spurious structural noise. The bias–variance tradeoff is sharper for graphs than for images: permutation symmetry is a strong prior, and weakening it (via identifiers or high-order tensors) must be paid for in sample complexity.

**Continuous features break the clean theory.** The GIN injectivity theorem assumes countable feature spaces. With continuous node attributes, sum-decomposition MLPs are not exactly injective, and the "1-WL ceiling" becomes an approximation statement. In practice continuous features *help* (they break symmetries that WL cannot), but the theory offers limited guidance on how much.

**The hierarchy measures the wrong thing for some tasks.** Node classification on homophilic graphs, link prediction, and recommender tasks rarely need beyond-1-WL power; oversmoothing and under-reaching (limited receptive fields) are the binding constraints, not expressivity. Conversely, for tasks like counting substructures or solving combinatorial problems, even 3-WL may be insufficient.

**Computational reality.** *k*-WL for *k ≥ 3* is *O(n⁴)* or worse per iteration — dead on arrival for graphs beyond a few hundred nodes. Subgraph and loopy variants [9] mitigate this but introduce their own hyperparameters (subgraph radius, marking policies) with incomplete theory.

**Isomorphism is the wrong equivalence.** Two molecules can be non-isomorphic yet functionally near-identical (a methyl shift), or isomorphic yet drawn from different distributions. WL-indistinguishability is a statement about *exact* structural identity; real tasks need *metric* notions of structural similarity, where GNNs' continuous embeddings (their advantage over WL noted by Xu et al. [2]) matter more than their distinguishing power.

## 7 Conclusion

The Weisfeiler–Leman hierarchy transformed graph learning from alchemy into engineering. We now know *exactly* what message passing can and cannot do: it is bounded by color refinement, and the Graph Isomorphism Network attains that bound through the deceptively simple device of injective multiset aggregation [2]. Above it stretches a strict, infinite hierarchy — *k*-WL, counting logic, homomorphism counts, universality theorems — each rung with neural counterparts of precisely characterized power and precisely quantified cost [3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9].

The mature lesson is not "always climb higher" but **calibrated expressivity**: match the WL level to the structural statistics the task requires, spend the computational budget where the signal lives (cycles for chemistry, long-range interactions for programs), and remember that the hierarchy measures discrimination while generalization demands restraint. The open frontier — subgraph-enhanced local methods, metric relaxations of isomorphism, and the expressivity–generalization Pareto front — is where the next decade of graph learning will be won.
