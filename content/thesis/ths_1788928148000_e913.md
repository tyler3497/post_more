# Hardware Trojan Detection in Integrated Circuits: Side-Channel Fingerprinting, Machine-Learning Classifiers, Golden-Chip-Free Anomaly Detection, and Split-Manufacturing Countermeasures

## Abstract

The globalization of the semiconductor supply chain has made hardware Trojans — stealthy malicious modifications inserted by untrusted foundries, third-party IP vendors, or compromised CAD tools — a first-order threat to integrated-circuit trustworthiness. This thesis develops a unified treatment of post-silicon hardware Trojan detection: classical side-channel fingerprinting pioneered by Agrawal et al. in 2007, statistical test generation via MERO that maximizes Trojan activation probability, and machine-learning classifiers trained on power and electromagnetic side-channel features from the TrustHub benchmark suite. A central emphasis is **golden-chip-free** anomaly detection — one-class SVM, autoencoders, and PCA-subspace residuals that dispense with trusted reference devices. It further analyzes **split manufacturing**, which divides FEOL and BEOL fabrication between untrusted and trusted foundries so no single adversary observes the complete netlist. Synthesizing quantitative results from the literature into a detection-theoretic framework with formal bounds, it closes with the fundamental limits of each approach and argues that only defense-in-depth — fingerprinting, learning, and manufacturing-level countermeasures combined — yields practical assurance.

## 1. Introduction

A modern integrated circuit traverses a deeply fragmented supply chain. Register-transfer-level design may be outsourced; soft IP cores are licensed from vendors whose environments cannot be audited; EDA tools are complex software stacks from a handful of vendors; and fabrication is concentrated in offshore foundries with full access to the complete layout database (GDSII). At each handoff, a motivated adversary may insert a **hardware Trojan**: additional logic dormant during manufacturing test yet, once triggered by rare internal states, delivering a payload from denial of service to silent cryptographic key exfiltration [4].

Three properties make the hardware Trojan problem uniquely difficult. First, Trojans are designed to evade conventional post-manufacturing functional and structural test: they hide behind *rare* trigger conditions, so the probability that a random or even coverage-driven test vector activates them is vanishingly small. Second, Trojan circuitry can be **ultrasmall** — a comparator-based Trojan may occupy well under 0.1% of the total gate count — so its signature in power, delay, or area is easily buried beneath inter-die and intra-die process variation. Third, the *verifier* of the chip is typically not the fabricator: the design house receives silicon from an entity it does not fully trust, and must authenticate a device against specifications it cannot physically re-derive without destructive reverse engineering.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the Trojan taxonomy and threat model. Section 3 details the methodology: side-channel fingerprint construction, statistical rare-node excitation, supervised classification, unsupervised anomaly detection, and split manufacturing. Section 4 deep-dives into four pillars — the supply-chain threat model; side-channel physics and fingerprint statistics; the machine-learning detection pipeline; and the security argument for split manufacturing. Section 5 presents empirical results with a detection-theoretic formulation and proofs. Section 6 enumerates limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

> **Definition 1.1 (Hardware Trojan).** A hardware Trojan is a tuple $T = (\tau, \pi)$ where $\tau$ is a *trigger* circuit, a combinational or sequential function of a subset of internal nodes that evaluates to true under a rare operating condition, and $\pi$ is a *payload* circuit whose effect is observable (functionally or parametrically) only after $\tau$ fires.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Trojan Taxonomy

The canonical classification of hardware Trojans, articulated in the survey literature and in DARPA Trust-in-IC program documents, partitions Trojans along three axes [5]:

- **Physical attributes**: the type (functional vs. parametric), size (number of added gates), distribution (tightly clustered vs. dispersed), and structure (layout altered vs. preserved).
- **Activation characteristics**: Trojans are *always-on* (e.g., a side-channel leakage path that continuously modulates power to transmit a key bit) or *triggered* (internally by rare logic conditions, or externally via a sensor or antenna).
- **Action characteristics**: the payload may *modify functionality* (e.g., privilege escalation in a processor), *degrade performance*, *leak information*, or *deny service*.

A combinational Trojan fires when a conjunction of $q$ rare-valued nodes simultaneously assumes its rare value; a sequential Trojan adds a state machine (classically a counter, as in the TrustHub AES-T100 benchmark) that advances toward activation only when specific patterns are observed. The adversary's design objective is a trigger probability so small that manufacturing test never observes activation, yet nonzero so the Trojan is usable in the field.

### 2.2 The Golden-Chip Problem

Nearly all parametric detection methods require a **golden chip**: a reference device or signature known to be Trojan-free. Acquiring one is the bootstrap problem of hardware assurance — Agrawal et al. [1] proposed reverse-engineering a small sample of fabricated ICs, an expensive, destructive, and statistically weak procedure, since a Trojan present in *all* sampled chips would poison the reference. This motivates the golden-chip-free techniques of Section 4.4: instead of trusting a physical artifact, one trusts a *model* of process variation and nominal simulation behavior, and flags deviations from it as anomalous.

### 2.3 Side-Channel Physics

Every switching event in CMOS logic dissipates dynamic power $P_{dyn} = \alpha C V_{DD}^2 f$. A Trojan adds gates, hence capacitance $C$ and activity $\alpha$ beyond the nominal design. Even when the Trojan is *not* triggered, its trigger-monitoring logic switches in response to its input nodes, producing a parametric signature in **transient supply current** ($I_{DDT}$), **quiescent current** ($I_{DDQ}$), **path delay** (added capacitive load), and **electromagnetic emanations** (localized current loops; EM probes provide spatial resolution that power rails cannot).

> **Theorem:** Let the measured side-channel vector be $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{s} + \mathbf{n}_p + \mathbf{n}_m + \mathbf{t}$, where $\mathbf{s}$ is the nominal signature, $\mathbf{n}_p$ process variation, $\mathbf{n}_m$ measurement noise, and $\mathbf{t}$ the Trojan contribution. Then any detector with false-alarm rate $\alpha$ has detection power bounded by the signal-to-interference ratio $\|\mathbf{t}\|^2 / \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_p + \Sigma_m)$.

This is the fundamental SNR formulation: detection is a battle between Trojan magnitude and the process-noise floor, and every technique in this thesis is, at bottom, an attempt to maximize that ratio.

## 3. Methodology

The detection methodology proceeds in four stages, each corresponding to a generation of the literature:

1. **Fingerprint construction (Agrawal et al. [1])**. Apply $M$ input vectors; record the side-channel response. From reverse-engineered golden chips, estimate the nominal mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$. For a chip under test with response $\mathbf{x}$, compute the Mahalanobis distance $d^2 = (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})$ and threshold against a $\chi^2$ value; chips outside the fingerprint are declared infected.

2. **Rare-node excitation (MERO [2])**. Identify internal nodes whose signal probability of a logic value is below a threshold $\theta_{rare}$ via logic simulation. Generate test vectors forcing each rare node to its rare value at least $N$ times — *multiple excitation of rare occurrences* — raising Trojan switching activity during measurement and achieving comparable coverage with ~85% fewer vectors than random patterns.

3. **Supervised classification on extracted features**. Collect power or EM traces across TrustHub benchmarks (AES-T100 through AES-T2000, RS232-T1000, BasicRSA). Extract PCA, wavelet, and spectral features; train SVM, random forest, ELM, or contrastive deep networks; evaluate with cross-validation.

4. **Unsupervised golden-chip-free modeling [3]**. Learn a trusted region from simulation-calibrated process models or from the population under test itself (assuming only a minority is infected). One-class SVM, autoencoder reconstruction error, or PCA-subspace projection distance then scores each chip; outliers are flagged without any golden reference.

The constructive countermeasure — **split manufacturing** — is evaluated as a methodology for *prevention* rather than detection: partition the layout so that FEOL layers (transistors, polysilicon) are fabricated at the untrusted foundry while BEOL layers (metal interconnect) are completed at a trusted facility. The untrusted foundry then sees gates but not their wiring, and Trojan insertion requires guessing the missing connectivity.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Supply-Chain Threat Model and Trojan Insertion Points

The IC supply chain can be modeled as a sequence of custodians, each with distinct capabilities:

| Stage | Custodian | Trojan insertion capability |
|---|---|---|
| RTL / IP integration | Design house, 3PIP vendor | Malicious RTL in soft IP cores |
| Logic synthesis & P&R | EDA tool vendor | Tool-level subversion of netlist |
| Fabrication (FEOL+BEOL) | Untrusted foundry | Full-layout Trojan insertion (strongest adversary) |
| Test & assembly | Test house | Limited; parametric tampering |
| Deployment | End user / system integrator | None (victim) |

The foundry-level adversary is the strongest: with the complete GDSII, it can perform *engineering change order* (ECO)-style insertion, adding a handful of gates into whitespace (filler-cell regions) and routing trigger inputs from existing nets with minimal perturbation of timing closure — leaving the footprint nearly unchanged. The 3PIP adversary is weaker in layout control but stronger in *stealth of specification*: a Trojaned AES core can leak key bits through a covert power channel while passing all functional tests, because the leakage path is *functionally invisible*.

> **Theorem:** An adversary with full layout access and knowledge of the test-vector set can construct a Trojan whose trigger condition is disjoint from all test vectors, making logic-testing detection probability exactly zero.

*Proof sketch.* Let $V \subset \{0,1\}^n$ be the test set. The adversary chooses a trigger conjunction over $q$ internal nodes whose joint rare condition is never satisfied by any $v \in V$ — possible whenever $|V| \ll 2^n$ and the node functions are not constant on $V$. The Trojan's observable behavior then coincides with the genuine circuit on all of $V$. ∎

This theorem is the formal justification for side-channel methods: since the trigger can always be hidden from the test set, detection must rely on the Trojan's *parametric* footprint, which exists whether or not the Trojan fires.

### 4.2 Side-Channel Fingerprinting: From Power Traces to Statistical Decision

Agrawal et al.'s IC fingerprinting [1] treats each chip as a point in a high-dimensional measurement space: path delays of $K$ sensitized paths, or transient-current waveforms under $M$ vectors. The *fingerprint* of the genuine population is the estimated distribution $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$, with inter-die variation shifting $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ per lot and intra-die variation widening $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$.

Two refinements proved essential. First, **differential and regional analysis**: comparing the *shape* of the transient waveform rather than absolute power (which is swamped by inter-die shifts), or partitioning the chip into regions so that a localized Trojan perturbs one region disproportionately. Rad, Plusquellic, and Tehranipoor [6] showed power-supply transient analysis could resolve Trojans as small as **three additional gates** in simulation — a landmark sensitivity result. Second, **self-referencing**: comparing *neighboring* dies or repeated measurements of the same die to cancel systematic variation.

The statistical decision rule is a hypothesis test:

- $H_0$: chip is genuine, $\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$.
- $H_1$: chip is infected, $\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\delta}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$, with unknown shift $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ induced by the Trojan.

The generalized likelihood-ratio test reduces, for known $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, to thresholding the Mahalanobis distance. The detection probability is $P_D = Q\left(Q^{-1}(P_{FA}) - \sqrt{\boldsymbol{\delta}^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\delta}}\right)$ — the closed-form embodiment of the SNR theorem. Every technique in this thesis is, at bottom, an attempt to increase the non-centrality parameter $\boldsymbol{\delta}^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\delta}$: MERO increases $\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|$ by exciting the Trojan; regional/EM analysis shrinks the effective $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ by spatial localization; learning-based methods estimate a better $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}$ from data.

### 4.3 Machine-Learning Classifiers on TrustHub Benchmarks

The TrustHub suite (hosted at trust-hub.org) provides the community's standard evaluation vehicle: synthesizable Trojan-inserted designs including AES cores with counter-based and comparator-based triggers (AES-T100 … AES-T2000), RS232 UART Trojans that leak data or deny service, and RSA variants. Each benchmark ships with the Trojan-free base design, enabling controlled genuine-vs.-infected experiments in simulation and on FPGA.

A modern ML detection pipeline has four stages: **acquisition** of $I_{DDT}$ or EM traces (thousands per chip across dozens of chips); **feature extraction** via PCA (95% variance retained), wavelet decompositions, or spectral statistics; **classification** with SVM, random forests, ELM, or contrastive deep networks [7], routinely exceeding 95% accuracy on TrustHub AES benchmarks and 99%+ under controlled aging [8]; and **validation** by $k$-fold cross-validation *across chips* — never across traces — to avoid leaking per-chip process signatures into both folds.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.svm import SVC
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score

# X: (n_traces, n_samples) EM/power traces; y: 0 genuine, 1 infected
pca = PCA(n_components=0.95, svd_solver="full")
Z = pca.fit_transform(X)          # denoise + decorrelate; kills measurement noise
clf = SVC(kernel="rbf", C=10.0, gamma="scale", class_weight="balanced")
scores = cross_val_score(clf, Z, y, cv=5, scoring="roc_auc")
print(f"mean AUC={scores.mean():.3f} ± {scores.std():.3f}")
# Guard: split folds by chip id, not by trace, to avoid process-signature leakage.
```

A subtle methodological pitfall deserves emphasis: if traces from the *same physical chip* appear in both training and test folds, the classifier may learn the chip's individual process signature rather than the Trojan's — inflating accuracy. Rigorous studies therefore perform **leave-one-chip-out** validation.

### 4.4 Golden-Chip-Free Detection with Unsupervised Anomaly Models

The golden-chip-free program, exemplified by Hu, Nowroz, Reda, and Koushanfar [3], removes the trusted reference entirely. The key insight is that the *population under test* is mostly genuine: if Trojans are rare in the supply (a realistic assumption — a foundry that infected every chip would be trivially caught by functional anomalies and would destroy its own business), then the genuine population can be estimated robustly from the data itself.

The method proceeds as follows. First, calibrate a **simulation-based process model**: run Monte Carlo simulations of the genuine netlist under the foundry's statistical compact models to predict the nominal side-channel distribution. Second, measure process-control-monitor (PCM) structures on the actual wafer to fit the *actual* process corner — correcting the simulation-to-silicon mismatch that would otherwise poison the trusted region. Third, construct the trusted region in the calibrated space and classify each chip by its distance to the region boundary.

> **Theorem:** Let a fraction $\epsilon < 1/2$ of the population be infected, and let the genuine distribution have bounded covariance. Then a robust estimator (e.g., minimum covariance determinant) recovers the genuine mean and covariance with error $O(\epsilon)$ in operator norm, so the induced detector's excess false-alarm rate vanishes as $\epsilon \to 0$.

In practice, unsupervised models take several forms: **one-class SVM** (minimum-volume hypersphere enclosing the genuine population), **autoencoders** (trained to reconstruct genuine traces; infected chips yield high reconstruction error), and **PCA-subspace residuals** (large $\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{x}\|^2$ flags anomalies).

Empirically, the DAC 2014 golden-chip-free study [3] reported that classification boundaries learned from simulation-calibrated data correctly classified all 80 Trojan-infested devices while misclassifying only a minority of genuine devices — with PCM measurements strictly improving the genuine acceptance rate. The residual gap is the price of abandoning the golden chip: without ground truth, the trusted region is necessarily conservative.

### 4.5 Split Manufacturing as a Constructive Countermeasure

Detection asks, "is this chip infected?" Split manufacturing asks the stronger question: "can the adversary insert a Trojan at all?" In the Rajendran et al. framework, fabrication is divided: the **front end of line** (transistors, contacts, lower metal layers) is built at an advanced but untrusted foundry, while the **back end of line** (upper metal layers completing the interconnect) is finished at a trusted facility. The untrusted foundry therefore observes the complete transistor-level netlist *without* the wiring that defines its function — a sea of gates with dangling pins.

The security argument is information-theoretic in flavor: to insert a targeted Trojan (e.g., one that leaks the AES key), the adversary must identify which gates implement the key register and route trigger inputs from them. With BEOL missing, the adversary faces a **netlist recovery** problem: inferring connectivity from FEOL geometry alone. Proximity heuristics — the empirical observation that connected cells are usually placed near each other — enable *proximity attacks* that recover a substantial fraction of nets, so naive splitting is insufficient. Three defenses close the gap: **wire lifting** (elevating security-critical nets to trusted BEOL layers), **placement perturbation** (separating connected cells at an area/wirelength cost), and **logic obfuscation/locking** (key-controlled gates so a recovered netlist is functionally opaque without the post-fabrication secret key).

> **Theorem:** If $m$ two-pin nets are lifted to the BEOL and the adversary's proximity hint yields at most $c$ candidate partners per pin, the expected number of correctly recovered lifted nets is at most $m/c$; choosing $m$ and the lifting policy to cover all key-dependent nets forces the adversary's targeted-Trojan success probability below any prescribed $\eta$ at $O(m \log c)$ trusted-BEOL cost.

The practical costs are real: split manufacturing requires compatible process design kits at two foundries and trusted BEOL capacity (often at an older, coarser node, constraining how much can be lifted). It also composes naturally with detection: split manufacturing raises the *cost* of insertion, while side-channel fingerprinting raises the *probability of catching* whatever slips through — a genuine defense in depth.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Quantitative Synthesis from the Literature

| Technique | Benchmark / setup | Reported result |
|---|---|---|
| Path-delay fingerprinting [1] | Explicit-payload Trojans, high process variation | 100% detection |
| Path-delay fingerprinting [1] | Implicit (counter-based) Trojan, 0.76% area | 36% detection — hard case |
| Power transient analysis [6] | Simulated benchmark circuit | Sensitivity down to 3 added gates |
| MERO test generation [2] | ISCAS benchmarks | ~85% test-length reduction vs. random |
| Golden-chip-free classification [3] | 80 infected + 40 genuine devices | All 80 infected caught; genuine acceptance improves with PCM calibration |
| ML + controlled aging [8] | AES-T100, 32-bin features | 99.15% genuine / 100% infected accuracy |
| Contrastive learning [7] | Side-channel traces | Improved separation vs. classical classifiers |

### 5.2 Detection-Theoretic Formulation and Proof

We formalize the detector's fundamental limit. Consider the binary hypothesis test of Section 4.2 with $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ unknown but constrained to a Trojan-feasible set $\mathcal{D}$ (small norm, sparse support corresponding to localized gates).

> **Theorem:** (Minimax detection bound.) Let $\mathcal{D} = \{\boldsymbol{\delta} : \|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_2 \le \rho,\ \|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_0 \le s\}$. For any detector with false-alarm rate at most $\alpha$, the worst-case miss rate satisfies $\beta^* \ge Q\left(\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{\min}\sqrt{s}} - Q^{-1}(\alpha)\right)$-type lower bounds decreasing in $\rho$ and increasing in the effective noise projected onto the sparse support.

*Proof.* Reduce to the hardest one-sparse alternative: the adversary concentrates the Trojan signature into the single measurement coordinate with the largest projected noise variance. The problem becomes detection of a bounded signal in Gaussian noise, where the Neyman–Pearson lemma gives the optimal likelihood-ratio test and ROC $P_D = Q(Q^{-1}(P_{FA}) - \rho/\sigma_{\max})$. Since the adversary chooses the coordinate, no detector beats this bound uniformly over $\mathcal{D}$. ∎

The bound makes the design trade-offs quantitative: MERO and rare-node excitation increase $\rho$ (signal amplitude); EM spatial localization and regional power analysis decrease $\sigma_{\max}$ (noise in the relevant coordinate); and larger Trojans (bigger $s$ with fixed total $\rho$) are paradoxically *easier* to detect because the adversary cannot concentrate signature arbitrarily.

### 5.3 Worked Numerical Example

Consider an AES core with transient-current standard deviation $\sigma = 2.1\,\mathrm{mA}$ per measurement after averaging ($N=1000$ repetitions). A comparator Trojan tapping 8 nets adds an estimated $\rho = 0.35\,\mathrm{mA}$ of correlated shift under MERO excitation, so the per-coordinate SNR is $\rho/\sigma \approx 0.167$. With $P_{FA} = 10^{-3}$ ($Q^{-1} \approx 3.09$), single-measurement $P_D = Q(3.09 - 0.167) \approx 0.17\%$ — useless. But aggregating $K = 400$ independent measurements, the effective SNR grows as $\sqrt{K}$: $P_D = Q(3.09 - 0.167\sqrt{400}) = Q(-0.25) \approx 60\%$. Doubling excitation or halving noise via EM localization pushes $P_D$ past 95%. This reproduces, in miniature, why the literature's successful results all combine *excitation* (bigger $\rho$) with *aggregation* (more coordinates): neither alone suffices against ultrasmall Trojans.

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating what these methods cannot do.

1. **The process-noise floor is fundamental.** As Trojans shrink below a few gates, their parametric signature falls beneath intra-die variation that no averaging can remove. Sub-3-gate Trojans in large SoCs remain beyond demonstrated sensitivity.
2. **Golden-chip-free methods trade trust, not eliminate it.** They replace trust in a physical artifact with trust in simulation models, PCM structures, and the "minority infected" assumption. A foundry that infects *every* chip, or tampers with the PCM structures, defeats population-based reasoning.
3. **ML classifiers are benchmark-bound.** Accuracies above 99% on TrustHub AES benchmarks are achieved in simulation or on FPGAs with careful per-study setups. Cross-fab, cross-lot, and cross-temperature generalization is far weaker, and adversarial ML attacks — a foundry crafting Trojans whose side-channel signature mimics the genuine class — are barely addressed in the current literature.
4. **Split manufacturing leaks through proximity and has economic costs.** Proximity attacks recover 70–90% of nets in naive splits; defeating them requires wire lifting and placement perturbation that cost area, power, and design effort, plus a trusted BEOL facility. It protects against foundry insertion but not 3PIP or EDA-tool adversaries.
5. **Always-on analog Trojans evade digital reasoning.** Parametric Trojans leaking through subthreshold or thermal channels without digital switching produce no $I_{DDT}$ signature under vector excitation; they require dedicated analog instrumentation outside the standard flow.
6. **Test-time vs. field-time gap.** All post-silicon methods certify the chip *at test time*. A time-bomb Trojan armed for years in the future is parametrically present but logically silent — detectable only if its trigger-monitoring logic switches measurably during test, which MERO-style excitation only probabilistically ensures.

## 7. Conclusion

Hardware Trojan detection has matured from Agrawal et al.'s foundational fingerprinting insight — that a chip's side-channel emanations constitute an identity that malicious additions must perturb — into a diversified discipline spanning statistics, machine learning, and manufacturing technology. The through-line of this thesis is the **signal-to-interference ratio**: every advance, from MERO's rare-node excitation to EM spatial localization, from supervised classifiers on TrustHub traces to golden-chip-free anomaly models, is a technique for amplifying the Trojan's whisper above the roar of process variation. Split manufacturing complements detection with prevention, denying the foundry adversary the complete netlist needed for targeted insertion.

No single method suffices. Fingerprinting needs golden chips that are hard to bootstrap; golden-chip-free methods need models that are hard to calibrate; ML needs data that is hard to generalize; split manufacturing needs a trusted facility that is hard to provision. The practical conclusion is **defense in depth**: excite rare nodes during test, fingerprint power and EM side channels against simulation-calibrated trusted regions, screen populations with unsupervised anomaly models, and split the fabrication of security-critical designs. Together, these layers make Trojan insertion expensive, risky, and likely to be caught — the most that hardware assurance, short of fully trusted domestic fabrication, can honestly promise.
