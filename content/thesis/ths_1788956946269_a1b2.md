# From PBFT to HotStuff to DAG-Based Consensus: View Changes, Linear Communication Complexity, and the Narwhal–Tusk Mempool Separation in Byzantine Fault-Tolerant State Machine Replication

## Abstract

Byzantine fault-tolerant state machine replication has evolved through three architectural epochs: the primary–backup quorum protocols of the 1990s, the linear leader-based protocols of the blockchain era, and the directed-acyclic-graph protocols that decouple data dissemination from ordering. This thesis traces that trajectory from Castro and Liskov's PBFT [1], whose $O(n^2)$ view-change subprotocol dominated failure recovery, through Yin et al.'s HotStuff [2], which achieved optimistic responsiveness with $O(n)$ per-view communication using threshold signatures [8], to Danezis et al.'s Narwhal–Tusk architecture [3], which separates a high-throughput mempool DAG from a consensus layer that adds *zero messages* of protocol overhead. We formalize the communication-complexity bounds motivating each transition, analyze HotStuff's three-chain commit rule and rotating-leader pipelining, dissect Narwhal's causal-order reliable broadcast and Tusk's randomized coin-based leader election, and evaluate Bullshark's fast-path refinement [4] against DAG-Rider [5] and SBFT [6]. We conclude with measured throughput–latency results, the garbage-collection-versus-fairness tension, and open questions on adaptivity.

## 1 Introduction

State machine replication (SMR) remains the canonical technique for building fault-tolerant services: a deterministic service is replicated across $n$ replicas, and clients observe a single logical machine so long as a sufficient fraction of replicas is correct [7]. The *Byzantine* variant of SMR admits replicas that fail arbitrarily — crashing, lying, colluding, or behaving adaptively — and is the foundation of permissioned blockchains, financial settlement systems, and critical infrastructure control planes. The central technical problem is deceptively simple: given that up to $f$ of $n = 3f+1$ replicas may be malicious, how can the correct replicas agree on a single, totally ordered sequence of commands while communicating as little as possible?

The classic answer, Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT) [1], organizes agreement around a stable primary and a three-phase vote (pre-prepare, prepare, commit) built on $2f+1$ quorums. Under a correct primary, PBFT decides in constant rounds, but its *view-change* — the subprotocol that replaces a faulty leader — costs $O(n^2)$ messages, an artifact that haunted every PBFT descendant for two decades. HotStuff [2] broke that barrier: by combining threshold signatures with a rotating-leader discipline borrowed from blockchain protocols, it made every phase, including leader failover, cost $O(n)$ messages while preserving responsiveness — the property that a correct leader drives consensus at the speed of the actual network rather than a worst-case delay bound.

A second, orthogonal revolution separated the *data path* from the *agreement path*. In classical protocols the leader is both the bottleneck for transaction dissemination and the single point of failure for ordering; the Narwhal mempool [3] instead lets every validator continuously disseminate transactions through a DAG built on reliable broadcast, while a consensus layer — Tusk for asynchrony, Bullshark for the eventually synchronous case [4] — imposes a total order by locally interpreting the DAG with no extra messages. This thesis examines the full arc: the mathematics of quorums, the engineering of linear view changes, and the systems design of DAG-based decoupling.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The impossibility landscape

The celebrated Fischer–Lynch–Paterson result [7] establishes that deterministic consensus is impossible in a fully asynchronous system with even one faulty process. Practical BFT protocols therefore operate under *partial synchrony*: the network may behave arbitrarily until some unknown Global Stabilization Time (GST), after which message delays are bounded by an unknown $\Delta$. Safety — agreement, validity, and total order — must hold in all executions, while liveness is guaranteed only after GST. This split is the source of every "safety under asynchrony, liveness under partial synchrony" claim in the literature, and it justifies randomized coin-based protocols such as Tusk [3] and DAG-Rider [5] that recover liveness during asynchrony by other means.

### 2.2 Quorums and the $3f+1$ bound

PBFT [1] and its descendants require $n \ge 3f+1$ replicas. The arithmetic is forced by *quorum intersection*: any two quorums of size $2f+1$ intersect in at least $f+1$ replicas, hence in at least one correct replica. That honest overlap is the channel through which prepared values propagate across views, and it is the entire reason a new leader can reconstruct a safe proposal after a view change. The following invariant is load-bearing for everything in this thesis:

> **Theorem:** In any system of $n = 3f+1$ replicas with at most $f$ Byzantine faults, if two quorums $Q_1, Q_2$ of size $2f+1$ each certify values $v_1, v_2$ for the same log position, then $v_1 = v_2$.

*Proof sketch.* $|Q_1 \cap Q_2| \ge 2(2f+1) - (3f+1) = f+1$. At least one replica in the intersection is correct, and a correct replica never certifies two different values for the same position in the same view. ∎

### 2.3 PBFT's three-phase normal case

In PBFT [1], a client sends a request to the primary, which assigns a sequence number $s$ in view $v$ and multicasts `⟨PRE-PREPARE, v, s, m⟩`. Each backup that accepts it multicasts `⟨PREPARE, v, s⟩`; a replica is *prepared* when it holds the pre-prepare plus $2f$ matching prepares — a $2f+1$ quorum. It then multicasts `⟨COMMIT, v, s⟩` and, upon collecting $2f+1$ commits, executes the request in sequence-number order and replies. The client accepts the result after $f+1$ matching replies. The normal case uses $O(n^2)$ messages (two all-to-all multicast rounds), and — critically — the view-change that replaces a faulty primary is also $O(n^2)$: each replica sends its prepared certificates to the new primary, which bundles them into a new-view message of size $O(n^2)$ authenticators.

The PBFT paper also introduced enduring optimizations [1]: MAC vectors instead of signatures in the normal case, batching of client requests under one pre-prepare, a read-only fast path, and tentative execution. But the quadratic view change survived every optimization, because collecting evidence from $2f+1$ replicas and redistributing it is inherently a gather-then-broadcast pattern.

### 2.4 Threshold signatures

The breakthrough that linearized BFT communication was the aggregation of $2f+1$ partial signatures into a single constant-size *quorum certificate* (QC), typically instantiated with BLS signatures [8]. Instead of forwarding $2f+1$ individual votes, a collector (or the leader) aggregates them into one signature verifiable against a single combined public key. SBFT [6] first demonstrated linear normal-case communication using designated collectors, but retained a heavy view change; HotStuff [2] applied the same primitive uniformly to every phase, including leader rotation, achieving $O(n)$ messages per view change for the first time.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis adopts a three-pronged methodology. First, we present the protocols *formally*: as state machines with explicit quorum rules, lock structures, and commit predicates, so that safety arguments can be checked against the quorum-intersection invariant rather than prose. Second, we analyze *communication complexity* asymptotically — counting messages and authenticator sizes per consensus decision, in both the fault-free steady state and the worst case of $f$ consecutive faulty leaders. Third, we corroborate the asymptotics with *measured performance*: the published WAN evaluations of Narwhal–Tusk [3] and Bullshark [4] (throughput in transactions per second versus commit latency for committees of 10–50 validators) and the LAN micro-benchmarks that expose PBFT's limits.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 PBFT's view change: the $O(n^2)$ bottleneck

View changes are where PBFT implementations historically failed — "view change is where the bodies are buried," in the engineering literature [1]. When $f+1$ backups suspect the primary (typically via timeout), each sends a `VIEW-CHANGE` message containing its latest stable checkpoint and every prepared certificate. The new primary waits for $2f+1$ such messages, constructs a `NEW-VIEW` containing the union of prepared requests, and the protocol restarts. The cost analysis is unforgiving:

| View-change step | Messages | Authenticators |
|---|---|---|
| $2f+1$ replicas send `VIEW-CHANGE` to new primary | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ each, $O(n^2)$ total payload |
| New primary broadcasts `NEW-VIEW` | $O(n)$ | $O(n^2)$ (all prepared certs) |
| Replicas verify and re-run prepare | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n^2)$ |

The $O(n^2)$ authenticator complexity is structural: without aggregation, the new leader must forward every quorum certificate it received. Subsequent PBFT variants (BFT-SMaRt, SBFT [6]) reduced the normal case to $O(n)$ with collectors and threshold signatures, but view changes remained quadratic — HotStuff's first claim to novelty was making *leader failover itself* linear [2].

### 4.2 HotStuff: rotating leaders and linear phases

HotStuff [2] reframes BFT replication around *rotating leaders*: every view has a designated leader, and views advance continuously rather than only on suspicion. Each view executes a simple pattern — the leader proposes a block extending the highest QC it knows, replicas vote with threshold-signature shares, the leader aggregates $2f+1$ shares into a QC and broadcasts it. Because every phase is leader-to-all plus all-to-leader with an aggregated QC, each view costs $O(n)$ messages and $O(n)$ authenticators.

The decisive simplification is the *three-chain commit rule*. Replicas maintain `lockedQC`, the highest QC they have voted for. A block $B$ becomes committed when three QCs chain consecutively: $QC(B)$, $QC(B')$ with $B'.\text{parent} = B$, and $QC(B'')$ with $B''.\text{parent} = B'$. The middle block's QC acts as a *prepare* certificate, and the third as the *commit* signal:

> **Theorem:** If an honest replica commits block $B$ via the three-chain rule in view $v$, then every QC formed in a view $> v$ extends $B$.

*Proof sketch.* Committing $B$ implies $2f+1$ replicas voted for the chain head and set `lockedQC` at least to $QC(B')$. Any later QC requires $2f+1$ votes, intersecting the locked set in $\ge f+1$ replicas, one honest — which refuses to vote for a block conflicting with its lock unless presented a higher-view QC extending it. Induction over views yields the claim. ∎

HotStuff therefore achieves *optimistic responsiveness*: a correct leader advances views at actual network speed, needing only the first $2f+1$ votes, while safety never depends on timing. The published deployment sustains throughput comparable to BFT-SMaRt over 100+ replicas with linear failover cost [2].

### 4.3 Chaining, pipelining, and the DiemBFT lineage

HotStuff's framework is generic enough to express PBFT, Tendermint, and Casper as instances [2], but its most influential instantiation is *Chained HotStuff*: the QC of view $v$ is embedded in the proposal of view $v+1$, so voting, proposing, and committing pipeline across consecutive views — each message simultaneously certifies the previous view and proposes the next. This is the design that the Diem blockchain productionized as **DiemBFT**, and that Aptos later refined into **Jolteon** and **Ditto**: Jolteon collapses the four-phase structure into two rounds in the common case, while Ditto adds a fallback to an asynchronous path so that the protocol degrades gracefully rather than stalling when the network does. The lesson of this lineage is that *the phase structure is a tunable parameter of the same framework*, not a new protocol each time.

### 4.4 The Narwhal–Tusk mempool separation

Classical protocols, including HotStuff, couple two logically distinct jobs: *disseminating transactions* (moving bulk data to all replicas) and *ordering them* (agreeing on a sequence). The leader is the bottleneck for both. Narwhal [3] severs the coupling with a dedicated mempool layer:

1. **Every validator** continuously batches client transactions and broadcasts them via reliable broadcast, forming vertices of a round-based DAG. Each vertex references $2f+1$ certificates from the previous round, so the DAG encodes causal history; reliable broadcast guarantees all honest validators eventually interpret the *same* DAG.
2. **Workers** scale dissemination horizontally: each validator runs multiple worker processes that perform the first step of reliable broadcast in parallel, so throughput scales with the number of workers rather than the leader's bandwidth.
3. **Consensus interprets the DAG** instead of re-transmitting data. Narwhal-HotStuff uses the DAG only for data availability and runs HotStuff over block *digests*; **Tusk** goes further, adding *zero messages* of its own: validators locally interpret every three DAG rounds as a consensus instance, use the first two rounds' links as all-to-all vote exchanges, and combine round-three threshold-signature shares into a *perfect shared coin* that elects a leader block from round one [3]. Once a leader is committed, its entire causal history is deterministically ordered.

The shared coin is the crucial ingredient: deterministic consensus is impossible in asynchrony [7], so Tusk embeds randomness *inside* the DAG — partial signatures on the round number, aggregated once $2f+1$ shares are present — giving a constant probability per instance of committing a useful leader, and hence expected constant-round latency. Measured on a WAN, Tusk sustains roughly 160,000 tx/sec at ~3 s latency, while Narwhal-HotStuff exceeds 130,000 tx/sec at under 2 s, versus ~1,800 tx/sec for baseline HotStuff [3].

## 5 Empirical Analysis and Formal Guarantees

The asymptotic story is summarized below, counting messages and authenticator sizes per committed decision ($n = 3f+1$):

| Protocol | Steady state | Faulty-leader recovery | Commit latency (rounds) | Extra consensus messages |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT [1] | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n^2)$ view change | 3 (pre-prepare/prepare/commit) | — |
| SBFT [6] | $O(n)$ (collectors) | $O(n^2)$ view change | 3–4 | — |
| HotStuff [2] | $O(n)$ per view | $O(n)$ (rotate leader) | 3 chained QCs | — |
| Narwhal-HotStuff [3] | $O(n)$ + DAG | $O(n)$ | 3 + DAG rounds | HotStuff over digests |
| Tusk [3] | DAG only | n/a (asynchronous) | ~7 rounds expected | **zero** |
| Bullshark [4] | DAG only | n/a (embedded) | 2 (sync fast path) / 6 exp. | **zero** |
| DAG-Rider [5] | DAG only | n/a (asynchronous) | 8 expected | **zero** |

Two formal guarantees are worth stating precisely, since they capture what each generation *proved* that the last could not.

> **Theorem:** HotStuff is the first partially synchronous BFT SMR protocol that is both *responsive* (a correct leader decides at actual network speed) and *linear* ($O(n)$ authenticator complexity per view, including view changes).

*Proof sketch.* Responsiveness follows because the leader advances on $2f+1$ votes without waiting for $\Delta$; linearity follows because every phase is a single leader broadcast plus aggregated shares, and leader rotation carries only the highest QC — $O(1)$ authenticators — rather than PBFT's bundle of prepared certificates. ∎

> **Theorem:** In Tusk, once all honest validators observe the same Narwhal DAG prefix, they commit identical leader sequences, and every transaction in a committed leader's causal history is eventually totally ordered.

*Proof sketch.* Reliable broadcast gives all honest parties the same DAG; the shared coin is deterministic given $2f+1$ round-$r$ shares, so leader election agrees; the commit rule (Lemma 3 of [3]: at least $f+1$ round-1 blocks satisfy it per instance) guarantees the adversary, who learns the leader only after the first two rounds are fixed, cannot retroactively suppress it with constant probability — yielding expected-constant-round termination. ∎

Measured results corroborate the theory. On a WAN with 50 validators, Bullshark's partially synchronous variant achieves ~125,000 tx/sec at ~2 s latency [4]; Tusk holds ~160,000 tx/sec at ~3 s under faults that inflate Narwhal-HotStuff's latency; and Narwhal's worker scale-out pushes throughput past 500,000 tx/sec with no latency increase [3]. The oft-cited LAN baseline is sobering for the classics: ~3,500 tx/sec for HotStuff and ~500 tx/sec for PBFT in the same benchmark family [4].

## 6 Limitations

The arc from PBFT to DAG-based consensus is not unqualified progress; each generation trades away something.

- **Trusted setup and cryptographic assumptions.** Threshold BLS signatures [8] require distributed key generation, and the perfect shared coin in Tusk [3] assumes an adaptively secure threshold scheme. A compromised dealer or a broken pairing assumption undermines every linearity claim at once.
- **The $O(n^2)$ worst case persists.** HotStuff's *amortized* linearity assumes an eventually correct leader; $f$ consecutive Byzantine leaders force $f$ linear view changes, i.e. $O(n^2)$ total work [2]. Rotating leadership bounds per-view cost, not adversarial cascades.
- **DAG protocols shift complexity, not eliminate it.** Narwhal's reliable broadcast is itself $O(n^2)$ in the worst case per vertex without erasure-coded dispersal; the mempool's throughput miracle depends on batching amortization and worker scale-out [3].
- **Garbage collection versus fairness.** Unbounded DAGs exhaust memory; collecting old rounds risks censoring slow validators' vertices. Bullshark's timestamped rounds [4] answer the dilemma only for the eventually synchronous case — the fully asynchronous setting still lacks a clean resolution.
- **Censorship and chain quality.** Leader-based protocols let a Byzantine leader silently exclude transactions; DAG protocols mitigate this (every validator disseminates), but Tusk's randomized leader election can still delay specific vertices for a geometric number of waves.
- **Latency floors.** DAG consensus pays a round tax: even Bullshark's fast path needs 2 DAG rounds (~4 message delays) versus HotStuff's chained pipeline. For latency-sensitive applications, the mempool separation is a throughput–latency trade, not a free lunch.

## 7 Conclusion

Three ideas, each a response to the previous generation's bottleneck, define modern BFT consensus. PBFT [1] showed that Byzantine SMR could be *practical*, paying for it with quadratic view changes. HotStuff [2] showed that threshold cryptography plus rotating leaders makes *every* phase — including failover — linear and responsive, and its chained, pipelined form became the template for DiemBFT, Jolteon, and Ditto. Narwhal and Tusk [3] showed that dissemination and ordering are separable concerns: build the DAG once with reliable broadcast, then order it with zero additional messages, whether by a partially synchronous protocol or a randomized asynchronous coin. Bullshark [4] and DAG-Rider [5] then demonstrated that the DAG itself can *be* the consensus protocol.

The trajectory's deeper lesson is about where complexity lives. PBFT concentrated it in the view change; HotStuff distributed it across uniform linear phases; the DAG school dissolved it into data structures — causal histories, quorum certificates as edges, coins as round metadata. Open problems remain: garbage collection with fairness under asynchrony, adaptive-adversary lower bounds for DAG commit latency, and erasure-coded dispersal that is $O(n)$ without trusted hardware. But the direction is clear: the leader is no longer the bottleneck, the view change is no longer special, and the DAG is the medium in which Byzantine agreement now happens.
