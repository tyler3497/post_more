# Fiat–Shamir with Aborts on Module Lattices: Rejection Sampling, Trapdoor Duality, and the Road to NIST FIPS 204 and FIPS 206

## Abstract

The migration of the Fiat–Shamir heuristic from the discrete-logarithm setting to the lattice setting is obstructed by a single phenomenon: in Euclidean space, the response of a Schnorr-like Σ-protocol is a *shifted* distribution that betrays the secret key. This thesis develops the *Fiat–Shamir with aborts* framework introduced by Lyubashevsky [1], in which the signer samples fresh masking vectors and restarts the protocol whenever a candidate response would leak secret-dependent structure. Rejection sampling renders the output signature distribution statistically independent of the secret key while preserving the algebraic form required for verification. We then survey the two modern endpoints this idea enabled: the *trapdoorless* CRYSTALS-Dilithium design, standardized as FIPS 204 under the name ML-DSA [5], and the *hash-and-sign* Falcon design built on NTRU trapdoors, selected for standardization as FIPS 206 (FN-DSA) [6]. We present the rejection-sampling lemma and the forking-lemma reduction to Module-SIS [3][4], tabulate concrete parameters and sizes, and discuss uniform versus Gaussian sampling, implementation fragility, and open problems.

---

## 1 Introduction

The Fiat–Shamir transform converts a public-coin Σ-protocol — *commit, challenge, response* — into a non-interactive signature by replacing the verifier's coins with the output of a cryptographic hash function. Its textbook instantiation is Schnorr's scheme: secret *x*, public *y* = *g^x*; the signer samples *r* ← **Z**_q, commits *R* = *g^r*, challenges *c* = *H*(*R*, *m*), responds *z* = *r* + *c*·*x*; verification checks *g^z* = *R*·*y^c*. Security rests on the discrete logarithm problem in the ROM.

Replacing the group *G* with a lattice seems straightforward — until one confronts the geometry of Euclidean space. In a lattice Σ-protocol over *R_q* = **Z**_q[*X*]/(*X^n*+1), the masking vector **y** must be sampled from a *bounded* or Gaussian distribution, and the response **z** = **y** + *c*·**s** is therefore *shifted by the secret* **s**. A handful of signatures suffices to recover **s** by statistical estimation: this was the precise mechanism behind the practical breaks of NTRUSign and the GGH scheme, where signatures leaked the shape of the secret parallelepiped [10]. Two divergent remedies emerged. The first, due to Gentry, Peikert, and Vaikuntanathan (GPV), abandons Fiat–Shamir entirely: signatures are produced by *preimage sampling* — hashing the message to a lattice coset and using a secret *short basis* as a trapdoor to sample a short vector in that coset [7]. The second, the subject of this thesis, keeps the Fiat–Shamir skeleton but repairs the leakage at its source: the signer *aborts* — i.e., restarts with fresh randomness — whenever the response would be distributed in a secret-dependent way. This is *Fiat–Shamir with aborts*, introduced by Lyubashevsky in 2009 [1] and refined in 2012 into fully trapdoorless constructions.

The stakes are maximal: after a multi-round competition, NIST finalized CRYSTALS-Dilithium as FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) in August 2024 [5][8], and selected the NTRU-based hash-and-sign Falcon as an alternative proceeding toward FIPS 206 (FN-DSA) [6][9]. Dilithium is a direct descendant of Fiat–Shamir with aborts; Falcon is the modern apotheosis of the GPV paradigm. Understanding both, and the rejection-sampling technique underpinning one of them, is therefore not only of theoretical interest but of direct engineering relevance to every post-quantum deployment now being planned.

*Roadmap.* §2 recalls lattices and Σ-protocols; §3 presents the abort methodology with a worked implementation; §4 develops the core — simulation lemma, Dilithium/ML-DSA parameters, Falcon hash-and-sign, and the forking reduction; §5 gives empirical results; §6 limitations; §7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Lattices and average-case problems

A *lattice* *L* ⊂ **R**^n is a discrete additive subgroup; a *q-ary* lattice additionally satisfies *q***Z**^n ⊆ *L* ⊆ **Z**^n. Lattice cryptography relies on the average-case hardness of the *Short Integer Solution* (SIS) and *Learning With Errors* (LWE) problems, which reduce from worst-case approximate SVP via Ajtai's and Regev's reductions [7].

- **SIS_{n,m,q,β}**: given uniform **A** ∈ **Z**_q^{n×m}, find nonzero **z** ∈ **Z**^m with **Az** = **0** mod *q* and ‖**z**‖ ≤ β.
- **LWE_{n,q,χ}**: distinguish (**A**, **As** + **e**) from uniform, for secret **s** and small error **e** ← χ.
- **Module variants** (MSIS, MLWE) replace **Z**_q with the ring *R_q* = **Z**_q[*X*]/(*X^n*+1) and vectors with module elements; the ring structure enables fast NTT-based arithmetic and compact keys, at the cost of additional algebraic structure that must be accounted for in security analysis [3].

### 2.2 Σ-protocols and the Fiat–Shamir transform

A Σ-protocol is a three-move public-coin protocol — commitment *t*, uniform challenge *c*, response *z* — whose special soundness and SHVZK lift, via the Fiat–Shamir transform *c* := *H*(*t*, *m*), to EUF-CMA security of the resulting signature in the ROM.

### 2.3 The leakage problem in the lattice setting

In Schnorr's scheme the mask *r* is uniform, so *z* = *r* + *c*·*x* is uniform independently of *x*. In the lattice analogue the mask **y** is drawn from a bounded distribution *D*, so **z** = **y** + *c*·**s_1** is *D shifted by the secret* — and averaging signatures exposes **s_1**. Lyubashevsky's insight [1]: *abort and resample* whenever the response would be secret-dependent. If the rejection rule's acceptance probability depends only on ‖**z**‖, the conditional distribution of accepted signatures is independent of the secret key, restoring the simulation argument.

### 2.4 The hash-and-sign alternative

GPV take the opposite route [7]. Key generation produces a matrix **A** ∈ **Z**_q^{n×m} together with a *short basis* **T** of the lattice Λ^⊥(**A**) = {**z** : **Az** = **0**}. To sign *m*, hash to a syndrome **u** = *H*(*m*) and use **T** to sample a short preimage **z** with **Az** = **u**, via a discrete-Gaussian sampler oblivious to the geometry of **T**. Verification checks **Az** = *H*(*m*) and ‖**z**‖ ≤ β. The trapdoor **T** is the secret key; its shortness is what makes sampling possible, while the public sees only the "long" basis **A**. Falcon instantiates this blueprint over NTRU lattices with a fast-Fourier sampler [6].

## 3 Methodology: The Fiat–Shamir-with-Aborts Signing Loop

### 3.1 The abstract protocol

Let **A** ∈ *R_q*^{k×ℓ} be public, **s_1** ∈ *S_η*^ℓ, **s_2** ∈ *S_η*^k short secrets (*S_η* = {polynomials with coefficients in [−η, η]}), and **t** = **As_1** + **s_2** the public key. To sign *m*:

1. **Commit.** Sample a masking vector **y** uniformly from *S*_{γ_1−1}^ℓ. Compute **w** = **Ay** ∈ *R_q*^k and extract its *high-order bits* **w_1** = HighBits_q(**w**, 2γ_2).
2. **Challenge.** Set *c* = *H*(μ ‖ **w_1**), where μ is a message digest and *c* ∈ *B*_60, the set of ring elements with exactly 60 coefficients in {−1, 1} and the rest 0 (so ‖*B*_60‖ > 2^256).
3. **Response.** Compute **z** = **y** + *c*·**s_1**.
4. **Rejection.** If ‖**z**‖_∞ ≥ γ_1 − β, restart at step 1. If ‖LowBits_q(**w** − *c*·**s_2**, 2γ_2)‖_∞ ≥ γ_2 − β, restart at step 1 (this guarantees the verifier recomputes the same high bits).
5. **Output.** σ = (**z**, **h**, *c*), where **h** is a small *hint* vector compensating for the dropped low bits of **t**. Verification recomputes **w_1**′ from **Az** − *c*·**t_1**·2^d using the hint, checks ‖**z**‖_∞ < γ_1 − β, and accepts iff *c* = *H*(μ ‖ **w_1**′). Parameters are tuned so the expected number of repetitions is between 4 and 7 [4].

> **Theorem: Rejection-sampling correctness.** *Let* **v** *be distributed as* D_{**v**,σ}^m *and let* **z** *be sampled from* D_σ^m. *Define the acceptance subroutine* 𝒜(**v**, **z**) *that outputs 1 with probability* min(D_σ^m(**z**) / (M·D_{**v**,σ}^m(**z**)), 1). *Then the probability that* 𝒜 *outputs 1 is at least* (1−ε)/M, *and conditioned on output 1 the distribution of* **v** *is within statistical distance* ε/M *of the distribution of* **z**. *In particular, the accepted output is statistically independent of the shift* **v** [1].

The lemma is the engine of the entire paradigm: by choosing the mask distribution *D* wide enough relative to the maximum shift ‖*c*·**s_1**‖_∞, the ratio of densities is bounded, and the accepted signatures are simulatable without the secret key.

### 3.2 Worked illustration: rejection sampling in one dimension

The following Python sketch captures the essence of the loop — sampling a shifted distribution and rejecting until the output is indistinguishable from the unshifted one:

```python
import random

def sample_mask(gamma1):
    # uniform mask y in [-(gamma1-1), gamma1-1], the Dilithium analogue
    return random.randint(-(gamma1 - 1), gamma1 - 1)

def sign_once(secret_shift, gamma1, beta):
    y = sample_mask(gamma1)
    z = y + secret_shift            # response: shifted by the secret
    if abs(z) >= gamma1 - beta:     # rejection rule: independent of secret
        return None                 # abort -> resample
    return z

def sign(secret_shift, gamma1=2**17, beta=60*2, trials=10_000):
    accepted = [z for _ in range(trials)
                if (z := sign_once(secret_shift, gamma1, beta)) is not None]
    mean = sum(accepted) / len(accepted)
    return len(accepted) / trials, mean

for s in (0, 150, -300):
    rate, mean = sign(s)
    print(f"shift={s:>5d}  acceptance={rate:.3f}  mean={mean:+.4f}")
```

Typical output shows acceptance ≈ 1 − 2β/γ_1 and mean ≈ 0 *for every shift*: the abort rule has erased the secret's footprint.

### 3.3 Polynomial-ring arithmetic (the module setting)

Dilithium operates in *R_q* = **Z**_q[*X*]/(*X*^256+1), *q* = 8380417 = 2^23 − 2^13 + 1, chosen so the NTT applies. A minimal sketch of the underlying ring operations:

```python
N, Q = 8, 17  # toy parameters: R = Z_17[X]/(X^8+1)

def add(f, g):  return [(a + b) % Q for a, b in zip(f, g)]
def neg(f):     return [(-a) % Q for a in f]

def mul(f, g):
    # schoolbook product reduced by X^8 + 1  (i.e. X^8 = -1)
    h = [0] * (2 * N - 1)
    for i, a in enumerate(f):
        for j, b in enumerate(g):
            h[i + j] = (h[i + j] + a * b) % Q
    for k in range(2 * N - 2, N - 1, -1):
        h[k - N] = (h[k - N] - h[k]) % Q   # fold X^8 -> -1
    return h[:N]

def inf_norm(f):
    return max(min(a, Q - a) for a in f)

# toy key relation: t = a*s1 + s2  (mod Q)
a  = [3, 1, 4, 1, 5, 9, 2, 6]
s1 = [1, 0, -1, 2, 0, 1, -1, 0]
s2 = [0, 1, 1, -2, 1, 0, 2, -1]
t  = add(mul(a, s1), s2)
print("t =", t, " ||t||_inf =", inf_norm(t))

# signing identity: A z - c t = A y - c s2  (what the verifier exploits)
y  = [2, -3, 1, 0, 4, -1, 2, -2]
c  = [1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, -1]          # sparse challenge, toy B_tau
z  = add(y, mul(c, s1))
lhs = add(mul(a, z), neg(mul(c, t)))
rhs = add(mul(a, y), neg(mul(c, s2)))
print("identity holds:", lhs == rhs)
```

In production the NTT replaces schoolbook multiplication, and ‖*B*_60‖ = 2^60 · C(256,60) > 2^256.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The rejection-sampling lemma, bimodal Gaussians, and why Dilithium samples uniformly

Two instantiations of the rejection-sampling idea dominate practice.

**Uniform variant (Lyubashevsky 2012; Dilithium).** The mask **y** is uniform over *S*_{γ_1−1}^ℓ. Since ‖*c*·**s_1**‖_∞ ≤ β = τ·η, the density ratio on the acceptance region is bounded by 1/(1 − 2β/γ_1), giving acceptance ≈ 1 − 2β/γ_1 per attempt. Choosing γ_1 ≫ β keeps repetitions in the single digits and ‖**z**‖_∞ small enough for the MSIS reduction.

**Bimodal Gaussian variant (Ducas–Durmus–Lepoint–Lyubashevsky, CRYPTO 2013).** The mask is drawn from a discrete Gaussian, and the response is **z** = **y** + (−1)^b·*c*·**s_1** for a random bit *b*. The two modes cancel the shift statistically, yielding acceptance probability 1/M with M = exp(12/α + 1/(2α^2)) for standard deviation σ = α·‖*c*·**s_1**‖. BLISS used this to achieve smaller signatures — but at the cost of *discrete Gaussian sampling*, which is notoriously hard to implement in constant time and was later exploited by cache-timing and fault attacks [10].

Dilithium's decisive engineering choice was to **abandon Gaussians entirely**: uniform sampling over a box is trivially constant-time, needs no floating point, and admits a clean rejection analysis. The price is larger parameters — a trade the designers accepted because simplicity of implementation dominates security arguments in the post-quantum migration [3][4].

### 4.2 CRYSTALS-Dilithium and ML-DSA: from specification to FIPS 204

**Key generation.** A 256-bit seed ρ expands via SHAKE-128 into **A** ∈ *R_q*^{k×ℓ} (NTT form); secrets (**s_1**, **s_2**) ∈ *S_η*^ℓ × *S_η*^k expand via SHAKE-256. The public key publishes ρ and **t_1** = Power2Round_q(**t**, *d*) — the high bits of **t** = **As_1** + **s_2** — roughly halving public-key size at the cost of the hint mechanism [4].

**Signing (deterministic).** The mask **y** derives deterministically as SHAKE-256(*K* ‖ μ ‖ κ) with attempt counter κ (a hedged variant mixes fresh randomness). Determinism removes the RNG-reuse failure mode that plagued early ECDSA deployments.

**Compression engineering.** Three mechanisms keep sizes competitive:

1. *High/low bits decomposition* — only **w_1** enters the hash; the verifier reconstructs it from **Az** − *c*·**t**.
2. *Power2Round* — the public key drops *d* low bits of **t**.
3. *Hints* **h** — a sparse binary vector (at most ω positions) marking where dropping **t_0** would flip a high bit: `MakeHint`/`UseHint` guarantee exact reconstruction of **w_1** [4].

**FIPS 204 parameter sets.** The standard defines three sets, all over the ring with *n* = 256, *q* = 8380417 [5]:

| Parameter set | NIST category | (k, ℓ) | Public key (B) | Secret key (B) | Signature (B) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-DSA-44 | 2 | (4, 4) | 1,312 | 2,560 | 2,420 |
| ML-DSA-65 | 3 | (6, 5) | 1,952 | 4,032 | 3,309 |
| ML-DSA-87 | 5 | (8, 7) | 2,592 | 4,896 | 4,627 |

Higher categories scale the module rank rather than the ring, so an optimized NTT implementation ports across levels almost trivially — a deliberate modularity goal of the designers [3].

### 4.3 Falcon: hash-and-sign over NTRU trapdoors

Falcon takes the GPV route over the NTRU lattice: key generation samples short *f*, *g* and solves *fG* − *gF* = *q* (mod φ); the public key is *h* = *g*·*f*^{−1}, the secret key the short basis ((*g*, −*f*), (*G*, −*F*)). Signing hashes the message to a target and runs **ffSampling** — a recursive fast-Fourier preimage sampler guided by the LDL decomposition ("Falcon tree") of the secret basis's Gram matrix — producing short (*s_1*, *s_2*) with *s_1* + *s_2 h* = *H*(*m*). Verification is one norm check plus one ring multiplication [6].

The trapdoor duality with Section 4.2 is sharp: Falcon's signatures are *half* the size of Dilithium's at comparable security, but signing requires high-precision floating-point Gaussian sampling over the Falcon tree — difficult to make constant-time and to validate. This implementation complexity is the documented reason NIST's FIPS 206 (FN-DSA) standardization lagged FIPS 204/205, remaining a draft pending final clearance as of 2026 [9].

| Parameter set | Claimed NIST level | Public key (B) | Secret key (B) | Signature (B) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Falcon-512 | 1 | 897 | 1,281 | 690 |
| Falcon-1024 | 5 | 1,793 | 2,305 | 1,330 |

### 4.4 Security reductions: the forking lemma to MSIS and MLWE

Security of Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts signatures is proved in two steps [3][4].

*Step 1 — key indistinguishability.* Under MLWE, the public key **t** = **As_1** + **s_2** is indistinguishable from uniform, so the reduction answers signing queries with simulated signatures produced via the rejection-sampling lemma *without* the secret key.

*Step 2 — forking.* Given a forger outputting (**z**, *c*) on a programmed oracle point *c* = *H*(μ ‖ **w_1**), the reduction rewinds to the same query, reprograms the oracle to *c*′ ≠ *c*, and obtains (**z**′, *c*′) with the *same* commitment **w_1**. Since **Az** − *c***t** and **Az**′ − *c*′**t** share high bits, their difference yields a short nonzero kernel vector — an MSIS solution of norm bounded by 2(γ_1 − β) plus challenge terms [3]. Dilithium's proof formalizes this as *self-target MSIS*.

> **Theorem: EUF-CMA security of Dilithium.** *If MLWE_{k,ℓ,η} and MSIS_{k,ℓ,β′} are hard, the Dilithium signature scheme is existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack in the (classical) random oracle model, with a tightness loss linear in the number of signing queries and the forking factor* [3].

QROM analyses (e.g., the modular KLS framework applied to Fiat–Shamir with aborts [2]) extend the argument to quantum adversaries at the cost of an additional square-root-type loss — one reason NIST's categories are defined conservatively [8].

## 5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 Sizes in context

| Scheme | Assumption | Signature | Public key | Classical security |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RSA-3072 | Factoring | 384 B | 387 B | ≈128 bits |
| ECDSA P-256 | ECDLP | 64 B | 64 B | 128 bits |
| ML-DSA-65 | MLWE/MSIS | 3,309 B | 1,952 B | NIST cat. 3 |
| Falcon-512 | NTRU | 690 B | 897 B | NIST cat. 1 |
| SLH-DSA-128s | Hash (SHA-2) | 7,856 B | 32 B | NIST cat. 1 |

Post-quantum signatures are one to two orders of magnitude larger than ECDSA — the central deployment cost of the migration, and the reason Falcon's compactness keeps it relevant despite its implementation difficulty [6][9].

### 5.2 Signing cost

Each signing-loop iteration is dominated by one matrix–vector NTT multiplication plus one SHAKE expansion, so ML-DSA signing completes in well under a millisecond on commodity x86-64 and verification — a single matrix–vector product — is faster still. Falcon verification is similarly cheap (one NTRU multiplication), while Falcon *signing* pays for the floating-point ffSampling tree traversal.

### 5.3 Conservative security estimates

The Dilithium specification analyzes primal/dual BKZ attacks from a deliberately adversary-favorable viewpoint and reports conservative core-SVP estimates of approximately 123/112, 182/171, and 252/236 classical/quantum bits for the three parameter sets [4]. Falcon-512 targets 128-bit classical security via the NTRU and SIS assumptions over the ring with *q* = 12289 [6].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Floating-point fragility (Falcon).** The Falcon tree's floating-point values must stay within tight bounds; deviations bias signatures and have enabled key-recovery attacks — the documented reason FIPS 206 trailed the other standards [9].
2. **Fault attacks on the abort loop.** Espitau *et al.* showed that faulting the rejection loop yields full key recovery from a single faulty signature [10]; the abort mechanism becomes an attack surface in the physical model, requiring redundant checks or infective computation.
3. **QROM tightness loss.** The forking-based reduction loses a factor related to the number of oracle queries; quantum analyses lose more [2]. Concrete parameters compensate with margin rather than tight proofs.
4. **Size and protocol ossification.** Multi-kilobyte signatures break assumptions baked into X.509, DNSSEC, and embedded TLS stacks [8].
5. **Structured assumptions.** Module and NTRU lattices carry extra algebraic structure; no attack exploits it at the standardized parameters, but the assumption is strictly stronger than worst-case SVP on general lattices [7].
6. **Determinism vs. side channels.** Deterministic signing makes the computation a function of (*sk*, *m*) alone, sharpening DPA-style averaging attacks; the hedged variant trades this against RNG dependence.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Fiat–Shamir with aborts resolved the central obstruction to lattice Schnorr-like signatures — secret-dependent responses — with one implementable idea: *restart rather than leak*. The simulation lemma (§3) turns a leaky Σ-protocol into a simulatable one; the forking lemma turns a forger into a Module-SIS solver. The uniform-sampling instantiation of this program became CRYSTALS-Dilithium and, through NIST's standardization process, FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) [5][8]; the parallel hash-and-sign lineage, refined through GPV trapdoors into Falcon's fast-Fourier NTRU sampler, became the FIPS 206 (FN-DSA) track [6][9].

The two standards embody an engineering trade-off: ML-DSA buys *implementation simplicity* — no Gaussians, no floating point — at the cost of larger signatures; FN-DSA buys *compactness* at the cost of a treacherous floating-point sampler. Open problems persist on both sides: tighter QROM reductions [2], floating-point-free constant-time Gaussian sampling, and fault-resistant abort handling [10].
