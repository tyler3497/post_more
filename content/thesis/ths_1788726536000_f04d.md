# Privacy Accounting for Deep Learning: Rényi Differential Privacy Composition, DP-SGD Mechanics, and Empirical Privacy Auditing

## Abstract

Differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) is the workhorse of privacy-preserving deep learning, yet its end-to-end privacy guarantee rests on a sophisticated accounting apparatus that converts thousands of noisy gradient steps into a single *(ε, δ)*-differential privacy statement [1]. This thesis develops the full accounting pipeline from first principles: the Rényi differential privacy (RDP) framework of Mironov [2], whose Rényi-divergence-based relaxation composes additively and converts tightly to approximate DP; the moments accountant of Abadi et al. [1], now understood as the special case of RDP applied to subsampled Gaussian mechanisms; privacy amplification by subsampling and its tight RDP analysis [3]; and the modern numerical accountants — privacy loss distributions, characteristic-function methods, and Fourier accountants — that squeeze the remaining slack from the analysis [4]. We then turn from theory to practice: the mechanics of DP-SGD (per-sample clipping, noise calibration, batch-size effects), recent results on fixed-size versus Poisson minibatching [5], and empirical privacy auditing, where membership-inference attacks furnish rigorous *lower* bounds on privacy leakage that complement analytic upper bounds [6]. The synthesis is a practitioner's map of the privacy–utility–computation frontier in private deep learning.

## 1 Introduction

Deep neural networks memorize. Trained on sensitive data — medical records, keystrokes, faces — they can regurgitate training examples when probed, a phenomenon now documented across language models, diffusion models, and classifiers. **Differential privacy (DP)** offers the gold-standard formalization of the promise that *one individual's data barely changes the model's behavior*, but naïvely applying DP to deep learning fails: the sensitivity of a gradient step is unbounded, and composing thousands of steps with classical composition theorems yields vacuous guarantees.

The breakthrough of Abadi et al. [1] was twofold — algorithmic and analytic. Algorithmically, **DP-SGD** modifies stochastic gradient descent in three minimal ways: compute *per-sample* gradients, **clip** each to a fixed ℓ₂ norm *C*, and add calibrated Gaussian noise to the averaged update. Analytically, the **moments accountant** tracks not a single privacy parameter but the entire moment-generating function of the privacy loss random variable, composing it across steps and converting to *(ε, δ)*-DP only at the end. The result: single-digit ε on MNIST and CIFAR-10 with manageable accuracy loss — the first demonstration that private deep learning could be practical.

In the decade since, the moments accountant has been subsumed and generalized by **Rényi differential privacy (RDP)** [2], a relaxation based on the Rényi divergence of order *α* that composes by simple addition and admits a tight conversion to *(ε, δ)*-DP. RDP is now the lingua franca of privacy accounting, implemented in Opacus, TensorFlow Privacy, autodp, and JAX-Privacy. Parallel developments tightened every component: subsampled RDP bounds [3], numerical privacy-loss-distribution (PLD) accountants that are essentially exact [4], and refined analyses of batching schemes that expose the privacy cost of implementation choices previously treated as irrelevant [5].

Yet a guarantee is only as trustworthy as its verification. **Empirical privacy auditing** closes the loop: by mounting the strongest available membership-inference attacks — often with adversarially crafted "canary" examples — auditors derive *lower bounds* on ε that can expose bugs in accounting code or looseness in the analysis [6]. The gap between ε_theory (analytic upper bound) and ε_emp (audited lower bound) is where the science now lives.

> **Thesis claim.** Privacy accounting is not bookkeeping — it is the central scientific instrument of private deep learning. Understanding RDP composition, amplification, numerical accounting, and empirical auditing as one integrated pipeline is prerequisite to training models whose privacy claims survive contact with adversaries.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Differential privacy, briefly

A randomized mechanism *M* satisfies *(ε, δ)*-DP if for all neighboring datasets *D, D′* (differing in one record) and all measurable *S*:

$$\Pr[M(D) \in S] \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[M(D') \in S] + \delta.$$

The **privacy loss random variable** at output *o* is *L(o) = ln(Pr[M(D)=o] / Pr[M(D′)=o])*. Pure DP bounds |L| ≤ ε pointwise; approximate DP allows the bound to fail with probability δ. Composition theorems bound the privacy loss of *M₁ × … × M_T*; classical **strong composition** gives ε growing as *O(√(T log(1/δ′)))*, already a vast improvement over the linear *O(T)* of basic composition — but still loose for *T* in the thousands.

### 2.2 The Gaussian mechanism

For a function *f* with ℓ₂-sensitivity *Δ₂ = max_{D∼D′} ‖f(D) − f(D′)‖₂*, releasing *f(D) + N(0, σ²Δ₂²I)* satisfies *(ε, δ)*-DP with *σ ≈ √(2 ln(1.25/δ))/ε* in the classical calibration. Its RDP curve is exactly *ε(α) = α/(2σ²)* for all *α > 1* — a straight line, the simplest possible [2]. This closed form is why the Gaussian mechanism dominates private deep learning: Laplace noise composes poorly under RDP and has heavier tails.

### 2.3 Why deep learning breaks naïve accounting

Three facts conspire against classical DP in deep learning:

1. **Unbounded sensitivity.** Gradients of neural losses have no a priori norm bound; without clipping, *Δ₂ = ∞*.
2. **Adaptive composition.** Each step's mechanism depends on previous outputs (the evolving parameters); the accountant must handle *adaptive* composition, where the choice of *M_t* can depend on *o₁…o_{t−1}*.
3. **Scale.** Modern training runs *T = 10³–10⁶* steps. Basic composition gives ε in the hundreds; even strong composition struggles. The accountant must exploit the *structure* of the subsampled Gaussian mechanism, not treat steps as black boxes.

### 2.4 Threat models

The standard DP-SGD analysis grants the adversary extraordinary power: observation of **all intermediate gradients** (equivalently, all checkpoints), knowledge of all but one record, and arbitrary auxiliary information. Auditing literature distinguishes this from realistic adversaries who see only the final model [6] — the gap between the two threat models explains much of the ε_theory vs. ε_emp discrepancy and motivates *hidden-state* analyses that model privacy amplification from unobserved intermediate steps.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is **analytic reconstruction**: we derive the accounting pipeline step by step, showing how each layer removes slack from the previous one, and we quantify the remaining gaps numerically.

### 3.1 Rényi differential privacy

Mironov's definition [2]: *M* satisfies *(α, ε)*-RDP for *α > 1* if for all neighboring *D, D′*,

$$D_\alpha(M(D)\,\|\,M(D')) := \frac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln \mathbb{E}_{o \sim M(D')}\left[\left(\frac{\Pr[M(D)=o]}{\Pr[M(D')=o]}\right)^{\!\!\alpha}\right] \le \varepsilon.$$

Three properties make RDP the ideal accounting language:

- **Additive composition.** If *M₁* is *(α, ε₁)*-RDP and *M₂* is *(α, ε₂)*-RDP (adaptively), the composition is *(α, ε₁ + ε₂)*-RDP. No square roots, no δ′ splitting — just addition, at every order *α* simultaneously.
- **Tight conversion.** *(α, ε)*-RDP implies *(ε + ln(1/δ)/(α−1), δ)*-DP for any *δ ∈ (0,1)*; optimizing over a grid of *α* values yields the tightest *(ε, δ)* pair. The conversion recovers advanced composition as a special case and improves on it substantially.
- **Subsampling amplification in closed form.** For Poisson subsampling at rate *q*, the RDP of the subsampled Gaussian mechanism admits the bound *ε(α) ≤ (1/(α−1)) ln(1 + q²·C(α,2)·(e^{ε₀(2)}−1) + …)*, a series in *q* whose leading term scales as *q²* — the *quadratic* amplification that makes DP-SGD viable [3].

> **Theorem (Mironov [2], composition).** *Let M₁, …, M_T be adaptively composed mechanisms, each (α, ε_t)-RDP. Then the composition is (α, Σ_t ε_t)-RDP.*

### 3.2 The moments accountant as subsampled RDP

Abadi et al.'s moments accountant [1] tracks *α_M(λ) = max_{D,D′} ln E[exp(λ·L)]*, the log-MGF of the privacy loss. Observe that *α_M(λ) = λ·ε_RDP(λ+1)*: the moments accountant **is** RDP accounting at integer orders, specialized to the subsampled Gaussian mechanism with a particular tail bound. Recognizing this identity unified a fragmented literature — every "moments accountant" implementation is an RDP accountant, and improvements to RDP (better subsampling bounds, numerical conversion) transfer automatically.

### 3.3 Numerical accountants: PLD and characteristic functions

RDP conversion to *(ε, δ)*-DP loses a small but measurable amount of tightness: the RDP curve does not losslessly encode the privacy profile *δ(ε)*. The **privacy loss distribution (PLD)** formalism [4] works directly with the distribution of *L*, composing via convolution (implemented with FFTs) and computing *δ(ε) = E[(1 − e^{ε−L})₊]* exactly up to discretization error. The **Analytical Fourier Accountant** and **characteristic-function** approaches [4] evaluate the same quantities in Fourier space, avoiding discretization artifacts. For DP-SGD with *T* steps, PLD accountants typically shave 10–30% off the reported ε relative to RDP — the difference between ε = 8 and ε = 6, which matters enormously for deployment thresholds.

### 3.4 Empirical auditing methodology

Auditing inverts the analysis: fix a mechanism *M* and a threat model, then construct the strongest membership-inference adversary and convert its *(TPR, FPR)* into a lower bound *ε_emp ≥ ln(TPR/FPR)* [6]. Modern audits use **canaries** — worst-case crafted examples, e.g., mislabeled points or gradient-aligned poison — inserted into training, then test whether the final model (or a sequence of checkpoints) reveals their presence. A tight audit (*ε_emp ≈ ε_theory*) certifies the analysis; a loose one (*ε_emp ≪ ε_theory*) signals either analytic slack or a weak attack, and distinguishing the two is an active research program.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 DP-SGD mechanics: clipping, noise, and the per-sample bottleneck

The DP-SGD update [1] is deceptively simple:

```python
def dpsgd_step(params, batch, C, sigma, lr):
    # 1. per-sample gradients (the expensive part)
    per_sample_grads = [grad(loss(params, x), params) for x in batch]
    # 2. clip each to l2-norm C  ->  sensitivity is now bounded by C
    clipped = [g * min(1.0, C / l2norm(g)) for g in per_sample_grads]
    # 3. aggregate + Gaussian noise scaled to sensitivity C
    noisy_grad = mean(clipped) + normal(0, (sigma * C / len(batch))**2)
    # 4. ordinary descent on the privatized gradient
    return params - lr * noisy_grad
```

Each design choice carries a deep rationale:

- **Per-sample clipping** (not batch clipping) is what bounds sensitivity: replacing one record changes the summed clipped gradients by at most *C* in ℓ₂ norm, regardless of the loss landscape. Batch clipping would let a single outlier dominate. The cost is computational — per-sample gradients defeat the vectorization that makes backprop fast, though "ghost clipping" and bookkeeping tricks (as in Opacus and recent JAX implementations) have narrowed the gap to ~1.3× overhead.
- **The clipping norm *C*** is a bias–variance knob, not just a privacy parameter. Small *C* discards gradient signal (bias toward zero for large gradients); large *C* demands more noise (variance). The folk wisdom — *C* in [0.1, 1.0] with learning rate scaled up — reflects that DP-SGD operates in a high-noise regime where the signal-to-noise ratio, not the raw gradient magnitude, governs convergence.
- **Noise multiplier *σ*** is the true privacy currency. RDP accounting maps *(σ, q, T)* → *(ε, δ)*; practitioners invert this map, choosing σ to hit a target ε. Doubling σ halves ε (roughly) but can collapse accuracy on hard tasks — the privacy–utility frontier is steepest exactly where practitioners want to operate.

### 4.2 Amplification by subsampling: why the batch scheme matters

Privacy amplification by subsampling is the engine of DP-SGD's feasibility: each step touches only a *q*-fraction of the data, so each record participates rarely, and the per-step privacy cost scales as *q²* rather than *q* under RDP [3]. But the *scheme* matters enormously:

| Batching scheme | RDP amplification | Practical notes |
|---|---|---|
| Poisson subsampling (each record included w.p. *q* independently) | Tightest known bounds; *O(q²)* leading term [3] | Variable batch sizes complicate systems; the analyzed scheme |
| Fixed-size shuffling (random partition each epoch) | Slightly weaker guarantees; recent work tightens them [5] | What everyone actually implements; constant memory |
| Fixed-size without replacement, single shuffle | Weaker; participation patterns correlate across epochs | Common bug: reusing one shuffle leaks more than analyzed |

Recent work [5] derived tighter RDP guarantees for fixed-size minibatches — with *and* without replacement — and showed empirically that fixed-size batches exhibit **lower gradient variance** than Poisson sampling in addition to their systems advantages. The lesson generalizes: *the accountant must match the implementation*, and mismatches (analyzing Poisson while implementing shuffling) were for years a quiet source of invalid ε claims. Modern libraries (Opacus ≥ 1.0, JAX-Privacy) now ship accountants parameterized by the actual batching scheme.

```rust
// RDP composition for T steps of subsampled Gaussian, pseudocode
fn rdp_dpsgd(sigma: f64, q: f64, steps: usize, orders: &[f64]) -> Vec<f64> {
    orders.iter().map(|&alpha| {
        let per_step = rdp_subsampled_gaussian(alpha, sigma, q); // Wang–Balle–Kasi bound [3]
        per_step * steps as f64                                   // additive composition [2]
    }).collect()
}
// convert: eps(delta) = min_alpha rdp[alpha] + ln(1/delta)/(alpha-1)
```

### 4.3 Tight composition and the conversion frontier

Given per-step RDP curves, three operations remain: compose (addition), convert to *(ε, δ)* (optimize over *α*), and choose hyperparameters. The conversion *ε(δ) = min_α [ε_RDP(α) + ln(1/δ)/(α−1)]* is where RDP beats the moments accountant's original tail bound: the original analysis fixed integer orders and a specific conversion, while the RDP view optimizes over a dense grid of real orders, typically cutting 20–40% off the reported ε for the same *(σ, q, T)*.

Numerical accountants [4] go further by abandoning RDP as an intermediate representation. The PLD of one DP-SGD step is a mixture — with probability *1−q* the record is absent (loss 0) and with probability *q* it contributes a Gaussian-shifted loss — and the *T*-step PLD is the *T*-fold convolution, computed via FFT in *O(T log T)*. The resulting *(ε, δ)* pairs are exact up to floating-point error, exposing the residual slack in RDP conversion (typically a few percent) and, more importantly, giving a **ground truth** against which analytic bounds are validated.

### 4.4 Hyperparameter tuning under privacy

A subtle privacy leak: selecting hyperparameters by training *k* candidate models and picking the best consumes privacy budget beyond the *k* runs themselves — the *selection* is data-dependent. The naïve fix (split the budget *k* ways) is ruinous; the RDP analysis of tuning [6] shows the additional cost grows only as *O(log k)* via a careful "report noisy argmax with candidate subsampling" construction. In practice, most practitioners tune non-privately on public proxy data or absorb the cost into a slightly larger ε — both defensible only when stated explicitly, since silent non-private tuning voids the headline guarantee.

### 4.5 Empirical auditing: lower bounds that bite

The auditing program [6] has matured from bug-hunting to a quantitative science:

- **Single-run auditing** ("Privacy Auditing with One Training Run") shows that one training run with many canaries can yield statistically valid lower bounds, replacing the *thousands* of retrainings early audits required — making auditing computationally feasible as a CI check.
- **Tightness results** (Nasr et al.) demonstrate that for DP-SGD, the standard RDP analysis is *tight* in its threat model: there exist adversaries (with crafted canaries and checkpoint access) achieving *ε_emp ≈ ε_theory*. The analysis is not loose — the threat model is strong.
- **The hidden-state gap.** When the adversary sees only the final model, *ε_emp* is often **orders of magnitude** below *ε_theory*. This is not a contradiction: the theory bounds a stronger adversary. But it raises the field's central open question — can we get *formal* guarantees for the realistic hidden-state threat model? Early results on "privacy amplification by iteration" suggest yes, under convexity or with additional noise, but general non-convex accounting remains open.

> **Theorem (audit lower bound [6]).** *If a membership-inference adversary achieves true positive rate TPR at false positive rate FPR against mechanism M, then M cannot satisfy ε-DP for any ε < ln(TPR/FPR) (up to statistical confidence corrections).*

Auditing thus provides the empirical counterpart to accounting's analytic upper bounds: together they bracket the true privacy leakage, and a mature deployment reports both.

## 5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

### The privacy–utility frontier (representative, δ = 10⁻⁵)

| Setting | σ | ε (RDP) | ε (PLD) | Accuracy (CIFAR-10, from-scratch CNN) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Non-private baseline | 0 | ∞ | ∞ | ~72% |
| Modest privacy | 0.8 | ~8.2 | ~7.4 | ~63% |
| Strong privacy | 1.5 | ~3.1 | ~2.8 | ~55% |
| Very strong | 3.0 | ~1.0 | ~0.9 | ~44% |

*(Illustrative values for q = 4096/50000, T ≈ 2000 steps; exact numbers depend on architecture and tuning.)* Two patterns dominate: (i) PLD accounting consistently improves on RDP by ~10%, and (ii) the accuracy cost is concentrated at small ε — moving from ε = 8 to ε = 3 costs far more accuracy than moving from ε = 3 to ε = 1 costs privacy. Pre-training on public data followed by private fine-tuning shifts the entire frontier outward and is now the default recipe for usable private models.

### Theoretical guarantees, summarized

> **Theorem (Abadi et al. [1]).** *T steps of DP-SGD with clipping norm C, noise multiplier σ, and Poisson sampling rate q satisfy (O(qε₀√(T log(1/δ))), δ)-DP, where ε₀ is the per-step guarantee — with the moments accountant giving the tight constant.*

> **Theorem (Mironov [2]).** *RDP composes additively at every order α simultaneously and converts to (ε + ln(1/δ)/(α−1), δ)-DP; optimizing over α recovers and improves advanced composition.*

> **Theorem (Wang–Balle–Kasiviswanathan [3]).** *The subsampled mechanism's RDP is bounded in terms of the base mechanism's RDP and q, with leading term quadratic in q — generalizing the moments accountant beyond Gaussians.*

> **Theorem (tightness, Nasr et al. [6]).** *There exist adversaries attaining ε_emp matching the RDP upper bound for DP-SGD, proving the standard analysis tight in its threat model.*

## 6 Limitations

**The threat-model gap.** The headline ε bounds a near-omnipotent adversary; realistic adversaries achieve far less leakage. Until hidden-state accounting matures, practitioners must choose between rigorous-but-pessimistic guarantees and realistic-but-informal ones — and most deployment decisions quietly assume the latter while reporting the former.

**Utility at scale.** DP-SGD's accuracy penalty grows with model size and task difficulty. On ImageNet-scale tasks from scratch, single-digit ε remains accuracy-prohibitive; the field's progress has come largely from public pre-training, which moves the privacy boundary rather than dissolving it — and raises the question of what "public" means when pre-training data is scraped.

**Hyperparameters and the tuning loophole.** Clipping norm, noise multiplier, learning rate, batch size, and training duration all interact with privacy in ways the accountant captures only if the tuning process itself is accounted for. The literature's reported frontiers often reflect extensive non-private tuning, overstating what a fully honest accounting would permit.

**Implementation fragility.** Privacy is a property of the *code*, not the paper. Floating-point leakage in noise samplers, insecure randomness, batching-scheme mismatches, and incorrect δ choices (δ must be *o(1/n)*, yet *δ = 10⁻⁵* is routinely used with *n < 10⁵*) have all produced published ε values that the actual implementation did not satisfy. Auditing catches some of these; code review and verified implementations must catch the rest.

**RDP's blind spots.** RDP cannot express all *(ε, δ)*-DP guarantees — some mechanisms have finite *(ε, δ)*-DP but infinite RDP at every order — and RDP conversion is not lossless. The PLD/characteristic-function accountants [4] address this but at greater implementation complexity, and they remain less widely deployed than RDP.

**Auditing lower bounds are attack-dependent.** *ε_emp ≪ ε_theory* proves nothing about the system's safety — only that the *tested* attacks failed. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, and the history of privacy is littered with "provably safe" systems broken by the next attack.

## 7 Conclusion

Privacy accounting for deep learning has traveled from the moments accountant's clever tail bound [1] to a mature engineering discipline: RDP's additive composition [2], tight subsampled bounds [3], numerically exact PLD accountants [4], batching-aware analyses [5], and empirical auditing that grounds theory in adversarial reality [6]. The pipeline — clip, noise, subsample, compose, convert, audit — is now standard equipment, implemented in every major DP library and increasingly required by regulators and review boards.

The frontier has shifted accordingly. The pressing questions are no longer "how do we account for composition?" but: *can we account tightly for the adversaries we actually face?* *can private training match non-private utility without public pre-training?* and *can auditing become a routine, automated part of the ML lifecycle rather than a research project?* Answering them will require the same blend of probability theory, numerical analysis, and adversarial thinking that built the accounting pipeline — applied now to the gap between the guarantee we can prove and the privacy we actually need.
