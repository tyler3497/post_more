# DRAM Disturbance Attacks and Defenses: Rowhammer, RowPress, and Half-Double Bit Flips with TRR, RFM, and PARA Mitigations in DDR5

## Abstract

## 1. Introduction

Modern computing rests on an uncomfortable foundation: the DRAM cells holding our most sensitive data are analog capacitors that leak charge, sit nanometers apart, and interact electrically with every access their neighbors receive. The discovery that repeatedly opening and closing a DRAM row — *hammering* — could flip bits in adjacent rows without ever touching them [6] shattered the assumption that memory isolation is a purely logical property. Attackers have since used disturbance-induced bit flips to escalate privileges, escape browser sandboxes, breach virtual-machine isolation, and extract cryptographic keys [4][5].

The first generation of the threat was conceptually simple. A DRAM bank is organized as rows of cells connected by wordlines; activating a row (*ACT*) copies its contents into the row buffer (sense amplifiers), and precharging (*PRE*) writes the data back and closes the row. Rapidly alternating ACT/PRE on an *aggressor* row perturbs neighboring *victim* rows through wordline coupling, bitline precharge disturbance, and passing-wordline leakage [6]. The minimum number of activations required to induce the first bit flip — the *Rowhammer threshold* $T_{RH}$ (equivalently $HC_{first}$ or $AC_{min}$) — has fallen relentlessly with technology scaling: from roughly 139,000 in DDR3, to 10,000 in DDR4, 4,800 in LPDDR4 [7], and lower still in DDR5. Every halving of $T_{RH}$ doubles the attacker's margin and halves the defender's reaction time.

This thesis makes three contributions. First, a unified physical and architectural model of DRAM disturbance that explains Rowhammer, Half-Double, and RowPress as manifestations of a single charge-redistribution phenomenon with different temporal signatures. Second, a taxonomy and formal analysis of the defense space — probabilistic refresh (PARA), deterministic counter-based tracking (Graphene), randomized in-DRAM samplers (PROTEAS), DDR5's controller-coordinated RFM and PRAC, and ECC — with quantitative security guarantees and failure modes. Third, a synthesis of empirical evidence across hundreds of tested chips into design guidance for disturbance-resilient memory systems.

> **Thesis statement:** No single in-DRAM heuristic can close the disturbance channel; only the composition of exact activation accounting (PRAC/Graphene-class tracking), probabilistic refresh (PARA), and controller-driven refresh management (RFM) — layered above ECC treated as a reliability mechanism rather than a security boundary — achieves a quantifiable, future-proof integrity guarantee as $T_{RH}$ continues to fall.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 DRAM Organization and the Access Protocol

A DRAM chip stores each bit in a one-transistor–one-capacitor (1T1C) cell: charge on the capacitor encodes the logical value, and the access transistor connects the capacitor to a bitline when the row's wordline is raised. Cells are arranged in banks; each bank contains tens of thousands of rows, and all cells in a row share a wordline. A memory access proceeds in three phases:

1. **Activation (ACT):** the wordline is driven high, cell charge is shared onto the bitlines, and the sense amplifiers (the *row buffer*) latch the full row — typically 8 KB.
2. **Read/Write (RD/WR):** column commands move data between the row buffer and the bus.
3. **Precharge (PRE):** the row buffer contents are written back into the cells, the wordline is lowered, and the bitlines are equalized to $V_{DD}/2$.

Key timing parameters govern the attacker's cadence. The row-cycle time $t_{RC}$ ($\approx$ 45–55 ns in DDR4) is the minimum interval between successive activations of the same bank; the minimum row-open time $t_{RAS}$ ($\approx$ 36 ns) is how long a row must stay open before precharge. Periodic refresh arrives every $t_{REFI}$ = 7.8 $\mu$s, and every cell must be refreshed within $t_{REFW}$ = 64 ms. An attacker can issue at most one ACT per $t_{RC}$ per bank, so the maximum hammer count per refresh window is $t_{REFW}/t_{RC} \approx 1.4 \times 10^6$ — comfortably above modern $T_{RH}$ values, which is precisely why the threat is practical.

### 2.2 The Disturbance Mechanism

When a wordline toggles, it capacitively couples to neighboring wordlines and to the bitlines of adjacent rows. Three coupled effects dominate [6][1]:

- **Wordline-to-wordline coupling:** the aggressor wordline's voltage swing injects charge into the victim wordline, partially turning on victim access transistors and leaking cell charge.
- **Passing-wordline leakage:** in folded-bitline architectures, wordlines pass *over* cells of neighboring rows, modulating those cells' leakage even without direct adjacency.
- **Bitline/sense-amplifier noise:** repeated activation disturbs the equalization voltage of shared bitline pairs, biasing victim-cell sensing — which is why flips are strongly *data-pattern dependent* (the RowStripe pattern maximizes flips) [6].

Crucially, disturbance is cumulative but *nonlinear*: each activation deposits a small perturbation, and a flip occurs when the accumulated charge loss crosses the sensing margin. Refresh resets the accumulation, so the security game is a race between the attacker's activation rate and the defender's mitigation rate within each $t_{REFW}$.

### 2.3 Classical Attack Patterns

- **Single-sided hammering** repeatedly activates one aggressor row adjacent to the victim.
- **Double-sided hammering** sandwiches the victim between two alternately hammered aggressors, roughly doubling the coupling [6].
- **One-location hammering** re-opens the same row without touching neighbors, evading detectors keyed on multi-row patterns.

The canonical analysis assumed the victim is always at distance one. Half-Double [4] and RowPress [1] demolished that assumption (Section 4).

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis follows a three-pronged methodology: (i) physical modeling of charge-transfer disturbance as a stochastic accumulation process; (ii) formal analysis of defense guarantees using probabilistic bounds and streaming-algorithm theory; and (iii) synthesis of published empirical measurements — 164 DDR4 chips characterized for RowPress [1], 40 DDR4 DIMMs fuzzed for Blacksmith [3], 42 DDR4 modules tested for TRRespass [2], and DDR5 data for PRAC/RFM-class mitigations [7] — into comparative quantitative tables.

> **Theorem: (Disturbance accumulation).** *Let each activation of an aggressor row transfer a random disturbance charge $\Delta \sim \mathcal{D}$ to a victim cell with initial sensing margin $M$. If refresh resets the accumulation every $t_{REFW}$ and activations arrive at rate at most $1/t_{RC}$, then the flip probability within one window is $\Pr[\sum_{i=1}^{N} \Delta_i > M]$ with $N \le t_{REFW}/t_{RC}$. Any mitigation that guarantees $\Pr[\text{flip}] < \epsilon$ must therefore either bound $N$ below $T_{RH}$ for every row or bound the tail of $\mathcal{D}$.*

> **Proof:** Directly from the accumulation model: a flip requires the cumulative perturbation to exceed the sensing margin before the next refresh restores the cell. Bounding $N$ (activation counting, as in PRAC/Graphene) or stochastically resetting the accumulation early (PARA's probabilistic refresh) are the only two structural options; in-DRAM heuristics like TRR attempt to approximate the former with bounded state, which is exactly where their bypasses arise. ∎

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Evolution of Aggressor Geometry: From Double-Sided to Many-Sided and Half-Double

Blacksmith [3] attacked the model's *temporal* assumptions. Its key finding: TRR samplers observe the activation stream only at deterministic instants within each $t_{REFI}$ window, so patterns whose energy falls *between* sampling instants are invisible. Blacksmith parameterizes patterns in the frequency domain — *frequency* (how often an aggressor is hit), *phase* (where hits land relative to $t_{REFI}$), and *amplitude* (how many aggressors participate) — and fuzzes this space automatically: bit flips on all 40 tested DDR4 DIMMs, 2.6× more modules than the prior state of the art, and on average 87× more flips per module [3]. The lesson is general: *any deterministic in-DRAM policy creates a blind spot that a fuzzer can find automatically*.

Half-Double [4], discovered by Google researchers, exploited the mitigation *mechanism itself*. Given three consecutive rows $A$, $B$, $C$, hammering the far row $A$ heavily and the near row $B$ only a few dozen times flips bits in $C$ — a victim at distance *two* from the primary aggressor. The mechanism: TRR detects the hammering of $A$ and issues a targeted refresh of $B$; that refresh itself activates $B$'s wordline, and the refresh-induced activation of $B$ couples into $C$. The defense's own mitigation becomes the near aggressor — confirmed by experiments simulating TRR refreshes with explicit accesses to $B$ [4]. Half-Double invalidates the distance-one victim model baked into every TRR generation and forces mitigations to consider *blast radius*.

```python
# Conceptual model: Half-Double disturbance accounting
def half_double_disturbance(far_hammers, near_hammers, trr_refreshes_of_B):
    # A's hammering disturbs B mildly; TRR refreshes of B act as extra
    # near-aggressor activations against C (distance-2 victim).
    effective_near = near_hammers + trr_refreshes_of_B
    return coupling(far_hammers, distance=2) + coupling(effective_near, distance=1)
```

### 4.2 RowPress: The Temporal Axis of Disturbance

Until 2023, every attack and defense in the literature counted *activations*. RowPress [1] showed that the *duration* an aggressor row remains open — $t_{AggON}$ — is an independent and far more potent disturbance axis. Across 164 commodity DDR4 chips from all three major manufacturers, increasing $t_{AggON}$ from the Rowhammer-minimum $t_{RAS}$ = 36 ns to the refresh interval 7.8 $\mu$s reduces the minimum activation count to first flip ($AC_{min}$) by **17.6× on average (up to 40.7×)**; at 70.2 $\mu$s (the JEDEC maximum row-open time) the reduction is **159.4× on average (up to 363.8×)**; and at $t_{AggON}$ = 30 ms, bit flips occur with **a single activation** [1].

Physically, RowPress is consistent with prolonged wordline voltage stress: while the wordline is held high, the passing-wordline field continuously biases neighboring cells' leakage, and the victim's storage capacitor drains steadily rather than in per-activation quanta. It affects a different set of cells than Rowhammer, responds differently to temperature, and defeats Rowhammer-protected systems: the authors demonstrate a user-level program inducing bit flips on a protected DDR4 system, and show that adaptive row policies (keeping rows open longer on row-buffer hits) *facilitate* the attack [1].

### 4.3 In-DRAM Tracking: TRR, DSAC, and PROTEAS

The in-DRAM defense lineage is a study in the cost of determinism. Classical TRR combines a *sampler* (which rows look hot?) with a *tracker* (a small table of candidate aggressors) and a *mitigation policy* (which tracked row's neighbors get refreshed at each REF). Reverse-engineering studies converge on tables holding on the order of 8–32 entries per bank — orders of magnitude fewer than the thousands of rows an attacker can touch per $t_{REFW}$. TRRespass overflows the table; Blacksmith dodges the sampler; Half-Double poisons the mitigation.

| Defense | State per bank | Policy | Reported max disturbance | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Classical TRR | ~8–32 entries, deterministic | Sample + targeted refresh | Broken by TRRespass/Blacksmith [2][3] | Deterministic blind spots |
| DSAC | Stochastic counters | Approximate counting | Improved vs. TRR | Vendor-proprietary, partial data |
| PROTEAS | 16 counters, PRNG | Randomized insert/evict | ≤ 2.1K acts [9] | Needs secret seed management |

### 4.4 Probabilistic and Counter-Based Defenses: PARA and Graphene

**PARA** (Probabilistic Adjacent Row Activation), proposed in the original Rowhammer paper [6], is disarmingly simple: every time the memory controller closes a row, it refreshes each adjacent row with independent probability $p$. No tracking state, no tables, no vendor secrets — and a genuine probabilistic guarantee:

> **Theorem: (PARA security bound).** *With per-close refresh probability $p$, the probability that an attacker achieves $T_{RH}$ consecutive unmitigated aggressor activations is at most $(1-p)^{T_{RH}}$. For $p = 0.001$ and $T_{RH} = 10^4$, this is below $10^{-4}$ per attack window, and the expected performance overhead is $2p$ extra refreshes per row close — negligible.*

> **Proof:** Each hammer is an independent trial; the attack succeeds only if all $T_{RH}$ trials "miss" the probabilistic refresh. Independence gives $(1-p)^{T_{RH}} \le e^{-pT_{RH}}$. ∎

PARA's elegance is also its limitation: it performs mitigations even when no attack is underway (wasted energy), and its guarantee degrades linearly as $T_{RH}$ falls — at $T_{RH} = 1000$, $p$ must rise tenfold for the same bound. Still, PARA-class sampling remains attractive precisely because it needs *zero* storage [7].

```haskell
-- Misra-Gries sketch: the heart of Graphene-style tracking
mgUpdate :: Int -> Row -> Table -> Table
mgUpdate k row table =
  case lookup row table of
    Just c  -> insert row (c+1) table          -- hit: increment
    Nothing -> if size table < k
               then insert row 1 table         -- miss, space: admit
               else decrementAll table         -- miss, full: age everything
-- Guarantee: any row with frequency > N/(k+1) survives in the table.
```

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 The Falling Threshold

| Memory generation | Representative $T_{RH}$ ($HC_{first}$) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| DDR3 | ~139,000 | Kim et al. [6] via [7] |
| DDR4 | ~10,000 | FPGA characterization [7] |

| DDR5 (early) | < 10,000, trending down | Fleet measurements [7] |

The trend is monotonic and shows no sign of saturating: each process shrink packs cells tighter, strengthening inter-cell coupling. Defenses parameterized for today's $T_{RH}$ are depreciating assets — a defense must be *threshold-agile*, which favors PRAC (programmable threshold) and PARA (tunable $p$) over fixed-table TRR.

### 5.3 Defense Cost–Security Trade-off

Formally, let $C$ be the state cost (counters/bits), $O$ the performance overhead, and $\epsilon$ the residual flip probability per $t_{REFW}$:

- **PARA:** $C = 0$, $O \propto p$, $\epsilon \le e^{-pT_{RH}}$ — cheapest, but $p$ must scale as $1/T_{RH}$.
- **Graphene:** $C \propto 1/T_{RH}$ counters, $O \approx 0$ when idle, $\epsilon = 0$ (deterministic) — exact, but area explodes as thresholds fall [9].
- **PROTEAS:** $C = 16$ counters/bank, $O < 3\%$, max disturbance 2.1K $< T_{RH}$ — the pragmatic middle, but relies on secret PRNG seeds.
- **PRAC + RFM (DDR5):** $C =$ per-row counters in array, $O \approx$ 8.4% even idle [7], $\epsilon \approx 0$ for activation-count attacks — strongest standardized guarantee, but must be extended to bound $t_{AggON}$ for RowPress.

> **Theorem: (No free lunch for bounded-state deterministic trackers).** *A deterministic tracker with $k$ entries per bank facing an adversary that can touch $m > k$ distinct rows per $t_{REFW}$ cannot guarantee identification of the hottest row; the adversary can always arrange for the true aggressor set to be disjoint from the tracked set at mitigation time. Hence deterministic bounded-state in-DRAM tracking is bypassable in principle — the formal content of the TRRespass result [2].*

> **Proof:** By the pigeonhole principle, at any mitigation instant at least $m - k$ touched rows are untracked. The adversary distributes activations over $m$ rows; choosing $m$ large enough pushes some untracked row past $T_{RH}$ before any tracked row triggers mitigation. Randomization (PROTEAS/PARA) defeats this by making the tracked set unpredictable. ∎

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **The RowPress accounting gap.** PRAC counts activations; RowPress damage scales with $t_{AggON}$. No standardized mechanism currently meters cumulative row-open time per row, and the JEDEC maximum $t_{AggON}$ already yields 159× amplification [1]. Closing this gap requires open-time-aware counters or architectural caps on row-open duration — both costly to row-buffer locality.

2. **Blast-radius uncertainty.** Half-Double proved distance-two effects; follow-on work hints at distance-four ("blaster") phenomena [10]. If the disturbance radius keeps growing with scaling, RFM's mitigation window must widen correspondingly, multiplying refresh energy.

3. **The threshold treadmill.** Every defense's cost scales inversely with $T_{RH}$, which falls each generation. Sub-1K thresholds would make even PRAC's per-row counters prohibitively expensive to service — motivating research into *proactive* refresh and fundamentally less-coupled cell designs.

4. **Composability and verification.** Real systems will layer PARA-like sampling, PRAC counters, RFM scheduling, and ECC, but the *composition* of probabilistic and deterministic guarantees has no clean formal treatment, and proprietary in-DRAM implementations remain unauditable black boxes [2].

5. **New substrates, new physics.** GPUHammer recently demonstrated Rowhammer on GDDR6/HBM GPU memories [7], and HBM stacks show distinct RowPress sensitivity [1]. Each new memory technology re-opens the characterization problem from scratch.

## 7. Conclusion

A decade after the first disturbance-induced bit flip, the DRAM integrity boundary is both better understood and more threatened than ever. We have traced the arc from single-sided hammering to many-sided TRR bypasses [2], frequency-domain fuzzing [3], the Half-Double weaponization of the defense itself [4], and the RowPress temporal axis that collapses activation budgets by two orders of magnitude [1]. Defensively, we analyzed the spectrum from zero-state probabilistic refresh (PARA) [6] through streaming-algorithm tracking (Graphene) [8] and randomized in-DRAM samplers (PROTEAS) [9] to DDR5's standardized RFM/PRAC contract [7] — and showed why ECC cannot serve as a security boundary [5].

Three principles emerge for the architect. **First, count exactly or randomize; never do deterministic approximation with bounded state** — the TRRespass/Blacksmith/Half-Double sequence is a constructive proof that determinism plus bounded state equals bypassability. **Second, meter time as well as count** — RowPress proves the activation counter is a necessary but insufficient sensor. **Third, treat the memory controller as the trust anchor**: in-DRAM heuristics are unauditable, while RFM/PRAC-style controller-coordinated protocols expose the mitigation contract to verification.
