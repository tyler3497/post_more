# Indistinguishability Obfuscation: Multilinear-Map Candidates, Zeroizing and Annihilation Attacks, and the LWE-Based Revival

## Abstract

Indistinguishability obfuscation (iO) is arguably the most powerful cryptographic primitive not known to follow from one-way functions: it yields functional encryption for all circuits, deniable encryption, multiparty key exchange, and traitor tracing. This thesis reconstructs the subject's full arc. We begin with the virtual-black-box impossibility of Barak et al. and iO as the "best possible" obfuscation guarantee, then analyze the first candidate of Garg, Gentry, Halevi, Raykova, Sahai and Waters, built on Garg–Gentry–Halevi multilinear maps and Barrington's branching-program theorem. We dissect the two cryptanalytic families that destroyed the multilinear-map substrate: the *zeroizing* attacks of Cheon, Han, Lee, Ryu and Stehlé and extensions, which exploited zero-testing leakage on encodings of zero; and the *annihilation* attacks of Miles, Sahai and Zhandry, the first polynomial-time cryptanalyses of candidate iO schemes, using nonlinear annihilating polynomials. Finally we present the lattice-based revival: the Jain–Lin–Sahai program constructing iO from well-founded assumptions — subexponential LWE, LPN over large fields, a Boolean PRG in NC⁰, and SXDH/DLIN on bilinear groups — via structured-seed PRGs, constant-degree multilinear maps, and FE-to-iO bootstrapping.

## 1 Introduction

In 2001, Barak, Goldreich, Impagliazzo, Rudich, Sahai, Vadhan and Yang initiated the theoretical study of program obfuscation and proved that the most natural formalization — *virtual black-box* (VBB) obfuscation — is impossible even for restricted function classes [1]. For over a decade, obfuscation was considered cryptographically unattainable.

The 2013 breakthrough of Garg, Gentry, Halevi, Raykova, Sahai and Waters [2], together with the companion result of Sahai and Waters on functional encryption [3], changed the conversation entirely: a candidate for the weaker but still extraordinarily useful iO notion. Press coverage captured the community's reaction — the first serious positive result, shifting the discourse from whether obfuscation is possible to how to achieve it [12]. In the six months following the original paper, more papers appeared on the ePrint archive with "obfuscation" in the title than in the previous seventeen years.

Yet the foundations were built on sand. The construction rested on a *graded encoding scheme* — an approximate, lattice-based instantiation of a multilinear map due to Garg, Gentry and Halevi (GGH13) — whose security was justified only in an idealized generic model. Beginning in 2014, a cascade of cryptanalytic results — *zeroizing* attacks [4,5], *annihilation* attacks [6,7], subfield and statistical attacks — dismantled every concrete candidate for general-purpose multilinear maps, and eventually the candidate obfuscators themselves. For several years, iO existed in a strange limbo: a primitive of staggering power, with no plausible instantiation from standard assumptions.

The resolution arrived from lattices. Starting in 2018, a line of work culminating in Jain, Lin and Sahai [10] constructed iO from the subexponential hardness of *well-founded* assumptions: Learning With Errors (LWE), Learning Parity with Noise (LPN) over large fields, a Boolean PRG in NC⁰, and SXDH/DLIN on bilinear groups.

> **Theorem:** *Under subexponential hardness of LWE with subexponential modulus-to-noise ratio, LPN over ℤₚ with inverse-polynomial error rate, the existence of a Boolean PRG in NC⁰ with polynomial stretch, and SXDH on asymmetric bilinear groups, subexponentially secure indistinguishability obfuscation for all polynomial-size circuits exists.* [10]

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The obfuscation hierarchy

An **obfuscator** 𝒪 is an efficient probabilistic algorithm mapping a circuit C to a circuit 𝒪(C) such that (i) *functionality*: 𝒪(C)(x) = C(x) for all x; (ii) *polynomial slowdown*: |𝒪(C)| ≤ poly(|C|). Security notions differ in what "unintelligibility" means:

- **Virtual black-box (VBB)**: for every PPT adversary A there exists a PPT simulator S with oracle access to C such that A(𝒪(C)) ≈ Sᶜ(1^|C|). *Impossible* in general [1].
- **Indistinguishability obfuscation (iO)**: for any two functionally equivalent circuits C₀, C₁ of equal size, 𝒪(C₀) ≈𝒸 𝒪(C₁). This is the *best possible* obfuscation in a precise sense: if any obfuscator hides some property, iO hides it too [1].
- **Differing-inputs obfuscation (diO)**: strengthens iO to circuits differing on a hidden input; implies VBB for point functions but is subject to impossibility results under falsifiable assumptions.

iO is "crypto-complete": iO plus one-way functions imply public-key encryption, NIZKs, deniable encryption, multiparty NIKE, traitor tracing, and succinct randomized encodings [2,3,10].

### 2.2 Multilinear maps and graded encodings

A κ-multilinear map e: 𝔾₁ × ⋯ × 𝔾_κ → 𝔾_T extends bilinear pairings to κ inputs, with e(g₁^{a₁}, …, g_κ^{a_κ}) = e(g₁,…,g_κ)^{∏aᵢ}. With no concrete construction from abelian varieties forthcoming, Garg, Gentry and Halevi proposed an *approximate* multilinear map — a **graded encoding scheme** — on lattices: elements are encoded at levels 0…κ, encodings at level i multiply to level i+j, and a *zero-testing* procedure decides whether a top-level encoding encodes zero [2].

Competing candidates followed — CLT13 (Coron–Lepoint–Tibouchi, CRT-based), GGH15 (graph-induced, directed encodings), CLT15 [8,9] — all analyzed in a **generic multilinear group model** where zero-testing reveals *only* whether an encoding is zero. That assumption would prove tragically optimistic.

### 2.3 Branching programs and bootstrapping

The GGHRSW candidate [2] obfuscates *matrix branching programs* obtained via **Barrington's theorem**: any depth-d fan-in-2 circuit becomes a width-5 permutation branching program of length 4^d. Each step's transition matrices are encoded in the multilinear map; evaluation multiplies encodings level by level, and a final zero-test reveals the output. Kilian-style randomization of the matrices plus "diagonal" dummy elements frustrates algebraic attacks [6]. A *bootstrapping* theorem then lifts iO for NC¹ to iO for all of P/poly by obfuscating an FHE decryption circuit.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis follows a three-part methodology. **First**, we reconstruct the security arguments of the multilinear-map-based candidates in the generic model, making explicit the idealized assumptions about zero-testing. **Second**, we formalize the two attack families — zeroizing and annihilation — as algebraic procedures over the encodings, exhibiting the polynomial identities each exploits and the countermeasures proposed (randomized branching programs, straddling set systems, post-zeroizing evasive-circuit constructions [8]). **Third**, we present the lattice-based revival [10] as a modular pipeline:

1. **sPRG**: a *structured-seed pseudorandom generator* with polynomial stretch, built from LPN over fields plus a PRG in NC⁰, whose output is computable by a degree-2 polynomial in a private seed [10].
2. **Constant-degree multilinear maps**: Lin's observation that degree-O(1) — ultimately degree-3 — multilinear maps suffice, sidestepping high-degree maps entirely [10,11].
3. **FE from sPRG**: collusion-resistant public-key functional encryption from polynomial hardness.
4. **iO from FE**: the FE-to-iO bootstrapping compiler, which inherently introduces the subexponential hardness requirement.

We validate technical claims against primary sources: the Barak et al. impossibility [1], the GGHRSW candidate [2], the Cheon et al. zeroizing attacks and their Coron et al. extension [5], the Miles–Sahai–Zhandry annihilation attacks [6] and their Apon et al. generalization [7], the post-zeroizing framework [8], and the Jain–Lin–Sahai well-founded construction with its EMS survey [10,11].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The GGHRSW candidate: anatomy of an obfuscator

The Garg et al. construction [2] proceeds in four stages. Given a circuit C:

1. **Barringtonize**: convert C to a length-n, width-w matrix branching program BP = {(inp(i), A_{i,0}, A_{i,1})}ᵢ₌₁ⁿ, where each A_{i,b} ∈ {0,1}^{w×w} is a permutation matrix, and evaluation on input x multiplies A₀ = ∏ᵢ A_{i,x_{inp(i)}}; output is 0 iff A₀ = I.
2. **Randomize**: choose random invertible matrices Rᵢ and replace A_{i,b} with R_{i-1} A_{i,b} Rᵢ^{-1}; append random diagonal "bookend" vectors s, t so that s·(∏ᵢ Ã_{i,x})·t = 0 iff the output is 0. This Kilian-style randomization was the principal defense against *algebraic* attacks [6].
3. **Encode**: encode each randomized matrix entry in the GGH13 graded encoding at level inp(i) (using straddling set systems to enforce that only "valid" multilinear monomials can be formed), and encode the bookends at complementary levels.
4. **Evaluate**: on input x, multiply the encodings level by level to reach a top-level encoding u_x; zero-test u_x to learn the output bit.

Security was argued in the generic graded-encoding model: an adversary seeing only handles to encodings and a zero-test oracle learns nothing beyond functionality, *provided* zero-testing leaks exactly one bit. The fatal flaw was that in GGH13, zero-testing publishes a ring element p_{zt}·c where c encodes zero with small noise — and products of such elements expose algebraic structure. Section 4.2 shows how.

```python
# Schematic: GGH13-style encoding and the zero-test leakage surface
# (illustrative pseudocode; real parameters live in R = Z[x]/(x^n+1))
def encode(m, level, params):
    r = sample_small_noise(params.sigma)          # fresh randomness
    return (r * params.g + m) * params.z**(-level)  # enc. of m at `level`

def zero_test(top_encoding, params):
    # publishes a ring element, not just a bit!
    v = top_encoding * params.p_zt               # p_zt = h * z^kappa / g
    return is_small(v)                            # True  <=> encodes 0
# Attack surface: the *value* v = h*(r*g + m)/g leaks correlations
# between h, g, r across many zero encodings.
```

### 4.2 Zeroizing attacks: when zero-testing leaks the ring

The **zeroizing attack** paradigm, introduced by Cheon, Han, Lee, Ryu and Stehlé at Eurocrypt 2015 and extended by Coron, Gentry, Halevi, Lepoint, Maji, Miles, Raykova, Sahai and Tibouchi [5], exploits a simple observation: if the adversary can obtain *low-level encodings of zero* — encodings c₁,…,c_ℓ at levels below κ with underlying plaintext 0 and small noise — then the zero-test outputs w_j = p_{zt}·c_j = h·r_j (for small r_j) are *correlated through the secret ring elements h and g*.

Concretely, in CLT13 the zero-test parameter is p_{zt} = Σ h_i·(g_i^{-1} mod p_i)·x₀/p_i; zero encodings expose vectors whose entries are linear forms in the secret primes. Collecting enough of them yields a lattice whose short vectors reveal the plaintext ring: for CLT13 the attack is a *total break* (secret primes recovered), while for GGH13 it yields a weak discrete-log attack [5]. The extensions in [5] remove the need for low-level zeroes: multiplying top-level encodings and applying lattice reduction (LLL/BKZ) to the resulting matrices of zero-test outputs recovers secrets even when zero encodings exist only at the top level.

| Attack | Target map | Requires low-level zeroes? | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cheon et al. (Eurocrypt '15) | CLT13, GGH13 | Yes | Key recovery (CLT13 total break) |
| Coron et al. [5] | CLT13 | No | Total break |
| Coron et al. [5] | GGH13 | No | Weak discrete-log |
| Hu–Jia | GGH13 | Yes | Secret parameter recovery |
| Subfield attacks | GGH13/CLT13 | No | Exploits subfield structure |

The countermeasure literature responded with **post-zeroizing obfuscation** [8]: Badrinarayanan, Miles, Sahai and Zhandry built an obfuscator for *evasive* functions and proved, via a new polynomial-analysis technique, that no generic-model adversary can even *create* an encoding of zero. But the general case remained exposed.

> **Theorem:** *There exist families of functionally equivalent branching programs whose GGH13-based obfuscations are efficiently distinguishable.* — the annihilation attack theorem of Miles, Sahai and Zhandry [6].

### 4.3 Annihilation attacks: cryptanalysis of the obfuscators themselves

The most significant cryptanalytic result for iO was the **annihilation attack** of Miles, Sahai and Zhandry (Crypto 2016) [6]: the first *polynomial-time* attack against candidate iO schemes instantiated over GGH13. Previous zeroizing attacks could not touch iO candidates, because obfuscators deliberately avoid publishing low-level zero encodings.

The attack works with **nonlinear annihilating polynomials**. Given two functionally equivalent branching programs BP₀, BP₁, the adversary evaluates both on many inputs, collects top-level encodings, and finds a polynomial P (of degree ≥ 2) that *annihilates* — evaluates to a zero encoding — for one program's encodings but not the other's. Because the underlying plaintexts differ algebraically even when the computed functions agree, the zero-test on P's output distinguishes the obfuscations. The attack broke the Brakerski–Rothblum, Barak et al. (BGK+14), Pass–Seth–Telang, Ananth et al. (AGIS14), and related candidates over GGH13 [6].

Apon, Döttling, Garg and Mukherjee [7] generalized the attack via **partial inequivalence**: an efficiently testable property of pairs of single-input branching programs that suffices for annihilation attacks. They exhibited natural NC¹ circuits whose Barrington branching programs are partially inequivalent — and, more alarmingly, showed that even the *bootstrapping circuits* used to lift NC¹-obfuscation to P/poly can yield partially inequivalent programs.

The GGHRSW candidate [2] survived annihilation *directly* only because of its extra randomization (random diagonal elements appended to the branching program make annihilating polynomials hard to find) [9] — but this defense was heuristic: the candidate "wasn't constructed to handle this direction of attacks," and a more sophisticated annihilation variant was widely expected.

### 4.4 The lattice revival: iO from well-founded assumptions

The modern resolution abandons multilinear maps as a *concrete* object and rebuilds iO from lattice assumptions plus a bilinear map. The Jain–Lin–Sahai construction [10] proves:

> **Theorem (JLS, informal):** *Assume subexponential security of (i) SXDH on asymmetric bilinear groups of prime order p = O(2^λ); (ii) LWE over ℤₚ with subexponential modulus-to-noise ratio 2^{k^ε}; (iii) LPN over ℤₚ with polynomially many samples and error rate 1/ℓ^δ; (iv) a Boolean PRG in NC⁰ with stretch n^{1+τ}. Then subexponentially secure iO for all polynomial-size circuits exists.* [10]

The architecture has four modules:

1. **Structured-seed PRG (sPRG).** The seed splits into public and private parts; pseudorandomness holds even given the public seed, and the output is computable by a *degree-2* polynomial in the private seed (coefficients derived by constant-degree computation on the public seed). JLS build the sPRG from LPN over fields: with LPN samples (A, b = sA + e + σ) and a PRG G in NC⁰, the sparse LPN error e is "corrected" by precomputation, expressing G(σ) as a degree-2 function of a short prepared secret [10].
2. **Low-degree multilinear maps suffice.** Building on Lin's program (with Tessaro reducing the required degree from 30+ to 3), the construction needs only constant-degree — ultimately *bilinear* — maps [10,11]. This is the decisive break from the multilinear-map era: no κ-linear map for large κ is ever instantiated.
3. **FE from sPRG.** From polynomial hardness of the four assumptions, JLS obtain collusion-resistant public-key *functional encryption* for all circuits: secret keys sk_f reveal only f(m), and the sPRG's degree-2 structure is what makes the FE construction go through with bilinear maps.
4. **iO from FE.** The Ananth–Jain / Bitansky–Vaikuntanathan FE-to-iO compiler bootstraps FE to iO. The compiler inherently requires *subexponential* hardness — the source of the subexponentiality in the theorem — because the security reduction guesses which functional key the adversary will attack.

The subsequent EMS survey by Jain, Lin and Sahai [11] refines the assumptions to three (LPN over fields, NC⁰ PRG, DLIN on symmetric bilinear groups) and systematizes the proof. Crucially, all assumptions are *well-founded*: LWE and LPN are rooted in coding theory and worst-case lattice problems with decades of cryptanalysis, SXDH/DLIN in pairing-based cryptography, and NC⁰ PRGs in complexity theory (Goldreich's candidate). None is a "designer assumption" invented to make the construction work — the failure mode that doomed the multilinear-map candidates.

## 5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

Because iO candidates are far from practical, "empirical" evidence in this area takes the form of *concrete cryptanalysis*: attack complexities measured against proposed parameters, and parameter regimes under which assumptions are believed to hold.

**Attack complexities.** The Cheon et al. zeroizing attack on CLT13 runs in polynomial time given low-level zero encodings, and was experimentally demonstrated to recover secret primes for proposed parameters [5]. The Coron et al. extension [5] removes the low-level-zero requirement at the cost of lattice-reduction (BKZ) on dimension proportional to the number of zero-test outputs — still polynomial for the proposed parameters, constituting a total break of CLT13. Annihilation attacks [6] are *polynomial-time* in the branching-program length: the distinguishing advantage is overwhelming, with the annihilating polynomial found via linear algebra over the evaluated encodings. Apon et al. [7] show partial inequivalence is efficiently testable, making the attack constructive rather than merely existential.

**Parameter regimes for the revival.** JLS [10] require LWE with modulus-to-noise ratio 2^{k^ε} for ε ∈ (0,1) — *subexponential* rather than the polynomial ratios used in FHE — because the FE-to-iO reduction loses a subexponential factor. For security parameter λ, the best known attacks (BKZ-based BDD reduction, ≈ 2^{Ω(k / log(q/σ))}) must remain subexponential; the LPN error rate 1/ℓ^δ with δ ∈ (0,1) similarly keeps information-set decoding subexponential. SXDH is required only on *bilinear* groups, where it has survived two decades of cryptanalysis.

**Efficiency.** The honest party's work in JLS is polynomial but astronomical: obfuscating a circuit of size s yields an obfuscated program of size poly(s) with a large fixed exponent. No implementation exists; the construction is a *feasibility* result — the central practical limitation, shared with all known iO constructions.

```python
# Parameter sanity check: subexponential LWE regime (illustrative)
import math
def lwe_attack_cost(k, logq_over_sigma):
    # BKZ/BDD heuristic: ~2^(0.292 * beta), beta ~ k / log(q/sigma)
    beta = k / logq_over_sigma
    return 2 ** (0.292 * beta)

for k, lq in [(512, 40), (1024, 60), (2048, 80)]:
    print(k, "-> ~2^%.1f" % math.log2(lwe_attack_cost(k, lq)))
# Security demands this be 2^{k^eps}-hard: subexponential, not merely
# superpolynomial. Polynomial-modulus LWE (FHE-style) does NOT suffice.
```

## 6 Limitations

1. **Subexponential hardness is essential, not an artifact.** Every known FE-to-iO compiler loses a subexponential factor; iO from *polynomial* LWE remains open and may be impossible via these routes [10]. The assumptions are therefore quantitatively stronger than those used for public-key encryption.
2. **No efficiency.** The JLS obfuscator's polynomial overhead has an impractically large degree; obfuscating even a 100-gate circuit would produce gigabytes of keys. iO remains a feasibility result, not a deployable tool.
3. **Bilinear-map assumptions are not post-quantum.** SXDH/DLIN fall to quantum attacks (Shor's algorithm on the pairing groups). Fully post-quantum iO — from LWE alone — is a major open problem; the deniable-encryption literature notes that JLS-style constructions are "not quantum-secure due to their use of bilinear maps" [10,11].
4. **The NC⁰ PRG assumption.** A Boolean PRG in NC⁰ with polynomial stretch n^{1+τ} is a strong, non-standard (though well-studied) complexity assumption; Goldreich's candidate has resisted proof and faces its own cryptanalytic scrutiny.
5. **Generic-model gaps persist.** The cryptanalysis era taught that idealized models of multilinear maps were unsound; the new constructions avoid multilinear maps but still rely on idealized modeling in parts of the FE security proof. The post-zeroizing program [8] showed how to harden analyses, but a fully standard-model proof of iO remains elusive.
6. **Impossibility boundaries.** Differing-inputs obfuscation for general circuits is impossible under falsifiable assumptions, and VBB remains impossible [1]; iO's "best possible" guarantee is the ceiling, and some applications inherently need more.

## 7 Conclusion

The history of indistinguishability obfuscation is a case study in the scientific method applied to cryptography. A bold candidate [2] built on an approximate multilinear map electrified the field [12]; a ruthless cryptanalytic program — zeroizing attacks exploiting zero-test leakage [5], annihilation attacks breaking the obfuscators themselves [6,7] — razed the concrete foundations; and a patient theoretical reconstruction [10,11] rebuilt the primitive on assumptions with decades of independent study. Three lessons endure. *First*, idealized models of new algebraic objects must be treated as hypotheses, not axioms — the generic multilinear group model concealed exactly the leakage that destroyed its instantiations. *Second*, cryptanalysis is constructive: annihilation attacks mapped the precise algebraic properties that any future candidate must avoid. *Third*, well-foundedness is a security property in its own right: LWE, LPN, and bilinear SXDH earned trust through years of failed attacks in contexts unrelated to obfuscation, and iO built on them inherits that confidence. The open frontier — polynomial-hardness iO, post-quantum iO, and practical efficiency — will likely require ideas as radical as the 2013 candidate itself.
