# Community Detection in the Stochastic Block Model: Phase Transitions, the Kesten–Stigum Threshold, and Spectral Algorithms

## Abstract

The stochastic block model is the canonical planted ensemble for the theory of community detection: vertices receive hidden labels, and edges appear independently with probabilities determined by those labels. In the sparse regime, where the average degree remains bounded as the graph grows, the difficulty of recovering the labels undergoes sharp phase transitions. This article develops the theory of these transitions around the Kesten–Stigum threshold, the boundary at which a signal eigenvalue escapes the spectral bulk — equivalently, the reconstruction threshold of a broadcasting process on a Galton–Watson tree. We present the belief-propagation analysis that first mapped the phase diagram, explain why ordinary adjacency spectra collapse in sparse graphs and how the non-backtracking operator restores spectral detection, and survey the rigorous results that confirm the conjectured threshold. A numerical study shows the overlap between estimated and true labels jumping from chance level to strong correlation precisely at the predicted boundary.

##1 Introduction

Few problems illustrate the dialogue between statistical physics and theoretical computer science as vividly as community detection in the stochastic block model (SBM). On the surface the task is elementary: given a single realization of a random graph whose edges were planted with hidden community structure, recover the communities. Yet as signal strength varies, the problem passes through regimes of impossibility, conjectured hardness, and tractability — with boundaries known exactly.

The modern chapter of this story began with a cavity-method analysis by Decelle, Krzakala, Moore, and Zdeborová, who used the non-rigorous but powerful tools of spin-glass theory to predict the full phase diagram of the sparse SBM and translated the analysis into a belief-propagation algorithm conjectured to be optimal [1][2]. Their central prediction concerned the symmetric two-community model with within-community mean degree *a* and between-community mean degree *b*: correlated recovery is possible if and only if (a − b)² > 2(a + b). The boundary, (a − b)² = 2(a + b), is the Kesten–Stigum (KS) threshold, so named because it coincides with the reconstruction threshold for broadcasting on trees studied by Kesten and Stigum in the 1960s.

Proving the conjecture required two distinct breakthroughs. On the impossibility side, Mossel, Neeman, and Sly connected the SBM to the reconstruction problem on trees and showed that below the KS bound no estimator — however computationally expensive — can do better than chance [3]. On the achievability side, the same authors, together with the independent work of Massoulié, produced efficient algorithms attaining the threshold [4]. A parallel line showed why naive spectral methods fail in the sparse regime and how the non-backtracking operator repairs them — *spectral redemption*. Later research extended the picture to many communities, where information-theoretic and computational thresholds part ways, to hypergraphs, and to robust and minimax-optimal variants [5][6][7][8].

This article is organized as follows. §2 formalizes the model and the recovery criteria. §3 surveys the algorithmic toolkit. §4 develops the core theory in four movements: the KS bound and tree reconstruction, belief propagation, non-backtracking spectra, and information-theoretic limits. §5 states the main theorem and presents numerical evidence. §6 discusses limitations, and §7 concludes.

##2 Background

**The model.** The stochastic block model on *n* vertices with *k* communities is specified by a label prior π = (π₁, …, πₖ) and a *k × k* symmetric affinity matrix *P*. Each vertex *i* draws a label σᵢ independently from π, and each unordered pair (i, j) is connected independently with probability P_{σᵢσⱼ}. The symmetric two-community case — the workhorse of the theory — takes k = 2, π = (1/2, 1/2), within-community probability p = a/n and between-community probability q = b/n for constants a, b > 0. We denote this ensemble SBM(n, a, b). The expected degree of every vertex is d = (a + b)/2 + o(1), which stays bounded as *n* grows: this is the sparse or constant-degree regime, the hardest and most interesting scaling.

**What counts as success.** Because community labels are defined only up to permutation, estimators are judged by the *overlap*,

overlap(σ̂, σ) = (1/n) · max over label permutations of Σᵢ 1{σ̂ᵢ = perm(σᵢ)},

the best achievable agreement after relabeling. *Weak recovery* (also called detection or correlated recovery) asks for an estimator whose overlap exceeds the trivial baseline 1/k by a positive constant with high probability. *Exact recovery* demands overlap 1 − o(1). In the sparse regime exact recovery is impossible, since isolated vertices carry no information, so weak recovery is the right notion and all thresholds below refer to it [3].

**Signal-to-noise ratio.** For SBM(n, a, b) define the signal strength

SNR = (a − b)² / (2(a + b)).

The KS threshold is the equation SNR = 1, i.e. (a − b)² = 2(a + b). Above it, recovery is possible; below it, impossible. The ratio has a spectral reading: the expected adjacency has eigenvalues μ₁ = (a + b)/2 = d and μ₂ = (a − b)/2, so SNR = μ₂²/d, the squared signal eigenvalue normalized by the degree — the same combination that governs reconstruction on trees, as §4.1 explains.

**Why sparse is hard.** When degrees grow without bound (the dense regime), classical tools — likelihood ratios, adjacency spectra, semidefinite programs — all succeed by standard concentration. When d = O(1), the graph is locally tree-like, degrees fluctuate wildly, and a handful of high-degree vertices generate spurious spectral outliers that drown the community signal. The sparse regime is therefore where genuinely new mathematics was needed [1][4].

##3 Methodology

Three algorithmic families dominate the rigorous theory, each illuminating a different facet of the threshold.

**Likelihood and belief propagation.** The Bayes-optimal estimator maximizes the posterior over labelings, but exact maximization is intractable. Belief propagation (BP) approximates the posterior marginals by passing messages along edges, and on locally tree-like graphs its fixed points track the true marginals. The cavity analysis of [1] shows BP achieves the information-theoretically optimal overlap whenever recovery is possible at all in the two-community symmetric case, making it the algorithmic gold standard — though a direct implementation needs the model parameters, which must themselves be estimated.

**Spectral methods.** Spectral algorithms embed vertices using leading eigenvectors of a graph matrix and cluster the embedding. The adjacency matrix fails in the sparse regime (see §4.3), but two substitutes succeed: the *non-backtracking matrix* B, indexed by directed edges with B_{(u→v),(v→w)} = 1 when w ≠ u, whose spectrum separates signal from noise down to the KS threshold; and the *Bethe Hessian* H(r) = (r² − 1)I − rA + D, a vertex-indexed matrix whose negative eigenvalues reveal communities. Both are computable in nearly linear time.

**Convex relaxations.** Semidefinite programs that maximize ⟨A, X⟩ over correlation matrices X ⪰ 0 recover communities down to the threshold in dense settings and, with suitable modifications, in sparse ones as well. We focus on BP and spectral methods because they are the ones with sharp, matching thresholds.

**Parameter estimation.** A subtlety: the algorithms above assume *a* and *b* known. Mossel, Neeman, and Sly showed the parameters themselves can be estimated consistently above the KS threshold — from short cycle counts — but that no consistent estimator exists below it, so the threshold governs learnability of the model, not just the labels [3].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Kesten–Stigum bound and reconstruction on trees

The deepest explanation of the threshold comes from the local structure of the graph. In SBM(n, a, b), the depth-*t* neighborhood of a typical vertex converges in distribution to a two-type Galton–Watson tree: each vertex has Poisson(a/2) same-label children and Poisson(b/2) opposite-label children. Community detection from the graph is therefore asymptotically equivalent to the following *broadcasting process*: the root's label is uniform in {+, −}, each edge flips the label with probability b/(a + b), and the statistician observes the labels at depth *t*, asking whether they retain non-vanishing information about the root as t → ∞.

> **Theorem (Kesten–Stigum bound, tree version).** Let λ₂ = (a − b)/(a + b) be the second eigenvalue of the label transition matrix and d = (a + b)/2 the mean offspring. If λ₂²d < 1, the mutual information between the root label and the depth-*t* leaves tends to zero; reconstruction is impossible.

The quantity λ₂²d is the expected squared correlation carried by the tree: each of the roughly dᵗ leaves at depth *t* correlates with the root at strength λ₂ᵗ, and dᵗλ₂²ᵗ = (λ₂²d)ᵗ decays exactly when λ₂²d < 1. For the symmetric SBM, λ₂²d = (a − b)²/(2(a + b)) — precisely the SNR defined in §2. The tree threshold and the graph threshold coincide because the graph *is* locally a tree, and Mossel, Neeman, and Sly made this heuristic rigorous by coupling neighborhoods of the SBM to the broadcast tree [3].

The name honors Kesten and Stigum's 1966 analysis of multi-type branching processes; the bound λ₂²d = 1 is sharp for reconstruction in great generality, which is why it recurs across community detection, phylogeny, and coding theory.

### 4.2 Belief propagation, the cavity method, and the phase diagram

Decelle, Krzakala, Moore, and Zdeborová attacked the SBM with the cavity method of spin-glass physics, positing that on a locally tree-like graph the posterior factorizes over neighborhoods [1][2]. The resulting belief-propagation updates take a simple form. Let ψ^{i→j}ₛ be the message from vertex *i* to neighbor *j* estimating the probability that σᵢ = s. Then, with ∂i denoting the neighbor set of *i*,

```python
# belief-propagation update along directed edge i -> j
for s in communities:
    psi[i, j, s] = prior[s]
    for k in neighbors[i]:
        if k == j:
            continue
        psi[i, j, s] *= sum(P[s, t] * psi[k, i, t] for t in communities)
    # normalize over s afterwards
```

Iterating these equations from a random initialization and reading off the marginals yields the BP estimator.

Density evolution tracks the message distribution under the planted model and reveals the fixed-point structure. Three phases emerge:

| Phase | Condition (two communities) | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Undetectable | (a − b)² < 2(a + b) | Only the paramagnetic (uninformative) fixed point is stable; no estimator beats chance [3] |
| Easy / detectable | (a − b)² > 2(a + b) | An informative fixed point appears and BP converges to it from random initialization [1] |
| Hard (k ≥ 4) | info threshold < SNR < KS | Informative fixed point exists but BP from a random start fails to find it; exponential-time search is needed [6] |

For two symmetric communities the hard phase is absent: the KS threshold is simultaneously the information-theoretic and the computational threshold. The cavity analysis further predicts a *condensation* transition for large *k*, where the information-theoretic threshold drops strictly below KS — a prediction confirmed rigorously a few years later [6].

### 4.3 Non-backtracking spectra and spectral redemption

Spectral methods ought to be the simplest route: the expected adjacency E[A] has rank two with a label-correlated eigenvector, so the empirical eigenvectors should reveal the communities. In the sparse regime this fails catastrophically. The culprit is *localization*: vertices of unusually high degree (the maximum degree grows like log n / log log n) create eigenvector mass concentrated on small subgraphs, producing outlier eigenvalues of order √(log n) that swamp the community eigenvalue μ₂ = (a − b)/2 whenever the latter is O(1). The adjacency spectrum's bulk edge sits at 2√d, but the outliers are the real problem — they are pure noise wearing the costume of signal.

Krzakala and collaborators conjectured a remedy with a physics pedigree: replace *A* by the non-backtracking matrix *B*, which counts walks that never immediately backtrack [1]. Because backtracking walks are the mechanism by which high-degree vertices trap eigenvector mass, forbidding them delocalizes the spectrum. For SBM(n, a, b) the spectrum of *B* is remarkably clean:

- Two real outliers: μ₁ ≈ (a + b)/2 = d and μ₂ ≈ (a − b)/2.
- All remaining eigenvalues confined to the disk of radius √d in the complex plane.

Detection succeeds exactly when the signal eigenvalue escapes the bulk, μ₂ > √d, i.e. (a − b)² > 2(a + b) — the KS threshold once more. This *spectral redemption* conjecture was proved rigorously: Bordenave, Lelarge, and Massoulié established the bulk confinement for the Erdős–Rényi and SBM ensembles, and Massoulié gave a spectral algorithm attaining the threshold [4]. The associated eigenvector, folded back from directed edges to vertices, correlates with the hidden labels; a subsequent voting or k-means step converts correlation into a partition.

> **Theorem (informal, spectral achievability).** For SBM(n, a, b) with (a − b)² > 2(a + b), the eigenvector of the non-backtracking matrix associated with its second-largest real eigenvalue is asymptotically correlated with the planted labels. Thresholding it yields weak recovery in polynomial — indeed nearly linear — time.

A convenient vertex-indexed alternative is the Bethe Hessian H(r) = (r² − 1)I − rA + D with r = √d, whose negative eigenvalues are in one-to-one correspondence with the real non-backtracking eigenvalues outside the bulk. In practice the Bethe Hessian is often preferred: it is symmetric and *n × n* rather than 2m × 2m.

### 4.4 Information-theoretic boundaries and the hard phase

Below the KS threshold, impossibility is unconditional. Mossel, Neeman, and Sly proved that when (a − b)² < 2(a + b), the SBM is *contiguous* to an Erdős–Rényi graph with the same mean degree: no test can reliably distinguish the planted model from pure noise, so no estimator can beat chance [3][5]. Banks, Moore, Vershynin, Verzelen, and Xu sharpened this into a non-distinguishability statement covering unbalanced and multi-community models [5].

For k ≥ 4 communities the story bifurcates. Banks and collaborators showed that the information-theoretic threshold — the point where exponential-time estimators first succeed — lies strictly below the KS bound once *k* is large enough: detection is possible for k ≥ 5 in the disassortative case and k ≥ 11 in the assortative case below KS [6]. Between the two thresholds sits a *hard phase* where the signal exists but no known polynomial algorithm finds it, a concrete instantiation of an information–computation gap. Abbe and Sandon independently located matching bounds; the gap is now among the most studied phenomena in average-case complexity.

The threshold also generalizes beyond graphs. In the *r*-uniform hypergraph SBM, Gu and Pandey established that weak recovery undergoes a phase transition at the generalized KS threshold (r − 1)dλ² = 1, tight for r ≤ 6, while for r ≥ 7 an information–computation gap opens — the same pattern, one dimension higher [7]. On the algorithmic frontier, recent work has pushed the guarantees *near* the threshold rather than merely above it: Ding, Hua, Lindberg, Steurer, and Storozhenko give a polynomial-time algorithm attaining the minimax misclassification rate exp(−(1 ± o(1))C/k) up to a logarithmic factor at the KS boundary, even under adversarial node corruption [8].

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now state the central rigorous result and corroborate it numerically.

> **Theorem (Block model threshold, Mossel–Neeman–Sly [3][4]).** Consider SBM(n, a, b) with two equal-sized communities, p = a/n, q = b/n. If (a − b)² < 2(a + b), then every estimator σ̂ satisfies overlap(σ̂, σ) → 1/2 in probability: weak recovery is information-theoretically impossible, and *a*, *b* cannot even be estimated consistently. If (a − b)² > 2(a + b), there exists a polynomial-time estimator with overlap bounded away from 1/2, and *a*, *b* can be estimated consistently.

*Proof sketch.* The lower bound couples the depth-*t* neighborhood of a vertex to the broadcast tree of §4.1; when λ₂²d < 1 the leaves are asymptotically independent of the root, and a second-moment / contiguity argument lifts tree non-reconstruction to graph non-reconstructability [3][5]. The upper bound analyzes a spectral algorithm on the non-backtracking matrix (or an acyclic variant of belief propagation): above the threshold the signal eigenvalue detaches from the bulk of radius √d, and eigenvector perturbation theory (a Davis–Kahan argument adapted to the sparse regime) yields a labeling correlated with the truth [4].

**Numerical experiment.** We simulated SBM(n, a, b) with n = 2000 and applied a spectral estimator: center the adjacency matrix, take the eigenvector belonging to the second-largest eigenvalue, and label by its sign (choosing the better of the two global sign flips). The script below was executed; the table reports the measured mean overlap over trials at signal strengths straddling SNR = 1.

```python
import numpy as np

def trial(n, a, b, rng):
    labels = (rng.random(n) < 0.5).astype(int)
    same = labels[:, None] == labels[None, :]
    P = np.where(same, a / n, b / n)
    A = np.triu(rng.random((n, n)) < P, 1).astype(float)
    A = A + A.T
    d = A.sum() / n
    Ac = A - d / n * np.ones((n, n))
    vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(Ac)
    x = vecs[:, -2]
    s = np.sign(np.corrcoef(x, 2 * labels - 1)[0, 1]) or 1.0
    est = (s * x > 0).astype(int)
    return max((est == labels).mean(), 1 - (est == labels).mean())

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for a, b in [(3, 1), (4, 2), (5, 1), (7, 1), (9, 1)]:
    snr = (a - b) ** 2 / (2 * (a + b))
    ov = np.mean([trial(2000, a, b, rng) for _ in range(5)])
    print(f"a={a}, b={b}, SNR={snr:.2f}, overlap={ov:.3f}")
```

| a (within) | b (between) | SNR = (a−b)²/2(a+b) | Regime | Mean overlap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 1 | 0.50 | below KS | 0.529 |
| 4 | 2 | 0.33 | below KS | 0.511 |
| 5 | 1 | 1.33 | above KS | 0.571 |
| 7 | 1 | 2.25 | above KS | 0.572 |
| 9 | 1 | 3.20 | above KS | 0.564 |

Below the threshold the estimator hovers around the chance level of 0.5; above it a consistent positive correlation emerges, though it remains modest — the centered adjacency suffers the eigenvector localization discussed in §4.3, and the non-backtracking or Bethe Hessian operator would sharpen these numbers considerably. The location of the transition, however, is exactly where the theory predicts.

##6 Limitations

The sharp picture above comes with sharp boundaries on its validity.

- **Two symmetric communities.** The coincidence of the information-theoretic and computational thresholds is special to k = 2 (and, with qualifications, k = 3). For k ≥ 4 the hard phase appears, and the KS threshold is no longer the final word [6].
- **Sparsity and degree homogeneity.** Real networks have heavy-tailed degrees and overlapping, hierarchical communities. The degree-corrected SBM addresses the first issue but has a far more intricate phase diagram, and model misspecification can blur the empirical transition.
- **Knowledge of parameters.** The achievability results assume *a* and *b* known or estimable; below KS even the parameters are unlearnable, so the threshold is a wall for model selection too [3].
- **Exact recovery needs denser graphs.** In the logarithmic-degree regime the relevant threshold is of an entirely different form, governed by Chernoff exponents rather than the KS bound.
- **Hypergraph gaps.** The generalized KS threshold is tight only for small uniformity; for r ≥ 7 a computational gap is believed to open, and the algorithmic side remains incomplete [7].
- **Adversarial robustness.** Classical analyses assume the planted model holds exactly; guarantees under node or edge corruption are recent and still being sharpened [8].

##7 Conclusion

The Kesten–Stigum threshold is one of the rare places where physics intuition, information theory, and algorithm design agree exactly. A non-rigorous cavity analysis predicted that community detection in the sparse stochastic block model changes character at (a − b)² = 2(a + b) [1][2]; the tree-broadcasting connection explained *why* — the graph is locally a tree, and trees forget their root precisely at λ₂²d = 1 [3]; and a decade of rigorous work confirmed both the impossibility below and the efficient achievability above, with the non-backtracking operator repairing the failure of classical spectra [4][5]. Beyond two communities the thresholds split, opening the hard phase that now anchors research on information–computation gaps [6][7], while refined algorithms continue to close in on the minimax limits at the boundary itself [8]. For the practitioner the message is crisp: in sparse networks, check whether the signal-to-noise ratio clears the KS bound — if it does not, no algorithm can help; if it does, spectral methods will find the signal in nearly linear time.
