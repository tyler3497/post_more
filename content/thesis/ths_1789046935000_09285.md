# Threshold Cryptography in Practice: Distributed Key Generation, FROST Schnorr Signatures, and Asynchronous Proactive Secret Sharing

## Abstract

Threshold cryptography replaces a single signing key — the catastrophic point of compromise in wallets, certificate authorities, and validator infrastructure — with a quorum of *n* key shares such that any *t* of them can sign or decrypt while any *t − 1* reveal nothing. This thesis gives a practitioner-oriented but proof-grade treatment of the full lifecycle of a threshold Schnorr key: its birth through **distributed key generation** (DKG), its use through the **FROST** two-round threshold signing protocol specified in RFC 9591, and its maintenance through **proactive secret sharing**, which periodically re-randomizes all shares so that a mobile adversary can never accumulate enough of them over time. We cover Shamir sharing and Feldman verifiable secret sharing, explain why Pedersen's DKG admits a public-key bias attack and how the Gennaro–Jarecki–Krawczyk–Rabin construction removes it, dissect FROST's nonce-binding technique that defeats rogue-key and ROS forgery attacks while preserving true *t*-of-*n* flexibility, and show how Herzberg–Jarecki–Krawczyk–Yung resharing converts a static secret into a perpetually fresh one without ever reconstructing it.

## 1 Introduction

In classical public-key cryptography, a private key is an indivisible asset: whoever holds it can sign, and whoever steals it can impersonate. Exchange wallet compromises, CA key leaks, and validator key theft have repeatedly shown that *storing* a key is a far weaker link than the mathematics protecting it. **Threshold cryptography** splits trust itself: the secret key is never assembled in one place; *n* parties each hold a share, and any quorum of *t* can jointly produce a signature indistinguishable from one made by the original key [1][2].

Three problems must be solved before this idea is deployable:

1. **Birth of the key.** A trusted dealer is itself a single point of compromise. *Distributed key generation* (DKG) lets the parties jointly sample a key that no one ever learns in full.
2. **Use of the key.** Naive distributed Schnorr invites *rogue-key* and *ROS* forgeries that exploit adversarial control over message ordering. FROST resolves this with a binding-factor technique needing only **two network rounds** while supporting arbitrary signer subsets [1][2][3].
3. **Longevity of the key.** A *(t, n)*-sharing defends a single moment, but keys live for years. A *mobile adversary* compromising one custodian per year eventually accumulates *t* shares. *Proactive secret sharing* defeats this by periodically refreshing every share without changing the secret [6].

> **Thesis:** Practical threshold Schnorr cryptography is now a mature engineering discipline: RFC 9591 standardizes two-round FROST signing with identifiable aborts, Feldman-verifiable DKG removes the trusted dealer (with fully unbiased variants available), and proactive resharing converts static secrets into mobile-adversary-resistant ones. The remaining gaps are round complexity under asynchrony, post-quantum instantiations, and the robustness-versus-abort tension in adversarial deployments.

Section 2 reviews Shamir sharing, Feldman VSS, and single-party Schnorr, and introduces the adversary models that make naive thresholding unsound. Section 3 presents the methodology: the FROST signing flow, the Pedersen and Gennaro–Jarecki–Krawczyk–Rabin DKG pipelines, and the Herzberg–Jarecki–Krawczyk–Yung refresh protocol. Section 4 deep-dives into the DKG complaint machinery, FROST's defense against ROS forgeries, single-round FROST with preprocessing, asynchronous proactive schemes, and deployment concerns. Section 5 states the formal security results and compares the protocol family; Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Shamir Secret Sharing and Lagrange Interpolation

Let **G** be a cyclic group of prime order *q* with generator *g*. A dealer holding a secret *s ∈ Z_q* picks a random degree-(*t − 1*) polynomial *f(x) = s + a₁x + ⋯ + a_{t−1}x^{t−1}* and gives party *i* the share *sᵢ = f(i)*. Any *t* shares reconstruct *s* via Lagrange interpolation at the origin, *s = Σ_{i∈S} λᵢ·sᵢ* with *λᵢ = Π_{j∈S, j≠i} j/(j − i)* mod *q*. Fewer than *t* shares give *zero* information about *s* in the information-theoretic sense. This *perfect secrecy* is the foundation of everything that follows — but plain Shamir sharing cannot detect a dealer distributing inconsistent shares, which is why *verifiable* variants are needed.

### 2.2 Feldman Verifiable Secret Sharing

Feldman's VSS augments Shamir sharing with public commitments *C_k = g^{a_k}* (with *a₀ = s*). A party receiving share *sᵢ* verifies *g^{sᵢ} ≟ Π_k C_k^{i^k}* without learning *s*. Commitments are homomorphic in the exponent: if every party runs a Feldman VSS instance as a dealer, the *sum* of the dealt polynomials is itself a valid sharing whose public key is the *product* of the individual commitments — exactly Pedersen's DKG [5].

### 2.3 Single-Party Schnorr Signatures

A Schnorr signature over message *m* with secret key *x* and public key *X = g^x* is *(R, z)*: sample fresh *k ← Z_q*, compute *R = g^k*, derive *c = H(R, X, m)*, respond *z = k + c·x*. Verification checks *g^z ≟ R · X^c*, with security reducing to discrete logarithms in the random-oracle model. Two properties make Schnorr uniquely threshold-friendly:

- **Linearity.** The response *z* is linear in both the nonce and the secret, so signature *shares* aggregate by addition.
- **No pairings or range proofs.** Unlike threshold ECDSA, no multiplicative-to-additive share conversion (and hence no Paillier encryption or oblivious transfer) is required.

The danger lies in the nonce: reusing a nonce *k* across two messages immediately exposes the secret key. In the multi-party setting, *deterministic* nonce derivation — safe in EdDSA — becomes catastrophic, because one party's deterministic nonce combined with another's randomness leaks shares [1]. FROST therefore mandates fresh, hedged randomness per signing operation.

### 2.4 Adversary Models: From Static to Mobile

Threshold protocols are analyzed against progressively stronger adversaries:

| Adversary | Capability | Defeated by |
|---|---|---|
| *Static, passive* (honest-but-curious) | Corrupts < *t* parties before the protocol starts; follows the protocol | Shamir + Feldman VSS |
| *Static, active* (malicious) | Corrupts < *t* parties; deviates arbitrarily | Complaint rounds, VSS verification, identifiable abort |
| *Adaptive* | Chooses whom to corrupt based on protocol transcript | Erasures, or DKG with forward-secure channels |
| *Mobile* | Corrupts different sets of parties over time (bounded per epoch) | Proactive secret sharing [6] |

A further axis is *network synchrony*: DKG complaint phases need a synchronous broadcast channel, whereas asynchronous VSS/DKG constructions (e.g., Cachin–Kursawe–Lysyanskaya–Strobl [7]) dispense with timing assumptions at the cost of heavier machinery.

> **Definition (Robustness vs. identifiable abort).** A protocol is *robust* if it always terminates with a valid signature even when up to *t − 1* parties misbehave. It has *identifiable abort* if a deviation causes an abort **and** names at least one culprit. FROST achieves the latter but not the former — a deliberate tradeoff discussed in §4.2 [2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 FROST Two-Round Signing

Fix a threshold *t*, participant set, and signer subset *S* of size *t* with Lagrange coefficients *λᵢ* (interpolating at 0). Each signer *i* holds a long-term share *sᵢ* of the secret *x*, with group public key *Y = g^x*.

**Round 1 — commitment.** Each signer samples fresh nonces *dᵢ, eᵢ ← Z_q* (never reused, never deterministic), publishes commitments *Dᵢ = g^{dᵢ}*, *Eᵢ = g^{eᵢ}*, and a coordinator collects them into *B = {(i, Dᵢ, Eᵢ)}* and distributes the signing package *(m, B)*.

**Round 2 — signature shares.** Each signer computes, for every participant *j*, the *binding factor* *ρⱼ = H₁(j, m, B)*, which cryptographically binds *j*'s nonce commitments to this exact message and participant set. It then forms its *group commitment share* *Rᵢ = Dᵢ · Eᵢ^{ρᵢ}*, with aggregate *R = Π_{i∈S} Rᵢ*, the challenge *c = H₂(R, Y, m)*, and its signature share *zᵢ = dᵢ + eᵢ·ρᵢ + λᵢ·sᵢ·c* (mod *q*). It deletes *(dᵢ, eᵢ)* and returns *zᵢ*.

**Aggregation.** The coordinator verifies each share individually — *g^{zᵢ} ≟ Rᵢ · Yᵢ^{c·λᵢ}* with public share *Yᵢ = g^{sᵢ}* — identifying any cheater on failure (*identifiable abort*), and outputs the standard Schnorr signature *(R, z)* with *z = Σᵢ zᵢ*, verified by the ordinary single-party check [1].

Why two nonces? A single-nonce variant is vulnerable to the Drijvers–et-al. attack: an adversary that opens many concurrent signing sessions can choose its commitment *after* seeing the honest parties' commitments and solve for a forgery. The binding factor *ρᵢ* — computed over the *entire* commitment list — forces the adversary to commit to its nonces before the challenge is known, which is precisely what the security proof needs [2].

### 3.2 Distributed Key Generation: Pedersen and GJKR

**Pedersen's DKG** (the workhorse of deployed systems) runs *n* parallel Feldman VSS instances [5]: each party *Pᵢ* samples *zᵢ ← Z_q*, deals a degree-(*t − 1*) polynomial *fᵢ* with *fᵢ(0) = zᵢ*, broadcasts commitments, and privately sends shares. Recipients verify against the commitments; failures trigger *complaints*, and an accused dealer must publicly reveal the disputed share or be disqualified. The agreed qualified set *QUAL* yields joint secret *s = Σ_{i∈QUAL} zᵢ* (never computed anywhere), shares *sⱼ = Σ_{i∈QUAL} fᵢ(j)*, and public key *Y = Π_{i∈QUAL} C_{i,0}*.

The flaw, shown by Gennaro, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Rabin (Eurocrypt 1999), is that a rushing adversary — one that sees honest parties' commitments before choosing its own contribution — can *bias* the distribution of the final public key *Y* [4]. The **GJKR protocol** adds reconstruction rounds forcing the final key to be statistically uniform even against computationally unbounded adversaries [4][7]. Most deployments accept Pedersen's bias (it does not help forge FROST signatures) and reserve GJKR-style fixes for proofs demanding uniformity.

### 3.3 Proactive Secret Sharing (HJKY)

Herzberg, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Yung observed that a mobile adversary defeats static sharing by compromising shares *across time* [6]. Their refresh protocol runs in epochs: every party *Pᵢ* deals a fresh random polynomial *δᵢ(x)* of degree *t − 1* with **zero constant term**, *δᵢ(0) = 0*, via Feldman VSS, and each party adds the sub-shares it receives to its current share: *sⱼ′ = sⱼ + Σᵢ δᵢ(j)*.

Because *Σᵢ δᵢ(0) = 0*, the shared secret *s = f(0)* — and the public key *Y = g^s* — are unchanged, while every share is freshly randomized. Pre-refresh shares become useless in combination with new ones, since they lie on a different polynomial; the adversary must now compromise *t* parties *within a single epoch* [6]. The protocol also supports share *recovery* for cleaned-up parties and redistribution to a *new* committee.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The DKG Complaint Machinery, Up Close

The complaint phase is where DKG protocols earn their robustness — and their complexity. A complaint by *Pⱼ* against dealer *Pᵢ* is resolved by forcing *Pᵢ* to broadcast the disputed share *fᵢ(j)* in the clear. If it verifies against *Pᵢ*'s commitments, the *complainer* is marked faulty (false complaints can frame honest dealers); otherwise *Pᵢ* is disqualified from *QUAL*. This must execute over a **synchronous broadcast channel** — all parties must agree on the identical complaint set, or they derive incompatible shares — which is why DKG is run once at setup rather than per operation [4][5].

```python
# Pedersen DKG, per-party logic (simplified; broadcast channel assumed)
def dkg_round(dealer_id, n, t, q, g):
    # Phase 1: deal
    coeffs = [randint(1, q-1) for _ in range(t)]      # coeffs[0] = z_i
    commits = [pow(g, a, p) for a in coeffs]          # Feldman commitments
    broadcast(commits)
    shares = {j: eval_poly(coeffs, j, q) for j in range(1, n+1)}
    send_privately(shares)
    # Phase 2: verify & complain
    for (i, (c_i, s_i)) in received_deals.items():
        if pow(g, s_i, p) != prod(c_i[k] ** (my_id ** k) for k in range(t)):
            broadcast(('complaint', i, my_id))
    # Phase 3: resolve -> QUAL set -> final share
    qual = resolve_complaints()                        # synchronous agreement
    my_share = sum(deal_shares[i] for i in qual) % q
    pubkey   = prod(commits[i][0] for i in qual) % p
    return my_share, pubkey
```

### 4.2 How the Binding Factor Defeats ROS Forgeries

The *ROS* (Random inhomogeneities in an Overdetermined Solvable system) problem captures the core danger: an adversary opening *ℓ* concurrent sessions and choosing its nonce contribution after seeing honest commitments can set up a linear system whose solution yields a forgery.

FROST's defense is the binding factor *ρᵢ = H₁(i, m, B)*. Because *ρᵢ* hashes the *full ordered commitment list B*, the adversary cannot choose its commitment after seeing others' — any change to its own commitment changes *B*, hence every *ρ*, hence the challenge *c*. The security proof shows any forger here can be rewound to solve two independent discrete logarithms, contradicting the assumption [2]. Two consequences follow:

1. **True threshold flexibility.** Any *t*-subset can sign; no fixed signing set and no pre-commitment to participants are needed, unlike earlier schemes requiring the full *n* or a designated subset.
2. **Abort, but identified.** Each share *zᵢ* is individually verifiable against the public share *Yᵢ*, so a party sending garbage is *named*. Robustness is sacrificed: with identifiable abort, the coordinator excludes the culprit and retries with a different subset — the right tradeoff for deployments where persistent cheaters are removed administratively [1][2].

### 4.3 Single-Round FROST and the Preprocessing Tradeoff

RFC 9591 standardizes the two-round protocol, but the original FROST paper describes an optimized variant: signers pre-generate and exchange nonce commitments in a **non-interactive preprocessing stage**, after which each signature costs only *one* round [2]. The catch is state: each preprocessed nonce pair may be used **at most once** — reuse across two messages leaks the long-term share, exactly as nonce reuse leaks a single-party Schnorr key. Implementations must therefore maintain a strictly consumed nonce pool, and preprocessing must be per signer set or the binding factors mismatch. The RFC deliberately leaves this variant out of scope, standardizing only the two-round flow whose security does not depend on fragile nonce-pool bookkeeping [1].

### 4.4 Asynchrony: From HJKY to APSS

Cachin, Kursawe, Lysyanskaya, and Strobl showed proactive cryptosystems can run over **asynchronous** networks using *asynchronous verifiable secret sharing* (AVSS), with termination guaranteed sans timing assumptions at the price of *n ≥ 3t + 1* parties [7]; Zhou, Schneider, and van Renesse's **APSS** refined this into a practical asynchronous proactive sharing scheme. The deployment rule is clean: bounded-delay permissioned settings use synchronous HJKY refresh (*n ≥ 2t + 1*, cheap); wide-area adversarial networks use AVSS-based refresh (*n ≥ 3t + 1*, heavier) [7]. Either way, the epoch cadence is a security parameter: the refresh interval must be shorter than the time a mobile adversary needs to compromise *t* parties — an operational measurement, and the most commonly under-specified part of proactive deployments.

### 4.5 Deployment Realities: Key Rotation, Identifiable Abort, and Hedged Nonces

Three engineering details separate a correct FROST deployment from a broken one:

1. **Hedged nonce generation.** RFC 9591's `nonce_generate` mixes fresh CSPRNG output with the signer's secret key material, so even a catastrophic RNG failure does not immediately expose the share [1].
2. **Share backup and recovery.** Proactive refresh *invalidates* old shares; a backup restored from before the last epoch is useless by design. Disaster recovery must back up the *current* epoch's shares or re-run recovery through the honest majority.
3. **Coordinator trust.** The FROST coordinator learns nothing secret — only publicly verifiable commitments and shares — but it can deny service by refusing to aggregate. Liveness-sensitive deployments replicate or rotate the coordinator role.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Formal Results

### 5.1 Security Theorems

> **Theorem (FROST unforgeability [2]).** FROST is *existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack* (TS-UF-0) assuming the hardness of the discrete logarithm problem in **G** and modeling *H₁, H₂* as random oracles, against any PPT adversary corrupting at most *t − 1* of the *n* participants. The reduction loses a factor linear in the number of signing sessions and hash queries.

> **Theorem (DKG correctness [4][5]).** Pedersen's DKG terminates with all honest parties holding consistent shares and agreeing on the public key *Y*, provided at most *t − 1* parties are malicious and broadcast is synchronous. The GJKR variant additionally guarantees *Y* statistically close to uniform even against a rushing adversary.

> **Theorem (Proactive security [6]).** The HJKY refresh protocol is secure against a mobile adversary that corrupts at most *t − 1* parties per epoch: the adversary's view across epochs is simulatable without the secret, and refreshed shares are independent of pre-refresh shares conditioned on the (unchanged) secret.

### 5.2 Quantitative Comparison

Round counts are for signing; DKG is one-time.

| Scheme | Signing rounds | Assumption | Threshold flexibility | Robustness | Standardized |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Naive distributed Schnorr | 2 | DLOG | fixed *n* | no | no |
| MuSig2 (2-round multisig) | 2 | OMDL/AGM | *n*-of-*n* only | no | — |
| **FROST** [1][2] | **2** (1 with preprocessing) | DLOG | any *t*-of-*n* subset | identifiable abort | **RFC 9591** |
| Threshold ECDSA (GG18/Lindell) | 7–9 | Paillier / OT | any *t*-of-*n* | identifiable abort | no |
| Threshold BLS | 1 | pairing-friendly curve | any *t*-of-*n* | yes | — |

FROST's advantage is structural: Schnorr linearity means signature shares aggregate by *addition*, whereas ECDSA's *k⁻¹* inversion forces expensive multiplicative-to-additive conversion. In practice, FROST signing shares cost each signer roughly two scalar multiplications, and RFC 9591 ciphersuites — FROST(P-256, SHA-256), FROST(secp256k1, SHA-256), FROST(Ed25519, SHA-512), FROST(Ed448, SHAKE256), FROST(ristretto255, SHA-512) — let deployers match their existing curve ecosystem, with the EdDSA ciphersuites producing signatures verifiable by *unmodified* Ed25519/Ed448 verifiers [1].

### 5.3 Implementation Landscape

Production-grade implementations exist in Rust (the ZF FROST workspace tracking RFC 9591), Go, and TypeScript, several with audited DKG modules implementing Feldman VSS [1]. The DKG specified alongside FROST in the CFRG drafts runs in two rounds with identifiable abort: exclude cheaters, don't tolerate them.

## 6 Limitations

1. **No robustness, by design.** A single malicious signer can force an abort; FROST identifies the culprit but cannot complete the signature without them. Systems requiring guaranteed liveness under active attack must either over-provision signers or use robust (heavier) protocols.
2. **Synchrony for setup.** Both Pedersen DKG and HJKY refresh need a synchronous broadcast channel for complaint resolution. Asynchronous alternatives exist [7] but roughly double the party requirement (*n ≥ 3t + 1*) and complicate deployment.
3. **Long-term-share erasure.** Adaptive-adversary security requires erasing ephemeral randomness (nonces, DKG dealing polynomials) after use. Without reliable erasure, a later corruption retroactively exposes past nonces — hedged generation limits but does not eliminate the damage.
4. **No post-quantum instantiation.** FROST rests entirely on the discrete logarithm problem; a cryptographically relevant quantum computer breaks every RFC 9591 ciphersuite. Threshold lattice signatures are active research, not yet standardized.
5. **Key recovery is impossible by construction.** If *n − t + 1* shares are permanently lost, the key is gone forever — which is the point, but it makes share-loss the dominant operational risk, ahead of share-theft.
6. **Bias subtleties in DKG.** Pedersen's DKG public-key bias is harmless for FROST signing but can invalidate proofs that assume a uniformly random key; deployments with such requirements should use the GJKR variant [4].

## 7 Conclusion

Threshold cryptography has crossed the gap from theory to infrastructure. The pipeline is complete and standardized: **Feldman-verifiable DKG** births a key no one ever holds; **FROST** lets any quorum sign in two rounds with unforgeability that survives concurrent sessions via the binding-factor construction; and **proactive secret sharing** keeps the key alive indefinitely against a mobile adversary by re-randomizing every share each epoch while the public key stands still. The tradeoffs are explicit and defensible — identifiable abort instead of robustness, synchrony at setup instead of asynchrony everywhere, hedged randomness instead of fragile state.

Open problems remain where practice outruns theory: fully asynchronous DKG with optimal resilience is still being refined [4]; post-quantum threshold signatures lack standards; and the operational science of choosing refresh cadences against realistic mobile adversaries is in its infancy. But the core result stands: with RFC 9591, threshold Schnorr is a deployable, audited, interoperable primitive — and the single key sitting alone on a server should now be regarded as what it is: a legacy design.
