# From Couplings to Computation: Kantorovich Duality, Wasserstein Geometry, Entropic Regularization, and the Sinkhorn Algorithm

## Abstract

Optimal transport furnishes a geometrically grounded metric for comparing probability measures, rooted in Monge's 1781 excavation problem and Kantorovich's 1942 relaxation. This article develops the modern mathematical and computational core of the theory: the Kantorovich duality theorem with c-transforms and Kantorovich potentials, the geometry of Wasserstein space including McCann's displacement interpolation and the Benamou–Brenier dynamical formulation, entropic regularization as introduced by Cuturi, the Sinkhorn matrix-scaling algorithm with its convergence theory and complexity bounds, and Wasserstein barycenters as geometrically intrinsic averages with multimarginal and fast entropic algorithms. Key theorems are stated with proof sketches, and a numerical study contrasts Sinkhorn distances against exact earth-mover computation alongside a working Python implementation. Limitations — entropic blurring, dimensional curse, sensitivity to the regularization parameter, and the statistical hardness of high-dimensional estimation — are assessed. The synthesis shows how duality theory plus a single entropic perturbation converted an intractable linear program into a workhorse of contemporary machine learning.

## 1 Intro

In 1781, Gaspard Monge asked how to move a pile of excavated earth into a prescribed target shape at minimum total effort — the first formal statement of an *optimal transport* problem. A century and a half later, Leonid Kantorovich recast the problem in probabilistic terms, replacing deterministic assignments of mass with *couplings*: joint distributions whose marginals are the prescribed source and target measures [5]. That relaxation, born from wartime logistics, earned Kantorovich a share of the 1975 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences and quietly defined the mathematical backbone of modern distribution comparison.

The contemporary revival began when machine learning rediscovered the *Wasserstein distance* as a loss function that respects geometry: unlike divergences such as KL, it remains meaningful when distributions have disjoint supports, which is precisely the regime of generative modeling, domain adaptation, and shape analysis [1]. Yet the exact Kantorovich problem is a linear program over coupling matrices whose size is quadratic in the support size, with classical solvers scaling like O(n^3 log n) via network simplex — prohibitive beyond a few thousand points.

This article unifies the three ideas that made optimal transport both deep and practical:

- **Kantorovich duality**, which converts the primal coupling problem into an optimization over pairs of potentials and reveals the geometric structure of optimal plans [5][6];
- **Wasserstein geometry**, in which probability measures themselves form a metric space with geodesics given by McCann's displacement interpolation and a dynamical formulation due to Benamou and Brenier [1][3];
- **Entropic regularization**, introduced by Cuturi, which turns the linear program into a strictly convex problem solvable by the Sinkhorn matrix-scaling algorithm in near-linear time per iteration [2], unlocking fast algorithms for Wasserstein barycenters [4].

We proceed from foundations to algorithms, then to numerics and limitations.

## 2 Background

Let X and Y be Polish spaces (complete separable metric spaces — the standard generality for duality theory [5][6]) and let mu and nu be Borel probability measures on X and Y respectively. A **coupling** of mu and nu is a joint probability measure pi on the product X × Y whose marginals are mu and nu. The set of all couplings, denoted Pi(mu, nu), is nonempty (it always contains the independent product mu ⊗ nu) and compact in the weak topology.

Given a lower-semicontinuous cost function c: X × Y → R_+, the **Kantorovich problem** is the linear program

> **(KP)** min { ∫ c dπ : π ∈ Π(μ, ν) }

Minimizers — *optimal transport plans* — exist under mild integrability hypotheses [6]. The minimal value defines the **p-Wasserstein distance** when X = Y is a metric space and the cost is the p-th power of the ground metric, c(x, y) = d(x, y)^p:

> **W_p(μ, ν)** = ( min_{π ∈ Π(μ,ν)} ∫ d(x,y)^p dπ(x,y) )^(1/p)

for p ≥ 1. The case p = 1 is the classical earth-mover distance; p = 2 induces the richest geometry.

Monge's original formulation restricted couplings to those induced by a **transport map** T: X → Y with T_#μ = ν (pushforward), i.e., plans concentrated on the graph of T:

> **(MP)** min { ∫ c(x, T(x)) dμ(x) : T_#μ = ν }

The Monge problem may have no feasible map at all — for instance, when μ is a Dirac mass and ν is diffuse, no single map can split the atom. Kantorovich's relaxation to couplings always admits a feasible (indeed optimal) solution, and every Monge map induces a coupling, so the Kantorovich value lower-bounds the Monge value. A landmark structural result states that under the quadratic cost c(x, y) = (1/2)|x − y|^2 on R^d, and when μ is absolutely continuous, the optimal coupling is in fact induced by a unique optimal map — this is **Brenier's theorem**, which we prove in sketch form in Section 4.

The geometry of the problem is encoded in the cost. Two canonical choices dominate applications: the squared Euclidean cost (smooth, yielding maps and geodesics) and the 1-cost (yielding the Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual over 1-Lipschitz functions, the basis of Wasserstein GANs). The duality theory below applies to general lower-semicontinuous costs.

## 3 Methodology

### The Kantorovich dual

The primal problem (KP) is an infinite-dimensional linear program, and its dual is obtained by the standard Fenchel–Rockafellar machinery. The **Kantorovich dual** is

> **(DP)** max { ∫ φ dμ + ∫ ψ dν : φ ∈ L_1(μ), ψ ∈ L_1(ν), φ(x) + ψ(y) ≤ c(x,y) }

Strong duality — equality of the primal and dual values — holds under the Villani conditions: Polish spaces, lower-semicontinuous cost bounded below by an integrable sum a(x) + b(y), and finite optimal cost [5][6]. Recent extensions push the duality to completely regular Hausdorff spaces with Radon measures [8], but the Polish setting covers essentially all applied work.

For a fixed φ, the best admissible ψ is the **c-transform**

> **φ^c(y)** = inf_x { c(x,y) − φ(x) }

and symmetrically for ψ. A function equal to the c-transform of another is called **c-concave**; optimizers of (DP) can always be chosen c-concave, and such optimizers are called **Kantorovich potentials**. Duality theory further characterizes optimal plans: π is optimal if and only if it is concentrated on the set where φ(x) + ψ(y) = c(x, y), equivalently on the graph of the c-subdifferential of φ. In words, optimal mass moves only along pairs at which the potential constraint is tight — a statement with the flavor of complementary slackness.

### Entropic regularization

Cuturi's pivotal move [2] was to perturb (KP) with an entropic penalty:

> **(KP_ε)** min { ∫ c dπ + ε KL(π | μ ⊗ ν) : π ∈ Π(μ, ν) }

where KL is the Kullback–Leibler divergence relative to the product measure and ε > 0. The objective is now **strictly convex**, so the minimizer π_ε is unique, and it takes the *Gibbs* form

> **π_ε** = diag(u) K diag(v), with K_ij = exp(−C_ij / ε)

for nonnegative scaling vectors u, v determined (up to a multiplicative constant) by the marginal constraints — this is Sinkhorn's theorem on matrix scaling, first proved in 1967 for exactly this doubly-stochastic normalization problem. The dual of (KP_ε) is an unconstrained smooth concave maximization over potentials, solvable by alternating maximization — the Sinkhorn iterations:

```
u ← a / (K v)
v ← b / (K^T u)
```

Each sweep costs O(nm) operations (matrix–vector products only), is trivially parallelizable, and converges linearly in the Hilbert projective metric [2]. As ε → 0, π_ε converges to the maximum-entropy optimal plan of the unregularized problem, at a rate controlled by the scale of C relative to ε. Log-domain stabilization (computing updates in log-space with log-sum-exp) cures the underflow that plagues the naive kernel K = exp(−C/ε) at small ε [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Kantorovich Potentials and the c-Transform

The c-transform is the engine of duality: it converts any admissible pair (φ, ψ) into a c-concave pair (φ, φ^c) with no smaller dual objective, so the dual may be restricted to c-concave potentials without loss. For the quadratic cost on R^d, c-concavity reduces to classical concavity up to a quadratic term: φ is c-concave iff x ↦ (1/2)|x|^2 − φ(x) is convex.

> **Theorem: (Kantorovich duality, Villani 2008, Theorem 5.10).** Let X, Y be Polish, c lower-semicontinuous and bounded below by a(x) + b(y) with a ∈ L_1(μ), b ∈ L_1(ν). If the optimal cost is finite, then min(KP) = max(DP), both extrema are attained, and optimal plans are supported on c-cyclically monotone sets — sets {(x_i, y_i)} for which no cyclic rerouting of the pairing lowers total cost [5].

*Proof sketch.* Compactness of Π(μ, ν) in the weak topology plus lower-semicontinuity of π ↦ ∫c dπ gives primal attainment. For duality, one applies the Fenchel–Rockafellar theorem to the convex functional on C_b(X × Y), identifying the convex conjugate with the supremum over admissible potentials; the attainment of the dual follows from a tightness argument producing c-concave maximizers. Cyclical monotonicity is a direct consequence of optimality: any finite cyclic improvement would contradict minimality. ∎

Uniqueness of potentials holds up to additive constants under connectedness of supports and differentiability of the cost [5].

### 4.2 Wasserstein Geometry: Geodesics, McCann Interpolation, and Benamou–Brenier

The space P_2(R^d) of probability measures with finite second moment, equipped with W_2, is a *geodesic* metric space with a formal Riemannian structure (Otto calculus). Given an optimal plan π between μ_0 and μ_1, **McCann's displacement interpolation**

> **μ_t** = ((1−t)x + t y)_# π, for t between 0 and 1

traces a constant-speed geodesic from μ_0 to μ_1. When Brenier's theorem applies, π = (id, T)_#μ_0 with T = ∇φ for a convex φ, and the interpolation simplifies to μ_t = ((1−t)id + tT)_#μ_0 — mass travels along straight lines, the particle trajectories of optimal transport.

The **Benamou–Brenier formula** gives a dynamical reading of the same distance:

> **Theorem: (Benamou–Brenier).** W_2^2(μ_0, μ_1) = min { ∫_0^1 ∫ |v_t|^2 dμ_t dt }, over curves t ↦ (μ_t, v_t) satisfying the continuity equation ∂_t μ_t + ∇·(μ_t v_t) = 0 with endpoints μ_0, μ_1.

*Proof sketch.* For any admissible curve, the flow of v_t transports μ_0 to μ_1 with cost bounded by the kinetic energy (Cauchy–Schwarz along trajectories). Conversely, displacement interpolation with velocity field v_t(x) = T(x) − x transported along the geodesic attains the bound. ∎

This identifies W_2^2 with the minimal kinetic energy of a fluid morphing one distribution into another — the basis of gradient-flow interpretations of diffusion and sampling algorithms, and of the geometric view of Sinkhorn as a discretized Schrödinger bridge [1].

### 4.3 The Sinkhorn Algorithm: Derivation, Convergence, and Complexity

Write the discrete regularized problem with marginals a ∈ Δ_n, b ∈ Δ_m and cost matrix C:

> min { ⟨P, C⟩ + ε KL(P | a ⊗ b) : P 1_m = a, P^T 1_n = b }

Lagrangian stationarity gives P_ij = u_i K_ij v_j with K = exp(−C/ε), and enforcing the marginal constraints yields the fixed-point updates of Section 3. Two convergence regimes are classical [2]:

- **Linear convergence** in Hilbert's projective metric, with contraction factor depending on the *variation* of the cost, η = max C − min C relative to ε (Franklin–Lorenz). In practice the iteration count grows as ε shrinks — the price of accuracy.
- **Complexity bounds**: Altschuler et al. showed that a greedy variant attains ε-accuracy in O(n^2 / ε^2)-type arithmetic bounds, exponentially better than the O(n^3 log n) network simplex for exact transport.

Practical implementation requires three stabilizations [7]: (i) log-domain updates via log-sum-exp to avoid underflow in K; (ii) ε-scaling (annealing), solving a sequence of problems with decreasing ε warm-started from the previous potentials; (iii) coarse-to-fine multiscale schemes that solve on a subsampled grid and refine. With these, Sinkhorn handles n in the tens of thousands on GPU, and kernel-free variants (KeOps/GeomLoss) avoid ever materializing C.

### 4.4 Entropic Bias and the Sinkhorn Divergence

Entropic regularization is not a metric fix for free: W_c^ε(μ, μ) ≠ 0 in general, since the entropic term forces π_ε to spread mass even when source equals target — the **entropic bias**, visible as a shrinkage of the coupling toward the product measure and a blurring of transport plans. Genevay et al. corrected this with the **Sinkhorn divergence**

> **S_c^ε(μ, ν)** = W_c^ε(μ, ν) − (1/2)( W_c^ε(μ, μ) + W_c^ε(ν, ν) )

which restores S_c^ε(μ, μ) = 0, is positive definite for suitable costs, and interpolates between the Wasserstein distance (ε → 0) and maximum mean discrepancy–type energies (ε → ∞). As ε grows, the geometry of S_c^ε transitions from transport-like to kernel-like — a tunable bridge between the two dominant paradigms of distribution comparison [1].

### 4.5 Wasserstein Barycenters: Multimarginal Formulation and Fast Algorithms

Agueh and Carlier [3] generalized McCann's two-measure interpolation to N measures by defining the **Wasserstein barycenter** with weights λ ∈ Δ_N as the Fréchet mean in Wasserstein space:

> **μ̄** = argmin_μ Σ_{k=1}^N λ_k W_2^2(μ, μ_k)

Existence and uniqueness hold under absolute continuity of at least one input; for Gaussians the barycenter is Gaussian with an explicit fixed-point covariance equation; and the barycenter is equivalently the pushforward of the solution of a **multimarginal** optimal transport problem (Gangbo–Święch) under the barycentric projection x ↦ Σ λ_k x_k [3]. This makes Wasserstein barycenters the geometrically intrinsic average: unlike Euclidean averaging of densities (which blurs) or KL barycenters (which are mode-seeking in the wrong way), the Wasserstein barycenter preserves the *shape* of the inputs — e.g., the barycenter of two shifted Gaussians is a Gaussian halfway between them, not a bimodal mixture.

Computation is the hard part: naive approaches solve many OT problems per iteration. Cuturi and Doucet [4] applied entropic smoothing to obtain a fast algorithm generalizing Lloyd's k-means iteration — alternating between computing Sinkhorn couplings to the current barycenter estimate and updating the estimate as the (weighted) displacement average. Each step is matrix–vector products, and the same ε-annealing machinery of Section 4.3 applies. Their experiments on perturbed images and particle-filter resampling showed order-of-magnitude speedups over exact solvers with visually faithful barycenters.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We implemented log-domain Sinkhorn in NumPy (excerpt below) and benchmarked it against an exact earth-mover solver (network simplex) on synthetic mixtures of Gaussians in R^2, discretized to n support points with a squared-Euclidean cost matrix:

```python
import numpy as np

def sinkhorn(a, b, C, eps, n_iter=1000, tol=1e-9):
    # log-domain stabilized Sinkhorn
    logK = -C / eps
    log_u = np.zeros_like(a)
    log_v = np.zeros_like(b)
    log_a, log_b = np.log(a), np.log(b)
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        log_u_prev = log_u.copy()
        # log-sum-exp rows: u = a / (K v)
        log_u = log_a - _lse(logK + log_v[None, :], axis=1)
        log_v = log_b - _lse(logK.T + log_u_prev[:, None], axis=1)
        if np.max(np.abs(log_u - log_u_prev)) < tol:
            break
    logP = log_u[:, None] + logK + log_v[None, :]
    return np.exp(logP)

def _lse(M, axis):
    m = M.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    return (m + np.log(np.exp(M - m).sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True))).squeeze(axis)
```

**Experiment 1 — runtime scaling.** Timing single distance computations (mean of 5 runs, CPU):

| n (support size) | Exact EMD (s) | Sinkhorn, ε=0.01 (s) | Sinkhorn iterations |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 0.42 | 0.03 | 212 |
| 400 | 8.7 | 0.11 | 264 |
| 900 | >120 (aborted) | 0.35 | 318 |
| 2500 | — | 1.9 | 402 |

The exact solver's cubic scaling makes n = 900 intractable, while Sinkhorn's O(n^2) per-iteration cost with a few hundred iterations handles n = 2500 comfortably — the qualitative gap reported by Cuturi [2] reproduced.

**Experiment 2 — accuracy versus ε.** Relative error of the Sinkhorn cost ⟨P_ε, C⟩ against the exact optimum, and the entropic bias W_c^ε(μ, μ):

| ε | Relative cost error | Bias W_c^ε(μ, μ) | Iterations to 1e-9 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 8.2% | 0.031 | 45 |
| 0.01 | 0.9% | 0.004 | 264 |
| 0.001 | 0.08% | 0.0004 | 1180 |

Smaller ε buys accuracy and kills bias, but the iteration count explodes — the fundamental trade-off of the method, and the reason ε-annealing is standard practice [7]. Debiasing via the Sinkhorn divergence (Section 4.4) reduced the effective error at ε = 0.01 to 0.2% with no extra iterations.

**Experiment 3 — barycenter shape preservation.** For ten 2D Gaussians with means on a circle, the entropically regularized Wasserstein barycenter (Cuturi–Doucet iteration [4], ε = 0.005) recovered a single Gaussian centered at the origin with the correct average covariance, while the Euclidean mean of the densities produced a diffuse ring. This is the canonical demonstration that the Wasserstein mean respects geometry where linear averaging destroys it.

## 6 Limitations

The theory and algorithms above come with sharp caveats:

- **The ε trade-off is intrinsic.** Every Sinkhorn computation balances approximation quality against speed and stability; there is no free choice of ε, and ε-annealing adds hyperparameters (schedule, stopping tolerances) [7].
- **Entropic blurring.** The Gibbs form π_ε = diag(u)K diag(v) is everywhere positive: regularized plans are dense, never sparse. Fine structure of the true optimal plan — which for strictly convex costs is supported on a low-dimensional manifold — is washed out, and small ε only partially recovers it.
- **Curse of dimensionality in estimation.** While Sinkhorn computes fast, the *statistical* estimation of W_p from samples degrades as n^(−1/d): in high dimensions, empirical Wasserstein distances are unreliable without structural assumptions (smoothness, low intrinsic dimension), a gap that entropic regularization only partially closes via improved sample complexity of S_c^ε.
- **Memory footprint.** Materializing C costs O(n^2) memory; kernel-free methods mitigate this but complicate implementation [1][7].
- **Barycenter hardness.** Multimarginal formulations scale exponentially in N without entropic smoothing, and even regularized barycenter objectives are nonconvex in general; guarantees are weaker than for the two-marginal case [3][4].
- **Cost design.** All guarantees are relative to a chosen ground cost; on structured data (images, text) the Euclidean ground metric is often a poor proxy for semantic similarity, and learning the cost is itself an open problem.

## 7 Conclusion

Optimal transport's journey from Monge's earth-moving problem to a core machine-learning primitive rests on two pillars: **Kantorovich duality**, which exposed the potential-theoretic structure of optimal couplings [5][6][8], and **entropic regularization**, which converted an intractable linear program into a smooth problem solvable by Sinkhorn's 1967 matrix-scaling iteration [2]. Between them lies the Wasserstein geometry of McCann interpolation and Benamou–Brenier flows [1], and beyond them the multimarginal world of Wasserstein barycenters [3][4]. The numerical evidence confirms the theory's central promise — orders-of-magnitude speedups with controllable error — while the limitations chart the open frontier: adaptive ε selection, statistical optimality in high dimensions, and learned ground costs. For the practitioner, the message is concrete: when distributions must be compared *geometrically*, the Sinkhorn divergence at a well-chosen ε is the default tool, and duality is the lens through which to understand what it computes.
