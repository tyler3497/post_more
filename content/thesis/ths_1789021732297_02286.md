# Simultaneous Localization and Mapping: Factor Graphs, iSAM2, and the Geometry of Loop Closure

## Abstract

Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) asks a simple question: how can a robot construct a map of an unknown environment while localizing itself within it, using only noisy exteroceptive and proprioceptive sensing? This thesis traces the field's intellectual arc from the *stochastic map* formulation of Smith, Self and Cheeseman, through the extended Kalman filter SLAM of the 1990s whose linearization-induced inconsistency was rigorously documented [1], to the Rao-Blackwellized particle filtering of FastSLAM [2] and its consistency analysis [3], and on to the factor-graph revolution crystallized in g2o [5] and GTSAM. We develop the Bayes tree data structure and the iSAM2 algorithm [4], which made truly incremental smoothing possible via incremental variable reordering and fluid relinearization. Loop closure is examined in its dual nature: as a geometric constraint in pose-graph optimization and as a place-recognition problem, contrasting the probabilistic generative modeling of FAB-MAP [6] with the bag-of-binary-words machinery of DBoW2 [7]. Finally, we survey robust back-ends that survive false loop closures, from switchable constraints [8] to graduated non-convexity [9], and close with empirical complexity results and current failure modes.

## 1 Introduction

A robot in an unmapped building faces a chicken-and-egg dilemma: to localize it needs a map, and to map it needs to localize. *Simultaneous Localization and Mapping* (SLAM) resolves this jointly, fusing odometry, inertial, laser, and visual measurements into a coherent trajectory and environment model [1]. Over four decades, the field's center of gravity has migrated decisively — from **recursive filtering** over a single Gaussian, through **particle-based** multi-hypothesis representations, to **sparse nonlinear least squares over factor graphs**, the formulation underpinning virtually every high-performance system in production.

This thesis makes five contributions to the reader's understanding:

1. A unified derivation showing how the stochastic-map EKF, FastSLAM, and GraphSLAM are approximate inference schemes over the *same* joint posterior — differing only in which independencies they exploit and which approximations they tolerate.
2. A precise account of why EKF-SLAM becomes **inconsistent** (overconfident), grounded in observability analysis rather than folklore.
3. A constructive exposition of the **Bayes tree** and **iSAM2** [4]: how a directed clique tree turns matrix-factorization updates into local graph edits, enabling incremental reordering and fluid relinearization with no periodic batch resets.
4. A dual treatment of **loop closure** — geometric (pose-graph optimization on manifolds) and perceptual (FAB-MAP's generative place model [6] versus DBoW2's vocabulary machinery [7]).
5. A survey of **robust back-ends**, from switchable constraints [8] to graduated non-convexity [9], that keep the optimizer honest when place recognition hallucinates.

**Notation.** Poses $x_i \in SE(2)$ or $SE(3)$; landmarks $m_j \in \mathbb{R}^d$; controls $u_i$; observations $z_k$. The full state is $\theta = \{x_{1:T}, m_{1:M}\}$; $\|e\|^2_\Sigma = e^\top \Sigma^{-1} e$ is the squared Mahalanobis norm.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The stochastic map

The modern SLAM problem was born in 1986–1987, when Smith, Self and Cheeseman introduced the *stochastic map*: a joint Gaussian over robot pose and landmark positions whose covariance explicitly encodes the **correlations** between every pair of estimates [1]. If two landmarks are observed from the same uncertain pose, their estimates become correlated; ignoring that correlation produces maps that are quietly, catastrophically wrong. The stochastic map keeps the full covariance, and its dense landmark–landmark block is precisely what later research spent twenty years trying to sparsify.

> **Theorem (Convergence of linear SLAM):** In the linear-Gaussian case, every landmark covariance submatrix decreases monotonically to a lower bound set by the initial pose uncertainty, and in the limit all estimates become fully correlated [1].

The catch is *linear*: real robots rotate, and rotation is nonlinear.

### 2.2 EKF-SLAM and the consistency trap

Extended Kalman filter SLAM linearizes the motion model $x_k = f(x_{k-1}, u_k) + w_k$ and observation model $z_k = h(x_k, m) + v_k$ about the current estimate at every step, maintaining the mean and covariance with the familiar predict–update cycle:

```python
import numpy as np

def ekf_slam_predict(mu, Sigma, u, Q):
    """EKF-SLAM prediction. mu: state mean, Sigma: dense covariance."""
    F = jacobian_motion(mu, u)
    mu_pred = motion_model(mu, u)
    Sigma_pred = F @ Sigma @ F.T
    Sigma_pred[:3, :3] += Q
    return mu_pred, Sigma_pred

def ekf_slam_update(mu, Sigma, z, R):
    H = jacobian_observation(mu)
    S = H @ Sigma @ H.T + R
    K = Sigma @ H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)
    mu_new = mu + K @ (z - observation_model(mu))
    Sigma_new = (np.eye(len(mu)) - K @ H) @ Sigma
    return mu_new, Sigma_new
```

Two structural defects doomed the EKF as a large-scale solution. First, **complexity**: every observation updates the full dense covariance, costing $O(n^2)$ per step in the number of landmarks $n$ [1]. Second, **inconsistency**: the filter's reported covariance becomes smaller than the true error covariance. Observability analysis pinned down the mechanism: the linearized SLAM system has a three-dimensional unobservable subspace (global translation and rotation), but the EKF evaluates Jacobians at *different* linearization points across time steps, spuriously injecting information along unobservable directions. The filter "learns" absolute orientation from nothing — a free lunch that manifests as overconfident, drifting estimates [1]. The verdict, via Julier and Uhlmann's counterexample and the analyses of Bailey, Huang and Dissanayake, is now theorem: **EKF-SLAM is inconsistent except in the linear case**, and no tuning repairs a structural observability violation.

### 2.3 FastSLAM: Rao-Blackwellization to the rescue

FastSLAM [2] attacked both defects with a factorization. Conditioned on the robot's *path*, landmark estimates become independent:

$$p(x_{1:t}, m \mid z_{1:t}, u_{1:t}) = p(x_{1:t} \mid z_{1:t}, u_{1:t}) \prod_{j=1}^{M} p(m_j \mid x_{1:t}, z_{1:t}).$$

A particle filter samples the trajectory posterior — each particle carrying its own bank of tiny EKFs, one per landmark. Updates touch only observed landmarks, yielding $O(M \log N)$ complexity, and each particle maintains its own **data-association hypothesis**, making the filter far more robust to ambiguous correspondences than the single-hypothesis EKF [2].

The honeymoon ended with Bailey, Nieto and Nebot's analysis [3]: FastSLAM *degenerates* with time. Particle depletion collapses trajectory diversity, and because each particle's map is conditioned on a single sampled path, the filter systematically underestimates uncertainty — it is **optimistic in the long term regardless of particle count**. The lesson stuck: sampling buys robustness to nonlinearity and data association, but pays with statistical guarantees.

| Era | Representative method | Per-step complexity | Consistency | Data association |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1986–2000 | EKF-SLAM (stochastic map) | $O(n^2)$ | Inconsistent (nonlinear) | Single hypothesis |
| 2002–2006 | FastSLAM / FastSLAM 2.0 | $O(M \log N)$ | Degenerates / optimistic | Multi-hypothesis (particles) |
| 2006–2012 | GraphSLAM, Square-Root SAM, iSAM/iSAM2 | $O(n)$–$O(n^{1.5})$ amortized | Consistent if relinearized properly | Resolved via optimization |
| 2011+ | g2o / GTSAM factor-graph back-ends | Solver-dependent, near-linear | Consistent (batch relinearization) | Robust kernels / switches |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 SLAM as MAP inference on a factor graph

All modern smoothing approaches start from the same maximum-a-posteriori objective. Assuming Gaussian noise and independent measurements, the posterior factors as

$$p(\theta \mid z) \propto \prod_i \phi_i(\theta_i), \qquad \phi_i(\theta_i) \propto \exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|h_i(\theta_i) - z_i\|^2_{\Sigma_i}\right),$$

and the MAP estimate is the minimizer of a **nonlinear least-squares** problem:

$$\theta^\star = \arg\min_\theta \sum_i \|h_i(\theta_i) - z_i\|^2_{\Sigma_i}. \tag{1}$$

A **factor graph** is the bipartite representation of this factorization: variable nodes (poses, landmarks) connected to factor nodes (odometry, observations, priors, loop closures). Its power is sparsity — each factor touches few variables — which survives into the linearized system. Linearizing (1) about $\theta_0$ yields the normal equations, or in square-root form $R\,\Delta = d$ with $R$ upper-triangular; the sparsity of $R$ is governed by **variable ordering**, which is why COLAMD-style heuristics and incremental reordering matter so much [4][5].

Gauss-Newton or Levenberg-Marquardt iterations solve successive linearizations until convergence. The general-purpose machinery for exactly this — vertices, edges, robust kernels, manifold-aware optimization — was crystallized in **g2o** [5]:

```cpp
// g2o pose-graph edge: a binary factor between two SE2 vertices
class EdgeSE2 : public g2o::BaseBinaryEdge<3, Eigen::Vector3d, VertexSE2, VertexSE2> {
 public:
  void computeError() override {
    const VertexSE2* v1 = static_cast<const VertexSE2*>(_vertices[0]);
    const VertexSE2* v2 = static_cast<const VertexSE2*>(_vertices[1]);
    // error = measurement^{-1} * (v1^{-1} * v2), on the SE(2) manifold
    _error = (_measurement.inverse() * v1->estimate().inverse() * v2->estimate()).toVector();
  }
};
// A Huber robust kernel attaches in one line:
edge->setRobustKernel(new g2o::RobustKernelHuber());
```

### 3.2 From batch to incremental: the iSAM lineage

Solving (1) from scratch at every timestep is wasteful: each new measurement perturbs only part of the solution. **Square-Root SAM** showed that smoothing beats filtering — keeping the whole trajectory is *cheaper*, because the information matrix stays sparse while the EKF's covariance densifies. **iSAM** made it incremental by updating the square-root information matrix $R$ with Givens rotations as new factors arrive, reusing prior factorization work.

But iSAM had two wounds: periodic **batch reordering** (to control fill-in on loopy trajectories) and periodic **batch relinearization** (to keep the linearization valid). Both are expensive, global, and anti-online. iSAM2 [4] heals both with a single data structure: the **Bayes tree**.

### 3.3 The Bayes tree and iSAM2

Eliminating variables from a factor graph in some ordering produces a chordal Bayes net; grouping the elimination cliques yields the **Bayes tree** — a directed tree whose nodes (cliques) store conditional densities $p(F_C \mid S_C)$ over frontal variables given separators. It is the square-root information matrix $R$ made legible as probability: matrix updates become **local tree edits** [4].

The iSAM2 update cycle is then beautifully mechanical:

1. **Add** new factors; convert affected factor-graph fragments into conditionals.
2. **Detach** the tree top containing all variables involved in new or relinearized factors.
3. **Re-eliminate** the detached part (constrained COLAMD) and reattach — *incremental variable reordering*, applied only where needed.
4. **Fluid relinearization**: relinearize a variable only when its estimate deviates from the linearization point by more than a threshold $\beta$.
5. **Back-substitution** down the tree yields the updated MAP estimate; marginal covariances are available locally.

No periodic batch step remains — iSAM2 is *fully* incremental [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The linearization trap, dissected

Why exactly does linearizing at the current estimate break consistency? The true nonlinear SLAM system is unobservable under global rigid transformations — shifting every pose and landmark by the same $SE(2)$ element leaves all relative measurements unchanged. A consistent estimator's information matrix must therefore retain a three-dimensional nullspace. The EKF, however, evaluates the motion Jacobian at $\hat{x}_{k-1|k-1}$ and the observation Jacobian at $\hat{x}_{k|k-1}$ — *different* points — so the nullspaces of the two linearizations no longer align, and the filter accumulates phantom information along the absolute-orientation direction [1].

> **Theorem (Observability-constrained consistency):** A linearized estimator is consistent only if the unobservable subspace of every Jacobian evaluated during the run coincides with that of the underlying nonlinear system. EKF-SLAM violates this whenever the state estimate moves between linearization points — i.e., always [1].

The remedies that survived all respect this theorem: linearize once per batch solve (smoothing), or enforce observability constraints explicitly. Filtering with a moving linearization point was the original sin of 1990s SLAM.

### 4.2 Why smoothing won: sparsity is destiny

The decisive argument for smoothing over filtering is algebraic. The EKF maintains the **covariance** $P$ — dense, because marginalizing out past poses correlates everything with everything. Smoothing maintains the **information matrix** $\Lambda = P^{-1}$ (or its square root $R$) — sparse, because conditioning on the full trajectory *decouples* measurements. For a trajectory with $T$ poses and local measurements, $\Lambda$ is block-tridiagonal plus landmark blocks: factorization costs $O(T + M)$ up to ordering effects, versus $O(M^2)$ per EKF step. GraphSLAM first exploited the information form at scale; Square-Root SAM showed the square-root factor is the numerically superior object; iSAM made it incremental; iSAM2 made it *fully* incremental. The complexity table in §2.3 is the trace of a field discovering that **the right linear-algebraic object was the information matrix all along**.

### 4.3 Inside the Bayes tree

The Bayes tree deserves its reputation as the most elegant data structure in SLAM. Start from the chordal Bayes net induced by elimination: each variable gets a conditional over its separator, and maximal cliques become tree nodes storing $p(F_C \mid S_C)$. The root's separator is empty, so multiplying all conditionals recovers the full posterior — the tree *is* the density, not an index into it [4].

Three operations make it computational rather than merely representational:

- **Incremental inference**: new factors touch variables $X_{\text{aff}}$; only cliques on paths from those variables to the root are detached and re-eliminated. Everything below the detachment frontier is provably unaffected.
- **Incremental reordering**: re-elimination applies COLAMD constrained to keep recently-accessed variables late in the ordering, bounding fill-in without a global reorder pass.
- **Fluid relinearization**: each variable tracks $\|\theta - \theta_0\|$ against a threshold $\beta$; only violators relinearize, and their cliques re-enter the detached set. Converged variables stop paying linearization tax.

The practical consequence [4]: per-step cost stays flat during exploration and spikes only locally — and briefly — when a large loop closure rewrites a big subtree. Partial state updates and marginal recovery fall out of the same structure.

### 4.4 Loop closure: where geometry meets appearance

Loop closure — recognizing a previously visited place — injects a long-range constraint that collapses accumulated drift. It has two halves that could hardly be more different.

**The geometric half** is pose-graph optimization: given relative-pose measurements $\tilde{T}_{ij} \in SE(3)$ between poses, minimize the sum of squared manifold residuals — a non-convex problem solved in practice by good initialization (odometry chaining, chordal relaxation) plus on-manifold Gauss-Newton, exactly the g2o/iSAM2 machinery.

**The perceptual half** — *place recognition* — decides which constraints to add. Two philosophies dominate:

- **FAB-MAP** [6] is generative and probabilistic. Images become bags of visual words; a Chow-Liu tree captures word co-occurrence; the system computes $p(L_i \mid \mathcal{Z}^k)$, the probability that the current observation comes from mapped place $L_i$ *versus a new place*. Perceptual aliasing is handled explicitly: indistinctive observations receive low probability. On the New College dataset it reached 48% recall at 100% precision, and FAB-MAP 2.0 scaled to a 1,000 km trajectory with an inverted index [6].
- **DBoW2** [7] is discriminative and engineered for speed. Binary descriptors are quantized through a vocabulary tree; an inverted index plus direct index enables sub-linear database queries; temporal consistency checks reject isolated false matches. It trades FAB-MAP's principled probabilities for real-time performance and became the loop-closure engine inside ORB-SLAM systems.

Both feed the same back-end — a candidate relative pose becomes a factor in (1) — and both occasionally lie. A single false loop closure can fold the entire map. Which brings us to robustness.

### 4.5 Robust back-ends: surviving false loop closures

Least squares has a breakdown point of zero: one bad measurement, unbounded damage. The classical fix is **M-estimation** — replace $\|r\|^2$ with a robust loss $\rho(r)$, solved by iteratively reweighted least squares with weight $w(r) = \rho'(r)/r$:

| Loss $\rho(r)$ | Behavior on large residuals | Weight $w(r) = \rho'(r)/r$ |
|---|---|---|
| Quadratic | Unbounded influence | 1 |
| Huber | Linear beyond threshold $k$ | $\min(1, k/\|r\|)$ |
| Cauchy | Logarithmic; diminishing | $1/(1 + (r/k)^2)$ |
| Truncated LS | Zero beyond $c$ (hard reject) | $1$ or $0$ |

Huber helps, but even Huber cannot save a pose graph poisoned with many false loop closures [8]. **Switchable constraints** instead make the *topology* part of the optimization: each loop-closure edge gets a switch variable $s_j \in [0,1]$ optimized jointly with the poses, so the optimizer itself turns off edges inconsistent with the emerging consensus, regularized by a prior pulling switches toward 1. The system digested up to **1,000 false positive loop closures** on standard benchmarks [8]. Siblings include dynamic covariance scaling (closed-form switch updates) and max-mixture models.

The modern endgame is **graduated non-convexity** (GNC) [9]. GNC exploits the Black–Rangarajan duality between robust losses and outlier processes: it optimizes a sequence of surrogate costs, starting convex and gradually morphing into the true non-convex robust cost (e.g., truncated least squares), warm-starting each stage from the last. Combined with certifiably optimal non-minimal solvers for the outlier-free subproblem, GNC-based pose-graph optimization tolerates **70–80% outliers**, outperforming RANSAC while needing no initial guess [9].

## 5 Empirical Results and Complexity Analysis

The theoretical complexities translate into stark practical differences:

| Method | Update cost (per step) | Memory | Handles loops incrementally? | False-closure tolerance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| EKF-SLAM | $O(n^2)$ time, $O(n^2)$ memory | Dense covariance | N/A (filter) | None — single hypothesis |
| FastSLAM (M particles) | $O(M \log n)$ | $O(Mn)$ | Partially (resampling) | Multi-hypothesis, short-term |
| Batch Gauss-Newton (g2o) | $O(n^{1.5})$ per full solve | Sparse $R$ | No — full re-solve | With robust kernels only |
| iSAM2 | $O(1)$ amortized explore; $O(k)$ local on closure | Bayes tree | **Yes** — subtree re-elimination | Via robust factors [8][9] |

Three empirical facts anchor the story. **First**, on 10,000-pose problems, iSAM2's per-step time stays in the low milliseconds during exploration, spiking only when loop closures trigger subtree re-elimination — versus batch solvers whose cost grows superlinearly with trajectory length [4]. **Second**, place recognition scales: FAB-MAP 2.0 processed a 1,000 km trajectory, and DBoW2 queries run in milliseconds against tens of thousands of images [6][7]. **Third**, robust back-ends changed what front-ends may get wrong: 1,000 injected false closures survived [8], 70–80% outlier rates survived [9] — modern systems can propose loop closures aggressively.

## 6 Limitations

- **Dynamic environments.** The static-world assumption underwrites every derivation above. Pedestrians, moved cars, and changing lighting violate it; most systems cope by outlier rejection rather than modeling, which fails gracefully until it suddenly doesn't.
- **Perceptual aliasing and appearance change.** FAB-MAP's generative model and DBoW2's vocabularies both degrade under severe illumination, weather, and seasonal change. Long-term (multi-year) place recognition remains unsolved [6][7].
- **Non-Gaussian noise.** Heavy-tailed sensor noise, multipath, and systematic calibration bias break the Gaussian factor assumption at the root of (1). Robust losses mitigate; they do not model.
- **Scale and observability in monocular SLAM.** Pure monocular systems cannot observe absolute scale; scale drift accumulates even with perfect loop closures unless anchored by metric sensors or known geometry.
- **Certifiability gaps.** GNC and convex relaxations offer empirical robustness and occasional optimality certificates, but no current incremental system *guarantees* global optimality online [9].
- **Lifelong operation.** Memory grows with the map; marginalization, sparsification, and submapping all discard information. Principled lifelong SLAM — unbounded operation with bounded compute and bounded error — remains open; iSAM2's local updates approach but do not solve it.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Four decades of SLAM trace a single arc: from one dense Gaussian (the stochastic map [1]), through sampling many hypotheses (FastSLAM [2], with its degenerative limits [3]), to optimizing sparse factor graphs — first in batch (g2o [5]), then truly incrementally via the Bayes tree and iSAM2 [4]. Loop closure matured from geometric afterthought to a dual discipline of manifold optimization and appearance-based place recognition (FAB-MAP [6], DBoW2 [7]), and the back-end learned to distrust its front-end through switchable constraints [8] and graduated non-convexity [9].

The factor-graph formulation won because it aligned the *mathematics* (sparse information matrices), the *algorithms* (incremental factorization), and the *engineering* (general solvers, robust losses) in one framework. What remains is the gap between empirical robustness and guaranteed correctness: certifiable, real-time, lifelong SLAM in dynamic worlds. The Bayes tree taught the field that the right data structure makes hard problems local; the next such insight — perhaps unifying neural scene representations with factor-graph inference — is where the next decade of theses will be written.
