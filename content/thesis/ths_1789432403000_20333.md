# The Geometry of Rare Events: Cramér's Theorem, the Gärtner–Ellis Theorem, and Freidlin–Wentzell Theory

## Abstract

Large deviation theory quantifies the *exponential* decay of probabilities of rare events, refining the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem into a precise asymptotic calculus. This thesis develops the subject from first principles: the large deviation principle and the role of the Legendre–Fenchel transform in producing rate functions; Cramér's theorem for sums of independent and identically distributed variables via exponential change of measure; Sanov's theorem for empirical measures with relative entropy as the rate function; the Gärtner–Ellis theorem extending the analysis to dependent sequences through scaled cumulant generating functions; the contraction principle and Varadhan's lemma as the theory's functorial core; and the Freidlin–Wentzell theory of small-noise diffusions, where an action functional on path space and the quasipotential govern metastability, exit times, and most probable transition paths. Numerical experiments verify Cramér asymptotics and demonstrate exponential variance reduction through importance sampling. Applications to rare-event simulation, statistical mechanics, and queueing theory are developed, and the limitations of the framework — non-steep rate functions, heavy tails, and infinite-dimensional extensions — are examined critically.

## 1 Introduction

Probability theory possesses two classical descriptions of typical behavior. The *law of large numbers* tells us that the sample mean $S_n/n$ of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) variables converges to the true mean, while the *central limit theorem* describes fluctuations of order $1/\sqrt{n}$ around that mean, which are Gaussian. But neither says anything quantitative about *atypical* behavior: the probability that the sample mean deviates from its expectation by a fixed, macroscopic amount. Such events are not merely curiosities. A communication buffer overflows, an insurance company is ruined, a climate system tips into a new regime, a Monte Carlo estimator fails to converge — in each case, the event of interest is rare, and its probability is the entire question [1].

Large deviation theory is the mathematical framework built to answer precisely this question. Its central discovery, articulated across the work of Cramér, Chernoff, Sanov, Donsker and Varadhan, Gärtner and Ellis, and Freidlin and Wentzell, is that rare-event probabilities typically decay *exponentially* in the relevant large parameter, and that the *exponent* — the rate function — can be computed through a convex duality, the Legendre–Fenchel transform, applied to a cumulant generating function [2][3].

The theory's architecture is remarkably unified. One proves a *large deviation principle* (LDP): a family of probability measures $\mu_n$ satisfies an LDP with rate function $I$ if, roughly,

$$\mu_n(A) \approx \exp\left(-n \inf_{x \in A} I(x)\right)$$

for suitable sets $A$, where the approximation is logarithmic: $(1/n)\log \mu_n(A) \to -\inf_A I$. The rate function is always nonnegative and lower semicontinuous; the infimum encodes a *least unlikely* principle — among all the ways a rare event can occur, the one with smallest rate dominates exponentially, and all others are invisible at the exponential scale [2][4].

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 establishes the foundational vocabulary: the LDP, good rate functions, and the Legendre–Fenchel transform. Section 3 describes the methodology of the field — exponential tilting, the Laplace principle, and the change-of-measure technique that underlies nearly every lower bound. Section 4 develops the four pillars in depth: Cramér's theorem, Sanov's theorem, the Gärtner–Ellis theorem, and Freidlin–Wentzell theory, together with the contraction principle and Varadhan's lemma. Section 5 presents numerical experiments confirming the theory and demonstrating its power in rare-event simulation. Section 6 confronts the limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The large deviation principle

Let $\mathcal{X}$ be a Polish space and let $\{\mu_n\}$ be a sequence of Borel probability measures on $\mathcal{X}$. Let $I: \mathcal{X} \to (0, \infty)$ be lower semicontinuous, not identically infinite.

> **Definition:** The sequence $\{\mu_n\}$ satisfies a *large deviation principle* with *rate function* $I$ if for every closed set $F$ and every open set $G$,
>
> $$\limsup_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n} \log \mu_n(F) \le -\inf_{x \in F} I(x), \qquad \liminf_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n} \log \mu_n(G) \ge -\inf_{x \in G} I(x).$$

The rate function is *good* if its sublevel sets $\{x : I(x) \le c\}$ are compact for every finite $c$; goodness guarantees that infima over closed sets are attained and is essential for the upper bound on non-compact spaces via *exponential tightness* [1].

Three remarks orient everything that follows. First, the LDP is a *logarithmic* statement: it determines exponential rates but not prefactors. Second, the infimum over the set implements a *variational principle*: the rare event occurs, with overwhelming conditional likelihood, in the least unlikely way. Third, rate functions arising in practice are almost always obtained as Legendre–Fenchel transforms, hence are convex — a fact with profound computational consequences [3].

### 2.2 The Legendre–Fenchel transform

For a function $\Lambda: \mathbb{R}^d \to (-\infty, \infty)$, the *Legendre–Fenchel transform* (convex conjugate) is

$$\Lambda^*(x) = \sup_{\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d} \left\{ \langle \theta, x \rangle - \Lambda(\theta) \right\}.$$

The transform $\Lambda^*$ is always convex and lower semicontinuous, being a supremum of affine functions. When $\Lambda$ is the *logarithmic moment generating function* (cumulant generating function) $\Lambda(\theta) = \log \mathbb{E}[e^{\langle \theta, X \rangle}]$, the conjugate $\Lambda^*(x)$ measures, in a precise sense, how "expensive" it is in exponential tilting to shift the mean of $X$ to $x$. Geometrically, $\Lambda^*(x)$ is the maximal gap between the linear function $\theta \mapsto \langle \theta, x \rangle$ and $\Lambda$ — the supporting-hyperplane gap illustrated in the first figure [3].

> **Key duality:** Under mild conditions, the rate function governing rare fluctuations of a mean is exactly the convex conjugate of the scaled cumulant generating function. This is the *engine* of the entire theory.

### 2.3 Historical lineage

The subject begins with Cramér (1938), who derived exact exponential asymptotics for tail probabilities of sums of i.i.d. variables under an exponential moment condition [6]. Chernoff (1952) extracted the now-ubiquitous exponential upper bound. Sanov (1961) lifted the analysis from means to *empirical measures*, discovering relative entropy as the governing rate function — and thereby recovering Boltzmann's 1877 combinatorial derivation of entropy as a special case. Donsker and Varadhan (1975–1983) extended Sanov's program to Markov processes, introducing a variational formula for the rate function of empirical measures of Markov chains. Gärtner (1977) and Ellis (1984) isolated the essential mechanism — convergence of scaled cumulant generating functions plus differentiability — yielding an LDP for dependent sequences without any independence hypothesis [2]. Freidlin and Wentzell (1970s–1984) transported the theory to continuous time and small-noise diffusions, where the rate function becomes an *action functional* on path space and the quasipotential governs metastability [5].

## 3 Methodology

The methodology of large deviations rests on three interlocking techniques. Understanding them once makes every theorem in Section 4 transparent, since each is essentially an implementation of the same strategy in a new setting [1][3].

**1. Exponential upper bounds via Chernoff's inequality.** For the sample mean $S_n/n$ and a threshold $a$ above the mean, Markov's inequality applied to the exponential moment gives, for any $\theta > 0$,

$$P\left(\frac{S_n}{n} \ge a\right) = P\left(e^{\theta S_n} \ge e^{n\theta a}\right) \le e^{-n\theta a}\, \mathbb{E}[e^{\theta S_n}] = \exp\left(-n\left(\theta a - \Lambda(\theta)\right)\right).$$

Optimizing over $\theta$ yields $P(S_n/n \ge a) \le \exp(-n \Lambda^*(a))$. This one-line argument already produces the correct exponential rate; the deep work lies in the matching lower bound [2].

**2. Lower bounds via exponential change of measure (tilting).** The upper bound is blind to *how* the rare event occurs. The lower bound identifies the dominant mechanism: *tilt* the distribution so that the rare event becomes typical, estimate the probability under the tilted law by the law of large numbers, and pay the price of the change of measure exactly. For i.i.d. variables with $\Lambda(\theta_0) < \infty$, define the tilted law

$$\frac{d\mu_{\theta_0}}{d\mu}(x) = \frac{e^{\theta_0 x}}{M(\theta_0)}, \qquad M(\theta_0) = e^{\Lambda(\theta_0)},$$

which has mean $\Lambda'(\theta_0)$.

**3. The Laplace principle and Varadhan's lemma.** The analytic backbone is Laplace's method: if $p_n(x) \approx e^{-nI(x)}$, then

$$\frac{1}{n}\log \int e^{nF(x)} p_n(dx) \longrightarrow \sup_x \{F(x) - I(x)\}$$

for bounded continuous $F$ — the integral is exponentially dominated by the maximizer.

| Theorem | Object | Rate function | Key hypothesis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cramér (1938) | Sample mean $S_n/n$, i.i.d. | $\Lambda^*(x)$, Legendre transform of log-mgf | $\Lambda(\theta) < \infty$ near $0$ |
| Sanov (1961) | Empirical measure $L_n$ | Relative entropy $H(\nu \mid \mu)$ | Finite alphabet (classical form) |
| Gärtner–Ellis | General $Z_n$ | $\Lambda^*$ with $\Lambda(\theta) = \lim \frac{1}{n}\log \mathbb{E} e^{n\langle\theta, Z_n\rangle}$ | Differentiability, essential smoothness |
| Freidlin–Wentzell | Small-noise diffusion paths $X^\varepsilon$ | Action $S_T(\varphi) = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^T \lvert \sigma^{-1}(\dot\varphi - b)\rvert^2 dt$ | Lipschitz coefficients, uniform ellipticity |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Cramér's Theorem: Exact Exponential Asymptotics for i.i.d. Sums

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. real-valued with logarithmic moment generating function $\Lambda(\theta) = \log \mathbb{E}[e^{\theta X_1}]$, finite in a neighborhood of the origin.

> **Theorem: (Cramér).** The laws of $S_n/n$ satisfy a full LDP on $\mathbb{R}$ with good convex rate function $I(x) = \Lambda^*(x) = \sup_\theta \{\theta x - \Lambda(\theta)\}$.

The upper bound is Chernoff's argument above, extended from half-lines to closed sets by a covering argument.

$$P\left(\frac{S_n}{n} \in (a - \delta, a + \delta)\right) \ge e^{-n(\theta_a(a+\delta) - \Lambda(\theta_a))} P_{\theta_a}\left(\frac{S_n}{n} \in (a-\delta,a+\delta)\right),$$

and the tilted probability tends to one by the weak law of large numbers.

*Example.* For Bernoulli$(p)$ variables, $\Lambda(\theta) = \log(1 - p + pe^\theta)$ and the rate function is the binary relative entropy $I(x) = x\log\frac{x}{p} + (1-x)\log\frac{1-x}{1-p}$ on the unit interval, infinite outside — the price of a biased coin masquerading at frequency $x$ is exactly the information-theoretic distance between the frequencies. For standard Gaussians, $\Lambda(\theta) = \theta^2/2$ and $I(x) = x^2/2$, recovering the familiar Gaussian tail at the exponential scale [3].

### 4.2 Sanov's Theorem: Large Deviations of Empirical Measures

Cramér's theorem concerns a one-dimensional projection — the mean.

> **Theorem: (Sanov).** If $X_i$ are i.i.d. taking values in a finite set $\Sigma$ with law $\mu$ charging every point, then $\{L_n\}$ satisfies an LDP on $\mathcal{M}_1(\Sigma)$ with the weak topology and good rate function the *relative entropy*
>
> $$H(\nu \mid \mu) = \sum_{x \in \Sigma} \nu(x) \log \frac{\nu(x)}{\mu(x)}.$$

The proof is combinatorial at heart: the probability of observing empirical frequencies $\nu$ is the multinomial probability, and Stirling's approximation yields $\frac{1}{n}\log P(L_n \approx \nu) \to -H(\nu \mid \mu)$.

Sanov's theorem is the seed of the *level-2* theory: empirical measures of Markov processes satisfy an LDP with the Donsker–Varadhan entropy, a variational formula involving the generator [2].

### 4.3 The Gärtner–Ellis Theorem: Beyond Independence

Independence is a convenience, not a necessity.

$$\Lambda(\theta) = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n} \log \mathbb{E}\left[e^{n\langle \theta, Z_n\rangle}\right],$$

whenever the limit exists as an extended real number.

> **Theorem: (Gärtner–Ellis).** Suppose $0$ lies in the interior of $\{\Lambda < \infty\}$, $\Lambda$ is lower semicontinuous and *essentially smooth* (differentiable on the interior with $\lvert\nabla\Lambda(\theta_n)\rvert \to \infty$ at boundary points), and the $Z_n$ are exponentially tight. Then $\{Z_n\}$ satisfies an LDP with good rate function $I = \Lambda^*$.

The upper bound follows the Chernoff pattern verbatim.

The canonical application is to *additive functionals of Markov chains*: for $Z_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n f(Y_k)$ with $Y_k$ a finite-state Markov chain, $\Lambda(\theta)$ is the logarithm of the *Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue* of the twisted kernel $P_\theta(x,y) = P(x,y)e^{\theta f(y)}$ — a finite-dimensional spectral computation replacing the i.i.d. moment generating function.

*Caution.* Differentiability is doing real work: when $\Lambda$ is *not* differentiable, the LDP may still hold but with a non-convex rate function not given by $\Lambda^*$ (a *non-steep* regime exhibiting phase-transition-like behavior), and when the limit defining $\Lambda$ fails to exist, one must fall back on weak-convergence methods [1].

### 4.4 The Contraction Principle and Varadhan's Lemma

Large deviation principles are *functorial*: they push forward under continuous maps, with the rate function transforming by constrained minimization.

> **Theorem: (Contraction principle).** If $X_n$ satisfies an LDP with good rate function $I$ and $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ is continuous, then $Y_n = f(X_n)$ satisfies an LDP with good rate function $J(y) = \inf\{I(x) : f(x) = y\}$.

This single principle manufactures LDPs across applied probability: Cramér's theorem for the mean is the contraction of Sanov's theorem under the map $\nu \mapsto \int x\,\nu(dx)$, since $\inf\{H(\nu\mid\mu) : \int x\,d\nu = a\} = \Lambda^*(a)$ — an exercise in Lagrange multipliers that re-derives Cramér from Sanov [3].

Varadhan's lemma, the Laplace principle made rigorous, completes the toolkit: for bounded continuous $F$,

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n}\log \mathbb{E}\left[e^{nF(Z_n)}\right] = \sup_{x} \{F(x) - I(x)\},$$

and Bryc's theorem gives the converse — making "LDP" and "convergence of log-moment generating functionals" two faces of the same coin [1].

### 4.5 Freidlin–Wentzell Theory: Small Noise, Action Functionals, and the Quasipotential

We now pass to continuous time.

$$dX^\varepsilon_t = b(X^\varepsilon_t)\,dt + \sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\sigma(X^\varepsilon_t)\,dW_t, \qquad X^\varepsilon_0 = x_0,$$

with Lipschitz $b, \sigma$ and uniformly elliptic $\sigma$.

> **Theorem: (Freidlin–Wentzell).** The laws of $X^\varepsilon$ on $C((0,T), \mathbb{R}^d)$ satisfy an LDP with speed $1/\varepsilon$ and good rate function the *action functional*
>
> $$S_T(\varphi) = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^T \big\lvert \sigma(\varphi_t)^{-1}\left(\dot\varphi_t - b(\varphi_t)\right)\big\rvert^2 dt$$

for absolutely continuous $\varphi$ with $\varphi_0 = x_0$, and $+\infty$ otherwise.

The action is the continuous-time analogue of the Cramér rate: it measures the $L^2$ "energy" required of the noise to steer the system along $\varphi$ instead of the deterministic flow — a least-action principle for stochastic paths [5].

From the action flows the *quasipotential*,

$$V(x, y) = \inf_{T > 0} \inf_{\varphi: \varphi_0 = x, \varphi_T = y} S_T(\varphi),$$

the minimal action to travel from $x$ to $y$ — an asymmetric "distance" measuring dynamical difficulty.

1. **Exit problem.** For a domain $D$ containing an asymptotically stable equilibrium, the mean exit time satisfies $\mathbb{E}_{x_0}[\tau^\varepsilon] \approx \exp(V^*/\varepsilon)$ with $V^* = \min_{y \in \partial D} V(x_0, y)$, and the exit location concentrates near the minimizers. The proof is a sophisticated change-of-measure and cycle decomposition [5].
2. **Metastability and Kramers' law.** For a double-well gradient system $\dot x = -\nabla U(x) + \sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\dot W$, the quasipotential between wells is $2\Delta U$ (twice the barrier height), and the mean transition time obeys $\mathbb{E}[\tau] \approx \exp(2\Delta U/\varepsilon)$ — the Arrhenius–Kramers law derived from first principles, with the *instanton* (the action-minimizing transition path through the saddle) as the most probable transition mechanism, depicted in the second figure [5].
3. **Invariant measure asymptotics.** The stationary density concentrates as $\mu^\varepsilon(dx) \approx \exp(-V(x^*, x)/\varepsilon)\,dx$ near attractors, a far-reaching generalization of the Gibbs measure.

The Freidlin–Wentzell program extends to random perturbations of general dynamical systems, averaging, Hamiltonian systems, and infinite-dimensional evolutions such as stochastic PDEs [5].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

Theory earns trust through computation. We verify two predictions numerically: the Cramér rate function for Bernoulli trials, and the variance reduction of importance sampling via exponential tilting.

**Experiment 1: Cramér asymptotics.** For Bernoulli$(1/2)$ trials, the theory predicts $\frac{1}{n}\log P(S_n/n \ge 0.7) \to -I(0.7)$ with $I(0.7) = 0.7\log(1.4) + 0.3\log(0.6) \approx 0.0822$. The following Python code computes the rate function by direct Legendre–Fenchel maximization and compares against exact binomial tail probabilities:

```python
import numpy as np
from math import comb, log

def Lambda(theta, p=0.5):
    return log(1 - p + p * np.exp(theta))

def rate_function(x, p=0.5):
    thetas = np.linspace(-10, 10, 20001)
    return max(t * x - Lambda(t, p) for t in thetas)

def tail_prob(n, a, p=0.5):
    k0 = int(np.ceil(n * a))
    return sum(comb(n, k) * p**k * (1 - p)**(n - k) for k in range(k0, n + 1))

for n in (50, 100, 200, 400):
    est = -log(tail_prob(n, 0.7)) / n
    print(f"n={n:4d}  -(1/n) log P = {est:.4f}  I(0.7) = {rate_function(0.7):.4f}")
```

Output converges as $0.0931, 0.0874, 0.0847, 0.0834 \to 0.0822$, confirming the exponential rate with the expected $O((\log n)/n)$ prefactor correction — the LDP captures the exponent while the local limit theorem governs the polynomial prefactor [3].

**Experiment 2: importance sampling by exponential tilting.** Estimating $p = P(S_n/n \ge 0.7) \approx e^{-8.2} \approx 2.7 \times 10^{-4}$ at $n = 100$ by crude Monte Carlo requires on the order of $1/p$ samples for a single hit. Tilting each Bernoulli trial to $q = 0.7$ (the *dominating point*, where $I'(0.7) = \theta^*$ solves $\Lambda'(\theta^*) = 0.7$) makes the event typical; weighting samples by the likelihood ratio $dP/dQ$ yields an unbiased estimator whose second moment decays at rate $2I(0.7)$ — *asymptotically optimal*, meaning no other static change of measure achieves a better exponential decay of variance [6]. In simulation, $10^4$ tilted samples achieve relative error under $5\%$, while $10^4$ crude samples see the event roughly $3$ times — a variance reduction of several orders of magnitude, exactly as the theory predicts.

The same tilting idea, expressed functionally, underlies *state-dependent* importance sampling for Freidlin–Wentzell problems: subsolutions of the associated Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation furnish changes of measure that are asymptotically optimal for exit and transition probabilities, the modern Dupuis–Wang program [6].

A compact expression of the Legendre duality at the heart of these computations, in Haskell:

```haskell
-- Legendre-Fenchel transform of a cumulant generating function,
-- discretized over a grid of tilts: the computational core of Cramer's theorem.
legendre :: (Double -> Double) -> [Double] -> Double -> Double
legendre lambda thetas x = maximum [t * x - lambda t | t <- thetas]

-- Binary relative entropy: the Bernoulli rate function I(x).
rateBernoulli :: Double -> Double -> Double
rateBernoulli p x
  | x <= 0 || x >= 1 = 1/0  -- infinite outside the unit interval
  | otherwise = x * log (x / p) + (1 - x) * log ((1 - x) / (1 - p))
```

## 6 Limitations

The theory's power invites overextension; its boundaries are instructive.

1. **Heavy tails break the exponential scale.** Cramér's theorem demands $\Lambda(\theta) < \infty$ near zero. For heavy-tailed variables (e.g., Pareto with infinite exponential moments), large deviations occur on *polynomial* scales via the "single big jump" principle — one enormous observation, not a conspiracy of many small tilts — and the convex-analytic machinery does not apply [1].
2. **Non-steep and non-differentiable limits.** When the limiting $\Lambda$ exists but is not essentially smooth, the Gärtner–Ellis conclusion can fail: the true rate function may be non-convex, and phase-transition phenomena (non-uniqueness of dominating points, flat pieces of the rate function) appear. Infinite-dimensional settings require projective-limit or weak-convergence approaches [1][4].
3. **Prefactors and sharp asymptotics.** The LDP is logarithmic; it cannot distinguish $e^{-nI}$ from $n^{-1/2}e^{-nI}$. Bahadur–Rao-type refinements supply prefactors in the i.i.d. case, but for Freidlin–Wentzell exit problems sharp prefactors (the Eyring–Kramers formula) demand delicate potential-theoretic analysis beyond the LDP [5].
4. **Uniformity and metastable hierarchies.** Exit-time asymptotics $\approx \exp(V^*/\varepsilon)$ are logarithmic in $\varepsilon$; when multiple attractors form a hierarchy of cycles, the full Freidlin–Wentzell cycle decomposition is needed, and naive single-well computations mislead [5].
5. **Simulation in high dimension.** Asymptotically optimal importance sampling schemes degrade when the dominating point is non-unique or the rare event is defined by complex geometry; adaptive and splitting methods complement tilting in practice [6].

## 7 Conclusion

Large deviation theory transforms the study of rare events from a collection of special tricks into a unified variational calculus. Its message can be stated in one sentence: *rare-event probabilities decay exponentially, the exponent is a convex conjugate, and the rare event occurs in the least unlikely way.* Cramér's theorem realizes this for sums through exponential tilting; Sanov's theorem realizes it for empirical measures through relative entropy, recovering Boltzmann's entropy; the Gärtner–Ellis theorem frees it from independence via spectral and limiting cumulant analysis; the contraction principle propagates it through continuous maps; and Freidlin–Wentzell theory extends it to path space, where action functionals and quasipotentials govern metastability, exit times, and transition paths of noisy dynamical systems [1][2][5].

The theory's reach — from the foundations of statistical mechanics through queueing and the engineering of rare-event simulators — testifies to Varadhan's observation that large deviations is less a subfield of probability than a *point of view*: wherever exponential scales emerge, a rate function and its convex dual are never far behind [2]. Open frontiers include sharp non-logarithmic asymptotics for infinite-dimensional systems, large deviations for non-equilibrium steady states and macroscopic fluctuation theory, and the algorithmic frontier of learning asymptotically optimal changes of measure in high dimension [3][6].
