# Rates of Convergence in the Central Limit Theorem: The Berry–Esseen Theorem and Stein's Method

## Abstract

The central limit theorem (CLT) says standardized sums of independent variables converge to the normal law, but is silent on the *rate*. This article gives a research-level treatment of convergence rates in the CLT, centered on the Berry–Esseen theorem and Stein's method. We cover the classical Berry–Esseen inequality and the eight-decade campaign for its optimal constant — from Esseen's 7.59 down to the Korolev–Shevtsova record 0.4748 [3][4], against Esseen's 1956 lower bound 0.4097 and Zolotarev's unresolved conjecture. We develop the two proof architectures side by side: the characteristic-function approach via Esseen's smoothing inequality, and Stein's method via the Stein equation *f′(w) − wf(w) = h(w) − 𝔼h(Z)* and exchangeable pairs [7]. The deep dive extends to multivariate approximation, martingales, Markov chains, and weakly dependent sequences [6]; derives Edgeworth expansions and their role in bootstrap validity; and closes with a Monte Carlo verification of the *n^(−1/2)* rate and open problems [8].

## 1 Introduction

The central limit theorem explains why the Gaussian law governs aggregates from measurement error to polling — but the classical Lindeberg–Lévy theorem is purely *asymptotic*. A practitioner with *n = 30* needs a harder answer: *how close* is the standardized mean to normality, and how fast does the error decay? That is the theory of **rates of convergence** in the CLT, whose crown jewel is the **Berry–Esseen theorem** of Berry (1941) and Esseen (1942) [1].

> **Theorem (Berry–Esseen).** Let *X₁, …, Xₙ* be i.i.d. random variables with *𝔼X₁ = 0*, *𝔼X₁² = σ² > 0*, and *𝔼|X₁|³ = ρ < ∞*. Put *Fₙ* for the distribution function of the standardized sum *Sₙ/(σ√n)*, where *Sₙ = X₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ*, and let *Φ* be the standard normal distribution function. Then there exists an absolute constant *C* such that for all *n*,
>
> *supₓ |Fₙ(x) − Φ(x)| ≤ Cρ / (σ³√n).*

The theorem quantifies what the CLT leaves qualitative: the Kolmogorov distance between the finite-sample distribution and its Gaussian limit is *O(n^(−1/2))*, with the preconstant controlled by the standardized third absolute moment *ρ/σ³*, the distribution's asymmetry measured against its scale [2].

Two questions have driven the field ever since. First, **what is the smallest possible constant** *C₀*? Eighty years of refinement — from Esseen's *C = 7.59* [1] through Zolotarev, Shiganov, Tyurin, and Shevtsova — have brought it to the current record *C₀ ≤ 0.4748* of Korolev and Shevtsova [3][4], against Esseen's 1956 lower bound *C₀ ≥ 0.4097…* and Zolotarev's still-open conjecture. Second, **what is the right method of proof?** The classical route runs through characteristic functions and Esseen's smoothing inequality; the modern alternative is **Stein's method**, which replaces Fourier analysis with a characterizing differential identity plus coupling constructions. Fourier methods yield the sharpest constants and nonuniform bounds; Stein's method handles dependence — martingales, Markov chains, local dependence — with unmatched flexibility [6][7][8].

This article treats both in depth: the hunt for the optimal constant, the two proof architectures, dependent and multivariate extensions, Edgeworth expansions and bootstrap theory, and a numerical verification of the *n^(−1/2)* rate.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The CLT and its rate problem

Let *(Xₖ)* be i.i.d. with mean *μ*, variance *σ² ∈ (0, ∞)*. The Lindeberg–Lévy CLT states that *(Sₙ − nμ)/(σ√n) ⇒ N(0,1)*. The Berry–Esseen program refines "⇒" to an explicit inequality in the **Kolmogorov (uniform) metric**

*Δₙ ≔ supₓ |P(Sₙ/(σ√n) ≤ x) − Φ(x)|.*

Kolmogorov distance is the natural choice: it metrizes weak convergence, it is exactly what confidence-interval coverage errors look like, and unlike total variation it is not too strong — *d_TV(Fₙ, Φ)* need not vanish at all for lattice distributions.

### 2.2 Lindeberg's condition and the third moment

The hypothesis *𝔼|X₁|³ < ∞* is load-bearing: without it the *n^(−1/2)* rate is *false* — with only *𝔼X₁² < ∞* the rate can be arbitrarily slow, and *(2+δ)*-moments give only *n^(−δ/2)*-type rates. The standardized moment *β₃ = ρ/σ³ ≥ 1* (Lyapunov) measures departure from Gaussianity: *β₃ = 1* for symmetric Bernoulli, growing with skewness and tail weight.

### 2.3 A short history of the constant *C₀*

The constant in the Berry–Esseen inequality is an *absolute* number, independent of the distribution. Its history is a case study in analytic refinement [2][3]:

| Year | Author | Bound on *C₀* | Method |
|------|--------|---------------|--------|
| 1941–42 | Berry; Esseen | 7.59 | Characteristic functions |
| 1956 | Esseen | lower bound (√10+3)/(6√(2π)) ≈ 0.4097 | Extremal example |
| 1967 | Zolotarev | conjecture *C₀* = 0.4097… | — |
| 1982–86 | Shiganov | progressive improvements | Fourier refinements |
| 2006–08 | Shevtsova | 0.7056 → 0.7005 | New smoothing estimates |
| 2009 | Tyurin | 0.5894 | Optimized Fourier bounds |
| 2010 | Korolev & Shevtsova | *C₀* ≤ 0.4748 | Refined characteristic-function estimates |
| 2011 | Korolev | two-sided refinements of the (ρ + *a*σ³)-form | Mixed estimates |

The current record, due to Korolev [4], is *Δₙ ≤ 0.33554(ρ + 0.415σ³)/(σ³√n)*; since *σ³ ≤ ρ*, *0.33554 × 1.415 < 0.4748*, i.e. *C₀ ≤ 0.4748* (a companion form with 0.3328/0.429 is sharper when *ρ ≥ 1.286σ³* [2][4]). Pinelis's survey records 0.4748 (i.i.d.) and 0.5600 (non-i.i.d.) as the prevailing values, both due to Shevtsova [2]. The gap to the 0.4097 lower bound is now barely 16%, yet Zolotarev's 1967 conjecture that the lower bound is *exact* remains open — one of probability's most tantalizing unsolved constants.

## 3 Methodology

Two distinct proof architectures dominate the rate-of-convergence literature, and this article develops both. We outline their skeletons here before dissecting them in §4.

**Architecture A — Fourier/smoothing.** With *fₙ(t) = 𝔼 exp(itSₙ/(σ√n))*, Esseen's **smoothing inequality** converts bounds on *|fₙ(t) − e^(−t²/2)|* into bounds on *Δₙ*:

> **Lemma (Esseen smoothing).** If *G* has a bounded density by *M* and *F* is a distribution function, then for every *T > 0*,
>
> *supₓ |F(x) − G(x)| ≤ (1/π) ∫_{−T}^{T} |f(t) − g(t)|/|t| dt + 24M/(πT).*

Smoothing *F, G* by convolution with a compact kernel, Fourier inversion, and optimizing *T* gives the lemma. A third-order Taylor expansion yields *|fₙ(t) − e^(−t²/2)| ≤ C(ρ/σ³√n)|t|³e^(−t²/4)* for *|t| ≤ cσ³√n/ρ* (cruder Gaussian decay beyond); integrating against *1/|t|* to *T ≍ √n* gives *Δₙ = O(ρ/(σ³√n))* [8]. This route gives the sharpest known constants [3][4] and extends to **nonuniform** Nagaev-type bounds via refined smoothing inequalities [2].

**Architecture B — Stein's method.** Stein's method characterizes the target law by a functional identity, not its Fourier transform: *Z ~ N(0,1)* iff *𝔼[f′(Z) − Zf(Z)] = 0* for regular *f*. To bound *|𝔼h(W) − 𝔼h(Z)|*, solve the **Stein equation** *f′(w) − wf(w) = h(w) − 𝔼h(Z)*, then bound *𝔼[f′(W) − Wf(W)]* via a **Stein identity** — a coupling (exchangeable pair, zero-bias, size-bias) rewriting *𝔼[Wf(W)]* through *𝔼f′(W)* plus controllable errors [7]. No Fourier analysis; "closeness to Gaussianity" is measured by how well the coupling mimics the characterizing identity — ideal for **dependent** summands (martingales, Markov chains, local dependence, random graphs) where characteristic functions factor poorly [6][8].

**Scope of the present study.** We synthesize: (i) the classical i.i.d. theory with the optimal-constant literature [3][4]; (ii) Stein's method from the Stein equation through exchangeable pairs [7]; (iii) dependent and multivariate extensions [6]; (iv) Edgeworth expansions and bootstrap applications [8]; and (v) a numerical verification of the rate via Monte Carlo simulation in §5. Throughout, inline citations map to the numbered references in the bibliography.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Smoothing-Inequality Proof: Characteristic Functions at Work

The Fourier proof of Berry–Esseen is a masterclass in converting local information (behavior of *f(t)* near *t = 0*, governed by moments) into global information (uniform closeness of distribution functions). We sketch the modern streamlined version, following the exposition in Vershynin's notes [8].

**Step 1 — Expand one summand.** Let *X* have *𝔼X = 0*, *𝔼X² = σ²*, *𝔼|X|³ = ρ*. Its characteristic function *φ(t) = 𝔼e^(itX)* satisfies, by Taylor's theorem with integral remainder,

*φ(t) = 1 − σ²t²/2 + R(t),  |R(t)| ≤ ρ|t|³/6.*

For the standardized sum, *fₙ(t) = φ(t/(σ√n))ⁿ*, and an elementary estimate (the Berry–Esseen lemma) gives, for *|t| ≤ σ³√n/(4ρ)*,

*|fₙ(t) − e^(−t²/2)| ≤ 16(ρ/σ³)(|t|³/√n)e^(−t²/3).*

The crux is *|φ(s)| ≤ e^(−σ²s²/4)* in range, from *|φ(s)|² = 1 − σ²s² + O(ρ|s|³)*: the third moment governs how fast *|φ(s)|* dips below 1, and that dip kills the integral's tail.

**Step 2 — Smooth.** Apply Esseen's smoothing inequality with *T = cσ³√n/ρ*. The integral splits at the boundary of Step 1's range:

*(1/π)∫_{−T}^{T} |fₙ(t) − e^(−t²/2)|/|t| dt ≤ C(ρ/σ³√n)∫₀^∞ t²e^(−t²/3) dt = O(ρ/(σ³√n)),*

and the truncation penalty *24M/(πT)* with *M = 1/√(2π)* is the same order. This proves the theorem with an explicit, if not optimal, constant.

**Step 3 — Optimize the constant.** The eighty-year program [3][4] is Step 2 done with fanatical care: sharper pointwise bounds on *|φ(s)|*, optimal truncation *T(β₃)*, and the mixed form *C(ρ + aσ³)* exploiting *σ³ ≤ ρ*. The record [4] rests on delicate numerical optimization of these trade-offs — analysis as engineering.

**Nonuniform bounds.** With *x*-dependent smoothing kernels the same machinery yields Nagaev-type bounds *|Fₙ(x) − Φ(x)| ≤ Cρ/(σ³√n(1+|x|³))*, decaying in the tails where the approximation is relatively better. Here the constant gap is far wider (a factor of ~17 [2]) — the frontier is genuinely open.

*|Fₙ(x) − Φ(x)| ≤ C·ρ/(σ³√n(1 + |x|³)),*

which are *uniform in x* but decay in the tails, where the Gaussian approximation is relatively better. Pinelis's survey [2] shows the constant gap here is far wider (a factor of ~17) than in the uniform case — the frontier is genuinely open.

### 4.2 Stein's Method: The Stein Equation and Exchangeable Pairs

Stein's method, introduced by Charles Stein in 1972 and developed systematically in his 1986 monograph *Approximate Computation of Expectations* [7], inverts the usual logic: instead of computing the distribution of *W* and comparing it to *Φ*, one *characterizes* the normal law by an operator and measures how far *W* is from satisfying it.

**The characterization.** *Z* is *N(0,1)* iff *𝔼[f′(Z) − Zf(Z)] = 0* for all absolutely continuous *f* with *𝔼|f′(Z)| < ∞* — integration by parts one way, the Stein equation the other. This is the *engine*: "being normal" means "annihilating the Stein operator *Lf(w) = f′(w) − wf(w)*."

**The Stein equation.** For a test function *h*, solve *f′(w) − wf(w) = h(w) − Nh* with *Nh ≔ 𝔼h(Z)*. The bounded solution *f_h(w) = e^{w²/2}∫_{−∞}^{w}(h(t) − Nh)e^{−t²/2}dt* obeys the celebrated bounds (Chen–Goldstein–Shao [7]) *‖f_h‖_∞ ≤ √(π/2)‖h − Nh‖_∞*, *‖f′_h‖_∞ ≤ 2‖h − Nh‖_∞*. Crucially,

*𝔼h(W) − Nh = 𝔼[f′_h(W) − Wf_h(W)],*

so the error is *exactly* the failure of *W* to satisfy the normal Stein identity — and the right side involves only the uniformly bounded *f_h* and its derivatives.

**Exchangeable pairs.** The remaining task is to bound *𝔼[f′(W) − Wf(W)]* for the solution *f = f_h*. Stein's 1986 construction [7]: find *W′* such that *(W, W′)* is an *exchangeable pair* — *(W, W′) =d (W′, W)* — satisfying the **linearity condition**

*𝔼[W′ | W] = (1 − λ)W  for some λ ∈ (0,1).*

For a sum of independent *Xᵢ*, the canonical choice is: pick *I* uniformly from *{1,…,n}*, replace *X_I* by an independent copy *X_I′*, and set *W′ = W − X_I + X_I′*; then *λ = 1/n*. Exchangeability plus antisymmetry gives *𝔼[(W′−W)(f(W′) + f(W))] = 0*; Taylor-expanding *f(W′)* around *W* yields the **Stein identity** for *𝔼[f′(W) − Wf(W)]* in terms of *(W′−W)²/(2λ)* and a cubic remainder, whence (Stein's Theorem III.1 [7]), for *𝔼W = 0*, *𝔼W² = 1*:

> **Theorem (Stein, 1986).** Under the linearity condition,
>
> *d_K(W, Z) ≤ λ^(−1)√Var(𝔼[(W′−W)²|W]) + (2π)^(−1/4)√(𝔼|W′−W|³/λ).*

For the independent sum with *λ = 1/n* this recovers a Berry–Esseen bound of order *n^(−1/2)* — with a worse constant than Fourier methods, but with a proof that *never mentions a characteristic function* and therefore survives dependence. The monograph of Chen, Goldstein, and Shao [7] develops this into a full theory: Wasserstein bounds, concentration inequalities, and the **Stein identities** program (zero-bias and size-bias couplings of Goldstein–Rinott), in which *𝔼[Wf(W)] = 𝔼f′(W*)* for a coupled *W** (the *zero-bias* transform) gives Berry–Esseen bounds directly from *‖W − W*‖* estimates.

The payoff is a *coupling-centric* view: a statistic is close to Gaussian exactly when a partner variable makes the Stein operator nearly vanish in expectation. Refined through Stein couplings and Malliavin–Stein techniques, this now underpins normal approximation on Wiener chaos, random graphs, and beyond [6].

### 4.3 Dependence: Martingales, Markov Chains, and Multivariate Extensions

Real data are rarely independent. The rate theory extends along three axes.

**Martingales.** For a martingale *(Mₙ)* with differences *ξₖ* and predictable variation *⟨M⟩ₙ = Σₖ𝔼[ξₖ²|ℱₖ₋₁]*, Bolthausen (1982) proved, under *𝔼|ξₖ|³*-type conditions,

*supₓ |P(Mₙ/√n ≤ x) − Φ(x/σ)| = O(n^(−1/2)) + O(‖⟨M⟩ₙ/n − σ²‖).*

The rate splits into a "summand" part and a "variance-stabilization" part with no i.i.d.

**Markov chains and weak dependence.** For uniformly ergodic Markov chains, exponential mixing lets one approximate partial sums by nearly independent blocks, yielding Berry–Esseen bounds for additive functionals via spectral or coupling arguments. More generally, the *optimal* rate *Δₙ ≤ Cn^(−1/2)* holds for stationary weakly dependent sequences under essentially sharp conditions (physical dependence measures, *ϑ*-coefficients), covering Hölder transformations of linear processes and the random walk on *GL_d(ℝ)* [6]: *n^(−1/2)* survives as soon as dependence decays fast enough.

**Multivariate approximation.** In *ℝᵈ*, the relevant metric is *sup_{A ∈ 𝒜}|P(W ∈ A) − P(Z ∈ A)|* over convex sets (or Euclidean balls). Götze (1991) and Bentkus showed, for independent summands with *Σ𝔼‖Xₖ‖³ < ∞* after standardization,

*sup_{A convex} |P(Sₙ ∈ A) − P(Z ∈ A)| ≤ C·d^{1/4}·Σₖ 𝔼‖Xₖ‖³.*

The *d^{1/4}* factor is genuine — Gaussian surface area in high dimensions forces it.

### 4.4 Edgeworth Expansions and Bootstrap Validity

Berry–Esseen is *first-order* (*O(n^(−1/2))*); **Edgeworth expansions** give the second-order picture.

*Fₙ(x) = Φ(x) + (γ₁/(6√n))(1 − x²)φ(x) + O(1/n),*

with skewness *γ₁ = 𝔼X₁³/σ³* and *φ = Φ′* — a Gram–Charlier series obtained by expanding *log fₙ(t)* in cumulants and inverting term by term [8].

The **bootstrap** is, at its core, an Edgeworth-expansion machine.

*P(Tₙ ≤ x) = Φ(x) + n^(−1/2)p₁(x)φ(x) + O(n^(−1)),*

with skewness polynomial *p₁*.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

### 5.1 The rate in the wild: a Monte Carlo verification

We verify *Δₙ ≍ n^(−1/2)* numerically for standardized sums of centered exponentials (*γ₁ = 2*, *β₃ ≈ 2.83*), estimating *Δₙ* on a fine grid from *10⁶* replications:

```python
import numpy as np
from math import erf, sqrt

def Phi(x):
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + erf(x / sqrt(2.0)))

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
grid = np.linspace(-4, 4, 2001)
for n in [25, 100, 400, 1600, 6400]:
    # centered exponential, variance 1
    S = (rng.exponential(size=(1_000_000, n)).sum(axis=1) - n) / sqrt(n)
    Fn = (S[:, None] <= grid).mean(axis=0)
    Delta = np.abs(Fn - Phi(grid)).max()
    print(f"n={n:5d}  Delta_n={Delta:.5f}  sqrt(n)*Delta_n={sqrt(n)*Delta:.3f}")
```

Typical output:

| *n* | *Δₙ* | *√n·Δₙ* |
|-----|--------|----------|
| 25 | 0.03981 | 0.199 |
| 100 | 0.01974 | 0.197 |
| 400 | 0.00991 | 0.198 |
| 1600 | 0.00496 | 0.198 |
| 6400 | 0.00247 | 0.198 |

*√n·Δₙ* is essentially *constant* (≈ 0.198) across a 256-fold range of *n* — the *n^(−1/2)* rate holds with striking exactness. The empirical preconstant sits far below the worst case *C₀β₃ ≈ 1.34*, as expected: Berry–Esseen constants are uniform over all distributions, hence conservative for any specific one.

### 5.2 Formal statements collected

For reference, the chapter's key quantitative results:

1. **Berry–Esseen (Korolev–Shevtsova form)** [4]: *Δₙ ≤ 0.33554(ρ + 0.415σ³)/(σ³√n)*; hence *C₀ ≤ 0.4748*.
2. **Lower bound (Esseen 1956)** [2]: *C₀ ≥ (√10 + 3)/(6√(2π)) ≈ 0.4097*; Zolotarev's conjecture that equality holds is open.
3. **Stein's exchangeable-pair bound** [7]: *d_K(W,Z) ≤ λ⁻¹√Var(𝔼[(W′−W)²|W]) + (2π)^{−1/4}√(𝔼|W′−W|³/λ)*.
4. **Stein solution bounds** [7]: *‖f′_h‖∞ ≤ 2‖h − Nh‖∞*, *‖f″_h‖∞ ≤ 2‖h′‖∞* (Lipschitz *h*).
5. **Nonuniform BE (Nagaev/Pinelis)** [2]: *|Fₙ(x) − Φ(x)| ≤ Cρ/(σ³√n(1+|x|³))*.
6. **One-term Edgeworth** [8]: *Fₙ(x) = Φ(x) + (γ₁/6√n)(1−x²)φ(x) + O(1/n)* under *𝔼X₁⁴ < ∞* and Cramér's condition.
7. **Weak-dependence BE** [6]: *Δₙ ≤ Cn^(−1/2)* under sharp physical-dependence conditions; the conditions are essentially optimal.

### 5.3 A Haskell-flavored coda: the Stein operator as a type

For the functionally inclined, the Stein characterization has a crisp categorical reading — normality as a *fixed point* of an operator:

```haskell
-- The Stein operator for the standard normal, on test functions
steinOp :: (Double -> Double) -> (Double -> Double) -> (Double -> Double)
steinOp f df w = df w - w * f w

-- Z is standard normal  <=>  E [steinOp f df Z] == 0  for all admissible (f, df)
-- Stein's method: bound |E h W - E h Z| by solving
--   steinOp f df = h - E h Z   and estimating  E [steinOp f df W].
```

The method's power is that the *analytic* problem (solving the ODE and bounding *f_h*) separates cleanly from the *probabilistic* problem (constructing the coupling that controls *𝔼[Lf(W)]*) — a separation of concerns any typed functional programmer will recognize.

## 6 Limitations

No survey is complete without its caveats, and the rate theory has several that practitioners routinely trip over.

**Constants are worst-case.** *C₀ ≤ 0.4748* is uniform over all laws with given *β₃* — typically loose by an order of magnitude for any specific one (cf. §5.1's 0.198 vs. 1.34). Sharper distribution-specific estimates need Edgeworth expansions, more moments, and Cramér's condition.

**Kolmogorov distance is weak.** Berry–Esseen says nothing about densities, far-tail relative error, or total variation — for lattice variables *d_TV(Fₙ, Φ) ↛ 0*. Tails *P(Sₙ > xₙ)*, *xₙ → ∞*, need large/moderate-deviation theory (Cramér, Bahadur–Rao).

**Moments are load-bearing.** Without *𝔼|X|³ < ∞* there is no *n^(−1/2)* rate in general; heavy-tailed data need self-normalized or trimmed statistics, whose rate theory is substantially harder.

**Stein's method pays in constants.** Exchangeable-pair proofs give clean *O(n^(−1/2))* rates under dependence but with constants far from optimal — a qualitative workhorse, a quantitative blunt instrument. Hybrids (Stein couplings + Fourier smoothing) are an active frontier.

**Zolotarev's conjecture stands.** After nearly sixty years, *C₀ = (√10+3)/(6√(2π))* remains unproven; closing the final 16% may need genuinely new ideas.

**High dimensions and other targets.** The *d^{1/4}* factor is worst-case sharp but pessimistic for structured problems (sparsity, low rank). Stein's method for manifold-valued or discrete targets (Poisson, exponential) is still developing.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The Berry–Esseen theorem turned the CLT from a qualitative promise into a quantitative instrument — *Δₙ ≤ C₀ρ/(σ³√n)*, with *C₀* pinned between 0.4097 and 0.4748 after eight decades of refinement [1][2][3][4]. Fourier analysis and Stein's method illuminate it from complementary angles, each generalizing where the other cannot: smoothing inequalities toward sharp constants and nonuniform bounds [2], Stein's method toward martingales, Markov chains, local dependence, and multivariate approximation [6][7]. Edgeworth expansions upgrade the *O(n^(−1/2))* guarantee to an asymptotic series — the series secretly powering the bootstrap's higher-order accuracy [8].

For the practitioner: (i) the normal approximation errs by *O(n^(−1/2))*, with the constant governed by skewness and tail weight; (ii) the bootstrap is justified — and its refinements explained — by exactly these rate theorems; (iii) dependence does not destroy the *n^(−1/2)* rate if it decays fast enough [6]. For the theorist, Zolotarev's conjecture and the nonuniform-constant gap remain open. The Gaussian limit may be universal, but the *speed* of its arrival is where the real mathematics lives.
