# Mechanistic Interpretability of Transformer Networks: Circuits, Superposition, and Feature Geometry

## Abstract

Mechanistic interpretability reverse-engineers transformer networks into human-understandable algorithms by decomposing the residual stream into circuits of attention heads and MLP neurons. This thesis develops the mathematical framework: the residual-stream decomposition, the QK/OV circuit parameterization of attention heads, induction heads as the mechanism of in-context learning, and the superposition hypothesis explaining polysemanticity through compressed-sensing geometry. We present empirical results on induction-head formation during training, sparse autoencoders for feature disentanglement, and probing of world models in large models. Limitations include the scaling gap between toy models and frontier systems, the lack of a complete theory of MLP computation, and the faithfulness problem in automated interpretation. We conclude with a research program toward guarantees on model behavior.

## 1 Introduction

Modern language models are among the most capable artifacts ever built—and among the least understood. A transformer with billions of parameters implements some algorithm that maps token sequences to next-token distributions, but the algorithm is written in the alien medium of weight matrices, not in code a human can read. **Mechanistic interpretability** is the research program that attempts to reverse-engineer these networks: to identify the features a model computes, the circuits that compute them, and the algorithms those circuits implement, with the rigor of a systems neuroscience of artificial minds [1][2].

The stakes are practical as well as scientific. If we cannot say *why* a model produced an output—whether it used a memorized fact, a spurious correlation, or a genuine reasoning circuit—we cannot certify safety properties, predict out-of-distribution behavior, or detect deception. Interpretability is therefore not a luxury: it is the measurement science on which AI assurance must rest.

> Theorem: (Informal, Elhage et al.) Every attention head in a transformer can be understood as the sum of a QK circuit, which determines *where* information moves, and an OV circuit, which determines *what* information moves and how the destination token is updated [1].

This thesis is organized as follows. §2 introduces the transformer architecture in the residual-stream picture. §3 develops the mathematical framework of circuits and the superposition hypothesis. §4 dives into induction heads, sparse autoencoders, and the geometry of features. §5 presents empirical results from the literature. §6 discusses limitations, and §7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The residual-stream picture

A decoder-only transformer processes a sequence of tokens $x_1, \dots, x_T$ by embedding each token into $\mathbb{R}^{d_{model}}$ and then iteratively updating a **residual stream** $r_t^{(l)}$ for each position $t$ and layer $l$:

$$
r_t^{(l+1)} = r_t^{(l)} + \sum_h \mathrm{AttnHead}_h^{(l)}(r_{\le t}^{(l)}) + \mathrm{MLP}^{(l)}(r_t^{(l)}).
$$

Every component *reads* from the residual stream and *writes* back into it additively. The final logits are obtained by projecting the last residual stream through the unembedding matrix $W_U$. This additive structure is what makes decomposition possible: the network's computation is a sum of many small, local updates to a shared communication channel [1].

A single attention head $h$ at layer $l$ computes, for destination position $t$:

$$
\mathrm{head}(r_t) = \sum_{s \le t} A_{t,s} \cdot (W_O W_V r_s), \qquad A_{t,s} \propto \exp\left(r_t^\top W_Q^\top W_K r_s\right).
$$

Two low-rank factorizations emerge naturally:

- The **QK circuit** $W_Q^\top W_K \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ (rank $\le d_{head}$) controls the attention pattern $A_{t,s}$: which source tokens the head attends to.
- The **OV circuit** $W_O W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ (rank $\le d_{head}$) controls what is written: each source token's residual stream is mapped through $W_V$, then $W_O$, and added to the destination.

Because attention is the *only* mechanism by which information moves between token positions, understanding a transformer's information flow reduces to understanding its heads' QK and OV circuits; MLPs, which act per-position, implement local computation and memory lookup [2][3].

### 2.2 Features, neurons, and polysemanticity

The classical hope—one neuron, one concept—fails dramatically in practice: individual neurons are **polysemantic**, firing for unrelated inputs (a neuron that responds to both baseball statistics and Python code, say). The **superposition hypothesis** explains this: models represent more features than they have dimensions by embedding features as *almost-orthogonal* directions in activation space, so that $m \gg d$ features can coexist in $\mathbb{R}^d$ with small interference [4].

Formally, if features $f_1, \dots, f_m$ are sparse (rarely co-active) and embedded as unit vectors $v_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with $|\langle v_i, v_j \rangle| \le \epsilon$ for $i \ne j$, then a Johnson–Lindenstrauss-type argument shows $m$ can grow exponentially in $d$ while keeping interference bounded. The network pays for this compression with polysemanticity: any fixed neuron (a coordinate direction) necessarily responds to many features at once. Interpretability must therefore work in the *feature basis*, not the neuron basis—a shift with deep consequences for methodology.

| Concept | Mathematical object | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Residual stream | $r_t^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{model}}$ | Shared communication channel |
| QK circuit | $W_Q^\top W_K$ | Attention pattern (where info moves) |
| OV circuit | $W_O W_V$ | Value transport (what info moves) |
| Feature | Direction $v \in \mathbb{R}^d$ | Human-meaningful variable |
| Superposition | $m \gg d$ almost-orthogonal $v_i$ | Cause of polysemanticity |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Circuit discovery by ablation

The workhorse method is **activation patching** (causal mediation analysis): run the model on a clean input and a corrupted input, then surgically replace activations of a candidate component with the corrupted values and measure the effect on the output. Components whose patching restores corrupted behavior are causally implicated in the computation [5].

```python
# Schematic: activation patching to localize a circuit
def patch_component(model, clean, corrupted, component):
    clean_acts = model.forward(clean, cache=True)
    def hook(acts):
        return corrupted_acts[component]  # intervene
    out = model.forward(clean, hooks={component: hook})
    return kl_divergence(out.logits, model(clean).logits)
# Small KL => component is causally downstream-irrelevant
```

Systematic patching across heads and positions yields a **circuit**: the minimal subgraph of components sufficient to reproduce the behavior. Path patching refines this to individual residual-stream paths (e.g., distinguishing a head's direct effect from its effect mediated through later heads) [5].

### 3.2 Sparse autoencoders

To move from the neuron basis to the feature basis, **sparse autoencoders (SAEs)** learn an overcomplete dictionary: given activations $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$, train

$$
\hat x = \sum_{i=1}^{m} f_i(x) d_i, \qquad f(x) = \mathrm{ReLU}(W_{enc} x + b),
$$

with $m \gg d$ dictionary vectors $d_i$ and a sparsity penalty (e.g. $L_1$ on $f(x)$ or top-$k$ gating) [6]. The learned latents $f_i$ are strikingly more monosemantic than neurons: SAE features in frontier models correspond to identifiable concepts (DNA sequences, HTTP status codes, specific people), and ablating them produces targeted behavioral changes. SAEs operationalize the superposition hypothesis into a scalable microscope.

### 3.3 Probing and the linear representation hypothesis

A complementary methodology trains **linear probes**: if a concept (say, the sentiment of a movie review, or the board state in Othello) can be decoded by a linear classifier from activations, the model represents it as a direction—the **linear representation hypothesis** [7]. Interventions along probe directions (activation steering) then test causality: adding the "honesty direction" to the residual stream measurably changes the model's truthfulness, suggesting these directions are not merely correlated but functionally operative [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Induction heads: the mechanism of in-context learning

The flagship success of circuit analysis is the **induction head**. Consider the pattern "... A B ... A": an induction head at the second "A" attends back to the "B" that followed the first "A", and copies it forward—completing the pattern. Mechanistically, this requires *two* heads in composition [9]:

1. A **previous-token head** in an early layer copies information from token $s-1$ into token $s$'s residual stream (its OV circuit shifts content one position forward).
2. The **induction head** in a later layer uses its QK circuit to match the current token against tokens whose (shifted) content equals it—i.e., it searches for "tokens preceded by something matching me"—and its OV circuit copies the *next* token's content forward.

In QK/OV terms, if $W_{QK}^{prev}$ implements "attend to previous token" and the induction head's $W_{QK}^{ind}$ matches on the previous-token-shifted subspace, the composition $W_{QK}^{ind} W_{OV}^{prev}$ yields induction behavior. Olsson et al. showed that induction heads form abruptly during training at precisely the phase transition where in-context learning emerges, across model scales—a striking case of a macroscopic capability being explained by a microscopic circuit [9].

### 4.2 The geometry of superposition

Why does superposition work, quantitatively? Elhage et al. studied toy models—small ReLU networks trained to represent $m$ sparse features in $d$ dimensions—and found phase transitions: as feature sparsity and importance vary, models snap between dedicating dimensions to features, superposing them in interpretable geometric arrangements (antipodal pairs, simplices, Platonic configurations), or discarding them [4]. The favored geometries minimize interference energy $\sum_{i<j} \langle v_i, v_j \rangle^2$ subject to sparsity, a problem with the flavor of spherical coding.

The practical upshot: SAE dictionary vectors in real models are not arbitrary—they inherit geometric structure from this optimization. Understanding that structure may let us predict *which* features a model of given size can represent, a kind of statistical mechanics of representations.

### 4.3 World models and linear structure

Probing has revealed that models build **world models**: linear representations of board states in Othello-GPT (a transformer trained only on move sequences nevertheless represents the full 8×8 board linearly) [10], of spatial and temporal coordinates in large language models, and of truth values as a consistent "true/false" direction across datasets. The most ambitious claim—that models maintain coherent, manipulable models of the world rather than surface statistics—is now supported by intervention experiments: editing the Othello board representation changes the model's subsequent moves as if the board had actually changed [10].

### 4.4 Grokking, phase transitions, and training dynamics

Interpretability also illuminates *learning*. In **grokking**, small transformers trained on modular arithmetic memorize the training set, then abruptly generalize thousands of steps later; circuit analysis shows the network transitions from a memorization circuit to a generalizing Fourier-multiplication circuit, with weight decay slowly killing the former [11]. More broadly, the formation of induction heads, the emergence of syntactic attention patterns, and the development of refusal circuits in safety-tuned models all appear as discrete developmental stages—suggesting that training dynamics, too, may be decomposable into the birth, competition, and death of circuits.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We summarize landmark empirical findings; "proofs" in this young field are typically careful causal experiments rather than theorems.

**Result 1 (Induction heads cause in-context learning).** Ablating induction heads in small transformers destroys few-shot learning performance while leaving other capabilities intact; the loss curves of in-context score and induction-head strength track each other quantitatively across training [9].

**Result 2 (SAE features are causal).** In Claude 3 Sonnet, SAE latents corresponding to concepts like "sycophancy" or "the Golden Gate Bridge" were identified among tens of millions of features; clamping the Golden Gate Bridge feature high caused the model to obsess over the bridge unprompted—demonstrating that dictionary features are not epiphenomenal but causally efficacious [6].

**Result 3 (Function vectors).** Simple in-context tasks (antonym, translation, capitalization) are mediated by compact **function vectors**—directions in activation space that, when transplanted into a zero-shot prompt, induce the task. These vectors are the residual-stream sum of a small set of heads' OV outputs, giving a complete circuit-level account of a behavioral capability [12].

**Result 4 (Refusal circuits).** Safety fine-tuning installs identifiable refusal circuitry: a small set of directions/mediators that trigger refusal on harmful requests. Adversarial suffixes (as in gradient-based jailbreaks) work precisely by suppressing these circuits' activations—an interpretability result that both explains attacks and suggests targeted defenses [13].

## 6 Limitations

1. **The scaling gap.** Nearly all complete circuit analyses (induction heads, grokking, IOI) concern models with $\le 10^8$ parameters on toy tasks. Whether the same methods yield *complete* explanations of frontier-model behaviors—multi-step reasoning, tool use, long-horizon planning—remains unproven.
2. **MLPs are poorly understood.** Attention heads have the clean QK/OV theory; MLPs (two-thirds of parameters) are usually treated as key–value memories, but a compositional theory of MLP *circuits*—how neurons combine into algorithms—is in its infancy.
3. **The faithfulness problem.** Automated interpretability (using models to label features or write circuit explanations) risks circularity: explanations that sound plausible but misdescribe the computation. Ground-truth benchmarks for explanation faithfulness barely exist.
4. **Feature completeness.** SAEs recover *a* dictionary, not provably *the* dictionary; reconstruction error and "dark matter" (unexplained variance) mean we cannot certify that all causally relevant features have been found.
5. **Polysemanticity of the microscope.** SAE latents themselves can be polysemantic or split/merge concepts across dictionary sizes; the ontology of features is not yet stable.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Mechanistic interpretability has moved, in a few years, from a fringe aspiration to a discipline with real explanatory victories: induction heads explaining in-context learning, superposition explaining polysemanticity, SAEs rendering millions of features legible, and refusal circuits explaining both alignment and its circumvention. The residual-stream decomposition and the QK/OV parameterization give the field something rare in deep learning—a shared mathematical language in which hypotheses can be stated precisely and tested causally.

The road ahead is clear in outline if not in detail: scale circuit analysis to frontier models and frontier behaviors, develop the missing theory of MLP computation, solve the faithfulness problem for automated interpretation, and convert mechanistic understanding into *guarantees*—proofs, not hopes, about what models will and will not do. If that program succeeds, interpretability will have done for AI what molecular biology did for medicine: replaced vitalism with mechanism, and superstition with engineering.
