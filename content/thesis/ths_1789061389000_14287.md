# Reverse-Mode Automatic Differentiation: Wengert Lists, Tape-Based Checkpointing, Higher-Order Adjoints, and the JAX–Enzyme Design Spectrum

## Abstract

Reverse-mode automatic differentiation (AD) is the algorithmic core of backpropagation: it computes the gradient of a scalar-valued function of $n$ inputs in time proportional to a small constant multiple of a single function evaluation, the *cheap gradient principle* of Baur and Strassen. This thesis reconstructs the mathematical and engineering lineage of reverse mode, from R. E. Wengert's 1964 decomposition of differentiable programs into sequential assignment lists — the Wengert list, progenitor of the modern computational graph — through the tape data structure that records intermediate values during a forward pass for adjoint propagation during a reverse sweep. We formalize the space–time trade-offs of naive tapes, develop binomial checkpointing (Revolve) achieving logarithmic temporal overhead with bounded memory, and address the perturbation confusion problem that haunts nested, higher-order differentiation, surveying the forward-over-reverse and mixed-mode strategies that make Hessians tractable. Finally, we contrast two contemporary implementations occupying opposite ends of the design spectrum: **JAX**, which differentiates through high-level functional tracing into XLA-compiled jacobians-transpose primitives, and **Enzyme**, which synthesizes adjoints from optimized LLVM IR to differentiate arbitrary compiled languages. We prove correctness of the adjoint recurrence, derive the optimal binomial checkpoint schedule, and present empirical micro-benchmarks consistent with published speedups, concluding with open problems in checkpoint-aware compilation and sparsity-exploiting reverse accumulation.

## 1. Introduction

Every neural network training run is a triumph of a single algorithm: reverse-mode automatic differentiation. Given $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ with $n$ in the billions, reverse mode computes $\nabla f$ at cost $\omega_b \cdot \mathrm{cost}(f)$ for a constant $\omega_b$ typically between 5 and 10 — compared with $n \cdot \mathrm{cost}(f)$ for forward mode [1][7]. This is not merely an optimization; it is what makes deep learning computationally possible.

Yet reverse mode is frequently reduced to a slogan ("backpropagation"), obscuring a sixty-year intellectual arc. The method's first explicit formulation is due to Linnainmaa (1976) in his master's thesis on Taylor expansions of rounding errors, though precursors existed [1]. The deeper structural idea, however, belongs to R. E. Wengert (1964): *any* differentiable computation can be decomposed into a **list** of elementary assignments, each with known partial derivatives, after which the chain rule becomes a mechanical procedure [1][4]. This "Wengert list" is the ancestor of the computational graph, the tape, and the SSA trace.

This thesis pursues four questions:

1. **Representation.** How do Wengert lists, computational graphs, and tapes relate, and what do their differing formalizations buy us? (Section 3–4)
2. **Memory.** Reverse mode is space-hungry: the tape records every intermediate value. How do checkpointing schedules compress this requirement to logarithmic overhead? (Section 4.2)
3. **Higher order.** What breaks when reverse mode is nested inside itself, and how do forward-over-reverse compositions restore correctness? (Section 4.3)
4. **Implementation.** How do JAX and Enzyme — tracing-based versus compiler-IR-based — instantiate this theory, and what are the resulting trade-offs? (Section 4.4)

Our contribution is a unified treatment: we prove the adjoint recurrence correct (Theorem 5.1), derive the binomial checkpointing schedule's optimality (Theorem 5.2), and ground both modern systems in this classical theory.

> **Theorem (Cheap gradient principle, Baur–Strassen 1983):** For a scalar function $f$ evaluated by a straight-line program of length $l$ over $\{+, \times\}$ (and smooth unary operations), the full gradient $\nabla f$ can be computed in time $\mathcal{O}(l)$, i.e., in a constant factor of the cost of evaluating $f$ alone.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Wengert list and the computational graph

Wengert's 1964 insight [1] is disarmingly simple. Let $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ be computed by a program. Introduce one variable $v_i$ per elementary operation, recording inputs first and the final output last:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_{1-n} &= x_1, \ldots, v_0 = x_n && \text{(independent variables)} \
v_i &= \varphi_i(v_j : j \prec i), && i = 1, \ldots, l \quad \text{(elementary steps)} \
y &= v_l && \text{(dependent variable)}
\end{aligned}
$$

where each $\varphi_i$ is an elementary operation (e.g., $+$, $\times$, $\sin$, $\exp$) applied to previously computed values ($j \prec i$ denotes data dependence). This *linearized* representation is the **Wengert list**. Viewed geometrically, the partial order $\prec$ induces a directed acyclic graph: the **computational graph**, whose vertices are the $v_i$ and whose edges $(j, i)$ exist iff $j \prec i$.

The graph and the list are the same object up to topological order, but the two perspectives motivate different implementations: the list motivates the *tape* (a sequential log), while the graph motivates the *expression DAG* (a structural artifact). Innes (2018) observes that these are special cases of static single assignment (SSA) form, where Wengert's numbering $v_i$ is precisely SSA's single-definition discipline [4].

### 2.2 Forward and reverse accumulation

For directional derivatives $\dot{v}_i = \frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x} \cdot \dot{x}$ (forward mode), the chain rule flows in the same direction as evaluation:

$$
\dot{v}_i = \sum_{j \prec i} \frac{\partial \varphi_i}{\partial v_j}\, \dot{v}_j.
$$

For adjoints $\bar{v}_i = \frac{\partial y}{\partial v_i}$ (reverse mode), the chain rule flows backward:

$$
\bar{v}_j = \sum_{i: j \prec i} \bar{v}_i\, \frac{\partial \varphi_i}{\partial v_j}, \qquad \bar{v}_l = 1.
$$

Baydin et al. (2018) stress the practical asymmetry: forward mode needs one pass per input direction ($n$ passes for the full gradient), while reverse mode needs one pass total for the full gradient — at the price of recording the forward trace [1].

| Property | Forward mode | Reverse mode |
|---|---|---|
| Quantities propagated | Tangents $\dot{v}_i$ | Adjoints $\bar{v}_i$ |
| Direction of propagation | Same as evaluation | Opposite to evaluation |
| Cost for $\nabla f$, $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ | $n \cdot \mathrm{cost}(f)$ | $\omega_b \cdot \mathrm{cost}(f)$, $\omega_b \approx 5$–$10$ |
| Memory overhead | $\mathcal{O}(1)$ per sweep | $\mathcal{O}(l)$ tape (naive) |
| Ideal regime | $n \ll m$ (few inputs) | $n \gg m$ (few outputs) |

### 2.3 The tape

Reverse mode needs every intermediate value $v_i$ *at the time the adjoint sweep reaches it*, because the local partials $\partial \varphi_i / \partial v_j$ are evaluated at the recorded point. The **tape** (also called the *Wengert list evaluated*, or *stack*) is the sequential log of the forward pass: operation codes, argument indices, and intermediate values. The adjoint sweep pops this tape in reverse.

Two tape organizations dominate practice:

- **Operation tapes** (PyTorch-style): each recorded op stores pointers to its inputs, its output, and a closure computing the vector-Jacobian product (VJP). Memory is pointer-heavy but flexible.
- **Value tapes / flat tapes** (ADOL-C, CoDiPack-style): contiguous arrays of doubles plus op codes; compact, cache-friendly, and amenable to checkpointing schedules.

The tape is the central cost center of reverse mode, and its compression is the subject of Section 4.2.

## 3. Methodology

Our method is threefold: (i) *formal reconstruction* — we restate Wengert's decomposition and derive the adjoint recurrence from first principles, proving correctness; (ii) *complexity analysis* — we derive memory–recompute trade-off curves for checkpointing schedules and prove the binomial schedule optimal; (iii) *comparative systems analysis* — we map JAX's tracing transformation and Enzyme's LLVM-IR synthesis onto the tape/graph formalism, using published benchmarks to anchor performance claims.

Notation: $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ with $l$ elementary steps; $\mathrm{cost}(f)$ denotes time to evaluate $f$ once; $\bar{v}_i$ denotes the adjoint of $v_i$; the *seed* $\bar{y}$ initializes the reverse sweep.

### 3.1 The adjoint recurrence, derived

Consider $y = v_l$ as a function of all intermediates. By the multivariate chain rule applied to the elementary decomposition:

$$
\frac{\partial y}{\partial v_j} = \sum_{i: j \prec i} \frac{\partial y}{\partial v_i} \frac{\partial \varphi_i}{\partial v_j}.
$$

Defining $\bar{v}_i := \partial y / \partial v_i$, this is exactly the reverse recurrence. Processing $i$ from $l$ down to $1$, each step performs a *fan-out accumulation*: the adjoint of $v_i$ is scattered to its arguments, scaled by local partials. The total work is bounded by the work of the forward pass times a small constant, because each elementary op has bounded fan-in (typically $\leq 2$) — this is the constructive core of the cheap gradient principle [1][5].

### 3.2 A minimal tape in code

To make the mechanism concrete, here is a pedagogical reverse-mode engine — essentially the Wengert list made executable:

```python
import math
from collections import defaultdict

class Var:
    def __init__(self, value, _tape=None):
        self.value = value
        self.grad = 0.0
        self._tape = _tape if _tape is not None else []

    def _record(self, op, args, local_partials):
        out = Var(op(*[a.value for a in args]), self._tape)
        # tape entry: (output, inputs, local partial derivatives)
        self._tape.append((out, args, local_partials))
        return out

    def __mul__(self, other):
        other = other if isinstance(other, Var) else Var(other, self._tape)
        return self._record(lambda a, b: a * b, (self, other),
                            (other.value, self.value))   # d(ab)/da = b, d(ab)/db = a

    def __add__(self, other):
        other = other if isinstance(other, Var) else Var(other, self._tape)
        return self._record(lambda a, b: a + b, (self, other), (1.0, 1.0))

    def sin(self):
        return self._record(math.sin, (self,), (math.cos(self.value),))

def backward(y):
    y.grad = 1.0                                  # seed: dL/dL
    for out, args, partials in reversed(y._tape):  # reverse sweep
        for arg, p in zip(args, partials):
            arg.grad += out.grad * p               # fan-out accumulation

# f(x1, x2) = sin(x1 * x2) + x1^2 ; tape records [v1=x1*x2, v2=sin(v1), v3=x1*x1, v4=v2+v3]
x1, x2 = Var(2.0), Var(3.0)
f = x1.sin() if False else (x1 * x2).sin() + x1 * x1
backward(f)
print(f"value={f.value:.6f}  df/dx1={x1.grad:.6f}  df/dx2={x2.grad:.6f}")
```

The tape grows linearly with the number of operations; the reverse loop touches each entry once. Every industrial system is a refinement of this loop.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 From Wengert lists to SSA: the representation ladder

The lineage of reverse-mode representations forms a ladder of increasing static structure:

1. **Wengert list (1964):** a *dynamic* linear trace — operations numbered in execution order [1].
2. **Computational graph:** the same data as a DAG, exposing parallelism and common subexpressions.
3. **Tape:** the list plus recorded values, optimized for the reverse sweep (ADOL-C, CoDiPack).
4. **SSA form (Innes 2018):** the list recognized as *static single assignment*; control flow becomes $\phi$-nodes, enabling *source transformation* — the adjoint program is generated ahead of time and handed to an optimizing compiler [4].

Innes's key move is to *not unroll the adjoint*: rather than replaying a dynamic tape, Zygote transforms the SSA IR so that primal and adjoint code coexist in one compiled program [4]. This is the philosophical bridge to Enzyme (Section 4.4): both differentiate *compiler intermediate representations* rather than dynamic traces.

> **Theorem:** Any straight-line program with $l$ elementary operations admits a Wengert list of length $l$; its adjoint program, obtained by reversing the list and inserting VJP closures, computes $\nabla f$ in $\Theta(l)$ additional arithmetic operations.

### 4.2 Tape-based checkpointing: the Revolve schedule

Naive reverse mode stores the entire tape: memory $\Theta(l)$, fatal for $l \sim 10^9$ (deep networks, long time-stepping loops). **Checkpointing** trades recomputation for memory: store only selected *snapshots* of the forward state, and recompute forward segments on demand during the reverse sweep.

The problem reduces to a scheduling question: given $c$ checkpoints and a computation of $l$ steps, minimize total recomputation. Griewank (1992) showed logarithmic temporal overhead is achievable, and Griewank and Walther (2000) gave the definitive answer — the **Revolve** algorithm [6]:

- **Binomial schedule:** With $c$ checkpoints and $t$ time steps, the optimal strategy partitions the interval binomially: the reverse sweep over $[0, l]$ with $c$ checkpoints costs $\approx \binom{c+r}{c}$ effective forward evaluations, where $r$ is the number of allowed recomputation rounds.
- **Logarithmic growth:** With a *fixed* memory budget $c$, the number of forward steps that can be reversed grows combinatorially in $c$; equivalently, temporal complexity grows *logarithmically* in the problem size for fixed memory [6].

Concretely, for $l$ steps and $c$ checkpoints, Revolve achieves reversal with $\mathcal{O}(l)$ peak memory reduced to $\mathcal{O}(c + l^{1/(c+1)})$ segment storage and total work $\mathcal{O}(l \log_{c} l)$ in the binomial regime — a dramatic improvement over the $\mathcal{O}(l)$ memory of the naive tape.

JAX exposes this directly as `jax.checkpoint` (formerly `remat`): users annotate memory-hungry segments (e.g., transformer layers), and JAX's transposition rules recompute the primal segment during the reverse pass instead of storing its activations [2]. Enzyme achieves a related effect at the LLVM level by *recomputing* values that are cheap to regenerate rather than spilling them to the tape — a static-analysis-driven, fine-grained checkpointing [3].

| Strategy | Peak memory | Extra forward work | Use case |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full tape | $\Theta(l)$ | $0$ | Default; $l$ small |
| Uniform checkpoints | $\Theta(l/k)$ | $\Theta(k)$ per segment | Periodic snapshots |
| Binomial (Revolve) | $\Theta(c)$ fixed | $\mathcal{O}(\log l)$ factor | Fixed-memory optimal [6] |
| Selective remat | $\Theta(l - \text{saved})$ | recompute of saved ops | Cheap ops (e.g., activations) |

### 4.3 Higher-order differentiation and perturbation confusion

Computing Hessians or differentiating through gradients — $\nabla^2 f$, $\frac{d}{d\theta}\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}$ — nests AD inside AD.

The standard cures:

- **Forward-over-reverse:** compute $\nabla f$ by reverse mode (a program), then differentiate that program by *forward* mode with a directional seed. This yields Hessian–vector products $Hv$ in $\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{cost}(f))$ — the workhorse of second-order optimization and meta-learning. Formally, $Hv = \left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\nabla f(x + \epsilon v)\right|_{\epsilon=0}$.
- **Reverse-over-forward:** for $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ with $n$ small, reverse over the forward program.
- **Mixed-mode with fresh tags:** each nesting level gets distinct perturbation tags (as in the *Stalingrad*/modern JAX transformation stack), guaranteeing correctness by construction [2].

JAX's architecture is instructive: `jax.grad` is not a monolith but a *program transformation* producing a function; composing `grad(grad(f))` simply applies the transformation twice, with the trace-based system keeping each level's cotangents distinct by construction [2].

A minimal Hessian–vector product in JAX:

```python
import jax, jax.numpy as jnp

def f(x):
    return jnp.sum(jnp.tanh(x) ** 2)

grad_f = jax.grad(f)

def hvp(x, v):
    # forward-over-reverse: d/dε ∇f(x + εv)|ε=0
    return jax.jvp(grad_f, (x,), (v,))[1]

x = jnp.array([0.5, -1.0, 2.0])
v = jnp.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.5])
print(hvp(x, v))   # H @ v, one reverse + one forward pass
```

### 4.4 The JAX–Enzyme design spectrum

Modern AD systems distribute along an axis: *when* differentiation happens relative to optimization and compilation.

**JAX: trace, transform, compile.** JAX traces Python/NumPy code into a flat *jaxpr* — essentially a typed Wengert list of primitive operations [2]. AD is a source-to-source transformation on the jaxpr: each primitive's transpose rule synthesizes the adjoint program; XLA then compiles the result. Because differentiation precedes XLA optimization, JAX sees high-level structure (map, scan, while_loop) and differentiates *through* structured control flow rather than unrolling it [2]. Strengths: composable transformations (`jit`, `grad`, `vmap`, `pmap`), arbitrary-order differentiation, accelerator codegen. Costs: tracing is *monomorphic* — new shapes/dtypes re-trigger compilation [2]; Python-level dynamism (data-dependent control flow on traced values) is restricted.

**Enzyme: optimize, then differentiate.** Enzyme is an LLVM compiler plugin that synthesizes adjoints for *optimized* LLVM IR [3]. It differentiates after the full LLVM optimization pipeline, so the adjoint inherits constant propagation, inlining, loop unrolling, and vectorization. Any language targeting LLVM (C, C++, Fortran, Julia, Rust, Swift) becomes differentiable without rewriting [3]. On ADBench, AD on optimized IR achieved a geometric mean **4.2× speedup over AD before optimization**, reaching state-of-the-art [3]; Enzyme also differentiates GPU kernels directly (SC'21 [EnzymeAD/Enzyme]). Costs: it requires *statically analyzable* IR — dynamic dispatch and opaque calls defeat it; and differentiation happens late, so high-level mathematical structure (a "softmax") is invisible.

The two systems thus embody complementary answers to the same Wengert-list question: JAX keeps the list *high-level and transformable*; Enzyme keeps the list *low-level and optimizable*.

> **Theorem (Correctness of the adjoint sweep):** Let $v_1, \ldots, v_l$ be a Wengert list for $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ with recorded values. Initializing $\bar{v}_l = 1$ and processing $i = l, \ldots, 1$ with $\bar{v}_j \mathrel{+}= \bar{v}_i \, \partial \varphi_i / \partial v_j$ for all $j \prec i$ yields $\bar{v}_{1-n} = \partial f / \partial x_1, \ldots, \bar{v}_0 = \partial f / \partial x_n$.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Induction on $i$ descending: $\bar{v}_i = \partial y / \partial v_i$ holds at $i = l$; the update is exactly the chain-rule expansion of $\partial y / \partial v_j$ through its successors, each already correct by the induction hypothesis. ∎

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Complexity theorems

**Theorem 5.1 (Cheap gradient, constructive).** A Wengert list of length $l$ admits a reverse sweep using at most $\omega \cdot l$ elementary operations, $\omega \leq 5$ for typical op sets, plus $\Theta(l)$ tape memory.

*Proof.* Each elementary $\varphi_i$ has bounded fan-in $k$ (unary/binary); its local partials cost $\mathcal{O}(1)$ given recorded values, and the fan-out accumulation touches each edge once. Summing over $l$ steps gives the bound. Memory: one tape entry per step. ∎

**Theorem 5.2 (Optimality of binomial checkpointing).** Among schedules using at most $c$ simultaneously live checkpoints to reverse $l$ steps, the Revolve binomial schedule minimizes total forward evaluations, achieving $\mathcal{O}(l)$ memory reduction to $\mathcal{O}(c)$ with recomputation factor $\mathcal{O}(\log l)$ for fixed $c$ [6].

*Proof sketch.* The problem is equivalent to the classic "reversible pebbling with recomputation" game; Griewank and Walther show the binomial placement solves the associated dynamic program, and a matching lower bound follows from counting distinct state configurations reachable with $c$ pebbles. ∎

### 5.2 Empirical anchors

- **Overhead ratios:** Baydin et al. report the widely reproduced empirical rule that reverse-mode AD costs $\omega_b \approx 5$–$10 \times$ the primal evaluation, versus $1$–$3 \times$ for forward mode, with the exact ratio program-dependent [1][7].
- **Enzyme on optimized IR:** 4.2× geometric-mean speedup of AD-after-optimization over AD-before-optimization on an ML benchmark suite including ADBench, achieving state-of-the-art gradient performance [3].
- **JAX tracing:** JAX's high-level tracing compiles a convex-quadratic benchmark 114× faster than the pure-Python baseline (4.12 s → 0.036 s) by fusing the traced program through XLA [2].

## 6. Limitations

1. **The tape's constant factors.** Reverse mode's $\omega_b \approx 5$–$10$ can dominate in latency-critical inference-adjacent loops; operator fusion (XLA) and Enzyme's post-optimization AD mitigate but do not eliminate this [1][3].
2. **Checkpoint scheduling is offline and rigid.** Revolve requires knowing $l$ in advance; adaptive or data-dependent loop bounds force conservative over-checkpointing. Online (dynamic) checkpointing remains less understood [6].
3. **Higher-order memory blowup.** Forward-over-reverse for Hessians composes tapes multiplicatively in the worst case; practical systems rely on HVP formulations that avoid materializing $H$ [1].
4. **Non-smoothness.** The Wengert formalism assumes differentiable elementaries; ReLU kinks, branching, and stochastic nodes require subgradient conventions, smoothing, or specialized estimators — outside the classical theory [1].
5. **Enzyme's static analyzability requirement.** Opaque foreign calls, dynamic dispatch, and inline assembly in LLVM IR cannot be differentiated; users must supply custom derivative rules [3].
6. **JAX's tracing restrictions.** Data-dependent Python control flow on traced values, in-place mutation, and dynamic shapes all violate tracing assumptions, forcing `lax` control-flow primitives and functional discipline [2].

---

## 7. Conclusion

Reverse-mode AD is best understood not as "backpropagation" but as the computational realization of the Wengert list: decompose, record, and traverse the decomposition in reverse. The tape is the list made concrete; checkpointing is the tape made frugal, with the Revolve schedule proving that logarithmic recomputation buys constant memory; higher-order AD is the list nested, with tag discipline restoring correctness; and JAX and Enzyme are two modern answers to *where in the compiler pipeline the list should live* — before optimization as a transformable trace, or after optimization as analyzable IR.

The open frontier is *checkpoint-aware compilation*: a system that, like Enzyme, reasons about recomputation at the IR level but, like JAX, preserves high-level structure — automatically placing Revolve-optimal checkpoints inside fused, compiled adjoint programs. The sixty-year arc from Wengert's lists to differentiable compilers suggests the next decade's gains will come not from faster adjoints, but from smarter tapes.
