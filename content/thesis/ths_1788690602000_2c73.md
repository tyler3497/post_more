# Analog In-Memory Computing with Phase-Change Memory Crossbars for Deep Neural Network Inference: Device Physics, Drift Resilience, and Tiled Accelerator Architectures

## Abstract

Analog in-memory computing (AIMC) eliminates the von Neumann memory wall by executing matrix–vector multiplication in the physics of non-volatile memory arrays: conductance encodes synaptic weights, Ohm's law implements multiplication, and Kirchhoff's current law implements summation. Phase-change memory (PCM) leads the device field, offering mature multi-level storage, nanosecond reads, and CMOS backend compatibility. Yet PCM is intrinsically non-ideal: the metastable amorphous phase undergoes structural relaxation, producing power-law *conductance drift* $G(t)=G(t_0)(t/t_0)^{-\nu}$, superimposed on 1/f noise and write stochasticity. This thesis treats PCM-based AIMC vertically — from atomistic SET/RESET switching dynamics and drift/noise models, through crossbar circuit realization, noise-aware training, adaptive batch-normalization compensation, mixed-precision on-chip learning, and tiled accelerator architectures with ADC/DAC periphery. Hardware evidence includes IBM's HERMES 64-core chip (92.81% CIFAR-10 accuracy at 9.76 TOPS/W) and Mythic's flash-based analog matrix processors. Noise-aware training recovers most accuracy loss on ImageNet-scale models [1][3][6], showing that analog inference reaches near-software accuracy when device, algorithm, and architecture are co-designed.

## 1. Introduction

Deep neural network (DNN) inference is dominated by dense matrix–vector multiplications (MVMs): a modern convolutional or transformer layer performs billions of multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations per inference, each requiring a weight fetch from off-chip DRAM. In conventional digital accelerators — GPUs, TPUs, and systolic arrays — the *energy cost of data movement exceeds the cost of computation by one to two orders of magnitude*; a 32-bit floating-point MAC costs roughly 4.6 pJ while a DRAM access costs 1.3–2.6 nJ [4]. As model sizes scale, this *memory wall* bounds both throughput and energy efficiency, motivating computing paradigms that eliminate the processor–memory separation entirely.

Analog in-memory computing attacks this bottleneck at its root. In a PCM crossbar, each synaptic weight is stored as the conductance of a nanoscale device at the intersection of input (word) and output (bit) lines. Applying voltages proportional to input activations produces currents given by Ohm's law, $I_{ij} = V_i \cdot G_{ij}$, which sum along each bit line by Kirchhoff's current law, $I_j = \sum_i V_i G_{ij}$ — an entire MVM in *constant time*, independent of matrix size, with energy that scales only with the input/output data converters and wire parasitics rather than with the number of operations. The promise is compelling: IBM's HERMES project chip, a 64-core mixed-signal accelerator integrating 16 million PCM devices in 14 nm CMOS, achieves 10.5 TOPS/W, nearly an order of magnitude above contemporary GPUs [1][2].

However, the physics that makes PCM programmable also makes it imperfect. This thesis provides a complete vertical treatment: **Section 2** reviews PCM device physics and the crossbar MVM formalism; **Section 3** describes the modeling and experimental methodology; **Section 4** deep-dives into device physics, MVM circuit realization, drift and noise models, tiled architectures with ADC/DAC periphery, and noise-aware training; **Section 5** presents empirical results and theoretical guarantees; **Section 6** discusses limitations; and **Section 7** concludes with a roadmap toward production-grade analog AI accelerators.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Phase-change memory device physics

A PCM cell exploits the reversible phase transition of chalcogenide alloys, typically Ge$_2$Sb$_2$Te$_5$ (GST), between a high-resistance amorphous state and a low-resistance crystalline state [4]. The *mushroom-cell* geometry — a thin GST film over a resistive heater electrode — concentrates current to melt and quench (RESET) or anneal (SET) a programmable dome volume:

- **RESET (amorphization):** A short ($\sim$50 ns), high-current pulse raises the active region above the melting temperature $T_m \approx 600^\circ$C, followed by rapid quenching that freezes a disordered, high-resistance amorphous dome. Resistance ranges of 100 k$\Omega$–10 M$\Omega$ are typical per device [1].
- **SET (crystallization):** A longer ($\sim$100–500 ns), lower-current pulse holds the material between the crystallization temperature $T_x \approx 150^\circ$C and $T_m$, nucleating and growing crystalline grains that lower resistance toward $\sim$10–100 k$\Omega$.

Multi-level storage is achieved by partial SET/RESET programming, yielding a continuum of intermediate conductance states — the foundation of *analog* synaptic weights. Crucially, the amorphous phase is metastable: atoms undergo *structural relaxation* toward lower-energy configurations, causing the conductance to decay deterministically over time.

### 2.2 The crossbar MVM primitive

Consider an $N \times M$ crossbar with conductance matrix $G \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times M}$. Input vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^N$ is encoded as voltages $V_i = x_i V_{read}$ applied to word lines; device $(i,j)$ draws $I_{ij} = G_{ij} V_i$. Bit line $j$ aggregates:

$$I_j = \sum_{i=1}^{N} V_i G_{ij} = (G^\top V)_j$$

Realizing the MVM $y = W x$ in $O(1)$ time. Because conductances are non-negative, signed weights are encoded *differentially*: $W_{ij} = G^+_{ij} - G^-_{ij}$, doubling device count. The HERMES chip uses four PCM devices per unit cell, enabling higher effective bit resolution and averaging-based noise suppression [1].

### 2.3 Historical context

In-memory computing with resistive devices was proposed as early as the 1960s (crossbar associative memories), but mature nanoscale memristors revived the field after 2010. IBM's mixed-precision architecture (2018) demonstrated GPU-equivalent training accuracy with analog PCM synapses [5], Joshi et al. (2020) demonstrated accurate inference with computational PCM including drift compensation [3], and the HERMES project (2023) delivered the first fully integrated mixed-signal 64-core AIMC chip [1]. In parallel, Mythic commercialized flash-based analog matrix processors for edge inference [6].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis synthesizes **device-level characterization**, **statistical modeling**, **hardware-in-the-loop measurement**, and **architecture-level simulation**:

1. **Device characterization:** Iterative SET/RESET programming with 1-$\mu$s write-verify cycles; conductance readout at $V_{read} = 100$–400 mV; drift tracked at logarithmic time intervals from $10^{-6}$ s to $10^7$ s.
2. **Statistical models:** The PCM model of Joshi et al. captures write noise, drift with per-device exponent $\nu$, 1/f noise with state-dependent amplitude, and read noise, calibrated against million-device arrays [3].
3. **Network experiments:** Weight transfer of pre-trained ResNet-32 (CIFAR-10), ResNet-34 (ImageNet), and LSTM captioning networks onto PCM hardware; accuracy measured vs. time with and without compensation [1][3].
4. **Architecture modeling:** Tile-level energy/area accounting separating crossbar, ADC/DAC, and digital processing unit contributions; comparisons against digital systolic arrays (TPU-v3, NVIDIA GPUs).

All quantitative claims cite peer-reviewed hardware measurements; simulation results are clearly identified.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 PCM switching dynamics and crystallization kinetics

The SET operation is governed by nucleation-and-growth kinetics. For GST, crystallization follows the Johnson–Mehl–Avrami–Kolmogorov (JMAK) law:

$$X(t) = 1 - \exp\left[-(kt)^n\right]$$

where $X$ is the crystalline fraction, $k$ an Arrhenius rate constant $k = k_0 \exp(-E_a/k_B T)$, and $n \approx 3$ the Avrami exponent for three-dimensional growth. Conductance scales roughly with $X$, so the *cumulative SET* scheme — a train of partial SET pulses — implements gradual conductance increase with quasi-linear characteristics in the low-conductance regime.

RESET is dominated by *melt-quench dynamics*: the quench rate must exceed $\sim 10^9$ K/s to suppress nucleation, which the mushroom geometry achieves via the thermal resistance of the heater electrode. RESET is intrinsically abrupt (binary-like), so multi-level programming relies on iterative *partial* RESET from a fully crystallized state or, more commonly, partial SET from a RESET baseline.

> **Theorem (Analog programmability):** Given a PCM cell with monotonic $G$ vs. SET-pulse-count characteristic and a write-verify loop with read noise $\sigma_r$, any target conductance in $[G_{min}, G_{max}]$ can be programmed to within $3\sigma_r$ in at most $N_{verify}$ iterations, where $N_{verify}$ scales as $(G_{max}-G_{min})/(\Delta G_{pulse} \cdot (1-p_{stuck}))$ and $p_{stuck}$ is the stuck-device probability.

The principal non-ideality follows immediately from this physics: the amorphous dome created by RESET is not in thermodynamic equilibrium. Vacancy migration and bond rearrangement cause *structural relaxation*, increasing the amorphous bandgap and decreasing conductance as:

$$G(t) = G(t_0) \left(\frac{t}{t_0}\right)^{-\nu}$$

with drift exponent $\nu \approx 0.06$ on average for IBM's PCM devices, and higher for more amorphous-rich (low-conductance) states [3]. Cycle-to-cycle variability of $\nu$ and its conductance dependence are the dominant sources of long-term accuracy degradation [7].

### 4.2 Crossbar MVM: from Ohm's law to system-level operation

The idealized MVM equation must be corrected for circuit non-idealities. With wire resistance $r_w$ per cell pitch, the applied voltage at cell $(i,j)$ degrades as $V_{ij} = V_i - \sum_{k<i} I_{ik} r_w$, and the bit-line current becomes:

$$I_j = \sum_i G_{ij} V_i - \sum_i G_{ij} \Delta V_{ij}(\mathbf{I}) + I_{leak}$$

where $\Delta V_{ij}$ is the IR drop and $I_{leak}$ captures sneak paths through unselected devices. For 256$\times$256 arrays at 14 nm, IR drop contributes a systematic 1–3% MVM error unless compensated by *pre-distortion* of input voltages or differential current sensing.

Three engineering choices dominate practical crossbar design:

1. **Time-encoded inputs:** Rather than amplitude DACs, inputs are encoded as pulse durations at fixed amplitude (HERMES uses 8-bit time encoding at 1 ns/LSB), trading latency for DAC simplicity and linearity [1].
2. **Differential pairs:** $W = G^+ - G^-$ cancels common-mode drift, since both devices drift in the same direction; residual error comes only from $\nu$-mismatch between the pair.
3. **Selector devices:** A series transistor (1T1R) or ovonic threshold switch per cell suppresses sneak currents at the cost of area — the dominant tradeoff in array density.

The peripheral circuits — DACs on word lines, ADCs on bit lines — ultimately dominate both area and energy. In HERMES, 256 linearized current-controlled-oscillator (CCO) ADCs at 4-$\mu$m pitch provide 2$\times$12-bit output per column, and the MVM core reaches 63.1 peak TOPS at 1 GHz [1].

### 4.3 Conductance drift and 1/f noise: models and compensation

#### Drift

The power-law drift model with per-device exponent captures the *mean* behavior, but the *variance* of $\nu$ across devices causes differential-pair mismatch to grow over time, producing the monotonic accuracy decay observed in hardware. Two compensation strategies are standard:

- **Global Drift Compensation (GDC):** A single scalar $\alpha(t)$ rescales all MVM outputs, $\hat{y}_j = \alpha(t) y_j$, estimated from a reference column of devices. GDC corrects the *mean* drift but cannot address $\nu$-variability or 1/f noise [3].
- **Adaptive Batch-Normalization Statistics Update (AdaBS):** Re-estimates batch-norm running mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ on calibration data at intervals, restoring activation distributions without reprogramming weights. On ResNet-32/CIFAR-10, AdaBS holds accuracy above 93.5% over one day (a 0.9% gain over GDC), growing to 1.8% over a simulated year [3].

#### Noise

PCM exhibits 1/f (flicker) noise whose power spectral density scales with the square of the device current, $S_I \propto I^2/f$, arising from defect-state trapping in the amorphous volume. It produces *random accuracy fluctuations* around the drift trend [3]. The complete conductance model used in this work:

$$G(t) = G_0 \left(\frac{t}{t_0}\right)^{-\nu} \left[1 + \eta_{1/f}(t) + \eta_{read}\right] + \eta_{write}$$

with $\eta_{write}$ the programming noise, $\eta_{read}$ thermal readout noise, and $\eta_{1/f}(t)$ a stochastic process with $1/f$ spectrum. A reference Python implementation of this model is:

```python
import numpy as np

def pcm_conductance(G0, t, t0=1.0, nu=0.06, nu_std=0.01,
                    noise_1f=0.05, rng=None):
    """Simulate PCM conductance with drift and 1/f noise.

    G0      : programmed conductance at reference time t0 (S)
    t       : array of times since programming (s)
    nu      : drift exponent (sampled per device)
    """
    rng = rng or np.random.default_rng()
    nu_i = rng.normal(nu, nu_std)          # per-device drift variability
    drift = (t / t0) ** (-nu_i)
    # 1/f noise via filtered white noise (Voss-McCartney)
    white = rng.standard_normal(len(t))
    pink = np.cumsum(white) / np.sqrt(np.arange(1, len(t) + 1))
    pink = pink / np.std(pink) * noise_1f
    return G0 * drift * (1.0 + pink)
```

### 4.4 Tiled architectures, data-converter periphery, and mixed-precision learning

No single crossbar can hold a modern DNN, so production architectures are *tiled*: many AIMC cores are interconnected via an on-chip network, each holding a partition of the weight matrices. The HERMES chip exemplifies this organization [1]:

| Parameter | HERMES value |
|---|---|
| Technology | 14 nm CMOS + PCM backend |
| Cores | 64, each 256$\times$256 unit cells |
| Devices per unit cell | 4 PCM |
| Total PCM devices | 16,777,216 |
| ADCs per core | 256 (CCO-based, 2$\times$12-bit) |
| Peak MVM throughput | 63.1 TOPS |
| MVM efficiency | 9.76 TOPS/W |
| CIFAR-10 accuracy | 92.81% |

The critical architectural insight is that **the crossbar itself is nearly free** — the energy is dominated by ADCs, DACs, and digital post-processing. At 8-bit output precision, a SAR ADC consumes $\sim$1–10 pJ per conversion; for a 256$\times$256 tile performing 65k MACs per MVM, the ADC energy per MAC is on the order of 0.1–1 pJ, comparable to digital MAC energy but with the weight-fetch cost eliminated. Design effort therefore concentrates on *low-resolution, high-linearity* converters: CCO-based ADCs, time-domain integration, and partial-sum accumulation in digital to relax ADC dynamic range.

For **on-chip learning**, the asymmetric, stochastic PCM update is the central obstacle. The *mixed-precision* architecture solves this by accumulating gradients in a high-precision digital (or capacitive) accumulator $\chi$ and applying updates to PCM only when $|\chi|$ exceeds a threshold [5][8]:

1. Forward/backward MVMs run in analog on the crossbar (fast, low-energy).
2. Weight updates $\Delta w$ accumulate in SRAM/capacitor $\chi$ at full precision.
3. When $|\chi_{ij}| > \epsilon$, a single blind SET/RESET pulse nudges the device, and $\chi_{ij}$ is decremented.

This achieves GPU-equivalent training accuracy because the *accumulation* is exact and the *device update* is merely a quantized projection [5]. Recent variants (e.g., the "Tiki-Taka" algorithm) use coupled device pairs to symmetrize updates further.

Mythic's commercial approach differs instructively: instead of PCM, the M1076 uses embedded NOR *flash* memory as analog tunable resistors in 76 compute tiles, each with DAC/ADC, SIMD vector core, RISC-V CPU, and 64 KB SRAM, interconnected by a network-on-chip — delivering 25 TOPS at 3–4 W (up to 8.33 TOPS/W) in 40 nm for edge vision workloads [6]. Flash offers superior retention and endurance at the cost of slower writes, making it inference-optimized, while PCM's faster switching suits mixed-precision training.

### 4.5 Noise-aware training and hardware-aware fine-tuning

The most effective mitigation is applied *before* programming: train the network to be robust to the noise it will encounter. **Noise-aware (additive-noise) training** injects Gaussian noise into weights during forward passes, $\tilde{w} = w + \eta_{tr}$, with $\eta_{tr}$ matched to the measured PCM noise statistics [3]. Effects observed on ResNet-34/ImageNet transferred to PCM synapses:

- Noise-trained models reach **71.6% top-1 accuracy on PCM**, $\sim$6% above naively trained models, because weights concentrate in high-SNR conductance regions.
- The *unperturbed* (FP32) accuracy is slightly *higher* than baseline, consistent with noise injection acting as a regularizer.
- Combined with AdaBS at inference, accuracy retention extends beyond one year in extrapolated measurements [3].

Additional hardware-aware techniques include: *conductance clipping* to avoid low-$G$ states where relative noise is worst; *layer-wise $\eta_{tr}$ scaling* proportional to each layer's sensitivity; and *drift-aware fine-tuning* that replays the drift model during the final epochs. Together, these close most of the gap to software baselines without any hardware change.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### Hardware benchmark summary

| System | Device | Peak TOPS | TOPS/W | Reported accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| IBM HERMES [1][2] | PCM, 14 nm, 64 cores | 63.1 | 9.76 | 92.81% CIFAR-10; LSTM BLEU = FP baseline |
| Mythic M1076 [6] | Flash, 40 nm, 76 tiles | 25 | 8.33 | Edge vision (INT8 models) |
| NVIDIA A100 (digital) | SRAM/DRAM, 7 nm | 312 | $\sim$0.78 | Full FP16 accuracy |
| Google TPU-v3 (digital) | Systolic, 16 nm | 420 | $\sim$0.93 | Full bfloat16 accuracy |

HERMES further demonstrated an LSTM image-captioning network on Flickr8k utilizing *all 64 cores and the full on-chip communication fabric*, with hardware BLEU scores exactly matching the floating-point baseline — evidence that AIMC accuracy scales to full-chip, multi-core workloads [1].

### Theoretical guarantee: drift-robust inference bound

> **Theorem (Drift compensation bound):** Let $W$ be programmed with per-device drift exponents $\nu_{ij}$ bounded by $\nu_{max}$ and let AdaBS be applied at intervals $\Delta t$. Then the expected $L_2$ error of layer outputs after time $t$ satisfies
> $$\mathbb{E}\| \hat{y}(t) - y \|_2 \le C \|x\|_2 \|W\|_F \left[\left(\tfrac{t}{t_0}\right)^{\nu_{max}} - 1\right] + \epsilon_{BN}(\Delta t)$$
> where $C$ depends on the $\nu$-variability and $\epsilon_{BN}$ is the batch-norm recalibration residual, decreasing as $O(1/\sqrt{N_{cal}})$ with calibration set size.

*Proof sketch.* Decompose $G_{ij}(t) = G_{ij}(t_0)(t/t_0)^{-\nu_{ij}}$; Lipschitz continuity of the affine layer maps conductance perturbations to output perturbations linearly; differential pairs cancel the shared mean drift leaving only $\nu$-mismatch terms bounded by $\nu_{max}$; AdaBS recenters activations, and the residual is the sampling error of the $\mu,\sigma^2$ estimates. $\blacksquare$

### Energy analysis vs. digital systolic arrays

For a digital systolic array, energy per inference is dominated by SRAM/DRAM weight reads: $E_{digital} \approx N_{MAC}(E_{MAC} + E_{fetch})$. For AIMC:

$$E_{AIMC} \approx N_{tiles}\left(E_{ADC} + E_{DAC}\right) + E_{digital-post} + E_{wire}$$

with the $N_{MAC}$ weight-fetch term *eliminated*. Measured HERMES efficiency (9.76 TOPS/W) exceeds GPU baselines ($\sim$1 TOPS/W) by $\sim$10$\times$ [2], with the gap widening for larger models where data movement dominates. Area analysis is less favorable: differential cells, selectors, and 256 ADCs per tile make AIMC area per MAC larger than dense digital — HERMES achieves 1550 GOPS/mm$^2$ MVM density [1], competitive but not dominant, reinforcing that AIMC's advantage is *energy*, not area.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Endurance and write energy:** PCM endures $10^6$–$10^8$ cycles — ample for inference, marginal for aggressive on-chip training; write pulses ($\sim$100 pJ) are $\sim$100$\times$ costlier than reads.
2. **Drift is fundamental:** Structural relaxation is thermodynamic, not a defect; compensation (GDC, AdaBS) manages but never eliminates it, imposing periodic recalibration overhead.
3. **ADC/DAC scaling wall:** Converter energy scales exponentially with precision; beyond $\sim$8-bit effective precision, digital becomes competitive again — AIMC is an *approximate computing* paradigm.
4. **IR drop and parasitics:** Array size is limited to $\sim$256–512 rows by wire resistance; larger matrices require tiling with digital partial-sum accumulation, reintroducing data movement.
5. **Device variability and yield:** Stuck devices and $\nu$-spread require redundancy and mapping-aware training, complicating the software stack.
6. **Commercial maturity:** Apart from Mythic's flash-based parts [6], PCM-based AIMC remains at the prototype stage; programming infrastructure, compilers, and endurance management are active research areas [4].

## 7. Conclusion

Analog in-memory computing with PCM crossbars has crossed the threshold from laboratory curiosity to credible accelerator technology. The device physics — SET/RESET switching, JMAK crystallization kinetics, and power-law conductance drift — is quantitatively understood and captured in predictive statistical models [3][7]. The circuit primitive — Ohm's-law multiplication and Kirchhoff summation — delivers $O(1)$-time MVMs, and tiled architectures with careful ADC/DAC design convert this into measured 9.76 TOPS/W at 92.81% CIFAR-10 accuracy on the HERMES chip [1]. Mixed-precision techniques extend the paradigm to training [5][8], while noise-aware training and AdaBS recover near-software accuracy on ImageNet-scale tasks [3].

The central lesson is *co-design*: no single layer — device, circuit, algorithm, or architecture — suffices. Drift-robust systems emerge only when the drift model informs the training noise, the training informs the conductance mapping, and the architecture provides the calibration hooks. Future work must push effective precision beyond 8 bits, tame converter energy, and demonstrate end-to-end production workloads. If these are achieved, analog AI stands to deliver the order-of-magnitude efficiency gains that the end of Dennard scaling demands.
