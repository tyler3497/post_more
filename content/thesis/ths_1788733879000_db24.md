# Pauseless Garbage Collection: Azul C4, ZGC Colored Pointers, and Shenandoah Concurrent Compaction

## Abstract

Pauseless garbage collection is the response of managed-runtime engineering to the worst-case latency demands of trading, billing, and real-time analytics systems, where a single stop-the-world pause of hundreds of milliseconds is a correctness-level failure. This thesis unifies the three most significant concurrent compacting collectors ever deployed in production Java virtual machines: Azul's C4 (the Continuously Concurrent Compacting Collector), Oracle's ZGC, and Red Hat's Shenandoah. Each reduces GC-induced mutator blocking to millisecond-scale levels, but they disagree sharply on mechanism. C4 leans on the Loaded Value Barrier and self-healing read barriers with Not-Marked-Through (NMT) metadata; ZGC embeds GC metadata directly into 64-bit object pointers (colored pointers) and heals them through load barriers backed by multi-mapped virtual memory; Shenandoah attaches a Brooks forwarding pointer to every object and enforces a to-space invariant through write barriers, SATB marking, and a Load Reference Barrier. We formalize their barrier invariants, compare their mutator-overhead models, review published latency and throughput measurements, and isolate the engineering trade-offs that make the choice among them a matter of workload, platform, and latency budget rather than principle.

## 1 Introduction

The fundamental tension in automatic memory management is the **throughput–latency trade-off**: any collector that must periodically determine object liveness and reclaim unreachable memory must either stop the mutator while it works or arrange for its work to be interleaved with mutator execution without introducing unsoundness. Classical *stop-the-world* collectors — serial, parallel throughput collectors — choose the former and pay in worst-case pause time that grows with heap size and live set. For applications measured in *tail latency* — limit-order books, fraud-scoring pipelines, stream-processing topologies — such pauses translate directly into lost revenue and violated service-level agreements [7][8].

The pauseless collectors examined here attack the problem by pushing nearly all collector work into **concurrent phases**, leaving stop-the-world phases only for root scanning and handshakes whose cost scales with thread count, not heap size. Azul's C4 was the first production collector to demonstrate this on multi-terabyte heaps, introduced on custom hardware with hardware-assisted read barriers and later ported to stock x86-64 [6]. ZGC, integrated into OpenJDK 11 as an experimental collector (JEP 333) and declared production-ready in JDK 15 (JEP 377), brought colored pointers and load barriers to mainline OpenJDK with pause times that do not increase with heap or live-set size [1]. Shenandoah, contributed by Red Hat and integrated via JEP 189 (JDK 12), took a different path: Brooks forwarding pointers and write barriers, trading somewhat looser pause guarantees for lower steady-state mutator overhead and full support for compressed oops and 32-bit builds [4][5].

This thesis contributes a **unified formal treatment** of the three barrier schemes as instantiations of one abstract protocol — *concurrent relocation with mutator-observable pointer invariants* — a **comparative overhead model**, a synthesis of **published empirical results**, and a **limitations analysis** of the residual stop-the-world phases and platform constraints bounding each design.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The concurrent compaction problem

Compacting collection is desirable because it eliminates fragmentation: after a copying or compacting cycle, the heap (or each region) contains only live objects laid out contiguously, so allocation reduces to a pointer bump. The difficulty is *concurrency*: while the collector copies a live object `o` from from-space address `a` to to-space address `a'`, the mutator may hold stale pointers to `a`, load `a` from a field, store through it, or create new pointers to it. A sound concurrent compactor must guarantee three properties simultaneously:

it must guarantee **no lost updates** (mutator writes during relocation survive and land on the to-space copy), **no dangling or torn references** (stale from-space addresses are never dereferenced after reuse), and **pointer convergence** (all stale pointers are eventually remapped before memory is reused).

Classical solutions include *stop-the-world* compaction (trivially sound), *Baker's treadmill* with read barriers that forward every load, and *Brooks's* (1984) indirection scheme where every object carries a forwarding pointer and all accesses go through it. The three collectors in this thesis are best understood as three different answers to one question: **where, in the instruction stream, do we place the check that keeps the mutator honest during relocation?**

> **Theorem:** In any concurrent compacting collector, the mutator must be intercepted at every operation that could expose a stale reference — loads, stores, or both — unless the collector can prove that no stale reference can reach the operation. The choice of interception point determines the barrier family.

*Proof sketch.* Relocation establishes a bijection `f` from old to new addresses. If the mutator could load or store through a stale pointer `p` while from-space is being reused, the write could land on memory now owned by a different object (unsoundness). Intercepting loads alone suffices if the barrier remaps before the value is used and before stores propagate stale values (ZGC, C4). Intercepting stores alone suffices only if every *use* of a reference first passes through an intercepted operation — Shenandoah achieves this with a *load-reference barrier* that normalizes pointers on load into the register/stack domain, combined with Brooks indirection [4][5]. ∎

### 2.2 Barrier taxonomy

Following the literature, we classify barriers along three axes:

| Axis | Values | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger operation | load, store, both | ZGC: load; Shenandoah: load+store; C4: load (LVB) |
| Metadata location | in-pointer bits, object header word, side table | ZGC: colored pointers; Shenandoah: Brooks word + mark bitmaps; C4: object header NMT bits |
| Healing | self-healing (fixes source), lazy, none | C4: self-healing LVB; ZGC: heal-on-load via multi-map; Shenandoah: Brooks indirection |

This taxonomy organizes Section 4.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Azul C4 Collector: Continuous Concurrent Compaction and the Loaded Value Barrier

Azul's C4 is a **generational**, continuously concurrent, compacting collector. Each generation cycles through logically sequential *Mark*, *Relocate*, and *Remap* phases, and — critically — the phases of different generations overlap in time, so the collector is always doing useful work without ever stopping the world for a full collection [6]. The name encodes the design: **C**ontinuously **C**oncurrent **C**ompacting **C**ollector, always doing useful work without ever stopping the world for a full collection [6].

The cornerstone of C4 is the **Loaded Value Barrier (LVB)**, an incarnation of the pauseless read barrier. The LVB is positioned in the compiled code stream immediately after every reference load, and it imposes two invariants on every reference value visible to the mutator [6][7]:

1. **Mark-through invariant.** All visible loaded reference values will have been "marked through" by the collector — i.e., the referenced object is known live — or the barrier itself performs the marking work.
2. **Relocation invariant.** During relocate/remap phases, the visible reference is the current to-space address; a stale from-space reference is corrected before use.

The metadata enabling this is the **NMT (Not Marked Through) bit** associated with each reference. A GC trap on NMT mismatch queues the reference to the marker's work list, sets the NMT bit to "marked through," and — crucially — **corrected the source memory location** from which the stale reference had been loaded [7]. This is *self-healing*: the barrier also fixes the heap slot the stale reference came from, so the slot never traps again:

> **Theorem:** With a self-healing read barrier, each heap reference slot triggers the slow path at most once per GC phase, bounding total barrier slow-path work by the number of reference slots rather than by dynamic load count.

This bound makes the LVB's cost *predictable*: no hot loop repeatedly pays barrier costs on the same data, unlike Baker-style barriers [8]. Relocation exploits page protection and virtual-page remapping so a trapped stale pointer is corrected against remapped page tables. Critics note that sustaining page remapping at a rate roughly 100× the object allocation rate is expensive [6], which is why C4's economics depend on the LVB keeping slow-path frequency low.

### 4.2 ZGC: Colored Pointers, Load Barriers, and Multi-Mapped Remapping

ZGC (JEP 333, experimental in JDK 11; production-ready per JEP 377 in JDK 15) is a *concurrent, single-generation, region-based, NUMA-aware, compacting* collector whose stop-the-world phases are limited to root scanning — hence pause times that do not increase with heap or live-set size [1]. JEP 439 later added a generational mode, reusing the same barrier philosophy with distinct metadata bits per generation [2].

ZGC's core design choice is the combination of **load barriers** with **colored object pointers (colored oops)**: GC metadata is stored in the unused high bits of 64-bit pointers. On x86-64 the original layout reserved 4 color bits — *Marked1*, *Marked0*, *Remapped*, *Finalizable* — alongside a 42-bit object address (4 TB address space), later extended to 44 bits (16 TB) [1][3]. The colored pointer thus carries the load barrier's three questions in the pointer itself — *Marked0/Marked1* (known live this cycle?), *Remapped* (address already final?), *Finalizable* (reachable only via finalizer?) — with marking colors double-buffered per cycle.

The load barrier is a small JIT-injected fragment on every heap reference load: it checks for a "bad color" for the current phase and, if found, takes corrective action and *heals* the pointer [3]. Because the metadata travels with the pointer, the barrier needs no object-header access and no side-table lookup. And crucially, colored pointers allow ZGC to **reclaim and reuse memory during relocation before all pointers into the reclaimed regions have been fixed**: the multi-mapped heap maps the same physical pages at three virtual addresses (one per color view), so a stale-colored pointer remains *dereferenceable* until the load barrier heals it [1][3]. This eliminates the need for a separate mark-compact fallback for full GCs — there is no full GC in ZGC's design, only concurrent cycles.

Generational ZGC (JEP 439) drops multi-mapping, moves young-generation marking from load barriers to **store barriers** (loads vastly outnumber stores), and uses double-buffered remembered sets with relocation needing no extra heap [2]. Stack references become *colorless* pointers, translated at barrier boundaries — a concession that a single shift instruction on x64 can both test and strip metadata when the encoding is chosen carefully [2].

| Property | ZGC (non-generational) | Generational ZGC (JEP 439) |
|---|---|---|
| Heap layout | single generation | young + old |
| Multi-mapped memory | yes (3 views) | no |
| Max heap | 16 TB | beyond 16 TB possible |
| Load barrier | colored-pointer test + heal | optimized, metadata in low bits |
| Store barrier | none | yes, for inter-generational marking |
| Stack pointers | colored | colorless, translated at barriers |

### 4.3 Shenandoah: Brooks Forwarding Pointers, SATB Marking, and the Load Reference Barrier

Shenandoah (JEP 189, JDK 12; product feature since JDK 15 via JEP 390) is a region-based concurrent compacting collector that takes the opposite barrier bet from ZGC: instead of stuffing metadata into pointers, it attaches a **Brooks forwarding pointer** — one extra word in every object's header — and leans on **write barriers** plus a **Load Reference Barrier (LRB)** [4][5].

The protocol maintains the *to-space invariant*: **all writes happen to the to-space copy**. A cycle proceeds as [5]:

1. **Concurrent mark** using *snapshot-at-the-beginning* (SATB) write barriers: every overwritten reference is logged, so objects live at mark-start are never lost even if the mutator mutates the graph concurrently.
2. **Concurrent evacuation**: live objects in the collection set are copied to free regions; the Brooks pointer is CAS-updated to the new address. Mutator writes during evacuation go through the write barrier, which resolves the forwarding pointer and writes to to-space.
3. **Concurrent update references**: heap references are rewritten to point directly at to-space copies (this phase is optional/early-updatable via `-XX:ShenandoahUpdateRefsEarly`).

Stop-the-world pauses occur only at brief synchronization points scaling with thread count, not heap size — typically 1–5 ms. The LRB makes the read side sound by normalizing every reference the mutator uses [4]. Unlike ZGC, Shenandoah fully supports compressed oops and 32-bit builds, since its metadata lives in the object, not in pointer bits [4]. The price is **footprint**: one extra word per object raises allocation pressure and cache misses, and under extreme allocation pressure Shenandoah degenerates into a stop-the-world collection (logged as `Degenerated GC`) [4].

### 4.4 Comparative Analysis: Barrier Taxonomy, Overhead Models, and Correctness Arguments

We now compare the three designs along the axes that matter to practitioners.

**Barrier hot-path cost.** C4's LVB and ZGC's load barrier execute on *loads*, the most frequent heap operation; both use a minimal fast path and amortize slow-path work through self-healing (C4) or multi-mapping (ZGC). Shenandoah's write barrier fires on the rarer operation, giving it lower steady-state overhead on read-heavy workloads — but its evacuation-time read path must still resolve Brooks pointers, and the LRB adds load-side cost during update-references [4][5].

**Space overhead.** C4's metadata is per-reference NMT state plus page-table machinery; ZGC's is pointer bits plus multi-mapped virtual address space (virtual, not physical — the same physical pages appear at multiple virtual addresses) [1]; Shenandoah's is one word per object plus SATB queues and mark bitmaps [4]. None is free; the choice is *where* the tax is levied: pointer width, virtual address space, or object footprint.

**Platform constraints.** ZGC originally required 64-bit with no compressed oops (relaxed in generational mode) [1][2]; Shenandoah runs on 32-bit and with compressed oops [4]; C4 shipped first on custom Azul hardware, then on stock x86-64 [6].

**Pause-time structure.** All three confine stop-the-world work to root scanning and phase handshakes, but their concurrent phases differ in mutator interference: ZGC's relocation can proceed with the mutator freely loading through multi-mapped views; Shenandoah's evacuation races the mutator's writes and resolves them via the Brooks word; C4's relocate/remap phases lean on page protection and LVB traps. Each design's *degenerate* behavior differs accordingly: Shenandoah falls back to degenerated/full GC under pressure, ZGC paces allocation with colored-pointer-aware heuristics and may stall mutators, and C4 relies on its always-on concurrent engine keeping up.

> **Theorem:** No concurrent compacting collector can guarantee both (a) zero mutator assistance and (b) bounded pause time under unbounded allocation rate. Every design therefore contains either a pacing/stall mechanism or a degenerate stop-the-world fallback.

*Proof sketch.* Relocation consumes collector bandwidth `R` bytes/s; the mutator allocates at rate `A`. If `A > R` persistently, the relocation set grows without bound and no finite concurrent cycle completes; the collector must either throttle the mutator (allocation pacing/stalls, as in ZGC and C4's back-pressure) or abandon concurrency (Shenandoah's degenerated GC). This is an information-theoretic capacity argument independent of barrier choice. ∎

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### Published pause-time and throughput figures

Reported figures (each under its own methodology; cross-comparison is hazardous):

- **ZGC** targets pause times not exceeding 10 ms, with ongoing work toward a 1 ms goal, independent of heap size from 8 MB to 16 TB [1]; with throughput typically within ~15% of G1 depending on workload [1].
- **Shenandoah** reports typical pauses of 1–5 ms dominated by root scanning, with throughput generally within a few percent of G1 on many workloads and allocation-rate-sensitive regressions where the forwarding word inflates working sets [4][5].
- **C4** claims effectively pauseless operation — no observable GC pauses, only *jitter* in the microsecond range — on heaps up to multiple terabytes, [6][7].

### Formal results established in this thesis

1. **Interception necessity** (§2.1): concurrent relocation requires interception at every operation that can expose a stale reference.
2. **Self-healing bound** (§4.1): each reference slot pays the LVB slow path at most once per phase.
3. **Capacity impossibility** (§4.4): unbounded allocation rate forces either mutator pacing or degenerate fallback.

Together these circumscribe the design space: the three collectors are not three approximations of one ideal but three *Pareto-optimal* points trading barrier frequency against metadata placement against fallback behavior.

## 6 Limitations

**Residual stop-the-world phases.** None of the three collectors is literally pauseless in all phases: root scanning, safepoint handshakes, and phase transitions still stop the world briefly. "Pauseless" is properly read as *pause times independent of heap size*, not *zero pauses* [1][6].

**C4's costs.** The software LVB on stock x86-64 is heavier than on Azul's barrier-aware hardware; the page-remapping engine's demand (remap rates ~100× allocation rate for comfortable operation, per published analysis [6]) consumes CPU and TLB resources that could otherwise serve the application; and C4 is a commercial product tied to Azul's JVM, limiting independent reproduction of results.

**ZGC's costs.** Colored pointers forgo compressed oops in non-generational mode and demand large virtual address reservations; load barriers sit on the hottest possible operation, and generational ZGC's redesign (store barriers, colorless stack pointers) concedes that cost [2]. NUMA awareness helps but does not eliminate cross-node relocation traffic [1].

**Shenandoah's costs.** The Brooks word inflates every object, raising allocation bandwidth and cache pressure; SATB barriers add write-side overhead; degenerated GCs under allocation pressure reintroduce multi-millisecond-to-second pauses precisely when the system is most loaded [4]. The generational mode (JEP 404) mitigates but does not eliminate these [4].

## 7 Conclusion

Pauseless garbage collection is not one invention but a family of protocols for the same hard problem: relocating live objects while the mutator runs, without losing updates, exposing stale pointers, or stalling the application. Azul's C4 showed that a self-healing read barrier plus page-remapping could make multi-terabyte heaps effectively pauseless [6][7]. ZGC showed that embedding GC state in the pointer itself, healed by load barriers over multi-mapped memory, could bring sub-10 ms pauses to mainline OpenJDK with no object-footprint cost [1][2]. Shenandoah showed that Brooks forwarding pointers with write barriers and a load-reference barrier could deliver concurrent compaction with compressed oops and 32-bit support at lower steady-state barrier cost [4][5].

The unifying lesson is the **conservation of complexity**: every design pays for concurrency somewhere — in barrier frequency, metadata placement, virtual-memory machinery, or degenerate-case behavior. The practitioner's decision reduces to workload anatomy: read/write mix, object-size distribution, heap trajectory, platform constraints, and the shape of the latency SLO. Future work lies in machine-checked verification of these protocols, adaptive barrier selection via online profiling, and the hybrid convergence already visible in generational ZGC and Shenandoah [2][4] — collectors that choose interception points dynamically rather than at design time.
