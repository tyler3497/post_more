# Conflict-Driven Clause Learning: Proof Complexity, VSIDS Branching Heuristics, and Resolution Lower Bounds for Modern SAT Solvers

## Abstract

Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL) has transformed Boolean satisfiability (SAT) from a theoretical curiosity into one of the most successful algorithmic paradigms in computer science, with modern solvers routinely deciding formulas with millions of variables and clauses. This thesis presents a unified treatment of the CDCL framework, spanning its algorithmic foundations, the theoretical guarantees and limits imposed by propositional proof complexity, and the empirically dominant heuristics that govern practical performance. We develop the standard CDCL loop — Boolean constraint propagation, conflict analysis via the implication graph, non-chronological backtracking, and restarts — with rigorous attention to the First Unique Implication Point (1UIP) learning scheme, and we formalize the equivalence between CDCL runs and resolution refutations. We analyze branching heuristics from the VSIDS family through the Learning Rate Based (LRB) successor, treating them as adaptive online optimization over proof-rule selection, and we examine the exponential lower bounds for the pigeonhole principle that delimit what even idealized solvers can achieve. The result is both a reference synthesis of the field and a framework for understanding why CDCL succeeds so often despite these worst-case barriers.

## 1 Introduction

The Boolean satisfiability problem — determining whether a propositional formula admits a satisfying assignment — occupies a singular position in computer science. As the canonical NP-complete problem [1], it is simultaneously a theoretical touchstone and, through the engineering miracle of modern SAT solvers, a routine practical task applied to hardware verification, software model checking, planning, cryptanalysis, and constraint optimization. The gap between NP-completeness and practical tractability is bridged by *Conflict-Driven Clause Learning* (CDCL), an algorithmic framework introduced in the GRASP solver by Marques-Silva and Sakallah [2] and refined in Chaff [3] and MiniSat [4], which today underlies essentially every competitive complete SAT solver, including Glucose [5], CaDiCaL, and MapleSAT.

CDCL extends classical DPLL backtracking search [1] with *learning*: every conflict triggers implication-graph analysis deriving a new clause — the *learned clause* — implied by the input formula, pruning entire search regions. As learned clauses accumulate and feed unit propagation, the solver's reasoning power compounds. Modern solvers add restarts, clause database reduction, and branching heuristics such as VSIDS and LRB, all unified theoretically by *proof complexity*.

Proof complexity studies the sizes of proofs in propositional proof systems. A CDCL run that concludes unsatisfiability can be viewed as constructing a resolution refutation [6], and indeed CDCL with sufficient restarts is equivalent to general resolution in proof-theoretic power [7]. This equivalence is a double-edged sword: it explains the strength of clause learning (CDCL polynomially simulates resolution) and simultaneously imports the *lower bounds* of resolution theory — most famously the exponential lower bound for the pigeonhole principle due to Haken [8] — as unconditional barriers on solver performance.

This thesis makes three principal contributions to the synthesis of these ideas:

1. **Algorithmic.** A rigorous exposition of the CDCL loop, the implication graph, 1UIP learning, and backjumping, including the lazy two-watched-literal structure that makes propagation efficient at industrial scale.
2. **Heuristic.** A technical analysis of branching heuristics from VSIDS to LRB, framing branching as online learning over variable selection.
3. **Complexity-theoretic.** A proof-complexity account of CDCL as a resolution engine, including the exponential pigeonhole lower bound and its implications.

> Theorem (informal): CDCL solvers with non-deterministic branching and unrestricted restarts are polynomially equivalent to general resolution as proof systems; consequently, any family of formulas requiring exponential-size resolution refutations requires exponential runtime from such solvers, regardless of heuristic choices.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The SAT Problem and CNF

Let $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ be Boolean variables. A *literal* is a variable $x_i$ or its negation $\neg x_i$. A *clause* is a disjunction of literals; a *CNF formula* $\phi$ is a conjunction of clauses. The SAT problem asks whether there exists an assignment $\alpha : X \to \{0,1\}$ satisfying all clauses of $\phi$. SAT is NP-complete [1]; a CDCL solver is a *complete* decision procedure: it returns SAT with a witness, or UNSAT with (implicitly) a refutation.

### 2.2 DPLL: The Classical Search Procedure

The Davis–Putnam–Logemann–Loveland procedure [1] performs depth-first search over partial assignments:

- **Unit propagation.** If a clause has all but one literal falsified, the remaining literal is forced to true.
- **Decision.** Select an unassigned variable and branch on a value.
- **Chronological backtracking.** On conflict, undo the most recent decision and flip it.

DPLL's weakness is *thrashing*: the same conflict can be rediscovered exponentially many times in different branches, since the reason for a conflict is never recorded. CDCL's central insight is that conflicts are *informative* — they justify learning new constraints.

### 2.3 GRASP and the Birth of Clause Learning

Marques-Silva and Sakallah's GRASP (1996, 1999) introduced conflict analysis and non-chronological backtracking [2]: tracing conflict reasons back through the implication graph, learning the negation of the responsible assignment set as a new clause, and backjumping to the earliest relevant decision level. Bayardo and Schrag's relsat (1997) added relevance-bounded learning [10]; the framework achieved dominance through Chaff [3] (VSIDS, two-watched literals) and MiniSat [4], whose minimal implementation became the substrate of the entire field.

### 2.4 The CDCL Loop

Modern CDCL solvers implement the following loop (following the presentation in [11]):

1. **BCP**: perform Boolean constraint propagation under the current trail; if a conflict arises, go to conflict analysis.
2. **Decide**: if all variables are assigned, return SAT; otherwise select a variable and value via the branching heuristic, increment the decision level, and push onto the trail.
3. **Analyze**: on conflict, construct the implication graph, apply a learning scheme (typically 1UIP) to derive a learned clause, compute the backjump level; if the conflict is at level 0, return UNSAT.
4. **Learn & backtrack**: add the learned clause, backjump to the assertion level, and enqueue the asserting literal.
5. **Restart** (periodically): clear the trail and restart search, retaining all learned clauses.

The loop's components interact: learning changes propagation behavior, branching determines which conflicts are encountered, and restarts reshuffle the search to exploit accumulated knowledge.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Implication Graph

The central structure of conflict analysis is the *implication graph* $G$: a directed acyclic graph whose vertices are the assigned literals of the current trail, annotated with decision levels. Decision literals have in-degree zero; each propagated literal $l$ has incoming edges from the literals of its *reason clause* — the clause that was unit and forced $l$. A conflict adds a special node $\kappa$ with incoming edges from the falsified literals. A *cut* separating decisions from $\kappa$ identifies assignments jointly responsible for the conflict; its conflict side yields a learned clause (negations of the cut's reason-side literals) implied by $\phi$ via resolution [6]. Different cuts correspond to different learning schemes.

### 3.2 Learning Schemes and the First Unique Implication Point

A *Unique Implication Point* (UIP) at decision level $d$ is a literal $u$ at level $d$ that dominates the conflict node: every path from the level-$d$ decision literal to $\kappa$ passes through $u$. The decision literal itself is always a UIP. The *First UIP* (1UIP) is the UIP closest to the conflict — the first one encountered when traversing backwards from $\kappa$.

The 1UIP learning scheme (introduced by Zhang et al. [6]) stops the resolution process at the first UIP and learns the corresponding clause. It is the dominant scheme in practice because it yields *asserting clauses*: clauses with exactly one literal from the current decision level. After backjumping to the second-highest decision level in the learned clause, the 1UIP literal is forced by unit propagation, driving search forward rather than merely recording the failure.

Formally, 1UIP analysis initializes the resolvent with the conflicting clause, then repeatedly resolves with the reason clause of the most recently assigned literal at the current level, stopping when the resolvent contains exactly one literal at that level — the 1UIP literal. The learned clause is this resolvent; the backjump level is the maximum decision level among the remaining literals. This is a sequence of resolution steps, establishing that every learned clause has a resolution derivation from $\phi$ [6] — the bridge to proof complexity.

> Theorem (Zhang et al. [6]): For any conflict, the 1UIP clause is logically implied by the clause database, and the analysis can be performed in time linear in the size of the implication graph.

### 3.3 Lazy Propagation: The Two-Watched-Literal Scheme

Chaff's decisive engineering contribution was the *two-watched-literal* scheme [3]: each clause watches exactly two literals, and propagation visits a clause only when a watched literal is falsified. On backtracking, watches need no updates — the invariant is maintained lazily. This is a major reason modern solvers sustain tens of millions of propagations per second.

```python
def propagate(clause_db, watches, trail, assignment):
    """Two-watched-literal unit propagation (simplified)."""
    while trail:
        lit = trail.popleft()
        falsified = -lit
        for clause in list(watches[falsified]):
            # find a replacement watch
            replacement = clause.find_unwatched_unfalsified(falsified, assignment)
            if replacement is not None:
                watches[falsified].remove(clause)
                watches[replacement].append(clause)
            elif clause.is_unit_under(assignment):
                unit_lit = clause.the_unassigned_literal()
                assignment[unit_lit] = True
                clause.reason = clause          # reason for conflict analysis
                trail.append(unit_lit)
            else:
                return clause                    # conflict: all literals falsified
    return None                                  # no conflict
```

### 3.4 Clause Quality: LBD and Database Reduction

Unbounded learning would slow propagation to a crawl, so solvers periodically delete learned clauses. Glucose's breakthrough was the *Literal Block Distance* (LBD) metric [5]: the number of distinct decision levels among a learned clause's literals. Low-LBD clauses (*glue clauses*, LBD $= 2$) connect few decision levels and are empirically precious; high-LBD clauses are discarded. LBD-based reduction plus aggressive dynamic restarts made Glucose the dominant solver of the 2010s and remains standard in CaDiCaL.

### 3.5 Restarts

Restarts discard the current trail (keeping learned clauses) and begin search anew. While seemingly wasteful, restarts combat the heavy-tailed runtime distribution of backtracking search: they let the solver exploit learned clauses in a fresh decision order. The Luby sequence and Glucose's dynamic restart policy (restart when recent LBD averages degrade relative to the global average) are theoretically motivated [12] and practically essential. Proof-theoretically, restarts with learned-clause retention are what elevate CDCL from tree-like to general resolution power [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 CDCL Runs as Resolution Proofs

The deepest theoretical fact about CDCL is its identification with the resolution proof system. A *resolution refutation* of $\phi$ is a sequence of clauses, each either from $\phi$ or derived by the resolution rule

$$\frac{C \lor x \qquad D \lor \neg x}{C \lor D}$$

ending in the empty clause $\bot$. Pipatsrisawat and Darwiche proved that CDCL with 1UIP learning and non-chronological backtracking *polynomially simulates* general resolution: any resolution refutation of size $s$ can be emulated by a CDCL run of size polynomial in $s$ [7]. Conversely, every learned clause has a resolution derivation, so CDCL runs are resolution proofs in disguise.

This equivalence reframes every solver component in proof-theoretic terms [11]:

| Solver component | Proof-system role |
|---|---|
| Boolean constraint propagation | Iterated unit resolution |
| Clause learning (1UIP) | General resolution rule application |
| Branching heuristic | Selection/sequencing of proof rules |
| Restarts | Re-initialization of proof search |
| Clause deletion | Forgetting proof lines (space-bounded reasoning) |

The practical consequence is profound: heuristic design is *proof-search design*. A branching heuristic selects which resolution steps to attempt next, and the empirical superiority of VSIDS over static orderings reflects its success at discovering short proofs.

### 4.2 VSIDS: Variable State Independent Decaying Sum

Introduced in Chaff [3], VSIDS maintains an *activity* score per variable, bumped whenever the variable appears in a learned clause, with periodic decay (multiplication by $1/\delta$, $\delta > 1$) that emphasizes recent conflicts. Branching selects the unassigned variable of highest activity. Key properties:

- **Conflict locality.** Variables involved in recent conflicts are likely involved in nearby conflicts; VSIDS concentrates search on the "active" region of the formula.
- **Adaptive decay.** Exponential decay implements a recency-weighted frequency count, making the heuristic responsive to the evolving proof state.
- **EVSIDS refinement.** MiniSat's exponential VSIDS bumps by an incrementally growing amount (equivalent to decay) and became the de facto standard [4].

Liang et al. [9] provided the first rigorous explanation of VSIDS's effectiveness, showing it approximates a *community-structure* exploitation: learned clauses tend to connect variables within the same community of the formula's variable-incidence graph, and VSIDS focuses branching inside the community currently under attack.

```python
class EVSIDS:
    def __init__(self, n_vars, decay=0.95):
        self.activity = [0.0] * n_vars
        self.bump_inc = 1.0
        self.decay = decay
    def bump(self, var):
        self.activity[var] += self.bump_inc
    def decay_all(self):
        self.bump_inc /= self.decay          # equivalent to decaying all scores
    def select(self, unassigned):
        return max(unassigned, key=lambda v: self.activity[v])
```

### 4.3 LRB: Learning Rate Based Branching

MapleSAT's LRB heuristic (Liang et al., 2016) [9] reframes branching as a *multi-armed bandit*: each variable is an arm, and assigning it yields a reward measured by the *learning rate* — learned clauses generated per decision involving that variable. LRB maintains an exponential moving average of each variable's learning rate:

$$LRB(v) \leftarrow (1 - \alpha) \cdot LRB(v) + \alpha \cdot r(v)$$

where $r(v)$ is the observed reward. Branching on the variable maximizing expected learning rate directly optimizes the solver's clause-production throughput — the engine of CDCL's compounding reasoning. MapleSAT's LRB won the SAT Competition 2016, and LRB/VSIDS hybrids remain state of the art in Kissat and CaDiCaL variants. The bandit formulation also explains why *phase saving* (remembering each variable's last assigned polarity) synergizes with LRB: it stabilizes the reward signal across restarts.

### 4.4 Resolution Lower Bounds: The Pigeonhole Principle

Haken (1985) proved that the pigeonhole principle formulas $PHP_n^{n+1}$ — $n+1$ pigeons into $n$ holes — require resolution refutations of size $2^{\Omega(n)}$ [8], via a *bottleneck counting* argument: any refutation must pass through many distinct "complex" clauses, since narrow clauses are falsified by partial matchings while wide clauses are too numerous to avoid.

The consequences for CDCL are unconditional and severe [7]:

1. **No heuristic escapes.** Since CDCL runs *are* resolution proofs, no branching heuristic, restart policy, or learning scheme can make a CDCL solver refute $PHP_n^{n+1}$ in subexponential time.
2. **Lower bounds transfer.** Every exponential resolution lower bound — for clique-coloring, Tseitin formulas on expanders, random $k$-SAT — is an exponential lower bound for CDCL.
3. **Separations matter.** Extended resolution (resolution plus the ability to introduce new variables as abbreviations) *does* refute PHP efficiently; this motivates solver extensions such as bounded variable addition and symmetry breaking.

Yet practice belies the pessimism: industrial instances rarely encode pure counting arguments, and CDCL's heuristics excel at the structured, community-rich formulas arising in verification. The lower bounds delineate the boundary of the possible; the heuristics determine how much of the possible is reached.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 Proof Sketch: CDCL Polynomially Simulates Resolution

We sketch the argument of Pipatsrisawat and Darwiche [7]. Given a resolution refutation $\pi$ of $\phi$ of size $s$, each resolution step $C \lor x$, $D \lor \neg x \vdash C \lor D$ is simulated by forcing a conflict on $x$ whose 1UIP analysis derives exactly $C \lor D$; non-chronological backtracking and restarts arrange decisions so each step's implication graph is available on demand. The simulation needs $O(s)$ conflicts at polynomial cost each. Conversely, every learned clause is obtained by iterated resolution of reason clauses [6]. Hence CDCL (with nondeterministic branching and restarts) and general resolution are polynomially equivalent proof systems.

### 5.2 Empirical Landscape

The proof-theoretic equivalence predicts that heuristic quality dominates performance, which SAT Competitions confirm. **MiniSat (2003)** [4] showed a clean 2,000-line implementation with EVSIDS and 1UIP could dominate and became the substrate most solvers descend from. **Glucose (2009)** [5] showed LBD-based clause management and dynamic restarts yield dramatic gains on industrial benchmarks. **MapleSAT (2016)** [9] showed LRB branching could decisively outperform EVSIDS on application instances. **CaDiCaL / Kissat** consolidated these advances with inprocessing and vivification. Ablation studies consistently rank *clause learning* as the dominant component — removing it collapses performance to DPLL levels — with branching heuristics and restarts contributing multiplicative but secondary gains [11].

### 5.3 The Pigeonhole Lower Bound: Proof Sketch

Haken's argument [8] for $PHP_n^{n+1}$: call a clause *complex* if it mentions many pigeons. Narrow clauses are falsified by partial matchings and cannot advance the proof, so any refutation must derive complex clauses — and it needs $2^{\Omega(n)}$ essentially different "bottleneck" clauses to do so. Hence every resolution refutation, and therefore every CDCL run, has size $2^{\Omega(n)}$. Modern strengthenings extend this to random $k$-SAT and to CDCL with bounded learning, showing the barrier survives the practical restrictions (clause deletion, limited learning) of real solvers.

## 6 Limitations, Open Problems, and Future Work

Despite its triumphs, the CDCL paradigm faces fundamental and practical limitations:

1. **Worst-case intractability is unconditional.** The resolution lower bounds are theorems: families like PHP defeat every CDCL solver regardless of heuristic innovation, unless the proof system itself is strengthened.
2. **Heuristic opacity.** VSIDS and LRB work empirically, but we lack a predictive theory of *which* heuristic suits a given instance; instance-aware heuristic selection remains heuristic.
3. **Proof-system ceiling.** CDCL is limited to resolution. Stronger systems — Extended Resolution, Cutting Planes, pseudo-Boolean reasoning — motivate extensions like XOR reasoning in CryptoMiniSat.
4. **Certification and trust.** Solvers emit DRAT/LRAT proofs for UNSAT results; scaling proof logging to inprocessing-heavy solvers like CaDiCaL remains demanding.
5. **Parallelism.** The inherently sequential nature of clause learning resists easy parallelization; portfolio and divide-and-conquer solvers scale imperfectly.
6. **Learning to branch.** Deep-learning branching policies have not yet matched hand-tuned heuristics at scale, though RL approaches to restart and branching selection [9, 12] are narrowing the gap.

> Open Problem: Is there a natural, efficiently checkable proof system that CDCL-like search can simulate and that admits polynomial refutations of the pigeonhole principle while remaining automatizable in practice?

## 7 Conclusion

Conflict-Driven Clause Learning is the rare algorithm that is simultaneously an engineering triumph and a mathematically profound object. Its loop — propagate, decide, analyze, learn, backjump, restart — is simple enough to implement in a weekend, yet governed by the deep theory of propositional proof complexity. The equivalence with resolution explains both its power and its limits: every learned clause is a resolution step, every run a refutation, every heuristic a proof-search strategy, and every exponential lower bound an unconditional wall.

The trajectory from GRASP through Chaff, MiniSat, Glucose, and MapleSAT to CaDiCaL shows a field advancing by *understanding its own proofs*: VSIDS exploits community structure, LBD measures clause utility in proof space, LRB optimizes proof-production rate, and restarts re-initialize proof search. The lower bounds of Haken and successors do not diminish these achievements; they map the territory, telling us exactly where heuristics can help and where only stronger proof systems will do. For the practitioner, the lesson is concrete: choose solvers by their proof-theoretic machinery, log certificates for trust, and expect the hardest formulas to be hard for reasons no tuning will erase. CDCL's unreasonable effectiveness is, in the end, entirely reasonable — it is resolution, mechanized and optimized, running at millions of inferences per second.
