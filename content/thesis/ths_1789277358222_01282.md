# Neural Radiance Fields and 3D Gaussian Splatting: Differentiable Rendering, Volume Integration, and the Geometry of View Synthesis

## Abstract

This article develops the mathematical and algorithmic foundations of modern neural view synthesis, tracing the lineage from classical volume rendering to *Neural Radiance Fields* (NeRF) and, ultimately, to *3D Gaussian Splatting* (3DGS). We derive the volume rendering integral as the continuous limit of alpha compositing, explain how its differentiability enables photometric optimization of continuous scene representations from sparse posed images, and analyze the spectral biases that positional and integrated encodings are designed to correct. We then examine the representational turn from implicit MLPs to explicit primitives: sparse voxel grids, multiresolution hash tables, and anisotropic 3D Gaussians optimized by tile-based differentiable rasterization. We compare convergence behavior, memory–quality Pareto fronts, and the projective geometry underlying EWA splatting, including its exactness limits under wide field-of-view cameras. The result is a unified account of how differentiable rendering recast image formation as an inverse problem, and why the field converged on primitives that trade neural generality for geometric structure and real-time performance.

## 1 Intro

For most of computer graphics history, *rendering* was a forward problem: given a known scene description — geometry, materials, lights — compute an image. The inverse problem — recovering the scene from images — belonged to computer vision, and it relied on explicit geometric reconstructions: sparse point clouds, dense meshes, depth maps. *Neural Radiance Fields* [1] collapsed this division. By formulating image formation as a differentiable process parameterized by a neural network, the method turned a stack of posed photographs into supervision for a continuous volumetric scene function, optimized purely by photometric loss. Novel-view synthesis quality leapt past every prior method, and a research program spanning hundreds of papers was born.

The conceptual core of that leap is not the neural network. It is the *rendering equation of the volume*: the integral

$$C(\mathbf{r}) = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t)\,\sigma(\mathbf{r}(t))\,\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(t),\mathbf{d})\,dt,$$

with transmittance $T(t) = \exp\left(-\int_{t_n}^{t} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s))\,ds\right)$, a continuous model of light transport through a participating medium that had been understood since Kajiya and Von Herzen [6]. What NeRF added was the insight that this integral is *differentiable end to end*: with stratified quadrature it becomes a composition of weighted sums whose gradients flow back into the scene function, so gradient descent can fit a 3D radiance field from nothing but 2D pixel errors.

Three years later, *3D Gaussian Splatting* [2] made a second, equally consequential move. It kept the differentiable volume-rendering objective but discarded the neural network, replacing the implicit field with a set of explicit, anisotropic 3D Gaussians and replacing ray-marched quadrature with a tile-based rasterizer. The result: image quality on par with the best neural-field methods, but at real-time frame rates and with training times measured in minutes rather than hours. This article argues that the trajectory from NeRF to 3DGS is not a sequence of disconnected breakthroughs but a coherent story about the *geometry of differentiable rendering*: what must be integrated, what must be differentiated, and where structure can replace capacity without sacrificing fidelity.

## 2 Background

### The volume rendering integral

Classical emission–absorption volume rendering models a medium that both emits light (with emitted radiance $\mathbf{c}$) and absorbs it (with extinction coefficient $\sigma$). Along a ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}$, the fraction of light surviving from $t_n$ to $t$ is the *transmittance* $T(t)$, and the rendered color is the transmittance-weighted integral of emitted radiance. Discretizing into $N$ segments with sample colors $\mathbf{c}_i$ and opacities $\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)$ yields the familiar front-to-back alpha compositing formula [1]:

$$\hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i\,(1 - \exp(-\sigma_i\delta_i))\,\mathbf{c}_i,\qquad T_i = \exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\sigma_j\delta_j\right).$$

Two properties of this formulation matter enormously. First, the sum is smooth in every parameter — $\sigma_i$, $\mathbf{c}_i$, and even the sample positions — which makes it a differentiable renderer in the weak sense needed for gradient-based optimization. Second, the *expected termination depth* $d(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_i T_i(1-e^{-\sigma_i\delta_i})\,t_i$ falls out of the same weights, so a "surface" can be extracted by thresholding the weight distribution even though no surface was ever modeled. NeRF exploits both: the scene is a pure volume, and geometry emerges as a concentration of density.

### Coordinate networks and spectral bias

A neural radiance field is a function $F_\Theta: (\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \mapsto (\mathbf{c}, \sigma)$, typically an MLP with 8+ layers, evaluated at 5D coordinates. Naive coordinate MLPs fail at high frequencies because of *spectral bias*: gradient descent on standard architectures learns low-frequency functions first and struggles to fit fine detail [7]. The remedy in the original NeRF is *positional encoding*,

$$\gamma(\mathbf{p}) = \left(\sin(2^0\pi\mathbf{p}), \cos(2^0\pi\mathbf{p}), \ldots, \sin(2^{L-1}\pi\mathbf{p}), \cos(2^{L-1}\pi\mathbf{p})\right),$$

a deterministic Fourier-feature lifting that shifts the optimization into a higher-dimensional space where high-frequency target functions are linearly accessible. The choice of encoding is not cosmetic — it sets the *function class* the network can express and the *conditioning* of the optimization, and much subsequent work (hash grids [3], integrated encodings [4], cone tracing [5]) can be read as successive answers to the question of how best to parameterize the input side of a coordinate network.

### Splatting and EWA filtering

Point-based rendering has its own classical lineage. *Splatting* projects 3D primitives onto the image plane as 2D kernels and accumulates them front-to-back — precisely the discrete alpha compositing of the volume integral, with each Gaussian contributing $\alpha_i = o_i\,G_i(\mathbf{x})$. *EWA (elliptical weighted average) splatting* [8] derives the 2D screen-space kernel as the projection of a 3D Gaussian covariance through the Jacobian of the projective mapping: $\Sigma' = J\,W\,\Sigma\,W^T J^T$, where $W$ is the viewing transform and $J$ the local affine approximation of the perspective projection. This is the projective-geometry core of 3DGS: an exact affine treatment would be intractable for rasterization, but the local affine approximation is differentiable and — critically — maps a 3D anisotropic Gaussian to a 2D anisotropic Gaussian, so the entire rendering reduces to sorting and alpha-blending 2D splats.

## 3 Methodology

### From posed images to a radiance field

The NeRF pipeline begins with camera calibration. A sparse set of input images $\{I_k\}$ with known (or estimated, via structure-from-motion) poses defines, for each pixel, a camera ray. For each ray the method samples $N$ points by *stratified sampling* — one sample uniformly at random within each of $N$ evenly spaced bins — evaluates $F_\Theta$ at each, applies positional encoding, and composites with the discrete volume formula. The loss is the squared photometric error $\sum_{\mathbf{r}} \|\hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) - C(\mathbf{r})\|_2^2$ over a batch of rays. A *hierarchical* (coarse-to-fine) scheme trains a coarse network on uniform samples, uses its weight distribution $w_i = T_i(1-e^{-\sigma_i\delta_i})$ to importance-sample a second pass, and supervises both — concentrating computation where the scene actually is. Nothing in this loop references ground-truth geometry; the 3D structure is whatever density field makes the re-rendered pixels match.

> **Theorem:** *The stratified quadrature estimator is unbiased.* If sample $t_i \sim \mathcal{U}[t_n + \tfrac{i-1}{N}(t_f-t_n),\, t_n + \tfrac{i}{N}(t_f-t_n)]$, then $\mathbb{E}[\hat{C}(\mathbf{r})]$ equals the Riemann sum over bin midpoints in expectation, and the estimator converges to the continuous integral as $N \to \infty$ under mild smoothness of $\sigma$ and $\mathbf{c}$.

### Encodings: from Fourier features to hash tables

The original positional encoding is deterministic and global; its bandwidth hyperparameter $L$ trades aliasing against underfitting. *Mip-NeRF* [4] replaced point samples with *conical frustums* and derived the *integrated positional encoding* (IPE): the expected value of $\gamma(\mathbf{x})$ under a multivariate Gaussian approximating the frustum, which admits a closed form $\mathbb{E}[\sin(\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{x})] = \sin(\mathbf{a}^T\boldsymbol{\mu})\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}^T\Sigma\mathbf{a}}$. The exponential attenuation is an analytic anti-aliasing filter — features whose wavelength is smaller than the footprint are automatically suppressed. *Instant-NGP* [3] attacked the cost problem instead: a multiresolution *hash table* of trainable feature vectors, indexed by spatial hash at $L$ resolutions and trilinearly interpolated, feeds a tiny MLP. Hash collisions at fine levels are disambiguated by coarser levels; the whole system trains in seconds on a single GPU because the network shrinks to a few layers while the encoding carries the capacity. *Zip-NeRF* [5] later combined both lines — anti-aliased cone casting with hash-grid features — achieving the best of both at the cost of slower rendering than rasterization-based methods.

### 3D Gaussian Splatting: representation and optimization

3DGS represents the scene as a set of anisotropic Gaussians, each parameterized by a center $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, a covariance $\Sigma = RSS^TR^T$ (rotation $R$ from a quaternion, scale $S$ diagonal), an opacity $o$, and view-dependent color via *spherical harmonics* (SH) coefficients. Optimization interleaves gradient descent with *adaptive density control*: Gaussians with large view-space positional gradients are *densified* — cloned if small (over-reconstruction) or split if large (under-reconstruction) — while near-transparent Gaussians are pruned. Initialization comes from the sparse SfM point cloud, so the representation starts near the true geometry and never queries empty space with an MLP. Rendering projects each Gaussian with the EWA covariance transform, sorts splats into screen-space tiles, and alpha-composites front-to-back in a custom CUDA rasterizer whose backward pass differentiates through sorting, projection, and blending — delivering $\geq$ 30 fps at 1080p [2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The differentiability contract of volume rendering

Differentiable rendering is often presented as a monolith, but NeRF uses only its *weakest* form: gradients of pixel color with respect to scene parameters, not with respect to visibility discontinuities or occlusion boundaries. Because the transmittance integral is smooth almost everywhere in $(\sigma, \mathbf{c})$, the photometric loss landscape is tractable — there are no step functions to soften, no Dirac deltas to reparameterize. This is why the original formulation succeeds where differentiable mesh rasterizers of the same era struggled: it never commits to a topology. The cost is *geometric ambiguity*: infinitely many density fields re-render the same training views (the classic shape–radiance ambiguity), and the method's success depends on the inductive biases of the MLP and the encoding to select smooth, plausible solutions.

### 4.2 Why conical frustums beat point samples

Point sampling a volume with a high-bandwidth encoding is a category error: a pixel integrates radiance over a footprint, not a ray. Mip-NeRF's [4] conical frustum casting makes the sample volume explicit and the IPE attenuation factor $e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}^T\Sigma\mathbf{a}}$ implements scale-aware band-limiting. The practical effect is the elimination of the "shimmering" aliasing artifacts that plague single-scale NeRF when the camera moves: distant geometry, whose footprint spans many encoding wavelengths, is represented by the low-pass-filtered expectation rather than by aliased point samples. Zip-NeRF [5] extended this to multisampled cones with downweighted distant samples, closing the remaining quality gap to brute-force supersampling at a fraction of the cost. The lesson generalizes: *any* coordinate-based renderer must match the bandwidth of its encoding to the scale of its integration footprint, or pay in aliasing.

### 4.3 Hash grids as learned, sparse function bases

Instant-NGP's [3] multiresolution hash encoding can be understood as a *sparse, learned radial-basis expansion*: each level stores feature vectors at grid vertices (hashed when the grid outgrows the table), and interpolation yields a piecewise-multilinear function per level. Concatenating $L$ levels gives the MLP a multi-scale dictionary of local features, and because only occupied regions receive gradient updates, capacity concentrates on surfaces automatically. The hash collisions that seem like a defect are load-bearing: they force the network to resolve ambiguity through multi-scale consensus, and empirically they regularize rather than corrupt. Training a NeRF-class model in ~5 seconds (vs. ~hours for the original) did not come from a better optimizer but from moving representational capacity *out* of the MLP and *into* a spatially indexed memory — a move 3DGS would push to its logical extreme by deleting the MLP entirely.

### 4.4 The projective geometry of splatting, and where it breaks

The EWA projection $\Sigma' = J W \Sigma W^T J^T$ is a *local* affine approximation of a projective map, exact only at the Gaussian's center. Under narrow fields of view the approximation is excellent; under wide-FoV or fisheye cameras, nonlinear distortion bends the true projected footprint away from any 2D Gaussian, and splatting-based methods degrade [2]. Recent work on exact ray–Gaussian intersection (evaluating the 3D Gaussian along the ray rather than projecting it) removes the approximation at the cost of ray-marching-style computation, partially surrendering the rasterization speedup. There is a genuine Pareto frontier here, not a free lunch: *affine splatting buys speed by assuming local projectivity*, and the assumption's failure mode is quantifiable in terms of the projective Jacobian's curvature across each splat's extent.

### 4.5 Convergence, capacity, and the memory–quality frontier

Empirically, the field's progression traces a clear frontier. Original NeRF [1]: hours of training, ~30 s/frame rendering, tens of MB. Instant-NGP [3]: seconds to minutes, tens of ms/frame, tens of MB. 3DGS [2]: ~30–50 min for peak quality, real-time rendering, but hundreds of MB to GBs of Gaussians for large scenes — explicit primitives are memory-hungry because redundancy is the price of avoiding the MLP. Zip-NeRF [5] sits at the quality extreme with slow rendering. No method dominates on all axes; the right choice depends on whether the binding constraint is training time, frame rate, memory, or geometric editability — and the trend is unmistakable: *structure (grids, hashes, Gaussians) consistently beats raw neural capacity per unit of compute*.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The quantitative record supports the narrative above. On the standard forward-facing and 360° benchmark scenes, Mip-NeRF [4] reduced mean error by 17% relative to NeRF — and by 60% on a challenging multiscale variant — while using half the parameters and training 7% faster, directly attributable to anti-aliased cone casting. Instant-NGP [3] matched NeRF's PSNR after ~5 seconds of training where the original required on the order of $10^5$ iterations (hours), a speedup of several orders of magnitude from encoding design alone. 3DGS [2] reported PSNR slightly *above* Mip-NeRF360 — then the quality leader — while rendering at $\geq$ 30 fps at 1080p, a regime no prior method had reached for unbounded scenes.

> **Theorem:** *Volume rendering is consistent under refinement.* As the number of quadrature samples $N \to \infty$ and the maximum bin width $\max_i \delta_i \to 0$, the discrete compositing sum converges to the continuous volume integral for continuous $\sigma$ and $\mathbf{c}$, and the gradient of the discrete sum converges to the gradient of the integral wherever the latter exists.

A second, subtler empirical regularity concerns *floaters* — spurious semi-transparent density in free space, the characteristic failure mode of NeRF-like optimization. Floaters are the visible symptom of the shape–radiance ambiguity: the optimizer explains view-dependent effects or sparse-view gaps with fog rather than geometry. The mitigations form a pattern: distortion losses that concentrate the weight distribution [5], stronger density-control heuristics [2], or explicit emptiness priors. All of them bias the solution toward *surfaces*, confirming that the volume formulation is a means to an end — differentiable optimization — rather than a claim that scenes are fog.

## 6 Limitations

Every method surveyed here inherits the same fundamental weakness: *it inverts images, not physics*. The photometric loss cannot distinguish a mirror from a painted texture, a shadow from albedo variation, or a floater from fog — the shape–radiance ambiguity is information-theoretic, not algorithmic, and no encoding or primitive choice removes it. Sparse-view reconstruction remains brittle; few-shot NeRF variants rely on depth priors or diffusion-model hallucination precisely because the inverse problem is underdetermined.

Second, *dynamic and unbounded scenes* strain all representations. Deforming Gaussians or time-conditioned fields handle small motions, but topology changes, long-range lighting variation, and city-scale captures still defeat clean solutions — 3DGS's memory footprint grows roughly with scene complexity, and pruning heuristics tuned for object-scale captures misbehave outdoors. Third, *relighting* is out of scope: baked spherical harmonics and view-dependent MLPs model outgoing radiance under fixed illumination, so none of these methods decomposes appearance into materials and lights; true inverse rendering (reflectance, visibility, incident light) remains a separate, harder problem.

Finally, the differentiability itself is a leash: gradients through hundreds of composited samples are well-behaved for photometric loss but provide no signal for discrete decisions — which primitive exists, what the topology is — so every method leans on heuristics (densification, pruning, proposal networks) whose hyperparameters are the field's least principled component.

## 7 Conclusion

The arc from NeRF to 3D Gaussian Splatting is best read as a progressive answer to one question: *what is the minimal machinery that makes image formation differentiable and invertible?* The first answer — a neural network plus the volume rendering integral [1] — proved that photometric gradients alone suffice to recover continuous 3D structure. The refinements — integrated encodings [4], hash tables [3], multisampled cones [5] — showed that the encoding, not the network depth, governs the quality–compute tradeoff. The final answer, so far — explicit anisotropic Gaussians with EWA rasterization [2] — showed that the network itself was optional: what matters is a primitive whose projection is differentiable and whose accumulation matches the volume integral.

What endures across all of these is the *volume rendering integral* as the interface between 3D representation and 2D supervision. Whether the field is stored in weights, hash tables, or Gaussian parameters, optimization flows through the same transmittance-weighted accumulation, and geometry emerges from the same concentration of opacity. The field's future — exact ray–Gaussian formulations, relightable primitives, generative priors over radiance fields — will likely keep this interface while continuing to trade neural generality for geometric structure. Differentiable rendering began as a trick for fitting volumes to photographs; it has become the substrate on which 3D vision is now built.
