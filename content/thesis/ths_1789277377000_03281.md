# Algebraic Effects and Handlers: From Row Polymorphism to Scoped Effects in Koka, Eff, and Frank

## Abstract

Algebraic effects and their handlers are among the most consequential developments in the theory of computational effects since Moggi's monadic metalanguage [2]. By treating effectful operations as the signature of an algebraic theory and handlers as its models, the framework of Plotkin and Power [3] and Plotkin and Pretnar [1] separates the *syntax* of effects from their *semantics*. Practical adoption then demanded static discipline: Koka's row-polymorphic effect system [4] tracks effects as extensible rows of labels with Hindley–Milner-style inference, while Eff [5] and Frank [6] made handlers a first-class primitive — culminating in Frank's operator design, where functional abstraction itself handles commands. Most recently, the field has confronted the boundary of algebraicity: scoped effects such as exception catching and local state delimit scopes whose continuations resist uniform handling, prompting the λ_sc calculus [7], functorial-algebra semantics [8], and a higher-order effects framework [9] generalizing algebraic, scoped, parallel, and latent effects under one interpreter. This thesis surveys that trajectory, from the free-model universal property through evidence-passing compilation to the two-level architecture of modern effect systems.

## 1 Intro

Computational effects — state, exceptions, nondeterminism, I/O, concurrency — were long handled by ad hoc, incompatible mechanisms: monad transformer stacks with quadratic composition cost and rigid ordering, or unchecked effect systems whose signatures lied by omission. The algebraic theory of effects asked instead: what if an effectful operation is exactly an operation symbol in an algebraic signature, and *handling* it is exactly interpreting that signature in a model?

Plotkin and Power's insight [3] was that the free model of an equational theory of effects induces the computational monad for that effect. Plotkin and Pretnar completed the picture: each handler yields a model of the theory, and each handling construct yields the homomorphism induced by the universal property of the free model [1]. A program denotes an element of the free algebra — a tree of operation invocations with values at the leaves — and a handler folds that tree. Because the continuation at each operation site can be *resumed*, possibly multiple times or not at all, coroutines, generators, backtracking search, and cooperative concurrency become libraries rather than language primitives.

This thesis traces three generations. The first is foundational: free models, handlers as homomorphisms, and the languages Eff and Koka that made effects *programmable*. The second is typological: Koka answered static tracking with row-polymorphic effect types and duplicate labels [4]; Frank eliminated the handling construct, generalizing function application into operator application under an *ambient ability* [6]. The third is expressive: scoped effects break algebraicity, and the field has responded with the $\lambda_{sc}$ calculus [7], functorial-algebra semantics [8], and a generic higher-order framework [9] subsuming scoped, parallel, and latent effects.

> **Thesis claim:** Row-polymorphic effect typing and handler-based interpretation jointly solve the *modularity* problem of effects, but full generality requires abandoning strict algebraicity: scoped operations demand either built-in scoping constructs with explicit forwarding or parameterized theories, and the mature position is a two-level design — algebraic operations for the sequential core, scoped operations for delimitation — compiled through evidence passing with tail-resumption optimization.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Monads and their discontents

Moggi's computational lambda-calculus [2] showed that notions of computation correspond to strong monads on a cartesian closed category: $T A$ is the type of computations returning $A$. The monadic approach dominated effectful functional programming, but composition of monads is not canonical: combining state and exceptions requires choosing a transformer order, and the resulting semantics is *fixed* by that choice. Handlers invert this relationship. Where a monad bakes the interpretation into the type, algebraic effects postpone interpretation to the handler site, so the same program can be run under many semantics — state as a handler, exceptions as a handler, logging as a handler — without rewriting the program.

### 2.2 Algebraic theories of effects

An *effect signature* $\Sigma$ is a set of operation symbols $\mathsf{op} : P \rightsquigarrow A$, each with a parameter type $P$ and an arity (result) type $A$. A computation is then a tree:

$$\mathsf{op}(p; x.\, k(x))$$

where $p : P$ is the parameter and $k : A \to \text{Comp}$ is the continuation. The classic examples:

| Effect | Operations | Signature |
|---|---|---|
| Exceptions | `raise` | $\mathsf{raise} : E \rightsquigarrow 0$ |
| Mutable state | `get`, `put` | $\mathsf{get} : 1 \rightsquigarrow S$, $\mathsf{put} : S \rightsquigarrow 1$ |
| Nondeterminism | `choose`, `fail` | $\mathsf{choose} : 1 \rightsquigarrow \mathsf{Bool}$, $\mathsf{fail} : 1 \rightsquigarrow 0$ |
| Input/output | `print`, `read` | $\mathsf{print} : \mathsf{String} \rightsquigarrow 1$, $\mathsf{read} : 1 \rightsquigarrow \mathsf{String}$ |

An *equational theory* adds axioms, e.g. $\mathsf{get}; \mathsf{put}(s) = \mathsf{return}\,()$. The free model over values $X$ is the initial algebra — operation trees quotiented by the equations — and that free model is a monad in $X$, recovering Moggi's picture as a special case.

### 2.3 Handlers as homomorphisms

A handler for $\Sigma$ with result type $B$ provides, for each $\mathsf{op} : P \rightsquigarrow A$, a clause

$$\mathsf{op}(p; k) \mapsto M_{\mathsf{op}}$$

where $k : A \to B$ is the *resumed continuation*, already handled, plus a `return` clause mapping values to $B$. Plotkin and Pretnar's central theorem [1]:

> **Theorem:** A handler for effect theory $\mathcal{T}$ with result type $B$ determines a model of $\mathcal{T}$ with carrier $B$; the handling construct $\mathbf{handle}\, M \,\mathbf{with}\, H$ denotes the unique homomorphism from the free model (the computation tree of $M$) to that model.

Because the homomorphism is applied recursively, operations *inside* continuations are handled by the same handler — the deep-handler semantics — which is what makes handlers compose where monad transformers tangle.

### 2.4 The languages: Eff, Koka, Frank

- **Eff** (Bauer & Pretnar [5]) was the first language built around algebraic effects and handlers, showing handlers could express backtracking, cooperative multithreading, and logic programming as user code.
- **Koka** (Leijen [4]) married algebraic effects to Hindley–Milner-style inference with *row-polymorphic* effect types: function arrows carry rows $\langle l_1, \dots, l_n \mid \mu \rangle$ of effect labels.
- **Frank** (Lindley, McBride & McLaughlin [6]) eliminated the handling construct: every *operator* is a handler, ordinary functions are operators handling nothing, and effect polymorphism flows from an *ambient ability*.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a *comparative formal survey with constructive demonstrations*. Its method has three parts:

1. **Primary-source reconstruction.** Every major claim is anchored in the original papers: the Plotkin–Pretnar handling calculus [1], Plotkin–Power algebraic operations [3], Leijen's Koka system [4], Bauer–Pretnar's Eff [5], the Frank design [6], Wu–Schrijvers–Hinze on scoped effects, the $\lambda_{sc}$ calculus [7], Yang et al.'s functorial algebras [8], and the higher-order framework [9].
2. **Executable micro-models.** Core mechanisms — deep handlers, row unification, scoped forwarding — are exhibited in Haskell and in Koka-like pseudocode, so that operational claims (e.g. the handler homomorphism law, tail-resumption) are checkable rather than asserted.
3. **Comparative analysis.** Koka, Eff, and Frank are compared along fixed axes: how effects are *declared*, how they are *tracked* in types, how handlers are *invoked*, how effect polymorphism is *achieved*, and how the design compiles to efficient code.

The scope is delimited deliberately: we treat only *typed* handler calculi and their implementations; untyped or dynamically-scoped systems (e.g. early delimited-continuation libraries) appear only as foils. Quantitative performance evaluation is represented through the published compilation strategies — evidence passing with tail-resume optimization in Koka — rather than fresh benchmarks, since handler performance is dominated by implementation choices already analyzed in the literature.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The handler homomorphism, concretely

Consider nondeterminism with a collecting handler in a Haskell-flavoured free monad:

```haskell
data Eff (sig :: [Type -> Type]) a where
  Pure :: a -> Eff sig a
  Op   :: Member e sig => e x -> (x -> Eff sig a) -> Eff sig a

data Choose x where
  Choose :: Choose Bool

handleAll :: Eff '[Choose] a -> [a]
handleAll (Pure x)          = [x]
handleAll (Op Choose k)     = handleAll (k True) ++ handleAll (k False)
```

The handler clause for `Choose` invokes the continuation *twice* — multi-shot resumption — and concatenates the results. This is the homomorphism property made visible: the handler replaces each operation node with its clause and recursively handles the subtrees. Deep handlers guarantee that `Op` nodes *inside* `k True` are again routed to `handleAll`, which is precisely why nested effects compose without explicit lifting [1][5].

A fundamental algebraic law governs this folding:

> **Theorem (Handler fusion):** For handlers $H_1, H_2$ where $H_2$'s clauses are homomorphic, $\mathbf{handle}\,(\mathbf{handle}\, M \,\mathbf{with}\, H_1)\,\mathbf{with}\, H_2 = \mathbf{handle}\, M \,\mathbf{with}\, (H_2 \circ H_1)$ for the appropriately composed handler. Fusion of this form underpins handler optimizations and the reasoning principles of [8].

### 4.2 Row polymorphism: Koka's answer to effect tracking

Koka's effect rows have the grammar

$$\epsilon ::= \langle\rangle \mid \mu \mid \langle l \mid \epsilon \rangle$$

where $l$ ranges over effect labels (`exn`, `div`, `st⟨h⟩`, `io`, …) and $\mu$ is a row variable. Rows are identified up to permutation of labels (Figure 2 of [4]). The typing judgment $\Gamma \vdash e : \tau \mid \epsilon$ reads "under $\Gamma$, $e$ has type $\tau$ and may perform effects $\epsilon$". Higher-order functions generalize over the row tail:

```text
fun map(xs : list<a>, f : (a) -> e b) : e list<b> { ... }
```

Here `e` is an effect variable; instantiating `map` with a pure function yields a pure `map`, while instantiating it with an exception-raising function yields an exceptionful one — no lifting, no transformers. Koka permits *duplicate labels* in rows, which is the key to precise typing of effect *elimination*: handling an exception removes exactly one `exn` label from the row, and row unification during inference ensures the residual row is principal [4].

The semantic payoff is that rows are not merely syntactic: Leijen proves absence of `exn` implies no unhandled exception, and `st⟨h⟩` heap labels indexed by a heap variable generalize Haskell's `runST`, encapsulating local state observably purely while composing with let-polymorphism without a value restriction [4].

Compilation is type-directed: row-typed programs compile by *evidence passing*, each handler contributing an evidence vector through which operations dispatch, like type-class dictionaries [4]; tail-position resumption — the common case for state and exception handlers — is optimized to skip continuation capture entirely, recovering performance competitive with hand-written state passing.

### 4.3 Frank: handlers without a handling construct

Frank's radical premise [6] is that the handling construct is redundant: *abstraction itself* can be the handler. A Frank operator has the shape

```text
map : {<Choose>X -> Y} -> List X -> List Y
map f nil         = nil
map f (x :: xs)   = f x :: map f xs
```

where `{<Choose>X -> Y}` is a suspended computation *requesting the ability* `<Choose>`, and the operator's clauses pattern-match on both values and commands. Because every operator handles its arguments' commands, Frank supports *multihandlers*: one operator interprets commands from several sources simultaneously, in direct style, with no nesting of `handle...with` blocks [6].

Frank's effect polymorphism is equally distinctive. Rather than quantifying over effect variables, Frank propagates an *ambient ability* inward: the ability available at a program point flows into subterms, and operators adapt polymorphically to whatever is ambient — effect polymorphism inferred the way Hindley–Milner infers type polymorphism. Core Frank elaborates operators into functions, case expressions, and *unary* handlers over a call-by-push-value backbone, for which Lindley et al. prove type soundness via small-step operational semantics [6].

The three languages thus occupy distinct points in the design space:

| | Eff [5] | Koka [4] | Frank [6] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Effect declaration | `effect` types with operations | built-in + user labels in rows | interfaces of commands |
| Tracking | effect sets in types | row-polymorphic rows $\langle l_1,\dots \mid \mu\rangle$ | bidirectional, ambient ability |
| Handling syntax | `handler` / `with ... handle` | `handle` / `mask` | operators (no separate construct) |
| Effect polymorphism | explicit quantification | row variables, duplicate labels | ambient ability propagation |
| Evaluation | strict, handlers as values | strict, evidence passing | strict, CBPV core |

### 4.4 Scoped effects: where algebraicity breaks

Not every useful operation is algebraic. Consider exception *catching*:

```text
catch : M a -> (E -> M a) -> M a
```

`catch` delimits a *scope*: its first argument is the scoped computation (in-scope), and the continuation after `catch` returns is the out-of-scope part. Algebraicity demands that operations commute with sequencing — $\mathsf{op}(p; x. k_1(x) \mathbin{>\!\!>\!=} k_2) = \mathsf{op}(p; x. k_1(x)) \mathbin{>\!\!>\!=} k_2$ — but `catch` inspects and delimits its scoped computation, so it cannot satisfy this law. Wu, Schrijvers, and Hinze showed that such *scoped effects* — also including local state (`local`), parallel composition, and pruning in nondeterminism — need dedicated treatment: their continuations have *two* parts, and handlers must receive both [10].

Early approaches encoded scoped operations as handlers, but at the cost of modularity: the encoding hard-wires one interaction semantics and prevents the scoped operation from being forwarded through handlers that do not know about it [7]. The $\lambda_{sc}$ calculus of van den Berg et al. [7] puts scoped effects on the same formal footing as algebraic ones by extending Eff-style syntax with:

1. **Scoped operation symbols** $\mathsf{sc} : P \rightsquigarrow A$ whose typing distinguishes the scoped computation from the continuation;
2. **Polymorphic handlers** (via let-polymorphism and $F_\omega$-style type operators), required because the in-scope and out-of-scope parts may have different types;
3. **Explicit forwarding clauses**, since scoped operations — unlike algebraic ones — cannot be forwarded uniformly: each handler must say how unknown scoped operations interact with its own interpretation.

With these ingredients $\lambda_{sc}$ proves the expected metatheory: progress, preservation, and a type-and-effect inference algorithm extending Koka-style row polymorphism to rows that may contain scoped labels [7].

Categorically, Yang et al. [8] gave scoped effects a principled semantics via an adjunction between locally finitely presentable categories and categories of *functorial algebras*, equivalent in expressivity to both the indexed model of Wu et al. and simulation by algebraic handlers — but strictly more convenient to implement. The model *automatically induces fusion laws* for scoped handlers, analogues of the algebraic fusion theorem above, enabling equational reasoning and optimization [8].

### 4.5 Higher-order effects: one framework to bind them

The most recent development generalizes beyond scoped effects entirely. Yang and Wu's framework [9] observes that algebraic, scoped, *parallel* (operations forking subcomputations), and *latent* (operations deferring computations) effects are all *higher-order* effects: operations whose signatures involve computations, not just values. A generic free monad parameterized by a higher-order signature functor, with a single fold-like interpreter, specializes to each class — putting previously ad hoc monad definitions, notably for parallel and bracketing effects, on formal footing for the first time [9].

Complementarily, recent work has translated scoped effects into *parameterized algebraic theories*, where operations carry an additional parameter of computations, yielding an equational proof system for scoped effects and alternative characterizations of their monads in terms of generators and equations [10]. The emerging consensus is a *two-level* architecture: algebraic operations for the sequential core — handled by the classical homomorphism machinery — plus scoped/higher-order operations for delimitation and control, with explicit forwarding mediating between levels.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The evidence for the thesis claim is distributed across theorems, implementations, and adoption data:

1. **The Plotkin–Pretnar representation theorem** [1] establishes that *every* handler denotes a model homomorphism, covering exceptions, state, nondeterminism, I/O, CCS relabelling/restriction, timeout, and rollback in a single construct.
2. **Koka's metatheory** [4] proves type soundness and the semantic content of rows (absence of `exn` implies no unhandled exception; `run` encapsulates `st⟨h⟩` observably purely), and its evidence-passing compiler with tail-resume optimization demonstrates that handler abstraction need not cost asymptotic performance.
3. **Frank's soundness result** [6] — a small-step operational semantics for Core Frank with a type-soundness proof — shows that eliminating the handling construct does not weaken the theory; multihandlers are elaborated to unary handlers, so Frank's expressiveness reduces to the classical one while its surface ergonomics improve.
4. **$\lambda_{sc}$'s metatheory** [7] proves progress and preservation for a calculus with *both* algebraic and scoped operations, plus a working inference algorithm — the first operational account with full modular composition of scoped handlers, directly addressing the ad hoc forwarding in Haskell libraries (`eff`, `polysemy`, `fused-effects`) identified by the authors.
5. **Equivalence results** [8] show the functorial-algebra model, the indexed model, and simulation-by-algebraic-handlers have equal expressivity for scoped operations, so the choice among them is an engineering decision, not a semantic gamble.
6. **Industrial adoption.** OCaml 5 (2022) shipped effect handlers as a production feature, and the Eio library rebuilt concurrent I/O in direct style on top of them — large-scale validation that handlers beat monadic encodings ergonomically in real systems.

```tla+
---- MODULE HandlerFusion ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Sequences
CONSTANT Op
VARIABLE tree, handler
(* Abstract model: handling folds an operation tree via the clause map. *)
Fold(t, h) == CASE t = <<"return", x>> -> h["return"][x]
               [] t = <<"op", p, k>>   -> h["op"][p, LAMBDA x: Fold(k[x], h)]
               [] OTHER                -> t
Fusion == \A t, h1, h2 : Fold(Fold(t, h1), h2) = Fold(t, Compose(h2, h1))
====
```

The TLA⁺ sketch states handler fusion as an invariant: folding a tree through two handlers sequentially equals folding through their composition — the property justifying fusion of nested `handle` blocks at compile time.

## 6 Limitations

The framework is not without genuine weaknesses, and intellectual honesty requires stating them:

- **Performance predictability.** Evidence passing with tail-resume optimization closes much of the gap [4], but multi-shot resumption (backtracking search, probabilistic programming) still allocates continuations proportional to search depth, with a less predictable cost model than explicit state passing.
- **Reasoning about resumption.** Multi-shot continuations complicate the "handler as fold" intuition when a continuation is invoked under a *different* handler context. Shallow handlers recover local reasoning but lose automatic re-handling; the deep/shallow trade-off has no universally accepted resolution.
- **Scoped forwarding burden.** $\lambda_{sc}$'s explicit forwarding clauses [7] restore modularity but tax every handler author with forwarding logic for uninterpreted scoped operations — untested at production scale, since no production language implements $\lambda_{sc}$ yet.
- **Effect inference brittleness.** Row-polymorphic inference is principal in the core calculus [4], but GADTs, higher-rank types, and first-class handlers can produce confusing errors; effect-error ergonomics remain an open human-factors problem.
- **Semantic pluralism as a cost.** Handlers' strength — one program, many interpretations — is also a verification burden: correctness must quantify over the handlers a program may run under, and modular verification logics for handlers are still maturing.

---

## 7 Conclusion

From Plotkin and Power's algebraic operations [3] through Plotkin and Pretnar's handlers-as-homomorphisms [1], the field established that computational effects are best understood as *syntax awaiting interpretation*. Eff [5] proved the idea programmable; Koka [4] proved it statically disciplined, with row polymorphism turning effect tracking into a principal, inferable discipline and evidence passing turning it into efficient code; Frank [6] proved the handling construct itself dispensable, dissolving handlers into generalized abstraction under an ambient ability. The frontier has now moved past algebraicity: scoped effects [10][7][8] and the higher-order framework [9] acknowledge that delimitation, parallelism, and latency need operations over computations, not just values — and supply the calculi, the categorical semantics, and the forwarding discipline to handle them modularly.

The two-level picture — algebraic core plus scoped/higher-order delimitation, typed by rows, compiled by evidence passing — is the stable end-state toward which the literature is converging. What remains is engineering: production-quality inference error messages, predictable cost models for multi-shot resumption, and the first industrial $\lambda_{sc}$-scale language. The theory, at least, is ready.
