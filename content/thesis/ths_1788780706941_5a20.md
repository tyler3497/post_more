# Programmable Data Planes: P4 Match-Action Pipelines, PSA Architectures, and In-Band Telemetry

## Abstract

Software-defined networking initially confined programmability to the control plane, leaving the forwarding path a fixed-function black box defined by slowly evolving standards such as OpenFlow. The **P4 language** (Programming Protocol-independent Packet Processors) inverts this assumption: it provides a domain-specific, declarative programming language for describing *how* a packet processor parses, matches, transforms, and deparses packets at line rate [1]. This thesis develops the complete theory and practice of programmable data planes centered on P4_16, the **match-action pipeline abstraction** inherited from Protocol Independent Switch Architecture (PISA), and the **Portable Switch Architecture (PSA)** and **Portable NIC Architecture (PNA)** target specifications. We analyze the programmable parser as a finite-state machine, the structure of match-action stages under per-stage resource constraints (TCAM, SRAM, PHV, ALU), the semantics of extern objects and functions (counters, meters, registers, hash units, digests), and the control-plane contract through P4Runtime. We then give a formal treatment of **In-Band Network Telemetry (INT)** in its three canonical modes — INT-XD, INT-MX, and INT-MD — including the INT shim and metadata header formats, postcard versus embedded-export trade-offs, and the telemetry report specification. Empirical sections compare programmable ASICs (Barefoot Tofino), FPGA and NPU implementations, and software targets (BMv2, eBPF, DPDK) on latency, throughput, and resource mapping. We close with production applications — SmartNIC offload and 5G User Plane Function (UPF) acceleration — and a candid treatment of limitations: stateful cross-packet consistency, limited arithmetic expressiveness, compiler mapping complexity, and the industrial discontinuation of the Tofino roadmap [13].

## 1 Introduction

The history of packet forwarding hardware is a history of *who gets to decide what a packet means*. In fixed-function switches, that decision was made at the foundry: a merchant-silicon pipeline understood Ethernet, IPv4, IPv6, MPLS, and VXLAN because its parser and its match tables were etched in hardware months before deployment. Adding a new encapsulation — NVGRE in 2011, Geneve in 2014 — required a new chip revision or a standards-battle over OpenFlow table type patterns that grew from 12 match fields in OpenFlow 1.0 to 41 by version 1.4 [1]. Each new protocol imposed a years-long standardization cycle on an industry that needed weeks.

Bosshart et al. identified the structural flaw: OpenFlow standardized the *interface* to forwarding behavior while freezing the *behavior itself* [1]. The P4 language responds by standardizing the interface to *programming* the forwarding behavior. P4 is a declarative, protocol-independent language in which the operator declares header formats, a programmable parser, match-action tables, actions, and control flow; a target-specific compiler then maps this description onto the resources of a particular device — a programmable ASIC, an FPGA, an NPU, or a software switch [2].

> **Theorem: (Protocol Independence).** Any packet-processing behavior expressible as a finite-state parse graph followed by a bounded sequence of exact, ternary, longest-prefix, or range matches and bounded header transformations is implementable in P4_16 without reference to any named protocol, subject only to the resource limits of the target architecture.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes background: the PISA abstract machine, P4_14 versus P4_16, and the architecture/program separation. Section 3 details our methodology for analyzing these systems. Section 4 — the deep dive — covers (i) the programmable parser and deparser, (ii) match-action pipelines and per-stage resources, (iii) PSA and PNA target architectures, (iv) extern functions and objects, and (v) In-Band Network Telemetry in depth. Section 5 presents empirical evidence from hardware and software targets. Section 6 states limitations honestly, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### The PISA abstract machine

The conceptual hardware model underlying all P4 targets is the **Protocol Independent Switch Architecture (PISA)**, introduced alongside the RMT (Reconfigurable Match Tables) work of Bosshart et al. [8]. A PISA device consists of three programmable stages arranged in series:

1. A **programmable parser** that extracts programmer-defined header fields into a typed packet header vector (PHV);
2. A **match-action pipeline** of *N* stages (typically 12–32 in commercial ASICs), each containing match memory (SRAM/TCAM), action ALUs, and stateful memory; and
3. A **programmable deparser** that serializes the (possibly modified) headers back into a byte stream.

Packets flow forward through the pipeline one stage per clock cycle; there is no backward information flow, which is the fundamental constraint that makes line-rate processing tractable and that simultaneously restricts expressiveness — a fact we return to in Section 6.

### From P4_14 to P4_16

The original P4_14 language (2014) described parsers, tables, actions, and control flow, but embedded stateful primitives (counters, meters, registers) directly in the language core [1]. **P4_16** restructured the language around a clean separation of concerns [2][9]:

- The *language core* defines types, expressions, parsers, controls, tables, actions, and functions — and nothing target-specific.
- An *architecture* (PSA, PNA, V1Model, TNA) defines the programmable blocks, intrinsic metadata, and package instantiation.
- *Externs* — extern objects and extern functions — provide the escape hatch to target-specific capabilities: counters, meters, registers, hash units, checksums, digests, and accelerators [7][10].

In P4_16, all stateful elements must be explicitly instantiated at compile time; tables are declared within control scope; and parser states form the only permitted out-of-order declarations. The current reference is P4_16 v1.2.x, with the PSA specification at v1.1–v1.2 [6][7].

### Architectures: PSA, PNA, and the ecosystem

The **Portable Switch Architecture (PSA)** is the industry-standard target model: six programmable blocks (IngressParser, Ingress, IngressDeparser, EgressParser, Egress, EgressDeparser) plus a fixed replication engine and buffer/queuing engine between them, assembled in the `PSA_Switch` package [7]. The **Portable NIC Architecture (PNA)** adapts this model to NICs and SmartNICs: three programmable blocks (`MainParser`, `MainControl`, `MainDeparser`), host and network ports, and data-plane-managed table entries with idle timeouts — designed for stateful offloads such as connection tracking [10]. Vendor targets extend the family: Intel Tofino's TNA (Tofino Native Architecture), AMD/Xilinx's Vitis Networking P4, and the P4-DPDK and P4-eBPF software backends of the `p4c` compiler [9][13].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis combines **specification analysis**, **program-level reasoning**, and **empirical measurement reports** from the literature:

- *Specification analysis.* We ground every architectural claim in the normative texts: the P4_16 language specification [2], the PSA specification v1.1.0/v1.2 [6][7], the PNA working draft [10], the INT dataplane specification v2.1 [3], and the telemetry report format v1.0 [4].
- *Program-level reasoning.* We construct representative P4_16 programs (parser state machines, match-action tables with actions, INT insertion logic) and reason about their compilation onto PISA stages, following the methodology of the P4K formal semantics project, which gives P4 a rigorous executable semantics in the K framework [12].
- *Empirical grounding.* We synthesize published measurements: Tofino-class ASICs forwarding at multi-terabit rates with sub-microsecond added latency; NPU and FPGA mappings and their inefficiencies [11]; and software targets (BMv2 at 1–5 Gbps single-core) [9]. Where numbers are cited, their provenance is explicit.

Inline citations `[n]` refer to the References section; every factual claim about a specification is traceable to a normative clause.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The programmable parser: a finite-state machine over bytes

The parser is P4's answer to protocol ossification. Rather than a fixed Ethernet→IPv4→TCP decoder, the programmer writes an explicit **finite-state machine** whose states extract typed headers from the byte stream and whose transitions are selected on already-extracted fields [2]:

```p4
header ethernet_t {
    bit<48> dst_addr;
    bit<48> src_addr;
    bit<16> ether_type;
}

header ipv4_t {
    bit<4>  version;
    bit<4>  ihl;
    /* ... */
    bit<16> total_len;
    bit<32> src_addr;
    bit<32> dst_addr;
}

parser IngressParser(packet_in pkt,
                    out headers hdr,
                    inout metadata meta,
                    inout standard_metadata_t smeta) {
    state start {
        pkt.extract(hdr.ethernet);
        transition select(hdr.ethernet.ether_type) {
            0x0800: parse_ipv4;
            0x86DD: parse_ipv6;
            default: accept;
        }
    }
    state parse_ipv4 {
        pkt.extract(hdr.ipv4);
        transition accept;
    }
    state parse_ipv6 {
        pkt.extract(hdr.ipv6);
        transition accept;
    }
}
```

Three properties make this design powerful and compilable:

1. **Bounded extraction.** Each `extract` consumes a statically known number of bits; the parser's total lookahead is bounded, so a hardware parser can be synthesized as a TCAM-driven state machine with a fixed parse budget per packet.
2. **Header validity.** Headers are *valid* or *invalid*; only valid headers participate in matching and deparsing. Setting `hdr.ipv4.setInvalid()` after decapsulation, or `setValid()` on a newly pushed header, is the primitive behind tunneling and NAT.
3. **Parser value sets.** PSA supports *value sets* — runtime-programmable transition predicates — so new encapsulations can be recognized without recompiling the parser [7].

> **Theorem: (Parser termination).** A P4_16 parser terminates on all inputs iff its state-transition graph is acyclic except for explicitly bounded loops; the compiler rejects unbounded parse cycles at compile time, guaranteeing a worst-case parse latency.

The **deparser** is the parser's mirror: an ordered `packet.emit(...)` sequence that serializes valid headers. Because only valid headers are emitted, adding, removing, or reordering headers — the essence of encapsulation, decapsulation, and INT insertion — reduces to manipulating header validity plus field assignment.

### 4.2 Match-action pipelines and per-stage resources

After parsing, the packet header vector (PHV) — a wide register file carrying all extracted fields plus metadata — traverses the match-action pipeline. Each stage performs, in one clock cycle: a **table lookup** (exact via SRAM hash, ternary via TCAM, longest-prefix via TCAM or algorithmic trie), followed by **action execution** (a bounded sequence of primitive operations on PHV fields and stateful externs) [8].

A representative ingress control illustrates the idiom:

```p4
control Ingress(inout headers hdr,
                inout metadata meta,
                in ingress_intrinsic_metadata_t ig_md) {
    action forward(bit<9> port) {
        ig_md.ucast_egress_port = port;
    }
    action drop() {
        ig_md.drop = true;
    }
    table ipv4_lpm {
        key = { hdr.ipv4.dst_addr : lpm; }
        actions = { forward; drop; NoAction; }
        size = 1024;
        default_action = drop();
    }
    Counter<bit<64>, bit<32>>(1024, PSA_CounterType_t.PACKETS_AND_BYTES) pkt_cnt;
    apply {
        if (hdr.ipv4.isValid()) {
            ipv4_lpm.apply();
            pkt_cnt.count((bit<32>) ig_md.ingress_port);
        }
    }
}
```

The deep architectural truth is that **every P4 program is a resource-allocation problem**. Each pipeline stage exposes finite quantities of:

| Resource | Role | Typical magnitude (ASIC class) |
|---|---|---|
| TCAM | Ternary/LPM/range match entries | 100s of K entries per device |
| SRAM | Exact-match hash tables, action data, stateful memory | 10s–100s of MB |
| PHV containers | Field transport between stages | ~4–6 Kb wide PHV |
| Stateful ALUs | Per-stage register read-modify-write | 1–4 per stage |
| Action buses | VLIW action instruction width | Bounded per stage |

*Table 1: Per-stage resources constraining P4 program mapping on PISA-class targets.*

The compiler's job — table placement, action scheduling across stages, PHV allocation, and dependency analysis — is NP-hard in the general case, and production compilers (Barefoot SDE, Intel P4 Studio) rely on heuristics plus programmer annotations. A program that is *semantically* correct may still fail to compile if it demands more TCAM in one stage than exists, or chains stateful operations with loop-carried dependencies the pipeline cannot honor [8][11].

Key semantic guarantees simplify reasoning [2]:

- **Sequential action semantics.** Actions execute as if sequentially; the compiler preserves the programmer-visible order.
- **Atomic extern methods.** Each extern method invocation is atomic with respect to concurrent packets; additionally, P4_16 provides `@atomic` blocks for multi-statement atomicity [2].
- **No backward flow.** A later stage cannot send data to an earlier stage except via recirculation — an explicit, expensive operation that re-injects the packet at the parser.

### 4.3 PSA and PNA: the portable target contracts

**PSA** defines the canonical switch pipeline [6][7]:

```
                    ┌──────────────┐
                    │ IngressParser│ (programmable)
                    └──────┬───────┘
                           ▼
                    ┌──────────────┐
                    │   Ingress    │ (programmable match-action)
                    └──────┬───────┘
                           ▼
              ┌─────────────────────────┐
              │ IngressDeparser + BQE   │ (deparser programmable;
              │ Replication Engine      │  buffering/queuing fixed)
              └────────────┬────────────┘
                           ▼
                    ┌──────────────┐
                    │ EgressParser │ (programmable)
                    └──────┬───────┘
                           ▼
                    ┌──────────────┐
                    │    Egress    │ (programmable match-action)
                    └──────┬───────┘
                           ▼
                    ┌──────────────┐
                    │EgressDeparser│ (programmable)
                    └──────────────┘
```

The PSA package exposes **intrinsic metadata** (ingress port, timestamps, parser errors), **packet paths** (normal, recirculate, clone — via `CloneSession`), and a fixed **extern suite**: `Counter`, `DirectCounter`, `Meter`, `DirectMeter`, `Register`, `Hash`, `Checksum`/`InternetChecksum`, `Random`, `Digest`, `ActionProfile`, and `ActionSelector` [7]. The extern-usage table in the PSA specification normatively restricts *where* each extern may be instantiated — e.g., `Digest` only in the ingress deparser, checksums only in parsers/deparsers — which is precisely the kind of portability contract that lets one P4 program compile to BMv2, DPDK, and hardware alike [7].

**PNA** re-targets the model at NICs [10]. Its distinctive features include:

- **Host ports and network ports** with `send_to_port`/`send_to_host` extern functions replacing PSA's egress-port model;
- **Data-plane-managed table entries**: the `add_entry`/`add_entry_if` extern functions and `pna_idle_timeout` table properties let the data plane learn and expire flows autonomously — the primitive behind stateful NAT and connection tracking offload;
- **Crypto accelerator externs** for inline IPsec encrypt/decrypt, demonstrating how PNA embraces domain-specific fixed-function accelerators alongside programmable logic;
- A single main pipeline (no separate ingress/egress), matching NIC hardware reality.

> **Theorem: (Architecture portability).** A P4_16 program written against PSA externs and intrinsic metadata compiles without source modification to any PSA-conformant target; behavioral differences are confined to documented target-specific parameters (e.g., counter byte-counting conventions, hash algorithm selections).

### 4.4 Extern functions and objects: the escape hatch, disciplined

Section 4.3 of the P4_16 specification introduces externs as the interface between P4 programs and architecture-specific functionality [2]. There are two varieties [13]:

1. **Extern functions** — declared like function prototypes (e.g., PNA's `add_entry`, `send_to_port`, `is_host_port`); the implementation is an opaque black box.
2. **Extern objects** — declared like C++ classes with constructors and methods (e.g., `Register<W,S>` with `read`/`write`, `Meter` with `execute`, `Hash` with `get`).

This discipline matters. In P4_14, counters and meters were *language primitives*, which meant every target had to implement identical semantics. In P4_16, they are *architecture libraries*: PSA defines their precise semantics (e.g., counters support `PACKETS`, `BYTES`, `PACKETS_AND_BYTES`; meters implement RFC 2697/2698-style two-rate three-color marking), while exotic targets may add their own — Xilinx's Vitis Networking P4, for instance, documents its own builtin extern set [13]. Stateful processing idioms built on registers — sketches for heavy-hitter detection [5], in-network aggregation [5], flowlet switching — all reduce to disciplined `Register` read-modify-write sequences within a stage's atomicity guarantees.

### 4.5 In-Band Network Telemetry: the data plane observes itself

**In-Band Network Telemetry (INT)** is arguably the flagship *application* of programmable data planes: because the parser and deparser are programmable, switches can embed measurement instructions and per-hop metadata directly into data packets at line rate — something impossible in fixed-function silicon without a standards cycle [3]. INT was first introduced at P4.org in 2015 and refined through the P4 Applications Working Group [5].

#### Roles and modes

Three roles partition INT processing along a path: the **INT source** (embeds instructions), **INT transit** nodes (add metadata), and the **INT sink** (strips INT headers and exports reports). Since v2.0/v2.1, the specification defines three modes of operation [3]:

| Mode | Packet modification | Export model | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **INT-XD** (eXport Data) | None | Each node exports per-packet metadata ("postcards") per its Flow Watchlist | Zero-overhead monitoring of selected flows |
| **INT-MX** (eMbed instruct(X)ions) | Instruction header only | Nodes export metadata directly to the collector following embedded instructions | Bounded packet growth; sink strips instructions |
| **INT-MD** (eMbed Data) | Instructions + per-hop metadata | Classic hop-by-hop: sink aggregates and selectively exports | Full path visibility in one report |

*Table 2: INT modes of operation per the INT dataplane specification v2.1 [3].*

#### Header formats

The INT header stack, inserted by the source (typically over a shim indicated by DSCP value `0x17` or a dedicated encapsulation), comprises [3]:

- **INT shim header** (4 bytes): `Type` (1 = INT), `Next protocol`, `Length` (total INT header length in 4-byte words minus 3), DSCP/flags.
- **INT metadata header** (8+ bytes): `Ver`, `Rep`, `C`, `E` (max-hop-count exceeded), `M` (MTU exceeded), reserved, `Instruction Bitmap` (which metadata each hop must emit), `Domain ID`, `DS Instruction`, `DS Flags`.
- **Metadata stack**: per-hop records whose per-hop length is fixed by the instruction bitmap — e.g., 4 bytes for Switch ID, 4 bytes for ingress/egress port IDs, 4 bytes for hop latency, 4 bytes for queue occupancy — each hop *prepends* its record, so the sink observes the path in reverse order.

The instruction bitmap is the elegant core: a 16-bit mask selecting from standardized metadata (node ID, port IDs, hop latency, queue occupancy/depth, ingress/egress timestamps) plus domain-specific extensions. A canonical example requests switch ID + queue occupancy at each of up to 8 hops; the source initializes `Remaining Hop Count = 8`, each hop decrements it, and the `E` bit signals diameter overflow [3].

In P4, INT insertion is a deparser/parser program: the source's ingress control sets a metadata flag; the deparser emits the shim + metadata headers and the source's own record; transit nodes' parsers recognize the shim and their deparsers prepend records; the sink strips everything and emits a **telemetry report** to the collector. The report format itself is standardized separately: postcard mode places telemetry metadata *before* the original headers in the report packet, with a common report-group header carrying node ID [4].

```p4
/* INT-MD source insertion sketch (simplified) */
header int_shim_t {
    bit<4>  int_type;      // 1 = INT
    bit<4>  rsvd;
    bit<8>  len;           // INT header words
    bit<6>  dscp;
    bit<2>  rsvd2;
}
header int_md_t {
    bit<4>  ver;           // 2
    bit<2>  rep;
    bit<1>  c; bit<1> e; bit<1> m; bit<7> rsvd;
    bit<8>  ins_cnt;
    bit<8>  max_hop_cnt;
    bit<8>  total_hop_cnt;
    bit<4>  ins_mask_0003;
    bit<4>  ins_mask_0407;
    bit<4>  ins_mask_0811;
    bit<4>  ins_mask_1215;
}
/* source: mark for insertion; deparser emits shim + metadata + own record */
```

#### Postcard versus embedded: the fundamental trade-off

- **Postcard (INT-XD)** imposes *zero* packet modification — ideal for latency-sensitive or MTU-constrained traffic — at the cost of *N* report packets per data packet across *N* hops, stressing the collector and requiring flow-watchlist configuration on every node [3][4].
- **INT-MD** concentrates all per-hop data into the packet itself (growing it by ~8–32 bytes per hop), minimizing collector fan-in but risking MTU exceedance (the `M` bit) and perturbing the traffic being measured — the observer effect, quantified in Section 5.

INT-MX splits the difference: only instructions travel in-packet; each node exports directly, so packet size stays constant along the path [3].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### Line-rate programmability is real

The central empirical claim of the programmable-data-plane program is that programmability need not sacrifice throughput. Evidence:

1. **RMT/PISA ASICs.** The RMT chip demonstrated 6.5 Tbps-class forwarding with a fully programmable parser and match-action pipeline at constant per-packet latency — the existence proof that P4's abstraction maps to competitive silicon [8]. Barefoot's Tofino (6.5 Tbps, 65 ports of 100 GbE class) ran arbitrary P4_16 programs — INT, in-network computing (NetCache [5]), stateful load balancing (SilkRoad [5]) — at line rate with nanosecond-scale added pipeline latency [13].
2. **Software targets.** BMv2, the reference behavioral model, prioritizes correctness over speed (1–5 Gbps single-core); the P4-DPDK and P4-eBPF backends trade some programmability for 10–40 Gbps on commodity servers [9]. The gap between BMv2 and hardware — three orders of magnitude — is the price of generality, and it is precisely why PSA's portability contract matters: develop on BMv2, deploy on ASIC.
3. **FPGA and NPU mappings.** An FPGA study of PISA mapping found that while parsers and exact-match stages map efficiently, TCAM emulation and wide crossbars are area-expensive on FPGAs; the authors propose network-specialized hard blocks to close the gap [11]. NPU-based targets (many-core packet processors) execute the ingress pipeline in parallel across cores but lose egress programmability, forcing algorithms like CoDel AQM into the ingress with queue-delay feedback — a concrete instance of architecture shaping algorithm design [14].

### INT overhead, measured

INT-MD's per-hop cost is deterministic: with switch ID (4 B) + queue occupancy (4 B) requested, each hop adds 8 bytes plus the 12-byte shim/metadata header at the source. For a 5-hop path, a 1500-byte packet grows by ~52 bytes (~3.5%); for 64-byte packets, the same path more than doubles packet size — the reason INT deployments sample flows or use INT-XD for small-packet traffic [3]. Postcard mode's cost instead appears at the collector: at 100 Gbps with 800-byte average packets, a 5-hop INT-XD deployment generates ~75M reports/second, requiring a dedicated streaming telemetry pipeline — the motivation for the standardized report format with report-group batching [4].

### Proof sketch: INT path reconstruction correctness

> **Theorem: (INT-MD path fidelity).** In INT-MD mode with per-hop metadata length *L* fixed by the instruction bitmap, the sink reconstructs the exact ordered sequence of INT-capable hops iff (i) no hop sets the E bit (hop count ≤ max), (ii) no hop sets the M bit (MTU respected), and (iii) every hop prepends exactly *L* bytes.

*Proof sketch.* By induction on hops: the source writes the header with `total_hop_cnt = 1` and its record; each transit hop *k* atomically prepends its *L*-byte record and increments the count (atomicity from Section 4.2's extern guarantees). Prepending yields reverse-path order in the packet, which the sink reverses. If (i) fails, records beyond the maximum are silently dropped (E bit set); if (ii) fails, the packet may fragment, invalidating offsets; if (iii) fails, record boundaries misalign. ∎

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating where the paradigm strains:

1. **Stateful cross-packet consistency.** P4's concurrency model gives atomicity only at action and extern-method granularity [2]. Algorithms needing multi-step read-modify-write across stages (e.g., consistent snapshots for telemetry) must use `@atomic` blocks or recirculation, both of which cost pipeline resources or throughput. There is no general transactional memory.
2. **Arithmetic poverty.** P4_16 deliberately omits division, modulo, floating point, and (on many targets) multiplication, since line-rate ALUs implement only add/sub/compare/shift [9]. Approximations — LPM-based leading-zero counts for square roots in CoDel [14], log-domain sketches — are the norm, and each approximation must be re-validated per application.
3. **Compilation complexity.** Mapping to per-stage resources is heuristic; small program edits can trigger phase transitions in compilability ("it compiled yesterday"). Debugging requires target-specific introspection, partially addressed by P4K-style formal semantics [12] but still expert-intensive.
4. **Control-plane scale.** P4Runtime (gRPC-based) programs tables at runtime, but controllers managing millions of entries across hundreds of switches face consistency and latency challenges the language does not solve.
5. **Industrial discontinuity.** Intel discontinued the Tofino roadmap in 2023 [9], removing the highest-profile merchant programmable ASIC. The ecosystem's center of gravity has shifted to DPUs/SmartNICs (NVIDIA BlueField, AMD Pensando), software targets, and research silicon — a reminder that language portability (PSA/PNA) is also a hedge against vendor churn.
6. **INT's observer effect and security.** INT headers expose internal topology, queue depths, and latencies to anyone who can read packets — an information leak if INT traffic crosses trust boundaries. The specification assumes INT domains are administratively scoped; enforcement is deployment practice, not language semantics [3].

## 7 Conclusion

Programmable data planes, as embodied by P4_16, the match-action pipeline, PSA/PNA, and In-Band Network Telemetry, represent a completed paradigm shift: forwarding behavior is now *software* with a hardware-grade execution contract. The parser-as-finite-state-machine dissolves protocol ossification; the PISA pipeline with its explicit per-stage resources makes line-rate performance *plannable* rather than accidental; externs discipline the boundary between portable programs and target capabilities; and INT turns the network into its own measurement instrument at a cost that is precisely characterizable per mode.

The trajectory is clear. As fixed-function merchant silicon cedes ground to DPUs, SmartNICs, and software targets, the value of P4 migrates from "program the switch" to "program the *path*" — with PNA's data-plane-managed entries and crypto accelerators pointing at stateful 5G UPF offload, and INT's postcard mode pointing at zero-touch observability [10][13]. The open problems — richer stateful consistency, compiler predictability, verified compilation via semantics like P4K [12] — are now well-posed precisely because the abstraction is stable. The data plane is programmable; the remaining work is making that programmability *routine*.
