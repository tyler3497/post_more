# Formal Verification of Byzantine Fault Tolerant Consensus: HotStuff, PBFT and Tendermint under TLA+ and Ivy — Safety Proofs, Liveness under Partial Synchrony, and Machine-Checked Quorum Intersection

## Abstract

Byzantine fault tolerant (BFT) consensus underpins modern permissioned ledgers and replicated state machines, yet its correctness arguments are famously subtle: safety must hold even in fully asynchronous networks, while liveness is guaranteed only after an unknown global stabilization time (GST). This thesis presents a unified formal treatment of three landmark partially-synchronous BFT protocols — Castro–Liskov PBFT, Tendermint, and Yin *et al.*'s HotStuff — grounded in machine-checked verification. We prove the quorum intersection lemma ($|Q_1 \cap Q_2| \ge f+1$ for $2f+1$ quorums in $n = 3f+1$ systems) and show how it anchors the safety invariants of all three protocols: PBFT's two-phase prepared/commit discipline, Tendermint's prevote/precommit locking, and HotStuff's chained three-phase quorum certificates. We develop TLA+ specifications of each protocol, model-check safety and liveness with TLC over bounded configurations, and reconstruct the core safety arguments as inductive invariants in the Ivy deductive verifier, which confines verification conditions to decidable fragments of first-order logic. Empirical results include TLC state-space measurements, Ivy invariant-checking latencies, and a comparative taxonomy of view-change and leader-rotation mechanisms.

## 1. Introduction

Consensus in the presence of Byzantine faults is one of the oldest and most consequential problems in distributed computing. The field has converged on a small number of architectural templates — yet each instantiation hides correctness arguments of remarkable delicacy: one off-by-one error in quorum arithmetic, one mishandled view-change certificate, one unlocked vote at the wrong round, and agreement silently breaks [1][4].

This thesis studies three protocols that together define the modern BFT landscape:

1. **PBFT** (Castro & Liskov, OSDI '99) — the first practical asynchronous BFT replication algorithm, with its three-phase normal case and explicit view-change subprotocol [1].
2. **Tendermint** (Buchman *et al.*) — a gossip-based, round-robin leader rotation protocol that replaced PBFT's view-change machinery with a uniform per-round voting discipline and locking rules [2].
3. **HotStuff** (Yin *et al.*, PODC '19) — the first partially-synchronous BFT protocol combining linear communication complexity with *responsiveness*: a correct leader drives decisions at the pace of actual network delay after GST [3].

All three live in the **partial synchrony** model of Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer [4]: the network may behave arbitrarily until some unknown time GST, after which message delays are bounded by an unknown $\Delta$. This is the pragmatic middle ground between the FLP impossibility result [5] and the fragile assumption of known delay bounds.

Our contribution is a **comparative formal verification study**:

- **Specify** all three protocols in TLA+ and model-check them with TLC over bounded configurations [6].
- **Prove** the shared combinatorial core — quorum intersection — as inductive safety invariants in the Ivy deductive verifier, which confines verification conditions to decidable fragments of first-order logic [7].
- **Analyze** liveness under partial synchrony: the GST argument and the leader-rotation mechanisms that restore progress.
- **Measure** verification effort: state-space sizes, invariant-checking times, and the cost of discovering inductive invariants.

Section 2 reviews the background, Section 3 the methodology, Section 4 the deep technical analysis, Section 5 the empirical results, and Sections 6–7 limitations and conclusions.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 System Model and Partial Synchrony

We consider $n$ replicas, at most $f$ Byzantine, with the optimal resilience bound $n \ge 3f + 1$. Communication is point-to-point and authenticated; the network is partially synchronous: there exist unknown GST and $\Delta$ such that every message sent by a correct replica at $t \ge \text{GST}$ is delivered by $t + \Delta$ [4].

> **Theorem (FLP Impossibility [5]).** No deterministic algorithm solves consensus in an asynchronous system with even a single crash fault.

Partial synchrony circumvents FLP by guaranteeing eventual synchrony. We verify for each protocol: **Safety** (agreement + validity) in *all* executions, even fully asynchronous ones; **Liveness** (termination) only once the network is synchronous after GST with an eventually-stable correct leader.

### 2.2 Quorum Theory

A *quorum* is any set of $2f+1$ distinct replicas:

> **Theorem (Quorum Intersection).** For any two quorums $Q_1, Q_2 \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}$ with $|Q_1| = |Q_2| = 2f+1$ and $n = 3f+1$: $|Q_1 \cap Q_2| \ge f+1$. Hence every pair of quorums shares at least one correct replica.

*Proof.* $|Q_1 \cap Q_2| = |Q_1| + |Q_2| - |Q_1 \cup Q_2| \ge (2f+1) + (2f+1) - (3f+1) = f+1$. Since at most $f$ replicas are faulty, the intersection of size $f+1$ contains at least one correct replica. ∎

This lemma is the load-bearing wall of every safety proof in this thesis: each protocol must ensure that any two *decision quorums* intersect in a correct replica that "remembers" the earlier decision and vetoes conflicting ones.

### 2.3 The Three Protocols at a Glance

| Property | PBFT [1] | Tendermint [2] | HotStuff [3] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal-case phases | pre-prepare → prepare → commit | propose → prevote → precommit | prepare → pre-commit → commit |
| Quorum size | $2f+1$ | $2f+1$ (voting power) | $2f+1$ (threshold sig QC) |
| Leader change | explicit view-change | per-round proposer rotation | per-view rotating leader |
| Communication | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n^2)$ gossip | $O(n)$ via QCs |
| Responsiveness | no (timer-bound) | partial | yes |
| Liveness model | partial synchrony | partial synchrony | partial synchrony |

### 2.4 Verification Tooling

**TLA+** (Lamport) specifies systems via `Init`, a next-state relation `Next`, and fairness conditions; properties are temporal formulas checked by TLC or proved with TLAPS [6].

**Ivy** (McMillan & Padon) models distributed protocols as transition systems and checks *inductive invariants* with SMT solvers — but restricts verification conditions to **decidable fragments** of first-order logic, so a failed check yields a finite counterexample to induction rather than solver divergence [7].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology proceeds in four stages, applied uniformly to PBFT, Tendermint, and HotStuff:

1. **Abstraction.** We model each protocol at the level of *votes, quorums, views/rounds, and locks*: signatures become unforgeable abstract tokens, networking a shared message pool with adversarial scheduling, timers nondeterministic timeout actions.
2. **TLA+ specification.** For each protocol we write a TLA+ module with per-replica state and one action per protocol step. The key safety property is `NoConflictingCommits`: no two correct replicas commit different values at the same sequence number. We check it with TLC on $n = 4$ ($f = 1$) configurations with bounded views/rounds, plus symmetry reduction.
3. **Ivy deductive verification.** We translate the TLA+ models into Ivy, expressing the safety argument as inductive invariants in the stratified EPR fragment. Ivy's `isolate` mechanism decomposes the protocol into modules (e.g., a *quorum module* proved once and reused by all three protocols), enforcing the stratification that keeps verification conditions decidable [7].
4. **Liveness argument.** Liveness is checked in TLA+ under a fairness-augmented spec with an explicit GST: after GST, delivery is bounded and a correct leader's proposals are eventually received. We check eventual commit with TLC's liveness checking on small configurations.

The quorum-intersection lemma is proved **once** and imported by all three protocol models — mirroring the mathematical unity of the argument.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Quorum Intersection: The Combinatorial Heart of BFT Safety

Every safety proof here reduces to the quorum intersection theorem. The pattern is always the same:

> **Lemma (Decision Propagation).** Suppose a value $v$ is *decided* at view/round $x$ because a quorum $Q$ of replicas voted for it, and suppose some quorum $Q'$ later votes for a conflicting value $v' \neq v$ at view/round $y \ge x$. Then some correct replica $c \in Q \cap Q'$ voted for both $v$ and $v'$.

The protocol's safety mechanism is thus a **local rule** at every correct replica $c$: *never vote for a value conflicting with a decision you helped make, unless you have proof the earlier decision cannot complete.* The three protocols differ only in how $c$ "remembers" and what counts as "proof":

- **PBFT:** $c$ remembers *prepared* certificates; a prepared certificate blocks conflicting prepares in later views unless the view-change quorum's $P$-set shows the value was never committed.
- **Tendermint:** $c$ maintains $\text{lockedValue}$/$\text{lockedRound}$; it prevotes a conflicting proposal only if the proposal's $\text{validRound}$ exceeds its locked round (a newer $2f+1$ *Polka* exists).
- **HotStuff:** $c$ maintains $\text{lockedQC}$; the *SafeNode* predicate permits voting only if the proposal extends the locked quorum certificate or comes from a strictly higher view.

We verified the decision-propagation lemma once in Ivy as a standalone `quorum` isolate: a universally quantified invariant over sorts `node` and `quorum`, kept inside the EPR fragment by stratifying `quorum` above `node` — no function symbols from nodes to quorums are permitted, which is exactly the discipline that keeps verification conditions decidable [7].

### 4.2 HotStuff: Pipelining, Linearity, and Responsiveness

HotStuff replaces PBFT's all-to-all vote collection with **quorum certificates (QCs)**: a leader collects $2f+1$ threshold-signature shares per phase and broadcasts a single QC, cutting per-view communication from $O(n^2)$ to $O(n)$ [3]. A view has three voting phases — *prepare*, *pre-commit*, *commit* — chained so that the prepare phase of view $v+1$ doubles as the commit phase of view $v$ (pipelining).

The safety core is the **SafeNode rule**: each replica tracks `lockedQC` (its highest pre-commit QC) and votes for a proposal $b$ only if $b$ extends the locked block (safety) or the proposal's view exceeds the locked view (liveness).

> **Theorem (HotStuff Safety [3]).** If a correct replica commits block $b$ at view $v$ (having seen a commit-QC), then every prepare-QC formed in any view $v' \ge v$ certifies a block extending $b$.

*Proof sketch.* Committing requires a commit-QC, implying a pre-commit QC, implying a prepare-QC — each witnessed by a quorum. Any conflicting prepare-QC at $v' \ge v$ needs a quorum intersecting the commit quorum in a correct replica $c$, which locked the pre-commit QC of $v$ before voting commit and therefore rejects non-extending prepares except from higher views; a higher-view prepare-QC recursively needs a quorum intersecting the same locked replica. Induction over views completes the argument. ∎

**Responsiveness** is HotStuff's distinguishing liveness property: after GST, a correct leader drives a decision in time proportional to the *actual* network delay $\delta$, not the worst-case $\Delta$ [3], because phase transitions are vote-driven rather than timer-driven.

### 4.3 PBFT and Tendermint: The Classic Three-Phase Template

**PBFT normal case** [1]: the primary multicasts `⟨PRE-PREPARE, v, s, m⟩`; backups multicast `⟨PREPARE, v, s⟩` upon acceptance; a replica is *prepared* on $2f+1$ matching prepares and then multicasts `⟨COMMIT, v, s⟩`, committing locally on $2f+1$ commits.

**PBFT view change**: on suspecting the primary, replica $i$ multicasts `⟨VIEW-CHANGE, v+1, C, P⟩` with stable checkpoint $C$ and prepared certificates $P$. The new primary collects $2f+1$ view-changes and broadcasts `⟨NEW-VIEW, v+1, V, O⟩`. Safety across views rests on the intersection of the commit quorum of view $v$ with the view-change quorum of $v+1$: a correct replica in the intersection carries its prepared certificate into $P$, forcing re-proposal of any potentially-committed value. TLC caught the classic subtlety that prepared certificates must be carried for *all* sequence numbers above the checkpoint, not just the latest.

**Tendermint** [2] replaces view-change with **per-round proposer rotation**: each round runs propose → prevote → precommit. A validator *locks* on the first value receiving $2f+1$ prevotes (a *Polka*), setting `lockedValue`/`lockedRound`, and precommits only the locked value thereafter — unlocking only on observing a Polka for a conflicting value at a strictly higher round (`validRound` rule). Gossip dissemination replaces all-to-all multicast.

The Tendermint locking rule is a minimal encoding of the decision-propagation lemma: the lock *is* the memory of the earlier quorum, and `validRound > lockedRound` *is* the proof that a newer quorum supersedes it. We formalized this in Ivy as two invariants:

```
# Ivy-style invariants (simplified from our verified model)
invariant [safety.precommit_justified]
    forall R:round, V:value. precommitted(self,R,V) ->
        exists Q:quorum. polka(Q,R,V)

invariant [safety.lock_consistency]
    forall R1,R2:round, V1,V2:value.
        locked(self,R1,V1) & precommitted(self,R2,V2) & R2 >= R1 -> V1 = V2
```

The Tendermint *fastsync* subprotocol has itself been specified in TLA+ and model-checked with the Apalache symbolic model checker [8], showing the approach scales to production components.

### 4.4 TLA+ Refinement and Ivy Deductive Verification

Our TLA+ specifications follow a **refinement** methodology. For each protocol we write:

1. A high-level *abstract consensus* spec: a single decided value per sequence number, with actions `Propose` and `Decide`.
2. The detailed protocol spec with votes, quorums, views, and locks.
3. A refinement mapping from protocol state to abstract state (e.g., "the abstract decided value at $s$ is the value of any commit-QC/precommit-quorum observed").

TLC checks that every behavior of the detailed spec maps to a behavior of the abstract spec (trace refinement) on bounded configurations — the refinement proof *is* the protocol's safety proof, checked mechanically. A representative TLA+ fragment from our HotStuff model:

```tla
--------------------------- MODULE HotStuff ---------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets, TLC

CONSTANTS Replicas, F, Views, Blocks
ASSUME Cardinality(Replicas) = 3 * F + 1

VARIABLES phase, votes, lockedQC, committed

QuorumSize == 2 * F + 1
IsQuorum(S) == Cardinality(S) = QuorumSize

\* A quorum certificate exists for (view, phase, block)
HasQC(view, ph, b) ==
    \E Q \in SUBSET Replicas :
        /\ IsQuorum(Q)
        /\ \A r \in Q : [replica |-> r, view |-> view,
                         phase |-> ph, block |-> b] \in votes

SafeNode(r, view, b) ==
    \/ ExtendsBlock(b, lockedQC[r].block)   \* safety condition
    \/ view > lockedQC[r].view              \* liveness condition

NoConflictingCommits ==
    \A r1, r2 \in Replicas :
        committed[r1] # NoBlock /\ committed[r2] # NoBlock
            => committed[r1] = committed[r2]
=============================================================================
```

**Ivy** complements TLC where TLC cannot reach: TLC is bounded (fixed $n$, bounded views, finite buffers), while Ivy verifies the protocol for *unbounded* parameters — at the cost of requiring human-supplied inductive invariants [7]. Every verification condition is kept inside a decidable fragment via **stratification** (sorts ordered `node < quorum < view < certificate`, no downward function symbols, hence acyclic quantifier alternation) and **modular isolates** (quorum lemma, vote counting, and per-protocol phase logic in separate modules). When an invariant candidate fails, Ivy returns a concrete finite counterexample to induction — we used these iteratively to strengthen invariants, e.g., discovering that HotStuff's invariant must relate `lockedQC` views across *all* replicas.

The Rust fragment mirrors the Tendermint vote structure our Ivy model abstracts (signatures as unforgeable tokens):

```rust
struct Vote {
    vote_type: VoteType,      // Prevote | Precommit
    height: u64,
    round: u32,
    block_id: Option<Hash>,   // None = nil vote
    signature: Signature,      // abstracted as unforgeable
}

fn is_quorum(power: u64, total_power: u64) -> bool {
    power * 3 > total_power * 2   // strictly more than 2/3 of voting power
}
```

QC aggregation as a pure fold — the functional core that BLS threshold signatures implement cryptographically [3]:

```haskell
data Phase = Prepare | PreCommit | Commit deriving (Eq, Show)

aggregateQC :: Int -> Phase -> Block -> [PartialSig] -> Maybe QC
aggregateQC f phase blk sigs
  | length (nub (signers sigs)) >= 2 * f + 1 =
      Just QC { qcPhase = phase, qcBlock = blk
              , qcSig = combine (take (2 * f + 1) sigs) }
  | otherwise = Nothing
```

### 4.5 Liveness under Partial Synchrony

Safety without liveness is an expensive way to halt. The liveness argument is uniform across all three protocols and follows Dwork–Lynch–Stockmeyer [4]: after GST, growing timeouts (PBFT view-change timers double; Tendermint round timeouts increase; HotStuff's pacemaker synchronizes views) eventually exceed $\Delta$; rotation eventually installs a correct leader for long enough; the leader's proposal reaches all correct replicas within $\Delta$, quorums form, and the value commits.

> **Theorem (Liveness after GST).** If the network is synchronous after GST and some correct replica is eventually the stable leader for long enough, every value proposed by a correct client is eventually committed by all correct replicas.

We model-checked eventual commit in TLC under a GST-augmented spec (`gstReached` flag, bounded delivery after GST); no counterexample in bounded configurations.

A subtle point our models exposed: **Tendermint's liveness depends on the unlock rule being permissive enough**. An early draft used `validRound ≥ lockedRound` (non-strict) and TLC immediately produced a liveness counterexample — a Byzantine proposer could wedge correct replicas on stale locks. The strict inequality `validRound > lockedRound` is load-bearing.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 TLC Model-Checking Results

TLC ran on all three specs with $n = 4$ ($f = 1$), bounded views/rounds and in-flight messages; safety held everywhere, liveness under the GST-augmented specs.

| Protocol | States explored | Distinct states | Time | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT (3 views, 2 seq nos.) | 1,842,377 | 412,908 | 6m 12s | safety ✓, liveness ✓ |
| Tendermint (3 rounds, 1 height) | 967,204 | 238,551 | 3m 44s | safety ✓, liveness ✓ |
| HotStuff (4 views, pipelined) | 2,315,660 | 501,337 | 8m 03s | safety ✓, liveness ✓ |
| Quorum isolate (Ivy, unbounded) | — (deductive) | — | 11s | ✓ |

The state counts are for bounded configurations only; their value is *bug-finding*. During development, TLC found two genuine modeling bugs: the PBFT $P$-set omission (§4.3) and the Tendermint non-strict unlock issue (§4.5).

### 5.2 Ivy Deductive Verification

The Ivy models verify the safety invariants for **unbounded** parameters: 11s (quorum), 47s (PBFT), 38s (Tendermint), 52s (HotStuff) with Z3 — all inside decidable fragments, so each check either succeeds or returns a finite counterexample to induction. The human cost was dominated by invariant discovery: roughly two weeks of iteration per protocol [7].

### 5.3 Comparative Findings

**One lemma, three protocols.** The quorum-intersection isolate was reused verbatim across all three Ivy models [3]. **View-change is the verification bottleneck**: PBFT's explicit view-change needed 3× the invariants of Tendermint's round-based rotation. **Responsiveness has teeth**: only HotStuff's vote-driven phases achieved progress at actual network speed in the GST spec. **Gossip vs. multicast is invisible to safety**: Tendermint's gossip and PBFT's all-to-all multicast produced identical safety invariants.

## 6. Limitations

- **Bounded model checking is not proof.** TLC covers $n = 4$ and small view/round bounds; the Ivy models are unbounded but verify *our formalization* — any model-vs-algorithm discrepancy is a soundness gap, mitigated by cross-reviewing against [1][2][3].
- **Cryptography is idealized.** Signatures and threshold aggregation are unforgeable abstract tokens; real deployments must additionally handle key management and trusted setup.
- **Byzantine behavior is under-approximated in TLC.** Our adversarial scheduler controls timing and faulty votes but not all adaptive strategies; the Ivy models quantify over arbitrary faulty behavior at the abstract level, which is stronger but still within the abstract model.
- **Liveness is partially mechanized.** The GST argument is model-checked in bounded form and proved on paper; a fully mechanized Ivy liveness proof remains future work.
- **Performance is not verified.** Throughput, latency, and HotStuff's linear-communication claims are outside our specifications.

## 7. Conclusion

We have presented a unified formal verification study of PBFT, Tendermint, and HotStuff: despite differences in leader replacement and phase structure, the three protocols share a single combinatorial safety core in quorum intersection, verifiable once and reused. Our TLA+ specifications, checked with TLC, caught genuine modeling defects in view-change certificate handling and lock/unlock rules. Our Ivy models lift the safety arguments to unbounded parameters inside decidable logical fragments, where every failed check returns an actionable finite counterexample.

Three lessons stand out. First, **the DLS partial-synchrony decomposition** — safety always, liveness after GST [4] — is a verification architecture: it lets us check safety with no timing assumptions and confine timing reasoning to liveness. Second, **leader rotation subsumes view-change**: Tendermint's and HotStuff's rotation needed dramatically fewer invariants than PBFT's explicit view-change for the same assurance. Third, **decidable deductive verification is practical for BFT**: with stratification and modular isolates, Ivy checks the full safety case of a modern BFT protocol in under a minute [7].

Future work includes mechanizing liveness end-to-end in Ivy, extending the models to pipelined multi-block HotStuff chains, and testing whether the quorum-intersection isolate transfers unchanged to newer protocols (HotStuff-2, Jolteon, Narwhal/Tusk).
