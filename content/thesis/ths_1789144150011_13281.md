# Chiplet Interconnects: UCIe Die-to-Die PHY, AIB/BoW, and the Economics of Heterogeneous Integration

## Abstract

The end of classical Dennard scaling has shifted the locus of system-on-chip (SoC) innovation from transistor miniaturization to *integration*: multiple small dies — chiplets — assembled in one package. This thesis unifies the die-to-die (D2D) interconnects that enable heterogeneous integration. We dissect the Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express (UCIe) standard, co-developed by AMD, Arm, Intel, TSMC, NVIDIA, and others, which defines a protocol layer reusing PCIe/CXL flits, a die-to-die adapter, and a physical layer for standard, advanced, and 3D packages [1][2]. We trace its predecessors — Intel's Advanced Interface Bus (AIB) and the OCP ODSA Bunch of Wires (BoW) PHY — and compare them on bandwidth density, energy per bit, reach, and bit error rate [4][5][6]. We then formalize the economics: yield models (Murphy, negative binomial) show how defect density makes sub-300 mm² chiplets dramatically cheaper than reticle-limited monolithic dice, with quantitative cost models showing 35–50% savings at 5 nm [7][8]. Finally, we treat the co-design of on-chip networks (NoC) with D2D links, derive latency budgets, and identify the remaining barriers to a fully open chiplet marketplace.

## 1 Introduction

For five decades, the economics of semiconductors were governed by a simple rule: each process generation halved transistor cost, and the most economical design was therefore a single, ever-larger *monolithic* die. Two physical walls now break that rule. First, wafer costs and mask-set costs at advanced nodes (N3/N2) have grown superlinearly — a leading-edge wafer can exceed \$20,000 — so a single defect on an 800 mm² reticle-limited die destroys an enormous amount of value. Second, heterogeneous functions — logic, SRAM, analog/RF, high-voltage I/O, DRAM — scale *differently*; analog and I/O gain little from N3 while paying its full price.

Heterogeneous integration resolves the tension by disaggregating the SoC into *chiplets*: small, independently manufactured dies, each built on the process node best suited to its function, then re-aggregated in an advanced package using dense die-to-die interconnects. The concept is not new — AMD's EPYC Rome shipped a 14 nm I/O die with 7 nm compute chiplets in 2019 [8] — but a proprietary, per-vendor interface regime fragmented the ecosystem. UCIe, ratified as version 1.0 in 2022 [2] and extended to 2.0 (2024) and 3.0 (2025) [3], is the industry's first attempt at an *open, full-stack* D2D standard: physical layer, protocol, software model, and compliance.

This thesis makes four contributions:

1. A layer-by-layer exposition of the UCIe stack — protocol, adapter, and PHY — including flit formats, module widths, package classes, and the 2.0 manageability/3D extensions.
2. A comparative history and technical analysis of the predecessors that UCIe subsumes: AIB and BoW.
3. A quantitative treatment of chiplet economics grounded in yield theory and published cost models.
4. A co-design framework for coupling on-chip networks with D2D links, with latency and bandwidth budgets.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Why die-to-die links differ from board-level SerDes

A conventional serializer/deserializer (SerDes) for PCIe or Ethernet is engineered for long reach (decimeters) over lossy channels, paying 5–20 pJ/bit and accepting ~100 ns of latency. A D2D link operates across at most tens of millimeters of package substrate — often under 2 mm on a silicon interposer — so the channel is electrically short, the signaling can be *source-synchronous parallel*, and the target energy drops below 1 pJ/bit with sub-2 ns latency. The tradeoff is density: thousands of microbump wires must fit along the die edge ("beachfront"), so *bandwidth density per mm of edge* and *per mm² of bump area* become the primary figures of merit, alongside shoreline utilization.

### 2.2 Package taxonomy

Three package classes dominate the taxonomy used by UCIe [1][3]:

- **Standard package** (organic substrate, 2D/MCM): bump pitch 100–130 µm, reach 10–25 mm, data rates 4–32 GT/s.
- **Advanced package** (2.5D: silicon interposer, EMIB, fan-out bridge; 3D hybrid bonding): bump pitch 25–55 µm, reach < 2 mm, data rates 4–32 GT/s.
- **UCIe-3D** (vertical face-to-face stacking, hybrid bonding): bump pitch down to ~1–10 µm, data rates ≤ 4 GT/s, dramatically higher density and power efficiency.

Organic laminate is cheap; silicon interposers (TSMC CoWoS) and embedded bridges (Intel EMIB) are expensive but enable an order of magnitude more wires per millimeter.

### 2.3 Lineage: from proprietary to open

Before UCIe, the industry relied on proprietary D2D links: AMD's Infinity Fabric, NVIDIA's NVLink-C2C, Apple's UltraFusion, and Intel's AIB. Two interfaces were explicitly opened to seed the ecosystem. Intel released AIB royalty-free and contributed its PHY to the CHIPS Alliance (Linux Foundation) [9]. The Open Compute Project's ODSA subproject released BoW (Bunch of Wires) under an open license, with silicon demonstrated in over ten companies across nodes from 65 nm to 5 nm [5]. UCIe's PHY is directly derived from AIB's physical design [3], while BoW's philosophy — extreme simplicity, standard-package friendliness — survives as an alternative design point.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis combines primary-source standards documentation (UCIe 1.x/2.0/3.0 overviews, the AIB white paper, the BoW PHY specification), peer-reviewed and industry cost modeling (notably the "Chiplet Actuary" quantitative model [7]), vendor disclosures (AMD ISSCC 2020 cost slides, GUC's 32 Gb/s UCIe silicon [3]), and classical yield theory. We treat the standard's electrical claims as *specification maxima* and cross-check bandwidth-density arithmetic independently:

$$\text{BW density} = \frac{(\text{lanes}) \times (\text{rate})}{(\text{shoreline length})}$$

For an advanced-package UCIe module with 64 lanes at 32 GT/s per direction: $64 \times 32\,\text{Gb/s} = 2.048\,\text{Tb/s}$ per direction per module; four modules per link give multi-TB/s-class bidirectional bandwidth consistent with the widely quoted ~1.35 TB/s/mm² at 45 µm bump pitch [1].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The UCIe stack: protocol, adapter, PHY

> **Theorem (Layered interoperability):** *A complete D2D standard must specify the full stack — transaction protocol, link reliability, and electrical signaling — because chiplets from different vendors share no private contract beyond the standard.*

UCIe defines exactly three layers [1][3]:

**Protocol layer.** Rather than inventing a new transaction layer, UCIe *reuses* established flit-based protocols: **CXL.io** (PCIe-compatible I/O), **CXL.mem** (memory semantics), **CXL.cache** (accelerator coherence), and a **streaming protocol** for raw, user-defined traffic. Multiprotocol multiplexing on one link is supported from UCIe 1.1 onward, letting, e.g., cache-coherent accelerator traffic and bulk DMA share a PHY.

**Die-to-die adapter.** This is the reliability and framing layer. It packs protocol flits into the wire format, computes CRC, and implements link-level retry on detected errors — a necessity because D2D channels at 32 GT/s target BER of $10^{-15}$ (advanced) to $10^{-27}$ (at ≤12–16 GT/s depending on package), and a flit error must be corrected or replayed without protocol-layer involvement. The adapter also handles parameter negotiation with the partner chiplet (width, speed, repair of defective lanes).

**Physical layer.** The PHY splits into *logical PHY* (serialization, lane deskew, training, scrambling) and *electrical PHY* (drivers/receivers). Two interfaces expose the PHY to the adapter:

- **RDI (Raw Die-to-die Interface):** protocol-agnostic raw bit pipe.
- **FDI (Flit-aware Die-to-die Interface):** hands flits to a protocol-aware controller with flow control and credit management.

A **sideband** channel, clocked at a fixed 800 MHz on an always-on auxiliary power domain, carries parameter exchange, link training handshakes, debug, and (from UCIe 2.0) the standardized manageability fabric; UCIe 3.0 extends sideband reach to 100 mm and adds priority packets for deterministic low-latency signaling, fast throttle, and emergency shutdown [3].

Flit formats merit attention. UCIe defines a **68-byte flit** (carried over 16 lanes) and **256-byte flits** in both standard and latency-optimized variants. The 256B standard flit amortizes CRC and header overhead over more payload — higher efficiency for bulk transfers — while the latency-optimized flit reduces serialization delay for small, latency-sensitive transactions such as coherence probes.

Module widths are standardized per package class: **x8/x16 modules** for standard packages, **x32/x64 modules** for advanced packages, with up to four modules ganged into one logical link [3].

### 4.2 Predecessors: AIB and BoW

| Parameter | AIB 1.0/2.0 | BoW (fast mode) | UCIe 1.0+ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Origin | Intel (donated; CHIPS Alliance) [9] | OCP ODSA, open license [5] | Multi-vendor consortium [2] |
| Signaling | Source-synchronous, SDR/DDR | Source-synchronous, single-ended data + differential clock | Clock-forwarded, NRZ |
| Lane rate | 2–6.4 Gb/s | 2–32 Gb/s | 4–32 GT/s (48/64 GT/s in 3.0) |
| Bump pitch | 25–55 µm (microbump) | 130–170 µm (organic) / 25–55 µm (advanced) | 100–130 µm (std) / 25–55 µm (adv) / <10 µm (3D) |
| Energy | 0.5–0.8 pJ/bit | <0.5 pJ/bit (organic), <0.25 pJ/bit (advanced) | 0.25–0.5 pJ/bit |
| Latency | < 2 ns | < 2 ns | < 2 ns (+ adapter) |
| Reach | < 25 mm | 25 mm (organic), 5 mm (advanced) | 10–25 mm (std), <2 mm (adv) |
| BER | n/a (design target ~1e-15) | <1e-15 | 1e-15 to 1e-27 |
| Edge density | < 1.6 Tb/s/mm | 1.034 Tb/s/mm (organic), 5.12 Tb/s/mm (advanced) | <10 Tb/s/mm |
| Link layer | AXI | BoW link layer (AXI/CHI) | Raw, streaming, PCIe, CXL |

*Table: D2D interface comparison (figures from the ODSA 2024 business-analysis white paper [6], UCIe/Wikipedia overviews [1][3]).*

**AIB** is the direct ancestor of UCIe's PHY: a short-reach, high-speed parallel interface supporting over 3,000 TX/RX signals, 2 Gb/s per pin (higher in AIB 2.0), compact shoreline usage via microbumps, and built-in redundancy for manufacturing yield [4]. It was production-proven in Intel's Stratix 10 and Agilex FPGAs over EMIB. Its limitation was scope: AIB specified only the PHY — no protocol, no software model, no compliance regime.

**BoW** takes the opposite design stance: *radical simplicity*. Each BoW *slice* is 16 single-ended data wires plus a forwarded differential clock pair, with optional FEC and AUX wires; slices stack into links and are unidirectional at any instant, composed into bidirectional links by configuration [5]. BoW deliberately spans *both* commodity organic laminate (130–170 µm pitch) and advanced packaging, trading peak density for ease of design, process portability, and low-cost assembly. Over a dozen companies validated it across 5–65 nm nodes [5]. Its cost is interoperability-by-negotiation: the flexibility that makes BoW attractive requires partner-by-partner agreement, precisely the fragmentation UCIe's stricter compliance program exists to end.

### 4.3 2D, 2.5D, and 3D integration

- **2D/MCM** places chiplets on a common organic substrate. It is the cheapest assembly, but 100+ µm bump pitch limits shoreline bandwidth, and 10–25 mm traces at 32 GT/s demand careful signal integrity work.
- **2.5D** inserts a silicon interposer (TSMC CoWoS) or embedded bridges (Intel EMIB, now EMIB-T with TSVs for power delivery [10]) between the chiplets and the substrate. Fine-pitch RDL (<2 µm lines) supports 45 µm — and soon 25 µm — bump pitch, pushing bandwidth density past 1 TB/s/mm². The price is a large, expensive silicon interposer whose own yield and cost must be modeled [7].
- **3D** stacks chiplets face-to-face with hybrid bonding at pitches approaching 1 µm (UCIe-3D targets ≤4 GT/s at these pitches [3]). Bandwidth density and energy improve by another order of magnitude, but thermal coupling becomes severe: a hot logic die stacked under memory chokes both.

### 4.4 Known-good-die economics and yield mathematics

The economic case for chiplets is a yield argument. Let defect density be $D_0$ (defects per unit area) and die area $A$. Three classical models describe die yield $Y$:

1. **Poisson:** $Y = e^{-D_0 A}$ — defects uniformly random; pessimistic for large die.
2. **Murphy:** $Y = \left(\frac{1 - e^{-D_0 A}}{D_0 A}\right)^2$ — defect density varies across the wafer; the industry workhorse.
3. **Negative binomial:** $Y = \left(1 + \frac{D_0 A}{\alpha}\right)^{-\alpha}$ — clustered defects with clustering parameter $\alpha$.

All three are *superlinear* in their punishment of area: doubling die area more than halves yield once $D_0 A \gtrsim 1$. The cost per *good* die is $C_{\text{good}} = C_{\text{wafer}} / (N_{\text{dies}} \cdot Y)$, so a reticle-limited 800 mm² die at 5 nm can cost several times more per good unit than four 200 mm² chiplets covering the same function, even after adding the D2D PHY area overhead (~10% in AMD's first EPYC MCM [8]) and packaging cost.

The quantitative literature confirms the crossover. The "Chiplet Actuary" model [7] finds that at 5 nm, die-defect cost exceeds 50% of a monolithic 800 mm² SoC's manufacturing cost, and multi-chiplet MCM assembly delivers up to ~35% total savings; at mature 14 nm the savings shrink because D2D plus packaging overhead (≥25% for MCM, ≥50% for 2.5D) eats the yield gain. AMD's own disclosures are starker: its quad-chiplet EPYC design cost ~0.59× the monolithic equivalent despite 10% more total silicon [8], and a hypothetical monolithic 16-core 7 nm Ryzen would have cost 2.1× the two-CCD chiplet implementation [8]. Three structural effects drive the result:

- **Yield:** small dice yield better (the math above).
- **Node binning:** only logic rides the expensive leading node; I/O, analog, and SRAM stay on cheaper mature nodes.
- **Reuse:** one validated chiplet (a CCD, an HBM controller, a UCIe PHY) ships across many products, amortizing NRE and mask costs.

The countervailing costs — *known-good-die* (KGD) testing (each chiplet must be fully tested *before* assembly, since a bad die bonded into a package scraps the good ones), advanced packaging, and D2D PHY area/power — set the crossover point. The Actuary model places it near 400 mm² at 5 nm: below that, monolithic wins; above it, chiplets win increasingly [7]. Finer granularity has diminishing returns: going from 3 to 5 chiplets saves <10% more at 800 mm² while raising overhead [7].

### 4.5 Co-design of NoC with die-to-die links

A chiplet's on-chip network must now treat a D2D link as a *first-class, but expensive, port*. The co-design principles:

1. **Bandwidth matching.** If the NoC's bisection bandwidth into the D2D adapter exceeds the link's sustained throughput, the adapter becomes a congestion collapse point; if it is lower, shoreline is wasted. UCIe's multi-module links (up to 4× x64 at 32 GT/s) exist precisely to let architects size the pipe to the NoC.
2. **Latency budgeting.** A D2D hop adds PHY serialization + adapter (CRC/retry) + package flight time. Measured D2D latencies are <2 ns for the raw PHY [6], but the full adapter round-trip for a retried flit can reach tens of nanoseconds — still an order of magnitude below off-package PCIe (~100 ns+), yet fatal for fine-grained cache-coherence traffic. Hence: keep coherence domains on-die; ship *coarse* transactions (DMA, message passing, CXL.mem) across chiplets.
3. **Flow-control continuity.** Credits must span the NoC↔adapter boundary (UCIe's FDI exposes credit management [3]) or head-of-line blocking at the adapter stalls the entire NoC.
4. **Power gating.** D2D PHYs burn static power per lane; the sideband's always-on domain [3] lets the mainband sleep while remaining discoverable — the package-level analog of PCIe L1 substates.
5. **Topology awareness.** Package routing is Manhattan on an interposer; placing high-traffic chiplet pairs adjacent minimizes both latency and interposer RDL congestion. EMIB-T's approach of confining routing within the D2D region [10] shows packaging and floorplanning co-evolving.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We validate the bandwidth-density claims by independent arithmetic and compile reported silicon evidence:

- **GUC 32 Gb/s UCIe silicon** (TSMC N3P + CoWoS): 5 Tb/s/mm full-duplex (10 Tb/s/mm unidirectional) bandwidth density with wide eye openings at BER $10^{-15}$ — the first public 32 GT/s UCIe PHY silicon [3].
- **Sarcina UCIe-S packaging IP**: simulated eye contours outside the mask at full 32 GT/s, BER $10^{-15}$, scaling to 256-bit+ shoreline [3].
- **AMD EPYC (Infinity Fabric, pre-UCIe)**: 0.59× cost vs. monolithic at 213 mm² chiplets, 14 nm [8] — the economic proof-of-concept the standard later generalized.
- **AMD RDNA3 (Navi 31)**: 5.4 TB/s cumulative Infinity Fanout bandwidth between GCD and six MCDs over organic fan-out, demonstrating TB/s-class D2D on *standard* packaging [8].

> **Theorem (Crossover):** *For defect density $D_0$ and packaging overhead fraction $p$, there exists a die area $A^*$ above which chiplet assembly is strictly cheaper than monolithic integration. $A^*$ decreases with $D_0$ and increases with $p$.*

*Proof sketch.* Monolithic cost per good die $C_m(A) = C_w/(N(A)Y(A))$ grows superlinearly in $A$ because $Y$ falls superlinearly (Murphy/negative-binomial). Chiplet cost $C_c(A) = k \cdot C_w/(N(A/k)Y(A/k)) \cdot (1+p)$ grows only mildly superlinearly in $A/k$ per chiplet. Since $Y(A) < Y(A/k)^k$ for clustered defects (Jensen-type inequality on the convex $-\log Y$), $C_m$ eventually dominates; continuity gives the crossing $A^*$. ∎

## 6 Limitations

1. **The marketplace is not yet real.** UCIe defines compliance tests [3], but true mix-and-match across vendors requires aligned *bump maps, voltages, ESD, and test access* — the "soft" interoperability work is incomplete, and BoW's history shows flexible standards drift toward bilateral agreements [6].
2. **KGD test cost is under-modeled.** Most cost analyses assume mature probe/test flows; at 25 µm pitch, known-good-die testing of every chiplet before bonding is a hard, expensive requirement.
3. **Thermal and power delivery.** 3D stacks and dense 2.5D assemblies concentrate heat; EMIB-T's TSV power delivery [10] helps but adds process complexity. UCIe's <2 ns, sub-0.5 pJ/bit figures assume benign thermal corners.
4. **Latency for fine-grained sharing.** D2D links cannot yet carry last-level-cache-coherent traffic at on-die latencies; coherence stays on-die, which constrains how finely systems may be disaggregated.
5. **Security.** A multi-vendor SiP widens the trust boundary; UCIe 2.0's manageability fabric [3] is a start, but attestation and secure provisioning across chiplets remain open problems.
6. **Capacity.** Advanced packaging (CoWoS-class) is supply-constrained, with lead times reported up to 18 months — an economic risk no PHY standard can fix.

## 7 Conclusion

UCIe represents the maturation of die-to-die interconnect from proprietary art to open infrastructure: it preserves AIB's proven PHY lineage, absorbs BoW's lessons on simplicity and cost, and adds the missing pieces — adapter-level reliability, protocol reuse, a software model, and compliance. The yield mathematics and silicon evidence jointly show that above a few hundred mm² at leading nodes, heterogeneous integration is not merely competitive but *structurally* cheaper, and the NoC↔D2D co-design rules derived here give architects a practical framework for spending their shoreline budget. The remaining work is less electrical than institutional: turning a standard into a marketplace. If the consortium sustains compliance rigor through the 3.0 generation's 64 GT/s and 3D extensions, the chiplet — not the reticle-limited die — becomes the atomic unit of system design.
