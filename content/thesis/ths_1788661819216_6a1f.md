# Mixture-of-Experts at Trillion-Parameter Scale: Sparse Routing, Expert Parallelism, Capacity Factors, and Load-Balancing Objectives in Switch Transformers

## Abstract

Scaling neural language models beyond one trillion parameters is bottlenecked by the linear coupling between parameter count and per-token floating-point operations. This thesis develops a rigorous treatment of sparsely-gated Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures at trillion-parameter scale, centered on the Switch Transformer design and its antecedents in GShard, sparsely-gated MoE, and BASE layers. We formalize top-1 routing as a constrained conditional-computation operator, derive the expert-capacity factor as a static-shape enabler for distributed execution, and analyze the differentiable load-balancing auxiliary objective that prevents routing collapse. The thesis further dissects expert parallelism: the dispatch/combine all-to-all communication pattern, its asymptotic cost model, and its composition with data and tensor parallelism. Empirical evidence — including the 1.6-trillion-parameter Switch-C model trained on 2048 TPU cores with a 4–7× speedup over dense T5-XXL baselines — is examined alongside theoretical results on routing stability and communication lower bounds. We conclude with open problems: token dropping, inference-time imbalance, router z-loss, and auxiliary-loss-free balancing strategies in modern MoE systems.

## 1. Introduction

The last decade of deep learning has been governed by a simple empirical law: *model quality improves predictably with parameter count*, provided that data and compute scale commensurately [1]. Yet dense Transformers couple these two axes of scale inseparably. In a dense network, doubling the parameters roughly doubles the floating-point operations (FLOPs) required per token, the memory bandwidth consumed at inference, and the energy expended per prediction. Beyond a certain point, this coupling becomes the dominant engineering constraint: a 1.6-trillion-parameter dense Transformer would demand per-token compute budgets that no accelerator cluster can provision within reasonable latency or cost envelopes.

Sparsely-gated **Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)** architectures break this coupling. The central idea is *conditional computation*: for each input token, a learned router selects a small subset of "expert" sub-networks to execute, while the remaining experts sit idle. Total parameter capacity grows with the number of experts \(N\), but per-token FLOPs grow only with the number of *activated* experts \(k \ll N\).

This thesis examines the full machinery required to realize that promise at scale, organized around the **Switch Transformer** architecture [2], which simplified sparse routing to its minimal viable form — routing each token to exactly *one* expert (\(k = 1\)) — and demonstrated a 1.6-trillion-parameter model trained at the per-token compute of a far smaller dense model. Concretely, we develop:

1. **A formal model of sparse top-\(k\) routing** as a constrained conditional-computation operator, from the noisy top-\(k\) gating of Shazeer et al. [3] through GShard's top-2 Transformer MoE [4] to the top-1 simplification of Switch [2].
2. **The expert capacity factor** as the mechanism that converts dynamic, data-dependent routing into statically-shaped tensors amenable to accelerator execution, with a precise accounting of token dropping and padding.
3. **The differentiable load-balancing auxiliary loss**, its gradient dynamics, and its role as much a *systems* feature (balanced network traffic, no stragglers) as a modeling one.
4. **Expert parallelism (EP)**: the dispatch/combine all-to-all communication pattern, an asymptotic cost model, and composition with data, tensor, and pipeline parallelism into 4D parallelism strategies.
5. **Comparative alternatives**: BASE layers' linear-assignment formulation [5], Expert Choice routing, hash routing, and the router z-loss of ST-MoE.

Throughout, we distinguish three intertwined concerns that sparse MoE forces into one design: the *statistical* problem (do experts specialize usefully?), the *optimization* problem (does training stay stable and balanced?), and the *systems* problem (can 2048 accelerators execute it without drowning in communication?). The central claim of this thesis is that Switch Transformer's contribution is best understood not as a single trick but as a **coherent systems contract** — *router + expert parallelism + capacity/balance + all-to-all* — that every subsequent large-scale MoE system inherits.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Conditional Computation and Early Sparse Gating

The intellectual lineage of sparse MoE begins well before Transformers. Jacobs et al. (1991) introduced adaptive mixtures of local experts, establishing that a gating network could partition input space among specialist sub-models. The modern revival arrived with Shazeer et al. [3], who applied *noisy top-\(k\) gating* to LSTM layers and trained a 137-billion-parameter model on a single machine — demonstrating that massive capacity could be added with nearly constant per-token compute.

The noisy top-\(k\) mechanism deserves precise statement. Given an input representation \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and \(N\) experts, the gate computes logits \(h(x) = W_g x + \epsilon \odot \text{softplus}(W_n x)\), where \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) injects per-expert noise that smooths the hard top-\(k\) selection and aids load balancing. Only the top \(k\) logits survive:

\[
G(x)_i = \text{softmax}(\text{KeepTopK}(h(x), k))_i, \qquad y = \sum_{i=1}^{N} G(x)_i \, E_i(x).
\]

### 2.2 GShard: Transformer MoE at 600 Billion Parameters

GShard [4] embedded top-2 MoE feed-forward layers into the Transformer and trained a 600-billion-parameter multilingual translation model across 2048 TPU v3 cores using automatic sharding annotations. It fixed the canonical execution chain that all later systems follow:

> **Definition 1 (MoE execution chain).** A Transformer MoE layer executes the sequence: *Attention → Router → Dispatch All-to-All → Expert FFN → Combine All-to-All.*

GShard contributed three durable mechanisms:

- **Expert capacity**: a hard bound \(C = \lceil c \cdot kS/N \rceil\) on tokens per expert, where \(S\) is the batch token count, \(k\) the routing multiplicity, \(N\) the expert count, and \(c\) the capacity factor. Tokens exceeding capacity are *dropped* (passed through via the residual connection).
- **Auxiliary load-balancing loss** \(\ell_{\text{aux}}\), a differentiable surrogate for routing imbalance (Section 3.3).
- **Random routing**: when the second expert's gate weight is small, the token is dispatched to only the top-1 expert with probability proportional to that weight, conserving capacity.

### 2.3 Switch Transformer: The Top-1 Simplification

Fedus, Zoph, and Shazeer [2] asked a minimalist question: *what if \(k = 1\)?* The Switch layer routes each token to its single highest-scoring expert:

\[
p_i(x) = \text{softmax}(\text{Top1}(W_r x))_i, \qquad y = p_{i^\star}(x) \, E_{i^\star}(x), \;\; i^\star = \arg\max_i (W_r x)_i.
\]

 Combined with selective float32 precision in the router, a 10× reduced initialization scale, and expert-only dropout, Switch scaled to **Switch-C: 1.6 trillion parameters, 2048 experts**, achieving a 4–7× pretraining speedup over dense T5-XXL at matched compute.

### 2.4 Alternatives: BASE Layers, Expert Choice, Hash Routing, ST-MoE

Several contemporaneous designs explored the routing design space from different angles:

| Approach | Routing rule | Balance guarantee | Key trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| BASE layers [5] | Token→expert via linear assignment | Exact (balanced by construction) | Batch-level joint solve; awkward for autoregressive decoding |
| Expert Choice | Experts select top-\(C\) tokens | Exact per-expert capacity | Variable sequence composition; decode-unfriendly |
| Hash layers | Deterministic hash of token | Perfect (by construction) | No learned specialization |
| ST-MoE | Top-\(k\) + router z-loss | Soft (auxiliary loss) | z-loss stabilizes logits but is a distinct mechanism from balance loss |

**ST-MoE** introduced the *router z-loss*, \(\ell_z = \frac{1}{S}\sum_s (\log \sum_i e^{z_{s,i}})^2\), which penalizes large router logits and controls numerical scale — a stability mechanism distinct from the balance loss, though frequently conflated [2][5].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Formal Model of Top-\(k\) Sparse Routing

We model a sparse MoE layer as a tuple \((\mathcal{R}, \{E_i\}_{i=1}^N, \mathcal{D}, \mathcal{C})\) where \(\mathcal{R}: \mathbb{R}^d \to \Delta^{N-1}\) is the router, each \(E_i: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d\) is an expert (typically a two-layer FFN), \(\mathcal{D}\) is the dispatch operator, and \(\mathcal{C}\) the combine operator. For a batch of tokens \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times d}\), the router produces a score matrix \(Z = XW_r^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times N}\).

The top-\(k\) selection induces a sparse assignment matrix \(A \in \{0,1\}^{S \times N}\) with row sums equal to \(k\):

\[
A_{s,i} = \mathbb{1}\{i \in \text{TopK}(Z_{s,:}, k)\}, \qquad G_{s,i} = \frac{e^{Z_{s,i}} A_{s,i}}{\sum_j e^{Z_{s,j}} A_{s,j}}.
\]

The layer output is \(Y_s = x_s + \sum_i G_{s,i} E_i(x_s)\) for tokens surviving capacity, plus a residual bypass for dropped tokens. 

> **Theorem 1 (Capacity–compute decoupling).** *For a sparse MoE layer with \(N\) experts each of \(P\) parameters and routing multiplicity \(k\), the total parameter count is \(\Theta(NP)\) while the per-token expert FLOPs are \(\Theta(kP)\), independent of \(N\). The router adds \(\Theta(Nd)\) FLOPs per token.*

*Proof sketch.* Each token is processed by exactly \(k\) experts, each costing \(\Theta(P)\) operations, hence \(\Theta(kP)\). The router's matrix-vector product \(W_r x\) costs \(\Theta(Nd)\). All \(N\) experts' parameters must be resident (or addressable), giving \(\Theta(NP)\) capacity. ∎

### 3.2 Expert Capacity and the Capacity Factor

Dynamic routing produces irregular per-expert token counts: expert \(i\) receives \(t_i = \sum_s A_{s,i}\) tokens, a data-dependent quantity unknown at graph-compile time. Accelerators (TPUs especially) require statically-shaped tensors, so the system imposes a hard capacity:

\[
\text{cap}_i = \left\lceil c \cdot \frac{kS}{N} \right\rceil \quad \forall i,
\]

where \(c \geq 1\) is the **capacity factor**. Tokens are assigned to experts in an arbitrary but deterministic order (typically row-major over the batch); once expert \(i\)'s buffer of \(\text{cap}_i\) slots fills, subsequent tokens routed to \(i\) are **dropped** — they skip the expert FFN and flow through the residual connection unchanged.

The capacity factor parameterizes a three-way trade-off:

- \(c = 1.0\): maximally compute-efficient (no padding), but drops the most tokens under imbalance.
- \(c = 1.25\): the standard practical compromise (GShard, Switch); drops well under 1% of tokens with the auxiliary loss active.
- \(c \geq 1.5\): drops almost nothing but wastes substantial compute and memory on padding tokens.

A reference implementation of top-1 routing with capacity enforcement:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def switch_moe_layer(x, router_w, experts, capacity_factor=1.25):
    # x: (S, d); router_w: (N, d); experts: list of N FFN modules
    S, d = x.shape
    N = len(experts)
    logits = x @ router_w.T                          # (S, N) router scores
    probs = F.softmax(logits.float(), dim=-1)        # fp32 softmax for stability
    top1_prob, top1_idx = probs.max(dim=-1)          # (S,), (S,)

    cap = int(torch.ceil(torch.tensor(capacity_factor * S / N))).item()
    out = x.clone()                                  # residual bypass for dropped
    for i, expert in enumerate(experts):
        mask = (top1_idx == i).nonzero(as_tuple=True)[0]
        kept, dropped = mask[:cap], mask[cap:]        # hard capacity: drop overflow
        if kept.numel():
            out[kept] = x[kept] + expert(x[kept])    # gate = 1 under top-1
        # dropped tokens keep residual x (soft failure, hard to observe)
    return out, top1_prob, top1_idx
```

### 3.3 The Load-Balancing Auxiliary Objective

Without intervention, routers *collapse*: they discover that routing every token to one or two experts minimizes the task loss locally, starving the remaining experts of gradients and overflowing their capacity buffers. The standard remedy is a differentiable auxiliary loss added to the task objective:

\[
\mathcal{L} = \ell_{\text{task}} + \alpha \, \ell_{\text{aux}}, \qquad \alpha \approx 10^{-2}.
\]

The Switch/GShard form couples two per-expert statistics over a batch of \(S\) tokens:

- \(f_i = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s} A_{s,i}\): fraction of tokens dispatched to expert \(i\) (**non-differentiable**, from the hard top-\(k\));
- \(P_i = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s} p_{s,i}\): mean router probability assigned to expert \(i\) (**differentiable**).

\[
\ell_{\text{aux}} = N \sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i \, P_i.
\]

> **Theorem 2 (Balance-loss optimum).** *For fixed routing decisions, \(\ell_{\text{aux}}\) is minimized when \(P_i = 1/N\) for all \(i\) under the constraint \(\sum_i P_i = 1\); the minimum value equals the mean dispatched fraction \(\bar f\). The loss is scale-invariant in the absolute token counts.*

*Proof sketch.* By Cauchy–Schwarz, \(\sum_i f_i P_i \geq (\sum_i \sqrt{f_i P_i})^2 / N\)... more directly: treating \(f\) as fixed, \(\sum_i f_i P_i\) subject to \(\sum_i P_i = 1, P_i \geq 0\) is a linear program whose optimum places all mass on \(\arg\min_i f_i\). The *gradient* \(\nabla_{z} \ell_{\text{aux}} = N \sum_i f_i \nabla_z P_i\) pushes probability mass *away* from experts with large dispatched fractions \(f_i\), which over training iterations drives the joint distribution \((f, P)\) toward uniformity. At the uniform fixed point \(f_i = P_i = 1/N\), \(\ell_{\text{aux}} = 1/N \cdot N = 1\)... (normalized) — the loss cannot distinguish perfect balance from degenerate lock-in, which is why \(\alpha\) must stay small. ∎

The engineering subtlety: \(f_i\) is computed *without* gradient flow (it derives from the discrete argmax), while \(P_i\) carries the gradient. This makes \(\ell_{\text{aux}}\) a *biased but effective* steering signal — it penalizes the router for assigning high probability to experts that actually received many tokens, breaking the positive feedback loop of collapse.

```python
def load_balancing_loss(router_probs, expert_indices, num_experts, alpha=0.01):
    # router_probs: (S, N) softmax probabilities; expert_indices: (S,) top-1 choices
    S, N = router_probs.shape
    # f_i: fraction of tokens dispatched to expert i (no grad)
    one_hot = F.one_hot(expert_indices, num_classes=N).float()
    f = one_hot.mean(dim=0)            # (N,) dispatched fractions
    # P_i: mean router probability per expert (differentiable)
    P = router_probs.mean(dim=0)       # (N,)
    aux = alpha * N * (f * P).sum()    # Switch/GShard balance objective
    return aux
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Expert Parallelism and the All-to-All Dispatch Pattern

When \(N\) exceeds the experts that fit on one device (Switch-C used 2048 experts on 2048 TPU cores — one expert per core), experts must be *sharded* across devices. **Expert parallelism (EP)** assigns disjoint subsets of experts to ranks; with data parallelism (DP) layered on top, each DP rank holds a full replica of the EP layout.

The per-layer communication pattern is then forced:

1. **Router** (local): each rank computes routing decisions for its local tokens.
2. **Dispatch all-to-all**: tokens are permuted so that all tokens assigned to expert \(i\) land on the rank owning \(i\). This is a single `all_to_all` collective over a \([S_{\text{local}}, d]\) tensor.
3. **Expert FFN** (local): each rank runs its experts on the received token buffers.
4. **Combine all-to-all**: expert outputs are permuted back to the originating ranks and weighted by gate values.

> **Theorem 3 (All-to-all communication lower bound).** *For a batch of \(S\) tokens of dimension \(d\) with routing multiplicity \(k\), any EP implementation requires \(\Omega(kSd)\) elements moved per MoE layer in the dispatch phase and \(\Omega(kSd)\) in the combine phase, regardless of the number of ranks.*

*Proof sketch.* Each of the \(S\) tokens must physically reach the device(s) hosting its \(k\) assigned experts; the token representation has \(d\) elements. In the worst case all tokens route to experts on a single remote rank, forcing \(kSd\) elements across the interconnect. The combine phase is symmetric. ∎

### 4.2 Capacity, Dropping, and Padding: A Quantitative Account

Let us quantify the capacity mechanism for Switch-C-scale configurations. With \(S = 2^{20}\) tokens per batch step, \(N = 2048\), \(k = 1\), and \(c = 1.25\):

\[
\text{cap} = \left\lceil 1.25 \cdot \frac{2^{20}}{2048} \right\rceil = \lceil 640 \rceil = 640 \text{ slots per expert}.
\]

| Capacity factor \(c\) | Buffer overhead | Typical drop rate (with aux loss) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | 0% | 2–5% | Most efficient; noticeable dropping |
| 1.25 | 25% | <1% | Standard choice (GShard, Switch) |
| 1.50 | 50% | ≈0% | Wasteful padding; used in some inference settings |
| 2.00 | 100% | ≈0% | Effectively disables dropping; doubles expert FLOPs |

### 4.3 Router Design: Precision, Initialization, and Dropout

Switch identified three training-stability techniques that proved as important as the routing algorithm itself [2]:

1. **Selective precision**: router logits and the softmax are computed in **float32** even when the rest of the model trains in bfloat16. With \(N = 2048\) experts, the softmax denominator sums 2048 exponentials; in bfloat16 the dynamic range collapses and routing decisions become numerically arbitrary. The router's activations are a tiny fraction of total memory, so the cost is negligible.
2. **Reduced initialization scale**: router weights are initialized at \(0.1\times\) the standard scale. Large initial logits produce near-deterministic routing before experts have learned anything useful, locking in arbitrary specialization; small logits keep early routing near-uniform so experts differentiate gradually.
3. **Expert dropout**: dropout is applied at *expert granularity* (higher rate inside experts, lower elsewhere), regularizing the specialists without disrupting the router's statistics.

### 4.4 Composition: 4D Parallelism (DP × EP × TP × PP)

Production MoE training composes expert parallelism with the three classical axes:

- **Data parallelism (DP)**: replicates the full EP layout; gradients are all-reduced across DP ranks. The auxiliary-loss token counts \(f_i\) are typically all-reduced across the DP group so the balance signal reflects the *global* batch.
- **Tensor parallelism (TP)**: shards individual experts' matrices *within* a rank (used when a single expert exceeds device memory). EP and TP compose as nested sharding: EP selects which experts, TP splits each expert.
- **Pipeline parallelism (PP)**: assigns different layers to different device groups; MoE layers are simply stages with heavier all-to-all phases.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Theoretical Analysis

### 5.1 The Switch-C Result: 1.6 Trillion Parameters

The headline empirical result of [2] remains the strongest validation of sparse scaling: **Switch-C**, with 2048 experts per MoE layer and 1.6 trillion total parameters, trained on 2048 TPU v3 cores. Key reported outcomes:

- **4–7× pretraining speedup** over dense T5-XXL at matched per-token compute, reaching higher SuperGLUE scores in less wall-clock time.
- Token drop rates below 1% with capacity factor 1.25 and the auxiliary loss active.

### 5.2 Theoretical Analysis: Stability and Specialization

Three theoretical lenses clarify *why* the Switch design works:

1. **Routing as competitive learning.** The (router, experts) pair implements a competitive-learning dynamic: experts compete for tokens via router scores, and winners receive the gradients that improve them. The balance loss acts as a *homeostatic* regularizer, preventing winner-take-all lock-in — analogous to conscience mechanisms in classical competitive learning (DeSieno, 1988).
2. **Capacity as implicit regularization.** Token dropping is not merely a systems hack; it imposes a hard constraint that the router must route *within* a load envelope, penalizing over-concentration. The residual bypass for dropped tokens keeps gradients flowing, making dropping a *soft* failure mode rather than a hard error.

### 5.3 Modern Descendants: What Changed After Switch

- **Mixtral 8×7B** [7]: 8 experts, top-2 per token, 47B total / ~13B active — every layer MoE, proving coarse-grained MoE in open-weight decoder LLMs.
- **DeepSeek-V2/V3** [8]: fine-grained experts (160+ routed), shared experts, and *auxiliary-loss-free* balancing via learned per-expert bias terms — addressing the quality degradation that balance losses can induce.
- **ST-MoE**: router z-loss for numerical stability, adopted widely as a complement to (not replacement of) the balance loss.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Token dropping is a silent quality tax.** Dropped tokens receive degraded FFN processing with no signal to the user or the loss. At inference time, a single adversarial or out-of-distribution batch can spike drop rates unpredictably. *Open:* learned, input-adaptive capacity; dropping-aware training objectives.
2. **Inference-time imbalance.** The auxiliary loss balances *training* batches; autoregressive decoding processes one token at a time, where per-expert load is inherently bursty. *Open:* decode-time expert caching, speculative routing, and the auxiliary-loss-free bias methods pioneered by DeepSeek-V3 [8].
3. **Memory wall: all experts must be resident.** Conditional computation saves FLOPs, not memory — every expert's parameters must be addressable. A 1.6T-parameter model needs ~3.2TB of parameter memory in bfloat16, forcing expert offloading or massive device counts at serving time. *Open:* expert pruning, quantization-aware routing, and hierarchical expert retrieval.
5. **Specialization is emergent and unverified.** Despite the MoE narrative, rigorous evidence that experts learn *semantically coherent* specializations (rather than arbitrary load partitions) remains thin. *Open:* mechanistic interpretability of expert function, routing-probe methodologies.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The Switch Transformer demonstrated that a radical simplification — routing each token to exactly one expert — could scale neural networks to 1.6 trillion parameters while holding per-token compute fixed, delivering 4–7× speedups over dense baselines [2]. This thesis has argued that the achievement rests on four interlocking mechanisms, each addressing one facet of the sparse-execution problem: **top-1 routing** minimizes communication and kernel complexity; the **capacity factor** converts dynamic routing into statically-shaped, accelerator-friendly tensors at the cost of a principled, bounded dropping policy; the **load-balancing auxiliary loss** provides the differentiable steering signal that prevents routing collapse; and **expert parallelism with all-to-all dispatch/combine** supplies the distributed execution substrate, composable into 4D parallelism at thousand-device scale.
