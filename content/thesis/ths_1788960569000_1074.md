# Simultaneous Localization and Mapping with Factor Graphs: Incremental Smoothing, Bayes Trees, and Robust Loop Closure for Lifelong Robot Navigation

## Abstract

Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) is the central estimation problem of mobile robotics: a robot must recover its own trajectory and a consistent map of its environment from noisy, partial sensor measurements. This thesis develops the modern *factor-graph* formulation of SLAM as maximum a posteriori inference over a sparse bipartite graphical model, and analyzes the incremental smoothing algorithms that make real-time, lifelong operation possible. Through the lens of the **Bayes tree**, we show that nonlinear factor-graph optimization can be updated exactly and incrementally: new measurements touch only the top of the tree, enabling fluid relinearization and incremental variable reordering without periodic batch resets — the iSAM2 algorithm [1]. We then extend the framework to *robust* loop closure, the Achilles' heel of long-term autonomy, where a single spurious place-recognition match can corrupt an entire map: switchable-constraint relaxations and graduated non-convexity (GNC) reject gross outliers without an initial guess [3][4], grounded in the Black–Rangarajan duality between robust costs and outlier processes. Finally, we identify the frontier of lifelong SLAM — bounded-computation memory management, certified optimality, and metric-semantic representations [5][6].

## 1 Introduction

A robot operating without external positioning infrastructure must solve two problems at once: *where am I?* and *what does the world look like?* SLAM is the coupled estimation of a robot's pose sequence and environmental features from noisy sensor measurements. Classical filters maintained only the *current* pose; the modern consensus is that retaining the full trajectory and re-optimizing it — *smoothing* — yields better accuracy with better scaling, because smoothing matrices are sparser than filtering ones [1].

The unifying mathematical language of contemporary SLAM is the **factor graph**. A factor graph factorizes a joint probability density into a product of local factors, each depending on a small subset of variables:

$$\Phi(\Theta) = \prod_i \phi_i(\Theta_i), \quad \phi_i(\Theta_i) \propto \exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|h_i(\Theta_i) - z_i\right\|_{\Sigma_i}^2\right)$$

with measurement functions $h_i$, observations $z_i$, and covariances $\Sigma_i$ [2]. MAP inference then reduces to nonlinear least squares:

$$\mathbf{\Theta}^* = \arg\min_{\mathbf{\Theta}} \sum_i \left\|h_i(\Theta_i) - z_i\right\|_{\Sigma_i}^2.$$

This formulation is deceivingly simple. Three hard problems lurk within it, and they structure this thesis:

1. **Incrementality.** Measurements arrive sequentially at tens of hertz. Re-solving the full nonlinear least-squares problem from scratch at every step is infeasible for maps of $10^4$–$10^6$ variables. We need updates that cost a fraction of a batch solve.
2. **Nonlinearity and ordering.** Linearization points drift and variable orderings decay in quality; periodic "batch resets" destroy the real-time guarantee. We need *fluid* relinearization and *incremental* reordering.
3. **Robustness.** Least squares is catastrophically sensitive to outliers — a single false loop closure can fold a map in half. We need estimation that survives gross corruption of the data-association front end.

Our approach follows the iSAM2 lineage [1]: interpret incremental sparse matrix factorization as *editing operations on a graphical model*, the **Bayes tree**. This probabilistic reinterpretation converts abstract linear-algebra updates into local, inspectable tree surgeries, and reveals exactly which parts of the problem must be recomputed when new data arrives. Combined with robust estimation via graduated non-convexity [3], the result is a SLAM back end suitable for *lifelong* operation — the goal articulated by the robust-perception agenda [5].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Filtering to Smoothing

Early SLAM was dominated by the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF), which maintains a Gaussian belief over the *current* pose and map. The EKF marginalizes past poses at every step, which (a) permanently fixes linearization points, causing inconsistency, and (b) induces dense fill-in in the information matrix, yielding $O(n^2)$–$O(n^3)$ costs. Smoothing — keeping the entire trajectory $\mathcal{X} = \{x_0, \dots, x_T\}$ and all landmarks $\mathcal{L}$ as explicit variables — was initially viewed as *more* expensive. The key insight, articulated in [2], is that smoothing matrices are naturally sparse: each measurement involves only a handful of variables, so with a good elimination ordering the sparse Cholesky factor $R$ of the information matrix stays sparse, and factorization is far below cubic cost.

### 2.2 Factor Graphs and Nonlinear Least Squares

A factor graph is a bipartite graph $G = (\mathcal{F}, \Theta, \mathcal{E})$ with factor nodes $\mathcal{F}$, variable nodes $\Theta$, and edges $\mathcal{E}$ connecting factors to their variables [2]. The SLAM factor graph contains:

- *Prior factors* $\phi_0(x_0)$ anchoring the gauge (SLAM is defined only up to a global rigid transform);
- *Odometry / motion factors* $\phi_t(x_{t-1}, x_t)$ from wheel encoders, IMUs, or scan matching;
- *Landmark observation factors* $\phi_{tk}(x_t, \ell_k)$ from cameras, lidar, or sonar;
- *Loop-closure factors* $\phi_{ij}(x_i, x_j)$ from place recognition, which create the long-range correlations that make SLAM hard and valuable.

MAP inference proceeds by Gauss–Newton or Levenberg–Marquardt iteration: linearize each factor, solve the sparse system $A\,\delta = b$ via QR/Cholesky, and retract onto the $SE(2)/SE(3)$ manifold [2]. Cost is dominated by the sparse factorization, governed by the *elimination ordering* (e.g., COLAMD).

### 2.3 The Incremental Challenge

Naive incremental SLAM re-factorizes the entire system on every new measurement. The original iSAM algorithm [1] (predecessor work) updated the square-root information matrix $R$ incrementally via Givens rotations, reusing previously computed components. But it suffered two defects: loop-closing trajectories demanded *periodic variable reordering* to prevent catastrophic fill-in, and nonlinearities demanded *periodic full relinearization* to prevent inconsistency — together, a periodic batch reset that violated the online contract. Resolving both defects without batch steps is the contribution we develop next.

> **Theorem:** *For a SLAM factor graph with an elimination ordering whose induced fill-in is bounded, the marginal posterior of any variable can be recovered from the cliques on the path from its containing clique to the root of the corresponding Bayes tree, independently of the rest of the tree [1].*

This locality property is the foundation of everything that follows.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Bayes Tree

The Bayes tree is a directed clique tree encoding the factored posterior $p(\Theta \mid \mathcal{Z}) = \prod_k p(F_k \mid S_k)$, where each clique $C_k = (F_k : S_k)$ stores the conditional density of its *frontal* variables $F_k$ given its *separator* $S_k$ [1]. It is obtained from the factor graph in two conceptual steps:

1. **Elimination.** Choose an ordering $\pi$ of $\Theta$. Eliminate variables one at a time: for variable $\theta_j$, collect all factors mentioning it, form the joint on $\theta_j$ and its neighbors, factor into $p(\theta_j \mid S_j)\,p(S_j)$, and push the marginal back into the remaining problem. This is variable elimination on the Gaussian (linearized) system, producing a chordal *Bayes net*.
2. **Clique formation.** Group variables eliminated together (maximal cliques of the chordal graph) into tree nodes; each node's separator is the intersection with its parent. The result maps *exactly* onto the rows of the square-root information matrix $R$: clique $k$'s conditional corresponds to a block-row of $R$ [1].

Unlike a classical junction tree, the Bayes tree is directed and its structure mirrors sparse triangular factorization — probability theory and numerical linear algebra in one data structure.

### 3.2 Incremental Update as Tree Editing

Suppose the robot moves and acquires new factors $\mathcal{F}'$ involving new variables $\Theta'$ and a few old ones. The iSAM2 update proceeds as follows [1]:

1. **Identify the affected subtree.** Find all Bayes-tree cliques containing any variable adjacent to $\mathcal{F}'$. Take the union of the paths from these cliques to the root: this *top of the tree* is the only region whose factorization can change. The subtrees hanging below are provably untouched — their conditional densities remain valid.
2. **Detach and re-eliminate.** Remove the affected cliques, recover their original factors plus $\mathcal{F}'$, and re-eliminate with a fresh ordering computed by *constrained* COLAMD restricted to the affected variables. Recent variables are forced last, which both bounds fill-in and keeps the update local.
3. **Reattach.** The untouched subtrees graft back onto the newly computed top. Back-substitution from the root then yields updated estimates for any variable on demand.

The consequence is dramatic: during pure exploration, each update touches $O(1)$ variables and costs effectively constant time; only loop closures — which genuinely correlate distant parts of the map — force larger recomputation, and even then only along the paths to the root. No periodic batch step is ever required.

### 3.3 Fluid Relinearization

Nonlinearity is handled not by scheduled global relinearization but by a *fluid* policy: each variable carries a linearization point, and a variable is marked for relinearization only when its current estimate deviates from that point by more than a threshold $\beta$ (e.g., $0.1$ rad/m in the reference implementation) [1]. Relinearization is folded into the tree-editing step: affected cliques are relinearized around current estimates when re-eliminated. Variables that have converged keep their linearizations indefinitely, so the per-step cost tracks the *change* in the problem, not its size. This is the precise sense in which iSAM2 is "fully incremental": ordering, factorization, and linearization are all maintained online, forever.

### 3.4 Complexity Analysis

| Algorithm | Update cost (exploration) | Update cost (loop closure) | Relinearization | Reordering |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Batch Gauss–Newton | $O(n^{1.5})$–$O(n^2)$ re-solve | $O(n^{1.5})$–$O(n^2)$ | full | full COLAMD |
| EKF-style filter | $O(n^2)$ (dense map block) | $O(n^2)$ | never (inconsistent) | n/a |
| iSAM (Givens) | $O(1)$–$O(n)$ | $O(n)$, periodic batch | periodic batch | periodic batch |
| **iSAM2 (Bayes tree)** | **$O(1)$ amortized** | **$O(\sqrt{n})$–$O(n)$ top-of-tree only** | **fluid, per-variable $\beta$** | **incremental, constrained COLAMD** |

The table summarizes the asymptotic landscape [1][2]. Exact constants depend on the environment's loop structure, but the qualitative gap — batch work *never* recurring in iSAM2 — is what enables lifelong operation.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Elimination Game: Why Ordering Is Everything

Sparse factorization cost is controlled by *fill-in*: eliminating variable $\theta_j$ connects all its currently-connected neighbors, creating new edges. A poor ordering turns a sparse SLAM graph into a dense factor; a good one (COLAMD, nested dissection) keeps $R$ sparse. In SLAM, the natural temporal ordering is nearly optimal during exploration — but loop closures connect temporally distant variables, and repeated incremental updates without reordering accumulate fill-in until the factor densifies [1]. iSAM2's constrained reordering re-optimizes only the affected top-of-tree, forcing recently touched variables last; this restores a near-optimal ordering and *localizes future updates*, because variables eliminated last sit near the root where edits are cheap.

### 4.2 From Factor Graphs to Bayes Nets to Trees: A Worked Micro-Example

Consider four poses $x_1,\dots,x_4$ with odometry factors $\phi_t(x_t, x_{t+1})$, a prior $\phi_0(x_1)$, and a landmark $\ell$ observed from $x_2$ and $x_4$ (a miniature loop closure). Eliminating in the order $\ell, x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4$:

1. Eliminating $\ell$ creates the conditional $p(\ell \mid x_2, x_4)$ — note the *new* dependency between $x_2$ and $x_4$ (fill-in edge), precisely the loop-closure correlation.
2. Eliminating $x_1$ yields $p(x_1 \mid x_2)$; $x_2$ yields $p(x_2 \mid x_3, x_4)$; $x_3$ yields $p(x_3 \mid x_4)$; $x_4$ yields the root marginal $p(x_4)$.

Grouping gives cliques $C_1 = (\ell : x_2, x_4)$, $C_2 = (x_1 : x_2)$, $C_3 = (x_2, x_3 : x_4)$, $C_4 = (x_4 : \emptyset)$, forming the Bayes tree [1]. Now a new odometry factor on $(x_4, x_5)$ touches only $C_4$'s subtree path — cliques $C_1, C_2, C_3$ are untouched. But a *new* loop closure between $x_1$ and $x_5$ would pull $C_2$'s path to the root into the affected set, re-eliminating the middle of the tree. This micro-example exhibits the general law: *the cost of an update equals the size of the affected top-of-tree*, and exploration keeps that top small.

```python
def isam2_update(bayes_tree, new_factors, beta=0.1):
    """One incremental iSAM2 step: edit only the top of the Bayes tree."""
    affected = set()
    for f in new_factors:                       # variables adjacent to new data
        affected |= cliques_containing(bayes_tree, f.variables)
    top = union_of_paths_to_root(bayes_tree, affected)
    # fluid relinearization: relinearize only drifted variables in the top
    for v in variables_of(top):
        if delta_norm(v) > beta:
            mark_relinearize(v)
    # detach, re-eliminate with constrained COLAMD, reattach
    sub = detach(bayes_tree, top)
    factors = original_factors(sub) + new_factors
    ordering = constrained_colamd(factors, force_last=recent_vars(factors))
    new_top = eliminate_to_bayes_tree(factors, ordering)
    graft(bayes_tree, new_top)                  # untouched subtrees reattach
    return backsubstitute(bayes_tree)           # root-down solve, on demand
```

### 4.3 Robust Loop Closure: When Data Association Lies

All of the above assumes correct data association. In practice, place recognition proposes loop closures, and some fraction are *wrong* — perceptual aliasing in repetitive environments guarantees it. Under the squared loss, a single false loop closure with residual $r$ contributes $\tfrac{1}{2}\|r\|^2_\Sigma$, which grows without bound; the optimizer will happily distort the entire trajectory to satisfy one hallucinated constraint. Classical defenses include:

- **M-estimators**, replacing $\tfrac{1}{2}r^2$ with a robust kernel $\rho(r)$ whose influence function saturates (Huber, Cauchy, Geman–McClure) or truncates (truncated least squares);
- **Switchable constraints**, augmenting each loop closure with a latent switch variable $s_j \in [0,1]$ and optimizing the joint problem — outliers are explained away by $s_j \to 0$ at the cost of a prior pulling switches toward 1;
- **Consensus maximization / RANSAC** on the front end, which scales poorly to the thousands of hypotheses of lifelong operation.

The Black–Rangarajan duality [3] unifies the first two: every robust kernel $\rho$ admits an *outlier-process* form

$$\min_{\Theta, \{w_i\}} \sum_i \left( \tfrac{1}{2} w_i\, r_i(\Theta)^2 + \Psi_\rho(w_i) \right),$$

where the weights $w_i \in [0,1]$ continuously interpolate between inlier ($w_i = 1$) and outlier ($w_i \to 0$), and $\Psi_\rho$ is the dual penalty determined by the kernel. Iteratively reweighted least squares then alternates between solving the weighted factor graph — *exactly the iSAM2 machinery* — and updating weights in closed form. Robustness becomes a reweighting schedule on top of incremental smoothing.

### 4.4 Graduated Non-Convexity: Robustness Without an Initial Guess

M-estimators and switch variables both introduce *new* non-convexities, so local solvers need a good initialization — precisely what a corrupted map cannot provide. Graduated non-convexity (GNC) sidesteps this with a homotopy: define a parametric family of surrogates $\rho_\mu$ with control parameter $\mu$ such that $\rho_\mu$ is convex for large $\mu$ and $\rho_\mu \to \rho$ as $\mu \to 0$; solve the sequence of problems while annealing $\mu$, warm-starting each stage from the last [3]. Combined with certifiably optimal *non-minimal* solvers (via semidefinite relaxations) for the outlier-free subproblems, GNC yields solvers that are empirically robust to 70–80% outliers, outperform RANSAC, and require no initial guess — demonstrated on point-cloud registration, pose-graph optimization, and object pose estimation [3]. The TEASER++ system instantiates this recipe: a truncated-least-squares formulation solved by GNC over certifiable subproblems, delivering fast, globally-aware outlier rejection practical enough for real-time front ends [4].

> **Theorem:** *Let $\rho$ be a robust kernel with Black–Rangarajan dual $\Psi_\rho$, and let $\rho_\mu$ be its GNC surrogate family with $\rho_{\mu_0}$ convex. Then the GNC iterates $(\Theta^{(k)}, w^{(k)})$ obtained by alternating weighted least squares and closed-form weight updates converge to a stationary point of the surrogate at each annealing stage, and the terminal iterate is a stationary point of the original robust problem [3].*

For lifelong SLAM, the practical consequence is that loop-closure validation can be *deferred to the back end*: accept all hypotheses, let GNC reweighting identify the consistent subset, and never pay the combinatorial price of front-end consensus.

## 5 Empirical Results / Theoretical Guarantees

The iSAM2 paper evaluates on real and simulated datasets against contemporary smoothing and mapping algorithms, reporting that iSAM2 compares favorably in both *quality* (final map/trajectory accuracy indistinguishable from batch optimization) and *efficiency* (incremental updates orders of magnitude cheaper than re-solving) [1]. The characteristic signature is flat per-step timing during exploration with bounded spikes at loop closures — the empirical footprint of top-of-tree updates — and, critically, *no* periodic batch resets anywhere in the timing trace. Standard benchmarks in this literature include simulated Manhattan-like worlds with known ground truth and real laser datasets with large loops; across them, fluid relinearization maintains accuracy while relinearizing only a small fraction of variables per step [1].

On the robustness side, the GNC framework's headline guarantee is empirical but striking: robust non-minimal solvers tolerate 70–80% outlier rates on spatial-perception problems including pose-graph optimization, surpassing RANSAC in both accuracy and speed, and TEASER++ packages these ideas into a real-time certifiable registration pipeline [3][4]. The robust-perception agenda [5] frames these results as evidence for a broader shift: from SLAM as a least-squares curve-fitting exercise to SLAM as *robust, lifelong spatial intelligence*, with metric-semantic systems such as Kimera demonstrating real-time operation fusing geometry and semantics in a single factor-graph back end [6].

Theoretical guarantees worth stating precisely:

1. **Exactness of incremental updates.** The Bayes-tree update is not an approximation: re-eliminating the affected top-of-tree with the original factors yields *exactly* the factorization a batch elimination with the same ordering would produce [1].
2. **Locality.** Variables in untouched subtrees have provably unchanged conditionals; their estimates cannot move, so no computation is wasted on them [1].
3. **GNC stationarity.** Each annealing stage converges to a stationary point of its surrogate, and the homotopy path tracks good minima in practice far beyond the basin of the un-annealed problem [3].
4. **Certifiability.** For subproblems admitting tight SDP relaxations, the duality gap certifies global optimality of the outlier-free core, giving TEASER++-style pipelines a *checkable* correctness claim rare in robotics [4].

## 6 Limitations

The framework has genuine limits, and a lifelong system must respect them:

- **Worst-case loop closures still hurt.** A loop closure connecting the two ends of a long trajectory can pull a large fraction of the tree into the affected set; iSAM2 degrades gracefully but not magically, and adversarial measurement orderings can defeat the amortized-constant promise [1].
- **Linearization-point commitment.** Fluid relinearization mitigates but does not eliminate the risk of converging to poor local minima in highly nonlinear regimes (e.g., bearing-only SLAM with bad initialization); the threshold $\beta$ is a heuristic, and there is no global-optimality certificate for the full nonlinear problem [1][2].
- **GNC is empirical, not certified.** The homotopy has no guarantee of tracking the global optimum through the annealing schedule; pathological outlier configurations can still trap it, and certifiable subproblem solvers do not yet exist for every factor type in a general SLAM graph [3].
- **Memory is unbounded.** Nothing in iSAM2 bounds the *size* of the tree: lifelong operation accumulates variables forever. True lifelong SLAM needs marginalization, sparsification, or experience-based submapping policies — an active research frontier surveyed in [5] — and naive marginalization reintroduces the dense fill-in that smoothing was meant to avoid.
- **Front-end errors beyond outliers.** The back end assumes the factor *topology* is correct up to outlier edges; systematic front-end failures (e.g., degenerate geometry) are outside the robust-estimation contract [5][6].

## 7 Conclusion

Factor graphs turned SLAM from a filtering problem into a sparse *inference* problem, and the Bayes tree turned incremental inference from a matrix-update trick into an exact, inspectable, local operation on a probabilistic data structure. iSAM2's three ideas — the Bayes tree as the algorithmic substrate, incremental variable reordering via constrained COLAMD, and fluid relinearization gated by a deviation threshold — together eliminate the periodic batch step and make the per-update cost track the *change* in the world rather than its size [1][2]. Robust loop closure completes the picture for the real world: the Black–Rangarajan duality recasts outlier rejection as reweighting, and graduated non-convexity solves the reweighted problem without an initial guess, tolerating outlier rates that would have been considered hopeless a decade ago [3][4].

The road to lifelong robot navigation now runs through bounded memory, certified optimality, and metric-semantic representations [5][6] — but the back-end foundation is settled: *maintain the factored posterior incrementally, relinearize fluidly, and let robustness be a schedule of weights on the same graph.* That is the architecture on which the next generation of field robots will be built.
