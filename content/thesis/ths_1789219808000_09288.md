# Neural Radiance Fields: Volumetric Rendering, Positional Encoding, and 3D Scene Reconstruction from Images

## Abstract

Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [1] reformulate 3D scene reconstruction as the optimization of a continuous volumetric function: a multilayer perceptron maps a single 5D coordinate — spatial position $(x, y, z)$ and viewing direction $(\theta, \phi)$ — to volume density $\sigma$ and view-dependent emitted radiance $\mathbf{c}$. Novel views are synthesized by querying this network along camera rays and compositing the outputs with classical, fully differentiable volume rendering. Two engineering choices make the optimization tractable: *positional encoding*, a deterministic Fourier feature map that lifts low-dimensional coordinates into a high-frequency representation and thereby defeats the spectral bias of MLPs [2], and *hierarchical sampling*, a coarse-to-fine ray-marching scheme that concentrates network queries near visible surfaces. This thesis develops NeRF's full machinery — the rendering integral and its quadrature, the NTK analysis of positional encoding [2, 6], conical-frustum anti-aliasing [3, 7], joint pose optimization [5], and hash encodings that compress training from hours to seconds [4] — evaluates it on synthetic and real benchmarks, and assesses its fundamental limitations.

## 1 Intro

View synthesis — generating photorealistic images from *unobserved* viewpoints — is one of the oldest problems in vision and graphics. Geometry-based pipelines reconstruct explicit structure (meshes, point clouds, voxels) and re-render it, committing early to a discrete topology and struggling with thin structures, transparency, and view dependence. Image-based rendering (IBR) instead interpolates between captured views, avoiding 3D reconstruction but demanding dense sampling and producing ghosting artifacts [8].

Neural Radiance Fields (Mildenhall et al., ECCV 2020 [1]) dissolve this dichotomy: the scene is a *continuous function* stored in network weights, supervised not by 3D ground truth but *only through its renderings* — posed photographs. Because volume rendering is differentiable end to end, training is stochastic gradient descent on a plain photometric loss. The result is simultaneously a reconstruction and a rendering method: fit once per scene, then query from arbitrary viewpoints.

Section 2 situates NeRF among volume rendering, implicit representations, and Fourier features; Section 3 specifies the method; Section 4 dives into five pillars — spectral bias and positional encoding, differentiable rendering, hierarchical sampling, conical anti-aliasing, and hash acceleration; Section 5 reports empirical and theoretical results; Sections 6–7 assess limitations and conclude.

The central claim is that NeRF's impact derives not from any single trick but from a *coherent stack*: a continuous representation matched to a differentiable renderer, an encoding matched to the optimizer's inductive bias, and a sampler matched to the geometry of occlusion. Remove any layer and the edifice collapses; understand all three and the method's successes — and failures — become predictable.

## 2 Background

**2.1 From the plenoptic function to neural fields**

NeRF is, in a precise sense, a learnable parameterization of the *plenoptic function* — the radiance arriving at every point in space from every direction — restricted to a static scene:

$$F_\Theta : (\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \mapsto (\mathbf{c}, \sigma),$$

where $\sigma \in \mathbb{R}_+$ is volume density and $\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is emitted radiance [1]. Density is deliberately *independent of viewing direction* — occupancy must not change as the camera moves, while appearance legitimately may (specularities, Fresnel effects) — so $\mathbf{d}$ is injected only late in the network, encoding an inductive bias toward multi-view consistency.

Unlike occupancy networks or signed distance functions supervised by 3D data [8], NeRF supervises the field *indirectly* through differentiable rendering, requiring only posed images (poses from structure-from-motion, e.g. COLMAP) and never explicit 3D supervision.

**2.2 Volume rendering**

Volume rendering predates neural networks by decades: the emission–absorption model of light transport through participating media gives, for a ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}$,

$$C(\mathbf{r}) = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t)\,\sigma(\mathbf{r}(t))\,\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(t), \mathbf{d})\,dt, \quad T(t) = \exp\!\left(-\int_{t_n}^{t} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s))\,ds\right),$$

where $T(t)$ is accumulated transmittance — the probability the ray reaches $t$ unabsorbed [1]. NeRF's rendering is a direct quadrature of this integral; its differentiability in every network weight is what makes gradient-based scene fitting possible.

> Theorem: *Differentiable compositing.* The alpha-composited color $\hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_i T_i (1 - e^{-\sigma_i \delta_i})\mathbf{c}_i$ is differentiable in each sample's $(\sigma_i, \mathbf{c}_i)$, with gradients decomposing into per-sample terms up to the transmittance prefix — so photometric gradients flow through the entire ray at cost linear in the number of samples.

This linearity is what makes NeRF trainable: hundreds of small MLP queries per ray, linear-time backpropagation.

**2.3 Why MLPs need positional encoding: spectral bias**

A plain MLP regressing $(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \mapsto (\mathbf{c}, \sigma)$ fails catastrophically, rendering scenes as blurred blobs. The reason is *spectral bias*: gradient-trained MLPs fit low-frequency components exponentially faster than high-frequency ones. Tancik et al. [2] made this precise via neural tangent kernel (NTK) theory — for low-dimensional inputs the NTK's eigenvalues decay rapidly with frequency, so fine detail converges impractically slowly.

The cure is to map inputs through *Fourier features* first. Rahimi and Recht [6] showed random sinusoidal features linearize shift-invariant kernels; Tancik et al. [2] showed such a mapping transforms the MLP's effective NTK into a stationary kernel with *tunable bandwidth*. NeRF's positional encoding is exactly such a mapping; Section 4.1 analyzes it in detail.

## 3 Methodology

**The scene function**

NeRF optimizes a fully connected network $F_\Theta$ mapping a 5D coordinate to density and color. The input position $\mathbf{p} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is first lifted by the positional encoding

$$\gamma(\mathbf{p}) = \big(\sin(2^0\pi\mathbf{p}), \cos(2^0\pi\mathbf{p}), \dots, \sin(2^{L-1}\pi\mathbf{p}), \cos(2^{L-1}\pi\mathbf{p})\big),$$

applied coordinate-wise, with $L=10$ for position (60-dimensional embedding) and $L=4$ for direction (24 dimensions) [1]. The encoded position passes through 8 fully connected layers of 256 ReLU units — with a skip connection re-injecting the input at the fifth layer — producing a 256-dimensional feature and a non-negative density $\sigma$. The feature is concatenated with the encoded direction and passed through one 128-unit layer and a sigmoid head to produce RGB [1].

**Differentiable rendering by quadrature**

To render a pixel, NeRF marches a ray through it, evaluating the network at $N$ stratified samples $t_i \sim \mathcal{U}[t_n + \tfrac{i-1}{N}(t_f - t_n),\, t_n + \tfrac{i}{N}(t_f - t_n)]$:

$$\hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i\,\big(1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)\big)\,\mathbf{c}_i, \qquad T_i = \exp\!\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_j\right),$$

with $\delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i$ [1]. The opacity $\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i\delta_i)$ and weights $w_i = T_i\alpha_i$ form a distribution along the ray that the network learns to concentrate at visible surfaces:

```python
def render_ray(sigmas, colors, deltas):
    """Volume-render one ray. sigmas/colors: (N,) / (N,3); deltas: (N,)."""
    alphas = 1.0 - np.exp(-sigmas * deltas)          # segment opacities
    trans = np.exp(-np.cumsum(np.concatenate([[0.0], sigmas[:-1] * deltas[:-1]])))
    weights = trans * alphas                          # compositing weights w_i
    rgb = np.sum(weights[:, None] * colors, axis=0)   # expected color
    depth = np.sum(weights * np.cumsum(deltas))       # expected termination
    return rgb, depth, weights
```

**Hierarchical volume sampling**

Uniform stratified sampling wastes network capacity on empty space. NeRF therefore trains *two* networks — coarse and fine — in a hierarchical scheme [1]:

1. The **coarse** network evaluates $N_c = 64$ stratified samples, producing weights $w_i$.
2. The normalized weights $\hat{w}_i = w_i / \sum_j w_j$ define a piecewise-constant PDF along the ray; $N_f = 128$ further samples are drawn by inverse transform sampling, concentrating queries where the coarse pass found matter.
3. The **fine** network evaluates all $N_c + N_f = 192$ samples for the final rendering.

Both networks are supervised jointly with

$$\mathcal{L} = \sum_{\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{R}} \Big[\,\|\hat{C}_c(\mathbf{r}) - C(\mathbf{r})\|_2^2 + \|\hat{C}_f(\mathbf{r}) - C(\mathbf{r})\|_2^2\,\Big],$$

over ray batches $\mathcal{R}$ of 4096 [1]; the coarse term ensures the coarse network learns a usable density field despite sparse samples.

| Hyperparameter | Value | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Position encoding bands $L$ | 10 | 60-dim input embedding |
| Direction encoding bands $L$ | 4 | 24-dim input embedding |
| MLP depth / width | 8 layers $\times$ 256 | Scene capacity |
| Coarse samples $N_c$ | 64 | Rough density estimate |
| Fine samples $N_f$ | 128 | Surface-concentrated detail |
| Ray batch size | 4096 | SGD batch |
| Training iterations | 100k–300k | Per-scene optimization |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Positional encoding and the spectral bias of MLPs

The positional encoding $\gamma$ is the component most responsible for NeRF's visual fidelity. In the NTK regime, training an MLP is kernel regression with an architecture-determined kernel; Tancik et al. [2] prove that for low-dimensional inputs this kernel is effectively low-pass, with eigenvalues decaying so fast in frequency that fine detail is unlearnable. Passing inputs through $\gamma(\mathbf{p}) = [\sin(2^k\pi\mathbf{p}), \cos(2^k\pi\mathbf{p})]_{k=0}^{L-1}$ replaces the NTK with a stationary kernel whose bandwidth is controlled by $\{2^k\}_{k<L}$ [2], letting the network fit detail up to the highest band's Nyquist limit while the smooth falloff of Fourier coefficients regularizes against noise.

```python
def positional_encoding(p, L):
    """p: (..., D) coordinates in [0,1]; returns (..., D*2*L) embedding."""
    freqs = 2.0 ** np.arange(L) * np.pi          # octave bands 2^0..2^{L-1}
    phases = p[..., None] * freqs               # (..., D, L)
    return np.concatenate([np.sin(phases), np.cos(phases)], axis=-1).reshape(
        p.shape[:-1] + (p.shape[-1] * 2 * L,))
```

Two subtleties matter. First, NeRF's encoding is *deterministic and axis-aligned* — fixed octave bands rather than the random frequencies of [6] — trading kernel-approximation guarantees for a multi-scale prior matched to natural-scene detail. Second, the encoding is a double-edged sword for *pose optimization*: Lin et al. [5] show high-frequency bands riddle the loss landscape with local minima when poses are jointly optimized, so bundle-adjusting NeRFs require *annealing* the encoding, gradually unmasking higher bands — a direct consequence of the same frequency analysis.

### 4.2 Differentiable volume rendering: numerics and geometry

The quadrature is what converts 2D supervision into 3D structure. Because $\hat{C}$ depends on $\sigma_i$ both through its own opacity $\alpha_i$ and through the transmittance $T_j$ of *all later samples* $j > i$, the loss gradient has the form

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \sigma_i} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{C}} \cdot \left[ T_i \delta_i e^{-\sigma_i\delta_i}\,\mathbf{c}_i \;-\; \delta_i \sum_{j>i} T_j \alpha_j\,\mathbf{c}_j \right].$$

The first term rewards density where the sample's own color matches the target; the second penalizes density that *occludes* correctly colored matter behind it. This is how multi-view consistency carves out geometry: a 3D point accumulates positive gradient only if it explains the observed color from *many* viewpoints at once, while floaters — density explaining one view but occluding correct structure in others — are suppressed. Visibility thus *emerges* from transmittance prefix products, with no explicit visibility reasoning.

On numerics: densities are ReLU outputs that can grow large, but $\alpha_i = 1 - e^{-\sigma_i\delta_i}$ saturates gracefully, and the cumulative transmittance is computed in log-space or stable prefix-product form. In half precision the exponentials underflow cleanly to opacity $0$ or $1$ rather than NaNs — a property exploited by accelerated implementations [4].

### 4.3 Hierarchical sampling and training dynamics

Hierarchical sampling is importance sampling with a *learned* proposal. After the coarse pass, the weights $w_i = T_i\alpha_i$ approximate the ray's visibility-weighted matter distribution; sampling the fine network proportionally to $w_i$ concentrates the 192 evaluations where they affect the image. Surfaces thus receive roughly an order of magnitude more samples than empty space — adaptively increasing quadrature resolution exactly where the integrand is non-smooth.

Training exhibits two phases:

- **Phase 1 (geometry discovery):** diffuse densities, transmittances near $1$, coarse-loss-dominated gradients; the network learns a blurry volumetric scaffold.
- **Phase 2 (appearance refinement):** weights concentrate, the fine network resolves texture, and the late-injected directional branch learns specularities without corrupting geometry.

This separation is *why* density precedes direction: had $\mathbf{d}$ entered at the first layer, view-dependent shortcuts could explain away geometric errors and multi-view consistency pressure would collapse [1].

### 4.4 Anti-aliasing: from rays to conical frustums

NeRF samples each pixel with a single infinitesimal ray — a point sample of a continuous signal — and aliases whenever views observe the scene at different scales: distant geometry shimmers, fine detail blurs or sparkles [3]. Supersampling is impractical, since each ray costs hundreds of MLP evaluations.

Mip-NeRF [3] replaces rays with *cones* from the pixel footprint, encoding not a point but the *integrated positional encoding* (IPE) — the expectation of $\gamma(\mathbf{x})$ under a Gaussian approximating each frustum segment:

$$\gamma_{\text{IPE}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})}[\gamma(\mathbf{x})],$$

which has a closed form: each sinusoid integrates to $\sin(2^k\pi\mu)\exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}(2^k\pi)^2\sigma^2)$, so high-frequency bands are *attenuated* in proportion to footprint size — a learned, continuous-space mipmap [3]. The result cuts average error by 17% on the original NeRF dataset and 60% on a multiscale variant, while running 7% faster at half the size [3]. Zip-NeRF [7] later married this machinery to grid-based acceleration, cutting error another 8–77% and training 24$\times$ faster than mip-NeRF 360.

### 4.5 Acceleration: multiresolution hash encodings

NeRF's original training cost — hours to days per scene — was prohibitive. Instant-NGP [4] attacked the *encoding*: it replaced the fixed sinusoidal map with a *multiresolution hash table* of trainable feature vectors. At each resolution level, the query point's voxel corners hash into a fixed-size table, their vectors are trilinearly interpolated and concatenated, and the result feeds a *tiny* MLP (2–4 narrow layers). Collisions — inevitable at fine levels — are disambiguated by the multiresolution structure: a collision at one level is resolved by distinct codes at others, and gradient averaging over colliding entries acts as a benign regularizer [4].

Fully fused CUDA kernels minimize memory bandwidth, yielding a speedup of *several orders of magnitude*: high-quality training in seconds, 1920$\times$1080 rendering in tens of milliseconds [4]. Conceptually, Instant-NGP completes an arc from a fixed Fourier basis [2], through random features [6], to a *learned, spatially adaptive* basis allocating capacity exactly where the scene needs it.

## 5 Empirical and Theoretical Results

**5.1 Benchmark performance**

Mildenhall et al. [1] evaluate on 8 synthetic Blender scenes (100 training views, known poses) and real forward-facing LLFF scenes (20–60 phone captures, COLMAP poses). On the synthetic benchmark NeRF decisively outperforms prior neural rendering and IBR baselines:

| Method | PSNR $\uparrow$ | SSIM $\uparrow$ | LPIPS $\downarrow$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| SRN | 22.26 | 0.846 | 0.170 |
| LLFF (IBR) | 24.88 | 0.911 | 0.114 |
| NeRF (no hierarchical) | 28.46 | 0.929 | 0.109 |
| **NeRF (full)** | **31.01** | **0.947** | **0.081** |

*Mean metrics over the 8 synthetic scenes, as reported in [1]; ablations show removing positional encoding or hierarchical sampling each costs several dB of PSNR.*

On real LLFF scenes NeRF likewise leads on all three metrics, most decisively on LPIPS — the perceptual metric most sensitive to sharp, view-consistent detail [1]. Mip-NeRF [3] cut average error another 17% on the same dataset, and Zip-NeRF [7] pushed further while training an order of magnitude faster.

**5.2 Theoretical results**

Three theoretical contributions anchor the method:

1. **Fourier features control the NTK bandwidth.** Tancik et al. [2] prove that composing an MLP with a Fourier map yields a stationary effective kernel with bandwidth set by the feature frequencies — converting spectral bias from a curse into a tunable dial; NeRF's $L=10$ octave bands are the practical instantiation.
2. **Coarse-to-fine registration enables joint pose optimization.** BARF [5] connects NeRF optimization to classical Lucas–Kanade alignment, showing the encoding induces a frequency-dependent basin of convergence; annealing from low to high frequencies widens the basin and enables joint recovery of poses and scene from imperfect initialization.
3. **Conical integration yields scale-equivariant encodings.** The IPE derivation in [3] shows integrating the sinusoidal basis over the pixel footprint reproduces exactly the attenuation schedule of classical mipmapping, but in the network's continuous feature space.

## 6 Limitations

NeRF's limitations follow directly from its design decisions:

- **Per-scene optimization.** A NeRF encodes *one* scene in its weights; new scenes require retraining from scratch (hours originally, seconds with [4]). The original formulation has no scene prior [8].
- **Static scenes only.** The 5D function has no time argument; motion, changing illumination, and deformation violate multi-view consistency and manifest as blur or floaters.
- **Unbounded and large-scale scenes.** The original parameterization assumes bounded, normalized content; distant backgrounds are poorly sampled. Contracted-space successors [7] help, but city-scale reconstruction remains an open frontier.
- **Pose dependence.** Accurate camera poses are a hard prerequisite; BARF [5] relaxes this to *imperfect* poses via joint optimization, but textureless or symmetric scenes can still defeat registration, and the coarse-to-fine schedule is heuristic.
- **Sampling cost and view-dependence artifacts.** Worst-case rendering still needs hundreds of network evaluations per pixel, and the directional branch can hallucinate view-dependent "cheats" when training views are sparse.
- **No relighting or editing.** The field bakes illumination into emitted radiance; decomposing appearance into material and lighting (inverse rendering proper) is beyond the original formulation.

## 7 Conclusion

Neural Radiance Fields demonstrated that a scene can be *both* reconstructed and rendered as a single differentiable object: a neural network that *is* the 3D model, supervised only by photographs [1]. Its lasting contributions are a stack of ideas that have each outgrown their origin — positional encoding as a general cure for spectral bias [2], differentiable volume rendering as a universal 3D supervision signal, hierarchical and conical sampling as learned quadrature [1, 3], and hash-based adaptive encodings as the path to real-time neural graphics [4]. Its limitations — per-scene fitting, static-world assumptions, pose dependence — define the current frontier rather than refuting the approach; each has spawned a subfield measured against the baseline developed here. The deeper lesson is methodological: NeRF succeeded because every component was *matched* to the optimizer's inductive biases — the encoding to the NTK spectrum, the sampler to the geometry of occlusion, the architecture to the physics of view dependence. Future representations will be judged by the same standard: not the cleverness of any one trick, but the coherence of the stack.
