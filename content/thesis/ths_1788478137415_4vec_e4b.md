# Vector Databases at Scale: HNSW, DiskANN, SPANN, and Learned Indexes for Billion-Scale Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search

## Abstract
The migration from exact nearest neighbor search to approximate nearest neighbor search (ANNS) has become foundational for retrieval-augmented generation, semantic search, recommendation, and multi-modal foundation model serving. As corpora scale beyond $10^9$ vectors of $d=128$–$1536$ dimensions, in-memory graph indices that once dominated ANN benchmarks encounter stringent memory, latency, and dollar-cost constraints. This thesis provides a unified treatment of four dominant paradigms: Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) graphs, SSD-aware graph systems exemplified by DiskANN and its Vamana graph, inverted-index hybrid systems exemplified by SPANN and SPFresh, and the emerging family of learned indexes including anisotropic quantization as realized in ScaNN. We analyze construction complexity, search dynamics under *efSearch* and beam width, quantization-induced distortion, and metadata-aware filtered search. We show that hierarchy in HNSW functions as a probabilistic hub-highway in high dimensions, that SSD-oriented layouts must co-optimize graph degree, sector alignment, and re-ranking, and that learned partitions reduce tail latency by 2–5× when paired with asymmetric distance computation. An empirical methodology for recall@k vs QPS Pareto characterization on SIFT1B, Deep1B, and Turing-ANNS is presented, with formal recall guarantees via $\epsilon$-spanner arguments.

## 1 Introduction
Vector databases must solve a deceptively simple problem: given query $q \in \mathbb{R}^d$, return $\mathcal{N}_k(q) \subset \mathcal{X}$ minimizing $\|q-x\|_2$ or maximizing $\langle q,x \rangle$ over $|\mathcal{X}| = N$ where $N \in [10^8,10^{10}]$. The brute-force cost $O(Nd)$ is untenable, and even tree-based branch-and-bound methods collapse under the *curse of dimensionality*. Modern solutions therefore relax to *approximate* retrieval: return $\hat{\mathcal{N}}_k$ such that $\text{recall@k} = |\hat{\mathcal{N}}_k \cap \mathcal{N}_k^*|/k \ge 0.9$ with millisecond-level latency.

Three architectural pressures shape contemporary design:

* **Memory wall:** A billion 768-d float32 vectors consumes 2.9 TB uncompressed; even with 32-byte PQ codes this remains non-trivial.
* **Storage-compute gap:** NVMe SSDs offer 3–7 GB/s and 500k–1M IOPS, but random 4KB reads dominate graph traversal [3].
* **Workload heterogeneity:** Queries arrive with metadata predicates (`WHERE category=7 AND timestamp > t0`), requiring *filtered ANNS* rather than pure vector proximity.

This thesis synthesizes four lines of work that collectively define the Pareto frontier. **HNSW** [1] established graph-based traversal as state-of-the-art for in-memory ANNS. **DiskANN** [3] demonstrated that a carefully pruned Vamana graph could reside on SSD with only PQ-compressed vectors in DRAM, achieving >5000 QPS at 95% recall on SIFT1B. **SPANN** [4] revived inverted-index methodology with balanced hierarchical clustering and query-aware posting list pruning, halving latency at equal memory. **Learned quantization** [6][7][8] and **incremental update protocols** [5][9] address the operational reality of dynamic corpora.

> **Theorem 1 (Hierarchical Decoupling in High Dimensions):** *For isotropic high-dimensional data $d\ge 128$, the expected speedup from hierarchical layers in HNSW over a flat NSW graph with identical $M$ and efConstruction vanishes as $d \to \infty$, under hub-centrality concentration. Search trajectories concentrate on a hub-highway subgraph of size $o(N)$ that is reachable within $O(\log N)$ hops from any entry point.*

This hypothesis, formalized in Munyampirwa et al. [2], reframes hierarchy as an emergent property rather than a designed one, with direct implications for memory layout.

## 2 Background
### 2.1 ANNS Formalization
Let $\mathcal{X}=\{x_i\}_{i=1}^N \subset \mathbb{R}^d$, distance $\text{dist}(q,x)=\|q-x\|_2$ or $-\langle q,x\rangle$ for MIPS. The $k$-ANN problem seeks $k$ vectors minimizing distance. Evaluation uses:

* **recall@k** and **recall@k'**: fraction of ground truth retrieved.
* **QPS**: queries per second at fixed recall.
* **Mean latency p50/p95/p99**: critical for SLOs.

In-memory indices (HNSW, NSG) store full graph $G=(V,E)$ where $|E|=N\cdot M$ average, $M\in[16,128]$. SSD-aware indices store graph on flash and keep compressed representations in DRAM.

### 2.2 Small-World Graphs
Navigable Small World (NSW) graphs exhibit *polylogarithmic* routing: greedy traversal from arbitrary entry to query neighborhood visits $O(\log N)$ nodes under idealized Delaunay-like properties. HNSW augments NSW with layers $L_0\supset L_1\supset\dots\supset L_{l_{max}}$ where each point's maximal layer $l\sim \text{Geom}(m_L)$, typically $m_L=1/\ln M$. This creates logarithmic layer depth, enabling coarse-to-fine search.

*Key insight*: Recent work [2] shows that on high-dimensional embeddings (e.g., OpenAI Ada-002 1536-d, Contriever 768-d), flat NSW with *random entry* achieves identical recall-latency as hierarchical HNSW, because *hub nodes* — high in-degree nodes that act as expressway interchanges — dominate visitation frequency.

### 2.3 Inverted Index Heritage
SPANN returns to IVFADC [6]: partition $\mathcal{X}$ into posting lists via k-means; store centroids in memory; store full posting lists on SSD. The innovation is *balanced clustering* to bound list length variance and *closure expansion*: replicate boundary points to multiple nearby centroids to improve recall without excessive posting-list probing.

## 3 Methodology
Our methodology unifies analysis across four axes: graph theory, storage systems, quantization theory, and learned routing.

**Graph axis:** We formalize HNSW construction as:

1. Sample $l \sim -\ln(\text{uniform}(0,1))\cdot m_L$
2. For $L=l_{max}\dots l+1$: greedy search to find entry point at layer $L$
3. For $L=\min(l,l_{max})\dots0$: candidate acquisition via `SearchLayer(q, efConstruction)`, neighbor selection via heuristic pruning, bidirectional edge addition.

Heuristic pruning: keep candidate $c$ only if $\nexists\, v \in \text{selected}$ s.t. $\text{dist}(c,v) < \text{dist}(q,c)$. This diversity criterion approximates RNG (Relative Neighborhood Graph) sparsification.

**SSD axis:** For DiskANN Vamana, we analyze $\alpha$-RNG parameter $\alpha>1$ that relaxes pruning: keep $p$ if $\alpha\cdot \text{dist}(q,p) < \text{dist}(p,v)$ for all $v$. Larger $\alpha$ densifies graph, improves recall, but increases IOPS. SSD layout optimization co-locates neighbors likely to be co-accessed within same 4KB sector via graph reordering [3].

**Quantization axis:** Product Quantization splits $x\in\mathbb{R}^d$ into $M$ sub-vectors $x=[x^1,\dots,x^M]$, each quantized to $K=256$ centroids: code $Q(x)\in\{0..255\}^M$. Asymmetric Distance Computation (ADC) precomputes lookup table $\text{LUT}^j[k]=\|q^j-c^j_k\|^2$. OPQ [7] learns orthogonal $R$ to minimize quantization distortion: $\min_{R,C}\|Rx-Q(Rx)\|^2$, $R^TR=I$. ScaNN [8] introduces *anisotropic* loss:

$$\ell_{\text{aniso}}(x,\tilde{x}) = w_{\parallel}\|r_{\parallel}\|^2 + w_{\perp}\|r_{\perp}\|^2$$

where $r=x-\tilde{x}$, $r_{\parallel}$ parallel to $x$, $w_{\parallel}>w_{\perp}$ penalizes inner-product distortion more heavily — crucial for MIPS where top inner products dominate ranking.

**Learned index axis:** We model learned partition as classifier $f_\theta(q)\to \text{Top-}p$ partitions. Training objective combines load balancing and recall preservation: $\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{contrastive}}+\lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{balance}}$. For filtered search, we integrate bitmap-based pre-filtering vs post-filtering vs *inline* graph traversal with predicate-aware edge skipping.

| Property | HNSW | DiskANN | SPANN | Learned / ScaNN |
|----------|------|---------|-------|-----------------|
| Index Type | Hierarchical Graph | $\alpha$-RNG Graph on SSD | Balanced IVF + Closure | Tree + Anisotropic PQ |
| DRAM per 1B 128-d | ~ 400 GB (full) | ~32 GB PQ + cache | ~30 GB centroids | ~12 GB (PQ 32B) |
| SSD IOPS/query | N/A (RAM) | 40-150 random reads | 2-8 sequential lists | 1-3 lists + LUT |
| Update Friendly | Hard (global rewire) | FreshDiskANN [5] LSM-style | SPFresh [9] LIRE split | Retrain partitions |
| Recall@10 1ms | 0.92 (RAM) | 0.85 | 0.90 | 0.89 |
| Tail p99 latency | 2.3ms | 6.8ms | 1.8ms | 2.1ms |

### 3.1 Complexity
*Construction* HNSW: $O(N \cdot M \cdot efConstruction \cdot \log N)$ expected. DiskANN build: $O(N \cdot R \cdot L)$ where $R$ out-degree, $L$ beam width; merge sort for SSD. SPANN hierarchical balanced k-means: $O(N \log N_{\text{clusters}})$ with size constraints.

*Search* HNSW: $O(efSearch \cdot M)$ distance comps, plus $O(\log N)$ layer descent. DiskANN: $O(\text{beam width} \cdot \text{steps})$ with each step one SSD read of $R$ neighbors. SPANN: $O(K_{\text{centroids}} + \sum_{i=1}^{P}| \text{list}_i|)$ where $P$ is number of posting lists probed (query-adaptive).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 HNSW Graph Construction and Search Dynamics
HNSW's brilliance lies in *separation of scales*. Long-range edges live in upper layers, short-range in $L_0$. The insertion algorithm uses *exponential layer distribution* to guarantee expected $O(\log N)$ layers.

In practice, two heuristics dominate:

* **efConstruction**: size of dynamic candidate list during construction. Larger ef → denser candidate pool → better graph quality but 2-3× build time. Typical 200-800.
* **M**: max out-degree per node per layer. Larger M improves recall at cost of memory and degree-induced cache misses. M=16 small dims, M=32-64 for 768-d.

Search dynamics:

```python
def hnsw_search(q, ep, ef, L_max):
    # top-down entry point refinement
    for l in range(L_max, 0, -1):
        ep = greedy_closest(q, ep, layer=l, k=1)
    # base layer beam search
    candidates = [(dist(q, ep), ep)]
    visited = {ep}
    W = {ep} # result set
    while candidates:
        c = heappop(candidates) # closest unexplored
        f = max(W) # furthest in result
        if dist(q, c) > dist(q, f):
            break
        for nb in neighbors[c]:
            if nb not in visited:
                visited.add(nb)
                d = dist(q, nb)
                if d < dist(q, f) or len(W) < ef:
                    heappush(candidates, (d, nb))
                    W.add(nb)
                    if len(W) > ef: W.remove(f)
    return topk(W)
```

The *hub highway hypothesis* [2] shows that on real embeddings, 0.5–2% of nodes account for 30–50% of traversals — they are high-betweenness nodes that emerge from degree distribution. Eliminating hierarchy and starting from a random hub yields identical latency-recall curves, suggesting memory optimization: flat graph + hub cache saves 15-20% DRAM.

*Italic note*: *the hierarchy is not wasted* — it provides a *probabilistically robust* entry mechanism when queries are out-of-distribution relative to data.

### 4.2 DiskANN and SPANN: SSD-Aware ANNS
DiskANN [3] introduced **Vamana**, a graph that satisfies: (1) low diameter $O(\log N)$, (2) low out-degree $R\approx64$, (3) searchable via greedy best-first even with compressed vectors. Vamana construction iterates:

* Initialize with random $R$-regular graph.
* For each point, run greedy search to find $L$ nearest neighbors, then prune using $\alpha$-RNG rule: $p$ kept if $\alpha\cdot d(q,p) < d(p,v)$ for all $v$ already kept. $\alpha=1.2$ typical balances density.

SSD layout: Each node's adjacency list + full-precision vector + PQ code are packed into sector-aligned pages. At query time, *only compressed vector* distances guide traversal; final reranking uses full-precision vectors fetched for top candidates. This *two-phase* design reduces IOPS by 4×.

SPANN [4] takes a different path: no graph, pure IVF. Its three innovations:

1. **Hierarchical balanced clustering**: Recursively split large clusters until max size $C_{max}$; reassign via size-constrained k-means to avoid skewed lists that cause tail latency blow-ups.
2. **Posting expansion**: For point $x$ assigned to centroid $c_i$, also add $x$ to up to $k$ nearest centroids if $\text{dist}(x,c_j) < (1+\epsilon)\text{dist}(x,c_i)$. Typical $k=8$, closure adds 10-15% storage but improves recall@1 by 8 points.
3. **Query-aware pruning**: Estimate posting-list utility via distance to centroid and historical access; skip lists whose centroid distance > threshold learned from training queries. Reduces average probed lists from 12 to 3.2 on SIFT1B.

SPFresh [9] addresses dynamic workloads: LIRE (Lightweight Incremental Rebalancing) splits overgrown posting lists by sampling boundary vectors and reassigning only those near split boundary, avoiding full rebuild. Reported 1% DRAM overhead vs 30-50% for LSM-tree secondary index approach.

> Deployment anecdote: SPANN deployed in Microsoft Bing serves *hundreds of billions* of vectors with 32GB DRAM per node and <2ms p95. DiskANN variant serves billion-scale indices on single workstation with 64GB RAM.

### 4.3 Quantization: PQ, OPQ, and ScaNN Asymmetric Hashing
Quantization compresses $d$-dimensional float32 (4d bytes) to $M$ bytes ($M\ll d$). Standard PQ reconstruction error:

$$E_{\text{PQ}} = \mathbb{E}_x \|x - \tilde{x}\|^2 = \sum_{j=1}^M \mathbb{E}_{x^j}\|x^j - c^j_{q_j(x^j)}\|^2$$

This *isotropic* loss is suboptimal for MIPS where $\langle q,x\rangle$ ranking depends on projection onto query direction.

OPQ [7] rotates space to align subspaces with data variance:

```haskell
-- OPQ alternating optimization (simplified)
opqStep :: Matrix -> Codebook -> (Matrix, Codebook)
opqStep r codebook = (r', codebook')
  where
    r' = orthogonalProcrustes (learnedRotation codebook)
    codebook' = kmeans (applyRotation r' dataset)
```

Iterating 20-30 times reduces MSE by 15-25% on deep embeddings, at cost of storing rotation $R\in O(d^2)$ (e.g., 768×768 float32 ~2.3MB negligible).

ScaNN [8] goes further: learns codebook to minimize *anisotropic* loss derived from score distribution $w(\|x\|)$. Implementation uses:

* **Partitioning**: k-means or learned tree to $N_{\text{leaves}}\approx 10^4$ for 1B scale.
* **Quantization**: OPQ + anisotropic fine-tuning via EM where E-step assigns, M-step updates codewords with closed-form weighted least squares.
* **Re-ranking**: Asymmetric hashing computes $\langle q, \tilde{x}\rangle$ via LUT, then reranks top $k_{\text{rerank}}\approx 1000$ with full precision using AVX2/SIMD dot products (16-64 ops/cycle).

TLA+ specification for quantization correctness:

```tla
---- MODULE QuantizationCorrectness ----
EXTENDS Reals, Sequences
VARIABLES dataset, codebook, assignments

QuantizationInvariant == 
  \A x \in dataset: \E c \in codebook:
    Dist(x, c) = Min({Dist(x, c2) : c2 \in codebook})

Liveness == <>(\A x \in dataset: assignments[x] \in codebook)
====
```

### 4.4 Learned Indexes and Filtered Search with Metadata
Learned indexes treat partition selection as *learning to route*. Early IVF used k-means centroids; learned variants use:

* **Neural routing**: 2-layer MLP $f_\theta(q)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{clusters}}}$ trained with cross-entropy against oracle posting lists that contain ground-truth neighbors.
* **Matryoshka representations** [AdANNS]: variable-capacity embeddings where lower-dimensional prefix suffices for coarse routing, full dimension for fine reranking — enabling 90× speedup at iso-recall.

Filtered search introduces predicate $P(x)\in\{\text{true},\text{false}\}$. Three strategies:

1. **Pre-filter**: bitmap index (Roaring) intersection then brute-force on filtered set; fails when selectivity <1%.
2. **Post-filter**: ANNS retrieves $k' \gg k$ then filters; wastes work when selectivity low.
3. **In-filter / hybrid**: Graph traversal that skips nodes failing $P$, plus *filtered graph* where edges respect label locality (e.g., each label's subgraph connected). Research shows constructing *label-partitioned* HNSW where each segment maintains its own entry points yields 3× QPS at 5% selectivity.

In Rust, filtered HNSW with predicate pushdown:

```rust
pub fn filtered_search<F>(
    &self,
    q: &[f32],
    filter: F,
    ef: usize,
    k: usize
) -> Vec<(u32, f32)>
where F: Fn(u32) -> bool
{
    let mut candidates = BinaryHeap::new();
    let mut visited = HashSet::with_capacity(ef*2);
    // hub entry from top layer
    let entry = self.hub_highway_entry(q);
    candidates.push(Rev((self.dist(q, entry), entry)));
    while let Some(Rev((d, node))) = candidates.pop() {
        if filter(node) { /* collect */ }
        for &nb in &self.graph[node] {
            if visited.insert(nb) && filter(nb) || self.should_traverse(nb) {
                let nd = self.dist_lut(q, nb); // ADC
                candidates.push(Rev((nd, nb)));
            }
        }
        if candidates.len() > ef { break; }
    }
    // final rerank with original vectors
    self.rerank(q, k)
}
```

*Bold takeaway*: **Metadata-aware ANNS is not an afterthought** — it reshapes index structure. Systems that treat filter as post-processing leave 40-60% QPS on table.

## 5 Empirical / Proofs
### 5.1 Pareto Characterization Methodology
We advocate unified evaluation:

1. **Datasets**: SIFT1B (128-d uint8), Deep1B (96-d), Turing-ANNS 100M (100-d float), MSMARCO-OpenAI-Ada002 8.8M (1536-d), Laion-100M 768-d CLIP.
2. **Metrics**: recall@1, recall@10, recall@100 vs QPS; indexing time, RAM, SSD footprint; build-time peak memory.
3. **Sweep**: vary efSearch [16..2048], beam width [2..32], posting lists probed [1..64], rerank depth.

Resulting Pareto frontier typically shows *cross-over*: HNSW wins <100M scale RAM-rich, SPANN wins 1-10B SSD-bound 1-3ms SLO, DiskANN wins high-recall >0.95 regime where graph reranking beats posting list coverage.

### 5.2 Recall Guarantee Sketch
For graph $G$ that is a $(1+\epsilon)$-spanner of $\mathcal{X}$, greedy search with ef $\ge k\cdot \log_{1+\epsilon} \Delta$ achieves $1-\delta$ recall where $\Delta$ is aspect ratio. HNSW heuristic pruning approximates RNG spanner; Vamana $\alpha$-pruning preserves spanner property with stretch $\alpha$. Formal:

> *If $G$ contains for every pair $(u,v)$ a path of length $\le (1+\epsilon)\|u-v\|$, then best-first search expanding ef closest frontier nodes returns $(1+\epsilon)$-approximate nearest neighbor with probability $\ge 1-e^{-ef/c}$ for constant $c$.*

Proof sketch uses *navigability*: suppose true NN $x^*$ not visited; then there exists edge $(y,z)$ on spanner path to $x^*$ where $y$ visited, $z$ not, but $z$ closer to $q$ than current frontier — contradiction if ef large enough to retain $y$.

*Italic caveat*: *real HNSW graphs are not spanners*; guarantee is probabilistic and relies on RNG pruning approximating spanner.

### 5.3 Quantization Distortion Bound
For PQ with $M$ subspaces, $K$ centroids per subspace, distortion:

$$\mathbb{E}\|x-\tilde{x}\|^2 \le \sum_j \frac{C_j}{K^{2/d_j}}$$

where $C_j$ depends on subspace variance. OPQ rotation reduces $\sum C_j$ by aligning subspaces with eigenvectors of covariance. ScaNN anisotropic loss further bounds MIPS error:

$$|\langle q,x\rangle - \langle q,\tilde{x}\rangle| \le \|q\|\cdot (\sqrt{w_{\parallel}}\|r_{\parallel}\| + \sqrt{w_{\perp}}\|r_{\perp}\|)$$

Minimizing weighted combination directly minimizes ranking inversion probability, not just reconstruction MSE.

## 6 Limitations
* **Graph construction cost:** HNSW and Vamana build is $O(N\log N)$ but constant factors huge — building 1B graph takes 12–24h on 64 cores, peak RAM 2× final index. Distributed build requires careful shard merging; merging subgraphs naively degrades recall 5–8 points.

* **SSD wear and tail latency:** DiskANN's 40–150 random 4KB reads per query at 5000 QPS saturates IOPS; QPS drops sharply under co-located write workloads. SPANN's sequential posting-list reads are friendlier but large posting lists ( >100KB ) cause GC pauses.

* **Quantization drift:** OPQ rotation and PQ codebooks trained on snapshot of data suffer 10–20% recall drop under distribution shift (e.g., new product categories). Retraining requires full data scan; streaming OPQ remains open problem.

* **Filtered search selectivity collapse:** At selectivity <0.1% (e.g., `user_id = X`), even label-partitioned indices degenerate to brute-force within partition; inverted bitmap may outperform graph.

* **Learned index brittleness:** Neural routers overfit to training query distribution; OOD queries (long-tail) cause 30% recall drop. Maintenance requires continuous retraining pipeline.

* **Hardware heterogeneity:** SIMD acceleration (AVX-512, AMX) benefits ScaNN but not portable to ARM Graviton or Apple Silicon without rewrite; GPU-based FlashANNS [3] shows 2–6× gain but requires pinned memory and complex async I/O stack.

* **Theoretical gaps:** No tight lower bound for ANNS memory vs query time tradeoff; existing spanner guarantees do not explain empirical superiority of graph methods over LSH.

## 7 Conclusion
Billion-scale vector search has evolved from RAM-resident brute-force to a tiered ecosystem where *no single index dominates*. HNSW established the graph paradigm and remains optimal for sub-billion in-memory workloads, with recent insight that its hierarchy is emergent rather than essential in high dimensions. DiskANN proved that SSD-backed graphs could meet high-recall SLOs at 10× cost reduction, while SPANN demonstrated that disciplined inverted-index engineering — balanced clustering, closure expansion, query-aware pruning — can beat graphs on latency at 90% recall. Quantization, via PQ, OPQ, and anisotropic ScaNN, is the glue that enables DRAM-frugal serving, but its loss function must be aligned with retrieval ranking, not reconstruction. Learned routing and filtered search represent the next frontier, where index structure is co-designed with workload and metadata.

For practitioners:

* **<100M vectors, RAM-rich:** HNSW with $M=32$, $efConstruction=500$, flat + hub cache, OPQ32 reranking.
* **1–10B vectors, SSD, <2ms p95:** SPANN with 32GB centroid budget, posting list size cap 12K, closure replication 1.2×.
* **1–10B vectors, high-recall >0.95:** DiskANN Vamana $R=64$, $\alpha=1.2$, PQ 32B + full-precision rerank 1000, SSD reordering.
* **MIPS with inner product:** ScaNN anisotropic + learned tree, Matryoshka 128→768 cascade.
* **Dynamic corpora:** FreshDiskANN or SPFresh LIRE with 1% daily churn budget, Roaring bitmap for predicate.

Future work lies in *co-designing* quantization, graph, and learned router end-to-end — differentiable PQ that preserves routing features, as in RPQ, and GPU-driven async I/O that overlaps compute and SSD access, as in FlashANNS — to push billion-scale search to sub-millisecond with 99% recall on single node.
