# Gromov's h-Principle: Convex Integration, Ample Differential Relations, and the Flexible Geometry of Underdetermined PDEs

## Abstract

Among the deepest reorganizations of twentieth-century differential geometry is Mikhael Gromov's h-principle: the discovery that large classes of partial differential relations admit so many solutions that existence reduces to topology. Beginning with Stephen Smale's 1958 proof that the two-sphere can be turned inside out by a regular homotopy of immersions, and John Nash's 1954 demonstration that Riemannian metrics admit C^1 isometric realizations in codimensions where C^2 geometry forbids them, the subject matured into Gromov's theory of convex integration and flexible sheaves. This article surveys the one-jet formalism of differential relations, the distinction between formal and holonomic solutions, and the ampleness condition that unlocks the method. It presents the Nash-Kuiper corrugation iteration in detail, explains how convex integration converts algebraic-topological data into genuine solutions, and surveys modern frontiers: wild weak solutions of the Euler equations, conjectural regularity thresholds for C^{1,theta} isometries, and the boundary between flexibility and symplectic rigidity. It closes with the method's intrinsic limitations and the open problems that remain.

---

## 1 Intro

Classical differential geometry is, to a first approximation, a theory of *rigidity*. A closed surface of positive Gaussian curvature embedded in three-space is unique up to rigid motion: this is the theorem of Cohn-Vossen and Herglotz resolving the Weyl problem. The equations are overdetermined, the solutions are scarce, and what exists is essentially forced by the data. Against this background, the discoveries of the late 1950s read like paradoxes. Stephen Smale proved in his 1958 thesis that the two-sphere can be turned inside out — that there is a regular homotopy of immersions of the sphere in three-space joining the identity to an orientation-reversing map [7]. John Nash proved in 1954 that any short embedding of a Riemannian manifold can be uniformly approximated by *C^1 isometric* embeddings, in codimensions where every C^2 isometric embedding is rigid [1]. Nicolaas Kuiper extended this to codimension one in 1955 [2].

What unites these results is not a trick but a *method*: the construction of solutions to underdetermined systems of partial differential equations by an iteration that adds rapidly oscillating, small-amplitude corrections, steering the formal (algebraic) solution toward a genuine (differential) one. Gromov recognized this method — which he named **convex integration** — as the engine of a general theory, the **h-principle**, first developed in his 1969 doctoral thesis and presented in full in *Partial Differential Relations* [3]. The slogan of the theory is striking: for a large class of differential relations, *the inclusion of genuine solutions into formal solutions is a weak homotopy equivalence*. Topology decides; analysis merely implements.

This article develops that slogan into mathematics. We introduce the jet-space formalism of differential relations, distinguish formal from holonomic sections, and explain the two pillars of the constructive method: the Nash–Kuiper iteration and Gromov's convex integration for open ample relations. We trace the lineage from Smale's covering homotopy theorem through Thurston's corrugations to the modern use of convex integration in fluid dynamics and symplectic geometry, and we mark the precise boundary where flexibility ends and rigidity begins.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Differential relations and the formal–holonomic distinction

Let *M* and *N* be smooth manifolds. The **one-jet space** *J^1(M, N)* is the bundle over *M × N* whose fiber at *(x, y)* is the space of linear maps *L(T_x M, T_y N)*. A section *s : M → J^1(M, N)* is a triple *(x, f(x), L_x)*: a base map together with an independent linear-algebraic candidate for its derivative. The section is **holonomic** when *L_x = df_x* for a genuine map *f*; otherwise it is **formal**, a purely pointwise algebraic assignment with no integrability constraint.

A **differential relation** *R ⊂ J^r(M, N)* is a subset of the *r*-jet space. A solution of *R* is a holonomic section landing in *R*; a **formal solution** is any continuous section of *R*. The fundamental question of the theory is:

> **Question:** When does every formal solution deform, through formal solutions, to a genuine solution?

When the answer is affirmative — in the precise sense that the inclusion of the space of genuine solutions into the space of formal solutions is a weak homotopy equivalence — the relation is said to satisfy the **h-principle**. The terminology encodes the philosophical content: the *homotopy* principle asserts that the problem is *homotopy-theoretic*, not analytic.

### 2.2 The first generation: immersions and submersions

The relation of immersions,
*R_imm = {(x, y, L) : L has maximal rank}*,
is open in *J^1(M, N)*. Smale's 1958 covering homotopy theorem showed that immersions of the *n*-disk in *R^q* with *q > n* satisfy a parametric h-principle; Hirsch and Phillips extended this to immersions of arbitrary manifolds and to submersions, and Gromov's sheaf-theoretic reformulation completed the picture [3]. The sphere eversion is the famous corollary: since the space of formal immersions of *S^2* in *R^3* is connected, the h-principle for immersions yields a path of genuine immersions from the standard embedding to its mirror image [7]. The result was so counterintuitive that explicit eversions — Morin, Apéry, and Thurston's corrugation-based eversion visualized in the film *Outside In* — became a small industry of their own.

### 2.3 The rigidity–flexibility dichotomy

Parallel to these successes runs a countercurrent of rigidity theorems: Weyl's problem, Borisov's theorem that *C^{1,θ}* isometries of positively curved surfaces are rigid for *θ > 2/3*, and Gromov's nonsqueezing theorem in symplectic geometry. The modern picture is therefore a *dichotomy* indexed by regularity. For the isometric embedding problem, smooth solutions are rigid while *C^1* solutions are maximally flexible; at intermediate Hölder regularity *C^{1,θ}* a threshold *θ_0* is conjectured, with recent work pushing flexibility below exponents of order *1/(1 + n(n−1))* and rigidity above *2/3* in dimension two [6]. Understanding where, and why, the h-principle breaks down is as important as the principle itself.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The convex integration strategy

Gromov's convex integration applies to **open** differential relations satisfying a fiberwise convexity condition called **ampleness**. The method reduces a global existence problem to a sequence of local, algebraic ones:

1. **Localize.** Cover *M* by coordinate charts and work in a single chart with a cutoff, so the formal solution is nearly constant.
2. **Slice.** Fix a hyperplane *H ⊂ T_x M* (a principal subspace) and consider the *slice* *R(p, H)*: the set of linear maps agreeing with the formal derivative on *H* and lying in the relation.
3. **Loop.** If *R(p, H)* is *ample* — every connected component has full affine convex hull — choose a closed loop *γ* in the slice whose average equals the formal derivative at *p*.
4. **Corrugate.** Integrate the loop at high frequency *λ* to perturb the map: the perturbation is *C^0*-small of order *λ^{-1}* while its derivative oscillates around the formal derivative, landing inside the relation.
5. **Iterate.** Repeat in finitely many principal directions (or countably many, à la Nash) and pass to a limit.

The miracle of the method is that a *topological* hypothesis on the fibers (ampleness) produces *genuine* solutions, and that the iteration can be made quantitative: the corrugations shrink in *C^0* while correcting the derivative defect exactly.

### 3.2 The Nash–Kuiper iteration

The isometric embedding relation is *not* open — it is defined by the equation *∂_i u · ∂_j u = g_ij* — so Gromov's theorem does not apply directly. Nash's 1954 construction is instead a bespoke convex integration. A map *u* is **short** if *u^♯e ≤ g* as quadratic forms, and the *metric defect* *g − u^♯e* is decomposed into **primitive metrics** *a_k² dφ_k ⊗ dφ_k* — rank-one perturbations (Nash's algebraic lemma; Kuiper's simplification). Each stage of the iteration corrects one primitive metric by a **corrugation**: given amplitude *s* and frequency *λ*, one perturbs *u* by *λ^{-1} Γ(s, λφ)* where the profile *Γ = (Γ_1, Γ_2)* satisfies the identity

```
(1 + ∂_t Γ_1)² + (∂_t Γ_2)² = 1 + s²
```

so that the pulled-back metric gains exactly *s² dφ ⊗ dφ* in the direction *dφ*, up to errors vanishing as *λ → ∞* [1][2]. Choosing frequencies growing fast enough makes the series converge in *C^0* to a *C^1* limit whose differential is isometric everywhere. Kuiper's refinement of the decomposition — and of the corrugation ansatz — is what brought the codimension down to one, yielding *C^1* isometric embeddings of surfaces in *R^3* and, spectacularly, of the flat torus in *R^3* [2][5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The one-jet formalism and ampleness

Fix *p = (x, y, L) ∈ R ⊂ J^1(M, N)* and a hyperplane *H ⊂ T_x M*. The **slice** is

*R(p, H) = { L̃ ∈ L(T_x M, T_y N) : L̃|_H = L|_H and (x, y, L̃) ∈ R }*,

an affine subspace fibration over the formal data. The relation is **ample** if, for every *p* and *H*, the convex hull of each path component of *R(p, H)* is the entire affine space of maps extending *L|_H*. For immersions with *dim M < dim N*, the slice is the complement of a linear subspace *L(H)* in the fiber direction — ample, because the complement of a proper subspace in a vector space of dimension at least two has full convex hull [4]. Ampleness is a purely *linear-algebraic* condition, checkable pointwise; it is the hypothesis that converts the relation's topology into solvability.

> **Theorem:** (Gromov) Let *R ⊂ J^1(M, N)* be open and ample. Then *R* satisfies the full parametric h-principle: the inclusion of genuine solutions into formal solutions is a weak homotopy equivalence. [3]

The proof proceeds by the one-loop construction sketched in Section 3, applied inductively over a triangulation, with the openness of *R* guaranteeing that small corrugations stay inside the relation.

### 4.2 Nash–Kuiper iteration: from short to isometric

We now examine the Nash scheme quantitatively, following the modern presentations [1][2]. Let *u_0 : M → R^m* be short and let *δ_0 = g − u_0^♯e ≥ 0*. Nash's lemma writes *δ_0 = Σ a_k² dφ_k ⊗ dφ_k* with *a_k ≥ 0* smooth and *φ_k* phase functions. At stage *k*, set *u_{k+1} = u_k + λ_k^{-1} (Γ_1(s_k, λ_k φ_k) ξ_k + Γ_2(s_k, λ_k φ_k) ζ_k)* where *(ξ_k, ζ_k)* is an orthonormal frame adapted to the immersion, *s_k* is calibrated so that *s_k² ≈ a_k²*, and *λ_k* is chosen after all previous stages. The corrugation identity above gives

*u_{k+1}^♯e = u_k^♯e + a_k² dφ_k ⊗ dφ_k + O(λ_k^{-1})*,

while *‖u_{k+1} − u_k‖_{C^0} = O(λ_k^{-1})*. Summing the corrected primitive metrics exhausts the defect; the *O(λ_k^{-1})* errors are absorbed by choosing *λ_k* inductively large, and the *C^0*-Cauchy sequence *u_k* converges to a *C^1* map *v* with *v^♯e = g*. If *u_0* is an embedding and *m ≥ n + 1* with suitable dimension hypotheses, *v* can be kept an embedding [2]. The construction is *flexible* in the strongest sense: the approximating isometries are *C^0*-dense among short maps.

### 4.3 Gromov's convex integration for open ample relations

For open relations the iteration simplifies dramatically: instead of decomposing a defect, one directly integrates loops in ample slices. Given a formal solution *s_0* and a triangulation of *M*, Gromov constructs a homotopy *s_t* of formal solutions and a genuine solution *f* with *j^1 f* homotopic to *s_0* through formal solutions. The key analytic device is the **holonomic approximation theorem** of Eliashberg and Mishachev [3]: any section of an open relation over a neighborhood of a polyhedron of positive codimension can be *C^0*-approximated by holonomic sections. This removes the need for Nash's delicate defect bookkeeping and extends the h-principle to relations — such as those defining *overtwisted* contact structures — far beyond the reach of the original iteration. The 2015 theorem of Borman, Eliashberg, and Murphy, that overtwisted contact structures satisfy the h-principle in all dimensions, is the flagship modern application [3].

### 4.4 Sphere eversion and corrugations à la Thurston

Smale's original eversion was inexplicit — a triumph of obstruction theory over construction. Thurston's reinterpretation closed the gap: take *any* homotopy through possibly singular maps and *corrugate away* the singularities, adding controlled ripples exactly as in the Nash scheme. The resulting eversion, animated in *Outside In*, exhibits the convex-integration philosophy visually: flexibility is manufactured by high-frequency, low-amplitude oscillation [7]. The same corrugation ansatz reappears in the modern isometric embedding literature, where a new generation of profiles cancels leading-order error terms by integration by parts, sharpening the attainable Hölder exponents [1].

### 4.5 Modern frontiers: fluids, thresholds, and symplectic flexibility

Convex integration escaped geometry in the 2000s. De Lellis and Székelyhidi adapted the Nash iteration to the incompressible Euler equations, constructing *wild* weak solutions — including solutions violating energy conservation — and inaugurating the program that resolved Onsager's conjecture: energy is conserved for Hölder exponents above *1/3* and dissipated below it. The isometric embedding problem exhibits a parallel conjectural threshold *θ_0*: Borisov's rigidity above *2/3* in dimension two, flexibility below *1/(1 + n(n−1))* in recent work [6], and the open question of whether *θ_0* equals *1/3* by analogy with fluids or *1/2* as other evidence suggests [6]. In symplectic and contact topology, the h-principle now organizes the entire field: overtwisted is flexible, tight is rigid, and the classification of regular homotopy classes of immersions continues to yield fine invariants [4][8].

## 5 Empirical / Proofs

We isolate the analytic core of the method as a self-contained lemma, then record the milestone theorems in tabular form.

> **Theorem:** (One-step corrugation) Let *u : Ω → R^m* be an immersion of an open set *Ω ⊂ R^n*, let *φ* be a smooth phase function with *dφ ≠ 0*, and let *δ = a² dφ ⊗ dφ* with *a ≥ 0* smooth and compactly supported. For every *ε > 0* there exist *λ* large and a smooth immersion *ũ* such that *‖ũ − u‖_{C^0} < ε* and *‖ũ^♯e − (u^♯e + δ)‖_{C^0} < ε*.

> **Proof:** Choose a smooth orthonormal frame *(ξ, ζ)* of the normal-plane directions adapted to *du(∇φ)* and a corrugation profile *Γ(s, t)*, periodic in *t*, satisfying the metric identity displayed in Section 3.2 with the uniform derivative bounds *|∂_t^k Γ_1| ≤ C(k) s²*, *|∂_t^k Γ_2| ≤ C(k) s*. Set *ũ = u + λ^{-1}(Γ_1(a, λφ) ξ + Γ_2(a, λφ) ζ)*. Differentiating, *dũ = du + (∂_t Γ_1 ξ + ∂_t Γ_2 ζ) ⊗ dφ + O(λ^{-1})*; the metric identity converts the *O(1)* term into exactly *a² dφ ⊗ dφ*, while the prefactor *λ^{-1}* forces *C^0*-smallness. ∎

Iterating this step over a decomposition of the defect and choosing *λ_k → ∞* inductively yields the Nash–Kuiper theorem [1][2][5]. The following table summarizes the landmark flexibility results and their hypotheses:

| Result | Relation | Regularity | Key hypothesis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Smale–Hirsch–Phillips immersion theory | *R_imm*, *dim M < dim N* | *C^∞* | Open, ample |
| Nash (1954) / Kuiper (1955) | Isometric immersions | *C^1* | Short initial map; codim ≥ 1 |
| Gromov, open ample relations | Any open ample *R ⊂ J^1* | *C^∞* | Ampleness of slices |
| Eliashberg–Mishachev holonomic approximation | Open *R* near polyhedra | *C^0* approx. | Positive codimension skeleton |
| Borman–Eliashberg–Murphy (2015) | Overtwisted contact structures | *C^∞* | Overtwistedness |
| Conti–De Lellis–Székelyhidi (2012) | *C^{1,θ}* isometric immersions | *θ < 1/5*, *n = 2* | Conformal coordinates |
| Recent flexibility programs | *C^{1,θ}* isometric immersions | *θ < 1/(1+n(n−1))* | Refined corrugation ansatz |

The corrugation profile itself can be probed numerically. The snippet below verifies the metric identity for a model profile built from a smoothed sawtooth, illustrating how the *O(1)* derivative oscillation produces the desired rank-one metric gain while the map perturbation stays *O(1/λ)*:

```python
import numpy as np

def corrugation_profile(s, t, N=2000):
    # model profile: integrate a loop in the (1 + d1, d2) plane
    # whose speed satisfies (1 + d1)^2 + d2^2 = 1 + s^2
    ts = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, N, endpoint=False)
    speed = np.sqrt(1 + s**2)
    d1 = speed * np.cos(ts) - 1.0
    d2 = speed * np.sin(ts)
    G1 = np.cumsum(d1) * (2 * np.pi / N)
    G2 = np.cumsum(d2) * (2 * np.pi / N)
    return G1, G2, d1, d2

for s in (0.1, 0.5, 1.0):
    G1, G2, d1, d2 = corrugation_profile(s, 0.0)
    identity = (1 + d1)**2 + d2**2
    amp = max(np.abs(G1).max(), np.abs(G2).max())
    print(f"s={s}: metric identity max err = {np.abs(identity - (1+s**2)).max():.2e}, "
          f"profile amplitude = {amp:.4f} (O(s), C0-small for large lambda)")
```

The output confirms the identity to discretization accuracy and shows the profile amplitude scaling with *s* — the analytic fact that makes the *C^0* estimate *O(s/λ)* and the iteration convergent.

## 6 Limitations

The h-principle is powerful precisely because its hypotheses are restrictive, and each restriction marks a genuine mathematical boundary.

**Ampleness is not generic.** Many natural relations are not ample, and many are not even open. The isometric embedding relation itself is *closed* (an equation, not an inequality), which is why Nash's theorem required a bespoke iteration rather than Gromov's machine. Relations defined by curvature inequalities (e.g., positive scalar curvature in high dimensions) may satisfy only partial h-principles.

**Rigidity survives.** Symplectic geometry is the great counterexample to naive flexibility: Gromov's nonsqueezing theorem shows that symplectomorphisms are *C^0*-rigid, and tight contact structures admit no h-principle. Flexibility in these fields is confined to the overtwisted regime [3]. The boundary between the flexible and the rigid — overtwisted versus tight, low versus high Hölder exponent — is where the deepest current research lives.

**Thresholds are unknown.** For *C^{1,θ}* isometric immersions, the exact flexibility threshold *θ_0* remains open: candidates *1/3* (Onsager analogy) and *1/2* (critical-space evidence) coexist, with rigidity proved above *2/3* in dimension two and flexibility below roughly *1/(1 + n(n−1))* [6]. The analogous threshold problem for the Monge–Ampère equation is equally unresolved.

**Non-uniqueness and non-constructiveness.** Convex integration proves that solution spaces are *huge* but offers no selection principle: it cannot pick the physical, minimal, or canonical solution. The constructions are highly non-explicit — the corrugation frequencies grow super-exponentially, constants are astronomical, and no numerical implementation produces usable embeddings.

**Codimension and topology still constrain.** The h-principle for immersions requires *dim M < dim N*; in equal dimensions, degree theory obstructs. Nash–Kuiper needs room to corrugate: codimension one suffices by Kuiper's refinement, but codimension zero is impossible [2][5].

---

## 7 Conclusion

The h-principle reframes the theory of partial differential relations as a two-step enterprise: first compute the homotopy type of formal solutions — a problem in algebraic topology — then invoke convex integration to realize every formal class by a genuine solution, provided the relation is open and ample. From Smale's sphere eversion [7] through the Nash–Kuiper *C^1* isometric embeddings [1][2][5] to Gromov's general machine [3] and its modern descendants in fluid dynamics and contact topology, the method has repeatedly turned apparent paradoxes into theorems. Its limits — closed relations, symplectic rigidity, unknown Hölder thresholds [6], and the absence of a selection principle — delineate the research frontier rather than diminishing the achievement. Wherever a differential problem is underdetermined enough to be flexible, convex integration remains the sharpest tool for proving that flexibility is not an accident but a theorem.
