# The Fast Fourier Transform: Cooley–Tukey Factorization, FFTW's Adaptive Planning, and Number-Theoretic Transforms

## Abstract

The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) converts between time and frequency domains, but naive evaluation costs Θ(n²) operations. The Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT), published in 1965, factors a DFT of composite size n = n₁n₂ into smaller DFTs, achieving O(n log n) complexity [1]. This work derives the Cooley–Tukey factorization from index re-mapping, presents the radix-2 decimation-in-time and decimation-in-frequency butterflies, and examines FFTW's self-optimizing architecture: a dynamic-programming planner that composes plans from tuned codelets and caches them as wisdom [2]. We then cross the ring boundary into number-theoretic transforms (NTTs), where the complex root of unity is replaced by a primitive root in a finite field, enabling exact integer convolution [3][4]. The Schönhage–Strassen choice of R = Z/(2^t + 1)Z, Harvey and van der Hoeven's FFT-prime program, and the practical NTT+CRT strategy are analyzed [5][6]. Complexity models, operation counts, and error trade-offs between floating-point FFT and exact NTT are derived, with limits and open questions discussed.

## 1 Introduction

The discrete Fourier transform is one of the most consequential linear operators in applied mathematics. Given n complex samples, the forward DFT is

Y[k] = Σ_{ℓ=0}^{n−1} X[ℓ] · ω_n^{ℓk},  0 ≤ k < n,

with ω_n = exp(−2πi/n) a primitive n-th root of unity. Direct evaluation of this matrix–vector product, whose matrix is the Vandermonde matrix on the roots of unity, requires Θ(n²) arithmetic operations [7]. Before 1965, computing a DFT of 1024 points already strained the fastest machines; the quadratic cost made spectral analysis, convolution, and digital filtering expensive.

The 1965 paper of Cooley and Tukey changed the economics of the DFT by showing that a DFT of *composite* length n = n₁n₂ can be re-expressed in terms of smaller DFTs of sizes n₁ and n₂, separated by multiplications with *twiddle factors* [1]. Applied recursively to n = 2^γ, this reduces the complexity to N log₂ N operations, "arguably the second most important algorithm in practice" after sorting [7]. The algorithm had in fact been known to Gauss as early as 1805, who gave radix-3 and radix-6 examples, and was re-invented repeatedly before Cooley and Tukey popularized it [1].

Yet the basic recursion is only the beginning. On modern hardware, the *fastest* FFT is not a fixed recursive program: memory hierarchy, register count, vector units, and problem size interact in ways no closed-form schedule captures. FFTW ("FFT in the West") answers this with *adaptive planning*: its planner searches a space of factorizations and codelet compositions with dynamic programming, timing candidates on the actual machine, and reusing plans through a mechanism called *wisdom* [2]. In parallel, a different community noticed that the FFT's algebraic skeleton needs no floating-point arithmetic at all: replacing C with a ring containing a principal root of unity yields the *number-theoretic transform* (NTT), which computes exact convolutions used in cryptography, integer multiplication, and computer algebra [3][5].

This article gives a unified, rigorous treatment of all three: (i) the Cooley–Tukey factorization and its butterfly networks, (ii) FFTW's planner/codelet architecture as a solution to the *portable performance* problem, and (iii) the theory and practice of NTTs, including Schönhage–Strassen, FFT primes, and Harvey–van der Hoeven's quasi-linear integer multiplication program.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The DFT and its algebraic structure

Let F be a field and ω ∈ F a *primitive n-th root of unity*: ω^n = 1 and ω^d ≠ 1 for 0 < d < n. The DFT of length n over F is the linear map DFT(ω) represented by the Vandermonde matrix V_{k,ℓ} = ω^{kℓ}. Over F = C with ω_n = exp(−2πi/n), this is the classical transform; over F = F_p with a primitive root g, it becomes the NTT. The inverse transform is (1/n)·DFT(ω^{−1}), so any fast forward algorithm yields a fast inverse [7].

> Theorem: If n is a power of 2 and ω ∈ F is a primitive n-th root of unity, then DFT(ω) can be computed using O(n log n) field operations.

*Proof sketch.* Split the polynomial f(x) = Σ a_j x^j into even and odd parts: f(x) = f_even(x²) + x·f_odd(x²). Evaluating at the n roots requires evaluating two half-degree polynomials at n/2 points; since (ω^{k+n/2})² = (ω^k)², the two half-problems share evaluation points, halving the work recursively [7].

### 2.2 Convolution and polynomial multiplication

The DFT's practical importance flows from the *convolution theorem*: convolution of sequences corresponds to pointwise multiplication in the frequency domain. Multiplying two polynomials of degree < n (hence integers, viewed as base-B polynomials) reduces to:

1. Evaluate both at n points (forward transforms),
2. Pointwise multiply,
3. Interpolate (inverse transform).

Each step costs O(n log n) via the FFT, giving quasi-linear multiplication — the basis of Schönhage–Strassen's O(n log n log log n) integer multiplication and all its descendants [3].

### 2.3 Historical landscape

- **Gauss (1805):** radix-3/radix-6 FFT steps in unpublished interpolation work [1].
- **Cooley & Tukey (1965):** general mixed-radix algorithm, O(N log₂ N) analysis [1].
- **Schönhage & Strassen (1971):** FFT-based integer multiplication in O(n log n log log n) [3].
- **Frigo & Johnson (late 1990s–2000s):** FFTW — self-adapting FFT software with codelets and planning [2].
- **Fürer (2007):** first asymptotic improvement over Schönhage–Strassen in 35 years, using the ring C[x]/(x^P + 1) [3].
- **Harvey & van der Hoeven (2016–):** "plain vanilla FFT primes" program pushing toward the conjectured optimal O(n log n) bound [4]; their 2019 work achieves O(n log n) integer multiplication.
- **Cryptographic era (2010s–):** NTTs over rings like Z_q (q = 12289, 8380417) become the engine of lattice-based schemes (Kyber, Dilithium, Falcon), where exactness is mandatory [6].

## 3 Methodology

Our treatment proceeds in three layers, each verified against the cited primary sources:

1. **Algebraic layer.** We derive the Cooley–Tukey factorization from index maps ℓ = ℓ₁n₂ + ℓ₂ and k = k₁ + k₂n₁, obtaining the general composite-size recursion with explicit twiddle factors [1][8].
2. **Systems layer.** We model FFTW's architecture from its published design: the *planner* (dynamic programming over a DAG of solver nodes), *codelets* (straight-line kernels generated by the `genfft` special-purpose compiler), and *wisdom* (persistent plan cache) [2].
3. **Finite-ring layer.** We replace C with Z/pZ and with Z/(2^t + 1)Z, derive existence conditions for primitive roots (p ≡ 1 mod n), and compare three exact-convolution strategies: direct NTT over a large prime, NTT+CRT over multiple word-size primes, and Schönhage–Strassen's Fermat-ring recursion [3][4][6].

> Definition (NTT): For a prime p with n | (p−1) and primitive n-th root ζ ∈ F_p^×, the number-theoretic transform of a ∈ F_p^n is â[k] = Σ_{ℓ=0}^{n−1} a[ℓ] ζ^{ℓk}, computed by the same Cooley–Tukey recursion as the complex FFT, with all arithmetic modulo p [5][6].

All complexity claims are stated in the *arithmetic model* (field operations) unless noted, and we distinguish carefully between asymptotic cost, constant factors, and hardware-level effects such as cache misses and vectorization.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Cooley–Tukey factorization

The heart of the algorithm is index re-mapping. With n = n₁n₂, write ℓ = ℓ₁n₂ + ℓ₂ (ℓ₁ ∈ [0,n₁), ℓ₂ ∈ [0,n₂)) and k = k₁ + k₂n₁ (k₁ ∈ [0,n₁), k₂ ∈ [0,n₂)). Substituting into the DFT sum, the cross term N₁n₂·N₂k₁ vanishes (its exponential is unity), and the transform factors [1]:

> Theorem (Cooley–Tukey): A DFT of composite size n = n₁n₂ decomposes into n₁ DFTs of size n₂, multiplication by the *twiddle factors* ω_n^{ℓ₂k₁}, and n₂ DFTs of size n₁. With appropriate data re-ordering, this is a two-dimensional DFT of size n₁ × n₂ with a transposed output.

The derivation in modern form [2]:

Y[k₁ + k₂n₁] = Σ_{ℓ₂=0}^{n₂−1} [( Σ_{ℓ₁=0}^{n₁−1} X[ℓ₁n₂ + ℓ₂] ω_{n₁}^{ℓ₁k₁} ) ω_n^{ℓ₂k₁} ] ω_{n₂}^{ℓ₂k₂}.

Reading this inside-out: the inner sum is a size-n₁ DFT over strided data; the bracketed term is a twiddle multiplication; the outer sum is a size-n₂ DFT. The two nested sums commute, and transposing the data layout between them yields the classic *four-step framework*. Recursion continues until sizes are small enough for direct (or unrolled) evaluation.

*Radix-2 specialization.* For n = 2^γ with n₁ = 2, n₂ = n/2, each stage performs n/2 *butterflies*. A butterfly computes, from inputs a, b and twiddle w:

| Signal | Expression |
|---|---|
| Upper output | t = a + w·b |
| Lower output | u = a − w·b |

There are γ = log₂ n stages of n/2 butterflies, hence n log₂ n arithmetic operations (one complex multiply each, since multiplication by 1 is skipped) [8]. Two orderings dominate implementations: *decimation-in-time* (DIT), which separates even/odd inputs and produces bit-reversed output, and *decimation-in-frequency* (DIF), which produces natural-order output from bit-reversed twiddle application [8].

*Radix choice.* Cooley and Tukey's original analysis treated a radix-r butterfly as O(r²) work and concluded the optimal radix is 3, closest to *e* [1]. This was wrong: the butterfly is itself a DFT and can be done in O(r log r), so the radix cancels in the complexity. In practice, large radices (32, 64) matter for exploiting register files, and even r = √n (unbounded radix) achieves O(n log n) with practical advantages for large n [1].

### 4.2 FFTW: planning, codelets, and wisdom

FFTW's central insight is that the fastest FFT for a given (size, machine, stride) triple cannot be predicted — it must be *measured*. FFTW therefore separates concerns [2]:

- **Codelets** are small, straight-line computational kernels (e.g., a size-8 DFT with a particular vectorization) generated by `genfft`, a special-purpose compiler that emits highly optimized C from symbolic DFT expressions, exploiting common-subexpression elimination and algebraic simplification of twiddle factors [2].
- **Plans** are recursive compositions of codelets and "solvers" (Cooley–Tukey factorizations, Rader's algorithm for prime sizes, Bluestein's chirp-z for arbitrary sizes). A plan is a DAG specifying exactly how to compute a given transform.
- **The planner** uses dynamic programming over the space of plans: for each subproblem it times candidate solvers on the actual hardware and memoizes the fastest. Planning modes range from `ESTIMATE` (heuristics only) to `MEASURE` and `PATIENT`/`EXHAUSTIVE` (progressively larger searches) [2].
- **Wisdom** persists measured plans to disk, so the cost of planning is amortized across runs and even across machines of the same architecture.

This architecture means FFTW implements "a space of *many* Cooley–Tukey variants" rather than one algorithm: different factorizations of n, different data re-orderings of the transpositions, different vector-loop structures, and different codelet choices are all first-class plan citizens [2]. The practical consequence is portability of *performance*: the same library is near-optimal on x86, ARM, and PowerPC because the search, not the author, adapts to the machine.

*Pseudocode (planner sketch):*

```c
plan fftw_plan(n, in, out):
    if n in wisdom_cache: return cached plan
    best = ESTIMATE-heuristic plan
    for solver in applicable_solvers(n):       /* Cooley–Tukey, Rader, Bluestein, codelet */
        for subplan in planner(factor sizes):   /* dynamic programming recursion */
            candidate = compose(solver, subplan)
            t = time_execution(candidate)       /* measured on this machine */
            if t < best.time: best = candidate
    wisdom_cache[n] = best
    return best
```

### 4.3 Number-theoretic transforms: exactness by design

The Cooley–Tukey recursion uses only the ring axioms plus a primitive n-th root of unity. Replace C with a finite ring R containing such a root, and every butterfly becomes exact integer arithmetic — no rounding error, no catastrophic cancellation. This is the *number-theoretic transform* [5][6].

*Existence of roots.* In F_p^× (cyclic of order p−1), a primitive n-th root exists iff n | (p−1). Cryptographic practice therefore uses *NTT-friendly primes*: 998244353 = 119·2²³ + 1 supports n up to 2²³; 1004535809 = 479·2²¹ + 1 supports 2²¹. Choosing the prime first and the transform length second is the standard discipline.

*Three exact-convolution strategies* [3][4][5]:

1. **Single large prime.** If the convolution's coefficient bound B satisfies B < p/2, one NTT over F_p suffices and the result lifts directly to Z. Simple, but word-size overflow limits n.
2. **NTT + CRT (Montgomery–Silverman).** Pick distinct word-size primes p_j, each ≡ 1 (mod n) and larger than the coefficient bound, do the convolution modulo each p_j, and combine with the Chinese Remainder Theorem to recover the integer result [5]. This is the workhorse of computer algebra systems and of Stage 2 factoring.
3. **Schönhage–Strassen.** Work in R = Z/(2^t + 1)Z with t a power of two: here 2 is a primitive 2t-th root of unity "for free," and the transform length is decoupled from any prime's factorization [3]. The recursion multiplies the *twiddle-factor* operands recursively, yielding O(n log n log log n). Fürer later replaced R with C[x]/(x^P + 1) to enable large-radix FFTs and reach O(n log n · 2^{O(log* n)}) [3]; Harvey and van der Hoeven's "vanilla FFT prime" program (primes p = a·2^m + 1 with efficient roots) pushed the bound to the now-conjectured-optimal O(n log n) [4].

*Negacyclic convolution.* Lattice cryptography typically needs products modulo x^n + 1, not x^n − 1. Using a primitive *2n*-th root ψ with ψ² = ζ (an n-th root), the "twist" a'[ℓ] = a[ℓ]·ψ^ℓ converts cyclic to negacyclic convolution with no zero-padding — a standard NTT optimization in Kyber/Dilithium implementations [6].

### 4.4 FFT vs. NTT: a decision-theoretic comparison

| Criterion | Complex FFT (e.g., FFTW) | Number-theoretic transform |
|---|---|---|
| Arithmetic domain | C (floating point) | F_p or Z/(2^t+1)Z (exact) |
| Rounding error | Yes — grows ~O(√n·ε) with n [2] | None — result is exact |
| Root requirement | ω_n = e^{−2πi/n} always exists | Need n \| (p−1) or Fermat ring |
| Arbitrary n | Bluestein/Rader handle any n [2] | Bluestein works; roots may not exist |
| Vectorization | Mature (SSE/AVX/NEON codelets) | Modular mult needs Montgomery/Barrett |
| Best use | Signal processing, PDEs, imaging | Cryptography, exact algebra, big-integer mult |
| Asymptotic mult | O(n log n) (floating) | O(n log n) (Harvey–v.d. Hoeven) [4] |

The trade-off is fundamental: floating-point FFTs buy *generality* (any length, any data) at the price of *approximate* answers, while NTTs buy *exactness* at the price of *ring constraints* (suitable primes, length limits, CRT bookkeeping). For a 4096-bit RSA-style multiplication, floating-point FFT with rounding and error analysis is standard; for a lattice-based key exchange, only the NTT is acceptable [5][6].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 Operation counts and the n log n law

For radix-2 DIT on n = 2^γ, the exact count is instructive. Each of the γ stages performs n/2 butterflies; each butterfly does 1 complex multiply and 2 complex adds (the multiply-by-unity cases are skipped) [8]. A complex multiply is 4 real multiplies + 2 real adds (3/3 with Karatsuba-style), so a length-1024 FFT costs ≈ 5120 complex multiplies ≈ 30k real flops — versus 10⁶ for the naive DFT. The ratio n²/(n log₂ n) = n/log₂ n grows without bound: at n = 2²⁰ the FFT is ~50,000× cheaper arithmetically.

> Theorem (Complexity): The Cooley–Tukey FFT computes the DFT of size n = ∏ p_i (prime factorization) in O(n Σ p_i) operations; for n = 2^γ this is 5n log₂ n real flops with the standard butterfly, asymptotically O(n log n) [1][8].

### 5.2 Why planning beats closed-form schedules: a cache model

Consider n = 2^20 complex doubles: 16 MB of data vs. a typical 1–2 MB L2/L3 cache. A naive recursive FFT streams 16 MB through cache γ = 20 times. FFTW's planner instead tiles the transform: it chooses factorizations n = n₁n₂ with n₁ sized to fit cache, performs n₂ cache-resident transforms, then transposes — reducing capacity misses by an order of magnitude [2]. No fixed schedule is optimal across the (n₁, cache size, associativity, prefetch) space; the planner's *measured* search is what makes the cache-oblivious ideal concrete. Published FFTW results show it matching or beating vendor-tuned libraries (Intel MKL, IBM ESSL) across architectures precisely because the search adapts [2].

### 5.3 NTT error analysis: there is none — and the cost of that

The NTT's exactness is not free. Benchmarks in the literature consistently show per-butterfly costs 2–4× higher than floating-point: modular multiplication requires Barrett or Montgomery reduction (an extra multiply and correction steps), and the CRT reconstruction in multi-prime strategies costs O(k·n) for k primes plus Garner/CRT overhead [5]. The break-even analysis:

- n < 2^10: schoolbook or Karatsuba wins; NTT overhead dominates.
- 2^10 ≤ n < 2^16: single-prime NTT is typically optimal (one prime suffices for the coefficient bound).
- n ≥ 2^16: multi-prime NTT+CRT or Schönhage–Strassen, depending on coefficient size [3][5].

Harvey and van der Hoeven's analysis of "admissible" primes p = a·2^m + 1 shows the transform of length L = 2^ℓ decomposes via Cooley–Tukey into short transforms of length 2^s with s = (lg m)², with twiddle multiplications in F_p costing O(m (lg m)²) bit operations each — the fine structure that yields the O(n log n) integer-multiplication bound [4].

### 5.4 Numerical stability of the floating-point FFT

FFTW's accuracy analysis [2] shows the Cooley–Tukey FFT is *backward stable*: the computed result is the exact DFT of slightly perturbed input, with relative error growing like O(ε √log n) for well-ordered variants (DIT/DIF with proper scaling), where ε is machine epsilon. This is why double-precision FFTs are trustworthy to ~15 digits even at n = 2^24, and why single precision suffices for most signal processing. The NTT sidesteps this analysis entirely — a qualitative, not merely quantitative, advantage in cryptographic settings.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Composite-size dependence.** Cooley–Tukey's recursion needs composite n. Prime lengths fall back to Rader's algorithm (converting a prime-size DFT to a size-(n−1) convolution) or Bluestein's chirp-z (embedding in a power-of-two convolution), both with larger constant factors and extra memory [2]. Truly "FFT for all n at full speed" remains elusive.
2. **Planner overhead.** FFTW's `MEASURE`/`PATIENT` planning can take seconds to minutes for large multidimensional problems; wisdom mitigates but does not eliminate this, and plans are invalidated by stride or alignment changes [2].
3. **NTT prime scarcity.** Word-size primes with large 2-adic order are rare; 998244353 caps at 2²³, forcing CRT multi-prime strategies (with their reconstruction cost) for longer transforms [5]. The Harvey–van der Hoeven program needs *admissible* primes whose existence is proven only for large m [4].
4. **Memory bandwidth walls.** At fixed clock speeds, large FFTs are bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound; the arithmetic O(n log n) advantage is partly masked by the memory wall on GPUs and many-core CPUs.
5. **Quantum irrelevance (for now).** The quantum Fourier transform is exponentially faster in principle but outputs a quantum state, not the classical coefficients — it does not accelerate classical FFT workloads.
6. **Exactness vs. generality.** NTTs cannot handle arbitrary real-valued data; the complex FFT cannot give exact integer results. Hybrid applications (e.g., verified numerics) must pay for both.

## 7 Conclusion

The fast Fourier transform is best understood not as one algorithm but as an *algebraic pattern* — factor the transform along a composite index map, recurse, and multiply by twiddle factors — instantiated across three regimes. Cooley and Tukey's 1965 factorization turned the DFT from quadratic to quasi-linear and ignited digital signal processing [1]. FFTW showed that the pattern's *performance* is a search problem: plan over factorizations, codelets, and memory layouts, measure on the metal, and remember the answer as wisdom [2]. The number-theoretic transform showed that the pattern's *arithmetic* is optional: the same butterflies over F_p or Z/(2^t + 1)Z give exact convolution, powering everything from Schönhage–Strassen integer multiplication to post-quantum cryptography [3][4][5][6].

Open directions remain: closing the constant-factor gap between NTT and floating-point FFT butterflies via better modular arithmetic; extending the Harvey–van der Hoeven O(n log n) bound to fully explicit, practical algorithms; and adaptive planning for NTT libraries to match what FFTW did for complex transforms. The DFT's story — from Gauss's unpublished notes to lattice-based key exchange — suggests the next factorization is already latent in the algebra, waiting for its Cooley and Tukey.
