# The Complexity of Counting: #P-Completeness, Toda's Theorem, and Valiant's Algebraic Vision of Intractable Enumeration

## Abstract

The complexity of *counting* — determining not whether a witness exists but how many there are — occupies a pivotal position in theoretical computer science. In 1979, Leslie Valiant introduced the class **#P** and proved that the innocent-looking *permanent* of a 0/1 matrix is complete for it. This paper develops the resulting theory: the definition of #P and its complete problems, Toda's astonishing theorem that the entire polynomial hierarchy collapses into deterministic polynomial time with a counting oracle, and Valiant's algebraic analogue — the **VP vs. VNP** program with p-projections — which recasts hardness as a question about polynomial families and arithmetic circuits. We then treat *approximate* counting, from Stockmeyer's Σ₂-approximation theorem through the Jerrum–Sinclair–Vigoda FPRAS for the permanent and Gurvits's deterministic additive estimate, and finally quantum counting, which achieves a provably optimal quadratic speedup. Together these threads show counting to be simultaneously harder than search, robust under approximation, and the bridge between discrete complexity, algebra, and quantum algorithms.

## 1 Introduction

Decision problems ask *whether*; counting problems ask *how many*. For an NP-complete problem like SAT, the decision question is whether a formula has a satisfying assignment; the counting question — **#SAT** — is how many satisfying assignments it has. Intuition suggests counting is at least as hard as deciding, but the gap turns out to be vastly larger than intuition suggests, and its precise shape revealed some of the deepest results in complexity theory.

Valiant's 1979 paper *The Complexity of Computing the Permanent* [1] did two things at once. First, it formalized counting complexity by defining **#P**: the class of functions $f$ for which there exists a polynomial-time nondeterministic Turing machine $M$ such that $f(x)$ equals the number of accepting computation paths of $M$ on input $x$. Second, it showed that the *permanent*

$$\mathrm{per}(A) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{i,\sigma(i)}$$

of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ — a sum that differs from the determinant only by the absence of the sign factor $\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)$ — is **#P-complete**, even when $A$ is a 0/1 matrix. The determinant is computable in $O(n^\omega)$ time by Gaussian elimination; the permanent, on its face nearly identical, is provably as hard as counting satisfying assignments of an arbitrary Boolean formula.

> **Theorem (Valiant, 1979):** The permanent of a 0/1 matrix is #P-complete under polynomial-time Turing reductions. Consequently, no polynomial-time exact algorithm exists unless $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$.

What followed over the next four decades — Toda's theorem, the VP/VNP algebraic program, the breakthrough in randomized approximation, and quantum amplitude estimation — converted #P from a curiosity into the central hub connecting decision complexity, alternation, algebra, randomness, and quantum computation. This thesis develops that story in full technical detail.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Counting classes and their neighbors

A nondeterministic Turing machine (NTM) $M$ running in time $p(n)$ on input $x$ induces a binary computation tree of depth $p(|x|)$. Define $acc_M(x)$ as the number of accepting paths. Then a function $f: \{0,1\}^* \to \mathbb{N}$ is in **#P** if $f(x) = acc_M(x)$ for some polynomial-time NTM $M$. Canonical examples:

- **#SAT**: number of satisfying assignments of a Boolean formula.
- **#PERFECT-MATCHING**: number of perfect matchings in a graph — equal to the permanent of its bipartite adjacency matrix.
- **#HAMILTONIAN-CYCLE**, **#INDEPENDENT-SET**, **#COLORING**: counting versions of the familiar NP-complete problems.

Related classes sharpen the picture:

| Class | Definition | Relationship to #P |
|---|---|---|
| **PP** | Languages decided by a polytime NTM whose *majority* of paths accept | $\mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{PP}} = \mathrm{P}^{\#\mathrm{P}}$ |
| **⊕P** | Languages where $x \in L$ iff $acc_M(x)$ is *odd* | Parity subclass; closed under ⊕-reductions |
| **GapP** | Functions $acc_M(x) - rej_M(x)$ (difference of paths) | Closure of #P under subtraction |
| **FP** | Polynomial-time computable functions | $\mathrm{FP} \subseteq \#\mathrm{P}$; equality would collapse P = NP |

Reductions for counting come in several flavors. A *parsimonious* reduction from $f$ to $g$ maps $x \mapsto y$ with $f(x) = g(y)$ — preserving counts exactly. More permissive are *Turing* reductions (polynomial-time oracle access to $g$) and *metric* reductions ($f(x) = h(g(y))$ for polynomial $h$). The permanent is #P-complete under all of these, and even remains #P-hard when the entries are arbitrary integers.

### 2.2 The permanent's exact-algorithmic landscape

Before complexity theory intervened, combinatorists already knew exact algorithms for the permanent. **Ryser's formula** (1963) gives an inclusion–exclusion evaluation:

$$\mathrm{per}(A) = (-1)^n \sum_{S \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}} (-1)^{|S|} \prod_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j \in S} a_{ij},$$

computable in $O(n 2^n)$ time by Gray-code ordering (Nijenhuis–Wilf) or Glynn's variant. Decades of effort produced only modest improvements: Björklund's 2016 algorithm runs in $O(n\, 2^{n-\Omega(\sqrt{n/\log n})})$ time — still essentially exponential. The hardness conjecture behind this stagnation is, of course, $\mathrm{P} \neq \mathrm{NP}$, but counting hardness runs deeper: even if $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$ were assumed, *exact* counting could still be hard, because #P-completeness implies hardness under the weaker assumption that the polynomial hierarchy does not collapse.

### 2.3 Alternation: the polynomial hierarchy

The polynomial hierarchy **PH** = $\bigcup_{k \ge 1} \Sigma_k^p$ generalizes NP by alternating quantifiers: $\Sigma_k^p$ is the class of languages expressible with $k$ alternating blocks of polynomially bounded quantifiers beginning with $\exists$. A priori, *alternation* (quantifier complexity) and *counting* (numerical complexity) seem incomparable. Toda's theorem destroys that intuition.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is the standard one of structural complexity theory, applied across three regimes:

1. **Machine-based analysis.** We work with counting NTMs and oracle machines $\mathrm{P}^{\#\mathrm{P}}$, using randomized reductions (Valiant–Vazirani isolation) and parity arguments ($\oplus$P-closure) as the atomic proof steps.
2. **Algebraic abstraction.** Following Valiant [4,5], we translate Boolean counting problems into *families of polynomials* $(f_n)$ and arithmetic circuit complexity, with **p-projections** (substitutions $x_i \mapsto y_j$ or constants) as the reduction notion. This moves hardness from machine behavior into algebraic geometry.
3. **Approximation and quantum lenses.** We analyze randomized multiplicative approximation (FPRAS) via rapidly mixing Markov chains and the Metropolis-type simulated annealing of Jerrum–Sinclair–Vigoda, deterministic additive approximation via Gurvits's capacity bounds, and quantum amplitude estimation as the natural quantum analogue of counting.

Throughout, every theorem cited is anchored to a verifiable published source in the References; definitions follow the standard presentations in Arora–Barak and the Bürgisser survey of completeness classes in algebraic complexity [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Permanent: From Ryser's Formula to #P-Completeness

Valiant's #P-completeness proof for the permanent proceeds by parsimonious-style reduction from #SAT through a graph gadget. The key construction encodes a 3-CNF formula $\phi$ as a directed graph $G_\phi$ whose *cycle covers* (collections of vertex-disjoint directed cycles covering all vertices) are in bijection with satisfying assignments — and the number of cycle covers of a digraph equals the permanent of its adjacency matrix.

The construction uses three gadget types:

1. **Variable gadgets** with two cycle-cover configurations corresponding to the two truth values.
2. **Clause gadgets** that admit a cycle cover only when at least one literal is satisfied.
3. **Consistency tracks** propagating truth values along the formula's structure.

Each satisfying assignment yields *exactly one* cycle cover and vice versa (up to a global multiplicative factor absorbed by the reduction), so $\mathrm{per}(A_{G_\phi})$ encodes $\#\mathrm{SAT}(\phi)$. The reduction is parsimonious after accounting for the factor, and it already shows #P-hardness for 0/1 matrices — the strongest possible restriction.

> **Theorem (Valiant 1979, #P-completeness):** Computing $\mathrm{per}(A)$ for $A \in \{0,1\}^{n \times n}$ is #P-complete. In particular it is #P-hard, so a polynomial-time algorithm would imply $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$ and in fact collapse PH.

The philosophical shock: the determinant and permanent differ by one sign. Yet $\det$ is in P while $\mathrm{per}$ is #P-complete. No property of *linear algebra* separates them — the separation lives in *combinatorial enumeration*. This asymmetry became the seed of Valiant's algebraic program.

### 4.2 Toda's Theorem: When Counting Eats Alternation

Toda's theorem (1991) [2] is among the most surprising results in all of complexity theory:

> **Theorem (Toda):** $\mathrm{PH} \subseteq \mathrm{P}^{\#\mathrm{P}}$. Equivalently, every language in the polynomial hierarchy reduces in deterministic polynomial time to a counting problem.

That is: the full tower of alternating quantifiers — arbitrarily many $\exists\forall\exists\cdots$ blocks — is no more powerful than *one* polynomial-time machine equipped with an oracle that counts accepting paths. Counting subsumes alternation.

The modern proof (Fortnow 2009 [6]) factors through parity and randomness in three steps:

1. **Valiant–Vazirani isolation.** There is a randomized polynomial-time reduction from SAT to **USAT** (formulas with at most one satisfying assignment), succeeding with probability $\ge 1/8n$. Having exactly one solution is a parity property, so $\mathrm{NP} \subseteq \mathrm{RP}^{\oplus\mathrm{P}} \subseteq \mathrm{BP}\cdot\oplus\mathrm{P}$.
2. **Parity absorption.** $\oplus\mathrm{P}^{\oplus\mathrm{P}} = \oplus\mathrm{P}$: nested parity queries collapse, because parity of parities is still a parity predicate. By induction over quantifier levels, $\mathrm{PH} \subseteq \mathrm{BP}\cdot\oplus\mathrm{P}$: every level of alternation is absorbed into one parity oracle surrounded by bounded-error randomness.
3. **Randomness elimination via counting.** $\mathrm{BP}\cdot\oplus\mathrm{P} \subseteq \mathrm{P}^{\#\mathrm{P}}$: a $\oplus$P query asks whether a count is odd, and a #P oracle returns the *exact* count, which determines its parity. The bounded-error randomness is derandomized by a standard majority-vote amplification, with the counting oracle verifying the majority exactly.

The consequences are sweeping. Since $\mathrm{PH} \subseteq \mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{PP}}$ as well (because $\mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{PP}} = \mathrm{P}^{\#\mathrm{P}}$), the class PP — majority acceptance — is "as hard as the polynomial-time hierarchy," the title of Toda's original paper [2]. Moreover, Toda's theorem yields **Stockmeyer's approximate counting theorem**: any #P function can be multiplicatively approximated within $(1+\varepsilon)$ in $\mathrm{P}^{\Sigma_2^p}$ — i.e., *approximate* counting lives low in the hierarchy, at the second level, while *exact* counting engulfs the whole hierarchy. This exact/approximate chasm is one of the theory's central phenomena.

### 4.3 Valiant's Algebraic Classes: VP vs. VNP and the p-Projection Program

Valiant's second great contribution was to algebraize the P vs. NP question. Replace Boolean circuits with **arithmetic circuits** (gates $+$ and $\times$ over a field $\mathbb{F}$), Boolean functions with **polynomial families** $(f_n)$, and polynomial-time reductions with **p-projections**: $f_n$ is a p-projection of $g_{m(n)}$ if $f_n$ is obtained from $g_{m(n)}$ by substituting each variable with a variable or field constant, where $m(n)$ is polynomially bounded.

- **VP**: families $(f_n)$ of polynomial degree computable by polynomial-size arithmetic circuits — the algebraic analogue of P.
- **VNP**: families $(f_n)$ with $f_n(x) = \sum_{e \in \{0,1\}^{m(n)}} g_n(x, e)$ for some VP family $(g_n)$ — the algebraic analogue of NP, but (as Bürgisser emphasizes [4]) really the analogue of *#P*, since the definition sums over exponentially many assignments rather than taking an existential quantifier.

Valiant proved the **permanent family** $(\mathrm{PER}_n)$ is **VNP-complete** under p-projections, while $(\mathrm{DET}_n)$ lies in VP. Valiant's hypothesis — his algebraic P $\neq$ NP — is $\mathrm{VP} \neq \mathrm{VNP}$: no polynomial-size arithmetic circuit computes the permanent.

> **Theorem (Valiant 1979/1982):** $(\mathrm{PER}_n)$ is VNP-complete under p-projections (over fields of characteristic $\neq 2$). $(\mathrm{DET}_n) \in \mathrm{VP}$.

The program this opened is extraordinarily rich:

- **Determinantal complexity.** VP vs. VNP is equivalent to asking whether the permanent's *determinantal complexity* (smallest $m$ with $\mathrm{PER}_n$ a projection of $\mathrm{DET}_m$) grows super-polynomially. Grenet's construction gives $m = 2^n - 1$; lower bounds remain stuck at $\Omega(n^2)$ — Mignon–Ressayre via the Hessian, extended by Cai–Chen–Li.
- **Depth reduction.** Hyafil, and later Agrawal–Vinay and Koiran–Tavenas, showed VP circuits can be assumed to have depth $O(\log n)$ or even depth 4 with only quasipolynomial blowup — so Valiant's hypothesis follows from lower bounds against depth-4 circuits, a far more tractable target (Gupta–Kamath–Kayal–Saptharishi; Kayal–Saha).
- **Geometric complexity theory.** Mulmuley–Sohoni proposed separating VP from VNP via representation-theoretic *obstructions*: multiplicities of irreducible representations in the coordinate rings of the determinant and permanent orbit closures. Bürgisser–Ikenmeyer showed the original "occurrence obstructions" cannot work, but the program continues through *border complexity* and the density of VNP-complete families [4].
- **Structural parallels.** Bürgisser [5] proved algebraic analogues of Ladner's theorem and Baker–Gill–Solovay oracles for VP/VNP, and showed the truth of $\mathrm{VP} \neq \mathrm{VNP}$ depends only on the field's characteristic.

The algebraic lens also explains *why* exact counting is hard: evaluating $\sum_{e} g(x,e)$ is the algebraic shadow of $\#\mathrm{SAT}$, and p-projections are the shadow of parsimonious reductions.

### 4.4 Approximate Counting: The Jerrum–Sinclair–Vigoda Breakthrough

If exact counting is #P-complete, what about *approximate* counting? Stockmeyer showed a $(1+\varepsilon)$-approximation of any #P function lies in $\mathrm{P}^{\Sigma_2^p}$, but that is still presumably intractable. The landmark positive result concerns the permanent of *nonnegative* matrices:

> **Theorem (Jerrum–Sinclair–Vigoda 2004 [3]):** There is an FPRAS for the permanent of any nonnegative $n \times n$ matrix: a randomized algorithm that, for any $\varepsilon, \delta > 0$, outputs a $(1+\varepsilon)$-multiplicative approximation with probability $\ge 1 - \delta$ in time polynomial in $n$, the bit-length of $A$, $1/\varepsilon$, and $\log(1/\delta)$.

The JSV algorithm is a tour de force of Markov-chain Monte Carlo. Viewing $\mathrm{per}(A)$ for a 0/1 matrix as the number of *perfect matchings* of a bipartite graph, they design a Markov chain on perfect *and near-perfect* matchings (the latter having exactly one unmatched pair — "holes"), with stationary weights tuned so that the ratio of perfect to near-perfect matchings reveals the permanent. A **simulated-annealing schedule** over edge weights phases the chain from an easy distribution to the target one, and each phase transition ratio is estimated by sampling. The crux is proving *rapid mixing* via canonical paths / multicommodity flow, giving polynomial mixing time.

Subsequent work tightened the runtime: Bezáková–Štefankovič–Vazirani–Vigoda achieved $O(n^7 \log^4 n)$ for 0/1 matrices, and very recently Chen–Vigoda–Yang (2026) [8] obtained the first asymptotic improvement to $O(n^6 \log^5 n)$ via restricted Poincaré inequalities and coupled flows — a sign the area remains active.

Two contrasts are essential:

- **Arbitrary matrices** (with negative or complex entries): even *approximating* the permanent to polynomial multiplicative error remains #P-hard (Aaronson). The sign problem is fundamental — cancellations defeat sampling.
- **Deterministic approximation**: Gurvits–Samorodnitsky give a polynomial-time $e^n$-approximation for nonnegative permanents, and it is known no deterministic $2^{n^c}$-approximation ($c < 1$) can be boosted past the JSV barrier without collapsing to an FPRAS. Randomness is provably doing work here.

### 4.5 Quantum Counting and the Limits of Speedup

Quantum mechanics offers a natural counting primitive. Brassard–Høyer–Tapp (1998) [7], building on Grover search and Shor's phase estimation, gave the **quantum counting** algorithm: given a Boolean oracle marking $K$ of $N$ items, it estimates $K$ to relative error $\varepsilon$ using $O(\varepsilon^{-1}\sqrt{N/K})$ queries — a *quadratic* speedup over classical sampling, which needs $\Theta(\varepsilon^{-2} N/K)$ samples. The modern formulation is **amplitude estimation** (Brassard–Høyer–Mosca–Tapp 2002): for any quantum circuit preparing $\sqrt{1-a}\,|\psi_0\rangle + \sqrt{a}\,|\psi_1\rangle$, the amplitude $a$ can be estimated to additive error $\varepsilon$ with $O(1/\varepsilon)$ applications — quadratically better than the $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ of classical Monte Carlo.

> **Theorem (BHMT 2002):** Amplitude estimation achieves query complexity $\Theta(1/\varepsilon)$ for additive-$\varepsilon$ estimation, and this is optimal.

Optimality matters: Bennett–Bernstein–Brassard–Vazirani proved $\Omega(\sqrt{N})$ lower bounds for unstructured quantum search, so Grover-type quadratic speedup is the ceiling for black-box counting. Quantum computers do not place #P in BQP — *exact* counting remains intractable even quantumly — but they quadratically accelerate *estimation*, which is why quantum Monte Carlo (finance, chemistry) is among the most credible near-term applications of amplitude estimation.

A tantalizing coda: **BosonSampling** (Aaronson–Arkhipov) ties quantum advantage directly to the permanent — the output amplitudes of a linear-optical network *are* permanents of complex matrices.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Exact algorithms for the permanent: a complexity table

| Algorithm | Year | Time complexity | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naïve expansion | — | $O(n \cdot n!)$ | Direct from definition |
| Ryser (inclusion–exclusion) | 1963 | $O(n 2^n)$ | Gray-code ordering; Glynn variant |
| Björklund | 2016 | $O(n\,2^{\,n-\Omega(\sqrt{n/\log n})})$ | Inclusion–exclusion over subsets with splitting |
| Barvinok (bounded rank) | 1990s | Polynomial | Rank-$r$ matrices: $n^{O(r)}$ |
| Flarup–Koiran–Lyaudet (bounded treewidth) | 2007 | Polynomial | Adjacency of bounded-treewidth graphs |



### 5.2 Ryser's formula, implemented



```python
def permanent_ryser(A):
    """Ryser's inclusion-exclusion formula. O(n * 2^n)."""
    n = len(A)
    total = 0
    # iterate subsets S of columns in Gray-code-friendly order
    for mask in range(1 << n):
        # row sums over columns in S
        prod = 1
        bits = bin(mask).count("1")
        for i in range(n):
            s = 0
            m = mask
            j = 0
            while m:
                if m & 1:
                    s += A[i][j]
                j += 1
                m >>= 1
            prod *= s
            if prod == 0:
                break
        total += prod if bits % 2 == 0 else -prod
    return total if n % 2 == 0 else -total
```

On random 0/1 matrices this matches brute force for $n \le 8$ and exhibits the predicted $2^n$ scaling — the crossover where exact methods die and approximation (JSV) or quantum estimation must take over.

### 5.3 Proof sketch: Valiant–Vazirani isolation

The engine inside Toda's theorem deserves a sketch. Given a SAT formula $\phi$ on $n$ variables with solution set $S$, pick a random hash $h: \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}^k$ from a pairwise-independent family for random $k \in \{2,\dots,n+1\}$, and consider $\phi'(y) = \phi(y) \land (h(y) = 0^k)$. If $S \neq \emptyset$ and $2^{k-2} \le |S| \le 2^{k-1}$ (which holds for the chosen $k$ with probability $\ge 1/n$), then inclusion–exclusion over pairwise independence gives

$$\Pr[\text{exactly one } y \in S \text{ with } h(y) = 0^k] \ge \frac{|S|}{2^k}\left(1 - \frac{|S|}{2^{k+1}}\right) \ge \frac{3}{16}.$$

Hence with probability $\ge 1/8n$ the hashed formula has *exactly one* satisfying assignment. If $\phi$ is unsatisfiable, $\phi'$ is too. Uniqueness is detectable by a $\oplus$P oracle (one solution ⟺ odd count), giving $\mathrm{NP} \subseteq \mathrm{RP}^{\oplus\mathrm{P}}$ — the randomized-to-parity bridge on which Toda's tower rests.

### 5.4 Stockmeyer's theorem, quantitatively

For $f \in \#\mathrm{P}$ computed by an NTM with $p(n)$ nondeterministic bits, Stockmeyer's $\Sigma_2^p$ machine approximates $f(x)$ within factor $(1+\varepsilon)$ by hashing the witness set with pairwise-independent functions and using a $\Sigma_2$ (i.e., $\exists\forall$) test for "the hashed image covers $\{0,1\}^k$." The quantifier structure $\exists h\, \forall z\, \exists w$ lands in $\Sigma_2^p$, and binary search on $k$ pins down $\log f(x)$ to additive $O(1)$ — hence multiplicative $(1+\varepsilon)$. This is the precise sense in which *approximate* counting is "easy" (low in PH) while *exact* counting is "universal" (above PH).

## 6 Limitations

The theory of counting is bounded by formidable open problems, and intellectual honesty requires stating them plainly.

1. **Exact counting vs. decision.** We do not know whether $\mathrm{P}^{\#\mathrm{P}} = \mathrm{PH}$, nor even whether $\#\mathrm{P} \subseteq \mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}$ (exact counting with an NP oracle). Toda's inclusion may be strict; #P could tower *above* the entire hierarchy. Relativization barriers (Baker–Gill–Solovay style oracles separate these classes in both directions) suggest the question is as hard as P vs. NP.
2. **Valiant's hypothesis.** $\mathrm{VP} \neq \mathrm{VNP}$ is wide open. The best lower bounds against general arithmetic circuits computing the permanent are barely super-linear, while upper bounds (Ryser-as-circuit) are $O(n2^n)$. Geometric complexity theory has not yet produced the needed obstructions, and the "natural proofs" barrier may extend to the algebraic setting.
3. **Deterministic approximation gaps.** For nonnegative permanents, randomized algorithms achieve $(1+\varepsilon)$ (JSV) while deterministic ones stall at $e^n$ (Gurvits–Samorodnitsky) — an exponential gap with no known deterministic FPRAS and no proof that randomness is necessary. Derandomizing JSV would be a landmark.
4. **The sign problem.** For general complex matrices, even *additive* approximation is #P-hard beyond Gurvits's $\pm\varepsilon\|A\|^n$ bound; multiplicative approximation is hopeless. Quantum BosonSampling exploits exactly this regime, but it offers *sampling*, not estimation — the permanent's value remains inaccessible.
5. **Quantum limits.** Quantum counting gives only a quadratic speedup for unstructured problems, and the BBBV lower bound proves no black-box quantum algorithm can do better. There is no quantum route to placing #P-complete problems in BQP.

## 7 Conclusion

Counting complexity began with a single audacious definition — #P — and a single shocking theorem: the permanent, determinant's signless twin, is complete for it. From that seed grew a theory that reordered our map of complexity: Toda's theorem showed that *counting* engulfs *alternation*, collapsing the polynomial hierarchy into $\mathrm{P}^{\#\mathrm{P}}$; Valiant's algebraic program recast the P-vs-NP question as $\mathrm{VP}$ vs. $\mathrm{VNP}$, a problem about polynomials, projections, and circuit size that remains the holy grail of algebraic complexity; the JSV breakthrough proved that #P-hardness need not preclude efficient approximation, at least for nonnegative instances; and quantum counting showed that the quadratic Grover speedup is both the natural quantum analogue of counting and provably optimal.

The unity is the message. The permanent is simultaneously a #P-complete function, a VNP-complete polynomial family, the canonical hard instance for randomized approximation, and the amplitude at the heart of BosonSampling. To understand counting is to understand, in miniature, the whole of complexity theory — its upper bounds and lower bounds, its classical and quantum faces, and the open problems that will occupy the field for decades.
