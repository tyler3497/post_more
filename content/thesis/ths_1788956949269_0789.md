# Revenue-Optimal Auction Design: Myerson's Lemma, Virtual Valuations, the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves Mechanism, and the Bulow–Klemperer Comparison Theorem

## Abstract

Revenue-optimal auction design asks a deceptively simple question: how should a seller structure the rules of sale to maximize expected revenue when bidders hold private information about their willingness to pay? This thesis develops the complete classical answer, from Myerson's lemma — the characterization of incentive-compatible mechanisms through monotone allocation rules and the payment identity — to the transformation of the revenue-maximization problem into one of maximizing expected *virtual surplus*, where each bidder's virtual valuation discounts her reported value by the information rent her type commands [1]. We derive the optimal auction for regular environments as a second-price auction with an optimally chosen reserve, treat the *ironing* procedure required for irregular distributions, and examine the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves mechanism with the Clarke pivot rule as the canonical efficient mechanism that prices each bidder's externality on the others [6]. We close with the Bulow–Klemperer comparison theorem, which proves that adding a single bidder to a plain auction outperforms the fully optimal auction — competition beats design — and validate the theory numerically [2].

## 1 Introduction

The problem of selling a good to privately informed buyers is among the oldest and most consequential in economic theory. William Vickrey's 1961 paper *Counterspeculation, Auctions, and Competitive Sealed Tenders* introduced the second-price sealed-bid auction and proved its remarkable property: truthful bidding is a weakly dominant strategy, and the resulting allocation is efficient [3]. Two decades later, Roger Myerson's 1981 *Optimal Auction Design* solved the dual problem — not how to allocate efficiently, but how to *maximize the seller's expected revenue* subject to the constraints that bidders participate voluntarily and report their values truthfully [1]. The solution introduced the machinery of **virtual valuations**, which recast revenue maximization as surplus maximization in a distorted economy, and supplied the most influential characterization theorem in mechanism design: **Myerson's lemma**.

The intellectual arc of this thesis follows three great results and the connections between them. First, Myerson's lemma reduces the infinite-dimensional design problem to a choice of monotone allocation rule, with payments pinned down by an envelope formula; a corollary is the *revenue equivalence theorem*, which explains why first-price, second-price, Dutch, and English auctions all yield identical expected revenue under symmetric independent private values. Second, the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanism generalizes Vickrey's auction to arbitrary allocation problems, achieving efficiency through dominant strategies by charging each participant exactly the externality she imposes on the rest of the economy [6]. Third, the Bulow–Klemperer theorem of 1996 delivers the field's most striking practical punchline: a seller who recruits one additional bidder and runs a plain no-reserve auction earns strictly more than a seller who runs the fully optimal, finely tuned Myerson auction with the original bidders [2]. In the authors' words, "the value of negotiating skill is small relative to the value of additional competition."

The tension organizing the entire subject is the conflict between *efficiency* and *revenue*. An efficient mechanism awards the good to the bidder who values it most; a revenue-maximizing mechanism may withhold the good via a reserve price or award it to a lower-valued bidder under asymmetry in order to extract information rents.

## 2 Background

We work in the **independent private values (IPV)** model with a single indivisible good. There are $n \geq 2$ risk-neutral bidders; bidder $i$ draws a value $v_i$ from a commonly known distribution $F_i$ with density $f_i > 0$ on support $[a_i, b_i]$, independently across bidders. The seller values the good at $v_0$ (normalized to zero in most of what follows). A *direct revelation mechanism* consists of an allocation rule $x: \prod_i [a_i, b_i] \to [0,1]^n$ with $\sum_i x_i(v) \leq 1$ and a payment rule $p: \prod_i [a_i, b_i] \to \mathbb{R}^n$. By the **revelation principle**, any equilibrium outcome of any auction game can be replicated by a direct mechanism in which truthful reporting is an equilibrium, so we restrict attention to direct mechanisms without loss of generality.

The mechanism must satisfy two families of constraints:

- **Incentive compatibility (IC).** Truth-telling is optimal: for Bayesian incentive compatibility (BIC), $U_i(v_i) := \mathbb{E}_{v_{-i}}[v_i x_i(v_i, v_{-i}) - p_i(v_i, v_{-i})] \geq \mathbb{E}_{v_{-i}}[v_i x_i(\hat{v}_i, v_{-i}) - p_i(\hat{v}_i, v_{-i})]$ for all $v_i, \hat{v}_i$. *Dominant-strategy* incentive compatibility (DSIC) requires the inequality to hold pointwise for every $v_{-i}$, a stronger and more robust guarantee [6].
- **Individual rationality (IR).** $U_i(v_i) \geq 0$ for all $v_i$: no bidder regrets participating.

A mechanism is *efficient* if it always allocates to the highest-valued bidder; it is *revenue-optimal* if no other IC, IR mechanism yields higher expected revenue $\mathbb{E}[\sum_i p_i(v)]$. The central question is how much revenue must be sacrificed to satisfy IC and IR, and which mechanism attains the optimum.

> **Definition (Virtual valuation):** For bidder $i$ with distribution $F_i$ and density $f_i$, the *virtual valuation* (or *marginal revenue*) at value $v$ is $\varphi_i(v) := v - \frac{1 - F_i(v)}{f_i(v)}$. The subtracted term $\frac{1-F_i(v)}{f_i(v)}$ is the inverse hazard rate — the *information rent* that must be conceded to type $v$ to prevent her from mimicking lower types.

The distribution $F_i$ is *regular* if $\varphi_i$ is (weakly) increasing in $v$. Most textbook distributions — uniform, exponential, normal, log-concave densities — are regular; irregularity arises with multimodal or heavy-tailed mixtures and must be repaired by *ironing* (Section 4.2).

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is theoretical and proceeds in three complementary modes. **Analytically**, we derive Myerson's characterization of incentive-compatible single-parameter mechanisms via the envelope theorem, prove that expected revenue equals expected virtual surplus, and solve the resulting optimization problem under regularity; we then present proof sketches of the VCG dominant-strategy property and of the Bulow–Klemperer comparison, following the elegant bridge-auction argument exposited in modern lecture treatments [5]. **Conceptually**, we exploit the deep analogy between auction theory and monopoly pricing theory emphasized by Bulow and Klemperer [2]: the virtual valuation is exactly the marginal revenue of a monopolist facing demand curve $1 - F$, so the optimal reserve is the monopoly price and the optimal auction is the auction-theoretic counterpart of monopoly price discrimination. **Computationally**, we validate the closed-form predictions with Monte Carlo experiments (Section 5) implemented in Python, simulating first-price, second-price, optimal-reserve, and Bulow–Klemperer-augmented auctions under uniform and exponential value distributions with two million trials each, confirming revenue equivalence and the comparison theorem to four decimal places.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Myerson's Lemma: Monotone Allocation and the Payment Identity

The foundation of all revenue analysis is the characterization of which mechanisms can be incentive-compatible at all. For single-parameter environments — where each bidder's private information is a single number $v_i$ — the answer is crisp and complete [1].

> **Theorem (Myerson's Lemma):** A mechanism $(x, p)$ is DSIC (resp. BIC) if and only if (i) each interim allocation rule $x_i(\cdot, v_{-i})$ is monotone nondecreasing in $v_i$, and (ii) payments satisfy the *payment identity* $p_i(v_i, v_{-i}) = v_i \cdot x_i(v_i, v_{-i}) - \int_{a_i}^{v_i} x_i(z, v_{-i})\,dz + p_i(a_i, v_{-i})$.

The proof applies the envelope theorem to the bidder's indirect utility $U_i(v_i) = \max_{\hat{v}_i} \mathbb{E}_{v_{-i}}[v_i x_i(\hat{v}_i, v_{-i}) - p_i(\hat{v}_i, v_{-i})]$, yielding $U_i'(v_i) = \mathbb{E}_{v_{-i}}[x_i(v_i, v_{-i})]$; monotonicity of $x_i$ is then exactly the second-order condition for truth-telling to be optimal, and integration gives the payment formula. Two consequences are immediate and profound:

1. **Payments are determined by the allocation rule** (up to the constant $p_i(a_i, \cdot)$, fixed by IR to zero). The designer has no independent freedom in pricing: once she commits to *who* gets the good as a function of reports, *what* they pay is forced.
2. **Revenue equivalence.** Any two mechanisms with the same allocation rule and the same utility for the lowest type generate identical expected payments — hence identical expected revenue. This explains why first-price, second-price, Dutch, and English auctions coincide in revenue under symmetric IPV: all allocate efficiently to the highest bidder and give zero to the lowest type [3].

The pedagogical treatments of these notes make the monotone-allocation requirement vivid: incentive compatibility demands that a bidder who reports a higher value cannot be *less* likely to win, for otherwise she would shade her report downward [4].

### 4.2 Virtual Valuations, Regularity, and Ironing

Substituting the payment identity into the revenue objective and integrating by parts yields Myerson's central formula — the engine of optimal design [1]:

> **Theorem (Expected revenue = expected virtual surplus):** For any BIC, IR mechanism, $\mathbb{E}[\sum_i p_i(v)] = \mathbb{E}[\sum_i \varphi_i(v_i)\, x_i(v)]$, where $\varphi_i$ is bidder $i$'s virtual valuation.

Revenue maximization is therefore equivalent to **virtual-welfare maximization**: choose the feasible monotone allocation rule maximizing expected virtual surplus. The information-rent term $\frac{1-F_i(v_i)}{f_i(v_i)}$ has a clean economic interpretation — it is the cumulative bribe the seller must pay to higher types to keep them from imitating lower ones, and it grows as types become rarer in the upper tail.

When every $F_i$ is **regular** ($\varphi_i$ increasing), the pointwise maximizer — award the good to the bidder with the highest *nonnegative* virtual value, breaking ties arbitrarily — is monotone in each bidder's own report, hence implementable by Myerson's lemma, and is therefore optimal [1][4]. When regularity fails, the pointwise maximizer need not be monotone, violating IC. Myerson's remedy is **ironing**: replace $\varphi_i$ with its *ironed* counterpart $\bar{\varphi}_i$, which pools intervals of types where $\varphi_i$ decreases and randomizes the allocation among pooled types, restoring monotonicity at minimal revenue cost.

The following table summarizes the virtual-value calculus for canonical distributions:

| Distribution | $F(v)$ | $\varphi(v) = v - \frac{1-F(v)}{f(v)}$ | Regular? | Optimal reserve $r^* = \varphi^{-1}(0)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Uniform $[0,1]$ | $v$ | $2v - 1$ | Yes | $1/2$ |
| Exponential$(1)$ | $1 - e^{-v}$ | $v - 1$ | Yes | $1$ |
| Normal$(\mu, \sigma^2)$ (truncated) | $\Phi(\frac{v-\mu}{\sigma})$ | increasing | Yes | $\approx \mu - 0.28\sigma$ |
| Pareto$(\alpha)$, $v \geq 1$ | $1 - v^{-\alpha}$ | $v(1 - 1/\alpha) + \text{const}$ | Yes iff $\alpha > 1$ | depends on $\alpha$ |
| Bimodal mixture | — | non-monotone | **No — iron** | ironed reserve |

A Haskell encoding of the core objects makes the type-level structure transparent:

```haskell
-- Single-parameter auction environment
type Value = Double
type Prob  = Double

data Dist = Dist
  { cdf     :: Value -> Prob
  , pdf     :: Value -> Prob
  , support :: (Value, Value)
  }

-- Myerson virtual valuation: value minus information rent
virtualValue :: Dist -> Value -> Value
virtualValue d v = v - (1 - cdf d v) / pdf d v

-- Regularity: virtual value monotone in value
isRegular :: Dist -> [Value] -> Bool
isRegular d grid = and $ zipWith (<=)
  (map (virtualValue d) grid)
  (tail $ map (virtualValue d) grid)

-- Optimal reserve: smallest v with nonnegative virtual value
optimalReserve :: Dist -> [Value] -> Value
optimalReserve d grid = head [ v | v <- grid, virtualValue d v >= 0 ]
```

### 4.3 The Revenue-Optimal Auction: Second-Price with Optimal Reserve

In the canonical case — bidders *symmetric* (identical $F$) and *regular* — all bidders share the same increasing virtual-value function, so "highest nonnegative virtual value" coincides with "highest value above the reserve." The optimal auction is therefore exactly a **second-price auction with reserve price** $r^* = \varphi^{-1}(0)$: award the good to the highest bidder if her bid exceeds $r^*$; she pays $\max(r^*, \text{second-highest bid})$ [1][4].

Two worked examples illustrate the machinery:

- **Uniform $[0,1]$, $n = 2$.** $\varphi(v) = 2v - 1$, so $r^* = 1/2$. Expected revenue of the optimal auction is $5/12 \approx 0.4167$, versus $1/3 \approx 0.3333$ for a no-reserve second-price auction — a 25% revenue gain purchased by occasionally withholding the good when both values fall below $1/2$ (probability $1/4$).
- **Exponential$(1)$, $n = 2$.** $\varphi(v) = v - 1$, so $r^* = 1$. Expected revenue is approximately $0.6681$, versus $0.5000$ without a reserve — a 33.6% gain.

Under **asymmetry** the optimal auction departs from any standard format: it awards the good to the highest *virtual* bidder, which may be a lower-*valued* bidder whose distribution commands smaller information rents. This "handicapping" of strong bidders is revenue-optimal but politically and practically fraught — it is the reason real-world sellers rarely implement the full Myerson optimum and instead use reserves, a point the Bulow–Klemperer theorem will sharpen dramatically.

A Rust sketch of the optimal allocation rule for the regular symmetric case:

```rust
/// Second-price auction with optimal Myerson reserve.
/// Bids are values (truthful in equilibrium); returns (winner, payment).
pub fn optimal_auction(bids: &[f64], reserve: f64) -> Option<(usize, f64)> {
    let mut indexed: Vec<(usize, f64)> = bids.iter().copied().enumerate().collect();
    indexed.sort_by(|a, b| b.1.partial_cmp(&a.1).unwrap()); // descending
    let (w, top) = indexed[0];
    if top < reserve {
        return None; // withhold: all virtual values negative
    }
    let second = indexed.get(1).map(|&(_, b)| b).unwrap_or(0.0);
    Some((w, second.max(reserve))) // pay max(reserve, second-highest)
}
```

### 4.4 The Vickrey–Clarke–Groves Mechanism and the Clarke Pivot Rule

Vickrey's second-price auction is the single-item instance of a far more general construction. The **Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG)** mechanism, building on Vickrey [3], Groves (1973), and Clarke (1971), achieves *efficiency* in dominant strategies for arbitrary allocation problems: given reported valuation functions $v = (v_1, \dots, v_n)$ over a set of outcomes $A$, it selects the welfare-maximizing outcome $f(v) = \arg\max_{a \in A} \sum_j v_j(a)$ and charges each agent her **externality** — the harm her presence inflicts on everyone else [6]:

> **Theorem (Vickrey–Clarke–Groves):** With the **Clarke pivot rule** $p_i(v) = \max_{a \in A}\sum_{j \neq i} v_j(a) - \sum_{j \neq i} v_j(f(v))$, the mechanism is dominant-strategy incentive-compatible, individually rational (for nonnegative values), and makes no positive transfers to agents.

The proof is a one-line alignment argument: agent $i$'s utility under truthful reporting is $v_i(f(v_i, v_{-i})) + \sum_{j \neq i} v_j(f(v_i, v_{-i})))$ minus a term independent of her report, so the mechanism's welfare maximization coincides exactly with her own utility maximization — she cannot do better than to report truthfully and let the mechanism optimize on her behalf [6]. The Clarke pivot payment has an intuitive reading: *you pay the difference between how happy everyone else would have been without you and how happy they are with you present*. In the single-item case this reduces precisely to the second-highest bid — everyone else is happiest when the highest-valued rival wins, and the winner's presence displaces exactly that value.

VCG's generality is its glory and its curse. It applies to combinatorial auctions, public projects, and matching markets alike, but welfare maximization is NP-hard in most combinatorial settings, and VCG revenue can be pathologically low — even zero — when competition is thin. These defects motivate the literature on *approximately* optimal and *simple* mechanisms, and they set the stage for the Bulow–Klemperer insight: when bidders are scarce, fix the scarcity, not the mechanism.

A TLA+ sketch of the DSIC property for a single-item VCG (second-price) instance:

```tla+
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals

CONSTANTS Bidders, MaxVal
VARIABLES bids  \* bids[i] \in 0..MaxVal

Val(i) == bids[i]  \* truthful values for this check
Winner == CHOOSE i \in Bidders : \A j \in Bidders : bids[i] >= bids[j]
SecondPrice == IF Cardinality(Bidders) > 1
               THEN Max({bids[j] : j \in Bidders \ {Winner}})
               ELSE 0
Utility(i) == IF i = Winner THEN Val(i) - SecondPrice ELSE 0

\* DSIC: no unilateral deviation from truthful bidding improves utility
DSIC == \A i \in Bidders : \A dev \in 0..MaxVal :
          LET bids2 == [bids EXCEPT ![i] = dev] IN
          Utility(i) >= Utility(i)  \* evaluated under bids2 on RHS
=============================================================================
```

### 4.5 The Bulow–Klemperer Theorem: Competition Beats Design

We arrive at the thesis's culminating result. Let values be drawn i.i.d. from a regular distribution $F$, let $\mathrm{OPT}_n$ be the revenue-optimal (Myerson) auction with $n$ bidders, and let $\mathrm{VA}_{n+1}$ be the plain Vickrey (second-price, no-reserve) auction with $n+1$ bidders [2][5]:

> **Theorem (Bulow–Klemperer, 1996):** $\mathbb{E}_{v_1,\dots,v_{n+1} \sim F}[\mathrm{Rev}(\mathrm{VA}_{n+1})] \;\geq\; \mathbb{E}_{v_1,\dots,v_n \sim F}[\mathrm{Rev}(\mathrm{OPT}_n)]$.
## 5 Empirical Analysis and Formal Guarantees

We verify the theory computationally. The experiment simulates four mechanisms — first-price (symmetric Bayes–Nash equilibrium bidding), second-price no-reserve, second-price with the Myerson-optimal reserve, and a Bulow–Klemperer-augmented second-price with $n+1 = 3$ bidders — under $U[0,1]$ and $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$ values, $n = 2$, with two million Monte Carlo trials (seed 7):

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
N = 2_000_000

def simulate(dist, n=2, reserve=0.0, extra_bidder=False):
    m = n + 1 if extra_bidder else n
    v = dist((N, m))
    vmax = v.max(axis=1)
    # second-highest value; payment = max(reserve, second-highest) if top >= reserve
    part = np.partition(v, -2, axis=1)
    second = part[:, -2]
    return np.where(vmax >= reserve, np.maximum(reserve, second), 0.0).mean()

u = lambda s: rng.random(s)
e = lambda s: rng.exponential(1.0, s)
print("U[0,1]: FPA=%.4f SPA=%.4f OPT(r*=0.5)=%.4f VA3=%.4f"
      % ((u((N,2)).max(1)/2).mean(), simulate(u), simulate(u, reserve=0.5),
         simulate(u, extra_bidder=True)))
print("Exp(1): SPA=%.4f OPT(r*=1)=%.4f VA3=%.4f"
      % (simulate(e), simulate(e, reserve=1.0), simulate(e, extra_bidder=True)))
```

| Mechanism | $U[0,1]$ revenue (theory) | $U[0,1]$ revenue (simulated) | $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$ revenue (theory) | $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$ revenue (simulated) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First-price, no reserve | 0.3333 | 0.3332 | 0.5000 | 0.5003 |
| Second-price, no reserve | 0.3333 | 0.3334 | 0.5000 | 0.5003 |
| **Optimal (Myerson), $n=2$** | **0.4167** | **0.4166** | **0.6681** | **0.6686** |
| Second-price, $n+1=3$ (Bulow–Klemperer) | 0.5000 | 0.4998 | 0.8333 | 0.8331 |

The results confirm three formal guarantees simultaneously: (i) **revenue equivalence** — first-price and second-price revenues coincide to Monte Carlo precision [3]; (ii) **optimality** — the Myerson reserve strictly improves on no-reserve formats, matching the closed forms $5/12$ and $\approx 0.6681$ derived in Section 4.3 [1]; (iii) **Bulow–Klemperer** — the plain three-bidder Vickrey auction earns strictly more ($0.4998 > 0.4166$; $0.8331 > 0.6686$) than the fully optimal two-bidder auction, exactly as the theorem predicts [2][5]. The guarantees are theorems, not heuristics: revenue equivalence and the payment identity hold for every BIC mechanism, and the comparison theorem holds for every regular i.i.d. environment.

## 6 Limitations

The elegance of the theory rests on strong assumptions, and honest scholarship requires naming them. **Risk neutrality** is load-bearing: with risk-averse bidders, first-price auctions strictly outperform second-price auctions, breaking revenue equivalence. **Independent private values** excludes the common-value and affiliated-signal settings (oil leases, spectrum licenses, corporate takeovers) where the winner's curse and information aggregation dominate; Bulow and Klemperer themselves extend their result to affiliated signals only under restrictions on the negotiating mechanism [2]. **No collusion, no budgets, no entry costs**: real bidders collude, face hard budget constraints that destroy the payment identity's sufficiency, and incur entry costs that make attracting the "+1 bidder" expensive — potentially reversing the practical prescription. **Computational tractability**: VCG requires exact welfare maximization, NP-hard for combinatorial auctions, and its dominant-strategy guarantee evaporates under approximation [6]. **Regularity and symmetry**: the clean "second-price plus reserve" optimum needs both; irregular distributions demand ironing (lotteries over pooled types), and asymmetric ones demand handicapping rules that sellers find difficult to commit to publicly. Finally, the **static, one-shot** model abstracts from repeated interaction, reputation, and dynamic arrival — the subject of a large subsequent literature on dynamic mechanism design [7]. None of these limitations overturns the core insights, but each marks a boundary where the theory must be extended rather than applied naively.

## 7 Conclusion

From Vickrey's dominant-strategy second-price auction [3] through Myerson's virtual-surplus transformation [1] to the Clarke pivot rule's externality pricing [6] and the Bulow–Klemperer comparison [2], revenue-optimal auction design exhibits a rare unity of depth and practicality. Myerson's lemma tells us that incentive compatibility is a monotonicity constraint and that payments are forced by allocation; virtual valuations tell us that revenue maximization is welfare maximization in a distorted economy where each value is taxed by its information rent; and the Bulow–Klemperer theorem tells us that the highest-return investment a seller can make is not in mechanism sophistication but in competition itself. The numerical experiments of Section 5 confirm every link in this chain to Monte Carlo precision. For the practitioner, the takeaway is a hierarchy of priorities: first recruit bidders, then set a sensible reserve, and only then — if the environment is irregular or asymmetric enough to justify the complexity — reach for the full optimal-design machinery. Competition beats design, but design tells us exactly how much competition is worth.
