# Differential Privacy at Scale: Definitions, Relaxations, Accounting, and Amplification

## Abstract

Differential privacy has evolved from a single worst-case definition into a family of quantitative frameworks that trade interpretability against analytical sharpness. This thesis surveys that landscape end to end: pure and approximate differential privacy with the Laplace and Gaussian mechanisms; Rényi differential privacy and zero-concentrated differential privacy, which express privacy loss through Rényi divergences and compose almost linearly; the differentially private stochastic gradient descent algorithm and its moments accountant, the first practical route to training deep networks with meaningful guarantees; privacy amplification by subsampling and by shuffling, which turn randomness in data access into concrete budget savings; and the composition theorems that bind these ideas together. We state definitions precisely, sketch the key proofs, quantify the privacy-utility tradeoffs reported in the literature, and close with the limitations and deployment realities of differential privacy at industrial scale.

## 1 Introduction

Differential privacy (DP), introduced by Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, and Smith [1], is the dominant mathematical framework for reasoning about the privacy of data analyses. Its central promise is *stability*: the output distribution of a randomized algorithm should barely change when a single record in the input dataset is added or removed. That promise is operationalized through a small set of parameters, most famously epsilon and delta, whose semantics have been scrutinized, relaxed, and generalized for two decades.

Three forces motivate the modern, pluralistic view of differential privacy. First, the classical epsilon-DP definition is *brittle*: many natural algorithms, notably those based on Gaussian noise, cannot satisfy it at all, and it composes additively in a way that rapidly exhausts any budget over thousands of adaptive queries. Second, machine learning workloads such as deep neural network training demand privacy accounting over *tens of thousands* of sequential, data-dependent steps, a regime where naive composition yields vacuous guarantees. Third, deployed systems — from the 2020 US Census Disclosure Avoidance System to industrial telemetry pipelines — need accounting frameworks that are both tight enough to preserve utility and simple enough to implement reliably in production.

This thesis develops the full stack. Section 2 reviews pure and approximate DP and the canonical noise mechanisms. Section 3 presents the methodology of divergence-based relaxations: Rényi DP [4] and zero-concentrated DP [3], together with the conversion lemmas that translate their guarantees back into the familiar epsilon-delta language. Section 4 dives into five pillars — pure and approximate DP, RDP and zCDP, DP-SGD with the moments accountant [5] and its analytical subsampled-RDP refinement [6], amplification by subsampling and shuffling [7][8], and the composition theorems that unify them. Section 5 collects proof sketches and the empirical privacy-utility numbers reported in the literature. Section 6 is candid about limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

Two datasets are *neighboring* if they differ in exactly one record; we consider both the add-or-remove and the replace-one-record variants, and all results below are agnostic to the choice. A randomized mechanism M maps datasets to output distributions. The *privacy loss* of an observed output o with respect to a neighboring pair D, D-prime is the log-likelihood ratio c(o) = log (Pr[M(D) = o] / Pr[M(D-prime) = o]), a random variable whose distribution fully characterizes what an adversary learns.

A mechanism satisfies **pure epsilon-DP** when, for all neighboring datasets and all measurable output sets S, the probability of landing in S can change by at most a multiplicative factor of exp(epsilon) [1]. **Approximate (epsilon, delta)-DP** relaxes this by permitting an additive delta slack, which absorbs the low-probability catastrophic events that pure DP forbids [1]. The *sensitivity* of a query f is the worst-case change in f over neighboring datasets, measured in the appropriate norm; calibrating noise to sensitivity is the foundational design principle of the field [1].

Two mechanisms dominate practice. The **Laplace mechanism** adds noise drawn from Lap(Delta-1 / epsilon) to a query of L1-sensitivity Delta-1 and achieves pure epsilon-DP [1]. The **Gaussian mechanism** adds isotropic Gaussian noise N(0, sigma-squared I); with sigma at least Delta-2 times sqrt(2 ln(1.25/delta)) divided by epsilon, it achieves (epsilon, delta)-DP for epsilon in the unit interval [1]. The Gaussian mechanism is the workhorse of private machine learning because its tails compose far more gracefully than Laplace noise under repeated queries.

Two meta-properties underpin everything that follows. *Post-processing immunity*: no function applied to the output of a DP mechanism can weaken its guarantee. *Composition*: running several DP mechanisms on the same data yields a combined guarantee that is a function of the individual ones — additively for pure DP, and sublinearly under the advanced composition theorem of Dwork, Rothblum, and Vadhan for approximate DP. Tracking these composed losses over long adaptive sequences is the central technical problem of the field.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is to treat differential privacy not as one definition but as a *family of divergences* over output distributions, each inducing its own accounting algebra. The privacy loss random variable c(o) is the common currency: pure DP bounds its essential supremum, approximate DP bounds its tails, and the divergence-based relaxations bound its moment generating function.

Concretely, we proceed in four stages. **First**, we fix the classical definitions and mechanisms (pure and approximate DP) and their composition rules, establishing the baseline whose brittleness motivates everything else [1]. **Second**, we introduce Rényi DP [4] and zero-concentrated DP [3], which bound the Rényi divergence D-alpha between output distributions on neighboring inputs for orders alpha greater than one, and we state the conversion lemmas that recover (epsilon, delta)-DP from either relaxation. **Third**, we study the moments accountant [5] as an applied instantiation of the RDP viewpoint: DP-SGD clips per-example gradients to bound sensitivity, injects Gaussian noise, and accumulates a bound on the log moment generating function of the privacy loss across training steps, with Poisson subsampling providing amplification analyzed tightly via subsampled RDP [6]. **Fourth**, we examine amplification by shuffling [7][8], which shows that anonymity alone can convert local-DP reports into strong central-DP guarantees, and we situate all of these inside the composition landscape.

Throughout, we distinguish the *central* (trusted curator) model from the *local* model in which each user randomizes their own data before release; shuffling lives at the boundary between them.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Pure and Approximate Differential Privacy

Pure epsilon-DP is the strongest and most interpretable guarantee in the family: it bounds the worst-case privacy loss with no exceptions, it is closed under post-processing, and it composes additively — k mechanisms with parameters epsilon-1 through epsilon-k compose to the sum of the epsilons [1]. Its price is rigidity. The Gaussian mechanism, arguably the most useful primitive in statistics and machine learning, cannot satisfy pure DP at any finite epsilon, because Gaussian tails are too heavy relative to a shifted Gaussian at arbitrarily many standard deviations. Any analysis that insists on pure DP must therefore use Laplace noise or worse, paying a substantial utility cost in high dimensions.

Approximate (epsilon, delta)-DP recovers the Gaussian mechanism by allowing a delta-probability failure event [1]. The parameter delta is conventionally required to be cryptographically small — smaller than the inverse of any polynomial in the dataset size n — so that the failure mode cannot be exploited at scale. But (epsilon, delta)-DP has its own weakness: composition. Basic composition adds both epsilons and deltas linearly, while advanced composition reduces the epsilon growth to roughly the square root of the number of compositions at the cost of a more complex formula and a small additional delta.

### 4.2 Rényi Differential Privacy and Zero-Concentrated Differential Privacy

Rényi differential privacy, introduced by Mironov [4], replaces the worst-case bound on the privacy loss with a bound on its *Rényi divergence* of order alpha.

Two properties make RDP an accounting breakthrough [4].

> Theorem: (RDP to DP conversion, Mironov [4]). If a mechanism is (alpha, epsilon)-RDP, then it is (epsilon + log(1/delta)/(alpha - 1), delta)-DP for every delta in the open unit interval.

By optimizing the right-hand side over alpha, one recovers the advanced composition theorem up to small constants and typically beats it by a wide margin, because the conversion exploits the actual shape of the privacy loss distribution rather than a worst-case bound on it [4].

Zero-concentrated differential privacy (zCDP), formulated by Bun and Steinke [3] building on Dwork and Rothblum's concentrated DP [2], is the natural special case in which the RDP profile is *linear*: a mechanism is rho-zCDP when D-alpha is at most rho times alpha for all alpha greater than one.

Differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD), introduced by Abadi et al.

The innovation of [5] is not the algorithm but its *accounting*.

The follow-up line of work on subsampled RDP [6] tightened this analysis further, giving exact RDP bounds for the subsampled Gaussian mechanism and an *analytical* moments accountant that evaluates the privacy loss in closed form rather than by numerical integration.

### 4.4 Privacy Amplification by Subsampling and Shuffling

*Amplification* refers to the phenomenon that a randomized selection procedure applied before a DP mechanism strengthens the resulting guarantee. The classical case is **amplification by subsampling**: running an epsilon-DP mechanism on a random q-fraction of the data yields a guarantee on the order of q times epsilon, because any single individual's data is absent from the computation with probability close to one minus q. The tight analyses of this effect via couplings and divergences, and its RDP refinements for the subsampled Gaussian mechanism, are what make DP-SGD's accounting work [6]. Intuitively, subsampling converts the *possibility* of participation into a multiplicative discount on the privacy loss, and Poisson subsampling is preferred in theory because each record's inclusion is an independent coin flip, which simplifies the divergence calculations.

**Amplification by shuffling** [7][8] exploits a different resource: *anonymity*. Consider the local model, where each of n users applies an epsilon-0-DP local randomizer to their own data. If the n reports are then uniformly permuted by a trusted shuffler before analysis, the anonymity of the shuffle hides which report came from whom, and the central guarantee improves dramatically: Erlingsson et al. showed that any permutation-invariant algorithm satisfying epsilon-0 local DP satisfies (O(epsilon-0 sqrt(log(1/delta)/n)), delta)-central DP [7]. Feldman, McMillan, and Talwar refined this to a nearly optimal bound scaling as (1 - e^{-epsilon-0}) times sqrt(e^{epsilon-0} log(1/delta)/n), via a simpler "hiding among the clones" analysis that also extends to approximate DP and RDP and yields the first asymptotically optimal analysis of noisy SGD under sampling without replacement [8].

The practical moral, emphasized in [7], is striking: several industrial local-DP deployments may be *much more private* than their advertised epsilon values suggest, provided the reports are genuinely anonymized.

### 4.5 Composition Theorems and the Conversion Landscape

Composition theorems are the load-bearing walls of differential privacy, and the frameworks differ principally in how gracefully they compose.

| Framework | Parameters | Composition rule | Conversion to (epsilon, delta)-DP |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pure DP | epsilon | additive: sum of epsilons [1] | exact (delta = 0) |
| Approximate DP | (epsilon, delta) | basic: additive; advanced: sublinear in epsilon | exact |
| RDP | (alpha, epsilon(alpha)) | additive per alpha: sum of epsilons [4] | epsilon + log(1/delta)/(alpha-1), optimized over alpha [4] |
| zCDP | rho | additive: sum of rhos [3] | rho + 2 sqrt(rho ln(1/delta)) [3] |

Three observations organize this table. **First**, RDP and zCDP compose additively with no approximation loss, which is why they dominate modern accounting: one simply sums the per-step profile and converts once at the end, rather than converting at every step and accumulating conversion slack [3][4]. **Second**, conversions are one-way and lossy in general: RDP and zCDP imply (epsilon, delta)-DP, and pure DP implies zCDP, but there exist mechanisms with meaningful (epsilon, delta)-DP guarantees whose RDP parameters are vacuous — the relaxations are not lossless descriptions of the privacy loss distribution. **Third**, the optimal composition theorem for (epsilon, delta)-DP is known to be computationally intractable in general, which is a pragmatic argument for working in RDP or zCDP natively and converting only for reporting: the intractable exact answer is well approximated by the tractable relaxation [4].

A unifying perspective is that each framework bounds a different functional of the privacy loss random variable — its supremum (pure DP), its tails (approximate DP), or its moment generating function (RDP, zCDP, moments accountant) — and composition theorems are, at bottom, statements about how those functionals behave under sums of independent or adaptively chosen losses.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Proof sketch: Laplace mechanism [1].** For a query f with L1-sensitivity Delta-1, the mechanism outputs f(D) + Z with Z drawn from Lap(Delta-1/epsilon). For neighboring D, D-prime and any output o, the ratio of densities is exp(-epsilon(|o - f(D)| - |o - f(D-prime)|)/Delta-1), which the triangle inequality bounds by exp(epsilon). Integrating over any measurable set gives pure epsilon-DP.

**Proof sketch: RDP composition [4].** The Rényi divergence of order alpha satisfies a quasi-chain-rule under adaptive composition: the joint divergence of the composed mechanism's output distribution factorizes, via Hölder's inequality applied to the density ratios, into the sum of the per-mechanism divergences at the same order alpha. Hence (alpha, epsilon-1)-RDP followed adaptively by (alpha, epsilon-2)-RDP is (alpha, epsilon-1 + epsilon-2)-RDP, with no dependence on adaptivity beyond the per-step bounds.

**Proof sketch: zCDP conversion [3].** If D-alpha is at most rho alpha for all alpha greater than one, then the moment generating function of the privacy loss satisfies E[exp((alpha-1) c)] ≤ exp(rho alpha (alpha-1)). A Chernoff bound, Pr[c > epsilon] ≤ exp(rho alpha (alpha-1) - (alpha-1) epsilon), optimized over alpha, yields the tail bound Pr[c > rho + 2 sqrt(rho ln(1/delta))] ≤ delta, which is exactly the (epsilon, delta)-DP guarantee.

**Empirical privacy-utility tradeoffs.** The DP-SGD paper [5] reported approximately 97% test accuracy on MNIST and 73% on CIFAR-10 at epsilon = 8 and delta = 1e-5, against non-private baselines near 99% and 86% respectively — the first demonstration that deep networks could be trained with single-digit epsilon budgets. The RDP-based analytical accountant [6] uniformly improves these budgets at fixed utility, typically saving a factor of two or more in epsilon for long training runs. In the shuffle model, the Feldman et al. bounds [8] show that with n = 10^6 users and epsilon-0 = 4 local randomization, the central epsilon after shuffling drops into the low single digits — a hundred-fold-class improvement attributable to anonymity alone [7][8].

The following Python snippet implements the core RDP accounting loop used in modern DP-SGD libraries: evaluate the Gaussian mechanism's RDP profile, accumulate it over training steps, and convert once to (epsilon, delta)-DP by optimizing over orders [4][6].

```python
import numpy as np

def rdp_gaussian(alpha: float, sigma: float) -> float:
    """RDP of order alpha for the Gaussian mechanism (unit L2 sensitivity) [4]."""
    return alpha / (2.0 * sigma ** 2)

def rdp_to_dp(eps_rdp: float, alpha: float, delta: float) -> float:
    """Convert (alpha, eps_rdp)-RDP to (eps, delta)-DP, Mironov Prop. 3 [4]."""
    return eps_rdp + np.log(1.0 / delta) / (alpha - 1.0)

def dp_sgd_epsilon(sigma: float, steps: int, q: float, delta: float) -> float:
    """Total (eps, delta)-DP for T subsampled Gaussian steps [5][6]."""
    # Per-step subsampled RDP (small-q approximation of the exact bound in [6])
    alphas = np.arange(2, 65)
    step_rdp = np.array([rdp_gaussian(a, sigma) for a in alphas]) * q ** 2
    total_rdp = step_rdp * steps            # RDP composes additively [4]
    epsilons = [rdp_to_dp(e, a, delta) for e, a in zip(total_rdp, alphas)]
    return float(min(epsilons))             # optimize over orders alpha

print(dp_sgd_epsilon(sigma=4.0, steps=10000, q=0.01, delta=1e-5))
```

**Deployed systems.** The 2020 US Census Disclosure Avoidance System manages its global privacy-loss budget in rho (zCDP) units across thousands of statistical tables, converting to (epsilon, delta) only for public reporting — the largest real-world deployment of concentrated-DP accounting [3]. Apple has shipped local differential privacy in consumer operating systems since 2016 for telemetry such as emoji suggestions and lookup hints, and Google's RAPPOR pioneered randomized-response-based local DP for Chrome statistics. As observed in [7], several such industrial local-DP deployments are substantially more private in the central model than their advertised local epsilon values indicate, whenever report collection is anonymized — a fact that shuffle-model architectures are now designed to exploit deliberately.

## 6 Limitations

The divergence-based relaxations have genuine blind spots. RDP and zCDP are *lossy* summaries of the privacy loss distribution: there exist mechanisms with meaningful (epsilon, delta)-DP guarantees for which no nontrivial RDP parameters exist, so accounting natively in RDP can be arbitrarily pessimistic for some algorithms. The parameters themselves are harder to interpret than epsilon and delta — a rho of 2.5 or an RDP profile carries little operational meaning for a policymaker deciding whether a data release is safe, and the final conversion to (epsilon, delta) reintroduces slack that the tight accounting was meant to avoid.

DP-SGD's limitations are practical as well as theoretical. Per-example gradient clipping requires per-sample gradient norms, which defeats the batch-level parallelism that makes GPU training fast; private training is routinely an order of magnitude slower than non-private training. Clipping introduces a bias–variance tradeoff governed by the threshold C that interacts poorly with adaptive optimizers, and the utility gap remains large on complex tasks: private ImageNet-scale training still lags far behind non-private accuracy at defensible epsilon values. The moments accountant's bounds, while vastly tighter than naive composition, are upper bounds on the *worst-case* privacy loss over neighboring datasets and over the randomness of adaptively chosen hyperparameters — they do not capture privacy amplification from early stopping, data augmentation, or other algorithmic choices unless those are explicitly modeled.

Amplification results come with trust caveats. Subsampling amplification assumes the adversary does not observe *which* records were sampled; in federated or interactive settings where participation is visible, the amplification evaporates. Shuffling amplification assumes an honest, non-colluding shuffler and genuine anonymity of reports — metadata, timing, or network identifiers can silently destroy the guarantee [7][8]. Finally, all of these frameworks quantify *relative* risk: they bound how much more an adversary learns from your participation versus your absence, not the absolute risk of the released statistics, and choosing epsilon remains an irreducibly normative decision that mathematics alone cannot make.

## 7 Conclusion

Differential privacy has grown from a single definition into an ecosystem of frameworks, each bounding a different functional of the privacy loss random variable. Pure and approximate DP [1] provide the interpretable foundation; Rényi DP [4] and zero-concentrated DP [3] provide the accounting algebra that makes composition tractable; the moments accountant [5] and its subsampled-RDP refinement [6] turned that algebra into the first practical private deep learning; and amplification by subsampling and by shuffling [7][8] showed that randomness in *data access* is as valuable as randomness in *noise*. The composition theorems of Section 4.5 are the threads that stitch these together: additivity where the divergence permits it, careful conversion where it does not.

The trajectory of the field points toward tighter, more automated accounting — numerical privacy-loss-distribution methods that are exact rather than upper bounds — and toward deployment architectures, like the shuffle model, that extract privacy from system design rather than from noise alone. But the central lesson of two decades is unchanged since [1]: privacy is a property of the *algorithm*, quantified in advance, composed explicitly, and budgeted like any other scarce resource. The frameworks surveyed here are the ledger in which that budget is kept.
