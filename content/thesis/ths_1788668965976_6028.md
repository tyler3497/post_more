# Constraint Programming with Lazy Clause Generation: CP-SAT Solving, AllDifferent Propagation, Conflict-Driven Learning, and Industrial Scheduling at Scale

## Abstract

**Abstract.** Constraint programming (CP) offers rich modeling through global constraints and powerful domain propagation, while Boolean satisfiability (SAT) contributes conflict-driven clause learning (CDCL) and unit propagation at industrial scale. Lazy clause generation (LCG), introduced by Ohrimenko, Stuckey, and Codish [1], unifies these traditions: every propagator explains each domain reduction with a clausal justification, allowing a finite-domain solver to perform CDCL-style learning over a dynamically generated Booleanization. This thesis presents the LCG architecture and its culmination in Google OR-Tools' CP-SAT solver, the dominant engine in contemporary scheduling and routing [2]. We formalize the Booleanization scheme $\mathcal{B}(x \le v)$, develop explanation generation for global constraints—Régin's bipartite-matching alldifferent propagator [3] and the explaining cumulative propagator of Schütt et al. [4]—and analyze how learned clauses, restarts, and large-neighborhood search produce the performance that has repeatedly won the MiniZinc Challenge and closed long-open job-shop instances [5]. We characterize CP-SAT's extension toward mixed-integer programming via an integrated simplex, discuss the theoretical limits of LCG, and identify open problems in explanation quality and learned search control.

## 1. Introduction

Constraint programming and SAT solving developed for decades as parallel traditions with complementary blind spots. CP descends from logic programming: variables with finite domains, constraints from simple arithmetic to global constraints such as `alldifferent` and `cumulative`, and search that interleaves *propagation* (deductive domain inference) with *branching*. SAT restricts itself to a single constraint class—the disjunctive clause—yet compensates through conflict-driven clause learning, two-watched-literal propagation, adaptive restarts, and phase saving, routinely handling industrial instances with tens of millions of clauses.

Classical CP solvers recorded *nogoods* [6] but never achieved the clause-database maturity of SAT: first-UIP learning, non-chronological backtracking, and activity-based forgetting. SAT encodings of global constraints, meanwhile, were weak (direct encodings lose propagation strength) or enormous (support and order encodings blow up on wide domains). The motivating question of this thesis is therefore: *can a solver propagate at the strength of CP while learning at the speed of SAT?*

The answer is **lazy clause generation**. The insight of Ohrimenko, Stuckey, and Codish [1] is deceptively simple: rather than eagerly translating a finite-domain model into a propositional formula, generate Boolean variables and clauses *lazily*, exactly when a propagator produces an inference requiring clausal explanation. The solver maintains a Booleanization of each integer variable as bound literals $\llbracket x \le v \rrbracket$; each propagator is instrumented to explain every domain reduction as a clause; and the resulting clauses feed a CDCL engine that treats them like learned conflict clauses—driving unit propagation, participating in conflict analysis, and being periodically forgotten by activity-based database reduction.

This hybrid is no academic curiosity. Its most successful realization, Google OR-Tools' **CP-SAT** solver [2], is built on the LCG lineage (via the Chuffed solver of Chu et al.), has dominated the MiniZinc Challenge for years, delivered breakthroughs on job-shop scheduling [5], and is the default engine for industrial vehicle routing, timetabling, and staff scheduling. The recent CP-SAT-LP variant [2] adds a simplex-based linear relaxation, blurring the boundary between CP and mixed-integer programming itself.

### 1.1 Contributions

1. A unified formal presentation of LCG: Booleanization, propagator explanation obligations, the LCG propagation loop, and its reduction to CDCL on a lazily constructed formula.
2. A deep analysis of explaining global propagators, with alldifferent (Régin's algorithm [3]) and cumulative (timetable, edge-finding, energetic reasoning [4]) as case studies, including the tension between explanation *minimality* and *computational cost*.
3. An architectural account of CP-SAT: half-reified linear constraints, presolve, the SAT backend, large-neighborhood search, portfolio workers, and MIP integration [2].
4. An empirical survey: MiniZinc Challenge results, the Google-versus-IBM job-shop challenge [5], and scaling on resource-constrained project scheduling.
5. A critical discussion of limitations and open problems.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Constraint Programming: Propagation and Global Constraints

A finite-domain CSP is a tuple $(X, D, C)$: variables $X = \{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$, finite domains $D(x_i) \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$, and constraints $C$. The classical CP loop, *propagate-and-search*, alternates **propagation**—applying idempotent, contracting, monotone propagators until a fixed point—with **branching**. Propagator strength is measured by consistency level: *bounds consistency* (only extrema guaranteed a support), *range consistency*, and *domain (generalized arc) consistency* (GAC), the strongest.

CP's distinguishing feature is the **global constraint**: a constraint with dedicated combinatorial algorithms.

| Constraint | Semantics | Classical propagator | Complexity |
|---|---|---|---|
| `alldifferent(X)` | $x_i \neq x_j$, $i \neq j$ | Régin [3]: bipartite matching | $O(n^{2.5})$ for GAC |
| `alldifferent` (bounds) | same | Puget [9]: sorting-based | $O(n \log n)$ for BC |
| `cumulative(S, D, R, C)` | resource capacity over time | Timetable / edge-finding [4] | $O(n^2)$–$O(n^3)$ |
| `circuit(X)` | Hamiltonian cycle | Subtour elimination | $O(n^2)$ |

Global constraints make scheduling models an order of magnitude smaller and stronger than linear encodings—but classical CP search was comparatively naive: chronological backtracking with nogood recording [6] that never matched SAT's learning engineering.

### 2.2 SAT Solving: CDCL

Modern SAT is dominated by **conflict-driven clause learning**. A CDCL solver maintains a partial assignment under unit propagation (two-watched-literal scheme), and on conflict performs *conflict analysis*: resolving the conflicting clause against propagation reasons along the implication graph until the first unique implication point (1-UIP). The learned clause is added to the database; the solver backjumps non-chronologically. LCG inherits three SAT advances wholesale:

1. **Activity-based heuristics** (VSIDS): branching by exponentially decayed conflict participation.
2. **Restart policies**: Luby sequences and glucose-style dynamic restarts keyed on LBD (literal block distance) [10].
3. **Clause database management**: periodic deletion of long, inactive, high-LBD clauses.

Proof complexity explains CDCL's power: with restarts it polynomially simulates general resolution, refuting pigeonhole-style instances exponentially faster than tree-like search.

### 2.3 Mixed-Integer Programming and the CP–MIP Interface

MIP solvers (CPLEX, Gurobi, SCIP) attack optimization via LP relaxations, cutting planes, presolve, and branch-and-bound. Their strength is the *dual bound*; their weakness is combinatorial structure that linearizes poorly—disjunctions, alldifferent, and sequencing produce weak relaxations and enormous trees. CP-SAT-LP [2] is the most aggressive fusion to date: a simplex solving a dynamic linear relaxation *inside* the LCG loop, generating cuts and reduced-cost fixings communicated to the CP layer as learned clauses and bound tightenings.

### 2.4 From Nogoods to LCG

Recording reasons for failure in CP predates LCG: Dechter's backjumping, Katsirelos and Bacchus's generalized *explanations* of domain reductions [6], and Lecoutre et al.'s SAT-like nogood processing. These approaches either recorded explanations over original variables (losing Boolean structure) or required full upfront Booleanization (losing CP's compactness). Ohrimenko, Stuckey, and Codish [1] resolved the tension—Booleanize on demand, explain every propagation step, let a SAT solver do the rest—and Chu et al.'s Chuffed provided the first high-performance implementation, catalyzing the line of work that produced CP-SAT.

## 3. Methodology / Formal Framework

### 3.1 Booleanization of Finite-Domain Variables

Let $x$ have initial domain $D_{\text{init}}(x) = [l, u]$. LCG introduces, *lazily*, Boolean variables for:

- **Bound literals**: $\llbracket x \le v \rrbracket$ for $l \le v < u$, true iff $x \le v$;
- **Equality literals**: $\llbracket x = v \rrbracket$ for $v \in [l, u]$, true iff $x = v$.

> **Proposition:** The bound literals with channeling clauses $\neg\llbracket x \le v \rrbracket \lor \llbracket x \le v+1 \rrbracket$ (the order encoding) exactly capture the domain: $x \in D(x)$ iff the assignment satisfies $\llbracket x \le v \rrbracket \leftrightarrow (x \le v)$ for all $v$.

Crucially, $\llbracket x \le v \rrbracket$ is created *only when it first appears in an explanation*. For domains of size $10^6$, the solver may materialize only a few hundred Booleans per variable—this is the "lazy" in lazy clause generation, and it is what scales where eager SAT encodings collapse. The domain is recovered from the trail: bounds from the extreme true/false bound literals, holes via equality literals. CP-SAT implements this as an integer trail with bound propagation events, mirroring a SAT solver's assignment trail.

### 3.2 The Explanation Obligation

Every propagator must satisfy the following contract: when it infers a bound $\llbracket x \le v \rrbracket$ (or removes a value, or detects failure), it produces an **explanation**—a clause

$$C = \neg l_1 \lor \neg l_2 \lor \cdots \lor \neg l_k \lor l$$

where $l_1, \ldots, l_k$ are literals true on the current trail and $l$ is the new inference, such that $C$ is *entailed* by the constraint.

> **Theorem:** If every propagator produces only entailed explanations, every clause added to the SAT database is a logical consequence of the model, and the LCG solver is sound.

*Proof sketch.* SAT unit propagation and conflict analysis are sound for the clause set; all generated clauses are entailed by the model, so every derived conflict reflects genuine inconsistency. Completeness follows because branching enumerates assignments to the Booleanization, with order-encoding channeling guaranteeing correspondence to integer assignments. ∎

Explanation *quality* is the central engineering variable. A correct explanation is easy; a strong (minimal, general) one is hard. For $x + y \le z$ inferring $\llbracket z \le 10 \rrbracket$ from $\llbracket x \le 5 \rrbracket$, $\llbracket y \le 5 \rrbracket$, the clause $\neg\llbracket x \le 5 \rrbracket \lor \neg\llbracket y \le 5 \rrbracket \lor \llbracket z \le 10 \rrbracket$ is natural and minimal. But a `cumulative` propagator's edge-finding argument over dozens of tasks admits many valid antecedent sets; distilling a *small* sufficient one is itself a research problem [4], and weaker (larger-antecedent) explanations produce learned clauses that fire less often.

### 3.3 The LCG Propagation Loop and CDCL Integration

The solver is a single CDCL engine over the lazily growing clause set:

```
loop:
    propagate:                          # unit propagation on clause DB
        while unit clause (l) exists: assign l
    cp_propagate:                       # wake propagators on domain events
        for each propagator p with pending events:
            for each inference (antecedents -> l):
                C = explain(p, antecedents, l)   # lazily create Booleans
                add clause C; enqueue l for unit propagation
    if conflict:
        analyze -> learned clause (1-UIP); backjump; possibly restart
    else if all assigned: return SAT
    else: branch (VSIDS over Boolean literals)
```

CP propagation is thus *theory propagation* in a DPLL(T)-like architecture, where each propagator is a theory solver obligated to produce explanations. Learned clauses freely mix literals from different constraints, achieving *cross-constraint learning* that classical per-constraint nogoods [6] could never exploit.

### 3.4 Restarts, Clause Forgetting, and Branching

LCG inherits SAT restart policies directly: Luby sequences and LBD-based dynamic restarts [10]. When restarts preserve the clause database, all cross-constraint knowledge survives. Clause forgetting by activity and LBD automatically bounds the memory footprint of lazy generation—an elegant solution to what would otherwise be unbounded growth, since explanations are themselves database clauses.

Branching needs care: pure VSIDS over $\llbracket x \le v \rrbracket$ literals can fragment search via equality literals. CP-SAT therefore uses a **portfolio**: some workers run VSIDS on the Booleanization, others use CP-style first-fail with domain splitting, and LNS workers perform large-neighborhood search around incumbents (Section 4.3).

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 AllDifferent Propagation via Régin's Bipartite Matching

The `alldifferent(x_1, \ldots, x_n)` constraint is CP's most studied global constraint, and its LCG treatment shows both the power and subtlety of explaining propagators.

**Régin's algorithm.** Build the *value graph*: bipartite, variables $X$ left, values $D = \bigcup_i D(x_i)$ right, edge $(x_i, v)$ iff $v \in D(x_i)$. Then:

> **Proposition (Régin [3]):** The constraint is domain consistent iff every edge of the value graph belongs to *some* maximum matching; other edges' values can be removed.

The algorithm: (i) compute a maximum matching $M$ (Hopcroft–Karp); (ii) orient matched edges value→variable and unmatched edges variable→value; (iii) compute strongly connected components (Tarjan); edges crossing components belong to no maximum matching and are pruned. Complexity $O(n^{2.5})$ for $O(n)$-sized domains—vastly better than naive tuple enumeration.

**Explaining the propagator.** Removing $v$ from $D(x_i)$ must be justified by a Hall violator: a variable set $K \ni x_i$ whose combined domains are tight. The practical explanation uses the Dulmage–Mendelsohn reachable set in the alternating digraph:

$$\bigwedge_{x_j \in K} \llbracket x_j \in D(x_j) \rrbracket \;\to\; \neg\llbracket x_i = v \rrbracket$$

encoded over bound/equality literals. The snag: the *minimal* Hall set is expensive to compute, so implementations use the SCC-reachable set—correct but sometimes larger than necessary, yielding weaker learned clauses. Explanation minimality directly affects performance, a recurring LCG theme.

**Bounds-consistency variant.** For large domains, Puget's $O(n \log n)$ sorting-based algorithm [9] applies a Hall-interval argument with simple interval antecedents $\llbracket x_j \ge l \rrbracket \land \llbracket x_j \le u \rrbracket$. CP-SAT uses bounds-consistent alldifferent pervasively, reserving domain consistency for small domains.

```minizinc
% MiniZinc: alldifferent with side constraints, typical of scheduling models
include "alldifferent.mzn";
int: n = 8;
array[1..n] of var 1..n: start;
constraint alldifferent(start);                    % Régin-style propagation
constraint forall(i in 1..n)(start[i] <= 10 - i);
solve minimize max(start);
```

### 4.2 The Cumulative Constraint: Timetable, Edge-Finding, Explanations

`cumulative`—tasks with starts $s_i$, durations $d_i$, demands $r_i$ on a resource of capacity $C$, requiring $\sum_{i : s_i \le t < s_i + d_i} r_i \le C$ at all $t$—is CP's most industrially important global constraint. Its propagators form a strength/cost hierarchy:

| Algorithm | Idea | Complexity | Explanation difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Timetable | mandatory parts, resource profile | $O(n \log n)$ sweep | easy: interval antecedents |
| Not-first/not-last | pairwise ordering | $O(n^2)$ | moderate |
| Edge-finding | "$i$ precedes $\Omega$" energy test | $O(n^2)$ | hard: subset arguments |
| Energetic reasoning | interval energy bounds | $O(n^3)$+ | very hard |

**Timetable reasoning** computes each task's mandatory part $[\max s_i, \min s_i + d_i)$ and accumulates a resource profile; overload at $t$ forces tasks away, explained by interval bound literals. **Edge-finding** [4] asks whether task $i$ must precede a set $\Omega$, via energy computations over release dates, deadlines, and processing times. Schütt, Feydy, Stuckey, and Wallace [4] showed how to *explain* these inferences: the explanation cites exactly the bound literals determining the energy calculation, producing reusable clauses.

> **Proposition:** An edge-finding inference "$i$ precedes $\Omega$" is explained by the clause over bound literals fixing $\min s_j$, $\max(s_j + d_j)$, $d_j$ for $j \in \Omega \cup \{i\}$; the energy argument depends only on these bounds, so the clause is entailed.

The catch, again, is minimality: the full $\Omega$ may hold dozens of tasks while a small core suffices, but minimal cores are NP-hard in general—implementations use greedy extraction, trading explanation strength for propagator speed. On RCPSP/max, explaining (vs. non-explaining) cumulative propagators solve an order of magnitude more instances [4].

### 4.3 The CP-SAT Architecture: Half-Reification, Presolve, Portfolios

Google OR-Tools' CP-SAT [2] is the definitive LCG solver; its architecture shows how far the paradigm goes with disciplined engineering.

**Half-reified linear constraints.** Everything compiles to $b \Rightarrow \sum_i a_i x_i \le \text{rhs}$ with Boolean enforcement literal $b$. Global constraints decompose into such constraints plus auxiliary Booleans, or use dedicated propagators still speaking the explanation protocol. One representation serves the SAT backend and presolve uniformly.

**Presolve.** Bound strengthening, probing, coefficient tightening, dominated-variable elimination, and clique detection run before search—routinely eliminating most variables in vehicle-routing formulations [7].

**SAT backend and simplex.** The core is CDCL over the Booleanization: watched propagation, 1-UIP learning, LBD restarts, phase saving. CP-SAT-LP [2] adds a simplex solving a *dynamic* linear relaxation: enforcement literals activate/deactivate rows as search descends, and the simplex generates cuts (Gomory, MIR) plus reduced-cost fixings communicated back to the CP layer as clauses and bound tightenings.

**Portfolio of workers.** Parallel workers run diverse strategies—VSIDS-driven CDCL, first-fail with domain splitting, LNS destroy-and-repair around incumbents, feasibility-pump styles—sharing learned clauses and bounds through shared memory. On any instance class, at least one strategy tends to excel; this robustness is a major source of CP-SAT's dominance.

```python
# OR-Tools CP-SAT: job-shop with disjunctive (no-overlap) machine constraints
from ortools.sat.python import cp_model

def solve_jobshop(jobs):
    model = cp_model.CpModel()
    horizon = sum(sum(d for _, d in job) for job in jobs)
    tasks = {}
    for j, job in enumerate(jobs):
        for t, (machine, dur) in enumerate(job):
            s = model.NewIntVar(0, horizon, f"s_{j}_{t}")
            e = model.NewIntVar(0, horizon, f"e_{j}_{t}")
            iv = model.NewIntervalVar(s, dur, e, f"iv_{j}_{t}")
            tasks[(j, t)] = (s, e, iv, machine)
    for m in {m for job in jobs for m, _ in job}:
        model.AddNoOverlap([tasks[(j, t)][2] for j, job in enumerate(jobs)
                            for t, (mm, _) in enumerate(job) if mm == m])
    for j, job in enumerate(jobs):                       # precedences
        for t in range(len(job) - 1):
            model.Add(tasks[(j, t + 1)][0] >= tasks[(j, t)][1])
    model.Minimize(max(tasks[(j, len(job) - 1)][1]
                       for j, job in enumerate(jobs)))
    solver = cp_model.CpSolver()
    solver.parameters.num_search_workers = 8
    solver.Solve(model)
    return solver.ObjectiveValue()
```

### 4.4 Search Control: Restarts, LNS, and Learning

A subtle LCG advantage: restarts discard propagator state but *keep explanations as clauses*, so expensive combinatorial reasoning (an $O(n^2)$ edge-finding inference) is amortized across the whole search as reusable clauses.

> **Proposition:** With clause persistence across restarts, learned clauses after $k$ restarts subsume the inferential power of all propagator inferences performed during those restarts. Restart-heavy strategies cannot lose propagation strength—only trail-specific assignments.

LNS adds a second dynamic: workers fix most variables to incumbent values and re-solve the residual subproblem with full learning enabled, so each LNS iteration generates clauses strengthening later iterations—*learning-guided* neighborhood search with no classical-CP analogue.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The MiniZinc Challenge

The MiniZinc Challenge [8] scores solvers on solution quality and time across 100 heterogeneous instances via Borda count. LCG-based solvers—first Chuffed, then CP-SAT—have taken gold in the overwhelming majority of editions since 2012. A specialist may win a single problem class, but across scheduling, packing, routing, and configuration, the learning solver's robustness dominates—and Borda scoring rewards exactly the cross-constraint learning robustness that LCG provides.

### 5.2 Job-Shop Scheduling: Google vs. IBM

The job-shop problem ($n$ jobs, $m$ machines, fixed machine sequences per job, minimize makespan) is scheduling's great benchmarking battleground. Da Col and Teppan compared OR-Tools (CP-SAT) against IBM CP Optimizer on classical benchmarks and large industrial-scale instances with known optima [5]:

| Benchmark family | CP-SAT | CP Optimizer |
|---|---|---|
| FT10 (10×10, opt. 930) | optimal, seconds | optimal, seconds |
| LA21–LA40 (15×10 – 15×15) | optimal on most | competitive, slower proofs |
| Industrial 100×20 | within 1–2% of optimum | similar quality, longer tail |

The deeper story is *optimality proofs*: CP-SAT's clause learning closes the gap decisively, since learned disjunctive-conflict clauses ("these operations cannot all precede that one") generalize across the tree in ways failure-directed search does not replicate. With the CP-SAT-LP simplex integration [2], several previously open JSP instances have since been closed outright.

### 5.3 RCPSP/max and Cumulative Explanations at Scale

On resource-constrained project scheduling with maximum time lags, Schütt et al. [4] showed that *explaining* cumulative propagators (timetable + edge-finding + energetic reasoning) solve an order of magnitude more instances than non-explaining ones—the clause-reuse mechanism of Section 4.4 in action.

### 5.4 Proof Sketch: LCG Simulates Resolution on the Booleanization

> **Theorem:** Lazy clause generation with 1-UIP learning and restarts polynomially simulates CDCL on the full eager Booleanization $\mathcal{B}(\Pi)$.

*Proof sketch.* Replay any CDCL refutation of $\mathcal{B}(\Pi)$: each eager unit-propagation step is either already a database clause or a propagator inference, whose explanation *is* the clause the eager solver used (standard global propagators achieve at least the consistency of eager unit propagation). Conflict analysis is identical under 1-UIP over the same implication-graph structure. Laziness only delays clause creation—every needed Boolean is created on first use—so the refutation replays with at most polynomial overhead. ∎

The converse fails: a single edge-finding step can require exponentially long resolution proofs in the eager encoding—the proof-complexity analogue of propagation strength, explaining why LCG beats eager SAT encodings on scheduling even with identical learning machinery.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**Explanation quality vs. propagator cost.** Computing *minimal* explanations (e.g., for Régin or edge-finding inferences) is often as hard as propagation itself; minimal unsatisfiable cores are NP-hard in general. Solvers use greedy approximations, leaving learning strength on the table. A theory of *approximately minimal* explanations with quality guarantees remains open.

**LCG-unfriendly constraints.** Some constraints admit no compact clausal explanation for their natural inferences—`bin_packing` dual-feasibility arguments may need exponentially many clauses—causing thrashing. Classifying which global constraints are "LCG-friendly" is an important open problem.

**The explanation flood.** Millions of propagation events can exhaust memory despite laziness. CP-SAT's aggressive clause forgetting bounds this, but forgetting means re-derivation. Adaptive policies predicting explanation *reuse value* (an LBD analogue for explanations) are a promising direction.

**Learned search control.** CP-SAT's portfolio uses fixed strategy assignments. Online bandit allocation of workers and learned branching via graph neural networks are nascent in LCG, because the lazily growing Booleanization makes feature extraction a moving target.

**Industrial-scale optimality.** Proving optimality on vehicle routing with thousands of visits, time windows, and driver breaks remains out of reach; solvers return strong solutions with weak dual bounds. The CP-SAT-LP simplex integration [2] is the most promising attack, but scaling the LP on millions of half-reified rows is itself a frontier.

**Certification.** SAT emits checkable DRAT proofs; LCG's clause database *is* a resolution proof, but propagator explanations are trusted, not verified. A fully certifying LCG solver—each explanation carrying a machine-checkable derivation—would be a landmark for trustworthy optimization.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Lazy clause generation is among the most successful technology transfers in combinatorial optimization: it took SAT's learning machinery—conflict analysis, 1-UIP, restarts, clause forgetting, watched literals—and married it to CP's modeling and propagation strength, with lazy Booleanization as the bridge keeping the union scalable. From Ohrimenko, Stuckey, and Codish's framework [1], through Régin's matching-theoretic propagation [3] and Schütt et al.'s explaining cumulative propagators [4], to the CP-SAT/CP-SAT-LP solvers dominating competitions and industrial practice [2][5], the trajectory is clear: the future of combinatorial solving is hybrid, and learning is the lingua franca. The open problems of Section 6—explanation minimality, LCG-friendliness, learned portfolios, certification—define the next decade's agenda. But the central lesson is secure: *a solver that can explain its inferences can learn from its mistakes*, and in combinatorial optimization, as in science, that is the beginning of wisdom.
