# Conflict-Free Replicated Data Types: Semilattice Convergence, Delta-State Propagation, and the Limits of Coordination-Free Consistency

## Abstract

Conflict-free replicated data types (CRDTs) provide a principled foundation for distributed systems that remain available under network partitions without sacrificing convergence. By constraining replica state to a join-semilattice with inflationary updates, or equivalently by requiring concurrent operations to commute, CRDTs guarantee *strong eventual consistency*: any two replicas that have observed the same set of updates converge to identical states without coordination. This article develops CRDT theory from first principles, contrasting state-based (convergent) and operation-based (commutative) designs, and presents the delta-state refinement that recovers state-based convergence guarantees at communication costs approaching operation-based designs. We analyze canonical constructions — grow-only and two-phase sets, last-writer-wins element sets, PN-counters, observed-remove maps, and replicated growable arrays for collaborative text — and examine the metadata overhead that remains their central practical cost. We survey verification efforts in proof assistants, the impossibility of Byzantine-tolerant strong convergence without added trust assumptions, and results delineating which data types admit coordination-free implementations. Empirical measurements from production deployments quantify the bandwidth, latency, and metadata trade-offs across the design space.

## 1. Introduction

The CAP theorem tells us that a distributed system cannot simultaneously provide consistency, availability, and partition tolerance [1]. Traditional strongly consistent systems — Paxos, Raft, two-phase commit — choose consistency and sacrifice availability whenever a partition separates replicas from a quorum. But an enormous class of real applications does not need linearizability. A collaborative document editor, a shopping cart, a social-media counter, or a geo-replicated key-value cache can tolerate temporary divergence, provided that divergence is *bounded and self-healing*: once the partition heals, all replicas converge to the same state, deterministically, without manual conflict resolution.

Conflict-free replicated data types, introduced by Shapiro, Preguiça, Baquero, and Zawirski [2, 3], turn this intuition into a mathematical discipline. A CRDT is a data type whose operations are designed so that concurrent updates never conflict: either the state space forms a join-semilattice and merge computes least upper bounds, or concurrent operations commute. In either case the order in which a replica receives updates does not affect the final state. The payoff is profound: replicas can accept writes during partitions, propagate updates over unreliable, unordered, duplicate-prone networks, and still converge. No leader election, no quorum, no consensus.

This article proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the consistency models and the lattice theory that underpins CRDT design. Section 3 presents the two classical families — state-based and operation-based CRDTs — with their correctness conditions and canonical constructions. Section 4 develops delta-state CRDTs, which decouple the *unit of convergence* from the *unit of dissemination*. Section 5 analyzes metadata overhead and compaction techniques. Section 6 surveys mechanized verification, Section 7 confronts the Byzantine setting and its fundamental limits, and Section 8 reports empirical results from real deployments.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Consistency models

The relevant correctness criterion is **strong eventual consistency (SEC)** [2]: (i) *eventual delivery* — every update delivered at some correct replica is eventually delivered at all correct replicas; (ii) *termination* — method executions terminate; (iii) *strong convergence* — correct replicas that have delivered the same updates have equivalent state. SEC is strictly stronger than plain eventual consistency, which permits replicas that have seen the same updates to disagree forever (or to require external reconciliation). The entire CRDT research program is the systematic construction of data types satisfying SEC by construction.

> **Theorem (Shapiro et al.):** If the set of states of an object ordered by ⪯ forms a join-semilattice, updates are inflationary, and merge computes the least upper bound, then the object satisfies strong eventual consistency [3].

The proof is short and instructive. Join is associative, commutative, and idempotent by the semilattice laws; hence the merge of any multiset of updates is independent of order and duplication. Any two replicas that have received the same updates compute the same least upper bound. The theorem reduces a global distributed-systems property to a local algebraic check — the single most important idea in the field.

### 2.2 Lattice essentials

A *partial order* (S, ⪯) is a *join-semilattice* when every pair of elements has a least upper bound, denoted s₁ ⊔ s₂. The canonical example is the powerset lattice (P(X), ⊆) with join = union. A grow-only set (G-Set) exploits exactly this: states are sets, merge is union, and adding an element is inflationary (S ⊆ S ∪ {e}). Convergence falls out of the algebra; no vector clocks, no tombstone negotiation.

| Property | State-based (CvRDT) | Operation-based (CmRDT) |
|---|---|---|
| Convergence mechanism | Join-semilattice merge | Commuting concurrent operations |
| Dissemination unit | Full state | Single operation |
| Delivery requirement | Any (duplicates OK) | Causal order, exactly-once |
| Bandwidth | High (full state) | Low (operation only) |
| Implementation burden | Low | High (middleware guarantees) |

## 3. Methodology: The Two Classical Families

### 3.1 State-based CRDTs

In the state-based model, each replica holds a state s, updates mutate local state monotonically (s ⪯ s′), and replicas periodically exchange states, merging with ⊔. The canonical constructions [3]:

- **G-Counter:** vector of per-replica counts; merge takes the pointwise maximum; value is the sum. Inflation holds because a replica only increments its own entry.
- **PN-Counter:** pair of G-Counters (increments, decrements); value is the difference.
- **G-Set / 2P-Set:** grow-only set as above; the two-phase set adds a second G-Set of tombstones, so remove(e) inserts e into the removed-set and lookup tests membership in added ∖ removed. The price: elements can never be re-added.
- **LWW-Element-Set:** each element carries a timestamp; add and remove are tagged; the operation with the greatest timestamp wins. Requires loosely synchronized clocks and breaks down under clock skew — a practical weakness analyzed in Section 6.
- **OR-Set (observed-remove set):** each add generates a unique tag; remove deletes only the tags *observed* at the replica performing the remove. Concurrent adds survive, sequential add-then-remove behaves intuitively. This is the workhorse set of the CRDT literature.
- **OR-Map / RGA:** maps and sequences built by composing the above, enabling JSON-like documents [4].

### 3.2 Operation-based CRDTs

In the operation-based model, updates are split into a *generator* (side-effect free, executed at the source, e.g. picking a unique timestamp) and an *effector* applied at every replica. Convergence requires that concurrent effectors commute and that delivery is *causal* — an operation is applied only after the operations that causally precede it [3]. The dissemination unit is a single operation, so bandwidth is minimal, but the middleware must guarantee causal broadcast with at-most-once... precisely, exactly-once, non-duplicating delivery. In practice this means sequence numbers, acknowledgments, and retransmission — reintroducing much of the complexity CRDTs were meant to escape.

### 3.3 Pure operation-based CRDTs

Baquero and colleagues observed that the generator/effector split can be simplified: in *pure* operation-based CRDTs [5], the operation itself carries all needed metadata, and replicas apply a generic causal-delivery layer (e.g. a tagged reliable causal broadcast). This unifies the programming model: the data type designer writes only the effector logic, while a reusable middleware provides causality. The cost is that the middleware must still track a version vector per sender.

```haskell
-- OR-Set effector (pure op-based sketch): concurrent adds commute,
-- remove only cancels observed tags
effectAdd   :: Tag -> Elem -> ORSet -> ORSet
effectAdd t e s = Map.insert e (Set.insert t (lookup e s)) s

effectRemove :: Set Tag -> Elem -> ORSet -> ORSet
effectRemove seen e s = Map.adjust (Set.difference seen) e s
```

Because `effectAdd` and `effectRemove` for *different* tags commute, and removes only reference tags the removing replica has observed, any two replicas receiving the same operation set converge — the operation-based analogue of the semilattice argument.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Delta-state CRDTs: small messages, lattice guarantees

State-based CRDTs pay a steep bandwidth price: every anti-entropy exchange ships the full state. Operation-based CRDTs pay a middleware price: causal, exactly-once delivery. Delta-state CRDTs [6] split the difference. A *delta* is the minimal state fragment capturing the effect of an update — a join-irreducible (or at least small) element δ such that the new state is s ⊔ δ. Deltas are themselves lattice elements, so they can be *merged into delta-groups* before transmission, buffered, and re-sent on loss without violating correctness: because join is idempotent, duplicate or reordered delta delivery is harmless.

Formally, a delta-state CRDT decomposes each mutator m into a delta-mutator m^δ with m^δ(s) = δ and m(s) = s ⊔ δ, plus a merge that joins states and delta-groups identically. The anti-entropy algorithm accumulates deltas per neighbor and ships them when convenient — over a backpressure-friendly, lossy, duplicating channel. The result preserves the *delivery contract of state-based replication* (anything goes) while approaching the *bandwidth of operation-based replication* (only what changed). Measurements in [6] show order-of-magnitude bandwidth reductions for counters and sets under realistic workloads.

### 4.2 Big delta-state: from deltas to operation logs

Taken to its limit, delta-state decomposition suggests shipping *causal histories* rather than states. Almeida et al. [7] develop this into a framework where delta-groups with causal context subsume both classical families: state-based CRDTs are deltas shipped whole; operation-based CRDTs are deltas shipped per-operation. This unification matters practically: a single anti-entropy protocol (join deltas, track a per-neighbor "what they have" summary) serves all data types, and system builders no longer choose between the two families per data type.

### 4.3 CRDTs for text: RGA, Logoot, and the Yjs/Automerge generation

Collaborative text editing is the flagship application. The replicated growable array (RGA) assigns each inserted character a unique, totally ordered identifier (e.g. ⟨timestamp, replica-id⟩) and stores characters in a linked structure keyed by identifier; concurrent insertions at the same position are ordered by identifier, deterministically and commutatively [4]. Logoot and its successors use dense position identifiers, trading identifier size against rebalancing complexity.

The modern generation — Yjs [8] and Automerge — generalizes RGA to rich text and JSON. Kleppmann and Beresford's conflict-free replicated JSON datatype [4] shows how to compose CRDTs into nested documents with deterministic conflict handling for concurrent assignments (multi-value registers) and list edits (RGA). Yjs's update-based encoding ships *operations as compressed deltas* over a single binary format, achieving the remarkable property that the on-wire format is simultaneously an operation log, a state snapshot, and a sync protocol — a design now used in production by several collaborative-editing platforms.

```python
# RGA insertion: identifier order decides concurrent placement
def insert_after(state, prev_id, char, replica):
    new_id = (logical_clock(), replica)      # unique, totally ordered
    # concurrent inserts after prev_id sort by new_id -> deterministic
    state.sequence.insert_sorted(prev_id, (new_id, char))
    return delta(new_id, char, prev_id)
```

### 4.4 Byzantine tolerance and its limits

All of the above assumes crash faults and correct replicas. Under Byzantine faults, a malicious replica can equivocate — send different updates to different peers — breaking the "same updates ⇒ same state" premise of SEC. Kleppmann [9] proved a sharp negative result: *Byzantine eventual consistency* with the standard SEC guarantees is unachievable for arbitrary data types without additional assumptions, because equivocation lets a Byzantine replica induce permanent, undetectable divergence between correct replicas' causal histories.

The escape routes are well understood: (i) restrict the threat model (e.g. tolerate Byzantine faults only for data types whose merge is *equivocation-tolerant*); (ii) add cryptographic accountability — signed updates with hash-chained causal histories make equivocation *detectable*, converting Byzantine faults into crash faults once detected [9]; (iii) accept weaker convergence (fork-consistent or fork*-consistent variants). Production systems such as Matrix's room-state resolution and Secure Scuttlebutt adopt variants of (ii). The lesson is precise: coordination-free convergence and Byzantine tolerance compose only through accountability, not through algebra alone.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Convergence proofs.** The semilattice theorem (Section 2.1) gives convergence for all state-based and delta-state designs by construction. Operation-based designs rely on the commutation of concurrent effectors, proved per data type; Gomes et al. [10] mechanized these proofs in Isabelle/HOL, verifying SEC for counters, sets, and registers and exposing subtle bugs in published LWW-register specifications. Their framework — encoding the network as a locale with a happens-before relation — has become the standard verification methodology.

**Metadata overhead.** The fundamental cost of CRDTs is metadata: unique tags per add (OR-Set), per-replica entries (G-Counter), tombstones (2P-Set), or position identifiers (RGA). For a 1 MB document with 100k characters, RGA identifiers can exceed the payload. Mitigations include: delta-state compaction [6], identifier block allocation (Yjs allocates identifier ranges per client, amortizing cost), and garbage collection of causally stable metadata — safe once all replicas have observed an update, detectable via version-vector summaries.

**Production measurements.** Geo-replicated deployments (e.g. Riak's CRDT-backed data types, Redis Enterprise's Active-Active CRDTs) report single-digit-millisecond local write latency with convergence lag bounded by anti-entropy round-trip time. In peer-to-peer editing, Yjs syncs documents of 10⁵ operations in tens of milliseconds over WebRTC, with update payloads of a few bytes per keystroke after compression. The consistent finding: CRDTs trade a constant-factor metadata and bandwidth overhead for the *elimination of coordination latency* — the right trade whenever availability under partition matters more than minimal storage.

## 6. Limitations

First, **not every data type admits a CRDT**. Recent work characterizes exactly which sequential specifications have coordination-free implementations: roughly, those whose operations can be made to commute or whose conflicts admit a deterministic resolution policy. Invariants spanning multiple objects (e.g. "account balance ≥ 0" across replicas, or referential integrity) generally require coordination — the celebrated counterexample being that no CRDT implements a bank account with a no-overdraft invariant under partitions [2]. Bounded counters with per-replica escrow recover some of this at the cost of partition-time limits.

Second, **clocks and timestamps** are a persistent hazard. LWW designs assume synchronized clocks; in practice clock skew causes silent data loss (a "later" remove with a smaller timestamp resurrects deleted data). Logical clocks (Lamport, vector) avoid wall-clock dependence but complicate the API.

Third, **metadata growth** is unbounded in the worst case for OR-Sets and RGA-style sequences without garbage collection, and safe GC requires a global stability notion — itself a coordination problem in disguise. Practical systems bound this with epoch-based compaction and accept brief coordination during GC.

Fourth, the **Byzantine gap** (Section 4.4): strong convergence under arbitrary faults needs cryptographic accountability infrastructure, eroding the simplicity that makes CRDTs attractive.

---

## 7. Conclusion

CRDTs are the rare distributed-systems idea that is simultaneously a clean mathematical theory and a deployed engineering practice. The semilattice insight — that convergence can be reduced to algebra — yields data types that stay available through partitions, converge deterministically, and compose into rich documents. Delta-state propagation [6, 7] has largely dissolved the classical state-vs-operation dilemma, giving lattice-grade delivery guarantees at operation-grade bandwidth. Mechanized verification [10] has hardened the designs, and production systems from collaborative editors to geo-replicated databases validate the trade-offs empirically.

The frontier is now well-defined: characterizing the exact boundary of coordination-free implementability, taming metadata through principled compaction, and extending accountability-based techniques to close the Byzantine gap. For the large and growing class of applications that value availability over strong consistency, CRDTs remain the most principled tool available — convergence by construction, not by consensus.
