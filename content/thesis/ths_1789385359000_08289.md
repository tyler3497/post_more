# Noisy Quantum Metrology: No-Go Theorems, the Purification Bound, and Quantum-Error-Corrected Recovery of Heisenberg Scaling

## Abstract

Quantum metrology promises estimation precision improving as the inverse of the probe number — Heisenberg scaling — quadratically better than the standard quantum limit imposed by uncorrelated probes. That promise is formulated for ideal, noiseless dynamics; real sensors are open systems, and decoherence has long been suspected of eroding quantum advantage. This thesis develops the theory of noisy quantum metrology. We review the quantum Cramér–Rao bound under completely positive maps, present the Escher–de Matos Filho–Davidovich purification bound that captures the crossover from Heisenberg to standard scaling under photon loss and dephasing, and describe the channel-geometry program proving that uncorrelated noise generically reduces quantum advantage to a constant factor rather than a scaling improvement. We then turn to the escape routes: quantum error correction for metrology, the sharp Hamiltonian-not-in-Lindblad-span criterion that separates correctable from uncorrectable noise, and environment-monitoring strategies that restore Heisenberg scaling when the noise carries recoverable structure.

## 1 Intro

Precision measurement is the oldest application of quantum mechanics and remains its most demanding. The central quantitative question of quantum metrology is simple: given *N* probes, a parameter θ encoded by a physical process, and ν repetitions, how small can the mean-square error of an unbiased estimator θ̂ be? For noiseless unitary encoding with separable probes, the answer is the *standard quantum limit* (SQL), Δθ ∼ 1/√N; with entanglement across probes, the answer can improve to the *Heisenberg limit* (HL), Δθ ∼ 1/N [2][4].

The gap between these two scalings is enormous at large N, which explains decades of effort toward entanglement-enhanced sensors. Yet nearly all of the foundational theory — the quantum Fisher information, the symmetric logarithmic derivative, the optimality of NOON and GHZ states — assumes perfectly unitary dynamics. A sensor is a system coupled to an environment: photons are lost, spins dephase, atoms spontaneously emit. The question this thesis addresses is therefore the practical one: **what survives of Heisenberg scaling once noise is included?**

The answer, developed over roughly 2011–2018, is a mature and surprisingly sharp theory with three pillars. First, general upper bounds on the quantum Fisher information for noisy channels, derived by purifying the noise and minimizing over Kraus representations [1]. Second, no-go theorems showing that under uncorrelated noise the Heisenberg limit is *generically* unattainable, leaving at most a constant-factor improvement [2][5]. Third, a precise characterization of when quantum error correction restores the Heisenberg limit — and when it provably cannot [3][4].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Ideal estimation theory in one page

A parameter θ is encoded in a family of states ρ_θ. For a measurement described by a POVM {Π_x}, the classical Fisher information of p(x|θ) = Tr[ρ_θ Π_x] bounds any unbiased estimator through the Cramér–Rao inequality, Var(θ̂) ≥ 1/F. Maximizing over measurements yields the quantum Fisher information

$$F_Q(θ) = \mathrm{Tr}[ρ_θ L_θ^2],$$

where the symmetric logarithmic derivative L_θ is defined by ∂_θ ρ_θ = ½(L_θ ρ_θ + ρ_θ L_θ), and the chain

> **Theorem (Quantum Cramér–Rao bound):** For ν independent repetitions,
> $$\mathrm{Var}(\hat θ) \ge \frac{1}{ν\, F(θ;\{Π_x\})} \ge \frac{1}{ν\, F_Q(θ)}.$$

For unitary encoding ρ_θ = e^{−iθH} ρ e^{iθH} and a pure probe, F_Q = 4(ΔH)²: precision is the variance of the generator. For N probes and a collective generator H = Σ_j h_j with bounded single-probe spectrum, separable states give F_Q ≤ N·const (SQL), while the GHZ state (|0⟩^{⊗N} + |1⟩^{⊗N})/√2 saturates F_Q = N² (HL). Geometry clarifies the picture: F_Q is, up to normalization, the Bures metric — metrology is the art of making the probe move as fast as possible through state space per unit of θ [2].

### 2.2 The noisy setting

Noise is modeled as a θ-dependent quantum channel ℰ_θ — a completely positive, trace-preserving map — acting as ρ_θ = ℰ_θ(ρ). Every channel admits a Kraus representation ℰ_θ(ρ) = Σ_l K_l(θ) ρ K_l(θ)† and a Stinespring dilation: a unitary U_θ on system plus environment, with the environment traced out. The crucial observation is that the QFI can only *decrease* under the partial trace, so the QFI of any purification upper-bounds the QFI of the noisy state. Because the Kraus representation is not unique — related representations differ by a θ-dependent unitary on the environment index — one may *minimize* this upper bound over all representations. That minimization is the engine of the entire field [1].

### 2.3 Phase estimation versus frequency estimation

Two resource accountings dominate the literature and must not be confused. In **phase estimation** (interferometry), the resource is the total probe number or energy N, and one compares Δφ ∼ 1/√N against Δφ ∼ 1/N. In **frequency estimation** (atomic spectroscopy, Ramsey magnetometry), N probes evolve for an interrogation time t, the experiment repeats for a total time T, and the relevant scalings are Δω ∼ 1/√(NT) versus Δω ∼ 1/(N√T)... more precisely the Heisenberg scaling reads Δω ∼ 1/(N√(T t)) optimized over t. The distinction matters because dephasing acts during the interrogation time: a GHZ state accumulates phase N times faster but also decoheres N times faster, and Huelga *et al.* showed already in 1997 that for frequency estimation under Markovian dephasing, maximally entangled states perform no better in scaling than product states [5].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The purification bound

The Escher–de Matos Filho–Davidovich method [1] bounds the noisy QFI without ever diagonalizing ρ_θ. Purify ℰ_θ(ρ) to |Ψ(θ)⟩_{SE}; then F_Q[ℰ_θ(ρ)] ≤ F_Q[|Ψ(θ)⟩] = 4(⟨Ψ̇|Ψ̇⟩ − |⟨Ψ|Ψ̇⟩|²), and the right-hand side is minimized over the unitary freedom in the Kraus representation:

> **Theorem (Escher–de Matos Filho–Davidovich bound):** For any Kraus representation {K_l(θ)} of ℰ_θ,
> $$F_Q[ℰ_θ(ρ)] \le 4\min_{\{K_l\}}\left[ \left\langle \sum_l \dot K_l^\dagger \dot K_l \right\rangle - \left| \left\langle i\sum_l K_l^\dagger \dot K_l \right\rangle \right|^2 \right],$$
> where dots denote ∂_θ and expectation values are in the input probe state ρ.

The bound is powerful because it is *attainable* in the relevant cases: it reproduces the exact QFI for lossy interferometry and dephased spectroscopy, and — decisively — it already accounts for adaptive and feedback-assisted strategies, since adaptivity can be absorbed into an enlarged channel [1].

### 3.2 Channel geometry and semidefinite programming

Demkowicz-Dobrzański, Kołodyński, and Guţă [2], and Kołodyński and Demkowicz-Dobrzański [5], developed a complementary geometric toolkit. The asymptotic QFI rate of N parallel uses of a channel is bounded by solving a minimization over Kraus representations subject to constraints that encode "how much of the parameter derivative can be pushed into the noise." When the channel's derivative lies in the span of the noise operators — the generic situation for full-rank uncorrelated noise — the QFI grows at most linearly in N: the SQL is asymptotically unavoidable, and the optimization reduces to a semidefinite program yielding the best achievable *constant* [2][5].

### 3.3 Computational recipe

The following routine evaluates the Escher bound for lossy optical phase estimation, the canonical testbed, and locates the crossover photon number N* ∼ 1/(1−η) beyond which Heisenberg scaling collapses:

```python
import numpy as np

def escher_loss_bound(N, eta):
    """Asymptotic Escher upper bound on QFI for lossy phase estimation.
    N: mean photon number; eta: transmissivity (0 < eta < 1)."""
    F_bound = eta * N / (1.0 - eta)   # F_Q <= eta*N/(1-eta)
    N_star = 1.0 / (1.0 - eta)        # Heisenberg -> SQL crossover scale
    return F_bound, N_star

for eta in (0.99, 0.9, 0.5):
    Fb, Ns = escher_loss_bound(1000, eta)
    print(f"eta={eta}: F_Q <= {Fb:8.1f}, N* ~ {Ns:5.0f}, "
          f"dphi >= {1/np.sqrt(Fb):.2e}  (HL would give 1.00e-03)")
```

Output: at 1% loss (η = 0.99), F_Q ≤ 99 000 versus the Heisenberg value N² = 10⁶ — already an order of magnitude lost at N = 1000, and the crossover sits at N* ∼ 100 photons.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Photon loss: the Escher transition in interferometry

Lossy optical interferometry is modeled by fictitious beam splitters of transmissivity η in each arm, diverting photons into unmonitored environment modes. Applying the purification bound and minimizing over Kraus representations gives, for large N,

$$F_Q \le \frac{η N}{1 - η}, \qquad \Delta\varphi \ge \sqrt{\frac{1-η}{η N}}.$$

Three consequences follow [1]. First, **any** nonzero loss (η < 1) forces the asymptotic scaling back to 1/√N, *independently* of the input state — NOON states, squeezed states, adaptive measurements, and feedback all succumb. Second, the bound is tight: it is saturated by explicit (if idealized) strategies, so it is the true ultimate limit, not a loose estimate. Third, the transition is quantitative rather than merely asymptotic: for N ≪ 1/(1−η) the state can still approach Heisenberg scaling, while for N ≫ 1/(1−η) the bound bites. Experiments on lossy phase estimation operate precisely in the pre-crossover regime, which is why demonstrated quantum advantages remain modest in absolute photon number [2].

The physical moral is a fragility hierarchy: the states most valuable in the ideal theory are the most vulnerable to noise. A NOON state (|N,0⟩ + |0,N⟩)/√2 loses *all* of its phase information upon the loss of a single photon, whereas a coherent state degrades gracefully. Optimal probe design under noise is therefore a compromise, not a maximization of ideal QFI [1][5].

### 4.2 Dephasing geometry: parallel versus perpendicular

For frequency estimation with N qubits, signal Hamiltonian G = (ω/2)Σ_j σ_z^{(j)}, and local dephasing, the geometry of the noise relative to the signal decides everything. Under **parallel** dephasing (Lindblad operators ∝ σ_z), the noise commutes with the signal; the GHZ coherence decays as exp(−Nγt), and optimizing the interrogation time yields at best a constant-factor improvement over product states — the Huelga *et al.* conclusion, now understood as a special case of the general theory [2][5].

Under **perpendicular** dephasing (noise ∝ σ_x, signal ∝ σ_z), the unprotected scaling is still SQL asymptotically, but the noise lies in a *different operator direction* than the signal — and that geometric fact is precisely what quantum error correction can exploit, as §4.3 shows [3][4].

The channel-geometry program makes this rigorous: for dephasing, depolarizing, spontaneous emission, and photon loss, the maximal asymptotic quantum enhancement is a constant factor, computable by semidefinite programming, with explicit optimal states and measurements [2][5].

### 4.3 Quantum error correction and the HNLS criterion

Kessler, Lovchinsky, Sushkov, and Lukin [3] proposed using quantum error correction *during* sensing: encode the probe in a code that corrects the noise while leaving the signal Hamiltonian's action intact, then read out the accumulated phase. For an NV-center spin sensor, for example, the magnetic signal rotates the qubit about one axis while bit-flip noise acts about another; a fast correction cycle suppresses the noise without touching the signal, extending the effective coherence time and the sensitivity [3].

Zhou, Zhang, Preskill, and Jiang [4] proved the definitive characterization. Consider Markovian noise with Lindblad operators {L_k} and signal Hamiltonian G. Define the **Lindblad span**

$$\mathcal{S} = \mathrm{span}\{I, L_k, L_k^\dagger, L_k^\dagger L_j\}.$$

> **Theorem (HNLS — Hamiltonian Not in Lindblad Span):** Heisenberg scaling is achievable with quantum error correction *if and only if* G ∉ 𝒮. If G ∈ 𝒮, then even with arbitrary fast and perfect QEC, the asymptotic scaling is bounded by the SQL.

The condition is both necessary and sufficient, and it has a clean geometric reading: the signal must point *outside* the operator subspace generated by the noise. The table summarizes the canonical cases:

| Noise model | Signal G | G ∈ 𝒮? | Best asymptotic scaling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parallel dephasing (L ∝ σ_z) | σ_z | Yes | SQL; constant-factor gain only |
| Perpendicular dephasing (L ∝ σ_x) | σ_z | No | Heisenberg, with QEC |
| Amplitude damping (L ∝ σ_−) | σ_z | Yes | SQL |
| Photon loss, any η < 1 | phase φ | Yes (full-rank channel) | SQL; no escape [1] |

The criterion is checkable with elementary linear algebra. For single-qubit dephasing, the Lindblad span collapses to {I, σ_a} along the noise axis a:

```haskell
-- HNLS criterion for single-qubit dephasing noise.
data Pauli = I | X | Y | Z deriving (Eq, Show)

-- For a jump operator L = sqrt(gamma) * sigma_a, the Lindblad span
-- span{I, L, L†, L†L} reduces to {I, sigma_a} up to scalars.
lindbladSpan :: Pauli -> [Pauli]
lindbladSpan a = [I, a]

-- Heisenberg scaling is QEC-recoverable iff the signal G lies
-- outside the Lindblad span.
hnlsHolds :: Pauli -> Pauli -> Bool
hnlsHolds signal noiseAxis = signal `notElem` lindbladSpan noiseAxis

-- hnlsHolds Z Z  == False   (parallel dephasing: no recovery)
-- hnlsHolds Z X  == True    (perpendicular: QEC restores HL)
```

Ancilla-assisted refinements extend the picture: entangling probes with a noiseless ancilla that never interacts with the sample can improve precision under depolarizing and amplitude-damping noise even where probe-only entanglement fails — though notably *not* for dephasing or erasure — and the gains are quantified rather than merely existential [7].

### 4.4 Monitoring the environment and other escape routes

The no-go theorems assume the environment is traced out — its information discarded. If the environment is *monitored*, the conclusions change. Albarelli, Rossi, Tamascelli, and Genoni [6] showed that continuous measurement of the environment (e.g., photodetection of the emitted quanta) can restore Heisenberg scaling under noise models where the unmonitored channel is SQL-limited: the lost information is not truly lost if someone is watching the drain. The ultimate bound then involves the QFI of the joint system–environment trajectory ensemble, and the optimal measurement generally combines the probe readout with the continuous record [6].

Two further loopholes deserve mention. **Non-Markovian noise**, with finite bath correlation times, permits interrogation strategies that outrun decoherence at short times and can beat the Markovian bounds. **Correlated noise** across probes violates the "uncorrelated" premise of the no-go theorems entirely; the bounds of [2][5] explicitly exclude inter-probe correlations, and suitably correlated environments can even *assist* metrology. Each escape route, however, replaces one resource with another — measurement bandwidth, control speed, or bath engineering — and honest accounting must price those in [4][6].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The theoretical guarantees compress into three quantitative statements:

1. **Loss kills scaling.** For phase estimation with transmissivity η < 1, Δφ ≥ √((1−η)/(ηN)) asymptotically for *every* probe state, measurement, and adaptive strategy [1]. At 10% loss, Heisenberg-like scaling survives only below N* ∼ 10 photons — a regime where the absolute precision is unremarkable.
2. **Uncorrelated noise caps enhancement at a constant.** For dephasing, depolarizing, spontaneous emission, and loss, the ratio of optimal quantum to optimal classical mean-square error tends to a finite constant as N → ∞, computable by SDP; no entangled strategy changes the exponent [2][5].
3. **HNLS is sharp.** Under Markovian noise, QEC-assisted Heisenberg scaling is possible exactly when the signal Hamiltonian lies outside the Lindblad span; otherwise the SQL is a hard ceiling even with perfect, arbitrarily fast correction [4].

On the experimental side, the QEC-for-sensing program moved from proposal to demonstration: repetitive quantum error correction during phase acquisition was implemented with NV centers in diamond, extending the useful sensing time and improving magnetic sensitivity under engineered noise beyond what the unprotected sensor achieves [8]. The demonstration used the Kessler *et al.* architecture [3] — a sensor qubit protected against bit-flip-type noise while accumulating a phase signal about an orthogonal axis — and confirmed the theory's central prediction: when the noise geometry permits it, active correction converts a decoherence-limited sensor back toward its ideal scaling. The remaining gap between these proof-of-principle devices and the asymptotic HNLS theorem is one of control speed and gate fidelity, not of principle [4][8].

## 6 Limitations

Honesty requires stating what the theory does not cover. First, the no-go theorems assume **uncorrelated** noise across probes; spatially or temporally correlated environments fall outside them, and real solid-state sensors often live precisely in that regime. Second, the HNLS theorem assumes **Markovian** Lindblad dynamics and effectively instantaneous, perfect correction cycles; finite gate speeds and faulty ancillas reintroduce SQL prefactors that the asymptotic analysis hides [4]. Third, the purification bound is tight for the canonical models but need not be tight in general — a gap between bound and achievability remains for exotic channels. Fourth, the analysis prices only the probe resource; the ancillas, control pulses, and measurement bandwidth consumed by QEC or environment monitoring are real costs that can erase the nominal advantage in wall-clock sensitivity [3][6]. Finally, constant-factor improvements — all that survives under parallel dephasing or loss — should not be dismissed: a factor of two in precision is a factor of four in averaging time, which is often the difference between a feasible experiment and a fantasy [2].

## 7 Conclusion

Noisy quantum metrology has converged on a clear verdict. Entanglement-enhanced scaling is real but fragile: uncorrelated noise generically demotes the Heisenberg limit to the standard quantum limit, leaving at most a constant-factor advantage whose value can be computed exactly [1][2][5]. Yet the theory is constructive, not merely negative. The purification bound tells experimentalists *where* the crossover lies for their loss budget; the channel-geometry program tells them the best constant available; and the HNLS criterion tells them, given their noise operators and their signal, whether quantum error correction can restore Heisenberg scaling — a yes-or-no question answerable by linear algebra [3][4]. Where the answer is yes, as in perpendicular dephasing or monitored environments, the quantum advantage survives decoherence by design rather than by luck [6][8]. The field's deepest lesson is thus a shift of perspective: in realistic sensing, the scarce resource is not entanglement but *noise geometry*, and precision is won by shaping the sensor's code space around the directions the environment cannot touch.
