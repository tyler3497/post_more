# Event-Driven Intelligence: Neuromorphic Computing with Spiking Neural Networks — From Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Dynamics to Loihi 2, SpiNNaker2, and Surrogate-Gradient Learning

## Abstract

This dissertation develops a rigorous account of neuromorphic computing with spiking neural networks (SNNs), an event-driven paradigm that replaces clocked multiply-accumulate execution with sparse, asynchronous spike communication. We formalize the leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron, analyze the non-differentiability barrier that long blocked gradient-based training, and show how surrogate-gradient methods — SuperSpike, SLAYER, and the exact adjoint method EventProp — restore backpropagation-compatible learning in deep SNNs. We survey the dominant silicon substrates, Intel's Loihi 2 with the open-source Lava framework and the SpiNNaker2 many-core platform, and compile published benchmarks: spiking policies on SpiNNaker2 achieve 24–32× energy reduction versus a GTX 1650 at comparable latency [3], and an event-based language model consumes 0.065 J versus 1.19 J on an A100 at batch one [4]. We analyze spike-timing-dependent plasticity as a local on-chip learning primitive and close with a critical account of limitations — static-benchmark accuracy gaps, sim-to-hardware mismatch, and toolchain immaturity.

---

## 1 Introduction

Modern AI computation rests on the von Neumann architecture: memory and processing separated by a shared bus, executing synchronous, clock-driven operations. This design carries a punishing energetic tax. Fetching a 32-bit operand from off-chip DRAM costs roughly two orders of magnitude more energy than the floating-point operation it feeds, so large-scale inference is increasingly *data-movement bound* rather than compute bound. The mammalian brain, by contrast, runs on about twenty watts. Its efficiency rests on three principles: *event-driven communication*, where neurons emit spikes only when their state crosses a threshold; *colocation of memory and compute*, with synaptic weights stored at the integration site; and *asynchronous temporal dynamics*, where information lives in spike timing as well as count.

Neuromorphic computing transplants these principles into silicon. The field has matured from analog curiosities into programmable digital platforms: Intel's Loihi 2, with up to one million neurons across 128 neurocores and graded-spike messaging [1][2], and SpiNNaker2, a many-core system demonstrated on event-based recurrent networks and exact event-based backpropagation [4]. Yet hardware alone does not make a paradigm. For years the field was stalled by an analytic obstruction: spike generation is a discontinuous Heaviside function whose derivative is zero almost everywhere, rendering gradient descent formally vacuous.

That obstruction has now been *partially dissolved*. Surrogate-gradient methods [5][6], the temporal credit-assignment algorithm SLAYER [7], and the exact adjoint formulation EventProp [4][8] permit training deep spiking networks with backpropagation-like machinery. Combined with substrates whose dynamic power scales with spike activity rather than model size, this yields a credible path to order-of-magnitude energy savings for edge inference, robotics, and always-on sensing. Our central claim is quantitative: for workloads with *temporal sparsity* — event-camera streams, closed-loop control, streaming audio — event-driven SNNs already beat GPUs by one to two orders of magnitude in energy per decision, while the remaining gap on static dense benchmarks marks the honest boundary of the paradigm.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Neural coding

A spiking neuron maps an input spike train to an output spike train. Three coding schemes dominate:

1. **Rate coding.** Information in the mean firing rate. Robust but slow and wasteful — *N* spikes cost *N* times one spike.
2. **Temporal (latency) coding.** Information in *when* spikes occur, e.g., time-to-first-spike (TTFS), where a single spike per neuron can suffice [8].
3. **Population coding.** Distributed patterns across neurons, trading neuron count for precision.

Recent work shows surrogate-gradient training can learn timing codes — inter-spike intervals, coincidence, cross-channel intervals — via SLAYER with learnable time constants and delays [7].

### 2.2 The leaky integrate-and-fire neuron

The LIF model describes subthreshold membrane potential *u(t)* by

$$\tau_m \frac{du}{dt} = -(u - u_{\text{rest}}) + R\,I(t),$$

emitting a spike when *u ≥ ϑ*, then resetting. The leak gives fading memory; without it the neuron is a perfect integrator driven to threshold by any DC bias. The leak makes the neuron a low-pass filter plus threshold detector. LIF is the native primitive of Loihi 2 (microcode-programmable [1]) and SpiNNaker2, and recurrent LIF networks are computationally universal.

### 2.3 Spike-timing-dependent plasticity

Biological synapses adjust efficacy by relative pre/post spike timing. Classical pair-based STDP [8]:

$$\Delta w = \begin{cases} A_+ e^{(t_{\text{pre}}-t_{\text{post}})/\tau_+}, & t_{\text{pre}} \le t_{\text{post}} \\ -A_- e^{-(t_{\text{pre}}-t_{\text{post}})/\tau_-}, & t_{\text{pre}} > t_{\text{post}} \end{cases}$$

STDP is *local* — it needs only spike times at the synapse — making it the natural candidate for on-chip learning. Hybrid rules (SSTDP) fuse global error signals with local windows [8]; earlier supervised temporal learners (ReSuMe, tempotron) preceded the surrogate-gradient era.

### 2.4 Address-event representation

Neuromorphic chips communicate via *address-event representation* (AER): a spike is a packet carrying the source neuron's address and a timestamp — or, on Loihi 2, an integer payload (graded spikes) [2][9]. Cores stay quiescent until spikes arrive; there is no global clock. Loihi 2 uses asynchronous sub-timestep communication with barrier synchronization, giving variable-length algorithmic timesteps (minimum 200 ns) that scale with load [1].

| Property | Clocked ANN accelerator | Event-driven neuromorphic chip |
|---|---|---|
| Activation | Every timestep, every neuron | Only on spike arrival |
| Data | Dense FP16/INT8 tensors | Sparse spike events (AER) |
| Energy scaling | ∝ model size × sequence length | ∝ spike count (activity) |
| Memory | Off-chip DRAM, shared bus | Local SRAM per neurocore |
| Communication | Synchronous, global clock | Asynchronous mesh NoC |

The energy row is the entire economic argument, quantified in Section 5. Mead's analog VLSI founded the field; SpiNNaker showed million-core simulation; Loihi (2017), Loihi 2 (2021), and SpiNNaker2 industrialized digital neuromorphic deployment.

## 3 Methodology

This dissertation synthesizes published theory, silicon specifications, and measured benchmarks. **Neuron model:** current-based LIF in discrete time, *u[t+1] = βu[t] + (1−β)(u_rest + RI[t]) − ϑS[t]* with *S[t] = H(u[t] − ϑ)*, the standard surrogate-gradient form [5][6]. **Learning rules compared:** (a) surrogate-gradient BPTT (SuperSpike-style fast-sigmoid pseudo-derivative [5]); (b) SLAYER with temporal error reassignment and Spikemax loss [7]; (c) EventProp exact adjoint gradients [4][8]; (d) local STDP and supervised hybrids [8] — compared on gradient exactness, memory cost, and hardware mappability. **Hardware targets:** Loihi 2 (128 neurocores, 1M neurons / 123M synapses, graded spikes, microcode-programmable neurons [1][2]) via the open-source, platform-agnostic Lava framework [9]; SpiNNaker2 (many-core ARM, MAC accelerators, DVFS [4]). **Energy accounting:** measured wall-power and energy-per-task from published tables only — no invented numbers; every quantitative claim is cited. **Workloads:** temporally structured tasks — DVS gesture recognition, closed-loop RL (CartPole, Acrobot), streaming language modeling — where event-driven sparsity is physically meaningful.

```python
# Canonical discrete-time LIF neuron with surrogate gradient (PyTorch-style)
import torch

class SpikeFn(torch.autograd.Function):
    @staticmethod
    def forward(ctx, u, theta=1.0, beta_sg=100.0):
        ctx.save_for_backward(u, torch.tensor([theta, beta_sg]))
        return (u >= theta).float()          # Heaviside: non-differentiable
    @staticmethod
    def backward(ctx, grad_s):
        u, p = ctx.saved_tensors
        theta, beta_sg = p.tolist()
        dsdv = 1.0 / (beta_sg * abs(u - theta) + 1.0) ** 2  # surrogate derivative
        return grad_s * dsdv, None, None

def lif_step(u, I, beta=0.9, theta=1.0):
    s = SpikeFn.apply(u, theta)   # spike (forward: exact discontinuity)
    u = beta * u + (1 - beta) * I # leak + integrate
    return u * (1 - s), s         # hard reset after spike
```

This is the conceptual core of modern SNN training: forward, the neuron spikes discontinuously; backward, the chain rule flows through a *surrogate derivative* — here the fast-sigmoid form used by SuperSpike [5] — that is non-vanishing near threshold and decays with |u − ϑ| [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The LIF Neuron as a Computational Primitive

The LIF neuron is the *lingua franca* of neuromorphic computing: simple enough for Loihi 2 microcode [1] and ARM-efficient SpiNNaker2 kernels [4], yet rich enough for universal recurrent computation. The refractory period bounds firing at *f_max = 1/τ_ref*; TTFS coding reads the answer from the *first* spike only, an extreme sparsity regime demonstrated on analog hardware [8].

> **Theorem (Event-driven energy scaling):** Let a core expend dynamic energy *ε_syn* per synaptic operation and *ε_spk* per spike-routing event, and let a layer with fan-in *F* emit *K* spikes over a task. Then *E_dyn = K(F·ε_syn + ε_spk) + E_leak*, independent of neuron count except through *K*. If mean firing probability per neuron per timestep is *p ≪ 1*, then *E_dyn ≈ p·E_dense*, the dense baseline's energy.

*Proof sketch.* Each spike triggers exactly *F* synaptic accumulations and one routing event; silent neurons trigger nothing. With *K = p·N·T* spikes over *T* timesteps versus *N·F* MACs per timestep for the dense baseline, the ratio is *p* up to the leakage floor. ∎

The advantage *equals the sparsity*: 50% activity saves 2×; 1% activity saves 100×. Measured SNNs on event-camera data routinely sit below 5% activity — the physical reason DVS benchmarks show the largest efficiency deltas [4].

### 4.2 Surrogate Gradients and the Non-Differentiability Barrier

The obstacle is analytic: *S = H(u − ϑ)* has *∂H/∂u = 0* almost everywhere, so the chain rule propagates zeros. **SuperSpike** (Zenke & Ganguli) replaces, in the backward pass only, the true derivative with a smooth *surrogate derivative* — a scaled fast-sigmoid derivative [5]:

$$\tilde{\sigma}'(u) = \frac{1}{(\beta_{\text{SG}}|u - \vartheta| + 1)^2}.$$

The steepness *β_SG* interpolates between the degenerate true derivative and a broad gradient window. Training is *robust to the choice of surrogate* — ReLU, arctangent, fast-sigmoid all succeed [6] — suggesting it acts as a useful descent direction, a view recent theory formalizes via the smoothed objective's geometry [6]. SuperSpike adds *eligibility traces* — per-synapse low-pass filters of presynaptic activity — enabling temporal credit assignment at 0.1 ms resolution [5].

```haskell
-- Fast-sigmoid surrogate derivative (SuperSpike / Neftci et al. 2019)
surrogateD :: Double -> Double -> Double -> Double
surrogateD beta theta u = 1.0 / (beta * abs (u - theta) + 1.0) ** 2

-- Eligibility trace: low-pass filtered presynaptic spikes
eligibility :: Double -> [Double] -> [Double]
eligibility alpha = scanl (\e s -> alpha * e + s) 0.0
```

**EventProp** takes the opposite stance: instead of smoothing the discontinuity, it computes *exact* gradients for continuous-time SNNs by treating spike times as implicit functions of weights and applying the adjoint-state method [4][8]. Gradients accumulate only at *events*, so the backward pass is itself event-driven with *O(spikes)* memory — the learning rule most aligned with neuromorphic hardware. Its GRU analogue underpins event-based training theory on SpiNNaker2 [4]. The field's working summary: *surrogates for scale, EventProp for principle*.

### 4.3 SLAYER and Temporal Credit Assignment

SLAYER (Spike Layer Error Reassignment in Time) attacks temporal credit assignment directly: which earlier spikes caused the error at time *t*? It backpropagates errors through time and reassigns them to causal spike events via a surrogate gradient of the spike train [8]:

$$\Delta w = -\eta \sum_t \frac{\partial E}{\partial s(t)}\frac{\partial s(t)}{\partial w}.$$

Treating the spike train — not the membrane potential — as the differentiable object aligns learning with the network's communication structure. Recent SLAYER results show networks with the surrogate *dH/dx = 1/(α|x|+1)²* (α = 100) and Spikemax loss learning genuine timing codes — inter-spike intervals, coincidence, cross-channel intervals [7]. Decisive augmentations: *learnable time constants τ* and *learnable axonal delays*, adapting each neuron's temporal receptive field [7].

### 4.4 Hardware Substrates: Loihi 2, Lava, and SpiNNaker2

**Intel Loihi 2** (Intel 4 process): 128 neurocores, up to 1M neurons and 123M synapses, with *microcode-programmable neuron models* — a break from Loihi 1's fixed LIF, permitting on-chip implementations of the Section 4.2–4.3 learning rules [1]. Key advances: **graded spikes** carrying integer payloads (a sigma-delta PilotNet SNN achieved MSE 0.035 with 323,815 synaptic ops vs. 0.0412 with 20,250,023 for rate-coded Loihi 1 — **60× fewer ops** at lower error [2]); asynchronous sub-timestep communication with per-timestep barrier sync (timesteps down to 200 ns [1]); 3D tiling with 4× inter-chip bandwidth plus Ethernet and event-sensor interfaces [2]; up to 5000× biological real-time [2].

**Lava** is Intel's open-source, *platform-agnostic* framework for event-based message passing [9]. Its process-and-channel model mirrors hardware semantics (quiescent until events), with a Python API over optimized C/CUDA/OpenCL and PyTorch/TensorFlow/YARP interop [9] — Intel's bid for framework convergence, since "software continues to hold back the field" (M. Davies) [2].

**SpiNNaker2** takes the many-core ARM route: processing elements with MAC accelerators, per-core DVFS, and a packet-switched NoC [4]. Demonstrated results: a 95%-pruned 3-layer EGRU language model (1,350 units) on one chip matches GPU perplexity exactly (81.4) at 0.065 J vs 1.19 J on an A100 at batch one — **18×** [4]; DVS gesture recognition at 96.83% accuracy for 0.023 J vs 0.067 J [4]; hardware-aware fine-tuned *spiking Q-networks* for CartPole (0.006 J vs 0.145 J, **24×**) and Acrobot (0.010 J vs 0.321 J, **32×**) versus a GTX 1650, at 0.335 W vs 7.39 W with comparable latency [3]; and on-chip event-based backpropagation with superior measured efficiency even before DVFS [4].

| Platform | Scale | Typical power | Signature result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intel Loihi 2 | 1M neurons, 128 cores | ~1–2 W | 60× fewer synops vs Loihi 1 [2] |
| SpiNNaker2 | 100k+ PEs (multi-chip) | 0.34–0.8 W | 24–32× energy vs GTX 1650 [3] |
| NVIDIA A100 (ref) | dense | 60–123 W | comparison baseline [4] |
| NVIDIA GTX 1650 (ref) | dense | 7.3 W | RL comparison baseline [3] |

### 4.5 STDP and Local Plasticity: The On-Chip Learning Primitive

Global gradient methods need backward passes and error buffering; STDP needs only local spike times — the natural primitive for *online, on-chip* adaptation where memory-compute colocation matters most. Recent Loihi 2 work demonstrates *continual learning* via co-designed SNNs: stateless graded-spike input relays, prototype populations across the neurocores, and a compact novelty-detector/modulator state machine gating plasticity — mapped by the Lava partitioner onto 41 of 128 neurocores [1]. This moves the field from frozen offline-trained deployments toward *lifelong learning at the edge*.

Classical STDP suffers unbounded growth and correlation sensitivity, addressed by weight-dependence, triplet-STDP, and homeostasis. Hybrid SSTDP rules modulate STDP windows with global errors, recovering supervised power while preserving locality [8]. The emerging division: *surrogate/EventProp training for initial optimization*, *STDP-like plasticity for continual on-chip adaptation* [1].

```tla+
--------------------------- MODULE SpikeRouting ---------------------------
\* Address-event spike delivery on a neuromorphic mesh (TLA+).
EXTENDS Naturals
CONSTANTS Cores
VARIABLES queues, now   \* per-core AER packet queues; barrier timestep

Init == queues = [c \in Cores |-> <<>>] /\ now = 0
Emit(c) == queues' = [queues EXCEPT ![c] = Append(@, now)] /\ UNCHANGED now
Route(c) == queues[c] /= <<>> /\ queues' = [queues EXCEPT ![c] = Tail(@)]
            /\ UNCHANGED now
Barrier == (\A c \in Cores : queues[c] = <<>>) /\ now' = now + 1
           /\ UNCHANGED queues
Next == \E c \in Cores : Emit(c) \/ Route(c) \/ Barrier
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<queues, now>>
=============================================================================
```

```rust
// Event-driven kernel: quiescent until a spike arrives.
struct NeuromorphicCore {
    neurons: Vec<LifState>,       // membrane potentials (local SRAM)
    weights: Vec<Vec<f32>>,       // colocated synaptic fan-in
    event_queue: Vec<SpikeEvent>, // AER packets
}
impl NeuromorphicCore {
    fn on_spike(&mut self, ev: SpikeEvent) {
        // Cost is O(spikes), never O(neurons):
        for (dst, w) in self.fanout(ev.src_id) {
            let n = &mut self.neurons[dst];
            n.current += w * ev.payload as f32;
            if n.potential >= n.threshold {
                n.potential = n.reset;
                self.event_queue.push(SpikeEvent::new(dst));
            }
        }
    }
}
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Result 5.1 (SpiNNaker2 vs. GTX 1650, closed-loop RL).** Spiking Q-networks on SpiNNaker2 reach maximum CartPole reward (200) at 0.335 W / 0.006 J per episode vs 7.39 W / 0.145 J on a GTX 1650 — **24×** — and Acrobot at 0.010 J vs 0.321 J — **32×** — with comparable latency (20 ms vs 19.7 ms) [3]. Latency parity with 22× lower power: the strongest published case for neuromorphic real-time control.

**Result 5.2 (SpiNNaker2 vs. A100, batch-one inference).** The EGRU language model matches GPU perplexity (81.4) at 0.065 J vs 1.19 J — **18×** — at 0.39 W vs 60 W [4]; DVS gestures: 96.83% accuracy at 0.023 J vs 0.067 J [4]. The authors state the crossover explicitly: GPU energy amortizes over batch size while SpiNNaker2's is batch-independent, so the advantage peaks at *batch one* — real-time edge inference [4].

**Result 5.3 (Loihi 2 graded spikes).** Sigma-delta PilotNet SNN: MSE 0.035 with 323,815 synaptic ops vs 0.0412 with 20,250,023 for rate-coded Loihi 1 — **60× fewer operations** at lower error [2].

**Result 5.4 (Surrogate training efficacy).** SuperSpike trains multilayer LIF nets at 0.1 ms resolution [5]; SLAYER nets with learnable τ and delays master timing codes [7]; deep SNNs sit within a few percent of ANN accuracy on DVS benchmarks, with spiking transformers extending the frontier [8].

| Workload | Comparison | Energy ratio | Regime |
|---|---|---|---|
| CartPole-v0 SNN policy | SpiNNaker2 vs GTX 1650 | 24× [3] | event-driven control |
| Acrobot-v1 SNN policy | SpiNNaker2 vs GTX 1650 | 32× [3] | event-driven control |
| EGRU language model (b=1) | SpiNNaker2 vs A100 | 18× [4] | event-gated recurrence |
| DVS gestures (b=1) | SpiNNaker2 vs A100 | 2.9× [4] | event-camera stream |
| PilotNet sigma-delta | Loihi 2 vs Loihi 1 | 60× synops [2] | graded-spike coding |

> **Corollary (Batch-one dominance):** For streaming inference at batch size one — the edge regime — event-driven architectures minimize energy per decision; for batched offline processing, dense accelerators amortize static power and keep the advantage [4]. The regimes partition the workload space rather than competing.

## 6 Limitations

**1. The static-benchmark gap.** On dense static datasets, surrogate-trained SNNs trail ANNs, and ANN-to-SNN conversion trades latency for accuracy — more timesteps recover accuracy but destroy the sparsity that motivates the paradigm. The strongest SNN results are on *event-native* data; frame-based inputs must first be *encoded* into spikes, an encoding cost rarely counted in comparisons.

**2. Sim-to-hardware mismatch.** Training is floating-point simulation; deployment faces quantized weights, finite synapse SRAM [1], and timing jitter. Hardware-aware fine-tuning [3] mitigates this with a hardware-in-the-loop phase, but the gap persists.

**3. Toolchain immaturity.** The stack is fragmented across simulators (NEST, Brian2, Norse), training libraries (snnTorch, SpikingJelly, SLAYER), and hardware SDKs (Lava, py-spinnaker2). Debugging a mistimed spike across an asynchronous mesh is qualitatively harder than debugging a tensor program — Davies's "software continues to hold back the field" still holds [2].

**4. Training-cost asymmetry.** Surrogate BPTT costs *O(T)* memory in timesteps; EventProp reduces this to *O(spikes)* but scales less readily to very deep nets. The community trains on GPUs and deploys to neuromorphic chips, so *training* energy is unchanged — only inference benefits, narrowing the system-level win for frequently retrained models.

**5. Latency at scale.** SpiNNaker2's EGRU took 170 ms vs 19.9 ms on the A100 [4] — energy won, latency lost 8.5×, disqualifying for strict latency SLOs. Loihi 2's sub-microsecond timesteps [1] help SNN-native workloads, but the general latency–energy Pareto frontier still favors GPUs for large dense models.

**6. Benchmark hygiene.** Cross-platform comparisons hinge on methodology (wall vs chip power, host overhead, batch normalization). The 24–32× figures [3] compare an optimized SNN against a general-purpose GPU kernel; equally optimized sparse GPU kernels would narrow the gap. Read these as *comparative engineering results on specific workloads*, not as having matched biology.

## 7 Conclusion

The arc of this dissertation: (1) **The neuron.** LIF — a low-pass filter plus threshold — is the sufficient primitive, and event-driven execution yields the energy-scaling theorem: dynamic energy ∝ spike count, not model size. (2) **The learning.** Surrogate gradients (SuperSpike [5], grounded theoretically [6]), temporal credit assignment (SLAYER [7]), and exact adjoints (EventProp [4]) recover gradient-based deep learning for spiking nets, including precise timing codes. (3) **The hardware.** Loihi 2 (1M neurons, graded spikes, Lava [1][2][9]) and SpiNNaker2 (many-core, DVFS [4]) are deployment substrates, not curiosities. (4) **The evidence.** Measured 18–32× energy reductions vs GPUs on control and batch-one inference [3][4], and 60× fewer synaptic ops from graded spikes [2]. (5) **The boundary.** Static benchmarks, deployment gaps, toolchain fragmentation, training asymmetry, and latency delimit today's deployability.

As event-based sensors proliferate at the edge, perception inputs become natively sparse, and event-driven processors become the *natural* computer for the signal. Remaining work is engineering: closing the deployment gap, unifying software around Lava, scaling exact event-based learning. The brain's twenty watts remain a distant asymptote — but the path is now paved with measured numbers, not aspirations.
