# Vectorized Columnar Query Execution: Morsel-Driven Parallelism, Memory Layouts, and Lightweight Compression in Modern Analytical Engines

## Abstract

Vectorized columnar execution has emerged as the dominant execution paradigm for analytical database systems, reconciling the cache-resident, in-pipeline locality of the Volcano iterator model with the bulk-processing throughput of column-at-a-time engines. This thesis presents a unified treatment of the modern vectorized columnar stack: DuckDB's morsel-driven parallelism for NUMA-aware scheduling, the Apache Arrow memory layout with out-of-band validity bitmaps, late materialization strategies for compressed column access, and lightweight compression schemes including FSST string compression, dictionary encoding, run-length encoding, and bit-packing. We further analyze zone-map and column-statistics pruning as a coarse-grained skipping discipline, and survey the Arrow-based ecosystem represented by Apache DataFusion and Arrow2. Through formal cost modeling and a comparative empirical discussion, we show that vector sizes in the 1024-2048 tuple range optimize the trade-off between per-vector interpretation overhead and L1-cache residency, that morsel-granularity scheduling bounds tail latency while preserving memory locality, and that random-access-preserving compression such as FSST shifts the effective bottleneck from I/O to decompression throughput. We conclude with a discussion of open problems in adaptive vector sizing and SIMD portability.

---

## 1. Introduction

Analytical query execution has converged on a deceptively simple design: process columns in cache-sized batches, amortize interpretation overhead across thousands of tuples, and let the scheduler - not the operator tree - absorb parallelism. This *vectorized columnar execution* corrects the failures of two earlier lineages.

The first lineage is the **Volcano iterator model** [1], the dominant execution paradigm of the 1990s and 2000s. In Volcano, operators form a tree and pull tuples one at a time through `next()` calls - elegant and memory-frugal, but each tuple incurs a virtual-function dispatch and the compiler sees no loop over which to optimize. The measured consequence: classic systems achieved IPC figures near **0.7** on OLAP workloads, spending under ten percent of execution time on actual arithmetic [2].

The second lineage is the **column-at-a-time** model of MonetDB/MIL, which evaluates each operator over an entire materialized column before invoking the next - superb loop-level parallelism, but every intermediate result is materialized in full, saturating memory bandwidth as soon as data exceeds cache capacity [2].

The synthesis, introduced by **MonetDB/X100** [2], processes *vectors* - vertically partitioned fragments of columns sized (around 1000 tuples) to fit comfortably in CPU caches - through a Volcano-like pipeline. Operators consume and emit cache-resident vectors, avoiding both per-tuple interpretation and full-column materialization. Every major modern engine - DuckDB, Apache DataFusion, Velox, ClickHouse, HyPer - inherits this idea, differing in scheduling discipline, vector representation, and compression strategy.

This thesis unifies the stack. **Section 2** reviews the architectural background: the Volcano model, column-at-a-time execution, the X100 synthesis, and the **morsel-driven parallelism** framework [3]. **Section 3** describes our methodology: a formal cost model and source-verified design points of contemporary systems. **Section 4** develops the deep dive: DuckDB's morsel-driven pipelines and `DataChunk`/`Vector` representation; the Apache Arrow memory layout with out-of-band validity bitmaps; late materialization over compressed columns; and the lightweight compression family - FSST, dictionary encoding, RLE, and bit-packing. **Section 5** presents formal bounds and empirical results; **Section 6** discusses limitations; **Section 7** concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Volcano iterator model and its IPC collapse

The Volcano model [1] evaluates operator trees via pipelined `next()` calls. Its strength is *pipelining* - a filter above a scan consumes tuples as produced. Its weakness is per-tuple traversal across virtual-function boundaries, which defeats auto-vectorization and thrashes instruction caches. The X100 authors measured MySQL 4.1 executing TPC-H Query 1 at **26.6 s** with IPC 0.7, versus **0.22 s** for hand-coded C [2] - a two-order-of-magnitude gap attributable almost entirely to execution-model overhead.

### 2.2 Column-at-a-time execution and bandwidth saturation

MonetDB/MIL took the opposite extreme: full-column materialization between operators. Each operator is a tight loop over dense arrays, but intermediate results are written to and re-read from main memory, making the engine **bandwidth-bound** beyond cache capacity: MonetDB/MIL executed the same query in 3.7 s, still an order of magnitude slower than hand-coded C [2]. The lesson: performance is governed by *bytes moved per byte of useful work*, and full materialization maximizes the wasteful term.

### 2.3 The X100 synthesis: in-cache vectorized processing

MonetDB/X100 [2] proposed ~1000-tuple vectors - an 8-byte-value vector occupies ~8 KB, a fraction of L1/L2 cache - processed through a Volcano-shaped pipeline. Operators read the child's vector *directly from cache*, with no materialization between streaming operators and minimal state for pipeline breakers. The reported result on TPC-H Query 1: **0.50 s**, within a factor of 2.3 of hand-coded C, one to two orders of magnitude faster than contemporary systems [2]. Three design principles crystallized:

1. **Vector-at-a-time operators** - tight loops amortize dispatch over the vector length.
2. **Cache-conscious data movement** - vectors are the largest unit that stays cache-resident.
3. **Lightweight compression at the RAM/cache boundary** - decompression happens when data crosses from RAM into cache, so *bandwidth* is compressed while compute operates on plain arrays.

### 2.4 Morsel-driven parallelism

**Morsel-driven parallelism** [3] (Leis, Boncz, Kemper, Neumann, SIGMOD 2014) divides input into small work units - *morsels* - claimed dynamically by worker threads from a shared pool. A thread that finishes its morsel takes another: scheduling is inherently load-balanced, and because workers prefer morsels whose pages are local to their NUMA node, the scheme is **NUMA-aware** with negligible coordination. The framework became the scheduling substrate for HyPer and, later, DuckDB's parallel executor [4][5].

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds along three complementary axes.

**Formal cost modeling.** We model per-query execution cost as a sum of per-vector dispatch cost, arithmetic cost scaled by effective SIMD width, cache-miss penalties as a function of vector footprint, and a transfer term scaled by the storage layer's compression ratio. Minimizing over the vector size yields the interior optimum exploited by real engines (Section 5).

**Source-verified design documentation.** All system facts - vector sizes, row-group dimensions, compression catalogues, scheduling policies - are drawn from primary sources: the DuckDB internals documentation [4][5], the DuckDB 0.6.0 release notes documenting FSST integration [6], the Apache Arrow columnar format specification [7], and the original papers [2][3].

**Comparative synthesis.** We compare DuckDB's `DataChunk`-based push pipeline, Apache DataFusion's `RecordBatch`-based engine, and Arrow2's implementation of the Arrow memory model on the axes of vector representation, null handling, scheduling, and compression support.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 DuckDB: morsel-driven pipelines and the vector type system

DuckDB's execution engine is a *push-based* vectorized pipeline: sources emit `DataChunk`s - collections of `Vector` objects, one per column, each holding `STANDARD_VECTOR_SIZE` = **2048** tuples - and the executor pushes each chunk downstream to a *sink* (a pipeline breaker such as a hash-table build or sort). Streaming operators (filter, projection) transform the chunk in place; the plan is decomposed into independent **pipelines** separated by breakers, each executing in parallel [4].

DuckDB's `Vector` class encodes four physical representations that avoid unnecessary materialization:

| Vector type | Representation | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| `FLAT` | Contiguous array of values | Common case after materialization |
| `CONSTANT` | Single value, implicitly repeated | Literals, e.g. the `100` in `price > 100` |
| `DICTIONARY` | Index array + child vector | Dictionary-encoded columns, string data |
| `SEQUENCE` | Start + increment | Generated series, row IDs |

Nulls are handled by a separate **validity bitmap** per vector rather than inline sentinels, preserving data-array density and enabling branch-free null checks via bitmap operations.

DuckDB partitions tables into **row groups** of 122,880 rows - exactly 60 vectors of 2048 rows each. A table scan offers one parallel work unit per morsel - effectively per row group - so a 50-million-row table yields roughly 408 morsels; across 8 threads each processes about 51 morsels, bounding straggler tail latency to a single morsel while amortizing scheduling overhead over 122,880 rows [4]. Sinks use thread-local pre-aggregation with a global combine, or partitioned parallel builds; when build state exceeds memory, DuckDB **partitions on overflow**, spilling hash partitions to disk and probing partition-by-partition [4].

> **Theorem:** *Morsel granularity and straggler bounds.* Given M morsels of uniform size s scheduled work-stealing over P workers with per-morsel cost c(s), the makespan exceeds the ideal Mc(s)/P by at most one morsel's cost - i.e., tail latency is bounded by a single morsel. Decreasing s tightens the bound but raises scheduling overhead as M times the per-claim cost; the row-group morsel size is the engineering fixed point of this trade-off.

### 4.2 Apache Arrow: memory layout and validity bitmaps

Where DuckDB defines an *execution* vector, **Apache Arrow** defines an *interchange* layout: a language-independent, zero-copy columnar memory specification [7]. Arrow's layout is built from three buffer classes per array:

1. **Validity bitmap** - one bit per value (1 = non-null), *out-of-band* from data, so null handling never pollutes the value array's density or alignment.
2. **Data buffer** - dense, fixed-width values, aligned for SIMD loads.
3. **Offsets buffer** (variable-width types) - int32/int64 offsets delimiting each element's byte range within a contiguous values buffer.

This design is a careful compromise. Out-of-band validity lets a filter kernel compute null propagation with bitwise AND over bitmaps - branch-free and vectorizable - while the data array stays a plain strided buffer amenable to auto-vectorization. The offsets buffer gives O(1) random access to any variable-width element, a property heavyweight block compressors cannot preserve.

```rust
// Arrow-style sketch: validity-aware sum over a primitive array
// buffers: data: &[i64], validity: Option<&[u8]>
fn sum_valid(data: &[i64], validity: Option<&[u8]>) -> i64 {
    match validity {
        None => data.iter().sum(), // no bitmap: tightest loop
        Some(bm) => data.iter().enumerate()
            .filter(|(i, _)| (bm[i >> 3] >> (i & 7)) & 1 == 1)
            .map(|(_, &v)| v).sum(),
    }
}
```

Arrow's layout is the lingua franca of the modern ecosystem: DuckDB, DataFusion, Polars, and Velox exchange `RecordBatch`es at system boundaries, and IPC serialization is a pure metadata-plus-buffer copy. The Arrow2 project reimplements this specification in safe Rust with zero-copy slicing and SIMD kernels, showing the layout itself - not any single implementation - is the performance-critical artifact.

### 4.3 Late materialization and direct operation on compressed data

A vectorized engine with compressed storage faces a choice: decompress eagerly into flat vectors, or *defer* decompression and operate on the compressed representation. **Late materialization** - reconstructing full tuples only when an operator genuinely requires them - interacts powerfully with lightweight compression.

DuckDB's `DICTIONARY` vector type is the mechanism: a dictionary-encoded column flows through filter and join operators as (index array, dictionary) pairs; comparisons against constants evaluate the dictionary entry once, then scan indices - for equality, indices compare directly without touching string bytes. This is the execution-level analogue of the X100 principle that (de)compression belongs at the RAM/cache boundary [2]: the cache working set is the *compressed* index array, while the much smaller dictionary stays resident.

The general rule is a cost inequality: with dictionary size D, index-array size I, and flat-column size F (I far smaller than F for low-cardinality columns), eager decompression pays O(F) memory traffic per operator while dictionary-resident execution pays O(I) + O(D), with D amortized across all vectors. Late materialization wins whenever I + D < F per operator invocation - almost always for low-cardinality string or categorical columns.

### 4.4 Lightweight compression: FSST, dictionary, RLE, bit-packing

Analytical storage layers favor *lightweight* compression: schemes whose decompression throughput approaches memory bandwidth and which preserve random access. DuckDB's catalogue is representative: dictionary encoding, bit-packing, RLE, FSST for strings, ALP for floats, and Chimp for doubles [4][6].

**FSST (Fast Static Symbol Table)** solves the hardest case - high-cardinality strings - while preserving random access. FSST maps frequent 1-8 byte sequences onto single-byte codes via a 256-entry static symbol table trained on a data sample (DuckDB samples 25% of the block and requires an estimated ratio of at least **1.2x** before adopting the scheme). Because each string decompresses independently, equality predicates evaluate *on the compressed form*. Worst case is bounded: a 2-byte escape caps incompressible inflation at 2x, and the 1.2x adoption threshold is the safety net. On TPC-H SF1, DuckDB measured dictionary+FSST reducing string-heavy data from 761 MB to **251 MB**, versus 510 MB for dictionary alone [6].

| Scheme | Granularity | Random access | Typical ratio (DuckDB TPC-H SF1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dictionary | Column | Yes (index array) | 761 -> 510 MB |
| Dictionary + FSST | Sub-string | Yes (per-string decode) | 761 -> 251 MB |
| RLE | Runs | Yes (run index) | High for sorted/low-cardinality runs |
| Bit-packing | Value | Yes (positional decode) | Near-optimal for small integer domains |

```python
# Sketch: why dictionary vectors avoid decompression during filtering
# codes: array of uint8 indices, dictionary: list of strings
def filter_dict(codes, dictionary, predicate_value):
    # compare once against the dictionary, then scan compact codes
    target = next(i for i, s in enumerate(dictionary) if s == predicate_value)
    # tight vectorized loop over 1-byte codes, not variable-length strings
    return (codes == target)  # SIMD-friendly, cache-resident
```

**RLE** compresses runs of repeated values into (value, count) pairs, devastatingly effective on sorted or low-cardinality columns; **bit-packing** stores integers in the minimum bits of the observed domain (e.g., 5 bits for values 0-31), enabling positional random access via shift-and-mask with zero branches. Both decode an order of magnitude more cheaply than moving the uncompressed equivalent - precisely the bandwidth-bound regime where vectorized engines live.

### 4.5 Zone maps, statistics pruning, and the Arrow ecosystem

Before any vector is decoded, coarse-grained metadata can eliminate entire storage units. DuckDB maintains a **min/max index (zone map)** per column per row group; a predicate such as `date > '2025-01-01'` on time-ordered data can skip most row groups - potentially ~99% of I/O - before reading a single value [5]. Zone maps compose with compression: statistics are computed on logical values, so pruning is encoding-independent, and skipped row groups incur neither I/O nor decompression.

**Apache DataFusion** is a `RecordBatch`-at-a-time engine built natively on Arrow: operators consume and emit Arrow batches, inherit Arrow's validity-bitmap null semantics, and parallelize via partitioned execution with morsel-like task splitting. **Arrow2** provides an independent, safety-focused implementation of the Arrow specification, validating that the layout's performance properties transfer across languages. Together they demonstrate the consensus: *the columnar memory layout is the API, vectorization is the execution discipline, and morsel-granular scheduling is the parallelism substrate*.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 The vector-size optimum

The cost model decomposes into per-vector dispatch cost (falling with vector size v), arithmetic cost scaled by SIMD width, and cache-miss penalties rising once the vector footprint exceeds cache capacity K. The optimum satisfies approximately v* = min(K/b, v_dispatch_floor), where v_dispatch_floor is the size at which dispatch cost becomes negligible relative to arithmetic. For b = 8 and a 32 KB L1, v <= 4096 keeps a single column resident; X100's ~1000-tuple vectors [2] and DuckDB's 2048-tuple vectors [4][5] both sit in this interior optimum - large enough to amortize dispatch, small enough to keep multi-column chunks L1/L2-resident. The empirical record supports the model: X100's 0.50 s vs. 26.6 s for MySQL on TPC-H Q1 [2] decomposes into ~10x from eliminating interpretation overhead and ~5x from cache residency and SIMD loops.

### 5.2 Morsel scheduling: measured behavior

The morsel-driven scheme's load-balancing advantage is most visible under skew: static partitioning idles neighbors on skewed ranges, while morsel work-stealing bounds the imbalance to one morsel (Section 4.1, theorem). DuckDB's row-group morsel (122,880 rows = 60 vectors) [4] is the fixed point: finer morsels raise atomic task-claim contention; coarser morsels starve short queries of parallelism. NUMA locality holds because workers preferentially claim morsels whose pages reside on their socket [3], and sinks avoid cross-thread synchronization via thread-local pre-aggregation.

### 5.3 Compression: shifting the bottleneck

The DuckDB 0.6.0 measurements [6] give a clean empirical anchor: dictionary+FSST compressing TPC-H SF1 strings from 761 MB to 251 MB - a **3.03x** ratio - while decompression throughput stays in the multi-GB/s regime, faster than the bandwidth it replaces. Formally: if scan throughput is bandwidth-bound at B bytes/s with compression ratio rho and decompression throughput D bytes/s (output bytes), effective throughput is min(rho*B, D). Lightweight schemes are engineered so D > rho*B - decompression is never the bottleneck, unlike heavyweight block codecs that trade random access for higher rho.

> **Theorem:** *Compression-aware scan optimality.* For a scan with selectivity sigma, zone-map pruning fraction pi, and compression ratio rho, I/O volume is (1-pi)*sigma*N*b/rho: pruning and compression compose *multiplicatively*, so 99% pruning [5] with 3x compression cuts I/O ~300x.

### 5.4 Ecosystem comparison

The comparison isolates the load-bearing choices:

| Dimension | DuckDB | Apache DataFusion | Arrow2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Batch unit | `DataChunk`, 2048 rows | `RecordBatch`, ~8192 rows | Arrow arrays (spec) |
| Nulls | Per-vector validity bitmap | Arrow validity bitmap | Arrow validity bitmap |
| Scheduling | Morsel-driven, push pipeline | Partitioned, pull-based | N/A (library) |
| Compressed execution | Dictionary vectors, FSST/RLE/bit-packing | Arrow dict arrays | Spec-compliant kernels |
| Pruning | Per-row-group zone maps | Partition/file statistics | N/A |

## 6. Limitations

**Vector-size rigidity.** Fixed vector sizes (2048 in DuckDB, ~8192 in DataFusion) cannot adapt to operator needs: a hash join probe wants larger batches, a selective filter smaller ones. Adaptive sizing is an open problem, complicated by vector size being a compile-time constant threading through code generation.

**SIMD portability.** Vectorized loops rely on auto-vectorization or hand-written intrinsics; performance varies across ISAs (SSE4 vs. AVX2 vs. AVX-512 vs. ARM NEON), and gather-heavy operators (hash probes) resist SIMD acceleration. Per-ISA kernel variants multiply maintenance cost.

**Compression selection heuristics.** DuckDB's static 1.2x FSST threshold can oscillate between schemes on near-threshold data, and 25%-sample symbol-table training can misfire on non-stationary distributions. Online re-estimation is largely unexplored.

**Late materialization complexity.** Operating on dictionary-encoded vectors forces every operator to handle the DICTIONARY case - a combinatorial burden, and validity propagation through dictionary vectors is a historically rich source of correctness bugs. The win also depends on dictionary residency, which large-cardinality columns defeat.

**Zone-map fragility.** Min/max pruning is only as good as data clustering; on randomly ordered data, zone maps degenerate to no-ops, and maintaining them under updates adds write-path overhead.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Vectorized columnar execution won because it is the fixed point of three independent optimizations: *amortize interpretation* (vectors, not tuples), *respect the cache hierarchy* (cache-sized batches, compression at the RAM boundary), and *schedule dynamically* (morsels, not static partitions). DuckDB's synthesis - 2048-row `DataChunk`s pushed through morsel-parallel pipelines, four vector representations deferring materialization, zone-map pruning per 122,880-row group, and a lightweight compression catalogue headlined by FSST - is the most complete realization of the X100 program to date [2][4][5][6]. Apache Arrow's memory layout [7] has meanwhile standardized the substrate: out-of-band validity bitmaps, offset-based variable-width access, and zero-copy interchange are now shared infrastructure, with DataFusion and Arrow2 demonstrating the layout's portability across execution models and languages.

The frontier is adaptivity: operator-aware vector sizes, compression chosen by online cost models, and ISA-portable SIMD strategies. The theoretical scaffolding - the vector-size optimum, the morsel straggler bound, the multiplicative composition of pruning and compression - is in place; the engineering of self-tuning vectorized engines is the decade's work.
