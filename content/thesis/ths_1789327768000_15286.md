# Pulsar Timing Arrays and Nanohertz Gravitational Waves: The NANOGrav Evidence

## Abstract

Fifteen years of precision radio timing of 67 millisecond pulsars by the North American Nanohertz Observatory for Gravitational Waves have produced the first compelling evidence for a nanohertz stochastic gravitational-wave background. The timing residuals exhibit inter-pulsar correlations that follow the Hellings-Downs pattern predicted for gravitational radiation in general relativity, with the gravitational-wave hypothesis favored over uncorrelated common noise by Bayes factors of 200 to 1000 and a frequentist optimal-statistic significance near four standard deviations. Under the standard model of inspiraling supermassive black hole binaries, the inferred characteristic strain amplitude is 2.4 (+0.7, -0.6) x 10^-15 at a reference frequency of one inverse year. Independent corroboration by the European, Indian, Parkes, and Chinese pulsar timing arrays in 2023 established cross-validation across instruments, methods, and skies. This article reconstructs the experiment from first principles: the millisecond pulsar timing model, the geometry of the Hellings-Downs correlation, the Bayesian and frequentist machinery of the detection, and the astrophysical interpretation of the signal as the unresolved superposition of supermassive black hole binaries, while confronting spectral shape, cosmic variance, and new-physics alternatives.

## 1 Introduction

The direct detection of gravitational waves by Advanced LIGO in 2015 opened the audio-band gravitational-wave sky, spanning roughly ten hertz to a few kilohertz, where the dominant sources are mergers of stellar-mass compact objects. Yet the gravitational-wave spectrum is enormously broad. At the opposite extreme of accessible frequencies lies the nanohertz band, from roughly one to one hundred nanohertz, where the wavelength of radiation is measured in light-years and the expected sources are among the most massive objects in the universe: pairs of supermassive black holes, millions to billions of times the mass of the Sun, spiraling together in the aftermath of galaxy mergers [1].

No human-built instrument can be sensitive at such frequencies. Instead, nature provides the detector. Millisecond pulsars are neutron stars spinning hundreds of times per second, whose lighthouse-like radio beams sweep past Earth with a regularity that rivals atomic clocks. By monitoring the arrival times of these pulses over years to decades, a *pulsar timing array* turns the Milky Way itself into a gravitational-wave antenna with arms thousands of light-years long. A passing gravitational wave stretches and squeezes the spacetime between Earth and each pulsar, imprinting a faint, correlated signature on the pulse arrival times [2].

In June 2023, four independent pulsar timing array collaborations announced evidence for a common low-frequency signal, and the North American Nanohertz Observatory for Gravitational Waves reported that the signal carries the distinctive quadrupolar angular-correlation fingerprint predicted by general relativity for a gravitational-wave background [1]. This article presents a complete, self-contained account of that result: the physics of pulsar timing, the geometry of the Hellings-Downs correlation, the statistical machinery that extracted the signal from fifteen years of data, and the astrophysical interpretation that is now driving one of the most active frontiers in gravitational-wave astronomy.

## 2 Background

**Millisecond pulsars as celestial clocks.** Ordinary pulsars spin down steadily, but a subset known as *millisecond pulsars* were spun up to periods of a few milliseconds by accretion from a binary companion, a process called recycling. The discovery of the first millisecond pulsar, PSR B1937+21, in 1982 demonstrated rotational stability at the level of parts in 10^15, comparable to the best terrestrial time standards [1]. The brightest millisecond pulsars can be timed with a precision of order one hundred nanoseconds over decade-long baselines, which is the enabling technology of the entire field.

**How gravitational waves enter the data.** A gravitational wave propagating across the line of sight perturbs the null geodesics of the radio pulses, producing a fractional frequency shift in the observed pulse train. The observable quantity is the *timing residual*: the difference between the measured pulse arrival time and the arrival time predicted by a deterministic model of the pulsar's spin, astrometry, and orbital motion. For a gravitational wave of strain amplitude h and frequency f, the induced residual scales roughly as h divided by f. At nanohertz frequencies, even a strain as small as 10^-15 produces residuals of order one hundred nanoseconds, comfortably within reach of precision timing [2].

The signal divides into two contributions with a crucial asymmetry:

- The **Earth term**: the gravitational wave passing Earth, common to all pulsars in the array and therefore the source of inter-pulsar correlations.
- The **pulsar term**: the wave passing each pulsar at the retarded time, thousands of years in the past, which is uncorrelated between different pulsars and acts as an additional source of red noise.

This distinction is the physical origin of the Hellings-Downs correlation: only the Earth term survives the cross-correlation of two pulsars, and its angular dependence encodes the tensorial character of the radiation [2].

**A four-decade road to evidence.** The possibility of using pulsars as gravitational-wave detectors was proposed independently by Sazhin and by Detweiler in the late 1970s. In 1983, Ron Hellings and George Downs, working at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, calculated the expected angular correlation pattern for an isotropic background and used four ordinary pulsars to set the first upper limit on the nanohertz background, at the level of 1.4 x 10^-4 of the critical density of the universe [2]. The field matured with the founding of NANOGrav in 2007 and the formation of the International Pulsar Timing Array. NANOGrav's 12.5-year data set, published in 2020, revealed a common-spectrum red-noise process shared by all pulsars but could not establish the spatial correlations needed to claim a gravitational-wave origin. The 15-year data set changed that, with 2,211 distinct pulsar pairs among 67 pulsars available to map the correlation pattern [1].

## 3 Methodology

The NANOGrav 15-year analysis is a masterclass in extracting a part-per-billion signal from overwhelmingly noisy data. The observational foundation consists of monthly radio observations of 67 millisecond pulsars spanning up to sixteen years, collected primarily with the Arecibo Observatory, the Green Bank Telescope, and the Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array, with the Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment joining the effort in 2019 [1].

The analysis proceeds in four stages:

1. **Timing-model fitting.** For each pulsar, a deterministic model of spin frequency and its derivatives, sky position, proper motion, parallax, dispersion-measure variations, and binary orbital parameters is fit to the measured times of arrival using the TEMPO2 and PINT software packages. The post-fit residuals are the data product of interest.

2. **Noise modeling.** Each pulsar's residuals contain white noise from radiometer noise and pulse jitter, modeled with per-backend scale factors, additive variances, and correlated terms, plus intrinsic red noise from rotational irregularities and time-variable dispersion. These are modeled as Gaussian processes with power-law spectra.

3. **Common-signal search.** The array is searched for a *common-spectrum* red process present in all pulsars. Two hypotheses are compared: an uncorrelated common process, and a gravitational-wave background whose inter-pulsar correlations follow the Hellings-Downs pattern.

4. **Dual statistical frameworks.** The collaboration performed both a fully Bayesian hierarchical analysis, sampling the joint posterior over pulsar noise parameters and gravitational-wave parameters, and a frequentist *optimal statistic* analysis built directly from weighted pulsar-pair cross-correlations. 

The stochastic background is parameterized by a power-law characteristic strain spectrum, h_c(f) = A (f / f_ref)^alpha, with reference frequency f_ref set to one inverse year (approximately 31.7 nanohertz). For a population of circular supermassive black hole binaries evolving purely by gravitational-wave emission, the expected spectral index is alpha = -2/3, equivalent to a timing-residual spectral index of gamma = 13/3 [1][5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Timing Residual and the Redshift Response

The fundamental observable of pulsar timing is the gravitational-wave-induced redshift of the pulse train. For a plane gravitational wave with propagation direction k and polarization tensors, the fractional frequency shift observed from a pulsar in direction n is given by an antenna-pattern response that depends on the geometric projection of the wave's polarization onto the Earth-pulsar baseline, multiplied by the difference between the wave's phase at Earth and its phase at the pulsar [2]. Integrating this redshift over the observing span converts frequency shifts into timing residuals, the quantity actually measured.

Because the pulsar term samples the wave thousands of years before the Earth term, the two are uncorrelated for distinct pulsars. The correlated part of the signal, and hence the entire detection strategy, rests on the Earth term alone. The Hellings-Downs correlation is nothing more than the sky-averaged and polarization-averaged product of two pulsars' antenna responses to the same Earth-term wave field [6].

> **Theorem (Hellings and Downs, 1983).** For an isotropic, unpolarized stochastic gravitational-wave background composed of the two tensor polarizations of general relativity, the ensemble-averaged cross-correlation of timing residuals between two pulsars separated on the sky by an angle zeta is
>
> Gamma(zeta) = 1/2 + 1/2 - x/4 + (3/2) x ln(x), with x = (1 - cos zeta)/2,
>
> for distinct pulsars, with an additional factor of one half times a Kronecker delta for the auto-correlation term. The curve is positive for small and large separations, passes through zero near 50 degrees, reaches its minimum near 90 degrees, and returns toward positive values at 180 degrees [2][6].

The quadrupolar shape of this curve, with its characteristic dip near ninety degrees, is the *smoking gun* of the gravitational-wave background: no known noise process produces inter-pulsar correlations with this angular dependence.

### 4.2 The Overlap Reduction Function and Cosmic Variance

In the modern literature, the Hellings-Downs curve is called the *overlap reduction function*. It is the gravitational-wave analog of the beam pattern in radio interferometry, quantifying how the array's sensitivity to the background depends on pulsar-pair geometry [6]. Several subtleties matter at the precision of current data sets. Even with perfect, noise-free measurements, the correlation estimated from a finite set of pulsar pairs fluctuates around the Hellings-Downs mean because the background itself is a single random realization of a Gaussian field. Bruce Allen calculated this variance exactly in 2023, showing that it sets an irreducible floor on how well the curve can be measured, and Allen and Romano extended the calculation to arbitrary pulsar sky distributions with optimal pair-weighting estimators [6].
**Alternative polarizations.** General relativity predicts only tensor transverse modes, but more general metric theories of gravity admit scalar and vector polarizations with different correlation signatures. NANOGrav searched its 15-year data for scalar-transverse correlations and found that the Hellings-Downs pattern remains the best fit, with the tensor modes accounting for essentially all of the signal [7].

### 4.3 Bayesian Evidence and the Optimal Statistic

Two complementary statistical machineries were brought to bear, and their agreement is central to the credibility of the result [1].

The **Bayesian analysis** constructs a Gaussian-process likelihood for the full array, with the gravitational-wave background entering through a covariance matrix whose off-diagonal blocks are scaled by the Hellings-Downs correlation. Markov-chain Monte Carlo sampling over hundreds of noise and signal parameters yields posterior distributions and, via thermodynamic integration, the Bayesian evidence for competing models. The headline numbers are striking: the Hellings-Downs-correlated model is favored over a model with only independent pulsar noise by a Bayes factor exceeding 10^14, and over an uncorrelated common-spectrum model by Bayes factors of 200 to 1000, depending on spectral modeling choices. A carefully constructed null distribution, built by scrambling the inter-pulsar correlations, gives a p-value of 10^-3 for these Bayes factors under the no-correlation hypothesis [1].

The **frequentist optimal statistic** takes a more direct route: it forms a weighted sum of the measured cross-correlations over all 2,211 pulsar pairs, with weights derived from the expected Hellings-Downs pattern and the estimated noise covariances. This yields a signal-to-noise ratio for the correlated signal and a p-value computed from the analytic null distribution. The collaboration reports p-values from 5 x 10^-5 to 1.9 x 10^-4, corresponding to approximately 3.5 to 4 standard deviations [1].

The following Python snippet illustrates the mathematical core of the analysis, computing the Hellings-Downs curve and a simplified optimal-statistic-style weighted correlation:

```python
import numpy as np

def hellings_downs(zeta_deg):
    """Overlap reduction function for distinct pulsars.
    zeta_deg : angular separation in degrees."""
    zeta = np.deg2rad(zeta_deg)
    x = (1.0 - np.cos(zeta)) / 2.0
    # guard the logarithm at zero separation
    safe = np.where(x > 0, x, 1.0)
    curve = 0.5 - x / 4.0 + 1.5 * x * np.log(safe)
    return np.where(x > 0, curve, 0.5)

def characteristic_strain(f_nHz, A=2.4e-15, f_ref_nHz=31.7, alpha=-2.0/3.0):
    """Power-law characteristic strain of the nanohertz background."""
    return A * np.power(f_nHz / f_ref_nHz, alpha)

# angular separations spanning the full sky
seps = np.linspace(0.5, 179.5, 360)
hd = hellings_downs(seps)
# quadrupolar signature: positive at small/large angles, minimum near 90 deg
print("HD(10 deg) =", float(hd[np.argmin(np.abs(seps - 10.0))]))
print("HD(90 deg) =", float(hd[np.argmin(np.abs(seps - 90.0))]))
```

### 4.4 The Supermassive Black Hole Binary Interpretation

The leading astrophysical explanation for the background is the cosmic population of supermassive black hole binaries. When galaxies merge, their central black holes sink to the center of the merger remnant through dynamical friction, form a bound binary, and harden through interactions with stars and gas until gravitational-wave emission takes over, driving the pair to coalescence. Each binary radiates quasi-monochromatic waves at twice its orbital frequency; the superposition of thousands of such systems across cosmic history produces a stochastic background whose characteristic strain follows the f^-2/3 power law derived by Phinney [5].

The measured amplitude is informative and slightly uncomfortable. NANOGrav infers A = 2.4 (+0.7, -0.6) x 10^-15 at one inverse year, assuming the fiducial spectral index [1]. Astrophysical population models can reproduce this amplitude, but doing so pushes several parameters toward the edges of prior expectations: black hole masses somewhat larger than local scaling relations suggest, high occupation fractions of binaries, and efficient orbital hardening that avoids stalling at parsec-scale separations [5]. The companion NANOGrav analysis of astrophysical implications concludes that the data are consistent with binary populations, while requiring either many parameters at the edges of expectations or a few parameters notably different from standard assumptions [5].

Environmental effects enrich the picture. At the lowest frequencies, coupling to gas disks and stellar scattering can accelerate binary evolution and suppress the background below the pure gravitational-wave-driven power law, while eccentricity redistributes power across harmonics.

## 5 Empirical Results

The June 2023 announcements represented an unprecedented convergence: four independent collaborations, using different telescopes, different pulsar samples, and different analysis pipelines, reported evidence for the same signal within weeks of one another.

| Collaboration | Pulsars | Key result | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| NANOGrav 15-yr | 67 | A = 2.4 (+0.7, -0.6) x 10^-15; Hellings-Downs correlations detected | 3.5-4 sigma (frequentist); Bayes factor 200-1000 vs uncorrelated [1] |
| EPTA + InPTA DR2 | 25 | Common red signal consistent with gravitational-wave background | ~3 sigma [3] |
| PPTA DR3 | 30 | Common-spectrum process; continuous-wave searches ongoing | Evidence for common process; no resolved continuous waves [8] |
| CPTA DR1 | 57 | Correlated signal with log A_c = -14.4 (+1.0, -2.8); Hellings-Downs search | 4.6 sigma at 14 nHz via discrete-frequency method [4] |

Several features of the NANOGrav measurement deserve emphasis. First, the free-spectrum analysis, which measures the common power independently in each frequency bin rather than assuming a power law, broadly tracks the expected spectrum but shows intriguing deviations: a deficit of power at the lowest frequency bin near 2 nHz and an excess near 16 nHz [1].  Second, the polarization analysis finds no significant evidence for scalar-transverse modes; when Hellings-Downs and scalar-transverse correlations are fit simultaneously, the tensor pattern accounts for essentially all of the signal, with median optimal-statistic signal-to-noise ratios of 3.5 for Hellings-Downs versus 3.0 for scalar-transverse [7]. Third, the recovered spectral index is broadly compatible with the 13/3 value expected for circular binaries, though the uncertainties remain large enough to accommodate environmental or cosmological alternatives.

The CPTA result is notable for an entirely different reason: with only 41 months of observations using the Five-hundred-meter Aperture Spherical Telescope, the most sensitive single dish in the world, it achieved competitive sensitivity through raw radiometer precision rather than long time baselines, demonstrating a complementary path to the nanohertz sky [4].

## 6 Limitations

Enthusiasm for the result must be tempered by a clear-eyed assessment of its limitations.

1. **Statistical significance is evidence, not discovery.** The 3.5 to 4 sigma significance reported by NANOGrav, and the 3 sigma claimed by EPTA, fall short of the 5-sigma standard conventionally required for a discovery claim in physics. The collaborations themselves describe the result as *evidence for* a gravitational-wave background, a careful choice of words [1][3].

2. **Cosmic variance is irreducible.** As Allen showed, the Hellings-Downs correlation measured from any finite pulsar set is a single draw from a distribution with intrinsic scatter. No amount of improved timing precision eliminates this variance; only more pulsars, and eventually the combination of all arrays under the International Pulsar Timing Array, can beat it down [6].

3. **The common process could be partly non-gravitational.** Clock errors, solar-system ephemeris uncertainties, and unmodeled interstellar-medium effects can all produce common or partially correlated signals. The Hellings-Downs angular dependence is the decisive discriminant, but subtle systematics that mimic quadrupolar patterns remain an active area of investigation [1].

4. **The astrophysical interpretation is not unique.** A cosmological background from inflation, cosmic strings, or first-order phase transitions in the early universe could contribute to or even dominate the signal, and the current spectral measurements cannot cleanly separate it from an astrophysical foreground of binaries [5].

5. **No individual source has been resolved.** Searches for continuous gravitational waves from single supermassive black hole binaries in the Parkes third data release found no significant detections, placing upper limits on strain amplitude near 7 x 10^-15 around 10 nHz [8]. Until a resolved binary is identified, ideally with an electromagnetic counterpart, the binary interpretation remains compelling but circumstantial.

6. **Spectral anomalies are unresolved.** The low-frequency deficit and mid-band excess in the NANOGrav free spectrum may be the first hints of rich spectral structure or may fade with more data. Distinguishing these possibilities requires longer baselines and broader frequency coverage [1].

## 7 Conclusion

The NANOGrav 15-year data set, corroborated by the European, Indian, Parkes, and Chinese arrays, has opened the nanohertz gravitational-wave window. The detection of Hellings-Downs correlations among 67 pulsars, with a strain amplitude of 2.4 x 10^-15 at one inverse year and a spectrum broadly consistent with inspiraling supermassive black hole binaries, represents the culmination of a forty-year experimental program that began with four ordinary pulsars and a theoretical calculation at JPL [1][2].

The road ahead is clear and rich. The third data release of the International Pulsar Timing Array will combine NANOGrav, EPTA, PPTA, and MeerKAT data into a set of unprecedented sensitivity, expected to push the significance past the discovery threshold and to map the first hints of anisotropy in the background. Longer baselines will resolve the spectral shape, testing for the low-frequency turnover predicted by environmental coupling and for the excess power that would betray cosmic strings or phase transitions. 

Perhaps most profoundly, pulsar timing arrays have demonstrated a new kind of observatory: one in which the instrument is the Galaxy itself, the clocks are dead stars, and the signal is the collective murmur of merging giants across cosmic time. The nanohertz sky has begun to speak. The task of the coming decade is to learn its language.
