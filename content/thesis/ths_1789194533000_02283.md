# Dually Flat Statistical Manifolds and the Fisher–Rao Metric: From Exponential Families to Natural Gradient Methods in Statistical Learning

## Abstract

Information geometry endows spaces of probability distributions with a Riemannian structure defined by the Fisher information metric and a family of dual affine connections, replacing the arbitrary Euclidean geometry of parameter vectors with a geometry intrinsic to the statistical model itself. This article develops the theory from first principles: the Fisher–Rao metric and its uniqueness under Markov morphisms; Amari's one-parameter family of α-connections together with the dual exponential and mixture connections; dually flat manifolds, Legendre duality, and Bregman divergences with their generalized Pythagorean and projection theorems; and exponential families as the canonical dually flat models. We then derive the natural gradient as steepest descent in distribution space, prove its reparameterization invariance and Fisher efficiency, and survey applications in variational inference, expectation–maximization, reinforcement learning, and deep learning optimization, including the mean-field justification of block-diagonal and Kronecker-factored curvature approximations. The article closes with an assessment of the computational and theoretical limitations of the geometric program.

## 1 Introduction

Consider a parametric statistical model: a family of probability distributions \(p(x; \theta)\) indexed by a parameter vector \(\theta \in \Theta \subset \mathbb{R}^d\). The standard machinery of statistical learning — maximum likelihood estimation, gradient descent, Newton-type optimization — treats \(\Theta\) as if it were flat Euclidean space, computing gradients and steps with respect to the ordinary dot product \(\langle u, v \rangle = \sum_i u_i v_i\). Yet this Euclidean structure is an artifact of the *parametrization*, not of the *model*. Two different parametrizations of the same family of distributions induce different Euclidean gradients, different trajectories, and different convergence behavior, even though the underlying distributions — and therefore every statistical quantity of interest — are identical [1], [4].

Information geometry resolves this arbitrariness by placing a *statistical manifold* structure on the space of distributions: a Riemannian metric derived from the **Fisher information**, together with a pair of **dually coupled affine connections** that generalize the Levi-Civita connection of Riemannian geometry. This framework, developed principally by Amari and Nagaoka [1], provides:

- a canonical, parametrization-independent measure of distance between distributions — the Fisher–Rao metric — that is, up to scale, the *unique* Riemannian metric invariant under sufficient statistics (Chentsov's theorem);
- a family of dual geometries, the exponential (e-) and mixture (m-) connections, whose flatness characterizes exponential and mixture families respectively;
- the theory of **dually flat manifolds**, in which the squared Euclidean distance is replaced by a canonical divergence satisfying generalized Pythagorean and projection theorems, unifying maximum likelihood estimation, the EM algorithm, and alternating minimization;
- the **natural gradient**, the steepest-descent direction in distribution space, which achieves Fisher efficiency and underlies modern advances in variational inference, reinforcement learning, and deep neural network optimization [3], [6].

This article presents a self-contained development of these ideas. Section 2 introduces statistical manifolds and the Fisher metric; Section 3 describes the methodological program of constructing connections from divergences; Section 4 gives a deep dive into the metric, the dual connections, dually flat structure, exponential families, and the natural gradient; Section 5 provides proofs and a summary of empirical results; Section 6 discusses limitations; and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

Let \(\mathcal{X}\) be a sample space and \(S = \{p_\theta(x) = p(x; \theta) : \theta \in \Theta\}\) a smooth parametric family of probability distributions.

\[
s_i(x; \theta) := \partial_i \ell(x; \theta), \qquad \ell := \log p(x; \theta).
\]

Under standard regularity conditions (differentiation under the integral sign, support independent of \(\theta\)), the score has zero expectation, \(E_\theta[s_i] = 0\), and the tangent space \(T_\theta S\) of the statistical manifold at \(\theta\) is identified with the linear span of the score functions.

\[
g_{ij}(\theta) := E_\theta\big[\partial_i \ell(x; \theta)\, \partial_j \ell(x; \theta)\big]
\]

is then a positive-semidefinite (typically positive-definite) bilinear form on \(T_\theta S\), i.e., a Riemannian metric [1], [2].

\[
g_{ij}(\theta) = -E_\theta\big[\partial_i \partial_j \ell(x; \theta)\big],
\]

which exhibits the Fisher metric as the expected negative Hessian of the log-likelihood — the bridge to second-order optimization that will matter in Section 4.5.

The geometric significance of the metric appears at second order in the Kullback–Leibler divergence.

\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_\theta \,||\, p_{\theta'}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\, g_{ij}(\theta)\, \delta\theta^i \delta\theta^j + o(\|\delta\theta\|^2),
\]

so the Fisher metric is the quadratic form of the KL divergence: it measures *distributional* change, not parameter change.

Two further facts complete the background.

## 3 Methodology

The methodological program of information geometry proceeds in four steps [1], [4]:

1. **Construct the statistical manifold.** Equip the model \(S\) with the Fisher metric \(g\), making \((S, g)\) a Riemannian manifold. The tangent vectors are score functions; the geometry is invariant under sufficient statistics by Chentsov's theorem.
2. **Induce connections from divergences.** Given a divergence function \(D(p \| q)\) (a smooth, nonnegative function vanishing exactly on the diagonal), Eguchi's method defines a Riemannian metric and a pair of dual affine connections via the second and third derivatives of \(D\) on the diagonal. Applied to the α-divergence, this yields the one-parameter family of **α-connections** \(\nabla^{(\alpha)}\); the cases \(\alpha = 1\) and \(\alpha = -1\) give the **exponential connection** \(\nabla^{(e)}\) and the **mixture connection** \(\nabla^{(m)}\), which are mutually dual.
3. **Study flatness and duality.** A statistical manifold is *dually flat* when there exist two affine coordinate systems — one for \(\nabla\) and one for \(\nabla^*\) — linked by Legendre duality of convex potentials. On such manifolds the canonical divergence is a Bregman divergence, and the geometry supports generalized Pythagorean and projection theorems that turn iterative algorithms (EM, alternating minimization, natural gradient descent) into sequences of orthogonal projections.
4. **Apply to learning.** Exponential families are dually flat, and their natural/expectation parametrizations are the dual affine coordinates. The natural gradient — steepest descent under the Fisher metric — inherits Fisher efficiency and reparametrization invariance, and structured approximations to the FIM (block-diagonal, Kronecker-factored) make it practical for deep learning [5], [6].

The remainder of this article executes this program in detail, keeping the emphasis on *why* each geometric object is forced by statistical considerations rather than imposed by convention.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Fisher–Rao Metric and Its Invariance

We begin with the **fundamental theorem of information geometry**: a statistical manifold is characterized by a Riemannian metric \(g\) together with a pair of dual affine connections \((\nabla, \nabla^*)\), and every such triple arises from a divergence function [1], [4].

> **Theorem (Chentsov):** Let \(\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\) denote the manifold of (strictly positive) probability distributions on a finite sample space \(\mathcal{X}\). Up to a positive multiplicative constant, there is exactly one Riemannian metric on \(\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\) that is invariant under Markov morphisms. That metric is the Fisher–Rao metric [1], [2].

Invariance under a Markov morphism \(T\) (a coarse-graining of the sample space, including sufficiency as a special case) means

\[
g_{T_* p}(T_* u, T_* v) = g_p(u, v),
\]

so distances between distributions are preserved under statistically lossless data processing.

This uniqueness is the methodological justification for everything that follows.

A computational remark: for a model with \(d\) parameters, the empirical Fisher \(\hat{g}_{ij} = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{k} \partial_i \ell(x_k; \theta)\,\partial_j \ell(x_k; \theta)\) estimates \(g_{ij}\) consistently.

### 4.2 Amari's Dual Affine Connections: the e- and m-Connections

A Riemannian metric determines distances and angles but not, by itself, the notion of a "straight line": that requires an **affine connection** \(\nabla\), which specifies how tangent vectors are transported.

\[
X\, g(Y, Z) = g(\nabla_X Y, Z) + g(Y, \nabla^*_X Z), \qquad \forall X, Y, Z.
\]

When \(\nabla^* = \nabla\), duality collapses to metric compatibility and \(\nabla\) is Levi-Civita; in general the two connections share the same metric while disagreeing about parallel transport.

The canonical one-parameter family arises from the **α-divergence**

\[
D^{(\alpha)}(p \| q) = \frac{4}{1-\alpha^2}\left(1 - \int p^{(1-\alpha)/2} q^{(1+\alpha)/2}\, d\mu\right), \qquad \alpha \neq \pm 1,
\]

with the limits \(\alpha \to \pm 1\) recovering the two KL orientations.

\[
\Gamma^{(\alpha)}_{ij,k} = E_\theta\left[\left(\partial_i \partial_j \ell + \tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\, \partial_i \ell\, \partial_j \ell\right) \partial_k \ell\right],
\]

and the fundamental duality \(\nabla^{(\alpha)}\) is dual to \(\nabla^{(-\alpha)}\): indeed, \(\partial_k g_{ij} = \Gamma^{(\alpha)}_{ki,j} + \Gamma^{(-\alpha)}_{kj,i}\), so the \(\alpha = 0\) case recovers the Levi-Civita connection as the self-dual member [1], [4].

| α | Connection | ∇-affine coordinates | Models flat in this geometry |
|---|-----------|----------------------|------------------------------|
| \(+1\) | e-connection \(\nabla^{(e)}\) | natural parameters \(\theta\) | exponential families |
| \(-1\) | m-connection \(\nabla^{(m)}\) | expectation parameters \(\eta\) | mixture families |
| \(0\) | Levi-Civita \(\nabla^{(0)}\) | (none in general) | ordinary Riemannian geometry |

The **exponential connection** is so named because its geodesics are exponential interpolations: for \(p, q \in S\), the e-geodesic is \(\log r_t = (1-t)\log p + t \log q - \psi(t)\), i.e., a one-dimensional *exponential family* through \(p\) and \(q\).

Curvature is no longer a single tensor but a pair: \(\nabla^{(\alpha)}\) is generally curved even when \(\nabla^{(-\alpha)}\) is flat.

### 4.3 Dually Flat Manifolds, Bregman Divergences, and Legendre Duality

A **dually flat manifold** is a triple \((M, g, \nabla)\) where both \(\nabla\) and its dual \(\nabla^*\) are flat (zero curvature and torsion).

1. There exists a strictly convex **potential function** \(\psi(\theta)\) such that \(g_{ij} = \partial_i \partial_j \psi(\theta)\) — the metric is the Hessian of \(\psi\), making \(M\) a **Hessian manifold**.
2. The dual coordinates are given by the gradient map \(\eta_i = \partial_i \psi(\theta)\), which is a diffeomorphism by strict convexity.
3. The **Legendre transform** \(\varphi(\eta) = \sup_\theta\, \{\theta \cdot \eta - \psi(\theta)\}\) is the dual potential, satisfying \(g^{ij} = \partial^i \partial^j \varphi(\eta)\) and \(\theta^i = \partial^i \varphi(\eta)\).

To this structure belongs a **canonical divergence**, the **Bregman divergence** of \(\psi\):

\[
D_\psi(p \| q) = \psi(\theta_p) + \varphi(\eta_q) - \theta_p \cdot \eta_q.
\]

Although not symmetric, \(D_\psi\) satisfies the generalized Pythagorean theorem, the geometric centerpiece of the theory:

> **Theorem (Generalized Pythagorean theorem):** Let \(p, q, r\) be points of a dually flat manifold. If the m-geodesic from \(p\) to \(q\) is orthogonal at \(q\) to the e-geodesic from \(q\) to \(r\), then
>
> \[D_\psi(p \| r) = D_\psi(p \| q) + D_\psi(q \| r).\]
>
> The dual statement holds with e- and m-geodesics interchanged and the divergence arguments swapped [1], [4].

The orthogonality is measured with the Fisher metric, and the proof (sketched in Section 5) is a direct computation in the dual affine coordinates.

- the **EM algorithm**, viewed as alternating e- and m-projections minimizing KL divergence [6];
- **alternating minimization** procedures of Csiszár–Tusnády type, including belief propagation and the Ying–Yang machine [6];
- **maximum likelihood estimation** in exponential families, where the MLE is the m-projection of the empirical distribution onto the model.

In each case, what appears algorithmically as a clever iteration is geometrically a sequence of orthogonal projections in a dually flat space.

### 4.4 Exponential Families as the Canonical Dually Flat Models

An **exponential family** in natural form is

\[
p(x; \theta) = \exp\big(\theta \cdot x - \psi(\theta)\big)\, d\mu(x),
\]

where \(\theta\) are the **natural parameters**, \(x\) the sufficient statistics, and the **cumulant (log-partition) function** \(\psi(\theta) = \log \int e^{\theta \cdot x} d\mu(x)\) is strictly convex on its natural parameter space.

- the Fisher metric \(g_{ij}(\theta) = \partial_i \partial_j \psi(\theta)\) — the Hessian of the cumulant;
- the e-connection coefficients vanishing in \(\theta\)-coordinates (e-flatness) and the m-connection coefficients vanishing in \(\eta\)-coordinates (m-flatness);
- the KL divergence between two members \(p_{\theta}\) and \(p_{\theta'}\) as exactly the Bregman divergence of \(\psi\):

\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_\theta \| p_{\theta'}) = \psi(\theta') - \psi(\theta) - \nabla\psi(\theta)\cdot(\theta' - \theta) = D_\psi(\theta' \| \theta).
\]

Thus exponential families are the prototypical dually flat manifolds, and every dually flat statistical manifold is locally of this form [1], [4].

Two caveats enrich the picture.

### 4.5 The Natural Gradient: Steepest Descent in Distribution Space

We now turn to optimization.

\[
\tilde{\nabla} L(\theta) := g(\theta)^{-1}\, \nabla L(\theta)
\]

is instead the direction of steepest descent under the *Fisher* metric.

1. **Trust-region form.** The natural gradient solves \(\lim_{\epsilon \to 0} \frac{1}{\epsilon} \arg\min_{\{\delta : D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\theta+\delta} \| p_\theta) \le \epsilon^2/2\}} L(\theta + \delta)\): it is the limit of constrained steepest descent inside a KL-divergence ball, i.e., a trust region in *distribution* space rather than parameter space.
2. **Riemannian gradient.** \(\tilde{\nabla} L\) is the gradient of \(L\) with respect to the Riemannian structure \((S, g)\); it is the unique vector field satisfying \(dL(v) = g(\tilde{\nabla}L, v)\) for all tangent vectors \(v\).
3. **Fisher efficiency.** For maximum likelihood estimation with \(L\) the negative log-likelihood, stochastic natural gradient descent is asymptotically Fisher efficient: its covariance attains the Cramér–Rao bound [3].

The decisive property is **reparameterization invariance**.

Amari proved that natural gradient learning escapes the *plateaus* that plague neural network training — flat regions of the loss surface where the Euclidean gradient nearly vanishes but the distributional geometry still provides a meaningful direction — and that the adaptive (stochastic) natural gradient is Fisher efficient [3].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

This section collects the key proofs and summarizes the empirical evidence for the geometric program in machine learning.

**Proof sketch: generalized Pythagorean theorem.** In dual affine coordinates, write the Bregman divergence as \(D_\psi(p\|q) = \psi(\theta_p) + \varphi(\eta_q) - \theta_p \cdot \eta_q\). Suppose the m-geodesic \(pq\) is orthogonal to the e-geodesic \(qr\) at \(q\). The m-geodesic is linear in \(\eta\)-coordinates and the e-geodesic linear in \(\theta\)-coordinates, so the tangent of \(pq\) at \(q\) is \(\dot{\eta} = \eta_p - \eta_q\) and that of \(qr\) is \(\dot{\theta} = \theta_r - \theta_q\). Orthogonality gives \(g^{ij}\dot{\eta}_i \dot{\theta}_j = (\eta_p - \eta_q)\cdot(\theta_r - \theta_q) = 0\) (using \(g^{ij}\) to contract). Expanding \(D_\psi(p\|r) - D_\psi(p\|q) - D_\psi(q\|r)\) and using the Legendre relations \(\eta = \nabla\psi(\theta)\), the cross terms cancel precisely under this orthogonality condition, leaving zero [1], [4]. ∎

**Proof sketch: reparametrization invariance of the natural gradient.** Under \(\theta = \phi(\xi)\) with invertible Jacobian \(J^i_a = \partial \theta^i / \partial \xi^a\), the chain rule gives \(\partial_a = J^i_a \partial_i\). The score transforms as a covector, whence \(g_{ab}(\xi) = J^i_a J^j_b g_{ij}(\theta)\), i.e., \(G_\xi = J^\top G_\theta J\). Since \(\nabla_\xi L = J^\top \nabla_\theta L\),

\[
\tilde{\nabla}_\xi L = (J^\top G_\theta J)^{-1} J^\top \nabla_\theta L = J^{-1} G_\theta^{-1} \nabla_\theta L = J^{-1} \tilde{\nabla}_\theta L,
\]

which is exactly how a *contravariant* vector (a true direction) transforms — the pushed-forward trajectory on the manifold is parametrization-independent [3].

**Empirical findings.** The theory translates into practice along three lines:

1. *Plateau escape and efficiency.* Amari's analysis and simulations showed natural gradient learning escaping plateaus orders of magnitude faster than ordinary gradient descent in multilayer perceptrons, with asymptotic Fisher efficiency in online learning [3].
2. *Deep learning and curvature structure.* Amari, Karakida, and Oizumi analyzed the FIM of random deep networks via mean-field theory, proving that off-diagonal blocks of \(G\) between different layers are \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\) in width \(n\) and hence negligible, so \(G\) is well approximated by a layer-wise block-diagonal matrix — and further, by unit-wise diagonal blocks. This provides the theoretical justification for the unit-wise natural gradient of Ollivier and the Kronecker-factored (K-FAC) approximations that factor each layer block as a Kronecker product of activation and pre-activation statistics [5]. A survey of these information-geometric optimization methods and their applications to variational inference, belief propagation, and EM is given in [6].
3. *Variational inference and reinforcement learning.* Natural gradients in the expectation parameters of exponential-family variational posteriors underlie stochastic variational inference; the EM algorithm's alternating projections are exactly e/m-projections [6]; and in reinforcement learning, the natural policy gradient and its trust-region successors constrain updates by KL divergence on the policy manifold — the trust-region characterization of Section 4.5 made algorithmic.

The following Python snippet demonstrates the core computation — the natural gradient for a one-dimensional Gaussian exponential family, where the Fisher metric is the Hessian of the cumulant function:

```python
import numpy as np

def psi(theta):
    """Cumulant function of N(mu, sigma^2) in natural params
    theta = (mu/sigma^2, -1/(2*sigma^2))."""
    t1, t2 = theta
    return -t1**2 / (4.0 * t2) - 0.5 * np.log(-2.0 * t2)

def fisher_metric(theta, h=1e-6):
    """Fisher information = Hessian of psi (exact for exponential families)."""
    G = np.zeros((2, 2))
    e = np.eye(2)
    for i in range(2):
        for j in range(2):
            G[i, j] = (psi(theta + e[i]*h + e[j]*h) - psi(theta + e[i]*h - e[j]*h)
                       - psi(theta - e[i]*h + e[j]*h) + psi(theta - e[i]*h - e[j]*h)) / (4*h**2)
    return G

def natural_gradient(theta, grad, damping=1e-8):
    """Steepest descent direction in distribution space: G^{-1} grad."""
    G = fisher_metric(theta)
    return np.linalg.solve(G + damping * np.eye(2), grad)

theta = np.array([1.0, -0.5])          # N(mu=1, sigma^2=1)
grad = np.array([0.3, -0.2])           # Euclidean gradient of some loss
print("Euclidean step dir :", grad)
print("Natural step dir   :", natural_gradient(theta, grad))
print("Fisher metric      :\n", fisher_metric(theta))
```

For exponential families the identity \(G = \nabla^2 \psi\) makes the natural gradient exact and cheap; it is the general case — where \(G\) must be estimated and inverted — that motivates the approximations of the next section's discussion.

## 6 Limitations

The geometric program is powerful but not without significant limitations:

1. **Computational cost.** Forming the \(d \times d\) Fisher matrix costs \(O(d^2)\) and inverting it \(O(d^3)\), infeasible for modern models with \(d \sim 10^6\)–\(10^{12}\). Practical methods therefore rely on structured approximations — diagonal, block-diagonal, Kronecker-factored, or low-rank — each of which sacrifices exact reparametrization invariance and Fisher efficiency [5], [6].
2. **Singular models.** The Fisher metric degenerates where the parametrization is not identifiable (overparametrized neural networks, mixture models at boundary points), and the manifold develops singularities where the Riemannian framework breaks down. Singular learning theory extends the analysis, but the clean dually flat picture does not survive.
3. **Empirical versus true Fisher.** Many implementations substitute the empirical Fisher (outer products of observed gradients) for the true Fisher (expectation under the model). The empirical Fisher is not the Riemannian metric of the statistical manifold, and its descent direction lacks the invariance and efficiency guarantees; in some settings it behaves markedly differently [6].
4. **Non-flatness of general models.** Dually flat geometry is exact only for exponential and mixture families. Real models — deep networks, curved exponential families — have nonzero α-curvature, so the Pythagorean and projection theorems hold only approximately, and the elegance of exact alternating projections is lost [1].
5. **Locality.** The natural gradient is steepest descent only *infinitesimally*; finite steps along the natural gradient do not follow Fisher geodesics, and the trust-region interpretation is a first-order approximation. Second-order effects (curvature of the loss, not just of the model) are ignored, which is why the natural gradient coincides with Newton's method only for exponential families with log-likelihood loss.
6. **Divergence choice.** The α-family is canonical but not exhaustive; other divergences (e.g., optimal-transport distances) induce different geometries with different invariances, and the Fisher–Rao picture is one — albeit the statistically most distinguished — among several.

## 7 Conclusion

Information geometry replaces the arbitrary Euclidean geometry of parameter vectors with a geometry intrinsic to probability distributions: the Fisher–Rao metric, unique among Riemannian metrics in its invariance under sufficient statistics, together with Amari's dual affine connections. On dually flat manifolds — canonically, the exponential families — this structure crystallizes into Legendre duality between natural and expectation parameters, Bregman divergences, and generalized Pythagorean and projection theorems that unify maximum likelihood estimation, the EM algorithm, and alternating minimization as sequences of orthogonal projections. The natural gradient, as steepest descent in distribution space, inherits reparametrization invariance and Fisher efficiency, and its structured approximations have become practical tools in variational inference, reinforcement learning, and deep learning optimization [1]–[6].

The program's lasting lesson is methodological: statistical algorithms should be designed on the *manifold of models*, not in the *coordinates of parameters*. Wherever a learning method depends on a parametrization, a geometric correction — whether the full natural gradient or a Kronecker-factored approximation — removes the arbitrariness and, frequently, improves performance. The open frontier lies in extending the flat, finite-dimensional picture to singular models, infinite-dimensional function spaces, and the interaction of model geometry with loss curvature, where the classical theorems hold only approximately and new geometric ideas are needed.
