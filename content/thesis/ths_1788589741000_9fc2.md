# Sparse Mixture-of-Experts at Trillion-Parameter Scale: Top-$k$ Gating, Expert Parallelism, Load-Balancing Auxiliary Losses, and Capacity Factors from GShard to Switch Transformers

## Abstract

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures decouple model capacity from per-token computation by routing each token to a small subset of expert feed-forward networks, enabling trillion-parameter models at near-constant floating-point cost. This thesis presents a unified treatment of the engineering principles that made such scale practical. We formalize top-$k$ gating, from the noisy top-2 router of Shazeer et al. [1] through GShard's group-wise top-2 scheme [2] to the top-1 Switch layer [3] and its successors; we derive the communication topology of expert parallelism, quantifying the all-to-all dispatch/combine cost that dominates distributed training; we analyze load-balancing auxiliary losses and prove that the Switch objective is minimized exactly at uniform routing; and we characterize capacity factors and their token-dropping semantics. Drawing on GShard, Switch Transformers, ST-MoE [4], BASE layers [5], GLaM [6], DeepSeek-V3 [7], and expert-choice routing [8], we distill the design trajectory and empirical scaling evidence, then identify open problems in routing stability, inference efficiency, and expert specialization.

## 1. Introduction

The dominant empirical regularity of deep learning is the *scaling law*: test loss falls as a power law in parameters, data, and compute [6]. The naive reading prescribes ever-larger **dense** models in which every parameter participates in every forward pass — coupling *capacity* (learnable parameters) to *cost* (FLOPs per token). At the trillion-parameter frontier this coupling is economically punishing: a dense 1.6T-parameter model needs $3 	imes 10^{12}$ multiply-adds per generated token, a regime only the largest labs can afford.

*Conditional computation* breaks the coupling. Dating to the adaptive mixtures of experts of Jacobs et al. (1991), the idea is that a model holds a vast inventory of specialized sub-networks ("experts") while each input activates only a sparse subset: with $E$ experts and $k \ll E$ consulted per token, capacity grows with $E$ while per-token FLOPs grow only with $k$. This is the architectural bet of the **sparsely-gated Mixture-of-Experts** lineage:

1. **Shazeer et al. (2017)** [1] demonstrated a 137B-parameter LSTM whose MoE layer used noisy top-$k$ gating, reaching state of the art on the One Billion Word benchmark at a fraction of dense compute.
2. **GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2020)** [2] ported MoE into the Transformer with position-wise expert FFN layers and automatic sharding (XLA SPMD), scaling multilingual NMT beyond **600 billion parameters**.
3. **Switch Transformers (Fedus, Zoph & Shazeer, 2021/2022)** [3] simplified routing to **top-1**, introduced a differentiable load-balancing loss and the expert *capacity factor*, trained in bfloat16, and scaled to **1.6 trillion parameters** with up to $7\times$ pretraining speedups over dense T5 baselines.

This thesis argues that four mechanisms — **top-$k$ gating**, **expert parallelism**, **load-balancing auxiliary losses**, and **capacity factors** — jointly constitute the engineering theory of large-scale sparse models. Each is necessary; none is sufficient alone: gating without load balancing collapses to a dense sub-model, parallelism without capacity bounds produces unbounded communication skew, and capacity without an auxiliary loss starves experts of training signal.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 From adaptive mixtures to sparsely-gated layers

Mixture-of-experts predates deep learning's modern era. Jacobs et al. (1991) trained competing expert networks with a gating network performing soft competition, but *dense* gating meant every expert processed every input — capacity without sparsity. The decisive step was **sparsity in the gate itself**. Shazeer et al. [1] introduced a sparsely-gated MoE layer for LSTMs:

$$y = \sum_{i=1}^{n} G(x)_i \, E_i(x), \qquad G(x) = \mathrm{softmax}\big(\mathrm{KeepTopK}(H(x), k)\big),$$

where $H(x)_i = (x \cdot W_g)_i + \epsilon \cdot \mathrm{Softplus}ig((x \cdot W_n)_iig)$ injects tunable Gaussian noise for exploration and load smoothing. With $n = 4096$ experts and $k = 4$, their 137B-parameter model activated a tiny fraction of parameters per token, regularized by *importance* and *load* auxiliary losses (coefficients of variation of gate mass and assignment counts) counteracting routing collapse (§4.3).

### 2.2 The Transformer MoE era

GShard [2] replaced every other Transformer feed-forward block with a position-wise MoE layer of $E$ experts, kept top-2 gating, and solved the systems problem: XLA's SPMD partitioner automatically sharded experts across up to 2048 TPU cores, with einsum annotations expressing the dispatch and combine as distributed tensor contractions. Switch Transformers [3] then asked whether $k=2$ was necessary at all, answering with the **Switch layer** ($k=1$), halving gating compute and communication and simplifying the capacity arithmetic.

Subsequent work explored four axes:

- **Routing alternatives.** BASE layers [5] replaced the learned gate with a per-batch *balanced assignment* solved by the auction algorithm, eliminating the auxiliary loss. Hash layers used a fixed hash function instead of learning. **Expert-choice routing** [8] inverted the assignment — each expert selects its top-$c$ tokens — guaranteeing perfect balance at the price of variable per-token compute.
- **Scale demonstrations.** GLaM [6] trained a 1.2T-parameter MoE (64 experts, top-2) outperforming GPT-3 at roughly one-third the FLOPs per token. ST-MoE [4] distilled design rules (smaller experts, 32 experts, router z-loss, capacity factor 1.25) into a 269B/32B-active family.
- **Fine-grained and shared experts.** DeepSeek-V3 [7] used finer expert granularity plus always-on *shared* experts, reaching 671B total / 37B active parameters with dropless device-limited routing.
- **Open hybrids.** Mixtral 8×7B popularized top-2 MoE for open models, confirming the recipe transfers to the 10–50B-active regime.

Table 1 summarizes the canonical configurations.

| Model | Total params | Experts / layer ($E$) | Top-$k$ | Capacity factor | Auxiliary loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shazeer et al. [1] | 137B | 4096 | 4 | — (none) | importance + load (CV) |
| GShard [2] | 600B | 128–512 | 2 | 2.0 (train) | noise + CV-style |
| Switch-T [3] | 1.6T | 128–2048 | **1** | 1.0–1.25 | $L = \alpha N \sum f_i p_i$, $\alpha=0.01$ |
| GLaM [6] | 1.2T | 64 | 2 | 2.0 | Switch-style |
| ST-MoE [4] | 269B | 32 | 2 | 1.25 | Switch-style + router z-loss |
| DeepSeek-V3 [7] | 671B | 256 (+1 shared) | 8 | dropless | Switch-style + bias-free balance |

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Formal setup

Consider a Transformer block whose position-wise FFN $\mathrm{FFN}: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d$ is replaced by an MoE layer of $E$ experts $\{E_1, \dots, E_E\}$, each an FFN of hidden dimension $d_{\mathrm{ff}}$. For token representation $x_s \in \mathbb{R}^d$ at position $s$ [2]:

$$\mathcal{G}_{s,E} = \mathrm{GATE}(x_s), \qquad \mathrm{FFN}_e(x_s) = W^o_e \cdot \mathrm{ReLU}(W^i_e \cdot x_s), \qquad y_s = \sum_{e=1}^{E} \mathcal{G}_{s,e} \cdot \mathrm{FFN}_e(x_s).$$

The gate is a sparse map $\mathcal{G}: \mathbb{R}^d \to \Delta^{E-1}$ with $\|\mathcal{G}(x)\|_0 \le k$; the canonical noisy top-$k$ construction [1][3]:

```python
def gate(x, W_g, W_noise, k, training=True):
    # x: [d], W_g: [d, E], W_noise: [d, E]
    logits = x @ W_g
    if training:
        noise = torch.randn(E) * F.softplus(x @ W_noise)
        logits = logits + noise
    topk_vals, topk_idx = torch.topk(logits, k)
    mask = torch.full_like(logits, float('-inf'))
    mask[topk_idx] = topk_vals          # KeepTopK: -inf elsewhere
    return torch.softmax(mask, dim=-1)   # sparse: only k nonzeros
```

The output is a *convex combination of at most $k$ expert evaluations* — the remaining experts contribute exactly zero and need not be computed.

### 3.2 Complexity analysis

Let a dense FFN cost $C_{\mathrm{ffn}} \approx 2 \cdot d \cdot d_{\mathrm{ff}}$ multiply-adds per token (two matmuls, ignoring activations). Then:

- **Dense layer:** $C_{\mathrm{dense}} = C_{\mathrm{ffn}}$ per token; total parameters $P_{\mathrm{dense}} \propto d \cdot d_{\mathrm{ff}}$.
- **MoE layer:** $C_{\mathrm{moe}} = k \cdot C_{\mathrm{ffn}} + C_{\mathrm{gate}}$, where $C_{\mathrm{gate}} \approx 2 \cdot d \cdot E$ is the gating matmul. Total parameters $P_{\mathrm{moe}} = E \cdot P_{\mathrm{dense}}$.

The ratio $rac{C_{\mathrm{moe}}}{C_{\mathrm{dense}}} pprox k + rac{E \cdot d}{d_{\mathrm{ff}}}$ shows **per-token FLOPs are essentially independent of $E$**: capacity scales multiplicatively in $E$ while cost scales additively in $k$ — the entire economic argument for sparsity, and why Switch could contemplate 1.6T parameters on a dense model's hardware budget [3].

Memory, however, does scale with $E$: all $E \cdot P_{\mathrm{dense}}$ parameters must be *stored* somewhere. This forces **expert parallelism** (§4.2): experts are sharded across devices, each device holding $E/D$ experts for $D$ devices, while non-expert parameters (attention, embeddings, layer norms) are replicated.

## 4. Deep Technical Dive

### 4.1 Top-$k$ gating: from noisy top-2 to the Switch layer

**Noisy top-$k** [1]. The noise serves two purposes: (i) *exploration* — under-selected experts still receive tokens and gradient; (ii) *differentiability of expected load* — the selection probability $P(x,i)$, expressed via the Gaussian CDF $\Phi$ of the noise margin, is smooth in the gating weights even though hard top-$k$ assignment is not.

**GShard's group-wise top-2** [2] partitions tokens into groups and applies top-2 gating in vectorized einsum form. The $k=2$ choice was inherited from [1] on the hypothesis that the gate needed at least two experts to *learn* — one choice provides no contrastive signal about the road not taken.

**The Switch simplification ($k=1$)** [3] showed top-1 matches or exceeds top-2 quality while halving expert compute and all-to-all communication:

$$y = p_{i^*} \, E_{i^*}(x), \qquad i^* = \arg\max_i \, \mathrm{softmax}(x W_r)_i,$$

The "lost contrastive signal" concern proved unfounded — with hundreds of experts and large batches, the gate receives ample gradient through the differentiable $p_{i^*}$.

> **Claim (sparse scaling):** At fixed per-token budget, increasing $E$ with $k$ fixed strictly increases capacity $P_{\mathrm{moe}} = E \cdot P_{\mathrm{dense}}$ without increasing $C_{\mathrm{moe}}$ — so sparse scaling dominates dense scaling at fixed FLOPs, *provided* routing quality does not degrade with $E$. Preserving routing quality as $E 	o 2048$ and beyond is the research program.

**Expert-choice routing** [8] offers the dual: each expert chooses its top-$c$ tokens, making balance exact by construction but per-token compute *variable* — complicating latency-sensitive inference, which is why token-choice remains dominant.

### 4.2 Expert parallelism and communication topology

With $E$ experts sharded over $D$ devices, a token routed to a remote expert must physically move. Each MoE layer therefore executes **dispatch → compute → combine** [2][3]:

1. **Dispatch:** an all-to-all sends each token to the device(s) owning its selected expert(s), packed into fixed-size per-expert *capacity buffers* (§4.4).
2. **Compute:** each device evaluates its local experts on the received buffer — a purely local batched matmul.
3. **Combine:** a second all-to-all returns outputs to the originating devices, where they are gate-weighted into the residual stream.

In GShard's SPMD formulation [2], dispatch and combine are `einsum` operations with sharding annotations (groups $G$, positions $S$, model dim $M$, capacity $C$, experts $E$ sharded over the mesh), which XLA lowers to all-to-all collectives automatically — the titular "automatic sharding."

**Communication cost.** Each all-to-all moves $O(k \cdot T \cdot d)$ elements per MoE layer per direction — *independent of $E$*, so expert count scales "for free" in communication too. The bad news is the constant: at $T = 2^{21}$, $d = 8192$, bf16, each direction moves tens of GB per layer per step, making interconnect bandwidth (not FLOPs) the binding constraint and motivating Switch's top-1 halving of this term [3].

**Skew**: without load balancing, the all-to-all becomes straggler-bound. Fixed capacity buffers convert skew into *dropping* (§4.4), trading statistical efficiency for determinism.

### 4.3 Load-balancing auxiliary losses

**The collapse dynamic.** Unconstrained gates exhibit a rich-get-richer loop: slightly preferred experts receive more tokens, more gradient steps, and become better still. Training collapses to a handful of active experts — trillion-parameter *storage* with billion-parameter *behavior* [1][3].

**The Switch auxiliary loss.** Fedus et al. [3] define, over a batch of $T$ tokens and $N$ experts:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{balance}} = \alpha \cdot N \cdot \sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i \cdot p_i,$$

$$f_i = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbf{1}\{\arg\max_j \, g(x_t)_j = i\}, \qquad p_i = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} g(x_t)_i,$$

The asymmetry is deliberate: $f_i$ is non-differentiable but $p_i$ is, so gradients flow through $p_i$ to the router. Total loss: $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LM}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{balance}}$, $lpha = 0.01$.

> **Theorem (uniformity minimizer):** $\sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i p_i$ subject to $\sum_i f_i = \sum_i p_i = 1$, $f_i, p_i \ge 0$, attains its global minimum $1/N$ uniquely at $f_i = p_i = 1/N$ for all $i$.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Lagrange multipliers on $L = \sum f_i p_i - \lambda(\sum f_i - 1) - \mu(\sum p_i - 1)$ give $p_i = \lambda$, $f_i = \mu$ for all $i$ at any interior stationary point — i.e., uniform $f$ and uniform $p$ — and boundary points yield strictly larger values. ∎
>
> In words: the auxiliary loss is a *differentiable surrogate for distance from uniform routing*.

**Taxonomy of balancing objectives** (Table 2):

| Objective | Formula | Differentiable? | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Importance CV [1] | $\mathrm{CV}(\sum_t g(x_t)_i)^2$ | Yes | Penalizes gate-mass concentration |
| Load CV [1] | $\mathrm{CV}(\sum_t P(x_t, i))^2$ | Yes (via noise CDF) | Penalizes assignment-count concentration |
| Switch [3] | $\alpha N \sum_i f_i p_i$ | Yes (via $p_i$) | Minimized at uniform; $\alpha = 0.01$ |
| Router z-loss [4] | $\frac{1}{T}\sum_t \big(\log \sum_i e^{x_t \cdot W_r}\big)^2$ | Yes | Penalizes large logits; stabilizes bf16 |
| None (BASE [5]) | balanced assignment via auction | N/A | Exact balance, no auxiliary term |

ST-MoE [4] added the **router z-loss** against unbounded gating logits, which saturate softmax and destabilize low-precision training. Penalizing the squared log-partition function keeps logits in a healthy dynamic range — a small term with outsized stability benefits.

### 4.4 Capacity factors and token dropping

Even with a balancing loss, instantaneous routing is never perfectly uniform, so expert parallelism provisions **fixed-size per-expert buffers**. The *expert capacity* is [3]:

$$C = \left\lceil \frac{T}{N} \times \text{capacity\_factor} \right\rceil,$$

i.e., each expert processes at most $C$ tokens per batch — the uniform share $T/N$ inflated by the capacity factor (typically $1.0$–$2.0$). Tokens routed to a full buffer are **dropped**: they skip the MoE layer and flow through the residual connection unchanged. Dropping is the systems price of determinism — buffer sizes are static-shape friendly and all-to-all volume is exactly $N \cdot C \cdot d$ regardless of routing skew.

The capacity factor mediates a three-way trade-off: too low ($1.0$) maximizes efficiency but drops tokens, creating train/inference mismatch; too high ($2.0+$) wastes memory bandwidth on half-empty buffers. Switch [3] chose $1.25$ for training and $2.0$ for evaluation; ST-MoE [4] standardized on $1.25$.

GShard [2] additionally used *group-wise* gating to bound dispatch-einsum memory. Modern dropless designs [7] replace dropping with *device-limited routing* (each token's experts constrained to $M$ devices), recovering dropped tokens' signal at the cost of more complex load management.

### 4.5 The design trajectory: GShard → Switch → ST-MoE → modern practice

The empirical arc is *progressive simplification plus targeted stabilization*:

1. **GShard** proved the systems recipe (SPMD sharding, position-wise MoE, 600B params) with top-2 routing inherited from [1].
2. **Switch** showed top-1 suffices, introduced the $f_i p_i$ loss and capacity-factor discipline, stabilized bfloat16 training, and reached 1.6T parameters with $7	imes$ pretraining speedups [3].
3. **ST-MoE** [4] converted folklore into prescriptions — 32 experts, *smaller* experts, expert dropout, router z-loss — its 269B (32B-active) model matched dense models trained with an order of magnitude more FLOPs.
4. **DeepSeek-V3** [7] pushed fine-grained segmentation (256 experts, top-8, plus shared experts) with bias-only balancing and no auxiliary loss, showing even the auxiliary-loss paradigm is optional given the right routing protocol.

The through-line: every generation *removes* a mechanism the previous one thought essential (top-2 → top-1; learned balance → auction/hash; auxiliary loss → bias-only) while *adding* exactly one stabilization term the previous one lacked.

### 5.2 Formal: FLOPs-independence of expert count

> **Proposition:** Per-token FLOPs satisfy $C_{\mathrm{moe}}(E) = k \cdot C_{\mathrm{ffn}} + O(E \cdot d)$ — expert count drops out of the cost as $E$ grows. Only the $k$ selected experts are evaluated; the gating matmul ($2Ed$ FLOPs) is a small fraction of one expert's cost whenever $d_{\mathrm{ff}} \gg E/k$. Communication (§4.2) is likewise $O(kTd)$ per layer, independent of $E$. ∎

The corollary is the *sparse scaling law* observed across [2][3][4][6]: at fixed FLOPs, loss decreases as $E$ grows until routing quality degrades — the regime the auxiliary losses of §4.3 postpone.

### 5.3 Why top-1 works: a gradient accounting

With top-1, the gate's gradient flows only through $p_{i^*}$ — seemingly impoverished versus top-2's relative preference signal. The resolution [3] is statistical: across $T \sim 10^6$ tokens and $E \sim 10^3$ experts, the *distribution* of $p_{i^*}$ values across the batch supplies the contrastive signal that top-2 provided within a token. Top-1 trades per-token gradient richness for doubled expert count at fixed compute.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Residual routing collapse.** The $f_i p_i$ loss encourages *uniformity*, not *specialization*: nothing requires experts to learn distinct functions, and probing studies find substantial redundancy. True specialization may need diversity-promoting objectives beyond load balance.
2. **Token dropping as information loss.** At capacity factor $1.0$, drop rates of several percent are common; the resulting train/eval mismatch is poorly characterized.
3. **All-to-all latency walls.** Communication is $O(kTd)$ independent of $E$, but the *constant* dominates, so interconnect topology dictates feasible $(E, k, T)$.
4. **Inference economics.** Serving requires all $E$ experts in accelerator memory — 1.6T parameters at bf16 is 3.2 TB — making MoE inference a memory-capacity problem favoring offloading, expert pruning, or distillation.
5. **Fine-tuning instability.** Pretrained routers are brittle under distribution shift; fine-tuning often collapses routing or requires freezing the gate.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts turned the scaling laws from a counsel of despair — *bigger is better but unaffordable* — into an engineering discipline. The four mechanisms form a complete loop: **top-$k$ gating** defines the sparse computation; **expert parallelism** distributes it; **load-balancing auxiliary losses** keep the distribution honest, with the Switch objective minimized at uniform routing; and **capacity factors** convert statistical skew into systems determinism via token dropping. From GShard's 600B-parameter sharding demonstration [2] through Switch's top-1 simplification and trillion-parameter training [3] to ST-MoE's stabilization prescriptions [4] and DeepSeek-V3's fine-grained, dropless, bias-balanced design [7], each generation simplified what the last thought essential while adding exactly the stabilization the last one lacked. The trillion-parameter sparse model is no longer a demonstration; it is the baseline for the next scaling regime.
