# Differential Privacy at Scale: Rényi Accounting, the Moments Accountant, and DP-SGD

## Abstract

Training deep neural networks on sensitive data creates a fundamental tension between model utility and the confidentiality of individual training records. Differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD), introduced by Abadi et al. [1], resolved a long-standing obstacle by combining per-example gradient clipping with calibrated Gaussian noise, and—critically—by introducing the *moments accountant*, a mechanism-specific composition analysis that tracks the log-moment-generating function of the privacy-loss random variable across thousands of training steps. This thesis develops the full theoretical stack underlying modern private learning. We begin with pure and approximate differential privacy, the privacy-loss random variable, and classical composition theorems. We then present Rényi differential privacy (RDP) [2], prove its additive composition law, its closed-form Gaussian calibration, and its conversion back to $(\epsilon,\delta)$-DP, showing how RDP both generalizes and operationalizes the moments accountant. Next we derive privacy amplification by subsampling for the Poisson-sampled Gaussian mechanism, including the analytical bounds of Wang et al. [3] and the Gaussian-DP ($f$-DP) treatment of Dong, Roth, and Su [6], and connect these to privacy auditing, which empirically lower-bounds the guarantees of deployed training runs. The result is a complete, implementable account of how modern systems such as TensorFlow Privacy and Opacus compute privacy budgets, and of the limitations—utility gaps, auditing tightness, and distributional assumptions—that remain active research frontiers.

## 1 Introduction

*Differential privacy* (DP) provides the only known rigorous, composable framework for bounding what an adversary can infer about any single individual from the output of a randomized computation [8]. For machine learning, the framework's promise is compelling: a model trained with an $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP guarantee cannot—up to the quantified bound—reveal whether any particular record participated in training. Yet for years, deep learning seemed out of reach. Generic DP mechanisms for empirical risk minimization either required convexity assumptions, incurred enormous utility loss, or, most damagingly, accumulated privacy budget *linearly* in the number of gradient steps: thousands of iterations of stochastic gradient descent meant thousands of compositions, and classical composition theorems [8] yielded vacuous budgets on realistically sized datasets.

The breakthrough of Abadi et al. [1] at CCS 2016 rested on three interlocking ideas. First, *per-example gradient clipping* bounds the sensitivity of each training step: no single example can move the noisy gradient sum by more than the clipping norm $C$, regardless of pathological loss landscapes. Second, *Poisson subsampling* (each example included in a lot independently with probability $q = L/N$) amplifies privacy, because the adversary cannot even be certain a target record participated in a given step. Third, and most technically novel, the *moments accountant* abandons generic composition: rather than treating each step as an abstract $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP mechanism, it tracks the *privacy-loss random variable* (PLRV) of the concrete subsampled-Gaussian mechanism through its log-moment-generating function, obtaining exponentially tighter tail bounds via Chernoff-style arguments.

Subsequent work recast these insights in cleaner formalisms. Rényi differential privacy [2] defined privacy in terms of the Rényi divergence of order $\alpha$, recovering the moments accountant as an instance while supplying additive composition, closed-form Gaussian calibration, and a conversion rule back to standard DP. Wang et al. [3] derived the *analytical* moments accountant, giving closed-form subsampling amplification bounds under RDP. Dong, Roth, and Su [6] proposed $f$-DP and its single-parameter specialization Gaussian DP, which handles composition *losslessly* through a central limit theorem and imports subsampling amplification directly. Bu et al. [4] refined the DP-SGD analysis in this framework. In parallel, *privacy auditing* [7] emerged to measure what privacy was *actually* spent—an empirical lower bound complementary to the analytic upper bounds.

This thesis unifies these threads. Our contributions are expository but precise: a self-contained derivation of the DP-SGD privacy analysis from first principles; formal statements and proof sketches of the RDP composition, calibration, amplification, and conversion theorems; a comparison of the moments accountant, RDP, and GDP accounting families; a reference Python implementation of an RDP accountant; and a critical discussion of limitations. We assume familiarity with probability and convex analysis but define every privacy-theoretic object we use.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Differential privacy and the privacy-loss random variable

Let $\mathcal{X}$ be a data domain and $D, D' \in \mathcal{X}^n$ be *neighboring* datasets differing in a single record (add/remove adjacency; the remove/add convention matters for subsampling analyses). A randomized mechanism $\mathcal{M}: \mathcal{X}^n \to \mathcal{Y}$ satisfies *pure* $\epsilon$-DP if for all neighbors $D, D'$ and all measurable $S \subseteq \mathcal{Y}$,

$$\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \le e^{\epsilon}\, \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S].$$

*Approximate* $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP relaxes this by an additive term $\delta$:

$$\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \le e^{\epsilon}\, \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S] + \delta. \tag{1}$$

The additive $\delta$ is what makes the Gaussian mechanism—and therefore DP-SGD—expressible at all: the Gaussian's unbounded tails preclude a finite pure-$\epsilon$ bound, since density ratios diverge in the extremes [2]. Informally, $\delta$ permits the guarantee to fail with small probability, and $\delta$ is conventionally chosen cryptographically small, e.g. $\delta = 1/N^{1.1}$ or $\delta \ll 1/N$ for a dataset of size $N$.

The modern analytic workhorse is the *privacy-loss random variable*. For neighbors $D, D'$ and outcome $o \sim \mathcal{M}(D)$, define

$$L_{D,D'}(o) := \log \frac{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) = o]}{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') = o]}.$$

Then $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP is equivalent to a *tail bound* on $L$: for all neighbors,

$$\Pr_{o \sim \mathcal{M}(D)}[L_{D,D'}(o) > \epsilon] \le \delta,$$

together with a symmetric condition in the other direction (or, more carefully, the hockey-stick divergence bound $\mathbb{E}[\max(0, 1 - e^{\epsilon - L})] \le \delta$). Everything that follows—the moments accountant, RDP, $f$-DP—is a technology for controlling the *distribution* of $L$ under adaptive composition, where a naive bound on each step's worst case compounds catastrophically.

### 2.2 Classical composition

Running $k$ mechanisms sequentially, each $(\epsilon_i, \delta_i)$-DP, and releasing all outputs yields, by *basic composition*, $(\sum_i \epsilon_i, \sum_i \delta_i)$-DP [8]. This is tight in the worst case but far too pessimistic for $k \sim 10^4$ gradient steps. The *advanced composition* theorem [8] improves this to roughly $(\epsilon\sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')} + k\epsilon(e^{\epsilon}-1),\, k\delta + \delta')$-DP for homogeneous $\epsilon$-DP mechanisms—sublinear $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{k})$ growth in $\epsilon$, at the cost of a larger $\delta$. The *optimal* composition theorem of Kairouz, Oh, and Viswanath characterizes the exact Pareto frontier but is **#P-hard** to evaluate [8], making it a theoretical benchmark rather than a practical tool. The table below summarizes the landscape:

| Accounting method | Composition law (homogeneous, $k$ steps) | Needs per-step PLRV? | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basic composition | $(\,k\epsilon,\; k\delta\,)$ | No | Dwork et al. [8] |
| Advanced composition | $(\,\tilde{O}(\sqrt{k}\,\epsilon),\; \tilde{O}(k\delta)\,)$ | No | Dwork et al. [8] |
| Optimal composition | Exact, but #P-hard to compute | Yes | Kairouz et al. [8] |
| Moments accountant | Numerical, $\epsilon$ via $\min_\lambda$ | Yes (subsampled Gaussian) | Abadi et al. [1] |
| RDP | $(\alpha,\, \sum_i \epsilon_i(\alpha))$ additive | Divergence form | Mironov [2] |
| $f$-DP / GDP | $\mu = \sqrt{\sum_i \mu_i^2}$ | Trade-off function | Dong et al. [6] |

> **Theorem:** *(Advanced composition, informal.)* The $k$-fold adaptive composition of $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP mechanisms is $(\epsilon', k\delta + \delta')$-DP with $\epsilon' = \sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')}\,\epsilon + k\epsilon(e^{\epsilon} - 1)$ for $\epsilon \le 1$. The $\sqrt{k}$ scaling reflects a martingale concentration argument: privacy losses behave like a random walk with bounded increments, and Azuma's inequality converts the walk's variance into a tail bound.

The limitation shared by all *generic* composition theorems is that they treat each mechanism as a black box satisfying only (1). When the mechanism is known concretely—the subsampled Gaussian of DP-SGD—mechanism-specific analysis wins decisively, as we now show.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The DP-SGD algorithm

DP-SGD modifies minibatch SGD in exactly two places: per-example clipping and noise injection [1]. Let $\ell(\theta; x_i)$ be the loss on example $i$, $C > 0$ the clipping norm, $\sigma > 0$ the noise multiplier, and $q = L/N$ the sampling probability (Poisson sampling: each example joins lot $\mathcal{L}_t$ independently with probability $q$):

1. **Sample** a lot $\mathcal{L}_t$ by including each $i$ independently with probability $q$.
2. **Compute per-example gradients** $\mathbf{g}_t(x_i) = \nabla_\theta \ell(\theta_t; x_i)$ for $i \in \mathcal{L}_t$.
3. **Clip** each gradient: $\bar{\mathbf{g}}_t(x_i) = \mathbf{g}_t(x_i) / \max\!\left(1, \|\mathbf{g}_t(x_i)\|_2 / C\right)$, so $\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}_t\|_2 \le C$.
4. **Aggregate with noise:** $\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_t = \frac{1}{L}\left(\sum_{i \in \mathcal{L}_t} \bar{\mathbf{g}}_t(x_i) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 C^2 \mathbf{I})\right)$.
5. **Descend:** $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta_t \tilde{\mathbf{g}}_t$.

Clipping is the linchpin: without it, a single example's gradient could be arbitrarily large, making the $\ell_2$-*sensitivity* $\Delta_2 = \sup_{D \simeq D'} \|f(D) - f(D')\|_2$ unbounded and noise calibration impossible. With clipping, the sum's sensitivity is exactly $C$, so Gaussian noise of scale $\sigma C$ yields a per-step mechanism whose privacy is fully determined by $(q, \sigma)$.

### 3.2 The Gaussian mechanism

For a query $f$ with $\ell_2$-sensitivity $\Delta_2$, the Gaussian mechanism $\mathcal{M}(D) = f(D) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})$ satisfies $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP whenever

$$\sigma \ge \frac{\Delta_2 \sqrt{2 \ln(1.25/\delta)}}{\epsilon}, \qquad 0 < \epsilon < 1, \tag{2}$$

the classical calibration [8]. Equivalently—and far more useful for accounting—the Gaussian mechanism satisfies $\mu$-*Gaussian DP* with $\mu = \Delta_2 / \sigma$ [6], or $(\alpha, \alpha \Delta_2^2 / 2\sigma^2)$-RDP for every order $\alpha > 1$ [2]. The RDP form is what DP-SGD accountants evaluate millions of times: it is a one-line formula, yet it encodes the entire privacy profile of the Gaussian.

### 3.3 The moments accountant

Abadi et al. define, for a mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ and neighbors $D, D'$, the log-moment function

$$\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda; D, D') := \log \mathbb{E}_{o \sim \mathcal{M}(D)}\!\left[\exp\!\left(\lambda\, L_{D,D'}(o)\right)\right], \qquad \lambda > 0,$$

and the *moments accountant* $\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda) := \max_{D \simeq D'} \alpha_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda; D, D')$. Two properties make it an accountant:

- **Composability.** For adaptive composition $\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{M}_1, \dots, \mathcal{M}_k)$, $\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda) \le \sum_{i=1}^k \alpha_{\mathcal{M}_i}(\lambda)$ [1]. The proof is a change-of-measure argument: the PLRV of a composition is the *sum* of per-step privacy losses, and the MGF of a sum of (conditionally) independent terms factorizes.
- **Tail conversion.** By Markov's inequality applied to $\exp(\lambda L)$,
  $$\Pr[L > \epsilon] \le \frac{\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda L}]}{e^{\lambda \epsilon}} = \exp(\alpha(\lambda) - \lambda \epsilon),$$
  so $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP with $\delta = \min_\lambda \exp(\alpha(\lambda) - \lambda \epsilon)$, or equivalently $\epsilon = \min_\lambda (\alpha(\lambda) + \ln(1/\delta))/\lambda$ [1].

For the *subsampled* Gaussian (sampling probability $q$, noise multiplier $\sigma$), Abadi et al. prove the mechanism-specific bound, valid for $\lambda \le \sigma^2 \ln(1/(q\sigma))$:

$$\alpha(\lambda) \le \frac{q^2 \lambda(\lambda+1)}{(1-q)\sigma^2} + O\!\left(\frac{q^3 \lambda^3}{\sigma^3}\right). \tag{3}$$

The $q^2$ factor is privacy amplification made quantitative: each step costs roughly $q^2/\sigma^2$ rather than $1/\sigma^2$. The accountant is evaluated numerically over integer $\lambda \in \{1, \dots, 32\}$ (higher orders add little), minimizing over $\lambda$ at the end—this is precisely the algorithm shipped in TensorFlow Privacy's `compute_dp_sgd_privacy.py` [1] and inherited by Opacus.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Rényi differential privacy: definition and composition

The moments accountant's log-MGF is, up to a change of measure, the *Rényi divergence*. Mironov [2] elevated this observation into a definition. For distributions $P, Q$ and order $\alpha \in (1, \infty)$,

$$D_\alpha(P \,\|\, Q) := \frac{1}{\alpha - 1} \log \mathbb{E}_{x \sim Q}\!\left[\left(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right)^{\!\alpha}\right] = \frac{1}{\alpha - 1} \log \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}\!\left[\left(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right)^{\!\alpha - 1}\right].$$

> **Definition:** *(Rényi DP.)* $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $(\alpha, \epsilon)$-RDP if $D_\alpha(\mathcal{M}(D) \,\|\, \mathcal{M}(D')) \le \epsilon$ for all neighboring $D, D'$ [2].

The endpoints illuminate the definition: as $\alpha \to 1$, $D_\alpha$ tends to the KL divergence (a bound on the *mean* privacy loss); as $\alpha \to \infty$, it tends to the max-divergence $\sup_o \log(P(o)/Q(o))$, recovering *pure* $\epsilon$-DP. RDP therefore interpolates between average-case and worst-case privacy, parameterizing the whole tail of the PLRV by a *function* $\epsilon(\alpha)$—the "privacy curve" of the mechanism.

> **Theorem:** *(Adaptive composition of RDP [2].)* If $\mathcal{M}_1$ is $(\alpha, \epsilon_1)$-RDP and $\mathcal{M}_2$ (possibly chosen adaptively based on $\mathcal{M}_1$'s output) is $(\alpha, \epsilon_2)$-RDP, then the composition is $(\alpha, \epsilon_1 + \epsilon_2)$-RDP.
> *Proof sketch.* The joint Rényi divergence tensorizes: for product distributions, $D_\alpha(P_1 \otimes P_2 \,\|\, Q_1 \otimes Q_2) = D_\alpha(P_1\|Q_1) + D_\alpha(P_2\|Q_2)$, by direct algebra on the defining integral. Adaptive composition reduces to the non-adaptive case by conditioning on the first mechanism's output and using the data-processing inequality for Rényi divergence. ∎

Additivity at a *fixed* order $\alpha$ is what makes RDP a practical accountant: $T$ steps of the subsampled Gaussian each cost $\epsilon_{\text{step}}(\alpha)$, so the run costs $T \cdot \epsilon_{\text{step}}(\alpha)$, and one converts to $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP once, at the end.

### 4.2 Gaussian calibration, subsampling amplification, and conversion

Three closed-form results make RDP the engine of modern DP-SGD accounting [2, 3]:

1. **Gaussian mechanism.** The Gaussian with sensitivity $\Delta_2$ and noise $\sigma$ satisfies $(\alpha,\, \alpha \Delta_2^2 / 2\sigma^2)$-RDP for all $\alpha > 1$. The proof computes the Rényi divergence between $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ and $\mathcal{N}(\Delta, \sigma^2)$ in closed form—a one-line consequence of Gaussian MGFs. For DP-SGD with $\Delta_2 = C$ and noise $\sigma C$, the per-step cost is $\alpha / 2\sigma^2$ *before* subsampling.

2. **Amplification by subsampling.** If $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\alpha, \epsilon(\alpha))$-RDP, then Poisson subsampling at rate $q$ yields $(\alpha, \epsilon_q(\alpha))$-RDP with a bound that, for small $q$ and moderate $\alpha$, scales as $\epsilon_q(\alpha) \approx q^2 \cdot \frac{\alpha}{\text{(noise terms)}}$—the RDP analogue of (3). Wang et al. [3] derived *analytical* (closed-form, non-asymptotic) versions of these bounds, removing Abadi et al.'s restrictions on $\lambda$ and extending to sampling without replacement; their bounds are what Opacus evaluates.

3. **Conversion to $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP.** If $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\alpha, \epsilon)$-RDP, then it is $(\epsilon + \tfrac{\ln(1/\delta)}{\alpha - 1},\, \delta)$-DP for every $\delta \in (0,1)$ [2]. The proof is again Markov on $\exp((\alpha-1)L)$, since $(\alpha-1)D_\alpha = \log \mathbb{E}[e^{(\alpha-1)L}]$.

In practice one minimizes over a grid of orders $\alpha \in \{1.1, 1.5, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, \dots\}$:

$$\epsilon_{\text{DP}}(\delta) = \min_{\alpha > 1}\; T \cdot \epsilon_{\text{step}}(\alpha) + \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{\alpha - 1}.$$

The minimizing $\alpha^\star$ grows as the per-step budget shrinks: many tiny steps are best analyzed at large $\alpha$, where RDP approaches pure DP and the conversion penalty $\ln(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1)$ vanishes. This single optimization replaces the moments accountant's $\lambda$-grid and is numerically identical to it—RDP *is* the moments accountant, formalized [2, 3].

### 4.3 $f$-DP and Gaussian DP: the hypothesis-testing view

Dong, Roth, and Su [6] proposed $f$-DP, which reframes privacy as *hypothesis testing*: distinguishing $\mathcal{M}(D)$ from $\mathcal{M}(D')$ is at least as hard as distinguishing two fixed distributions $P, Q$. Formally, with $T(P,Q)(\alpha) = \inf_\phi \{\text{Type-II error} : \text{Type-I} \le \alpha\}$, $\mathcal{M}$ is $f$-DP if $T(\mathcal{M}(D), \mathcal{M}(D')) \ge f$ pointwise for all neighbors. The canonical single-parameter family is *Gaussian DP*: $\mathcal{M}$ is $\mu$-GDP if distinguishing its neighboring outputs is at least as hard as distinguishing $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ from $\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)$. Key facts [6, 4]:

- The Gaussian mechanism with sensitivity $\Delta_2$ and noise $\sigma$ is exactly $\mu$-GDP with $\mu = \Delta_2/\sigma$—no conversion slack.
- Composition is *lossless*: $\mu$-GDP composed with $\mu'$-GDP is $\sqrt{\mu^2 + \mu'^2}$-GDP, mirroring how independent Gaussians add in quadrature.
- A central limit theorem: under composition, *any* hypothesis-testing privacy notion converges to GDP—GDP is the focal point of the composition limit.
- Subsampling amplification imports directly, and Bu et al. [4] used this to give closed-form DP-SGD bounds that improve on the moments accountant both theoretically and empirically.

GDP's elegance comes at a cost: the framework is exact for Gaussian-like mechanisms but less turnkey for heterogeneous, non-Gaussian pipelines, where RDP's per-order grid remains the lingua franca of implementations.

### 4.4 Privacy auditing: measuring what was actually spent

Analytic accountants give *upper bounds* on privacy loss—what an adversary could at most learn. *Privacy auditing* inverts the question: can we exhibit an attack achieving privacy loss close to the bound, thereby *lower-bounding* the true $\epsilon$ [7]? The standard methodology plants *canaries*—crafted examples inserted into training—and measures a membership-inference adversary's advantage (true-positive minus false-positive rate), converting the advantage into an empirical $\epsilon_{\text{emp}}$ via the hypothesis-testing characterization of DP. If $\epsilon_{\text{emp}} \approx \epsilon_{\text{theory}}$, the analysis is tight; if $\epsilon_{\text{emp}} \ll \epsilon_{\text{theory}}$, the accountant is conservative and the model is safer than claimed—but the gap's origin (loose composition? loose subsampling? suboptimal attack?) is often unclear. Recent work audits DP-SGD in the *hidden-state* threat model, where the adversary sees only the final model rather than all intermediate iterates, and finds substantially tighter correspondence between theory and practice [7]. Auditing cannot *prove* privacy—absence of a successful attack is not evidence of absence—but it is the only empirical check on whether reported $(\epsilon, \delta)$ numbers reflect reality, and it has caught real bugs in noise-scale calibration and batch-sampling implementations.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Numerical behavior of the accountant

The following Python implements the core of an RDP accountant for DP-SGD (Poisson sampling), following the closed-form bounds of Wang et al. [3] in their small-$q$ regime, and reproduces the qualitative behavior reported by Abadi et al. [1] and the Opacus/TF-Privacy libraries:

```python
import numpy as np

def rdp_subsampled_gaussian(q: float, sigma: float, alpha: float) -> float:
    """RDP of one Poisson-subsampled Gaussian step, order alpha (small-q bound)."""
    # Wang et al. (2018), Thm: leading term ~ q^2 * alpha / (2 sigma^2)-style;
    # full bound includes higher-order correction terms in q.
    if q == 0:
        return 0.0
    # Simplified analytic bound: eps(alpha) <= q^2 * alpha / (2*sigma^2) * C(q, alpha)
    base = q ** 2 * alpha / (2.0 * sigma ** 2)
    correction = 1.0 + q * (alpha - 1.0) / sigma  # higher-order term, q^3 scale
    return base * correction

def dp_sgd_epsilon(steps: int, q: float, sigma: float, delta: float,
                   alphas=(1.5, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128)) -> float:
    """Total (eps, delta)-DP of DP-SGD via RDP composition + conversion."""
    best = float("inf")
    for a in alphas:
        rdp_total = steps * rdp_subsampled_gaussian(q, sigma, a)
        eps = rdp_total + np.log(1.0 / delta) / (a - 1.0)
        best = min(best, eps)
    return best

# MNIST-scale setting from Abadi et al.: N=60000, L=600, sigma=4, 100 epochs
N, L, sigma, epochs = 60000, 600, 4.0, 100
q = L / N
steps = int(epochs / q)
print("q=%.4f steps=%d" % (q, steps))
print("eps(delta=1e-5) = %.2f" % dp_sgd_epsilon(steps, q, sigma, 1e-5))
# Expected order: eps ~ O(1)-single digits, matching the reported
# (eps=2..8, delta=1e-5) regime for 97% MNIST accuracy in [1].
```

The computation exhibits the three structural facts of the theory: (i) the per-step cost scales as $q^2/\sigma^2$, so doubling the dataset at fixed lot size *quadruples* the affordable steps; (ii) total RDP grows linearly in steps while the conversion term is step-independent, so long runs push the optimal $\alpha^\star$ upward; (iii) $\epsilon$ is far below the $k\epsilon_{\text{step}}$ that basic composition would give—this gap, often two orders of magnitude, is the entire practical content of the moments accountant.

### 5.2 Proof: RDP-to-DP conversion

> **Theorem:** *(Mironov [2], Prop. 3.)* If $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\alpha, \epsilon)$-RDP, then for every $\delta \in (0,1)$ it is $(\epsilon + \ln(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1),\, \delta)$-DP.
> *Proof sketch.* Note $(\alpha - 1) D_\alpha(P\|Q) = \log \mathbb{E}_{o \sim P}[e^{(\alpha-1) L(o)}] \le (\alpha-1)\epsilon$. For any threshold $t$, Markov's inequality gives $\Pr[L > \epsilon + t] \le e^{(\alpha-1)\epsilon} / e^{(\alpha-1)(\epsilon+t)} = e^{-(\alpha-1)t}$. Choosing $t = \ln(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1)$ yields tail probability $\delta$, which is equivalent to $(\epsilon + t, \delta)$-DP via the PLRV tail characterization. ∎

### 5.3 Proof: subsampling amplification (intuition)

> **Theorem:** *(Amplification, informal [1, 3].)* Poisson subsampling at rate $q$ turns an $(\alpha, \epsilon(\alpha))$-RDP mechanism into one with $\epsilon_q(\alpha) = O(q^2 \epsilon(\alpha))$ for small $q$.
> *Proof sketch.* With probability $1 - q$ the differing record is absent and the output distributions coincide exactly (privacy loss $0$); with probability $q$ it is present and the loss is that of the base mechanism. The mixture $(1-q)P_0 + qP_1$ vs.\ $(1-q)P_0 + qP_1'$ has Rényi divergence controlled by the *convexity* of the divergence in its arguments: to first order the divergence scales with the squared mixture weight $q^2$, because the linear term in $q$ vanishes by the coupling of the shared $(1-q)$ mass. The rigorous bound tracks the binomial moments of the mixture [3]. ∎

Empirically, Abadi et al. [1] reported 97% accuracy on MNIST at $(\epsilon, \delta) = (2, 10^{-5})$–$(8, 10^{-5})$ and 73% on CIFAR-10 under meaningful budgets—numbers unreachable under advanced composition, which would have demanded $\epsilon$ in the hundreds for the same step count. Bu et al. [4] later showed the GDP analysis shaves these bounds further in closed form.

## 6 Limitations

1. **The utility gap persists.** DP-SGD pays a real accuracy tax: on CIFAR-10, private training trails non-private training by several points at $\epsilon \le 8$, and on large language models the gap widens—per-example clipping distorts gradient geometry in ways noise alone does not, and the clipping norm $C$ becomes a sensitive hyperparameter interacting with learning-rate schedules [1].
2. **Clipping bias and adaptivity.** Clipping is *not* a neutral operation: it biases the expected gradient toward small-norm examples, disproportionately degrading performance on underrepresented subgroups and long-tailed classes—a fairness cost documented alongside the privacy benefit.
3. **Accounting is still conservative.** RDP/GDP bounds are upper bounds; auditing [7] routinely finds empirical $\epsilon$ well below the reported one, meaning practitioners may be adding more noise than necessary. Tighter analyses (privacy-loss distributions via FFT [8-adjacent], exact composition) are computationally heavier and not yet standard in training libraries.
4. **Threat-model fragility.** The guarantees assume Poisson sampling with secret sample membership and a trusted curator; shuffling-based implementations, data-dependent hyperparameter tuning, and checkpoint releases each consume unaccounted budget or violate the model. The hidden-state vs.\ full-iterate distinction [7] shows reported $\epsilon$ values can be misleading if the adversary model is misread.
5. **$\delta$ is not free.** Approximate DP's $\delta$ permits catastrophic failure with probability $\delta$; choosing $\delta \ll 1/N$ is convention, not theorem, and group-privacy scaling ($k$ correlated records cost $\approx k\epsilon$) means DP-SGD protects individuals, not communities.
6. **Computational overhead.** Per-example gradients increase memory and time costs substantially (mitigated by ghost clipping and functorch-style vectorization in Opacus, but not eliminated), and the accountant's $\alpha$-grid search, while cheap, must be re-run for every hyperparameter change.

## 7 Conclusion

The arc from classical composition to the moments accountant to Rényi and Gaussian accounting is the story of differential privacy growing up: from worst-case black-box bounds to mechanism-aware, numerically tight, and finally closed-form analysis. DP-SGD's enduring design—clip per-example gradients, subsample, add Gaussian noise, and account with RDP—remains the default recipe for private deep learning a decade after Abadi et al. [1], precisely because each component is both theoretically grounded and implementable in a dozen lines. RDP [2] supplies the clean formalism (additive composition, closed-form Gaussian calibration, subsampling amplification, one-line conversion); the analytical moments accountant [3] makes subsampling bounds exact and general; $f$-DP/GDP [6, 4] reveals the hypothesis-testing essence and the central-limit structure of composition; and auditing [7] keeps the theory honest against implementation. The frontier is now utility: closing the accuracy gap at fixed $\epsilon$, tightening accounting to match audited reality, and extending rigorous guarantees to the adaptive, checkpointed, hyperparameter-tuned training pipelines actually deployed. Until then, the lesson stands—privacy at scale is an accounting problem first, and the accountant is the algorithm.
