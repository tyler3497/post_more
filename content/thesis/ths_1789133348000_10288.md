# Serializable Isolation Without Locking: Timestamp Ordering, Snapshot Isolation Anomalies, and Serializable Snapshot Isolation

## Abstract

Snapshot isolation (SI) delivers lock-free, non-blocking reads over multiversion storage, but its snapshot-and-first-committer-wins discipline admits non-serializable executions — most famously write skew — that violate application invariants no single transaction breaks. This thesis reconstructs the theory that closes that gap without reintroducing locks. Beginning from the ANSI SQL-92 phenomena and their inadequacy for multiversion systems, we formalize SI in the language of Atul Adya's generalized isolation theory and its multiversion phenomena *G-single*, *G2-item*, and *G2-predicate*. We then develop the multiversion serialization graph (MVSG) analysis of Fekete et al., whose central result is that every SI anomaly cycle contains two adjacent read–write antidependencies — a *dangerous structure* — between concurrent transactions. Aborting only the *pivot* transaction of such a structure yields Serializable Snapshot Isolation (SSI): full serializability with SI's performance profile. We detail Cahill et al.'s runtime realization via non-blocking SIREAD predicate locks, rw-antidependency tracking, and commit-ordering optimizations, and PostgreSQL's production deployment since version 9.1. Empirical evidence from the PostgreSQL implementation shows near-SI throughput with low abort rates, establishing SSI as the definitive answer to lock-free serializability in multiversion engines.

## 1. Introduction

The tension between concurrency and correctness is the oldest unresolved conflict in database systems. Strict two-phase locking (S2PL) guarantees serializability, but at the cost of blocking: readers wait for writers, writers wait for readers, and deadlocks demand detection and resolution. Multiversion concurrency control (MVCC) broke this symmetry by keeping old versions of data, allowing every read to proceed against a consistent snapshot without ever blocking on a concurrent write. *Snapshot isolation* (SI), introduced by Berenson et al. in 1995 [1], packaged this insight into a crisp protocol: read from a stable snapshot, buffer writes privately, and commit only if no concurrent transaction has written the same data (*first-committer-wins*).

SI was an immediate industrial success — Oracle, PostgreSQL (pre-9.1), and SQL Server all shipped it, Oracle and early PostgreSQL even labeling it "serializable." But the label was aspirational. SI permits *write skew*: two transactions that each preserve an invariant in isolation can jointly violate it, as in the canonical bank example where `r1(x=50, y=50) r2(x=50, y=50) w1(x=-20) w2(y=-30) c1 c2` leaves the final committed state `X1, Y2` violating the constraint `X+Y > 0` [8]. No ANSI phenomenon fires; the execution is simply not equivalent to any serial order. Applications were left to defend themselves with materialized conflict tables, explicit locking, or `SELECT FOR UPDATE` — reintroducing the very blocking SI was meant to eliminate.

This thesis presents the theory and engineering that resolves the dilemma: *serializable isolation without locking*. The resolution has three layers. First, a precise formal language for what SI does and does not forbid, drawn from Adya's generalized theory of isolation [9]. Second, the structural theorem of Fekete et al. [2]: every serialization-graph cycle admissible under SI contains two *adjacent* read–write antidependencies between *concurrent* transactions — a *dangerous structure* — so detecting and aborting only the *pivot* transaction at its junction breaks every anomaly cycle while committing all genuinely serializable executions. Third, the runtime mechanism of Cahill, Röhm, and Fekete [3], realized in PostgreSQL 9.1 by Ports and Grittner [4], that tracks these antidependencies with *non-blocking* SIREAD predicate locks, so serializability is achieved with monitoring rather than mutual exclusion.

The central claim is strong and practical: **serializability in a multiversion system requires no locks, only the detection of a specific local graph pattern among concurrent transactions** — and that detection can be made cheap enough for production.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The ANSI phenomena and their failure under MVCC

ANSI SQL-92 defined isolation levels by three *phenomena* to be avoided: *dirty reads*, *non-repeatable reads*, and *phantoms*. Berenson et al. [1] demonstrated that this definition was inadequate on two counts. First, the phenomena are ambiguous — their strict, broad, and strictest interpretations admit different lock-based implementations, and locking engines routinely provided guarantees stricter than their nominal ANSI level. Second, and more consequentially, phenomena defined in terms of single-version locking *fail to characterize multiversion systems at all*: SI avoids all three ANSI phenomena yet permits executions with no serial equivalent, such as write skew. Snapshot isolation was not even definable in the ANSI vocabulary; the paper had to define it from scratch [1].

### 2.2 Snapshot isolation mechanics

A system providing SI assigns each transaction `T` a *start timestamp* `start(T)` and exposes a *snapshot*: every committed version as of that timestamp, with uncommitted and later-committed versions invisible. The protocol is:

1. **Reads** consult the snapshot only; they never block and are never blocked.
2. **Writes** are buffered in a private workspace, invisible until commit.
3. **Commit** applies the *first-committer-wins* (FCW) rule: `T` aborts if any transaction concurrent with `T` has already committed a write to an item `T` also writes. Read–write overlap is unchecked — this is the source of both SI's performance and its anomalies.

FCW prevents *lost update* (concurrent writers to the same item serialize by abort), but disjoint write sets pass certification silently.

### 2.3 Adya's generalized formalism

Atul Adya's dissertation [9] and the subsequent ICDE paper with Liskov and O'Neil recast isolation in terms of *histories with version orders* and classified anomalies as *phenomena* over dependency graphs. Single-version phenomena include *G0* (write cycles, i.e., dirty writes), *G1a* (aborted reads), *G1b* (intermediate reads), and *G1c* (circular information flow). For multiversion systems, Adya added:

- ***G-single***: a dependency cycle with exactly one read–write antidependency;
- ***G2-item***: a cycle with two or more item-level antidependencies;
- ***G2-predicate***: a cycle involving predicate-based antidependencies (phantoms).

> **Theorem:** Snapshot isolation forbids *G0*, *G1a–c*, and *G-single*, but admits *G2-item* and *G2-predicate* [9].

Write skew is precisely *G2-item*: two antidependencies, one per account. This classification explains *why* the ANSI phenomena missed it — ANSI never named an antidependency at all.

### 2.4 Timestamp ordering: the lock-free lineage

SSI belongs to a lineage of *timestamp-ordering* schedulers rather than locking ones. *Basic timestamp ordering* (BTO) assigns each transaction a timestamp and executes conflicting operations in timestamp order, aborting violators; *Thomas's write rule* relaxes this by ignoring obsolete writes. *Multiversion timestamp ordering* (MVTO) extends the idea: each write creates a new version stamped with the writer's timestamp, and reads select the newest version not newer than the reader. SI can be viewed as MVTO with a static snapshot timestamp plus optimistic write certification. SSI completes the picture: MVTO-style snapshots plus *graph-based* certification that detects exactly the cycles SI misses.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis follows the dependency-graph methodology of [2], applied to histories over multiversion data.

**Model.** A *history* `H` is a partially ordered set of operations `r_i(x_j)`, `w_i(x_j)`, `c_i`, `a_i` with a *version order* `≪` per item. Transactions have intervals `[start(T), commit(T)]`; two transactions are *concurrent* when their intervals overlap.

**The multiversion serialization graph (MVSG).** Nodes are committed transactions. Directed edges encode dependencies:

| Edge | Notation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| write–read | `T1 --wr--> T2` | `T2` reads a version of `x` written by `T1` |
| write–write | `T1 --ww--> T2` | `T2` overwrites `T1`'s version of `x` |
| read–write | `T1 - -rw- -→ T2` | `T1` reads a version of `x` that `T2` later overwrites (dashed) |

An execution is serializable iff its MVSG is acyclic — the classical serialization-graph theorem, lifted to versions [2].

**Anomaly detection as subgraph detection.** Under SI, `ww` and `wr` edges are constrained by FCW and snapshot visibility; only `rw` edges can create *new* cycles. The key structural results of [2]:

1. **Every cycle in an SI history's MVSG contains at least two `rw` edges** (Adya's result, via [2]).
2. **Refinement:** the two `rw` edges can be chosen *adjacent* (consecutive) in the cycle, and each connects two *concurrent* transactions.
3. **Pivot theorem:** call an `rw` edge between concurrent transactions a *vulnerable edge*, and two consecutive vulnerable edges `Tin --rw--> Tpivot --rw--> Tout` a *dangerous structure*, with `Tpivot` the *pivot*. **Every non-serializable execution admitted by SI contains a dangerous structure.**

The methodology is therefore: *track only `rw` edges among concurrent transactions; abort a transaction exactly when it becomes the pivot of a dangerous structure.* This is a *local* decision requiring no global graph — the essence of SSI.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Timestamp ordering without locking: from BTO to SSI

Timestamp-ordering schedulers replace lock acquisition with timestamp comparison. In BTO, operation `o_i(x)` with timestamp `ts(T_i)` is rejected if a conflicting operation with a larger timestamp has already been processed; Thomas's write rule additionally ignores a write that is already obsolete. Multiversion timestamp ordering removes most rejections by retaining every version:

```python
def mvto_read(txn, x, versions):
    # newest version with write-timestamp <= txn.snapshot_ts
    candidates = [v for v in versions[x] if v.wts <= txn.snapshot_ts]
    return max(candidates, key=lambda v: v.wts)

def mvto_write(txn, x, value, versions):
    # create a new version stamped with the writer's commit timestamp
    versions[x].append(Version(data=value, wts=txn.commit_ts, writer=txn.id))
```

Reads never reject; writes never block. SI is this scheme with the snapshot frozen at `start(T)` and commit gated by FCW. SSI adds the missing certification: whereas optimistic concurrency control (Kung–Robinson) validates *entire* read sets against concurrent writes — aborting on *any* overwritten read — SSI validates only the dangerous-structure pattern, aborting far less often. The progression is:

1. **BTO / Thomas:** order by timestamp, reject violators — no versions, high aborts.
2. **MVTO:** versions absorb read–write conflict — reads never abort.
3. **SI:** MVTO + FCW on write–write — admits *G2* cycles.
4. **SSI:** SI + pivot detection — serializable, aborts only pivots.

### 4.2 The write-skew anomaly, formalized

Consider two transactions maintaining `x + y > 0`, with `x = y = 50`:

```
r1(x=50, y=50)  r2(x=50, y=50)  w1(x=-20)  w2(y=-30)  c1  c2
```

Each transaction reads a consistent snapshot, withdraws from a different account, and passes FCW because the write sets `{x}` and `{y}` are disjoint. The final state `x = -20, y = -30` violates the invariant. In MVSG terms:

- `T1` reads `y=50`, overwritten by `T2` → `T1 - -rw- -→ T2`
- `T2` reads `x=50`, overwritten by `T1` → `T2 - -rw- -→ T1`

The graph contains the cycle `T1 → T2 → T1` with two adjacent `rw` edges — both between concurrent transactions. The dangerous structure is `T1 --rw--> T2 --rw--> T1` with pivot `T2` (symmetrically, pivot `T1`). No lock-based phenomenon fires; only the antidependency cycle reveals the anomaly. Automated static detectors for such patterns in application code — notably the VLDB 2007 work of Jorwekar, Fekete, Ramamritham, and Sudarshan on detecting SI anomalies — build directly on this graph characterization.

### 4.3 Dangerous structures and the pivot theorem

The dangerous structure `Tin --rw--> Tpivot --rw--> Tout` deserves careful unpacking:

- `Tin --rw--> Tpivot`: `Tin` read data that `Tpivot` overwrote; both concurrent.
- `Tpivot --rw--> Tout`: `Tpivot` read data that `Tout` overwrote; both concurrent.

Fekete et al. prove that **aborting every pivot makes all remaining executions serializable**, because every SI anomaly cycle contains at least one pivot, and removing pivots destroys all cycles [2]. Two subtleties matter:

1. **False positives.** A dangerous structure does not imply an actual cycle — the three transactions may still be serializable (notably the *read-only anomaly* patterns). SSI therefore aborts some transactions that could have committed. The theory accepts this: precision is sacrificed for a local, online decision procedure.
2. **Commit ordering refinement.** In any SI cycle there is a transaction that commits first; call the structure's outgoing neighbor `Tout`. The abort can be deferred and made conditional: **only abort the pivot if `Tout` commits before `Tpivot` and `Tin`** (Theorem 2.1 of [2], as implemented in [4]). In write skew, `Tout = Tin = T1`: if `T1` commits first, `T2` (the pivot) aborts and `T1` commits — recovering exactly first-committer-wins semantics for the two-transaction case.

### 4.4 SSI: SIREAD locks and rw-antidependency tracking

Cahill, Röhm, and Fekete [3] gave the first *automatic, online* SSI implementation. The mechanism:

- **SIREAD locks** (predicate locks): every serializable transaction takes lightweight *read* locks on data it reads — relation, page, or tuple granularity. Crucially, **SIREAD locks never block**; they exist only to detect overlap when a concurrent transaction writes. A write checks for conflicting SIREAD locks held by concurrent transactions and records an `rw` antidependency edge.
- **Dangerous-structure detection:** the engine maintains, per transaction, its incoming and outgoing `rw` edges to concurrent transactions. When a transaction acquires both — becoming a pivot — and the commit-ordering condition holds, it is aborted with a serialization failure.
- **Lock retention:** because an `rw` edge can be discovered after the reader commits (a writer may commit later), SIREAD locks and edge summaries must be retained past commit until no concurrent transaction remains active.
- **Summarization:** to bound memory, fine-grained locks are promoted (tuple → page → relation) when the lock table fills, at the cost of coarser — and more false-positive-prone — conflict detection.

```rust
struct TxnState {
    id: TxnId,
    snapshot: Timestamp,
    in_rw: Vec<TxnId>,   // concurrent txns whose reads I overwrote
    out_rw: Vec<TxnId>,  // concurrent txns that overwrote my reads
    committed: bool,
}

fn on_write(writer: &mut TxnState, x: Item, sireads: &SireadTable) {
    for reader_id in sireads.conflicting_readers(x, writer.snapshot) {
        let reader = txns.get_mut(reader_id);
        // rw-antidependency: reader --rw--> writer
        reader.out_rw.push(writer.id);
        writer.in_rw.push(reader_id);
        try_abort_pivot(reader_id);
        try_abort_pivot(writer.id);
    }
}

fn is_pivot(t: &TxnState) -> bool {
    !t.in_rw.is_empty() && !t.out_rw.is_empty()
}
```

The read-only optimization [4]: if `Tin` is read-only, an anomaly requires `Tout` to have committed *before* `Tin` took its snapshot — otherwise the cycle's remaining edges could not exist. PostgreSQL exploits this to let long-running read-only transactions proceed, and `SERIALIZABLE READ ONLY DEFERRABLE` transactions simply wait until a *safe snapshot* (one with no concurrent read–write transactions) is available — the only case where a serializable transaction blocks where a repeatable-read one would not [5].

### 4.5 PostgreSQL's predicate-locking realization

PostgreSQL 9.1 replaced its nominal "serializable" (actually SI) level with true SSI [4][5]. Design points:

- Predicate locks surface in `pg_locks` with mode `SIReadLock`; their granularity follows the query plan and degrades gracefully under pressure.
- Serialization failures raise SQLSTATE `40001` with `could not serialize access due to read/write dependencies among transactions`, and applications are expected to retry — the standard contract of optimistic schemes.
- SIREAD locks never participate in deadlock detection, because they never block.
- The implementation tracks only `rw` edges between concurrent transactions, exactly as the theory prescribes, plus the two commit-ordering optimizations of §4.3.

The result is a system where application developers can ignore concurrency anomalies — the database aborts the rare dangerous execution — while retaining SI's non-blocking read path.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: pivots suffice

> **Theorem:** Let `H` be a history admitted by snapshot isolation. If every pivot transaction of every dangerous structure in `H` is aborted, the committed projection of `H` is serializable.

*Proof sketch* (after [2]). Suppose the committed transactions contain a cycle `C` in the MVSG. By the refinement lemma, `C` contains two adjacent vulnerable `rw` edges `Tin --rw--> Tpivot --rw--> Tout` with all three pairwise concurrent. `Tpivot` is a pivot of a dangerous structure; by hypothesis it was aborted — contradiction. ∎

The commit-ordering optimization preserves the theorem: Fekete et al. show that in any SI cycle, some transaction commits before the others; orienting the dangerous structure so that `Tout` is that earliest committer, the abort is required only when `Tout` commits first, which is exactly when the cycle could still be forming [2][4].

### 5.2 Production evidence: PostgreSQL SSI

Ports and Grittner [4] evaluated SSI in PostgreSQL 9.1 against SI baselines:

- **Throughput:** serializable mode performed *similarly to snapshot isolation* across the tested workloads — the predicate-locking and edge-tracking overhead did not materially change the performance profile, confirming that SSI preserves SI's scalability rather than regressing to S2PL behavior.
- **Abort rates:** serialization failures were rare in realistic workloads; dangerous structures are uncommon when transactions are short and contention is moderate. The false-positive rate — dangerous structures without genuine cycles — remained low enough that retry-based applications saw no pathological behavior.
- **Safe snapshots:** acquiring a safe snapshot for a `DEFERRABLE` transaction over 1,200 trials showed a median latency of 1.98 seconds, with 90% succeeding within 6 seconds and all within 20 seconds — acceptable for the long-running analytic transactions the feature targets [4].
- **Correctness:** the implementation was validated against anomaly-inducing workloads (write skew, the batch-processing and `SUM`-aggregation examples from the PostgreSQL documentation [5]) where repeatable-read mode commits both transactions but serializable mode deterministically aborts one with error `40001`.

Comparative summary:

| Property | S2PL serializable | SI | SSI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Readers block writers | Yes | No | No |
| Writers block readers | Yes | No | No |
| Deadlocks possible | Yes | No | No |
| Write skew admitted | No | **Yes** | No |
| Aborts on conflict | On deadlock | On FCW violation | On FCW violation or pivot |
| Anomaly detection | Via blocking | None | Dangerous-structure monitoring |
| Read-only scalability | Poor | Excellent | Excellent |

## 6. Limitations

SSI is not free, and its limits should be stated precisely.

1. **False positives are inherent.** The dangerous structure is a *sufficient* but not *necessary* condition for a cycle at the moment of detection; SSI aborts some serializable executions. Under high contention with long transactions, abort rates climb and retry storms can erode the throughput advantage over S2PL.
2. **Memory pressure from predicate locks.** SIREAD locks must be retained past commit until overlapping transactions finish. Hot relations force promotion to page or relation granularity, coarsening conflict detection and increasing false positives — a direct space–precision tradeoff.
3. **All participants must opt in.** SSI's guarantees hold among transactions running at the serializable level; mixing serializable and weaker levels reopens anomalies, and SSI monitoring adds overhead even to transactions that would never conflict.
4. **No help for non-SI anomalies.** SSI addresses serialization; it does not fix application-level races outside the database, nor does it make `DEFERRABLE` waits disappear — safe-snapshot acquisition still delays transaction start by seconds in contended systems [4].
5. **Theoretical boundary.** The pivot theorem is specific to SI's snapshot-plus-FCW discipline. Weaker variants (read-committed snapshots, parallel SI) admit cycles with different structure, and SSI as formulated does not cover them [7].

## 7. Conclusion

Serializable isolation without locking is not a paradox but a theorem with an implementation. Adya's formalism [9] gave us the vocabulary to name what SI misses — *G2-item* and *G2-predicate* cycles built from read–write antidependencies. Fekete et al. [2] showed these cycles always contain a local, detectable pattern: two adjacent `rw` edges between concurrent transactions, whose pivot can be aborted to break every cycle. Cahill et al. [3] turned the pattern into an online algorithm with non-blocking SIREAD locks, and PostgreSQL 9.1 [4][5] proved it in production: true serializability at essentially SI's performance, with aborts confined to genuine or near-genuine anomalies.

The deeper lesson is architectural. Locking conflates *detection* of conflict with *prevention* of concurrency; SSI separates them. By monitoring a single edge type (`rw`) among a restricted transaction population (concurrent ones) for a single local pattern (the dangerous structure), a multiversion engine obtains the strongest isolation guarantee while readers and writers never wait for each other. Timestamp ordering, long dismissed as a textbook curiosity beside two-phase locking, thus finds its most successful expression not in rejecting operations by timestamp, but in certifying snapshots by graph structure — serializability as *observation* rather than *exclusion*.
