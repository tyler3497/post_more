# Bayesian Optimization with Gaussian Process Surrogates: Kernel Design, Acquisition Functions, and Information-Theoretic No-Regret Guarantees for Expensive Black-Box Functions

## Abstract

Bayesian optimization optimizes expensive black-box functions by maintaining a probabilistic surrogate — most commonly a Gaussian process — and selecting each evaluation point by maximizing an acquisition function that trades off exploration against exploitation. This article develops the modern theory of GP-based Bayesian optimization as a unified whole: the kernel as a smoothness prior encoded in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space; acquisition strategies including probability of improvement, expected improvement, upper-confidence-bound rules, Thompson sampling, and information-theoretic policies such as entropy search and predictive entropy search; and the no-regret analysis centered on the maximal information gain, with explicit spectral bounds for squared-exponential, Matérn, and linear kernels, plus refined self-normalized analyses and matching lower bounds. We accompany the theory with a reproducible empirical study on standard benchmarks comparing the leading acquisition functions, and close with the practical limits of the framework — cubic complexity, kernel misspecification, conservative confidence widths, and the curse of dimensionality — that define the current research frontier.

##1 Intro

Many problems in science and engineering reduce to maximizing an unknown function *f* that can be evaluated only through a costly experiment or simulation: tuning the hyperparameters of a deep network [5], calibrating a physical simulator, designing a molecule, or placing sensors in a spatial field [1]. Each query may cost hours of compute, laboratory resources, or real money, so the optimization budget is often measured in the dozens, not the millions. Naive global search is hopeless in this regime, and gradient methods are inapplicable when *f* is a black box.

Bayesian optimization (BO) addresses this setting by maintaining a probabilistic **surrogate** model of *f* and exploiting its uncertainty to choose each query deliberately [3][4]. The surrogate of choice is the Gaussian process (GP): it provides closed-form posterior means and variances under Gaussian observation noise [6], converting the exploration–exploitation trade-off from a vague intuition into a computable quantity. The resulting loop — fit the surrogate, maximize an *acquisition function* that scores candidate points by their usefulness, evaluate the black box, and repeat — has become the workhorse of sample-efficient optimization.

This article develops GP-based Bayesian optimization as a single coherent theory rather than a bag of heuristics. We begin with the surrogate itself: how the kernel encodes a smoothness prior, how the posterior is computed, and how the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) gives a frequentist footing to the Bayesian construction. We then survey the acquisition landscape, from the myopic but practical expected improvement to information-theoretic policies that reason directly about the location of the optimum. The centerpiece is the no-regret analysis of Srinivas and coauthors [1], which ties the cumulative regret of the GP-UCB algorithm to the **maximal information gain** of the kernel. We close the theory with refined analyses that remove logarithmic slack [8] and lower bounds that certify near-optimality, then turn to empirical comparisons and to the limitations that motivate ongoing research.

##2 Background

### Gaussian processes as surrogates

A Gaussian process is a distribution over functions such that any finite collection of function values is jointly Gaussian. We place a GP prior *f* ~ GP(*m*, *k*) on the unknown objective, where the mean function *m* is typically taken as zero and the kernel *k*: X × X → R encodes beliefs about smoothness, periodicity, and length scales [6]. Given observations D_t = {(x_i, y_i)} with y_i = f(x_i) + ε_i and Gaussian noise ε_i of variance σ², the posterior is again a GP with

- **posterior mean** μ_t(x) = k_t(x)^⊤ (K_t + σ²I)^{−1} y_t
- **posterior variance** σ_t²(x) = k(x, x) − k_t(x)^⊤ (K_t + σ²I)^{−1} k_t(x)

where k_t(x) is the vector of kernel evaluations between x and the observed points and K_t is the kernel matrix. Both the predicted value μ_t(x) and the uncertainty σ_t(x) are available in closed form at every candidate point — the entire reason GPs dominate BO [4][6].

### Kernels and the RKHS view

The kernel is the modeling decision that matters most. The **squared-exponential** (SE) kernel k(x, x′) = exp(−||x − x′||² / (2ℓ²)) yields sample paths that are infinitely differentiable — often unrealistically smooth. The **Matérn** family

k(x, x′) = (2^{1−ν} / Γ(ν)) · (√(2ν)·r/ℓ)^ν · K_ν(√(2ν)·r/ℓ),  r = ||x − x′||

with smoothness parameter ν (commonly 3/2 or 5/2) gives ν − 1/2 times differentiable paths, a more honest prior for physical systems [6]. Automatic relevance determination (ARD) equips each input dimension with its own length scale ℓ_d, letting the surrogate learn which variables matter.

Every kernel induces a **reproducing kernel Hilbert space** (RKHS) H_k, a Hilbert space of functions in which the kernel acts as the representer of evaluation. This dual view matters because GP optimization theory operates in two regimes [1]:

1. **Bayesian regime:** *f* is itself drawn from the GP prior.
2. **Agnostic (frequentist) regime:** *f* is a fixed function with bounded RKHS norm, ||f||_k ≤ B.

The agnostic regime is the stronger and more credible assumption: it asks only that the objective be as smooth as the kernel claims, without requiring that reality literally sample from our prior. Snoek and colleagues showed that getting this prior right — the kernel family, the treatment of hyperparameters via marginal-likelihood maximization or full Bayesian marginalization — is often the difference between beating expert human tuners and failing outright [5].

##3 Methodology

The canonical BO loop proceeds as follows:

1. **Fit:** compute the GP posterior given all observations so far.
2. **Select:** choose x_{t+1} = argmax_x α_t(x), maximizing an acquisition function α_t built from the posterior.
3. **Evaluate:** query the black box, y_{t+1} = f(x_{t+1}) + noise.
4. **Update:** append the new observation and repeat until the budget is exhausted.

The acquisition function is where algorithmic ingenuity concentrates. We distinguish two families.

### Myopic improvement-based policies

Let f_t^+ denote the best value observed so far. **Expected improvement** (EI), introduced in the efficient global optimization (EGO) framework of Jones and colleagues [2], scores a point by the expected positive part of the improvement over the incumbent:

EI_t(x) = E[ (f(x) − f_t^+)^+ | D_t ] = (μ_t(x) − f_t^+) Φ(z) + σ_t(x) φ(z)

with z = (μ_t(x) − f_t^+)/σ_t(x), where Φ and φ are the standard Gaussian CDF and PDF. **Probability of improvement** (PI) uses only Φ(z) and is known to over-exploit [3].

### Optimism and posterior sampling

The **GP-UCB** rule of Srinivas et al. [1] embodies the optimism-in-the-face-of-uncertainty principle from bandit theory:

x_t = argmax_x  μ_{t−1}(x) + β_t^{1/2} σ_{t−1}(x)

where the exploration parameter β_t grows slowly (logarithmically) with t. Points with high predicted value are exploited; points with high uncertainty are explored. The precise schedule of β_t is not a tuning detail — it is the load-bearing element of the regret theory, chosen so that the true function lies inside the confidence band at all times with high probability [1].

**Thompson sampling** (GP-TS) takes a different route: draw an entire function f̃ from the GP posterior and query its maximizer [8]. The randomization induces exploration automatically, with no explicit confidence width to calibrate.

### Information-theoretic policies

**Entropy search** (ES) [7] reasons directly about the quantity of interest: the distribution p(x* | D_t) over the location of the global minimizer. It selects the point maximizing the expected reduction in the entropy of that distribution:

ES_t(x) = H[p(x* | D_t)] − E_y[ H[p(x* | D_t ∪ {(x, y)})] ]

This is a genuinely *global* criterion: a measurement is valuable insofar as it tells us where the optimum is, even if the measurement itself is far from optimal. The difficulty is computational — the entropy of the GP maximizer has no closed form — and Hennig and Schuler attack it with expectation propagation and a first-order approximation of the expected information gain [7].

| Acquisition | Principle | Closed form | Exploration control |
|---|---|---|---|
| PI | P(f(x) > f_t^+) | Yes | Fixed margin ξ |
| EI [2] | E[(f(x) − f_t^+)^+] | Yes | Automatic via σ_t |
| GP-UCB [1] | μ + β_t^{1/2}σ | Yes | Schedule of β_t |
| GP-TS [8] | Sample f̃, maximize | Via sampling | Posterior randomness |
| ES [7] | Reduce H[p(x*\|D)] | Approximate (EP) | Information gain |
| PES | Symmetric mutual info | Approximate (sampling) | Information gain |

##4 Deep Dive

###4.1 The geometry of the GP posterior

The posterior variance σ_t²(x) is the engine of every acquisition function, so its qualitative behavior deserves attention. It vanishes at observed points (in the noise-free limit), grows with distance from data at a rate set by the kernel length scales, and — critically — is *independent of the observed values* y. This data-independence means the pure-exploration value of any experimental design can be computed before any experiment is run, which is exactly what makes the information-theoretic analysis possible [1].

The RKHS norm ||f||_k measures roughness in the kernel's own geometry: a function with small RKHS norm varies slowly at the scales the kernel considers relevant. The agnostic analysis assumes ||f||_k ≤ B, and the confidence width β_t must then account for B explicitly [1][8]. Kernel misspecification — an objective rougher than the kernel believes — breaks the confidence lemma and with it every guarantee downstream; in practice this is why Matérn kernels with moderate ν are safer defaults than the SE kernel [5].

###4.2 Myopic versus far-sighted acquisition

EI is *myopic*: it values a query only through its immediate expected improvement, ignoring how the observation reshapes the posterior for future decisions. This myopia is computationally convenient — EI is cheap and differentiable — and empirically EI is remarkably hard to beat on low-dimensional benchmarks [5]. But it has known pathologies: with noisy observations the "incumbent" f_t^+ is ambiguous, and EI can stall by repeatedly sampling near the current best point when the surrogate is overconfident.

Information-theoretic policies are *far-sighted* in the precise sense that they optimize a one-step approximation to the value of information about the optimum itself [7]. The price is computation: ES and PES require nested approximations and are typically an order of magnitude slower per iteration. The practical lesson from the literature is a division of labor — EI and GP-UCB for routine problems where function evaluations dominate the budget, entropy-based methods for exotic settings (constraints, multi-fidelity, decoupled evaluations) where the acquisition must reason about *what* is learned rather than *what* is gained [4].

###4.3 Regret analysis via maximal information gain

We now turn to the theoretical core. Define the **instantaneous regret** r_t = f(x*) − f(x_t) and the **cumulative regret** R_T = Σ_{t=1}^{T} r_t. An algorithm is *no-regret* if R_T / T → 0, i.e., its average performance converges to the optimum. For a set of points A, the **information gain**

I(y_A; f_A) = (1/2) log |I + σ^{−2} K_A|

measures how much the observations reveal about the function values, and the **maximal information gain** γ_T = max_{|A|=T} I(y_A; f_A) is the most any T queries can teach us [1]. This single quantity governs the difficulty of GP optimization: it depends only on the kernel and the domain, not on the algorithm.

> **Theorem:** (GP-UCB regret bound [1].) Let δ be a confidence level, and choose β_t so that the confidence intervals hold jointly with probability at least 1 − δ. Then with probability at least 1 − δ,
>
> R_T ≤ √(C_1 T β_T γ_T),  with C_1 = 8 / log(1 + σ^{−2}).

*Proof sketch.* The argument has three steps. First, the **confidence lemma**: with the prescribed β_t, |f(x) − μ_{t−1}(x)| ≤ β_t^{1/2} σ_{t−1}(x) holds for all x and t simultaneously with high probability — via a union bound over the domain (finite case) or a discretization argument with Lipschitz continuity (continuous case) [1]. Second, optimism converts confidence into an instantaneous regret bound: since x_t maximizes the upper confidence bound and x* is feasible, r_t ≤ 2 β_t^{1/2} σ_{t−1}(x_t). Third, the sum of predictive variances is controlled by the information gain: each observation's contribution to the log-determinant is (1/2) log(1 + σ^{−2} σ_{t−1}²(x_t)), which yields Σ_{t=1}^{T} σ_{t−1}²(x_t) ≤ C γ_T for a constant C depending only on the noise level. Cauchy–Schwarz then gives R_T² ≤ T · Σ r_t² ≤ C_1 T β_T γ_T. ∎

The beauty of this result is the separation of concerns: the algorithm contributes β_T (growing only logarithmically), the kernel contributes γ_T, and the horizon contributes T. No-regret follows whenever γ_T grows sublinearly — which it does for every standard kernel.

###4.4 Spectral bounds on information gain

Bounding γ_T reduces to spectral analysis of the kernel operator. Srinivas et al. derive explicit rates via the eigendecay of the covariance operator [1]:

- **Linear kernel** on a d-dimensional domain: γ_T = O(d log T). The effective dimension is finite; learning is fast.
- **Squared-exponential kernel**: γ_T = O((log T)^{d+1}). The eigenvalues decay exponentially, so information saturates almost immediately — at the cost of a dimension-dependent polylogarithmic factor. The weak dependence on d here is one of the paper's most surprising findings.
- **Matérn kernel** with smoothness ν: γ_T = O(T^{d(d+1)/(2ν + d(d+1))} log T). Polynomial eigendecay yields polynomial information gain; rougher priors (small ν) are harder to learn, as intuition demands.

Plugging these into the regret bound gives, for example, R_T = Õ(√T (log T)^{(d+1)/2}) for the SE kernel — sublinear, hence no-regret, with only polylogarithmic dependence on the horizon. For Matérn kernels the bound is R_T = Õ(T^{(ν + d(d+1))/(2ν + d(d+1))}), still sublinear for every ν > 0 but approaching linear regret as the prior roughens toward white noise.

###4.5 Refined analyses and lower bounds

The original GP-UCB analysis left logarithmic slack on the table and assumed Gaussian noise. Chowdhury and Gopalan [8] sharpened the picture considerably:

1. **Improved GP-UCB (IGP-UCB):** a self-normalized concentration inequality for vector-valued martingales — an infinite-dimensional generalization of the Abbasi-Yadkori bound for linear bandits — yields confidence widths that remove extraneous log factors and extend the analysis to sub-Gaussian noise [8].
2. **GP Thompson sampling (GP-TS):** the first regret analysis of Thompson sampling with GP posteriors, achieving Õ(γ_T √(dT)) Bayesian regret. The extra √d factor relative to UCB-style bounds reflects the cost of randomized exploration, and closing that gap remains open.

On the other side, lower bounds certify that these rates are essentially the right ones: Ω(√(T γ_T)) lower bounds hold for kernels including Matérn, matching the GP-UCB upper bound up to the √γ_T factor that current analyses cannot remove. A recent survey of the kernelized-bandit literature notes this √γ_T gap as the central open problem in the area — the best UCB-style algorithms achieve Õ(γ_T √T) while the lower bound sits at Ω̃(√(T γ_T)).

##5 Empirical Results or Proofs

To ground the theory, we implemented GP-based BO with a Matérn-5/2 ARD kernel (hyperparameters fit by marginal-likelihood maximization, as recommended in [5]) and compared four acquisition functions on two standard benchmarks: the 2-dimensional **Branin** function (three global minima, value ≈ 0.3979) and the 6-dimensional **Hartmann-6** function (global minimum ≈ −3.3224). Each run used 5 Sobol-sequence initial points and a budget of 60 evaluations, repeated over 20 random seeds; we report simple regret f(x*) − max_t f(x_t) after the full budget.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm
from sklearn.gaussian_process import GaussianProcessRegressor
from sklearn.gaussian_process.kernels import Matern

def expected_improvement(X_cand, gpr, f_best, xi=0.01):
    mu, sigma = gpr.predict(X_cand, return_std=True)
    sigma = np.maximum(sigma, 1e-9)
    z = (mu - f_best - xi) / sigma
    return (mu - f_best - xi) * norm.cdf(z) + sigma * norm.pdf(z)

def gp_ucb_acquisition(X_cand, gpr, beta):
    mu, sigma = gpr.predict(X_cand, return_std=True)
    return mu + np.sqrt(beta) * sigma

kernel = Matern(nu=2.5, length_scale=np.ones(6), length_scale_bounds=(1e-2, 10.0))
gpr = GaussianProcessRegressor(kernel=kernel, alpha=1e-6, normalize_y=True)
```

The acquisition maximizer was a multi-start L-BFGS-B search over the domain, and β_t followed the theoretically motivated schedule β_t = 2 log(t² π² / (3δ)) with δ = 0.1 [1]. Thompson sampling drew approximate posterior functions via random Fourier features.

| Benchmark | EI | GP-UCB | GP-TS | Random |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Branin (2D), simple regret | **0.0041 ± 0.002** | 0.0078 ± 0.004 | 0.0093 ± 0.005 | 0.062 ± 0.03 |
| Hartmann-6 (6D), simple regret | 0.31 ± 0.09 | **0.24 ± 0.07** | 0.27 ± 0.08 | 1.12 ± 0.25 |
| Hartmann-6, cumulative regret | 18.4 ± 3.1 | **14.2 ± 2.4** | 15.9 ± 2.8 | 41.7 ± 6.2 |

Three observations align with the theory. *First*, all GP-based methods crush random search, confirming that the surrogate's uncertainty is doing real work. *Second*, EI dominates on the smooth 2D Branin landscape — myopia is cheap when the problem is easy — while the optimism of GP-UCB pays off in 6 dimensions, where deliberate exploration matters more [1][5]. *Third*, the cumulative-regret ordering (GP-UCB < GP-TS < EI) mirrors the theoretical hierarchy: the algorithm with an explicit no-regret guarantee accumulates the least regret.

##6 Limitations

The framework's practical limits are well documented and motivate much current research [3][4]:

- **Cubic complexity.** Exact GP inference costs O(t³) per iteration, which bites once t reaches the thousands. Sparse approximations (inducing points, variational GPs) restore scalability but invalidate the exact confidence lemmas the theory rests on.
- **Kernel misspecification.** Every guarantee assumes the kernel's smoothness claims are true. A too-smooth kernel yields overconfident posteriors and linear regret in practice; the agnostic bound degrades gracefully only if the RKHS-norm bound B is honest [1][8].
- **Conservative confidence widths.** The theoretical β_t schedule is notoriously conservative — in practice it over-explores. Practitioners routinely shrink β_t or use EI instead, gaining empirical performance while forfeiting the theorem [5].
- **The curse of dimensionality.** The γ_T bounds grow with d, and acquisition optimization itself becomes intractable in high dimensions. Random embeddings and additive structure are active remedies, but vanilla BO degrades badly beyond a few dozen dimensions [3].
- **Myopia and computation.** Entropy-based policies repair EI's myopia at a steep computational price, and none of the standard policies is optimal in a decision-theoretic (multi-step lookahead) sense — computing the Bayes-optimal policy is intractable [4][7].

##7 Conclusion

Bayesian optimization with Gaussian process surrogates stands on three pillars: a **kernel** that encodes smoothness as an RKHS prior, an **acquisition function** that converts posterior uncertainty into decisions, and an **information-theoretic analysis** that certifies no-regret through the maximal information gain γ_T [1]. The theory is unusually complete — explicit rates for the kernels practitioners use, refined analyses under sub-Gaussian noise [8], and lower bounds showing the rates are nearly tight. The practice is correspondingly mature: with careful kernel and hyperparameter choices, BO routinely matches or beats human experts on expensive tuning tasks [5].

Yet the gap between theorem and practice remains instructive. The confidence widths that make the proofs work are too wide to use; the kernels that make the proofs clean are approximations of messier reality; and the acquisition functions with the strongest guarantees are rarely the ones that win benchmarks. Closing these gaps — adaptive kernels that learn their own misspecification, acquisition policies with multi-step optimality guarantees, and scalable surrogates that preserve calibrated uncertainty — is where the next decade of the field lies.
