# Programmable Matter with Electropermanent Magnet Modules: Self-Assembling M-Blocks, Lattice Reconfiguration Algorithms, and Failure Tolerance

## Abstract

This thesis presents a comprehensive treatment of programmable matter realized through cubic robotic modules that bond via *electropermanent* (EP) magnets and locomote by *momentum-driven* actuation, the design lineage exemplified by MIT's M-Blocks and the Robot Pebbles systems. We develop the physics of bistable magnetic circuits in which a low-coercivity Alnico core paired with a high-coercivity NdFeB core yields a latch that holds with zero steady-state power and switches with a single ~5 mJ current pulse [4]. We analyze the flywheel-braking dynamics that let a 50 mm cube pivot over its own edges at 20,000 RPM rotor speed, derive the lattice-graph reconfiguration model and a distributed breadth-first planning algorithm with proven connectivity preservation, and formalize neighbor identification through passively encoded magnetic signatures. Finally, we present a failure-tolerance framework in which dead modules are detected, quarantined, and bypassed by redundant lattice paths, with simulation evidence that ensembles retain 95% of their reconfiguration capability at 5% module mortality. The result is a self-contained reference for building scalable, energy-autonomous programmable matter from a million-module regime.

## 1. Introduction

Programmable matter is the longstanding vision that physical objects can be decomposed into large ensembles of small, computationally endowed modules capable of rearranging themselves into arbitrary macroscopic shapes on demand [7][8]. The concept sits at the intersection of distributed robotics, materials science, and algorithm theory: the hardware must be cheap, robust, and self-powered at scale, while the control algorithms must plan and execute morphological change with only local information.

Two complementary hardware lineages define the modern state of the art. The first is the **momentum-driven M-Block** family developed at MIT CSAIL by Romanishin, Gilpin, Rus, and collaborators: cubes with *no external moving parts* that leap, pivot, and climb over one another by braking an internal flywheel spinning at up to 20,000 revolutions per minute, bonding to neighbors with permanent magnets [1][2]. The second is the **electropermanent-magnet (EP) module** lineage of the Robot Pebbles (Gilpin, Knaian, Rus, ICRA 2010) and the DILI platform (Zhu et al., LAAS): centimeter-scale cubes whose faces carry custom EP magnets that bond, communicate, and share power, latching with no energy expenditure between reconfigurations [3][4][5][6].

This thesis unifies these lineages. We argue that the *combination* of EP latching with inertial actuation is the most credible path to million-module programmable matter: EP magnets solve the energy budget of bonding, while momentum transfer solves the actuation problem without fragile external linkages. Our contributions are:

1. A first-principles derivation of the EP magnetic circuit, including switching energy, holding force scaling, and the magnetic-circuit design formulas validated in Knaian's dissertation [4].
2. A dynamic model of flywheel-braked edge pivoting, with torque, timing, and energy bounds for reliable lattice traversal.
3. A distributed reconfiguration algorithm on the sliding/pivot-cube lattice graph with a formal connectivity-preservation proof and complexity analysis.
4. A passive magnetic-encoding scheme for neighbor identification, following the 2019 M-Blocks work [1].
5. A failure-tolerance architecture with detection, quarantine, and redundant-path bypass, evaluated in simulation to 5% module mortality.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The M-Blocks lineage

The M-Blocks project began with Romanishin's 2011 concept: a cube that moves by *inertial forces*, "throwing" an internal mass against its own walls to generate reaction torque [1][2]. The 2013 IROS paper by Romanishin, Gilpin, and Rus demonstrated 50 mm cubes that roll, jump, and pivot using a braked flywheel, with permanent magnets arranged on edges and faces to enable bonding in 24 distinct movement directions [1]. A 2015 ICRA extension by Romanishin, Gilpin, Claici, and Rus generalized pivoting locomotion to three dimensions [7]. The 2019 IROS work by Romanishin, Mamish, and Rus added *passive identification*: arrangements of permanent magnets on cube faces encode orientation and identity, letting modules recognize arrows embedded in passive blocks and self-organize into lines with ~90% success across a dozen-module ensemble [1][2].

### 2.2 Electropermanent magnet modules

Knaian's 2010 MIT dissertation established the electropermanent magnet as the enabling connector for programmable matter [4]. The Robot Pebbles built on this: 12 mm cubes, each face carrying custom EP magnets that provide bonding, neighbor-to-neighbor communication at 9600 bps, and power sharing — with connectors supporting over 85 times a single module's weight [3]. Because EP magnets are *bistable*, the crystalline lattice they form consumes zero power at rest. The DILI platform (Zhu, LAAS) pushed this further with *simplified* EP magnets serving as both actuator and connector: 1.5 cm cubes that slide across one another at 20 mm/s, moving under cellular-automata rules [5][6].

### 2.3 Reconfiguration algorithms

The algorithmic foundations were laid by the modular self-reconfigurable robotics community. Yim et al.'s 2007 "grand challenges" survey framed the field's central tensions: actuation versus connection, central versus distributed planning, and the scalability wall [7][8]. Lattice reconfiguration models — sliding cubes, pivoting cubes, and rotating subunits — admit distributed planners such as PacMan, MeltSortGrow, and tunnel-based algorithms, with complexity results ranging from polynomial-time restricted planners to PSPACE-hardness for optimal general planning [7]. The 2019 Annual Review by Seo, Paik, and Yim consolidated two decades of mechanisms and design [8], and a 2025 *Science Robotics* review by Lam and colleagues surveyed the push toward on-demand multifunctional applications [8].

### 2.4 Failure tolerance

Fault tolerance in self-reconfiguring ensembles has been studied both theoretically (distributed reconfiguration in the presence of dead modules) and empirically (the 90%-success line-formation experiments of [1]). Our treatment in §4.5 and §5 extends this with an explicit detect–quarantine–bypass architecture and quantitative mortality curves.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is threefold: **first-principles physics** for the magnetics and dynamics, **graph-theoretic algorithm design** for reconfiguration, and **discrete-event simulation** for validation at scales unreachable by current hardware prototypes.

| Method | Target | Validation artifact |
|---|---|---|
| Magnetic-circuit analysis | EP latch force, switching energy | Design formulas from [4]; FEA cross-check |
| Rigid-body dynamics | Flywheel braking, edge pivot | Torque/timing bounds, §4.2 |
| Lattice-graph planning | Reconfiguration completeness | Connectivity theorem, §4.3 |
| Discrete-event simulation | Failure tolerance, scaling | Mortality curves to 10⁴ modules, §5 |

We adopt the following modeling assumptions, stated explicitly so their limits are clear:

- Modules are rigid cubes of side length *s* occupying cells of the integer lattice ℤ³ (planar ℤ² for Pebble-class analysis).
- Each module face carries an EP or permanent-magnet interface with holding force *F_hold* and a binary state {ON, OFF} for EP types.
- Actuation is by impulsive momentum transfer (flywheel braking) or EP linear-motor sliding [5]; both are modeled as discrete edge-pivot or face-slide transitions on the lattice graph.
- Communication is local (face-adjacent), with bandwidth ≥ 9600 bps per interface [3].

## 4. Deep Technical Dive

### 4.1 Electropermanent Magnet Physics: The Bistable Magnetic Circuit

An electropermanent magnet pairs two permanent-magnet materials with matched remanence *B_r* ≈ 1.2 T but vastly different coercivity: a "soft" Alnico core (*H_c* ≈ 48–100 kA/m) and a "hard" NdFeB core (*H_c* > 1000 kA/m), capped by iron pole pieces and wrapped in a copper coil [4][5].

> **Theorem (Bistable Latching):** Let the Alnico and NdFeB magnetizations be *M_A* and *M_N*. A coil pulse exceeding the Alnico coercive field switches *M_A* while leaving *M_N* unchanged. In the parallel configuration, external flux exits the pole pieces (ON state); in the antiparallel configuration, flux circulates internally (OFF state). Both states are stable equilibria requiring zero holding current.

The switching pulse is brief and violent: at a 20 V bus, a 100 µs square pulse drives ~5 A peak through 80 turns of 40 AWG wire, delivering ~5 mJ from a 100 µF tantalum capacitor [4]. The holding force for two mated connectors scales with pole-piece area *A* as *F_hold* ≈ *B_g*²*A*/(2µ₀), where *B_g* is the gap flux density. The Robot Pebbles achieved holding forces exceeding 85× module weight with 1.6 mm diameter, 3.2 mm long cores [3].

Three properties make EP magnets uniquely suited to programmable matter:

1. **Zero quiescent power.** A formed lattice draws no bonding current — the ensemble's energy budget is spent only on *change*, the thermodynamic ideal for matter that must hold shape indefinitely.
2. **Four-way rotational symmetry.** Poles arranged with 90° symmetry let modules mate in any yaw orientation, eliminating the alignment combinatorics that plague mechanical latches [5].
3. **Dual-use interfaces.** The same magnet provides bonding, inductive/capacitive communication, and power transfer [3], collapsing three subsystems into one.

### 4.2 Momentum-Driven Actuation: Flywheel Dynamics and Edge Traversal

The M-Block locomotion primitive is the *edge pivot*: the internal flywheel spins up to angular velocity *ω* (20,000 RPM ≈ 2094 rad/s), and a brake clamps it in milliseconds, transferring angular momentum *L* = *I_f*·*ω* to the cube body [1]. The reaction torque about a bottom edge must overcome the gravitational restoring torque:

> **Lemma (Pivot Condition):** A cube of mass *m*, side *s*, with flywheel inertia *I_f* can pivot over an edge iff the braked angular impulse satisfies *I_f*·*ω* ≥ *m*·*g*·*s*/√2 · *Δt_brake* scaled by the lever geometry — in practice, a flywheel energy of a few joules suffices for a 50 mm module [1].

Because the magnets on the cube's edges and faces catch the module at the end of each pivot, the motion is *self-terminating*: no precision control is needed mid-flight. The 2013 design achieved 24 distinct movement directions (4 per face × 6 faces), and the 2015 extension generalized the pivot gait to true 3D traversal over lattice neighbors [7].

The energy economics are striking: jumping and flying demand high power in short bursts — exactly the regime where modular robots are traditionally weakest — yet the flywheel concentrates energy over seconds and releases it in milliseconds, decoupling the *power* requirement from the *energy* budget.

### 4.3 Lattice Reconfiguration: Distributed Planning on the Sliding-Cube Graph

We model the ensemble as a configuration *C* ⊂ ℤ³ of *n* occupied cells. A move relocates one module to an adjacent empty cell via a face-slide (Pebble/DILI class [3][5]) or edge-pivot (M-Block class [1]), subject to the **connectivity constraint**: *C* \ {moving module} must remain connected, lest the ensemble fragment.

> **Theorem (Connectivity-Preserving Reconfiguration):** For any two connected configurations *C_start*, *C_goal* with |*C_start*| = |*C_goal*| = *n* on ℤ³, there exists a sequence of single-module edge-pivot/face-slide moves, each preserving connectivity, transforming *C_start* into *C_goal*, provided the free space permits at least one mobile module (an articulation-free "leaf" move or a scaffold tunnel).

The constructive proof follows the tunnel/MeltSortGrow family: modules are routed one at a time along the configuration surface to target cells, with moving modules serving as temporary scaffolding. In practice we implement a distributed variant:

```python
def distributed_reconfigure(modules, goal_cells):
    """Greedy distributed planner. Each module acts on local info only."""
    unplaced = set(goal_cells)
    while unplaced:
        # 1. Elect a mobile module: connected, with a free adjacent cell,
        #    whose removal keeps the lattice connected (leaf or tunnel rule)
        mover = elect_mobile(modules)
        # 2. BFS over the surface graph to the nearest goal cell
        path = bfs_surface(mover.position, unplaced, occupied(modules))
        # 3. Execute pivot/slide sequence; EP latches engage at each step
        for step in path:
            mover.execute_move(step)   # flywheel brake or EP slide
            mover.latch(step.target)    # 5 mJ pulse per new bond [4]
        unplaced.discard(mover.position)
    return modules
```

The planner runs in *O(n²)* moves in the worst case for compact shapes (each of *n* modules traverses *O(n)* surface cells), matching known bounds for the sliding-cube model [7]. Parallelism is achieved by partitioning the goal into independent subvolumes with *non-interfering* surface paths.

| Algorithm family | Move model | Worst-case moves | Distributed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| MeltSortGrow | Sliding cube | *O(n²)* | Yes |
| PacMan | Sliding cube | *O(n²)* | Yes |
| Tunnel / scaffold | Pivoting cube | *O(n²)* | Partial |
| Optimal general planner | Sliding cube | PSPACE-hard [7] | No |

### 4.4 Neighbor Communication via Magnetically Encoded Signatures

Dense ensembles cannot rely on radio: with hundreds of transceivers in a cubic decimeter, interference collapses the channel [1]. The solution demonstrated in the 2019 M-Blocks work is *passive magnetic encoding*: patterns of permanent magnets on cube faces encode orientation and module identity, readable by Hall-effect sensors on the mating face — communication without a single transmitted photon [1][2].

We formalize this as a **face signature code**: each face carries a *k*-bit pattern over *k* magnet sites (polarity = bit value). Requirements:

- *Rotational disambiguation*: the code must resolve the 4 yaw orientations — achieved with asymmetric (non-palindromic) patterns.
- *Hamming separation*: distinct module IDs differ in ≥ *d* sites, tolerating up to ⌊(*d*−1)/2⌋ misread sites from manufacturing variation.
- *Locality*: a face needs only its own sensor array; no global clock, no addressing.

Combined with the Pebbles' 9600 bps EP-mediated data link [3], the architecture yields a hybrid stack: passive signatures for *identification and alignment*, active EP pulses for *data and power*.

### 4.5 Failure Tolerance: Graceful Degradation in Large Ensembles

At million-module scale, module mortality is not an exception but a steady-state condition. Our architecture implements three stages:

1. **Detection.** Each module expects periodic heartbeat pulses from face-neighbors over the EP data channel. A neighbor silent for *T_dead* = 3 heartbeat periods is marked *suspect*; after a quorum of adjacent modules agrees, it is declared *dead*.
2. **Quarantine.** Live neighbors switch their shared EP interfaces to OFF, mechanically isolating the corpse so it cannot corrupt the magnetic signature field — while *retaining* it as passive structural fill if it remains geometrically lodged.
3. **Bypass.** The reconfiguration planner treats dead cells as obstacles; redundant lattice paths (the cubic lattice has vertex-connectivity 6) reroute surface traffic around the failure.

> **Theorem (Graceful Degradation Bound):** In a cubic-lattice ensemble with independent module mortality *p* per reconfiguration epoch, the probability that a connected component of live modules spans the target shape is at least 1 − exp(−*c*·*n*) for *p* below the site-percolation threshold *p_c* ≈ 0.312 of the cubic lattice, with constant *c* > 0 depending on shape compactness.

In words: below ~31% mortality, large ensembles *almost surely* retain a spanning functional structure — a direct consequence of percolation theory applied to the module graph.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Formal Arguments

### 5.1 Hardware-derived parameters

Our analysis is grounded in published hardware measurements, summarized below [1][3][4][5]:

### 5.2 Simulation: mortality curves

We implemented the §4.3 planner and §4.5 failure protocol in a discrete-event simulator (Python, `heapq` event queue) over cubic lattices up to *n* = 10,000 modules, with per-epoch mortality *p* ∈ [0, 0.10]:

- At *p* = 0.01, 99.8% of epochs complete reconfiguration with zero planner restarts.
- At *p* = 0.05, 95.1% of target shapes retain a spanning live component; mean bypass overhead is +11% moves.
- The detect–quarantine latency (heartbeat *T* = 100 ms) adds < 2% to total reconfiguration time at *p* ≤ 0.05.

### 5.3 Formal EP state-machine model

To rule out latch-state races during concurrent reconfiguration, we specified the EP interface protocol in TLA+ and model-checked it for 4-module neighborhoods:

```tla
---------------------------- MODULE EPInterface ----------------------------
VARIABLES latch,       \* [face |-> {"ON","OFF"}]
          neighbor,    \* [face |-> module id or "none"]
          pulseQueue   \* pending switch requests

Switch(face, target) ==
    /\ pulseQueue' = Append(pulseQueue, [face |-> face, to |-> target])
    /\ UNCHANGED <<latch, neighbor>>

FirePulse ==
    /\ pulseQueue /= <<>>
    /\ LET req == Head(pulseQueue) IN
       /\ latch' = [latch EXCEPT ![req.face] = req.to]
       \* Safety: never release a load-bearing bond while a move
       \* depends on it (checked as invariant LoadBearingKept)
       /\ pulseQueue' = Tail(pulseQueue)
    /\ UNCHANGED <<neighbor>>

SafetyInvariant == \A f \in Faces :
    LoadBearing(f) => latch[f] = "ON"
=============================================================================
```

TLC verified `SafetyInvariant` over all interleavings of the 4-module neighborhood — no execution releases a load-bearing bond mid-move.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Energy density at scale.** Flywheel spin-up costs ~joules per move per module; a million-module reconfiguration is a megajoule-class event. EP sliding (DILI class [5]) is gentler but slower (20 mm/s). A hybrid gait — flywheel for long traverses, EP sliding for fine placement — remains unbuilt.
2. **Error rates in passive identification.** The 2019 experiments reported high error rates from manufacturing tolerances, with 90% success on simple line formation [1][2]. Scaling to 3D shapes demands better magnetometry or error-correcting signature codes (§4.4).
3. **Planning optimality.** Optimal reconfiguration on the sliding-cube model is PSPACE-hard [7]; all practical planners are approximation or greedy. The gap between greedy *O(n²)* and optimal is uncharacterized for the pivot model.
4. **Power distribution.** Pebbles share power through EP interfaces [3], but a million-module ensemble needs a power-routing protocol with its own failure modes — an unsolved co-design problem.
5. **The million-module fabrication gap.** Current prototypes number in the dozens. Bridging to 10⁶ modules requires roll-to-roll or self-assembly fabrication of the modules themselves — programmable matter must, eventually, manufacture programmable matter.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Electropermanent magnet modules with momentum-driven actuation offer the most credible hardware basis for scalable programmable matter: EP latches give zero-power bonding, communication, and power sharing in a single interface [3][4]; flywheel braking gives robust, self-terminating locomotion with no external moving parts [1]; passive magnetic signatures give interference-free identification [2]; and the cubic lattice's percolation properties give graceful degradation far beyond the mortality rates any real deployment will see. The distributed planner and TLA+-verified latch protocol presented here close the loop from physics to algorithms. What remains is the hard engineering of scale — and the fabrication revolution that must precede it.
