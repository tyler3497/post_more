# Multiparty Session Types: Linear-Logic Foundations, Global Protocols, Projection, and Deadlock-Freedom

## Abstract

Session types are a typed discipline for structured, communication-centered programming in which protocols are types: a channel's session type prescribes the entire conversation that must occur over it. This thesis develops the full arc from binary session types to multiparty session types, from their linear-logic foundations in the Curry-Howard correspondence to the engineering of deadlock-free distributed protocols. We present the Caires-Pfenning interpretation of intuitionistic linear logic propositions as session types, where proofs are processes and cut elimination is communication, and Wadler's classical variant, and show how deadlock freedom is inherited from the logic's normalization. We then develop multiparty session types after Honda, Yoshida, and Carbone: global types that choreograph interactions among many roles, endpoint projection with its merging operator, and the subject reduction, session fidelity, and progress theorems. Finally we survey session-type inference and embedding in real languages such as Rust and Haskell via Ferrite and Rumpsteak, and the mechanization of the metatheory in Coq and Agda, closing with a critical account of the open problems that remain.

---

## 1 Intro

Concurrent and distributed software fails most often not in its sequential logic but in its communication: messages sent to channels no one listens to, protocols violated midway through a session, deadlocks in which every participant waits for another. Session types [1] attack this failure mode at the type level. Where an ordinary type describes the shape of a value, a session type describes the *shape of a conversation*: send an integer, then receive a boolean, then terminate. Because the type is linear, the channel can be used exactly as prescribed, neither dropped nor duplicated, and the type checker becomes a protocol verifier.

The theory matured in two great waves. The first wave, binary session types, gave two-party dialogues a rigorous semantics and a practical type system. The second wave was logical: Caires and Pfenning [2] discovered that intuitionistic linear logic propositions *are* session types under the Curry-Howard lens, so that cut elimination *is* communication and deadlock freedom falls out of normalization; Wadler [3] then recast the correspondence classically. The third and most practically consequential wave, and the main subject of this thesis, is the multiparty generalization of Honda, Yoshida, and Carbone [1]: *global types* that choreograph the entire interaction of many roles, together with *projection*, the algorithm that derives each participant's local session type from the global description, with guarantees of communication safety, session fidelity, and progress.

This thesis unifies these strands. Our contributions are expository and analytic:

1. A self-contained presentation of the linear-logic foundations, contrasting the intuitionistic (Caires-Pfenning) and classical (Wadler) readings and their differing guarantees.
2. A technical development of multiparty session types: global type syntax, the projection relation with its merge operator, linearity, and the proof architecture of subject reduction and progress.
3. A survey of how session types are inferred and embedded in real languages (Rust via Ferrite and Rumpsteak, Haskell and Links lineage), with quantitative evaluation data.
4. An account of mechanized metatheory in Coq and Agda, including the subtle soundness traps that mechanization uncovered.

## 2 Background

### Binary session types

A binary session type describes one end of a two-party conversation. The grammar, in the style of Honda's original formulation, is

```haskell
data Session
  = Send Type Session    -- !T.S : send a T, continue as S
  | Recv Type Session    -- ?T.S : receive a T, continue as S
  | Select [(Label, Session)]  -- (+) internal choice
  | Offer  [(Label, Session)]  -- (&) external choice
  | End                    -- termination
```

Every type has a *dual*: `Send` dualizes to `Recv`, `Select` to `Offer`, and `End` to `End`. Two endpoints of a channel are typed dually, so that when one sends, the other receives, and when one selects, the other offers. Linearity is non-negotiable: a session channel must be consumed exactly once along its type, which rules out both protocol abandonment and double-use.

The operational semantics is the pi-calculus. The typing judgment `Gamma |- P` says that process `P` uses channels according to `Gamma`. The two crown theorems of the binary theory are *subject reduction* (types are preserved by reduction) and *session fidelity* (the communication actually performed agrees with the type), plus *progress* (a well-typed, non-terminated process configuration never gets stuck).

### Linear logic in brief

Linear logic, introduced by Girard, treats assumptions as resources: the structural rules of weakening and contraction are not admissible, so every hypothesis must be used exactly once. The connectives split into *multiplicatives* (tensor `(*)`, lollipop `(-o)`) governing concurrent, resource-sensitive composition, and *additives* (with `(&)`, plus `(+))`) governing choice. The exponential `!A` reintroduces controlled sharing. The crucial rule is *cut*: from a proof of `A` and a proof of `A |- B`, one obtains a proof of `B`, and *cut elimination* shows every proof normalizes to a cut-free one. It is this normalization that will become deadlock freedom.

### Global protocols and choreography

Binary session types suffice for client-server dialogues but not for protocols with three or more parties, such as a broker mediating between buyer and seller, or a consensus round among replicas. The naive approach of composing pairwise session types loses the global picture: the correctness of the whole is no longer checkable from the parts. Multiparty session types restore it by writing the protocol once, *globally*, as a choreography, and projecting it onto each role.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is threefold.

**Logical reconstruction.** We present the Caires-Pfenning [2] judgmental interpretation of intuitionistic linear logic as a session-typed pi-calculus, and Wadler's classical reading [3], isolating precisely where each obtains its deadlock-freedom guarantee and where the two readings differ in expressiveness. The logic supplies not only the type system but the *proof technique*: subject reduction becomes cut reduction, and progress becomes cut elimination.

**Choreographic type theory.** Following Honda, Yoshida, and Carbone [1], we develop global types `G`, the projection relation `G |r T` (global type `G` projects onto local type `T` for role `r`), the merging operator that reconciles the views of non-participating roles, and the linearity condition that rules out races on shared channels. The metatheory is organized around the standard triad: subject reduction, session fidelity, progress.

**Evidence from practice and mechanization.** We survey embeddings of session types in real languages, notably the Ferrite embedding of session types in Rust [4] and the Rumpsteak framework for asynchronous multiparty session types in Rust [5], and we review the fully mechanized metatheories in Coq [6] and Agda, including recent multiparty subject-reduction proofs [7] and synthetic reconstructions [8]. Throughout, claims about the literature are checked against the primary sources.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Propositions as sessions: the linear-logic correspondence

The breakthrough of Caires and Pfenning [2] is a *tight operational correspondence*: intuitionistic linear logic propositions are session types, proofs are processes, and cut elimination steps are communication steps. Under their interpretation, the logical connectives acquire a session reading:

| Linear logic connective | Session-type reading |
|---|---|
| `A (*) B` (tensor) | send a channel of type `A`, continue as `B` |
| `A -o B` (lollipop) | receive a channel of type `A`, continue as `B` |
| `A & B` (with) | external choice: offer `A` or `B` |
| `A (+) B` (plus) | internal choice: select `A` or `B` |
| `1` (one) | terminated session |
| `!A` (of course) | replicated (shared) server providing `A` |

The typing rules of the session-typed pi-calculus *coincide* with the sequent rules of intuitionistic linear logic; a typed process is literally a proof term. Consequently:

> **Theorem:** (Deadlock freedom by cut elimination [2].) A well-typed closed process configuration either reduces or is terminated; its reduction sequence mirrors the cut-elimination sequence of the corresponding proof, and hence cannot deadlock.

This is a remarkable inversion of the usual situation: instead of *proving* deadlock freedom as a difficult property of an ad hoc system, one *inherits* it from a century of proof theory. Wadler [3] gave the classical counterpart: using one-sided sequents of classical linear logic, the calculus CP, with a single output construct and a single input construct dual to each other, achieving greater symmetry at the cost of a subtler relationship to standard session-typed languages. Wadler further presented GV, a session-typed functional language, with a translation into CP that, for the first time, formalized the connection between a *standard* presentation of session types and a *standard* presentation of linear logic. A notable expressiveness result [3] is that the intuitionistic reading enforces *locality* of shared channels while the classical reading admits processes that break locality; there is a strict inclusion between the typable classes, later formalized via United Linear Logic.

The price of the logical approach is also logical in character: processes whose interactions correspond to proofs with arbitrarily long cut-reduction sequences, notably some unbounded interactions, are inherently ill-typed. Extensions exist, but they must negotiate with the logic itself.

### 4.2 Global types, projection, and the merge operator

A global type `G` describes the choreography of an entire multiparty session [1]:

```
G ::= p -> q : <U> . G        -- p sends q a value of sort U, continue as G
    | p -> q : { l_i : G_i }  -- p offers q a labeled choice
    | mu t . G | t            -- recursion
    | end
```

A global type is a *shared agreement* among all participants: every role sees the same `G`. From `G`, each role `r` derives its local session type via the *projection* relation `G |r T`, defined coinductively [1]:

- If `r` is not a participant of `G`, its projection is `end`.
- If `G = p -> r : { l_i(S_i) . G_i }` (message to `r`), the projection is an *external choice* over the labels, receiving each payload and continuing with `G_i |r T_i`.
- If `G = r -> q : { l_i(S_i) . G_i }` (message from `r`), the projection is an *internal choice*.
- If `G = p -> q : ...` with `r` distinct from both, then `r` must project each continuation `G_i` and *merge* the results.

The merge operator is the subtle heart of projection.

Linearity of global types rules out a different pathology: races on explicit channels.

### 4.3 Deadlock freedom, fidelity, and progress

For multiparty sessions the three fundamental properties are [1]:

1. **Communication safety.** Well-typed processes never perform a communication error: no arity mismatch, no label mismatch, no receiving on a channel whose peer has closed.
2. **Session fidelity.** The observable communication of a well-typed process follows the projected local type, and therefore the global choreography: the processes really do enact the protocol they were checked against.
3. **Progress.** A well-typed session never deadlocks: either every participant has reached `end`, or some communication step is enabled. In the asynchronous setting this requires care, because buffered messages decouple send from receive.

The asynchronous calculus of [1] equips each session with message queues; sends enqueue and receives dequeue.

Asynchronous multiparty session *subtyping* refines the picture further: a local implementation may reorder sends before receives relative to the projection (a standard optimization for overlapping computation and communication) as long as the reordering is safe.

### 4.4 Session-type inference and embedding in real languages

Session types began as a calculus-level discipline, but their adoption depends on embedding in real languages without burdening programmers.

**Inference.** The classical question is how much of a session type can be *inferred* rather than annotated. For the functional session-typed language of Gay and Vasconcelos, the contextual-metaprogramming approach of [6] gives type schemes for the communication constants (`send`, `receive`, `select`, `new`, `fork`) in a linear lambda calculus: `send` takes a value and a channel and returns the continuation channel, with linearity on the second arrow; `receive` returns a pair of value and continuation channel. Context *splitting* distributes linear resources between subderivations. Full inference remains delicate: equirecursive session types make type equivalence undecidable in general, though sound algorithms exist for restricted fragments, and context-free session types recover decidability with a bidirectional algorithmic system.

**Embedding in Rust.** Rust's affine type system is a natural host for linear session discipline, and several embeddings exploit it:

- *Session Types for Rust* (Laumann) encodes duality at the type level: `Send<A, P>` dualizes to `Recv<A, P::Dual>`, and Rust's trait resolution computes duals automatically.
- *Ferrite* [4] takes the judgmental methodology seriously: session types are embedded as a typed DSL in Rust whose combinators correspond to the typing rules of the underlying logic, so ill-typed protocols are ill-typed Rust programs. Its evaluation re-implemented the communication layer of the Servo browser engine's canvas component (roughly 750 lines added and 620 deleted out of 300,000) and demonstrated smooth animation frame rates, showing the embedding is not a toy.
- *Rumpsteak* [5] targets asynchronous `async`/`await` Rust with multiparty session types, incorporating *k-multiparty compatibility* for global safety and asynchronous subtyping for local optimization. Benchmarked against three previous Rust session-type implementations, Rumpsteak was 1.7 to 8.6 times more efficient while expressing strictly more examples, and its subtyping algorithm decisively outperformed the alternatives it was benchmarked against.

```rust
// Sketch of the duality encoding (after the Rust session-types tradition)
trait HasDual { type Dual: HasDual; }
struct Send<A, P: HasDual>(PhantomData<(A, P)>);
struct Recv<A, P: HasDual>(PhantomData<(A, P)>);
impl<A, P: HasDual> HasDual for Send<A, P> {
    type Dual = Recv<A, P::Dual>;
}
type Atm = Recv<String, Choose<Rec<AtmInner>, Eps>>;
type Client = <Atm as HasDual>::Dual; // computed, not written by hand
```

### 4.5 Mechanized metatheory: Coq, Agda, and the traps mechanization found

Session-type metatheory is notoriously subtle, and mechanization has repeatedly paid for itself by finding real bugs.

At the multiparty level, Tirore, Bengtson, and Carbone [7] give a mechanized proof of subject reduction for multiparty asynchronous session types, formalizing traces, the linearity condition that rules out channel races, and the projection theorem in a proof assistant.

The lesson of this line of work is methodological: for substructural, name-binding-heavy metatheory, the mechanized proof is not a certificate of a paper proof but a *discovery instrument* that refines the theory itself.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

We now state the central theorems with proof sketches and quantitative evidence.

> **Theorem 1** (Subject reduction, multiparty asynchronous [1]). If `Gamma |- P` with `Gamma` coherent for global type `G`, and `P -> P'`, then there exist `Gamma'` and `G'` with `Gamma' |- P'`, where `G'` is the reduct of `G` and `Gamma'` is coherent for `G'`.

*Proof sketch.* By induction on the reduction derivation. The communication case uses the duality of the projected endpoints and the queue typing invariant: a send enqueues a message of the payload sort, and the receiver's type head matches the queue head by coherence. The key lemma is *queue-type correspondence*: the concatenation of queue contents with the receiver's remaining type is dual-consistent with the sender's remaining type. Recursion is handled coinductively via the equirecursive reading of `mu`-types. The mechanized version [7] formalizes this as a trace-based argument over global-type reductions.

> **Theorem 2** (Session fidelity [1]). The sequence of communications performed by a well-typed process is a prefix of the traces of its global type.

*Proof sketch.* Each reduction step consumes exactly the head interaction of the current global type (Theorem 1), so the induced communication sequence is a trace of `G` by construction.

> **Theorem 3** (Progress [1]). If `Gamma |- P` with `Gamma` coherent and non-terminated, then `P -> P'` for some `P'`.

*Proof sketch.* Coherence implies that the projected local types are pairwise dual and the queues are consistent. If every queue is empty and every local type is `end`, the session terminated; otherwise, some participant's type head is a send (enabled immediately) or a receive whose dual send is either already queued or enabled at the dual endpoint, so a redex exists.

> **Theorem 4** (Deadlock freedom from cut elimination [2][3]). Every closed well-typed process in the linear-logic session calculus reduces to a terminated configuration.

*Proof sketch.* Typing derivations are proofs; reductions are cut reductions; strong normalization of the logic yields termination, and the absence of stuck non-terminal configurations is exactly deadlock freedom.

**Quantitative evidence.** The Rumpsteak evaluation [5] provides the hard numbers: against three prior Rust session-type implementations, Rumpsteak achieved a 1.7x to 8.6x efficiency improvement while supporting arbitrary asynchronous message reordering that the others reject; its novel asynchronous subtyping algorithm, proved sound and decidable, was benchmarked exponentially faster than both a k-multiparty-compatibility checker and a binary subtyping algorithm on the same workloads. The Ferrite evaluation [4] demonstrated feasibility at scale by porting Servo's canvas communication layer (750 additions, 620 deletions across 300,000 lines of Rust) with no loss of the 24-frames-per-second animation budget.

A minimal global protocol, for concreteness:

```
G_auth =  C -> S : <login> .
           S -> C : { ok   : C -> S : <request> . S -> C : <response> . end,
                      fail : end }
```

Projection onto `C` yields `!<login> . &{ ok: !<request> . ?<response> . end, fail: end }`, and onto `S` its dual `?<login> . (+){ ok: ?<request> . !<response> . end, fail: end }`. The merge operator is not needed here since `C` and `S` both participate in every interaction; a third-party auditor role projecting this `G` would merge the `ok` and `fail` branches and discover the merge undefined, correctly rejecting the choreography as unobservable to an outsider.

## 6 Limitations

The theory, for all its elegance, has real boundaries.

1. **Expressiveness of projection.** Many natural global protocols are unprojectable: any choreography in which a role must act on information it cannot observe is rejected, and the merge operator's partiality is a genuine restriction, not a technicality. Weaker notions of projectability exist but complicate the metatheory.
2. **The linearity tax.** Exact-once channel usage is the source of all the guarantees and of much of the ergonomic pain. Real protocols need failure, timeout, and cancellation; integrating exceptions with session fidelity required dedicated extensions (e.g., the Links-based work with effect handlers), and the interaction of failure with linearity remains delicate.
3. **Inference gaps.** Full type inference for equirecursive session types is undecidable in general; practical systems rely on annotations, bidirectional checking, or restricted fragments such as context-free session types where equivalence is decidable. Rust embeddings additionally fight the borrow checker and the lack of linear types in the host language.
4. **Asynchrony and subtyping complexity.** Asynchronous subtyping is undecidable in its full generality; decidable fragments (as in Rumpsteak) buy decidability by restricting reordering patterns. The k-multiparty compatibility check is similarly expensive in the worst case.
5. **Mechanization scale.** The Coq and Agda developments cover core calculi, not production embeddings; the gap between the mechanized core and the Rust or Links implementation is bridged by trust, not proof. Binder representations (locally nameless, de Bruijn) remain a major cost driver.
6. **Beyond the logic.** The linear-logic reading inherits the logic's blind spots: unbounded or non-terminating interactions that are operationally sensible can be ill-typed, and extensions that recover them must weaken the very correspondence that made the theory attractive.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Session types began as a clever typing discipline for pi-calculus channels and became, through the linear-logic correspondence, a chapter of proof theory; through multiparty session types, they became an engineering discipline for distributed protocols. The arc is satisfying: write the protocol once as a global type, project it to each role, and obtain communication safety, session fidelity, and progress by theorem rather than by testing. The embeddings in Rust and the mechanized metatheories in Coq and Agda show the theory surviving contact with reality, and the quantitative results demonstrate that the guarantees need not cost performance.

The open frontier is making the discipline *invisible*: inference that recovers protocols from code, failure handling that preserves fidelity, and verified compilation from mechanized cores to running systems. Until then, multiparty session types remain what they have always been at their best: the rare concurrency theory whose guarantees are strong enough to be worth the discipline they demand.
