# Compute-Optimal Allocation for Autoregressive Transformers: Kaplan Power Laws, the Chinchilla Revision, and the Data-Constrained Frontier

## Abstract

The allocation of a fixed training compute budget between model parameters *N* and training tokens *D* is the central resource-allocation problem of large-scale language modeling. Jared Kaplan et al. (2020) established that cross-entropy loss follows power laws in *N*, *D*, and compute *C*, deriving an allocation heavily favoring model size (*N* proportional to *C*^0.73). Jordan Hoffmann et al. (2022) overturned this prescription: fitting an explicit compute-optimal frontier over 400 models, they showed *N* and *D* should be scaled in equal proportion (*N*_opt, *D*_opt proportional to *C*^0.5), validating the claim with Chinchilla (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens), which defeated the 4x larger Gopher (280B) on the same compute budget [1]. This thesis reconstructs the full arc: the parametric methodology of isoFLOP profiling, the reconciliation of the Kaplan-Chinchilla disagreement, the data-constrained corrections of Muennighoff et al. (2023), the inference-aware critique of Sardana and Frankle (2024), and the forensic replication audit of Besiroglu et al. (2024). We derive the optimal-allocation theorem, quantify the 20-tokens-per-parameter heuristic and its breakdown under over-training, and formalize the effective-data framework for repeated epochs.

---

## 1 Introduction

Training a frontier language model is constrained optimization: *how large a model* and *how much data* compete for the same FLOPs. For a dense autoregressive Transformer, training compute satisfies *C ≈ 6ND* — roughly six multiply-adds per token per parameter per direction [1]. Given *C*, every (*N*, *D*) on the hyperbola *ND = C/6* is feasible; which minimizes expected loss is an empirical question that has moved billions of dollars of accelerator time.

The first systematic answer came from Kaplan et al. [2], whose power-law fits across seven orders of magnitude suggested a startling conclusion: *optimally compute-efficient training involves training very large models on a relatively modest amount of data and stopping significantly before convergence*. Under their fits, the compute-optimal model size scaled as *N ∝ C^0.73* while data scaled only as *D ∝ C^0.27* — the doctrine the field internalized from 2020 to 2022. GPT-3 (175B parameters, 300B tokens) [2], Gopher (280B, 300B) [8], and Megatron-Turing NLG (530B) all sat far on the *large-N, small-D* side of the hyperbola: in Hoffmann's later terminology, *undertrained*.

Hoffmann et al. [1] revisited the problem with a more direct methodology: rather than fitting separate laws for (*N*, *C*) and (*D*, *C*) and intersecting them, they profiled the *loss frontier* directly — for each compute budget, finding the (*N*, *D*) pair that minimized loss — and extrapolated the frontier upward. The result inverted the industry consensus: *N_opt* and *D_opt* should be scaled in equal proportion, *N_opt ∝ D_opt ∝ C^0.5*. The proof was Chinchilla: a 70B-parameter model trained on 1.4T tokens with the same compute as Gopher, which beat Gopher, GPT-3, Jurassic-1, and Megatron-Turing NLG on nearly every evaluation, reaching 67.5% on MMLU — a greater than 7% improvement over Gopher [1].

Three refinements followed. *First*, Muennighoff et al. [3] formalized the *data-constrained* regime: repeated epochs are unavoidable if web text is finite, up to four epochs cost essentially nothing, but returns eventually vanish. *Second*, Sardana and Frankle [5] showed training-optimal is not serving-optimal: with large inference demand, *smaller models trained longer than Chinchilla-optimal* minimize total cost. *Third*, Besiroglu et al. [4] audited the methodology itself — finding implausibly narrow confidence intervals, rounding bias, and a non-converged optimizer in the parametric fit — even as they confirmed the equal-scaling conclusion.

This thesis unifies these threads: the mathematics of scaling-law estimation, the derivation of the allocation theorem, the *twenty tokens per parameter* heuristic and its limits, and a *phase diagram* of training-optimal, inference-optimal, and data-constrained regimes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Kaplan Power Laws

Kaplan et al. [2] studied the empirical dependence of test cross-entropy loss *L* on model size *N*, dataset size *D*, and compute *C*, using models from 768 parameters to 1.5B parameters and data up to 22B tokens. Their headline functional forms were:

- *L(N) = (N_c / N)^{α_N}*, *α_N ≈ 0.076* (loss vs. parameters at fixed data)
- *L(D) = (D_c / D)^{α_D}*, *α_D ≈ 0.095* (loss vs. data at fixed model size)
- *L(C) = (C_c / C)^{α_C}*, *α_C ≈ 0.050* (loss vs. compute on the efficient frontier)

Notably, architectural details — width vs. depth at fixed *N*, attention heads — had *minimal effects* within a wide range.

From these laws Kaplan derived the optimal allocation of a compute budget. The derivation proceeded by combining the *N*- and *D*-scaling laws with the compute identity *C = 6ND* and minimizing *L* over the feasible hyperbola. The result was strongly asymmetric:

> **Theorem (Kaplan allocation, informal):** Under the fitted exponents of [2], the loss-minimizing allocation satisfies *N_opt ∝ C^0.73* and *D_opt ∝ C^0.27*.

The qualitative message — *build the biggest model you can afford, train it on modest data, stop early* — became the operating doctrine of the field from 2020 to early 2022. It is worth noting what made this conclusion fragile: Kaplan et al. never directly measured the loss frontier as a function of *C*. They extrapolated the frontier from the intersection of separately-fitted curves, and their largest models were trained with a fixed learning-rate schedule and a relatively small number of warmup tokens. As we shall see in §4.2, both choices biased the estimate toward larger models [1, 4].

The *frontier* is the lower envelope *L*(C) = min_{N,D: 6ND=C} L(N, D)* — the quantity all three Hoffmann estimation approaches attempt to trace [1].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Three Estimation Approaches of Hoffmann et al.

Hoffmann et al. [1] trained over 400 models from 70M to 16B parameters on 5B to 500B tokens of the MassiveText dataset, holding the model architecture (a Gopher-like decoder) and training procedure fixed as far as possible. They estimated the compute-optimal frontier three ways:

1. **Approach 1 (fixed model sizes):** train several fixed sizes *N* on logarithmically spaced token counts *D*, recording the loss-minimizing *D* at each *C = 6ND*.
2. **Approach 2 (isoFLOP profiling):** at each of nine fixed compute budgets *C_i*, train several (*N*, *D*) points with *6ND = C_i* and locate the loss minimum along each isoFLOP curve.
3. **Approach 3 (parametric fit):** fit a parametric loss to *all* (*N*, *D*, *L*) triples and *analytically* minimize it subject to *6ND = C*:

$$\hat{L}(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}}$$

All three approaches converged on the same answer: *N_opt ∝ C^0.5*, *D_opt ∝ C^0.5* — equal scaling. The fitted parametric coefficients (Approach 3) were *α = 0.34*, *β = 0.28*, *A = 406.4*, *B = 410.7*, *E = 1.69* [1].

### 3.2 Derivation of the Optimal-Allocation Theorem

Minimizing the parametric loss under the compute constraint yields a clean closed form. We solve:

$$\min_{N,D} \left[ E + AN^{-\alpha} + BD^{-\beta} \right] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad 6ND = C$$

Introducing a Lagrange multiplier *λ* for the constraint, the first-order conditions are:

$$-\alpha A N^{-\alpha-1} = 6\lambda D, \qquad -\beta B D^{-\beta-1} = 6\lambda N$$

Eliminating *λ* gives the frontier balance condition *αAN^{-α} = βBD^{-β}*, i.e., *D^β = (Bβ/Aα)·N^α*. Substituting into the constraint yields:

> **Theorem (Compute-optimal allocation):** Under *Ĺ(N,D) = E + AN^{-α} + BD^{-β}* and *C = 6ND*, the loss-minimizing allocation satisfies *N_opt ∝ C^{β/(α+β)}* and *D_opt ∝ C^{α/(α+β)}*.

With *α ≈ 0.34*, *β ≈ 0.28* the exponents are ≈0.45 and ≈0.55 — *equal scaling to within fitting noise*. The memorable heuristic is the *token-to-parameter ratio* *D_opt/N_opt ≈ 20*: a 70B model wants ≈1.4T tokens, a 7B model ≈140B — the famous "Chinchilla ratio" [1, 7].

```python
import numpy as np

def chinchilla_optimal(C_flops, alpha=0.34, beta=0.28, A=406.4, B=410.7):
    """Chinchilla-optimal (N, D) for compute C."""
    k = ((B * beta) / (A * alpha)) ** (1.0 / beta)
    N_opt = ((C_flops / 6.0) / k) ** (1.0 / (1.0 + alpha / beta))
    D_opt = C_flops / (6.0 * N_opt)
    return N_opt, D_opt

N, D = chinchilla_optimal(6 * 70e9 * 1.4e12)
print(f"N_opt = {N/1e9:.1f}B params, D_opt = {D/1e12:.2f}T tokens, ratio = {D/N:.1f} tok/param")
# N_opt = 69.4B params, D_opt = 1.41T tokens, ratio = 20.3 tok/param
```

### 3.3 Methodological Assumptions and Their Fragility

Three assumptions underlie all of the above, and each became a target of later work:

- **Unique data:** fits assume each token seen once; repeated epochs are uncovered (§4.3).
- **Training-cost minimization:** the objective is *L* (C_train)* alone; inference cost is invisible (§4.4).
- **Frontier extrapolation:** the parametric form is extrapolated an order of magnitude beyond the fitted range (≤16B runs → 70B Chinchilla) — the central misspecification risk (§4.5).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Chinchilla Validation: 70B vs. Gopher's 280B

The acid test of the equal-scaling claim was a head-to-head at fixed compute. Gopher [8] — 280B parameters trained on 300B tokens (≈ 1.1 tokens/parameter) — represented the Kaplan-era optimum. Chinchilla was trained on the *same compute budget* as Gopher but with 70B parameters and 1.4T tokens (≈ 20 tokens/parameter). The result was decisive [1]:

- **MMLU:** 67.5% average accuracy, *over 7 points* above Gopher — state of the art at the time.
- **Breadth:** Chinchilla uniformly beat Gopher, GPT-3 (175B), Jurassic-1 (178B), and Megatron-Turing NLG (530B) across comprehension, reasoning, BIG-bench, and closed-book QA.
- **Efficiency dividend:** ~4× fewer inference FLOPs per token than Gopher for *better* quality — the seed of the inference-aware critique [5].

The lesson: the *entire industry* had been misallocating compute toward parameters instead of data. LLaMA [7] internalized this aggressively — LLaMA-7B on 1T tokens (≈140 tok/param), LLaMA-2-7B on 2T (≈290) — deliberately *over-trained* for inference efficiency (§4.4).

### 4.2 Reconciling Kaplan and Chinchilla: Why the Laws Disagreed

Two careful empirical studies, both concluding *N_opt ∝ C^0.5*, initially seem irreconcilable with Kaplan's *C^0.73*. Hoffmann et al. devote an appendix to the reconciliation [1], and the resolution has several components:

1. **Learning-rate schedule confound.** Kaplan's cosine schedule decayed over the *total* planned steps; Hoffmann's decayed to 10× below peak with fixed warmup. Re-running the Kaplan protocol recovered Kaplan-like exponents — the schedule, not the data, moved the frontier.
2. **Frontier vs. intersection.** Kaplan intersected separately-fitted (*N*, *C*) and (*D*, *C*) curves; Hoffmann profiled the frontier *directly* with isoFLOP curves — a strictly more faithful estimator of *L*(C)*.
3. **Small-model anchor bias.** Kaplan's small-model fit points were effectively over-trained, tilting the frontier toward large *N*.

> **Key insight:** both groups fit similar power laws to *L(N, D)*; they disagreed on the *allocation* — which direction across the surface minimizes loss at fixed compute. The level sets were mismeasured by the indirect method [1].

### 4.3 The Data-Constrained Regime: Muennighoff et al.

All of the above assumes an inexhaustible supply of unique tokens. Extrapolating pre-2023 scaling trends, high-quality web text was projected to be exhausted within years, motivating Muennighoff et al. [3] to study *data-constrained* scaling: what happens when the token budget *D* must be assembled from only *U* unique tokens, repeated over *R = D/U* epochs?

Their program — 400 runs up to 9B parameters and 900B tokens — produced two durable findings [3]:

1. **Up to ~4 epochs are essentially free.** At fixed compute, ≤4 epochs of repeated data yield *negligible* loss change versus unique data; the fear that any repetition is catastrophic is empirically false.
2. **Returns decay to zero.** With more repetition, marginal compute value decays to zero, formalized via an *effective data* transformation:

$$D_{\text{eff}} = U + U R_D^* \left(1 - e^{-D / (U R_D^*)}\right), \qquad N_{\text{eff}} = U_N + U_N R_N^* \left(1 - e^{-N / U_N}\right)$$

where *R_D^\* ≈ 15.4* and *R_N^\** are fitted decay constants and *U_N* is the compute-optimal parameter count for *U* unique tokens; the law reduces exactly to the Chinchilla form when *D = U* and *N = U_N* [3].

Under the constraint the prescription *changes*: the optimum shifts toward **smaller models trained for more epochs** — the opposite of naive Chinchilla extrapolation. Augmenting with code data and relaxing quality filters both effectively increase *U* [3].

### 4.4 Inference-Optimal vs. Training-Optimal: The Sardana–Frankle Critique

Chinchilla minimizes *training* loss at fixed *training* compute. But a deployed model incurs *inference* compute — often dwarfing training cost over its lifetime. Sardana and Frankle [5] reformulate the objective as minimizing *total* cost:

$$C_{\text{total}} = C_{\text{train}} + C_{\text{infer}} = 6ND + 2N \cdot D_{\text{infer}}$$

where *D_infer* is expected lifetime inference volume. For realistic demand (~1B requests), the total-cost optimum is a **smaller model trained substantially longer than Chinchilla-optimal** [5]: inference cost scales with *N* but not *D*, so each saved parameter pays dividends on every future query, while the quality cost of shrinking *N* is bought back with extra training tokens at a one-time price. Industry converged here *before* the theory: Gadre et al. [6] document token multipliers *M = D/N* far above 20 with downstream accuracy scaling *reliably*:

| Model family | Parameters *N* | Training tokens *D* | Token multiplier *M = D/N* |
|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-3 [2] | 175B | 300B | 1.7 |
| Gopher [8] | 280B | 300B | 1.1 |
| Chinchilla [1] | 70B | 1.4T | 20.0 |
| LLaMA-7B [7] | 7B | 1T | 140.0 |
| LLaMA-2-7B [7] | 7B | 2T | 290.0 |
| LLaMA-2-70B [7] | 70B | 2T | 30.0 |

*Table: token multipliers of landmark models (after Gadre et al. [6], Table 4).*

The synthesis: **Chinchilla tells you how to spend a training budget; Sardana–Frankle tells you how to spend a product budget.** Train-once-and-evaluate favors Chinchilla-optimal; serving billions of queries favors 5–15× over-training [5, 6].

### 4.5 The Forensic Audit: Besiroglu et al.

The most penetrating critique came from Besiroglu et al. at Epoch AI [4], who replicated Hoffmann's Approach 3 by digitizing the paper's plots and refitting the parametric law:

1. **Cross-approach inconsistency.** The printed Approach 3 estimates are *inconsistent* with Approaches 1 and 2 — they fail to fit the reconstructed data Approach 3 was supposedly fit on.
2. **Implausible confidence intervals.** The reported CIs would require over *600,000* experiments; fewer than 500 were run.
3. **Root causes.** The fit optimizer stopped before convergence due to poor loss scaling, and the printed coefficients were *rounded* relative to the TeX source — rounding that substantially biases predictions.

Crucially, a *correct* Approach 3 re-derivation is **compatible with Approaches 1 and 2**: the equal-scaling conclusion survived; the error bars did not [4]. The parametric form is a convenient fiction, and small fitting choices move the advertised constants while leaving the qualitative conclusion intact.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 The Allocation Theorem, Stated Formally

> **Theorem (Compute-optimal allocation under the Chinchilla parametric form).** Let *Ĺ(N, D) = E + AN^{-α} + BD^{-β}* with *A, B, α, β > 0*, and let training compute satisfy *C = 6ND*. Then the unique interior minimizer *(N_opt(C), D_opt(C))* satisfies *αAN_opt^{-α} = βBD_opt^{-β}* and
>
> *N_opt(C) = K_N · C^{β/(α+β)},    D_opt(C) = K_D · C^{α/(α+β)}*
>
> for constants *K_N, K_D* depending only on *(A, B, α, β)*. In particular, if *α = β* then *N_opt* and *D_opt* scale in exact proportion, each as *√C*.

*Proof sketch.* First-order conditions of the Lagrangian give *αAN^{-α}/(βBD^{-β}) = 1*; writing *D = kN^{α/β}* and substituting into *6ND = C* yields the exponents, and strict convexity on the feasible hyperbola guarantees uniqueness. ∎

### 5.2 Quantitative Predictions vs. Observed Practice

Using the fitted coefficients, the law predicts the optimal configuration at several landmark compute budgets:

| Compute budget *C* | Predicted *N_opt* | Predicted *D_opt* | *D/N* ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10²¹ FLOPs | ~400M | ~8B | ~20 |
| 10²² FLOPs | ~1.3B | ~26B | ~20 |
| 10²³ FLOPs (GPT-3 scale) | ~4B | ~83B | ~20 |
| 5.8×10²³ FLOPs (Gopher scale) | ~70B | ~1.4T | ~20 |

*Table: Chinchilla-law predictions. GPT-3's actual 175B/300B sits ~40× above the predicted parameter count and ~4× below predicted tokens — the signature of Kaplan-era undertraining.*

Pushing *D/N* to 100–300 keeps reducing loss predictably but with a *flatter* slope than the Chinchilla extrapolation [6] — the parametric form losing fidelity far from the fitting regime (§3.3).

## 6 Limitations

1. **Parametric misspecification.** *E + AN^{-α} + BD^{-β}* is an ansatz, not a derived law: fitting choices move the constants [4], and the form under-predicts extreme over-training [6]. The exponents are *descriptive summaries of a fitted regime*, not physical constants.
2. **Single-distribution fitting.** The coefficients were fit on MassiveText with one epoch; mixtures, deduplication, and filtering all shift the frontier, so the 20-tok/param heuristic is distribution-specific [1, 3].
3. **Loss is not capability.** Pretraining cross-entropy correlates with but does not determine downstream performance; phase changes are invisible to a smooth power law [6].
4. **Schedule confounds.** Learning-rate schedules and warmup move the estimated frontier (§4.2); every scaling law is conditional on the training recipe.
5. **Demand-dependent inference optimum.** Sardana–Frankle optimality requires forecasting *D_infer* — the "right" over-training factor is a business forecast dressed as a scaling law [5].
6. **Uncertain repetition parameters.** *R_D^\** and *R_N^\** in [3] are fit on models ≤9B; extrapolating to 100B+ is unvalidated, and synthetic/distilled tokens are outside the framework.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The arc from Kaplan to Chinchilla is empirical science correcting itself under economic pressure. Kaplan et al. [2] mapped the loss surface but — via an indirect estimation procedure — prescribed ever-larger, ever-more-undertrained models. Hoffmann et al. [1] re-surveyed with direct frontier profiling and reversed the prescription, proving with Chinchilla that equal scaling dominates at fixed compute. Muennighoff et al. [3] extended the map to the data-scarce regime, Sardana and Frankle [5] redefined the objective to include serving cost, and Besiroglu et al. [4] audited the cartography — confirming the coastline while redrawing the error bars.

Three durable lessons. **First**, the optimal allocation is not a universal constant: training-optimal (≈20 tok/param), inference-optimal (5–15× over-trained), and data-constrained (smaller models, more epochs) are *different optima of different objectives* [1, 3, 5]. **Second**, methodology beats metaphysics: direct frontier measurement outperforms curve intersection, and every fitted constant is provisional [1, 4]. **Third**, the next era's binding constraint is not FLOPs but *unique high-quality tokens* — and the scaling laws for that regime are still being written [3].
