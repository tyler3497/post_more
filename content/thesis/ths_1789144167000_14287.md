# Identifiability and Double Machine Learning for Causal Inference: Pearl's Do-Calculus, Back-Door and Front-Door Criteria, and Neyman-Orthogonal Estimation of Structural Parameters

## Abstract

This thesis develops the modern theory of causal effect identification and estimation from observational data, unifying Pearl's graphical/structural program with the semiparametric machine-learning program of modern econometrics. We begin with structural causal models (SCMs), the do-operator, and the three rules of do-calculus, proving how the back-door and front-door criteria reduce interventional queries $P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x))$ to ordinary statistical estimands. We then present Shpitser and Pearl's ID algorithm — the complete solution to identification in semi-Markovian models — and the hedge structure that witnesses non-identifiability. The second half turns from identification to estimation: we derive Neyman orthogonality as the antidote to regularization bias, develop double/debiased machine learning (DML) with cross-fitting, and prove that DML yields $\sqrt{N}$-consistent, asymptotically normal estimators even when nuisance functions are learned at $o(n^{-1/4})$ rates. A reproducible simulation study confirms the theory, and we close with the frontier's open problems: unmeasured confounding, positivity violations, and the hardness of interventional evaluation.

**Keywords:** causal inference, do-calculus, structural causal models, identifiability, Neyman orthogonality, double machine learning, semiparametric estimation.

##1 Introduction

Science has always demanded more than prediction. A model that predicts loan defaults from credit scores does not tell a bank whether lowering interest rates would *cause* fewer defaults; a recommender system that predicts clicks does not tell engineers whether a layout change would *cause* more engagement. This gap — between *seeing* ($P(y \mid x)$) and *doing* ($P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x))$) — is the founding distinction of causal inference [1][6].

The tragedy of observational data is that treatment is not randomized: selection into treatment shares causes with the outcome, so naive regression mixes causal effect with **confounding bias**. Two mathematical cultures arose to disentangle them. The *structural* culture, pioneered by Pearl [1], encodes causal assumptions in graphs and structural equations, turning identification into graphical logic. The *potential outcomes* culture, descended from Neyman and Rubin, encodes assumptions as conditional independencies between potential outcomes and treatment assignment. These cultures are mathematically compatible [5][7], but the structural program contributes something the potential-outcome program cannot state natively: a *complete, algorithmic* answer to "can this causal query be computed from this graph and this observational distribution at all?" — the **identifiability problem**.

This thesis has three goals. **First**, to present Pearl's do-calculus and the identifiability machinery with proof-level precision. **Second**, to show that identification is only half the battle: estimating identified functionals with modern machine learning introduces regularization bias and overfitting that classical semiparametric theory cannot absorb, and to show how **Neyman-orthogonal scores** plus **cross-fitting** resolve this, yielding double/debiased machine learning [2][4]. **Third**, to demonstrate empirically that the theory binds in practice.

> **Theorem: (Informal roadmap)** Under an identified structural causal model, the interventional distribution is a functional of the observational distribution. If that functional admits a Neyman-orthogonal representation and nuisance components converge faster than $n^{-1/4}$, the DML estimator is $\sqrt{N}$-consistent and asymptotically normal with variance attaining the semiparametric efficiency bound [2].

The remaining sections: §2 reviews SCMs, DAGs, and interventions; §3 develops do-calculus and the identification criteria; §4 derives Neyman orthogonality, cross-fitting, and DML; §5 gives proof sketches and a simulation study; §6 states limitations; §7 concludes.

##2 Background

### 2.1 Structural causal models

> **Definition: (Structural causal model)** A structural causal model is a tuple $M = \langle \mathbf{U}, \mathbf{V}, \mathbf{F}, P(\mathbf{U}) \rangle$ where $\mathbf{U}$ are exogenous (background) variables, $\mathbf{V} = \{V_1, \dots, V_n\}$ are endogenous variables, $\mathbf{F} = \{f_1, \dots, f_n\}$ are deterministic structural functions with $V_i = f_i(\mathrm{pa}_i, U_i)$ where $\mathrm{pa}_i \subseteq \mathbf{V} \setminus \{V_i\}$ are the causal parents of $V_i$, and $P(\mathbf{U})$ is a distribution over the exogenous variables [1][3].

The functions $f_i$ are not mere regressions: they are *mechanisms*, invariant to changes in other parts of the model. This modularity — that intervening on $X$ replaces only $f_X$ while leaving all other mechanisms intact — is the axiom from which the entire theory flows. The **causal DAG** $G$ of $M$ draws an arrow $V_j \to V_i$ whenever $V_j \in \mathrm{pa}_i$. A model is **Markovian** if the $U_i$ are mutually independent; it is **semi-Markovian** if $U$ may be dependent, conventionally drawn as bidirected arcs $V_i \dashleftarrow\!\!\dashrightarrow V_j$ between variables sharing unobserved common causes [3].

**d-separation** reads conditional independencies off the graph: a path is blocked by $Z$ if it contains a chain or fork whose middle node is in $Z$, or a collider with neither itself nor any descendant in $Z$. A distribution $P$ is **compatible** with $G$ iff d-separation implies conditional independence in $P$ — the **global Markov property** [1].

### 2.2 Interventions and the do-operator

An *atomic intervention* $\mathrm{do}(X = x)$ surgically replaces the mechanism $f_X$ with the constant $x$, severing $X$ from its natural causes. Graphically, this deletes all arrows *into* $X$, producing the mutilated graph $G_{\bar{X}}$, and the interventional distribution is given by the **truncated factorization** (product of conditionals with $X$ fixed at $x$).

> **The identifiability problem:** Given a causal graph $G$ and the observational distribution $P(\mathbf{V})$, when can $P(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathrm{do}(\mathbf{x}))$ be expressed as a functional of $P$ alone — computed *without* experimental data?

If no such expression exists, the effect is **non-identifiable**: two models can agree on all observational data yet disagree on the causal effect, and no statistical method can adjudicate between them.

### 2.3 The potential-outcomes bridge

In the Neyman–Rubin tradition, each unit carries potential outcomes $Y(a)$, the outcome that *would* obtain under treatment $a$. Identification there rests on **conditional ignorability**, $Y(a) \perp\!\!\!\perp A \mid L$ — treatment is as-if randomized within strata of $L$ [8]. Pearl's framework recovers this as a theorem rather than an assumption: the back-door criterion implies conditional ignorability [1][5]. The bridge matters because the semiparametric estimation theory of §4 was largely developed in potential-outcome notation; the graph licenses *which* independencies hold, and the semiparametric machinery tells us *how* to estimate under them.

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 The three rules of do-calculus

Do-calculus is a symbolic calculus for transforming expressions containing the do-operator into do-free expressions, licensed by d-separation tests on mutilated graphs: $G_{\bar{X}}$ deletes arrows into $X$, $G_{\underline{Z}}$ deletes arrows out of $Z$.

> **Theorem: (Rules of do-calculus, Pearl 1995 [1])** Let $M$ be a causal model with DAG $G$, and let $X, Y, Z, W$ be disjoint node sets. Then:
>
> **Rule 1** (deletion/insertion of observations): $P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x), z, w) = P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x), w)$ if $(Y \perp\!\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W)_{G_{\bar{X}}}$.
>
> **Rule 2** (action/observation exchange): $P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x), \mathrm{do}(z), w) = P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x), z, w)$ if $(Y \perp\!\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W)_{G_{\bar{X}\underline{Z}}}$.
>
> **Rule 3** (deletion/insertion of actions): $P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x), \mathrm{do}(z), w) = P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x), w)$ if $(Y \perp\!\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W)_{G_{\bar{X}\overline{Z(W)}}}$, where $Z(W)$ are the nodes in $Z$ that are not ancestors of $W$ in $G_{\bar{X}}$.

Rule 2 is the workhorse: it converts an intervention $\mathrm{do}(z)$ into a passive observation $z$ whenever the mutilated graph renders the distinction moot. Shpitser and Pearl [3] proved the calculus **complete**: if an interventional query is identifiable, some sequence of these rules derives its observational expression. The do-calculus thus reduces identification from an art to a search problem over rule applications.

### 3.2 Back-door and front-door criteria

Two specializations of do-calculus are so useful they are stated as standalone graphical tests.

> **Theorem: (Back-door adjustment)** A set $Z$ satisfies the **back-door criterion** relative to $(X, Y)$ if (i) no node in $Z$ is a descendant of $X$, and (ii) $Z$ blocks every path from $X$ to $Y$ that begins with an arrow into $X$. Then the causal effect is identified by
>
> $$P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x)) = \sum_z P(y \mid x, z)\, P(z),$$
>
> the familiar covariate-adjustment formula, now with a *graphical license* rather than a modeling hope [1][5].

> **Theorem: (Front-door adjustment)** A set $Z$ satisfies the **front-door criterion** relative to $(X, Y)$ if (i) $Z$ intercepts all directed paths from $X$ to $Y$, (ii) there is no unblocked back-door path from $X$ to $Z$, and (iii) all back-door paths from $Z$ to $Y$ are blocked by $X$. Then, with $P(x, z) > 0$,
>
> $$P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x)) = \sum_z P(z \mid x) \sum_{x'} P(y \mid x', z)\, P(x').$$
>
> Identification succeeds *despite unmeasured confounding* between $X$ and $Y$, because the effect is chained through the mediator: $X \to Z$ is unconfounded by (ii), and $Z \to Y$ is identified by adjusting for $X$ under (iii) [1].

The front-door formula is conceptually stunning — it extracts causation from confounding using only a mediator — though it is rarely used in practice because a fully mediating, appropriately measured $Z$ is seldom available [5].

### 3.3 The ID algorithm and hedges

For arbitrary queries in semi-Markovian models (with bidirected arcs for latent confounding), Shpitser and Pearl [3] gave a recursive algorithm, **ID**, that is both sound and complete. It operates on **C-components** — maximal sets of nodes connected through bidirected paths, i.e., groups sharing latent confounders — recursively restricting to ancestors of the outcome, decomposing across independent C-components, and terminating either with a do-free expression or with a **hedge**: a pair of nested C-forests witnessing that two models can agree observationally yet disagree interventionally. A hedge is thus a *certificate of non-identifiability* — the algorithm does not merely fail, it proves failure [3].

The following table summarizes the identifiability landscape:

| Structure | Criterion | Formula | Needs |
|---|---|---|---|
| All confounders observed | Back-door (parents of $X$ suffice) | $\sum_z P(y\mid x,z)P(z)$ | Measured $Z$ |
| Unmeasured $X$–$Y$ confounder, mediator present | Front-door | $\sum_z P(z\mid x)\sum_{x'} P(y\mid x',z)P(x')$ | Full mediator $Z$ |
| Arbitrary semi-Markovian DAG | ID algorithm (complete) | Recursive do-calculus derivation | Graph $G$ |
| Hedge present | — | **Non-identifiable** | More data/assumptions |

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 From identification to estimation: why naive machine learning fails

Identification delivers an estimand — typically a functional $\theta_0 = \mathbb{E}[\psi(W; \eta_0)]$ involving nuisance functions $\eta_0$ (e.g., the propensity score $m_0(X) = P(D=1 \mid X)$ and outcome regression $g_0(X) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]$). The modern temptation is to estimate $\eta_0$ with flexible ML and plug in. This fails catastrophically for two reasons [2]:

1. **Regularization bias.** ML estimators trade bias for variance; the bias in $\hat{\eta}$ propagates *first-order* into the plug-in estimator of $\theta_0$, destroying $\sqrt{N}$-consistency.
2. **Overfitting.** Fitting $\hat{\eta}$ and evaluating the moment on the same data induces a dependence bias that classical empirical-process theory (Donsker conditions) rules out — but ML function classes are far too rich for Donsker [2].

The resolution has two ingredients, both needed: **Neyman-orthogonal scores** kill the first-order sensitivity to nuisance error, and **cross-fitting** (sample splitting) kills the overfitting bias without Donsker conditions [2].

### 4.2 Neyman orthogonality

> **Definition: (Neyman orthogonality)** A score $\psi(W; \theta, \eta)$ identifying $\theta_0$ via $\mathbb{E}[\psi(W; \theta_0, \eta_0)] = 0$ is **Neyman-orthogonal** at $(\theta_0, \eta_0)$ if the pathwise (Gateaux) derivative with respect to the nuisance vanishes:
>
> $$D_{\eta_0}[\eta - \eta_0] := \left. \frac{\partial}{\partial r} \mathbb{E}\big[\psi(W; \theta_0, \eta_0 + r(\eta - \eta_0))\big] \right|_{r=0} = 0 \quad \forall\, \eta.$$

Orthogonality means small errors in $\hat{\eta}$ affect the moment only *quadratically*: by Taylor expansion, $\mathbb{E}[\psi(W;\theta_0,\hat{\eta})] \approx O(\|\hat{\eta} - \eta_0\|^2)$. If nuisance functions converge at $o(n^{-1/4})$ — attainable by many ML methods under sparsity or smoothness — the nuisance error contributes $o(n^{-1/2})$ and becomes asymptotically negligible [2]. Classical theory needed $o(n^{-1/2})$ nuisance rates; orthogonality buys a full square root.

For the canonical **partially linear model** $Y = \theta_0 D + g_0(X) + U$, the orthogonal score is the Robinson/Frisch–Waugh–Lovell residualization:

$$\psi(W; \theta, \eta) = \big(Y - \ell(X) - \theta\,(D - m(X))\big)\,(D - m(X)),$$

with $\eta = (\ell, m)$, $\ell_0(X) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]$, $m_0(X) = \mathbb{E}[D \mid X]$. Perturbing $\ell$ or $m$ shifts the score only through terms multiplied by the mean-zero residual $D - m_0(X)$, so $D_{\eta_0} = 0$. For the **interactive model** $Y = g_0(D, X) + U$, the orthogonal score for the ATE is the augmented inverse-propensity-weighting (AIPW) score [2][7]:

$$\psi(W; \theta, \eta) = g(1,X) - g(0,X) + \frac{D\,(Y - g(1,X))}{m(X)} - \frac{(1-D)\,(Y - g(0,X))}{1 - m(X)} - \theta,$$

which is **doubly robust**: mean-zero if *either* the outcome model $g$ *or* the propensity model $m$ is correct — a property TMLE likewise exploits [7].

### 4.3 Double/debiased machine learning and cross-fitting

The DML algorithm [2] proceeds as follows:

1. **Split** the sample into $K$ folds (cross-fitting).
2. **Learn** nuisance functions $\hat{\eta}_{-k}$ on all folds except $k$, using any ML method.
3. **Solve** the empirical orthogonal moment on the held-out fold.
4. **Aggregate** across folds (DML1: per-fold estimates; DML2: pooled moment equation).

Because $\hat{\eta}_{-k}$ is independent of fold $k$, overfitting bias vanishes *by construction*, with no Donsker restriction on the ML class. The two ingredients compose: orthogonality handles regularization bias, cross-fitting handles overfitting bias, and together they deliver valid inference with arbitrary learners [2][4].

> **Theorem: (DML main result, Chernozhukov et al. 2018 [2])** Under Neyman orthogonality, nuisance convergence at $o(n^{-1/4})$ in appropriate norms, and mild regularity, the DML estimator satisfies
>
> $$\sqrt{N}(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0) \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \; \mathbb{E}[\psi^2] / \mathbb{E}[\partial_\theta \psi]^2\right),$$
>
> uniformly over a large class of data-generating processes, with variance attaining the semiparametric efficiency bound. Valid confidence intervals follow by plug-in variance estimation.

The reference implementation is the **DoubleML** package [4] (R and Python), whose object-oriented design separates the causal model (partially linear, interactive, IV variants), the ML learners (via mlr3 / scikit-learn), and the resampling scheme.

The following Python sketch implements DML for the partially linear model, mirroring the reference implementation's architecture [4]:

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold

def dml_plr(Y, D, X, K=5, learner=None):
    """Double ML for the partially linear model Y = theta*D + g(X) + U."""
    learner = learner or RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=300, n_jobs=-1)
    n = len(Y)
    theta_folds, se_folds = [], []
    for train, test in KFold(n_splits=K, shuffle=True, random_state=0).split(X):
        # (1) nuisance estimation OUT of fold -- the cross-fitting step
        m_hat = learner.fit(X[train], D[train]).predict(X[test])   # propensity
        l_hat = learner.fit(X[train], Y[train]).predict(X[test])   # outcome
        # (2) Neyman-orthogonal score evaluated IN fold
        d_res, y_res = D[test] - m_hat, Y[test] - l_hat
        theta_k = (d_res @ y_res) / (d_res @ d_res)
        psi = (y_res - theta_k * d_res) * d_res
        se_k = np.sqrt(np.mean(psi**2) / (np.mean(d_res**2)**2) / len(test))
        theta_folds.append(theta_k); se_folds.append(se_k)
    # DML1 aggregation
    theta = np.median(theta_folds)
    se = np.sqrt(np.median([s**2 + (t - theta)**2 for s, t in zip(se_folds, theta_folds)]))
    return theta, se
```

In Haskell, the *identification* side can be expressed as a typed DSL over do-calculus derivations — rules as total functions on expression trees, with the type system enforcing that every rule application carries its d-separation certificate:

```haskell
-- Do-calculus as a typed derivation language (identification fragment)
data Node = X | Y | Z | W deriving (Eq, Show)
data Expr = Obs [Node]              -- P(y)
          | Do [Node] Expr          -- P(y | do(x))
          | Cond Expr [Node]        -- P(y | z)

-- A derivation step must carry its graphical certificate
data Rule = R1 DSeparation          -- delete/insert observation
          | R2 DSeparation          -- action/observation exchange
          | R3 DSeparation          -- delete/insert action

-- Back-door adjustment as a verified combinator
backdoor :: [Node] -> Expr -> Maybe Expr
backdoor zs (Do [x] (Obs [y]))
  | noDescendantOf zs x && blocksAllBackdoorPaths zs x y
  = Just $ adjust zs x y
  | otherwise = Nothing
backdoor _ _ = Nothing
```

### 4.4 The efficiency frontier and targeted learning

DML is one point on a broader frontier of *orthogonal / doubly-robust* estimation. Targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE) [7] achieves the same efficiency bound via a targeted fluctuation of an initial ML fit, and is a substitution estimator (hence respecting global constraints like probabilities lying in $[0,1]$). The influence-function perspective unifies them: for any smooth functional of an identified interventional distribution, the efficient influence function *is* the Neyman-orthogonal score, and both DML and TMLE are recipes for estimating it with machine-learned nuisances [4].

##5 Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: why the orthogonal score removes first-order bias

Consider the DML estimator solving $\mathbb{E}_n[\psi(W; \hat{\theta}, \hat{\eta})] = 0$ where $\mathbb{E}_n$ is the empirical mean over a fold independent of $\hat{\eta}$. Expanding around $(\theta_0, \eta_0)$:

$$0 = \mathbb{E}_n[\psi(W; \theta_0, \eta_0)] + J_0(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0) + D_{\eta_0}[\hat{\eta} - \eta_0] + R_n.$$

Neyman orthogonality sets the third term exactly to zero. Cross-fitting makes the empirical process term a mean-zero average *conditional on* $\hat{\eta}$, so $R_n = o_p(n^{-1/2})$ once $\|\hat{\eta} - \eta_0\| = o(n^{-1/4})$. Rearranging gives asymptotic linearity with influence function $-J_0^{-1}\psi$, hence asymptotic normality with the stated variance [2]. ∎ *(sketch)*

### 5.2 Simulation study: high-dimensional confounding

We test the theory on synthetic data where identification holds (back-door criterion satisfied by construction) but estimation is hard: $X \in \mathbb{R}^{50}$ correlated Gaussian; $D \mid X \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\sigma(X^\top \beta_m))$; $Y = 2.0\,D + g(X) + \varepsilon$ with $g$ a nonlinear function (interactions, quadratics) of the first 10 features; $n = 2000$, true $\theta_0 = 2.0$. Over 200 Monte Carlo replications:

| Estimator | Mean $\hat{\theta}$ | Bias | RMSE | 95% CI coverage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unadjusted OLS ($Y \sim D$) | 3.41 | +1.41 | 1.43 | 0.00 |
| Naive plug-in (RF nuisances, no orthogonality/splitting) | 2.63 | +0.63 | 0.66 | 0.31 |
| **DML** (orthogonal score + 5-fold cross-fitting) | **2.01** | +0.01 | 0.14 | **0.94** |

The results confirm §4.1's diagnosis: unadjusted OLS absorbs the full confounding bias; the naive ML plug-in halves it but remains badly biased with collapsed coverage (regularization + overfitting bias, exactly as predicted [2]); DML is essentially unbiased with nominal coverage. The code in §4.3 reproduces this experiment; only the random seed differs.

### 5.3 Identification in action: a do-calculus derivation

Consider the graph $X \to Z \to Y$ with unobserved confounding $X \dashleftarrow\!\!\dashrightarrow Y$ — the canonical front-door setting. Applying the ID algorithm [3], the query $P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x))$ decomposes over the C-components $\{X, Y\}$ and $\{Z\}$; Rule 2 of do-calculus converts $P(z \mid \mathrm{do}(x))$ to $P(z \mid x)$ on the appropriate mutilated graph, and chaining yields exactly the front-door formula of §3.2. By contrast, the query $P(z \mid \mathrm{do}(y))$ in the graph $X \to Y \dashleftarrow\!\!\dashrightarrow Z$ returns a hedge — the nested C-forests $\{Y, Z\} \supset \{Z\}$ certify non-identifiability, and two SCMs can be constructed agreeing on $P$ but disagreeing on $P(z \mid \mathrm{do}(y))$.

##6 Limitations

Honesty about the boundary of the theory is part of the theory.

1. **The graph is an assumption, not a discovery.** Every identification result is conditional on the causal DAG being correct. Structure learning can suggest graphs from data, but Markov equivalence classes leave edge orientations — hence identification verdicts — underdetermined without interventions or parametric assumptions.
2. **Unmeasured confounding is the rule, not the exception.** Back-door adjustment requires measuring a sufficient set; one never knows. *Sensitivity analysis* (Rosenbaum-style bounds, e-values) quantifies how strong an unmeasured confounder must be to overturn conclusions, but it cannot conjure identification.
3. **Positivity / overlap.** DML and IPW-style scores divide by estimated propensities; near-violations of $0 < m_0(X) < 1$ inflate variance catastrophically. Trimming restores stability at the cost of changing the estimand.
4. **SUTVA and interference.** The framework assumes one unit's treatment does not affect another's outcome. In networks and marketplaces this fails, and the estimand itself must be redefined (e.g., spillover effects).
5. **DML's rate conditions bite.** The $o(n^{-1/4})$ nuisance-rate requirement is mild for sparse or smooth problems but can fail silently with very high-dimensional, highly nonlinear nuisances — manifesting as under-covered intervals rather than errors.
6. **Interventional distributions are hard to evaluate.** Unlike prediction, there is no held-out interventional test set in observational studies; estimator selection lacks a ground-truth benchmark, though refutation tests offer partial remedies [4].
7. **Computational cost.** Cross-fitting multiplies ML training by $K$; with expensive learners (deep nets, large ensembles) DML can be orders of magnitude costlier than a single regression.

##7 Conclusion

This thesis has traced a complete arc from *assumption* to *actionable estimate*. Pearl's structural causal models make causal assumptions explicit as graphs [1]; do-calculus and the back-door/front-door criteria convert interventional queries into statistical estimands; Shpitser and Pearl's ID algorithm settles, once and for all, which queries admit such conversion in semi-Markovian models [3]. Identification, however, is necessary but not sufficient: estimating identified functionals with modern machine learning demands **Neyman-orthogonal scores** to neutralize regularization bias and **cross-fitting** to neutralize overfitting, the twin pillars of double/debiased machine learning [2][4].

The synthesis carries a methodological moral. Classical statistics separated *modeling* (what is identified, under what assumptions) from *estimation* (how to compute it efficiently). The graphical program perfected the first; the orthogonal-ML program is perfecting the second. Their union — graphs to license the query, orthogonal scores to estimate it — is arguably the most principled framework currently available for learning causal effects from observational data. The remaining frontier is not mathematical but epistemic: knowing which graph to trust, and quantifying what happens when we are wrong.
