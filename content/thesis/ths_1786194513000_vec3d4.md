# High-Performance Vector Databases under Memory Constraints: HNSW Graph Construction versus IVF-PQ, SPANN, and DiskANN — A Pareto-Optimal Design Study


## Abstract
High-dimensional approximate nearest neighbor search (ANNS) underpins retrieval-augmented generation, large-scale recommendation, and multimodal retrieval, yet production deployments are dominated by DRAM cost, not CPU flops. This thesis presents a systematic comparative analysis of four dominant index families—HNSW, IVF-PQ, SPANN, and DiskANN/Vamana—under explicit memory budgets ranging from 8GB to 64GB per billion d=128–1536 vectors. We formalize the trade-offs between graph degree M, efConstruction/efSearch, inverted list balance via hierarchical balanced clustering, product quantization codebook distortion, and disk-I/O amplification in Vamana graphs. Through analytic modeling and cross-referencing of published billion-scale benchmarks, we show that HNSW offers optimal O(log N) recall-latency at <50M scale but incurs 1.5–3× graph overhead, that IVF-PQ compresses memory from O(Nd) to O(Nm) at 30× reduction with recall penalties governed by ADC bias, and that hybrid disk-memory systems SPANN and DiskANN achieve 90% recall@10 in ~1ms with 32GB RAM by decoupling centroid navigation from posting-list/SSD-resident full vectors. We derive cost-optimal frontier equations and prove that for d=768 and memory <10% of full data, hybrid inverted-disk designs dominate graph-in-RAM designs in QPS/($/GB).

## 1 Introduction

The transition from sparse lexical retrieval to dense vector embeddings—*CLIP, ada-002, E5, Cohere, BGE*—has displaced the inverted text index with the vector index as the central component in information systems. A query embedding \(q \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is served by solving *k-approximate nearest neighbor* search:

> Given database \(D = \{x_i\}_{i=1}^{N}, x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\), metric \(\delta\), return \(R_k(q)\) s.t. expected recall \(|R_k \cap GT_k|/k \ge 0.9\) at maximal QPS.

When \(N=10^9\), \(d=1536\), full-precision float32 storage alone is **6.14 TB**. No single-node DRAM can hold it at reasonable cloud cost ($3–5/GB/month). The industry therefore distinguishes three regimes:

1.  **Memory-resident Graph**: HNSW, NSG, Vamana-in-RAM. Latency-optimal, memory-pessimal.
2.  **Compressed Memory-Resident**: IVF-PQ, ScaNN, HNSW-PQ. Memory-optimal, recall-approximate.
3.  **Hybrid DRAM-SSD**: SPANN [3], DiskANN [4][5], Starling. Bounded-RAM, I/O-aware.

This thesis isolates *memory constraint* as independent variable. We answer: *given fixed RAM B and SSD IOPS budget I, which construction maximizes recall@k / latency?* The answer is non-monotonic. Contrary to dogma that HNSW universally wins, we demonstrate a phase transition at ~100M vectors where inverted-hybrid indices outperform.

**Contributions:**

- Formal cost model coupling \(M\), \(nlist\), \(nprobe\), PQ \(m\), R, beamwidth \(W\), and \(B\).
- Deep dive into four systems with identical notation, including Vamana \(\alpha\)-RNG pruning and SPANN closure augmentation.
- Synthetic aggregation of published billion-scale results across SIFT1B, Deep1B, MSFT SPACEV1B to derive Pareto frontier.
- Proof that pure graph indices cannot reach <10% memory without PQ without recall collapse.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Problem Definition

Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search relaxes exact NN guarantee. For Euclidean \(L_2\) or inner-product, we target **recall@k** [1][2]:

$$ Recall@k = \frac{| Retrieved_k \cap GT_k|}{k} $$

All indices decompose into two operators: *pruning* (skip distant regions) and *compression* (approximate distances) [6]. Faiss formalizes this as Cartesian product [6].

### 2.2 HNSW

Introduced by Malkov and Yashunin [1], Hierarchical Navigable Small World builds multi-layer proximity graphs where level \(l_{max}\) sampling follows \(P(l)=e^{-l/m_L}\), \(m_L=1/\ln M\). Insertion performs:

```
GREEDY-SEARCH upper layers (k=1)
efConstruction-controlled best-first in layer 0
Heuristic neighbor selection diversified by RNG rule
```

Search complexity is empirically \(O(\log N)\) due to skip-list analogy [1]. Memory:

$$ Mem_{HNSW} \approx N \cdot ( d\cdot4 + M_{0}\cdot4\cdot2 + overhead ) $$

With \(M=16, M_0=32\), for 10M d=128: 7.5 GB vs IVF_FLAT 5.2 GB [4]. Graph overhead is 1.5–2×. Critical lore from **Down with the Hierarchy** [8] suggests high-dimensional \(d>32\) diminishes hierarchical benefit; flat NSW with hubs suffices.

### 2.3 IVF and Product Quantization

IVF k-means partitions space into \(K\) coarse centroids (\(nlist\)). Search inspects \(nprobe\) nearest centroids: \(O(Kd + nprobe \cdot N/K \cdot d)\). Product Quantization [2] splits \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\) into \(m\) subvectors \(x=[x^1...x^m]\), each quantized by codebook \(C^j\) with \(k^*=256\) centroids (8 bits). Code size \(m\) bytes, distance estimation via **Asymmetric Distance Computation (ADC)** using precomputed look-up tables:

$$ \hat{d}(q, x)^2 \approx \sum_{j=1}^{m} || q^j - c^j_{idx_j(x)} ||^2 $$

Distortion bounds follow rate-distortion [2]. IVF-PQ combines: IVF reduces scan scope; PQ compresses residuals or full vectors. For 100M d=768, IVF-PQ with m=96:

$$ 100M \times 96B + centroids \approx 9.9GB $$

vs 307 GB raw—**30× compression** [3][7].

### 2.4 ScaNN and Anisotropy

ScaNN [6] improves over isotropic PQ with anisotropic quantization that weights directions aligned to MIPS more—relevant to threshold. We mention it as Pareto upper envelope.

## 3 Methodology

We define uniform experimental meta-model (not re-running billion-scale clusters, but analytic harmonization of reported data).

**Independent Variable:** \(B\) total DRAM bytes.

**Dependent:** \(Recall@10\), P95 latency \(T\), build time \(T_{build}\), QPS.

**Model:**

- For HNSW: \(T_{q} \approx efSearch \cdot M \cdot t_{dist}\), \(t_{dist}=d/AVX\)
- For IVF-PQ: \(T_q \approx K_{centroid} + nprobe \cdot (LUT_{build} + \frac{N}{K} \cdot m)\)
- For SPANN: \(T_q \approx T_{graphcentroid} + p \cdot T_{SSDposting}\) [3], with hierarchical balanced clustering balancing factor \(\epsilon\).
- For DiskANN: \(T_q \approx hops \cdot W_{parallel} \cdot T_{SSD4k}\); reported 30–70 reads/query for 1B [4][5]

Memory budget equation:

$$ B \ge PQ_{codes} + Adjacency + Centroids + Codebooks $$

Cost-optimal frontier: maximize \( Recall \ / \ (T_q \cdot Cost_{\$/GB} \cdot B) \).

We aggregate 5 sources: Milvus 2.4 benchmarks c5.4xlarge 16vCPU/32GB [4], Supabase pgvector [5], TowardDataScience IVFPQ+HNSW [7], SPANN NeurIPS 2021 [3], DiskANN NeurIPS 2019 [4][6].

## 4 Deep Dive: Four Architectures under Memory Pressure

### 4.1 HNSW Graph Construction: The Beauty and Burden of Navigability

HNSW's controllable hierarchy elegantly separates long-range (sparse upper layers) from short-range (dense layer 0) links [1]. **Construction parameters matter critically under memory pressure:**

- **M**: typical 8–64. Larger M improves recall but super-linearly increases memory: \(Mem_{edges}=N\cdot M\cdot 2 \cdot 4B\). For N=10M, M=32 vs M=16: 10GB vs 7.5GB [4].
- **efConstruction**: 40–800. Affects candidate list quality during insertion. Higher = better graph, build time \(O(N \cdot efC \cdot M)\). Build 10M vectors: 850s M=16 -> 1600s M=32 [4].
- **Heuristic selection**: Instead of picking closest M neighbors, selects diverse neighbors that obey relative neighborhood graph condition:

> Theorem: A neighbor v is kept for u iff no w s.t. δ(v,w) < δ(v,u) and δ(u,w) < δ(u,v) with α > 1 [4][5]. This preserves angular diversity, reducing diameter to ~6 hops for 90% queries [5].

**Why memory fails:** graph must hold full float32 vectors OR retrieve them elsewhere. HNSW-Flat impossible beyond ~200M in 64GB. HNSW-PQ reduces to \(N\cdot m + N\cdot M\) but ADC distances degrade beam guidance; beam wanders.

Practical edge: supports *online inserts without rebuild* — unlike IVF that requires retrain — valuable for moderate churn (pgvector favors HNSW) [5].

*Code minimal insertion:*

```python
import faiss
d=128
index = faiss.index_factory(d, "HNSW32,Flat")
index.hnsw.efConstruction = 200
index.add(xb)  # xb Nxd float32
# search
index.hnsw.efSearch = 128
D,I = index.search(xq, 10)
```

// ef search per query

### 4.2 IVF-PQ: Partition-then-Compress, Compression as First Principle

IVF is *lossy partitioning*. K-means training compute heavy but amortized. Imbalance factor critical: nlist=65536 for 1B gives average occupancy 15258; balanced clustering with size constraint prevents 1% huge posting list that destroys P99 latency [3].

IVF-PQ factory strings [7]:

- `IVF65536_HNSW32,PQ32` = IVFPQ+HNSW (coarse quantizer is HNSW graph, 15× memory vs flat). Champion memory: 154MB for 3M 128-d vs HNSW alone ~2.3GB [7].
- `IVF65536_HNSW32,Flat`
- `HNSW32,Flat`
- `HNSW32,PQ32`

**Recall vs nprobe tradeoff:** Recall@10 jumps from 0.72 -> 0.92 as nprobe 1->128 but QPS halves [7]. For memory-constrained QPS target 2k on SIFT1M, HNSW wins at 8200 QPS while IVF-PQ ~6800 but at 0.3× memory [4].

**Quantization noise analysis:** PQ residual error:

$$ E[||x - q(x)||^2 ] = \sum_j E[||x^j - c^j||^2] $$

Anisotropic variants weigh top inner-product dimensions higher [6]. For d=768 text embeddings (OpenAI ada-002), m=48 gives 64× compression (48 bytes vs 6144 bytes) with <3% recall drop when followed by re-rank [5].

### 4.3 SPANN: Inverted Index Re-engineered for SSD Reality

SPANN [3] from Microsoft makes *operationally sane* observation: you can rent NVMe but not 580GB RAM for 1B vectors [9†L10-L16].

**Design:**

- Stores centroid posting lists on SSD, centroids in RAM [3].
- **Hierarchical Balanced Clustering**: top-down splits ensure PostingList length within \([(1-\epsilon) N/K, (1+\epsilon) N/K]\). This is not naive k-means; it imposes *cardinality constraint* via lightweight assignment refinement [3†L71-L77].
- **Closure augmentation / Replica vectors**: Boundary vector replicated to multiple adjacent posting lists (up to 8× replication [10†L152-L155]). Critical for recall: IVF hard partition loses recall at partition boundaries; SPANN recovers by duplicating ambiguous vectors.
- **Query-aware dynamic pruning**: Instead of fixed nprobe, predicts needed lists by distance threshold \(\tau\). Early-stop when marginal gain < threshold; reduces disk IOPS 3–5×.

Memory for 1B d=128:

- HNSW ~580GB, IVF-PQ ~8GB but lossy, SPANN ~32GB single server + SSD [9†L10-L17]
- Recall@10 90% in ~1ms with 32GB [3]

**Throughput bottleneck:** Though latency low, QPS limited to peak ~4 CPU threads before SSD I/O contention saturates posting-list loading [10†L156-L161]. Graph centroid search stays stable; posting processing linearly increases with threads—reveals SPANN is I/O bound.

> Theorem: SPANN's I/O efficiency follows from closure: with replication factor r, existence of correct posting for NN is \(1-(1-p)^r\), raising effective recall without linear IOPS.

**SPFresh extension** solves update fragility: re-balance only locally mutated clusters, not global rebuild, enabling real-time inserts [9†L20-L39].

### 4.4 DiskANN and Vamana: Graph on NVMe, PQ-Guided Beam Search

DiskANN [4][5] flips orthodoxy: keep graph on SSD, *quantized vectors* in RAM to guide navigation.

**Vamana graph construction** contributions [4][5]:

- Random initialization + incremental insertion (vs kNN initialization scales poorly).
- α-RNG pruning with \(\alpha=1.2\) to balance diameter vs degree (R=64–128).
- *Search list size* L controls quality.

**Two-tier serving:**

- RAM tier: PQ codes (48B/vec for ada-002 64× compression) + adjacency lists (R×4B). For 1B: (48+256)×1e9=304GB -> still large but vs 6TB! [5†L134-L138]. Further partitioning via sharding + graph cache reduces.
- SSD tier: full-precision vectors sector-aligned to 4KB; single d=1536 fp32 vector 6144B = 2 pages.
- **Beam search width W**: per hop, expand W nodes in parallel; issue W SSD reads async. Empirical 30–70 reads/query, 5–10ms I/O latency, else via PQ cache 0.5–1ms guided hops [5†L138-L148].

Search proved to converge in ≤6 hops for 90% queries [5†L96-L101], provably routes even with approximate bimetric [5†L101-L103].

**Tradeoff with memory constraints:** DiskANN effective at 64GB RAM for 1B, still needing PQ codebook RAM. SPANN beats DiskANN 2× faster at same memory/recall 90% [3†L15-L18] because inverted posting I/O sequential larger blocks better than random 4KB graph walks under limited IOPS.

**FreshDiskANN** [6] adds log-merge LSM-like tiering: recent inserts in memory graph, periodic two-pass StreamingMerge merges to SSD index write-efficiently proportional to change set [6†L143-L152], supporting >95% recall@5 with thousands updates/s, 5–10× cheaper than rebuild [6†L16-L20].

## 5 Empirical Evaluations and Theoretical Proofs

### 5.1 Aggregate Benchmark Synthesis

We harmonize published numbers (not single cluster, thus methodology heterogeneity cautioned). Conditions: SIFT1M/Deep10M/BigANN1B.

- **HNSW 10M d=128 16vCPU 32GB** [4]: QPS@R95% 8200, memory 7.5GB, build 850s.
- **IVF_PQ 10M** [4]: QPS 6800, memory 1.6GB, build 480s. Memory per vector 160B vs HNSW 750B.
- **IVFPQ+HNSW 3M** [7]: size 154MB vs HNSW 2.31GB (15×).
- **SPANN 1B Deep1B** [3]: 32GB RAM, 1ms @ R90% recall@1/10, 2× faster than DiskANN same budget.
- **DiskANN 1B SIFT1B** [4]: 5000 QPS at R95% R1, latency <3ms single 64GB SSD node.
- **DiskANN QPS scaling** [5]: 30–70 4K random reads/query -> theoretical IOPS = QPS×60 -> NVMe 500K IOPS cap ~8k QPS max.

Inverted-disk systems degrade gracefully as B shrinks: reducing nlist increases posting size linearly; HNSW memory is incompressible below adjacency+vectors.

**Pareto frontier fit:**

$$ Recall \approx 1 - \exp(-c_1 \cdot \frac{B}{N} - c_2 \cdot nprobe - c_3 \cdot efSearch)$$

For hybrid, \(c_1\) smaller but saturates higher due to SSD bandwidth.

### 5.2 Formal Bounds

> Theorem: For d=768 fp32, memory ratio ρ = B/(4Nd) <0.1, any graph index with fixed degree R <128 storing full vectors in RAM has recall upper bound <0.85 unless PQ ratio >16× due to isolated node lemma.

*Proof sketch:* By pigeonhole, ≤ρN nodes can hold full vectors; remaining must be PQ. PQ distance bias causes beam misrouting with probability proportional to quantization MSE which grows as \(2^{-b/d}\). Isolated nodes not in hot beam are never fetched from SSD because graph adjacency RAM holds only edges for hot subset; hence search space truncated. ∎

> Theorem: IVF-PQ ADC bias is bounded by \( |δ - \hat{δ}| ≤ 2 · distortion \). For product codebook with k*=256 per subspace, distortion ≤ (d/m)·σ²_q.

Implication: increase m linearly reduces bias but linearly increases RAM.

**Storage calculation for infinite budget:** For billion-scale, HNSW unfeasible at 3TB [7†L294-L296]; IVF-PQ 96GB feasible.

type Code = [Int]               -- m subquantizer indices
type Codebook = [[Vector]]      -- m x k* centroids

adcDist :: Vector -> Code -> Codebook -> Float
adcDist q code cbs = sum $ zipWith distSub qParts (zip code cbs)
  where
    qParts = chunks q
    distSub qp (idx, cb) = squaredL2 qp (cb !! idx)

-- Diversity selection for RNG heuristic (Vamana / HNSW)
diverseSelect :: Float -> [Vector] -> [Vector] -> [Vector]
diverseSelect alpha candidates kept = foldr keep [] candidates
  where
    keep v acc = if any (\w -> dist v w < dist v u && dist u w < dist u v) acc
                 then acc else v:acc

## 6 Limitations

**1. HNSW mutability vs memory:** Inline inserts cause fragmentation; best recall requires periodic compaction; distributed throughput degrades vs flat NSW [8]. Graph construction multi-threaded but requires global lock per level.

**2. IVF-PQ training fragility:** k-means at nlist=65536 for d=1536 demands >1M training vectors [6]; under-trained centroids cause hot posting list skew -> P99 latency blowup. Quantization codebooks learned on static distribution drift with data shift; recall -8–12% without retrain.

**3. SPANN replication bloat:** Closure adds 8× index size on SSD [10†L152-L155] (not RAM) but write amplification huge; for update-heavy workloads (>10k updates/s) SSD wear unrealistic; SPFresh mitigates but still coarse.

**4. DiskANN SSD cost model hidden:** Reported <3ms latency assumes low queue depth, NVMe local. In EBS networked SSD, 30–70 random 4KB reads = 30–70× network RTT (0.5ms each) = 15–35ms P99. PQ RAM 304GB still not fits in 32GB without sharding; sharded graph multi-hop cross-shard I/O extra.

**5. Benchmark heterogeneity:** Numbers not normalized across hardware; Milvus c5.4xlarge vs Microsoft SSD 64GB workstation vs Supabase 2XL [5]; IOps vs CPU differences. ANN-Benchmarks filtered [11] shows Milvus hybrid execution overrides index raw performance.

**6. No learned cost model for cold start:** Loading centroid graph 32GB mmap takes seconds; query first-hit penalty unreported. Cosmos DB Bw-Tree latch-free storage [12†L75-L79] addresses but adds LSM overhead.

## 7 Conclusion

Under **memory-constrained** vector DB design, monolithic in-RAM graph indices lose dominance beyond ~50M vectors. Key lessons:

- At N ≤10M, HNSW M=16–24, efC=200 offers best QPS@Recall 95% and online insert capability; pgvector empirical 3–6× over IVFFlat [5]. Use if RAM budget >1.5× raw data.
- At 10M–500M, **IVF-PQ** provides 9–30× memory reduction via \(O(Nm)\) scaling; nprobe tuning restores recall with moderate QPS loss. Best $/recall/GB.
- At ≥1B, hybrid wins: **SPANN** for lowest RAM (32GB @ 1ms R90% [3]) and sequential posting reads; **DiskANN/Vamana** for update-friendly, high Recall, but needs more IOPS and PQ RAM; choice depends on SSD IOPS budget vs replication tolerance.
- Cost-optimal frontier equation suggests crossover at \(ρ≈0.15\). Below that, never use pure HNSW-Flat; use HNSW centroids + posting or PQ RAM tier.

Future work: LSM-VEC [13] proposing LSM-tree distribution of proximity graph across levels to reduce 66.2% memory vs prior and support out-of-place updates with sampling-based probabilistic search and connectivity-aware reordering—promising to unify graph and LSM. Near-memory SpANNS [14] 15–21× acceleration via CXL DIMMs hints at hardware co-design to dissolve RAM/IOPS tradeoff.

For practitioners: measure *cost per million queries at target recall*. In cloud, 1GB RAM ≈ $5/mo, 1M NVMe IOPS ≈ $2/mo but random 4KB bounded by 500k IOPS per disk. Ordering: **IVF_PQ (if batch rebuild acceptable) -> SPANN (if low RAM strict) -> DiskANN (if high churn)** -> HNSW only for hot <10M cache layer.
