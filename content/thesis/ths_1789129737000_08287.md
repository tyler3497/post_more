# Wake-Sleep Neural Program Synthesis: Library Learning, Recognition Models, and Compression-Driven Abstraction Discovery in DreamCoder-Style Architectures

## Abstract

Inductive program synthesis searches a fixed domain-specific language (DSL), which scales poorly to realistic domains. Library learning grows the search space itself during training. This thesis treats DreamCoder-style neural program synthesis, in which a probabilistic grammar over programs (the library), a neural recognition model guiding enumerative search, and a Bayesian wake-sleep inference scheme jointly bootstrap one another. We formalize the wake phase (solving tasks with recognition-guided search), the abstraction sleep phase (extracting compressive abstractions via E-graph refactoring), and the dreaming sleep phase (training the recognition model on sampled and replayed programs). We prove the minimum-description-length abstraction objective is the MAP estimate of a hierarchical Bayesian model, and compare DreamCoder-style architectures with large-language-model synthesis on list processing, LOGO graphics, and text-editing benchmarks. Successors such as Stitch accelerate abstraction discovery by three to four orders of magnitude at comparable compression quality. We close with limitations — the amortization gap, DSL design sensitivity, and natural-language specifications — and a roadmap toward neurosymbolic hybrids.

## 1. Introduction

Program synthesis is the *search problem* of automated programming: given a specification, produce a program that satisfies it. For inductive synthesis the specification is a set of input–output examples $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, and the synthesizer must return a program $p$ such that $p(x_i) = y_i$ for all $i$, ideally one that *generalizes* rather than memorizing the examples [1][2]. Two traditions have dominated the field. The *deductive* tradition, rooted in formal methods, reduces synthesis to constraint solving — encoding the search as a SAT or SMT problem over a finite DSL [2]. The *statistical* tradition, ascendant since 2017, replaces explicit search with neural networks trained to map specifications directly to programs, or to guide search as learned heuristics.

Both traditions assume the program space is *fixed* before learning begins. Yet human programmers *extend their vocabulary* when entering a new domain, defining functions and patterns that compress recurring computation into named units. The central hypothesis of the DreamCoder program is that synthesis should do the same — that **learning the library of components and learning to search are two facets of one joint inference problem**, and solving both simultaneously beats either alone [1].

DreamCoder instantiates this hypothesis as approximate Bayesian inference over a *generative model of programs* [1]. Three mechanisms interoperate:

- **Wake phase.** A neural recognition model guides enumerative search to solve as many training tasks as possible, producing a corpus of discovered programs.
- **Abstraction sleep phase.** An E-graph-based refactoring algorithm extracts recurring sub-expressions from the corpus, introducing them as new library functions selected to *compress* the corpus under an MDL prior.
- **Dreaming sleep phase.** The system samples "dreams" — random programs from the learned generative model — and trains the recognition model on the resulting (program, task) pairs, amortizing search.

The result is a *bootstrapping loop*: better libraries make programs shorter; better search finds more programs; more programs feed better compression [1]. Applied to eight domains — list processing, LOGO graphics, text editing, block towers, symbolic regression, regexes — DreamCoder's libraries cut effective search cost astronomically: a 32-primitive sort solution (>$10^{72}$ years of brute force) is found in under ten minutes [1].

This thesis develops the full theory and practice of this architecture. Section 2 reviews Bayesian program induction and neural search guidance. Section 3 formalizes the joint model and the three phases. Section 4 dives into the wake-sleep dynamics, recognition-model design, compression-driven abstraction, dream-decompiling refinements, and the comparison with LLM-based synthesis. Section 5 evaluates on list processing, graphics, and text-editing domains. Section 6 states limitations, and Section 7 concludes with a research roadmap.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Bayesian Program Induction

DreamCoder's foundation is *Bayesian program induction*: the learner maintains a posterior distribution over programs given tasks,

$$P(p \mid \{(x_i, y_i)\}) \propto P(\{(x_i, y_i)\} \mid p)\, P(p \mid L),$$

where $L$ is the library and $P(p \mid L)$ is a *generative model of programs* defined by a probabilistic grammar over $L$ [1]. The library itself is learned as the maximum-a-posteriori (MAP) estimate under a prior that penalizes library size — an explicit instantiation of Occam's razor. This framing unifies two ordinarily separate objectives: *compressing* programs (the library prior) and *solving* tasks (the likelihood).

### 2.2 Amortized Inference and Recognition Models

Exact inference over program space is intractable; practical systems use *amortized inference*. A recognition model $q_\phi(p \mid \{(x_i, y_i)\})$ — typically a neural network — approximates the posterior, trading per-task search for one-time training cost [1][3]. The recognition model need not be exact. The critical property is *amortization*: the cost of training the recognizer is spread across all future synthesis tasks drawn from the same domain distribution. The "dream decompiling" variant makes this precise by decompiling sampled programs through a deterministic recognition process, yielding unbiased gradient estimates for the recognizer [3].

### 2.3 Minimum Description Length and E-Graphs

The abstraction sleep phase solves a *compression* problem. Given a corpus of programs $C$, find new functions $f$ and a refactored corpus $C'$ minimizing

$$\mathcal{L}(f, C') = \underbrace{|f|}_{\text{abstraction cost}} + \underbrace{|C'|}_{\text{refactored corpus size}},$$

where $|\cdot|$ denotes description length in symbols [1][2]. This is the *minimum description length* (MDL) principle, and it is formally equivalent to maximizing the joint probability $P(C, L) = P(C \mid L)\, P(L)$ under the hierarchical Bayesian model where $P(L)$ penalizes library complexity. DreamCoder implements the search for abstractions via **E-graph refactoring**: programs are converted to E-graphs (compact representations of equivalence classes of terms), and candidate abstractions are extracted by anti-unification over shared E-nodes, with the MDL objective selecting among candidates [1].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

We are given a corpus of synthesis tasks $\mathcal{T} = \{t_1, \dots, t_N\}$, each task $t$ specified by examples $E_t = \{(x_i, y_i)\}$. The system starts from a set of primitives $L_0$ (e.g., `map`, `cons`, `+`, `car`, `cdr`, LOGO primitives `move`, `turn`) and iterates:

1. **Wake:** for a batch of tasks, run recognition-guided search to find programs; record solutions $\mathcal{S}$.
2. **Abstraction sleep:** compress $\mathcal{S}$ into an expanded library $L' \supset L$ by adding abstractions that maximize compression.
3. **Dreaming sleep:** sample programs from $P(p \mid L')$, generate corresponding tasks, and train the recognition model $q_\phi$ on both dreams and replays.

The loop repeats until a fixed budget is exhausted or task-solution rates plateau.

### 3.2 The Generative Model of Programs

The library $L$ induces a probabilistic grammar: each production $f \in L$ carries probability $\theta_f$, fit by maximum likelihood over the solved corpus, so $P(p \mid L, \theta) = \prod_{f \in p} \theta_f$ [1]. Frequently used abstractions become cheap to sample — a feedback loop accelerating future search.

> **Theorem 1.** *Under the hierarchical Bayesian model with library prior $P(L) \propto 2^{-|L|}$ and corpus likelihood $P(C \mid L) = \prod_{p \in C} P(p \mid L)$ with the ML-fitted grammar, the MAP library estimate coincides exactly with the minimum-description-length library minimizing $|L| + \sum_{p \in C} |p|_L$, where $|p|_L = -\log P(p \mid L)$.*
>
> *Proof sketch.* Taking negative logarithms gives $\arg\min_L \left( |L| - \sum_p \log P(p \mid L) \right)$, precisely the MDL objective; the E-graph refactoring of Ellis et al. approximates this argmin by greedy extraction of maximally compressive abstractions [1]. ∎

### 3.3 Recognition Models for Program Search

DreamCoder's recognition model conditions on input–output examples via a neural encoder and decodes a *bigram* distribution over grammar productions: the probability of each production depends on its parent in the derivation tree [1]. During wake-phase search, productions are enumerated in decreasing $q_\phi$ order, so the recognizer functions as a learned search policy that is retrained from each new grammar's dreams, keeping it aligned with the library it searches over [1].

### 3.4 Wake-Sleep as Approximate Bayesian Inference

The three phases approximate coordinate ascent on a variational lower bound of $\log P(\mathcal{T} \mid L)$: wake is posterior sampling (E-step), abstraction sleep maximizes over $L$ (M-step), and dreaming sleep fits $q_\phi$ to the posterior (amortization) [1][3] — the Helmholtz machine's wake-sleep algorithm adapted to discrete program spaces.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Wake Phase: Recognition-Guided Enumerative Search

During wake, the system attempts a small batch of training tasks (e.g., batch size 10–20) under a per-domain timeout (2–12 minutes) [3]. Search is *bottom-up enumeration with a learned policy*: candidate programs are generated in order of decreasing $q_\phi(p \mid E_t)$ probability, each candidate is executed on the task's examples, and the first program consistent with all examples is recorded. Two mechanisms make this tractable: **library compression shortens target programs** (enumeration is exponential in length, so compression yields exponential savings [1]), and **the recognizer prunes dead branches** by down-weighting task-irrelevant productions.

```python
# Wake-phase search: recognition-guided best-first enumeration (simplified)
import heapq

def wake_search(examples, grammar, recognizer, timeout, beam=10**5):
    frontier = [(-recognizer.logprob(p, examples), p)
                for p in grammar.initial_productions()]
    heapq.heapify(frontier)
    seen, start = set(), time()
    while frontier and time() - start < timeout:
        neglogp, prog = heapq.heappop(frontier)
        if prog in seen: continue
        seen.add(prog)
        if all(eval(prog, x) == y for x, y in examples):
            return prog                      # solution found
        for child in grammar.expand(prog):   # productions ranked by q_phi
            heapq.heappush(frontier, (-recognizer.logprob(child, examples), child))
    return None
```

### 4.2 Abstraction Sleep: E-Graph Refactoring and Compression

The abstraction sleep phase converts solved programs into an **E-graph** — a data structure representing many equivalent terms compactly via equivalence classes (E-classes) — and searches for *abstraction candidates*: lambda terms with holes such that substituting corpus subterms into the holes reproduces large portions of the corpus [1]. The classic implementation proceeds by anti-unification: given two program fragments, their least general generalization is a candidate abstraction, and the E-graph makes it possible to consider all pairs simultaneously.

Each candidate $f$ is scored by its *compression gain* $\Delta(f) = |C| - (|f| + |C[f]|)$, where $C[f]$ is the corpus rewritten with calls to $f$. Positive-gain candidates are added greedily, deepest-first, so abstractions call previously learned abstractions — the *hierarchically organized layers* of DreamCoder's libraries [1]. From raw primitives the system discovers `map`/`fold`/sorting combinators (list processing) and polygon/symmetry abstractions (LOGO).

**Stitch** (Bowers et al., POPL 2023), a corpus-guided *top-down* synthesis of abstractions that is 3–4 orders of magnitude faster and uses two orders of magnitude less memory while matching or exceeding DreamCoder's compression quality [2]. Stitch's branch-and-bound search synthesizes abstractions directly from primitives, pruning via syntactic match counts against the corpus.

```haskell
-- Abstraction sleep: compression objective (illustrative)
-- Given corpus C and candidate abstraction f, compute description-length gain.
compressionGain :: Corpus -> Abstraction -> Double
compressionGain corpus f =
    descLen corpus
    - (descLen (abstractionBody f) + descLen (refactor corpus f))
  where
    refactor c f = map (rewriteWith f) c   -- replace matched subterms by calls to f
    descLen      = fromIntegral . sum . map termSize
```

### 4.3 Dreaming Sleep: Training the Recognition Model

The dreaming sleep phase trains $q_\phi$ to *amortize* search from two sources: **replays** — (task, solution) pairs discovered during wake; and **dreams** — programs sampled from $P(p \mid L, \theta)$, executed to synthesize fresh input–output tasks.

Dreams matter because replays cover only tasks the current search can already solve; dreams expose the recognizer to the full distribution induced by the library [1]. The "dream decompiling" refinement (Grand et al.) replaces naive dream training with an unbiased estimator: programs are sampled from the prior and *decompiled* through the recognition process, correcting for the mismatch between the dream distribution and the task distribution and yielding measurably better held-out generalization on list processing, text editing, block towers, symbolic regression, LOGO, and regex domains [3].

### 4.4 Compression-Driven Abstraction Discovery: Theory and Practice

Why does compression discover *meaningful* abstractions rather than arbitrary syntactic chunks? The wake-sleep loop provides natural selection: abstractions capturing genuine domain structure (map/fold in list processing, polygon primitives in LOGO) are *reused* in subsequent cycles, raising their production probabilities [1]. This is why held-out generalization improves across cycles even though training-task solution counts grow modestly: a library capturing real structure generalizes better [3].

| Abstraction discovery method | Search strategy | Speed vs. DreamCoder refactor | Memory | Quality (compressivity) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DreamCoder E-graph refactoring [1] | Deductive, bottom-up anti-unification | 1× (baseline) | 1× (baseline) | High |
| Stitch CTS [2] | Corpus-guided top-down branch-and-bound | 10³–10⁴× faster | ~10⁻²× | Comparable or better |
| Dream decompiling [3] | Amortized Bayesian (dream-based) | — (orthogonal) | — | Better held-out generalization |

### 4.5 Comparison with LLM-Based Synthesis

Large language models (Codex, AlphaCode, and successors) approach synthesis from the opposite direction: instead of searching a DSL guided by a learned policy over a learned library, they *sample* programs from a model trained on billions of lines of human-written code, conditioned on natural-language specifications [5][6]. The comparison is instructive along five axes:

| Axis | DreamCoder-style | LLM-based (Codex/AlphaCode) |
|---|---|---|
| Specification | Input–output examples (inductive) | Natural language + examples |
| Knowledge source | Learned from scratch per domain | Pretrained on internet-scale code |
| Programs produced | Short, library-compressed, interpretable | Often verbose, idiomatic, opaque |
| Search cost | Amortized via recognizer; exponential in compressed length | Massive sampling (AlphaCode: thousands of candidates) + test filtering [6] |
| Generalization | Compositional reuse of learned abstractions | Memorization-tinted; struggles out-of-distribution |

LLMs excel where natural-language specifications and mainstream languages dominate: Codex dramatically exceeded prior models on docstring-to-code tasks [5][6], and AlphaCode reached top-54% on Codeforces by sampling thousands of candidates and clustering by test behavior [6]. But this costs *interpretability* and *data efficiency*: enormous sampling overhead, single-pass generation without self-verification [6], and a DSL inherited from pretraining — LLMs cannot invent a *new* vocabulary of abstractions for a novel domain the way DreamCoder does.

The two paradigms are increasingly *hybridized*. **LILO** (Grand, Wong et al., ICLR 2024) combines LLM-guided synthesis with Stitch-style compression: the LLM proposes candidate solutions in a general-purpose language, and Stitch compresses them into a documented, interpretable library — yielding richer libraries than either alone [4]. This neurosymbolic direction suggests the field's endgame: LLMs supply the search prior (breadth), while compression-driven library learning supplies the abstraction machinery (depth).

```rust
// Stitch-style corpus-guided top-down abstraction search (conceptual sketch)
fn stitch_search(corpus: &Corpus, max_arms: usize) -> Vec<Abstraction> {
    let mut best: Vec<(Abstraction, f64)> = vec![];
    let mut agenda: Vec<PartialAbstraction> = vec![PartialAbstraction::hole()];
    while let Some(partial) = agenda.pop() {
        // Upper-bound compression via syntactic match counts against corpus
        let ub = upper_bound_compression(&partial, corpus);
        if ub <= best_gain(&best) { continue; }          // branch and bound
        for ext in partial.extensions() {                // grow from DSL primitives
            if let Some(abs) = ext.as_complete() {
                best.push((abs.clone(), compression_gain(&abs, corpus)));
                best.truncate(max_arms);
            } else { agenda.push(ext); }
        }
    }
    best.into_iter().map(|(a, _)| a).collect()
}
```

## 5. Empirical Evaluation

### 5.1 Domains and Protocol

We follow the evaluation protocol of Ellis et al. [1] and the decompiling follow-up [3] on three representative domains:

- **List processing.** Tasks: sorting, filtering, mapping with arithmetic predicates, run-length encoding, list reversal with conditions. Primitives: `car`, `cdr`, `cons`, `+`, `-`, `=`, `if`, `is-empty`, `Y` (fixpoint).
- **LOGO graphics.** Tasks: drawing polygons, spirals, symmetric figures, nested shapes. Primitives: `move`, `turn`, `pen-up`, `pen-down`, `repeat`.
- **Text editing.** Tasks: reformatting strings, extracting fields, case transformations, delimiter handling. Primitives: character predicates, `concat`, `substr`, regex-like matchers.

Per-cycle wake timeouts are 12 min (batch 10–20) on 20–40 CPUs [3]; metrics are held-out solve rate, median solution length, and corpus compression ratio.

### 5.2 Results

**List processing.** After six cycles, DreamCoder solves 100% of training and ~75% of held-out tasks, including sorting — a 32-primitive solution beyond brute force, found in under 10 minutes in the learned library [1]. The library contains `map`, `fold`, `filter`, and sorting combinators discovered by compression; dream-decompiled recognizers add 5–15 absolute points on held-out tasks [3].

**LOGO graphics.** The system discovers polygon-drawing abstractions, symmetry operators, and spiral generators; held-out solutions compress 4–10× relative to primitive-only programs [1].

**Text editing.** Learned abstractions capture character-class predicates and field-extraction combinators; held-out delimiter-aware reformatting tasks are solved far above the no-library baseline [1][3].

### 5.3 Ablations

Removing the abstraction sleep phase collapses held-out performance: without compression, target programs remain too long for enumeration [1]. Removing the dreaming phase preserves solvability but slows search by an order of magnitude. Jointly learning both — the core claim of [1] — dominates either alone.

## 6. Limitations

**Amortization gap.** Dreams may underrepresent the task distribution and replays cover only solvable tasks; this KL gap between $q_\phi$ and the true posterior bounds the search savings [3]. Dream decompiling narrows but does not close it.

**DSL design sensitivity.** Wake-sleep learning demotes rather than eliminates the DSL designer: the primitive set determines which abstractions are reachable within a few compression steps, and a poor choice (e.g., lacking recursion) strands the system regardless of compute [1].

**Specification bottleneck.** DreamCoder-style systems consume input–output examples, not natural language; conditioning recognizers on docstrings remains an open integration problem, partially addressed by LILO-style hybrids [4].

**Computational cost.** Even with Stitch's 10³–10⁴× speedup in abstraction learning [2], the wake phase remains expensive: each cycle requires minutes of parallel enumerative search per domain. Scaling to domains with thousands of tasks (rather than hundreds) will require further amortization, perhaps via learned *task curricula* or neural surrogates for program execution.

**Evaluation scope.** Held-out tasks are drawn from the same distribution as training tasks; true out-of-distribution transfer (e.g., list processing to tree processing) is largely untested and likely poor without additional mechanisms.

## 7. Conclusion

DreamCoder-style synthesis reframes the problem as *joint inference over programs, libraries, and search policies*: the wake phase solves tasks with recognition-guided search, the abstraction sleep phase compresses solutions into hierarchical libraries, and the dreaming sleep phase amortizes search [1][2][3]. The MDL objective selecting abstractions is exactly the MAP estimate of a hierarchical Bayesian model (Theorem 1).

The empirical record — sorting from list primitives, geometric abstractions from LOGO commands, text combinators from character predicates — shows compression pressure alone can recover much of a hand-crafted DSL's vocabulary [1]. Successors attacked the two main costs: Stitch accelerates abstraction learning by thousands of times [2], and dream decompiling improves recognizer generalization [3]. Meanwhile LLMs supply complementary strengths — natural-language conditioning and internet-scale priors — and the most promising current work (LILO [4]) fuses the two: LLMs propose, compression disposes.

The roadmap is clear: close the amortization gap with better dream distributions, extend recognizers to multimodal specifications (examples *plus* language), and push library learning toward open-ended domains. If these succeed, the vision that motivated DreamCoder — a system that *learns to program by learning the language of programming* — scales from eight small domains to real software engineering.
