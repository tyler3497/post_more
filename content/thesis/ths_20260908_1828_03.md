# P4 Programmable Data Planes: Match-Action Pipelines, In-Band Network Telemetry, and In-Network Computing

## Abstract

The fixed-function switch data plane has been displaced by a programmable substrate in which packet forwarding is determined by a program written in the P4-16 domain-specific language. This thesis develops a rigorous account of P4 programmable data planes: the P4-16 language and the Portable Switch Architecture (PSA), the match-action table abstraction that unifies routing, switching, and access-control tables into a single programmable primitive, the PISA packet-processing pipeline, and the P4Runtime control-plane API. We formalize a cost model for match-action pipelines quantifying stage occupancy, TCAM/SRAM consumption, and parser complexity, and prove a throughput bound relating pipeline depth to per-stage latency. Two applications are analyzed: In-Band Network Telemetry (INT), which embeds per-hop measurements into data packets via a 16-bit instruction bitmap and metadata stack, and SwitchML-style in-network computing, which aggregates machine-learning gradients at line rate for up to 5.5x distributed-training speedups. We close with limitations - no floating-point arithmetic, per-stage resource walls, recirculation penalties, INT/MTU interactions - and future directions in verified compilation and converged in-network computing fabrics.

## 1. Introduction

For four decades, the network switch was a *fixed-function device*: the set of protocols it understood - Ethernet bridging, IP routing, MPLS label switching, VXLAN encapsulation - was burned into silicon at manufacture, and innovation in forwarding behavior required a new application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC) generation. The control plane was programmable through software-defined networking, but the data plane remained opaque, an immutable artifact of the vendor's roadmap. The introduction of the P4 programming language in 2014, with the P4-16 specification published in 2016 [1], inverted this assumption: the data plane itself became a programmable object, described by a protocol-independent program compiled to a reconfigurable match-action pipeline.

This thesis argues that programmable data planes constitute a *computational substrate* in the same sense as the general-purpose CPU or the GPU - a hardware target with a well-defined programming model, a compiler toolchain, and a resource model that admits formal analysis. Three consequences follow. First, the match-action table, P4's central abstraction, subsumes the heterogeneous menagerie of legacy tables (longest-prefix-match, exact-match, ternary, access-control lists) into a single primitive with a uniform cost model, enabling precise reasoning about table composition and resource contention. Second, *In-Band Network Telemetry (INT)* [2] exploits data-plane programmability to embed fine-grained measurements - switch identifiers, queue occupancies, hop latencies, ingress and egress timestamps - directly into the packets being measured, replacing coarse sampled statistics with complete, per-hop, per-packet observability. Third, *in-network computing* turns the switch into an active participant in distributed computation: SwitchML [3] performs gradient aggregation for distributed machine-learning training inside the switch data plane at sub-round-trip-time latency, reducing fabric traffic and delivering up to 5.5x end-to-end training speedups.

The thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 situates P4 within the history of data-plane programmability, from the PISA/RMT architecture to the PSA and Tofino-class targets. Section 3 describes our methodology: formal modeling of match-action pipelines, specification-driven analysis of INT, and quantitative evaluation using published SwitchML benchmarks. Section 4 develops the four deep-dive topics: the P4-16 language, match-action tables and pipeline constraints, INT, and SwitchML-style in-network computing. Section 5 presents empirical results and proofs, including a throughput bound for staged pipelines. Section 6 enumerates limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From Fixed-Function ASICs to PISA

The architectural breakthrough that made P4 hardware-realizable was the *Protocol-Independent Switch Architecture (PISA)*, an evolution of the Reconfigurable Match Table (RMT) design by Bosshart et al. at SIGCOMM 2014. PISA decomposes a switch into a programmable parser, a sequence of *match-action stages* (typically 12 to 32), and a programmable deparser. Each stage provides a fixed quantum of resources: ternary content-addressable memory (TCAM) for wildcard matches, static RAM (SRAM) for exact-match tables, stateful arithmetic logic units (ALUs) for register updates, and a slice of the *packet header vector* (PHV) - a wide register file (often 4-8 Kbit) that carries parsed headers and metadata between stages [5].

Crucially, the *match-action* paradigm is not new - what is new is its programmability. OpenFlow had already standardized a match-action abstraction in 2008, but OpenFlow's tables were fixed in structure and semantics; a switch supported a predetermined pipeline, and the controller could only populate it. P4 generalizes the idea: the *number, width, and match semantics of tables*, the *set of parsed headers*, and the *actions* are all defined by the programmer. As the P4-16 specification states, the data plane "is configured at initialization time to implement the functionality described by the P4 program" and has no built-in knowledge of any network protocol [1].

### 2.2 Architectures: V1Model, PSA, and TNA

P4-16 is *architecture-dependent*: a program is written against an architecture model that defines the programmable blocks (parsers, controls, deparsers) and the *extern* functions available (counters, meters, registers, hash units). The principal architectures are:

| Architecture | Blocks | Externs | Target |
|---|---|---|---|
| V1Model | 6 (parser, checksum verify, ingress, egress, checksum update, deparser) | Counters, meters, registers, direct variants | BMv2 software switch |
| PSA (Portable Switch Architecture) | 6 + ingress deparser/parser | Counter, Meter, Register, Hash, Random, Digest, ActionProfile/Selector | Portable across ASICs |
| TNA (Tofino Native Architecture) | Tofino-specific | Stateful ALUs, hash distribution units | Intel Tofino 1/2/3 |

The PSA, standardized by the P4 Language Consortium, is the industry convergence point: it defines four primary externs - `Counter`, `Meter`, `Register`, and `Hash` - plus `ActionProfile` and `ActionSelector` for equal-cost multipath (ECMP) group selection [1]. V1Model remains the reference for the BMv2 behavioral model, the correctness-first software target (1-5 Gbit/s single-core). The p4c compiler provides backends for BMv2, DPDK, eBPF, and Tofino (the latter via the proprietary Barefoot/Intel SDE).

### 2.3 P4Runtime and the Control Plane

A P4 program defines the data-plane schema; the *control plane* populates it. P4Runtime is the P4.org-standardized, gRPC/protobuf-based API through which a controller inserts table entries, configures multicast groups, reads counters, and receives digests and packet-in events. Compilation of a P4 program yields two artifacts: a *data-plane configuration* (e.g., a `.json` for BMv2 or a Tofino binary) and a *P4Info* file describing every table, action, counter, and meter - the schema the controller uses to address data-plane objects by numeric ID. This clean separation - program defines structure, runtime fills state - is what makes P4 programs *protocol-independent* while remaining operationally deployable [1][6].

A minimal P4Runtime table-entry insertion illustrates the pattern:

```python
# Python: insert an LPM entry via P4Runtime (p4runtime_lib style)
import p4runtime_lib.helper as helper

table_entry = p4info_helper.buildTableEntry(
    table_name="MyIngress.ipv4_lpm",
    match_fields={"hdr.ipv4.dstAddr": ("10.0.1.0", 24)},  # LPM match
    action_name="MyIngress.ipv4_forward",
    action_params={"dstAddr": "00:11:22:33:44:55", "port": 3})
switch.WriteTableEntry(table_entry)
print("Installed LPM entry: 10.0.1.0/24 -> port 3")
```

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds along three complementary axes:

1. **Specification-driven formalization.** We derive the pipeline model directly from the P4-16 language specification [1] and the INT dataplane specification [2], treating the specifications as ground truth for semantics. Where the specifications admit implementation freedom (e.g., TCAM vs. SRAM realization of ternary tables), we parameterize the model and derive bounds that hold across realizations.
2. **Cost-model analysis.** We define a resource-consumption model for match-action pipelines - stage occupancy, TCAM/SRAM width-depth products, PHV container allocation, and parser state count - and prove a throughput bound (Theorem 1) relating pipeline depth, per-stage latency, and sustained line rate.
3. **Empirical evaluation from published artifacts.** We analyze the quantitative results reported for SwitchML [3]: training speedups up to 5.5x, fabric traffic reduction up to 2.27x on 100 Gbit/s networks, and aggregation latency at sub-RTT. We reproduce the INT overhead analysis from the specification, computing per-hop byte costs for representative instruction bitmaps.

> **Theorem 1 (Pipeline Throughput Bound).** Let a match-action pipeline consist of S stages, each with worst-case combinational latency tau seconds, clocked at frequency f <= 1/tau. If every packet traverses exactly the S stages in order (feed-forward, no recirculation), the pipeline sustains a packet rate R = f packets/second independent of S, and a line rate L = f * Bbar bits/second for mean packet size Bbar bits. Recirculation of a fraction rho of packets reduces effective throughput to R_eff = f / (1 + rho).

*Proof sketch.* Feed-forward pipelining admits one packet per stage per clock, so the initiation interval is one clock regardless of depth; latency is S/f but throughput is f. Recirculation re-injects packets at the ingress, consuming one additional pipeline slot per recirculated packet; with fraction rho recirculating once, the effective arrival rate of *new* packets is f/(1+rho). QED.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The P4-16 Language: Headers, Parsers, Tables, Actions

P4-16 programs are built from six core constructs. A minimal IPv4-forwarding excerpt illustrates the idiom:

```p4
/* P4-16 (v1model): header, parser, table, actions */
#include <core.p4>
#include <v1model.p4>

header ethernet_t {
    bit<48> dstAddr;
    bit<48> srcAddr;
    bit<16> etherType;
}

header ipv4_t {
    bit<4>  version;
    bit<4>  ihl;
    bit<8>  diffserv;
    bit<16> totalLen;
    bit<32> srcAddr;
    bit<32> dstAddr;
}

struct headers { ethernet_t ethernet; ipv4_t ipv4; }

parser MyParser(packet_in packet, out headers hdr,
                inout metadata meta, inout standard_metadata_t sm) {
    state start { transition parse_ethernet; }
    state parse_ethernet {
        packet.extract(hdr.ethernet);
        transition select(hdr.ethernet.etherType) {
            0x0800: parse_ipv4;
            default: accept;
        }
    }
    state parse_ipv4 {
        packet.extract(hdr.ipv4);
        transition accept;
    }
}

control MyIngress(inout headers hdr, inout metadata meta,
                  inout standard_metadata_t sm) {
    action drop()      { mark_to_drop(sm); }
    action ipv4_forward(bit<48> dstAddr, bit<9> port) {
        sm.egress_spec = port;
        hdr.ethernet.srcAddr = hdr.ethernet.dstAddr;
        hdr.ethernet.dstAddr = dstAddr;
        hdr.ipv4.ttl = hdr.ipv4.ttl - 1;
    }
    table ipv4_lpm {
        key     = { hdr.ipv4.dstAddr : lpm; }
        actions = { ipv4_forward; drop; NoAction; }
        size    = 1024;
        default_action = drop();
    }
    apply { ipv4_lpm.apply(); }
}
```

The *parser* is a finite-state machine: each state extracts a header and transitions on field values, terminating in `accept` or `reject`. Tables bind a *key* (fields with match kinds `exact`, `lpm`, `ternary`, `range`, `selector`) to *actions* - straight-line code fragments parameterized by control-plane-supplied data. The `apply` block expresses data-dependent control flow over table invocations. Type safety is notable: P4-16 has arbitrary-width bitstrings (`bit<48>`), restricted implicit casts, and a deliberately simplified inference scheme whose metatheory has been mechanized in the Petr4 project [6].

The *deparser* reassembles the packet from headers, emitting only headers marked valid. Between parser and deparser sit the ingress and egress match-action controls - the programmable heart of the pipeline.

### 4.2 Match-Action Tables and the Pipeline Resource Model

A match-action unit performs three operations per packet: (i) construct a lookup key from packet fields or metadata, (ii) look the key up in the table, selecting an action and its parameters, and (iii) execute the action. The generality of this primitive is its power: longest-prefix routing, exact-match bridging, ternary ACLs, ECMP group selection via `ActionSelector`, and even stateful NAT via `Register` externs are all instances of the same abstraction [1].

Programmability is bounded by per-stage resources, and the compiler's job is fundamentally a *packing problem*:

| Resource | Typical Tofino-class budget | Consumed by |
|---|---|---|
| TCAM (ternary) | ~0.5-1 Mbit per stage | `ternary`/`lpm` keys, ACLs |
| SRAM | ~1-2 Mbit per stage | Exact-match tables, registers, counters |
| Stateful ALUs | 4-8 per stage | Register read-modify-write, meters |
| PHV containers | 4-8 Kbit total | Parsed headers + metadata |
| Parser states | hundreds | Protocol nesting depth |

Key consequences follow. *Table width* (key bits) and *depth* (entries) trade against each other within fixed SRAM/TCAM. Wide keys force tables to span stages; deep tables consume multiple stages' SRAM. Stateful operations are confined to one ALU per register per stage - a register cannot be updated twice in one pass without recirculation, which Theorem 1 shows costs throughput. The PHV is the scarcest resource in practice: every header field and metadata bit occupies containers, and *INT's per-hop metadata must be carried in the PHV* between the parser and the stage that inserts it.

The compiler (p4c plus target backends) performs *table placement*: mapping logical tables to physical stages subject to dependency constraints. A table that reads a field written by another table's action must be placed downstream. This is NP-hard in general (reducible from scheduling with resource constraints), and production compilers use heuristic list-scheduling; placement failure - "table too large for pipeline" - is the characteristic compile error of P4 development, and resolving it requires algorithmic restructuring, e.g., splitting a wide ternary table into a cascade of narrower exact-match stages.

### 4.3 In-Band Network Telemetry: Measuring the Network with Its Own Packets

INT, specified by the P4.org Applications Working Group [2], inverts the traditional telemetry model. Instead of the switch exporting statistics *about* packets (sFlow, NetFlow, IPFIX sampling), the switch writes measurements *into* the packets themselves. Three roles exist:

- **INT source**: inserts a fixed INT header (12 bytes: INT shim, metadata header with domain, instruction bitmap, remaining hop count) plus optionally its own metadata, typically by cloning or by matching probe traffic.
- **INT transit**: at each hop, reads the 16-bit *instruction bitmap* and pushes the requested metadata onto a *metadata stack*. Bit 0 requests the 4-byte switch ID; bit 2 requests hop latency; bit 3 requests queue ID plus 24-bit queue occupancy; bits 4-5 request 8-byte ingress/egress timestamps; bit 15 requests a checksum complement. Each set bit (except bits 4-6, which take 8 bytes) contributes 4 bytes per hop [2].
- **INT sink**: strips the INT headers and metadata stack and forwards a *telemetry report* (often via a postcard or mirror to a collector) containing the complete per-hop record.

1. The source selects probe packets by match (e.g., a dedicated UDP port).
2. Each transit hop pushes metadata per the instruction bitmap.
3. The sink strips INT headers and emits a report to the collector.

The overhead is deterministic and computable. For an instruction bitmap requesting switch ID, hop latency, and queue occupancy (bits 0, 2, 3), each transit hop appends 12 bytes; across a 5-hop path with the 12-byte fixed header, the packet grows by 12 + 5 x 12 = 72 bytes. If hop latency is the only quantity of interest, the per-hop cost is 4 bytes - a remarkable economy relative to per-packet export. The specification carefully handles the MTU interaction: a transit switch that cannot insert metadata without exceeding egress MTU must *insert nothing* and set the M bit, rather than fragment - IPv6 forbids intermediate fragmentation, and fragmenting telemetry-bearing packets would defeat correlation at the collector [2].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Pipeline Performance Envelope

We instantiate Theorem 1 with Tofino-class parameters: a 12.8 Tbit/s device processes minimum-size packets at roughly 19 billion packets/second across its serdes lanes. A single deep pipeline clocked near 1 GHz sustains line rate *independent of program complexity*, provided the program is feed-forward - the fundamental performance argument for the staged architecture. The proof's recirculation term is the quantitative cost of algorithmic expressiveness: an INT implementation that recirculates 10% of packets to handle deep metadata stacks (rho = 0.1) pays a ~9% throughput tax, R_eff = f/1.1.

### 5.2 INT Overhead Measurements

From the INT v2.0/v2.1 dataplane specifications [2], we tabulate deterministic per-hop costs:

| Instruction bitmap | Metadata per hop | 5-hop path total (incl. 12 B fixed) |
|---|---|---|
| Switch ID only (bit 0) | 4 B | 32 B |
| + hop latency (bit 2) | 8 B | 52 B |
| + queue occupancy (bit 3) | 12 B | 72 B |
| Full baseline (bits 0-8) | 40 B | 212 B |

For a 1500-byte MTU path, the full-baseline configuration consumes ~14% of the MTU in telemetry - acceptable for dedicated probe traffic, prohibitive for production data traffic, which is why deployments restrict INT to sampled flows or probe packets and rely on the M-bit mechanism to degrade gracefully under MTU pressure [2].

### 5.3 SwitchML: Aggregation at Line Rate

The SwitchML evaluation [3] on a Tofino-based testbed with up to 16 workers reports:

- **Training speedup**: up to 5.5x on real models (e.g., deep image classifiers) versus NCCL ring all-reduce baselines, with gains largest where communication dominates compute.
- **Traffic reduction**: up to 2.27x less fabric traffic on 100 Gbit/s networks, because each gradient chunk traverses each link once rather than W times.
- **Scalability**: aggregation goodput independent of worker count W; ring all-reduce latency grows as O(W).
- **Quantization fidelity**: fixed-point scaling with k fractional bits introduces bounded relative error O(2^-k), empirically negligible for convergence on the evaluated models.

These numbers validate the thesis's central claim: moving computation into the match-action pipeline does not merely accelerate the network - it *changes the asymptotic communication complexity* of the distributed algorithm, from O(W) per-worker traffic to O(1).

### 5.4 Correctness Argument for In-Network Aggregation

> **Theorem 2 (Aggregation correctness).** Let W workers each contribute value v_w for chunk slot s, with the switch maintaining register A_s initialized to 0 and an arrival bitmap B_s. If the P4 program atomically executes A_s <- A_s + v_w; B_s <- B_s union {w} on each arrival, and multicasts A_s exactly when |B_s| = W, then every worker receives the sum of v_w over all workers for slot s, provided each worker's contribution arrives at least once and the end-host protocol retransmits slots never acknowledged.## 6. Limitations

**No floating-point arithmetic.** P4 targets provide integer ALUs only; SwitchML's quantization is a workaround, not a solution. Workloads requiring true floating point (e.g., in-network inference with nonlinear activations) must approximate with tables or offload - a fundamental expressiveness ceiling of current match-action hardware.

**Per-stage resource walls.** TCAM, SRAM, ALU, and PHV budgets are fixed per stage; programs that exceed them fail at compile time with limited diagnostic guidance. Table placement is NP-hard, and heuristic compilers can reject programs that a smarter placement would accept. Stateful state is *stage-local*: a register updated in stage 5 cannot be read in stage 3, forcing algorithmic contortions.

**Recirculation and cloning costs.** Features requiring a second pass (deep INT stacks, multi-update state machines) pay the throughput tax of Theorem 1 or consume mirror/clone bandwidth. There is no free lunch for non-feed-forward computation.

**INT's MTU and deployment friction.** Per-hop metadata growth interacts adversarially with MTU; the M-bit degrades gracefully but silently drops visibility. INT also requires *every hop* on the path to be programmable and INT-aware - a single legacy hop breaks the metadata stack semantics - limiting deployment to greenfield or fully-upgraded fabrics [2].

**Control-plane scalability.** P4Runtime programs tables entry-by-entry over gRPC; installing millions of entries or reacting to microsecond-scale events from the controller reintroduces the control-plane latency the data plane was meant to escape. Fast-path reactions must live in the data plane (e.g., digest-triggered local failover), with the controller handling only slow-path policy.

**Ecosystem concentration.** The highest-performance P4 targets remain proprietary (Tofino via the Intel SDE), while the open-source p4c backends (BMv2, eBPF, DPDK) trade orders of magnitude of throughput for accessibility. The P4 ecosystem's center of gravity is thus split between research software switches and vendor-controlled silicon [5].

## 7. Conclusion

This thesis has presented programmable data planes as a computational substrate: P4-16 gives the language, PSA gives the portable architecture, match-action tables give the universal primitive, P4Runtime gives the control interface, INT gives observability, and SwitchML gives proof that the network can *compute*, not merely forward. The formal results - the pipeline throughput bound and the aggregation correctness argument - together with measured speedups of up to 5.5x and deterministic telemetry overheads, establish both the power and the price of the paradigm: line-rate performance for feed-forward programs, paid for in per-stage resource budgets, integer-only arithmetic, and co-design obligations with control and transport protocols.

The research frontier is clear. Type systems and verified compilation (Petr4 [6]) promise data-plane programs with machine-checked guarantees. Congestion-aware INT - telemetry that adapts its instruction bitmap to network conditions - would make observability itself a closed loop. And the SwitchML pattern, generalized, points toward a *converged in-network computing fabric* in which switches routinely execute reductions, filters, and consensus steps for distributed systems. The fixed-function switch is not coming back; the interesting question is what we will teach the programmable one to do next.
