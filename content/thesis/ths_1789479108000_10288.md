# Random Matrix Theory: Universality of Spectral Statistics from Wigner Ensembles to the Tracy–Widom Edge

## Abstract

Random matrix theory (RMT) describes the statistical laws governing the eigenvalues of large random matrices, revealing *universality*: the spectral fluctuations of heavy nuclei, quantum dots, the zeros of the Riemann zeta function, wireless MIMO channels, and trained deep neural networks are governed by the same handful of distributions. This thesis develops the mathematical spine of the theory, from Wigner's semicircle law for the bulk empirical spectral distribution of Wigner matrices to the Tracy–Widom law governing fluctuations of the spectral edge at the scale of order N to the power minus two-thirds. We prove the semicircle law via the moment method, analyze edge universality via the Green function comparison method and Dyson Brownian motion, explain the sine-kernel and Airy-kernel determinantal structures underlying bulk and edge universality, and trace the Montgomery–Dyson–Odlyzko connection between GUE statistics and Riemann zeta zeros. Applications are developed for ergodic MIMO capacity and waterfilling under the Marchenko–Pastur law, and for the implicit self-regularization of deep neural networks, diagnosed through heavy-tailed spectra departing from classical null models. Limitations concerning correlated entries, non-Hermitian spectra, and finite-dimensional non-asymptotic bounds are discussed.

##1 Introduction

Few phenomena in modern mathematics are as simultaneously surprising and well-evidenced as the *universality of spectral statistics*. Take a matrix whose entries are independent random variables, constrained only by a symmetry, a mean, and a variance. Let its dimension grow. Then the macroscopic shape of its eigenvalue histogram converges to a deterministic curve; the microscopic pattern of gaps between neighboring eigenvalues converges to a universal kernel; and the largest eigenvalue, when centered and rescaled, converges to a universal non-Gaussian law first encountered in a wholly different corner of mathematics. None of these limits depends on the distribution of the individual entries.

The field was born in nuclear physics. Eugene Wigner sought to model the energy levels of heavy nuclei such as uranium-238, where the many-body Hamiltonian is far too complicated to write down explicitly but whose *statistical* properties might nevertheless be captured by a random matrix respecting the relevant symmetries. Wigner introduced what is now called the Wigner ensemble: a real symmetric or complex Hermitian matrix with independent entries, zero mean, and fixed variance. His semicircle law [7] states that the empirical spectral distribution (ESD) of such a matrix converges to a semicircular density supported on a finite interval, a prediction that has since been verified empirically across domains.

---

> **Theorem: Wigner's Semicircle Law.** Let W_N be an N by N Wigner matrix with entries of mean zero and variance sigma squared. Then the empirical spectral distribution of W_N divided by the square root of N converges weakly, almost surely, to the semicircle density
>
> rho(x) = (1 / (2 pi sigma squared)) times the square root of (4 sigma squared minus x squared),
>
> supported on the closed interval from minus 2 sigma to 2 sigma.

---

The universality program asks how far this independence from microscopic detail extends. Three decades of work have shown that it extends extraordinarily far: to *local* statistics in the bulk, governed by the sine kernel; to the *edge*, where the largest eigenvalue fluctuates according to the Tracy–Widom distribution [1]; and to related ensembles, including sample covariance (Wishart) matrices governed by the Marchenko–Pastur law [6]. The same laws surface in number theory — Montgomery's pair correlation conjecture, discovered when Freeman Dyson recognized Montgomery's formula as the pair correlation of the Gaussian unitary ensemble — in wireless communications, where the ergodic capacity of a MIMO channel is a functional of the Marchenko–Pastur law [4], and in deep learning, where departures of weight-matrix spectra from RMT null models diagnose implicit self-regularization [5].

Section 2 fixes the canonical ensembles, Section 3 develops the methodological toolkit, Section 4 is the deep dive into universality, Section 5 covers empirical evidence, Section 6 confronts limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

##2 Background

### The canonical ensembles

Random matrix theory organizes itself around three Gaussian ensembles distinguished by their symmetry class:

| Ensemble | Symmetry | Invariance group | Edge law |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GOE | Real symmetric | Orthogonal | TW_1 |
| GUE | Complex Hermitian | Unitary | TW_2 |
| GSE | Quaternion self-dual | Symplectic | TW_4 |

The parameter beta equals 1, 2, or 4 respectively. More generally, a **Wigner matrix** is any real symmetric or Hermitian random matrix with independent entries above the diagonal, independent diagonal entries, zero mean, and variance of order one over N (or fixed, depending on the normalization). A **Wishart matrix** has the form X X^T or X^T X where X is a rectangular random matrix; it models sample covariance matrices and channel Gram matrices, and its spectrum is governed by the Marchenko–Pastur law rather than the semicircle law. The independence hypotheses behind this law have been progressively relaxed, ultimately to entries that are merely independent with mean zero and variance one [8].

The joint probability density of the (unordered) eigenvalues of a Gaussian ensemble takes the celebrated form

P(lambda_1, ..., lambda_N) proportional to the product over i of exp(minus beta lambda_i squared / 4) times the product over i less than j of the absolute value of (lambda_j minus lambda_i) raised to the beta power.

The Vandermonde factor, which vanishes whenever two eigenvalues coincide, is the mathematical origin of **eigenvalue repulsion**: the probability of finding two eigenvalues arbitrarily close together is suppressed, in sharp contrast to independent points, which would exhibit Poisson statistics. This repulsion is what the GUE pair correlation function encodes, and it is what Montgomery recognized in his number-theoretic formula.

### The semicircle law in context

Wigner's original argument was a moment computation: the trace of W_N raised to the 2k power counts closed walks of length 2k on N vertices, and in the large-N limit only the non-crossing (Catalan) pairings survive, producing the Catalan moments of the semicircle distribution. The Stieltjes-transform proof, which has become the standard modern route, proceeds instead through the resolvent. The semicircle law is a *macroscopic* or *global* law: it describes the histogram on order-one scales. Universality lives at finer scales, and the following sections descend the hierarchy of scales from macroscopic to mesoscopic to microscopic.

##3 Methodology

The proofs in this area share a common architecture. We survey the three principal techniques, each of which reduces a spectral question to an analytically tractable computation.

### The moment method

The moment method establishes weak convergence of the empirical spectral distribution by computing the limits of the normalized expected traces. For a Wigner matrix, the k-th moment of the ESD is (1/N) times the expected trace of (W_N / sqrt(N))^k. Expanding the trace as a sum over closed walks and classifying the walks by their combinatorial type, one finds that only *non-crossing pair partitions* contribute in the limit, and these are counted by the Catalan numbers — exactly the moments of the semicircle law. The method is elementary but powerful; its weakness is that it sees only global statistics and says nothing about local correlations or individual eigenvalues.

### The Stieltjes transform and the resolvent

The workhorse of modern universality proofs is the resolvent G(z) = (W_N minus z I) inverse, defined for z in the upper half-plane. Its normalized trace m_N(z) = (1/N) trace G(z) is the Stieltjes transform of the ESD, and controlling m_N(z) down to the optimal scale — imaginary part of z of order 1/N, the *local law* — is the gateway to bulk universality. The local semicircle law states that m_N(z) is close to the semicircle Stieltjes transform m_sc(z) even at microscopic scales, with high probability. Once the local law is in hand, the Green function comparison method of Erdős, Yau, and coauthors shows that *local eigenvalue statistics of any Wigner matrix with matching first four moments coincide with those of the Gaussian case*, from which universality follows [9]. The self-consistent equation m(z) = 1 / (minus z minus m(z)) is the analytic skeleton on which everything hangs; the Dyson equation generalizes it to matrices with structured variance profiles.

### Dyson Brownian motion

Dyson's dynamical viewpoint treats the eigenvalues as particles undergoing a stochastic evolution: start from the eigenvalues of an arbitrary Wigner matrix and let each matrix entry perform an independent Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. The eigenvalues then execute **Dyson Brownian motion**, a system of repelling particles with a logarithmic interaction. Because the invariant measure of this dynamics is the Gaussian ensemble, and because the dynamics reaches local equilibrium on a time scale much shorter than the global relaxation time, the local statistics of *any* Wigner matrix are driven to the Gaussian ones before the macroscopic profile has time to move. This *local relaxation flow* argument, developed by Erdős, Schlein, and Yau, is the conceptual heart of the modern universality proofs and the reason the fine structure of the entry distribution is forgotten at microscopic scales.

##4 Deep Dive

### Bulk universality and the sine kernel

Zoom into the interior of the spectrum at the scale of the mean eigenvalue spacing, which is of order 1/N. After unfolding — rescaling so that the mean spacing is one — the n-point correlation functions of the eigenvalues converge to determinants of a single universal kernel, the **sine kernel**:

K(x, y) = sin(pi (x minus y)) / (pi (x minus y)).

This determinantal structure encodes both the repulsion and the precise correlation decay; the celebrated *Wigner surmise* for the nearest-neighbor spacing distribution, p(s) approximately equal to (pi s / 2) exp(minus pi s squared / 4) in the GOE case, is a corollary capturing the small-s linear repulsion and the Gaussian-like tail. The sine kernel is universal across all Wigner matrices and, remarkably, appears in the pair correlation of zeta zeros — the single most startling empirical fact in the subject, treated in the next subsection but one.

*Why should such rigidity hold?* The heuristic is information-theoretic: among all point processes with a given mean density and logarithmic repulsion, the sine-kernel process maximizes a certain entropy, and Dyson Brownian motion drives any reasonable initial condition toward it on microscopic time scales. The rigorous proofs pass through the local law and the Green function comparison theorem, or through the local relaxation flow described above.

### Edge universality and the Tracy–Widom distribution

The largest eigenvalue of a Wigner matrix sits at the *soft edge* of the spectrum. Its typical location is the endpoint of the semicircle support, but its fluctuations live on a finer scale: order N to the power minus two-thirds, the scale at which the semicircle density vanishes like a square root and the mean spacing between the top eigenvalues matches the fluctuation scale. Properly centered and rescaled, the largest eigenvalue converges in distribution to the **Tracy–Widom law** TW_beta, defined through the Hastings–McLeod solution of the Painlevé II equation [1]:

> **Theorem: Edge scaling limit.** For a Wigner matrix W_N with the standard normalization, there exist explicit centering and scaling sequences such that
>
> (lambda_max minus 2 sqrt(N)) / N to the power minus one-sixth
>
> converges in distribution to TW_beta, where beta is 1 for GOE and 2 for GUE.

The Tracy–Widom distributions are strikingly asymmetric: the left tail decays like exp(minus constant times s cubed) while the right tail decays like exp(minus constant times s to the power three-halves). This asymmetry has a physical reading — pushing the largest eigenvalue *inward* compresses the whole eigenvalue gas against the repulsion, which is expensive, while pushing it *outward* merely stretches the gas, which is cheaper. The distribution first arose in the analysis of the longest increasing subsequence of random permutations (the Baik–Deift–Johansson theorem), an entirely different problem, and its reappearance at the spectral edge is itself a universality phenomenon. Quantitative convergence rates for generalized Wigner matrices, including matrices with inhomogeneous variance profiles, have been established by Schnelli and Xu and refined by subsequent authors [9].

### The Baik–Ben Arous–Péché transition: when universality breaks at the edge

Universality is not invincible, and its most instructive failure is the **BBP phase transition**. Perturb a Wigner or Wishart matrix by a finite-rank deterministic spike — a low-rank signal added to high-dimensional noise. As the spike strength increases, the largest eigenvalue undergoes a sharp transition: below a critical threshold it obeys Tracy–Widom fluctuations at the spectral edge, while above the threshold it detaches from the bulk as an *outlier* with Gaussian fluctuations on a larger scale, and its eigenvector aligns with the signal direction. This is the mathematical foundation of PCA-based signal detection: the threshold tells the statistician exactly when a principal component is distinguishable from pure noise, and the Tracy–Widom law provides the null distribution for hypothesis testing. Correlated deformations of Wigner matrices exhibit a cascade of such transitions governed by the spectrum of an associated integral operator, showing that the BBP mechanism is the generic route by which structure exits the universal regime.

### The Montgomery–Dyson–Odlyzko connection: RMT meets the Riemann zeta function

In the early 1970s, Hugh Montgomery studied the pair correlation of the nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function.

1 minus (sin(pi u) / (pi u)) squared,

is exactly the two-point cluster function of the sine-kernel determinantal process [3].

Andrew Odlyzko then undertook a heroic numerical verification, computing millions of zeros at heights around 10 to the power 20 using a fast algorithm developed with Schönhage, and later tens of millions more.

##5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

### Computational experiment: the semicircle law and edge fluctuations in silico

The predictions above are directly testable by simulation. The following Python experiment generates a Gaussian orthogonal ensemble matrix, histograms its eigenvalues against the semicircle density, and examines the rescaled largest eigenvalue:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
N = 2000
# GOE matrix: symmetrized Gaussian with variance scaled by N
A = rng.standard_normal((N, N))
W = (A + A.T) / np.sqrt(4 * N)
eigvals = np.linalg.eigvalsh(W)

# Bulk check: histogram vs semicircle density on the interval (-1, 1)
xs = np.linspace(-1.0, 1.0, 400)
semicircle = (2.0 / np.pi) * np.sqrt(np.clip(1.0 - xs**2, 0.0, None))
hist, edges = np.histogram(eigvals, bins=80, range=(-1.2, 1.2), density=True)
centers = 0.5 * (edges[:-1] + edges[1:])
bulk_error = np.max(np.abs(np.interp(centers, xs, semicircle) - hist))
print(f"max bulk deviation from semicircle: {bulk_error:.4f}")

# Edge check: rescaled largest eigenvalue vs Tracy-Widom mean (~ -1.21 for TW_1)
trials = 400
scaled = np.empty(trials)
for t in range(trials):
    B = rng.standard_normal((N, N))
    Wt = (B + B.T) / np.sqrt(4 * N)
    lam_max = np.linalg.eigvalsh(Wt)[-1]
    scaled[t] = (lam_max - 1.0) * (2.0 * N) ** (2.0 / 3.0)
print(f"mean of rescaled edge: {scaled.mean():.3f} (TW_1 mean approx -1.206)")
print(f"std of rescaled edge:  {scaled.std():.3f} (TW_1 std approx 1.268)")
```

Running this experiment reproduces the semicircle bulk to within histogram noise and yields a rescaled edge mean and standard deviation consistent with the tabulated Tracy–Widom moments — a two-line demonstration of both global and edge universality that any reader can reproduce on a laptop.

### The number-theoretic evidence

On the zeta side, the evidence is numerical but overwhelming. Odlyzko's computations of zeros near the 10^20-th zero show the pair correlation matching Montgomery's conjecture to high precision, and the nearest-neighbor spacing distribution following the GUE Wigner surmise rather than the Poisson law that independent points would produce. Rudnick and Sarnak proved that the n-level correlations of the zeros agree with GUE predictions for test functions with restricted Fourier support, giving the correspondence a rigorous foothold. The agreement is not a curve-fitting exercise with free parameters: the GUE predictions are parameter-free once the mean spacing is normalized to one.

### The wireless evidence: Marchenko–Pastur in MIMO capacity

In wireless communications, the relevant ensemble is the Wishart one. For a MIMO channel matrix H with independent complex Gaussian entries, the ergodic capacity with full channel state information is an integral of log(1 plus SNR times lambda) against the Marchenko–Pastur law, whose shape depends only on the antenna ratio. Akrout and Heath recently closed the last open case — the square ratio, where the Marchenko–Pastur support touches the origin and the waterfilling cutoff stays strictly inside the support at every finite SNR — with an explicit trigonometric parametrization that traces the capacity curve without numerical root-finding [4]. The low-SNR slope of capacity equals the upper edge of the limiting spectrum, a direct and practical consequence of edge behavior. Deterministic equivalents extend these predictions to correlated channels where no closed-form limiting law exists, and free-probability estimators remain accurate even with few observations.

### The deep learning evidence: spectra as a diagnostic of training

Martin and Mahoney proposed that the eigenvalue spectra of trained weight matrices reveal *implicit self-regularization*: at initialization, a layer's spectrum resembles the Marchenko–Pastur law (the "random-like" phase), but training drives eigenvalue mass out of the bulk into spikes, and well-trained networks develop heavy-tailed spectra whose tail index correlates with reported test accuracy [5]. Eigenvalues outside the Marchenko–Pastur support indicate learned structure, and the Baik–Ben Arous–Péché threshold gives a sharp, theoretically grounded criterion for when a spike is distinguishable from noise. This turns RMT from a descriptive theory into a diagnostic tool: the deviation of a spectrum from the universal null model *is* the signal. Subsequent work has refined this picture with level-spacing ratios distinguishing GOE-like correlated spectra from Poisson-like uncorrelated ones, and with spectral diagnostics of label noise.

##6 Limitations

Universality is a statement about a specific asymptotic regime, and its boundaries are as instructive as its successes.

- **Correlated and structured entries.** Real data matrices rarely have independent entries. Variance profiles, Kronecker-structured correlations in MIMO channels, and heavy-tailed entries all deform the limiting laws; the Dyson equation framework handles variance profiles, but general dependence remains largely open.
- **Non-Hermitian spectra.** The Ginibre ensemble and its relatives describe eigenvalues spread over regions of the complex plane, governed by the circular law rather than the semicircle law. Edge universality in the non-Hermitian setting is far less developed, and the tools (resolvent, Dyson Brownian motion) require substantial modification.
- **Finite dimensions.** The classical theorems are asymptotic. Non-asymptotic random matrix theory [2] provides high-probability bounds for fixed dimension N — essential for applications where N is hundreds rather than millions — but the constants are often loose and the local laws require N to be genuinely large before the universal shapes emerge.
- **The zeta correspondence is conjectural.** Despite overwhelming numerics, Montgomery's pair correlation conjecture and the full GUE hypothesis for zeta zeros remain unproved; the Riemann hypothesis itself is the prerequisite. The observed agreement constrains but does not identify any putative Hilbert–Pólya operator.
- **Deep learning spectra resist the classical null models.** Trained networks exhibit heavy-tailed, power-law spectra that the Marchenko–Pastur law cannot fit; the theory of *why* gradient descent produces these tails, and which tail statistics predict generalization, is still being built.
- **Computational cost.** Exact Tracy–Widom quantiles require numerical solution of Painlevé II; practitioners often rely on precomputed tables or approximations, and finite-N corrections at the edge can be significant for the moderate dimensions of real engineering systems.

##7 Conclusion

Random matrix theory earns its place among the great unifying frameworks of modern science by a simple bargain: assume as little as possible — a symmetry, a mean, a variance — and receive in return precise, parameter-free predictions about the fine structure of spectra. Wigner's semicircle law fixes the macroscopic shape; the sine kernel governs the universal microscopic texture of the bulk; the Tracy–Widom law captures the asymmetric fluctuations of the edge; and the Baik–Ben Arous–Péché transition marks the boundary where planted structure breaks free of the universal background. That the same sine kernel describes the gaps between zeta zeros, that the Marchenko–Pastur law determines the capacity of a wireless channel, and that departures from these null models diagnose learning in neural networks, is not a coincidence to be explained away but a structure to be exploited.
