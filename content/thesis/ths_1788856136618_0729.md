# The Neural Tangent Kernel and the Infinite-Width Theory of Deep Learning: Lazy Training, Kernel Ridge Regression Equivalence, Spectral Bias, and the Edge of Chaos

## Abstract

This thesis develops a unified theoretical account of neural network training in the infinite-width limit through the **Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)** framework. We derive the NTK from first principles, showing that under gradient flow the network function evolves according to a linear differential equation governed by a kernel that converges to a deterministic, time-invariant limit as hidden-layer widths tend to infinity [1]. This linearization yields an exact equivalence between wide-network training and **kernel ridge regression**, with convergence rates dictated by the eigen-decomposition of the limiting kernel Gram matrix. Diagonalizing the NTK explains **spectral bias** — the empirical phenomenon that networks learn low-frequency components before high-frequency ones [4][5] — as a consequence of power-law eigenvalue decay. We contrast the **NTK parameterization** with the standard parameterization, delineate the **lazy-training** and **feature-learning** regimes via scaling arguments [2][3][7], extend the construction to convolutional and transformer architectures, connect initialization at the **edge of chaos** to the conditioning of the limiting kernel [6], and analyze **finite-width corrections** that drive kernel evolution in practical networks [9].

---

## 1. Introduction

The empirical success of deep neural networks stands in tension with their theoretical opacity: training is non-convex in parameter space, architectures are overparameterized, and classical statistical learning theory fails to explain generalization [1]. The infinite-width program resolves part of this mystery by identifying a regime in which training becomes *exactly tractable*.

Two limits are central. First, at initialization, an infinitely wide network is equivalent to a **Gaussian process** (the NNGP limit): each output is a centered Gaussian random field whose covariance is computed recursively through the architecture [1]. Second, *during training*, gradient descent on the parameters induces a dynamics on the network function governed by a new kernel — the **Neural Tangent Kernel** (NTK). The key theorem of Jacot, Gabriel, and Hongler states that the NTK converges in probability to a deterministic limit as widths grow, and crucially *remains constant throughout training* [1]. Training therefore becomes linear in function space: a kernel method with a frozen kernel.

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 establishes background: the Gaussian-process limit, gradient flow, and kernel ridge regression. Section 3 lays out the methodology: NTK parameterization, the NTK recursion, and spectral analysis techniques. Section 4 provides a deep dive into five facets: NTK derivation and function-space dynamics, the kernel ridge regression equivalence, spectral bias, parameterization and training regimes, and architectural extensions with finite-width corrections. Section 5 presents empirical evidence and proof sketches; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Gaussian Process Limit at Initialization

Consider a fully-connected network with $L$ layers. In the **NTK parameterization** [1], the $\ell$-th layer pre-activations are

$$
z^{(\ell)}(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\ell-1}}} W^{(\ell)} a^{(\ell-1)}(x) + \beta b^{(\ell)}, \qquad a^{(\ell)}(x) = \sigma(z^{(\ell)}(x)),
$$

with $W^{(\ell)}, b^{(\ell)}$ having i.i.d. standard normal entries. As $n_1, \dots, n_{L-1} \to \infty$ sequentially, the outputs converge to a centered Gaussian process with covariance $\Sigma^{(L+1)}$ defined by the recursion

$$
\Sigma^{(1)}(x,x') = \frac{1}{n_0} x^\top x' + \beta^2, \qquad \Sigma^{(\ell+1)}(x,x') = \mathbb{E}_{f \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma^{(\ell)})}[\sigma(f(x))\sigma(f(x'))] + \beta^2.
$$

This is the *NNGP* (neural network Gaussian process) kernel, the infinite-width Bayesian limit [1][8].

### 2.2 Gradient Flow

Continuous-time gradient descent — **gradient flow** — on parameters $\theta$ with learning rate $\eta$ obeys

$$
\frac{d\theta_t}{dt} = -\eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t).
$$

The chain rule lifts this to the function values $f_t(x) = f(x; \theta_t)$:

$$
\frac{df_t(x)}{dt} = \big\langle \nabla_\theta f_t(x), \tfrac{d\theta_t}{dt} \big\rangle = -\eta \sum_{\mu} \hat{\Theta}_t(x, x_\mu)\,\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial f(x_\mu)},
$$

where $\hat{\Theta}_t(x, x') = \langle \nabla_\theta f(x;\theta_t), \nabla_\theta f(x';\theta_t) \rangle$ is the **empirical NTK** [1]. Training in parameter space is thereby recast as training in function space, governed by the geometry encoded in $\hat{\Theta}_t$.

### 2.3 Kernel Ridge Regression

Kernel ridge regression with kernel $k$ and regularization $\lambda$ solves

$$
\min_{f \in \mathcal{H}_k} \sum_{i=1}^n (f(x_i) - y_i)^2 + \lambda \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_k}^2,
$$

with closed-form solution $f^\star(x) = k(x, X)(k(X,X) + \lambda I)^{-1} y$ by the representer theorem. The infinite-width network will be shown to reach *exactly* this solution with $k = \Theta_\infty$ [1].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology combines three analytical tools:

1. **Law of large numbers at infinite width.** Each hidden layer contributes a sum over $n_\ell$ i.i.d. terms; the $1/\sqrt{n}$ normalization in the NTK parameterization makes both forward covariances and tangent-kernel entries converge in probability to deterministic limits [1]. The convergence is sequential in the layers: take $n_1 \to \infty$, then $n_2 \to \infty$, and so on.

2. **The NTK recursion.** The limiting kernel $\Theta_\infty^{(\ell)}$ satisfies, with $\Theta_\infty^{(1)} = \Sigma^{(1)}$ [1]:

$$
\Theta_\infty^{(\ell+1)}(x,x') = \Theta_\infty^{(\ell)}(x,x')\,\dot{\Sigma}^{(\ell+1)}(x,x') + \Sigma^{(\ell+1)}(x,x'),
$$

where $\dot{\Sigma}^{(\ell+1)}(x,x') = \mathbb{E}_{f \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{(\ell)})}[\dot{\sigma}(f(x))\dot{\sigma}(f(x'))]$ captures gradient propagation through the nonlinearity.

3. **Spectral analysis.** Diagonalize the $n \times n$ Gram matrix $\Theta_\infty(X,X) = Q \Lambda Q^\top$. Residual dynamics decouple along eigenvectors: the component along eigenvector $q_k$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_k$ decays as $\exp(-\eta \lambda_k t)$ under squared loss [1]. Learning speed is therefore a direct readout of the eigenvalue spectrum.

We validate theory against numerics using the `neural_tangents` library, which implements exact translation rules from tensor operations to kernel operations [8].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Derivation of the NTK and Gradient Flow in Function Space

The derivation begins with the identity for gradient flow on parameters $\theta_t \in \mathbb{R}^p$ [1]:

$$
\frac{d}{dt} f_t(x) = -\eta \,\hat{\Theta}_t(x, X)\,\nabla_{f(X)}\mathcal{L}(f_t(X), y).
$$

> **Theorem (NTK convergence; Jacot et al. 2018 [1]).** *For a network of depth $L$ with Lipschitz nonlinearity, as $n_1, \dots, n_{L-1} \to \infty$ sequentially, the empirical NTK converges in probability at initialization to the deterministic kernel $\Theta_\infty^{(L)} \otimes I_{n_L}$.*

The deeper statement — the one that makes the theory powerful — is that $\hat{\Theta}_t$ *stays constant during training* in the limit [1]. Heuristically, each parameter moves by $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ while there are $O(n)$ parameters contributing to each kernel entry; the kernel's time derivative is $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ and vanishes. Rigorously, Jacot et al. show that the parameter trajectory remains in a ball around initialization whose radius shrinks with width, so the linearization

$$
f_t^{\text{lin}}(x) = f_0(x) + \big\langle \nabla_\theta f_0(x), \theta_t - \theta_0 \big\rangle
$$

remains uniformly close to the true $f_t$ for all $t$ [1]. Training is therefore *kernel gradient descent on a convex functional cost* — convex in function space even though the parameter-space cost is non-convex.

For least-squares loss $\mathcal{L} = \tfrac{1}{2}\|f(X) - y\|^2$, the dynamics reduce to the linear ODE

$$
\frac{d}{dt} f_t(X) = -\eta\,\Theta_\infty(X,X)\,(f_t(X) - y),
$$

with closed-form solution

$$
f_t(X) = \big(I - e^{-\eta \Theta_\infty(X,X) t}\big) y + e^{-\eta \Theta_\infty(X,X) t} f_0(X).
$$

Convergence requires only that $\Theta_\infty(X,X)$ be positive definite — proven for data on the sphere with non-polynomial activations [1].

The following Python sketch computes the empirical NTK for a small MLP and compares it against the analytic limit via `neural_tangents` [8]:

```python
import jax.numpy as jnp
import neural_tangents as nt
from neural_tangents import stax

# Finite-width MLP: empirical NTK via Jacobians
init_fn, apply_fn, _ = stax.serial(
    stax.Dense(512, parameterization='ntk'), stax.Relu(),
    stax.Dense(512, parameterization='ntk'), stax.Relu(),
    stax.Dense(1, parameterization='ntk'))

key = jax.random.PRNGKey(0)
_, params = init_fn(key, (-1, 20))
f = lambda p, x: apply_fn(p, x).ravel()
J = jax.jacfwd(f, argnums=0)          # parameter Jacobian
empirical_ntk = lambda x1, x2: sum(
    (a * b).sum() for a, b in zip(
        jax.tree_util.tree_leaves(J(params, x1)),
        jax.tree_util.tree_leaves(J(params, x2))))

# Infinite-width analytic NTK: same architecture, exact kernel fn
kernel_fn = nt.batch(nt.empirical_kernel_fn(apply_fn, ...))  # placeholder
```

The empirical kernel concentrates around the analytic one with fluctuations of order $1/\sqrt{n}$ [1][9].

### 4.2 Equivalence to Kernel Ridge Regression

As $t \to \infty$, the linearized dynamics converge to

$$
f_\infty(x) = \Theta_\infty(x, X)\,\Theta_\infty(X,X)^{-1} y + \big(f_0(x) - \Theta_\infty(x,X)\Theta_\infty(X,X)^{-1} f_0(X)\big).
$$

The first term is exactly the kernel ridge regression predictor with *zero regularization* ($\lambda \to 0^+$); the second is a zero-mean Gaussian residual from initialization that vanishes in relative terms as data grow [1]. This is the precise sense in which *an infinitely wide network trained by gradient descent is a kernel machine*.

The equivalence has a striking corollary for **generalization**: the infinite-width network inherits the generalization behavior of kernel regression with $\Theta_\infty$, including its sample-complexity scalings. Subsequent work proved non-vacuous generalization bounds for wide networks by passing through this equivalence [1]. Chizat et al. further clarified that this "lazy" regime is one instance of a broader phenomenon in differentiable programming: when the model's scale dominates its curvature at initialization, training barely moves parameters [2].

The kernel recursion can be expressed functionally. In Haskell-like pseudocode, the infinite-width kernel transform for a dense layer followed by a nonlinearity is:

```haskell
-- Kernel pair: (NNGP covariance Sigma, NTK Theta)
type KernelPair = (Kernel, Kernel)

denseNtk :: KernelPair -> KernelPair
denseNtk (sigma, theta) = (sigma', theta')
  where
    sigma' x x' = expectGP sigma (\f -> act (f x) * act (f x')) + beta^2
    theta'  x x' = theta x x' * expectGP sigma (\f -> act' (f x) * act' (f x'))
                 + sigma' x x'
```

Each tensor operation (dense, convolution, pooling) maps to such a kernel transformation — the core design principle of the `neural_tangents` library [8].

### 4.3 Eigen-Decomposition and Spectral Bias

Project the residual $r_t = f_t(X) - y$ onto the eigenbasis of $\Theta_\infty(X,X) = \sum_k \lambda_k q_k q_k^\top$:

$$
q_k^\top r_t = e^{-\eta \lambda_k t}\, q_k^\top r_0.
$$

Components aligned with large eigenvalues converge exponentially faster. For data on the sphere $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$, the NTK is a dot-product kernel, $\Theta_\infty(x,x') = \kappa(x^\top x')$, and its eigenfunctions are the **spherical harmonics**; eigenvalues decay as $\lambda_k \asymp k^{-d}$ for ReLU networks [1]. Low-degree (low-frequency) harmonics therefore dominate early training.

This is the kernel-theoretic explanation of **spectral bias**: Rahaman et al. showed empirically that deep networks fit low frequencies before high frequencies [4], and Xu et al. formalized the *Frequency Principle* — for targets like $y = \sin(x) + \sin(10x)$, networks fit $\sin(x)$ first and only later acquire the high-frequency component [5]. Basri et al. connected the two observations by computing NTK spectra explicitly: the convergence time of frequency component $k$ scales as $\lambda_k^{-1}$, growing polynomially in $k$ [4][5].

| Frequency band | NTK eigenvalue scaling (ReLU, $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$) | Relative convergence time |
|---|---|---|
| Low ($k \sim 1$) | $\lambda_k \asymp 1$ | $O(1)$ |
| Mid ($k \sim \sqrt{d}$) | $\lambda_k \asymp k^{-d}$ | $O(k^{d})$ |
| High ($k \gg d$) | exponentially suppressed | effectively unlearnable early |

*Table: spectral bias as eigenvalue decay. Learning high frequencies requires exponentially (in $1/\lambda_k$) more gradient steps [1][4][5].*

A corollary is a theoretical motivation for **early stopping**: stopping at time $t$ implements spectral regularization, fitting only eigen-components with $\lambda_k \gtrsim (\eta t)^{-1}$ — a low-pass filter on the target function [1].

### 4.4 NTK Parameterization vs. Standard Parameterization: Lazy and Feature Learning

The standard (LeCun/He) parameterization initializes $W^{(\ell)}_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1/n_{\ell-1})$ with no $1/\sqrt{n}$ prefactor in the forward pass. In the infinite-width limit with fixed learning rate, this scaling sends the network into the **mean-field** regime rather than the NTK regime [7]. Yang's *Tensor Programs* framework classifies parameterizations by exponents $(a, b, c)$ controlling weight scaling, initialization variance, and learning-rate scaling with width [7]:

| Parameterization | Forward scaling | Init. variance | Limit | Kernel frozen? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NTK [1] | $n^{-1/2} W x$ | $\Theta(1)$ | Kernel (lazy) | Yes |
| Standard / mean-field [7] | $W x$, $\mathrm{Var} = n^{-1}$ | $\Theta(n^{-1})$ | Mean-field (rich) | No — features learned |
| $\mu$P (maximal update) [7] | tuned per layer | tuned | Feature learning at any width | No |

Geiger et al. probed the crossover empirically by scaling the last layer as $\alpha h^{-1/2}$: for $\alpha \sqrt{h} \gg 1$ the network is **lazy** (NTK frozen, features fixed at initialization); for $\alpha \sqrt{h} \ll 1$ it enters **feature learning**, where the tangent kernel itself evolves and hidden representations adapt to data [3]. The transition occurs at $\alpha^\star \asymp h^{-1/2}$. In the feature regime, dynamics are linear only for $t \ll t_1 \sim \alpha\sqrt{h}$; fitting the data then requires a nonlinear phase that reshapes the kernel [3].

> **Theorem (informal; Chizat et al. 2019 [2]).** *For a model $h(w)$ with scale factor $\alpha$, the "laziness" $\kappa_{\alpha h}(w_0) \propto \alpha^{-1}$: large output scale at initialization implies parameters move negligibly relative to the function's movement — lazy training.*

Whether laziness helps or hurts depends on architecture and data: Geiger et al. found fully-connected networks often generalize better lazy, while CNNs on CIFAR-10 benefit from feature learning [3]. The NTK limit is thus not universally optimal — it is one endpoint of a continuum [7].

A compact Rust sketch of the eigenvalue-decay computation underlying spectral bias:

```rust
// Power-law fit of NTK Gram eigenvalues: lambda_k ~ k^{-p}
fn spectral_decay(eigs: &[f64]) -> f64 {
    let n = eigs.len() as f64;
    let (sx, sy, sxx, sxy) = eigs.iter().enumerate()
        .map(|(k, &l)| ((k as f64 + 1.0).ln(), l.ln()))
        .fold((0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0), |(sx, sy, sxx, sxy), (x, y)| {
            (sx + x, sy + y, sxx + x * x, sxy + x * y)
        });
    // slope of log-log regression = -p
    (n * sxy - sx * sy) / (n * sxx - sx * sx)
}
```

### 4.5 Convolutional and Transformer NTKs, the Edge of Chaos, and Finite-Width Corrections

**Convolutional NTKs.** Arora et al. and the `neural_tangents` framework extended the NTK to CNNs: translation equivariance makes the kernel depend on patch covariances, and pooling/global-average layers map to corresponding kernel contractions [8]. Empirically, infinite-width CNTKs with the right architecture reach competitive CIFAR-10 accuracy *without any feature learning*, confirming that much of CNN performance is already present in the frozen kernel [8].

**Transformer NTKs.** Hron et al. derived the infinite-width limit of attention: with the $1/\sqrt{d}$ scaling inside softmax attention, queries, keys, and values become Gaussian processes and the NTK gains terms propagating through the attention weights. The shaped-transformer line of work further studies joint infinite-depth-and-width limits where feature learning survives [5 in search: 2306.17759]. Attention's NTK is notably *non-stationary* and data-dependent in ways the MLP kernel is not, reflecting that attention weights are themselves functions of the input [7].

**Edge of chaos.** The limiting kernel is only well-conditioned if signals propagate at initialization. Schoenholz et al.'s mean-field theory shows that random networks exhibit ordered and chaotic phases separated by a critical line — the **edge of chaos** — where the correlation depth scale $\xi_c$ diverges [6]. In the ordered phase, $\Theta_\infty(x,x') \to \mathrm{const}$ for all pairs (representations collapse; gradients vanish); in the chaotic phase, $\Theta_\infty(x,x') \to 0$ for $x \neq x'$ (representations shatter; gradients explode) [6]. Seleznova et al. showed that at the edge of chaos, finite-width NTK fluctuations are minimized and the kernel concentrates fastest — criticality is the regime where the infinite-width idealization is most faithful [9].

**Finite-width corrections.** Real networks have finite width, so $\hat{\Theta}_t$ evolves. The leading correction scales as $O(1/n)$: kernel entries fluctuate at initialization with variance $\propto 1/n$, and training-induced change satisfies $\|\hat{\Theta}_t - \hat{\Theta}_0\|_F = O(t/(\eta n))$ in the lazy phase [9]. Depth amplifies fluctuations multiplicatively — the *depth-to-width ratio* $L/n$ controls the deviation from the frozen-kernel ideal, which is why very deep networks at fixed width escape the NTK regime even at large $n$ [9]. These corrections are not mere nuisances: kernel evolution *is* feature learning, and quantifying it is the frontier connecting NTK theory to practical deep learning [3][7][9].

## 5. Empirical Evidence and Proofs

**Proof sketch — constancy of the NTK [1].** Fix depth $L$ and let $n = \min_\ell n_\ell$. Under gradient flow with $\eta = O(1)$: (i) at initialization, $\|\hat{\Theta}_0 - \Theta_\infty\| = O(n^{-1/2})$ by the CLT applied layer-wise; (ii) the parameter displacement satisfies $\|\theta_t - \theta_0\|_2 = O(n^{-1/2})$ uniformly in $t$, because each gradient step moves parameters by $O(\eta/\sqrt{n})$ and the loss decreases monotonically; (iii) the NTK is locally Lipschitz in $\theta$ with constant $O(1)$, so $\|\hat{\Theta}_t - \hat{\Theta}_0\| = O(n^{-1/2})$ for all $t$. Letting $n \to \infty$ yields a frozen, deterministic kernel. Positive-definiteness on the sphere follows from expanding $\kappa$ in spherical harmonics with strictly positive coefficients for non-polynomial $\sigma$ [1].

**Numerical confirmation.** Jacot et al. trained wide MLPs and tracked $\|\hat{\Theta}_t - \Theta_\infty\|_F$: it shrinks as $n^{-1/2}$ and stays flat in $t$ [1]. Lee et al. verified that linearized dynamics predict true training trajectories for widths $\geq 512$ on MNIST/CIFAR-10 subsets. Geiger et al.'s $\alpha$-scaling experiments directly exhibit the lazy/feature-learning phase transition, with kernel change $\|\Delta\Theta\|_F$ jumping by orders of magnitude across $\alpha^\star$ [3]. Rahaman et al. and Xu et al. confirmed frequency-ordered learning on synthetic targets [4][5], and spectral-bias measurements on ResNet-18/CIFAR-10 show the same low-to-high frequency progression in realistic settings.

**Edge-of-chaos numerics.** Schoenholz et al. measured correlation depth scales $\xi_c$ across the $(\sigma_w, \sigma_b)$ plane: $\xi_c$ diverges precisely on the critical line $\chi_1 = 1$, and only networks within a few $\xi_c^{-1}$ of criticality train at large depth [6]. Seleznova et al. computed NTK variance as a function of initialization: dispersion $E[\Theta^2]/E[\Theta]^2$ is minimized at criticality and grows in both ordered and chaotic phases [9].

## 6. Limitations

The NTK theory, for all its elegance, has sharp boundaries:

- **No feature learning by construction.** The frozen kernel cannot explain representation learning — arguably the core advantage of deep over shallow methods [3][7]. Tasks requiring hierarchical feature discovery (e.g., grokking, where networks transition from lazy memorization to rich generalization) lie outside the theory.
- **Width requirements are impractical.** Bounds require $n = \mathrm{poly}(n_{\text{data}}, L, \lambda_{\min}^{-1})$; realistic networks operate far below the asymptotic regime, where the kernel evolves substantially [9].
- **Depth pathology.** At fixed width, increasing depth drives the network away from the NTK limit; the relevant parameter is $L/n$, and deep ResNets live in a regime the basic theory does not cover [9]. Skip connections and normalization change the kernel recursion qualitatively.
- **Optimization idealizations.** The cleanest results assume gradient flow with infinitesimal learning rate. Finite learning rates, SGD noise, momentum, and adaptive optimizers (Adam) all break exact kernel equivalence; the edge-of-stability phenomenon shows real training operating outside the stable-flow regime.
- **Spectral bias is a double-edged sword.** The same eigenvalue decay that explains generalization also implies exponentially slow learning of high-frequency structure — a fundamental limitation for tasks like fitting fine textures or solving high-frequency PDEs without architectural remedies (e.g., Fourier features).
- **Transformers only partially covered.** Attention's data-dependent weights and layer normalization complicate the infinite-width analysis; the proportional depth-and-width limits needed for faithful transformer theory remain active research [7].

---

## 7. Conclusion

The Neural Tangent Kernel distills deep learning into a single mathematical object: in the infinite-width limit, gradient-based training is kernel gradient descent with a frozen, deterministic kernel, exactly equivalent to kernel ridge regression [1]. Its eigen-decomposition explains *when* things are learned — spectral bias as eigenvalue decay [4][5] — and its parameterization dependence delineates lazy training from feature learning [2][3][7]. The edge of chaos determines whether the limiting kernel is well-conditioned enough to train at all [6], and finite-width corrections quantify the departure from the ideal [9].

The theory's greatest value may be as a *baseline*: it tells us precisely what infinite width buys, so that the residual — feature learning, the thing finite networks actually do — can be studied as a deviation from a solved model. As Yang's Tensor Programs program shows, the NTK limit is one point in a landscape of scaling limits; mapping the rest is the central task of deep learning theory today [7].
