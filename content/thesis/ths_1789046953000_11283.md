# Consensus under Asynchrony: FLP Impossibility, Paxos, Raft, and Mechanized Verification in TLA+

## Abstract

Distributed consensus asks unreliable processes to agree on a single value despite crashes, delays, and message loss. This thesis examines the problem from four complementary angles: the Fischer–Lynch–Paterson impossibility result, which proves that no deterministic protocol can guarantee consensus in a fully asynchronous system with even one crash fault [1]; Lamport's Paxos, which achieves safety under asynchrony while confining the FLP-imposed risk to liveness [2]; Ongaro and Ousterhout's Raft, which re-derives equivalent safety through a stronger-leader decomposition designed for understandability and which ships with a machine-checked TLA+ specification [3]; and mechanized verification in TLA+, where model checking and proof assistants expose exactly which invariants carry the safety argument and where informal reasoning drops assumptions [7][8]. The central claim is that consensus under asynchrony is a layered design discipline: impossibility defines the boundary, quorum intersection provides the mechanism, leader-based decomposition provides the engineering structure, and formal specification provides the audit trail.

## 1. Introduction

The consensus problem asks *n* processes, each starting with an initial value, to decide irrevocably on a single common value:

- **Agreement (safety):** no two correct processes decide different values.
- **Validity:** a decided value must be some process's initial input.
- **Termination (liveness):** every correct process eventually decides.

These properties look innocent. In synchronous systems, where message delays and relative process speeds are bounded, consensus is solvable even under Byzantine faults. In asynchronous systems, where no such bounds exist, Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson showed in 1985 that deterministic termination cannot be guaranteed once even a single process may crash [1]. The result is frequently misread as "consensus is impossible".

Consensus is the load-bearing primitive of fault-tolerant computing: state machine replication — running the same deterministic state machine on *n* replicas and agreeing on command order — reduces to consensus on a sequence of values, and underlies replicated databases and coordination services [3].

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 fixes the system model and vocabulary. Section 3 describes the methodology: a comparative analysis of the canonical impossibility proof, the two dominant protocol families, and their formal specifications. Section 4 develops each strand in depth. Section 5 surveys the formal and empirical evidence for correctness. Section 6 states the limitations honestly. Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 System model

We work in the standard asynchronous message-passing model. Processes communicate by sending messages; there is no bound on delivery latency, no bound on relative process speeds, and no shared clock. The fault model is *crash failure* (fail-stop): a faulty process halts permanently and sends no further messages. Byzantine behavior is excluded from the core analysis, though we note where protocols extend to it.

A *configuration* is the complete state of the system: each process's local state plus the set of in-flight messages. A *step* takes a configuration to a successor by delivering one message to one process. An *execution* is an infinite sequence of steps; an *admissible* execution delivers every message sent to a non-faulty process eventually and gives every non-faulty process infinitely many steps.

### 2.2 Safety versus liveness

Safety properties say that nothing bad ever happens; liveness properties say that something good eventually happens. The FLP result [1] is a theorem about liveness: it shows that *some* admissible execution of *any* deterministic protocol never decides. It says nothing against safety, and indeed Paxos-class protocols maintain agreement and validity in *every* execution, including fully adversarial ones [2].

### 2.3 Quorum systems

A quorum system over a set of *n* processes is a family of subsets such that any two quorums intersect. Majority quorums, `|Q| > n/2`, are the canonical choice: any two majorities share at least one process. Quorum intersection is the mechanical heart of both Paxos and Raft.

### 2.4 Related evasions of FLP

Since the impossibility proof appeared, four standard evasions have been developed: **randomization** (coin flips recover termination with probability 1 [4]), **timing assumptions** (partial synchrony, in which bounds exist but are unknown), **failure detectors** (oracles that suspect crashed processes), and **stronger synchronization** (shared memory, atomic broadcast). Paxos and Raft take the partial-synchrony route: safe in asynchrony, live when the system stabilizes.

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is a *comparative formal analysis*, not an experimental systems paper. The method has three components:

1. **Proof reconstruction.** We restate the FLP bivalency argument and the Paxos safety argument in a uniform notation, isolating which assumptions each lemma consumes. This exposes the sense in which Paxos is "FLP-optimal": it concedes precisely the property FLP rules out and nothing more.
2. **Protocol comparison.** We compare single-decree Paxos, Multi-Paxos, and Raft along fixed axes — roles, quorum discipline, leader election, log structure, and failure handling — drawing on the primary sources [2][3] and on Howard and Mortier's mapping between Paxos and Raft in shared terminology [5].
3. **Specification audit.** We examine what TLA+ specifications of these protocols actually prove: which invariants are stated, which are machine-checked, and how refinement mappings connect abstract voting to concrete message passing [6][7][8].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The FLP impossibility theorem and the bivalency argument

Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson [1] prove that no deterministic protocol solves consensus in the asynchronous crash-fault model. The proof introduces *valency*. A configuration *C* is *0-valent* if every admissible execution from *C* decides 0 (and 1-valent symmetrically); it is *bivalent* if both decisions are reachable. The argument has two lemmas.

> **Theorem (FLP, 1985).** No deterministic algorithm can guarantee consensus among `n ≥ 2` asynchronous processes tolerating a single crash fault [1].

**Lemma 1 (existence of a bivalent initial configuration).** With at least one all-0 and one all-1 initial configuration, an adjacency argument on the chain of initial configurations differing in one process's input shows some initial configuration is bivalent: two adjacent configurations cannot both be univalent with different valences, since a protocol cannot distinguish them if the differing process crashes immediately.

**Lemma 2 (bivalence is preserved).** From any bivalent configuration *C*, there exists an admissible execution reaching another bivalent configuration *C′* via a step that *avoids* deciding. The core is a commutation argument: if some step *e = (p, m)* applied to *C* yields a 0-valent configuration while another ordering yields 1-valence, delaying the delivery of *m* — legitimate, since the system is asynchronous — constructs a successor from which both valences remain reachable, because steps of different processes on disjoint message sets commute.

Chaining Lemma 2 indefinitely produces an infinite non-deciding admissible execution. Note the precise mechanics: the adversary never *breaks* the protocol; it merely schedules messages. Because a slow process is indistinguishable from a crashed one, no process can safely wait for the missing message, yet proceeding risks contradicting a decision already forced elsewhere.

The philosophical content is worth stating plainly. FLP does not say distributed systems cannot agree; it says they cannot agree *on a deadline* when the network is adversarial.

### 4.2 Paxos: single-decree consensus and quorum intersection

Lamport's Paxos, first circulated in 1990 and published in 1998, with the readable "Paxos Made Simple" version in 2001 [2], solves single-decree consensus: a set of *acceptors* agree on at most one value. The roles are:

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| **Proposer** | Issues proposals with strictly increasing ballot numbers |
| **Acceptor** | Votes at most once per ballot; remembers highest ballot seen |
| **Learner** | Observes quorums of acceptances and learns the chosen value |

A ballot has two phases:

1. **Phase 1a (Prepare):** proposer sends `prepare(n)` to a majority of acceptors, where `n` exceeds every ballot it has used.
2. **Phase 1b (Promise):** an acceptor receiving `prepare(n)` with `n` greater than any ballot it has promised responds with a promise never to accept ballots below `n`, attaching the highest-numbered ballot it has already accepted and its value.
3. **Phase 2a (Accept):** if the proposer hears promises from a majority, it sends `accept(n, v)` where `v` is the value of the highest accepted ballot reported, or any value if none was reported.
4. **Phase 2b (Accepted):** an acceptor receiving `accept(n, v)` accepts it unless it has since promised a higher ballot.

Safety rests on two invariants, P1 and P2, of which the nontrivial one is:

> **Theorem (Paxos safety).** If a proposal with value *v* is chosen (accepted by a majority), then every proposal issued with a higher ballot number also has value *v* [2].

*Proof sketch.* Suppose proposal `(n, v)` is chosen by majority `Q₁`. Consider any higher ballot `m > n` with proposer collecting promises from majority `Q₂`. By quorum intersection, `Q₁ ∩ Q₂` is nonempty; pick acceptor *a* in it. Acceptor *a* accepted `(n, v)` and therefore reports a ballot numbered at least `n` when promising for `m` (promises are monotonic in the ballot number). The proposer of `m` adopts the value of the highest reported accepted ballot. An induction over ballot numbers shows that the highest reported value at `m` must be *v`: any ballot between `n` and `m` that completed Phase 2a was forced to carry `v` by the same argument. Hence `(m, v)`. ∎

This is the entire safety argument, and it is worth admiring its economy: agreement follows from nothing but quorum intersection plus monotonic ballots. Liveness is where FLP bites. Two proposers can duel forever, each preempting the other's ballot — the classic *livelock*. Paxos therefore distinguishes a *distinguished proposer* (leader) whose proposals are the only ones issued during stable periods; leader election is a liveness mechanism, not a safety mechanism.

The following TLA+ fragment sketches the Phase 2b action and the agreement invariant in the style of Lamport's specifications, where `msgs` is the set of sent messages and `accVoted` records each acceptor's vote:

```tla
---- MODULE SingleDecreePaxos ----
EXTENDS Integers, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Acceptors, Quorums, Ballots, Values
VARIABLES accPromised, accVoted, msgs

Phase2b(a, b, v) ==
  /\ \E m \in msgs : m.type = "2a" /\ m.bal = b /\ m.val = v
  /\ accPromised[a] <= b
  /\ accVoted' = [accVoted EXCEPT ![a] = <<b, v>>]
  /\ msgs' = msgs \cup {[type |-> "2b", acc |-> a, bal |-> b, val |-> v]}

Agreement ==
  \A m1, m2 \in msgs :
    (m1.type = "2b" /\ m2.type = "2b"
      /\ QuorumOf(m1) /\ QuorumOf(m2)) => m1.val = m2.val
====
```

`Agreement` is the property TLC checks on bounded instances; the inductive invariant behind it is exactly the ballot-monotonicity argument above, formalized as `SafeAt(b, v)` predicates in the machine-checked developments [7].

### 4.3 Multi-Paxos: from single decree to replicated log

Running one Paxos instance per log slot is correct but wasteful: each slot would pay the full two phases. *Multi-Paxos* observes that Phase 1 can be executed once for all slots. A stable leader runs Phase 1 with a single ballot number, learns which slots already hold accepted values, and then fills slots with Phase 2 alone — one round trip per command in the steady state.

Two refinements matter. First, *leader stickiness*: re-running Phase 1 is only needed on leader change, so the common case is cheap. Second, *value inheritance*: when a new leader takes over, Phase 1b responses may reveal values already chosen in some slots; the new leader must re-propose exactly those values (the P2c rule), preserving safety across leadership changes.

Multi-Paxos is also where the operational difficulties live: log compaction via snapshots and reconfiguration (removing failed acceptors, adding new ones). Historically the gap between "Multi-Paxos the algorithm" and "Multi-Paxos the system" is where bugs nested. This gap motivated Raft.

### 4.4 Raft: decomposing consensus for understandability

Ongaro and Ousterhout's Raft [3] produces results equivalent to Multi-Paxos but restructures the algorithm around three separable subproblems — *leader election*, *log replication*, and *safety* — plus a membership-change protocol. The deliberate design goal was understandability, validated by a user study in which students answered questions about Raft significantly better than about Paxos [3].

**Leader election.** Servers are *followers*, *candidates*, or *leaders*. Followers time out (randomized election timeouts, e.g. 150–300 ms, comfortably above the ~10 ms broadcast time) and become candidates, incrementing their term and requesting votes. A candidate receiving a majority becomes leader for the term. The randomization is the anti-livelock device: it makes simultaneous candidacies unlikely, so the FLP-style duel terminates in practice. Terms play the role of Paxos ballot numbers.

**Log replication.** The leader accepts client commands, appends them to its log as `(term, index, command)` entries, and replicates via `AppendEntries` RPCs. An entry is *committed* once stored on a majority; the leader then applies it and notifies followers. The critical strengthening relative to Paxos is the **Leader Completeness Property**: any committed entry is present in the logs of all future leaders. This is enforced by the election restriction — a candidate's log must be at least as up-to-date as a majority's — which eliminates Paxos-style value inheritance on leader change.

The safety argument decomposes into checkable invariants:

> **Theorem (Raft safety).** *Election Safety:* at most one leader per term. *Log Matching:* if two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, all preceding entries are identical. *Leader Completeness:* committed entries appear in all future leaders' logs. *State Machine Safety:* applied entries are identical across servers [3].

A Python sketch of the follower timeout logic captures the randomized-election essence that the TLA+ specification abstracts as nondeterministic choice:

```python
import random, threading, time

class Follower:
    def __init__(self):
        self.term = 0
        self.reset_timer()

    def reset_timer(self):
        # Randomized election timeout: the FLP-evasion in miniature.
        # Deterministic timeouts let two candidates duel forever;
        # randomization breaks the symmetry with probability 1.
        self.deadline = time.monotonic() + random.uniform(0.150, 0.300)

    def on_heartbeat(self, leader_term):
        if leader_term >= self.term:
            self.term = leader_term
            self.reset_timer()   # leader is alive; stay a follower

    def tick(self):
        if time.monotonic() > self.deadline:
            self.become_candidate()  # no heartbeat: start an election
```

**Membership change.** Raft's joint-consensus reconfiguration moves from configuration `C_old` to `C_new` through an intermediate `C_old,new` in which majorities of *both* configurations are required. Because any two majorities of the union intersect appropriately, at most one leader can be elected during the transition, preserving Election Safety throughout [3]. This is quorum intersection applied to the meta-problem of changing the quorums themselves.

### 4.5 Mechanized verification in TLA+

TLA+ (Temporal Logic of Actions) specifies a system as an initial predicate and a next-state action; TLC, its explicit-state model checker, explores all reachable states of finite instances and checks invariants and temporal properties. The Raft paper itself shipped a ~400-line TLA+ specification of the core algorithm with a hand proof of the key safety lemma [3]; subsequent work pushed much further.

The verification landscape has three tiers of rigor:

1. **Model checking (TLC).** Finite instances (e.g., 3–5 servers, bounded terms and log lengths) are exhaustively explored. This catches design bugs — and historically *has* caught bugs in published protocols — but proves nothing about unbounded instances.
2. **Machine-checked proof (TLAPS / Coq).** The safety argument is carried out in a proof assistant. The Verdi project verified Raft's safety in Coq (~50,000 lines, 90 invariants, ~18 months of effort), while TLA+-native efforts proved reconfiguration safety with far smaller developments at a higher abstraction level [8].
3. **Refinement.** Lamport's Paxos specification refines an abstract *Voting* algorithm: the message-passing protocol is shown to implement abstract votes, with a refinement mapping (`votes ← msg2b`, `maxBal ← maxBal`) that makes the correspondence checkable [7]. This is the gold standard — the proof explains *why* the protocol works by exhibiting the abstract algorithm it implements.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

Because this is a formal-methods thesis, "empirical results" means *verification artifacts*: what has actually been checked, at what scale, and with what gaps.

| Protocol | Artifact | Method | Scale | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single-decree Paxos | Lamport TLA+ spec + Voting refinement | TLAPS / manual proof | Unbounded (proof) | Safety proved; liveness excluded per FLP [7] |
| Multi-Paxos (vRA) | DistAlgo → TLA+ translation | TLC + manual proof | 154–217 line specs | Per-slot safety proved; state-reduction variants checked [6] |
| Raft (core) | Ongaro TLA+ spec (~400 lines) | Hand proof of Log Completeness | Bounded (TLC) | Safety lemma proved; type safety not mechanized [3] |
| Raft (full) | Verdi framework | Coq | ~50k lines, 90 invariants | Static Raft safety verified end-to-end [8] |
| Raft reconfiguration | ReconfRaft TLA+ (3,189 lines) | TLAPS | Unbounded (proof) | First verified safety proof for Raft reconfiguration [8] |
| Raft (modular) | Compositional TLA+ | TLC, assume-guarantee | Module-wise | Election + replication checked separately, composed [9] |

Three observations follow. First, verification cost scales brutally with abstraction level: 400 lines of TLA+ versus 50,000 lines of Coq for the same algorithm reflects the difference between specifying the protocol and verifying an implementation. Second, refinement mappings are the highest-value artifact: once Paxos refines abstract Voting, the safety proof is inherited rather than repeated [7].

## 6. Limitations

Honesty requires stating what this analysis does *not* establish.

1. **Liveness is assumed, not proved.** Every protocol discussed is live only under partial synchrony or randomization. FLP [1] guarantees that adversarial schedules exist; production systems bet, correctly in practice, that they will not encounter them.
2. **Crash faults only.** The core results assume fail-stop behavior. Byzantine Paxos variants exist and have been formalized [7], but the quorum requirements (`3f+1`), cryptographic machinery, and proof complexity are substantially greater, and Raft does not address Byzantine faults at all.
3. **Model checking is bounded.** TLC results cover small instances; the step from "no counterexample at 5 servers and 3 terms" to "correct for all instances" is discharged only in the TLAPS/Coq developments, which are expensive and cover selected protocol fragments.
4. **The abstraction gap.** Verified specifications describe abstract protocols, not deployed code. The Verdi effort [8] closed this gap for Raft at enormous cost; most systems rely on the (usually sound) assumption that the implementation faithfully follows the specification. Bugs historically live exactly in this gap — in log compaction, snapshotting, and reconfiguration corner cases.
5. **Performance is out of scope.** This thesis says nothing about throughput, tail latency, or wide-area deployment. Protocols optimized for those axes (EPaxos, Mencius, Fast Paxos) trade quorum size and message delays against the structures analyzed here [6].

---

## 7. Conclusion

The story of consensus under asynchrony has a clean three-act structure. **Act one:** Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson prove that deterministic termination is impossible with one crash fault, and the bivalency argument locates the impossibility exactly in the scheduler's power to delay the decisive message [1].

The practical upshot is a design discipline: *never* sacrifice safety for liveness (FLP tells you liveness was never guaranteed anyway); *always* isolate the mechanism that carries agreement (quorum intersection); *decompose* so each subproblem can be understood and checked in isolation (Raft's lesson); and *specify* the protocol in a checkable language before trusting the implementation. Consensus is not finished — reconfiguration, Byzantine faults.
