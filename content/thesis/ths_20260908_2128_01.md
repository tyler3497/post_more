# Conflict-Driven Clause Learning: From Implication Graphs to Certified Unsatisfiability in Modern SAT Solvers

## Abstract

Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL) is the dominant architecture for complete Boolean satisfiability (SAT) solving, routinely deciding industrial formulas with millions of variables despite the problem's NP-completeness. This article gives a systematic, proof-theoretic and engineering account of the CDCL loop and the heuristics that made it dominant: implication-graph-based conflict analysis with the first unique implication point (1UIP) learning scheme; VSIDS and its exponential successor EVSIDS for conflict-guided branching; literal block distance (LBD) as a quality measure for learned-clause management and database reduction; restart policies — Luby's universal sequence, geometric schedules, and Glucose-style dynamic restarts keyed to LBD statistics; inprocessing techniques such as clause vivification and probing; and the DRAT/LRAT proof-logging pipeline with mechanically verified checkers. We survey the proof-complexity characterization of CDCL as polynomially equivalent to general resolution, its empirical dominance in the SAT competitions, and its integration into DPLL(T) satisfiability modulo theories solvers. We conclude with hardness limits, open problems, and a roadmap for the next decade of solver design.

## 1. Introduction

The Boolean satisfiability problem — deciding whether a CNF formula admits a satisfying assignment — is the canonical NP-complete problem, yet also the most industrially successful one. Modern CDCL solvers decide formulas with millions of variables from hardware model checking, software verification, AI planning, and cryptanalysis. The gap between worst-case complexity and practical performance is the product of three decades of advances in proof theory, algorithm engineering, and heuristic design.

The lineage is well documented: DPLL supplied the backtracking skeleton; GRASP introduced conflict analysis and non-chronological backtracking, mining failed branches for *learned clauses* [1]; Chaff added VSIDS branching and two-watched-literal propagation, making the inner loop essentially free [2]; MiniSat distilled the architecture into the extensible core behind a generation of solvers and the modern SMT stack [3]; and Glucose added learned-clause *quality* measurement via literal block distance (LBD) [4].

Section 2 fixes notation and reviews the DPLL-to-CDCL transition. Section 3 presents the CDCL loop as executable methodology plus the community's evaluation protocol. Section 4 dives into five load-bearing mechanisms: 1UIP conflict analysis, VSIDS/EVSIDS branching, LBD-based clause management, restart policies, and inprocessing with proof certification. Section 5 covers empirical results, the resolution-equivalence theorems, and the DRAT/LRAT pipeline; Section 6 discusses limitations and open problems.

## 2. Background

A CNF formula *F* is a conjunction of *clauses*, each clause a disjunction of *literals* (a variable *x* or its negation ¬*x*). A *partial assignment* maps a subset of variables to truth values; *unit propagation* (Boolean constraint propagation, BCP) repeatedly assigns the forced literal of any clause whose other literals are falsified.

DPLL adds *decisions* (choose a variable and polarity) and *chronological backtracking* on conflict. Its weakness is amnesia: the same reason for failure is rediscovered exponentially often, and its proof system is only *tree-like* resolution — exponentially weaker than general resolution.

GRASP changed the contract [1]. On conflict, the solver *analyzes* rather than merely backtracking: the propagation chain is recorded in an *implication graph*, and a new clause — a logical consequence of *F* — is derived by resolution along it. Backtracking jumps non-chronologically to the *assertion level*, the deepest level where the learned clause is unit. Each learned clause excludes the current conflict *and* a family of symmetric conflicts, so CDCL search is resolution proof search, not tree-like backtracking.

| Feature | DPLL | CDCL |
|---|---|---|
| Backtracking | Chronological (one level) | Non-chronological (assertion level) |
| Conflict response | None stored | Learns an asserting clause via resolution |
| Branching | Static or look-ahead heuristics | Conflict-guided: VSIDS / EVSIDS |
| Propagation | Counter-based | Two watched literals |
| Restarts | Absent | Standard; learned clauses are retained |
| Proof system | Tree-like resolution | General resolution (with restarts) [9] |

Chaff's engineering is still used by every modern solver [2]. The *two-watched-literal* scheme attaches two watched literals per clause; a clause can only become unit or conflicting when a watched literal is falsified, so other assignments cost nothing — no per-clause visit, no undo work. *VSIDS* (Variable State Independent Decaying Sum) maintains per-variable *activity*, bumped on conflict-analysis participation and periodically decayed, focusing branching on variables from *recent* conflicts. Together these turned clause learning from a good idea into a fast one.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The CDCL core loop

The entire architecture can be stated as one procedure. Let `decide()` pick an unassigned variable, `propagate()` run unit propagation to fixpoint, `analyze(conflict)` derive a learned clause and assertion level, and `backjump(level)` undo assignments. In Python-like pseudocode:

```python
def cdcl(F):
    trail, level = [], 0
    while True:
        conflict = propagate(F, trail)          # unit propagation to fixpoint
        if conflict is not None:
            if level == 0:
                return "UNSAT"                  # empty clause derivable
            clause, blevel = analyze(conflict)  # 1UIP resolution + minimization
            F.append(clause)                    # learn: kept across restarts
            backjump(blevel)                    # non-chronological backtrack
            if should_restart():
                restart()                       # keep clauses, drop trail
        elif all_assigned(trail):
            return "SAT", trail                 # model found
        else:
            if should_reduce():
                reduce_db()                     # LBD-guided clause deletion
            decide(trail, level)                # VSIDS/EVSIDS branching
```

Three properties make the loop complete and effective: (i) `analyze` returns an *asserting* clause, forcing progress after every backjump; (ii) learned clauses survive restarts, so work accumulates monotonically; (iii) `reduce_db` bounds memory while preserving the most inferentially useful clauses [4][10].

### 3.2 The watched-literal data structure

Unit propagation dominates runtime — often above 80% — so its data structure is the solver's most consequential choice. In Rust-like pseudocode, each clause watches two literals; only falsification of a watched literal triggers a scan:

```rust
fn propagate(&mut self, lit: Lit) -> Option<ClauseId> {
    // inspect only clauses watching `lit`
    for cid in take(&mut self.watches[lit]) {
        let c = &mut self.clauses[cid];
        // try to find a non-falsified replacement watch
        if let Some(new_watch) = c.find_replacement(lit, &self.assign) {
            self.watches[new_watch].push(cid);
        } else if c.other_watch(lit).is_unassigned(&self.assign) {
            self.assign.set(c.other_watch(lit), Reason::Propagated(cid));
            self.trail.push(c.other_watch(lit));
        } else {
            return Some(cid); // conflict: all literals falsified
        }
    }
    None
}
```

Backtracking touches no clause metadata: unwatched assignments vanish. Hence millions of propagations per second, and restart-heavy strategies that would be ruinous under counter-based schemes are cheap.

### 3.3 Evaluation protocol

Progress in SAT is measured, not asserted. The community's methodology, centered on the annual SAT competitions, is worth stating precisely:

1. **Benchmark families.** *Industrial* (bounded model checking, verification), *crafted* (combinatorial designs, pigeonhole variants), and *random* (uniform 3-SAT near the phase transition). Solvers are ranked per family; no single heuristic dominates all three.
2. **Scoring.** Penalized average runtime (PAR-2): timeouts count as twice the limit. Rankings use solved-instance counts first, PAR-2 as tiebreak.
3. **Cactus plots.** Instances sorted by solving time per solver; area under the curve shows coverage and speed. Glucose's 2009 debut — 176 of 234 industrial instances against MiniSat's 132 — was read off such plots [4].
4. **Proof checking.** UNSAT answers on competition instances must be accompanied by machine-checkable proofs (§4.5, §5), so the methodology is *certified*, not merely empirical.

| Family | Typical origin | What it stresses |
|---|---|---|
| Industrial | BMC, EDA, verification | Propagation throughput, clause quality |
| Crafted | Pigeonhole, parity, graph problems | Proof complexity, restart behavior |
| Random | Uniform *k*-SAT at phase transition | Branching heuristics, look-ahead |

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The 1UIP Scheme and Implication-Graph Conflict Analysis

Conflict analysis is resolution made operational. During propagation the solver records, for every implied literal *l*, its *antecedent* clause — the clause that forced *l* — and its *decision level* δ(*l*). A conflict yields an *implication graph*: decision literals as roots, implied literals as nodes, edges from antecedent literals to implied ones, and a special conflict node κ fed by the two falsified literals of the conflicting clause.

Learning proceeds by resolving the conflicting clause against antecedents, walking the trail backwards from κ. The standard stopping criterion is the **first unique implication point (1UIP)**: the single literal at the current decision level that dominates κ — every path from the level's decision literal to κ passes through it. The clause obtained at that moment is *asserting*: after backjumping to the second-highest decision level among its literals, it is unit and forces the negation of the 1UIP literal. In Haskell-like pseudocode, the resolution walk is a fold over the trail:

```haskell
-- analyze: resolve conflicting clause with antecedents back to the 1UIP
analyze :: Trail -> Clause -> (Clause, Level)
analyze trail conflict = go conflict (reverse trail)
  where
    go learned [] = (learned, 0)
    go learned (l : rest)
      | isUIP learned l = (minimize learned, assertionLevel learned)
      | otherwise       = go (resolve learned (antecedent l) l) rest
    -- resolve: (C ∨ l) ⊗ (D ∨ ¬l) = C ∨ D, then drop current-level literals
```

Two refinements matter enormously. *Conflict-clause minimization* [3] deletes literals by self-subsuming resolution — if the remaining literals imply ¬*l*, *l* is redundant — shortening learned clauses by tens of percent. Second, the clause's *glue* structure (distinct decision levels spanned) predicts future usefulness far better than length [4].

> **Theorem 1 (Learning as resolution).** Each conflict-analysis step derives its learned clause by a sequence of resolution inferences from the input formula and previously learned clauses. The sequence of learned clauses produced over a full CDCL run therefore constitutes a resolution refutation when the formula is unsatisfiable [9].

### 4.2 VSIDS, EVSIDS, and Adaptive Branching Heuristics

Branching is where search meets learning. Chaff's VSIDS [2] scores each *variable* by activity: variables in conflict analysis are *bumped*, and all activities are periodically multiplied by a decay factor, so recent conflicts dominate. The unassigned variable of maximal activity is chosen next; the effect is self-reinforcing focus on the "hot" region until it is exhausted.

Modern solvers use **EVSIDS** (exponential VSIDS): the *bump* grows geometrically while activities stay fixed, rescaled only near floating-point overflow. This avoids the periodic O(*n*) decay pass and adapts the effective decay rate to the conflict rate. EVSIDS-family solvers have won nearly every SAT competition of the last decade.

Complementary mechanisms stabilize branching:

- **Phase saving.** Each variable reuses its last assigned polarity on re-decision, making restarts nearly free in lost search direction; now universal [3].
- **Rephasing.** Periodically resetting saved phases (best-known, all-false, or random) diversifies search and counteracts phase lock-in.
- **Randomized tie-breaking.** A small fraction of decisions (often under 2%) are random, guaranteeing that no region of the search space is permanently starved.

### 4.3 LBD-Based Learned Clause Management and Database Reduction

A CDCL solver learns one clause per conflict — millions on hard instances. Keeping all of them stalls propagation, yet deleting the wrong ones destroys the learning benefit: the solver's need to *forget* [10]. MiniSat deleted half the learned clauses by activity past a growing bound [3]; Glucose replaced activity with a structural measure [4]:

> **Definition (Literal Block Distance).** For a learned clause *C*, LBD(*C*) is the number of distinct decision levels among the literals of *C*. A clause with LBD ≤ 2 is called a *glue clause*.

The intuition: a low-LBD clause connects a few blocks of the search space with explicit resolution links, so it will propagate *across* restarts and *between* decision subtrees. Audemard and Simon showed that LBD predicts clause usefulness better than length or activity, that glue clauses are disproportionately responsible for future propagations, and that *aggressive* deletion — keeping the database small — beats conservative retention once LBD guides the choice [4]. Modern solvers tier the database:

| Tier | LBD range | Policy |
|---|---|---|
| Glue / permanent | ≤ 2 | Never deleted |
| Core / Tier-2 | 3–6 | Protected; demoted on disuse |
| Local | > 6 | Deleted first; half removed at each reduction |

Reduction runs on a growing conflict budget (Glucose starts near 20,000, adding a few hundred per round), and clauses used recently in propagation earn LBD *updates* that rescue undervalued clauses. The lesson of [10] stands: forgetting is a first-class heuristic, as load-bearing as branching.

### 4.4 Restart Policies: Luby, Geometric, and Glucose-Style Dynamic Restarts

Because learned clauses are retained, restarting — discarding the trail and beginning at level zero — re-enters search with an enriched formula rather than wasting work. Restarts combat heavy-tailed runtimes, and Huang showed the restart *policy* substantially changes efficiency on industrial benchmarks [5].

Three families dominate:

1. **Luby sequence.** A universal schedule optimal up to constants for Las Vegas algorithms: intervals follow 1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 4, … scaled by a base unit [6]. MiniSat's default; still the theoretically grounded choice.
2. **Geometric schedules.** Intervals grow by a constant factor (e.g. 100, 150, 225, … with factor 1.5). Simple, predictable, and effective on satisfiable instances where deep dives pay off.
3. **Glucose-style dynamic restarts.** The solver compares a fast exponential moving average of recent learned-clause LBDs against a slow global average; when the fast average exceeds it by a threshold — the search is learning *bad* clauses — it restarts immediately [4]. The policy adapts to proof quality rather than wall-clock time.

> **Theorem 2 (Restarts and proof power).** Clause learning with asserting clauses and *unlimited* restarts p-simulates general resolution: any resolution refutation can be reproduced by a CDCL solver with only polynomial overhead [9]. Without restarts, the correspondence collapses toward tree-like behavior.

The moral: restarts are not a hack for heavy tails but the mechanism lifting CDCL from DPLL-strength reasoning to full resolution strength.

### 4.5 Inprocessing: Vivification, Probing, and the DRAT Certification Pipeline

Between restarts, modern solvers *simplify* the formula in place — *inprocessing*. Two techniques carry most of the weight:

- **Clause vivification.** For a learned (or original) clause *C* = (*l*₁ ∨ … ∨ *l*ₖ), propagate ¬*l*₁ ∧ … ∧ ¬*l*ᵢ and test whether *l*ᵢ₊₁ is implied; if so, the clause can be shortened, and if a conflict arises, the clause subsumes to a smaller one. Vivification routinely removes a large fraction of literals from learned clauses at modest cost.
- **Probing / failed-literal detection.** Tentatively assign a literal and propagate; conflict proves the literal globally false. Applied to high-activity variables, probing finds equivalences and unit consequences BCP misses.

Every simplification must be *certifiable*: the solver logs each added or deleted clause in the **DRAT** format, expressive enough for all known preprocessing and inprocessing [7]. The independent checker DRAT-trim validates the proof in time comparable to solving and can trim it to the unsatisfiable core. **LRAT** adds antecedent *hints* to each step, enabling simple checkers mechanically verified in Coq and ACL2 — closing the trust gap between a solver's UNSAT claim and a machine-checked theorem [8].

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof complexity: the resolution characterization

Interpreting a CDCL run as a proof system — the solver's output on unsatisfiable inputs is a *refutation* — yields the deepest theoretical result in the area:

> **Theorem 3 (CDCL ≡ general resolution).** A CDCL solver with an asserting learning scheme, nondeterministic branching, and unlimited restarts polynomially simulates general resolution, and is polynomially simulated by it. Earlier work established the same equivalence under stronger assumptions (branching on assigned variables, or a preprocessing phase); the definitive formulation requires no such extras [9].

The proof's key device is *clause absorption*: the solver absorbs each clause of a resolution refutation, after which unit propagation behaves *as if* the clause were present. The practical reading: exponential resolution lower bounds (e.g. the pigeonhole principle) are inherited by CDCL, so no heuristic tuning makes the solver polynomial on formulas with only exponentially long refutations. Heuristics choose *which* short proof to find; they cannot conjure one that does not exist.

### 5.2 Empirical record

The LBD revolution is the field's cleanest controlled experiment. Audemard and Simon's ablation on 100 SAT-Race 2006 benchmarks: MiniSat solved 70, 74 with aggressive deletion alone, 79 with LBD retention alone, 82 with both; on 234 industrial instances Glucose solved 176 against MiniSat's 132, PicoSat's 153, RSat's 139 [4]. Two decades of SAT competitions confirm the pattern: every winner since 2009 descends from the MiniSat–Glucose lineage, differing in branching, restart scheduling, and inprocessing intensity — not architecture.

### 5.3 Certified unsatisfiability

The DRAT pipeline turns UNSAT answers into checkable artifacts: a sequence of clause additions (each RAT relative to the current formula) and deletions. DRAT-trim checks the proof backwards in time comparable to solving, overcoming the blowup that doomed earlier trace checkers [7]. LRAT's hint annotations make checking simple enough to verify in Coq and ACL2, so the final trust anchor is a mechanically checked theorem rather than a 100,000-line C++ solver [8].

### 5.4 Integration into SMT: the DPLL(T) framework

CDCL's largest deployment is inside SMT solvers. The DPLL(T) architecture abstracts each theory atom to a Boolean variable and runs CDCL on the Boolean *skeleton*; a theory solver checks each assignment and, on conflict, returns a *theory lemma* learned like any other clause. Theory *propagation* feeds implied literals directly into the implication graph. This lazy integration, on the assumption-based incremental interface pioneered by MiniSat [3], lets one CDCL core decide linear arithmetic, bit-vectors, arrays, and uninterpreted functions: learning, restarts, and clause management operate unchanged above an arbitrary theory oracle.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

CDCL's dominance should not be mistaken for completeness of understanding.

- **Inherited hardness.** Resolution lower bounds are absolute: pigeonhole, parity, and clique-coloring force exponential CDCL runs regardless of heuristics [9]. Extended resolution escapes some bounds, but no practical solver systematically exploits it.
- **Parallelism.** The CDCL loop is stubbornly sequential: implication graphs, activities, and clause databases are global mutable state. Portfolio solvers scale modestly; divide-and-conquer parallelism and certified proofs for parallel runs remain open.
- **Certification gaps.** DRAT captures all *known* techniques [7], but each new inprocessing rule must be proven RAT-compatible, and proof files for hard instances reach tens of gigabytes. LRAT verification [8] reduces checking cost but not proof size.
- **Heuristic opacity.** VSIDS, EVSIDS, and dynamic restarts are validated empirically, not derived. We lack a predictive theory of *which* learned clauses will matter, and machine-learned branching policies have yet to beat hand-tuned EVSIDS consistently.
- **Random and crafted frontiers.** Uniform random 3-SAT near the phase transition still favors look-ahead solvers over CDCL, and crafted combinatorial instances expose the gap between resolution strength and CDCL's realized strength.

Open problems: optimal *dynamic* restart policies beyond Luby universality [5][6]; a principled account of clause deletion to replace the empirical "delete half" rule [10]; certified checkers for the full inprocessing repertoire; parallel CDCL with independently verifiable proofs.

---

## 7. Conclusion

CDCL succeeded by unifying three previously separate ideas: *resolution* as the proof system, *conflict analysis* as the learning mechanism, and *aggressive engineering* — watched literals, VSIDS/EVSIDS, LBD-based forgetting, adaptive restarts — as the performance substrate. The theoretical endpoint, polynomial equivalence with general resolution [9], and the practical endpoint, machine-checked proofs via DRAT and LRAT [7][8], are two views of one achievement: search that explains itself. The frontier is not a new architecture but a deeper science of the heuristics: knowing, not guessing, which clauses to keep, when to restart, and where to branch.
