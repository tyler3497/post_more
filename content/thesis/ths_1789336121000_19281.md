# The Navier–Stokes Millennium Problem: Partial Regularity, the Caffarelli–Kohn–Nirenberg Theorem, and the Landscape of Critical Spaces

## Abstract

The three-dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes equations occupy the center of the Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium formulation of existence and smoothness, one of seven prize problems each carrying a one-million-dollar award [1]. This thesis surveys the analytical machinery developed around the problem over nine decades: Jean Leray's 1934 construction of global weak solutions [1], the Ladyzhenskaya–Prodi–Serrin regularity criteria, the Caffarelli–Kohn–Nirenberg partial regularity theorem — which confines the possible singular set to one-dimensional parabolic Hausdorff measure zero [2] — the theory of scaling-critical function spaces culminating in Koch and Tataru's BMO^{-1} well-posedness result [5], the Beale–Kato–Majda blow-up criterion [6], and the recent convex-integration non-uniqueness theorems of Buckmaster and Vicol [3] alongside Tao's finite-time blow-up for an averaged model equation [4]. We explain why the problem remains open, what each advance rules out, and where the analytical frontier now lies.

## 1. Introduction

Few equations in mathematical physics carry the cultural weight of the incompressible Navier–Stokes system. On a domain of three-dimensional space, the motion of a viscous fluid of unit density is governed by

$$\partial_t u + (u \cdot \nabla) u + \nabla p = \nu \Delta u + f, \qquad \nabla \cdot u = 0,$$

with velocity field $u$, pressure $p$, kinematic viscosity $\nu > 0$, and forcing $f$. The Clay Millennium formulation [1] asks, in essence, for a clean dichotomy: either prove that smooth, physically reasonable initial data always launch smooth solutions existing for all time, or exhibit a single counterexample — smooth data whose solution develops a singularity in finite time. Despite the equation's ubiquity in engineering and its apparent innocence, neither alternative has been established.

The difficulty is structural. The nonlinearity $(u \cdot \nabla)u$ is *supercritical* with respect to the only known a priori conserved quantity, the kinetic energy: energy can cascade from large scales to small ones faster than viscosity can dissipate it, and no known estimate forbids the cascade from reaching arbitrarily small scales in finite time. This thesis traces the five great lines of attack: weak solutions and their partial regularity, conditional regularity criteria, the critical-space well-posedness program, blow-up criteria, and the convex-integration revolution that has redrawn the map of what "solution" even means.

---

## 2. Background

The modern theory begins with Jean Leray's 1934 *Acta Mathematica* memoir [1]. Leray introduced *weak solutions*: velocity fields satisfying the equations in the sense of distributions, constructed as limits of regularized problems. For initial data of finite kinetic energy, he proved global-in-time existence of such solutions in three dimensions. Eberhard Hopf extended the construction to bounded domains with Dirichlet boundary conditions. These **Leray–Hopf weak solutions** satisfy the *energy inequality*

$$\|u(t)\|_{L^2}^2 + 2\nu \int_0^t \|\nabla u(s)\|_{L^2}^2\, ds \le \|u_0\|_{L^2}^2,$$

but they are not known to be unique or smooth. Leray himself conjectured that singularities might genuinely occur, a conjecture that remains neither proved nor disproved.

A decisive refinement came from the Soviet and Italian schools. Ladyzhenskaya, Prodi, and Serrin independently discovered *conditional regularity* criteria: a Leray–Hopf solution that additionally satisfies

$$u \in L^r_t L^s_x \quad \text{with} \quad \frac{2}{r} + \frac{3}{s} = 1, \;\; 3 < s \le \infty,$$

on a time interval must be smooth there. These **Ladyzhenskaya–Prodi–Serrin (LPS)** conditions are *scaling-critical*: the relation $2/r + 3/s = 1$ is exactly the one preserved by the natural scaling symmetry of the equations (see §3). The message is that *critical* integrability rules out singularities — but the energy inequality only controls the *subcritical* quantity $L^\infty_t L^2_x \cap L^2_t \dot{H}^1_x$, leaving a gap the criteria cannot close.

A key technical notion crystallized: a **suitable weak solution** is a Leray–Hopf solution that additionally satisfies a *local* energy inequality [10],

$$\int |u|^2 \phi\, dx + 2\nu \iint |\nabla u|^2 \phi\, dxdt \le \iint \left(|u|^2(\partial_t\phi + \Delta\phi) + (|u|^2 + 2p)\, u \cdot \nabla\phi\right) dxdt,$$

for all nonnegative test functions $\phi$. Suitability is the hypothesis that unlocks the deepest known regularity theorem.

## 3. Methodology

Three methodological pillars support everything that follows.

**Scaling analysis.** If $u(x,t)$ solves Navier–Stokes with pressure $p(x,t)$, then for every $\lambda > 0$ the rescaled pair

$$u_\lambda(x,t) = \lambda u(\lambda x, \lambda^2 t), \qquad p_\lambda(x,t) = \lambda^2 p(\lambda x, \lambda^2 t)$$

is also a solution. A function space $X$ of initial data is *critical* when its norm is invariant under $u_0 \mapsto \lambda u_0(\lambda \cdot)$. The energy space $\dot{H}^{1/2}$, $L^3$, and BMO^{-1} are critical; $L^2$ is subcritical (its norm blows up under zooming in) and $H^1$ is supercritical. Criticality is the dividing line where the equation's own symmetry neither helps nor hurts — the natural arena for well-posedness.

**Mild solutions and Picard iteration.** Projecting onto divergence-free fields with the Leray projector $\mathbb{P}$, the system becomes the integral equation

$$u(t) = e^{t\Delta}u_0 - \int_0^t e^{(t-s)\Delta} \mathbb{P} \nabla \cdot (u \otimes u)(s)\, ds.$$

Kato's method treats this as a fixed-point problem for the bilinear operator $B(u,v)$, using smoothing estimates of the heat semigroup to obtain local existence, uniqueness, and smoothness for data in critical spaces.

**The ε-regularity strategy.** Caffarelli, Kohn, and Nirenberg's insight [2] was to work with *scale-invariant local quantities*. On a parabolic cylinder $Q_r(z_0) = B_r(x_0) \times (t_0 - r^2, t_0)$, the dimensionless number

$$\Phi(r, z_0) = \frac{1}{r^2} \iint_{Q_r(z_0)} \left(|u|^3 + |p|^{3/2}\right) dxdt$$

is invariant under the scaling above. If this quantity is *small*, the solution must be regular at $z_0$ — a *smallness implies smoothness* principle. The proof proceeds by contradiction and blow-up: rescale a putative singularity to unit size, extract a limit solving a linear system, and contradict minimality. Covering arguments then convert the local criterion into a global bound on the *size* of the singular set.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Caffarelli–Kohn–Nirenberg theorem: ε-regularity and the singular set

> **Theorem (Caffarelli–Kohn–Nirenberg, 1982) [2].** Let $(u,p)$ be a suitable weak solution of the three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations. Then there exists an absolute constant $\varepsilon_0 > 0$ such that if $\Phi(r, z_0) < \varepsilon_0$ for some $r > 0$, $u$ is Hölder continuous in a neighborhood of $z_0$. Moreover, the singular set $\mathcal{S}$ — the set of points where $u$ fails to be regular — satisfies $\mathcal{H}^1_P(\mathcal{S}) = 0$, where $\mathcal{H}^1_P$ denotes one-dimensional parabolic Hausdorff measure.

The conclusion is deliberately *partial*: singularities are not ruled out, but the set where they can occur is *thin* — of parabolic Hausdorff dimension at most one. In particular, no space-time curve of singularities can exist; Leray's imagined line vortices are impossible for suitable weak solutions.

The proof proceeds by contradiction and blow-up: smallness of $\Phi$ at scale $r$ forces even smaller relative size at a smaller scale, via compactness against the linearized Stokes system; iterating gives Hölder continuity. A Vitali covering argument then shows the set where $\Phi(r, z_0) \ge \varepsilon_0$ for all small $r$ — which must contain $\mathcal{S}$ — has vanishing $\mathcal{H}^1_P$ measure.

Later simplifications by Lin (1998, blow-up analysis), Ladyzhenskaya–Seregin (1999), and Vasseur (2007, De Giorgi methods) streamlined the argument without weakening it. More recently, *quantitative* versions have emerged: a 2024 result in *Advances in Mathematics* gives a logarithmic improvement, constructing regularity intervals in one spatial direction whose length depends explicitly on local energies [7]. The frontier question — whether the Hausdorff dimension bound can be pushed below one, or whether Scheffer-type examples show it sharp — remains active.

### 4.2 Critical spaces: from Ladyzhenskaya–Prodi–Serrin to BMO^{-1}

The well-posedness program asks: in which critical spaces does small data guarantee global smooth solutions, and large data guarantee local ones? The chain of inclusions, each step enlarging the admissible class, is one of the subject's great narratives:

| Space $X$ | Critical norm | Small-data global result | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^3)$ | $\| \xi \| \hat{u} \in L^2$ | Fujita–Kato (1964) | Classical |
| $L^3(\mathbb{R}^3)$ | $\|u\|_{L^3}$ | Kato (1984), Giga–Miyakawa | Classical |
| $\dot{B}^{-1+3/p}_{p,\infty}$, $3 < p < \infty$ | Besov norm | Cannone, Planchon (1990s) | Classical |
| BMO^{-1}$(\mathbb{R}^3)$ | Carleson / heat-flow norm | Koch–Tataru (2001) [5] | Largest "reasonable" |
| $\dot{B}^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}$ | $\sup_t \sqrt{t}\,\|e^{t\Delta}u_0\|_\infty$ | Bourgain–Pavlović (2008) | **Ill-posed** (norm inflation) |

> **Theorem (Koch–Tataru, 2001) [5].** There exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that for divergence-free $u_0$ with $\|u_0\|_{\mathrm{BMO}^{-1}} < \varepsilon$, the Navier–Stokes equations admit a unique global mild solution, smooth for $t > 0$ and decaying appropriately.

The space BMO^{-1}, defined via the heat-flow Carleson norm

$$\|u_0\|_{\mathrm{BMO}^{-1}} = \sup_{x_0, R} \left( \frac{1}{|B_R|} \int_0^{R^2} \int_{B_R(x_0)} |e^{t\Delta}u_0|^2\, dxdt \right)^{1/2},$$

Koch and Tataru's proof works in an adapted path space controlling $\sup_t \sqrt{t}\,\|u(t)\|_{L^\infty}$ together with Carleson tent-space seminorms — a framework that has since become standard.

The table's final row is a warning: Bourgain and Pavlović proved *norm inflation* — hence ill-posedness — in the largest critical Besov space $\dot{B}^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}$. The well-posedness program therefore *terminates*: BMO^{-1} is essentially optimal for the mild-solution approach. Any proof of global regularity for large data must exploit structure beyond critical well-posedness.

The scaling symmetry itself can be encoded and checked mechanically. The following Haskell fragment expresses the criticality condition used throughout this analysis:

```haskell
-- Scaling symmetry of 3D Navier-Stokes: u_l(x,t) = l * u(l*x, l^2*t)
-- A norm ||.||_X is critical iff ||u_l||_X = ||u||_X for all l > 0.

type Field = (Double, Double, Double) -> (Double, Double, Double)

rescale :: Double -> Field -> Field
rescale l u = \(x,y,z) ->
  let (a,b,c) = u (l*x, l*y, l*z) in (l*a, l*b, l*c)

-- Homogeneous Sobolev norm of order s scales as l^(s - 1/2) in 3D:
-- criticality <=> s = 1/2, i.e. H^{1/2}; L^3 and BMO^{-1} share the exponent.
scalingExponent :: Double -> Double
scalingExponent s = s - 0.5   -- 3*(1/2 - 1/3) - 1/2 ... reduced form

isCritical :: Double -> Bool
isCritical s = abs (scalingExponent s) < 1e-12
```

### 4.3 Blow-up criteria: Beale–Kato–Majda and refined continuation principles

If singularities do form, *how* must they announce themselves? Blow-up criteria give necessary conditions on any finite-time singularity, constraining the search for counterexamples and guiding numerics.

> **Theorem (Beale–Kato–Majda, 1984) [6].** Let $u$ be a smooth solution on a time interval with maximal existence time $T^* < \infty$. Then
> $$\int_0^{T^*} \|\omega(t)\|_{L^\infty}\, dt = \infty, \qquad \omega = \nabla \times u.$$

In words: no singularity without unbounded, time-integrated vorticity. The proof controls higher Sobolev norms by $\|\omega\|_{L^\infty}$ via logarithmic Sobolev-type estimates for the singular integral operators relating $\nabla u$ to $\omega$ (the Biot–Savart law). The criterion is *almost* critical: $\int \|\omega\|_\infty dt$ is dimensionless under Navier–Stokes scaling.

Refinements have proliferated: the criterion can be localized to single vorticity components, to the *direction* of vorticity (Constantin–Fefferman), and to scaling-critical norms like $L^\infty_t L^3_x$. Each refinement shrinks the set of *a priori* possible blow-up scenarios. Yet none closes the problem, because all known criteria are *conditional*: they describe what blow-up would look like, not whether it occurs.

A computational sanity check on the LPS numerology — verifying that a candidate singular profile violates the critical relation — is routine in numerical studies:

```python
import numpy as np

def lps_exponent(r, s):
    """Return 2/r + 3/s. Regularity holds if == 1 (critical) and norm is small."""
    return 2.0 / r + 3.0 / s

# Energy-class control: u in L^inf_t L^2_x  =>  exponent = 3/2 > 1 (subcritical: insufficient)
print("energy class:", lps_exponent(np.inf, 2.0))
# Prodi-Serrin endpoint: u in L^inf_t L^3_x  =>  exponent = 1 (critical)
print("LPS endpoint:  ", lps_exponent(np.inf, 3.0))
# Beale-Kato-Majda: int ||omega||_inf dt = inf  <=>  omega in L^1_t L^inf_x
print("BKM quantity:  ", lps_exponent(1.0, np.inf), "(dimensionless)")
```

### 4.4 Convex integration, non-uniqueness, and averaged blow-up models

The most dramatic recent development abandons regularity *within* a solution class and instead interrogates the class itself. De Lellis and Székelyhidi imported *convex integration* — Nash's technique from isometric embeddings — into fluid mechanics, resolving Onsager's conjecture for Euler. Buckmaster and Vicol extended the method past the viscous term:

> **Theorem (Buckmaster–Vicol, 2019) [3].** There exist infinitely many weak solutions of the 3D Navier–Stokes equations with the same finite-energy initial data — in particular, nontrivial solutions emerging from *zero* initial data. These solutions can realize any prescribed smooth non-negative energy profile.

The construction uses *intermittent* Beltrami flows: concentrating the perturbation on a small spatial fraction tames the viscous error $\nu \Delta w \sim \nu \lambda^2 w$ that would otherwise destroy the iteration at high frequencies. The solutions are *very weak* — distributional solutions with finite energy but no energy inequality — so they do not contradict CKN [2] or Leray. Whether non-uniqueness persists for *Leray–Hopf* solutions (which obey the energy inequality) is now the central open question of the weak-solution program; subsequent work by Buckmaster, Colombo, and Vicol pushes non-uniqueness toward solutions smooth outside a fractal set of singular times of Hausdorff dimension below one [3].

A complementary line probes whether the *equation itself* can blow up. Tao considered an *averaged* Navier–Stokes system $\partial_t u = \Delta u + \tilde{B}(u,u)$, where $\tilde{B}$ averages the true bilinearity over rotations, dilations, and order-zero Fourier multipliers while preserving the cancellation $\langle \tilde{B}(u,u), u \rangle = 0$ (hence the energy identity).

The proof analyzes a dyadic ODE system of Katz–Pavlovic type, engineering a cascade that defeats dissipation. The *supercriticality barrier* formalized here is profound: any proof of global regularity for true Navier–Stokes *cannot* rely only on harmonic-analysis estimates plus the energy identity — it must exploit finer structure of the genuine nonlinearity $B(u,u)$ that averaging destroys.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

"Empirical" in this context means *quantitative and computational* rather than laboratory. Three strands deserve emphasis.

1. **Quantitative partial regularity.** The recent logarithmic improvement of CKN [7] replaces qualitative covering arguments with explicit bounds: given local energy bounds, one can *compute* intervals of regularity in one spatial direction — partial regularity made *effective*, a prerequisite for any computer-assisted analysis of potential singularities.

2. **Numerical blow-up searches.** High-resolution computations (notably Hou–Luo's scenario for axisymmetric flow with swirl) have produced *candidate* singular profiles with self-similar structure. None is proven to correspond to a genuine finite-time singularity, and several candidates were later excluded by refined criteria. The numerics function as an *experimental* wing: they propose scenarios that analysis must then confirm or kill.

3. **Sharpness results.** Scheffer's constructions of blowing-up solutions to the Navier–Stokes *inequality* [9], recently sharpened with prescribed energy profiles via convex integration, show that CKN partial regularity is *sharp* in its natural generality: the theorem says exactly as much as can be said without stronger hypotheses. This is a *negative* empirical result of great value — it tells us where not to look.

The proof techniques across these strands share a common grammar: scale-invariant quantities, contradiction via blow-up limits, and monotonicity formulas. Mastering that grammar is, arguably, the real prerequisite for engaging the Millennium problem.

## 6. Limitations

Honesty requires stating what the surveyed machinery does *not* deliver.

- **Partial regularity is not regularity.** CKN [2] leaves open a singular set of parabolic Hausdorff dimension up to one. No known argument shrinks this to zero for suitable weak solutions, and Scheffer-type examples [9] suggest the bound may be sharp in full generality.
- **Critical well-posedness stops at BMO^{-1}.** Bourgain–Pavlović ill-posedness in $\dot{B}^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}$ shows the mild-solution program cannot be pushed further by soft methods; large-data global regularity, if true, needs genuinely new ideas.
- **Non-uniqueness results concern very weak solutions.** Buckmaster–Vicol [3] solutions violate the energy inequality; they do not touch Leray–Hopf uniqueness, the class relevant to physics. Convex integration has not produced a Leray–Hopf counterexample.
- **Tao's blow-up is for an averaged model.** The averaged bilinearity $\tilde{B}$ [4] discards geometric structure (in particular the precise vortex-stretching term $\langle S, \omega \otimes \omega \rangle$) that might be exactly what prevents blow-up in the true equations.
- **Numerics cannot resolve the problem.** Any finite computation is consistent with both eventual regularity and blow-up just beyond resolution; only proof decides.
## 7. Conclusion

Nine decades after Leray, the Navier–Stokes regularity problem stands as a monument to the gap between *existence* and *control*. We possess global weak solutions but not their uniqueness; a partial regularity theory of remarkable precision but not full regularity; sharp critical-space well-posedness up to an optimal endpoint but not large-data global existence; blow-up criteria describing every hypothetical singularity but no singularity; and, via convex integration, wild solutions proving that the equations, unadorned by admissibility criteria, do not select a unique future.

The deepest lesson of the surveyed literature is *negative* in the best scientific sense: Tao's supercriticality barrier [4] proves that whole families of plausible proof strategies *cannot* work, while Buckmaster–Vicol [3] proves that whole families of plausible *solution concepts* do not determine dynamics. Each impossibility result is a fence narrowing the terrain on which the true answer — regularity or blow-up — must live.

Whether the eventual resolution comes from a refined understanding of vortex stretching, a computer-assisted quantitative argument built on [7], or an explicit blow-up construction completing Tao's program, it will have to thread every needle described here: respect scaling, survive the ε-regularity gauntlet, and explain why the wild solutions are unphysical. The Millennium prize awaits whoever finds the thread.
