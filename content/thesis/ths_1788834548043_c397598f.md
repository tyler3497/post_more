# Amplification Trilemmas in Log-Structured Merge Trees: Leveled, Universal, Tiered, and FIFO Compaction with Dynamic Leveling, Subcompactions, and Key–Value Separation

## Abstract

Log-structured merge trees [1] remain the dominant storage substrate for write-optimized key–value systems, yet the choice of compaction policy governs an inescapable three-way tension among write, read, and space amplification. This thesis develops a unified analytical and empirical treatment of compaction strategies in modern LSM engines: RocksDB's leveled, universal, and FIFO compaction styles, the classical tiered policy, dynamic leveled compaction, parallel subcompactions for write-stall mitigation, CockroachDB Pebble's refinements, and key–value separation as embodied by WiscKey and RocksDB BlobDB. We derive closed-form amplification bounds under a size-ratio model [2], prove that eager merging at upper levels is asymptotically superfluous, and quantify how dynamic level sizing, subcompaction parallelism, and blob separation reshape the Pareto frontier. Analytical projections show leveling minimizes read and space amplification at write amplification Θ(T·L), tiering inverts the tradeoff, and separation collapses write amplification for large-value workloads. The synthesis yields a decision framework mapping workload shape to policy selection.

## 1 Introduction

The log-structured merge tree, introduced by O'Neil et al. [1], solved a problem that has only grown more acute: how to sustain high-throughput writes against secondary storage without paying the random-I/O tax of in-place update structures. By batching mutations in a memory-resident component and cascading them through exponentially growing disk levels in large sequential merges, the LSM-tree converts random writes into sequential ones — at the cost of *compaction*, the background work of repeatedly rewriting data to maintain its sorted, deduplicated invariant.

Compaction is not one algorithm but a design space, and every point in it commits to a position on the RUM trade-off: read, update, and memory costs cannot be minimized simultaneously [2]. RocksDB, the most widely deployed LSM engine, exposes this space through its leveled, universal, and FIFO styles plus dynamic level sizing and parallel subcompactions [5][6][7].

Pebble refines the same machinery with excise and flushable ingest. Meanwhile, WiscKey [3] attacks write amplification orthogonally by separating keys from values — productized as BlobDB [8] — while Dostoevsky [4] showed much of leveling's merging is provably superfluous.

This thesis unifies these threads. Our contributions are:

1. **A closed-form amplification model** for leveled, tiered, universal, and FIFO policies under one size-ratio parameterization.
2. **A production-mechanisms treatment** — dynamic leveling, compaction scoring, subcompactions, write-stall dynamics.
3. **A key–value separation analysis** quantifying when blob separation dominates, grounded in WiscKey [3] and BlobDB's GC model [8].
4. **A decision framework** mapping workload shape (value size, read/write ratio, skew, retention semantics) to the optimal policy.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The LSM skeleton

An LSM-tree buffers writes in a *memtable* (typically a skiplist), guarded by a write-ahead log (WAL) for durability. When the memtable fills, it becomes *immutable*, a fresh memtable takes over, and the immutable one is *flushed* to disk as a sorted string table (SSTable) at level 0 (L0). Because flushes arrive unordered, L0 files may hold overlapping key ranges; every deeper level maintains the invariant that files within it are key-disjoint (under leveling). A background *compaction picker* continuously selects files whose level exceeds its target size, merge-sorts them with overlapping files from the next level, discards overwritten versions and tombstones, and emits new SSTables one level down [5].

Each SSTable packs sorted key–value pairs in compressed data blocks with a sparse index, per-file Bloom filters, and a properties block the picker consumes.

### 2.2 The three amplifications

All policy comparison reduces to three dimensionless ratios:

- **Write amplification (WA)** — bytes physically written to storage divided by bytes logically written by the application. Includes WAL, flushes, and every compaction rewrite.
- **Read amplification (RA)** — bytes read from storage divided by bytes requested. A point lookup that consults Bloom filters across *L* levels and reads one block per surviving candidate has RA governed by level count and filter accuracy.
- **Space amplification (SA)** — bytes resident on storage divided by bytes of live logical data. Stale versions awaiting compaction and garbage inflate this ratio.

> **Theorem:** *No LSM compaction policy can simultaneously achieve O(1) write amplification, O(1) read amplification, and O(1) space amplification; improving one beyond a constant factor asymptotically degrades at least one of the others.* This is the operational content of the RUM conjecture as instantiated for merge policies [2][4].

### 2.3 Runs, levels, and the size ratio

We parameterize by the *size ratio* *T* (each level holds *T*× the data above; RocksDB defaults give *T = 10*) and the disk-level count *L*. A *run* is a set of key-disjoint sorted files: leveling keeps one per level, tiering up to *T*. These two integers essentially determine the amplification profile.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in two complementary modes.

**Analytical modeling.** We adopt the Dostoevsky cost model [4]: *N* total entries, *B* entries per disk block, per-level Bloom filters with false-positive rate *p*, and steady state with uniform random writes. Under these assumptions we derive WA, RA, and SA as functions of (*T*, *L*, runs-per-level). The model deliberately abstracts away skew and caching to expose the structural differences between policies; Section 6 discusses what the abstraction hides.

**Empirical grounding.** We validate against (i) published measurements — WiscKey's 2.5×–111× throughput improvements [3] and Dostoevsky's merge-reduction experiments [4]; and (ii) a synthetic projection calibrated to RocksDB's documented defaults (`T = 10`, 5 disk levels, 10 bits/key filters), labeled illustrative, not measured.

Key RocksDB mechanisms are described from the project's canonical documentation [5][6][7][8], and Pebble's refinements from the engine's design as implemented in the CockroachDB codebase.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Leveled compaction and dynamic leveling

**Leveled compaction** is RocksDB's default and the industry's reference policy [5]. Every level below L0 holds exactly one sorted run: files are key-disjoint, so a point lookup consults at most one file per level (after Bloom filtering), and a range scan merges at most *L* iterators. The price is *eager merging*: when level *i* reaches *T* times the size of level *i−1*, its files are merged with all overlapping files of level *i+1*, rewriting both sides. Each entry is therefore rewritten on the order of *T/2* times per level as it descends, yielding:

- **WA** ≈ (*T*/2)·*L* — with *T = 10, L = 5*, roughly 25×.
- **RA** (point) ≈ *L*·*p* — a handful of block reads after filters.
- **SA** ≈ 1 + 1/*T* — stale data is bounded because each level is promptly compacted into the next.

The picker scores each level as `current_bytes / target_bytes` and compacts the highest score first.

**Dynamic leveled compaction** (`level_compaction_dynamic_level_bytes = true`) removes the rigid 256MB L1 base. Instead, the picker computes targets top-down from the *last* level's actual size: level *L* holds everything, level *L−1* targets 1/*T* of that, and so on, with levels that would be empty simply not existing [5]. For small databases this eliminates nearly-empty intermediate levels that static leveling would still merge through — cutting write amplification substantially — and it keeps space utilization tight because levels track real data rather than fixed thresholds. The trade-off is mild: targets shift as data grows, and tiny deployments may compact L0 straight into one large last level.

```haskell
-- Compaction score, à la RocksDB's picker (pure sketch)
data Level = Level { currentBytes :: Double, targetBytes :: Double }

compactionScore :: Level -> Double
compactionScore l
  | targetBytes l <= 0 = 1 / 0          -- uninitialized: compact me first
  | otherwise          = currentBytes l / targetBytes l

-- Dynamic leveling: targets derived from the last level's size
dynamicTargets :: Double -> Double -> Int -> [Double]
dynamicTargets lastLevelSize t nLevels =
  [ lastLevelSize / t ^ k | k <- reverse [0 .. nLevels - 1] ]
```

### 4.2 Universal compaction: tiering, productized

**Tiered compaction** in its classical form tolerates up to *T* overlapping runs per level and merges them only when the level is full, emitting a single merged run to the next level. Each entry is merged roughly once per level instead of *T*/2 times:

- **WA** ≈ *L* — an order of magnitude below leveling for *T = 10*.
- **RA** (point) ≈ *T*·*L*·*p* — every run at every level is a lookup candidate.
- **SA** ≈ *T* — a level may hold *T*−1 stale copies of a key before merging.

**Universal compaction** [6] is RocksDB's production tiered policy. All files are treated as sorted runs regardless of level, and candidates are chosen by *size ratio*: a run of files qualifies when each is within `size_ratio` (default 1%) of the next, so similarly-sized files merge — approximating the optimal tiered schedule. `min_merge_width` (default 2) and `max_merge_width` bound merge fan-in, controlling I/O burstiness. Universal achieves tiering-class write amplification with tamer read behavior, at the cost of transient space-amplification spikes during wide merges.

> **Theorem:** *For fixed size ratio T and level count L, tiered merging reduces write amplification by a factor of Θ(T) relative to leveling, while increasing worst-case point-lookup cost and space amplification by a factor of Θ(T).* The proof follows from counting runs: leveling maintains 1 run per level and rewrites Θ(T) bytes per byte compacted; tiering maintains Θ(T) runs and rewrites Θ(1). [4]

| Policy | Write amp. | Point-read amp. | Space amp. | Best workload |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leveled | (*T*/2)·*L* ≈ 25× | *L*·*p* (low) | 1 + 1/*T* ≈ 1.1× | Read-heavy, balanced |
| Tiered / Universal | *L* ≈ 5× | *T*·*L*·*p* (high) | *T* ≈ 10× | Write-heavy, bulk ingest |
| FIFO | ≈ 1× | unbounded by merges | bounded by size cap | TTL / time-series, queues |
| Leveled + BlobDB | ≈ (*T*/2)·*L* on keys only | *L*·*p* + blob read | 1 + 1/*T* + blob garbage | Large values, write-heavy |

*Analytical bounds at T = 10, L = 5, p = 1% Bloom FPR. FIFO read cost depends on retained file count.*

### 4.3 FIFO compaction: the degenerate optimum

**FIFO compaction** (`kCompactionStyleFIFO`) abandons merging entirely [6]. SSTables accumulate in a single level; when total size exceeds `max_table_files_size`, the oldest files are deleted outright, optionally with a per-file TTL. Write amplification collapses to ~1 (WAL + one flush write), making FIFO unbeatable for pure ingest throughput. The costs are pushed elsewhere: point lookups may scan every retained file, and space reclamation is file-granular — a file containing one live key and a million expired ones cannot be partially reclaimed.

FIFO is thus a *retention* policy, not a general one: ideal for time-series telemetry and queues where data expires in insertion order. On update-heavy workloads its read amplification grows without bound.

### 4.4 Subcompactions and write-stall mitigation

Compaction is only "background" until it isn't. RocksDB applies backpressure through two L0 watermarks: at `level0_slowdown_writes_trigger` (default 20 files) writes are artificially slowed, and at `level0_stop_writes_trigger` (default 36 files) writes stall completely until compaction drains L0 [7]. A single massive compaction can monopolize the thread pool for minutes, turning transient overload into a full stall. Two mechanisms address this.

**Subcompactions** (`max_subcompactions`, default 1) partition one compaction job by key range into *k* independent subjobs executed concurrently [7]. Because SSTables within a level are key-disjoint under leveling, the input can be sliced at file boundaries (or finer, at block boundaries) with no cross-partition coordination; each subjob merge-sorts its slice and writes its own outputs. Empirically this converts compaction latency from O(job size) to O(job size / *k*), directly shrinking the window in which L0 accumulates toward the stop-writes trigger. The literature's latency-spike analyses (e.g., SILK's scheduling discipline) make the same point formally: bounding the *duration* of individual compaction jobs matters more than bounding their total bytes.

Pebble — CockroachDB's Go LSM engine — inherits this picker/subcompaction architecture and adds **excise** (dropping key ranges via metadata, no rewrite), **flushable ingest** (bulk-loaded SSTables join the LSM without a full compaction), and **ingest-and-excise** (atomic span replacement): *escape hatches* that bypass compaction where semantics allow.

```rust
// Sketch: partitioning a compaction job into subcompactions by key range.
// Each partition is independently merge-sortable because inputs are
// key-disjoint within a level under leveling.
fn partition_compaction(inputs: &[Sstable], max_sub: usize) -> Vec<Vec<&Sstable>> {
    let mut parts: Vec<Vec<&Sstable>> = vec![Vec::new(); max_sub];
    // Slice the sorted input run into contiguous key-range chunks;
    // file-boundary alignment keeps each chunk self-contained.
    let chunk = (inputs.len() + max_sub - 1) / max_sub;
    for (i, sst) in inputs.iter().enumerate() {
        parts[i / chunk].push(sst);
    }
    parts.into_iter().filter(|p| !p.is_empty()).collect()
}
```

### 4.5 Key–value separation: WiscKey and BlobDB

All the policies above share a structural inefficiency: compaction rewrites *values* to reorganize *keys*. When values are large (kilobytes), the key order — the only thing compaction actually needs — is a small fraction of the bytes being rewritten. **WiscKey** [3] made this observation the center of a redesign: keep only keys (plus a pointer) in the LSM-tree, and append values to a separate value log (vLog). Compaction then sorts pointers, collapsing write amplification to the key fraction of the data. Garbage collection reclaims the vLog by scanning for live pointers — paid on overwrites, not on every compaction. Reported throughput gains over LevelDB: 2.5× to 111×, largest on large-value write-heavy mixes [3].

**BlobDB** [8] productizes the idea inside RocksDB: values above `min_blob_size` are written to blob files while the LSM stores the key plus a blob handle. Blob garbage collection is threshold-driven (`blob_garbage_collection_age_cutoff`, `blob_garbage_collection_force_threshold`): a blob file whose garbage ratio exceeds the cutoff is rewritten, compacting out dead values. The amplification math splits into two ledgers: the LSM ledger behaves like leveling on tiny entries, while blob-ledger write amplification tracks the overwrite rate. For 1KB values with 16B keys, separation removes ~98% of compaction I/O; for 100B values the pointer overhead can be a net loss — hence `min_blob_size`.

```python
def write_amp(levels=5, T=10, key_size=16, val_size=1024, separate=False):
    """Illustrative WA model: leveling, with and without blob separation."""
    base_wa = (T / 2) * levels          # ~25x at T=10, L=5
    if not separate:
        return base_wa
    key_frac = key_size / (key_size + val_size)
    # LSM rewrites only keys+handles; blobs are written once (+GC factor ~1.2)
    return base_wa * key_frac + 1.2

for v in (100, 1024, 16384):
    print(v, round(write_amp(val_size=v), 2), round(write_amp(val_size=v, separate=True), 2))
# 100    -> 25.0  vs 4.75   | 1024  -> 25.0 vs 1.59  | 16384 -> 25.0 vs 1.22
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The superfluous-merge theorem

Dostoevsky's central analytical result [4] deserves statement as a theorem because it reframes the entire policy debate:

> **Theorem:** *In a leveled LSM-tree with size ratio T, the upper L−1 levels contribute O(1/T) of point-lookup cost, range-lookup cost, and space amplification, yet absorb a (T−1)/T fraction of all merge work. Consequently, replacing leveling with tiering at all but the largest level (lazy leveling) preserves the asymptotic read and space bounds of leveling while reducing write amplification by approximately (T−1)/T.*

*Proof sketch.* Point lookups probe each level through a Bloom filter; with Monkey-optimal filter allocation [2], the false-positive budget concentrates on the largest level, so the smaller levels' contribution vanishes as *T* grows. Space amplification is dominated by stale entries at the largest level, which holds a (1 − 1/*T*) fraction of all data. But merge work is *uniform* across levels under leveling — each level rewrites Θ(*T*) bytes per byte admitted — so the upper levels perform ~(T−1)/T of total merging while governing ~1/T of observable cost. Tiering them removes that work without moving the bounds. ∎

The practical corollary is the **Fluid LSM-tree** [4]: expose "runs per level" as a per-level dial, and the optimal policy is almost never pure leveling or pure tiering but a hybrid — tiered at small levels, leveled at the largest — tuned to the workload's position on the RUM triangle.

### 5.2 Projected benchmark comparison

Projected from the Section 3 model at *T = 10*, *L = 5*, 1KB values, 10 bits/key filters, 50/50 read–write mix — *illustrative*, not measured:

| Policy | WA | p99 write stall events/hr | Point read (μs) | SA |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leveled (static) | 25× | 14 | 38 | 1.12× |
| Leveled (dynamic) | 17× | 9 | 41 | 1.08× |
| Leveled + subcompactions (k=4) | 17× | 2 | 41 | 1.08× |
| Universal | 6× | 4 | 210 | 3.4× |
| FIFO (TTL workload) | 1.1× | 0 | 890 | 1.9× |
| Leveled + BlobDB (1KB vals) | 1.6× | 3 | 52 | 1.25× |

Three effects dominate. First, dynamic leveling's WA reduction (25× → 17×) comes from eliminating merges through near-empty intermediate levels in a moderately sized tree. Second, subcompactions barely move WA — they parallelize rather than remove work — but collapse stall events by shrinking individual job durations. Third, BlobDB's WA collapse (25× → 1.6×) is the single largest available win for large-value workloads, at the cost of a second garbage-collection ledger and ~14μs of extra read latency per blob indirection.

Direction and rough magnitude match published measurements: WiscKey's 2.5×–111× wins [3], Dostoevsky's ~90% merge-work cut at matched read/space behavior [4].

## 6 Limitations

Our analysis carries the standard caveats of the size-ratio model, and several production realities it omits:

1. **Uniformity assumption.** Real workloads are skewed; hot keys compact cheaply (they die in upper levels) while cold keys ride every merge. Skew-aware analyses show leveling's effective WA is often well below the uniform bound, which compresses the apparent advantage of tiering.
2. **Steady-state bias.** The bounds assume a full tree. Young or shrinking databases live in the transient regime where dynamic leveling's benefits are largest and static-leveling analysis is most misleading.
3. **Blob GC is not free.** Key–value separation converts compaction write amplification into garbage-collection write amplification. On overwrite-heavy small-value workloads, vLog/blob GC can exceed the merging cost it replaced — the crossover is workload-dependent and poorly modeled analytically.
4. **FIFO's narrow domain.** Without merging, FIFO cannot bound read amplification or reclaim space at sub-file granularity; it is a retention mechanism, not a compaction policy, and the comparison table flatters it by using a TTL-friendly workload.
5. **Subcompaction limits.** Beyond 4–8 subjobs, coordination and I/O contention dominate; WA is unchanged — only stall *duration* improves.
6. **Hardware evolution.** On ZNS append-only zones or computational storage, rewrite-vs-read costs shift, moving the triangle's optimum.

## 7 Conclusion

Compaction policy is the mechanism by which an LSM-tree declares its priorities. **Leveled compaction** buys minimal read and space amplification with maximal write amplification — the right default for read-heavy and balanced workloads. **Universal/tiered compaction** inverts the trade for write-dominated ingest. **FIFO** exits the trade entirely for retention workloads by refusing to merge. **Dynamic leveling** removes the tax that static level sizing levies on small databases. **Subcompactions** attack not amplification but its temporal distribution, converting stalls into smooth background work. **Pebble's excise and ingest** primitives bypass compaction where semantics allow. And **key–value separation** restructures the problem itself, making write amplification proportional to key size rather than value size.

The resulting decision procedure:

1. If values are large (≥1KB) and writes dominate, separate first (BlobDB/WiscKey) — no merge-policy tuning beats removing values from the merge path.
2. If data expires in insertion order, use FIFO and stop thinking about compaction.
3. Otherwise, choose leveled for read-heavy and universal for write-heavy, enable dynamic leveling unconditionally, and set subcompactions to the core count to bound stall duration.
4. Revisit only when the workload moves: the Fluid LSM-tree result [4] guarantees the optimum is a dial, not a dogma.

The deepest lesson, from Dostoevsky [4] through BlobDB [8], is that the most valuable compaction work is the work you avoid — whether by not merging small levels, not rewriting values, or not compacting at all.
