# Automated Theorem Proving: The Resolution Calculus, DPLL/CDCL SAT Solving, SMT Decision Procedures, and Superposition — Soundness, Completeness, and Complexity

## Abstract

Automated theorem proving reduces logical entailment to computation. This thesis presents a unified account of the four dominant refutational paradigms: Robinson's resolution calculus for first-order logic, the Davis–Putnam–Logemann–Loveland procedure and its modern conflict-driven clause-learning (CDCL) descendants for propositional satisfiability, satisfiability modulo theories (SMT) via the lazy DPLL(T) architecture, and the superposition calculus for equational first-order logic. We define each calculus precisely, sketch the semantic-tree and model-construction arguments behind their soundness and refutational completeness, and chart the complexity landscape: NP-completeness of SAT, undecidability of first-order validity, and exponential proof-complexity lower bounds for resolution on the pigeonhole principle. We then explain the engineering that turned exponential worst cases into industrial practice — watched literals, VSIDS branching, first-UIP learning, restarts, and theory solvers — and review Nelson–Oppen combination and E-matching as the bridges to program verification. We close with standing limitations: ordering fragility in superposition, quantifier reasoning in SMT, and the gap between lower bounds and practical performance.

## 1 Intro

The dream of *mechanized reasoning* — a machine that decides whether a claim follows from given premises — is among the oldest in computing, traceable to Leibniz and formalized by Frege, Hilbert, and Gödel. Automated theorem proving (ATP) is the engineering discipline that made a working approximation of this dream real. Rather than searching for proofs in the natural-deduction style of human mathematicians, modern ATP systems overwhelmingly adopt a **refutational** stance: to prove that a set of axioms Γ entails a conjecture φ, they show that Γ together with the negation of φ is *unsatisfiable*, deriving a contradiction from the combined clause set [1].

This single architectural decision — refutation over direct proof, clauses over arbitrary formulas — underlies four calculi that dominate the field:

1. **First-order resolution** (Robinson, 1965): a single inference rule, lifted by unification, that is refutationally complete for full first-order logic [1].
2. **DPLL and CDCL** (Davis–Putnam–Logemann–Loveland, 1962; Marques-Silva & Sakallah, 1996; Moskewicz et al., 2001): backtracking search with clause learning that made propositional satisfiability practically decidable at industrial scale [2][3][4].
3. **SMT / DPLL(T)** (Nelson–Oppen, 1979; de Moura & Bjørner, 2008): the combination of a SAT core with specialized decision procedures for background theories such as arithmetic, arrays, and bit-vectors [6][7].
4. **Superposition** (Bachmair & Ganzinger, 1994): ordered resolution with built-in equality reasoning, the calculus behind the E, Vampire, and SPASS provers [8].

This thesis defines each calculus, proves or sketches its soundness and completeness, analyzes its complexity, surveys the empirical engineering that made it scale, and confronts its limitations.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Clausal form and Herbrand semantics

Every first-order sentence can be converted to **conjunctive normal form (CNF)** — a conjunction of *clauses*, each a disjunction of *literals* (atoms or negated atoms) — through a satisfiability-preserving pipeline: eliminate equivalences and implications, push negations inward to negation normal form, standardize variables apart, Skolemize existentials, drop universals, and distribute disjunctions over conjunctions [1]. Skolemization replaces each existentially quantified variable by a fresh function of the enclosing universal variables; it preserves satisfiability but not logical equivalence, which is exactly what a refutational calculus needs.

The bridge from first-order to propositional reasoning is **Herbrand's theorem**: a set of clauses S is unsatisfiable if and only if some finite set of *ground instances* of clauses from S (instances over the Herbrand universe of all ground terms) is propositionally unsatisfiable [1]. Every completeness proof in this thesis is, at bottom, a fight to lift a ground-level argument through this theorem.

### 2.2 Unification and the lifting lemma

First-order inference must instantiate variables. **Unification** computes, for two terms s and t, a substitution σ with sσ = tσ; Robinson's algorithm returns the *most general unifier* (mgu) when one exists [1]. The **lifting lemma** states that every ground resolution step between ground instances of clauses C and D is an instance of a single resolution step between C and D themselves using the mgu. Completeness for the first-order calculus therefore follows from completeness of the ground calculus plus lifting [8].

### 2.3 What "complete" means

A calculus is *sound* if every derived clause is a logical consequence of its premises, and *refutationally complete* if every unsatisfiable clause set admits a derivation of the empty clause □. Full first-order validity is undecidable (Church, Turing, 1936), so refutational completeness is the strongest guarantee available: on unsatisfiable inputs the calculus *must* terminate with a refutation, while on satisfiable inputs it may run forever.

## 3 Methodology

We present the four calculi as inference systems over clause sets, each characterized by its generating inferences, its redundancy notion, and its search strategy.

### 3.1 Resolution

Binary resolution and factoring suffice. Let C ∨ A and D ∨ ¬B be clauses (renamed apart) with mgu σ of atoms A and B. The **binary resolvent** is (C ∨ D)σ [1]. **Factoring** merges unifiable literals within one clause: from C ∨ A ∨ B with mgu σ of A and B, derive (C ∨ A)σ. A *resolution refutation* is a sequence of clauses ending in □.

### 3.2 DPLL

The Davis–Putnam–Logemann–Loveland procedure decides propositional CNF by recursive case splitting [2]. Davis and Putnam's original 1960 procedure eliminated variables by resolution — powerful but memory-explosive; Logemann and Loveland replaced elimination with the **splitting rule**, yielding the backtracking search used ever since [2]:

```python
def dpll(clauses, assignment):
    clauses = unit_propagate(clauses, assignment)
    if clauses is None:
        return None            # conflict: empty clause derived
    if not clauses:
        return assignment      # all clauses satisfied
    var = pick_branching_variable(clauses)
    for value in (True, False):
        result = dpll(simplify(clauses, var, value),
                      extend(assignment, var, value))
        if result is not None:
            return result
    return None
```

**Unit propagation** repeatedly satisfies unit clauses; the **pure literal rule** assigns variables occurring with a single polarity. Both are sound simplifications that prune the search tree.

### 3.3 CDCL

Conflict-driven clause learning augments DPLL with three ideas pioneered by GRASP and Chaff [3][4]:

- *Conflict analysis*: when propagation hits a conflict, resolve the conflicting clause with the reasons of propagated literals along the **implication graph** until a single literal from the current decision level remains — the **first unique implication point (1-UIP)** — producing a learned clause.
- *Non-chronological backtracking*: jump back to the assertion level of the learned clause rather than the previous decision.
- *Restarts and forgetting*: periodically restart search (keeping learned clauses) and delete low-quality learned clauses to control memory.

Clause learning is resolution in disguise: every learned clause is derivable by resolution from the input, so CDCL proofs *are* resolution proofs [5].

### 3.4 DPLL(T) and SMT

Satisfiability modulo theories decides formulas over background theories T (linear arithmetic, arrays, bit-vectors, uninterpreted functions). The **lazy DPLL(T)** architecture couples a CDCL core with theory solvers: the Boolean abstraction of the formula is searched propositionally; each full or partial assignment is checked by theory solvers; theory-inconsistent assignments yield **theory lemmas** (conflict clauses over theory atoms) fed back to the SAT core, which learns them like ordinary clauses [6]. Theories are combined à la **Nelson–Oppen** for disjoint, stably infinite signatures, exchanging equalities over shared variables.

### 3.5 Superposition

Superposition extends ordered resolution with native equality handling [8]. Given a **reduction ordering** ≻ on terms (typically KBO or LPO, total on ground terms), inferences are restricted to **maximal** literals, and paramodulation into smaller sides is forbidden. The flagship rule:

> **Superposition (right):** from C ∨ s ≈ t and D ∨ L[u] (u a non-variable subterm of L), with mgu σ of s and u, where sσ ⋠ tσ and the rewritten literal is maximal, derive (C ∨ D ∨ L[t])σ.

**Selection** functions may additionally force resolution on designated negative literals. Redundancy — tautology deletion, subsumption, demodulation by unit equalities — is not an optimization but part of the completeness argument [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Unification, Factoring, and the Mechanics of First-Order Resolution

Unification is the engine room. Robinson's original algorithm repeatedly decomposes term pairs and applies bindings, failing on the **occurs check** when a variable would bind to a term containing itself [1]. Linear-time unification (Martelli–Montanari; Paterson–Wegman) replaced the naive quadratic algorithm in serious provers, but the conceptual content is unchanged: the mgu is the *least committed* instantiation making two atoms identical, and the lifting lemma guarantees no ground proof is lost by working at the general level.

Factoring looks like a minor addition but is load-bearing: without it, resolution is incomplete for first-order logic (though complete for propositional logic). Consider C = P(x) ∨ P(y); the ground instance P(a) ∨ P(a) collapses to P(a), and only factoring reproduces this collapse at the lifted level [1].

```haskell
-- Binary resolution with factoring, in the style of a kernel
resolve :: Clause -> Clause -> [Clause]
resolve c1 c2 =
  [ apply sigma (rest1 ++ rest2)
  | (lit1, rest1) <- pickLiteral c1
  , (lit2, rest2) <- pickLiteral c2
  , opposite lit1 lit2
  , sigma <- maybeToList (mgu (atomOf lit1) (atomOf lit2)) ]
```

### 4.2 Ordered Resolution, Selection, and the Redundancy Criterion

Unrestricted resolution drowns in its own inferences: every pair of clauses with complementary unifiable literals generates a resolvent, and the set explodes combinatorially. **Ordered resolution** tames this with an atom ordering: resolve only on literals that are maximal (or selected) in their clause [8]. On the ground level, the completeness proof constructs a model incrementally — the *candidate model* — from clauses in increasing order, showing that if □ is not derived, the constructed interpretation satisfies the clause set. The ordering restrictions emerge naturally from this construction: a clause can only be "productive" (contribute to the model) if its maximal literal is not already falsified, and inferences need only involve productive clauses.

**Redundancy** formalizes "inferences we may skip": a clause is redundant if it follows from strictly smaller clauses; an inference is redundant if its conclusion follows from smaller clauses or is already present [8]. Subsumption, tautology deletion, and simplification are instances. Crucially, a *fair* saturation process — one that eventually considers every non-redundant inference — is complete, so provers can aggressively delete without losing refutations.

### 4.3 CDCL: Implication Graphs, the First-UIP Scheme, and Clause Learning as Resolution

Modern CDCL is best understood through the **implication graph**: nodes are assigned literals annotated with decision levels; edges record propagation reasons (the clause that forced each implied literal); decision literals are sources [5]. When two complementary literals are both implied, the graph contains a conflict. Conflict analysis performs a sequence of resolution steps between the conflict clause and reason clauses, walking back along the graph; the **1-UIP scheme** stops at the first literal on the current level that dominates the conflict — the unique implication point closest to it. The resulting learned clause contains exactly one literal from the current decision level, so backjumping to the second-highest level in the clause makes it unit and forces progress [3].

Why does learning help exponentially? Plain DPLL corresponds to *tree-like* resolution, where each derived clause is used once; learned clauses are reused arbitrarily, giving CDCL the power of *DAG-like* resolution [5]. With restarts, CDCL polynomially simulates general resolution (Pipatsrisawat & Darwiche, 2011), collapsing a major proof-complexity gap. The remaining machinery — **VSIDS** branching heuristics that bump activities of variables in recent conflicts, **two-watched-literal** propagation that visits a clause only when a watched literal is falsified [4], Luby-sequence restarts, and Glucose-style clause-database reduction — is engineering that makes the asymptotic power reachable.

```tla
------------------------------- MODULE CDCL -------------------------------
VARIABLES trail, learned, level
Decide ==
  /\ \E v \in Vars \ trail : trail' = trail \cup {ChooseLiteral(v)}
  /\ level' = level + 1
Propagate ==
  /\ \E c \in Clauses \cup learned : IsUnit(c, trail)
  /\ trail' = trail \cup {ForcedLiteral(c, trail)}
Conflict ==
  /\ \E c \in Clauses \cup learned : IsFalsified(c, trail)
  /\ learned' = learned \cup {AnalyzeConflict(trail)}
=============================================================================
```

### 4.4 SMT: Theory Combination and the DPLL(T) Architecture

Real verification problems are not purely Boolean: they mix arithmetic constraints, array reads and writes, and bit-vector operations. SMT solves the **quantifier-free** fragment over combined theories. The Nelson–Oppen method purifies the formula so each atom belongs to one theory, then lets theory solvers exchange *equalities between shared variables* until agreement or contradiction; completeness requires the theories to be stably infinite with disjoint signatures [6].

DPLL(T) made this lazy and incremental [6]. The SAT core treats each theory atom as a Boolean variable. Whenever the trail becomes theory-relevant, solvers are consulted *incrementally* (push/pop of literals); on conflict they return a small inconsistent subset as a theory lemma. **Theory propagation** goes further: solvers deduce entailed literals and inject them as if propagated. Z3's architecture exemplifies the mature form — a CDCL core, an E-graph-based congruence-closure solver for equality with uninterpreted functions, simplex-based arithmetic, and E-matching for quantified formulas [7]. The SMT-LIB standard and annual SMT-COMP competitions turned this into a measurable engineering discipline.

| Component | Role in DPLL(T) | Representative technique |
|---|---|---|
| SAT core | Boolean search, learning | CDCL with 1-UIP, VSIDS [4][5] |
| EUF solver | Equality, uninterpreted functions | Congruence closure, E-graphs [7] |
| Arithmetic solver | Linear (in)equalities | Simplex, branch-and-bound |
| Array solver | Select/store axioms | Lazy axiom instantiation |
| Combination | Multi-theory consistency | Nelson–Oppen equality exchange [6] |
| Quantifiers | Universal instantiation | E-matching with triggers [7] |

### 4.5 Superposition: Term Orderings and Equational Inference

Equality is ubiquitous in mathematics and verification, yet naive handling — adding the equality axioms (reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, congruence) as clauses — is catastrophic: transitivity and congruence generate unbounded inferences. Superposition instead *builds in* equality via ordered paramodulation [8].

A **reduction ordering** ≻ is well-founded, monotonic, and stable under substitution; KBO (Knuth–Bendix) weights symbols and compares lexicographically on ties, while LPO (lexicographic path) uses a precedence with recursive subterm checks. Inferences apply only to **strictly maximal** literals, and rewriting replaces larger terms by smaller ones, so every inference strictly decreases a well-founded measure on the ground level — the key to the model-construction completeness proof [8].

```rust
// Sketch of a KBO-style comparison used to gate inferences
fn kboprecedence(s: &Term, t: &Term, w: &Weights) -> Ordering {
    let (ws, wt) = (weight(s, w), weight(t, w));
    if ws != wt { return ws.cmp(&wt); }
    match (s, t) {
        (Term::App(f, a), Term::App(g, b)) if f == g =>
            lex_compare(a, b, w),
        (Term::App(f, _), Term::App(g, _)) =>
            precedence(f).cmp(&precedence(g)),
        _ => Ordering::Equal,
    }
}
```

Superposition with selection and simplification is refutationally complete for first-order logic with equality; it degrades gracefully to ordered resolution on non-equational problems [8]. Its practical dominance shows in the CASC competitions, where Vampire and E perennially lead. **Hierarchic superposition** extends the calculus with background theories such as linear arithmetic, abstracting background-sorted subterms — an active frontier with new completeness results for arithmetic fragments [9].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Soundness and completeness, precisely stated

> **Theorem (Robinson, 1965):** The resolution calculus is *sound* — every resolvent is a logical consequence of its parents — and *refutationally complete*: if a clause set S is unsatisfiable, there exists a resolution derivation of □ from S [1].

The proof factors through the ground case: semantic trees show that ground resolution derives □ from any unsatisfiable finite ground set, Herbrand's theorem supplies the finite unsatisfiable ground instance set, and the lifting lemma promotes the ground refutation to the first-order level [1][8].

> **Theorem (Bachmair & Ganzinger, 1994):** Superposition with a reduction ordering, literal selection, and the standard redundancy criterion is refutationally complete for equational first-order logic [8].

Here the proof is a model construction: assuming a saturated set without □, clauses are processed in increasing order, and *productive* clauses extend a convergent rewrite system whose normal forms define a model — a Herbrand interpretation over equivalence classes of terms.

> **Theorem (Pipatsrisawat & Darwiche, 2011):** CDCL SAT solvers with restarts polynomially simulate general resolution [5].

Combined with Haken's lower bound, this pins CDCL's proof-theoretic strength exactly at resolution:

> **Theorem (Haken, 1985):** Every resolution refutation of the pigeonhole principle PHP with n+1 pigeons and n holes has size exponential in n.

### 5.2 Complexity landscape

| Problem | Complexity | Consequence for ATP |
|---|---|---|
| Propositional SAT | NP-complete (Cook–Levin) | No polynomial algorithm expected; CDCL exploits structure |
| First-order validity | Undecidable (Church–Turing) | Refutational completeness is the ceiling |
| Equational FOL validity | Undecidable in general | Superposition may diverge on satisfiable inputs |
| QF SMT (decidable theories) | Decidable; NP-hard fragments | Nelson–Oppen + DPLL(T) scales in practice [6] |
| Resolution proof size (PHP) | Exponential lower bound | Some unsatisfiable formulas defeat all resolution-based solvers |

### 5.3 Empirical performance

Theory aside, the empirical record is striking. Pre-CDCL DPLL solvers stalled at hundreds of variables; Chaff-era solvers handled *millions* of clauses on bounded-model-checking instances [4]. The two-watched-literal scheme made propagation effectively constant-time per clause visit; VSIDS focused search on recently conflicted variables; restarts diversified the search without discarding learned knowledge [4][5]. The SAT competitions (running since 2002) document a sustained exponential improvement in solved instances, and proof-producing solvers now emit DRAT certificates checkable by independent verifiers [4]. In SMT, Z3 and CVC5 discharge verification conditions with hundreds of thousands of theory atoms in seconds, underpinning tools from Dafny to bounded model checkers [7]. Superposition provers routinely solve CASC problems with deep equational reasoning that no SAT-based method touches [8].

## 6 Limitations

The calculi are powerful but brittle in well-understood ways.

- **Undecidability is inescapable.** Refutational completeness guarantees a refutation *exists* for unsatisfiable inputs, not that saturation terminates on satisfiable ones. Superposition provers diverge on hard satisfiable problems, and no ordering or selection strategy fixes this in general.
- **Orderings are fragile.** Superposition's performance depends critically on the term ordering and symbol precedence; a poor KBO weight assignment can turn a seconds-long proof into a timeout, and precedence selection remains more art than science [8].
- **Resolution lower bounds bite.** Exponential lower bounds for resolution (pigeonhole, parity, clique-coloring principles) transfer to CDCL; crafted benchmarks still defeat every solver [5].
- **SMT quantifiers are heuristic.** E-matching with triggers is incomplete in general and trigger selection dramatically affects performance; quantified SMT remains the least robust component of Z3-class solvers [7].
- **Theory combination costs.** Nelson–Oppen's equality exchange can explode on many shared variables, and non-convex theories force expensive case splits [6].
- **Proofs versus models.** Refutational methods certify unsatisfiability elegantly but produce models awkwardly; applications needing *witnesses* (synthesis, counterexample generation) require additional machinery.

---

## 7 Conclusion

From Robinson's single rule of inference to the layered architectures of Z3 and Vampire, automated theorem proving has converged on a small set of deep ideas: refutation instead of direct proof, clauses as the lingua franca, unification as instantiation, orderings as search control, learning as proof reuse, and theory solvers as oracles. Each calculus surveyed here — resolution, DPLL/CDCL, DPLL(T), superposition — is sound, refutationally complete for its logic, and bounded by complexity results that are sharp: NP-completeness below, undecidability above, exponential proof-size lower bounds in between. The practical miracle is that these worst cases rarely describe industrial instances, and fifty years of engineering — watched literals, VSIDS, 1-UIP learning, restarts, congruence closure, E-matching — converted a theoretical curiosity into infrastructure that verifies hardware, software, and mathematics daily.
