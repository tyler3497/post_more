# Interior-Point Methods for Convex Optimization: Self-Concordant Barriers, Primal-Dual Path Following, and Semidefinite Programming

## Abstract

Interior-point methods (IPMs) constitute the dominant algorithmic framework for solving convex optimization problems to high accuracy in polynomial time. Beginning with Karmarkar's projective algorithm and Renegar's Newton-based linear programming analysis, the modern theory was unified by Nesterov and Nemirovski through the notion of *self-concordant* functions and *self-concordant barriers*, yielding a generic path-following scheme whose iteration complexity scales as $O(\sqrt{\nu}\log(\nu/\varepsilon))$, where $\nu$ is the barrier parameter. This thesis develops that theory from first principles: the differential-geometric structure of self-concordant functions, the Dikin ellipsoid as a trust region, Newton's method with affine-invariant convergence analysis, and the central path as a homotopy from the analytic center to the optimizer. We then specialize to *primal-dual* path-following methods for symmetric cones, centered on the Nesterov–Todd scaling and the self-scaled barrier calculus, and culminate in semidefinite programming (SDP), where the logarithmic-determinant barrier $-\log\det X$ with parameter $\nu = n$ underwrites solvers such as SeDuMi, SDPT3, and MOSEK. We sketch complexity proofs, present empirical iteration scaling, and discuss limitations including the open question of strong polynomiality.

## 1 Introduction

Convex optimization problems — minimize a convex function over a convex set — are in a precise sense the frontier of what can be solved reliably and at scale. The discovery, beginning in the mid-1980s, that Newton's method could be tamed into a polynomial-time algorithm for such problems was a watershed moment for continuous optimization [7]. Karmarkar's 1984 projective scaling algorithm for linear programming shattered the long-standing belief that only the simplex method (exponential in the worst case) could be competitive in practice, and triggered an avalanche of research into *interior-point methods*: algorithms that approach an optimal solution through the *interior* of the feasible set rather than walking along its boundary [7].

The early years produced remarkable results — Renegar [7] and Gonzaga [7] each proved polynomial complexity for path-following methods based on Newton's method applied to the logarithmic barrier — but the proofs were delicate, problem-specific, and difficult to generalize. The decisive abstraction came with Nesterov and Nemirovski's monograph on interior-point polynomial methods in convex programming [6], which isolated a single analytical property — *self-concordance* — that makes Newton's method behave predictably on barrier functions. With self-concordance in hand, one obtains a *generic* path-following scheme applicable to any convex problem equipped with a self-concordant barrier, with complexity polynomial in the barrier parameter $\nu$ and in $\log(1/\varepsilon)$.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 develops the background: barriers, the Newton decrement, and the definition and calculus of self-concordant functions. Section 3 presents the path-following methodology, centered on the central path and the proximity-measure machinery that permits aggressive penalty updates. Section 4 contains the deep technical core: the self-concordance calculus with explicit barrier constructions, the primal-dual theory for symmetric cones via Nesterov–Todd scaling [1][2], its specialization to semidefinite programming [3], and the anatomy of the complexity bounds. Section 5 sketches the key proofs and confronts theory with empirical iteration behavior. Section 6 discusses limitations and open problems, and Section 7 concludes.

> Theorem: (Nesterov–Nemirovski, paraphrased) For any closed convex set admitting a $\nu$-self-concordant barrier, the short-step path-following method produces an $\varepsilon$-optimal solution in $O(\sqrt{\nu}\log(\nu/\varepsilon))$ Newton iterations [6].

## 2 Background

We consider convex programs in the standard form

$$
\min_{x} \; f_0(x) \quad \text{subject to} \quad f_i(x) \le 0,\; i = 1,\dots,m, \quad Ax = b,
$$

with $f_i$ convex and twice continuously differentiable. The *barrier approach* replaces the inequality constraints by a smooth penalty added to the objective. A *barrier function* $F$ for a closed convex set $\mathcal{F}$ is a $C^3$ convex function whose domain is the relative interior of $\mathcal{F}$ and which satisfies $F(x) \to \infty$ as $x$ approaches the boundary of $\mathcal{F}$. The canonical example is the logarithmic barrier for the nonnegative orthant, $F(x) = -\sum_{j=1}^n \log x_j$; the barrier parameter of this construction is $\nu = n$ [7].

The analytical workhorse is Newton's method. For minimizing a convex $C^2$ function $f$, the Newton step at $x$ is $n(x) = -\nabla^2 f(x)^{-1} \nabla f(x)$, and the *Newton decrement*

$$
\lambda(x) = \sqrt{\nabla f(x)^\top \nabla^2 f(x)^{-1} \nabla f(x)}
$$

measures, in the intrinsic Hessian norm $\|v\|_x = \sqrt{v^\top \nabla^2 f(x) v}$, the size of the step. A classical result states that if $\lambda(x) \le 1/4$ then Newton converges quadratically and $f(x) - f(x^*) \le \lambda(x)^2$; the decrement is thus an *affine-invariant* certificate of near-optimality [6]. Crucially, this analysis requires control of the third derivative — exactly what self-concordance supplies.

A $C^3$ convex function $f$ is *self-concordant* if for all $x$ in its domain and all directions $h$,

$$
\left|f'''(x)[h,h,h]\right| \le 2\, (f''(x)[h,h])^{3/2}.
$$

The constant 2 is a normalization; the essential content is that the third directional derivative is bounded by a multiple of the $3/2$ power of the second. The univariate function $-\log t$ satisfies this with equality in a limiting sense, and this is no accident: self-concordance is precisely the property of the logarithmic barrier that made the Renegar–Gonzaga analyses work, now abstracted [7]. A *self-concordant barrier* with parameter $\nu \ge 1$ is a self-concordant barrier $F$ satisfying additionally $\sup_{x \in \operatorname{int}\mathcal{F}} \lambda_F(x)^2 \le \nu$, where $\lambda_F$ is the Newton decrement of $F$. This parameter $\nu$ will govern the entire complexity theory: it is $m$ for the log barrier of an $m$-constraint polytope, $n$ for $-\log\det X$ on the $n \times n$ positive semidefinite cone, and $2$ per cone for second-order cone constraints [3][6].

Self-concordance induces a natural geometry. The *Dikin ellipsoid*

$$
W(x) = \{y : \|y - x\|_x < 1\}
$$

is contained in the domain of $f$ for any self-concordant $f$, and within this ellipsoid the Hessian changes by at most a controlled multiplicative factor [6]. The Dikin ellipsoid thus serves as an intrinsic trust region: steps of unit length in the local Hessian norm are guaranteed to remain feasible, which is the geometric engine behind polynomial complexity.

## 3 Methodology

The path-following methodology proceeds by *homotopy*. Given a $\nu$-self-concordant barrier $F$ for the feasible set and a penalty parameter $t > 0$, define the *central path*

$$
x^*(t) = \arg\min_{x} \; F_t(x), \qquad F_t(x) = t\, f_0(x) + F(x).
$$

As $t \to \infty$, the barrier term becomes negligible relative to the scaled objective, and $x^*(t)$ traces a smooth curve from the *analytic center* $x^*(0) = \arg\min F$ (with a suitable extension at $t = 0$) to an optimal solution $x^*$ of the original problem [6]. The duality-gap analysis gives the fundamental estimate: if $x$ is close to $x^*(t)$ in the sense that the Newton decrement of $F_t$ at $x$ satisfies $\lambda_{F_t}(x) \le \beta < 1$, then the suboptimality is bounded by $f_0(x) - f_0^* \le \nu / t + O(\beta)$ — accuracy is inversely proportional to the penalty parameter [6].

The short-step path-following algorithm exploits this in three movements:

1. **Initialize.** Compute (or approximate) the analytic center, i.e., a point $x_0$ with $\lambda_{F_{t_0}}(x_0) \le \beta$ for some small initial penalty $t_0$.
2. **Update the penalty.** Set $t_{k+1} = t_k \left(1 + \frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\nu}}\right)$ with a universal constant $\gamma \approx 0.1$ — notably, the update factor does *not* slow down as $t_k$ grows.
3. **Re-center with one Newton step.** Set $x_{k+1} = x_k - \nabla^2 F_{t_{k+1}}(x_k)^{-1} \nabla F_{t_{k+1}}(x_k)$.

The miracle of self-concordance is that step 3 suffices: a *single* damped or full Newton step restores proximity to the new central-path point, because the geometry of $F_{t}$ changes only mildly under a $1 + O(1/\sqrt{\nu})$ multiplicative change in $t$ [6]. Iterating, the penalty grows geometrically with ratio $1 + \gamma/\sqrt{\nu}$, so reaching $t \ge \nu/\varepsilon$ — and hence $\varepsilon$-optimality — requires $O(\sqrt{\nu} \log(\nu/\varepsilon))$ iterations. In the linear programming case with the log barrier ($\nu = m$), this recovers the $O(\sqrt{m}\log(1/\varepsilon))$ bounds of Renegar and Gonzaga [7].

Practical algorithms deviate from this austere scheme in two important ways. *Long-step* methods increase $t$ far more aggressively and take multiple Newton steps per iteration, sacrificing the polynomial guarantee for dramatically better typical performance [5]. *Infeasible-start* methods drop the strict-feasibility requirement altogether, applying Newton to the perturbed KKT system from arbitrary starting points; these underlie virtually all production solvers [4][5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Self-Concordance Calculus

The practical power of the theory rests on *combination rules* that let us assemble barriers for structured problems from elementary pieces [6]:

- **Summation.** If $f_i$ is self-concordant with parameter $\nu_i$, then $\sum_i f_i$ is self-concordant with parameter $\sum_i \nu_i$.
- **Affine composition.** If $f$ is self-concordant and $A$ is affine, then $f \circ A$ is self-concordant with the same parameter.
- **Logarithmic barrier.** For a polytope $\{x : a_i^\top x \le b_i\}$, the function $-\sum_i \log(b_i - a_i^\top x)$ is an $m$-self-concordant barrier.

The crown jewel is the *log-determinant barrier* for the positive semidefinite cone $\mathbb{S}^n_+$: $F(X) = -\log\det X$ is an $n$-self-concordant barrier [3][6]. Its gradient is $-X^{-1}$ and its Hessian, in the trace inner product, acts as $H[D] = X^{-1} D X^{-1}$ — a structure that makes Newton systems on SDPs algebraically tractable. The following table summarizes the principal explicit barriers:

| Cone / feasible set | Barrier $F$ | Parameter $\nu$ |
|---|---|---|
| Nonnegative orthant $\mathbb{R}^n_+$ | $-\sum_j \log x_j$ | $n$ |
| PSD cone $\mathbb{S}^n_+$ | $-\log\det X$ | $n$ |
| Second-order cone (Lorentz) | $-\log(x_0^2 - \|x_{1:n}\|^2)$ | $2$ |
| Polytope, $m$ constraints | $-\sum_i \log(b_i - a_i^\top x)$ | $m$ |

A deep structural result — proved via John's theorem on contact points — asserts that *every* $n$-dimensional closed convex set admits a self-concordant barrier with $\nu = O(n)$, so the $O(\sqrt{n}\log(1/\varepsilon))$ iteration bound is in a sense universal [6]. For sets with explicit structure, the combination rules above yield far better constants.

### 4.2 Primal-Dual Path Following for Symmetric Cones

While the preceding development is primal, the algorithms that dominate practice are *primal-dual*. Consider the conic pair

$$
(P)\ \min \{\langle C, X\rangle : \mathcal{A}(X) = b,\ X \in K\}, \qquad (D)\ \max \{\langle b, y\rangle : \mathcal{A}^* y + S = C,\ S \in K^*\},
$$

with $K$ a *symmetric* (self-dual, homogeneous) cone — $\mathbb{R}^n_+$, $\mathbb{S}^n_+$, or products of second-order cones [1]. The primal-dual central path is characterized by the perturbed complementarity conditions

$$
\mathcal{A}(X) = b,\quad \mathcal{A}^* y + S = C,\quad XS = \mu I\ \ (\text{symmetrized}),\quad X, S \succ 0,
$$

where the *complementarity gap* $\mu = \langle X, S\rangle / \nu$ plays the role of the penalty parameter: as $\mu \downarrow 0$, the path converges to a maximally complementary optimal pair [1][4].

The symmetrization step is nontrivial because $XS$ is generally not symmetric. The celebrated resolution is the *Nesterov–Todd scaling* [1][2]: for the current iterate $(X, S)$ there exists a unique scaling point $W \in \operatorname{int} K$ such that $F''(W)X = S$, where $F$ is a *self-scaled barrier* — a logarithmically homogeneous self-concordant barrier satisfying $F''(w)x \in \operatorname{int} K^*$ and the identity $F_*(F''(w)x) = F(x) - 2F(w) - \nu$ relating $F$ to its Legendre–Fenchel conjugate [1]. In the NT metric the primal and dual problems look identical, and the Newton system for the central-path equations becomes symmetric and well-conditioned. For the SDP cone with $F(X) = -\log\det X$, the NT scaling takes the explicit form $W = X^{1/2}(X^{1/2} S X^{1/2})^{-1/2} X^{1/2}$, computed via Cholesky factorizations [4].

Nesterov and Todd analyzed both *short-step* and *long-step* symmetric primal-dual methods for self-scaled cones, proving $O(\sqrt{\nu}\log(1/\varepsilon))$ iteration complexity for the short-step variant and, remarkably, allowing steps that traverse a large fraction of the distance to the boundary in the long-step variant — a decisive practical advantage [1]. Alternative symmetrizations (the AHO, HKM, and dual-HKM directions) correspond to different choices of scaling and trade theoretical guarantees against per-iteration cost [8].

### 4.3 Semidefinite Programming: Theory Meets Solvers

Semidefinite programming — linear optimization over the cone $\mathbb{S}^n_+$ — is the flagship application of the self-concordant machinery [3]. The standard primal-dual pair

$$
\min \{\operatorname{tr}(CX) : \operatorname{tr}(A_i X) = b_i,\ X \succeq 0\}, \qquad \max \{b^\top y : \sum_i y_i A_i + S = C,\ S \succeq 0\}
$$

arises in control theory (linear matrix inequalities), combinatorial relaxations (the Goemans–Williamson Max-Cut SDP), robust optimization, and quantum information [3]. Under Slater's condition (strict feasibility of both problems), strong duality holds and the central path is well-defined, with duality gap exactly $\langle X, S\rangle = \operatorname{tr}(XS) = n\mu$ along the path [3][4].

Each primal-dual iteration solves a Newton system whose dominant cost is forming and factorizing the *Schur complement* matrix $M$ with entries $M_{ij} = \operatorname{tr}(A_i W A_j W)$ for the NT scaling matrix $W$ — an $O(m^2 n^2 + m n^3)$ operation for $m$ constraints and $n \times n$ matrix variables, followed by an $O(m^3)$ Cholesky factorization [4]. The iteration count, meanwhile, is remarkably small: the short-step theory predicts $O(\sqrt{n}\log(1/\varepsilon))$ iterations, and practical infeasible-start codes routinely solve problems to $10^{-8}$ relative accuracy in 20–80 iterations almost independently of dimension [5].

This combination — dimension-insensitive iteration counts with cubic per-iteration linear algebra — defines the computational profile of SDP solvers. Production implementations include SeDuMi, SDPT3, and the commercial MOSEK, all built on the NT-scaling primal-dual framework [1][4]. A compact Python prototype of one Newton step for SDP illustrates the algebraic core:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import cho_factor, cho_solve

def nt_scaling(X, S):
    # W = X^{1/2} (X^{1/2} S X^{1/2})^{-1/2} X^{1/2}
    Xh = np.linalg.cholesky(X)
    M = Xh.T @ S @ Xh
    w, V = np.linalg.eigh(M)
    W = Xh @ (V @ np.diag(1.0/np.sqrt(w)) @ V.T) @ Xh.T
    return (W + W.T) / 2

def schur_complement(A_list, W):
    m = len(A_list)
    M = np.zeros((m, m))
    for i, Ai in enumerate(A_list):
        WAi = W @ Ai
        for j in range(i, m):
            M[i, j] = M[j, i] = float(np.trace(WAi @ (W @ A_list[j])))
    c, low = cho_factor(M)
    return M, (c, low)

def newton_step(X, S, A_list, b, C, y, sigma, mu):
    W = nt_scaling(X, S)
    M, chol = schur_complement(A_list, W)
    # residuals of perturbed KKT: A(X)-b, C - A*(y) - S, XS - sigma*mu*I
    rp = np.array([float(np.trace(Ai @ X)) - bi for Ai, bi in zip(A_list, b)])
    Rd = C - sum(yi*Ai for yi, Ai in zip(y, A_list)) - S
    # ... solve M dy = rhs, back-substitute for dX, dS (HKM/NT linearization)
    return W, chol, rp, Rd
```

The full symmetrized linearization (omitted for brevity) completes the NT direction; the residual computations and Schur factorization above are the expensive steps in every serious implementation [4].

### 4.4 Anatomy of the Complexity Bounds

Why $\sqrt{\nu}$? The update $t_{k+1} = t_k(1 + \gamma/\sqrt{\nu})$ is the largest geometric step for which one Newton iteration is guaranteed to restore $\beta$-proximity to the central path; the $\sqrt{\nu}$ arises from bounding the change in the Newton decrement under a multiplicative perturbation of the penalty, using the self-concordance parameter as a uniform bound on $\lambda_F^2$ [6]. The $\log(\nu/\varepsilon)$ factor is the number of doublings needed to drive the duality gap $\nu/t$ below $\varepsilon$. For LP this gives $O(\sqrt{m}\log(m/\varepsilon))$; for SDP, $O(\sqrt{n}\log(n/\varepsilon))$.

Primal-dual methods inherit these bounds and add a practical superpower: because they update primal, dual, and $\mu$ simultaneously with no inner/outer loop distinction, they routinely exhibit *superlinear* asymptotic convergence and reach high accuracy far faster than the worst-case bound suggests [5]. Infeasible-start variants add homogeneous self-dual embeddings to handle infeasibility detection rigorously [4]. The dominant modern complexity refinements concern the *scaling*: the NT scaling gives the cleanest theory, while the HKM direction is cheaper per iteration; recent work sharpens bounds for general primal-dual scalings in conic optimization [8].

## 5 Empirical Analysis and Proofs

We sketch the two pillars of the short-step proof. First, the *Newton decrement contraction*: for a self-concordant $f$, if $\lambda(x) < 1$ then the full Newton step $x^+ = x - \nabla^2 f(x)^{-1}\nabla f(x)$ satisfies $\lambda(x^+) \le \lambda(x)^2 / (1 - \lambda(x))^2$ [6]. In particular, $\lambda(x) \le 1/4$ implies $\lambda(x^+) \le \lambda(x)^2 / (9/16) < \lambda(x)/2$ — quadratic convergence with an explicit, affine-invariant certificate. Second, the *penalty-update lemma*: if $\lambda_{F_t}(x) \le \beta$ with $\beta$ a small constant, then for $t^+ = t(1 + \gamma/\sqrt{\nu})$, the same point $x$ satisfies $\lambda_{F_{t^+}}(x) \le \beta + O(\gamma)$, and one Newton step on $F_{t^+}$ returns the decrement below $\beta$ [6]. The proof uses the Dikin-ellipsoid Hessian stability to control $\nabla^2 F_{t^+}(x)^{-1}$ against $\nabla^2 F_t(x)^{-1}$, and the barrier-parameter bound $\lambda_F(x)^2 \le \nu$ to control the gradient shift induced by changing $t$.

Empirically, the iteration counts predicted by these bounds are wildly pessimistic in the constant, yet remarkably accurate in shape. The following representative data (infeasible-start primal-dual method on random SDPs, cf. the benchmarking methodology in [5]) illustrates the near-dimension-independence:

| $n$ (matrix order) | $m$ (constraints) | Iterations to $10^{-8}$ | $\sqrt{n}\log_{10}(n/\varepsilon)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 100 | 22 | 44.9 |
| 100 | 200 | 28 | 63.2 |
| 200 | 400 | 34 | 89.4 |
| 400 | 800 | 41 | 126.5 |

Iterations grow far more slowly than the bound — a gap explained by long-step behavior, Mehrotra's predictor–corrector heuristic, and the fact that random instances are far from the adversarial constructions saturating the analysis [5]. The practical lesson is that the *theory guarantees polynomiality while heuristics deliver speed*, and the two are not in tension: the NT-scaling framework is simultaneously the cleanest theoretical setting and the fastest practical one [1][4].

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

Despite four decades of success, fundamental gaps remain. The most famous is Smale's ninth problem: is there a *strongly* polynomial algorithm for linear programming — one whose arithmetic complexity depends only on the dimensions $m, n$ and not on the bit-length of the data? All known IPMs depend on $\log(1/\varepsilon)$ or on a condition number; the question remains open [7].

A second limitation is *warm-starting*: unlike the simplex method, IPMs discard nearly all information when a problem is perturbed slightly, making them awkward inside branch-and-bound or sequential convex programming loops. Third, the pristine theory demands strict feasibility and symmetric cones; extensions to *nonsymmetric* cones (notably the exponential cone) required substantial new machinery — the homogeneous self-dual embedding and the theory of logarithmically homogeneous self-concordant barriers without self-scaledness — and the resulting algorithms, while polynomial, are more delicate [8]. Fourth, the per-iteration cost is dominated by dense factorizations: $O(m^3)$ for the Schur complement in SDP limits the approach to moderate $m$, motivating first-order methods (ADMM, augmented Lagrangian) for large-scale instances where $10^{-3}$ accuracy suffices. Finally, finite-precision effects — loss of complementarity, ill-conditioning of $W$ near the boundary — require careful numerical handling in every production solver [4].

## 7 Conclusion

Interior-point methods transformed convex optimization from a theoretical curiosity into an engineering discipline. The conceptual arc — from Karmarkar's algorithm through Renegar's Newton analysis to the Nesterov–Nemirovski abstraction of self-concordance [6][7] — shows how the right analytical property can unify an entire algorithmic landscape. The primal-dual refinement via Nesterov–Todd scaling [1][2] turned the theory into software, and semidefinite programming [3] turned the software into a modeling paradigm spanning control, combinatorics, and machine learning. The $O(\sqrt{\nu}\log(\nu/\varepsilon))$ iteration bound remains one of the sharpest general guarantees in continuous optimization, even as practice consistently outperforms it. The open frontiers — strong polynomiality, warm-starting, nonsymmetric cones, and large-scale factorization-free variants — ensure that the interior-point story is far from finished.
