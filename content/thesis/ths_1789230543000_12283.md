# Sound Approximation by Construction: Abstract Interpretation, Galois Connections, Widening and Narrowing, and the Numerical Domain Hierarchy

## Abstract

Abstract interpretation is the theory of *sound approximation* of program semantics, founded by Patrick and Radhia Cousot beginning with interval analysis (1976) and unified in their 1977 lattice model of fixpoint approximation. This thesis develops the mathematical core: concrete and abstract semantics as complete lattices, abstraction and concretization organized into Galois connections, and abstract transfer functions designed by calculus from the collecting semantics. We study the two extrapolation operators that make infinite-state analysis computable — *widening*, which accelerates ascending Kleene iteration and guarantees termination on non-Noetherian domains, and its dual *narrowing*, which recovers precision without sacrificing soundness. We climb the hierarchy of numerical domains: the non-relational interval domain, Miné's weakly relational octagon domain based on difference bound matrices, and the fully relational polyhedral domain of Cousot and Halbwachs. Each is analyzed along the cost–precision tradeoff, from linear-time intervals to cubic-time octagons to exponential polyhedra. We close with the empirical validation in the Astrée analyzer, which proved the absence of runtime errors in hundreds of thousands of lines of Airbus avionics code with no false alarms [9][10].

---

## 1 Introduction

Every non-trivial question about program behavior is undecidable in general — Rice's theorem assures us that no algorithm can exactly decide any non-trivial semantic property of all programs. The engineering response cannot therefore be exactness; it must be *sound approximation*: computing a description that contains the truth, possibly with spurious elements, and never omits a genuine behavior. Static analysis lives entirely within this contract. A static analyzer that claims "no division by zero can occur" is trusted precisely to the extent that its over-approximation is guaranteed to cover all concrete executions.

Before 1977, static analyses were designed empirically: an analysis was invented, implemented, and its soundness argued by hand, often with subtle errors. The Cousots' contribution was to replace invention with *construction*: starting from a formal concrete semantics (typically the *collecting semantics*, the set of all reachable states), successive abstractions — each formalized as a Galois connection between lattices — derive the abstract semantics, the abstract transfer functions, and even the fixpoint iteration strategy, each step provably sound by construction [1][2]. Verification then reduces to computing a post-fixpoint of an abstract semantic function.

This thesis reconstructs that program in full technical detail. Section 2 recalls the lattice-theoretic background: complete lattices, the powerset lattice of program states, and the collecting semantics as the least fixpoint of a concrete semantic functional. Section 3 develops the methodology: Galois connections and Galois insertions, best correct approximations, and the calculational design of abstract transfer functions. Section 4 is the deep dive, with five subsections covering Galois connections in the wild, widening and narrowing operators (including threshold widening and the A2I meta-interpretation approach), and the three great numerical domains: intervals, octagons, and polyhedra. Section 5 gives the soundness proof skeleton and the empirical case of Astrée. Section 6 examines fundamental limitations: imprecision from non-distributivity, the absence of a best widening, and scalability walls. Section 7 concludes.

> **Principle:** *Soundness is a property of the abstraction, not of the implementation. Abstract interpretation separates the question "is the approximation correct?" (answered mathematically via Galois connections) from "is the implementation efficient?" (answered algorithmically via widening and domain design).*

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Concrete semantics and the collecting semantics

Let a program \(P\) operate over a set of states \(\Sigma\) (each state mapping program variables to values, plus a program counter). The *maximal trace semantics* \(S^\infty \llbracket P \rrbracket\) is the set of all finite and infinite execution traces. From it, the *collecting semantics* is derived by projection onto reachable states: the set \(Reach \subseteq \Sigma\) of states reachable from the initial states \(I \subseteq \Sigma\) by the transition relation \(\to\).

The collecting semantics is expressed as a fixpoint. Define the concrete semantic functional over the complete lattice \(\langle \mathcal{P}(\Sigma), \subseteq \rangle\):

$$
F(X) \;=\; I \;\cup\; \mathrm{post}(X), \qquad \mathrm{post}(X) = \{ s' \mid \exists s \in X : s \to s' \}.
$$

\(F\) is monotone, so by Knaster–Tarski it has a least fixpoint \(\mathrm{lfp}\, F = Reach\), the limit of the Kleene iteration \(X_0 = \emptyset\), \(X_{n+1} = F(X_n)\). This iteration is *not* computable in general — \(\mathcal{P}(\Sigma)\) is infinite and may never stabilize — so abstract interpretation replaces it by a computable lattice \(\langle A, \sqsubseteq \rangle\) and an abstract functional \(F^\sharp\) whose fixpoint over-approximates \(\mathrm{lfp}\, F\) [2][13].

### 2.2 Complete lattices and fixpoint theory

We work throughout with complete lattices: partially ordered sets in which every subset has a least upper bound (join, \(\sqcup\)) and greatest lower bound (meet, \(\sqcap\)), with bottom \(\bot\) and top \(\top\). The powerset lattice \(\langle \mathcal{P}(\Sigma), \subseteq, \emptyset, \Sigma, \cup, \cap \rangle\) is the canonical example. The abstract domains we study — intervals, octagons, polyhedra — are (mostly) complete lattices under set inclusion of their concretizations, though polyhedra with the inclusion order require care at \(\top\).

Two theorems govern fixpoint computation: **Knaster–Tarski** (every monotone \(F\) has a least fixpoint, the meet of all post-fixpoints) and **Kleene** (for \(\sqcup\)-continuous \(F\), \(\mathrm{lfp}\, F = \bigsqcup_n F^n(\bot)\)). The core move: any abstract post-fixpoint \(X^\sharp\) with \(F^\sharp(X^\sharp) \sqsubseteq X^\sharp\) is sound — it over-approximates \(\mathrm{lfp}\, F\) — provided \(F^\sharp\) soundly approximates \(F\). Soundness reduces to finding *some* post-fixpoint, not necessarily the least [4].

### 2.3 The abstraction/concretization pair

An abstraction is formalized by two monotone maps between the concrete lattice \(\langle C, \sqsubseteq_C \rangle\) and the abstract lattice \(\langle A, \sqsubseteq_A \rangle\):

- **Abstraction** \(\alpha : C \to A\) — maps a concrete property to its best abstract description.
- **Concretization** \(\gamma : A \to C\) — maps an abstract element to the set of concrete states it represents.

The pair \((\alpha, \gamma)\) forms a **Galois connection** when \(\alpha(c) \sqsubseteq_A a \iff c \sqsubseteq_C \gamma(a)\) for all \(c, a\).

## 3 Mathematical Framework: Calculational Design

### 3.1 The best correct approximation

Given a concrete monotone function \(f : C \to C\) and a Galois connection \((\alpha, \gamma)\), the **best correct approximation** of \(f\) on \(A\) is defined by the formula

$$
f^\sharp_{\text{best}} \;=\; \alpha \circ f \circ \gamma.
$$

This is the most precise abstract function that is sound: any sound abstract function \(f^\sharp\) (satisfying \(\alpha \circ f \sqsubseteq f^\sharp \circ \alpha\), or equivalently \(f \circ \gamma \sqsubseteq \gamma \circ f^\sharp\)) satisfies \(f^\sharp_{\text{best}} \sqsubseteq f^\sharp\) [4].

### 3.2 Composition of abstractions

Galois connections compose, which yields *hierarchies of abstractions*: maximal trace semantics → big-step semantics → collecting semantics → interval abstraction → sign abstraction, each step sound and each simpler, all organized in the *lattice of abstract interpretations* of a language.

### 3.3 The soundness contract

The full soundness theorem of abstract interpretation states: if

1. \((\alpha, \gamma)\) is a Galois connection between concrete and abstract complete lattices,
2. \(F^\sharp\) is a sound abstraction of \(F\) (i.e., \(\alpha \circ F \circ \gamma \sqsubseteq F^\sharp\)),
3. \(X^\sharp\) is an abstract post-fixpoint (\(F^\sharp(X^\sharp) \sqsubseteq X^\sharp\)),

then \(\mathrm{lfp}\, F \sqsubseteq \gamma(X^\sharp)\).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Galois Connections in Practice: From Sets to Signs to Intervals

The Galois connection \(\alpha(S) \sqsubseteq a \iff S \subseteq \gamma(a)\) is deceptively simple, yet it organizes all of abstract interpretation.

| Level | Abstract element | \(\gamma\) (concretization) | Precision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concrete | \(\{-3, 5\}\) | identity | exact |
| Intervals | \([-3, 5]\) | \(\{z \mid -3 \le z \le 5\}\) | loses nothing here |
| Signs | \(\top_\pm\) | \(\mathbb{Z}\) | loses bounds |
| Parity | \(\top\) | \(\mathbb{Z}\) | loses everything |

Each level is a Galois abstraction of the one below.

> **Theorem (Fixpoint transfer):** *If \((\alpha, \gamma)\) is a Galois connection, \(F\) is monotone on \(C\), and \(F^\sharp\) soundly abstracts \(F\), then \(\alpha(\mathrm{lfp}\, F) \sqsubseteq \mathrm{lfp}\, (\alpha \circ F \circ \gamma) \sqsubseteq X^\sharp\) for every abstract post-fixpoint \(X^\sharp\). Thus abstraction of the exact answer is bounded by the best abstract fixpoint, which is bounded by whatever post-fixpoint the analyzer actually computes.*

A subtle point: not all useful abstractions are Galois connections.

### 4.2 Widening and Narrowing: Termination Without Tears

Interval lattices have infinite ascending chains \([0,0] \sqsubset [0,1] \sqsubset \cdots\), so Kleene iteration may never stabilize.

The standard widening for intervals extrapolates unstable bounds to infinity:

$$
[a,b] \;\nabla\; [c,d] \;=\; \left[
\begin{array}{ll}
-\infty & \text{if } c < a \\
a & \text{otherwise}
\end{array},
\begin{array}{ll}
+\infty & \text{if } d > b \\
b & \text{otherwise}
\end{array}
\right].
$$

Applied to the ascending sequence \([0,0], [0,1], [0,2], \ldots\), widening jumps \([0,0] \nabla [0,1] = [0, +\infty]\) and the iteration stabilizes immediately.

**Narrowing** \(\Delta\) is the dual operator for *descending* refinement: after widening overshoots, iterate \(Y_0 = X^\sharp\) (the widened post-fixpoint), \(Y_{n+1} = Y_n \Delta F^\sharp(Y_n)\), finitely many steps, to claw back precision. For intervals:

$$
[a,b] \;\Delta\; [c,d] \;=\; \left[
\begin{array}{ll}
c & \text{if } a = -\infty \\
a & \text{otherwise}
\end{array},
\begin{array}{ll}
d & \text{if } b = +\infty \\
b & \text{otherwise}
\end{array}
\right].
$$

If the loop body actually establishes \(x \le 10\) (e.g., a loop condition \(x < 10\) guards the back-edge), narrowing refines \([0, +\infty] \Delta F^\sharp([0,+\infty])\) to \([0, 10]\) in one step — recovering the exact bound that naive widening destroyed [3].

```python
def kleene_with_widening(F_sharp, widen, narrow, bottom):
    """Abstract fixpoint iteration with widening then narrowing."""
    x = bottom
    # Ascending phase: widen until stabilization
    while True:
        fx = F_sharp(x)
        if fx <= x:          # post-fixpoint reached
            break
        x = widen(x, fx)     # extrapolate (terminates by widening property)
    # Descending phase: narrow to recover precision
    y = x
    while True:
        fy = F_sharp(y)
        y2 = narrow(y, fy)
        if y2 == y:
            break
        y = y2
    return y
```

**Widening with thresholds** (Blanché et al.) jumps not to \(\pm\infty\) but to the next program-derived threshold (e.g., constants in guards), preserving far more precision [4]. **Delayed widening** performs plain Kleene steps before widening begins. The A2I framework (Cousot, Giacobazzi, Ranzato, 2019) automates this design: a *meta* abstract interpreter analyzes the abstract interpreter itself to decide where and when widening fires [3].

> **Theorem (No best widening):** *For any widening \(\nabla\) on a non-Noetherian lattice, there exists a strictly more precise widening \(\nabla'\). Widenings form an infinite strictly-improving hierarchy — precision of extrapolation is itself an unbounded design space [4].*

### 4.3 The Interval Domain: Non-Relational Abstraction

The interval domain, introduced by Cousot and Cousot in 1976, is the simplest useful numerical abstraction.

$$
\mathrm{Int} \;=\; \{\bot\} \cup \{[l, u] \mid l, u \in \bar{\mathbb{I}}, l \le u\},
$$

where \(\bar{\mathbb{I}} = \mathbb{I} \cup \{-\infty, +\infty\}\) for \(\mathbb{I} \in \{\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}\}\) (or machine integers, where overflow is treated as an error) [3].

The Galois insertion \(\langle \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I}), \subseteq \rangle \galois{\alpha_i}{\gamma_i} \langle \mathrm{Int}, \sqsubseteq \rangle\) is given by \(\alpha_i(S) = [\min S, \max S]\) and \(\gamma_i([l,u]) = \{z \mid l \le z \le u\}\) [3].

- **Addition:** \([a,b] +^\sharp [c,d] = [a+c, b+d]\)
- **Multiplication:** \([a,b] \times^\sharp [c,d] = [\min(ac,ad,bc,bd), \max(ac,ad,bc,bd)]\)
- **Guard** \(x \le k\): \([a,b] \sqcap [-\infty, k]\)

All operations are \(O(1)\) per variable, and the domain is *non-relational*: it tracks each variable independently, so it cannot express \(x \le y\).

**Symbolic enhancement:** Miné's linearization and symbolic constant propagation (2006) preprocess expressions into affine forms with interval coefficients *before* transfer functions run, recovering relationality within the interval domain itself [11].

### 4.4 The Octagon Domain: Weakly Relational Analysis

The octagon domain of Antoine Miné (2001, journal version 2006) occupies the sweet spot between intervals and polyhedra: it captures conjunctions of constraints of the form

$$
\pm x \pm y \le c, \qquad x, y \text{ program variables},\; c \in \mathbb{Z} \cup \mathbb{Q} \cup \mathbb{R},
$$

i.e., *unary* constraints (\(x \le c\), \(-x \le c\)) plus *binary* difference constraints (\(x - y \le c\), \(x + y \le c\)) [6][7].

The representation is the algorithmic core.

The key algorithms:

- **Closure (normalization):** a Floyd–Warshall-like shortest-path closure puts the DBM in canonical form in \(O(n^3)\), yielding best-precision transfer functions and a decision procedure for entailment and equivalence [6].
- **Join:** pointwise maximum of closed matrices, then re-closure.
- **Widening:** pointwise: keep bounds stable across iterations, jump growing bounds to \(+\infty\) — but *only on a subset of entries*, with thresholds, to preserve relational precision [7].
- **Forget/projection:** eliminate a variable by removing its rows/columns (after closure).

Octagons proved decisive in practice: the domain was incorporated into Astrée and was *key* to proving the absence of runtime errors in large critical embedded flight-control software for Airbus aircraft [6].

### 4.5 The Polyhedral Domain: Full Relational Precision

The polyhedral domain of Cousot and Halbwachs (1978) represents abstract states as *convex polyhedra*: conjunctions of arbitrary linear inequalities \(\sum a_i x_i \le c\).

The domain hierarchy, with asymptotic costs per operation over \(n\) variables, is summarized below:

| Domain | Expressible constraints | Memory | Time/op | Precision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Signs | \(x \in \{<0, =0, >0\}\) | \(O(n)\) | \(O(1)\) | minimal |
| Intervals | \(x \le c\) | \(O(n)\) | \(O(n)\) | non-relational |
| Octagons | \(\pm x \pm y \le c\) | \(O(n^2)\) | \(O(n^3)\) | weakly relational |
| Polyhedra | \(\sum a_i x_i \le c\) | exponential worst-case | exponential worst-case | fully relational |

> **Tradeoff law:** *Every step up the relationality ladder buys precision at super-linear cost. The art of abstract interpretation is choosing the cheapest domain that proves the property at hand — and combining domains (reduced products) when no single domain suffices [9].*

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Soundness Proofs

### 5.1 The soundness proof skeleton

We sketch the soundness proof for a calculationally designed interpreter, where \(\llbracket P \rrbracket^\sharp\) is the widened abstract fixpoint:

1. **Local soundness:** each transfer function satisfies \(\alpha \circ f \circ \gamma \sqsubseteq f^\sharp\), proved once per operator by calculation [3].
2. **Functional soundness:** by monotonicity, \(\alpha \circ F \circ \gamma \sqsubseteq F^\sharp\).
3. **Fixpoint soundness:** the widened iteration yields a post-fixpoint \(X^\sharp\); by fixpoint transfer, \(\mathrm{lfp}\, F \sqsubseteq \gamma(X^\sharp)\) [4].
4. **Alarm soundness:** no alarm for division by zero means \(\gamma(X^\sharp)\) contains no zero divisor on any concrete trace — no true error is missed [10].

Note the asymmetry: the proof guarantees *no false negatives*; false positives are bounded only by the precision of the chosen domains and widenings. An analyzer is thus a *verifier* parameterized by precision [12].

### 5.2 Astrée: abstract interpretation at industrial scale

The decisive empirical validation is **Astrée**, developed from 2001 at ENS by Cousot, Miné, Feret, Mauborgne, Rival, and colleagues [9][10][12]. Astrée proves the *absence of runtime errors* — out-of-bounds access, invalid dereference, division by zero, arithmetic overflow — in synchronous control-command C programs.

Results:

- **Airbus A340/A380 flight-control software:** hundreds of thousands of lines analyzed in a few hours, with **zero false alarms** — a world first for fully automatic verification at that scale [6][10].
- The analyzer combines generalist abstractions (intervals, octagons) with *domain-specific* abstractions for digital filters, integrators, and floating-point rounding drift — abstractions designed by the same Galois-connection calculus, specialized to the control-command application domain [9].

The paper "Why does Astrée scale up?" (Cousot et al., FMSD 2009) attributes the success not merely to algorithms and data structures but to *semantic decisions made sound by the abstract interpretation framework*: trace partitioning, packing of relational domains, staged widenings with thresholds, and floating-point-aware transfer functions — each a sound abstraction choice, composed freely because the theory guarantees composition preserves soundness [9].

## 6 Limitations

**Fundamental limits of approximation.** No computable abstraction can be both sound and complete for all programs and properties. Abstract transfer functions are non-distributive over joins — \(\llbracket \text{if } b \text{ then } P \text{ else } Q \rrbracket^\sharp \sqsupset \llbracket P \rrbracket^\sharp \sqcup \llbracket Q \rrbracket^\sharp\) in general — so path-merging loses information recoverable only by *disjunctive* completions (trace partitioning, powerset domains) at exponential cost [9].

**No best widening.** Widenings can always be strictly improved; there is no canonical extrapolation operator, and widening design remains partly empirical despite the A2I meta-theory [3][4].

**Domain walls.** Intervals express no relations; octagons cannot express ternary (\(x + y + z \le c\)) or non-linear constraints; polyhedra cannot express disjunctions. Floating-point rounding forces linearization, itself a precision loss [8][11].

**Scalability vs. relationality.** Fully relational domains do not scale to millions of lines; Astrée's success depended on *packing* and *specialization* — engineering the theory enables but does not automate [9].

**Specification limits.** Astrée proves absence of *runtime errors*, not functional correctness: the program may compute the wrong answer flawlessly. Abstract interpretation verifies *properties expressible as abstractions of the semantics*; a property outside the chosen abstraction hierarchy is invisible by construction [10][13].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Abstract interpretation transformed static analysis from a craft of clever heuristics into a mathematical discipline: semantics as lattices, abstractions as Galois connections, transfer functions by calculation, termination by widening, and precision recovery by narrowing. The hierarchy of numerical domains — from the linear-time intervals of Cousot and Cousot (1976) through Miné's cubic octagons to the exponential polyhedra of Cousot and Halbwachs — maps the full cost–precision frontier, and the theory tells us exactly what each step costs and buys.

The deepest lesson is methodological: *design the abstraction, and the analyzer follows*. Astrée's verification of Airbus flight-control software with zero false alarms came not from a better bug-finding heuristic but from a lattice of sound abstractions — intervals, octagons, filter domains, partitioning — composed under the soundness theorem [9][10]. Fifty years after the first interval analysis, the Cousots' program remains the most successful bridge between programming-language theory and industrial verification: soundness proved once, by construction, with precision as a tunable engineering parameter.
