# Exponential Resolution Lower Bounds, Frege Proof Systems, and the Prospect of Separating NP from coNP

## Abstract

Propositional proof complexity studies the minimal length of certificates of unsatisfiability for formulas in conjunctive normal form, interrogating the boundary between feasible reasoning and intractability. This thesis develops the field from the Cook–Reckhow definition of a propositional proof system and its exact equivalence with the question whether NP equals coNP, through the landmark lower bounds delineating the power of concrete systems. We reconstruct Haken's bottleneck-counting argument for exponential resolution lower bounds on the pigeonhole principle, the Ben-Sasson–Wigderson width–size tradeoff, the Chvátal–Szemerédi average-case hardness of random CNFs, and the pseudo-width method of Razborov with its sparse-graph extension by de Rezende, Nordström, Risse, and Sokolov. We then examine Frege systems, extended Frege, and bounded-depth Frege, their correspondence with bounded arithmetic via propositional translations, and the feasible-interpolation barrier. We close with the outstanding open problems: the absence of nontrivial Frege lower bounds, the automatizability of resolution, and the path from concrete lower bounds toward NP versus coNP.

## 1 Intro

Every modern SAT solver, handed an unsatisfiable formula, is expected to do more than answer "unsatisfiable": it must *prove* it, producing a certificate whose correctness can be checked efficiently. Proof complexity is the mathematical study of such certificates — how long they must be, which logical principles a given system captures succinctly, and how these questions relate to the separations of computational complexity. The field was founded in essentially modern form by Cook and Reckhow in 1979 [1], who defined a propositional proof system and proved that a *polynomially bounded* proof system exists exactly when NP equals coNP — converting a question about automated reasoning into a question about complexity classes.

> Theorem: (Cook–Reckhow [1]) There exists a propositional proof system in which every tautology admits a proof of polynomial length if and only if NP = coNP.

The proof is definitional and devastating: if NP = coNP, accepting paths of a nondeterministic machine for TAUT furnish polynomial certificates; conversely, a polynomially bounded system *is* an NP decision procedure for the coNP-complete set TAUT. Proving *any* superpolynomial lower bound against *all* proof systems would therefore separate NP from coNP.

Since no unconditional lower bound is known even for Frege systems — the formalization of textbook propositional reasoning — the field proceeds by divide and conquer: fix a concrete system, and prove it needs long proofs for an explicit family of tautologies. This thesis surveys that program, from Haken's 1985 exponential resolution lower bound for the pigeonhole principle [2], through random-formula hardness [3], to the modern pseudo-width machinery [7]; from Frege systems to their correspondence with bounded arithmetic [4][8]; and from feasible interpolation to the open frontiers that may — or may not — lead to NP versus coNP.

## 2 Background

### Propositional proof systems

A *propositional proof system* is a polynomial-time computable function P whose range is exactly TAUT; a string π with P(π) = τ is a *P-proof* of τ [1]. Two strength notions organize the field:

- **Simulation.** P *simulates* Q if Q-proofs translate to P-proofs with polynomial blowup.
- **p-simulation.** The translation itself runs in polynomial time.

A system is *polynomially bounded* if every tautology has a polynomial-length proof. Krajíček and Pudlák showed the *optimal proof system* question — whether one system p-simulates all others — is equivalent to strong statements about complete disjoint NP-pairs and the consistency of first-order theories [4]. Most lower bounds target *refutation* systems: to certify that φ is a tautology, refute the CNF encoding of its negation.

### Resolution

Resolution is the theoretical backbone of the DPLL procedure underlying SAT solvers, with a single rule on clauses (disjunctions of literals):

> **Rule (RES):** From C ∨ x and D ∨ ¬x, infer the *resolvent* C ∨ D.

A *refutation* of a CNF F is a clause sequence ending in the empty clause ⊥, each clause an axiom or a resolvent of earlier ones. *Size* is the clause count; *width* the maximum clause size. Tree-like resolution (each derived clause used at most once) corresponds to DPLL backtracking search [8]; general DAG-like resolution is exponentially stronger on some formulas, yet still far from universal.

### The pigeonhole principle

The field's workhorse hard example is the *pigeonhole principle*: n + 1 pigeons cannot be placed injectively into n holes. Its CNF PHP_n uses variables p_{i,j} (pigeon i from 1 to n + 1, hole j from 1 to n):

1. **Pigeon clauses:** ⋁_j p_{i,j} for each i — every pigeon sits somewhere;
2. **Hole clauses:** ¬p_{i,j} ∨ ¬p_{k,j} for each j and i < k — no hole holds two pigeons.

Variants — *functional* PHP, *onto* PHP, and the *weak* PHP with m ≫ n pigeons — calibrate different strengths of counting reasoning.

### Frege systems and extensions

A *Frege system* is textbook-style reasoning: finitely many axiom schemes and rules over a schematically complete language. Reckhow's robustness theorem says all Frege systems p-simulate each other [1][5], so "Frege" is a single proof-theoretic degree. *Extended Frege* (EF) adds the *extension rule* — a fresh variable x with axiom x ↔ φ abbreviating any formula — letting proofs name and reuse subformulas like circuits. Cook showed extended resolution already proves PHP in polynomial size [2], so the extension rule crosses the barrier defeating plain resolution. *Bounded-depth Frege* restricts formulas to bounded alternation depth (the AC^0 analogue) and is weak enough for exponential lower bounds [5].

### Bounded arithmetic

Theories of *bounded arithmetic* — fragments of Peano arithmetic with induction restricted to bounded formulas, like Buss's S^1_2 and Cook's PV — capture feasible reasoning. The Krajíček–Pudlák program [4] connects them to proof complexity via *propositional translations*: theorems of S^1_2 translate to polynomial-size bounded-depth Frege proofs, while PV corresponds to extended Frege [8]. Lower bounds then become unprovability of reflection principles, and independence results yield conditional separations — the deepest logic–complexity bridge in the field.

## 3 Methodology

Lower bounds are proved by a small repertoire of techniques:

**The restriction method.** A *restriction* ρ is a partial variable assignment; a refutation π of F restricts to a refutation of F↾ρ. Haken's bottleneck counting [2] is the archetype: hit a purported short refutation with a random partial matching of pigeons to holes, and show every refutation contains "complex" clauses that survive only rarely — a union bound then forces exponential size. Modern refinements — expander-tailored restrictions, Razborov's *pseudo-width* [6], switching lemmas for bounded-depth Frege [5] — follow the same template: simplify, then count.

**Width–size tradeoffs.** Ben-Sasson and Wigderson [9] proved that if an n-variable CNF of initial width w_0 needs a clause of width w in every refutation, then every refutation has size exp(Ω((w − w_0)^2 / n)). Width has a clean Prover–Adversary game characterization, so combinatorial lower bounds on width lift automatically to size.

**Feasible interpolation.** If short P-proofs of A(x, y) → B(x, z) yield small interpolating circuits, monotone circuit lower bounds transfer to P [5]. Resolution and cutting planes admit it; Frege's status is open — the method stalls exactly where direct combinatorics does.

**Pseudorandomness for the weak PHP.** With m ≫ n pigeons, counting arguments fail, yet resolution still needs exponential size. Razborov's pseudo-width method [6] measures clauses against random *injections* from holes to pigeons over expander graphs; de Rezende, Nordström, Risse, and Sokolov [7] extended it to sparse expanders, closing a decade-old gap between dense and constant-degree regimes.

**Translations from bounded arithmetic.** Provability of a principle in a weak theory yields short proofs in the corresponding system; unprovability of a reflection principle yields separations [4][8]. This converts metamathematics into proof complexity.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Haken's bottleneck counting

Haken's 1985 theorem [2] — every resolution refutation of PHP_n has size 2^{Ω(n)} — proceeds by *bottleneck counting* over random partial matchings. A partial matching μ injectively maps some pigeons to holes; the restriction ρ_μ fixes p_{i,j} according to μ and leaves the rest unset. Call a clause *complex* if it contains many positive literals p_{i,j} with distinct pigeons — a claim that is "almost true" and hard to refute.

Haken's lemma: every refutation contains complex clauses forming a *bottleneck* — cut the DAG of the refutation and a complex clause must appear. For a random partial matching covering a constant fraction of pigeons, any fixed complex clause is falsified with probability at most 1 − c for an absolute constant c > 0. If |π| were 2^{o(n)}, a union bound yields a matching killing *every* complex clause while π↾ρ_μ stays a valid refutation of the residual formula — but the residual formula still embeds a smaller PHP whose refutation needs a complex clause. Contradiction.

The argument's engine is *symmetry*: PHP_n is symmetric under pigeon and hole permutations, so no small clause set covers all matchings. The template recurs throughout the field — in weak-PHP lower bounds [6][7], matchings become random injections into sparse expanders and literal-count becomes pseudo-width.

### 4.2 Width, size, and random formulas

The Ben-Sasson–Wigderson theorem [9] reshaped the enterprise:

> Theorem: (Ben-Sasson–Wigderson [9]) If every resolution refutation of an n-variable CNF of initial width w_0 contains a clause of width at least w, then every refutation has size at least exp(Ω((w − w_0)^2 / n)).

Width is characterized by a game: the Prover queries variables, the Adversary answers consistently with F; if the Adversary survives until w + 1 literals are remembered, width w is necessary. A random restriction then shrinks a purported narrow refutation while preserving unsatisfiability — contradiction unless the refutation is large.

Chvátal and Szemerédi [3] applied this to *random* k-CNFs: above the satisfiability threshold, random formulas need exponential resolution refutations with high probability. Expansion of the random hypergraph forces some wide clause in every refutation. Hardness for resolution is thus generic, not a PHP pathology — most unsatisfiable formulas are hard, explaining solver struggles near the phase transition.

| Proof system | Hard family | Lower bound | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resolution | PHP_{n+1→n} | 2^{Ω(n)} | [2] |
| Resolution | Random k-CNF | 2^{Ω(n)} a.a.s. | [3] |
| Resolution | Weak onto-FPHP, sparse expanders | exp(n^{Ω(1)}) | [7] |
| Bounded-depth Frege | PHP_{n+1→n} | 2^{n^{Ω(1)}} | [5] |
| Frege | — | *no nontrivial bound known* | [5][8] |
| Extended Frege | — | *no nontrivial bound known* | [8] |

The last two rows are the field's embarrassment and its engine.

### 4.3 Frege, extended Frege, and bounded arithmetic

No superpolynomial lower bound is known for Frege — not even beyond the AC^0 barrier [5]. What exists are separations *within* the weak region: exponential lower bounds for bounded-depth Frege proofs of PHP [5], via switching lemmas mirroring circuit lower bounds for parity. Extended Frege is the critical threshold: EF p-simulates extended resolution and corresponds to Cook's PV — theorems of PV translate to polynomial-size EF proofs, and PV proves EF's reflection principle [8]. So an EF lower bound would separate PV from stronger theories [4].

The conditional landscape: EF is strictly stronger than bounded-depth Frege (unconditionally, via PHP [5]); resolution is strictly weaker than extended resolution (Haken's bound plus Cook's polynomial ER upper bound for PHP [2]); Frege sits between — but whether the extension rule adds power over schematic Frege, the *Frege versus extended Frege* problem, is open and arguably the most important concrete question after NP versus coNP.

### 4.4 The weak pigeonhole principle and pseudorandomness

The *weak* PHP — m pigeons, n holes, m far larger than n — defeats naive counting, yet resolution still needs exponential size. Razborov's pseudo-width method [6] assigns each clause a *pseudo-width* relative to a random injection from holes to pigeons: a clause is pseudo-wide if the injection kills it with small probability. Every refutation of the weak functional PHP contains pseudo-wide clauses, and counting over the injection space forces exponentially many clauses.

De Rezende, Nordström, Risse, and Sokolov [7] pushed pseudo-width to *sparse* unbalanced expanders — the regime between constant-degree expanders and dense graphs that resisted analysis for a decade. Their exponential lower bounds for onto-functional PHP and perfect-matching formulas over sparse graphs, via a new closure operation preserving expansion under the injection distribution, show pseudo-width is a general technology, potentially applicable to other longstanding open problems.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

> Theorem: (Cook–Reckhow [1]) NP = coNP iff some propositional proof system is polynomially bounded.

*Proof sketch.* (⇒) If NP = coNP, TAUT ∈ NP; accepting paths of a nondeterministic machine deciding TAUT are polynomial certificates, and the verifier is the proof system. (⇐) If P is bounded by q, then τ ∈ TAUT iff ∃π with |π| ≤ q(|τ|) and P(π) = τ — an NP predicate; TAUT's coNP-completeness gives coNP ⊆ NP. ∎

> Theorem: (Haken [2]) Every resolution refutation of PHP_n has size 2^{Ω(n)}.

*Proof sketch.* Haken's bottleneck lemma: every refutation contains a complex clause (many positive literals on distinct pigeons), and complex clauses are unavoidable. A random partial matching covering a constant fraction of pigeons falsifies any fixed complex clause with probability at most 1 − c. If |π| = 2^{o(n)}, a union bound gives a matching killing every complex clause while the restricted refutation survives on the residual PHP — which still needs a complex clause. Contradiction. ∎

> Theorem: (Ben-Sasson–Wigderson [9]) Width w needed (initial width w_0, n variables) implies size exp(Ω((w − w_0)^2 / n)).

*Proof sketch.* Take a refutation minimizing fat clauses; hit it with a random restriction setting each variable with probability ≈ (w − w_0)/n. With positive probability all clauses wider than (w + w_0)/2 shrink below w while F stays unsatisfiable — contradicting width minimality unless the union bound fails, forcing exponential size. ∎

> Theorem: (de Rezende–Nordström–Risse–Sokolov [7]) For explicit sparse expanders with n right vertices and m = exp(O(n^{1/16})) left vertices of polylogarithmic left-degree, refuting the perfect matching formula in resolution needs size exp(Ω(n^{1/8})).

*Proof sketch.* Extend pseudo-width [6] to sparse graphs: axioms get small pseudo-width under an injection distribution; expansion forces some clause of pseudo-width Ω(n^{1/8}) in every refutation; a random injection kills each pseudo-narrow clause with constant probability, so a union bound forces exponentially many clauses. A new vertex-set closure operation maintains expansion under the distribution — the technical core. ∎

The Cook–Reckhow definition demands polynomial-time *verification*; this Python checker is the kind of program it requires:

```python
def check_resolution_refutation(axioms: list[frozenset], steps: list[tuple]) -> bool:
    """Verify a DAG-like resolution refutation. Clauses are frozensets of
    ints (x_i = i, ~x_i = -i); each step is (a, b, pivot)."""
    derived = list(axioms)
    for a, b, p in steps:
        ca, cb = derived[a], derived[b]
        assert p in ca and -p in cb, "pivot must occur with opposite signs"
        resolvent = (ca - {p}) | (cb - {-p})
        assert not any(-q in resolvent for q in resolvent), "no tautological resolvent"
        derived.append(frozenset(resolvent))
    return len(derived[-1]) == 0
```

Tree-like resolution is DPLL search; a TLA+ sketch makes the operational reading precise:

```tla+
------------------------------ MODULE DPLL ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Vars, Clauses
VARIABLES assign, stack
Init == assign = [v \in Vars |-> "unassigned"] /\ stack = << >>
Decide == \E v \in Vars : assign[v] = "unassigned"
         /\ assign' = [assign EXCEPT ![v] = "true"]
         /\ stack' = Append(stack, v)
Backtrack == \E C \in Clauses : IsFalsified(C, assign) /\ Len(stack) > 0
         /\ assign' = [assign EXCEPT ![Head(stack)] = "false"]
         /\ stack' = Tail(stack)
=============================================================================
```

Each root-to-leaf DPLL path is a tree-like derivation, so the lower bounds say *no* branching heuristic makes DPLL efficient on these formulas — theorems about algorithms disguised as theorems about proofs. Finally, the propositional translation behind the bounded-arithmetic correspondence [4][8], in Haskell:

```haskell
-- Schematic translation of bounded-arithmetic proofs into propositional
-- Frege proofs, one per input length n.
data BFormula = BAtom String | BAnd BFormula BFormula | BBoundedForall String BFormula

translate :: Int -> BFormula -> PropFormula
translate n (BAtom x)            = PVar (x ++ "_" ++ show n)
translate n (BAnd f g)           = PAnd (translate n f) (translate n g)
translate n (BBoundedForall v f) =
    foldr1 PAnd [translate n (subst v (show i) f) | i <- [0 .. n-1]]
-- A proof of a forall-bounded sentence in S^1_2 yields, for each n,
-- a bounded-depth Frege proof of (translate n phi) of size poly(n).
```

## 6 Limitations

**The Frege barrier.** No nontrivial lower bound is known for Frege, let alone extended Frege [5][8]. Restrictions fail because Frege proofs express counting and survive them; feasible interpolation for Frege would imply circuit lower bounds beyond reach; width is meaningless outside clause systems. Frege lower bounds may require a breakthrough in circuit complexity, since Frege proofs correspond to formulas and EF proofs to circuits.

**Automatizability.** Even for resolution, finding short refutations when they exist may be hard: resolution is not automatizable unless W[P] is tractable [6]. Worst-case lower bounds coexist with spectacular solver performance on industrial instances — theory cannot yet say *which* instances are easy [7].

**Interpolation limits.** Feasible interpolation transfers circuit bounds only to systems admitting it — resolution, cutting planes, some algebraic systems. For sum-of-squares it fails outright; for Frege it would demand unknown circuit bounds. The method is confined to the weak-to-intermediate region where combinatorics already works.

**Barriers to scaling up.** Natural-proofs-style considerations and oracle constructions for the optimal-proof-system question [4] warn that the path from concrete lower bounds to NP ≠ coNP will not be routine scaling of current methods. And the bounded-arithmetic correspondence [4][8] is tight only up to extended Frege — for stronger systems the logical counterparts are poorly understood, leaving lower bounds there without metamathematical scaffolding.

## 7 Conclusion

Proof complexity began with a definition — Cook and Reckhow's proof system [1] — turning the oldest question in computer science into one about certificate length. The field has since mapped the lower hierarchy precisely: resolution falls to bottleneck counting and width [2][9], random formulas are hard on average [3], and the weak pigeonhole principle over sparse graphs yields to pseudo-width [7]. Translations between bounded arithmetic and propositional systems [4][8] built a two-way logic–complexity bridge.

Yet the summit is untouched. Frege has no nontrivial lower bound; extended Frege is characterized logically but not combinatorially; and the Cook–Reckhow equivalence stands as beacon and reproach — every bound so far targets a system too weak to matter for NP versus coNP. The way forward lies in the *methods*: pseudo-width generalized to sparse graphs [7], interpolation refined for new circuit bounds, bounded arithmetic extended to stronger reasoning. Whether any lens brings Frege into focus — and whether beyond it lies a proof that no polynomially bounded proof system exists — remains the deepest open problem in feasible reasoning.
