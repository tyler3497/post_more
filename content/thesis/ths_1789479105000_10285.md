# Transmission Spectroscopy of Exoplanet Atmospheres in the JWST Era: Retrieval Methods and Molecular Detections

## Abstract

The James Webb Space Telescope has revolutionized the characterization of exoplanet atmospheres through transmission spectroscopy, delivering spectra with resolving power and precision unattainable by Hubble or Spitzer. This thesis reviews the theoretical foundations of transit depth retrieval, in which wavelength-dependent variations of order 10-100 parts per million encode molecular abundances, thermal structure, and cloud properties of the terminator atmosphere. We examine landmark results: the detection of CO2, H2O, CO, and photochemically produced SO2 in the hot Saturn WASP-39b; the contested CH4 and CO2 detections in the habitable-zone sub-Neptune K2-18b and the Hycean-versus-mini-Neptune debate they provoked; and JWST/MIRI eclipse and phase-curve observations of TRAPPIST-1 b and c that disfavor thick CO2 atmospheres. We analyze Bayesian retrieval frameworks, cloud and haze parameterizations, and hydrodynamic atmospheric escape. Finally, we assess systematic limitations including stellar contamination, and outline the path toward characterizing temperate rocky worlds with future observatories.

---

##1 Introduction

The spectroscopic study of exoplanet atmospheres is among the most demanding enterprises in modern astrophysics. The basic obstacle is one of contrast: a transiting hot Jupiter blocks roughly one percent of its host star's light, yet the *atmospheric* signal — the wavelength-dependent variation of that transit depth — is a hundred times smaller still. Before the launch of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), the Hubble Space Telescope's Wide Field Camera 3 and the Spitzer Space Telescope's IRAC photometry had detected water vapor, alkali metals, and aerosols in a few dozen atmospheres, but the narrow wavelength coverage and modest resolving power of those facilities left the primary carbon-bearing molecules — CO2, CO, CH4 — frustratingly out of reach [3].

JWST changed the calculus: NIRSpec, NIRISS, NIRCam, and MIRI span the optical to the mid-infrared at resolving powers up to several thousand, routinely achieving tens of ppm per spectral bin. The WASP-39b Early Release Science program delivered the first definitive CO2 detection [1], a full volatile inventory spanning Na, H2O, CO2, CO, and SO2 [2][3], and the first photochemically produced species identified in an exoplanet atmosphere [2]. Parallel programs reached smaller, cooler planets: K2-18b's methane and carbon dioxide detections ignited the Hycean-versus-mini-Neptune debate [6][7], while MIRI eclipse photometry of TRAPPIST-1 began testing whether temperate M-dwarf rocky planets retain atmospheres at all [8][9].

This thesis develops the full pipeline of transmission spectroscopy in the JWST era, from the radiative physics of the transit chord to Bayesian retrieval, photochemical modeling, cloud physics, and atmospheric escape, anchored throughout in these landmark results.

##2 Background

### The transit depth spectrum

When a planet transits its host star, the fractional dimming is

$$\delta(\lambda) = \left(\frac{R_p(\lambda)}{R_\star}\right)^2,$$

where the apparent planetary radius $R_p(\lambda)$ is wavelength-dependent because the atmosphere is opaque at molecular absorption bands and transparent in between. The atmosphere contributes an annulus of area approximately $2\pi R_p H$, where $H$ is the pressure scale height

$$H = \frac{k_B T}{\mu m_H g}.$$

Here $T$ is the local temperature, $\mu$ the mean molecular weight, $m_H$ the hydrogen mass, and $g$ the surface gravity. The amplitude of spectral features is therefore of order

$$\Delta \delta \sim \frac{2 R_p H}{R_\star^2},$$

typically 10-100 ppm per scale height for hot Jupiters and far less for rocky planets. This scaling explains the field's history: inflated hot gas giants first, sub-Neptunes and terrestrials only now — the signal grows with temperature, shrinks with gravity and mean molecular weight, and is diluted by the square of the stellar radius, favoring small M-dwarf hosts.

> Theorem: The transmission spectrum probes the terminator. Starlight that reaches the observer during transit has traversed the planet's limb at grazing incidence, so the effective path length exceeds the vertical scale height by a factor of order $\sqrt{2\pi R_p/H}$, and the measured spectrum samples a thin crescent of atmosphere at the day-night boundary, not the dayside or nightside in bulk.

### Molecular band assignments

JWST's near- and mid-infrared bands map cleanly onto the major volatile carriers (Table 1): H2O at 1.4, 1.9, and 2.7 micrometers; CH4 at 2.3 and 3.3; CO2 in the deep 4.3 micrometer band; CO at 2.3 and 4.6; NH3 near 3.0; and photochemical SO2 at 4.0 micrometers. The relative strengths of the carbon carriers encode metallicity and C/O — fossil records of where and how the planet accreted its envelope.

### The JWST instrument suite

NIRSpec's PRISM mode delivers 0.5-5.5 micrometers in a single transit [3]; the G395H grating trades coverage for resolving power near 600 across 3-5 micrometers [2]; NIRCam grism spectroscopy spans 2.0-4.0 micrometers [4]; NIRISS/SOSS covers 0.6-2.8 micrometers for sub-Neptune studies; and MIRI extends to 5-28 micrometers, enabling terrestrial-planet eclipse photometry at 12.8 and 15 micrometers [8]. JWST's critical advance is wavelength *continuity*: overlapping bands measured simultaneously break the abundance-temperature-cloud degeneracies that plagued HST and Spitzer data.

##3 Methodology

### Bayesian retrieval

Atmospheric retrieval is the inverse problem of inferring atmospheric state vectors $\theta$ — molecular volume mixing ratios, the pressure-temperature profile, cloud parameters, and a reference radius — from the observed transmission spectrum $d$. In the Bayesian formulation,

$$p(\theta \mid d) \propto \mathcal{L}(d \mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta),$$

the forward model computes a model spectrum $F(\theta)$ by integrating the radiative transfer equation along limb paths through a discretized atmosphere, and the likelihood $\mathcal{L}$ is typically Gaussian in the flux residuals. The posterior is explored with nested sampling algorithms (MultiNest, dynesty) or Markov chain Monte Carlo, which also return the Bayesian evidence $\mathcal{Z}$ used for model comparison — for example, quantifying the detection significance of SO2 by comparing evidences with and without that opacity source [2].

Two philosophies compete. *Free retrievals* fit each molecular abundance independently, making minimal assumptions but risking unphysical combinations; *chemically consistent retrievals* impose equilibrium chemistry given metallicity, C/O, and the temperature profile. The WASP-39b analyses used both: free retrievals established detections, while equilibrium grids constrained metallicity to 3-10 times solar with sub-solar to solar C/O [2].

### Opacities and forward modeling

Line-by-line opacities from ExoMol and HITRAN, sampled at resolving powers of order one million and then down-sampled, underpin all modern forward models. Collision-induced absorption by H2-H2 and H2-He pairs sets the continuum in hydrogen-dominated atmospheres; Rayleigh scattering by H2 and He dominates the optical slope; and Mie scattering by condensate particles must be parameterized (see Section 4.3). The transmission contribution function — the derivative of transit depth with respect to log-pressure — typically peaks between 1 mbar and 100 mbar, meaning transmission spectroscopy is intrinsically a probe of the *upper* atmosphere.

### The transit light source effect

A subtle but now unavoidable systematic is stellar contamination: unocculted starspots and faculae make the transit chord's spectrum differ from the disk-integrated stellar spectrum, imprinting spurious slopes and even molecular-looking features on the transmission spectrum. For active M dwarfs like TRAPPIST-1 and K2-18, this "transit light source effect" can rival the planetary signal, and modern retrievals fit spot and facula covering fractions jointly with the atmospheric parameters.

```python
import numpy as np

def scale_height(T, mu, g):
    """Pressure scale height in meters."""
    kB = 1.380649e-23   # Boltzmann constant, J/K
    mH = 1.6735575e-27  # hydrogen mass, kg
    return kB * T / (mu * mH * g)

def feature_amplitude_ppm(Rp, H, Rs):
    """One-scale-height transit-depth contrast, in ppm."""
    return 2.0 * Rp * H / Rs**2 * 1e6

# WASP-39b-like hot Saturn: T=1170 K, mu~2.3, g~4.3 m/s^2
H = scale_height(1170.0, 2.3, 4.3)
print(f"H = {H/1e3:.0f} km")
# Rp = 1.27 Rjup, Rs = 0.93 Rsun
amp = feature_amplitude_ppm(1.27*7.149e7, H, 0.93*6.957e8)
print(f"delta per scale height ~ {amp:.0f} ppm")
```

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Molecular Feature Inventory and Band Assignments

The WASP-39b ERS campaign is the Rosetta Stone of JWST transmission spectroscopy because four independent instrument modes observed the same planet and agreed. NIRSpec PRISM (0.5-5.5 micrometers) detected Na at 19σ, H2O at 33σ, CO2 at 28σ, and CO at 7σ, while the non-detection of CH4 combined with the strong CO2 feature favored super-solar metallicity [3]. NIRSpec G395H at higher resolution confirmed CO2 at 28.5σ and H2O at 21.5σ, and identified SO2 as the source of the 4.1 micrometer absorption at 4.8σ [2]. NIRCam (2.0-4.0 micrometers) independently detected H2O and placed an upper limit on CH4, with equilibrium models favoring 1-100 times solar metallicity and a sub-stellar C/O ratio [4]. Cross-correlation analysis of the unbinned G395H spectrum further confirmed CO at 6.6σ, rising to 7.5σ when isotopologue lines including 13C16O were included — demonstrating that high-resolution techniques can extract even isotopologue ratios from JWST data [5].

> Theorem: The carbon inventory is a formation diagnostic. In chemical equilibrium, the partitioning of carbon among CO, CO2, and CH4 is exquisitely sensitive to temperature, metallicity, and C/O: hot atmospheres lock carbon in CO, cool atmospheres in CH4, and high metallicity boosts CO2 quadratically. Measuring all three simultaneously breaks the degeneracies that single-molecule detections cannot.

### 4.2 Photochemistry: SO2 as a Disequilibrium Tracer

The detection of SO2 at 4.0 micrometers in WASP-39b was unanticipated by equilibrium models and is best explained as a photochemical product [2][3]. The proposed pathway begins with photolysis of H2S by stellar ultraviolet photons, liberating atomic sulfur that is successively oxidized to SO and then SO2. Because SO2 is destroyed in the deep atmosphere and accumulates only where UV photons penetrate, its abundance directly traces the radiation field and vertical mixing — a disequilibrium signature no adjustment of metallicity or C/O can mimic. WASP-39b is thus the first exoplanet with *direct* evidence of active photochemistry, validating a decade of predictions, and the SO2 abundance further constrains the eddy diffusion coefficient and stellar UV flux.

### 4.3 Cloud and Haze Modeling

Clouds and hazes are the great confounders of transmission spectroscopy. A high-altitude cloud deck truncates the limb path, muting all molecular features uniformly; a photochemical haze of small particles imposes a Rayleigh-like slope rising toward the blue; and large condensate particles produce gray opacity. Modern retrievals parameterize this with some combination of:

1. A cloud-top pressure $P_c$ below which the atmosphere is opaque,
2. A haze power-law opacity $\sigma = \sigma_0(\lambda/\lambda_0)^\gamma$ with enhancement factor and slope,
3. Mie scattering from log-normal particle distributions of candidate condensates (MnS, Na2S, MgSiO3, KCl),
4. Patchy or terminator-asymmetric cloud coverage fractions.

The WASP-39b best-fit models required moderate cloud opacity alongside 10 times solar metallicity [1], illustrating the central degeneracy: muted features can mean either high clouds or a high mean molecular weight (metal-rich) atmosphere with a small scale height. JWST's broad wavelength coverage breaks this degeneracy because clouds mute features achromatically while metallicity changes relative band strengths — the strong CO2 feature at 4.3 micrometers in WASP-39b could not be reproduced by any cloudy solar-metallicity model, forcing the super-solar solution [2][3].

### 4.4 Atmospheric Escape and Retention

Extreme-ultraviolet irradiation drives hydrodynamic escape: in the energy-limited regime the mass-loss rate scales as $\dot{M} \propto F_{XUV} R_p^3 / (G M_p)$, potentially stripping primordial hydrogen envelopes over gigayear timescales and sculpting the radius valley between super-Earths and sub-Neptunes. The metastable helium triplet at 10830 angstroms, now routinely measured in transmission, directly traces escaping gas. TRAPPIST-1 is the empirical laboratory for M-dwarf escape: after billions of years of elevated XUV flux, JWST/MIRI observations increasingly suggest the inner planets lost that fight. Phase curves of b and c show dayside brightness temperatures consistent with no heat redistribution and no detectable nightside emission, strongly disfavoring atmospheres at or above 1 bar [10]. If even these planets are bare rocks, it sharpens the question of which M-dwarf rocky planets, if any, can retain atmospheres — with profound implications for habitability.

### 4.5 Transit Depth Retrieval and Degeneracies

Retrieval is only as trustworthy as its degeneracies are understood. The dominant ones are:

- **Abundance vs. cloud-top pressure**: deeper clouds hide molecules, mimicking lower abundances.
- **Temperature vs. mean molecular weight**: both set the scale height and hence feature amplitude.
- **Reference radius vs. baseline abundances**: the absolute transit depth anchors the pressure grid.
- **Metallicity vs. C/O**: both reshape the carbon chemistry; only multi-molecule detections separate them.
- **Stellar heterogeneity vs. haze slope**: unocculted spots mimic blueward slopes.

The WASP-39b results are robust precisely because the same planet was observed from 0.5 to 5.5 micrometers: the Na line wings constrain the pressure baseline, the H2O bands constrain temperature, the CO2/CO/CH4 triplet pins down metallicity and C/O, and the SO2 feature demands photochemistry — no single degenerate model reproduces all of them simultaneously [2][3][4].

##5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

### WASP-39b: the complete volatile inventory

WASP-39b is a roughly Saturn-mass, Jupiter-radius planet with an equilibrium temperature near 1170 K orbiting a G-type star. The ERS program observed it with four modes, and their convergence is the strongest validation of JWST transmission spectroscopy to date. PRISM (0.5-5.5 micrometers) detected Na, H2O, CO2, and CO at 19σ, 33σ, 28σ, and 7σ; the CH4 non-detection favored super-solar metallicity and an unanticipated 4 micrometer feature was attributed to SO2 at 2.7σ [3]. G395H (3-5 micrometers, R~600) gave 221 ppm per bin: CO2 at 28.5σ, H2O at 21.5σ, SO2 confirmed at 4.8σ, with 3-10 times solar metallicity and sub-solar to solar C/O [2]. NIRCam (2.0-4.0 micrometers) independently confirmed H2O, bounded CH4 from above, and favored 1-100 times solar metallicity with sub-stellar C/O, suggesting heavy solid accretion during formation [4]. Cross-correlation of the unbinned G395H spectrum confirmed CO at 6.6σ, rising to 7.5σ with isotopologue templates including 13C16O [5]. Together these observations constitute a proof of concept: a single JWST program measured a nearly complete volatile inventory, a photochemical product, and formation-diagnostic elemental ratios for a planet 750 light-years away.

### K2-18b: the Hycean debate

K2-18b is an 8.6 Earth-mass, 2.6 Earth-radius sub-Neptune orbiting in the habitable zone of a cool M dwarf. JWST NIRISS and NIRSpec transmission spectra revealed methane and carbon dioxide at roughly percent-level abundances, a depletion of ammonia, and a tentative feature attributed to dimethyl sulfide (DMS), a molecule produced predominantly by marine life on Earth. The discovery team argued these are consistent with a "Hycean" world — a liquid water ocean beneath a hydrogen-rich atmosphere, where the ocean elevates the CO2/CH4 ratio and dissolves NH3 [7]. The interpretation was immediately contested. Photochemical-climate modeling showed a lifeless Hycean atmosphere sustains less than 1 ppm of CH4 against photodestruction, in violent tension with the observed percent-level abundance; sustaining it would require an Archaean-like methanogenic biosphere, or the Hycean hypothesis must yield to a gas-rich mini-Neptune at 100 times solar metallicity, which produces the observed CH4 and CO2 thermochemically with no ocean and no life required [6]. An independent comprehensive reanalysis of the same NIRISS and NIRSpec data confirmed the CH4 detection but found no statistically significant evidence for CO2 or DMS, suggesting an oxygen-poor sub-Neptune composition that requires neither an ocean nor biology [7]. Mid-infrared MIRI observations subsequently claimed DMS and DMDS at roughly 3σ, which critics noted could alternatively be ethane, a photochemical byproduct [7]. The K2-18b saga is a case study in the field's central epistemic challenge: at the edge of detectability, the same data support a habitable ocean world, a lifeless mini-Neptune, or an oxygen-poor sub-Neptune depending on modeling assumptions — resolvable only with repeated transits, broader coverage, and stricter statistical standards for biosignature claims.

### TRAPPIST-1 b and c: the atmosphere retention test

The TRAPPIST-1 system — seven rocky planets around an ultracool dwarf — is the premier laboratory for whether M-dwarf rocky planets keep atmospheres. Five MIRI eclipses of TRAPPIST-1 b at 15 micrometers gave 861±99 ppm, consistent with a dark airless rock with no heat redistribution and ruling out Venus-like and O2-dominated CO2-rich atmospheres beyond 6σ; self-consistent modeling excluded even 100 ppm of CO2 at 3σ above 0.3 bar [9]. A twin 12.8 micrometer program measured 452±86 ppm versus 775±90 ppm at 15 micrometers, fit either by an airless planet with a fresh ultramafic surface (albedo near 0.19) or by a thick pure-CO2 atmosphere with photochemical hazes inverting the temperature profile and flipping CO2 into emission — a degeneracy broadband photometry alone cannot break [8]. Full-orbit MIRI phase curves of b and c show dayside brightness temperatures of 490±17 K and 369±23 K with no detectable nightside emission or phase offset, strongly disfavoring atmospheres above 1 bar [10]. The emerging picture — the two most observable temperate rocky planets known are probably bare rocks — is a sobering data point for M-dwarf habitability and a triumph of JWST's ability to answer the question at all.

##6 Limitations

Transmission spectroscopy in the JWST era remains bounded by systematics that no photon collecting area can remove. *Stellar contamination* is foremost: for M-dwarf hosts, unocculted spots and faculae can imprint wavelength-dependent transit depth variations comparable to the planetary signal, and joint stellar-planetary retrievals are still maturing. *Limb darkening* uncertainties propagate directly into transit depths at the tens-of-ppm level. *Aerosol degeneracies* persist for cloudy targets where muted spectra admit both metal-rich and cloudy interpretations, broken only by the broadest wavelength coverage. *Model-dependent inferences* — metallicity, C/O, and especially biosignature claims — rest on the completeness of opacity databases and the fidelity of photochemical networks, both known to be incomplete for sulfur and hydrocarbon chemistry. The K2-18b DMS debate exemplifies the *statistical* limitation: features at 2-3σ significance in a handful of transits cannot bear the weight of a life-detection claim, and independent reanalyses of identical data reaching different conclusions [7] reveal how sensitive retrievals are to data-reduction choices. Finally, *MIRI's precision* for terrestrial planets remains at the edge: eclipse depths of hundreds of ppm with ~90 ppm uncertainties leave room for thin or exotic atmospheres that future phase curves and repeated eclipses must close.

---

##7 Conclusion

In four years of operations, JWST transmission spectroscopy has progressed from the first CO2 detection in a hot Saturn [1] to complete volatile inventories with photochemical tracers [2][3][4][5], to contested molecular detections in a habitable-zone sub-Neptune that forced the field to formalize its standards of evidence for life [6][7], to eclipse and phase-curve observations suggesting the nearest temperate rocky planets are bare rocks [8][9][10]. The methodological arc is equally important: Bayesian retrieval with nested sampling, free versus chemically consistent modeling, cross-correlation isotopologue detections, joint stellar-contamination fits, and photochemical forward models have matured into a standard toolkit. The path forward is clear — repeated transits of sub-Neptunes to settle K2-18b-class debates, MIRI phase curves across the TRAPPIST-1 system to map the atmosphere-retention boundary, and eventually direct spectroscopy of temperate terrestrials with the Habitable Worlds Observatory. The transit depth, a number measured in parts per million, has become the most information-dense observable in planetary science.
