# Automatic Memory Management: Mark-Sweep, Copying, Generational, and Concurrent Garbage Collectors from Baker to ZGC

## Abstract

Automatic memory management frees the programmer from explicit storage reclamation, but every collector pays for that freedom somewhere: in *throughput*, in *pause time*, or in *space*. This thesis surveys six decades of garbage collection, from McCarthy's 1960 mark–sweep collector [1] and the reference-counting lineage, through Dijkstra's tri-color abstraction [4], Cheney's semispace copying [2], and Baker's real-time incremental copying [3], to the modern concurrent collectors — Azul's C4 [8], Shenandoah [9], and ZGC [10] — that promise sub-millisecond pauses on terabyte heaps. We formalize the tri-color invariant underwriting all incremental tracing, derive the read- and write-barrier taxonomy that preserves it under mutator concurrency, and analyze generational collection's card-table machinery. Published evaluations of the Metronome [7], C4 [8], Shenandoah [9], and ZGC [10] collectors anchor a quantitative comparison of the design space. The thesis closes by characterizing the fundamental pause–throughput–space tradeoff and the open problems that remain for hard real-time and very-large-heap collection.

## 1 Introduction

In 1960, John McCarthy observed that a program which builds list structure faster than it discards it faces a bookkeeping problem he called *the erasure problem*: deciding, in general, when a storage cell can never be referenced again cannot be settled at compile time, so the decision must be made — safely and automatically — at runtime [1]. His answer, a mark–sweep collector embedded in the first LISP system, founded a research program that is still accelerating. Sixty-five years later, collectors such as ZGC relocate live objects on terabyte heaps while the application keeps running, pausing it for mere microseconds [10].

The stakes have risen with the heaps. Managed runtimes now underpin payment authorization, telecom switches, and electronic trading, and the service-level agreements governing them are written in milliseconds — often in the low tens of them — while the heaps under management have grown from kilobytes to terabytes. Every collector design is an answer to a single three-cornered question: **how much throughput** will you sacrifice, **how much space** will you spend, and **how long** may the world stop while you clean up? No design escapes the triangle; the history of the field is the history of choosing which corner to stand in, and of increasingly clever engineering that pushes the corners outward.

This thesis develops the full arc. Section 2 reconstructs the classical foundations: reference counting and its cycle pathology, mark–sweep, the tri-color abstraction of Dijkstra and colleagues [4], and the copying collectors culminating in Cheney's algorithm [2]. Section 3 fixes the methodology — the axes, metrics, and the minimum-mutator-utilization yardstick by which the field compares collectors. Section 4 is the deep dive: tri-color coordination and the barrier taxonomy; Cheney's scan and Baker's real-time copying [3] with its treadmill refinement [5]; generational collection with card tables and remembered sets; and the modern concurrent triumvirate of Shenandoah [9], ZGC [10], and Azul's C4 [8]. Section 5 turns quantitative, grounding each design in published measurements and proving the key safety invariants. Section 6 is honest about what none of these collectors can do, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Reference counting and the cycle problem

The oldest automatic technique attaches a counter to every object, incremented on each new reference and decremented when a reference dies; an object whose count reaches zero is reclaimed immediately [6].

The second defect is fatal in the general case: **reference cycles never reach zero**.

### 2.2 Mark–sweep: McCarthy 1960

McCarthy's collector [1] works in two phases.

### 2.3 The tri-color abstraction

Dijkstra, Lamport, Martin, Scholten, and Steffens reframed marking as a graph-coloring process that could run *concurrently* with the mutator [4].

- **white** — not yet visited, a candidate for reclamation;
- **grey** — visited, but its outgoing references not yet scanned (the wavefront);
- **black** — visited and fully scanned.

The algorithm's correctness rests on a single invariant, the **tri-color invariant**: *no black object may point to a white object*.

### 2.4 Copying collection: Fenichel, Yochelson, and Cheney

Copying collectors divide the heap into two equal *semispaces* and always allocate in one of them.

### 2.5 Barriers and the road to concurrency

Once the collector and the mutator run simultaneously, every pointer load or store is a potential invariant violation.

## 3 Methodology

Comparing collectors requires fixing the axes first, because vendors and papers otherwise talk past each other.

1. **Throughput** — mutator work completed per unit of wall-clock time, i.e., how much the collector slows the application overall.
2. **Pause time** — both the *maximum* stop-the-world pause and the *distribution* of pauses. A collector with a 10 ms average and a 2 s tail is unusable for interactive service, which is why tail behavior dominates modern evaluation.
3. **Space overhead** — the fraction of the heap the collector reserves for itself: empty semispaces, card tables, forwarding words, mark bitmaps.
4. **Mutator overhead** — the steady-state tax of barriers on loads and stores, paid even when no collection is occurring.
5. **Scalability** — how pause time and throughput degrade as heap size and live-set size grow into the hundreds of gigabytes and beyond.

We adopt the terminology *stop-the-world* (all mutator threads suspended), *concurrent* (collector runs alongside mutators), *incremental* (collection is interleaved with mutator execution in bounded quanta), and *parallel* (multiple collector threads, possibly still stop-the-world).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Tri-Color Abstraction and the Mutator Coordination Problem

Consider the marking wavefront mid-cycle: black objects behind it are fully processed, grey objects form the frontier, and white objects ahead are untouched.

> **Theorem:** (Tri-color safety.) Suppose a tracing cycle begins with all objects white and the roots grey, moves objects only from white to grey to black, never reclaims a white object while the grey set is nonempty, and never creates an edge from a black object to a white object. Then, when the grey set becomes empty, every remaining white object is unreachable, and reclaiming them is safe. [4]

*Proof sketch.* By induction on the coloring steps: every object reachable from a root is greyed when first discovered and blackened only after all its children are grey, so no reachable object can remain white while grey is nonempty. The invariant guarantees the collector never "loses" a reachable white object behind the wavefront. ∎

A running mutator can break the invariant in exactly two ways [4]:

1. **Insertion:** it stores a pointer to a white object into a black object — the white object is now reachable but sits behind the wavefront, invisible to the collector.
2. **Deletion:** it overwrites the *only* pointer to a white object before the collector traverses it — the object becomes unreachable but was never marked, so a naive sweeper would wrongly keep it (harmless) or, worse, the collector's bookkeeping of the wavefront becomes inconsistent.

The barrier taxonomy exists to close these two holes:

| Barrier | Intercepts | Action on violation | Invariant preserved | Cost profile |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Read barrier** (Baker [3]) | Pointer loads | Grey the white referent on load | Strong: mutator never sees white | Per-load check; can be heavy |
| **Write barrier, Dijkstra style** (incremental update) | Pointer stores | Grey the white *target* (or re-grey the source) | Strong: no black-to-white edge survives | Per-store check; cheap fast path |
| **Write barrier, Steele style** | Pointer stores | Grey the *overwritten* referent | Weak: catches deletions | Per-store check |
| **Write barrier, Yuasa style** (snapshot-at-the-beginning) | Pointer stores | Grey the overwritten referent; mark a superset | Snapshot: marks everything live at cycle start | Per-store check; floating garbage |

In executable form, the abstract marking loop is:

```python
def tricolor_mark(roots):
    grey = set(roots)          # wavefront: reached, not scanned
    black = set()              # fully scanned
    # every other object is implicitly white
    while grey:
        obj = grey.pop()
        for child in obj.references():
            if is_white(child):
                grey.add(child)     # white -> grey
        black.add(obj)              # grey -> black
    # grey empty: reclaim all white objects
    sweep_white_objects()
```

Every concurrent collector in Section 4.4 is this loop with the `while` body distributed across threads and the `is_white` / `references()` operations guarded by barriers.

### 4.2 Copying Collectors: Cheney's Scan and Baker's Real-Time Copying

Cheney's algorithm [2] is the tri-color loop made physical.

```python
def cheney_collect(roots):
    flip_semispaces()              # to-space becomes from-space and vice versa
    scan = free = to_space_base
    for r in roots:
        r.update(copy(r))          # roots point at to-space replicas
    while scan < free:             # grey set nonempty iff scan < free
        for field in scan.fields():
            field.update(copy(field.read()))
        scan += size(scan)         # grey -> black: advance the wavefront

def copy(obj):
    if obj.forwarded():
        return obj.forwarding_address()
    new_addr = free
    move_object(obj, new_addr)     # bitwise copy to to-space
    obj.set_forwarding(new_addr)
    free += size(obj)
    return new_addr
```

> **Theorem:** (Cheney scan completeness.) Cheney's breadth-first scan visits each reachable object exactly once, installs its forwarding address before any reference to the object is followed, and terminates with the to-space containing precisely the reachable objects in a compacted layout. [2]

*Proof sketch.* `copy` is idempotent via the forwarding check, so each reachable object is moved at most once; every field of every moved object is passed through `copy` when the scan pointer reaches it, so reachability is closed under the scan; termination follows because `scan` advances monotonically and `free` grows only by copying. ∎

Baker's 1978 breakthrough made this *incremental* [3].

His 1992 "treadmill" paper showed the same real-time structure needs no copying at all: link every object into one of four doubly-linked lists (white, grey, black, free) and cycle the *meaning* of the colors at each flip, using a read barrier to darken white cells the mutator touches [5].

| | Mark–sweep [1] | Copying (Cheney [2] / Baker [3]) |
|---|---|---|
| Compaction | None — fragmentation accumulates | Free — live data always compacted |
| Space overhead | Mark bits only | Half the heap idle (semispace) |
| Pause profile | Proportional to heap (classic) | Proportional to live set; constant per operation (Baker) |
| Allocation | Free-list search | Pointer bump — cheapest possible |
| Weakness | Fragmentation, sweep cost | Copies long-lived data repeatedly; needs forwarding/barriers |

### 4.3 Generational Collection: The Hypothesis, Card Tables, and Write Barriers

Copying's weakness — repeatedly copying long-lived objects — and mark–sweep's weakness — scanning the whole heap every cycle — are both addressed by one empirical observation, the **weak generational hypothesis**: *most objects die young*, so collecting the young objects frequently and the old objects rarely yields the most reclaimed bytes per unit of collector work [6][10].

The subtlety is the roots of a minor collection: pointers *from* old objects *to* young objects must be found without scanning the entire old generation.

```c
// Card-marking write barrier (conceptual)
void store_reference(Object **slot, Object *value) {
    *slot = value;
    if (is_old(slot) && is_young(value)) {
        card_table[card_index(slot)] = DIRTY;
    }
}
```

At the next minor collection, only dirty cards are scanned for old-to-young pointers.

> **Theorem:** (Generational completeness with remembered sets.) If the remembered set records every old-to-young pointer created since the last minor collection, and the minor collection scans the roots, the remembered set, and the nursery transitively, then it reclaims exactly the unreachable young objects. [6]

*Proof sketch.* Any path from a root to a young object either stays within the young generation or crosses from old to young; the remembered set covers all crossings by construction, and transitive scanning from those entry points covers the rest. ∎

### 4.4 Concurrent Low-Pause Collectors: Shenandoah, ZGC, and C4

The modern low-pause collectors all run the tri-color loop concurrently with the mutator and differ in *where they put the metadata* and *which barrier they pay for*.

**Shenandoah** [9] adds a **Brooks forwarding pointer** — an indirection word in every object header. GC threads concurrently evacuate regions, and the mutator's *read barrier* redirects every access through the forwarding pointer to the to-space replica. Stop-the-world pauses shrink to root scanning alone, independent of heap size; the price is one extra word per object and a barrier on the hot read path [9].

**ZGC** [10] takes the opposite approach: instead of a word *in* the object, it stores metadata *in the pointer itself*. **Colored pointers** stash mark/relocate/remap bits in unused address bits, and a **load barrier** inspects those bits on every reference load, healing stale pointers and triggering relocation work concurrently. Because no per-object header word is needed and barriers are simple bit tests, ZGC achieves pause times measured in *microseconds* that do not grow with heap or live-set size, scaling from megabytes to terabytes [10].

**C4**, Azul's continuously concurrent compacting collector [8], pioneered the commercial form of this design a decade earlier: a *loaded value barrier* (read barrier) on every reference load, concurrent marking, concurrent relocation, and concurrent remapping, with no stop-the-world fallback — compaction happens on every cycle, concurrently, even across generations simultaneously.

```rust
// Load-barrier fast path in the spirit of Shenandoah/ZGC: resolve
// the reference through its forwarding/color metadata before use.
fn load_barrier(obj: *const Object) -> *const Object {
    let bits = color_bits(obj);          // ZGC: metadata in the pointer
    if bits.needs_remap() {
        return slow_path_remap(obj);    // heal the pointer, maybe relocate
    }
    // Shenandoah equivalent: follow the Brooks forwarding pointer
    let fwd = read_forwarding_word(obj);
    if fwd != obj { return fwd; }
    obj
}
```

> **Theorem:** (Load-barrier forwarding correctness.) If every reference loaded by the mutator is resolved through the forwarding pointer (Brooks-style) or the color bits (ZGC-style) before dereference, and the collector never reuses a from-space page until all references into it have been remapped, then the mutator observes only the to-space replica and concurrent relocation is safe. [8][9][10]

*Proof sketch.* The barrier establishes the invariant that the mutator's *view* of the heap contains no from-space pointers; the collector's reclamation condition (remap-complete before reuse) ensures the replica the mutator sees is never freed beneath it. ∎

| Collector | Barrier | Pointer/object metadata | Generations | Pause goal | Heap scale |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shenandoah [9] | Read (load-reference) | Brooks forwarding word per object | Optional generational mode | Milliseconds, heap-independent | 100s of GB |
| ZGC [10] | Load barrier | Colored pointers (bits in address) | Generational (JEP 439) | Microseconds, heap-independent | Up to 16 TB |
| C4 [8] | Loaded value barrier (read) | Not-before/through LVB | Simultaneous generational | Sub-millisecond sustained | 100+ GB, high allocation |

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

Numbers discipline the design space. Representative published results, ordered by pause ambition:

| Collector | Typical max pause | Throughput vs. best | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parallel / Serial (stop-the-world) | Seconds on large heaps | Baseline (highest) | [6] |
| G1 | Milliseconds to seconds | Small loss vs. Parallel | [6] |
| Metronome | ~6 ms at 50% MMU; ~2 ms worst case reported | Moderate barrier/scheduling cost | [7] |
| Shenandoah | Single-digit ms, heap-independent | Trades CPU and space for pauses | [9] |
| C4 | Low ms sustained on 100+ GB heaps | Barrier + concurrency overhead | [8] |
| ZGC | Microseconds, heap-independent | ~15% vs. G1 on tested workloads | [10] |

Two methodological points matter. First, **MMU** [7] is the honest latency metric: Metronome's guarantee is not "pauses average 6 ms" but "in *every* window of the chosen duration, the mutator is guaranteed at least 50% of the CPU." Time-based scheduling — interleaving the collector at regular intervals rather than doing work proportional to allocation — is what makes such guarantees provable, and it is the decisive break from Baker's work-based pacing [7]. Second, the throughput column shows the triangle at work: ZGC's microsecond pauses cost roughly fifteen percent throughput against G1 on the workloads Oracle measured, and C4 and Shenandoah explicitly trade concurrent CPU cycles and space for pause-time improvements [8][9][10].

On the proof side, the snapshot-at-the-beginning discipline deserves its formal statement, since it underwrites the most widely deployed concurrent markers:

> **Theorem:** (SATB snapshot conservativeness.) With a Yuasa-style snapshot-at-the-beginning write barrier, the set of objects marked during a concurrent cycle is a superset of the objects live at the start of marking. Objects that die during marking ("floating garbage") are retained this cycle and reclaimed in the next. [6]

*Proof sketch.* The barrier greys the overwritten referent on every pointer store, so no object reachable at cycle start can become unreachable-without-being-marked: any path that existed at the start is preserved in the marked set even if the mutator later deletes it. ∎

The complementary Dijkstra-style barrier preserves the *strong* invariant instead: by greying the white target of every store into a black object, it guarantees no black-to-white edge ever persists, at the cost of possible re-scanning when the mutator undoes the collector's work [4][6].

## 6 Limitations

No collector escapes the triangle, and several hard limits deserve explicit statement.

- **Floating garbage and completeness lag.** Snapshot-based concurrent collectors deliberately retain garbage for a cycle [6]; on workloads with high turnover this inflates the floating-garbage fraction and forces larger heap headroom.
- **Fragmentation without compaction.** Non-moving collectors fragment; compacting collectors pay barrier and remapping costs. There is no free compaction [8].
- **The barrier tax.** Read barriers sit on the hottest path in the runtime — every reference load. ZGC's and C4's achievement is making that tax small, not zero [8][10].
- **Space overhead.** Semispace designs idle half the heap; forwarding words cost a word per object [9]; card tables cost roughly one byte per 512 bytes of heap; colored pointers consume address bits, constraining the usable address space [10].
- **Reference strengths.** Weak, soft, and phantom references plus finalizers interact badly with concurrent marking: an object must be kept alive until the collector *decides* it is dead, which serializes part of the cycle [6].
- **Root scanning remains.** Even Shenandoah and ZGC stop the world to scan thread stacks and global roots; eliminating that pause requires concurrent stack scanning, still an active engineering frontier [9][10].
- **Real-time needs reservations.** Bounded pauses require bounded allocation rates and schedulability analysis — time-based scheduling [7] only guarantees MMU if the application respects its allocation budget. A collector cannot manufacture real-time behavior from an unbounded workload.
- **Precision of roots.** Conservative collectors (notably for C and C++) cannot move objects safely, forfeiting compaction entirely [6].

## 7 Conclusion

The sixty-five-year arc from McCarthy's two-phase sweeper [1] to ZGC's microsecond pauses [10] is, at its core, the story of a single invariant — *no black-to-white edges* [4] — and the steadily shrinking cost of preserving it while the program runs. Reference counting failed on cycles; mark–sweep failed on fragmentation and whole-heap pauses; copying fixed fragmentation at the price of half the heap [2]; Baker made copying real-time with the read barrier [3]; generations exploited the mortality curve of objects [6][10]; and the modern concurrent collectors moved the barrier onto the load path and the metadata into the pointer itself [8][9][10].

The triangle endures: pause time is bought with throughput and space, and every row of the comparison tables is a receipt. What has changed is the exchange rate — microsecond pauses on terabyte heaps are now a production reality rather than a research dream. The open frontiers are clear: fully concurrent root scanning, sub-millisecond pauses as the *default* rather than the premium option, hardware assistance for barriers, and collectors that adapt their pacing to the application's allocation behavior automatically. The erasure problem McCarthy named in 1960 will not be solved — it will be managed, faster and more quietly, for the next sixty-five years.
