# Pauseless Garbage Collection: Colored Pointers in ZGC, Brooks Forwarding in Shenandoah, and the Lilliput Compact Object Headers Project

## Abstract

Pauselessness is the defining ambition of modern managed-runtime garbage collection: reclaim and compact a multi-terabyte heap without ever suspending application threads long enough to violate a service-level objective. This thesis examines the three flagship engineering programs that brought concurrent, compacting collection into the open-source HotSpot JVM and shrank the metadata it depends on. We analyze the *Z Garbage Collector* (ZGC), which encodes GC state in **colored pointers** and routes all correction through **load barriers** backed by multi-mapped virtual memory; *Shenandoah*, which adds a **Brooks forwarding pointer** to every object header and combines snapshot-at-the-beginning (SATB) marking with region-based concurrent evacuation; and Project **Lilliput** (JEP 450/JEP 519), which compresses the classic 96–128 bit object header into a single 64-bit word, cutting live-data footprint by 10–20% and forcing every concurrent collector to renegotiate where GC metadata lives. Using the formal invariants behind barriers, forwarding, and tri-color marking, we compare pause-time models, mutator overhead, and ISA constraints on x86-64 and AArch64, and conclude with measured results and the open problems that bound the sub-millisecond pause goal.

## 1. Introduction

The history of garbage collection is, in large measure, the history of shrinking the pause. Early *mark-compact* and *copying* collectors suspended the world for the entire collection; generational refinements shortened typical pauses but left worst-case behavior coupled to heap size and fragmentation [6]. For latency-sensitive services a single 200 ms pause can breach a 99.9th-percentile SLO, and that cost drove the first industrial *pauseless* designs. The landmark was Azul Systems' **C4** (Continuously Concurrent Compacting Collector) in the Zing JVM, presented by Click, Tene, and Wolf at ISMM 2011 [9], which compacted the heap while the mutator never stopped, using a *loaded value barrier* (LVB) and self-healing read barriers.

OpenJDK answered with two complementary open-source projects. **ZGC**, led by Per Lidén's team at Oracle, chose *colored pointers* and *load barriers*, turning every reference load into an opportunity for the collector to heal stale metadata, with sub-10 ms (and now sub-millisecond) pause goals independent of heap size [1]. **Shenandoah**, led by Red Hat (JEP 189), chose the older but battle-tested **Brooks forwarding pointer** (Brooks, 1969) placed in every object header, combined with SATB concurrent marking and region-based evacuation, accepting extra header cost in exchange for write-barrier-only overhead on the mutator [6][7].

Parallel to both, Project **Lilliput** attacked the *metadata density* problem: HotSpot object headers historically occupy 96–128 bits regardless of object size, and with average object sizes of 32–64 bytes, headers can consume more than 20% of the live heap. JEP 450 (JDK 24, experimental) and JEP 519 (JDK 25, product) fold the header into a single 64-bit word — forcing a redesign of where locking, GC, and forwarding state are stored [2][3].

This thesis unifies these three threads. Section 2 reviews the concurrent-collection fundamentals they share. Section 3 describes our comparative methodology. Section 4 dives into each mechanism: colored pointers and load barriers, Brooks pointers and evacuation, multi-mapping and generational evolution, and the Lilliput header. Section 5 presents empirical results and proof sketches of the core invariants; Section 6 maps the limitations that bound current designs.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Concurrent marking and the tri-color abstraction

All concurrent collectors in this family implement Dijkstra's *tri-color* abstraction: objects are white (unvisited), gray (visited but not scanned), or black (visited and scanned). The fundamental correctness challenge is that the mutator — the running application — can *hide* live objects from the collector by overwriting the last reference to them while marking is in progress. Two classical solutions exist: **Snapshot-At-The-Beginning (SATB)**, where every *store* passes through a *write barrier* recording the overwritten value into a SATB queue (guaranteeing snapshot-reachable objects are retained, at the cost of floating garbage), and **incremental update**, where every store records the *new* value.

> **Theorem (SATB correctness):** If every reference store during concurrent marking enqueues the overwritten referent, and every object allocated during marking is treated as live (allocation-black), then no object live at the end of marking is reclaimed. *Proof sketch:* An object is lost only if its last incoming edge is destroyed during marking; that destruction is exactly a store event, whose old referent enters the SATB queue and is therefore marked. ∎

Shenandoah uses SATB; ZGC instead performs *concurrent marking through load barriers*, where the act of loading a reference performs marking work.

### 2.2 Relocation and the forwarding problem

Compaction requires moving objects while the mutator holds raw references to them. The classic solution, due to Brooks (1969), adds an **indirection (forwarding) pointer** to each object: the mutator never uses the object's address directly for field access semantics; instead, a *barrier* resolves the forwarding pointer to the current copy. A concurrent copy then proceeds in three steps: allocate a new copy, atomically swing the forwarding pointer, and lazily let barriers heal references. Because the forwarding word is always the authoritative address, no mutator can observe a half-copied object.

ZGC avoids per-object forwarding words entirely. Its **colored pointers** encode metadata in the unused high bits of a 64-bit virtual address, and the collector *multi-maps* the physical heap — the same physical pages mapped into virtual memory multiple times, once per color view. A pointer's color selects its view; the *load barrier* remaps to the canonical physical address. This lets the collector reuse a region's memory *before* all pointers into it are healed, because stale-colored references still resolve to valid memory through their own view [1][8].

### 2.3 Barriers: load vs. store, read vs. write

A *barrier* is compiler/JIT-injected code on the reference access path:

| Collector | Barrier type | Fires on | Typical cost model |
|---|---|---|---|
| ZGC (non-generational) | Load barrier | Every reference load | High frequency, must be cheap (few instructions) |
| Generational ZGC | Load + store barriers | Loads + reference stores | Marking moved to stores; loads only fix relocation |
| Shenandoah | SATB + write barrier | Reference stores | Lower frequency; Brooks deref on access |
| C4 (Azul) | Loaded value barrier | Reference loads | Self-healing; page-protection traps |
| G1 (for contrast) | SATB + card marks | Stores | Region remembered sets |

The intuition is a frequency trade: loads vastly outnumber stores, so Shenandoah's write-barrier design has lower steady-state mutator overhead, while ZGC pays per-load cost for tighter, heap-size-independent pause bounds [7][8].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is an *analytic comparison* grounded in primary engineering artifacts rather than a single new experiment. Our method has three legs:

1. **Mechanism reconstruction** from design documents: the ZGC wiki and Generational ZGC notes [1][4], the Shenandoah JEP and wiki cycle description [6][7], and JEP 450/519 plus Lilliput performance data [2][3][5].
2. **Invariant formalization**: the core safety invariant of each mechanism (Brooks forwarding coherence, colored-pointer view consistency, SATB completeness) with Dijkstra-style proof sketches identifying which barrier events discharge each obligation.
3. **Quantitative comparison** of *pause-time models* (root-set vs. live-set vs. heap proportionality), *mutator overhead* (barrier frequency × cost), and *metadata density*, using ZGC's 10 ms → sub-1 ms trajectory [1], Shenandoah's per-phase pauses [7], and Lilliput's footprint and SPECjvm2008 results [5].

We deliberately avoid re-running benchmarks; JVM GC performance is notoriously machine-sensitive, and the published artifact data is more representative than any single-machine experiment.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 ZGC: Colored Pointers, Load Barriers, and Multi-Mapped Memory

ZGC's central insight is that a 64-bit virtual address has *spare bits*. On x86-64 and AArch64, user-space pointers use at most 48 significant bits (with extensions to 52/57 in newer hardware), leaving the top bits free for metadata. ZGC's classic (non-generational) layout on a 4 TB heap is:

```
bit 63          46 45 44 43 42 41                      0
 +---------------+---+---+---+---+---------------------+
 | (unused)      | M0| M1| R | F |  object offset      |
 +---------------+---+---+---+---+---------------------+
```

- **F (Finalizable):** the object needs finalizer processing.
- **R (Remapped):** the pointer has been healed to the current physical view.
- **M0/M1:** alternating mark colors for the current and previous marking cycle.

The collector maps every physical page multiple times into virtual memory, once per live color view. During relocation, live objects are copied to new regions; mutators holding stale-colored pointers are safe because their view still resolves to valid memory, and the **load barrier** heals them on next access:

```java
// Conceptual shape of the ZGC load barrier (JIT pseudocode)
oop load_barrier(oop ref) {
    if (is_colored_stale(ref)) {          // color != current good view
        ref = remap_to_good_view(ref);    // strip color, select good mapping
        if (object_was_relocated(ref))
            ref = forwarding_address(ref);
        mark_if_marking(ref);             // concurrent marking work
        set_color(ref, GOOD | REMAPPED);  // self-healing: future loads are cheap
    }
    return ref;
}
```

The self-healing property is crucial: after the first barrier fixes a reference, the color is updated so subsequent loads take the fast path. ZGC's cycle therefore has only *three* stop-the-world pauses — *Pause Mark Start*, *Pause Mark End*, *Pause Relocate Start* — each dominated by root scanning (thread stacks), hence proportional to the *root set*, not the heap or live set [1]. Everything else runs concurrently.

**Generational ZGC** (JEP 439, JDK 21; design notes in [4]) rebalanced this design by *moving marking work from load barriers to store barriers*: colored pointers gained generational metadata bits, a *store barrier* records inter-generational references into double-buffered remembered sets, and load barriers were slimmed to relocation healing. It also dropped multi-mapping in favor of dense regions and "relocations without additional heap memory."

### 4.2 Shenandoah: Brooks Pointers, SATB, and Region Evacuation

Shenandoah takes the opposite philosophical bet: pay a *fixed per-object cost* to make the common path simple. Every object header carries a **Brooks forwarding pointer** — one extra machine word — through which the mutator's access path dereferences:

```rust
// Conceptual read path through a Brooks pointer (illustrative Rust)
fn read_field(obj: *const ObjHeader) -> *const Obj {
    let fwd = unsafe { (*obj).brooks_ptr };
    let current = if is_evacuation_in_progress(fwd) { fwd } else { obj as *const Obj };
    unsafe { (*current).field }
}
```

> **Theorem (Brooks forwarding coherence):** During concurrent evacuation, every mutator access resolves through the forwarding word, and the forwarding word is swung by a single atomic CAS from the from-copy to the to-copy only after the to-copy is fully constructed. Therefore no mutator ever observes a partially copied object, and reads and writes to either copy are serialized through one canonical address. ∎

Shenandoah's cycle is region-based and phase-rich. A representative cycle [7]:

```
GC(3) Pause Init Mark 0.771ms
GC(3) Concurrent marking 76480M->77212M(102400M) 633.213ms
GC(3) Pause Final Mark 1.821ms
GC(3) Concurrent cleanup 77224M->66592M(102400M) 3.112ms
GC(3) Concurrent evacuation 66592M->75640M(102400M) 405.312ms
GC(3) Pause Init Update Refs 0.084ms
GC(3) Concurrent update references 75700M->76424M(102400M) 354.341ms
GC(3) Pause Final Update Refs 0.409ms
GC(3) Concurrent cleanup 77244M->56620M(102400M) 12.242ms
```

The three-to-five pauses (Init Mark, Final Mark, Init/Final Update Refs) are all root-scanning dominated — typically 0.1–2 ms — while marking, evacuation, and reference updating run concurrently [7]. SATB write barriers (recording overwritten referents) discharge the tri-color correctness proof; the evacuation's correctness rests on the Brooks invariant. The costs are a Brooks word per object and a *degenerate* fallback — under extreme allocation pressure Shenandoah can fall back to a full stop-the-world collection [7].

### 4.3 Multi-Mapping, Region Evacuation, and the ISA Boundary

Both collectors are *region-based* (ZGC's *ZPages* of 2 MB/32 MB/large sizes; Shenandoah's uniformly-sized regions), which decouples pause work from total heap size: a cycle evacuates a *collection set* of regions, not the whole heap. But their relocation metadata lives in opposite places — ZGC in the *pointer*, Shenandoah in the *object* — and that choice interacts sharply with the instruction set architecture.

On **x86-64**, the top bits of a pointer are genuinely unused (canonical-form addressing), so colored pointers are nearly free to decode: masking is a single `AND`. On **AArch64**, the architecture's *Top-Byte-Ignore* (TBI) feature similarly permits metadata in bits [56:64), which both ZGC's AArch64 port and memory-tagging extensions exploit; ZGC has shipped on Linux/AArch64 since JDK 13 [1]. The deeper constraint is *address-space reservation*: classic ZGC multi-maps the heap once per color view, reserving several times the heap size in virtual address space — harmless on 64-bit but the reason generational ZGC abandoned multi-mapping [4].

Region evacuation is where the pause models diverge subtly. ZGC needs no per-object forwarding word because the *load barrier + view remap* plays that role; the price is that *every* reference load must at least test the color. Shenandoah's write barrier fires only on stores, but every object pays 8 bytes forever, and evacuation must *update all references* in a dedicated concurrent phase, whereas ZGC heals references lazily through barriers.

### 4.4 Lilliput: Compact Object Headers and the Metadata Squeeze

Project Lilliput reframes the entire discussion: GC metadata has a *density* cost. In HotSpot, object headers historically occupy 96–128 bits regardless of object size, and with average object sizes of 32–64 bytes, headers can consume more than 20% of the live heap [2].

JEP 450 compresses this into a single 64-bit word (experimental in JDK 24 via `-XX:+UseCompactObjectHeaders`; productized by JEP 519 in JDK 25, and slated to become the default) [2][3]:

```
bit 63                                            0  (LSB -> MSB)
 +--------+----------+---------------+------------------+
 | unused | hash 25b | klass 22b     | lock 2b + GC 5b  |
 +--------+----------+---------------+------------------+
   (10b, incl. 4b reserved for Valhalla)
```

Concretely: 2 bits of locking state plus 5 more bits of GC-internal state (7 bits total), a 22-bit compressed class pointer, and a 25-bit identity hash code — 54 bits used, 10 reserved, four already earmarked for Project Valhalla's value-object flags [2]. Locking state migrated to *object monitor tables* (side tables) so the header no longer needs to inflate in place.

The measured payoff: Lilliput experiments report **10–20% reductions in live data size**, with SPECjvm2008 runs on the JEP 450 prototype mostly neutral-to-positive (Sunflow +5.13% with compact headers; CryptoRsa −1.7%, a known regression under investigation) [5]. The structural implication for pauseless collectors: with only 7 bits for GC/locking state, there is *no room* for a Brooks word in the header, so Shenandoah-style forwarding must move to side tables, while ZGC's pointer-side metadata is unaffected — a quiet vindication of keeping GC state in the pointer rather than the object.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Pause-time models

The table below summarizes what each design's pauses are *proportional to*:

| Collector | # STW pauses per cycle | Each pause ∝ | Heap-size independent? |
|---|---|---|---|
| ZGC (non-generational) | 3 | Root-set size (thread stacks) | Yes |
| Generational ZGC | 3 (+ young-only cycles) | Root-set size | Yes |
| Shenandoah | 3–5 | Root-set size (stack scan) | Yes, barring Degenerated GC |
| G1 (contrast) | 2+ (young + mixed) | Live set of collected regions | Partially |

ZGC's published goal trajectory: pause times not exceeding **10 ms**, not increasing with heap or live-set size (8 MB to 16 TB), with ongoing work targeting **sub-millisecond** maxima independent of heap, live-set, *and root-set* size [1]. Shenandoah's pauses in the published cycle are sub-2 ms per root-scanning phase on a 100 GB heap [7] — the design meets its "handful of milliseconds" goal whenever the concurrent phases keep up with allocation.

### 5.2 Proof: colored-pointer view consistency

> **Theorem (ZGC view consistency):** Let physical page *P* be mapped at virtual views *V₁…Vₖ*, one per color. An object relocated from *P* to *Q* while mutators hold color-*c* references into *P* is never observed torn or freed prematurely. *Proof sketch:* The old view *V_c(P)* is unmapped only after (a) all GC roots have been remapped and (b) a load-barrier epoch guarantees no mutator can still materialize a *c*-colored reference — because every load path remaps through the barrier, any reference a mutator *uses* is healed before dereference. Physical reuse of *P* therefore happens only when no reachable colored pointer can name it. ∎

This is why ZGC can reclaim a region's memory *before* every stale pointer is healed — the central trick of the original ZGC proposal [8] — and why the load barrier, not a remembered set, discharges the proof.

### 5.3 Proof: SATB + Brooks evacuation composition (Shenandoah)

> **Theorem:** Shenandoah's SATB marking plus Brooks-pointer evacuation never reclaims a live object and never exposes a torn copy. *Proof sketch:* (i) By the SATB theorem of §2.1, the marked set is a superset of objects live at mark end. (ii) By Brooks coherence (§4.2), mutator accesses during evacuation resolve to a complete copy. (iii) The *update references* phase rewrites every field and root to the to-copy; the final pause re-scans roots, closing the race where a mutator loaded a from-reference after the concurrent update pass. ∎

### 5.4 Throughput and footprint measurements

No pauseless design is free. ZGC's original design target accepted up to ~15% throughput reduction versus G1 on some workloads [8], the price of per-load barriers; generational ZGC's store-barrier rebalancing directly attacks this cost [4]. Shenandoah's write barriers are cheaper per-event, but every object carries the Brooks word. Lilliput's 10–20% footprint reduction [2] compounds with either collector: smaller heaps mean fewer regions to evacuate and better cache locality of the object graph.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Barrier tax is workload-dependent.** Load barriers penalize pointer-chasing, allocation-light workloads most; generational ZGC's shift of marking to store barriers acknowledges the original all-load-barrier design overpaid there [4].
2. **ISA and ABI constraints.** Colored pointers need unused high bits and OS cooperation for multi-mapping; architectures without generous virtual space or TBI-like features cannot host the design. Pointer masking also interacts with compressed oops and JNI code manipulating raw addresses.
3. **Metadata bit scarcity.** Lilliput's 64-bit header leaves 7 bits for locking+GC; Shenandoah's Brooks word no longer fits, and Valhalla has claimed 4 of the 10 reserved bits [2]. Future GC features must live in side tables or pointer bits.
4. **Degenerate fallbacks.** Shenandoah's concurrent evacuation can lose the race against allocation, degenerating to a full STW collection [7]; ZGC avoids this but pays with stricter demands on barrier performance and heap headroom.
5. **Pause ∝ root set.** Both collectors' remaining pauses scale with thread-stack scanning. ZGC's sub-millisecond roadmap targets root-set independence via concurrent stack scanning [1]; applications with tens of thousands of threads remain the worst case.
6. **Correctness proof burden.** Each new barrier optimization re-opens the formal argument, and production confidence ultimately comes from years of stress testing, not theorems.

## 7. Conclusion

Pauseless garbage collection in OpenJDK was achieved not by one idea but by three complementary ones. **ZGC** showed that GC metadata can live in the *pointer*: colored pointers plus multi-mapped memory turn every load into a healing event and reduce pauses to root-scanning. **Shenandoah** showed that metadata can live in the *object*: a Brooks forwarding word makes concurrent evacuation almost boringly correct, at the cost of a write barrier and an extra header word. **Lilliput** showed that metadata has a *density* cost worth attacking directly: a 64-bit header reclaims a fifth of the heap and forces both collectors to reckon with where their state lives.

The trajectory is clear: pause goals from 10 ms toward sub-millisecond; headers from 128 bits to 64; marking migrating between load and store barriers as workloads dictate. The remaining frontier — concurrent root scanning, Valhalla's bit demands, and workload-adaptive barrier strategies — will decide whether "pauseless" becomes a property applications can assume rather than a feature they must tune.
