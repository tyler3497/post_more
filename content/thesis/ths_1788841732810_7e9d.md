# Raft Consensus in Production: Leader Election, Log Replication Safety, and Principled Optimizations Beyond Paxos

## Abstract

Raft has become the dominant consensus protocol in production, powering etcd, TiKV, CockroachDB, and Consul, largely because decomposing consensus into *leader election*, *log replication*, and *safety* makes it tractable to implement correctly [1][2]. This thesis presents a rigorous treatment of Raft: we formalize the election-safety and state-machine-safety invariants, prove why the election restriction — a candidate must hold an up-to-date log — is the load-bearing innovation behind single-term commit, and analyze the joint-consensus reconfiguration procedure that preserves safety across membership changes. We examine production-hardening mechanisms absent from the original paper: PreVote against disruptive servers, log compaction via snapshotting, and optimizations including request batching, AppendEntries pipelining, and read-only fast paths. Comparing Raft with Paxos and reviewing the machine-checked TLA+ specification [3], we argue Raft's true contribution is methodological — consensus made *understandable* without sacrificing rigor — and identify remaining frontiers: Byzantine tolerance, reconfiguration liveness, and sub-millisecond commit latency.

## 1. Introduction

Consensus — getting a set of unreliable machines to agree on a single sequence of values despite crashes — is the foundational problem of distributed systems. The classical solution, Paxos [4], is notoriously difficult to understand, implement, and extend; Lamport himself noted that "Paxos is simple" only after years of failed attempts to explain it, and the engineering literature is littered with systems that implemented Paxos subtly wrong [5]. Raft was designed explicitly as an *understandable* alternative: by decomposing consensus into three relatively independent subproblems — leader election, log replication, and safety — and by constraining the protocol so that the leader's log is always authoritative, Raft reduces the state space an implementer must reason about [1].

The stakes of getting this right are high. Modern infrastructure depends on Raft-based systems: Kubernetes stores all cluster state in etcd [6], TiKV and CockroachDB replicate transactional data with Raft groups, and Consul uses Raft for service discovery state. A safety violation — two servers applying different commands at the same log index — would mean divergent databases, split-brain writes, and data loss. This thesis therefore treats Raft not as folklore but as a formal object: we state its invariants precisely, sketch the proofs, analyze the reconfiguration protocol that the original paper's readers most often misunderstand, and survey the optimizations that separate a textbook implementation from one that survives production.

> **Thesis claim:** Raft achieves its understandability not by weakening consensus but by *restricting the leader's power* — the election restriction guarantees that any elected leader's log contains every committed entry, which collapses the commit rule to a single-term majority check and makes the entire safety argument inductive over terms.

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the system model and Paxos background. Section 3 presents the core protocol and its formal safety argument. Section 4 — the deep dive — analyzes leader election dynamics, the log matching property, joint-consensus reconfiguration, and log compaction. Section 5 surveys empirical results and production optimizations. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 System Model

We assume the standard asynchronous crash-recovery model: a fixed set of *N = 2f + 1* servers communicate over an unreliable network that may drop, delay, duplicate, or reorder messages but not corrupt them undetectably (checksums are assumed). Servers may crash and later recover with stable storage intact; Byzantine (arbitrary) behavior is explicitly out of scope — Raft is a *crash-fault-tolerant* (CFT) protocol, and Byzantine fault tolerance requires fundamentally different machinery [7].

Time is divided into *terms*, numbered with monotonically increasing integers. Each term begins with an election; at most one leader is elected per term, though network partitions may cause terms to pass with no leader at all. Servers are always in one of three states — **leader**, **follower**, or **candidate** — and the term number acts as a logical clock: any message carrying a stale term is rejected, which is the protocol's primary defense against split-brain.

### 2.2 Why Paxos Was Not Enough

Paxos [4] solves consensus with optimal message complexity and a minimal set of assumptions, but its opaqueness is legendary. The core difficulty is that Paxos has no distinguished leader in its single-decree form: every proposer may drive any instance, so the protocol's safety argument requires reasoning about ballot numbers across *all* proposers simultaneously. Multi-Paxos adds a stable leader, but the literature never converged on a canonical, complete specification — "Paxos Made Live" [5] documents how Google's Chubby team discovered that the published algorithm omits critical details: master leases, exactly-once semantics under failover, snapshot transfer, and membership changes were all engineered from scratch, and several early implementations contained safety bugs.

Raft's insight is that these engineering concerns should be *part of the protocol specification*, not left as exercises. Leader election is a first-class subproblem with its own timeouts and RPCs; log replication has explicit consistency checks; membership change has a defined two-phase procedure. The TLA+ specification [3] — roughly 200 lines — has been model-checked for small configurations, giving Raft a formal foundation that Multi-Paxos folklore never had.

## 3. Methodology: The Core Protocol and Its Safety Argument

### 3.1 Protocol Sketch

Each server maintains a persistent **log** of entries, where each entry carries a *(term, index)* pair and a state-machine command. The leader accepts client requests, appends them to its log, and replicates them via `AppendEntries` RPCs; once an entry is stored on a majority of servers, the leader marks it **committed**, applies it to its state machine, and notifies followers through subsequent RPCs. Leaders are elected via `RequestVote` RPCs: a follower that hears nothing for a randomized *election timeout* (typically 150–300 ms) becomes a candidate, increments its term, votes for itself, and requests votes. A candidate wins with a majority.

The two RPCs carry the protocol's entire safety logic:

```go
type AppendEntriesArgs struct {
    Term         int        // leader's term
    LeaderId     int
    PrevLogIndex int        // index of log entry immediately preceding new ones
    PrevLogTerm  int        // term of PrevLogIndex entry
    Entries      []LogEntry // entries to store (empty for heartbeat)
    LeaderCommit int        // leader's commitIndex
}

type RequestVoteArgs struct {
    Term         int // candidate's term
    CandidateId  int
    LastLogIndex int // index of candidate's last log entry
    LastLogTerm  int // term of candidate's last log entry
}
```

### 3.2 The Log Matching Property and Leader Completeness

Two invariants carry the safety proof [1]:

> **Theorem (Log Matching):** If two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, then the logs are identical in all entries up through that index.

This follows by induction from the consistency check: `AppendEntries` is rejected unless the follower's log contains an entry at `PrevLogIndex` with term `PrevLogTerm`, so any accepted entry's entire prefix matches the leader's.

> **Theorem (Leader Completeness):** If a log entry is committed in a given term, then that entry will be present in the logs of the leaders of all higher-numbered terms.

Leader Completeness is where Raft's *election restriction* does its work. A voter grants its vote only if the candidate's log is **at least as up-to-date** as its own, where "up-to-date" is compared first by last-entry term, then by log length. Because any committed entry sits on a majority of servers, and any election-winning candidate must collect votes from a majority, the two majorities intersect — and the intersecting server, by the election restriction, forces the candidate's log to contain the committed entry. This is the crux: *the election restriction, not the commit rule, is what makes Raft safe*.

The commit rule then becomes simple: the leader may advance its `commitIndex` for entries of its *current term* once they are stored on a majority. Entries from prior terms are committed indirectly, by committing a current-term entry that follows them — a subtle point, and the source of a famous counterexample (Figure 8 in [1]) showing why committing old-term entries by counting replicas alone is unsafe.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Leader Election Dynamics: Why Randomization Matters

A naive election scheme — fixed timeouts, first candidate wins — suffers from *split votes*: if several followers time out simultaneously, each votes for itself, no majority forms, and the term is wasted. Raft's fix is disarmingly simple: each follower picks its election timeout uniformly at random from an interval (e.g., [150, 300] ms) [1]. The candidate with the shortest timeout starts first, and because RPC round trips (~1 ms in a datacenter) are far shorter than the timeout spread, it typically secures a majority before others wake.

```tla
\* TLA+ sketch: a server becomes candidate only after its randomized timeout fires
BecomeCandidate(i) ==
    /\ state[i] = "follower"
    /\ electionTimer[i] = 0
    /\ state' = [state EXCEPT ![i] = "candidate"]
    /\ currentTerm' = [currentTerm EXCEPT ![i] = currentTerm[i] + 1]
    /\ votedFor' = [votedFor EXCEPT ![i] = i]
```

Formally, with *n* servers and timeout interval width *W* against broadcast latency *δ*, the probability of a split vote is bounded by roughly *n·δ/W*, which is negligible for datacenter *δ* — but the analysis degrades on WANs, which is why production systems tune or disable elections across regions. Measurements on the etcd Raft implementation show median election completion under 10 ms in-cluster, with the 99th percentile dominated by the randomized timeout draw itself [6].

### 4.2 The Consistency Check as an Inductive Invariant

The `PrevLogIndex`/`PrevLogTerm` check is more than an optimization — it is the mechanism that maintains Log Matching across leader changes. When a new leader takes over, its log may disagree with a follower's; the leader then *decrements* `nextIndex` for that follower and retries, walking backward until the logs converge at a matching prefix, then overwriting the divergent suffix. Because the leader's log is authoritative (Leader Completeness), this forced convergence never destroys a committed entry.

This repair procedure is O(log length) in the worst case, and naive implementations suffer when a partitioned follower returns with a long divergent tail. Production libraries accelerate it with *conflict-term probing*: the follower rejects with the term and first index of the conflicting entry, letting the leader skip entire terms in one round trip — an optimization from the etcd Raft library's `MsgAppResp` rejection hints [6].

### 4.3 Joint Consensus: Safe Membership Changes

Changing cluster membership is where naive consensus implementations most often break safety: if the system switches directly from configuration *C_old* to *C_new*, the two majorities may not overlap, and two leaders can be elected simultaneously in the same term. Raft's solution is **joint consensus**, a two-phase procedure [1][2]:

| Phase | Configuration in log | Majorities required |
|---|---|---|
| Stable | *C_old* | majority of *C_old* |
| Intermediate | *C_old,new* (joint) | majority of *C_old* **and** majority of *C_new* |
| Final | *C_new* | majority of *C_new* |

During the joint phase, elections and commits require majorities of *both* configurations, so any two majorities — old or new — intersect. Safety is preserved by the same quorum-intersection argument as the base protocol. Once *C_old,new* is committed, the leader appends *C_new* and replicates it; servers not in *C_new* step down once they observe it.

The subtlety implementers miss: a removed leader in *C_old* that is absent from *C_new* may still disrupt the cluster by forcing spurious elections before it learns of its removal. Raft handles this by having such servers step down upon receiving `AppendEntries` with the new configuration, and modern implementations add PreVote (Section 5.2) as a second line of defense.

Recent work has questioned whether joint consensus is the *simplest* correct approach — single-server changes can be done with a one-phase protocol [8] — but joint consensus remains the general, proven mechanism, and its TLA+ specification has been model-checked for configurations up to five servers [3].

### 4.4 Log Compaction and Snapshotting

An ever-growing log is operationally untenable: a recovering server would need to replay from genesis, and storage would grow without bound. Raft compacts via **snapshotting**: each server periodically serializes its state machine, discards log entries preceding the snapshot, and records the snapshot's last included index and term. A lagging follower that has fallen behind the discarded prefix receives an `InstallSnapshot` RPC containing the full snapshot instead of incremental entries [1].

The correctness subtlety is that the snapshot's *(lastIncludedIndex, lastIncludedTerm)* pair must participate in the Log Matching invariant exactly as a log entry would — otherwise a leader could not verify consistency against a snapshotted follower. The Raft dissertation proves that treating the snapshot metadata as a synthetic log entry preserves all invariants [2]. In practice, snapshot generation must be *non-blocking* (copy-on-write or fork-based) to avoid stalling the state machine, and chunked transfer is needed so that a multi-gigabyte snapshot does not monopolize the replication stream — both are implemented in etcd's v3 snapshot machinery [6].

### 4.5 Performance Optimizations: Batching, Pipelining, and Fast Reads

A textbook Raft implementation commits one client request per two RPC round trips (propose + majority ack). Production systems layer several optimizations, each of which must be proved not to weaken safety:

- **Request batching.** The leader buffers client proposals for a few milliseconds and replicates them in a single `AppendEntries`. This amortizes RPC overhead and is safety-neutral: batching only changes *when* entries are proposed, never the commit rule. Throughput gains of 5–10× are typical [6].
- **AppendEntries pipelining.** Instead of waiting for one batch's acknowledgment before sending the next, the leader streams entries and tracks in-flight indices per follower. Correctness holds because the consistency check is per-RPC: a rejection still triggers the decrement-and-retry repair, which now may need to walk back across multiple in-flight batches. Implementations bound in-flight bytes to cap the repair cost.
- **Read-only fast path.** Linearizable reads need not go through the log. The leader verifies it is still leader (heartbeat round to a majority, or a *lease* on its leadership) and serves the read from its state machine. The lease-based variant trades a bounded staleness assumption (clock skew) for single-RTT reads; etcd implements the quorum-check variant to avoid clock dependence [6].

| Optimization | Latency effect | Safety argument |
|---|---|---|
| Batching | amortizes RPC cost; +5–10× throughput | commit rule unchanged |
| Pipelining | hides RTT; higher throughput | per-RPC consistency check preserved |
| Read fast path | 1 RTT vs 2 RTT | leader-validity check replaces log commit |
| PreVote | fewer disruptions | never grants real votes |

## 5. Empirical Results and Production Evidence

The strongest empirical evidence for Raft is not a benchmark but an adoption curve. The etcd Raft library — a faithful Go implementation of [1] with the production extensions above — underpins Kubernetes, where every cluster's control plane depends on it [6]. TiKV implements *Multi-Raft*, partitioning data into thousands of small Raft groups to parallelize replication, and reports linear throughput scaling with group count. CockroachDB layers distributed transactions atop Raft-replicated ranges.

On the verification side, the TLA+ specification of Raft [3] has been model-checked with TLC for configurations up to 3–5 servers, checking the Election Safety and State Machine Safety invariants across millions of states. While model checking is not a proof, the specification's small size — the core algorithm is under 200 lines of TLA+ — means the checked state space covers the protocol's interesting interleavings, and several bugs in early drafts were caught by the model checker before publication [2].

Comparative studies of Paxos-family protocols consistently find Raft's *implementation defect rate* lower: the "Paxos Made Live" experience [5] — months of debugging membership, snapshots, and exactly-once semantics — maps directly onto subproblems Raft specifies up front. EPaxos [7] achieves higher throughput than Raft under low contention by eliminating the leader bottleneck, but at the cost of a far more intricate dependency-tracking protocol; the engineering consensus has largely favored Raft's simplicity over EPaxos's peak performance for strongly consistent metadata stores.

## 6. Limitations

Raft's limitations are well-defined consequences of its design choices, and honesty about them matters more than advocacy:

1. **No Byzantine tolerance.** A single malicious server can violate safety arbitrarily. Systems facing adversarial networks need PBFT-family protocols [7], which Raft cannot approximate — the quorum-intersection arguments assume honest majorities.
2. **Leader bottleneck.** All writes flow through the leader, capping throughput at one server's network capacity and adding a WAN RTT to every commit in geo-distributed deployments. Leaderless protocols (EPaxos [7]) or multi-leader schemes address this at significant complexity cost.
3. **Reconfiguration liveness.** Joint consensus preserves *safety* during membership changes, but liveness can stall if the old leader is partitioned: the cluster may be unable to commit *C_old,new* until an election succeeds, and edge cases in leader transfer during reconfiguration have produced real bugs in production libraries.
4. **Election disruptions.** Without PreVote, a partitioned server with a higher term can repeatedly depose a healthy leader upon rejoining. PreVote mitigates but does not eliminate the class: it converts disruptive elections into harmless pre-elections only when the candidate cannot reach a majority.
5. **Timing assumptions.** Raft guarantees safety asynchronously but needs *partial synchrony* for liveness — broadcast latency must be well below the election timeout. On unpredictable networks, operators must widen timeouts, trading failover speed for stability, and there is no fully satisfactory auto-tuning scheme in wide deployment.

## 7. Conclusion

Raft's enduring contribution is not a faster consensus algorithm but a *better-specified* one. By elevating leader election, log replication, and membership change to first-class, formally specified subproblems — and by discovering the election restriction that makes the safety proof inductive over terms — Ongaro and Ousterhout produced a protocol that working engineers can implement, test, and extend with confidence [1][2]. The production record of etcd, TiKV, and CockroachDB [6] validates that understandability is not the enemy of rigor but its vehicle: the TLA+ specification [3] exists precisely because the protocol was designed to be written down completely.

The frontiers are clear. Marrying Raft's understandability with Byzantine tolerance, eliminating the leader bottleneck without EPaxos-level complexity, and making reconfiguration live under partitions remain open engineering problems. Whatever supersedes Raft will likely inherit its deepest lesson: that a consensus protocol is a *human artifact* first, and its most important property may be that a competent engineer can hold the entire safety argument in their head at once.
