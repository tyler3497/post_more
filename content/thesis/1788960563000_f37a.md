# Fine-Grained Complexity Theory: the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis, Orthogonal Vectors, APSP Conjectures, and Reductions within P

## Abstract

Fine-grained complexity theory replaces the coarse polynomial-versus-exponential dichotomy of classical complexity with a quantitative calculus of conditional lower bounds *within* polynomial time. Anchored on three widely believed but unproven conjectures — the **Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH)**, the **APSP conjecture**, and the **3-SUM conjecture** — it has produced an intricate web of reductions explaining why the naive running times of dozens of fundamental problems (edit distance, longest common subsequence, all-pairs shortest paths, Fréchet distance, graph diameter) appear optimal. This thesis gives a self-contained, doctoral-level account of the framework. We define fine-grained reductions, prove the central Williams reduction from $k$-SAT to $k$-Orthogonal Vectors via the split-and-list gadget, trace the OV-to-sequence-problem reduction web of Backurs–Indyk and Abboud–Backurs–Vassilevska Williams, and develop the subcubic equivalence class of Williams & Williams linking APSP, negative-triangle detection, and $(\min,+)$-matrix product. We survey best-known algorithms that nearly match the conjectured bounds, discuss the non-uniform circuit lower bounds implied by the Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture, and close with the central open question: proving any *unconditional* super-quadratic lower bound in a natural computational model.

---

## 1 Introduction

Classical complexity theory is remarkably successful at separating *easy* problems from *hard* ones: $P \ne NP$ conditional hardness explains why travelling salesman admits no polynomial-time algorithm. Yet it is nearly silent on a question of equal practical importance: among problems already known to be solvable in polynomial time, which ones can be solved *faster*? Why has the naive $O(n^3)$ algorithm for all-pairs shortest paths (APSP) resisted improvement for over half a century? Why does edit distance still require quadratic time despite decades of effort? The polynomial hierarchy gives no leverage here — these problems are all firmly in $P$.

**Fine-grained complexity theory**, crystallized over the last fifteen years, addresses this gap by adopting precise running-time conjectures as axioms and propagating them through a refined notion of reduction. Three conjectures serve as the load-bearing pillars [8]:

1. **SETH** (Impagliazzo–Paturi [1]): $k$-SAT on $n$ variables requires $2^{(1-o(1))n}$ time for unbounded $k$; equivalently, no $O(2^{(1-\varepsilon)n})$ algorithm exists for any fixed $\varepsilon > 0$ once $k$ is large enough.
2. **The APSP conjecture** (Williams & Williams [5]): there is no $O(n^{3-\varepsilon})$-time algorithm, for any $\varepsilon > 0$, for all-pairs shortest paths on $n$-node graphs with integer edge weights in $[-M, M]$.
3. **The 3-SUM conjecture** (Gajentaan–Overmars): no $O(n^{2-\varepsilon})$-time algorithm decides whether three integers from a set of $n$ sum to zero.

From these axioms, hundreds of conditional lower bounds have been derived, forming three great "hardness hubs": **quadratic** (under SETH, centered on Orthogonal Vectors), **cubic** (under APSP, centered on min-plus product), and **quadratic for 3-SUM-flavored problems**. This thesis develops the theory rigorously, emphasizing the reduction machinery that transfers hardness between problems while preserving the *exact exponent* of the running time.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From NP-hardness to fine-grained hardness

A classical Karp reduction maps instances of problem $A$ to instances of problem $B$ in polynomial time, transferring membership in $P$. Fine-grained reductions must do more: they must transfer the *precise exponent* of the conjectured optimal running time. Let $T_1(n), T_2(n)$ be time bounds. We say $(A, T_1) \le_{FGR} (B, T_2)$ if, roughly, a $T_2^{1-\varepsilon}$-time algorithm for $B$ would yield a $T_1^{1-\delta}$-time algorithm for $A$, for some $\delta > 0$ depending on $\varepsilon$ [8]. Concretely, the reduction runs in $T_1^{1-\delta}$ time and issues oracle calls to $B$ on instances whose *total* size is controlled so that no call dominates the source bound.

> **Theorem:** (Williams [2]) Assume SETH. Then for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $c \ge 1$ such that Orthogonal Vectors on $n$ vectors in dimension $d = c\log n$ requires $n^{2-\varepsilon}$ time.

This single theorem is the seed from which an enormous reduction web grows.

### 2.2 The Orthogonal Vectors problem

**Orthogonal Vectors (OV).** *Given:* two sets $A, B \subseteq \{0,1\}^d$ with $|A| = |B| = n$. *Determine:* do there exist $a \in A$, $b \in B$ with $\langle a, b\rangle = \sum_{j=1}^{d} a_j b_j = 0$?

The naive algorithm checks all $n^2$ pairs in $O(n^2 d)$ time. Folklore algorithms run in $\tilde{O}(n + 2^d)$ time via fast zeta transforms, so OV is easy when $d = o(\log n)$; the hard regime is $d = \omega(\log n)$, and the conjecture is stated for $d = c\log n$ with $c$ growing as $\varepsilon \to 0$ [7].

The **Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture (OVC)** states precisely what the theorem above asserts: no universal $\varepsilon > 0$ admits an $n^{2-\varepsilon}$-time algorithm for all constants $c$. SETH *implies* OVC, but OVC is strictly weaker — it is conceivable that SETH fails while OVC holds, which is why many modern results are stated under OVC directly [7].

### 2.3 The APSP conjecture and subcubic equivalence

APSP on dense graphs is solvable in $O(n^3)$ by Floyd–Warshall. The APSP conjecture asserts no $O(n^{3-\varepsilon})$ algorithm exists, even for integer weights. Williams & Williams [5] showed this conjecture is *equivalent* (under subcubic reductions, which preserve the exponent $3$ up to arbitrarily small $\varepsilon$) to a remarkable list of problems:

- detecting a negative-weight triangle in a weighted graph,
- verifying a $(\min,+)$-matrix product,
- finding a minimum-weight cycle,
- the replacement-paths problem,
- checking metricity of a distance matrix.

The $(\min,+)$-product $C = A \star B$, defined by $C[i,j] = \min_k (A[i,k] + B[k,j])$, is the algebraic heart of the class: APSP is exactly the $(\min,+)$-closure of the weight matrix, and repeated squaring computes it.

### 2.4 3-SUM

The 3-SUM problem — decide whether $a + b + c = 0$ for elements of an $n$-element set — admits an $O(n^2)$ algorithm and is conjectured to admit no $n^{2-\varepsilon}$ algorithm. It underpins hardness for computational-geometry problems (e.g., point-on-line queries, polygon containment), forming a third pillar largely orthogonal to the SETH and APSP webs [8].

## 3 Methodology

Our method throughout is *conditional and exact*: assume a conjectured time lower bound for a hub problem, construct a reduction to a target problem that is *fine-grained* — i.e., it transforms an $n$-size instance of the hub into instances of the target of total size $\tilde{O}(n)$ in sub-$T_1$ time — and read off a matching conditional lower bound for the target.

The central gadget technique is the **split-and-list** construction of Williams [2]: split the $n$ variables of a $k$-SAT formula into two halves, enumerate all $2^{n/2}$ partial assignments on each side, and encode each partial assignment as a vector whose coordinates record *which clauses are left unsatisfied*. Two partial assignments are jointly satisfying exactly when their vectors are orthogonal. We formalize this below with executable pseudocode.

To make the theory falsifiable, we also survey the best-known *algorithms* that nearly match the conjectured bounds: for OV in $d = c\log n$, algorithms running in $n^{2 - 1/O(\log c)}$ time (Abboud–Williams–Yu; Chan–Williams), showing the conjecture's quantitative form is nearly tight [7]; and for APSP, Williams' $n^3 / 2^{\Omega(\sqrt{\log n})}$-time algorithm, which beats $n^3$ by a subpolynomial factor [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 SETH implies OV hardness: the split-and-list reduction

Let $\varphi$ be a $k$-CNF formula on variables $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with $m$ clauses $C_1, \dots, C_m$. Partition variables into $L = \{x_1, \dots, x_{n/2}\}$ and $R = \{x_{n/2+1}, \dots, x_n\}$. For each partial assignment $u \in \{0,1\}^{n/2}$ to $L$, define the vector $\mathrm{vec}(u) \in \{0,1\}^m$ by

$$\mathrm{vec}(u)_j = 1 - \mathrm{sat}(u, C_j),$$

where $\mathrm{sat}(u, C_j) = 1$ iff some literal of $C_j$ is satisfied by $u$ alone. Define $\mathrm{vec}(v)$ analogously for $v$ a partial assignment to $R$. Then $\langle \mathrm{vec}(u), \mathrm{vec}(v)\rangle = 0$ iff every clause is satisfied by at least one of $u, v$ — i.e., iff $(u,v)$ satisfies $\varphi$ [2]. The sets $A = \{\mathrm{vec}(u)\}$, $B = \{\mathrm{vec}(v)\}$ each have $N = 2^{n/2}$ vectors in dimension $m = O(n)$ (for fixed $k$, we sparsify to keep $d = O(n)$, then reduce dimension to $c\log n$ via standard hashing gadgets).

> **Theorem:** (SETH $\Rightarrow$ $k$-OV lower bound [2]) If $k$-SAT requires $2^{(1-o(1))n}$ time, then OV on $N$ vectors in dimension $c\log N$ requires $N^{2-o(1)}$ time: an $O(N^{2-\varepsilon}\mathrm{poly}(d))$ OV algorithm would give a $2^{(1-\delta)n}$-time $k$-SAT algorithm for $\delta = \varepsilon/2 - o(1)$, contradicting SETH.

The same construction generalizes to **$k$-OV**: $k$ sets of vectors, seeking a $k$-tuple with coordinatewise product $0$; SETH implies an $n^{k-o(1)}$ lower bound [2]. The following Python code implements the gadget for 3-SAT:

```python
from itertools import product

def vec_of_partial(assign, clauses, half_vars):
    # assign: dict var -> 0/1 on one half; returns bit-vector over clauses
    v = []
    for cl in clauses:  # cl: list of (var, polarity)
        sat = any(assign.get(var) == pol for var, pol in cl if var in half_vars)
        v.append(0 if sat else 1)
    return tuple(v)

def reduce_3sat_to_ov(n, clauses):
    L = set(range(n // 2)); R = set(range(n // 2, n))
    A = [vec_of_partial(dict(zip(L, bits)), clauses, L)
         for bits in product([0, 1], repeat=len(L))]
    B = [vec_of_partial(dict(zip(R, bits)), clauses, R)
         for bits in product([0, 1], repeat=len(R))]
    # exists u in A, v in B with <u,v> == 0  <=>  formula satisfiable
    return [a for a in set(A)], [b for b in set(B)]
```

The construction is *exact*: no approximation, no error — the orthogonal pair exists iff the formula is satisfiable. This exactness is what lets SETH's $2^{(1-o(1))n}$ bound convert cleanly into the $N^{2-o(1)}$ OV bound with $N = 2^{n/2}$.

### 4.2 The OV reduction web: sequence problems inherit quadratic hardness

Once OV hardness is established, a family of reductions transfers it to problems on strings and curves. The landmark results:

- **Edit Distance** (Backurs–Indyk [3]): computing edit distance between strings of length $n$ requires $n^{2-o(1)}$ time under SETH. The reduction encodes vector sets as gadgets in the dynamic-programming alignment grid so that the optimal alignment cost reveals whether an orthogonal pair exists.
- **LCS and sequence similarity** (Abboud–Backurs–Vassilevska Williams [4]): longest common subsequence, dynamic time warping, and Fréchet distance all require $n^{2-o(1)}$ time under SETH.
- **Graph diameter**: the diameter of a sparse graph with $m$ edges requires $m^{2-o(1)}$ time to approximate within factor better than $3/2$ under SETH, resolving long-standing questions about BFS-based heuristics.

The table below summarizes the current conditional landscape:

| Problem | Baseline algorithm | Conditional lower bound | Hypothesis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Orthogonal Vectors ($d = c\log n$) | $O(n^2 d)$ | $n^{2-o(1)}$ | SETH [2] |
| Edit distance | $O(n^2)$ | $n^{2-o(1)}$ | SETH [3] |
| LCS / DTW / Fréchet | $O(n^2)$ | $n^{2-o(1)}$ | SETH [4] |
| Graph diameter ($3/2$-approx) | $O(mn)$ | $m^{2-o(1)}$ | SETH |
| APSP (integer weights) | $O(n^3)$ | $n^{3-o(1)}$ | APSP [5] |
| $(\min,+)$-product | $O(n^3)$ | $n^{3-o(1)}$ | APSP [5] |
| 3-SUM | $O(n^2)$ | $n^{2-o(1)}$ | 3-SUM [8] |

The reductions in [3, 4] are technically delicate: they must embed the *combinatorial structure* of OV (bipartite orthogonality) into the *alignment structure* of edit distance, using separator gadgets to force the alignment to "choose" one vector from each set and penalize non-orthogonal choices. The crucial property is that the constructed strings have length $\tilde{O}(n)$ so that a subquadratic edit-distance algorithm would break the quadratic OV barrier.

### 4.3 The subcubic equivalence class: APSP, triangles, and min-plus

Williams & Williams [5] defined **subcubic reducibility**: $A \le_3 B$ if a truly subcubic ($n^{3-\varepsilon}$) algorithm for $B$ implies a truly subcubic algorithm for $A$, with only polylogarithmic overhead. Under this relation, an astonishingly large class of problems collapses into a single equivalence class:

> **Theorem:** (Subcubic equivalence [5]) The following are equivalent under subcubic reductions: APSP; negative-triangle detection; $(\min,+)$-product verification; minimum-weight cycle; replacement paths; second shortest simple path; metricity checking; maximum subarray in a matrix.

The proof architecture is a triangle of implications. $(\min,+)$-product reduces to APSP by embedding matrices as tripartite graphs; negative-triangle detection reduces to $(\min,+)$-product by a splitting argument on high-degree vertices (the classic Fredman trick: $A[i,k] + B[k,j] < A[i,k'] + B[k',j]$ iff $A[i,k] - A[i,k'] < B[k',j] - B[k,j]$, which enables dominance-product techniques); and APSP reduces to negative-triangle detection via repeated squaring with witness recovery. Each direction loses at most an arbitrarily small $\varepsilon$ in the exponent, so either *all* admit truly subcubic algorithms or *none* do.

Chan, Vassilevska Williams & Xu [6] later extended this web to *small-weight and unweighted* variants: directed unweighted APSP is fine-grained equivalent to rectangular $(\min,+)$-products and to counting versions of APSP, revealing that the cubic barrier persists even for 0/1 weights and that many problems are equivalent to their own *counting* variants.

### 4.4 The formal machinery: fine-grained reductions

A **fine-grained reduction** from $(A, T_1)$ to $(B, T_2)$, denoted $(A,T_1) \le_{FGR} (B,T_2)$, is a (possibly randomized) algorithm that, given $\varepsilon > 0$, runs in time $T_1(n)^{1-\delta}$ for some $\delta = \delta(\varepsilon) > 0$, makes oracle calls to $B$ on instances of sizes $n_1, \dots, n_q$, and satisfies $\sum_i T_2(n_i)^{1-\varepsilon} \le T_1(n)^{1-\delta}$ [8]. The key consequence:

> **Theorem:** If $(A,T_1) \le_{FGR} (B,T_2)$ and $B$ is solvable in $T_2^{1-\varepsilon}$ time, then $A$ is solvable in $T_1^{1-\delta}$ time. Hence a conjectured lower bound for $A$ transfers to $B$.

This formalism explains *why* exponents transfer so cleanly: the reduction's own cost is strictly smaller than the source bound, and the oracle calls are budgeted so they cannot exceed it either. Contrast with Karp reductions, which ignore exponents entirely. The theory also supports *randomized* reductions (needed for some SETH-based results via the isolation lemma) and *non-uniform* variants connected to circuit lower bounds [7].

### 4.5 OVC, SETH, and the algorithmic frontier

The relationship between SETH and OVC is the subject of active research. We know:

- SETH $\Rightarrow$ OVC (Williams [2]; tightened quantitatively by later work).
- OVC does *not* obviously imply SETH; it is a strictly weaker assumption and hence a *safer* basis for lower bounds.
- OVC implies **non-uniform circuit lower bounds**: refuting OVC (i.e., finding a universal $n^{2-\varepsilon}$ OV algorithm) would imply new lower bounds against explicit circuit classes — a striking connection between fine-grained algorithms and classical complexity [7].

On the algorithmic side, the best known results come remarkably close to the conjectured bounds. For $d = c\log n$, OV can be solved in $n^{2 - 1/O(\log c)}$ time [7], so the exponent gap is only in the *rate* at which the savings vanish as $c$ grows. For APSP, the best known algorithm runs in $n^3 / 2^{\Omega(\sqrt{\log n})}$ time — a genuine but subpolynomial improvement [5]. These near-misses are characteristic of fine-grained complexity: the conjectures assert that the naive exponents are *essentially* optimal, while allowing subpolynomial improvements that the framework is deliberately too coarse to rule out.

## 5 Empirical Results / Theoretical Guarantees

Fine-grained complexity is a *theoretical* discipline: its "experiments" are reductions and algorithms. The guarantees it provides are conditional theorems of the form "Hypothesis $H$ implies problem $P$ requires time $T^{1-o(1)}$." The table in §4.2 collects the principal such guarantees. We highlight three quantitative milestones:

1. **OVC quantitative tightness.** The $n^{2-1/O(\log c)}$-time algorithm for $d = c\log n$ shows that any improvement to the *form* of OVC (a universal $\varepsilon$) is the only remaining gap; the dimension-dependent savings are already optimal up to the $O(\log c)$ factor [7].
2. **Subcubic equivalences are two-sided.** Unlike typical hardness results, [5] gives *equivalences*: a breakthrough on *any* member (say, a truly subcubic metricity test) would propagate to *all*, including APSP. This makes the class highly falsifiable — and its fifty-year survival highly suggestive.
3. **From quadratic to cubic: no reduction is known between the hubs.** Despite the mature theory, there is no known fine-grained reduction from OV to APSP or vice versa; the quadratic and cubic worlds remain separate, and relating them is a major open problem [8].

## 6 Limitations

The framework's central limitation is its *conditionality*: every lower bound rests on an unproven conjecture, and a single algorithmic breakthrough (e.g., a $2^{0.9n}$-time SAT algorithm) would collapse large portions of the theory. More subtly:

- **Word-RAM vs. other models.** Most results are stated for the word-RAM with $O(\log n)$-bit words; transferring them to, say, the cell-probe or arithmetic-circuit models requires care.
- **No unconditional super-quadratic bounds.** Despite decades of effort, no *unconditional* $\omega(n^2)$ lower bound is known for any explicit problem in a natural general model — fine-grained complexity sidesteps rather than solves this barrier.
- **Subpolynomial slack.** The framework cannot rule out $n^2 / 2^{\sqrt{\log n}}$-type improvements, which it treats as consistent with the conjectures; practitioners should not read the bounds as claiming *zero* improvement is possible.
- **Dimension sensitivity.** OV hardness requires $d = \omega(\log n)$; for constant or $O(\log n)$-with-small-constant dimension, genuinely subquadratic algorithms exist, so reductions must carefully control dimension growth.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Fine-grained complexity has transformed our understanding of polynomial-time computation from a flat landscape into a structured one, with SETH, APSP, and 3-SUM as mountain peaks joined by ridges of fine-grained reductions. The Williams split-and-list reduction [2] anchors the quadratic world; the Williams & Williams subcubic equivalences [5] organize the cubic world; and the formal reduction machinery [8] ensures that every conditional bound is stated with the precision the subject demands. The deepest open questions are now clear: (1) *unconditional* lower bounds — can any fine-grained bound be deconditionalized, even in restricted models? (2) *inter-hub relations* — are the SETH and APSP worlds connected by reductions, or genuinely distinct? and (3) *OVC vs. SETH* — does the weaker, safer Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture already imply all the algorithmic consequences we care about [7]? Progress on any of these would reshape the field.
