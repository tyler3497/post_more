# Neural Ordinary Differential Equations and Continuous-Depth Learning: The Adjoint Sensitivity Method, Augmented Dynamics, FFJORD Density Estimation, and Stiff-Solver Tradeoffs

## Abstract

Neural ordinary differential equations (NODEs) replace the discrete layer stack of deep networks with a continuous-depth model in which the hidden state evolves according to $dh/dt = f(h(t), t, \theta)$, where $f$ is a neural network and the output is produced by a black-box ODE solver [1]. This thesis develops the mathematical and algorithmic foundations of continuous-depth learning. We derive the adjoint sensitivity method, which computes gradients by solving a second, augmented ODE backward in time at $O(1)$ memory cost independent of solver steps, and contrast it with checkpointing-based ANODE [2]. We prove the topological expressivity barrier of vanilla NODEs and show how augmentation restores universality [4]. We extend the framework to density estimation through FFJORD, which pairs the instantaneous change-of-variables formula with Hutchinson's trace estimator for unbiased, $O(D)$-time log-likelihoods [3], and to stochastic dynamics via the stochastic adjoint [5]. Finally, we characterize the accuracy–compute Pareto frontier of adaptive solvers on stiff problems [6], arguing that solver choice is a first-order architectural decision.

---

## 1. Introduction

Residual networks compose near-identity updates $h_{t+1} = h_t + f(h_t, \theta_t)$ — recognizably an **Euler discretization** of a continuous transformation with unit step size. Lu et al. and Haber and Ruthotto made this explicit in 2017; Chen, Duvenaud and colleagues asked the decisive question: *what happens as we add more layers and take smaller steps?* [1].

In the limit, the recurrence becomes the ODE $dh/dt = f(h(t), t, \theta)$ with $h(0) = x$, integrated by a black-box solver to yield $h(T)$ — a *single* parameter vector $\theta$ governing the entire continuous-depth evolution. This unlocks:

- **Constant memory cost** during training, via the *adjoint sensitivity method*, regardless of how many internal steps the solver takes [1];
- **Adaptive computation**, because adaptive-step solvers spend more function evaluations on difficult inputs and fewer on easy ones;
- **Explicit precision–speed tradeoffs**, controlled by solver tolerances rather than by architecture surgery;
- **Continuous-time latent dynamics**, naturally suited to irregularly sampled time series;
- **Invertible generative modeling** with exact likelihoods, free of the partitioning constraints of discrete flows [3].

![Residual network as Euler discretization of an ODE flow](/thesis/ths_1788787767176_a7f3-0.webp)

The remainder develops theory and evidence: Section 2 covers ODE and solver background, Section 3 the core methodology, Section 4 a deep dive into four pillars (adjoint sensitivities [2], augmented dynamics [4], FFJORD [3], stiff solvers [6]), Section 5 empirical evaluations, Section 6 limitations, and Section 7 conclusions.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Ordinary differential equations: existence, uniqueness, and regularity

A neural ODE is only well-defined if its initial value problem admits a unique solution. The classical guarantee is the **Picard–Lindelöf theorem**: if $f$ is continuous in $t$ and *Lipschitz continuous* in $h$ on a domain containing $(0, x)$, then there exists $\epsilon > 0$ such that the IVP has a unique solution on $[0, \epsilon]$. Neural networks with smooth activations (tanh, ELU, softplus) are locally Lipschitz, so the theorem applies; networks with ReLU are Lipschitz too, though only piecewise smooth — a distinction that will matter profoundly in Section 4.1.

Uniqueness implies **integral curves cannot cross**: the flow map $\Phi_T : x \mapsto h(T)$ is a *homeomorphism* [4], so any classifier $g(\Phi_T(x))$ must separate classes with a homeomorphic feature map — a genuine topological restriction.

### 2.2 Numerical solvers: from Euler to Dormand–Prince

In practice, $\Phi_T$ is approximated by a numerical integrator. The solver landscape divides along two axes:

1. **Fixed-step vs. adaptive-step.** Euler ($h_{n+1} = h_n + \Delta t \, f$) and classical RK4 use a prescribed step; adaptive methods (Dormand–Prince 5(4), Fehlberg, Tsitouras) estimate local truncation error at each step and shrink or grow $\Delta t$ to satisfy user tolerances `rtol` and `atol`.
2. **Explicit vs. implicit.** Explicit methods evaluate $f$ at known states; implicit methods solve a (nonlinear) system per step, at higher per-step cost but with vastly superior stability on *stiff* problems.

| Solver | Order | Adaptive | Stiff-capable | Cost per step | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Euler | 1 | No | No | 1 NFE | Pedagogical / fixed compute |
| Midpoint / RK2 | 2 | No | No | 2 NFE | Cheap smooth flows |
| RK4 | 4 | No | No | 4 NFE | Non-stiff, fixed budget |
| Dormand–Prince (dopri5) | 5(4) | Yes | No | ~6 NFE | Default for NODEs |
| Tsitouras 5(4) | 5(4) | Yes | No | ~6 NFE | Modern default |
| Implicit Euler / Rosenbrock | 1–2 | Yes | Yes | 1 NFE + Jacobian solve | Stiff dynamics |

**Stiffness** is the regime where Jacobian eigenvalues span many orders of magnitude in decay rate: explicit solvers are forced onto the *fastest* timescale, and adaptive controllers explode the NFE count. A-stability and L-stability (stability function $R(z)$ bounded, respectively vanishing, on $\Re(z) \le 0$) characterize methods that survive stiffness [6].

### 2.3 Related continuous models

Latent ODEs extended NODEs to irregular time series with VAE-style inference, and neural SDEs add instantaneous noise [5]; the adjoint method itself is decades old in optimal control and was repurposed for deep learning by Chen et al. [1].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The neural ODE training loop

Training a NODE follows a fixed choreography:

1. **Specify the vector field** $f(h, t, \theta)$ as a neural network (typically an MLP or small convnet, with smooth activations preferred for solver friendliness).
2. **Integrate forward**: $h(T) = \mathrm{ODESolve}(h(0), f, 0, T, \theta)$ with a chosen solver and tolerances.
3. **Evaluate the loss** $L(h(T))$, e.g. cross-entropy for classification or negative log-likelihood for flows.
4. **Compute gradients** $\partial L / \partial \theta$ — *without* backpropagating through the solver's internal operations — via the adjoint sensitivity method.
5. **Update** $\theta$ with a standard optimizer (Adam is the usual choice).

```python
import torch
from torchdiffeq import odeint_adjoint as odeint  # adjoint-backed solver

class ODEFunc(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = torch.nn.Sequential(
            torch.nn.Linear(dim, 64), torch.nn.Tanh(),
            torch.nn.Linear(64, 64), torch.nn.Tanh(),
            torch.nn.Linear(64, dim))
    def forward(self, t, h):
        return self.net(h)          # dh/dt = f(h, t, theta)

class NODEClassifier(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim, n_classes):
        super().__init__()
        self.f = ODEFunc(dim)
        self.head = torch.nn.Linear(dim, n_classes)
    def forward(self, x):
        t = torch.tensor([0., 1.])
        hT = odeint(self.f, x, t, method='dopri5', rtol=1e-3, atol=1e-4)[-1]
        return self.head(hT)
```

Note the deliberate choice of `tanh` over ReLU: piecewise-linear vector fields make the reverse-time ODE ill-conditioned, a theme we develop in Section 4.1.

### 3.2 The adjoint sensitivity equations

Define the **adjoint state** $a(t) = \partial L / \partial h(t)$, the sensitivity of the loss to the hidden state at time $t$. Differentiating the identity $L = L(h(T))$ through the ODE yields the adjoint ODE

$$\frac{da(t)}{dt} = -a(t)^\top \frac{\partial f}{\partial h}\big(h(t), t, \theta\big), \qquad a(T) = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h(T)},$$

and the parameter gradient follows by quadrature:

$$\frac{dL}{d\theta} = -\int_{T}^{0} a(t)^\top \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta}\big(h(t), t, \theta\big)\, dt.$$

The algorithmic move is to solve an **augmented ODE backward in time** for $s(t) = [h(t), a(t), \partial L/\partial\theta(t)]$ from $s(T) = [h(T), \partial L/\partial h(T), 0]$ — requiring only the *terminal* state, hence **$O(1)$ memory** in solver steps [1]. Each backward step needs one reverse-mode vector–Jacobian product, costing roughly two to three forward evaluations.

### 3.3 Continuous change of variables for density estimation

For generative modeling, let $z(t)$ follow $dz/dt = f(z(t), t, \theta)$ from $z(t_0) \sim p_0$ to $x = z(t_1)$. The **instantaneous change-of-variables** formula [1],

$$\frac{d\log p(z(t))}{dt} = -\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z(t)}\right),$$

gives $\log p(x) = \log p_0(z_0) - \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \operatorname{tr}(\partial f/\partial z)\, dt$. The trace replaces the $O(D^3)$ log-determinant of discrete flows, but exact evaluation still costs $O(D^2)$ — the bottleneck FFJORD eliminates (Section 4.3).

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Adjoint Sensitivity Method: O(1) Memory, and Its Discontents

> **Theorem (Adjoint sensitivity, informal).** *Let $h(t)$ solve $dh/dt = f(h,t,\theta)$ with $h(0)=x$, and let $L$ be a scalar loss on $h(T)$. Then $dL/d\theta$ can be computed by a single reverse-time integration of an augmented ODE of dimension $2D + |\theta|$, using memory independent of the number of forward solver steps.*

The method's elegance conceals two sharp edges, both diagnosed by Gholami, Keutzer and Biros [2]:

- **Numerical irreversibility.** The backward pass reconstructs $h(t)$ by integrating the *forward* ODE backward in time. For dissipative or ReLU-driven vector fields this reverse integration is ill-conditioned: small errors amplify exponentially, so the $h(t)$ used in the backward pass differs from the $h(t)$ of the forward pass, corrupting gradients. ReLU activations are particularly treacherous because the reverse ODE through a piecewise-linear field need not even be well-posed.
- **Optimize-then-discretize inconsistency.** The adjoint derivation optimizes the *continuous* problem and only then discretizes, whereas backpropagating through the solver (discretize-then-optimize) differentiates the *actually computed* function. For small step sizes the two gradients can diverge, and training can destabilize.

**ANODE** [2] resolves both issues with classical *checkpointing*: store $K$ forward checkpoints (memory $O(L) + O(N_t)$), then re-integrate locally between them during the backward pass. Gradients become *unconditionally accurate* at the same asymptotic compute as the reverse solve; binomial checkpointing trades a logarithmic compute factor for near-$O(1)$ memory. **ANODEV2** extends this to coupled evolution of weights and activations with KKT-derived optimality conditions.

In practice: the pure adjoint method suffices with smooth activations and moderate tolerances, but for ReLU-heavy or convolutional vector fields, checkpointing is the responsible default.

### 4.2 Augmented Dynamics and the Topology of Learnable Maps

The homeomorphism property of ODE flows (Section 2.1) is not a curiosity — it is a hard expressivity ceiling. Dupont, Doucet and Teh [4] proved that NODEs can only learn feature maps homeomorphic to the input space, and exhibited concrete functions they cannot represent.

> **Proposition (Topological obstruction, Dupont et al.).** *There exist continuous functions $g: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d$ that no neural ODE flow $\Phi_T$ can approximate arbitrarily well, because $\Phi_T$ is a homeomorphism while $g$ need not be. In particular, any task requiring trajectories to cross in $\mathbb{R}^d$ — e.g., mapping the 1D points $-1 \mapsto +1$ and $+1 \mapsto -1$ while fixing $0$ — is unrepresentable.*

The remedy proposed in [4] is disarmingly simple — **augment the state space**. Concatenate each input $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with $p$ zeros, $a(0) = 0 \in \mathbb{R}^p$, and solve the ODE in $\mathbb{R}^{d+p}$:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix} h(t) \\ a(t) \end{bmatrix} = f\left(\begin{bmatrix} h(t) \\ a(t) \end{bmatrix}, t\right), \qquad \begin{bmatrix} h(0) \\ a(0) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Trajectories that must cross in $\mathbb{R}^d$ can now *lift* into the extra dimensions and pass over one another, like a highway interchange resolving an at-grade intersection. The consequences measured in [4] are striking:

- **Universality restored**: augmented NODEs (ANODEs) fit the unrepresentable toy functions and learn dramatically faster;
- **Simpler flows**: the learned vector fields are nearly linear, so adaptive solvers need far fewer NFEs — augmentation *reduces* compute despite increasing dimension;
- **Better generalization**: on MNIST, CIFAR-10, SVHN and ImageNet, ANODEs match or beat NODE accuracy with lower loss and greater stability.

Augmentation thus converts a topological defect into a computational advantage: extra dimensions are not a cost but a shortcut.

### 4.3 FFJORD: Free-Form Density Estimation via Stochastic Trace Estimation

Continuous normalizing flows inherit the change-of-variables formula of Section 3.3, but the exact trace costs $O(D^2)$ per evaluation — prohibitive for images. Grathwohl et al. [3] observed that an *unbiased stochastic* estimate suffices for maximum-likelihood training, via **Hutchinson's trace estimator**: for any random vector $\epsilon$ with $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon] = 0$ and $\mathrm{Cov}(\epsilon) = I$ (Rademacher or standard Gaussian),

$$\operatorname{tr}(A) = \mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\big[\epsilon^\top A \epsilon\big].$$

Applied to $A = \partial f/\partial z$, this is a single **vector–Jacobian product**, computable in $O(D)$ time by reverse-mode autodiff:

```python
def unbiased_log_density_dynamics(f, z, eps):
    """Returns (dz/dt, d logp/dt) with Hutchinson trace estimate."""
    z = z.requires_grad_(True)
    dz_dt = f(z)                                   # vector field
    (vjp,) = torch.autograd.grad(dz_dt, z, eps,   # eps^T df/dz
                                 create_graph=True)
    dlogp_dt = -(vjp * eps).sum(-1, keepdim=True)  # unbiased trace est.
    return dz_dt, dlogp_dt
```

This is **FFJORD** (Free-form Jacobian of Reversible Dynamics) [3]. "Free-form" is the operative word: because no determinant structure is needed, $f$ can be *any* architecture — unrestricted MLPs, convolutional stacks — with no partitioning of dimensions (unlike Real NVP or Glow) and no ordering constraints (unlike autoregressive flows). The model remains exactly invertible (integrate backward), supports **one-pass sampling**, and trains by unbiased maximum likelihood. On CIFAR-10, FFJORD reaches ~3.40 bits/dim, competitive with Glow's 3.35 among exact-likelihood models while sampling an order of magnitude faster; on MNIST it achieves ~0.99 bits/dim versus Glow's 1.05 [3].

![FFJORD continuous normalizing flow: base distribution warped by ODE vector field](/thesis/ths_1788787767176_a7f3-2.webp)

The price is estimator variance, mitigated with fixed Rademacher probes, multiscale architectures, and Jacobian regularization — *stochastic linear algebra* is what makes continuous-depth generative modeling scale.

### 4.4 Stiff Solvers, Adaptive Steps, and the NFE–Tolerance Pareto Frontier

All of the above assumes the solver can integrate the learned vector field efficiently. **Stiffness** breaks that assumption. In scientific applications — chemical kinetics, systems biology — the vector field contains reactions spanning $10^{-4}$ to $10^{11}$ in rate constants. Kim et al. [6] demonstrated the failure mode on the classical **Robertson problem**:

$$\begin{aligned}
A &\xrightarrow{0.04} B, \\
B + B &\xrightarrow{3\times 10^7} C + B, \\
B + C &\xrightarrow{10^4} A + C,
\end{aligned}$$

whose species concentrations evolve on timescales separated by *eleven orders of magnitude*. Explicit adaptive solvers (dopri5) respond to stiffness by collapsing the step size until NFEs explode or integration stalls entirely; the adjoint backward pass, which must re-integrate the same stiff field, suffers identically.

> **Theorem (informal, stiffness barrier).** *For a stiff vector field with stiffness ratio $S = \max|\Re(\lambda_i)| / \min|\Re(\lambda_i)| \gg 1$, any explicit method with bounded stability region requires $\Omega(S)$ steps per unit time for stability, independent of accuracy requirements — while an A-stable implicit method requires only $O(1)$.*

Kim et al. [6] showed that stiff neural ODEs *can* be learned, but only with a disciplined recipe: deep networks with rectified activations, careful scaling of network outputs *and* loss terms (so that species at concentration $10^{-8}$ and $10^0$ contribute comparably), and stabilized gradient computations. Their work also highlights the central economic fact of continuous-depth learning — the **NFE–tolerance Pareto frontier**:

- Loosening `rtol`/`atol` from $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-3}$ cuts NFEs 3–10× at modest accuracy cost;
- NFEs **grow during training** as vector fields complexify, making early-epoch timing benchmarks misleading;
- The adjoint backward pass roughly doubles forward NFEs, so tolerance choices compound;
- For stiff problems the tradeoff shifts from steps-vs-accuracy to linear-solver-cost-vs-steps.

![Adaptive solver step-size versus tolerance tradeoff with function-evaluation counts](/thesis/ths_1788787767176_a7f3-3.webp)

The lesson: **the solver is part of the architecture** — tolerance schedules and method selection deserve the same attention as learning rates.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation

We consolidate representative results reproducing the qualitative trends of [1, 2, 3, 4, 6]; absolute numbers vary with implementation, but the orderings are robust.

### 5.1 Classification: continuous-depth ResNets

On MNIST, a NODE classifier with a small convolutional vector field matches a comparable ResNet while using constant memory:

| Model | Test error (MNIST) | Memory vs. steps | NFE (forward, trained) |
|---|---|---|---|
| ResNet (6 blocks) | ~0.4% | $O(\text{depth})$ | 6 (fixed) |
| NODE + adjoint [1] | ~0.4% | $O(1)$ | 30–80 (adaptive) |
| Augmented NODE [4] | ~0.3% | $O(1)$ | 15–40 (simpler flow) |
| ANODE checkpointed [2] | ~0.4% | $O(L)+O(N_t)$ | 30–80 (exact grads) |

The headline is the *memory* column — $O(1)$ at any effective depth — while NFEs grow over training as flows complexify, a growth augmentation visibly reverses [4].

### 5.2 Density estimation: FFJORD vs. discrete flows

Bits per dimension (lower is better) on standard benchmarks [3]:

| Model | MNIST | CIFAR-10 | Sampling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Real NVP | 1.06 | 3.49 | One pass |
| Glow | 1.05 | 3.35 | One pass |
| CNF (exact trace) [1] | — | intractable at scale | ODE solve |
| **FFJORD** [3] | **0.99** | **3.40** | One ODE solve |

FFJORD alone combines unrestricted architecture, exact likelihoods, and one-pass sampling; the cost is wall-clock — CIFAR-10 runs take days on multiple GPUs.

### 5.3 Gradient fidelity: adjoint vs. checkpointing

On CIFAR-10/100 with ReLU-based ResNet vector fields [2], naive adjoint training can diverge from reverse-time instability while ANODE checkpointing converges to lower loss and higher accuracy — at the price of the $O(N_t)$ buffer. With smooth activations the gap narrows: *activation choice is a numerical-analysis decision*.

### 5.4 Stiff dynamics: the Robertson problem

Kim et al. [6] fit neural ODEs to Robertson kinetics across eleven decades of timescale: unscaled MSE losses ignore trace species (output/loss scaling is mandatory), explicit dopri5 succeeds only after scaling reformulation while implicit Rosenbrock methods handle raw stiffness at higher per-step cost, and stabilized reverse tolerances are required for gradient sanity.

### 5.5 Stochastic extension

Li et al. [5] generalize the adjoint to SDEs, deriving a *stochastic adjoint* SDE whose solution is the gradient at $O(1)$ memory and $O(L \log L)$ time (the log factor from Brownian-tree noise caching), and fit neural stochastic dynamics competitively on a 50-dimensional motion-capture dataset.

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires enumerating where continuous-depth learning still falls short:

- **Compute cost.** NODEs run 2–10× slower per epoch than parameter-matched discrete networks; FFJORD on CIFAR-10 trains for days [3]. Memory savings are real, but wall-clock binds.
- **NFE growth.** Vector fields complexify during training, so epochs *slow down over time*; kinetic-energy and Jacobian regularization mitigate but do not eliminate this.
- **Adjoint numerical error.** Reverse-solve inaccuracies can corrupt gradients enough to diverge for ReLU/convolutional fields [2]; checkpointing fixes this at a memory cost.
- **Expressivity.** Vanilla NODEs are homeomorphisms; trajectory-crossing tasks require augmentation [4] or time-dependent parameters.
- **Stiffness.** Implicit solvers need Jacobian factorizations ($O(D^3)$ naive per step); the recipe in [6] works but is labor-intensive.
- **Stochastic adjoint complexity.** Noise caching, Itô–Stratonovich corrections, and high-order SDE solvers make [5] far harder to implement correctly.
- **Theory gaps.** No sharp generalization bounds, no characterization of NFE growth as overfitting versus genuine complexity, and no principled tolerance-scheduling theory.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Neural ODEs reframe depth as *time*: one vector field, integrated by a solver, replaces the discrete layer stack. The **adjoint sensitivity method** makes training memory-constant via a backward ODE — with the caveat, clarified by ANODE [2], that reverse-time integration demands numerical respect. **Augmentation** [4] dissolves the topological expressivity barrier, turning extra dimensions into computational shortcuts. **FFJORD** [3] pairs the instantaneous change-of-variables formula with Hutchinson's trace estimator for free-form, exactly-invertible generative models with unbiased likelihoods at $O(D)$ cost. And **stiff neural ODEs** [6] establish that solver selection is a first-order architectural decision, not an implementation detail.

The stochastic adjoint [5] extends the edifice to SDEs — *differentiable programming over continuous-time stochastic processes with constant memory*. Open problems remain: taming NFE growth, cheap stiff integration, and a learning theory for continuous depth. But the direction is clear: the continuous limit is not an approximation of deep learning; it is what deep learning was approximating all along.
