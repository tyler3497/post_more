# Tropical Geometry and the Max-Plus Algebra: From Piecewise-Linear Varieties to Phylogenetic Trees and Combinatorial Optimization

## Abstract

Tropical geometry recasts algebraic geometry over the tropical, or max-plus, semiring (ℝ ∪ {−∞}, max, +), where polynomials become piecewise-linear convex functions and algebraic varieties degenerate into polyhedral complexes. This thesis develops the subject from its algebraic foundations to two of its most striking applications: the tropical Grassmannian of lines, which coincides exactly with the Billera–Holmes–Vogtmann space of phylogenetic trees, and max-plus linear algebra, where the eigenvalue of a matrix is its maximum cycle mean and classical linear programming becomes combinatorial. We prove the duality between tropical hypersurfaces and regular subdivisions of Newton polytopes, state the fundamental theorem of tropical geometry in its three equivalent forms, and derive the four-point condition of tree metrics from tropical Plücker relations. Worked numerical examples, a balancing-condition proof, and executable code accompany the theory, alongside a frank account of realizability obstructions and computational limits.

## 1 Introduction

Classical algebraic geometry studies solution sets of polynomial equations over fields such as ℂ. Tropical geometry performs the same study over the *tropical semiring*, in which addition is replaced by maximum (or minimum) and multiplication by ordinary addition [3]. The name is a nod to the Brazilian mathematician Imre Simon, who pioneered the use of the min-plus algebra in automata theory in the 1970s; the adjective "tropical" was coined in his honor by Christian Choffrut and popularized by Jean-Éric Pin and Dominique Perrin [2]. For decades this algebra remained a niche tool of operations research and discrete-event systems, until Grigory Mikhalkin's enumerative breakthroughs of the early 2000s showed that counts of complex algebraic curves could be recovered from their tropical, piecewise-linear shadows [7].

The subsequent decade saw tropical geometry crystallize into a full-fledged field. Speyer and Sturmfels introduced the tropical Grassmannian and proved that its two-dimensional case is *exactly* the space of phylogenetic trees used by evolutionary biologists [1]. Meanwhile, Butkovič and others consolidated max-plus linear algebra — the study of matrices over the tropical semiring — into a theory with its own spectral theory, convex geometry, and optimization algorithms [4][5]. More recently, tropical ideas have penetrated statistics and machine learning: kernel density estimation, logistic regression, and MCMC sampling have all been tropicalized to work natively on tree space [6][8][10].

What follows builds the algebraic groundwork, explains tropicalization, treats the semiring, duality, the fundamental theorem, phylogenetics, and max-plus optimization in depth, and closes with proofs, computations, limitations, and conclusions.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Valued fields and the tropical shadow

Tropical geometry arises whenever one studies a variety over a *valued field* K — a field equipped with a valuation v: K* → ℝ satisfying v(ab) = v(a) + v(b) and v(a + b) ≥ min(v(a), v(b)), with equality when v(a) ≠ v(b). Canonical examples include ℚ with a p-adic valuation and the field of Puiseux series ℂ{{t}} with the order-of-vanishing valuation [2]. The tropical operations model precisely how valuations behave: multiplication adds valuations, while addition takes the minimum (or maximum, depending on convention) whenever the two valuations differ.

### 2.2 The tropical semiring

> **Definition:** The **max-plus tropical semiring** is the set 𝕋 = ℝ ∪ {−∞} equipped with tropical addition a ⊕ b = max(a, b) and tropical multiplication a ⊗ b = a + b. The additive identity is −∞, the multiplicative identity is 0, and the operations satisfy all semiring axioms, including distributivity, with ⊕ idempotent (a ⊕ a = a) [3].

The min-plus variant (ℝ ∪ {+∞}, min, +) is isomorphic via negation x ↦ −x, and conventions differ across subfields: algebraic geometers often use min, while optimizers and phylogeneticians typically use max [2][3]. Two structural facts dominate everything that follows. First, there is **no additive inverse**: the equation 2 ⊕ k = 3 has no solution in 𝕋, so one cannot subtract — a deficit that forces tropical linear algebra onto genuinely new ground [4]. Second, tropical multiplication is invertible (a ⊘ b = a − b), so 𝕋 is a semifield in the multiplicative sense, and tropical monomials x^{⊗n} = n·x are just linear forms.

### 2.3 Tropical polynomials and hypersurfaces

A tropical polynomial F(x₁,…,xₙ) = ⊕_{a∈A} (c_a ⊗ x₁^{⊗a₁} ⊗ ⋯ ⊗ xₙ^{⊗aₙ}), with A ⊂ ℕⁿ finite and c_a ∈ ℝ, evaluates to the *piecewise-linear convex function* F(x) = max_{a∈A}(c_a + a·x) [1]. Its **tropical hypersurface** V(F) is the locus where this maximum is attained at least twice — equivalently, the set of points where F is not differentiable [2]. This locus is an (n−1)-dimensional polyhedral complex in ℝⁿ.

## 3 Methodology

The central methodological device of the field is **tropicalization**: given an algebraic variety X ⊂ (K*)ⁿ over a valued field K, one applies the valuation coordinatewise and takes the Euclidean closure to obtain trop(X) ⊂ ℝⁿ [2]. In practice the pipeline is:

1. **Choose the valued field.** For theoretical work this is often ℂ{{t}} with its t-adic valuation; for computational work one uses fields where valuations are algorithmically accessible.
2. **Extract combinatorial data.** The Newton polytope of each defining polynomial — the convex hull of its exponent vectors — governs the shape of the tropicalization through regular subdivisions induced by the valuations of the coefficients [11].
3. **Compute via Gröbner theory.** The tropical variety is a polyhedral subcomplex of the Gröbner fan of the defining ideal; software such as the Singular library `tropicalNewton.lib` implements starting-point and link computations that make tropical varieties of Grassmannians and other moduli spaces explicitly computable [12].
4. **Interpret polyhedrally.** Edges, vertices, and their integer weights satisfy the *balancing condition*, which encodes the enumerative and intersection-theoretic content of the original variety [7].

This methodology converts hard algebro-geometric questions — intersection numbers, curve counts, moduli degenerations — into polyhedral combinatorics that can be drawn, counted, and programmed.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The tropical semiring and max-plus algebra

The idempotency of ⊕ gives tropical algebra a flavor closer to lattice theory than to ring theory. Matrices over 𝕋 multiply by the rule (A ⊗ B)_{ij} = ⊕_k (a_{ik} ⊗ b_{kj}) = max_k(a_{ik} + b_{kj}), so that max-plus matrix multiplication is exactly the all-pairs longest-path (or, with a sign change, shortest-path) computation on a weighted digraph [4]. The tropical determinant is the assignment problem: tdet(A) = max_{σ ∈ Sₙ} Σ_i a_{i,σ(i)}, the maximum-weight perfect matching in a bipartite graph [5].

> **Definition:** A **tropical linear space** is the tropicalization of a classical linear subspace of (K*)ⁿ. Equivalently, it is a polyhedral complex cut out by tropical linear equations whose Plücker coordinates satisfy the tropical Plücker relations [1].

Tropical convexity replaces convex combinations Σ λᵢxᵢ (Σλᵢ = 1) with tropical combinations ⊕ (λᵢ ⊗ xᵢ), producing *tropical polytopes* — ordinary polytopes that are also tropically convex — whose combinatorics underlies tropical linear programming [5]. Because subtraction is unavailable, the passage from classical to tropical statements is rarely a formality: it typically requires re-proving linear-algebraic facts in a setting where Gaussian elimination does not exist, a program carried out systematically in the max-linear systems literature [4][9].

### 4.2 Tropical hypersurfaces and duality with Newton polytopes

The deepest elementary fact in the subject is the duality between a tropical hypersurface and a subdivision of its Newton polytope. Let F(x) = max_{a∈A}(c_a + a·x) and let Δ = conv(A) ⊂ ℝⁿ be its Newton polytope. Lifting each exponent a to height c_a and projecting the upper hull yields a **regular subdivision** of Δ. The duality theorem states [7][2]:

- Connected components of ℝⁿ \ V(F) (regions where a single linear form dominates) correspond bijectively to vertices of the subdivision;
- Edges of V(F) correspond to edges of the subdivision, orthogonally: a tropical edge is perpendicular to its dual polytope edge;
- Vertices of V(F) correspond to 2-dimensional cells of the subdivision.

Moreover, V(F) satisfies the **balancing condition**: at each vertex, the weighted sum of the primitive integer direction vectors of the incident edges is zero, where each edge is weighted by the lattice length of its dual cell [7]. A short proof is given in Section 5.

The following Python snippet evaluates a tropical polynomial and locates its hypersurface in one variable, the tropical roots being exactly the kinks of the piecewise-linear graph:

```python
import numpy as np

def tropical_poly(coeffs):
    # coeffs[i] is the coefficient of x^{otimes i}; -inf means absent
    def F(x):
        terms = [c + i * x for i, c in enumerate(coeffs) if c > -np.inf]
        return max(terms)
    return F

def tropical_roots(coeffs, lo=-10.0, hi=10.0, n=20001):
    # a root is a kink: the argmax set has size >= 2
    xs = np.linspace(lo, hi, n)
    roots = []
    for x in xs:
        vals = [c + i * x for i, c in enumerate(coeffs) if c > -np.inf]
        m = max(vals)
        if sum(1 for v in vals if abs(v - m) < 1e-9) >= 2:
            roots.append(round(float(x), 6))
    # cluster consecutive detections
    out = []
    for r in roots:
        if not out or r - out[-1] > 1e-3:
            out.append(r)
    return out

F = tropical_poly([0.0, 1.0, -np.inf, 2.0])  # 0 + x + 2x^3 tropically
print(tropical_roots([0.0, 1.0, -np.inf, 2.0]))
```

### 4.3 Tropicalization and the fundamental theorem

For X ⊂ (K*)ⁿ an algebraic variety with vanishing ideal I(X), the **fundamental theorem of tropical geometry** asserts the equivalence of three descriptions of trop(X) [2]:

1. **Hypersurface intersection:** trop(X) = ⋂_{f ∈ I(X)} V(trop(f)), the intersection of the tropical hypersurfaces of all tropicalized polynomials in the ideal;
2. **Valuation image:** the Euclidean closure of {(v(x₁), …, v(xₙ)) : x ∈ X};
3. **Initial ideals:** the set of weight vectors w ∈ ℝⁿ for which the initial ideal in_w(I(X)) contains no monomial.

The equivalence of (1)–(3) is the engine of the field [11]. A subtlety: a finite *generating* set of I(X) need not cut out trop(X) — the intersection of the tropical hypersurfaces of the generators is in general only a **tropical prevariety**. A finite set whose tropical hypersurfaces do cut out trop(X) exactly is called a **tropical basis**; the Plücker quadrics form a tropical basis for the Grassmannian, which is what makes Section 4.4 possible [1].

| Classical object | Tropical counterpart |
|---|---|
| Variety over K | Balanced weighted polyhedral complex |
| Polynomial | Piecewise-linear convex function |
| Zero set | Non-differentiability locus |
| Degree | Lattice volume / mixed volume |
| Linear space | Tropical linear space |
| Grassmannian G(k,n) | Tropical Grassmannian |

This dictionary, in the form presented by Verschelde, guides nearly every translation between the two worlds [11].

### 4.4 Phylogenetic tree space and the tropical Grassmannian

The most celebrated application of tropical geometry identifies a classical object of algebraic geometry with a classical object of biology. The Grassmannian G(2, n) of 2-planes in n-space embeds via Plücker coordinates p_{ij} into projective space, cut out by the quadratic Plücker relations p_{ij}p_{kl} − p_{ik}p_{jl} + p_{il}p_{jk} = 0. Tropicalizing these relations with the max convention turns each into the condition that the maximum of {w_{ij} + w_{kl}, w_{ik} + w_{jl}, w_{il} + w_{jk}} be attained at least twice — which is precisely the **four-point condition** characterizing tree metrics [1].

> **Theorem:** (Speyer–Sturmfels) The tropical Grassmannian G°(2, n) is a simplicial fan known as the space of phylogenetic trees. It has 2ⁿ⁻¹ − n − 1 vertices and (2n−5)!! facets, and its homotopy type is a bouquet of (n−2)! spheres of dimension n−4 [1].

Concretely, a point of G°(2, n) is a vector (w_{ij}) of pairwise leaf distances satisfying the four-point condition, i.e. a metric tree with n labeled leaves. The cones of the fan correspond to tree topologies; moving across a codimension-one face performs a nearest-neighbor interchange. This gives tree space the structure of a tropical linear space and equips it with the **tropical metric**, under which intrinsic geodesics can be computed far more cheaply than under the older Billera–Holmes–Vogtmann (BHV) metric [6][10].

The statistical payoff has been substantial: tropical kernel density estimation of gene-tree distributions outperforms BHV-based estimators in both speed and accuracy on multispecies-coalescent simulations [6]; tropical logistic regression classifies trees without leaving the tropical projective space [8]; and MCMC samplers now walk the tropical Grassmannian directly [10].

| Feature | BHV space | Tropical Grassmannian |
|---|---|---|
| Geometry | CAT(0) cubical complex | Simplicial fan (tropical linear space) |
| Metric | BHV geodesic metric | Tropical Hilbert / quotient metric |
| Geodesic computation | O(n⁴) | Nearly linear in practice |
| Statistical tools | Fréchet means, PCA | KDE, logistic regression, MCMC [6][8][10] |
| Algebraic origin | None | Tropicalization of Plücker ideal [1] |

### 4.5 Max-plus linear algebra and combinatorial optimization

In max-plus algebra the eigenvalue problem A ⊗ x = λ ⊗ x takes a purely combinatorial form. The landmark result, due to Cuninghame-Green, states that every finite square matrix has a unique eigenvalue, and it equals the **maximum cycle mean** of its weighted digraph: λ(A) = max_σ ω(σ)/ℓ(σ), where σ ranges over directed cycles, ω(σ) is the cycle weight, and ℓ(σ) its length [4][5]. The eigenvalue is thus the solution of a classic combinatorial optimization problem, computable in O(n³) by Karp's algorithm [4].

The associated eigenvectors describe the periodic regime of the discrete-event system x(k+1) = A ⊗ x(k), which models synchronization phenomena from railway timetables to manufacturing pipelines: λ is the asymptotic growth rate (throughput), and eigenvectors are the stationary schedules [4]. The Kleene star A* = I ⊕ A ⊕ A² ⊕ ⋯ encodes all longest paths and solves the max-plus Bellman equation; max-plus linear programs, of the form maximize cᵀ ⊗ x subject to A ⊗ x ⊕ b ≤ x, are solvable by policy-iteration algorithms that generalize Howard's method for stochastic games [4][9]. Tropical supereigenvectors extend the spectral theory to reducible matrices via Frobenius normal forms, giving a complete basis theory for all eigenspaces [9].

Max-plus matrix multiplication in Haskell makes the longest-path interpretation explicit:

```haskell
type Tropical = Double  -- -1/0 encodes -infinity

tAdd :: Tropical -> Tropical -> Tropical
tAdd = max

tMul :: Tropical -> Tropical -> Tropical
tMul = (+)

-- (A ⊗ B)[i][j] = max_k (A[i][k] + B[k][j]): longest paths through k
tMatMul :: [[Tropical]] -> [[Tropical]] -> [[Tropical]]
tMatMul a b =
  [[ maximum [ (a !! i !! k) `tMul` (b !! k !! j)
             | k <- [0 .. length b - 1] ]
   | j <- [0 .. length (head b) - 1] ]
  | i <- [0 .. length a - 1] ]
```

## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: the balancing condition

*Claim.* Let V(F) ⊂ ℝ² be a plane tropical curve. At every vertex v, Σ_e m_e u_e = 0, where the sum runs over incident edges, u_e is the primitive integer direction vector of e pointing away from v, and m_e is the lattice length of the dual edge in the Newton subdivision.

*Proof.* Near v, F(x) = max over a small set of affine forms ℓ_a(x) = c_a + a·x. The vertex is dual to a 2-cell P of the regular subdivision of the Newton polygon Δ. The incident edges of V(F) are perpendicular to the edges of P, and their lattice lengths m_e are exactly the lattice lengths of those dual edges. Traversing the boundary of P, the edge vectors sum to zero; rotating each by 90° (the duality rotation) and scaling by lattice length preserves this vanishing, yielding Σ m_e u_e = 0. ∎

This argument is the local form of Mikhalkin's correspondence: balancing is the combinatorial residue of the fact that V(F) is a Hausdorff limit of amoebas of complex curves [7].

### 5.2 Worked example: a tropical line

Take F(x, y) = 0 ⊕ x ⊕ y in the max convention. The three affine forms are 0, x, y. The maximum is attained twice precisely on: the ray {(t, t) : t ≤ 0} (0 = x ≥ y gives the ray in direction (−1, −1)... more carefully, 0 = x ≥ y yields x = 0 ≥ y, the negative y-axis; x = y ≥ 0 yields the diagonal ray (1,1); 0 = y ≥ x yields the negative x-axis). Up to the standard change of conventions this is the tripod with vertex at the origin, dual to the unimodular triangulation of the triangle conv{(0,0), (1,0), (0,1)} — each edge of the tripod is perpendicular to one side of the triangle, illustrating the duality of Section 4.2 [2][11].

### 5.3 Worked example: a max-plus eigenvalue

Consider the 3×3 matrix over 𝕋 (with ε = −∞):

A = [[2, 5, ε], [ε, 1, 4], [3, ε, 2]]

The directed cycles and their means are:

| Cycle | Weight ω | Length ℓ | Mean ω/ℓ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1→1 | 2 | 1 | 2.0 |
| 2→2 | 1 | 1 | 1.0 |
| 3→3 | 2 | 1 | 2.0 |
| 1→2→3→1 | 5+4+3 = 12 | 3 | 4.0 |
| 1→3→2→1 | ε (missing edge) | — | — |

The maximum cycle mean is λ(A) = 4, attained on 1→2→3→1, so the unique max-plus eigenvalue is 4 [4]. An eigenvector is obtained from any column of the Kleene star of (λ^{⊗−1} ⊗ A); e.g. normalizing so the critical cycle has weight 0, one eigenvector is x = (0, −1, 1)ᵀ, and indeed A ⊗ x = (4, 3, 5)ᵀ = 4 ⊗ x. The critical graph (edges belonging to maximum-mean cycles) governs the entire periodic regime of the dynamical system x(k+1) = A ⊗ x(k) [9].

### 5.4 Computational verification

Using the Singular library `tropicalNewton.lib`, the tropical Grassmannian G(3,6) is computed as a simplicial complex of 1035 tetrahedra, and the links of G(3,8) are shown to be non-simplicial [12]. On the phylogenetics side, tropical KDE on simulated gene trees under the multispecies coalescent consistently beats BHV-based density estimation in accuracy per unit compute time [6].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Realizability gaps.** Not every balanced polyhedral complex is the tropicalization of an algebraic variety. Brugallé and Shaw exhibit tropical curves, lines on surfaces, and even linear spaces that admit no complex approximant; from a purely combinatorial viewpoint these pathological objects are nearly indistinguishable from realizable ones, so tropical methods can prove *necessary* but not *sufficient* conditions for classical existence problems [7].
2. **No subtraction.** The absence of additive inverses means there is no tropical analogue of Gaussian elimination, no general theory of tropical division, and no exact sequence machinery; each classical linear-algebraic fact must be rebuilt combinatorially, and some (e.g. a full rank-nullity theory) remain partial [4][5].
3. **Computational hardness.** Computing tropical varieties requires Gröbner-fan traversals whose complexity explodes with the size of the generating set; tropical prevariety computations become infeasible quickly as complexity grows, and even finding a first tropical starting point is nontrivial [12].
4. **Metric and statistical caveats.** The tropical metric on tree space, while fast, is not CAT(0) like the BHV metric, so Fréchet means can be non-unique and some classical statistical guarantees do not transfer [6][10].
5. **Convention fragility.** The min/max schism demands careful translation of formulas and software interfaces; sign errors between conventions are a notorious source of mistakes [2][3].

## 7 Conclusion

Tropical geometry earns its keep twice over. As pure mathematics, it replaces the analytic complexity of degenerating varieties with polyhedral combinatorics governed by Newton polytopes, balancing, and the fundamental theorem — a machine that has settled enumerative problems unreachable by classical means [7][2]. As applied mathematics, the same max-plus algebra that defines tropical varieties also *is* the natural language of scheduling, shortest paths, and discrete-event systems [4], while the tropical Grassmannian hands evolutionary biology a computationally tractable, algebraically principled tree space [1][6][8][10].

Open frontiers include scaling tropical basis algorithms to real phylogenetic datasets, extending tropical statistics with full rigor, and mapping exactly which balanced complexes are realizable [12][7]. The tropical semiring is small — two operations on the real line — but the mathematics it generates shows no sign of exhausting itself.
