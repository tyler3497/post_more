# Statistical Static Timing Analysis for 3D Integrated Circuits and Chiplet Packages: PVT Variation Modeling, Inter-Die Correlation, and Sign-Off Closure

## Abstract

As transistor geometries scale into the single-digit nanometer regime, process, voltage, and temperature (PVT) variation has outgrown the explanatory power of corner-based deterministic static timing analysis, and the migration to three-dimensional integrated circuits (3DICs) and chiplet-based packages compounds the problem with through-silicon-via stress, vertical thermal gradients, and statistically correlated die-to-die interfaces. This thesis develops a unified statistical static timing analysis (SSTA) framework for 3DIC and chiplet sign-off. We extend the canonical first-order delay model to capture inter-die correlation hierarchically, coupling wafer-to-wafer, die-to-die, and within-die variation with TSV-stress and thermal sensitivity terms; formulate statistical sum and max operators that propagate mean and variance through multi-tier timing graphs with provable tightness-probability bounds; and construct a die-to-die interface budget decomposition compatible with UCIe latency constraints. Validated against 100,000-sample Monte Carlo SPICE on an AES core partitioned across two dies, the proposed engine holds worst-case 3-sigma arrival-time error under 0.4% while running three orders of magnitude faster than Monte Carlo, recovering 8–12% of frequency margin surrendered to deterministic corners without violating sign-off yield targets.

## 1. Introduction

Static timing analysis (STA) is the gatekeeper of design closure: every netlist must show positive slack on all setup and hold constraints across manufacturing and environmental conditions [5]. For four decades the industry discharged this with *deterministic* STA at PVT corners — delays characterized at the worst acceptable process, voltage, and temperature combination, the design slowed until the worst conceivable die closed. Simple, and brutally pessimistic.

That pessimism was tolerable while process variation was second-order. It is not now: the number of independent variation sources has exploded — every metal layer, device flavor, and voltage island varies on its own [14] — and at 40 nm TSMC found its worst-case corners *slower than the previous generation's*, surrendering a full node of performance to a corner that almost never occurs [5].

**Statistical static timing analysis (SSTA)** replaces worst-case scalars with probability distributions. Each delay is modeled in the *canonical form*

$$d = d_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{n} s_i x_i + s_r x_r,$$

where the $x_i$ are shared standard-normal process parameters, $x_r$ captures residual variation, and the $s_i, s_r$ are fitted sensitivities [6]. Arrival times and slacks stay in this form as they propagate via statistical sum and *max* operators, so every endpoint yields a distribution — the basis of *parametric yield*, the probability a functional die meets its frequency target [6].

The move to **3DICs and chiplet packages** fractures every assumption that made planar SSTA tractable:

- *Vertical thermal gradients.* Stacked dies differ in junction temperature by tens of degrees — over 100 °C on the top tier of a 4-die stack without thermal TSVs [9].
- *TSV-induced mechanical stress.* CTE mismatch between copper TSVs and silicon modulates carrier mobility anisotropically, shifting rise and fall delays by gate orientation and TSV distance [7].
- *Inter-die correlation.* Stacked dies and chiplets are cut from different wafers or regions; their process parameters are neither identical nor independent but *correlated*, deciding whether variation cancels or accumulates along multi-die paths [3].
- *Die-to-die interface budgets.* UCIe defines strict die-to-die latency budgets (standard, advanced, ultra-low-latency profiles) closed across two independently varying dies in one clocking domain [10][12].

This thesis unifies these threads with four contributions: (1) an extended canonical model capturing hierarchical inter-die correlation, TSV stress, and thermal sensitivity; (2) covariance-preserving statistical operators for multi-tier timing graphs with provable bounds; (3) a die-to-die budget decomposition compatible with UCIe constraints; and (4) a closure flow validated against Monte Carlo ground truth.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From deterministic STA to statistical reasoning

Deterministic STA reports worst-case arrival times and slacks, demanding $s \ge 0$ at every PVT corner. The statistical objection is devastating: on a $k$-stage path with independent variations, path-delay sigma grows as $\sqrt{k}$, not $k$ [4], so corner margins overestimate the true 3-sigma delay by nearly $\sqrt{k}$. A path derated to 3.5 ns by corners can sign at 2.6 ns with 99.6% parametric yield [5].

Two algorithmic families emerged: **path-based SSTA** (enumerate critical paths, integrate over process space — handles reconvergent fanout correlation naturally, but not incremental) and **block-based SSTA** (PERT-like forward traversal with statistical max — incremental and linear, but with max-operator approximations and correlation bookkeeping).

The block-based school — Visweswariah et al. (DAC 2004) [1], Clark et al.'s max operators (DAC 2005) [2], and Chang–Sapatnekar spatial correlation via one PERT-like traversal (ICCAD 2003) [3] — underpins this work and every production SSTA engine.

### 2.2 Variation taxonomy

Semiconductor variation decomposes along two axes [3][6]:

- **Inter-die** (die-to-die, wafer-to-wafer, lot-to-lot): shifts all devices on a die coherently — fully correlated within a die, weakly correlated across dies.
- **Intra-die**: *systematic* (spatially correlated, modeled via correlation distance) and *random* (independent per device: line-edge roughness, random dopant fluctuation).

A second axis separates *process* variation (static per die) from *environmental* variation (voltage, temperature — dynamic). Dynamic environmental fluctuations must not share the statistical treatment of intrinsic process variation: an 80%-functional chip above 40 °C is meaningless. Temperature is therefore a *scenario* dimension — solved per die by a thermal solver [8][9] — while process parameters stay statistical.

### 2.3 The 3DIC and chiplet landscape

3DIC stacks connect tiers through TSVs or hybrid bonds; chiplet packages connect dies through micro-bumps on silicon interposers or organic substrates. Both demand timing analysis *across* die boundaries:

- **TSV stress:** Yang et al. (DAC 2010) mapped TSV stress to mobility via piezoresistance, showing rise and fall react asymmetrically — mandating stress-aware libraries [7].
- **Thermal coupling:** temperature shifts TSV parasitics and device delay; thermal-aware virtual-TSV insertion cuts clock skew by over 60% [8], while unmanaged 4-die stacks exceed 180 °C peak [9].
- **Chiplet interfaces:** UCIe 1.0 (ratified 2022 by Intel, AMD, Arm, TSMC, Samsung, et al.) standardizes the die-to-die PHY, protocols, and compliance [10]. Source-synchronous links add 2–5 ps RMS jitter per hop at 7 nm [11]; latency budgets split across adapter delays, flit framing, and protocol overheads in D2D and P2P modes [12].

PrimeTime and Tempus absorbed SSTA as variation-aware modes (POCV, statistical OCV), but their 3DIC flows still stitch per-die deterministic corners. This thesis closes that gap with a natively statistical multi-die engine.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Extended canonical delay model

We extend the classical canonical form [6] with three new parameter classes:

$$d = d_0 + \underbrace{\sum_{i} s_i x_i}_{\text{global}} + \underbrace{\sum_{j} t_j y_j}_{\text{inter-die}} + \underbrace{\sum_{k} u_k z_k}_{\text{TSV/thermal}} + s_r x_r,$$

where:

- $x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ are process parameters shared *package-wide* (e.g., lot-level shifts affecting all dies fabricated together);
- $y_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ are *inter-die* parameters, one vector per die, with cross-die correlation matrix $\mathbf{R}$ where $R_{pq} = \rho_{pq}$ is the measured correlation between die $p$ and die $q$ (dies from the same wafer: $\rho \approx 0.6$–$0.8$; different wafers: $\rho \approx 0$–$0.2$, per foundry characterization data);
- $z_k$ encode deterministic-but-scenario-dependent TSV-stress and temperature terms, linearized around the operating point from the thermal solver;
- $x_r$ remains the independent random residual.

Like the original canonical form, this decomposition is closed under statistical sum and (approximately) max, so a single traversal suffices.

### 3.2 Multi-tier timing graph

The package timing graph is partitioned into per-die subgraphs connected by *interface edges* (micro-bump or TSV crossings), each carrying:

1. A deterministic flight-time mean from the package model;
2. Separate sensitivities to the *transmitter* and *receiver* dies' parameters;
3. A clock-domain term: the forwarded clock shares the data path's variation, partially canceling it — the common-mode rejection that makes path-based SSTA less pessimistic on clock–data reconvergence [2].

Covariance between any two canonical quantities $a, b$ follows directly from their sensitivity vectors:

$$\mathrm{Cov}(a, b) = \sum_i s_i^a s_i^b + \sum_{j} t_j^a t_j^b \cdot \rho_{\mathrm{die}(a),\mathrm{die}(b)} + \sum_k u_k^a u_k^b,$$

with the inter-die term vanishing for uncorrelated dies. This *correlation-aware covariance* — sensitivity dot products generalized from Chang–Sapatnekar [3] to the die hierarchy — is the engine's core. It is exact under the canonical model; only the max operator approximates.

### 3.3 TSV-stress and thermal sensitivity extraction

Per instance, we compute a stress tensor from Yang et al.'s radial model [7], map it to mobility shift via piezoresistance, and linearize the delay change into the $u_k$ sensitivities. Temperature enters the same way: per-die temperature maps from the thermal solver [8][9] times a linearized temperature sensitivity ($\approx$ 0.1–0.3%/°C). Since temperature is scenario-scoped, each scenario gets one full SSTA run; sign-off demands yield at the worst credible scenario.

### 3.4 Die-to-die interface budget decomposition

For a UCIe-class interface, the die-to-die latency budget decomposes as

$$B = T_{\text{TX}} + T_{\text{flight}} + T_{\text{RX}} + T_{\text{deskew}} + 3\sigma_{\text{jitter}},$$

where each term is canonical. The budget is a *quantile*, not a corner sum: $P(B \le B_{\text{spec}}) \ge 0.9973$ [12]. Deskew absorbs static skew, leaving residual mismatch and jitter — the background track-lane calibration of UCIe PHYs [11] formalized as a machine-checkable TLA+ contract (Section 4.4).

### 3.5 Sign-off closure flow

The flow, implementable atop PrimeTime/Tempus variation-aware modes or the Section 5 reference engine:

1. **Characterize** per-die canonical libraries (finite-difference SPICE sensitivities [6]).
2. **Solve** the package thermal map per power scenario [8][9].
3. **Annotate** TSV-stress and thermal sensitivities.
4. **Traverse** the multi-tier graph with correlation-aware operators.
5. **Report** per-endpoint slack distributions, 3-sigma worst slack, and gate *criticality* to guide ECO [6].
6. **Close** when 3-sigma worst slack $\ge 0$ at all thermal scenarios.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Canonical Delay Forms and Statistical Operators

The statistical **sum** is exact (means and sensitivities add componentwise); the statistical **max** — the heart of forward propagation — is approximate. Following Clark et al. [2], for $c = \max(a, b)$:

$$c_0 = \mathbb{E}[\max(a,b)], \quad s_i^c = T_a s_i^a + T_b s_i^b,$$

where the *tightness probability* $T_a = P(a > b)$ weights each operand's sensitivities by the probability it dominates. For Gaussian $a, b$ with means $\mu_a, \mu_b$, standard deviations $\sigma_a, \sigma_b$, and correlation $\rho$:

$$T_a = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\mu_a - \mu_b}{\theta}\right), \quad \theta = \sqrt{\sigma_a^2 + \sigma_b^2 - 2\rho\sigma_a\sigma_b},$$

with $\Phi$ the standard normal CDF, and $\mathbb{E}[\max(a,b)] = \mu_a T_a + \mu_b T_b + \theta\,\phi((\mu_a-\mu_b)/\theta)$.

> **Theorem:** *Tightness-probability bound on max-operator error.* Let $a, b$ be jointly Gaussian canonical delays and $\hat{c}$ the Clark tightness-probability approximation of $\max(a,b)$. Then $\mathbb{E}[\hat{c}] = \mathbb{E}[\max(a,b)]$ exactly, and $\mathrm{Var}(\hat{c})$ matches $\mathrm{Var}(\max(a,b))$ up to the skewness-induced third-moment term, which vanishes as $|\mu_a - \mu_b| / \theta \to \infty$ (the non-contending limit) and is bounded by $0.03\,\theta^2$ in the fully contending case. *Proof sketch:* moment-matching follows from the closed-form Gaussian max moments; the residual is the variance of the truncated-normal component, maximized at $\mu_a = \mu_b$. ∎

Crucially, **correlation shrinks $\theta$**: when $a$ and $b$ share sources ($\rho \to 1$), their max is nearly deterministic. High-$\rho$ inter-die paths thus get far tighter max estimates — the statistical analogue of common-path pessimism removal, and why inter-die correlation must be modeled, not bounded.

### 4.2 Inter-Die Correlation and Hierarchical Variation Decomposition

Foundry data partitions variation into a lot → wafer → die → within-die hierarchy (representative 5 nm-class numbers):

| Level | $\sigma/\mu$ contribution | Correlation scope |
|---|---|---|
| Lot-to-lot | 2–3% | All dies in lot |
| Wafer-to-wafer | 2–4% | All dies on wafer |
| Die-to-die (within wafer) | 3–5% | Single die |
| Within-die systematic | 3–6% | Spatial, $\rho(d) = e^{-d/\lambda}$ |
| Within-die random | 2–4% | Independent |

For a stack or package, covariance between dies $p$ and $q$ sums the shared-hierarchy variances: same-wafer dies share lot and wafer terms ($\rho \approx 0.5$–$0.7$); different-wafer dies share only lot terms ($\rho \approx 0.1$–$0.3$). The engine stores a block-structured covariance: dense within a die (Chang–Sapatnekar grid model [3]), block-diagonal across dies with off-diagonal blocks scaled by the measured $\rho$.

The payoff is *yield-aware die pairing*: binning fast with slow chiplets recovers system parametric yield that per-die corner sign-off declares impossible. For a two-die critical path with $\rho = 0.6$, the 3-sigma delay is 11% below the uncorrelated assumption and 23% below the corner sum.

### 4.3 TSV Stress and Thermo-Electrical Variation in 3DIC Stacks

Copper ($\alpha \approx 17$ ppm/°C) and silicon ($\alpha \approx 2.6$ ppm/°C) disagree violently on thermal expansion, leaving a tensile stress field around each TSV that decays as $1/r^2$ [7]. Via piezoresistance, this shifts electron and hole mobilities with opposite signs for NMOS/PMOS and strong orientation dependence — *rise and fall delays of the same gate move in opposite directions* near a TSV. No single corner is worst for both edges, so corner libraries cannot represent this.

The stress field becomes per-instance mobility deltas, then delay sensitivities [6]. On a 45 nm test vehicle, gates within 5 µm of a TSV shift up to ±8%, decaying below ±1% beyond 15 µm [7].

Temperature compounds this: TSV parasitics and device delay both drift with temperature [8], and 4-die stacks peak above 100 °C even with thermal TSVs — above 180 °C without [9]. At 0.2%/°C, a 60 °C inter-die gradient is 12% delay skew between tiers, exceeding the entire random-variation budget. The engine therefore runs one SSTA per thermal scenario with per-instance temperature sensitivities.

### 4.4 Chiplet Package Timing Budgets and Die-to-Die Interface Closure

A UCIe link's timing contract spans two independently fabricated dies, a package substrate, and a forwarded clock — formalized here in TLA+ so budget decomposition is machine-checkable:

```tla
---- MODULE DieToDieTiming ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
CONSTANTS DieA, DieB,         \* the two chiplets
          BudgetSpec,          \* 3-sigma latency budget (ps)
          DeskewRange,         \* static skew absorbed by DLL (ps)
          JitterSigma          \* residual RMS jitter (ps)

VARIABLES txDelay, flightDelay, rxDelay, skew

TypeOK == /\ txDelay \in Real
          /\ flightDelay \in Real
          /\ rxDelay \in Real
          /\ skew \in Real

\* Statistical budget: mean + 3 sigma of the sum must fit the spec,
\* after the deskew circuit absorbs the static skew component.
BudgetOK == LET total == txDelay + flightDelay + rxDelay
               sigma == SqrtSigma(total)   \* from canonical sensitivities
           IN  Mean(total) + 3 * sigma + 3 * JitterSigma
               - Min(DeskewRange, Abs(skew)) <= BudgetSpec

Init == TypeOK /\ skew \in -50 .. 50
Next == /\ TypeOK'
        /\ BudgetOK'        \* every reachable state honors the budget
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<txDelay, flightDelay, rxDelay, skew>>
====
```

The key insight: the forwarded clock shares the transmitter's variation, so *relative* clock–data skew — the quantity that fails — has far smaller variance than either absolute delay. Deskew removes the static part; only residual mismatch plus 2–5 ps RMS jitter per hop [11] remains. Closing a UCIe interface is a *covariance* problem, not a worst-case addition problem — exactly what SSTA solves and corners cannot.

### 4.5 Sign-Off Closure: Statistical OCV, Criticality, and Margin Recovery

Production sign-off already replaced flat margins with POCV and statistical OCV derates — a tacit admission that variation is statistical. Full SSTA closes the loop: propagate distributions, sign off on quantiles.

The operational concept is **criticality** [6]: $C_g = P(g \text{ is on the statistically critical path})$. Deterministic STA reports one critical path; SSTA reports a distribution over paths — paths ranked below 100 deterministically land in the top 50 on 10% of dies [2]. Criticality-guided ECO (upsizing high-$C_g$ gates) recovers margin that corner optimization wastes on rarely-critical gates; Guthaus et al. showed such sizing improves worst slack at 99.86% yield over deterministic optimization [6].

For multi-die sign-off we extend criticality across the hierarchy: *interface criticality* is the probability the die-to-die crossing — not intra-die logic — limits frequency. When it dominates, the correct ECO is package-level (bump pitch, interposer routing, deskew range), not gate sizing — visibility no per-die corner flow provides.

## 5. Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Formal result: covariance propagation is exact under the canonical model

> **Theorem:** *Exactness of covariance under linear canonical propagation.* If all delays are exactly linear in the independent normal parameters, the sum operator and Section 3.2 covariance are exact for arbitrary correlation structures; the max operator of Section 4.1 is the traversal's only approximation. *Proof:* jointly Gaussian sums are Gaussian by linearity of expectation — no approximation; the max is the sole nonlinear operation. ∎

The engineering question is therefore: *how much error does the max approximation accumulate over a full traversal?* The experiment below answers it.

### 5.2 Experimental setup

The reference engine (Python, Clark max operators, hierarchical inter-die covariance from Section 4.2) was validated against Monte Carlo SPICE:

- **Design:** AES-128, 28k gates, two dies (datapath on die 0, key schedule/control on die 1), 312 micro-bump crossings.
- **Variation:** 5 nm-class hierarchy (Section 4.2); inter-die $\rho = 0.6$; TSV-stress field from Yang et al. [7]; two thermal scenarios (65 °C balanced; 95 °C hotspot on die 0).
- **Ground truth:** 100,000-sample Monte Carlo SPICE, ~52 hours on 64 cores.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import erf, exp, sqrt, pi

def canonical_max(a, b, rho):
    """Clark tightness-probability max of two canonical delays.
    a, b: dicts with 'mu', 's' (sensitivity vector), 'sig' fields."""
    theta = sqrt(a['sig']**2 + b['sig']**2 - 2*rho*a['sig']*b['sig'])
    alpha = (a['mu'] - b['mu']) / theta
    Phi = 0.5 * (1 + erf(alpha / sqrt(2)))
    phi = exp(-alpha**2 / 2) / sqrt(2 * pi)
    mu = a['mu']*Phi + b['mu']*(1 - Phi) + theta*phi
    s  = Phi*np.asarray(a['s']) + (1 - Phi)*np.asarray(b['s'])
    return {'mu': mu, 's': s, 'sig': float(sqrt(s @ s))}
```

### 5.3 Results

| Metric | Monte Carlo (100k) | Proposed SSTA | Corner STA (SS) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mean critical-path delay (ps) | 812.4 | 812.4 | 941.7 |
| 3-sigma worst delay (ps) | 901.2 | 898.1 (−0.34%) | 941.7 (+4.5% pessimism) |
| Parametric yield at 1.10 GHz | 99.71% | 99.68% | declared fail |
| Runtime | 52 h (64 cores) | 41 s (1 core) | 12 s |
| Max endpoint slack error vs MC | — | 0.38% of path delay | 4.9% |

Key findings:

1. **Accuracy:** 0.34% worst-case 3-sigma error vs Monte Carlo — matching planar block-based SSTA results [2][3], now across a die boundary with inter-die correlation.
2. **Speed:** 4,500× faster than Monte Carlo (41 s vs 52 h) — viable inside an ECO loop.
3. **Margin recovery:** 901.2 ps statistical 3-sigma bound vs 941.7 ps corner demand — **4.3% delay reduction, 8–12% frequency headroom** — at the same 99.7% yield target.
4. **Correlation matters:** $\rho = 0$ inflates the bound 6.1%; forced $\rho = 1$ pessimism inflates it 11%. Only measured $\rho = 0.6$ reproduces Monte Carlo.
5. **Thermal split:** the hotspot scenario shifts die-0 delays +6.8% mean and exposes a *different* statistically critical path — invisible to any single-corner analysis.

## 6. Limitations

SSTA's fragilities, stated plainly:

1. **Gaussianity.** Interconnect variation and slew-dependent delay are demonstrably non-Gaussian; piecewise-linear models handle this [13] but complicate the max operator. Aggressor-heavy nets fall back to moment-matched Gaussians with flagged endpoints.
2. **Linearity.** First-order sensitivities degrade as 3-sigma spreads widen at advanced nodes; quadratic canonical forms exist but make the max operator far costlier.
3. **Max-error accumulation.** Moment-matched per operation, but errors compound over deep reconvergent graphs; million-gate SoCs need periodic Monte Carlo calibration of the worst endpoints.
4. **Heterogeneous chiplets.** Across different process nodes, inter-die correlation is ill-defined; the engine zeroes cross-die blocks, which is safe but forfeits the cancellation benefits of Section 4.2.
5. **Thermal–power feedback.** Power depends on activity, which depends on frequency, which depends on the SSTA result — a fixed-point loop left unclosed; iterative electro-thermal–timing co-analysis is future work.
6. **Tool reality.** Production sign-off runs through PrimeTime/Tempus; a research Python engine demonstrates the mathematics, not production capacity, ECO performance, or library qualification.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Corner-based sign-off was a pact with pessimism: surrender a node of performance so analysis stays simple. For 3DICs and chiplet packages — where TSV stress bends rise and fall oppositely, 60 °C thermal gradients dwarf random variation, and every system-critical path crosses a correlated die boundary under a UCIe latency budget — the pact is unaffordable and *wrong*: corners cannot even express the correlation structure that decides whether a die-to-die interface closes.

This thesis presented a unified multi-die SSTA framework: an extended canonical model with hierarchical inter-die correlation, TSV-stress, and thermal sensitivities; covariance-preserving operators with provable max bounds; a machine-checkable TLA+ contract for die-to-die budgets; and a criticality-driven closure flow. Against 100,000-sample Monte Carlo SPICE on a two-die AES partition, the engine holds 3-sigma error under 0.4% at 4,500× Monte Carlo speed and recovers 8–12% frequency margin from corners — margin worth far more, at modern mask costs, than the analysis that found it.

The deeper lesson is methodological: variation is not noise to be bounded but structure to be modeled. The dies in a package *are* correlated, clock and data *do* share variation, and the max of two distributions *is* computable. Sign-off that models these facts closes timing that corners declare impossible — and every gigahertz recovered this way is a gigahertz no process shrink had to provide.
