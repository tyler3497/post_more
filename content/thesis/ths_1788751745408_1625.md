# Formal Verification of Raft Consensus with TLA+ Refinement Mapping and Liveness Proofs: Leader Election Safety, Log Replication Invariants, and Model Checking at Scale

## Abstract

The Raft consensus algorithm [2] has become the dominant protocol for crash-fault-tolerant state machine replication, yet production experience shows that informal arguments about its safety are fragile: subtle defects in leader election and membership changes have repeatedly escaped code review. This thesis presents a layered formal verification campaign for Raft that unifies three complementary techniques: exhaustive model checking of a TLA+ specification [1][3] with the TLC checker, a refinement-mapping proof that a pipelined implementation faithfully simulates the abstract protocol, and liveness proofs in the style of IronFleet [4] and Verdi [6]. We formalize the canonical safety properties -- Election Safety, Leader Completeness, State Machine Safety, and the Log Matching property -- as inductive TLA+ invariants, verify them across bounded configurations totaling tens of millions of distinct states, prove that implementation-level pipelining is a stuttering refinement of the specification, and establish eventual leader election and eventual log commitment under weak fairness and eventual synchrony. We additionally repair and verify the single-server membership change mechanism whose safety proof Ongaro [1] deliberately excluded [5], yielding a layered assurance argument: model checking finds bugs, refinement carries guarantees to code, and liveness proofs bound temporal behavior.

## 1 Introduction

Distributed consensus is the load-bearing wall of modern infrastructure. Every strongly consistent datastore -- etcd, Consul, TiKV, CockroachDB's range layer -- ultimately reduces to the problem of getting a set of crash-prone servers to agree on a single, totally ordered log of commands. The Raft algorithm of Ongaro and Ousterhout [2] displaced Paxos in industry not because it is more powerful, but because it is more *teachable*: by decomposing consensus into leader election, log replication, and safety as separable subproblems, Raft made correct implementation plausible for working engineers. Yet plausibility is not proof. Jepsen analyses, postmortems, and the well-documented 2014 discovery of a safety violation in Raft's single-server membership change [5] all demonstrate that even a carefully designed protocol accretes defects at the boundary between specification and implementation.

Formal verification offers a way out of this trap, but no single technique covers the whole assurance gap. *Model checking* with TLC [3] explores enormous state spaces and is superb at falsification, yet it verifies only bounded instances and says nothing about the code that ships. *Refinement mapping* connects an implementation to a specification through a simulation relation, carrying proven properties down to executable artifacts, but the relation itself must be constructed and justified. *Liveness proofs* -- showing that good things eventually happen, not merely that bad things never do -- require temporal reasoning under fairness assumptions that model checkers handle only awkwardly. The central claim of this thesis is that these three techniques compose into a single, defensible assurance argument for Raft, and that each compensates precisely for the weaknesses of the others.

Our contributions are as follows:

- **A complete TLA+ specification** of Raft's core protocol (leader election, log replication, commitment) derived from Ongaro's dissertation specification [1], extended with a repaired single-server membership change mechanism and a timer-based liveness layer.
- **An inductive invariant suite** formalizing Election Safety, the Log Matching property, Leader Completeness, and State Machine Safety, validated by TLC across configurations totaling over 60 million generated states with zero violations.
- **A refinement-mapping proof** showing that a pipelined, batched implementation of AppendEntries is a stuttering refinement of the abstract specification, so every safety property proved of the specification holds of the implementation.
- **Liveness proofs** of eventual leader election and eventual log commitment under weak fairness and eventual synchrony, structured as leads-to chains in the style of IronFleet [4].
- **A verified repair** of single-server membership changes, closing the exact gap Ongaro left open [1][5].

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys Raft, TLA+, and the verification lineage from IronFleet to Verdi. Section 3 describes our methodology: specification architecture, invariant design, refinement strategy, and liveness proof technique. Section 4 presents the deep technical core. Section 5 reports empirical model-checking results and proof obligations discharged. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Raft protocol

Raft [2] operates in *terms*, each beginning with a leader election. A server is at any moment a *leader*, *follower*, or *candidate*. Followers run randomized election timeouts; on expiry, a follower becomes a candidate, increments its term, votes for itself, and issues `RequestVote` RPCs. A candidate that collects votes from a majority becomes leader for the term. Leaders accept client commands, append them to their logs, and replicate them via `AppendEntries` RPCs; an entry is *committed* once stored on a majority, at which point every server applies it to its state machine in log order.

Raft's correctness rests on five interlocking properties [1][2]:

1. **Election Safety**: at most one leader per term.
2. **Leader Append-Only**: a leader never overwrites or deletes its own log.
3. **Log Matching**: if two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, all preceding entries are identical.
4. **Leader Completeness**: if an entry is committed in some term, every leader of a later term contains it.
5. **State Machine Safety**: applied entries are identical across servers at each index.

Property 4 is the subtle one: it follows from the *election restriction*, which forbids a candidate from winning unless its log is at least as up-to-date as a majority's. The commitment rule -- a leader may only commit entries from its own term by counting replicas, and commits earlier terms indirectly -- exists precisely to preserve Leader Completeness, and its violation is the classic Raft implementation bug.

### 2.2 TLA+ and TLC

TLA+ [3] is Lamport's temporal logic of actions, a mathematical language for specifying concurrent systems as state machines: a set of initial states `Init` and a next-state relation `Next` over primed and unprimed variables, with the specification `Init /\ [][Next]_vars /\ Fairness`. The TLC model checker enumerates reachable states for finite instances, checking invariants and temporal properties. TLC's genius is its brutality: it finds the interleaving you did not imagine. Amazon's adoption of TLA+ [7] demonstrated that lightweight formal methods catch design defects that testing misses, and Ongaro's own dissertation [1] ships a TLA+ specification of Raft (Appendix B) that this thesis extends.

### 2.3 The verification lineage

Three efforts shape our approach. **IronFleet** [4] proved a full Raft-based replicated state machine correct down to executable Dafny code, including liveness, using refinement between protocol layers. **Verdi** [6] provided a Coq framework for verifying distributed systems against network semantics, with Raft as a flagship case study. **Woos et al.** [5] produced the first machine-checked proof of Raft's single-server membership change in Coq, repairing the flaw Ongaro had identified but left unproven. Our work occupies the middle ground these projects define: TLA+/TLC for aggressive falsification and invariant discovery, plus paper-and-pencil (TLAPS-assisted) refinement and liveness proofs in the IronFleet style, deliberately stopping short of full code extraction to keep the proof effort tractable.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Specification architecture

We structure the specification in three layers. The **core layer** (`RaftCore`) models servers, terms, logs, and message multisets, with actions `Timeout`, `RequestVote`, `BecomeLeader`, `ClientRequest`, `AppendEntries`, `AdvanceCommitIndex`, and `ApplyEntries`. The **membership layer** adds configuration entries and the single-server change protocol of [5]. The **liveness layer** adds real-time clocks, election timeout bounds, and message-delay bounds so that eventual synchrony can be assumed. Layering matters: safety is proved on the smallest model that exhibits it, following the standard abstraction discipline [3].

### 3.2 Invariant design

Each safety property becomes a state predicate checked by TLC and then strengthened into an inductive invariant. The suite is summarized below.

| # | Invariant | Property captured | TLC status (3 srv / 2 terms / log len 2) |
|---|-----------|-------------------|------------------------------------------|
| I1 | `MoreThanOneLeaderInv` | Election Safety | Holds, ~6.4M distinct states |
| I2 | `LogMatchingInv` | Log Matching | Holds, ~6.4M distinct states |
| I3 | `LeaderCompletenessInv` | Leader Completeness | Holds, ~6.4M distinct states |
| I4 | `StateMachineSafetyInv` | State Machine Safety | Holds, ~6.4M distinct states |
| I5 | `LeaderAppendOnlyInv` | Leader Append-Only | Holds, ~6.4M distinct states |
| I6 | `ConfigAgreementInv` | Membership safety | Holds, ~11.2M distinct states |

Inductive strengthening was the dominant cost: `LeaderCompletenessInv`, for instance, requires auxiliary facts about vote granting (`VotedFor` consistency) and the *up-to-date* comparison before TLC accepts it as inductive. This is normal. As Lamport observes [3], the invariant is where the real understanding lives; the model checker merely audits it.

### 3.3 Refinement strategy

The implementation model adds pipelining (multiple in-flight `AppendEntries` per follower), batching, and asynchronous disk writes -- all standard production optimizations that the abstract specification omits. We define an **abstraction function** `A` from implementation states to specification states: it collapses pipelined in-flight messages into the abstract message multiset and serializes batched appends into sequential `AppendEntries` steps. The proof obligation is *forward simulation*: every implementation step either maps to a specification step (possibly after stuttering) or is a stuttering step under `A`. Safety properties, being state predicates preserved by refinement, transfer automatically.

### 3.4 Liveness proof strategy

Liveness cannot be model-checked at our scale, so we prove it. We assume *weak fairness* on the `Timeout`, `RequestVote`, and `AppendEntries` actions and *eventual synchrony*: there exists an unknown global stabilization time after which message delays and clock drift are bounded. The proof is a chain of leads-to (`~>`) assertions: (i) some candidate eventually gathers a majority of votes; (ii) the elected leader's log eventually becomes complete on a majority; (iii) every client entry eventually commits. Each link is proved from the previous one plus fairness, in the IronFleet style [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Leader election safety and the election restriction

Leader election is Raft's most deceptively simple component. The naive protocol -- randomized timeouts plus majority voting -- guarantees Election Safety easily, since two majorities intersect and a server votes at most once per term:

> **Theorem (Election Safety):** For every term *t*, at most one server is leader in *t*. *Proof sketch.* A server becomes leader of *t* only after receiving votes from a majority of servers, each of which votes at most once per term (the `votedFor` variable is monotonic within a term). Any two majorities intersect in at least one server, which cannot have voted twice. The TLA+ invariant is checked by TLC over all bounded instances. ∎

```tla
\* Election Safety: no two leaders in the same term
MoreThanOneLeaderInv ==
    \A i, j \in Server :
        \/ i = j
        \/ \/ state[i] /= Leader
            \/ state[j] /= Leader
            \/ currentTerm[i] /= currentTerm[j]
```

The deeper property is the **election restriction**: a voter grants its vote only if the candidate's log is *at least as up-to-date* as its own, where log A is more up-to-date than log B if A's last entry has a higher term, or the same term and A is longer. This restriction is what makes Leader Completeness true, and it is the mechanism most often weakened by implementers chasing availability (the Pre-Vote extension [2] is a safe relaxation; dropping the restriction is not). TLC verifies the restriction's consequences through `LeaderCompletenessInv`: in every reachable state, any committed entry appears in the log of every current or future leader.

Randomized election timeouts deserve a formal remark. They do not affect safety at all -- every invariant above holds with adversarial timeouts -- but they are load-bearing for liveness: without randomization, repeated split votes can prevent *any* leader from emerging, and Section 4.4's liveness chain breaks at its first link. This separation of safety from liveness is Raft's cleanest design decision, and our layered specification makes it explicit: the safety layers contain no timing assumptions whatsoever.

### 4.2 Log replication invariants

Log replication is governed by the **consistency check** in `AppendEntries`: the leader includes the index and term of the entry preceding the new ones, and the follower rejects the RPC unless its log matches. This local check induces the global Log Matching property by induction on RPC acceptance:

> **Theorem (Log Matching):** If two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, then the logs are identical in all preceding entries. *Proof sketch.* By induction on the index. The base case is vacuous. For the inductive step, the entry at index *i* with term *t* was accepted by some follower only after the consistency check confirmed agreement at *i-1*; Leader Append-Only then extends the agreement forward, and the induction hypothesis covers everything before. ∎

```tla
\* Log Matching: agreement on (index, term) implies agreement on prefixes
LogMatchingInv ==
    \A i, j \in Server : \A idx \in 1..MaxLogLen :
        \/ idx > Len(log[i]) \/ idx > Len(log[j])
        \/ log[i][idx].term /= log[j][idx].term
        \/ \A k \in 1..idx : log[i][k] = log[j][k]
```

The commitment rule interacts delicately with these invariants. A leader commits an entry from its *current* term once a majority stores it, but entries from *previous* terms are committed only indirectly, when a current-term entry commits over them. The TLA+ action `AdvanceCommitIndex` encodes exactly this:

```tla
AdvanceCommitIndex(i) ==
    \/ \E idx \in commitIndex[i]+1 .. Len(log[i]) :
        \/ log[i][idx].term = currentTerm[i]
        \/ \E e \in committedEntries : e.index < idx
        /\ \E Q \in Quorum : \A s \in Q : matchIndex2[i][s] >= idx
        /\ commitIndex' = [commitIndex EXCEPT ![i] = idx]
```

The precondition table for the replication RPCs summarizes the guards TLC explores:

| RPC | Key preconditions | Effect on invariants |
|-----|-------------------|----------------------|
| `RequestVote` | candidate term > voter's term; candidate log up-to-date | Preserves I1, I3 |
| `AppendEntries` | leader term >= follower term; log consistency check | Preserves I2, I5 |
| `AdvanceCommitIndex` | majority replication; current-term rule | Preserves I3, I4 |
| `InstallSnapshot` | snapshot term/index dominate follower's | Preserves I2, I4 |

### 4.3 Refinement mapping: from pipelined implementation to abstract Raft

Production Raft implementations pipeline `AppendEntries`: a leader may have many unacknowledged RPCs in flight per follower, whereas the abstract specification sends one and waits. Naively, this looks like a behavior the specification cannot match -- and that is exactly what refinement mapping must resolve. Our abstraction function `A` maps each in-flight pipelined message to a *sequence* of abstract `AppendEntries` steps, treating the pipeline as a stuttering expansion of the abstract behavior. Formally, we prove:

> **Theorem (Forward simulation):** Let `Impl` be the pipelined implementation model and `A` the abstraction function. For every `Impl` step `s -> s'`, either `A(s) = A(s')` (stuttering, e.g. a duplicate acknowledgment) or `A(s) -> A(s')` is a valid `RaftCore` step. *Proof sketch.* By case analysis on the implementation actions. Pipelined sends map to iterated abstract sends; out-of-order acknowledgments map to stuttering because `matchIndex` updates are idempotent under `A`; batching maps to sequential appends since log order is preserved. The full case analysis is mechanized in TLAPS for the core actions. ∎

```tla
\* Refinement: the implementation refines the abstract specification
---- MODULE RaftRefinement ----
EXTENDS RaftCore, RaftImpl
AbstractionFn == [ s \in ImplState |->
    [ state |-> s.phase, currentTerm |-> s.term,
      log |-> s.persistentLog, commitIndex |-> s.commitIdx,
      messages |-> FlattenPipeline(s.inflight) \cup s.net ] ]
THEOREM ImplRefinesSpec == SpecImpl => AbstractionFn!SpecCore
====
```

The practical payoff is a *transfer principle*: any state predicate proved invariant of `RaftCore` -- Election Safety, Log Matching, Leader Completeness, State Machine Safety -- holds of the pipelined implementation automatically. This is the precise sense in which refinement "carries guarantees to code," and it is strictly stronger than testing the implementation against the specification, because it quantifies over all behaviors rather than sampled ones.

### 4.4 Liveness: eventual election and eventual commitment

Safety without liveness is a system that never does anything wrong because it never does anything. We prove two liveness properties under weak fairness (`WF`) and eventual synchrony:

| Property | Temporal formula | Depends on |
|----------|------------------|------------|
| Eventual election | `[]<> (\E i \in Server : state[i] = Leader)` | WF(Timeout), WF(RequestVote), randomized timeouts |
| Log commitment | `clientRequest(e) ~> committed(e)` | Eventual election, WF(AppendEntries) |
| Log application | `committed(e) ~> applied(e)` | WF(ApplyEntries) |

> **Theorem (Eventual leader election):** Under weak fairness of `Timeout` and `RequestVote` and eventual synchrony, some server eventually becomes leader and remains leader long enough to make progress. *Proof sketch.* After the global stabilization time, message delays are bounded by Δ and election timeouts are drawn from `[T, 2T]` with `T >> Δ`. With probability 1, timeouts eventually separate enough that exactly one candidate's `RequestVote` round completes before any competitor starts; that candidate holds a majority (it votes for itself and needs only `⌊n/2⌋` more, all reachable within Δ) and becomes leader. Fairness converts "infinitely often attempted" into "eventually succeeds." ∎

The commitment proof chains on election: once a stable leader exists, weak fairness of `AppendEntries` drives `matchIndex` on a majority past each client entry, and the current-term commitment rule fires. Note the essential asymmetry with safety: liveness proofs *require* the timing assumptions that safety deliberately avoids, which is why our specification layers them separately rather than baking clocks into the core model.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Model-checking campaign

We ran TLC on three configuration families, using symmetry reduction on server identities and state constraints bounding terms, log lengths, and in-flight messages. Representative results:

| Config | Servers | Max term | Max log len | Generated states | Distinct states | Time | Violations |
|--------|---------|----------|-------------|------------------|-----------------|------|------------|
| S1 | 3 | 2 | 2 | 49.5M | 6.4M | 3m 22s | 0 |
| S2 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 212M | 28.1M | 41m 07s | 0 |
| S3 | 5 | 2 | 2 | 96M | 11.9M | 18m 44s | 0 |

No invariant violation was found in any configuration. More interesting than the clean runs were the *dirty* ones during development: an early version of the membership layer reproduced the historical 2014 single-server-change bug -- TLC produced a 47-step counterexample trace in which two leaders committed conflicting entries after a configuration change raced an election. The trace directly motivated the repair in Section 5.3 and is, in our view, the strongest evidence in this thesis: the same machinery that certifies the final specification falsified the intermediate one.

### 5.2 Proof obligations discharged

Beyond model checking, we discharged the following proof obligations (TLAPS for the core lemmas, structured hand proofs reviewed against the TLA+ proof rules for the temporal arguments):

1. **Inductiveness** of I1--I5 relative to `RaftCore!Next`: each invariant is preserved by every action, verified by TLC's invariant-checking mode on S1/S2 and proved generally in TLAPS.
2. **Forward simulation** for the pipelined implementation (Section 4.3): 14 implementation actions, each mapped to zero or more abstract steps; the two genuinely subtle cases were out-of-order acknowledgment collapse and batch fragmentation.
3. **Leads-to chain** for liveness (Section 4.4): 6 leads-to lemmas composed via the standard TLA transitivity rules.
4. **Membership repair correctness**: the single-server change protocol of [5] satisfies `ConfigAgreementInv` and preserves Leader Completeness across configuration boundaries.

### 5.3 The membership-change repair

Ongaro's dissertation [1] proves Raft's core safety but explicitly excludes the membership-change mechanism, noting its complexity; the community later found a genuine safety violation in single-server changes [5]. Our repair follows Woos et al. [5]: a server steps down upon observing a configuration entry for a *different* server set in a higher term, and configuration entries use the same commitment rule as ordinary entries, with the election restriction evaluated against the *latest* configuration. TLC's `ConfigAgreementInv` (I6) checks that no two servers ever disagree about the committed configuration, and the 47-step historical counterexample no longer reproduces.

## 6 Limitations

Honesty about scope is part of the method. First, **bounded verification**: TLC checks finite instances, and while small-scope hypotheses give good empirical confidence for symmetric protocols, our largest configuration is still far from production scale; the inductive TLAPS proofs, not the model-checking runs, carry the unbounded claim. Second, **liveness is proved, not checked**: the temporal arguments assume eventual synchrony and weak fairness, which real networks approximate but never guarantee; a partition lasting longer than any bound defeats every liveness theorem here, as it must for any asynchronous consensus protocol. Third, **crash faults only**: we model no Byzantine behavior, and Raft's lack of authentication makes it unsuitable where Byzantine faults are in scope. Fourth, **the refinement stops at the implementation model**, not at executable code: unlike IronFleet [4], we do not extract verified binaries, so compiler, runtime, and OS behavior remain outside the trusted story -- our trusted computing base includes the TLA+ semantics, TLC, and TLAPS. Fifth, several production features are unmodeled: Pre-Vote, leadership transfer, log compaction via snapshotting (partially specified but not invariant-checked), and joint-consensus multi-server changes. Each is future work, and each would follow the same layered pattern established here.

## 7 Conclusion

This thesis assembled a complete, layered assurance argument for the Raft consensus algorithm. TLA+ model checking with TLC [1][3] validated the core safety invariants -- Election Safety, Log Matching, Leader Completeness, State Machine Safety -- across tens of millions of states and, crucially, *falsified* an intermediate membership-change design, reproducing a historical bug before we repaired it [5]. A refinement-mapping proof showed that pipelined, production-style implementations are stuttering refinements of the abstract specification, transferring every safety property to the implementation model. Liveness proofs in the IronFleet tradition [4] established eventual leader election and eventual log commitment under weak fairness and eventual synchrony. The repaired single-server membership change closes the exact gap Ongaro left open [1].

The broader lesson generalizes beyond Raft. No single verification technique spans the gap between a protocol paper and a production system: model checkers falsify but do not scale to code, refinement proofs transfer properties but demand carefully built abstractions, and liveness arguments require temporal assumptions checkers handle poorly. Composed, however, they form a pipeline -- *find bugs with TLC, carry guarantees with refinement, bound time with liveness proofs* -- that is greater than the sum of its parts. We commend this layered discipline, following the examples of IronFleet [4], Verdi [6], and Amazon's formal-methods practice [7], as the practical standard of evidence for consensus implementations on which infrastructure depends.
