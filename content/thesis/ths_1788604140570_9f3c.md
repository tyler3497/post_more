# Concurrent Heap Allocator Design in the Modern Era: Free-List Sharding in mimalloc, Message-Passing Deallocation in snmalloc, Delayed Free, and the Heap-Security Frontier

## Abstract

Dynamic memory allocation remains one of the most consequential systems problems of the multicore era: every allocation request interleaves with threads, NUMA topology, operating-system paging, and the adversary. This thesis surveys the architectural advances defining modern heap allocator design, focusing on *mimalloc*, the Microsoft Research allocator built on per-page **free-list sharding** [1]; *snmalloc*, which replaces remote-free locking with **message-passing** deallocation [3]; and the hardened allocators *Scudo* and *PartitionAlloc*, which trade throughput for probabilistic protection via quarantine, randomization, and hardware tagging [5]. We formalize the segregated-size-class allocator as an abstract machine, derive contention and fragmentation bounds for sharded and message-passing designs, and examine **delayed free** as both a performance technique and a security mitigation. We evaluate allocator-induced pathologies — false sharing of metadata, blowup, per-thread cache divergence — and the impact of huge pages and NUMA-aware span placement. The central claim: modern allocators are best understood as *concurrent data-structure servers* with an explicit security contract, not mere memory managers.

## 1. Introduction

The `malloc` interface has changed little since the 1970s, yet the machinery behind it has been rewritten three times. The first generation — Doug Lea's *dlmalloc* and its descendants — treated the heap as a single data structure protected by a global lock, optimizing for single-threaded fragmentation and speed. The second generation, led by *jemalloc* and *tcmalloc*, recognized that concurrency is the dominant cost: per-thread caches, arena sharding, and span-based page management reduced lock contention to the point that allocation could scale nearly linearly on dozens of cores. The third generation, which is the subject of this thesis, starts from a different premise: that **locality, not merely parallelism**, and **security, not merely speed**, are the first-order design objectives [1].

Two artifacts exemplify this shift. *mimalloc*, introduced by Leijen, Zorn, and de Moura at Microsoft Research in 2019 [1], observes that sequential allocations should usually come from the same 64 KiB page, and achieves this through **free-list multi-sharding**: each page carries not one but three free lists — a local free list for the owning thread, a `thread_free` list that remote threads update with a single compare-and-swap (CAS), and the page's main allocation list. Because a program easily maintains *thousands* of these lists, contention is distributed statistically rather than engineered away with complex lock-free algorithms. The result is an allocator with bounded metadata overhead (~0.2%), bounded worst-case allocation time, and measured speedups of 7–14% over *tcmalloc* and *jemalloc* on real workloads such as Redis [1].

*snmalloc*, developed by Liétar, Parkinson, Shamis, Wintersteiger, Chisnall, and colleagues at Microsoft Research and Imperial College London [3], attacks the *producer/consumer* workload — where objects are allocated on one thread and freed on another — a pattern that defeats the thread-caching strategies of its predecessors. Rather than locking a remote slab or bouncing cache lines on a shared free list, snmalloc treats deallocation as **message passing**: a freeing thread enqueues the object into the owning thread's message queue, and thousands of remote frees are absorbed with a *single* atomic operation. It couples this with a bump-pointer free-list data structure requiring only 64 bits of metadata per 64 KiB slab [3].

In parallel, a security-driven reimagining of allocation has unfolded. *Scudo*, the LLVM hardened allocator deployed on Android and Fuchsia [5], and *PartitionAlloc*, Chromium's allocator, embed mitigations — guard pages, randomized chunk placement, delayed-free quarantine, and Memory Tagging Extension (MTE) support — directly into allocation policy. These mechanisms are not free: quarantine delays recycling and inflates resident memory; randomization degrades locality. Understanding their cost models is now as important as understanding throughput.

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the classical segregated-free-list architecture and the formal vocabulary of fragmentation, contention, and blowup. Section 3 states our methodology: a combination of published measurement, analytical modeling, and code-level architectural analysis. Section 4 performs the deep dive — mimalloc's sharding, snmalloc's message passing, delayed free and hazard epochs, NUMA and huge-page awareness, and the security subsystem. Section 5 derives empirical comparisons and formal statements about bounds. Section 6 catalogs limitations and open problems. Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The segregated-size-class allocator

Almost all modern allocators share a common skeleton. Requests are rounded up to the nearest member of a fixed set of **size classes** — typically growing geometrically with a ratio around 1.125–1.25, so that *internal fragmentation* (waste within a block) is bounded by a constant factor [1]. For each size class, the allocator maintains pages or slabs whose blocks are all of that class. Allocation pops from a free list; deallocation pushes back. Large requests bypass the class system and are satisfied with dedicated spans mapped directly from the operating system.

> **Definition 1 (Fragmentation).** *External fragmentation* is memory lost because free blocks are scattered across pages in unusable combinations; *internal fragmentation* is memory lost inside allocated blocks beyond the requested size. A size-class system with growth ratio $r$ bounds internal fragmentation by $(r-1)$ of the request [1].

### 2.2 Contention and the blowup pathology

When threads share an allocator, two failure modes dominate. **Contention** arises when multiple threads hammer the same metadata — the classic symptom is cache-line bouncing on a shared free-list head, measurable as a collapse in scalability. **Blowup**, characterized by Berger, McKinley, Blumofe, and Wilson, is subtler: an allocator may consume asymptotically more memory than the program needs, by a factor proportional to the number of threads, because per-thread caches hoard memory that other threads could use. mimalloc explicitly claims boundedness — no blowup, bounded worst-case allocation time — as a design invariant [1].

| Pathology | Symptom | Classical remedy | Modern remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Metadata contention | Scalability collapse | Per-thread caches | Free-list sharding [1] |
| Blowup | Unbounded RSS growth | Cache size caps | Bounded per-page lists [1] |
| Remote-free storms | Lock convoy on slab | Deferred coalescing | Message passing [3] |
| False sharing | Cache-line ping-pong | Padding, coloring | Page-local heaps [1] |

### 2.3 The thread-cache dilemma

Thread-local caching is the workhorse optimization: freeing a block into a thread-local list costs no synchronization, and reallocation is a pointer bump. But pure thread caching breaks down on **producer/consumer** workloads, where thread A allocates and thread B frees: B's cache fills with objects owned by A's pages, and without a return path the memory is stranded [3]. snmalloc's message-passing design [3] and mimalloc's per-page `thread_free` list [1] are two different answers to the same question: *how does a freed block get home cheaply?*

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis follows three complementary methods:

1. **Architectural reconstruction.** We reconstruct allocator data structures from the primary design documents: the mimalloc technical report MSR-TR-2019-18 [1], its official documentation [2], the snmalloc ISMM 2019 paper [3] and repository [4], and the Scudo hardened-allocator specification [5].
2. **Analytical modeling.** Where the literature states invariants — e.g., mimalloc's 0.2% metadata bound, or snmalloc's single-atomic batch absorption — we restate them as formal claims and examine the conditions under which they hold.
3. **Empirical synthesis.** We report the published benchmark comparisons (mimalloc versus jemalloc/tcmalloc on Redis and concurrent suites [1]; snmalloc on producer/consumer microbenchmarks [3]) and their implications for workload sensitivity.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 mimalloc: free-list sharding as a statistical contention strategy

The central insight of mimalloc is deceptively simple: *do not share free lists; shard them so finely that sharing becomes a rare event* [1]. Memory is obtained from the OS in 4 MiB **segments**, subdivided into **pages** — typically 64 KiB on 64-bit systems — each page dedicated to a single size class [2]. Every page carries three free lists:

- **`free`**: the page's main allocation list, used by the owning thread;
- **`local_free`**: blocks freed by the owning thread, collected lazily;
- **`thread_free`**: an *atomic* list to which *any* thread may push a block belonging to this page, using a single lock-free CAS.

Because there are thousands of pages in a live heap, there are thousands of independent `thread_free` lists, and the probability that two threads concurrently push to the same list is small — mimalloc deliberately invokes the analogy to randomized data structures such as skip lists, where a random oracle substitutes for elaborate balancing logic [2]. After a fixed number of allocations the page's free list is deliberately exhausted, forcing the thread through a slower path that merges `local_free` and `thread_free` back into `free` — an amortized maintenance design that bounds the worst-case allocation time (*wcat*) [1].

Three further properties deserve emphasis:

- **Page-local heaps.** Each thread owns a heap whose pages are disjoint from other threads' pages. Sequential allocations by one thread therefore land in the same page, improving *application-level* reference locality — a deliberate break from allocators that optimize only allocator-internal costs [1].
- **Eager page purging.** When a page becomes empty — more likely under sharding, since blocks of one class cluster per page — mimalloc marks it unused to the OS (reset or decommit), reducing real memory pressure and long-run fragmentation [2].
- **Secure mode.** An optional build adds guard pages, randomized allocation, and encrypted free lists at roughly 10% average benchmark cost [2].

> **Theorem 1 (Sharding contention bound, informal).** *Let $T$ threads free blocks uniformly at random across $P$ pages. The expected number of CAS retries on any `thread_free` list in mimalloc is $O(T^2/P)$; as $P \gg T^2$, remote frees are effectively wait-free in expectation.* This is the statistical core of the design: contention is managed by *multiplicity*, not by *mechanism* [1][2].

### 4.2 snmalloc: deallocation as message passing

snmalloc begins where thread caching fails: the producer/consumer workload, in which deallocation happens on a different thread than allocation [3]. Its design has two pillars.

**Pillar 1: lock-free remote-free via message queues.** Each thread owns a lock-free message queue. A thread freeing an object owned by another allocator does not touch the remote slab at all; it enqueues the object as a *message*. The owning thread drains its queue in batches during its own allocation path, absorbing *thousands* of remote deallocations with a *single* atomic operation [3][4]. No locks are taken on the free path, regardless of ownership — the fast path has just two branches on Linux/Clang [4].

**Pillar 2: the bump-pointer free list.** snmalloc's slab metadata is strikingly economical: just 64 bits of metadata suffice for each 64 KiB slab [3]. Allocation resembles bump-pointer allocation within a slab. The implementation has since evolved significantly — the metadata layout changed substantially after the 2019 paper — but the message-passing remote-free mechanism remains the stable core [4].

A useful contrast with mimalloc clarifies the design space:

| Dimension | mimalloc [1] | snmalloc [3] |
|---|---|---|
| Remote-free primitive | Per-page atomic `thread_free` CAS | Per-allocator message queue |
| Atomic ops per remote free | One CAS | Amortized ~1 per *batch* |
| Metadata per slab | Page headers + 3 lists | 64 bits per 64 KiB slab |
| Locality objective | Same-page sequential allocation | Producer/consumer throughput |
| Large-object strategy | Segment-level pages (up to huge) | Large address ranges, sparse metadata |

> **Theorem 2 (Batch absorption).** *In snmalloc, $k$ remote frees enqueued to one allocator's message queue are reclaimed with $O(1)$ atomic operations, independent of $k$, when drained in a single batch [3].* The amortized synchronization cost per remote free thus tends to zero as batch size grows — the precise property that makes producer/consumer workloads tractable.

### 4.3 Delayed free: quarantine, hazard epochs, and deferred reclamation

**Delayed free** — holding freed blocks out of circulation for a time — serves two masters. As a *performance* technique, deferring the return of remote frees avoids synchronizing on every deallocation; as a *security* technique, it converts deterministic use-after-free exploitation into a probabilistic race the attacker usually loses.

Scudo's **quarantine** is the canonical security instance: deallocated chunks are retained in a quarantine buffer and only recycled once size thresholds are crossed, so a dangling pointer is less likely to reference attacker-controlled fresh data [5]. The design exposes the cost explicitly: quarantine is "fairly costly in terms of performance and memory footprint," is disabled by default, and is tuned through options such as `quarantine_size_kb` (256 KiB default on 64-bit) and `quarantine_max_chunk_size` [5]. A per-thread quarantine cache offloads the global quarantine to reduce contention — the same sharding instinct seen in mimalloc, now in service of security.

A second, subtler form of delayed free appears in **hazard-epoch** and RCU-style reclamation: blocks are retired into an epoch and only reused once all threads have passed a quiescent point — a correctness-flavored delay rather than a security-flavored one.

The unifying principle is temporal decoupling: *the moment of `free()` is separated from the moment of reuse*. The allocator chooses the delay distribution — quarantine size, epoch length, message-queue drain interval — and with it, the position on the security/performance Pareto frontier.

```c
/* Epoch-based deferred reclamation: retire now, reclaim after a grace period */
typedef struct retired_block {
    struct retired_block* next;
    void (*reclaim)(void*);   /* runs once all threads pass quiescent state */
} retired_block_t;

void retire_block(retired_block_t* b) {
    unsigned e = current_epoch();
    enqueue(&retire_list[e % N_EPOCHS], b);   /* no synchronization on free path */
}

void quiescent_point(void) {
    /* advance global epoch once every thread has checked in */
    if (atomic_all_threads_observed(global_epoch))
        try_reclaim(retire_list[global_epoch % N_EPOCHS]);
}
```

### 4.4 NUMA awareness and huge pages

On multisocket machines, *where* memory lives matters as much as *how* it is managed. A page allocated on socket 0 and touched from socket 1 pays a cross-interconnect latency penalty on every access. Modern allocators respond in three ways:

1. **Per-thread heaps as NUMA proxies.** Since threads are typically pinned near their data, mimalloc-style page-local heaps implicitly cluster memory near the allocating core [1].
2. **Span placement policy.** Allocators request large spans from the OS and carve them locally; first-touch page placement then binds physical pages to the touching NUMA node — an OS cooperation the allocator must not defeat by migrating spans across threads carelessly.
3. **Huge-page exploitation.** 2 MiB pages shrink TLB pressure for large heaps, but interact badly with fine-grained sharding: a sparsely used 64 KiB page inside a 2 MiB huge page still pins the whole huge page. How eager purging [2] interacts with transparent huge pages remains an open measurement problem.

### 4.5 The security subsystem: Scudo, PartitionAlloc, and hardware tagging

Hardened allocators invert the classical priority order: security first, performance second, with the performance budget spent explicitly. The Scudo design [5] combines:

- **Segregated Primary/Secondary allocators.** Small objects come from a size-classed Primary; large allocations are individually mapped by the Secondary and *unmapped on free*, so large-object use-after-free deterministically faults.
- **Quarantine** (Section 4.3): delayed reuse of small chunks [5].
- **Randomization.** Chunk placement within the Primary is randomized, and thread-cache assignment can be randomized, defeating deterministic heap-layout manipulation [5].
- **Checksum-protected metadata and zero-on-free options**, raising the bar for metadata-corruption exploits.
- **MTE integration.** On ARMv8.5+ hardware, Scudo supports Memory Tagging Extension: 4-bit tags per 16-byte granule, checked by hardware on access, turning linear overflows and use-after-free into probabilistic faults detected at the point of misuse [5].

PartitionAlloc (Chromium) follows a parallel philosophy: *partitioning* — isolating object types into separate regions so that a corruption in one partition cannot forge pointers into another — plus delayed-free quarantine and, increasingly, MTE-backed tagging. **Heap security is now a first-class allocator subsystem**, with its own configuration surface (Scudo exposes dozens of runtime options [5]) and its own performance accounting.

> **Theorem 3 (Quarantine probabilistic bound, informal).** *If freed chunks are quarantined until $Q$ bytes of subsequent allocation occur, an attacker's dangling pointer references the original (now-quarantined) contents with probability approaching 1 as $Q$ exceeds the attacker's allocation budget; reuse-before-expiry requires winning a race against the quarantine drain rate [5].* Security here is measured in *economics*, not absolutes — the quarantine taxes the attacker's memory budget.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Published performance evidence

The mimalloc technical report evaluates the allocator against jemalloc, tcmalloc, and others across sequential and concurrent benchmarks [1]. The headline results:

- **Redis workload:** mimalloc outperforms tcmalloc by ~7% and jemalloc by ~14% in throughput, with similar peak memory usage [1].
- **Concurrent scalability:** mimalloc "consistently outperforms" the compared allocators across the benchmark suite, attributed to the near-absence of shared mutable metadata — the sharding design removes the scalability cliffs that thread-cache flushes and arena locks create [1].
- **Locality dividend:** because allocations cluster per page, application-level cache behavior improves; an allocator optimization visible in *program* performance counters, not just allocator microbenchmarks [1].

For snmalloc, the ISMM 2019 evaluation focuses on producer/consumer benchmarks — exactly the workload where thread-caching allocators degrade — and reports superior performance to existing allocators there, with the message-passing scheme absorbing remote frees without locks [3]. The snmalloc authors note that the implementation has evolved since the paper, and current performance should be measured against the living codebase [4].

### 5.2 Formal statements

We collect the chapter's claims in proof-sketch form:

> **Claim 1 (mimalloc metadata bound).** *mimalloc's metadata overhead is approximately 0.2% of managed memory, with internal fragmentation bounded by the size-class growth ratio [1].* *Proof sketch.* Page headers are $O(1)$ per 64 KiB page; free lists reuse the blocks themselves as intrusive list nodes, so no per-block external metadata is stored. The class ratio $r = 1.125$ bounds per-block waste by $(r-1) \approx 12.5\%$.*

> **Claim 2 (snmalloc fast path).** *The snmalloc allocation fast path executes with two branches and no atomic operations for thread-local frees [3][4].* *Proof sketch.* Ownership is determined by address-range metadata lookup; same-owner frees push to a thread-local structure with no synchronization; remote frees enqueue a message — a single atomic enqueue amortized over batches.*

> **Claim 3 (Quarantine memory tax).** *A quarantine of size $Q$ increases steady-state RSS by up to $Q$ bytes relative to immediate reuse [5].* This is the fundamental security/memory trade-off of delayed free, and the reason Scudo disables quarantine by default and exposes it as a tunable [5].

### 5.3 Allocator-induced performance pathologies

Beyond raw throughput, allocators induce *pathologies* — behaviors where the allocator, not the program, determines performance:

1. **False sharing of metadata.** A shared free-list head on one cache line, touched by many cores, produces coherence traffic proportional to operation rate. Sharding (mimalloc) and message passing (snmalloc) both eliminate the shared head as a hot spot [1][3].
2. **Blowup.** Per-thread caches that never return memory create RSS proportional to thread count. Bounded designs — mimalloc's per-page lists, snmalloc's batched drains — keep the bound independent of thread count [1][3].
3. **Cache divergence on producer/consumer.** When frees land on a different thread than allocations, naive thread caches strand memory; the workload snmalloc was built to serve [3].
4. **Purge latency.** Eager page purging reclaims memory but issues `madvise`/decommit syscalls; mis-tuned thresholds convert memory savings into syscall storms [2].
5. **Quarantine bloat.** Security delay directly inflates footprint; operators must size `quarantine_size_kb` against the threat model [5].

## 6. Limitations

No allocator dominates all workloads, and the designs surveyed here carry explicit limitations:

- **mimalloc's statistical argument has tail risk.** Theorem 1 is *expected-case*: adversarial free patterns (all threads freeing into one page) collapse the sharding advantage back to CAS contention on a single list. The design bets real programs are not adversarial — usually true, but a caveat for hostile multi-tenant settings.
- **snmalloc's message queues add latency to reuse.** A remotely freed block is unavailable until the owner drains its queue; tight cross-thread allocate-free-reuse cycles can inflate footprint. The post-2019 metadata redesign [4] also means the paper's constants no longer describe the implementation.
- **Delayed free is a tax, not a fix.** Quarantine raises the cost of use-after-free exploitation but does not eliminate the bug class; combined with MTE it becomes strong, but MTE requires recent ARM hardware with granule-alignment costs [5].
- **Huge-page and NUMA interactions are under-studied.** The interplay of 64 KiB sharded pages with transparent huge pages, and of per-thread heaps with thread migration, lacks rigorous measurement.
- **Formal verification lags.** The lock-free protocols (message queues, multi-shard CAS lists) are subtle; machine-checked proofs of linearizability remain rare, though the snmalloc authors' verification background suggests a natural next step [3].

## 7. Conclusion

Three ideas define the modern heap allocator. **Sharding** — mimalloc's thousands of per-page free lists — shows that contention can be dissolved statistically rather than synchronized away [1]. **Message passing** — snmalloc's lock-free remote-free queues — shows that cross-thread deallocation is a communication problem with a communication-theoretic solution [3]. **Delayed free** — Scudo's quarantine and its epoch-based cousins — shows that *when* memory is reused is as consequential as *how*, for security as much as for performance [5].

The deeper lesson is a change of identity. The allocator is no longer a subroutine that hands out bytes; it is a concurrent, NUMA-aware, security-critical subsystem whose policy choices — shard granularity, drain interval, quarantine size, tag width — are exposed to operators as first-class configuration. As hardware tagging becomes ubiquitous, the allocator's security contract will harden from probabilistic to architectural. The research frontier is therefore the co-design of allocator, operating-system paging, and hardware memory safety — a stack in which the humble free list, sharded a thousand ways, still has the last word.
