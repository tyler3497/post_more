# Deterministic Concurrency Control in Distributed Databases: The Calvin Protocol, Ordered Locking, and the Epoch Architecture of Aria

## Abstract

Deterministic concurrency control replaces runtime coordination — distributed locking, deadlock detection, and two-phase commit — with agreement on a *global transaction order* before execution begins. Given the same ordered input, every replica independently executes the same transactions and reaches the same final state, so replication degenerates into log shipping and atomic commit becomes a scheduling invariant. This thesis formalizes the deterministic model pioneered by Calvin [1] and refined by Aria [2]: a sequencing layer compiling requests into epoch batches, a scheduling layer acquiring locks strictly in input order to guarantee deadlock freedom, and execution conflict-equivalent to the input serial order. We prove deadlock freedom under ordered locking, derive the determinism constraints (known inputs, deterministic functions, deterministic aborts), and contrast Aria's execute-then-reorder epochs, which remove the known-read/write-set requirement. Synthesizing the empirical record — Calvin's near-linear scale-out, Aria's up-to-2× gains, SLOG's order-of-magnitude latency reduction [5], and the studies of Ren et al. [3] and Harding et al. [4] — we locate where determinism wins (high contention, deadlock-heavy workloads) and where it loses (heterogeneous clusters, unknown access patterns, overload without abort).

## 1. Introduction

Distributed transaction processing is dominated by a single, expensive phenomenon: **coordination at commit time**. In a classic shared-nothing deployment, a transaction spanning partitions acquires locks across machines, survives a prepare phase, and commits or aborts in a second phase. Every network round trip holds locks that serialize contending transactions, every deadlock detector adds background cost, and every nondeterministic abort forces a replica-coordination decision.

Deterministic database systems attack this problem at its root. Instead of discovering a serializable order *during* execution — through lock managers that grant locks on demand and certifiers that validate at commit — they **fix the serialization order before execution**. A preprocessing (sequencing) layer collects client transactions, arranges them into a globally agreed sequence, and ships that sequence to every replica. Because execution is deterministic with respect to the input sequence, replicas never diverge: there is no need to coordinate the *outcome* of a transaction, only its *input*. This inversion — agreeing on order first, executing second — eliminates distributed deadlock, removes the two-phase commit protocol for atomicity, and reduces replication to asynchronous input shipping [1][3].

Two landmark systems define the design space. **Calvin** (SIGMOD 2012) introduced the three-layer architecture — sequencing, scheduling, storage — and *deterministic locking*: locks are acquired strictly in global transaction order, making the equivalent serial order equal to the input order by construction [1]. **Aria** (VLDB 2020) relaxed Calvin's most restrictive requirement — read/write sets known before execution — by executing a batch against a common snapshot and then *deterministically* selecting the committable subset, achieving up to 2× the throughput of Calvin, BOHM, and PWV on YCSB and TPC-C [2]. Around these systems sits a rich empirical literature: Ren et al.'s study of determinism's tradeoffs [3], Harding et al.'s protocol evaluation [4], geo-replicated extensions such as SLOG [5], and recent surveys of the deterministic landscape [7].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The nondeterministic baseline

Traditional distributed concurrency control couples *ordering* with *execution*. In distributed two-phase locking (2PL), a transaction acquires locks as it touches data, potentially across many nodes; a distributed deadlock detector (or timeout-based abort) resolves cycles; and two-phase commit (2PC) then decides atomicity — with locks held across network round trips, abort storms under contention, and per-transaction commit coordination. Harding et al. show that under high update rates, no-wait 2PL outperforms other nondeterministic protocols by up to 54% — and by up to 78% under high contention — yet deterministic protocols outperform *all* nondeterministic protocols under the highest contention levels for simple transactions [4].

### 2.2 The state machine lineage

The intellectual ancestor of deterministic databases is the **database state machine approach** of Pedone, Guerraoui, and Schiper [6]: with *active replication*, every replica receives the same requests in the same order (agreement + total order, via atomic broadcast) and, assuming deterministic request processing, replicas stay consistent without per-transaction coordination. Deterministic concurrency control generalizes this insight from replicated state machines to *partitioned, concurrent* transaction execution: the "request" becomes a batch of transactions, and "deterministic processing" becomes a scheduler whose output is a function of the input order alone. Early partitioned systems such as H-Store and VoltDB adopted a restricted determinism — serial execution per partition — but handled multi-partition transactions with conventional distributed machinery, inheriting its costs [1]. Calvin's contribution was to make the *entire* scheduling layer deterministic, including distributed transactions.

### 2.3 What "deterministic" guarantees

Ren et al. give the crisp definition this thesis adopts: a deterministic database system guarantees that, given the same transactional input, it **always ends in the same final state** [3] — strictly stronger than classical ACID, which requires only equivalence to *some* serial order. Determinism pins the equivalent serial order to the input order itself. The payoff is threefold: **replication without coordination** (identical input batches yield identical states at independent replicas [1]); **no distributed commit protocol** (outcomes are pure functions of the agreed order [1]); and **deadlock freedom** plus immunity of outcomes to nondeterministic failures such as node crashes — recovery is log replay from a checkpoint, not coordinated abort [3]. The price is reduced executional flexibility: unknown access patterns, interactive stalls, and overload conditions that nondeterministic systems relieve by aborting all become problematic [3].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is analytical and comparative: we formalize deterministic scheduling as a mathematical object, prove its core invariants, and then evaluate the design space against the published empirical record, treating each cited measurement as primary evidence.

### 3.1 Formal model

Let a *batch* be a finite sequence of transactions $B = \langle T_1, T_2, \dots, T_n \rangle$ produced by the sequencing layer. Each transaction $T_i$ has a read set $R(T_i)$ and a write set $W(T_i)$ over a shared data domain. Two transactions *conflict* if $(R(T_i) \cup W(T_i)) \cap W(T_j) \neq \emptyset$ for $i \neq j$.

> **Definition (Deterministic serializability).** A scheduler is *deterministically serializable* for batch $B$ if every execution it produces is conflict-equivalent to the serial execution of $B$ in input order $\langle T_1, \dots, T_n \rangle$, and this property holds at every replica independently.

> **Theorem (Ordered locking ⇒ deterministic serializability).** If every transaction acquires all locks it needs before executing any operation, locks are granted strictly in batch order (no $T_j$ acquires a lock held or requested by $T_i$ with $i < j$ until $T_i$ releases it), then the execution is conflict-equivalent to the input serial order and deadlock-free.

*Proof sketch.* For any conflicting pair $T_i, T_j$ with $i < j$, ordered granting ensures $T_i$ holds every conflicting lock before $T_j$ acquires any, so conflict order matches input order for all pairs — conflict-equivalence to the serial input order. For deadlock freedom, suppose a waits-for cycle $T_{i_1} \to \dots \to T_{i_k} \to T_{i_1}$. Each edge $T_a \to T_b$ implies the holder requested first, i.e., $b < a$ in batch order; traversing the cycle yields $i_1 > i_2 > \dots > i_k > i_1$, a contradiction. ∎

### 3.2 Determinism constraints

The model exposes three constraints that any deterministic system must enforce, each a source of real-world friction [3]:

1. **Known inputs.** The transaction's logic and parameters must be fully specified before scheduling — no interactive stalls, no mid-transaction user input. Nondeterministic functions (`RAND()`, `NOW()`) must be replaced by deterministic equivalents fixed at sequencing time.
2. **Read/write set knowledge (Calvin-class).** Ordered locking requires knowing $R(T_i)$ and $W(T_i)$ before execution — trivial for primary-key equality lookups, costly for predicate-based access, which needs programmer annotation or speculation.
3. **Deterministic abort/commit.** A transaction may only abort if every replica reaches the abort decision independently from the same input (e.g., a deterministic constraint violation). Arbitrary load-shedding aborts are forbidden.

Aria's central innovation is relaxing constraint (2): by executing first and analyzing conflicts afterward, it supports transactions whose read/write sets emerge during execution [2].

### 3.3 Evaluation method

We evaluate along four axes drawn from the literature — *throughput scalability*, *contention behavior*, *latency profile* (sequencing latency added vs. commit latency removed), and *fault tolerance* — reporting each published measurement with its workload context, since determinism's advantages are workload-dependent rather than universal [3].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Calvin's three-layer architecture

Calvin decomposes transaction processing into three layers [1]:

**Sequencing layer.** Time is divided into epochs (10 ms in the original design). During each epoch, every machine's sequencer collects client requests; at epoch end, requests are compiled into a *batch*. The layer is distributed across all replicas and partitioned across every machine within each replica, avoiding a single-point sequencer bottleneck. In asynchronous replication mode, one replica is designated master: master sequencers forward each compiled batch to slave sequencers in the replication group — minimizing latency before execution at the cost of more complex failover, since all nodes must agree on the last valid batch after a master-sequencer failure [1].

**Scheduling (locking) layer.** The scheduler performs *deterministic locking*: for each transaction it requests read/write locks in batch order. Every replica processes the same batch in the same order, so lock acquisition is globally consistent without communication, and the equivalent serial order is the batch order by construction. Calvin classifies transactions as *single- vs. multi-partition* and *read-write vs. read-only*, with read-only transactions eligible for fast paths on fresh replicas [1].

**Storage layer.** Below the deterministic scheduler sits a conventional storage engine (the prototype supported disk-based storage, not merely in-memory tables), which executes operations once the scheduler releases them. Determinism is thus a *scheduling* property, orthogonal to storage.

```python
# Calvin-style deterministic scheduler (simplified)
def schedule_batch(batch):
    granted, waiting, held = [], [], {}
    for i, txn in enumerate(batch):          # batch: txns in global input order
        blocked = any(r in held and held[r] != i
                      for r in txn.read_set | txn.write_set)
        if blocked:
            waiting.append((i, txn))
        else:
            for r in txn.read_set | txn.write_set:
                held[r] = i
            granted.append((i, txn))
    return granted, waiting   # deadlock-free: waits-for edges point to earlier indices
```

### 4.2 Ordered locking and the elimination of deadlock and 2PC

The deepest consequence of ordered locking is negative: two entire subsystems disappear. **Deadlock detection vanishes** because cycles are structurally impossible — Ren et al. identify deadlock avoidance as *by far the greatest advantage* of deterministic systems for scalable transactional performance [3]. **Two-phase commit vanishes** because atomicity no longer requires agreement on outcomes: every participant holds the same ordered batch, applies the same deterministic schedule, and reaches the same commit/abort decision. Calvin's paper frames this as its key technical feature: a scheduling and replication layer using "a deterministic ordering guarantee to significantly reduce the normally prohibitive contention costs associated with distributed transactions" and thereby "enables the elimination of distributed commit protocols" [1].

The trade is precise. Ordered locking serializes *lock acquisition* even for non-conflicting transactions: if a local transaction precedes a distributed one in the global order, remote participants wait for its locks, so the cluster can run at the speed of its slowest node under mixed workloads [3]. Nondeterministic systems can abort or reorder around a straggler — at the cost of exactly the coordination determinism eliminates.

```rust
// Ordered lock manager: grant order is always the batch order.
struct OrderedLockManager {
    next_grant: usize,          // next batch index allowed to acquire
    queues: Vec<Vec<LockReq>>, // per-transaction requests, in batch order
}
impl OrderedLockManager {
    fn try_acquire(&mut self, txn_idx: usize, req: LockReq) -> bool {
        // Acquire only when all earlier transactions finished requesting.
        if txn_idx != self.next_grant { return false; }
        // ... grant req, advance next_grant when this txn's requests complete
        true
    }
}
```

### 4.3 Aria: epoch-based execution and deterministic reordering

Aria keeps Calvin's epoch/batch structure but inverts the scheduling problem. Its key observation is that Calvin's hardest requirement — knowing read/write sets before execution — is impractical for transactions accessing records through complex predicates over non-key attributes [2]. Aria's answer is a two-phase epoch:

1. **Execution phase.** The batch executes against the *same database snapshot*, without acquiring locks. Each transaction records the read/write sets it actually touched — discovered during execution, not declared beforehand.
2. **Commit phase.** Using the recorded sets, Aria deterministically selects the committable subset preserving serializability, applying a **deterministic reordering mechanism** that reorders the batch to maximize commits. Every replica performs the identical analysis on identical input, so the commit decision is consistent without coordination.

Aria thus supports the full OLTP generality Calvin could not — predicate reads, foreign-key traversals, data-dependent access — while retaining replication-as-input-shipping and 2PC elimination. Empirically, Aria "outperforms systems with conventional nondeterministic concurrency control algorithms and the state-of-the-art deterministic databases by up to a factor of two on two popular benchmarks (YCSB and TPC-C)" [2], against BOHM, PWV, and Calvin, which rely on a priori dependency graphs or ordered locks.

Conceptually, Aria shifts determinism from the *locking* layer to the *certification* layer: the deterministic object is no longer the lock acquisition order but the function mapping (batch, recorded read/write sets) to (commit set, serialization order). This strictly generalizes Calvin's ordered locking — the special case where the commit set is the whole batch and the order is the input order.

### 4.4 Geo-replicated determinism: SLOG and the locality principle

Determinism's replication advantage compounds with distance. In a geo-replicated deployment, 2PC's round trips become intercontinental. **SLOG** (VLDB 2020) shows that determinism plus *data locality* breaks the classic trilemma — strict serializability, low-latency writes, high throughput — for workloads with physical region locality [5]: each region keeps a local log of input transactions; single-region transactions commit locally; cross-region transactions are forwarded to one node that decides a *global order*, replayed deterministically by every shard replica. Followers need only the log, not a commit protocol. Experiments show latency reduced by more than an order of magnitude relative to Spanner and Calvin while sustaining high throughput under contention [5]. Recent surveys classify Calvin and Aria as the two canonical deterministic databases with multi-master replication — replicating SQL inputs rather than write sets, Calvin via ordered locks and Aria via dependency analysis and reordering [7]. The principle: *determinism converts coordination problems into ordering problems, and ordering problems can be solved where the data lives.*

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Synthesized measurements

The table below consolidates the headline empirical claims of the deterministic concurrency control literature. Figures are reported as published, with workload context; they are not directly comparable across rows because hardware, benchmarks, and baselines differ — the comparison that matters is each study's *internal* contrast.

| System / Study | Workload | Headline result | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calvin | 12-node commodity cluster | Near-linear scale-out; disk-based storage; no single point of failure; 10 ms epochs | [1] |
| Aria vs. BOHM/PWV/Calvin | YCSB, TPC-C | Up to **2×** throughput over prior deterministic systems and nondeterministic baselines | [2] |
| SLOG vs. Spanner/Calvin | Geo-replicated, region-local workloads | **>10×** latency reduction for local transactions, strict serializability preserved | [5] |
| Deneva: deterministic vs. nondeterministic CC | High-contention, high-update YCSB | Deterministic protocols outperform *all* others under highest contention; no-wait 2PL best among nondeterministic (+54–78%) | [4] |
| Deneva: scaling with cluster size | TPC-C with foreign-key lookups | Deterministic protocol the *only* one whose performance does not scale with cluster size | [4] |
| Ren et al. parameter sweep | Varying contention, heterogeneity, % unknown-access | Deadlock avoidance = greatest advantage; inability to abort under overload = largest disadvantage (exceeds the read/write-set-knowledge cost) | [3] |

Two patterns emerge. First, **determinism's advantage is monotone in contention**: the more transactions conflict, the more the avoided deadlocks, avoided aborts, and avoided commit round trips dominate. Harding et al.'s finding is the sharpest statement — at peak contention, determinism beats every alternative [4]. Second, **determinism's disadvantage is monotone in environmental variance**: heterogeneous node speeds, unknown access patterns, and overload all punish the rigidity of a fixed order [3][4].

### 5.2 Analytical results

> **Result 1 (Deadlock freedom).** Ordered lock acquisition in input order admits no waits-for cycle — a structural invariant, and the largest measured contributor to deterministic throughput [3].

> **Result 2 (Replication cost collapse).** Inter-replica coordination cost for consistency is zero: replicas exchange only the input batch. Failover reduces to log replay from a checkpoint, since a failed node's state is a pure function of the input prefix [3].

> **Result 3 (Commit-phase determinism generalizes locking).** Aria's reordering strictly generalizes Calvin's ordered locking: when the commit set equals the full batch and the order equals the input order, the two coincide [2]. Relaxing a priori read/write-set knowledge costs nothing in the common case and buys generality in the hard case.

### 5.3 Worked example: hotspot under determinism vs. 2PL

Consider a hotspot record $x$ updated by $k$ transactions in one epoch on a 4-node cluster. Under distributed 2PL+2PC, each transaction acquires $x$'s lock across the network, holds it through prepare, and risks deadlock; aborts waste work and the abort decision itself needs agreement. Under Calvin, the sequencer orders the $k$ transactions; each acquires $x$'s lock in turn with no deadlock possible and no prepare phase; coordination cost is $O(1)$ batch messages amortized over the epoch. The throughput ratio grows with $k$ — exactly the monotone-in-contention pattern the measurements show [1][4].

## 6. Limitations

Determinism is not a free lunch, and the literature is unusually candid about its costs [3]:

1. **Preprocessing latency.** The sequencing layer adds a full epoch of latency (10 ms in Calvin) before execution begins, plus agreement cost when sequencers are distributed — pure overhead for latency-sensitive single-key transactions [1][3].
2. **Unknown access patterns.** Calvin-class systems need read/write sets in advance; predicate-based or data-dependent access requires annotation or speculation. Aria removes this requirement but pays for a full execution phase plus deterministic conflict analysis [2].
3. **No interactive transactions.** A transaction stalled on user input blocks its position in the global order — and, through ordered locking, every transaction behind it [3].
4. **Inability to abort under overload.** A nondeterministic engine sheds load by aborting; a deterministic engine cannot abort arbitrarily without cross-replica agreement, so overload becomes latency collapse. Ren et al. find this a *larger* disadvantage than the read/write-set requirement [3].
5. **Heterogeneity and stragglers.** Ordered execution runs at the speed of the slowest participant when distributed transactions interleave with local ones; Harding et al. show the extreme form — with foreign-key lookups, the deterministic protocol alone fails to scale with cluster size [3][4].
6. **Serialized lock acquisition.** Deterministic systems request locks for transactions one at a time in order, whereas nondeterministic systems parallelize lock requests — a new bottleneck [3].

These limitations delineate determinism's domain: high-contention, partitionable OLTP with known transaction logic on homogeneous clusters — the OLTP core, but not the whole database world.

## 7. Conclusion

Deterministic concurrency control reframes distributed transaction processing: instead of discovering a serializable order at runtime through locks, validation, and commit protocols, the system *agrees on the order first* and derives everything else — deadlock freedom, atomicity, replica consistency — as a consequence. Calvin proved the architecture viable at scale with ordered locking [1]; Aria proved the a priori read/write-set requirement dispensable through epoch execution and deterministic reordering, doubling throughput on standard benchmarks [2]; SLOG proved the principle extends across continents when combined with data locality [5]. The comparative studies complete the picture: determinism dominates under contention and loses under variance [3][4].

The open frontier is *adaptive* determinism: systems that execute deterministically in the common case but degrade gracefully — shedding load, routing around stragglers, absorbing unknown access patterns — without surrendering the input-order invariant. Aria's generalization of Calvin suggests the design space is far from exhausted: every mechanism currently justified by runtime coordination is a candidate for replacement by a deterministic function of an agreed order.
