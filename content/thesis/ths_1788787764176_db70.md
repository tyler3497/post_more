# Homotopy Continuation for Solving Polynomial Systems: Polyhedral Homotopies, Bernstein Mixed Volume, Adaptive Path Tracking, and Endgames for Singular Solutions

## Abstract

Numerical homotopy continuation is the principal algorithmic framework for computing *all* isolated complex solutions of square systems of polynomial equations, a task central to kinematics, computer vision, chemical reaction networks, and algebraic statistics. This thesis develops the method from first principles: probability-one homotopies built with the Morgan–Sommese *gamma trick*; the Bernshtein–Kushnirenko–Khovanskii root bound expressed through Newton polytopes and mixed volume; the polyhedral homotopies of Huber and Sturmfels, whose path count attains the mixed-volume optimum; adaptive predictor–corrector path tracking in projective space; and endgame techniques — winding-number extrapolation, Puiseux series recovery, and *deflation* — that resolve singular endpoints. We survey the major software ecosystems (PHCpack, Bertini, HomotopyContinuation.jl), quantify the Bézout-to-mixed-volume gap on benchmark systems, and analyze failure modes including path jumping, ill-conditioning, and the #P-hardness of mixed-volume computation.

## 1. Introduction

Let *F* : **C**ⁿ → **C**ⁿ be a square system of *n* polynomial equations in *n* unknowns. The problem of computing *all* of its isolated complex zeros arises pervasively: inverse kinematics of serial-link robots [5], the five-point relative-pose problem in computer vision, steady states of mass-action chemical reaction networks, and Nash equilibria of finite games all reduce, after suitable formulation, to solving polynomial systems. Two cultures compete for this problem. *Symbolic* methods — Gröbner bases, resultants, triangular decompositions — manipulate the equations exactly but suffer doubly-exponential worst-case complexity and degrade catastrophically on floating-point data. *Numerical* methods instead approximate the solution set directly, and among them **homotopy continuation** is distinguished by a remarkable property: it is a *global* solver, guaranteed (with probability one) to find every isolated solution, not merely one [1].

The idea is disarmingly simple. Rather than attacking *F* directly, one constructs a *start system* *G* whose solutions are known or trivially computed, and embeds both into a one-parameter family — a **homotopy** *H*(*x*, *t*) with *H*(*x*, 0) = *G*(*x*) and *H*(*x*, 1) = *F*(*x*). Under mild genericity conditions, each solution of *G* extends to a continuous **solution path** *x*(*t*) satisfying *H*(*x*(*t*), *t*) = 0, and these paths are followed numerically from *t* = 0 to *t* = 1 using predictor–corrector schemes. Every isolated zero of *F* is then reached by at least one tracked path.

Three engineering questions dominate the design of such a method, and they organize this thesis:

1. **How many paths must be tracked?** Tracking excess paths that diverge or merge is the dominant cost. The classical answer is the *Bézout number*, the product of the degrees; the sharper answer is the *mixed volume* of the Newton polytopes [2][7].
2. **How are paths followed reliably?** Predictor–corrector tracking with adaptive step-size control, projective compactification to handle paths diverging to infinity, and adaptive multiprecision for ill-conditioned stretches [4].
3. **What happens at singular endpoints?** When the Jacobian drops rank at *t* = 1, Newton correction stalls and the path admits only a *Puiseux* (fractional-power) expansion. *Endgames* and *deflation* recover these solutions with full accuracy [6][8].

Our contribution is expository and synthetic: a unified, implementation-aware account of the modern homotopy continuation pipeline, from the gamma trick to singular endgames, grounded in the literature and illustrated with worked computations.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The total-degree homotopy

The classical entry point is Bézout's theorem: a system *F* = (*f*₁, …, *f*ₙ) with deg *f*ᵢ = *d*ᵢ has at most *D* = ∏ᵢ *d*ᵢ isolated zeros in affine space (exactly *D* in projective space, counting multiplicity, for generic coefficients). The **total-degree start system**

```text
G(x) = (x₁^d₁ − 1, x₂^d₂ − 1, …, xₙ^dₙ − 1) = 0
```

has precisely *D* regular solutions — tuples of roots of unity — obtainable in closed form. The homotopy

```text
H(x, t) = (1 − t)·G(x) + γ·t·F(x),   t ∈ [0, 1]
```

then defines *D* paths. Here *γ* ∈ **C** is a random complex constant, the **gamma trick** of Morgan and Sommese [1]: for a fixed real convex combination, paths may collide at intermediate *t*, destroying completeness; a generic complex *γ* rotates the discriminant off the tracking interval, so that with *probability one* no two paths intersect for *t* < 1 and every isolated solution is reached.

### 2.2 Path tracking via the Davidenko equation

Differentiating the identity *H*(*x*(*t*), *t*) = 0 along a path yields **Davidenko's differential equation**

```text
J_H(x(t), t) · dx/dt + ∂H/∂t (x(t), t) = 0,
```

where *J_H* is the Jacobian of *H* with respect to *x*. In practice one rarely integrates this ODE directly; instead a **predictor–corrector** loop is used at each step:

1. **Predict**: advance along the tangent (Euler step) or a higher-order Taylor/Hermite approximation to obtain a trial point at *t* + Δ*t*.
2. **Correct**: apply Newton's method to *H*(·, *t* + Δ*t*) = 0 starting from the trial point.
3. **Adapt**: accept or reject the step based on the Newton contraction rate, adjusting Δ*t* to keep the corrector converging in a few iterations.

Paths that diverge to infinity are handled by tracking in **projective space**: homogenizing *H* and following the paths on **P**ⁿ removes the artificial distinction between finite and infinite endpoints, with a random affine chart (a "patch") chosen to keep coordinates bounded [1][4].

### 2.3 Regular and singular solutions

A solution *x** of *F*(*x*) = 0 is **regular** if rank *J_F*(*x**) = *n*, and **singular** otherwise; its **multiplicity** is the dimension of the local algebra **C**{*x*} / ⟨*F*⟩ at *x**. Newton's method converges quadratically near regular solutions but degrades to linear (or worse) near singular ones, and the predictor–corrector tracker slows dramatically as the Jacobian becomes ill-conditioned approaching a singular endpoint. The entire endgame machinery of §4.5 exists to handle this regime.

## 3. Methodology

The modern homotopy continuation pipeline decomposes into five stages, each with well-understood algorithmic choices:

| Stage | Task | Representative techniques |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Root count | Bound the number of isolated solutions | Bézout number; multihomogeneous Bézout; **mixed volume** [2][7] |
| 2. Start system | Build *G* with exactly that many known solutions | Total-degree; *m*-homogeneous; **polyhedral (binomial) per mixed cell** [2] |
| 3. Homotopy | Connect *G* to *F* generically | Gamma trick; coefficient-parameter; regeneration; monodromy loops [1][4] |
| 4. Path tracking | Follow each path *t*: 0 → 1 | Predictor–corrector; adaptive stepsize; projective tracking; adaptive precision [4][5] |
| 5. Endgame & certification | Resolve singular endpoints; refine | Winding-number endgames; Puiseux series; **deflation** [6]; Smale *α*-theory |

Beyond the total-degree homotopy, two refinements deserve mention here because they frame the polyhedral story. **Multihomogeneous homotopies** partition variables into groups and use a start system respecting that structure, giving the multihomogeneous Bézout number — often far smaller than the total degree for systems with natural variable groupings (e.g., the eigenvalue problem). **Coefficient-parameter homotopies** exploit families *F*(*x*; *p*) depending on parameters *p*: one expensive "generic" solve is performed once, and subsequent instances are solved cheaply by tracking from the generic parameter values — the workhorse of real-time applications such as robot control [1].

The remainder of this thesis deepens each stage, with emphasis on the polyhedral homotopy (optimal path count), adaptive tracking (robustness), and endgames (singular solutions).

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Probability-one homotopies and the gamma trick

The gamma trick deserves a closer look because it is the cheapest insurance in the subject. Consider tracking with *γ* = 1 and real *t*. The set of (*x*, *t*) with *H*(*x*, *t*) = 0 is an algebraic curve; its projection onto the *t*-axis has finitely many *critical values* where paths can merge or branch. If any critical value is real and lies in [0, 1], the tracker may silently jump between paths or follow a path into a singularity, and completeness is lost. Choosing *γ* uniformly at random on the complex unit circle makes the probability that a critical value lands on the segment {*γt* : *t* ∈ [0, 1]} equal to zero — the discriminant is a proper algebraic set, hence of measure zero. Consequently, *with probability one*, all path crossings are pushed off the tracking interval [1].

In practice this is combined with projective tracking (a random affine patch keeps diverging paths regular [1][4]) and adaptive multiprecision, making "probability one" a practical reality.

### 4.2 Bernstein's theorem and the mixed volume

For *sparse* systems — where each equation involves only a few monomials — the Bézout number is a gross overestimate. The correct combinatorial invariant is the **mixed volume** of the **Newton polytopes**. Given *f*(*x*) = Σ_{*a* ∈ *A*} *c_a x^a*, its Newton polytope is *P* = conv(*A*) ⊂ **R**ⁿ. The normalized mixed volume MV(*P*₁, …, *P*ₙ) is the coefficient of *λ*₁⋯*λ*ₙ in the homogeneous degree-*n* polynomial Vol(*λ*₁*P*₁ + ⋯ + *λ*ₙ*P*ₙ), normalized so that MV(Δ, …, Δ) = *n*!·Vol(Δ).

> **Theorem:** (Bernshtein [7], 1975). Let *f*₁, …, *f*ₙ be Laurent polynomials with Newton polytopes *P*₁, …, *P*ₙ. Then the number of isolated common zeros in the torus (**C***)ⁿ is at most MV(*P*₁, …, *P*ₙ), and equals it for generic choices of coefficients.

The bound is sharp in a strong sense — Bernshtein's *second* theorem gives an explicit genericity criterion: equality holds whenever, for every nonzero *v* ∈ **R**ⁿ, the *initial system* in_v(*f*) (the subsystem supported on the face of each polytope minimizing ⟨*v*, ·⟩) has no zeros in (**C***)ⁿ. Kushnirenko's earlier unmixed case and Khovanskii's extensions complete the circle of ideas now called the **BKK bound** [7].

*Why it matters computationally.* The mixed volume counts the torus solutions (zero coordinates are handled by facial analysis); for cyclic-5, MV = 70 versus a Bézout number of 3,125. The polyhedral homotopy is the constructive counterpart of this theorem.

### 4.3 Polyhedral homotopies via lifting and mixed subdivisions

Huber and Sturmfels [2] turned Bernshtein's theorem into an algorithm. Fix supports *A*ᵢ ⊂ **Z**ⁿ and a generic **lifting** *ω* : ∪ᵢ *A*ᵢ → **R**. The lifting induces a *regular subdivision* of the Minkowski sum *P* = Σᵢ *P*ᵢ; its **mixed cells** — cells *Q* = Σᵢ *Q*ᵢ with dim *Q*ᵢ summing appropriately — are the combinatorial skeleton of the homotopy. For each mixed cell, the corresponding **binomial start system** (obtained by keeping only the lifted-minimal monomials) is solved in closed form via integer linear algebra (Smith normal form / unimodular triangulation), and each of its MV(*Q*₁, …, *Q*ₙ) solutions seeds a path. The homotopy itself is

```text
H_i(x, t) = Σ_{a ∈ A_i} c_a · x^a · t^{ω_i(a)},   i = 1, …, n,
```

so that at *t* → 0⁺ the system degenerates, cell by cell, into the binomial start systems, while at *t* = 1 it is the target.

> **Theorem:** (Huber–Sturmfels [2], 1995). For generic lifting *ω*, the polyhedral homotopy has exactly MV(*P*₁, …, *P*ₙ) solution paths, and every isolated zero of a generic system with the given supports is reached.

The path count is *optimal*: no structure-respecting homotopy uses fewer paths. Mixed-volume computation (#P-hard in general, practical at moderate scale via MixedVol/DEMiCs) is integrated into PHCpack [3] and HomotopyContinuation.jl [5].

A concrete illustration is instructive. Consider

```text
f₁ = a·x²y + b·x + c = 0,      f₂ = d·xy² + e·y + f = 0,
```

with generic coefficients. Each equation has total degree 3, so the Bézout number is 9. The Newton polytopes are *P*₁ = conv{(0,0), (1,0), (2,1)} and *P*₂ = conv{(0,0), (0,1), (1,2)}. The Minkowski sum *P*₁ + *P*₂ has hull (0,0), (1,0), (2,1), (3,3), (1,2), (0,1) with Euclidean area 4; subtracting the areas of *P*₁ and *P*₂ (each 1/2) gives MV(*P*₁, *P*₂) = 3. Indeed, eliminating *x* and *y* via *u* = *xy* yields the cubic *ad·u*³ + (*ae* + *bd*)*u*² + *be·u* − *cf* = 0, confirming exactly 3 torus solutions. The polyhedral homotopy tracks **3 paths instead of 9** — and this gap widens dramatically with dimension, as §5 shows.

A short self-contained check in Python:

```python
def area(poly):
    s = 0.0
    n = len(poly)
    for i in range(n):
        x1, y1 = poly[i]
        x2, y2 = poly[(i + 1) % n]
        s += x1 * y2 - x2 * y1
    return abs(s) / 2

P1  = [(0,0), (1,0), (2,1)]
P2  = [(0,0), (0,1), (1,2)]
P12 = [(0,0), (1,0), (2,1), (3,3), (1,2), (0,1)]  # Minkowski-sum hull
MV  = area(P12) - area(P1) - area(P2)
print("mixed volume:", MV)   # -> 3.0  (Bézout number: 9)
```

### 4.4 Adaptive predictor–corrector path tracking

Given the homotopy, the bulk of wall-clock time is spent in path tracking, and its engineering determines practical success. A modern tracker combines:

- **Predictors.** Tangent (Euler) by default; higher-order Taylor/Hermite or Runge–Kutta predictors [5] trade Jacobian factorizations for fewer steps.
- **Correctors.** Newton's method on *H*(·, *t*) = 0 at frozen *t*, typically with a strict limit (2–3 iterations); failure to converge triggers step rejection.
- **Step-size control.** The step Δ*t* is adapted so that the corrector converges rapidly but not trivially — the classic heuristic targets a contraction factor in a prescribed window, growing Δ*t* on easy stretches and shrinking it near ill-conditioning.
- **Projective tracking.** Paths diverging to infinity are the silent killers of affine trackers: the coordinates blow up, the step controller collapses, and the path is wasted. Tracking the homogenized system on **P**ⁿ with a randomly chosen affine patch eliminates this failure mode almost surely [1][4].
- **Adaptive precision.** When the condition number of *J_H* exceeds ~1/√*u* (*u* the unit roundoff), double precision can no longer resolve the path; Bertini's adaptive multiprecision [4] raises precision on the fly rather than paying multiprecision costs everywhere.

*Path jumping* — silently switching between close paths — is mitigated by conservative step control, multiprecision, and *α*-theory/Krawczyk certification.

A minimal tracking session in HomotopyContinuation.jl [5] reads:

```julia
using HomotopyContinuation
@var x y
# sparse cubic pair from §4.3 (Bézout 9, mixed volume 3)
F = System([x^2*y + 2x + 1, x*y^2 + 3y - 1]; parameters = [])
res = solve(F; start_system = :polyhedral)
println(nsols = nsolutions(res))   # 3, each found by exactly one path
```

### 4.5 Endgames for singular endpoints and deflation

When a path converges to a *singular* solution *x** (rank *J_F*(*x**) < *n*), the corrector stalls: the Jacobian is nearly singular, Newton steps shrink, and naive tracking crawls toward *t* = 1 without ever resolving the endpoint accurately. The local analytic structure saves us. If the path approaches *x** with **winding number** (cycle number) *m* — meaning *m* distinct paths permute cyclically around *x** — then *x*(*t*) admits a **Puiseux expansion**

```text
x(t) = c_0 + c_1·s^{1/m} + c_2·s^{2/m} + ⋯,    s = 1 − t → 0⁺.
```

**Endgames** exploit this structure rather than fighting it [8]:

1. **Estimate the winding number** *m* by tracking once around a small loop in *t* and counting the permutation cycle, or by successive *t*-halving and monitoring coordinate scaling.
2. **Extrapolate to *s* = 0.** The *Cauchy endgame* [8] samples the path on a circle around the endpoint and applies Cauchy's integral formula to extract *c*₀ directly; the *power-series endgame* of Morgan–Sommese–Wampler fits the Puiseux series and truncates by a criterion on the tail; *Morgan's endgame* uses a fractional-power change of variables. All deliver several correct digits where naive tracking delivers none.
3. **Refine.** The endgame output seeds Newton (or deflated Newton) iteration on *F* itself.

For higher multiplicity, **deflation** [6] augments *F*(*x*) = 0 with equations from directional derivatives along Jacobian kernel directions, e.g.

```text
D₁(F)(x, λ) = [ F(x) ;  J_F(x)·λ ;  B·λ − 1 ] = 0,
```

with *B* a random normalization matrix. The augmented system has (*x**, *λ**) as a regular (or lower-multiplicity) solution, restoring quadratic Newton convergence; stages are bounded by the multiplicity [6]. Standard in PHCpack [3] and Bertini [4], deflation composes with endgames: the endgame supplies the starting point, deflation the convergence rate.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation

We evaluate the central quantitative claim — that structure-aware homotopies drastically reduce work — through the root-count gap and the behavior of trackers on representative systems. The figures below are standard benchmarks from the PHCpack test suite and the homotopy literature [3][5]; the worked row is computed in §4.3.

| System | *n* | Bézout number | Mixed volume | Paths saved |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| cyclic-5 | 5 | 3,125 | 70 | 97.8% |
| sparse cubic pair (§4.3) | 2 | 9 | 3 | 66.7% |
| circle ∩ hyperbola | 2 | 4 | 4 | 0% (already sharp) |

For the canonical cyclic-5 benchmark, a total-degree homotopy tracks 3,125 paths of which all but 70 diverge, while the polyhedral homotopy tracks exactly the 70 convergent paths [2][3] — the milliseconds spent on the mixed volume pay for themselves thousands of times over.

Qualitative tracker behavior, consistent across PHCpack, Bertini, and HomotopyContinuation.jl reports [3][4][5], follows a consistent pattern:

- **Regular paths** consume a few dozen predictor–corrector steps each; total cost scales nearly linearly in the number of paths, and paths are *embarrassingly parallel*.
- **Near-singular stretches** inflate step counts sharply; adaptive precision recovers reliability at proportional cost [4].
- **Singular endpoints** are the domain of endgames: the Cauchy endgame typically recovers 6–10 correct digits from a path whose raw tracker stalls at 2–3 digits, and one deflation stage restores quadratic convergence for multiplicity-2 points [6][8].

The HomotopyContinuation.jl benchmarks [5] further show that a careful Julia implementation matches or exceeds the legacy C/Ada codes (Bertini, PHCpack) on dense benchmarks while offering polyhedral homotopies and certified tracking in a single environment.

## 6. Limitations

Homotopy continuation is powerful but not omnipotent; its failure modes are well catalogued [1][4]:

- **Path jumping.** When two paths pass close together — near a near-singular parameter value — the tracker may switch paths silently. No local error estimator detects this; only global consistency checks (e.g., monodromy loops or certification) catch it. Conservative step control and multiprecision reduce but do not eliminate the risk.
- **Cost of the mixed volume.** #P-hard in general; for dense high-dimensional systems the start-system setup can dominate, and the total-degree homotopy may paradoxically win.
- **High-multiplicity singularities.** Deflation stages multiply the system size (each stage adds *n* + 1 equations and unknowns), and the number of stages can reach the multiplicity; deeply singular points remain expensive [6].
- **Positive-dimensional components.** The square-system pipeline finds *isolated* solutions. Positive-dimensional solution sets require witness sets, slicing, and numerical irreducible decomposition — a strictly larger apparatus [1].
- **Real vs. complex.** The method computes *complex* solutions; extracting the real ones is trivial filtering, but counting or certifying *real* roots is a separate, harder problem (real numerical algebraic geometry [4]).
- **Coefficient sensitivity.** Endgames and deflation need accurate coefficients; for noisy data, multiplicity itself is ill-posed.

## 7. Conclusion

Homotopy continuation reduces the global problem of solving polynomial systems to the local problem of following curves — and then solves the curve-following problem with remarkable completeness. The arc traced in this thesis runs from the gamma trick, which makes path tracking *probabilistically* safe, through Bernshtein's theorem and the Huber–Sturmfels polyhedral homotopy, which make it *combinatorially* optimal, to adaptive projective tracking and singular endgames, which make it *numerically* robust. With PHCpack [3], Bertini [4], and HomotopyContinuation.jl [5], solving thousands of paths is routine; the frontier is certification, real solving, and positive-dimensional sets. For the practitioner, the lesson is crisp: *count roots with the mixed volume, track in projective space with a random gamma, and never trust a singular endpoint without an endgame.*
