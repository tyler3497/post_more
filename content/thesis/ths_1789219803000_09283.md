# Lattice-Based Cryptography: From Learning With Errors to the NIST Post-Quantum Standards ML-KEM and ML-DSA

## Abstract

Lattice-based cryptography now underpins the first generation of post-quantum public-key standards. We survey the theoretical lineage from the *learning with errors* (LWE) problem of Regev [1], through its ring and module variants [3], to the CRYSTALS-Kyber key-encapsulation mechanism [4] and the CRYSTALS-Dilithium signature scheme [5], standardized by NIST as FIPS 203 (ML-KEM) and FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) in August 2024 [6][7]. We define the core lattice problems — SVP, SIVP, GapSVP, and SIS — and explain how worst-case to average-case reductions convert presumed-hard worst-case geometry into average-case cryptographic assumptions, both in Regev's original quantum reduction [1] and Peikert's classical reduction [2]. We dissect the module-lattice instantiations: the number-theoretic transform, Fujisaki–Okamoto CCA lifting, Fiat–Shamir with aborts, and concrete Core-SVP security estimates. We close with the limitations of the current picture: structured-lattice attack surfaces, reduction tightness, side-channel fragility of NTT code, and the case for cryptographic agility.

## 1 Intro

Public-key cryptography rests on computational hardness. For half a century, the load-bearing assumptions have been integer factorization (RSA), the finite-field discrete logarithm (Diffie–Hellman), and the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm (ECDSA/ECDH). All three collapse in polynomial time on a sufficiently large quantum computer: Shor's algorithm solves both factorization and discrete logarithms, and the only reason these schemes survive today is that no cryptographically relevant quantum computer yet exists. The transition to *post-quantum* cryptography is therefore not a response to an observed break, but a preemptive migration against a break whose arrival date is unknown and whose retrospective damage — harvested ciphertexts decrypted years later — begins accumulating the moment data is intercepted.

Lattice problems are the leading replacement foundation, for reasons that are simultaneously *pragmatic* and *structural*. Pragmatically, no quantum algorithm is known to outperform the best classical algorithms for approximate worst-case lattice problems; the decades-long failure of quantum cryptanalysis against lattices is itself evidence. Structurally, lattices admit worst-case to average-case reductions: a handful of lattice problems are known to be hard *on the average* precisely when they are hard *in the worst case*, a property essentially unique among cryptographic assumptions [1][2]. Average-case hardness is what cryptography needs, since keys and ciphertexts are random instances, not adversarially chosen ones.

This post traces the full arc:

1. The *foundational* theory: lattices, short vectors, and the LWE problem of Regev (2005) [1].
2. The *structural* variants: Ring-LWE over ideal lattices [3] and its module generalization, which recover the efficiency lost in the unstructured setting.
3. The *standardized* systems: CRYSTALS-Kyber [4] and CRYSTALS-Dilithium [5], selected by NIST in July 2022 and published as FIPS 203 and FIPS 204 in August 2024 [6][7].
4. The *empirical* record: concrete security estimates, performance, deployment, and cryptanalysis.
5. The *limitations*: what the reductions do and do not guarantee.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Lattices and hard problems

A *lattice* Λ in ℝⁿ is a discrete additive subgroup: the set of all integer linear combinations of a basis **B** = {**b**₁, …, **b**ₙ} of linearly independent vectors. The same lattice admits infinitely many bases, and the central theme of lattice algorithms is that a *good* (short, nearly orthogonal) basis makes problems easy while a *bad* basis makes them hard. Let λ₁(Λ) denote the length of the shortest nonzero vector — the *first successive minimum*.

The principal computational problems are:

- **SVP (Shortest Vector Problem):** given a basis of Λ, find a nonzero vector **v** ∈ Λ with ‖**v**‖ = λ₁(Λ). The *approximation* variant SVP_γ asks for ‖**v**‖ ≤ γ·λ₁(Λ).
- **SIVP_γ (Shortest Independent Vectors Problem):** find *n* linearly independent lattice vectors each of length at most γ·λₙ(Λ).
- **GapSVP_γ:** decide whether λ₁(Λ) ≤ d or λ₁(Λ) > γ·d, promised one holds.
- **BDD (Bounded-Distance Decoding):** given a target **t** within distance α·λ₁(Λ) of the lattice, find the nearest lattice vector. This is the decoding view that underlies decryption.
- **SIS (Short Integer Solution):** given a uniformly random matrix **A** ∈ ℤ_q^{n×m}, find a nonzero *short* vector **x** ∈ ℤ^m with **Ax** ≡ 0 (mod q). Ajtai showed SIS is hard on the average whenever approximating SVP is hard in the worst case, and used it to build a one-way function and collision-resistant hash family — the first worst-case to average-case connection in lattice cryptography.

Best known algorithms for approximating SVP to polynomial factors run in time 2^Θ(n): lattice sieving achieves roughly 2^(0.292n) for exact SVP, and block-reduction (BKZ) with blocksize β costs about 2^(0.292β). No quantum algorithm improves these exponents in the worst case — the bedrock claim on which everything below stands.

### 2.2 Learning with errors

The *learning with errors* problem, introduced by Regev [1], is a noisy linear algebra problem that is both cryptographically flexible and provably connected to worst-case lattice geometry. Let n, q ≥ 2 be integers, **s** ∈ ℤ_qⁿ a secret, and χ an error distribution over ℤ_q (typically a discrete Gaussian of width αq). The distribution A_{**s**,χ} samples a uniform **a** ∈ ℤ_qⁿ, an error e ← χ, and outputs the pair

(**a**, b = ⟨**a**, **s**⟩ + e mod q).

*Search-LWE* asks to recover **s** from polynomially many samples; *decision-LWE* asks to distinguish A_{**s**,χ} samples from uniform pairs. Regev showed the two are polynomially equivalent for suitable parameters [1]. Informally, each sample is a noisy linear equation in the secret; without noise the system solves trivially by Gaussian elimination, and the entire difficulty is concentrated in the small, secret errors.

> Theorem: (Regev [1], informal) Let n be the dimension, q a modulus, and α the noise rate with αq ≥ 2√n. If there is an efficient algorithm solving (average-case) LWE_{q,χ_α}, then there is an efficient *quantum* algorithm approximating worst-case SIVP and GapSVP to within Õ(n/α) factors.

The reduction is *iterative* and quantum: an LWE oracle is used to sample progressively narrower discrete Gaussians on the dual lattice, bootstrapping down to the smoothing parameter. Peikert later gave a *classical* reduction from GapSVP to LWE under somewhat more restrictive parameters [2], and subsequent work (notably the line culminating in classical reductions with polynomial modulus) removed much of the quantumness, at the cost of tighter parameter constraints. The qualitative message is stable: *breaking LWE on random instances is at least as hard as solving worst-case lattice problems that have resisted decades of algorithmic attack*.

## 3 Methodology

The methodology of lattice-based cryptography has two layers: *reduction* (how hardness is argued) and *instantiation* (how abstract problems become efficient schemes).

**Reduction methodology.** A worst-case to average-case reduction has the form: *if an adversary breaks the scheme on a non-negligible fraction of random instances, then we solve a worst-case lattice problem on every instance*. The reductions manipulate the *discrete Gaussian* D_{Λ,s}, the distribution that assigns each lattice point **x** probability proportional to exp(−π‖**x**‖²/s²). Above the *smoothing parameter* η(Λ), a discrete Gaussian behaves like a continuous one modulo the lattice's fundamental parallelepiped, which is what lets reductions convert geometric information into uniform-looking samples. Regev's reduction alternates a *classical* step (BDD → LWE oracle calls via a closest-vector oracle) with a *quantum* step (Fourier sampling to produce narrower Gaussians), iterating until the Gaussian is narrow enough to reveal short vectors directly [1][2].

**Instantiation methodology.** Raw LWE has a quadratic public-key bottleneck: the matrix **A** ∈ ℤ_q^{m×n} costs Õ(n²) storage. Three algebraic refinements trade geometric generality for efficiency:

| Variant | Underlying object | Key size | Reduction basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| LWE [1] | Random matrices over ℤ_q | Õ(n²) | Worst-case SVP/SIVP on general lattices (quantum or classical) |
| Ring-LWE [3] | Ideals in cyclotomic rings R_q | Õ(n) | Worst-case SVP on *ideal* lattices (quantum) |
| Module-LWE [4] | Modules over R_q of rank k | Õ(kn) | Interpolates between the two; hardness scales with k·n |

Module-LWE is the workhorse of the NIST standards: with ring degree n = 256 fixed for NTT efficiency, the module rank k ∈ {2, 3, 4} tunes security while keeping the fast polynomial arithmetic of the ring setting.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The LWE problem and Regev's quantum reduction

Regev's 2005 paper [1] did two things at once: it defined LWE, and it built a public-key cryptosystem whose semantic security reduces to worst-case lattice hardness. The cryptosystem is conceptually a lattice analogue of ElGamal with noise: the public key is a batch of LWE samples (**A**, **b** = **A**^T**s** + **e**), and encryption of a bit m adds the bit scaled by ⌊q/2⌋ to a random subset-sum of the samples. Decryption subtracts the secret's contribution and rounds; correctness holds when the accumulated noise stays below q/4.

The reduction's core is an *oracle transformation*: given an oracle that distinguishes LWE samples from uniform (the decision problem), one constructs a *bounded-distance decoder* for the lattice, and from the decoder plus quantum Fourier sampling, one samples the discrete Gaussian D_{Λ*,r} on the *dual* lattice. Iterating with r shrinking geometrically drives the Gaussian below the smoothing parameter, at which point short dual vectors — and hence short primal vectors — emerge. The approximation factor achieved is Õ(n/α), polynomial in the dimension: cryptographically, this means LWE with noise rate α = 1/poly(n) inherits hardness from *polynomial-factor* approximations of worst-case problems, the regime where lattice algorithms are exponentially slow and conjectured to remain so.

A subtle but essential point: the reduction needs the modulus q to exceed roughly n/α (up to log factors). Concrete schemes use far larger q for correctness and efficiency, which only *strengthens* the assumption relative to what is proven.

### 4.2 Ring-LWE and ideal lattices

Plain LWE's Õ(n²) keys motivated an algebraic compression: replace vectors over ℤ_q with elements of a *ring*. In Ring-LWE [3], one works in R_q = ℤ_q[x]/(f(x)) for a cyclotomic polynomial f (typically xⁿ + 1 with n a power of two). A secret s(x) ∈ R_q and errors e(x) with small coefficients define samples (a(x), a(x)·s(x) + e(x)). One ring element encodes n coefficients, so keys shrink from Õ(n²) to Õ(n), and multiplication can exploit the Number Theoretic Transform (NTT) in O(n log n) time.

The geometry changes: R_q corresponds, via the *canonical embedding* of the number field into ℂⁿ, to an *ideal lattice* — a lattice closed under multiplication by ring elements. Lyubashevsky, Peikert, and Regev proved a quantum worst-case to average-case reduction for Ring-LWE, but with caveats that persist as limitations (see §6): hardness is relative to SVP on *ideal* lattices rather than general lattices, and the proven error distribution is non-spherical with secret parameters [3]. The Chinese Remainder Theorem decomposition of the ring — the same structure that makes the NTT fast — is also what makes the reduction go through, tying efficiency and provability to the same algebraic feature.

```python
# Illustrative Ring-LWE toy: key generation over R_q = Z_q[x]/(x^8 + 1)
q, n = 3329, 8  # toy degree; real deployments use n = 256
import random

def add(a, b): return [(x + y) % q for x, y in zip(a, b)]
def mul(a, b):  # naive negacyclic convolution mod x^n + 1
    t = [0] * (2 * n)
    for i, x in enumerate(a):
        for j, y in enumerate(b):
            t[i + j] = (t[i + j] + x * y) % q
    for i in range(n, 2 * n):
        t[i - n] = (t[i - n] - t[i]) % q
    return t[:n]

def small_sample(eta):  # centered binomial ~ discrete Gaussian stand-in
    return [sum(random.getrandbits(1) for _ in range(2 * eta)) - eta
            for _ in range(n)]

a = [random.randrange(q) for _ in range(n)]
s = small_sample(2)
e = small_sample(2)
b = add(mul(a, s), e)          # public: (a, b = a*s + e); secret: s
```

### 4.3 Module-LWE and CRYSTALS-Kyber (standardized as ML-KEM)

Kyber [4] is a key-encapsulation mechanism whose CPA-secure core is *Module-LWE* public-key encryption, lifted to IND-CCA2 security by a Fujisaki–Okamoto transform with implicit rejection. Concretely, KeyGen samples **A** ∈ R_q^{k×k} (from a public seed ρ, so only 32 bytes are stored), short secrets **s**, **e** ∈ R_q^k with coefficients from a centered binomial distribution, and publishes **t** = **As** + **e**. Encryption of a message m samples short **r**, **e**₁, e₂ and outputs compressed (**u** = **A**ᵀ**r** + **e**₁, v = **t**ᵀ**r** + e₂ + ⌈q/2⌋·m). Decryption computes v − **s**ᵀ**u** ≈ ⌈q/2⌋·m + (small noise) and rounds each coefficient. Compression (`Compress_q(x, d)`) discards low-order bits of ciphertext components to shrink bandwidth at the cost of a precisely analyzed rounding noise folded into the decryption-failure bound.

Parameters (n = 256, q = 3329 throughout) [4]:

| Scheme (FIPS 203 name) | k | η₁, η₂ | d_u, d_v | pk / ct (bytes) | NIST level | Core-SVP (classical) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kyber512 (ML-KEM-512) | 2 | 3, 2 | 10, 4 | 800 / 768 | 1 (≈ AES-128) | ≈ 2¹¹⁸ |
| Kyber768 (ML-KEM-768) | 3 | 2, 2 | 10, 4 | 1184 / 1088 | 3 (≈ AES-192) | ≈ 2¹⁸³ |
| Kyber1024 (ML-KEM-1024) | 4 | 2, 2 | 11, 5 | 1568 / 1568 | 5 (≈ AES-256) | ≈ 2²⁵⁶ |

The modulus q = 3329 = 13·2⁸ + 1 is NTT-friendly, and decryption-failure probabilities are driven below 2⁻¹³⁹ (Kyber512) to 2⁻¹⁷⁴ (Kyber1024) — negligible enough to defeat failure-boosting chosen-ciphertext attacks [4]. Note that ML-KEM-768 is the *recommended default* parameter set in FIPS 203 [6][7].

### 4.4 CRYSTALS-Dilithium (standardized as ML-DSA)

Dilithium [5] is a module-lattice signature built on the *Fiat–Shamir with aborts* paradigm. The public key is (**A**, **t** = **As₁** + **s₂**) over R_q = ℤ_q[x]/(x²⁵⁶ + 1) with q = 8380417 = 2²³ − 2¹³ + 1; signing samples a masking vector **y**, computes **w** = **Ay**, hashes to a sparse challenge c, and returns **z** = **y** + c**s₁** — *restarting* (the "abort") unless **z** and the hinted high bits of **w** − c**s₂** satisfy norm bounds that keep the signature distribution independent of the secret. Verification recomputes the high bits from **Az** − c**t** and checks the hash and bounds. Security rests jointly on Module-LWE (key indistinguishability) and Module-SIS (forgery ⇒ short vector), and the design deliberately *avoids discrete Gaussian sampling* — the historically fragile, timing-leak-prone component of earlier lattice signatures — using uniform sampling with rejection instead, which admits clean constant-time implementations [5].

| Scheme (FIPS 204 name) | k × ℓ | η | pk / sig (bytes) | NIST level | Core-SVP (classical) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dilithium2 (ML-DSA-44) | 4 × 4 | 2 | 1312 / 2420 | 2 | ≈ 2¹²³ |
| Dilithium3 (ML-DSA-65) | 6 × 5 | 4 | 1952 / 3309 | 3 | ≈ 2¹⁸² |
| Dilithium5 (ML-DSA-87) | 8 × 7 | 2 | 2592 / 4627 | 5 | ≈ 2²⁵² |

## 5 Empirical and Theoretical Results

**The standardization record.** NIST's post-quantum process, launched in 2016, evaluated 82 submissions across multiple rounds. In July 2022 NIST selected Kyber for general encryption/key establishment and Dilithium, Falcon, and SPHINCS+ for signatures; on August 13, 2024 it published the first three standards — FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA, hash-based backup) — with a Falcon-based FIPS 206 (FN-DSA) in draft and HQC selected in March 2025 as a code-based backup KEM [6][7].

| Milestone | Date | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| NIST PQC call for proposals | 2016 | 82 submissions received |
| Round-3 selections | July 5, 2022 | Kyber (KEM); Dilithium, Falcon, SPHINCS+ (signatures) |
| FIPS 203/204/205 finalized | August 13, 2024 | ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA published |
| HQC selected as backup KEM | March 2025 | Code-based alternative to ML-KEM |
| FN-DSA (Falcon) | draft (FIPS 206) | Compact signatures, pending finalization |

**Concrete security.** The Core-SVP methodology estimates the cost of one shortest-vector oracle call inside BKZ lattice reduction and converts it to bit-security. The table entries above (2¹¹⁸–2²⁵⁶ classical) come from the submitters' analyses [4][5] and have survived years of third-party cryptanalysis, including the community's "estimate-all-the-things" re-evaluations. A compact estimator sketch:

```python
# Illustrative Core-SVP estimate: BKZ blocksize beta needed vs. lattice dimension
def core_svp_bits(n, q, sigma):
    # heuristic: root Hermite factor delta ~ (beta/(2*pi*e) * (pi*beta)**(1/beta))**(1/(2*(beta-1)))
    # security ≈ 0.292 * beta (classical sieving exponent); solve for target bits
    import math
    for beta in range(50, 1200):
        delta = (beta / (2 * math.pi * math.e)
                 * (math.pi * beta) ** (1 / beta)) ** (1 / (2 * (beta - 1)))
        # LWE dimension n must satisfy q/sigma ~ delta^(2n) heuristic bound
        if delta ** (2 * n) > q / sigma:
            return 0.292 * beta
    return None

print(core_svp_bits(512, 3329, 2.0))  # ≈ order of Kyber512's claimed strength
```

**Performance and deployment.** On modern x86-64, AVX2-optimized ML-KEM-768 runs key generation, encapsulation, and decapsulation in the low hundreds of thousands of cycles — competitive with or faster than ECDH at comparable classical strength — and ML-DSA signing/verification similarly outperform RSA-2048 by wide margins [4][5]. The Open Quantum Safe project ships liboqs with all three standards, hybrid X25519+ML-KEM-768 key exchange has been experimentally deployed in TLS 1.3 by major browsers and CDNs, and firmware-signing pipelines are migrating to ML-DSA [6].

**Cryptanalytic status.** No attack threatens the standardized parameter sets. Notable episodes sharpened practice rather than breaking schemes: decryption-failure *chosen-ciphertext* attacks against early Kyber implementations were answered by the negligible-DFR analysis plus the FO transform's implicit rejection [4]; the 2023 "KyberSlash" timing vulnerabilities were implementation flaws in reference division code, fixed without changing the algorithm; and continued scrutiny of the algebraic structure (e.g., hints and side-channel-assisted NTT leakage) informs masking guidance but has not dented Core-SVP estimates.

## 6 Limitations

The theory is strong but not airtight, and intellectual honesty requires enumerating the gaps.

1. **Structured variants prove less.** The tightest worst-case reductions apply to *unstructured* LWE [1][2]. Ring-LWE's reduction is quantum, targets *ideal* lattices only, and uses a non-spherical secret error distribution [3]. Module-LWE interpolates, but the standardized schemes rely on concrete cryptanalysis (Core-SVP) far more than on the asymptotic theorems. If ideal/module lattices turned out to admit faster algorithms than general lattices — as happened historically for *overstretched* NTRU parameters — the reductions would not save the schemes.
2. **Approximation-factor looseness.** Reductions guarantee hardness for polynomial approximation factors; concrete parameters aim at *exponential* (2^Θ(n)) security. The bridge between the two is heuristic lattice-reduction analysis, not theorem.
3. **Side channels and misuse.** The mathematics assumes black-box oracles; real NTT implementations have leaked secrets through timing, power, and cache behavior. Dilithium's rejection-sampling design helps, but constant-time, masked implementations remain an active engineering frontier.
4. **Failure and agility risk.** Kyber's correctness is probabilistic (negligible but nonzero DFR); ML-KEM's implicit-rejection decapsulation mitigates failure oracles, but the ecosystem's near-total bet on *one* mathematical family (lattices) is itself a concentration risk — which is precisely why NIST standardized SLH-DSA and selected HQC as non-lattice hedges [6][7].
5. **Proof-model gaps.** Tight QROM security proofs for the exact standardized KEM transforms arrived only after deployment momentum had begun; conservative parameter choices absorb the slack, but the proofs remain less clean than practitioners would like.

## 7 Conclusion

Lattice-based cryptography has traveled, in under two decades, from Regev's worst-case to average-case reduction [1] to mandatory federal standards [7]. The intellectual core — noisy linear algebra over ℤ_q whose average-case hardness mirrors the worst-case geometry of lattices — survived the translation through rings [3] and modules into Kyber [4] and Dilithium [5] without losing its essential character: every ciphertext is a perturbed linear system, every signature a zero-knowledge proof of short-vector knowledge, and every security argument a reduction to problems that have resisted both classical and quantum attack for decades. The standards are not the end of the story. Structured-lattice cryptanalysis, tight quantum proofs, side-channel-hardened implementations, and the non-lattice hedges (SLH-DSA, HQC, FN-DSA) define the next decade's work. But the migration has begun, and its foundation — *learning with errors* — is the rare cryptographic assumption that is both provably deep and practically fast.
