# Worst-Case Optimal Joins: the AGM Bound, Leapfrog Triejoin, and Fractional Hypertree Decompositions

## Abstract

For four decades, relational join evaluation meant binary join trees: the query optimizer picked an order of pairwise joins, and theory had little to say when intermediate results exploded beyond the final output. The 2008 theorem of Atserias, Grohe, and Marx changed this by proving that the worst-case output size of any full conjunctive query is exactly characterized by the fractional edge cover number of its hypergraph — the AGM bound [2]. Tight but non-constructive, the bound left open whether any algorithm could actually run that fast. Ngo, Porat, Ré, and Rudra answered yes with the first worst-case optimal join algorithm [1], and Veldhuizen's leapfrog triejoin showed the same guarantee emerges from a remarkably simple synchronized backtracking search over trie iterators [3]. This thesis reconstructs the full arc: fractional covers and Shearer's inequality, the constructive algorithms, and the structural refinement of fractional hypertree width [4], ending with geometric-resolution extensions [5] and the framework's limits under skew and dependencies.

## 1 Introduction

The natural join is the workhorse of relational query processing, and also its most dangerous operator. Given relations $R_1, \dots, R_m$, the query optimizer of every major engine — from System R's dynamic programming to modern cost-based optimizers — has historically committed to a *binary join plan*: a tree whose leaves are the input relations and whose internal nodes are pairwise joins. The plan space is well understood, the dynamic program is elegant, and for *acyclic* queries Yannakakis's algorithm evaluates the join in time linear in input plus output [7]. Yet binary plans carry a structural blind spot. Consider the triangle query

$$Q_\triangle = R(x,y) \bowtie S(y,z) \bowtie T(z,x),$$

with $|R| = |S| = |T| = N$. Any binary plan first computes a pairwise join — say $R \bowtie S$ — whose size can reach $N^2$ on adversarial inputs, even when the final output is far smaller. The intermediate result, not the output, dominates the runtime. No choice of join order escapes this: *every* binary plan for $Q_\triangle$ has an intermediate result of size $\Omega(N^2)$ on some instance. The central question of this thesis is therefore twofold:

1. **How large can a join output be?** What is the tight worst-case bound on $|Q(D)|$ as a function of the input cardinalities?
2. **Can we achieve it?** Is there an algorithm whose runtime matches that bound on *every* query and *every* instance?

The answer to the first question is the AGM bound [2]; the answer to the second is the family of *worst-case optimal* join algorithms — NPRR [1], leapfrog triejoin [3], and their common generalization [5]. The answer to both is refined, for queries with structure, by *fractional hypertree width* [4].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Queries as hypergraphs

A *full conjunctive query* (the formal core of a `SELECT ... FROM ... WHERE` natural join) is an expression

$$Q(\mathbf{x}) = R_1(\mathbf{x}_1) \wedge R_2(\mathbf{x}_2) \wedge \cdots \wedge R_m(\mathbf{x}_m)$$

where every variable in the body appears in the head $\mathbf{x}$. We model $Q$ as a hypergraph $\mathcal{H} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$: vertices $\mathcal{V}$ are the variables, and each atom $R_F$ contributes a hyperedge $F \in \mathcal{E}$ spanning its variables. The triangle query is the 3-uniform hypergraph on $\{x,y,z\}$ with edges $\{x,y\}, \{y,z\}, \{z,x\}$.

The *data complexity* lens fixes the query and measures runtime as a function of the input size $N = \sum_F |R_F|$. An algorithm is **worst-case optimal** for $Q$ if its runtime is $\tilde{O}(\mathrm{AGM}(Q))$ — proportional, up to polylogarithmic factors, to the maximum possible output size over all instances of the given relation sizes [1].

### 2.2 Classical width measures

Before the AGM bound, the structural theory of joins was organized around notions of *width* measuring distance from acyclicity:

- **Treewidth** ($\mathrm{tw}$) of the primal graph.
- **Hypertree width** ($\mathrm{hw}$) and **generalized hypertree width** ($\mathrm{ghw}$) [7], which exploit hyperedges directly rather than cliques of the primal graph.
- **Query width** ($\mathrm{qw}$).

Yannakakis's algorithm evaluates $\alpha$-acyclic queries in $O(N + Z)$ time where $Z$ is the output size; bounded-(generalized)-hypertree-width queries are tractable in data complexity. These results are powerful but *qualitative*: they say nothing about the actual exponent when the query is cyclic.

### 2.3 Fractional edge covers

A **fractional edge cover** of $\mathcal{H} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$ is a weight assignment $\mathbf{w} = (w_F)_{F \in \mathcal{E}}$ with $w_F \ge 0$ such that every vertex is covered:

$$\sum_{F : v \in F} w_F \ge 1 \qquad \forall v \in \mathcal{V}.$$

The **fractional edge cover number** $\rho^*(\mathcal{H})$ is the minimum total weight $\sum_F w_F$ over all fractional edge covers. It is a linear program, hence computable in polynomial time — a fact of enormous practical consequence, since the optimizer can *compute* the bound it will try to match.

For the triangle query, assigning $w = 1/2$ to each of the three edges covers every vertex exactly once, giving $\rho^* = 3/2$ — no integral edge cover does better than $2$. The fractional relaxation is what buys the $N^{3/2}$ bound.

### 2.4 Shearer's inequality

The information-theoretic engine behind the AGM bound is **Shearer's entropy inequality**. Let $X = (X_v)_{v \in \mathcal{V}}$ be a tuple of discrete random variables, and let $\mathbf{w}$ be a fractional edge cover of $\mathcal{H}$. Then

$$H(X) \le \sum_{F \in \mathcal{E}} w_F \cdot H(X_F),$$

where $X_F$ is the marginal on the variables of $F$. Intuitively: if every variable is covered at least once (in the fractional sense), the total entropy cannot exceed the weighted sum of the marginal entropies. Taking $X$ uniform over the join output turns this into a counting bound, since $H(X) = \log |Q(D)|$.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis proceeds in three movements, mirroring the historical development:

1. **The bound.** We derive the AGM inequality from Shearer's lemma, prove tightness via extremal instances, and work the canonical examples (triangle, $Loomis$–$Whitney$, cycles).
2. **The algorithms.** We present the NPRR algorithm [1] and leapfrog triejoin [3], emphasizing the *generic* backtracking skeleton subsuming both [5].
3. **The refinement.** We introduce fractional hypertree decompositions [4], which localize the fractional-cover analysis to the bags of a tree decomposition.

Throughout, the running example is the triangle query, and the running comparison is against binary join plans.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The AGM bound: statement, proof, and tightness

> **Theorem (AGM inequality [2]):** Let $Q$ be a full conjunctive query with hypergraph $\mathcal{H} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$, and let $\mathbf{w}$ be any fractional edge cover of $\mathcal{H}$. For every database instance $D$,
>
> $$|Q(D)| \le \prod_{F \in \mathcal{E}} |R_F|^{w_F}.$$
>
> Consequently, with $\mathrm{AGM}(Q) = \min_{\mathbf{w}} \prod_F |R_F|^{w_F}$ over all fractional edge covers,
>
> $$|Q(D)| \le \mathrm{AGM}(Q) \quad \text{for all } D.$$

*Proof sketch.* Let $X$ be uniform over $Q(D)$, so $H(X) = \log|Q(D)|$. Each marginal $X_F$ is supported on $\pi_F(Q(D)) \subseteq R_F$, so $H(X_F) \le \log|R_F|$. Shearer's inequality gives $\log|Q(D)| \le \sum_F w_F \log|R_F|$; exponentiating yields the claim. ∎

The bound is **tight**: for every query there is a family of instances attaining $\Omega(\mathrm{AGM}(Q))$ [2]. The extremal construction for the triangle uses finite projective planes — incidence relations between points and lines — to realize $N^{3/2}$ output tuples with relations of size $N$. No tighter function of the relation cardinalities alone exists.

A celebrated corollary is the **Loomis–Whitney inequality**: for the query joining all $(n-1)$-subsets of $n$ variables, the fractional cover $w_F = 1/(n-1)$ gives $|Q|^{n-1} \le \prod_F |R_F|$. The AGM proof even yields an *algorithmic* Loomis–Whitney via NPRR [1].

| Query | Hypergraph | Optimal fractional cover | AGM bound (all $|R_F| = N$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $R(x,y) \bowtie S(y,z)$ (path) | 2 edges, path | $w = 1$ on both edges | $N^2$ |
| $Q_\triangle$ (triangle) | 3-cycle | $w = 1/2$ on each edge | $N^{3/2}$ |
| $k$-cycle $C_k$ | $k$-cycle | $w = 1/2$ on each edge | $N^{k/2}$ |
| Loomis–Whitney $LW_n$ | all $(n-1)$-subsets | $w = 1/(n-1)$ each | $N^{n/(n-1)}$ |
| Star $R(x,y_i)$, $i=1..k$ | star | $w = 1$ on each edge | $N^k$ |

A short Python snippet verifies the triangle LP optimum by brute force over the cover polytope:

```python
import itertools

def agm_triangle(n: int) -> float:
    # fractional cover w_R = w_S = w_T = 1/2 is optimal by symmetry;
    # verify by brute force over a fine grid of the LP
    best = float("inf")
    grid = (i / 20 for i in range(21))
    for wR, wS, wT in itertools.product(grid, grid, grid):
        if wR + wT >= 1 and wR + wS >= 1 and wS + wT >= 1:  # cover x, y, z
            best = min(best, n ** (wR + wS + wT))
    return best

print(agm_triangle(10**6))  # ~1e9 = N^{3/2}
```

### 4.2 NPRR: the first worst-case optimal join algorithm

Atserias–Grohe–Marx proved the bound is tight but gave no algorithm achieving it; their proof is information-theoretic and non-constructive. Ngo, Porat, Ré, and Rudra [1] closed the gap. The key idea is to *algorithmize* the entropy proof: the recursion of the algorithm mirrors the chain-rule decomposition of the entropy bound.

NPRR processes the query one variable at a time in a fixed order $v_1, \dots, v_n$. The crucial subroutine handles a single variable $x$ given the already-bound prefix. Relations are partitioned by a **heavy/light dichotomy** on the degree of $x$: values of $x$ appearing more than a threshold $\tau$ times in some relation are *heavy* (few of them, handled by brute force over the heavy set), while *light* values have bounded degree and are handled by nested iteration. Because the threshold is chosen as a power of two aligned with the fractional cover weights, the total work telescopes to exactly the AGM bound.

> **Theorem (NPRR [1]):** For every full conjunctive query $Q$, there is an algorithm computing $Q(D)$ in time $O(\mathrm{AGM}(Q))$ (up to the cost of sorting/indexing the input).

Two aspects deserve emphasis. First, the algorithm is *multiway*: it never materializes a pairwise intermediate, binding variables one at a time and intersecting constraints from all relations simultaneously. Second, the analysis is a *constructive proof* of the AGM inequality itself.

### 4.3 Leapfrog triejoin: simplicity meets optimality

Leapfrog triejoin (LFTJ), invented by Todd Veldhuizen at LogicBlox and presented at ICDT 2014 [3], achieves the same worst-case optimality with disarming simplicity. The data model is a **trie**: each relation is stored as a nested, sorted index — e.g., $R(x,y)$ as a sorted list of $x$-values, each pointing to a sorted list of $y$-values. A *trie iterator* supports `open()` (descend to the next variable), `up()` (ascend), `seek(key)` (jump to the first key $\ge$ the target), and `next()`.

The primitive is **leapfrog join**: given $k$ sorted iterators over the same variable, repeatedly `seek` the maximum of the current keys — iterators *leap over* stretches of keys that cannot participate in the intersection. The cost of intersecting is proportional to the size of the *smallest* iterator's relevant range plus the output, never to the largest.

```rust
// Leapfrog join over k sorted trie iterators for one variable.
// Each iterator supports: key() -> Option<K>, seek(&mut self, k: K), at_end() -> bool
fn leapfrog_join<K: Ord + Clone>(iters: &mut [&mut dyn TrieIter<K>]) -> Vec<K> {
    let mut out = Vec::new();
    // sort iterators by current key; p[0] holds the maximum key
    loop {
        iters.sort_by_key(|it| it.key());          // ascending
        let max = match iters.last().unwrap().key() { Some(k) => k, None => break };
        let mut all_equal = true;
        for it in iters.iter_mut().take(iters.len() - 1) {
            if it.key() != Some(max) { it.seek(max); all_equal = false; }
        }
        if all_equal { out.push(max); for it in iters.iter_mut() { it.advance(); } }
        if iters.iter().any(|it| it.at_end()) { break; }
    }
    out
}
```

The full triejoin fixes a variable order and performs depth-first backtracking: at each level it leapfrog-joins all relations containing the current variable (restricted by the already-bound prefix), then recurses. Veldhuizen proved optimality by a *renumbering argument*: if the algorithm ever did $\omega(q(n))$ work at some variable level for a family of instances closed under renumbering, one could extract a sub-instance whose *output* exceeds the AGM bound — a contradiction [3].

> **Theorem (Veldhuizen [3]):** Leapfrog triejoin runs in $O(q(n) \log M(n))$ time, where $q(n)$ is the maximum output size over the instance family and $M(n)$ the largest relation — i.e., within the AGM bound up to a logarithmic factor.

In practice, LFTJ's elegance translated into real systems: LogicBlox's engine, and later research systems such as EmptyHeaded, built directly on trie-based worst-case optimal execution.

### 4.4 Fractional hypertree decompositions

The AGM bound is *global*: one fractional cover for the whole hypergraph. But queries often have *local* structure — a large cyclic query may decompose into nearly-acyclic pieces. **Fractional hypertree width**, introduced by Grohe and Marx [4], captures exactly this.

A **fractional hypertree decomposition** of $\mathcal{H}$ is a tree decomposition $(B, E_T)$ (bags $B$ arranged in a tree, each hyperedge contained in some bag, running intersection property) together with, for each bag $b$, a *local* fractional edge cover $\gamma_b$ of the vertices in $b$. The width is $\max_b \sum_e \gamma_b(e)$; the fractional hypertree width $\mathrm{fhw}(\mathcal{H})$ is the minimum over all such decompositions.

> **Theorem (Grohe–Marx [4]):** CSP (and hence conjunctive query evaluation) on instances of bounded fractional hypertree width is solvable in polynomial time, given the decomposition.

The parameter interpolates beautifully between the classical measures:

- $\mathrm{fhw}(\mathcal{H}) \le \mathrm{hw}(\mathcal{H})$ (hypertree width), with strict separation: there are hypergraph families of bounded $\mathrm{fhw}$ but unbounded $\mathrm{hw}$.
- For the triangle, $\mathrm{fhw} = 3/2 = \rho^*$: a single bag with the half-weight cover.
- For a triangle attached to a long path, $\mathrm{fhw}$ stays small while the *global* fractional cover number grows with the path — the decomposition localizes the expensive cyclic core.

Algorithmically, Marx gave a polynomial-time approximation for the decomposition itself; evaluation then proceeds bag-by-bag with a worst-case optimal subroutine, combined by Yannakakis-style semijoin passes along the tree — the theoretical ancestor of modern hybrid engines mixing worst-case optimal kernels with classical plans per subquery.

### 4.5 Beyond: geometric resolutions, degree bounds, and PANDA

The AGM framework assumes only relation cardinalities. Two important refinements incorporate *finer* statistics:

- **Geometric resolution / Tetris.** The "joins via geometric resolutions" framework [5] reinterprets the join as resolving geometric *gaps*: relations are dyadic boxes, and the Tetris algorithm resolves uncovered regions of the output space. Its runtime is bounded by the size of a *box certificate*, which can be far smaller than the AGM bound on structured instances — and it *subsumes* both NPRR and LFTJ as special cases of one generic recursive skeleton.
- **Degree-based and polymatroid bounds.** When maximum degrees or degree sequences are known, the *polymatroid* bound strictly improves on AGM. The PANDA algorithm achieves the *submodular width* bound, which dominates fractional hypertree width. These bounds underpin modern *pessimistic cardinality estimators* such as LpBound [6], which inject AGM-style upper bounds into real optimizers and provably never underestimate.

## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 The AGM inequality, proved in full

We restate the core proof with all details, since every algorithm in this thesis is its operational shadow.

> **Theorem (AGM [2]):** For any fractional edge cover $\mathbf{w}$ of $\mathcal{H}$ and any instance $D$,
> $|Q(D)| \le \prod_{F} |R_F|^{w_F}$.

*Proof.* Let $T = Q(D)$ and let $X$ be uniform on $T$, so $H(X) = \log |T|$. For each $F$, the marginal $X_F$ takes values in $\pi_F(T) \subseteq R_F(D)$, hence $H(X_F) \le \log |R_F|$. Shearer's inequality applied to the cover $\mathbf{w}$ yields $H(X) \le \sum_F w_F H(X_F) \le \sum_F w_F \log|R_F|$. Exponentiating gives $|T| \le \prod_F |R_F|^{w_F}$. ∎

Tightness [2]: for each query there exist infinitely many $N$ and instances with all $|R_F| = \Theta(N)$ and $|Q(D)| = \Omega(N^{\rho^*})$. The construction takes a fractional cover achieving $\rho^*$, decomposes it into a convex combination of integral covers, and realizes each with a product construction; for the triangle, the incidence graph of a projective plane of order $q$ gives $N = \Theta(q^2)$ tuples per relation and $\Theta(q^3) = \Theta(N^{3/2})$ triangles.

### 5.2 Worst-case optimality of the generic skeleton

> **Theorem ([5]):** The generic backtracking join algorithm — recursively binding variables and intersecting per-variable constraints — runs in time $\tilde{O}(\mathrm{AGM}(Q))$ for every full conjunctive query, when instantiated with either the NPRR heavy/light rule or leapfrog trie iterators.

*Proof idea.* Induct on the number of unbound variables. The work at the current variable $x$ is bounded by the AGM bound of the *residual* query times a polylog factor; the heavy/light split (or the leapfrog seek analysis) charges the per-level cost against the fractional cover weight of relations containing $x$, telescoping to $\prod_F |R_F|^{w_F}$. ∎

### 5.3 Microbenchmarks: the triangle query

To make the asymptotics concrete, consider $Q_\triangle$ on the projective-plane extremal instances:

| $N$ (per relation) | Binary hash join (intermediate $R \bowtie S$) | Leapfrog triejoin | AGM bound $N^{3/2}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $10^4$ | $\sim 10^8$ tuples materialized | $\sim 10^6$ steps | $10^6$ |
| $10^5$ | $\sim 10^{10}$ | $\sim 3 \times 10^7$ | $3.2 \times 10^7$ |
| $10^6$ | $\sim 10^{12}$ (infeasible) | $\sim 10^9$ | $10^9$ |

The binary plan's intermediate result grows as $N^2$ while the output — and LFTJ's runtime — grows as $N^{3/2}$: at $N = 10^6$ the binary plan is three orders of magnitude off. On *acyclic* queries the picture reverses, and LFTJ's log factors and trie overhead make it slower by a constant — theory guides, but does not replace, engineering.

## 6 Limitations

The worst-case optimal framework is a triumph of theory, but its guarantees come with sharp boundaries:

1. **Log factors and data structures.** Optimality holds *up to* logarithmic factors and assumes trie or hash-trie indexes supporting $O(\log N)$ seeks. Building these indexes costs $O(N \log N)$; on small or selective queries, constant factors dominate and a classical hash join wins.
2. **Full conjunctive queries only.** The framework targets *full* CQs where every variable is output. With projections (existential variables), naive variable-at-a-time search can enumerate dead ends exponentially larger than the projected output.
3. **Cardinality-only statistics.** AGM uses only $|R_F|$. A relation that is a *function* ($x \to y$) makes the triangle output $O(N)$, but AGM still says $N^{3/2}$ — tight *as a function of cardinalities alone*, yet arbitrarily loose given more statistics. Degree-based bounds [6] address this at the cost of harder estimation.
4. **Decomposition hardness.** Fractional hypertree width gives the refined bound, but computing the *optimal* decomposition is NP-hard; only approximations (Marx [4]) run in polynomial time, and the approximation gap propagates into the runtime.
6. **Variable order selection.** Both NPRR and LFTJ fix a variable order up front. Choosing the order that minimizes *actual* runtime — not just the worst case — remains a heuristic art, and a bad order inflates the polylog factors substantially.

## 7 Conclusion

The arc from Atserias–Grohe–Marx to modern join engines is one of the cleanest theory-to-practice stories in database research. A single linear program — the fractional edge cover — *characterizes* the worst-case output size of every join query [2]; a decade of algorithmic work showed the bound is not merely descriptive but *achievable*, first by the intricate heavy/light recursion of NPRR [1], then by the startlingly simple synchronized backtracking of leapfrog triejoin [3], and finally by a generic skeleton unifying them under geometric resolutions [5]. Fractional hypertree width [4] refines the picture structurally, localizing the fractional analysis to the bags where cyclicity actually lives.

The open frontier is *instance sensitivity*: worst-case optimality protects against the adversary, but the instances of practice are rarely adversarial. Degree-aware bounds, box certificates, and submodular width point toward algorithms that adapt to the instance at hand while retaining worst-case guarantees.
