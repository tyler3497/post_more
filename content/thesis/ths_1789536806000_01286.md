# Mechanistic Interpretability of Transformer Language Models: Induction Heads, Superposition and Polysemanticity, Sparse Autoencoder Feature Extraction, and Circuit-Level Analysis of In-Context Learning

## Abstract

Mechanistic interpretability seeks to reverse-engineer transformer language models into human-understandable algorithms, replacing post-hoc attribution with causal circuit analysis grounded in the model's own weights. This article develops the four pillars of the modern framework: **induction heads**, the two-head attention circuit that implements `A B ... A → B` pattern completion through *QK/OV composition*; **superposition and polysemanticity**, the phenomenon by which networks represent more features than they have neurons by encoding them as nearly-orthogonal overlapping directions; **sparse autoencoders**, the dictionary-learning method that recovers monosemantic features from superposed activations at production scale; and **circuit-level tracing of in-context learning**, which connects abstract attention primitives to emergent behavior. We present the residual-stream formalism, the geometry of superposed representations, the training objective and evaluation protocol for sparse autoencoders, and the causal ablation methodology used to verify circuits empirically. The article closes with compressed-sensing recovery guarantees, scaling behavior of feature extraction, and open problems in automated circuit discovery.

## 1 Introduction

Deep neural networks remain, for the most part, *black boxes*: we can measure their inputs and outputs with exquisite precision, yet the internal computations that map one to the other resist human comprehension. For large language models, this opacity is not merely an aesthetic problem. Models are deployed in settings where **reliability, safety, and predictability** matter, and where failures can be subtle, adversarial, or high-stakes. The field of *mechanistic interpretability* responds with an ambitious program: to reverse-engineer trained networks into understandable algorithms, expressed in terms of features the network represents and circuits the network computes [1].

This article is organized around four connected discoveries that define the modern mechanistic understanding of transformers:

1. **Induction heads** — attention heads that complete repeated patterns, and whose formation explains much of in-context learning [6].
2. **Superposition and polysemanticity** — the geometric reason individual neurons appear to respond to many unrelated concepts at once [2].
3. **Sparse autoencoders (SAEs)** — a scalable, unsupervised dictionary-learning method that decomposes superposed activations into interpretable, monosemantic features [3][4][5].
4. **Circuit-level analysis of in-context learning** — end-to-end case studies, such as indirect object identification in GPT-2 Small, that verify the framework causally [7].

Each pillar builds on the last: induction heads provide the canonical example of a *circuit*; superposition explains why naive neuron-level analysis stalls; sparse autoencoders supply the *features* that circuits are built from; and in-context learning circuits demonstrate the methodology applied to genuinely emergent behavior.

---

> **Definition:** *Mechanistic interpretability.* The attempt to reverse-engineer neural networks into human-understandable algorithms by identifying the features a network represents and the circuits, composed of neurons, attention heads, and their connections, that transform those features [1].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The residual stream and the transformer circuits formalism

Elhage and colleagues introduced a mathematical framework that treats the transformer's **residual stream** as the central object of analysis [1]. Each token position carries a vector in a high-dimensional space; attention heads and MLP layers read from this stream and write back into it, and the final unembedding reads the accumulated result. Crucially, because the residual stream is a linear communication channel, each component's contribution can be studied *independently* of the others.

An attention head is decomposed into two circuits:

- The **QK circuit**, formed by the product of the query and key weight matrices, determines *where* the head attends — the attention pattern as a function of the input.
- The **OV circuit**, formed by the product of the output and value matrices, determines *what* the head writes — how the attended-to information is transformed and added to the residual stream.

This decomposition is powerful because it converts questions about behavior into questions about linear algebra. Two heads in different layers can be understood as *composing*: the OV circuit of an earlier head writes information that the QK circuit of a later head reads. Three kinds of composition arise — Q-composition, K-composition, and V-composition — and the induction head circuit is the canonical example of **K-composition** [1][6].

### 2.2 From neurons to features

Classical interpretability studied individual **neurons** as the unit of analysis, cataloguing the inputs that maximally activated them. This program repeatedly encountered a frustrating phenomenon: *polysemantic* neurons that fired for seemingly unrelated concepts — a single neuron responding to legal text, DNA sequences, and Hebrew script, for instance [2][8].

The **linear representation hypothesis** proposes a way out: rather than individual neurons, the fundamental units are *directions* in activation space.

| Level of analysis | Unit | Interpretable? | Handles superposition? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neuron-level | Single neuron | Sometimes | No — polysemanticity |
| Direction-level | Arbitrary vector | With effort | Partially |
| Feature-level (SAE) | Learned dictionary atom | Yes — monosemantic | Yes — by construction |
| Circuit-level | Composition of features | Yes — algorithmic | Yes — causal verification |

### 2.3 Causal methodology

Mechanistic claims must be **causal**, not merely correlational. The standard toolkit includes:

- **Ablation**: zeroing or replacing a component's activations and measuring the change in a behavioral metric, establishing necessity.
- **Activation patching**: substituting activations from a counterfactual run to localize where information flows [7].
- **Path patching**: patching along specific computational paths to isolate individual circuit edges.
- **Direct logit attribution**: reading a component's contribution to the output logits through the unembedding, exploiting the linearity of the residual stream.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Identifying induction heads

An **induction head** implements the operation: given a sequence containing `A B ... A`, increase the logit of `B` — predicting the token that followed the earlier occurrence of the current token [6]. Detection proceeds via an *induction score*: synthetic sequences of repeated random tokens are fed to the model, and for each head we measure how strongly it attends from the second occurrence of a token back to the token that followed the first occurrence, combined with how much its output shifts the logits toward the correct continuation. Heads scoring near one are induction heads; the distribution is strongly bimodal, cleanly separating induction from non-induction heads.

The induction circuit requires **two heads working in composition**:

1. A **previous-token head** in an earlier layer, whose OV circuit copies information about token *i − 1* into the residual stream at position *i*.
2. The **induction head** itself, whose QK circuit matches the current token against the previous-token head's output (K-composition) and whose OV circuit copies the *next*-token information forward, boosting the logit of `B`.

### 3.2 Training sparse autoencoders

A sparse autoencoder is trained on the *activations* of a frozen language model to reconstruct them through a sparse, overcomplete bottleneck [3]. Given activations *x* in R^d, the encoder produces latents *f(x) = ReLU(W_enc x + b_enc)* in R^m with *m >> d*, and the decoder reconstructs *x̂ = W_dec f(x) + b_dec*. The loss balances reconstruction fidelity against sparsity:

```python
# SAE training objective (L1-penalized variant)
def sae_loss(x, x_hat, f, lmbda):
    recon = ((x - x_hat) ** 2).mean()      # reconstruction fidelity
    sparsity = lmbda * f.abs().sum()       # sparsity pressure on latents
    return recon + sparsity
```

Modern variants replace the L1 penalty with **TopK** or **JumpReLU** activations that enforce an exact sparsity budget of *k* active latents per token, improving the fidelity–sparsity Pareto frontier [9]. Evaluation combines *reconstruction quality* (variance explained, downstream cross-entropy when the SAE is spliced into the model), *sparsity* (mean L0, the number of active latents), and *interpretability* — measured by automated methods in which a separate model writes and then validates natural-language explanations of each feature's activating examples [3].

### 3.3 Automated interpretability and feature steering

Once features are extracted, each latent is assigned a natural-language description from its top-activating contexts, and the description is *scored* by how well it predicts the feature's activations on held-out examples [3][5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Induction heads: the canonical circuit

The induction head discovery is the cleanest existence proof that transformers contain discrete, reverse-engineerable algorithms [6]. Its significance is twofold. First, it is a *complete* mechanistic story: from the QK/OV weight matrices, through the two-head composition, to the behavioral prediction on `A B ... A` sequences, every step is derived from the weights rather than hypothesized from behavior. Second, it explains **in-context learning**: Olsson and colleagues showed that the emergence of induction heads during training coincides with a sharp phase change in few-shot in-context learning ability, and that ablating induction heads degrades in-context learning while leaving other capabilities largely intact [6].

Formally, let the previous-token head *h_prev* write into the residual stream, and let the induction head *h_ind* read it. The attention score of *h_ind* at position *i* to position *j* is approximately large when token *j* equals the token at position *i* (matching via K-composition through *h_prev*'s output), and the OV circuit of *h_ind* then copies information about token *j + 1* forward.

> **Theorem:** *Induction mechanism.* In an attention-only transformer, an induction head composed with a previous-token head implements the map from context `A B ... A` to an increased logit for `B`, where the attention pattern is determined by the K-composition of the induction head's QK circuit with the previous-token head's OV circuit [1][6].

### 4.2 Superposition and polysemanticity

Why do networks use superposition at all? Elhage and colleagues studied the phenomenon in **toy models** — small ReLU networks trained to reconstruct sparse feature vectors — and found that superposition is often *loss-optimal*: when features are sparse (few active per input) and vary in importance, the network minimizes loss by packing many features into few neurons as nearly-orthogonal directions, accepting small interference costs in exchange for large capacity gains [2].

The geometry is precise. In the sparse regime, learned feature directions form a structured, interpretable arrangement (for example, antipodal pairs or vertices of polytopes in the toy models); as feature density or importance heterogeneity grows, the model transitions through phases — from dedicated neurons, to superposition with one feature per direction-pair, to dense interference where recovery becomes impossible [2]. This phase structure predicts, and largely matches, what is observed in real language models: some neurons are monosemantic (dedicated directions), while most participate in superposition [4].

Scherlis and colleagues sharpened the capacity argument, showing theoretically and empirically that polysemanticity expands the class of functions a fixed-width network can compute — it is not a training artifact but a *feature* of gradient-based learning under capacity constraints [8].

A naive fix — forcing neurons to be sparse with activations like SoLU — was shown to fail instructively: polysemanticity was not eliminated but *pushed into other components*, notably the LayerNorm scale, demonstrating that superposition is a property of the optimization pressure, not of any single architectural choice [2].

> **Definition:** *Superposition.* A representational regime in which a network with *n* neurons represents *m > n* features by assigning each feature to a direction in activation space, with features sharing neurons; individual neurons are then *polysemantic*, responding to multiple unrelated features [2].

### 4.3 Sparse autoencoders: dictionary learning at scale

Sparse autoencoders operationalize the *dictionary learning* strategy for undoing superposition: learn an overcomplete basis of directions such that each activation is a sparse combination of basis elements, then treat the basis elements — not the neurons — as the units of analysis [4]. The intellectual lineage runs from classical sparse coding of natural images through compressed sensing: *sparsity is what makes an overcomplete representation identifiable* [4].

Three results established the method:

1. **Proof of concept.** Cunningham and colleagues trained SAEs on a small language model and showed the learned features were more interpretable, by automated scoring, than neurons or alternative decompositions. Ablating a single SAE feature removed a capability (pronoun prediction) with less collateral disruption than ablating neurons — direct evidence that features, not neurons, are the right causal units [3].
2. **Monosemanticity at depth.** Bricken and colleagues decomposed the 512-neuron MLP layer of a one-layer transformer with expansion factors from 1× up to 256× (131,072 features), trained on 8 billion tokens. Their focused run learned 4,096 features including interpretable detectors for DNA sequences, legal language, and HTTP requests — features invisible at the neuron level [4].
3. **Production scale.** Templeton and colleagues applied SAEs to the residual stream of Claude 3 Sonnet, extracting tens of millions of features. The resulting atlas included safety-relevant features — detectors for deceptive reasoning, sycophancy, and disallowed content — and demonstrated *feature steering*: clamping a feature's activation reliably modulated the corresponding behavior in generated text, a causal intervention with direct safety applications [5].

> **Lemma:** *Sparse identifiability.* If activations are generated as sparse linear combinations of an overcomplete dictionary of nearly-orthogonal feature directions, then a sparse autoencoder with sufficient capacity and data recovers the generating dictionary up to permutation and scaling, provided the sparsity level is below the compressed-sensing recovery threshold [2][4].

The lemma's conditions are the crux of current research: real activations are only *approximately* sparse, and features are only *approximately* orthogonal [9].

### 4.4 Circuit-level analysis of in-context learning

The methodology comes together in end-to-end circuit analyses. The landmark case study is **indirect object identification (IOI)** in GPT-2 Small: given *"When Mary and John went to the store, John gave a drink to ___"*, the model predicts *Mary* [7]. Wang and colleagues reverse-engineered the full circuit:

- **Duplicate-token and induction heads** detect the repeated name (*John* appears twice).
- **S-inhibition heads** suppress attention to the duplicated subject.
- **Name-mover heads** copy the remaining name (*Mary*) to the output, directly boosting its logit.

Every edge was verified by path patching, and the circuit achieves high faithfulness: knocking it out destroys IOI performance while preserving general language modeling [7].

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Quantitative findings

The empirical record of the framework is unusually concrete for interpretability research:

| Result | Measurement | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Induction heads drive ICL | Ablating induction heads sharply degrades few-shot ICL; their formation coincides with the ICL phase change in training | [6] |
| SAE features beat neurons | Automated interpretability scores higher for SAE latents than neurons or PCA/ICA directions | [3] |
| Single-feature ablation | Removing one SAE feature eliminates pronoun prediction with minimal side effects | [3] |
| Monosemantic decomposition | 512-neuron MLP decomposed into 4,096 interpretable features (up to 131,072 at 256× expansion) | [4] |
| Production-scale atlas | Tens of millions of features from Claude 3 Sonnet, including safety-relevant and steerable features | [5] |
| IOI circuit faithfulness | Identified circuit explains the large majority of IOI task performance under ablation | [7] |

### 5.2 Formal guarantees

The theoretical backbone connects superposition to **compressed sensing**. In the idealized model, an activation *x* in R^n is a *k*-sparse combination of *m > n* feature directions: *x = D f* with *||f||_0 = k*. Classical results establish that when the dictionary *D* satisfies incoherence conditions, the sparsest solution is unique and recoverable by convex (ℓ1) optimization provided *k* is on the order of *n / log(m/n)*. The SAE objective is the learned, amortized analogue of this recovery problem: rather than solving an optimization per activation, the encoder *learns* the recovery map.

> **Theorem:** *Sparse recovery.* Let *x = D f* with *f* k-sparse in R^m, and let *D* have normalized columns with mutual coherence *μ*. If *k < (1/μ + 1)/4*, then *f* is the unique sparsest representation of *x*, and ℓ1 minimization recovers it exactly. A sparse autoencoder trained to convergence on such data approximates this recovery map with its encoder [2][4].

A second guarantee concerns the *geometry* of superposition itself. By the Johnson–Lindenstrauss phenomenon, *m* feature directions can be embedded in R^n with pairwise inner products bounded by roughly *sqrt(log m / n)* — nearly orthogonal. Interference between features therefore scales as *sqrt(k log m / n)* for *k* simultaneously active features, which is negligible precisely when features are sparse relative to dimension [2]. This is the quantitative form of the claim that superposition is cheap when features are sparse — and expensive when they are dense, predicting the phase transitions observed in toy models.

### 5.3 Causal verification protocol

Empirical circuit claims are adjudicated by a standardized protocol [7]:

1. **Hypothesize** the circuit from attention patterns and direct logit attribution.
2. **Patch** each hypothesized edge via path patching; retain only edges with significant causal effect.
3. **Ablate** the full circuit and measure the performance drop (*necessity*); ablate its complement and confirm performance is preserved (*sufficiency up to residual*).
4. **Steer** individual features or heads and confirm the predicted behavioral change (*mechanistic control*) [5].

## 6 Limitations

The framework's successes should not obscure its boundaries:

- **Feature splitting and absorption.** SAE latents do not always correspond one-to-one with ground-truth features: a single concept may split across multiple latents, or a broad latent may absorb narrower ones, depending on hyperparameters and the sparsity budget [9].
- **Dead and ultra-rare latents.** A fraction of SAE latents never activate on the training distribution, wasting capacity; conversely, extremely rare but important features (e.g., deception-related) may be missed entirely at practical dictionary sizes [5].
- **Computational cost.** Training production-scale SAEs requires billions of activation samples and substantial compute; the current cost of a full-model feature atlas remains prohibitive for all but the largest labs [5].
- **The polysemy confound.** Not all apparent polysemanticity is superposition: a neuron firing for both "financial bank" and "river bank" may reflect shared *word-form* processing before disambiguation rather than compressed unrelated concepts. Standard metrics cannot always distinguish the two, risking overestimates of superposition [8].
- **Incomplete circuits.** Even the IOI circuit, the gold standard, leaves residual performance unexplained; most published circuits are partial, and automated circuit discovery at scale remains an open problem [7].
- **Interpretability ≠ safety.** Steerable features are dual-use: the same feature clamping that suppresses undesired behavior could amplify it. Mechanistic understanding is a capability multiplier whose safety value depends on who wields it [5].

## 7 Conclusion

Mechanistic interpretability has moved, in half a decade, from a speculative program to an empirical science with verified artifacts: induction heads that implement pattern completion through QK/OV composition [6], a geometric theory of superposition that predicts when polysemanticity is loss-optimal [2][8], sparse autoencoders that decompose production models into millions of monosemantic, steerable features [3][4][5], and complete causal circuits for behaviors as complex as indirect object identification [7].

The central thesis is now well supported: **neural networks are not inscrutable** — they are *compressed*, and the compression scheme (superposition of sparse features, composed into circuits) is itself reverse-engineerable. The path forward is clear: scale feature extraction to full models and full training runs, automate circuit discovery beyond hand-verified case studies, and close the loop from understanding to *control* — models whose every significant behavior traces to a verified circuit. Whether that goal is achievable at frontier scale is the defining open question of the field.
