# Hierarchical Navigable Small Worlds and the Geometry of Approximate Nearest-Neighbor Search in Vector Databases

## Abstract

*Approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) search is the computational backbone of modern vector databases, retrieval-augmented generation, and embedding-based recommender systems.* This thesis presents a unified geometric and algorithmic treatment of **Hierarchical Navigable Small Worlds (HNSW)** — the graph index that dominates production vector search — situating it within navigable small-world theory, metric-space search, and the curse of dimensionality. We formalize the layer-allocation distribution that produces skip-list-like exponential hierarchies, dissect the greedy beam-search procedure and its *ef* recall–latency tradeoff knob, and analyze the neighbor-selection heuristics that approximate relative-neighborhood diversity while keeping degree bounded. We connect HNSW to Kleinberg's navigability condition [2], contrast hierarchical graphs with flat small worlds and quantization-based indices, and trace the lineage to SSD-resident billion-scale systems such as **DiskANN/Vamana** [3] and the **Faiss** toolkit [5]. A comparative empirical survey over ANN-Benchmarks harnesses [4] establishes where graph methods dominate and where they break. We close with hard limitations — intrinsic dimensionality, dynamic updates, and filter interaction — and argue that the geometry of scale-separated proximity graphs, not ever-larger flat structures, is the correct organizing principle for modern vector search.

## 1. Introduction

The nearest-neighbor problem is deceptively simple to state and notoriously hard to solve at scale. Given a corpus $P = \{p_1, \dots, p_n\} \subset \mathcal{X}$ in a metric space $(\mathcal{X}, d)$ and a query $q \in \mathcal{X}$, return the point of $P$ minimizing $d(q, \cdot)$. Exact solutions in high dimension are essentially impossible to accelerate: Beyer *et al.* formalized the intuition that under mild conditions, the ratio between the nearest and farthest neighbor distances concentrates near one as dimension grows, defeating branch-and-bound pruning [5]. This *curse of dimensionality* motivated a principled retreat: Indyk and Motwani [6] defined the $c$-approximate nearest neighbor problem — return $p \in P$ with $d(q,p) \le (1+\varepsilon)\, d(q, P)$ — and showed that sublinear query times are achievable through *locality-sensitive hashing* (LSH), inaugurating three decades of approximate search research.

Yet LSH-family methods suffer a structural weakness: they answer queries by probing many independent hash buckets, and their memory footprint and hash-table count grow steeply with the demanded recall. The alternative that has decisively won the engineering war is the **proximity graph** approach: build a sparse directed graph over $P$ whose edges connect near neighbors, then answer queries by *greedy graph traversal* — starting from an entry point, repeatedly moving to the neighbor closest to $q$ until no improvement is possible. Naive greedy traversal on a flat $k$-NN graph gets stuck in local minima and wanders; the breakthrough, crystallized in Malkov and Yashunin's HNSW [1], was to impose a **controllable hierarchy of nested proximity graphs** that separates links by their characteristic distance scale. The result is a fully graph-based index with logarithmic query complexity scaling, no auxiliary coarse-quantizer structure, and recall–latency tradeoffs that swept every ANN benchmark of the late 2010s — and remain the default choice in production systems (Milvus, Qdrant, Weaviate, pgvector) today.

This thesis develops HNSW from first principles: the geometry of the hierarchy, the probabilistic analysis of its scaling, the heuristics that make it practical, its empirical behavior, and its theoretical debts to — and departures from — the navigable small-world literature.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The metric search landscape

ANN methods cluster into four families, each with a characteristic *knob* trading recall for speed [4]:

| Family | Canonical representative | Recall knob | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hashing | LSH, FALCONN | number of tables / probes | Provable $(1+\varepsilon)$ guarantees | Heavy memory, weak at high recall |
| Quantization | IVF-PQ, OPQ | `nprobe`, code size | Tiny memory, GPU-friendly | Coarse quantizer ceiling on recall |
| Trees / space partition | Annoy, FLANN | number of trees searched | Fast build, simple | Collapses in high intrinsic dimension |
| **Proximity graphs** | **HNSW**, NSG, DiskANN | `efSearch` | Best recall–latency frontier | Slow build, memory-hungry graph |

Graph methods dominate the upper-right of every recall–QPS plot on ANN-Benchmarks [4]; the rest of this thesis explains *why*.

### 2.2 From Milgram to Kleinberg: navigable small worlds

The story begins outside databases. Milgram's small-world experiments and the Watts–Strogatz model established that real networks combine high clustering with short average path lengths. But short paths existing is not the same as short paths being *findable* by a decentralized agent that sees only its neighbors' coordinates. Kleinberg [2] resolved this: consider an $n \times n$ grid where each node $u$ adds one long-range link to $v$ with probability proportional to $d(u,v)^{-\alpha}$. Then **greedy geographic routing succeeds in $O(\log^2 n)$ expected steps if and only if $\alpha = 2$** (in general, $\alpha$ equal to the lattice dimension):

> **Theorem (Kleinberg navigability, informal):** *In a $d$-dimensional lattice augmented with long-range edges sampled with probability $\propto r^{-\alpha}$, decentralized greedy search finds its target in polylogarithmic expected time iff $\alpha = d$; for $\alpha \neq d$ the expected delivery time is polynomial in the lattice size.*

The intuition is scale-freeness of *progress*: when $\alpha = d$, at every distance scale there is constant probability that the next hop halves the remaining distance, so $O(\log n)$ halvings each cost $O(\log n)$ steps. This is the deep geometric idea HNSW operationalizes: rather than relying on luck to generate long-range edges with the right distribution, *engineer* the scale separation explicitly into a hierarchy.

### 2.3 Skip lists as a design metaphor

Malkov and Yashunin [1] observe that HNSW's multi-layer structure is "similar to the skip list structure": each element is assigned a maximum layer drawn from an exponentially decaying distribution, exactly as skip-list nodes are assigned random tower heights. This similarity is not cosmetic — it is what makes the expected layer depth $O(\log n)$, the descent $O(\log n)$ hops, and the construction *incremental* (insertions proceed top-down from the current entry point, reusing the search routine itself). It also admits "straightforward balanced distributed implementation" [1].

## 3. Methodology

We now specify HNSW precisely, following the five algorithms of Malkov and Yashunin [1]: `INSERT`, `SEARCH-LAYER`, `K-NN-SEARCH`, `SELECT-NEIGHBORS-SIMPLE`, and `SELECT-NEIGHBORS-HEURISTIC`. Our presentation emphasizes the geometry; engineering details ( SIMD distance kernels, memory layouts, lock-free insertion) follow Faiss [5] and hnswlib (https://github.com/nmslib/hnswlib).

### 3.1 The layered graph

HNSW maintains layers $0, 1, \dots, L_{\max}$. Layer $\ell$ is a proximity graph over the subset of elements whose assigned maximum layer is at least $\ell$; layer $0$ contains every element, higher layers are exponentially sparser. Each element $q$ draws its maximum layer $l(q)$ as:

$$l(q) = \Big\lfloor -\ln\big(U(0,1)\big) \cdot m_L \Big\rfloor,$$

where $U(0,1)$ is uniform and $m_L$ is a normalization factor, conventionally set to $m_L = 1/\ln M$ for the target degree $M$ [1]. Consequently $\Pr[l(q) \ge \ell] = \exp(-\ell / m_L) = M^{-\ell}$, so layer $\ell$ holds roughly $n M^{-\ell}$ elements — an exponentially decaying pyramid. Edge degree is capped at $M_{\max} = M$ per layer (and $M_{\max 0} = 2M$ at layer 0, where connectivity matters most).

The scale-separation property is the crux: because higher layers contain fewer, sparser points, their edges are *long* (characteristic distance $\sim M^{\ell/d}$); lower layers' edges are short. A search descending the pyramid therefore moves from coarse, global hops to fine, local hops — exactly Kleinberg's halving cascade, but constructed rather than sampled.

### 3.2 Insertion and search

Insertion of a new element $q$ proceeds in two phases [1]:

1. **Top-down greedy descent** (with `ef = 1`): starting at the global entry point in layer $L_{\max}$, run greedy `SEARCH-LAYER` down to layer $l(q)+1$, each time re-seeding the entry point with the nearest element found. This positions $q$ in $O(\log n)$ hops.
2. **Layer-wise connection**: for each layer $\ell = \min(L_{\max}, l(q))$ down to $0$, run `SEARCH-LAYER` with beam width `efConstruction` to collect candidates, select $M$ neighbors via the heuristic (§4.3), add bidirectional edges, and shrink overfull neighborhoods.

A $k$-NN query mirrors insertion: greedy descent from the top with `ef = 1`, then a **beam search** at layer 0 with beam width `efSearch` (= `ef`). The single parameter `ef` is the recall knob: larger `ef` explores more candidate paths, raising recall at the cost of distance computations.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The hierarchy as engineered navigability

Flat NSW (the predecessor of HNSW [1]) builds a single proximity graph with an approximate-Delaunay edge structure. Empirically it navigates well, but its degree grows with local density and its greedy search degrades toward polylogarithmic-with-large-constant behavior, because long jumps are accidental rather than guaranteed. HNSW fixes this by construction.

Consider a query descending from layer $L$. At layer $\ell$, the inter-point spacing among the $nM^{-\ell}$ resident points is roughly $(nM^{-\ell})^{-1/d}$ in $d$-dimensional volume-normalized space. A greedy step on this layer moves by distances of that order, so after $O(M)$ steps the search has advanced one "layer-scale" and can descend. With $L_{\max} \approx \log_M n$ layers and $O(1)$–$O(M)$ hops per layer, total search cost is $O(M \log_M n)$ distance computations — **logarithmic in $n$** — and the celebrated empirical curves confirm it: doubling the corpus adds a constant number of hops, not a proportional one [1][5]. Kleinberg's condition is thus satisfied *deterministically*: each layer plays the role of one distance scale, and the pyramid guarantees the long-range links that random sampling only provides in expectation.

### 4.2 The *ef* parameter: a microscope on the recall–latency frontier

The beam width `ef` is the single most important tuning parameter in vector search, and HNSW's elegance lies in exposing it at query time with no reindexing:

- `ef = 1` during descent phases: pure greedy routing, minimal cost.
- `ef = efSearch` at layer 0: the beam that actually determines quality.
- `efConstruction` at build time: wider beams during insertion produce better-connected graphs, at quadratic-ish build cost.

Empirically, recall@k as a function of `ef` is a saturating curve: small `ef` yields steep gains (the beam escapes the easy local minima), while pushing recall from 0.99 to 0.999 costs disproportionately more distance computations — the signature of a *heavy-tailed* distribution of "hard" queries whose true neighbors sit across graph bottlenecks. Production guidance crystallizes to three rules of thumb: set `M ∈ [16, 64]` (higher for high intrinsic dimension), `efConstruction ≥ 2·efSearch`, and choose `efSearch` from the recall target via the ANN-Benchmarks curve [4] rather than folklore.

### 4.3 Neighbor selection: why naive $k$-NN edges fail and diversity wins

The most subtle algorithm in HNSW is `SELECT-NEIGHBORS-HEURISTIC`. Connecting each node to its $M$ *nearest* candidates seems natural but is geometrically wrong: on clustered data, all $M$ nearest neighbors of a boundary point lie inside one cluster, and the graph loses the *inter-cluster* edges needed for global connectivity — the Delaunay graph between clusters is never approximated [1].

The heuristic instead enforces a **relative-neighborhood diversity** condition. Scanning candidates in order of increasing distance from $q$, a candidate $e$ is accepted only if it is closer to $q$ than to any already-accepted neighbor $e'$:

> **Heuristic (diversity filter):** *Accept $e$ iff $d(q, e) < d(e, e')$ for every previously accepted $e'$. Prefer candidates that open new directions over candidates that duplicate existing ones, even if the latter are nearer.*

Formally this approximates the *relative neighborhood graph* (RNG): an edge $(q,e)$ survives iff no witness $w$ satisfies $\max(d(q,w), d(e,w)) < d(q,e)$. The effect is dramatic on clustered data — the paper's canonical figure shows the heuristic selecting a cross-cluster bridge element that naive selection misses, "maintaining the global connectivity" [1]. DiskANN's Vamana generalizes this into the **$\alpha$-RNG rule**: keep $e$ iff $d(q,e) \le \alpha \cdot d(e,e')$ for all accepted $e'$, with $\alpha > 1$ deliberately *retaining* some longer edges to reduce hop count at the cost of degree [3]. The lineage from HNSW's heuristic to Vamana's $\alpha$-RNG is the clearest example of one algorithmic idea — diversity-constrained pruning — being re-tuned across the memory/SSD boundary.

### 4.4 The graph zoo: HNSW, NSG, and Vamana compared

HNSW is not the only proximity graph; understanding its position requires the alternatives:

| Index | Construction | Degree control | Key idea | Best setting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **HNSW** [1] | Incremental top-down | $M$, $M_{\max0}=2M$ | Scale-separated layers | General-purpose RAM |
| NSG (Fu *et al.* 2017) | k-NN graph → search-collect → prune | Fixed $R$ | Monotonic search network approximation | In-memory, tuned build |
| **Vamana** [3] | Random init → greedy self-search → $\alpha$-RNG prune | $R$, $\alpha$, $\gamma$ | SSD-friendly random-access layout | Billion-scale disk |

All three share the greedy-beam-search query path; they differ in how edges are chosen and where the index lives. The practical takeaway, validated across ANN-Benchmarks [4] and the Faiss study [5]: for in-RAM corpora up to ~10⁸ vectors, HNSW's build simplicity and query-time tunability make it the default; past RAM capacity, Vamana's disk-aware beam expansion (expanding several nodes per SSD round-trip) is required, and it still borrows HNSW's core traversal.

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

**Benchmark evidence.** The ANN-Benchmarks framework [4] evaluates dozens of implementations on standardized datasets (SIFT-1M, GIST-1M, DEEP-1B) with ground-truth $k=100$ neighbors, plotting queries-per-second against recall. Across its history, graph methods — HNSW (via hnswlib and Faiss), NSG, and later DiskANN — have defined the Pareto frontier: at recall@10 = 0.95 on SIFT-1M, HNSW answers thousands of queries per second on a single thread where IVF-PQ variants need an order of magnitude more memory or lose several recall points [4][6]. The framework's headline observation is worth quoting: *"very different approaches to k-NN search yield comparable quality-performance trade-offs"* — yet within that envelope, proximity graphs consistently sit at the frontier's edge [4].

**Billion-scale evidence.** DiskANN [3] moved the frontier past RAM: on SIFT-1B (one billion 128-d vectors), a single 16-core machine with 64 GB RAM and one SSD serves **>5,000 QPS at <3 ms mean latency with 95%+ 1-recall@1**, where FAISS IVF baselines with comparable footprint plateau near 50% recall. In the high-recall regime DiskANN indexes 5–10× more points per node than HNSW/NSG [3].

**Theory.** What is *proven*? Less than practitioners assume:

1. **Layer depth:** $\mathbb{E}[L_{\max}] = O(\log n)$ follows directly from the exponential allocation; each layer's expected size decays geometrically.
2. **Navigability at scale separation:** Kleinberg's theorem [2] gives polylogarithmic greedy routing *given* the right long-edge distribution; HNSW's hierarchy constructs a deterministic analogue, and Malkov–Yashunin's experiments show the predicted logarithmic hop scaling [1].
3. **Limits of proof:** No published result gives HNSW a $(1+\varepsilon)$-recall guarantee or a rigorous $O(\log n)$ query bound on adversarial data. The diversity heuristic's RNG approximation is justified empirically (Figure 2 of [1]) rather than by theorem. This proof gap — between a method that *works* and a method that is *understood* — remains the field's central open problem.

> **Theorem (informal synthesis):** *Under the idealized assumption that each layer's proximity graph is a $c$-approximate Delaunay graph and that layer $\ell$ has characteristic edge length $\Theta(M^{\ell/d})$, greedy descent followed by bounded beam search at layer 0 visits $O(M \log_M n + \text{ef} \cdot \Delta)$ nodes, where $\Delta$ is the local degree. The $\log n$ term is the hierarchy; the $\text{ef} \cdot \Delta$ term is the price of approximation.*

## 6. Limitations

A thesis that only praised HNSW would be marketing, not scholarship. Its weaknesses are real and well-documented:

1. **Intrinsic dimensionality.** Graph navigability degrades as the data's *intrinsic* dimension rises. On datasets like GloVe or high-dimensional text embeddings, the degree needed to maintain connectivity grows, memory balloons ($n \times M$ edges plus vectors), and the recall–latency curve flattens. No graph index repeals the curse of dimensionality [6]; it only pushes the wall outward.
2. **Build cost and dynamism.** Construction is $O(n \log n)$ with a large constant (`efConstruction` beam searches per insertion); billion-scale builds take days even on strong machines [3]. Deletions are worse: removing a node orphans its in-neighbors' edges, and most implementations (including early hnswlib) mark-and-lazy-delete, leaking graph quality over churn. True dynamic vector databases layer compaction and rebuild heuristics atop the raw index.
3. **Filtered search.** Real queries carry predicates ("nearest red shoes under $50"). Pre-filtering destroys the entry-point geometry; post-filtering wastes the beam on discarded candidates. The interaction of graph traversal with attribute filters is an active research area with no clean solution — a genuine gap between the ANN literature and database practice.
4. **Memory footprint.** $M=16$ with 4-byte ids costs 64 bytes/edge/direction; bidirectional edges at layer 0 ($M_{\max0}=32$) plus the vectors themselves make HNSW one of the most memory-hungry indices per point — the very pressure that motivated quantization hybrids (HNSW over PQ codes) and DiskANN's SSD tier [3][6].
5. **Theoretical opacity.** As §5 noted, the method's guarantees are empirical. Adversarial datasets can be constructed where greedy beam search fails systematically, and the diversity heuristic has known failure modes on pathological cluster geometries.

## 7. Conclusion

Hierarchical Navigable Small Worlds succeeded because they turned a *theorem about random networks* into an *engineering discipline for constructed ones*. Kleinberg [2] showed that navigability demands long-range edges distributed across distance scales with exponent matched to dimension; Malkov and Yashunin [1] showed that rather than hoping such edges appear, one can *build* the scales as nested graph layers with exponentially decaying membership — a skip list for metric space. The diversity heuristic solved the connectivity problem that naive proximity graphs could not, the `ef` beam gave practitioners a single query-time knob, and the ANN-Benchmarks methodology [4] let the field measure rather than argue.

The lineage continues: Vamana's $\alpha$-RNG [3] re-tuned the diversity rule for SSDs, Faiss [5] industrialized the whole zoo, and billion-vector serving on one machine went from fantasy to routine. Yet the deepest lesson is geometric, not algorithmic: **search efficiency in high dimensions is governed by scale separation, and the index that explicitly represents scales beats the index that merely hopes for them.** The open frontier — filtered search, dynamic graphs, and a real theory of beam-search recall — will be won by whoever extends that geometric insight, not by whoever tunes $M$ and `ef` hardest.
