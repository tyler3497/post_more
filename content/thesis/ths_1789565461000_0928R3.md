# Yau's Solution of the Calabi Conjecture: Complex Monge–Ampère Equations and Kähler–Einstein Metrics

## Abstract

In 1954, Eugenio Calabi conjectured that on a compact Kähler manifold, every de Rham class representable by a real closed (1,1)-form contains a unique Kähler metric with prescribed Ricci form — in particular, every compact Kähler manifold with vanishing first Chern class admits a Ricci-flat Kähler metric. The conjecture reduces to solving a fully nonlinear elliptic equation, the complex Monge–Ampère equation, for which no general existence theory existed. In 1976–1978, Shing-Tung Yau proved the conjecture via the continuity method, establishing the decisive $C^0$, $C^2$, and $C^{2,\alpha}$ a priori estimates for the complex Monge–Ampère operator. This article reconstructs Yau's proof: Kähler geometry and the $\partial\bar\partial$-lemma, reduction of the conjecture to the Monge–Ampère equation, openness via the linearized operator, and the closedness argument through Yau's estimates — including the celebrated $C^2$ estimate via the Aubin–Yau Laplacian inequality. We explain the consequences: the existence of Calabi–Yau metrics, the Kähler–Einstein problem on Fano manifolds and the Yau–Tian–Donaldson conjecture, and the role of Ricci-flat metrics in string theory and mirror symmetry.

## 1 Introduction

Few conjectures have reshaped geometry as profoundly as Calabi's. Let $M$ be a compact complex manifold admitting a Kähler metric $\omega_0$, and let $\tilde{\omega}$ be any real closed $(1,1)$-form representing the first Chern class $c_1(M)$. Calabi asked [1]: does there exist a Kähler metric $\omega$ in the same Kähler class $[\omega_0]$ whose Ricci form equals $\tilde{\omega}$? When $c_1(M) = 0$, taking $\tilde{\omega} = 0$ predicts a **Ricci-flat Kähler metric** — a metric of $SU(n)$ holonomy — on every compact Kähler manifold with vanishing first Chern class.

> **Theorem (Yau [2]):** Let $(M, \omega_0)$ be a compact Kähler manifold and $\tilde{R}$ a real closed $(1,1)$-form representing $c_1(M)$. Then there exists a unique Kähler metric $\omega$ cohomologous to $\omega_0$ with Ricci form $\operatorname{Ric}(\omega) = \tilde{R}$.

The statement is analytic: writing $\omega = \omega_0 + i\partial\bar\partial\varphi$ for a potential $\varphi$ (possible by the $\partial\bar\partial$-lemma), the Ricci condition becomes the **complex Monge–Ampère equation**
$$(\omega_0 + i\partial\bar\partial\varphi)^n = e^{F}\omega_0^n,$$
a fully nonlinear second-order elliptic PDE for $\varphi$, where $F$ is determined by $\tilde{R}$. In 1976, nothing like a general existence theory for such equations existed; the real Monge–Ampère equation was understood only through the difficult work of Pogorelov and Calabi. Yau's proof — via the continuity method and a sequence of brilliant a priori estimates — not only settled the conjecture but created the analytic toolkit of modern complex geometry.

This article presents Yau's theorem and its proof in full, then follows its consequences: Calabi–Yau manifolds and their moduli, the Kähler–Einstein problem on Fano manifolds culminating in the Yau–Tian–Donaldson theorem [3], and the physics of Ricci-flat metrics in string compactification.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Kähler geometry and Ricci curvature

A **Kähler manifold** is a complex manifold $(M, J)$ with a Hermitian metric $g$ whose associated $(1,1)$-form $\omega(X, Y) = g(JX, Y)$ is closed. Locally $\omega = i g_{j\bar{k}}\, dz^j \wedge d\bar{z}^k$ with $(g_{j\bar{k}})$ positive-definite Hermitian. The **Ricci form** is $\operatorname{Ric}(\omega) = -i\partial\bar\partial \log \det(g_{j\bar{k}})$, a real closed $(1,1)$-form representing $2\pi c_1(M)$. The **$\partial\bar\partial$-lemma** states that on a compact Kähler manifold, any two cohomologous real closed $(1,1)$-forms differ by $i\partial\bar\partial\varphi$ for a smooth function $\varphi$, unique up to constants.

The sign of $c_1(M)$ divides Kähler geometry into three worlds: $c_1 < 0$ (general type; Aubin and Yau independently constructed Kähler–Einstein metrics [4]), $c_1 = 0$ (Calabi–Yau; Yau's theorem), and $c_1 > 0$ (Fano; obstructed, resolved by K-stability [3]).

### 2.2 The continuity method

To solve a nonlinear PDE $F(\varphi) = 0$, the **continuity method** embeds it in a family $F_t(\varphi_t) = 0$ for $t \in [0,1]$, with $F_0$ solvable and $F_1 = F$. One shows the set $S = \{t : \text{solution exists}\}$ is both open and closed in $[0,1]$:

- **Openness** follows from the implicit function theorem once the linearized operator is invertible (an isomorphism between Hölder spaces).
- **Closedness** requires **a priori estimates**: uniform bounds on solutions $\varphi_t$ in a strong norm, independent of $t$, so that a convergent subsequence yields a solution at a limit point.

The entire difficulty of Yau's proof lies in closedness: obtaining $C^{2,\alpha}$ estimates for the complex Monge–Ampère equation with no prior theory to lean on.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Reduction to the Monge–Ampère equation

Given $\tilde{R}$ representing $c_1(M)$, write $\operatorname{Ric}(\omega_0) - \tilde{R} = i\partial\bar\partial F$ by the $\partial\bar\partial$-lemma, normalized so $\int_M e^F \omega_0^n = \int_M \omega_0^n$. Seeking $\omega = \omega_0 + i\partial\bar\partial\varphi$ with $\operatorname{Ric}(\omega) = \tilde{R}$ is equivalent to
$$\det(g_{j\bar{k}} + \varphi_{j\bar{k}}) = e^{F} \det(g_{j\bar{k}}), \qquad \omega_0 + i\partial\bar\partial\varphi > 0,$$
i.e., $(\omega_0 + i\partial\bar\partial\varphi)^n = e^F \omega_0^n$. Uniqueness follows from the maximum principle: the difference of two solutions satisfies a linear elliptic equation without zeroth-order term.

The continuity path is $(\omega_0 + i\partial\bar\partial\varphi_t)^n = e^{tF + c_t}\omega_0^n$, where $c_t$ normalizes the volume. At $t = 0$, $\varphi_0 = 0$ solves the equation.

### 3.2 Openness: the linearized operator

Linearizing the Monge–Ampère operator at a solution gives $L(\psi) = \Delta_{\omega}\psi$, the Laplacian of the unknown metric $\omega = \omega_0 + i\partial\bar\partial\varphi_t$. Since $\int_M \psi\, \omega^n = 0$ removes the kernel (constants), $L$ is an isomorphism $C^{2,\alpha}_0 \to C^{0,\alpha}_0$ by Schauder theory. The implicit function theorem then yields openness of $S$.

### 3.3 Closedness: Yau's a priori estimates

The heart of the proof is a hierarchy of estimates, each feeding the next:

1. **$C^0$ estimate:** $\sup_M |\varphi| \leq C$. Yau's proof uses Moser iteration on the equation $\Delta_{\omega_0}\varphi$ controlled via the Monge–Ampère equation, combined with the Sobolev and Poincaré inequalities. The normalization $\int_M \varphi\, \omega_0^n = 0$ fixes the constant.

2. **$C^2$ estimate:** $\sup_M |\partial\bar\partial\varphi|_{\omega_0} \leq C$. This is the deepest estimate. Yau considers the quantity $\log \operatorname{tr}_{\omega_0}\omega - A\varphi$ and applies the maximum principle, using the **Aubin–Yau second-order inequality**: at a maximum point of the trace, the bisectional curvature of $\omega_0$ appears with a favorable sign, and
$$\Delta_{\omega}(\log \operatorname{tr}_{\omega_0}\omega) \geq -C_1 \operatorname{tr}_{\omega}\omega_0 - C_2,$$
which after the $C^0$ bound controls $\operatorname{tr}_{\omega_0}\omega$, hence all second derivatives via the determinant equation.

3. **$C^{2,\alpha}$ estimate:** Follows from the Evans–Krylov theory for concave fully nonlinear uniformly elliptic equations, applicable once the $C^2$ bound gives uniform ellipticity [5].

Higher regularity then follows by bootstrapping (Schauder estimates for the linearized equation). Closedness of $S$ follows, and $1 \in S$.

# Pseudocode: continuity-method solver for the complex Monge-Ampere equation
def calabi_yau_metric(omega0, F, n_steps=50):
    phi = zeros_like(omega0.potential)  # t = 0 solution
    for k in range(1, n_steps + 1):
        t = k / n_steps
        # Newton iteration for (w0 + i ddbar phi)^n = exp(t*F + c) w0^n
        phi = newton_solve(monge_ampere_residual, phi, rhs=exp(t*F))
        assert is_kahler(omega0, phi)  # positivity preserved by estimates
    return omega0 + i_ddbar(phi)

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Aubin–Yau $C^2$ estimate in detail

Let $\omega = \omega_0 + i\partial\bar\partial\varphi$ and consider $u = \log \operatorname{tr}_{\omega_0}\omega$. In normal coordinates for $\omega_0$ at the maximum point $p$ of $u - A\varphi$, diagonalize $\omega$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_i$. A computation using the commutation relations for covariant derivatives gives
$$\Delta_{\omega} u \geq \frac{1}{\operatorname{tr}_{\omega_0}\omega}\sum_{i,j} R_{i\bar{i}j\bar{j}}(\omega_0)\frac{\lambda_j}{\lambda_i} - C\operatorname{tr}_{\omega}\omega_0,$$
where $R_{i\bar{i}j\bar{j}}$ is the bisectional curvature of $\omega_0$. If the bisectional curvature is bounded below — always true on a compact manifold — the curvature term is controlled by $\operatorname{tr}_{\omega}\omega_0$, and the maximum principle applied to $u - A\varphi$ yields an upper bound on $\operatorname{tr}_{\omega_0}\omega$ at $p$, hence everywhere. Since $\prod_i \lambda_i = e^F$ is bounded, an upper bound on $\sum_i \lambda_i$ gives two-sided bounds on each $\lambda_i$: uniform ellipticity.

### 4.2 Calabi–Yau manifolds and their moduli

A **Calabi–Yau manifold** is a compact Kähler manifold with $c_1 = 0$ (and, in the strict sense, $H^{i}(\mathcal{O}_M) = 0$ for $0 < i < n$). Yau's theorem equips each with a Ricci-flat Kähler metric, unique in each Kähler class. The **moduli space** of Calabi–Yau structures locally splits as a product of complex-structure moduli and (complexified) Kähler moduli — the Bogomolov–Tian–Todorov theorem proving unobstructedness of complex deformations [6]. The Weil–Petersson metric on moduli space and the special geometry of the period map are central to mirror symmetry [7].

### 4.3 The Fano case and K-stability

On Fano manifolds ($c_1 > 0$), Kähler–Einstein metrics need not exist: Matsushima's reductivity and the **Futaki invariant** give obstructions [8]. The **Yau–Tian–Donaldson conjecture**, proved by Chen–Donaldson–Sun [3], characterizes existence by **K-stability**, an algebro-geometric condition on degenerations (test configurations). The proof extends Yau's continuity method to conical Kähler–Einstein metrics along a divisor, with a priori estimates adapted to cone singularities — a direct descendant of Yau's original strategy.

### 4.4 String theory and mirror symmetry

In string theory, spacetime is $M^{3,1} \times X$ with $X$ a Calabi–Yau 3-fold carrying a Ricci-flat metric; supersymmetry demands exactly the $SU(3)$ holonomy that Yau's theorem provides [9]. **Mirror symmetry** predicts that Calabi–Yau 3-folds occur in pairs $(X, \check{X})$ with Hodge numbers reflected, $h^{1,1}(X) = h^{2,1}(\check{X})$, exchanging the Kähler and complex moduli — a prediction verified in thousands of examples via the Greene–Plesser construction and Batyrev's toric duality [7]. The Strominger–Yau–Zaslow conjecture interprets mirror symmetry as T-duality along special Lagrangian torus fibrations, with the Ricci-flat metric collapsing along the fibers.

### 4.5 The Kähler–Ricci flow

An alternative, parabolic route to canonical metrics is the **Kähler–Ricci flow** $\partial_t \omega = -\operatorname{Ric}(\omega)$. Cao proved long-time existence on manifolds with $c_1 \leq 0$ and convergence to the Kähler–Einstein (or Ricci-flat) metric [10] — a parabolic analogue of the continuity method where Yau's estimates reappear as Perelman-style non-collapsing and curvature bounds. On Fano manifolds the flow may develop singularities, and the Hamilton–Tian conjecture (proved by Chen–Wang) describes singularity formation via Kähler–Ricci solitons.

## 5 Empirical and Computational Aspects

Numerical Calabi–Yau metrics are computed by discretizing the Monge–Ampère equation. Donaldson's **balanced metrics** algorithm approximates the Ricci-flat metric via embeddings by sections of $L^k$, converging as $k \to \infty$ [11]. More recently, machine-learning approaches parametrize the Kähler potential by neural networks and minimize the Monge–Ampère residual $\|(\omega_0 + i\partial\bar\partial\varphi_\theta)^n/\omega_0^n - e^F\|$, achieving high accuracy on quintic 3-folds and enabling numerical tests of mirror-symmetry predictions for Yukawa couplings.

## 6 Limitations

- **Non-constructive:** Yau's proof gives no formula for the Ricci-flat metric; explicit Calabi–Yau metrics are known only in highly symmetric cases.
- **Fano obstructions:** K-stability is difficult to verify in practice; many Fano manifolds remain undecided.
- **Singular varieties:** Extending the estimates to singular Kähler–Einstein metrics (klt pairs) requires the full pluripotential theory of [12].
- **Numerics:** Machine-learned metrics lack rigorous error certification; the Monge–Ampère residual does not control the metric error without stability estimates.

## 7 Conclusion

Yau's solution of the Calabi conjecture is a landmark where hard analysis met deep geometry: a fully nonlinear PDE, attacked with no existing theory, yielded through sheer force of a priori estimates to produce the Ricci-flat metrics that geometry and physics demanded. The proof's architecture — continuity method, $C^0$ via Moser iteration, $C^2$ via the maximum principle on the trace, $C^{2,\alpha}$ via Evans–Krylov — became the template for geometric analysis, from the Kähler–Einstein problem to the Yamabe problem to mean curvature flow. The Calabi–Yau manifolds it produced now sit at the crossroads of algebraic geometry, differential geometry, and theoretical physics. Nearly fifty years on, the estimates still have not been bettered — only extended, to singular settings Yau could hardly have foreseen.
