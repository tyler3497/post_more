# Single-Cell Trajectory Inference: RNA Velocity, Diffusion Pseudotime, and Waddington Landscapes

## Abstract

Single-cell RNA sequencing captures only static snapshots of gene expression, yet biological processes such as differentiation unfold in time. Trajectory inference reconstructs the order and direction of cellular transitions from a single snapshot. This article unifies three major paradigms: RNA velocity, which estimates the time derivative of spliced mRNA from unspliced-to-spliced ratios using a kinetic model of transcription, splicing, and degradation [1][2]; diffusion pseudotime, which orders cells by diffusion-like random walks on a cell-similarity graph and robustly recovers lineage branching [3][4]; and the Waddington epigenetic landscape, formalized through velocity-guided Markov chains and continuous vector fields that identify initial, intermediate, and terminal states [5][6]. We derive the steady-state and dynamical velocity models, analyze their identifiability and failure modes, and show how directed Markov-chain frameworks generalize pseudotime with directionality. Case studies in pancreatic endocrinogenesis, hippocampal neurogenesis, and hematopoiesis demonstrate where each method succeeds and where all of them fail [8].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Pseudotime and the snapshot problem

Pseudotemporal ordering, introduced by Monocle, frames differentiation as a one-dimensional latent variable: each cell is assigned a scalar pseudotime value, and genes are modeled as smooth functions of that variable [7]. The algorithm reduces dimensionality, constructs a minimum spanning tree over cells, and orders cells along the longest path. Although effective on linear processes such as myoblast differentiation, the approach requires the root state to be supplied externally and struggles with branching topologies, where a tree must be fit and branch points located heuristically.

### 2.2 Diffusion maps and the geometry of cell similarity

Diffusion maps provide the geometric foundation on which modern trajectory inference is built [4]. Given cells $x_1, \dots, x_N$ in gene-expression space, one defines a Gaussian affinity kernel between neighbors,

$$w(x_i, x_j) = \exp\left(-\frac{\|x_i - x_j\|^2}{\sigma^2}\right),$$

row-normalizes it to obtain a Markov transition matrix $P = D^{-1}W$, and studies the spectral decomposition of $P$. The leading nontrivial eigenfunctions of $P$ parametrize the slow coordinates of the data manifold — the directions along which random walks mix slowly, which for developmental data correspond to differentiation axes [4]. Crucially, powers $P^t$ of the transition matrix define a family of **diffusion distances** between cells that integrate over all paths of length $t$, making them far more robust than shortest-path distances to gaps and sampling noise in the cloud.

### 2.3 The Waddington landscape, informally

Conrad Waddington's 1957 metaphor of the epigenetic landscape — a marble rolling downhill through a terrain of bifurcating valleys — has organized thinking about development for decades. In its original form it was qualitative: valleys represented canalized fates, ridges represented barriers, and bifurcations represented fate decisions. The modern program of trajectory inference can be read as an attempt to *measure* this landscape: to estimate, from snapshot data, the vector field that pushes each cell state toward its future, and the potential function whose gradient that vector field approximately follows [6].

> **Theorem (informal):** Under mild regularity conditions on the sampling density, the diffusion operator on the cell-similarity graph converges to the Fokker-Planck operator of a diffusion process on the underlying expression manifold, so that random-walk-based pseudotimes converge to a well-defined continuum limit [4].

## 3 Methodology

We now formalize the three paradigms. Table 1 summarizes their inputs, outputs, and characteristic timescales.

| Method | Signal used | Output | Direction | Handles branches |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Monocle pseudotime [7] | Spliced expression | Scalar ordering | Requires root | Heuristic |
| Diffusion pseudotime [3] | Expression graph | Random-walk distance | Requires root | Yes |
| Steady-state velocity [1] | Unspliced, spliced counts | Gene-wise time derivative | Intrinsic | Via projection |
| Dynamical velocity (scVelo) [2] | Unspliced, spliced counts | Velocity plus latent time | Intrinsic | Via projection |
| CellRank [5] | Velocity plus graph | Fate probabilities, macrostates | Intrinsic | Yes |
| dynamo vector fields [6] | Velocity or labeling data | Continuous vector field | Intrinsic | Yes |

### 3.1 The kinetic model of RNA velocity

For each gene, let $u(t)$ denote the abundance of unspliced pre-mRNA and $s(t)$ the abundance of spliced mRNA. The standard model of transcriptional kinetics is the pair of ordinary differential equations

$$\frac{du}{dt} = \alpha(t) - \beta u, \qquad \frac{ds}{dt} = \beta u - \gamma s,$$

where $\alpha(t)$ is the transcription rate, $\beta$ the splicing rate, and $\gamma$ the degradation rate [1]. The **RNA velocity** of the gene is defined as the time derivative of the spliced species, $v = ds/dt = \beta u - \gamma s$. A positive velocity means the gene is being upregulated (unspliced transcripts exceed what splicing can clear at the current spliced level); a negative velocity means it is being downregulated. Because unspliced reads can be counted from intronic alignments in ordinary droplet-based protocols, both $u$ and $s$ are available for thousands of genes per cell, yielding a high-dimensional velocity vector for every cell that predicts its transcriptional future on a timescale of hours [1].

### 3.2 From velocities to transition probabilities

Gene-wise velocities live in gene space, but cells live on a low-dimensional manifold. The standard pipeline smooths counts over nearest neighbors, estimates velocities per gene, and projects them into the embedding. CellRank goes further: it combines the velocity direction with expression similarity to define a Markov chain over cells, where the transition probability from cell $i$ to cell $j$ is large when $j$ lies in the direction of $i$'s velocity vector and is transcriptionally similar to $i$ [5]. The resulting chain is then coarse-grained into dynamical macrostates, classified as initial, intermediate, or terminal by their incoming and outgoing probability flux.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The steady-state model and the phase portrait

The original velocyto model [1] assumes that each gene has passed through a full cycle of induction and repression, and that the observed cells sample the steady states of that cycle. Setting both time derivatives to zero gives $u = \alpha/\beta$ and $s = \alpha/\gamma$ at equilibrium, so steady-state cells satisfy a linear relation $u = (\gamma/\beta)\, s$. In the unspliced-spliced phase plane, cells undergoing a complete pulse trace an almond-shaped loop: during induction, unspliced transcripts rise rapidly and spliced levels follow with a lag; during repression, transcription switches off and both species decay. The slope of the line fitted through the steady-state cells estimates the ratio $\gamma/\beta$ (with $\beta$ conventionally fixed to one), and the velocity of each cell is the signed residual from that line: cells above it are inducing, cells below it repressing [1].

The almond shape is the model's diagnostic signature -- genes that do not display it carry no directional information and must be excluded. The weaknesses are structural rather than statistical. The model assumes constant transcription, splicing, and degradation rates, a single shared splicing rate across genes, full observation of both kinetic phases, and no basal transcription. When only the induction phase is sampled -- common for lineage markers switched on abruptly -- the slope fit is biased and the inferred velocities can point *backward*, from mature cells toward progenitors [8].

### 4.2 Dynamical modeling and latent time in scVelo

scVelo discards the steady-state assumption and fits the full kinetic model by likelihood [2]. Transcription is modeled as a pulse: the rate $\alpha(t)$ switches on at time $t_0$ and off at time $t_1$, with gene-specific $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$. The ODE system then admits a closed-form solution -- piecewise exponential approach toward $u_{ss} = \alpha/\beta$ and $s_{ss} = \alpha/\gamma$ during induction, followed by exponential decay after switch-off. An expectation-maximization algorithm alternates between assigning each cell a **latent time** $t_i$ (the E-step: which point along the gene's kinetic curve best explains the observed unspliced-spliced pair) and updating the kinetic parameters (the M-step). Because each gene initially receives its own latent time, a consensus cell-level latent time is recovered afterward by aligning the gene-wise times, yielding a data-driven internal clock [2].

This generalization extends velocity to transient populations -- cells caught mid-pulse that never reach steady state -- and enables systematic driver-gene detection by correlating expression with latent time. The cost is identifiability: scaling $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$ together with the time axis leaves the phase curve invariant, so only relative quantities are estimable and scVelo fixes $\beta = 1$ (or uses a stochastic mode with second moments). Moreover, the EM fit can converge to time-reversed solutions for genes with weak phase-portrait curvature, reproducing the backward-flow failure on some datasets [8].

### 4.3 Diffusion pseudotime as a random-walk metric

Diffusion pseudotime abandons kinetics entirely and asks a geometric question: how many random-walk steps separate a root cell from every other cell on the similarity graph [3]? Given the Markov matrix $P$ from the diffusion-map construction, DPT uses its spectral decomposition -- eigenvalues $\lambda_k$ and right eigenvectors $\psi_k$ -- to define

$$\mathrm{dpt}(r, x)^2 = \sum_k \left(\frac{\lambda_k}{1 - \lambda_k}\right)^2 \big(\psi_k(r) - \psi_k(x)\big)^2,$$

which accumulates diffusion distances over all walk lengths, weighting the slow eigenfunctions most heavily. Branching is detected by comparing walks conditioned on alternative endpoints: cells whose walks diverge belong to distinct lineages, and metastable states appear as regions where walks linger before escaping [3]. Because the distance integrates over *all* paths rather than a single shortest path, it is robust to dropout, density variation, and sampling gaps in a way that curve-fitting pseudotimes are not.

```haskell
-- Diffusion pseudotime from the eigen-decomposition of the Markov matrix.
dptFromRoot :: [Double] -> [[Double]] -> Int -> [Double]
dptFromRoot lambdas psis root =
  let w k = (lambdas !! k / (1 - lambdas !! k)) ^ 2
      coord k i = sqrt (w k) * ((psis !! k) !! i - (psis !! k) !! root)
      ks = [0 .. length lambdas - 1]
      ncells = length (head psis)
  in [ sqrt (sum [ coord k i ^ 2 | k <- ks ]) | i <- [0 .. ncells - 1] ]
```

The price of this robustness is direction: DPT is intrinsically undirected, and choosing the wrong root mirrors the entire ordering. In practice the root is supplied by marker genes, experimental time labels, or -- closing the loop with the kinetic paradigm -- by RNA velocity itself [5].

### 4.4 Velocity-guided Markov chains: the measurable Waddington landscape

CellRank [5] and dynamo [6] turn velocity from a visualization aid into a quantitative landscape. CellRank builds a cell-by-cell transition matrix that blends velocity direction with expression similarity, then applies Generalized Perron Cluster Cluster Analysis (GPCCA) to find **macrostates** -- sets of cells the chain rarely leaves. Macrostates with large incoming flux and high self-transition probability are classified terminal; those with dominant outgoing flux are initial. For each cell and each terminal macrostate, CellRank solves a linear system for the *absorption probability*: the probability that a random walk starting at the cell is eventually absorbed in that terminal state. The result is a fate matrix with one row per cell and one column per terminal fate -- a probabilistic Waddington landscape in which every cell holds graded membership over possible futures rather than a single hard label [5].

dynamo takes the complementary continuum view [6]: it learns a smooth vector-field function $f(x)$ over expression space, by kernel or neural-network regression on velocity samples, such that $dx/dt = f(x)$ holds everywhere and not only at observed cells. From $f$, dynamo computes Jacobians at fixed points (stability analysis of attractors), extracts gene-regulatory interactions from the Jacobian's structure, and finds **least-action paths** between attractors -- the most probable transition trajectories, which are the mathematical realization of Waddington's valleys. Metabolic-labeling data (new versus total RNA) can replace splicing kinetics inside the same framework, yielding absolute rather than relative velocities [6].

> **Definition:** A *Waddington potential* for a learned vector field $f$ is a scalar function $U$ with $f \approx -\nabla U$; its minima are terminal fates, its saddles are bifurcation points, and least-action paths between minima follow valley floors [6].

## 5 Empirical Results

Three developmental systems anchor the empirical record.

1. **Pancreatic endocrinogenesis.** scVelo's dynamical model resolved the transient endocrine progenitor states of the developing mouse pancreas and recovered a shared latent time ordering progenitors before alpha, beta, delta, and epsilon cells [2]. CellRank on the same data automatically identified ductal and endocrine progenitors as initial states, computed fate probabilities toward each hormone-producing lineage, and recovered gene-expression trends along individual lineages -- including the delta-cell lineage, where splicing kinetics alone were ambiguous [5].
2. **Hippocampal neurogenesis.** On dentate gyrus data, scVelo disentangled the heterogeneous kinetics of granule-cell maturation and pinpointed fate-commitment stages with driver genes peaking at specific latent times [2].
3. **Human hematopoiesis with metabolic labeling.** Conventional splicing-based velocity on human hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells produced flows pointing backward, from mature megakaryocytes and erythrocytes toward progenitors -- a documented failure mode driven by intron mispriming and non-constant transcription rates [8]. Using metabolically labeled (new versus total) RNA within dynamo's kinetic framework recovered the correct forward flow from stem cells to mature lineages and, via differential geometry, revealed asymmetric regulation in the PU.1-GATA1 circuit and the early appearance of megakaryocytes [6].

A systematic benchmark of 45 trajectory-inference methods found that no single method dominates every topology, and that accuracy collapses when a user-supplied root or direction prior is wrong -- a central motivation for reading direction from the data itself via velocity [9].

## 6 Limitations

The honest assessment, following the field's own review [8]:

- **Identifiability.** The dynamical model cannot jointly identify absolute rates and absolute time; only ratios and relative latent times are estimable. Velocity magnitudes are therefore not comparable across genes or datasets.
- **Assumption violations.** Time-varying transcription rates, bursty transcription, and alternative splicing all break the constant-rate ODEs; the steady-state model additionally requires observing both induction and repression phases.
- **Measurement artifacts.** Intron mispriming in droplet protocols biases unspliced counts, and genes induced abruptly have too little unspliced signal to fit, producing reversed flows [6].
- **Projection distortion.** High-dimensional velocity vectors projected onto UMAP embeddings can disagree with the true direction, because UMAP does not preserve the local geometry the projection assumes; streamline plots should never be the sole evidence [8].
- **Root dependence of DPT.** Diffusion pseudotime remains undirected, so velocity-plus-DPT pipelines inherit velocity's failure modes whenever the root is chosen from corrupted velocities.
- **Timescale mismatch.** Velocity predicts hours ahead while differentiation unfolds over days; extrapolating a local derivative across a global trajectory is valid only where the vector field varies slowly.

## 7 Conclusion

Trajectory inference has matured from geometric curve-fitting to kinetic modeling. RNA velocity supplies the missing arrow of time by exploiting the unspliced-to-spliced ratio [1][2]; diffusion pseudotime supplies a noise-robust metric geometry for ordering and branching [3][4]; and velocity-guided Markov chains together with learned vector fields supply the quantitative Waddington landscape -- macrostates, fate probabilities, attractors, and least-action paths [5][6]. None of the components is trustworthy in isolation: velocities need manifold-aware aggregation, pseudotime needs direction, and landscapes need validation against perturbation or lineage tracing. The frontier is multimodal kinetics -- metabolic labeling, chromatin accessibility, and protein measurements feeding joint models -- and uncertainty quantification that reports when the arrow of time cannot be read [8]. The marble is real; the task now is to measure the hill.
