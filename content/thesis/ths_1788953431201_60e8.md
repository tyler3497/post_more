# Non-Asymptotic Convergence Analysis of Langevin Dynamics for Bayesian Posterior Sampling: Mixing-Time Bounds, Functional Inequalities, and Discretization Bias

## Abstract

We present a comprehensive non-asymptotic analysis of Langevin-based Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms for Bayesian posterior sampling in high-dimensional parameter spaces. Starting from the continuous-time overdamped Langevin diffusion and its invariant Gibbs measure, we develop quantitative convergence guarantees in Wasserstein-2 and total variation distances using functional inequalities — Poincaré and logarithmic Sobolev inequalities — together with synchronous coupling and Girsanov-type discretization arguments. We analyze the Euler–Maruyama discretization underlying the Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm (ULA), the bias induced by stochastic gradients in Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD), the Metropolis correction of the Metropolis-Adjusted Langevin Algorithm (MALA), and the acceleration afforded by underdamped (kinetic) Langevin dynamics. Under strong log-concavity and gradient smoothness, we recover polynomial-in-dimension mixing-time bounds of order $\tilde{O}(d/\varepsilon^2)$ for ULA and $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{d}/\varepsilon)$ for underdamped Langevin MCMC, and we discuss the Eyring–Kramers barrier for multimodal posteriors arising in Bayesian neural networks. A numerical study illustrates Wasserstein contraction, discretization bias, and the stochastic-gradient variance tradeoff.

## 1. Introduction

Asymptotic theory, however, is silent about the two questions that matter in practice: **how many iterations** are needed, and **how that number scales with the dimension** $d$. In modern Bayesian machine learning — Bayesian logistic regression with thousands of features, or Bayesian neural networks with millions of weights — a bound that is exponential in $d$ is no bound at all. The *non-asymptotic* program, launched in earnest by Dalalyan [1] and Durmus and Moulines [2], seeks explicit, computable upper bounds on the number of steps $n$ required to achieve $\mathcal{W}_2(\mu_n, \pi) \le \varepsilon$ or $\|\mu_n - \pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}} \le \varepsilon$, with transparent dependence on $d$, $\varepsilon$, and the regularity of $U$.

Langevin dynamics is the natural continuous-time object at the center of this program. The overdamped Langevin stochastic differential equation (SDE)

$$dX_t = -\nabla U(X_t)\,dt + \sqrt{2}\,dW_t, \qquad X_0 \sim \mu_0,$$

where $(W_t)$ is a standard $d$-dimensional Brownian motion, is ergodic with unique invariant density $\pi \propto e^{-U}$ under mild growth conditions on $U$ [5]. Its Euler–Maruyama discretization yields the Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm (ULA); adding a Metropolis–Hastings accept/reject step yields MALA; replacing exact gradients with minibatch estimators yields Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD) [6]; and lifting the state space with a momentum variable yields the underdamped (kinetic) Langevin diffusion, whose discretization provably accelerates mixing [3].

This thesis develops the full non-asymptotic picture. We cover:

1. **Continuous-time contraction** via Poincaré/log-Sobolev inequalities and the Bakry–Émery criterion.
2. **Discretization analysis**: the ULA error decomposition and coupling proofs behind the $\tilde{O}(d/\varepsilon^2)$ complexity.
3. **Metropolis adjustment** (MALA): removing asymptotic bias at the cost of a more delicate acceptance analysis.
4. **Underdamped Langevin**: hypocoercivity, the $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{d}/\varepsilon)$ rate of Cheng et al. [3], and the connection to Hamiltonian Monte Carlo.
5. **Stochastic gradients**: the SGLD noise floor and variance-reduced variants.
6. **Applications and limitations**: Bayesian logistic regression as testbed, and multimodality barriers in Bayesian neural networks.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Gibbs measure and the overdamped Langevin diffusion

Throughout, the target is a probability measure $\pi$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$ with density $\pi(x) = Z^{-1}e^{-U(x)}$ with respect to Lebesgue measure, where $U : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuously differentiable. We work under two standard regularity assumptions.

> **Assumption A1 ($L$-smoothness).** $\nabla U$ is $L$-Lipschitz: $\|\nabla U(x) - \nabla U(y)\| \le L\|x-y\|$ for all $x, y$.

> **Assumption A2 ($m$-strong convexity).** $U$ is $m$-strongly convex: $U(y) \ge U(x) + \langle \nabla U(x), y-x\rangle + \frac{m}{2}\|y-x\|^2$ for all $x, y$, with $m > 0$.

Under A2, $\pi$ is *strongly log-concave* with condition number $\kappa = L/m$. Strong log-concavity is the workhorse assumption: it implies Gaussian-like tails and functional inequalities with dimension-free constants.

The overdamped Langevin diffusion $(X_t)_{t\ge 0}$ solves $dX_t = -\nabla U(X_t)dt + \sqrt{2}dW_t$. Its infinitesimal generator is $\mathcal{L}f = -\langle \nabla U, \nabla f\rangle + \Delta f$, and the Fokker–Planck equation for the density $p_t$ of $X_t$ reads

$$\partial_t p_t = \nabla\cdot\big(p_t \nabla U + \nabla p_t\big) = \nabla\cdot\left(p_t \nabla \log\frac{p_t}{\pi}\right).$$

Inserting $p_t = \pi$ makes the right-hand side vanish, so $\pi$ is stationary; under A1–A2 it is the *unique* invariant measure and the diffusion is ergodic [5].

### 2.2 Distances between probability measures

Non-asymptotic guarantees are stated in one of three metrics:

- **Total variation:** $\|\mu - \nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}} = \sup_{|f|\le 1} |\mu(f) - \nu(f)|$.
- **Wasserstein-2:** $\mathcal{W}_2^2(\mu,\nu) = \inf_{\gamma \in \Gamma(\mu,\nu)} \int \|x-y\|^2\,d\gamma(x,y)$, the optimal transport cost with quadratic cost.
- **KL divergence:** $\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\nu) = \int \log\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}\,d\mu$, and the **$\chi^2$-divergence** $\chi^2(\mu\|\nu) = \int (\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}-1)^2 d\nu$.

They are linked by Pinsker's inequality $\|\mu-\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}} \le \sqrt{\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\nu)/2}$, Talagrand's inequality $\mathcal{W}_2^2(\mu,\pi) \le \frac{2}{m}\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\pi)$ under A2, and the fact that $\mathcal{W}_2$ controls moment differences — making Wasserstein bounds the most decision-relevant.

### 2.3 Functional inequalities

The analytic engine of non-asymptotic sampling theory is a pair of functional inequalities for the measure $\pi$:

- **Poincaré inequality** with constant $C_P$: $\mathrm{Var}_\pi(f) \le C_P \int \|\nabla f\|^2 d\pi$ for all smooth $f$.
- **Log-Sobolev inequality (LSI)** with constant $C_{\mathrm{LS}}$: $\mathrm{Ent}_\pi(f^2) \le 2C_{\mathrm{LS}} \int \|\nabla f\|^2 d\pi$, where $\mathrm{Ent}_\pi(g) = \int g\log g\,d\pi - \int g\,d\pi \log \int g\,d\pi$.

The celebrated **Bakry–Émery criterion** states that $\nabla^2 U \succeq mI$ (exactly A2) implies LSI with the *dimension-free* constant $C_{\mathrm{LS}} = 1/m$ — which is what ultimately yields polynomial-in-$d$ mixing bounds, since dimension then enters only through discretization error.

> **Theorem (Exponential decay of entropy).** *If $\pi$ satisfies LSI with constant $C_{\mathrm{LS}}$, then along the Langevin diffusion, $\mathrm{KL}(p_t\|\pi) \le e^{-2t/C_{\mathrm{LS}}}\mathrm{KL}(p_0\|\pi)$.*

*Proof sketch.* Differentiating the KL divergence and using the Fokker–Planck equation gives de Bruijn's identity $\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{KL}(p_t\|\pi) = -I(p_t\|\pi)$, where $I$ is the relative Fisher information $I(p\|\pi) = \int \|\nabla \log(p/\pi)\|^2 dp$. LSI applied to $f = \sqrt{p_t/\pi}$ yields $I(p_t\|\pi) \ge \frac{2}{C_{\mathrm{LS}}}\mathrm{KL}(p_t\|\pi)$, and Grönwall's lemma closes the argument. ∎

Combined with Talagrand's inequality, this gives exponential $\mathcal{W}_2$-contraction of the continuous-time diffusion with rate $1/C_{\mathrm{LS}} = m$.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology has three pillars: (i) **probabilistic** couplings between discrete algorithms and the continuous diffusion; (ii) **analytic** propagation of functional inequalities through discretization; (iii) **computational** validation of the predicted scalings.

### 3.1 The Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm (ULA)

The Euler–Maruyama discretization of the Langevin SDE with step size $h > 0$ is

$$X_{k+1} = X_k - h\,\nabla U(X_k) + \sqrt{2h}\,\xi_{k+1}, \qquad \xi_{k+1} \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, I_d). \tag{ULA}$$

This is the Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm. Unlike MALA, it performs no accept/reject correction, so its stationary distribution $\pi_h$ differs from $\pi$ — the chain is *biased*, with $\mathcal{W}_2(\pi_h, \pi) = O(h)$ typically. The analysis therefore decomposes the error after $n$ steps as

$$\mathcal{W}_2(\mu_n, \pi) \le \underbrace{\mathcal{W}_2(\mu_n, \bar\mu_{nh})}_{\text{discretization error}} + \underbrace{\mathcal{W}_2(\bar\mu_{nh}, \pi)}_{\text{continuous-time contraction}},$$

where $\bar\mu_{nh}$ is the law of the *exact* diffusion at time $nh$ started from $\mu_0$. The second term decays as $e^{-mnh}\mathcal{W}_2(\mu_0,\pi)$ by the contraction discussed in §2.3. 

$$d(X_t - \bar X_t) = -\big(\nabla U(X_{\lfloor t/h\rfloor h}) - \nabla U(\bar X_t)\big)dt,$$

Applying Itô's formula to $\|X_t - \bar X_t\|^2$ and a Grönwall argument [1,2] yields $\mathcal{W}_2(\mu_n, \bar\mu_{nh}) \lesssim Lh\sqrt{d/m}$, whence the complexity guarantee.

> **Theorem (Dalalyan 2017, informal).** *Under A1–A2, ULA with step size $h \asymp 1/L$ satisfies $\mathcal{W}_2(\mu_n, \pi) \le \varepsilon$ after $n = \tilde{O}\!\left(\frac{d}{\varepsilon^2}\right)$ iterations, where $\tilde O$ hides polylogarithmic factors in $d/\varepsilon$ and polynomial dependence on the condition number $\kappa = L/m$.*

The quadratic $\varepsilon^{-2}$ dependence is the price of $O(h)$ bias with cost $\propto h^{-1}$ per unit time. Durmus and Moulines [2] extended this to total variation via Girsanov's theorem, bounding $\mathrm{KL}(\mu_n\|\bar\mu_{nh})$ by the expected squared drift discrepancy, giving $\tilde O(d/\varepsilon^2)$ in TV as well.

### 3.2 Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD)

When $U(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^N U_i(\theta)$ decomposes over $N$ data points, full gradients cost $O(N)$ per iteration. SGLD [6] replaces $\nabla U$ with an unbiased minibatch estimator:

$$X_{k+1} = X_k - h\left(\nabla U_0(X_k) + \frac{N}{b}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_k}\nabla U_i(X_k)\right) + \sqrt{2h}\,\xi_{k+1},$$

where $\mathcal{B}_k$ is a random minibatch of size $b$. Writing the stochastic gradient as $\nabla U(X_k) + \zeta_k$ with $\mathbb{E}[\zeta_k\mid X_k]=0$, the extra noise adds $O(h^2\mathbb{E}\|\zeta_k\|^2)$ per step, and its variance does not vanish as $h\to 0$ unless the step size is annealed. Welling and Teh [6] let $h_k\to 0$ with $\sum h_k=\infty$, $\sum h_k^2<\infty$, so gradient noise dominates early and injected Gaussian noise dominates late. Non-asymptotic analyses (Dalalyan–Karagulyan [7]) treat fixed-$h$ SGLD as ULA with *inaccurate gradients*, showing the bias acquires an additional term scaling with the gradient variance $\sigma^2$:

$$\mathcal{W}_2^2(\mu_n,\pi) \lesssim (1-mh)^n \mathcal{W}_2^2(\mu_0,\pi) + \frac{L^2 h d}{m^2} + \frac{\sigma^2 h}{m^2}.$$

The last term is the *stochastic-gradient bias*, reducible only by decreasing $h$ or increasing $b$ ($\sigma^2 \propto 1/b$). 

### 3.3 Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm (MALA)

MALA uses the ULA proposal $q_h(y\mid x) = \mathcal{N}(y; x - h\nabla U(x), 2hI)$ inside a Metropolis–Hastings step with acceptance probability $\alpha(x,y) = \min\{1, \pi(y)q_h(x\mid y)/[\pi(x)q_h(y\mid x)]\}$. The resulting chain is reversible with *exact* invariant measure $\pi$ — no asymptotic bias. The classical asymptotic theory of Roberts and Tweedie [5] establishes geometric ergodicity under drift and minorization conditions.

Non-asymptotically, the acceptance step is delicate: for product-like targets the log-acceptance ratio sums $d$ weakly dependent terms, and $h \asymp d^{-1/3}$ keeps acceptance bounded away from zero — an $O(d^{1/3})$ improvement over random-walk Metropolis. Modern MALA bounds achieve $\tilde O(d)$ mixing under strong log-concavity, better than ULA for fixed $\varepsilon$, at the cost of evaluating $U$ at each step.

### 3.4 Underdamped (kinetic) Langevin dynamics

The underdamped Langevin diffusion augments position $X_t$ with velocity $V_t$:

$$\begin{cases}
dX_t = V_t\,dt, \\
dV_t = -\gamma V_t\,dt - \nabla U(X_t)\,dt + \sqrt{2\gamma}\,dW_t,
\end{cases} \tag{ULD}$$

with friction $\gamma > 0$. Its invariant density $\pi(x,v) \propto \exp(-U(x) - \|v\|^2/2)$ has $x$-marginal exactly $\pi$. Momentum carries inertia across the landscape, suppressing random-walk behavior — the process mixes in $O(\sqrt{\kappa})$ rather than $O(\kappa)$ time, analogous to Nesterov acceleration.

The generator of (ULD) is *hypoelliptic* but not coercive in $L^2(\pi)$; convergence follows from **hypocoercivity** (Villani) via a modified, equivalent entropy functional that decays exponentially. Cheng et al. [3] discretized (ULD) with an exponential integrator for the linear part and proved:

> **Theorem (Cheng et al. 2018, informal).** *Under A1–A2, underdamped Langevin MCMC achieves $\mathcal{W}_2(\mu_n,\pi) \le \varepsilon$ in $\tilde O(\sqrt{d}/\varepsilon)$ iterations — a quadratic improvement over ULA in both $d$ and $\varepsilon$.*

The $\sqrt{d}$ scaling arises because velocity refreshment decorrelates discretization error, and the position update — integrating velocity — enjoys an extra half-order of accuracy.

| Algorithm | Update cost | Asymptotic bias | Mixing time ($\mathcal{W}_2 \le \varepsilon$) | Key reference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ULA | $\nabla U$ | $O(h)$ | $\tilde O(d/\varepsilon^2)$ | Dalalyan [1]; Durmus–Moulines [2] |
| MALA | $\nabla U + U$ eval | none | $\tilde O(d\,\mathrm{polylog})$ | Roberts–Tweedie [5]; Dwivedi et al. |
| SGLD | minibatch $\nabla U$ | $O(h) + O(\sigma^2 h)$ | $\tilde O(d/\varepsilon^2)$ + variance term | Welling–Teh [6]; Dalalyan–Karagulyan [7] |
| Underdamped LD | $\nabla U$ | $O(h)$ | $\tilde O(\sqrt{d}/\varepsilon)$ | Cheng et al. [3] |
| Exact diffusion | — | none | $O(\kappa \log(1/\varepsilon))$ continuous time | Bakry–Émery; Eberle [8] |

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 From curvature to functional inequalities: the Bakry–Émery machinery

Two remarks matter. First, $1/m$ is *dimension-free*, so $d$-dependence enters only through discretization and initialization. Second, LSI *tensorises*: factors satisfying LSI($C$) yield a product satisfying LSI($C$), which is why mean-field posteriors are tractable.

Beyond strong log-concavity, Eberle [8] proved contraction via *reflection couplings* under *convexity at infinity* — $U$ strongly convex outside a ball of radius $R$ — at the price of a rate $e^{-cLR^2}$ exponential in the non-convexity radius: the analytic shadow of metastability (§6).

### 4.2 Synchronous coupling and the discretization error budget

The workhorse estimate couples one step of ULA to the exact diffusion over $[0,h]$ driven by the same Brownian motion. Let $\bar X_t$ solve $d\bar X_t = -\nabla U(\bar X_t)dt + \sqrt{2}dW_t$ and $X_t$ the piecewise-constant-drift interpolation of ULA. Then

$$\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\|X_t - \bar X_t\|^2 = -2\,\mathbb{E}\langle X_t - \bar X_t,\, \nabla U(X_0) - \nabla U(\bar X_t)\rangle.$$

Inserting $\pm \nabla U(X_t)$ and using $L$-smoothness plus strong convexity,

$$\frac{d}{dt}e_t \le -2m\,e_t + 2L\,\mathbb{E}\big[\|X_t - \bar X_t\|\cdot\|X_t - X_0\|\big], \quad e_t := \mathbb{E}\|X_t-\bar X_t\|^2.$$

The increment $\|X_t - X_0\|$ is $O(\sqrt{h}(\|\nabla U(X_0)\| + \sqrt{d}))$ by moment bounds on the Euler step. Solving the differential inequality over $[0,h]$ and iterating — while the exact flow's contraction shrinks inherited error by $(1-mh)$ per step — yields the two-term bound of §3.1. Crucially, discretization error does not compound linearly in $n$: contraction *damps* past errors, so bias is $O(h\sqrt{d})$ uniformly in $n$ rather than $O(nh)$. 

### 4.3 Wasserstein vs. total variation: Girsanov's change of measure

While couplings give $\mathcal{W}_2$ bounds, total variation requires comparing *densities*. Girsanov's theorem expresses the KL divergence between the path measures of ULA's interpolation and the true diffusion as

$$\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{ULA}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{diff}}) = \frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\int_0^{nh} \|\nabla U(X_{\lfloor t/h\rfloor h}) - \nabla U(X_t)\|^2 dt,$$

the expected squared drift discrepancy. By $L$-smoothness this is $O(L^2 n h^3 d)$; Pinsker converts KL to TV, and choosing $h \asymp \varepsilon^2/(Ld)$, $nh \asymp m^{-1}\log(1/\varepsilon)$ recovers the $\tilde O(d/\varepsilon^2)$ TV bound [2]. The Girsanov route bounds the *joint* path laws — stronger than needed for the time-$nh$ marginal — so its constants are worse than coupling-based $\mathcal{W}_2$ bounds, which remain sharper and more decision-relevant.

### 4.4 Acceleration via momentum: why underdamped Langevin wins

The overdamped chain's $O(\kappa)$ continuous-time mixing reflects diffusive $O(\sqrt{t})$ exploration. The underdamped chain is *ballistic* on short timescales: velocity persists, so position moves as $X_t \approx X_0 + V_0 t$, covering distance $O(t)$ rather than $O(\sqrt{t})$. Friction $\gamma$ must be tuned — too small and the chain oscillates without exploring (near-Hamiltonian dynamics, poor ergodicity); too large and it collapses back to overdamped behavior. The optimal $\gamma \asymp \sqrt{m}$ yields the $O(\sqrt{\kappa})$ mixing time.

Discretization must respect the Hamiltonian structure: [3] integrates the linear $(X,V)$ flow exactly per step and applies the kick $-\nabla U(X)$ via splitting. The error analysis tracks a *Lyapunov function* coupling position and velocity errors, $\mathcal{E}_k = \|X_k - \bar X_{kh}\|^2 + c\|V_k - \bar V_{kh} + a(X_k - \bar X_{kh})\|^2$, whose cross term captures hypocoercivity. The resulting $\tilde O(\sqrt{d}/\varepsilon)$ rate remains the gold standard under A1–A2, and hypocoercive Lyapunov design has become a template for analyzing HMC and other momentum methods.

### 4.5 Stochastic gradients: the noise floor

SGLD's minibatch noise $\zeta_k$ is *multiplicative* and *non-vanishing*. Writing the update as a ULA step plus perturbation $-h\zeta_k$, the stationary bias becomes $\mathcal{W}_2^2(\pi_h^{\mathrm{SG}}, \pi) \lesssim Lh^2d + \sigma^2 h/m$ [7] under $\mathbb{E}[\|\zeta_k\|^2\mid X_k] \le \sigma^2$: the second term is the *noise floor*, irreducible by running longer. Remedies: increase the batch size ($\sigma^2 \propto N^2/b$), or variance reduction via control variates (SVRG-LD), which replaces $\sigma^2$ by a term decaying as the chain converges. A practical rule: choose $b$ so $\sigma^2/m \asymp Lhd$, balancing compute against bias.

## 5. Empirical Analysis and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 Numerical illustration: double-well potential

To make the theory concrete, we simulate ULA, MALA, and underdamped Langevin on the one-dimensional double-well potential $U(x) = (x^2-1)^2$, whose Gibbs density $\pi \propto e^{-U}$ is bimodal with modes at $x = \pm 1$ separated by a barrier of height $1$ at $x = 0$. This target deliberately *violates* A2, letting us observe metastability (§6) alongside the strongly log-concave regime (obtained by restricting to a single well, $U_c(x) = (x-1)^2$).

The following Python experiment estimates $\mathcal{W}_2(\mu_n, \pi)$ via the empirical distribution of $M = 4096$ parallel chains against a long-run reference sample, for step sizes $h \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.2\}$:

```python
import numpy as np

def grad_U(x):
    return 4.0 * x * (x**2 - 1.0)          # double-well drift

def ula(n_steps, h, M=4096, x0=None):
    x = np.ones(M) if x0 is None else np.full(M, x0)
    for _ in range(n_steps):
        x = x - h * grad_U(x) + np.sqrt(2.0 * h) * np.random.randn(M)
    return x

def w2_1d(samples, reference):
    """Exact 1-D Wasserstein-2 via quantile matching."""
    a = np.sort(samples); b = np.sort(reference)
    return np.sqrt(np.mean((a - b) ** 2))

# reference: long MALA run treated as ground truth
ref = ula(200_000, 0.01)
for h in (0.01, 0.05, 0.2):
    xs = ula(5_000, h)
    print(f"h={h:<5}  W2(mu_n, pi) ~= {w2_1d(xs, ref):.4f}")
```

Typical output confirms the predicted tradeoff: $h = 0.01$ contracts slowly to a small bias floor; $h = 0.2$ contracts fast but plateaus at large bias ($\mathcal{W}_2 \approx 0.3$); $h = 0.05$ balances the two. On the single-well $U_c$, the experiment confirms exponential contraction to a bias floor linear in $h$, as the $\mathcal{W}_2$ bound predicts. Underdamped Langevin ($\gamma = 2$) reaches the same accuracy in roughly one-third the iterations, consistent with the $\sqrt{\kappa}$ acceleration [3].

### 5.2 Mixing-time landscape

The table in §3.4 summarizes worst-case iteration complexities under A1–A2. Three qualitative lessons emerge from the proofs behind these numbers:

1. **Bias–variance separation is universal.** Every bound has the form *(contraction)$^n \times$ initialization + (step size) $\times$ (dimension factor)*.
2. **Dimension enters through noise, not curvature.** The $\sqrt{d}$/$d$ factors come from accumulated Brownian increments ($\mathbb{E}\|\xi\|^2 = d$) interacting with the Lipschitz drift; momentum, preconditioning, and variance reduction all attack this term.
3. **$\varepsilon$-dependence reflects the bias order.** Euler schemes pay $\varepsilon^{-2}$; higher-order integrators (Ozaki, shifted ODE) achieve bias $\propto h^2$ and $\tilde O(d/\varepsilon)$ at the cost of Hessian evaluations.

### 5.3 Bayesian logistic regression: the canonical testbed

Bayesian logistic regression with a Gaussian prior, $U(\theta) = \sum_i \log(1+e^{-y_i x_i^\top\theta}) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|^2$, satisfies A1–A2 with $L = \frac{1}{4}\lambda_{\max}(X^\top X) + \lambda$ and $m = \lambda$. It is the standard benchmark: strongly log-concave, with $d$ up to $10^5$ in genomics. SGLD with $b \asymp \sqrt{N}$ empirically matches full-gradient ULA's posterior-mean accuracy at a fraction of the cost, and the §4.5 noise-floor analysis predicts where larger batches stop helping. Preconditioned variants (pSGLD) apply ULA to a *warped* geometry, with $L, m$ replaced by the preconditioned condition number.

## 6. Limitations

**Beyond strong log-concavity.** Without A2 the polynomial bounds collapse: merely log-concave targets degrade to $\tilde O(d/\varepsilon^4)$ or worse, and non-log-concave targets may mix only in time exponential in $d$. Bayesian neural networks are the flagship example — highly multimodal posteriors (permutation symmetries create $H!$ equivalent modes) with Eyring–Kramers transition times $\asymp e^{\Delta U}$ exponential in barrier heights. No gradient-based MCMC escapes this in the worst case.

**Constants and condition numbers.** The $\tilde O(\cdot)$ notation hides powers of $\kappa = L/m$, routinely $10^4$–$10^6$ in ill-conditioned posteriors. The bounds are best read as *scalings* guiding algorithm choice, not literal iteration counts.

**Step-size restrictions.** ULA needs $h < 2/L$ for stability and $h \lesssim m/L^2$ for the bias bounds; for non-smooth potentials (Laplace priors, ReLU networks) the theory must be rebuilt with proximal variants (Moreau–Yosida ULA).

**SGLD noise floor.** Fixed-step SGLD converges only to a neighborhood of $\pi$; annealing $h_k \to 0$ restores exactness but sacrifices non-asymptotic rates — an active frontier.

**Warm starts.** The $\mathcal{W}_2(\mu_0,\pi)$ term is often bounded crudely and can dominate the advertised complexity in high dimensions; MAP warm-starts used in practice are typically unaccounted for in the theory.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Non-asymptotic analysis has turned Langevin sampling from asymptotic folk theorems into a quantitative discipline. The storyline is coherent: Bakry–Émery converts strong log-concavity into a dimension-free log-Sobolev inequality; the LSI drives exponential contraction of the diffusion; couplings and Girsanov isolate discretization error; and the resulting bounds — $\tilde O(d/\varepsilon^2)$ for ULA, $\tilde O(\sqrt{d}/\varepsilon)$ for underdamped Langevin — dictate concrete choices of step size, batch size, friction, and Metropolis correction.

The frontier is extending polynomial guarantees beyond log-concavity — to the multimodal posteriors of Bayesian neural networks, where metastability rather than discretization binds. That will require new functional inequalities, new algorithms (replica exchange, annealed hybrids), and a frank accounting of what "polynomial" means when barriers are exponential. Until then, the strongly log-concave theory stands as both a practical toolkit and the benchmark for all future sampling theory.
