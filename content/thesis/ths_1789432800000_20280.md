# Tropical Degenerations and Correspondence: Piecewise-Linear Methods in the Enumerative Geometry of Rational Curves

## Abstract

Tropical geometry replaces algebraic varieties over valued fields with piecewise-linear combinatorial skeletons, and the surprising discovery of the past two decades is how much enumerative information survives this drastic degeneration. This article develops the theory of planar tropical curves and its applications to classical curve counting: from the max-plus semifield, amoebas, and the balancing condition, through Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem, which identifies the genus-zero Gromov–Witten invariants of the complex projective plane with weighted counts of trivalent tropical graphs through generic point configurations. We explain the combinatorial engines that make the theory effective — lattice path algorithms, floor diagrams, and relative tropical invariants — and show how they recover Kontsevich's recursion, the Caporaso–Harris formula, node polynomials, and the logarithmic asymptotics of Welschinger invariants. A section of worked enumerations verifies the classical numbers tropically, and we close by mapping the boundary of the method: higher genus, descendant invariants, and the non-toric frontier.

##1 Introduction

Enumerative geometry asks a deceptively simple question: how many geometric objects of a given type satisfy a prescribed set of incidence conditions? For plane curves the flagship numbers are the **Gromov–Witten invariants** of the complex projective plane — for instance, the twelve rational cubics through eight general points. Classical approaches compute these numbers through intersection theory on moduli spaces of stable maps, a machinery of formidable technical weight [1]. Tropical geometry offers a radically different route: degenerate the algebraic curve to a piecewise-linear graph, count the graphs with suitable integer multiplicities, and recover the classical answer exactly.

The degeneration is implemented by a *valuation*. Given an algebraic curve defined over a field with a non-Archimedean valuation, such as the field of Puiseux series, the image of the curve under the coordinatewise valuation is a subset of real Euclidean space — its **tropicalization** — which is a finite polyhedral complex carrying the essential discrete invariants of the original curve: dimension, degree, and (generically) genus [8]. The miracle, proved by Mikhalkin, is that the count of tropical curves through a generic configuration of points, each weighted by an explicit lattice multiplicity, equals the classical count [1], [2]. The present article is a self-contained tour of this correspondence and the combinatorial technology it spawned.

What makes the tropical approach more than a curiosity is *computability*. Tropical enumerative problems reduce to finite combinatorics: counting lattice paths in a Newton polytope, enumerating floor diagrams, or intersecting cycles on a tropical moduli space. These techniques have produced new proofs of Kontsevich's recursion [9], a tropical derivation of the Caporaso–Harris formula [4], an effective theory of tropical descendant invariants [3], [5], and deep results on real enumerative geometry through Welschinger invariants [2], [6].

##2 Background

### The tropical semifield

Tropical geometry is algebraic geometry over the **tropical semifield** $\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}$, equipped with the operations

- tropical addition $a \oplus b = \max(a, b)$,
- tropical multiplication $a \odot b = a + b$.

The element $-\infty$ is the additive identity, and $0$ is the multiplicative identity. A *tropical polynomial* in $n$ variables is a formal expression

$$f(x) = \bigoplus_i (a_i \odot x_1^{\odot i_1} \odot \cdots \odot x_n^{\odot i_n}) = \max_i \left( a_i + \langle i, x \rangle \right),$$

where the exponents $i = (i_1, \dots, i_n)$ range over a finite subset of $\mathbb{Z}^n$ and the coefficients $a_i$ lie in $\mathbb{T}$ [2]. The *tropical hypersurface* defined by $f$ is the **corner locus**: the set of points $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ where the maximum is attained at least twice, i.e. where $f$ fails to be locally linear. In one variable this gives the familiar picture: a tropical polynomial is a convex piecewise-linear function, and its tropical roots are precisely the points where the slope jumps.

```haskell
-- Max-plus (tropical) arithmetic on the extended reals
type Tropical = Double   -- the value -Infinity plays the role of the tropical zero

tadd :: Tropical -> Tropical -> Tropical
tadd = max

tmul :: Tropical -> Tropical -> Tropical
tmul = (+)

-- Evaluate a tropical polynomial, given as (exponent, coefficient) pairs
teval :: [(Int, Tropical)] -> Tropical -> Tropical
teval terms x =
  foldr tadd (-1/0) [ a `tmul` (fromIntegral e `tmul` x) | (e, a) <- terms ]
```

### Tropical curves and the balancing condition

A **parametrized tropical curve** in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a continuous map $h: \Gamma \to \mathbb{R}^n$ from a metric graph $\Gamma$ (with unbounded ends allowed) such that every edge is mapped affinely with integer slope, and the following **balancing condition** holds at every vertex $v$:

> **Theorem (Balancing).** Let $E(v)$ be the set of edges incident to a vertex $v$ of a tropical curve, with integer direction vectors $u_e \in \mathbb{Z}^n$ pointing outward and positive integer edge weights $w_e$. Then $\sum_{e \in E(v)} w_e \, u_e = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}^n$.

The balancing condition is the tropical shadow of the classical fact that the sum of the orders of vanishing of a rational function on a complete curve is zero [2], [8]. It is the single combinatorial axiom that makes tropical intersection theory behave like algebraic intersection theory.

A tropical curve in the plane has a well-defined **degree** and **genus**:

- *Genus.* The genus of a tropical curve is the first Betti number of the underlying graph $\Gamma$. Remarkably, for a sufficiently general classical curve over a valued field, the genus of the curve equals the genus of its tropicalization [8].
- *Degree.* A planar tropical curve has degree $d$ when it has $3d$ unbounded ends whose direction vectors are $d$ copies each of $(-1,0)$, $(0,-1)$, and $(1,1)$. These are exactly the directions dictated by the degree-$d$ Newton triangle.

For enumerative purposes one works with **trivalent** (3-valent) tropical curves, for which every vertex multiplicity is computed from a lattice determinant: if the three outgoing primitive direction vectors at a vertex $v$ are $u_1, u_2, u_3$, the **vertex multiplicity** is $m(v) = |\det(u_1, u_2)|$, equal to twice the Euclidean area of the dual triangle in the Newton subdivision. The multiplicity of the whole curve is the product $m(h) = \prod_v m(v)$ over all vertices [1].

##3 Methodology

### From amoebas to tropical limits

The geometric bridge between classical and tropical geometry is the **amoeba**. For an algebraic curve $C \subset (\mathbb{C}^*)^2$ and a parameter $t > 1$, the scaled logarithmic map

$$\mathrm{Log}_t(z_1, z_2) = \left( \frac{\log|z_1|}{\log t}, \; \frac{\log|z_2|}{\log t} \right)$$

sends $C$ to its amoeba $\mathcal{A}_t(C) \subset \mathbb{R}^2$, a semi-algebraic set with tentacles stretching to infinity. Mikhalkin's fundamental theorem on amoebas states that as $t \to \infty$, these amoebas converge in the Hausdorff metric to a piecewise-linear spine — and that spine is precisely a tropical curve [1], [2]. The integer weights on the tropical edges record how many tentacles of the amoeba collapse onto each spine edge.

### The moduli space and the correspondence theorem

Fix a degree $d$ and genus $g$. The space of parametrized tropical curves of that degree and genus through a fixed configuration $\mathcal{X}$ of points in the tropical projective plane $\mathbb{TP}^2$ forms a polyhedral complex — a tropical analogue of the moduli space $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(\mathbb{CP}^2, d)$ of stable maps [2], [3]. Intersecting the evaluation cycles in this tropical moduli space yields a weighted count

$$N_{g,d}^{\mathbb{T}} = \sum_{h} m(h),$$

summed over all tropical curves $h$ of degree $d$ and genus $g$ passing through $\mathcal{X}$, where $m(h)$ is the lattice multiplicity defined above. Mikhalkin's **correspondence theorem** asserts:

> **Theorem (Mikhalkin correspondence).** For every $g$ and $d$, the classical count of complex curves of degree $d$ and genus $g$ through a generic configuration of points equals the tropical count: $N_{g,d}^{\mathbb{C}} = N_{g,d}^{\mathbb{T}}$ [1].

The proof proceeds by degenerating the point configuration: choose the points in very general position, tropicalize the classical curves through them, and show that the tropicalizations are exactly the contributing tropical curves, with the classical intersection multiplicity on the moduli space factorizing as the product of the tropical vertex multiplicities. A version of the theorem holds for arbitrary smooth toric surfaces, with the Newton polygon of the surface dictating the allowed directions of unbounded edges [1], [2].

The method is not confined to the complex numbers. For real enumerative geometry, the same tropical curves are counted with a *different* multiplicity — a sign-weighted product reflecting Welschinger's real invariants — and one recovers the count of real rational curves through real points, including the logarithmic asymptotics established by Itenberg, Kharlamov, and Shustin [2].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Lattice path enumeration

The correspondence theorem becomes an effective algorithm once the point configuration is *stretched*. Place the $3d - 1 + g$ marked points with rapidly increasing vertical separation. Mikhalkin showed that the tropical curves through such a stretched configuration are in bijection with certain **lattice paths** in the Newton triangle $\Delta_d = \mathrm{conv}\{(0,0), (d,0), (0,d)\}$ [1].

The algorithm works as follows:

1. Fix a linear order on the stretched points $x_1, \dots, x_{3d-1+g}$.
2. Each contributing tropical curve determines a lattice path $\gamma$ from $(0,d)$ to $(d,0)$ inside $\Delta_d$, monotone in a suitable sense, whose steps encode the positions of the vertices.
3. The multiplicity $m(h)$ of the curve factorizes as a product of contributions attached to the steps of $\gamma$, each computable from the local lattice geometry.

Summing the weighted paths reproduces the Gromov–Witten numbers. Gathmann and Markwig extended this picture to the relative setting, deriving the Caporaso–Harris recursion from tropical relative invariants and lattice path combinatorics [4]. The lattice path viewpoint turns an intersection-theoretic computation into a finite search that a computer can execute directly.

### 4.2 Floor diagrams

A more refined combinatorial gadget was introduced by Brugallé and Mikhalkin: the **floor diagram** [6], [7]. When the points are stretched vertically, each tropical curve decomposes into horizontal strips ("floors") connected by vertical "elevators". Collapsing each floor to a vertex and each elevator to a directed weighted edge yields a weighted oriented graph — the floor diagram — which encodes the curve uniquely.

Key results in this framework include:

- *Labeled floor diagrams* (vertices labeled $1, \dots, d$ compatibly with the orientation) of genus zero are in bijection with labeled trees, hence counted by Cayley's formula; the Kontsevich numbers emerge as explicit statistics on trees [6].
- The **node polynomials** of Göttsche's conjecture — the statement that for fixed cogenus $\delta$, the number of $\delta$-nodal plane curves of degree $d$ through the appropriate number of points is a polynomial in $d$ for $d$ sufficiently large — are proved and rendered computable via floor diagrams [6].
- Brugallé gave a purely algebro-geometric proof of the floor diagram correspondence by degenerating the projective plane to a chain of rational ruled surfaces, showing that the tropical combinatorics is the shadow of a classical degeneration [7].

### 4.3 Relative invariants and the Caporaso–Harris formula

Classically, Caporaso and Harris computed the numbers $N(d, g)$ of complex plane curves of degree $d$ and genus $g$ through $3d + g - 1$ general points by a recursion involving *relative* Gromov–Witten invariants — counts of curves with prescribed tangency to a fixed line [4]. Gathmann and Markwig rebuilt this theory tropically: they defined tropical relative invariants, showed they satisfy the same recursion, and re-derived the Caporaso–Harris formula entirely through lattice path combinatorics [4]. Fomin and Mikhalkin pushed the floor diagram technology into the relative setting as well, enumerating rational curves of given degree with maximal tangency to a line through a collection of points [6]. The agreement of the tropical and classical recursions is itself a strong consistency check on the correspondence.

### 4.4 Descendant invariants and Psi-conditions

Beyond point insertions, Gromov–Witten theory studies **descendant invariants**: intersection numbers involving the Psi-classes $\psi_i$, the first Chern classes of the cotangent line bundles at the marked points on the moduli space. Markwig and Rau constructed tropical Psi-classes on the tropical moduli space $\mathcal{M}_{0,n}(\mathbb{R}^2, d)$, proved a tropical WDVV equation, and showed that the resulting tropical descendant numbers equal their classical counterparts [3]. Their work includes a generalization of Mikhalkin's lattice path algorithm that counts rational plane tropical curves subject to both Psi-conditions and evaluation conditions. Blomme extended this program to invariants with **line constraints**, which are of particular interest because they appear as coefficients of the $J$-function in mirror symmetry; his recursive tropical formulas handle one Psi-condition of arbitrary power paired with a line, and the case of two lines each paired with a first-power Psi-condition [5].

##5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### Worked enumeration: rational cubics

The smallest nontrivial test of the correspondence is the count of rational plane cubics through eight points. Mikhalkin exhibits the full tropical picture: for one generic configuration there are nine rational tropical cubics, eight of multiplicity $1$ and one of multiplicity $4$ (carrying an edge of weight $2$, drawn bold), for a total weighted count of $12$; for another configuration there are ten curves, nine of multiplicity $1$ and one of multiplicity $3$ — again totaling $12$ [1]. The number $12$ is invariant, and it is exactly the classical number of rational cubics through eight general points. No other multiplicity partition of $12$ can occur.

![rational tropical cubics through eight marked points](ths_1789432800000_20280-1.webp)

### The Kontsevich numbers, tropically

Let $N_d$ denote the number of rational plane curves of degree $d$ through $3d - 1$ general points. The classical values, computed by Kontsevich's recursion, are:

| $d$ | $N_d$ | Tropical interpretation |
|-----|--------|--------------------------|
| 1 | 1 | single line through 2 points |
| 2 | 1 | single conic through 5 points |
| 3 | 12 | nine/ten tropical cubics, weighted as above [1] |
| 4 | 620 | weighted lattice paths in $\Delta_4$ [1], [4] |
| 5 | 87304 | floor diagram enumeration [6] |
| 6 | 26312976 | floor diagram enumeration [6] |

The recursion itself admits a tropical proof. Gathmann and Markwig defined tropical stable maps and the tropical moduli space with the "correct" compactification behavior even for degenerate curves, then intersected the boundary divisors on $\mathcal{M}_{0,n}(\mathbb{R}^2, d)$ exactly as in Kontsevich's classical argument; the resulting identity is precisely the recursion

$$N_d = \sum_{\substack{d_1 + d_2 = d \\ d_1, d_2 \geq 1}} \left( d_1^2 d_2^2 \binom{3d-4}{3d_1-2} - d_1^3 d_2 \binom{3d-4}{3d_1-1} \right) N_{d_1} N_{d_2}, \qquad N_1 = 1,$$

derived purely tropically [9]. The following short program implements it and reproduces the table above:

```python
from math import comb

memo = {1: 1}

def kontsevich(d: int) -> int:
    """Genus-zero Gromov-Witten invariants N_d of the projective plane."""
    if d not in memo:
        total = 0
        for d1 in range(1, d):
            d2 = d - d1
            term = (d1**2 * d2**2 * comb(3*d - 4, 3*d1 - 2)
                    - d1**3 * d2 * comb(3*d - 4, 3*d1 - 1))
            total += term * kontsevich(d1) * kontsevich(d2)
        memo[d] = total
    return memo[d]

for d in range(1, 7):
    print(d, kontsevich(d))
```

Each value agrees with the weighted tropical count: the recursion's binomial coefficients count distributions of marked points across the two components of a reducible tropical curve, while the polynomial factors in $d_1, d_2$ count intersection points of the components with each other and with the two auxiliary lines [9].

### Real counts and Welschinger asymptotics

Counting the same tropical curves with Welschinger's signed multiplicities gives lower bounds and, in the stretched-point limit, precise logarithmic asymptotics for the number of real rational curves through real points — the theorem of Itenberg, Kharlamov, and Shustin, recovered tropically as a statement about the growth of weighted tree statistics [2], [6]. This is a genuine theorem of real algebraic geometry proved by tropical means.

### Descendant numbers

The tropical descendant counts of Markwig–Rau and Blomme have been implemented algorithmically: the generalized lattice path algorithm enumerates rational plane tropical curves satisfying Psi-conditions and point or line evaluations, and the resulting integers match the classical descendant Gromov–Witten numbers, including the coefficients of the $J$-function that mirror symmetry predicts [3], [5].

##6 Limitations

The correspondence is powerful but not universal, and its boundaries are instructive.

- **Higher genus and superabundance.** While Mikhalkin's theorem covers arbitrary genus for toric surfaces, the tropical moduli spaces in higher genus contain *superabundant* combinatorial types — for example, non-realizable maps from an elliptic curve to $\mathbb{TP}^1$ — that admit no classical lift. Controlling which tropical curves are realizable remains a subtle problem [2].
- **Real invariants beyond genus zero.** Welschinger's signed count is defined for rational curves; in positive genus there is, at the time of writing, no corresponding real invariant, so the tropical real correspondence stops at genus zero [2].
- **Non-toric ambient varieties.** The cleanest statements require toric surfaces or toric degenerations. Nishinou and Siebert extended the genus-zero correspondence to higher-dimensional toric manifolds via logarithmic geometry, but for general non-toric varieties (notably Calabi–Yau threefolds) the tropical approach is far less developed [1].
- **Descendant and relative complexity.** Tropical Psi-classes and relative invariants require delicate compactifications of tropical moduli spaces; the definitions must be chosen carefully even for mildly degenerate curves [3], [9].
- **Computational growth.** Lattice path and floor diagram counts grow extremely fast with degree; the combinatorial explosion limits direct enumeration to modest degrees despite the algorithms being finite.

##7 Conclusion

Tropical geometry has transformed enumerative geometry from a purely intersection-theoretic discipline into one with a vivid combinatorial and computational interface. The arc surveyed here — from the max-plus semifield and the balancing condition, through amoeba degenerations and Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem, to lattice paths, floor diagrams, and tropical Psi-classes — shows a single coherent idea at work: *degenerate until only combinatorics remains, then prove that the combinatorics remembers the geometry*. The payoffs are concrete: tropical proofs of Kontsevich's recursion and the Caporaso–Harris formula, effective computation of node polynomials, tropical access to real Welschinger invariants, and a growing descendant theory tied to mirror symmetry [1], [3], [4], [5], [6], [9].

The frontier now lies in extending the correspondence beyond its toric heartland — to higher genus with full realizability control, to open invariants and tropical discs on K3 surfaces, and to the enumerative geometry of Calabi–Yau varieties where tropical methods are only beginning to take hold. If the history of the subject is any guide, each extension will first look impossible, then combinatorial, and finally inevitable.
