# Arithmetization of the Ethereum Virtual Machine: PLONKish Constraint Systems, Lookup Arguments, and Recursive Proof Composition for Zero-Knowledge Virtual Machines

## Abstract

Zero-knowledge virtual machines (zkVMs) embed an execution engine inside a succinct proof system, proving correct execution of arbitrary programs without revealing inputs. This thesis examines the arithmetization pipeline that makes a *zero-knowledge EVM* (zkEVM) possible: the PLONKish constraint systems introduced by Gabizon, Williamson, and Ciobotaru [1] and generalized in the Halo 2 book [2]; the lookup arguments of Plookup [3] and its reconciliation with PLONK in PlonKup [4]; and recursive proof composition — from Halo's accumulation [8] through Nova's folding [9] to HyperNova's CCS generalization [10] — compressing millions of constraints into one verifier-friendly proof. We formalize the trace-to-polynomial pipeline of production zkEVMs [5, 6], derive the grand-product permutation and lookup identities, analyze prover and verifier asymptotics, and survey empirical performance. We show that the combination of custom gates, large lookup tables, and folding-based recursion reduces the cost of proving EVM execution by orders of magnitude relative to naive circuit compilation, while identifying open problems in trusted-setup transparency, arithmetization of state access, and soundness under hash-function conjectures.

## 1 Introduction

The promise of *verifiable computation* — outsourcing execution while retaining cryptographic certainty of correctness — has motivated three decades of research since the introduction of zero-knowledge proofs [11]. Yet only in the last decade have proof systems become efficient enough to contemplate proving the execution of a general-purpose processor: first RISC-style instruction sets, and, more ambitiously, the Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM), a 256-bit stack machine with dynamic memory, persistent storage, and a notoriously SNARK-unfriendly instruction set dominated by bitwise and byte-level operations.

A zero-knowledge virtual machine resolves a tension. The classical approach to verifiable computation — compiling each program into a bespoke arithmetic circuit — demands cryptographic expertise from every developer. A zkVM instead interposes a *fixed, general-purpose circuit* between the developer and the proving system: the circuit verifies the correct execution of programs targeting a standard instruction set architecture (ISA), so developers write ordinary Rust or Solidity and obtain proofs automatically [6]. The cost of this generality is enormous: every cycle of the machine must be expressible as polynomial constraints, and the EVM's 256-bit words map poorly onto the 31- or 64-bit fields that make constraint systems efficient.

Three ideas bridge that gap:

1. **PLONKish arithmetization.** A rectangular matrix of field elements with *advice*, *fixed*, and *instance* columns; polynomial constraints of bounded degree applied to every row; custom gates activated by selector columns; and copy constraints enforced by a *permutation argument* over Lagrange bases [1, 2].
2. **Lookup arguments.** Polynomial protocols, notably Plookup [3], that prove each element of a query list appears in a precomputed table — collapsing expensive bitwise logic into a single table-membership check, at the cost of large committed tables.
3. **Recursive proof composition.** Accumulation schemes [8], folding schemes [9, 10], and nested recursion that compress many proofs (or sequential execution steps) into one succinct proof verifiable in milliseconds.

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys the background: polynomial IOPs, the KZG commitment scheme, and the R1CS/AIR/PLONKish arithmetization landscape. Section 3 describes our methodology: the trace-to-proof pipeline and how we validate the constructions. Section 4 is a deep dive into the four pillars — PLONKish systems, lookups, zkEVM table design, and recursion. Section 5 presents empirical results and theoretical guarantees, Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Polynomial interactive oracle proofs

Modern succinct proof systems are constructed by *compiling* a polynomial interactive oracle proof (IOP) with a polynomial commitment scheme (PCS). In a polynomial IOP, the prover commits to polynomials and the verifier queries evaluations at random points; soundness reduces to the Schwartz–Zippel lemma, i.e., two distinct degree-*d* polynomials agree on at most *d* out of |𝔽| field points. The PCS — most commonly the Kate–Zaverucha–Goldberg (KZG) scheme [7] — compresses each committed polynomial into a constant-sized group element and produces evaluation proofs checkable with a bilinear pairing:

> **Theorem (KZG evaluation binding).** Let *pp* be honestly generated KZG parameters for degree bound *d*. For any PPT adversary, the probability of producing a commitment *C* and two distinct evaluation proofs claiming *f(z) = y₁* and *f(z) = y₂* with *y₁ ≠ y₂* is negligible, under the *d*-SDH assumption.

KZG requires a *trusted setup* (a powers-of-tau ceremony), but the setup is *universal* and *updatable*: the same structured reference string (SRS) serves all circuits up to the degree bound [1].

### 2.2 The arithmetization zoo: R1CS, AIR, and PLONKish

Early zk-SNARKs such as Groth16 consumed *rank-1 constraint systems* (R1CS): each constraint is a product of two linear forms equal to a third. R1CS is simple but rigid — every operation must factor through bilinear multiplication gates, and wire values cannot be referenced across time steps without explicit copies.

*Algebraic intermediate representations* (AIR), underlying STARKs [12], flip the orientation: the computation is an *execution trace*, a table whose rows are consecutive machine states, and constraints enforce local transition relations across consecutive rows. STARKs need no trusted setup (FRI-based commitments) and are post-quantum plausible, but proofs are large (tens to hundreds of kilobytes) and the verifier is not constant-time.

PLONKish arithmetization [2], our principal focus, generalizes PLONK [1] with custom gates and lookup arguments (the "UltraPLONK" extension). A circuit is a rectangular matrix; polynomial constraints may reference cells at *relative* row offsets (with wraparound); and *equality constraints* between arbitrary cells are enforced not by copying but by the permutation argument of Section 4.1. | System | Setup | Proof size | Verifier | Post-quantum | Arithmetization |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Groth16 | circuit-specific trusted | ~200 B | ~3 pairings | no | R1CS |
| PLONK [1] | universal updatable SRS | ~400 B | O(1) pairings | no | PLONKish |
| Halo 2 (IPA) | transparent | ~5–10 KB | O(log n) | no | PLONKish + lookups |
| STARK [12] | transparent | 40–200 KB | polylog(n) | plausible | AIR |
| Nova [9] + SNARK | transparent | O(log |F|) | O(log |F|) | no | relaxed R1CS / CCS |

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is constructive and comparative. We:

1. **Formalize** the PLONKish pipeline — circuit matrix → wire polynomials → gate identity, permutation identity, and lookup identity → quotient polynomial → KZG opening — following [1, 2, 4], and derive each identity from the grand-product construction so that soundness error terms are explicit.
2. **Reconstruct the zkEVM pipeline** from public design documents: the Polygon Zero zkEVM's table decomposition (CPU, arithmetic, byte-packing, logic, memory, Keccak) with cross-table lookups [5], and ZisK's RISC-V zkVM architecture with its recursion layer [6].
3. **Compare recursion strategies** — nested PLONK recursion, Halo-style accumulation [8], Nova folding [9], and HyperNova's folding for customizable constraint systems [10] — on prover work, recursion overhead, and trust assumptions.
4. **Ground performance claims** in published figures: proof sizes, verification times, and per-transaction proving costs reported by production systems.

A note on rigor: where design documents are drafts (as with [5]), we treat stated constraint counts as engineering targets and rely on the underlying papers for the theorems.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 PLONKish constraint systems: columns, gates, and the permutation argument

A PLONKish circuit is a matrix of *n* rows and *m* columns over a prime field 𝔽, where *n* is the size of a multiplicative subgroup *H ⊂ 𝔽ˣ* (typically a power of two, enabling FFTs). Each column carries a role [2]:

- **Fixed** columns are fixed by the circuit itself: constants and the *selector* polynomials that switch gates on or off per row.
- **Advice** columns hold the prover's witness values.
- **Instance** columns hold public inputs shared by prover and verifier.

A *configuration* specifies polynomial constraints *E₀, …, Eₑ* — multivariate polynomials of degree at most *d* (the maximum constraint degree) — that must vanish on every row of *H*, plus a set of *equality constraints* between arbitrary cells.

The innovation of PLONK [1] is to enforce equalities through a single **permutation argument** rather than explicit copy gates. Let *a, b, c* be the wire polynomials interpolating the advice columns over *H*, and let *σ* be a permutation of *H × {1,2,3}* mapping each cell to the next cell it must equal. With random challenges *β, γ* the prover shows:

> **Theorem (Permutation identity).** The wire assignments satisfy all copy constraints *iff* the following identity holds as polynomials, where *Z* is the grand-product accumulator with *Z(1) = 1*:
>
> *Z(ωX)·(a(X)+β·X+γ)(b(X)+β·k₁X+γ)(c(X)+β·k₂X+γ) = Z(X)·(a(X)+β·σ₁(X)+γ)(b(X)+β·σ₂(X)+γ)(c(X)+β·σ₃(X)+γ).*

The accumulator *Z* is defined by a linear scan over the subgroup, and its final value *Z(ωⁿ) = 1* acts as a checksum on the multiset of permuted values. The key algebraic fact exploited by PLONK is that the permutation *σ* decomposes into a product of coset shifts, so the polynomials *σᵢ(X)* are cheap to evaluate [1].

Custom gates generalize the single PLONK gate *qₗa + qᵣb + qₒc + qₘab + q𝚌 = 0* to arbitrary degree-*d* polynomials over wider wire sets, selected per row by selector columns:

```python
# Pseudo-code: PLONKish gate identity evaluation on one row
def gate_identity(row, wires, selectors, challenges):
    # wires: dict column -> value at row (with relative offsets)
    # selectors: q_arith, q_bool, q_range, ... enable gates per row
    acc = 0
    acc += selectors['q_mul'] * (wires['a'] * wires['b'] - wires['c'])
    acc += selectors['q_add'] * (wires['a'] + wires['b'] - wires['c'])
    acc += selectors['q_bool'] * (wires['a'] * (1 - wires['a']))  # booleanity
    acc += selectors['q_xor8'] * xor8_identity(wires)             # custom gate
    return acc  # must equal 0 on every row of H
```

All expressions — gate identities, the permutation identity, and lookup identities — are linearly combined with powers of a random challenge *y* into a single polynomial *H(X)*, divided by the vanishing polynomial *Z_H(X)* to obtain the quotient *Q(X)*; a KZG opening of *Q* at one random point convinces the verifier that every constraint held on every row [2]. This *vanishing argument* is what makes the prover's work quasilinear and the proof constant-sized.

### 4.2 Lookup arguments: Plookup and PlonKup

Bitwise operations are the Achilles' heel of arithmetic arithmetization: proving *c = a ⊕ b* on 8-bit values needs 25–32 constraints in the naive bit-decomposition style [3]. **Plookup** [3] replaces computation with *table membership*. Given a query polynomial *f* (values on *H*) and a table polynomial *t*, the prover shows {*f(h) : h ∈ H*} ⊆ {*t(h)*} via another grand product. Sorting the multiset union of queries and table values, one proves the sorted sequence advances by steps drawn from the table, compressing the argument into a handful of commitments of degree *n* rather than *d·log n* as in prior work [3].

> **Theorem (Plookup soundness, informal).** If the prover passes the sorted-multiset grand-product check with random challenge *δ*, then except with probability *O(n/|𝔽|)* every query value occurs in the table.

PlonKup [4] integrates lookups directly into the PLONK protocol rather than running a separate sub-protocol, yielding a *fully succinct* zk-SNARK whose gate set and table set are configured once. This is the design production zkEVMs adopt: UltraPLONK-style circuits where XOR, range checks, and byte decomposition are *lookups into fixed tables* rather than gates — shrinking circuits for SNARK-unfriendly primitives by an order of magnitude [1, 3, 4].

Production circuits use tag columns selecting which table applies per row [2].

### 4.3 zkEVM construction: from EVM traces to constraint tables

A zkEVM proves, for an Ethereum block, that sequential EVM execution transforms the pre-state root into the post-state root. The pipeline has five stages:

1. **Trace generation.** An instrumented EVM (the *executor*) replays the block, emitting an *execution trace*: per-opcode records of stack, memory, storage, and program counter.
2. **Table decomposition.** The trace is fanned out into specialized tables. The Polygon Zero design [5] is representative: a CPU table driving execution, plus arithmetic, byte-packing, logic, memory, Keccak-*f*, and Keccak-sponge tables. Each table has its own column layout and local constraints; *cross-table lookups* (CTLs) enforce consistency — e.g., every arithmetic operation claimed by the CPU table must appear in the arithmetic table.
3. **Polynomial encoding.** Each column is interpolated over the subgroup *H* into a wire polynomial; selectors and tables become fixed columns.
4. **Constraint aggregation.** Gate, permutation, and lookup identities are combined into the quotient polynomial and committed with KZG [1, 7].
5. **Recursion and aggregation.** Segment proofs (per-chunk STARKs in Polygon's design [5]) are recursively composed into one final proof cheap enough for on-chain verification.

The EVM's 256-bit words are the central difficulty. Polygon Zero works over a small Goldilocks-class field and proves 256-bit arithmetic by *limb decomposition*: a 256-bit value is four 64-bit limbs, with range checks on each limb via lookups and carry constraints between them [5]. Keccak, the EVM's hash, is arithmetized as its own table (the Keccak-*f* permutation over a 5×5×64 state) with sponge constraints stitching absorb/squeeze phases [5]. Memory consistency — proving that loads return the most recent stores — is handled by timestamped memory tables with initialization/finalization boundary constraints, an AIR-style technique imported into the STARK layer.

The *Polynomial Identity Language* (PIL) used by the Polygon Hermez zkEVM takes a different route: the entire state machine is expressed as polynomial identities over committed columns, with the prover (based on a STARK backend) generating proofs per batch and an aggregation circuit folding them [6]. Either way, the lesson is the same: **the zkEVM is not one circuit but a federation of tables glued by lookups**.

### 4.4 Recursive proof composition: accumulation, folding, and HyperNova

Proving a full Ethereum block in one circuit is infeasible — the constraint count reaches hundreds of millions. Recursion solves this by proving *verification itself* inside the proof system: prove each segment, then prove "I verified the segment proofs," collapsing *k* proofs into one.

Three generations of techniques matter:

- **Halo-style accumulation [8].** Bowe, Grigg, and Hopwood observed that verifying an IPA-based proof inside a circuit is expensive, but its *expensive parts* can be deferred: an *accumulator* carries the deferred pairing/IPA checks forward, and only the final accumulator is fully verified. This yields recursion *without a trusted setup*, at the cost of the accumulator growing across steps.
- **Nova folding [9].** Kothapalli, Setty, and Tzialla replace SNARK recursion with a *folding scheme*: a primitive that reduces checking *two* instances of a relation to checking *one*, via a random linear combination. Crucially, folding avoids SNARKs entirely in the recursion loop — the prover's per-step work is dominated by *two multiexponentiations* of size *O(|F|)*, the recursion overhead is constant (two group scalar multiplications in-circuit), and no FFTs or trusted setup are needed [9]. The resulting IVC proof is *O(|F|)* group elements, compressible to *O(log |F|)* with a final zkSNARK.
- **HyperNova [10].** Generalizes folding from R1CS to *customizable constraint systems* (CCS), which subsume PLONKish, R1CS, and AIR *without overhead*. Its folding scheme costs a *single* multi-scalar multiplication of size equal to the number of variables — optimal for MSM-based commitments — and supports folding *multiple* instances at once, enabling proof-carrying data. Its *a la carte* cost profile is exactly what zkVMs need: proving a step of a stateful machine (EVM, RISC-V) costs only as much as the circuit for *the instruction actually executed*, not the union of all instructions [10].

> **Theorem (Nova IVC, informal).** Given a folding scheme for committed relaxed R1CS, there exists an IVC scheme whose prover at each step performs work *O(|F|)* dominated by two MSMs, with constant recursion overhead and no trusted setup [9].

For zkEVMs, the practical pattern is hybrid: fast STARK provers handle segments (no trusted setup, transparent, GPU-friendly), and a SNARK/folding recursion layer compresses segment proofs into the final succinct proof [5, 6]. ---

## 5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

**Asymptotics.** For a circuit with *n* gates and maximum degree *d*:

| Phase | PLONK [1] | Halo 2 (KZG) | Nova [9] per step |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prover | O(n log n) | O(n log n) | O(\|F\|) field ops + 2 MSMs |
| Proof size | ~400 B (9 𝔾₁) | ~5–10 KB | O(\|F\|) → O(log \|F\|) compressed |
| Verifier | O(1) pairings | O(log n) | O(log \|F\|) |
| Setup | universal SRS | universal SRS / none (IPA) | transparent |

**Concrete figures from the literature.** The PLONK paper reports proof construction 7.5–20× cheaper in group exponentiations than Sonic for fully succinct verification, with proofs of 9 group elements (~400 bytes) verifying in ~6 ms [1]. Plookup reduces an 8-bit XOR from 25–32 constraints to a single lookup [3]. Nova's evaluation shows the folding-based IVC prover is the fastest in the literature, with recursion overhead of just two in-circuit scalar multiplications [9]. On the systems side, Polygon's Type 1 zkEVM prover targets full mainnet blocks at sub-cent per-transaction proving cost, with the final proof verified on Ethereum L1 [5].

**Security guarantees.** PLONK is a *zk-SNARK*: complete, knowledge-sound in the algebraic group model under *d*-SDH, and zero-knowledge via blinding factors [1]. Nova's IVC is knowledge-sound assuming the folding scheme's soundness and the discrete-logarithm hardness on the chosen curve cycle [9]. STARK-based layers inherit transparency and plausible post-quantum security from hash-based commitments [12] — though the recursion layer that compresses them typically reintroduces pairing-based assumptions.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Trusted setup vs. transparency.** KZG-based PLONKish systems inherit a universal SRS and its ceremony risk; IPA variants avoid it at the cost of larger proofs. No deployed zkEVM is simultaneously transparent, succinct, and pairing-free.
2. **State access arithmetization.** Merkle–Patricia trie path verification dominates zkEVM cost: each storage access requires hashing along a ~10-level path, and Keccak is expensive in any arithmetization. Stateless designs and Verkle-tree migration are active research, not solved engineering.
3. **Soundness gaps.** Security proofs for lookup arguments and folding schemes live in idealized models (AGM, random oracle); the Fiat–Shamir transform's concrete security for these protocols, and the collision resistance of the arithmetization-friendly hashes they use, are conjectural [9, 10].
4. **Prover economics.** Proving remains orders of magnitude more expensive than execution — GPU clusters and proving markets exist precisely because a single machine cannot keep up with mainnet throughput at low latency.
5. **Formal verification deficit.** Machine-checked proofs of these protocols are nascent (e.g., early Lean formalizations of Halo 2 gadgets); the gap between paper proof and deployed code is bridged mostly by audits.

## 7 Conclusion

The zero-knowledge virtual machine is a triumph of *arithmetization engineering*. PLONKish constraint systems [1, 2] provide the flexible substrate — matrix circuits, custom gates, and the permutation argument that makes copy constraints essentially free. Lookup arguments [3, 4] tame the SNARK-unfriendly operations that dominate real instruction sets, turning bitwise logic into table membership. And recursive composition — from Halo's accumulation [8] through Nova's folding [9] to HyperNova's folding for customizable constraint systems [10] — makes it possible to prove computations far larger than any single circuit, with per-step costs proportional to the instruction executed rather than the machine's full complexity.

The trajectory is clear: STARK provers for raw throughput, folding-based recursion for aggregation, and lookup-heavy PLONKish circuits for the awkward corners of the EVM. The open problems — transparent succinctness, cheap state proofs, and machine-checked soundness — define the next decade of the field. The remaining work is to make it trustless, formally verified, and cheap enough to disappear into infrastructure.
