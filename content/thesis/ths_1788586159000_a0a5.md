# Neural Audio Codecs for Low-Bitrate Streaming: Residual Vector Quantization in EnCodec and DAC, Causal Streaming Architectures, Psychoacoustic Losses, and Generative Token Modeling versus Opus at 3-12 kbps

## Abstract

Neural audio codecs have displaced decades of hand-engineered psychoacoustic design at ultra-low bitrates by learning analysis-synthesis transforms end-to-end from data. This thesis presents a unified treatment of **residual vector quantization (RVQ)** as the central mechanism behind state-of-the-art neural codecs — Meta's **EnCodec** [1], Descript's **Audio Codec (DAC)** [2], and their predecessor **SoundStream** [3] — analyzing how learned encoder-decoder pairs with *causal* convolutional structure, adversarial and multi-resolution spectral losses, and discrete token streams at 50-100 Hz enable intelligible wideband speech and music at 3-12 kbps, bitrates where classical codecs such as Opus [7] and EVS [8] degrade sharply. We derive the RVQ bit-allocation trade-off, formalize streaming causality constraints on receptive fields and algorithmic delay, and dissect psychoacoustically-motivated loss functions that suppress quantization noise under the masking curve. We then examine the second-order transformation by which codec tokens become the *tokenization layer* for generative language models — **AudioLM** [4] and **MusicGen** [5] — recasting audio generation as next-token prediction over hierarchical semantic-acoustic tokens. Quantitative analysis covers compression ratios, ViSQOL/MUSHRA/MOS evaluations reported in the literature, real-time factors on mobile DSPs and NPUs, and a head-to-head comparison with Opus and EVS at matched bitrates. We conclude with open problems in error resilience, variable-bitrate conditioning, and neural-statistical hybrid coding.

## 1. Introduction

Digital audio compression has historically been governed by the rate-distortion paradigm of classical information theory: transform coding (MDCT in AAC, MP3), linear prediction (CELP in AMR-WB, EVS), and psychoacoustic bit allocation models hand-tuned over decades [7][8]. At bitrates above roughly 24 kbps for speech, these designs perform admirably. Below 12 kbps, however, classical codecs enter a regime of rapidly collapsing quality — metallic artifacts, bandwidth truncation, and temporal smearing become unavoidable because fixed codebooks and hand-designed predictors cannot allocate bits where they matter most across the enormous diversity of real-world audio.

Neural audio codecs invert this design philosophy. Rather than fixing the transform and quantizer and optimizing only the bit allocation, models such as **EnCodec** [1] and **DAC** [2] learn the *entire* analysis-synthesis chain — encoder, quantizer, and decoder — jointly from data, using adversarial training to optimize perceptual quality directly rather than mean-squared error on waveforms. The result is a class of systems transmitting **discrete tokens** at 50-100 Hz and 1.5-12 kbps, matching classical-codec quality at a fraction of the bitrate.

This thesis is organized around five interlocking questions:

1. **How does residual vector quantization (RVQ) structure the rate-distortion frontier?** We derive the cascade quantization model, analyze codebook collapse pathologies, and explain the exponential-moving-average updates, factorized and L2-normalized codebooks, and quantizer dropout techniques that stabilize training [1][2].
2. **What does streaming impose?** Real-time communication demands *causal* architectures — no look-ahead beyond a bounded algorithmic delay. We formalize the causality constraint on convolutional receptive fields, streaming normalization, and chunk-wise inference [1][3].
3. **What replaces the psychoacoustic model?** We show how multi-scale STFT discriminators, feature-matching losses, and mel-spectral reconstruction losses implicitly learn masking-curve behavior, and where explicit psychoacoustic weighting still helps.
4. **Why do tokens matter beyond compression?** We analyze how AudioLM [4] and MusicGen [5] treat codec tokens as a *discrete audio language*, enabling generative modeling, and how the residual hierarchy (coarse-to-fine codebooks) maps onto semantic-versus-acoustic modeling stages.
5. **How do neural codecs compare to Opus and EVS at 3-12 kbps, and what does deployment cost?** We synthesize reported MOS/MUSHRA/ViSQOL evaluations and analyze real-time inference budgets on mobile DSPs.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Classical audio coding: transforms, prediction, psychoacoustics

Classical perceptual codecs share a common skeleton: a time-frequency transform (MDCT), a psychoacoustic model estimating the *masking threshold* — the inaudible energy level per critical band — and a quantizer that shapes noise below that threshold. Speech codecs instead exploit the source-filter model: linear predictive coding (LPC) captures vocal-tract resonances, and the residual excitation is coded with algebraic codebooks (ACELP in AMR-WB and EVS) [8]. **Opus** [7] hybridizes both worlds: SILK (LPC-based) for speech and CELT (MDCT-based) for music, switching dynamically. These designs are marvels of engineering, but their fixed transforms and codebooks cap adaptability: at 6 kbps, Opus must discard high-frequency content and coarsely quantize the excitation, producing the familiar "underwater" quality.

### 2.2 Neural waveform models and the road to codecs

The neural era began with *generative* waveform models — WaveNet's dilated causal convolutions demonstrated that raw audio could be modeled sample-by-sample with striking fidelity. **HiFi-GAN** [6] then showed that adversarial training with multi-period and multi-scale discriminators could synthesize high-fidelity waveforms from mel spectrograms in real time. The conceptual leap to *codecs* was to insert a learned quantizer between an encoder and such a GAN decoder: **SoundStream** [3] combined a fully convolutional encoder, RVQ, and a GAN decoder with a *quantizer dropout* mechanism enabling a single model to operate across bitrates. EnCodec [1] and DAC [2] refined this recipe — EnCodec adding LSTM layers and a Transformer-based entropy model for further compression, DAC improving codebook utilization, snake activations, and loss balancing to reach near-transparent quality at higher rates.

### 2.3 Vector quantization fundamentals

Vector quantization maps a continuous vector $z \in \mathbb{R}^d$ to the nearest entry of a finite codebook $\mathcal{C} = \{e_1, \dots, e_K\}$:

> **Theorem (Lloyd optimality conditions):** For a fixed codebook size $K$ and a stationary source distribution, the distortion-minimizing quantizer satisfies (i) the nearest-neighbor partition condition and (ii) the centroid condition, where each codeword is the conditional expectation of its Voronoi cell. Alternating these updates (the Lloyd-Max algorithm) converges to a local optimum of the distortion.

Neural codecs replace the Lloyd iteration with gradient-based learning through a non-differentiable $\arg\min$ via the **straight-through estimator**, plus a commitment loss $\|z - \mathrm{sg}[e_k]\|^2$ anchoring encoder outputs to codewords.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 System model: the RVQ autoencoder

We consider a waveform $x \in \mathbb{R}^{T}$ sampled at $f_s$ (typically 24 or 48 kHz). The encoder $E$ produces a latent sequence $z = E(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{T/S \times d}$, where $S$ is the total striding factor (e.g., $S = 320$ gives 75 Hz frames at 24 kHz). The **residual vector quantizer** with $N_q$ stages operates as:

1. Initialize residual $r_0 = z_t$ for each frame $t$.
2. For $i = 1, \dots, N_q$: $k_i = \arg\min_k \|r_{i-1} - e^{(i)}_k\|$, $r_i = r_{i-1} - e^{(i)}_{k_i}$.
3. Quantized latent: $\hat{z}_t = \sum_{i=1}^{N_q} e^{(i)}_{k_i}$.

The bitrate follows directly: with codebook size $K = 1024$ (10 bits per stage), frame rate $f = f_s/S$, and $N_q$ stages,

$$\text{bitrate} = f \cdot N_q \cdot \log_2 K \quad \text{bits/second}.$$

For EnCodec at 24 kHz with $S = 320$ ($f = 75$ Hz) and $N_q \in \{2, 4, 8, 16, 32\}$, this yields **1.5, 3, 6, 12, 24 kbps** — the celebrated variable-bitrate ladder from a single model [1].

The decoder $G$ reconstructs $\hat{x} = G(\hat{z})$. Training minimizes a composite objective:

$$\mathcal{L} = \lambda_t \mathcal{L}_{\text{time}} + \lambda_f \mathcal{L}_{\text{freq}} + \lambda_g \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}} + \lambda_{fm} \mathcal{L}_{fm} + \lambda_c \mathcal{L}_{\text{commit}},$$

where the terms are time-domain L1, multi-scale mel-spectral L1/L2, hinge adversarial losses from waveform and STFT discriminators, discriminator feature matching, and the VQ commitment loss.

### 3.2 Causal streaming architecture

Real-time communication tolerates only bounded algorithmic delay. A convolutional encoder-decoder is **causal** if every output frame depends only on current and past inputs. For a stack of convolutions with kernel sizes $k_j$ and strides $s_j$, the *causal receptive field* extends $R = 1 + \sum_j (k_j - 1)\prod_{m<j} s_m$ samples into the past. EnCodec enforces causality by left-padding convolutions and replacing non-causal normalization with **streaming-compatible** alternatives; its LSTM layers are uni-directional [1]. SoundStream [3] similarly uses causal convolutions with a total algorithmic delay of only a few tens of milliseconds. DAC [2], targeting high-fidelity offline compression, relaxes strict causality but retains a fully convolutional structure amenable to chunked inference with overlap-add.

### 3.3 Codebook learning: collapse, factorization, and dropout

Naive VQ training suffers **codebook collapse**: a small subset of codewords attracts all assignments, wasting capacity. Three remedies dominate:

- **EMA updates**: codewords track exponential moving averages of assigned latents, with dead-code revival re-initializing unused entries [3].
- **Factorized, L2-normalized codes**: DAC projects latents to a low-dimensional space (e.g., 8-D) and L2-normalizes before lookup, dramatically improving utilization — from under 50% to near 100% of the 1024-entry codebooks [2].
- **Quantizer dropout**: during training, a random prefix $n \le N_q$ of RVQ stages is used, forcing coarse codebooks to carry maximal information and yielding a *single* model that decodes gracefully at any lower bitrate [1][3].

### 3.4 Psychoacoustic and adversarial losses

Classical codecs shape quantization noise using explicit masking curves. Neural codecs achieve an analogous effect *implicitly*: multi-resolution STFT discriminators penalize spectral discrepancies at multiple time-frequency trade-offs, while mel-scale reconstruction losses emphasize perceptually weighted frequency resolution [6]. DAC [2] further balances loss magnitudes across scales to prevent any single discriminator from dominating. The empirical consequence is noise shaped into high-energy, well-masked regions — a learned psychoacoustic model.

```python
# Residual vector quantization: the core of EnCodec / DAC / SoundStream
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

class ResidualVQ(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_stages=8, codebook_size=1024, dim=128):
        super().__init__()
        self.n_stages = n_stages
        # Factorized codebooks: low-dim, L2-normalized (DAC-style [2])
        self.codebooks = torch.nn.Parameter(
            torch.randn(n_stages, codebook_size, 8))
        self.proj_in = torch.nn.Linear(dim, 8)
        self.proj_out = torch.nn.Linear(8, dim)

    def forward(self, z, n_active=None):
        # z: [B, T, dim] encoder latents
        n_active = n_active or self.n_stages  # quantizer dropout [1][3]
        residual = F.normalize(self.proj_in(z), dim=-1)
        quantized, indices = 0.0, []
        for i in range(n_active):
            cb = F.normalize(self.codebooks[i], dim=-1)      # [K, 8]
            dist = (residual.pow(2).sum(-1, keepdim=True)
                    - 2 * residual @ cb.T + cb.pow(2).sum(-1))
            k = dist.argmin(-1)                               # nearest codeword
            q = F.embedding(k, cb)                             # straight-through
            q = residual + (q - residual).detach()             # ST estimator
            quantized = quantized + q
            residual = residual - q                            # next-stage residual
            indices.append(k)
        return self.proj_out(quantized), torch.stack(indices, -1)
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The RVQ cascade as successive refinement

RVQ is an instance of **successive refinement** in multi-terminal information theory: stage $i$ encodes the residual of stages $1..i-1$, so the reconstruction after $n$ stages is a refinement of the reconstruction after $n-1$. This yields two powerful properties. First, *embedded bitstreams*: truncating stages degrades quality gracefully, enabling bitrate scalability without re-encoding — the mechanism behind EnCodec's 1.5-24 kbps ladder [1]. Second, a **coarse-to-fine information hierarchy**: early stages capture phonetic and prosodic structure while late stages encode fine timbral texture. This hierarchy is precisely what AudioLM [4] exploits: its autoregressive stage models only coarse (semantic + early acoustic) tokens, delegating fine acoustic detail to a non-autoregressive refinement stage.

> **Theorem (Successive refinability):** A source is successively refinable if the rate-distortion function is achievable at rate $R_1$ with distortion $D_1$ and the *conditional* rate-distortion function given the first description achieves $(R_2 - R_1, D_2)$ for $R_2 > R_1$. Gaussian sources under MSE are successively refinable; learned RVQ codecs approximate this property empirically through quantizer dropout training.

The bit-allocation question — how many stages $N_q$ versus codebook size $K$ — trades temporal resolution against per-frame fidelity. EnCodec's choice of 75 Hz frames with up to 32 stages of 10-bit codes gives fine-grained scalability; DAC's 86 Hz frames with 9 stages targets higher fidelity per stage [2].

### 4.2 Streaming causality: delay, receptive fields, and state

Streaming imposes three coupled constraints: **algorithmic delay** (future samples the model may peek at — must be near zero), **computational latency** (must process faster than real time), and **statefulness** (convolutional padding and LSTM hidden states must carry across chunks without recomputation). EnCodec's streaming design achieves an algorithmic delay of roughly 13 ms at 24 kHz with a real-time factor well below 1 on a single CPU core [1]. Key techniques include:

- *Causal padding*: left-pad only, so no future context leaks.
- *Chunked streaming convolutions*: cached intermediate activations avoid $O(R)$ recomputation per chunk.
- *Uni-directional recurrence*: LSTMs propagate state forward; Transformers, if used, employ causal masking with KV-caching.

### 4.3 Psychoacoustic losses: learning the masking curve

We can interpret the discriminator ensemble as a *learned, signal-adaptive masking model*. Consider the multi-scale STFT discriminator operating on complex spectrograms at window sizes $\{2048, 1024, 512, 256\}$: adversarial pressure forces the generator to match the true distribution at each resolution, which — because the discriminator itself is a deep network trained on real audio — concentrates capacity on perceptually salient structure. Formally, the hinge loss

$$\mathcal{L}_D = \mathbb{E}_x[\max(0, 1 - D(x))] + \mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}}[\max(0, 1 + D(\hat{x}))]$$

drives the generator toward $\hat{x}$ indistinguishable from $x$ under every learned feature projection $D^{(l)}$, and the feature-matching term $\sum_l \|D^{(l)}(x) - D^{(l)}(\hat{x})\|_1$ acts as a perceptual distance in a learned auditory space. Empirical analyses show quantization noise concentrating in high-energy spectral regions — exactly where simultaneous masking renders it inaudible — recovering the classical psychoacoustic bit-allocation principle *without* an explicit auditory model [2][6].

### 4.4 From compression to generation: AudioLM and MusicGen

The most consequential property of neural codecs is that their output is a **discrete token sequence** — a native alphabet for language models. **AudioLM** [4] builds a two-stage hierarchy: (i) *semantic tokens* from a self-supervised model (w2v-BERT) quantized by k-means, capturing phonetic/content structure at 25 Hz; (ii) *acoustic tokens* from SoundStream's RVQ, capturing waveform detail. A decoder-only Transformer first autoregressively extends semantic tokens (continuation of linguistic content), then predicts coarse acoustic tokens conditioned on semantics, and finally refines fine acoustic tokens non-autoregressively. This factorization yields coherent speech continuation and piano music generation from short prompts.

**MusicGen** [5] simplifies the recipe: a single autoregressive Transformer over EnCodec tokens, conditioned on text (via T5/FLAN-T5 embeddings) or melody, with an *RVQ-aware delay pattern* — the now-standard "delay interleave" where codebook $i$ at time $t$ is predicted at step $t + i - 1$, respecting the coarse-to-fine dependency. Trained on 20k hours of licensed music, MusicGen demonstrates that a compression codebook learned for *fidelity* doubles as a *generative prior* over musical audio, producing 30-second stereo excerpts judged competitive with reference recordings in human studies.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AudioLM [4] | w2v-BERT k-means + SoundStream RVQ | 25 / 50 Hz | 12 (acoustic) | AR semantic → AR coarse acoustic → NAR fine | Audio prompt |

### 4.5 Real-time inference on mobile DSPs

Deployment shifts the bottleneck from quality to **compute budget**. A typical EnCodec encoder-decoder pass at 24 kHz requires on the order of 1-5 GMACs per second of audio — feasible on modern smartphone NPUs but heavy for always-on DSP paths. Optimization strategies include:

1. **Operator fusion and quantization**: INT8 quantization of convolutions (with quantization-aware training to protect the RVQ bottleneck, where precision loss is catastrophic), depthwise-separable factorization of transposed convolutions.
2. **Streaming state caching**: KV-cache-style reuse of convolutional activations across 10-20 ms chunks, reducing per-chunk compute to the incremental receptive field.
3. **Asymmetric complexity**: in one-way streaming (e.g., voice uplink), the encoder runs on-device while the heavier GAN decoder runs in the cloud — but decoder-side deployment matters for playback devices.
4. **Hardware mapping**: Hexagon DSP / ARM Ethos NPUs accelerate the convolutional backbone; the RVQ nearest-neighbor lookup (a batched GEMM + argmin) maps efficiently to vector units.

Reported real-time factors: EnCodec runs at ~0.1-0.3x real time on a laptop CPU for the full autoencoder [1]; heavily optimized INT8 variants approach real-time on flagship mobile SoCs, though sub-20 ms end-to-end latency with the GAN decoder remains an active engineering frontier.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Compression efficiency

At 24 kHz mono, raw PCM requires 384 kbps (16-bit). EnCodec at 6 kbps therefore achieves a **64:1 compression ratio**; at 1.5 kbps, 256:1. DAC at 8 kbps on 44.1 kHz audio compresses 705.6 kbps PCM by 88:1 while reporting near-transparent quality on music [2]. These ratios exceed classical codecs' usable range: Opus at 6 kbps is restricted to narrowband-ish speech with audible artifacts, whereas EnCodec at 6 kbps delivers wideband quality.

### 5.2 Subjective and objective quality: the literature's verdict

| Codec | Bitrate | Test | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| EnCodec [1] | 6 kbps | MUSHRA (speech+music) | Outperforms Opus at 12 kbps; matches EVS at 9.6 kbps |
| EnCodec [1] | 12 kbps | MUSHRA | Competitive with Opus at 24 kbps |
| DAC [2] | 8 kbps (44.1 kHz) | MUSHRA / CMOS | Near-transparent; preferred over EnCodec at matched rates on music |
| SoundStream [3] | 6 kbps | MUSHRA / ViSQOL | Beats Opus at 6 kbps; ViSQOL gains of 0.3-0.5 MOS-LQO |
| Opus [7] | 6-12 kbps | — | Reference baseline; quality degrades steeply below 12 kbps for music |

Objective metrics corroborate: multi-scale spectral distances and ViSQOL scores favor neural reconstructions, though the community rightly treats GAN-era metrics with caution, since adversarial training can *optimize the metric* without improving perception — hence the continued reliance on MUSHRA listening tests.

### 5.3 Formal analysis: rate-distortion of the RVQ cascade

Let $D_n$ be the expected distortion after $n$ RVQ stages. Under high-rate quantization theory with per-stage codebook size $K$ in $d$ dimensions, each stage reduces the residual variance approximately geometrically:

$$D_n \approx D_0 \cdot \rho^n, \qquad \rho \approx c(d) \cdot K^{-2/d},$$

where $c(d)$ is a lattice constant. With DAC's factorized 8-D codebooks and $K = 1024$, $\rho \approx c \cdot 1024^{-1/4} \approx 0.18c$ per stage — explaining the rapid quality climb over the first 4-6 stages and diminishing returns thereafter. Quantizer dropout training [1] explicitly optimizes the *entire* distortion profile $\{D_1, \dots, D_{N_q}\}$ rather than only $D_{N_q}$, which is why truncated bitstreams remain near-optimal: the model learns a nested family of near-rate-distortion-optimal codes.

> **Theorem (Nested optimality under dropout):** If each RVQ stage is trained to minimize $\mathbb{E}[\|r_{n-1} - Q_n(r_{n-1})\|^2]$ with the residual distribution induced by all previous stages, and stage prefixes are sampled during training, then the expected distortion of the $n$-stage prefix decoder is within a bounded gap of the optimal $n$-stage RVQ — the gap vanishing as codebook utilization approaches 100%.

### 5.4 Error resilience and packet loss

A critical weakness relative to classical codecs: neural decoders are *generative*, so a lost packet's tokens, if concealed naively (e.g., zero-fill), produce plausible-but-wrong audio — arguably worse than Opus's engineered packet-loss concealment. Mitigations include forward error correction over token packets, interleaving RVQ stages across packets (losing fine stages degrades gracefully thanks to successive refinability), and training-time packet-loss simulation. This remains the least mature axis of neural codec deployment.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Generalization vs. training distribution.** GAN-decoder codecs can hallucinate — producing clean-sounding but *unfaithful* reconstructions (phoneme substitution in noisy speech) when inputs fall off-manifold. Classical codecs never hallucinate; they degrade predictably.
2. **Compute and power.** Even optimized, the GAN decoder costs 10-100x the DSP budget of an Opus decoder, challenging always-on mobile and hearing-aid form factors.
3. **Latency of generative token modeling.** AudioLM/MusicGen-style autoregressive generation over 50-75 Hz tokens with 4-12 codebooks requires thousands of sequential steps per second of audio — minutes of wall-clock per minute of audio without speculative or masked-parallel decoding advances.
4. **Metric gaming.** Adversarial training optimizes fool-the-discriminator, which correlates with but does not equal human perception; several studies report objective-metric inflation unconfirmed by MUSHRA.
5. **Bitrate rigidity.** RVQ ladders are discrete ($N_q \in \{2,4,8,\dots\}$); truly continuous variable-bitrate operation and joint source-channel coding remain open.

## 7. Conclusion

Neural audio codecs represent a phase transition in compression: by learning the transform, quantizer, and perceptual loss jointly, RVQ-based systems — SoundStream [3], EnCodec [1], DAC [2] — deliver at 3-12 kbps the quality classical codecs need 2-4x the bitrate to achieve, with causal streaming architectures and real-time factors compatible with deployment. Their deeper significance, however, lies in what the tokens *enabled*: AudioLM [4] and MusicGen [5] recast audio as a language-modeling problem over codec tokens, unifying compression and generation in a single discrete representation. The road ahead — error resilience, sub-10 ms mobile inference, faithful (non-hallucinating) reconstruction, and neural-statistical hybrid codes — will determine whether learned codecs fully displace a half-century of hand-engineered design or settle into a productive coexistence with it.
