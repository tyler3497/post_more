# Magnetic Confinement Fusion: Tokamak Equilibrium, Neoclassical Transport, and the Burning-Plasma Regime

## Abstract

Magnetic confinement in the tokamak rests on three interlocking foundations: magnetohydrodynamic equilibrium, collisional transport in toroidal geometry, and the power-balance threshold for self-heated burn. This article derives the Grad-Shafranov equation for axisymmetric equilibria, develops the Hinton-Hazeltine neoclassical transport framework including the banana, plateau and Pfirsch-Schlüter regimes with the bootstrap current as a self-generated current source, and reconstructs the Lawson criterion in its modern triple-product form. We apply these tools to the ITER baseline scenario (15 MA, 5.3 T, Q=10, 500 MW of fusion power), the IPB98(y,2) confinement scaling and its H-mode assumptions, and the empirical record compiled across magnetic and inertial concepts. We examine disruptions, edge-localized modes, tearing modes and density limits as the principal failure channels of the burning-plasma regime, and survey steady-state advanced scenarios that trade bootstrap fraction against magnetohydrodynamic stability.

## 1 Introduction

The fusion of light nuclei powers every star in the sky, and the reaction most accessible on Earth is the fusion of deuterium and tritium:

**D + T → ⁴He (3.5 MeV) + n (14.1 MeV)**

with 17.6 MeV released per reaction. The cross section becomes appreciable only when ion temperatures reach roughly 10 keV — on the order of one hundred million kelvin — at which no material surface can touch the fuel. Confinement must therefore be *magnetic*: charged particles gyrate around field lines, and a cleverly shaped field can hold a burning plasma away from every wall simultaneously.

The tokamak — from the Russian *toroidal'naya kamera s magnitnymi katushkami*, toroidal chamber with magnetic coils — became the leading magnetic concept after the 1968 T-3 results at Novosibirsk demonstrated electron temperatures near 1 keV in a toroidal device, a leap that redirected the world program almost overnight. Three questions have organized tokamak physics ever since:

1. **Equilibrium:** what magnetic configurations can a hot, pressurized plasma sustain without flying apart?
2. **Transport:** how quickly do particles and energy leak across the magnetic field, and what sets that rate?
3. **Burn:** what plasma conditions make fusion self-heating dominate over externally supplied heating?

This article treats each question with the machinery the field actually uses: the *Grad-Shafranov equation* for axisymmetric equilibria, the *Hinton-Hazeltine neoclassical theory* of collisional transport in toroidal geometry, and the *Lawson criterion* in its modern triple-product form. We then apply all three to ITER, the international device under construction in southern France that is designed to produce 500 MW of fusion power from 50 MW of heating — a gain of ten — and thereby enter the burning-plasma regime [7].

## 2 Background

### The machine

A tokamak confines plasma in a toroidal vacuum vessel using two superposed magnetic field components. External coils generate a strong *toroidal* field B_φ (5.3 T in ITER), while a *toroidal plasma current* I_p (15 MA in ITER) driven through the plasma itself generates a *poloidal* field B_θ. Superposed, the two trace helical field lines winding around nested toroidal surfaces of constant magnetic flux. The field-line pitch is quantified by the **safety factor**

*q ≈ r B_φ / (R B_θ)*

in cylindrical approximation, the number of toroidal transits per poloidal transit. Because parallel transport along field lines is enormously faster than cross-field transport, each flux surface is nearly isothermal and isobaric: the plasma organizes itself into onion-like shells of constant pressure [8].

### Figures of merit

Tokamak performance is quoted in a compact set of dimensionless and engineering quantities:

- **Beta**, β = 2μ₀⟨p⟩/B², the ratio of plasma pressure to magnetic pressure. Stability limits β to a few percent.
- **Energy confinement time**, τ_E = W/P_heat, the ratio of stored thermal energy to heating power.
- **Fusion gain**, Q = P_fus/P_in. Q = 1 is scientific breakeven; Q ≥ 5 is conventionally the *burning-plasma* regime where alpha self-heating exceeds auxiliary heating; ITER targets Q = 10.
- **Triple product**, nTτ_E, the Lawson parameter in its most useful form.

### The ITER baseline

The reference scenario against which all burning-plasma physics is measured is the ITER 15 MA baseline [7][10]:

| Parameter | Symbol | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Major radius | R₀ | 6.2 m |
| Minor radius | a | 2.0 m |
| Toroidal field | B_T | 5.3 T |
| Plasma current | I_p | 15 MA |
| Fusion power | P_fus | 500 MW |
| Auxiliary heating | P_aux | 50 MW |
| Target gain | Q | 10 |
| Alpha heating | P_α | 100 MW |

With P_α = 100 MW against P_aux = 50 MW, the baseline is unambiguously a burning plasma: the fusion products heat the fuel harder than the injected beams and waves do.

## 3 Methodology

This article follows the tripartite methodology of the field itself [10]:

1. **Analytic magnetohydrodynamics** for equilibrium and ideal stability boundaries, checked against free-boundary numerical solvers that handle realistic coil sets and divertor geometry [1].
2. **Drift-kinetic theory** for collisional (neoclassical) transport, in the Hinton-Hazeltine formulation, with turbulent (anomalous) transport treated through validated scalings and gyrokinetic simulation [2].
3. **Zero-dimensional power balance** for the Lawson criterion and confinement projections, anchored to the multi-machine empirical database rather than to any single device [3].

*Notation:* ψ denotes poloidal magnetic flux; SI units throughout; temperatures are quoted in energy units (keV) so that Boltzmann's constant is absorbed. Bracketed numbers are literature citations, never intervals.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Grad-Shafranov Equation and Axisymmetric Equilibrium

Assume an axisymmetric, steady, ideally conducting plasma. Writing the magnetic field in terms of the poloidal flux function ψ(R, Z),

**B = ∇ψ × ∇φ + F(ψ) ∇φ**,

the toroidal component is encoded in F(ψ) = RB_φ. Force balance ∇p = **j** × **B** then forces both the pressure and F to be flux functions, p = p(ψ), and Ampère's law collapses to a single nonlinear elliptic equation, the **Grad-Shafranov equation**:

**Δ*ψ = −μ₀R² dp/dψ − F dF/dψ**

with the elliptic operator Δ* = R ∂/∂R(R⁻¹ ∂/∂R) + ∂²/∂Z². The two source terms are the plasma pressure gradient and the toroidal-field pressure gradient; specifying p(ψ) and F(ψ) plus boundary conditions determines the entire magnetic geometry — flux-surface shapes, the safety-factor profile, and the location of the magnetic axis.

Real devices need a *divertor*: the last closed flux surface (the separatrix) is made to pass through an X-point, so that exhaust plasma streams along open field lines into a remote chamber rather than striking the main wall. Analytic solutions with this topology are scarce — the Solovév profiles p′(ψ) and FF′(ψ) linear in ψ give the classic D-shaped equilibria used for ITER scoping [9] — so production work uses free-boundary solvers that iterate ψ against external coil currents with Newton-type methods [1]. The elliptic operator is discretized on (R, Z) grids; its core stencil is:

```rust
// Grad-Shafranov Delta* stencil on a uniform (R, Z) grid (illustrative)
fn delta_star(psi: &[Vec<f64>], r: &[f64], dr: f64, dz: f64, i: usize, j: usize) -> f64 {
    let d2r = (psi[i+1][j] - 2.0*psi[i][j] + psi[i-1][j]) / (dr*dr);
    let d1r = (psi[i+1][j] - psi[i-1][j]) / (2.0*dr);
    let d2z = (psi[i][j+1] - 2.0*psi[i][j] + psi[i][j-1]) / (dz*dz);
    d2r - d1r / r[i] + d2z
}
```

Equilibrium reconstruction is not academic bookkeeping: every stability and transport calculation takes the Grad-Shafranov solution as its geometric input, and real-time solvers drive plasma shape control on every major tokamak.

> **Theorem:** (Grad-Shafranov equilibrium) In axisymmetric ideal MHD, force balance reduces exactly to Δ*ψ = −μ₀R²p′(ψ) − F(ψ)F′(ψ); flux surfaces ψ = const are simultaneously surfaces of constant pressure, density, and temperature to lowest order.

### 4.2 The Safety Factor, Beta Limits, and MHD Stability

The safety factor profile q(ψ) = dΦ_tor/dψ_pol measures magnetic shear — how the field-line pitch varies across the plasma. It is the single most important stability parameter:

> **Theorem:** (Kruskal-Shafranov) An axisymmetric toroidal equilibrium with edge safety factor below unity is ideally unstable to the m=1, n=1 external kink. Stable operation requires q > 1 everywhere; practical scenarios hold q₉₅ ≥ 3.

Above this floor, pressure-driven limits take over. The **Troyon limit** caps the normalized beta β_N = β(%)·a(m)·B_T(T)/I_p(MA) at roughly 2.8–3.5 without wall stabilization [12]; exceeding it drives kink-ballooning modes that terminate the discharge. Current-profile-driven *tearing modes* are subtler: they grow on resistive timescales at rational surfaces where q = m/n, and the *neoclassical* tearing mode is self-sustained because the island flattens the pressure and thereby erases the bootstrap current inside it — a positive feedback loop that clamps β_N well below the ideal limit unless actively stabilized by electron-cyclotron current drive. Sawtooth oscillations periodically flatten the core when q drops below unity on axis, expelling fast particles and seeding those islands.

The picture is therefore one of *profile management*: the equilibrium is a controllable object, and advanced scenarios shape q(ψ) and p(ψ) jointly to sit in the stable window between the kink limit below and the pressure limit above.

### 4.3 Neoclassical Transport: Banana Orbits and the Bootstrap Current

In a torus the field strength varies as 1/R, so particles with small parallel velocity mirror-trap on the outboard side and execute *banana orbits* whose radial width is

*w_b ≈ q ρ_i / √ε*,

with ρ_i the ion gyroradius and ε = r/R the inverse aspect ratio — centimeters in a reactor, vastly larger than the gyroradius itself. Collisions knock trapped particles across field lines in random-walk steps of w_b, enhancing diffusion far above the classical level. Hinton and Hazeltine's 1976 kinetic theory organizes the result by collisionality ν* = ν_eff/ω_b, the effective collision frequency normalized to the bounce frequency [2]:

| Regime | Collisionality | Scaling of χ_i |
|---|---|---|
| Banana | ν* < ε^{3/2} | ∝ q² ε^{−3/2} ρ_i² ν_i |
| Plateau | ε^{3/2} < ν* < 1 | ∝ q² ρ_i² v_th / R |
| Pfirsch-Schlüter | ν* > 1 | ∝ q² ρ_i² ν_i |

Reactor cores sit deep in the *banana regime*, where transport is set by the trapped-particle fraction √ε. The same trapped-particle physics yields a remarkable dividend: the **bootstrap current**, a self-generated toroidal current driven by the pressure gradient through trapped-passing friction,

*j_bs ∝ −(ε^{1/2}/B_θ) dp/dr*,

with collisionality-dependent coefficients tabulated by Sauter and collaborators. Because it requires no external drive, the bootstrap current is the only credible path to *steady-state* tokamak operation; DIII-D has demonstrated fully non-inductive discharges with bootstrap fractions above 80 percent and ion transport reduced to the neoclassical level inside internal transport barriers [5]. A compact estimate:

```haskell
-- Sauter-style bootstrap fraction estimate (illustrative)
bootstrapFraction :: Double -> Double -> Double -> Double
bootstrapFraction epsInv betaPol q95 =
  min 1.0 (1.46 * sqrt epsInv * betaPol / q95)

main :: IO ()
main = print (bootstrapFraction 0.32 1.8 3.0)
```

Neoclassical theory thus plays a dual role: it sets the *irreducible minimum* of transport — the floor below which no magnetic configuration can go — and it supplies the bootstrap current that may free the tokamak from pulsed inductive operation.

### 4.4 Turbulence, H-mode, and Confinement Scaling

Measured transport exceeds neoclassical predictions by one to two orders of magnitude in most regimes: small-scale turbulence driven by ion-temperature-gradient (ITG), trapped-electron-mode (TEM), and electron-scale instabilities dominates. The great empirical discovery of 1982 was the **H-mode** (high-confinement mode) on ASDEX, in which the plasma spontaneously bifurcates to a state with an edge transport barrier — the *pedestal* — roughly doubling τ_E [13]. The accepted mechanism is E×B flow-shear suppression of turbulence, with the pedestal gradient ultimately limited by peeling-ballooning instabilities that erupt as *edge-localized modes* (ELMs), quasi-periodic bursts that must be tamed to protect the divertor. Modern pedestal analysis finds ion heat transport near the neoclassical floor in the barrier region, a direct vindication of Hinton-Hazeltine in the steep-gradient limit [6].

Because first-principles turbulent transport remains too expensive for routine design, the community projects performance with empirical scalings fit to dozens of devices. The standard is **IPB98(y,2)** [10]:

*τ_E,th = 0.0562 · I_p^{0.93} B_T^{0.15} n_e^{0.41} P^{−0.69} R^{1.97} M^{0.19} κ^{0.78} ε^{0.58}*,

with I_p in MA, B_T in T, n_e in 10¹⁹ m⁻³, P in MW, R in m, M in amu, κ elongation and ε = a/R. Confinement quality is quoted as H₉₈ = τ_E/τ_IPB98; ITER's Q = 10 mission assumes H₉₈ ≈ 1. The scaling's strong size dependence (R^{1.97}) is the quantitative argument for building large: confinement improves faster than volume degrades it. A discharge is usefully viewed as a finite-state process — ramp-up, flat-top, ramp-down — with interlocks on every transition:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE IterPulse ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals
VARIABLES phase, pAux, pFus
Phases == {"rampup", "flattop", "rampdown", "dwell"}
TypeOK == phase \in Phases
Init == phase = "rampup" /\ pAux = 0 /\ pFus = 0
FlattopOK == phase = "flattop" => pAux \in 0..73 /\ pFus \in 0..500
Next == \/ (phase = "rampup"   /\ phase' = "flattop")
        \/ (phase = "flattop"  /\ phase' = "rampdown")
        \/ (phase = "rampdown" /\ phase' = "dwell")
        \/ (phase = "dwell"    /\ phase' = "rampup")
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<phase, pAux, pFus>> /\ []FlattopOK
=============================================================================
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### The Lawson criterion, derived

Consider a 50/50 D–T plasma of volume V. Fusion power is P_fus = (n²/4)⟨σv⟩E_fusV with E_fus = 17.6 MeV; only the alpha-particle share E_ch = 3.5 MeV heats the plasma, so self-heating is P_α = P_fus/5. Energy leaves by conduction, convection and radiation at the rate W/τ_E with stored energy W = 3nk_BTV (electrons plus ions at common temperature T). Ignition — self-sustaining burn with no external heating — requires P_α ≥ W/τ_E, which rearranges to

**nTτ_E ≥ 12 T² / (⟨σv⟩ E_ch)**,

up to profile and impurity factors [3][4]. The right-hand side depends only on temperature through the reactivity; using the Bosch-Hale parameterization of ⟨σv⟩ [11], the threshold has a broad minimum near ion temperatures of 14 keV — the reason every burning-plasma design targets 10–20 keV rather than the hottest achievable plasma [8].

> **Theorem:** (Lawson) A D–T plasma ignites when alpha self-heating exceeds total losses, equivalently nTτ_E ≥ 12T²/(⟨σv⟩E_ch) modulo profile factors; the threshold is minimized near T_i ≈ 14 keV.

The shape of the threshold follows from the Gamow form of the reactivity:

```python
import numpy as np

def reactivity_gamow(T):
    # illustrative D-T form: <sigma v> ~ T^-2/3 exp(-19.94 T^-1/3), T in keV
    return T**(-2.0/3.0) * np.exp(-19.94 * T**(-1.0/3.0))

def triple_product_threshold(T, E_ch=3500.0):
    # n T tau_E >= 12 T^2 / (<sigma v> E_ch), arbitrary units
    return 12.0 * T**2 / (reactivity_gamow(T) * E_ch)

Ts = np.logspace(np.log10(3.0), np.log10(60.0), 400)
T_opt = Ts[np.argmin(triple_product_threshold(Ts))]
print('threshold minimum near T =', round(float(T_opt), 1), 'keV (illustrative)')
```

### Where the world stands

The achieved triple product across concepts has been compiled systematically against the Lawson criterion [3]. For D–T, ignition demands roughly 3×10²¹ keV s m⁻³ at the optimum temperature; ITER targets 5.4×10²¹ keV s m⁻³ at 10 keV average temperature, a comfortable margin that absorbs profile and impurity penalties [7]. The record holders:

| Device | Concept | Triple product (keV s m⁻³) | Milestone |
|---|---|---|---|
| JET (DTE2) | Tokamak | ~10²¹ | 59 MJ fusion energy record |
| JT-60U | Tokamak | 1.5×10²¹ | Highest tokamak triple product |
| NIF (2022) | Inertial | — | Laboratory ignition |
| ITER (target) | Tokamak | 5.4×10²¹ | Q = 10 burning plasma |

JET's 2021–2022 deuterium-tritium campaign produced 59 MJ in a single pulse — the energy record — while JT-60U holds the tokamak triple-product record at 1.5×10²¹ keV s m⁻³, within a factor of a few of reactor requirements [3][7]. In parallel, inertial confinement crossed its own Lawson threshold at NIF in December 2022, the first laboratory ignition by any approach [3].

Auxiliary empirical laws bound the operating space. The **Greenwald density limit** n_G[10²⁰ m⁻³] = I_p[MA]/(πa²[m²]) marks the density at which confinement collapses and disruptions loom; ITER's baseline sits near 85 percent of it, a deliberate choice that trades fusion power (∝ n²) against disruption risk. Confinement quality H₉₈ ≈ 1 has been held across the multi-machine database spanning two orders of magnitude in size — the empirical basis for trusting IPB98(y,2) at ITER scale [10].

## 6 Limitations

The burning-plasma regime is a physics threshold, not an engineering guarantee. Its principal failure channels are:

1. **Disruptions.** A loss of positional or MHD control quenches the plasma in milliseconds, dumping megajoules into the wall and driving *runaway electron* beams that can melt plasma-facing components. ITER's disruption-mitigation system must shatter-pellet-inject with near-perfect reliability.
2. **Edge-localized modes.** Unmitigated type-I ELMs in ITER would exceed divertor ablation limits by an order of magnitude; resonant magnetic perturbations and pellet pacing are the leading suppression tools.
3. **Neoclassical tearing modes.** Seed islands from sawteeth metastasize into confinement-killing NTMs at high β_N, demanding localized current drive for stabilization.
4. **The density limit.** Operating near Greenwald maximizes fusion power but narrows the disruption margin; the trade is fundamental.
5. **Steady state.** Inductive current drive is intrinsically pulsed; true steady state needs bootstrap fractions approaching unity plus efficient external current drive, a regime demonstrated only at reduced performance [5].
6. **The nuclear island.** Tritium self-sufficiency (breeding ratio above unity), 14 MeV neutron damage to structural steels, and remote maintenance of an activated device are unsolved at power-plant scale — physics does not imply technology.
7. **Extrapolation risk.** Zero-dimensional scalings like IPB98(y,2) are regressions, not derivations; their ITER projections carry stated uncertainties of tens of percent, and burning-plasma-specific effects (alpha-particle-driven Alfvén modes, self-heated profile stiffness) have never been tested at Q = 10 [10].

## 7 Conclusion

Magnetic confinement fusion stands on an unusually complete theoretical tripod. The Grad-Shafranov equation tells us which magnetic equilibria a burning plasma can inhabit and gives us the numerical tools to build them coil by coil [1][9]. Neoclassical theory tells us the irreducible minimum of collisional transport, explains the bootstrap current that may liberate the tokamak from pulsed operation, and has survived half a century of experimental test since Hinton and Hazeltine [2][5]. The Lawson criterion, sharpened into the triple-product threshold, tells us exactly how far the world program has come and what remains: a factor of a few in nTτ_E separates the best tokamaks from ignition, and ITER is engineered to cross it with margin [3][7][10].

Crossing it will not end the story. A burning plasma is a new physical object — dominantly self-heated, rich in energetic-particle modes, unforgiving of control lapses — and the step from ITER's Q = 10 to a power plant's steady, economical Q → ∞ runs through materials, tritium breeding, and disruption-proof control as much as through plasma physics. The equilibrium, transport, and burn foundations laid out here are necessary. Whether they prove sufficient is the experiment now under construction.
