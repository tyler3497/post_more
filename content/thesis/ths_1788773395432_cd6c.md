# Analog Vector–Matrix Multiplication in Memristor Crossbar Arrays: Ohm's-Law and Kirchhoff-Law In-Memory Computation, 1T1R Device Non-Idealities, Differential-Pair Weight Encoding, and Hardware-Aware Training for Neural Network Accelerators

## Abstract

Memristor crossbar arrays promise to dissolve the von Neumann memory wall by performing analog vector–matrix multiplication (VMM) directly in the memory fabric, exploiting Ohm's law for multiplication and Kirchhoff's current law for accumulation in a single step. This thesis develops the full stack of memristive in-memory computing: mapping signed weights onto positive conductances via differential-pair encoding, 1T1R selector arrays that suppress sneak paths, and the three dominant non-idealities — conductance drift, stuck-at faults, and IR drop — that corrupt the ideal linear VMM. We formalize hardware-aware training as optimization over a noisy, asymmetric conductance manifold and prove an error bound for differential-pair compensation under bounded write noise. Surveying landmark demonstrations — Yao et al.'s fully hardware memristor CNN above 96% MNIST accuracy [2], Ambrogio et al.'s equivalent-accuracy analog training at 28,065 GOPS/W [3], and resistive compute-in-memory chips [6] — we quantify two-order-of-magnitude efficiency gains over GPUs and identify endurance, retention, and ADC-area overhead as the residual barriers. The analysis closes with a roadmap toward multi-megabit analog accelerators and closed-loop calibration for reliable analog deep learning.

## 1 Introduction

The dominant cost of deep learning is not arithmetic but data movement: in von Neumann machines, weight tensors traversing the memory hierarchy incur an energy toll one to two orders of magnitude above the MAC operation itself. As models grow, this *memory wall* dominates latency and power, and post-Dennard scaling offers diminishing relief.

In-memory computing embeds computation in the physics of memory devices. The **memristor crossbar array** stores synaptic weights as analog conductances and performs VMM in a single analog step: row voltages induce, by Ohm's law, currents proportional to each input–conductance product, and Kirchhoff's current law sums them along each column — an **O(1)-time VMM** regardless of array dimension, versus O(N²) digitally [5].

> Theorem: The ideal N×M crossbar computes **y** = **Gx** in a single read step, with analog energy O(NM) versus O(NM log G) digitally.

The idealization collides with physical reality. Real memristors suffer from *conductance drift*, *stuck-at faults*, *write noise and asymmetric update nonlinearity*, and *IR drop* along word/bit lines that distorts applied voltages across large arrays [6]. The central question of this thesis: how can neural networks retain software-equivalent accuracy on hardware that is fundamentally analog, noisy, and imperfect?

Section 2 establishes device and circuit background; Section 3 formalizes the weight-to-conductance methodology; Section 4 dissects VMM physics, 1T1R architectures, non-idealities, differential-pair encoding, and hardware-aware training; Section 5 derives error bounds and surveys landmark chips; Sections 6–7 confront limitations and conclude.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Memristor as a Circuit Element

In 1971 Chua postulated the memristor as the fourth fundamental passive element, relating flux linkage φ to charge q with memristance M = dφ/dq [1]; the nanoscale TiO₂ realization by Strukov et al. launched resistive-switching research. For computing, the essence is simpler: a **two-terminal device whose resistance depends on applied-voltage history**, retaining its programmed conductance without power — a non-volatile, analog-tunable resistor.

### 2.2 Device Technologies

Three material families dominate in-memory computing research, each with distinct trade-offs:

| Technology | Mechanism | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Filamentary ReRAM** (HfOₓ, TaOₓ) | Oxygen-vacancy filament formation/rupture | CMOS-compatible, fast (<10 ns), high endurance (>10⁹) | Abrupt SET, nonlinear updates, high write noise |
| **Phase-change memory (PCM)** | Amorphous/crystalline phase transition (GST) | Gradual RESET, mature process, good retention | Asymmetric potentiation/depression, drift of amorphous states |
| **ECM/CBRAM** | Metallic filament (Ag, Cu) | Low-voltage switching | Poor retention, large cycle-to-cycle variation |

### 2.3 The Crossbar Fabric and the 1T1R Cell

A crossbar arranges devices at the intersections of perpendicular word lines (WL) and bit lines (BL). In the naive **0T1R (passive)** array, every device is directly connected, and *sneak-path currents* flow through unselected cells, corrupting reads and writes and wasting energy. The standard remedy is the **1T1R cell**: each memristor in series with an access transistor. The transistor provides:

1. **Selection** — only the addressed cell conducts during programming;
2. **Compliance control** — the gate voltage bounds filament formation during SET, setting the programmed conductance;
3. **Sneak-path elimination** — unselected cells present an open circuit.

Yao et al. used this architecture — eight 2048-cell 1T1R arrays implementing a complete five-layer CNN [2].

### 2.4 Neural Network Workloads

The workload that motivates everything is the deep neural network layer:

$$y_j = \sigma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N} W_{ji}\, x_i + b_j\right)$$

For inference, the inner sum dominates compute in convolutional and fully-connected layers; convolutions map to VMM via *im2col* unrolling. Training adds transposed VMM for backpropagation and outer-product updates — also accelerable in situ [3][8].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 From Weights to Conductances: The Mapping Problem

Trained weights **W** are signed reals; memristor conductances are positive and bounded, G ∈ [G_min, G_max]. The mapping bridges this gap in three steps:

**Step 1 — Normalization.** Scale weights to the conductance window:

$$W' = W / \max|W|, \qquad G_{\text{target}} = G_{\min} + (W' + 1)(G_{\max} - G_{\min})/2$$

**Step 2 — Differential-pair encoding.** Represent each signed weight as the difference of two conductances:

$$w_{ji} = \alpha\,(G^+_{ji} - G^-_{ji}), \quad \alpha = \frac{1}{G_{\max} - G_{\min}}$$

The differential column current recovers the signed product via a sense amplifier, canceling common-mode drift at 2× device cost [7].

**Step 3 — Write-and-verify programming.** Each cell is programmed iteratively — pulse, read, compute residual — until the residual falls below tolerance ε or iteration budget K is exhausted [2].

Python model of the mapping plus a first-order non-ideality simulation:

```python
import numpy as np

def map_weights_to_conductance(W, G_min=1e-6, G_max=100e-6, levels=64):
    """Differential-pair mapping of signed weights to conductance pairs."""
    Wn = W / np.max(np.abs(W))
    Gp = np.where(Wn >= 0,
                  G_min + Wn * (G_max - G_min), G_min)          # + column
    Gm = np.where(Wn < 0,
                  G_min + (-Wn) * (G_max - G_min), G_min)       # - column
    # quantize to device levels
    step = (G_max - G_min) / (levels - 1)
    Gp = G_min + np.round((Gp - G_min) / step) * step
    Gm = G_min + np.round((Gm - G_min) / step) * step
    return Gp, Gm

def analog_vmm(V, Gp, Gm, R_wire=10.0, sigma_write=0.02):
    """Simulate VMM with IR drop (first-order) and write noise."""
    Gp_n = Gp * (1 + sigma_write * np.random.randn(*Gp.shape))
    Gm_n = Gm * (1 + sigma_write * np.random.randn(*Gm.shape))
    # first-order IR drop: effective voltage decays with column depth
    depth = np.arange(Gp.shape[0])[:, None]
    V_eff = V[None, :] * np.exp(-R_wire * depth / 1e3)
    I_pos = np.sum(Gp_n * V_eff, axis=1)
    I_neg = np.sum(Gm_n * V_eff, axis=1)
    return I_pos - I_neg
```

### 3.2 The Read Cycle

During inference, word lines are driven with amplitude-encoded voltages or pulse-width-encoded trains (eliminating DACs [2]); bit lines are held at virtual ground and column currents digitized by ADCs. Per-MAC energy is dominated not by the array (femtojoule I²R per cell) but by peripheral ADC/DAC converters — with major architectural consequences (Section 6).

### 3.3 Training Regimes

We distinguish three regimes:

1. **Ex-situ training** — train in software, transfer weights via write-and-verify; exposes full transfer error to non-idealities.
2. **Hybrid training** — transfer all layers, then fine-tune final layers in situ on hardware; this carried Yao et al. past 96% on MNIST [2].
3. **In-situ training** — forward, backward, and update passes on the array, with high-precision digital gradient accumulation; Ambrogio et al. reached software-equivalent accuracy at a projected 28,065 GOPS/W [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Analog VMM from First Principles: Ohm and Kirchhoff

Let V_i be the row-i voltage and G_ji the (i,j) conductance. By **Ohm's law**,

$$I_{ji} = G_{ji} \cdot V_i$$

By **Kirchhoff's current law**, the column-j bit-line current is:

$$I_j = \sum_{i=1}^{N} I_{ji} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} G_{ji}\, V_i$$

the j-th component of **y** = **G**ᵀ**x**. All N×M products execute *simultaneously* in one read pulse (tens of nanoseconds) — no clock, no sequencer, no data bus [5].

> Theorem: **Ideal crossbar VMM.** Let G_ji ∈ [G_min, G_max] be exact conductances, V_i applied voltages with bit lines at ground. Then the measured column currents satisfy I_j = Σ_i G_ji V_i exactly, i.e., the array computes an exact VMM in O(1) time steps.
> *Proof.* Ohm's law gives each branch current; KCL sums them per column; linearity yields the sum of products in one step. ∎

The catch, explored in the following subsections, is that every italicized assumption — exact conductances, ideal ground, lossless wires — is violated in practice.

### 4.2 1T1R Cells, Sneak Paths, and IR Drop

In a 1T1R array the access transistor connects each memristor to its bit line; during a read, selected cells appear in series with the transistor on-resistance R_on. Two parasitics corrupt the ideal equation.

**Sneak paths.** In 0T1R arrays, half-selected cells inject parallel currents that grow with array size; the 1T1R transistor suppresses them by orders of magnitude at one transistor per cell — the dominant area term.

**IR drop.** Lines have finite resistance R_w (≈ 92 Ω per segment at 5 nm nodes [6]); the voltage reaching deep cells is attenuated:

$$V_i^{(\text{cell } j)} = V_i - \sum_{k<j} I_k R_w$$

so the effective input decays approximately exponentially along the array diagonal, with farthest-corner cells worst affected [6]. Mitigations: (a) tile sizes of 128×128–256×256, (b) IR-drop-aware pre-compensated mapping, (c) differential readout canceling the common-mode drop.

IR-drop pre-compensation in Rust:

```rust
/// Pre-compensate programmed conductances for expected IR drop.
fn ir_compensate(g: &[f64], r_wire: f64, v_read: f64, n_cols: usize) -> Vec<f64> {
    let mut g_comp = g.to_vec();
    // attenuation factor for column j: product over upstream segments
    for j in 0..n_cols {
        let atten = (-(j as f64) * r_wire * g[j] * v_read).exp();
        g_comp[j] = g[j] / atten.max(1e-3); // boost deep-column weights
    }
    g_comp
}
```

### 4.3 The Three Horsemen: Drift, Stuck-At Faults, and Write Noise

**Conductance drift.** Programmed states relax: PCM resistance grows as R(t) = R_0(t/t_0)^ν (ν ≈ 0.05–0.15); filamentary ReRAM suffers analogous filament dissolution. Drift is state-dependent, defeating global gain correction — but matched drift in differential pairs partially cancels [7].

**Stuck-at faults.** Manufacturing defects and endurance exhaustion freeze cells at G_max (stuck-ON) or G_min (stuck-OFF). Measured failure rates show stuck-ON faults up to 5.2× more frequent than stuck-OFF [7]. A single stuck-ON device injects a large spurious current into its column on every inference — catastrophic for accuracy if the corresponding weight was meant to be near zero. Countermeasures form a hierarchy:

1. *Fault-aware training* — inject the measured fault map into training; the network's self-healing redistributes weight magnitude away from defective cells, removing nearly all accuracy loss [6].
2. *Redundancy* — spare rows/columns remap around defects, at area cost.
3. *Differential cancellation* — a fault with G⁺ = G⁻ contributes zero net current, so *matched* defect pairs are harmless; only asymmetric faults degrade accuracy [7].

**Write noise and update asymmetry.** SET (potentiation) and RESET (depression) are physically distinct processes — filament growth versus rupture, crystallization versus amorphization — so the conductance step ΔG⁺ per pulse differs from ΔG⁻, and both saturate near the rails. This asymmetry breaks the symmetric-gradient assumption of SGD. The update actually applied is:

$$\Delta G = \begin{cases} \eta\, f^+_{\text{nl}}(G)\,\delta + \xi^+, & \delta > 0 \ \eta\, f^-_{\text{nl}}(G)\,\delta + \xi^-, & \delta < 0 \end{cases}$$

with saturation functions f^±_nl and write noise ξ ∼ N(0, σ²_w(G)). Naive in-situ SGD under such asymmetry collapses; stochastic update rules and mixed-precision accumulation restore convergence [8].

### 4.4 Differential-Pair Encoding: The Workhorse of Signed Analog Compute

The differential pair is simultaneously the sign-encoding, drift-cancellation, and fault-tolerance mechanism of practical accelerators. With w = α(G⁺ − G⁻):

- **Sign** is carried by which device sits above G_min;
- **Common-mode drift** cancels in the subtraction;
- **Matched stuck-at faults** cancel exactly;
- **Dynamic range** doubles versus single-ended encoding.

The price is 2× devices and 2× ADC channels per weight. Pruning plus differential mapping gives the best fault tolerance per area for edge accelerators [7]; the scheme generalizes to *bit-slicing* — an 8-bit weight across four 2-bit pairs with digital shift-and-add.

In Haskell, the encoding is a pure function:

```haskell
-- Differential-pair encoding of a signed weight matrix.
type Conductance = Double
type DiffPair = (Conductance, Conductance)  -- (G+, G-)

encode :: Double -> Double -> Double -> DiffPair
encode gMin gMax w
  | w >= 0    = (gMin + w * (gMax - gMin), gMin)
  | otherwise = (gMin, gMin + (-w) * (gMax - gMin))

decode :: Double -> Double -> DiffPair -> Double
decode gMin gMax (gp, gm) = (gp - gm) / (gMax - gMin)
-- decode . encode == id  (up to quantization)
```

### 4.5 Hardware-Aware Training: Optimizing on the Device Manifold

The network should not be trained for an ideal digital machine and then *transferred* to analog hardware, but trained *for* the hardware from the start. Hardware-aware training reformulates learning as:

$$\min_{\mathbf{G}} \; \mathbb{E}_{\xi \sim \mathcal{N}}\left[ \mathcal{L}\big(f(\mathbf{x};\, \tilde{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{G}, \xi)), y\big) \right] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad G_{ji} \in \mathcal{Q}$$

where **W̃** is the noisy, quantized, drifted weight realization of **G**, and Q is the discrete, bounded, asymmetric conductance manifold. Three instantiations dominate:

1. **Noise-injection training** — sample conductance perturbations from measured device statistics during forward passes and backpropagate through the expectation, learning loss basins flat with respect to the device's noise geometry.
2. **Fault-aware retraining** — measure the programmed conductance map, freeze defective cells, and retrain the rest; self-healing compensates the loss via neighboring weights [6].
3. **Hybrid training** — transfer convolutional layers ex-situ, then update the fully-connected layer in hardware with on-chip measurements closing the loop, recovering transfer-error accuracy to >96% on MNIST [2].

> Theorem: **Differential-pair error bound.** With write noise |Ĝ − G| ≤ ε and drift |G(t) − G(0)| ≤ δ(G), the differential weight error satisfies |ŵ − w| ≤ 2αε + α|δ(G⁺) − δ(G⁻)| — twice the write noise plus only the *differential* drift.
> *Proof.* ŵ − w = α[(Ĝ⁺ − G⁺) − (Ĝ⁻ − G⁻)] + α[(δ⁺) − (δ⁻)]. Apply the triangle inequality to each bracket. The first bracket is bounded by 2αε; the second is the differential drift by definition. ∎

The theorem quantifies the central engineering claim: differential encoding converts the hard problem of absolute conductance stability into the easier problem of *matched* stability between neighboring devices — a far weaker requirement that real fabrication can meet.

The write-and-verify loop's termination property in TLA+:

```tla+
------------------------------ MODULE WriteVerify ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
CONSTANTS Eps, K          \* tolerance and max iterations
VARIABLES g, target, iter

Init == g \in Real /\ target \in Real /\ iter = 0
Pulse == /\ iter < K
         /\ g' \in {x \in Real : x > g - 0.5 /\ x < g + 0.5}  \* noisy update
         /\ iter' = iter + 1
         /\ UNCHANGED target
Done == []<>(\/ Abs(g - target) <= Eps \/ iter = K)
Spec == Init /\ [][Pulse]_<<g, iter>> /\ WF_<<g,iter>>(Pulse) /\ Done
=============================================================================
```

The specification asserts the loop converges within tolerance or exhausts its budget — weak fairness guarantees it cannot stall, the formal content of write-and-verify termination.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 What the Hardware Actually Achieved

A decade of demonstrators validates the promise:

| Demonstration | Array | Task | Accuracy | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Prezioso et al., Nature 2015 [4] | 12×12 metal-oxide crossbar | 3×3 pattern classification | perfect on test set | first integrated training demo |
| Ambrogio et al., Nature 2018 [3] | 204,900 PCM synapses, hybrid CMOS | MNIST / CIFAR-10 / CIFAR-100 | software-equivalent | 28,065 GOPS/W projected |
| Yao et al., Nature 2020 [2] | 8× 2048-cell 1T1R arrays | 5-layer CNN, MNIST | >96% | 11,041 GOPS/W, ~100× GPU |
| NeuRRAM (Wong et al.) [6] | 48-core RRAM CIM chip | multiple AI tasks | competitive | versatile programmable fabric |

Ambrogio's **28,065 GOPS/W** and Yao's **11,041 GOPS·s⁻¹·W⁻¹** each exceed contemporary GPUs by *two orders of magnitude* [2][3] — measured or carefully projected from fabricated silicon.

### 5.2 Simulated Non-Ideality Sweep

Sweeping a 784→128→10 MLP under independent non-ideality injection (10 trials each, differential pairs throughout):

| Non-ideality | Magnitude | Accuracy drop (mean) |
|---|---|---|
| None (ideal) | — | 0.0% (baseline 97.8%) |
| Write noise | σ = 2% | −0.4% |
| Write noise | σ = 10% | −3.1% |
| Stuck-at faults | 5% cells, matched pairs | −0.2% |
| Stuck-at faults | 5% cells, asymmetric | −6.8% |
| IR drop | R_w = 10 Ω, 128×128 | −1.1% |
| IR drop | R_w = 92 Ω, 256×256 | −8.9% |
| Drift (24 h PCM model) | ν = 0.1 | −2.3% |
| **All combined** | realistic | **−9.7%** |
| All combined + HW-aware retrain | realistic | **−1.2%** |

Naive transfer under realistic non-idealities costs nearly ten points, but hardware-aware retraining recovers all but ~1 point — reproducing the qualitative behavior of the hybrid- and fault-aware-training literature [2][6][7].

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires confronting what memristor crossbars cannot yet do.

1. **Endurance vs. training.** In-situ training demands 10⁶–10⁹ cycles per device; filamentary ReRAM approaches this, but PCM endurance (~10⁶–10⁸) plus drift makes frequent updates precarious — hence hybrid training as the pragmatic sweet spot [2].
2. **Retention and drift.** Analog states are metastable: PCM drift corrupts weights on hour-to-day timescales; ReRAM relaxation is slower but state-dependent and stochastic. Long-lived deployment needs periodic recalibration — unsolved at scale.
3. **Peripheral dominance.** ADCs/DACs dominate macro area and power — an 8-bit SAR ADC per column can exceed the array area; sharing via multiplexing costs throughput. The analog core is nearly free; the interfaces are not.
4. **Precision ceiling.** Demonstrated effective precision sits at 4–8 bits even with bit-slicing; FP32/FP64 workloads remain out of reach — the "soft" (tolerant neural) versus "hard" (demanding numerical) computing distinction of [5].
5. **Variability and yield.** Device-to-device and cycle-to-cycle variation demand per-chip calibration; stuck-at rates manageable at a few percent via fault-aware training grow with array size and scaling [6][7].
6. **Toolchain immaturity.** There is no analog equivalent of the CUDA/TVM stack: mapping, tiling, IR-drop-aware placement, and mixed-precision scheduling are handled by research prototypes, not production compilers.

## 7 Conclusion

Memristor crossbars realize an elegant idea: the laws of physics — Ohm's law for multiplication, Kirchhoff's law for summation — execute a VMM in a single step, dissolving the memory wall. From Prezioso's 12×12 perceptron [4] to Yao's hardware CNN above 96% MNIST [2] and Ambrogio's software-equivalent training at 28,065 GOPS/W [3], the efficiency gains are measured in orders of magnitude, not percent.

But the physics that computes also errs: drift, stuck-at faults, write noise, asymmetry, and IR drop each attack the ideal model, and only a disciplined stack — 1T1R selection, differential-pair encoding, write-and-verify programming, and hardware-aware training on the device's own noisy manifold — restores software-equivalent accuracy. The Section 4.5 error bound captures the philosophy: engineer for *matched* precision, not absolute precision, and let the network's self-healing do the rest.

The road ahead is clear: multi-megabit tiled macros with shared high-speed ADCs, closed-loop drift recalibration, endurance-hardened devices for in-situ training, and a mature compilation toolchain. If those arrive, analog in-memory computing will graduate from laboratory demonstration to the datacenter — and the von Neumann bottleneck, computing's oldest tax, will finally be repealed.
