# Beyond Reward Models: A Unified Theory of Direct Preference Optimization and Preference-Based Alignment

## Abstract

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) reformulates reinforcement learning from human feedback as a supervised classification problem over pairwise preference data, eliminating the explicit reward model and the online reinforcement learning loop. This thesis develops a unified theoretical account of preference-based alignment. We derive the DPO objective from the Kullback-Leibler-constrained reward maximization problem through an exact reparameterization that expresses the reward function as a scaled log-ratio of optimal and reference policies, and show that the resulting logistic loss shares its global optimizer with its RLHF counterpart under the Bradley-Terry preference model. We analyze DPO's gradient dynamics, characterize the implicit reward it induces, and examine its generalizations: Identity Preference Optimization (IPO), which relaxes the Bradley-Terry assumption; Kahneman-Tversky Optimization (KTO), which handles unary feedback via prospect-theoretic utilities; and reference-free variants ORPO and SimPO. We address the surviving pathologies - reward hacking, overoptimization, length bias, and offline distribution shift - and survey formal results on equivalence, regularization, and sample complexity, closing with the open theoretical problems that remain.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models trained by next-token prediction on web-scale corpora are fluent but unaligned: they complete prompts with statistically likely text rather than the responses a user actually wants - helpful, truthful, and safe [4]. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) became the standard remedy. In the InstructGPT recipe [4], built on Christiano et al. [7], a supervised fine-tuned model is followed by a learned reward model trained on human pairwise comparisons, maximized with PPO under a KL penalty anchoring the policy to the supervised baseline. Strikingly, a 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT was preferred by raters over the 175B GPT-3 [4].

Yet the RLHF pipeline is operationally punishing: four models in memory (policy, reference, reward model, critic), on-policy rollouts during training, and all of PPO's hyperparameter sensitivity. Worse, the reward model is only a proxy, and optimizing it aggressively produces *reward hacking* - degenerate outputs scoring highly under the learned reward while degrading true quality - forcing careful KL-budget tuning [7].

In 2023, Rafailov et al. [1] showed the pipeline collapses: the KL-constrained reward maximization at RLHF's heart admits a closed-form optimal policy, which inverts to express the *reward* as a function of the *policy*. Substituting into the Bradley-Terry model [8] yields a binary classification loss over preference pairs - Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) - whose global optimizer coincides with RLHF's, with no reward model, no rollouts, no RL. The policy itself is the reward model.

This thesis develops a unified theoretical account of that collapse and its aftermath:

1. A self-contained derivation of DPO from the KL-constrained RLHF objective via the reparameterization identity.
2. An analysis of DPO's gradient dynamics and the implicit reward it induces.
3. A comparative theory of the DPO family - IPO [2], KTO [3], ORPO [5], SimPO [6] - as alternative choices of divergence, utility, and data assumption.
4. An account of the surviving pathologies: overoptimization, length bias, likelihood displacement, offline shift.
5. Formal results on equivalence, regularization, and sample complexity, with empirical context.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Preferences and the Bradley-Terry model

Modern alignment begins with triples $(x, y_w, y_l)$: an annotator declares $y_w$ preferable to $y_l$ given $x$. Learning from comparisons needs a probabilistic model of preference given latent quality; the canonical choice is the Bradley-Terry model [8], developed for paired-comparison experiments and familiar as the mathematics behind Elo ratings:

$$P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)\big),$$

where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid and $r(x, y)$ is a latent reward function. The model assumes *transitivity* and *independence of irrelevant alternatives*: preference probability depends only on the reward difference. Real annotators routinely violate both - exhibiting cycles, context effects, and ties - and much of this thesis concerns the consequences (Sections 4.2 and 6). Still, Bradley-Terry remains the lens through which RLHF and DPO interpret preference data, because it converts ranking into a differentiable likelihood.

### 2.2 The RLHF pipeline

RLHF proceeds in three stages [4]:

1. **Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)** on human demonstrations, yielding $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.
2. **Reward modeling**: fit a scalar $r_\phi$ by maximum likelihood under Bradley-Terry:

$$\mathcal{L}_R(r_\phi) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\log \sigma(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l))\big].$$

3. **RL fine-tuning** of the policy against the learned reward with a KL penalty:

$$\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big] - \beta\, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big). \tag{1}$$

The KL term is load-bearing: without it, PPO discovers adversarial gibberish maximizing $r_\phi$ - the *reward hacking* phenomenon documented since [7]. The coefficient $\beta$ trades preference satisfaction against proximity to the SFT model, one of several knobs making RLHF brittle and expensive [4].

A classical calculation, however, reveals structure hidden inside (1). For fixed $r_\phi$ and $\beta$, the KL-constrained objective has a closed-form optimizer:

$$\pi^*(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r_\phi(x,y)\right), \tag{2}$$

with partition function $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(r_\phi(x,y)/\beta)$ - the exponential-tilting solution of KL-regularized maximization. DPO's entire contribution is to read (2) backwards.

## 3 Methodology: Deriving DPO from the RLHF Optimum

### 3.1 The reparameterization identity

Rearranging (2) for the reward gives the **reparameterization identity**:

$$r_\phi(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x). \tag{3}$$

Every KL-regularized optimal policy $\pi^*$ therefore *defines* its reward, up to the prompt constant $\beta\log Z(x)$. The reward-to-policy mapping is bijective (modulo per-prompt shifts): the language model is, in the paper's phrase, "secretly a reward model" [1].

### 3.2 The DPO loss

Inserting (3) into the Bradley-Terry likelihood, the partition function cancels exactly since only reward *differences* matter:

$$P^*(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\!\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right).$$

Replacing $\pi^*$ with the parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$ and maximizing log-likelihood over the preference data yields the DPO objective [1]:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]. \tag{4}$$

This is logistic regression on the *implicit reward margin*: with $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y) = \beta\log(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x))$, (4) is $-\log\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l))$. No reward model, no on-policy sampling, one hyperparameter ($\beta$); DPO matched or exceeded PPO-RLHF on sentiment, summarization, and dialogue at a fraction of the compute [1].

> **Theorem (informal; Rafailov et al. [1], Proposition 1).** *Under the Bradley-Terry model, the global minimizer of (4) coincides with the optimal policy of the KL-constrained RL objective (1) for the maximum-likelihood reward.*

A full statement and proof sketch appear in Section 5.

### 3.3 Gradient dynamics: what DPO actually does to the policy

Differentiating (4) yields the per-example gradient:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}} = -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\Big[\underbrace{\sigma\big(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)\big)}_{\text{weight } w}\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big]. \tag{5}$$

Two features matter: the update *increases* $y_w$'s likelihood while *decreasing* $y_l$'s, and the weight $w = \sigma(\hat{r}_l - \hat{r}_w)$ down-weights already-correct pairs, concentrating learning where the implicit reward disagrees with the data - an *adaptive margin* method.

$\beta$ plays a dual role: the KL penalty strength in (1), and the implicit-reward scale - hence sigmoid sharpness - in (4). Small $\beta$ sharpens preference fitting but weakens the anchor to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, risking degenerate drift; large $\beta$ keeps the policy near the reference at the cost of underfitting. Practical values cluster in $[0.1, 0.5]$ [1].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Every policy is a reward model: the implicit reward

Equation (3) holds for *any* policy: $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y) = \beta\log(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x))$ is the unique reward (up to a prompt constant) making $\pi_\theta$ the KL-constrained optimizer. A DPO-trained model can therefore *serve as its own reward model* - scoring candidates for reranking, best-of-$n$ sampling, or synthetic-data filtering without ever training $r_\phi$ [1].

The identity also reframes alignment geometrically: policies and rewards (modulo per-prompt shifts) are in one-to-one correspondence under the exponential-tilting map (2), so choosing an alignment algorithm is choosing *how to move through policy space* in response to preference data.

One subtlety: because $\hat{r}_\theta$ is defined relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, the implicit reward inherits the reference's blind spots - where $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ assigns negligible probability, the ratio is unreliable. DPO's reward is trustworthy precisely on the reference's support: a first hint of the offline coverage problem of Section 4.5.

### 4.2 IPO: when Bradley-Terry fails

DPO assumes Bradley-Terry correctly describes preferences. With noisy annotators or intransitive preferences, the logistic loss is a liability: for a mislabeled pair, $-\log\sigma(\cdot)$ grows without bound as the margin goes negative, so a few corrupted pairs dominate the gradient and drive overconfident, overfitted margins [2].

Azar et al. [2] abandon Bradley-Terry entirely with $\Psi$PO, an objective optimizing the *probability of being preferred* directly:

$$\max_\pi\; \mathbb{E}_{x,\,y\sim\pi,\,y'\sim\mu}\big[\Psi\big(p^*(y \succ y' \mid x)\big)\big] - \tau^{-1}\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}),$$

where $\mu$ is a reference sampling distribution and $\Psi$ a monotone link. DPO is the logit choice $\Psi(q) = \log\frac{q}{1-q}$, which *implies* Bradley-Terry; the identity $\Psi(q) = q$ yields Identity Preference Optimization (IPO) with a squared-error loss [2]:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IPO}}(\pi_\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\left(\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)} - \frac{1}{2\tau}\right)^2\right]. \tag{6}$$

Instead of pushing the log-ratio margin to infinity, IPO regresses it toward the finite target $1/(2\tau)$: a corrupted pair incurs bounded rather than unbounded influence. The price is slower fitting on clean, strongly-separated data, where DPO's aggressive margin-pushing converges faster [2].

> **Theorem (informal; Azar et al. [2]).** *The IPO objective upper-bounds the KL divergence between the learned policy and the reference, and its unique minimizer is the KL-regularized optimal policy for the preference-probability objective. DPO and IPO are both instances of $\Psi$PO under different link functions $\Psi$.*

$\Psi$PO thus reframes DPO as *one point* in a design space parameterized by $\Psi$ - a perspective organizing the subsequent literature.

### 4.3 Beyond pairs and references: KTO, ORPO, and SimPO

Real feedback is often *unary*: a thumbs-up or thumbs-down on a single response, with no paired alternative, for which the Bradley-Terry likelihood cannot even be written. Kahneman-Tversky Optimization (KTO) [3] replaces the logistic model with a *prospect-theoretic* utility - concave for gains, convex for losses, steeper for losses (*loss aversion*) - applied to the implicit reward relative to a reference point $z_0 = \mathbb{E}[\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})]$. The loss needs only binary desirability labels:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KTO}} = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\big[\lambda - v(x,y)\big],$$

where $v$ is the prospect value and $\lambda$ encodes loss aversion. KTO matches DPO from unpaired binary judgments alone - far cheaper to collect than pairwise rankings [3].

Odds-Ratio Preference Optimization (ORPO) [5] attacks a different inefficiency: DPO requires a separate SFT stage to produce $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. ORPO folds alignment into SFT itself by augmenting the negative log-likelihood with an odds-ratio penalty,

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ORPO}} = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}} + \lambda \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OR}}\big], \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OR}} = -\log\sigma\!\left(\log\frac{\mathrm{odds}_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\mathrm{odds}_\theta(y_l\mid x)}\right),$$

with $\mathrm{odds}_\theta(y\mid x) = \pi_\theta(y\mid x)/(1-\pi_\theta(y\mid x))$. No reference model is loaded - halving memory, unifying the pipeline - at the cost of the KL anchor, so the preference term must be balanced against the SFT term [5].

SimPO [6] likewise drops the reference model but keeps two stages, replacing the implicit reward with a *length-normalized* log-likelihood $\hat{r}(x,y) = \frac{\beta}{|y|}\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ plus target margin $\gamma$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SimPO}} = -\mathbb{E}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\frac{\beta}{|y_w|}\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \frac{\beta}{|y_l|}\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x) - \gamma\right)\right].$$

Length normalization directly combats DPO's verbosity bias (Section 4.4), and dropping $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ cuts memory; SimPO has matched or beaten DPO on AlpacaEval and ArenaHard [6].

| Method | Needs $\pi_{\text{ref}}$? | Data | Key idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| DPO [1] | Yes | Pairs $(y_w, y_l)$ | Logistic loss on implicit-reward margin |
| IPO [2] | Yes | Pairs | Squared loss; robust to BT misspecification |
| KTO [3] | Yes | Unary good/bad | Prospect-theoretic utility, loss aversion |
| ORPO [5] | No | Pairs | Odds-ratio term fused with SFT |
| SimPO [6] | No | Pairs | Length-normalized reward + margin $\gamma$ |

### 4.4 Pathologies that survive the collapse

Eliminating the explicit reward model does not eliminate Goodhart's law; it relocates it. The implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ is still a proxy, with well-documented pathologies:

**Likelihood displacement.** The gradient (5) increases the *margin* $\hat{r}_w - \hat{r}_l$, achievable by decreasing both $\log\pi_\theta(y_w)$ and $\log\pi_\theta(y_l)$ so long as the rejected response falls faster. Empirically, DPO training often *reduces* the absolute likelihood of the chosen responses - the model becomes relatively more preferring yet absolutely less fluent on its own training examples. Likelihood-preservation terms and reward normalization (e.g., SimPO's margin [6]) mitigate this.

**Length bias.** Because sequence log-probability sums over tokens, longer responses accumulate margin more easily. DPO-trained models grow systematically verbose, and human raters - who conflate length with quality - reinforce the bias. Length normalization as in SimPO [6] is the standard correction.

**Overoptimization of the implicit reward.** Just as PPO over-optimizes $r_\phi$, aggressive DPO over-optimizes $\hat{r}_\theta$: the policy drifts off the reference's support where the implicit reward is uncalibrated, and true quality degrades while the proxy margin keeps rising. The KL anchor $\beta$ is the only brake - hence $\beta$ tuning remains critical despite DPO's one-knob reputation [1].

### 4.5 Online versus offline: the distribution-shift gap

DPO is an *offline* algorithm: it learns from a fixed dataset $\mathcal{D}$ drawn from a behavior distribution $\mu$. But the implicit reward is only reliable on $\pi_{\text{ref}}$'s support, and as $\pi_\theta$ moves away from $\mu$, the loss optimizes preferences over responses the data never covered. Offline guarantees therefore require a *coverage* assumption - that $\mu$ places sufficient mass wherever the optimal policy does - with sample complexity scaling in the concentrability coefficient. Without coverage, offline DPO confidently prefers responses where its implicit reward is pure extrapolation.

*Online* (iterative) variants close the loop: sample $y \sim \pi_\theta$, obtain fresh preferences, and retrain. This restores the exploration missing from offline DPO - the preference-learning analogue of on-policy RL. The cost is per-iteration annotation, but the payoff is regret bounds offline methods cannot achieve, since only preferences on the policy's own outputs are ever needed. The consensus: offline DPO is an excellent *first* step (cheap, stable, strong); online iteration is required for continued gains - mirroring behavior cloning versus full RL [2].

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Equivalence of DPO and KL-constrained RLHF

> **Theorem 1 (Rafailov et al. [1], Proposition 1).** *Assume preferences follow the Bradley-Terry model with the maximum-likelihood reward $r^*$. Then the policy minimizing the DPO loss (4) is identical to the policy maximizing the KL-constrained RL objective (1) under $r^*$.*

*Proof sketch.* The MLE reward $r^*$ maximizes the BT likelihood over $\mathcal{D}$, with RL optimum (2). By (3), any policy's implicit reward differs from $r^*$ only by a per-prompt constant, which cancels in preference probabilities - so maximizing BT likelihood over rewards equals maximizing it over policies, i.e., the DPO objective (4). The exponential-tilting map (2) is bijective between rewards (modulo constants) and policies, hence the optimizers coincide. ∎

The theorem demands that BT *perfectly* models preferences and that the reward class is unrestricted. Both fail in practice - precisely why IPO [2] and $\Psi$PO exist.

### 5.2 Regularization and the IPO bound

> **Theorem 2 (Azar et al. [2], informal).** *The IPO loss (6) is uniquely minimized by the policy that maximizes the preference-probability objective under KL regularization, and along the optimization path the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})$ is controlled by the regularization parameter $\tau$.*

Whereas DPO's logistic loss lets the implicit-reward margin - and with it the KL from the reference - grow without bound on noisy data, IPO's quadratic target $1/(2\tau)$ acts as a soft ceiling: bounded influence implies bounded drift.

### 5.3 Sample complexity

Under a linear reward $r(x,y) = \langle\phi(x,y), w^*\rangle$ of dimension $d$ and a coverage coefficient $C$ bounding the density ratio between the optimal policy and the data distribution, offline DPO-style estimators achieve

$$N \;=\; \tilde{O}\!\left(\frac{C\, d}{\varepsilon^2}\right)$$

preference pairs to reach $\varepsilon$-suboptimality - the familiar parametric rate, inflated by coverage. Online variants replace $C$ with an eluder-dimension-like quantity over the policy's own trajectory, yielding $\tilde{O}(d\sqrt{T})$ regret without global coverage. These rates explain *why* offline DPO plateaus: once the policy exhausts $\mathcal{D}$'s coverage, further gradient steps fit noise, and only fresh on-policy data helps.

### 5.4 Empirical vignettes

The original DPO paper [1] evaluated sentiment control, TL;DR summarization, and Anthropic Helpful-Harmless dialogue; in each, DPO matched or exceeded PPO-RLHF in a single stable training phase. Subsequent work is consistent: KTO [3] reaches DPO-level win rates from strictly weaker unary supervision; ORPO [5] shows folding SFT and alignment into one stage need not sacrifice quality; SimPO [6] tops DPO on AlpacaEval 2 and ArenaHard while using less memory, implicating length bias as a first-order confound. The through-line: the *data and its biases* dominate the choice of loss - a humbling lesson for theorists.

A minimal reference implementation (sequence log-probs precomputed):

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def dpo_loss(pi_logps_w, pi_logps_l, ref_logps_w, ref_logps_l, beta=0.1):
    """DPO loss, Eq. (4). All inputs: (batch,) tensors of summed log-probs."""
    logr_w = pi_logps_w - ref_logps_w   # log pi_theta(y_w|x) / pi_ref(y_w|x)
    logr_l = pi_logps_l - ref_logps_l
    logits = beta * (logr_w - logr_l)    # implicit-reward margin
    return -F.logsigmoid(logits).mean()

def implicit_reward(pi_logps, ref_logps, beta=0.1):
    """The policy-as-reward-model: r_hat(x, y), Eq. (3) up to a constant."""
    return beta * (pi_logps - ref_logps)
```

Classic silent bug: `pi_logps` must *sum* per-token log-probs *excluding* padding, under teacher forcing on the exact response tokens - including prompt log-probs or padding silently corrupts the margin.

## 6 Limitations

**The Bradley-Terry straitjacket.** DPO inherits transitivity, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and the reduction of rich judgment to a binary coin flip per pair. Ties, "both bad" pairs, and cyclic preferences are discarded or shoehorned into the logistic likelihood, where they exert outsized influence [2], [8].

**Noise intolerance.** The logistic loss is unbounded for mislabeled pairs, so DPO overfits annotation noise into overconfident margins - the raison d'etre of IPO's bounded quadratic loss [2].

**Verbosity and likelihood displacement.** DPO favors longer responses and can *decrease* the absolute probability of chosen responses while increasing their margin (Section 4.4): higher implicit reward need not mean better generations - the proxy problem moved inside the policy, unsolved.

**Offline coverage.** All offline guarantees assume the data distribution covers the optimal policy's support. In practice the policy drifts off-support, the implicit reward extrapolates, and performance plateaus - the fundamental argument for online iteration (Section 4.5).

**No calibrated scalar reward.** The implicit reward is defined only up to a per-prompt constant relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, making it awkward for inference-time uses like best-of-$n$ reranking across prompts, where calibrated absolute scores matter.

**Hyperparameter sensitivity.** Despite its "one-knob" reputation, $\beta$ interacts strongly with dataset size, pair difficulty, and model scale: too small and the policy collapses into degenerate high-margin modes, too large and preferences are underfit [1].

## 7 Conclusion

DPO began as a mathematical observation - the KL-constrained RLHF optimum inverts to read rewards off policies - and became a research program. The reparameterization identity (3) is the field's central equation: it collapses reward modeling into the policy, converts RL into classification, and reveals DPO, IPO, KTO, ORPO, SimPO as alternative geometries on one policy-reward manifold [1], [2], [3], [5], [6].

Yet the collapse is not a conclusion. The pathologies of RLHF - overoptimization, bias exploitation, distributional shift - survive inside the implicit reward, and the theory surveyed here shows they are *structural*, not artifacts of PPO. The frontier: rigorous online preference learning with exploration guarantees, alignment under intransitive or pluralistic preferences violating Bradley-Terry [8], debiased objectives disentangling quality from verbosity, and a scaling theory predicting when offline losses plateau. The reward model is dead; long live the questions it was hiding.
