# Particle-in-Cell Simulation of Vlasov–Maxwell Plasmas for Magnetic Confinement Fusion: Boris Pushers, Charge-Conserving Deposition, and Tokamak Edge Turbulence

## Abstract

The particle-in-cell (PIC) method is the dominant algorithmic framework for first-principles kinetic simulation of collisionless plasmas in magnetic confinement fusion (MCF). This thesis presents a rigorous treatment of electromagnetic PIC simulation of the Vlasov–Maxwell system, covering the Boris particle pusher, charge-conserving current deposition in the formulation of Esirkepov, Yee-grid finite-difference time-domain Maxwell solvers, and their application to tokamak edge and scrape-off-layer (SOL) turbulence. We derive the discrete equations, analyze numerical stability, energy–momentum conservation properties, and the numerical Cherenkov instability; we establish the standard verification ladder of linear Landau damping and the two-stream instability; and we survey the δ*f* variance-reduction method, gyrokinetic ordering and its PIC realizations (XGC, GTC, ORB5), and GPU-accelerated exascale implementations. The thesis argues that charge-conserving deposition is the single most consequential algorithmic choice in modern electromagnetic PIC, and that total-*f* gyrokinetic PIC, deployed on exascale hardware, is the only presently credible route to predictive whole-volume tokamak modeling.

## 1. Introduction

The hot, magnetized plasmas of magnetic confinement fusion devices are *collisionless* on the timescales of interest: the Coulomb collision time exceeds the electron plasma period ω<sub>pe</sub><sup>−1</sup> by many orders of magnitude. Kinetic theory, not fluid theory, is therefore the appropriate first-principles description. The governing system is the Vlasov–Maxwell set of equations: the collisionless Vlasov equation describes the evolution of the phase-space distribution function *f*<sub>*s*</sub>(**x**, **v**, *t*) of each species *s*, while Maxwell's equations determine the self-consistent electromagnetic fields.

Analytic solution is impossible for nonlinear, multi-scale configurations such as the tokamak edge, where steep pedestals, open and closed field-line regions, neutral recycling, and turbulent filaments coexist across five or more decades of spatiotemporal scale. The particle-in-cell method replaces the continuous distribution with a finite sample of *macro-particles*, evolves their characteristics through the Lorentz force, and couples them to field equations discretized on a grid. The algorithm is conceptually simple — *deposit, solve, gather, push* — yet its numerical analysis spans symplectic structure, discrete continuity equations, dispersion-modified stability limits, and variance scaling of Monte Carlo noise.

This thesis develops the modern electromagnetic PIC algorithm from first principles, with three organizing questions:

1. **Which discrete structures must the algorithm preserve?** The Boris pusher preserves phase-space volume; Esirkepov's deposition scheme preserves the discrete continuity equation exactly; the Yee grid preserves the correct dispersion of Maxwell's equations. Each is analyzed in turn.
2. **How is correctness established?** Through the canonical benchmark hierarchy: linear Landau damping, the two-stream instability, and the numerical Cherenkov instability, each providing quantitative, falsifiable predictions.
3. **How does PIC reach the tokamak edge?** Via δ*f* noise reduction, gyrokinetic order reduction, and petascale/exascale implementations such as XGC, which have demonstrated first-principles low-to-high (L–H) confinement mode bifurcation [5][8].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Vlasov–Maxwell System

For each species *s* with charge *q*<sub>*s*</sub> and mass *m*<sub>*s*</sub>, the distribution function *f*<sub>*s*</sub>(**x**, **v**, *t*) evolves by

$$\frac{\partial f_s}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla_\mathbf{x} f_s + \frac{q_s}{m_s}\left(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\right)\cdot\nabla_\mathbf{v} f_s = 0 \tag{1}$$

coupled to Maxwell's equations

$$\begin{align}
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} &= \rho/\epsilon_0, \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0 \
\nabla\times\mathbf{E} &= -\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}, \qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J} + \mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}}{\partial t}. \tag{2}
\end{align}$$

The Vlasov equation is a statement of *Liouville's theorem in phase space*: the distribution is constant along particle trajectories, which are characteristics of the PDE. PIC exploits this structure directly — the particles follow characteristics, and the fields mediate the mean-field coupling.

### 2.2 The PIC Algorithm Cycle

The standard electromagnetic PIC cycle per timestep Δ*t* is:

1. **Charge and current deposition**: interpolate particle positions (and velocities) onto grid charge density ρ and current density **J**.
2. **Field solve**: advance **E** and **B** via the discretized Maxwell equations.
3. **Field gather**: interpolate fields back to particle positions.
4. **Particle push**: integrate the Lorentz equation of motion for each macro-particle.

This thesis examines steps 2–4 with full analytic rigor and treats step 1 through the lens of *exact discrete charge conservation*.

### 2.3 Stability Constraints

Explicit electromagnetic PIC is constrained by the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition for the field solver and by plasma-physics resolution requirements:

| Constraint | Requirement | Physical origin |
|---|---|---|
| CFL | *c*Δ*t* < Δ*x*/√*d* | Light-wave propagation on the Yee grid |
| Plasma frequency | ω<sub>pe</sub>Δ*t* ≲ 0.2 | Resolve electron oscillations |
| Debye length | Δ*x* ≲ λ<sub>D</sub> | Avoid finite-grid (grid-heating) instability |
| Cyclotron frequency | ω<sub>ce</sub>Δ*t* ≲ 0.3 | Resolve gyro-motion with Boris push |
| Skin depth | Δ*x* ≲ *c*/ω<sub>pe</sub> | Resolve electromagnetic plasma modes |

Violating the Debye-length constraint triggers the *finite-grid instability* — aliasing of grid modes into particle motion — which remains the most common silent failure mode in production PIC runs.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Boris Particle Pusher

The relativistic Lorentz equation of motion,

$$\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt} = \frac{q}{m}\left(\mathbf{E} + \frac{\mathbf{u}}{\gamma}\times\mathbf{B}\right), \qquad \mathbf{u} = \gamma\mathbf{v}, \tag{3}$$

is discretized by the Boris (1970) scheme, which separates electric acceleration from magnetic rotation. Given proper velocity **u**<sup>*n*−1/2</sup> and fields at time *n*:

$$\begin{align}
\mathbf{u}^{-} &= \mathbf{u}^{n-1/2} + \frac{q\Delta t}{2m}\mathbf{E}^n, \
\mathbf{u}' &= \mathbf{u}^{-} + \mathbf{u}^{-}\times\mathbf{t}, \qquad \mathbf{t} = \frac{q\Delta t}{2m\gamma^n}\mathbf{B}^n, \
\mathbf{u}^{+} &= \mathbf{u}^{-} + \mathbf{u}'\times\mathbf{s}, \qquad \mathbf{s} = \frac{2\mathbf{t}}{1+t^2}, \
\mathbf{u}^{n+1/2} &= \mathbf{u}^{+} + \frac{q\Delta t}{2m}\mathbf{E}^n. \tag{4}
\end{align}$$

The magnetic step is a *pure rotation* of the velocity vector — exactly norm-preserving, hence |**u**| (and the Lorentz factor γ) is unchanged by the magnetic field, as required physically. The scheme is second-order accurate, time-centered, and volume-preserving in phase space; its long-time energy behavior is bounded rather than secularly drifting, a consequence of its symplectic-like structure.

> **Theorem (Boris magnetic rotation):** The map **u**<sup>−</sup> → **u**<sup>+</sup> defined by (4) is a proper rotation of ℝ³ through angle 2·arctan|**t**| about the **B**-axis, and is exactly orthogonal. In particular |**u**<sup>+</sup>| = |**u**<sup>−</sup>| for all Δ*t*.

The Boris pusher has competitors — notably the Vay (2008) pusher [2], which correctly handles the **E**+**v**×**B** cancellation in ultrarelativistic regimes where Boris's velocity-dependent rotation axis introduces error — but Boris remains the default for MCF applications where *v*/*c* ≪ 1.

```python
def boris_push(u, E, B, q_over_m, dt):
    """Boris particle pusher: leapfrog, second-order, norm-preserving."""
    um = u + 0.5 * q_over_m * dt * E          # first half E-kick
    gamma = np.sqrt(1.0 + np.dot(um, um) / c**2)
    t = 0.5 * q_over_m * dt * B / gamma        # rotation vector
    uprime = um + np.cross(um, t)
    s = 2.0 * t / (1.0 + np.dot(t, t))
    up = um + np.cross(uprime, s)              # magnetic rotation
    return up + 0.5 * q_over_m * dt * E        # second half E-kick
```

### 3.2 The Yee Grid Maxwell Solver

Yee's (1966) staggered grid [3] discretizes **E** on cell edges and **B** on cell faces, with electric and magnetic components offset by half a cell and half a timestep. The update equations

$$\begin{align}
\mathbf{E}^{n+1} &= \mathbf{E}^n + \frac{\Delta t}{\epsilon_0}\left(\nabla_h\times\mathbf{B}^{n+1/2} - \mathbf{J}^{n+1/2}\right), \
\mathbf{B}^{n+3/2} &= \mathbf{B}^{n+1/2} - \Delta t\,\nabla_h\times\mathbf{E}^{n+1}, \tag{5}
\end{align}$$

are second-order accurate in space and time, naturally divergence-preserving for **B**, and free of the spurious modes that afflict collocated discretizations. The staggered arrangement means that the discrete curl of **E** lives exactly where **B** lives, and vice versa — the fundamental mimetic property that makes FDTD the standard choice in electromagnetic PIC.

### 3.3 Esirkepov Charge-Conserving Current Deposition

If the deposited current **J** does not satisfy the discrete continuity equation

$$\frac{\rho^{n+1} - \rho^n}{\Delta t} + \nabla_h\cdot\mathbf{J}^{n+1/2} = 0, \tag{6}$$

then Gauss's law ∇·**E** = ρ/ε₀ is violated at every timestep, accumulating a non-physical electrostatic component that no Poisson correction can reliably remove in a production run. Esirkepov's (2001) scheme [1] constructs, for arbitrary-order particle shape factors, a current density that satisfies (6) *exactly* on the Yee grid by decomposing each particle's motion into a sequence of axis-aligned virtual displacements and assigning each segment's current to the staggered faces it crosses. Combined with Villasenor–Buneman for linear shapes and generalized to third- and fourth-order form factors (PQS, PCS), Esirkepov's method is the rigorous answer to the oldest consistency problem in electromagnetic PIC.

### 3.4 The δ*f* Method

Standard ("full-*f*") PIC samples the *entire* distribution with macro-particles, so the statistical noise in moments scales as 1/√*N*<sub>p</sub> relative to the *full* density — catastrophic when the physics of interest (micro-turbulence) is a 10<sup>−3</sup>–10<sup>−4</sup> perturbation. The δ*f* method splits

$$f = f_0 + \delta f, \tag{7}$$

where *f*₀ is a known equilibrium (typically Maxwellian) handled analytically, and only the perturbation δ*f* is sampled by weighted macro-particles with weights *w* = δ*f*/*f*. The noise then scales with |δ*f*|, yielding a signal-to-noise improvement of order *f*₀/|δ*f*| — often three to four orders of magnitude. The method is essential to gyrokinetic turbulence simulation and is reviewed in its total-*f* hybrid form in §4.4.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Spectral Anatomy of the Numerical Cherenkov Instability

The most dangerous numerical artifact of relativistic electromagnetic PIC is the *numerical Cherenkov instability* (NCI): the Yee solver's light waves are subluminal on the grid (phase velocity < *c* for oblique modes), so a relativistic particle beam resonantly emits spurious grid radiation, seeding exponential growth that masquerades as physical instability. The analysis proceeds through the *numerical dispersion relation*, in which aliased beam modes ω − *k*·**v**<sub>b</sub> = 2π*m*/Δ*t* intersect the subluminal electromagnetic branch. Mitigations form a hierarchy:

1. **Lehe's solver** [4]: modifies the finite-difference stencil coefficients to restore superluminal phase velocity along the beam axis, eliminating the resonance for propagation along one chosen direction.
2. **Spectral (PSATD) solvers**: advance Maxwell's equations analytically in Fourier space, yielding the exact vacuum dispersion ω = *ck* and removing the NCI entirely at the cost of global communication.
3. **Digital filtering and high-order shapes**: raise the effective aliasing cutoff, suppressing but not eliminating the growth.

For MCF applications with *v*/*c* ≪ 1 the NCI is subdominant, but any PIC code with relativistic electrons (runaway-electron studies, RF heating) must address it explicitly.

### 4.2 Finite-Grid Instability and Energy Conservation

When Δ*x* > λ<sub>D</sub>, aliasing couples the grid-scale Langmuir modes into a non-physical heating channel: the plasma *self-heats* until the Debye length grows to match the grid. This is not a Courant-type blowup but a slow, silent corruption — the worst kind. Two cures exist: resolution (the honest cure) and *energy-conserving* PIC formulations, which discretize the particle–field coupling so that a discrete total energy is exactly conserved, converting would-be heating into bounded oscillation. The trade-off is a modified dispersion relation and, typically, momentum non-conservation. Production MCF codes choose resolution; energy-conserving variants are preferred in astrophysical contexts where Debye-length resolution is hopeless.

### 4.3 Gyrokinetic Ordering and the Cancellation Problem

For phenomena with ω ≪ ω<sub>ci</sub> (drift waves, ITG/TEM turbulence), resolving the ion gyro-orbit is pure overhead: the gyrokinetic ordering averages over the gyro-angle, reducing 6D phase space to 5D (guiding-center position plus parallel velocity and magnetic moment). The electromagnetic gyrokinetic system, however, suffers the *cancellation problem*: the adiabatic electron response nearly cancels in Ampère's law, leaving a small residual computed as the difference of two large, noisy terms. The explicit mixed-variable/pullback formulation resolves this by splitting the vector potential into adiabatic and non-adiabatic parts, enabling the total-*f* electromagnetic XGC algorithm [5] — the first to combine electromagnetic fluctuations with the separatrix, SOL, and neutral recycling in a whole-volume model.

### 4.4 Total-*f* versus δ*f*: The XGC Approach to the Tokamak Edge

The tokamak edge defeats pure δ*f*: the distribution near the separatrix is strongly non-Maxwellian (loss-cone, X-point orbit loss), so no analytic *f*₀ is adequate. XGC's *total-f* scheme [5][8] combines a δ*f*-like low-noise treatment of the fine-grained distribution with a coarse-grained 5D Eulerian evolution of the background, evaluated on an unstructured triangular mesh conforming to the magnetic equilibrium and vessel wall, with finite-element field solves via PETSc. The payoff is historic: using ~90% of the 27-petaflop Titan machine, XGC achieved the first first-principles demonstration of the L–H mode bifurcation, identifying the synergy between Reynolds-stress-driven and X-point orbit-loss-driven sheared flows [8]. Whole-volume ITER modeling is projected to require *trillions* of marker particles [7].

### 4.5 Exascale PIC: GPUs, AMR, and Performance Portability

Electromagnetic PIC is an ideal GPU workload — the particle push is embarrassingly parallel, and deposition is a scatter-add amenable to atomic or tiled algorithms — but only if the implementation is performance-portable across vendor architectures. The Exascale Computing Project's WarpX code demonstrates the modern design: block-structured adaptive mesh refinement (AMR), spectral and FDTD Maxwell solvers, Esirkepov deposition, and a single-source C++/Kokkos-style portability layer achieving near-roofline performance on NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel GPUs. PIConGPU pioneered GPU PIC a decade earlier and remains the reference for performance-portable pushers, solvers, and higher-order form factors on accelerators [6]. The architectural lesson is uniform: *separate the physics specification from the execution mapping*, and the same algorithmic core — Boris, Yee, Esirkepov — runs from laptops to exascale.

## 5. Empirical Results and Benchmarks

### 5.1 Linear Landau Damping

The definitive verification of any electrostatic PIC implementation is linear Landau damping of a Langmuir wave. Initializing a Maxwellian plasma with a density perturbation δ*n*/*n*₀ = *A* cos(*kx*), kinetic theory predicts exponential decay of the field energy at the Landau rate

$$\gamma_L = -\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8}}\,\frac{\omega_{pe}}{|k|^3\lambda_D^3}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2k^2\lambda_D^2} - \frac{3}{2}\right), \tag{8}$$

for *k*λ<sub>D</sub> ≪ 1. A correct implementation reproduces the theoretical rate to within a few percent over many e-foldings; *under-resolved* velocity space or excessive particle noise produces premature saturation or a spurious growth phase. This single test simultaneously validates deposition, field solve, gather, push, and noise properties — it is the PIC community's *sine qua non*.

### 5.2 The Two-Stream Instability

Two counter-streaming electron beams with drift ±*v*₀ are linearly unstable with maximum growth rate γ ≈ ω<sub>pe</sub>/2√2 at *kv*₀/ω<sub>pe</sub> ≈ 1/√2. The benchmark verifies that the code captures the correct linear growth *and* the nonlinear saturation mechanism — particle trapping and phase-space vortex (BGK) formation. It is the minimal test of a code's ability to handle kinetic instability without artificial dissipation.

### 5.3 Verification Ladder for Tokamak PIC

| Level | Test | What it proves |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Landau damping, two-stream | Kinetic correctness of the core loop |
| 2 | Cold-plasma dispersion, light-wave propagation | Maxwell solver fidelity |
| 3 | Energy/momentum conservation to round-off | Discretization consistency |
| 4 | Cyclotron orbits, **E**×**B** drift, banana orbits | Guiding-center correctness |
| 5 | ITG/TEM linear growth vs. eigenvalue codes (GENE, GS2) | Gyrokinetic physics |
| 6 | L–H bifurcation, heat-flux width scaling | Predictive edge capability [5][7] |

Skipping levels is the standard route to publishing artifacts instead of physics.

```rust
// Sketch: charge-conserving Esirkepov deposition (x-direction, 1D slice)
fn deposit_jx(jx: &mut [f64], x_old: f64, x_new: f64, q: f64, dt: f64, dx: f64) {
    // virtual displacement split at cell boundaries; each segment's
    // current is assigned to the staggered face it crosses, so that
    // (rho_new - rho_old)/dt + div_h(J) == 0 holds exactly.
    let mut xr = x_old;
    while (xr - x_new).abs() > 1e-14 {
        let face = ((xr / dx).floor() + 0.5) * dx; // nearest staggered face
        let j = q * (x_seg - xr) / (dt * dx);      // current of this segment
        let idx = (face / dx).round() as usize;
        jx[idx] += j;
        xr = x_seg;
    }
}
```

## 6. Limitations

1. **Particle noise.** PIC is a Monte Carlo method: moment noise decays only as 1/√*N*<sub>p</sub>. Even with δ*f*, electromagnetic edge simulations require 10<sup>9</sup>–10<sup>12</sup> markers, dominating computational cost. Noise also seeds spurious transport if uncontrolled.
2. **Collisions.** The Vlasov model is collisionless; adding Fokker–Planck collisions (Monte Carlo Langevin operators) is standard but introduces its own noise and timestep constraints. Neoclassical transport in the edge *requires* them.
3. **Scale separation.** A full-*f* PIC of a tokamak resolving λ<sub>D</sub> and ω<sub>pe</sub> while simulating energy-confinement timescales is infeasible; gyrokinetic, drift-kinetic, and hybrid reductions are not optional conveniences but necessities.
4. **The cancellation problem** in electromagnetic gyrokinetics restricts algorithm choice; implicit and mixed-variable formulations add complexity and cost.
5. **Boundary physics.** The SOL couples plasma to material walls: sputtering, recycling, and sheath physics are modeled crudely (logical sheath, Monte Carlo neutrals) relative to the kinetic fidelity of the core algorithm.
6. **Verification gaps.** Nonlinear, whole-volume edge turbulence has no analytic solution; validation rests on inter-code benchmarks (XGC vs. GENE, GTC, GEM, ORB5, EUTERPE [5]) and experimental comparison, both weaker than the linear-theory ladder of §5.

## 7. Conclusion

Particle-in-cell simulation of Vlasov–Maxwell plasmas rests on three discrete pillars — the Boris pusher's volume-preserving rotation, Esirkepov's exactly charge-conserving deposition, and the Yee grid's mimetic Maxwell solver — each preserving a structural property of the continuous system that naive discretization destroys [1][2][3]. Validated against Landau damping and the two-stream instability, this algorithmic core scales from textbook benchmarks to the most demanding problem in computational plasma physics: the tokamak edge. There, δ*f* and total-*f* methods tame the noise, gyrokinetic ordering tames the timescales, and exascale GPU implementations such as XGC and WarpX supply the raw throughput — culminating in first-principles predictions of the L–H transition and ITER divertor heat loads [5][7][8]. The frontier is now electromagnetic, whole-volume, multi-physics PIC: kinetic plasma, neutrals, and wall interactions in a single self-consistent simulation. The algorithms of the 1960s–2000s, running on the machines of the 2020s, remain the sharpest tool we have.
