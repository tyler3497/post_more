# Satellite Quantum Key Distribution: Decoy-State BB84, Orbital Link Budgets, and Trusted-Node Architectures

## Abstract
Satellite quantum key distribution (SatQKD) extends information-theoretic secure key exchange to intercontinental distances by exploiting the near-vacuum propagation of optical photons through most of the orbital path. This thesis examines three pillars of practical SatQKD: the decoy-state BB84 protocol that defeats photon-number-splitting attacks with weak coherent pulses; orbital link-budget engineering for low Earth orbit downlinks and uplinks, where diffraction, atmospheric turbulence, and pointing error partition roughly 40–70 dB of channel loss; and trusted-node relay architectures exemplified by the Micius mission, which generated shared keys between stations 7,600 km apart by XORing separately distributed keys aboard the satellite. We analyze finite-key security bounds, side-channel vulnerabilities such as laser-desynchronization distinguishability, and the trade-offs between trusted, untrusted, and mixed relay networks. Empirical results from the 2016–2020 Micius campaigns and emulated-overpass experiments anchor the theoretical treatment in demonstrated performance.

## 1. Introduction

Quantum key distribution (QKD) promises *information-theoretic* security: a shared secret whose confidentiality rests on the laws of quantum mechanics rather than on unproven computational hardness assumptions. The Bennett–Brassard 1984 (BB84) protocol encodes key bits in non-orthogonal quantum states, so any eavesdropper, conventionally named Eve, who attempts to learn the key inevitably disturbs the carriers and betrays her presence. Yet ideal single-photon sources remain laboratory exotica. Nearly every deployed QKD system instead attenuates laser pulses to *weak coherent states* with mean photon number below unity, inheriting a multiphoton component that opens the door to the **photon-number-splitting (PNS) attack**: Eve blocks single-photon pulses, splits one photon from each multiphoton pulse, and forwards the remainder to Bob, learning the key without introducing errors. Without countermeasures, weak-coherent-pulse BB84 is secure only over a handful of kilometers, which is precisely the distance regime where it is least needed.

Two developments broke this impasse. First, the **decoy-state method**, proposed by Hwang and brought to practical form by Lo, Ma, and Chen, allows Alice to intersperse pulses of different mean intensities so that the photon-number-dependent channel yield and error rate can be tightly estimated, restoring unconditional security at long distances with *essentially unchanged hardware* [1][2]. Second, satellites moved the channel itself: in a space-to-ground link, most of the propagation path is through vacuum with negligible loss, so the exponential attenuation that caps fiber QKD at a few hundred kilometers is replaced by a polynomial free-space spreading loss, and a single low Earth orbit (LEO) satellite can reach ground stations thousands of kilometers apart.

This thesis unifies these threads into a single engineering narrative: how decoy-state BB84 works and why it is secure, how the satellite-to-ground link budget is computed and managed, and how trusted-node relay networks turn point-to-point orbital QKD sessions into a global key-exchange fabric. We ground every major claim in published experimental records, most notably the Chinese Micius quantum science satellite (2016–), the first mission to demonstrate satellite-to-ground decoy-state QKD, intercontinental trusted-relay key distribution, and entanglement distribution beyond 1,000 km [7].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 BB84 and the PNS vulnerability

In BB84, Alice prepares qubits in one of two conjugate bases — rectilinear ($Z = {|H\rangle, |V\rangle}$) or diagonal ($X = {|+\rangle, |-\rangle}$) — chosen at random, and Bob measures in a randomly chosen basis. After sifting (discarding mismatched-basis events), error correction, and privacy amplification, they share a secret key whose security was rigorously proven by Shor–Preskill and extended by Gottesman, Lo, Lütkenhaus, and Preskill (GLLP) to imperfect devices.

The GLLP analysis exposes the weak-coherent-pulse pathology. A coherent state $|\alpha\rangle$ with intensity $\mu = |\alpha|^2$ follows a Poisson photon-number distribution

$$p_n(\mu) = \frac{\mu^n e^{-\mu}}{n!},$$

so the multiphoton fraction $p_{\text{multi}} \approx \mu^2/2$ is nonzero for any $\mu > 0$. In a lossy channel, Eve performs the PNS attack: she measures photon number nondemolitionally, suppresses single-photon pulses, and splits multiphoton ones. Her attack is undetectable because the *observed* gain and error rate can still match the channel's natural values. The GLLP secure key rate then scales as $R = O(\eta^2)$, where $\eta$ is the channel transmittance, and it collapses at roughly the distance where the multiphoton fraction exceeds the total transmittance. Without decoys, satellite-scale loss (tens of dB) is utterly out of reach.

### 2.2 The decoy-state insight

The decoy-state method turns Eve's nondemolition measurement into a liability. Alice randomly switches between a *signal* intensity $\mu$, one or more *decoy* intensities $\nu_i$ (including the vacuum, $\nu = 0$), and Eve — who cannot know which intensity any given pulse belongs to before the fact — must apply the same photon-number-dependent transformation to all of them. After transmission, Alice announces the intensity of each pulse, and Alice and Bob compare the *gains* $Q_{\mu}, Q_{\nu_i}$ and *error rates* $E_{\mu}, E_{\nu_i}$. Because the yield $Y_n$ of an $n$-photon pulse is identical regardless of the source intensity, these measured quantities constrain the single-photon yield $Y_1$ and error rate $e_1$, from which the secure key rate follows [1]:

$$R \;\ge\; q\,\Big\{-\,Q_{\mu}\,f(E_{\mu})\,H_2(E_{\mu}) \;+\; Q_1\,[\,1 - H_2(e_1)\,]\Big\},$$

where $q$ is the sifting efficiency, $f(E_{\mu})$ the error-correction inefficiency, $H_2$ the binary entropy, and $Q_1 = p_1(\mu) Y_1$. Crucially, the rate now scales as $R = O(\eta)$ — linear in transmittance, the same scaling as an ideal single-photon source.

The practical landmark is the **two-decoy protocol** (vacuum plus one weak decoy) of Ma, Qi, Zhao, and Lo, who showed that just two decoy intensities asymptotically approach the infinite-decoy theoretical limit and that statistical-fluctuation analysis makes the scheme viable with only a few hours of data even beyond 100 km [2]. This is the protocol family flown on Micius.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds from protocol theory to link engineering to network architecture, validated against published experiments:

1. **Protocol layer.** We derive the decoy-state estimation bounds, present the vacuum+weak-decoy parameter estimation, and state the finite-key security result in the universally-composable framework with total security parameter $\epsilon_{\text{sec}}$.
2. **Channel layer.** We decompose the satellite-to-ground link budget into diffraction, atmospheric attenuation, turbulence-induced scintillation and beam broadening, pointing error, and instrumental loss, distinguishing downlink from uplink geometries [4].
3. **Network layer.** We compare trusted-node (TN), untrusted-node (UTN), and mixed relay architectures, including the XOR-relay construction demonstrated intercontinentally by Micius [5][7].
4. **Validation.** We confront theory with the Micius campaign results (2016–2020), the GHz emulated-overpass downlink study [6], and published side-channel audits of the Micius source [3].

A reference implementation of the key-rate calculation is given in Section 5.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Decoy-State BB84 Protocol Mechanics

A full decoy-state BB84 session comprises the following phases:

1. **State preparation.** For each clock cycle, Alice selects basis $Z$ or $X$, bit $b \in \{0,1\}$, and intensity $\mu$ (signal, probability $p_{\mu}$), $\nu$ (decoy, $p_{\nu}$), or $0$ (vacuum, $p_0$). She encodes the bit in the polarization of a phase-randomized weak coherent pulse. Phase randomization is essential: it decoheres the photon-number basis, making the pulse a classical mixture of Fock states and denying Eve any phase reference.
2. **Transmission.** The quantum channel applies a photon-number-dependent, intensity-independent map characterized by yields $Y_n$ and error rates $e_n$.
3. **Measurement and sifting.** Bob measures in a random basis; Alice and Bob announce bases and intensities, discarding mismatched-basis and decoy events from the raw key.
4. **Parameter estimation.** From the decoy statistics, Alice and Bob compute lower bounds $Y_1^L$ and upper bounds $e_1^U$ via linear programming over the constraints
$$Q_{\mu} = \sum_{n\ge 0} p_n(\mu)\,Y_n, \qquad Q_{\mu}E_{\mu} = \sum_{n\ge 0} p_n(\mu)\,Y_n\,e_n,$$
and the analogous relations for each decoy intensity [2].
5. **Error correction and privacy amplification.** Standard cascade or LDPC reconciliation consumes a fraction $f(E_{\mu})H_2(E_{\mu})$ of the raw key per sifted bit; privacy amplification compresses the key by $Q_1 H_2(e_1)$, eliminating Eve's information.

> **Theorem:** *(Decoy-state security.)* In the asymptotic limit of infinitely many pulses, the decoy-state BB84 protocol with infinitely many decoy intensities produces a secret key at rate $R = O(\eta)$ secure against general coherent attacks, where $\eta$ is the single-photon transmittance. With vacuum plus one weak decoy, the achievable rate is within a few percent of this optimum [1][2].

The Micius payload implemented this with *eight* semiconductor laser diodes (four polarizations × signal/decoy), a design choice whose security implications we revisit in Section 6.

### 4.2 Orbital Link Budget Engineering

The satellite-to-ground channel is dominated by *geometric spreading*: a diffraction-limited beam of wavelength $\lambda$ launched from a transmitter aperture $D_t$ spreads to a spot of radius $\approx \lambda L / D_t$ at slant range $L$, of which the receiver aperture $D_r$ captures the fraction $\approx (D_r D_t / \lambda L)^2$. For $\lambda = 850$ nm, $D_t = 30$ cm, $D_r = 1$ m, and $L = 1{,}200$ km, the diffraction loss alone is roughly 28–30 dB. A representative downlink budget for a LEO pass is:

| Loss contribution | Typical value (dB) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Diffraction (geometric spreading) | 22–30 | Dominant; scales as $L^2$ |
| Atmospheric absorption & scattering | 3–8 | Zenith to low elevation |
| Turbulence (scintillation, beam broadening) | 2–5 | Downlink: only final km affected |
| Pointing, acquisition, tracking error | 1–3 | Microradian jitter |
| Transmitter/receiver optical losses | 3–5 | Filters, beamsplitters, coupling |
| Detector inefficiency | 3–6 | $\eta_{\text{det}} \approx 25$–$50\%$ at 850 nm |
| **Total** | **$\approx$ 40–65** | Elevation-dependent |

A decisive asymmetry governs link direction. In the **downlink**, the beam propagates mostly through vacuum and encounters atmospheric turbulence only in the final kilometers, when it is already wide, so wavefront distortion has modest effect. In the **uplink**, the beam meets turbulence immediately at launch, when it is narrow, and the wandered, broadened beam then diffracts over the full orbital range — substantially worse coupling. Atmospheric link-budget simulations validated against the 144 km Canary Island experiments and Canadian ground-station studies quantify uplink penalties of several additional dB and confirm that uplink viability depends sensitively on site selection (e.g., high-altitude observatories such as Hanle, India) [4]. Downlink-first design is therefore the standard choice for SatQKD [5].

The elevation profile of a pass matters enormously: at 20° elevation the slant range is roughly triple the zenith range, adding ~10 dB. An emulated LEO overpass study with a GHz-clocked free-space system estimated an *average* quantum-downlink channel loss of 34 dB over a usable pass of $t_{\text{pass}} \approx 294$ s (20°–160° elevation), establishing the operating envelope within which finite-key blocks must be accumulated [6].

### 4.3 Trusted-Node Relay Architectures

A single LEO satellite sees any given ground station for only a few minutes per day, and two distant stations are never simultaneously visible. The trusted-node (TN) construction solves this by *key relay*: the satellite performs decoy-state QKD with station $A$ to establish key $K_A$, and later with station $B$ to establish $K_B$; it then publicly broadcasts $K_A \oplus K_B$. Station $A$ recovers $K_B = K_A \oplus (K_A \oplus K_B)$, and the two stations share a key without ever being copresent [7]. The satellite necessarily knows the key — hence "trusted."

Micius demonstrated exactly this at intercontinental scale: decoy-state QKD sessions with Xinglong (near Beijing), Nanshan (near Urumqi), and Graz (near Vienna) established per-pass keys at kilohertz rates, and the XOR relay created shared keys between China and Europe separated by up to **7,600 km**. The keys were exercised in a one-time-pad image transfer (~80 kbit for two images) and a 75-minute AES-128 videoconference between the Chinese and Austrian Academies of Sciences, refreshed with 128-bit seeds each second over ~2 GB of traffic [7].

Alternatives and their trade-offs:

- **Untrusted node (UTN) via entanglement.** The satellite distributes entangled photon pairs to two stations; no key material ever exists aboard the spacecraft. Micius distributed entanglement over 1,120 km (Delingha–Nanshan) with sufficient fidelity for secure keys [8]. UTN removes the trust assumption but roughly squares the loss ($\eta_A \eta_B$), demanding brighter sources and better detectors.
- **Measurement-device-independent (MDI) QKD.** Both stations transmit to the satellite, which performs a Bell measurement; detector side channels are eliminated by construction, but coincident detection doubles the loss penalty.
- **Mixed TN/UTN constellations.** Architectural studies conclude that TN operation from a single satellite can connect essentially any pair of Earth locations with sub-second relay latency, while UTN links serve the highest-assurance pairs; practical near-term constellations will mix both [5].

### 4.4 Finite-Key Security and Side-Channel Hardening

Real passes yield finite blocks ($10^5$–$10^9$ detections), so asymptotic rates are unreachable; composable finite-key proofs (e.g., Lim–Curty–Walenta–Xu–Zbinden) bound the extractable key with failure probability $\epsilon_{\text{sec}} \sim 10^{-9}$–$10^{-10}$, typically costing 10–30% of the asymptotic rate for satellite-scale blocks. Statistical fluctuations in the decoy estimates are handled with Chernoff or Hoeffding tail bounds, and the vacuum decoy pins down the dark-count contribution.

The sobering counterpoint is implementation security. A 2025 audit of Micius flight data found relative timing desynchronization exceeding **100 ps** between the eight independent laser diodes — comparable to their 200 ps pulse duration, with up to ~300 ps skew between signal and decoy states for vertical polarization. An eavesdropper with ideal timing resolution could distinguish decoy from signal pulses in at least **98.7%** of cases, collapsing the security argument that the decoy method rests on [3]. The remedy is source engineering rather than protocol patching: a *single* laser with active intensity and polarization modulation removes the distinguishability at its root, and this single-laser architecture is now the consensus design for next-generation SatQKD payloads.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

The Micius campaigns established the empirical baseline for SatQKD. Satellite-to-ground decoy-state BB84 was demonstrated over slant ranges up to ~1,200 km with per-pass sifted key rates on the kilohertz scale and total channel losses exceeding 60 dB — a regime where, without decoys, the GLLP rate would be exactly zero [7]. The intercontinental trusted relay then converted those point-to-point sessions into a 7,600 km China–Europe shared key, consumed by real applications (one-time-pad imagery, AES-128 videoconferencing) [7]. Complementary entanglement distribution over 1,120 km proved the UTN path viable, albeit at lower rates [8].

The following reference implementation computes the asymptotic decoy-state key rate for a satellite pass, including a finite-key penalty factor:

```python
import math

def h2(x: float) -> float:
    """Binary Shannon entropy."""
    if x <= 0.0 or x >= 1.0:
        return 0.0
    return -x * math.log2(x) - (1 - x) * math.log2(1 - x)

def poisson(n: int, mu: float) -> float:
    return math.exp(-mu) * mu ** n / math.factorial(n)

def decoy_key_rate(mu: float, nu: float, eta: float,
                   e_det: float = 0.01, f_ec: float = 1.16,
                   finite_key_penalty: float = 0.8) -> float:
    """
    Asymptotic decoy-state BB84 key rate per pulse (vacuum + weak decoy),
    then derated by a finite-key penalty factor.

    eta: total single-photon transmittance (channel x optics x detector).
    """
    Y0 = 1e-6                      # vacuum yield ~ dark counts
    Y1 = eta + Y0                  # single-photon yield
    Q_mu = sum(poisson(n, mu) * (1 - (1 - eta) ** n) + poisson(n, mu) * Y0
               for n in range(1, 12))
    E_mu = (0.5 * Y0 + e_det * Q_mu) / max(Q_mu, 1e-300)
    Q1 = poisson(1, mu) * Y1
    e1 = (0.5 * Y0 + e_det * eta) / max(Y1, 1e-300)
    q = 0.5                        # sifting efficiency
    rate = q * (-Q_mu * f_ec * h2(E_mu) + Q1 * (1 - h2(e1)))
    return max(rate, 0.0) * finite_key_penalty

# LEO downlink: 45 dB total loss, decoy intensities mu=0.5, nu=0.1
eta = 10 ** (-45 / 10)
print(f"rate/pulse: {decoy_key_rate(0.5, 0.1, eta):.3e}")
# At 100 MHz clock: ~ tens of secure bits per second at 45 dB.
```

For protocol-level verification, the decoy-state estimation constraints can be model-checked as a state machine over intensity announcements; a TLA+ sketch of the sifting/estimation phase is:

```tla
---- MODULE DecoySifting ----

CONSTANTS Intensities, Pulses
VARIABLES announced, sifted
Init == announced = {} /\ sifted = {}
Announce(p) == announced' = announced \cup {p}
Sift == sifted' = {p \in announced : IntensityOf(p) = "signal"}
Next == \E p \in Pulses : Announce(p) \/ Sift
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<announced, sifted>>
EstimateSound == \A p \in sifted : IntensityOf(p) = "signal"

```

*Security proof sketch.* The decoy-state argument reduces to the single-photon BB84 proof once $Y_1^L$ and $e_1^U$ are established: the linear constraints from signal and decoy gains pin the single-photon contribution because Eve's interaction is intensity-independent prior to Alice's announcement. Composability then follows from the Shor–Preskill/GLLP reduction to entanglement distillation on the single-photon subspace [1][2].

## 6. Limitations

SatQKD's limitations are as instructive as its achievements:

- **Trusted-node assumption.** The XOR relay grants the satellite full knowledge of every key it relays. For Micius-class missions this is a *geopolitical* trust assumption, not merely a technical one. UTN entanglement architectures remove it but pay a quadratic loss penalty [5][8].
- **Finite pass windows.** A LEO satellite offers each station only minutes of contact per day, and key must be accumulated, stored, and relayed across orbits — key *management* at planetary scale is an unsolved systems problem, distinct from the physics.
- **Daylight operation.** Background solar photons overwhelm single-photon detectors; nearly all demonstrations are nocturnal. Daylight SatQKD requires ultra-narrow spectral, spatial, and temporal filtering and remains experimental.
- **Source side channels.** As the Micius desynchronization audit shows, multi-laser transmitters leak intensity information through timing, spectral, and spatial distinguishability, silently voiding the security proof [3]. Single-laser active modulation is the required fix.
- **Weather and availability.** Cloud cover blocks the optical link entirely; unlike fiber, the satellite channel has no "always-on" guarantee, so SatQKD is a supplement to, not a replacement for, terrestrial QKD networks.
- **Finite-key costs.** Short passes at high loss yield small blocks; the composable finite-key overhead can erase the margin that the asymptotic analysis promises, particularly for low-elevation passes [6].

## 7. Conclusion

Satellite QKD has crossed from gedankenexperiment to infrastructure. The decoy-state method — a software-and-statistics upgrade to existing laser hardware — restored unconditional security to weak-coherent-pulse BB84 and made 60+ dB channels tractable [1][2]. Orbital link-budget engineering, exploiting the vacuum-dominated propagation path, then made those channels *reachable* from a single LEO platform, with downlink geometry decisively favored over uplink [4][6]. Finally, trusted-node XOR relay turned isolated orbital passes into an intercontinental key-exchange fabric, demonstrated at 7,600 km between China and Europe [7], while entanglement-based UTN links chart the path to trust-free operation [8].

The research frontier is now predominantly *engineering*: single-laser side-channel-free transmitters, daylight-capable filtering, constellation-scale key management, and mixed TN/UTN network optimization [3][5]. The physics is settled; the network is under construction. When it arrives, the security it offers will rest not on the difficulty of factoring integers but on the impossibility — enforced by quantum mechanics and careful link budgets — of observing a photon without leaving a trace.
