# From Distributed Keys to Aggregated Signatures: FROST, BLS Aggregation, and the Pedersen DKG Pipeline at Scale

## Abstract

Threshold signatures distribute signing authority across *n* parties so that any *t* of them can jointly produce a signature indistinguishable from a single-key signature, while no coalition of *t − 1* can forge. This thesis unifies the three pillars of threshold signatures at scale: (i) the FROST protocol, the flexible round-optimized Schnorr threshold scheme specified in RFC 9591 [1], reducing signing to two rounds with preprocessing-separated nonces; (ii) BLS signature aggregation over pairing-friendly curves, whose homomorphic structure yields non-interactive threshold signing with constant-size aggregates [2][3]; and (iii) Pedersen distributed key generation (DKG), the canonical dealer-free key bootstrap, with the Gennaro–Jarecki–Krawczyk–Rabin (GJKR) correction repairing its uniformity flaw [5]. We analyze the binding-factor defense against concurrent Schnorr forgery attacks, the Lagrange interpolation at the heart of share aggregation, rogue-key defenses in BLS multisets, and the communication profiles of dealer-based, Pedersen, and GJKR key generation. Benchmarks are tabulated, security theorems stated, and limitations — broadcast assumptions, rogue-key hazards, adaptive corruptions, pairing cost — examined.

## 1 Introduction

The central question of threshold cryptography is deceptively simple: *how can a group of mutually distrustful parties jointly hold a private key such that a quorum can act while no minority can?* The answer determines the security architecture of certificate authorities, blockchain validator sets, cryptocurrency custody systems, and distributed PKI. Two families of threshold signature schemes dominate contemporary practice. The first builds on **Schnorr signatures** over prime-order groups — the discrete-logarithm lineage whose state of the art is FROST [1], a scheme that achieves two-round signing with unlimited concurrency. The second builds on **pairing-based BLS signatures** [2][3], whose bilinear maps render threshold signing non-interactive: each party signs locally and the signatures are aggregated by simple group addition, requiring no coordination beyond message agreement.

Both families, however, presuppose a solved key-generation problem. If a single trusted dealer distributes shares, the dealer is a single point of compromise. **Distributed key generation** removes the dealer: Pedersen's 1991 protocol [5], in which every participant runs a verifiable secret sharing (VSS) instance in parallel, remains the conceptual template for all practical DKG. Yet its subtle biasability — discovered by Gennaro, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Rabin — reminds us that *distributed* does not imply *correct*, and that protocol composition demands proof rather than intuition.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys the mathematical foundations: Shamir secret sharing, Feldman and Pedersen VSS, Lagrange interpolation, and the Schnorr and BLS signature primitives. Section 3 presents the methodology: formal protocol specifications, including a Haskell model of Pedersen DKG. Section 4 is the deep dive: the FROST two-round protocol and its binding-factor defense; BLS aggregation with rogue-key countermeasures; Boldyreva's non-interactive threshold BLS; and the Pedersen–GJKR key-generation pipeline. Section 5 gives empirical benchmarks and formal security statements. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.


## 2 Background

### 2.1 Shamir Secret Sharing

Shamir's (1979) secret-sharing scheme is the substrate of nearly all threshold cryptography. To share a secret *s ∈ ℤ_q* among *n* parties with threshold *t*, a dealer samples a random polynomial

*f(x) = s + a₁x + a₂x² + ⋯ + a_{t−1}x^{t−1}*

of degree *t − 1* and distributes shares *sᵢ = f(i)* to party *i*. Any *t* shares reconstruct *s = f(0)* via Lagrange interpolation, while any *t − 1* shares reveal nothing information-theoretically:

*s = Σᵢ λᵢ(0)·sᵢ*, where *λᵢ(x) = Π_{j≠i} (x − j)/(i − j)* are the Lagrange basis polynomials.

### 2.2 Verifiable Secret Sharing: Feldman and Pedersen

Plain Shamir sharing trusts the dealer to distribute consistent shares. **Verifiable secret sharing (VSS)** lets recipients verify that their shares lie on a single degree-*t−1* polynomial. In **Feldman VSS**, the dealer broadcasts commitments *Cⱼ = g^{aⱼ}* for each coefficient *aⱼ*; party *i* checks *g^{sᵢ} = Πⱼ Cⱼ^{iʲ}*. Feldman VSS is computationally hiding (the commitments reveal *g^{s}*) but verifiable. **Pedersen VSS** uses commitments of the form *Cⱼ = g^{aⱼ}h^{bⱼ}* with an auxiliary generator *h* of unknown discrete log relative to *g*, achieving *information-theoretic* hiding of the secret at the cost of an extra blinding polynomial [5]. The distinction matters for DKG: Feldman-based DKG leaks the public key during the protocol (which is fine — the public key is public), while Pedersen-based variants preserve secrecy of individual contributions.

### 2.3 Schnorr Signatures

In a prime-order group, a Schnorr signature on message *m* under secret key *x* is the pair *(R, z)* where *R = gᵏ* for random nonce *k*, *c = H(R ‖ X ‖ m)*, and *z = k + c·x*, verifiable by *gᶻ = R·Xᶜ* with *X = gˣ*. Schnorr signatures are linear in the secret key — the property that makes them *thresholdizable*: if each party holds an additive share *xᵢ* of *x* and a nonce share *kᵢ*, the sum of partial responses *zᵢ = kᵢ + c·λᵢ·xᵢ* yields a valid aggregate *z*. The peril lies in the nonces: concurrent Schnorr threshold sessions admit forgery attacks when adversarial parties can adapt their contributions after observing honest ones [1].

### 2.4 BLS Signatures and Pairings

BLS signatures [2] operate over a pairing *e: G₁ × G₂ → G_T* of prime-order groups. Key generation picks *x ∈ ℤ_q*, public key *X = g₂ˣ*; signing computes *σ = H(m)ˣ* where *H* maps messages to *G₁*; verification checks *e(σ, g₂) = e(H(m), X)*. BLS signatures are *unique* (deterministic, one valid signature per key–message pair) and **aggregatable**: signatures *σᵢ = H(mᵢ)^{xᵢ}* aggregate by group multiplication *σ = Πᵢ σᵢ*, verifiable with *n + 1* pairings instead of *2n* [2][3]. This multiplicative homomorphism — absent in Schnorr — is what collapses threshold BLS signing to a single non-interactive round.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is constructive and comparative. For each protocol family we (i) state the exact syntax and round structure, (ii) identify the cryptographic assumption set, (iii) derive the aggregation equation from Lagrange interpolation, and (iv) assess the communication complexity in the number of parties *n* and threshold *t*. The analysis is grounded in the normative specifications: RFC 9591 for FROST [1], the CFRG BLS signature draft [2], the Boldyreva threshold-BLS framework [4], and the Pedersen/GJKR DKG literature [5][6][7].

### 3.1 A Haskell Model of Pedersen DKG

To make the DKG pipeline concrete, we model its algebraic core in Haskell. The model captures Feldman-commitment verification and share aggregation — the two operations every participant performs — abstracting the group as a type class so the same code describes both the discrete-log and pairing settings:

```haskell
{-# LANGUAGE MultiParamTypeClasses #-}
-- Algebraic core of Pedersen DKG: n parallel Feldman VSS instances,
-- one per dealer, with share summation. Network rounds elided.
module PedersenDKG where

type Scalar = Integer   -- elements of Z_q
type Party  = Int

class Group g where
  gmul :: g -> g -> g    -- group operation
  gpow :: g -> Scalar -> g -- scalar multiplication
  ggen :: g              -- generator

-- A polynomial f(x) = c0 + c1 x + ... over Z_q
newtype Poly = Poly [Scalar]

evalPoly :: Poly -> Scalar -> Scalar
evalPoly (Poly coeffs) x = sum (zipWith (*) coeffs (iterate (* x) 1))

-- Feldman commitments: C_j = g^{a_j} for each coefficient
commit :: Group g => g -> Poly -> [g]
commit g (Poly coeffs) = map (gpow g) coeffs

-- Party i verifies share s_i against commitments of dealer d
verifyShare :: Group g => [g] -> Party -> Scalar -> Bool
verifyShare comms i s =
  gpow ggen s == foldl gmul (head comms)
    [ gpow (comms !! j) (fromIntegral i ^ j) | j <- [1 .. length comms - 1] ]

-- Pedersen DKG: every party deals; final share is the sum of
-- accepted dealers' shares; public key is the product of commitments.
dkgAggregate :: [[Scalar]] -> [Poly] -> ([Scalar], [Poly])
dkgAggregate shares polys =
  (map sum (transpose shares), polys)  -- s_j = Σ_d f_d(j)
```

### 4.1 FROST: Two-Round Schnorr Threshold Signing

FROST (Flexible Round-Optimized Schnorr Threshold), specified in RFC 9591 [1] and its CFRG draft lineage [7], addresses the central tension of Schnorr threshold signing: minimizing network rounds without admitting the concurrency forgeries that plague naive multi-round Schnorr. The protocol assumes participants already hold Shamir shares *sᵢ* of the signing key *x* (key generation is explicitly out of scope in the RFC; Appendix C specifies a trusted-dealer bootstrap [1]).

**Round 1 — Commitment.** Each participant *i* in the signing set *S* (|S| = t) samples two fresh nonces *(dᵢ, eᵢ)* and publishes hiding/binding commitments *(Dᵢ = g^{dᵢ}, Eᵢ = g^{eᵢ})*. Crucially, this round can be executed as a *preprocessing* phase, arbitrarily far in advance of knowing the message — signing operations can then complete in a single online round.

**Round 2 — Signing.** A coordinator (who may be one of the signers or an external party [7]) assembles the signing package: the message *m*, the set *S*, and all commitments. Each signer computes the **binding factor**

*ρᵢ = H₁(i, m, {(j, Dⱼ, Eⱼ)}_{j∈S})*

and the group commitment *R = Σᵢ (Dᵢ + ρᵢ·Eᵢ)*, then the challenge *c = H₂(R, X, m)* and their response share

*zᵢ = dᵢ + eᵢ·ρᵢ + λᵢ·c·sᵢ*

where *λᵢ* is the Lagrange coefficient for *i* relative to *S*. The coordinator aggregates *z = Σᵢ zᵢ* and outputs *(R, z)* — a Schnorr signature verifiable by any standard verifier, and, for the Ed25519/Ed448 ciphersuites, compatible with RFC 8032 verifiers [1].

The protocol is secure under the **discrete logarithm assumption in the random oracle model**, with a proof via the generalized forking lemma, tolerating a static adversary corrupting up to *t − 1* participants. Two deliberate design decisions deserve emphasis. First, FROST signatures are *not deterministic*: deriving nonces deterministically from the message would permit a complete key-recovery attack in the multi-party setting [1]. Second, the RFC deliberately omits the Schnorr21 optimization that would reduce per-signer scalar multiplications from linear to constant, because that optimization introduces malleability that breaks the guarantee about which signers participated [1].

### 4.2 The Binding Factor: Defeating Concurrency Forgeries

The binding factor *ρᵢ* is FROST's decisive innovation. Prior Schnorr threshold schemes either required three or more rounds (e.g., Stinson–Strobl, GJKR-based signing [5]) or restricted concurrency to defeat the following attack: an adversary controlling one signer observes honest parties' commitment values, then chooses its own nonce commitment *after* seeing theirs, biasing the aggregate nonce *R* in a way that enables Wagner-style forgery across many concurrent sessions [1]. By binding each signer's effective nonce *Dᵢ + ρᵢEᵢ* to the *entire* commitment vector and the message via the random oracle *H₁*, FROST makes such adaptive biasing infeasible: any change to the adversary's commitment alters every *ρⱼ*, invalidating the algebraic relation the forgery depends on. The upshot is *unlimited concurrency*: arbitrarily many signing sessions may proceed in parallel with the same long-term shares, a property essential for high-throughput custody and validator infrastructure.

### 4.3 BLS Aggregation: Homomorphism as a Scaling Primitive

Where FROST optimizes the discrete-log setting, BLS signatures [2] change the game structurally. The pairing equation

*e(σ, g₂) = e(H(m), X)*

is bilinear, so products of signatures verify against products of keys: for *σ = Πᵢ σᵢ* with *σᵢ = H(mᵢ)^{xᵢ}*,

*e(σ, g₂) = Πᵢ e(H(mᵢ), Xᵢ)*,

verifiable in *n + 1* pairings rather than *2n* [2]. Three consequences follow for threshold deployments at scale:

1. **Non-interactive threshold signing.** With Shamir-shared *x* and public Lagrange coefficients, each party computes *σᵢ = H(m)^{λᵢxᵢ}* locally; anyone aggregates *σ = Πᵢ σᵢ*. No rounds, no coordinator trust, no nonce management — the signing protocol is a single broadcast of partial signatures.
2. **Verifier indistinguishability.** The aggregate is byte-identical to a single-party BLS signature under the group key; verification is the standard BLS verify [4].
3. **Batch economics.** In validator networks (e.g., Ethereum's BLS12-381 validator signatures) [3].

The price is the **rogue-key attack**: an adversary registering *X* = g₂^{x}·(Π_{honest} Xᵢ)^{−1}* can forge aggregates unless the scheme enforces a defense. The CFRG draft standardizes three: *proof of possession* (each signer proves knowledge of its secret key at registration), *message augmentation* (prepend the public key to the message), and *distinct-message* requirements for the basic scheme [2]. Threshold deployments universally adopt proof of possession, since all signers sign the same message.

### 4.4 Boldyreva Threshold BLS and Adaptive Security

Boldyreva's threshold BLS construction (PKC 2003) is the canonical pairing-based threshold scheme: key generation shares *x* via Shamir (dealer or DKG), signing is non-interactive as above, and the scheme inherits BLS's short, unique signatures. Its elegance made it the default choice for decentralized systems — until the fine print caught up: the original security proof held only against a **static** adversary that chooses corruptions before the protocol begins.

Bacho and Loss (CCS 2022) gave the first adaptively-secure proof, but under strong non-standard assumptions (one-more discrete log in the algebraic group model) [4]. Das and Ren subsequently constructed the first adaptively-secure threshold BLS scheme from standard assumptions — DDH and co-CDH in asymmetric pairing groups in the random oracle model — while preserving non-interactive signing, single-party verification compatibility, and practical efficiency, and additionally supplying an efficient DKG for key setup [4]. For static security their scheme needs only CDH, matching plain BLS. The trajectory illustrates a general lesson: *thresholdizing a signature scheme is easy; proving the threshold version under realistic adversarial models is the actual research.*

### 4.5 The Pedersen DKG Pipeline and the GJKR Correction

All the signing machinery above assumes a shared key. **Pedersen DKG** (1991, published 1992) generates it without a dealer [5][6]: each of the *n* parties acts as a dealer in a Feldman VSS instance, sharing a random *zᵢ* via polynomial *fᵢ* of degree *t − 1* and broadcasting commitments *C_{i,j} = g^{a_{i,j}}*. Parties verify received shares against commitments; failed verifications trigger complaints, and a complained-against dealer must publicly reveal the disputed share. Parties that survive form the qualified set *QUAL*; the shared secret is *s = Σ_{i∈QUAL} zᵢ*, each party's share *sⱼ = Σ_{i∈QUAL} fᵢ(j)*, and the public key *Y = Π_{i∈QUAL} Πⱼ C_{i,j}^{0ʲ}* [6].

The protocol is simple, efficient (*O(n²)* communication, constant rounds), and — as Gennaro, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Rabin showed — **biased**. An adaptive adversary can observe honest parties' contributions and then choose whether its own VSS instances complete, skewing the distribution of *s* away from uniform [5][6]. The GJKR protocol repairs this by secret-sharing each *zᵢ* a second time (effectively committing to contributions before seeing others'), restoring uniform key generation at the cost of additional rounds — a repair adopted, with modifications, by production systems such as the Keep Network's threshold relay [8]. Pedersen's original protocol nevertheless remains the reference design: every modern DKG — GJKR, Neji et al.'s bias-removal without extra sharing rounds, and recent broadcast-free synchronous constructions [7] — is best understood as *Pedersen DKG plus a fix for a specific adversarial capability*.



## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmarks

The following table synthesizes published implementation measurements for FROST (Ristretto/Ed25519 ciphersuite, RFC 9591 test-vector implementations) and threshold BLS (BLS12-381) on commodity 64-bit hardware. Figures are order-of-magnitude representative; exact values vary by library and curve.

| Operation | Scheme | Cost per party | Rounds | Aggregate size |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Commitment (preprocess) | FROST [1] | 2 scalar mults | 1 (offline) | — |
| Signature share | FROST [1] | 2 scalar mults + 2 hashes | 1 (online) | 64 B (R,z) |
| Aggregation | FROST [1] | *t* scalar mults (coordinator) | — | 64 B |
| Partial sign | Threshold BLS [2][4] | 1 hash-to-curve + 1 scalar mult | 0 | 96 B (G₂) |
| Aggregation | Threshold BLS [2][4] | *t* point additions | 0 | 96 B |
| Aggregate verify | Threshold BLS [2] | 2 pairings | — | — |
| Pedersen DKG share verify | DKG [5][6] | *O(n·t)* mults | 3 broadcasts | *O(n²)* total |
| GJKR DKG | DKG [5] | *O(n·t)* mults (higher constant) | 5+ broadcasts | *O(n²)* total |

Two scaling laws emerge. First, **signing cost is essentially independent of *n*** in both families: FROST's online phase is constant per signer, and BLS threshold signing is a single local operation. Second, **key generation is the quadratic bottleneck**: every DKG variant pays *Ω(n²)* communication, which is why large validator sets (thousands of parties) use DKG only for committee-sized subgroups, or amortize one DKG over many epochs.

### 5.2 Security Statements

> Theorem: (FROST unforgeability, informal [1]). Let *G* be a prime-order group in which the discrete logarithm problem is hard. Then the FROST two-round signing protocol of RFC 9591 is existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack (TS-UF-0 in the terminology of [1]) against a static adversary corrupting at most *t − 1* of *n* participants, in the random oracle model, with arbitrarily many concurrent signing sessions.

> Theorem: (Threshold BLS adaptive security, informal [4]). Under the DDH and co-CDH assumptions in asymmetric pairing groups, the Das–Ren threshold BLS scheme is existentially unforgeable against an adaptive adversary corrupting up to *t − 1* parties, in the random oracle model. Signing is non-interactive and the aggregate signature verifies with the standard BLS verification algorithm.

> Theorem: (GJKR key uniformity, informal [5]). The Gennaro–Jarecki–Krawczyk–Rabin DKG protocol outputs a public key uniformly distributed over *G* (conditioned on the adversary's view containing no information about honest contributions), secure against a static adversary corrupting *t < n/2* parties, assuming authenticated channels and a broadcast medium.

The three theorems compose into the deployment recipe this thesis advocates: **GJKR (or a modern bias-free variant) for key generation, FROST where Schnorr compatibility is required, threshold BLS where non-interactivity and aggregation dominate.**

## 6 Limitations

1. **Broadcast and synchrony assumptions.** Pedersen DKG and GJKR require a broadcast channel and synchronous rounds; in asynchronous networks these must be simulated by Byzantine broadcast subprotocols, multiplying cost. Recent work removes broadcasts at the price of expected-constant rounds and *O(κn³)* communication [7].
2. **The *t < n/2* barrier.** Pedersen-style DKG tolerates only a minority of active faults; dishonest-majority DKG exists but requires fundamentally different (and far heavier) machinery.
3. **Rogue keys and registration.** BLS aggregation's defenses all push complexity into key registration — proof of possession must itself be verified and stored, and message-augmentation changes the signed payload format [2].
4. **Pairing cost and quantum exposure.** Pairings remain an order of magnitude costlier than group exponentiations, and both the discrete-log and pairing assumptions fall to Shor's algorithm; neither family is post-quantum.
5. **Coordinator trust in FROST.** The coordinator learns the message and the signer set and can mount denial-of-service by withholding aggregation; it learns nothing about key material, but availability still depends on it [1][7].
6. **Determinism vs. multi-party nonces.** FROST's mandatory randomized nonces complicate auditing and replay protection relative to deterministic schemes [1].
7. **Adaptive DKG remains expensive.** Fully adaptively-secure DKG (Canetti et al.) roughly doubles the round and communication cost of GJKR; most deployments accept static security for key generation while demanding adaptive security for signing [5].

## 7 Conclusion

Threshold signatures at scale rest on an elegant division of labor. **FROST** shows that the discrete-logarithm world can have two-round Schnorr threshold signing with unlimited concurrency, provided nonces are bound to the full session context — a technique now enshrined in RFC 9591 [1]. **BLS aggregation** shows that pairings buy something stronger: non-interactive threshold signing with constant-size aggregates, at the cost of registration-time rogue-key defenses and heavier verification [2][3][4]. And **Pedersen DKG**, with the GJKR correction, shows that the shared key itself can be born distributed — no dealer, no single point of compromise — provided the protocol is hardened against the bias attacks that simplicity invites [5][6][7].

The open frontier is not a better signing equation but cheaper, more robust key generation: asynchronous DKG with optimal resilience and *o(n³)* communication, post-quantum threshold schemes with comparable ergonomics, and formally verified implementations that close the gap between the theorems of Section 5 and the code that actually signs.
