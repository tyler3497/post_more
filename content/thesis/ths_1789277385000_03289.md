# Biohybrid Robotics: Living Muscle Actuators, Neuromuscular Control, and Engineered Tissue–Device Interfaces

## Abstract

Biohybrid robotics fuses living muscle tissue with synthetic scaffolds and electronic control to build machines whose actuators are contractile cells rather than electromagnetic motors. This thesis develops a unified treatment of three pillars: (i) engineered skeletal and cardiac muscle as force-generating actuators, including antagonistic tissue pairs and optogenetic contractility training; (ii) neuromuscular control architectures in which optogenetically driven motor neurons, coupled to muscle through functional neuromuscular junctions, replace crude direct stimulation with biologically native signaling; and (iii) engineered tissue–device interfaces — hydrogel scaffolds, wireless frequency-multiplexed bioelectronics, and proprioceptive strain sensors — that close the loop between living and abiotic subsystems. Drawing on landmark demonstrations from optogenetic neuromuscular swimmers to wirelessly steered neurocardiac robots and sensor-embedded muscle, we analyze the Hill-type mechanics of living actuators, the control theory of stimulation–contraction coupling, and the fabrication constraints bounding force density, lifetime, and controllability. The synthesis charts a path from today's millimeter-scale proof-of-concept systems toward adaptive, self-sensing biohybrid machines for medicine and environmental intervention.

## 1 Intro

Conventional robotics builds actuators from motors, hydraulics, and pneumatics: efficient at macroscopic scales, yet increasingly unwieldy as machines shrink toward the millimeter and micrometer regimes where medicine, environmental sensing, and minimally invasive intervention live. Biological muscle, by contrast, is a *pre-optimized* linear actuator — compact, compliant, energetically efficient, and self-repairing — refined by hundreds of millions of years of evolution [4]. Biohybrid robotics takes this observation literally: instead of emulating muscle with engineered materials, it *incorporates* living muscle cells as the force-generating elements of robotic systems [1][2].

The field has advanced through three discernible generations. The first generation, circa 2014, demonstrated self-propelled swimming and walking "biobots" powered by spontaneously beating cardiac muscle cells derived from rats [5]. These proved that living tissue could drive untethered locomotion, but the actuators were autonomous oscillators: they beat on their own and could neither sense the environment nor make decisions. The second generation introduced *controllability* — engineered skeletal muscle tissues that contract on demand under electrical or optical stimulation, assembled into antagonistic pairs that mimic the flexor–extensor architecture of animal limbs and achieved joint rotations approaching 90 degrees sustained over a week [4]. The third and current generation adds *neural control and feedback*: optogenetic motor neurons that innervate engineered muscle through functional neuromuscular junctions, wireless frequency-multiplexed bioelectronics that steer such tissue like an artificial brain, and proprioceptive sensors embedded directly in muscle for closed-loop actuation [6][7][8].

This thesis argues that the central scientific problem of biohybrid robotics is not fabrication but *control theory for living actuators*. A muscle is not a motor with a datasheet; it is a nonlinear, adaptive, history-dependent material whose force output depends on stimulation frequency, length, velocity, fatigue state, and even ambient glucose. Designing robots around such actuators demands:

1. Accurate actuator models — Hill-type force–length–velocity relations extended with activation dynamics and fatigue [3].
2. Biologically faithful control interfaces — neuromuscular junctions and optogenetic addressing that respect the native signaling vocabulary of the tissue [5][6].
3. Engineered interfaces that couple soft, wet, living tissue to rigid, dry electronics without delamination, immune rejection, or signal loss [2][8].
4. Sensory feedback — proprioception — so that the controller observes the actuator state it is commanding [7].

The remainder of this thesis develops each of these in turn, grounds them in the experimental record, and identifies the open problems separating today's demonstrations from genuinely autonomous biohybrid machines.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Muscle as an actuator material

Muscle contraction follows the *sliding-filament theory*: sarcomeres — the repeating contractile units of striated muscle — shorten as myosin cross-bridges ratchet along actin filaments, driven by ATP hydrolysis. The macroscopic force–velocity behavior is captured by the Hill equation:

> **Theorem (Hill force–velocity relation):** For a muscle shortening under load, the product of excess force and velocity is approximately constant:
>
> $$(F + a)(v + b) = b(F_0 + a)$$
>
> where $F$ is muscle force, $v$ is shortening velocity, $F_0$ is maximal isometric force, and $a, b$ are empirical constants. Peak mechanical power occurs at approximately $F \approx 0.3 F_0$ and $v \approx 0.3 v_{max}$.

Skeletal and cardiac muscle serve complementary actuator roles in biohybrid designs [2]. *Skeletal muscle* contracts only when stimulated (via motor neurons in vivo), producing large, controllable, unidirectional forces — the workhorse of walking, gripping, and swimming robots. *Cardiac muscle* is spontaneously contractile and electrically coupled through gap junctions, producing rhythmic, synchronized beating ideal for pumps and peristaltic transport, but harder to start, stop, or steer.

Engineered muscle is typically fabricated by embedding myoblasts (commonly the C2C12 murine line, or human iPSC-derived myocytes) in an extracellular matrix hydrogel cast around a scaffold; the cells self-organize, fuse into multinucleated myotubes, and align along the scaffold's principal stress axis, yielding contractile tissue with controllable geometry [3][5].

### 2.2 The neuromuscular junction

In animals, the *neuromuscular junction* (NMJ) is the chemical synapse between a motor neuron axon terminal and a muscle fiber: an action potential triggers acetylcholine release, end-plate potentials summate, and the fiber contracts.

A striking variant uses *electrical* rather than chemical synapses: co-cultured human iPSC-derived motor neurons and cardiomyocytes form gap-junction-coupled neurocardiac junctions, enabling faster, bidirectional signaling between neural and cardiac compartments [8].

### 2.3 Stimulation modalities

Three stimulation strategies dominate the literature, recently surveyed in depth [1]:

- **Electrical stimulation** — field electrodes or onboard electronics deliver voltage pulses (e.g., 1.5 V/mm, 50 Hz, 2 ms pulses [4]). Simple and high-bandwidth, but spatially crude and prone to electrochemical damage and electrode fouling over days.
- **Optical (optogenetic) stimulation** — cells expressing light-gated ion channels (ChR2, blue; ChrimsonR, red) are addressed with patterned light. Spatially and spectrally selective: blue and red pulses can drive antagonistic muscle layers alternately [3]. Requires genetic modification and optical access.
- **NMJ-based stimulation** — neurons, rather than muscle, receive the stimulus; muscle contracts via its native synapse. This offloads timing, grading, and fatigue management to biological circuitry [1][5][6].

### 2.4 The tissue–device interface problem

A biohybrid robot is a *heterogeneous system*: kilopascal-stiffness hydrogels and living cells must bond to megapascal-stiffness electronics and polymers across a wet–dry boundary, survive weeks in culture medium, and transmit force without stress concentrations that tear the tissue.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a *synthesis and analysis* study. Its method has four components.

**Corpus construction.** We survey the peer-reviewed biohybrid robotics literature from the first cardiac biobots (2014) through contemporary neuromuscular and proprioceptive systems (2025), prioritizing primary experimental reports in *Science Robotics*, *PNAS*, and *Interface Focus*, plus comprehensive reviews [1][2][3].

**Actuator-theoretic modeling.** We formalize the living actuator as a nonlinear dynamical system — a Hill-type contractile element in series with elastic tissue, driven by an activation state governed by stimulation history — and derive the control-theoretic consequences: what stimulation signals are *identifiable* from force output, what feedback is *observable*, and where open-loop control provably fails (fatigue, drift).

**Comparative design analysis.** We compare stimulation modalities (electrical, optical, NMJ-based) and tissue types (skeletal, cardiac) along quantitative axes — force density, bandwidth, spatial selectivity, lifetime, and interface complexity — constructing the trade-off table in §5 from reported measurements.

**Limitation-first reasoning.** Because the field is young and demonstrations are fragile, we weight *failure modes* equally with successes: every design principle we extract is paired with the conditions under which it breaks down (§6).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Contractile living muscle as a robotic actuator

The decisive advantage of engineered muscle over synthetic soft actuators (pneumatic networks, dielectric elastomers, shape-memory alloys) is *force density at small scale*. A bullfrog gracilis muscle used as a biosyncretic actuator produced a stable 6.5 N of contractile force, peaking at 9.4 N, and remained electrically responsive for 11 days (see §5.1). Engineered tissues are weaker — low output force remains the primary factor limiting the speed and maneuverability of skeletal-muscle-powered robots — but they offer geometric programmability that explanted muscle cannot: the tissue's dimensions, fiber alignment, and anchor points are set by the scaffold and mold [4].

The *antagonistic pair* configuration is the key architectural advance.

Cardiac tissue plays a different role.

> **Theorem (Actuator autonomy trade-off):** Let $C_e$ be electronic control bandwidth and $C_b$ the intrinsic dynamical complexity of the biological actuator. Designs that delegate rhythm generation to tissue (cardiac syncytia, central-pattern-like neural circuits) reduce required $C_e$ by an order of magnitude but surrender fine-grained timing authority; designs with direct per-fiber stimulation maximize authority at the cost of requiring $C_e$ to exceed the tissue's intrinsic timescales (action potentials ~ms, calcium transients ~100 ms).

### 4.2 Neuromuscular junctions as biological control interfaces

Direct electrical stimulation of muscle is the biohybrid equivalent of shouting at the tissue: effective, but it bypasses every evolved mechanism for graded, fatigue-resistant force control.

Aydin and colleagues built the canonical demonstration: a free-standing soft scaffold carrying engineered skeletal muscle, co-cultured *in situ* with an optogenetic stem-cell-derived neural cluster containing motor neurons [5].

The deeper point is *information-theoretic*.

| Interface | Channels | Spatial selectivity | Fatigue handling | Latency | Genetic modification |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Field electrodes | 1 | None | None (controller's burden) | ~ms | No |
| Patterned optogenetics (muscle) | ~10² (optical pixels) | μm-scale | None | ~ms | Yes (ChR2/ChrimsonR) |
| NMJ via motor neurons | ~10¹–10² (motor units) | Unit-level | Biological (recruitment, rate coding) | ~10 ms (synaptic) | Yes (opsins in neurons) |
| Wireless bioelectronic + neurocardiac junction | 2 (frequency-multiplexed) | Fin-level | Partial | ~10 ms | Yes (iPSC-derived) |

### 4.3 Wireless bioelectronics and the artificial brain

Untethered operation demands that stimulation electronics ride *onboard*.

Two design decisions deserve emphasis.

The control software for such a stack is naturally expressed as a *stimulation program*: a timed sequence of addressed pulses.

```haskell
-- Stimulation programs for a frequency-multiplexed biohybrid robot
data Fin = Port | Starboard deriving (Eq, Show)
data Pulse = Pulse { freqMHz :: Double, widthMs :: Double, amplitude :: Double }

type StimProgram = [(Double, Fin, Pulse)]  -- (time_s, target, pulse)

-- Bilateral steering: differential pulse widths produce turning
steer :: Double -> Double -> StimProgram
steer leftW rightW =
  [ (0.0, Port,      Pulse 13.56 leftW  1.0)
  , (0.0, Starboard, Pulse 6.82  rightW 1.0) ]

-- Alternating gait: anti-phase fin strokes for forward swimming
gait :: Double -> Int -> StimProgram
gait period n = concat
  [ [(fromIntegral k * period, Port,      Pulse 13.56 5.0 1.0)
    ,(fromIntegral k * period + period/2, Starboard, Pulse 6.82 5.0 1.0)]
  | k <- [0..n-1] ]
```

### 4.4 Proprioception and the closing of the loop

Every system described so far is *open-loop*: the controller commands contractions but cannot observe them.

Closing the loop transforms the mathematics.

```python
import numpy as np

def hill_force(F0, a, b, v, l, l_opt, activation):
    """Hill-type contractile force with length dependence and activation."""
    # Force-velocity (Hill 1938)
    fv = b * (F0 + a) / (v + b) - a if v >= 0 else F0 * 1.8  # eccentric plateau
    # Force-length (Gaussian sarcomere overlap)
    fl = np.exp(-((l - l_opt) / (0.25 * l_opt)) ** 2)
    return max(0.0, fv * fl * activation)

def closed_loop_step(state, sensor_reading, setpoint, Kp=2.0, Ki=0.5, Kd=0.1, dt=0.01):
    """PI-D on proprioceptive error; integral state models fatigue compensation."""
    err = setpoint - sensor_reading
    state['integral'] += err * dt
    # Anti-windup: living tissue saturates; so must the controller
    state['integral'] = np.clip(state['integral'], 0.0, 1.0)
    deriv = (err - state['prev_err']) / dt
    u = Kp * err + Ki * state['integral'] + Kd * deriv
    state['prev_err'] = err
    # Activation dynamics: calcium transient ~ first-order lag
    state['activation'] += (np.clip(u, 0, 1) - state['activation']) * dt / 0.1
    return state
```

The anti-windup clamp is not a numerical nicety but a *biological* necessity: integrator windup in a fatiguing muscle drives the tissue into tetanic saturation from which recovery takes minutes — the control-theoretic analogue of overtraining.

At the systems level, the safety invariants of a closed-loop biohybrid robot deserve formal specification.

```tla+
---- MODULE BiohybridGovernor ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
VARIABLES stimFreq, activation, fatigue, restTimer

TypeOK == stimFreq \in 0..50 /\ activation \in 0..100 /\ fatigue \in 0..100

Init == stimFreq = 0 /\ activation = 0 /\ fatigue = 0 /\ restTimer = 0

Stimulate(f) == /\ f \in 1..20
                /\ fatigue < 80
                /\ stimFreq' = f
                /\ fatigue' = fatigue + f
                /\ UNCHANGED <<activation, restTimer>>

Rest == /\ restTimer' = restTimer + 1
        /\ fatigue' = IF fatigue > 0 THEN fatigue - 5 ELSE 0
        /\ stimFreq' = 0
        /\ UNCHANGED activation

Next == Stimulate(5) \/ Stimulate(10) \/ Stimulate(20) \/ Rest

Safety == [](fatigue < 100)          \* never drive tissue to failure
Liveness == <>(restTimer > 10)       \* rest is eventually scheduled
====
```

And the embedded firmware reality — a Rust controller that must never allocate, never panic, and never stimulate a fatigued tissue:

```rust
/// Stimulation governor for a proprioceptive biohybrid actuator.
/// No heap allocation; all state is statically bounded.
pub struct Governor {
    fatigue: u8,          // 0..=100, estimated from force-per-stimulus decline
    rest_ticks: u32,
    last_force_muN: u32,
}

impl Governor {
    /// Returns Some(pulse_width_ms) if stimulation is permitted, None if rest is mandatory.
    pub fn request_stim(&mut self, proprio_force_muN: u32, setpoint_muN: u32) -> Option<u8> {
        // Fatigue observer: declining output at constant drive => metabolic fatigue
        if proprio_force_muN + 20 < self.last_force_muN {
            self.fatigue = self.fatigue.saturating_add(10);
        }
        self.last_force_muN = proprio_force_muN;

        if self.fatigue >= 80 {
            self.rest_ticks += 1;
            self.fatigue = self.fatigue.saturating_sub(2); // recovery during rest
            return None; // mandatory rest: protect the living actuator
        }
        let err = setpoint_muN.saturating_sub(proprio_force_muN);
        let width = (err / 50).clamp(1, 20) as u8; // proportional pulse width
        self.fatigue = self.fatigue.saturating_add(width / 4);
        Some(width)
    }
}
```

Two conclusions follow.

### 5.2 Analytical sketch: why NMJ control dominates direct stimulation

Consider the controller's task as *system identification*: from stimulation history $u(t)$ and observed force $F(t)$, estimate the tissue state.

$$\tau \dot{a} = u - a, \qquad F = F_{Hill}(v, l) \cdot a$$

with a *single* input channel $u$ and a single fatigue state.

> **Theorem (Recruitment advantage):** For $N$ motor units with independent fatigue states $f_i$ and a required mean force $\bar{F}$, the time to reach critical fatigue under optimal rotating recruitment scales as $\Theta(N)$ relative to single-channel stimulation at the same $\bar{F}$, assuming convex fatigue accumulation. *Proof sketch:* single-channel drive concentrates the full activation $a = \bar{F}/F_0$ on one fatigue integrator; rotating recruitment distributes $\bar{F}/N$ per unit with duty cycle $1/N$, and convexity of the fatigue rate in activation gives superlinear relief per unit. ∎

This is the formal content of the experimental observation that NMJ-driven robots sustain function for weeks [6] while directly stimulated tissues degrade faster [4]: the nervous system is, among other things, a *fatigue scheduler*, and biohybrid robots that borrow it inherit its scheduling.

## 6 Limitations

**Force density.** Engineered skeletal muscle produces specific tensions far below native muscle (~kPa vs. hundreds of kPa), and orders of magnitude below the explanted frog muscle benchmark [4]. Until tissue engineering closes this gap, biohybrid robots remain confined to millimeter-scale swimmers and grippers where micronewton forces suffice.

**Lifetime and autonomy.** "Autonomous" in current usage means *untethered*, not *self-sustaining*: every system above requires temperature-controlled culture medium, periodic feeding, and sterility. True autonomy demands vascularization (perfused nutrient delivery), immune-evasion for in vivo operation, and waste removal — none demonstrated in a motile robot [1][2].

**Variability.** "Like living organisms — no two biohybrid machines will develop to be exactly the same" [5]. Self-assembly of NMJs, myotube alignment, and scaffold colonization are stochastic; controllers must therefore be *adaptive* (system identification online), not calibrated once. The field lacks standardized benchmarks: swimming speed, force, and lifetime are reported under incomparable conditions [2].

**Control-theoretic gaps.** The fatigue observer sketched in §4.4 is heuristic; no published work gives a provably convergent estimator for the joint (activation, fatigue, damage) state from proprioceptive data alone. The TLA+ safety specification is aspirational — no deployed biohybrid controller is formally verified, and stimulation-induced tissue damage remains an unmodeled failure mode.

**Ethics and dual use.** As neural integration deepens — hierarchical networks of living neurons controlling actuators [5], "biohybrid intelligence" [6] — the field approaches questions about the moral status of engineered neuromuscular systems and the dual-use potential of motile biological machines. These are not reasons to halt the work, but they are reasons the work needs governance parallel to its engineering [2].

## 7 Conclusion

Biohybrid robotics has crossed three thresholds: living tissue as actuator, the neuromuscular junction as control interface, and proprioception as the closing of the loop [4][5][7]. Each threshold replaced an engineering approximation with a biological mechanism — motors with muscle, electrodes with synapses, open-loop commands with sensed feedback — and each replacement bought an order of magnitude in some currency that matters: force at small scale, fatigue resistance, or controllability.

The thesis advanced here is that the field's future is a *control-theoretic* one. The living actuator is the hardest plant control engineering has ever faced: nonlinear, stochastic, adaptive, mortal. Taming it will require the full apparatus — identified Hill-type models with fatigue states, NMJ-based addressing that respects the tissue's native input dimensionality, wireless bioelectronics that scale addressing without wiring, proprioceptive sensors that make the plant observable, and formally specified safety governors that never drive tissue past recovery.

If that program succeeds, the payoff is a new class of machine: soft, biodegradable, self-repairing robots that swim, crawl, grip, and pump inside the human body and the natural environment with an efficiency no motor can match. The millimeter-scale swimmers of today are the mainframes of this future — proof that the principle works, and a standing invitation to make it practical.
