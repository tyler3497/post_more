# Optimal Control Theory: Pontryagin's Maximum Principle, the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman Equation, and Model Predictive Control

## Abstract

Optimal control theory asks a deceptively simple question: among all admissible control trajectories that steer a dynamical system, which one minimizes a prescribed cost? Three pillars of the classical answer — **Pontryagin's maximum principle** (PMP), the **Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman** (HJB) equation, and **model predictive control** (MPC) — form a complete arc from necessary conditions to sufficient conditions to computation. This article develops that arc: we state PMP in Bolza form with adjoint equations, transversality, and the Hamiltonian maximum condition; we characterize the value function through the HJB equation and Crandall–Lions viscosity solutions, resolving the endemic nonsmoothness of optimal value functions; we derive the linear–quadratic regulator as the tractable special case where the Riccati equation yields explicit feedback; and we show how MPC converts the two-point boundary value problem of PMP and the PDE of HJB into a sequence of finite-dimensional optimizations, with stability from terminal ingredients. Sketches of the needle-variation, viscosity comparison, and MPC Lyapunov arguments are included.

## 1 Intro

Consider a controlled dynamical system evolving on a state space $\mathbb{R}^n$ according to

$$
\dot{x}(t) = f(t, x(t), u(t)), \qquad x(0) = x_0,
$$

where the control signal $u(\cdot)$ takes values in a compact set $U \subset \mathbb{R}^m$. The designer wishes to minimize a cost functional of Bolza type,

$$
J(x_0; u) = \int_0^T \ell(t, x(t), u(t))\, dt + g(x(T)),
$$

over all measurable controls with values in $U$. This is the canonical *finite-horizon optimal control problem* — the mathematical skeleton beneath spacecraft trajectory design, process control, robotics, and reinforcement learning.

Three traditions attack this problem. The *variational* tradition, culminating in **Pontryagin's maximum principle**, perturbs a candidate optimal control with infinitesimal "needle" variations and extracts a necessary condition: there must exist a costate trajectory $p(\cdot)$ such that the optimal control maximizes a Hamiltonian at almost every instant [1]. The *dynamic programming* tradition considers the optimal cost as a function of the initial state — the *value function* — and derives the **Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation**, a first-order fully nonlinear PDE whose solution synthesizes optimal feedback [5]. The *computational* tradition, epitomized by **model predictive control**, sidesteps both the boundary-value problem and the PDE by repeatedly solving finite-horizon optimizations online and applying only the first control action [7].

> **Thesis:** PMP and HJB are not competing answers but dual descriptions of the same extremal structure — the costate of PMP is the gradient of the value function of HJB wherever the latter is differentiable — and MPC is their industrial embodiment: a practical synthesis through repeated numerical optimization with provable stability guarantees.

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the classical antecedents; Section 3 lays out the Bolza problem and the Hamiltonian formalism; Section 4 dives into PMP, HJB and viscosity solutions, LQR/Riccati theory, and MPC; Section 5 gives proof sketches and a worked example; Section 6 confronts limitations.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From calculus of variations to optimal control

The classical calculus of variations (Euler, Lagrange, Hamilton) studies unconstrained extremals with *open* control sets and smooth variations. Pontryagin's decisive insight was to handle **control constraints** — $U$ need not be open — by replacing derivative stationarity with a *maximum condition* over the admissible set [2]. Where Euler–Lagrange requires the control in the interior of its domain, PMP allows bang-bang controls jumping between boundary points of $U$, precisely what time-optimal and fuel-optimal problems produce.

### 2.2 Direct versus indirect methods

Numerical optimal control splits into two philosophies [1]:| Approach | Core idea | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Indirect methods** | Discretize the PMP optimality conditions; solve a two-point boundary value problem | High accuracy, exploits structure, cheap per iteration | Requires costate guess; sensitive; hard with constraints |
| **Direct methods** | Discretize the control and state first (collocation, shooting); solve a nonlinear program | Robust initialization; handles constraints naturally | Large-scale NLP; accuracy limited by mesh |
| **Dynamic programming** | Solve the HJB PDE for the value function globally | Global optimality; yields feedback policy | Curse of dimensionality; value function rarely smooth |
| **MPC** | Repeated online finite-horizon optimization | Handles constraints; feedback via re-optimization | Needs stability ingredients; real-time computation |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Bolza problem and the Hamiltonian

Fix a horizon $T > 0$ and an initial state $x_0$. We consider the Bolza problem

$$
\min_{u(\cdot) \in \mathcal{U}} J(x_0; u), \qquad
J(x_0; u) = \int_0^T \ell(t, x, u)\, dt + g(x(T)),
$$

subject to $\dot{x} = f(t, x, u)$, $x(0) = x_0$, $u(t) \in U$ almost everywhere on $(0, T)$.

The central object is the **Hamiltonian**. In the "abnormal/normal" convention with multiplier $\eta \in \{0, 1\}$,

$$
H_\eta(t, x, u, p) = p^\top f(t, x, u) - \eta\,\ell(t, x, u),
$$

where $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the *costate* (adjoint) variable — the Lagrange multiplier of the differential-equation constraint, evolving backward in time. When $\eta = 1$ the extremal is *normal*; when $\eta = 0$ it is *abnormal*: the cost drops out and the conditions become purely geometric [1].

### 3.2 Methodological roadmap

Our methodology proceeds in four stages: **(1) necessary conditions** via needle variations — adjoint equation, transversality, maximum condition [1][2]; **(2) global description** through the value function, the HJB equation, and viscosity theory [5][6]; **(3) the tractable LQR specialization**, where both frameworks collapse to the Riccati equation and explicit feedback [3][4]; and **(4) computational synthesis** via MPC as repeated finite-horizon optimization with stabilizing terminal ingredients [7][8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Pontryagin's maximum principle

#### 4.1.1 Statement of the theorem

> **Theorem:** (Pontryagin's maximum principle, fixed horizon, Bolza form.) Let $(x^*, u^*)$ be optimal for the Bolza problem above. Then there exist a scalar $\eta \in \{0, 1\}$ and an absolutely continuous costate trajectory $p^*: (0, T) \to \mathbb{R}^n$ such that $(\eta, p^*(t))$ is never identically zero (nontriviality), and:
>
> 1. **Adjoint equation:** $-\dot{p}^*(t) = \nabla_x H_\eta(t, x^*(t), u^*(t), p^*(t))$ almost everywhere.
> 2. **Transversality:** $p^*(T) = -\eta\,\nabla g(x^*(T))$ for a free terminal state (orthogonal to the terminal set $X_T$ when constrained).
> 3. **Maximum condition:** for almost every $t$,
>
>    $$
>    H_\eta(t, x^*(t), u^*(t), p^*(t)) = \max_{u \in U} H_\eta(t, x^*(t), u, p^*(t)).
>    $$
>
>    Equivalently, the optimal control is a pointwise maximizer of the Hamiltonian [1][2].

Several features deserve emphasis. First, the maximization is *pointwise in time*: an infinite-dimensional optimization over control trajectories reduces to a family of finite-dimensional maximizations, one per instant, once the costate is known. Second, the adjoint equation is *linear* in $p$ and integrated *backward* — the costate measures the sensitivity of the optimal cost to state perturbations, the shadow price of the dynamics. Third, nontriviality prevents the vacuous all-zero multiplier solution.

#### 4.1.2 Free final time and the vanishing Hamiltonian

When the final time $T$ is itself a decision variable (minimum-time problems), an additional condition appears: the maximized Hamiltonian vanishes along the extremal, $H_\eta \equiv 0$ a.e., reflecting stationarity with respect to time shifts [2]. For autonomous problems with fixed $T$, the Hamiltonian is instead constant along extremals.

#### 4.1.3 Bang-bang controls and switching functions

A celebrated consequence arises when the Hamiltonian is *affine* in the control. For the double integrator $\ddot{y} = u$ with $|u| \le 1$, maximization gives $u^*(t) = \operatorname{sign}(p_2(t))$ whenever $p_2(t) \neq 0$: the optimal control saturates at the boundary of $U$ almost everywhere — a **bang-bang** control — switching only when the *switching function* $p_2$ crosses zero [2].

#### 4.1.4 The proof idea: needle variations

Perturbing $u^*$ on a tiny interval around a Lebesgue point $\tau$ by an arbitrary $v \in U$ would strictly decrease the cost if the maximum condition failed there — contradicting optimality. A separating hyperplane for the resulting cone of endpoint variations yields the costate, and the adjoint equation conserves $\langle p(t), \delta x(t)\rangle$ along linearized variations [1][2]. The full sketch is in §5.1.

### 4.2 The Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation and viscosity solutions

#### 4.2.1 The value function and dynamic programming

Define the *value function*

$$
V(t, x) = \inf_{u(\cdot) \in \mathcal{U}} \left\{ \int_t^T \ell(s, x(s), u(s))\, ds + g(x(T)) \;\middle|\; x(t) = x \right\}.
$$

Bellman's *dynamic programming principle* (DPP) states that for any small $\delta > 0$, the optimal cost splits into the cost over $(t, t+\delta)$ plus the value function at the resulting state. Dividing by $\delta$ and letting $\delta \to 0$ — under smoothness assumptions on $V$ — yields the **Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation**

$$
-V_t(t, x) + \sup_{u \in U} \left\{ -\nabla_x V(t, x)^\top f(t, x, u) - \ell(t, x, u) \right\} = 0,
$$

with terminal condition $V(T, x) = g(x)$. The DPP also yields a *verification theorem*: if a smooth function $W$ satisfies the HJB equation and a feedback law attains the infimum in the Hamiltonian at each point, then $W = V$ and the feedback is optimal — the sufficiency counterpart to PMP's necessity.

#### 4.2.2 The smoothness problem

The fatal flaw of the classical theory is that value functions are *generically nonsmooth*. Even the minimum-time function for linear systems with bounded controls is typically only Lipschitz, with kinks along switching surfaces; the eikonal equation $|\nabla V| = 1$ already exhibits gradient discontinuities at the medial axis — and wherever $\nabla_x V$ fails to exist, the classical HJB equation is meaningless.

#### 4.2.3 Viscosity solutions: the Crandall–Lions framework

> **Theorem:** (Value function as viscosity solution.) Under standard regularity and boundedness hypotheses on $f$, $\ell$, and $g$, the value function $V$ is the unique viscosity solution of the HJB equation with the given terminal data [5].

A continuous function $V$ is a **viscosity subsolution** if, whenever a smooth test function $\varphi$ touches $V$ from above at $(t_0, x_0)$ (i.e., $V - \varphi$ attains a local maximum there), the PDE inequality holds with the derivatives of $\varphi$ substituted:

$$
-\varphi_t(t_0, x_0) + \mathcal{H}(t_0, x_0, -\nabla_x \varphi(t_0, x_0)) \le 0.
$$

A **viscosity supersolution** reverses the contact (touching from below) and the inequality. A viscosity solution is both. Intuitively, the PDE is tested only against smooth functions that kiss the graph of $V$ — at kinks, no smooth function can touch from both sides simultaneously with arbitrary gradients, so the definition naturally accommodates nondifferentiability.

#### 4.2.4 Reconciling PMP and HJB

The two frameworks meet through the *sensitivity relations*. Wherever $V$ is differentiable along an optimal trajectory, the PMP costate satisfies $p^*(t) = -\nabla_x V(t, x^*(t))$ — the costate is the negative spatial gradient of the value function. More generally, $p^*(t)$ belongs to the *super*differential of $V$ at $(t, x^*(t))$. Thus PMP describes the *local* extremal structure along a single trajectory, while HJB describes the *global* field of extremals; viscosity theory is what lets the global description survive nonsmoothness [5].

### 4.3 The linear–quadratic regulator and Riccati theory

#### 4.3.1 Problem formulation

The most important tractable special case takes linear dynamics and quadratic cost:

$$
\dot{x} = Ax + Bu, \qquad
J = \int_0^T \left( x^\top Q x + u^\top R u \right) dt + x(T)^\top P_1 x(T),
$$

with $Q \succeq 0$, $R \succ 0$, $P_1 \succeq 0$ symmetric. With unconstrained controls ($U = \mathbb{R}^m$), the PMP maximum condition reduces to stationarity: $\nabla_u H = 0$ [3].

#### 4.3.2 The Riccati differential equation

Applying PMP with $H = p^\top(Ax + Bu) - (x^\top Qx + u^\top Ru)$ gives $u^* = -R^{-1}B^\top p$ (absorbing the factor of two), the adjoint equation $-\dot{p} = A^\top p - 2Qx$, and $p(T) = -2P_1 x(T)$. The decisive ansatz is that the costate is linear in the state: $p(t) = -2P(t)x(t)$. Substituting yields the **matrix Riccati differential equation**

$$
-\dot{P} = A^\top P + PA - PBR^{-1}B^\top P + Q, \qquad P(T) = P_1,
$$

integrated backward in time. The optimal control is then the linear feedback law

$$
u^*(t) = -R^{-1}B^\top P(t)\, x(t),
$$

valid from *any* initial state — the two-point boundary value problem collapses to a single matrix ODE [3].

#### 4.3.3 The infinite-horizon limit and the algebraic Riccati equation

Sending $T \to \infty$ and dropping the terminal penalty, $P(t)$ converges (under stabilizability of $(A, B)$ and detectability of $(Q^{1/2}, A)$) to a constant positive semidefinite $P_\infty$ satisfying the **algebraic Riccati equation** (ARE)

$$
A^\top P + PA - PBR^{-1}B^\top P + Q = 0.
$$

Among its solutions, the *stabilizing* one — for which $A - BR^{-1}B^\top P$ is Hurwitz — gives the optimal infinite-horizon feedback $u = -R^{-1}B^\top P_\infty x$ [4].

The following compact Python routine integrates the Riccati ODE backward for a double integrator and verifies the closed loop:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

A = np.array([[0.0, 1.0], [0.0, 0.0]])   # double integrator
B = np.array([[0.0], [1.0]])
Q = np.eye(2); R = np.array([[1.0]]); P1 = np.eye(2); T = 10.0

def riccati_rhs(t, P_flat):
    P = P_flat.reshape(2, 2)
    dP = -(A.T @ P + P @ A - P @ B @ np.linalg.inv(R) @ B.T @ P + Q)
    return dP.flatten()

sol = solve_ivp(riccati_rhs, (T, 0.0), P1.flatten(), dense_output=True)  # backward
P0 = sol.y[:, -1].reshape(2, 2)
K = np.linalg.inv(R) @ B.T @ P0   # optimal gain, u = -K x
print("P(0) =\n", P0)
print("K =", K.ravel())
print("Closed-loop eigenvalues:", np.linalg.eigvals(A - B @ K))
```

Running the script yields a positive definite $P(0)$, a stabilizing gain, and both closed-loop eigenvalues in the open left half-plane — the Riccati solution certifies optimality *and* stability simultaneously.

#### 4.3.4 The scalar sanity check

For $\dot{x} = u$ with cost $\int_0^\infty (x^2 + u^2)\, dt$, the ARE gives $P_\infty = 1$, the feedback $u^* = -x$, and $V(x) = x^2$; the HJB equation is satisfied with the minimizing $u = -V'/2 = -x$. All three frameworks agree exactly — a miniature of the general theory.

### 4.4 Model predictive control

#### 4.4.1 The receding-horizon idea

The HJB PDE is intractable in high dimensions, and the PMP boundary value problem is fragile to initialize. **Model predictive control** takes a third path: at each sampling instant $t_k$ with measured state $x_k$, solve the finite-horizon optimal control problem

$$
\min_{\mathbf{u}} \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \ell(x_{k+i|k}, u_{k+i|k}) + V_f(x_{k+N|k}) \quad \text{s.t. dynamics, } x_{k+i|k} \in \mathcal{X},\; u_{k+i|k} \in \mathcal{U},\; x_{k+N|k} \in \mathcal{X}_f,
$$

apply only the first element $u_{k|k}^*$ to the plant, then repeat at $t_{k+1}$ with the new measurement. Each step re-plans from the current state, converting open-loop optimization into feedback [7].

#### 4.4.2 Stability: the terminal ingredients

Stability is *not* automatic in MPC — a finite-horizon cost with no terminal penalty can produce a closed loop that oscillates or diverges, because the truncated problem is myopic about what happens beyond the horizon [8]. The classical remedy adds three *terminal ingredients*:

| Ingredient | Role in the stability proof |
|---|---|
| **Terminal cost** $V_f(x)$ | Control Lyapunov function bounding the infinite-horizon tail cost |
| **Terminal set** $\mathcal{X}_f$ | Control-invariant set where a local controller takes over |
| **Terminal controller** $\kappa_f(x)$ | Locally stabilizing law (often the LQR gain), admissible inside $\mathcal{X}_f$ |

The stability argument is a Lyapunov descent: letting $V_N^*(x_k)$ denote the optimal MPC cost, the tail of the optimal predicted sequence — extended by one step of the terminal controller — is *feasible* at $t_{k+1}$ (recursive feasibility), and its cost is at most $V_N^*(x_k) - \ell(x_k, u_k^*)$, so

$$
V_N^*(x_{k+1}) - V_N^*(x_k) \le -\ell(x_k, u_k^*) \le 0,
$$

with strict decrease away from the origin. Hence $V_N^*$ is a Lyapunov function and the origin is asymptotically stable [7]. Modern analyses also establish stability *without* terminal ingredients, provided the horizon is long enough relative to a controllability bound — trading design effort for online computation [8].

#### 4.4.3 MPC as the computational synthesis

MPC inherits from PMP the necessary conditions inside the online optimizer (direct transcription solves the KKT system, the discrete-time PMP), and from HJB the value-function idea — the MPC cost $V_N^*$ is a finite-horizon approximation of $V$, and the terminal cost $V_f$ approximates the infinite-horizon tail. As $N \to \infty$ the MPC law recovers the infinite-horizon optimal feedback: computation asymptotically recovers theory [7][8].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: PMP via needle variations

We sketch the argument for a fixed-horizon problem with free terminal state [2]:

1. **Variation.** Let $u^*$ be optimal and $\tau$ a Lebesgue point of $u^*$. Define $u_\varepsilon$ to equal an arbitrary $v \in U$ on $(\tau - \varepsilon, \tau)$ and $u^*$ elsewhere.
2. **Endpoint sensitivity.** The perturbed endpoint satisfies $x_\varepsilon(T) = x^*(T) + \varepsilon\, \Phi(T, \tau)[f(v) - f(u^*(\tau))] + o(\varepsilon)$, with $\Phi$ the transition matrix of the linearized dynamics.
3. **Cost variation.** The first-order cost change is $\varepsilon\,\langle p(\tau), f(v) - f(u^*(\tau))\rangle + o(\varepsilon)$, where $p$ solves the adjoint equation with $p(T) = -\nabla g(x^*(T))$.
4. **Contradiction.** If $H(\tau, v) > H(\tau, u^*(\tau))$ for some $v$, the needle strictly decreases the cost for small $\varepsilon$ — impossible. Hence the maximum condition holds at every Lebesgue point, i.e., almost everywhere [2].

The full theorem adds the multiplier $\eta$ to handle the abnormal case and transversality for constrained endpoints [1].

### 5.2 Proof sketch: the viscosity comparison principle

Let $u$ be a subsolution and $v$ a supersolution with $u \le v$ on the parabolic boundary, and suppose $u - v$ attains a positive maximum $M > 0$ interiorly. *Doubling variables*, maximize $\Phi(t, x, s, y) = u(t, x) - v(s, y) - \frac{|x - y|^2 + |t - s|^2}{2\alpha}$. The maximizer $(t_\alpha, x_\alpha, s_\alpha, y_\alpha)$ satisfies $|x_\alpha - y_\alpha| \to 0$ as $\alpha \to 0$. Applying the viscosity inequalities there and using uniform continuity of the Hamiltonian yields $M \le 0$, a contradiction. Hence $u \le v$ everywhere — comparison holds, and viscosity solutions are unique [5][6].

### 5.3 LQR optimality via completing the square

For the infinite-horizon LQR, let $P \succeq 0$ solve the ARE and set $K = R^{-1}B^\top P$. For any admissible $u$, differentiating $x^\top Px$ along trajectories gives

$$
x^\top Qx + u^\top Ru = \frac{d}{dt}(x^\top Px) + (u + Kx)^\top R (u + Kx).
$$

Integrating from $0$ to $\infty$ with $x(\infty) = 0$, the cost equals $x_0^\top P x_0$ plus a nonnegative term, minimized uniquely by $u = -Kx$ [4]. The Riccati solution *is* the value function, $V(x) = x^\top P x$ — smooth, so HJB needs no viscosity theory here.

### 5.4 Numerical illustration: bang-bang time-optimal control

For the double integrator $\ddot{y} = u$, $|u| \le 1$, driven from $(1, 0)$ to the origin in minimum time, PMP gives $u^* = \operatorname{sign}(p_2)$ with $H \equiv 0$; since $p_2$ is affine in time it crosses zero at most once, so the optimal control has a single switch. The switching curve is $y = -\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{y}|\dot{y}|$, the policy applies $u = -1$ then $u = +1$, and $T^* = 2$ — three methods, one extremal [2][8].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Necessity is not sufficiency.** PMP characterizes extremals, not minimizers. Abnormal extremals ($\eta = 0$) ignore the cost entirely, and non-convex problems admit merely stationary extremals. Global optimality requires the HJB verification theorem or convexity [1][2].
2. **Nonsmoothness is the rule.** Value functions develop kinks along switching surfaces; viscosity theory restores well-posedness but not explicit formulas. Numerical HJB solvers scale poorly beyond a handful of state dimensions [5][6].
3. **Boundary value problems are fragile.** Indirect methods need a costate initialization that is rarely available; state inequality constraints introduce measure-valued multipliers and costate jumps [1].
4. **MPC pays in real time.** Each sampling instant requires solving a (possibly nonconvex) NLP within the sampling period. Terminal ingredients shrink the feasible region and need offline design; horizon-based stability without them shifts the burden to computation [7][8].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Optimal control theory is a single story told in three dialects. **Pontryagin's maximum principle** gives the local, trajectory-wise necessary conditions: adjoint equation, transversality, pointwise Hamiltonian maximization [1][2]. The **Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation** gives the global characterization through the value function, rescued from endemic nonsmoothness by Crandall–Lions viscosity theory [5][6]. The **linear–quadratic regulator** is the special case where both collapse to the Riccati equation and explicit linear feedback [3][4]. **Model predictive control** is the engineering synthesis: repeated finite-horizon optimization in a receding horizon, with terminal ingredients guaranteeing stability [7][8]. The costate of PMP is the gradient of the value function of HJB, and the value function of MPC is its finite-horizon shadow — one geometry of optimality, seen from three vantage points.
