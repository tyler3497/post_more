# Provable Adversarial Robustness in Deep Networks: Neyman–Pearson Randomized-Smoothing Certificates, Interval Bound Propagation, and Lipschitz-Constrained Architectures

# Provable Adversarial Robustness in Deep Networks: Neyman–Pearson Randomized-Smoothing Certificates, Interval Bound Propagation, and Lipschitz-Constrained Architectures

## Abstract

This thesis develops a unified treatment of *certified* adversarial robustness for neural networks: defenses that carry a mathematical proof that no adversary within a prescribed perturbation budget can alter the model's decision. We present three canonical certification frameworks. First, **randomized smoothing**, which converts any base classifier into a smoothed classifier with a tight ℓ₂ robustness radius derived from the Neyman–Pearson lemma [1], certified via Monte Carlo estimation with Clopper–Pearson confidence bounds. Second, **interval bound propagation (IBP)** and its convex-relaxation generalizations, which propagate input perturbation intervals through the network to obtain sound upper bounds on the worst-case logit margin [3], including the convex outer adversarial polytope dual-network construction [2]. Third, **Lipschitz-constrained architectures**, where bounding the global Lipschitz constant of the network converts per-input margin information into an immediate certificate. We characterize the *robustness–accuracy tradeoff* both theoretically, through the robust-error decomposition and the TRADES surrogate [4], and empirically via certified-accuracy curves; we interpret the tradeoff through the lens of robust versus non-robust features [5] and discuss how denoising pre-processing extends smoothing certificates to pretrained classifiers [6]. Our synthesis yields practical guidance on choosing a certification methodology under constraints of scalability, certificate strength, and computational budget.

---

## 1. Introduction

The discovery that imperceptible perturbations can flip the predictions of state-of-the-art classifiers revealed a fundamental brittleness in deep learning. *Empirical defenses* — those evaluated only against known attacks — repeatedly collapsed when stronger adaptive attacks emerged, inaugurating what the literature describes as an "arms race" between defenses and attacks [2]. **Certified defenses** break this cycle by construction: instead of asserting robustness against a fixed attack suite, they compute, for each input, a *certificate* — a radius R(x) such that the classifier provably returns the same label for every perturbation within an ℓ_p ball of radius R(x).

Formally, given a classifier f: ℝ^d → Y, an input x with label y, and a perturbation budget ε, we say f is *certifiably robust* at x when

> **Theorem (Robustness Certificate):** A classifier f admits a certificate of radius R(x) > 0 at input x if g(x) = y and, for all δ with ‖δ‖_p ≤ R(x), the certified prediction remains invariant: g(x + δ) = g(x).

The certificate must hold *for all* adversaries within the budget, including adversaries unknown to the defender. This thesis compares the three families that define the field: **probabilistic smoothing certificates**, **deterministic convex-relaxation certificates**, and **Lipschitz-margin certificates**, and quantifies the central empirical phenomenon — the **robustness–accuracy tradeoff** [4] — together with its feature-level explanation [5].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Threat model

We consider the standard norm-bounded threat model: an adversary may replace input x by any x′ with ‖x − x′‖_p ≤ ε, typically p ∈ {2, ∞}. The defender must guarantee correct classification over the entire ε-ball B_p(x, ε). *Empirical* robustness (e.g., PGD accuracy) measures performance against a specific optimizer; *certified* robustness lower-bounds performance against *every* optimizer — the distinction motivates Gowal et al.'s demonstration that PGD can dramatically under-report true vulnerability even on small networks [3].

### 2.2 Hypothesis-class considerations

Certification requires the classifier's reachable-output set under input perturbations to be tractable. Three strategies make this tractable:

1. **Randomize the classifier** so its *smoothed* decision regions are analyzable via hypothesis testing [1].
2. **Over-approximate the reachable set** with a convex superset that admits efficient optimization [2, 3].
3. **Constrain the function's sensitivity** (Lipschitz constant) so margins imply robustness directly.

Each strategy induces a distinct training paradigm, verification algorithm, and tightness profile.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is comparative and reconstructive: we derive the three certificate families from first principles, state their tightness guarantees, and design a unified experimental protocol measuring **certified accuracy** — the fraction of test points that are both correctly classified *and* certified at radius ε.

### 3.1 Randomized smoothing and the Neyman–Pearson certificate

Given a base classifier f: ℝ^d → Y, define the *smoothed classifier*

> g(x) = argmax_{c ∈ Y} ℙ_{δ ∼ 𝒩(0, σ²I)}[f(x + δ) = c].

The key result of Cohen et al. [1] is a *tight* ℓ₂ guarantee:

> **Theorem (Cohen–Rosenfeld–Kolter):** Let c_A be the class returned by g(x), p_A = ℙ(f(x+δ) = c_A), and p̄_B ≥ max_{c ≠ c_A} ℙ(f(x+δ) = c). Then g(x + δ) = c_A for all ‖δ‖₂ ≤ R with
>
> R = (σ/2)(Φ⁻¹(p_A) − Φ⁻¹(p̄_B)),
>
> where Φ⁻¹ is the standard Gaussian inverse CDF, and this bound is *tight*: some base classifier f attains it with equality.

The proof is a striking application of the **Neyman–Pearson lemma** from hypothesis testing: among all binary tests between 𝒩(x, σ²I) and 𝒩(x+δ, σ²I) with fixed false-positive rate, the likelihood-ratio test maximizes the true-positive rate, which yields the worst-case base classifier the adversary could face [1]. Because p_A and p̄_B cannot be computed exactly for deep f, Cohen et al. estimate them by Monte Carlo sampling with *Clopper–Pearson* exact confidence intervals, abstaining when the confidence requirement fails — a certified prediction or abstention protocol, not a silent one.

Extensions include Laplacian-noise smoothing for ℓ₁ certificates [1] and denoised smoothing, which prepends a learned denoiser so that *pretrained* classifiers inherit smoothing certificates without retraining [6].

```python
def certify(x, f, sigma, n0=100, n=100_000, alpha=0.001):
    """Monte Carlo certification of the smoothed classifier (Cohen et al. [1])."""
    import numpy as np
    from scipy.stats import norm, binomtest
    noise = lambda m: np.random.normal(0, sigma, size=(m,) + x.shape)
    counts0 = np.bincount([f(x + d) for d in noise(n0)])
    cA = int(np.argmax(counts0))
    hits = sum(f(x + d) == cA for d in noise(n))
    pA = binomtest(hits, n).proportion_confint(confidence_level=1 - alpha)[0]
    return (cA, 0.5 * sigma * (norm.ppf(pA) - norm.ppf(1 - pA))) if pA > 0.5 else ("ABSTAIN", 0.0)
```

### 3.2 Interval bound propagation and convex relaxations

IBP [3] propagates axis-aligned boxes through the network. For an input box [x̲, x̄] = [x − ε**1**, x + ε**1**], affine layers propagate bounds analytically and ReLU clips them:

> **Definition (IBP propagation):** For layer z = Wv + b with v ∈ [v̲, v̄], the output box is [z̲, z̄] where z̲ = W⁺v̲ + W⁻v̄ + b and z̄ = W⁺v̄ + W⁻v̲ + b, splitting W into positive and negative parts; ReLU maps [v̲, v̄] ↦ [max(v̲, 0), max(v̄, 0)].

Gowal et al. [3] showed that although IBP bounds are loose for *general* networks, training with a weighted robust loss (κ·standard + (1−κ)·worst-case over the IBP box) induces networks whose bounds *self-tighten*: MNIST verified error fell from 3.67% to 2.23% at ε = 0.1 (ℓ∞) and CIFAR-10 from 78.22% to 72.91% at ε = 8/255.

The convex-relaxation program of Wong and Kolter [2] generalizes IBP: each ReLU's non-convex graph over [l, u] is replaced by its convex hull (the *triangle relaxation*), converting worst-case-loss estimation into a linear program; remarkably, the LP's dual is itself representable as a deep network, so verified training costs only a few extra forward/backward passes. Their MNIST CNN certifies < 5.8% error against *any* ℓ∞ attack at ε = 0.1 [2].

### 3.3 Lipschitz-constrained architectures

If f is L-Lipschitz (‖f(x) − f(x′)‖ ≤ L‖x − x′‖), the logit margin m(x) = f_{y}(x) − max_{c≠y} f_c(x) yields an immediate certificate R(x) = m(x)/(√2·L). Architectures achieving small *certifiable* L include:

- **Orthogonal/spectral-normalized layers**: constraining each layer's spectral norm σ_max(W) ≤ 1 gives global L ≤ 1 for 1-Lipschitz activations.
- **Lipschitz-margin training** (Tsuzuku-style losses that jointly maximize margins and minimize the layer-wise Lipschitz constant).
- **GloRo-style** constructions that compute a provable lower bound on the margin during the forward pass.

The appeal is *deterministic, single-forward-pass* certification with no Monte Carlo or LP solve; the cost is expressiveness: 1-Lipschitz networks are provably less expressive for certain functions, a structural source of the accuracy tradeoff.

| Method | Certificate type | Norm | Scalability | Tightness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Randomized smoothing [1] | Probabilistic (high-confidence) | ℓ₂ (ℓ₁ variants) | Scales to ImageNet | Tight for the smoothed classifier |
| Convex polytope [2] | Deterministic LP dual | ℓ∞ | Small/medium CNNs | Loose for deep nets |
| IBP + verified training [3] | Deterministic interval | ℓ∞ | Large CNNs, downscaled ImageNet | Self-tightening via training |
| Lipschitz margin | Deterministic analytic | ℓ₂ | Very fast (1 forward pass) | Conservative |

```haskell
-- IBP bound propagation as a pure function over interval layers
data Interval = I { lo :: Double, hi :: Double }

affineIBP :: [[Double]] -> [Double] -> [Interval] -> [Interval]
affineIBP w b vs = [ I (dot wPlus loV + dot wMinus hiV + bi)
                       (dot wPlus hiV + dot wMinus loV + bi)
                   | (row, bi) <- zip w b
                   , let (wPlus, wMinus) = (map (max 0) row, map (min 0) row)
                   , let (loV, hiV) = (map lo vs, map hi vs)
                   , let dot a c = sum (zipWith (*) a c) ]
  where relu (I l h) = I (max l 0) (max h 0)
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Neyman–Pearson geometry of smoothing

The tightness result [1] rests on a reduction: for *any* base classifier f with fixed (p_A, p̄_B), the adversary's best-case perturbation δ is countered by the likelihood-ratio test between the two Gaussian location families. The certified radius is therefore the *largest radius consistent with optimal hypothesis testing*, not an artifact of a particular relaxation. The σ hyperparameter controls the fundamental dial: larger σ grows R but degrades the base classifier's clean accuracy, since f(x + δ) must be correct under heavy noise [1].

### 4.2 Training for certifiability

All three frameworks require the *base model* to be adapted:

1. **Noise-augmented training** (smoothing): train f on 𝒩(x, σ²I)-corrupted inputs so p_A stays high [1]; MACER-style objectives directly maximize the expected certified radius.
2. **Verified training with IBP loss** [3]: minimize a convex combination of the standard and IBP worst-case cross-entropy with an ε-ramping schedule; the ramp is essential, since starting at full ε collapses optimization.
3. **Smoothed adversarial training**: Salman et al. generate *smoothed* PGD attacks against the smoothed classifier and train on them, improving certified ℓ₂ accuracy [6 discusses the smoothed-classifier attack landscape].

### 4.3 The robustness–accuracy tradeoff: theory

Zhang et al. [4] decompose robust error into natural plus boundary error:

> ℛ_rob(f) = ℛ_nat(f) + ℛ_bdy(f),

where boundary error counts points classified correctly whose ε-ball intersects the decision boundary. They derive the tightest uniform upper bound on the robust-natural gap via classification-calibrated surrogates, inspiring **TRADES**:

```rust
// TRADES-style objective: standard loss + beta * KL(f(x) || f(x_adv))
// KL divergence between clean and adversarial logits acts as the
// boundary-error regularizer from Zhang et al. [4].
fn trades_loss(logits_clean: &[f32], logits_adv: &[f32], y: usize, beta: f32) -> f32 {
    let ce = cross_entropy(logits_clean, y);
    let kl = kl_divergence(softmax(logits_clean), softmax(logits_adv));
    ce + beta * kl
}
```

Ilyas et al. [5] provide the *feature-level* explanation: standard training exploits **non-robust features** — highly predictive but brittle patterns — while certified training forces reliance on **robust features** alone. Their constructed "robust datasets" (inputs stripped of non-robust features) train models that are naturally robust, proving the tradeoff is a property of the *data geometry*, not merely of optimization [5].

### 4.4 Certified accuracy as the evaluation standard

The community metric is the **certified-accuracy curve**: the fraction of test inputs both correctly classified and certified at radius ≥ r, plotted against r. Key empirical facts:

- On CIFAR-10 (ℓ₂, σ = 0.25), smoothed ResNet-110 certifies ~61% at r = 0.5 with ~77% clean accuracy, vs. 95% clean for the standard model [1].
- IBP-trained CNNs reach ~34% *verified* accuracy on CIFAR-10 at ε = 8/255 (ℓ∞) [3].
- Denoised smoothing recovers usable certificates from off-the-shelf pretrained classifiers, narrowing the gap between standard pretraining and verified training [6].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We consolidate the following reproducible findings from the literature:

1. **Smoothing dominates ℓ₂ certification at scale.** The Neyman–Pearson certificate [1] remains the only method certifying ImageNet-scale models with non-vacuous radii; the price is Monte Carlo inference cost (n = 100,000 samples per point) and a σ-driven accuracy penalty.
2. **IBP training is the scalable ℓ∞ option.** With ε-ramping and the κ-weighted loss, IBP [3] trains the largest verifiable networks to date; verified accuracy trails empirical (PGD) accuracy by a large margin, reflecting bound looseness.
3. **TRADES quantifies the tradeoff.** The β hyperparameter in [4] traces a clean Pareto frontier: β → 0 recovers natural accuracy, β → ∞ approaches the boundary-error floor.
4. **Features explain the tradeoff.** Models trained on Ilyas et al.'s robust-feature datasets [5] inherit robustness without adversarial training, supporting the hypothesis that certified training is *feature selection*, not merely margin maximization.
5. **Pretrained models are certifiable.** Denoised smoothing [6] shows certification need not require certified-from-scratch training, enabling certificates for foundation-scale models.

> **Proposition (Certificate composition):** If f₁ has Lipschitz certificate R₁ and smoothed classifier g₂ certifies R₂ probabilistically, the sequential composition g₂ ∘ f₁ certifies min-scaled radii only under joint Lipschitz/smoothing analysis — certificates do *not* compose for free. This is an open design problem.

## 6. Limitations

- **Norm myopia.** All certificates bind an ℓ_p ball, while human perception and semantic adversaries (rotations, corruptions) fall outside it; ℓ_p certificates are neither necessary nor sufficient for real-world robustness [5].
- **High-dimensional curse.** Blum et al. show that for ℓ_p, p > 2, on high-dimensional images, the smoothing noise dominates the signal and certificates become vacuous — smoothing is fundamentally an ℓ₂ (and ℓ₁) tool [1].
- **Computational cost.** Certification costs range from 100k forward passes per input (smoothing) to doubled training with LP-dual networks [2]; none are free at deployment.
- **Tightness gaps.** IBP bounds are loose for deep networks unless the network adapts during training [3]; convex relaxations scale poorly beyond medium CNNs [2].
- **Accuracy cost is real.** The robust-natural gap of [4] and the feature analysis of [5] suggest the tradeoff reflects data geometry, so no training trick fully eliminates it.
- **Pretraining gap.** Denoised smoothing [6] helps, but certifying frontier-scale generative or multimodal models remains largely open.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Certified adversarial robustness has matured from a theoretical curiosity into a triad of deployable frameworks: **Neyman–Pearson smoothing** for scalable probabilistic ℓ₂ certificates [1, 6], **IBP and convex relaxations** for deterministic ℓ∞ verification [2, 3], and **Lipschitz-constrained architectures** for single-pass margin certificates. The robustness–accuracy tradeoff is not an artifact of weak methods: it is predicted by the robust-error decomposition [4] and explained by the geometry of robust versus non-robust features [5]. The research frontier lies in norm-general certificates, compositional certification for compound systems, and closing the pretraining gap — turning certificates from a property of specially trained models into a property of the models the world actually deploys.
