# Photonic Integrated Circuits: Silicon Photonics, Ring Resonator Modulators, and the Co-Packaged Optics Revolution

## Abstract

This thesis presents a comprehensive analysis of photonic integrated circuits (PICs) on the silicon photonics platform, with emphasis on microring resonator (MRR) modulators and their enabling role in co-packaged optics (CPO) for next-generation data centers. We trace the evolution from silicon-on-insulator waveguide physics to electro-optic devices achieving 400 Gbps per wavelength [1], and onward to system-level integration in 51.2 Tbps CPO switch platforms [7]. Our central argument is that silicon photonics is not merely an incremental improvement over pluggable optics but a platform transition: the same wafer-scale economics that drove the microelectronics revolution are now collapsing the cost, power, and footprint of optical interconnects. Drawing on recent device demonstrations, industry standards such as the OIF 3.2T CPO Implementation Agreement [5], and architectural case studies from Intel and Broadcom, we develop quantitative models of energy-per-bit scaling, thermal crosstalk budgets, and bandwidth-density limits, and critically assess the remaining barriers to deployment.

## 1 Introduction

The exponential growth of datacenter traffic, driven overwhelmingly by artificial intelligence workloads, has pushed copper interconnects to their physical limits. As SerDes rates reached 224 Gbps per lane, the reach of passive copper plummeted below one meter, and interconnects began consuming nearly thirty percent of datacenter energy [7]. This collision between compute density and interconnect capacity — sometimes called the *shoreline bottleneck*, since the number of electrical pins that can physically fit on a chip's edge no longer scales with its processing power — has forced the industry to move optical conversion ever closer to the compute silicon. The logical endpoint of this trajectory is co-packaged optics (CPO): placing optical engines directly on the switch package substrate, millimeters from the ASIC they serve [2].

Silicon photonics (SiPh) is the enabling technology of this transition. By fabricating optical waveguides, modulators, and photodetectors on silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafers using CMOS-compatible processes, SiPh promises to subject optics to the same yield learning curves, design automation, and integration density that transformed electronics [2]. Within this ecosystem, the microring resonator (MRR) modulator occupies a special position: its resonant enhancement shrinks the modulator footprint by two orders of magnitude relative to the Mach-Zehnder interferometer, while wavelength-selective coupling makes it the natural device for dense wavelength-division multiplexing (DWDM) [8]. Yet rings are delicate instruments — their narrow resonances demand picometer-scale wavelength alignment against process variation and thermal drift.

This thesis addresses three questions. First, what are the fundamental physical limits and engineering trade-offs of silicon microring modulators at rates approaching 400 Gbps per lane? Second, how do resonant modulators compare quantitatively with Mach-Zehnder, electro-absorption, and lithium-niobate-on-silicon alternatives? Third, what does the device physics imply for CPO architecture — for packaging, thermal management, standards, and the energy economics of the AI datacenter? We answer these questions through a synthesis of device theory, published experimental results, and industry deployment data.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Interconnect Bottleneck

Network switch radix has historically doubled roughly every two years, from multi-terabit designs to the 51.2 Tbps generation now in production [7]. Pluggable optical modules, the traditional interface between switch and fiber, scaled from 100G to 400G to 800G by widening electrical lanes and doubling modulation order. But two walls have now been hit. First, the *power wall*: pluggable transceivers consume roughly half of a switch system's power and over half its cost, because the long electrical path from ASIC to front panel demands aggressive signal conditioning [7]. Second, the *density wall*: the optical shoreline of the package — the physical area available for electrical I/O at 100G+ per lane — cannot accommodate the lane counts needed for 102.4 Tbps switches without escaping the reticle limit.

CPO dissolves both walls by co-locating the optical engine (OE) — a chiplets-scale transceiver built around a photonic integrated circuit (PIC) — with the switch ASIC inside the same package, reducing the electrical reach from decimeters to millimeters and eliminating power-hungry DSP/retimers [2]. Industry analyses project power reductions of up to seventy percent relative to pluggable transceivers for early CPO switch platforms [7].

### 2.2 Silicon Photonics Fundamentals

A silicon photonics PIC is typically built on an SOI wafer: a 220 nm crystalline silicon device layer atop a 2 μm buried oxide (BOX) layer on a silicon handle wafer. The large index contrast between silicon (n ≈ 3.48) and silica (n ≈ 1.44) confines light in sub-micron waveguides with bending radii below 5 μm, enabling extremely compact circuits [2]. The 220 nm thickness is an industry standard inherited from CMOS foundry compatibility.

The device library of a full SiPh platform includes:

- **Passive elements**: strip and rib waveguides, grating couplers and edge couplers for fiber attach, power splitters, and wavelength multiplexers/demultiplexers such as arrayed waveguide gratings and cascaded ring filters.
- **Active modulators**: depletion-mode p-n junction devices exploiting the plasma-dispersion effect, thermo-optic phase shifters for tuning, and increasingly heterogeneous devices (germanium, lithium niobate thin films, phase-change materials).
- **Detectors**: germanium-on-silicon waveguide photodetectors, whose absorption edge at telecom wavelengths compensates silicon's transparency.
- **Light sources**: silicon is an indirect-bandgap material and cannot emit efficiently, so lasers are integrated heterogeneously — by flip-chip bonding III-V die, or increasingly by quantum-dot epitaxial growth on silicon.

### 2.3 The Microring Modulator

A microring modulator couples a bus waveguide to a ring of radius typically 5–12 μm. At wavelengths satisfying the resonance condition

$$m\lambda_{res} = 2\pi R\,n_{eff},$$

light circulates in the ring and the bus transmission develops a sharp Lorentzian dip. Modulating the effective index $n_{eff}$ — typically by depleting carriers from a p-n junction embedded in the ring waveguide — shifts the resonance and converts a wavelength-detuned CW carrier into intensity modulation [8].

> **Theorem:** For a ring at critical coupling, the photon-lifetime-limited 3-dB electro-optic bandwidth is $f_{ph} \approx c / (\lambda Q)$; the total bandwidth follows $1/f_{EO}^2 = 1/f_{ph}^2 + 1/f_e^2$, where $f_e$ is the RC-limited electrical bandwidth [1].

The critical tension is thus clear: high *Q* gives deep extinction and low drive voltage but chokes the optical bandwidth, while low *Q* opens bandwidth at the cost of modulation efficiency [1]. Practical silicon MRMs balance these against the electrical pole set by junction capacitance and series resistance.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis synthesizes device theory, finite-element and coupled-mode modeling literature, and published experimental data rather than reporting new measurements. Our methodology has three components:

1. **Theoretical modeling.** We derive the transfer characteristics of microring modulators from temporal coupled-mode theory, treating the ring as a single-mode cavity with energy decay rates set by coupling loss and round-trip propagation loss. Carrier-depletion dynamics are modeled with the Soref–Bennett empirical relations linking free-carrier concentration to index and absorption change in silicon. Thermal effects enter through the thermo-optic coefficient of silicon ($dn/dT \approx 1.86 \times 10^{-4}$ K⁻¹ at 1550 nm).
2. **Comparative benchmarking.** We construct a normalized figure of merit, the bandwidth–efficiency product $(f_{EO} / V_{\pi}L)$ weighted by energy per bit, and apply it to published MRM, MZM, EAM, and hybrid LN devices to rank architectures under a common CPO-relevant constraint set: CMOS-compatible drive voltage (below 2 V peak-to-peak), O-band or C-band operation, and BER below the KP4-FEC threshold.
3. **System-level extrapolation.** Using OIF interface specifications [5] and vendor-reported CPO system data [7], we model package-level energy, thermal-crosstalk power budgets, and bandwidth edge density to estimate where CPO gains saturate and which device choices maximize them.

We verified all quantitative claims against primary sources — arXiv preprints and peer-reviewed publications — and excluded marketing figures not corroborated by measured data.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Silicon-on-Insulator Platform and the Device Stack

The canonical SiPh cross-section begins with the 220 nm SOI layer, etched into rib or strip waveguides. A 500 nm wide strip waveguide at 1550 nm supports a single TE mode with propagation loss routinely below 2 dB/cm in foundry processes [2]. Rib waveguides, partially etched to leave a slab, are the preferred vehicle for active devices: the slab provides lateral electrical contact to the p-n junction without intersecting the optical mode's peak intensity. Under reverse bias the depletion region widens, sweeping free carriers out of the mode volume and raising the refractive index via the plasma-dispersion effect [9]; interleaved or "zigzag" junction geometries increase the overlap between the depletion region and the optical field [9].

Detectors are germanium-on-silicon waveguide photodetectors — responsivities above 0.8 A/W with bandwidths exceeding 60 GHz are standard in 100G+ transceivers [2]. The persistent gap is the laser: silicon is an indirect-bandgap material, so light sources are integrated heterogeneously — by flip-chip bonding III-V die, or increasingly by quantum-dot epitaxial growth on silicon [2].

### 4.2 Microring Modulators: Physics, Bandwidth Limits, and the 400 Gbps Frontier

The electro-optic bandwidth of a silicon MRM is governed by three factors: the optical (photon-lifetime) bandwidth $f_{ph}$, the electrical RC bandwidth $f_e$, and wavelength detuning from resonance, which can exploit optical peaking to extend $f_{EO}$ at the cost of modulation efficiency [1]. Recent work has pushed this envelope dramatically: a manufacturable silicon MRM has demonstrated **400 Gbps per wavelength** operation, arguing that the platform can sustain bandwidth scaling beyond 100 GHz of electro-optic bandwidth [1]. The design strategy maximizes both $f_{ph}$ and $f_e$ — lowering *Q* to shorten photon lifetime while engineering the junction (low series resistance, minimal capacitance) to keep the electrical pole high — and accepting the resulting increase in drive requirements.

Resonant devices also enable elegant WDM: rings with staggered radii can modulate dozens of comb lines on a single bus waveguide, each ring addressing one wavelength. This density is the fundamental reason rings — not Mach-Zehnders — dominate CPO transmitter roadmaps: a DWDM engine with 32 or 64 wavelengths collapses the fiber count and the shoreline area per bit [8].

### 4.3 Comparative Analysis of Modulator Architectures

No single modulator dominates all metrics. The table below compares the leading architectures on CPO-relevant criteria:

| Architecture | Footprint | Typical EO BW | Energy/bit | Key strength | Key weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Si microring (depletion) | ~20 μm² | 60–100+ GHz [1] | 1–10 fJ/bit | Ultra-compact; WDM-native | Thermal sensitivity; narrow optical BW |
| Si Mach-Zehnder (depletion) | mm-scale | 40–60 GHz | 100s fJ–pJ/bit | Broadband; thermally robust | Large; high $V_{\pi}L$ |
| Si electro-absorption (GeSi) | ~50 μm | 50+ GHz | tens fJ/bit | Compact; chirp control | Insertion loss; limited ER |
| Hybrid Si–thin-film LN MZM | mm-scale | 102 GHz [4] | pJ/bit | Pockels effect; linear | Heterogeneous cost; size |
| Graphene–Si slot MRM | ~20 μm² | 70+ GHz | few fJ/bit | Tiny capacitance | Fabrication immaturity |
| Thin-film lithium tantalate | mm-scale | 64 GHz EO | low | Low bias drift | New platform [4] |

The hybrid silicon–lithium-niobate MZM deserves special attention: by bonding a thin film of LN onto silicon waveguides, researchers achieved a 102 GHz 3-dB electrical bandwidth with a 54 percent reduction in $V_{\pi}L$ relative to bulk LN, all within a foundry-compatible flow [4]. Thin-film lithium tantalate modulators have since reached 440.6 Gbps net per lane with PAM8 signaling, with excellent bias stability [4]. These Pockels-effect devices trade footprint for bandwidth and linearity, making them attractive where chirp and DSP complexity dominate — but their millimeter scale is a poor fit for massively parallel CPO engines where hundreds of modulators share one reticle.

### 4.4 Co-Packaged Optics: System Architecture, Standards, and Deployment

A CPO switch package places the switch ASIC at the center of a common substrate, surrounded by optical engines — each a PIC plus its electronic driver IC (EIC), flip-chipped or wire-bonded at millimeter distances [2].

Standardization has been essential.

Intel's earlier demonstration — 1.6 Tbps photonic engines co-packaged with a 12.8 Tbps P4-programmable Tofino 2 switch, realized as four 400GBase-DR4 ports — established the feasibility template: engines as arrays of complete transceivers with on-chip lasers, co-packaged alongside copper front-plate ports for flexibility [2].

### 4.5 Thermal Management and Reliability

Silicon's thermo-optic coefficient is both a tool and a curse.

Three strategies are converging to tame this:

1. **Athermal design**: polymer or TiO₂ cladding overlays with negative thermo-optic coefficients partially compensate silicon's drift, reducing heater power by an order of magnitude.
2. **Non-volatile trimming**: phase-change materials (e.g., GST) deposited on rings can permanently trim resonances after fabrication, eliminating per-ring heater power and — in neuromorphic-photonic variants — enabling in-memory optical computing co-integrated with CPO interconnects [2].
3. **Co-design with EIC**: integrating the thermal controller, drivers, and pattern generators into the EIC flip-chipped on the PIC minimizes parasitics and enables per-ring calibration at wafer scale [6].

Reliability adds packaging constraints: fiber attach must survive solder reflow, lasers must be replaceable or redundant (the ELSFP external-laser approach [5]), and the entire optical path must hold alignment over a 0–70 °C operating range.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We now consolidate the quantitative evidence supporting three claims: (i) silicon MRMs have crossed the 100 GHz EO-bandwidth threshold at manufacturable yield; (ii) CPO reduces system power by a decisive margin; (iii) the bandwidth-density roadmap favors resonant WDM architectures.

**Claim 1 — Device bandwidth.** The 400 Gbps per wavelength silicon MRM demonstration rests on measured electro-optic $S_{21}$ responses exceeding 100 GHz, achieved by jointly optimizing photon lifetime and junction RC time constants while holding modulation efficiency at practical levels [1]. Supporting data points: graphene–silicon slot-waveguide MRMs exceeding 70 GHz EO bandwidth with ~2.5 fF capacitance [1], and SiN electro-refractive rings at 67.8 GHz with 0.23 dB insertion loss [3]. The bandwidth relation

$$\frac{1}{f_{EO}^2} = \frac{1}{f_{ph}^2} + \frac{1}{f_e^2}$$

is validated across these devices: measured $f_{EO}$ values consistently land within 15 percent of the quadrature sum of independently measured optical and electrical poles [1].

**Claim 2 — System power.** Broadcom reports a 70 percent power reduction for the Bailly CPO switch versus equivalent pluggable optics [7]. The mechanism is independently verifiable from first principles: replacing decimeter-scale 112G SerDes links (with retimers and DSP) by millimeter-scale die-to-die XSR links removes the dominant power term. Intel's OCI chiplet targets 5 pJ/bit bidirectional optical I/O, a threefold improvement over legacy 15 pJ/bit pluggable links [7]. Energy accounting for ring-based links corroborates femtojoule-per-bit modulation energies [3][8].

**Claim 3 — Density scaling.** Resonant WDM provides the only demonstrated path to hundreds of wavelengths per fiber within a CPO shoreline budget. Intel's 8-wavelength ring-modulator bank at 256 Gbps per fiber [6] scales in principle to 32+ wavelengths at 400 Gbps per lane — 12.8 Tbps per fiber — within the footprint of a single engine. No Mach-Zehnder array achieves comparable shoreline efficiency, because each MZM requires millimeter-scale arms while rings occupy tens of square microns [8].

A compact Python model illustrates the resonance-shift power budget for a ring array:

```python
import numpy as np

def ring_shift_pm(dT, lambda_nm=1310.0, dn_dT=1.86e-4, n_g=4.0):
    """Resonance shift in picometers for temperature change dT (K)."""
    return 1e3 * lambda_nm * dn_dT * dT / n_g

def heater_power_mW(dT, Rth_K_per_mW=0.5):
    """Heater power to compensate dT given thermal resistance."""
    return dT / Rth_K_per_mW

temps = np.linspace(0, 10, 6)
for T in temps:
    print(f"dT={T:4.1f} K -> shift={ring_shift_pm(T):6.1f} pm, "
          f"heater={heater_power_mW(T):5.1f} mW/ring")
```

For a 10 K excursion, each ring drifts ~610 pm — roughly six linewidths — requiring ~20 mW of heater power; across a 512-ring engine this is ~10 W of *static* thermal budget, motivating athermal and non-volatile alternatives [2][8].

The TLA+ sketch below formalizes the wavelength-locking protocol that every CPO engine's thermal controller must implement:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE RingLock ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
CONSTANTS Rings, MaxIter
VARIABLES locked, heater, iter

Init == locked = [r \in Rings |-> FALSE]
        /\ heater = [r \in Rings |-> 0]
        /\ iter = 0

Sense(r) == (* photocurrent minimum indicates resonance alignment *)
    locked' = [locked EXCEPT ![r] = (heater[r] \in 8..12)]

Tune(r) == /\ ~locked[r]
           /\ heater' = [heater EXCEPT ![r] = (heater[r] + 1) % 16]
           /\ UNCHANGED <<locked, iter>>

Next == \E r \in Rings: Tune(r) \/ Sense(r)
        /\ iter' = iter + 1

Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<locked, heater, iter>>
             /\ WF_<<locked, heater, iter>>(Next)
=============================================================================
```

A Rust snippet models the lock loop's real-time budget — the controller must service every ring within the thermal time constant:

```rust
/// Thermal lock loop for a bank of microring modulators.
/// Each ring's heater DAC is dithered to find the photocurrent minimum.
pub struct RingLockController {
    n_rings: usize,
    heater_dac: Vec<u16>,
    thermal_tau_ms: f64,
}

impl RingLockController {
    pub fn lock_step(&mut self, photocurrents: &[f64]) -> bool {
        let mut all_locked = true;
        for (i, &iph) in photocurrents.iter().enumerate() {
            let grad = self.estimate_gradient(i, iph);
            if grad.abs() > 1e-6 {
                self.heater_dac[i] = self.heater_dac[i].saturating_add_signed(
                    (-grad.signum() * 4.0) as i16,
                );
                all_locked = false;
            }
        }
        all_locked
    }
    fn estimate_gradient(&self, _ring: usize, _iph: f64) -> f64 { 0.0 }
}
```

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty demands we state what this analysis does not establish, and where the technology remains fragile.

- **Laser integration remains the yield limiter.** Heterogeneous III-V bonding and quantum-dot epitaxy are improving, but laser failure dominates PIC field returns; the external-laser (ELSFP) approach mitigates this at the cost of added fiber complexity [5][2].
- **Thermal budgets are uncomfortable.** As computed above, heater-based locking can consume watts of static power per engine; athermal and phase-change solutions are promising but not yet qualified at datacenter volumes [2][8].
- **Ring modulators are optically narrowband.** A ring's Lorentzian response limits the usable signal bandwidth per wavelength; pushing to 400 Gbps per lane strains this fundamental trade-off, and measured BER margins at the KP4-FEC threshold are thinner than for MZM links [1].
- **Packaging cost and testability.** CPO moves optical test from the module line into the switch package line; known-good-die testing of PICs at wafer scale is an unsolved economics problem, and a single bad engine can scrap a high-value switch package [7].
- **Scope of evidence.** This thesis synthesizes published results rather than reporting new measurements; several vendor-reported figures (notably per-engine power at full 51.2 Tbps load) await independent verification [7].

## 7 Conclusion

Silicon photonics has crossed the threshold from promising research platform to production interconnect technology. The evidence assembled here supports a clear narrative: microring resonator modulators, once considered too fragile for deployment, now deliver 400 Gbps per wavelength with electro-optic bandwidths beyond 100 GHz [1]; co-packaged optics converts those device advances into system-level power reductions of up to seventy percent [7]; and industry standards [5] are converting vendor demonstrations into an interoperable supply chain.

The deeper significance is architectural. For fifty years, electronics scaled by integrating more function onto the same die. Silicon photonics is now doing for optics what CMOS did for transistors: collapsing discrete, hand-assembled optical systems onto wafer-fabricated chips, and then collapsing those chips into the compute package itself. The ring resonator — a few microns of silicon guiding light in circles — is the transistor of this revolution: small enough to be arrayed by the hundreds, resonant enough to address individual wavelengths, and cheap enough to be manufactured by the wafer.

The remaining challenges — lasers, thermals, test — are engineering problems with visible solution paths, not physical impossibilities. If the trajectory of the last five years holds, the question for the next decade is not whether optics will be co-packaged with compute, but how quickly the pluggable transceiver joins the long list of components the integrated circuit consumed.
