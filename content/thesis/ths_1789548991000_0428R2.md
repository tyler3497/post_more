# The Free Energy Principle: Variational Inference, Active Inference, and Markov Blankets

## Abstract

The free energy principle (FEP) proposes that any self-organizing system that persists over time must minimize a variational bound — free energy — on the surprise of its sensory states, where surprise is the negative log probability of sensations under the system's own model of the world. Introduced by Karl Friston, the principle unifies perception, learning, and action selection under a single imperative: approximate Bayesian inference implemented by gradient flows on free energy. This survey develops the mathematical core — the free energy lemma, the decomposition of free energy into accuracy minus complexity, and the role of Markov blankets in partitioning system from environment — and its extension to active inference, where action selection minimizes expected free energy, decomposing into epistemic (information-seeking) and pragmatic (goal-seeking) value. We review the process theory of predictive coding, empirical evidence from perceptual and motor neuroscience, and applications in robotics and machine learning. We close with the principal criticisms: unfalsifiability, the gap between principle and process theory, and the challenge of scaling variational inference to deep hierarchical models.

## 1 Introduction

In 2006, Karl Friston published a paper proposing that the brain's responses could be understood as minimizing a single quantity — variational free energy — and that this minimization subsumes perception, learning, and action [1]. The claim was audacious: not merely a model of a cognitive function, but a *principle* from which the organization of any persisting biological system could be derived. The 2010 review "The free-energy principle: a unified brain theory?" brought the idea to a wide audience and ignited one of the most vigorous debates in theoretical neuroscience [2].

The principle rests on a simple observation. Any system that maintains its structure over time — a cell, a brain, an organism — must resist the dispersive tendency of random fluctuations. Formally, it must occupy a limited repertoire of states, which means its sensory states have low entropy. Since the system cannot evaluate the entropy of its sensations directly, it instead minimizes an upper bound: variational free energy, a quantity borrowed from statistical physics that is always greater than or equal to surprise (negative log model evidence). Minimizing free energy is therefore approximately minimizing surprise, and — via Bayes' rule — approximately performing Bayesian inference about the hidden causes of sensations [2].

> **The Free Energy Lemma:** For any system possessing a Markov blanket, internal states minimize variational free energy if and only if they encode Bayesian beliefs about external states. The dynamics of internal states can then be read as approximate Bayesian inference.

This survey proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the probabilistic setup: generative models, recognition densities, and the free energy bound. Section 3 develops the variational calculus of the principle: the accuracy–complexity decomposition, generalized coordinates of motion, and predictive coding as process theory. Section 4 deepens the analysis across four fronts: Markov blankets and the particular partition; active inference and expected free energy; hierarchical message passing and precision; and the relationship to reinforcement learning and optimal control. Section 5 sketches key derivations and surveys empirical support. Section 6 addresses limitations and criticisms. Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Surprise, entropy, and self-organization

Let $s$ denote sensory states and $\vartheta$ the hidden states and parameters causing them. A system persists if the entropy $H[p(s)]$ of its sensory states remains low: a fish out of water encounters surprising sensations and soon ceases to exist as an organized system. Surprise is $-\ln p(s)$, the negative log probability of sensations under the system's generative model $p(s, \vartheta) = p(s \mid \vartheta)\, p(\vartheta)$.

The system cannot compute surprise directly because the marginal $p(s) = \int p(s, \vartheta)\, d\vartheta$ is intractable. Instead, introduce an arbitrary *recognition density* $q(\vartheta)$ and define variational free energy:

$$F(s, q) = \mathbb{E}_q[\ln q(\vartheta) - \ln p(s, \vartheta)] = D_{KL}(q(\vartheta) \,\|\, p(\vartheta \mid s)) - \ln p(s).$$

Since the KL divergence is non-negative, $F \geq -\ln p(s)$: free energy bounds surprise from above [2]. Minimizing $F$ with respect to $q$ simultaneously (a) makes $q$ approximate the true posterior $p(\vartheta \mid s)$ — perception as inference — and (b) makes sensations less surprising — adaptive behavior.

### 2.2 Accuracy minus complexity

Free energy admits a revealing decomposition:

$$F(s, q) = -\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q[\ln p(s \mid \vartheta)]}_{\text{accuracy}} + \underbrace{D_{KL}(q(\vartheta) \,\|\, p(\vartheta))}_{\text{complexity}}.$$

Minimizing free energy maximizes the accuracy of predictions while minimizing the complexity of beliefs relative to priors — an Occam's-razor trade-off identical in form to the evidence lower bound (ELBO) maximized in variational autoencoders [4]. This correspondence has made the FEP a meeting point between theoretical neuroscience and deep generative modeling.

### 2.3 The Markov blanket

A **Markov blanket** is the set of variables that renders a subset conditionally independent of the rest: sensory and active states blanket internal states from external states [2]. Formally, with partition into external $\eta$, sensory $s$, active $a$, and internal $\mu$ states, the blanket $b = \{s, a\}$ satisfies $p(\eta, \mu \mid b) = p(\eta \mid b)\, p(\mu \mid b)$. The blanket induces a *particular partition* of the system: internal states can only influence external states vicariously through active states, and external states influence internal states only through sensory states. This is the formalization of the perception–action loop, and the free energy lemma shows that internal dynamics minimizing $F$ necessarily encode beliefs about external states [2].

## 3 Methodology

Our exposition follows Friston's variational program with attention to mathematical precision:

1. **Derive** the free energy bound and its decompositions from the generative model, stating assumptions (ergodicity, existence of a non-equilibrium steady state) explicitly.
2. **Construct** the process theory: under Gaussian (Laplace) assumptions, derive predictive coding update equations as gradient descent on free energy.
3. **Extend** to action via active inference: policies selected to minimize expected free energy, with its epistemic/pragmatic decomposition.
4. **Evaluate** against empirical neuroscience (perceptual inference, motor control, psychopathology) and engineering applications (robotics, control).
5. **Critique** the principle's falsifiability and the gap between the variational principle and any specific neural implementation.

We work primarily with continuous-state models in generalized coordinates of motion, noting where discrete-state formulations (used in active inference simulations) differ [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The free energy lemma and Bayesian mechanics

The deepest claim of the FEP is the **free energy lemma**: given a Markov blanket, the most likely path of internal states minimizes free energy, and at that minimum the internal states parameterize the posterior over external states. Recent work on *Bayesian mechanics* generalizes this: any system with a Markov blanket at non-equilibrium steady state can be described as if it performs variational inference, with the amplitude of random fluctuations playing the role of temperature [7].

The argument proceeds via the Fokker–Planck description of stochastic dynamics. At steady state, the probability density over states can be written in terms of a surprisal (negative log density) whose gradients drive deterministic flow, plus solenoidal flow that circulates on iso-surprisal contours. Conditional on the blanket, the surprisal of particular states factorizes into terms interpretable as likelihood, prior, and approximate posterior — and the variational free energy emerges as the functional whose gradients reproduce the internal flow [7]. This reframes the FEP from a normative claim about brains to a *descriptive* claim about a broad class of stochastic systems.

### 4.2 Active inference: acting to minimize expected free energy

Perception minimizes free energy with respect to beliefs; **action** minimizes it with respect to the world. In active inference, an agent selects among policies $\pi$ (sequences of actions) by minimizing *expected* free energy:

$$G(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{q(o, \vartheta \mid \pi)}[\ln q(\vartheta \mid \pi) - \ln p(o, \vartheta)] = -\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q[D_{KL}(q(\vartheta \mid o, \pi) \,\|\, q(\vartheta \mid \pi))]}_{\text{epistemic value}} - \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q[\ln p(o)]}_{\text{pragmatic value}}.$$

Policies are thus chosen to maximize a sum of **epistemic value** (expected information gain — curiosity, exploration) and **pragmatic value** (expected log probability of preferred outcomes — goal seeking) [5]. Exploration and exploitation fall out of a single objective rather than being traded off by an external hyperparameter.

Crucially, preferences enter as *prior beliefs* about outcomes: the agent believes it will occupy unsurprising, preferred states, and acts to fulfill that belief. This dissolves the classical separation between reward functions and inference — value is just another prior [5]. Discrete-state active inference models, implemented in the `pymdp` and SPM (Statistical Parametric Mapping) toolboxes, have simulated saccadic eye movements, foraging, and social cognition [6].

### 4.3 Predictive coding as process theory

The FEP is a *principle*, not an algorithm; **predictive coding** is its leading process theory under Gaussian assumptions. Assume the recognition density is Gaussian (Laplace approximation) and the generative model is hierarchical, with each level predicting the activity of the level below. Gradient descent on free energy yields:

$$\dot{\mu} = -\partial_\mu F = \text{prediction errors} \times \text{precision} - \text{prior constraints},$$

where prediction errors are passed forward and predictions passed backward. **Precision** (inverse variance) weights prediction errors, implementing attention: high-precision errors drive belief updating, low-precision errors are discounted [4].

Bogacz's tutorial derives these updates rigorously and shows their equivalence to the Kalman filter in the linear case and to backpropagation-like schemes in hierarchical networks [4]. The mapping to cortical microcircuitry — superficial pyramidal cells encoding prediction errors, deep pyramidal cells encoding predictions — is the FEP's most specific anatomical prediction and the focus of ongoing laminar fMRI and electrophysiology studies.

### 4.4 Relations to reinforcement learning and control

Active inference subsumes several control frameworks as special cases. When epistemic value is suppressed and policies are evaluated myopically, expected free energy reduces to expected utility, recovering Bayesian decision theory. The relationship to optimal control is developed in Friston's 2010 formulation, where motor commands emerge from proprioceptive predictions: the agent does not compute inverse models but predicts the sensory consequences of movement and lets spinal reflexes fulfill them [3].

Relative to model-based reinforcement learning, active inference differs in two respects: (a) exploration is *intrinsic* (epistemic value) rather than bolted on via bonuses, and (b) there is no separate reward signal — preferences are priors. Recent benchmarks show deep active inference agents achieving competitive sample efficiency on continuous control tasks, though scaling to high-dimensional problems remains harder than for model-free RL [6].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proof Sketches

### 5.1 Derivation: free energy bounds surprise

Starting from $-\ln p(s) = -\ln \int p(s,\vartheta)\,d\vartheta$, multiply and divide by $q(\vartheta)$ and apply Jensen's inequality:

$$-\ln p(s) = -\ln \mathbb{E}_q\left[\frac{p(s,\vartheta)}{q(\vartheta)}\right] \leq -\mathbb{E}_q\left[\ln \frac{p(s,\vartheta)}{q(\vartheta)}\right] = F(s,q).$$

Equality holds iff $q(\vartheta) = p(\vartheta \mid s)$. Minimizing $F$ therefore drives $q$ toward the posterior while simultaneously selecting actions that make $p(s)$ large. ∎

A minimal numerical illustration of the accuracy–complexity trade-off:

```python
import numpy as np

def free_energy(mu, s, prior_mu=0.0, prior_var=1.0, lik_var=0.5):
    # Gaussian recognition density q = N(mu, 1); model p(s|t)=N(t, lik_var), p(t)=N(prior_mu, prior_var)
    accuracy = -0.5 * ((s - mu) ** 2 / lik_var)
    complexity = 0.5 * ((mu - prior_mu) ** 2 / prior_var)
    return complexity - accuracy   # F = complexity - accuracy

s = 2.0
mus = np.linspace(-1, 3, 9)
print([(round(m, 2), round(free_energy(m, s), 3)) for m in mus])
```

### 5.2 Empirical support

| Domain | Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Binocular rivalry | Perceptual alternation rates follow from precision-weighted prediction errors | Levelt's propositions from free energy [4] |
| Mismatch negativity | EEG responses to oddballs scale with precision-weighted prediction error | Canonical predictive-coding signature |
| Motor control | Grip-force modulation anticipates load; deafferented patients fail | Proprioceptive predictions drive action [3] |
| Psychopathology | Schizophrenia as aberrant precision (dopamine); autism as over-precise sensory evidence | Computational psychiatry applications [5] |
| Birdsong | Songbird vocal learning modeled as active inference | Cross-species generality |

Computational psychiatry is arguably the FEP's most productive application: disorders become *false inference* — e.g., hallucinations as overly precise priors, or Parkinsonian bradykinesia as imprecise proprioceptive predictions [5].

## 6 Limitations

**Falsifiability.** The most persistent criticism is that the FEP is unfalsifiable: since *any* system with a Markov blanket can be described as minimizing free energy [7], the principle risks vacuity. Defenders reply that falsifiability attaches to *process theories* (e.g., specific predictive-coding architectures) and *generative models* fitted to data, not to the principle itself — much as the principle of least action is not itself a falsifiable theory of any particular system.

**Principle versus mechanism.** Even granting the mathematics, the FEP does not specify *how* any particular brain minimizes free energy. The mapping from variational updates to neural circuits remains schematic, and competing process theories (sampling-based inference, neural dynamics) are equally compatible with the principle.

**Scaling.** Exact or Laplace-approximate inference scales poorly to the deep, high-dimensional generative models needed for real-world perception. Whether the brain's approximations match those of machine learning's variational methods — and whether either scales — is unresolved.

**The dark room problem.** If agents minimize surprise, why don't they seek dark, silent rooms? The active inference answer — priors over preferred states make the dark room *surprising* given prior preferences — is formally adequate but strikes critics as moving the explanatory burden into the priors [5].

## 7 Conclusion

The free energy principle offers a rare thing in neuroscience: a single mathematical object — variational free energy — from which perception, learning, attention, and action can all be derived. Its Bayesian-mechanics formulation extends the reach further, suggesting that inference-like dynamics are generic features of persistent stochastic systems [7]. Whether the principle ultimately explains the brain or merely redescribes it, it has already reshaped computational psychiatry, inspired new approaches to robotics and control, and forged a productive bridge to variational methods in machine learning. The hard work ahead lies in process theories precise enough to fail — and in experiments designed to make them do so.
