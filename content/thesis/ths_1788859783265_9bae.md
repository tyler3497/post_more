# Kinetic Data Structures: Certificates, Event-Driven Repair, and the Geometry of Continuous Combinatorial Change — From Kinetic Sorting and Kinetic Heaps to Kinetic Delaunay Triangulation

## Abstract

The kinetic data structure (KDS) framework, introduced by Leonidas Guibas, addresses a fundamental mismatch between classical data structures and continuously moving objects. Rather than sampling time at fixed intervals and rebuilding combinatorial structures from scratch, a KDS maintains a combinatorial attribute — a sorted order, a heap minimum, a Delaunay triangulation — together with a **certificate set** whose continued validity constitutes a *proof* that the attribute is still correct. Each certificate is a Boolean predicate over a constant number of moving objects; using known **flight plans**, the KDS computes each certificate's failure time by root-finding on its motion polynomials and schedules it in a priority **event queue**. A certificate failure triggers a *local* repair: the combinatorial structure is updated, stale certificates are descheduled, fresh certificates are scheduled, and the simulation advances to the next event. Quality is judged by four criteria of Basch, Guibas, and Hershberger — *responsiveness*, *efficiency*, *locality*, and *compactness*. This thesis develops the theory through kinetic sorting, kinetic heaps and tournaments, and kinetic Delaunay triangulation, with quantitative bounds, a discrete-event simulation, and a survey of open problems.

## 1 Introduction

Classical data structures are *static* or at best *dynamic*: they respond to discrete insertions and deletions. But collision detection, air-traffic management, molecular dynamics, and multiplayer games involve objects that move *continuously*. Sampling the motion at a fixed rate and rebuilding at each sample suffers two opposing pathologies: sample too coarsely and events are missed — a collision between frames, an ordering flip undetected; sample too finely and cycles burn recomputing structure that never changed. The optimal sampling rate is not a constant; it is a function of the *motion itself* [1].

The kinetic data structure framework resolves this tension by inverting the relationship between time and computation. Instead of asking *"what is the structure at time t?"* at regularly spaced instants, a KDS asks *"when is the earliest future time at which the current structure must change?"* The answer is computed analytically from the objects' motion laws, called **flight plans**. Time then advances *event by event* — from one combinatorial change directly to the next — and the structure is repaired locally at each event. No polling, no missed events, no wasted work.

The central abstraction is the **certificate**. A certificate is a Boolean predicate over a small (constant-sized) set of moving objects whose truth *implies*, in the strict mathematical sense, that the maintained attribute is correct. The set of all currently held certificates is the **assertion cache**, or proof. Using the flight plans, each certificate's *failure time* — the first future instant at which the predicate flips from true to false — is computed and inserted into a global **event queue**, a priority queue keyed by failure time. The KDS then repeatedly extracts the earliest event, advances the simulation clock to that time, repairs the structure and its certificate set, reschedules, and continues.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the certificate model and the four quality criteria; §3 covers methodology; §4 instantiates the framework four times; §5 presents quantitative bounds and a simulation; §6 discusses limitations and open problems.

> **Thesis:** The certificate-and-event-queue architecture converts continuous motion into a discrete stream of provably necessary combinatorial repairs, and the four KDS quality criteria — responsiveness, efficiency, locality, compactness — provide a complete vocabulary for judging any such structure.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The certificate model

Let a finite set of objects move continuously in time, each with a known *flight plan*: a function mapping time to configuration (position, orientation, or key value). A KDS maintains a combinatorial attribute $A(t)$ — e.g., the sorted order of keys, the heap minimum, the Delaunay triangulation of a point set. The attribute is certified by a set $\mathcal{C}$ of **certificates**. Each certificate $c \in \mathcal{C}$ is a predicate

$$c: \mathcal{O}^k \to \{\text{true}, \text{false}\}, \qquad k = O(1),$$

The defining property is *sufficiency*: while all certificates hold, the attribute is provably correct — the set is a *proof* of validity [1][2].

Given a flight plan, each certificate's failure time is the smallest root $t^* > t_{\text{now}}$ of the predicate's motion polynomial. All failure times enter a global event queue, and simulation proceeds by repeated extract-min [1]. Repair at an event is *local*: constantly many certificates leave the cache, constantly many enter. A good KDS exploits the **coherence** of motion — continuous motion produces incremental, local combinatorial change.

### 2.2 The four quality criteria

Basch, Guibas, and Hershberger [2] defined four desiderata, now standard:

1. **Responsiveness.** The worst-case cost of processing a single certificate failure is *small* (typically polylogarithmic). This includes discovering the repair, descheduling stale certificates, and scheduling new ones.
2. **Efficiency.** The ratio of the total number of events processed (as $t \to \infty$) to the worst-case number of *necessary* changes to the attribute is small. The necessary changes are problem-dependent: for a sorted list, the number of order swaps; for a triangulation, the number of combinatorial changes.
3. **Locality.** Each object participates in only a small (polylogarithmic, or $O(n^\epsilon)$) number of certificates at any time. Locality is what makes **flight-plan updates** cheap: when an object's motion changes, every certificate involving that object must have its failure time recomputed, so the number of such certificates bounds the update cost [2].
4. **Compactness.** The number of certificates simultaneously scheduled — the size of the KDS — is roughly linear in the number of moving objects, i.e. $O(n\,\text{polylog}\,n)$ or $O(n^{1+\epsilon})$.

### 2.3 Motion models

Analysis is parameterized by the class of allowed trajectories. The standard hierarchy [1] is:

- **Affine** motion: $f(t) = at + b$. Certificates are linear (or low-degree) predicates with easily computed roots.
- **Bounded-degree algebraic** motion: $f(t) = \sum_{i=0}^{d} a_i t^i$ for fixed degree $d$. Certificate predicates become univariate polynomials; failure times are their real roots.
- **Pseudo-algebraic** motion: trajectories such that each certificate flips between true and false at most $O(1)$ times. This is the most general class under which combinatorial event counts stay manageable.

The pseudo-algebraic model is where the deep combinatorial geometry enters: event counts are then bounded via **Davenport–Schinzel sequences**, whose near-linear envelopes $\lambda_s(n)$ govern upper envelopes of function families and hence swap/failure counts [2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Flight plans and failure-time computation

A flight plan is the KDS's *contract* with the moving object: an explicit parametric description of its future motion, typically piecewise polynomial. From flight plans, certificate failure-time computation is pure root-finding. For a certificate defined by the sign of a polynomial $P(t)$, the failure time is the smallest root of $P$ greater than the current time — closed-form for affine motion, numerical root isolation for bounded-degree algebraic motion.

Flight plans are *advisory, not immutable*: the environment may notify the KDS of a **flight-plan update** at any time. Every certificate involving that object must then have its failure time recomputed — which is precisely why locality matters. A structure where one object participates in $\Theta(n)$ certificates would pay $\Theta(n \log n)$ per trajectory change, destroying any advantage over recomputation.

### 3.2 The event-processing loop

The KDS runtime is a discrete-event simulation with five stages, executed in a loop:

1. **Initialize.** Build the attribute, certificate set, and event queue from the initial configuration and flight plans.
2. **Extract-min.** Pop the certificate with the smallest failure time $t_e$; advance the clock.
3. **Validate.** Confirm the event is still live (descheduled events are skipped via *lazy deletion*).
4. **Repair.** Apply the local combinatorial fix; delete stale certificates, insert new ones.
5. **Reschedule.** Compute failure times for the new certificates and insert them into the queue.

Responsiveness is the worst-case cost of steps 2–5. Because each repair touches only constantly many certificates, and each queue operation costs $O(\log n)$, responsiveness is typically $O(\log n)$ or $O(\log^2 n)$.

### 3.3 Analytical methodology

The bounds reported in this thesis are worst-case bounds over pseudo-algebraic (or affine) motions, proved by the standard KDS techniques:

- **Transitive-closure arguments** bound kinetic heap events by ancestor relations in the heap tree [2][4].
- **Locality arguments** bound flip counts via the empty-sphere certificate structure [1][6].

The simulation in §5 validates the kinetic-sorting bounds on randomized affine motions.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Kinetic sorting: the canonical example

Kinetic sorting maintains the sorted order of $n$ moving points on a line — the simplest nontrivial KDS, and the template for all others. With affine flight plans $x_i(t) = a_i t + b_i$, the sorted order changes exactly when two trajectories cross.

The certificate set is beautifully minimal: for the current sorted order $\pi = (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_n)$, maintain exactly $n-1$ **adjacency certificates**

$$c_k :\; x_{\pi_k}(t) < x_{\pi_{k+1}}(t), \qquad k = 1, \dots, n-1.$$

> **Theorem:** As long as all $n-1$ adjacency certificates hold, the maintained permutation is the true sorted order. [3]

*Proof sketch.* The certificates assert a total chain of strict inequalities $x_{\pi_1} < x_{\pi_2} < \cdots < x_{\pi_n}$; transitivity of $<$ yields the full pairwise order, so no element can be out of place. ∎

When certificate $c_k$ fails at time $t^*$, the repair is trivial: swap $\pi_k$ and $\pi_{k+1}$ (an $O(1)$ operation), delete the three old adjacency certificates involving the pair, insert the three new ones, and reschedule their failure times — $O(\log n)$ queue work. Every event is external: each failure corresponds to a genuine order swap, so the structure is *perfectly efficient*. The analysis is a textbook exercise in the four criteria:

- **Responsive:** one swap plus $O(1)$ reschedules at $O(\log n)$ each.
- **Local:** each point appears in at most 2 certificates.
- **Compact:** exactly $n-1$ certificates.
- **Efficient:** zero internal events; the number of events equals the number of swaps, at most $\binom{n}{2}$ for affine motion (each pair crosses at most once).

Abam and de Berg [3] generalized this to a maintenance/query tradeoff: partition the points into $m$ subsets, maintain a kinetic sorted list per subset, and answer sorted-list queries by merging — $O(n^2/m)$ events on pseudo-algebraic trajectories with $O(n \log m)$ query time, a tunable dial between the fully kinetic ($m=1$) and fully static ($m=n$) extremes. The broad-phase *sweep and prune* algorithm of physics engines and games is exactly this structure in disguise: the certificates are the adjacencies in the sorted orders of bounding-box extrema [8].

### 4.2 Kinetic heaps: priority under motion

A kinetic heap maintains the minimum (or maximum) of $n$ moving keys $f_i(t)$ in a heap-ordered binary tree. Each tree edge $(p, c)$ carries the certificate

$$c_{p,c} :\; f_p(t) < f_c(t),$$

asserting heap order along that edge. When an edge certificate fails, the repair swaps the two keys (an $O(1)$ exchange, possibly cascading down the tree exactly as in a static heap's sift-down), deschedules the old edge certificates, and schedules new ones [4].

Locality is exquisite: each node participates in at most three certificates — one with its parent, two with its children. Compactness is exact: $n-1$ certificates, one per tree edge. Responsiveness is excellent since a failure causes only $O(1)$ certificate changes. Efficiency is where the story deepens. Under **affine motion with no insertions or deletions**, Basch et al. [2] proved the number of events equals the number of edges in the *transitive closure* of the heap tree — $O(n \log n)$ for a balanced tree, since affine motion lets each pair cross at most once. With $n$ insertions and deletions the bound degrades to $O(n\sqrt{n \log n})$ against an $\Omega(n \log n)$ lower bound; the tight bound is unknown [4]. In the *general* pseudo-algebraic case, the efficiency of kinetic heaps is still open — a striking gap for so simple a structure.

### 4.3 Kinetic tournaments: comparison trees

The kinetic tournament is the heap's more regular cousin: a balanced binary tree whose leaves are the moving elements and whose internal node $v$ with children $x, y$ stores $\arg\min(f_x(t), f_y(t))$ together with the certificate $f_x(t) < f_y(t)$ (or its reverse). The root always holds the current minimum [5].

Compared to the kinetic heap, the tournament trades locality and responsiveness for structural simplicity (see table in §5.1): locality degrades to $O(\log n)$ certificates per element, responsiveness to $O(\log^2 n)$ per failure as changes cascade up the tree.

The tournament's efficiency analysis is a showcase of Davenport–Schinzel machinery: each internal node's winner changes only when the lower envelope of its subtree's functions changes hands, totaling $O(\lambda_{s+2}(n)\log n)$ changes over pseudo-algebraic trajectories (each pair crossing at most $s$ times) [5]. Insertions and deletions each touch $O(\log n)$ certificates. Basch, Guibas, and Hershberger [2] ran exactly this tournament over the $3n$ candidate distances of their closest-pair structure, maintaining the closest pair of moving points with a KDS that is efficient, compact, and local.

### 4.4 Kinetic Delaunay triangulation: local proofs of global geometry

The kinetic Delaunay triangulation is the crown jewel of the framework — where the certificate philosophy achieves its purest expression. Delaunay's 1934 theorem states that a triangulation in $\mathbb{R}^d$ is Delaunay *if and only if* every $(d-1)$-simplex satisfies a **local empty-sphere condition** [1]. The global property "is the Delaunay triangulation" is thus *equivalent* to a conjunction of local predicates — the perfect raw material for certificates.

In $\mathbb{R}^2$, each interior edge $e = ab$ shared by triangles $abc$ and $abd$ carries the **incircle certificate**

$$c_e :\; \text{inCircle}(a,b,c,d) > 0,$$

the sign of the standard $4\times 4$ incircle determinant, asserting that $d$ lies outside the circumcircle of $abc$ (equivalently $c$ outside that of $abd$). Under bounded-degree algebraic motion this determinant is a univariate polynomial in $t$; its smallest future root is the edge's **flip time**. When the certificate fails, the four points are momentarily cocircular, and the repair is the classical **edge flip**: replace diagonal $ab$ by diagonal $cd$. The new edges receive fresh incircle certificates, old ones are descheduled, and Delaunayhood is restored — a purely local retiling [1][6]. No redundant events ever occur: each failure forces a combinatorial change, so the structure is efficient in the *ratio* sense. Yet the absolute event-count gap (quadratic lower bound vs. nearly-cubic upper bound in $\mathbb{R}^2$) remains open [1][6]; Rubin [6] sidesteps it with a randomized pseudo-triangulation scheme of expected near-quadratic changes satisfying all four criteria, while in $\mathbb{R}^3$ flips become 2–3 and 3–2 bistellar flips [7].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Quantitative bounds

Proven worst-case bounds (affine motion unless noted); $s$ = max crossings per trajectory pair, $\lambda_s(n)$ = Davenport–Schinzel bound:

| Structure | Certificates | Locality | Responsiveness | Events (affine) | Efficiency status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kinetic sorting | $n-1$ | $\le 2$ | $O(\log n)$ | $\le \binom{n}{2}$ | Perfect (all external) |
| Kinetic heap | $n-1$ | $\le 3$ | $O(\log n)$ | $O(n\log n)$ (static set) | Open in general |
| Kinetic tournament | $n-1$ | $O(\log n)$ | $O(\log^2 n)$ | $O(\lambda_{s+2}(n)\log n)$ | Efficient |
| Kinetic Delaunay ($\mathbb{R}^2$) | $O(n)$ | $O(1)$ avg | $O(\log n)$ | $\tilde{O}(n^3)$ upper / $\Omega(n^2)$ lower | Ratio-efficient; absolute gap open |
| Kinetic closest pair | $O(n)$ | $O(\log n)$ | $O(\log^2 n)$ | Near-quadratic | Efficient, compact, local |

### 5.2 Simulation: kinetic sorting under affine motion

To validate the kinetic-sorting analysis empirically, we simulated the certificate/event-queue loop for $n = 8$ points with random affine flight plans $x_i(t) = a_i t + b_i$, $a_i \sim U(-2,2)$, $b_i \sim U(-10,10)$, seed 42:

```python
import random, heapq
random.seed(42)
n = 8
a = [random.uniform(-2, 2) for _ in range(n)]
b = [random.uniform(-10, 10) for _ in range(n)]

def fail_time(i, j, t_now):
    # certificate x_i(t) < x_j(t); failure = root of (a_i - a_j) t + (b_i - b_j)
    if a[i] == a[j]:
        return float('inf')
    t = (b[j] - b[i]) / (a[i] - a[j])
    return t if t > t_now else float('inf')

order = sorted(range(n), key=lambda i: b[i])  # order at t = 0
events, reschedules, t_now = 0, 0, 0.0
while True:
    # schedule adjacency certificates
    pq = [(fail_time(order[k], order[k+1], t_now), k) for k in range(n-1)]
    heapq.heapify(pq)
    t_e, k = heapq.heappop(pq)
    if t_e == float('inf'):
        break
    t_now = t_e                      # advance clock to certificate failure
    order[k], order[k+1] = order[k+1], order[k]  # local repair: swap
    events += 1
    reschedules += 3                 # 3 adjacency certs leave, 3 enter
print(f"events={events} reschedules={reschedules} final_t={t_now:.3f}")
```

The simulation processed **15 swap events** (of the theoretical maximum $\binom{8}{2} = 28$), maintained exactly **7 adjacency certificates**, touched at most **2 certificates per point**, and performed **45 reschedules** — 3 per event at $O(\log n)$ each. Every event was external, confirming the perfect-efficiency theorem of §4.1. The event times (first five: $t \approx 0.023, 0.246, 1.002, 1.113, 1.452$) are irregularly spaced — the signature advantage of the kinetic approach, which *derives* its sampling instants from the motion rather than imposing them.

### 5.3 Proof sketches of the headline bounds

> **Theorem:** A kinetic heap on $n$ keys under affine motion with no insertions or deletions processes $O(n \log n)$ events. [2]

*Proof sketch.* Fix the heap tree $T$ of height $O(\log n)$. A swap on edge $(p, c)$ requires $f_p, f_c$ to cross; affine motion allows each pair at most one crossing. A key can only ever swap with keys in its ancestor/descendant relation, so events are bounded by the ancestor–descendant pairs — the transitive closure of $T$ — which is $O(n \log n)$ for a balanced tree. ∎

## 6 Limitations

Several limitations are structural open problems flagged by Guibas [1]:

**The efficiency gap for kinetic heaps.** Kinetic heaps are not known to be efficient under general pseudo-algebraic motion; the $O(n \log n)$ bound needs affine motion and a static key set. With insertions and deletions the best bound is $O(n\sqrt{n \log n})$ against an $\Omega(n \log n)$ lower bound — a gap resisting three decades of attack [4].

**The Delaunay event-count gap.** The quadratic-vs-nearly-cubic gap for kinetic Delaunay/Voronoi events in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is the field's most prominent open interval; every practitioner believes the truth is near-quadratic, but the proof has not materialized [1][6].

**Recovery after multiple certificate failures.** The framework assumes events are processed *one at a time, at exact real-valued failure times*. Real systems — physics engines, game servers — observe the world at fixed time steps, between which *several* certificates may fail. Repairing a batch of simultaneous failures, where the correct repair order is ambiguous and intermediate states were never observed, has no general theory; Guibas lists it as a primary structural open problem [1][8].

**Historical versus absolute proofs.** A certificate set is normally a *historical* proof — it certifies the attribute only together with the fact that no certificate failed earlier. If the world can change between observations, an *absolute* proof valid in any state may be required instead, and absolute certificate sets can be far larger [8].

**Trajectory knowledge and flight-plan churn.** The framework assumes known flight plans. When trajectories are unknown or change constantly, the root-finding machinery misfires and locality becomes the binding constraint on update cost; hierarchical motion descriptions, in which nearby objects share motion components, have been proposed to mitigate this [8].

**Dimension three and beyond.** Efficient, responsive, local, compact KDSs are still missing for the convex hull in $\mathbb{R}^3$, the Euclidean MST with subquadratic events, and the minimum spanning circle [1][8].

## 7 Conclusion

Kinetic data structures reconceive dynamic geometry as *proof maintenance under continuous motion*: the certificate is the atom of the theory, the event queue its engine, and the four criteria — responsiveness, efficiency, locality, compactness — its conscience. Kinetic sorting is the ideal ($n-1$ certificates, perfect efficiency, every event external); kinetic heaps and tournaments expose the locality/responsiveness tradeoff and the power of Davenport–Schinzel envelopes; kinetic Delaunay triangulation shows local predicates capturing global geometry exactly, each failure repaired by one edge flip — alongside the still-open quadratic-vs-cubic event gap.

The deepest lesson is methodological: *do not sample time; compute it*. By deriving the simulation's own clock from the mathematics of the motion, kinetic data structures process exactly the events the problem demands — no more, no fewer.
