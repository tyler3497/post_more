# Full-Flow Staged-Combustion Rocket Engine Cycles: Raptor, RD-180, and the Thermodynamics of High-Pressure Propulsion

## Abstract

Staged-combustion cycles represent the thermodynamic ceiling of chemical rocket propulsion: where open cycles discard the turbine-drive gas overboard, closed cycles route every gram of propellant through the main combustion chamber. This thesis develops a comparative theory of high-pressure liquid rocket engine cycles, centered on the full-flow staged-combustion (FFSC) cycle as realized in SpaceX's Raptor and contrasted with its partial-flow ancestors: the oxygen-rich staged-combustion RD-180 and BE-4, the fuel-rich staged-combustion RS-25, and the open gas-generator (Merlin, F-1) and expander cycles. We derive the cycle power balances linking turbine inlet temperature, pump discharge pressure, and chamber pressure; show why full-flow staging permits chamber pressures above 300 bar by passing the entire propellant mass through moderate-temperature preburners; and explain the materials bargains — oxygen-compatible metallurgy, SX500-class superalloys — that make oxygen-rich operation survivable. Specifications are compiled for seven engines spanning 8 to 330 bar. We quantify diminishing specific-impulse returns with pressure, the thrust-density payoff, and the penalties of closed cycles: start-transient complexity, preburner chemistry constraints, and reusability trade-offs.

## 1. Introduction

Every liquid rocket engine answers one question: *what happens to the turbine exhaust that drives the propellant pumps?* A **gas-generator** cycle dumps it overboard; a **staged-combustion** cycle injects it into the main chamber and burns it to completion; a **full-flow staged-combustion** (FFSC) cycle goes to the limit — *all* fuel through a fuel-rich preburner driving the fuel turbopump, *all* oxidizer through an oxidizer-rich preburner driving the oxidizer turbopump [5].

The stakes are thermodynamic. Pump power scales with the pressure rise the pumps must deliver — chamber pressure plus cooling-jacket, injector, and preburner drops. Turbine power scales with turbine mass flow and extractable enthalpy drop, capped by blade temperature limits. Partial-flow cycles divert only a fraction of one propellant through the preburner, forcing high turbine temperatures; FFSC sends the *entire* inventory through the turbines, extracting the same power far cooler — longer life, headroom past 300 bar [5][9].

> **Thesis statement.** *FFSC is the thermodynamic endgame of chemical rocket power cycles: gasifying 100% of both propellants through dual preburners decouples turbine power from turbine temperature, enabling 300+ bar chamber pressures with cooler, longer-lived turbomachinery — purchased with the most complex start transients, the most aggressive materials requirements, and a parts count only modern manufacturing has made tractable.*

Section 2 reviews ideal-rocket relations and cycle taxonomy; Section 3 the power-balance methodology; Section 4 Raptor, the RD-180/BE-4 lineage, the RS-25/IPD, and open-cycle alternatives; Section 5 data and quantitative checks; Sections 6–7 limitations and conclusions.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The ideal rocket: why chamber pressure matters

> **Theorem (Ideal thrust).** *For steady, quasi-one-dimensional, adiabatic flow, the vacuum thrust is F = ṁ·v_e + (p_e − p_a)·A_e, and the specific impulse is I_sp = F / (ṁ·g_0), where ṁ is propellant mass flow, v_e exhaust velocity, p_e exit pressure, p_a ambient pressure, A_e exit area, and g_0 standard gravity.*

The **characteristic velocity** c* = p_c·A_t / ṁ measures combustion energy release (∝ √(T_c/M)); the **thrust coefficient** C_F = F / (p_c·A_t) measures nozzle effectiveness. Then:

> **Definition (Decomposition of specific impulse).** *I_sp = c* · C_F / g_0. Chemistry sets c*; the nozzle and chamber pressure set C_F.*

Chamber pressure matters through several channels. Thrust per unit throat area is proportional to p_c, so higher pressure means a smaller, lighter engine per unit thrust. Higher p_c suppresses nozzle flow separation at sea level, permitting larger expansion ratios and higher C_F. Higher pressure also accelerates chamber chemical kinetics, allowing a shorter characteristic chamber length L* and a more compact chamber [5]. The direct effect on c* is weak (logarithmic, via dissociation suppression) — which is why I_sp shows *diminishing returns* with pressure, as Section 5 quantifies — while thrust-to-weight improves dramatically.

### 2.2 Taxonomy of pump-fed power cycles

Liquid engines are classified by turbine-drive-fluid path [5]:

| Cycle | Turbine drive fluid | Fate of turbine exhaust | Typical p_c range | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pressure-fed | none (tank pressure) | n/a | < 20 bar | Kestrel, AJ10 |
| Gas-generator (open) | fuel-rich or oxidizer-rich gas | dumped overboard | 70–110 bar | Merlin 1D, F-1, Vulcain |
| Expander (open/closed) | vaporized fuel from cooling jacket | overboard / into chamber | 35–110 bar | RL10 (open expander) |
| Oxidizer-rich staged combustion | O₂-rich preburner gas | into main chamber | 140–270 bar | RD-180, RD-170, BE-4 |
| Fuel-rich staged combustion | H₂-rich preburner gas | into main chamber | 200–210 bar | RS-25, RD-0120 |
| Full-flow staged combustion | O₂-rich *and* fuel-rich preburner gases | into main chamber | 270–350 bar | Raptor, (RD-270, IPD*) |

*The Soviet RD-270 (1962–1970) and the U.S. IPD (2004–2005) were FFSC developments that never flew [5][8].

The **gas-generator** cycle burns a small propellant fraction (typically 2–5%) in a separate combustor to drive the turbines, dumping the exhaust at a matching I_sp cost. The **expander** cycle routes fuel through the cooling jacket to vaporize and drive the turbine before injection — elegant but power-limited by wall heat transfer, capping it at modest thrust with superb efficiency [1]. **Staged combustion** closes the cycle: preburner exhaust is injected into the main chamber. Because the preburner operates *above* chamber pressure, the pumps must deliver enormous discharge pressures — the cycle's fundamental mechanical burden.

### 2.3 The turbine bargain: power, temperature, and mass flow

For a pump raising mass flow ṁ through pressure rise Δp with fluid density ρ and efficiency η_p:

> **Theorem (Pump power).** *P_pump = ṁ · Δp / (ρ · η_p). The turbine must supply at least this power: P_turbine = ṁ_t · c_p · T_in · η_t · (1 − (p_out/p_in)^((γ−1)/γ)) ≥ P_pump, where ṁ_t is the mass flow through the turbine.*

This relation is the staged-combustion design space in one line. Turbine inlet temperature is limited by blade materials (~800–1000 K with margin); turbine pressure ratio by pump discharge requirements. The free variable is **ṁ_t, the propellant fraction through the preburner**: small in partial-flow cycles (forcing high T_in), unity per circuit in full-flow — the same power at far lower temperature, the decisive advantage Raptor exploits [5][9].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Closed-cycle power-balance framework

Our method is a systems-level power balance reconstructing each engine's pump pressure ladder: chamber pressure p_c, plus injector drop (~15–25% of p_c), cooling-jacket drop (~p_c^0.8 via the Bartz correlation), and preburner drop for staged cycles (the preburner sits above p_c so its exhaust can inject into the chamber). Turbine power must then satisfy the pump-power theorem given the cycle's turbine mass-flow fraction — explaining why the RD-180's pumps discharge far above 267 bar, why BE-4's 140-bar chamber is deliberate derating, and why Raptor's full-flow arrangement targets 300+ bar without superalloy-melting turbine temperatures [2][4][6].

### 3.2 Empirical method

All specifications are published manufacturer, test-program, or NASA values, cited inline, with versions stated where they evolved. Computed quantities derive transparently from cited thrust/I_sp pairs in Section 5, so every number is traceable.

### 3.3 Scope and limits of the analysis

This is a *cycle-level* synthesis, not a component CFD study: injector acoustics, preburner kinetics, and rotordynamics are abstracted away. Its value is comparative — the same power-balance lens applied uniformly across seven engines reveals which performance differences are chemistry, which are cycle architecture, and which are simply pressure.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Full-flow staged combustion: the Raptor architecture

SpaceX's Raptor is the first flown FFSC engine [5]: LOX/liquid methane at mixture ratio 3.6, two independent turbopumps — fuel pump driven by a **fuel-rich preburner**, oxidizer pump by an **oxidizer-rich preburner** — the full exhaust of both, the entire propellant inventory gasified, injected into the main chamber [1].

The consequences are decisive: **cooler turbines** (full mass flow through each turbine extracts rated power at moderate preburner temperatures — longer life, better reusability); **no interpropellant turbine seal** (each turbopump sees one chemistry, eliminating a notorious failure point); **gas-gas injection** (hot-gas propellants mix and burn faster, permitting a smaller chamber at higher pressure); and **record chamber pressures** — Raptor 1 ~270 bar, Raptor 2 300 bar (exceeding the RD-180's 267-bar record), Raptor 3 targeting ~330–350 bar [1][5][10]. February 2019 firings reached 268.9 bar, breaking the record by ~1% [10].

Raptor 2: 230 tf (2,256 kN) sea-level thrust, 327 s sea-level / 350 s vacuum I_sp, ~650 kg/s mass flow, thrust-to-weight 141. Raptor 3: 250 tf sea-level (2,452 kN), 275 tf vacuum, thrust-to-weight 164 at ~1,525 kg [1]. Methane does not coke — keeping the fuel-rich preburner and cooling passages clean across reuse cycles — while staying dense enough for a compact booster [9].

| Version | Sea-level thrust | Chamber pressure | Sea-level I_sp | Dry mass | Thrust-to-weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Raptor 1 | 185 tf (1,814 kN) | ~270 bar | ~330 s | 2,080 kg | 88.9 |
| Raptor 2 | 230 tf (2,256 kN) | 300 bar | 327 s | 1,630 kg | 141.1 |
| Raptor 3 | 250 tf (2,452 kN) | ~330–350 bar | ~350 s* | 1,525 kg | 163.9 |

*I_sp varies by source and nozzle variant; vacuum-optimized values reach ~380 s [1][10].

Materials were a pacing technology: the oxidizer-rich preburner bathes turbine hardware in hot, high-pressure oxygen that ignites most metals. SpaceX developed its SX500 superalloy for this environment [9], echoing the Soviet Union's decades-long development of oxygen-compatible metallurgy for the RD-170 family.

### 4.2 Oxygen-rich staged combustion: the RD-180/RD-170 lineage and BE-4

Valentin Glushko's bureau made the opposite bargain from the Americans: where the U.S. pursued fuel-rich (hydrogen) staged combustion, Glushko mastered **oxidizer-rich** staged combustion for kerosene. The logic is chemical: a kerosene *fuel-rich* preburner produces soot that cokes passages and blades, while an *oxidizer-rich* preburner burns cleanly — at the cost of bathing turbomachinery in hot, high-pressure oxygen that combusts ordinary alloys [2].

The **RD-180** is a two-chamber derivative of the four-chamber RD-170 built for the Energia super-heavy launcher. With LOX-lead start, oxidizer-rich preburner, and one turbopump assembly feeding both chambers, it delivers 860,200 lbf (3,830 kN) sea-level and 933,400 lbf (4,150 kN) vacuum thrust, at 311.3/337.8 s sea-level/vacuum I_sp, chamber pressure 3,722 psia (267 bar), mixture ratio 2.72, nozzle ratio 36.87 [2]. NASA Marshall hot-fire testing confirmed the oxidizer lead start/shutdown modes that eliminate coking and unburned-kerosene pollution [3]. For two decades it held the flown-engine chamber-pressure record, until Raptor surpassed it [10].

Blue Origin's **BE-4** applies the same ORSC cycle to methane — avoiding kerosene's coking while retaining the architecture's heritage logic. But BE-4 is deliberately *conservative*: chamber pressure is only 140 bar, roughly half of Raptor 2's, trading peak performance for lower development risk and reusability margin [4]. It produces 2,460 kN sea-level thrust (uprated toward 2,847 kN), vacuum I_sp ≈ 340 s; its turbopump generates ~70,000 hp at 19,000 rpm, discharge near 5,000 psi [4]. First flight: Vulcan Centaur, January 2024; also powers New Glenn [4]. Same cycle family and propellants as Raptor — a different point on the performance–risk–reusability frontier.

### 4.3 Fuel-rich staged combustion: RS-25 and the hydrogen lineage

The American lineage runs on hydrogen. The **RS-25** (Space Shuttle Main Engine) uses a **fuel-rich dual-shaft** cycle: two fuel-rich preburners drive the fuel and oxidizer turbopumps, with hydrogen first routed through the cooling jacket so its heat pickup adds turbine drive energy [6]. At mixture ratio 6.03 it delivers 2,279 kN vacuum thrust (1,860 kN sea level), vacuum I_sp 452.3 s — among the highest ever for a chemical engine — at 2,994 psi (206 bar), throttling 67–109% [6].

Hydrogen dictates the fuel-rich choice: hydrogen-rich preburner gas is benign to turbine materials, whereas an oxygen-rich preburner in a hydrogen engine would be a standing detonation hazard. The penalty is density — roughly one-fourteenth of kerosene — demanding enormous volumetric pump flow and heavy turbomachinery per unit thrust. The 78:1 nozzle and 452 s vacuum I_sp show what the cycle achieves when efficiency, not thrust density, is the objective [6].

The hydrogen FFSC endpoint was the **Integrated Powerhead Demonstrator (IPD)**, a joint NASA/Air Force program: a full-flow, hydrogen-fueled *powerhead* with dual preburners and hydrostatic bearings, targeting the 250,000-lbf class as a future reusable engine's front end [8]. Stennis testing in 2004–2005 accumulated 300 seconds across 21 of 26 tests at up to full power; no flight engine was funded [8]. The IPD validated FFSC powerhead physics a decade before Raptor flew the cycle — on hydrogen, with its pump-size penalty, rather than methane.

### 4.4 The open and regenerative alternatives: gas-generator and expander cycles

The **gas-generator** cycle dominates operational launch through simplicity: SpaceX's **Merlin 1D** burns LOX/RP-1 at 9.7 MPa, producing 845 kN sea-level thrust (981 kN vacuum) at 282/311 s sea-level/vacuum I_sp and thrust-to-weight 184, via pintle injector and single-shaft turbopump [7]. Its ancestor, the Saturn V's **F-1**: 690 tf sea-level thrust at 80 atm, 265.4 s sea-level I_sp [11]. The 2–4% I_sp penalty from dumped turbine exhaust is real — but so are benign starts, deep throttling, and low-cost manufacturing, the attributes behind Merlin's Falcon 9 workhorse status [1][7].

The **expander** cycle (RL10 family) uses the fuel's cooling-jacket heat pickup alone to drive the turbines — no preburner. Outstanding I_sp at modest pressures, but power-limited by wall heat transfer to upper-stage thrust classes [1]. The taxonomy is a ladder of *turbine energy recovery*: expander (heat-limited) → gas-generator (dump it) → staged combustion (burn it) → full-flow (burn all of it, twice).

### 5.1 Comparative engine data

Three patterns emerge. **First**, pressure and thrust-to-weight track turbine-energy recovery — though Merlin's T/W of 184 shows structural efficiency matters as much as cycle. **Second**, I_sp is dominated by chemistry, not cycle: the hydrogen RS-25's 452 s dwarfs every hydrocarbon engine, and Raptor's 300 bar buys only ~16 s of sea-level I_sp over the RD-180's 267 bar — the diminishing returns of Section 2.1. **Third**, within a propellant family the closed cycle's margin is decisive: Raptor's 327 s vs. Merlin's 282 s at sea level is the value of recovering the turbine exhaust.

### 5.2 Quantitative checks

Mass flows from published thrust/I_sp pairs, and the Δv payoff of staged-combustion I_sp via the Tsiolkovsky equation:

```python
# Mass flow from published thrust and sea-level Isp
g0 = 9.80665
engines = (
    ("Merlin 1D (gas-generator)", 845.0, 282.0),
    ("RD-180 (ORSC)", 3830.0, 311.0),
    ("Raptor 2 (FFSC)", 2256.0, 327.0),
)
for name, thrust_kn, isp_sl in engines:
    mdot = thrust_kn * 1000.0 / (isp_sl * g0)
    print(name, round(mdot, 1))
```

```python
# Tsiolkovsky: delta-v payoff of closed-cycle Isp at fixed mass ratio
import math
g0 = 9.80665
def delta_v(isp_s, mass_ratio):
    return isp_s * g0 * math.log(mass_ratio)
for isp in (282.0, 311.0, 327.0):
    print(isp, round(delta_v(isp, 8.0) / 1000.0, 2))
```

The mass-flow check reproduces Raptor's published ~650–700 kg/s flow within pairing uncertainty [1]. The Δv comparison shows the 45-second sea-level I_sp gap between Merlin and Raptor 2 is worth roughly 0.9 km/s at mass ratio 8 — a decisive booster-stage margin, and the quantitative reason closed cycles dominate high-energy design.

Pump-power scaling illustrates the turbine bargain: raising chamber pressure from Merlin's 97 bar to Raptor 2's 300 bar roughly triples required pump pressure rise, tripling pump power per unit mass flow. In a gas-generator cycle the turbine mass-flow fraction is fixed at a few percent, so turbine inlet temperature would have to rise destructively; in FFSC the fraction is unity on each circuit — the power is available at moderate temperature by construction [5][9].

### 5.3 Start-transient state machine

Closed cycles pay for steady-state elegance with the most complex start sequences in propulsion:

```python
# Staged-combustion start sequence as a state machine
sequence = {
    "purge": "chill_in",
    "chill_in": "preburner_ignition",
    "preburner_ignition": "turbine_spinup",
    "turbine_spinup": "mainstage",
    "mainstage": "steady_state",
}
state = "purge"
while state != "steady_state":
    print(state)
    state = sequence.get(state)
print("steady_state")
```

Each transition is a coupled thermofluid event: preburner ignition timing sets turbine spin-up, which sets pump discharge pressure, which sets preburner feed pressure — a feedback loop choreographed to avoid spikes or flameout. Raptor's development is largely the history of mastering this loop at 300 bar; the RD-180's LOX-lead start reflects the same concern in oxidizer-rich chemistry [2][3][10].

## 6. Limitations

**Data provenance.** Published specifications are nominal values at specific ratings; Raptor 3 figures describe an engine still in qualification, and I_sp values vary with nozzle variant [1][10]. We use the most widely cited figures and state the version.

**Cycle-level scope.** The power-balance framework abstracts injector acoustics, preburner kinetics, and rotordynamics — any of which can dominate a real program. Combustion instability at 300+ bar remains active research; this analysis does not predict stability margins.

**Materials and life.** "Cooler turbines" is relative: oxidizer-rich preburner gas at any temperature is a severe materials environment, and long-life reusability at 300 bar rests on test programs, not the decades of flight heritage the RS-25 accumulated [6][8].

**Chemistry ceiling.** No cycle beats the I_sp limit set by propellant energy: methalox vacuum I_sp asymptotes near 380 s regardless of pressure, bounding the payoff of pushing past 350 bar [1][5]. The next leap needs different chemistry or physics, not a better cycle.

**Unflown architectures.** The IPD demonstrated the FFSC powerhead but never a flight engine, and no full-flow *hydrogen* engine has flown; Raptor's methane success does not automatically transfer to hydrogen's pump-size penalty [8].

## 7. Conclusion

The arc from the F-1's open gas-generator cycle to Raptor's full-flow staged combustion is the arc of turbine-exhaust recovery: dump it, then burn it, then burn all of it through two preburners. Turbine power must come from somewhere, and the only free variable is how much propellant mass flows through the turbine. Full-flow staging sets that fraction to unity, decoupling turbine power from turbine temperature — unlocking 300+ bar chamber pressures with cooler, longer-lived turbomachinery and no interpropellant seal [5][9].

The data support the theory with nuance. Within hydrocarbons the closed-cycle margin is large (Raptor's 327 s vs. Merlin's 282 s at sea level); across propellants chemistry dominates (RS-25's 452 s on hydrogen); pressure shows diminishing I_sp returns but dramatic thrust-density gains (Raptor 2's T/W of 141 at 300 bar) [1][6][7]. The RD-170 → RD-180 → BE-4 lineage shows one cycle family tuned across the performance–risk–reusability frontier: 267 bar of record pressure vs. 140 bar of conservative reusability [2][4].

FFSC is thus the *closure* of the chemical rocket cycle design space: no turbine exhaust remains to recover. Future gains must come from propellants, materials, manufacturing, and operations — where Raptor's real revolution, methane-fueled rapid reusability at unprecedented power density, is being fought.
