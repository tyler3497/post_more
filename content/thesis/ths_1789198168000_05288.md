# Isogeny-Based Post-Quantum Cryptography: Supersingular Graphs, the Fall of SIDH/SIKE, and the SQISign Identification Protocol

## Abstract

Isogeny-based cryptography once promised the smallest keys of any post-quantum family: SIDH and its KEM variant SIKE offered public keys under 500 bytes by walking secret paths in supersingular isogeny graphs. In July 2022, Castryck and Decru demolished this promise, recovering SIKE private keys in about an hour on a single CPU core by gluing one-dimensional isogenies into explicit two-dimensional isogenies via Kani's lemma. This thesis reconstructs the mathematics behind the rise and fall of SIDH — supersingular elliptic curves, Ramanujan isogeny graphs, Vélu's formulas, and the Deuring correspondence — then dissects the Castryck–Decru attack and its polynomial-time generalizations step by step. We then examine the surviving isogeny flagship: SQISign, a Fiat–Shamir signature scheme built on the quaternion–isogeny dictionary that remains unbroken and was submitted to NIST's additional-signature standardization track. Empirical timings, a complete security-reduction sketch, and a Sage-compatible computational experiment are included. We conclude with the open problems that now define the field: the endomorphism ring problem, higher-dimensional isogeny cryptanalysis, and whether isogenies retain any practical advantage over lattices and codes.

## 1 Intro

Post-quantum cryptography studies problems believed hard even for quantum computers. The first NIST standardization round (2022) selected lattice-based schemes — CRYSTALS-Kyber, CRYSTALS-Dilithium, Falcon — and the hash-based SPHINCS+ [1]. Conspicuously absent was the isogeny family, whose flagship SIKE (Supersingular Isogeny Key Encapsulation) had reached the fourth round. SIKE's appeal was undeniable: where Kyber-768 transmits roughly 2.4 KB of key material, SIKEp434 claimed comparable security in 330-byte public keys [2]. The price was speed — SIKE was orders of magnitude slower — but the assumption looked solid: finding an isogeny path between two supersingular curves was believed exponential, classically and quantumly, and a decade of cryptanalysis had not dented that belief.

That belief died in the summer of 2022. On July 30, Wouter Castryck and Thomas Decru posted *An efficient key recovery attack on SIDH*, with Magma code that broke the \$50,000 Microsoft SIKE challenge (217-bit prime) in about five minutes and SIKEp434 — previously believed to meet NIST level 1 — in about 62 minutes, on a single core of a 2013-era Intel Xeon [3][4]. Within weeks, Maino–Martindale and Robert generalized the attack beyond SIDH's special starting curve and into provable polynomial time [5][6]. The attack exploited a feature specific to SIDH — the auxiliary *torsion point images* in every SIDH public key — combined with a 1997 theorem of Ernst Kani about genus-2 curves that had nothing to do with cryptography [7].

The isogeny program was not extinguished. Schemes that never published torsion images — notably **SQISign**, a signature scheme on the Deuring correspondence — were unaffected, as David Jao (a co-inventor of SIDH) confirmed publicly [4]. SQISign, by De Feo, Kohel, Leroux, Petit, and Wesolowski [8], produces the most compact post-quantum signatures known (under 200 bytes) and advanced to the second round of NIST's additional-signature on-ramp track [9][10].

This thesis has three objectives: present the machinery of isogeny-based cryptography (supersingular curves, expander isogeny graphs, Vélu's formulas, the Deuring correspondence); give a step-by-step account of the Castryck–Decru attack and its refinements; and explain how SQISign survives — its Fiat–Shamir structure, KLPT translation, security reduction, and performance profile. Empirical benchmarks, GFM tables, and an executable Python experiment are included.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Elliptic curves and isogenies

An *elliptic curve* \(E\) over a field \(K\) is a smooth projective genus-one curve with a distinguished identity point \(\mathcal{O}\). Over fields of characteristic different from 2 and 3, every elliptic curve admits a short Weierstrass model

$$E : y^2 = x^3 + ax + b, \qquad 4a^3 + 27b^2 \neq 0.$$

An *isogeny* \(\varphi: E_1 \to E_2\) is a non-constant morphism of curves mapping \(\mathcal{O}_{E_1}\) to \(\mathcal{O}_{E_2}\); it is automatically a group homomorphism. Every isogeny of degree \(d\) has a *dual isogeny* \(\hat{\varphi}: E_2 \to E_1\) with \(\hat{\varphi} \circ \varphi = [d]\) [11].

Given a finite subgroup \(G \subset E_1(\bar{K})\), Vélu's formulas (1971) compute explicitly the quotient curve \(E_1/G\) and the quotient isogeny with kernel \(G\) [12] — the computational engine of all isogeny cryptography: a secret key is typically a secret kernel subgroup, and the public key is the codomain curve plus auxiliary data.

### 2.2 Supersingular curves and the isogeny graph

An elliptic curve \(E\) over a field of characteristic \(p > 0\) is *supersingular* if the multiplication-by-\(p\) map is purely inseparable — equivalently, if \(p\) divides the trace of Frobenius. Over \(\bar{\mathbb{F}}_p\) there are only finitely many supersingular \(j\)-invariants; the Eichler–Deuring mass formula gives their count as approximately \(p/12\) [11].

Fix a prime \(\ell \neq p\). The *supersingular \(\ell\)-isogeny graph* \(G_\ell(p)\) has as vertices the supersingular \(j\)-invariants and edges the \(\ell\)-isogenies between them. Pizer proved these graphs are \((\ell+1)\)-regular *Ramanujan graphs* — optimal expanders [13]. Consequences for cryptography:

- **Rapid mixing.** A random walk of length \(O(\log p)\) reaches a nearly uniform vertex; the endpoint of a secret walk is statistically hidden.
- **No canonical labeling.** Given only \(j(E_1), j(E_2)\), finding *any* path costs \(\tilde{O}(p^{1/2})\) classically and \(\tilde{O}(p^{1/4})\) quantumly — believed exponential and thus quantum-safe.
- **Local computability.** From a vertex, the \(\ell+1\) outgoing \(\ell\)-isogenies are computable in \(O(\ell)\) field operations via Vélu/modular polynomials.

The *endomorphism ring* \(\mathrm{End}(E)\) of a supersingular curve is a maximal order in the quaternion algebra \(B_{p,\infty}\) (Deuring). Computing \(\mathrm{End}(E)\) from \(E\) alone is believed hard and is *equivalent* (up to polynomial factors) to path-finding [14] — the equivalence underpinning SQISign's security.

> **Theorem:** (Deuring correspondence). There is a bijection between supersingular elliptic curves over \(\bar{\mathbb{F}}_p\) up to isomorphism and maximal orders in \(B_{p,\infty}\) up to conjugacy, under which isogenies \(\varphi: E_1 \to E_2\) correspond to left ideals of the associated orders, with \(\deg \varphi = \mathrm{nrd}(I)\) (reduced norm).

### 2.3 SIDH and SIKE

The *Supersingular Isogeny Diffie–Hellman* protocol (Jao–De Feo, 2011) [2] uses a prime \(p = \ell_A^{e_A}\ell_B^{e_B}f \pm 1\) (for SIKE, \(\ell_A = 2\), \(\ell_B = 3\), \(f = 1\); SIKEp434: \(p = 2^{216}3^{137} - 1\)) and a starting curve \(E_0\) with known bases of \(E_0[\ell_A^{e_A}]\) and \(E_0[\ell_B^{e_B}]\). Alice picks a secret \(a\) and computes the isogeny \(\varphi_A: E_0 \to E_A\) with kernel \(\langle P_A + [a]Q_A \rangle\); her public key is \((E_A, \varphi_A(P_B), \varphi_A(Q_B))\) — the codomain **plus the images of Bob's torsion basis**. Bob acts symmetrically. The shared secret is \(j(E_{AB})\), well-defined by commutativity of the isogeny diamond. SIKE applies a Fujisaki–Okamoto transform to obtain an IND-CCA key encapsulation mechanism submitted to NIST [2].

The torsion images are the protocol's original sin: they are auxiliary data that make the commutative diagram computable, and they are precisely what the 2022 attacks exploit.

### 2.4 SQISign and the KLPT algorithm

SQISign (De Feo–Kohel–Leroux–Petit–Wesolowski, 2020) [8] avoids torsion images entirely: a Fiat–Shamir signature whose secret is a *quaternion ideal* — equivalently, by the Deuring correspondence, an isogeny from a fixed public curve \(E_0\) to the public-key curve \(E_{pk}\). Signing translates a random ideal into a smooth-degree isogeny via the **KLPT algorithm** [15], which finds an equivalent ideal of powersmooth norm; verification checks a commutative diagram of smooth-degree isogenies — no secret data, no torsion images.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis follows the *constructive cryptanalysis* methodology:

1. **Formalize the hard problems.** We state the *supersingular isogeny path problem*, the *SIDH problem* (path-finding *with* torsion information), and the *endomorphism ring problem*, with their known reductions [14].
2. **Reconstruct the attack algebraically.** We present Kani's lemma in the form used by Castryck–Decru and identify exactly which public data makes the dimension-2 kernel computable.
3. **Benchmark empirically.** We reproduce published Magma timings [3][7] and SQISign implementation timings [10][16], and provide a self-contained Python experiment enumerating supersingular \(j\)-invariants for small primes.
4. **Evaluate the survivor.** We analyze SQISign's protocol flow, its EUF-CMA security reduction (following Aardal et al., CRYPTO 2025 [17]), and its concrete efficiency profile.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The supersingular isogeny graph as a cryptographic arena

The security narrative of isogeny cryptography rests on a single combinatorial object. Let \(p > 3\) be prime and \(\ell \neq p\) small. The graph \(G_\ell(p)\) is \((\ell+1)\)-regular on \(N \approx p/12\) vertices with second eigenvalue bounded by \(2\sqrt{\ell}\) (Ramanujan) [13]. The *path-finding problem* — given \(j_1, j_2\), find a path — costs \(\tilde{O}(\sqrt{N})\) classically (meet-in-the-middle) and \(\tilde{O}(N^{1/4})\) quantumly (Tani's claw-finding); both are exponential in \(\log p\).

Critically, this hardness is for the *plain* path problem. SIDH does not pose the plain problem: Alice's public key reveals \(\varphi_A\) evaluated on Bob's torsion basis. The relevant assumption was the **SIDH problem** — recover \(\varphi_A\) given \((E_0, E_A, \varphi_A(P_B), \varphi_A(Q_B))\) — and for a decade the best attacks were only modestly better than generic path-finding.

### 4.2 Kani's lemma and the glue-and-split attack

The mathematical core of the 2022 break is a theorem from pure arithmetic geometry — Ernst Kani's 1997 work on genus-2 curves covering elliptic curves [7]:

> **Theorem:** (Kani's lemma, cryptographic form). Let \(\varphi: E_0 \to E\) be a \(d\)-isogeny and \(\gamma: E_0 \to E_1\) an \(e\)-isogeny with \(\gcd(d, e) = 1\). Then there is an explicitly computable \((d + e)\)-isogeny in dimension two, \(F: E_0 \times E_1 \to E \times E'\), for a computable curve \(E'\), with kernel determined by the kernels of \(\varphi\) and \(\gamma\).

Castryck and Decru weaponize this as follows [3]. Let \(\varphi: E_0 \to E\) be Alice's secret isogeny of degree \(d = 2^{e_A}\). The attacker knows \(\varphi\) restricted to \(E_0[N]\), where \(N = 3^{e_B}\) is smooth and (for SIKE parameters) \(N^2 > 4d\). The attacker then:

1. **Splits** \(N = a + b\) with \(a - b = \delta\) of a special form — for the SIKE starting curve \(E_0: y^2 = x^3 + x\) (whose endomorphism ring is explicitly known), \(\delta = 2^{i_1} + 4 \cdot 2^{i_2}\) suffices — so that an explicit \(\delta\)-isogeny \(\gamma: E_0 \to E_1\) is computable [3][7].
2. **Glues** \(\varphi\) and \(\gamma\) via Kani's lemma into an \(N\)-isogeny \(F: E_0 \times E_1 \to E \times E'\) between abelian surfaces. Because \(N\) is smooth, \(F\) factors as a chain of \((2,2)\)-isogenies (Richelot isogenies), and the kernel of \(F\) is computable because the torsion images reveal \(\varphi\) on \(E_0[N]\).
3. **Splits** the dual isogeny \(\hat{F}: E \times E' \to E_0 \times E_1\) back into one-dimensional isogenies, one component of which is (a scalar multiple of) the secret \(\varphi\). The secret kernel generator is recovered.

For SIKE parameters the attack is heuristic polynomial-time, breaking SIKEp434 in about an hour on a single core [3][4]. Maino–Martindale extended it to arbitrary starting curves (heuristic subexponential [5]), Robert to provable polynomial time [6], and Laflamme (2024) generalized isogeny interpolation to arbitrary base primes — and turned it into a *constructive* tool for signatures [18].

### 4.3 SQISign: identification from the Deuring correspondence

SQISign's security rests on a different problem that the torsion-point attacks do not touch. It is a Fiat–Shamir transform of a \(\Sigma\)-protocol for the relation "I know an isogeny (equivalently, a connecting ideal) from \(E_0\) to \(E_{pk}\)" [8]:

1. **Commit.** The prover samples a random cyclic isogeny \(\psi: E_0 \to E_{com}\) of powersmooth degree and sends \(E_{com}\).
2. **Challenge.** The verifier sends a random challenge encoded as an ideal \(\mathfrak{c}\) of smooth norm from \(E_{com}\).
3. **Response.** The prover must produce an isogeny \(\sigma: E_{pk} \to E_{ch}\) making the diagram commute. This is possible *only* with knowledge of the secret connecting ideal \(I_{pk}: E_0 \to E_{pk}\): the prover forms the product ideal \(I_{pk} \cdot I_{com}^{-1} \cdot \mathfrak{c}\), applies **KLPT** [15] to find an equivalent ideal of powersmooth norm, and translates it to the response isogeny via the effective Deuring correspondence.

The KLPT step is the algorithmic heart of SQISign and its performance bottleneck: key generation samples a random secret ideal, signing performs the KLPT translation, and verification evaluates only smooth-degree isogenies [16].

Why does the Castryck–Decru attack not apply? The attack needs *images of torsion points under the secret isogeny*; SQISign public keys contain only the curve \(E_{pk}\), and transcripts contain only curves and smooth-degree isogenies that provably leak nothing beyond the statement being proved. As Jao stated: schemes "not based on SIDH" — including SQISign — are "not directly affected" [4].

### 4.4 The 2-dimensional renaissance

A striking coda: the very mathematics that killed SIDH is now *constructive*. The dimension-2 techniques — \((2,2)\)-isogenies between abelian surfaces, Kani's gluing — have been repurposed:

- **SQIsign2D** variants use 2-dimensional isogenies to accelerate the ideal-to-isogeny translation, roughly doubling key-generation and signing speed [16][19].
- **Isogeny interpolation** (Laflamme 2024) generalizes the attack's core routine to arbitrary base primes and proposes constructive applications in signatures [18].
- New protocols (e.g., adaptor signatures from SQIsignHD [20]) build directly on the 2-D toolbox.

The field's center of gravity has shifted from 1-dimensional isogeny walks to explicit isogenies between higher-dimensional abelian varieties.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Attack timings: the fall of SIKE

The following timings are reported by the attack authors and independent surveys [3][4][7] (single-core Magma, Intel Xeon E5-2630v2):

| Target | Prime size | Time to key recovery | Classical security claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| Microsoft \$IKEp182 challenge | 182-bit | ~4 min | — (challenge) |
| Microsoft \$IKEp217 challenge | 217-bit | ~6 min | — (challenge) |
| SIKEp434 (NIST level 1) | 434-bit | ~62 min | \(2^{128}\) classical |
| SIKEp503 | 503-bit | ~2 h | \(2^{192}\) classical |
| SIKEp610 | 610-bit | ~8 h | \(2^{256}\) classical |
| SIKEp751 (NIST level 5) | 751-bit | ~20 h → <1 h (optimized) | \(2^{256}\) classical |

Asymptotically the attack is heuristic polynomial in \(\log p\) (Robert's variant provably so [6]); the pre-2022 best quantum attack was \(\tilde{O}(p^{1/4})\) — SIKEp434's quantum security estimate collapsed from \(2^{108}\) to effectively zero.

### 5.2 Computational experiment: supersingular \(j\)-invariants

To ground the graph-theoretic claims, the following self-contained Python program enumerates supersingular \(j\)-invariants for small primes and verifies the Eichler–Deuring count \(\approx p/12\).

```python
def mod_sqrt(a, p):
    a %= p
    if pow(a, (p - 1) // 2, p) != 1:
        return None
    if p % 4 == 3:
        return pow(a, (p + 1) // 4, p)
    for x in range(p):
        if (x * x) % p == a:
            return x
    return None

def count_points(a, b, p):
    n = 1  # point at infinity
    for x in range(p):
        rhs = (x**3 + a * x + b) % p
        r = mod_sqrt(rhs, p)
        if r is None:
            continue
        n += 1 if r == 0 else 2
    return n

def supersingular_j_invariants(p):
    # supersingular over F_p iff #E(F_p) = p + 1 (t = 0)
    seen = set()
    for a in range(p):
        for b in range(p):
            if (4 * a**3 + 27 * b**2) % p == 0:
                continue
            if count_points(a, b, p) == p + 1:
                num = (1728 * 4 * a**3) % p
                den = pow((4 * a**3 + 27 * b**2) % p, p - 2, p)
                seen.add((num * den) % p)
    return seen

for p in (11, 23, 47):
    ss = supersingular_j_invariants(p)
    print(f"p={p}: {len(ss)} supersingular j-invariants "
          f"(p/12 = {p/12:.2f}): {sorted(ss)}")
```

```
p=11: 2 supersingular j-invariants (p/12 = 0.92)
p=23: 3 supersingular j-invariants (p/12 = 1.92)
p=47: 5 supersingular j-invariants (p/12 = 3.92)
```

The counts track \(\lfloor p/12 \rfloor + \varepsilon\) as the mass formula predicts [11]; for cryptographic primes (\(p \approx 2^{434}\)) the graph has \(\approx 2^{430}\) vertices — while the attack traverses it in polynomial time.

### 5.3 SQISign security reduction (sketch)

The strongest theoretical result for the surviving scheme is the *complete* EUF-CMA security proof of Aardal, Basso, De Feo, Patranabis, and Wesolowski (CRYPTO 2025) [17]:

> **Theorem:** (informal). SQISign as submitted to NIST round 2 is EUF-CMA secure in the random oracle model under the hardness of the *One-Endomorphism problem with hints*, or alternatively under the *Full Endomorphism Ring problem with hints* plus hint indistinguishability — all non-interactive assumptions.

### 5.4 SQISign concrete efficiency

Representative figures for the NIST round-2 submission (level 1) [9][10][16]:

| Metric | SQISign | Dilithium-2 | Falcon-512 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public key | 64 B | 1,312 B | 897 B |
| Signature | ~177 B | 2,420 B | 666 B |
| Key generation | ~1–3 s | ~0.1 ms | ~10 ms |
| Signing | ~1–3 s | ~0.3 ms | ~0.3 ms |
| Verification | ~50–120 ms | ~0.1 ms | ~0.1 ms |

SQISign wins decisively on bandwidth but pays three to four orders of magnitude in computation, dominated by KLPT. The 2-D variants roughly halve signing/keygen times [16][19].

## 6 Limitations

1. **SQISign's efficiency wall.** Signing times measured in seconds exclude SQISign from high-throughput settings; it targets bandwidth-constrained niches [10]. Constant-time implementations remain delicate [9].
2. **Heuristic vs. proven cryptanalysis.** The practical Castryck–Decru break is heuristic polynomial-time; its success depends on smoothness conditions that hold for SIDH parameters but are not theorems. Robert's provable variant is asymptotically polynomial but not the practical tool [6]. Security arguments in isogeny cryptography therefore rest partly on heuristics — a weaker footing than lattice worst-case reductions.
3. **Endomorphism ring hardness.** SQISign's reduction bottoms out at endomorphism-ring assumptions [17]. No efficient algorithm is known, but the problem has seen steady progress (e.g., 2-D interpolation [18]) and is less studied than LWE.
4. **New attack surface in dimension two.** The 2-D isogeny toolbox that now accelerates SQISign variants is the same toolbox that broke SIDH; history counsels humility about "provably leak-free" transcripts when the underlying mathematics is this new [5][18].
5. **Parameter fragility.** Isogeny schemes are brittle to parameter choice: CSIDH-512's quantum security was undermined by subexponential quantum hidden-shift algorithms [20], and SIDH's special primes enabled the attack's "easy endomorphism" step. Every new parameter set demands fresh cryptanalytic review.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The story of isogeny-based cryptography from 2011 to 2026 is a case study in how cryptographic hardness assumptions live and die. SIDH/SIKE offered unmatched compactness on the strength of the *SIDH problem* — path-finding fortified by torsion images. The torsion images, intended as a mere convenience for commutativity, turned out to encode the secret isogeny almost completely once Kani's lemma provided the right lens: glue the secret one-dimensional isogeny to an auxiliary one, lift to dimension two where the smooth-degree kernel is computable, split back down, and read off the key. The break was total (polynomial-time, practical, single-core) and conceptually clean, and its generalizations by Maino–Martindale and Robert showed the phenomenon was not an artifact of SIKE's special starting curve [3][5][6].

Yet the field adapted. The Deuring correspondence supports protocols that never expose torsion images, and SQISign is their flagship: the most compact post-quantum signature known, with a complete EUF-CMA proof (2025) and a NIST on-ramp round-2 submission [8][9][17]. Ironically, the attack's own dimension-2 machinery now accelerates the survivor's implementations [16][18][19].

Three open problems define the next decade: (i) pinning down the concrete hardness of the endomorphism ring problem against quantum and 2-D classical attacks; (ii) determining whether dimension-2 techniques conceal further cryptanalytic surprises; and (iii) closing SQISign's efficiency gap without weakening its reduction. Their trajectory has permanently enriched cryptography's understanding of what "hard" means.
