# PAC-Bayesian Generalization Theory for Deep Learning: Flat Minima, the Marginal Likelihood, Double Descent, and Non-Vacuous Bounds

## Abstract

Why do over-parameterized neural networks, capable of memorizing random labels, generalize when trained by stochastic gradient descent? This thesis develops a unified account grounded in Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) Bayesian learning theory, the first framework to deliver *numerically non-vacuous* generalization guarantees for deep networks with millions of parameters trained on tens of thousands of examples. We derive the classical PAC-Bayes bounds of McAllester, Catoni, and Seeger from the Donsker–Varadhan change of measure, and show how optimizing the bound directly over Gaussian posteriors — the Dziugaite–Roy program — operationalizes flatness as the KL-divergence cost between a data-independent prior and a data-fitted posterior. We connect flat minima to the marginal likelihood via the Laplace approximation's Occam factor, interpret double descent through the interpolation threshold and effective model complexity, and reproduce the empirical record: PAC-Bayes certificates on MNIST in the 16–22% range versus ~3% held-out estimates. Limitations, including vacuity at ImageNet scale, prior dependence, and the reparameterization critique, are treated honestly.

## 1 Introduction

The central embarrassment of modern statistical learning theory is also its most productive open problem. Classical uniform-convergence machinery — VC dimension, Rademacher complexity, covering numbers — yields generalization bounds for deep networks that are *vacuous by several orders of magnitude* [1]. A bound that certifies test error below 3.0 is not merely loose; it is logically empty, since the 0–1 loss is already bounded by 1. Yet networks with far more parameters than training points routinely generalize. The theory is not merely imprecise — it appears to be measuring the wrong quantity.

PAC-Bayesian theory offers a different contract. Rather than charging the complexity of the *entire hypothesis class*, it charges the complexity of the *learned posterior relative to a fixed prior*, quantified by the Kullback–Leibler divergence $\mathrm{KL}(Q \,\|\, P)$ [4]. The decisive turn, due to Dziugaite and Roy, was to treat the bound as an *optimization objective*: choose the posterior $Q$ to minimize the bound itself, and the flatness of the minimum found by SGD becomes the mechanism that keeps $\mathrm{KL}(Q \,\|\, P)$ small [1].

This thesis pursues four intertwined threads:

1. **The anatomy of PAC-Bayes bounds**: the square-root, linear (Catoni), and binary-KL (Seeger) forms, their derivations, and their tightness ordering.
2. **Flat minima and the marginal likelihood**: why the volume of a basin, not merely its depth, controls generalization, and how the Bayesian Occam factor formalizes this.
3. **Double descent**: the empirical phenomenon in which test risk decreases *past* the interpolation threshold, subsuming the classical U-curve [2][3].
4. **Non-vacuous computation**: what it takes, numerically, to certify a deep network — and where the program still fails.

Our stance is that PAC-Bayes is not the final theory of deep learning, but it is the only one that has produced *numbers* — checkable, numerical, non-vacuous numbers — on real networks. That alone makes it the load-bearing framework of this thesis.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The learning-theoretic setup

Let $\mathcal{Z} = \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$ be the data space with unknown distribution $\mathcal{D}$, and let $\ell : \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{Z} \to [0, 1]$ be a bounded loss over hypotheses $h \in \mathcal{H}$. From an i.i.d. sample $S_m = \{z_1, \dots, z_m\}$ we define the *population risk* $R_{\mathcal{D}}(h) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \mathcal{D}}[\ell(h, z)]$ and the *empirical risk* $\hat{R}_{S_m}(h) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \ell(h, z_i)$. The classical goal is a high-probability upper bound on $R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)$ in terms of $\hat{R}_{S_m}(h)$ plus a complexity penalty, holding uniformly or for the algorithm's output.

The PAC-Bayesian departure is to randomize: the learner outputs a *distribution* $Q$ over hypotheses (the posterior), and the guarantee concerns the *expected* risk $\mathbb{E}_{h \sim Q}[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)]$, with complexity charged as $\mathrm{KL}(Q \,\|\, P)$ against a *data-free prior* $P$ fixed before observing $S_m$ [4].

> **Theorem (McAllester, 1999 — the square-root bound).** For any prior $P$ fixed before seeing data, any $\delta \in (0,1]$, with probability at least $1 - \delta$ over $S_m$, for *all* posteriors $Q$:
>
> $$\mathbb{E}_{h \sim Q}[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)] \;\le\; \mathbb{E}_{h \sim Q}[\hat{R}_{S_m}(h)] \;+\; \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{KL}(Q \,\|\, P) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{m}}{\delta}}{2m}}.$$

The bound is *uniform over posteriors*: because it holds simultaneously for all $Q$, we may optimize the right-hand side over $Q$ after seeing data without invalidating the certificate [4].

### 2.2 The three canonical forms

PAC-Bayes bounds come in a family whose members trade tightness against computability. The table below summarizes the canonical trio.

| Bound | Form (upper bound on $\mathbb{E}_Q R_{\mathcal{D}}$) | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| McAllester (1999) | $\hat{R}_Q + \sqrt{(\mathrm{KL} + \log(2\sqrt{m}/\delta))/(2m)}$ | Simple, closed-form | Loose; square root punishes small KL weakly but large KL quadratically |
| Catoni (2007) | $\hat{R}_Q/(1-\lambda/2) + (\mathrm{KL} + \log(1/\delta))/(m\lambda(1-\lambda/2))$ | Tunable $\lambda$; minimizer is the Gibbs posterior | Hyperparameter $\lambda$ must be fixed a priori or union-bounded |
| Seeger / PAC-Bayes-$\mathrm{kl}$ (2002) | $\mathrm{kl}^{-1}\!\left(\hat{R}_Q, \frac{\mathrm{KL} + \log(2\sqrt{m}/\delta)}{m}\right)$ | Provably tightest of the three; sharp near realizability | Requires numerical inversion of binary $\mathrm{kl}$ |

Here $\mathrm{kl}(p \,\|\, q) = p\ln\frac{p}{q} + (1-p)\ln\frac{1-p}{1-q}$ is the Bernoulli KL, and $\mathrm{kl}^{-1}(p, c) = \sup\{q : \mathrm{kl}(p \,\|\, q) \le c\}$ is its inversion [4]. Seeger's form is strictly tighter than McAllester's — it *implies* it via Pinsker's inequality $\mathrm{kl}(p\,\|\,q) \ge 2(p-q)^2$ — and it was the engine of the first non-vacuous deep-network bounds [1].

> **Theorem (Seeger's binary-KL bound).** With probability at least $1 - \delta$, for all $Q$:
>
> $$\mathrm{kl}\!\left(\mathbb{E}_{h\sim Q}[\hat{R}_{S_m}(h)] \;\middle\|\; \mathbb{E}_{h\sim Q}[R_{\mathcal{D}}(h)]\right) \;\le\; \frac{\mathrm{KL}(Q \,\|\, P) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{m}}{\delta}}{m}.$$

Catoni's bound deserves special attention because its right-hand-side minimizer has a closed form: the *Gibbs posterior* $Q^*_\lambda \propto P \cdot \exp(-\lambda m \hat{R}_{S_m})$, which interpolates between the prior ($\lambda \to 0$) and empirical risk minimization ($\lambda \to \infty$) [4]. At temperature $\lambda = m$ with log-loss, this recovers Bayesian posterior inference — the formal bridge between PAC-Bayes and Bayesian deep learning.

### 2.3 Flat minima: the geometric intuition

Hochreiter and Schmidhuber argued, in the language of minimum description length (MDL), that a *flat* minimum — where the loss varies slowly over a large neighborhood of parameters — can be described with low precision, whereas a *sharp* minimum requires high precision, and hence flat minima generalize better [5]. Keskar et al. later demonstrated empirically that large-batch methods are attracted to sharp minimizers while small-batch methods find flatter ones with better test performance [5]. The PAC-Bayesian formalization is disarmingly direct: perturb the weights with Gaussian noise of variance $s^2$; if the minimum is flat, the perturbed network retains low training error, so the posterior $Q = \mathcal{N}(w, s^2 I)$ achieves small empirical risk *while* keeping $\mathrm{KL}(Q \,\|\, P)$ modest [1].

### 2.4 Double descent: the empirical curve

The classical bias–variance trade-off predicts a U-shaped test-risk curve as model capacity grows. Belkin, Hsu, Ma, and Mandal showed instead that risk follows a *double descent*: it descends, rises to a peak at the *interpolation threshold* (the smallest capacity achieving zero training error), and then descends again — often below the classical minimum — as capacity increases further [2]. Nakkiran et al. extended this to modern deep networks, introducing *effective model complexity* (EMC): the maximum number of samples on which a training procedure can achieve near-zero training error, which grows with architecture size, training time, and data [3]. Double descent is the phenomenon any complete generalization theory must now survive.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Deriving PAC-Bayes from change of measure

All PAC-Bayes bounds flow from a single master inequality. The *Donsker–Varadhan variational formula* states that for any measurable $\phi$ and distributions $Q \ll P$,

$$\mathbb{E}_{h \sim Q}[\phi(h)] \;\le\; \mathrm{KL}(Q \,\|\, P) + \ln \mathbb{E}_{h \sim P}[e^{\phi(h)}].$$

Choosing $\phi(h) = \lambda m\,(R_{\mathcal{D}}(h) - \hat{R}_{S_m}(h))$ and controlling the moment-generating term $\mathbb{E}_P[e^{\phi}]$ by Hoeffding's lemma (for bounded loss) yields McAllester's bound; choosing the Bernoulli MGF (Maurer) yields Seeger's; choosing a linear $\phi$ yields Catoni's [4]. The modern survey literature documents the full refinement ladder — variance-adaptive (Tolstikhin–Seldin), jointly quasi-convex (Thiemann), and small-sample (Foong–Bruinsma–Burt–Turner) forms — each tightening the MGF control [4].

Our methodology in this thesis is therefore *constructive and computational*:

1. **Derive** the bound family from Donsker–Varadhan, making the tightness ordering (McAllester < Catoni < Seeger) explicit.
2. **Operationalize** the bound as a training objective over Gaussian posteriors $Q_{w,s^2} = \mathcal{N}(w, s^2 I_d)$, following Dziugaite and Roy [1].
3. **Evaluate** the tightest (inverted-$\mathrm{kl}$) form numerically, with the $\mathrm{kl}$-inversion computed by bisection.
4. **Connect** the KL term to flatness (Hessian spectra) and to the Bayesian marginal likelihood (Laplace–Occam), and test the resulting picture against double descent [2][3][6].

### 3.2 The Dziugaite–Roy pipeline

The breakthrough of [1] was procedural. Given an SGD-trained weight vector $w_{\mathrm{SGD}}$ on MNIST:

- **Prior:** $P = \mathcal{N}(w_0, \sigma_P^2 I)$, centered at the *random initialization* $w_0$ (data-free, hence valid), with tuned $\sigma_P$.
- **Posterior:** $Q = \mathcal{N}(w, \sigma_Q^2 I)$ with $w$ initialized at $w_{\mathrm{SGD}}$.
- **Optimization:** minimize Seeger's bound (via a differentiable upper bound on $\mathrm{kl}^{-1}$) over $(w, \sigma_Q^2)$ by gradient descent. The posterior mean drifts from $w_{\mathrm{SGD}}$ to a nearby flatter point while $\sigma_Q^2$ grows to the largest noise the loss tolerates.
- **Evaluation:** compute the final certificate with the *exact* inverted-$\mathrm{kl}$ bound on fresh Monte-Carlo draws, so the reported number is a rigorous upper bound, not an optimization artifact.

The reported certificates — test-error upper bounds of 16–22% against ~3% true test error on binary MNIST — were the first non-vacuous numbers for networks with millions of parameters [1]. The recipe has since been tightened (better $\mathrm{kl}^{-1}$ upper bounds, data-dependent priors via differential privacy [7], improved risk-certificate evaluation), but the skeleton endures.

### 3.3 A reference implementation of bound evaluation

The following Python code evaluates Seeger's inverted-$\mathrm{kl}$ certificate from Monte-Carlo estimates — the exact computation at the heart of [1]:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import xlogy

def binary_kl(p: float, q: float) -> float:
    q = min(max(q, 1e-12), 1 - 1e-12)
    return xlogy(p, p / q) + xlogy(1 - p, (1 - p) / (1 - q))

def kl_inverse(p: float, c: float, tol: float = 1e-9) -> float:
    """sup{q : kl(p || q) <= c} via bisection; q >= p."""
    lo, hi = p, 1.0
    while hi - lo > tol:
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if binary_kl(p, mid) <= c:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    return lo

def seeger_certificate(emp_risk_q: float, kl_qp: float,
                       m: int, delta: float = 0.05) -> float:
    """PAC-Bayes-kl upper bound on E_Q[R_D]."""
    c = (kl_qp + np.log(2 * np.sqrt(m) / delta)) / m
    return kl_inverse(emp_risk_q, c)

# Dziugaite-Roy regime: binary MNIST, m = 55000
print(seeger_certificate(emp_risk_q=0.03, kl_qp=4000.0, m=55000))
```

The Gibbs posterior that minimizes Catoni's bound admits a clean functional rendering:

```haskell
-- Gibbs posterior: Q*_λ ∝ P · exp(-λ · empiricalRisk)
gibbsPosterior :: Double -> (Hypothesis -> Double)
               -> (Hypothesis -> Double) -> Hypothesis -> Double
gibbsPosterior lambda priorDensity empiricalRisk h =
    let unnormalized = priorDensity h * exp (-lambda * empiricalRisk h)
        -- normalizer Z(λ) = E_P[exp(-λ R̂)] computed by quadrature/MCMC
        z = normalizingConstant lambda priorDensity empiricalRisk
    in unnormalized / z
```

And the flatness probe at the core of the method — Hessian spectra via matrix-free products — is naturally expressed in Rust:

```rust
/// One Hessian-vector product via the Pearlmutter trick: Hv = ∇(∇L · v).
fn hessian_vector_product(
    grad_fn: &dyn Fn(&[f64]) -> Vec<f64>,
    w: &[f64],
    v: &[f64],
    eps: f64,
) -> Vec<f64> {
    let g = grad_fn(w);
    let dot: f64 = g.iter().zip(v.iter()).map(|(a, b)| a * b).sum();
    // finite-difference directional derivative of the gradient
    let w_fwd: Vec<f64> = w.iter().zip(v.iter()).map(|(x, d)| x + eps * d).collect();
    let w_bwd: Vec<f64> = w.iter().zip(v.iter()).map(|(x, d)| x - eps * d).collect();
    let g_fwd = grad_fn(&w_fwd);
    let g_bwd = grad_fn(&w_bwd);
    g_fwd.iter().zip(g_bwd.iter())
        .map(|(a, b)| (a - b) / (2.0 * eps))
        .collect()
}
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Anatomy of the bound: three terms, three stories

Every PAC-Bayes bound decomposes into the same three ingredients, and each tells a different story about learning:

- **The empirical term** $\mathbb{E}_{h\sim Q}[\hat{R}_{S_m}(h)]$: the *stochastic* training error. Note the subtlety — the bound certifies the *randomized* predictor, not the deterministic weights. The gap between stochastic and deterministic error is itself a flatness measure: at a flat minimum, Gaussian noise barely moves the loss [1].
- **The complexity term** $\mathrm{KL}(Q \,\|\, P)/m$: the price, in nats per sample, of moving the posterior away from the prior. For $Q = \mathcal{N}(w, s^2 I)$ and $P = \mathcal{N}(w_0, \sigma_P^2 I)$, this is available in closed form and grows with $\|w - w_0\|^2$ and with the precision ratio — it penalizes both *distance traveled* during training and *sharpness* (small $s^2$) [1].
- **The confidence term** $\ln(2\sqrt{m}/\delta)/m$: the union-bound price of uniformity over all posteriors. It vanishes as $m \to \infty$ and is the reason optimizing the bound post-hoc is legal [4].
## 5 Empirical Analysis and Formal Guarantees

### 5.1 A worked numerical example

To make the certificate concrete, consider the Dziugaite–Roy operating point with plausible optimized values: stochastic empirical risk $\hat{R}_Q = 0.03$, $\mathrm{KL}(Q \,\|\, P) = 4{,}000$ nats, $m = 55{,}000$, $\delta = 0.05$. The three bounds give:

| Bound | Complexity input | Certificate |
|---|---|---|
| McAllester (sqrt) | $(4000 + \ln(2\sqrt{55000}/0.05)) / (2 \cdot 55000)$ | $0.03 + 0.201 \approx 0.231$ |
| Catoni ($\lambda = 1$) | tuned linear trade-off | $\approx 0.03/0.5 + 4009.2/27500 \approx 0.206$ |
| Seeger ($\mathrm{kl}^{-1}$) | $\mathrm{kl}(0.03 \,\|\, \cdot) \le 4009.2/55000$ | $\approx 0.183$ |

The ordering McAllester > Catoni > Seeger is visible numerically: Seeger's inversion saves roughly five points of error over the square-root form at the same KL [4]. All three are non-vacuous ($< 1$) and all three sit an order of magnitude above the true ~3% test error — the characteristic PAC-Bayes signature: *valid but loose* [1].

### 5.2 Formal guarantees: the tightness ladder

> **Proposition (Tightness ordering).** For fixed $\hat{R}_Q$, $\mathrm{KL}$, $m$, $\delta$: Seeger's $\mathrm{kl}^{-1}$ bound is everywhere $\le$ McAllester's square-root bound (via Pinsker), and there exist regimes where Catoni's bound with optimal $\lambda$ beats McAllester while Seeger beats both. No bound in the trio is uniformly improvable without additional assumptions [4].

> **Theorem (PAC-Bayes with data-dependent priors, Dziugaite–Roy 2018).** If the prior $P(S)$ is chosen by an $\epsilon$-differentially private mechanism, then with probability at least $1 - \delta$, for all $Q$:
>
> $$\mathrm{kl}(\mathbb{E}_Q[\hat{R}_S] \,\|\, \mathbb{E}_Q[R_{\mathcal{D}}]) \;\le\; \frac{\mathrm{KL}(Q \,\|\, P(S)) + \ln\frac{2\sqrt{m}}{\delta}}{m} + O\!\left(\frac{\epsilon}{m}\right).$$
>
> In particular, priors centered at SGLD iterates remain valid, unlocking substantially tighter certificates [7].

> **Theorem (Flat minima via log-Sobolev, Haddouche et al. 2025).** Under a log-Sobolev inequality for the posterior with constant $c_{LS}$, the generalization gap is controlled by the expected squared gradient norm — a first-order flatness measure — plus $\mathrm{KL}(Q \,\|\, P)/m$, giving a rigorous PAC-Bayesian formalization of "flat minima generalize" without second-order assumptions [6].

### 5.3 Empirical protocol checklist

Any claim of a non-vacuous bound must survive the following audit, distilled from [1][7][8]:

1. **Prior data-freeness**: the prior must be fixed before observing the bound's data — initialization weights, or a differentially private / held-out mechanism [7].
2. **Optimize, then evaluate exactly**: report the bound both at the raw SGD solution and after bound-optimization, with the final number from exact $\mathrm{kl}^{-1}$ bisection [1].
3. **Monte-Carlo rigor**: fold the stochastic empirical risk's own estimation error into the certificate, and report the deterministic network's test error alongside so the flatness is visible [1].

### 5.4 What double descent demands of the theory

The double-descent record — random features, trees, two-layer nets, ResNets, transformers — is too broad to dismiss [2][3]. A satisfactory theory must explain three facts: the U-curve below interpolation, the peak at the threshold, and the second descent. PAC-Bayes addresses all three — via $\hat{R}_Q$, the KL peak, and implicit bias keeping $\mathrm{KL}(Q \,\|\, P)$ small — with *computable numbers* attached [2][3][4].

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating where the program stalls.

**Scale.** The bounds degrade catastrophically with task difficulty. ImageNet-scale certificates remain vacuous; even on CIFAR-10 they are borderline. The KL term appears to grow superlinearly in problem complexity while $m$ grows only linearly in data-collection budget [1][8].

**The stochasticity gap.** PAC-Bayes certifies a *randomized* predictor. Practitioners deploy deterministic networks. The derandomization step — arguing the deterministic net inherits the stochastic guarantee — relies on flatness itself and is not part of the certificate [1].

**Prior dependence.** The tightest bounds lean on data-dependent priors, whose validity rests on differential-privacy or sample-splitting arguments that are technically delicate and empirically fiddly [7]. A bound is only as trustworthy as its prior's data-freeness proof.

**The reparameterization critique.** Hessian-based flatness is not parameterization-invariant: one can rescale layers to make any minimum arbitrarily "flat" without changing the function [5]. PAC-Bayes answers that the *certificate* is invariant — but the *optimization* that finds a good posterior is still guided by the non-invariant geometry, so the critique bites at the level of the algorithm, if not the theorem [6].

**The cold-posterior problem.** Bayesian deep learning routinely finds that tempered posteriors ($T < 1$) outperform the true Bayes posterior — a fact PAC-Bayes accommodates (Catoni's $\lambda$ is a temperature) but does not explain [4].

**What PAC-Bayes cannot do.** It cannot explain *why* SGD finds flat minima (an optimization-dynamics question), and its bounds say nothing about out-of-distribution generalization. It is a theory of the *solution found*, not of the *algorithm that found it* — though algorithm-dependent priors are beginning to close that gap [7][8].

## 7 Conclusion

PAC-Bayesian theory occupies a unique position in the science of deep learning: it is the only generalization framework that has produced *numerically non-vacuous, checkable certificates* for real, over-parameterized networks [1]. Its three-term anatomy — empirical risk, KL complexity, confidence — turns the folk geometry of flat minima into an optimization objective and a number; its Gibbs-posterior form forges the formal link to Bayesian inference and the marginal likelihood's Occam factor [4][6]; and its KL term, read correctly, traces the shape of the double-descent curve, peaking at the interpolation threshold and relaxing in the over-parameterized regime where implicit bias reigns [2][3].

The program is incomplete — vacuity at ImageNet scale, the stochasticity gap, prior fragility, and the reparameterization critique are real and unsolved [5][7][8]. But the standard for a theory of generalization should be *contact with reality*, and PAC-Bayes is the only framework currently making numerical contact. The path forward: richer posterior families with tractable KL, tighter data-dependent priors, and bounds that charge the algorithm rather than just the solution. Until then, the flat minimum — wide, noise-tolerant, cheap to describe — remains our best-certified explanation of why deep learning works.
