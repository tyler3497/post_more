# Homomorphic Encryption: LWE Foundations, the CKKS Approximate-Arithmetic Scheme, and Bootstrapping

## Abstract

Homomorphic encryption allows computation directly on encrypted data, and the modern theory of the subject rests on a single hardness assumption: Learning With Errors (LWE). This thesis develops the full arc from Regev's worst-case-to-average-case reduction for LWE [2], through the ring-algebraic efficiency of Ring-LWE [3] and Gentry's bootstrapping blueprint for fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) [1], to the two great practical lineages that followed: leveled schemes that avoid bootstrapping via modulus switching [4][5], and the CKKS scheme for approximate arithmetic over the complex numbers [6] with its sine-based approximate bootstrapping [7]. We formalize the CKKS encoding through the canonical embedding, prove its depth-bounded precision theorem, and analyze the bootstrapping race from HElib's six-minute refresh through FHEW's sub-second blind rotation [8] to TFHE's sub-0.1-second external product [9], benchmarking the trade-offs surveyed in [10]. We close with the field's open wounds: circular security, CKKS IND-CPA^D leakage, and the bootstrapping tax.

##1 Introduction

In 1978, Rivest, Adleman, and Dertouzos asked whether one could compute on encrypted data without ever decrypting it — the dream of *privacy homomorphisms*. For thirty years the answer was fragmentary: RSA and ElGamal were multiplicatively homomorphic, Paillier additively homomorphic, but no scheme supported *both* operations arbitrarily. The breakthrough arrived in 2009, when Craig Gentry's Stanford Ph.D. thesis presented the first *fully* homomorphic encryption (FHE) scheme, based on ideal lattices [1]. Gentry's construction proved that arbitrary computation on encrypted data is *possible in principle*.

Yet Gentry's scheme was galactic in its inefficiency — thirty minutes per bit operation — and rested on non-standard assumptions, including the *sparse subset sum* problem of his "squashing" step. The subsequent decade and a half can be read as a systematic campaign to replace every exotic ingredient of Gentry's blueprint with something standard, efficient, and well-understood:

- **Regev's Learning With Errors (LWE)** [2] became the universal foundation, with a worst-case-to-average-case reduction from lattice problems believed hard even for quantum computers.
- **Brakerski, Gentry, and Vaikuntanathan (BGV)** [4] showed that *modulus switching* could manage noise growth well enough to evaluate deep circuits *without* bootstrapping at all — a leveled FHE from standard assumptions.
- **Brakerski and Vaikuntanathan** [5] pushed further, basing FHE on plain LWE with no ideal-lattice structure.
- **Cheon, Kim, Kim, and Song (CKKS)** [6] broke with the exact-arithmetic orthodoxy entirely: by treating encryption noise as *approximation error*, they built the first practical FHE scheme for real and complex numbers, the natural domain of machine learning.
- **Bootstrapping itself** was reinvented twice over: first by Ducas and Micciancio's FHEW, which refreshed a ciphertext in under a second via the *blind rotation* [8], then by Chillotti et al.'s TFHE, which cut that to under 0.1 seconds with the *external product* [9].

This thesis traces that arc in full technical detail. Section 2 establishes the lattice and LWE background. Section 3 describes our methodology: how we formalize, compare, and benchmark the schemes. Section 4 is the deep dive — LWE and Regev's reduction, modulus switching and leveled FHE, the CKKS encoding and rescaling machinery, and bootstrapping from Gentry to TFHE. Section 5 gives formal theorems with proof sketches and quantitative benchmarks. Section 6 confronts the limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

##2 Background

### Lattices and hard problems

A *lattice* Λ ⊂ ℝⁿ is a discrete additive subgroup: all integer linear combinations of *n* linearly independent basis vectors. Two problems dominate the cryptographic landscape:

- **GapSVP_γ**: given a lattice, decide whether its shortest nonzero vector has length ≤ 1 or > γ.
- **SIVP_γ**: find *n* linearly independent lattice vectors of length at most γ times the optimum.

Both are conjectured hard to approximate within polynomial factors, even for quantum algorithms — the bedrock belief underlying all lattice cryptography and the NIST post-quantum standards.

### The LWE problem

Regev [2] introduced Learning With Errors as a generalization of learning parity with noise. Fix a dimension *n*, a modulus *q* = poly(*n*), and an error distribution χ over ℤ_q concentrated near zero (typically a discrete Gaussian). For a secret **s** ∈ ℤ_qⁿ, the LWE distribution A_{**s**,χ} outputs samples

> **Definition (LWE):** pairs (**a**_i, b_i) ∈ ℤ_qⁿ × ℤ_q with **a**_i uniform and b_i = ⟨**a**_i, **s**⟩ + e_i (mod q), e_i ← χ.

The *search* problem asks to recover **s**; the *decision* problem asks to distinguish A_{**s**,χ} from uniform. Regev's landmark theorem gives a *quantum* reduction from worst-case GapSVP~/SIVP to decision-LWE [2]; Peikert later gave a classical reduction under more restrictive parameters. The consequence is profound: breaking LWE on *average* implies solving *every* instance of a worst-case lattice problem.

### Ring-LWE and ideal lattices

Plain LWE has a quadratic overhead — keys and ciphertexts scale as *n*² — which motivated Lyubashevsky, Peikert, and Regev to introduce *Ring-LWE* [3]. Working in the cyclotomic ring R = ℤ[X]/(X^N + 1) with N a power of two, samples become (a, a·s + e) with a, s, e ∈ R_q. Security reduces to worst-case problems on *ideal lattices* [3]. The payoff is twofold: quasi-linear key sizes and a native *SIMD* structure — via the Chinese Remainder Theorem, one ring element encrypts a whole vector of plaintext slots, and one homomorphic operation acts on all slots at once.

### Homomorphic encryption syntax and noise

An FHE scheme provides `KeyGen`, `Enc`, `Dec`, `Eval` such that `Dec(Eval(f, Enc(m))) = f(m)` for any circuit *f*. Every lattice-based ciphertext carries *noise* — the error term *e* — which grows with each homomorphic operation. Addition grows noise additively; multiplication grows it far faster. When noise exceeds q/2, decryption fails. Managing this growth is the entire engineering problem of FHE, and the three great techniques are **modulus switching** [4], **rescaling** [6], and **bootstrapping** [1].

##3 Methodology

This thesis proceeds by *reduction and comparison*: we formalize each scheme as an instantiation of the same RLWE template, isolate the noise-management technique that defines it, and prove or sketch the theorem that justifies it. Our method has four components:

1. **Foundational formalization.** We state Regev's LWE definition and reduction theorem precisely [2], then lift to Ring-LWE [3], since every scheme we analyze is an RLWE scheme in disguise.
2. **Noise-growth accounting.** For BGV [4], CKKS [6], and FHEW/TFHE [8][9], we derive noise growth and show how modulus switching, rescaling, and blind rotation bound it.
3. **CKKS precision analysis.** Because CKKS is *approximate*, correctness is a statement about *precision*, not exactness. We prove the depth-bounded precision-loss theorem of [6]: evaluating a depth-*d* circuit loses only O(d) bits of precision.
4. **Empirical benchmarking.** We tabulate published bootstrapping timings from [7][8][9] and the [improved CKKS bootstrapping](https://eprint.iacr.org/2018/1043), interpreting them through the survey taxonomy of [10].

All theorems are stated in standard cryptographic language; proofs are sketched with the key reduction or noise calculation made explicit. Code fragments illustrate the core algorithms at toy scale.

##4 Deep Dive

###4.1 The LWE Problem and Regev's Worst-Case Reduction

The genius of LWE is that it looks trivially easy and is conjecturally very hard. Given enough samples (**a**_i, ⟨**a**_i, **s**⟩ + e_i), the secret **s** is *information-theoretically* determined — yet no efficient algorithm recovers it, because each equation is perturbed by unknown noise. The following toy implementation (over a small modulus, for illustration only) shows the mechanics:

```python
import random

def lwe_sample(n, q, s, sigma=2):
    """One LWE sample (a, <a,s> + e mod q)."""
    a = [random.randrange(q) for _ in range(n)]
    e = int(random.gauss(0, sigma))          # small error
    b = (sum(x*y for x, y in zip(a, s)) + e) % q
    return a, b

def lwe_encrypt_bit(pk, mu, n, q):
    """Regev-style encryption of a bit mu in {0,1}."""
    A, b = pk
    S = random.sample(range(len(A)), k=n)    # random subset
    a_sum = [sum(A[i][j] for i in S) % q for j in range(n)]
    b_sum = (sum(b[i] for i in S) + mu * (q // 2)) % q
    return a_sum, b_sum
```

Regev's public-key scheme encrypts a bit μ by masking a random subset-sum of samples with μ·⌊q/2⌋; security reduces to decision-LWE.

> **Theorem (Regev [2]):** For appropriate parameters, there is a quantum polynomial-time reduction from worst-case GapSVP~_{n/α} and SIVP~_{n/α} to decision-LWE_{n,q,χ}. In particular, decision-LWE is at least as hard as approximating short lattice vectors in the worst case.

The reduction is iterative: an LWE oracle yields ever-narrower discrete Gaussians on the lattice, eventually revealing short vectors. This worst-case-to-average-case connection is why LWE became the foundation of modern FHE.

###4.2 Modulus Switching and Leveled FHE Without Bootstrapping

Gentry's original bootstrapping was conceptually brilliant but practically devastating: homomorphically evaluating the decryption circuit is enormously expensive, and his "squashing" step introduced the non-standard sparse-subset-sum assumption. Brakerski, Gentry, and Vaikuntanathan [4] asked a heretical question: *what if we never bootstrap at all?*

Their answer was **modulus switching**. After a homomorphic multiplication at modulus *q*, the noise *e* is large relative to *q*. But if we *scale the entire ciphertext down* to a smaller modulus q' < q — mapping c ↦ ⌊(q'/q)·c⌉ — the noise scales down proportionally while the message, which lives in the *high-order bits*, is preserved. Repeating this after every multiplication level keeps noise essentially *constant* across levels, at the cost of consuming one modulus level per multiplication. A chain of moduli q_0 < q_1 < … < q_L supports depth-*L* circuits: a *leveled* FHE scheme.

The BGV scheme [4] combines modulus switching with **key switching** (relinearization): multiplication of ciphertexts (c_0 + c_1·s) ⊗ (d_0 + d_1·s) produces a degree-2 term in *s*², which key switching collapses back to linear form using an encrypted hint of *s*². Together with Ring-LWE packing, BGV evaluates SIMD arithmetic circuits with only polylogarithmic overhead [Gentry–Halevi–Smart](https://eprint.iacr.org/2011/566).

Brakerski and Vaikuntanathan [5] then showed the same blueprint works over *plain* LWE, proving FHE from essentially the minimal lattice assumption.

| Scheme | Assumption | Noise control | Bootstrapping |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gentry '09 [1] | Ideal lattices + SSSP | Bootstrapping (squashed circuit) | Required |
| BV '11 [5] | Standard LWE | Modulus switching | Optional |
| BGV '12 [4] | Ring-LWE | Modulus switching + key switching | Optional |
| CKKS '17 [6] | Ring-LWE | Rescaling (approximate) | Optional |

###4.3 CKKS: Approximate Arithmetic over the Canonical Embedding

All the schemes above are *exact*: decryption must recover the plaintext bit-perfectly. Cheon, Kim, Kim, and Song [6] observed that real-world data — sensor readings, financial figures, neural-network weights — is *already approximate*, and that insisting on exact decryption wastes enormous computational budget. Their CKKS scheme (also called HEAAN) encrypts *vectors of complex numbers* and guarantees only that decryption returns an *approximation* of the true result, with rigorously bounded error.

The mathematical engine is the **canonical embedding**. For R = ℤ[X]/(X^N+1), the embedding σ: R → ℂ^{N/2} evaluates a polynomial at the primitive 2N-th roots of unity. CKKS *encodes* a message vector **z** ∈ ℂ^{N/2} as

> m(X) = ⌊ Δ · σ^{-1}(**z**) ⌉ ∈ R,

scaling by Δ = 2^p to reserve *p* bits of fixed-point precision, then encrypts m(X) under RLWE. Homomorphic addition and multiplication act *slot-wise* on the encoded vectors — true SIMD over ℂ^{N/2}.

The masterstroke is **rescaling**, the approximate analogue of modulus switching. After multiplying ciphertexts at scale Δ², CKKS divides by Δ (rounding), dropping the scale back to Δ and — crucially — discarding the *least significant bits*, which contain mostly noise. Noise is thus managed *exactly like floating-point rounding error*. The precision theorem of [6] states:

> **Theorem (CKKS precision [6]):** Evaluating a depth-*d* arithmetic circuit under CKKS loses at most O(d) bits of precision relative to unencrypted fixed-point arithmetic — exceeding the inherent rounding error of plaintext floating-point computation by at most one bit.

In Haskell-flavored pseudocode, the encode/decode round-trip is:

```haskell
-- Canonical embedding sigma: R -> C^(N/2); Encode scales by Delta
encode :: Double -> Vector (Complex Double) -> Poly
encode delta z = roundPoly (scalePoly delta (sigmaInverse z))

decode :: Double -> Poly -> Vector (Complex Double)
decode delta m = sigma (scalePoly (1/delta) (toDoublePoly m))

-- Approximate correctness: decode (encode z) ~= z, error < 2^{-p}
prop_roundtrip :: Vector (Complex Double) -> Bool
prop_roundtrip z = maxNorm (decode delta (encode delta z) - z) < 2**(-p)
```

Because the error is relative and bounded, CKKS suits workloads — logistic regression, neural-network inference, genomic statistics — where a few bits of error are irrelevant.

###4.4 Bootstrapping: From Gentry's Blueprint to FHEW and TFHE

Bootstrapping remains the crown jewel of FHE theory. Gentry's idea [1]: to refresh a noisy ciphertext c = Enc(m), *homomorphically evaluate the decryption circuit* on (an encryption of) the secret key:

> c' = Eval(Dec, Enc(**s**), c) = Enc(Dec_**s**(c)) = Enc(m),

with *fresh* noise independent of the accumulated noise in c. A scheme is *bootstrappable* if it can evaluate its own decryption circuit (plus one NAND gate) — then circular security yields unlimited computation.

For CKKS, bootstrapping is subtler: decryption involves a *modular reduction* [·]_q, which is not a polynomial. Cheon et al. [7] approximate it with a scaled *sine function* — exploiting that e^{2πix} periodicity mimics modular wrap-around — evaluated via Taylor/Chebyshev approximations, bracketed by CoeffsToSlots/SlotsToCoeffs linear transforms (homomorphic DFTs). The 2018 implementation refreshed 128 slots in 139.8 s (~1.1 s amortized per slot) [7]; Chen–Chillotti–Song's improved bootstrapping [Eurocrypt 2019](https://eprint.iacr.org/2018/1043) cut this by two orders of magnitude to ~0.01 s/slot using level-collapsing DFT tricks and Chebyshev approximation.

Meanwhile, the *exact* line took a different path. Ducas and Micciancio's FHEW [8] replaced Gentry-style recryption with the **blind rotation**: an LWE ciphertext is lifted into a homomorphic *accumulator* (a ring-GSW ciphertext), and a sequence of controlled rotations by the encrypted secret-key bits homomorphically evaluates the decryption rounding function. Bootstrapping dropped from HElib's ~6 minutes per batch to about *half a second* on a PC [8]. Chillotti et al.'s TFHE [9] then reformulated the whole procedure around the **external product** — a cheap product between a GSW and an LWE ciphertext — reaching *under 0.1 seconds* and shrinking the bootstrapping key from 1 GB to 24 MB [9].


The modern taxonomy [10] is therefore threefold: **BGV/BFV** maximize throughput for exact integer SIMD; **DM/CGGI (FHEW/TFHE)** minimize latency and enable *programmable bootstrapping* — evaluating arbitrary lookup tables during the refresh; **CKKS** maximizes throughput for approximate real-number arithmetic, the workhorse of privacy-preserving machine learning.

##5 Empirical Evidence and Formal Proofs

We now state the load-bearing theorems with proof sketches, then benchmark.

> **Theorem 1 (FHE from bootstrappability — Gentry [1]):** If a somewhat homomorphic scheme can homomorphically evaluate its own (squashed) decryption circuit plus one additional NAND gate, then under weak circular security it yields a fully homomorphic encryption scheme.

*Proof sketch.* Given a noisy ciphertext c at the depth limit, publish an encryption of the secret key and homomorphically run Dec_**s**(c). The output encrypts the same message with fixed, small noise. Repeating this *recryption* after each layer of the target circuit evaluates circuits of arbitrary depth. ∎

> **Theorem 2 (Leveled FHE without bootstrapping — BGV [4]):** Under Ring-LWE, there exists a leveled FHE scheme whose per-gate computation is polynomial in the security parameter and the depth bound, with noise growth managed entirely by modulus switching and key switching.

*Proof sketch.* Modulus switching maps (c, q) ↦ (⌊(q'/q)c⌉, q'); the noise e ↦ (q'/q)e + rounding, so the noise-to-modulus ratio is preserved up to an additive rounding term. Choosing the chain q_0,…,q_L with q_{i+1}/q_i bounding one multiplication's growth keeps noise below q_0/2 throughout a depth-*L* circuit. Key switching relinearizes s²-terms with noise linear in the decomposition base. ∎

> **Theorem 3 (CKKS approximate correctness [6]):** Decryption after a depth-*d* homomorphic computation returns the true result perturbed by error 2^{-p+O(d)}, i.e., precision degrades only linearly in depth.

*Proof sketch.* Encoding rounds to the nearest multiple of Δ^{-1}, contributing ≤ 2^{-p-1} per slot. Each rescaling after multiplication rounds to modulus q', adding relative error 2^{-p}; additions are exact in the fixed-point representation. By induction over the circuit, total relative error is O(d·2^{-p}). ∎

**Bootstrapping latency across eras** (published figures, single-threaded CPU unless noted):

| Era / Scheme | Bootstrapping cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Gentry '09 [1] | ~30 min / bit op | Ideal lattices; proof of possibility |
| HElib (BGV) [8] | ~6 min / batch | Halevi–Shoup implementation |
| FHEW [8] | ~0.5 s | Blind rotation; worst-case lattice hardness |
| TFHE [9] | < 0.1 s | External product; 24 MB key (was 1 GB) |
| CKKS '18 [7] | 139.8 s / 128 slots (~1.1 s amortized) | Sine-approximated modular reduction |
| CKKS improved [CCS19](https://eprint.iacr.org/2018/1043) | ~0.01 s / slot | Chebyshev + level-collapsed DFT |

The trajectory is unmistakable: five orders of magnitude in a decade, split between latency (TFHE) and throughput (CKKS) — the trade-off map of [10].

##6 Limitations

For all its triumphs, the LWE-to-FHE edifice has cracks that honest exposition must name.

**Circular security remains unproven.** Every bootstrapping construction encrypts its own secret key (or key-switching hints derived from it) and assumes the scheme stays secure — *circular* or *KDM* security. No reduction from LWE to circular security is known; it is an assumption, not a theorem [1][4].

**CKKS leaks under decryption oracles.** The approximate-decryption paradigm has a sharp edge: because the "noise" encodes the message's low-order bits, an adversary with access to a decryption oracle (the IND-CPA^D model) can extract the secret key from the *error itself*. CKKS must therefore never be deployed where attackers observe decryptions of adversarially chosen ciphertexts.

**The bootstrapping tax persists.** Even at 0.1 s (TFHE) or 0.01 s/slot (CKKS), bootstrapping dominates runtime: privacy-preserving neural-network inference still runs 10⁴–10⁶× slower than plaintext. Programmable bootstrapping [9] helps by fusing function evaluation into the refresh, but arbitrary deep programs remain impractical.

**Parameters are fragile.** Security estimates for (R)LWE rest on lattice-attack cost models (BKZ with sieving) that are periodically revised; several "128-bit" parameter sets have been retrospectively downgraded. Side channels — particularly the discrete Gaussian sampler — have yielded practical key-recovery attacks against naive implementations.

**Expressiveness gaps.** BGV/BFV handle exact integers but not comparisons or branching without expensive digit extraction; CKKS handles smooth arithmetic beautifully but non-polynomial functions (ReLU, max, sign) require costly polynomial approximation per evaluation [7]. No single scheme dominates all workloads — the field is, and will remain, a portfolio.

---

##7 Conclusion

From Regev's noisy linear equations [2] to Gentry's audacious recryption [1], from the algebraic efficiency of Ring-LWE [3] through the bootstrapping-free leveled world of BGV [4][5], to CKKS's heretical embrace of approximation [6] and the sub-second bootstrapping revolutions of FHEW [8] and TFHE [9], homomorphic encryption has traveled from impossibility folklore to deployable — if still expensive — technology. The unifying lesson is that *noise is the central object of study*: LWE makes noise the source of security, modulus switching and rescaling make it manageable, and bootstrapping makes it renewable.

The open frontiers are clear: prove (or circumvent) circular security, close the IND-CPA^D gap for approximate schemes, push bootstrapping latency down another order of magnitude, and harden parameter selection against evolving lattice attacks. Until then, FHE remains what it has always been — the most powerful idea in cryptography that we cannot quite afford, getting cheaper every year.
