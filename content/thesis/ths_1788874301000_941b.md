# Distributed Graph Algorithms in the CONGEST Model: Spanners, Ruling Sets, Network Decompositions, and Lower Bounds via Communication Complexity

## Abstract

The CONGEST model captures the fundamental tension between locality and bandwidth in distributed graph computation: *n* processors connected by an arbitrary network must solve graph problems while each edge carries only *O*(log *n*) bits per synchronous round. This thesis surveys the state of the art in distributed graph algorithms in CONGEST along four classical axes. We present the randomized spanner constructions of Baswana and Sen, achieving (2*k*−1)-stretch with *O*(*k*·*n*^{1+1/*k*}) edges in *O*(*k*) rounds, and discuss their optimality under the Erdős girth conjecture. We analyze ruling sets, from the seminal *O*(log *n*)-round deterministic construction of Awerbuch, Goldberg, Luby, and Plotkin to the recent near-optimal *O*(log log *n*) ruling sets. We trace network decompositions from their 1989 introduction through Panconesi–Srinivasan to the polylogarithmic-time breakthrough of Rozhoň and Ghaffari and the strong-diameter CONGEST constructions of Chang–Ghaffari. Finally, we develop the two-party communication-complexity framework of Das Sarma et al., proving the Ω̃(√*n* + *D*) lower bounds that circumscribe every algorithm above.

## 1 Introduction

Distributed graph algorithms study what a network of processors can compute about the very graph that connects them. In the **CONGEST model** — the standard synchronous message-passing model — computation proceeds in discrete rounds: each node receives the previous round's messages, performs arbitrary local computation, and sends a message of at most *B* = *O*(log *n*) bits to each neighbor [1]. Nodes have unique identifiers, and complexity is measured in rounds.

The bandwidth restriction is what makes CONGEST both realistic and mathematically rich. Without it, we obtain the **LOCAL model**, in which a *T*-round algorithm is exactly a function of the *T*-hop neighborhood of each node. With it, even collecting the *T*-hop neighborhood may be infeasible: a node of degree Δ cannot learn its full neighborhood in fewer than Ω(Δ / log *n*) rounds in the worst case. This gap between *seeing far* (locality) and *seeing wide* (bandwidth) drives the entire field [6].

This thesis is organized around four pillars that together span much of the modern theory of CONGEST:

1. **Spanners** — sparse subgraphs that approximately preserve distances, the central tool for distance computation and routing-table construction.
2. **Ruling sets** — a distance-relaxed generalization of maximal independent sets, the canonical symmetry-breaking primitive.
3. **Network decompositions** — partitions of the graph into low-diameter clusters with a proper coloring of the cluster graph, the master tool behind nearly all deterministic algorithms.
4. **Lower bounds via communication complexity** — the reduction framework that proves unconditional round lower bounds by simulating distributed algorithms as two-party protocols.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The model

Let *G* = (*V*, *E*) be an *n*-node undirected graph with diameter *D* and maximum degree Δ. In CONGEST, each node *v* ∈ *V* initially knows its own identifier and, in the *KT*₁ variant, the identifiers of its neighbors [6]. Computation is synchronous: round *t* consists of message receipt, unbounded local computation, and message sending, with *O*(log *n*) bits per edge per round. An algorithm *succeeds with high probability* (w.h.p.) if it succeeds with probability at least 1 − 1/*n*ᶜ for a constant *c* ≥ 1.

> **Theorem:** (Trivial diameter bound). Any algorithm in which some node's output depends on the input of a node at distance *D* requires Ω(*D*) rounds.

This observation explains the ubiquitous "+ *D*" term in CONGEST complexity bounds: information simply cannot travel faster than one hop per round.

### 2.2 Locality, symmetry breaking, and LCLs

A *locally checkable labeling* (LCL) is a problem whose correctness can be verified by inspecting constant-radius neighborhoods; MIS, (Δ+1)-coloring, and ruling sets are canonical LCLs. **Luby's algorithm** computes an MIS in *O*(log *n*) randomized rounds, the prototype of distributed symmetry breaking, while **Linial's lower bound** shows Ω(log* *n*) rounds are necessary for *O*(Δ²)-coloring even in LOCAL. Randomization is provably helpful for LCLs, but *derandomization* is the central challenge — and network decompositions are its principal instrument.

### 2.3 Communication complexity preliminaries

In the two-party communication model, Alice receives *x* ∈ {0,1}ᴷ and Bob receives *y* ∈ {0,1}ᴷ; they exchange bits to compute *f*(*x*, *y*). The workhorse is **set disjointness** DISJᴷ:

> **Theorem:** (Kalyanasundaram–Schnitger; Razborov). *CC*(DISJᴷ) = Ω(*K*) and *CC*ᴿ(DISJᴷ) = Ω(*K*).

That is, deciding whether two *K*-bit sets intersect requires linear communication even with randomness — and every CONGEST lower bound in this thesis is, at bottom, a reduction from DISJᴷ.

### 2.4 The Erdős girth conjecture

A *k*-spanner of *G* is a subgraph *H* with dist_H(*u*,*v*) ≤ (2*k*−1)·dist_G(*u*,*v*) for all *u*, *v*. The **Erdős girth conjecture** asserts the existence of graphs with girth exceeding 2*k* and Ω(*n*^{1+1/*k*}) edges — each its own unique (2*k*−1)-spanner, so the conjecture implies (2*k*−1)-spanners need Ω(*n*^{1+1/*k*}) edges worst-case. Proven for *k* ∈ {1, 2, 3, 5} and open otherwise; all spanner optimality claims below are conditioned on it.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology pairs *upper bounds* (explicit algorithms with proven round complexity) against *lower bounds* (impossibility results), measuring the gap. The upper bounds in Sections 4.1–4.3 are protocols with precise round counts in *n*, *D*, and Δ. The lower bounds in Section 4.4 follow a single reusable template: fix a two-party function *f* with large communication complexity (typically DISJᴷ); construct a *family of lower-bound graphs* {*G*ₓ,ᵧ} whose predicate *P* encodes *f*(*x*, *y*) across a small cut *C* between Alice's and Bob's sides; show that a *T*-round CONGEST algorithm for *P* yields a two-party protocol for *f* with *O*(*T*·|*C*|·log *n*) bits; conclude *T* = Ω(*CC*(*f*)/(|*C*| log *n*)).

This template, introduced by Das Sarma et al. [1] and refined by Abboud, Censor-Hillel, and Khoury [7], is developed formally in Section 4.4. We complement it with the information-theoretic *local information cost* framework of Davies [6], which bounds the total number of bits any algorithm must move, yielding trade-offs between rounds and communication beyond the reach of cut-based reductions.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Spanners in the CONGEST Model

**Definition.** A subgraph *H* ⊆ *G* is an *α-spanner* if dist_H(*u*, *v*) ≤ α · dist_G(*u*, *v*) for all *u*, *v* ∈ *V*. We focus on stretch α = 2*k*−1, where the girth conjecture gives matching sparsity bounds.

**The Baswana–Sen construction.** The randomized algorithm of Baswana and Sen [2] builds a (2*k*−1)-spanner with expected *O*(*k*·*n*^{1+1/*k*}) edges in *O*(*k*) distributed rounds, proceeding in *k*−1 clustering phases:

> **Theorem:** (Baswana–Sen [2]). For every integer *k* ≥ 1, every *n*-node graph has a (2*k*−1)-spanner with *O*(*k*·*n*^{1+1/*k*}) edges in expectation, constructible by a randomized algorithm in *O*(*k*) CONGEST rounds.

Each phase *i* samples surviving clusters independently with probability *n*^{−1/*k*}. Unsampled vertices attach to a neighboring sampled cluster (adding one spanner edge) or, if no sampled neighbor exists, add *all* incident edges — an event whose probability is small precisely because sampling is dense enough. The stretch argument is inductive: any omitted edge (*u*, *v*) connects vertices whose clusters were both sampled in some phase, and the cluster tree supplies a detour of length at most 2*k*−1.

The following Python sketch captures the logic of a single phase (a pedagogical simulation, not a distributed implementation):

```python
import random

def baswana_sen_phase(vertices, edges, clusters, k, n, spanner):
    """One phase of Baswana-Sen: sample clusters, attach or dump edges."""
    p = n ** (-1.0 / k)
    sampled = {c for c in clusters if random.random() < p}
    for v in vertices:
        if v in sampled:
            continue
        # nearest neighboring sampled cluster
        nbrs = [(u, c) for (u, w) in edges[v] for c in [clusters[w]]
                if c in sampled]
        if nbrs:
            u, c = min(nbrs, key=lambda t: edge_weight(v, t[0]))
            spanner.add((v, u))          # attach edge to spanner
            clusters[v] = c              # join the sampled cluster
        else:
            for (u, w) in edges[v]:
                spanner.add((v, u))      # low-probability dump
    return clusters
```

**Distributed realization.** Each phase needs only neighbor-to-neighbor communication (to find the nearest sampled cluster), so the algorithm runs in *O*(*k*) CONGEST rounds [10]. For *k* = Θ(log *n*) this yields an *O*(log *n*)-spanner with *O*(*n* log *n*) edges in *O*(log *n*) rounds — the workhorse behind sublinear-time distance approximation [8].

**Limits.** Davies [6] shows any *poly*(*n*)-time CONGEST algorithm building a (2*t*−1)-spanner with *O*(*n*^{1+1/*t*+ε}) edges must move Ω̃(*t*^{−2}·*n*^{1+1/(2*t*)}) bits — far above the trivial Ω̃(*n*) bound. Spanners are thus *provably communication-heavy*, with direct consequences for routing-table lower bounds.

### 4.2 Ruling Sets

**Definition.** A set *S* ⊆ *V* is an (*α*, *β*)-*ruling set* if (i) dist(*u*, *v*) ≥ α for distinct *u*, *v* ∈ *S* (packing) and (ii) every node is within distance *β* of *S* (covering). An MIS is exactly a (2, 1)-ruling set. Ruling sets relax the covering radius to buy speed: the right primitive when exact MIS is too expensive but approximate symmetry breaking suffices.

**The classical construction.** Awerbuch, Goldberg, Luby, and Plotkin showed in 1989 that a deterministic *O*(log *n*)-ruling set can be computed in *O*(log *n*) rounds — a bound that stood essentially unimproved for over three decades.

**Modern nearly-optimal results.** Rozhoň et al. [5] gave a deterministic algorithm computing an *O*(log log *n*)-ruling set in Õ(log *n*) rounds — an *exponential* improvement in the ruling parameter at essentially the same round complexity:

> **Theorem:** (Balliu–Brandt–Kuhn–Olivetti, STOC 2022; via [5]). Any deterministic algorithm computing a *poly*(log log *n*)-ruling set requires Ω̃(log *n*) rounds.

The lower bound holds in the LOCAL model and hence in CONGEST: the Awerbuch et al. round complexity was essentially right all along, and the progress was entirely in the *quality* of the ruling set. Ruling sets also supply the sparsification step in many CONGEST protocols — cluster centers chosen as a ruling set are simultaneously well-separated and covering — and the Baswana–Sen sampling above is, in spirit, a randomized ruling-set construction.

### 4.3 Network Decompositions

**Definition.** A *strong* (*d*, ℓ)-*network decomposition* partitions *V* into clusters of *strong* diameter ≤ *d* (measured inside the induced subgraph) whose *cluster graph* is ℓ-colorable; in the *weak* variant, diameter is measured in *G*. Modern work uses (*C*, *D*): *C* colors, diameter *D*.

Network decompositions are the master tool of deterministic distributed computing: given a (*C*, *D*)-decomposition, any *sequential* greedy algorithm can be simulated by processing color classes one at a time, with same-colored clusters working in parallel — they are non-adjacent, so their computations do not interfere. This yields deterministic algorithms running in *O*(*C*·*D*) rounds plus the cost of building the decomposition.

**History of parameters.** Table 1 summarizes the long march toward efficient decompositions.

| Year | Authors | Colors *C* | Diameter *D* | Rounds | Model |
|------|---------|-----------|--------------|--------|-------|
| 1989 | Awerbuch–Goldberg–Luby–Plotkin | exp *O*(√(log *n* log log *n*)) | exp *O*(√(log *n* log log *n*)) | exp *O*(√(log *n* log log *n*)) | LOCAL |
| 1992 | Panconesi–Srinivasan | exp *O*(√log *n*) | exp *O*(√log *n*) | exp *O*(√log *n*) | LOCAL |
| 2015 | Elkin [3] | *O*(*n*^ε) | *O*(1) | *O*(1) | randomized LOCAL |
| 2020 | Rozhoň–Ghaffari | *O*(log *n*) | *O*(log³ *n*) weak | *O*(log⁸ *n*) | CONGEST |
| 2021 | Grunau–Ghaffari–Rozhoň | *O*(log *n*) | *O*(log² *n*) weak | *O*(log⁵ *n*) | CONGEST |
| 2021 | Chang–Ghaffari [4] | *O*(log *n*) | *O*(log² *n*) strong | *O*(log¹¹ *n*) | CONGEST |
| 2022 | Elkin–Haidar–Rozhoň–Ghaffari | *O*(log *n*) | *O*(log² *n*) strong | *O*(log⁵ *n*) | CONGEST |
| 2024 | Rozhoň et al. [5] | *O*(log *n*) | *O*(log *n*) | Õ(log² *n*) | LOCAL/CONGEST |

The 2020 breakthrough of Rozhoň and Ghaffari — the first *polylogarithmic*-time deterministic network decomposition after three decades — unlocked polylog-time deterministic (Δ+1)-coloring in CONGEST. The key subsequent challenge was *strong* diameter with *small* messages: the classical weak-to-strong transformation needs unbounded messages, and Chang–Ghaffari [4] gave the first CONGEST-native reduction. The 2024 result [5] is near-optimal, since merely *using* a decomposition costs Ω(*C*·*D*) = Ω(log² *n*) rounds. A related notion, *sparse neighborhood covers* [9], covers every *r*-neighborhood by a cluster of diameter *O*(*r*) with bounded overlap; covers suffice for distance approximation and routing while admitting faster constructions — a recurring theme: *relax the structure, keep the application*.

### 4.4 Lower Bounds via Communication Complexity

We now formalize the reduction framework [1] that proves unconditional CONGEST lower bounds.

**Definition.** Fix *K*, *f*: {0,1}ᴷ × {0,1}ᴷ → {TRUE, FALSE}, and a graph predicate *P*. A family {*G*ₓ,ᵧ} is a *family of lower-bound graphs* w.r.t. *f* and *P* if: (1) *V* = *V*ₐ ∪ *V*ᵦ is fixed; (2) only edges inside *V*ₐ × *V*ₐ may depend on *x*; (3) only edges inside *V*ᵦ × *V*ᵦ may depend on *y*; (4) *G*ₓ,ᵧ satisfies *P* iff *f*(*x*, *y*) = TRUE.

> **Theorem:** (Simulation theorem [1]). Let {*G*ₓ,ᵧ} be a family of lower-bound graphs with cut *C* = *E*(*V*ₐ, *V*ᵦ). Then any deterministic CONGEST algorithm deciding *P* needs Ω(*CC*(*f*) / (|*C*| log *n*)) rounds, and any randomized algorithm needs Ω(*CC*ᴿ(*f*) / (|*C*| log *n*)) rounds.

*Proof sketch.* Alice simulates *V*ₐ and Bob simulates *V*ᵦ; per round, only the *O*(log *n*) bits over each of the |*C*| cut edges cross the boundary. Hence *T* rounds yield a two-party protocol with *O*(*T*·|*C*|·log *n*) bits, and the communication lower bound for *f* gives the round lower bound. ∎

The following sketch compiles a CONGEST decider into such a protocol:

```python
def congest_to_two_party(congest_alg, G_family, x, y, T):
    """Compile a T-round CONGEST decider into a two-party protocol."""
    # Alice holds V_A (depends on x), Bob holds V_B (depends on y)
    transcript = []
    for t in range(T):
        # each side simulates its nodes' round-t computation locally
        msgs_A = congest_alg.round_messages(side="A", state_A, t)
        msgs_B = congest_alg.round_messages(side="B", state_B, t)
        # only cut edges cross the Alice/Bob boundary: O(|C| log n) bits
        crossing = [m for m in msgs_A + msgs_B if crosses_cut(m)]
        transcript.extend(crossing)
        exchange(transcript)   # both sides learn the crossing messages
    # total communication: O(T * |C| * log n) bits
    return decide_from(transcript)
```

**The Das Sarma et al. lower bounds.** Taking *f* = DISJᴷ and *P* to be spanning-connected-subgraph verification, Das Sarma et al. [1] construct lower-bound graphs in which Alice's and Bob's inputs are encoded along Θ(√*n*) disjoint paths of length Θ(√*n*), joined by a constant-size cut. This yields:

> **Theorem:** (Das Sarma et al. [1]). Verifying MST, shortest *s*–*t* path, shortest-path tree, or minimum cut requires Ω̃(√*n* + *D*) CONGEST rounds. Moreover, *approximating* any of these problems to *any* ratio α ≥ 1 requires Ω(√(*n*/(*B* log *n*)) + *D*) rounds, even for randomized (Monte Carlo) algorithms on graphs of diameter *O*(log *n*).

The striking feature is the *absence* of α: there is no time–approximation trade-off for these problems — the exact algorithms of Kutten–Peleg (*O*(*D* + √*n* log* *n*) for MST) are essentially optimal.

**Beyond √*n*.** Abboud, Censor-Hillel, and Khoury [7] pushed the framework to *quadratic* and *near-quadratic* lower bounds for exact diameter, radius, and all-pairs problems, while the information-theoretic framework of Davies [6] adds *bit*-complexity bounds (not just round bounds) in the *KT*₁ model.

## 5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

Table 2 pairs the best known upper and lower bounds for the central problems, measuring tightness.

| Problem | Best upper bound | Lower bound | Gap |
|---------|-----------------|-------------|-----|
| MST | *O*(*D* + √*n* log* *n*) (Kutten–Peleg) | Ω̃(*D* + √*n*) [1] | polylog |
| (2*k*−1)-spanner | *O*(*k*) rounds, *O*(*k n*^{1+1/*k*}) edges [2] | Ω̃(*t*^{−2} *n*^{1+1/(2*t*)}) bits [6] | open |
| (2, β)-ruling set | β = *O*(log log *n*), Õ(log *n*) rounds [5] | Ω̃(log *n*) rounds for β = poly(log log *n*) | nearly tight |
| Network decomposition | *O*(log *n*) colors, *O*(log *n*) diameter, Õ(log² *n*) rounds [5] | Ω(log² *n*) to *use* it | tight |
| (Δ+1)-coloring (det.) | poly(log *n*) rounds via [4] + RG20 | Ω(log* *n*) (Linial) | large |
| Diameter (exact) | *O*(√*n* log* *n* + *D*) | Ω̃(√*n* + *D*) [1]; near-quadratic for APSP variants [7] | polylog |

Three observations emerge. First, the MST row is essentially *closed*: bounds match up to polylog factors, a rare complete story in distributed complexity. Second, ruling sets and network decompositions are near-optimal in the strong sense that the algorithms match lower bounds on *using* the objects, not just building them. Third, spanners remain the least understood: the communication lower bound [6] is far from the *O*(*k*)-round upper bound, and closing this gap is a major open problem.

## 6 Limitations

**Conditional optimality.** Spanner sparsity bounds rest on the Erdős girth conjecture, open for all *k* ∉ {1, 2, 3, 5}. Unconditional lower bounds on spanner *size* are weaker, and the distributed *round* complexity of optimal spanners is essentially unexplored.

**Deterministic vs. randomized gaps.** Randomization still buys exponential speedups for several problems (e.g., MIS: *O*(log *n*) randomized vs. deterministic Õ(log^{5/3} *n*) [5]). Whether deterministic CONGEST algorithms can match randomized ones for LCLs in full generality remains open.

**Model sensitivity.** The simulation lower bounds prove Ω(*CC*(*f*)/(|*C*|·*B*)) rounds, so in the LOCAL model (*B* = ∞) they say nothing — correctly, since LOCAL admits *O*(*k*)-round spanners and *O*(log* *n*)-round coloring. The information-cost bounds [6] assume *KT*₁, and separations between *KT*₀ and *KT*₁ are known.

**The diameter floor.** Every non-trivial global problem inherits the Ω(*D*) bound, so "sublinear-time" claims are meaningful only for small-diameter networks, where the √*n* term dominates and the Das Sarma et al. bounds bite hardest.

**Scope.** This thesis treats static, fault-free, synchronous networks; dynamic, asynchronous, and Byzantine variants have substantially different landscapes and are outside our scope.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The CONGEST model distills distributed computing to its essence: *what can be computed when bandwidth, not just locality, is scarce?* **Spanners** show that near-optimal distance sparsification is achievable in *O*(*k*) rounds, yet provably communication-heavy. **Ruling sets** demonstrate that approximate symmetry breaking is nearly as cheap as the lower bounds allow. **Network decompositions** provide the universal derandomization engine, transformed by the 2020 polylog breakthrough into a practical CONGEST tool with small messages and strong diameter. And the **communication-complexity lower bounds** of Das Sarma et al. draw a firm line — Ω̃(√*n* + *D*) — that no cleverness in approximation can cross for the central optimization problems.

The frontier is clear: close the spanner communication gap, derandomize the remaining randomized LCL algorithms, and determine whether strong-diameter (*O*(log *n*), *O*(log *n*))-decompositions admit truly polylogarithmic CONGEST constructions. The methods developed here — cluster sampling, ruling-set sparsification, color-scheduled simulation, and cut-based communication reductions — are the toolkit with which those landmarks will be reached.
