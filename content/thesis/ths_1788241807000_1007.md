# Expressivity Limits of Graph Neural Networks: The Weisfeiler–Lehman Hierarchy, Higher-Order k-WL Networks, and Subgraph GNN Completeness Theorems

## Abstract

Message-passing graph neural networks (MPNNs) have become the dominant architecture for learning on graph-structured data, yet their discriminative power is sharply bounded: the seminal results of Xu et al. and Morris et al. established that standard MPNNs are exactly as expressive as the one-dimensional Weisfeiler–Lehman (1-WL) graph isomorphism test, also known as color refinement. This thesis develops a complete account of that boundary and the three principal routes beyond it. We formalize the 1-WL ceiling through the injectivity characterization of neighborhood aggregation, then ascend the k-dimensional WL hierarchy, distinguishing the folklore k-FWL and oblivious k-OWL variants and their strict separation theorems. We analyze tensorized invariant graph networks, whose equivariant linear layers are governed by Bell numbers and which achieve universality at polynomial tensor order. Finally, we treat subgraph GNNs and the Equivariant Subgraph Aggregation Networks (ESAN) framework, which recover strictly greater-than-1-WL power while retaining near-linear scalability, and connect them to the graph reconstruction conjecture. Proof sketches of the central completeness and separation theorems are given, together with empirical results on synthetic indistinguishability benchmarks (CSL, EXP) and molecular datasets.

## 1 Introduction

The question of *what a neural network can distinguish* is, for graph learning, reducible to a question first asked in 1968: given two non-isomorphic graphs, can a combinatorial procedure tell them apart? The Weisfeiler–Lehman (WL) isomorphism test [8] refines node colorings by hashing multisets of neighbor colors, and its one-dimensional form — *color refinement* — became, fifty years later, the exact yardstick of graph neural network expressivity. Xu et al. [1] and Morris et al. [2] proved independently that message-passing neural networks (MPNNs) can distinguish precisely those graphs that 1-WL distinguishes: no more, no less.

This characterization is both a triumph and a limitation: it gives the first complete, rigorous account of the representational power of a widely deployed architecture class, yet 1-WL is a weak test — it cannot distinguish regular graphs of equal degree and fails on the Cai–Fürer–Immerman constructions [9] that separate every fixed level of the hierarchy.

Three research programs have emerged to break the 1-WL ceiling:

1. **Higher-order WL networks**, beginning with Morris et al.'s k-GNNs [2], which lift message passing from nodes to k-tuples and match the k-dimensional WL test.
2. **Tensorized invariant/equivariant networks**, pioneered by Maron et al. [3], which characterize all permutation-equivariant linear layers on k-tensors and prove universality at high tensor order.
3. **Subgraph GNNs**, formalized by Bevilacqua et al. in the ESAN framework [4], which represent a graph as a bag of subgraphs and process the bag equivariantly, achieving strictly greater-than-1-WL power at a fraction of the cost of higher-order methods.

This thesis unifies these programs. We prove, or sketch proofs of, the central theorems: the GIN completeness theorem, the strictness of the k-WL hierarchy, the Bell-number characterization of equivariant layers, and the ESAN expressivity lower bounds. We analyze computational trade-offs rigorously and close with the open problems that define the frontier: sparse higher-order methods, the reconstruction conjecture's role in subgraph completeness, and whether any polynomial-time architecture can approach the power of the full WL hierarchy.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Message-Passing Neural Networks

We consider graphs $G = (V, E)$ with $|V| = n$, node features $x_v \in \mathbb{R}^d$. An MPNN [10] computes node representations through $T$ rounds of neighborhood aggregation:

$$h_v^{(0)} = x_v, \qquad h_v^{(t+1)} = \phi^{(t)}\!\left(h_v^{(t)},\, \bigoplus_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} \psi^{(t)}\!\left(h_v^{(t)}, h_u^{(t)}, e_{vu}\right)\right)$$

where $\bigoplus$ is a permutation-invariant aggregator (sum, mean, max), and $\phi^{(t)}, \psi^{(t)}$ are learnable functions, typically MLPs. A graph-level readout applies a further invariant aggregation over $\{h_v^{(T)}\}$. The architectures of Kipf & Welling (GCN), Hamilton et al. (GraphSAGE), and Veličković et al. (GAT) are all instances of this schema, differing in their choice of aggregator and message function.

### 2.2 The 1-WL Test (Color Refinement)

The 1-dimensional Weisfeiler–Lehman test iteratively refines a coloring $c_t : V \to \mathbb{N}$:

$$c_0(v) = \ell(v), \qquad c_{t+1}(v) = \mathrm{RELABEL}\!\left(c_t(v),\, \{\!\!\{ c_t(u) : u \in \mathcal{N}(v) \}\!\!\}\right)$$

where $\{\!\!\{\cdot\}\!\!\}$ denotes a multiset and RELABEL injectively maps each distinct pair to a new color. The test declares two graphs *possibly isomorphic* if their color histograms coincide at convergence (reached within $n$ iterations [7]). It is *necessary but not sufficient* for isomorphism: Babai, Erdős, and Selkow [11] showed it succeeds on almost all graphs, yet it fails systematically on regular graphs — the six-cycle $C_6$ and the disjoint union $2C_3$ receive identical colorings at every iteration.

```haskell
-- Color refinement in Haskell: the combinatorial core of 1-WL
type Color = Int
refine :: Graph -> Map Node Color -> Map Node Color
refine g coloring =
  let sig v = (coloring ! v, sort [coloring ! u | u <- neighbors g v])
      sigs  = nub [sig v | v <- nodes g]
      table = Map.fromList (zip sigs [0..])
  in Map.fromList [(v, table ! sig v) | v <- nodes g]

wlStable :: Graph -> Map Node Color
wlStable g = fixpoint (refine g) (initialColors g)
```

### 2.3 Permutation Symmetry

Any graph learning function worth the name must respect node relabeling: a graph-level function $f$ must be *permutation invariant*, $f(P \cdot G) = f(G)$, and a node-level function must be *equivariant*, $F(P \cdot G) = P \cdot F(G)$, for all permutation matrices $P$. This symmetry constraint is the root of both the elegance and the weakness of MPNNs: symmetric aggregation over neighborhoods is precisely what ties them to 1-WL.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is *theoretical analysis with empirical grounding*. We proceed by:

1. **Formal reduction**: We reduce each architecture class to a combinatorial procedure (1-WL, k-FWL, k-OWL, or a subgraph WL variant) and prove two directions — *simulation* (the network can implement the test) and *upper bounding* (the network cannot exceed the test).
2. **Complexity accounting**: We track the asymptotic cost of each expressivity gain — message size, parameter count, and time complexity as functions of $n$ and $k$.
4. **Empirical validation**: We report published results on synthetic benchmarks purpose-built to expose WL limitations (CSL, EXP/CEXP) and on real molecular datasets (ZINC, ogbg-molhiv), where increased expressivity must translate to predictive performance to be practically meaningful.

> **Theorem (Meta-theorem of the field):** Every known "beyond-1-WL" architecture is characterized by *some* WL variant: either a higher-order tuple test, a tensor-equivariant test, or a subgraph-multiset test. Expressivity gains always correspond to enriching the *objects being colored* — from nodes, to tuples, to subgraphs.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The 1-WL Ceiling: Injective Aggregation and the GIN Completeness Theorem

Xu et al. [1] established the foundational correspondence. The key insight is that an MPNN layer is *exactly* a differentiable relaxation of a 1-WL refinement step: both compute a function of the node's own state and the *multiset* of neighbor states. The WL test uses an injective hash (RELABEL); the MPNN uses a learned aggregator. Hence:

> **Theorem (Xu et al. [1], GIN completeness):** Let $\mathcal{A}$ be the class of MPNNs. (i) For any $A \in \mathcal{A}$, if 1-WL deems $G_1, G_2$ possibly isomorphic, then $A(G_1) = A(G_2)$ — no MPNN exceeds 1-WL. (ii) There exists an MPNN — the Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN) — that distinguishes *all* pairs 1-WL distinguishes, provided the aggregation and readout are injective.

The GIN update,

$$h_v^{(t+1)} = \mathrm{MLP}^{(t)}\!\left((1 + \epsilon^{(t)})\, h_v^{(t)} + \sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} h_u^{(t)}\right),$$

achieves injectivity on multisets because *summation over a countable domain is injective up to multiset identity*, and MLPs are universal approximators of the remaining injective map. This yields a crisp diagnosis of popular architectures: GCN's mean aggregation and GraphSAGE's max aggregation are *not* injective on multisets (mean conflates $\{a,a\}$ with $\{a\}$; max conflates multiplicities entirely), so both are *strictly weaker* than 1-WL. Empirically, Xu et al. showed GIN attaining state-of-the-art on graph classification benchmarks available at the time, confirming that maximal WL power translates to practical gains.

```python
# GIN layer: injective multiset aggregation (Xu et al. 2019)
class GINLayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.mlp = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(dim, dim), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(dim, dim))
        self.eps = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(1))  # learnable (1 + eps)
    def forward(self, h, edge_index):
        # sum aggregation: injective on countable multisets
        agg = scatter_add(h[edge_index[1]], edge_index[0], dim=0)
        return self.mlp((1 + self.eps) * h + agg)
```

The ceiling, however, is real. *Corollary:* No MPNN can distinguish $C_6$ from $2C_3$; no MPNN can count cycles of length $\geq 4$ in general (Chen et al. [12] later sharpened this to precise counting limitations); and no MPNN can separate the Cai–Fürer–Immerman pairs that fool every fixed level of 1-WL. The remainder of this thesis is about escaping this corollary.

### 4.2 The Weisfeiler–Lehman Hierarchy: k-FWL, k-OWL, and Higher-Order Networks

To surpass color refinement, Morris et al. [2] generalized both the test and the network. The *folklore k-dimensional WL* (k-FWL) colors $k$-tuples $\mathbf{v} \in V^k$ rather than nodes. Initialization assigns equal colors to tuples whose induced labeled subgraphs are isomorphic; refinement aggregates over all tuples differing in exactly one coordinate:

$$C_{t+1}^k(\mathbf{v}) = \mathrm{RELABEL}\!\left(C_t^k(\mathbf{v}),\, \{\!\!\{ (C_t^k(\mathbf{v}[1{\leftarrow}w]), \dots, C_t^k(\mathbf{v}[k{\leftarrow}w])) : w \in V \}\!\!\}\right)$$

The *oblivious* variant (k-OWL) instead keeps $k$ separate multisets, one per coordinate. Grohe's analysis [7] relates them precisely: **k-FWL has the same distinguishing power as (k+1)-OWL**, and the hierarchy is strict:

> **Theorem (Cai–Fürer–Immerman [9]; hierarchy strictness):** For every $k \geq 1$, $(k{+}1)$-WL is *strictly* more powerful than $k$-WL. There exist graph pairs — the CFI gadgets built from twisted hypercubes — that $k$-WL cannot distinguish but $(k{+}1)$-WL can.

Morris et al.'s *k-GNN* [2] implements the neural counterpart: message passing between $k$-sets of nodes, with a hierarchical variant composing representations across orders. They proved k-GNNs match k-WL in distinguishing power and showed 2- and 3-GNNs outperforming 1-WL-limited models on molecular benchmarks. The price is steep: $O(n^k)$ tuples and parameter tensors that grow combinatorially.

| Architecture | Distinguishing power | Time complexity | Space |
|---|---|---|---|
| MPNN / GIN | 1-WL | $O(n + m)$ per layer | $O(n)$ |
| k-GNN (Morris et al.) | k-FWL | $O(n^k)$ tuples | $O(n^k)$ |
| 2-IGN (Maron et al.) | $\geq$ 1-WL, $\approx$ 2-WL | $O(n^2)$ tensor ops | $O(n^2)$ |
| ESAN, node-deletion | strictly $>$ 1-WL (DS-WL) | $O(n \cdot \mathrm{MPNN})$ | $O(n)$ bags |
| Full reconstruction GNN | $\geq$ 3-WL (conjectured) | exponential in general | — |

### 4.3 Tensorized Universality: Invariant and Equivariant Graph Networks

Maron et al. [3] attacked expressivity from the symmetry side. Instead of message passing, they asked: *what is the space of all permutation-equivariant linear layers on graph tensors?* For edge-valued data $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, the answer is startlingly clean:

> **Theorem (Maron et al. [3], basis characterization):** The space of permutation-invariant linear layers $\mathbb{R}^{n^2} \to \mathbb{R}$ has dimension **2**; the space of permutation-equivariant linear layers $\mathbb{R}^{n^2} \to \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ has dimension **15**. For $k$-tuple data, the dimensions are the $k$-th and $2k$-th **Bell numbers** $B_k, B_{2k}$.

The Bell numbers count set partitions — each basis element corresponds to an equality pattern among tensor indices. Because the basis size depends only on $k$, not on $n$, these layers transfer across graph sizes. Stacking equivariant layers with pointwise nonlinearities and an invariant readout yields *Invariant Graph Networks* (IGNs). Maron et al. proved that 2-IGNs can approximate any MPNN to arbitrary precision, and — in the follow-up universality result [5] — that IGNs of tensor order $k = \mathrm{poly}(n)$ are *universal* approximators of invariant graph functions (Keriven and Peyré [6] gave an alternative Stone–Weierstrass-based proof, later extended to the equivariant case).

Universality at $k = \mathrm{poly}(n)$ is theoretically satisfying and practically useless — an $n^{O(1)}$-order tensor is infeasible. The real contribution of the IGN program is conceptual: it shows that *expressivity is controlled by tensor order exactly as it is controlled by WL dimension*, unifying the algebraic and combinatorial views. The 2-IGN sits, roughly, at the power of 2-WL while operating on dense $n \times n$ tensors — a different point on the expressivity–efficiency frontier than sparse k-GNNs.

### 4.4 Subgraph GNNs and the ESAN Framework: Completeness Beyond Tuples

The third route — and the most practically consequential — represents a graph not as a tuple structure but as a *bag of subgraphs*. Bevilacqua et al. [4] formalized this as Equivariant Subgraph Aggregation Networks (ESAN):

1. **Select** a bag of subgraphs $S(G) = \{\!\!\{G_1, \dots, G_m\}\!\!\}$ via a policy $\pi$ — e.g., *node deletion* ($G_i = G \setminus \{v_i\}$), *edge deletion*, or *ego-networks*.
2. **Encode** each subgraph with a base encoder (any MPNN), obtaining representations equivariant under joint permutation of the bag.
3. **Aggregate** the bag with a permutation-invariant readout (DeepSets-style) to produce the graph embedding.

> **Theorem (Bevilacqua et al. [4], ESAN lower bounds):** ESAN with the node-deletion policy and a 1-WL-powerful base encoder is *strictly more expressive* than 1-WL. Formally, its power is lower-bounded by the *DS-WL* test (WL applied to the disjoint union of subgraphs) and, with suitable policies and encoders, by stronger subgraph WL variants — while remaining far cheaper than $k$-WL for $k \geq 2$.

The intuition is crisp: two graphs indistinguishable to 1-WL *often contain distinguishable subgraphs*. Deleting one node from $C_6$ yields a path $P_5$; deleting one node from $2C_3$ yields $C_3 \sqcup P_2$ — and 1-WL easily separates these bags. This connects subgraph GNNs to the *graph reconstruction conjecture* of Kelly and Ulam: if a graph is determined by its node-deleted deck (as almost all graphs are), then a sufficiently powerful subgraph architecture is *complete*.

ESAN also clarified the design space: the *policy* (which subgraphs) and the *encoder symmetry* (how the bag is processed) independently control expressivity. Complementary results sharpened the picture: Bouritsas et al. [14] showed that augmenting MPNNs with *subgraph isomorphism counts* strictly increases power, and Papp et al.'s DropGNN showed that even *random* node dropout, aggregated over runs, provably increases expressivity.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof Sketch: GIN Achieves 1-WL (Xu et al. [1])

*Upper bound.* By induction on layers: assume $h_v^{(t)}$ is a function of $c_t(v)$. The multiset $\{\!\!\{h_u^{(t)} : u \in \mathcal{N}(v)\}\!\!\}$ is then a function of $\{\!\!\{c_t(u)\}\!\!\}$, so $h_v^{(t+1)} = \phi(h_v^{(t)}, \bigoplus_u \psi(h_u^{(t)}))$ is a function of $c_{t+1}(v)$. If 1-WL assigns identical color histograms to $G_1, G_2$, the multisets of final embeddings coincide, and any invariant readout produces identical outputs. ∎

*Lower bound (simulation).* Map colors to one-hot encodings over a countable set. Summation is injective on multisets of one-hot vectors; an MLP then implements RELABEL by universal approximation. Composing $T$ such layers simulates $T$ WL iterations; an injective readout (e.g., sum + MLP) reproduces the histogram comparison. ∎

### 5.2 Proof Sketch: Strictness of the k-WL Hierarchy

The Cai–Fürer–Immerman construction [9] replaces each vertex of a base expander graph with a gadget of even/odd parity, producing pairs $G_k, H_k$ that $k$-WL cannot distinguish (the gadgets' internal symmetries defeat every $k$-tuple coloring) but $(k{+}1)$-WL can (a $(k{+}1)$-tuple spans the gadget boundary and detects the parity twist). The construction is polynomial in $k$, so the separation is effective, not merely existential. ∎

### 5.3 Empirical Results

The **CSL dataset** (circulant skip-link graphs: 41-node 4-regular graphs in 10 isomorphism classes, pairwise 1-WL-indistinguishable) is the canonical expressivity benchmark. Reported results [2, 4]:

- **GIN / 1-WL MPNNs**: chance-level accuracy (~10%) — the expected failure, confirming the ceiling.
- **3-GNN / PPGN**: near-perfect classification, confirming that 3-WL power suffices.
- **ESAN (node-deletion policy, DS-GNN encoder)**: near-perfect accuracy at $O(n)$ subgraph encodings — strictly-beyond-1-WL power without tensorial cost.

On **EXP/CEXP** (600 pairs encoding the 1-WL vs. higher-order gap propositionally), ESAN variants separate all pairs that any fixed higher-order method separates within the tested regime. On real data, the picture is nuanced but favorable: ESAN improves over base MPNNs on **ZINC** (constrained solubility regression) and **ogbg-molhiv** (ROC-AUC gains), and Bouritsas et al. [14] report consistent gains from subgraph-count features on TUDatasets. The improvements are modest in absolute terms — molecular benchmarks are noisy and feature-rich — but they are *systematic*, and ablations confirm the gains track expressivity rather than capacity.

```rust
// Sketch: k-FWL refinement over k-tuples (Morris et al. 2019)
fn k_fwl_step(colors: &HashMap<Tuple, Color>, g: &Graph, k: usize) -> HashMap<Tuple, Color> {
    let mut sigs: HashMap<Signature, Color> = HashMap::new();
    let mut next = HashMap::new();
    for v in all_tuples(g, k) {
        // aggregate over all w in V, all k positions: the folklore neighborhood
        let mut mset = Vec::new();
        for w in g.nodes() {
            let mut coords = Vec::with_capacity(k);
            for j in 0..k { coords.push(colors[&v.with(j, w)]); }
            mset.push(coords);
        }
        mset.sort();
        let sig = (colors[&v], mset);
        let c = *sigs.entry(sig).or_insert(sigs.len() as Color);
        next.insert(v, c);
    }
    next
}
```

## 6 Limitations

**Computational cliffs.** Every expressivity gain has a price, and the price is steep: k-GNNs cost $O(n^k)$; IGNs need dense tensors; ESAN multiplies base-encoder cost by the bag size ($n$ subgraphs for node deletion). On large sparse graphs — social networks, recommender graphs — none of these scale naively, and practitioners overwhelmingly still use 1-WL-limited MPNNs. Sparse and local variants [13] mitigate but do not eliminate the gap.

**WL is not the whole story.** Distinguishing non-isomorphic graphs is a *proxy* for representation quality, not the objective itself. A more expressive model can overfit harder, and on many benchmarks the 1-WL ceiling is not the binding constraint — features, optimization, and data scarcity are. Expressivity theorems are worst-case and existential; they do not guarantee learnability by gradient descent.

**Upper bounds remain open.** We know MPNNs $\equiv$ 1-WL exactly, and we have lower bounds for k-GNNs, IGNs, and ESAN — but *tight* characterizations of subgraph GNNs are incomplete. The exact WL power of ESAN under arbitrary policies is unknown; the reconstruction conjecture, which would imply near-completeness of deck-based methods, remains unproven after 80 years.

**Continuous features and geometry.** The WL framework is inherently discrete. Real graphs carry continuous node/edge attributes and, in chemistry, 3D geometry — where equivariant networks for $E(3)$ symmetry (not just $S_n$) dominate. The WL hierarchy says little about these regimes, and the interaction between combinatorial expressivity and geometric expressivity is poorly understood.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The expressivity theory of graph neural networks is, at its core, the story of a single equality — **MPNN = 1-WL** — and three escapes from it. Xu et al. [1] and Morris et al. [2] drew the boundary with the GIN completeness theorem; the k-WL hierarchy [2, 7, 9] showed the boundary is the first rung of an infinite strict ladder; Maron et al. [3, 5] re-derived the ladder algebraically through Bell-numbered equivariant layers and proved tensorized universality; and Bevilacqua et al. [4] demonstrated that bags of subgraphs climb past the first rung at a fraction of the tensorial cost, tying neural expressivity to the venerable reconstruction conjecture.

The practical moral is measured: use GIN-class injective aggregation as the default — anything less surrenders power needlessly; reach for subgraph methods (ESAN, subgraph counts [14]) when the task demands distinguishing regular or symmetric structures, as in chemistry; and reserve higher-order tensor methods for small graphs where their cost is affordable. The theoretical moral is sharper: *expressivity is about which objects you color* — nodes, tuples, or subgraphs — and every future architecture will be judged, explicitly or implicitly, against the WL variant it simulates.
