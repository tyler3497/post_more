# Fully Homomorphic Encryption: From Gentry's Bootstrapping Blueprint to the BGV, BFV, and CKKS Schemes — Noise Growth, Modulus Switching, Programmable Bootstrapping, and RLWE Security

## Abstract

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) allows arbitrary computation on encrypted data without decryption, a capability first envisioned by Rivest, Adleman, and Dertouzos in 1978 and first realized by Craig Gentry in 2009 [1]. This article surveys the modern FHE landscape from Gentry's bootstrapping blueprint through the second-generation leveled schemes — Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan (BGV) [2], Brakerski/Fan–Vercauteren (BFV) [3], and the approximate-arithmetic Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song (CKKS) scheme [4] — to fast gate bootstrapping in FHEW and TFHE with programmable bootstrapping [5]. We explain noise growth under homomorphic operations, the modulus-switching and key-switching machinery that tames it, Gentry's recryption paradigm and its descendants, the Ring Learning With Errors (RLWE) reductions underwriting security [6], and the state of practical deployment in private neural-network inference [7] and encrypted databases.

---

## 1 Intro

The dream is simple to state and was, for three decades, impossible to realize: *compute on data you cannot see*. Rivest, Adleman, and Dertouzos called the primitive *privacy homomorphisms* in 1978 [1], and partial answers arrived quickly — RSA is multiplicatively homomorphic, Paillier additively so — but arbitrary computation on ciphertexts remained out of reach.

Craig Gentry's 2009 Stanford thesis changed the field overnight [1]. His construction had two movements: a *somewhat* homomorphic scheme on ideal lattices evaluating bounded-degree polynomials, and **bootstrapping** (*recryption*) — homomorphically evaluating the scheme's own decryption circuit to convert bounded depth into unbounded computation. What followed was a Cambrian explosion. Brakerski and Vaikuntanathan moved the foundation to the Learning With Errors problem, and Brakerski, Gentry, and Vaikuntanathan then showed how to obtain *leveled* FHE — enough depth for any a priori bounded circuit — without bootstrapping at all, via **modulus switching** [2]. Fan and Vercauteren transplanted scale-invariant techniques to the ring setting as BFV [3]. Cheon, Kim, Kim, and Song abandoned exactness entirely, designing CKKS for *approximate* arithmetic on real and complex numbers [4]. Ducas and Micciancio, then Chillotti and coauthors, drove gate bootstrapping below a tenth of a second and made it *programmable*, evaluating arbitrary lookup tables during the refresh [5].

This article is a technical tour of that lineage: how noise grows, how it is managed, how bootstrapping works in its classical and modern forms, what security actually rests on, and where the technology stands in deployment today.

## 2 Background

### Lattices, LWE, and Ring-LWE

All practical FHE schemes descend from lattice problems. The **Learning With Errors** (LWE) problem asks an adversary to distinguish noisy linear equations from uniform ones: given pairs *(a, b)* with *b = inner(a, s) + e* over *Z_q*, where *s* is secret and *e* is small noise, recover *s* or distinguish the samples from random. Regev showed LWE is as hard as worst-case lattice problems such as GapSVP, via a quantum reduction [6]. **Ring-LWE** (RLWE) replaces vectors with polynomials in a cyclotomic ring *R_q = Z_q[X]/(X^n + 1)*, gaining quasi-linear efficiency; Lyubashevsky, Peikert, and Regev proved RLWE pseudorandom under worst-case hardness of ideal-lattice problems [6].

> **Theorem (RLWE worst-case to average-case, informal):** For power-of-two cyclotomic rings, the decision Ring-LWE problem is at least as hard as approximating worst-case problems on ideal lattices, under a quantum reduction [6].

### Somewhat, leveled, and fully homomorphic encryption

A **somewhat homomorphic encryption** (SHE) scheme evaluates circuits of bounded depth before accumulated noise destroys the message. A **leveled** FHE scheme takes a depth bound *L* at key-generation time and evaluates any depth-*L* circuit — sufficient for nearly all applications, since the circuit is usually known in advance. A **pure** (or *true*) FHE scheme evaluates circuits of *unbounded* depth, which in practice means it implements bootstrapping.

The universal enemy is **noise**. Every RLWE ciphertext has the form *(a, b)* with *b = a·s + m·scale + e*; decryption computes *b − a·s* and rounds away the small error *e*. Addition adds errors, multiplication roughly squares them, and once the error approaches the modulus, decryption fails — noise accounting is the heart of FHE design.

## 3 Methodology

This survey is organized around the *mechanisms* rather than a chronological catalog, because the same small set of ideas recurs across generations:

1. **Noise bookkeeping** — tracking how the error term evolves under addition, multiplication, and key switching, and the *invariant* each scheme maintains (BGV keeps noise essentially constant across levels; BFV keeps it scale-invariant; CKKS folds noise into the message itself).
2. **Noise management** — modulus switching, rescaling, relinearization/key switching, and bootstrapping as progressively more powerful refresh operators.
3. **Security reduction** — what hardness assumption each mechanism consumes, and where *circular security* (encrypting the secret key to itself, needed for bootstrapping) enters.
4. **Empirical grounding** — asymptotics are checked against implemented libraries (SEAL, HElib, OpenFHE, Lattigo, Concrete), because constant factors decide deployability [9].

We assume familiarity with public-key encryption and basic algebra; all scheme-specific notation is introduced where used.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The second generation: BGV and BFV

The first truly practical lineage begins with Brakerski and Vaikuntanathan's LWE-based SHE (FOCS 2011), which introduced **relinearization**: a degree-2 ciphertext *(d0, d1, d2)* from multiplication is collapsed back to a standard pair via a *key-switching key* of encrypted powers of *s²*, at modest noise cost [2].

**BGV** (Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan, ITCS 2012) systematized this into a leveled scheme with two enduring innovations [2]:

- **Modulus switching.** Ciphertexts live in a descending chain of moduli *q_L > q_{L−1} > … > q_0*. After each multiplication level, the ciphertext is scaled from modulus *q_l* to *q_{l−1}*, which shrinks the noise by roughly the same factor. The noise stays essentially *constant* across levels instead of compounding exponentially — the defining BGV invariant.
- **Key switching.** A generalization of relinearization that converts a ciphertext decryptable under one key into one decryptable under another (or the same) key, enabling the modulus-switch machinery and, later, SIMD-style ciphertext packing and rotations.

BGV uses *least-significant-digit* encoding: the message sits in the low-order bits, noise in the high-order region below the modulus. **BFV** (Brakerski 2012; Fan–Vercauteren 2012) flips this to *most-significant-digit* encoding, placing a scaled message *floor(q/t)·m* high in the modulus [3]. Its signature move is **scale invariance**: multiplication first computes the tensor product over the integers, then scales the result down by *t/q*, rounding away the bulk of the noise in one step. 
| Scheme | Encoding | Noise management | Plaintext space | Sweet spot |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BGV | least-significant-digit | modulus switching chain | finite fields, integers mod *t* | exact arithmetic, SIMD batching |
| BFV | most-significant-digit | scale-invariant *t/q* rounding | integers mod *t* | exact integer arithmetic |
| CKKS | canonical embedding, scaled | rescaling (drop a modulus prime) | approximate reals/complex | ML inference, signal processing |
| FHEW/TFHE | LWE phase, MSB | gate bootstrapping per operation | bits / small integers | Boolean circuits, comparisons |

A minimal Python sketch captures the RLWE-flavored encryption both BGV and BFV share (toy parameters, not secure):

```python
import numpy as np
n, q, t = 8, 97, 5          # ring degree, ciphertext modulus, plaintext modulus
def keygen():
    s = np.random.randint(-1, 2, n)          # ternary secret
    a = np.random.randint(0, q, n)
    e = np.random.randint(-1, 2, n)          # small error
    return s, (a, (a * s + e) % q)            # toy polynomial multiply omitted
def encrypt(pk, m):
    a, b = pk
    u = np.random.randint(-1, 2, n)
    e1 = np.random.randint(-1, 2, n)
    e2 = np.random.randint(-1, 2, n)
    # BFV-style: message scaled into high-order bits
    m_scaled = (m * (q // t)) % q
    return ((a * u + e1) % q, (b * u + e2 + m_scaled) % q)
```

Decryption computes *c1 − c0·s*, divides by *q/t*, and rounds — succeeding exactly when the accumulated noise stays below *q/(2t)*. Every parameter choice in real deployments is this inequality, generalized across levels.

### 4.2 Approximate arithmetic: CKKS

Machine learning does not need exact integers; it needs floats. **CKKS** (Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song, Asiacrypt 2017) made a radical bargain: treat the RLWE noise *as part of the message* and compute *approximately* [4]. A complex vector is encoded via the canonical embedding into a plaintext polynomial, scaled by a factor Δ, and encrypted. After each multiplication the scale squares to Δ², so a **rescaling** operation divides by Δ and drops one modulus prime from the chain — simultaneously managing scale and noise, mirroring BGV's modulus switching but in service of precision rather than exactness.

CKKS evaluates real-number arithmetic — polynomials, transforms, logistic regression, neural networks — faster than any exact scheme at comparable precision [9]. The price is real:

- Precision is finite and *degrades* with depth; each rescale loses roughly log Δ bits.
- CKKS ciphertexts are malleable by design, and the approximate decryption output can leak the secret key if an adversary observes many decryptions — the **IND-CPA-D** attacks of Li and Micciancio (2021). CKKS must never be deployed where the decryptor returns results to an untrusted party without noise flooding.
- Bootstrapping CKKS requires homomorphically evaluating the *modular reduction* function — the scheme's hardest operation — via sine/cosine polynomial approximations (Cheon et al., Eurocrypt 2021) or direct minimax approximation [10].

> **Theorem (CKKS rescaling correctness, informal):** If a ciphertext at level *l* encrypts *m* with scale Δ and error bounded well below Δ, then rescaling yields a level *(l−1)* ciphertext encrypting *m* with scale Δ and relative error essentially unchanged [4].

### 4.3 Noise growth and modulus switching

To see why modulus switching works, track the BGV *noise invariant*. Write a ciphertext at level *l* as *(c0, c1)* with *c0 + c1·s = m + t·e* over *R_{q_l}*. Fresh ciphertexts have *|e|* small (call the bound *B*). Addition gives noise at most *2B*; multiplication of two noise-*B* ciphertexts gives noise on the order of *B²·poly(n)* — the quadratic blowup that kills naive schemes.

Modulus switching maps *(c, q_l)* to *(c′, q_{l′})* with *q_{l′} < q_l* via *c′ = round((q_{l′}/q_l)·c)*, choosing the rounding offset so the message is preserved. The new noise is approximately *(q_{l′}/q_l)·e* plus a small rounding term. Choose the chain so each ratio cancels one multiplication's growth, and the invariant *|e| ≤ B* holds at *every* level. Depth *L* then costs a modulus of size roughly *B^L* — exponential in *L*, but *L* is fixed at setup, and bootstrapping (or a fresh key) is unnecessary for bounded circuits. Gentry, Halevi, and Smart later optimized this bootstrapping-adjacent machinery with packed ciphertext techniques that shrank public keys from gigabytes to megabytes [5].

In Haskell-flavored pseudocode, the invariant can be read as a type-level budget:

```haskell
-- Phantom-typed noise budget: well-formed only below the level's bound
newtype Cipher (l :: Nat) (b :: Nat) = Cipher (Poly, Poly)
mul :: Cipher l b -> Cipher l b -> Cipher l (b*b)          -- quadratic blowup
modSwitch :: Cipher (l+1) (b*b) -> Cipher l b               -- restore budget
```

### 4.4 Bootstrapping: Gentry's blueprint and programmable gates

**Gentry's blueprint** has three steps [1]:

1. Build a *somewhat* homomorphic scheme whose evaluable depth exceeds (a squashed variant of) its own decryption circuit — a *bootstrappable* scheme.
2. *Squash* the decryption circuit: add a hint to the public key (a sparse subset of key bits summing to the secret key) so decryption becomes a low-degree polynomial, at the cost of the sparse-subset-sum assumption.
3. *Recrypt*: to refresh a noisy ciphertext *c*, homomorphically evaluate *Decrypt* on encryptions of the secret-key bits and the bits of *c*. The inner decryption strips the outer noise; the result encrypts the same message with only the *fixed* noise of one fresh homomorphic decryption.

**FHEW** (Ducas–Micciancio, Eurocrypt 2015) bootstraps LWE ciphertexts encrypting single bits in under a second using a *homomorphic accumulator*: an RLWE encryption of a test polynomial is blindly rotated by the ciphertext's phase, then a sample is extracted [5]. **TFHE** (Chillotti et al., Asiacrypt 2016) reformulated the accumulator with the GSW *external product*, cutting bootstrapping below 0.1 seconds and the key material from 1 GB to 24 MB [5].

The killer feature is **programmable bootstrapping (PBS)**: the test polynomial encodes an arbitrary univariate function as a lookup table, so bootstrapping *simultaneously* evaluates any function *f* on the message while refreshing the noise. Comparisons, ReLU activations, and S-boxes become nearly free — a capability BGV/BFV/CKKS bootstrapping lacks [9]. 
A TLA+-style sketch of the bootstrapping invariant:

```tla
---- MODULE Bootstrap ----
VARIABLES ct, noise
Refresh == /\ noise' = FreshNoise
           /\ Decrypt(ct') = Decrypt(ct)   \* message preserved, noise reset
====
```

CKKS bootstrapping instead chains *ModRaise*, *CoeffToSlot*, approximate modular reduction (*EvalMod*), and *SlotToCoeff* [10] — refreshing thousands of packed values at once: high throughput, high latency, the complement of TFHE's gate bootstrapping.

### 4.5 Security reductions to RLWE

Beneath the engineering lies a clean reductionist story. BGV, BFV, and CKKS are all semantically secure under the **decision RLWE** assumption: ciphertexts *(a, a·s + e + encode(m))* are pseudorandom [6]. RLWE itself reduces, via a quantum polynomial-time reduction, to worst-case approximate shortest-vector problems on ideal lattices. The chain is:

*worst-case ideal-lattice problems → (quantum) decision-RLWE → IND-CPA security of BGV/BFV/CKKS.*

Two caveats deserve emphasis:

1. **Circular (KDM) security.** Bootstrapping publishes an encryption of the secret key. Standard IND-CPA does not cover key-dependent messages; FHE needs *circular security*, which is an additional assumption. Brakerski and Vaikuntanathan showed RLWE-based FHE can achieve KDM security for certain function classes [8], but full circular security for arbitrary circuits remains conjectural.
2. **The quantum reduction is one-directional insurance, not a hardness proof.** Parameter selection therefore follows the Homomorphic Encryption Standard, targeting ≥128-bit security against the best known lattice attacks (BKZ with sieving).

> **Theorem (Gentry's bootstrapping theorem, informal):** If a bootstrappable somewhat-homomorphic scheme exists and is circular-secure, then a fully homomorphic scheme exists [1].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### Benchmarks and libraries

Asymptotics mislead; implementations decide. The mature open-source ecosystem now spans Microsoft SEAL (BFV, CKKS), IBM HElib (BGV, CKKS), OpenFHE (BGV, BFV, CKKS, FHEW/TFHE — the PALISADE successor), Tune Insight's Lattigo (Go), and Zama's Concrete and tfhe-rs (TFHE-style PBS) [9]. Representative published figures:

- **TFHE gate bootstrapping:** <0.1 s per binary gate in the original Chillotti et al. implementation [5]; modern implementations reach tens of milliseconds, with programmable bootstrapping evaluating an arbitrary univariate function in the same operation.
- **CKKS bootstrapping:** refreshes a full packed vector (thousands of slots) in seconds to minutes depending on parameters, achieving on the order of 20–30 bits of precision after *EvalMod* [10]. Throughput per slot is unmatched; latency is the cost.
- **BGV/BFV bootstrapping:** minutes per bootstrap in HElib/OpenFHE, but amortized over SIMD-packed vectors it delivers the highest throughput for exact small-integer arithmetic [9].

### Private inference: CryptoNets and descendants

The landmark deployment is **CryptoNets** (Gilad-Bachrach et al., ICML 2016): a convolutional network trained on plaintext, converted to use polynomial activations (the square function in place of ReLU), and evaluated homomorphically on encrypted MNIST images, reaching ~99% accuracy with tens of thousands of predictions per hour [7]. The protocol is minimal — the client encrypts, the server computes blindly, the client decrypts — and neither the data nor even the *prediction* leaks to the server.

Descendants pushed further: Faster CryptoNets via sparsity and pruning, Gazelle's hybrid HE/garbled-circuit protocols, and Delphi's HE-friendly architectures. The CKKS era shifted the field to approximate networks on floats, while TFHE's programmable bootstrapping reopened exact ReLU and max-pooling via lookup tables. Private inference — encrypted medical scoring, private biometric matching — is now a genuine product category.

### Encrypted databases

The database story is older and more cautious. **CryptDB** (Popa et al., SOSP 2011) showed SQL over encrypted data using *onion* encryption — deterministic and order-preserving layers peeled per query — but it is not FHE and leaks under active attack. True FHE query processing evaluates predicates and aggregations as circuits: equality via polynomial tests, range queries via programmable bootstrapping comparisons, aggregations as homomorphic sums. A homomorphic filter over a million rows remains a batch job, not an interactive query — but for outsourced analytics with strong privacy requirements, leveled BFV/CKKS pipelines are deployed in pilot systems today, often composed with differential privacy on outputs.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires the debit column:

- **Performance.** FHE carries a 10⁴–10⁶× overhead versus plaintext for general circuits. Leveled circuits and SIMD packing recover much of it for structured workloads, but interactive latencies remain out of reach for deep exact circuits.
- **CKKS leakage.** Approximate decryption outputs can leak the secret key to a decrypting adversary; CKKS is IND-CPA but not IND-CPA-D secure without countermeasures such as noise flooding [4]. Treat decrypted CKKS outputs as sensitive.
- **Circular security is assumed, not proven.** Every bootstrapped deployment bets that encrypting the secret key to itself is safe — plausible, standardized, but beyond the RLWE reduction.
- **Quantum note.** Lattice schemes are *candidates* for post-quantum security — the reduction goes through quantum algorithms — but cryptanalysis (BKZ/sieving improvements) steadily erodes concrete bit-security, demanding conservative parameters.

## 7 Conclusion

Seventeen years after Gentry's thesis, FHE has traveled from theoretical curiosity to deployed, standardized technology [1]. The intellectual spine is unchanged: noise is the enemy, and every advance — modulus switching [2], scale invariance [3], approximate arithmetic [4], programmable gate bootstrapping [5] — is a better way to manage it. The security story, anchored in Ring-LWE's worst-case-to-average-case reductions [6], is among the strongest in cryptography, qualified chiefly by the circular-security assumption bootstrapping demands.

The frontier is no longer *whether* FHE works but *where it pays*: private ML inference [7], encrypted analytics, and hybrid protocols that spend FHE only where its unique power — computing on data that stays encrypted end to end — justifies the cost. The trajectory of the last decade suggests the remaining overheads are engineering problems, not impossibility results. Gentry's blueprint, once a curiosity, is becoming infrastructure.
