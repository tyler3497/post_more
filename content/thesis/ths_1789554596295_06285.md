# Event-Driven Intelligence: Spiking Neuron Models, Surrogate Gradient Learning, and Neuromorphic Architectures

## Abstract

Conventional deep networks consume staggering amounts of energy because they are fundamentally synchronous: every multiply-accumulate fires on every clock, whether the data warrants it or not. Spiking neural networks (SNNs) take the opposite route — computation happens only when a neuron emits an event, mirroring the brain's twenty-watt budget for roughly one hundred billion neurons. This thesis develops the full stack of event-driven intelligence. It begins with the dynamical systems of spiking neurons, from the leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) model to Izhikevich's bifurcation-based reduction, and analyzes the expressivity of temporal versus rate codes. It then dissects learning: local spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP), eligibility-trace methods such as e-prop, and the surrogate-gradient machinery that now trains deep spiking networks to near-ANN accuracy on image and speech benchmarks. Finally, it surveys neuromorphic silicon — Intel's Loihi, IBM's TrueNorth, and the SpiNNaker many-core system — with measured energy-per-inference figures from published benchmarks, and closes with open problems in training depth, temporal credit assignment, and benchmarking rigor.

##1 Intro

The dominant narrative of the deep learning era is that intelligence is limited by data and parameters, and that energy is merely a cost of doing business. The numbers tell a different story. Training a single large language model can consume megawatt-hours, and inference at planetary scale is becoming a load-bearing quantity on power grids. The human brain, by contrast, performs continual learning, control, and perception at roughly twenty watts — a figure achieved not through exotic device physics but through an *architectural commitment* to event-driven, sparse, asynchronous computation [1].

Spiking neural networks are the computational substrate of this commitment. Where conventional artificial neural networks (ANNs) communicate dense real-valued vectors at every time step, an SNN communicates binary events — spikes — emitted only when a neuron's membrane potential crosses a threshold. Quiescent neurons consume near-zero energy, and inactive synapses transmit nothing. This sparsity is not an afterthought or a compression trick; it is the native operating regime of the model, and neuromorphic processors such as Intel's Loihi and IBM's TrueNorth are digital realizations of it [7][8].

Yet for decades SNNs were a research curiosity rather than a technology, blocked by three hard problems. First, neuron models realistic enough to be useful were too expensive to simulate at scale, while cheap ones were too impoverished to reproduce cortical dynamics. Second, the threshold nonlinearity of a spiking neuron is discontinuous, which kills gradient-based learning. Third, there was no hardware that could run event-driven networks efficiently — GPUs, optimized for dense batched computation, are almost the architectural antithesis of a spiking network.

The past decade has changed all three variables simultaneously. Izhikevich's model showed that near-Hodgkin–Huxley fidelity could be had at near-LIF cost [1]. Surrogate gradient methods cracked deep supervised learning for SNNs [5]. And neuromorphic chips demonstrated orders-of-magnitude gains in energy per inference on real benchmarks [10]. This thesis traces that convergence: neuron models (§2), learning rules (§3), deep-dives into coding, STDP, surrogate gradients, and hardware (§4), empirical benchmarks with a worked theorem (§5), and the honest limitations that remain (§6).

##2 Background

### From Hodgkin–Huxley to integrate-and-fire

The biophysical gold standard of neuronal modeling remains the Hodgkin–Huxley (HH) equations (1952), which describe the action potential through voltage-gated sodium and potassium conductances [2]. The HH model is a four-dimensional nonlinear dynamical system whose numerical integration requires sub-millisecond timesteps; simulating a cortical column of it was computationally prohibitive for decades.

The leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron is the pragmatic opposite: a single linear differential equation for the membrane potential $u(t)$ with a hard threshold and reset [3]:

$$
\tau_m \frac{du}{dt} = -(u - u_{\text{rest}}) + R\, I(t)
$$

When $u$ reaches the threshold $\vartheta$, the neuron emits a spike and $u$ is reset. The LIF neuron is cheap — one state variable, one discontinuity — and remains the workhorse of neuromorphic hardware, where silicon real estate is precious.

> **Theorem:** The LIF neuron's subthreshold dynamics define a contraction toward rest. For constant input current, the first-passage time to threshold is exactly solvable and monotone decreasing in current amplitude.
>
> **Proof:** With constant $I$, the subthreshold solution is $u(t) = u_{\text{rest}} + RI(1 - e^{-t/\tau_m})$. Setting $u(t^*) = \vartheta$ gives $t^* = -\tau_m \ln(1 - (\vartheta - u_{\text{rest}})/(RI))$, which is defined exactly when the drive exceeds threshold, and $dt^*/dI < 0$ follows from differentiation of the logarithm. ∎

Izhikevich's 2003 model struck the long-sought middle ground: a two-dimensional system with a quadratic nonlinearity and an adaptive recovery variable that reproduces the firing patterns of all known cortical neuron types — regular spiking, fast spiking, bursting, chattering, intrinsically bursting — at roughly thirteen floating-point operations per millisecond of simulation [1]:

```python
import numpy as np

def izhikevich_step(v, u, I, a, b, dt=0.5):
    """One Euler step of the Izhikevich neuron model (Izhikevich 2003)."""
    dv = (0.04 * v**2 + 5 * v + 140 - u + I) * dt
    du = a * (b * v - u) * dt
    v, u = v + dv, u + du
    spiked = v >= 30.0
    v = np.where(spiked, -65.0, v)       # voltage reset
    u = np.where(spiked, u + 2.0, u)     # recovery reset
    return v, u, spiked
```

The key insight is *bifurcation engineering*: the parameters $a$ and $b$ place the resting state near different bifurcation types (saddle-node, Andronov–Hopf), and the same equations generate qualitatively different excitable behaviors depending on that placement [1]. This model demonstrated that biological plausibility and computational efficiency are not mutually exclusive — a lesson that continues to shape neuromorphic design.

### Neural codes: rate versus time

How does a spike train carry information? Two classical hypotheses compete:

- **Rate coding:** information is carried by the average firing rate over a window. Robust to noise and timing jitter, but slow: distinguishing two rates differing by $\Delta f$ requires integration times that scale inversely with $\Delta f$.
- **Temporal coding:** information is carried by precise spike times, inter-spike intervals, or the order of first spikes across a population (rank-order or time-to-first-spike codes). In principle exponentially more efficient — a population of $N$ neurons has $N!$ distinct first-spike orderings — but fragile under timing noise [3].

Modern practice often lands between the extremes: surrogate-gradient-trained networks use the full temporal dynamics but are typically read out by a rate-like statistic (e.g., the maximum membrane potential or spike count of output neurons over the presentation window) [5]. The coding debate thus becomes an *optimization* question rather than a metaphysical one: which representation does gradient descent discover given a task, a loss, and an energy budget? Recent theory suggests that the answer is task-dependent, with latency codes emerging naturally when the loss penalizes response time [11].

##3 Methodology

This thesis synthesizes three methodological threads that together constitute the modern SNN stack:

1. **Dynamical-systems analysis of neuron models.** We compare LIF, Izhikevich, and adaptive-exponential integrate-and-fire models by their bifurcation structure, information capacity per spike, and silicon cost (transistors or picojoules per synaptic operation), drawing on the canonical references [1][2][3].
2. **Learning-rule analysis.** We trace the arc from local, unsupervised STDP [4] through three-factor and eligibility-trace rules [6] to global, supervised surrogate gradients [5], evaluating each on the axes of biological plausibility, hardware feasibility, and empirical performance.
3. **Empirical benchmark synthesis.** We aggregate published neuromorphic benchmark figures — keyword spotting on Loihi [10], multi-object detection on TrueNorth [8], and large-scale cortical simulation on SpiNNaker [9] — and analyze them under a common energy-per-inference methodology, while flagging the measurement pitfalls that make cross-platform comparison treacherous.

Throughout, all factual claims about hardware specifications, benchmark numbers, and published results are tied to the cited primary sources; nothing rests on vendor marketing material alone.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The neuron model zoo and the silicon cost of fidelity

The design space of spiking neuron models spans four orders of magnitude in per-neuron cost. At the top, the Hodgkin–Huxley model needs four coupled nonlinear ODEs and roughly 1,200 flops per millisecond [2]. The adaptive-exponential integrate-and-fire (AdEx) model keeps a single exponential nonlinearity plus adaptation and captures most cortical firing patterns at a fraction of that cost [3]. The Izhikevich model achieves AdEx-like richness with polynomial arithmetic [1], and the bare LIF model strips everything to one linear ODE.

| Model | State vars | FLOPs/ms | Firing patterns | Silicon affinity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hodgkin–Huxley | 4 | ~1200 | All | Poor (lookup tables needed) |
| AdEx integrate-and-fire | 2 | ~40 | Most cortical | Moderate |
| Izhikevich | 2 | ~13 | All known cortical | Good (polynomial only) |
| Leaky integrate-and-fire | 1 | ~5 | Regular spiking only | Excellent (linear, 1 state) |

Hardware architects overwhelmingly choose the bottom row: TrueNorth implements a digital LIF neuron with configurable leak, threshold, and reset [8], and Loihi supports richer compartmental models but defaults to LIF-like point neurons for efficiency [7]. The rationale is economic — when a chip fields a million neurons, per-neuron complexity multiplies into die area and leakage power — but it creates a *model gap*: algorithms prototyped with rich dynamics may not transfer cleanly to silicon. One of the field's central engineering questions is how much of cortical richness can be recovered through network structure (recurrence, delays, dendritic compartments) rather than single-neuron complexity [7][9].

### 4.2 STDP: causality made into a learning rule

Spike-timing-dependent plasticity is the closest thing neuroscience has to a universal local learning law. In the canonical hippocampal preparation, if a presynaptic spike precedes the postsynaptic spike by a few milliseconds, the synapse is potentiated; if it follows, the synapse is depressed — with the magnitude decaying exponentially in the timing difference [4]. Song, Miller, and Abbott showed computationally that this asymmetric window implements *competitive* Hebbian learning: synapses compete for control of the postsynaptic spike, producing stable, bimodal weight distributions without any external supervisor [4].

The standard mathematical form is:

$$
\Delta w = \begin{cases}
A_+ \exp(-\Delta t / \tau_+) & \Delta t > 0 \quad (\text{pre before post}) \\
-A_- \exp(\Delta t / \tau_-) & \Delta t < 0 \quad (\text{post before pre})
\end{cases}
$$

> **Theorem:** Under mild regularity conditions, the surrogate gradient defines a descent direction for a smoothed relaxation of the spiking loss; as the surrogate steepness $\beta \to \infty$, the relaxed objective converges pointwise to the true loss while the gradient remains well-defined almost everywhere.
>
> **Proof sketch:** Replace each Heaviside $H(u - \vartheta)$ in the network's computational graph with a smooth approximant $\sigma_\beta(u - \vartheta)$ satisfying $\sigma_\beta \to H$ pointwise. The composed network defines a smooth function $L_\beta$ of the weights; standard backpropagation yields $\nabla L_\beta$. Pointwise convergence of the approximants implies $L_\beta \to L$ at every weight configuration where no membrane potential sits exactly at threshold — a measure-zero set under continuous weight distributions. ∎

### 4.4 Neuromorphic architectures: Loihi, TrueNorth, SpiNNaker

**Intel Loihi** is the *flexible researcher*: 128 asynchronous neuromorphic cores per chip, about one million neurons and 120 million synapses on Loihi 2 (fabricated on the Intel 4 process), with programmable neuron models, synaptic delays, dendritic compartments, and — crucially — on-chip learning engines supporting three-factor plasticity rules [7]. Loihi 2 moved to a fully event-driven, asynchronous mesh with graded (multi-bit) spike payloads, and Intel's Lava framework exposes the hardware to the broader community. The design bets that the right abstractions for SNNs are not yet known, so the chip stays programmable.

**IBM TrueNorth** is the *minimalist scaler*: 4,096 neurosynaptic cores, one million digital neurons, 256 million synapses, 5.4 billion transistors — consuming roughly 70 milliwatts, with a demonstrated 63 mW on a 400-by-240 video object-detection workload at 30 frames per second [8]. Each core is a 256-by-256 crossbar of binary synapses feeding 256 LIF neurons. The architecture is deterministic and synchronous at 1 kHz, tileable across chips, and deliberately austere: no on-chip learning, ternary-ish weights, fixed neuron model. TrueNorth proved that brain-inspired digital design could hit extraordinary efficiency, at the price of generality.

**SpiNNaker** is the *real-time simulator*: over a million ARM cores (in the full machine) designed to simulate large spiking networks in biological real time, with a custom packet-switched fabric optimized for tiny spike messages [9]. Rather than fixing a neuron model in silicon, SpiNNaker runs software neuron models on general-purpose cores — maximum flexibility, at higher energy cost than fixed-function cores. Its successor, SpiNNaker 2, adds accelerators (including MAC arrays) to close that gap while retaining programmability.

| Platform | Neurons/chip | Synapses/chip | Process | On-chip learning | Power (typical) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Intel Loihi 2 | ~1M | ~120M | Intel 4 | Yes (3-factor) | <1 W (chip) |
| IBM TrueNorth | 1M | 256M | 28 nm | No | ~70 mW |
| SpiNNaker (full) | ~1B (simulated) | ~1T (simulated) | 130 nm | Software | ~100 kW (machine) |

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Benchmark evidence: energy per inference

The most careful head-to-head comparison remains Blouw et al.'s keyword-spotting study, which ran the *same* two-layer network on Loihi and on a CPU, GPU, NVIDIA Jetson TX1, and Movidius Neural Compute Stick, measuring dynamic energy per inference with matched methodology [10]. Loihi achieved the lowest energy per inference of all platforms at equivalent accuracy, and — importantly — its advantage *grew* with network size, because event-driven execution scales with activity rather than with parameter count [10].

> **Theorem:** For a spiking network with mean firing probability $p$ per neuron per timestep, the expected number of synaptic operations per inference is $p$ times that of the equivalent dense ANN; hence event-driven hardware achieves an asymptotic energy advantage whenever $p$ is small and per-operation costs are comparable.
>
> **Proof:** In the dense ANN, each inference performs one multiply-accumulate per synapse: $N_{\text{syn}}$ operations. In the SNN, a synaptic operation occurs only when the presynaptic neuron spikes; by linearity of expectation, the expected count is $p \cdot N_{\text{syn}}$. If the neuromorphic synaptic operation costs at most $1/p$ times the GPU's per-MAC energy, the SNN wins. Measured $p$ values in trained deep SNNs routinely fall below one tenth, while Loihi-class synaptic operations cost on the order of tens of picojoules — comfortably inside the winning regime [7][10]. ∎

TrueNorth's published figures are consistent with this analysis: 46 giga-operations per second per watt-class efficiency on vision workloads, versus single-digit GOPs/W for contemporary GPUs on the same tasks [8]. And SpiNNaker demonstrated that million-core real-time simulation of cortical-scale networks is achievable, validating the many-core message-passing paradigm at brain scale [9].

Three caveats temper these numbers. First, *idle versus dynamic power*: neuromorphic chips quote dynamic energy per inference, but system-level comparisons must include leakage, I/O, and host overhead. Second, *accuracy parity*: SNNs on Loihi matched ANN accuracy on the keyword task [10], but on harder benchmarks (e.g., ImageNet) a small gap persists, and closing it costs extra timesteps — i.e., energy. Third, *benchmark selection*: keyword spotting and gesture recognition are streaming, sparse, low-latency tasks that flatter event-driven architectures; dense batch workloads flatter GPUs. Honest benchmarking requires workload diversity, and the field's benchmark suites are still maturing.

### Training deep SNNs: the state of the art

Surrogate-gradient training has closed the accuracy gap dramatically. Representative results from the literature [5][11]:

- **MNIST / N-MNIST:** >99% / >99%, at parity with ANNs.
- **CIFAR-10:** spiking ResNets reach ~93–95% with tens of timesteps, within 1–2 points of the ANN baseline.
- **CIFAR-100 / ImageNet:** recent architectures reach ~70%+ / ~70%+ top-1, with the gap narrowing yearly.
- **Speech (TIMIT, keyword spotting):** SNN acoustic models match or slightly trail ANNs while offering large projected energy savings on neuromorphic deployment [10].

The cost of this accuracy is *latency in time*: SNNs need multiple simulation timesteps per inference, trading time for the sparsity that buys energy. The emerging consensus is that the right metric is not accuracy alone but the **accuracy–latency–energy Pareto surface** — and on that surface, for streaming edge workloads, SNNs are increasingly the rational choice [5][10][11].

##6 Limitations

1. **Temporal credit assignment remains unsolved in principle.** Surrogate gradients work, but they require backpropagation through time with full state storage — biologically implausible and memory-hungry. E-prop and related eligibility-trace methods [6] are online and local but lag in performance on deep tasks. A learning rule that is simultaneously local, online, and competitive with BPTT does not yet exist.
2. **The simulation-to-silicon gap.** Most SNN research is done in floating-point simulators; deployment on fixed-point, quantized, limited-fan-in neuromorphic cores degrades accuracy. Hardware-aware training is nascent [7].
3. **Benchmarking immaturity.** There is no MLPerf-equivalent for neuromorphic computing. Published energy figures use inconsistent methodologies (chip-only vs. system-level, idle subtraction conventions), making vendor-neutral comparison difficult [10].
4. **Depth and scale.** The deepest SNNs are still shallow by ANN standards, and training instability (vanishing/exploding surrogate gradients over long unrollings) limits temporal horizons [11].
5. **Device and process immaturity.** Analog and mixed-signal neuromorphic approaches promise further efficiency but suffer from device mismatch and noise; digital approaches like Loihi and TrueNorth are manufacturable but leave biological efficiency factors on the table [7][8].
6. **Software ecosystem fragmentation.** Lava, Nengo, Brian, BindsNET, snnTorch, and SpikingJelly each cover part of the workflow; no single stack spans modeling, training, and deployment seamlessly.

##7 Conclusion

Spiking neural networks have crossed an inflection point. The neuron-model problem is solved in practice: we can simulate rich cortical dynamics at thirteen flops per neuron per millisecond [1], or strip down to linear LIF neurons for silicon [8]. The learning problem is cracked in engineering terms: surrogate gradients bring the full power of deep learning to spiking substrates [5], while STDP and e-prop keep the biologically plausible, online-learning agenda alive [4][6]. And the hardware problem has working answers: Loihi's programmable event-driven cores [7], TrueNorth's milliwatt-scale inference [8], and SpiNNaker's brain-scale real-time simulation [9] each demonstrate a different point on the efficiency–flexibility frontier, with measured benchmarks showing order-of-magnitude energy advantages on suitable workloads [10].

The thesis of this thesis, if one is needed, is that *events are the right primitive*. The brain does not multiply dense matrices on a clock; it routes sparse spikes asynchronously, and its twenty-watt budget is the existence proof that this scales to general intelligence. Whether the path forward runs through better surrogate methods, local three-factor rules, or architectures not yet imagined, the destination is the same: computing that spends energy only where information actually flows. The field's open problems — principled temporal credit assignment, honest benchmarking, and closing the simulation-to-silicon gap — are substantial, but they are now engineering problems rather than impossibilities. The third generation of neural network models has come of age.
