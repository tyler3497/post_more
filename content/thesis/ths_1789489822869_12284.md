# Single-Particle Cryo-EM: Maximum-Likelihood 3D Reconstruction, Fourier Shell Correlation, and the Resolution Revolution

## Abstract

Single-particle cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) determines the three-dimensional structure of a macromolecule from hundreds of thousands of extremely noisy two-dimensional projection images of individual particles frozen in random orientations in vitreous ice. Between 2012 and 2017 the method underwent a *resolution revolution* [5] that carried it from low-resolution "blobology" to routine near-atomic resolution, a transformation recognized by the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry [10]. This thesis develops the mathematical and computational foundations of the technique: vitrified specimen preparation and the image-formation model under the weak-phase approximation, the contrast transfer function (CTF) and its estimation and correction, maximum-likelihood 3D reconstruction with expectation--maximization and Bayesian regularization as implemented in RELION [3], and the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (FSC) framework that prevents overfitting and anchors the FSC = 0.143 resolution criterion [4]. We trace how direct electron detectors and beam-induced motion correction enabled landmark structures such as the TRPV1 ion channel [6] and the ribosome at near-atomic resolution [7], and we survey the modern frontier of heterogeneous reconstruction, from discrete 3D classification to continuous neural latent-space models such as cryoDRGN [9].

## 1 Introduction

Structural biology asks a deceptively simple question: given a purified solution of a macromolecule, what is the three-dimensional arrangement of its atoms? For half a century the dominant answer was X-ray crystallography, whose atomic models demanded well-ordered crystals that many of biology's most interesting machines — membrane channels, the ribosome, the spliceosome — stubbornly refused to form. Electron microscopy offered an alternative: image the molecule directly. But electrons damage biological matter, so each particle image is dominated by noise. The single-particle paradigm turns this weakness into a statistical strength: instead of one perfect image, collect hundreds of thousands of noisy two-dimensional projections of identical particles in random orientations, estimate each particle's unknown orientation, and combine them into a consistent three-dimensional density.

The intellectual lineage is long: Henderson and Unwin's 1975 bacteriorhodopsin model at 7 Å proved that electrons could image unstained protein; Dubochet's vitrification [1] froze specimens in glass-like amorphous ice; and Frank's computational machinery aligned and averaged thousands of noisy projections. Yet through the 2000s, reconstructions stalled at tens of angstroms — the era ruefully called *blobology* [5]. Three developments broke the stalemate: direct electron detectors recording movies rather than single frames, so beam-induced motion could be measured and corrected; maximum-likelihood and Bayesian algorithms — above all RELION [3] and cryoSPARC [8] — with the gold-standard refinement protocol that made resolution estimates trustworthy [4]; and rigorous validation via independent half-set refinements and the FSC = 0.143 criterion. The watershed arrived in 2013: Liao and colleagues reported the TRPV1 ion channel at 3.4 Å from single particles alone [6], and Bai and colleagues reached near-atomic resolution for the ribosome from only about thirty thousand particles [7]. In 2017 the Nobel Prize in Chemistry honored Dubochet, Frank, and Henderson for developing cryo-EM for high-resolution structure determination of biomolecules in solution [10].

This thesis reconstructs how the method works. Section 2 covers the physical and mathematical background [1][2]; Section 3 the processing pipeline from movie to map; Section 4 the four technical pillars — Fourier geometry, the regularized likelihood objective, CTF correction, and heterogeneous reconstruction; Section 5 the empirical record and the key theorems; Section 6 the limitations; Section 7 the outlook.

## 2 Background

**Vitrified specimens.** Biological matter imaged at room temperature in the vacuum of an electron microscope dehydrates and is destroyed by the beam. Dubochet and colleagues showed that plunging a thin aqueous film into liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen freezes water so rapidly — cooling rates on the order of one hundred thousand kelvin per second — that it vitrifies into amorphous ice, preserving the specimen in a near-native hydrated state without crystalline diffraction [1]. The particles are trapped in random orientations in vitreous ice a few hundred angstroms thick. Because freezing is effectively instantaneous on molecular timescales, distinct conformational states of a dynamic machine are captured side by side — the foundation of heterogeneous reconstruction.

**The dose problem and the theoretical limit.** Ionizing radiation breaks bonds: a dose of a few electrons per square angstrom already degrades the high-resolution content of a biological specimen. A cryo-EM exposure is therefore limited to roughly 20--60 electrons per square angstrom in total, spread across a movie of frames. Henderson analyzed this fundamental trade-off in 1995, calculating that the radiation-damage limit in principle permits 3 Å resolution for particles of about 38 kDa and larger, given perfect detectors and perfect image processing [2]. For two decades the field fell far short of that bound, limited not by physics but by detectors and algorithms.

**Image formation.** A thin biological specimen is to an excellent approximation a *weak-phase object*: the electron wave's amplitude is barely attenuated, but its phase is shifted in proportion to the specimen's projected electrostatic potential. Defocusing the objective lens converts these phase shifts into visible amplitude contrast. In Fourier space the observed image is, up to noise, the product of the particle's projection transform and the contrast transfer function:

$$\mathrm{CTF}(k) = -\sin\big(\pi\lambda\,\Delta z\,k^2 - \tfrac{\pi}{2}C_s\lambda^3 k^4 + \phi\big),$$

where $k$ is the spatial frequency, $\lambda$ the electron wavelength, $\Delta z$ the defocus, $C_s$ the spherical aberration coefficient, and $\phi$ an additional phase shift. The CTF oscillates, passing through zero at a series of spatial frequencies and reversing contrast between them; its squared modulus is further multiplied by envelope functions describing partial spatial and temporal coherence, specimen drift, and the detector's modulation transfer function. Because the CTF annihilates information at its zeros, micrographs are recorded across a defocus range so that the zeros of one image are covered by the passbands of another.

**The projection-slice theorem.** The mathematical heart of all tomographic reconstruction is the Fourier slice theorem: the two-dimensional Fourier transform of a projection of a three-dimensional object equals a central slice through the object's three-dimensional Fourier transform, oriented perpendicular to the projection direction. Reconstruction is therefore, in principle, the inverse problem of assembling a 3D Fourier volume from many 2D slices of unknown orientation — *unknown*, because each particle lies in the ice at a random, unmeasured rotation.

## 3 Methodology

A modern single-particle pipeline is a chain of statistical estimation problems. The canonical stages are:

1. **Specimen preparation and vitrification.** Purified complex is applied to a grid, blotted to a thin film, and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane [1]. Ice thickness and air--water interface behavior dominate downstream success.
2. **Automated data collection.** Software drives the microscope to acquire thousands of movies across a defocus range, typically 0.5--3.0 micrometers underfocus.
3. **Motion correction and dose weighting.** Direct detectors record each exposure as a movie of frames. Beam-induced motion is estimated by aligning frames, which are summed with dose-dependent weights that downweight later, more damaged frames.
4. **CTF estimation.** The power spectrum of each micrograph shows concentric *Thon rings* — the squared CTF ringing against the background. Fitting software estimates defocus, astigmatism, and phase shift per micrograph.
5. **Particle picking.** Candidate positions are located by template matching or trained neural pickers, yielding hundreds of thousands of boxed particle images.
6. **Two-dimensional classification.** Particles are aligned and clustered into class averages by multi-reference maximum-likelihood alignment [3], which boosts the signal-to-noise ratio and discards contaminants, ice artifacts, and damaged particles.
7. **Ab initio reconstruction and 3D classification.** A low-resolution initial model is built without a reference — by stochastic gradient descent as in cryoSPARC [8] — and the particle set is partitioned into conformational or compositional classes.
8. **High-resolution 3D refinement.** Orientations and translations are refined against the current best map by maximizing the regularized likelihood; the two half-sets are refined independently under the gold-standard protocol [4].
9. **Bayesian polishing.** Per-particle motion trajectories, per-frame B-factors, and per-particle CTF parameters are estimated from the data themselves.
10. **Post-processing.** The final map is sharpened with a negative B-factor from the Guinier plot, masked to remove solvent noise, and its resolution is reported from the masked half-map FSC at the 0.143 threshold [4].

RELION frames this as maximum *a posteriori* estimation: the log-likelihood of all particle images given the 3D map, marginalized over each particle's unknown orientation and translation, plus a data-estimated prior on the map's power spectrum that regularizes high-frequency noise [3]. Expectation--maximization alternates between computing each particle's posterior over sampled orientations (the E-step) and updating the map by weighted back-projection damped by the prior (the M-step). Because orientations are marginalized rather than discretely assigned, the algorithm degrades gracefully at low signal-to-noise ratio, where hard projection matching accumulates correlated alignment error and noise bias.

Overfitting is the existential threat of this procedure: with enough parameters, any algorithm can align pure noise into a plausible-looking map — the notorious *Einstein-from-noise* effect.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Fourier Geometry of Projection and Reconstruction

The slice theorem reduces reconstruction to a problem in Fourier space.

$$P_R(x, y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} V(R^{-1}(x, y, z))\,dz,$$

and its 2D Fourier transform $\hat{P}_R(k_x, k_y)$ equals the slice $\hat{V}(R^{-1}(k_x, k_y, 0))$ through the 3D transform. Unlike tomography, single-particle data have no missing wedge: random orientations cover the full sphere of projection directions, so the Fourier volume is sampled isotropically — provided the orientation distribution is uniform. When particles adsorb to the air--water interface in preferred orientations, whole regions of Fourier space go unsampled, and the map suffers directional resolution loss that no algorithm can fully repair.

A minimal implementation of the resolution metric itself is instructive.

```python
import numpy as np

def fourier_shell_correlation(map1, map2, apix=1.0):
    """Gold-standard FSC between two independently refined half-maps."""
    f1 = np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fftn(map1))
    f2 = np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fftn(map2))
    n = map1.shape[0]
    grids = [np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fftfreq(n))] * 3
    kx, ky, kz = np.meshgrid(*grids, indexing="ij")
    shells = np.floor(np.sqrt(kx**2 + ky**2 + kz**2) * n).astype(int)
    fsc, resolutions = [], []
    for s in range(1, n // 2):
        mask = shells == s
        num = np.sum((f1[mask] * np.conj(f2[mask])).real)
        den = np.sqrt(np.sum(np.abs(f1[mask])**2) * np.sum(np.abs(f2[mask])**2))
        fsc.append(num / den)
        resolutions.append(n * apix / s)  # Angstrom
    return np.array(resolutions), np.array(fsc)
```

The resolution is reported as the first shell where this curve drops below 0.143 [4].

### 4.2 The Regularized Likelihood Objective and the Expectation--Maximization Engine

RELION's contribution [3] was to replace heuristic alignment scores with a proper generative model.

$$P(X_i \mid V, \phi_i, t_i, \sigma) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{\|X_i - \mathrm{CTF}_i \cdot P_{\phi_i, t_i} V\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).$$

The marginal likelihood integrates out the nuisance variables:

$$\mathcal{L}(V, \sigma) = \prod_i \int P(X_i \mid V, \phi, t, \sigma)\,p(\phi, t)\,d\phi\,dt,$$

and the MAP objective adds a prior $p(V)$ encoding the belief that the true signal's power spectrum falls off smoothly while noise does not — in practice a Gaussian prior whose variance $\tau^2(k)$ at each spatial frequency is estimated from the data themselves, which is what makes the approach *empirical Bayes* [3].

This marginalization is the decisive advantage over projection matching.

The functional structure of one EM iteration can be sketched in a pure functional language, which makes the separation of concerns crisp:

```haskell
-- One EM iteration: the E-step accumulates weighted back-projections
-- under the orientation posterior; the M-step solves the regularized
-- normal equations. The prior damps frequencies where signal < noise.
emStep :: PriorSpectrum -> [ParticleImage] -> Volume -> (Volume, NoiseModel)
emStep prior imgs vol =
  let stats = foldMap (eStep vol) imgs   -- sufficient statistics, parallelizable
      vol'  = mStep prior stats          -- argmax of the log-posterior
      noise = estimateNoise vol' imgs    -- re-estimate sigma^2 per shell
  in (vol', noise)
```

> **Theorem (Gold-standard protection against overfitting).** Let $H_1, H_2$ be two disjoint random halves of the particle set, refined independently with no shared parameters, references, or alignment information. Then the expected Fourier shell correlation between the two resulting half-maps at any shell dominated by noise is zero, and any systematic positive correlation measures signal present independently in both halves.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Overfitting is, by definition, the fitting of noise in the data to the reference. Because the noise realizations in $H_1$ and $H_2$ are independent and the refinements exchange no information, the noise components of the two half-maps are independent random fields with zero mean; their shell cross-correlation has expectation zero by the independence of the underlying noise. Signal, being the common underlying density, correlates. Hence positive FSC can only arise from genuine signal, up to the statistical fluctuations quantified by the number of Fourier voxels per shell. ∎

A tiny dose-weighting kernel in Rust illustrates the kind of per-frame computation that runs billions of times during polishing:

```rust
/// Accumulate one movie frame with dose-dependent weight.
/// Later frames carry more radiation damage, modeled here as a
/// resolution-dependent B-factor that grows with accumulated dose.
fn dose_weight(frame: &[f32], dose: f32, k2: f32, out: &mut [f32]) {
    let b_factor = 8.0 * dose; // empirical slope, e-/A^2 -> A^2
    let w = (-0.25 * b_factor * k2).exp();
    for (o, &x) in out.iter_mut().zip(frame.iter()) {
        *o += w * x;
    }
}
```

### 4.3 The Contrast Transfer Function: From Thon Rings to Wiener Restoration

The CTF is both the source of phase contrast and the great corrupter of high-resolution information. Its zeros annihilate entire resolution shells in any single image, and its sign reversals flip contrast between them; correction therefore combines phase flipping with Wiener filtering, and post-processing compensates residual amplitude falloff by B-factor sharpening, validated by the FSC [3][4].

| Parameter | Symbol | Typical value | Role in reconstruction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electron wavelength (300 kV) | $\lambda$ | 0.0197 Å | Sets the CTF oscillation rate |
| Defocus range | $\Delta z$ | 0.5--3.0 µm | Trades contrast against CTF zero positions |
| Spherical aberration | $C_s$ | 2.7 mm (uncorrected) | Adds the quartic term to the CTF phase |
| Total exposure | $D$ | 20--60 e⁻/Å² | Bounded by radiation damage |
| Pixel size | — | 0.5--1.5 Å | Must satisfy Nyquist for target resolution |
| Ice thickness | — | 200--800 Å | Thicker ice adds background noise |

For completeness, the gold-standard pipeline's central invariant can be stated as a formal specification.

```tla
---------------------------- MODULE GoldStandard ----------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANT Particles
VARIABLES halfA, halfB, exchanged
Init == /\ halfA \in SUBSET Particles
        /\ halfB = Particles \ halfA
        /\ exchanged = FALSE
Refine(h) == UNCHANGED <<halfA, halfB, exchanged>>  \* local MAP refinement
CrossTalk == exchanged' = TRUE   \* forbidden: any shared alignment info
Next == \/ \E h \in {halfA, halfB} : Refine(h)
        \/ CrossTalk
\* The safety property the FSC unbiasedness theorem requires:
Invariant == halfA \cap halfB = {} /\ exchanged = FALSE
=============================================================================
```

### 4.4 Heterogeneity: From Discrete Classes to Continuous Neural Fields

A frozen specimen is an ensemble, not a single structure. Discrete 3D classification extends the likelihood model to a mixture of $K$ volumes, but fails for continuous motion, where no finite $K$ suffices and splitting particles starves each class of signal. Multi-body refinement models a few rigid bodies rotating relative to one another; neural approaches go further. CryoDRGN trains a variational autoencoder whose decoder is a coordinate-based network representing density conditioned on a latent variable, combining exact inference over orientations with variational inference over conformation [9]. Applied to the ribosome and the spliceosome, it recovered heterogeneity invisible to discrete classification and revealed previously unobserved states [9].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

> **Theorem (Fourier slice theorem).** Let $P_\theta$ be the projection of $V$ along direction $\theta$. Then $\widehat{P_\theta}(k_x, k_y) = \hat{V}(R_\theta^{-1}(k_x, k_y, 0))$.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Write the projection as the line integral along $z$ after rotating the volume, take the 2D Fourier transform, and exchange the order of integration. The $z$-integral becomes the Fourier transform evaluated at $k_z = 0$, i.e., a central slice through the 3D transform perpendicular to the projection direction. ∎

> **Theorem (Rosenthal--Henderson resolution criterion).** For two independently refined half-maps, the spatial frequency at which the FSC equals 0.143 estimates the resolution at which the correlation between the combined full map and the unknown ideal structure equals 0.5.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Under the assumption that signal and noise are uncorrelated and the noise is independent between halves, the FSC between half-maps, $F_{1/2}$, relates to the correlation $C$ between the full map and the ideal map by $C = \sqrt{2F_{1/2}/(1 + F_{1/2})}$. Setting $C = 0.5$ — the crystallographic convention for an interpretable map — and solving gives $F_{1/2} = 0.143$ [4]. ∎

The empirical record vindicates the theory. Rosenthal and Henderson showed that resolution improves with the logarithm of particle number, the slope set by an overall B-factor describing alignment accuracy and specimen quality; extrapolation of $\ln(N)$ plots predicts the particle count for a target resolution [4]. The landmark structures trace a clear arc:

| Structure | Year | Resolution | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bacteriorhodopsin (2D crystals) | 1975/1990 | 7 Å → 3.5 Å | Proof that electrons could image unstained protein |
| TRPV1 ion channel [6] | 2013 | 3.4 Å | First near-atomic membrane protein by single particle |
| 80S ribosome [7] | 2013 | ~4.5 Å | Near-atomic from only ~30,000 particles |
| Mammalian mitoribosome | 2014 | 3.2 Å | De novo model building in cryo-EM maps |
| Human hemoglobin | 2017 | 3.2 Å | 64 kDa, approaching Henderson's 38 kDa bound [2] |
| Apoferritin (modern) | 2020s | < 2 Å | Routine atomic resolution; ordered water visible |

Each row was enabled by the full stack: detectors, motion correction, likelihood refinement, and gold-standard validation. The TRPV1 result [6] is the canonical demonstration — a tetrameric membrane protein, previously intractable, resolved to 3.4 Å with visible side chains. The ribosome work [7] showed Bayesian refinement extracting near-atomic detail from an order of magnitude fewer particles than earlier methods required, because marginalization uses data more efficiently than hard orientation assignment. GPU-accelerated implementations made such refinements routine [3], and cryoSPARC showed that stochastic optimization could deliver ab initio structures in hours [8].

## 6 Limitations

The method's power should not obscure its boundaries. **Preferred orientation** remains the most common failure mode: many complexes adsorb to the air--water interface in a handful of poses, leaving Fourier space anisotropically sampled and the map directionally blurred. **The size floor** stands near 50 kDa for asymmetric particles, though rigidly bound scaffolds — DARPins, nanobodies, antibody fragments — are pushing it down. **Radiation damage** is fundamental: no algorithm recovers information the beam destroyed. **Model bias** is the perennial epistemic hazard; the defenses are gold-standard halves [4], phase-randomized FSC tests, and tilt-pair validation. **Heterogeneity** is underdetermined in principle: finite data cannot distinguish arbitrarily fine continuous motion from noise, and neural methods [9] trade expressiveness for validation difficulty. **Computational cost** is nontrivial: million-particle datasets consume terabytes and thousands of GPU-hours.

## 7 Conclusion

Single-particle cryo-EM has traveled from 7 Å blobs of bacteriorhodopsin to atomic maps in which ordered water molecules are visible, fulfilling the promise Henderson calculated in 1995 [2] and earning the 2017 Nobel Prize [10]. The transformation was the compounding of physical insight — vitrification [1] — with statistical rigor — maximum-likelihood reconstruction [3] and the gold-standard FSC [4] — and with instrumentation — direct detectors and motion correction that together constituted the resolution revolution [5]. The frontier has moved from *whether* a structure can be determined to *which* structures in the ensemble are present and how they interconvert, with neural latent-variable models [9] beginning to render molecular movies rather than molecular statues. The next decade belongs to in situ tomography and time-resolved experiments — but the core will remain noisy projections, principled inference, and honest validation.
