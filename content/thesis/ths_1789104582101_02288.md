# Channel Polarization and Successive Cancellation Decoding: From Arikan's Capacity-Achieving Construction to Polar Codes in 5G NR Control Channels

## Abstract

Polar codes, introduced by Erdal Arıkan in 2008–2009, were the first explicitly constructed family of codes provably achieving the symmetric capacity of any binary-input discrete memoryless channel with $O(N \log N)$ encoding and decoding complexity [1]. The central mechanism — channel polarization — recursively synthesizes $N$ virtual bit-channels from $N$ uses of a physical channel $W$ such that, as $N \to \infty$, a fraction $I(W)$ become asymptotically noiseless while the rest become pure-noise; data on the former and frozen bits on the latter attains capacity under successive cancellation (SC) decoding [1]. Arıkan and Telatar bounded block error as $2^{-N^{\beta}}$ for $\beta < 1/2$ [2], while Korada and Urbanke characterized the polarization exponent and showed larger kernels can exceed $1/2$ [3]. Plain polar codes underperform at practical lengths, but successive cancellation list (SCL) decoding and CRC-aided concatenation reversed this verdict, propelling polar codes into 5G New Radio, where TS 38.212 mandates polar coding for all control channels [4]. This thesis develops the polarization mathematics, SC/SCL algorithms, the 5G rate-matching profile, and the construction's empirical performance and limitations.

## 1. Introduction

Before 2009, information theory lived with a conspicuous gap. Shannon's noisy-channel coding theorem (1948) proved that capacity-achieving codes *exist*, and two practical families — turbo codes (1993) and low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes — approached capacity empirically, but no explicit construction with *proven* capacity achievement *and* polynomial decoding complexity existed for the general binary-input discrete memoryless channel (B-DMC) [1]. Random linear codes achieved capacity but lacked tractable decoders; Reed–Muller codes had structure but no capacity proof.

Arıkan's channel polarization closed this gap in one move [1]. Rather than fighting channel noise uniformly, one can *redistribute* reliability: a recursive transform synthesizes $N$ virtual bit-channels $\{W_N^{(i)}\}$ from $N$ copies of $W$ whose capacities polarize toward 0 or 1, with $\sum_i I(W_N^{(i)}) = N \cdot I(W)$ conserved. As $N$ grows, the fraction of near-perfect indices converges to $I(W)$. The coding strategy is then simple: place information on the $K \approx N \cdot I(W)$ most reliable channels, freeze the rest, and decode sequentially with a **successive cancellation** decoder of complexity $O(N \log N)$.

> **Theorem (Channel Polarization, Arıkan 2009 [1]):** For any B-DMC $W$ and any $\delta > 0$,
> $$\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{2^n}\left|\left\{i : I\!\left(W_{2^n}^{(i)}\right) \in (1-\delta, 1]\right\}\right| = I(W),$$
> and the complementary fraction of indices with $I(W_{2^n}^{(i)}) \in [0, \delta)$ converges to $1 - I(W)$.

The practical arc of polar codes is equally instructive. At short-to-moderate block lengths — precisely the regime of control signaling — plain SC decoding is mediocre: the error exponent $\beta < 1/2$ means convergence to capacity is *slow* [2]. Two remedies proved decisive. First, **SCL decoding** retains a list of $L$ candidate decoding paths instead of committing greedily at each bit, recovering most of the gap to maximum-likelihood performance [5]. Second, concatenating an outer **cyclic redundancy check (CRC)** — the CA-polar scheme — lets the decoder select the correct path from the list, and in the 5G NR profile the CRC bits are *distributed* within the polar input vector to enable path pruning and early termination [6]. The combination yields the strongest known finite-length performance among standardized codes at lengths of a few hundred bits, which is exactly why 3GPP selected polar codes for the control plane of 5G NR [4].

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews B-DMCs, the Bhattacharyya parameter, and the recursive transform. Section 3 presents the methodology: the one-step polarization transform, its $N$-fold recursion, and frozen-set selection. Section 4 deep-dives into successive cancellation decoding, list decoding, CRC-aided constructions, and the 5G NR rate-matching profile. Section 5 states the key theorems and empirical performance evidence. Section 6 discusses limitations — minimum-distance weakness, SC error propagation, non-universality — and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Binary-Input Discrete Memoryless Channels

A **binary-input discrete memoryless channel (B-DMC)** $W : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ maps input alphabet $\mathcal{X} = \{0,1\}$ to a discrete output alphabet $\mathcal{Y}$ with transition probabilities $W(y|x)$, used independently at each channel use. The **symmetric capacity** is

$$I(W) = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \sum_{x \in \{0,1\}} \tfrac{1}{2} W(y|x) \log_2 \frac{W(y|x)}{\tfrac{1}{2}W(y|0) + \tfrac{1}{2}W(y|1)},$$

the mutual information under uniform input. A companion reliability measure is the **Bhattacharyya parameter**

$$Z(W) = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \sqrt{W(y|0)\,W(y|1)} \in [0,1],$$

which upper-bounds the maximum-likelihood decision error for one uncoded bit: $Z(W) \approx 0$ means a near-perfect channel, $Z(W) \approx 1$ a near-useless one. $I(W)$ and $Z(W)$ satisfy $I(W) + Z(W) \ge 1$ and $I(W)^2 + Z(W)^2 \le 1$, so polarization of one implies polarization of the other.

### 2.2 The $2 \times 2$ Kernel and Reed–Muller Connections

Arıkan's recursion is built on the kernel $F = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, whose $n$-fold Kronecker power $G_N = F^{\otimes n}$ (with a bit-reversal permutation) is the polar generator matrix [1]. Notably, the row space of $F^{\otimes n}$ is closely related to Reed–Muller codes: $RM(r,n)$ is the span of rows of $F^{\otimes n}$ with Hamming weight at least $2^{n-r}$, whereas polar codes select rows by *channel reliability* rather than weight [3] — the information-theoretic selection rule that unlocks capacity.

Key properties of the construction used throughout:

- **Encoding complexity:** $x_1^N = u_1^N G_N$ is computed by the Kronecker recursion in $O(N \log N)$ operations.
- **Block length constraint:** classical polar codes require $N = 2^n$; arbitrary lengths need puncturing/shortening (see §4.4).
- **Channel dependence:** the frozen set must be tailored to $W$; the construction is not *universal* — a single frozen set does not achieve compound capacity across all B-DMCs [8].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The One-Step Polarization Transform

Consider two independent copies of $W$. Combining them into the pair of synthesized channels $(W^-, W^+)$ via the kernel $F$ defines, for $u_1, u_2 \in \{0,1\}$ and $(y_1,y_2) \in \mathcal{Y}^2$:

$$W^-(y_1, y_2 \mid u_1) = \sum_{u_2 \in \{0,1\}} \tfrac{1}{2} \, W(y_1 \mid u_1 \oplus u_2)\, W(y_2 \mid u_2),$$

$$W^+(y_1, y_2, u_1 \mid u_2) = \tfrac{1}{2} \, W(y_1 \mid u_1 \oplus u_2)\, W(y_2 \mid u_2).$$

Here $W^-$ is the "worse" channel (decoding $u_1$ without knowing $u_2$) and $W^+$ the "better" channel (decoding $u_2$ given $u_1$ as side information). The transform conserves total capacity — $I(W^-) + I(W^+) = 2I(W)$ — while *spreading* reliability apart: $I(W^-) \le I(W) \le I(W^+)$, with strict inequality unless $W$ is already extreme [1]. For the binary erasure channel $BEC(\varepsilon)$, the parameters evolve exactly as $\varepsilon^- = 2\varepsilon - \varepsilon^2$ and $\varepsilon^+ = \varepsilon^2$, a clean closed form that makes the BEC the canonical laboratory for polarization analysis.

### 3.2 Recursive Synthesis and the Martingale Argument

Recursive application yields the tree of $2^n$ channels $W_N^{(i)}$. Arıkan's proof rests on a martingale argument [1]:

1. Let $B_1, B_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. fair bits choosing the "$-$" or "$+$" branch at each recursion level, and define the random channel process $W_n = W_{n-1}^{B_n}$ with $W_0 = W$.
2. Then $\{I(W_n)\}$ is a bounded **martingale**, converging almost surely to a $\{0,1\}$-valued random variable $I_\infty$ with $\mathbb{E}[I_\infty] = I(W_0) = I(W)$.
3. Polarization of $Z(W_n)$ (and hence $I(W_n)$) follows from showing the process is "polarizing": it is absorbed at the extremes because $Z(W^+) = Z(W)^2$ contracts superlinearly on good branches while $Z(W^-) \le 2Z(W) - Z(W)^2$ keeps bad branches from recovering.

The speed of absorption is governed by the **error exponent** $\beta$: Arıkan and Telatar proved $P_e \le 2^{-N^\beta}$ for any $\beta < 1/2$ under SC decoding [2]. The exponent $1/2$ is intrinsic to the $2 \times 2$ kernel; Korada and Urbanke showed that any $\ell \times \ell$ kernel none of whose column permutations is upper triangular polarizes, and that kernels of size at least $16$ (e.g., based on BCH codes) achieve exponents arbitrarily close to $1$ [3].

### 3.3 Frozen-Set Selection

Given target rate $R < I(W)$, the **frozen set** $\mathcal{F} \subset \{1,\dots,N\}$ is chosen as the $N - K$ indices with the largest $Z(W_N^{(i)})$ (least reliable). Practical construction methods include:

| Method | Principle | Accuracy / Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Bhattacharyya recursion | Exact evolution on BEC; bound elsewhere | Exact for BEC only |
| Density evolution | Track LLR densities through $f, g$ updates | Accurate; $O(N \cdot \text{bins})$ |
| Gaussian approximation (GA) | Assume LLRs Gaussian; track means only | Good; $O(N)$ |
| Monte Carlo simulation | Simulate SC decoder, rank by empirical error | Universal; expensive |
| 5G reliability sequence | Fixed, channel-independent ordering $Q_0^{N_{\max}-1}$ | Standardized [4] |

5G NR instead specifies a *universal* reliability sequence $Q_0^{1023}$ ($N_{\max} = 1024$), from which any code's sets are extracted by sub-sampling [4] — trading a fraction of a decibel of optimality for a deterministic, hardware-friendly construction.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Successive Cancellation Decoding

The SC decoder estimates the input bits $\hat{u}_1^N$ sequentially: at step $i$, it decides $\hat{u}_i$ from the channel output $y_1^N$ and the *previous* decisions $\hat{u}_1^{i-1}$ using the synthesized channel $W_N^{(i)}$, freezing $\hat{u}_i = 0$ for $i \in \mathcal{F}$. The recursion is implemented on the factor graph with two message updates on log-likelihood ratios (LLRs):

```python
import numpy as np

def f(a: np.ndarray, b: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Upper (minus) node update: LLR of u1 given (y1, y2)."""
    # min-sum approximation of 2*atanh(tanh(a/2)*tanh(b/2))
    return np.sign(a) * np.sign(b) * np.minimum(np.abs(a), np.abs(b))

def g(a: np.ndarray, b: np.ndarray, u_hat: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Lower (plus) node update: LLR of u2 given (y1, y2, u1_hat)."""
    return b + (1 - 2 * u_hat) * a

def sc_decode(llr: np.ndarray, frozen: set) -> np.ndarray:
    """Recursive SC decoder. llr: channel LLRs of length N = 2^n."""
    n = len(llr)
    if n == 1:
        return np.array([0]) if 0 in frozen else np.array([int(llr[0] < 0)])
    a, b = llr[:n//2], llr[n//2:]
    u1 = sc_decode(f(a, b), {i for i in frozen if i < n//2} ... )  # left subtree
    u2 = sc_decode(g(a, b, u1), ...)                               # right subtree
    x = np.empty(n, dtype=int); x[::2] = u1 ^ u2; x[1::2] = u2
    return np.concatenate([u1, u2])
```

The decoder does $O(\log N)$ work per leaf: $O(N \log N)$ time, $O(N)$ memory [1]. The union bound $P_e \le \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} Z(W_N^{(i)})$ is minimized by selecting $\mathcal{I}$ as the $K$ smallest-$Z$ indices.

> **Theorem (SC achieves capacity [1]):** For any B-DMC $W$ with $I(W) > 0$ and any $R < I(W)$, there exists a sequence of polar codes of rate $\ge R$ with block error under SC decoding bounded by $P_e(N, R) \le \mathcal{O}(N^{-1/4})$, with $O(N \log N)$ encoder/decoder complexity.

The Achilles' heel is **error propagation**: one wrong hard decision corrupts all subsequent $g$-updates. This motivated list decoding.

### 4.2 Successive Cancellation List (SCL) Decoding

SCL decoding maintains up to $L$ candidate paths, forking each information bit into both hypotheses and pruning to the $L$ best by a **path metric** [5]:

$$\mathrm{PM}_i^{(\ell)} = \mathrm{PM}_{i-1}^{(\ell)} + \begin{cases} 0 & \hat{u}_i^{(\ell)} = \mathrm{HD}(\lambda_i^{(\ell)}) \ |\lambda_i^{(\ell)}| & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Complexity rises to $O(L N \log N)$ with $O(LN)$ memory; practical hardware uses $L \in \{2, 4, 8\}$.

*Ordered comparison of decoding strategies:*

1. **SC ($L=1$):** greedy, $O(N \log N)$; optimal asymptotically but weak at short lengths.
2. **SCL with small $L$:** near-ML for moderate $N$; the standard workhorse [5].
3. **CRC-aided SCL (CA-SCL):** list decoded first, then CRC selects the surviving path — closes the remaining gap [5][6].
4. **GRAND / ML variants:** optimal but exponential in general; of theoretical interest [5].

```haskell
-- Sketch: path metric update in a functional SCL decoder
data Path = Path { metric :: Double, bits :: [Bit] }

extendPath :: Double -> Path -> [Path]
extendPath llr p =
  [ p { metric = metric p + penalty 0 llr, bits = bits p ++ [B0] }
  , p { metric = metric p + penalty 1 llr, bits = bits p ++ [B1] } ]
  where penalty b l = if (l < 0) == (b == B1) then 0 else abs l

prune :: Int -> [Path] -> [Path]
prune l paths = take l (sortOn metric (concatMap (extendPath 0) paths))
```

### 4.3 CRC-Aided Polar Codes

Plain polar codes have poor **minimum distance** at finite lengths, so list decoding alone gains little: the true codeword often falls off the list *or* a wrong list member wins. Concatenating an outer CRC changes the game: the CRC both *increases* effective minimum distance and provides a selection criterion among the $L$ candidates [5][6]. The 5G NR design goes further with a **distributed CRC**: instead of appending all CRC bits at the end, they are interleaved among the information positions so that each CRC bit becomes available to the SCL decoder *during* the sequential pass, enabling:

- **Path pruning:** paths failing a partial CRC check are killed early, freeing list slots;
- **Early termination:** decoding can abort when all paths fail, saving power [6].

For downlink control, 3GPP specifies a 24-bit CRC (CRC24C); uplink uses CRC16/CRC11 variants depending on payload size $A$ [4]. CA-polar is the direct reason 5G control channels meet $10^{-5}$–$10^{-6}$ block error targets at SNRs where plain polar codes would fail by decibels [5].

### 4.4 The 5G NR Polar Coding Chain (TS 38.212)

The standardized DCI/PDCCH chain is a five-stage pipeline [4]:

```
DCI payload (A bits)
  → CRC attachment (24-bit CRC24C, RNTI-masked)
  → Polar encoding (mother code N = 2^n, N ≤ 512 DL / 1024 max)
  → Sub-block interleaving (32 sub-blocks, triangular interleaver)
  → Rate matching (puncturing / shortening / repetition to E bits)
  → Channel interleaver (only for specific cases) → PDCCH mapping
```

Key standardized choices:

- **Mother code length:** $N = 2^{\lceil \log_2 E \rceil}$ capped at $n_{\max} = 9$ ($N = 512$) for downlink; $n_{\max} = 10$ ($N = 1024$) for uplink with larger payloads [4].
- **Rate matching:** if $E < N$, the code is *punctured* (for low rates) or *shortened* (for high rates, where the shortened bits are known zeros); if $E > N$, repetition is applied. The frozen set is re-derived from the reliability sequence excluding incapable/shortened positions.
- **Sub-block interleaver:** a 32-column triangular interleaver spreads burst errors before rate matching, decoupling the puncturing pattern from the polarization structure.
- **Uplink control (PUCCH/PUSCH UCI):** $12 \le K \le 19$ use shortened Reed–Muller codes; $K \ge 20$ switch to the polar chain with $n_{\max} = 10$ [4].

The following Rust sketch captures the rate-matching decision logic:

```rust
/// Select polar rate-matching mode per 3GPP TS 38.212 §5.4.1
enum RateMatch { Puncturing, Shortening, Repetition }

fn rate_match_mode(k: usize, e: usize) -> (usize, RateMatch) {
    // mother code length: smallest power of two >= e, capped by n_max
    let n = e.next_power_of_two().min(1 << 9); // DL: n_max = 9
    let r = k as f64 / e as f64;               // effective code rate
    let mode = if e >= n {
        RateMatch::Repetition
    } else if r <= 7.0 / 16.0 {
        RateMatch::Puncturing   // low rate: puncture first P = N - E bits
    } else {
        RateMatch::Shortening  // high rate: shorten last S = N - E bits
    };
    (n, mode)
}
```

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The Core Theorems

Three results form the theoretical backbone of polar coding:

> **Theorem 1 (Polarization [1]):** As $N = 2^n \to \infty$, the synthesized channels polarize: a fraction $I(W)$ become perfect and $1 - I(W)$ become useless, with total capacity conserved.

> **Theorem 2 (Capacity under SC [1]):** Polar codes with SC decoding achieve symmetric capacity $I(W)$ for any B-DMC, with $O(N \log N)$ complexity and block error $P_e \le \mathcal{O}(N^{-1/4})$.

> **Theorem 3 (Error exponent [2]):** The block error satisfies $P_e \le 2^{-N^{\beta}}$ for any $\beta < 1/2$; the exponent $1/2$ is optimal for the $2 \times 2$ kernel but can be exceeded with larger kernels, reaching arbitrarily close to $1$ with BCH-based kernels [3].

Theorem 2 follows from the union bound $\Pr[\text{error}] \le \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} Z(W_N^{(i)})$; Theorem 3 refines it via *rough polarization* ($O(\log N)$ steps to an intermediate regime) then *fine polarization* with doubly-exponential contraction $Z \mapsto Z^2$ [2].

### 5.2 Finite-Length Performance

The asymptotic story is triumphant; the finite-length story required engineering. Representative BLER behavior (AWGN, BPSK [5][6]):

| Code | $(N, K)$ | Decoder | Gap to finite-length bound @ BLER $10^{-3}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plain polar | (1024, 512) | SC | ~1.5 dB |
| Plain polar | (1024, 512) | SCL, $L=8$ | ~0.7 dB |
| CA-polar (CRC24) | (1024, 512) | SCL, $L=8$ | ~0.3 dB |
| CA-polar (CRC24) | (512, 256) | SCL, $L=8$ | ~0.2 dB |
| 5G NR PDCCH profile | (512, $K\le 140$) | CA-SCL, $L=8$ | meets $10^{-5}$ target |

The row-2 to row-3 jump — CRC concatenation — is the single largest finite-length improvement and the reason 5G adopted CA-polar for *all* control channels [5]. Distributed CRC adds 0.1–0.3 dB and enables early termination, aborting most failed decodes within the first half of the block [6].

### 5.3 Why Short Lengths Favor Polar Codes

Control channels carry tens to a few hundred bits — a regime where LDPC codes suffer from short cycles and turbo codes from interleaver limitations. Polar codes' $O(N \log N)$ decoder involves no iterative message passing and follows a deterministic schedule, unlike belief propagation whose iteration count is data-dependent. For ultra-reliable low-latency communication (URLLC), that deterministic latency is a structural advantage of successive cancellation.

## 6. Limitations

Polar codes have real weaknesses worth stating plainly.

- **Slow polarization.** The $2^{-N^{\beta}}$, $\beta < 1/2$ scaling closes the gap to capacity slowly; at $N = 1024$ plain SC is still ~1.5 dB from the bound [2]. Larger kernels raise the exponent [3] but were not adopted by 5G.
- **Minimum distance.** Polar codes selected purely by reliability have poor distance spectra — many low-weight codewords — which is why the CRC outer code is load-bearing rather than ornamental [5]. A "polar code" in any deployed system is really a *concatenated* code.
- **Error propagation in SC.** One wrong hard decision poisons all subsequent $g$-updates. SCL mitigates this at $O(L)$ cost, but sequentiality caps throughput; node-based pruning (Rate-0/Rate-1/REP/SPC) recovers parallelism at added complexity [6].
- **Non-universality.** The frozen set is channel-dependent; a code constructed for one SNR is suboptimal at another [8]. The 5G reliability sequence [4] is a pragmatic universal compromise, not a theoretical fix — the compound-capacity problem for polar codes requires staircase/RS-concatenated variants with much larger block lengths.
- **Rate-matching friction.** The $N = 2^n$ constraint forces puncturing/shortening for arbitrary $E$, degrading the polarization structure in ways frozen-set redesign only partially compensates [4].
- **Data channels rejected.** 3GPP chose LDPC — not polar — for 5G *data* channels (PDSCH/PUSCH), where long blocks and high throughput favor LDPC's parallel belief propagation.

> **Open problem:** Constructing a *universal*, capacity-achieving polar family with $O(N \log N)$ decoding and competitive finite-length performance at $N \le 1024$ — without reliance on an outer CRC — remains unsolved and is arguably the most important theoretical gap between Arıkan's theorem and engineering practice.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Channel polarization is one of the deepest ideas in modern coding theory: a recursive, deterministic transform that *sorts* a channel's randomness into perfect and useless extremes, achieving with $O(N \log N)$ effort what random coding achieves only in expectation [1]. The successive cancellation decoder that exploits this structure is elegant, analyzable, and — with the list and CRC augmentations — practical enough to carry the control signaling of the world's 5G networks [4][5][6].

The trajectory from Arıkan's 2009 paper to TS 38.212 illustrates a broader pattern: a clean asymptotic theorem meets finite-length reality (slow convergence, weak distance), and the gap is closed not by a better theorem but by better *engineering* — list decoding, distributed CRC, sub-block interleaving, and a standardized reliability sequence. The open problems — universality, larger-kernel decoders, CRC-free finite-length optimality — will keep polarization an active frontier into the 6G era, where short-packet ultra-reliable communication raises the premium on codes that perform at $N$ in the hundreds.
