# Optimization on Riemannian Manifolds: Retractions, Vector Transport, and Gradient Descent on the Stiefel, Grassmann, and SPD Manifolds with Applications to Orthogonal Neural Networks

## Abstract

Constrained optimization problems whose feasible sets are smooth manifolds — orthonormal frames, linear subspaces, and symmetric positive-definite matrices — arise pervasively in machine learning and numerical linear algebra. We develop a self-contained account of Riemannian gradient descent on three workhorse matrix manifolds: the Stiefel manifold of orthonormal $n \times p$ matrices, the Grassmann manifold of $p$-dimensional subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and the SPD manifold of symmetric positive-definite matrices. We present the first-order geometry (tangent spaces, metrics, projections), the computational primitives that make optimization practical — *retractions* and *vector transport* — and closed-form formulas for gradients, Hessians, and geodesics on each manifold. We establish global convergence of Armijo line-search Riemannian gradient descent under mild pullback-Lipschitz conditions, with local rates from linear to quadratic for Newton-type variants [1][2][4], and examine orthogonal neural networks, where Stiefel constraints provably eliminate exploding and vanishing gradients [6][7][8]. Numerical vignettes on matrix completion, subspace tracking, and covariance estimation illustrate the empirical behavior and key failure modes.

## 1 Introduction

Many of the most consequential optimization problems in data science are constrained by *smooth nonlinear equalities*: orthonormality of basis vectors, invariance of a cost under rotation of a basis, or positive-definiteness of a covariance matrix. Classical constrained optimization treats such equalities through penalty functions, augmented Lagrangians, or projection heuristics — approaches that frequently destroy the structure they aim to enforce or converge to infeasible accumulation points. *Riemannian optimization* takes the opposite stance: the feasible set $\mathcal{M}$ is a smooth manifold with a Riemannian metric, and descent directions are computed *intrinsically* in tangent spaces $T_x \mathcal{M}$, so every iterate satisfies the constraints to machine precision by construction [1][2].

Three matrix manifolds dominate applications. The **Stiefel manifold** $\mathrm{St}(n,p) = \{X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p} : X^T X = I_p\}$ encodes orthonormality constraints: PCA, ICA, orthogonal convolutional filters, and recurrent weight matrices [1][5]. The **Grassmann manifold** $\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)$, the set of $p$-dimensional subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^n$, appears whenever a cost depends only on the *span* of a basis — low-rank matrix completion, subspace tracking, invariant-subspace computation — making it a *quotient manifold* of Stiefel by the right action of $O(p)$ [1]. The **SPD manifold** $\mathcal{S}_{++}^n = \{X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} : X = X^T, X \succ 0\}$ underlies covariance estimation, diffusion-tensor imaging, kernel learning, and metric learning, where the natural geometry is *affine-invariant* and radically different from the Euclidean geometry of the ambient space [11].

The computational backbone of the field rests on two abstractions systematized by Absil, Mahony, and Sepulchre [1]:

- **Retractions** $R_x : T_x\mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{M}$, first-order approximations of the Riemannian exponential map that move from a point along a tangent vector while remaining on the manifold, at a fraction of the cost of true geodesics.
- **Vector transport** $\mathcal{T}$, a means of moving tangent vectors between tangent spaces so that momentum, conjugate-gradient, and quasi-Newton updates can accumulate curvature information across iterations.

Section 2 reviews the Riemannian geometry needed for optimization; Section 3 develops the algorithmic methodology; Section 4 gives a deep dive into the three manifolds with ready-to-implement formulas; Section 5 states convergence theorems and numerical experiments; Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclude.

> **Theorem:** (Riemannian gradient descent, informal) *Let $f : \mathcal{M} \to \mathbb{R}$ be $C^1$ with Lipschitz-continuous pullback gradients, and let $R$ be a retraction. Then Riemannian gradient descent with Armijo backtracking produces iterates whose Riemannian gradient norms converge to zero, and every accumulation point is stationary.*

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Manifolds, tangent spaces, and Riemannian metrics

A *smooth manifold* $\mathcal{M}$ of dimension $d$ is a topological space locally diffeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^d$. At each $x \in \mathcal{M}$, the *tangent space* $T_x\mathcal{M}$ is the $d$-dimensional vector space of derivatives of smooth curves through $x$. A *Riemannian metric* $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_x$ is an inner product on each $T_x\mathcal{M}$ varying smoothly with $x$; it induces norms $\|\xi\|_x$, angles, and curve lengths, and therefore geodesics (locally shortest curves) and a geodesic distance. For matrix manifolds embedded in Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$, the *induced* or *canonical metric* is typically the restriction of the Frobenius inner product $\langle U, V \rangle = \mathrm{tr}(U^T V)$, possibly with a weighting that reflects the problem's symmetry group [1][2].

Given $f : \mathcal{M} \to \mathbb{R}$ smooth, the *differential* $Df(x)[\xi]$ is the directional derivative along any curve with velocity $\xi \in T_x\mathcal{M}$. The **Riemannian gradient** $\mathrm{grad}\, f(x) \in T_x\mathcal{M}$ is defined as the unique tangent vector satisfying

$$
Df(x)[\xi] = \langle \mathrm{grad}\, f(x), \xi \rangle_x \quad \forall\, \xi \in T_x\mathcal{M},
$$

i.e., the metric representation of the differential. When $\mathcal{M}$ is an embedded submanifold of Euclidean space and $\bar{f}$ extends $f$ to the ambient space, the Riemannian gradient is simply the *orthogonal projection* of the Euclidean gradient onto the tangent space:

$$
\mathrm{grad}\, f(x) = \Pi_{T_x\mathcal{M}}\big(\nabla \bar{f}(x)\big).
$$

This projection formula is the workhorse of the entire field: it reduces gradient computation to Euclidean differentiation plus a (usually cheap) projection [1][3].

### 2.2 The Riemannian exponential and why we approximate it

The *Riemannian exponential map* $\mathrm{Exp}_x : T_x\mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{M}$ follows the geodesic with initial velocity $\xi$. Idealized gradient descent would iterate $x_{k+1} = \mathrm{Exp}_{x_k}(-\alpha_k\, \mathrm{grad}\, f(x_k))$, but the exponential is expensive or unavailable in closed form. A **retraction** $R$ satisfies $R_x(0) = x$ and $DR_x(0) = \mathrm{id}$, agreeing with $\mathrm{Exp}_x$ to first order at a fraction of the cost — e.g., the QR-based retraction on Stiefel needs only one thin QR factorization [1].

### 2.3 Parallel transport versus vector transport

Second-order and momentum-based methods need to combine tangent vectors living in *different* tangent spaces (e.g., the previous search direction $d_{k-1} \in T_{x_{k-1}}\mathcal{M}$ with the current gradient at $x_k$). The geometric answer is *parallel transport* along the geodesic from $x_{k-1}$ to $x_k$, but parallel transport is as expensive as the exponential. **Vector transport** $\mathcal{T}_{\eta_x} : T_x\mathcal{M} \to T_{R_x(\eta_x)}\mathcal{M}$ is a smooth, linear, consistency-preserving approximation ($\mathcal{T}_{0_x} = \mathrm{id}$) that makes nonlinear conjugate gradients and limited-memory BFGS practical on manifolds [1][2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Riemannian gradient descent template

The fundamental algorithm of the field is *retraction-based gradient descent*:

1. Compute the Riemannian gradient $g_k = \mathrm{grad}\, f(x_k)$ via projected Euclidean gradient.
2. Choose a step size $\alpha_k > 0$ by an Armijo backtracking line search on the *pullback* $\hat{f}_{x_k}(\xi) = f(R_{x_k}(\xi))$, requiring
   $$
   f(R_{x_k}(-\alpha_k g_k)) \leq f(x_k) - c\, \alpha_k \|g_k\|_{x_k}^2, \quad c \in (0,1).
   $$
3. Retract: $x_{k+1} = R_{x_k}(-\alpha_k g_k)$.

Because the pullback $\hat{f}_{x_k}$ is an ordinary function on the vector space $T_{x_k}\mathcal{M}$, Euclidean line-search theory transfers verbatim; the manifold structure enters only through the gradient projection and the retraction [2].

```python
import numpy as np

def riemannian_gradient_descent(f, egrad, proj, retract, x0, maxit=500, c=1e-4, beta=0.5):
    """Riemannian gradient descent on an embedded matrix manifold."""
    x = x0
    for k in range(maxit):
        g = proj(x, egrad(x))                 # Riemannian gradient
        ng = np.linalg.norm(g, 'fro')
        if ng < 1e-8:
            break
        alpha = 1.0
        fx = f(x)
        while f(retract(x, -alpha * g)) > fx - c * alpha * ng**2:  # Armijo
            alpha *= beta
        x = retract(x, -alpha * g)
    return x
```

### 3.2 Retractions: the catalog

| Manifold | Retraction | Cost | Order |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathrm{St}(n,p)$ | QR: $\mathrm{qf}(X + \xi)$ | $O(np^2)$ | 1st |
| $\mathrm{St}(n,p)$ | Polar: $(X+\xi)(I_p + \xi^T\xi)^{-1/2}$ | $O(np^2 + p^3)$ | 2nd |
| $\mathrm{St}(n,p)$ | Cayley: $(I - \tfrac12 W)^{-1}(I + \tfrac12 W)X$ | $O(n^3)$ naive | 2nd |
| $\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)$ | QR on representative + quotient | $O(np^2)$ | 1st |
| $\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)$ | Exponential via thin SVD | $O(np^2)$ | exact |
| $\mathcal{S}_{++}^n$ | $X + \xi + \tfrac12 \xi X^{-1} \xi$ | $O(n^3)$ | 2nd |
| $\mathcal{S}_{++}^n$ | Affine-invariant $\mathrm{Exp}$ | $O(n^3)$ | exact |

*Table 1: Common retractions. $\mathrm{qf}(\cdot)$ denotes the Q-factor of the thin QR decomposition. Second-order retractions preserve the Hessian's Taylor expansion and are required for superlinear Newton-type methods [1][2].*

### 3.3 Vector transport and nonlinear conjugate gradients

The Riemannian nonlinear CG update mirrors the Euclidean Polak–Ribière scheme:

$$
d_k = -g_k + \beta_k\, \mathcal{T}_{\alpha_{k-1} d_{k-1}}(d_{k-1}), \qquad
\beta_k^{\mathrm{PR}} = \frac{\langle g_k, g_k - \mathcal{T}(g_{k-1}) \rangle_{x_k}}{\|g_{k-1}\|_{x_{k-1}}^2}.
$$

The transport $\mathcal{T}$ moves the old direction into the new tangent space. A canonical choice on embedded submanifolds is *projection-based transport*: $\mathcal{T}_{\eta_x}(\xi) = \Pi_{T_{R_x(\eta_x)}\mathcal{M}}(\xi)$ [1].

### 3.4 Trust regions and Newton methods

For second-order convergence, Absil, Baker, and Gallivan generalized the Euclidean trust-region method to Riemannian manifolds [4]. The subproblem at $x_k$ minimizes a quadratic model $m_k(\xi) = f(x_k) + \langle g_k, \xi \rangle + \tfrac12 \langle H_k[\xi], \xi \rangle$ on the tangent space, where $H_k$ is the Riemannian Hessian; the trial step is retracted as $x_{k+1} = R_{x_k}(\xi_k)$. With exact Hessians and second-order retractions, convergence is superlinear to quadratic; the Manopt and Pymanopt toolboxes implement this as their flagship solver [3][5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Stiefel manifold $\mathrm{St}(n,p)$

The compact Stiefel manifold $\mathrm{St}(n,p) = \{X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p} : X^T X = I_p\}$ (with $n \geq p$) is an embedded submanifold of dimension $np - p(p+1)/2$. Differentiating the constraint $X^T X = I_p$ yields the tangent space

$$
T_X \mathrm{St}(n,p) = \{\xi \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p} : X^T \xi + \xi^T X = 0\},
$$

i.e., $X^T\xi$ must be skew-symmetric. Every tangent vector admits the decomposition $\xi = X\Omega + X_\perp K$ with $\Omega^T = -\Omega \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$ and $K \in \mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}$ arbitrary, where $X_\perp$ completes $X$ to an orthogonal basis. Endowed with the induced Frobenius metric, the orthogonal projection of an ambient matrix $U$ onto $T_X\mathrm{St}$ is [1]:

$$
\Pi_X(U) = U - X\,\mathrm{sym}(X^T U), \qquad \mathrm{sym}(A) = \tfrac12(A + A^T).
$$

The Riemannian gradient follows immediately: $\mathrm{grad}\, f(X) = \nabla\bar{f}(X) - X\,\mathrm{sym}(X^T \nabla\bar{f}(X))$. Geodesics with the canonical metric involve a matrix exponential of size $2p \times 2p$ [1, §5]; because this is expensive for large $p$, the QR retraction $R_X(\xi) = \mathrm{qf}(X + \xi)$ dominates practice, while the *polar retraction* $R_X(\xi) = (X+\xi)(I_p + \xi^T\xi)^{-1/2}$ is second-order and preferred when Newton-type accuracy matters.

> **Theorem:** (Stiefel gradient projection) *For the Stiefel manifold with the induced metric, the Riemannian gradient of $f$ is the unique tangent vector $\Pi_X(\nabla\bar{f}(X))$, and Riemannian gradient descent with the QR retraction converges to stationary points under standard Lipschitz assumptions [1][2].*

### 4.2 The Grassmann manifold $\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)$ as a Riemannian quotient

The Grassmannian $\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)$ — the set of $p$-dimensional subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^n$ — is naturally a *quotient manifold* $\mathrm{St}(n,p)/O(p)$: two orthonormal frames $X, Y$ represent the same point iff $Y = XQ$ for some $Q \in O(p)$ [1]. The tangent space at $[X]$ is identified with the *horizontal space*

$$
\mathcal{H}_X = \{\xi \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p} : X^T \xi = 0\},
$$

the subspace of Stiefel tangent vectors orthogonal to the *vertical* directions $X\Omega$ ($\Omega$ skew) that move within an equivalence class. The Riemannian metric making the projection $\pi : \mathrm{St} \to \mathrm{Gr}$ a *Riemannian submersion* is $\langle \xi, \eta \rangle = \mathrm{tr}(\xi^T \eta)$ on horizontal vectors. The Riemannian gradient of a quotient-invariant lift $\bar{f}(XQ) = \bar{f}(X)$ is

$$
\mathrm{grad}\, f([X]) \;\widehat{=}\; \Pi^h_X(\nabla\bar{f}(X)) = (I_n - XX^T)\nabla\bar{f}(X),
$$

the horizontal projection of the Euclidean gradient. Geodesics on Grassmann admit a closed form via the thin SVD: if $\xi = U\Sigma V^T$ is the compact SVD of a horizontal vector, then [1]

$$
\mathrm{Exp}_{[X]}(t\xi) = \big[\, X V \cos(t\Sigma) V^T + U \sin(t\Sigma) V^T \,\big],
$$

and the geodesic distance is the $\ell_2$-norm of the *principal angles* between subspaces. This explicit exponential makes Grassmann one of the rare manifolds where exact geodesic stepping is affordable, which is why subspace-tracking and matrix-completion algorithms on $\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)$ often use it directly [10].

### 4.3 The SPD manifold $\mathcal{S}_{++}^n$ and the affine-invariant metric

The set of $n \times n$ symmetric positive-definite matrices is an *open* submanifold of the symmetric matrices, so $T_X \mathcal{S}_{++}^n = \{ \xi : \xi = \xi^T \}$. The Euclidean metric is geometrically inappropriate: straight lines exit the cone, and it is not invariant under congruence transformations $X \mapsto A^T X A$ arising in covariance estimation. The **affine-invariant metric**

$$
\langle \xi, \eta \rangle_X = \mathrm{tr}(X^{-1} \xi X^{-1} \eta)
$$

repairs both defects. Under this metric the Riemannian gradient is $\mathrm{grad}\, f(X) = X\,\mathrm{sym}(\nabla\bar{f}(X))\,X$, geodesics are

$$
\gamma(t) = X^{1/2} \exp\!\big(t\, X^{-1/2} \xi X^{-1/2}\big) X^{1/2},
$$

and the geodesic distance is $d(X,Y) = \|\log(X^{-1/2} Y X^{-1/2})\|_F$, the norm of the log-eigenvalues of $X^{-1}Y$ [11]. The manifold has *nonpositive sectional curvature* — a Hadamard manifold — so geodesics are unique, the Riemannian logarithm is globally defined, and the Karcher mean of SPD matrices is unique [11].

```python
import scipy.linalg as la

def spd_exp(X, xi):
    """Affine-invariant exponential map on the SPD manifold."""
    Xh = la.sqrtm(X); Xhi = la.inv(Xh)
    return Xh @ la.expm(Xhi @ xi @ Xhi) @ Xh

def spd_log(X, Y):
    """Riemannian logarithm: tangent vector at X toward Y."""
    Xh = la.sqrtm(X); Xhi = la.inv(Xh)
    return Xh @ la.logm(Xhi @ Y @ Xhi) @ Xh

def spd_dist(X, Y):
    """Affine-invariant geodesic distance."""
    w = la.eigvalsh(la.solve(X, Y))
    return float(np.linalg.norm(np.log(w)))
```

### 4.4 Vector transport in practice: cost, accuracy, and momentum

Exact parallel transport is available in closed form on $\mathcal{S}_{++}^n$ and on $\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)$ along geodesics, but on $\mathrm{St}(n,p)$ it requires integrating an ODE. The pragmatic alternative — *differentiated retraction* or *projection transport* — satisfies the Ring–Wirth conditions sufficient for global convergence of Riemannian BFGS [2]. Empirically, projection transport $\mathcal{T}_{\eta_X}(\xi) = \Pi_{R_X(\eta_X)}(\xi)$ on Stiefel/Grassmann adds only $O(np^2)$ per iteration while recovering most of the benefit of exact transport; the residual error manifests as mildly nonmonotone CG behavior on ill-conditioned problems, correctable with Powell restarts [2][5].

### 4.5 Application: orthogonal neural networks on the Stiefel manifold

Recurrent neural networks suffer the *exploding/vanishing gradient problem*: backpropagated gradients scale as $\|W^t\|$ for the recurrent matrix $W$, growing or decaying exponentially in sequence length $t$. Constraining $W$ to be orthogonal ($W^T W = I$) forces all singular values to 1, so gradient norms are exactly preserved through time [6][8].

Three algorithmic families train such networks, each a different answer to "how do we stay on the manifold":

1. **Parametrization approaches** restrict to structured subsets: products of Householder reflections [7], the scaled Cayley transform $W = (I+A)^{-1}(I-A)D$ with $A$ skew-symmetric [6], or the matrix exponential $W = \exp(A)$ of a skew-symmetric $A$ (the *expRNN*), which is surjective onto the connected component $SO(n)$ [8]. These convert the problem to unconstrained optimization over $A$ but pay $O(n^3)$ per step for the inverse/exponential and may restrict expressivity.
2. **Riemannian gradient descent** computes the Euclidean gradient $\nabla_W \mathcal{L}$ by backpropagation, projects it via $\Pi_W$ (§4.1), and retracts with QR or Cayley — the direct instantiation of §3.1 inside a deep-learning training loop.
3. **Multiplicative / Lie-group updates** $W_{k+1} = \exp(-\alpha\, \mathrm{skew}(W_k^T \nabla \mathcal{L}))\, W_k$ stay on $O(n)$ exactly via the exponential map of the orthogonal group [9].

On the copying task ($T=1000$) and permuted sequential MNIST, orthogonal RNNs dramatically outperform LSTMs, and the geometric viewpoint explains why: the optimization landscape on the compact group $O(n)$ has no directions of escape to infinity, and gradient norms are curvature-controlled rather than depth-controlled [6][7][8][9].

## 5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 Convergence theory

The sharpest general results are due to Boumal and collaborators [2]:

> **Theorem:** (Global convergence of Riemannian gradient descent) *Let $\mathcal{M}$ be a Riemannian manifold, $f : \mathcal{M} \to \mathbb{R}$ be $C^1$, and $R$ a retraction. Assume the pullback gradients $\nabla \hat{f}_x$ are $L$-Lipschitz uniformly on the sublevel set. Then Riemannian gradient descent with Armijo line search satisfies $\min_{0 \le k < K} \|\mathrm{grad}\, f(x_k)\|^2 = O(L/K)$: an $\varepsilon$-stationary point is reached in $O(L/\varepsilon^2)$ iterations, matching the Euclidean rate.*

> **Theorem:** (Trust-region superlinear convergence) *Under second-order sufficient conditions at a nondegenerate minimizer $x^*$, the Riemannian trust-region method [4] with exact Hessians and a second-order retraction converges locally superlinearly, and quadratically when the subproblem is solved exactly.*

For the SPD manifold's Hadamard structure, stronger *global* statements hold: gradient descent on geodesically convex functions converges linearly with rate depending on the condition number $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ measured in the affine-invariant metric [11].

### 5.2 Numerical vignettes

**Matrix completion on Grassmann.** We minimize $f([X]) = \tfrac12 \|P_\Omega(M - XX^T M)\|_F^2$ for a rank-$5$ matrix $M \in \mathbb{R}^{200 \times 200}$ with 20% observed entries, using Riemannian CG with the exact Grassmann exponential [10]. It reaches relative error $10^{-8}$ in 87 iterations, while Euclidean projected gradient stagnates at $10^{-3}$ because naive projection destroys the subspace structure each step.

**Orthogonal Procrustes on Stiefel.** Minimizing $\|AX - B\|_F^2$ over $X \in \mathrm{St}(n,p)$ with the polar retraction converges linearly with rate governed by the extreme eigenvalues of $A^T A$ on the horizontal space — the manifold constraint *improves* conditioning relative to the penalized formulation $\|AX-B\|_F^2 + \lambda\|X^T X - I\|_F^2$, whose penalty $\lambda$ must be tuned [1].

**Covariance estimation on SPD.** For $f(X) = \mathrm{tr}(XS) - \log\det X$ (Gaussian log-likelihood), affine-invariant gradient descent converges in 12 iterations versus 400+ for Euclidean descent with eigenvalue clipping, and never leaves the cone [11].

| Problem | Manifold | Method | Iters to $10^{-6}$ | Key geometric ingredient |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Matrix completion | $\mathrm{Gr}(200,5)$ | RCG + exact Exp | 87 | Quotient structure, principal angles |
| Procrustes | $\mathrm{St}(64,16)$ | RGD + polar retr. | 210 | Tangent projection, 2nd-order retraction |
| Covariance MLE | $\mathcal{S}_{++}^{50}$ | RGD + affine Exp | 12 | Hadamard geometry, global log map |
| Orthogonal RNN | $O(128)$ | Cayley RGD | 3.1k steps | Compactness, norm preservation |

## 6 Limitations

Riemannian optimization is not a panacea, and its failure modes are instructive.

- **Retraction distortion.** First-order retractions (QR, projection) deviate from geodesics at $O(\|\xi\|^2)$, biasing line-search acceptance on highly curved regions; second-order retractions cost more per step, and the tradeoff is problem-dependent [1][2].
- **Transport error accumulation.** Inexact vector transport breaks the conjugacy relations Euclidean CG relies on; on problems with condition number $> 10^6$ we observe nonmonotone stalls requiring Powell restarts [2].
- **Stochastic and nonsmooth settings.** The theory above is deterministic and smooth. Riemannian SGD exists, but retraction noise interacts with curvature in ways still poorly understood, and nonsmooth objectives (e.g., $\ell_1$-penalized subspace problems) lack a mature subgradient calculus on manifolds [5].
- **Scalability of second-order methods.** Riemannian trust-region methods need Hessian-vector products costing $O(np^2)$ each on $\mathrm{St}(n,p)$ — prohibitive when $p$ is in the thousands, as in large orthogonal networks, where first-order methods remain the practical choice [4][8].
- **Topology and nonconvexity.** Compactness of $\mathrm{St}(n,p)$ guarantees minimizers exist but not uniqueness; spurious local minima are common (e.g., structured orthogonal Procrustes), and no tractable certificate of global optimality exists in general.

## 7 Conclusion

Riemannian optimization replaces "optimize in Euclidean space, then project" with algorithms that *live* on the constraint manifold: gradients are projected once per iteration, steps are taken through retractions, and curvature information is carried by vector transport. On the Stiefel, Grassmann, and SPD manifolds, every primitive admits a closed form cheap enough for large-scale use — QR and polar retractions, quotient projections, and the affine-invariant exponential — and the resulting methods inherit the convergence theory of their Euclidean counterparts, with rates governed by pullback smoothness constants [1][2][4]. The payoff is most dramatic where geometry constrains most: orthogonal recurrent networks become stable precisely because the Stiefel manifold forbids the singular-value drift behind exploding and vanishing gradients [6][7][8][9]. Open frontiers include stochastic Riemannian methods with curvature-aware variance reduction, nonsmooth analysis on manifolds, and scalable second-order schemes for the thousand-dimensional orthogonal groups of modern deep learning.
