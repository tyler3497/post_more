# MLIR: A Multi-Level Intermediate Representation Framework for Heterogeneous Compiler Infrastructure

![Figure 1: Progressive lowering through stacked dialect layers, from tensor/linalg abstractions down to the LLVM dialect and LLVM IR.](ths_1789075773000_17283-0.webp)

## Abstract

This article presents a rigorous treatment of MLIR (Multi-Level Intermediate Representation), the extensible compiler infrastructure introduced by Lattner et al. at CGO 2021 [1]. Unlike LLVM IR's single-level, flat instruction stream, MLIR permits operations, types, and attributes drawn from several abstraction levels — graph-level tensors, structured loops, and machine-level instructions — to coexist inside one SSA module, organized by extensible dialects and lowered progressively toward targets [2][4]. We formalize the core IR ontology (operations, blocks, regions), the dialect mechanism with its TableGen-based Operation Definition Specification (ODS), and the interface-based rewrite machinery that drives folding, canonicalization, and dialect conversion. Two full compilation case studies are traced end to end: the TensorFlow→XLA bridge via the MHLO dialect and the CIRCT hardware-design stack [8]. The transform dialect's fine-grained scheduling of optimizations [5] and the structured code-generation pipeline [6] are analyzed in depth, followed by measured results from the original evaluation and an honest account of the framework's limitations.

## 1 Introduction

Modern compilers suffer from *software fragmentation*: every domain — machine learning, linear algebra, hardware design, signal processing — reinvents its own intermediate representation, optimizer, and code generator, because no shared infrastructure addresses problems that live *above* the level of a control-flow graph of scalar instructions [1]. LLVM IR solved fragmentation at the bottom of the stack: it is a superb lingua franca for the last mile of code generation, but compiling *down to it* from a framework such as TensorFlow, and *optimizing at the source level*, requires recovering structure that LLVM IR deliberately discards. MLIR attacks this fragmentation directly. Rather than fixing a single abstraction level, it is a *meta-infrastructure*: a small, precisely specified IR substrate (operations, blocks, regions, attributes, types) on which an unbounded family of coexisting dialects can be defined [2]. Dialects declare operations whose semantics live at whatever level suits the domain — a single `linalg.matmul` on immutable tensors, or a single `llvm.fmuladd` on SSA registers — and the compiler *progressively lowers* the module through a stack of dialects until only the target dialect remains (Figure 1). Crucially, high-level and low-level operations may coexist in the same module simultaneously; lowering is a schedule of partial conversions, not a monolithic phase boundary [1][4].

This article gives a PhD-level account of that design. Section 2 situates MLIR against LLVM IR and its domain-specific predecessors. Section 3 states the methodology: a formal reconstruction of MLIR's IR ontology from the language reference and rationale documents. Section 4 is the deep dive — the SSA core, dialects and ODS, regions and interface-based rewrites, progressive lowering with the XLA→MHLO case study, and the transform dialect. Section 5 reports the empirical case studies of the original CGO evaluation together with follow-on results from the structured code-generation and sparse-compilation lines of work [6][7]. Section 6 catalogs limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 LLVM IR: the flat-level baseline

LLVM IR is a typed, static-single-assignment (SSA) intermediate representation with a *single* abstraction level: functions contain basic blocks, basic blocks contain linear sequences of typed instructions, and control flow is expressed through explicit branches and φ-nodes [9]. This uniformity is its strength — optimizers, backends, and linkers can be written once — but it forces every frontend to *prematurely* flatten domain structure. A TensorFlow matmul becomes a loop nest before the framework-level optimizer ever sees it; a polyhedral scheduler must first *raise* the loops back out of the CFG. Extensibility exists but is second-class: intrinsics smuggle domain operations into the instruction stream, and metadata carries side information that optimizers are free to (and do) drop.

### 2.2 The domain-IR proliferation

The response was a Cambrian explosion of bespoke IRs, each carrying its own toolchain debt [1]:

- **XLA/HLO** — a graph-level IR for linear-algebra programs with tuple-shaped operations and a dedicated compiler for TPUs/GPUs.
- **Halide / TVM Relay** — schedule-vs-algorithm separation and tensor-level dataflow, each with custom lowering to LLVM.
- **FIRRTL** — an IR for digital circuits, the compilation target of the Chisel hardware-construction language.
- **Swift SIL, Rust MIR, Julia IR** — language-specific mid-level IRs with flow-sensitive typing or borrow checking.

Each reimplemented parsing, verification, diagnostics, pass management, and testing. The MLIR paper's central economic claim is that this *infrastructure* — not any particular set of operations — is the reusable part, and that a sufficiently expressive meta-IR can host all of these abstractions in one module [1].

### 2.3 What MLIR adds

MLIR keeps LLVM's SSA discipline but generalizes the container hierarchy. Where LLVM IR has *instruction → basic block → function → module*, MLIR has *operation → block → region → operation* — a recursive nesting in which any operation may own regions containing blocks containing further operations [2]. This single generalization buys:

1. **Multi-level coexistence** — `linalg.matmul` and `llvm.fmuladd` in one module.
2. **Structured control flow** — `scf.for` and `affine.for` as region-carrying operations instead of branch soups [3].
3. **Extensible types and attributes** — dialect-defined type systems (tensors with encodings, hardware bit-vectors) checked by per-operation verifiers.
4. **Interface-driven reuse** — transformations query abstract interfaces (`MemoryEffectOpInterface`, `DialectInlinerInterface`) rather than hardcoding dialect knowledge [3].

## 3 Methodology

Our treatment is reconstructive rather than experimental: we derive MLIR's design from first principles and ground every structural claim in the primary sources — the CGO 2021 paper [1], the MLIR language reference [2], the interfaces and rationale documents [3][4], the transform-dialect paper [5], the structured code-generation paper [6], the sparse-compilation paper [7], the CIRCT documentation [8], and the MLIR tutorial [9].

The analysis proceeds along three axes:

1. **Ontological** — what are the primitive IR units (operations, blocks, regions, values, attributes, types) and what invariants relate them (§4.1–4.3)?
2. **Mechanistic** — how does a module *move* through the dialect stack: folding, canonicalization, dialect conversion, bufferization (§4.4)?
3. **Pragmatic** — how do expert users *schedule* that movement for performance: the transform dialect and structured codegen (§4.5)?

The two end-to-end case studies — TensorFlow/XLA via MHLO and CIRCT for hardware — serve as existence proofs that the abstraction is not merely elegant but industrially load-bearing. Quantitative claims are drawn only from published evaluations [1][6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Operations, Types, and Attributes: The SSA Core

The *operation* is MLIR's atomic unit of computation, generalizing LLVM's instruction. Every operation carries [2]:

- **A name** — a namespaced string such as `arith.addi` or `linalg.matmul`, whose prefix identifies the owning dialect.
- **Operands** — a list of SSA *values* consumed by the operation.
- **Results** — a list of SSA values produced; unlike LLVM instructions, an operation may return *multiple* results, written `%r:2`.
- **Regions** — nested control-flow graphs owned by the operation (§4.3).
- **Attributes** — compile-time constant metadata (integers, strings, arrays, dictionaries, affine maps).
- **Successors** — for terminator operations, the list of target blocks.
- **A location** — source provenance used by diagnostics.

The generic textual form makes this anatomy explicit:

```mlir
%result:2 = "mydialect.morph"(%input, %bias)
    ({ ^bb0(%a: f32): ... }, { ^bb1: ... })
    { some_attr = true, map = affine_map<(d0) -> (d0)> }
    : (tensor<4xf32>, f32) -> (tensor<4xf32>, f32)
    loc("model.py":10:8)
```

Values are in SSA form: each result is defined exactly once, and use–def chains are explicit [2]. Attributes, by contrast, are *not* values — they are compile-time data, which lets the IR distinguish cleanly between runtime operands and static configuration (a distinction LLVM IR blurs with immediates and metadata).

![Figure 2: Anatomy of an MLIR operation — operands, results, attributes, nested regions of blocks, and type annotations.](ths_1789075773000_17283-1.webp)

Types are likewise extensible. The *builtin* type system provides `index`, signless integers (`i32`), floats (`f32`), shaped types (`tensor<?x?xf32>`, `memref<4x4xf32>`, `vector<8xf32>`), and function types; dialects may add their own (e.g. `!hw.array` in CIRCT or `!llvm.ptr` in the LLVM dialect) [2]. Shaped types cleanly separate the *tensor* abstraction (immutable, value-semantic, sized by the program's math) from the *memref* abstraction (mutable buffer with layout and address space) — a separation that makes *bufferization*, the lowering of tensors to explicit memory, a well-defined compiler phase rather than an ad hoc rewrite.

> **Theorem (SSA well-formedness):** In a verified MLIR module, every SSA value has exactly one definition, every use is dominated by its definition within the region structure, and every operation's operands, results, and attributes satisfy the verifier registered for its name. The verifier — generated from ODS or written in C++ — is the *only* trusted semantic gate; passes may assume verified IR [2][4].

### 4.2 Dialects and the Operation Definition Specification

A *dialect* is a namespace bundling related operations, types, and attributes: `arith` (integer/float arithmetic), `linalg` (structured tensor algebra), `affine` (polyhedral loops), `scf` (structured control flow), `cf` (unstructured branches), `tensor`, `memref`, `vector`, `func`, and the `llvm` dialect that mirrors LLVM IR itself [2][9]. Dialects are loaded into an `MLIRContext` on demand; a module mixing `linalg`, `scf`, and `llvm` operations is ordinary, not exceptional — this *dialect interleaving* is the multi-level essence of Figure 1.

Defining a dialect by hand in C++ would be prohibitive, so MLIR provides ODS — the **Operation Definition Specification** — a declarative TableGen framework. An operation is specified once, and ODS generates the C++ class, parser, printer, verifier, and builders [3]:

```tablegen
def MyDialect_ScaleOp : MyDialect_Op<"scale", [Pure]> {
  let summary = "scale a tensor by a scalar";
  let arguments = (ins AnyTensor:$input, F32:$factor);
  let results = (outs AnyTensor:$output);
  let assemblyFormat = "$input `by` $factor attr-dict `:` type($input)";
}
```

The `[Pure]` trait declares the absence of memory effects, which the canonicalizer, dead-code elimination, and LICM consume through the `MemoryEffectOpInterface` without knowing the dialect exists [3]. This is the *interface* pattern that pervades MLIR: instead of hardcoding special cases for each new operation, the framework defines abstract capabilities — folding (`FoldOpInterface`), type inference (`InferTypeOpInterface`), inlining legality (`DialectInlinerInterface`), loop-likeness (`LoopLikeOpInterface`), branching (`BranchOpInterface`) — and dialects *opt in* declaratively. Generic passes then operate over interfaces, so a transformation written once works for dialects not yet invented. As the rationale document stresses, this interface mechanism is what lets MLIR scale to hundreds of dialects without the pass manager collapsing into a case analysis [4].

A representative stack of production dialects by abstraction level:

| Level | Dialects | Representative operations |
|---|---|---|
| Graph / domain | `linalg`, `tensor`, `sparse_tensor`, `tosa` | `linalg.matmul`, `tensor.generate` |
| Structured loops | `affine`, `scf` | `affine.for`, `scf.for`, `scf.if` |
| Memory / data-parallel | `memref`, `vector` | `memref.alloc`, `vector.transfer_read` |
| Control flow | `cf`, `func` | `cf.br`, `cf.cond_br`, `func.func` |
| Machine | `llvm`, `nvvm`, `spirv` | `llvm.fmuladd`, `llvm.alloca` |

The `llvm` dialect wraps all of LLVM IR as MLIR operations, so the final step — `mlir-translate --mlir-to-llvmir` — reuses existing LLVM backends untouched [9].

### 4.3 Regions, Blocks, and Interface-Based Rewrites

*Blocks* are sequences of operations ending in a *terminator*; *regions* are lists of blocks attached to an operation [2]. In an SSACFG region, blocks are basic blocks and terminators are branches; in a single-block region (e.g. the body of `scf.for`), the block is simply a scope. Two design decisions make regions powerful:

1. **Block arguments replace φ-nodes.** Where LLVM IR merges control-flow-dependent values with `phi`, MLIR passes them as explicit block arguments on branch successors: `cf.br ^bb3(%2 : tensor<*xf32>)` binds `%2` to `%3` in `^bb3(%3 : tensor<*xf32>)`. Values that are *not* control-flow dependent are referenced directly across the region hierarchy — no threading required [2].
2. **Regions are hierarchical, not flat.** An `scf.for` owns the region of its loop body; an `linalg.generic` owns the region computing its scalar body. Passes treat regions as *opaque by default*: generic rewrites do not descend into them unless the operation's interfaces permit it, which makes cross-region analyses (inlining, loop-invariant code motion) *simpler* than interprocedural analysis — uses from different regions appear in one use–def chain rather than behind call-argument correspondence tables [4].

> **Theorem (Region isolation):** An operation carrying the `IsolatedFromAbove` trait (e.g. `func.func`) is forbidden from referencing SSA values defined outside its regions, except for a restricted set of globals. The verifier rejects violations, so transformations may treat such operations as closed compilation units [2].

*Interface-based rewrites* are how the IR is *simplified* before it is lowered. Three mechanisms dominate:

1. **Folding** — each operation may implement `fold()`, constant-folding itself given constant operands; the folder runs opportunistically during pattern application [3].
2. **Canonicalization** — every dialect registers *canonicalization patterns* per operation (algebraic identities like `add(x, 0) → x`, strength reduction, redundant-cast elimination); the `canonicalize` pass applies them greedily to a fixpoint [3].
3. **Inlining and DCE/CSE** — driven by dialect inliner interfaces and memory-effect queries, hence dialect-agnostic [3].

![Figure 3: The rewrite pipeline — canonicalization patterns, operation folding, dialect conversion, inlining, and CSE/DCE driven by declarative interfaces.](ths_1789075773000_17283-2.webp)

The pipeline in Figure 3 is noteworthy because it is *open*: a new dialect ships its canonicalization patterns with its ODS definitions, and the existing passes immediately exploit them. This is the practical payoff of the interface design — optimization reuse across the dialect ecosystem without central coordination.

### 4.4 Progressive Lowering: Dialect Conversion and the XLA→MHLO Story

*Progressive lowering* is MLIR's signature compilation strategy: rather than one cliff from domain IR to machine IR, the module descends a *dialect stack* through a scheduled sequence of *partial* conversions [1][4]. The **dialect conversion framework** is the machinery: for each step the user supplies a `TypeConverter` (e.g. tensor types → memref types), a set of *conversion patterns* rewriting illegal operations into legal ones, and a *legalization* criterion (which operations must be gone afterward). The driver applies patterns to a fixpoint, materializing type conversions at boundaries, and fails loudly if any illegal operation survives — verifiability, not best effort [3].

A canonical descent for a dense tensor program:

1. **Tiling/fusion at the `linalg` level** — `linalg.matmul` on immutable `tensor<...>` values is tiled and fused while shapes are still symbolic.
2. **Bufferization** — `tensor` → `memref`: immutable values become explicit allocations; the framework inserts `memref.alloc`/`dealloc` and proves the transformation memory-safe.
3. **`linalg` → `scf`/`affine`** — structured ops expand to explicit loop nests over memrefs.
4. **`scf` → `cf`** — structured control flow flattens to branches over SSA blocks.
5. **`arith`/`memref`/`cf` → `llvm`** — the last mile into the LLVM dialect, then `mlir-translate` to LLVM IR and the standard backend.

![Figure 4: A lowering flow example — linalg.matmul on tensors, expanded to an affine loop nest over memrefs, then to LLVM-dialect operations.](ths_1789075773000_17283-3.webp)

The **XLA→MHLO story** is the canonical industrial validation of this model. TensorFlow's compiler bridge converts TensorFlow graph operations into **MHLO** — *Meta HLO*, an MLIR dialect capturing XLA's HLO instruction set as first-class operations on tensors [1][9]. Because MHLO is a dialect rather than a foreign IR, the TensorFlow→MHLO step composes with MLIR's folding, canonicalization, and shape-inference passes *before* the program ever reaches XLA; XLA then consumes the HLO it already understands. MHLO was subsequently donated to the community and standardized as StableHLO, but the architectural lesson stands: an existing, production compiler (XLA) was *connected to* rather than *rewritten for* the new infrastructure, exactly the "connecting existing compilers together" goal of the CGO paper [1]. Torch-MLIR follows the same pattern for PyTorch, lowering the `torch` dialect through `linalg` toward LLVM [9].

### 4.5 The Transform Dialect and Structured Code Generation

Pass pipelines are *coarse-grained*: a pass runs over the whole module, and performance engineers cannot easily say "tile *this* matmul by 128×128, then vectorize *that* loop" [5]. The **transform dialect** answers by making the optimization schedule itself an MLIR program. Transform operations manipulate *handles* — SSA values in the transform IR that name *payload* operations in the program being compiled — so a schedule like the following is ordinary IR, verifiable and composable [5]:

```mlir
transform.sequence failures(propagate) {
^bb0(%arg0: !transform.any_op):
  %matmul = transform.structured.match ops{["linalg.matmul"]} in %arg0
  %tiled, %loop = transform.structured.tile_to_forall_op %matmul
      tile_sizes [128, 128]
  %vec = transform.structured.vectorize %tiled
  transform.yield
}
```

The transform dialect thus turns *optimization expertise* into a reusable, inspectable artifact — schedules can be autotuned, searched, and versioned like code — while the underlying transformations remain the same pattern-based rewrites. This is the substrate of the "composable and modular code generation" line of work, which demonstrates structured tiling, fusion, and vectorization of `linalg` programs reaching performance competitive with hand-tuned libraries on GEMM-class kernels [6]. The sparse-tensor compiler extends the same philosophy to sparsity: a `sparse_tensor` dialect with encoding attributes on tensor types drives code generation for compressed formats (CSR, COO, DCSR) through the identical progressive-lowering pipeline [7].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation

The CGO 2021 paper evaluates MLIR as *infrastructure* rather than as a single optimizer, so its evidence is architectural and integrative [1]:

1. **TensorFlow/XLA integration.** The end-to-end TF→MHLO→XLA bridge replaced a bespoke lowering path with dialect conversions reusing MLIR's verifier, diagnostics, and pass manager. The paper reports that expressing HLO as a dialect *reduced* the compiler's trusted custom code while enabling MLIR-level optimizations (constant folding, shape refinement) to run on HLO programs for the first time — optimizations previously unavailable inside XLA's closed IR [1].
2. **Dialect authoring cost.** Case studies on adding new IRs (including a Fortran-oriented dialect and the `affine` polyhedral dialect) show that ODS-generated parsers, printers, and verifiers collapse thousands of lines of hand-written C++ into tens of lines of TableGen, with the generated verifier catching malformed IR that previously required custom checking code [1][3].
3. **Interface reuse.** Transformations written against interfaces — the inliner, LICM, loop-invariant code motion over `LoopLikeOpInterface` — apply unchanged to dialects developed independently, which the paper demonstrates by running generic passes over the `affine` and `linalg` dialects without dialect-specific code paths [1][4].

Follow-on work supplies the performance numbers the original paper deliberately deferred. The structured code-generation pipeline [6] reports tiled, fused, vectorized `linalg` programs near machine peak on dense kernels — competitive with hand-tuned libraries — while retargeting across CPUs and GPUs via dialect switches. The sparse compiler [7] reduces sparsity support to a *dialect plus encoding attributes* through the standard pipeline, with generated sparse kernels competitive with hand-written ones. CIRCT [8] provides the non-ML existence proof: `hw`, `comb`, `seq`, and `firrtl` dialects carry Chisel-generated circuits through optimization to Verilog emission, reusing MLIR's pass manager, FileCheck-based testing, and diagnostic engine for hardware — a domain LLVM IR cannot express at all.

## 6 Limitations

An honest account must record where the design strains:

- **Dialect combinatorics.** Freedom to define dialects is freedom to fragment: the ecosystem now contains overlapping dialects (`tosa` vs. `linalg` vs. `stablehlo` for tensor programs), and choosing — or converting between — them is a real engineering tax [4].
- **Lowering-schedule expertise.** Progressive lowering replaces one cliff with a *schedule* of conversions, bufferization decisions, and transform scripts; authoring a correct, performant schedule for a new target remains expert work [3][5].
- **Verifier as specification.** The verifier is the semantic gate, but ODS verifiers check *structural* well-formedness; subtle semantic bugs in custom verifiers or conversion patterns produce miscompilations that no framework-level check catches. The framework raises the floor but does not remove the need for rigorous testing (hence MLIR-Forge-style fuzzing efforts).
- **Compile-time cost.** Greedy pattern rewriting to fixpoint is not free; large modules spend significant time in canonicalization and conversion, and debugging which pattern fired requires IR-dump archaeology.
- **Region semantics are subtle.** Cross-region use–def chains simplify some analyses but complicate others: every transformation must reason about region isolation, and the verifier is the backstop when a pass forgets [4].

## 7 Conclusion

MLIR reframes compiler construction from *building an IR* to *instantiating a framework*: a minimal SSA substrate of operations, blocks, and regions; dialects as the unit of abstraction; ODS as the declarative definition language; interfaces as the reuse mechanism; and progressive lowering as the compilation strategy [1][2][3][4]. The evidence [1][5][6][7][8] suggests the bet is paying off: domains that once rebuilt compiler toolchains now share verification, diagnostics, rewriting, and scheduling machinery, differing only in their dialects. Against LLVM IR's flat level, MLIR's contribution is not a better instruction set but a better *tower*: multiple levels of abstraction, simultaneously present, lowered on a schedule the engineer controls. The remaining challenge is social as much as technical — converging the dialect ecosystem so that the tower's floors line up.
