# From Garbled Truth Tables to Authenticated Preprocessing: A Comparative Study of Yao's Garbled Circuits, the GMW Protocol, and SPDZ for Maliciously Secure Multi-Party Computation

## Abstract

Secure multi-party computation (MPC) allows *n* mutually distrustful parties to evaluate a joint function of their private inputs while revealing nothing beyond the prescribed output [1]. This thesis compares the three canonical frameworks spanning the field's forty-year arc: Yao's garbled circuits, delivering constant-round two-party computation against semi-honest adversaries [1,3]; the Goldreich–Micali–Wigderson (GMW) paradigm of gate-by-gate secret-sharing evaluation, extending to any number of parties [2]; and the SPDZ protocol family, which achieves malicious, dishonest-majority security through input-independent offline preprocessing of authenticated Beaver triples [8]. We develop the simulation paradigm underlying all security proofs, derive Beaver's multiplication identity [5], quantify the IKNP oblivious-transfer extension that made Yao's protocol practical [6], and dissect SPDZ's information-theoretic MAC mechanism with its batched verification check. Benchmarks drawn from the MP-SPDZ framework [11] ground the communication-versus-computation trade-offs. We close with the field's fundamental limits: the impossibility of fairness under dishonest majority, the concrete cost of malicious oblivious transfer, and the preprocessing bottleneck that still dominates real deployments.

---

## 1. Introduction

The foundational question of secure multi-party computation is simple: *can parties holding private inputs x₁, …, xₙ compute a public function f(x₁, …, xₙ) such that no party learns anything about the others' inputs beyond what the output itself reveals?* Yao's 1986 formulation — the millionaires' problem of two parties comparing wealth without disclosure — launched modern cryptography's most ambitious engineering program [1]. The field converged on three architectural families whose differences illuminate every major design axis: the trust model (semi-honest versus malicious), the corruption threshold (honest versus dishonest majority), the round complexity (constant versus depth-linear), and the function representation (Boolean versus arithmetic circuits).

This thesis studies all three families in a unified framework. **Yao's garbled circuits** [1,3] achieve constant-round two-party computation: one party (the *garbler*) encrypts the circuit's truth tables with random *wire labels*, and the other (the *evaluator*) obtains exactly one label per input wire via oblivious transfer, decrypting precisely one row per gate. **The GMW protocol** [2] takes the opposite approach: parties maintain *secret shares* of every wire value and evaluate the circuit gate-by-gate, using oblivious transfer to compute AND gates. It generalizes to any number of parties with an honest majority. **SPDZ** [8] hardens the secret-sharing approach for a *malicious* adversary corrupting up to *n − 1* parties, attaching information-theoretic MACs to every share and generating all correlated randomness in an offline phase, leaving an online phase whose per-gate cost is a handful of field operations and broadcasts.

We present (i) a precise, notation-consistent treatment of all three protocols; (ii) worked derivations of Beaver's multiplication identity [5], point-and-permute, the IKNP OT-extension construction [6], and the half-gates garbling scheme [7]; (iii) the SPDZ offline/online decomposition with its authenticated-share algebra and batched MAC check; and (iv) a quantitative comparison grounded in published implementations [4,9,10,11].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Simulation Paradigm

Security in MPC is defined by comparison to an *ideal world* in which a trusted third party collects inputs, computes *f*, and returns the output. A protocol is secure if, for every real-world adversary, there exists a *simulator* interacting only with the ideal functionality that produces an indistinguishable view [3].

> **Theorem (Simulation-based security, informal).** A protocol Π securely realizes a functionality *F* if for every probabilistic polynomial-time adversary *A* there exists a simulator *S* such that the adversary's view and the honest parties' outputs in the real execution are computationally indistinguishable from those in the ideal execution.

Two adversary classes dominate: a **semi-honest** adversary follows the protocol but mines its transcript for extra information, while a **malicious** adversary deviates arbitrarily. The corruption threshold further divides protocols: **honest-majority** protocols (BGW, CCD, GMW) tolerate *t < n/2* (or *t < n/3*) corruptions, while **dishonest-majority** protocols (Yao's two-party protocol, SPDZ) tolerate up to *n − 1* corruptions but must sacrifice *fairness* and *guaranteed output delivery* — Cleve's impossibility result rules these out without an honest majority.

### 2.2 Oblivious Transfer: The Completeness Primitive

*Oblivious transfer* (OT) is the minimal complete primitive for MPC: Kilian showed that OT alone suffices for general secure computation. In 1-out-of-2 OT, a sender holds *(m₀, m₁)* and a receiver holds a choice bit *b*; the receiver learns *m_b* while the sender learns nothing about *b* and the receiver learns nothing about *m_{1−b}*. Every protocol in this thesis consumes OT in bulk — Yao needs one OT per evaluator input bit [1,3], GMW needs OT per AND gate [2] — which is why OT *extension* [6] is the enabling technology of practical MPC.

### 2.3 Secret Sharing and Beaver Triples

Additive secret sharing splits *x* over a field F into shares *x₁, …, xₙ* with Σ*xᵢ = x*; any *n − 1* shares reveal nothing. Addition is *free* — each party adds shares locally. Multiplication is the hard gate. Beaver [5] showed that a pre-distributed random triple *(a, b, c)* with *c = a·b* reduces secure multiplication to opening two masked values:

> **Theorem (Beaver's circuit randomization [5]).** Given *[x]*, *[y]* and a shared random triple *([a], [b], [c])* with *c = ab*, the parties compute *[z]* of *z = xy* by broadcasting *d = x − a* and *e = y − b* and setting *[z] = [c] + d·[b] + e·[a] + de*.

*Proof sketch.* *(d + a)(e + b) = de + db + ea + ab = xy* using *c = ab*; since *a*, *b* are uniform and independent of *x*, *y*, the opened *d*, *e* are perfect one-time-pad masks. ∎

Our methodology is that of theoretical cryptography: each protocol is an explicit algorithm sequence, its security argued via simulation, its cost measured in rounds, bandwidth per gate, and symmetric-key operations. Notation: κ is the security parameter (128), F a finite field, *[x]* an additive sharing, ⟨*x*⟩ an authenticated sharing. Cost comparisons draw on MP-SPDZ [11], which implements all major variants behind one compiler; historical claims rest on the primary literature [1,2,3,5,6,7,8,9,10].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Yao's Garbled Circuits and Point-and-Permute

Yao's protocol [1,3] evaluates a Boolean circuit *C* for *f* as follows. The garbler assigns every wire *w* two random κ-bit *labels*, *W_w^0* and *W_w^1*, for the semantic values 0 and 1. For each gate *g* with input wires *a*, *b* and output wire *c*, she encrypts the output label for every input combination:

```
γ[i,j] = Enc_{W_a^i, W_b^j}( W_c^{g(i,j)} )    for i, j ∈ {0,1}
```

The four ciphertexts are randomly permuted — the *garbled gate* — hiding *g*'s truth table. The garbler sends all garbled gates plus her own input labels; the evaluator gets his labels via 1-out-of-2 OT per input bit [3], then decrypts exactly one row per gate, recovering exactly one output label. Semantic security (in practice a fixed-key block cipher [7]) guarantees he learns nothing beyond his evaluation path and the output.

**Point-and-permute** eliminates trial decryption: each label carries a random *select bit* *p_w^b*, and rows are ordered by the select bits *(p_a^i, p_b^j)*, so the evaluator decrypts exactly the row indexed by his labels' select bits — a deterministic, free permutation.

**Free-XOR** (Kolesnikov–Schneider) makes XOR gates costless: the garbler fixes a global random offset *R* and sets *W_w^1 = W_w^0 ⊕ R* for every wire, so *W_c^{a⊕b} = W_a^a ⊕ W_b^b* needs no ciphertext. **Half gates** [7] then reduce AND gates to *two* ciphertexts while staying free-XOR-compatible: each AND gate splits into a *garbler half-gate* (garbler knows one input) and an *evaluator half-gate*, each garbled with a single ciphertext:

```
T_G = H(W_a^0) ⊕ H(W_a^1) ⊕ p_b · R        (garbler half-gate)
T_E = H(W_b^0) ⊕ H(W_b^1) ⊕ W_a^0 ⊕ p_a · R (evaluator half-gate)
```

The evaluator recovers *W_c = H(W_a) ⊕ p_a·T_G ⊕ W_b* in the first half and analogously in the second. Zahur, Rosulek, and Evans proved 2κ bits per AND gate optimal within a broad class of garbling schemes [7]; "slicing-and-dicing" later beat the bound at 1.5κ + 5 bits from outside that class. The optimization lineage:

| Scheme | AND-gate cost | XOR-gate cost | Compatible with free-XOR |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classical Yao [1] | 4 ciphertexts | 4 ciphertexts | No |
| Point-and-permute + GRR | 3 ciphertexts | 0 | Partial |
| Free-XOR (Kolesnikov–Schneider) | 3 ciphertexts | 0 | Yes |
| **Half gates [7]** | **2 ciphertexts (2κ bits)** | 0 | Yes |
| Slicing-and-dicing | 1.5κ + 5 bits | 0 | Yes |

The Python sketch below garbles a single AND gate with point-and-permute, illustrating the core data structure:

```python
import hashlib

def garble_and_gate(Wa, Wb, Wc):
    """Wa, Wb, Wc: (label_0, label_1) pairs. Returns garbled table
    indexed by point-and-permute select bits."""
    table = {}
    for i, w_a in enumerate(Wa):
        for j, w_b in enumerate(Wb):
            out = Wc[1] if (i and j) else Wc[0]
            key = hashlib.sha256(w_a + w_b).digest()[:16]
            ct = bytes(x ^ y for x, y in zip(out, key))
            table[(w_a[0] & 1, w_b[0] & 1)] = ct
    return table
# Evaluator with (w_a*, w_b*) decrypts one row:
#   w_c* = table[(lsb(w_a*), lsb(w_b*))] XOR sha256(w_a* + w_b*)[:16]
```

### 4.2 The GMW Protocol and Secret-Sharing Evaluation

Where Yao encrypts the circuit, GMW [2] *distributes* it. Each party *Pᵢ* XOR-shares its input bits, and the parties evaluate gate-by-gate while every wire value stays XOR-shared:

- **XOR and NOT gates are free:** each party XORs shares locally (one party flips its share for NOT).
- **AND gates require interaction:** from shares of *a* and *b*, compute shares of *a ∧ b* without revealing either.

The AND gate is reduced to OT. For two parties, *P₁* holding shares *a₁, b₁* prepares masked cross-terms for each of *P₂*'s four possible share-pairs *(a₂, b₂)*; *P₂* uses two OTs (keyed on *a₂* and *b₂*) to retrieve the consistent mask, and both combine to obtain fresh shares of *a ∧ b*. The multiparty generalization uses 1-out-of-4 OT per AND gate per party pair. Since each AND layer's OTs depend on the previous layer's outputs, GMW's round complexity is linear in circuit *depth* — its principal disadvantage versus Yao's constant rounds — but per-gate symmetric-key work is minimal and it extends to *n* parties with honest majority [2].

The GMW *compiler* [2] lifts semi-honest security to malicious security generically: each party commits to its input and randomness and proves in zero knowledge that every message is consistent with its committed state. It is a feasibility landmark rather than a practical tool, motivating the direct malicious-secure constructions of §4.4.

### 4.3 Oblivious Transfer and the IKNP Extension

Both Yao (one OT per evaluator input bit) and GMW (OTs per AND gate) are OT-hungry: a realistic circuit demands *millions* of OTs, while public-key OT costs milliseconds each. Ishai, Kilian, Nissim, and Petrank [6] resolved this asymmetry with **OT extension**: from κ *base OTs* (done with public-key cryptography) plus only symmetric-key operations, the parties derive *m ≫ κ* effective OTs.

The IKNP construction: the receiver samples a random κ-bit string *s* and, for each of the *m* extended OTs, picks random *tⱼ* defining the matrix row *(tⱼ, tⱼ ⊕ s)*; the sender acts as OT-receiver in κ base OTs to learn, per column *i*, either the *tⱼ* column or the *(tⱼ ⊕ s)* column according to *sᵢ* — a matrix transposition. The sender then holds *qⱼ = tⱼ ⊕ (rⱼ · s)* with *rⱼ* the receiver's choice bit, and masks its messages as *H(j, qⱼ) ⊕ m_{j,0}* and *H(j, qⱼ ⊕ s) ⊕ m_{j,1}*; the receiver, knowing *tⱼ*, unmasks exactly the chosen one. Security reduces to the correlation-robustness of *H*. Amortized cost is a few hashes per OT — orders of magnitude cheaper than public-key OT — and refinements cut communication to essentially one short string per extended OT. OT extension moved Yao's protocol from theoretical curiosity to the engine behind billion-gate garbled-circuit evaluations [6].

```python
P = 2**61 - 1  # Mersenne61 field, as in MP-SPDZ backends [11]

def beaver_multiply(sh_x, sh_y, triple, party_id):
    """One party's share of z = x*y via a Beaver triple [5].
    triple: this party's shares (a_i, b_i, c_i), c = a*b (mod P)."""
    a_i, b_i, c_i = triple
    d, e = broadcast((sh_x - a_i) % P), broadcast((sh_y - b_i) % P)
    z_i = (c_i + d * b_i + e * a_i) % P
    if party_id == 0:
        z_i = (z_i + d * e) % P     # P0 adds the public term
    return z_i
# sum_i z_i = c + d*b + e*a + d*e = (a+d)(b+e) = x*y; d, e mask x, y.
```

### 4.4 SPDZ: Offline/Online Preprocessing with Authenticated Shares

SPDZ (Damgård–Pastro–Smart–Zakarias [8]) targets the strongest standard setting: *n* parties, *malicious* adversary, *dishonest majority* (*t = n − 1*). Its insight is architectural: an **offline phase** generates input-independent correlated randomness with heavy cryptography, and an **online phase** consumes it with cheap field arithmetic and broadcasts.

**Authenticated shares.** Every *x ∈ F* is shared as ⟨*x*⟩ = (*[x]*, *[m(x)]*, *[α]*): an additive sharing *[x]*, a global MAC key *[α]* that is *never reconstructed*, and the MAC *m(x) = α·x*, itself additively shared. Linear operations are local: ⟨*x*⟩ + ⟨*y*⟩ = ⟨*x + y*⟩ and *c*·⟨*x*⟩ = ⟨*cx*⟩ by MAC linearity. Multiplication consumes an *authenticated* Beaver triple (⟨*a*⟩, ⟨*b*⟩, ⟨*c*⟩), *c = ab*:

1. Compute ⟨*d*⟩ = ⟨*x*⟩ − ⟨*a*⟩, ⟨*e*⟩ = ⟨*y*⟩ − ⟨*b*⟩ and *open* *d*, *e*.
2. Set ⟨*z*⟩ = ⟨*c*⟩ + *d*·⟨*b*⟩ + *e*·⟨*a*⟩ + *de* — local given public *d*, *e*.

**The MAC check.** Naive opening would let a corrupt party lie about its share. SPDZ instead opens values *partially* during computation and verifies *all* of them in one batched check at the end. For opened *x₁, …, x_q*, parties sample a public random vector *r*, locally compute ⟨*u*⟩ = Σ*rⱼ*·(⟨*m(xⱼ)*⟩ − *xⱼ*·⟨*α*⟩), commit to their shares of *u*, then open and check *u = 0*:

> **Theorem (SPDZ MAC-check soundness [8]).** If any opened value is inconsistent with its MAC, the batched check passes with probability at most *q/|F|* (plus a negligible term from the commitment), where *q* is the number of checked values. With |F| ≈ 2¹²⁸, cheating is detected except with negligible probability.

The commit-then-open ordering is essential — it stops a rushing adversary from choosing its share last. On failure the protocol *aborts*, unavoidable without honest majority.

**The offline phase.** Generating authenticated triples under a malicious majority is the protocol's hard core. The original SPDZ [8] used somewhat-homomorphic encryption (SHE): parties encrypt shares, homomorphically combine them into encryptions of *a*, *b*, *ab*, and attach zero-knowledge proofs of plaintext knowledge plus *sacrificing* — extra triples whose random linear combinations are checked to catch malformed ones. **MASCOT** [9] replaced SHE with OT-based triple generation (actively secure OT extension with consistency checks), improving concrete efficiency by roughly an order of magnitude. **Overdrive** [10] returned to packed SHE, pushing triple generation toward hundreds of thousands per second on commodity hardware. All three plug into the same online phase, and all are available in MP-SPDZ [11].

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Security Arguments

**Security arguments.** Yao's semi-honest security is proved via a simulator that, given only the output, fabricates a garbled circuit evaluating to it; indistinguishability follows from the encryption's security — the first complete proof is Lindell and Pinkas [3]. Malicious security comes from *cut-and-choose*: the garbler produces *s* circuits, the evaluator opens a random subset and evaluates the rest (cheating probability 2^{−s}); Lindell–Pinkas [4] gave the first fully simulated, concretely efficient instantiation. GMW's semi-honest security follows from the secret-sharing invariant plus OT security [2]; the GMW compiler extends it to malicious adversaries at generic-ZK cost. SPDZ's malicious security [8] rests on (i) *correct* authenticated triples from the offline phase [9], and (ii) the online MAC check, letting the simulator extract the adversary's effective inputs from the opened *d*, *e* values.

**Empirical landscape.** MP-SPDZ [11] — over thirty protocol variants behind one compiler — makes the trade-offs concrete. Representative orders of magnitude on LAN hardware:

| Protocol | Adversary | Rounds | Online comm. per AND gate | Offline cost per triple |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yao + half gates [7] | Semi-honest, 2PC | *O(1)* | 2κ bits (≈ 32 B) | None (OT extension amortized) |
| GMW [2] | Semi-honest, honest majority | *O(depth)* | *O(n)* OTs (extension-amortized) | None |
| SPDZ [8] | Malicious, dishonest majority | *O(mult. depth)* | 2 field elements opened | SHE/OT-based [9,10] |
| MASCOT [9] | Malicious, dishonest majority | *O(mult. depth)* | Same online as SPDZ | ~10× faster than SHE-SPDZ |

The AES-128 circuit — the field's standard benchmark, roughly 34,000 gates with only about 6,800 non-XOR (AND) gates after free-XOR optimization — evaluates in well under a second with semi-honest garbled circuits on commodity hardware, while SPDZ-family evaluation pays a modest online overhead but a heavy preprocessing bill: generating millions of authenticated triples dominates, with the offline phase often running minutes to hours ahead of a seconds-long online phase. This asymmetry is a *feature*: it matches deployments (periodic auctions, joint risk scoring) where parties precompute long before inputs arrive.

## 6. Limitations

**Fairness and guaranteed output.** Cleve's impossibility result is absolute: with a dishonest majority, no protocol guarantees all parties receive the output or prevents abort-after-learning. SPDZ provides security *with abort* only; fairness needs an honest majority or external enforcement (deposits, contracts).

**Preprocessing bottleneck.** SPDZ shifts cost rather than eliminating it. Authenticated triple generation — via SHE [8], OT [9], or packed SHE [10] — remains heavy, often dominating total runtime by one to two orders of magnitude over the online phase. Silent preprocessing and pseudorandom correlation generators are active research aimed at this gap.

**Malicious OT and cut-and-choose overhead.** Lifting Yao to malicious security via cut-and-choose [4] multiplies garbling work by the statistical security parameter (tens to hundreds of circuits historically; modern refinements reduce this but never to one). The GMW compiler [2] is asymptotically polynomial but concretely impractical due to generic zero-knowledge proofs.

**Function representation.** All three families evaluate circuits, not RAM programs; data-dependent control flow must be unrolled into circuits, leaking a runtime upper bound and often exploding in size. Oblivious-RAM techniques mitigate this at significant constant-factor cost.

**Assumption fragility.** Yao and GMW rely on symmetric-key primitives plus OT; SPDZ's offline phase adds SHE/OT hardness assumptions. None are post-quantum secure in standard instantiations — lattice-based variants exist but are not yet practical at scale.

## 7. Conclusion

Yao's garbled circuits [1,3], the GMW protocol [2], and SPDZ [8] trace a clean trajectory: from Yao's constant-round encryption of truth tables, through GMW's distributed gate-by-gate secret sharing, to SPDZ's authenticated preprocessing that made *malicious, dishonest-majority* MPC practical. Each step preserved the previous step's core trick — Yao's label encryption became GMW's OT-based AND gate, whose Beaver-triple generalization [5] became SPDZ's authenticated multiplication — while hardening the adversary model. The enabling technologies were the cost reducers: point-and-permute, free-XOR, and half gates [7]; IKNP OT extension [6]; and MASCOT [9] and Overdrive [10] for preprocessing. Forty years after Yao's millionaires, the open frontier is *efficiency at scale*: silent preprocessing, post-quantum instantiations, and RAM-model MPC without circuit blowup. The pattern so far — a clever algebraic identity, an aggressive preprocessing split, relentless engineering in frameworks like MP-SPDZ [11] — suggests these will yield too.
