# Learned Indexes for Read-Heavy Key–Value Stores: Recursive Model Indexing, Piecewise Linear Approximation, and Gapped Learned Structures

## Abstract

Classical ordered indexes — B+ trees, adaptive radix trees, and binary search over sorted arrays — treat the key distribution as adversarial and pay a logarithmic price per lookup regardless of structure present in the data. The learned-index paradigm, introduced by Kraska et al. [1], reframes index lookup as empirical distribution function regression: a model approximating the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the keys predicts the position of a query key in a sorted array, and a bounded "last-mile" search corrects the residual error. This thesis presents a unified treatment of three mature learned-index designs for read-heavy key–value stores — the Recursive Model Index (RMI) with its hierarchy of piecewise linear regression models, the PGM-index with optimal piecewise linear approximation under a worst-case error bound, and ALEX with model-guided gapped arrays and exponential search — together with the methodology and findings of the Search on Sorted Data (SOSD) benchmark on 200M-key datasets [4]. We derive the approximation-theoretic foundations, analyze space–time complexity bounds, dissect why benchmark results systematically favor learned structures on low-entropy distributions, and characterize the failure modes — adversarial distributions, write-heavy drift, and poisoning attacks — that bound their deployment.

---

## 1. Introduction

Indexing is among the oldest problems in computer science, and its solutions have long been distribution-agnostic. A B+ tree guarantees $O(\log_B n)$ I/Os for any key set of size $n$; binary search guarantees $\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$ comparisons. These guarantees are worst-case optimal against an adversary, but the keys in real key–value stores — timestamps, user IDs, geospatial cell identifiers, popularity ranks — are emphatically not adversarial: their empirical CDFs are often smooth and richly structured.

> **Theorem:** (Index-as-model correspondence) For any sorted array $A$ of $n$ keys, the function $F(k) = |\{x \in A : x \le k\}| / n$ — the empirical CDF — satisfies that $n \cdot F(k)$ equals the rank of $k$. Any index that maps a key to its position in $A$ is therefore an approximator of the empirical CDF, and any CDF approximator with uniform error $\varepsilon$ yields a correct index after a search over a window of at most $2\varepsilon$ positions [1][5].

This correspondence is the foundation of the learned-index program. Rather than storing routing keys in a tree, a learned index *fits* the CDF with a compact statistical model and corrects the prediction error with a bounded local search. The consequences are striking: on 200M-key datasets, SOSD reports that a well-tuned RMI answers lookups in roughly 70–270 ns where a B-tree needs 520–610 ns and plain binary search 770–800 ns, at a space overhead of a few percent rather than the 16%+ typical of trees [4].

Three design generations have emerged since 2018, each addressing a weakness of its predecessor:

1. **RMI (Recursive Model Index)** [1] — a hand-tuned hierarchy of models trading space for accuracy via CDFShop optimization;
2. **PGM-index** [2] — optimal piecewise linear approximation with provable worst-case bounds, removing the tuning burden;
3. **ALEX** [3] — an updatable learned index combining models with gapped arrays and exponential search, extending the paradigm to read-write workloads.

This thesis develops all three in a common analytic framework, then situates them within the SOSD benchmark [4] and its follow-on studies, which remain the most rigorous empirical evaluation of learned indexes at the 200M-key scale.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The classical landscape

Before learned indexes, the read-heavy ordered-index landscape was dominated by three families:

| Structure | Lookup time | Space overhead | Strength |
|-----------|-------------|----------------|----------|
| Binary search (sorted array) | $O(\log n)$ comparisons | 0% | Zero overhead, cache friendly |
| B+ tree | $O(\log_B n)$ | ~16% [4] | Range scans, updates |
| Adaptive Radix Tree (ART) | $O(k)$, $k$ = key bytes | 25–47% [4] | Byte-wise key decomposition |

All three are *distribution-oblivious*: their lookup cost is independent of the shape of the key CDF. Interpolation search is the classical exception — it exploits uniformity to achieve $O(\log \log n)$ expected time — but it degrades to $O(n)$ on adversarial data and is rarely used in production. Learned indexes can be understood as a *robust, bounded-error generalization of interpolation search*.

### 2.2 The CDF view of indexing

Given sorted keys $k_1 < k_2 < \dots < k_n$, define the empirical CDF as above. A learned index is a function $\hat{F}$ such that the predicted position $\hat{p}(q) = \hat{F}(q) \cdot n$ satisfies $|\hat{p}(q) - \mathrm{rank}(q)| \le \varepsilon$ for a known error bound $\varepsilon$ (possibly data-dependent). Lookup then consists of (i) model evaluation, a handful of arithmetic operations, and (ii) a local search — binary or exponential — over $[\hat{p} - \varepsilon, \hat{p} + \varepsilon]$.

### 2.3 RadixSpline and the single-pass alternative

RadixSpline (Kipf et al., 2020), evaluated in SOSD [4], is the minimalist design: a single-pass error-bounded linear spline over the CDF accelerated by a radix table, with under 1% space overhead and latency competitive with RMI on smooth datasets.

## 3. Methodology

Our treatment follows a three-part methodology: (i) formal construction of each index with complexity analysis; (ii) an approximation-theoretic account of *why* learned indexes beat trees — via CDF smoothness and the cost structure of modern CPUs; and (iii) synthesis of SOSD benchmark evidence at the 200M-key scale [4], cross-checked against the ALEX [3] and PGM-index [2] primary evaluations.

For complexity notation, let $n$ be the number of keys, $m$ the number of model parameters or segments, and $\varepsilon$ the worst-case prediction error in positions. We assume 64-bit integer keys stored in a sorted array, single-threaded in-memory lookup, and report both asymptotic bounds and measured nanosecond-scale latencies.

### 3.1 Fitting the CDF: regression vs. approximation

Two distinct mathematical traditions underlie learned indexes:

- **Regression (RMI, ALEX):** minimize mean squared error over the data. Cheap to fit, but error bounds are measured empirically after training — no worst-case guarantee.
- **Approximation (PGM-index):** construct a piecewise linear function with *guaranteed* maximum absolute error $\varepsilon$, minimizing the number of segments — a geometric curve-fitting problem solvable in linear time [2].

The distinction matters: regression adapts to the *average* case and can be tuned by an optimizer (CDFShop), while approximation controls the *worst* case and yields provable bounds. SOSD's regression analysis [4] shows that both can reach the Pareto frontier, but they get there by different routes.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Recursive Model Index: staged piecewise regression

The RMI [1] organizes models into $L$ stages. Stage 0 contains a single root model $f^{(0)}$; each model in stage $\ell$ routes the key to one of $B$ models in stage $\ell+1$ by computing $j = \lfloor B \cdot f^{(\ell)}(q) \rfloor$ (clamped); leaf models predict the position directly. Formally, with two stages:

$$\hat{p}(q) = f^{(1)}_{j}(q), \quad j = \big\lfloor B \cdot f^{(0)}(q) \big\rfloor$$

Training proceeds top-down: the root is trained on the full CDF, the key space is partitioned by the root's prediction into $B$ shards, and each second-stage model is trained on its shard. Each model is typically linear regression $f(x) = ax + b$, and per-model min/max error statistics define the last-mile search window.

The RMI's power comes from *sharding the hard problem*: a globally non-linear CDF decomposes into shards that are locally near-linear. Its principal weakness is the tuning burden — the large design space (stages, branching factor, model types) requires CDFShop grid-search optimization [1][4].

```python
import numpy as np

def train_two_stage_rmi(keys, B):
    # Fit a 2-stage RMI with linear regression per model.
    n = len(keys)
    ranks = np.arange(n, dtype=np.float64)
    # Stage 0: linear regression on full CDF
    a0, b0 = np.polyfit(keys, ranks / n, 1)
    # Shard keys by root prediction
    shard = np.clip((a0 * keys + b0) * B, 0, B - 1).astype(int)
    models, err = [], np.zeros(B)
    for j in range(B):
        m = shard == j
        if m.sum() > 1:
            a, b = np.polyfit(keys[m], ranks[m], 1)
            pred = a * keys[m] + b
            err[j] = np.max(np.abs(pred - ranks[m]))
        else:
            a, b = 0.0, ranks[m][0] if m.sum() else 0.0
        models.append((a, b))
    return (a0, b0), models, err

def rmi_lookup(q, root, models, B, keys):
    a0, b0 = root
    j = int(np.clip((a0 * q + b0) * B, 0, B - 1))
    a, b = models[j]
    p = int(a * q + b)
    # last-mile search would scan [p - err[j], p + err[j]]
    return p
```

On SOSD's 64-bit datasets, tuned RMIs achieve 54–402 ns per lookup at ~3% space overhead [4] — but the 402 ns figure on `osmc64` (a 1-D projection of 2-D spatial data, notoriously hard to model) shows the cost of irregular CDFs: more stages, larger error windows, slower lookups.

### 4.2 The PGM-index: optimal piecewise linear approximation

Ferragina and Vinciguerra recast learned indexing as a problem in computational geometry [2]. Plot each key as the point $(k_i, i)$ — key on the horizontal axis, rank on the vertical. A learned index is a *curve fitting* of this point set: find the minimum-complexity function $h$ with $|h(k_i) - i| \le \varepsilon$ for all $i$. Restricting $h$ to piecewise linear functions yields the PGM-index, and the key algorithmic result is:

> **Theorem:** (Optimal PLA) Given $n$ points and error bound $\varepsilon$, the minimum number of segments of a piecewise linear $\varepsilon$-approximation can be computed in $O(n)$ time, and the resulting segmentation is optimal [2].

The construction is recursive: the bottom level is the optimal PLA of the key set; each higher level is the optimal PLA of the *first key of each segment* of the level below (a classic "index of indexes" reminiscent of B-trees, but over learned segments rather than key ranges). Querying descends the levels: at each level, the segment covering $q$ is found by binary search over the $O(m)$ segment keys, the linear model predicts the position within $\pm\varepsilon$, and the final level's prediction is corrected by binary search over a $2\varepsilon$ window. Total query time is $O(\log m \cdot \text{height} + \log \varepsilon)$, with height typically 2–4.

The PGM-index's signature achievements are its *provable worst-case bounds* — the first learned index to offer them — and its *parameter-freeness*: $\varepsilon$ is the only knob, directly trading space ($m$ segments) against search cost. Three variants extend the design: a distribution-aware variant adapting to query skew, a compressed variant exploiting model repetitiveness, and a fully dynamic multicriteria variant supporting updates with logarithmic amortized cost [2].

> **Corollary:** Because the PLA construction is optimal, no piecewise linear $\varepsilon$-index can use fewer segments; the PGM-index is therefore space-optimal within its model class. Empirically, $\varepsilon = 64$ on 200M keys yields only thousands of segments — kilobytes of index [2].

Gæde et al. [6] extend this geometric view to dynamic maintenance with $O(\log^2 n)$ updates and worst-case query guarantees, while Ferragina and Vinciguerra's follow-up [5] shows the PGM-index remains competitive in 0.05%-overhead small-space regimes.

### 4.3 ALEX: gapped arrays and model-guided exponential search

RMI and PGM-index are *static*: they assume a fixed sorted array. ALEX (Ding et al., 2020) [3] brings learned indexing to read-write workloads by redesigning the storage layer around the model. Its two core innovations:

1. **Gapped arrays.** Each data node stores keys in an array with deliberate gaps, positioned according to the node's linear model: key $k$ is placed near position $\lfloor a k + b \rfloor$. If the model is accurate, inserts land in empty gaps with no shifting; the *density* of each node (fraction filled) is kept within bounds, and nodes expand, split, or merge based on a cost model weighing expected lookup benefit against retraining cost.

2. **Exponential search for last-mile correction.** When a prediction is off, ALEX searches *exponentially outward* from the predicted position rather than binary searching a fixed window — $O(\log d)$ for true error $d$, beating binary search's $O(\log \varepsilon)$ whenever $d \ll \varepsilon$.

The result is dramatic on read-heavy workloads: ALEX beats the original learned index by up to 2.2× on read-only workloads with up to 15× smaller index size, and beats B+ trees by up to 4.1× across read-write mixes while never performing worse, with up to 2000× smaller index footprint [3]. The cost model is the subtle machinery: it tracks per-node statistics (number of inserts, prediction accuracy, density) and triggers structural modifications only when the expected benefit exceeds the retraining cost — effectively a learned *self-tuning* index.

```python
def exponential_search(arr, q, predicted):
    # Search outward from predicted position; O(log d) for true error d.
    n = len(arr)
    if arr[predicted] == q:
        return predicted
    if arr[predicted] < q:
        bound = 1
        while predicted + bound < n and arr[predicted + bound] < q:
            bound *= 2
        lo, hi = predicted + bound // 2, min(predicted + bound, n - 1)
    else:
        bound = 1
        while predicted - bound >= 0 and arr[predicted - bound] > q:
            bound *= 2
        lo, hi = max(predicted - bound, 0), predicted - bound // 2
    import bisect
    i = bisect.bisect_left(arr, q, lo, hi + 1)
    return i if i <= hi and arr[i] == q else -1
```

### 4.4 Why learned indexes win: a micro-architectural account

SOSD's most surprising finding is not *that* learned indexes win but *why* [4]. A linear-regression analysis of lookup latency against candidate explanatory metrics (cache misses, branch misses, instruction count, model error) found that no single metric explains performance — a multi-metric view is required. Key insights:

- **Model inference is cheap; pointer chasing is expensive.** Evaluating a linear model is a few register-resident multiply-adds. A B-tree traversal is 4–5 dependent pointer dereferences, each a potential ~90 ns DRAM miss. Learned indexes replace dependent memory accesses with arithmetic plus one localized search.
- **Branch misses can help.** Counter-intuitively, the regression coefficient on branch misses was *negative*: at fixed cache-miss and instruction counts, indexes incurring more branch misses did better — aggressive strategies trade instructions for fewer cache misses, the true bottleneck [4].
- **The error–inference frontier.** For a fixed $\log_2$ error of 7, an RMI achieves 250 ns on `amzn` while a PGM-index needs 480 ns — the PGM's higher inference cost (segment binary search) outweighs its comparable accuracy. Model class choice is a first-order performance decision, not a detail [4].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The SOSD benchmark design

SOSD (Marcus et al., 2020) [4] is the definitive benchmark: eight datasets, each 200M 64-bit keys — `amzn` (book popularity), `face` (Facebook IDs), `logn`/`norm` (lognormal/normal synthetic), `osmc` (OpenStreetMap S2 cell IDs), `uden`/`uspr` (dense/sparse uniform integers), `wiki` (Wikipedia timestamps) — with 10M uniform equality lookups per dataset, single-threaded, on AWS c5.4xlarge. It compares RMI, RadixSpline, PGM, ART, B-tree, binary search, FAST, and interpolation search variants.

Representative 64-bit lookup latencies (ns) [4]:

| Dataset | RMI | RadixSpline | B-tree | Binary search | ART |
|---------|-----|-------------|--------|---------------|-----|
| amzn64 | 266 | 288 | 601 | 804 | n/a |
| face64 | 334 | 461 | 592 | 784 | 391 |
| logn64 | 179 | 120 | 597 | 784 | 309 |
| norm64 | 71.5 | 70.5 | 592 | 785 | 266 |
| osmc64 | 402 | 437 | 599 | 785 | n/a |
| uden64 | 54.3 | 53.9 | 592 | 784 | 112 |
| uspr64 | 169 | 214 | 591 | 785 | 287 |
| wiki64 | 222 | 218 | 608 | 802 | n/a |

Size overhead: RMI ~3%, RadixSpline and PGM <1%, B-tree 16%, ART 25% [4].

Three conclusions follow. First, **learned indexes dominate B-trees on every dataset** — 2–8× faster at a fraction of the space. Second, **dataset difficulty is CDF irregularity**: smooth distributions yield sub-100 ns lookups; the jagged `osmc` CDF costs 400+ ns. Third, **RMI vs. RadixSpline vs. PGM is a wash at the frontier** — the choice depends on build-time budget and tuning tolerance.

### 5.2 Proof sketch: the PGM-index query bound

> **Theorem:** (PGM query complexity) A PGM-index built with error $\varepsilon$ answers predecessor queries in $O(\log m \cdot h + \log \varepsilon)$ time and occupies $\Theta(m)$ space, where $m$ is the number of bottom-level segments and $h$ the index height [2].

*Proof sketch.* Each level is a PLA over the segment keys of the level below with the same error $\varepsilon$; evaluating the model locates the correct child segment within $\pm\varepsilon$, corrected by binary search in $O(\log \varepsilon)$. Finding the starting segment per level costs $O(\log m_\ell)$ over sorted segment keys. Summing over $h$ levels plus the final $O(\log \varepsilon)$ correction gives the bound; space is $\Theta(m)$ since each higher level has at most as many segments as the one below. ∎

### 5.3 ALEX under read-write pressure

On read-write YCSB-style workloads, ALEX's gapped arrays pay off: inserts landing in gaps cost $O(1)$ plus model evaluation, versus $O(\log n)$ node splits in B-trees, with measured speedups up to 4.1× on mixed workloads [3]. The failure mode is *model drift*: adversarial insert patterns degrade prediction accuracy and trigger cascading splits — the cost model detects this and retrains, but retraining is $O(n)$ per node, so sustained adversarial writes can keep ALEX in a retraining loop.

## 6. Limitations

**Adversarial and high-entropy distributions.** Learned indexes exploit CDF structure; on near-uniform random keys with fine-grained irregularity (SOSD's `osmc64`), the advantage over B-trees shrinks to ~1.5× while tuning effort grows. A formal lower bound: any piecewise linear $\varepsilon$-approximation of a $1$-Lipschitz-but-nowhere-smooth CDF needs $\Omega(n/\varepsilon)$ segments, erasing the space advantage.

**Write amplification and drift.** Static designs (RMI, PGM) require full rebuilds on bulk updates; ALEX handles inserts but its cost model assumes the future resembles the past. Poisoning attacks show a sharper vulnerability: inserting just 10% adversarial keys can inflate the PGM-index's optimal segment count by up to 120×, an intrinsic weakness of the PLA objective itself [2].

**Tuning and build cost.** RMI's CDFShop grid search can take minutes to hours on 200M keys; the PGM-index builds in linear time but its single $\varepsilon$ knob still requires workload knowledge.

**Last-mile search dominance.** As datasets grow, the $O(\log \varepsilon)$ correction term becomes a floor: even a perfect model cannot beat the cost of verifying the key, so learned-index speedups saturate. SOSD's regression analysis confirms that beyond a point, shrinking model error further does not reduce latency [4].

**Range queries and secondary indexes.** The literature — including SOSD — focuses on point lookups and lower bounds. Multi-dimensional and string-key learned indexes remain active research with weaker guarantees.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Learned indexes have crossed from provocative idea to engineering reality. The trajectory from RMI's hand-tuned model hierarchies [1] through the PGM-index's optimal, provably bounded piecewise linear approximation [2] to ALEX's model-guided gapped arrays for dynamic workloads [3] shows a field converging on a stable abstraction: *the index as a bounded-error CDF model plus a local search*. The SOSD benchmark [4] provides the empirical anchor — on 200M-key datasets, learned structures answer lookups 2–8× faster than B-trees at <3% space overhead — and its micro-architectural analysis explains why: arithmetic replaces pointer chasing, and the memory hierarchy rewards prediction over traversal. Open problems are now well-defined: robustness to drift, principled auto-tuning, worst-case-guaranteed dynamic structures [6], and poisoning-resistant objectives. The practical recommendation: PGM-index or RadixSpline for static workloads, tuned RMI when the last nanoseconds count, ALEX when writes cannot be ignored.
