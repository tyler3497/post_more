# Spiking Neural Networks and Neuromorphic Computing: From Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Dynamics to Event-Driven Hardware

## Abstract

This thesis develops a rigorous treatment of spiking neural networks (SNNs) and neuromorphic computing. Beginning from the leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron as the canonical spiking abstraction, we derive its subthreshold dynamics, discretization, and the credit-assignment barrier posed by the non-differentiable spike mechanism. We then present the surrogate-gradient framework that makes backpropagation through time (BPTT) tractable in deep SNNs, contrast it with spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) as a local Hebbian learning rule, and formalize ANN-to-SNN conversion through rate coding and threshold balancing. Finally, we survey the three dominant neuromorphic architectures — IBM TrueNorth, Intel Loihi, and the University of Manchester's SpiNNaker — and quantify their energy efficiency against the von Neumann baseline. We conclude that event-driven sparse computation, not merely spike-based representation, is the operative source of neuromorphic advantage, and we identify the accuracy–latency–sparsity trilemma as the field's central open problem.

## 1 Introduction

Deep learning has succeeded overwhelmingly on clocked, synchronous, von Neumann substrates: dense matrix multiplications executed on GPUs, with activations represented as floating-point numbers. The human brain — the existence proof of efficient intelligence — does none of this. It computes with ~86 billion neurons communicating through stereotyped electrical pulses (action potentials, or *spikes*), asynchronously, sparsely, and at a total power budget of roughly 20 watts.

Spiking neural networks formalize this third generation of neural models [7]. Where a conventional artificial neural network (ANN) propagates real-valued activations through layers in lockstep, an SNN propagates *events*: binary impulses in time. Computation is *event-driven* — a neuron consumes energy and occupies downstream synapses only when it fires — which suggests the possibility of orders-of-magnitude energy savings for inference workloads dominated by sparse activations.

Three threads define the modern field. **First**, neuron models: the leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron and its adaptive extensions provide the dynamical substrate. **Second**, learning algorithms: because the spike-generation mechanism is non-differentiable, classical backpropagation fails, and the field has split between *local plasticity rules* (STDP) and *surrogate-gradient descent*, which re-enables gradient-based optimization. **Third**, hardware: neuromorphic processors such as TrueNorth [5], Loihi [4], and SpiNNaker [8] physically instantiate event-driven computation, escaping the memory-wall bottleneck of the von Neumann architecture.

This thesis unifies these threads. We derive the LIF model (§2), present the surrogate-gradient method with full mathematical detail (§4.1), analyze STDP as a correlation-based rule (§4.2), formalize ANN-to-SNN conversion (§4.3), and benchmark neuromorphic hardware (§4.4). We close with empirical energy-efficiency analysis (§5), limitations (§6), and conclusions (§7).

## 2 Background: The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron

### 2.1 Continuous dynamics

The LIF neuron models the membrane potential $U(t)$ as a leaky integrator driven by input current $I(t)$:

$$\tau_m \frac{dU}{dt} = -(U(t) - U_{\mathrm{rest}}) + R\, I(t)$$

where $\tau_m = RC$ is the membrane time constant, $R$ the membrane resistance, and $U_{\mathrm{rest}}$ the resting potential. When $U(t)$ reaches the threshold $V_{\mathrm{th}}$, the neuron emits a spike, $S(t) = \delta(t - t_f)$, and the potential resets to $U_{\mathrm{reset}}$ (possibly with a refractory period during which integration is disabled).

> **Theorem (Integrate-and-fire latency):** For constant current $I > (V_{\mathrm{th}} - U_{\mathrm{rest}})/R$, the first-spike latency of the LIF neuron is $t_f = -\tau_m \ln\!\left(1 - \frac{V_{\mathrm{th}} - U_{\mathrm{rest}}}{RI}\right)$. The steady-state firing rate is therefore $r(I) = \left[t_{\mathrm{ref}} - \tau_m \ln\!\left(1 - \frac{V_{\mathrm{th}} - U_{\mathrm{rest}}}{RI}\right)\right)\right]^{-1}$, a saturating nonlinearity analogous to the ReLU-to-sigmoid family.

This rate–current relation is the theoretical basis of *rate coding*: firing frequency carries the activation magnitude, which underwrites ANN-to-SNN conversion (§4.3).

### 2.2 Discrete-time form for simulation and training

Neuromorphic simulators and GPU training discretize the LIF dynamics with time step $\Delta t$. Defining the leak factor $\beta = \exp(-\Delta t / \tau_m)$ and the integrated input $x[t]$, the iterative model used by [2] is:

```
U[t] = β · (U[t−1] − V_reset·S[t−1]) + x[t]     # subthreshold integration
S[t] = H(U[t] − V_th)                             # Heaviside spike mechanism
```

where $H(\cdot)$ is the Heaviside step function. This recurrence exposes the fundamental difficulty of SNN training: the forward pass is perfectly well-defined, but $\frac{dS}{dU} = \delta(U - V_{\mathrm{th}})$ — the Dirac delta — is zero almost everywhere and infinite at the threshold. Standard automatic differentiation yields *dead gradients*: no learning signal propagates through silent or subthreshold neurons.

### 2.3 Why spikes are hard to train

The dead-gradient problem interacts with a second pathology. An unrolled SNN is a recurrent network with shared weights across time; backpropagation through time (BPTT) across $T$ time steps multiplies Jacobian factors that depend on the membrane trajectory. As shown by [6], the implicitly recurrent nature of feedforward SNNs induces severe exploding/vanishing gradients unless the surrogate derivative and membrane leak are carefully calibrated. Training deep SNNs therefore requires *both* a differentiable substitute for the spike mechanism *and* normalization of the temporal dynamics — addressed respectively by surrogate gradients and membrane-potential batch normalization.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds along four axes:

1. **Neuron model derivation.** We work with the discrete-time iterative LIF model above, with optional adaptive thresholds ($V_{\mathrm{th}}[t] = b_0 + \eta\, a[t]$, $a[t] = \rho a[t-1] + S[t-1]$) for sparse-firing variants.
2. **Surrogate-gradient learning.** We replace $\frac{dS}{dU}$ with smooth approximants and derive BPTT through the unrolled dynamics, following the taxonomy of [1].
3. **Local plasticity (STDP).** We analyze the classical asymmetric Hebbian window and its relation to stochastic gradient descent on correlation objectives.
4. **Hardware benchmarking.** We compare TrueNorth, Loihi, and SpiNNaker on process node, neuron/synapse capacity, power, and synaptic operations per second per watt (SOPS/W), contrasting with GPU baselines via Horowitz's energy estimates for the memory wall.

All equations use $t \in \{1, \dots, T\}$ for discrete time, $U$ for membrane potential, $S \in \{0,1\}$ for spikes, and $W$ for synaptic weights.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Surrogate Gradients: Differentiating the Non-Differentiable

The surrogate-gradient method [1] resolves the dead-gradient problem with a pragmatic separation: use the exact, non-differentiable spike function in the *forward* pass, but substitute a smooth *surrogate derivative* in the *backward* pass. Formally, define the spike function $S = \Theta(U - V_{\mathrm{th}})$ and a surrogate $\sigma(U)$ with $\sigma'(U)$ replacing $\Theta'(U)$ during backpropagation.

Common surrogate derivatives (parameterized by steepness $\alpha$) include:

| Surrogate | $\tilde{\sigma}'(u)$, $u = U - V_{\mathrm{th}}$ | Support |
|---|---|---|
| Fast sigmoid | $\frac{1}{(1 + \|\alpha u\|)^2}$ | Global, heavy tails |
| Arctangent | $\frac{\alpha}{2(1 + (\frac{\pi}{2}\alpha u)^2)}$ | Global, moderate tails |
| Triangular | $\max(0, 1 - \alpha\|u\|)$ | Local, $\|u\| < 1/\alpha$ |
| Rectangular (boxcar) | $\mathbf{1}_{\|u\| < \alpha/2}$ | Local, hard window |

With the surrogate in place, BPTT through the unrolled LIF dynamics yields the spatio-temporal backpropagation (STBP) equations [2]:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial S[t]} \frac{\partial S[t]}{\partial U[t]} \frac{\partial U[t]}{\partial I[t]} \frac{\partial I[t]}{\partial W}$$

where $\frac{\partial S[t]}{\partial U[t]}$ is replaced by the surrogate, and $\frac{\partial U[t]}{\partial U[t-1]} = \beta (1 - S[t-1])$ propagates error through the membrane's leaky memory. The reset term $(1 - S[t-1])$ gates the temporal gradient: fired neurons forget their past, which regularizes gradient explosion.

Three design decisions dominate practical performance:

- **Surrogate shape.** Sharper surrogates ($\alpha$ large) concentrate credit near threshold and sharpen decision boundaries but shrink the population receiving gradients; wider surrogates stabilize early training. Learnable and asymmetric surrogates [recent work] adapt $\alpha$ per layer.
- **Temporal batch normalization.** Normalizing the input current $x[t]$ across the batch (as in [6]) prevents membrane saturation and keeps $U[t]$ in the surrogate's active region.
- **Latency–accuracy trade-off.** STBP-trained SNNs reach ANN-level accuracy on CIFAR-100 with as few as $T = 10$ time steps [6], versus ~1000 steps for conversion-based SNNs — direct training buys latency at the cost of training complexity.

```python
class LIFNode(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, tau=2.0, v_th=1.0, surrogate_alpha=4.0):
        super().__init__()
        self.beta = math.exp(-1.0 / tau)   # leak factor per dt=1
        self.v_th = v_th
        self.alpha = surrogate_alpha
        self.v = 0.0
    def forward(self, x):
        # forward: exact Heaviside; backward: fast-sigmoid surrogate
        self.v = self.beta * self.v + x    # integrate (no reset shown for brevity)
        spike = SpikeFn.apply(self.v - self.v_th, self.alpha)
        self.v = self.v * (1.0 - spike.detach())  # hard reset
        return spike

class SpikeFn(torch.autograd.Function):
    @staticmethod
    def forward(ctx, u, alpha):
        ctx.alpha = alpha
        return (u >= 0).float()
    @staticmethod
    def backward(ctx, grad_out):
        # d/du fast-sigmoid surrogate: 1 / (1 + |alpha*u|)^2
        u = ctx.saved_tensors[0] if ctx.saved_tensors else None
        return grad_out / (1 + (ctx.alpha * u).abs())**2, None
```

### 4.2 Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity: The Local Alternative

Long before surrogate gradients, computational neuroscience proposed that synapses learn from *local* spike timing alone. Spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) strengthens a synapse when the presynaptic spike *precedes* the postsynaptic spike (causal, long-term potentiation) and weakens it when the order reverses (long-term depression):

$$\Delta w_{ij} = \begin{cases}
A_+ \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta t}{\tau_+}\right), & \Delta t = t_j^{\mathrm{post}} - t_i^{\mathrm{pre}} > 0 \\[4pt]
-A_- \exp\!\left(\frac{\Delta t}{\tau_-}\right), & \Delta t < 0
\end{cases}$$

STDP is a *Hebbian, unsupervised, local* rule: each update uses only the two spike times at that synapse, requiring no global error signal. This locality is precisely what makes STDP attractive for on-chip learning — Intel Loihi's programmable learning engine implements STDP-like rules directly in silicon [4]. Recent theory connects STDP to stochastic gradient descent on correlation-based objectives, and to surrogate gradients themselves: surrogate-based training can be reframed as *three-factor* learning (pre × post × modulatory signal), unifying global and local views [1].

Empirically, however, pure STDP has not scaled to deep supervised benchmarks: stacking STDP-trained layers yields feature hierarchies (e.g., V1-like Gabor filters) but lags supervised SNNs by 10–20 points of accuracy on CIFAR-10. Its role today is strongest in *continual, online, and few-shot* regimes where backprop is impractical.

### 4.3 ANN-to-SNN Conversion: Inheriting Deep Learning's Wins

Conversion sidesteps SNN training entirely: train a conventional ANN with ReLUs, then map each ReLU unit to an integrate-and-fire neuron whose firing *rate* approximates the ReLU activation [3]. The mapping exploits the rate–current relation of §2.1: with weight normalization, the time-averaged spike rate of the SNN layer converges to the ANN activation:

$$r_i \approx \frac{1}{V_{\mathrm{th}}} \left[\sum_j w_{ij} r_j + b_i\right]_+$$

Three corrections are needed for near-lossless conversion:

1. **Weight/threshold balancing** rescales thresholds per layer to the maximum ANN activation, preventing both saturation and dead neurons [3].
2. **Architectural constraints:** batch normalization must be folded into weights, max-pooling replaced by average-pooling, biases handled via constant input currents, and softmax moved off-chip.
3. **Latency:** because rates must be estimated by counting spikes over time, converted SNNs typically need $T \approx 200$–$1000$ steps, versus $T \approx 10$ for directly trained SNNs [6] — the conversion tax is *latency*, paid at inference.

Conversion remains the highest-accuracy route to SNNs (near-zero accuracy drop on ImageNet with VGG-16 [3]), but its latency cost undermines the very event-driven efficiency it seeks. Calibration methods that reduce $T$ to tens of steps are the active frontier.

### 4.4 Neuromorphic Hardware: TrueNorth, Loihi, and SpiNNaker

Neuromorphic hardware escapes the von Neumann memory wall by *collocating memory and compute* and communicating only via sparse spike events. The three canonical platforms differ sharply in design philosophy:

| Platform | Organization | Neuron model | Learning | Scale | Power |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| IBM TrueNorth (2014) [5] | Digital, 4096 neurosynaptic cores, 2-D mesh | Discrete LIF, programmable | Inference only (off-chip training) | 1M neurons, 256M synapses | ~70 mW typical |
| Intel Loihi (2018) [4] | Digital async, 128 neuro-cores, mesh | Current-based LIF w/ dendritic compartments, delays | On-chip: programmable plasticity (STDP-like), 3-factor rules | 130K neurons, 130M synapses | ~30–100 mW (workload-dep.) |
| SpiNNaker (2014) [8] | 18 ARM968 cores/chip, hexagonal torus | Arbitrary (software-defined: LIF, Izhikevich, …) | Flexible (simulated in software) | 1M+ cores target; billion-neuron scale | ~1 W/chip, ~90 kW full machine |

**TrueNorth** [5] is a 5.4-billion-transistor, 28 nm digital chip: each of 4096 cores holds 256 neurons × 256 synapses of local SRAM, and cores communicate via an asynchronous packet-switched fabric. Because spikes are the only traffic and inactive neurons consume no dynamic power, TrueNorth demonstrated 400×240 video object detection at 30 fps for **63 mW** — over 6000 frames/sec/watt, ~100× the efficiency of contemporary GPUs on the same task.

**Loihi** [4] added what TrueNorth lacked: on-chip learning. Its 14 nm, 60 mm² die integrates 128 neuromorphic cores plus three embedded x86 cores; each neuro-core implements dendritic compartments, synaptic delays, and a programmable learning engine supporting STDP and reward-modulated rules. Loihi 2 extends this with graded (non-binary) spike messages and a fully programmable neuron model, trading strict bio-plausibility for algorithmic flexibility.

**SpiNNaker** [8] takes the opposite approach: general-purpose ARM cores running event-driven neural simulation in software, connected by a bespoke multicast routing fabric optimized for small spike packets. Where TrueNorth and Loihi fix the neuron model in silicon, SpiNNaker can simulate *any* model — it is a neuroscience instrument and a real-time brain-scale simulator (target: 1% of the human brain in real time), not an inference accelerator.

> **Theorem (Event-driven energy bound):** For a network with mean firing rate $r$ spikes/neuron/sec, $N$ neurons, and per-synaptic-operation energy $E_{\mathrm{sop}}$, event-driven inference costs $P = N r \bar{k} E_{\mathrm{sop}}$ where $\bar{k}$ is mean fan-out, versus $P_{\mathrm{vN}} = N \bar{k} E_{\mathrm{MAC}} f$ for clocked dense evaluation at frame rate $f$. The neuromorphic advantage $\frac{P_{\mathrm{vN}}}{P} = \frac{E_{\mathrm{MAC}} f}{E_{\mathrm{sop}} r}$ grows linearly with sparsity ($1/r$) — sparsity, not spikes per se, is the operative variable.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

**Energy-efficiency analysis vs. von Neumann.** The memory wall dominates modern inference: Horowitz's widely cited estimates put a 32-bit DRAM access at ~640 pJ versus ~3.7 pJ for a 32-bit multiply — memory movement, not arithmetic, is the cost center. TrueNorth's 46 GSOPS/W at ~70 mW [5] compares against ~10–20 GOPS/W for GPUs of its era on sparse workloads: a genuine two-to-three-order-of-magnitude gap *on event-sparse tasks*. The caveat is workload dependence: on dense, high-rate workloads the advantage collapses, since $r \to f$ in the bound above.

**Accuracy–latency–sparsity trilemma.** The literature now quantifies a three-way trade-off [1][2][6]:

- *Conversion* [3]: highest accuracy (ANN parity), but $T \sim 10^2$–$10^3$ steps and dense early-layer firing.
- *Direct surrogate-gradient training* [2][6]: $T \sim 10$ steps, ANN-competitive on CIFAR-100, but training costs $O(T)$ memory for BPTT and needs careful surrogate tuning.
- *Sparse/event-coded training*: minimal spikes and maximal efficiency, but a persistent 1–3% accuracy gap and brittle optimization.

**Key numerical results.** Rueckauer et al. [3] converted VGG-16 and Inception-v3 to SNNs with <1% accuracy drop on ImageNet; Ledinauskas et al. [6] trained ResNet-50 SNNs directly with surrogate gradients on CIFAR-100 using only 10 time steps; Loihi demonstrated >3000× better energy-delay product than CPUs on sparse-coding (LASSO) workloads [4]. No method yet dominates all three axes simultaneously.

## 6 Limitations

- **The surrogate is not the gradient.** Surrogate derivatives are biased estimators; the mismatch between the true (Dirac) gradient and the surrogate can cause optimization to converge to solutions that are fragile under the exact dynamics, especially with sharp surrogates and deep stacks [1].
- **BPTT memory scaling.** Direct training stores the full unrolled state ($O(N \cdot T)$), which is prohibitive for long sequences; online alternatives (e-prop, RTRL variants) trade exactness for $O(N)$ memory but remain less mature.
- **Conversion latency.** Rate-coded conversion needs hundreds of time steps; latency-sensitive applications cannot afford the integration window [3][6].
- **Hardware rigidity vs. flexibility.** TrueNorth's fixed neuron model cannot implement new learning rules; SpiNNaker's flexibility costs watts per neuron far above ASICs; Loihi sits between but remains research-access hardware [4][5][8].
- **Benchmark monoculture.** Most SNN results are reported on MNIST/CIFAR and their neuromorphic variants (N-MNIST); evidence on large-scale, real-world event streams remains thin [7].

## 7 Conclusion

Spiking neural networks have matured from a neuroscience curiosity into a credible computing paradigm with a rigorous training theory. The surrogate-gradient framework [1] and spatio-temporal backpropagation [2] closed most of the accuracy gap to ANNs at low latency; ANN-to-SNN conversion [3] provides an immediately deployable bridge; and neuromorphic chips [4][5][8] demonstrate that event-driven architectures can beat von Neumann systems by orders of magnitude on sparse workloads.

The central lesson of this thesis is that *sparsity, not spike-based representation, is the operative source of neuromorphic advantage*: the energy bound of §4.4 shows efficiency gains scaling with $1/r$, and every empirical win — TrueNorth's 63 mW vision pipeline, Loihi's sparse-coding EDP — is a sparsity win. The field's grand challenge is therefore the accuracy–latency–sparsity trilemma: achieving ANN-level accuracy at few time steps with few spikes, on hardware flexible enough to run tomorrow's learning rules. Solving it would move neuromorphic computing from an efficiency curiosity to the default substrate for always-on, edge intelligence.
