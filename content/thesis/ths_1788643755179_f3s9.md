# Cryo-EM Single-Particle Analysis: Maximum-Likelihood 3D Refinement, Contrast Transfer Function Correction, and FSC Resolution Validation

## Abstract

Single-particle cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has matured from a specialist low-resolution imaging technique into a primary method for determining atomic-resolution structures of biological macromolecules. The modern pipeline rests on three computational pillars: *maximum-likelihood 3D refinement*, which treats particle orientations and class assignments as hidden variables in a Bayesian expectation–maximization framework [1][3]; *contrast transfer function (CTF) correction*, which restores phases and amplitudes distorted by the objective lens of the electron microscope [7]; and *Fourier shell correlation (FSC) validation*, which provides a statistically principled, overfitting-resistant measure of map resolution through independent half-set refinement [2][3]. This thesis develops each pillar from first principles — the Fourier slice theorem and the weak-phase image formation model, regularized marginal-likelihood optimization, CTF theory through spherical aberration and astigmatism, and the Rosenthal–Henderson argument that assigns resolution at FSC = 0.143 — and surveys how these methods enabled the resolution revolution after 2012. We analyze benchmark reconstructions, derive the key theorems, and discuss the remaining limits: preferred orientation, beam-induced motion, conformational heterogeneity, and the path toward true atomic resolution.

---

## 1 Introduction

Henderson and Unwin's 1975 7 Å bacteriorhodopsin structure proved electrons could reveal near-atomic protein detail. The following decades added the ingredients: Dubochet's 1988 vitrification, embedding biomolecules in glass-like hydrated ice; Frank's single-particle framework, replacing crystals with computational averaging of noisy projections; and the maximum-likelihood methods of Sigworth and Scheres that made heterogeneous classification rigorous [4][5]. Yet until the early 2010s, reconstructions rarely exceeded 8–10 Å, limited by detector technology.

The turning point arrived with the commercialization of direct electron detectors — first complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) devices in 2012 — whose detective quantum efficiency (DQE) at high spatial frequency dramatically exceeded that of photographic film and CCD cameras [6]. Compensation of beam-induced motion at the level of individual movie frames became possible, and within two years the field produced 3–4 Å maps of membrane proteins and viruses routinely. The 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Dubochet, Frank, and Henderson, formalized the transformation.

This thesis examines the computational core of the modern pipeline. We focus on three interlocking problems, each of which had to be solved for the revolution to occur:

1. **3D refinement and classification** — determining the unknown projection orientations of up to millions of particle images, and sorting conformationally heterogeneous particles into homogeneous classes, using *maximum-likelihood* (ML) and *maximum-a-posteriori* (MAP) estimation solved by expectation–maximization [1][3].
2. **CTF estimation and correction** — measuring and inverting the oscillatory, sign-flipping contrast transfer function that modulates every micrograph, including defocus, astigmatism, and higher-order aberrations [7].
3. **Resolution validation** — quantifying the resolution of a reconstruction without circularity, through the gold-standard FSC procedure and the 0.143 threshold criterion derived by Rosenthal and Henderson [2][3].

The argument proceeds from the physics of image formation (Section 2), through the standard computational pipeline (Section 3), into detailed technical treatments of each pillar (Section 4), empirical benchmarks and proofs (Section 5), limitations (Section 6), and conclusions (Section 7).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Image formation and the projection approximation

A cryo-EM specimen is a thin (∼20–100 nm) vitreous-ice layer of randomly oriented macromolecules. Under the *weak-phase-object approximation*, the scattered wave interferes with the unscattered wave at the image plane, giving contrast proportional to the projected Coulomb potential. The ideal image of density *V*(**r**) in orientation *φ* is a 2D projection:

> **Theorem (Fourier slice theorem):** The 2D Fourier transform of a projection of a 3D object along direction *φ* equals the central slice through the 3D Fourier transform of the object, perpendicular to that direction. Formally, ℱ₂[*P_φ V*](**s**) = *Ṽ*(**s**⊥) where **s**⊥ lies in the plane through the origin normal to the projection direction.

Reconstruction is therefore a problem of assembling 3D Fourier space from noisy 2D slices of unknown orientation. The measured image *X_i* of particle *i* is modeled as

*X_i* = CTF_i · *P_{φ_i} V* + *N_i*,

where CTF_i is the contrast transfer function of the *i*th micrograph, *P_{φ_i}* is the projection operator for the hidden orientation *φ_i* (including 2D translations and class assignment), and *N_i* is noise. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of a raw particle image is typically 0.05–0.1 in power terms: the particle is essentially invisible by eye in a single frame, and only alignment and averaging of 10⁴–10⁶ particles recover the signal [5][8].

### 2.2 The contrast transfer function

In the weak-phase approximation, the microscope's contrast transfer function is

CTF(**s**) = −[√(1 − *Q*²)·sin *χ*(**s**) + *Q*·cos *χ*(**s**)] · *E*(**s**),

with the phase aberration function

*χ*(**s**) = *πλs*²(Δ*f* − ½*λ*²*s*²*C_s*),

where *λ* is the electron wavelength, *s* = |**s**| is spatial frequency, Δ*f* is defocus, *C_s* is spherical aberration, *Q* is the amplitude contrast fraction (typically ∼0.07–0.1), and *E*(**s**) is the envelope function damping high frequencies due to finite source size, energy spread, and detector response [7]. The sine term produces the characteristic *Thon rings* — concentric oscillations in the power spectrum of each micrograph — with contrast reversals at the zeros of CTF. Accurate estimation of Δ*f* (and astigmatic defocus) from these rings is the prerequisite for any high-resolution reconstruction, and is performed by programs such as CTFFIND4 and Gctf [9].

### 2.3 Statistical framework

Modern refinement is Bayesian. The unknowns comprise the 3D map(s) Θ and, for each particle, hidden variables *φ* (Euler angles, translations, class label). Maximum-likelihood estimation integrates out the hidden variables; the MAP variant adds a regularization prior on the map. The marginal log-likelihood

*L*(Θ) = Σ_i log ∫ *P*(*X_i* | *φ*, Θ) *P*(*φ* | Θ) *dφ*

is optimized by expectation–maximization: the E-step computes posterior probabilities Γ_{iφ} over orientations per particle, and the M-step reconstructs the map by posterior-weighted back-projection [1][3]. Gaussian Fourier-space noise with frequency-dependent variance makes the weighting a Wiener-like filter, and adaptive Tikhonov regularization (signal-power spectrum *τ*² estimated from the data) suppresses overfitting to noise [1].

## 3 Methodology

The contemporary single-particle pipeline, as implemented in RELION [1] and cryoSPARC [10], proceeds through the following stages.

1. **Specimen preparation and data collection.** Protein is vitrified on holey-carbon grids; movies (30–60 frames, ∼30–60 e⁻/Å² total dose) are recorded on direct detectors in counting mode.
2. **Motion correction.** Movie frames are aligned (whole-frame or patch-based) into a dose-weighted sum, down-weighting damaged late frames at high frequency [11].
3. **CTF estimation.** For each motion-corrected micrograph, defocus and astigmatism are fit to the Thon rings, and micrographs with poor fits or excessive drift are discarded [9].
4. **Particle picking.** Particles are located by template matching, Laplacian-of-Gaussian filters, or trained neural networks (e.g., crYOLO, Topaz).
5. **2D classification.** Particles are aligned and clustered in 2D; classes showing secondary structure validate the data and allow junk removal.
6. **Ab initio model generation.** An initial 3D reference is built, e.g., by stochastic gradient descent in cryoSPARC, minimizing model bias from external templates [10].
7. **3D classification.** ML classification sorts particles into *K* 3D classes, separating conformations and damaged particles [1][3].
8. **Gold-standard 3D refinement.** The set is split into independent halves refined against separate references from a common starting model; the half-map FSC measures resolution while the split prevents noise alignment [3].
9. **CTF refinement and Bayesian polishing.** Per-particle defocus, astigmatism, beam tilt, and higher-order aberrations are refined; per-particle trajectories are re-estimated with a smooth-motion prior (Bayesian polishing) and dose weighting re-optimized [11].
10. **Post-processing.** Maps are sharpened by a B-factor estimated from the Guinier plot, masked, and assessed by FSC, local resolution, and map–model correlation.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Contrast transfer function estimation and correction

Defocus generates the phase contrast that makes particles visible, but the CTF's oscillations attenuate and reverse contrast across frequencies. Lower defocus moves the first zero to higher frequency, preserving high-resolution detail at the cost of low-frequency visibility — so data span a defocus range (typically 0.5–2.5 µm), with the CTF estimated per micrograph.

**Estimation.** CTFFIND4 [9] fits the micrograph power spectrum to theoretical CTF² modulated by an envelope, optimizing defocus, astigmatism, and phase shift by cross-correlation; modern estimators add anisotropic magnification, beam tilt, and higher-order aberrations [11]. Defocus accuracy of ∼10–50 nm is required for sub-3 Å reconstructions.

**Correction.** *Phase flipping* multiplied each Fourier component by the sign of the CTF, restoring phases but not amplitudes. Full correction uses Wiener filtering:

*Ṽ_corr*(**s**) ∝ Σ_i CTF_i*(**s**)·*X̃_i*(**s**) / (Σ_i |CTF_i(**s**)|² + 1/SNR(**s**)),

where the sum runs over particles and the CTF zeros of any one micrograph are filled by data from micrographs at different defocus. In practice, CTF multiplication is folded into the ML forward model (Section 4.2): the reference projections are multiplied by each particle's CTF before comparison, which is statistically optimal and avoids explicit deconvolution [1].

**Aberrations and phase plates.** Residual beam tilt introduces a phase error linear in *s*; per-particle and per-optics-group refinement recovers it [11]. The Volta phase plate generates in-focus phase contrast but its spatially varying CTF is harder to estimate, so most high-resolution work remains defocus-based.

### 4.2 Maximum-likelihood 3D classification and refinement

The ML approach, introduced to cryo-EM by Sigworth (1998) and extended to 3D classification by Scheres et al. (2007), replaces hard assignment of orientations with *integration over uncertainty* [4][5]. The key insight: at SNR ∼0.1, no single orientation assignment is trustworthy; the posterior Γ_{iφ} carries the information, and the map update is a posterior-weighted average.

> **Theorem (EM monotonicity):** Each iteration of the expectation–maximization algorithm does not decrease the marginal likelihood *L*(Θ). The E-step sets Γ^{(n)}_{iφ} = *P*(*φ* | *X_i*, Θ^{(n)}); the M-step maximizes the expected complete-data log-likelihood, yielding the weighted back-projection update for Θ^{(n+1)}.

Concretely, RELION's MAP objective is

Θ* = argmax_Θ [Σ_i log Σ_φ Γ-prior-weighted *P*(*X_i*|*φ*,Θ) − ||*V*||²/(2*τ*²)],

where the likelihood of particle *i* given orientation *φ* is a Gaussian in Fourier space,

*P*(*X_i* | *φ*, Θ) ∝ exp(−||*X_i* − CTF_i·*P_φ V*||²/(2*σ_i*²)),

with frequency-dependent noise variance *σ_i*² estimated from the data. The regularization strength *τ*² is set to the estimated signal power per resolution shell each iteration — RELION's "regularized likelihood optimization" [1]: strong at high frequencies where signal is weak, relaxing as refinement converges.

**Gold-standard refinement.** To prevent overfitting rigorously, the dataset is split into two halves that are refined independently from the same starting model, and resolution is measured only between the two half-maps [3]. Because noise is independent between halves, any correlation must arise from genuine signal — with one caveat: the mask applied during refinement can correlate the halves (Section 4.4).

**3D classification.** A discrete class label *k* joins the hidden variables; the M-step reconstructs *K* maps simultaneously with soft particle assignments [1]. This unsupervised sorting was decisive for the 3.4 Å TRPV1 structure [12]. Focused classification with signal subtraction targets small domains, and multi-body refinement models continuous rigid-body motions of domains [13].

### 4.3 Bayesian polishing and per-particle CTF refinement

Even after frame alignment, particles move on independent trajectories while radiation damage erodes high-resolution information through the movie. Bayesian polishing [11] re-estimates trajectories *per particle* against projections of the current map, regularized by a Gaussian-process smoothness prior with data-learned hyperparameters. Per-frame B-factors implement exposure filtering: early frames dominate high frequencies, late frames only low frequencies.

Each particle sits at a slightly different ice height, so its defocus differs from the micrograph average by hundreds of nanometers; per-particle defocus and astigmatism refinement recovers this [11], along with per-group beam tilt, trefoil, tetrafoil, and anisotropic magnification. For large particles at high resolution, *Ewald sphere curvature* matters — the depth of field 2*d*²/*λ* shrinks below the particle diameter — and correcting it was key to the 1.25 Å apoferritin reconstruction [14].

### 4.4 FSC-based resolution validation

**Definition.** The Fourier shell correlation between two volumes *F*₁, *F*₂ is

FSC(*r*) = Σ_{|**s**|∈*r*} *F*₁(**s**)*F*₂*(**s**) / √[Σ|*F*₁|² Σ|*F*₂|²],

the normalized cross-correlation per resolution shell, measuring reconstruction consistency versus spatial frequency [8].

> **Theorem (Rosenthal–Henderson 0.143 criterion):** Let *C_ref* = √(2·FSC/(1+FSC)) be the estimated correlation between the full-dataset map and a perfect reference, the cryo-EM analogue of the crystallographic figure of merit. Then *C_ref* = 0.5 ⟺ FSC ≈ 0.143. The resolution of a reconstruction is therefore assigned at the frequency where the gold-standard half-map FSC falls to 0.143 [2].

The 0.5 level is the conventional interpretability threshold, shared with X-ray crystallography. Alternatives include FSC = 0.5 (conservative; masked maps, map–model) and the half-bit criterion, with a frequency-dependent threshold from shell information content [15].

**Gold standard and its pitfalls.** Independent half-set refinement with unmasked FSC became the standard after Scheres and Chen (2012) showed that common refinement inflates FSC through noise alignment [3]. Two caveats remain:

- *Mask-induced correlation.* Tight masks applied during refinement correlate the halves. The accepted remedy is *phase randomization*: phases beyond a cutoff are randomized in the half-maps, the masked FSC is recomputed, and genuine resolution must exceed the phase-randomized FSC [16].
- *Anisotropy.* Preferred orientation produces direction-dependent resolution. The 3D FSC [17] and local-resolution maps (ResMap, blocres) quantify this; a single global number can be misleading.

**Map–model FSC.** The correlation between the experimental map and a map from the fitted atomic model validates the model against the data — the standard check before PDB deposition.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 Landmark reconstructions

These reconstructions mark the capability frontier, each combining the three pillars with successive detector and algorithm generations.

| Structure | Year | Resolution (Å) | Particles | Detector | Key advance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TRPV1 ion channel [12] | 2013 | 3.4 | ~88,000 | K2 Summit | First de novo membrane-protein side chains; ML 3D classification |
| 20S proteasome | 2013 | 3.3 | ~126,000 | K2 Summit | Frame alignment; near-atomic asymmetric particle |
| β-galactosidase | 2014 | 3.2 | ~144,000 | Falcon II | Dose weighting; direct-detector benchmark |
| Apoferritin [14] | 2020 | 1.25 | ~363,000 | K3 + cold FEG | Ewald-sphere correction; aberration refinement |
| GABA_A receptor | 2019 | 2.5–3.0 | ~200,000 | K3 | Heterogeneous membrane complex; focused classification |

Resolution scales with particle number through the Rosenthal–Henderson plot: ln(*N*/*d*) is linear in 1/*d*² [2]. Its slope gives an effective B-factor (typically 50–150 Å²) quantifying per-particle information loss; extrapolation predicts the particle count for a target resolution, with diminishing returns when the B-factor dominates.

### 5.2 Resolution criteria compared

| Criterion | Threshold | Use case | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gold-standard FSC | 0.143 | Unmasked half-maps | *C_ref* = 0.5 vs. perfect map [2] |
| FSC | 0.5 | Masked maps, map–model | Conservative; historical standard |
| Half-bit | frequency-dependent | Small datasets | Information-theoretic; shell voxel count |
| Phase-randomized FSC | must lie below | Mask validation | Detects mask-induced overfitting [16] |

### 5.3 Reference implementations

The core CTF and FSC computations are compact:

```python
import numpy as np

def ctf(s, lam=0.0197, df=1.5e4, Cs=2.7e7, Q=0.07):
    """Weak-phase CTF. s: spatial freq (1/Angstrom, array);
    lam: wavelength (A); df: defocus (A); Cs: spherical aberration (A)."""
    chi = np.pi * lam * s**2 * (df - 0.5 * lam**2 * s**2 * Cs)
    return -(np.sqrt(1 - Q**2) * np.sin(chi) + Q * np.cos(chi))

def fsc(vol1, vol2, nbins=100):
    """Gold-standard FSC between two half-map Fourier volumes."""
    F1, F2 = np.fft.fftn(vol1), np.fft.fftn(vol2)
    kx = np.fft.fftfreq(vol1.shape[0])
    r = np.sqrt(sum(g**2 for g in np.meshgrid(kx, kx, kx, indexing='ij')))
    bins = np.linspace(0, 0.5, nbins + 1)
    out = np.zeros(nbins)
    for b in range(nbins):
        m = (r >= bins[b]) & (r < bins[b+1])
        num = np.sum(F1[m] * np.conj(F2[m])).real
        den = np.sqrt(np.sum(np.abs(F1[m])**2) * np.sum(np.abs(F2[m])**2))
        out[b] = num / den if den > 0 else 0.0
    return bins[:-1], out
```

A minimal ML 3D refinement loop follows the EM structure of Section 4.2:

```python
def ml_refine(particles, ctfs, refs, sigmas, n_iter=25, K=1):
    """particles: list of 2D images; ctfs: per-particle CTFs;
    refs: K initial 3D references. Returns refined maps."""
    for it in range(n_iter):
        # E-step: posterior over (orientation, class) per particle
        posteriors = [expectation_step(p, c, refs, s)
                      for p, c, s in zip(particles, ctfs, sigmas)]
        # M-step: posterior-weighted back-projection + Tikhonov prior
        refs = [maximization_step(particles, ctfs, posteriors, k)
                for k in range(K)]
        # Adaptive regularization: tau^2 <- signal power per shell
        refs = [apply_wiener_prior(v, estimate_tau2(v)) for v in refs]
    return refs
```

Production codes add adaptive angular sampling, GPU parallelism, and the gold-standard split [1][10]; the statistical skeleton is unchanged.

### 5.4 Sketch: consistency of the ML estimator

> **Theorem (informal consistency):** Under the Gaussian image model of Section 2.1, with known CTFs and noise spectra, the maximum-marginal-likelihood 3D map converges in probability to the true map as the number of particles *N* → ∞, provided the orientation distribution has full support and the map is identifiable up to a global rotation.

*Sketch.* The marginal likelihood is a well-specified M-estimator: E-step posteriors concentrate on true orientations, and the M-step (filtered back-projection) inverts the projection operator, which is injective on band-limited functions. Regularization vanishes as *τ*²/*N* → 0, so MAP inherits ML consistency. Model bias from poor initialization ("Einstein from noise") is a finite-sample non-convexity issue, mitigated by gold-standard validation and ab initio references [3][10].

## 6 Limitations, Open Problems, and Future Work

**Preferred orientation.** Proteins adsorbing to the air–water interface denature or adopt narrow orientational distributions; missing views elongate the map along the beam axis. 3DFSC [17] quantifies the anisotropy; remedies include tilted collection and faster-spraying grids. Sample preparation, not computation, remains the dominant failure mode.

**Motion and damage.** Polishing [11] recovers much of the loss, but the least-damaged early frames are also the most mobile. Faster detectors and helium cooling continue to push the B-factor down.

**Heterogeneity.** Discrete classification assumes finite states; continuous flexibility needs multi-body refinement [13], 3D variability analysis, or neural-field methods (cryoDRGN) — yet flexible regions still resolve worse than the rigid core.

**High-resolution aberrations.** Below ∼2 Å, Ewald-sphere curvature [14], higher-order aberrations, and breakdown of the projection approximation demand correction — standard in RELION 3.1+ but requiring excellent data.

**Validation gaps.** FSC measures self-consistency, not correctness — a consistently wrong map can yield a plausible FSC. Map–model FSC and half-map deposition in the EMDB are the community's answers.

**Compute scale.** Million-particle datasets with per-particle refinement need GPU clusters for weeks; stochastic optimization [10] and on-the-fly processing aim at real-time pipelines.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Three computational ideas made cryo-EM an atomic-resolution technique: *maximum-likelihood 3D refinement*, integrating over orientational uncertainty and classifying heterogeneous particles without supervision [1][3]; *CTF estimation and correction* [7][9], extended to per-particle aberrations [11]; and *gold-standard FSC validation* [2][3], resistant to noise-fitting pathologies. With direct detectors and motion correction, they delivered the resolution revolution of the 2017 Nobel Prize. The remaining frontiers — preferred orientation, continuous heterogeneity, the sub-1.5 Å regime — are as much specimen physics as algorithms, and will define the next decade of structural biology.
