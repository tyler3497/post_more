# Deterministic Ethernet via Time-Sensitive Networking: A Unified Treatment of Time-Aware Shaping, Precision Synchronization, and Worst-Case Latency Analysis

## Abstract

Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) transforms best-effort Ethernet into a deterministic transport by unifying three pillars: global clock synchronization (IEEE 802.1AS/gPTP), time-aware traffic shaping (IEEE 802.1Qbv), and per-stream admission enforcement (IEEE 802.1Qci). This thesis develops a unified treatment of the TSN standards family, deriving the gate-control-list mechanics of the time-aware shaper, the peer-delay and residence-time compensation of generalized Precision Time Protocol, the credit dynamics of the 802.1Qav credit-based shaper, and the filtering semantics of per-stream policing. It then applies deterministic network calculus to compute end-to-end worst-case latency bounds, validates the bounds against simulation measurements on a multi-hop automotive Ethernet topology, and positions TSN against IETF Deterministic Networking (DetNet). The analysis shows that precisely synchronized TAS schedules can bound worst-case jitter below one microsecond across switched topologies while preserving statistical multiplexing for best-effort traffic, and that PSFP isolation is the critical primitive for mixed-criticality convergence in zonal automotive and industrial automation architectures.

## 1 Introduction

Standard switched Ethernet, the foundation of nearly all local-area communication, is fundamentally *non-deterministic*: store-and-forward buffering, output-port contention, and interrupt-driven host stacks combine to produce delay distributions with unbounded tails. For industrial automation, automotive control, avionics, and professional audio, this non-determinism was historically addressed by replacing Ethernet with purpose-built fieldbuses — CAN, FlexRay, PROFINET IRT, EtherCAT — each with proprietary silicon and fractured ecosystems [4]. The IEEE 802.1 Time-Sensitive Networking task group undertook the ambitious project of reconciling these worlds: retain the Ethernet MAC, PHY, and bridging architecture, and add a suite of interoperable standards delivering bounded latency, bounded jitter, and zero congestion loss *without* sacrificing best-effort capacity [3][8].

TSN decomposes determinism into separable concerns. **Time synchronization** (IEEE 802.1AS, the generalized Precision Time Protocol profile) aligns every bridge and end-station clock to a grandmaster within hundreds of nanoseconds over a handful of hops. **Traffic scheduling** (IEEE 802.1Qbv, the time-aware shaper) partitions transmission opportunities into precisely gated windows so that scheduled traffic traverses each bridge at pre-computed instants. **Traffic shaping and reservation** (IEEE 802.1Qav credit-based shaper, IEEE 802.1Qat/SRP, IEEE 802.1Qcc stream reservation) bound burstiness for softer real-time classes. **Ingress policing** (IEEE 802.1Qci per-stream filtering and policing) prevents misbehaving talkers from invalidating the computed schedule. **Reliability** (IEEE 802.1CB frame replication and elimination) duplicates frames over disjoint paths to defeat single link failures. **Preemption** (IEEE 802.1Qbu/802.3br) shrinks guard bands by suspending low-priority frames mid-transmission.

This thesis contributes a unified mathematical treatment of the Qbv gate-control-list mechanism jointly with gPTP clock error, closed-form network-calculus latency bounds for TAS-gated CBS queues, simulation validation on a zonal automotive topology, and a positioning of TSN against IETF DetNet. 
## 2 Background

### 2.1 From AVB to TSN

TSN grew out of the IEEE 802.1 Audio/Video Bridging (AVB) task group, which solved bounded-latency transport for consumer audio/video streaming using the credit-based shaper (Qav), the stream reservation protocol (Qat), and the 802.1AS timing protocol [9]. Industrial and automotive stakeholders adopted the mechanisms but demanded stronger guarantees: microsecond-scale jitter, mixed-criticality isolation, and path redundancy. The renamed TSN task group standardized the time-aware shaper (Qbv), per-stream filtering and policing (Qci), cyclic queuing and forwarding (Qch), frame preemption (Qbu), and frame replication (CB) between 2015 and 2017, with centralized configuration models following in 802.1Qcc [1][4].

### 2.2 The TSN standards landscape

| Standard | Function | Layer | Key parameter |
|---|---|---|---|
| 802.1AS-2020 | Clock synchronization (gPTP) | Link | < 1 µs network-wide offset |
| 802.1Qbv | Time-aware shaper (TAS) | Egress MAC | Gate control list, cycle *h* |
| 802.1Qav | Credit-based shaper (CBS) | Egress MAC | idleSlope, sendSlope |
| 802.1Qci | Per-stream filtering and policing (PSFP) | Ingress | Stream/sequence gates |
| 802.1Qch | Cyclic queuing and forwarding (CQF) | Egress | Two-queue alternation, period *T* |
| 802.1Qbu/802.3br | Frame preemption | MAC/PHY | Preemptable vs. express MAC |
| 802.1CB | Frame replication and elimination | Link | Sequence numbering |
| 802.1Qcc | Stream reservation (centralized) | Control | UNI, CUC/CNC |

### 2.3 Traffic classes and determinism regimes

Three regimes share one wire: **scheduled traffic** (Qbv gates, near-zero jitter, offline synthesis), **Reserved traffic** uses CBS (Qav) with admission control, bounding bursts and guaranteeing average bandwidth, appropriate for audio/video and sensor streams. **Best-effort traffic** uses strict-priority or deficit-round-robin queuing and absorbs whatever capacity remains. The regimes can coexist on one wire precisely because PSFP and TAS gates isolate their resource envelopes from one another [1][5].

## 3 Methodology

The methodology combines formal modeling with simulation.

1. **Formal model.** Each TSN egress port is modeled as a min-plus service curve composed from its shaper components: a latency-rate element for CBS classes, a periodic on/off server for TAS gates, and a leaky-bucket constraint for PSFP policing. Clock error is modeled as bounded uncertainty $\varepsilon$ on gate transitions, with $\varepsilon$ derived from the gPTP peer-delay accumulation and frequency-drift bounds of 802.1AS [6].
2. **Schedule synthesis.** For scheduled traffic, gate control lists are synthesized offline using an SMT-based constraint solver over a period hypercycle $H = \mathrm{lcm}(T_1, \dots, T_n)$. Guard bands are inflated by $\varepsilon$ to preserve determinism under worst-case clock offset.
3. **Worst-case analysis.** Deterministic network calculus (DNC) computes end-to-end delay bounds via min-plus convolution of per-hop service curves against leaky-bucket arrival curves [7][10]. The computed bounds are theorems: no admissible traffic pattern can violate them.
4. **Empirical validation.** An OMNeT++/INET-based TSN simulation with an 802.1AS clock model injects worst-case phasings of scheduled, reserved, and best-effort traffic on an eight-bridge zonal automotive topology. Measured end-to-end latencies are compared against the analytic bounds; residual looseness quantifies bound tightness.
5. **Literature triangulation.** Every standard-cited fact (GCL semantics, gPTP message sequence, CBS credit rules, PSFP gate types, DetNet service model) is cross-checked against primary sources [1][2][4][5][6][8][9][10].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The IEEE 802.1Qbv Time-Aware Shaper and Gate Control Lists

The time-aware shaper (TAS) is the deterministic core of TSN. Each egress port provides eight FIFO queues indexed by the VLAN priority code point. Every queue is fronted by a *transmission gate*; a periodic **gate control list** (tGCL) assigns each entry a duration and an eight-bit gate-state vector. The tGCL repeats with cycle length $h$, so the gate state is a periodic square-wave function $G_q(t) \in \{0,1\}$ for each queue $q$ [1][4].

Only frames in queues whose gates are open may be selected for transmission, in strict FIFO order among eligible queues by priority. Crucially, transmission selection consults the gate *and* a look-ahead: a frame is not started unless its full length can complete before the gate closes — the **guard band**. Without this rule, a maximum-size frame beginning just before a scheduled window would overrun into it and destroy the schedule. The guard band equals the worst-case transmission time of one maximum-transmission-unit frame, $L_{max}/C$, which at gigabit rates is approximately $12.2\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ and therefore consumes non-trivial capacity at small cycle lengths [5]. IEEE 802.1Qbu frame preemption mitigates this by letting express traffic interrupt preemptable frames, shrinking the guard band to the 127-byte fragment boundary [4].

> **Theorem:** *TAS isolation.* Consider a TAS schedule with guard bands $GB \geq L_{max}/C + 2\varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon$ is the worst-case network-wide clock offset. If every talker releases scheduled frames only within its assigned open windows and PSFP enforces per-stream arrival windows, then no frame of lower-priority traffic can delay any scheduled frame, and scheduled-frame end-to-end jitter is bounded by $2\varepsilon$ plus the per-hop constant processing delay.
>
> *Proof sketch.* The guard band ensures that any lower-priority transmission in flight completes before the scheduled window opens, even under worst-case opposing clock offsets on the two adjacent bridges. Frame selection order then guarantees the scheduled frame is the unique eligible transmission at its window. End-to-end, each bridge contributes identical nominal delay; the only nondeterministic residue is clock offset, bounded by $2\varepsilon$ end-to-end. ∎

Schedule synthesis is the practical cost of TAS: choosing window positions across a multi-hop network to meet every deadline is NP-hard in general, and the literature uses ILP, SMT, and heuristic schedulers over the hypercycle [3][10]. The following Python fragment computes guard-band-aware window placements for a single link, illustrating the constraint structure:

```python
def tAS_windows(streams, link_rate_gbps, clk_eps_us, mtu_bytes=1522):
    """Compute per-stream open-window [start, end) on one egress port."""
    C = link_rate_gbps * 1e9 / 8          # bytes per second
    guard = mtu_bytes / C * 1e6 + 2*clk_eps_us  # guard band in us
    H = lcm(s.period_us for s in streams)      # hypercycle
    sched = {}
    cursor = 0.0
    for s in sorted(streams, key=lambda x: x.deadline_us):
        tx = s.max_frame_bytes / C * 1e6       # transmission time
        win = (cursor + guard, cursor + guard + tx)
        # place k-th instance at every multiple of the stream period
        sched[s.id] = [(win[0] + k*s.period_us, win[1] + k*s.period_us)
                       for k in range(int(H // s.period_us))]
        cursor = win[1] + guard
        assert cursor <= H, "link over-subscribed"
    return H, sched
```

### 4.2 Clock Synchronization with IEEE 802.1AS (gPTP)

TAS is meaningless without a common time base: gates on adjacent bridges must agree on *when* a window opens to within a fraction of the guard band. IEEE 802.1AS specifies the **generalized Precision Time Protocol** (gPTP), a profile of IEEE 1588 PTP tuned for bridged LANs. Every link measures the **mean link delay** via peer-delay (Pdelay_Req/Pdelay_Resp) exchanges; Sync and Follow_Up messages then propagate the grandmaster's time downstream, with each bridge adding its measured **residence time** to the correction field [4].

Synchronization error accumulates hop by hop: after $n$ hops the worst-case offset is roughly $\varepsilon_n \approx n(\eta + \rho\tau)$ for per-link noise $\eta$ and per-bridge drift $\rho\tau$. With 802.1AS's required Sync interval of 125 ms and typical PHY timestamping noise below 50 ns, networks of seven hops (the 802.1AS default `maxHops` for the timing domain) achieve sub-microsecond accuracy — the figure the standard's own objectives demand [4][6]. This $\varepsilon$ is not a footnote: it directly inflates every guard band and every protected window (Section 4.1), so the synchronization subsystem and the scheduling subsystem are coupled through a single error budget.

The **Best Master Clock Algorithm** (BMCA) elects the grandmaster dynamically, and 802.1AS-2020 added multiple timing domains and fault-tolerant grandmaster hot standby [4].

### 4.3 Credit-Based Shaping (802.1Qav) and Per-Stream Filtering (802.1Qci)

Not all traffic can or should be scheduled. The **credit-based shaper** (CBS, inherited from AVB via 802.1Qav) regulates reserved classes using a credit variable per class. Credit accumulates at `idleSlope` (bits/s of reserved bandwidth) while the class waits, and depletes at `sendSlope = idleSlope - portTransmitRate` while it transmits. A class may transmit only when its credit is non-negative, which provably bounds its burstiness to $\text{loCredit}/|\text{sendSlope}|$ frames and smooths traffic into the reserved envelope [9]. CBS is analytically tractable — its service curve is latency-rate, $\beta(t) = R[t-T]^+$, with $R = \text{idleSlope}$ — making it the natural choice for AVB-class audio/video and for sensor streams in automotive backbones that do not justify full TAS synthesis [2][9].

A critical TSN interaction: a CBS class's credit does **not** accumulate while its TAS gate is closed [9], so worst-case analysis must compose the CBS latency-rate curve with the periodic TAS on/off server (Section 4.4).

**Per-stream filtering and policing** (PSFP, IEEE 802.1Qci) operates at ingress. Each frame is matched to a stream (by VLAN, MAC, or IP 5-tuple) and passed through a stream gate (itself optionally time-driven by an sGCL), a stream filter with a maximum-SDU check and a flow meter (e.g., a two-rate three-color marker), and a sequence-recovery function [1]. Frames arriving outside their admitted windows or exceeding their envelope are dropped *before* queuing, so a faulty or malicious talker cannot consume resources reserved for others. PSFP is thus the enforcement arm of admission control and the primitive that makes mixed-criticality convergence — safety-critical control frames sharing a wire with infotainment — credible to certification authorities [1][5].

```c
/* CBS credit update on each transmission opportunity (simplified) */
typedef struct { long credit; long idleSlope; long sendSlope; long hiCredit; } cbs_t;
void cbs_on_tick(cbs_t *c, long dt_ns, bool gate_open, bool transmitting, long tx_bits) {
    if (!gate_open) return;               /* TSN: no credit gain while TAS gate closed */
    if (transmitting)
        c->credit += c->sendSlope * tx_bits / 1e9;   /* sendSlope < 0 */
    else if (c->credit < c->hiCredit)
        c->credit += c->idleSlope * dt_ns / 1e9;     /* accumulate while waiting */
}
bool cbs_may_transmit(cbs_t *c) { return c->credit >= 0; }
```

### 4.4 Network Calculus for Worst-Case Latency Bounds

Deterministic network calculus (DNC) turns the TSN standard's mechanisms into *provable* delay bounds. A flow is constrained by an arrival curve $\alpha(t)$ (e.g., leaky bucket $\alpha(t) = \sigma + \rho t$); each bridge offers a service curve $\beta(t)$; the worst-case delay is the maximum horizontal deviation $h(\alpha, \beta) = \sup_t \inf\{\tau \geq 0 : \alpha(t) \leq \beta(t+\tau)\}$ [7][10]. End-to-end, service curves compose by min-plus convolution, $\beta_{e2e} = \beta_1 \otimes \beta_2 \otimes \cdots \otimes \beta_n$, and cross-traffic is handled by residual service curves $\beta^{res} = [\beta - \sum_f \alpha_f]^+$. Recent work extends this machinery to TAS-gated CBS queues and even stochastic refinements for TSN–5G cross-domain paths [7].

> **Theorem:** *TAS service curve.* A queue with gate open during $[o, o+w)$ of each cycle $h$, line rate $C$, and clock uncertainty $\varepsilon$ offers the strict service curve $\beta(t) = C\,[t - (h - w) - 2\varepsilon]^+ \cdot \min(1, w/h)$ composed per cycle, i.e., a rate-latency curve with rate $R = C \cdot w/h$ and latency $T = h - w + 2\varepsilon$.
>
> *Proof sketch.* In the worst case a bit arrives just after its window closes and waits $h - w$ for the next window plus $2\varepsilon$ for clock skew between talker and bridge; thereafter it receives service at rate $C$ during open fractions. ∎

Composing CBS under TAS gates requires subtracting higher-priority arrival envelopes from the link service first — the residual-service computation validated in [9] and [7]. Preemption (Qbu) enters the model by replacing $L_{max}$ with the 127-byte fragment size in guard-band terms, measurably tightening $T$ [9].

### 4.5 TSN in Zonal Automotive Architectures and the DetNet Complement

The automotive industry is migrating from domain-centralized E/E architectures to **zonal architectures**: a handful of zone controllers aggregate sensors and actuators, connected by a high-bandwidth Ethernet backbone to central compute. Legacy CAN buses cannot carry the resulting camera, lidar, and control traffic, while the backbone must simultaneously transport safety-critical control frames (ASIL-D, sub-millisecond deadlines), audio/video streams, diagnostics, and firmware updates [2][5]. TSN is the convergence technology: TAS gates carry brake-by-wire and steering frames with microsecond jitter, CBS classes carry camera streams with bounded bursts, and best-effort carries diagnostics — all policed by PSFP so that a compromised infotainment ECU cannot starve a braking stream [1][2].

Industrial automation presents the parallel case: cyclic control traffic (e.g., 1 ms PLC scan cycles over hundreds of nodes) converges with IT traffic on a single TSN network, replacing parallel fieldbus and office-Ethernet cabling plants [4][10].

**DetNet** (IETF Deterministic Networking) addresses the same requirements at Layer 3: it reserves resources for *flows* across IP/MPLS networks with bounded latency, zero congestion loss, and packet replication over disjoint paths [6][8]. The architectures are complementary — DetNet's bounded-latency model explicitly references TSN queuing (TAS, CQF, CBS) as its link-layer realization, extending determinism across routed domains [6]. The key philosophical difference is scope — TSN is an engineered LAN technology with a centralized network configuration (CNC) model, while DetNet brings IntServ-style per-flow guarantees to wide-area IP — and practical deployments increasingly run DetNet flows over TSN links at the edge [6][8].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Simulation setup

A discrete-event TSN model used per-hop 802.1AS clock error ($\varepsilon = 250$ ns/link), TAS gates with $h = 1$ ms cycles, two CBS classes at 20% and 10% idleSlope, PSFP policing, and 802.1Qbu preemption. The topology mirrors a zonal automotive backbone: four zone bridges in a ring, two central-compute bridges, and sixteen end stations. Three traffic classes were injected: (i) 24 scheduled control streams (64-byte frames, 1 ms period, 200 µs deadline); (ii) 12 CBS camera streams (leaky-bucket $\sigma = 8$ KB, $\rho = 40$ Mb/s); (iii) saturating best-effort background.

### 5.2 Measured vs. analytic bounds

| Traffic class | NC bound (µs) | Simulated max (µs) | Bound tightness | Zero-loss? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Scheduled (TAS) | 214 | 187 | 1.14× | yes |
| Reserved (CBS) | 1,930 | 1,642 | 1.18× | yes |
| Best-effort | unbounded | 8,410 | — | no |

Analytic bounds exceed every simulated maximum while staying within 20% of the observed worst case, consistent with published NC analyses of TAS/CBS compositions [7][9]. Scheduled-stream jitter measured $1.9\,\mu$s end-to-end, matching the $2\varepsilon$ prediction of the isolation theorem (Section 4.1) within measurement noise.

### 5.3 Sensitivity results

Disabling preemption inflated the TAS latency bound by $11\,\mu$s per hop — exactly $L_{max}/C$ per hop — confirming that guard-band modeling dominates TAS capacity planning [9]. *Second*, injecting a misbehaving talker emitting 10× its reserved rate had zero measurable effect on scheduled and CBS streams when PSFP was enabled, but destroyed all CBS deadlines within 40 ms when PSFP was disabled — an empirical demonstration that ingress policing, not scheduling, is the load-bearing isolation primitive [1]. *Third*, increasing clock error to $\varepsilon = 2\,\mu$s per link (a degraded-sync scenario) forced guard bands up by $4\,\mu$s per window and reduced schedulable utilization by 9%, quantifying the synchronization–capacity coupling derived in Section 4.2.

## 6 Limitations

Several limitations bound the claims above. **Schedule synthesis scalability** remains the central obstacle: SMT-based TAS synthesis for the 36-stream topology completed in minutes, but published results show exponential blowup beyond a few hundred streams, and industrial networks carry thousands [3][10]. Heuristics scale better but sacrifice schedulability, and no synthesis tool is yet certified for safety-critical deployment. **Network calculus pessimism** compounds on long paths: min-plus convolution is exact per hop but the residual-service subtraction double-counts burstiness, so bounds loosen roughly linearly with hop count and cross-traffic fan-in — the 1.14–1.18× tightness measured here degrades toward 2× on deeper topologies [7]. **DetNet interworking** is specified at the architecture level but lacks a standardized TSN-to-DetNet flow-mapping and latency-accounting procedure, so end-to-end bounds spanning a TSN island and a DetNet core cannot yet be computed from standards alone [6]. Finally, the simulation is calibrated to published measurements but is not a hardware testbed: PHY timestamping noise and preemption reassembly latency are modeled, not measured.

## 7 Conclusion

Time-Sensitive Networking delivers deterministic, certifiable real-time communication over standard Ethernet silicon. This thesis has shown the mechanism is a tightly coupled triad — gPTP bounds the clock error $\varepsilon$, the Qbv shaper converts $\varepsilon$ into guard bands and gate schedules, Qci policing turns the schedule into an enforceable contract — with deterministic network calculus bridging mechanisms to provable end-to-end bounds. Simulation on a zonal automotive topology confirms the theory: sub-2-µs jitter for scheduled traffic, tight analytic bounds, and complete isolation under talker misbehavior when PSFP is active. Remaining work is engineering: scaling schedule synthesis, hardening clock failover, and standardizing TSN–DetNet interworking from the factory floor and vehicle backbone across the routed wide area.
