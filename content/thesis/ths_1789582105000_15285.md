# Distributed Key Generation Without a Trusted Dealer: Pedersen DKG, Feldman Verifiable Secret Sharing, and the Asynchronous Frontier

## Abstract

Distributed key generation (DKG) eliminates the single trusted dealer from threshold cryptosystems: a set of parties jointly establishes a public key together with Shamir shares of the corresponding secret key, while no party ever learns the secret. This article develops the theory from first principles. We reconstruct Feldman's non-interactive verifiable secret sharing, the commitment layer that makes Pedersen's joint-Feldman DKG possible, and Pedersen's dual-generator scheme with information-theoretic hiding. We then analyze the *rushing-adversary bias attack* that undermines naive joint-Feldman key generation and the commit-then-reveal repair of Gennaro, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Rabin. Moving beyond the synchronous broadcast model, we trace the line from the first asynchronous verifiable secret sharing through Kate and Goldberg's Internet-scale asynchronous DKG to modern optimally resilient constructions with constant expected rounds and sub-quartic communication, including batched and round-optimal designs. The treatment closes with the ideal-functionality formalization, a complexity comparison of the major protocols, and the open problems that keep DKG an active research frontier.

##1 Intro

Threshold cryptography promises resilience: a secret key that no single machine holds, no single compromise exposes, and no single failure destroys. The standard realization is a *(t, n)*-threshold scheme: a secret *s* split into *n* shares such that any *t* reconstruct *s* while any *t − 1* reveal nothing. Almost every description of such a system begins with a quiet assumption: *the parties already hold shares of a secret key*. Distributed key generation is the protocol that makes this assumption true without reintroducing a trusted dealer.

If one dealer distributes all shares, that dealer knows the secret — thresholding is pointless. The fix is for every party to act as dealer simultaneously and combine the results. This is the **joint-Feldman** or **Pedersen DKG** approach: each party *P_i* picks a random *z_i*, shares it via Feldman's verifiable secret sharing, and the joint secret becomes *x = Σ_i z_i* [3]. By linearity, summing received shares yields valid shares of the sum, and the public key follows from the published commitments. Elegant — but defective: a rushing adversary controlling some dealers can *bias* the public key, choosing among exponentially many candidates after seeing everyone else's contributions [3].

This article is a complete account of that story and what came after. Section 2 fixes the primitives: Shamir's secret sharing, Feldman's computationally-hiding VSS, and Pedersen's unconditionally-hiding VSS. Section 3 develops joint-Feldman mechanics — dealing, verification, complaints, disqualification, key extraction. Section 4 is the deep dive: the verification equation, the hiding/binding duality, the bias attack and its repair, asynchronous DKG, and the batched frontier. Section 5 gives proof sketches and a complexity comparison; Sections 6 and 7 close with limitations and open directions.

##2 Background

### Secret sharing without verifiability

Shamir's 1979 scheme is the substrate of everything here. To share a secret *s* among *n* parties with threshold *t*, the dealer samples a random polynomial *f* of degree *t − 1* with *f(0) = s* and hands *f(i)* to party *P_i*. Any *t* shares determine *f* via Lagrange interpolation; any fewer reveal nothing about *s*. In Haskell-like pseudocode:

```haskell
-- share secret s among n parties, threshold t: f(0) = s
share :: Fq -> Int -> Int -> IO [(Int, Fq)]
share s t n = do
  rest <- replicateM (t - 1) randomField
  let f = s : rest
  return [(i, eval f (fromIntegral i)) | i <- [1..n]]
```

The scheme is *information-theoretically* secure against a passive adversary. Its fatal weakness against an *active* dealer is equally clear: nothing stops a malicious dealer from handing out points that do not lie on any single degree-*(t − 1)* polynomial — shares may be mutually inconsistent, or the "secret" underdetermined. **Verifiable secret sharing (VSS)** adds exactly this guarantee: shareholders can check that their shares are consistent with one dealing, without compromising secrecy [2].

### Feldman's VSS: commitments in the exponent

Feldman's 1987 construction [1] is the canonical non-interactive VSS and the commitment layer of Pedersen's DKG. The dealer picks *f(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + … + a_{t−1} x^{t−1}* with *a_0 = s*, and broadcasts commitments *C_j = g^{a_j}* for a public generator *g*. Any shareholder receiving candidate share *s_i* checks the Feldman equation:

> **Theorem (Feldman verification [1]):** Share *s_i* is consistent with the broadcast commitments iff *g^{s_i} = ∏_{j=0}^{t−1} C_j^{i^j}*.

The check works by the homomorphism of exponentiation: *g^{f(i)} = g^{Σ a_j i^j} = ∏ (g^{a_j})^{i^j}*. The scheme is *computationally hiding* and *unconditionally binding*. Crucially, *C_0 = g^{a_0} = g^s* is public — precisely what a DKG wants (the product of these values is the group public key), and precisely why Feldman alone is unsuitable when the secret must stay hidden from everyone [1].

### Pedersen's VSS: hiding through a second generator

Pedersen's 1991 scheme [2] repairs the hiding weakness at the cost of computational binding. The dealer samples a random *blinding polynomial r(x)* and publishes two-generator commitments *C_j = g^{a_j} h^{b_j}*, where *h* is a second generator whose discrete log relative to *g* is unknown. Each shareholder receives a *pair (f(i), r(i))* and verifies *g^{f(i)} h^{r(i)} = ∏ C_j^{i^j}*. Hiding is now *information-theoretic* — for every candidate secret some blinding polynomial is consistent with the commitments — while binding becomes computational, since two openings of one commitment would reveal *log_g h*.

| Property | Feldman VSS [1] | Pedersen VSS [2] |
|---|---|---|
| Hiding | Computational (DL assumption) | **Unconditional** |
| Binding | **Unconditional** | Computational (DL assumption) |
| Reveals *g^s* publicly | Yes | No |
| Generators needed | 1 | 2 (*log_g h* unknown) |
| Share sent per party | 1 field element | 2 field elements |
| Role in DKG | Per-dealer commitment layer | Commit-then-reveal bias defense |

Both schemes are *non-interactive* in the dealing phase — one broadcast of commitments plus private share delivery — which is why they compose so cleanly into distributed key generation.

### The DKG ideal functionality

Modern analyses express DKG security through an **ideal functionality**: an incorruptible trusted party that samples *x* uniformly, hands Shamir shares *f(i)* to each party *P_i*, and publishes *y = g^x* to all. A real protocol *securely realizes* this functionality if no efficient adversary can distinguish the real execution from the ideal one. As we will see, Pedersen's original protocol realizes only a *weaker* variant in which the adversary may bias the distribution of *y* — and closing that gap is the central technical drama of the field [3].

##3 Methodology

### The joint-Feldman (Pedersen DKG) protocol

The protocol of Pedersen [2][3] proceeds in five phases, per Gennaro et al. [3], using additive notation for the field and multiplicative notation for the group, with public generator *g* of prime order *q*.

**Phase 1 — Dealing.** Every party *P_i* deals in a Feldman VSS: it samples *z_i*, forms *f_i(x) = z_i + a_{i,1} x + … + a_{i,t−1} x^{t−1}*, broadcasts commitments *A_{i,k} = g^{a_{i,k}}*, and sends *s_{ij} = f_i(j)* privately to each *P_j*.

**Phase 2 — Verification and complaints.** Each *P_j* verifies every received share against the commitments. On failure, *P_j* broadcasts a *complaint*; the accused *P_i* broadcasts the disputed share and all parties re-check it. Parties whose broadcast shares fail, or who fail to answer, are **disqualified** — every honest party reaches the same verdict, since every check is against public commitments.

**Phase 3 — Commitment round.** Each survivor's *A_{i,0} = g^{z_i}* was already broadcast in Phase 1, so this phase is implicit in joint-Feldman.

**Phase 4 — Reconstruction.** Shares of disqualified dealers can be reconstructed from honest parties' shares, though key extraction needs only the qualified set.

**Phase 5 — Key extraction.** With *QUAL* the non-disqualified dealers, each *P_j* computes *sk_j = Σ_{i ∈ QUAL} f_i(j)* and the public key is *pk = ∏_{i ∈ QUAL} A_{i,0} = g^{Σ z_i}*. By linearity the *sk_j* are valid Shamir shares of *x = Σ z_i* with *g^x = pk*. The secret *x* is constructed **nowhere** — it exists only as a sum of contributions [3].

```python
# Pedersen DKG key extraction
def extract_key(QUAL, commitments, my_shares, q):
    pk, sk = 1, 0
    for i in QUAL:
        pk *= commitments[i][0]       # g^{z_i}
        sk = (sk + my_shares[i]) % q  # sum of shares
    return pk, sk  # pk = g^{sum z_i}; sk shares f = sum f_i
```

### The complaint discipline

The complaint mechanism converts Feldman VSS from a single-dealer primitive into a distributed one. Because commitments are binding and public, a complaint is *decidable by everyone*: either the accused reveals a share satisfying the verification equation (proving the complainer wrong) or it cannot (proving itself corrupt). This public decidability is why the disqualification set is consistent across all honest parties with no extra agreement protocol — in the synchronous setting with a broadcast channel. Removing that broadcast channel is exactly the asynchronous problem of Section 4.4.

### The bias attack

Gennaro, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Rabin showed in 1999 that the protocol above does **not** realize the ideal DKG functionality [3]. The attack exploits *rushing*: the adversary sees all honest commitments before choosing its own behavior. Controlling *c* dealers, it decides per corrupted dealer *whether to get disqualified* in the complaint round — e.g., by sending an invalid share to a collaborator who complains, or by behaving honestly. Each binary choice includes or excludes that dealer's *z_i* from the final sum, steering the public key among *2^c* values chosen *after* the honest contributions are fixed. This breaks verifiable randomness, leader election, and any protocol whose proof assumes uniform *pk*.

> **Theorem (GJKR bias [3]):** In Pedersen's DKG, a rushing adversary controlling *c* dealers can force the final public key to lie in an adversarially chosen subset of *2^c* values determined after all honest contributions are fixed. The shared secret is therefore not uniformly random.

### The GJKR repair: commit, then reveal

The 1999 repair separates *hiding* from *extraction* [3]. **Round 1:** every party shares via Pedersen VSS with unconditionally hiding commitments *C_{i,k} = g^{a_{i,k}} h^{b_{i,k}}*, fixing the qualified set while all contributions remain information-theoretically hidden. **Round 2:** the parties re-share the *same* secrets via Feldman VSS, opening the hiding and producing the extractable discrete-log public key, while the qualified set is already frozen. Since the adversary must choose its disqualification behavior before learning anything about honest contributions, the bias attack collapses. Follow-up work showed the repaired protocol securely realizes threshold discrete-log cryptosystems [3][7].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Feldman Commitments and the Verification Equation

The verification equation deserves a closer look, because its algebraic shape explains both the power and the limits of the construction. Given commitments *C_j = g^{a_j}* and a claimed share *s* for party index *i*, the check *g^s ≟ ∏ C_j^{i^j}* is a *public* computation: anyone can verify any revealed share against the commitments. This publicity makes complaints decidable and disqualification consistent. The cost is *O(t)* group exponentiations per verification, hence *O(n²t)* total work in the naive implementation.

The binding property is unconditional because the commitments *are* the polynomial "in the exponent": exactly one coefficient vector is consistent with them, regardless of computational power.

### 4.2 Pedersen VSS: Information-Theoretic Hiding and Its Price

Pedersen's variant of verifiable secret sharing replaces Feldman's exponentiated commitments with a dual-generator form that hides the shared polynomial information-theoretically, at the cost of a trusted setup for the second generator and a more delicate complaint round [2]. The scheme is the workhorse of the GJKR approach: it keeps the adversary's view independent of honest contributions until the reveal phase, which is exactly what defeats rushing attacks.

```rust
// Pedersen VSS share verification against commitments C_j
fn verify_pedersen_share(
    commitments: &[GroupElement], // C_j = g^{a_j} * h^{b_j}
    i: Scalar, s_i: Scalar, t_i: Scalar, // index, share pair
    g: GroupElement, h: GroupElement,
) -> bool {
    let lhs = g * s_i + h * t_i;
    let mut rhs = GroupElement::identity();
    let mut pow = Scalar::one();
    for c in commitments {
        rhs = rhs + c * pow;
        pow = pow * i;
    }
    lhs == rhs
}
```

### 4.3 The Rushing Adversary and the GJKR Repair



1. **Commit phase (Pedersen VSS):** all parties deal via Pedersen's unconditionally hiding VSS. The complaint round runs and *QUAL* is finalized while the adversary's view is information-theoretically independent of honest secrets.
2. **Reveal phase (Feldman VSS):** each qualified party re-shares its *same* secret *z_i* via Feldman VSS, opening the Pedersen commitment. Because *QUAL* is already fixed, the adversary's disqualification choices are spent; the revealed contributions combine into an unbiased key.


### 4.4 Asynchronous DKG: From Cachin et al. to Abraham et al.

Everything so far assumed a synchronous network with a broadcast channel — assumptions that fail on the open Internet, where messages are delayed arbitrarily and no party can distinguish a slow honest peer from a silent corrupt one. The asynchronous line begins with **Cachin, Kursawe, Lysyanskaya, and Strobl (CCS 2002)**, who gave the first asynchronous verifiable secret sharing (AVSS) with optimal resilience *f < n/3* [2]. **Kate and Goldberg (ICDCS 2009)** then built the first realistic asynchronous DKG for the Internet [4], with the key insight that the complaint round cannot be a round at all — it must be an *event-driven* process where each party advances upon receiving enough messages, never upon a timer. The modern breakthrough is **Abraham et al. (PODC 2021)** [5]: an A-DKG with optimal resilience, *constant* expected rounds, and *Õ(n³)* communication, via Proposal Election, Verifiable Gather, and leaderless Validated Asynchronous Byzantine Agreement [5].

```tla+
---- MODULE AsyncDKG ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Parties, T  \* T = threshold, |Parties| = n, T < n/3 faulty
VARIABLES commits, shares, qual

Init == /\ commits = [p \in Parties |-> {}]
        /\ shares  = [p \in Parties |-> {}]
        /\ qual    = {}

\* p receives dealer's commitments and its private share
ReceiveDeal(p, dealer) ==
    /\ commits' = [commits EXCEPT ![p] = @ \cup {dealCommit[dealer]}]
    /\ shares'  = [shares  EXCEPT ![p] = @ \cup {dealShare[dealer][p]}]

\* p's verification of dealer is complete and valid
VerifyDealer(p, dealer) ==
    /\ FeldmanCheck(commits[p][dealer], shares[p][dealer])
    /\ verificationMatrix' = [verificationMatrix EXCEPT ![p][dealer] = TRUE]

\* enough verified dealers -> propose candidate QUAL set
ProposeQual(p, S) ==
    /\ Cardinality(S) = Cardinality(Parties) - T
    /\ \A d \in S : verificationMatrix[p][d] = TRUE
    /\ ByzantineAgreement(p, S)   \* all honest parties converge on one set
====
```


### 4.5 Batched and Round-Optimal Modern Constructions


**Katz (2023)** [7] attacks round complexity in the synchronous setting: a *round-optimal fully secure* DKG with guaranteed output delivery, plus a robust variant tolerating *t < n/2* corruptions — designated subsets contribute randomness, parties commit via a hash, and the output combines contributions from subsets on which everyone agrees. Together with the universally-composable treatment of committed DKG [7], the field is converging on protocols that are simultaneously round-efficient, adaptively secure, and composable.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### Proof sketch: correctness of joint-Feldman key extraction

> **Lemma 1 (Linearity of qualified shares):** Let *QUAL* be the non-disqualified dealers, with committed polynomials *f_i* and shares *s_{ij} = f_i(j)*. Then *sk_j = Σ_{i∈QUAL} s_{ij} = f(j)* for *f = Σ_{i∈QUAL} f_i*, a degree-*(t−1)* polynomial with *f(0) = Σ_{i∈QUAL} z_i*.

*Proof.* Evaluating a sum of polynomials equals the sum of evaluations; degrees add without exceeding *t − 1*. ∎

> **Theorem (Key consistency):** All honest parties compute the same *pk = ∏_{i∈QUAL} A_{i,0}*, and any *t* honest shares *sk_j* reconstruct *x = Σ_{i∈QUAL} z_i* with *g^x = pk*.

*Proof.* *QUAL* is identical across honest parties since disqualification is decided against public commitments. By Lemma 1 the *sk_j* are Shamir shares of *x*; interpolation recovers *f(0) = x*, and *∏ A_{i,0} = ∏ g^{z_i} = g^x*. ∎

What this proof does *not* show — and cannot, without the GJKR repair — is that *x* is uniformly random. The rushing-adversary argument of Section 3 demonstrates the gap: correctness and even secrecy can hold while the *distribution* of the key is adversarially steered.

### Proof sketch: bias elimination in commit-then-reveal

> **Theorem (GJKR [3], informal):** In the two-phase protocol, if the Pedersen VSS phase fixes *QUAL* while honest contributions are information-theoretically hidden, then the final public key is statistically close to uniform over the group, regardless of the adversary's rushing strategy.

*Proof sketch.* Consider a hybrid in which honest Pedersen commitments are replaced by commitments to independent random values; by unconditional hiding, the adversary's view — hence its choice of which corrupted dealers survive into *QUAL* — is identically distributed in both worlds. In the hybrid world *QUAL* is chosen independently of the honest *z_i*, so the sum includes at least one uniform honest contribution (an honest dealer majority guarantees a survivor), making the total uniform. ∎

### Empirical note: implementation reality

Theory aside, DKG lives or dies in implementation. Eggimann's 2023 systems thesis [8] implements the full asynchronous DKG sharing phase and validates a subtle point often glossed over in papers: all honest parties must enumerate candidate qualified sets in the *same deterministic order*, because in asynchrony different parties observe different subsets of completed dealings. Measurements confirm the expected cubic communication scaling, with per-party work dominated by the *O(nt)* Feldman verifications — the bottleneck that batched constructions [6] amortize.

##6 Limitations

**The synchrony assumption is load-bearing.** Pedersen's DKG and the GJKR repair assume synchronous broadcast, which over point-to-point networks requires Byzantine agreement — in the deterministic optimally-resilient setting, *O(κn⁴)* communication and *O(t)* rounds, potentially dwarfing the DKG itself [7].

**Adaptive security remains delicate.** Static-adversary proofs fix the corrupt set before the protocol starts, but real attackers corrupt adaptively — and Feldman commitments are perfectly binding, so a simulator cannot equivocate them mid-protocol. The modern fix, committed DKG with *perfectly hiding* commitments to key shares, is stronger than what most deployed implementations provide [7].

**Asynchrony costs cubic communication.** The *Õ(n³)* of Abraham et al. [5] improves vastly on *Ω(n⁴)*, but for *n = 100* it still means on the order of a million messages. Batched designs [6] amortize this when many keys are needed, but a single-shot asynchronous DKG among a large committee remains expensive.

**Long-term key management is out of scope.** DKG solves *generation*, not *maintenance*: shares must be refreshed proactively, committees must evolve, and robust proactive refresh for the asynchronous setting is still being refined [4].

**Quantum vulnerability.** Every protocol here rests on the discrete-logarithm assumption, which a cryptographically relevant quantum computer breaks outright. Post-quantum VSS exists but lacks the non-interactive efficiency and clean DKG composition of the discrete-log world; post-quantum threshold key *generation* remains largely open.

##7 Conclusion

Distributed key generation is the foundation beneath threshold signatures, randomness beacons, and consensus validators: the mechanism by which a group acquires a shared secret nobody ever held. The arc runs from Feldman's 1987 insight — a polynomial committed *in the exponent*, making shares publicly checkable [1] — through Pedersen's parallel-dealer construction [2], the rushing-adversary bias [3] and the commit-then-reveal repair, to the asynchronous frontier where optimal resilience, constant expected rounds, and sub-quartic communication coexist [5].

Three lessons endure. First, *hiding and binding are never free simultaneously*. Second, *the network model is part of the protocol*: asynchrony forced new agreement primitives now valuable in their own right. Third, *the adversary model must match the deployment*.

Open problems: post-quantum DKG with practical efficiency, single-shot asynchronous DKG with near-quadratic communication, and fully composable adaptive security that survives contact with real implementations. Until they are solved, every threshold deployment is a bet — and the mathematics above is how to make that bet an informed one.
