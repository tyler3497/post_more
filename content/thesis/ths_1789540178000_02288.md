# Federated Learning under Heterogeneity: Convergence Dynamics of FedAvg, Client-Drift Correction, Secure Aggregation, Privacy Amplification, and Byzantine-Robust Fusion

## Abstract

Federated learning enables collaborative training across decentralized clients without centralizing raw data, yet its practice is shaped by three intertwined challenges: statistical heterogeneity that induces client drift, an honest-but-curious aggregation server that motivates cryptographic protection, and potentially Byzantine participants that threaten the integrity of the learned model. This thesis develops a unified treatment of the federated optimization pipeline. We formalize Federated Averaging (FedAvg) and characterize how multiple local epochs pull client trajectories toward heterogeneous local optima, producing a drift term that vanishes only under data homogeneity; we then review control-variate and proximal corrections, notably SCAFFOLD and FedProx, that restore convergence guarantees. We analyze secure aggregation via pairwise masking and its logarithmic-overhead descendants, quantify privacy amplification arising from client sampling, shuffling, and secure aggregation combined with differential privacy, and study Byzantine-robust aggregation rules such as Krum and the geometric median. Together, these layers form a principled architecture for robust, private federated learning at scale.

---

##1 Intro

The dominant paradigm of modern machine learning — collect all data in one data center, then train — is untenable in settings where data is born at the edge, is privacy-sensitive, or is simply too voluminous to move. **Federated learning** (FL), introduced by McMahan et al. [1], inverts this paradigm: the model travels to the data, and only *updates* (gradients or model deltas) travel back. Hospitals can jointly train a pathology classifier without sharing patient scans; keyboard applications can learn language models from billions of devices without ever observing a keystroke [10].

Yet this elegant picture conceals a hostile reality. Three structural forces conspire against naive federation:

1. **Statistical heterogeneity.** Client datasets are non-IID: a hospital in one region sees a different disease mix than another; a phone user's typing style is idiosyncratic. The seminal FedAvg algorithm [1] performs well empirically, but local epochs on heterogeneous objectives push each client toward its *own* optimum, and the averaged model drifts from the true global minimizer.
2. **An untrusted aggregator.** Model updates can leak training data through inversion and membership attacks. The server that computes the average is typically only *honest-but-curious*, motivating cryptographic aggregation that reveals the sum but hides each summand [4][5].
3. **Byzantine participants.** In open or large-scale federations, some clients may be compromised and submit arbitrary vectors. A single Byzantine client defeats plain averaging entirely [8], demanding aggregation rules with formal robustness guarantees.

This thesis unifies these three strands. Its contributions are:

- A precise characterization of *client drift* in FedAvg, with the drift decomposition, convergence rates under heterogeneity, and the control-variate and proximal corrections that tame it [1][2][3].
- A technical exposition of secure aggregation protocols, from pairwise masking to (poly)logarithmic-overhead constructions, including dropout tolerance via secret sharing [4][5].
- A quantitative account of *privacy amplification* — how client sampling, shuffling, and secure aggregation strengthen differential privacy guarantees — grounded in the moments accountant and amplification-by-shuffling analyses [6][7].
- A comparative study of Byzantine-robust aggregation rules (Krum, coordinate-wise median, trimmed mean, geometric median), with resilience definitions, statistical rates, and the tension between robustness and privacy [8][9].
- Empirical benchmarks and a limitations analysis that maps open problems: heterogeneity-aware robustness, fair participation, and communication efficiency under all three constraints simultaneously [10].

##2 Background

### The federated optimization problem

Consider a federation of *K* clients. Client *k* holds a private dataset *D_k* of size *n_k*, and the total data mass is *n = sum_k n_k*. The global objective is the weighted empirical risk

$$F(w) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k F_k(w), \qquad p_k = \frac{n_k}{n},$$

where *F_k(w) = (1/n_k) sum_{(x,y) in D_k} l(w; x, y)* is the local objective and *l* is the per-sample loss. When the *D_k* are drawn IID from one distribution, each *F_k* is an unbiased proxy of *F*; when they are not — the realistic *non-IID* regime — the local objectives may differ arbitrarily, subject only to a heterogeneity bound to be formalized in ##3.

### FedAvg and FedSGD

McMahan et al. [1] proposed **FedSGD** (one full-batch gradient per client per round) and its generalization **FedAvg**: each client runs *E* local epochs of minibatch SGD before returning, cutting communication rounds by 10–100x on non-IID data — though the analysis long lagged the practice.

### Threat models

We work with three standard threat models, often in combination:

- *Honest-but-curious server*: follows the protocol but attempts to infer private data from observed updates. Countered by secure aggregation and differential privacy [4][6].
- *Malicious server or eavesdropper*: may deviate from the protocol. Countered by verifiable aggregation and stronger cryptographic primitives (beyond our core scope).
- *Byzantine clients*: up to *f* of *n* participants may submit arbitrary vectors, adaptively and collusively. Countered by robust aggregation rules satisfying formal resilience properties [8][9].

A central tension — developed throughout this thesis — is that **privacy and robustness pull in opposite directions**: secure aggregation hides individual updates, while robust aggregation needs to *inspect* them to reject outliers.

##3 Methodology

Our methodology is analytic and comparative, grounded in the formal frameworks of the cited literature and validated by simulation experiments described in ##5.

### Notation and assumptions

We adopt the following standard assumptions, each stated precisely so that every theorem in ##5 is conditional on an explicit, checkable premise.

- **A1 (Smoothness).** Each *F_k* is *L*-smooth: gradients are *L*-Lipschitz continuous.
- **A2 (Bounded heterogeneity).** There exist constants *G >= 0* such that for all *w*, *(1/K) sum_k ||grad F_k(w) - grad F(w)||^2 <= G^2*. When *G = 0* the data are effectively homogeneous.
- **A3 (Bounded stochastic variance).** Local minibatch gradients satisfy *E(||g_k(w) - grad F_k(w)||^2) <= sigma^2*.
- **A4 (Strong convexity or PL).** For convex rates, each *F_k* is *mu*-strongly convex; non-convex results target stationarity, *E||grad F(w)||^2 -> 0*.
- **A5 (Partial participation).** Each round samples *m <= K* clients uniformly at random.

### The FedAvg reference implementation

The following Python sketch mirrors Algorithm 1 of [1] and is used for the drift experiments in ##5:

```python
import numpy as np

def fedavg_round(w, clients, E, B, eta, m):
    """One FedAvg round. clients: list of (X_k, y_k) datasets."""
    K = len(clients)
    chosen = np.random.choice(K, size=m, replace=False)
    deltas, weights = [], []
    for k in chosen:
        Xk, yk = clients[k]
        wk = w.copy()
        # E local epochs of minibatch SGD on F_k
        for _ in range(E):
            for xb, yb in minibatches(Xk, yk, B):
                g = grad_loss(wk, xb, yb)
                wk = wk - eta * g
        deltas.append(wk - w)
        weights.append(len(yk))
    # weighted aggregation at the server
    agg = sum(d * (nk / sum(weights)) for d, nk in zip(deltas, weights))
    return w + agg

def client_drift(w_t, w_star_k, trajectory):
    """Mean squared deviation of local iterates from local optima."""
    return float(np.mean([np.sum((w - w_star_k) ** 2) for w in trajectory]))
```

We quantify non-IID-ness with a Dirichlet concentration parameter *alpha*: smaller *alpha* yields more skewed label distributions across clients (pathological when *alpha -> 0*). All synthetic experiments in ##5 sweep *alpha* over a geometric grid, reporting test accuracy and the drift diagnostic above.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 FedAvg Mechanics and the Anatomy of Client Drift

Consider full-batch local gradient descent with *E* local steps and step size *eta*, no sampling noise, all clients participating. Client *k* evolves as *w_{t,0}^k = w_t*, *w_{t,e+1}^k = w_{t,e}^k - eta grad F_k(w_{t,e}^k)*, and the server sets *w_{t+1} = sum_k p_k w_{t,E}^k*. Expanding the recursion,

$$w_{t+1} - w_t = -\eta \sum_{e=0}^{E-1} \sum_k p_k \nabla F_k(w_{t,e}^k).$$

If all *w_{t,e}^k* stayed near *w_t*, the right-hand side would approximate *-eta E grad F(w_t)*, i.e., gradient descent on the global objective. But the iterates *do not* stay near *w_t*: each drifts toward the local minimizer *w_k^\** at a rate governed by the heterogeneity *G* of assumption A2. The per-round update is therefore a *biased* estimate of the global gradient, and the bias compounds over rounds.

> **Theorem (Informal drift decomposition).** Under A1–A3, one FedAvg round satisfies *E(w_{t+1}) - w_t = -eta E grad F(w_t) + b_t*, where the bias *||b_t||* is bounded by a term proportional to *eta^2 E^2 L G* plus a variance term in *sigma*. The bias vanishes if and only if local steps collapse (*E = 1*, recovering FedSGD) or heterogeneity vanishes (*G = 0*).

Two pathologies follow. First, **more local work can hurt**: on strongly heterogeneous data, large *E* amplifies bias and FedAvg can converge to a worse neighborhood than FedSGD, or diverge with fixed step sizes [3]. Second, **partial participation injects variance** of order *G^2/m* that no learning-rate schedule removes [2][3].

### 4.2 Convergence Theory under Non-IID Data

The modern convergence theory of FedAvg, developed across [2][3] and successors, establishes that FedAvg *does* converge under heterogeneity — but to a neighborhood whose radius is dictated by *G*, unless corrected.

> **Theorem (FedAvg, strongly convex, after Karimireddy et al. [3]).** Under A1–A4 with *mu*-strongly convex *F_k*, FedAvg with appropriately decayed step sizes satisfies *E(F(w_T)) - F^\* <= O(exp(-mu eta E T)) + O(G^2/(mu^2 E T)) + O(sigma^2/(mu^2 m E T))*. The first term is the optimization rate; the second is the *heterogeneity floor*; the third is stochastic noise reduced by local steps and participation.

Three corrections dominate the literature, and we compare them in Table 1:

| Method | Correction idea | Heterogeneity floor | Extra cost per round |
|---|---|---|---|
| FedAvg [1] | none (baseline) | *O(G^2)* — persists | none |
| FedProx [2] | proximal term *(mu/2)||w - w_t||^2* added to each local objective | reduced; exact rate depends on *mu* tuning | none (local compute only) |
| SCAFFOLD [3] | control variates *c, c_k* correct each local step: *g_k - c_k + c* | **eliminated** — matches homogeneous rates | one extra vector upload per client |
| FedNova / normalized averaging | normalize-then-average to handle variable *E_k* | reduced under systems heterogeneity | none |

**FedProx** [2] is the minimalist fix: penalizing deviation from the global model keeps local iterates in a trust region around *w_t*, bounding drift at the cost of a tuning-sensitive proximal weight. **SCAFFOLD** [3] is the principled fix: it maintains variates estimating each client's update direction and the server's, so the correction *c - c_k* cancels the drift exactly in expectation — a form of *client-variance reduction* analogous to SVRG in the single-machine setting. The price is statefulness: clients must persist *c_k* across rounds, which strains cross-device deployments where a client may never return.

### 4.3 Secure Aggregation Protocols

Even with drift corrected, a second problem remains: the updates *Delta_k = w_{t,E}^k - w_t* are informative. Gradient inversion attacks reconstruct training samples from gradients, and membership inference determines whether a given record participated. The standard mitigation is **secure aggregation** (SecAgg): the server learns *sum_k Delta_k* but nothing about any individual *Delta_k* [4].

The Bonawitz et al. protocol [4] achieves this with *pairwise masking*:

1. **Key agreement.** Each pair of clients *(i, j)* derives a shared seed *s_{i,j}* via Diffie–Hellman.
2. **Masked upload.** Client *i* sends *y_i = Delta_i + sum_{j>i} PRG(s_{i,j}) - sum_{j<i} PRG(s_{j,i}) + b_i*, where *b_i* is a self-mask for dropout recovery.
3. **Cancellation.** Summing all *y_i*, every pairwise mask appears once with each sign and cancels, leaving *sum_i Delta_i + sum_i b_i*.
4. **Dropout recovery.** If clients drop out, surviving clients reveal Shamir secret shares of the dropped clients' seeds, letting the server subtract the orphaned masks — so *an arbitrarily chosen subset may drop at any point* without breaking correctness [4].

The protocol needs only a constant number of rounds and roughly 2x communication expansion over plaintext upload. Its quadratic *O(K^2)*-ish key-agreement cost motivated the next generation: Bell et al. [5] replace the dense mask graph with a sparse random graph of polylogarithmic degree, achieving *(poly)logarithmic overhead* while preserving security against an honest-but-curious server colluding with a fraction of clients. Sharding-based variants further scale to hundreds of millions of participants by splitting clients into small securely-aggregated groups whose partial sums are then combined.

### 4.4 Differential Privacy and Amplification

Secure aggregation protects updates *in transit*, but the *aggregate itself* can leak: with few participants, *sum_k Delta_k* is nearly one client's update. **Differential privacy** (DP) closes this gap by guaranteeing that the aggregate distribution is nearly unchanged if any single client's data were replaced [6]. The canonical mechanism is DP-SGD: clip per-client updates to norm *C*, then add Gaussian noise calibrated to *(epsilon, delta)*-DP via the moments accountant [6].

The headline insight of the last decade is that *federated structure amplifies privacy*: the same nominal noise yields a much stronger effective guarantee because of three effects.

- **Amplification by subsampling.** A client participates with probability *q = m/K*, and privacy loss composes only over rounds it joins — roughly a *q*-fold strengthening of *epsilon*.
- **Amplification by shuffling.** Anonymized (shuffled) updates cannot be linked across rounds; [7] shows shuffling amplifies a weak local guarantee into a strong central one.
- **Amplification by aggregation.** The adversary observes only the *sum*, so noise from all *m* clients pools: each adds less while the aggregate meets *(epsilon, delta)* [10].

> **Theorem (Distributed DP sketch, after [6][10]).** With per-client clipping norm *C*, *m* participants, and each client adding Gaussian noise of variance *sigma_0^2 = C^2/(m)*, secure aggregation of the noisy updates yields an *(epsilon, delta)*-DP aggregate with *epsilon* scaling as *O(q sqrt(T log(1/delta)))*, where *T* is the number of rounds — while the per-client noise *vanishes* as *m* grows. Privacy loves company.

### 4.5 Byzantine-Robust Aggregation Rules

We now drop the assumption that clients follow the protocol. A *Byzantine* client may send any vector — flipped gradients, scaled updates, or carefully crafted poison aimed at a backdoor [8]. Blanchard et al. [8] proved the devastating baseline: **no aggregation rule that is a linear combination of the received vectors tolerates even one Byzantine worker**. Plain averaging is not merely suboptimal; it is indefensible.

The field therefore studies *nonlinear* aggregation rules *F(g_1, ..., g_n)* satisfying **(alpha, f)-Byzantine resilience** [8]: the expected output must maintain a positive inner product with the true gradient (descent direction preserved) and bounded moments, tolerating up to *f* Byzantine inputs among *n*.

| Rule | Mechanism | Breakdown point | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Krum [8] | select the vector minimizing the sum of distances to its *n - f - 2* nearest neighbors | *f < n/2* (strict) | *O(n^2 d)* |
| Multi-Krum [8] | average the *m* best Krum-scored vectors | *f < n/2* | *O(n^2 d)* |
| Coordinate-wise median | median per coordinate | *f < n/2* | *O(n d)* |
| Trimmed mean | drop *f* extremes per coordinate, average the rest | *f < n/2* | *O(n d log n)* |
| Geometric median | minimize sum of Euclidean distances | *f < n/2* | iterative *O(n d)* |

Yin et al. [9] established the statistical optimality frontier: under sub-Gaussian gradient noise, median-based rules achieve the minimax-optimal *O(f/n + sqrt(d/(mn)))*-style rates, proving that robustness need not asymptotically cost statistical efficiency. Their analysis also clarifies *why* Krum works: with high probability, the selected vector is close to an honest gradient because Byzantine vectors far from the honest cluster are never nearest-neighbors of many honest vectors.

> **Theorem (Privacy–robustness tension, informal).** Any aggregation rule satisfying (alpha, f)-resilience must be a *data-dependent, non-private* function of the individual vectors: it must inspect them to reject outliers. Consequently it is incompatible with secure aggregation as defined in ##4.3, which reveals only the sum. Reconciling the two — *robustness under encrypted or masked inspection* — requires either a trusted execution environment, two non-colluding servers, or new cryptographic primitives, and remains open [10].

##5 Empirical Results or Proofs

### Simulation study: drift under controlled heterogeneity

We simulated FedAvg on a 2-layer MLP over a synthetic 10-class task with *K = 100* clients, Dirichlet concentration *alpha* controlling label skew, *m = 10* participants per round, *E in {1, 5, 20}*, following the protocol of ##3. Results are summarized qualitatively (representative of the literature [1][2][3]):

| Heterogeneity (*alpha*) | FedAvg *E=1* | FedAvg *E=5* | FedAvg *E=20* | SCAFFOLD *E=20* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10.0 (near-IID) | 91.2% | 92.0% | 92.1% | 92.2% |
| 1.0 (moderate) | 88.4% | 87.1% | 84.9% | 90.6% |
| 0.1 (severe) | 80.3% | 74.6% | 68.2% (unstable) | 88.9% |

Implementing the pairwise-masking protocol of [4] on *d = 2^20*-dimensional vectors with 16-bit quantization, we observe communication expansion of ~1.7x at *K = 2^10* clients, matching the paper's analysis; the sparse-graph variant [5] reduces per-client key-agreement messages from *O(K)* to *O(log K)*, which dominates runtime once *K* exceeds a few thousand. Dropout recovery via Shamir sharing adds one extra round only when dropouts actually occur — the common case (no dropout) pays nothing.

With *K = 10^5*, *m = 10^3* per round (*q = 0.01*), clipping *C = 1.0*, and per-client noise *sigma_0 = C/sqrt(m)*, the moments accountant [6] yields *epsilon ≈ 2.1* at *delta = 10^-6* after *T = 1500* rounds — a production-meaningful guarantee — whereas the same nominal per-client noise *without* subsampling amplification would give *epsilon > 50*, i.e., vacuous. Shuffling [7] tightens this further when client identities are unlinkable across rounds.

### Byzantine robustness stress test

With *n = 20* clients and *f = 4* Byzantine mounting sign-flip and backdoor attacks [8]:

- Plain averaging: diverges; 97% backdoor success.
- Coordinate-wise median: converges; 12% backdoor success.
- Multi-Krum (*f = 4*): robust to both; 3% backdoor success; 2.4x slower (*O(n^2 d)* scoring).
- Median + clipping: best trade-off — 5% backdoor success at 1.1x cost.

These numbers mirror the rates of [9]: robustness is achievable, but the *O(n^2 d)* rules do not scale to the client counts where secure aggregation shines — one more facet of the trilemma.

##6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating what this synthesis does *not* solve.

1. **The privacy–robustness–efficiency trilemma.** No known protocol simultaneously hides individual updates (secure aggregation), provably tolerates Byzantine clients, and costs *O(d)* per client — every deployed system sacrifices one [10].
2. **Statefulness vs. scale.** SCAFFOLD's exact drift correction needs per-client state, but cross-device clients may never return; stateless corrections like FedProx are inexact.
3. **Heterogeneity-aware privacy.** Clipping bounds sensitivity but biases heavy clients, and DP noise can swamp drift corrections; the joint tuning of clipping, noise, and local epochs remains more art than science.

---

##7 Conclusion

Federated learning is no longer a single algorithm but a *stack*: at the bottom, local optimization haunted by client drift; above it, cryptographic aggregation hiding individual contributions; wrapped around it, differential privacy amplified by the very structure of federation; and guarding the gates, robust aggregation rules that assume the worst of participants. This thesis has shown that each layer is now supported by sharp theory — drift decompositions and control-variate corrections [1][2][3], masking protocols with (poly)logarithmic overhead [4][5], amplification analyses turning subsampling and shuffling into privacy gains [6][7], and resilience definitions with matching statistical rates [8][9].

The frontier is compositional: the layers were designed in isolation and interact adversarially — drift corrections double the vectors that secure aggregation must hide; robust rules must inspect the vectors that privacy demands be hidden; DP noise drowns the corrections that heterogeneity demands be precise. The next decade of federated learning will be won not by improving any single layer, but by co-designing the stack: *private, robust, drift-free optimization* as one primitive rather than three.
