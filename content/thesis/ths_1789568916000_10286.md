# Certified Adversarial Robustness via Randomized Smoothing: Certificates, Trade-offs, and Fundamental Limits

## Abstract

Randomized smoothing converts any base classifier into a provably robust smoothed classifier by predicting the most probable label under isotropic Gaussian noise, yielding a tight certificate that the prediction is constant inside an ℓ2 ball whose radius grows with the noise scale and the class margin. This thesis develops the theory, practice, and limits of that program. We trace the lineage from differential-privacy-based smoothing through the tight Neyman–Pearson analysis, explain the Monte Carlo certification protocol with Clopper–Pearson confidence bounds, and survey training advances such as Gaussian data augmentation, adversarially trained smoothed classifiers, and denoised smoothing for pretrained models and black-box APIs. We then examine the geometry behind the guarantee, its generalization to arbitrary norms via Wulff crystals and Lipschitz methods, and the fundamental dimensionality barriers that make high-dimensional ℓp certification for p greater than 2 essentially vacuous. Empirically, smoothing remains the most scalable certified defense on ImageNet-scale vision models, while theory shows precisely where it cannot go.

##1 Introduction

Deep neural networks remain brittle to imperceptible adversarial perturbations: carefully crafted inputs of tiny ℓp norm reliably flip the predictions of otherwise high-performing classifiers [8]. The dominant empirical defenses, most notably adversarial training, offer strong resistance against known attacks but come with no formal guarantee that an *unknown* or future attack could not do better. Certified defenses close this gap by producing, for each input, both a prediction and a *certificate*: a radius R such that the classifier's prediction is provably constant for every perturbation within an ℓ2 ball of radius R around the input. The difficulty has always been scale. Exact verification methods based on mixed-integer programming or layer-wise relaxations scale only to small networks or low-dimensional inputs and have never been demonstrated at ImageNet scale [1].

*Randomized smoothing* is the first certified defense to scale to realistic networks and datasets. The idea is disarmingly simple. Given an arbitrary base classifier f, define the *smoothed classifier* g(x) as the class most likely to be returned by f when x is corrupted by isotropic Gaussian noise. Intuitively, if f already classifies most noisy copies of x as the same class, then the majority vote is stable: small adversarial shifts of the mean of the Gaussian leave the argmax of the class probabilities unchanged. The surprise of Cohen et al. [1] was that this intuition admits a *tight* analysis — the certified radius R equals σ/2 times the gap between the inverse Gaussian CDFs of the top two class probabilities, and no larger radius can be certified from the same probability information alone. That tightness result turned smoothing from a heuristic into a principled, optimal-on-information defense.

Since then, the field has grown in three directions. Practically, researchers have asked how to *train* base classifiers so that their noisy-class probabilities concentrate on the correct label — via Gaussian augmentation, adversarial training of smoothed classifiers, and denoising preprocessors that retrofit robustness onto pretrained models and even proprietary vision APIs [2][3][4]. Geometrically, the analysis has been generalized beyond the Gaussian to arbitrary smoothing distributions and norms, revealing elegant connections to the Lipschitz continuity of smoothed functions and the Wulff crystals of norm geometry [5]. And negatively, a sharp literature on *fundamental limits* has shown that smoothing's certificates decay catastrophically with dimension for p greater than 2, effectively ruling out nontrivial ℓ∞ certification for high-dimensional images with isotropic noise [6][7].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reconstructs the intellectual background, from PixelDP's differential-privacy foundations to the tight Gaussian guarantee. Section 3 presents the full certification methodology: the two-stage Monte Carlo protocol, confidence intervals, abstention, and training recipes. Section 4 dives into four theoretical pillars: the Neyman–Pearson derivation, the Lipschitz geometry of smoothing, denoised smoothing for pretrained models, and general-norm theories. Section 5 reports empirical results and a proof sketch of the tight radius. Section 6 catalogs the limitations — dimensionality curses, the accuracy–robustness trade-off, sample complexity, and geometric blind spots — and Section 7 concludes with open directions.

##3 Methodology

### The two-stage certification protocol

Certifying an input x proceeds in two Monte Carlo stages.

```python
from scipy.stats import norm

    """Monte Carlo certification of a smoothed classifier (Cohen et al. [1])."""
    noise = np.random.randn(n0, *x.shape) * sigma
    votes = [base_classifier(x + e) for e in noise]
    cA = max(set(votes), key=votes.count)
    countsA = sum(1 for v in votes if v == cA)
    if countsA <= n0 // 2:
        return cA, 0.0  # abstain: no certified radius
```

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Neyman–Pearson Lemma and the Tight Radius

The proof of the radius formula is a jewel of applied probability.

> **Theorem (Tight certified radius [1]).** Let f be any deterministic or random base classifier, let g be its σ-smoothed classifier, and let pA and pB be as defined in Section 2 with pA + pB ≤ 1. Then g(x') = cA for all x' with ℓ2 distance below R = σ/2 (Φ⁻¹(pA) − Φ⁻¹(pB)). Moreover, this radius is tight: there exists a base classifier f consistent with pA and pB for which g(x') ≠ cA at every distance exceeding R.

##6 Limitations


**The curse of dimensionality for p greater than 2.** Kumar et al. [6] prove that for i.i.d. smoothing distributions, the largest certifiable ℓp radius for p greater than 2 decays as O(1/d^{1/2 − 1/p}) in the dimension d; for ℓ∞ this is O(1/√d). Zhang et al. [7] sharpen the point: random smoothing may be *unable* to certify ℓ∞ robustness for high-dimensional images at all, since the required noise scale would obliterate clean accuracy long before certifying a meaningful radius. Yang et al. [5] show this barrier is not an artifact of the Gaussian — it holds for every smoothing scheme that uses only label statistics, via Banach space cotype theory. Smoothing is, in a precise sense, an ℓ2 defense.

**The accuracy–robustness trade-off.** The parameter σ is a genuine knob, not a free lunch. Larger σ widens certificates but feeds the base classifier noisier inputs, eroding clean accuracy; Cohen et al. train one model per σ and report the full frontier. On ImageNet, σ = 1.0 certifies impressively large radii but sacrifices so much clean accuracy that the certified-accuracy curve is dominated by σ = 0.25 at small radii. Whether this trade-off is fundamental or an artifact of current training is contested: Salman et al. [3] show better training pushes the frontier outward, but no training method has collapsed it.

**Sample complexity and abstention.** Rigorous certificates demand many forward passes — typically 100,000 per input in published evaluations — making certification orders of magnitude slower than inference. The Clopper–Pearson bound is conservative: with n samples, the certified radius is penalized by the confidence width, so small-n certificates are weak certificates. And the protocol abstains rather than erring, so reported "certified accuracy" conflates classifier quality with the abstention rate.

**Geometric blind spots.** The certificate is a *ball*: it guarantees nothing about structured or semantic perturbations — rotations, translations, patch attacks, or ℓ0-sparse corruptions — unless a bespoke smoothing distribution is designed for them [5]. The tightness theorem cuts both ways: with only (pA, pB), the certificate cannot exploit any additional structure of f, so on highly confident inputs the guarantee is systematically conservative relative to the smoothed classifier's true robustness.

##7 Conclusion

Randomized smoothing transformed certified robustness from a small-network curiosity into an ImageNet-scale engineering discipline. Its core — the Neyman–Pearson-tight radius, the two-stage Monte Carlo protocol with abstention, and the σ-controlled accuracy–robustness frontier — is by now textbook material, and its training extensions (Gaussian augmentation, SmoothAdv, denoised smoothing) have steadily pushed the empirical frontier while retrofitting robustness onto pretrained and even black-box models. The general theory, from Lipschitz geometry to Wulff crystals, reveals smoothing as one instance of a broader principle: convolve a bounded function with noise, and its regularity becomes your certificate. Yet the fundamental limits are equally crisp: dimensionality curses forbid nontrivial ℓp certificates for p greater than 2 from label statistics alone, and the σ trade-off, sample complexity, and ball-shaped guarantees circumscribe where smoothing can ever apply. The open frontier is not larger radii but *richer certificates* — smoothing distributions matched to semantic threat models, tighter use of model structure beyond two probability scalars, and certification protocols fast enough for deployment. Until then, randomized smoothing stands as both the strongest practical certified defense for ℓ2 vision and a precise map of where provable robustness must look next.
