# Type-Directed Program Synthesis: Liquid Types, Bidirectional Checking, and Refinement-Driven Code Generation

## Abstract

Program synthesis promises correct-by-construction software, yet tools scale only when specifications are expressive enough to capture intent and restricted enough to stay machine-checkable. This thesis develops *type-directed program synthesis*, in which a single refinement type serves as specification, search heuristic, and verification certificate. We integrate three mature ideas. **Bidirectional type checking** separates type *synthesis* from *checking*, yielding a syntax-directed discipline where expected types flow downward and inferred types flow upward; the direction-change points are exactly where a synthesizer must decide. **Liquid types** restrict dependent refinements to an SMT-decidable logic, recovering inference through predicate abstraction and reducing verification to Horn-constraint solving. **Refinement-driven synthesis**, pioneered by Synquid, exploits the compositionality of typing derivations: a goal type decomposes into independent subgoal types for program fragments, pruning the search space combinatorially. We present the core judgments, the constraint pipeline, and the round-trip synthesis algorithm with worked examples, sketch soundness arguments, survey empirical results, and map open frontiers: effectful synthesis, neural guidance, and the path from typed holes to automatic refinement-driven code generation.

## 1. Introduction

Program synthesis — automatic construction of programs from specifications — is among the oldest aspirations of computer science, from early deductive synthesis to industrial successes such as FlashFill and the syntax-guided synthesis (SyGuS) community. The central tension never changed: expressive specifications defeat automation, while tractable ones under-specify, admitting hordes of technically-correct but useless candidates.

Types occupy a privileged middle ground. A type is already a partial specification that programmers write (or that can be inferred); type checking is decidable, compositional, and fast. *Refinement types* strengthen this bargain by decorating types with logical predicates — `{v:Int | v >= 0}` is the type of natural numbers, `{v:List a | len v = n}` the type of lists of length exactly `n`. This thesis argues that the Curry–Howard reading of refinements is not merely a slogan but an engineering blueprint: three independently developed technologies — bidirectional checking, liquid inference, and refinement-directed search — compose into a synthesis architecture whose soundness is inherited from its type system and whose scalability is inherited from modern SMT solving.

The contributions of this thesis are expository: a unified account of bidirectional judgments as the synthesizer's *control plane*, liquid inference as Horn-constraint solving over qualifier lattices, and refinement-driven synthesis as *goal decomposition* — the property that lets type-based specifications prune search combinatorially. We add proof sketches for the metatheory, a survey of published empirical results, and a candid assessment of limitations and open frontiers. Section 2 surveys the background, Section 3 fixes the core calculus and inference pipeline, Section 4 is the deep dive, Section 5 covers results and proofs, Section 6 limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From types to refinement types

From Freeman and Pfenning's ML refinements (1991) through Xi and Pfenning's *Dependent ML* (1998–1999), the bargain has been explicit: restrict refinements to decidable theories, keep checking decidable.

A refinement type has the shape `{v:B | r}`, where `B` is a base (Hindley–Milner) type, `v` is the *value variable* standing for the inhabitant, and `r` is a Boolean-valued expression — the *refinement predicate* — drawn from a decidable logic [7]. The type `{v:Int | v mod 2 == 0}` denotes even integers; `x:{v:Int | v > 0} -> {v:Int | v > x}` denotes functions mapping positive integers to something larger. Refinements may mention program variables, recovering *value dependence* while every verification condition is discharged by the SMT solver [2].

### 2.2 SMT solvers as oracles

Modern synthesis and verification lean entirely on satisfiability modulo theories (SMT) solvers such as Z3 and CVC5. These tools decide combinations of theories — quantifier-free linear integer arithmetic (QF_LIA), uninterpreted functions with equality (QF_UF), arrays, bitvectors — via the Nelson–Oppen combination method and CDCL(T) search. The solver is an *oracle for validity*: subtyping `{v:B | r1} <: {v:B | r2}` holds iff `⟦Γ⟧ ∧ r1 ∧ ¬r2` is unsatisfiable. Everything undecidable is quarantined inside the solver's theories.

### 2.3 Bidirectional typing

In the bidirectional discipline [6], every typing rule splits in two — one for when the result type is known, one for when it is unknown:

| Judgment | Reading | Information flow |
|---|---|---|
| `Γ ⊢ e ⇒ T` | `e` **synthesizes** type `T` | bottom-up, from term to type |
| `Γ ⊢ e ⇐ T` | `e` **checks against** `T` | top-down, from expected type to term |

Introduction forms are checked against known types; elimination forms synthesize types from their components. The modes meet at *change-of-direction* rules — ascription `(e : T)` and subsumption — making the discipline *local*: each rule consults only immediate subterms, so errors (and, as we shall see, synthesis goals) decompose precisely.

### 2.4 The synthesis landscape

Program synthesis techniques divide roughly into three families. **Deductive synthesis** derives programs by constructive proof from logical specifications; it is general but labor-intensive. **Enumerative and constraint-based synthesis**, epitomized by the SyGuS format, searches a grammar of candidates pruned by counterexamples; it is automatic but scales poorly, because specifications (examples, grammars) do not decompose. **Type-directed synthesis**, our subject, uses refinement types as specifications. Its distinguishing claim, demonstrated by Synquid [3], is *modularity*: typing derivations are compositional, so a whole-program specification decomposes into per-part specifications — a combinatorial reduction in search.

## 3. Methodology

We fix a core calculus and its bidirectional rules, then describe the pipeline from programs to Horn constraints to SMT queries; Section 4 elaborates this skeleton.

### 3.1 The core calculus λ^R

We work in a call-by-value lambda calculus with refinement types, algebraic datatypes, and general recursion — expressive enough to model the interesting cases, small enough to reason about precisely.

**Syntax.** Terms and types:

```
e ::= x | c                       (variables, constants)
    | λx. e | e1 e2               (abstraction, application)
    | (e : T)                     (ascription)
    | let x = e1 in e2            (let-binding)
    | fix f. e                    (recursion)
    | Nil | Cons e1 e2            (list constructors)
    | case e of {Nil → e1; Cons h t → e2}

T ::= {v:B | r}                   (refined base type)
    | x:T1 → T2                   (dependent function type)
    | ∀α. T                       (polymorphism)
B ::= Int | Bool | List B | ...
r ::= e                           (Boolean-valued refinement expression)
```

The refinement `r` may mention the value variable `v` and any program variables in scope. We write `Nat` for `{v:Int | v >= 0}` and `{List a | len v = n}` for length-indexed lists.

**Judgments.** Three mutually defined judgments carry the system:

- `Γ ⊢ e ⇒ T` — *synthesis*: from `e`, compute its (most precise) type.
- `Γ ⊢ e ⇐ T` — *checking*: verify `e` against the expected type `T`.
- `Γ ⊢ T1 <: T2` — *subtyping*: every value of `T1` is a value of `T2`.

Selected rules (the full set is standard):

```
(x : T) ∈ Γ
──────────── (Var⇒)        Γ ⊢ e1 ⇒ x:T1 → T2    Γ ⊢ e2 ⇐ T1
Γ ⊢ x ⇒ T                 ───────────────────────────────── (App⇒)
                                          Γ ⊢ e1 e2 ⇒ T2[e2/x]

Γ, x:T1 ⊢ e ⇐ T2
────────────────── (Lam⇐)        Γ ⊢ e ⇐ T
Γ ⊢ λx.e ⇐ x:T1 → T2             ───────────── (Ann⇒)
                                 Γ ⊢ (e : T) ⇒ T

Γ ⊢ e ⇒ T′    Γ ⊢ T′ <: T
───────────────────────── (Sub)  — the change-of-direction rule
Γ ⊢ e ⇐ T
```

Subtyping on refined bases delegates to the solver:

```
Valid(⟦Γ⟧ ∧ r1 ⇒ r2)
──────────────────────────────── (Sub-Base)
Γ ⊢ {v:B | r1} <: {v:B | r2}
```

where `⟦Γ⟧` conjoins the refinements in `Γ`. Function subtyping is contravariant in the domain, as usual.

> **Theorem (Bidirectional soundness):** If `Γ ⊢ e ⇐ T` (or `Γ ⊢ e ⇒ T`) is derivable, then `e` is well-typed at `T` in the underlying declarative type-assignment system. ∎

> **Theorem (Bidirectional completeness for annotated terms):** If `e` is declaratively well-typed, there is an elaboration `e⁺` inserting ascriptions at β-redexes such that `e⁺` checks bidirectionally. ∎

### 3.2 The inference pipeline

Liquid type inference proceeds in four stages, each a total function on the output of the previous one:

1. **Elaboration.** Parse, desugar, and insert refinement *templates* `{v:B | κ}` wherever annotations are missing, each `κ` a fresh Horn variable.
2. **Constraint generation.** Walk the derivation; each (Sub-Base) use emits a Horn clause over the `κ` variables, e.g. `κ1 ∧ (v > x) ⇒ κ2`.
3. **Horn solving.** Compute the *strongest* qualifier-conjunction solution for each `κ` by greatest fixed point over a finite qualifier set `Q`.
4. **SMT discharge.** Substitute the solution into each clause and ask the SMT solver for validity. Success means the program type-checks; a counterexample yields a local error report.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Bidirectional checking: the control plane of synthesis

For synthesis, bidirectional typing is the *control plane* locating decisions: types flow upward in synthesis mode (`⇒`), downward in checking mode (`⇐`). Most of a derivation is pure *propagation*; genuine decisions occur only at direction changes — at (Ann⇒) where a type is *chosen*, at elimination sites where a component must be *selected*, and at (Sub) where precision may be *weakened*.

A synthesis query is a checking judgment `Γ ⊢ ? ⇐ T` with a hole to fill, and the checking rules *decompose* it structurally: a function-typed goal forces a lambda, pushing a smaller goal into an extended context; a datatype goal forces a constructor or case split, each argument an independent subgoal. Search happens only at elimination positions, each candidate immediately disciplined by the downward-flowing expected type.

Crucially, failure is *local and informative*: a rejected candidate's failing sub-derivation yields a *residual goal* — a sharper specification for what remains — pruning whole families of candidates that would fail the same way. The ascription `(e : T)`, the unique rule moving from checking to synthesis, is thus the natural language of specifications: to specify is to ascribe; to synthesize is to fill the hole.

### 4.2 Liquid types and predicate abstraction

Liquid types [1] ask how much dependent typing survives automatic inference. The answer: unknown refinements are not arbitrary predicates but conjunctions over a finite set of *qualifiers* `Q` — templates like `⋆ >= 0` or `⋆ = x + 1`, instantiated with in-scope variables. Inference computes, per Horn variable `κ`, the *strongest* qualifier conjunction satisfying all constraints, which makes the system predictable: adding qualifiers only gains precision.

```haskell
{-@ max :: x:Int -> y:Int -> {v:Int | v >= x && v >= y} @-}
max x y = if x > y then x else y
```

Elaboration inserts a template `{v:Int | κ}`; the branches emit `(x > y) ∧ (v = x) ⇒ κ` and `(¬(x > y)) ∧ (v = y) ⇒ κ`. With qualifiers `{⋆ >= x, ⋆ >= y}` the fixed point yields `κ := (v >= x) ∧ (v >= y)`, discharged by one SMT query. Missing `⋆ >= y` would produce the weaker `v >= x` and a precise error — *the else-branch does not establish the postcondition*. The qualifier set is the system's *resolution knob* — and the principal source of false alarms (Section 6).

### 4.3 Constraint generation and SMT discharge

Constraint generation mirrors the bidirectional derivation: application synthesizes the function's type, checks the argument, and substitutes it into the result refinement; branching checks each arm under the guard and its negation, gaining path-sensitivity for free; every subsumption emits a Horn clause `⟦Γ⟧ ∧ r_actual ⇒ κ_expected`. A greatest-fixed-point solver over the qualifier lattice then weakens each `κ` until all clauses hold — termination following from finiteness of `Q`:

```python
def solve_horn(clauses, qualifiers):
    sol = {k: conjunction(qualifiers[k]) for k in horn_vars(clauses)}
    changed = True
    while changed:                       # at most sum |Q_k| iterations
        changed = False
        for (ante, head) in clauses:
            if not smt_valid(implies(subst(ante, sol), subst(head, sol))):
                if head is FALSE: raise TypeError("verification failed")
                sol[head] = weaken(sol[head], ante, qualifiers[head])
                changed = True
    return sol
```

Discharging to SMT needs an embedding into SMT-LIB: base types map to sorts, and datatype *measures* like `len` map to uninterpreted functions axiomatized by their constructor equations:

```rust
// len is uninterpreted; constructor axioms pin it down.
fn embed_len_axioms(s: &mut Solver) {
    s.assert(len(nil()) == 0);
    s.assert(forall(|h: Term, t: Term| len(cons(h, t.clone())) == 1 + len(t)));
}
fn check_subtype(env: &Env, r1: &Ref, r2: &Ref) -> bool {
    !solver.is_sat(&and(env.to_smt(), r1.to_smt(), not(r2.to_smt())))
}
```

*Refinement reflection* [2] lifts terminating functions into the logic for extrinsic proofs about real code — adding definitions, never unbounded search, so the decidable core remains.

### 4.4 Refinement-driven synthesis: the Synquid algorithm

Given a goal type `T` and typed components, the synthesizer must produce `e` with `⊢ e ⇐ T`; naive enumeration faces `O(c^d)` candidates. Synquid's [3] insight is that the bidirectional derivation *decomposes* the goal — lambdas, constructors, and case splits are forced structurally — so search happens only at elimination positions, each checked against the downward-flowing expected type. *Round-trip checking* propagates that type through eliminations, and failures are analyzed into residual goals pruning whole candidate families.

Two mechanisms complete the picture. **Condition abduction** solves for unknown `if`-guards: the weakest qualifier-conjunction `g` under which the then-branch checks under `g` and the else-branch under `¬g`. **Termination** permits recursion only on arguments decreasing under a well-founded metric from the goal type. For `replicate :: n:Nat -> x:a -> {v:List a | len v = n}`:

1. Function goal → `λn. λx. ? ⇐ {len v = n}`.
2. Abduce guard `g`; try `Nil` under `g`, `Cons`-term under `¬g`.
3. `Nil` needs `n = 0`, so `g := (n <= 0)`.
4. Under `n > 0`, `Cons x ?` with `? ⇐ {len v = n - 1}` is solved recursively by `replicate (dec n) x`.

```haskell
replicate = \n x -> if n <= 0 then Nil else Cons x (replicate (dec n) x)
```

The search explores *typing derivations*, not syntax trees, and derivations compose: the combinatorial payoff of type-based specifications.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Metatheory: what is guaranteed

Three theorems anchor the framework; we sketch each.

> **Theorem (Synthesis soundness):** If the synthesizer returns a term `e` for goal `T`, then `⊢ e ⇐ T` is derivable. *Proof sketch.* Every decomposition step mirrors a valid rule application, every component choice is round-trip-checked, and abduced guards are SMT-verified before commitment; induction on the trace yields a derivation. ∎

> **Theorem (Decidability of liquid checking):** For a fixed finite qualifier set `Q`, checking `Γ ⊢ e ⇐ T` is decidable. *Proof sketch.* Generation is syntax-directed and total; solving terminates by finiteness of `Q`; each SMT query is over decidable theories. ∎

> **Theorem (Relative completeness):** If some well-founded `e` with `⊢ e ⇐ T` exists in the library and qualifier language, breadth-first synthesis returns *some* `e′` with `⊢ e′ ⇐ T`. *Proof sketch.* Enumeration by increasing size; sound pruning never discards a completable derivation. ∎

Completeness is thus *relative* to the qualifier language and component library — which tells the user exactly which knobs to turn when synthesis fails.

### 5.2 Empirical results

**Synquid (PLDI 2016) [3].** The prototype synthesized more complex programs than prior tools — including several sorting algorithms and operations on balanced search trees — and handled most benchmarks tackled by existing synthesizers, *often starting from significantly more concise and intuitive user input*. The conciseness claim is the crucial one: because refinement types decompose, the user writes one type where example-based tools need dozens of examples.

**Local refinement typing / FUSION (2017) [5].** Cosman and Jhala's FUSION algorithm needs signatures only for top-level exports and cyclic dependencies, then synthesizes the *most precise* refinement types for all intermediate terms. On the Liquid Haskell suite (~12 KLOC) it used roughly *half as many templates* and ran nearly *2× faster*; on theorem-proving benchmarks it was *10–50× faster* and avoided false alarms that had made verification impossible.

**Liquid Haskell (ICFP 2014) [2].** Run over 10,000+ lines of widely used Haskell libraries, the checker proved *96% of all recursive functions terminating* with only *1.7 lines of termination annotations per 100 lines of code* — deep properties of legacy code at modest annotation cost, with stratification of binders keeping the system sound under laziness.

| System | Scale | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| Synquid [3] | dozens of synthesis benchmarks | sorting, BST ops from concise types |
| FUSION [5] | ~12 KLOC Liquid Haskell suite | ½ templates, ~2× faster; 10–50× on proofs |
| Liquid Haskell [2] | 10,000+ LOC libraries | 96% termination, 1.7 annots/100 LOC |

### 5.3 A note on proof engineering

Refinement *reflection* [2] is the bridge from type checking to proof engineering: terminating functions are lifted into the refinement logic, so extrinsic proofs about existing code can be written as typed terms while SMT automation discharges the arithmetic. For synthesis, reflection matters because synthesized components can carry *proofs* of their specifications, making the synthesizer's output auditable by an independent checker.

## 6. Limitations

No honest account omits the framework's sharp edges. We enumerate the principal ones.

**Qualifier fragility.** Precision is bounded above by the qualifier set `Q`. A missing qualifier causes *false alarms* or synthesis failure, and diagnosing "which qualifier am I missing" remains a dark art; automatic qualifier generation from program text helps but is heuristic.

**Higher-order and stateful invariants.** The decidable fragment handles first-order data well but struggles with higher-order invariants (e.g., "this callback preserves the invariant") and with mutable state, where refinements must thread through a heap model. Research systems exist, but they are far from the push-button maturity of the first-order pipeline.

**Arithmetic beyond the solver.** Nonlinear arithmetic, transcendental functions, and bit-precise reasoning fall outside the efficiently decidable theories; the solver may time out or return `unknown`, which the pipeline must conservatively treat as failure. Programs whose correctness hinges on such reasoning cannot be handled without manual proof.

**Termination metrics.** Synthesis requires well-founded recursion, and while metrics can sometimes be inferred from the goal type, in general the user supplies them. A wrong or missing metric silently shrinks the searchable program space.

**Search blowup.** Goal decomposition prunes *combinatorially*, but the worst case remains exponential in the component library size. Large libraries (hundreds of components) still overwhelm the enumerator; practical deployments rely on careful library curation and, increasingly, learned ranking of candidates.

**Annotation at cyclic dependencies.** FUSION reduced but did not eliminate annotations: recursive definitions and module boundaries still need signatures. The system is *locally* predictable but not annotation-free.

**Error messages.** When SMT discharge fails, the counterexample is a model of the negated verification condition — precise for experts, cryptic for newcomers. Bridging the gap between solver models and programmer intuition remains open.

## 7. Conclusion

This thesis has presented type-directed program synthesis as the composition of three ideas. **Bidirectional checking** provides the control plane: expected types flow downward, inferred types flow upward, and every change of direction marks a decision point for the synthesizer. **Liquid types** provide the inference engine: dependent refinements in an SMT-decidable logic, with unknowns solved as strongest qualifier conjunctions over Horn constraints. **Refinement-driven synthesis** provides the search strategy: goal types decompose along the derivation, round-trip checking turns failures into sharper residual goals, and condition abduction turns branching into solving.

The empirical record shows the approach scales beyond toy examples; the metatheory shows it rests on solid ground.

Three frontiers stand out. **Effectful synthesis** extends refinements to state, exceptions, and concurrency, where specifications must describe *protocols*, not just values. **Neural guidance** uses learned models to rank candidates and propose qualifiers, attacking qualifier fragility and search blowup with data — while keeping the type system as the soundness backstop that pure neural code generation lacks. **Interactive synthesis** generalizes Agda-style typed holes [4]: programmer and synthesizer collaborate, the hole's expected type serving as a live, progressively refined specification. In the limit, the distinction between *writing* a program and *proving* it correct disappears — the oldest dream of the field, and refinement types are its most practical embodiment.
