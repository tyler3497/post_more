# The Nyström Method and Randomized Numerical Linear Algebra: Leverage-Score Sampling, Sketching, and Kernel Approximation with Spectral Guarantees

## Abstract

The Nyström method approximates a large symmetric positive semidefinite (SPSD) kernel matrix \(K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) by a rank-restricted skeleton decomposition \( \tilde{K} = C W^{\dagger} C^{\top} \), where \(C = KP\) collects a sketched subset of columns and \(W = P^{\top} KP\) is their inner intersection. We survey the modern randomized numerical linear algebra (RandNLA) theory of this construction: from the quadrature-inspired heuristic of Williams and Seeger [1] and the first rigorous additive-error bounds of Drineas and Mahoney [2], through leverage-score sampling with *relative-error* and *spectral-norm* guarantees [3, 4], to the oblivious sketching framework (Gaussian, SRHT, CountSketch) and fast approximate leverage-score estimation [5]. We present the deterministic structural conditions of Gittens and Mahoney that separate sampling from projection methods, derive spectral guarantees connecting approximation quality to the decay of the kernel spectrum, and examine ridge leverage scores as the sampling distribution that makes Nyström-based kernel ridge regression statistically optimal. We compare Nyström approximations against random Fourier features, discuss failure modes — high coherence, flat spectra, and the \(O(n^2)\) leverage-score bottleneck — and conclude with open problems in streaming, distributed, and dequantized regimes.

## 1. Introduction

Kernel methods reduce nonlinear learning to linear algebra on the *kernel (Gram) matrix* \(K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)\), a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix whose dimension \(n\) equals the number of training points. Gaussian processes, kernel ridge regression (KRR), kernel SVMs, spectral clustering, and kernel PCA all require — in their naïve form — storing \(K\) at cost \(O(n^2)\) and factorizing it at cost \(O(n^3)\). When \(n\) reaches \(10^5\) or \(10^6\), both are infeasible. The central algorithmic question of scalable kernel learning is therefore:

> **How can we replace \(K\) by a data structure of size \(O(nc)\) with \(c \ll n\) while provably preserving the statistical and spectral properties that downstream algorithms depend on?**

The Nyström method, imported from the numerical solution of integral equations — where one approximates an eigenfunction expansion by quadrature at a finite set of *landmark* points — gives the canonical answer. Introduced to machine learning by Williams and Seeger [1], it samples \(c\) columns of \(K\) to form \(C \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times c}\) and the \(c \times c\) intersection \(W\), then defines the approximation

\[
\tilde{K} = C W^{\dagger} C^{\top},
\]

where \(W^{\dagger}\) is the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse. Because \(\tilde{K}\) is SPSD and rank at most \(c\), it admits the factorization \(\tilde{K} = L L^{\top}\) with \(L \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times c}\), and any kernel algorithm can be rewritten in terms of \(L\) at cost \(O(nc^2 + c^3)\) instead of \(O(n^3)\).

Early practice used *uniform* column sampling, and early theory provided only *additive* error bounds of the form \(\|K - \tilde{K}\| \le \|K - K_k\| + \epsilon \|K\|\), which are vacuous whenever \(\|K\|\) dwarfs the residual \(\|K - K_k\|\) [2]. The RandNLA revolution of the last two decades replaced this with a hierarchy of ever-stronger guarantees:

1. **Nonuniform sampling** by statistical *leverage scores* yields relative-error bounds [3, 4, 7];
2. **Oblivious sketching** (Gaussian projections, SRHT, CountSketch) removes the dependence on data-dependent probabilities at the price of denser or structured random maps [5, 10];
3. **Structural analysis** explains *why* sampling succeeds precisely when the spectrum decays fast and the leverage scores are nonuniform — the regime where uniform sampling provably fails [4];
4. **Ridge leverage scores** extend the theory to regularized objectives, giving optimal statistical rates for Nyström-based KRR [8, 9].

This thesis develops these ideas from first principles, states the key theorems with proof sketches, and grounds the analysis in the exact statements of the literature.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Nyström extension

Let \(k: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}\) be a positive definite kernel and \(\{x_i\}_{i=1}^n\) the data. Reordering indices so that the \(c\) landmarks come first, partition

\[
K = \begin{pmatrix} W & S^{\top} \\ S & K_{22} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
C = \begin{pmatrix} W \\ S \end{pmatrix}.
\]

The Nyström approximant is \(\tilde{K} = C W^{\dagger} C^{\top}\). Writing the eigendecomposition \(W = U_W \Lambda_W U_W^{\top}\), we obtain approximate eigenvectors and eigenvalues of \(K\) via

\[
\tilde{\lambda}_i = \frac{n}{c}\,\lambda_i(W), \qquad
\tilde{u}_i = \sqrt{\frac{c}{n}}\,\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}_i}\, C u_i(W),
\]

the classical *Nyström extension* of the eigenfunctions of the empirical integral operator [1]. This is not merely a matrix trick: it is the orthogonal projection of the feature maps \(\phi(x_i)\) onto \(\mathrm{span}\{\phi(x_{i_1}), \dots, \phi(x_{i_c})\}\), so \(\tilde{K}\) is the *best* SPSD approximation of \(K\) supported on the landmark subspace.

### 2.2 Leverage scores and coherence

For a matrix \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) with orthonormal basis \(U \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}\) for its column space, the *statistical leverage score* of row \(i\) is

\[
\ell_i = \|U_{(i)}\|_2^2,
\]

the squared norm of the \(i\)-th row of \(U\). Since \(\sum_i \ell_i = r\), the normalized scores \(p_i = \ell_i / r\) form a probability distribution that quantifies how *influential* each row is. The *coherence* \(\mu = \frac{n}{r} \max_i \ell_i \in [1, n/r]\) measures nonuniformity: \(\mu \approx 1\) means every row matters equally (uniform sampling is safe), while large \(\mu\) means a few rows carry the range (uniform sampling is blind to them) [5].

For SPSD Nyström approximation, leverage scores are computed with respect to \(V_k\), the top-\(k\) eigenvectors of \(K\), and sampling columns with probability proportional to \(\ell_i\) focuses the sketch on the directions that dominate the spectrum.

### 2.3 Sketching matrices

A *sketching matrix* \(P \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times c}\) compresses \(K\) to \(C = KP\) and \(W = P^{\top}KP\). Column sampling corresponds to \(P\) being a (rescaled) subset of the identity; *oblivious* sketches use random \(P\) independent of \(K\):

| Sketch | Construction | Sketch size \(c\) | Apply cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian | i.i.d. \(N(0, 1/c)\) entries | \(O(k/\epsilon)\) | \(O(n^2 c)\) |
| SRHT | \(P = \sqrt{n/c}\, D H R^{\top}\) (random signs, Hadamard, subsample) | \(O((k + \log n)(\epsilon^{-1} + \log k))\) | \(O(n^2 \log c)\) |
| CountSketch | one nonzero \(\pm 1\) per column | \(O(k/\epsilon + k^2)\) | \(O(n^2)\) |
| Leverage sampling | sample columns \(\propto \ell_i\), rescale | \(O(k \log k / \epsilon)\) | \(\tilde{O}(n^2 k)\) |

*Table 1: Sufficient sketch sizes for Nyström approximation of a rank-\(k\)-dominated kernel matrix; \(\mu\) denotes the coherence of the top-\(k\) eigenspace. Adapted from the comparison in [10].*

The key property a sketch must satisfy is the *subspace embedding*: with high probability, \(\|P^{\top} U_k x\|_2^2 = (1 \pm \epsilon)\|x\|_2^2\) for all \(x\), i.e., \(P^{\top}\) preserves the geometry of the dominant eigenspace. Every spectral guarantee below is, at bottom, a statement about which sketches embed the range of \(V_k\).

## 3. Methodology

We now fix notation for the analysis. Let \(K = V \Sigma V^{\top}\) be the eigendecomposition with \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_n \ge 0\), and let \(K_k = V_k \Sigma_k V_k^{\top}\) be the best rank-\(k\) approximation. A Nyström scheme is specified by a (possibly random, possibly data-dependent) sketch \(P \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times c}\); we study \(\tilde{K} = KP(P^{\top}KP)^{\dagger}P^{\top}K\).

Our methodological stance follows Gittens and Mahoney [4]: rather than analyzing each sampling distribution in isolation, identify *deterministic structural conditions* on the pair \((K, P)\) that imply a good approximation, and *then* show that concrete randomized constructions satisfy those conditions with high probability. This cleanly separates the linear algebra (what must hold) from the probability (why it holds), and it yields *a posteriori* certificates: given any computed sketch, one can evaluate the structural quantities and bound the error actually achieved.

Three canonical constructions organize the field:

- **Uniform sampling** (\(P\) = rescaled identity columns): \(O(nc)\) time, no preprocessing; provably good only under low coherence.
- **Leverage-score sampling** (\(P\) = identity columns sampled \(\propto \ell_i\)): adapts to nonuniformity; requires approximating the scores themselves, e.g. via SRHT-based fast estimators in \(\tilde{O}(n^2 k)\) or input-sparsity time [5].
- **Oblivious projection** (Gaussian/SRHT/CountSketch): no score estimation; sketch application dominates cost.

A crucial subtlety: the plain Nyström approximant uses \(W^{\dagger}\), but the analyses of [2] and [4] often replace it by \(W_k^{\dagger}\), the pseudoinverse of the best rank-\(k\) approximation of \(W\), because truncating the small eigenvalues of \(W\) stabilizes the inversion when the landmark submatrix is ill-conditioned — a routine but theoretically important *regularization* of the extension.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 From additive to relative error: the Drineas–Mahoney bound and its limits

The first rigorous guarantee for Nyström approximation of Gram matrices is due to Drineas and Mahoney [2]. Sampling \(c = \Omega(k \epsilon^{-4} \log(1/\delta))\) columns with probabilities proportional to the squared diagonal entries \(K_{ii}^2 / \sum_j K_{jj}^2\), they prove:

> **Theorem (Drineas–Mahoney [2]):** With probability at least \(1 - \delta\),
> \[
> \|K - C W_k^{\dagger} C^{\top}\|_{\xi} \;\le\; \|K - K_k\|_{\xi} \;+\; \epsilon \sum_{i=1}^n K_{ii}^2,
> \]
> for \(\xi \in \{2, F\}\) (spectral and Frobenius norms).

The bound is *additive*: the excess error scales with \(\sum_i K_{ii}^2\), a data-dependent constant independent of the target rank. When the spectrum decays slowly — \(\|K - K_k\| \approx \|K\|\) — the guarantee is nearly vacuous, yet empirically the method works well. This gap between weak worst-case theory and strong practice motivated the entire structural program of [4]: the right question is not "how large can the error be for adversarial \(K\)?" but "which *properties of K* control the error?"

### 4.2 Leverage scores and the structural theory of Gittens–Mahoney

Gittens and Mahoney [4] prove that the Nyström error is governed by two deterministic quantities: how well \(P^{\top}\) preserves the top eigenspace, and how much *energy* of \(K\) leaks outside the sketched columns. Their master bound states, informally, that if \(P^{\top} V_k\) is well-conditioned and the residual \(K - K_k\) is small in the appropriate norm, then \(\|K - \tilde{K}\|_2 \le \|K - K_k\|_2 (1 + \epsilon)\).

Consequences for uniform sampling are sharp. Let \(\mu\) be the coherence of \(V_k\). Then:

> **Theorem (Gittens [4], uniform sampling):** If \(c = \Omega(\mu k \log k)\) columns are sampled uniformly (with or without replacement), then with high probability
> \[
> \|K - C W^{\dagger} C^{\top}\|_2 \;\le\; \|K - K_k\|_2 \left(1 + \frac{2n}{c}\right).
> \]

The factor \(2n/c\) exposes the *coherence bottleneck*: when \(\mu\) is large, uniform sampling needs \(c\) proportional to \(\mu k\), which can approach \(n\). Leverage-score sampling removes \(\mu\) from the bound entirely — at the cost of computing the scores. This is the precise sense in which *nonuniformity of leverage scores is the empirical signature of problems where uniform sampling fails and importance sampling wins*, confirmed across genetics, astronomy, and term–document data [4].

### 4.3 Sketching, subspace embeddings, and the Woodruff program

Oblivious sketches sidestep score estimation. The modern analysis, systematized in the sketching literature [5, 10], shows that any *subspace embedding* for \(\mathrm{range}(V_k)\) yields a Nyström approximant with

\[
\|K - \tilde{K}\|_F \;\le\; (1 + \epsilon)\,\|K - K_k\|_F,
\]

a true *relative-error* guarantee in Frobenius norm. Spectral-norm relative error is strictly harder — it requires controlling \(\|V_k^{\top} P P^{\top} V_k - I\|_2\) and the cross terms between the top and tail eigenspaces — and is where fast spectral decay enters as an assumption rather than a conclusion.

The practical trade-off is captured in Table 1: Gaussian sketches need the fewest columns but cost \(O(n^2 c)\) to apply; CountSketch applies in input-sparsity time \(O(n^2)\) but needs \(O(k^2)\) extra columns; SRHT splits the difference via the fast Walsh–Hadamard transform. A subtle point often missed: these sketch sizes guarantee approximation of the *best rank-\(k\)* error, but the constants and the \(\epsilon\)-dependence differ in the spectral norm, where the best known bounds require oversampling by factors depending on the *stable rank* \(\|K\|_F^2 / \|K\|_2^2\) rather than the exact rank.

```haskell
-- Sketch-then-Nyström in a functional style: the sketch P is a
-- random linear operator; approximation quality = how well P^T
-- embeds the top-k eigenspace (subspace embedding property).
nystrom :: Sketch P -> Matrix -> Matrix
nystrom p k =
  let c = k <#> p          -- sketched columns  C = K P
      w = tr p <#> c       -- inner block       W = P^T K P
  in c <#> pinv w <#> tr c -- C W^+ C^T
```

### 4.4 Ridge leverage scores and statistically optimal kernel ridge regression

For *regularized* objectives, plain leverage scores are the wrong distribution. The *ridge leverage score* of point \(i\) at regularization \(\lambda\) is

\[
\ell_i(\lambda) \;=\; \bigl[K (K + \lambda n I)^{-1}\bigr]_{ii},
\]

with \(\sum_i \ell_i(\lambda) = d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda) = \mathrm{tr}\bigl(K(K + \lambda n I)^{-1}\bigr)\), the *effective dimension*. Sampling \(c = \tilde{O}(d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)/\epsilon)\) columns proportionally to \(\ell_i(\lambda)\) yields a Nyström preconditioner \(\tilde{K}\) such that KRR with \(\tilde{K}\) attains the *same statistical risk* as exact KRR, up to \((1+\epsilon)\) factors — this is the "less is more" phenomenon of Rudi, Camoriano, and Rosasco: Nyström landmarks act as *computational regularization*, and the number of landmarks needed scales with the statistical complexity \(d_{\mathrm{eff}}\), not with \(n\).

Exact ridge leverage scores cost \(O(n^3)\), but they can be *approximated* well enough by recursive sampling schemes [7] or estimated incrementally in a streaming setting with a fixed space budget depending only on the effective dimension [8]. This closes the loop: the theoretically optimal sampling distribution is also, up to constants, efficiently computable.

```rust
// CountSketch: one signed nonzero per column; applies to K in O(nnz) time.
// Trade-off: needs O(k/eps + k^2) columns for a (1+eps) Frobenius guarantee.
fn count_sketch(n: usize, c: usize, rng: &mut impl Rng) -> Vec<(usize, f64)> {
    (0..n).map(|_| (rng.gen_range(0..c), if rng.gen_bool(0.5) { 1.0 } else { -1.0 })).collect()
}
```

### 4.5 Nyström versus random features: two geometries of approximation

Random Fourier features (Rahimi–Recht) approximate the *kernel function* by an explicit finite-dimensional feature map \(z(x) \in \mathbb{R}^D\) with \(k(x,x') \approx z(x)^{\top} z(x')\); Nyström approximates the *kernel matrix* by a data-dependent low-rank factorization. The comparison is instructive:

- **Data dependence:** Nyström adapts to the empirical spectrum (good when eigenvalues decay fast); random features are data-independent (good when they are not, or when streaming).
- **Approximation target:** Nyström controls \(\|K - \tilde{K}\|\); random features control \(\sup_{x,x'} |k(x,x') - z(x)^{\top} z(x')|\) uniformly, a stronger pointwise guarantee that costs \(D = \Omega(d_{\mathrm{eff}} \log)\) features.
- **SPSD structure:** Nyström preserves positive semidefiniteness exactly; random-feature Gram matrices are SPSD by construction but approximate a *different* kernel.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We now state the sharpest available guarantees and sketch their proofs, following [4] and the relative-error analysis underlying [10].

**Spectral bound under leverage-score sampling.** Let \(p_i = \ell_i / k\) with \(\ell_i\) the rank-\(k\) leverage scores of \(K\), and sample \(c = O(k \log(k/\delta)/\epsilon^2)\) columns. Then with probability \(\ge 1 - \delta\),

\[
\|K - C W^{\dagger} C^{\top}\|_2 \;\le\; \|K - K_k\|_2 + \epsilon \|K - K_k\|_2 \cdot \kappa,
\]

where \(\kappa\) depends on the conditioning of \(P^{\top} V_k\). The proof has three steps:

1. **Embedding:** By a matrix Chernoff bound, leverage-score sampling with \(c = \Omega(k \log k)\) gives \(\|V_k^{\top} P P^{\top} V_k - I_k\|_2 \le 1/2\); i.e., \(P^{\top}\) is a subspace embedding for \(\mathrm{range}(V_k)\).
2. **Structural decomposition:** Write \(K - \tilde{K} = (I - \Pi_C) K (I - \Pi_C)\) where \(\Pi_C\) is the projection onto \(\mathrm{range}(C)\); the embedding implies \(\mathrm{range}(C)\) nearly contains \(\mathrm{range}(V_k)\).
3. **Tail control:** The residual is bounded by \(\|K - K_k\|_2\) plus cross terms \(\|V_k^{\top} K V_\perp\|\), which vanish for the exact eigendecomposition and are controlled by the embedding quality otherwise.

**Empirical scaling.** On MNIST (\(n = 3.3 \times 10^6\)), the large-scale Nyström implementation of [3] computes a spectral embedding in under an hour on a 4 GB machine — versus the impossibility of forming \(K\) at all. Across the datasets studied in [4], the observed error tracks \(\|K - K_k\|_2\) closely *whenever the spectrum decays fast*, and leverage-score sampling uniformly dominates uniform sampling precisely on the datasets with nonuniform scores, validating the structural theory quantitatively.

```python
import numpy as np

def nystrom_approx(K, c, leverage=None, reg=1e-10):
    """Nyström approximation with optional leverage-score sampling."""
    n = K.shape[0]
    if leverage is None:
        idx = np.random.choice(n, c, replace=False)          # uniform
    else:
        p = leverage / leverage.sum()
        idx = np.random.choice(n, c, replace=False, p=p)    # importance
    C = K[:, idx]
    W = K[np.ix_(idx, idx)]
    # Stabilized inversion: truncate tiny eigenvalues of W
    w, V = np.linalg.eigh(W)
    keep = w > reg * w.max()
    Winv = (V[:, keep] / w[keep]) @ V[:, keep].T
    return (C @ Winv @ C.T, idx)

def rank_k_leverage_scores(K, k):
    """Exact rank-k leverage scores (for analysis; use SRHT sketches in practice)."""
    w, V = np.linalg.eigh(K)
    Uk = V[:, -k:]
    return np.sum(Uk**2, axis=1)
```

**Relative-error kernel \(k\)-means.** As a downstream application, the Nyström approximation yields provable guarantees for kernel \(k\)-means: with the sketch sizes in Table 1, the clustering cost on \(\tilde{K}\) is within \((1+\epsilon)\) of optimal, and the analysis cleanly separates the *sketch size* (statistics) from the *application time* (computation) [10].

## 6. Limitations

1. **The \(O(n^2)\) score-estimation bottleneck.** Leverage-score sampling is only as cheap as the score estimates. Fast estimators via SRHT run in \(\tilde{O}(n^2 k)\) [5] — better than \(O(n^3)\), but still quadratic in \(n\), which dominates the \(O(nc^2)\) Nyström assembly for very large \(n\). Truly input-sparsity-time score estimation remains open for general SPSD matrices.
2. **Flat spectra defeat every column method.** When \(\sigma_i\) decays slowly (e.g., linear kernels on isotropic data), \(\|K - K_k\|\) is large for any feasible \(k\), and no column-sampling scheme — uniform, leverage, or adaptive — can do better than the information-theoretic limit. Here random features or explicit dimensionality reduction are preferable.
3. **High coherence without structure.** If \(\mu \approx n/k\), uniform sampling needs \(c \approx n\); leverage sampling needs accurate scores, which are hardest to estimate exactly when they are most nonuniform. Adversarial matrices (e.g., a single dominant diagonal entry) exhibit this pathology.
4. **The pseudoinverse instability.** Inverting \(W\) amplifies noise in its small eigenvalues; the \(W_k^{\dagger}\) truncation fixes the theory but introduces a tuning parameter, and in finite precision the choice of truncation threshold interacts with the sketch size.
5. **Streaming and distributed gaps.** Single-pass Nyström with optimal space (depending only on \(d_{\mathrm{eff}}\)) exists [8], but *mergeable* sketches for distributed Nyström with spectral guarantees, and lower bounds matching the known upper bounds, are incomplete.
6. **Beyond SPSD.** The Nyström construction is intrinsically tied to positive semidefiniteness. Extending spectral guarantees to indefinite kernels (e.g., in Kreĭn-space methods) or to nonsymmetric attention matrices requires different factorizations (CUR, generalized Nyström) whose theory is less mature.

## 7. Conclusion

The Nyström method's journey — from a quadrature heuristic for integral equations, through Williams and Seeger's kernel-machine speed-up [1], Drineas and Mahoney's first additive bounds [2], the large-scale engineering of Kumar, Mohri, and Talwalkar [3], to the structural theory of Gittens and Mahoney [4] and the sketching synthesis surveyed here — mirrors the maturation of randomized numerical linear algebra itself. The central lesson is that *approximation quality is a property of the interaction between the sketch and the spectrum*: leverage scores quantify where the range lives, subspace embeddings certify that the sketch preserves it, and spectral decay determines how much rank is needed. Ridge leverage scores extend this picture to regularized learning, where Nyström landmarks become a form of computational regularization attuned to the statistical effective dimension.

Open frontiers include input-sparsity-time spectral-norm guarantees, single-pass optimal-space streaming Nyström, and a unified theory covering indefinite and nonsymmetric generalizations. What is no longer open is the basic question: with the right (possibly data-dependent) sketch, a kernel matrix of dimension \(n\) can be faithfully represented in \(O(nc)\) space with \(c\) scaling in the *intrinsic* complexity of the learning problem — not in \(n\).
