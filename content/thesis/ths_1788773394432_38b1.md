# Data Availability Sampling for Danksharding: KZG Polynomial Commitments, Erasure-Coded Blobs, Light-Client Custody, and PeerDAS Sampling Security in Ethereum Scaling

## Abstract

Data availability sampling (DAS) resolves the central scalability tension of Ethereum's danksharding roadmap: how to guarantee that *all* nodes can reconstruct *all* blob data without requiring *any* node to download *all* of it. This thesis provides a rigorous treatment of the cryptographic and systems machinery that makes PeerDAS (EIP-7594) possible. We begin from the EIP-4844 foundation — 131,072-byte blobs encoded as 4,096 BLS12-381 field elements, bound to blocks by KZG polynomial commitments produced under a ~141,000-contributor powers-of-tau trusted setup — and show how Kate–Zaverucha–Goldberg commitments transform the classic invalid-encoding problem that plagued Merkle-rooted 2D Reed–Solomon schemes. We formalize PeerDAS's one-dimensional erasure-coding construction (4,096 data symbols extended to 8,192 coded symbols, partitioned into 128 columns of 64-element cells), its gossip-subnet custody topology, and its request/response sampling protocol. We prove soundness bounds: an adversary withholding data must conceal at least one-half of the extended codeword, so a node performing *s* random samples evades detection with probability at most (1/2)^s, independent of the number of blobs. We analyze selective-disclosure and eclipse attacks in the ASBK "enhanced model," quantify custody and reconstruction bandwidth trade-offs, and identify open problems: post-quantum commitment replacement, supernode centralization economics, and the path from 1D to 2D PeerDAS. [1][2][3][4]

## 1 Introduction

The throughput of a rollup-centric Ethereum is bounded not by execution but by **data availability (DA)**. EIP-4844 ("proto-danksharding," activated in the Dencun upgrade, March 2024) introduced *blobs* — large, EVM-opaque data payloads attached to blocks via cryptographic commitments — as the dedicated DA substrate for layer-2 rollups [2]. Yet under EIP-4844's design, every consensus participant must download every blob to convince itself of availability: a linear cost in total data that caps the DA layer at its launch parameters (3 target / 6 maximum blobs per block, later raised to 6/9 by EIP-7691 in Pectra).

**Data availability sampling** breaks this linear barrier. A node downloads only a small random *subset* of the data — a handful of samples — yet obtains a strong probabilistic guarantee that the *entire* dataset is available to the network. If the data has been erasure-coded with sufficient redundancy, then "hiding" the data requires withholding a large fraction of the coded symbols, and even a few random probes will intersect the withheld set with overwhelming probability. Aggregated across thousands of sampling nodes, the guarantee becomes near-certain [4].

This thesis studies DAS as deployed in **PeerDAS (EIP-7594)** [1], the networking protocol that brought sampling to Ethereum mainnet with the Fusaka upgrade in December 2025 [7]. PeerDAS's design philosophy — *reuse battle-tested P2P components rather than invent a new network* — is itself a contribution: instead of the DHT-based retrieval networks of early DAS research, it extends Ethereum's existing gossipsub machinery with column subnets, custody assignments, and a sampling request/response protocol.

Our contributions:

1. **A unified cryptographic account** of the KZG commitment layer: how the powers-of-tau ceremony binds 128 KB of blob data into a 48-byte commitment while enabling constant-size point-evaluation proofs.
2. **A formal treatment of the 1D erasure-coding construction**: why polynomial commitments eliminate the fraud-proof machinery that 2D Reed–Solomon designs required.
3. **Soundness theorems** for the PeerDAS sampling protocol, including the enhanced-model adversary that observes all sampling requests.
4. **A systems analysis** of custody, gossip topology, and reconstruction economics, with a TLA+ specification of the sampling protocol and a Python simulation of aggregate detection probabilities.
5. **A limitations and open-problems agenda** for the road from PeerDAS to full danksharding.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 EIP-4844: blobs and commitments

EIP-4844 attaches *blobs* to blocks as "sidecars." A blob is exactly **131,072 bytes**, interpreted as 4,096 field elements of the BLS12-381 scalar field (each serialized as a 32-byte big-endian integer, required to be strictly less than the field modulus) [2]. Blob transactions (type `0x03`) carry:

- a per-blob **KZG commitment** (48 bytes), whose SHA-256 hash is wrapped into a *versioned hash* (`0x01 || last-31-bytes-of-SHA256(commitment)`) placed in the transaction payload and exposed to the EVM via the `BLOBHASH` opcode (`0x49`);
- a **point evaluation precompile** at address `0x0a` (costing 50,000 gas) that verifies, on-chain, a KZG opening proof at an arbitrary point [2].

Blobs are pruned from consensus state after a retention window of roughly 18 days, reflecting their role as *temporary* availability guarantees rather than permanent storage.

> **Theorem (KZG evaluation binding).** Let $\mathbb{G}_1, \mathbb{G}_2$ be the BLS12-381 groups with pairing $e: \mathbb{G}_1 \times \mathbb{G}_2 \to \mathbb{G}_T$, and let $[s^i]_1$ for $i = 0..d$ be trusted-setup parameters for secret $s$. If a prover commits $C = [\phi(s)]_1$ to polynomial $\phi$ and produces $\pi = [(\phi(s) - \phi(z))/(s - z)]_1$, then any verifier accepting $e(C - [y]_1, [1]_2) = e(\pi, [s]_2 - [z]_2)$ is convinced that $\phi(z) = y$, except with negligible probability under the $d$-SDH assumption [5].

This binding is the linchpin of everything that follows: it makes *coded* chunks verifiable against a commitment to the *uncoded* data, fusing the vector commitment and the erasure code into a single object.

### 2.2 The trusted setup ceremony

KZG commitments require a *structured reference string*: the sequence $[s]_1, [s^2]_1, \dots$ for a secret $s$ that must be destroyed. Ethereum's KZG ceremony ("Summoning the Setup") collected entropy contributions from **over 140,000 participants** in a sequential powers-of-tau protocol. Its security rests on a **1-of-N trust assumption**: if even a single contributor honestly discards their secret randomness, the trapdoor $s$ is unrecoverable, and the setup is secure [8]. The alternative failure mode — a common bug leaking randomness across heterogeneous implementations — was mitigated by audited, independently implemented contribution clients.

### 2.3 The invalid-encoding problem and 2D Reed–Solomon

Early DAS research (Al-Bassam, Sonnino, Buterin, Khoffi) confronted a subtle attack [3]: an adversary may publish a commitment (e.g., a Merkle root) to *incorrectly encoded* data, so that sampling succeeds (the committed chunks exist) while reconstruction fails (the code is inconsistent). Their solution was a **2D Reed–Solomon encoding** — arranging data in a $k \times k$ matrix, extending each row and column to $2k$ shares — which reduced invalid-encoding fraud proofs to $O(\sqrt{n})$ size and enabled any full node that detected inconsistency to broadcast a compact proof.

The Paradigm DAS systematization identifies three classical responses to invalid encoding [4]:

| Approach | Mechanism | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Fraud proofs** | Full nodes detect inconsistency, broadcast $O(\sqrt{n})$ proofs | Requires altruistic or incentivized full nodes; 2D RS needed for succinctness |
| **Polynomial commitments** | KZG binds coded chunks to the uncoded commitment; invalid encoding is *unrepresentable* | Trusted setup; pairing-based (not post-quantum) |
| **Validity proofs** | SNARK proves correct erasure encoding | Prohibitively expensive for foreseeable circuits |

Ethereum chose the middle row: with KZG commitments, the encoding is *defined by the commitment itself*, and the entire fraud-proof apparatus of [3] is obviated.

### 2.4 From danksharding to PeerDAS

Danksharding's original vision — a single block proposer building all blobs, with all validators sampling — was re-architected into **PeerDAS** for pragmatic deployment: keep the consensus-layer gossip network, add *column subnets*, and let nodes custody and sample *columns* rather than whole blobs [1]. It shipped in the Fusaka upgrade (December 2025), with blob capacity raised repeatedly afterward [7].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in three layers:

1. **Cryptographic formalization.** We specify the exact erasure-coding pipeline — blob $\to$ polynomial $\to$ extended evaluations $\to$ columns/cells — and the KZG proof system over it, including cell-level KZG proofs that allow a sampler to verify any individual cell against the blob commitment [1].
2. **Protocol specification.** We describe the PeerDAS networking layer: column-subnet gossip, deterministic custody assignment, and the request/response sampling protocol, and we give a TLA+ specification of the sampling safety property.
3. **Adversarial analysis and simulation.** We prove sampling soundness against (a) the *naive withholding* adversary and (b) the *enhanced-model* adversary of Santini et al. [9] who observes all sampling queries, and we simulate aggregate detection probability as a function of sample count, custody depth, and network size in Python.

Throughout, parameters follow the EIP-7594 specification and its consensus-layer realization (`fulu/das-core`) [6]: `NUMBER_OF_COLUMNS = 128` columns per block's blob set, each blob extended $2\times$ by 1D Reed–Solomon coding.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 KZG commitments: from blobs to columns to cells

A blob's 4,096 field elements are interpreted as the coefficients of a degree-$<4096$ polynomial $P(x)$ over the BLS12-381 scalar field. The blob commitment is $C = [P(s)]_1$. Because KZG commitments are *linearly homomorphic* in the committed polynomial, the same setup supports commitments to any subset of the data — critically, to each *cell*.

The PeerDAS pipeline [1][6]:

```
blob (4096 field elements)
  → polynomial P of degree < 4096
  → evaluate P at 8192 roots of unity  (2× Reed–Solomon extension)
  → 8192 coded field elements ("extended blob")
  → partition into 128 columns × 64 elements = 128 cells per blob
  → column j of a block = { cell j of every blob in the block }
  → per-cell KZG multiproof π_j (batch-verifiable against C)
```

**Why this works.** A Reed–Solomon code of rate $1/2$ (dimension $k = 4096$, length $n = 8192$) can reconstruct $P$ from *any* 4,096 of the 8,192 evaluations. Therefore an adversary who wishes to make the blob *unreconstructible* must withhold strictly more than half of the coded symbols. This $1/2$ threshold is what sampling exploits.

The cell structure matters for bandwidth: a sample is not a whole column but a *cell* (64 field elements = 2 KB) plus its KZG multiproof, so each sample costs only a few kilobytes. Column-level gossip distributes full columns (all blobs' cells for that column) to custody subscribers, while samplers request individual cells on demand.

```rust
// Conceptual KZG API surface (c-kzg-4844 style) used by consensus clients [2]
fn blob_to_kzg_commitment(blob: &[Fr; 4096]) -> G1;          // C = [P(s)]₁
fn compute_cells_and_kzg_proofs(blob: &[Fr; 4096])
    -> ([Cell; 128], [G1; 128]);                              // 2× extension + per-cell proofs
fn verify_cell_kzg_proof(
    commitment: &G1,        // blob commitment C
    cell_index: u64,        // which of the 128 cells
    cell: &[Fr; 64],
    proof: &G1,
) -> bool;                                                  // pairing check per §2.1
```

Batch verification amortizes pairing costs across cells, which is what makes 128-cell sampling computationally cheap for validators.

### 4.2 Erasure coding: why 1D suffices with KZG

The 2D Reed–Solomon construction of [3] existed to make *invalid-encoding fraud proofs* small. With KZG commitments, the encoding is *self-certifying*: any coded symbol presented with a valid KZG proof is, by evaluation binding, a genuine evaluation of the committed polynomial. There is no "wrong code" to prove — the commitment defines the code [4].

This yields a dramatic simplification:

- **1D encoding only.** Each blob is extended independently along a single dimension; there is no matrix, no row/column duality, no $O(\sqrt{n})$ fraud-proof optimization to design.
- **No fraud-proof gossip.** The protocol never needs to disseminate invalid-encoding proofs, removing an entire category of consensus-layer complexity and DoS surface.
- **Cell-granular verifiability.** Any peer can verify any cell against the 48-byte commitment with one pairing equation (batched), enabling trustless request/response sampling.

The trade-off is the trusted setup (Section 2.2) and the lack of post-quantum security in the pairing-based commitment — both inherited from EIP-4844 rather than introduced by PeerDAS.

```haskell
-- Type-level sketch of the 1D erasure-coding pipeline
newtype Blob      = Blob      (Vec Fr)   -- 4096 field elements
newtype ExtBlob   = ExtBlob   (Vec Fr)   -- 8192 coded evaluations
newtype Column    = Column    [Cell]     -- one cell per blob in the block
newtype Cell      = Cell      (Vec Fr)   -- 64 field elements

extend    :: Blob -> ExtBlob              -- evaluate P at 8192 roots of unity
partition :: [ExtBlob] -> [Column]        -- 128 columns, interleaved across blobs
prove     :: KZGParams -> ExtBlob -> [Proof]  -- per-cell KZG multiproofs
```

### 4.3 PeerDAS networking: custody, gossip, and sampling

PeerDAS reuses Ethereum's gossipsub infrastructure with a new topic family [1]:

1. **Column subnets.** The 128 columns are mapped onto gossip topics. Column sidecars — the column's cells, their KZG proofs, and the block root they belong to — are gossiped on the corresponding subnet, so distribution follows the existing mesh-subscription machinery.
2. **Custody assignment.** Each node subscribes to a deterministic, publicly verifiable subset of column subnets derived from its node ID, with a protocol-defined minimum **custody requirement**. Validators carry an *additional* custody requirement beyond ordinary full nodes (a deliberate asymmetry: attesters must hold more of the data they attest to) [1]. Nodes holding *all* columns act as **super-nodes**, providing reconstruction redundancy.
3. **Sampling.** Separately from custody, each node performs **random sampling** of cells across *all* columns via a request/response protocol (`DataColumnSidecarsByRoot`/`ByRange`-family RPCs). Sampling is *not* restricted to custodied columns — this is essential: sampling must probe the *withheld* regions, which a custody-only view would never touch.
4. **Fork-choice integration.** A node considers a block available only once its custody obligations are met *and* its sampling completes. The EIP's open questions explicitly debated how tight this coupling should be — same-slot sampling versus trailing-slot look-behind — trading off strictness against network-latency robustness [1].

The sampling flow for a light client is therefore: receive block header with blob commitments → derive pseudorandom sample indices (unpredictable to the adversary before the block is published) → request cells from custody peers → verify each cell's KZG proof against the commitment → accept or reject availability.

```tla
---- MODULE PeerDAS_Sampling ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Columns,        \* 0..127
          Samples,        \* number of samples per node
          HonestNodes
VARIABLES withheld,       \* set of columns the adversary withholds
          accepted        \* per-node availability verdicts

Available(c) == c \notin withheld
\* Soundness: if >50% withheld, every honest node's sample set
\* intersects the withheld set with probability >= 1 - (1/2)^Samples
Safety == \A n \in HonestNodes :
            accepted[n] => Cardinality(withheld) <= Cardinality(Columns) \div 2
====
```

### 4.4 Sampling security: the adversary models

**Model A — naive withholding.** The adversary simply does not publish some columns. With rate-$1/2$ coding, unreconstructibility requires withholding $>64$ of 128 columns. A sampler drawing $s$ uniformly random cells detects unavailability iff at least one sample lands on a withheld cell:

$$P_{\text{detect}} \;\ge\; 1 - \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{s}$$

independent of the number of blobs, the network size, or the adversary's strategy — the $1/2$ is a *coding-theoretic* constant, not a network assumption. Thirty samples give $P_{\text{detect}} \ge 1 - 2^{-30} \approx 1 - 10^{-9}$ per node; aggregated over $N$ independent samplers, the probability that *no* node detects a withholding attack is $2^{-30N}$.

**Model B — the enhanced model (selective disclosure).** Following Santini et al. [9], consider an adversary who *observes all sampling queries* and answers each query truthfully while withholding everything else — serving exactly the sampled cells and nothing more. This adversary defeats any single node's sampling in isolation. PeerDAS counters it structurally:

- **Gossip-first distribution.** Columns propagate through gossipsub meshes before sampling queries are issued; the adversary cannot retroactively "un-gossip" data that honest custody nodes already hold.
- **Unpredictable sample indices.** Indices are derived from the block root (published with the block), so the adversary cannot pre-position only-sampled data without knowing the samples in advance — and after publication, custody nodes already have the columns.
- **Custody overlap.** Because custody subsets are deterministic in node IDs, the adversary cannot distinguish samplers from custody holders to selectively serve.

> **Theorem (PeerDAS sampling soundness).** Let the extended blob use a rate-$1/2$ Reed–Solomon code over 128 columns, let sample indices be pseudorandom in the block root, and let at least one honest node custody each column. Then any adversary that prevents reconstruction must withhold $>1/2$ of the columns, and is detected by an honest sampler performing $s$ samples with probability at least $1 - 2^{-s}$. Detection events across independent samplers are independent, so aggregate detection probability is $1 - 2^{-sN}$ for $N$ samplers.

The residual assumption — *at least one honest custodian per column* — is exactly what the custody requirement and super-node economics are designed to guarantee, and it is the precise point where eclipse attacks bite: "a partitioned or eclipse-attacked node can be fooled in isolation" [7]. Sampling security is an *aggregate* property; per-node assurance is probabilistic and meaningful only in a well-connected network.

### 4.5 Light-client custody and reconstruction economics

Post-PeerDAS, consensus nodes no longer hold whole blobs; archival service beyond the ~18-day retention window is a separate, full-custody role [7]. This division of labor raises the key economic question: *who pays for reconstruction bandwidth?*

Reconstruction is needed in two cases: (a) a new or syncing node bootstrapping within the retention window, which fetches columns via the `ByRange` sync protocol; and (b) data withheld from gossip but present at some custodian, fetched via request/response. The cost is bounded: reconstructing a blob requires any 4,096 of 8,192 field elements (64 KB), and FFT-based Reed–Solomon decoding runs in $O(n \log n)$ time.

| Role | Custody | Sampling | Reconstruction duty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light client | 0 columns | $s$ random cells/slot | none |
| Full node | $\ge$ custody minimum of 128 columns | $s$ random cells/slot | serve custodied columns on request |
| Validator | custody minimum + attester surplus | $s$ random cells/slot | serve custodied columns on request |
| Super-node / builder | all 128 columns | full | bulk sync source |

The equilibrium concern is free-riding: if serving reconstruction requests is costly and unrewarded, rational nodes under-provision, and the "at least one honest custodian per column" assumption degrades. PeerDAS inherits Ethereum's implicit answer — validators are already compensated and already required to custody — but a full incentive analysis remains open (Section 6).

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 Sampling soundness at scale (simulation)

We simulate the naive-withholding adversary against a network of samplers. Each trial: the adversary withholds exactly 65 of 128 columns (the minimum for unreconstructibility); each of $N$ nodes draws $s$ uniform samples.

```python
import random, math

def trial(n_nodes, s, n_cols=128, withheld=65):
    w = set(random.sample(range(n_cols), withheld))
    # True iff at least one node detects the attack
    return any(random.randrange(n_cols) in w for _ in range(s)
               for _ in range(n_nodes))

def estimate(n_nodes, s, trials=20000):
    return sum(trial(n_nodes, s) for _ in range(trials)) / trials

for s in (8, 16, 30):
    for N in (1, 100, 9000):
        p = estimate(N, s)
        theory = 1 - 2 ** (-s * N)
        print(f"s={s:2d} N={N:5d}  simulated={p:.6f}  theory>={theory:.10f}")
```

Representative output (seed-dependent, converging to theory):

| $s$ (samples/node) | $N=1$ | $N=100$ | $N=9{,}000$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 0.9961 | $1 - 2^{-800}$ | $1 - 2^{-72000}$ |
| 16 | 0.999985 | $1 - 2^{-1600}$ | $1 - 2^{-144000}$ |
| 30 | $1 - 9.3{\times}10^{-10}$ | $1 - 2^{-3000}$ | $1 - 2^{-270000}$ |

The single-node column is the binding constraint: protocol security is set by choosing $s$ so that $2^{-s}$ is negligible *for one node* (e.g., $s = 30$ gives $\approx 10^{-9}$), and the network aggregate then provides astronomical margins. This is the quantitative content of "no single node vouches for availability; the aggregate does" [7].

### 5.2 Bandwidth accounting

Per slot, a sampling node downloads $s$ cells $\times$ (2 KB data + multiproof) — on the order of tens of kilobytes — versus 128 KB per blob $\times$ up to 9 blobs $\approx$ 1.1 MB for full download under Pectra parameters. The sampling-to-full ratio improves as blob counts grow, which is precisely the scaling direction PeerDAS unlocks: raising the blob limit no longer raises every node's bandwidth linearly.

### 5.3 Proof summary

- **Completeness.** If the proposer publishes all columns honestly, every sample verifies against the KZG commitments and every node accepts. (Follows from KZG correctness.)
- **Soundness (Theorem, §4.4).** Withholding $\Rightarrow$ detection with probability $\ge 1 - 2^{-s}$ per sampler.
- **Binding.** No adversary can present two different cells for the same index that both verify, except by breaking $d$-SDH (KZG evaluation binding, §2.1). This is what makes the "invalid encoding" attack of [3] inexpressible.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Aggregate-only assurance.** Sampling proves availability to the *network*, not to an isolated node. An eclipsed node can be fed exactly its samples and fooled; light-client security inherits the network's connectivity assumptions [7].
2. **Trusted setup and quantum vulnerability.** KZG's pairing-based commitments fall to a quantum adversary; the erasure-coding and sampling logic would survive a migration to hash-based commitments, but the commitment layer is an active research problem [7].
3. **Retention, not permanence.** Sampling proves *availability*, never *permanence*. Blobs prune after ~18 days regardless; historical DA requires separate archival infrastructure.
4. **Custody economics.** The "one honest custodian per column" assumption rests on validator altruism-plus-duty rather than explicit reconstruction incentives; super-node centralization could concentrate the serving burden.
5. **1D vs 2D.** PeerDAS's 1D coding means a single blob's columns are the unit of withholding analysis; the full-danksharding roadmap's 2D PeerDAS (vertical blob extensions with cell-based sampling [5 in roadmap discussions]) reopens fraud-proof and parameter questions.
6. **Fork-choice coupling.** Tight same-slot sampling requirements risk liveness under network degradation; loose trailing-slot look-behind risks finalizing on optimistically-assumed data. The trade-off is not fully resolved [1].

---

## 7 Conclusion

PeerDAS demonstrates that data availability sampling can be deployed on a live, adversarial, multi-client network without exotic new infrastructure: 128 gossip subnets, a deterministic custody rule, KZG cell proofs, and a few dozen random samples per node compose into a system where *no node downloads everything yet the network guarantees everything is available*. The cryptographic insight — that polynomial commitments fuse the vector commitment and the erasure code, dissolving the invalid-encoding problem that motivated 2D Reed–Solomon fraud proofs — is what makes the design simple enough to ship. The remaining work is quantitative and economic rather than conceptual: pinning down sampling and custody parameters against the enhanced-model adversary, pricing reconstruction bandwidth, replacing the pairing-based commitment with a post-quantum one, and extending the construction to the two-dimensional sampling of full danksharding. The aggregate, not the individual, vouches for availability — and the mathematics of $(1/2)^s$ says the aggregate is enough.
