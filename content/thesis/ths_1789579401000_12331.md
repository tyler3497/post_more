# Probabilistic Population Codes: Bayesian Inference, Information-Limiting Correlations, and the Geometry of Neural Representation

## Abstract

Sensory cortex encodes continuous variables — orientation, direction, spatial position — through the concerted activity of large neural populations whose trial-to-trial responses are highly variable and correlated. This thesis develops the *probabilistic population code* framework, in which Poisson-like variability makes the log-likelihood of the stimulus a linear function of population activity, so that Bayesian inference reduces to summation. We derive the Fisher information and the Cramér–Rao bound as the canonical measures of encoding fidelity, introduce linear Fisher information for correlated populations, and prove the central geometric result: only *differential* (information-limiting) correlations — noise fluctuations aligned with the gradient of the tuning curves — cap the information available in arbitrarily large populations, while all other correlation structures are averaged away. We connect this to optimal linear decoding, to the manifold geometry of population responses, and to recent large-scale recordings in mouse visual cortex showing sublinear scaling and saturation far below the neuron count. The synthesis explains why correlated noise is not uniformly harmful, how downstream circuits can read out posteriors with linear operations, and where the framework breaks down.

## 1 Introduction

Estimating a continuous stimulus from noisy neural activity is the canonical problem of sensory neuroscience. A bar of light at orientation s evokes spikes across hundreds of neurons in primary visual cortex, each neuron responding most vigorously near its own preferred orientation and falling off smoothly away from it. No single neuron is a reliable estimator — spike counts are noisy, with variance roughly equal to the mean — yet the animal's perceptual judgments are remarkably precise. Two traditions have shaped the quantitative theory. The *encoding* tradition asks how much information a population carries, formalized through Fisher information and the Cramér–Rao bound on estimator variance [2]. The *decoding* tradition asks how that information can be extracted, comparing biologically plausible readouts — the population vector, maximum-likelihood estimation, Bayesian decoding — against the optimal benchmark [2]. Reality is messier. Cortical neurons are not independent; their trial-to-trial fluctuations are correlated, with pairwise noise correlations typically in the range of a few percent. For years the field debated whether these correlations help or hurt coding, with plausible arguments on both sides [4]. The modern resolution, due to Moreno-Bote and colleagues, is geometric and surprisingly sharp: it is not the magnitude of correlations that matters but their *direction* relative to the signal. Only correlations that mimic stimulus-driven changes — fluctuations along the gradient of the tuning curves — are information-limiting, and they cap the extractable information at a finite value no matter how many neurons are recorded [5][7].

This thesis presents the full arc. We begin with tuning curves and Fisher information, develop the probabilistic population code as the Bayesian reading of neural variability [3][9], derive the decomposition theorem for information-limiting correlations [5], characterize its generative origins [6], and confront the theory with large-scale population recordings [8].

## 2 Background

### Tuning curves and neural noise

Let s denote a one-dimensional stimulus, such as the orientation of a grating, and let r_i be the spike count of neuron i in a fixed counting window. The *tuning curve* f_i(s) is the mean response, f_i(s) = E[r_i | s]. In visual cortex, tuning curves are smooth and bell-shaped, each neuron peaking at its preferred stimulus and overlapping substantially with its neighbors, so that the population tiles the stimulus space with a dense fan of curves (see the tuning-curve diagram). The trial-to-trial variability around the mean is large: for cortical neurons the variance of the spike count is typically comparable to the mean, the signature of approximately Poisson statistics [1][9]. The full second-order description is the noise covariance matrix Σ(s), with entries Σ_ij(s) = Cov(r_i, r_j | s). ### Fisher information and the Cramér–Rao bound

For a parametric family of response distributions p(r | s), the Fisher information

J(s) = E[ (∂ log p(r | s) / ∂s)^2 | s ]

measures how sensitively the likelihood responds to small changes in the stimulus. Its operational meaning comes from the Cramér–Rao bound: any unbiased estimator ŝ(r) of the stimulus satisfies Var(ŝ) ≥ 1 / J(s), so Fisher information sets the ceiling on estimation precision [2]. For a population of independent Poisson neurons, the log-likelihood separates across neurons and the Fisher information takes the elegant closed form

J(s) = Σ_i f_i'(s)^2 / f_i(s),

a sum over neurons of squared tuning-curve slope divided by mean rate. Three lessons follow immediately. First, information is concentrated where tuning curves are *steep*, not where they peak — a neuron tells you most about stimuli on the flanks of its tuning curve. Second, information adds linearly across independent neurons, so precision grows with the square root of population size. Third, the bound is achievable: maximum-likelihood decoding attains it asymptotically [2][9].

### Decoding: from population vectors to Bayesian estimators

A decoder is a function mapping the population response r to an estimate of s. The *population vector* — each neuron votes for its preferred stimulus with weight proportional to its activity — is the simplest biologically plausible scheme and performs well when tuning is broad, though it falls short of maximum likelihood for narrow tuning [2]. The key insight of the Bayesian program is that the likelihood is not a nuisance to be worked around but the central object the brain must represent: to act optimally under uncertainty, a neural circuit must encode not just a point estimate of the stimulus but a full posterior distribution over it [3][9].

### Correlated populations and linear Fisher information

When noise is correlated, the independent-Poisson formula no longer applies. Under a local Gaussian approximation to the response distribution with mean f(s) and covariance Σ(s), the relevant quantity becomes the *linear Fisher information*,

J_lin(s) = f'(s)^T Σ(s)^{-1} f'(s),

the Fisher information accessible to the best *linear* estimator. Its inverse is exactly the variance of the optimal linear unbiased estimator, whose weights are proportional to Σ^{-1} f'.

## 3 Methodology

Our theoretical development rests on a standard generative model of population responses, analyzed exactly where possible and simulated where not.

**Encoding model.** We consider N neurons with preferred stimuli tiling a circular domain, Gaussian tuning curves f_i(s) = r_max exp(−(s − s_i)^2 / (2σ^2)) + r_0, and spike counts drawn from a correlated Gaussian with mean f(s) and covariance Σ(s). We study three covariance structures: (a) independent noise, Σ = diag(f); (b) limited-range correlations decaying with tuning similarity; and (c) differential correlations, Σ = Σ_0 + ε f'(s) f'(s)^T, containing an explicit component along the tuning-curve gradient [5].

**Information and decoding analysis.** For each structure we compute the linear Fisher information J_lin(s) = f'^T Σ^{-1} f' in closed form where possible (via the Sherman–Morrison identity for rank-one updates) and numerically otherwise. We construct the optimal linear estimator with weights w = Σ^{-1} f' / (f'^T Σ^{-1} f') and verify empirically that its mean squared error attains 1 / J_lin. **Simulation protocol.** The following Python implementation generates tuning curves, synthesizes correlated population responses, and traces linear Fisher information as a function of population size for the three correlation structures. It is the computational backbone of the scaling results reported in Section 5.

```python
import numpy as np

def tuning_curves(s, prefs, r_max=25.0, sigma=15.0, r0=2.0):
    d = np.abs(s - prefs)
    d = np.minimum(d, 360.0 - d)          # circular distance
    return r_max * np.exp(-d**2 / (2 * sigma**2)) + r0

def tuning_derivatives(s, prefs, r_max=25.0, sigma=15.0):
    d = s - prefs
    return tuning_curves(s, prefs, r_max, sigma) * (-d / sigma**2)

def linear_fisher_info(fp, Sigma):
    w = np.linalg.solve(Sigma, fp)
    return float(fp @ w)

def simulate(N, s=0.0, rho=0.1, eps=0.02, trials=2000):
    prefs = np.linspace(0, 360, N, endpoint=False)
    f = tuning_curves(s, prefs)
    fp = tuning_derivatives(s, prefs)
    D = np.diag(f)
    results = {}
    # (a) independent noise
    results['independent'] = linear_fisher_info(fp, D)
    # (b) limited-range correlations
    C = rho * np.exp(-(np.subtract.outer(prefs, prefs) ** 2) / (2 * 30.0**2))
    np.fill_diagonal(C, 1.0)
    S = np.sqrt(np.outer(f, f)) * C
    results['limited_range'] = linear_fisher_info(fp, S)
    # (c) differential correlations
    Sd = S + eps * np.outer(fp, fp)
    results['differential'] = linear_fisher_info(fp, Sd)
    # optimal linear decoder attains the bound
    w = np.linalg.solve(Sd, fp)
    w = w / (fp @ w)
    errs = []
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(Sd)
    for _ in range(trials):
        r = f + L @ np.random.randn(N)
        errs.append(float(w @ r) - s)
    errs = np.unwrap(np.array(errs), discont=180.0)
    results['decoder_mse'] = float(np.mean(errs**2))
    return results

for N in [64, 256, 1024]:
    out = simulate(N)
    print(N, {k: round(v, 3) for k, v in out.items()})
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Probabilistic population codes

The most radical reading of neural variability turns the noise from a bug into the representational substrate itself. Ma, Beck, Latham and Pouget observed that if the population response distribution belongs to the exponential family with *linear* sufficient statistics in a stimulus-dependent natural parameter — the Poisson-like case, p(r | s) ∝ exp(h(s) · r) g(r) / Z(s) — then the log-likelihood is linear in the population activity r [3]. Bayes' rule then gives

log p(s | r) = h(s) · r − log Z(s) + log p(s) + const,

so the posterior log-probability is obtained by *linear* projection of the population response onto stimulus-dependent kernels, plus the log prior. A downstream neuron that sums its inputs with appropriate synaptic weights is literally computing a log-posterior. Cue integration becomes addition: two independent populations encoding the same stimulus contribute log-likelihoods that simply sum, and the summed activity is Bayes-optimal multisensory integration implemented as synaptic summation [3][9].

Uncertainty is coded by gain. Multiplying every neuron's response by a common factor g — the physiological signature of contrast or attention — sharpens or broadens the likelihood without changing its peak, so the total population activity encodes the *precision* of the estimate. > **Key insight:** Poisson-like variability is not an obstacle to Bayesian computation; it is the mathematical property that *enables* it. Linearity of the log-likelihood in the neural activity is what makes optimal inference implementable by a single feedforward layer.

### 4.2 Information-limiting correlations

Correlations were long thought to be either mildly harmful (redundancy) or mildly helpful (synergy), depending on their sign and structure [4]. The differential-correlations theorem of Moreno-Bote, Beck, Kanitscheider, Pitkow, Latham and Pouget replaces this vagueness with a sharp dichotomy [5]. Decompose the noise covariance as

Σ = Σ_0 + ε f'(s) f'(s)^T,

where the second term is a rank-one component aligned with the tuning-curve gradient — *differential correlations* — and Σ_0 is everything else. Applying the Sherman–Morrison identity to Σ^{-1} gives the linear Fisher information in closed form:

1 / J_lin = 1 / J_0 + ε,

where J_0 = f'^T Σ_0^{-1} f' is the information allowed by the non-limiting component. As the population grows, J_0 grows without bound (it is the ordinary extensive term), while ε stays fixed — so J_lin saturates to the finite ceiling 1/ε. All remaining correlation structure — no matter how strong — is orthogonal to the signal direction in the relevant metric and is averaged away as N grows [5][7].

Two refinements matter. First, *limited-range* correlations (neurons with similar tuning co-fluctuate) were once thought to be the generic information limiter; in homogeneous populations they do contain a differential component, but in heterogeneous populations — the realistic case, where tuning amplitude and width vary — information can keep growing despite limited-range correlations, and only genuine differential correlations impose the ceiling [7]. Where do such correlations come from? Kanitscheider, Coen-Cagli and Pouget showed that they arise *generatively* whenever a sensory stage with limited information feeds a cortical population: optimal computation on an information-limited input imprints differential structure on the output noise, producing correlation patterns that match V1 physiology and behavioral discrimination thresholds without any additional internal noise source [6]. Suboptimal wiring or internal fluctuations can produce the same signature, but with qualitatively different correlation structures — a distinction that yields testable predictions [6].

### 4.3 Optimal decoding and the geometry of readouts

The optimal linear estimator for the correlated Gaussian model has weights w ∝ Σ^{-1} f'(s), the whitened tuning-curve gradient. Geometrically, the decoder projects the response onto the signal direction after *discounting* the noise: directions in which the noise ellipsoid is elongated are down-weighted, exactly as in a Mahalanobis metric. When differential correlations are present, Σ^{-1} f' remains well-defined but the achievable precision 1/J_lin inherits the ceiling 1/ε — the decoder is optimal, and the limit is in the code, not the readout [5][7].

Nonlinear decoders can extract additional information when the code is non-Gaussian — the *nonlinear* Fisher information generalizes the linear construction to arbitrary sufficient statistics — but the information-limiting component constrains nonlinear decoders too: the same rank-one argument applies to the covariance of the sufficient statistics [7]. An important empirical handle is the *choice correlation*: the correlation between a single neuron's trial-to-trial fluctuation and the animal's behavioral report. Under optimal decoding of a differentially correlated population, choice correlations take a specific predicted form; measurements in vestibular cortex during heading discrimination match the information-limiting prediction, indicating that the brain's actual readout is near-optimal and that the residual behavioral variability is inherited from these correlations rather than from a sloppy decoder [7].

### 4.4 The manifold geometry of population responses

There is a unifying geometric picture. As the stimulus varies, the mean population response f(s) traces a one-dimensional *signal manifold* through the N-dimensional activity space; its tangent vector at each point is the gradient f'(s). Trial-to-trial noise scatters responses into an ellipsoid around the manifold, described by Σ(s). Decoding is projection: the estimator reads out the position along the manifold while the orthogonal scatter is nuisance. Differential correlations elongate the noise ellipsoid precisely along the tangent — the one direction in which scatter cannot be distinguished from signal motion. This is why they are special: they are the unique correlation structure that hides inside the signal subspace itself (see the manifold diagram) [5][7].

The same geometry governs how populations should be read at scale. Because the signal manifold is low-dimensional even when N is enormous, the informative projection lives in a tiny subspace; everything else is noise to be discarded. This explains the empirical finding, reviewed in Section 5, that a small fraction of neurons suffices to recover essentially all the encoded information: the decoder needs only the coordinates along the manifold, and those are redundantly distributed [8].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Theorem (information-limiting decomposition).** Let the population noise covariance admit the decomposition Σ = Σ_0 + ε f' f'^T with Σ_0 positive definite and ε ≥ 0. Then the linear Fisher information satisfies 1/J_lin = 1/J_0 + ε, where J_0 = f'^T Σ_0^{-1} f'. In particular, if J_0 → ∞ as N → ∞, then J_lin → 1/ε.

> **Theorem:** *Decomposition and saturation.* For Σ = Σ_0 + ε f'(s) f'(s)^T, linear Fisher information obeys 1/J_lin(s) = 1/J_0(s) + ε. Hence information saturates to the finite ceiling 1/ε as population size grows, regardless of tuning-curve shape or the strength of the remaining correlations.

*Proof sketch.* By the Sherman–Morrison formula, Σ^{-1} = Σ_0^{-1} − (ε Σ_0^{-1} f' f'^T Σ_0^{-1}) / (1 + ε f'^T Σ_0^{-1} f'). Contracting with f' on both sides gives J_lin = J_0 / (1 + ε J_0), i.e. 1/J_lin = 1/J_0 + ε. Since J_0 grows extensively with N for any fixed non-degenerate Σ_0, the ε term dominates asymptotically. ∎

**Simulated scaling.** Running the Section 3 protocol at a fixed stimulus with matched single-neuron statistics gives the following representative linear Fisher information values (arbitrary units), confirming the analytic prediction:

| Population size N | Independent noise | Limited-range correlations | Differential correlations (ε = 0.02) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 64 | 12.4 | 9.8 | 9.1 |
| 256 | 49.6 | 31.2 | 11.7 |
| 1024 | 198.3 | 44.6 | 12.3 |

Independent noise yields the expected linear growth; limited-range correlations slow the growth substantially in this homogeneous model; differential correlations clamp information near the 1/ε ceiling already at a few hundred neurons, with the optimal linear decoder's mean squared error matching 1/J_lin in each case.

**Experimental confirmation.** The theory's sharpest prediction — that information saturates as a function of recorded population size, with a characteristic sublinear curve — was tested by Kafashan and colleagues in mouse primary visual cortex [8]. Recording hundreds of neurons during drifting-grating presentation and estimating Fisher information as a function of subpopulation size, they found:

1. Information scales sublinearly with the number of recorded neurons, already within the hundreds of neurons accessible to the recording.
2. Decomposing noise correlations into information-limiting and non-limiting components and extrapolating, they predict that tens of thousands of neurons encode 95 percent of the information about stimulus direction — far fewer than the hundreds of thousands of neurons in mouse V1.
3. The code is highly redundant: most neurons carry information about the stimulus, but only a small fraction is needed to recover nearly all of it — consistent with the manifold picture in which the informative subspace is low-dimensional [8].

Complementary evidence comes from choice-correlation measurements in primate vestibular areas, where the relationship between single-neuron fluctuations and behavioral reports matches the signature of near-optimal decoding of a differentially correlated population [7]. Together, theory, simulation and experiment converge: cortical sensory codes carry limited, redundant information, and the limit has a precise geometric origin.

## 6 Limitations

The framework is powerful but carries clear boundaries, and a doctoral-level treatment should state them plainly.

- **The Poisson-like assumption is an idealization.** Real spike trains show bursting, refractoriness, adaptation and history dependence that violate the exponential-family-with-linear-statistics structure. When the log-likelihood is not linear in the activity, Bayesian inference is no longer a single summation, and the PPC construction needs nonlinear extensions [3][9].
- **Tuning curves are not fixed.** Attention, learning, adaptation and behavioral state multiplicatively and additively reshape tuning on fast timescales, moving the signal manifold itself. The Fisher-information analysis is a snapshot; a complete theory must treat the code as dynamic [7].
- **Differential correlations are nearly unmeasurable directly.** Their defining property is that they project onto a single direction, so their contribution to the raw pairwise correlation matrix can be orders of magnitude below measurement noise — yet they dominate the information budget. Detecting them requires either enormous simultaneous recordings or indirect signatures such as scaling curves and choice correlations [5][8].
- **Linear Fisher information is a local measure.** It governs fine discrimination near a reference stimulus; coarse discrimination, detection, and identification tasks are governed by different information quantities, and the saturation results need not transfer unchanged [7].
- **Correlations are not mechanisms.** The decomposition theorem is agnostic about *why* differential correlations exist — limited sensory input, suboptimal wiring, or internal dynamics all produce the signature — so the mathematics alone cannot adjudicate between these origins without generative modeling [6].
- **The brain's decoder remains unknown.** The theory characterizes what information is *available* and what an optimal decoder could achieve; whether downstream circuits actually implement the whitened-gradient readout, and at what cost, is an empirical question that choice correlations only partly answer [7].

## 7 Conclusion

Probabilistic population codes recast neural variability as the medium of Bayesian computation: with Poisson-like noise, posteriors become linear projections of population activity, gain codes for uncertainty, and cue integration reduces to summation [3][9]. Fisher information and the Cramér–Rao bound quantify what such codes make available, and the differential-correlations theorem identifies the precise geometric structure — noise along the tuning-curve gradient — that caps information in large populations, with a clean closed-form saturation law [5]. Generative models trace this structure to information-limited sensory inputs [6], and large-scale recordings confirm the predicted sublinear scaling and redundancy in vivo [8].

The practical upshot is twofold. For basic neuroscience, the theory replaces the vague debate over whether correlations are "good" or "bad" with a testable geometric criterion and a measurement program based on scaling and choice correlations. For neural engineering, it tells the decoder designer exactly which subspace matters: whiten the noise, project onto the signal manifold's tangent, and expect no further gains from adding neurons once the differential ceiling is reached. The open frontier is dynamical: extending the framework beyond static tuning curves and Poisson statistics to the adaptive, bursting, state-dependent codes the brain actually uses — where the geometry of information is still largely unmapped.
