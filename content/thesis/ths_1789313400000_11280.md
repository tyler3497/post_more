# Bootstrapping Fully Homomorphic Encryption: From Gentry's Ideal Lattices to CKKS Approximate Refreshing and TFHE Programmable Bootstrapping

## Abstract

Since Craig Gentry's 2009 breakthrough, bootstrapping — homomorphically re-encrypting a noisy ciphertext under its own decryption circuit — has been the defining operation of fully homomorphic encryption. This article traces the arc from Gentry's ideal-lattice construction and its bootstrappability theorem, through the modulus-switching leveled schemes that delayed the need for refreshing, to the two modern workhorses of practical FHE: the CKKS scheme, whose approximate nature forces a fundamentally different, sine-approximation-based bootstrapping procedure that refreshes the modulus while only approximately refreshing the noise, and the FHEW/TFHE line, where blind rotation turns bootstrapping into a fast gate-level primitive that can simultaneously evaluate arbitrary univariate functions — programmable bootstrapping. We examine noise-management machinery including modulus switching, rescaling, and composable average-case noise analysis, compare precision and throughput trade-offs across schemes, and survey real deployments from encrypted machine-learning inference to confidential blockchains.

---

## 1 Introduction

The dream of *privacy homomorphisms* — computing on data while it remains encrypted — was articulated in 1978 and stood open for three decades. It fell in 2009, when Craig Gentry showed how to convert a *somewhat* homomorphic encryption (SHE) scheme, capable of only bounded-depth evaluation, into a *fully* homomorphic one via a technique he called **bootstrapping**: homomorphically evaluating the scheme's own decryption circuit on an encryption of the secret key, thereby producing a fresh ciphertext encrypting the same plaintext with independent, small noise [1].

Every practical lattice-based FHE scheme is **inherently noisy**. Decryption of a ciphertext $(a, b)$ under secret $s$ computes an inner product $\langle c, s \rangle$ whose deviation from the encoded message — the *noise* — must stay below a correctness threshold. Homomorphic addition grows noise additively; homomorphic multiplication grows it multiplicatively, and after enough operations the noise drowns the message. The entire engineering of FHE is therefore **noise management**: a toolbox of modulus switching, rescaling, key switching, and — when all else is exhausted — bootstrapping, the only known method for unbounded-depth computation [1].

This article reconstructs that engineering discipline from first principles. We begin with Gentry's blueprint and the ideal-lattice construction that proved FHE possible, then follow two diverging lineages that define the modern landscape. The first leads through leveled schemes to **CKKS approximate bootstrapping** (Cheon et al., 2018): because CKKS treats noise as part of the message, classical homomorphic decryption cannot *remove* noise, so CKKS bootstrapping instead *raises the modulus* and approximates the resulting modular reduction with a scaled sine polynomial — refreshing the available depth while only approximately refreshing the noise [4]. The second leads through **FHEW and TFHE gate bootstrapping** (Ducas–Micciancio 2015; Chillotti et al. 2016), where *blind rotation* refreshes an LWE ciphertext in under a tenth of a second, and where that same rotation can be *programmed* to evaluate arbitrary lookup-table functions for free — the **programmable (functional) bootstrap** that now powers encrypted neural-network inference [2][4].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Lattices and Learning With Errors

Modern FHE rests on the **Learning With Errors (LWE)** problem: given $m$ samples $(a_i, \langle a_i, s \rangle + e_i)$ with $a_i$ uniform, secret $s$, and small Gaussian error $e_i$, recovering $s$ is as hard as worst-case lattice problems such as approximate shortest vector. Its ring variant **RLWE** works over the cyclotomic ring $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^N+1)$ with $N$ a power of two, yielding compact ciphertexts and a native polynomial-multiplication structure that enables SIMD packing [1][2].

A canonical LWE ciphertext encrypting a message $m$ under secret $s$ is

$$c = (a,\; b = \langle a, s \rangle + \Delta \cdot m + e) \in \mathbb{Z}_q^{n+1},$$

where $\Delta$ is a scaling factor separating message from noise. Decryption computes $b - \langle a, s \rangle = \Delta m + e$ and rounds away $e$, succeeding exactly when $|e| < \Delta/2$. This inequality — **noise below half the scaling factor** — is the single invariant that every technique in this article exists to protect.

### 2.2 Somewhat, leveled, and fully homomorphic encryption

A **somewhat homomorphic** scheme evaluates circuits of bounded multiplicative depth before noise breaches the threshold. A **leveled** scheme takes a depth bound $L$ at key-generation time and provisions a *modulus chain* $q_0 > q_1 > \cdots > q_L$, consuming one level per multiplication. A **fully homomorphic** scheme evaluates circuits of arbitrary depth — and, since Gentry, the universal route from the second to the third has been bootstrapping [1].

Gentry's original construction used **ideal lattices**: the secret key is a short basis of an ideal $J$ in a number ring, the public key a "bad" basis of the same ideal, and a ciphertext has the form $v + x$ with $v \in J$ a lattice vector and $x$ a small error encoding the message. Security reduced to worst-case problems on ideal lattices. Because the scheme could evaluate only shallow circuits, Gentry *squashed* its decryption circuit — expressing it as a low-degree polynomial via a sparse-subset-sum hint — until the scheme could evaluate decryption plus one NAND gate, at which point his **bootstrappability theorem** fired:

> **Theorem (Gentry's bootstrapping theorem):** Let $\mathcal{E}$ be a somewhat homomorphic encryption scheme that can homomorphically evaluate its own decryption circuit $D_{\text{Add}}$ and $D_{\text{Mult}}$ — i.e., $\mathcal{E}$ is *bootstrappable* — and assume circular (KDM) security of encryptions of the secret key. Then there exists a fully homomorphic encryption scheme $\mathcal{E}^\ast$ derived from $\mathcal{E}$, in which any ciphertext can be *recrypted* into a fresh ciphertext with bounded noise, enabling unbounded-depth evaluation [1].

Early implementations by Smart–Vercauteren and Gentry–Halevi confirmed the construction worked but was far from practical: key generation alone struggled past dimension 2048, and a single recryption took tens of minutes [1].

## 3 Methodology

This article treats bootstrapping as an **algorithmic primitive** and compares three design families — (a) exact re-encryption à la Gentry, (b) approximate modulus-refreshing in CKKS, and (c) blind-rotation gate refreshing in FHEW/TFHE — along five axes: *correctness model* (exact vs. approximate), *latency* (seconds per bootstrap), *throughput* (slots or gates per second), *key material*, and *security assumptions*.

To fix intuition about how noise compounds, consider a minimal Python sketch of leveled noise accounting, the kind of estimator every FHE deployment runs before choosing parameters:

```python
def leveled_noise(depth, q_chain, sigma, delta):
    """Track worst-case noise through a leveled circuit (illustrative)."""
    noise = sigma * 6.0          # fresh-encryption noise bound
    for level in range(depth):
        noise = noise**2 / delta  # multiplication squares noise
        q = q_chain[level]
        if noise * 2 > q // 4:    # correctness invariant violated
            return level, noise   # depth at which we must bootstrap
        noise *= q_chain[level+1] / q  # modulus switching shrinks noise
    return depth, noise
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Gentry's blueprint and the cost of exact re-encryption

Gentry's framework separates two problems: (i) building an SHE scheme whose decryption circuit is shallow, and (ii) *squashing* that circuit — adding a hint to the public key (a large set of vectors, one sparse subset of which sums to the secret key) so that decryption becomes a low-degree polynomial the SHE scheme can evaluate. The encrypted secret key — the **bootstrapping key** — then lets anyone homomorphically decrypt: given $c = \text{Enc}(m)$ with large noise, compute $\text{Enc}(\text{Dec}_{sk}(c))$, which is a *fresh* encryption of $m$ whose noise depends only on the decryption circuit, not on the computation that produced $c$ [1].

The decade after 2009 attacked both costs. The **BGV** scheme (Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan, 2011) introduced **modulus switching**: instead of bootstrapping when noise grows, switch the ciphertext from modulus $q$ to a smaller modulus $p$, mapping $(a, b) \mapsto (\lfloor (p/q)a \rceil, \lfloor (p/q)b \rceil)$. The noise scales down by the ratio $p/q$ while the message is preserved, so a long chain of moduli supports deep circuits *without any bootstrapping at all* — leveled FHE. **BFV** (Brakerski 2012; Fan–Vercauteren 2012) made noise growth scale-invariant, and **GSW** (Gentry–Sahai–Waters, 2013) replaced relinearization with an approximate-eigenvector multiplication whose noise grows only quasi-additively. Bootstrapping retreated to a last resort — until applications demanded unbounded depth again.

### 4.2 CKKS approximate bootstrapping: refreshing the modulus instead of the noise

The **CKKS scheme** (Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song, 2017) broke with the exact-message tradition: it encrypts vectors of complex numbers, treats the encryption noise as part of the *approximation error*, and provides a **rescaling** operation that divides both message and noise by the scaling factor $\Delta$ after each multiplication — emulating fixed-point arithmetic. Up to $N/2$ values pack into one ciphertext for SIMD evaluation, making CKKS the natural choice for encrypted machine learning [4].

CKKS bootstrapping (Cheon et al., 2018) confronts a conceptual wall: if noise *is* the message, then homomorphically decrypting cannot remove it — the "fresh" ciphertext would carry the same approximation error. The resolution is to refresh the **modulus** rather than the noise. Concretely, the procedure [4][5]:

1. **ModRaise.** Lift the ciphertext from the small modulus $q$ to a large modulus $Q$. The decrypted value is now $\langle c, s \rangle$ interpreted as an integer in a wide range, equal to $m + q \cdot I$ for an unknown multiple $I$ of the original modulus.
2. **CoeffsToSlots.** Apply a homomorphic linear transform (a DFT-like map) moving the coefficients of the plaintext polynomial into the SIMD slots, so the modular reduction can be applied slot-wise.
3. **EvalMod.** Homomorphically evaluate the modular reduction $t \mapsto [t]_q$. Because the true modular function is discontinuous, CKKS approximates it with a **scaled sine**: $$[t]_q \;\approx\; \frac{q}{2\pi}\,\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi t}{q}\right),$$ fitting the sine with a low-degree polynomial (originally via Taylor expansion and double-angle iteration) [5].
4. **SlotsToCoeffs.** Invert the linear transform, returning a ciphertext at a large modulus whose noise now permits further multiplications.

The catch is precision: every stage injects error — the sine fit, the homomorphic DFT, and the key-switching after each linear map. Han–Ki replaced the Taylor sine with a composite approximation, and Lee et al. used multi-interval Remez fits of an arcsine-sine composition to widen the approximation range [4].

The Haskell sketch below captures the *shape* of rescaling — the operation that makes CKKS leveled evaluation possible between bootstraps:

```haskell
-- CKKS-style rescaling: divide out the scaling factor after multiplication
-- (illustrative; real implementations work over RNS limbs)
rescale :: Integer -> Integer -> Ciphertext -> Ciphertext
rescale q delta (CT a b) =
  let q' = q `div` delta
      shrink x = round (fromIntegral x * fromIntegral q' / fromIntegral q)
  in CT (map shrink a) (shrink b)  -- noise and message both scale by 1/delta
```

### 4.3 The FHEW/TFHE line: blind rotation and gate bootstrapping

Parallel to the approximate-arithmetic line, Ducas and Micciancio's **FHEW** (2015) returned to Gentry's original gate-level vision with a radically faster mechanism. FHEW encrypts single bits as LWE samples and refreshes them with **blind rotation**: an accumulator polynomial $ACC = X^{\,b - \sum s_i a_i} \cdot v(X)$ is homomorphically rotated by the *secret* phase of the input ciphertext, using a bootstrapping key of encryptions of the secret bits; extracting a coefficient yields a fresh LWE encryption of a function of the message. The whole refresh took **under one second**, with a roughly 1 GB bootstrapping key [2].

**TFHE** (Chillotti–Gama–Georgieva–Izabachène, ASIACRYPT 2016; journal version 2020) reformulated FHEW's blind rotation in terms of a single **external product** between GSW and LWE ciphertexts, and replaced exact gadget decomposition with an approximate one. The payoff, measured on identical security parameters, was dramatic: bootstrapping in **under 0.1 seconds**, the key shrinking from 1 GB to **24 MB**, with strictly better noise propagation [2]. The scheme works over the **torus** $\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$: TLWE samples encrypt torus elements, TRLWE samples encrypt polynomials, and TRGSW samples encrypt bits for the CMux-driven rotation loop. The reference library remains the canonical implementation [3].

Because TFHE bootstraps *every gate*, there is no noise management at all in the programmer's model: each NAND (or arbitrary gate) output is born fresh, so circuits of unbounded depth evaluate without parameter growth. The cost is throughput — one 0.1-second bootstrap per gate is slow for arithmetic-heavy workloads, the mirror image of CKKS's SIMD strength.

### 4.4 Programmable bootstrapping: a free function evaluation

The blind-rotation machinery contains a remarkable degree of freedom: the *test polynomial* $v(X)$ in the accumulator can encode any function, and the rotation selects its coefficients according to the message. Choosing $v$ to encode a **lookup table** turns the bootstrap into a **programmable (functional) bootstrap**: given an encryption of $m$ (with small noise), output a fresh encryption of $f(m)$ for *any* univariate $f$, at no extra cost beyond the refresh itself. Correctness requires only that the input noise stay below half the LUT spacing — the same $\Delta/2$ invariant from Section 2.1.

Chillotti, Joye, and Paillier showed this enables efficient homomorphic inference of deep neural networks, evaluating activations like the sigmoid as LUTs inside the bootstrap rather than as expensive polynomial approximations [4]. Subsequent work generalized multi-value and multi-input functional bootstraps, effectively giving TFHE a built-in "evaluate any small function" instruction. Conceptually, programmable bootstrapping collapses two of FHE's hardest costs — refreshing *and* non-linear function evaluation — into one operation, which is why TFHE-family libraries now dominate encrypted-inference deployments.

### 4.5 Noise management unified

Across all families, the same four primitives recur:

| Primitive | Role | Where it dominates |
|---|---|---|
| **Modulus switching** | Scale $(a,b)$ from $q$ to $p < q$, shrinking noise by $p/q$ | BGV/BFV leveled evaluation |
| **Rescaling** | Divide by scaling factor $\Delta$ after multiplication | CKKS fixed-point arithmetic |
| **Key switching** | Re-encrypt under a new key after automorphisms or decomposition | CKKS rotations, TFHE dimension switching |
| **Bootstrapping** | Re-encrypt via homomorphic decryption to reset noise | Gentry exact, CKKS approximate, TFHE gate-level |

Two modern developments deserve emphasis. First, **average-case noise analysis** has replaced loose worst-case bounds: a composable componentwise noise model for CKKS predicts precision loss with theoretical estimates that never deviate more than 0.01 bits from experiment, even for full bootstrapping circuits — turning parameter selection from black art into engineering [6]. Second, the community has converged on **standardized parameters** (the Homomorphic Encryption Security Standard), mapping ring dimension $N$ and modulus sizes to 128-bit security targets, so deployments no longer hand-roll lattice estimates [4].

A Rust-flavored sketch of the TFHE programmable-bootstrap call shape — how a deployment invokes the primitive — is illustrative:

```rust
// Illustrative call shape of a programmable bootstrap (TFHE-style API).
// LUT encodes f; the bootstrap returns a FRESH encryption of f(m).
fn programmable_bootstrap(ct: &LweCiphertext, lut: &[u64], bsk: &BootstrappingKey) -> LweCiphertext {
    let mut acc = encode_test_polynomial(lut); // test poly holds the LUT
    for (a_i, key_bit) in ct.mask().iter().zip(bsk.iter()) {
        acc = cmux(key_bit, rotate(acc, a_i), acc); // blind rotation loop
    }
    sample_extract(acc, 0) // coefficient 0 now encrypts f(m), noise reset
}
```

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

The performance landscape is best read as a precision–throughput trade-off surface:

| Scheme | Message space | Bootstrap latency | Precision after refresh | SIMD throughput |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gentry 2009 [1] | bits | tens of minutes | exact | none |
| BGV/BFV (leveled) | integers | n/a (leveled) | exact | high (batching) |
| FHEW (2015) [2] | bits | under 1 s | exact | none (gate-level) |
| TFHE (2016/2020) [2] | torus elements | under 0.1 s | exact | none (gate-level) |
| CKKS (2018 bootstrap) [4] | complex vectors | seconds–minutes | ~10 bits | very high ($N/2$ slots) |
| CKKS, modern [7][8] | complex vectors | seconds–minutes | 15–25+ bits | very high ($N/2$ slots) |

Three quantitative results anchor the comparison. **First**, the TFHE external-product reformulation cut bootstrapping latency by an order of magnitude and key size by a factor of roughly forty at unchanged security — the single largest practical jump in bootstrapping history [2]. **Second**, composable noise estimation for CKKS now predicts the precision loss of arbitrary circuits, including full bootstrapping, to within 0.01 bits of measured values with high probability, which makes aggressive parameter choices (smaller $N$, larger depth) *provably* safe rather than hopeful [6]. **Third**, high-precision CKKS bootstrapping via odd-degree sine series reduces the bootstrap error by about 25% relative to the EUROCRYPT 2022 baseline while supporting up to $2^{12}$ slots at the same error level where prior work managed only $2^4$ [7]; Chebyshev-based modular interpolation attacks the same error from the integer-point side [8].

The CKKS bootstrap pipeline decomposes into measurable stages, each with a characteristic depth and error contribution:

| Stage | Operation | Typical depth cost | Error source |
|---|---|---|---|
| ModRaise | $q \to Q$ lifting | 0 | none (exact) |
| CoeffsToSlots | homomorphic DFT | 2–3 levels | linear-transform rounding |
| EvalMod | sine-polynomial fit | 4–8 levels | polynomial approximation |
| SlotsToCoeffs | inverse DFT | 2–3 levels | linear-transform rounding |

Notably, the linear transforms and the sine fit consume the bulk of the fresh depth the bootstrap just created — a well-tuned bootstrap is one where the *net* depth gain, after paying for its own circuit, is maximized. Low-latency restructuring of the transforms around roots of unity directly attacks this overhead [5].

## 6 Limitations

The picture is not uniformly rosy, and the honest limitations shape where each scheme is deployed:

1. **CKKS precision erosion.** Every CKKS bootstrap *loses* precision — the sine approximation and the DFTs are not free — so applications needing, say, 40 correct bits must provision parameters for far more, and some computations (exact comparisons, long iterative solvers) remain out of reach. Approximate bootstrapping refreshes *depth*, not *accuracy* [4][6].
2. **TFHE throughput.** Gate-level bootstrapping at ~0.1 s per gate yields on the order of tens of gates per second per core; a 64-bit multiplication becomes thousands of bootstraps. TFHE excels at Boolean logic, comparisons, and small LUTs, and loses badly to CKKS on vector arithmetic [2][3].
3. **Bootstrapping latency in CKKS.** A single CKKS bootstrap still takes seconds to minutes at 128-bit security parameters — acceptable when amortized over $2^{15}$ slots, punishing otherwise [5][7].
4. **Circular security.** All bootstrapping keys encrypt key material under itself. KDM/circular security remains a non-standard assumption without worst-case reductions in the original Gentry framework [1].
5. **CKKS decryption oracles.** The approximate error is a known attack vector: IND-CPA-D adversaries with decryption oracles can extract secret keys from CKKS's approximate outputs. Mitigations (noise flooding, exact variants) exist but add cost [6].
6. **Key material and memory.** Rotation and bootstrapping keys reach gigabytes for deep CKKS parameter sets; TFHE's 24 MB looks svelte only by comparison [2][4].
7. **Parameter selection.** Despite standard tables and tight estimators [6], choosing $(N, q, \Delta)$ for a novel circuit still demands expertise; misconfiguration fails silently into wrong answers rather than clean errors.

## 7 Conclusion

Seventeen years after Gentry's STOC paper, bootstrapping has diversified from a single theoretical trick into a small family of engineering disciplines. Gentry's exact re-encryption [1] proved FHE possible and named the price — circular security and heavy keys. Modulus switching and rescaling deferred the bill, giving us leveled FHE practical enough to ship. CKKS approximate bootstrapping [4] redefined the operation for the approximate world: refresh the modulus, approximate the modular reduction, accept the precision loss, and let SIMD amortization carry the cost — with a still-active research frontier pushing precision upward [5][7][8]. The FHEW/TFHE line [2] went the opposite direction, making the bootstrap so fast it could run per gate, and then discovered the bootstrap could *compute* — programmable bootstrapping folding arbitrary univariate functions into the refresh [4].

The practical upshot is a genuine division of labor, visible in real deployments: CKKS-family libraries (OpenFHE, Lattigo, HEAAN-line) carry encrypted machine learning and statistical computation, while TFHE-family libraries (TFHE-rs, Concrete) carry encrypted Boolean logic, comparisons, neural-network activations, and confidential smart contracts. Tight composable noise analysis [6] and standardized parameters have moved both from research artifacts to infrastructure.
