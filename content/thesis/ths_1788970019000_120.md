# Neuromorphic Computing with Spiking Neural Networks: Event-Driven Architectures from TrueNorth and Loihi to BrainScaleS

## Abstract

Neuromorphic computing abandons the von Neumann separation of memory and processing for massively parallel, event-driven architectures that co-locate state, computation, and communication in the manner of neural tissue. This thesis develops a rigorous account of spiking neural networks (SNNs) — the *third generation* of neural network models — as both a computational formalism and a silicon design principle. We derive the leaky integrate-and-fire neuron and spike response models, analyze address-event representation as the interconnect primitive, and trace three landmark systems: IBM's **TrueNorth**, a digital neurosynaptic array of one million neurons drawing 70 mW [1]; Intel's **Loihi**, an asynchronous manycore processor with programmable on-chip plasticity [2]; and Heidelberg's **BrainScaleS**, a wafer-scale mixed-signal system accelerating biological dynamics $10^4\times$ [3,4]. We then examine the learning crisis of deep SNNs — the nondifferentiability of the spike — and show how surrogate gradient methods restore backpropagation-through-time to the spiking regime [5]. A formal comparison of energy, latency, and scalability trade-offs across digital, mixed-signal, and analog implementations concludes the analysis.

## 1 Introduction

The end of Dennard scaling has re-opened a question Carver Mead posed in 1990 [6]: what if we built computers the way the brain is built? A human cortex performs on the order of $10^{16}$ synaptic operations per second on roughly 20 W — a figure no digital supercomputer approaches. The brain achieves this through a design radically different from the stored-program computer: *memory and processing are inseparable*, communication is *sparse and event-driven*, and computation proceeds *in continuous time* rather than lockstep clock cycles.

**Spiking neural networks** are the algorithmic counterpart of this architecture. Neurons communicate via stereotyped, all-or-none *spikes* — impulses whose information lies in their precise timing or rate. This temporal dimension is not an implementation detail; it is the central computational resource. Yet spiking computation is alien to conventional hardware: the sparse, irregular, fine-grained parallelism of an SNN maps poorly onto SIMD accelerators built for dense matrix multiplication. *Neuromorphic computing* is the co-design discipline that closes this gap — chips whose circuits embody neural dynamics directly, so that the energy cost of an inference scales with the number of spikes rather than the number of multiply-accumulate operations.

This thesis proceeds bottom-up. Section 2 establishes the mathematical foundations of spiking neuron models and spike-based communication. Section 3 describes the methodology of neuromorphic co-design. Section 4 compares three canonical systems spanning the digital–analog spectrum — **TrueNorth** (§4.1), **Loihi** (§4.2), **BrainScaleS** (§4.3) — plus the AER interconnect (§4.4). Section 5 turns to learning, from local spike-timing-dependent plasticity to surrogate gradient methods, with formal arguments and empirical results. Section 6 confronts limitations honestly, and Section 7 concludes with the research frontier.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From McCulloch–Pitts to the Spike Response Model

The first generation of neural models used binary threshold units; the second replaced the threshold with continuous, differentiable nonlinearities — the sigmoid and ReLU units of modern deep learning. The *third generation* restores the temporal dynamics of real neurons [5]: state evolves in continuous time, and output is emitted as discrete events.

The workhorse of the third generation is the **leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF)** neuron:

$$\tau_m \frac{dV}{dt} = -(V(t) - V_{\text{rest}}) + R\, I(t)$$

where $V(t)$ is the membrane potential, $\tau_m$ the membrane time constant, $R$ the input resistance, and $I(t)$ the summed synaptic current. When $V(t)$ crosses a threshold $V_{\text{th}}$, the neuron emits a spike — mathematically a Dirac delta $\delta(t - t^f)$ — and the membrane resets to $V_{\text{reset}}$, often followed by an absolute refractory period $\tau_{\text{ref}}$. The "leak" term gives the neuron a fading memory: coincident inputs arriving within a few $\tau_m$ summate strongly, while temporally dispersed inputs decay away. *Time itself becomes a computational variable.*

A more general formulation is the **Spike Response Model (SRM)**, expressing membrane potential as a convolution of spike trains with kernels:

$$V_i(t) = \sum_j w_{ij} \sum_f \epsilon(t - t_j^f - d_{ij}) + \sum_{f'} \eta(t - t_i^{f'}) + u_{\text{rest}}$$

with postsynaptic kernel $\epsilon$, refractory kernel $\eta$, axonal delays $d_{ij}$, and weights $w_{ij}$. The neuron is a *temporal filter bank* followed by a threshold nonlinearity.

> **Theorem:** *Computational universality of spiking networks.* A finite network of SRM spiking neurons with fixed real-valued weights and programmable delays can simulate any Turing machine, provided the thresholding function is non-constant and the kernels have bounded support. The proof constructs NAND gates from coincidence detection and delay lines from axonal propagation, embedding a synchronous Boolean circuit in continuous time.

### 2.2 Neural Codes: Rate, Time, and Population

Three coding hypotheses dominate:

1. **Rate coding.** Information rides the mean firing rate $r = \langle N_{\text{spikes}} \rangle / T$. Robust to noise, but wasteful — latency scales with the integration window.
2. **Temporal coding.** Information rides *precise spike times* — time-to-first-spike, rank-order, or phase. A single spike per neuron can encode a full analog value, enabling single-spike inference.
3. **Population coding.** Stimuli are represented by distributed patterns across many neurons; redundancy confers fault tolerance, which neuromorphic systems exploit to survive device mismatch and dead neurons.

The coding choice is not academic: it determines whether a chip sustains 100 Hz firing (rate) or operates at ~1 Hz mean rates with sparse precisely-timed events (temporal). Energy per inference differs by *orders of magnitude* between the regimes.

### 2.3 Address-Event Representation

The **Address-Event Representation (AER)** protocol transmits each spike as a digital packet — the *address* of the firing neuron — multiplexed over a shared high-speed bus. Because biological firing is sparse (cortical mean rates of 1–10 Hz against microsecond axonal timescales), one bus time-multiplexes thousands of neurons with negligible collision probability. AER converts the $O(N^2)$ wiring problem of neural connectivity into an $O(N)$ multiplexed interconnect, with routing tables implementing arbitrary fan-out. Every major neuromorphic platform — TrueNorth, Loihi, BrainScaleS, SpiNNaker — is, at its communication core, an AER machine.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis follows a **comparative co-design methodology**: each neuromorphic system is treated as a point in a five-dimensional design space, and we analyze how its coordinates determine its capabilities.

| Design dimension | Options | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Substrate | Digital / mixed-signal / analog | Precision vs. energy vs. speed |
| Neuron model | LIF, AdEx, Izhikevich, multicompartment | Biological fidelity vs. silicon cost |
| Plasticity | Fixed, STDP, programmable microcode | Inference-only vs. on-chip learning |
| Time base | Real-time, accelerated ($10^3$–$10^4\times$) | Experiment throughput vs. sensor coupling |
| Programming model | Corelet graphs, NxSDK, PyNN | Accessibility vs. hardware efficiency |

Empirical claims rest on published measurements from the primary architecture papers and follow-up system studies [1,2,3,4]. Cross-system figures are normalized to *synaptic operations per second per watt* (SOPS/W), the community-standard efficiency metric, while noting its limitations as a proxy for application-level energy.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 TrueNorth: The Digital Neurosynaptic Array

IBM's **TrueNorth**, reported in *Science* in 2014 [1], is the canonical digital neuromorphic processor. In 28 nm CMOS with 5.4 billion transistors, one chip integrates **4,096 neurosynaptic cores**, each with 256 digital neurons, 256 axons, and a $256 \times 256$ binary synaptic crossbar — **1 million neurons and 256 million synapses** on a $4.3\,\text{cm}^2$ die.

The architectural insight is *determinism through discretization*. Each neuron is a parameterized digital LIF unit updated on a 1 kHz tick; spikes travel between cores over an asynchronous 2-D mesh as addressed packets. Everything is synchronous-digital, so the chip is functionally deterministic and bit-reproducible — a radical simplification for the software stack, at the cost of continuous-time dynamics.

The headline figure is power: **70 mW** per chip at 46 billion synaptic operations per second per watt — roughly $10^4\times$ the efficiency of a contemporary CPU on equivalent workloads. But TrueNorth carries a deliberate amputation: **synapses are non-plastic**. Weights are loaded at configuration time and never change. Training happens *offline* (notably with ternary-weight-aware backpropagation) and frozen models are deployed. TrueNorth proved million-neuron digital SNNs feasible and ferociously efficient; it left learning to its successors.

### 4.2 Loihi: Programmable Plasticity on an Asynchronous Mesh

Intel's **Loihi** (2018) [2] is TrueNorth's philosophical counterpoint: where TrueNorth froze learning out of silicon, Loihi makes *learning the central architectural feature*. In 14 nm FinFET, each chip holds **128 neuromorphic cores** plus three embedded x86 management cores — up to 131,072 neurons and 130 million synapses per chip, tiled into systems as large as **Pohoiki Springs: 768 chips, 100 million neurons** [7].

Three design decisions distinguish Loihi:

1. **Asynchronous design.** Cores are fully event-driven with no global clock; activity propagates only when spikes occur, and idle silicon consumes near-zero dynamic power.
2. **Programmable learning engines.** Every core executes *microcode-programmed* plasticity rules — arbitrary functions of pre- and post-synaptic spike traces, evaluated at configurable epochs. STDP, reward-modulated STDP, and three-factor rules are all expressible; the chip commits to no single theory of learning.
3. **Hierarchical connectivity.** Multicast spike routing with population-level addressing exploits structured sparsity, cutting synaptic memory for convolutional topologies by an order of magnitude versus flat AER.

Empirically, Loihi demonstrated on-chip learning of robotic control policies, sparse coding dictionaries, and mapping tasks, with the follow-up survey [7] reporting $100\times$ energy advantages over GPUs on event-driven workloads such as dynamic-vision-sensor gesture recognition. Loihi 2 (2021) added graded spikes, richer neuron models, and $10\times$ density improvement [7].

### 4.3 BrainScaleS: Accelerated Analog Emulation at Wafer Scale

Heidelberg's **BrainScaleS** takes the opposite path: *emulate neural dynamics directly in analog circuits, and run them fast* [3]. The first-generation system integrates an entire 20 cm silicon **wafer** — uncut — with 384 HICANN ASICs in 180 nm CMOS, each implementing analog AdEx neuron circuits and synapse arrays with on-chip STDP.

Its defining parameter is the **$10^4\times$ acceleration factor**: one wall-clock second emulates nearly three hours of biological time. A learning experiment taking a year in real time completes in under an hour — making previously impossible experiments (evolutionary optimization of plasticity rules, long-timescale consolidation) routine. The price is *variability*: transistor mismatch demands per-neuron calibration, and the BrainScaleS OS maintains an availability database mapping around defective components [9].

**BrainScaleS-2** (65 nm) answers with *hybrid plasticity* [4]: a digital plasticity processing unit alongside each analog core combines analog speed with digital programmability — Loihi's microcoded learning philosophy transplanted into an accelerated analog substrate. In-the-loop training, embedding the physical chip inside the gradient computation, has produced deep SNNs matching software baselines [4].

### 4.4 Interconnect and Scaling: The AER Fabric

All three systems confront the same scaling law. A cortex-scale network ($10^{11}$ neurons, $10^4$ fan-out) implies $10^{15}$ synapses — and a communication fabric to match. AER multiplexing is necessary but not sufficient; the deeper question is *routing*.

| System | Interconnect | Routing model | Scale demonstrated |
|---|---|---|---|
| TrueNorth | 2-D mesh, dimension-order | Deterministic + multicast | 16 chips, 16M neurons |
| Loihi | 2-D mesh, asynchronous | Hierarchical multicast | 768 chips, 100M neurons |
| BrainScaleS-1 | Wafer-scale L1/L2 | 64:1 time-multiplex + packet-switch | 1 wafer, ~200k neurons |
| SpiNNaker | Triangular torus | Multicast packet routing | 1M ARM cores |

The emerging consensus is *hierarchical hybrid routing*: dense local connectivity via crossbars, sparse long-range connectivity via packet-switched AER. No existing system demonstrates a credible path to $10^{15}$ synapses — the interconnect, not the neuron circuit, is the binding constraint on brain-scale neuromorphic computing.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 The Learning Problem, Formalized

The central obstacle to deep SNNs is visible in a single equation. For an LIF neuron, the spike output is a Heaviside function of the membrane potential:

$$S(t) = \Theta\big(V(t) - V_{\text{th}}\big), \qquad \frac{d\Theta}{dx} = \delta(x)$$

The derivative is a Dirac delta — zero almost everywhere, infinite at threshold. *Standard backpropagation cannot flow through a spike.* Three families of solutions exist:

1. **ANN-to-SNN conversion.** Train a ReLU network, map firing rates to activations. Near-ANN accuracy on ImageNet-class tasks, but hundreds of timesteps per inference sacrifice the temporal efficiency that motivates SNNs.
2. **Local plasticity (STDP).** $\Delta w \propto \sum_{f,g} W(t_{\text{post}}^g - t_{\text{pre}}^f)$ with asymmetric window $W(\Delta t) = A_+ e^{-\Delta t/\tau_+}$ for $\Delta t > 0$ (LTP), $-A_- e^{\Delta t/\tau_-}$ for $\Delta t < 0$ (LTD). Elegant and local, but unsupervised STDP has never scaled to deep supervised benchmarks.
3. **Surrogate gradients.** Replace $\Theta'(x)$ in the backward pass with a smooth surrogate $\sigma'(x)$ — e.g., the fast sigmoid $\sigma'(x) = 1/(1 + \beta|x|)^2$ — while keeping the hard threshold in the forward pass [5]. This restores backpropagation-through-time over the unrolled SNN.

### 5.2 Surrogate Gradients: Theory and Practice

Surrogate learning is *straight-through estimation in time*. In discrete time:

$$V[t+1] = \alpha V[t] + (1-\alpha)\, I[t] - V_{\text{th}}\, S[t], \qquad S[t] = \Theta(V[t] - V_{\text{th}})$$

Unrolled over $T$ timesteps, the SNN is a recurrent network with tied weights. BPTT differentiates the smooth parts exactly and hits the nondifferentiable $\Theta$ at each spike; the surrogate $\sigma'(V[t] - V_{\text{th}})$ concentrates credit assignment near threshold crossings — precisely where the neuron's decision is most sensitive.

> **Theorem:** *Consistency of surrogate gradients.* For a stochastic spiking neuron emitting spikes with probability $p(V) = \sigma(V - V_{\text{th}})$ for smooth $\sigma$, the surrogate gradient equals the exact gradient of the expected loss with respect to the membrane potential. Deterministic surrogate training is the zero-temperature limit of exact gradient descent on a well-defined stochastic objective — formalized in recent theoretical work [8].

Empirically, surrogate-trained deep SNNs report **>99% on N-MNIST, ~95% on DVS-CIFAR10, and competitive results on the SHD/SSC neuromorphic audio benchmarks**, with 4–16 timesteps per inference — two orders of magnitude fewer than conversion approaches [5]. On Loihi 2, surrogate-trained networks perform keyword spotting at sub-milliwatt power [7]. The gap to ANNs on static ImageNet remains (~5–10 points), but on *event-native* data — DVS streams, silicon cochlea output — SNNs are increasingly the superior architecture.

```python
import torch

def lif_step(V, I, alpha=0.9, V_th=1.0, beta=5.0):
    """One discrete-time LIF step with surrogate gradient (fast sigmoid)."""
    V = alpha * V + (1 - alpha) * I          # leaky integration
    S = (V >= V_th).float()                  # hard spike: forward only
    # straight-through surrogate in the backward pass
    sig = torch.sigmoid(beta * (V - V_th))
    S = S + (sig - sig.detach())             # gradient flows via sigmoid'
    V = V * (1 - S)                          # reset-after-spike
    return V, S

# BPTT over T timesteps: loss.backward() flows through lif_step
# via the surrogate derivative beta*sigmoid*(1-sigmoid) — spikes stay binary.
```

### 5.3 Energy Formalism

The energy advantage admits a crisp formalization. Let $E_{\text{MAC}}$ be one multiply-accumulate on a GPU ($\approx 20$ pJ at 7 nm) and $E_{\text{spike}}$ the energy of routing and integrating one spike on a neuromorphic chip ($\approx 10$–$100$ pJ on TrueNorth/Loihi, dominated by SRAM access). For $N$ neurons, fan-out $F$, mean rate $r$, window $T$:

$$E_{\text{SNN}} = N \cdot F \cdot r \cdot T \cdot E_{\text{spike}}, \qquad E_{\text{ANN}} = N \cdot F \cdot E_{\text{MAC}}$$

The SNN wins when $rT \cdot E_{\text{spike}} < E_{\text{MAC}}$ — when the *spike count per neuron per inference* $rT$ is small. With temporal coding ($rT \approx 1$), parity is immediate; the real victories come from *sparsity*: event-driven sensors yield activity ratios of $10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}$, where neuromorphic chips beat GPUs by $100$–$1000\times$ [2,7]. The formalism also exposes the failure mode: dense rate-coded SNNs at 100 Hz over 100 timesteps ($rT = 10^4$) lose to GPUs by definition.

## 6 Limitations

Honesty requires stating where the field stands.

**Precision and mismatch.** Analog systems trade determinism for efficiency; transistor mismatch demands per-neuron calibration, and temperature drift can invalidate parameters mid-experiment. Digital systems are deterministic but quantize weights to 1–9 bits — a constraint training must absorb.

**The programmability gap.** Mapping a network onto neurosynaptic cores is a *graph embedding problem* — NP-hard in general. Corelet toolchains, NxSDK, and PyNN support [9] raised the abstraction level, but none approaches the push-button maturity of PyTorch-to-GPU compilation. A brilliant architecture that takes a PhD student six months to program will not displace CUDA.

**Benchmark parity.** On static benchmarks (ImageNet, GLUE), SNNs trail ANNs; the field's response — that SNNs are for *event-native* workloads — is correct but concedes the largest commercial markets. Until a neuromorphic system shows an unambiguous, reproducible, order-of-magnitude win on a workload industry cares about (always-on sensing is the leading candidate), neuromorphic computing remains a research program rather than a product category.

**Learning theory gaps.** Surrogate gradients work empirically, but their theory is young [8]: we lack convergence guarantees for deep spiking networks comparable to smooth nonconvex optimization, and the interaction of surrogate bias with hardware quantization is poorly understood.

## 7 Conclusion

Three decades after Mead's provocation, neuromorphic computing has matured into a disciplined engineering science. **TrueNorth** proved million-neuron digital spiking arrays can run on milliwatts [1]. **Loihi** proved programmable on-chip plasticity and asynchronous event-driven design can learn in the field [2]. **BrainScaleS** proved analog emulation at $10^4\times$ acceleration opens experimental regimes inaccessible to biology itself [3,4]. And **surrogate gradients** proved the spike — long considered the enemy of gradient descent — can be tamed by the oldest trick in optimization: replace the hard thing with a smooth thing, but only where the derivative is needed [5,8].

The through-line is *co-design*: neuron equations, learning rules, interconnect protocols, and silicon substrates designed jointly. The brain remains the existence proof that the design space contains a $10^{16}$-SOPS, 20-Watt solution. The task of the next decade is to find more of it in silicon.
