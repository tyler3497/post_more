# Fine-Grained Complexity and the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis: Orthogonal Vectors, Conditional Lower Bounds, and the Quadratic-Time Barrier for Problems in P

## Abstract

The Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH), introduced by Impagliazzo and Paturi and grounded in the work of Impagliazzo, Paturi, and Zane, asserts that the naive $O^*(2^n)$ algorithm for $k$-SAT is essentially optimal as $k \to \infty$. While SETH was originally conceived to explain the hardness of NP-complete problems, its most striking modern application lies inside P: through the **Orthogonal Vectors (OV) conjecture** and the machinery of **fine-grained reductions**, SETH now supplies the first convincing explanation for why textbook polynomial-time problems — edit distance, longest common subsequence, Fréchet distance, graph diameter, and all-pairs shortest paths — resist genuinely faster algorithms after decades of effort. This thesis develops the full theory: the definition of SETH and the sparsification lemma, Williams' reduction from CNF-SAT to OV, the $k$-OV hierarchy, the Abboud–Backurs–Vassilevska Williams reduction web, the nondeterministic variant NSETH and its nonreducibility barrier, and the delicate interplay between refuting SETH and proving circuit lower bounds. We prove representative lower bounds, survey the empirical record of reductions, and delineate the fundamental limitations of the conditional-hardness paradigm.

## 1 Introduction

Classical complexity theory, anchored in $P \ne NP$, tells us *that* certain problems are hard — it is silent on *how hard*. Nothing in $P \ne NP$ rules out a $2^{o(n)}$-time algorithm for 3-SAT. For polynomial-time problems the theory is even more impoverished: the dynamic programs for **edit distance** and **longest common subsequence (LCS)** have run in $\Theta(n^2)$ time since the 1970s, yet $P \ne NP$ offers no evidence that the quadratic barrier is real rather than an artifact of imagination [2]. Do genuinely subquadratic algorithms exist, hiding just behind better ideas — or is there a principled reason the exponents we observe are optimal?

*Fine-grained complexity* replaces $P \ne NP$ with precise quantitative hypotheses. The two most central are the **Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH)** of Impagliazzo and Paturi [1] and the **Orthogonal Vectors (OV) conjecture**: no $O(n^{2-\varepsilon})$-time algorithm for OV on $n$ binary vectors of dimension $\omega(\log n)$.

This thesis gives a self-contained account of the theory at full research depth: the precise formulation of SETH, the sparsification lemma, and the evidence for and against it (§2–§3); the Orthogonal Vectors conjecture, Williams' SAT-to-OV reduction, the $k$-OV hierarchy, and the subcubic APSP program (§4); the Abboud–Backurs–Vassilevska Williams reduction web with a worked proof sketch (§4–§5); the nondeterministic refinement **NSETH**, its nonreducibility consequences, and the refutation–versus–lower-bound duality (§4.5); and a candid assessment of limitations and open problems (§6–§7).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Exponential-time hypotheses

Let $s_k = \inf\{\delta : \text{$k$-SAT is solvable in $O^*(2^{\delta n})$ time}\}$, where $O^*$ suppresses polynomial factors. The sequence $\{s_k\}$ is nondecreasing. The **Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH)** of Impagliazzo and Paturi states that $s_3 > 0$; the **Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH)** states the far stronger claim

> **Hypothesis (SETH):** $\lim_{k\to\infty} s_k = 1.$ Equivalently: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $k \ge 3$ such that $k$-SAT on $n$ variables admits no $O(2^{(1-\varepsilon)n})$-time algorithm [1][8].

The fastest known $k$-SAT algorithms run in $O(2^{(1-c/k)n})$ time, converging to the trivial $2^n$ bound exactly as SETH predicts. The key technical tool making SETH usable is the **sparsification lemma** of Impagliazzo, Paturi, and Zane: for each fixed $k$ and $\varepsilon > 0$, a $k$-CNF formula on $n$ variables becomes, in $O^*(2^{\varepsilon n})$ time, a disjunction of at most $2^{\varepsilon n}$ formulas each with $O(n)$ clauses [1]. Clause count may thus be assumed linear in variable count — the starting point of nearly every fine-grained reduction from SAT.

### 2.2 The Orthogonal Vectors problem and conjecture

In the **Orthogonal Vectors (OV)** problem we are given $A, B \subseteq \{0,1\}^d$ with $|A| = |B| = n$, and must decide whether some $a \in A$, $b \in B$ satisfy $\langle a, b \rangle = 0$. The trivial algorithm takes $O(n^2 d)$ time; improved algorithms reach $n^{2 - 1/O(\log(d/\log n))}$, but no truly subquadratic algorithm is known.

> **Conjecture (OVC):** For every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a constant $c = c(\varepsilon) > 0$ such that OV on $n$ vectors in $\{0,1\}^d$ with $d = c \log n$ admits no $O(n^{2-\varepsilon})$-time algorithm.

It is a celebrated theorem of R. Williams that **SETH implies OVC** [7]: the reduction splits a $k$-CNF formula on $n$ variables into two halves of $n/2$ variables each, enumerates all $2^{n/2}$ partial assignments as binary vectors whose coordinates encode clause satisfaction, and shows that the formula is satisfiable iff two orthogonal vectors exist. A $O(n^{2-\varepsilon})$-time OV algorithm would then yield a $O^*(2^{(1-\varepsilon/2)n})$-time SAT algorithm, refuting SETH.

### 2.3 Fine-grained reductions

A **fine-grained reduction** from $P$ to $Q$ converts any $O(n^{b-\varepsilon})$-time algorithm for $Q$ into an $O(n^{a-\delta})$-time algorithm for $P$, propagating hardness from exponent $a$ to exponent $b$. Introduced by Vassilevska Williams and Williams for the subcubic setting [4] and extended to the quadratic setting by Abboud, Backurs, and Vassilevska Williams [2], the paradigm turns isolated hardness results into *equivalence classes* of problems sharing a common conjectured barrier.

### 2.4 The classical baselines

The problems at stake are among the most studied in all of computer science:

| Problem | Classical algorithm | Running time | Best known |
|---|---|---|---|
| Edit distance | Wagner–Fischer DP | $\Theta(n^2)$ | $n^{2}/2^{\Omega(\sqrt{\log n})}$ |
| LCS | Hunt–Szymanski DP | $\Theta(n^2)$ | $n^{2}/2^{\Omega(\sqrt{\log n})}$ |
| Fréchet distance (discrete) | Eiter–Mannila DP | $\Theta(n^2)$ | $n^{2}/\log^{O(1)} n$ |
| Graph diameter (unweighted) | BFS from each vertex | $\Theta(nm)$ | $\tilde{O}(m\sqrt{n})$ approx. |
| APSP | Floyd–Warshall | $\Theta(n^3)$ | $n^3/2^{\Omega(\sqrt{\log n})}$ |

Every improvement in fifty years has been *subpolynomial* — polylogarithmic or $2^{O(\sqrt{\log n})}$ factors [6]. Fine-grained complexity is the theory that explains why.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is that of the fine-grained complexity program itself: we proceed by **conditional analysis**, assuming a small number of explicit quantitative hypotheses and deriving the tightest possible consequences through reductions whose efficiency we track precisely. Specifically:

1. **Hypothesis formalization.** SETH, OVC, the $k$-OV hierarchy, the 3SUM conjecture, and the APSP conjecture in their strongest standard forms, with known implications (SETH $\Rightarrow$ OVC).
2. **Reduction construction.** For each target problem we describe the gadget-based reduction from OV — how binary vectors become strings or point sets, and how orthogonality becomes a threshold on the target objective — verifying the *linear* blowup that transfers the $n^{2-\varepsilon}$ barrier intact.
3. **Tightness auditing.** Each lower bound $n^{c-o(1)}$ is compared against the best known upper bound; cases where they meet (edit distance, LCS) are marked *tight* [2].
4. **Meta-theoretic reflection.** We examine the hypotheses themselves: NSETH and nonreducibility [5], evidence from restricted models (resolution, branching programs), and the duality that *refuting* SETH or OVC yields faster algorithms or breakthrough circuit lower bounds.

We do not assume the hypotheses are true; we map the exact web of consequences under each combination of truth values.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 SETH, the sparsification lemma, and the structure of $s_k$

The sparsification lemma deserves its name: it converts a dense $k$-CNF formula into a *sparse* one, at the cost of an arbitrarily small additive $\varepsilon$ in the exponent. Formally:

> **Theorem (Sparsification Lemma [1]):** For each $\varepsilon > 0$ and fixed $k$, there is an $O^*(2^{\varepsilon n})$ algorithm mapping a $k$-CNF formula $F$ on $n$ variables to formulas $F_1, \dots, F_t$ with $t \le 2^{\varepsilon n}$, each on the same $n$ variables with $O(n)$ clauses, such that $F$ is satisfiable iff some $F_i$ is.

This lemma makes SETH *robust*: $s_3 > 0$ iff $s_k > 0$ for some $k \ge 3$, and reductions may assume $m = O(n)$ clauses. Impagliazzo and Paturi showed $\{s_k\}$ increases infinitely often under ETH, and Cygan et al. proved SETH equivalent to analogous hypotheses for Hitting Set, Set Splitting, and NAE-$k$-SAT — evidence it captures something intrinsic rather than a CNF artifact [6].

*Why believe SETH?* The strongest evidence is **upper-bound stagnation**: after decades of SAT research, the best $k$-SAT exponent is $1 - \Theta(1/k)$, converging to 1 exactly as predicted. Beck and Impagliazzo showed the resolution proof system underlying modern CDCL solvers cannot refute SETH. The counterpoint is conceptual: SETH asserts the optimality of *brute force*, and any genuinely new algorithmic idea for SAT would refute it.

### 4.2 The Orthogonal Vectors conjecture and Williams' reduction

Williams' reduction from CNF-SAT to OV is the keystone of the quadratic-time program. Given a $k$-CNF formula $\phi$ on $n$ variables with $m = O(n)$ clauses (by sparsification), split variables into halves $X_1, X_2$. For each partial assignment $\alpha$ to $X_1$ define $v_\alpha \in \{0,1\}^m$ with coordinate $j$ set iff clause $C_j$ is **not** satisfied by $\alpha$; define $w_\beta$ analogously for $X_2$. Then $\langle v_\alpha, w_\beta \rangle = 0$ iff $\alpha \cup \beta$ satisfies $\phi$. The families have $N = 2^{n/2}$ vectors in dimension $O(\log N)$.

> **Theorem (Williams [7]):** If OV on $N$ vectors in dimension $c \log N$ is solvable in $O(N^{2-\varepsilon})$ time for some $\varepsilon > 0$, then $k$-SAT is solvable in $O^*(2^{(1-\varepsilon/2)n})$ time for all sufficiently large $k$, refuting SETH.

Consequently SETH implies the Orthogonal Vectors conjecture. The reduction is *fine-grained*: the instance size $N$ is $2^{n/2}$ and the dimension is logarithmic in $N$, so the $n^{2-\varepsilon}$ barrier transfers with a precise quantitative link between the OV speedup and the SAT speedup.

### 4.3 The $k$-OV hierarchy and higher exponents

Generalizing OV, the **$k$-OV problem** asks, given $k$ families $A_1, \dots, A_k \subseteq \{0,1\}^d$ of $n$ vectors each, whether there exist $a_i \in A_i$ with $\bigwedge_{j=1}^d \bigvee_{i=1}^k \lnot a_i[j]$ — a $k$-wise orthogonal tuple. The naive algorithm runs in $O(n^k d)$ time, and the **$k$-OV conjecture** asserts no $O(n^{k-\varepsilon})$ algorithm exists for $d = c \log n$. SETH implies the full hierarchy: the same splitting trick with $k$ parts of $n/k$ variables reduces $k$-SAT to $k$-OV.

The hierarchy yields lower bounds at *arbitrary polynomial exponents*: $k$-LCS requires $n^{k-o(1)}$ time under SETH [2], as does $k$-dominating set. The $k$-OV hierarchy is the fine-grained analogue of the $W$-hierarchy in parameterized complexity — one conjecture per exponent, all implied by a single hypothesis about satisfiability. In the *low-dimensional* regime ($d = c\log n$ for small $c$) the SETH implication does not apply and $O(1.35^d n)$-time algorithms exist [6]; the boundary between the algorithmic and conjecturally-hard regimes remains poorly understood.

### 4.4 The Abboud–Backurs–Vassilevska Williams reduction web

The landmark FOCS 2015 paper of Abboud, Backurs, and Vassilevska Williams [2] showed that the classic sequence-similarity problems are *OV-hard*:

> **Theorem (Abboud–Backurs–Vassilevska Williams [2]):** Under SETH, there is no $O(n^{2-\varepsilon})$ algorithm for edit distance, LCS, or dynamic time warping on strings of length $n$ over a constant-size alphabet, for any $\varepsilon > 0$.

The reduction encodes each vector $a \in A$ as a *gadget string* $G(a)$ and each $b \in B$ as $H(b)$, concatenating all gadgets with separator symbols. Orthogonality of a pair $(a,b)$ is made equivalent to the edit distance (or LCS length) between the corresponding gadget pair crossing a threshold, and the separators force any optimal alignment to respect gadget boundaries. Because the encoding is linear in $n \cdot d$ and the threshold gap is constant, an $O(n^{2-\varepsilon})$ algorithm for edit distance would decide OV in $O(n^{2-\varepsilon'})$ time.

Abboud, Hansen, Vassilevska Williams, and Williams extended the program in [3] by simulating *branching programs* with edit distance: any size-$S$ branching program can be encoded so that edit distance evaluates it. Corollary: **shaving even a polylog factor** off edit distance beyond known improvements would yield new circuit lower bounds — "a polylog shaved is a lower bound made." Bringmann and Künnemann proved the discrete Fréchet distance requires $n^{2-o(1)}$ time under SETH [6]. The web now spans string matching, regex matching, CFG parsing, graph diameter, and radius — all rooted at OV.

### 4.5 NSETH, nonreducibility, and the refutation duality

Carmosino, Gao, Impagliazzo, Mihajlin, Paturi, and Schneider introduced **NSETH** [5]: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $k$ such that $k$-TAUT has no $O(2^{(1-\varepsilon)n})$-time *nondeterministic* algorithm. Its power lies in a *nonreducibility* theorem:

> **Theorem (Carmosino et al. [5]):** Under NSETH, there is no deterministic (or zero-error probabilistic) fine-grained reduction from $k$-SAT to 3SUM, to APSP, or to model checking of first-order graph properties.

In other words, the absence of SETH-hardness for 3SUM and APSP is itself *explained*: these problems cannot be SETH-hard via the standard deterministic reductions unless NSETH fails. This justifies the field's use of three independent conjectures — SETH/OVC, 3SUM, APSP — rather than one: they inhabit provably distinct hardness worlds (modulo NSETH).

The deepest meta-result is the **refutation duality**. Williams proved that a truly subquadratic OV algorithm implies new *circuit lower bounds* ($E^{NP}$ lacks $2^{o(n)}$-size depth-2 threshold circuits, among others). Gao, Impagliazzo, Kolokolova, and Williams [9] extended this: falsifying OVC even in the low-dimensional regime yields nonuniform lower bounds against $ACC$ and related classes. The program is thus win–win: either the barriers hold and dozens of problems are provably stuck, or they fall and complexity theory gains historic circuit lower bounds.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We sketch the representative lower bound — edit distance requires $n^{2-o(1)}$ time under SETH — following [2][3], then summarize the field's empirical record in a table.

**Proof sketch (OV $\le$ Edit Distance).** Let $A, B \subseteq \{0,1\}^d$ be an OV instance with $|A|=|B|=n$. For a vector $a$, define the coordinate gadget

```python
def coord_gadget(bit_a, bit_b):
    # Returns the pair of symbols whose edit cost encodes
    # whether bit_a AND bit_b == 1 (non-orthogonal at this coordinate)
    if bit_a == 1 and bit_b == 1:
        return ("X", "Y")   # expensive to align: cost 1
    return ("X", "X")       # free to align: cost 0
```

Concatenating coordinate gadgets with unique separators yields strings $S_a, T_b$ of length $O(d)$ with $ed(S_a, T_b)$ equal (up to a constant) to the Hamming weight of $a \land b$ — zero iff $a \perp b$. Form $S = \bigcirc_{a \in A} S_a$ and $T = \bigcirc_{b \in B} T_b$ with separator blocks forcing any optimal alignment to match exactly one gadget pair $(S_a, T_b)$, minimized precisely when that pair is orthogonal. The construction has $|S|,|T| = O(nd)$, constant alphabet, and linear build time.

If edit distance on length-$N$ strings were solvable in $O(N^{2-\varepsilon})$ time, the OV instance would be decided in $O((nd)^{2-\varepsilon}) = O(n^{2-\varepsilon} \cdot \mathrm{poly}(d))$ time; for $d = O(\log n)$ this is $O(n^{2-\varepsilon'})$ for some $\varepsilon' > 0$, contradicting OVC and hence SETH. ∎

The bound is **tight**: Wagner–Fischer runs in $O(n^2)$, so the $n^{2-o(1)}$ lower bound meets the upper bound — the first *tight* conditional lower bound for a problem in P that resisted unconditional analysis for fifty years. The same gadget architecture yields:

| Target problem | Lower bound (under SETH) | Best upper bound | Tight? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Edit distance | $n^{2-o(1)}$ [2] | $O(n^2)$ | Yes |
| LCS (binary alphabet) | $n^{2-o(1)}$ [2] | $O(n^2)$ | Yes |
| Discrete Fréchet distance | $n^{2-o(1)}$ [6] | $O(n^2)$ | Yes |
| $k$-LCS | $n^{k-o(1)}$ [2] | $O(n^k)$ | Yes |
| Graph diameter (sparse) | $n^{2-o(1)}$ (Roditty–Vassilevska Williams) | $O(nm)$ | Yes (sparse) |
| Dynamic time warping | $n^{2-o(1)}$ [2] | $O(n^2)$ | Yes |
| Regular-expression matching | $n^{2-o(1)}$ | $O(nm)$ | Essentially |
| APSP | $n^{3-o(1)}$ (APSP conj.) [4] | $O(n^3)$ | Yes |

The **subcubic program**: Vassilevska Williams and Williams [4] defined *subcubic reducibility* and proved APSP, negative-triangle detection, replacement paths, radius, and a dozen other cubic-time problems *equivalent* — either all admit $O(n^{3-\delta})$ algorithms or none do. Williams' 2014 $n^3/2^{\Omega(\sqrt{\log n})}$ APSP algorithm via circuit complexity remains the only super-polylogarithmic improvement, still short of the cubic barrier.

**Empirical pattern.** Across more than a decade of fine-grained reductions, *no* lower bound has ever been violated by a subsequent algorithm — a perfect track record. The exponents cluster exactly at the naive-algorithm exponents ($n^2$, $n^3$, $n^k$), precisely as the hypotheses predict; the reductions themselves are unconditional mathematics, with the hypothesis as the only assumption.

## 6 Limitations

The conditional-hardness paradigm has sharp, well-documented limitations that any honest account must confront.

**1. The hypotheses might be false.** SETH asserts the optimality of brute force — the boldest claim in complexity theory. One algorithmic breakthrough for $k$-SAT would collapse the edifice: every SETH-based bound would evaporate. Williams' own APSP breakthrough [4] came from *circuit complexity*, a direction nobody expected to yield faster shortest paths.

**2. Conditionality is not explanation.** A reduction from OV to edit distance shows that *if* OV is hard *then* edit distance is hard — not that OV is hard. The theory explains the *relative* structure of hardness, not its absolute origin; unconditional bounds in general models remain far out of reach.

**3. The NSETH barrier cuts both ways.** Under NSETH, 3SUM and APSP cannot be shown SETH-hard by deterministic reductions [5], forcing *multiple* independent conjectures (SETH, 3SUM, APSP) of unknown relationship. A fast 3SUM algorithm would leave the SETH and APSP programs untouched — the web is less unified than one might hope.

**4. Approximation and parameterization escape.** The bounds are for *exact* worst-case computation. Approximation algorithms routinely beat them: edit distance admits $O(\mathrm{polylog}\, n)$-approximations in near-linear time, and diameter admits $2$-approximations in $\tilde{O}(m\sqrt{n})$ time. Read "$n^{2-o(1)}$ under SETH" as exact worst-case complexity, not practical solvability.

**5. Model sensitivity.** The conjectures target the Word RAM with $O(\log n)$-bit words. Stronger models shift or erase the barriers; the "truly subquadratic" quantifiers hide polylogarithmic factors that matter enormously in practice.

**6. Fine-grained reductions are lossy.** Reductions preserve exponents only up to $o(1)$ and need technical conditions like $d = \omega(\log n)$; exact threshold behavior at $d = c \log n$ is not pinned down, and the low-dimensional regime admits nontrivial algorithms [6].

## 7 Conclusion

Fine-grained complexity has accomplished something classical complexity theory never could: it has given *quantitative, falsifiable, and so far unfalsified* explanations for the running times of the most basic algorithms in computer science. The chain of reasoning — from the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis through the sparsification lemma, Williams' reduction to Orthogonal Vectors, the $k$-OV hierarchy, and the Abboud–Backurs–Vassilevska Williams reduction web — converts a single bold conjecture about satisfiability into tight $n^{2-o(1)}$, $n^{3-o(1)}$, and $n^{k-o(1)}$ lower bounds for edit distance, LCS, Fréchet distance, diameter, APSP, and dozens more problems [1][2][3][4]. The nondeterministic refinement NSETH [5] explains why 3SUM and APSP stand apart, and the refutation duality guarantees that disproving the hypotheses would yield circuit lower bounds of historic significance [9].

The central open questions: *first*, can SETH/OVC, 3SUM, and APSP be unified, or does NSETH separate them permanently? *Second*, what is the true complexity of OV at $d = c \log n$, where algorithms and lower bounds meet only asymptotically? *Third*, can "a polylog shaved is a lower bound made" [3] become an unconditional bound? *Fourth*, does SETH survive — or will the next great idea do to $k$-SAT what Williams did to APSP?

Whatever the answers, the methodology is permanent: explicit-exponent hypotheses, exponent-preserving reductions, equivalence classes organizing our ignorance. If the barriers fall, we gain algorithms — or circuit lower bounds. If they stand, we finally understand *why* the textbook algorithms are optimal. In either world, the investigation pays for itself.
