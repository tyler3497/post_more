# Computational Optimal Transport: Kantorovich Geometry, Entropic Regularization, and Algorithmic Scaling from Sinkhorn Iterations to Gromov–Wasserstein

## Abstract

Optimal transport (OT) provides a geometrically faithful framework for comparing probability measures, interpolating between distributions, and constructing losses that remain meaningful when supports are disjoint or low-dimensional. This thesis develops the computational theory of optimal transport from the classical Monge–Kantorovich formulation through the modern algorithmic breakthroughs that made it practical: entropic regularization and the Sinkhorn algorithm [1], near-linear-time approximation theory [5], and the Gromov–Wasserstein extension to unaligned metric spaces [3][4]. We give precise statements of Kantorovich duality and Brenier's theorem, derive the Sinkhorn iteration as block-coordinate ascent on the dual, and analyze its convergence through the Hilbert projective metric and modern approximation guarantees. We then examine sliced, unbalanced, and Gromov–Wasserstein variants, and demonstrate how OT losses reshaped generative modeling [6] and domain adaptation. A numerical study contrasts exact linear programming against entropic schemes on synthetic mixtures, quantifying the accuracy–speed trade-off as a function of the regularization strength. We close by delineating fundamental limitations: the curse of dimensionality in sample complexity, the bias induced by entropic smoothing, numerical instability in the small-regularization regime, and the NP-hardness of exact Gromov–Wasserstein computation.

## 1 Introduction

Few problems in mathematics have been asked as early, answered as partially, and revived as completely as the problem of *optimal transport*. In 1781, Gaspard Monge asked how to move a pile of sand into a prescribed excavation with minimal work — the first variational formulation of a transportation problem. Two centuries later, Leonid Kantorovich relaxed Monge's deterministic transport maps into probabilistic *couplings*, earning the 1975 Nobel Prize in Economics and founding the linear-programming duality perspective that underlies everything that follows.

For most of the twentieth century, optimal transport lived primarily as analysis: the Monge–Ampère equation, the geometry of the Wasserstein space, and the celebrated results of Brenier, McCann, and Ambrosio–Gigli–Savaré. The *computational* renaissance began only in the 2010s, driven by machine learning's need for a discrepancy between probability measures that is (i) geometrically meaningful, (ii) well-defined even when distributions have disjoint supports, and (iii) cheap enough to embed in a training loop. The pivotal moment was Cuturi's 2013 introduction of *Sinkhorn distances* [1]: by adding an entropic penalty to the Kantorovich program, the linear program collapses into a matrix-scaling problem solvable by the Sinkhorn–Knopp fixed-point iteration at several orders of magnitude lower cost. The comprehensive synthesis of Peyré and Cuturi [2] then organized the entire field — duality, regularization, gradient flows, barycenters — into a single computational edifice.

This thesis reconstructs that edifice with algorithmic precision. Our contributions are expository but substantive:

- A self-contained derivation of the Kantorovich primal and dual, with the c-transform and the Brenier–McCann characterization of optimal maps (§2).
- The entropic regularization of the transport program, the closed-form structure of the optimal coupling, and the Sinkhorn iteration derived as dual block-coordinate ascent (§3).
- Convergence analysis: Hilbert-metric contraction, the Franklin–Lorenz log-domain rates, and the near-linear-time approximation guarantees of Altschuler, Weed, and Rigollet, including the Greenkhorn greedy variant (§4.1).
- Scalable surrogates: sliced Wasserstein distances (§4.2), unbalanced transport with Kullback–Leibler relaxation of marginal constraints (§4.3), and the Gromov–Wasserstein distance between metric measure spaces [3][4] with its entropic and sliced variants (§4.4).
- Wasserstein barycenters as Fréchet means under the 2-Wasserstein metric (§4.5).
- Applications: Wasserstein GANs [6], OT-based domain adaptation, and minibatch formulations.
- An empirical study with reproducible code comparing exact and entropic solvers (§5), and a candid account of limitations (§6).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Monge and Kantorovich problems

Let $\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{Y}$ be Polish spaces and $\mu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})$, $\nu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})$ probability measures. Given a lower-semicontinuous cost $c: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y} \to [0, \infty)$, **Monge's problem** seeks a measurable map $T: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ pushing $\mu$ forward to $\nu$ ($T_{\sharp}\mu = \nu$) minimizing

$$\inf_{T: T_{\sharp}\mu = \nu} \int_{\mathcal{X}} c(x, T(x)) \, d\mu(x). \tag{MP}$$

This problem is fragile: it may admit no feasible map (mass cannot split), and it is non-convex in $T$. **Kantorovich's relaxation** (1942) replaces deterministic maps by *transport plans* — couplings $\pi \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y})$ with marginals $\mu$ and $\nu$:

$$\inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu, \nu)} \int_{\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}} c(x, y) \, d\pi(x, y), \tag{KP}$$

where $\Pi(\mu, \nu)$ is the set of all couplings. (KP) is a linear program over an infinite-dimensional convex set; under mild conditions it always attains a minimizer, and its value defines the **Wasserstein distances**: for $c(x,y) = d(x,y)^p$,

$$W_p(\mu, \nu) = \left(\inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu, \nu)} \int d(x,y)^p \, d\pi\right)^{1/p}.$$

*Why the coupling view matters.* It permits mass splitting, restores convexity, and — crucially for computation — admits a dual formulation with only two potential functions.

### 2.2 Kantorovich duality

> **Theorem: Kantorovich Duality.** Let $c$ be lower-semicontinuous and bounded below. Then
>
> $$\min_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int c \, d\pi = \max_{(f, g) \in \Phi_c} \int_{\mathcal{X}} f \, d\mu + \int_{\mathcal{Y}} g \, d\nu,$$
>
> where $\Phi_c = \{(f, g): f(x) + g(y) \le c(x,y)\ \forall x, y\}$. The maximum is attained, and the constraints can be restricted to *c-concave* potentials related by the c-transform $f^c(y) = \inf_x \{c(x,y) - f(x)\}$.

For $c(x,y) = d(x,y)$ ($p = 1$), the dual collapses to the Kantorovich–Rubinstein formula over 1-Lipschitz functions:

$$W_1(\mu, \nu) = \sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}} \le 1} \int f \, d\mu - \int f \, d\nu. \tag{KR}$$

This identity is the mathematical engine of Wasserstein GANs [6]: the critic approximates the supremum over 1-Lipschitz functions.

### 2.3 Brenier's theorem and displacement interpolation

> **Theorem: Brenier (1991).** Let $\mu$ be absolutely continuous w.r.t. Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^d$ with finite second moment, $c(x,y) = \|x - y\|^2$. Then the optimal coupling for $W_2$ is unique and induced by a map $T = \nabla \varphi$ for some convex $\varphi$: $\pi^* = (\mathrm{id}, T)_{\sharp} \mu$.

Moreover, McCann's *displacement interpolation* $\mu_t = ((1-t)\mathrm{id} + tT)_{\sharp}\mu$, $t \in [0,1]$, is the constant-speed geodesic in the Wasserstein space $\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d)$. This geometric structure — geodesics, barycenters, gradient flows — is what distinguishes OT from divergences like KL: it metrizes weak convergence and lifts the ground geometry to the space of measures.

### 2.4 The statistical curse of dimensionality

Before algorithms, a warning from statistics. The empirical measure $\hat{\mu}_n$ converges to $\mu$ in $W_p$ at rate $\mathbb{E}[W_p(\hat{\mu}_n, \mu)] \asymp n^{-1/d}$ for $d \ge 3$ (Dudley, 1969), up to the $n^{-1/2}$ rate in low dimensions. In high dimensions, *any* estimator of the Wasserstein distance needs exponentially many samples — a fundamental sample-complexity barrier that motivates regularized and sliced variants (§4.2–4.3), whose sample complexity can be dimension-independent [2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Entropic regularization

The discrete Kantorovich problem — with histograms $a \in \Delta_n$, $b \in \Delta_m$ and cost matrix $C \in \mathbb{R}_+^{n \times m}$ — is the linear program

$$\min_{P \in U(a,b)} \langle P, C \rangle, \qquad U(a,b) = \{P \ge 0 : P\mathbf{1}_m = a,\ P^\top \mathbf{1}_n = b\}. \tag{LP}$$

Exact solvers (network simplex, Hungarian for the assignment case) cost $O(n^3 \log n)$ — prohibitive beyond a few thousand points. Cuturi [1] proposed smoothing (LP) with the negative entropy $H(P) = -\sum_{ij} P_{ij} \log P_{ij}$:

$$\min_{P \in U(a,b)} \langle P, C \rangle - \varepsilon H(P), \qquad \varepsilon > 0. \tag{EOT}$$

The strictly convex objective admits a unique minimizer with a remarkable closed form. Forming the Lagrangian with dual variables $f \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $g \in \mathbb{R}^m$ and differentiating,

> **Theorem: Structure of the entropic coupling.** The unique solution of (EOT) is
>
> $$P^*_{ij} = u_i K_{ij} v_j, \qquad K_{ij} = e^{-C_{ij}/\varepsilon},$$
>
> where $u, v > 0$ are scaling vectors determined (up to a multiplicative constant) by the marginal constraints $P^*\mathbf{1}_m = a$, ${P^*}^\top \mathbf{1}_n = b$.

That is, the optimal plan is a *diagonal scaling* of the Gibbs kernel $K$. The marginal constraints become the matrix-scaling equations $u \odot (Kv) = a$, $v \odot (K^\top u) = b$, solved by alternating normalization — the **Sinkhorn–Knopp iteration**:

$$u^{(\ell+1)} = \frac{a}{K v^{(\ell)}}, \qquad v^{(\ell+1)} = \frac{b}{K^\top u^{(\ell+1)}}. \tag{Sinkhorn}$$

Each iteration costs $O(nm)$ matrix–vector products; convergence is linear in the Hilbert projective metric (see §4.1), yielding effectively $O(n^2)$ algorithms — *lightspeed* relative to $O(n^3)$ LP solvers [1].

### 3.2 The dual perspective

Substituting $P_{ij} = u_i K_{ij} v_j$ into the Lagrangian gives the unconstrained smooth dual

$$\max_{f, g} \ \langle f, a \rangle + \langle g, b \rangle - \varepsilon \sum_{ij} e^{(f_i + g_j - C_{ij})/\varepsilon} + \varepsilon, \tag{D}$$

and the Sinkhorn updates are exactly **block-coordinate ascent** on (D): each row- or column-normalization optimizes one potential while fixing the other. This viewpoint explains differentiability — the map $(a, b, C) \mapsto P^*$ is smooth — which is why entropic OT slots into deep-learning pipelines as a *differentiable layer* [2].

### 3.3 Log-domain stabilization

For small $\varepsilon$, the kernel entries $e^{-C_{ij}/\varepsilon}$ underflow to zero in floating point. The standard remedy is the **log-domain** formulation: parametrize $u = e^{f/\varepsilon}$, $v = e^{g/\varepsilon}$ and evaluate the updates with stabilized log-sum-exp reductions. An equivalent and widely used alternative is performing the scaling in the log domain with periodic recentering. In practice, libraries such as POT (Python Optimal Transport) implement both the stabilized kernel iteration and a pure log-domain variant; the latter is mandatory when $\varepsilon \lesssim 10^{-2}$ relative to the cost scale.

### 3.4 Rounding to feasible couplings

Sinkhorn iterates produce a plan whose marginals satisfy the constraints only asymptotically. Altschuler et al. [5] give a rounding procedure that converts any approximately feasible matrix into $P \in U(a,b)$ while increasing the cost by at most a controlled amount — the key technical ingredient that turns Sinkhorn into a provable $\delta$-approximation algorithm for the *unregularized* problem (LP).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Convergence analysis of Sinkhorn: from Hilbert metrics to near-linear time

The classical analysis (Franklin–Lorenz, 1989) studies Sinkhorn as a contraction in the **Hilbert projective metric** $d_H(u, u') = \log \frac{\max_i u_i/u'_i}{\min_i u_i/u'_i}$. For a positive kernel $K$, each scaling step contracts $d_H$ by the Birkhoff factor

$$\kappa(K) = \frac{\sqrt{\eta(K)} - 1}{\sqrt{\eta(K)} + 1} < 1, \qquad \eta(K) = \max_{i,j,k,\ell} \frac{K_{ik}K_{j\ell}}{K_{jk}K_{i\ell}}.$$

> **Theorem: Linear convergence of Sinkhorn.** The iterates $u^{(\ell)}, v^{(\ell)}$ converge linearly to the unique scalings with rate $\kappa(K)^2$ per full sweep in the Hilbert metric, and the dual objective gap shrinks geometrically.

However, $\kappa(K) \to 1$ as $\varepsilon \to 0$ (since $K$ becomes nearly singular), so classical theory degrades precisely in the regime of interest. The breakthrough of Altschuler, Weed, and Rigollet [5] sidestepped iteration complexity and directly bounded the *total* work needed for a $\delta$-approximate solution of (LP):

> **Theorem: Near-linear-time OT approximation (Altschuler–Weed–Rigollet, 2017).** For cost matrix with $\|C\|_\infty \le L$, Sinkhorn with $\varepsilon = \delta/(4\log n)$ followed by the rounding scheme returns $P \in U(a,b)$ with $\langle P, C \rangle \le \min_{U(a,b)} \langle \cdot, C \rangle + \delta$ in $O(n^2 L^3 \log n / \delta^3)$ arithmetic operations.

This was the first proof that general OT distances admit *near-linear-time* approximation. The same paper introduced **Greenkhorn**, a greedy coordinate-descent variant that updates only the single most-violated row or column per step, achieving the same guarantee with substantially better empirical performance [5].

| Method | Per-iteration cost | Iterations for $\delta$-accuracy | Total complexity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Network simplex (exact) | — | — | $O(n^3 \log n)$ |
| Hungarian (assignment) | — | — | $O(n^3)$ |
| Sinkhorn [1] | $O(n^2)$ | $\tilde{O}(L^2/\delta^2)$ | $\tilde{O}(n^2 L^2/\delta^2)$ |
| Greenkhorn [5] | $O(n)$ | $\tilde{O}(n L^3/\delta^3)$ | $\tilde{O}(n^2 L^3/\delta^3)$ |
| Sliced $W_2$ (§4.2) | $O(n \log n)$ | $L$ projections | $O(Ln \log n + Ln d)$ |

The table's last row anticipates the next section: when even $O(n^2)$ is too slow, projections save the day.

### 4.2 Sliced Wasserstein distances

The 1-Wasserstein distance on the real line has a closed form via quantile functions: $W_p^p(\mu, \nu) = \int_0^1 |F_\mu^{-1}(t) - F_\nu^{-1}(t)|^p dt$, computable in $O(n \log n)$ by sorting. The **sliced Wasserstein distance** exploits this by averaging one-dimensional projections:

$$SW_p^p(\mu, \nu) = \int_{S^{d-1}} W_p^p(P_{\theta\sharp}\mu, P_{\theta\sharp}\nu) \, d\sigma(\theta), \tag{SW}$$

where $P_\theta(x) = \langle x, \theta \rangle$ and $\sigma$ is uniform on the sphere. Monte Carlo estimation with $L$ random directions costs $O(Ln \log n + Lnd)$ — dramatically cheaper than $O(n^2)$ Sinkhorn for large $n$. $SW_p$ is a genuine metric on $\mathcal{P}_p(\mathbb{R}^d)$, metrizes weak convergence, and — critically — its sample complexity is $O(n^{-1/2})$ *independent of dimension* [2]. The price is geometric: slicing discards cross-directional coupling structure, so $SW_p$ is a weaker discriminator of high-dimensional shape than $W_p$. Variants such as max-sliced and distributional sliced Wasserstein partially recover discriminative power.

### 4.3 Unbalanced optimal transport

Classical OT requires equal total mass — a poor fit for noisy data, partial matching, or measures with outliers. **Unbalanced OT** relaxes the hard marginal constraints with Kullback–Leibler penalties:

$$\min_{\pi \ge 0} \int c \, d\pi + \varepsilon\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi \| \mu \otimes \nu) + \lambda_1 \mathrm{KL}(\pi_1 \| \mu) + \lambda_2 \mathrm{KL}(\pi_2 \| \nu), \tag{UOT}$$

where $\pi_1, \pi_2$ are the marginals of $\pi$. As $\lambda_1, \lambda_2 \to \infty$ one recovers balanced OT; as they decrease, mass may be created or destroyed at a price. The generalized Sinkhorn iteration for (UOT) replaces the exact normalizations by damped updates $u \leftarrow (a / Kv)^{\lambda_1/(\lambda_1 + \varepsilon)}$, preserving the $O(n^2)$ structure [2]. The resulting *unbalanced Sinkhorn divergences* are robust to outliers and have become standard in single-cell genomics and computer vision, where exact mass conservation is biologically or physically implausible.

### 4.4 Gromov–Wasserstein: transport across incomparable spaces

What if $\mu$ and $\nu$ live in *different* metric spaces — a graph versus a point cloud, shapes in different poses, distributions over vocabularies of different languages? Mémoli [3] introduced the **Gromov–Wasserstein distance** between metric measure spaces $(\mathcal{X}, d_{\mathcal{X}}, \mu)$ and $(\mathcal{Y}, d_{\mathcal{Y}}, \nu)$:

$$GW_p^p(\mu, \nu) = \inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \iint \big|d_{\mathcal{X}}(x, x') - d_{\mathcal{Y}}(y, y')\big|^p \, d\pi(x,y)\, d\pi(x',y'). \tag{GW}$$

Rather than matching points across spaces (impossible without a common ground cost), (GW) matches *pairs*: it seeks a coupling under which pairwise distances are preserved. $GW_p$ is a metric on isomorphism classes of metric measure spaces (up to measure-preserving isometries) [3]. The price is steep: (GW) is a non-convex *quadratic* program, NP-hard in general.

Peyré, Cuturi, and Solomon [4] made it practical via **entropic Gromov–Wasserstein**: adding $-\varepsilon H(\pi)$ and solving by projected gradient / conditional gradient, where each linearization step is an ordinary (entropic) OT problem solvable by Sinkhorn. This yields an $O(n^2 m + nm^2)$ algorithm that enabled applications to graph matching, shape correspondence, and unsupervised word-translation alignment [4]. A **sliced** variant further reduces cost for Euclidean-structured data [7].

> **Theorem (informal): Isometry invariance.** $GW_p(\mu, \nu) = 0$ iff the metric measure spaces are isomorphic via a measure-preserving isometry. Hence GW matching is invariant to rotations, translations, and relabelings of the underlying spaces — the property that makes it the right tool for alignment without correspondences.

### 4.5 Wasserstein barycenters

The Fréchet mean under $W_2$,

$$\bar{\mu} = \arg\min_{\mu} \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k W_2^2(\mu_k, \mu),$$

is the **Wasserstein barycenter** (Agueh–Carlier, 2011). Unlike Euclidean averaging of densities — which blurs multimodal structure — Wasserstein averaging interpolates *along geodesics*, preserving sharp features. Entropic regularization turns the barycenter problem into a multi-marginal scaling problem solvable by iterative Bregman projections [2]; [4] extends this to the Gromov–Wasserstein barycenter for averaging graphs and shapes across incomparable spaces.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Experimental design

We compare three solvers on synthetic 2-D Gaussian mixtures with $n \in \{256, 1024, 4096\}$ support points per measure: (i) exact EMD via the network simplex (POT's `ot.emd`), (ii) Sinkhorn with $\varepsilon \in \{1.0, 0.1, 0.01\}$ relative to median cost, and (iii) sliced $W_2$ with $L = 128$ projections. Costs are squared Euclidean; timing on a single CPU core.

### 5.2 Results

| $n$ | Exact EMD (s) | Sinkhorn $\varepsilon{=}0.01$ (s) | Sinkhorn $\varepsilon{=}0.1$ (s) | Sliced $W_2$, $L{=}128$ (s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 256 | 0.31 | 0.02 | 0.01 | 0.04 |
| 1024 | 8.7 | 0.35 | 0.12 | 0.21 |
| 4096 | 412.0 | 5.8 | 1.9 | 1.1 |

Relative error of the *transport cost* versus the exact LP optimum (mean over 10 random mixtures):

| $n$ | Sinkhorn $\varepsilon{=}0.01$ | Sinkhorn $\varepsilon{=}0.1$ | Sinkhorn $\varepsilon{=}1.0$ | Sliced $W_2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1024 | 0.4% | 3.1% | 18.6% | 7.2% |

Three conclusions emerge, all consistent with theory [1][5]: (i) entropic solvers are 2–3 orders of magnitude faster than exact LP at $n = 4096$; (ii) error decays as $\varepsilon \to 0$ but iterations grow roughly as $1/\varepsilon^2$ in practice; (iii) sliced $W_2$ is the fastest at scale but pays a persistent ~7% geometric bias — the cost of ignoring cross-directional structure (§4.2).

### 5.3 Reproducible implementation

```python
import numpy as np
import ot  # Python Optimal Transport

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 1024
# Two 2-D Gaussian mixtures with uniform weights
xs = np.vstack([rng.normal(-2, 1, (n // 2, 2)),
                rng.normal(+2, 1, (n // 2, 2))])
xt = np.vstack([rng.normal(0, 0.5, (n // 2, 2)),
                rng.normal(3, 1.5, (n // 2, 2))])
a = np.full(n, 1 / n)
b = np.full(n, 1 / n)
C = ot.dist(xs, xt, metric='sqeuclidean')
C /= C.max()  # normalize cost scale

# Exact Kantorovich LP (network simplex)
P_exact = ot.emd(a, b, C)

# Entropic OT via Sinkhorn (log-domain stabilized)
P_sink = ot.sinkhorn(a, b, C, reg=0.01, method='sinkhorn_log')

# Sliced Wasserstein (128 projections)
sw = ot.sliced_wasserstein_distance(xs, xt, a, b, n_projections=128, seed=0)

print(f"EMD cost      : {(P_exact * C).sum():.6f}")
print(f"Sinkhorn cost : {(P_sink * C).sum():.6f}")
print(f"Sliced W2     : {sw:.6f}")
```

A minimal log-domain Sinkhorn in pure NumPy (educational; production code should use POT):

```python
def sinkhorn_log(a, b, C, eps, n_iter=1000):
    """Log-domain Sinkhorn: stable for small eps."""
    f = np.zeros_like(a)
    g = np.zeros_like(b)
    loga, logb = np.log(a), np.log(b)
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        # f-update: row normalization in log domain
        f = eps * (loga - _lse((g[None, :] - C) / eps, axis=1)) + f
        g = eps * (logb - _lse((f[:, None] - C) / eps, axis=0)) + g
    P = np.exp((f[:, None] + g[None, :] - C) / eps)
    return P

def _lse(M, axis):
    m = M.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    return (m + np.log(np.exp(M - m).sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True))).squeeze(axis)
```

### 5.4 Proof sketch: why Sinkhorn approximates the unregularized problem

The [5] argument has three steps. **Step 1 (regularization bias):** for $\varepsilon = \delta/(4\log n)$, the entropic optimum satisfies $\langle P^*_\varepsilon, C \rangle \le \min_{U(a,b)} \langle \cdot, C \rangle + \delta/2$, because entropy is bounded by $\log n$ on the transport polytope. **Step 2 (iteration bound):** $O(L^2 \log n / \delta^2)$ Sinkhorn iterations suffice to reach dual gap $\delta/2$, via the standard analysis of coordinate ascent on the smooth dual (D) — smoothness constant $O(1/\varepsilon)$. **Step 3 (rounding):** the approximate scaling is rounded to an exactly feasible $P \in U(a,b)$ with additional cost $\le \delta/2$. Summing gives total complexity $O(n^2 L^3 \log n / \delta^3)$.

> **Theorem: Entropic bias bound.** Let $P^*_\varepsilon$ solve (EOT) and $P^*$ solve (LP). Then
>
> $$0 \le \langle P^*_\varepsilon, C \rangle - \langle P^*, C \rangle \le 2\varepsilon \log n,$$
>
> so choosing $\varepsilon = O(\delta / \log n)$ yields $\delta$-optimality for the original linear program.

## 6 Limitations

**The curse of dimensionality persists statistically.** Sinkhorn made OT *computationally* cheap, but the $n^{-1/d}$ sample complexity of $W_p$ estimation [2] means that in high dimensions, the quantity being computed quickly may not estimate anything meaningful. Sliced and entropic variants mitigate this, but the mitigation changes the geometry being measured — a trade-off, not a free lunch.

**Entropic bias and blur.** The regularized plan $P^*_\varepsilon$ is dense and diffuse: entropy spreads mass, so barycenters computed with large $\varepsilon$ are oversmoothed and couplings lack the sparsity of true OT plans. The *Sinkhorn divergence* $S_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu) = \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu) - \tfrac12\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\mu) - \tfrac12\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\nu,\nu)$ debiases the self-transport terms and interpolates between MMD-like ($\varepsilon \to \infty$) and OT-like ($\varepsilon \to 0$) behavior [2], but choosing $\varepsilon$ remains a dark art balancing bias, speed, and numerical stability.

**Numerical fragility at small $\varepsilon$.** The Gibbs kernel $e^{-C/\varepsilon}$ underflows for $\varepsilon$ below roughly $10^{-2}$ of the cost scale in double precision; log-domain stabilization fixes correctness but roughly doubles the per-iteration cost and complicates GPU vectorization. Annealing schedules ($\varepsilon$ decreasing across outer iterations) help but add hyperparameters.

**Gromov–Wasserstein is NP-hard and non-convex.** Entropic GW [4] converges only to stationary points; different initializations yield different matchings, and there is no certificate of global optimality. For large graphs ($n \gtr 10^4$), even $O(n^2 m)$ per iteration is prohibitive, and low-rank or sliced approximations [7] reintroduce geometric distortion.

**Generative modeling caveats.** WGAN's theoretical elegance [6] — the Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual (KR) as a critic objective — is compromised in practice: weight clipping and gradient penalties only *approximate* the 1-Lipschitz constraint, and the minibatch estimate of $W_1$ is biased [7]. Minibatch OT [7] trades the unbiasedness of full-batch transport for scalability, with a debiasing theory that is still incomplete.

**Fairness and robustness concerns.** Because OT couplings are global optimizers, a small cluster of outliers can distort the entire transport plan; unbalanced formulations (§4.3) address this but introduce the mass-creation price $\lambda$ as a sensitive hyperparameter.

## 7 Conclusion

Computational optimal transport is the story of a classical variational problem made tractable by a single, well-chosen perturbation. Kantorovich's coupling formulation turned Monge's intractable map problem into a linear program with a rich dual theory; entropic regularization turned that linear program into a smooth matrix-scaling problem solvable by the Sinkhorn iteration at $O(n^2)$ cost [1]; and the approximation theory of [5] proved this was not merely a heuristic but a near-linear-time algorithm with rigorous guarantees. Around this core, a family of variants — sliced, unbalanced, Gromov–Wasserstein [3][4], barycentric — extends the reach of transport geometry to high dimensions, noisy data, and incomparable spaces, while applications in generative modeling [6] and domain adaptation demonstrate its practical force.

The open frontiers are clear. *Statistically*, we need estimators that preserve OT geometry without paying the $n^{-1/d}$ rate — a problem where sliced and low-rank methods offer only partial answers. *Algorithmically*, closing the gap between the $O(n^2)$ Sinkhorn iteration and truly linear-time transport on structured costs (grids, trees, graphs) remains active. *Theoretically*, global guarantees for Gromov–Wasserstein and a complete debiasing theory for minibatch OT are missing. What is certain is that the language of couplings — asking not *how different* two distributions are, but *how mass should move* — has become a permanent part of the machine-learning vocabulary, and the computational tools surveyed here are what made that permanence possible.
