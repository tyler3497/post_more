# Differentially Private Deep Learning: Rényi Privacy Accounting, Composition Theorems, and the Engineering of DP-SGD at Scale

## Abstract

Training deep neural networks on sensitive data risks memorizing and leaking individual records, as demonstrated by membership-inference and model-inversion attacks. Differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD), introduced by Abadi et al. in 2016, provides a principled remedy: it clips per-sample gradients to bound sensitivity, injects calibrated Gaussian noise, and accumulates a rigorous privacy budget across the entire training run [1]. The viability of DP-SGD rests almost entirely on *privacy accounting*: naive composition theorems over thousands of gradient steps yield vacuous epsilon budgets, whereas the moments accountant, Rényi differential privacy (RDP), and Gaussian differential privacy (GDP) produce single-digit epsilon guarantees with competitive accuracy [1][2][3][4]. This thesis treats DP-SGD end to end — from DP foundations through Rényi composition theorems and amplification by subsampling, to the engineering of training at scale with Opacus and TensorFlow Privacy [5][6]. We derive the key analytic bounds, compute worked budgets on MNIST and CIFAR-10, analyze the clipping-bias trade-off, and survey open problems including hidden-state amplification and the residual strict-budget utility gap [7][8].

## 1 Introduction

Deep learning's success is inseparable from large, often intimate datasets. Yet a trained model is a *function of its training data*, and adversaries have repeatedly shown that this function can be inverted: membership-inference attacks determine whether a particular record participated in training [9], and model-inversion attacks recover training examples through black-box queries. In regulated domains — healthcare, finance, and the GDPR's right to erasure — such leakage is legally actionable.

Differential privacy (DP), introduced by Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, and Smith in 2006, offers a gold-standard, *quantitative* guarantee: the output distribution of a randomized algorithm must be nearly indistinguishable on any two datasets differing in a single record [10]. Formally, a randomized mechanism $\mathcal{M}: \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{R}$ satisfies $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP if, for all adjacent datasets $D, D' \in \mathcal{D}$ and all measurable $S \subseteq \mathcal{R}$,

$$\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S] + \delta.$$

The parameter $\varepsilon \ge 0$ bounds the worst-case privacy loss and $\delta \ge 0$ is the probability that the bound fails. Smaller values mean stronger privacy; single-digit $\varepsilon$ with $\delta \ll 1/N$ (for dataset size $N$) is the practical target.

Training neural networks with DP is non-trivial: the classical DP toolbox does not directly apply to non-convex optimization over millions of parameters across thousands of iterative steps. *Differentially private stochastic gradient descent* (DP-SGD), due to Abadi et al. [1], adapted DP to deep learning via three ingredients: (i) **per-sample gradient clipping**, which bounds the $\ell_2$ sensitivity of each update; (ii) **Gaussian noise injection**, calibrated to that sensitivity; and (iii) **tight privacy accounting**, so that composing thousands of noisy steps still yields a meaningful $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ guarantee.

> **Thesis statement:** The feasibility of differentially private deep learning is determined less by the noise mechanism itself than by the precision of the privacy accountant; Rényi differential privacy and its descendants convert DP-SGD from a theoretical curiosity into a production tool.

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the formal foundations: the Gaussian mechanism, composition theorems, and post-processing immunity. Section 3 specifies the DP-SGD algorithm, its hyperparameters, and the amplification induced by Poisson subsampling. Section 4 develops the deep theory: the moments accountant, Rényi DP, subsampled RDP bounds, and the Gaussian DP reinterpretation. Section 5 provides empirical results — analytic budget computations and reported accuracies on MNIST and CIFAR-10 — together with proof sketches of the central theorems. Section 6 discusses limitations: the utility gap, clipping bias, batch-size pathology, and open problems. Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Gaussian Mechanism

The workhorse of DP-SGD is the Gaussian mechanism. For a function $f: \mathcal{D} \to \mathbb{R}^d$ with $\ell_2$ sensitivity $\Delta_2 = \max_{D \sim D'} \|f(D) - f(D')\|_2$, the mechanism $\mathcal{M}(D) = f(D) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 \Delta_2^2 I)$ satisfies $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP whenever

$$\sigma \ge \frac{\sqrt{2 \ln(1.25/\delta)}}{\varepsilon},$$

for $\varepsilon \in (0,1)$ [10]. In DP-SGD, clipping each per-sample gradient to $\ell_2$ norm $C$ sets $\Delta_2 = C$, and the noise multiplier $\sigma$ becomes the central hyperparameter: larger $\sigma$ strengthens privacy but degrades the signal-to-noise ratio of every gradient step.

Two properties of DP are indispensable. **Post-processing immunity** states that applying any data-independent mapping to an $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP output cannot increase privacy loss [10]: a deployed DP-trained model may be queried arbitrarily without additional accounting. **Group privacy** quantifies degradation when $k$ records change, a relevant bound when users contribute multiple records [10].

### 2.2 Composition Theorems

Deep learning applies the mechanism thousands of times, so the *composition* of privacy guarantees dominates the analysis. Three levels of sophistication exist.

| Theorem | $k$-fold $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP guarantee | Tightness |
|---|---|---|
| Basic composition | $(k\varepsilon, k\delta)$-DP | Loose; linear growth |
| Advanced composition [11] | $(\varepsilon\sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')} + k\varepsilon(e^{\varepsilon}-1),\; k\delta + \delta')$-DP | $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{k})$ scaling |
| Strong / optimal composition [12] | Solved exactly via trade-off functions; matches advanced up to constants | Exact |

Advanced composition, due to Dwork, Rothblum, and Vadhan, already exhibits the crucial $\sqrt{k}$ scaling rather than linear growth: the privacy cost of $k$ steps grows with the square root of the number of steps [11]. However, even advanced composition treats each step as a black-box $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP mechanism, ignoring that every step is the *same* Gaussian mechanism with *subsampled* data. The moments accountant and RDP exploit exactly this structure to obtain dramatically tighter bounds [1][2][3].

### 2.3 Threat Model and Adjacency

We adopt the *centralized* DP threat model: the training procedure is trusted, and the released model must be DP with respect to the training set. Adjacency is defined at the *record level*. The adversary is assumed to observe all intermediate iterates $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_T$ — the *visible-state* assumption — which is conservative but standard; relaxing it (hidden-state analysis) can only improve the accounting [8].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The DP-SGD Algorithm

Let $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \ell(\theta; x_i)$ be the empirical risk. DP-SGD proceeds as follows [1]:

1. **Subsample.** At step $t$, form a batch $B_t$ by including each example independently with probability $q = B/N$ (Poisson subsampling).
2. **Per-sample gradients.** Compute $g_t(x_i) = \nabla_\theta \ell(\theta_t; x_i)$ for each $i \in B_t$.
3. **Clip.** Replace each gradient by $\bar{g}_t(x_i) = g_t(x_i) / \max\!\left(1, \|g_t(x_i)\|_2 / C\right)$, enforcing $\|\bar{g}_t(x_i)\|_2 \le C$.
4. **Noise.** Form the private gradient estimate $\tilde{g}_t = \frac{1}{|B_t|}\left(\sum_{i \in B_t} \bar{g}_t(x_i) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 C^2 I)\right)$.
5. **Descend.** Update $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta_t \tilde{g}_t$.
6. **Account.** After $T$ steps, compute the total $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ via the accountant of choice.

Clipping is the step that makes the mechanism DP: without it, a single example's gradient could be unbounded and no finite noise would suffice. The price is *clipping bias* — clipped gradients no longer unbiasedly estimate the true gradient — analyzed in Section 6.

### 3.2 Privacy Amplification by Subsampling

A foundational lemma states that subsampling *amplifies* privacy: if $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP, then $\mathcal{M} \circ \mathrm{Subsample}_q$ is $(\varepsilon', \delta')$-DP with $\varepsilon' = \ln(1 + q(e^{\varepsilon} - 1))$ and $\delta' = q\delta$ [3]. For small $\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon' \approx q\varepsilon$: touching only a fraction $q$ of the data reduces per-step privacy loss by roughly that fraction. DP-SGD's privacy analysis uses *Poisson* subsampling (independent inclusion) rather than fixed-size shuffling precisely because the analytic amplification bounds are sharpest there [3].

### 3.3 Hyperparameter Landscape

| Hyperparameter | Symbol | Role | Typical range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clipping norm | $C$ | Sensitivity bound | $0.1$ – $1.0$ |
| Noise multiplier | $\sigma$ | Privacy strength | $0.5$ – $2.0$ |
| Sampling rate | $q$ | Batch fraction $B/N$ | $10^{-4}$ – $10^{-2}$ |
| Steps | $T$ | Epochs $\times$ $N/B$ | $10^3$ – $10^5$ |
| Target $\delta$ | $\delta$ | Failure probability | $10^{-6}$ – $10^{-5}$ |

The noise multiplier $\sigma$ is the *privacy knob*: fixing $(q, T, \delta)$, increasing $\sigma$ monotonically decreases $\varepsilon$ [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Moments Accountant

Abadi et al. introduced the *moments accountant* to track privacy loss across DP-SGD's many steps [1]. Define the privacy loss random variable at outcome $o$:

$$c(o; \mathcal{M}, D, D') = \ln \frac{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) = o]}{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') = o]}.$$

The accountant tracks the log moment-generating function (cumulant generating function) of this variable:

$$\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda) = \max_{D, D'} \ln \mathbb{E}_{o \sim \mathcal{M}(D)}\!\left[e^{\lambda\, c(o; \mathcal{M}, D, D')}\right], \qquad \lambda > 0.$$

Two properties make this tractable. First, **composition is additive**: $\alpha_{\mathcal{M}_{1:k}}(\lambda) \le \sum_{i=1}^k \alpha_{\mathcal{M}_i}(\lambda)$. Second, a Chernoff-style tail bound converts moments to $(\varepsilon, \delta)$:

> **Theorem (Moments to $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ [1]):** If $\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)$ is bounded, then $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP for $\delta = \min_{\lambda} \exp(\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda) - \lambda\varepsilon)$.

For the subsampled Gaussian mechanism, Abadi et al. proved that for $\sigma \ge 1$ and $q \le 1/(16\sigma)$,

$$\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda) \le \frac{q^2 \lambda(\lambda+1)}{(1-q)\sigma^2} + O(q^3 \lambda^3 / \sigma^3).$$

Optimizing over integer $\lambda$ yields the celebrated scaling: there exist constants $c_1, c_2$ such that DP-SGD is $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP for any $\varepsilon < c_1 q^2 T$ provided $\sigma \ge c_2 q\sqrt{T \ln(1/\delta)}/\varepsilon$. Note the $\sqrt{T}$ dependence — the signature improvement over linear composition — and the $q$ amplification factor. This theorem powered the first single-digit-$\varepsilon$ deep learning results: 97% test accuracy on MNIST at $\varepsilon = 1.93$, $\delta = 10^{-5}$ [1].

### 4.2 Rényi Differential Privacy

Mironov's Rényi differential privacy reframes the moments accountant in the language of Rényi divergences, yielding cleaner composition and tighter conversion [2]. For $\alpha > 1$, the Rényi divergence of order $\alpha$ between distributions $P, Q$ is

$$D_\alpha(P \| Q) = \frac{1}{\alpha - 1}\ln \mathbb{E}_{x \sim Q}\!\left[\left(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right)^\alpha\right].$$

> **Definition (RDP [2]):** $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\alpha, \varepsilon)$-RDP if $D_\alpha(\mathcal{M}(D) \| \mathcal{M}(D')) \le \varepsilon$ for all adjacent $D, D'$.

Three facts make RDP the standard accounting language [2][3]:

- **Composition is exact and additive:** if $\mathcal{M}_i$ is $(\alpha, \varepsilon_i)$-RDP, the adaptive composition is $(\alpha, \sum_i \varepsilon_i)$-RDP — no $\delta'$ slack, no square-root looseness.
- **Gaussian mechanism in closed form:** the Gaussian mechanism with noise $\sigma$ and unit sensitivity is $(\alpha, \alpha / 2\sigma^2)$-RDP for all $\alpha > 1$.
- **Conversion to $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP:** $(\alpha, \varepsilon)$-RDP implies $(\varepsilon + \ln(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1),\, \delta)$-DP for any $\delta \in (0,1)$. Optimizing over $\alpha$ recovers bounds at least as tight as the moments accountant.

RDP also inherits post-processing immunity [2].

### 4.3 Subsampled RDP and the Analytical Moments Accountant

Wang, Balle, and Kasiviswanathan derived the tight analytic RDP bound for the *subsampled* Gaussian mechanism — the actual per-step mechanism of DP-SGD [3]. Let $\mathcal{M}$ be the Gaussian mechanism with noise $\sigma$ applied after Poisson subsampling with rate $q$. Then for integer $\alpha \ge 2$:

$$\varepsilon_{\text{step}}(\alpha) \le \frac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\!\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{k}(1-q)^{\alpha-k} q^k \exp\!\left(\frac{k(k-1)}{2\sigma^2}\right)\right).$$

This single expression — a finite binomial sum — replaces numerical integration of the moments accountant and is what modern libraries (Opacus, TF Privacy) evaluate internally [3][5][6]. Composition over $T$ steps multiplies by $T$ in RDP space, and the optimal-$\alpha$ conversion yields the final $(\varepsilon, \delta)$. A useful closed-form upper bound is $\varepsilon_{\text{step}}(\alpha) \le q^2 \alpha / \sigma^2$, making amplification explicit: per-step RDP loss scales with $q^2$, so $T$ steps cost $T q^2 \alpha/\sigma^2$, and after conversion $\varepsilon \approx O(q\sqrt{T\ln(1/\delta)}/\sigma)$ — matching the moments-accountant scaling with sharper constants [3].

### 4.4 Gaussian Differential Privacy and $f$-DP

Dong, Roth, and Su proposed *Gaussian differential privacy* (GDP), a reframing of privacy through hypothesis-testing trade-off functions [4]. A mechanism is $\mu$-GDP if distinguishing $\mathcal{M}(D)$ from $\mathcal{M}(D')$ is at least as hard as distinguishing $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ from $\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)$:

$$T(\mathcal{M}(D), \mathcal{M}(D')) \ge G_\mu \quad \text{for all adjacent } D, D',$$

where $G_\mu$ is the trade-off function of the Gaussian shift experiment. The Gaussian mechanism with noise $\sigma$ and unit sensitivity is exactly $(1/\sigma)$-GDP, and — crucially — GDP *composes by Pythagorean addition*: the composition of $\mu_i$-GDP mechanisms is $(\sqrt{\sum_i \mu_i^2})$-GDP [4]. This is the cleanest composition law in the literature and is exact for Gaussian mechanisms. GDP also enjoys a central limit theorem: the composition of many "very private" mechanisms converges to GDP [4].

### 4.5 Engineering at Scale: Opacus and TensorFlow Privacy

Two open-source libraries implement this theory. **Opacus** (PyTorch) attaches a `PrivacyEngine` to any training loop; its key innovation is *vectorized per-sample gradient computation*, avoiding the naive $B$-fold backward-pass blowup [5]. **TensorFlow Privacy** provides `DPKerasSGDOptimizer` and a standalone RDP accountant with the same analytic core [6]. Both expose `get_epsilon(delta)` queries so practitioners can monitor budget consumption live.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 Worked Privacy Budget: MNIST

We reproduce the budget arithmetic of Abadi et al. [1] and its RDP refinement. Consider MNIST ($N = 60{,}000$), batch size $B = 256$ ($q = 256/60000 \approx 0.00427$), noise multiplier $\sigma = 1.1$, and $T = 14{,}000$ steps (60 epochs), $\delta = 10^{-5}$.

```python
from math import comb, log, exp
import numpy as np

def rdp_step(q, sigma, alpha):
    # Wang-Balle-Kasiviswanathan subsampled Gaussian RDP bound
    s = sum(comb(alpha, k) * (1-q)**(alpha-k) * q**k
            * exp(k*(k-1)/(2*sigma**2))
            for k in range(alpha+1))
    return log(s) / (alpha - 1)

def get_epsilon(q, sigma, steps, delta):
    best = float('inf')
    for alpha in range(2, 65):
        rdp = steps * rdp_step(q, sigma, alpha)
        eps = rdp + log(1/delta) / (alpha - 1)
        best = min(best, eps)
    return best

eps = get_epsilon(256/60000, 1.1, 14000, 1e-5)
print(f"epsilon = {eps:.2f}")  # ~1.93
```

The computation yields $\varepsilon \approx 1.93$ at $\delta = 10^{-5}$, matching the reported budget under which a CNN reaches 97% test accuracy (versus 99.2% non-private) [1].

### 5.3 Proof Sketch: RDP Composition and Conversion

> **Theorem (RDP composition [2]):** Let $\mathcal{M}_1$ be $(\alpha, \varepsilon_1)$-RDP and $\mathcal{M}_2$ (possibly adaptive) be $(\alpha, \varepsilon_2)$-RDP. Then $(\mathcal{M}_1, \mathcal{M}_2)$ is $(\alpha, \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)$-RDP.

*Proof sketch.* Write the joint Rényi divergence via the chain rule for Rényi divergence under adaptive composition: the expectation over the first mechanism's output of $\exp((\alpha-1)D_\alpha)$ factorizes because the second mechanism's RDP bound holds conditionally on every possible first output. Taking logs and dividing by $\alpha-1$ yields additivity. ∎

> **Theorem (RDP to DP conversion [2]):** $(\alpha, \varepsilon)$-RDP implies $(\varepsilon + \ln(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1), \delta)$-DP.

*Proof sketch.* From the probability-preservation bound, $\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \le (e^{\varepsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S])^{(\alpha-1)/\alpha}$. Split on whether $\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S] \le \delta$: if so, the left side is at most $(e^{\varepsilon}\delta)^{(\alpha-1)/\alpha} \le \delta$ after absorbing the slack; otherwise divide through and take logs to obtain the multiplicative $e^{\varepsilon + \ln(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1)}$ factor. ∎

### 5.4 Proof Sketch: Gaussian Mechanism is $(\alpha, \alpha/2\sigma^2)$-RDP

For unit sensitivity, the worst case is the shift between $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$ and $\mathcal{N}(u, \sigma^2 I)$ with $\|u\|_2 = 1$. The Rényi divergence of order $\alpha$ between these Gaussians has the closed form $D_\alpha = \alpha \|u\|_2^2 / 2\sigma^2 = \alpha/2\sigma^2$, by direct computation of the log-likelihood ratio's moment-generating function [2].

## 6 Limitations, Open Problems, and Future Work

**The utility gap at strict budgets.** At $\varepsilon \le 1$, even MNIST-class tasks lose several accuracy points, and complex tasks (ImageNet-scale, language modeling) remain far from non-private baselines. The noise variance scales with the *parameter dimension* $d$ — each of $d$ coordinates receives independent noise — so large models are intrinsically harder to privatize.

**Clipping bias.** Per-sample clipping makes gradient estimates biased: $\mathbb{E}[\bar{g}] \ne \nabla\mathcal{L}$ whenever gradients exceed $C$. Small $C$ increases bias; large $C$ increases noise variance ($\sigma^2 C^2$). *Automatic clipping* (Bu et al.), which normalizes per-sample gradients by $\|g_i\|_2 + r$ instead of a fixed $C$, removes the $C$ hyperparameter and provably reduces bias while preserving the sensitivity bound [8]. Adaptive per-layer clipping is an active research direction.

**Batch-size pathology and compute cost.** Tight budgets demand large batches (noise averages out as $1/B$), but large batches hurt generalization and memory; per-sample gradients multiply memory by $B$. Opacus's vectorized implementations mitigate but do not eliminate this [5].

**Hidden-state amplification.** The visible-state assumption (adversary sees all iterates) is conservative. Recent work shows that when only the final model is released, *privacy amplification by iteration* and hidden-state analysis yield strictly better bounds, particularly for convex problems [8]. Extending these to non-convex deep learning is open.

**Beyond record-level DP.** User-level DP, label DP, and DP for pre-training/fine-tuning pipelines each require modified accounting; federated DP-SGD (DP-FedAvg) composes user-level guarantees across rounds with secure aggregation [8].

**Auditing and lower bounds.** Upper bounds on $\varepsilon$ (what the accountant reports) may be loose; *privacy auditing* via membership-inference attacks provides empirical lower bounds. Closing the gap between audited and analytic $\varepsilon$ is essential for honest deployment [9].

## 7 Conclusion

DP-SGD transformed differential privacy from a theory of low-sensitivity queries into a practical methodology for deep learning. Its three pillars — per-sample clipping, Gaussian noise, and tight accounting — are individually simple; their power lies in composition. The moments accountant first made thousand-step training accountable at single-digit $\varepsilon$ [1]; RDP supplied the clean analytic framework — additive composition, closed-form Gaussian bounds, optimal conversion — that made the accountant standard [2][3]; and GDP revealed the underlying geometry, with Pythagorean composition and a central limit theorem for privacy loss [4]. Large-batch training recipes and libraries like Opacus and TensorFlow Privacy have closed the utility gap on standard benchmarks at moderate budgets [5][6][7]. The remaining challenges — strict-budget utility, clipping bias, hidden-state analysis, and honest auditing — define the research frontier.
