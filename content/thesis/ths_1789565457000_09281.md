# Condensed Mathematics: Liquid Vector Spaces, the Clausen–Scholze Framework, and Analytic Rings

## Abstract

Condensed mathematics, introduced by Dustin Clausen and Peter Scholze, replaces topological spaces by sheaves on the site of profinite sets, repairing the defective homological algebra of categories that mix algebra and topology. This article surveys the framework from its foundations to its most delicate analytic layer: liquid vector spaces. We explain why condensed abelian groups form an abelian category while topological abelian groups do not, how the analytic rings **Z**-solid and **R**-liquid encode non-archimedean and archimedean functional analysis through solid and p-liquid modules, and why the theorem that the liquid analytic ring is indeed analytic — the heart of liquid functional analysis — became the target of one of the most ambitious computer formalizations ever attempted. We describe the Liquid Tensor Experiment, in which a team led by Johan Commelin verified the Clausen–Scholze theorem in the Lean proof assistant, completing the verification in July 2022 and simplifying the original argument. We close with analytic stacks, gaseous vector spaces, and the open problems that remain.

##1 Intro

Categories that carry algebra and topology simultaneously are notoriously ill-behaved. The category **TopAb** of topological abelian groups is not abelian: the identity map from the real numbers with the discrete topology to the real numbers with the usual topology is a continuous bijection, hence a monomorphism and an epimorphism, yet it is not an isomorphism — and this failure is invisible to kernels and cokernels, which are both trivial [1][4]. Homological algebra, which depends on kernels, cokernels, and exact sequences behaving correctly, collapses in such a setting. Classical remedies — compactly generated spaces, bornological and locally convex topologies, quasi-abelian categories — each repair part of the damage while introducing new pathologies of their own.

Condensed mathematics, developed by Dustin Clausen and Peter Scholze beginning around 2018, proposes a more radical cure: *separate the algebra from the topology entirely*. A topological space X is replaced by the sheaf S ↦ C(S, X) of continuous maps out of profinite sets S. The resulting objects, called *condensed sets*, live in a category with excellent formal properties, and all of algebra — groups, rings, modules, and their derived categories — can be developed internally to it. As Kiran Kedlaya has put it, the theory is "technology for doing commutative algebra over topological rings" [1].

This article traces the framework from its sheaf-theoretic foundations through its two great analytic completions — *solid* modules for non-archimedean analysis and *liquid* vector spaces for archimedean analysis — to the unifying language of *analytic rings* and the emerging theory of *analytic stacks*. The centerpiece is the theorem of Clausen and Scholze that the liquid analytic ring **R**-liquid(p) satisfies the analytic ring axioms: a statement so subtle that Scholze publicly challenged the mathematical community to verify it by computer, launching the Liquid Tensor Experiment [1][5].

##2 Background

### The condensed site

A *condensed set* is a sheaf of sets on the site of profinite sets, where coverings are finite families of jointly surjective maps [8]. Concretely, it is a contravariant functor T from profinite sets to sets satisfying three axioms:

```haskell
-- Sheaf axioms for a condensed set, in schematic form
class Condensed t where
  emptyAx    :: t Empty      -- T of the empty set is a point
  coprodAx   :: t (S1 :|: S2) -> (t S1, t S2)
               -- finite coproducts go to products
  descentAx  :: Epi f =>
                t S -> (t S', Equalizer (t pi1) (t pi2))
               -- descent along surjections S' -> S
```

The underlying set of T is T evaluated at a point. Every compactly generated topological space embeds fully faithfully into condensed sets via X ↦ Hom(-, X), and compactly generated topological vector spaces embed fully faithfully into condensed vector spaces [7]. Inside the condensed world, the category of condensed abelian groups is automatically abelian — every category of sheaves of abelian groups on any site is — so the full machinery of homological algebra applies without qualification [1][4].

### Historical development

The story begins with the pro-étale site introduced by Bhargav Bhatt and Scholze in 2013: the pro-étale site of a single point turns out to be isomorphic to the site of profinite sets, and in 2018 Clausen and Scholze realized that this site alone is rich enough to realize large classes of topological spaces as sheaves [1]. Scholze's 2019 lecture notes, *Lectures on Condensed Mathematics*, gave the first systematic exposition, and the 2020 masterclass *Condensed Mathematics and Complex Geometry of Numbers* pushed the theory toward complex geometry. Coincidentally, in 2019 Clark Barwick and Peter Haine introduced *pyknotic objects*, a closely related theory whose main differences from condensed mathematics are set-theoretic: pyknotic theory depends on a choice of Grothendieck universes, whereas condensed mathematics can be developed strictly within ZFC [1].

Two analytic completions dominate applications:

- **Solid abelian groups**, the non-archimedean theory: the analytic ring **Z**-solid yields a derived category D(**Z**-solid) = D(Solid), and solid **Q**_p-vector spaces form an abelian category with a tensor product containing all Fréchet spaces over **Q**_p [4][8].
- **Liquid vector spaces**, the archimedean theory: for 0 < p ≤ 1, the analytic ring **R**-liquid(p) produces the abelian category of p-liquid real vector spaces, a well-behaved enlargement of the category of Banach spaces [1][2][7].

##3 Methodology

### Why extremally disconnected sets

The condensed site can equivalently be described using compact Hausdorff spaces or, most usefully, *extremally disconnected* compact Hausdorff spaces: every compact Hausdorff space is a quotient of an extremally disconnected one, and these are precisely the projective objects of the category of compacta [8]. Working over extremally disconnected test objects S, the sheaf condition becomes a concrete equalizer diagram, and many arguments reduce to explicit combinatorics of measures on S. The cost of this generality is set-theoretic: one works with κ-condensed objects for an uncountable strong limit cardinal κ, so that all constructions stay within a fixed universe of test spaces [1].

### Analytic rings: the unifying machine

The decisive abstraction of Clausen and Scholze is the *analytic ring*. It consists of a condensed animated ring A together with, for every extremally disconnected set S, a condensed A-module A[S] of "A-valued measures on S," satisfying two axioms [2]. The second axiom is the deep one: it formally implies that the inclusion of the derived category of *complete* modules D(A) into all condensed A-modules admits a left adjoint — the *completion* functor — which in turn induces a *completed tensor product* on D(A) [2].

The power of the definition is visible in its examples:

| Analytic ring | Underlying ring | Measures on S | Complete modules |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trivial | A | free modules A[S] | all condensed A-modules |
| **Z**-solid | **Z** | solid measures | solid abelian groups |
| **R**-liquid(p), 0 < p ≤ 1 | **R** | measures M-sub-p(S) | p-liquid **R**-vector spaces |
| **Z**((q))-gaseous | Laurent series ring | polynomial-growth series | gaseous **R**-vector spaces |

Each row is a complete functional-analytic universe with its own tensor product, and morphisms of analytic rings organize into an ∞-category **AnRing** admitting the usual base-change constructions [2].

### How proofs are done

Arguments in condensed mathematics proceed by *internal* algebra: one works with internal Hom-objects, derived completeness, and the completion adjunction rather than with epsilons and deltas. A typical analytic-ring verification reduces to a combinatorial statement about the measure functors on extremally disconnected sets. The following toy computation illustrates the kind of growth condition that appears — here, the polynomial-growth test defining the gaseous coefficient ring:

```python
def is_polynomial_growth(coeffs, k_max=10, tail=200):
    """Check whether a coefficient sequence has at most polynomial growth,
    i.e. there exist k, m with |a_n| * n**(-k) -> 0.  The gaseous ring
    Z((q))^gas consists of Laurent series whose coefficients satisfy
    exactly this condition."""
    import math
    seq = coeffs[-tail:]
    for k in range(k_max + 1):
        scaled = [abs(a) * (n + 1) ** (-k) for n, a in enumerate(seq)]
        if max(scaled[-50:]) < 1e-6:
            return True, k
    return False, None
```

The real verifications replace this naive test with exact combinatorial bounds — for the liquid theorem, bounds in terms of binomial coefficients that assemble, as the test sets vary, into arbitrary polynomial (but never super-polynomial) growth [9].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The condensed topos: points, shape, and size

Condensed sets *nearly* form a topos: all the expected categorical structure is present, but size must be managed explicitly through the cardinal parameter κ [7]. The terminal object is a point, and evaluating a condensed set at the point recovers its underlying set. Limits and colimits of sheaves are computed pointwise up to sheafification, and the embedding of compactly generated spaces preserves the limits that analysts care about.

A subtle point is *light* condensed mathematics, developed for the analytic stacks program: by restricting the site to light profinite sets, one obtains a variant with better formal behavior for geometric applications, at the price of no longer containing all classical topological spaces [3]. The tension between the full and light theories is one of the theory's live research fronts.

### 4.2 Solid modules and non-archimedean analysis

The analytic ring **Z**-solid = (**Z**, S ↦ **Z**-solid[S]) is the foundation of non-archimedean functional analysis in the condensed setting. Its complete modules — the solid abelian groups — form a Grothendieck abelian category whose derived category D(Solid) carries a well-behaved tensor product [2][8]. Base-changing to **Q**_p yields solid **Q**_p-vector spaces: an abelian category with tensor product containing every Fréchet space over **Q**_p, inside which duality theory, nuclearity, and the usual theorems of non-archimedean analysis acquire clean homological formulations [4]. In his 2021 Fields Institute lecture, Clausen presented solid **Z**-modules as the new base category for non-archimedean functional analysis, explicitly contrasting them with the liquid **R**-modules that play the same role over the archimedean base [10].

### 4.3 Liquid vector spaces: the archimedean theorem

Fix a real number p with 0 < p ≤ 1. Clausen and Scholze define an analytic ring structure **R**-liquid(p) = (**R**, S ↦ M-sub-p(S)), where M-sub-p(S) is a space of **R**-valued measures on S subject to a sub-p growth condition [2]. The associated complete condensed **R**-vector spaces are the *p-liquid vector spaces*, and the induced completed tensor product is the *p-liquid tensor product*.

> **Theorem (Clausen–Scholze, the liquid theorem):** For every 0 < p ≤ 1, the pair (**R**, S ↦ M-sub-p(S)) satisfies the analytic ring axioms. Hence p-liquid real vector spaces form an abelian category with a completed tensor product, serving as a well-behaved enlargement of the category of Banach spaces over **R**.

That this is *true* is a deep result — the verification of the second analytic-ring axiom, Theorem 6.5 in the condensed literature, is the single hardest analytic input of the theory [2]. The archimedean case resists all naive approaches: the Hahn–Banach theorem, the engine of classical functional analysis, is non-constructive in exactly the way that breaks down over the condensed site, and the proof must instead control explicit combinatorial bounds on measures. Once established, the payoff is immediate: the polynomial functional calculus for operators extends from complete topological vector spaces to p-liquid spaces, where entire functions act on endomorphisms through the condensed internal Hom [7].

### 4.4 Analytic rings as a unifying language

Analytic rings turn "which completions are allowed" into algebraic data. A morphism of analytic rings A → B is a map of underlying condensed rings whose forgetful functor preserves completeness; these assemble into the ∞-category **AnRing**, in which one can form pushouts, localizations, and base change exactly as in ordinary algebra [2]. Beyond solid and liquid, Scholze has described a *gaseous* analytic structure on the real numbers, built from the Laurent series ring **Z**((q)) with polynomial-growth coefficients:

> **Working definition (gaseous vector space):** A condensed **R**-vector space V is gaseous when the shift operator 1 − q·Shift acts invertibly on maps out of a fixed projective generator of the theory. Every p-liquid vector space is gaseous, for every p — yet the gaseous category is strictly larger and still well-behaved.

Unlike the liquid parameter p, the gaseous parameter is immaterial: any choice in the open unit interval yields the same theory [9]. Gaseous vector spaces remain largely unexplored, but their existence shows that the liquid theory is not the end of the archimedean story.

### 4.5 Analytic stacks and the new analytic geometry

The current frontier is the Clausen–Scholze lecture course *Analytic Stacks* (2023–2024, given jointly at IHES and the Max Planck Institute), whose stated purpose is to propose new foundations for analytic geometry [3][11]. The syllabus runs through light condensed abelian groups, analytic rings, analytic stacks themselves, and examples — reimagining complex-analytic spaces, rigid-analytic varieties, and adic spaces as geometric objects built from analytic rings. A striking early dividend of the condensed perspective is geometric: replacing the usual derived category D(O_X) of a scheme by a derived category of condensed modules, Clausen and Scholze define for every separated finite-type morphism f a direct image with compact support f_!, obtaining a full six-functor formalism for coherent sheaves on schemes [4].

##5 Empirical/Proofs

The most dramatic "experiment" in condensed mathematics is not a laboratory measurement but a computer verification: the **Liquid Tensor Experiment**. In December 2020, Scholze publicly challenged the community to formally verify the liquid theorem — the analytic-ring axiom for **R**-liquid(p) — writing that the argument was so subtle he wanted independent machine-checked certainty [1][5]. A team led by Johan Commelin took up the challenge in the Lean proof assistant:

1. **December 2020** — Scholze poses the formalization challenge; the Commelin team begins encoding condensed abelian groups, solid modules, and the measure combinatorics in Lean.
2. **Mid-2021** — the central analytic argument is verified, roughly six months in, confirming the core of the Clausen–Scholze proof [1].
3. **July 2022** — the full challenge is completed; the machine-checked proof is declared done [1][5].
4. **Aftermath** — the formalization *simplified* the original proof: the computer tracked exactly which inputs were used and showed that the heavy reliance on stable homotopy groups of spheres (via the Breen–Deligne resolution) cited by Clausen and Scholze could be dispensed with — far less of that machinery was needed than the original argument had assumed [6].

The project, spanning roughly eighteen months, also drove major infrastructure development in Lean's mathematical library and attracted a wave of algebraists to formalization [5][6]. It stands as the first formal verification of a result at the research frontier of arithmetic geometry, and its "test suite" — executable checks that the formalized objects behave as intended — has become a model for how to trust large formalizations [6].

What, mathematically, was verified? At its heart, the liquid theorem is a statement that a certain explicit complex built from the measure functors M-sub-p on extremally disconnected sets is exact — a piece of infinitary combinatorics about how measures restrict along surjections. The condition p ≤ 1 is sharp: the binomial-coefficient bounds that control the measures close up into polynomial growth exactly in this range, and the formalization confirmed that no hidden analytic input beyond these bounds is required [2][9].

##6 Limitations

Condensed mathematics is powerful but not without genuine costs:

- **Set-theoretic overhead.** Every construction is parameterized by a strong limit cardinal κ, and arguments must be checked for κ-independence. The light variant needed for analytic stacks does not contain all classical spaces, so results must be transferred between the two settings with care [1][3].
- **Narrow archimedean scope.** The liquid theorem covers 0 < p ≤ 1 and Banach-like spaces; there is no comparable theory for arbitrary complete locally convex spaces, and the gaseous enlargement is still in its infancy [2][9].
- **Computational inaccessibility.** Condensed objects are sheaves on enormous sites; explicit computations are rare, and most theorems are structural rather than numerical. The Liquid Tensor Experiment consumed many person-years of expert labor — machine verification is possible but far from routine [5][6].
- **Adoption barrier.** The framework demands retraining: analysts must replace topological arguments with internal sheaf-theoretic ones, and the literature remains largely in lecture notes and seminars rather than textbooks [8][10].

##7 Conclusion

Condensed mathematics re-founds the interaction of algebra and topology by moving both into a single sheaf-theoretic universe where homological algebra works as it should. Solid modules have tamed non-archimedean functional analysis; liquid vector spaces have done the same for the archimedean world, at the price of the deepest single theorem in the subject — one whose verification required a landmark human–machine collaboration. Analytic rings now provide the common language, analytic stacks promise a unified analytic geometry, and gaseous vector spaces hint that the taxonomy of "complete" modules is not yet exhausted. The open problems — the light/full comparison, the scope of liquid analysis, and the geometrization of the gaseous theory — will likely occupy the next decade. What is already clear is Kedlaya's verdict: condensed mathematics is becoming the standard technology for commutative algebra over topological rings [1].
