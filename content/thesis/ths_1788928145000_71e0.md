# Learned Index Structures for Database Storage Engines: Recursive Model Indexes, ALEX Gapped Arrays, PGM-Index with Error Bounds, and Worst-Case Guarantees

## Abstract

Learned index structures replace the pointer-chasing comparators of classical indexes with *models* that predict a key's position in sorted order, reframing indexing as cumulative distribution function (CDF) approximation. This thesis unifies the three landmark designs — Kraska et al.'s Recursive Model Index (RMI) [1], Ding et al.'s updatable ALEX [2], and Ferragina and Vinciguerra's PGM-index [3] — within a single analytical framework, and contrasts their guarantees with B-trees. We derive the error-correction cost model shared by all learned lookups, formalize ALEX's gapped-array insertion mechanics and its direct-hit theorem, present the PGM-index's optimal piecewise-linear construction with its *O(n/ε)* space and *O(log ε)* last-mile bounds, and evaluate worst-case behavior under the SOSD benchmark [4] and adversarial workloads [5]. We conclude with a decision calculus for storage-engine architects: where learned structures provably dominate, where B-trees remain undefeated, and which open problems — distribution shift, concurrency, and worst-case dynamic updates — still gate production adoption.

---

## 1. Introduction

For five decades, the B-tree and its variants have been the undisputed workhorses of database storage engines. Their appeal is easy to state: *distribution-independent* worst-case guarantees. A B-tree of fanout *B* locates any key among *n* in *O(log_B n)* comparisons regardless of how adversarial the key distribution is, and its range scans are asymptotically optimal. Yet this generality is also a tax. As Kraska et al. observed in their seminal 2018 paper, an index is, at bottom, a *model*: a B-tree maps a key to a position in a sorted array, a hash index maps a key to a position in an unsorted array, and a Bloom filter predicts set membership [1]. If an index is a model, then *machine learning* can supply better models — models that exploit the structure of the actual data distribution rather than assuming the worst case.

This reframing launched the field of **learned index structures**. The core insight is disarmingly simple: the position of a key *k* in a sorted array of *n* keys is *n · F(k)*, where *F* is the empirical CDF of the data. Any function that approximates *F* is therefore an index, and its *prediction error* can be corrected by a bounded local search — the so-called **last-mile search**. The engineering question is how to learn *F* accurately, compactly, and (for mutable workloads) incrementally.

Three designs define the field's trajectory:

1. **RMI (Recursive Model Index)** [1] — a hierarchy of models trained top-down on static data, demonstrating that learned structures can beat B-trees by large margins on read-only workloads.
2. **ALEX** [2] — an updatable, adaptive learned index that answers the obvious objection to RMI (*"what about inserts?"*) with gapped arrays and model-based insertion.
3. **PGM-index** [3] — a compressed learned index built on *optimal* piecewise-linear approximation, restoring the provable worst-case bounds that RMI sacrificed.

This thesis makes four contributions. **First**, we present a unified cost model for learned lookups that decomposes latency into *model evaluation*, *error correction*, and *structural overhead*. **Second**, we give a precise exposition of each design's mechanics, including ALEX's direct-hit theorem and the PGM-index's linear-time optimal segmentation. **Third**, we ground the theory in the SOSD benchmark [4] and the robustness literature [5], quantifying exactly where learned indexes win and where they collapse. **Fourth**, we compare worst-case guarantees against B-trees and survey recent work on dynamic learned indexing with worst-case bounds [6], closing with practical guidance for engine builders.

## 2. Background

### From comparisons to predictions

A classical ordered index over sorted keys *x_1 < x_2 < … < x_n* answers the **rank query**: given *k*, return *rank(k) = |{x_i ≤ k}|*. Every other point operation reduces to rank: *member(k)* checks *A[rank(k)] = k*, *predecessor(k)* returns *A[rank(k)−1]*, and *range(x, y)* scans from *rank(x)* forward [3]. A B-tree answers rank by *comparing* keys along a root-to-leaf path, touching *O(log_B n)* nodes — each touch a potential cache miss.

A learned index instead *computes* an approximation *ĥ(k) ≈ rank(k)* and then corrects the residual error *|ĥ(k) − rank(k)| ≤ ε* with a local search over at most *2ε* positions. When *ε* is small, the correction fits in one or two cache lines, and the entire lookup costs a few arithmetic operations plus a handful of memory accesses — often dramatically fewer than a B-tree traversal. The price is that *ε* depends on how well the model fits the data: on smooth, learnable CDFs it is tiny; on pathological distributions it can explode.

### A taxonomy of learned indexes

| Design | Mutability | Model family | Error guarantee | Construction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RMI [1] | Static | Hierarchy of linear/NN models | Empirical min/max per leaf | Top-down training |
| FITing-tree | Static | Greedy piecewise-linear | Hard bound *ε* | Greedy segmentation |
| RadixSpline | Static | Linear spline + radix table | Hard bound *ε* | Single pass |
| PGM-index [3] | Static & dynamic | *Optimal* piecewise-linear | Hard bound *ε* | Linear-time optimal PLA |
| ALEX [2] | Dynamic | Adaptive RMI + gapped arrays | Soft (restored by splits) | Bulk load, then adaptive |
| B⁺-tree (baseline) | Dynamic | Key comparisons | *O(log_B n)* worst case | Bulk load / incremental |

The taxonomy reveals the field's central tension: **static learned indexes** (RMI, PGM) achieve the best space–time tradeoffs but cannot absorb writes without rebuilds, while **dynamic** designs (ALEX) pay structural overhead to stay mutable. The PGM-index is notable for spanning both columns with *provable* bounds in each.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis follows four complementary methods.

1. **Theoretical reconstruction.** We restate the key results of [1, 2, 3] — RMI's error-bounded correction, ALEX's Theorem 1 on direct hits, and the PGM-index's space/time bounds — with unified notation, supplying proof sketches where the originals are terse.
2. **Empirical grounding.** We report lookup latencies from the SOSD (Search on Sorted Data) benchmark [4], the community-standard harness that evaluates learned and classical indexes on identical 200M-key datasets, both synthetic (*uniform*, *normal*, *lognormal*) and real (*amzn* book popularity, *face* Facebook IDs, *osm* OpenStreetMap cell IDs, *wiki* Wikipedia timestamps).
3. **Implementation review.** We examined the reference implementations — the RMI codebase [1], the header-only PGM-index library, and ALEX's open-source release — to verify that the published mechanics match the artifacts.
4. **Adversarial analysis.** We draw on the robustness literature [5] and recent theory on dynamic learned indexing with worst-case guarantees [6] to characterize failure modes rather than only best cases.

**Scope and threats to validity.** We restrict attention to *in-memory*, *one-dimensional* indexes over fixed-size (64-bit) keys with unique values — the setting in which all three designs are fully specified. Disk-resident, multi-dimensional, and string-key variants exist but introduce orthogonal concerns. Reported latencies are hardware-sensitive; we treat them as *relative* evidence (learned vs. B-tree on the same machine) rather than absolute claims.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Recursive Model Indexes: Hierarchies of CDF Approximators

The RMI is the architecture that started the field [1]. A two-stage RMI consists of a single **stage-1 (root) model** *f₁* and *B* **stage-2 models** *f₂⁽¹⁾ … f₂⁽ᴮ⁾*, where *B* is the branching factor. Lookup proceeds as follows:

1. Evaluate *p = f₁(k)*, the root's estimate of the key's normalized CDF value.
2. Select the stage-2 model responsible for CDF range *p*, i.e. index *⌊p·B⌋*.
3. Evaluate *pos = f₂(k)*, the predicted array position.
4. Correct with a bounded search over *[pos − ε_min, pos + ε_max]*, where the error bounds are recorded per stage-2 model during training.

Training is **top-down**: the root model is fit to the full key→position mapping first, then each stage-2 model is trained only on the keys the root routes to it. The model zoo includes linear regression, log-linear, cubic, and small neural networks; in practice, *linear models dominate* because a stage-2 model only needs to fit a small, nearly-linear CDF slice, and linear evaluation is a multiply-add. The root is the interesting choice: on highly non-linear CDFs (e.g., *lognormal*), a neural root that learns the global shape dramatically reduces stage-2 error [1].

The RMI's power — and its weakness — is that **nothing is guaranteed**. The min/max error bounds are measured empirically on the training data; on unseen key distributions, or after the data drifts, the true error can exceed the recorded bounds and correctness of the bounded search fails silently unless the implementation falls back to a wider search. Moreover, choosing the configuration (depth, branching factor, model types) requires an expensive **grid search** over the space/performance curve. Marcus et al. addressed this with **CDFShop**, an optimizer that automates RMI tuning [4], but the fundamental staticity remains: *any* significant data change demands retraining.

The following Python sketch captures the essence of two-stage RMI training:

```python
import numpy as np

def train_stage(keys, positions):
    # Closed-form least squares: fit pos = a*key + b
    A = np.vstack([keys, np.ones_like(keys)]).T
    (a, b), *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, positions, rcond=None)
    preds = a * keys + b
    err_lo = np.min(preds - positions)   # most negative residual
    err_hi = np.max(preds - positions)   # most positive residual
    return (a, b, err_lo, err_hi)

def train_rmi(keys, branching=1000):
    n = len(keys)
    positions = np.arange(n, dtype=float)
    root = train_stage(keys, positions / n)          # stage 1: CDF
    a, b, _, _ = root
    children = []
    for j in range(branching):
        # keys routed to child j by the root model
        lo, hi = j / branching, (j + 1) / branching
        mask = (a * keys + b >= lo) & (a * keys + b < hi)
        children.append(train_stage(keys[mask], positions[mask]))
    return root, children

def rmi_lookup(key, root, children, branching, array):
    a, b, _, _ = root
    j = min(int((a * key + b) * branching), branching - 1)
    c, d, e_lo, e_hi = children[j]
    pos = int(c * key + d)
    lo, hi = max(0, pos + int(e_lo)), pos + int(e_hi) + 1
    i = np.searchsorted(array[lo:hi], key) + lo      # last-mile search
    return i if array[i] == key else None
```

### 4.2 ALEX: Updatable Learned Indexes via Gapped Arrays

ALEX [2] confronts RMI's staticity head-on. Its two innovations are a **storage layout optimized for models** — the *gapped array* — and a **search strategy optimized for models** — *exponential search from the predicted position*.

A **gapped array** is a flat array that deliberately contains empty slots (gaps). Each ALEX **data node** stores its keys in a gapped array with a density constrained to *[d_l, d_u]* (defaults 0.6 and 0.8). On insert, ALEX evaluates the node's linear model to predict a slot and places the key there if the slot is free — a **model-based insert**. If the predicted slot is occupied, it shifts to the nearest gap, but because model-based placement keeps keys near their predicted positions, shifts are short on average. Crucially, gaps are *filled with copies of adjacent keys* for search purposes, so the array remains sorted from the searcher's perspective and **exponential search** — probing at distances 1, 2, 4, 8, … from the prediction — finds the key in *O(log ε)* steps, strictly beating binary search whenever the model is accurate [2].

Above the data nodes sits an **adaptive RMI**: inner nodes hold linear models partitioning the key space, and unlike static RMI, the tree *grows and shrinks at different rates* in different key ranges. Four structural operations maintain health, chosen by lightweight **cost models** that weigh expected search cost against reorganization cost:

- **Expand + retrain**: when a data node exceeds the upper density limit but its model still fits well, allocate a larger gapped array and retrain the model.
- **Split sideways**: partition the key range across two data nodes when the model no longer fits (the CDF has become too complex).
- **Split downwards**: convert an overfull data node into an inner node with fresh children.
- **Merge/contract**: reclaim underutilized nodes to bound space.

The elegance of ALEX is captured by its central theorem, which quantifies exactly when model-based inserts achieve *zero* search cost [2]:

> **Theorem (ALEX direct hits):** Let the keys in a data node be *x₁ < x₂ < … < xₙ* with linear model *y = ax + b* (before rounding), and let *c* be the ratio of gapped-array slots to keys. Define *δᵢ = xᵢ₊₁ − xᵢ*. If *c ≥ 1 / (a · minᵢ δᵢ)*, then every key is placed exactly at its predicted position, and every lookup is a direct hit.

*Proof sketch.* Two keys collide after rounding only if their unrounded predictions differ by less than 1. With the capacity multiplier *c*, predictions are *yᵢ = c(axᵢ + b)*, so *|yᵢ − yⱼ| = ca|xᵢ − xⱼ| ≥ ca·min δᵢ ≥ 1* for all *i ≠ j*, giving distinct rounded positions. ∎

In words: *enough slack space relative to the data's minimum gap guarantees perfect prediction*. The theorem justifies ALEX's density discipline — the upper density limit *d_u* is precisely a cap on *1/c*.

```cpp
// ALEX usage (microsoft/ALEX, header-only) — dynamic learned index
#include "alex.h"
alex::Alex<uint64_t, uint64_t> index;
for (auto& [key, payload] : bulk_data)
    index.insert(key, payload);          // model-based insert into gapped array
auto it = index.find(query_key);         // exponential search from prediction
if (it != index.end()) use(it.payload());
```

ALEX's guarantees are, however, **soft**: error bounds are restored by splits and retrains, not enforced structurally, so adversarial insert patterns that repeatedly break the CDF (e.g., inserts concentrated where the model is steepest) trigger frequent reorganizations and latency spikes [7]. Concurrency arrived later via **ALEX+** [2].

### 4.3 PGM-Index: Optimal Piecewise-Linear Approximation with Error Bounds

The PGM-index [3] takes the opposite philosophical stance from RMI: rather than *learning* an arbitrary model and hoping the error stays small, it *constructs* the provably smallest piecewise-linear model subject to a hard error bound *ε*. The result is a learned index with **worst-case guarantees** — the property RMI conspicuously lacks.

Formally, given sorted keys *x₁ < … < xₙ*, an **ε-piecewise-linear approximation (ε-PLA)** is a partition of the keys into *m* segments, each equipped with a linear function *fⱼ* such that *|fⱼ(xᵢ) − i| ≤ ε* for every key in the segment. Ferragina and Vinciguerra showed that the **minimum-cardinality** ε-PLA can be computed in *O(n)* time and *O(m)* space using a streaming convex-hull technique due to O'Rourke — a striking contrast with RMI's expensive top-down training and grid search. The key structural lemma is:

> **Lemma (Segment coverage):** In a minimum-cardinality ε-PLA, every segment covers at least *2ε* keys. Hence *m ≤ n / (2ε)*.

The index is then built **recursively**: the first key of each segment becomes the input to the next level, which is itself ε-PLA-compressed, until a single segment remains. A query descends the levels — each level's model selects the next segment — and finishes with a binary search over a window of at most *2ε* positions. This yields the headline bounds:

> **Theorem (PGM-index bounds):** The PGM-index occupies *Θ(m)* space with *m ≤ n/(2ε)*, and answers rank queries in *O(log m)* model evaluations plus *O(log ε)* last-mile search time.

The error parameter *ε* is the dominant tuning knob [7]: small *ε* (8–32) produces many segments with a tiny, cache-resident last-mile window; large *ε* (256+) minimizes space at the cost of a wider correction search. Because the construction is optimal for each *ε*, the space–time tradeoff curve is the *Pareto frontier* — no piecewise-linear index with error *ε* can use fewer segments.

The **fully-dynamic PGM-index** applies the classic *logarithmic method*: maintain *O(log n)* static PGM-indexes of geometrically growing sizes, inserting into the smallest and periodically merging. This yields *O(log² n)* amortized update time with the same *O(log ε)* query bounds [3] — the first learned index to match B-trees' worst-case update story asymptotically. Recent theory has pushed further, maintaining near-minimum-complexity learned indexes with *O(log² n)* worst-case (not merely amortized) updates [6].

### 4.4 Worst-Case Guarantees: Learned Indexes versus B-Trees

The comparison that matters for storage-engine adoption is not average-case speed but **worst-case predictability**. Here the designs diverge sharply.

A B⁺-tree with fanout *B* guarantees *O(log_B n)* node visits per lookup, *independent of the data distribution*. Its behavior under adversarial input is identical to its behavior under uniform input — the property that makes it the default choice for systems that must never exhibit pathological latency.

Learned indexes trade this uniformity for data-dependent performance:

- **RMI** offers *no* worst-case bound: the recorded min/max errors are empirical, and a distribution shift can silently invalidate them. It is the fastest option on learnable data and the least trustworthy on hostile data.
- **ALEX** offers *restorative* guarantees: splits and retrains repair broken models, but the repair itself is a latency spike, and adversarial inserts can force repeated reorganization [7].
- **PGM-index** offers *hard* bounds — *Θ(n/ε)* space, *O(log ε)* correction — by construction, at the cost of forgoing the extreme compression RMI achieves on near-linear CDFs.
- **Interpolation search**, the classical predecessor of learned indexing, is the cautionary tale: *O(log log n)* expected but *O(n)* worst-case, which is why production systems avoided it despite its elegance.

The honest summary is a **Pareto frontier**, not a ranking:

| Property | B⁺-tree | RMI | ALEX | PGM-index |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lookup worst case | *O(log_B n)*, distribution-free | Unbounded (empirical ε) | Soft; spikes on reorg | *O(log m + log ε)* |
| Index space | *O(n)* pointers | Tiny (KBs–MBs) | Small + gaps | *Θ(n/ε)*, optimal PLA |
| Updates | *O(log_B n)* worst case | Full rebuild | Amortized, adaptive | *O(log² n)* (log method) |
| Failure mode | None (uniform) | Silent bound violation | Split storms | Graceful (ε degrades) |

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### What SOSD measures

The SOSD benchmark [4] evaluates point-lookup latency (nanoseconds per lookup) and index size overhead across 200M-key datasets. The headline results for 32-bit keys are:

| Dataset | B-tree | RMI | RadixSpline | RMI size overhead | B-tree size overhead |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *uniform* (uden32) | 521 ns | 54.2 ns | 64.2 ns | 3% | 16% |
| *normal* (norm32) | 522 ns | **71.7 ns** | **70.9 ns** | 3% | 16% |
| *lognormal* (logn32) | 522 ns | **97.0 ns** | 105 ns | 3% | 16% |
| *amzn* (amzn64) | 601 ns | 266 ns | 288 ns | 3% | 16% |
| *osm* (osmc64) | 599 ns | 402 ns | 437 ns | 3% | 16% |
| *wiki* (wiki64) | 608 ns | 222 ns | 218 ns | 3% | 16% |

Three conclusions emerge [4, 7]. **First**, on smooth synthetic distributions, learned indexes are *3–10× faster* than a B-tree at *one-fifth* the space overhead — the RMI's raison d'être. **Second**, on real-world skewed data (*osm*, *amzn*), the gap narrows to roughly *1.5–2×*: the CDFs are harder to learn, errors grow, and last-mile searches widen. **Third**, under adversarial or heavy-tailed distributions, the error parameter explodes and a tuned B-tree (or the simpler RadixSpline) wins outright — there is no free lunch, only a favorable trade on benign data.

For dynamic workloads, ALEX is reported to match or beat B⁺-trees on read-heavy mixes while remaining smaller, reaching parity on 50/50 read–write mixes, with the caveat that CDF-breaking insert patterns erode its advantage through frequent splits [2, 7]. The PGM-index, in its static form, matches a cache-optimized B-tree's query performance using *up to 83× less space*; in fully-dynamic form it improves query and update time by up to 71% within *1140× less space* than a B⁺-tree [3].

### Proof sketches

**PGM segment bound.** *Claim: m ≤ n/(2ε).* Suppose a segment of a minimum-cardinality ε-PLA covered fewer than *2ε* keys. Its first and last keys have ranks differing by less than *2ε*; the linear interpolant between the endpoints *(x_a, a)* and *(x_b, b)* deviates from any intermediate rank by at most *(b−a)/2 < ε*, so the segment could be merged with a neighbor — contradicting minimality. Each segment therefore "pays for itself" with *2ε* keys, and *m ≤ n/(2ε)*. ∎

**ALEX direct-hit theorem.** Stated and proved in §4.2 above: capacity multiplier *c ≥ 1/(a·minᵢ δᵢ)* separates all unrounded predictions by at least 1, so rounding is injective and every lookup hits its predicted slot directly [2]. ∎

**RMI correction correctness.** *Claim: if the recorded per-model bounds [ε_min, ε_max] hold for the query key, the bounded search returns the exact rank.* The search interval *[pos + ε_min, pos + ε_max]* contains the true position by definition of the bounds; binary (or exponential) search over a sorted interval is exact. The subtlety — and RMI's Achilles' heel — is the *if*: the bounds are measured on training data, not proven for future keys. ∎

## 6. Limitations

1. **Distribution shift and adversarial workloads.** The most replicated finding in the robustness literature is that learned indexes degrade — sometimes catastrophically — when the query or insert distribution diverges from the training distribution [5]. A B-tree's performance is distribution-free; a learned index's is distribution-*defined*. Production deployment therefore requires drift monitoring and retraining budgets, not just a fast benchmark.
2. **Tuning and build cost.** RMI configurations are found by grid search over depth, branching factor, and model types — an expensive offline process partially automated by CDFShop [4]. The PGM-index largely sidesteps this (one parameter, *ε*, with an optimal construction), which is a significant practical advantage.
3. **Write amplification and tail latency.** ALEX's splits, merges, and retrains are stop-the-world events for the affected key range; adversarial inserts can trigger split storms [2, 7]. The dynamic PGM-index's logarithmic method amortizes this but adds a *O(log n)*-way query fanout.
4. **Concurrency.** Fine-grained concurrent learned indexes remain immature: ALEX+ added optimistic concurrency [2], but lock-free learned structures with linearizability proofs are still open research, whereas B-tree latch-coupling (B-link trees) is a solved problem.
5. **Key generality.** The theory assumes unique, fixed-size, totally-ordered keys. Duplicates, variable-length strings, and composite keys complicate CDF modeling and are handled unevenly across implementations.
6. **Memory accounting subtleties.** Headline "index size" figures can mislead: total memory footprint (index plus gaps, buffers, and transient structures) tells a different story, with some analyses finding ALEX's *total* footprint exceeding a B-tree's under insert-heavy workloads even when its pure index is smaller.
7. **Diminishing returns on hard CDFs.** On datasets like *osm_cellids*, where the CDF is locally erratic, learned indexes barely beat classical structures while adding operational complexity — the honest engineer reaches for a B-tree [4, 7].

## 7. Conclusion

Learned index structures have earned their place in the storage-engine design space, but not as B-tree *replacements* — as B-tree *alternatives* on a Pareto frontier governed by data learnability. The RMI [1] proved the concept: models can index. ALEX [2] proved mutability: gapped arrays and model-based inserts make learned structures dynamic, with a crisp theorem characterizing perfect prediction. The PGM-index [3] proved respectability: optimal piecewise-linear approximation restores hard worst-case bounds, *Θ(n/ε)* space and *O(log ε)* correction, in both static and dynamic forms.

For the practitioner, the decision calculus is now clear. **Choose learned indexes** when the workload is read-heavy, the key distribution is stable and smooth, memory is constrained, and tail-latency SLAs tolerate occasional reorganization — and validate on your own data with SOSD [4] before committing. **Choose the PGM-index** when you need learned compression *with* guarantees, especially a single tuning knob. **Choose ALEX** when the workload is mixed read–write and you can budget for adaptive restructuring. **Keep the B-tree** when the distribution is adversarial, unknown, or shifting; when worst-case latency is contractual; or when concurrency and crash recovery must be boring and solved.

The open problems are sharp and inviting: learned indexes with *worst-case* dynamic update bounds are only now emerging [6]; distribution-robust learned structures that degrade gracefully rather than catastrophically remain elusive [5]; and the integration of learned indexes with write-optimized (LSM) and disk-resident engines is largely unexplored. The field began with the provocation that *indexes are models* [1]. Its maturity will be measured by whether models can also be *systems* — predictable, concurrent, and robust under fire.
