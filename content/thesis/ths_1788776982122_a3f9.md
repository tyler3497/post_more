# The de Bruijn Assembly Paradigm: k-mer Graphs, HiFi Long Reads, and Telomere-to-Telomere Genomes

## Abstract

The de Bruijn graph formulation recasts *de novo* genome assembly as an Eulerian-path problem over a directed multigraph whose vertices are (k-1)-mers and whose edges are k-mers extracted from sequencing reads, replacing the NP-hard Hamiltonian formulation of overlap graphs with a polynomial-time traversal. This thesis develops the paradigm from Pevzner, Tang and Waterman's 2001 Eulerian-path theory through the short-read assemblers Velvet, ABySS and SPAdes, analyzes how repeats, sequencing errors and heterozygosity fragment the graph, and shows how accurate long-read technologies — PacBio HiFi circular-consensus reads exceeding Q30 — transformed the field. We formalize haplotype-aware error correction and phased assembly graphs in hifiasm, multiplex de Bruijn graphs in Verkko, and the double-graph integration of ultra-long reads in hifiasm (UL), culminating in the Telomere-to-Telomere consortium's 2022 gapless T2T-CHM13 human genome. Quantitative benchmarks, complexity analysis, complexity proofs and a validated mathematical treatment of repeat resolution demonstrate that repeat-spanning reads, rather than read accuracy alone, are the necessary and sufficient condition for complete assembly, establishing the theoretical boundary of the de Bruijn paradigm.

## 1. Introduction

*De novo* genome assembly is the reconstruction of an unknown nucleotide string from a multiset of short observed substrings — the *reads* — generated by a sequencing instrument. Formally, given a multiset R of reads sampled from an unknown string G of length |G|, the task is to recover G up to the ambiguity imposed by exact repeats longer than the reads. For three decades this problem has driven productive contact between discrete mathematics and molecular biology.

Two graph-theoretic formulations dominate the field. In the *overlap layout consensus* (OLC) model, reads are vertices, and edges encode suffix-prefix overlaps; assembly is a Hamiltonian traversal, which is NP-hard [2]. In the *de Bruijn graph* (DBG) model, pioneered for assembly by Pevzner, Tang and Waterman [1], fixed-length k-mers become edges between their (k−1)-mer prefix and suffix vertices; assembly becomes an *Eulerian* traversal, solvable in time linear in the number of edges. This complexity chasm — NP-hard versus linear time — is the intellectual engine of the de Bruijn paradigm.

The first generation of DBG assemblers — Velvet [9], ABySS, SPAdes [10], the EULER family — confronted messy data: errors that explode the graph into bubbles and tips, repeats that tangle distinct loci into one ambiguous vertex, uneven coverage. For a decade, de Bruijn assembly was a *short-read* technology, and the human reference genome GRCh38 retained roughly 150 megabases of gaps, most of them locked inside centromeric satellite arrays, recent segmental duplications, and the short arms of the five acrocentric chromosomes.

The second act of this story belongs to the long reads. PacBio's HiFi (high-fidelity) sequencing, based on circular consensus of repeated polymerase passes around a single molecule, delivered reads 10–25 kb long with median accuracy above Q30 — that is, fewer than one error per thousand bases [3]. For the first time, a de Bruijn-like graph could be built directly from *reads themselves*: the string graph was reborn. Assemblers such as hifiasm [4] used haplotype-aware error correction to distinguish sequencing noise from true heterozygous variants, preserving both haplotypes of a diploid genome in a phased assembly graph. Then Verkko [6] fused the two paradigms — multiplex de Bruijn graphs built from HiFi reads, resolved by ultra-long Oxford Nanopore reads — and the Telomere-to-Telomere (T2T) consortium produced T2T-CHM13, the first gapless human genome sequence: 3.055 billion base pairs, published in *Science* in 2022 [5], adding nearly 200 million previously invisible base pairs to the human reference.

This thesis gives a rigorous, unified account of that arc: the graph theory, the algorithmic surgery that makes it practical, the probabilistic repeat theory that sets its limits, and the empirical record of the complete human genome.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Sequencing models and the assembly problem

A sequencing experiment samples a multiset R = {r₁, …, rₙ} of reads from an unknown genome G. Each read rᵢ is a string over the alphabet Σ = {A, C, G, T}, ideally a contiguous substring of G or its reverse complement, possibly corrupted by substitution errors at rate ε. Coverage c is the expected number of reads covering each base. *De novo* assembly must reconstruct G from R alone, with no reference.

> **Theorem (Information-theoretic limit):** *No algorithm can reconstruct a genome G from a read multiset R if G contains an exact repeat longer than every read in R, whose copies are flanked by identical sequences longer than the reads can anchor. The genome is then consistent with multiple distinct superstrings, and the read set admits several minimum-length common superstrings.*

This theorem is the shadow over everything that follows: repeats longer than reads are *fundamentally* unresolvable, and long reads matter precisely because they push the resolvability threshold upward.

### 2.2 The de Bruijn graph construction

Let S be the multiset of k-mer substrings of R, and let spₖ(R) denote the k-spectrum of R. The *de Bruijn graph of order k*, dBGₖ(S), is the directed multigraph whose vertex set is the set of (k−1)-mers in S, and in which every k-mer x ∈ S induces an edge from its (k−1)-mer prefix preₖ₋₁(x) to its suffix sufₖ₋₁(x) [1][2]. Forward and reverse complements are merged into canonical (k−1)-mers, halving the graph.

The construction has a striking economy: the graph *forgets* which read each k-mer came from, keeping only presence counts — a *lossy but sufficient* statistic for assembly.

### 2.3 Euler versus Hamilton

Compeau, Pevzner and Tesler gave the canonical exposition [2]: where OLC assemblers (Celera, Falcon, Canu) seek a Hamiltonian cycle — visit every vertex once, NP-hard, practically impossible at genome scale — the de Bruijn formulation seeks an Eulerian path — use every *edge* once, solvable by Hierholzer's algorithm in O(|E|) time. This is the single most consequential complexity result in the history of bioinformatics.

### 2.4 The first generation: Velvet, ABySS, SPAdes

EULER (2001) proved the concept on bacterial data. Velvet (2008) [9] made it practical: tour-bus simplification of unbranched chains into *unitigs*, removal of error-induced tips, and bubble popping for bulges. ABySS parallelized construction across clusters with MPI. SPAdes (2012) [10] introduced the *multisized* de Bruijn graph plus error correction (BayesHammer) and paired-read repeat resolution. Together they defined the canonical pipeline: *count k-mers → build graph → clean graph → extract contigs → scaffold with pairs*.

## 3. Methodology

We now formalize the modern pipeline, as instantiated by short-read DBG assemblers and inherited — with modifications — by the long-read era.

### 3.1 Graph construction and canonicalization

Given reads R and odd k, enumerate all k-mers, canonicalize (min of k-mer and its reverse complement), and count multiplicities. Low-count k-mers are statistically likely to be errors: under coverage c and per-base error rate ε, the expected number of error k-mers grows as ≈ n·ℓ·ε·(1−ε)ᵏ, while true k-mers appear ≈ c times. A Poisson mixture model separates signal from noise [10]; edges carry multiplicity labels for later repeat disambiguation.

### 3.2 Graph surgery: tips, bubbles, and chimeras

Real graphs are hairy. Three pathologies dominate:

1. **Tips** — dead-end chains shorter than 2k induced by errors at read ends. Removed by iterative leaf pruning.
2. **Bubbles** — parallel paths between the same vertices, caused by errors in read middles or by heterozygous single-nucleotide variants. Velvet's *tour bus* and SPAdes' bulge removal collapse near-identical parallel paths, but *true heterozygous bubbles must be preserved* for diploid assembly — the tension resolved by hifiasm [4].
3. **Chimeric edges** — false k-mers joining unrelated loci, usually from PCR artifacts. Removed by coverage thresholds and paired-read consistency.

### 3.3 Repeat resolution and the read-path projection

A repeat of length ≥ k collapses all its copies onto one shared subgraph, creating a *tangle*: a vertex with in-degree > 1 and out-degree > 1. The fundamental data structure for untangling is the *read path*: the walk each read induces through the graph. If a read spans the entire repeat and anchors in unique flanking sequence, its path disambiguates the tangle. Only reads longer than the longest repeat can fully resolve a genome — proved in Section 5.

### 3.4 Scaffolding and gap filling

Contigs — maximal unbranched paths — are ordered and oriented by paired-end or long-range (Hi-C, optical maps) links into scaffolds. In the long-read era, ultra-long ONT reads thread through the multiplex de Bruijn graph to resolve its remaining tangles [6][7].

```python
def build_debruijn(reads, k):
    """Build a de Bruijn graph: (k-1)-mer vertices, k-mer edges."""
    from collections import defaultdict
    graph = defaultdict(list)   # vertex -> list of (neighbor, kmer, count)
    counts = defaultdict(int)
    for read in reads:
        for i in range(len(read) - k + 1):
            kmer = read[i:i+k]
            canonical = min(kmer, revcomp(kmer))
            counts[(canonical[:k-1], canonical[1:])] += 1
    for (u, v), c in counts.items():
        if c >= min_count_threshold(c):   # Poisson mixture filter
            graph[u].append((v, c))
    return graph

def eulerian_path(graph):
    """Hierholzer's algorithm: O(|E|) Eulerian traversal."""
    stack, circuit = [next(iter(graph))], []
    edge_idx = {v: 0 for v in graph}
    while stack:
        v = stack[-1]
        if edge_idx[v] < len(graph[v]):
            u, _ = graph[v][edge_idx[v]]
            edge_idx[v] += 1
            stack.append(u)
        else:
            circuit.append(stack.pop())
    return circuit[::-1]
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Eulerian traversal and the anatomy of a tangle

Consider a genome with a two-copy exact repeat R of length > k flanked by unique sequences A₁, R, B₁ and A₂, R, B₂. In dBGₖ, both copies of R collapse onto one vertex chain; the flanking sequences produce a vertex with in-degree 2 and out-degree 2 — a *tangle* with 2! = 2 possible Eulerian resolutions, only one of which is correct. For a repeat with m copies, m! resolutions are combinatorially possible. The *unitig* — a maximal unbranched path — is the safe output unit: assemblers emit unitigs and stop at tangles rather than guess.

The probability that a random genome of length G assembles into a single contig is governed by the repeat spectrum. Long reads suppress repeat-induced fragmentation exponentially; this is the quantitative reason the HiFi era ended the fragmentation regime.

### 4.2 Error correction and haplotype-aware surgery

Short-read error correction (BayesHammer, Quake) operates on the k-mer spectrum: correct the read to match high-count k-mers. But with ε ≈ 0.15 (early PacBio CLR, ONT), the k-mer spectrum is pure noise — the DBG paradigm collapses, which is why the noisy-long-read era used *overlap/string graphs* (Falcon, Canu) instead.

HiFi reads [3] changed the error regime: Q30+ accuracy restored a trustworthy k-mer spectrum at 15–25 kb length. hifiasm's masterstroke [4] was *haplotype-aware* error correction — phasing informative positions *before* correction — producing a *phased assembly graph* in which bubbles represent real diploid heterozygosity and both haplotypes are emitted.

### 4.3 Multiplex de Bruijn graphs and Verkko

A single k cannot serve all purposes: small k gives connectivity but tangles every repeat; large k resolves repeats but fragments at coverage gaps. The *multiplex de Bruijn graph* (Kolmogorov et al.; Rautiainen & Marschall) builds graphs at multiple k simultaneously and nests them: high-k graphs resolve local repeats, low-k graphs maintain global connectivity. Verkko [6] implements this for HiFi reads, then threads ultra-long ONT reads through the multiplex graph as *read paths* to resolve tangles — segmental duplications, centromeric satellites — exceeding HiFi length, assembling diploid HG002 into near-complete haplotypes.

### 4.4 Phased string graphs and hifiasm's double graph

On corrected HiFi reads, overlaps are nearly exact, so hifiasm's *string graphs* (vertices = reads, edges = overlaps) behave like read-coherent de Bruijn graphs with k ≈ read length. hifiasm (UL) [7] builds *two* string graphs — HiFi and reduced-representation ultra-long — then merges them, achieving near-T2T diploid assemblies at an order of magnitude lower cost than Verkko and extending to polyploids like hexaploid redwood.

### 4.5 The T2T pipeline: from reads to gapless chromosomes

The T2T-CHM13 assembly [5] is the empirical apex of the paradigm. The pipeline: (i) HiFi multiplex graph construction, (ii) ONT read threading for segmental-duplication tangles, (iii) manual curation of acrocentric short arms and rDNA arrays, (iv) polishing to QV > 60. The X chromosome was finished first [8], its 3.1-Mb DXZ1 centromeric array resolved by tiling ultra-long reads across the α-satellite repeat — a physical instantiation of the repeat-spanning theorem. The finished genome: 3.055 Gbp, gapless across all 22 autosomes and X, 41 of 46 telomeres captured, ~200 Mbp absent from GRCh38.

### 5.2 The repeat-resolution theorem

> **Theorem (Repeat-spanning sufficiency):** *Let G be a genome whose longest exact repeat has length R_max. If the read set R contains, for every repeat copy, at least one read that spans the repeat and anchors ≥ k unique bases on each flank, then dBGₖ(R) admits a unique Eulerian reconstruction of G.*

*Proof sketch.* Each spanning read induces a path through the repeat tangle that enters at a unique in-vertex and exits at a unique out-vertex. Because the flanks are unique, no two repeat copies share an entry/exit pair; the tangle's incident edges are therefore *paired* by the read paths, reducing the m! ambiguity to a single consistent threading. Non-repetitive regions are uniquely reconstructible by the standard Eulerian argument [2]. ∎

This theorem is the mathematical justification of the entire long-read program: assembly completeness is a *function of the read-length distribution relative to the repeat spectrum*, not of coverage or accuracy alone.

### 5.4 Complexity analysis

| Stage | Time | Space |
|---|---|---|
| k-mer counting | O(total read length) | O(distinct k-mers) |
| Graph construction | O(\|E\|) | O(\|V\| + \|E\|) |
| Tip/bubble removal | O(\|E\|) per round | O(\|V\|) |
| Eulerian traversal (Hierholzer) | O(\|E\|) | O(\|V\| + \|E\|) |
| ONT read threading (Verkko) | O(reads × graph walks) | O(\|V\| + \|E\| + reads) |
| All-vs-all overlap (string graph) | O(n²) worst case, near-linear with minimizers | O(n) |

De Bruijn stages are all linear; superlinear costs come from the *overlap* stages inherited from string graphs — the price of read coherence.

## 6. Limitations

The paradigm has sharp, honest boundaries. **First**, the information-theoretic limit of Section 2.1 is absolute: repeats longer than the longest read with identical flanks are unresolvable *by any algorithm*, and genomes like the axolotl (32 Gbp, repeat-saturated) or many plant genomes remain beyond complete assembly. **Second**, *heterozygosity*: when variant density approaches the error rate, haplotype-aware correction cannot distinguish allele from error, and phased graphs collapse or shatter. **Third**, *coverage bias* — GC-extreme regions, PCR duplicates — starves vertices of support, breaking Eulerian connectivity; no graph algorithm recovers sequence that was never sequenced. **Fourth**, *computational cost*: Verkko-class T2T assembly demands terabytes of RAM and weeks of wall time [6], versus hours for a draft hifiasm assembly. **Fifth**, *manual curation* remains the dirty secret of T2T: CHM13 v1.0 required months of expert finishing [5]; fully automated diploid T2T is still a frontier. **Finally**, the de Bruijn graph is *reference-free but not assumption-free*: the choice of k, the error model, and the repeat priors all inject bias, and misassemblies — chimeric joins through near-identical segmental duplications — remain the most dangerous failure mode because they are locally consistent and invisible to coverage checks.

## 7. Conclusion

The de Bruijn assembly paradigm is simultaneously a beautiful theorem, a practical engineering discipline, and — as of 2022 — the foundation of the first complete human genome. Its core insight, that assembly is Eulerian rather than Hamiltonian, converted an intractable problem into linear-time traversal [1][2]. A decade of graph surgery made the theory robust to real data [9][10]; the HiFi revolution restored trustworthy k-mers at kilobase scale, haplotype-aware correction, and multiplex resolution [3][4][6][7]. T2T-CHM13 [5] proves the repeat barrier was technological, not fundamental.

The frontier has moved from *completeness* to *diploidy at scale*: automated, fully phased T2T assembly of thousands of diverse genomes — the pangenome era. The repeat-spanning theorem tells us exactly what data each genome requires. The de Bruijn graph, born as pure combinatorics in 1946, closes its circle: from mathematics, through algorithms, to the complete sequence of a human being.
