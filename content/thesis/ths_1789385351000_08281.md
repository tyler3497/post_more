# Valuations, Balancing, and the Space of Trees: Tropical Geometry from Phylogenetic Statistics to Enumerative Counts

## Abstract

Tropical geometry replaces the arithmetic of a valued field with the piecewise-linear algebra of the tropical semiring, where addition becomes minimization and multiplication becomes ordinary addition. Polynomials become convex piecewise-linear functions and varieties become weighted polyhedral complexes, yet degree, genus, and enumerative invariants survive as combinatorial data. We develop the subject along an axis from pure algebra to applied statistics: the idempotent semiring, Maslov dequantization, and valuation-based tropicalization with the Fundamental Theorem's three characterizations; tropical linear spaces and the Speyer–Sturmfels tropical Grassmannian, where tropical lines are phylogenetic trees [3]. We compare the Billera–Holmes–Vogtmann CAT(0) orthant geometry of tree space with the tropical metric program — tropical line segments, tropical convexity, and tropical kernel density estimation of gene-tree distributions [4][5][6]. We then present Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem [2] as the field's enumerative engine, with the twelve rational cubics as a worked example, and close with honest limitations: realizability gaps, superabundance, genericity hypotheses, and the computational cost of Gröbner and secondary fans.

## 1 Intro

Few constructions in modern mathematics are as audacious as tropical geometry's: it keeps the *syntax* of algebraic geometry — polynomials, varieties, intersections, moduli — while replacing the *semantics* of arithmetic. Addition is demoted to taking a minimum, multiplication to ordinary addition, and the resulting world is one in which curves are finite graphs, surfaces are glued polygons, and theorems of enumerative geometry become exercises in counting graphs with multiplicities. The surprise is not that this works at all but how much it remembers: degrees, genera, and even Gromov–Witten invariants of toric varieties are preserved, now computable by combinatorics [1][2].

The field's most striking intellectual property is its capacity to travel. The same min-plus formalism that underlies shortest-path algorithms and the Viterbi decoder turns out, in the hands of Speyer and Sturmfels [3], to parametrize spaces of phylogenetic trees, so that evolutionary biologists' trees are literally tropical lines. A lineage that begins with Maslov's dequantization of mathematical physics thus runs through valuation theory, polyhedral combinatorics, enumerative geometry, and computational biology. This essay follows that lineage end to end, and argues that tropical geometry's central contribution is methodological: it is a *degeneration technique* that makes global invariants local and combinatorial, and its applications succeed precisely where the degenerate picture captures the essence of the original problem.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The tropical semiring

The **tropical semiring** is the set T = R ∪ {∞} equipped with the operations

- **tropical addition:** x ⊕ y = min(x, y),
- **tropical multiplication:** x ⊗ y = x + y,

with ∞ as additive identity (x ⊕ ∞ = x) and 0 as multiplicative identity (x ⊗ 0 = x). It is a semiring but not a ring: there is no additive inverse, because the minimum operation is *idempotent*, x ⊕ x = x. An equivalent convention, the max-plus algebra on R ∪ {−∞}, is obtained by the isomorphism x ↦ −x; the choice is conventional, and we work with min-plus throughout except where ultrametrics favor max-plus conventions [1][4].

The absence of subtraction is not a defect but the engine of the subject: idempotency forces the Frobenius identity (x ⊕ y)⊗n = x⊗n ⊕ y⊗n, and it is what makes tropical polynomials piecewise linear.

### 2.2 Tropical polynomials and their zero loci

A **tropical polynomial** in n variables is a tropical linear combination of monomials with real coefficients:

F(x) = ⊕_{a ∈ A} (c_a ⊗ x_1^{⊗a_1} ⊗ ⋯ ⊗ x_n^{⊗a_n}) = min_{a ∈ A} (c_a + ⟨a, x⟩),

where A ⊂ N^n is finite and each c_a ∈ R. Thus a tropical polynomial *is* a piecewise-linear convex function on R^n, the lower envelope of finitely many affine-linear forms. The **tropical hypersurface** T(F) is defined as the locus where F is not differentiable — equivalently, the set of points x where the minimum is attained at least twice. This is an (n−1)-dimensional polyhedral complex in R^n [1][3]. The balancing condition already appears here: at each vertex of a tropical curve, the weighted primitive direction vectors of the incident edges sum to zero.

### 2.3 Maslov dequantization

The philosophical justification for the min-plus convention is **Maslov dequantization**. Consider the family of operations

x ⊕_h y = −h log(e^{−x/h} + e^{−y/h}), x ⊗_h y = x + y,

for h > 0. As h → 0+, the Laplace principle gives x ⊕_h y → min(x, y): the dominant (smallest) term wins exponentially. Thus the tropical semiring is the *classical limit* (h → 0) of ordinary arithmetic conjugated by the logarithmic map [1].

### 2.4 Valuations

Algebraically, tropicalization is governed by **valuations**. A valued field (K, v) with v: K× → R satisfies v(xy) = v(x) + v(y) and the ultrametric inequality v(x + y) ≥ min(v(x), v(y)), with equality when v(x) ≠ v(y). The Puiseux series field C{{t}} — formal power series with rational exponents and bounded-below support — with valuation v sending a series to its lowest exponent, is the universal example: it is algebraically closed of characteristic zero, and the valuation of a sum is the minimum of the valuations unless cancellation occurs. Tropicalization is, in essence, the functor that records only valuations, discarding the coefficients [1][3].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Three characterizations: the Fundamental Theorem

Let K be an algebraically closed field with a nontrivial valuation and I an ideal in the Laurent polynomial ring in n variables. The **tropicalization** trop(I) admits three equivalent descriptions [1]:

1. **Valuation image:** { (v(z_1), ..., v(z_n)) : z ∈ V(I) }, the coordinatewise valuation of the classical variety;
2. **Tropical prevariety:** ⋂_{f ∈ I} T(trop(f)), the intersection of the tropical hypersurfaces of the tropicalizations of *all* polynomials in I;
3. **Gröbner fan slice:** the polyhedral subcomplex of the Gröbner fan of I cut out by the weight cone, consisting of weights w for which the initial ideal in_w(I) contains no monomial.

> **Theorem:** (Fundamental Theorem of Tropical Geometry). The three sets above coincide.

The equivalence of (1) and (2) is the statement that tropicalization detects exactly the valuation-theoretic shadow of the variety; the equivalence with (3) makes tropicalization *computable* via Gröbner bases over valued fields, and identifies tropical varieties as subcomplexes of Gröbner fans — the bridge between tropical geometry and computational commutative algebra [1].

### 3.2 Weights and the structure theorem

Each maximal cell of trop(V(I)) carries a positive integer **multiplicity**, defined via the initial ideal: for a weight w in the relative interior of a maximal cell, the multiplicity is the sum of the multiplicities of the minimal primes of in_w(I) not containing monomials. The **Structure Theorem** asserts that trop(V(I)) is a pure-dimensional weighted polyhedral complex satisfying the **balancing condition**: at every codimension-one face, the weighted primitive normal vectors of the adjacent maximal cells sum to zero [1][3].

### 3.3 Tropical linear spaces and the Plücker ideal

The class of tropical varieties most relevant to phylogenetics is the **tropical linear spaces**. Let Gr(k, n) be the Grassmannian of k-planes in C^n, embedded by Plücker coordinates into projective space; its ideal I_{k,n} is generated by quadratic Plücker relations. The **tropical Grassmannian** G_{k,n} is the tropicalization trop(I_{k,n}): a polyhedral fan parametrizing all tropicalized k-planes, i.e., all tropical linear spaces of dimension k−1 in tropical projective space [3].

The faces of G_{k,n} correspond to monomial-free initial ideals of the Plücker ideal, and the entire object is a subfan of the Gröbner fan — hence computable in principle, though the Gröbner fan of the Plücker ideal grows explosively. Already G_{3,6} is a simplicial complex glued from 1035 tetrahedra [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The idempotent algebra beneath the geometry

Before geometry, there is algebra, and the tropical semiring deserves study as an algebraic object in its own right. It is the primordial example of an **idempotent semiring**, and its module theory is the theory of **tropical convexity**: a subset of the tropical projective torus is tropically convex if it contains the tropical line segment between any two of its points. Tropical linear algebra replaces Gaussian elimination with the assignment problem: the tropical determinant of a matrix is its optimal assignment value, and tropical eigenvalues govern the asymptotics of matrix powers [1].

### 4.2 Tropical curves: graphs with memory of algebra

A **tropical curve** is a metric graph — a finite graph whose edges have lengths, with unbounded rays permitted — equipped with positive integer weights on edges, satisfying the balancing condition at every vertex: the weighted primitive direction vectors of incident edges sum to zero. Abstractly, a tropical curve of genus g is a metric graph of first Betti number g; concretely in the plane, it is a piecewise-linear graph dual to a regular subdivision of its Newton polygon [2][3].

The duality is the combinatorial heart of the subject. Given a plane tropical curve C of degree d (meaning its unbounded rays have total weight d in each of the three coordinate directions), its Newton polygon is the triangle Δ_d with vertices (0,0), (d,0), (0,d). A regular subdivision of Δ_d induced by the coefficients determines C and vice versa:

- vertices of C ↔ two-dimensional cells of the subdivision,
- edges of C ↔ interior edges of the subdivision (perpendicular to them), with the tropical weight equal to the lattice length of the dual segment,
- unbounded rays of C ↔ boundary edges of Δ_d,
- components of the complement of C ↔ lattice points of Δ_d.

The **genus** of C is the first Betti number of the graph — equivalently the number of interior lattice points not used as subdivision vertices — and balancing is the combinatorial residue of the fact that C came from algebra [1][2].

### 4.3 The tropical Grassmannian and the tree theorem

The crown jewel of the applied side is the theorem of Speyer and Sturmfels [3]:

> **Theorem:** (Speyer–Sturmfels). Lines in tropical projective space are trees. The tropical Grassmannian G_{2,n} — the tropicalization of the Plücker ideal of 2-planes — equals the space of phylogenetic trees on n labeled leaves.

The proof runs through **ultrametrics** and the four-point condition. A metric d on the label set is a tree metric if and only if, for every four leaves, the maximum of the three pairwise sums d(i,j) + d(k,l) is attained at least twice — a condition that is *already tropical*: it says that the Plücker-style expression attains its maximum twice. Dress and Terasawa observed that ultrametrics form a tropical linear space, and Speyer–Sturmfels show that the full tree space is precisely the tropical variety of the Plücker relations. Higher Grassmannians G_{k,n} are thus the natural generalization of tree space to "higher trees" — tropical linear spaces whose combinatorics is only beginning to be explored [3][4].

### 4.4 Two geometries of tree space: BHV versus tropical

The classical geometric model of tree space is due to Billera, Holmes, and Vogtmann: **BHV space** is a CAT(0) cubical complex in which each orthant R_{≥0}^{n−3} corresponds to a binary tree topology, parametrized by its internal edge lengths, glued along faces where edges collapse. The CAT(0) property gives unique geodesics, computable in polynomial time by the Owen–Provan algorithm, and supports Fréchet means and other statistics [5].

The tropical viewpoint offers a competing geometry. Realizing tree space as the tropical linear space of ultrametrics (in the max-plus convention) equips it with **tropical convexity** and the **tropical metric**, under which the tropical line segment between two trees can be computed faster than the BHV geodesic, and geodesic triangles have bounded complexity — in BHV space, by contrast, the dimension of a geodesic triangle can be arbitrarily high [4]. Lin and Sturmfels showed that tropical segments behave more subtly than BHV geodesics: while a BHV geodesic between very different topologies typically traverses the star tree at the origin, the tropical segment bypasses it, preserving more shared structure [5].

This is not merely aesthetic. Weyenberg and coauthors built **tropical kernel density estimation** on tree space: using the tropical metric to weight the influence of nearby trees, they estimated gene-tree distributions under the multispecies coalescent model and found the tropical KDE faster and more accurate than BHV-based alternatives, with a successful application to Apicomplexa data [6]. Here tropical geometry is not a metaphor but a working statistical technology.

### 4.5 Enumerative geometry: the correspondence principle

The founding triumph of the subject is Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem [2]:

> **Theorem:** (Mikhalkin). Let N_{g,d} be the number of complex curves of genus g and degree d in the complex projective plane through 3d − 1 + g generic points, and let N^{trop}_{g,d} be the number of tropical curves of the same discrete data through a generic configuration in the tropical projective plane, counted with vertex multiplicities. Then N_{g,d} = N^{trop}_{g,d}.

The multiplicity of a tropical curve is the product over its trivalent vertices of the lattice area of the dual triangle (equivalently, the absolute value of the determinant of two incident primitive edge directions). The proof degenerates the point configuration to a vertically stretched position, where tropical curves become **floor diagrams** — decorated graphs amenable to exact combinatorial enumeration — and shows that each tropical curve lifts to precisely mult(C) complex curves. The first nontrivial case: there are 12 rational cubics through 8 points, and the tropical computation recovers 12 as a weighted count of three floor diagrams [2].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Worked example: the tropical line

Let F(x, y) = x ⊕ y ⊕ 0 = min(x, y, 0). The plane divides into three regions where a single term dominates; the corner locus, where the minimum is attained twice, consists of three rays from the origin with primitive directions (1,1), (−1,0), and (0,−1), each of weight 1. The direction vectors sum to zero — balancing holds. The dual subdivision is the standard triangulation of the triangle with vertices (0,0), (1,0), (0,1): three unit triangles meeting at the single trivalent vertex [1][3].

### 5.2 The twelve rational cubics, tropically

For degree d = 3, the Newton triangle Δ_3 has 10 lattice points. Stretching 8 point constraints vertically, the tropical rational cubics through them correspond to floor diagrams: connected graphs on 3 ordered floors with elevators (vertical edges) between consecutive floors, weighted by positive integers summing appropriately. There are exactly three unmarked floor diagrams; equipping them with markings compatible with the stretched configuration and weighting each by the product of squares of its edge weights gives contributions 5 + 3 + 4 = 12. The diagram contributing 4 carries an edge of weight 2 — the tropical shadow of a classical cubic with a higher-order contact [2].

| Degree d | Point constraints 3d − 1 | Rational curves N_{0,d} |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 | 1 |
| 2 | 5 | 1 |
| 3 | 8 | 12 |
| 4 | 11 | 620 |
| 5 | 14 | 87304 |

Each entry is now computable by tropical combinatorics rather than intersection theory on moduli spaces [2].

### 5.3 Proof sketch: why balancing holds

Let C be a curve over the Puiseux series field K and Γ = trop(C) its tropicalization. Near a vertex v of Γ, the branches of C tropicalizing near v correspond to the edges incident to v; along the e-th branch, the valuations of the coordinate functions grow linearly with slope equal to the primitive direction u_e, and the ramification index contributes the weight w_e. The sum Σ_e w_e u_e is the vector of total orders of zeros minus poles of the coordinate functions on the part of C over v — and a meromorphic function on a complete curve has total order zero. Hence the sum vanishes: balancing is the residue theorem, tropicalized [1][2].

### 5.4 Computation: min-plus matrices are shortest paths

```python
import numpy as np

INF = float("inf")

def trop_mul(A, B):
    """Min-plus matrix product: (A ⊗ B)[i,j] = min_k A[i,k] + B[k,j]."""
    n = A.shape[0]
    C = np.full((n, n), INF)
    for i in range(n):
        for k in range(n):
            if A[i, k] < INF:
                C[i, :] = np.minimum(C[i, :], A[i, k] + B[k, :])
    return C

def all_pairs_shortest(W):
    """Tropical Kleene star: min-plus powers stabilize at shortest paths."""
    n = W.shape[0]
    D = W.copy()
    np.fill_diagonal(D, 0.0)
    P = D.copy()
    for _ in range(n - 1):
        P = np.minimum(P, trop_mul(P, D))
    return P

def is_tree_metric(d):
    """Four-point condition: for all quadruples, the max of the three
    pairwise sums is attained at least twice (max-plus formulation)."""
    n = d.shape[0]
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i + 1, n):
            for k in range(j + 1, n):
                for l in range(k + 1, n):
                    s = sorted([d[i,j] + d[k,l], d[i,k] + d[j,l], d[i,l] + d[j,k]])
                    if not abs(s[2] - s[1]) < 1e-9:
                        return False
    return True

# Tree with internal edge of length 2 separating {0,1} from {2,3},
# leaf edges of length 1.
d = np.array([[0, 2, 5, 5],
              [2, 0, 5, 5],
              [5, 5, 0, 2],
              [5, 5, 2, 0]], dtype=float)
print("tree metric:", is_tree_metric(d))   # True
print(all_pairs_shortest(np.array([[0, 3, INF],
                                  [INF, 0, 1],
                                  [INF, INF, 0]], dtype=float)))
```

The four-point condition in the code is the computational form of the Plücker tropicalization: a dissimilarity map is a tree metric exactly when it lies on the tropical Grassmannian G_{2,n} [3]. The shortest-path routine is tropical linear algebra in action — the same min-plus product whose geometry the essay studies.

## 6 Limitations

Tropical geometry is powerful but not omnipotent, and its boundaries are instructive.

1. **Realizability and superabundance.** Not every balanced weighted graph lifts to an algebraic curve. In genus 1, Speyer's *well-spacedness* criterion characterizes realizable tropical elliptic curves; in higher genus, *superabundant* curves — whose deformation space exceeds the expected dimension — may fail to lift, and no complete combinatorial criterion is known. The tropical moduli space and the Berkovich skeleton agree only after careful compactification [1].

2. **Genericity hypotheses.** The correspondence theorem counts curves through *generic* configurations with toric transversality. Special configurations, non-toric ambient varieties, and small positive characteristic all require substantial extra machinery; Nishinou–Siebert and Tyomkin cover large parts of this territory, but it remains technically demanding [2].

3. **The BHV–tropical comparison is incomplete.** Tropical segments are cheaper and better-behaved than BHV geodesics in several respects [4][5], but the tropical metric does not yet support the full statistical toolkit (confidence regions, PCA analogues) built on CAT(0) geometry, and the biological interpretation of tropical convexity is less established than that of orthant geometry.

4. **Computational complexity.** Enumerating regular subdivisions means enumerating cones of the secondary fan, whose size grows explosively; Gröbner-fan computations of tropical varieties stall on modest examples despite the combinatorial setting. The tropical Grassmannian G_{3,6} is computable, but G_{3,7} and beyond remain largely out of reach [1][3].

5. **Dependence on embedding.** Tropicalization is not intrinsic: it depends on the chosen embedding into a torus (equivalently, the choice of coordinates). Different embeddings give different — though related — tropical varieties, so the "tropicalization of a variety" is properly the tropicalization of an embedded variety [1].

These are active research frontiers, not defects: each marks a place where the dictionary between the classical and tropical worlds is still being written.

## 7 Conclusion

Tropical geometry replaces fields with idempotent semirings and thereby trades algebraic subtlety for combinatorial transparency — and the trade is astonishingly favorable. From the min-plus algebra of shortest paths, through Maslov dequantization and valuation theory, to the balancing condition on tropical curves, the duality with Newton subdivisions, and the identification of phylogenetic tree space with the tropical Grassmannian, a single principle recurs: *the dominant term wins, and geometry lives where dominance is contested*.

The subject's two great applied victories illustrate complementary virtues. Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem [2] shows that degeneration can be a *counting machine*: intricate intersection numbers on moduli spaces become weighted counts of graphs. The tropical tree-space program [3][4][5][6] shows that the same degeneration can be a *statistical technology*: re-geometrizing evolutionary biology's central object yields faster algorithms and better density estimates on real data.

The deepest lesson may be methodological. Tropicalization is the most elementary member of a family of degeneration techniques — toric degenerations, Gröbner degenerations, Berkovich skeletons — that make global invariants local and combinatorial. Its power comes with an obligation: to check, in each application, that the degenerate picture remembers what matters.
