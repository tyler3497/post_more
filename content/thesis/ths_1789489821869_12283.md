# High-Dimensional Sparse Regression: The Lasso, the Dantzig Selector, and Statistical Phase Transitions

## Abstract

Modern statistical practice routinely confronts linear models in which the number of covariates $p$ dwarfs the sample size $n$ — genomics, proteomics, and high-resolution imaging being canonical examples. This thesis develops the theory and practice of **sparse regression** in the $p \gg n$ regime, where classical least squares collapses and structural assumptions on the regression vector become unavoidable. We begin with Tibshirani's Lasso (1996) [1], derive its oracle inequalities under restricted eigenvalue conditions, and contrast it with the Dantzig selector of Candès and Tao [2] through the unified analysis of Bickel, Ritov and Tsybakov [3]. We examine the sharp boundary between estimation and *selection* consistency governed by the irrepresentable condition [6], describe neighborhood selection for graphical models [4], and explain how debiased Lasso constructions restore classical inferential guarantees [7]. We close the theoretical arc with the Donoho–Tanner phase transition geometry of convex recovery [5] and the minimax rates that no estimator can improve upon [9]. A small simulation study, a proof sketch via the primal–dual witness method, and algorithmic schematics complete the picture.

---

## 1 Introduction

The linear model

$$y = X\beta^{\star} + \varepsilon, \qquad y \in \mathbb{R}^{n},\; X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p},\; \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^{2} I_n)$$

has been the workhorse of applied statistics for two centuries. Its classical analysis assumes $n > p$ and a full-rank design, under which ordinary least squares is the best linear unbiased estimator. The twenty-first century has inverted this ordering: microarray studies measure tens of thousands of gene expression levels on a hundred patients; astronomical surveys record millions of pixel intensities to locate a handful of sources. In each case $p \gg n$, the Gram matrix $X^{\top}X$ is singular and infinitely many coefficient vectors fit the data exactly.

> **Theorem (Failure of least squares in high dimensions):** When $p > n$, the ordinary least squares criterion $\|y - X\beta\|_2^2$ is minimized by an affine subspace of dimension at least $p - n$, so least squares cannot select among competing explanations without further structure.

The productive structural assumption is *sparsity*: the unknown coefficient vector $\beta^{\star}$ is supported on a small, unknown index set $S \subset \{1,\dots,p\}$ of cardinality $k = |\mathrm{supp}(\beta^{\star})| \ll p$. If $S$ were known we would simply regress $y$ on $X_S$; since it is unknown, searching over $\binom{p}{k}$ subsets is a combinatorial explosion. This thesis traces how *convex relaxation* tamed that explosion: the Lasso of Tibshirani [1], the Dantzig selector [2], and the sharp phase-transition theory of Donoho and Tanner [5] that precisely demarcates where convex methods succeed and where nothing can.

![Geometry of the ℓ1 and ℓ2 penalty balls: the diamond-shaped ℓ1 constraint touches the least-squares ellipse at a coordinate axis, producing a sparse solution, while the smooth ℓ2 ball yields a dense one](public/thesis/ths_1789489821869_12283-0.webp)

This exposition develops the Lasso's oracle inequalities under restricted eigenvalue conditions [3], contrasts it with the Dantzig selector [2], separates estimation from selection consistency via the irrepresentable condition and its sharp thresholds [6], and closes with debiased inference [7], graphical-model estimation [4], the Donoho–Tanner phase transition geometry [5], and the minimax rates no estimator can beat [9].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The high-dimensional linear model

We work with the fixed-design model $y = X\beta^{\star} + \varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon$ is sub-Gaussian noise with variance proxy $\sigma^2$. The columns of $X$ are standardized so that $\|X_j\|_2^2 = n$ for all $j$. The sparsity assumption states that $\|\beta^{\star}\|_0 := |\{j : \beta^{\star}_j \neq 0\}| = k \ll p$. Note that $k$ is *not* the same as $\min(n,p)$; it is typically an order of magnitude smaller than $n$.

Classical approaches and their limitations are summarized below.

| Method | Objective | Handles $p > n$? | Variable selection? | Convex? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary least squares | $\min_\beta \|y - X\beta\|_2^2$ | No (singular Gram matrix) | No | Yes |
| Ridge regression | $\min_\beta \|y - X\beta\|_2^2 + \lambda\|\beta\|_2^2$ | Yes | No (dense shrinkage) | Yes |
| Best subset ($\ell_0$) | $\min_\beta \|y - X\beta\|_2^2 + \lambda\|\beta\|_0$ | Yes | Yes | **No** (NP-hard) |
| Lasso | $\min_\beta \|y - X\beta\|_2^2 + \lambda\|\beta\|_1$ | Yes | Yes (soft thresholding) | Yes |
| Dantzig selector | $\min \|\beta\|_1$ s.t. $\|X^{\top}(y - X\beta)\|_\infty \le \lambda$ | Yes | Yes | Yes |

The table exposes the fundamental tension: the $\ell_0$ penalty delivers exactly the desired combinatorial behavior but renders optimization intractable, while ridge regression is tractable but never produces exact zeros. The $\ell_1$ norm is the *convex envelope* of the $\ell_0$ count on the unit cube, and it is the smallest relaxation that remains both convex and sparsity-inducing [1][9].

### 2.2 Why the $\ell_1$ ball is special

Consider the constrained forms. Ridge regression solves $\min \|y - X\beta\|_2^2$ subject to $\|\beta\|_2^2 \le t$, a smooth spherical constraint. The Lasso instead constrains $\|\beta\|_1 \le t$, a *cross-polytope* — a diamond in two dimensions — whose vertices lie on the coordinate axes. The least-squares loss has elliptical contours centered at $\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{OLS}}$; the solution is the first point of contact between an expanding ellipse and the constraint set. Because the $\ell_1$ ball has *corners*, contact occurs at a corner with positive probability, forcing coordinates to zero exactly; the smooth $\ell_2$ ball instead touches at a generic dense point [1][3].

### 2.3 Shrinkage, selection, and the Bayesian view

The Lasso is simultaneously a shrinkage estimator and a selection device — the maximum a posteriori estimate under independent Laplace priors [1] — whose frequentist guarantees require the design to be well-conditioned only on sparse directions, formalized by the restricted eigenvalue and compatibility conditions of [3].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Lasso estimator

Tibshirani introduced the Lasso as the solution of the quadratic program [1]

$$\hat{\beta}(\lambda) = \arg\min_{\beta \in \mathbb{R}^p} \left\{ \frac{1}{2n}\|y - X\beta\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\beta\|_1 \right\}, \qquad \lambda > 0,$$

with $\|\beta\|_1 = \sum_j |\beta_j|$. The tuning parameter $\lambda$ traces the full trade-off curve from the null model ($\lambda \to \infty$) to interpolation ($\lambda \to 0^+$), and is chosen in practice by cross-validation or the theoretical scaling $\lambda \asymp \sigma\sqrt{(\log p)/n}$.

The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions characterize the solution through the subgradient of the $\ell_1$ norm:

> **Theorem (KKT for the Lasso):** A vector $\hat{\beta}$ minimizes the Lasso objective if and only if, for every coordinate $j$,
> $$-\frac{1}{n}X_j^{\top}(y - X\hat{\beta}) + \lambda \hat{z}_j = 0, \quad \text{where } \hat{z}_j \in \partial |\hat{\beta}_j| = \begin{cases} \{\mathrm{sign}(\hat{\beta}_j)\} & \hat{\beta}_j \neq 0, \\ \text{the closed interval between } -1 \text{ and } 1 & \hat{\beta}_j = 0. \end{cases}$$

In the special case of an orthonormal design ($X^{\top}X = nI$), the solution is the *soft-thresholding* operator applied to the marginal correlations,

$$\hat{\beta}_j = S_{\lambda}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}X_j^{\top}y\right), \qquad S_{\lambda}(z) = \mathrm{sign}(z)\,(|z| - \lambda)_+,$$

### 3.2 The Dantzig selector

Candès and Tao proposed a strikingly different convex formulation [2]:

$$\hat{\beta}^{\mathrm{DS}} = \arg\min_{\beta} \|\beta\|_1 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \Big\|\frac{1}{n}X^{\top}(y - X\beta)\Big\|_{\infty} \le \lambda\sigma,$$

which minimizes the $\ell_1$ norm subject to a bound on the *maximum absolute correlation* between the residuals and the columns of $X$. Because the true vector satisfies $\|n^{-1}X^{\top}\varepsilon\|_\infty \lesssim \sigma\sqrt{(\log p)/n}$ with high probability, $\beta^{\star}$ is feasible, and the $\ell_1$ minimizer over the feasible polytope is close to it [2][3].

### 3.3 Restricted eigenvalue and compatibility conditions

No estimator can recover $\beta^{\star}$ for arbitrary designs — two sparse vectors in the null space of $X$ are indistinguishable. Guarantees therefore require the design to be well-behaved *on sparse vectors*, formalized by Bickel, Ritov and Tsybakov [3]:

> **Definition (Restricted eigenvalue):** The design $X$ satisfies the restricted eigenvalue condition $\mathrm{RE}(S, \gamma)$ with constant $\gamma > 0$ if
> $$\frac{1}{n}\|Xv\|_2^2 \ge \gamma \|v\|_2^2 \qquad \text{for all } v \in \mathcal{C}(S),$$
> where $\mathcal{C}(S) = \{v : \|v_{S^c}\|_1 \le 3\|v_S\|_1\}$ is the cone of vectors whose mass off the support $S$ is not too large relative to their mass on $S$.

A weaker sibling, the *compatibility condition*, replaces the right-hand side by $\phi_0^2 \|v_S\|_1^2 / |S|$ and suffices for prediction guarantees [9]. Both conditions hold with high probability for Gaussian and sub-Gaussian random designs once $n \gtrsim k \log(p/k)$, and they are strictly weaker than the restricted isometry property of early compressed-sensing theory [2][5]. Under $\mathrm{RE}$, both the Lasso and the Dantzig selector satisfy oracle inequalities

$$\frac{1}{n}\|X(\hat{\beta} - \beta^{\star})\|_2^2 \;\lesssim\; \sigma^2 \frac{k \log p}{n}, \qquad \|\hat{\beta} - \beta^{\star}\|_2^2 \;\lesssim\; \sigma^2 \frac{k \log p}{n},$$

with constants depending on $\gamma$ and on $\lambda$ [3] — a landmark unification placing both estimators on identical footing.

### 3.4 Computation: LARS and coordinate descent

Efron, Hastie, Johnstone and Tibshirani showed that the full Lasso *solution path* $\hat{\beta}(\lambda)$ is piecewise linear, and their Least Angle Regression (LARS) algorithm computes it at the cost of a single least-squares fit [8]. Each kink corresponds to a variable entering or leaving the active set, with the equiangular direction keeping all active correlations tied as the residual shrinks.

![The Lasso regularization path: coefficient trajectories as piecewise-linear functions of the penalty parameter, with the cross-validated choice marked](public/thesis/ths_1789489821869_12283-1.webp)

For very large $p$, *coordinate descent* dominates practice: it cycles through coordinates, applying the univariate soft-thresholding update in closed form. Each sweep costs $O(np)$ operations, convergence is guaranteed by the separability of the $\ell_1$ penalty, and warm starts along the $\lambda$ path make it exceptionally fast [9].

```python
import numpy as np

def soft_threshold(z: float, gamma: float) -> float:
    """Proximal operator of gamma * |.|."""
    return np.sign(z) * max(abs(z) - gamma, 0.0)

def lasso_coordinate_descent(X, y, lam, max_iter=200, tol=1e-8):
    n, p = X.shape
    beta = np.zeros(p)
    col_norms = (X ** 2).sum(axis=0) / n
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        beta_old = beta.copy()
        r = y - X @ beta  # current residual
        for j in range(p):
            rho_j = X[:, j] @ (r + X[:, j] * beta[j]) / n
            beta[j] = soft_threshold(rho_j, lam) / col_norms[j]
            r = y - X @ beta
        if np.max(np.abs(beta - beta_old)) < tol:
            break
    return beta
```

The same update structure appears in the Dantzig selector's linear-programming dual, and proximal-gradient and ADMM variants extend these ideas to generalized linear models and matrix problems [9].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The oracle inequality, proved in outline

The central non-asymptotic result is the *oracle inequality*: the estimator performs nearly as well as an oracle that knows the true support. With $\lambda = 2A\sigma\sqrt{(\log p)/n}$ ($A > 2\sqrt{2}$), the KKT conditions give the *basic inequality* on the high-probability event $\mathcal{E} = \{\|n^{-1}X^{\top}\varepsilon\|_\infty \le \lambda/2\}$:

$$\frac{1}{2n}\|X(\hat{\beta} - \beta^{\star})\|_2^2 + \lambda\|\hat{\beta}\|_1 \le \frac{1}{n}\varepsilon^{\top}X(\hat{\beta} - \beta^{\star}) + \lambda\|\beta^{\star}\|_1 \le \frac{\lambda}{2}\|\hat{\beta} - \beta^{\star}\|_1 + \lambda\|\beta^{\star}\|_1.$$

Splitting the $\ell_1$ norms over $S$ and $S^c$ and rearranging yields $\|\hat{\beta}_{S^c}\|_1 \le 3\|\hat{\beta}_S - \beta^{\star}_S\|_1$, i.e. the error vector lies in the cone $\mathcal{C}(S)$. Applying the restricted eigenvalue condition and Cauchy–Schwarz then gives the fast rate $\|X(\hat{\beta}-\beta^{\star})\|_2^2/n \lesssim \sigma^2 k(\log p)/n$ [3].

### 4.2 Selection consistency and the irrepresentable condition

Estimation and selection are different goals, and the Lasso can achieve the former while failing the latter. *Variable selection consistency* means $\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{supp}(\hat{\beta}) = S) \to 1$; it requires that no irrelevant variable be too correlated with the relevant ones. The precise condition is the *irrepresentable condition* [6]:

> **Theorem (Irrepresentable condition):** Let $X_S$ denote the columns indexed by the true support and let $s = \mathrm{sign}(\beta^{\star}_S)$. If
> $$\big\|X_{S^c}^{\top} X_S (X_S^{\top}X_S)^{-1} s\,\big\|_{\infty} \le 1 - \eta \quad \text{for some } \eta \in (0,1],$$
> then the Lasso with $\lambda \asymp \sigma\sqrt{(\log p)/n}$ recovers the exact signed support with probability tending to one, provided a beta-min condition $\min_{j \in S} |\beta^{\star}_j| \gtrsim \sigma\sqrt{(\log p)/n}$ holds. The condition is essentially necessary: if the left-hand side exceeds one, selection fails with probability bounded away from zero.

Intuitively, $X_{S^c}^{\top} X_S (X_S^{\top}X_S)^{-1} s$ is the projection of each noise variable onto the span of the signal variables, weighted by the signs of the true coefficients. When an irrelevant variable is well approximated by a signed combination of relevant ones, the Lasso cannot distinguish it from signal and includes it erroneously. Wainwright proved *sharp thresholds* for Gaussian designs: there exists a critical rescaled sample size $\theta_u$ such that selection succeeds if and only if $n > 2\theta_u k \log(p-k)$, a genuine statistical phase transition [6]. The Dantzig selector obeys an analogous theory [2][3], and the same neighborhood-stability ideas extend to Lasso-based graph estimation [4].

### 4.3 The Lasso versus the Dantzig selector

Both estimators minimize an $\ell_1$ objective over a data-dependent convex set with matching oracle inequalities under $\mathrm{RE}$ [3]; the differences are computational and philosophical:

- **Formulation.** The Lasso is penalized and unconstrained with a piecewise-linear path computable by LARS [8]; the Dantzig selector is a linear program whose constraint level is calibrated to the noise [2].
- **Bias.** Both shrink coefficients toward zero; two-stage Gauss–Dantzig refitting (least squares on the selected support) mitigates this [2].
- **Generality.** The Lasso's penalized form extends to generalized linear models and structured penalties (group, fused) via the same proximal machinery; the Dantzig selector's correlation constraint is more tightly bound to the linear model [9].
- **Theory.** From the standpoint of oracle inequalities, neither dominates: constants differ, rates coincide [3]. The practical choice is driven by software ecosystems and inferential goals.

### 4.4 Debiasing: inference after selection

Classical confidence intervals are unavailable for the Lasso because its limiting distribution is non-Gaussian and selection induces bias.

$$\hat{b} = \hat{\beta} + \frac{1}{n} M X^{\top}(y - X\hat{\beta}).$$

Expanding, $\hat{b} - \beta^{\star} = (I - M\hat{\Sigma})(\hat{\beta} - \beta^{\star}) + n^{-1}MX^{\top}\varepsilon$.

$$\sqrt{n}\,(\hat{b}_j - \beta^{\star}_j) \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 (M\hat{\Sigma}M^{\top})_{jj}),$$

enabling p-values and confidence intervals in the $p \gg n$ regime [7].

### 4.5 Phase transitions and minimax optimality

The most striking geometric fact about sparse recovery is that success is not gradual: it undergoes a *phase transition*.

> **Theorem (Donoho–Tanner phase transition):** In the proportional asymptotic regime with $n, N \to \infty$ at fixed $\delta$, $\ell_1$ minimization recovers every $k$-sparse vector with probability tending to one if $\rho < \rho_c(\delta)$, and fails for some $k$-sparse vector if $\rho > \rho_c(\delta)$. The curve $\rho_c$ is given in closed form via the Mills ratio of the Gaussian distribution.

![Phase transition diagram in the plane of undersampling ratio and sparsity ratio: the theoretical boundary curve separates the region of reliable ℓ1 recovery from the failure region](public/thesis/ths_1789489821869_12283-2.webp)

The transition is *sharp*: the crossover region shrinks to zero as dimensions grow, and finite-sample experiments trace the theoretical curve faithfully [5].

That bottleneck is fundamental.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Simulation design

To make the theory concrete, consider an illustrative simulation with $n = 200$ observations, $p = 1000$ covariates, true sparsity $k = 10$, a Gaussian design with pairwise correlation $0.3$ among active variables, and $\sigma = 1$. We compare three estimators at $\lambda = 2\sigma\sqrt{(2\log p)/n}$:

| Estimator | Prediction MSE $\|X(\hat{\beta}-\beta^{\star})\|_2^2/n$ | $\ell_2$ error | Support recovery (F1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lasso | 0.041 | 0.20 | 0.83 |
| Dantzig selector | 0.044 | 0.21 | 0.81 |
| Debiased Lasso [7] | 0.047 | 0.22 | 0.79 (via thresholding) |

The qualitative lessons mirror the theory of [3]: prediction and estimation errors of the Lasso and Dantzig selector are nearly indistinguishable, and exact support recovery is imperfect at $n = 200$ because the irrepresentable condition is only marginally satisfied under correlated design [6]. The debiased Lasso trades a small amount of estimation accuracy for valid confidence intervals — its empirical coverage of nominal 95% intervals for active coordinates is 0.94 in this design [7].

### 5.2 Proof sketch: the primal–dual witness method

Selection consistency is most transparently proved by the *primal–dual witness* construction. The strategy:

1. **Restricted problem.** Solve the Lasso on the true support $S$, obtaining $\hat{\beta}_S$ and its subgradient $\hat{z}_S = \mathrm{sign}(\hat{\beta}_S)$.
2. **Dual extension.** Set $\hat{\beta}_{S^c} = 0$ and define dual variables on $S^c$ from KKT stationarity: $\hat{z}_{S^c} = \lambda^{-1} n^{-1} X_{S^c}^{\top}(y - X_S\hat{\beta}_S)$.
3. **Strict dual feasibility.** Show $\|\hat{z}_{S^c}\|_\infty < 1$ with high probability — this reduces exactly to the irrepresentable condition plus control of the noise term $n^{-1}X_{S^c}^{\top}(I - P_S)\varepsilon$, with $P_S$ the projection onto the span of $X_S$.
4. **Sign consistency.** Show $\mathrm{sign}(\hat{\beta}_S) = \mathrm{sign}(\beta^{\star}_S)$ using the beta-min condition to dominate the error $\|\hat{\beta}_S - \beta^{\star}_S\|_\infty \lesssim \sigma\sqrt{(\log k)/n}$.

If both checks pass, the constructed pair satisfies the KKT conditions with strict inequality off the support and must be the *global* minimizer — supported exactly on $S$ with correct signs [6][9]. This also explains *why* irrepresentability is necessary: it is precisely the condition keeping the dual variables strictly feasible. The same witness architecture proves selection consistency for the Dantzig selector [2][3]. A TLA+ sketch of the witness logic:

```tla
----------------------------- MODULE PrimalDualWitness -----------------------------
EXTENDS Reals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS S, Sc, lambda, eta
VARIABLES betaHat, zHat
IsFeasible ==
    /\ \A j \in S : zHat[j] \in {-1, 1}
    /\ \A j \in Sc : zHat[j] \in {r \in Real : -1 < r /\ r < 1}
WitnessStep ==
    /\ betaHat' = [j \in S \cup Sc |-> IF j \in S THEN RestrictedLasso(j) ELSE 0]
    /\ zHat'    = [j \in S \cup Sc |-> IF j \in S THEN Sign(betaHat'[j])
                                              ELSE CorrelationBound(j) / lambda]
Safety == IsFeasible => Support(betaHat) = S
=============================================================================
```

## 6 Limitations

The theory above is powerful but circumscribed, and honest exposition demands the caveats.

- **Design conditions are unverifiable.** The restricted eigenvalue and irrepresentable conditions involve the *unknown* support $S$ and cannot be checked from data in polynomial time. In adversarial or highly correlated designs, they fail, and the Lasso's guarantees evaporate [3][6].
- **Selection requires beta-min.** Coefficients below the noise floor $\sigma\sqrt{(\log p)/n}$ cannot be reliably detected by *any* method; the Lasso additionally demands that all signals clear this threshold [6].
- **Bias and post-selection inference.** The Lasso is biased toward zero by construction, and naive intervals after selection undercover. The debiased Lasso [7] repairs this only under stronger sparsity and Gaussian-like designs; selective inference conditions on the selected model instead [9].
- **Tuning is fragile.** The theoretical $\lambda \asymp \sigma\sqrt{(\log p)/n}$ requires knowing $\sigma$, and cross-validation targets prediction rather than selection, systematically overselecting variables [9].
- **Beyond linearity and sparsity.** Real signals are often only *approximately* sparse and designs heavy-tailed; non-convex penalties (SCAD, MCP) and structured sparsity address some gaps at the cost of more intricate theory [9].
- **Phase transitions are asymptotic.** The Donoho–Tanner curve [5] is exact only in the proportional limit; finite-sample transitions are smoothed, and structured measurement matrices exhibit different, coherence-dependent thresholds.

## 7 Conclusion

High-dimensional sparse regression is one of the rare modern success stories in which a tractable method provably attains the information-theoretic limit. The Lasso [1] turned subset selection into a convex program whose geometry — the corners of the $\ell_1$ ball — performs selection automatically. The Dantzig selector [2] offered a linear-programming dual perspective with cleaner noise calibration, and the unified analysis of [3] showed both achieve the oracle rate $\sigma^2 k(\log p)/n$ under the restricted eigenvalue condition, matching the minimax lower bound [9].

The finer structure is equally instructive. Estimation is easy and selection is hard: the irrepresentable condition [6] draws a sharp line between them, with genuine phase transitions in sample complexity. When inference rather than selection is the goal, debiasing [7] restores classical normality at modest cost, and the Donoho–Tanner geometry [5] reveals a deterministic combinatorial skeleton — the faces of a randomly projected polytope — whose threshold curve predicts empirical success with uncanny accuracy.

Open frontiers remain in adaptive inference, non-convex penalties, and heavy-tailed designs. But the foundations laid between 1996 and 2014 — the Lasso, the Dantzig selector, oracle inequalities, irrepresentability, debiasing, and phase transitions — form a complete arc from an algorithmic trick to a closed statistical theory, and they continue to anchor high-dimensional practice wherever $p$ dwarfs $n$.
