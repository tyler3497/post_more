# The Jamming Transition: Force Networks, Marginal Stability, and the Statistical Physics of Amorphous Solids

## Abstract

The jamming transition is the zero-temperature, density-driven arrest of a disordered assembly of repulsive particles into a mechanically rigid amorphous solid. At the isostatic point J, where the mean coordination number satisfies the Maxwell criterion z = 2d, rigidity emerges from an unjammed fluid of non-interacting grains: the pressure, shear modulus, and excess contact number vanish as power laws of the distance to jamming, while the bulk modulus and the coordination number jump discontinuously, revealing a hybrid first-second-order character. This thesis develops a unified account of jamming as a critical phenomenon: we review the Liu-Nagel phase diagram [1] and point-J [2], derive the scaling laws governing elastic moduli and vibrational spectra from marginal-stability arguments [3], analyze force-chain networks and isostatic contact topologies [4], connect jamming to the Gardner transition in the infinite-dimensional hard-sphere solution [6][7], and extend the framework to shear jamming, fragile states, and discontinuous shear thickening in frictional grains and dense suspensions [8][11]. We synthesize simulation evidence, exact mean-field predictions, and the complete mathematical theory of jamming [5] into a coherent statistical physics of amorphous solids.

## 1 Intro

Few problems in condensed matter sit as squarely at the intersection of geometry, mechanics, and statistical physics as the jamming transition. Consider a box of soft, frictionless spheres — a crude but powerful model of emulsions, foams, and granular packings — and compress it slowly. At low packing fraction φ the particles rattle freely: the system is a fluid with zero shear modulus and zero pressure. At a sharply defined threshold φ_J, the grains can no longer avoid one another, a spanning contact network forms, and the assembly acquires rigidity. This *point J* was identified by O'Hern, Silbert, Liu, and Nagel as a special point at zero temperature and zero applied stress where packings are isostatic and where multiple observables display power-law scaling reminiscent of an ordinary critical point [2].

The significance of jamming extends far beyond soft spheres. Liu and Nagel proposed a unifying phase diagram whose axes are temperature, shear stress, and inverse density, suggesting that the glass transition, colloidal gelation, and granular arrest are facets of one underlying jamming surface [1]. Decades of work have vindicated the depth of this proposal while complicating it: the transition mixes discontinuous jumps (coordination number, bulk modulus, rattler fraction) with continuous vanishings (pressure, shear modulus, excess contacts), a hybrid character now understood as a transition with quenched disorder [12]. Meanwhile, the contact forces organize into heterogeneous *force chains* that percolate the sample and bear its load [4], the vibrational spectrum develops a plateau and a boson peak controlled by proximity to isostaticity [3], and the free-energy landscape fractures hierarchically at a Gardner transition that embeds jamming inside glass theory [6].

This thesis presents a self-contained, PhD-level treatment of the statistical physics of jamming. Section 2 reviews the background: the Liu-Nagel diagram, Maxwell constraint counting, and the phenomenology of point J. Section 3 describes the numerical methodology — energy minimization protocols, dynamical-matrix analysis, and scaling collapse — that produces the empirical facts. Section 4 dives deep into four topics: critical phenomenology at point J, force networks and marginal stability, the Gardner transition, and shear jamming with dense-suspension rheology.

## 2 Background

### The Liu-Nagel jamming diagram

The jamming phase diagram introduced by Liu and Nagel organizes disordered arrest along three control parameters: temperature T, applied shear stress Σ, and the inverse of the packing fraction 1/φ [1]. Inside the jammed region the system supports a yield stress and behaves as an amorphous solid; outside it flows. The diagram's power lies in its suggestion that molecular glasses (low T), colloidal suspensions (low stress), and granular packings (high density) jam for the same structural reason: the particles run out of room to rearrange. The boundary of the jammed region is the *jamming surface*, and its most studied point is the zero-temperature, zero-stress limit at random close packing — point J.

### Point J and isostaticity

O'Hern and collaborators studied ensembles of soft repulsive spheres quenched to their inherent structures by conjugate-gradient energy minimization. They found that each configuration jams at its own threshold φ_c, but the distribution of φ_c narrows with system size so that essentially all configurations jam at a single density φ_J in the thermodynamic limit — about 0.64 for monodisperse spheres in three dimensions and about 0.84 for bidisperse disks in two dimensions [2]. At φ_J the packings are *isostatic*: the mean coordination number equals the Maxwell count z_c = 2d, where d is the spatial dimension. Approaching from above, the pressure p, the shear modulus G, and the excess contacts Δz = z − z_c vanish as power laws, while the first peak of the pair correlation function diverges — signatures of criticality. Yet the bulk modulus B and the fraction of non-rattler particles jump discontinuously, and the coordination number itself jumps from zero below jamming to z_c above it [2][12].

### Maxwell constraint counting

The isostatic condition follows from a simple and exact counting argument. A packing of N frictionless spheres in d dimensions has Nd positional degrees of freedom, and each contact supplies one constraint (the normal force). Mechanical stability requires at least as many constraints as degrees of freedom, so the mean coordination number must satisfy z ≥ 2d. Equality defines the *isostatic point*, where the system is marginally rigid: removing a single contact creates a floppy mode, adding one over-constrains the structure [3].

### Force chains and contact networks

Load in a granular packing is not carried uniformly. Photoelastic experiments and simulations reveal that interparticle forces concentrate along quasi-linear *force chains* that percolate through the contact network, with a broad distribution of contact forces P(f) showing a plateau or small peak at weak forces [4]. The geometry of this network — which contacts exist, how forces balance at each grain, and how the fabric responds to shear — determines the macroscopic rheology. Understanding jamming therefore requires understanding the statistics of the contact network itself, a theme that recurs in the modern theory [5].

## 3 Methodology

The empirical foundation of jamming physics rests on athermal simulation protocols that have become standard across the field.

- **Configuration generation.** Random initial configurations of N soft spheres (harmonic or Hertzian repulsion, often 50:50 bidisperse to suppress crystallization) are placed at high packing fraction and relaxed by conjugate-gradient or FIRE energy minimization until forces vanish to numerical precision. Decompression in small steps toward φ_J, re-minimizing at each step, traces the jammed branch; the threshold φ_c of each sample is located by bisection [2].
- **Elastic moduli.** The bulk and shear moduli are extracted from the curvature of the minimized energy under affine deformations, or equivalently from the stress response to small imposed strains. Pressure follows from the virial.
- **Vibrational analysis.** The dynamical matrix — the Hessian of the potential energy at the minimum — is diagonalized to obtain the vibrational density of states D(ω). Its low-frequency plateau and the boson peak are the spectral fingerprints of marginal stability [3].
- **Scaling collapse.** Finite-size scaling of the jamming fraction F_J(φ, N) collapses onto a universal function of the scaled variable (φ − φ_J) N^{1/2} in both two and three dimensions, for compression and for shear jamming alike [12].
- **Mean-field theory.** In the limit of infinite spatial dimension, hard-sphere glasses admit an exact replica-theory solution. The full replica-symmetry-breaking (fullRSB) solution predicts jamming critical exponents and force/gap distributions analytically [6], and the complete mathematical theory of jamming has since been extended to finite dimensions with quantitative agreement [5].

```python
import numpy as np

def density_of_states(eigenvalues, n_bins=200):
    """Build D(omega) from the eigenvalues of the dynamical matrix."""
    omega = np.sqrt(np.maximum(eigenvalues, 0.0))
    counts, edges = np.histogram(omega, bins=n_bins)
    centers = 0.5 * (edges[:-1] + edges[1:])
    width = edges[1] - edges[0]
    return centers, counts / width / len(omega)

def crossover_frequency(delta_phi):
    """omega* scales with the square root of excess contacts."""
    excess_contacts = np.sqrt(delta_phi)
    return excess_contacts

def fit_pressure_scaling(delta_phi, pressure):
    """Fit p ~ (delta_phi)^alpha on the jammed branch."""
    coeffs = np.polyfit(np.log(delta_phi), np.log(pressure), 1)
    return coeffs
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Point J: Critical Phenomenology and Scaling

The scaling theory of point J is built on the excess contact number Δz = z − z_c as the central order parameter. Numerical studies establish that Δz grows as the square root of the distance above jamming, Δz ∼ (Δφ)^{1/2}, where Δφ = φ − φ_J [2][3]. This single relation organizes the mechanics: because the shear modulus is proportional to the excess contacts while the bulk modulus jumps to a finite value, the ratio G/B ∼ Δz vanishes at jamming, and jammed solids become anomalously soft to shear relative to compression.

For a repulsive potential of the form V ∼ δ^α, where δ is the particle overlap, the pressure scales as p ∼ (Δφ)^{α−1} and the shear modulus as G ∼ (Δφ)^{α−3/2}. For harmonic repulsions (α = 2) this gives p ∼ Δφ and G ∼ (Δφ)^{1/2}; for Hertzian contacts (α = 5/2) one finds p ∼ (Δφ)^{3/2} and G ∼ Δφ. The potential-independence of Δz ∼ (Δφ)^{1/2}, however, signals a geometric universality that transcends the microscopic force law [2][5].

The hybrid character of the transition deserves emphasis. The vanishing of p, G, and Δz is continuous, but the coordination number jumps from zero to z_c, the bulk modulus jumps to a finite value, and the rattler fraction jumps as well [12]. Recent work interprets this as a first-order transition with quenched disorder: the discontinuity lives in quantities controlled by the contact topology, while the continuous sector is governed by the diverging correlation of force balance.

### 4.2 Force Networks, Isostaticity, and Marginal Stability

Force balance at each grain, Σ_j f_{ij} n_{ij} = 0 over contacts j of particle i, defines a high-dimensional constraint satisfaction problem on the contact graph. At isostaticity the number of force variables exactly matches the number of balance equations, so a *unique* set of contact forces is determined by the geometry alone — up to an overall scale set by the pressure [3]. This is why the force distribution P(f) at jamming is a property of the packing geometry rather than of the preparation history.

*Marginal stability* is the principle that jammed packings sit at the edge of mechanical stability: they are stable, but only barely, against the opening of contacts and the closing of gaps. Wyart and collaborators showed that this marginality constrains the distributions of weak forces and small gaps through stability inequalities: too many weak contacts would allow collective rearrangements, so the system self-organizes to distributions that saturate the bounds [3][5]. The result is power-law tails,

- P(f) ∼ f^{θ} for small contact forces,
- g(h) ∼ h^{−γ} for small interparticle gaps h,

with exponents locked by scaling relations. The infinite-dimensional solution gives θ_e ≈ 0.42311 for extended forces, θ_l ≈ 0.17462 for localized buckling forces, and γ ≈ 0.41269 for the gap distribution [6] — values confirmed in finite-dimensional simulations to remarkable precision. The pseudogap in P(f) is the mechanical analogue of the Coulomb gap in disordered electronic systems: stability against single-particle excitations depletes the density of weak constraints.

The vibrational spectrum reflects the same marginality. Above a crossover frequency ω* ∼ Δz the density of states is flat — a plateau of anomalous, extended but non-plane-wave modes — while below ω* the Debye ω^{d−1} law is recovered. The crossover length l* ∼ 1/Δz diverges at jamming and sets the scale over which force propagation becomes anomalous, unifying the boson peak of glasses with the mechanics of granular packings [3].

> Theorem: (Isostatic rigidity and uniqueness of forces) A packing of N frictionless spheres in d dimensions with periodic boundary conditions is mechanically rigid only if its mean contact number satisfies z ≥ 2d. At equality, z = 2d, the packing is isostatic: the contact forces are uniquely determined by the contact geometry and force balance alone, and the removal of any single contact introduces a zero-frequency floppy mode.

### 4.3 The Gardner Transition and Fractal Landscapes

Perhaps the deepest recent advance is the incorporation of jamming into equilibrium glass theory via the Gardner transition. In the infinite-dimensional hard-sphere solution, compressing a glass basin below a critical pressure triggers a *roughness transition*: each smooth free-energy basin fractures into a hierarchy of sub-basins within sub-basins, an ultrametric tree of states described by full replica symmetry breaking [6][7]. This marginal Gardner phase is characterized by:

1. **Fractal basins** — the free-energy landscape becomes rough on all scales, with barriers of every size.
2. **Marginal stability** — every sub-basin sits at the edge of instability, generating an abundance of soft modes.
3. **Isostaticity at jamming** — the Gardner phase terminates at the jamming line, where packings are isostatic and the critical exponents follow from the fullRSB solution.

The Gardner transition thus explains *why* jammed packings are marginally stable: marginality is inherited from the fractal landscape of the parent glass [6]. Remarkably, the jamming exponents are dimension-independent, so the infinite-dimensional predictions apply directly to two- and three-dimensional experiments [7]. The complete mathematical theory of jamming has since derived the scaling laws, the force and gap distributions, and the vibrational anomalies from first principles, in quantitative agreement with simulations [5].

### 4.4 Shear Jamming, Fragile States, and Dense-Suspension Rheology

Jamming is not restricted to isotropic compression. Bi, Zhang, Chakraborty, and Behringer demonstrated that a granular assembly below φ_J can be *shear-jammed*: applying sufficient shear strain builds an anisotropic contact network that rigidifies the system along the compressional direction, even though the packing fraction lies below the isotropic threshold [8]. The shear-jamming phase diagram in the (φ, γ) plane contains three regimes:

- **Unjammed** — no spanning rigid network; the system flows.
- **Fragile** — a percolating force network exists but is stable only against compatible loads; reversing the shear direction destroys rigidity. Fragile states have vanishing potential energy yet non-zero contact forces, and they collapse under perturbation [10].
- **Shear-jammed** — a robust rigid network stable against general perturbations, with isotropic-like percolation of strong forces.

The distinction between fragile and shear-jammed states lies in mechanical stability rather than in network topology alone: fragile networks are transient and relax to unjammed configurations under accurate energy minimization [10]. Frictional particles enrich the picture further, with a non-equilibrium first-order jamming transition and re-entrant flow at finite stress [9].

The same physics governs dense suspensions. Discontinuous shear thickening (DST) — the dramatic viscosity jump in cornstarch suspensions under sudden stress — is now understood as a dynamically driven excursion toward the shear-jamming manifold: at high stress, lubrication films rupture, frictional contacts proliferate, and the suspension transiently jams [11]. The DST state shares the anisotropic contact fabric and the stress-bearing force network of shear-jammed grains, unifying suspension rheology with the zero-temperature jamming paradigm [8][11].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The quantitative predictions of jamming theory have been tested against simulations and experiments with unusual precision. The table below summarizes the principal scaling laws for harmonic repulsions, comparing exponents observed in three-dimensional simulations with the infinite-dimensional fullRSB predictions.

Several results rise to the level of mathematical proof or exact prediction. **(i)** Maxwell counting gives the exact isostatic threshold z_c = 2d for frictionless spheres, and simulations find z → 6 in three dimensions and z → 4 in two dimensions at φ_J, after excluding rattlers [2][3]. **(ii)** The fullRSB solution yields exact rational combinations for the exponents θ_e, θ_l, γ, and κ, which match finite-dimensional numerics to within a few percent — a striking success of mean-field theory [6]. **(iii)** Finite-size scaling of the jamming probability collapses for system sizes spanning orders of magnitude with the single scaling variable (φ − φ_J) N^{1/2}, for both compression and shear jamming in two and three dimensions [12]. **(iv)** The force distribution P(f) measured in static granular packings, jammed foams, and supercooled liquids near the glass transition shows the same plateau at small forces, confirming the universality of force statistics across athermal and thermal amorphous systems [4].

## 6 Limitations

The theory as presented has well-defined boundaries. First, the cleanest results concern *frictionless, athermal* spheres; real grains have friction, which shifts the isostatic count (z_c = d + 1 for infinitely frictional particles), introduces a range of jamming densities depending on preparation, and enables the re-entrant phenomena absent in the frictionless case [9]. Second, thermal fluctuations and activity are excluded: the framework describes the T = 0 skeleton, and extending it to finite temperature or active matter requires confronting activated rearrangements the athermal theory omits. Third, while the infinite-dimensional solution is exact, its translation to finite dimensions relies on the empirically observed dimensional independence of jamming exponents — a remarkable fact that is verified numerically but not yet derived from first principles [7]. Finally, the complete mathematical theory [5] assumes idealized repulsive potentials; systems with attraction, adhesion, or long-range interactions jam by different routes that the present framework does not cover.

## 7 Conclusion

The jamming transition has matured from a phenomenological proposal into one of the most quantitatively successful theories of disordered matter. From the Liu-Nagel phase diagram [1] and the discovery of point J [2], through the scaling theory of isostatic elasticity [3] and the statistics of force networks [4], to the exact infinite-dimensional solution with its Gardner transition [6][7] and the complete mathematical theory [5], a coherent picture has emerged: amorphous solids are *marginally stable* assemblies whose rigidity, vibrations, and rheology are controlled by proximity to an isostatic critical point. The extension to shear jamming, fragile states, and discontinuous shear thickening [8][10][11] shows that the same principles govern driven granular flows and dense suspensions, while the hybrid first-second-order character of the transition [12] resolves the long-standing tension between its abrupt jumps and its critical scaling. Open frontiers — friction, finite temperature, experimental Gardner physics, and attractive jamming — define the next decade of the field.
