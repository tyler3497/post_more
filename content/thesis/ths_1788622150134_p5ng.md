Processing-in-memory (PIM) promises to dissolve the memory bandwidth wall by relocating computation into DRAM banks, where internal bandwidth exceeds pin-limited external bandwidth by an order of magnitude. This thesis presents a unified analytical and empirical treatment of the three commercial PIM architectures most relevant to data analytics: UPMEM's general-purpose DRAM Processing Units (DPUs), Samsung's HBM-PIM (Aquabolt-XL) near-bank AI engines, and SK hynix's GDDR6-AiM accelerator-in-memory. We detail the UPMEM DPU microarchitecture — 14-stage in-order RISC core, 64 MB MRAM, 64 KB WRAM, 24 KB IRAM, and 24 tasklets under an SPMD model with no inter-DPU communication — alongside its host-orchestrated transfer protocol. We contrast this with HBM-PIM's per-bank FP16 SIMD units (1.2 TFLOPS, drop-in JEDEC compatibility) and AiM's BF16 MAC datapaths with on-die activation functions (1 TFLOPS/chip). Using the roofline model, we formalize the transfer-amortization inequality governing offload profitability, analyze DRAM refresh interference, and show why irregular pointer-chasing workloads favor PIM's massive context counts. Synthesizing PrIM benchmark results and industrial measurements, we identify when near-data compute wins — and the programmability, communication, and arithmetic limits that still constrain it.

# Processing-in-Memory Architectures for Data Analytics: UPMEM DPU Programming, HBM-PIM Near-Bank Compute, and the Memory Bandwidth Wall

## 1. Introduction

For over three decades, the *memory bandwidth wall* has throttled the performance of data-intensive computing. While arithmetic throughput of processors has improved by orders of magnitude, the bandwidth and energy cost of moving data between off-chip DRAM and compute cores have improved far more slowly. For analytical database kernels — scans, filters, hash joins, aggregations — and for machine-learning inference, this imbalance is decisive: the workloads are overwhelmingly *memory-bound*, spending the majority of execution time and energy shuttling operands across a narrow, latency-dominated bus rather than transforming them [5][9].

Processing-in-memory (PIM) attacks this asymmetry by relocating computation into or adjacent to the memory arrays. Long confined to simulation (EXECUBE, IRAM, DIVA), PIM recently reached silicon in three landmark architectures. **UPMEM** shipped the first publicly available general-purpose PIM system: DDR4 DIMMs in which each DRAM bank is paired with a 32-bit RISC core (a *Data Processing Unit*, DPU) supporting up to 24 hardware threads called *tasklets* [1][2]. **Samsung's HBM-PIM** (Aquabolt-XL) placed a DRAM-optimized AI engine — a programmable computing unit (PCU) — inside *every bank* of an HBM2 stack, achieving 1.2 TFLOPS of FP16 throughput at bank-level parallelism while remaining a drop-in replacement for JEDEC HBM2 [4][6]. **SK hynix's GDDR6-AiM** (Accelerator-in-Memory) embedded 16 processing units per GDDR6 die, delivering 1 TFLOPS of BF16 MAC throughput plus on-die activation functions at ISSCC 2022 [7][8].

This thesis develops a unified, analytically grounded treatment of these architectures for *data analytics* — a workload class whose irregular access patterns (pointer chasing, hash probing) and low operational intensity make it the natural beneficiary of near-data compute. Our contributions are: (i) a detailed microarchitectural and programming-model account of the UPMEM DPU, including its tasklet scheduling discipline and host-DPU transfer protocol; (ii) a comparative analysis of Samsung's near-bank HBM-PIM and SK hynix's GDDR6-AiM, contrasting general-purpose bank-attached cores with fixed-function AI engines; (iii) a roofline-model-based formalization of *when* PIM offloading is profitable, including the DRAM refresh-versus-compute tradeoff and a characterization of irregular workloads; and (iv) a synthesis of empirical results from the PrIM benchmark suite and industrial measurements, with attention to the transfer-cost amortization condition that governs real-world speedups.

> **Theorem:** *For a kernel with operational intensity $I$ (operations per byte) offloaded to a PIM device with internal bandwidth $B_{\text{PIM}}$ and a host-to-device transfer phase over bandwidth $B_{\text{bus}}$, offloading is strictly profitable only when the sum of PIM compute time and round-trip transfer time is less than host compute time plus single-direction data movement.*

## 2. Background

The memory wall, articulated by Wulf and McKee in 1995 and revisited by Mutlu and others in the many-core era, observes that DRAM latency and bandwidth improve at roughly 7–10% per year while processor throughput doubles every two years [5]. A single DRAM access costs **100–1000×** the energy of a 32-bit add, and warehouse-scale profiling showed processors stalled on memory over 50% of cycles [5]. Data movement, not arithmetic, is the first-order cost of analytics.

### DRAM organization and the internal bandwidth opportunity

DRAM's *internal* bandwidth — aggregate row-buffer bandwidth across all banks — exceeds pin-limited external bandwidth by one to two orders of magnitude. A modern HBM2 stack delivers ~256 GB/s externally but over 1 TB/s of aggregate internal bank bandwidth; a fully populated UPMEM server exposes ~1.7 TB/s of aggregate MRAM-to-WRAM bandwidth across 2,556 DPUs [1]. PIM architectures exist precisely to *consume operands at internal bandwidth* rather than external bandwidth.

The literature distinguishes two families [5][9]:

- **PIM (true in-memory):** computation performed *using* the memory array itself — e.g., Ambit-style bulk bitwise operations via triple-row activation, or analog matrix-vector multiplication exploiting Ohm's and Kirchhoff's laws in RRAM/PCM crossbars.
- **PNM (processing-near-memory):** general-purpose or domain-specific cores placed *adjacent* to the memory, on the logic die of a 3D stack (HMC/HBM) or, as in UPMEM, interleaved with DRAM banks on the same die. All three architectures studied here are PNM designs, since they attach explicit processing units to banks rather than repurposing cell physics.

### The roofline model

The roofline model bounds attainable performance by peak compute or the product of operational intensity and bandwidth [5]. PIM *raises the slanted roof* (internal vs. external bandwidth) but *lowers the flat roof* (weaker bank-attached cores), so offloaded kernels migrate diagonally and can become compute-bound — the defining failure mode of naive offloading.

### Why data analytics is the canonical PIM workload

Analytical queries combine streaming scans with near-zero reuse, irregular pointer chasing in hash joins, and tiny effective intensity — defeating caches and leaving DRAM bandwidth as the throughput determinant. On the PrIM suite, UPMEM DPUs beat high-end GPUs on memory-bound kernels (vector add, select) while losing on compute-intensive ones [1].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is a *synthesis and formalization* study rather than a new measurement campaign. Our evidence base comprises: (a) the published PrIM benchmark results and microbenchmarks characterizing 2,556-DPU and 640-DPU UPMEM systems, including per-DPU MRAM-to-WRAM bandwidth measurements of 534–700 MB/s and aggregate figures of 333.75 GB/s–1.7 TB/s [1]; (b) IRISA transfer-cost analyses quantifying host-to-DPU copy overheads and programmer recommendations [2][3]; (c) the ISSCC 2021 FIMDRAM paper and Samsung's Hot Chips 33 disclosure for HBM-PIM (1.2 TFLOPS, 2.5× system gain on Xilinx Alveo, >60% energy reduction) [4][6]; (d) SK hynix's ISSCC 2022 and Hot Chips 2024 disclosures for GDDR6-AiM (1 TFLOPS/chip BF16, 512 GFLOPS/die sustained, 512 GB/s internal bandwidth) [7][8]; and (e) recent optimization studies of the UPMEM v1B DPU microarchitecture [10].

We apply the roofline model with *device-specific* bandwidths, derive a transfer-amortization inequality for offload profitability, and model DRAM refresh interference as a duty-cycle loss. Comparisons are normalized by operational intensity and energy per operation. All "profitability" claims refer to end-to-end wall-clock time including transfers — the metric the UPMEM programming guide identifies as decisive [1][2].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The UPMEM DPU: microarchitecture and tasklet programming

A UPMEM PIM module is a standard DDR4-2400 DIMM containing two ranks of eight PIM chips; each chip integrates eight DPUs, yielding 64 DPUs per rank and **128 DPUs per DIMM**. Each DPU is bonded to a dedicated 64 MB DRAM bank called *Main RAM* (MRAM), a 64 KB SRAM scratchpad called *Working RAM* (WRAM), and a 24 KB instruction store called *Instruction RAM* (IRAM) [1][2]. The DPU itself is an in-order, 14-stage pipelined 32-bit RISC core running at approximately 400 MHz (UPMEM v1B). Its ISA natively supports 32-bit integer addition/subtraction and 8-bit integer multiplication; 32-bit multiplication, division, and *all* floating-point operations are emulated in software — a fact with severe consequences for analytics kernels that assume cheap FP arithmetic [1][10].

The DPU implements fine-grained multithreading with 24 hardware threads, which UPMEM calls **tasklets**. Tasklets execute a single program under the SPMD model, operate on disjoint data partitions, share WRAM and IRAM, and synchronize via mutexes, barriers, handshakes, and semaphores [1]. A scheduling rule requires consecutive instructions from the *same* tasklet to be separated by a minimum issue distance, so programmers run 11+ tasklets to keep the 14-stage pipeline full and hide MRAM latency [1][2][10]. Tasklets in *different* DPUs share nothing — no shared memory, no direct communication channel. All inter-DPU exchange is mediated by the host CPU through explicit copies, which is the architecture's most consequential limitation for joins and shuffles [1].

Host-side orchestration follows a rigid five-step protocol: allocate a DPU set, load the kernel binary into IRAM, copy input from host DRAM to MRAM, launch execution on the ranks, and copy results back [2][10]. Because DPUs and host DRAM live in disjoint address spaces, every byte of input pays the narrow DDR bus twice (in and out). A canonical DPU kernel therefore stages data through WRAM explicitly:

```c
/* DPU-side: parallel vector addition, NR_TASKLETS = 24 */
#include <defs.h>
#include <mram.h>
#include <alloc.h>
#include <perfcounter.h>

__mram_noinit uint32_t A[N];   /* input in MRAM  */
__mram_noinit uint32_t B[N];   /* input in MRAM  */
__mram_noinit uint32_t C[N];   /* output in MRAM */

int main(void) {
    unsigned int tid = me();              /* tasklet id: 0..23 */
    unsigned int chunk = N / NR_TASKLETS;
    unsigned int start = tid * chunk;
    /* stage a WRAM-resident tile through the scratchpad */
    __dma_aligned uint32_t bufA[256], bufB[256], bufC[256];
    for (unsigned int i = start; i < start + chunk; i += 256) {
        mram_read(&A[i], bufA, 256 * sizeof(uint32_t));  /* MRAM -> WRAM */
        mram_read(&B[i], bufB, 256 * sizeof(uint32_t));
        for (unsigned int j = 0; j < 256; j++)
            bufC[j] = bufA[j] + bufB[j];                /* compute in WRAM */
        mram_write(bufC, &C[i], 256 * sizeof(uint32_t)); /* WRAM -> MRAM */
    }
    return 0;
}
```

Measured per-DPU MRAM-to-WRAM bandwidth is only ~534–700 MB/s — modest per core, but 2,556 DPUs aggregate to ~1.7 TB/s, exceeding the host's external bandwidth by more than an order of magnitude [1].

> **Theorem:** *Aggregate internal bandwidth scales linearly with DPU count, while the host-to-PIM transfer bandwidth is fixed by the DDR interface. Therefore, for fixed problem sizes, there exists a critical DPU count beyond which execution time is dominated entirely by transfers, and further scaling is futile.*

### 4.2 Samsung HBM-PIM: near-bank AI engines in Aquabolt-XL

Samsung's approach, presented at ISSCC 2021 under the internal codename FIMDRAM and productized as **HBM-PIM (Aquabolt-XL)**, embeds a *Programmable Computing Unit* (PCU) inside every DRAM bank of an HBM2 stack [4][6]. Each PCU is a 16-wide SIMD engine executing FP16 arithmetic at 300 MHz; with 32 PCUs per die the stack delivers **1.2 TFLOPS** of programmable in-memory throughput while exploiting bank-level parallelism to provide roughly 4× the processing bandwidth of an off-chip solution [4].

Three decisions distinguish HBM-PIM. *Precision and domain*: the PCU is an FP16 SIMD datapath for AI inference (speech, NLP), natively serving low-intensity GEMV kernels. *Command compatibility*: in-memory ops ride conventional DRAM commands, so the stack runs in standard or FIM mode with **no controller or application changes** — unmodified TensorFlow graphs ran on Aquabolt-XL [6]. *Integration*: operands never leave the die; Samsung reported >2× performance and >70% energy reduction, and a 2.5× system gain with >60% energy cut on a Xilinx Alveo accelerator at Hot Chips 33 [4][6].

The cost is capacity: each PCU-equipped die carries only 4 Gb versus 8 Gb for a standard HBM2 die, so Samsung builds 6 GB stacks from four 4 Gb PIM dies plus four 8 Gb conventional dies [4]. This is the *silicon-area tax* of near-bank compute — logic and sense-amplifier-adjacent datapaths consume die area that would otherwise hold cells — and it recurs in every bank-level PIM design. ### 4.3 SK hynix GDDR6-AiM: accelerator-in-memory for deep learning

SK hynix's **GDDR6-AiM**, disclosed at ISSCC 2022, takes the fixed-function route further: 16 *processing units* (PUs) per GDDR6 die, each paired with bank groups, running at 1 GHz to deliver **1 TFLOPS of BF16 MAC throughput per chip** at 16 Gb/s/pin, plus on-die activation support (sigmoid, tanh, GELU, ReLU, Leaky ReLU) implemented with lookup tables and linear interpolation [7][8]. Where UPMEM offers programmability and HBM-PIM offers a programmable SIMD engine, AiM offers a *domain accelerator*: the datapath is hardwired for multiply-accumulate and nonlinearities, trading generality for energy efficiency — SK hynix claims up to 16× speedup on targeted computations and ~80% power reduction versus moving data to a discrete accelerator [7].

The Hot Chips 2024 disclosure grounds the design in sustained numbers: 512 GFLOPS/die with 512 GB/s internal versus 32 GB/s external bandwidth — a 16× ratio [8].

| Architecture | Substrate | Compute element | Precision | Peak throughput | Internal bandwidth | Capacity cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| UPMEM DPU [1][2] | DDR4 DIMM | 32-bit RISC core per bank (24 tasklets) | INT32 (FP emulated) | ~2.5–3 TOPS int (2,556 DPUs) | ~1.7 TB/s aggregate MRAM-to-WRAM | None (separate DIMMs) |
| Samsung HBM-PIM [4][6] | HBM2 stack | 16-wide SIMD PCU per bank @300 MHz | FP16 | 1.2 TFLOPS/stack | ~4× off-chip equiv. | 4 Gb vs 8 Gb per PIM die |
| SK hynix GDDR6-AiM [7][8] | GDDR6 chip | 16 PUs per die @1 GHz + activation LUT | BF16 | 1 TFLOPS/chip | 512 GB/s per die | Area of PU + LUT per die |

The table exposes the fundamental spectrum: *generality decreases and arithmetic density increases* moving from UPMEM to HBM-PIM to AiM. For data analytics — dominated by integer predicates, hashing, and pointer chasing rather than dense GEMM — UPMEM's general-purpose cores are the natural fit, while the two vendor PIMs target the low-intensity linear algebra of neural inference.

### 4.4 Formalizing the bandwidth wall: roofline, refresh, and irregularity

**Roofline placement.** Consider a database *select* kernel with operational intensity around 0.25 operations per byte (one comparison per 4-byte element). On a host with 100 GB/s of external bandwidth, the roofline bound is roughly 25 GFLOP/s — deeply memory-bound. On a 2,556-DPU UPMEM system with 1.7 TB/s of aggregate internal bandwidth, the bound rises to 425 GFLOP/s, but the DPU flat roof (integer throughput at 400 MHz across 2,556 in-order cores) may bind first for kernels with heavier per-element work. The PrIM results confirm this migration empirically: UPMEM wins where the kernel stays on the slanted roof and loses where it hits the flat one [1].

**The transfer-amortization condition.** Because UPMEM input crosses the DDR bus twice, offloading is profitable only when round-trip transfer plus PIM compute time beats single-direction movement plus host compute time — for small problem sizes the extra transfer term dominates and PIM always loses [1][2]. HBM-PIM and GDDR6-AiM sidestep this term entirely by computing *in place*, which is why their drop-in compatibility is architecturally significant [4][7].

**DRAM refresh versus compute.** DRAM banks under PIM compute still require periodic refresh, which contends with host accesses and in-memory operations for bank availability, degrading effective internal bandwidth and injecting jitter that breaks SPMD barrier assumptions. Near-bank engines that interleave compute with the DRAM command stream (as HBM-PIM's PCU does) can schedule around refresh; general-purpose DPUs treating MRAM as flat memory cannot [4][10].

**Irregular workloads and pointer chasing.** Analytics is not all streaming scans. Hash joins, graph traversals, and index probes chase pointers with no spatial locality, defeating host prefetchers and wasting most of each fetched cache line. PIM changes the economics: a DPU can chase a pointer chain *locally* at MRAM latency without ever occupying the external bus, converting wasted external bandwidth into useful internal latency tolerance — tasklets exist precisely to hide that latency [1]. This is the strongest analytical case for UPMEM in databases: for pointer-chasing kernels, the relevant metric is not bandwidth at all but *concurrent outstanding misses*, and 2,556 DPUs × 24 tasklets = 61,344 concurrent contexts is a miss-parallelism figure no host CPU can approach.

> **Theorem:** *For a pointer-chasing kernel with miss latency L and C independent contexts, throughput is bounded by C/L regardless of external bandwidth. A PIM system with far more concurrent contexts than the host therefore outperforms the host on such kernels even when internal and external bandwidths are comparable, because the bottleneck is latency parallelism, not bytes per second.*

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**PrIM on UPMEM.** Gómez-Luna et al.'s study of 16 diverse workloads on up to 2,556 DPUs remains the empirical anchor [1]. Memory-bound kernels — vector addition, select, unique, image histogram — outperform a high-end CPU and, on several, a high-end GPU; the GPU's enormous external bandwidth is squandered on low-intensity kernels while the DPUs' aggregate 1.7 TB/s internal bandwidth is fully usable. Compute-bound kernels (e.g., dense GEMM in FP32) collapse on UPMEM: software-emulated floating point on a 400 MHz in-order core cannot compete with a GPU's tensor units, confirming the roofline-flat-roof prediction of §4.4. Energy measurements show the DPUs are competitive on memory-bound kernels precisely because they eliminate the most expensive operation in the system — off-chip data movement, at roughly 100 pJ/bit versus ~1 pJ/bit for on-chip movement [1][5].

**Transfer costs.** IRISA's analyses quantify the amortization condition: host-to-DPU copies traverse the same DDR4 bus as regular traffic, so the transfer fraction dominates effective speedup for short kernels, vindicating the maximize-granularity rule [2][3]. Inter-DPU communication through the host has latency orders of magnitude above intra-DPU WRAM sharing — shuffle-heavy analytics must be restructured into independent partitions or they lose [1].

**HBM-PIM measurements.** Samsung's ISSCC 2021 silicon (20 nm, 6 GB stack, 2.4 Gb/s/pin) demonstrated the PCU sustaining its 1.2 TFLOPS on FP16 workloads at bank-level parallelism; the Hot Chips 33 system integration with Xilinx Alveo delivered the headline **2.5× system performance with >60% energy reduction** on AI inference, and Aquabolt-versus-Aquabolt-XL comparisons showed >2× performance with >70% energy savings [4][6]. These are system-level, end-to-end figures — the transfer-amortization term is zero because computation happens in place.

**GDDR6-AiM measurements.** SK hynix reported 16× acceleration on targeted AI computations versus a CPU+DRAM baseline and ~80% power reduction, with the Hot Chips 2024 update grounding the architecture in sustained numbers: 512 GFLOPS/die, 512 GB/s internal versus 32 GB/s external bandwidth [7][8]. The 16× internal/external ratio is the empirical counterpart of our roofline argument: the device monetizes exactly the bandwidth gap that the memory wall describes.

## 6. Limitations

**Programmability and the two-program burden.** UPMEM forces a split compilation model — host orchestrator plus DPU kernel — with manual tiling across MRAM/WRAM, manual tasklet-count tuning, and no transparent coherence between the disjoint address spaces [1][2]. This is a regression to the early-GPGPU era's explicit memory management, and it raises the bar for database engine integration: a query optimizer would need cost models for the transfer-amortization inequality, a capability no current optimizer possesses.

**No inter-DPU communication.** The absence of any DPU-to-DPU channel makes shuffle, the heart of distributed analytics, a host-mediated operation. Workloads that cannot be partitioned into independent blocks — sort-merge joins, large aggregations — are structurally unsuitable [1].

**Weak and narrow arithmetic.** Software-emulated floating point and division on the DPU impose 10–100× slowdowns relative to native integer ops [10]; the 24 KB IRAM caps kernel complexity; and the in-order pipeline punishes branchy code. HBM-PIM and AiM, conversely, are *too* narrow: FP16/BF16 SIMD and fixed MAC datapaths cannot express the integer hashing and string predicates that dominate analytics.

**Capacity and area taxes.** HBM-PIM sacrifices half the capacity of each PIM die; every bank-level engine pays silicon area that could have been storage [4]. Refresh contention, thermal density of compute-adjacent-to-cells, and the lack of mature virtual-memory and protection support for PIM contexts further constrain deployment.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Processing-in-memory has crossed from simulation to silicon, and the three architectures examined here map the design space with unusual clarity. **UPMEM** proves that general-purpose, bank-attached cores programmed under SPMD with explicit scratchpad staging can defeat GPUs on memory-bound analytics — provided kernels are long, partitions independent, and arithmetic integer-friendly [1][2][10]. **Samsung's HBM-PIM** proves that near-bank SIMD engines, driven through standard DRAM commands, can deliver 2.5× system-level gains on AI inference with zero software changes, at the price of die capacity and domain specificity [4][6]. **SK hynix's GDDR6-AiM** proves that fixed-function in-memory accelerators can convert a 16× internal/external bandwidth ratio into order-of-magnitude efficiency wins for BF16 deep learning [7][8].

The unifying principle is the one formalized in §4.4: PIM is profitable exactly when the workload's operational intensity places it under the host's bandwidth roof while remaining under the device's compute roof, and when transfer costs — zero for in-place PIM, decisive for UPMEM — do not erase the gain. For data analytics, the most compelling frontier is not dense linear algebra but *irregular* workloads: pointer chasing, hash probing, and sparse traversal, where tens of thousands of tasklet contexts convert the memory wall from a bandwidth problem into a latency-parallelism opportunity. Outstanding challenges are systemic: cost-model-driven offloading in query optimizers, inter-PIM communication, and multi-tenant isolation. The wall has not fallen — but for the first time, we can measure exactly where it stands.
