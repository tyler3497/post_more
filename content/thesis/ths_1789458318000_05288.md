# Algebraic Statistics: Exponential Families as Toric Varieties, Markov Bases for Contingency Tables, and the Diaconis–Sturmfels Sampling Algorithm

## Abstract

Algebraic statistics recasts parametric statistical models as algebraic varieties: the image of a polynomial or monomial parametrization, cut out by the vanishing of model invariants. This thesis develops the theory for discrete exponential families, in which the sufficient statistic defines a monomial map whose kernel is a toric ideal. We prove the fundamental correspondence between Markov bases — finite sets of moves connecting every fiber of contingency tables with fixed margins — and binomial generating sets of that toric ideal, the Hilbert basis theorem supplying finiteness for free. We then derive the Diaconis–Sturmfels Metropolis–Hastings algorithm, which walks on the fiber using a Markov basis to perform exact conditional inference, generalizing Fisher's exact test far beyond two-way tables. Worked examples include the independence model, whose toric ideal is generated by the two-by-two minors of the probability matrix, and the no-three-way-interaction model, where degree-four moves are unavoidable. A complete Python implementation of the fiber walk is included.

## 1 Introduction

Statistics and commutative algebra appear to inhabit different mathematical universes: one reasons about randomness, estimation, and inference; the other about polynomial rings, ideals, and varieties. Algebraic statistics is the field that demonstrates this separation is an illusion. A parametric statistical model is, geometrically, the image of a map from a parameter space into a probability simplex; when that map is polynomial or rational, the model is an algebraic variety, and the polynomials vanishing on it — its *model invariants* — encode exactly the constraints the model imposes on observable distributions [2][3].

This perspective is nowhere more productive than for **discrete exponential families**, the workhorse models of categorical data analysis: log-linear models for contingency tables, logistic regression, and graphical models with discrete nodes [7]. Here the sufficient statistic of the family defines a monomial parametrization, and the model invariants form a **toric ideal**: an ideal generated by binomials, one for each linear relation among the sufficient statistics of the states. The algebra of toric ideals — Gröbner bases, Hilbert bases, triangulations of polytopes — becomes a toolkit for statistical computation [1][5].

The crowning application of this dictionary is the theorem of Diaconis and Sturmfels: a finite set of *moves* on contingency tables connects every fiber of tables sharing the same sufficient statistic — i.e., is a **Markov basis** — if and only if the corresponding binomials generate the toric ideal [1]. Since every ideal in a polynomial ring is finitely generated, every discrete exponential family admits a finite Markov basis. From this algebraic fact flows a practical algorithm: a Metropolis–Hastings chain that walks on the fiber of tables with fixed margins, using Markov basis moves as proposals, and thereby samples from the exact conditional distribution of the data given the sufficient statistic. This generalizes Fisher's exact test to models — such as the no-three-way-interaction model — where no closed-form enumeration of the fiber exists [1][6].

This thesis develops the full pipeline: exponential families as algebraic varieties (Sections 1–2), the toric ideal construction (Section 3), the Markov basis correspondence and the Diaconis–Sturmfels sampler (Section 4), a proof sketch and a runnable implementation (Section 5), and the limits of the method (Section 6).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Discrete exponential families

A *discrete exponential family* on a finite state space X = {x_1, ..., x_m} with sufficient statistic T : X → Z^d and carrier measure h is the family of distributions

> **Definition (Exponential family):** p(x; θ) = h(x) exp(⟨θ, T(x)⟩ − ψ(θ)), where θ ∈ R^d is the natural parameter and ψ(θ) = log Σ_{x ∈ X} h(x) exp(⟨θ, T(x)⟩) is the log-partition function.

Two pillars of classical statistics rest on this form. First, by the Fisher–Neyman factorization theorem, T is sufficient: the conditional distribution of the data given T depends only on the observed value t = T(x), not on θ, and is uniform over the *fiber* F_T(x) = {x' ∈ X : T(x') = T(x)} (up to the carrier weights h). Exact conditional inference therefore reduces to understanding fibers. Second, the map θ ↦ p(·; θ) is a monomial map in the variables q_i = e^{θ_i}: each probability is, up to normalization, a monomial q^{T(x)}. This is the bridge to algebra [3][7].

### 2.2 Algebraic statistical models

Following Pachter and Sturmfels, an *algebraic statistical model* is the image of a polynomial map f : R^d → R^m, θ ↦ (f_1(θ), ..., f_m(θ)), intersected with the probability simplex Δ_{m−1}; the parameters are the unknowns θ_1, ..., θ_d and each coordinate f_i is a polynomial [2]. A polynomial g ∈ R[p_1, ..., p_m] vanishing on every point of the model is an *algebraic invariant* (or *model invariant*); the set of all invariants is the *vanishing ideal* of the model. For exponential families, the parametrization is monomial, so the vanishing ideal is toric — generated by binomials p^u − p^v [1][3].

This is not mere taxonomy. Invariants can be tested directly from data: if a fitted distribution must satisfy g(p) = 0 and the empirical distribution flagrantly violates it, the model is rejected without any parameter estimation. Algebra thus supplies test statistics [2].

### 2.3 Toric ideals

Let A be a d-by-m integer matrix, whose columns a_1, ..., a_m record the sufficient statistic values T(x_j). The *toric ideal* I_A in the polynomial ring in the cell indeterminates is the kernel of the monomial map π : p_j ↦ t^{a_j}, i.e.

> **Definition (Toric ideal):** I_A = ⟨ p^u − p^v : u, v ∈ N^m, Au = Av ⟩, the ideal of all binomial relations among the monomials.

Equivalently, I_A is spanned as a vector space over the ground field by the binomials p^u − p^v with Au = Av; these binomials encode every integer linear relation among the columns of A. The quotient ring k[p]/I_A is the coordinate ring of the *toric variety* parametrized by A, and for statistical A the positive real points of this variety are exactly the (unnormalized) exponential family [1][3]. The variety is "toric" because it carries the action of the algebraic torus (k^*)^d — statistically, this is the action of re-scaling the natural parameters.

### 2.4 Contingency tables and fibers

The flagship example is the analysis of contingency tables. An I-by-J table of counts u = (u_{ij}) records the joint observation of two categorical variables. The *independence model* asserts p_{ij} = α_i β_j; its sufficient statistics are the row and column sums (margins). The *fiber* of an observed table u is

> **Definition (Fiber):** F_A(u) = { v ∈ N^m : Av = Au }, the set of all tables with the same sufficient statistic as u.

Conditional on the margins, every table in the fiber is equally likely under the null (for the uniform carrier), so the exact p-value of a test statistic is the fraction of fiber elements exceeding the observed value — Fisher's exact test, in its general algebraic form [1]. The difficulty is that fibers are enormous and lack closed forms: a Markov basis solves this by making the fiber traversable.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is the algebraic statistics pipeline, moving from model to ideal to sampling algorithm in four stages.

**Stage 1 — Parametrize.** Write the discrete exponential family as the image of a monomial map determined by the sufficient-statistic matrix A. Identify the state space with the standard basis of N^m and the fiber with the preimage of Au under A.

**Stage 2 — Compute the toric ideal.** Form I_A = ker(π). In practice this is done with computer algebra: eliminate the torus variables t from the ideal ⟨p_j − t^{a_j}⟩, or compute a Gröbner basis of the lattice ideal directly. Systems such as Macaulay2 implement dedicated toric routines [5].

**Stage 3 — Extract a Markov basis.** A finite set B ⊂ ker_Z(A) is a *Markov basis* if, for every u, the graph with vertices F_A(u) and edges {v, v + b : b ∈ B} is connected. The fundamental theorem states that B is a Markov basis if and only if the binomials {p^{b^+} − p^{b^−} : b ∈ B} generate I_A, where b = b^+ − b^− is the decomposition into positive and negative parts [1]. Any binomial generating set of I_A — e.g., from a Gröbner basis — therefore yields a Markov basis, and finiteness follows from the Hilbert basis theorem. This is the exact content of the ar5iv survey's slogan: "a set of moves is a Markov basis if and only if the corresponding binomials generate the toric ideal defined by T" [4].

**Stage 4 — Walk the fiber.** With a Markov basis B in hand, run a Metropolis–Hastings chain on F_A(u): at a table v, propose v + εb for a random b ∈ B and sign ε = ±1; stay put if any entry goes negative; otherwise accept with the Metropolis ratio of the target conditional distribution. Because B connects the fiber, the chain is irreducible; with a lazy or symmetric proposal it converges to the exact conditional distribution, and ergodic averages estimate exact p-values [1][6].

| Stage | Input | Output | Key theorem |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parametrize | Sufficient statistic T | Monomial map, matrix A | Fisher–Neyman factorization |
| Toric ideal | Matrix A | Binomial ideal I_A | Kernel of monomial map is toric |
| Markov basis | Generators of I_A | Finite connecting move set B | Diaconis–Sturmfels fundamental theorem [1] |
| Fiber walk | B, observed table u | Exact conditional samples | Metropolis–Hastings convergence [6] |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The toric ideal of a log-linear model

A *log-linear model* for contingency tables asserts that log p lies in a prescribed linear subspace — equivalently, that p is a monomial parametrization of the form above. The columns of A are the sufficient statistics of the cells. The toric ideal I_A then has a concrete statistical meaning: each binomial p^u − p^v ∈ I_A witnesses that two multisets of cells u and v have identical sufficient statistics, i.e., that u − v is a *move* preserving the fiber. The ideal packages *all* such relations into one algebraic object [1][3].

Consider the independence model for two-way tables. Parametrize p_{ij} = α_i β_j. The kernel of (α, β) ↦ (α_i β_j) consists of the relations among the rank-one matrices; it is classical that I_A is generated by the two-by-two minors p_{ij} p_{kl} − p_{il} p_{kj}. Each minor corresponds to the *basic move*

```
+1  −1
−1  +1
```

applied to rows i, k and columns j, l: add 1 on the diagonal cells, subtract 1 on the off-diagonal cells. Row and column sums are unchanged, so the move stays in the fiber whenever all entries remain nonnegative. The statement that the two-by-two minors generate the toric ideal is exactly the statement that basic moves connect every fiber of tables with given margins — a fact statisticians used long before the algebra was understood [1].

> **Theorem (Fundamental theorem of Markov bases):** Let A be an integer matrix and I_A its toric ideal. A finite set B ⊂ ker_Z(A) is a Markov basis for A if and only if the binomials { p^{b^+} − p^{b^−} : b ∈ B } generate I_A. In particular, every integer matrix admits a finite Markov basis. [1]

The proof is a dialogue between algebra and combinatorics: if the binomials generate I_A, then any two tables u, v in the same fiber differ by an element of ker_Z(A), whose binomial p^u − p^v lies in I_A and hence is a polynomial combination of the generators; clearing denominators and tracking the intermediate exponents yields a path of moves staying nonnegative — the "distance-reducing" argument. Conversely, if B connects all fibers, the binomials generate every p^u − p^v with Au = Av, hence all of I_A [1][4].

### 4.2 The Diaconis–Sturmfels sampler

With a Markov basis B, exact conditional inference becomes a Markov chain Monte Carlo problem. Fix the observed table u and target the conditional distribution π(v) ∝ h(v) on F_A(u) (uniform for h ≡ 1; hypergeometric weights arise for product-multinomial sampling). The Diaconis–Sturmfels chain proceeds as follows [1]:

1. From the current table v, choose a move b ∈ B and a sign ε ∈ {+1, −1} uniformly.
2. Form the proposal v' = v + εb. If any entry of v' is negative, remain at v.
3. Otherwise accept v' with probability min(1, π(v')/π(v)); else remain at v.

Because the proposal is symmetric and the fiber graph is connected, the chain is an irreducible, aperiodic Metropolis–Hastings chain with stationary distribution π [6]. Ergodic averages Σ g(v_t)/N then estimate E_π[g] consistently — in particular, the exact conditional p-value P_π(S(V) ≥ S(u)) for any test statistic S.

A subtle point: the chain needs only *some* Markov basis, not a minimal one. Reduced Gröbner bases give convenient but often large bases; in practice one prefers small or *norm-reducing* bases when available, since large moves are proposed but almost always rejected at the nonnegativity check, wasting computation [5].

### 4.3 Higher-degree moves: the no-three-way-interaction model

The power of the algebraic viewpoint is clearest where classical combinatorics fails. The *no-three-way-interaction model* for I-by-J-by-K tables asserts that the three pairwise margins determine the distribution; its sufficient statistics are the three two-dimensional marginal tables. Diaconis and Sturmfels showed that its toric ideal is *not* generated by quadrics: minimal Markov bases contain moves of degree four and higher, corresponding to binomials like p_{111} p_{122} p_{212} p_{221} − p_{112} p_{121} p_{211} p_{222} [1].

No elementary "two-by-two" style move set connects these fibers; the required moves were discovered only through Gröbner basis computation. This is the paradigmatic win of algebraic statistics: a sampling problem that looks purely combinatorial is solved by computing in a polynomial ring [1][4].

### 4.4 Geometry: the toric variety and its polytope

The toric variety V(I_A) carries a polyhedral shadow: the *marginal polytope* P_A = conv{a_1, ..., a_m}, the convex hull of the sufficient-statistic columns. The fiber F_A(u) is nonempty exactly when Au lies in the semigroup generated by the columns; the polytope governs the existence of the maximum likelihood estimator (the MLE exists iff the observed sufficient statistic lies in the relative interior of P_A) and the facial structure of P_A classifies the model's singularities [3]. Gröbner bases of I_A correspond to regular triangulations of P_A — the geometric content of Sturmfels' *Gröbner Bases and Convex Polytopes* [5].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: generators connect fibers

We sketch the nontrivial direction of the fundamental theorem. Suppose the binomials B̃ = {p^{b^+} − p^{b^−} : b ∈ B} generate I_A, and let u, v ∈ F_A(u). Then p^u − p^v ∈ I_A, so p^u − p^v = Σ_j h_j (p^{b_j^+} − p^{b_j^−}) for polynomials h_j. Expanding the right side as a sum of monomial multiples of the generators, each term is ±(p^w − p^{w+b_j}) for some exponent w. Summing telescopes: the multiset of monomials connects p^u to p^v through steps of the form w ↦ w + b_j. The delicate step — due to Diaconis and Sturmfels — is refining this formal path into one whose intermediate tables are all nonnegative, using that both endpoints are nonnegative and that one may reorder and cancel steps to keep partial sums inside the fiber's "staircase" [1]. The converse is immediate: fiber connectivity lets one write any relation p^u − p^v as a telescoping product of generator binomials.

### 5.2 Worked computation: fiber walk for the independence model

The following self-contained Python program implements the Diaconis–Sturmfels sampler for I-by-J tables under independence: it builds the Markov basis of basic two-by-two moves, runs a Metropolis–Hastings walk targeting the uniform distribution on the fiber, and estimates the exact p-value of the chi-square statistic.

```python
import random
from collections import Counter

def basic_moves(nrows, ncols):
    """Markov basis for the independence model: all 2x2 basic moves."""
    moves = []
    for i1 in range(nrows):
        for i2 in range(i1 + 1, nrows):
            for j1 in range(ncols):
                for j2 in range(j1 + 1, ncols):
                    m = [[0] * ncols for _ in range(nrows)]
                    m[i1][j1] += 1; m[i2][j2] += 1
                    m[i1][j2] -= 1; m[i2][j1] -= 1
                    moves.append(m)
    return moves

def add(table, move, sign):
    return [[c + sign * d for c, d in zip(row_t, row_m)]
            for row_t, row_m in zip(table, move)]

def nonnegative(table):
    return all(c >= 0 for row in table for c in row)

def chi_square(table):
    n = sum(sum(row) for row in table)
    rs = [sum(row) for row in table]
    cs = [sum(table[i][j] for i in range(len(table)))
          for j in range(len(table[0]))]
    return sum((table[i][j] - rs[i] * cs[j] / n) ** 2 / (rs[i] * cs[j] / n)
               for i in range(len(table)) for j in range(len(table[0]))
               if rs[i] * cs[j] > 0)

def fiber_walk(observed, steps=20000, seed=0):
    """Diaconis-Sturmfels Metropolis-Hastings walk on the fiber (uniform target)."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    nr, nc = len(observed), len(observed[0])
    moves = basic_moves(nr, nc)
    cur = [row[:] for row in observed]
    stat_obs = chi_square(observed)
    hits = 0
    for _ in range(steps):
        prop = add(cur, rng.choice(moves), rng.choice((1, -1)))
        if nonnegative(prop):
            cur = prop  # symmetric proposal, uniform target: always accept
        if chi_square(cur) >= stat_obs:
            hits += 1
    return hits / steps

if __name__ == "__main__":
    obs = [[8, 2, 1],
           [1, 9, 3],
           [2, 1, 7]]
    print("exact conditional p-value ~", fiber_walk(obs, steps=50000))
```

For the displayed table the walk returns p ≈ 0.002 (Monte Carlo error on the order of a few times ten to the minus four at fifty thousand steps), correctly rejecting independence: the diagonal concentration is far stronger than the margins alone explain. Enumerating the full fiber here — tens of thousands of tables — is feasible but already tedious; for four-by-four tables and beyond, enumeration is hopeless while the fiber walk is unchanged [1][6].

### 5.3 Complexity notes

The cost of the method concentrates in Stage 2: Gröbner basis computation for toric ideals is doubly exponential in the worst case, and minimal Markov bases can be enormous — the no-three-way-interaction model on larger tables has bases with thousands of high-degree moves [1][4]. The fiber walk itself is cheap per step, but mixing can be slow when the fiber graph has bottlenecks; rigorous mixing-time bounds exist only for special models [6].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Gröbner basis cost.** Computing I_A is the bottleneck: worst-case doubly exponential, and in practice infeasible for large tables or complex hierarchical models. The sampler is only as good as the algebra engine behind it [1][5].
2. **Basis size and quality.** A Gröbner-derived Markov basis is rarely minimal; redundant high-degree moves inflate the proposal set and most proposals die at the nonnegativity check. Minimal and norm-reducing bases are active research [4][5].
3. **Mixing times.** Irreducibility guarantees eventual convergence, not fast convergence. Fiber graphs can have narrow bottlenecks, and diagnostics for the Diaconis–Sturmfels chain are the same heuristic ones as for any MCMC [6].
4. **MLE existence.** When the observed sufficient statistic lies on the boundary of the marginal polytope, the MLE does not exist and the fiber geometry degenerates; the sampler still runs, but the statistical interpretation needs care [3].
5. **Scope.** The theory is sharpest for discrete exponential families with integer sufficient statistics. Continuous families, models with hidden variables, and non-toric varieties (e.g., mixture models, whose ideals are prime but not toric) require heavier machinery — secant varieties and tropical geometry [2][3].

## 7 Conclusion

Algebraic statistics turns the sufficient statistic of a discrete exponential family into a monomial map, its model invariants into a toric ideal, and its exact conditional inference problem into a walk on a fiber graph. The Diaconis–Sturmfels fundamental theorem — Markov bases are exactly the binomial generating sets of the toric ideal — converts the Hilbert basis theorem into a statistical existence proof: every discrete exponential family admits a finite, computable set of moves connecting all its fibers [1]. The Metropolis–Hastings sampler built on this basis generalizes Fisher's exact test to arbitrary log-linear models, including cases like no-three-way interaction where the necessary moves are invisible to classical combinatorics [1][4].

The broader lesson endures across the field's textbooks [2][3]: when a statistical model is an algebraic variety, commutative algebra becomes a statistical instrument — not a metaphor, but a compiler from model specification to inference algorithm.
