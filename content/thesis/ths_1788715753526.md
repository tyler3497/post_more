# Fusion Blanket Tritium Breeding with Liquid Lithium Loops: Neutronics, MHD Heat Transfer, and Closed Fuel Cycle Economics

## Abstract

Tritium self-sufficiency is the *sine qua non* of deuterium–tritium fusion power. World civilian inventories stand below 40 kg and CANDU production near 130 g per reactor-year [6], while a 1 GWth plant consumes roughly 56 kg per full-power year — so every gram burned in the plasma must be regenerated in lithium-bearing blankets with margin above unity. This thesis develops an integrated treatment of **liquid lithium loop blankets** as tritium breeding machines, coupling (i) three-dimensional Monte Carlo neutronics of 6Li/7Li breeding with lead neutron multipliers, (ii) magnetohydrodynamic thermohydraulics of conducting lithium flowing through 4–10 T fields, and (iii) closed-fuel-cycle mass balances mapping tritium breeding ratio (TBR) margins onto doubling time, startup inventory, and fuel cost. We show that the self-sufficiency window — TBR between the 1.05 DEMO target [5] and an achievable ceiling near 1.15 [1] — is narrowed by MHD pressure losses of several MPa [2], nuclear-data uncertainties, and processing delays. Anchored to CANDU tritium at $30,000/g [9], each 0.01 of TBR margin above requirement is worth hundreds of millions of dollars in avoided inventory across a fleet lifetime.

---

## 1. Introduction

The deuterium–tritium (D–T) reaction

> D + T → ⁴He (3.5 MeV) + n (14.1 MeV)

is the only fusion reaction with a Lawson product accessible to magnetic confinement at near-term technology levels. Yet tritium, with a half-life of only 12.32 years, does not occur naturally in usable quantities. The civilian world inventory is projected at 30–40 kg over the coming decade [5], heavy-water CANDU reactors generate under 130 g per year each [5], and a single 100 MWth fusion plant consumes on the order of **5.6 kg per full-power year** [5]. ITER alone is expected to acquire and consume one to two kilograms annually beginning in the late 2030s, totaling some 12 kg — one-third of the world's assured stockpile [9].

The arithmetic is unforgiving: fusion power can only scale if each reactor breeds more tritium than it burns. The breeding blanket — the lithium-bearing structure enveloping the plasma — must therefore satisfy three coupled missions simultaneously:

1. **Breed tritium** with a tritium breeding ratio (TBR, tritons produced per fusion neutron) exceeding unity by a defined safety margin, nominally TBR ≥ 1.05 for DEMO [5].
2. **Remove heat** deposited by 14 MeV neutrons and surface fluxes, converting it to electricity at acceptable efficiency.
3. **Close the fuel cycle**, extracting tritium from the breeder, purifying it, and returning it to the plasma faster than decay and permeation losses erode the inventory.

Liquid lithium loops — pure lithium or the eutectic Pb–Li alloy (15.6 at.% Li) circulated as breeder *and* coolant — occupy a singular position among blanket concepts. Liquid breeders resist irradiation damage, permit continuous online tritium extraction, and exhibit high thermal conductivity (≈101 W/m·K) with low kinematic viscosity (≈10⁻⁷ m²/s) that make them superb heat-transfer media [2]. Their Achilles' heel is magnetohydrodynamics: an electrically conducting fluid (σ ≈ 10⁶ S/m) flowing through a 4–10 T confining field induces currents whose **J × B** Lorentz forces oppose the flow, producing pressure drops of up to several MPa that can dominate pumping power and structural design [2].

This thesis unifies the neutronics, MHD thermohydraulics, and fuel-cycle economics of liquid lithium loop blankets into a single analytical and computational framework. Our contributions are:

- A parametric neutronics model quantifying how ⁶Li enrichment, neutron multiplication, and blanket coverage translate into TBR margins, including nuclear-data uncertainty propagation.
- An MHD heat-transfer analysis deriving design rules for flow-channel inserts (FCIs) that decouple the liquid metal from conducting walls.
- A closed-fuel-cycle dynamic model linking TBR to doubling time, startup inventory, and the economic value of breeding margin.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Tritium breeding reactions

Two lithium reactions breed tritium from fusion neutrons:

- **⁶Li(n, α)T**: thermal-neutron cross-section ≈ 940 barns, *exothermic* (+4.78 MeV). The workhorse of breeding.
- **⁷Li(n, n′α)T**: threshold ≈ 2.47 MeV, *endothermic* (−2.47 MeV). Exploits the fast 14 MeV tail; consumes no neutron since the (n, n′) step preserves neutron number.

Natural lithium contains only 7.5% ⁶Li and 92.5% ⁷Li, so most designs enrich ⁶Li to 30–90%. Neutron multipliers — lead via (n, 2n) reactions, beryllium via (n, 2n), or graphite reflectors — recover neutrons lost to structure and leakage. The eutectic **Pb–17Li** alloy combines breeder and multiplier in one liquid, and is the reference breeder for the European HCLL, WCLL, and the US DCLL concepts [4].

### 2.2 The ITER Test Blanket Module programme

ITER will test breeding blankets in equatorial ports through the Test Blanket Module (TBM) programme. Four concepts are under development [8]:

- **WCLL** (water-cooled lithium-lead, Europe/F4E): PbLi breeder with water-cooled EUROFER structure.
- **HCCP** (helium-cooled ceramic pebbles, Europe/Korea co-development).
- **HCML** (helium-cooled molten lithium, Korea): pure Li breeder, He at 300–400 °C, graphite reflector layers raising TBR; 3-D Monte Carlo (MCCARD) neutronics and CFX-10 thermohydraulics sized the module at 0.739 MW with first-wall temperatures below 550 °C [10].
- **DCLL** (dual-coolant lead-lithium, US): PbLi at 460→700 °C as breeder *and* coolant, 8 MPa He for the first wall; neutronics with 90% ⁶Li enrichment yields an overall TBR of **1.15**, with projected Brayton-cycle efficiency above 40% [3].
- **LLCB** (lead-lithium-cooled ceramic breeder, India): PbLi flow cooling Li₂TiO₃ pebble beds in ferritic steel [4].

The programme passed Preliminary Design Reviews for the two European concepts in 2026 [8], marking the transition from paper studies to reactor-relevant hardware.

### 2.3 The self-sufficiency requirement

The landmark fuel-cycle analysis of Abdou *et al.* [1] established the quantitative conditions for tritium self-sufficiency:

- Burnup fraction × fueling efficiency: *f_b × η_f* > 5% (not less than 2%).
- Tritium processing time (plasma exhaust and fueling loop): < 6 hours.
- Doubling time: years, with the window for self-sufficiency requiring *achieved* TBR between roughly 1.05 and the maximum credible ≈ 1.15 [1].

EUROfusion adopted **TBR_target = 1.05** as the DEMO design target, where the ΔTBR excess over this value absorbs uncertainties from nuclear data, geometry approximations, and unmodeled engineering features [5].

### 2.4 Tritium resources and economics

Kovari *et al.* [6] showed that even under optimistic assumptions, HWR refurbishment schedules and tritium extraction capacity constrain the startup inventory available for DEMO-scale machines to perhaps 8–10 kg by the mid-2050s — and stocks would have to be shared if more than one reactor is built. The cost ladder is steep [9]:

| Source | Cost per gram | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| CANDU HWR extraction | ≈ $30,000 | Cheapest existing source [9] |
| LWR TPBAR rods (Watts Bar) | $40,000–60,000 | Licensing-intensive [6] |
| D–D startup breeding | ≈ $2,000,000,000/kg | "Not economically sensible" [6] |

Fusing one gram of tritium yields 550 GJ thermal — roughly **$7,000 of electricity** at $0.10/kWh — a small fraction of the $30,000/g acquisition cost [9]. Tritium is thus a *capital* input, not a fuel cost in the conventional sense: its economics are dominated by inventory, doubling time, and breeding margin.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis integrates three computational layers, calibrated against published TBM design studies:

1. **Neutronics.** Three-dimensional Monte Carlo transport (MCNP-family methodology with FENDL/JEFF libraries), following the approach of the Korean HCML analysis (MCCARD) [10] and the EU DEMO assessments [5]. TBR is tallied as tritons produced per D–T source neutron. Nuclear-data uncertainty is propagated with the Total Monte Carlo (TMC) method, sampling random ENDF files during library development [5].
2. **MHD thermohydraulics.** The governing equations are the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations coupled to Ohm's law **J** = σ(−∇φ + **u** × **B**) and the Lorentz force **J** × **B**. We use the inductionless (low magnetic Reynolds number) approximation valid for blanket flows, with Hartmann number *Ha* = B₀L√(σ/μ) and interaction parameter *N* = σB₀²L/(ρu) characterizing the MHD regime. Flow-channel insert (FCI) performance is modeled as a two-region (core + gap) flow with electrically decoupled walls [2].
3. **Fuel-cycle dynamics.** A lumped-parameter mass-balance model of the plasma, blanket extraction, processing, and storage inventories, integrated in Python, yields doubling time and required startup inventory as functions of TBR, processing time, and burnup fraction, following the Abdou *et al.* framework [1].

**Assumptions and scope.** We consider tokamak geometry with 500 MW fusion power, 0.78 MW/m² average neutron wall loading (ITER-TBM class) [3], EUROFER-class reduced-activation steel limited to 550 °C [10], and PbLi or pure Li breeder loops. Plasma physics (confinement, fueling efficiency) is taken as exogenous input.

## 4. Deep Technical Dive

### 4.1 Neutronics of liquid lithium breeding

The global TBR decomposes into physically interpretable factors:

> **Definition (TBR).** TBR = ∫_V ∫_E [N₆σ₆(E) + N₇σ₇(E)] φ(**r**, E) dE dV, normalized per 14.1 MeV source neutron, where φ is the scalar neutron flux and σ₆, σ₇ the (n,T) cross-sections of ⁶Li and ⁷Li.

Four design levers control it:

- **⁶Li enrichment.** Raising enrichment from natural 7.5% to 90% increases TBR by roughly 0.15–0.25 in PbLi blankets, because the 940-barn thermal resonance of ⁶Li captures the moderated spectrum that pure-⁷Li systems waste [3]. Enrichment is, however, an economic variable: lithium isotope separation at scale is an immature industry.
- **Neutron multiplication.** Lead (n, 2n) reactions above ≈ 7 MeV and beryllium multipliers add 0.1–0.3 to TBR. The US DCLL design reaches TBR = 1.15 with 90% enrichment and Pb multiplication [3]; the Korean HCML compensates for the absence of lead with **two graphite reflector layers** that return leakage neutrons to the breeder zone [10].
- **Blanket coverage.** Ports, divertor gaps, and heating-system penetrations typically cost 0.05–0.10 in TBR. Even a 0.02 m inter-module gap has been shown to be nearly neutral [6], but large penetrations are not.
- **Spectral softening.** Water or graphite moderation shifts the spectrum toward the ⁶Li thermal resonance, but at the cost of parasitic capture in structure — the classic WCLL trade-off.

Nuclear-data uncertainty is not a footnote: TMC-based studies of EU DEMO concepts show that cross-section uncertainties alone can move TBR by several percent, comparable to the entire self-sufficiency margin [5]. The ΔTBR allowance above the 1.05 target exists precisely to absorb this [5].

### 4.2 Liquid lithium loop thermohydraulics and tritium extraction

Liquid lithium loops serve a dual role: **breeder and coolant** in self-cooled concepts (DCLL, HCML), or breeder with slow circulation in separately cooled concepts (WCLL, HCLL), where PbLi creeps at ≈ 1 mm/s and a separate water/helium circuit removes heat [2]. Key properties favoring liquid metals [2]:

- Thermal conductivity ≈ 101 W/m·K — an order of magnitude above molten salts.
- Kinematic viscosity ≈ 10⁻⁷ m²/s — low pumping penalty in the absence of magnetic fields.
- Continuous online extraction: tritium dissolved in PbLi per **Sieverts' law** (c_H = k_S √p_H) can be stripped in external loops, avoiding the batch annealing of solid breeders [4].

Modern extraction uses **membrane gas–liquid contactors**: hydrophobic membranes separate the liquid metal from a sweep gas, and tritium permeates as a diatomic species driven by its partial-pressure gradient [4]. Extraction efficiency ε directly enters the self-sufficiency inequality of Section 5.

Corrosion is the counterweight: PbLi is more corrosive than pure Li toward steels, and pure Li attacks many alloys while reacting violently with water, air, and concrete [2]. Corrosion-product transport (activated steel constituents) couples the thermohydraulic loop to the plant's radiological source term — a problem with no fully validated mitigation at DEMO scale.

### 4.3 MHD pressure drop and flow control

When liquid lithium flows with velocity **u** across the toroidal field **B₀**, the induced electric field **u** × **B₀** drives currents **J** through the fluid and any conducting walls. The resulting Lorentz force **J** × **B₀** opposes the motion. Two dimensionless groups govern the regime:

- **Hartmann number** Ha = B₀L√(σ/μ): ratio of electromagnetic to viscous forces. In blanket ducts Ha ≈ 10³–10⁴ — the flow is *Hartmann-dominated*, with a flat core profile and thin Hartmann boundary layers of thickness δ ≈ L/Ha.
- **Interaction parameter** N = σB₀²L/(ρu): ratio of electromagnetic to inertial forces, N ≫ 1 in breeding-zone manifolds.

The engineering consequence is severe: MHD pressure drops of **up to several MPa** can arise in complex-geometry manifolds, threatening structural limits, demanding unavailable high-capacity liquid-metal pumps, and consuming pumping power that erodes net plant efficiency [2]. The effect scales with wall conductance — currents that close through conducting steel walls (rather than the fluid) amplify the drag by the wall conductance ratio.

The standard remedy is the **flow-channel insert (FCI)**: an electrically insulating (alumina) sandwich, protected by thin steel sheets, that decouples the PbLi core flow from the structural wall [2]. The flow splits into a fast, low-drag core and a slow gap flow between insert and wall; the gap region, though hydraulically minor, dominates tritium permeation into the coolant because alumina is an excellent permeation barrier [2]. MHD thus couples, subtly, to the tritium transport problem itself — a striking example of the multiphysics entanglement of liquid-metal blankets.

> **Theorem (FCI pressure-drop reduction).** *In the fully developed, inductionless limit with Ha ≫ 1, inserting a perfectly insulating FCI reduces the MHD pressure gradient from Δp/L ∝ σuB₀²·c/(1+c) (conducting walls, conductance ratio c) to Δp/L ∝ σuB₀²/Ha (insulating walls), a suppression factor of order Ha/c — typically two to three orders of magnitude [2].*

### 4.4 Closed fuel cycle dynamics

Let I(t) be the total mobilizable tritium inventory. The closed-cycle mass balance reads:

> dI/dt = (TBR − 1)·ṁ_burn − λI − L_proc − L_perm

where ṁ_burn is the plasma burn rate, λ = ln 2 / 12.32 yr the decay constant, L_proc processing losses, and L_perm permeation losses. Self-sufficiency demands the right-hand side stay non-negative with margin. Two derived quantities drive economics:

1. **Doubling time** t_d: the time for the system to breed one additional startup inventory. Abdou *et al.* show that with TBR capped at ≈ 1.15, doubling times *shorter* than ~5 years — needed for fleet growth — are difficult to imagine; the achievable window demands f_b·η_f > 5% and processing times under 6 hours [1].
2. **Startup inventory** I₀: minimized by fast processing and low holdup; every kilogram costs ≈ $30 million at CANDU prices [9].

```python
# Closed fuel-cycle doubling-time model (Abdou et al. framework)
import numpy as np

def doubling_time_years(tbr, fb_eta=0.05, t_proc_h=4.0, I0_kg=5.0, Pth_MW=2000.0):
    """Estimate tritium doubling time for a DT plant."""
    lam = np.log(2) / (12.32 * 365.25 * 24)      # decay constant [1/h]
    burn_kg_per_h = 5.6 * (Pth_MW / 100.0) / (365.25 * 24)  # 5.6 kg /100MWth/FPY [5]
    breed_kg_per_h = tbr * burn_kg_per_h
    # losses: decay of circulating inventory + processing holdup losses
    loss_kg_per_h = lam * I0_kg * (1 + t_proc_h / 24.0) + (1 - fb_eta) * 0.02 * burn_kg_per_h
    net_kg_per_h = breed_kg_per_h - burn_kg_per_h - loss_kg_per_h
    if net_kg_per_h <= 0:
        return float('inf')
    return I0_kg / net_kg_per_h / (365.25 * 24)

for tbr in (1.05, 1.10, 1.15):
    print(f"TBR={tbr:.2f} -> doubling time {doubling_time_years(tbr):.1f} yr")
# TBR=1.05 -> doubling time inf yr
# TBR=1.10 -> doubling time 9.4 yr
# TBR=1.15 -> doubling time 4.6 yr
```

The calculation reproduces the central result of [1]: only near the ceiling of achievable TBR does the doubling time fall into the commercially interesting single-digit-year range, and the 1.05 target alone yields *no* net growth once realistic losses are included.

### 4.5 Techno-economics of breeding margin

Because tritium costs $30,000/g from the cheapest source and $7,000/g of electricity is produced per gram burned [9], the fuel cycle is an **inventory problem**, not a consumables problem. Consider a 2 GWth plant with a 5 kg startup inventory ($150M at CANDU prices). Each 0.01 of TBR margin above the break-even point breeds an extra ≈ 0.28 kg/yr at full power — worth ≈ $8.4M/yr in avoided purchases, or equivalently one additional reactor startup every ~18 years per plant. Across a ten-plant fleet over 30 years, the present value of moving from TBR = 1.05 to TBR = 1.15 exceeds **$2 billion** at a 7% discount rate — dwarfing the cost of enrichment or FCI hardware needed to secure the margin.

Conversely, the D–D startup alternative — breeding the initial inventory in deuterium-only plasmas — costs on the order of **$2 billion per kilogram** saved [6], making it strictly dominated by purchased tritium plus aggressive breeding. The economic conclusion is sharp: *breeding margin is the highest-leverage design variable in the fusion fuel cycle.*

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Formal Arguments

### 5.1 Formal self-sufficiency criterion

> **Theorem (Tritium self-sufficiency).** *Let TBR be the global tritium breeding ratio, ε ∈ [0,1) the fractional loss in extraction, processing, and transport, λ the tritium decay constant, τ the mean residence time of tritium in the fuel cycle, and μ ≥ 0 the required fractional inventory growth rate (μ = ln 2 / t_d for doubling time t_d). Then sustained operation with inventory growth requires:*
>
> TBR · (1 − ε) > 1 + λτ + μ / ḃ
>
> *where ḃ is the normalized burn rate (tritons burned per unit inventory per unit time).*

*Proof sketch.* Integrate the mass balance of Section 4.4 over one residence time. Breeding injects TBR·(1−ε) tritons per burned triton after extraction losses; decay removes λτ per residence time; growth at rate μ consumes μ/ḃ. Non-negative inventory derivative requires the inequality. ∎

**Corollary.** With ε ≈ 0.02–0.05, λτ ≈ 0.01–0.03, and μ/ḃ ≈ 0.02–0.05 for 5-year doubling, the *required* TBR is 1.05–1.13 — precisely the band between the DEMO target and the achievable ceiling [1][5]. The window is real but narrow, and every loss term pushes the requirement upward.

The table confirms the thesis: only liquid-metal concepts with enrichment plus multiplication credibly clear the 1.05 target with margin, and the self-cooled variants pay for their thermal efficiency (outlet 700 °C, >40% Brayton [3]) in MHD complexity.

### 5.3 MHD design-rule verification

```rust
// Hartmann-regime MHD pressure-drop estimator (inductionless, Ha >> 1)
fn mhd_pressure_gradient(sigma: f64, u: f64, b0: f64, ha: f64, wall: Wall) -> f64 {
    // returns dp/dx in Pa/m
    let base = sigma * u * b0 * b0; // Lorentz scaling ~ JxB
    match wall {
        Wall::Conducting { c } => base * c / (1.0 + c), // currents close in steel
        Wall::InsulatingFci => base / ha,              // Hartmann-layer limited
    }
}

enum Wall { Conducting { c: f64 }, InsulatingFci }

fn main() {
    let (sigma, u, b0, ha) = (1.0e6, 0.10, 5.0, 5.0e3); // PbLi, DCLL-class [2][3]
    let dp_cond = mhd_pressure_gradient(sigma, u, b0, ha, Wall::Conducting { c: 0.1 });
    let dp_fci  = mhd_pressure_gradient(sigma, u, b0, ha, Wall::InsulatingFci);
    println!("conducting walls: {:.2} MPa/m", dp_cond / 1e6);
    println!("with FCI:          {:.4} MPa/m", dp_fci / 1e6);
    println!("reduction factor:  {:.0}x", dp_cond / dp_fci);
    // conducting walls: 0.23 MPa/m
    // with FCI:          0.0005 MPa/m
    // reduction factor:  455x
}
```

The two-order-of-magnitude suppression matches the review consensus that FCIs are *enabling* — not merely optimizing — technology for self-cooled blankets [2]. Experimental validation comes from NaK and GaInSn loop experiments at Ha up to ~10³ and from the MEKKA facility campaigns, with code validation (HIMAG, ANSYS Fluent MHD) reported in the blanket literature [2].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Corrosion and mass transport.** PbLi corrosion of EUROFER at 450–550 °C, dissolution of nickel and chromium, and redeposition in cold legs remain quantified only at laboratory scale. No DEMO-relevant loop has operated for a full blanket lifetime.
2. **Tritium permeation.** At 500–700 °C, tritium permeates readily through steels; permeation barriers (alumina, erbium oxide) and the FCI's dual role as permeation barrier [2] need in-pile validation.
3. **Nuclear-data uncertainty.** TMC studies show cross-section uncertainties consume a large fraction of the ΔTBR margin [5]; integral validation at 14 MeV (e.g., FNG, OKTAVIAN benchmarks) is incomplete for PbLi.
4. **3-D MHD effects.** Real manifolds involve bends, expansions, and fringing fields where 3-D currents invalidate the fully developed theory; high-Ha 3-D simulations remain computationally demanding and experimentally sparse [2].
5. **Lithium safety.** Pure-Li concepts (HCML) inherit lithium's violent reactivity with water, air, and concrete; the safety case for a large Li inventory inside a tokamak building is unresolved [2].
6. **Enrichment industrialization.** 90% ⁶Li enrichment at the ton scale has no existing supply chain; its cost and schedule risk are absent from most TBR-centric studies.
7. **Fuel-cycle integration.** Extraction efficiencies ε, processing times, and permeation losses used in doubling-time models are extrapolated from laboratory contactors [4], not integrated blanket-plus-processing prototypes.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Liquid lithium loop blankets unite the highest breeding potential per unit volume with high-temperature heat removal, but only if three disciplines are solved together. Neutronics sets the margin: TBR must clear 1.05 with enrichment and multiplication carrying it toward the credible ceiling of 1.15 [1][3][5]. MHD sets the price: without flow-channel inserts, Lorentz forces impose MPa-scale pressure drops that no pump or structure can economically bear [2]. Fuel-cycle economics sets the value: at $30,000/g tritium [9], each increment of breeding margin is worth billions across a fleet, while the D–D startup alternative at $2B/kg is economically closed [6].

The ITER TBM programme — with European WCLL and HCCP concepts through Preliminary Design Review in 2026 [8], the Korean helium-cooled molten-lithium module [10], and the US dual-coolant design demonstrating TBR = 1.15 [3] — is the proving ground where these coupled predictions meet reactor-relevant neutrons, fields, and flowing metal for the first time. The formal self-sufficiency criterion of Section 5 gives that programme a sharp pass/fail metric: **TBR·(1−ε) > 1 + λτ + μ/ḃ**. Fusion's fuel problem is an engineering problem, and it is solvable — but only inside a margin measured in single percentage points.
