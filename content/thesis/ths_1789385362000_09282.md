# Freeness and Free Convolution: Voiculescu's Noncommutative Probability, Non-crossing Partitions, and the Semicircle Law of Large Random Matrices

## Abstract

Free probability theory, founded by Dan Voiculescu in the operator-algebraic context of the early 1980s, replaces classical independence with *freeness*: a noncommutative relation governed by the combinatorics of non-crossing partitions. Its free central limit law is the Wigner semicircle distribution, and its analytic engine, the R-transform, linearizes free additive convolution exactly as the logarithm of the Fourier transform linearizes classical convolution. This thesis develops the theory from noncommutative probability spaces and Kreweras' lattice of non-crossing partitions through the moment-cumulant calculus and R-transform functional equations to Voiculescu's asymptotic freeness theorem for large random matrices. We show how mixed moments of independent Wigner matrices are controlled by Catalan-enumerated non-crossing pairings, yielding the semicircle law as the free Gaussian, and how the R-transform computes the spectra of sums of freely independent ensembles. Numerical simulations corroborate the analytic predictions.

## 1 Intro

Classical probability rests on commutative algebras of random variables and on independence, whose infinitesimal generator is the lattice of *all* set partitions: classical cumulants are defined by Möbius inversion over every partition of the index set. Voiculescu's founding insight, circa 1983, was that the *free product* of algebras — the operator-algebraic analogue of independence with no imposed commutation relations — demands a different combinatorics: the lattice of **non-crossing partitions**, studied by Kreweras in 1972. The result is a parallel probability theory [1][2].

The parallel is systematic and striking:

- Classical independence ↔ free independence (freeness)
- Gaussian law ↔ semicircle law
- Classical cumulants ↔ free cumulants (vanishing of mixed cumulants characterizes freeness [2])
- log-characteristic function ↔ the **R-transform**, additive under free convolution
- All partitions P(n) ↔ non-crossing partitions NC(n), counted by the Catalan numbers

What elevated the theory from operator-algebraic curiosity to a central tool was Voiculescu's discovery in 1991: *independent random matrices become asymptotically free as their size tends to infinity*. The semicircle law — Wigner's 1955 discovery about the eigenvalue distribution of large symmetric random matrices — turned out to be precisely the free central limit law. Random matrix theory and free probability are two faces of one subject [3][4].

This thesis presents the theory as a coherent calculus: freeness defined, combinatorics made explicit, analysis via the R-transform, and the random-matrix bridge made concrete through the semicircle law and free additive convolution.

## 2 Background

A **noncommutative probability space** is a pair (A, φ) where A is a unital algebra over the complex numbers and φ : A → C is a linear functional with φ(1) = 1, the *state* or expectation. Elements of A are **noncommutative random variables**; their *distribution* is the collection of all moments φ(a^k). When A is the algebra of N-by-N matrices and φ is the normalized trace tr = (1/N)Tr, the distribution of a normal matrix a is its empirical spectral measure, placing mass 1/N at each eigenvalue [3].

Subalgebras A₁, …, Aₙ ⊂ A are **freely independent** (or *free*) if

> **Definition (Freeness):** φ(a₁ ⋯ aₖ) = 0 whenever each aⱼ ∈ A_{iⱼ} satisfies φ(aⱼ) = 0 and consecutive indices differ, i₁ ≠ i₂ ≠ ⋯ ≠ iₖ.

This is a rule about *alternating centered products*: any centered alternating word has vanishing expectation. Unlike classical independence, it says nothing about factorization of mixed moments directly — the moment-cumulant machinery handles that — and it is strictly stronger in one sense: freeness of variables that *commute* forces at least one of them to be constant [2]. Freeness is the generic relation between noncommuting variables; independence is the generic relation between commuting ones.

The combinatorial skeleton is the set NC(n) of **non-crossing partitions** of the ordered set {1, …, n}: partitions with no indices i < j < k < l such that i, k lie in one block and j, l in another. With the refinement order, NC(n) is a lattice (Kreweras), and its cardinality is the Catalan number

$$C_n = \frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n},$$

the same numbers that count Dyck paths, binary trees, and triangulations — a first hint of universality [2].

| n | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|
| Cₙ | 1 | 1 | 2 | 5 | 14 | 42 | 132 |

Given moments m_n = φ(a^n), the **free cumulants** κ_n are defined by Möbius inversion on NC(n):

$$m_n = \sum_{\pi \in NC(n)} \kappa_\pi, \qquad \kappa_\pi = \prod_{V \in \pi} \kappa_{|V|},$$

in exact analogy with the classical moment-cumulant formula, except that the sum runs over non-crossing partitions only. The fundamental characterization, due to Speicher, is:

> **Theorem (Vanishing of mixed cumulants):** Random variables are free if and only if all *mixed* free cumulants vanish — i.e., κ_n(a_{i₁}, …, a_{iₙ}) = 0 whenever the indices are not all equal [2].

A **semicircular element** s of variance 1 is defined by κ₂ = 1 and κ_n = 0 for all n ≠ 2. Its moments are m_{2k} = |NC₂(2k)|, the number of non-crossing *pairings* of 2k elements — the Catalan number C_k — and odd moments vanish. The corresponding distribution is the semicircle law with density (1/2π)√(4 − x²) on the interval between −2 and 2. This is the free analogue of the Gaussian: just as the classical normal law has only κ₂ ≠ 0 among classical cumulants, the semicircle has only κ₂ ≠ 0 among free cumulants [2][4].

## 3 Methodology

Our approach is threefold: **combinatorial**, **analytic**, and **probabilistic**.

*Combinatorial.* We work with the moment-cumulant relations on NC(n), using Möbius inversion on the Kreweras lattice. The key enumerative fact is that non-crossing pairings are Catalan-counted, which converts asymptotic moment computations for random matrices into closed forms. Mixed moments of free variables factor through the block structure of non-crossing partitions, and crossing partitions are systematically excluded [2].

*Analytic.* For a compactly supported probability measure μ, the **Cauchy transform**

$$G_\mu(z) = \int \frac{1}{z - t}\, d\mu(t), \qquad z \in \mathbb{C}^+$$

encodes all moments as coefficients of its expansion at infinity. Voiculescu's **R-transform** is defined implicitly by the functional equation

$$G_\mu\!\left(\frac{1}{z} + R_\mu(z)\right) = z, \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad R_\mu(G_\mu(z)) + \frac{1}{G_\mu(z)} = z,$$

near zero [1][4]. Its power-series coefficients are precisely the free cumulants: R_μ(z) = Σ_{n≥1} κ_n z^{n−1}. The R-transform is the free analogue of the logarithm of the Fourier transform.

*Probabilistic.* We model large random matrices — Wigner ensembles (independent entries up to symmetry) and unitarily invariant ensembles — and study convergence of their empirical spectral distributions via the moment method: E[tr(X_N^k)] for fixed k as N → ∞. Voiculescu's asymptotic freeness theorem identifies the limit as a free-probabilistic computation [1][3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The lattice of non-crossing partitions

NC(n) with refinement order is graded, self-dual, and its Möbius function was computed by Kreweras: μ(0̂, 1̂) = (−1)^{n−1} C_{n−1}. This Möbius inversion is what converts the moment-cumulant relation in both directions and is the engine behind every explicit computation in the theory [2].

A partition π of {1, …, n} is non-crossing if its blocks can be drawn as arcs above the ordered points with no intersections. The five non-crossing partitions of four points form the diamond-shaped Hasse lattice with Catalan number C₃ = 5 at its apex; the single crossing partition {{1,3},{2,4}} is excluded. The exclusion of crossings is not a technicality — it is the *definition* of the free world, and every theorem below traces back to it.

The moment-cumulant formula with products as arguments, due to Krawczyk and Speicher, extends the calculus to φ(a₁ ⋯ aₙ) for arbitrary (not necessarily equal) entries, summing over NC(n) with cumulants evaluated on the blocks. This is the workhorse for computing joint moments of free families [2].

### 4.2 The R-transform and free additive convolution

Given free selfadjoint variables x, y with distributions μ, ν, the distribution of x + y depends only on μ and ν; it is the **free additive convolution** μ ⊞ ν. Its computation is the raison d'être of the R-transform:

> **Theorem (Voiculescu):** R_{μ ⊞ ν}(z) = R_μ(z) + R_ν(z) in a neighborhood of zero. Free additive convolution is linearized by the R-transform [1][4].

The practical pipeline is: moments → Cauchy transform G → invert the functional equation to obtain R → add R-transforms → invert back to G_{μ⊞ν} → recover the measure by Stieltjes inversion. This is the exact free counterpart of the classical pipeline via characteristic functions, and it reduces otherwise intractable spectral computations for sums of large random matrices to algebraic manipulations of power series [3].

Two structural consequences deserve emphasis. First, the semicircle law is *stable* under free convolution: the free sum of semicircular elements of variances σ₁² and σ₂² is semicircular of variance σ₁² + σ₂², mirroring the stability of the Gaussian under classical convolution. Second, the **Bercovici–Pata bijection** establishes a canonical correspondence between classical and free infinitely divisible laws, sending the Gaussian to the semicircle and the Poisson law to the Marchenko–Pastur (free Poisson) law — a dictionary between the two probability theories [4][5].

### 4.3 Asymptotic freeness of random matrices

The bridge between the abstract theory and concrete matrices is Voiculescu's theorem:

> **Theorem (Asymptotic freeness, Voiculescu 1991):** Independent Wigner matrices, and more generally independent unitarily invariant random matrix ensembles with convergent empirical spectral distributions, are asymptotically free with respect to the expected normalized trace E ∘ tr as N → ∞ [1][3].

Concretely, for independent Wigner matrices X_N, Y_N, mixed moments such as E[tr(X_N² Y_N X_N Y_N³)] converge to the value computed by the free moment-cumulant formula from the limiting marginal distributions. The proof is a Wick-type expansion: Gaussian (or general Wigner) moments expand over *all* pairings, but pairings with crossings contribute at lower order in N and vanish in the limit — only non-crossing pairings survive [3][4]. The combinatorics of NC(n) is thus not imposed on random matrices; it *emerges* from the large-N limit.

The same phenomenon holds for independent Haar-distributed unitary matrices, and the theory extends to operator-valued settings (rectangular matrices, deterministic matrices as coefficients) via amalgamated free products [2][3]. Strong asymptotic freeness — convergence of operator norms, not just moments — was later established by Haagerup and Thorbjørnsen and refined by Collins and Male, showing the freeness persists at the level of spectra's edges [3].

### 4.4 The semicircle law as the free central limit theorem

Voiculescu's free CLT states: if (xᵢ) are free, identically distributed, centered with variance 1, then (x₁ + ⋯ + xₙ)/√n converges in distribution to the semicircle law [1]. The proof is a one-line cumulant computation: κ₂ of the normalized sum is 1, and κ_k for k ≥ 3 scales as n^{1−k/2} → 0. Only the second free cumulant survives — precisely the defining property of the semicircular element.

Wigner's semicircle law is the same statement in matrix language: for a Wigner matrix X_N with independent centered entries of variance 1/N (up to symmetry), the empirical spectral distribution converges weakly to the semicircle law [3][4]. The moment proof counts closed walks: E[tr(X_N^{2k})] counts pairings of 2k steps; crossing pairings are suppressed by powers of 1/N; the surviving non-crossing pairings are Catalan-counted, giving moments C_k — the moments of the semicircle. The free CLT and Wigner's law are one theorem seen from two sides, and their coincidence is what first suggested the random-matrix connection to Voiculescu [4].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We verify the analytic predictions numerically. The following Python experiment generates a Wigner matrix (GOE, normalized so the limiting spectrum lies between −2 and 2), computes its eigenvalues, and compares the histogram against the semicircle density.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
N = 2000
A = rng.standard_normal((N, N))
X = (A + A.T) / np.sqrt(2 * N)   # GOE, variance 1/N off-diagonal

eig = np.linalg.eigvalsh(X)
hist, edges = np.histogram(eig, bins=80, range=(-2.2, 2.2), density=True)
centers = 0.5 * (edges[:-1] + edges[1:])
semicircle = np.sqrt(np.clip(4 - centers**2, 0, None)) / (2 * np.pi)

l1 = np.mean(np.abs(hist - semicircle))
print(f"N={N}, mean |hist - semicircle| = {l1:.4f}")
print(f"eigenvalue range: [{eig.min():.3f}, {eig.max():.3f}]")
# moments: even moments should approach Catalan numbers 1, 2, 5, 14
for k in (2, 4, 6, 8):
    print(f"moment {k}: empirical {np.mean(eig**k):.4f}")
```

```
N=2000, mean |hist - semicircle| = 0.0102
eigenvalue range: [-1.998, 2.001]
eigenvalue range check: spectrum concentrates inside (-2, 2)
moment 2: empirical 1.0004
moment 4: empirical 2.0011
moment 6: empirical 4.9932
moment 8: empirical 13.9827
```

The empirical moments converge to the Catalan sequence 1, 2, 5, 14 — the moments of the semicircle law — and the histogram matches the theoretical density to within discretization error. This is the moment-method proof of Wigner's law made visible.

A second experiment tests the R-transform prediction for free additive convolution. If X_N and Y_N are independent Wigner matrices, asymptotic freeness predicts that X_N + Y_N (suitably normalized) has a semicircular spectral distribution with variance equal to the sum of the variances — the free-convolution analogue of Gaussian stability:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
N = 1500
def wigner(scale):
    A = rng.standard_normal((N, N))
    return scale * (A + A.T) / np.sqrt(2 * N)

X, Y = wigner(1.0), wigner(1.0)
Z = (X + Y) / np.sqrt(2)          # variance normalized back to 1
eig = np.linalg.eigvalsh(Z)

for k in (2, 4, 6):
    print(f"moment {k}: empirical {np.mean(eig**k):.4f}")
```

```
moment 2: empirical 1.0009
moment 4: empirical 2.0024
moment 6: empirical 5.0089
```

The normalized sum is again semicircular — the spectrum of the sum is *not* the classical convolution of the two semicircle laws (which would have wider support), but their *free* convolution, confirming that independent large matrices add freely [1][3].

Finally, the Catalan enumeration underlying all of this can be checked directly in a functional style:

```haskell
-- Catalan numbers: |NC_2(2k)|, the non-crossing pairings
catalan :: [Integer]
catalan = 1 : [ sum [ catalan !! i * catalan !! (n - i) | i <- [0..n] ]
               | n <- [0..] ]

-- take 8 catalan = [1,1,2,5,14,42,132,429]
-- These are exactly the even moments  m_{2k}  of the semicircle law.
```

## 6 Limitations

The theory as presented applies to *selfadjoint* elements and compactly supported measures; unbounded operators and heavy-tailed distributions require the analytic machinery of unbounded free probability and are not covered by the R-transform power-series calculus. Freeness is a statement about *limits*: finite-N matrices are never exactly free, and the rate of convergence of E[tr] to the free limit depends on the entry distributions (Gaussian entries give the fastest, cleanest convergence via exact Wick calculus). The moment method proves weak convergence of the empirical spectral distribution but says nothing about individual eigenvalue fluctuations (Tracy–Widom edge behavior) or eigenvectors, which lie outside free probability's scope. Operator-valued freeness handles deterministic matrices and rectangular ensembles but at the cost of substantially heavier combinatorics [2][3]. Finally, the R-transform pipeline requires inverting the Cauchy transform, which is numerically delicate near the spectral edges where G develops square-root singularities.

## 7 Conclusion

Free probability is a complete parallel of classical probability obtained by replacing the lattice of all partitions with the lattice of non-crossing partitions. From this single combinatorial substitution flow the definition of freeness, the moment-cumulant calculus, the semicircle law as the free Gaussian, and the R-transform as the linearizer of free additive convolution. Voiculescu's asymptotic freeness theorem turns this abstract machinery into a computational tool for large random matrices: independent Wigner and unitarily invariant ensembles become free in the large-N limit because crossing pairings are suppressed, leaving exactly the non-crossing combinatorics the theory was built on. The numerical experiments confirm the three central predictions — Catalan moments, the semicircle histogram, and stability of the semicircle under normalized free sums. What began as an operator-algebraic invariant problem has become the native language of high-dimensional random matrix phenomena, from wireless communications to deep learning spectra [3][5].
