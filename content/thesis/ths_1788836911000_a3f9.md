# Learned Index Structures Beyond B-Trees: RMI Error Bounds, PGM-Index Piecewise Linear Approximation, ALEX Dynamic Inserts with Gapped Arrays, and Learned Bloom Filter Sandwiching

## Abstract

The learned index programme, initiated by Kraska et al. [1], reframes classical index structures as statistical models mapping keys to record positions, replacing the fixed partitioning logic of B-trees with models learned from the empirical key distribution. This paper provides a rigorous survey of four pillars of the modern learned-index literature: the Recursive Model Index (RMI) and its bounded-error "last-mile" correction semantics; the PGM-index, whose optimal piecewise linear approximation (PLA) recasts CDF modeling as a computational-geometry problem with provable worst-case bounds [2]; ALEX, which reconciles learned prediction with dynamic workloads through model-based insertion into gapped arrays and cost-model-driven retraining [3]; and learned Bloom filters, where a learned classifier is sandwiched between a front and backup filter to dominate the classical false-positive/memory frontier [1,5]. We develop the error-bound algebra common to all four, analyze lookup and update complexity, survey empirical evidence on real-world key sets, and map each design onto production deployments in LSM-tree engines such as RocksDB and modern columnar stores. We close by identifying open problems: robustness under adversarial and heavy-tailed distributions, concurrency, and closing the gap between learned and traditional structures under update-heavy skewed workloads.

## 1. Introduction

For fifty years the B-tree and its variants have been the canonical answer to the ordered-index problem: given a set $S$ of $n$ keys, support predecessor lookup, range iteration, and updates in $O(\log_B n)$ I/Os with distribution-agnostic worst-case guarantees. The learned-index thesis of Kraska et al. [1] proposes a deceptively simple inversion. An index over a sorted array $A$ is, functionally, a function $f: \mathcal{U} \rightarrow [0, n)$ mapping a key $x$ to its rank (position) in $A$. Since rank is monotone in $x$, $f(x)/n$ is precisely the empirical cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the keys. Rather than navigating a fixed structure, one can *learn* $\hat{f} \approx f$ with a statistical model and correct its bounded prediction error $\varepsilon$ with a short "last-mile" search — typically binary or exponential search over $[pos-\varepsilon, pos+\varepsilon]$ [4].

The promise is substantial: models with a few hundred parameters can replace millions of B-tree entries, yielding an order-of-magnitude reduction in memory while matching or exceeding lookup latency [1,3]. Yet the original RMI construction is heuristic — user-tuned model hierarchies with no worst-case guarantees — and static: it supports no inserts at all. The subsequent literature has responded along three axes:

1. **Guarantees.** The PGM-index [2] restricts $\hat{f}$ to piecewise linear functions of *minimum complexity* for a prescribed error $\varepsilon$, obtaining optimal worst-case query bounds and fully dynamic variants [8].
2. **Dynamism.** ALEX [3] introduces *gapped arrays* — leaf layouts that reserve slack so model-predicted insertion positions stay valid — plus cost models that decide when to expand, split, or retrain.
3. **Beyond point indexes.** Learned models can augment even approximate membership structures: sandwiched learned Bloom filters achieve strictly better false-positive rates than classical filters at equal memory [1,5].

This paper treats these four designs uniformly as instances of the same abstraction — *an $\varepsilon$-bounded predictor of rank, paired with a correction mechanism and (optionally) a slack policy for updates* — and asks where each stands relative to B-trees in theory and in deployment.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The rank-prediction view

Let $S = \{x_1 < x_2 < \cdots < x_n\}$ be sorted. Define $\text{rank}(x) = |\{x_i \in S : x_i \leq x\}|$. A *learned index* is a function $h: \mathcal{U} \rightarrow [0,n]$ such that

> **Definition 2.1 ($\varepsilon$-learned index).** $h$ is an $\varepsilon$-learned index for $S$ if $|h(q) - \text{rank}(q)| \leq \varepsilon$ for every query key $q \in \mathcal{U}$ [8].

Point lookup then reduces to evaluating $h(q)$ and searching the interval $[h(q)-\varepsilon, h(q)+\varepsilon]$ in the data array. If model evaluation costs $T(h)$ and the array is resident, lookup costs $T(h) + O(\log \varepsilon)$ [2,4]. Everything hinges on three quantities: the *model complexity* $|h|$ (bits or parameters), the *error bound* $\varepsilon$, and the evaluation time $T(h)$.

### 2.2 Classical baseline

A B+-tree with fanout $B$ guarantees $O(\log_B n)$ I/Os per point query, supports arbitrary insert/delete workloads in $O(\log_B n)$ amortized time, and stores $\Theta(n/B)$ inner nodes. Its weakness from the learning viewpoint is that it ignores the data distribution entirely: a uniformly random 64-bit key set and a heavily skewed log-normal set produce the same structure and the same cost. Learned indexes exploit the empirical CDF's regularity — smooth CDFs admit tiny models — to compress the index far below the B-tree's $\Theta(n)$ space, though they sacrifice the distribution-free worst-case guarantee [4,6].

### 2.3 What "learned" does not mean

It is worth stating precisely. A learned index is not a black-box neural network applied to database problems; it is a *regression of the empirical CDF* with an explicit, verifiable error bound, plus a deterministic correction pass [4,7]. The ML component is interchangeable — linear regression, radix models, spline interpolation, small neural networks — and the strongest theoretical results (PGM-index) use no machine learning at all in the statistical sense, only piecewise linear geometry.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis follows a uniform methodology applied to each design:

- **Model class.** We identify the function family used to approximate the CDF (multi-level regression for RMI, PLA segments for PGM-index, per-node linear models over gapped arrays for ALEX, learned classifiers for Bloom filters).
- **Error-bound accounting.** We track how the local error bound $\varepsilon$ composes through hierarchies and how the last-mile search converts bounded prediction error into exact answers.
- **Update semantics.** For dynamic designs, we characterize the slack/buffering policy and its amortized cost.
- **Evaluation protocol.** Following the Search on Sorted Data (SOSD) benchmark methodology [6], we consider throughput and 99th-percentile latency on real-world key sets (osm, fb, wiki timestamps) versus synthetic uniform data, since real-world CDFs are substantially harder — SOSD reports average RMI latency $2.92\times$ higher on real than synthetic data [7].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Recursive Model Index: hierarchies of approximate rank

The RMI [1] is a $L$-level hierarchy. The root model $m^{(1)}$ maps a key $x$ to a child index $p = \lfloor m^{(1)}(x)/n \cdot C_2 \rfloor$; each second-stage model $m^{(2)}_p$ is trained only on the keys routed to partition $p$, and so on recursively until leaf models predict absolute positions. The construction is deliberately general: the user supplies a *specification* — model type per level, branching factor, leaf granularity — and a grid-search optimizer traces the space/performance Pareto frontier [4,7].

> **Theorem (informal, RMI error composition [1]).** If each stage model $m$ satisfies $|m(x) - \text{target}(x)| \leq \varepsilon_{\text{stage}}$ over its partition and partitions are routed exactly, then the leaf prediction satisfies $|m_{\text{leaf}}(x) - \text{rank}(x)| \leq \varepsilon_{\text{total}}$ for $\varepsilon_{\text{total}}$ the composition of stage errors; last-mile search over the corresponding window returns the exact answer.

RMI's decisive empirical result — up to $70\%$ faster than cache-optimized B-trees at $10\times$ less memory on real data [1] — rests on two facts: (i) each model evaluation is a handful of fused multiply-adds, far cheaper than a cache-miss-prone B-tree descent; (ii) real CDFs are locally smooth, so small linear models achieve tiny $\varepsilon$. Its weaknesses are equally well documented: no worst-case bound on $\varepsilon$ across arbitrary data (a pathological key set can force an unbounded number of models), manual hyperparameter tuning, no inserts, and degraded performance on highly non-linear CDFs — the so-called "hard" datasets in SOSD [6,7].

```python
# Conceptual RMI lookup: predict, then correct within the error bound.
def rmi_lookup(key, stages, eps_total, data):
    idx = stages[0].predict(key)                       # root -> child partition
    for model in stages[1:]:
        idx = model.predict(key, partition=idx)        # route through hierarchy
    lo, hi = max(0, idx - eps_total), idx + eps_total
    return binary_search(key, data, lo, hi)             # last-mile search
```

### 4.2 PGM-Index: optimal piecewise linear approximation

Ferragina and Vinciguerra [2] transform the problem into computational geometry. Given the points $(x_i, i)$ for $i = 1..n$, find the *minimum-cardinality* set of segments such that every point lies within vertical distance $\varepsilon$ of some segment. The resulting piecewise linear approximation (PLA) is simultaneously the model and its error certificate: each segment is a linear function $s_j(x) = a_j x + b_j$ valid over a key interval, and the $\varepsilon$-bound holds by construction.

> **Theorem (PGM-index [2]).** There exists a linear-time greedy algorithm computing a PLA whose segment count is $O(m)$, where $m$ is the optimal number of segments for error $\varepsilon$ (approximation factor $2$ in practice). Indexing the segments' endpoints recursively yields a multi-level PGM-index with $O(\log_m n)$ levels, $O(m)$ space, and worst-case lookup $O(\log m \cdot \log \varepsilon)$; in the external-memory model this is I/O-optimal.

The proof's core is the *streaming convex-hull* construction: maintaining the feasible-slope interval for the current segment in $O(1)$ amortized time per point and cutting a new segment exactly when the interval empties — a direct adaptation of the Douglas–Peucker-style optimal partitioning from computational geometry [2]. Because the recursion is built over the segment endpoints themselves rather than the raw keys, the index is *purely learned*: no B-tree nodes appear anywhere.

The dynamic PGM-index [2,8] applies the logarithmic method: it maintains $O(\log n)$ static PGM-indexes of exponentially growing capacities, flushing and merging on overflow. This yields the first learned index with provable worst-case update bounds — $O(\log^2 n)$ amortized insert time — though Gæde et al. [8] show that range queries under deletions can degrade to $O(N + \sum (\varepsilon + \log |h_i|))$ where $N$ is the historical maximum size. Three variants address production concerns: the *compressed* PGM-index exploits repetitiveness across segments (e.g., periodic slopes in timestamp data); the *distribution-aware* variant re-optimizes $\varepsilon$ per segment under a query workload; the *multicriteria* variant auto-tunes the space–time frontier in seconds over hundreds of millions of keys [2]. Empirically, PGM-index shrinks the best previous learned index (FITing-tree) by $63.3\%$ and B-trees by over four orders of magnitude at equal query time [2].

### 4.3 ALEX: model-based inserts into gapped arrays

ALEX [3] attacks the static-only limitation head-on. Its central data structure is the *gapped array*: a leaf node stores keys densely packed in sorted order but with *gaps* — empty slots — distributed so that the node's linear model predicts each key's position. On insert of key $x$, ALEX evaluates the model, obtains predicted position $p$, and inserts $x$ at the nearest gap by shifting at most a few neighbors; no retraining is needed as long as the model stays accurate. This is model-based insertion: the layout *invariant* is that occupancy tracks the learned CDF, so future inserts land near their predicted positions with high probability.

Each node stores its data density between configurable bounds (e.g., min $0.6$, target $0.7$, max $0.8$ [3]). When density is violated or the model's accuracy degrades beyond a threshold, a *cost model* chooses among four actions: expand-and-retrain (resize the array, recompute the model), split sideways (partition the key range), split down (add a level), or merge with a sibling [3]. The cost model weights expected search cost (proportional to the number of shifts and the last-mile search window) against retraining cost, making ALEX *adaptive* rather than heuristic.

> **Theorem (informal, ALEX [3]).** Under the density invariants, point lookup costs $O(\log n + S)$ where $S$ is the expected shift distance, which is small when the node's model approximates the local CDF; inserts cost expected $O(\log n + S)$ amortized over the retraining schedule chosen by the cost model.

ALEX's reported results are striking: up to $2.2\times$ faster than the original RMI on read-only workloads with $15\times$ smaller index size, and up to $4.1\times$ faster than B+-trees across read-write workloads with up to $2000\times$ smaller index size [3]. The caveat, documented in follow-up work, is that ALEX's large-range "last-mile" searches degrade on real-world datasets where local CDFs are jagged; the CARMI analysis [7] and the "Are Updatable Learned Indexes Ready?" study both observe ALEX underperforming B-trees on adversarial insert patterns, motivating hybrids like LIPP's precise-position scheme and CARMI's cost-based node-type selection.

### 4.4 Learned Bloom filters and sandwiching

The same "models instead of structures" insight applies to approximate membership. A classical Bloom filter with $m$ bits and $k$ hashes for $n$ keys has false-positive rate $\approx (1 - e^{-kn/m})^k$. Kraska et al. [1] observe that a learned classifier $F(x) \in [0,1]$ estimating membership probability can *pre-filter* queries: if $F(x) < \tau$ (threshold), answer "not in set"; otherwise consult a small *backup* Bloom filter sized only for the false negatives of $F$. The total FPR becomes $\text{FPR} = \text{FPR}_F + (1-\text{FPR}_F)\cdot \text{FPR}_{\text{backup}}$, and because the backup filter is built over the much smaller false-negative set, the combined structure uses substantially less memory at equal FPR [1].

*Sandwiching* [5] tightens this further: place a small classical Bloom filter *before* the learned model, so that the many obvious non-members are rejected without invoking the classifier at all (saving inference cost), and keep the backup filter after it. Mitzenmacher's analysis shows the sandwiched construction strictly dominates both the plain learned filter and the classical filter on the FPR-vs-memory curve whenever the learned oracle has nontrivial accuracy — the front filter handles the easy negatives, the model the hard ones, the backup the false negatives [5]. In deployment terms, sandwiching is the membership analogue of the RMI's last-mile search: the model's error is not a correctness violation but a bounded resource cost, paid in bits of the backup structure.

| Structure | Model class | Error guarantee | Updates | Space vs B-tree/BF |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RMI [1] | Multi-level regression | Empirical $\varepsilon$, no worst case | None (static) | $\sim 10\times$ smaller |
| PGM-index [2] | Optimal PLA segments | Provable $\varepsilon$; I/O-optimal lookup | Logarithmic-method, $O(\log^2 n)$ amortized | $10^4\times$ smaller (static) |
| ALEX [3] | Per-node linear + gapped arrays | Empirical; cost-model retraining | Native inserts/deletes, cost-model adaptive | Up to $2000\times$ smaller |
| Sandwiched LBF [1,5] | Classifier + front/backup BF | FPR bound via backup filter | Rebuild on distribution shift | Strictly better FPR/memory frontier |

## 5. Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

**Proof sketch: PGM-index optimality.** The greedy segmentation maintains, for the active segment, the convex polygon of feasible $(a, b)$ parameters consistent with all points seen since the last cut; each new point $(x_i, i)$ intersects this polygon with the strip $|a x_i + b - i| \leq \varepsilon$. The polygon empties exactly when no single line fits the window within $\varepsilon$, at which point the algorithm emits the segment and starts fresh. Optimality within factor $2$ follows from the classic argument that any feasible solution needs a distinct segment for each emitted cut plus at most one more [2].

**Empirical picture (synthesized from [2,3,6,7]).** On SOSD's real-world datasets (osm cell IDs, Facebook user IDs, Wikipedia timestamps), the ranking is consistent: PGM-index and RMI dominate the latency–memory Pareto frontier for read-only point lookups, with PGM-index offering the best space at equal latency thanks to optimal segmentation; ALEX matches RMI read performance while adding native updates; classical B-trees and ART sit strictly inside the frontier on both axes [6,7]. On synthetic uniform data, gaps narrow — smooth CDFs are easy for everyone — but real CDFs exhibit sharp local curvature (e.g., bursty timestamps, clustered geo IDs) that punishes fixed structures and rewards adaptivity [6]. For learned Bloom filters, sandwiching reduces memory by $2$–$5\times$ versus classical filters at FPR $10^{-3}$–$10^{-4}$ on URL and malware-key workloads, provided the classifier is trained on representative non-members [1,5].

**Deployment in RocksDB and columnar engines.** The natural insertion point is the LSM-tree's SSTable layer: each SSTable is immutable, sorted, and queried by point lookup — precisely the static RMI/PGM-index setting. Replacing per-SSTable index blocks (and optionally Bloom filters) with a PGM-index yields the four-order-of-magnitude space win measured in [2] while keeping block-cache pressure low; prototype integrations report unchanged p99 read latency at substantially reduced memory. In columnar engines, learned indexes over sorted dictionary-encoded columns accelerate selective scans: the model predicts the row range for a predicate, and the columnar scan's last-mile correction is amortized over the range. Dynamic variants (ALEX-style gapped layouts) map onto in-memory row stores and memtable indexes, where insert rates are high and the cost model can absorb retraining during compaction pauses. The common deployment pattern is therefore *learned indexes at the immutable layer, adaptive learned structures at the mutable layer* [2,3,7].

## 6. Limitations

- **Worst-case adversarial data.** Except for PGM-index, learned indexes offer no distribution-free guarantees; an adversary (or merely a pathological but natural key set, e.g., cryptographic hashes with adversarial clustering) can force RMI error windows to cover the whole array or ALEX into perpetual retraining [6,8].
- **Heavy-tailed and shifting distributions.** Model accuracy degrades under distribution shift; ALEX's cost model mitigates this but cannot guarantee convergence, and learned Bloom filters require retraining when the non-member distribution drifts [3,5].
- **Write-heavy skewed workloads.** Under adversarial insert patterns (e.g., monotonically decreasing keys), gapped arrays degenerate: predicted positions cluster, shift distances blow up, and ALEX can underperform a plain B-tree — the failure mode motivating LIPP and CARMI [7].
- **Concurrency and recovery.** Learned structures complicate lock-free reads (model evaluation races with retraining) and crash recovery (models must be rebuilt or logged); ALEX+ adds optimistic concurrency, but production-grade MVCC integration remains open [7].
- **Tuning and operability.** RMI's specification grid search, PGM-index's $\varepsilon$ selection, and ALEX's density thresholds are expert knobs; the multicriteria PGM-index auto-tuner [2] is a step toward self-driving operation, but learned indexes are not yet "set and forget" infrastructure.
- **Theoretical gaps.** Optimal dynamic learned indexing with *worst-case* (not amortized) update bounds and tight space lower bounds for $\varepsilon$-approximate rank prediction remain open; Gæde et al. [8] provide the current frontier.

## 7. Conclusion

Learned indexes have matured from the provocative "indexes are models" slogan [1] into a small family of rigorously understood structures. RMI established the rank-prediction abstraction and the empirical space/latency win; the PGM-index supplied the missing theory — optimal piecewise linear approximation with provable worst-case bounds and I/O optimality [2]; ALEX supplied the missing dynamism — gapped arrays and cost-model-driven adaptation that keep predictions valid under inserts [3]; and sandwiched learned Bloom filters showed the paradigm extends to approximate membership with strictly better FPR/memory trade-offs [5]. The unifying lesson is the $\varepsilon$-bounded predictor plus deterministic correction: learning proposes, geometry and search dispose. For practitioners, the deployment calculus is now clear — learned indexes belong at the immutable, sorted layers of LSM and columnar systems, adaptive variants at the mutable layers — while the research frontier moves to adversarial robustness, concurrency, and self-tuning. The B-tree is not dead; it is being demoted from universal default to the robust fallback underneath a learned fast path.
