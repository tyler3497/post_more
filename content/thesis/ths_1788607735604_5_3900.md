# Orbital Trust Architectures: A Comparative Analysis of Trusted-Node and Entanglement-Based Quantum Key Distribution in Low-Earth-Orbit Constellations

## Abstract

The Micius mission demonstrated that satellite-to-ground quantum key distribution (QKD) over *1,200 km* exceeds fiber channel efficiency by roughly *twenty orders of magnitude*, establishing space as the only physically credible substrate for intercontinental quantum cryptography [3]. Yet scaling from single-satellite demonstrations to operational constellations forces a confrontation between two irreconcilable trust models: the **trusted-node** architecture, in which the satellite stores and XOR-relays keys and must be absolutely trustworthy, and the **entanglement-based** architecture, in which the satellite can be built "even by your enemy" without compromising security [2]. This thesis compares these architectures through atmospheric propagation physics, develops a composable link-loss model incorporating diffraction, extinction, Kolmogorov turbulence, and pointing error, and formulates constellation scheduling as constrained optimization over finite contact windows and finite-key security bounds. We argue quantitatively that near-term global quantum networks will be *hybrid*: trusted-node constellations for bulk throughput, augmented by entanglement-based passes for high-assurance users. Published flight data support per-pass yields of ~10⁵–10⁶ bits, with constellation sizing laws derived for continuous intercontinental coverage under realistic cloud-cover statistics [5][8].

## 1. Introduction

Quantum key distribution promises *information-theoretic* security — secrecy whose guarantee rests on the laws of quantum mechanics rather than on unproven computational hardness assumptions [1]. Fiber-based QKD networks, notably the Beijing–Shanghai backbone, have achieved regional deployment at the scale of thousands of kilometers of fiber. However, fiber attenuation scales **exponentially** with distance: at 1,200 km through standard telecom fiber, even an ideal 10-GHz single-photon source with perfect detectors would yield one sifted bit per *six million years* [3]. Free-space satellite links, by contrast, concentrate almost all propagation loss in a vacuum of effectively zero absorption; atmospheric interaction is confined to the lowest ~10–20 km of the path. This asymmetry is the entire physical argument for satellite QKD.

The Micius heritage establishes the empirical baseline:

- **2017**: Decoy-state BB84 satellite-to-ground QKD over ~1,200 km slant range, kbps-scale sifted key rates, published in *Nature* [3].
- **2017**: Intercontinental trusted-node relay between China and Austria over *7,600 km* [11].
- **2020**: Entanglement-based QKD over *1,120 km* between Delingha and Nanshan, the first demonstration that a satellite need not be trusted [2].
- **2025**: The Jinan-1 quantum *microsatellite* demonstrated real-time QKD with compact, mobile ground stations, including a China–South Africa link spanning nearly *13,000 km* with 1.07 million secure bits per pass [4].

Yet Micius is one 500-kg-class platform in a sun-synchronous orbit with ~5 minutes of useful contact per ground station per night. A *constellation* — tens to hundreds of smaller platforms with portable optical ground stations (OGS) — is the only path to continuous, global, weather-resilient service [4][5]. Constellation design is not merely a matter of orbital mechanics; the security architecture dictates what the network can promise.

> **Theorem (Architecture–Trust Correspondence):** In any prepare-and-measure satellite QKD network where the satellite performs classical post-processing on key material (trusted-node mode), the end-to-end security of a key shared between ground stations A and B is upper-bounded by the *physical security* of the satellite platform: an adversary with root access to the spacecraft obtains the key with probability 1. In entanglement-based operation, the same adversary obtains zero information-theoretic advantage. ∎

This single theorem organizes the thesis. Sections 2–4 develop the background, propagation model, and scheduling formalism; Section 5 quantifies per-pass performance; Section 6 examines limitations including the Micius side-channel controversy; Section 7 concludes with a hybrid-architecture recommendation.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 QKD Protocols in the Space Domain

Practical satellite QKD to date has relied almost exclusively on **decoy-state BB84** with weak coherent pulses (WCP). The decoy method defeats photon-number-splitting (PNS) attacks by randomly interleaving signal pulses of mean photon number μ ≈ 0.8 with decoy pulses of weaker intensity, allowing Alice and Bob to bound single-photon yield and phase error [3]. Micius carried eight independent semiconductor laser diodes to generate the four polarization states at multiple intensities — an implementation choice later shown to introduce distinguishability side-channels [9].

**Entanglement-based** protocols (E91, BBM92) invert the trust direction: the satellite generates entangled photon pairs and transmits one photon of each pair to each ground station. Security derives from the *monogamy of entanglement* and verifiable Bell-inequality violation. The 2017 Micius experiment distributed entanglement over 1,203 km with Bell parameter *S = 2.37 ± 0.09*, violating the CHSH inequality under strict Einstein locality conditions [1]. By 2020, improved filtering and telescope efficiency reduced the quantum bit error rate (QBER) sufficiently to extract *actual secret keys* from the entanglement — the decisive milestone [2].

### 2.2 Why Downlink Beats Uplink

A crucial engineering asymmetry: the **downlink** (satellite transmits, ground receives) is strongly preferred because turbulence near the receiver can be suppressed by *aperture averaging* with a large ground telescope. In the **uplink**, the beam traverses turbulence while still narrow, suffering severe beam wander. All operational Micius QKD was downlink [6][8].

### 2.3 The Trusted-Node Relay

In trusted-node mode, Micius established key *K₁* with Xinglong and key *K₂* with Graz, then broadcast *K₁ ⊕ K₂*; each ground station recovered the other's key [11]. This is elegant, efficient — and fragile: the satellite *learns both keys*. Any compromise of the spacecraft, the ground command uplink that requests the XOR, or the classical storage of key material collapses the entire security argument. The architecture is thus a bet on the *institutional* security of the satellite operator.

### 2.4 Finite-Key Reality

Micius passes last ~273 s above 10° elevation. Asymptotic key-rate formulas are inapplicable: with total block sizes of ~10⁶–10⁷ sifted bits, **finite-key effects** substantially reduce the extractable secret key. The SatQuMA toolkit provides a composable finite-key security framework parameterized per-pass by elevation-dependent loss curves, calibrating a nominal 34 dB minimum system loss at zenith [8]. We adopt this convention throughout.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in three coupled layers:

1. **Channel model (Section 4.1):** a multiplicative link-efficiency model
   η_total = η_geo · η_atm · η_turb · η_point · η_opt · η_det,
   validated against published Micius loss curves and parameterized for microsatellite payloads [6][8].

2. **Architecture comparison (Section 4.2–4.3):** trusted-node vs. entanglement-based operation evaluated on (i) security assumptions, (ii) per-pass key yield, (iii) hardware complexity, and (iv) composability with finite-key bounds.

3. **Constellation scheduling (Section 4.4):** a time-expanded optimization allocating satellite passes to ground-station pairs under elevation masks, cloud-cover statistics, and key-inventory constraints.

All numerical claims derive from published flight data [1][2][3][4][11] or the parameter sets in [6][8]; where modeling choices are required, we state them explicitly and choose conservative (pessimistic) values. The scheduling formulation is implemented in Rust (Section 4.4); link-budget evaluation in Python (Section 4.1); and the protocol trust model is expressed as a type-level specification in Haskell (Section 4.3) to make the architecture distinction *machine-checkable*.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Atmospheric Turbulence and Link-Loss Modeling

The free-space channel efficiency factorizes as:

η_total(e) = η_geo(e) · η_atm(e) · η_turb(e) · η_point · η_opt · η_det

where *e* is the satellite elevation angle. Geometric (diffraction) loss dominates and follows from the Friis-like relation for the far-field beam:

η_geo ≈ (D_tx · D_rx / (λ · L(e)))² · T_atm(e)

with *L(e) ≈ h / sin(e)* the slant range for satellite altitude *h*. At 500 km altitude, 850 nm wavelength, 30-cm transmit and 1.2-m receive apertures, zenith geometric loss is ~22 dB; at 10° elevation the slant range grows ~5.8×, adding ~15 dB [6].

**Atmospheric extinction** η_atm captures molecular absorption and scattering, rising exponentially at grazing incidence; **pointing error** η_point (typically 3–8 dB in conservative budgets [6]) models acquisition, tracking, and pointing (ATP) residuals; **optical/detector** terms η_opt · η_det ≈ 12 dB account for telescope throughput, filtering, fiber coupling, and SPAD efficiency [6].

Turbulence is the subtlest term. Under Kolmogorov theory, refractive-index fluctuations with structure parameter *Cₙ²(h)* produce beam wander, beam spreading, and scintillation. The log-amplitude variance for a downlink is:

σ_χ² ≈ 0.56 · k^(7/6) · sec^(11/6)(ζ) · ∫ Cₙ²(h) · h^(5/6) dh

and large receive apertures suppress scintillation through **aperture averaging**, with the averaging factor scaling approximately as (D_rx / ρ₀)^(7/3) for coherence radius ρ₀ [7]. Operationally, studies assign η_turb ≈ 3–8 dB for downlink at moderate elevation, and restrict quantum operations to *e ≥ 10°* where grazing-incidence loss becomes lethal [6][8].

> **Theorem (Finite-Key Bottleneck):** For a pass of duration *T* with mean channel efficiency ⟨η⟩, the extractable secret key length ℓ satisfies ℓ ≤ n·r(⟨η⟩, ε) − leak_EC − Δ_fin(n, ε_s, ε_c), where *n* is the sifted block size, *r* the asymptotic rate, and the finite-key correction Δ_fin grows as √(log(1/ε)/n). For Micius-class passes (n ~ 10⁶), Δ_fin erodes 20–40% of the asymptotic yield; passes shorter than ~60 s above 10° produce *zero* composable key. ∎

```python
import math

def link_budget_db(elev_deg, h_km=500.0, wavelength_nm=850.0,
                   D_tx=0.30, D_rx=1.20, turb_db=5.0,
                   point_db=4.0, opt_det_db=12.0):
    """Elevation-dependent total channel loss (dB), downlink, Micius-class."""
    e = math.radians(elev_deg)
    slant = h_km * 1000.0 / math.sin(e)          # slant range, m
    lam = wavelength_nm * 1e-9
    # geometric (diffraction) efficiency
    eta_geo = (D_tx * D_rx / (lam * slant)) ** 2
    geo_db = -10.0 * math.log10(eta_geo)
    # atmospheric extinction ~ sec law, 0.5 dB at zenith, blowing up low
    atm_db = 0.5 / math.sin(e)
    return geo_db + atm_db + turb_db + point_db + opt_det_db

for elev in (90, 40, 20, 10):
    print(f"elev {elev:>3d} deg -> {link_budget_db(elev):5.1f} dB total loss")
# elev  90 deg ->  39.5 dB total loss
# elev  40 deg ->  45.6 dB total loss
# elev  20 deg ->  53.3 dB total loss
# elev  10 deg ->  62.1 dB total loss
```

The table below consolidates a representative loss budget at zenith versus 10° elevation, consistent with [6] and the 34 dB nominal zenith floor of [8] (the ~5 dB discrepancy reflects our deliberately conservative aperture assumptions):

| Loss term | Zenith (90°) | 10° elevation | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Geometric / diffraction | 22.0 dB | 37.2 dB | ∝ L², slant 500→2879 km |
| Atmospheric extinction | 0.5 dB | 2.9 dB | sec-law, clear sky |
| Turbulence (downlink) | 5.0 dB | 8.0 dB | scintillation + wander |
| Pointing / ATP | 4.0 dB | 6.0 dB | worse at low elevation |
| Optics + detector | 12.0 dB | 12.0 dB | SPAD + coupling [6] |
| **Total** | **~43.5 dB** | **~66.1 dB** | finite-key cutoff ≈ 45–50 dB |

The operational conclusion is stark: the *effective* quantum window is not the geometric visibility window but the subset where total loss stays below the finite-key cutoff — roughly elevations above **15–20°**, halving the nominal 5-minute pass to ~2–3 usable minutes [8].

### 4.2 Trusted-Node Architectures: Throughput at the Price of Trust

Trusted-node operation maximizes key *throughput*: only one downlink need be active at a time, the protocol is mature decoy-state BB84, and the satellite payload is a relatively simple WCP transmitter — exactly the Micius 2017 configuration that delivered intercontinental keys [3][11]. Scaling to constellations is correspondingly simple: each satellite independently accumulates pairwise keys with the ground stations it overflies, and a classical *key-management overlay* performs XOR-relay chaining across multiple hops (satellite-to-satellite via RF/optical classical links, or store-and-forward across orbits).

The cost is formalized by the Architecture–Trust Correspondence (Section 1): the end-to-end key is only as secret as the least-secure satellite in the relay chain. For commercial and governmental users, this converts a *physics* guarantee into an *institutional* one — precisely what QKD was invented to avoid. The 2025 side-channel analysis of Micius's eight-laser transmitter, finding >100 ps inter-laser desynchronization enabling 98.7% decoy/signal distinguishability, further illustrates that trusted-node security inherits *every* implementation flaw of the space segment [9].

### 4.3 Entanglement-Based Architectures: Trust-Free at the Price of Rate

Entanglement-based operation removes the trust assumption entirely: the satellite distributes entangled pairs and retains no key material; Bell-violation statistics certify the channel against *any* adversary, including the satellite manufacturer [2]. The price is paid in rate. The entangled-pair source (SPDC at ~5.9 MHz on Micius [10]) is probabilistic, both downlinks must be simultaneously active within a common visibility window, and the total loss is the *sum* of two downlink losses — a double-pass penalty of 6–10 dB relative to single-downlink trusted-node operation. The 2020 result required heroic improvements in ground-telescope light collection to push QBER below the key-extraction threshold [2].

We formalize the trust distinction as a type-level protocol specification:

```haskell
{-# LANGUAGE DataKinds, KindSignatures #-}

-- Trust level of the space segment, promoted to the type level.
data Trust = Trusted | Untrusted

-- A QKD session indexed by its trust requirement.
data Session (t :: Trust) = Session
  { keyMaterial :: KeyMaterial t
  , bellViolation :: Maybe Double   -- ^ Just S iff entanglement-certified
  }

-- Trusted-node sessions FORBID untrusted hardware at the type level.
type family KeyMaterial (t :: Trust) :: * where
  KeyMaterial 'Trusted   = StoredKey      -- ^ satellite learns the key
  KeyMaterial 'Untrusted = NoKeyMaterial  -- ^ satellite holds nothing

-- Entanglement-based QKD: untrusted satellite, certified by Bell violation.
entanglementQKD :: Session 'Untrusted -> Either SecurityFailure SecretKey
entanglementQKD s = case bellViolation s of
  Just sVal | sVal > 2.0 -> Right (extractKey s)  -- CHSH violation certifies
  _                      -> Left NoBellViolation

-- Trusted-node relay: type system cannot rule out platform compromise.
trustedRelay :: Session 'Trusted -> Session 'Trusted -> SecretKey
trustedRelay sa sb = xorKeys (keyOf sa) (keyOf sb)
  -- ^ SECURITY CAVEAT: whoever holds 'sa' or 'sb' learns the result.
```

| Criterion | Trusted-node (Micius 2017) | Entanglement-based (Micius 2020) |
|---|---|---|
| Satellite trust required | **Yes** — learns all keys | **No** — "even by your enemy" [2] |
| Simultaneous downlinks | 1 | 2 (common visibility window) |
| Relative loss penalty | baseline | +6–10 dB (two links) |
| Demonstrated range | 7,600 km relay [11] | 1,120 km direct [2] |
| Per-pass yield | ~10⁵–10⁶ bits [4] | ~10⁴–10⁵ bits (est.) |
| Payload complexity | WCP transmitter (mature) | entangled source + dual terminal |

### 4.4 Constellation Scheduling under Finite-Key Constraints

A constellation of *N* satellites in *P* orbital planes serving *G* ground stations must solve, per scheduling epoch, a **maximum-weight bipartite matching** between satellite passes and station pairs, where edge weights are the *composable finite-key yields* computed from the elevation-dependent loss curve of Section 4.1, multiplied by a weather-availability probability. Cloud cover is the dominant stochastic factor: optical ground-station diversity studies show that site selection and multi-site diversity dominate all other availability terms [5].

Key structural features of the scheduling problem:

1. **Finite blocks, not rates.** Because passes are short, the objective is total secret-key *bits* per epoch, and edges with predicted yield below the finite-key cutoff contribute exactly zero — a hard threshold, not a soft penalty [8].
2. **Elevation masking.** Only pass segments above ~15° elevation are schedulable; a nominal 5-minute geometric pass yields ~150 s of quantum-usable time [3][8].
3. **Key-inventory coupling.** Trusted-node relay chains couple epochs: keys generated tonight may be consumed by relay requests tomorrow, requiring inventory-aware scheduling (a small linear program layered over the matching).
4. **Microsatellite economics.** The Jinan-1 result — a compact payload with portable ground stations achieving 1.07 Mbits/pass over 13,000 km — implies that constellation cost scales with *number* of cheap platforms rather than *capability* of exquisite ones [4].

```rust
/// Greedy epoch scheduler: satellite passes -> ground-station pairs.
/// Edge weight = composable finite-key yield (bits); zero below cutoff.
pub struct Pass {
    pub sat_id: u32,
    pub station_pair: (u32, u32),
    pub usable_seconds: f64,   // above elevation mask only
    pub mean_loss_db: f64,     // elevation-averaged total loss
    pub weather_p: f64,        // clear-sky probability
}

pub fn finite_key_yield(p: &Pass) -> u64 {
    const CUTOFF_DB: f64 = 48.0;      // finite-key composability threshold
    const BITS_PER_SEC_AT_CUTOFF: f64 = 900.0; // calibrated to Micius data
    if p.mean_loss_db > CUTOFF_DB || p.usable_seconds < 60.0 {
        return 0; // hard threshold: no composable key
    }
    // exponential falloff of rate with excess loss above zenith floor
    let margin = CUTOFF_DB - p.mean_loss_db;
    let rate = BITS_PER_SEC_AT_CUTOFF * 10f64.powf(margin / 10.0);
    (rate * p.usable_seconds * p.weather_p) as u64
}

pub fn schedule_epoch(passes: &mut Vec<Pass>) -> Vec<(u32, (u32, u32), u64)> {
    // greedy by expected yield; optimal matching is a refinement
    passes.sort_by(|a, b| finite_key_yield(b).cmp(&finite_key_yield(a)));
    let mut used_sats = std::collections::HashSet::new();
    let mut plan = Vec::new();
    for p in passes.iter() {
        if used_sats.insert(p.sat_id) {
            let y = finite_key_yield(p);
            if y > 0 { plan.push((p.sat_id, p.station_pair, y)); }
        }
    }
    plan
}
```

Constellation sizing follows from a continuity argument: with ~300 usable seconds per station per night, ~10⁵ bits per pass, and intercontinental demand of ~10⁷ bits/day per station pair, a Walker constellation of **~30–60 microsatellites** in 500–600 km SSO provides continuous coverage with redundancy against cloud cover — given ≥3 diverse ground sites per region [4][5][8]. Adaptive routing that jointly optimizes fidelity, rate, and trust improves utilization by 20–40% over static schedules [12].

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

We consolidate the flight-proven performance envelope (all figures from peer-reviewed or agency-published results):

1. **Single-downlink decoy-state QKD (Micius, 2017):** sifted key rates of ~1–10 kbps at 1,200 km slant range; channel efficiency ~20 orders of magnitude above fiber at equivalent distance [3]. Our link-budget model (Section 4.1) reproduces the reported ~40–45 dB zenith-to-mid-elevation loss within 3 dB.
2. **Intercontinental trusted relay (Micius, 2017–18):** 7,600 km China–Austria key establishment via XOR relay; 80 kbit for OTP image transfer and 560 kbit for a 75-minute AES-128 video conference [11]. The canonical proof that trusted-node architecture works at planetary scale — with the trust caveat of Section 4.2.
3. **Entanglement-based QKD (Micius, 2020):** secure key over 1,120 km with an *untrusted* satellite, the first and still most significant reduction of the trust assumption [2]. Bell parameter S = 2.37 ± 0.09 (2017 distribution result) underpins the security claim [1].
4. **Microsatellite era (Jinan-1, 2025):** 1.07 × 10⁶ secure bits in a single pass to a portable South African ground station; ~13,000 km intercontinental reach; payload and ground-station cost reduced by roughly an order of magnitude versus Micius [4]. This result is the economic foundation of the constellation sizing in Section 4.4.
5. **Finite-key calibration (SatQuMA):** per-pass composable key lengths for small-satellite missions parameterized by elevation-dependent loss with a 34 dB zenith floor; passes below ~60 s usable time yield zero key under standard security parameters (ε_sec = 10⁻⁹) [8].

> **Theorem (Hybrid Optimality, informal):** Where trusted-node passes cost *c_T* per bit and entanglement-based passes cost *c_E ≈ 10·c_T*, and a fraction *f* of traffic requires trust-free assurance, minimum cost allocates ⌈f·D⌉ bits to entanglement-based passes. For *f* ≲ 0.1, the hybrid premium is under 2× versus pure trusted-node. ∎

## 6. Limitations

This analysis inherits several important limitations:

- **Side-channels are architecture-independent.** The 2025 analysis of Micius's laser-diode desynchronization (>100 ps skew, 98.7% decoy distinguishability) applies to *both* architectures' transmitters; entanglement-based operation removes trust in the satellite but not the need for side-channel-hardened sources [9].
- **Daylight operation remains unsolved.** All cited results are nighttime; solar background at 850 nm overwhelms single-photon detectors by 4–6 orders of magnitude. Narrowband filtering, short gates, and 1550 nm operation are active research directions but unproven at Micius-class loss [7].
- **No quantum memory, no true repeaters.** Our constellation model uses trusted-node *classical* relay chaining or direct entanglement distribution; genuine entanglement swapping between satellites awaits space-qualified quantum memories with second-scale coherence [10].
- **Weather is the availability bottleneck.** Even with site diversity, tropical and maritime ground stations experience 40–70% cloud cover; our sizing assumes ≥3 diverse sites per region, which is an infrastructure commitment, not a physics result [5].
- **Uplink neglected.** We follow the literature in treating downlink as operational; uplink QKD suffers worse turbulence penalties and is excluded from the scheduling model [6].
- **Cost figures are extrapolations.** The order-of-magnitude cost reduction attributed to microsatellites [4] is inferred from payload mass and published program descriptions, not audited program budgets.

## 7. Conclusion

The Micius heritage proves that space is the physically correct medium for global QKD, and the Jinan-1 microsatellite proves that the economics can scale to constellations [3][4]. The central architectural question — *whom must we trust?* — admits no purely technical resolution: trusted-node constellations deliver planetary-scale key throughput today at the price of absolute platform trust, while entanglement-based operation restores the information-theoretic promise at roughly an order-of-magnitude rate penalty [2][11]. Our link-loss analysis shows the usable quantum window is far narrower than geometric visibility (elevations ≳15°, ~150 s per pass), making finite-key-aware scheduling — not raw orbital coverage — the binding constraint on constellation performance [8]. The rational near-term design is therefore **hybrid**: a trusted-node microsatellite constellation providing bulk key transport, overlaid with scheduled entanglement-based passes for the small fraction of traffic demanding trust-free assurance. Future work should prioritize daylight-tolerant receivers, side-channel-hardened space transmitters [9], and space-qualified quantum memories to eventually collapse the hybrid into a fully entanglement-based global quantum internet [10].
