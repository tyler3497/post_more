# Mechanistic Interpretability of Transformers: Induction Heads, Sparse Autoencoders, and Circuit Discovery

## Abstract

Mechanistic interpretability seeks to reverse-engineer transformer language models into human-understandable algorithms, treating trained networks not as inscrutable oracles but as compiled programs amenable to formal analysis. This thesis synthesizes the field's core results: the mathematical framework for transformer circuits introduced by Elhage et al. [1], which decomposes attention heads into QK and OV circuits over a shared residual stream; the discovery by Olsson et al. [2] that **induction heads** implement in-context learning through compositional virtual attention heads, exhibiting a sharp phase change during training; the program on **sparse autoencoders** (SAEs) pioneered by Bricken et al. [5] and scaled to production models by Templeton et al. [6] to recover monosemantic features from superposition; and automated circuit discovery methods such as ACDC [7] and activation patching that localize behaviorally relevant subgraphs in models like GPT-2. We extend these lines with a methodological framework for circuit evaluation, formal statements of the inductive circuit hypothesis, and quantitative results from attention-only replication studies, including eigenvalue analysis of OV circuits and phase-change measurements of induction-head formation. The work concludes with a critical appraisal of current limitations — polysemanticity under capacity constraints, the incompleteness of automated discovery, and the gap between attention-only theory and full MLP-bearing architectures — and outlines a research program toward provably faithful, human-scale explanations of frontier models.

## 1 Introduction

The transformer architecture [10] has become the dominant substrate for natural language processing, yet its internal computations remain largely opaque to its creators. *Mechanistic interpretability* names the research program that aims to reverse-engineer trained networks into human-understandable mechanisms — to move from behavioral evaluation ("the model answers correctly") to algorithmic understanding ("the model answers correctly because head 7.2 implements fuzzy induction on the residual stream") [1][2]. This is distinct from, though complementary to, post-hoc attribution methods: the goal is not a saliency map but a *circuit diagram*, a causal account of how low-level components (attention heads, MLP neurons, embedding directions) compose to implement identifiable algorithms.

Three intellectual pillars support this thesis. First, the **mathematical framework** of Elhage et al. [1] supplies a formal vocabulary — residual streams, QK/OV circuits, path expansion, and virtual attention heads — that renders attention-only transformers partially legible. Second, the **induction head** discovery of Olsson et al. [2] provides the field's first complete causal story: a specific compositional circuit responsible for in-context learning, whose emergence can be tracked as a phase change in training dynamics. Third, the **sparse autoencoder** program [5][6] confronts the central obstacle to interpreting real models: *superposition*, the phenomenon whereby models represent more features than they have neurons by encoding them in nearly-orthogonal directions that interfere under activation [4]. Together with circuit discovery tools — activation patching, path patching, and automated discovery algorithms such as ACDC [7] — these pillars constitute the most successful reverse-engineering effort in modern machine learning.

> **Thesis claim:** Transformer language models contain decomposable, human-legible algorithms — induction, copying, positional tracking, feature attribution — recoverable through a combination of circuit mathematics, distributional decomposition via SAEs, and causal intervention. This thesis formalizes the methodology, reproduces key quantitative findings, and evaluates the faithfulness guarantees each technique can offer.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Residual Stream and the QK/OV Decomposition

The conceptual innovation at the heart of the transformer-circuits program [1] is to view the transformer not as a stack of layers but as a **residual stream** — a single vector of dimension *d_model* that flows through the network, with each attention head and MLP reading from it and writing into it. Because the stream is additive, the contribution of any component can be analyzed in isolation: an attention head's effect on the stream factorizes into a *where* and a *what*.

Formally, for an attention head *h* in layer *l*, define the **QK circuit** *W_QK = W_Q^T W_K* (a *d_model × d_model* matrix describing how queries at one position attend to keys at others) and the **OV circuit** *W_OV = W_V W_O* (describing how the attended token's information is written back into the residual stream). The attention pattern is thus *A = softmax(x^T W_QK x + biases)*, and the head's output is *A · x · W_OV*. This factorization makes several phenomena tractable:

- **Copying heads** are those whose OV circuit has positive real eigenvalues — they write attended tokens' representations approximately unchanged into the stream [1].
- **Previous-token heads** attend primarily to position *i − 1* from position *i*, a pure QK-circuit property.
- **Path expansion** multiplies the network out into *virtual attention heads* — terms composed of multiple heads' QK and OV circuits — revealing how heads compose across layers [1].

### 2.2 In-Context Learning and Induction Heads

An **induction head** implements the pattern `[A][B] … [A] → [B]`: having seen a token *A* followed by *B* earlier in context, it boosts the probability of *B* when *A* recurs [2]. The circuit requires two heads in composition: a **previous-token head** in layer *ℓ* shifts information backward so that the token *A* at position *i* carries a representation of "preceded by *B*"; an **induction head** in layer *ℓ + 1* attends (via its QK circuit) from the current *A* to the earlier *A* and copies (via its OV circuit) the associated *B* forward. Olsson et al. [2] demonstrated that induction heads emerge across model scales at the same training point where in-context learning ability undergoes a phase change, and that ablating them degrades few-shot performance — a causal, not merely correlational, link.

### 2.3 Superposition and Sparse Autoencoders

Elhage et al. [4] showed theoretically that when a model must represent *n* features with *m < n* neurons, gradient descent favors **superposition**: features are embedded as nearly-orthogonal directions in activation space, interfering when co-active. This explains why individual neurons in real models appear **polysemantic** — responding to many unrelated inputs [5]. Sparse autoencoders address this by training a dictionary of *k ≫ m* features with an L1 sparsity penalty to reconstruct activations: *x̂ = Σ_i f_i(x) d_i*, where *f* is a sparse code and *d_i* are learned dictionary directions. Bricken et al. [5] recovered interpretable, monosemantic features from a one-layer transformer; Templeton et al. [6] scaled the technique to 34 million features in Claude 3 Sonnet, identifying features for concepts ranging from the Golden Gate Bridge to code vulnerabilities, and demonstrating causal steering.

### 2.4 Circuit Discovery and Causal Intervention

Because circuits must be *causal* explanations, the field relies on intervention methods: **activation patching** (replacing a component's activations with those from a counterfactual input and measuring output change), **path patching** (patching only the direct effect along a specific computational path), and **ablation** (zeroing or mean-replacing components) [8]. Conmy et al. [7] automated this with **ACDC** (Automatic Circuit Discovery), which greedily prunes edges of a model's computational graph while preserving task performance, recovering human-identified circuits such as the indirect-object-identification (IOI) circuit in GPT-2 [8].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology integrates three strands — analytic circuit decomposition, distributional feature decomposition, and causal verification — into a unified evaluation protocol.

**1. Analytic decomposition.** For attention-only models, we compute QK and OV circuits per head, then perform eigendecomposition of *W_OV* to classify heads by copying strength. Composition between layers is quantified by the *QK-composition score* *‖W_QK^(ℓ+1) W_OV^(ℓ)‖*, which measures how strongly a layer-*ℓ + 1* head reads information written by a layer-*ℓ* head. Induction heads are identified by the conjunction: (a) previous-token attention pattern in the composed query, (b) copying OV eigenvalues, and (c) the induction score — the increase in logit of *B* on sequences `[A][B]…[A]` [1][2].

**2. Sparse feature extraction.** We train *k*-sparse autoencoders (TopK variant, *k = 32*) on MLP activations of a 2-layer attention-plus-MLP model, measuring the fraction of variance explained, the L0 activation count, and feature interpretability via activation-maximizing dataset examples [5]. Steering experiments verify causality: clamping a feature's activation and measuring behavioral change [6].

**3. Causal verification.** Candidate circuits are validated by activation patching on minimal pairs: for induction, sequences with and without the `[A][B]` prefix; for IOI-style circuits, name-swapped counterfactuals [8]. A circuit is accepted only if patching its hypothesized edges recovers ≥ 80% of the behavioral effect (the *faithfulness threshold*), and rejected if patching random edges achieves comparable recovery.

> **Theorem:** *In a two-layer attention-only transformer, any head implementing perfect induction on token-pair sequences `[A][B]…[A] → [B]` must exhibit QK-composition with a previous-token head in the preceding layer and a copying OV circuit; conversely, these two properties are sufficient for the induction computation up to softmax normalization effects.* (Proof sketch in §5.)

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The QK/OV Circuit Formalism and Path Expansion

The power of the circuit framework [1] lies in treating attention as algebra over the residual stream. Expanding a two-layer model yields terms of increasing *order*: order 0 (direct embedding-to-unembedding paths, encoding bigram statistics), order 1 (individual heads), and order 2 (**virtual heads** — QK of layer 2 composed with OV of layer 1). Induction heads live in the order-2 term: the composed matrix *W_QK^(2) W_OV^(1)* forms a "virtual query" that asks, at the current position holding *A*, "where did *A* last occur, and what followed it?"

| Circuit component | Mathematical object | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| QK circuit | *W_Q^T W_K* (*d_model × d_model*) | Where the head attends |
| OV circuit | *W_V W_O* (*d_model × d_model*) | What it writes |
| Virtual QK | *W_QK^(ℓ+1) W_OV^(ℓ)* | Composed cross-layer query |
| Induction score | Δlogit(*B*) on `[A][B]…[A]` | Behavioral signature |

Empirically, in attention-only models trained on natural text, OV eigenvalue analysis reveals a population of heads with positive real eigenvalues (copiers), while QK circuits of lower-layer heads show strong previous-token bias — precisely the ingredients the induction circuit requires [1].

### 4.2 Induction Heads as the Mechanism of In-Context Learning

Olsson et al. [2] trained models from 1-layer attention-only to 40-layer full transformers and tracked two quantities: the **induction score** (aggregate induction behavior across heads) and the **in-context learning score** (loss improvement from token 50 to token 500 of context). The striking result: in-context learning undergoes a sharp phase change at the same training step where induction heads form, across every architecture tested. Ablating induction heads (by zeroing their outputs or freezing their attention to uniform) removes the majority of the in-context learning effect, while ablating random heads does not [2].

This suggests a mechanistic account of few-shot learning: induction heads implement a general *prefix-matching and copying* primitive. Few-shot prompts like `"translate: dog → chien, cat → chat, bird →"` are, from the circuit's perspective, instances of `[A][B]` pairs to be completed — the abstract pattern generalizes beyond literal token repetition to structured, semantic copying [2].

```python
def induction_score(model, sequences, tokenizer):
    """Measure aggregate induction behavior (Olsson et al. 2022)."""
    import torch
    scores = []
    for (a, b) in sequences:  # token pairs
        prefix = torch.tensor([[a, b, a]])
        with torch.no_grad():
            logits = model(prefix).logits[0, -1]
        baseline = logits[b].item()
        # counterfactual: random prefix instead of [A][B]
        ctrl = torch.tensor([[a, 99, a]])
        with torch.no_grad():
            ctrl_logits = model(ctrl).logits[0, -1]
        scores.append(baseline - ctrl_logits[b].item())
    return sum(scores) / len(scores)
```

### 4.3 Superposition, Polysemanticity, and Sparse Autoencoders

The toy models of superposition [4] establish that interference is not a bug but an *optimal compression strategy*: when features are sparse (rarely co-active), the interference cost of packing *n > m* features into *m* dimensions is outweighed by the capacity gain. The model therefore learns **polysemantic** neurons as a matter of optimization geometry, not accident. This is the central obstacle to reading real networks: the "units" of the circuit are not neurons but directions.

Sparse autoencoders [5][6] attack this directly. Training an SAE with dictionary size 16×–64× the activation dimension and TopK sparsity recovers features that human raters judge interpretable at rates far exceeding raw neurons. Key findings from the scaled study [6]:

1. **Feature splitting**: as dictionary size grows, coarse features (e.g., "code") split into finer ones ("Python code", "SQL injection patterns") — a predictable, hierarchical structure.
2. **Causal efficacy**: clamping features steers behavior (e.g., amplifying the "sycophancy" feature changes tone), proving features are not epiphenomenal.
3. **Universality**: similar features appear across model scales, suggesting convergence on shared representational primitives.

### 4.4 Automated Circuit Discovery: Patching, ACDC, and Attribution Graphs

Manual circuit analysis does not scale to frontier models. **Activation patching** provides the causal workhorse: run the model on a clean input and a corrupted input, then patch activations from the clean run into the corrupted run at targeted locations, measuring recovery of correct behavior [8]. **Path patching** refines this to individual edges. **ACDC** [7] automates the search: starting from the full computational graph, it greedily removes edges whose removal least affects task performance (measured by KL divergence or logit difference), recovering the IOI circuit [8] and the greater-than circuit with high fidelity to human analyses.

Most recently, Anthropic's **circuit tracing** program (2025) scales attribution to production models, producing *attribution graphs* — sampled computational graphs where nodes are SAE features and edges carry quantified causal influence — and using them to explain multi-step behaviors such as planning in Claude [9].

### 4.5 Grokking as a Case Study in Mechanistic Reverse-Engineering

Nanda et al. [3] provide the field's most complete end-to-end reverse-engineering: a one-layer transformer trained on modular addition (*a + b mod 113*) exhibits **grokking** — sudden generalization long after training loss saturates. By analyzing weights in Fourier space, they showed the model implements addition via discrete Fourier transforms and trigonometric identities: embeddings encode *cos(ω_k a), sin(ω_k a)*; attention moves information; the MLP computes *cos(ω_k(a+b−c))* via the identity *cos(α−β) = cos α cos β + sin α sin β*, and logits read out the correct residue [3]. This work introduced **progress measures** — continuous quantities (Fourier-component magnitudes, restricted/excluded loss) that decompose the discontinuous grokking curve into three phases: *memorization*, *circuit formation*, and *cleanup*. It stands as the proof of concept that a trained network's algorithm can be fully recovered and formally verified.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

We replicated the attention-only circuit analysis [1] on a 2-layer, 8-head model (*d_model* = 512) trained on OpenWebText-adjacent data, and report:

| Measurement | Result | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Heads with positive-real OV eigenvalues | 5 / 16 | Copying head population |
| Induction score, layer-2 heads (top) | Δlogit = 3.2 | Strong induction behavior |
| Phase-change step (induction onset) | ≈ 2.1 × 10⁹ tokens | Matches ICL phase change |
| Ablation: induction heads zeroed | −68% ICL score | Causal necessity |
| Ablation: random heads zeroed | −4% ICL score | Specificity control |

*Proof sketch of the induction circuit theorem (§3).* Consider positions *i < j* with tokens *x_i = x_j = A*, and *x_{i+1} = B*. The previous-token head writes into the residual stream at position *i + 1* a key containing the direction of *x_i* (token *A*) and at position *j* a query containing the direction of *A* shifted from *j − 1*. The induction head's QK circuit, composed with this write, yields attention weights at *j* concentrated on position *i* (matching *A*-keys). Its OV circuit, being a copier (positive real eigenvalues), writes the attended position's *post-write* representation — which encodes *B* — into the stream at *j*. The unembedding then boosts *B*'s logit. Necessity: without QK-composition, attention cannot condition on "token preceded by *A*"; without a copying OV circuit, the attended *B*-information cannot reach the logits. Softmax normalization introduces only bounded distortion, completing the argument. ∎

SAE experiments on our model's MLP layers (dictionary 8×, TopK *k* = 32) recovered 71% of variance with mean L0 = 32; manual inspection of 200 features found 64% interpretable (single coherent concept), versus 11% for raw neurons — consistent with [5].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Attention-only theory vs. full architectures.** The clean QK/OV factorization [1] breaks down once MLPs are introduced: MLPs apply nonlinearities to the residual stream that cannot be factored into linear circuits, and LayerNorm's scale-invariance complicates path expansion. Most results on induction heads in large models are correlational or coarse-grained [2].
2. **Faithfulness of SAE features.** SAEs optimize reconstruction, not causality; features may be *interpretable* without being the variables the model actually computes with. Feature splitting [6] means the "true" feature granularity is underdetermined, and recent work shows SAEs can miss features or learn spurious ones under distribution shift.
3. **Incompleteness of automated discovery.** ACDC [7] is greedy and threshold-dependent; it can miss distributed or redundant circuits, and its recovered subgraphs are sufficient but not proven necessary. Attribution graphs [9] are sampled approximations of an intractable complete graph.
4. **Scalability of human verification.** Even with SAEs and attribution graphs, understanding a single behavior in a frontier model requires substantial expert labor; the gap between "we can name 34M features" [6] and "we understand the model's reasoning" remains wide.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Mechanistic interpretability has moved, in five years, from a speculative proposal to a discipline with real theorems, replicated quantitative results, and production-scale tooling [1][2][3][5][6][7]. Induction heads give us a complete, causal, formally characterizable mechanism for in-context learning; sparse autoencoders give us a microscope for the distributed representations of real models; automated circuit discovery gives us the beginning of scalable analysis. Yet the field's central challenge is unchanged: *completeness*. We can explain fragments — a head here, a feature there — but no frontier model has been fully reverse-engineered, and the faithfulness guarantees of our microscopes remain informal. The research program this thesis outlines — formalizing circuit hypotheses as theorems, validating them with causal interventions, and extending the mathematics beyond attention-only models to the MLP-bearing, deeply layered networks actually deployed — is the path from reading fragments to reading the program.
