## Abstract

Superoptimization asks: for a given program fragment, what is the fastest *equivalent* program? Classical answers were confined to short instruction sequences explored by exhaustive enumeration, trading completeness for a search space too small for the most interesting optimizations. This thesis develops the stochastic view — pioneered by STOKE [1], which casts superoptimization as Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling over the space of all programs with a cost function jointly encoding correctness and performance — alongside the enumerative-synthesis view of Souper [3] and the formal verification machinery of Alive [7] and Alive2 [8] that makes machine-found rewrites trustworthy. We present the Metropolis–Hastings rule on program spaces, the design of proposal distributions, the interaction between stochastic search and SMT-based equivalence checking, and empirical results: STOKE matching or beating `gcc -O3` on Hacker's Delight kernels [1], Bansal–Aiken speedups of 1.7×–10× on compute kernels [5], and Souper-derived rewrites shipped into LLVM and MSVC [3]. We close with limitations and a research agenda.

# Stochastic Superoptimization: Markov Chain Monte Carlo Program Search, Peephole Optimization Synthesis, and Formal Verification of Equivalence

## Abstract

Superoptimization asks: for a given program fragment, what is the fastest *equivalent* program? Classical answers were confined to short instruction sequences explored by exhaustive enumeration, trading completeness for a search space too small for the most interesting optimizations. This thesis develops the stochastic view — pioneered by STOKE [1], which casts superoptimization as Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling over the space of all programs with a cost function jointly encoding correctness and performance — alongside the enumerative-synthesis view of Souper [3] and the formal verification machinery of Alive [7] and Alive2 [8] that makes machine-found rewrites trustworthy. We close with limitations and a research agenda.

## 1. Introduction

A compiler transforms programs into programs, preserving meaning while improving efficiency. Traditional optimization encodes human expertise as *rules*: pattern-match, check a precondition, rewrite. But the space of equivalent programs dwarfs any rule set, and the optimal program frequently lies outside it. *Superoptimization*, introduced by Massalin in 1987 [9], asks the question directly: given a target program $T$, find an equivalent rewrite $R$ minimizing a runtime cost $\kappa(R)$.

The original superoptimizers enumerated all instruction sequences up to some length and tested each for equivalence. This is complete in the bounded sense — if an optimal program of length $\ell$ exists, it will be found — but it is also brutally limited: the number of programs grows exponentially in $\ell$, capping practical search at lengths of roughly 10–15 instructions on modern ISAs [1].

Two intellectual revolutions followed. The first replaced brute force with **solvers**: encode both the target and candidate program into the theory of bitvectors and let an SMT solver such as Z3 decide equivalence [3, 7, 8]. The second replaced exhaustive exploration with **stochastic search**: treat the program space as a probability distribution, bias it toward correctness and speed, and sample with Markov Chain Monte Carlo [1]. This thesis argues that the modern superoptimizer is properly understood as the *composition* of these two ideas: a stochastic or enumerative *proposer* that suggests candidate rewrites, coupled to a *verifier* — test-based, SMT-based, or both — that certifies equivalence.

Our contributions are expository and analytical: (1) a unified probabilistic formulation of stochastic program search — cost-function decomposition, Metropolis–Hastings acceptance on program spaces, and proposal design; (2) a taxonomy of synthesis strategies — enumerative (Souper [3]), counterexample-guided, and stochastic (STOKE [1]); (3) a verification-oriented account of refinement semantics in Alive/Alive2 [7, 8]; (4) an empirical synthesis of reported results; and (5) a critical discussion of limitations and a research agenda.

The remainder of this thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the background: superoptimization's lineage from Massalin through Bansal and Aiken [5, 6], program synthesis, and the SMT foundations. Section 3 presents the methodology: the MCMC formulation, proposal design, cost-function engineering, and the verification pipeline. Section 4 deep-dives into four topics: proposal distributions and ergodicity, performance cost models, enumerative synthesis à la Souper, and the formal semantics of refinement. Section 5 collects empirical results. Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclude.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The superoptimization lineage

The field begins with Massalin's *Superoptimizer: A Look at the Smallest Program* (1987) [9], which exhaustively generated all machine-code sequences up to a length bound and retained those satisfying a set of input/output test vectors. The approach was *complete* but confined to extremely short sequences on small ISAs.

Bansal and Aiken's *Automatic Generation of Peephole Superoptimizers* (ASPLOS 2006) [5] observed that enumeration, while intractable as an online optimizer, is acceptable *offline*: enumerate once, discover optimal rewrites, and distill them into a peephole optimizer that runs in linear time. Their system learned databases of "thousands to millions of optimizations" versus the hundreds in hand-written optimizers, reporting **1.7×–10× speedups on compute-intensive kernels** [5]. Extended to *binary translation*, superoptimization learned PowerPC→x86 translation rules automatically, reaching a median 67% of native compiler performance [6].

> **Theorem (informal completeness of bounded enumeration):** *For any fixed instruction set $I$ and length bound $L$, enumerating all sequences over $I$ of length at most $L$ and testing equivalence against a target yields the length-optimal rewrite, if one exists within the bound.* The price is a search space of size $O(|I|^L)$.

The theorem explains both the appeal and the ceiling of enumeration. Schkufza, Sharma, and Aiken shattered that ceiling in *Stochastic Superoptimization* (ASPLOS 2013) [1] by abandoning completeness: sacrifice the guarantee of finding the optimum, and in return search a space orders of magnitude larger — the space of *all* programs.

### 2.2 Program synthesis and CEGIS

Superoptimization is a special case of *program synthesis*: given a specification (here, an existing program plus a cost metric), produce a program. The dominant synthesis paradigm of the era was *counterexample-guided inductive synthesis* (CEGIS) [10], in which a synthesizer proposes candidates and a verifier refutes them with counterexamples until convergence. Souper [3] follows a CEGIS-like loop at the LLVM IR level: extract a DAG of instructions, enumerate cheaper RHS candidates, and use Z3 to check equivalence.

### 2.3 SMT solvers and the theory of bitvectors

Modern verifiers encode programs into quantifier-free bitvector logic (QF_BV): `%r = add i32 %a, %b` becomes `r = bvadd(a, b)`. Equivalence of loop-free fragments is the validity of $\forall \vec{x}.\, T(\vec{x}) = R(\vec{x})$, discharged by checking unsatisfiability of the negated form. The subtlety, as Alive demonstrated [7], is *undefined behavior*: LLVM's `nsw`/`nuw` flags, `poison`, and `undef` mean "equivalence" is properly a *refinement* relation, not equality. Alive2 [8] provides the most widely used formalization of these semantics.

| Era | System | Search strategy | Verification | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1987 | Massalin [9] | Exhaustive enumeration | I/O test vectors | Very short sequences |
| 2006 | Bansal–Aiken [5] | Exhaustive, offline | Test + manual | Peephole rules (x86) |
| 2008 | Bansal–Aiken [6] | Exhaustive, offline | Test | Binary translation rules |
| 2013 | STOKE [1] | MCMC stochastic search | Test + optional formal | x86-64 binaries |
| 2015 | Alive [7] | — (DSL, not a searcher) | SMT (Z3) refinement | LLVM peephole rules |
| 2017 | Souper [3] | Enumerative synthesis | SMT (Z3) | LLVM IR peepholes |
| 2021 | Alive2 [8] | — (validator) | SMT bounded validation | LLVM IR |
| 2023 | Minotaur [4] | Enumerative (SIMD) | Alive2 semantics | Vector IR |

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The stochastic formulation

STOKE's core move [1] is to define a *cost function* over programs that blends correctness and performance, and then to sample low-cost programs with MCMC. Given a target $T$, define for any candidate rewrite $R$:

$$c(R; T) = \alpha \cdot \text{eq}(R, T) + \beta \cdot \text{perf}(R)$$

where $\text{eq}(R,T)$ measures how far $R$ is from agreeing with $T$ on a set of test inputs (e.g., a weighted Hamming distance over registers and flags, or a strict 0/$\infty$ term), and $\text{perf}(R)$ is a performance model — either a static sum of instruction latencies or a dynamic measurement. The sampler performs a random walk: from the current rewrite $R_t$, propose $R^* \sim q(\cdot \mid R_t)$ via a mutation, and accept with the Metropolis–Hastings probability

$$\alpha(R_t \to R^*) = \min\left(1,\, \frac{\pi(R^*)}{\pi(R_t)} \cdot \frac{q(R_t \mid R^*)}{q(R^* \mid R_t)}\right), \qquad \pi(R) \propto e^{-\beta c(R; T)}.$$

In STOKE's implementation the proposal distribution is symmetric enough that the Hastings ratio simplifies to the Boltzmann form $\min(1, e^{-\beta(c(R^*)-c(R_t))})$: downhill moves are always accepted, uphill moves accepted with exponentially decaying probability, controlled by the inverse temperature $\beta$ [1].

```python
# Pseudocode of the STOKE MCMC superoptimization loop (after [1])
def stoke(target, beta=0.1, iterations=200_000):
    best = current = target            # start from the unoptimized program
    best_cost = current_cost = cost(current, target)
    for _ in range(iterations):
        proposal = mutate(current)     # opcode/operand/swap/insert/delete
        c_new = cost(proposal, target) # eq term via testcases, perf via model
        delta = c_new - current_cost
        if delta <= 0 or random() < math.exp(-beta * delta):
            current, current_cost = proposal, c_new
            if c_new < best_cost and equivalent(proposal, target):
                best, best_cost = proposal, c_new
    return best
```

The initial state is the target itself (or its compiled form), so the chain begins at a correct program and the correctness term acts as a *basin*: mutations that break equivalence incur a large cost and are usually rejected, while performance-improving mutations accumulate. This is why the method works at all — the chain never needs to discover correctness from scratch.

### 3.2 Proposal distributions over program space

A mutation is drawn from a mixture of move types [1]: **opcode** (replace a random instruction's opcode), **operand** (replace one operand), **swap** (exchange two instructions' positions), and **instruction** (delete or insert an instruction). The mixture weights are hyperparameters; gradually introducing the performance component (annealing $\beta$ over time) improves results [2]. Ergodicity requires a connected proposal graph — any program reachable from any other via mutations — which holds because opcode and instruction moves can transform any bounded-length sequence into any other.

### 3.3 The verification pipeline: from tests to SMT

STOKE validates candidates against *test cases* generated from the target — fast but unsound. The modern pipeline, inherited by Souper [3] and Minotaur [4], is: (1) **extract** a loop-free fragment; (2) **propose** candidates (MCMC mutations or enumerative RHS generation); (3) **filter** cheaply on concrete inputs; (4) **verify** with an SMT solver, encoding both sides in QF_BV with refinement semantics for UB; (5) **generalize** the concrete rewrite into a reusable rule with an inferred precondition (Alive-Infer [11]).

Step 4 is where Alive [7] and Alive2 [8] earn their place: a rewrite "equivalent" on all concrete tests can still miscompile when the source exhibits undefined behavior that the target does not. The refinement check $\text{src} \sqsubseteq \text{tgt}$ requires the target to have *no more* defined behavior than the source.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Proposal design: why naive mutations fail and what fixes them

A uniform random mutation over x86-64 is overwhelmingly likely to produce a program that disagrees with the target on every test. Three design choices make the chain productive: **starting from the target** (the chain begins in the basin of correct programs, with the $\text{eq}$ term forming a steep potential well); **locality of moves** (single-instruction mutations degrade the correctness term gracefully); and **temperature scheduling** (low $\beta$ lets the chain wander; cooling concentrates mass on correct programs — simulated annealing inside the MCMC framework [1]). Learning a better proposal — e.g., a neural proposal network trained on past successful mutations [12] — is the most promising direction for accelerating stochastic search.

### 4.2 Cost models: static latencies versus measured truth

STOKE's performance term is evaluated hundreds of thousands of times per run, so it must be cheap. The original system used a *static* model — per-opcode expected latencies measured once per microarchitecture (e.g., Haswell timings [1, 7]) — summed over the rewrite. Static models ignore port contention and $\mu$op fusion but guide search well enough in aggregate; final candidates are validated by *actual execution*. Souper instead uses *instruction count* weighted by LLVM's cost model — structural rather than microarchitectural wins [3]:

| Cost model | Evaluation cost | Fidelity | Used by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Static latency sum | Nanoseconds | Medium (per-uarch tables) | STOKE [1] |
| Instruction count | Nanoseconds | Low (structural only) | Souper [3] |
| Profile-weighted | Microseconds | Medium-high | STOKE (Cexe) [7] |
| Hardware measurement | Milliseconds | Ground truth | Final validation |

### 4.3 Enumerative synthesis: Souper's CEGIS loop

Souper [3] attacks the same problem from the opposite direction. For each integer-typed SSA value in the LLVM IR being compiled, it extracts the DAG of instructions computing it (the *left-hand side*, LHS), then enumeratively synthesizes right-hand sides (RHS) of increasing cost, asking Z3 whether any RHS refines the LHS. Key engineering: a reduced Souper IR, dominance-based candidate pruning, Redis caching of LHS→RHS results across compilations, and profitability filtering (a cheaper RHS is useless if it doesn't dominate its inputs or computes values on paths where they weren't previously needed).

Out of 15,846 unique LHSs extracted from real code, Souper found cheaper RHSs for 935 within a one-minute timeout; 40 high-value ones were manually generalized, verified in Alive, and implemented in MSVC, with additional rewrites landing in LLVM [3]. Minotaur [4] later extended the same architecture to SIMD, reusing Alive2's UB model.

### 4.4 Formal semantics: refinement, poison, and why tests are not enough

The deepest idea in the verification half of this thesis is that compiler correctness is a *refinement* relation. In LLVM IR, `%x = add nsw i32 %a, 1` produces a *poison* value on signed overflow, and poison propagates through uses. Alive's insight [7] is that a peephole rewrite is correct iff for every input, the target refines the source: wherever the source is defined, the target agrees; the target may introduce UB only where the source already had it. Alive2 [8] is the reference formalization of the exact rule.

> **Counterexample (why naive equivalence fails):** the rewrite `%r = add nsw %x, 1; use(%r)` $\Rightarrow$ `use(add %x, 1)` (dropping `nsw`) yields a wrapped value on overflow where the source yields poison — the target is *more defined* than the source. Alive rejects the unconditional rewrite and demands a precondition, which Alive-Infer synthesizes automatically [11].

### 4.5 Putting it together: the hybrid superoptimizer

The architectural thesis: the strongest practical superoptimizer combines (1) a **stochastic proposer** (MCMC over program space, à la STOKE) for breadth — it finds rewrites no enumerator would reach; (2) an **enumerative synthesizer** (à la Souper) for depth in the peephole regime; and (3) a **refinement verifier** (à la Alive2) as the single trust gate. The pipeline is *modular* in the trust sense: bugs in the proposer cost performance; only bugs in the verifier cost correctness.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Correctness argument (sketch)

We sketch why the MCMC-plus-verifier architecture is sound *provided the verifier is sound*.

> **Proposition:** *Let $\mathcal{V}$ be a sound refinement checker: $\mathcal{V}(S, T) = \text{true} \implies S \sqsubseteq T$. If the superoptimizer emits only rewrites $R$ with $\mathcal{V}(R, T) = \text{true}$, then every emitted rewrite refines the target, regardless of the proposer's behavior.*

The proposition explains the division of labor: all the cleverness of stochastic search is performance-relevant only; the trusted computing base is the encoder plus the SMT solver (and, in practice, the UB formalization — precisely what Alive2 [8] hardens).

Convergence of the underlying chain follows from standard MCMC theory: with an ergodic proposal and the Metropolis–Hastings acceptance rule, the chain's stationary distribution is $\pi(R) \propto e^{-\beta c(R;T)}$ [14]. Annealing $\beta \to \infty$ concentrates the chain on global minima of $c$ — the usual simulated-annealing guarantee, with the usual caveat that finite-time behavior is what matters.

### 5.2 Reported empirical results

| System | Benchmark | Result |
|---|---|---|
| STOKE [1] | 25 Hacker's Delight kernels, from `llvm -O0` x86-64 | Matched or beat `gcc -O3`; several beat expert hand-written assembly |
| Bansal–Aiken [5] | Compute kernels | 1.7×–10× over conventional optimizing compiler |
| Bansal–Aiken [6] | SPEC (PowerPC→x86 translation) | Median 67% of native compiler performance |
| Souper [3] | 15,846 LHSs from real code | 935 cheaper RHSs in ≤60s each; 40+ shipped to MSVC/LLVM |
| Alive [7] | LLVM InstCombine corpus | Dozens of miscompilation bugs found and fixed |
| Alive2 [8] | LLVM unit tests via bounded validation | 28 miscompilations reported, incl. 7 self-fixed |
| Alive-Infer [11] | 54 Souper patterns + Alive suite | Weaker-than-LLVM preconditions for 73 optimizations; generalized 54 Souper patterns |

The STOKE result deserves emphasis: starting from *unoptimized* (`-O0`) binaries, stochastic search alone discovered code competitive with a production optimizing compiler.

### 5.3 Worked example: absolute value

A canonical Hacker's Delight kernel [1, 10]:

```asm
# gcc -O3 output for int abs(int x):
movl  %edi, %eax
sarl  $0x1f, %eax     # mask = x >> 31 (sign extension)
xorl  %eax, %edi      # x ^ mask
subl  %eax, %edi      # (x ^ mask) - mask
movl  %edi, %eax
retq
```

STOKE's search rediscovers the branchless identity $|x| = (x \oplus \text{mask}) - \text{mask}$ and, more interestingly, finds *register-allocation variants* that eliminate the redundant final `movl` — a peephole the compiler's fixed pass order missed.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Loop-free fragments only.** Both STOKE and Souper operate on straight-line code; loops, whose performance dominates real programs, are out of scope.
2. **Heuristic cost models.** Static latency sums mislead; the chain can overfit to the model. Final measurement helps but does not feed back into search.
3. **Proposal inefficiency.** Rejection rates are high; millions of iterations are needed for modest kernels. Learned proposals [12] help but add training infrastructure.
4. **Solver fragility.** Z3 queries on large bitvector DAGs time out; Souper's one-minute budget leaves most LHSs unoptimized.
5. **UB semantics as moving target.** LLVM's `poison`/`undef` semantics have changed repeatedly; Alive2's model [8] is the best available but bounded and therefore incomplete.
6. **No compositional guarantees.** Peephole rewrites are verified in isolation; interaction with surrounding passes is validated only empirically.
7. **Engineering cost of integration.** Souper's lesson: discovering a rewrite is the easy part; profitably implementing it in a production compiler required manual generalization, Alive verification, and new dataflow analyses [3].

---

## 7. Conclusion

Superoptimization has traveled from Massalin's exhaustive enumerator [9] through Bansal and Aiken's offline rule learners [5, 6] to STOKE's Markov chains over program space [1] and Souper's solver-backed synthesizer [3] — with Alive [7] and Alive2 [8] supplying the formal conscience without which machine-found rewrites could not be trusted. The unifying abstraction is the *propose–verify* pipeline: let any search procedure, however heuristic, suggest rewrites, and let a single sound refinement checker decide what ships. This separation is what makes stochastic methods *safe*: MCMC's randomness affects only performance, never correctness.

The frontier is clear: learned proposals to cut iteration counts [12], tighter integration with bounded translation validation [8], and refinement checking for loop-bearing fragments. The deepest lesson is methodological: when the artifact is as subtle as a compiler's output, *search* and *proof* are complements, not alternatives.
