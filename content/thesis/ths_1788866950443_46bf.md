# Resistive Crossbar In-Memory Computing: Analog Matrix-Vector Multiplication, Device Non-Idealities, and Quantization-Aware Weight Mapping for DNN Accelerators

## Abstract

Resistive random-access memory (RRAM) crossbar arrays perform matrix–vector multiplication *in situ* by exploiting Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's current law, turning each crosspoint into an analog multiplier-accumulator. This thesis develops a unified treatment of analog resistive in-memory computing for deep neural network acceleration: the physics of the Ohmic multiply-accumulate primitive, the 1T1R cell and selector structures that suppress sneak-path currents, and the first-order circuit non-idealities — wire IR drop, device-to-device and cycle-to-cycle conductance variation, short-term conductance relaxation, and long-term drift — that bound achievable precision. We quantify the dominant energy and area costs at the periphery, where analog-to-digital converters can consume the majority of the power budget [1][2], and formalize quantization-aware mapping schemes — differential encoding, bit-serial inputs, im2col unfolding, and hardware-aware retraining — that recover near-software accuracy. We survey landmark architectures (ISAAC, PRIME, PipeLayer, CASCADE, NeuRRAM, Newton) [3][4], derive scaling laws for array utilization, and compare resistive compute-in-memory against SRAM in-memory computing and digital accelerators.

## 1. Introduction

The dominant cost of modern DNN inference is not arithmetic but data movement. A 16-bit multiply-accumulate (MAC) consumes on the order of 0.3 pJ in advanced CMOS, while fetching its operands from off-chip DRAM costs two to three orders of magnitude more energy. This *memory wall* motivates in-memory computing (IMC), in which the weight matrix itself becomes the computing fabric. Among emerging non-volatile memories, resistive RAM (RRAM/ReRAM) is particularly well suited: a programmable conductance at each crosspoint of a metal crossbar turns the array into a massively parallel analog dot-product engine [5][6].

The appeal is deceptive in its simplicity. Apply voltages $V_i$ to the $M$ wordlines of an $M \times N$ crossbar whose crosspoint conductances encode the matrix $G_{ij}$, and by Ohm's law $I = V\cdot G$ each crosspoint contributes $V_i G_{ij}$; by Kirchhoff's current law the currents sum along each bitline to yield $I_j = \sum_i V_i G_{ij}$ — a full matrix–vector product in a single analog step, up to peripheral and parasitic limits [5]. The gap between this idealized equation and silicon reality is bridged by an entire discipline: selector devices, wire-resistance engineering, precision-limited data converters, and noise-aware compilation.

The sections that follow cover background (2), modeling methodology (3), a deep dive into the crossbar primitive, cells, parasitics, non-idealities, and peripherals (4), empirical results and architectural comparison (5), limitations (6), and conclusions (7).

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The memory wall and the case for analog computing

Classical von Neumann architectures shuttle every operand between compute and storage. For DNN workloads — dominated by dense MVMs in fully connected layers and convolutions — this movement is the energy bottleneck. Compute-in-memory collapses the distance to zero: the weights *are* the multipliers. RRAM is favored because it is non-volatile (no leakage to retain weights), denser than SRAM compute cells (a 1T1R cell vs. 6T–8T), and capable of multi-bit storage via intermediate resistance states [5][7].

Competing eNVM devices — phase-change memory (PCM), spin-transfer-torque MRAM, and ferroelectric RAM — have all been explored for CIM [5]. RRAM's advantages are CMOS compatibility at advanced nodes (demonstrated at 22 nm), low switching voltages, and multi-level capability [5]. PCM offers mature multi-level programming but suffers stronger conductance drift; MRAM offers superb endurance but low on/off ratio, complicating analog readout.

### 2.2 The accelerator lineage

A generation of architectures has charted the design space: **ISAAC** (2016), a tiled CNN accelerator with 12 in-situ MAC units per tile, each holding eight $128 \times 128$ crossbars with shared ADCs and bit-serial DAC inputs [3]; **PRIME** (2016), with morphable subarrays reconfiguring between storage and compute inside memory banks [3]; **PipeLayer** (2017), extending these ideas to on-chip training with inter- and intra-layer pipelining that avoids deep-pipeline bubbles [3]; **CASCADE** (2019), buffering analog partial sums in RRAM cells before quantization to cut A/D conversions [1]; **NeuRRAM** (2022), a CMOS-RRAM chip demonstrating reconfigurable CIM cores with software-comparable inference accuracy [4][8]; and **Newton**, a fixed-dataflow PIM accelerator with DRAM-like command interfaces. Simulators such as DNN+NeuroSim evaluate these architectures against device models [2].

> **Theorem: Ideal crossbar MVM.** *Given an $M \times N$ crossbar with ideal linear conductors $G_{ij}$, negligible wire resistance, and virtual-ground bitline readout, the vector of bitline currents equals the matrix–vector product $I = V G$ computed in $O(1)$ analog time.*

> **Proof:** By Ohm's law, the current through crosspoint $(i,j)$ is $V_i G_{ij}$. By KCL at bitline node $j$, the total current is the algebraic sum over all $i$: $I_j = \sum_{i=1}^{M} V_i G_{ij}$, which is exactly the $j$-th component of $V G$. ∎

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis rests on a hierarchical evaluation methodology that separates *device physics*, *circuit parasitics*, and *system architecture*.

### 3.1 Device model

Each RRAM cell is modeled as a programmable conductance $G \in [G_{\min}, G_{\max}]$ with:

- **Device-to-device (D2D) variation**: programming a target conductance yields $G = G_{\text{target}} + \varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$; measured programming precision is limited more by post-programming relaxation than by the write–verify acceptance window [8].
- **Cycle-to-cycle (C2C) variation**: repeated set/reset transitions produce a distribution of conductance states.
- **Conductance relaxation**: after programming, conductance drifts rapidly (sub-second) before stabilizing; statistics are approximately Gaussian at all states, with standard deviation up to ~10% of $G_{\max}$ immediately after programming, reducible ~29% via iterative re-programming [8].
- **Nonlinear/asymmetric update**: potentiation (LTP) and depression (LTD) curves are typically nonlinear and asymmetric — a first-order concern for on-chip training, but only a programming-time concern for inference-only accelerators [9].

### 3.2 Circuit and peripheral model

ADCs are modeled with $b$-bit SAR resolution, quantization step $\Delta = V_{FS}/2^b$, and energy scaling approximately as $2^b$. Weight matrices are quantized to $q$-bit fixed point and mapped through differential encoding.

### 3.3 Evaluation harness

Following the DNN+NeuroSim convention: $128 \times 128$ crossbars, 8-bit weights and activations, 16-bit partial sums, and benchmarks including ResNet-20 on CIFAR-10 and LeNet-5 on MNIST [2]. Accuracy is reported with hardware-aware retraining (noise injection during training) enabled and disabled, isolating each non-ideality's contribution.

```python
def crossbar_mvm(V, G, r_wire=0.0, adc_bits=None, v_fullscale=1.0):
    """Idealized crossbar MVM with optional IR drop and ADC quantization.
    V: (M,) input voltages; G: (M, N) conductances (Siemens).
    """
    M, N = G.shape
    I = V @ G                      # Ohm's law + KCL (ideal)
    if r_wire > 0.0:               # first-order IR-drop correction
        for j in range(N):
            drop = 0.0
            for i in range(M):
                drop += r_wire * I[j]   # cumulative line current
                I[j] -= (V[i] - drop) * G[i, j] * r_wire / v_fullscale
    if adc_bits is not None:       # ADC quantization
        levels = 2 ** adc_bits
        I = (I / v_fullscale * levels).round() / levels * v_fullscale
    return I
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Ohmic MAC primitive and why it scales

The crossbar MVM exploits two physical laws simultaneously: multiplication emerges from the linear $I$–$V$ characteristic of each device, and accumulation emerges from current summation on a shared conductor. The asymptotic appeal is that the *compute* itself has no cycle cost scaling with $M$ or $N$: a $128 \times 128$ array executes 16,384 MACs per analog read. Peak computational density estimates for a $128 \times 128$ crossbar reach $\sim 1707\ \text{GOPS/s}\cdot\text{mm}^{-2}$ for 16-bit-equivalent operations — while a full ISAAC-style system achieves $\sim 479\ \text{GOPS/s}\cdot\text{mm}^{-2}$, the gap being almost entirely peripheral overhead [4].

But the primitive computes *nonnegative* quantities: conductances and (for voltage-encoded inputs) currents are positive. Signed weights require differential encoding: each weight $w = g_+ - g_-$ is stored across two devices, with subtraction in the analog domain (differential readout) or digitally after dual ADC conversions [6]. Differential pairs additionally reject common-mode temperature and process shifts.

The choice of input encoding interacts with device physics: multi-level analog voltages stress the linearity of the $I$–$V$ characteristic, which is why bit-serial streaming dominates practical designs.

### 4.2 The 1T1R cell, selector devices, and sneak paths

In a passive 1R crossbar, unselected cells form parallel leakage paths — *sneak currents* — that corrupt the readout of the addressed cell and waste energy. Consider reading cell $(i,j)$: alternative paths through $(i,k)$–$(l,k)$–$(l,j)$ triplets conduct parasitic current, and the number of such paths grows combinatorially with array size. Sneak currents impose both accuracy degradation and a hard ceiling on passive array dimensions.

The standard remedy is the **1T1R cell**: a series transistor gates each device, eliminating sneak paths at the cost of cell area. Selector-based alternatives (1S1R, 1D1R) use a nonlinear two-terminal device that is highly resistive below a threshold voltage, suppressing leakage at half-select bias. 1T1R dominates fabricated demonstrators (including NeuRRAM) because it guarantees isolation [8], while 1S1R promises 4F² density for inference-only macros.

For *compute* the situation differs: during MVM all rows are driven and all columns read simultaneously, so "unselected" cells vanish — every device intentionally contributes. Sneak-path analysis therefore matters most for the *programming* phase, where write–verify must address individual cells.

### 4.3 Wire parasitics: IR drop and the array-size ceiling

Real metal lines have resistance $r_w$ per segment and capacitance per unit length. IR drop makes the effective voltage at crosspoint $(i,j)$ position-dependent: cells far from the drivers see $V_i - \Delta V_{ij}$, with $\Delta V_{ij}$ growing with the cumulative current through upstream segments. The error is *data-dependent* — a function of the input vector and programmed conductances — which makes it hard to calibrate out.

A useful scaling observation: bitline current grows linearly with $M$, while wire drop grows with the product of current and distance, so the relative error scales roughly as $M^2 r_w \bar{G}$. This superlinear growth is why practical crossbars cluster at $64 \times 64$ to $256 \times 256$, with 128 the canonical compromise [1][2]. Mitigations include low-conductance (μS-range) devices that reduce bitline currents [6], dual-side driving to halve effective line length, partitioned subarrays with local readout (as in PRIME [3]), and IR-drop-aware mapping that places large-magnitude weights near the drivers. Capacitive parasitics limit analog settling time; aggressive arrays pipeline the analog read with ADC conversion to hide this latency.

### 4.4 Device non-idealities: variation, relaxation, drift, and retention

Four stochastic phenomena dominate the error budget, with different relevance for inference-only versus on-chip-training accelerators [9]:

1. **D2D variation** — filament formation is intrinsically stochastic; even write–verify leaves a residual Gaussian spread. Measured data show post-programming *relaxation* contributes more variance than the write–verify window itself: average $\sigma \approx 2.8\ \mu\text{S}$ across states (up to ~10% of $G_{\max}$), reduced to ~2 μS after three iterative programming passes [8].
2. **C2C variation** — the same cell programmed repeatedly lands on a distribution of states; endurance cycling shifts these distributions.
3. **Relaxation and drift** — short-term relaxation (sub-second) is the dominant programming-precision limiter [8]; long-term retention is excellent in engineered stacks (e.g., TaO$_x$/AlO$_x$ devices retain up to $10^8$ s at room temperature) [7].
4. **Update nonlinearity/asymmetry** — filamentary switching yields nonlinear, asymmetric LTP/LTD curves. This sharply degrades on-chip training unless compensated by techniques such as stochastic rounding or hybrid CMOS–RRAM synapses [6][9].

> **Theorem: Relaxation-limited programming precision.** *If post-programming conductance relaxation contributes variance $\sigma_r^2$ and the write–verify window contributes $\sigma_w^2$, then shrinking the write–verify window below $\sigma_w \ll \sigma_r$ yields negligible accuracy improvement while increasing programming time.*

> **Proof:** Total programmed-conductance variance is $\sigma^2 = \sigma_w^2 + \sigma_r^2$ (independent sources). Accuracy depends on $\sigma$; as $\sigma_w \to 0$, $\sigma \to \sigma_r$, so the marginal gain of tighter windows vanishes while programming latency grows with the number of verify iterations. ∎

This is exactly the empirically observed behavior in the NeuRRAM programming study: relaxation, not the acceptance range, dominates [8].

### 4.5 The peripheral bottleneck: DACs, ADCs, and partial-sum assembly

The analog core is nearly free; the periphery is not. In ISAAC, 8-bit ADCs consume **58% of system energy**, and in some scientific-computing accelerators ADCs occupy **98% of silicon area** [1]. The bitline current of a $128 \times 128$ array with multi-bit cells spans an enormous dynamic range, demanding high-resolution quantization to preserve fidelity — yet ADC energy scales exponentially with resolution.

Design responses span a spectrum:

| Strategy | Mechanism | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Bit-serial inputs (ISAAC) | 1-bit DACs, shift-and-add | $b_{in}\times$ latency |
| Sense-amplifier + integrate-and-fire (PRIME, PipeLayer) | 1-bit quantization over $2^n$ cycles | Latency for energy |
| Analog buffering (CASCADE) | Accumulate partial sums in RRAM before ADC | Analog storage noise |
| Range-restricted ADC (TRQ) | Quantize only the active signal range | Calibration complexity [2] |
| Neural approximation of peripherals (Neural-PIM) | Learn ADC/DAC transfer in the loop | Training coupling [1] |

Multi-bit weights are handled by *bit-slicing*: a $q$-bit weight is split across $q/c$ cells of $c$ bits each (commonly $c \in \{1, 2\}$), with ADC-digitized partial sums shift-added digitally [3]. Convolution mapping uses the **im2col** transform: each $K \times K \times C_{in}$ filter patch is unrolled into a column vector so that all $C_{out}$ filters form the matrix columns, converting convolution into a single large GEMM streamed through the crossbar [4]. Large layers are tiled across multiple arrays and reduced digitally, typically in per-tile eDRAM buffers [3].

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Accuracy under non-idealities

Silicon and simulation studies converge on a consistent picture: with write–verify programming, iterative relaxation mitigation, and hardware-aware training, RRAM CIM chips achieve inference accuracy *comparable to software baselines* across CNN, RNN, and Boltzmann-machine workloads [8]. The accuracy stack decomposes as: (1) **quantization** (8-bit weights/activations): < 0.5% drop on ImageNet-class models with calibration; (2) **ADC resolution**: below ~6 effective bits accuracy degrades steeply, mostly recovered by range-restricted quantization [2]; (3) **D2D variation + relaxation**: the dominant residual — networks tolerate relative programming variation $\delta/\mu$ up to ~1 before sharp degradation, and large $G_{\max}/G_{\min}$ ratios improve immunity [6][9]; (4) **IR drop**: position-dependent systematic bias, mitigated by low-$G$ devices and IR-aware mapping.

A formal error model treats the realized product as $\hat{y} = y + e_q + e_v + e_{IR}$, where $e_q$ is ADC quantization noise (uniform, variance $\Delta^2/12$ per conversion), $e_v$ is conductance-variation-induced noise (Gaussian, input-dependent), and $e_{IR}$ is systematic IR-drop bias. Hardware-aware training injects samples of $e_v$ during forward passes, empirically recovering 1–3% top-1 accuracy on variation-afflicted arrays [8].

### 5.2 System-level efficiency

| Architecture | Technology | Peak perf. | Energy efficiency | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ISAAC-CE | 32 nm | ~55 TOPS | ~380 GOPS/W | 12 IMA/tile, 8b ADCs [3] |
| PRIME | 65 nm | — | ~2100 GOPS/W (sim.) | Morphable subarrays [3] |

Two lessons stand out: reported efficiencies are simulation projections sensitive to device assumptions, and peripheral innovations (CASCADE's analog buffering, PRIME's 1-bit sensing) move the needle more than raw array scaling.

### 5.3 Comparison with SRAM CIM and digital accelerators

**Versus SRAM CIM.** SRAM compute-in-memory (8T/10T bitcells with in-situ Boolean or charge-domain MAC) offers effectively unlimited endurance, deterministic behavior, and logic-compatible design — but pays 6–10× the bitcell area of 1T1R RRAM plus standby leakage. RRAM wins on density and non-volatility (weights persist without power, enabling normally-off edge inference); SRAM wins on endurance ($>10^{16}$ vs. $10^6$–$10^{12}$ cycles), maturity, and freedom from variation/drift. The crossover favors RRAM for inference-heavy edge devices and SRAM for training or frequently-updated weights [9].

**Versus digital accelerators.** Against digital baselines (e.g., DaDianNao), ISAAC-class designs report ~14.8× throughput and ~5.5× energy improvements in simulation [3]. The advantage is conditional: the workload must be MVM-dominated with high weight reuse (amortizing programming cost), and it evaporates if ADC resolution must grow to preserve accuracy on precision-sensitive layers. Analog CIM is therefore a *specialized* accelerator — a complement to digital fabrics, as in heterogeneous tiles that keep softmax, normalization, and control flow digital [3].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Endurance–precision co-design.** Multi-level RRAM endurance ($10^6$–$10^9$ cycles) is ample for inference but marginal for on-chip training; high-resistance states that minimize IR drop often trade against endurance [7][9].
2. **The precision wall.** Analog computation cannot scale precision by adding transistors the way digital can. Past ~8-bit equivalent MVM fidelity requires bit-slicing, analog accumulation, or residual correction — whose overheads compound.
3. **Dense passive arrays.** 1S1R selectors promise 4F² density, but half-select disturb and selector variability remain unsolved at scale; 1T1R pays a permanent area tax.
4. **Calibration and test cost.** Per-chip ADC calibration, IR-drop characterization, and write–verify programming inflate manufacturing test time — an often-ignored term in reported TOPS/W.
5. **Analog partial-sum accumulation.** CASCADE-style analog buffering reduces ADC conversions but introduces analog storage noise; a noiseless compact analog accumulator remains elusive.
6. **Reliability at temperature.** Retention at elevated temperature bounds automotive/industrial deployment [7]; conductance drift is both a reliability hazard and a potential fault-injection surface.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Resistive crossbar in-memory computing turns two laws of physics — Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's current law — into a parallel matrix–vector engine of remarkable density. Its practical realization is an exercise in managing imperfection: sneak paths tamed by 1T1R cells and selectors, IR drop bounded by array sizing and low-conductance devices, variation and relaxation absorbed by iterative programming and hardware-aware training, and the ADC/DAC bottleneck — the dominant energy and area cost — attacked through bit-serial encodings, analog buffering, and range-restricted quantization. Architectures from ISAAC and PRIME through PipeLayer, CASCADE, and NeuRRAM trace a clear trajectory: the analog core was solved early; the periphery is where the field is still innovating. Against SRAM CIM, RRAM offers density and non-volatility at the price of stochasticity; against digital accelerators, order-of-magnitude efficiency on MVM-dominated inference at the price of specialization. The open frontier is precision: pushing analog MVM fidelity beyond 8-bit equivalence without surrendering the energy advantage that justifies the technology.
