# Quantum Error Mitigation: Zero-Noise Extrapolation, Probabilistic Error Cancellation, and Clifford Data Regression

## Abstract

Noisy intermediate-scale quantum devices suffer gate infidelities, decoherence, and readout errors that corrupt the expectation values on which near-term algorithms depend. Quantum error mitigation recovers accurate estimates without the qubit overhead of fault tolerance, trading additional circuit executions and classical post-processing for suppressed bias. This article develops the three most influential mitigation protocols — zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE), probabilistic error cancellation (PEC), and Clifford data regression (CDR) — within a single statistical framework of biased estimators, sampling overhead, and noise characterization. We derive Richardson extrapolation for ZNE and its digital noise-scaling implementations, the quasi-probability representation underlying PEC and its realization through sparse Pauli-Lindblad noise models, and the learning-based ansatz of CDR together with the variable-noise extension that unifies it with ZNE. Benchmark results from superconducting hardware are compared against information-theoretic lower bounds on mitigation cost, and we identify the regimes in which each method is provably optimal or necessarily infeasible.

## 1 Introduction

Fault-tolerant quantum computation promises arbitrarily accurate logical operations, but only at the price of thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit — a resource regime that remains experimentally out of reach. In the interim, the *noisy intermediate-scale quantum* (NISQ) era has produced processors with tens to hundreds of imperfect qubits. Algorithms that run on such hardware — variational eigensolvers, quantum approximate optimization, and digital quantum simulation — almost never require the full output distribution, only a single real number: the expectation value of an observable.

*Quantum error mitigation* (QEM) is the discipline of recovering accurate expectation values from noisy hardware without encoding into a quantum code. Where error *correction* detects and reverses errors, mitigation accepts the noise as given and cancels its effect through modified circuit executions, randomized sampling, or classical post-processing [6]. Every mitigation protocol faces the same iron triangle:

- **Bias** — how far the mitigated estimator's mean lies from the ideal, noiseless value;
- **Variance (sampling overhead)** — how many additional circuit executions are needed to keep statistical fluctuations under control;
- **Characterization burden** — how much must be learned about the device's noise before mitigation can begin.

The three protocols examined here occupy sharply different corners of this triangle. *Zero-noise extrapolation*, introduced together with PEC by Temme, Bravyi, and Gambetta [1], deliberately amplifies noise and extrapolates the measured trend back to the zero-noise limit; it demands almost no noise characterization but pays in model bias. *Probabilistic error cancellation* represents the inverse of the noise channel as a quasi-probability distribution over implementable operations, yielding an unbiased estimator at the price of an exponential sampling overhead that grows with circuit depth [1] and [5]. *Clifford data regression* learns a correction map from training circuits that are classically simulable, requiring no explicit noise model at all while inheriting whatever bias the chosen regression ansatz imposes [3]. A later hybrid, *variable-noise CDR*, fuses the second and third approaches and outperforms both parents in benchmark trials [4].

The remainder of this article derives each protocol from first principles and evaluates where each breaks down.

## 2 Background: Noise Models and the Mitigation Problem

### The noisy circuit as a quantum channel

An ideal computation applies a unitary circuit $U$ to an initial state and estimates $\langle O \rangle_{\mathrm{ideal}} = \mathrm{Tr}(O U \rho_0 U^\dagger)$ for some observable $O$. On real hardware, each gate is followed by a noise channel $\mathcal{E}_j$, so the executed map is the composition

$$\mathcal{E} = \mathcal{E}_m \circ \mathcal{U}_m \circ \cdots \circ \mathcal{E}_1 \circ \mathcal{U}_1,$$

and the measured expectation value $\langle O \rangle_{\mathrm{noisy}} = \mathrm{Tr}(O \, \mathcal{E}(\rho_0))$ deviates from the ideal. The dominant physical mechanisms are:

1. **Depolarizing and Pauli noise** — stochastic Pauli errors, well approximated after *Pauli twirling* (randomized conjugation by Pauli gates), which converts arbitrary coherent errors into an incoherent Pauli channel without changing the circuit's logical action [5].
2. **Amplitude damping and dephasing** — energy relaxation with timescale $T_1$ and phase decoherence with timescale $T_2$.
3. **Readout error** — classical misclassification of the final measurement, mitigated separately by confusion-matrix inversion.

A key model is the *sparse Pauli-Lindblad* model, in which noise across a two-qubit gate layer is generated by a Lindblad dissipator with local Pauli jump operators [5]:

$$\mathcal{L}(\rho) = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} \lambda_k \left( P_k \rho P_k^\dagger - \rho \right),$$

where $\mathcal{K}$ indexes weight-one and weight-two Pauli operators supported on the processor's connectivity graph, and the rates $\lambda_k \geq 0$ are learned from experiment. Because the parameter count scales linearly in qubit number, the model is efficiently learnable and invertible — the two properties PEC requires.

### The statistical formulation

All mitigation protocols estimate the same scalar $\mu = \langle O \rangle_{\mathrm{ideal}}$ from data collected at a controllable *noise strength*. Writing the noisy expectation as $E(\lambda)$ with $\lambda = 0$ the ideal machine, a mitigated estimator $\hat{\mu}$ has

$$\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\mu}) = \underbrace{(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}] - \mu)^2}_{\text{squared bias}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu})}_{\text{variance}}.$$

Unmitigated estimation has small variance but large bias; mitigation reduces bias and inflates variance. The *sampling overhead* $\eta$ is the ratio of the mitigated estimator's variance to the raw estimator's variance at fixed shot count — the factor by which the shot budget must grow to restore the original precision [6].

## 3 Methodology: A Unified Estimation Framework

Let a *protocol* be a rule mapping an ideal circuit $C$ and observable $O$ to (a) a distribution over modified circuits $\{C_j\}$, and (b) a classical post-processing function of their outcomes approximating $\langle O \rangle_{\mathrm{ideal}}$:

| Aspect | ZNE | PEC | CDR |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core idea | Measure at amplified noise, extrapolate to zero | Invert noise channel via quasi-probability sampling | Learn correction from simulable training circuits |
| Noise model needed | None (weak-noise expansion only) | Yes (accurate channel characterization) | None (learned implicitly) |
| Estimator bias | Model-dependent extrapolation bias | Unbiased if model exact | Ansatz-dependent regression bias |
| Sampling overhead | Modest (few noise points, modest constants) | $\gamma^{2m}$-type exponential in circuit size | Small (training set + one target run) |
| Classical cost | Curve fitting | Distribution sampling | Regression + classical simulation |
| Key reference | [1] [2] | [1] [5] | [3] [4] |

## 4 Deep Dive: Three Pillars of Error Mitigation

### 4.1 Zero-Noise Extrapolation: Richardson's Deferred Approach

In the weak-noise regime — where the product of per-gate error rate, circuit depth, and system size is small — the noisy expectation value admits a power-series expansion in the noise strength $\lambda$:

$$E(\lambda) = \mu + a_1 \lambda + a_2 \lambda^2 + a_3 \lambda^3 + \cdots.$$

The physical device operates at $\lambda = 1$. If we can *amplify* the noise to known scale factors, say $\{1, 3, 5\}$, and measure the corresponding expectations, then classical Richardson extrapolation combines these measurements with weights chosen to cancel the leading powers of $\lambda$:

$$\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{Rich}} = \sum_j c_j \, E(r_j), \qquad \sum_j c_j = 1, \quad \sum_j c_j r_j^k = 0 \;\; (k = 1, \dots, n)$$

cancels the first $n$ powers of the noise perturbation, leaving residual bias of order $\lambda^{n+1}$ [1].

> **Theorem:** *Richardson cancellation.* If $E(\lambda)$ is analytic near $\lambda = 0$, the $n$-th order Richardson combination has bias $O(\lambda^{n+1})$ and variance bounded by $(\sum_j |c_j|)^2 \sigma^2 / N$. The coefficients grow rapidly with $n$: higher-order cancellation trades exponentially growing variance for shrinking bias.

The appeal of ZNE is that it is *noise-agnostic*: it never requires the experimenter to know $\mathcal{E}_j$, only to be able to scale it. The catch is twofold. First, the weights $c_j$ alternate in sign and grow in magnitude, so statistical fluctuations are amplified by the $\ell_1$ norm of the coefficients. Second, the construction collapses if $E(\lambda)$ is not well approximated by a low-degree polynomial over the probed range; at large noise the expectation saturates toward the maximally mixed value and extrapolation becomes unstable.

### 4.2 Digital Noise Scaling by Unitary Folding

ZNE is only as good as its noise-scaling mechanism. The original proposal scaled noise by stretching control pulses in time — effective, but requiring low-level hardware access that most users lack. The *digital* approach of Giurgica-Tiron and collaborators replaces physical stretching with *unitary folding*: a gate $G$ is replaced by the logically equivalent sequence $G G^\dagger G$, tripling its duration and approximately tripling its accumulated noise under the assumption that noise is gate-local and time-stationary [2]. Folding can be applied globally or to a random local subset, achieving continuous scale factors between 1 and 3.

A compact Rust sketch illustrates the transformation on a gate list:

```rust
/// Fold a circuit to amplify its noise by approximately `scale`.
fn unitary_fold(circuit: &[Gate], folds: u32, fraction: f64) -> Vec<Gate> {
    let mut out = Vec::new();
    for (i, g) in circuit.iter().enumerate() {
        out.push(g.clone());
        let local = (i as f64) < fraction * (circuit.len() as f64);
        for _ in 0..folds {
            out.push(g.dagger());
            out.push(g.clone());
        }
        if local {
            out.push(g.dagger());
            out.push(g.clone());
        }
    }
    out
}
```

Folding rests on an idealization: it scales *gate-local* noise faithfully only when the noise is depolarizing-like. In practice, randomized compiling and Pauli twirling are applied first to enforce this structure, and residual deviations appear as a systematic error in the effective scale factor.

### 4.3 Probabilistic Error Cancellation and Quasi-Probability Representations

Where ZNE treats the noise as a black box, PEC opens it. Suppose the noise channel $\mathcal{E}$ following an ideal gate $\mathcal{U}$ has been characterized. The *inverse* channel $\mathcal{E}^{-1}$ — which would undo the noise if it were physically realizable — is expanded as a linear combination of implementable operations $\{\mathcal{B}_i\}$:

$$\mathcal{E}^{-1} = \sum_i \eta_i \, \mathcal{B}_i,$$

where the coefficients $\eta_i$ are real but *not* all positive: they form a *quasi-probability* distribution. Because some $\eta_i$ are negative, the inverse cannot be applied directly; instead, the experiment samples $\mathcal{B}_i$ with probability $p_i = |\eta_i| / \gamma$, where $\gamma = \sum_i |\eta_i| \geq 1$ is the *one-norm* of the quasi-probability vector, and multiplies the outcome by $\gamma \cdot \mathrm{sign}(\eta_i)$. The resulting estimator is *unbiased* — its expectation equals the ideal value exactly, provided the noise model is exact [1].

> **Theorem:** *Unbiasedness of PEC.* If $\mathcal{E}^{-1} = \sum_i \eta_i \mathcal{B}_i$ holds exactly, the PEC estimator satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{PEC}}] = \langle O \rangle_{\mathrm{ideal}}$.

Unbiasedness comes at a steep price. The sampling overhead for a circuit in which PEC is applied at $m$ locations scales as $\gamma^{2m}$: the variance is inflated by the square of the one-norm at every mitigated gate. PEC is therefore practical only for shallow circuits with low noise rates — precisely the regime where the overhead remains manageable [5].

The modern, scalable realization of PEC replaces full tomographic characterization with the sparse Pauli-Lindblad model of Section 2. After Pauli twirling, each noisy layer is a Pauli channel; the Lindblad form is inverted *analytically* simply by negating the rates, and the inverse expands into a quasi-probability distribution over Pauli corrections sampleable in linear time in the number of model coefficients [5]. In a landmark demonstration on superconducting processors, van den Berg and collaborators applied this scheme to layered circuits with tens of qubits, recovering near-ideal expectation values where unmitigated estimates were heavily biased [5]. The experiment also confirmed the theory's warning: the measured sampling overhead grows exponentially with the number of mitigated layers, and any *model mismatch* reintroduces bias that no amount of sampling can remove.

A Haskell sketch captures the core sampling loop:

```haskell
-- | One PEC-mitigated shot: sample corrections, weight by sign, rescale.
pecShot :: [(Double, Double, PauliCorr)] -> Double -> IO Double
pecShot qps gamma = do
  outcome <- runCircuitWith =<< mapM sampleCorrection qps
  let signs = product [s | (s, _, _) <- qps]
  return (gamma * signs * outcome)
  where
    sampleCorrection (s, p, corr) = do
      r <- randomRIO (0.0, 1.0)
      return $ if r < p then Just corr else Nothing
```

### 4.4 Clifford Data Regression and Variable-Noise CDR

Clifford data regression takes an entirely different route: instead of modeling the noise, it *learns* the map from noisy to ideal expectation values. Circuits composed of Clifford gates are efficiently simulable classically (the Gottesman-Knill theorem), so for such circuits both the noisy measurement $X_i^{\mathrm{noisy}}$ (from hardware) and the exact value $X_i^{\mathrm{exact}}$ (from simulation) are available. CDR constructs *training circuits* resembling the target — obtained by replacing most non-Clifford rotations with nearby Clifford gates — and fits a simple ansatz, typically linear,

$$X^{\mathrm{exact}} \approx a_1 \, X^{\mathrm{noisy}} + a_2,$$

by least squares over the training set [3]. The fitted coefficients are then applied to the noisy measurement of the actual target circuit. On a 16-qubit energy estimation task on IBM hardware this reduced the error by an order of magnitude, with comparable gains on 64-qubit noisy simulators [3].

> **Theorem:** *Exactness on Clifford circuits.* For a Clifford target circuit under gate-independent noise, the linear CDR ansatz fitted on Clifford training data recovers the exact expectation value up to shot noise.

The ansatz is also CDR's weakness: when the true noisy-to-ideal map is strongly nonlinear, a linear fit is misspecified and residual bias persists. *Variable-noise CDR* (vnCDR), introduced by Lowe and collaborators, fuses CDR with ZNE: training data are generated from near-Clifford circuits *and* at multiple noise levels obtained by folding, with regression performed jointly over circuits and noise scales [4]. For the energy of an 8-qubit Ising model, vnCDR reduced the absolute error by a factor of 33 over the unmitigated result, outperforming ZNE by a factor of 20 and plain CDR by a factor of 1.8 [4].

### 4.5 Extrapolation Models, Inference, and Bias–Variance Trade-offs

Common extrapolation models:

- **Linear:** robust, low variance, biased whenever curvature is present.
- **Polynomial (Richardson):** cancels $n$ orders exactly; variance grows with the weight $\ell_1$ norm.
- **Exponential:** $E(\lambda) = c_0 + c_1 e^{-c_2 \lambda}$ — motivated by depolarizing models where fidelity decays multiplicatively; often best at larger depths but unstable when data are nearly flat [2].
- **Adaptive Bayesian:** places priors over model families and noise scales, selecting the extrapolation by marginal likelihood; demonstrated 18x to 24x error reductions over unmitigated circuits [2].

Best practice, distilled from large-scale hardware studies, is to *compose* mitigations: readout-error mitigation and Pauli twirling first (cheap, and they remove structured error), then ZNE with a modest scale-factor range, choosing the extrapolation function by cross-validation on held-out noise points [2]. The scale factors must be *calibrated*, not assumed: folding triples the gate count but need not exactly triple the noise.

A minimal Python implementation of Richardson ZNE makes the mechanics concrete:

```python
import numpy as np

def richardson_weights(scales, order):  # weights with sum c_j r_j^k = delta_{k0}
    r = np.asarray(scales, dtype=float)
    V = np.vander(r, N=order + 1, increasing=True).T  # V[k, j] = r_j^k
    b = np.zeros(order + 1); b[0] = 1.0
    return np.linalg.solve(V, b)

def zne_estimate(scales, values, order):
    w = richardson_weights(scales, order)
    est = float(w @ np.asarray(values))
    overhead = float(np.sum(np.abs(w)) ** 2)  # variance inflation factor
    return est, overhead

# Example
print(zne_estimate([1, 3, 5], [0.80, 0.55, 0.36], order=2))
```

The printed variance-inflation factor for $\{1,3,5\}$ at second order is approximately $140$.

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Theoretical Guarantees

The three methods have been validated on superconducting hardware in complementary regimes. Digital ZNE with unitary folding and adaptive inference achieved 18x to 24x error reductions over unmitigated circuits and remained effective at larger qubit numbers than earlier studies had tested [2]. PEC with sparse Pauli-Lindblad models demonstrated near-ideal recovery of expectation values on layered circuits, with model parameters scaling linearly in qubit count [5]. CDR delivered order-of-magnitude error reduction on a 16-qubit energy estimation task on IBM hardware and on 64-qubit noisy simulators [3], while vnCDR's joint regression over circuits and noise levels beat both parents across random-circuit and Ising benchmarks [4].

Theory complements these experiments with two kinds of guarantees. *Upper bounds* show what each protocol achieves under its assumptions: Richardson ZNE attains $O(\lambda^{n+1})$ bias [1]; PEC is exactly unbiased under a perfect noise model [1]; CDR is exact on Clifford circuits under gate-independent noise [3]. *Lower bounds* show what no protocol can avoid: the sampling overhead of any unbiased mitigation scheme grows exponentially in the circuit's total fault rate [6]. These bounds delineate a *mitigation frontier*: below a certain noise-depth product, mitigation is cheap and nearly exact; beyond it, only error correction — or better hardware — can help.

A compact TLA+ specification captures the protocol-level contract shared by all three methods:

```tla
---- MODULE Mitigation ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals

CONSTANT Scales, ShotBudget

VARIABLES round, estimate, shotsUsed

Init ==
    /\ round = 0
    /\ estimate \in Real
    /\ shotsUsed = 0

Next ==
    \/ \E s \in Scales :                    \* measure at a noise scale
       /\ shotsUsed + 1 <= ShotBudget
       /\ shotsUsed' = shotsUsed + 1
       /\ UNCHANGED <<round, estimate>>
    \/ /\ round < Cardinality(Scales)       \* classical post-processing
       /\ round' = round + 1
       /\ estimate' \in Real
       /\ UNCHANGED shotsUsed
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<round, estimate, shotsUsed>>
====
```

## 6 Limitations and Fundamental Overhead Bounds

No mitigation method escapes the exponential: suppressing bias at fixed precision requires sampling overhead exponential in the product of error rate and circuit volume [6]. Concretely:

1. **ZNE breaks down outside the perturbative regime.** When the noise-depth product is large, $E(\lambda)$ saturates and ceases to be analytic; extrapolation then amplifies noise rather than canceling it, and higher-order Richardson weights explode the variance.
2. **PEC demands a faithful noise model.** The unbiasedness theorem assumes the characterized channel equals the true channel. In practice, models are learned from finite data and drift over time; non-Markovian effects, leakage, and crosstalk outside the Pauli-Lindblad ansatz all inject bias that PEC cannot detect [5].
3. **CDR inherits ansatz bias.** The linear correction is exact only when the noisy-to-ideal map is affine over the relevant circuit family. For deep, highly entangling, non-Clifford circuits the map curves, and the near-Clifford training distribution may not cover the target's neighborhood [3]. vnCDR mitigates this at the cost of additional folded executions [4].
4. **Composition is not free.** Stacking readout mitigation, twirling, ZNE, and PEC multiplies sampling overheads; the total shot budget is the product of the parts.
5. **Mitigation does not produce better states.** All three protocols improve *estimators of expectation values*, not the quantum state itself. They cannot increase state fidelity, distill entanglement, or substitute for error correction in algorithms requiring coherent deep circuits.

The practical upshot is a division of labor that has emerged on real hardware: ZNE for quick, low-characterization wins on shallow-to-moderate circuits; PEC when an accurate noise model exists and unbiasedness is worth the exponential price; CDR and its variable-noise extension when the target circuit is close to Clifford-simulable and training data are cheap. The review literature emphasizes that these are complements, not competitors, and that the optimal choice depends on the noise's structure, the circuit's depth, and the available shot budget [6].

## 7 Conclusion

Zero-noise extrapolation, probabilistic error cancellation, and Clifford data regression answer the same question three different ways: how much can be learned about an ideal quantum computation from a noisy one? ZNE answers with *analyticity* — noise is a smooth perturbation whose zero limit can be inferred. PEC answers with *inversion* — noise is a known channel whose inverse, though unphysical, can be simulated by quasi-probability sampling. CDR answers with *learning* — noise is an unknown map identifiable from classically simulable examples. Their hybrid, variable-noise CDR, shows that these answers compose: regression over circuit space and extrapolation over noise strength together outperform either alone.

Characterization is becoming cheaper through sparse Pauli-Lindblad models, inference more principled through Bayesian model selection, and the methods are being composed into standardized cloud-hardware pipelines. Yet the exponential lower bounds are not going away — mitigation extends the reach of noisy processors by constant and polynomial factors, buying time and utility while fault tolerance matures. Knowing *which* mitigation to apply, *where* its assumptions hold, and *when* its cost exceeds its benefit is as important as the protocols themselves.
