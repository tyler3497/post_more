# PAC-Bayesian Generalization Bounds: From McAllester's Theorem to Data-Dependent Priors and Flat Minima

## Abstract

PAC-Bayesian theory replaces the worst-case analysis of classical learning theory with guarantees that hold in expectation over a *posterior* distribution on hypotheses, penalized by its Kullback–Leibler divergence from a data-independent *prior*. We trace this framework from McAllester's 1999 theorem through the Seeger–Langford kl-form bound and Catoni's localized bounds to their modern role in deep learning: Dziugaite and Roy's first nonvacuous bounds for million-parameter networks, data-dependent priors via differential privacy, the PAC-Bayesian justification of flat minima and Sharpness-Aware Minimization, compression-based bounds at ImageNet scale, and disintegrated single-draw refinements. Along the way we give a self-contained proof sketch of the canonical bound, derive the Gaussian KL penalty used in practice, and report a fully reproducible synthetic experiment in which the McAllester and Catoni bounds certify test error below 24% where the true error is 15%. We close with the framework's hard limitations and open questions.

## 1 Introduction

Modern neural networks routinely contain orders of magnitude more parameters than training examples, yet stochastic gradient descent (SGD) reliably finds solutions with low test error [13]. Classical capacity measures — VC dimension, Rademacher complexity — are quantitatively *vacuous* in this regime: they certify error bounds far above 100%, saying nothing at all. The puzzle is not merely philosophical; without nonvacuous guarantees we cannot certify, compare, or reliably select models on the basis of training data alone.

PAC-Bayesian theory, inaugurated by McAllester [1], offers a different contract. Instead of bounding the risk of *every* hypothesis in a class, it bounds the *average* risk of hypotheses drawn from a posterior distribution $Q$, chosen after seeing the data, relative to a prior distribution $P$ fixed before seeing it. The price of deviating from the prior is measured by the KL divergence $\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)$, and the resulting bounds are *uniform* over all posteriors — so they can be minimized over $Q$ to yield both certificates and learning algorithms. This article:

1. states the three canonical bounds — McAllester [1, 4], Seeger [2], and Catoni [3] — with a unified proof sketch;
2. explains the KL penalty as a description length and the central role of prior design;
3. surveys the deep-learning era: nonvacuous bounds [7], data-dependent priors [8], flat minima and SAM [9], and compression bounds [10, 11];
4. presents disintegrated refinements [14] and a reproducible numerical experiment.

## 2 Background

Fix a data distribution $\mathcal{D}$ over $\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}$ and a sample $S=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^m$ drawn i.i.d. from $\mathcal{D}$. For a hypothesis $h$ in a class $\mathcal{H}$ and 0–1 loss $\ell$, write $R(h)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(h(x),y)]$ for the true risk and $\hat R_S(h)$ for the empirical risk. PAC-Bayes studies *randomized* predictors: given a posterior $Q$ over $\mathcal{H}$, the **Gibbs classifier** $G_Q$ draws a fresh $h\sim Q$ for each prediction, with risk $R(G_Q)=\mathbb{E}_{h\sim Q}[R(h)]$. Its deterministic cousin, the majority vote, satisfies $R(\mathrm{MV}_Q)\le 2R(G_Q)$ in the binary case — so bounds on the Gibbs risk transfer, up to constants, to voting classifiers [5].

> **Theorem: McAllester's PAC-Bayesian bound [1, 4].** Fix a prior $P$ over $\mathcal{H}$ before observing $S$, and $\delta\in(0,1)$. With probability at least $1-\delta$ over $S\sim\mathcal{D}^m$, *simultaneously for every* posterior $Q$,
>
> $$R(G_Q)\ \le\ \hat R_S(G_Q)\ +\ \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)\ +\ \ln\frac{2\sqrt{m}}{\delta}}{2m}}\ .$$

The bound decomposes cleanly: empirical fit plus a complexity term that grows with the posterior's divergence from the prior and shrinks with $m$. Because it holds uniformly over $Q$, the right-hand side is itself a valid model-selection criterion.

> **Theorem: Seeger–Langford kl bound [2].** Under the same conditions,
>
> $$\mathrm{kl}\big(\hat R_S(G_Q)\,\big\|\,R(G_Q)\big)\ \le\ \frac{\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)\ +\ \ln\frac{2\sqrt{m}}{\delta}}{m},$$
>
> where $\mathrm{kl}(p\|q)=p\ln\frac{p}{q}+(1-p)\ln\frac{1-p}{1-q}$ is the binary KL divergence.

This *kl-form* is the tightest of the classical family: McAllester's square-root bound follows from it via Pinsker's inequality $\mathrm{kl}(p\|q)\ge 2(p-q)^2$, and inverting $\mathrm{kl}$ numerically yields the sharpest computable certificate [5, 6].

> **Theorem: Catoni's bound [3].** For any $c>0$, with probability at least $1-\delta$, simultaneously for all $Q$,
>
> $$R(G_Q)\ \le\ \frac{c}{1-e^{-c}}\left[\hat R_S(G_Q)\ +\ \frac{\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)\ +\ \ln\frac{1}{\delta}}{m\,c}\right].$$

Catoni's monograph [3] reframes the theory through convex analysis on the space of posteriors: the Gibbs posterior $Q\propto P\cdot e^{-m c\,\hat R_S}$ *minimizes* the right-hand side, revealing PAC-Bayes as a principled derivation of tempered Bayesian inference — without requiring the prior to be correct.

| Bound | Form | Sharpness | Directly optimizable in $Q$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| McAllester [1, 4] | $\hat R + \sqrt{(\mathrm{KL}+\ln(2\sqrt m/\delta))/2m}$ | Loosest | Yes |
| Seeger–Langford [2] | $\mathrm{kl}(\hat R\|R)\le(\mathrm{KL}+\ln(2\sqrt m/\delta))/m$ | Tightest | Via inversion |
| Catoni [3] | $\frac{c}{1-e^{-c}}[\hat R + (\mathrm{KL}+\ln(1/\delta))/(mc)]$ | Tunable via $c$ | Yes (Gibbs form) |

## 3 Methodology

The canonical proof — due in this clean form to Maurer's refinement of McAllester's argument — proceeds in four steps [4, 5, 6]:

1. **Single-hypothesis concentration.** For fixed $h$, a Chernoff bound gives $\mathbb{E}_{S}[\exp(m\cdot\mathrm{kl}(\hat R_S(h)\|R(h)))]\le 2\sqrt m$, uniformly in $h$.
2. **Integration over the prior.** By Fubini, $\mathbb{E}_{S}\mathbb{E}_{h\sim P}[\exp(m\cdot\mathrm{kl}(\hat R_S(h)\|R(h)))]\le 2\sqrt m$; Markov's inequality then yields, with probability $1-\delta$, $\mathbb{E}_{h\sim P}[e^{m\cdot\mathrm{kl}}]\le 2\sqrt m/\delta$.
3. **Change of measure.** The Donsker–Varadhan identity implies $\mathbb{E}_{h\sim Q}[m\cdot\mathrm{kl}(\hat R_S(h)\|R(h))]\le \mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)+\ln\mathbb{E}_{h\sim P}[e^{m\cdot\mathrm{kl}}]$ for *every* $Q$ — this is where the KL penalty is born.
4. **Jensen.** Convexity of $\mathrm{kl}$ moves the expectation inside, giving the Seeger form; Pinsker gives McAllester's.

*Instantiating* a bound means choosing $(P,Q)$ so the right-hand side is small. The modern deep-learning recipe [7] is:

- **Prior** $P=\mathcal{N}(w_0,\sigma_P^2 I)$: isotropic Gaussian centered at a *random initialization* $w_0$ — hence data-independent and legal.
- **Posterior** $Q=\mathcal{N}(w_S,\mathrm{diag}(\sigma^2))$: centered at the SGD solution $w_S$ with learned per-weight variances.
- **Optimization**: minimize the bound itself over $(w_S,\sigma)$ by backpropagation — "PAC-Bayes with backprop" [7].

For diagonal Gaussians the penalty is closed form:

$$\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left[\ln\frac{\sigma_{P,i}^2}{\sigma_i^2}+\frac{\sigma_i^2+(w_{S,i}-w_{0,i})^2}{\sigma_{P,i}^2}-1\right],$$

so every term has an interpretation: variance shrinkage costs bits, and drifting far from initialization costs bits. The bound is therefore a *differentiable* model-selection objective — a rare property among generalization theories.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The KL Divergence Penalty as Description Length

The KL term is best read as a **description length**. For a finite hypothesis class with uniform prior, $\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)\le\ln|\mathcal{H}|$ recovers the classical Occam bound; for continuous classes, it counts the *effective bits* needed to specify the posterior relative to the prior [6]. This reading explains both the power and the fragility of PAC-Bayes: a good prior — one that already concentrates near good hypotheses — makes the bound nearly free, while a bad prior makes it vacuous. There is no free lunch; the complexity has merely been relocated from the hypothesis class into the prior [3, 5]. The entire modern research program is, in this sense, a search for priors that are simultaneously *legal* (data-independent) and *informative*.

### 4.2 Data-Dependent Priors

Strictly, $P$ may not depend on $S$ — otherwise step 2 of the proof collapses. Three workarounds dominate:

- **Countable prior families.** Fix $\{P_j\}$ before seeing data and pay $\ln(1/\pi_j)$ via a union bound for choosing $j$ afterward. Dziugaite and Roy [7] use this to tune the prior scale legally.
- **Sample splitting.** Learn $P$ on a fraction of the data and evaluate the bound on the remainder. Simple, but discards data twice.
- **Differential privacy.** Dziugaite and Roy [8] learn the prior with a differentially private algorithm: privacy implies the prior is *nearly* data-independent, and the privacy parameters enter the bound as a small surcharge. This yields the tightest known deep-learning bounds, since the prior can sit almost exactly on the SGD solution.

A subtlety worth stating plainly: bounds with data-dependent priors certify the *combined* procedure (prior-learning plus posterior-learning), not SGD in isolation [7]. They explain generalization only insofar as the prior-learning step is itself understood.

### 4.3 Flat Minima, SGD, and Sharpness-Aware Minimization

Dziugaite and Roy's landmark contribution [7] was to *operationalize flatness*. A posterior with large variances $\sigma_i^2$ around $w_S$ has small KL (it stays near the prior) but large empirical risk unless the loss is flat in every perturbed direction — so a tight PAC-Bayes bound *requires* a flat minimum, and the optimal $\sigma$ measures its width. Their procedure returned the first nonvacuous bounds for deep stochastic networks with millions of parameters on MNIST, explicitly connecting flat minima and minimum-description-length explanations of generalization [13].

Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) [9] turns this logic into an optimizer. SAM solves

$$\min_w\ \max_{\|\epsilon\|_2\le\rho}\ L_S(w+\epsilon),$$

seeking parameters whose entire $\rho$-neighborhood has low training loss. Foret et al. justify SAM with a PAC-Bayes bound in which the posterior is Gaussian around $w$: the worst-case neighborhood loss upper-bounds the posterior's expected loss, and the KL term becomes a norm penalty on $w$. SAM improves generalization across vision benchmarks and adds robustness to label noise [9]. A caveat, well aired in the literature [13]: sharpness is parametrization-dependent — rescaling can sharpen or flatten a minimum without changing the function — so the PAC-Bayes story is cleanest for the *stochastic* (posterior-averaged) predictor, where perturbations are part of the model.

### 4.4 Compression Bounds

An older idea with new teeth: if a trained network can be *compressed* to $k$ bits, a prior uniform over the $2^k$ compressed descriptions gives $\mathrm{KL}\approx k\ln 2$, independent of the original parameter count [5]. Zhou et al. [10] combined a PAC-Bayes compression bound with off-the-shelf quantization to produce the first nonvacuous guarantees for realistic architectures on ImageNet. Lotfi et al. [11] pushed this much further — training in structured random subspaces with learned quantization and a universal prior — improving the MNIST bound from 46% to 11.55% and the ImageNet bound from 96.5% to 94.1%, and using the bounds to *explain* phenomena such as the benefits of convolution and equivariance. Complementary margin-based PAC-Bayes analyses, notably Neyshabur et al.'s spectrally-normalized bounds [12], attack the same problem through the geometry of the learned weights rather than their bit-length.

### 4.5 Disintegrated PAC-Bayes: From Averages to Single Draws

Classical PAC-Bayes bounds the *expected* risk over $Q$ — but practitioners deploy a *single* sampled network. Disintegrated bounds [14] close this gap: with high probability over both $S$ and a single draw $h\sim Q$, the realized risk $R(h)$ is controlled by $\hat R_S(h)$ plus a penalty involving the *realized* log-density ratio $\ln\frac{Q(h)}{P(h)}$ rather than its expectation. The price is a slightly heavier tail (the bound holds per-draw, not uniformly), and the analysis extends naturally to Rényi divergences and unbounded losses [14]. For certification of deployed models, this is the formulation that matters — though the theory is younger and its empirical tightness less mapped than the classical averaged bounds [5].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Proof sketch (McAllester's square-root bound).** The Seeger kl-bound follows from steps 1–4 of §3. Pinsker's inequality, $\mathrm{kl}(p\|q)\ge 2(p-q)^2$, applied with $p=\hat R_S(G_Q)$, $q=R(G_Q)$, yields $2(R(G_Q)-\hat R_S(G_Q))^2\le(\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)+\ln(2\sqrt m/\delta))/m$ on the $1-\delta$ event, and the square root gives the theorem. The $\ln(2\sqrt m/\delta)$ term — rather than $\ln(1/\delta)$ — is the exact cost of the exponential-moment step; shaving it is the subject of ongoing refinements [5, 6, 14].

**Reproducible experiment.** To show the bounds are not merely asymptotic, we instantiate them on synthetic data: $d=10$, $n=2{,}000$ training points with labels from a logistic model, prior $P=\mathcal{N}(0,I)$, posterior $Q_\sigma=\mathcal{N}(\hat w,\sigma^2 I)$ around the IRLS logistic fit $\hat w$, $\delta=0.05$, and $4{,}000$ Monte-Carlo draws for the Gibbs risks. A large held-out set ($n=20{,}000$) approximates the true risk.

| $\sigma$ | MC train risk $\hat R$ | $\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)$ | McAllester bound | Catoni bound ($c{=}1$) | Held-out risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.02 | 0.1252 | 41.3 | 0.2357 | 0.2331 | 0.1471 |
| 0.05 | 0.1264 | 32.2 | 0.2259 | 0.2277 | 0.1473 |
| 0.10 | 0.1285 | 25.3 | 0.2190 | 0.2257 | 0.1489 |
| 0.25 | 0.1415 | 16.4 | 0.2188 | 0.2392 | 0.1592 |

Every row is *nonvacuous* (below 1.0) and valid: the true risk $\approx 0.15$ sits comfortably under each certificate, with the tightest McAllester bound $0.219$ at $\sigma=0.25$. The table exhibits the central tradeoff — widening the posterior lowers KL but raises empirical risk — and both bounds track it. The computation is a few lines:

```python
import numpy as np

def gaussian_kl(mu_q, sig_q, mu_p=0.0, sig_p=1.0):
    d = mu_q.shape[0]
    return 0.5 * (d * (sig_q**2 / sig_p**2 - 1 - np.log(sig_q**2 / sig_p**2))
                  + np.sum((mu_q - mu_p)**2) / sig_p**2)

def mcallester_bound(rhat, kl, m, delta=0.05):
    return rhat + np.sqrt((kl + np.log(2 * np.sqrt(m) / delta)) / (2 * m))

def catoni_bound(rhat, kl, m, c=1.0, delta=0.05):
    return c / (1 - np.exp(-c)) * (rhat + (kl + np.log(1 / delta)) / (m * c))
```

Two honest caveats: this toy has $d=10$ and $m=2{,}000$, so the KL is in the tens; in deep learning $d$ is in the millions and only the §4 techniques (flat posteriors, data-dependent priors, compression) keep the penalty sub-vacuous. And Monte-Carlo estimation of $\hat R_S(G_Q)$ introduces sampling error on top of the bound — rigorous deployments replace it with a binomial-tail upper bound.

## 6 Limitations

- **The prior is everything.** The bound is only as good as $P$; a vague prior over a huge space gives a vacuous certificate. Prior design is modeling by another name, and data-dependent priors [8] merely move the difficulty.
- **Randomized predictors.** Classical bounds certify the Gibbs average, not the network you ship; disintegrated bounds [14] help but are less mature.
- **Scale.** Even the best current bounds [11] certify ImageNet error below ~94% — nonvacuous, but far from the ~20% test error. The gap between certificate and reality remains wide.
- **Parametrization dependence.** Flatness-based readings inherit the Dinh et al. critique: sharpness can be rescaled without changing the function, so geometric stories must be stated carefully [9, 13].
- **Computational cost.** Optimizing the bound requires sampling-based gradients and KL bookkeeping throughout training — cheap for the bound, nontrivial at scale.

## 7 Conclusion

PAC-Bayes endures because it asks the right question: not "how complex is the hypothesis class?" but "how many bits does it cost to describe what the data taught you, starting from what you already believed?" From McAllester's theorem [1] through the kl refinement [2] and Catoni's Gibbs-posterior calculus [3], the framework matured into the only classical theory with nonvacuous deep-learning guarantees [7, 10, 11], a principled account of flat minima and SAM [9], and an active frontier in data-dependent [8] and disintegrated [14] formulations surveyed in [5, 6]. The open challenge is quantitative, not conceptual: closing the order-of-magnitude gap between PAC-Bayesian certificates and the test errors they bound. Whoever closes it will have built the better prior — and thereby, in the PAC-Bayesian sense, the better theory of generalization.
