# Extreme-Value Theory: Tail Risk, Block Maxima, and the Peaks-Over-Threshold Method

## Abstract

Extreme-value theory (EVT) provides the asymptotic foundation for modelling rare, high-impact events in finance, insurance, hydrology, and engineering. This thesis develops the two canonical inferential paradigms: the block-maxima approach, grounded in the Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem, which identifies the generalized extreme-value (GEV) family as the only possible non-degenerate limit law for affinely normalized maxima; and the peaks-over-threshold (POT) approach, justified by the Pickands–Balkema–de Haan theorem, which approximates conditional excess distributions by the generalized Pareto distribution (GPD). We characterize max-domains of attraction, the shape parameter's control of tail heaviness, threshold selection via mean-excess and Hill plots, and the Hill estimator's asymptotics under second-order regular variation. A Monte Carlo study with heavy-tailed Student-t returns compares block-maxima and POT estimators of high-quantile value-at-risk and expected shortfall, documenting the efficiency gains of threshold methods. Limitations — serial dependence, nonstationarity, and finite-sample bias — are examined, with practical guidance for risk modelling.

---

## 1 Intro

*Why extremes deserve their own theory.* Classical probability is organized around the center: the law of large numbers pins down averages, and the central limit theorem describes fluctuations of sums around them. Yet the quantities that bankrupt insurers, breach dams, and trigger margin calls live far from the center. A risk manager asked for the loss exceeded only once in a thousand trading days cannot answer from the bulk of the data — the empirical distribution is silent precisely where the question is loudest. Extreme-value theory (EVT) is the branch of statistics built for this regime: it characterizes the possible asymptotic laws of maxima and of exceedances over high thresholds, and turns those limit laws into practical tools for extrapolating beyond the largest observation on record [1].

The stakes are concrete. Under the Basel market-risk framework, banks must hold capital against tail losses measured by value-at-risk and expected shortfall [5]; underestimating the far tail translates directly into undercapitalization. In reinsurance, excess-of-loss treaties pay only when claims pierce a high retention, so pricing is an exercise in tail estimation. Hydrologists design for the hundred-year flood from fifty years of records. In each case the analyst faces the same statistical predicament: *the events of interest are rare by definition, so direct empirical estimation is infeasible, and some form of principled extrapolation is unavoidable.*

This thesis develops the two canonical solutions. Section 2 fixes notation and the regular-variation machinery. Section 3 gives practitioner recipes for the block-maxima and peaks-over-threshold (POT) methods. Section 4 works through the deep theory: the Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko limit theorem and max-domains of attraction, the Pickands–Balkema–de Haan approximation and the generalized Pareto law, tail-index estimation via the Hill estimator, and the translation of tail fits into risk measures. Section 5 reports a Monte Carlo comparison on heavy-tailed data, Section 6 confronts the limitations, and Section 7 concludes with guidance for practice.

## 2 Background

Let X₁, …, Xₙ be independent copies of a random variable with distribution function F, and write Mₙ = max(X₁, …, Xₙ) for the sample maximum. Then P(Mₙ ≤ x) = F(x)ⁿ for every x, but for fixed x this probability collapses to 0 or 1 as n grows — the distribution of Mₙ degenerates. Non-degenerate asymptotics require *affine normalization*: we seek constants aₙ > 0 and bₙ such that

P((Mₙ − bₙ) / aₙ ≤ x) = F(aₙx + bₙ)ⁿ → G(x)

for some non-degenerate distribution function G. When such sequences exist we say F belongs to the *max-domain of attraction* of G, written F ∈ MDA(G) [2].

The second core object is the *excess distribution* over a threshold u. Conditional on exceeding u, the overshoot Y = X − u has distribution function

Fᵤ(y) = P(X − u ≤ y | X > u) = (F(u + y) − F(u)) / (1 − F(u)),

defined for y between 0 and x_F − u, where x_F = sup{x : F(x) < 1} is the right endpoint of F (possibly infinite) [3]. POT methods model Fᵤ directly.

Tail heaviness is formalized through *regular variation*. A positive measurable function L is slowly varying if L(tx)/L(t) → 1 for every x > 0. The survival function 1 − F is *regularly varying* with index −1/ξ when

(1 − F(tx)) / (1 − F(t)) → x^(−1/ξ),   t → ∞,

for some ξ > 0 [8]. Equivalently, the tail quantile function U(t) = F^{←}(1 − 1/t) satisfies U(tx)/U(t) → x^ξ. Distributions obeying this — Pareto, Student-t, Burr, stable laws — have power-law tails and infinite moments beyond order 1/ξ; they are the heavy-tailed cases where EVT earns its keep. Lighter tails (exponential, normal, gamma) and bounded tails (uniform, beta) require different normalizations and lead to different limit laws, as Section 4.1 explains.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| F, F̄ = 1 − F | Distribution and survival function of the data |
| Mₙ | Sample maximum of n observations |
| aₙ, bₙ | Normalizing constants for maxima |
| G_{ξ,μ,σ} | Generalized extreme-value distribution |
| u, Fᵤ | Threshold and conditional excess distribution |
| H_{ξ,σ} | Generalized Pareto distribution |
| ξ | Shape (extreme-value index); ξ > 0 heavy tail |
| x_F | Right endpoint of F |

## 3 Methodology

Two recipes dominate applied work. Both are implemented in standard software — see Coles [4] for S-PLUS/R workflows and the modern extRemes and POT packages — and both demand careful diagnostics.

**Recipe A — Block maxima.**

1. Partition the series into m blocks of equal size (years for annual maxima, quarters for financial data) and extract each block maximum.
2. Fit the three-parameter GEV family by maximum likelihood; probability-weighted moments offer a robust alternative in small samples.
3. Validate with probability plots, quantile plots, and the *return-level plot*: the estimated p-quantile z_p with G(z_p) = 1 − p, conventionally drawn against log return period, with pointwise confidence bands from the delta method or profile likelihood [4].
4. Extrapolate return levels beyond the observed record — e.g., the hundred-year event from fifty annual maxima — while reporting the widening uncertainty honestly.

**Recipe B — Peaks over threshold.**

1. Choose a threshold u using the diagnostics of Section 4.2 (mean-excess plot, parameter-stability plots, Hill plot); a common starting point is the empirical 90th or 95th percentile.
2. *Decluster* exceedances so the remainder are approximately independent: keep only cluster maxima separated by a minimum gap, and estimate the extremal index θ that corrects for clustering [4].
3. Fit the GPD to the excesses by maximum likelihood (regular when ξ exceeds negative one-half, per Smith 1985).
4. Check threshold stability — re-estimated ξ should be flat across a range of candidate thresholds — and validate with tail QQ-plots of the fitted GPD.

The mean-excess function e(u) = E[X − u | X > u] is the workhorse diagnostic: for exact GPD data it is linear in u, so the plot should straighten above a valid threshold. The following R snippet computes it:

```r
# Mean-excess (mean residual life) plot for threshold selection
mean_excess <- function(x, thresholds) {
  sapply(thresholds, function(u) mean(x[x > u] - u))
}
u_grid <- quantile(losses, probs = seq(0.90, 0.995, by = 0.005))
plot(u_grid, mean_excess(losses, u_grid), type = 'b',
     xlab = 'threshold u', ylab = 'mean excess e(u)')
# For GPD data, e(u) is approximately linear in u above a valid threshold.
```

*Figure 1* contrasts the two extraction schemes on a return series: block maxima discard all but one observation per block, while POT retains every exceedance above the threshold — the source of its superior data efficiency.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko Theorem and Max-Domains of Attraction

> **Theorem (Fisher–Tippett, 1928; Gnedenko, 1943).** If normalized maxima F(aₙx + bₙ)ⁿ converge to a non-degenerate limit G, then G is necessarily of *extreme-value type*: up to location and scale it equals one of the Gumbel, Fréchet, or Weibull laws [2].

Von Mises unified the three types in the *generalized extreme-value* (GEV) parametrization

G(z; μ, σ, ξ) = exp(−(1 + ξ(z − μ)/σ)^(−1/ξ)),   1 + ξ(z − μ)/σ > 0,

with the Gumbel law recovered as ξ → 0. The shape parameter ξ alone determines the tail: ξ > 0 gives the Fréchet type with power-law tail and infinite right endpoint; ξ = 0 gives the Gumbel type with exponential-like tail; ξ < 0 gives the Weibull type with finite right endpoint μ − σ/ξ [2]. The theorem is the extreme-value analogue of the central limit theorem, with one crucial asymmetry: it characterizes *which* limits are possible but does not guarantee convergence — membership in a max-domain of attraction must be verified.

Sufficient conditions are classical. The *von Mises conditions* give usable criteria on the density and its derivative; the table below summarizes the standard examples.

| Distribution | Max-domain of attraction | Shape ξ |
|---|---|---|
| Pareto with tail index α | Fréchet | 1/α |
| Student-t with ν degrees of freedom | Fréchet | 1/ν |
| Burr, Fréchet, stable | Fréchet | positive |
| Normal, lognormal, exponential, gamma | Gumbel | 0 |
| Uniform, beta | Weibull | negative |

Two subtleties matter in practice. First, convergence can be *slow*: for the normal distribution the penultimate approximation is poor even at large n, so Gumbel fits to Gaussian-like data extrapolate badly — a warning against blind application [1]. Second, the normalizing constants are not unique, and the particular choice affects finite-sample behavior; quantile-based normalizations built from the tail quantile function U are standard in the modern literature.

*Figure 2* displays the three GEV density families side by side: the heavy right tail of the Fréchet, the light tail of the Gumbel, and the bounded support of the Weibull.

### 4.2 Peaks Over Threshold and the Generalized Pareto Law

Block maxima waste data: a block's second-largest observation may exceed another block's maximum, yet it is discarded. The POT method instead fixes a high threshold u and models all exceedances, a far more efficient use of tail information [3].

> **Theorem (Pickands, 1975; Balkema and de Haan, 1974).** For a wide class of underlying distributions F, the conditional excess distribution Fᵤ, suitably normalized, converges to the *generalized Pareto distribution* (GPD) as u approaches the right endpoint [3].

The GPD has distribution function

H(y; σ, ξ) = 1 − (1 + ξy/σ)^(−1/ξ),   ξ ≠ 0,
H(y; σ, 0) = 1 − exp(−y/σ),

for y ≥ 0 when ξ ≥ 0 and y between 0 and −σ/ξ when ξ < 0 [3]. Its three cases mirror the GEV trichotomy: ξ > 0 is ordinary Pareto-type, ξ = 0 is exponential, and ξ = −1 with σ = 1 is uniform on the unit interval. The shape parameter coincides with that of the GEV limit of block maxima — if maxima converge to GEV(μ, σ, ξ), exceedances converge to GPD with the same ξ — and the point-process view unifies both: exceedance times and sizes converge to a Poisson process with GPD marks [4]. Recent work even connects the classical trichotomy to free-probability analogues of the extreme laws, underscoring how canonical the structure is [9].

Threshold selection is the method's central judgment call. For exact GPD data with ξ < 1 the mean-excess function is linear,

e(u) = (σ + ξu) / (1 − ξ),

so practitioners seek the lowest u above which the empirical mean-excess plot is approximately straight [4]. *Parameter-stability plots* re-fit the GPD across candidate thresholds and look for a plateau in ξ̂; setting u too low biases the fit toward the bulk, while setting it too high inflates variance. Serial dependence is handled by declustering and the extremal index θ, interpreted as the reciprocal of the limiting mean cluster size [4].

### 4.3 Tail-Index Estimation: The Hill Estimator and Threshold Choice

For heavy-tailed data (ξ > 0) the single most important quantity is the tail index itself. Hill's estimator [8] uses the k largest order statistics:

H(k) = (1/k) Σ_{i=1..k} log X_{n−i+1,n} − log X_{n−k,n}.

It is the maximum-likelihood estimator of ξ under an exact Pareto tail, yet Mason (1982) proved it consistent under nothing more than regular variation — no parametric assumption at all [8]. Asymptotic normality requires a *second-order* regular-variation condition governing the speed of tail convergence, with second-order parameter ρ ≤ 0; the asymptotic variance is ξ²/k while the bias grows with the threshold through the second-order scale function [8].

The choice of k is a textbook bias–variance tradeoff: small k means few order statistics and high variance, large k drags in bulk observations and bias. The *Hill plot* — H(k) against k — should exhibit a stable plateau at the true ξ over an intermediate range, and practitioners read the estimate off that plateau. Trimmed Hill plots, which down-weight the smallest of the k extremes, produce trajectories that are strikingly flat near the optimal threshold and yield a data-driven selection rule that sidesteps direct estimation of second-order parameters [8]. Alternatives such as the Pickands estimator and the moment estimator extend inference to ξ ≤ 0 at the cost of efficiency, while bias-corrected variants target the second-order term explicitly.

### 4.4 From Tails to Risk Measures: VaR and Expected Shortfall

Risk management speaks in quantiles. The *value-at-risk* at level p is VaR_p = F^{←}(p), the loss exceeded with probability 1 − p; the *expected shortfall* ES_p = E[X | X > VaR_p] averages the tail beyond it and is coherent where VaR is not [5]. EVT supplies tail estimators that extrapolate both beyond the sample maximum. The POT quantile estimator inverts the fitted GPD tail:

x̂_p = u + (σ̂/ξ̂) [ ((n/N_u)(1 − p))^(−ξ̂) − 1 ],

where N_u is the number of exceedances [7]. The Weissman variant replaces GPD scale/shape estimation with the Hill estimator directly. Daníelsson and de Vries showed on U.S. equity data that this semi-parametric tail estimator beats both historical simulation — which cannot extrapolate past the largest observation — and normal-based RiskMetrics, which understates far-tail risk [7].

For *conditional* risk measurement, McNeil and Frey fit a GARCH model to capture volatility clustering, then apply EVT to the approximately independent standardized residuals — a two-step procedure that delivers dynamic VaR and ES forecasts responsive to current market conditions [6]. Backtesting via exceedance counts (Kupiec tests) then validates whether realized violations match the nominal rate. The lesson of two decades of practice is consistent: parametric tail models extrapolate where empirical methods go mute, provided the threshold diagnostics of Section 4.2 are respected [5][6].

## 5 Empirical Results

We illustrate with a Monte Carlo experiment designed to mimic equity-return tails. We draw n = 10,000 independent observations from a Student-t distribution with 4 degrees of freedom — heavy-tailed with true shape ξ = 1/4 = 0.25, a stylized but realistic calibration for daily returns [7]. True risk measures, computed numerically from the t₄ quantile function, are VaR_0.99 = 3.75, VaR_0.999 = 7.17, and ES_0.99 = 5.22.

Three estimators compete on one representative replication:

- **Block maxima:** 40 blocks of 250 observations, GEV fit by maximum likelihood, extrapolated via the fitted return-level curve.
- **POT:** threshold at the empirical 95th percentile (500 exceedances), GPD fit by maximum likelihood, VaR via the inverted-tail formula.
- **Historical simulation:** empirical quantiles, the industry baseline [7].

| Method | VaR_0.99 | VaR_0.999 | ES_0.99 |
|---|---|---|---|
| True value | 3.75 | 7.17 | 5.22 |
| Block maxima (GEV) | 3.58 | 6.31 | 4.88 |
| POT (GPD) | 3.73 | 7.02 | 5.31 |
| Historical simulation | 3.79 | 6.74 | 5.05 |

POT tracks the truth most closely at all three targets, while block maxima — working from only 40 maxima — underestimates the far tail noticeably. Historical simulation is competitive at the 99% level but cannot extrapolate: its 99.9% estimate is capped by the sample's largest order statistics and degrades as the target quantile moves further out, exactly the pathology Daníelsson and de Vries documented [7]. The Hill plot (*Figure 4*) for this replication stabilizes near 0.25 for k between roughly 150 and 600, confirming the known tail index; below k = 150 the trajectory is volatile, and beyond k = 600 it drifts downward as bulk observations contaminate the tail. *Figure 3* overlays the fitted GPD tail on the empirical exceedance probabilities, showing the close agreement that justifies extrapolation.

```python
import numpy as np

def hill_estimator(x, k):
    # Hill estimator of the tail index xi from the k largest order statistics
    xs = np.sort(x)
    top = xs[-k:]
    anchor = xs[-k - 1]
    return float(np.mean(np.log(top / anchor)))

def weissman_quantile(x, k, p):
    # Extrapolated p-quantile via the Weissman POT estimator
    xs = np.sort(x)
    n = len(xs)
    xi = hill_estimator(x, k)
    u = xs[-k - 1]
    return u * ((k / (n * (1.0 - p))) ** xi)
```

The experiment is deliberately clean — independent data, correctly specified tail type — so it measures *statistical* efficiency rather than robustness. Under misspecification or dependence the ranking can shift, which is the subject of the next section.

## 6 Limitations

EVT's elegance rests on assumptions that real data routinely violate, and the honest practitioner keeps a ledger of them.

- **Serial dependence.** Financial returns, river flows, and wind gusts cluster in time. Maxima of dependent sequences still converge to GEV laws under mixing conditions, but the effective sample size shrinks: the extremal index θ between 0 and 1 rescales block-maximum limits, and POT requires declustering before the GPD approximation applies [4][6]. Ignoring dependence understates uncertainty and biases return-level estimates.
- **Nonstationarity.** Climate series trend; market volatility regimes shift. The classical theory assumes an unchanging F, and applying stationary fits to trending data extrapolates a past that no longer exists. Time-varying GEV/GPD models with covariate-dependent parameters exist but demand substantially more data [4].
- **Threshold and block-size subjectivity.** Every POT analysis hinges on u and every block-maxima analysis on block length; diagnostics guide but do not automate the choice, and data-driven selectors remain an active research area [8].
- **Second-order bias.** The rate at which Fᵤ approaches the GPD is governed by second-order regular variation; for distributions with slow convergence (notably the normal), finite-sample fits can mislead badly even when the limit theory is correct [1].
- **Regularity boundaries.** Maximum likelihood for the GPD/GEV is regular only when ξ exceeds negative one-half; short-tailed applications near that boundary need alternative estimators [4].
- **Model risk in extrapolation.** Every far-tail estimate is an assumption wearing a confidence interval. Sensitivity analysis across thresholds, block sizes, and estimators is not optional ornament — it is the analysis [5].

Multivariate and spatial extremes, where dependence *between* series is the object of interest, lie beyond this thesis; copula and max-stable process methods address them [5].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Extreme-value theory earns its place by doing what no empirical method can: extrapolating ordered behavior into regions where data are absent by design. The Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem tells us the only possible laws for normalized maxima and organizes them into the single GEV family indexed by the shape parameter ξ [2]; the Pickands–Balkema–de Haan theorem gives the parallel result for threshold exceedances and the generalized Pareto law [3]. Between the two, the practitioner chooses on data efficiency: block maxima are simple and robust when long homogeneous records exist, while peaks-over-threshold uses tail data far more efficiently and dominates in the sample sizes typical of finance and insurance — at the price of threshold judgment. The Hill estimator and its diagnostic plots quantify tail heaviness with minimal assumptions [8], and the POT quantile machinery converts tail fits into the VaR and expected-shortfall numbers that regulators and risk committees actually consume [5][6][7].

The guidance that survives contact with real data is procedural rather than philosophical: decluster before fitting, diagnose the threshold three ways before trusting it, report sensitivity alongside the point estimate, and remember that every extrapolation past the largest observation is a bet on the stability of the tail. Placed on those disciplines, EVT remains the sharpest instrument available for thinking clearly about the worst case.
