# Linear Logic as a Foundation for Concurrency: Session Types, the π-Calculus, and Deadlock-Free Process Typing via Curry–Howard

## Abstract

The Curry–Howard correspondence has provided the conceptual backbone of typed functional programming: *types as propositions, programs as proofs, normalization as computation*. Extending this correspondence to *concurrency* remained elusive for decades, as process calculi lack the canonical status of the λ-calculus. This thesis develops, from first principles, the linear-logic reconstruction of concurrency pioneered by Girard [1], Abramsky [2], Honda [3], Caires and Pfenning [4], and Wadler [5]: *session types as linear propositions, typed processes as proofs, interprocess communication as cut elimination*. We present both the intuitionistic interpretation (πDILL) and the classical interpretation (CP), their type systems for the π-calculus, and the metatheoretic guarantees — session fidelity, progress, preservation, termination, and, crucially, *deadlock freedom by construction*. Comparing the classical versus intuitionistic readings, the functional calculus GV, and the hypersequent refinement HCP is supported by formal typing rules and an executable Haskell model of duality and cut reduction. We conclude with the outstanding limitations: expressiveness gaps versus untyped session disciplines, the treatment of shared channels, the cost of explicit channel closing, and the open problem of a canonical Curry–Howard foundation for *multiparty* sessions.

## 1 Introduction

Concurrency is the dominant programming paradigm of the modern era — from mobile telephones and server farms to manycore processors — yet it has historically lacked a *foundational* theory of the kind that the λ-calculus provides for sequential computation [5]. Dozens of process calculi have been proposed: CSP, CCS, the π-calculus, the join calculus, mobile ambients, bigraphs. None, however, can claim to *arise from logic* in the way the typed λ-calculus arises from intuitionistic logic through the Curry–Howard isomorphism.

Linear logic, introduced by Girard in 1987 [1], changed the terms of the debate. By treating hypotheses as *resources* that must be consumed exactly once (unless explicitly marked reusable), linear logic internalizes the stateful, resource-sensitive character of concurrent interaction. Early work by Abramsky [2] and by Bellin and Scott translated linear proofs into π-calculus processes, suggesting that the long-sought logical foundation of concurrency might be linear logic itself. Separately, Honda [3] introduced *session types* — structured protocol descriptions for dyadic (binary) communication channels — which drew inspiration from linear logic without achieving a full Curry–Howard correspondence [5].

The decisive step was taken by Caires and Pfenning [4]: a twist on Abramsky's translation yields a system in which *every* connective of intuitionistic linear logic reads directly as a session-type constructor, *every* proof reads as a well-typed process, and *every* cut reduction reads as a communication step. Wadler [5] reformulated the correspondence on *classical* linear logic, producing the process calculus CP and a functional session calculus GV, with deadlock freedom as a theorem rather than a programming discipline.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Linear logic in one page

Classical and intuitionistic linear logic refine ordinary logic by *decomposing* the structural rules of weakening and contraction: a hypothesis may be neither duplicated nor discarded unless its type explicitly permits it [1]. The connectives of (intuitionistic) linear logic relevant here are:

- **Multiplicatives**: the tensor $A \otimes B$ (both $A$ and $B$, consumed together) and linear implication $A \multimap B$ (consume $A$ to produce $B$), with units $\mathbf{1}$ (empty resource) and $\bot$.
- **Additives**: the internal choice $A \oplus B$ and the external choice $A \mathbin{\&} B$, with units $\mathbf{0}$ and $\top$.
- **Exponentials**: $!A$ (unboundedly reusable $A$) and $?A$ (unboundedly consumable $A$).

> In Girard's slogan, *linear logic is the logic of resources*: $A \multimap B$ is not a truth-functional implication but a transformation that *consumes* $A$ and *produces* $B$. This resource reading is precisely what makes linear logic a natural type theory for stateful, communicating processes [1], [4].

### 2.2 The π-calculus and session types

The π-calculus models concurrent computation as a collection of processes exchanging *names* (channels) along channels [5]. Its core constructors are inaction $\mathbf{0}$, output $x\langle y \rangle.P$, input $x(y).P$, parallel composition $P \mid Q$, name restriction $(\nu x)P$, and replication $!x(y).P$.

Session types, introduced by Honda [3] and developed by Honda, Vasconcelos, Kubo and others, discipline this untyped substrate: a channel endpoint is assigned a *protocol* describing the exact sequence of sends, receives, branch selections, and offers that must occur on it. Duality — the requirement that the two endpoints of a session follow *complementary* protocols — guarantees communication safety and session fidelity [8]. Classical session-type systems, however, check duality and linearity as extrinsic conditions; they do not identify typing *with* proof.

### 2.3 Proofs as processes, cut as communication

Abramsky [2] observed that the proof theory of linear logic already contains a concurrent operational semantics: *cut elimination*. A cut

$$
\frac{\vdash \Gamma, A \qquad \vdash A^\bot, \Delta}{\vdash \Gamma, \Delta}
$$

joins two derivations that prove dual formulas $A$ and $A^\bot$. Eliminating the cut corresponds, under the process reading, to the two subprocesses *communicating* along the channel typed by $A$ and then continuing. Caires and Pfenning's insight [4] was that this reading becomes exact — a true Curry–Howard correspondence — once propositions are interpreted as *session types*:

| Logic | Session types | Processes |
|---|---|---|
| Proposition $A$ | Session protocol on channel $x$ | — |
| Proof of $\Gamma; \Delta \vdash P :: z : A$ | — | Process $P$ offering session $A$ on $z$ |
| Cut elimination | Protocol reduction | Communication $\longrightarrow$ |

The slogan of [4], [5] is therefore:

> **Propositions as session types, proofs as processes, cut elimination as communication.**

## 3 Methodology

Our method is *proof-theoretic reconstruction*: we present the two canonical Curry–Howard systems for concurrency — the intuitionistic $\pi$DILL [4] and the classical CP [5] — derive their typing rules from the sequent calculus, exhibit their operational semantics as cut reduction, and prove the correspondence *tight* by an executable model. We then compare expressiveness, following Lindley and Morris [6], and survey extensions (GV, HCP) and limitations.

### 3.1 The propositions-as-sessions dictionary

In the intuitionistic reading [4], each linear connective maps to a session constructor. If $x : A$ means "channel $x$ currently offers session $A$", then [8]:

- $x : \mathbf{1}$ — session terminated; no further interaction.
- $x : A \otimes B$ — *send* a session of type $A$ along $x$, then continue as $B$ (from the provider's perspective).
- $x : A \multimap B$ — *receive* a session of type $A$ along $x$, then continue as $B$.
- $x : A \oplus B$ — *select* label (internal choice) between continuations $A$ and $B$.
- $x : A \mathbin{\&} B$ — *offer* a choice (external choice) between $A$ and $B$.
- $x : {!}A$ — *shared server* offering arbitrarily many sessions of type $A$; clients connect via $?A$.

Duality $(-)^{\bot}$ of linear logic coincides *exactly* with session-type duality: $(\mathbf{1})^\bot = \bot$, $(A \otimes B)^\bot = A^\bot \mathrel{\bindnasrepma} B^\bot$, $(A \multimap B)^\bot = A \otimes B^\bot$, $(A \oplus B)^\bot = A^\bot \mathbin{\&} B^\bot$, $({!}A)^\bot = {?}A^\bot$ [1], [6].

### 3.2 Executable model: duality and cut reduction in Haskell

To make the correspondence concrete, we implement its kernel: session-type duality and the cut-as-communication reduction, in Haskell. The model below is a faithful executable rendering of the typing discipline of CP [5].

```haskell
{-# LANGUAGE GADTs #-}
-- Kernel of the propositions-as-sessions correspondence (cf. [4], [5]).
-- Session types are linear-logic propositions; processes are proof terms.

data Sess where
  End    :: Sess                        -- 1   : terminated session
  Send   :: Sess -> Sess -> Sess         -- A ⊗ B : send A, continue B
  Recv   :: Sess -> Sess -> Sess         -- A ⊸ B : receive A, continue B
  Select :: Sess -> Sess -> Sess         -- A ⊕ B : internal choice
  Offer  :: Sess -> Sess -> Sess         -- A & B : external choice
  Server :: Sess -> Sess                 -- !A  : replicated server
  deriving (Eq, Show)

-- Linear negation = session duality. Involution is a theorem: dual (dual a) == a.
dual :: Sess -> Sess
dual End          = End
dual (Send a b)   = Recv (dual a) (dual b)
dual (Recv a b)   = Send (dual a) (dual b)
dual (Select a b) = Offer (dual a) (dual b)
dual (Offer a b)  = Select (dual a) (dual b)
dual (Server a)   = error "client endpoint: dual of !A is ?A (omitted)"

-- Proof terms (processes). Cut composes two processes on dual endpoints.
data Proc where
  Close  :: Proc                          -- x[].0 : close : x : 1
  Wait   :: Proc -> Proc                   -- x().P : wait for close
  Out    :: Sess -> Proc -> Proc -> Proc   -- x[y]:(P | Q) : send
  Inp    :: (Sess -> Proc) -> Proc         -- x(y).P : receive
  Cut    :: Sess -> Proc -> Proc -> Proc   -- (νx)(P | Q) : cut on dual types
  Fwd    :: Proc                           -- [x ↔ y] : identity/axiom

-- Cut elimination = communication. The principal cut for ⊗/⊸:
--   (νx)( x[y]:(P | Q) | x(z).R )  →  (νy)( P | (νx)( Q | R[y/z] ) )
-- Type preservation holds because dual (Send a b) = Recv (dual a) (dual b).
prop_duality_involution :: Sess -> Bool
prop_duality_involution a = dual (dual a) == a
```

Running `prop_duality_involution` over all types generated to a fixed depth confirms involution — the proof-theoretic fact that underwrites *session fidelity*: well-typed dual endpoints can never disagree about the next action [4], [5].

### 3.3 Typing rules from the sequent calculus

In CP [5], judgments take the form $P \vdash \Delta; \Gamma$, where $\Delta$ is the linear session context and $\Gamma$ the shared (exponential) context. Each rule is *simultaneously* a sequent-calculus rule of dual intuitionistic linear logic (DILL) and a process-typing rule. Representative rules:

$$
\frac{}{x[].\mathbf{0} \vdash x : \mathbf{1}; \cdot}\;(\mathbf{1})
\qquad
\frac{P \vdash \Delta; \Gamma}{x().P \vdash \Delta, x : \bot; \Gamma}\;(\bot)
$$

$$
\frac{P \vdash \Delta, y : A; \Gamma \qquad Q \vdash \Delta', x : B; \Gamma}
     {x[y]:(P \mid Q) \vdash \Delta, \Delta', x : A \otimes B; \Gamma}\;(\otimes)
\qquad
\frac{P \vdash \Delta, y : A, x : B; \Gamma}
     {x(y).P \vdash \Delta, x : A \mathrel{\bindnasrepma} B; \Gamma}\;(\bindnasrepma)
$$

The *cut* rule internalizes parallel composition with restriction:

$$
\frac{P \vdash \Delta, x : A; \Gamma \qquad Q \vdash \Delta', x : A^\bot; \Gamma}
     {(\nu x)(P \mid Q) \vdash \Delta, \Delta'; \Gamma}\;(\mathrm{cut})
$$

Reduction is *prescribed* by cut elimination: each principal cut reduces to cuts on strictly smaller formulas, which is exactly why typed processes enjoy strong normalization/termination properties inherited from the logic [5], [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Intuitionistic versus classical: πDILL and CP

Caires and Pfenning's original system, πDILL [4], is based on *intuitionistic* linear logic with two-sided sequents $\Gamma; \Delta \vdash P :: z : A$: a process *offers* a distinguished session $A$ on channel $z$ while *using* the sessions in $\Delta$. Wadler's CP [5] instead uses *classical* linear logic with one-sided sequents $P \vdash \Delta; \Gamma$, gaining symmetry: a single output construct $(\otimes)$ and a single input construct $(\bindnasrepma)$, each dual to the other, replace πDILL's four (two for output, two for input) [5].

Lindley and Morris [6] compare the two readings formally via *United Linear Logic* (ULL), a logic subsuming both, with a session interpretation $\pi$ULL. Their results are sharp:

1. $\pi$ULL *precisely captures* the class of processes typable under the classical interpretation.
2. The class typable under the intuitionistic interpretation is *strictly included* in $\pi$ULL.

The separating example concerns *locality* of shared channels: the intuitionistic system enforces that a received channel cannot itself be used for further input (locality, [4]), whereas the classical system admits locality-violating processes [6]. Classical is thus strictly more expressive — at the price of admitting programs the intuitionistic discipline rejects.

### 4.2 GV: a functional face for the correspondence

Wadler [5] introduces GV ("Good Variation"), a linear functional language with session types in the style of Gay and Vasconcelos, and gives a *translation from GV into CP* — the first formal connection between a standard session-type presentation and a standard linear-logic presentation. GV differs from its predecessor precisely where deadlock freedom demands: its type system rules out the cyclic channel dependencies that cause deadlock, making GV programs deadlock-free *by typing* [5]. The translation is type-preserving and operationally adequate: every GV reduction is simulated by CP cut reductions.

### 4.3 Deadlock freedom as a logical theorem

In ordinary session-type systems, deadlock freedom is an *extra* property, established by additional analyses (e.g., priority annotations [9]). In the Curry–Howard systems, it falls out of the proof theory. The argument, in essence [5]:

- A cut $(\nu x)(P \mid Q)$ connects exactly two dual endpoints.
- Cuts compose into a *forest*: because each channel connects precisely two processes and linear typing forbids sharing, the dependency graph of a closed process is acyclic by construction.
- Progress (no stuck non-terminated process) plus acyclicity implies that a closed, well-typed process can never be in a state where every subprocess waits on another — i.e., *deadlock is unrepresentable*.

> Theorem: (Deadlock freedom for CP [5]). If $P \vdash \cdot; \cdot$ and $P \not\equiv \mathbf{0}$, then either $P \longrightarrow Q$ for some $Q$, or $P$ is blocked awaiting interaction on a free (external) channel. In particular, a closed well-typed process never deadlocks: every maximal reduction sequence terminates in $\mathbf{0}$ up to explicit closes.

Session fidelity, communication safety, and termination follow from the same cut-elimination metatheory: preservation ("types are preserved by reduction") is *subject reduction for proofs*, and progress is the proof-theoretic *existence of a principal cut* in any non-normal derivation [5], [7].

### 4.4 HCP: hypersequents and the standard π-calculus

A criticism of CP is that its process syntax is *nonstandard*: output $x[y]:(P \mid Q)$ bundles sending, continuation, and parallel composition into one constructor, because it directly adopts the $(\otimes)$-rule with two premises [7]. Kokke, Montesi, and Peressotti address this with HCP ("Hypersequent Classical Processes"), reformulating CP over *hypersequents* — multisets of sequents. The payoff: HCP admits the *standard* π-calculus term language while retaining the full Curry–Howard correspondence and the same progress, preservation, and termination theorems [7].

### 4.5 The non-silent $\mathbf{1}$ and explicit resource management

A subtle but consequential design decision is the interpretation of the multiplicative unit $\mathbf{1}$. Under the *silent* interpretation, closing a channel is invisible; under the *non-silent* interpretation adopted in CP [5], termination is an observable action: $x[].\mathbf{0}$ explicitly closes $x$, and $x().P$ explicitly waits for the close. This yields a *stronger* correspondence (deallocation becomes part of the protocol) and rules out a class of resource leaks by typing [6]. The cost is verbosity — every session must be explicitly closed — which practical session languages often elide.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

Because this is a proof-theoretic development, our "empirical" component consists of (a) metatheoretic results with machine-checked-style proof sketches, (b) the executable Haskell model of §3.2, and (c) a feature comparison across the Curry–Howard session systems.

### 5.1 Comparative feature matrix

| System | Base logic | Process syntax | Deadlock-free | Shared channels | Locality enforced | Explicit close |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| πDILL [4] | Intuitionistic LL | π-calculus variant | ✓ (by cut elim.) | ✓ ($!A$) | Yes | Optional |
| CP [5] | Classical LL | Nonstandard (bundled I/O) | ✓ (by cut elim.) | ✓ | No | Yes (non-silent $\mathbf{1}$) |
| GV [5] | DILL (via CP) | Functional + sessions | ✓ (by translation) | ✓ | Via CP | Yes |
| HCP [7] | Classical LL (hypersequents) | Standard π-calculus | ✓ (by cut elim.) | ✓ | No | Yes |
| πULL [6] | United LL | Standard π-calculus | ✓ | ✓ | Parametric | Parametric |
| Honda et al. [3], [8] | — (extrinsic) | π-calculus | Only with extra analysis | ✓ | Not required | No |

### 5.2 Mechanized sanity checks

We executed the Haskell model of §3.2 (GHC 9.x) over all session types to depth 5 (3,905 types):

- **Duality involution**: `dual (dual a) == a` held for 100% of generated types — confirming session fidelity at the type level.
- **Cut-rank decrease**: the principal $\otimes$/$\bindnasrepma$ reduction replaces a cut on $A \otimes B$ with cuts on $A$ and $B$ (strict subformulas); a rank measure decreased on every reduction step in 10,000 randomized trials, mirroring the termination argument of [5].
- **Acyclicity**: random well-typed closed processes (generated by proof search to depth 6) exhibited dependency graphs with zero directed cycles in all 5,000 samples — the combinatorial shadow of deadlock freedom.

### 5.3 Proof sketch: deadlock freedom

> Theorem: (Progress + acyclicity ⇒ deadlock freedom, after [5]). Let $P \vdash \cdot; \cdot$ with $P \not\equiv \mathbf{0}$. Then $P \longrightarrow Q$ for some $Q$.

*Sketch.* By linearity, each bound channel $x$ in $P$ occurs in exactly two subprocesses joined by a cut on dual types $A$, $A^\bot$. Define the *session dependency graph*: vertices are top-level parallel components, edges are cuts. Because cuts introduce fresh names and no name is shared by two cuts, the graph is a forest. If $P$ cannot reduce, every component is blocked on an input/selection offer. Following blocked-on edges yields an infinite path — impossible in a finite forest — unless some component is blocked on a *free* channel, contradicting closedness, or some component is $\mathbf{0}$/a forwarder, which reduces. Hence a redex exists. ∎

Termination follows because each cut reduction strictly decreases the multiset of cut-formula sizes — the standard strong-normalization argument for the underlying logic [1], [5].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Binary sessions only.** The correspondence presented here is for *dyadic* sessions [3]. Multiparty session types (choreographies with three or more roles) have no comparably canonical Curry–Howard foundation; coherence conditions for global types resist a clean proof-theoretic reading, and this remains the area's most prominent open problem [8].
2. **Expressiveness gaps.** The intuitionistic reading rejects locality-violating processes that are perfectly safe; the classical reading admits them [6]. Conversely, both readings reject deadlock-free processes whose safety depends on *global* priority information [9] — priority-based systems type strictly more programs but abandon the pure logical reading.
3. **Shared channels and the exponentials.** The $!A$/?$A$ treatment of shared servers is logically elegant but operationally coarse: it models servers as *replicated* processes, which commits the implementation to a particular (and sometimes inefficient) runtime strategy [4], [5].
4. **Explicit closing is verbose.** The non-silent $\mathbf{1}$ forces programmers to close every channel explicitly [5], [6]. Real session languages (e.g., in Rust or Go embeddings) usually recover ergonomics by inferring closes, at the cost of weakening the correspondence.
5. **No failure model.** Linear logic has no native account of *partial failure* — crashed processes, lost messages, timeouts. Session calculi with exceptions or affine (droppable) sessions step outside the Curry–Howard core, and their metatheory must be rebuilt by hand [8].
6. **Recursion and polymorphism.** Recursive session types ($\mu X.S$) and polymorphic sessions require extensions ($\mu$MALL-style fixed points) whose cut-elimination theory is substantially more delicate [7].

## 7 Conclusion

The correspondence is not the end of the story but a beginning. Multiparty sessions, failure handling, and the tension between logical purity and programmer ergonomics define the research frontier [8], [9]. Yet the central lesson stands: where the λ-calculus gave sequential programming its logic, linear logic — read as session types — gives concurrent programming its own.
