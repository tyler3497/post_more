# From Specification to Code: Syntax-Guided Synthesis, Counterexample-Guided Induction, and the Verifier-in-the-Loop Synthesis Stack

## Abstract

Program synthesis replaces manual implementation with search over programs constrained by a formal specification. This thesis develops a unified account of the modern synthesis stack: the syntax-guided synthesis (SyGuS) formulation coupling logical specifications with grammatical search-space constraints [1], the counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS) architecture alternating candidate generation with verification [4], the Sketch-style paradigm of programmer-provided partial programs with holes [4], version-space algebra compactly representing exponentially many consistent hypotheses in programming-by-example [6], and emerging large-language-model synthesizers closed around formal verifiers [9]. We trace the lineage from SAT-based sketch completion and the enumerative CEGIS solvers that dominated early Syntax-Guided Synthesis Competitions [3], through version-space learners deployed in spreadsheet systems, to neural-symbolic hybrids using SMT solvers as oracles for LLM-generated code. We characterize the complexity of synthesis (Σ₃⁰-complete for recursive programs [7]), compare search strategies on competition benchmarks, and identify open problems in compositional synthesis, specification inference, and the semantic gap of neural priors.

## 1 Introduction

The oldest dream of computer science is that machines write their own programs. Church's 1957 formulation of the synthesis problem — given a logical specification, derive a circuit implementing it — predates the first commercial compiler, and yet most production software is still written by hand [6]. The gap is a problem of *search*: the space of programs consistent with a specification is astronomically large, and specifications are either too weak (input-output examples) to determine a unique program, or too strong (complete formal models) to be cheaper than the program itself.

Program synthesis research resolves this tension along three axes. First, the **specification axis**: from logical formulas in decidable theories, through partial programs called *sketches*, to raw input-output examples, to informal natural language. Second, the **search-space axis**: from the full grammar of a Turing-complete language down to tightly constrained grammars or sketch holes that encode programmer insight. Third, the **search-strategy axis**: from exhaustive enumeration through constraint solving, version-space learning, stochastic search, and learned neural policies [6], [8].

This thesis argues that these axes are best understood through a single unifying framework — *syntax-guided synthesis* — introduced by Alur, Bodík, and collaborators in 2013 [1]. SyGuS formalizes synthesis as finding an expression from a context-free grammar *G* that satisfies a logical specification φ in a background theory *T*. Virtually every practical synthesizer is an instantiation of this framework: Sketch fixes the grammar implicitly through holes and generators [4]; FlashFill-style programming-by-example compiles examples into a grammatical space searched via version-space algebra; CEGIS provides the iterative architecture connecting an inductive *synthesizer* with a deductive *verifier*; and LLM-guided pipelines are probabilistic instantiations of the same loop, where the neural network proposes candidates and a symbolic verifier supplies counterexamples.

The contributions of this thesis are fourfold. We formalize the SyGuS problem and its arithmetical-hierarchy status (Σ₃⁰-complete for recursive programs [7]); analyze the CEGIS loop as a learning algorithm with termination conditions; present version-space algebra as the data-structure counterpart of SyGuS for the example regime; and examine neural-symbolic hybrids that use SMT solvers and proof assistants as oracles for LLM-generated code.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Classical Formulation

Classical program synthesis is stated as follows: given a specification φ(*x*, *f*(*x*)) — typically a formula in first-order logic over some background theory *T* — find a function *f* such that ∀*x*. φ(*x*, *f*(*x*)) holds [6]. Church's original formulation targeted circuits: synthesize a Boolean circuit from a monadic second-order specification [8].

> **Definition (Classical synthesis):** Given a background theory *T* and a specification formula φ relating inputs *x* and the unknown function *f*, the synthesis problem is to find a term *f\** in the language of *T* such that ⊨*T* ∀*x*. φ(*x*, *f\**(*x*)), or report that none exists.

The classical formulation suffers from two defects. First, it is often undecidable or intractably hard: synthesis of recursive programs from first-order specifications is Σ₃⁰-complete in the arithmetical hierarchy [7]. Second, it says nothing about *how the program should look* — its syntactic form, its idioms, its efficiency — and programmers care deeply about these properties.

### 2.2 The Three Pillars of Practical Synthesis

Practical synthesis systems address these defects through three complementary ideas, each the subject of a major line of research.

**Syntax guidance.** Rather than searching over all terms of the background theory, the user supplies a syntactic template — a grammar, a sketch with holes, or a domain-specific language — that restricts candidate programs [1]. The grammar is not an engineering hack but part of the problem definition: it makes the search space finitely enumerable and guarantees the synthesized program has a desirable shape.

**Inductive learning with verification.** Rather than proving existence directly, the synthesizer *guesses* a candidate consistent with a finite set of observations and then *verifies* it against the full specification; failures produce counterexamples that refine the next guess [8]. This counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS) loop, pioneered in Solar-Lezama's Sketch system [4], is the dominant architecture of modern synthesizers.

**Compact hypothesis representation.** In the example-driven regime, the set of programs consistent with the observations is enormous. Version-space algebra represents this set symbolically — as a DAG-structured object whose nodes denote sets of programs — so that learning from new examples reduces to set intersection, and ranking reduces to traversal [6].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is analytical and comparative: we formalize each synthesis paradigm, analyze its algorithmic properties, and compare paradigms against benchmarks from the Syntax-Guided Synthesis Competition (SyGuS-Comp), which has evaluated solvers on thousands of benchmarks since 2014 [3]. The analysis proceeds as follows:

1. **Formalization.** Each paradigm is cast as an instance of the SyGuS problem: a triple (φ, *T*, *G*) of specification, theory, and grammar, or an approximation thereof. This enables apples-to-apples comparison of search strategies.
2. **Algorithmic analysis.** For each paradigm we derive the core algorithm — the CEGIS loop, the VSA intersection procedure, the sketch-to-SAT encoding — and characterize soundness, completeness, and complexity.
3. **Empirical grounding.** Claims are grounded in published competition results [3], industrial deployments, and measured solver performance.

A note on terminology: we use *synthesizer* for the component that proposes candidates and *verifier* for the component that checks candidates against the specification.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The SyGuS Problem and SyGuS-IF

The syntax-guided synthesis problem [1] is: given a background theory *T*, a correctness specification φ possibly mentioning an unknown function *f*, and a context-free grammar *G* of candidate implementations, find an expression *e* ∈ *L*(*G*) such that φ[*f* ↦ *e*] is valid in *T*.

The grammar is not merely a search heuristic; it is a *contract*. Consider synthesizing `max(x, y)` [8]:

```python
# SyGuS-IF encoding of the max problem (SMT-LIB based) [2]
(set-logic LIA)
(synth-fun f ((x Int) (y Int)) Int
  ((i Int) (c Int) (b Bool))
  ((i Int (c x y (+ i i) (ite b i i)))
   (c Int (0 1))
   (b Bool ((<= i i)))))
(declare-var x Int) (declare-var y Int)
(constraint (>= (f x y) x))
(constraint (>= (f x y) y))
(constraint (or (= (f x y) x) (= (f x y) y)))
(check-synth)
# Solution: ((define-fun f ((x Int) (y Int)) Int (ite (<= x y) y x)))
```

Here the grammar restricts integer expressions to variables, constants 0 and 1, addition, and conditionals guarded by comparisons — a space small enough to search but expressive enough to contain the answer. The SyGuS-IF interchange format [2], built on SMT-LIB 2, standardizes exactly this triple, and the annual SyGuS-Comp has used it to compare solvers on a shared benchmark suite since 2014 [3], with tracks for conditional linear arithmetic, invariant synthesis, and programming-by-example [3].

The theoretical status of SyGuS is subtle. For loop-free programs over decidable theories, the problem is decidable but NEXPTIME-hard in general; recursion pushes it to Σ₃⁰-completeness [7] — one full level of the arithmetical hierarchy above satisfiability (Σ₁⁰) and validity (Π₁⁰). This is why every practical system restricts the grammar or settles for semi-decision procedures.

### 4.2 CEGIS: The Architecture of Modern Synthesis

Counterexample-guided inductive synthesis is an instance of the oracle-guided inductive synthesis framework [8], structured as a feedback loop: a *synthesizer* proposes a candidate program *P* consistent with the current example set *E*; a *verifier* checks *P* against φ; on success the loop terminates, and on failure the verifier returns a counterexample input *c* with ¬φ(*P*(*c*)), which is added to *E* [4].

> **Theorem (CEGIS soundness):** If the verifier is sound and the synthesizer returns programs consistent with all examples in *E*, any program emitted by the loop satisfies φ.

The proof of relative completeness is straightforward: each iteration eliminates at least the current candidate, so with finitely many candidates the loop cannot cycle forever. In practice the art of CEGIS lies in the inductive step: among all programs consistent with *E*, finding one likely to generalize. Strategies include:

| Strategy | Inductive bias | Representative systems |
|---|---|---|
| Enumeration by size | Smallest consistent program generalizes best (Occam) | Enumerative SyGuS solvers [3] |
| Constraint solving | Encode "consistent with E" as SAT/SMT | Sketch [4], symbolic solvers |
| Stochastic search | MCMC over program space weighted by fit | Stochastic SyGuS solvers [3] |
| Neural proposal | Learned prior over programs | LLM-guided synthesis [9], [5] |

Notably, the enumerative strategy — exhaustive search ordered by program size, discarding observationally equivalent candidates — won the first SyGuS-Comp in 2014 [3] and remains the dominant technique for logical specifications, because the grammar itself is the strongest available bias and modern SMT solvers make equivalence pruning cheap [5].

### 4.3 Sketching: Partial Programs as Syntactic Contracts

Sketching, introduced in Solar-Lezama's 2008 Berkeley dissertation, reframes synthesis as *completing a partial program* [4]. The programmer writes a sketch — an ordinary program in which some expressions are replaced by *holes* (`??`) or *generators* (`{| ... |}`) denoting sets of alternatives:

```haskell
-- Sketch-style: synthesize the missing low-level details
int mult(int x, int y) implements spec {
  int r = 0;
  for (int i = 0; i < ??; i++) {      -- hole: loop bound unknown
    r = r {| + x | - x | + y | - y |}; -- generator: choose the update
  }
  return r;
}
```

The sketch expresses the programmer's high-level algorithmic insight while leaving the details the machine fills in. Semantically, a sketch denotes a finite set of programs — exactly the language *L*(*G*) of an implicit grammar — and the specification is a set of test cases plus assertions [4]. The Sketch compiler reduces completion to SAT: holes become symbolic unknowns, the sketch is unrolled into a Boolean formula asserting correctness on the test inputs, and a SAT solver finds the assignment [8].

Sketching makes two enduring contributions. First, *syntactic bias from the programmer* is the most powerful lever in synthesis: a good sketch shrinks the search space by orders of magnitude more than any search heuristic. Second, its CEGIS procedure — inductive synthesis from tests, validation, counterexample-driven refinement — became the template for the entire field [4], [8]. The Sketch system synthesized intricate bit-manipulation routines, concurrent data structures, and stencil kernels that had resisted fully automatic synthesis [4].

The limitation of sketching is also its strength: it demands a programmer who already knows the shape of the solution. Sketching is therefore best viewed not as full automation but as *amplification* — the programmer supplies the insight that is hard to search for, and the machine supplies the exhaustive case analysis that is tedious by hand.

### 4.4 Version-Space Algebra: Compact Sets of Hypotheses

In programming-by-example (PBE), the specification is a set of input-output pairs, and the hypothesis space — all programs in a DSL consistent with the examples — is astronomically large. Version-space algebra (VSA), introduced by Lau, Domingos, and Weld and industrialized by Gulwani's group for spreadsheet string processing, represents this space *symbolically* rather than by enumeration [6]. A version space is a set of programs; VSA defines an algebra over such sets with three operations: **union** (⋓) of alternative subexpressions, **join** (⋈) composing subexpression spaces without materializing the product, and **filter/intersection** (⋂) restricting to programs consistent with a new example.

The key data structure is a *version-space DAG* in which each node denotes a set of expressions, with the total number of represented programs exponential in the DAG size. Learning from a new example intersects the version space with the example's constraint in time polynomial in the DAG size [6]. Ranking — selecting the single most likely program — is then a max-weight traversal, where weights encode an Occam-style prior favoring small, general programs.

```
Version space for: {"John Smith" -> "J. Smith"}
  Concat(VS1, VS2)                    -- join: 10^3 x 10^3 programs
    VS1 = { ConstStr("J."), SubStr(...) }
    VS2 = { SubStr(Word(2)), ConstStr(" Smith") }
  Stored as 5 nodes, 8 edges  =>  ~10^6 programs represented
```

This compactness is what makes PBE deployable: FlashFill, shipped in Microsoft Excel, synthesizes string transformations from one or two examples in under a second, searching spaces of 10³⁰ programs or more without enumerating them [6]. VSA is the data-structure realization of the SyGuS idea for the example regime — the DSL is the grammar *G*, the examples are the specification φ, and intersection is the inductive step of CEGIS.

### 4.5 LLM-Guided Synthesis and the Verifier in the Loop

The newest pillar replaces the symbolic inductive synthesizer with a large language model trained on code, while retaining a symbolic verifier as the oracle. The architecture is CEGIS with a stochastic proposer [9], [5]: the LLM samples candidates conditioned on the specification, a symbolic tool (SMT solver, test suite, proof assistant) checks each candidate, and failures — counterexamples, error messages, failed proof goals — are fed back into the model's context for refinement.

This *verifier-in-the-loop* design recovers the soundness theorem of CEGIS: regardless of how candidates are produced, only verifier-approved programs are emitted. What the LLM contributes is a powerful inductive bias — a prior over programs shaped by billions of lines of human code — that makes the first guess dramatically better than enumeration. Where classical CEGIS might need hundreds of iterations, an LLM often proposes an idiomatic implementation immediately, and the verifier's role shrinks to catching hallucinations [9].

The approach also changes what counts as a specification. LLMs accept natural-language descriptions that no SMT solver could parse, effectively *translating* informal intent into formal candidates the verifier can check. Work combining LLM generation with reactive synthesis from temporal specifications demonstrates the pattern: few-shot prompting converts natural language to a formal specification language, and a correct-by-construction synthesizer takes over from there [9].

> **Theorem (Sample complexity of verifier-in-the-loop):** If the LLM assigns probability at least *p* to the set of correct programs in *L*(*G*) and the verifier is sound, the expected number of proposals before acceptance is at most 1/*p* — exponentially better than blind enumeration whenever the learned prior concentrates on plausible programs.

The caveat is that *p* is unobservable and the prior can concentrate on *plausibly wrong* programs — the characteristic failure mode of neural synthesis. The verifier is therefore not optional scaffolding but the load-bearing component: it converts a stochastic generator into a sound synthesizer. Hybrid systems that constrain LLM output to a grammar (constrained decoding) or feed verifier counterexamples back as in-context data are the current frontier [5].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Formal Results

We compare the paradigms on three axes: benchmark performance, industrial deployment, and theoretical guarantees.**Competition benchmarks.** SyGuS-Comp has run annually since 2014, growing from 250 benchmarks and 5 solvers to over 1500 benchmarks across tracks including general SyGuS, conditional linear arithmetic, invariant synthesis, and programming-by-example [3]. Two robust findings emerge. First, *enumerative CEGIS with observational-equivalence pruning* dominates the logical-specification tracks — the 2014 winner was the enumerative solver [3], and enumeration remains the baseline to beat [5]. Second, no single strategy dominates all tracks: symbolic solvers excel at invariant synthesis, while VSA-based learners own the PBE tracks. The optimal inductive bias depends on the specification modality — a fact the SyGuS triple (φ, *T*, *G*) predicts, since grammar and theory jointly determine which search strategy is tractable.

**Industrial deployment.** The clearest real-world success is PBE in spreadsheets: FlashFill-style synthesis, built on version-space algebra, ships to hundreds of millions of users and synthesizes string transformations from one or two examples in interactive time [6]. Sketch-style synthesis sees narrower but deeper deployment in superoptimization and verified lifting, where a sketch captures the target idiom and the synthesizer discovers optimal instruction sequences. LLM-guided synthesis is newest: verifier-in-the-loop pipelines are being integrated into proof assistants and hardware-design flows [9].

**Formal results.** The theoretical landscape is now well mapped:| Result | Statement | Source |
|---|---|---|
| SyGuS formulation | Synthesis = (φ, *T*, *G*) triple; SyGuS-IF standardizes it | [1], [2] |
| CEGIS soundness | Sound verifier ⇒ emitted programs correct | [4], [8] |
| CEGIS relative completeness | Finite *G* + complete verifier ⇒ termination | [4] |
| Σ₃⁰-completeness | Recursive synthesis strictly harder than SAT/validity | [7] |
| VSA compactness | Exponential programs in polynomial DAG; intersection in polytime | [6] |
| Neural sample complexity | Expected proposals ≤ 1/*p* with sound verifier | §4.5 |

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

**The specification bottleneck.** Every paradigm assumes a specification exists, yet writing specifications remains the hardest part of using synthesis. Logical specifications are expensive; examples underdetermine intent (the *ambiguity problem*); natural language is ambiguous in ways no verifier can resolve. Specification *inference* — mining likely specifications from code corpora, tests, or executions — is the least developed pillar of the stack.

**Compositionality.** CEGIS and VSA struggle with large programs: the search space grows exponentially in program size, and counterexamples for a 1000-line program rarely localize the fault. Compositional synthesis — synthesizing components against local contracts and composing them — works in restricted domains but lacks a general theory; the SyGuS grammar formalism is inherently whole-program.

**The semantic gap in neural synthesis.** LLMs propose programs from a distribution shaped by *surface statistics* of code, not by semantics. The verifier closes the soundness gap but not the *efficiency* gap: on problems far from the training distribution, the prior assigns negligible mass to correct programs and the loop degrades to expensive random search. Characterizing the generalization of neural priors in semantic terms is an open problem [5], [9].

**Unrealizability.** Proving that *no* program in *L*(*G*) satisfies φ is as important as finding one, and strictly harder: it requires reasoning about the entire grammar at once. Recent work on unrealizability proofs via grammar abstractions [7] is promising but incomplete, and most CEGIS implementations simply time out rather than reporting unrealizability.

**Human factors.** Sketching showed that programmer insight is the strongest search bias available, yet we have almost no theory of *which* insights help or how to elicit them. The LLM paradigm sidesteps the question by accepting natural language — but then the insight is buried in a prompt rather than a sketch, and we cannot analyze what the machine actually used.

## 7 Conclusion

Program synthesis has converged on a coherent architecture: constrain the search space syntactically, search it inductively, and verify candidates deductively. The SyGuS framework [1] names the problem; CEGIS [4], [8] names the loop; sketching [4] names the human interface; version-space algebra [6] names the data structure; and the verifier-in-the-loop [9] names the neural extension. Each paradigm answers the same question — *where does the bias come from that makes search tractable?* — and their answers are complementary: from the grammar, from the programmer, from the examples, from the data distribution of human code.

The practical consequence is that synthesis is now an engineering discipline with reusable components: grammars and sketches for bias, SAT/SMT solvers for verification, VSA DAGs for hypothesis management, and language models for proposal generation.
