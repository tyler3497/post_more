# Neural Radiance Fields and 3D Gaussian Splatting: A Unified Theory of Differentiable Rendering for Novel View Synthesis

## Abstract

We present a comprehensive theoretical and empirical analysis of differentiable rendering for novel view synthesis, tracing the trajectory from *Neural Radiance Fields* (NeRF) [1] to *3D Gaussian Splatting* (3DGS) [2]. We derive how NeRF parameterizes a continuous 5D radiance field with a multilayer perceptron, overcomes spectral bias through sinusoidal positional encoding, and concentrates quadrature samples via hierarchical transmittance-weighted sampling. We examine the anti-aliasing extensions of mip-NeRF [3], which replaces rays with conical frustums and integrates encodings analytically, and the acceleration breakthrough of Instant-NGP [4], whose multiresolution hash encoding reduces training from days to seconds. We then analyze 3DGS's explicit representation: anisotropic 3D Gaussians optimized by interleaved density control and rasterized by a visibility-aware tile-based splatting kernel achieving real-time 1080p rendering. We prove convergence of the hierarchical quadrature, quantify the quality–speed Pareto frontier, and discuss open problems in dynamic scenes and relighting. On Mip-NeRF 360, 3DGS achieves 27.4 dB PSNR at 134 fps versus mip-NeRF 360's 29.2 dB at 0.06 fps.

## 1. Introduction

Novel view synthesis — the task of rendering a scene from camera viewpoints never observed — is the central problem of image-based rendering. Classical approaches, from light-field interpolation to multi-plane images, discretize either geometry or appearance, and their quality degrades sharply under sparse input views or complex view-dependent effects such as specularities and refractions. The 2020 introduction of *Neural Radiance Fields* by Mildenhall et al. [1] reframed the problem: instead of reconstructing explicit geometry, one optimizes a *continuous volumetric scene function* represented by a neural network, supervised only by photometric error through a *differentiable* volume-rendering operator.

The NeRF breakthrough rested on three pillars: (i) a 5D implicit representation mapping spatial position **x** = (x, y, z) and viewing direction **d** = (θ, φ) to emitted radiance **c** and volume density σ; (ii) sinusoidal *positional encoding* that lifts low-dimensional coordinates into a high-frequency Fourier feature space, defeating the spectral bias of MLPs; and (iii) *hierarchical volume sampling*, in which a coarse network proposes a piecewise-constant transmittance distribution that a fine network then re-samples. The cost was extreme: training consumed 1–2 days on a V100 GPU, and rendering a single frame required hundreds of MLP evaluations per pixel — roughly 30 seconds per frame at 800×800.

Three years later, Kerbl et al. [2] inverted the paradigm with 3D Gaussian Splatting: replace the implicit MLP with an *explicit* set of anisotropic 3D Gaussians, optimize them with interleaved densification and pruning, and render with a differentiable tile-based rasterizer descended from point-based graphics. The result — state-of-the-art quality with real-time frame rates — ended the implicit/explicit debate in favor of a hybrid truth: continuous volumetric reasoning for optimization, discrete splatted primitives for rendering.

This thesis unifies both paradigms under one theoretical framework. **Our contributions:**

1. A self-contained derivation of the volumetric rendering equation from radiative transfer theory, and its reduction to the alpha-compositing quadrature used by NeRF [1].
2. A convergence analysis of hierarchical stratified sampling, with an asymptotic error bound of *O(N⁻¹)* for the coarse pass and *O(M⁻²)* adaptive refinement for the fine pass.
3. An exposition of integrated positional encoding in mip-NeRF [3] as exact expectation of Fourier features over a conical frustum, with the Gaussian approximation of the frustum indicator.
4. A formal treatment of 3D Gaussian Splatting [2]: anisotropic covariance parameterization via scale–rotation decomposition, the EWA volume-splatting projection, tile-based sorting, and the densification heuristics as a form of adaptive mesh refinement.
5. Empirical comparison across the quality–speed Pareto frontier, with reproducible benchmark numbers from the Mip-NeRF 360, Tanks & Temples, and Deep Blending datasets.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Volumetric Rendering Equation

Radiance fields are grounded in classical radiative transfer. Let **r**(t) = **o** + t**d** be a camera ray parameterized by distance t ∈ [tₙ, t_f]. Under the emission–absorption model (no in-scattering), the radiance observed along the ray is given by the *volume rendering integral*:

$$C(\mathbf{r}) = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t)\,\sigma(\mathbf{r}(t))\,\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(t), \mathbf{d})\,dt$$

where the *transmittance* $T(t) = \exp\left(-\int_{t_n}^{t} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s))\,ds\right)$ is the probability that the ray travels from tₙ to t without striking a particle. The density σ(**x**) ≥ 0 acts as a differential opacity; the color **c**(**x**, **d**) is view-dependent, permitting specularities.

> **Theorem: Differentiability of the volume renderer.** The mapping (σ, **c**) ↦ C(**r**) is Fréchet-differentiable wherever σ is integrable, with gradients obtainable by backpropagating through the quadrature. *Proof sketch.* The integral is linear in **c** and depends on σ through the smooth exponential transmittance; differentiating under the integral sign and applying the chain rule yields adjoint equations formally identical to the rendering pass run in reverse [1].

For numerical evaluation, NeRF partitions [tₙ, t_f] into *N* bins and draws one stratified sample per bin, $t_i \sim \mathcal{U}[t_n + \tfrac{i-1}{N}(t_f - t_n),\, t_n + \tfrac{i}{N}(t_f - t_n)]$. The quadrature estimate is:

$$\hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i \left(1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)\right) \mathbf{c}_i, \qquad T_i = \exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \sigma_j \delta_j\right)$$

with δᵢ = tᵢ₊₁ − tᵢ. The term αᵢ = 1 − exp(−σᵢδᵢ) is the familiar *alpha* of compositing; the sum is exactly front-to-back alpha blending. This reduction is what makes the entire pipeline a differentiable program amenable to gradient descent.

### 2.2 Neural Fields and Spectral Bias

An MLP $F_\Theta: \mathbb{R}^5 \to \mathbb{R}^4$ with ReLU activations is a universal approximator, yet in practice it exhibits *spectral bias*: gradient descent fits low-frequency components exponentially faster than high-frequency ones. For a radiance field containing sharp texture edges and thin geometry, naive coordinate input yields washed-out reconstructions. NeRF's remedy is the *positional encoding* [1]:

$$\gamma(p) = \left(\sin(2^0\pi p), \cos(2^0\pi p), \ldots, \sin(2^{L-1}\pi p), \cos(2^{L-1}\pi p)\right)$$

applied coordinate-wise to **x** (L = 10) and **d** (L = 4). This maps the input onto a high-dimensional hypersphere where the neural tangent kernel becomes approximately stationary with a tunable bandwidth — effectively converting the MLP into a learned Fourier series whose frequency support is controlled by *L*.

### 2.3 Point-Based Rendering and Splatting

Long before neural fields, the graphics community rendered unstructured point clouds via *splatting*: each 3D point is projected to a 2D footprint (a Gaussian kernel) and accumulated in screen space with depth ordering [2]. Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) splatting provides the correct projective transformation of an anisotropic 3D Gaussian through the viewing Jacobian. 3DGS revives this machinery, but with a decisive twist — the Gaussians' parameters (position, covariance, opacity, spherical-harmonic color) are *learned by gradient descent through the rasterizer* rather than fixed by a reconstruction heuristic.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 NeRF: Implicit Radiance Fields with Hierarchical Sampling

The NeRF architecture splits evaluation into two stages. The first 8 layers (256 channels each) process γ(**x**) and emit σ plus a 256-dimensional feature vector; a final branch concatenates γ(**d**) and predicts RGB **c**. Crucially, density is a function of position alone — enforcing multiview geometric consistency — while color depends on direction, modeling non-Lambertian effects.

*Hierarchical sampling* proceeds as follows:

1. **Coarse pass.** Sample N_c = 64 stratified points, evaluate the coarse network, and compute normalized weights $w_i = T_i(1 - \exp(-\sigma_i\delta_i))$, which form a piecewise-constant PDF along the ray approximating where visible surfaces lie.
2. **Fine pass.** Draw N_f = 128 samples by inverse-transform sampling from the coarse weight distribution, concentrate evaluations near surfaces, and evaluate the fine network on all N_c + N_f points.

The loss $\mathcal{L} = \|\hat{C}_c - C_{gt}\|_2^2 + \|\hat{C}_f - C_{gt}\|_2^2$ supervises both passes. This is importance sampling where the proposal is learned online — the coarse network amortizes the cost of discovering geometry.

```python
def volumetric_render(sigmas, colors, deltas):
    """Front-to-back alpha compositing quadrature (Eq. 4, NeRF [1])."""
    alphas = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigmas * deltas)          # per-sample opacity
    trans  = torch.cumprod(1.0 - alphas + 1e-10, dim=-1) # T_i
    trans  = torch.cat([torch.ones_like(trans[..., :1]), trans[..., :-1]], dim=-1)
    weights = trans * alphas                            # w_i = T_i * alpha_i
    return (weights[..., None] * colors).sum(dim=-2), weights
```

### 3.2 Mip-NeRF: Anti-Aliasing via Conical Frustums

Standard NeRF casts an infinitesimal ray per pixel — a zero-width sample of a pixel footprint that is, in reality, an area. When training and test views observe the scene at different scales, this mismatch produces aliasing (jaggies) or over-blurring. Mip-NeRF [3] replaces each ray with a *cone* whose radius grows with distance, and renders each conical *frustum* segment as a whole. The key innovation is the *integrated positional encoding* (IPE): rather than encoding the frustum centroid, mip-NeRF computes the expected encoding under a Gaussian approximating the frustum,

$$\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})}[\gamma(\mathbf{x})] = \gamma(\boldsymbol{\mu}) \odot \exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\right),$$

in closed form, because the expectation of sinusoids under a Gaussian is analytic. The diagonal covariance of the frustum is derived from its axial moments. Mip-NeRF additionally replaces the two-network hierarchy with a single multiscale MLP queried at continuous scales, cutting model size in half while reducing error 17% on the original NeRF dataset and 60% on a multiscale variant [3].

### 3.3 Instant-NGP: Multiresolution Hash Encoding

Instant-NGP [4] attacks training cost at the encoding level. Instead of fixed Fourier features, it stores trainable feature vectors in *L* hash tables at geometrically increasing grid resolutions; a query trilinearly interpolates within each level and concatenates the results for a tiny MLP (2–4 layers). Hash collisions across distant voxels are resolved statistically: gradients from visible surfaces dominate, and the multiresolution structure disambiguates scale. Implemented in fully-fused CUDA kernels, Instant-NGP trains a NeRF-quality field in *seconds* and renders 1080p in tens of milliseconds — a speedup of several orders of magnitude [4], [7].

### 3.4 3D Gaussian Splatting: Explicit Anisotropic Primitives

3DGS [2] discards the MLP entirely. The scene is a set of *N* (typically 1–5 million) 3D Gaussians, each parameterized by:

| Parameter | Symbol | Representation | Count |
|---|---|---|---|
| Position | **μ** | ℝ³ | 3 |
| Covariance | Σ | **R S S**ᵀ **R**ᵀ (quat + scale) | 7 |
| Opacity | α | sigmoid(logit) | 1 |
| Color | **c** | Spherical harmonics, degree 3 | 48 |

The covariance is parameterized by a scaling vector **s** and a unit quaternion **q** to guarantee positive semi-definiteness during gradient descent: Σ = **R S S**ᵀ **R**ᵀ. For rendering, each 3D Gaussian is projected to a 2D Gaussian via the EWA approximation:

$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}' = \mathbf{J}\,\mathbf{W}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{W}^\top\mathbf{J}^\top,$$

where **W** is the view matrix and **J** the Jacobian of the projective transform at **μ**. The renderer tiles the screen into 16×16 blocks, culls Gaussians against tile frustums, sorts surviving splats by depth with a GPU radix sort, and alpha-composites front-to-back with early termination — all in shared memory. Because sorting and blending are per-tile and parallel, 1080p rendering exceeds 100 fps.

*Optimization and density control* interleave with rendering [2]:

1. Initialize from sparse SfM points (COLMAP), with isotropic covariances from nearest-neighbor distances.
2. Every 100 iterations, prune Gaussians with α < ε or oversized in world/view space.
3. Densify where the positional gradient ‖∇_μ ℒ‖ exceeds τ: *clone* small Gaussians in under-reconstructed regions; *split* large Gaussians (halving scale) in over-reconstructed regions.
4. Optimize with Adam on a combined ℒ₁ + D-SSIM loss against ground-truth views.

This is adaptive mesh refinement in the space of radial basis functions: representational capacity flows toward geometric complexity, as driven by the photometric residual.

```python
def project_gaussian(mu, scale, quat, W, J):
    """EWA projection of a 3D Gaussian to screen space [2]."""
    R = quat_to_matrix(quat)                 # 3x3 rotation
    S = torch.diag(scale)                    # 3x3 scaling
    Sigma = R @ S @ S.T @ R.T                # PSD covariance
    Sigma_prime = J @ W @ Sigma @ W.T @ J.T  # 2x2 screen covariance
    return Sigma_prime[:2, :2]
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Convergence of the Hierarchical Quadrature

We analyze the bias and variance of NeRF's two-stage estimator. Let C be the true integral and Ĉ the stratified estimator with N_c coarse samples.

> **Theorem: Stratified quadrature error bound.** For σ, **c** Lipschitz on [tₙ, t_f], the stratified estimator satisfies 𝔼[Ĉ] = C + O(N⁻²) and Var(Ĉ) = O(N⁻¹), whereas naive Monte Carlo achieves only O(N⁻¹ᐟ²) RMSE. *Proof sketch.* Within each stratum of width Δ = (t_f − tₙ)/N, the integrand's variation is O(Δ); the midpoint-rule bias per stratum is O(Δ³), summing to O(N⁻²). Variance per stratum is O(Δ³), summing to O(N⁻²), hence RMSE O(N⁻¹). ∎

The fine pass performs *adaptive* importance sampling with proposal q(t) ∝ ŵ(t) from the coarse weights. When the coarse approximation captures the transmittance profile, the effective sample size concentrates near surfaces and the fine estimator's variance drops to O(M⁻²) in the number of fine samples M — explaining why N_c + N_f = 192 total samples suffice for photorealistic quality where uniform sampling would need thousands.

### 4.2 Why Positional Encoding Works: A Kernel Perspective

Tancik et al.'s Fourier-feature analysis shows that γ(·) transforms the MLP's neural tangent kernel into a stationary kernel k(**x** − **x**′) whose bandwidth is set by the frequency schedule {2⁰, …, 2^{L−1}}. Low *L* underfits (blurry), high *L* overfits to training views (floater artifacts). Mip-NeRF's IPE [3] makes this tradeoff *scale-aware*: the Gaussian damping factor exp(−½diag(Σ)) attenuates exactly those frequency bands whose period is smaller than the frustum cross-section — a continuous, per-sample analogue of mipmap level selection.

### 4.3 The Splatting Pipeline as Differentiable Rasterization

The 3DGS rasterizer [2] is a masterclass in GPU-aware differentiable rendering. Its stages:

1. **Culling & projection.** Each Gaussian's 3D covariance is projected (EWA) and its 99% confidence ellipse tested against tile bounds; typically >90% of Gaussians are culled per tile.
2. **Depth sorting.** A single radix sort over (tile_id, depth) keys orders all splat instances; the sort is the pipeline's dominant cost at O(K log K) for K instances.
3. **Front-to-back blending.** Per-pixel threads walk the sorted list, accumulating $C = \sum_i \mathbf{c}_i \alpha_i \prod_{j<i}(1-\alpha_j)$ with early exit when transmittance saturates — mirroring NeRF's quadrature but over *primitives* rather than *samples*.
4. **Backward pass.** Analytic gradients flow through blending weights to opacity, color, covariance, and position; the sort indices are treated as constants (a straight-through approximation justified by the piecewise-constant nature of permutations).

The crucial insight is that alpha-compositing is *order-dependent but permutation-differentiable almost everywhere*: ties in depth form a measure-zero set, so ignoring the sort in the backward pass is sound.

### 4.4 Spherical Harmonics vs. View-Direction MLPs

NeRF models view dependence with an MLP branch on γ(**d**) — maximally expressive but requiring network evaluation per sample. 3DGS instead stores degree-3 spherical harmonics (16 coefficients × 3 channels) per Gaussian and evaluates the SH basis analytically per view direction. SH degree 3 captures diffuse plus low-frequency glossy lobes; mirror-like specularities spanning few degrees are under-fit. The tradeoff is deliberate: SH evaluation is O(1) per splat with no network, enabling the real-time budget.

### 4.5 Dynamic Scenes: Deformation Fields over Gaussian Soups

Extending to time-varying scenes, both paradigms add a temporal dimension. For NeRF, D-NeRF and Nerfies learn a deformation field **x** ↦ **x** + Δ(**x**, t) warping observations into a canonical static field. For 3DGS, the explicit representation admits a more direct approach: per-Gaussian trajectories over positions, rotations, and opacities, optimized jointly with appearance. Because Gaussians are discrete, topology changes are handled by opacity modulation and densification in time — operations with no clean analogue in a continuous MLP field.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We report representative figures from the original publications on standard benchmarks (Mip-NeRF 360 [3], Tanks & Temples, Deep Blending), measured at 1080p on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 / A100-class GPU unless noted.

| Method | Train time | Render FPS | PSNR ↑ (Mip-360) | SSIM ↑ | LPIPS ↓ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| NeRF [1] | ~24 h | 0.03 | 23.9 | 0.65 | 0.43 |
| Mip-NeRF [3] | ~24 h | 0.03 | 25.7 | 0.71 | 0.36 |
| Mip-NeRF 360 | ~48 h | 0.06 | 29.2 | 0.84 | 0.21 |
| Instant-NGP [4] | ~5 min | ~60 | 25.5 | 0.70 | 0.37 |
| Plenoxels-style voxel | ~12 min | ~15 | 25.3 | 0.69 | 0.38 |
| 3DGS [2] | ~35–51 min | ~134 | 27.4 | 0.81 | 0.23 |
| 3DGS (7k iters) | ~6 min | ~134 | 25.9 | 0.77 | 0.28 |

*Quality–speed Pareto analysis.* Plotting PSNR against log-FPS reveals three regimes: (i) the *quality frontier* (Mip-NeRF 360, 3DGS full) where 3DGS sacrifices ~1.8 dB for a 2,200× speedup; (ii) the *interactive frontier* (Instant-NGP, early-stopped 3DGS) trading ~3 dB for real-time rates with minutes of training; and (iii) the dominated region (vanilla NeRF) superseded on both axes. No method dominates 3DGS: it is Pareto-optimal across the full quality range, which is the empirical content of the claim that explicit primitives ended the implicit monopoly [2].

*Proof of real-time feasibility.* The rasterizer's per-frame cost is dominated by the radix sort over K tile–splat instances and the per-pixel blend loop. With N ≈ 2×10⁶ Gaussians, frustum and tile culling reduce K to ≈ 4×10⁶; sorting at ~1 GB/s effective throughput costs ≈ 4 ms, and blending with average depth complexity ≈ 40 costs ≈ 3 ms at 1080p — totaling under 10 ms (≥100 fps), consistent with the reported 134 fps [2]. By contrast, NeRF's per-pixel cost is N_c + N_f = 192 MLP evaluations × ~1M pixels ≈ 2×10⁸ network forwards per frame — five orders of magnitude beyond the real-time budget, a gap no hardware scaling closes.

*Anti-aliasing verification.* On the multiscale Blender benchmark [3], mip-NeRF reduces average error by 60% relative to NeRF and matches brute-force 4× supersampling while running 22× faster — direct evidence that analytic frustum integration dominates stochastic supersampling. For 3DGS, the follow-up Mip-Splatting introduces a 3D smoothing filter plus a 2D mip filter on the projected covariance, recovering alias-free rendering at varying focal lengths [5].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Unbounded scenes and background collapse.** NeRF's contracted parameterization (as in Mip-NeRF 360) handles unbounded content, but 3DGS's Gaussians distribute poorly at extreme distances; distant backgrounds often require an order of magnitude more primitives or a separate sky model, inflating memory beyond 700 MB for large scenes [2].
2. **View-dependent effects.** Degree-3 spherical harmonics cannot represent mirror reflections or sharp specular lobes; both NeRF's directional MLP and 3DGS's SH exhibit failures on highly glossy materials, and neither models refraction or global illumination.
3. **Geometry fidelity.** Photometric supervision alone leaves density under-constrained in textureless regions. NeRF produces "floaters" (spurious density in free space); 3DGS produces spiky outlier Gaussians. The distortion regularizer of Mip-NeRF 360 mitigates this for implicit fields, but no equally principled regularizer exists yet for Gaussian soups.
4. **Memory footprint.** 3DGS stores ~59 floats per Gaussian; a 5M-Gaussian scene exceeds 1 GB. Compression via vector quantization and pruning is active research, with recent methods reaching 10–20× reduction [6].
5. **Dynamic and deformable scenes.** Temporal extensions assume modest motion; fast topology changes, thin structures in motion, and long sequences still defeat both paradigms. 4D Gaussian methods remain an order of magnitude more expensive than their static counterparts.
6. **Relighting.** Both methods bake illumination into appearance (radiance, not reflectance). Decomposing into albedo, roughness, and lighting — inverse rendering — requires additional priors and remains unsolved at 3DGS quality levels.

## 7. Conclusion

The arc from NeRF [1] to 3D Gaussian Splatting [2] is the arc of differentiable rendering maturing from a proof of concept into infrastructure. NeRF established that a continuous volumetric function, supervised only by images through a differentiable renderer, could synthesize photorealistic novel views — and identified the three enabling techniques: positional encoding against spectral bias, hierarchical sampling against the curse of quadrature, and the emission–absorption model as the right physics. Mip-NeRF [3] made the sampling *correct* across scales via analytic frustum integration; Instant-NGP [4] made the encoding *fast* via learned hash tables; 3DGS made the rendering *real-time* by returning, with full differentiability, to the explicit primitives of classical point-based graphics.

The deeper lesson is representational: there is no fundamental conflict between continuous fields and discrete primitives. The volume-rendering integral is agnostic to whether σ(**x**) comes from an MLP or a sum of Gaussians; what matters is that the forward operator is differentiable and the backward pass is affordable. Future systems will likely hybridize further — neural fields for coarse structure, splatted primitives for detail, hash encodings for speed — converging on a unified differentiable renderer where representation is a compiler-level choice rather than a research bet. The remaining grand challenges — relightable inverse rendering, dynamic topology, and unbounded scenes at real-time rates — define the next five years of the field.
