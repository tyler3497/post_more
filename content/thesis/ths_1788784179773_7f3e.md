# Mechanized Metatheory in Coq: Autosubst, Locally Nameless Representation, and the POPLMark Challenge ## Abstract Mechanized metatheory — machine-checked formalization of programming language semantics, type soundness, and related results — remains one of the most demanding applications of interactive theorem proving. This thesis examines three pillars that have shaped binder mechanization in the Coq proof assistant: **Autosubst**, the signature-driven framework that synthesizes parallel de Bruijn substitutions and decides their equational theory; the **locally nameless representation**, the dominant engineering compromise between named variables and pure de Bruijn indices; and the **POPLMark challenge**, the benchmark suite that, since 2005, has measured how close the community is to a world in which every programming languages paper ships with machine-checked proofs. We reconstruct the mathematical core of the σ-calculus of explicit substitutions, present Autosubst's term-rewriting decision procedure, analyze the locally nameless discipline of freshness and cofinite quantification, and evaluate solution coverage across the POPLMark benchmark. Our analysis draws on the original Schäfer–Tebbi–Smolka papers, Stark's dissertation, the Aydemir et al. challenge proposal, and the POPLMark Reloaded retrospective. ## 1. Introduction The mechanization of programming language metatheory is distinguished from most formal verification efforts by a single, pervasive source of difficulty: **variable binding**. Binders induce α-equivalence, capture-avoiding substitution, freshness side conditions, and Barendregt-style naming conventions that informal proofs handle with tacit discipline but that proof assistants must be taught to manage explicitly [5]. This thesis investigates how the Coq ecosystem has confronted the binder problem over the last two decades. Three developments anchor our narrative. First, the **POPLMark challenge** (Aydemir et al., 2005) posed concrete benchmarks — type soundness of System F\<:, transitivity of subtyping with complex induction principles, and the full language with records — turning anecdotal frustration with binders into a measurable scientific question [5]. Second, the **locally nameless representation** (Aydemir, Charguéraud, Pierce, Pollack, Weirich, 2008; Charguéraud, 2012) became the dominant encoding of binders in Coq: bound variables are de Bruijn indices, free variables are names, and a *local closure* invariant mediates between the two [6]. Third, **Autosubst** (Schäfer, Tebbi, Smolka, 2015) and **Autosubst 2** (Stark, Schäfer, Kaiser, 2019) automated the most tedious layer — the equational theory of substitutions — by generating substitution operations from annotated inductive definitions and deciding substitution equations by rewriting [1][2]. Our thesis is that these three strands are best understood together: the *challenge* defines the acceptance criteria, the *representations* determine how much manual infrastructure a formalizer must build, and *automation* determines how much of that infrastructure disappears. Section 2 surveys the representational landscape and the benchmark history from POPLMark to POPLMark Reloaded [8]. Section 3 lays out our methodology. Section 4 dives into the σ-calculus and its completeness theorem, Autosubst's rewriting decision procedure, the locally nameless proof discipline, and the three parts of the POPLMark benchmark. Section 5 presents the empirical results; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes. > **Theorem (Informal roadmap):** Every syntactic identity provable in the de Bruijn substitution algebra of the untyped λ-calculus is decidable by rewriting with the σ-calculus reduction rules, and this decision procedure can be internalized as a Coq tactic. The remainder of this thesis explains why this is true, why it matters for mechanized metatheory, and where its limits lie [3]. --- ## 2. Background and Related Work ### 2.1 The binder problem Informal metatheory rests on a constellation of conventions that are second nature to the working semanticist but invisible to a proof assistant: α-equivalence ("terms up to renaming of bound variables"), Barendregt's variable convention ("all bound variables are distinct from all free variables"), and capture-avoiding substitution, defined as a *partial* operation guarded by freshness side conditions. Coq has no native notion of binding, so every development must choose a representation [7]: - **Named variables** with α-equivalence as an explicit relation demand heavy freshness bookkeeping, though libraries such as Metalib package much of it. - **De Bruijn indices** (de Bruijn, 1972) replace names by natural numbers counting binders to the binding occurrence; α-equivalence becomes syntactic equality, at the cost of arithmetic *lifting* and *shifting* in every substitution lemma. - **Higher-order abstract syntax (HOAS)** represents object binders by meta-level (Coq) functions, inheriting α-equivalence and substitution for free, but adequacy and exotic terms complicate reasoning [7]. - **Locally nameless** combines named free variables with de Bruijn bound variables, recovering readable statements while keeping substitution capture-free by construction [6]. ### 2.2 The POPLMark challenge In 2005, Aydemir et al. published *Mechanized Metatheory for the Masses: The POPLMark Challenge*, proposing benchmarks based on the metatheory of **System F\<:** — System F with subtyping and records [5]. The challenge was deliberately chosen to stress binders at both the term and type levels, syntactic forms with variable numbers of components, and proofs requiring complex induction principles. It is organized in three parts: 1. **Part 1A (transitivity of subtyping):** prove transitivity of the algorithmic subtyping relation for F\<:, a proof that resists structural induction and requires a custom induction principle (induction on a pair of derivations ordered by the size of the middle type). 2. **Part 1B (type soundness):** prove preservation and progress for the full language including records. 3. **Part 2 (full language with records and pattern matching):** scale the development to the complete calculus. Solutions were contributed in many systems — Twelf, Isabelle/HOL, Coq, Matita — using representations spanning the whole taxonomy above, which is precisely why the challenge succeeded as a *comparative* instrument. ### 2.3 POPLMark Reloaded Fourteen years later, Abel et al. proposed **POPLMark Reloaded** (JFP 2019): a new benchmark centered on *strong normalization via logical relations*, with contexts as Kripke worlds [8]. The motivation was explicit: binder automation had matured substantially since 2005 (partly through tools like Autosubst), so the new frontier was logical-relations proofs — ubiquitous in modern PL research yet rarely mechanized. The shift from "can you handle binders" to "can you handle logical relations" marks the field's progress. ### 2.4 Autosubst and the σ-calculus Parallel to the benchmark effort, Schäfer, Tebbi, and Smolka attacked the *automation* side. Their 2015 CPP paper proved in Coq that the equational theory of the de Bruijn substitution algebra is complete with respect to the σ-calculus and hence **decidable** [3]. Autosubst (ITP 2015) turned this into engineering: given an annotated inductive definition, it synthesizes the substitution operation and a complete set of substitution lemmas, and provides a tactic that solves substitution equations by rewriting — making "substitution lemma management" disappear from proof scripts [1]. Autosubst 2 (CPP 2019) generalized the framework to multi-sorted, variadic, and modular syntax via *vector substitutions* [2]. > **Theorem (σ-calculus completeness, Schäfer–Smolka–Tebbi 2015):** Rewriting with the reduction rules of the σ-calculus is complete for the equational theory of the de Bruijn substitution algebra. Consequently, equality of λ-terms under substitution is decidable by normalization [3]. --- ## 3. Deep Dive ### 4.1 The de Bruijn substitution algebra and the σ-calculus De Bruijn indices eliminate names: the term λx. λy. x y becomes λλ. 1 0, where each index counts the binders between the occurrence and its binder. The price is that substitution must *shift* indices when traversing binders. Schäfer, Smolka, and Tebbi organize this into an algebraic structure. A **substitution** σ is a function ℕ → term mapping indices to terms; instantiation `s[σ]` replaces every free variable. The algebra is generated by four primitives of the σ-calculus (Abadi et al., 1991) [3]: | Primitive | Notation | Definition | |---|---|---| | Identity | `ids` | `ids x = var x` | | Shift | `↑` | `↑ x = var (x+1)` | | Cons / extension | `s · σ` | `(s · σ) 0 = s`, `(s · σ) (x+1) = σ x` | | Composition | `σ ∘ τ` | `(σ ∘ τ) x = (σ x)[τ]` | Instantiation is then defined recursively, with the crucial binder case: ```haskell -- de Bruijn instantiation, binder case (Haskell rendering of the Coq definition) inst :: Term -> Subst -> Term inst (Var x) sigma = sigma x inst (App s t) sigma = App (inst s sigma) (inst t sigma) inst (Lam s) sigma = Lam (inst s (Var 0 `cons` (sigma `comp` shift))) ``` The key algebraic identities — the ones that traditionally appear as dozens of hand-proved lemmas — are: - `s[σ][τ] = s[σ ⨟ τ]` (composition), - `s[ids] = s` (identity), - `(ids x)[σ] = σ x` (evaluation), - `rename ξ s = s[ren ξ]` (renamings as substitutions). > **Theorem (Completeness of the σ-calculus):** Let ≡σ be the congruence generated by the σ-calculus reduction rules. Then `s ≡σ t` iff `s` and `t` are equal in the de Bruijn substitution algebra. Hence the equational theory is **decidable** by rewriting to σ-normal form [3]. > **Proof:** *(Sketch, following Schäfer–Smolka–Tebbi.)* Soundness is by induction on the rewrite rules. For completeness, the authors construct a *normalization model* in which the σ-rules are shown normalizing and confluent; Stark later mechanized and extended the convergence argument in her dissertation [4]. Decidability follows because equality of normal forms is syntactic equality. ∎ This theorem is the theoretical foundation of Autosubst: if rewriting decides the theory, then a tactic that rewrites aggressively can discharge *all* assumption-free substitution equations automatically. ### 4.2 Autosubst: from signatures to automation Autosubst's workflow has three stages [1][2]. First, **annotation**: the user writes the inductive syntax with *binding annotations* (e.g. `Lam (s : {bind term})`). From this single annotated definition, the tool generates the substitution and renaming operations, the complete set of substitution lemmas, and an Ltac tactic `autosubst`. Second, **rewriting decision procedure**: the tactic normalizes both sides of an equation with respect to the σ-calculus rules plus the generated lemmas, closing the goal by reflexivity when normal forms coincide. Because of the completeness theorem, this succeeds on *every* equation of the assumption-free fragment — the user never applies a substitution lemma manually again. Third, **multi-sorted generalization (Autosubst 2)**: the original framework could not handle mutually inductive syntax. Autosubst 2 compiles second-order HOAS *signatures* into potentially mutual inductive de Bruijn term sorts and introduces **vector substitutions**, which fuse the application of several parallel substitutions into a single instantiation operation per sort [2]. The resulting equational theory is both simpler and more expressive; the tool supports many-sorted, variadic, and modular syntax [4]. In practice, the effect on proof scripts is dramatic. A preservation proof for a language with binders that previously interleaved dozens of `rewrite` steps for substitution commutation lemmas now delegates all of them to `autosubst`. Stark's dissertation reports case studies — including System F\<: fragments and the POPLMark-style developments — in which the substitution infrastructure shrinks from hundreds of lines of lemmas to a generated, trusted base [4]. ```python # Conceptual sketch of the Autosubst 2 pipeline (Python pseudocode) def autosubst2(signature): sorts = compile_hoas_to_debruijn(signature) # mutual inductives inst = {s: vector_instantiation(s) for s in sorts} lemmas = prove_substitution_theory(inst) # σ-calculus rewriting tactic = build_rewriting_tactic(lemmas) # decides equations return sorts, inst, lemmas, tactic ``` ### 4.3 Locally nameless: the engineering compromise The locally nameless representation keeps bound variables as de Bruijn indices but uses *names* for free variables [6]: ```haskell data Term = BVar Nat -- bound variable (de Bruijn index) | FVar Name -- free variable (name/atom) | Lam Term | App Term Term ``` Terms are required to be **locally closed**: they contain no *free* de Bruijn indices (indices not under a matching binder). Two substitution operations coexist: `{x ↦ a}` substitutes a term for a *name*, and `[n ↦ a]` substitutes for a *de Bruijn index*. The payoff is that capture is impossible *by construction* in both operations — `{x ↦ b}` cannot capture because `Lam` binds no names, and `[n ↦ b]` cannot capture because `b` is locally closed — and opening a binder (`t^x`, replacing index 0 by a fresh name) is the primitive used in typing rules [6]. The cost is a distinctive proof discipline: **freshness side conditions** (`x ∉ fv(t)`) in typing and opening rules; **cofinite quantification** (Aydemir et al., 2008), which quantifies over *all* names outside a finite set `L` to yield stronger induction hypotheses; and opening/closing lemmas that must still be proved per language, though libraries like **Metalib** and Charguéraud's **TLC** package the generic parts. Locally nameless became the *de facto* standard for Coq metatheory precisely because its statements read like the informal ones — `Γ, x:T ⊢ t^x : U` mirrors the paper rule — while its substitution is total and well-behaved. The "Abstracting Syntax" development of Aydemir, Weirich, and Zdancewic provides a reusable locally nameless metatheory library with reference implementations of Fsub (System F\<:) in several styles [7]. ### 4.4 The POPLMark challenge, part by part **Part 1A — Transitivity of subtyping.** The algorithmic subtyping of F\<: resists structural induction: the transitivity proof needs induction on the *pair* of derivations ordered by the size of the middle type `Q`. It has since been solved in every major representation; the locally nameless solutions use a custom measure induction, and the de Bruijn AFP solution by Berghofer carries it out with explicit lifting lemmas [9]. **Part 1B — Type soundness.** Preservation and progress for F\<: with records demand substitution lemmas at both the term and type levels, plus the interaction between type substitution and term typing. This is exactly the layer Autosubst automates: with generated substitutions and the rewriting tactic, the "substitution does not break typing" infrastructure — historically the bulk of the development — is discharged or generated. **Part 2 — Records and beyond.** Scaling to the full language exercises *modularity* of the binder infrastructure: adding records must not require re-proving the substitution theory. This motivated Autosubst 2's modular, multi-sorted design [2]. | Challenge part | Core difficulty | Representative solution | Representation | |---|---|---|---| | 1A transitivity | Custom induction on derivation pairs | Berghofer, AFP [9] | de Bruijn indices | | 1B soundness | Term/type substitution lemmas | Aydemir et al. metatheory lib [7] | Locally nameless | | 2 records | Modularity of binder infrastructure | Various (Twelf, Matita) | HOAS / de Bruijn | | Reloaded | Logical relations, Kripke worlds | Abel et al. [8] | Various | ### 4.5 Putting it together: what automation buys the benchmark The narrative arc is now clear. POPLMark (2005) asked: *can you even do this?* — and the price was the substitution-lemma bureaucracy. Autosubst (2015) and Autosubst 2 (2019) attacked exactly that price: the completeness theorem [3] guarantees the bureaucracy is *decidable*, and the tool turns decidability into a tactic. POPLMark Reloaded (2019) then moved the goalposts to logical relations, on the explicit grounds that binder handling had become a solved-ish engineering problem [8]. A modern POPLMark-style development thus reads: locally nameless (or Autosubst-generated de Bruijn) syntax, cofinite quantification for freshness, Autosubst discharging the substitution equations, and human effort concentrated on the *mathematically* hard parts — the Part 1A induction measure, the logical relation, the Kripke structure. The binder problem has not vanished, but it has been factored into a decidable fragment handled by machine and a creative fragment reserved for the human. --- ## 5. Stark's dissertation case studies report that developments previously dominated by substitution lemmas become dominated by the *interesting* cases of the main theorems [4]. 2. **Locally nameless pays a fixed infrastructure tax.** The "Abstracting Syntax" library amortizes opening/closing/freshness lemmas across developments, but each new binder *form* (e.g., pattern matching with multiple binders) still requires new infrastructure [7]. This is the tax Autosubst 2's signature-driven approach is designed to eliminate [2]. 3. **The hard parts stay hard.** Part 1A's transitivity induction and Reloaded's logical relations resist automation in every representation tried so far; no tactic discharges them. The benchmark history thus validates a division of labor: decidable binder bureaucracy to the machine, creative induction and semantic construction to the human. --- ## 6. Limitations and Threats to Validity **Scope of the completeness theorem.** The decidability result covers the *assumption-free* equational theory — equations between terms and substitutions with no hypotheses about particular variables. Equations under typing assumptions, freshness hypotheses, or semantic side conditions still require manual proof. The tactic is a powerful simplifier there, not a decision procedure. **Representation lock-in.** Autosubst commits the development to de Bruijn representation. If a project needs named variables for readability of extracted code or for interfacing with nominal libraries, the generated infrastructure does not transfer. Locally nameless developments face the dual lock-in: the freshness/cofinite discipline permeates every definition. **Autosubst 2 maturity.** The multi-sorted, variadic, modular generalization is newer and its tooling less battle-tested than the original Autosubst; Stark's dissertation is candid that some advanced features (full modularity across independently developed syntax fragments) remain prototype-stage [4]. **Benchmark validity.** POPLMark measures *feasibility*, not *cost*: a solution existing in the AFP says little about the person-hours required. POPLMark Reloaded's logical-relations benchmarks are, by the authors' own account, aspirational — full coverage remains future work [8]. Our effort comparisons therefore rest on author-reported figures, which are not independently audited. **Version drift.** The developments we cite span Coq 8.4 through 8.12+; tactic behavior (especially Ltac rewriting performance) and library APIs have changed. Claims about "what the tactic discharges" are relative to the versions documented in the sources. **Finally, this thesis proves nothing new.** It is a synthesis and reconstruction, not a mechanized artifact; readers who need machine-checked certainty should consult the cited developments directly [1][2][3][7][9]. --- ## 7. Conclusion Twenty years after the POPLMark challenge asked how close we are to machine-checked metatheory for the masses, the honest answer is: *the binder problem has been factored, not eliminated* — and the factorization is the achievement. The σ-calculus completeness theorem [3] isolated a decidable core — the equational theory of substitutions — and Autosubst turned that core into push-button automation [1][2]. The locally nameless representation [6] gave the community a shared engineering discipline with readable statements and total operations. The POPLMark benchmarks [5], and their Reloaded successors [8], kept score and moved the goalposts from binding to logical relations as automation improved. The remaining hard problems are exactly the ones no rewriting tactic can touch: inventing the right induction measure for transitivity, constructing the Kripke logical relation for strong normalization, and designing the language so its metatheory is tractable in the first place. That division — decidable bureaucracy to the machine, mathematical invention to the human — is the durable lesson of mechanized metatheory in Coq. --- ## References [1] Steven Schäfer, Tobias Tebbi, and Gert Smolka.
