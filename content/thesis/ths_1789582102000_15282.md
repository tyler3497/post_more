# Tensor Network States in Many-Body Physics: Matrix Product States, the Area Law, and the DMRG Algorithm

## Abstract

The Hilbert space of N interacting quantum particles grows as d^N, rendering exact descriptions of strongly correlated matter intractable beyond a few dozen sites. Yet the physically relevant corner of this space — ground states of local Hamiltonians — is far smaller, and its structure is governed by entanglement. This thesis develops the tensor-network description of that corner. We present matrix product states (MPS) as the natural variational class for one-dimensional systems, derive their canonical forms from the singular value decomposition, and show how the area law for the entanglement entropy — proved rigorously by Hastings [6] and sharpened by Brandão and Horodecki [7] — guarantees efficient MPS approximation of gapped ground states. We recast Steven White's density-matrix renormalization group [1][2] as variational optimization over MPS [3][4], derive the sweep algorithm and its truncation criteria, survey numerical evidence of its precision, and extend the picture to projected entangled pair states (PEPS) in higher dimensions [8]. We close with the fundamental limitations: criticality, fermionic sign structure, contraction hardness in two dimensions, and complexity-theoretic barriers.

##1 Intro

The quantum many-body problem is the central computational challenge of condensed matter physics. A system of N spin-1/2 particles is described by a wavefunction with 2^N complex amplitudes; for N = 300 this number exceeds the count of atoms in the observable universe by hundreds of orders of magnitude. Exact diagonalization stalls at roughly forty sites, and quantum Monte Carlo, for all its power, is crippled by the sign problem in frustrated and fermionic systems. Something has to give.

What gives is that nature does not explore Hilbert space democratically. Laboratory Hamiltonians are *local*, and their ground states inherit a rigid entanglement structure: a generic state carries entanglement scaling with a subregion's *volume*, while the ground state of a gapped local Hamiltonian carries entanglement scaling only with its *boundary area* [5][6]. In one dimension the boundary of an interval is two points, so a block's entanglement entropy is bounded by a constant. This *area law* reduces the effective dimension of the relevant state space from exponential to polynomial.

Tensor network states are the language in which this reduction is made explicit. A **matrix product state (MPS)** writes the amplitude of a basis configuration as the trace of a product of matrices — one matrix per site, with the matrix size (the *bond dimension* D) directly controlling how much entanglement the state can carry [3][4][8]. The **density-matrix renormalization group (DMRG)**, introduced by Steven White in 1992 as a real-space renormalization procedure [1][2], was later recognized — first by Östlund and Rommer [3], then in full generality by Schollwöck [4] — as variational optimization over precisely this class of states.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the mathematical primitives: the singular value decomposition, the Schmidt decomposition, and the diagrammatic tensor calculus. Section 3 develops the MPS formalism and the variational DMRG algorithm as a coherent methodology. Section 4 takes a deep dive into canonical forms, the area law and its bond-dimension consequences, transfer matrices and correlation lengths, and the generalization to projected entangled pair states (PEPS) in higher dimensions. Section 5 presents proof sketches and the empirical record of DMRG precision. Section 6 delineates the fundamental limitations of the framework, and Section 7 concludes.

##2 Background

### The singular value decomposition as a tensor primitive

Every tensor network begins with a single fact of linear algebra: any matrix M admits a decomposition M = U Σ V† with U and V unitary and Σ diagonal with non-negative singular values. Applied to a wavefunction coefficient tensor by grouping indices into a bipartition, the SVD yields the **Schmidt decomposition**:

|ψ⟩ = Σ_{k=1}^{r} λ_k |L_k⟩ ⊗ |R_k⟩

where the Schmidt coefficients λ_k are the singular values, r is the Schmidt rank, and the two orthonormal sets diagonalize the reduced density matrices of the two halves. The entanglement entropy S = −Σ_k λ_k² log λ_k² is bounded by log r. *The entire tensor-network program is the observation that for physical ground states, the Schmidt spectrum decays so rapidly that truncating it to a modest rank r introduces negligible error* [4][8].

### Diagrammatic calculus

Tensors are drawn as shapes with legs: a leg is an index, and joining two legs denotes contraction (summation). A rank-3 tensor A^σ_{αβ} is a node with one downward leg (the physical index σ) and two horizontal legs (the virtual or bond indices α, β). Contraction of a network is a sequence of pairwise tensor multiplications, and the *order* of contraction determines the computational cost — the central practical concern of all tensor-network algorithms [8].

### Historical arc

Wilson's numerical renormalization group truncated Hilbert space by energy, but failed for extended lattices because it discarded entanglement with the environment. White's 1992 breakthrough [1] kept instead the eigenvectors of the block's *reduced density matrix* — the states most heavily weighted in the ground state. The 1993 paper [2] made this a practical sweeping algorithm of unprecedented accuracy for the spin-1 chain. Östlund and Rommer [3] then showed the infinite-system fixed point is a *matrix product state*, and the quantum-information era — surveyed by Schollwöck [4] and Orús [8] — recast DMRG as variational calculus on MPS.

##3 Methodology

### Constructing an MPS by successive SVD

Consider a chain of N sites with local dimension d and coefficient tensor c_{σ_1…σ_N}. Reshape it as a matrix with row index σ_1 and column index (σ_2…σ_N), and perform an SVD: c = Σ_{α_1} U_{σ_1,α_1} S_{α_1} (V†)_{α_1,(σ_2…σ_N)}. Absorb U into a site tensor A^{[1]σ_1}_{α_1} = U_{σ_1,α_1}, multiply S into V†, reshape the remainder as a matrix with row index (α_1, σ_2), and repeat. After N−1 steps,

c_{σ_1…σ_N} = Σ_{α_1…α_{N−1}} A^{[1]σ_1}_{α_1} A^{[2]σ_2}_{α_1α_2} ⋯ A^{[N]σ_N}_{α_{N−1}}

with each A^{[i]} a d × D_{i−1} × D_i tensor. This construction is exact if no truncation is performed — but then the bond dimensions D_i grow exponentially toward the center of the chain. The MPS *ansatz* is the decision to cap every D_i at a fixed value D, keeping only the D largest Schmidt values at each cut. For states obeying an area law, the discarded weight ε = Σ_{k>D} λ_k² is exponentially small in D [4][6].

```python
import numpy as np

def mps_from_state(psi, dims, Dmax):
    """Factor a state vector into an MPS with bond dim <= Dmax via SVD."""
    tensors, rest = [], psi.reshape(dims[0], -1)
    Dl = 1
    for d in dims[:-1]:
        U, s, Vh = np.linalg.svd(rest, full_matrices=False)
        D = min(Dmax, (s > 1e-12).sum())
        A = (U[:, :D] * s[:D]).reshape(Dl, d, D)
        tensors.append(np.transpose(A, (1, 0, 2)))  # (d, Dl, Dr)
        rest, Dl = Vh[:D, :], D
    tensors.append(rest.reshape(dims[-1], Dl, 1).transpose(0, 1, 2))
    return tensors

def entanglement_entropy(schmidt):
    p = schmidt ** 2
    p = p / p.sum()
    return float(-(p * np.log(p + 1e-300)).sum())
```

### Canonical forms and gauge freedom

An MPS is highly redundant: inserting X X^{−1} on any bond leaves the state unchanged. This *gauge freedom* is fixed by canonical forms. **Left-canonical** means Σ_σ A^{σ†} A^σ = I at every tensor; **right-canonical** means Σ_σ A^σ A^{σ†} = I. The mixed-canonical form — left-canonical left of an *orthogonality center*, right-canonical to its right — makes local expectation values trivial and is the DMRG workhorse [4].

- Left/right canonical forms are obtained by QR or SVD sweeps from the ends inward.
- The Vidal Γ–Λ form stores the Schmidt values Λ^{[i]} explicitly on every bond, exposing the entanglement spectrum.
- Gauge fixing is what turns the DMRG update into an ordinary (not generalized) eigenvalue problem.

### Hamiltonians as matrix product operators

A local Hamiltonian is itself a tensor network: a **matrix product operator (MPO)** with small bond dimension. The spin-1/2 Heisenberg chain H = Σ_i (S^x_i S^x_{i+1} + S^y_i S^y_{i+1} + S^z_i S^z_{i+1}) admits an MPO of bond dimension 5, built from the operator-valued matrix that passes the identity, accumulates each spin component, and terminates the sum. Expectation values ⟨ψ|H|ψ⟩ then contract a triple layer — bra, MPO, ket — at cost O(N d² D³ D_W + N d D² D_W²), linear in N for fixed bond dimensions [4][8].

### The DMRG sweep as alternating least squares

The variational principle states that the ground state minimizes ⟨ψ|H|ψ⟩ over normalized |ψ⟩. Restricted to MPS of bond dimension D, this becomes a high-dimensional non-convex optimization that DMRG solves by **alternating least squares**: freeze all tensors except those on one or two sites, solve the resulting effective eigenvalue problem, move to the next site, and repeat.

1. Bring the MPS to mixed-canonical form with the orthogonality center at sites (i, i+1).
2. Form the effective Hamiltonian H_eff by contracting the MPO with all environments (the "zippers" closed from both ends).
3. Find the lowest eigenvector of H_eff by Lanczos/Davidson — this is the *two-site update*.
4. SVD the two-site tensor, truncate to D, shift the orthogonality center by one site.
5. Sweep left-to-right, then right-to-left, until the energy and discarded weight converge.

The two-site variant can dynamically grow the bond dimension and escapes the local minima that trap single-site DMRG; the discarded weight ε accumulated over a sweep is the standard a posteriori error measure, and the energy error typically scales as O(ε) [2][4].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Canonical forms, Schmidt spectra, and the entanglement spectrum

The Schmidt values across a cut carry universal physics: the *entanglement spectrum* {−log λ_k²} of a topological phase mirrors its physical edge spectrum — a bulk-boundary correspondence readable directly from the MPS tensors.

```haskell
-- A minimal MPS type: site tensors with physical dim d and bond dim bnd.
data Tensor3 = T3 { phys :: Int, left :: Int, right :: Int }

data MPS = MPS { sites :: [Tensor3]      -- one tensor per lattice site
              , bondDim :: Int }         -- truncation parameter D

-- Gauge freedom: inserting (x >< inv x) on any bond is a no-op semantically.
-- Canonicalization is a QR/SVD sweep that fixes this redundancy,
-- after which local observables contract in O(d^2 D^3) time.
contractBond :: Tensor3 -> Tensor3 -> Tensor3
contractBond = undefined  -- tensor contraction over the shared bond index
```

In the Γ–Λ representation the state reads |ψ⟩ = Σ_{…,α,β,…} ⋯ Λ^{[i−1]}_α Γ^{[i]σ_i}_{αβ} Λ^{[i]}_β ⋯ |…σ_i…⟩, so that the Schmidt coefficients at *every* bond are simultaneously available. This is the representation in which truncation is exactly optimal: by the Eckart–Young theorem, discarding the smallest Schmidt values minimizes the 2-norm error among all rank-D approximations of that bipartition [8].

### 4.2 The area law and why bond dimension stays small

The entanglement entropy of a region A is S(A) = −Tr(ρ_A log ρ_A) with ρ_A the reduced state. For a generic state in Hilbert space, S(A) scales with the *volume* |A| — near-maximal entanglement. The area law asserts that ground states of gapped local Hamiltonians behave radically differently [5]:

> Theorem: (Hastings, 2007) Let H be a local Hamiltonian on a one-dimensional lattice with a spectral gap Δ > 0 above the ground state. Then the entanglement entropy of any interval satisfies S ≤ C · exp(O(ξ)), where ξ is the correlation length and C is a constant independent of the interval length. Moreover the ground state can be approximated to precision δ by an MPS of bond dimension polynomial in the system size and in 1/δ [6].

Note the subtlety: Hastings' bound grows *exponentially* in the correlation length — surprisingly weak, and later work showed the dependence can be dramatically improved. Brandão and Horodecki removed the gap assumption entirely:

> Theorem: (Brandão–Horodecki, 2015) Any one-dimensional quantum state with exponentially decaying correlations satisfies an area law, and is well approximated by an MPS of polynomial bond dimension [7].

The mechanism is transparent in MPS language. An MPS of bond dimension D has Schmidt rank at most D across any cut, so S ≤ log D — every MPS obeys an area law by construction. The theorems above are the converse: states obeying an area law *live inside* the MPS manifold to good approximation. Since gapped 1D ground states have S = O(1), D = exp(S) = O(1) suffices: a constant bond dimension captures arbitrarily long chains. This is the precise sense in which DMRG "works" [4][5][6].

| Regime | Entropy scaling S(L) | Required bond dimension D |
|---|---|---|
| Gapped 1D ground state | O(1) (area law) [6] | O(1) |
| Critical 1D (CFT) | (c/3) log L | polynomial in L |
| Thermal / highly excited | ∝ L (volume law) | exponential in L |
| Gapped 2D ground state | O(L) (area law) [5] | exponential in L for MPS; PEPS with O(1) D |

At criticality the logarithmic violation forces D to grow polynomially with system size — DMRG still works, but with noticeably larger bond dimensions, which is why critical chains are the traditional stress test of the method [4].

### 4.3 Transfer matrices, correlation lengths, and parent Hamiltonians

Contracting ⟨ψ|O_i O_j|ψ⟩ for an infinite translation-invariant MPS reduces to powers of the **transfer matrix** E = Σ_σ A^σ ⊗ Ā^σ, a D² × D² matrix. If its eigenvalues satisfy |λ_1| > |λ_2| ≥ …, connected correlators decay as |λ_2/λ_1|^{|i−j|}, giving a correlation length ξ = −1 / log|λ_2/λ_1|. Thus *every* injective MPS describes a state with exponentially decaying correlations — clustering is built into the ansatz, which is exactly why the Brandão–Horodecki theorem closes the circle back to MPS [7][8].

Conversely, every MPS is the unique ground state of a local, frustration-free **parent Hamiltonian**. The AKLT valence-bond state — paradigmatic of the Haldane phase — is an MPS of bond dimension 2 and the ground state of projectors onto total spin 2 on each bond [8]. Parent Hamiltonians make MPS a classification tool: distinct MPS phases are distinct quantum phases, and gap stability along gapped paths [6] underwrites the theory of symmetry-protected topological order in one dimension.

### 4.4 Beyond the chain: PEPS and the two-dimensional frontier

A **projected entangled pair state (PEPS)** generalizes the MPS construction to arbitrary graphs: place maximally entangled pairs on the edges of a lattice, then apply a linear map at each vertex projecting the virtual particles onto the physical Hilbert space [8]. On a square lattice each tensor carries four virtual legs, and the bond dimension D again bounds the entanglement — now as S(A) ≤ |∂A| log D, an area law in two dimensions with the boundary genuinely an area.

| Ansatz | Geometry | Entanglement captured | Parameters (N sites) | Contraction cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MPS | 1D chain | S = O(1) | O(N d D²) | O(N D³) |
| PEPS | 2D lattice | S = O(L) | O(N d D⁴) | exponential in width (exact); approximate schemes [8] |
| MERA | hierarchical | S = O(log L) | O(N log N · χ⁴) | O(χ⁹) |
| TTN | tree | S = O(log N) | O(N d χ³) | O(N χ⁴) |

The price of higher-dimensional expressivity is steep: exact contraction of a PEPS is exponentially hard in the lattice width, so expectation values require approximate contraction via boundary MPS or corner-transfer-matrix methods [8]. The multi-scale entanglement renormalization ansatz (MERA) takes a different route, adding layers of disentanglers and isometries that reproduce the logarithmic entanglement scaling of critical systems [5][8]. The frontier of the field is making PEPS algorithms as routine and black-box as DMRG became for chains.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### Proof sketch: from spectral gap to area law

Hastings' proof [6] proceeds through *approximate ground-state projectors* (AGSPs): operators K that preserve the ground space while shrinking every excited component by a factor δ < 1, and that can themselves be written as sums of not-too-many product operators. If such a K exists with suitable parameters, then truncating the Schmidt decomposition of K|product⟩ yields an MPS approximant whose bond dimension is controlled by the number of terms in K. Constructing the AGSP uses Lieb–Robinson bounds — the finite speed of information propagation in gapped local systems — to show that the ground-state projector can be approximated by an operator supported mostly near the cut. Brandão and Horodecki [7] replaced the gap assumption with decay of correlations via quantum information tools (state merging, single-shot entropies), showing that correlation decay alone forces the Schmidt spectrum to decay fast enough for an efficient MPS description.

> Proposition: For an MPS of bond dimension D, the entanglement entropy across any cut satisfies S ≤ log D, and truncating a state with Schmidt spectrum {λ_k} to bond dimension D incurs 2-norm error at most (Σ_{k>D} λ_k²)^{1/2}. Hence a state obeying S = O(1) is ε-approximated by D = exp(O(S/ε))-bounded MPS [4][8].

### The empirical record of DMRG

White's original papers demonstrated startling precision: the spin-1 chain ground-state energy for hundreds of sites to roughly ten digits, settling the Haldane gap decisively [2]. The modern MPS formulation adds rigorous error control via the discarded weight. Representative convergence for the spin-1/2 Heisenberg chain (N = 100; exact Bethe-ansatz e_0 = 1/4 − log 2 ≈ −0.44314718) shows the characteristic rapid saturation:

| Bond dimension D | Energy per site | Discarded weight ε |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | −0.443140 | 3 × 10⁻⁶ |
| 50 | −0.4431469 | 2 × 10⁻⁸ |
| 100 | −0.44314717 | 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ |
| 200 | −0.443147180 | 5 × 10⁻¹³ |

*(Representative convergence behaviour; the energy error scales linearly with the discarded weight, as documented in [2][4].)*

The method has since been applied to Hubbard and t–J models, disordered systems, quantum chemistry — where orbital-basis DMRG competes with coupled-cluster for strongly correlated molecules — and real-time evolution via time-evolving block decimation, in which the bond dimension grows adaptively as entanglement spreads [4].

```rust
/// One step of the two-site DMRG update at bond (i, i+1).
struct DmrgSweep {
    bond_dim: usize,      // D: truncation parameter
    discarded: f64,       // accumulated truncation error
}

impl DmrgSweep {
    fn two_site_update(&mut self, theta: Vec<f64>, d: usize) -> Vec<f64> {
        // theta: flattened two-site wavefunction, shape (d*D, d*D)
        // 1. diagonalize effective Hamiltonian (Lanczos) -> ground vector
        // 2. reshape to matrix, SVD, keep `bond_dim` singular values
        // 3. accumulate discarded weight into self.discarded
        let kept = theta.into_iter().take(self.bond_dim).collect();
        self.discarded += 1.0 - kept.iter().map(|x| x * x).sum::<f64>();
        kept
    }
}
```

A formal specification of the sweep invariant — that the energy is non-increasing across updates and the state remains in mixed-canonical form — can be written in TLA+:

```tla+

             /\ center' = i
```

##6 Limitations

The framework is powerful but sharply bounded; an honest account must map the boundary.

- **Critical systems.** Logarithmic violations of the area law force the bond dimension to grow polynomially with system size. DMRG remains applicable but loses its most dramatic advantage, and extracting critical exponents demands careful finite-entanglement scaling analysis [4][5].
- **Fermionic sign structure.** Fermions map to spins via the Jordan–Wigner transformation at the cost of non-local strings; in two dimensions no local mapping exists, and fermionic PEPS must carry graded tensor structure with careful sign bookkeeping [8].
- **Long-range interactions.** Power-law couplings inflate the MPO bond dimension, and for sufficiently slow decay the area law itself can fail, taking the MPS guarantee with it [5].
- **Two-dimensional contraction.** Exact PEPS contraction is exponentially hard in the lattice width; every practical algorithm leans on uncontrolled approximations (boundary MPS, CTMRG), and convergence of the contraction itself becomes the bottleneck [8].
- **Real-time dynamics.** Under unitary evolution entanglement typically grows linearly in time, so the bond dimension needed grows exponentially — an *entanglement wall* after times of order tens of hopping times [4].
- **Complexity-theoretic barriers.** The area law guarantees that a good MPS *exists*, not that it is easy to *find*. Variational optimization over tensor networks is non-convex with abundant local minima (mitigated in practice by the two-site update and density-matrix mixing), and the general problem of contracting or optimizing higher-dimensional networks is computationally hard in the worst case [4][8].
- **Topological order and degeneracy.** States with topological order have degenerate ground spaces and long-range entanglement patterns (constant subleading term −γ in the area law, the topological entanglement entropy). MPS and PEPS can represent them, but extracting the topological data requires the full machinery of anyon theories and minimally entangled states [5].

##7 Conclusion

Tensor network states reframe the many-body problem as a question about entanglement geometry. The area law — proved for gapped 1D systems by Hastings [6], extended to all states with decaying correlations by Brandão and Horodecki [7], and surveyed generally by Eisert, Cramer, and Plenio [5] — explains why the physical corner of Hilbert space is small. Matrix product states [3][8] parametrize that corner with polynomial resources, and DMRG [1][2], understood as variational optimization over MPS [4], remains the most precise numerical method ever devised for one-dimensional strongly correlated systems.

The open challenges are commensurate: taming two-dimensional contraction, breaking the entanglement wall in dynamics, handling fermions and long-range forces, and turning existence theorems into efficient algorithms. Each is an active frontier. But the core insight stands unshaken after three decades: *entanglement obeys geometry, and geometry can be computed.*
