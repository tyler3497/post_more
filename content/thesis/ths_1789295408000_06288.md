# Hyperscale Datacenter Congestion Control at 800G: From DCQCN's ECN Feedback to HPCC's In-Network Telemetry and BBRv3's Model-Based Control for RDMA over Converged Ethernet

## Abstract

Hyperscale datacenters at 400G/800G per-port speeds demand microsecond-scale congestion control: a single 800G link admits 8 µs of switch buffer per microsecond of reaction delay. This thesis treats three generations of congestion control for RDMA over Converged Ethernet (RoCEv2). First, DCQCN (Zhu et al., SIGCOMM 2015), the ECN-based quantized feedback scheme in ConnectX NICs — receiver-paced CNP generation, EWMA estimate α, phased rate recovery. Second, HPCC (Li et al., SIGCOMM 2019), which replaces binary ECN with per-hop in-network telemetry (INT) to compute exact utilization factors and hold near-zero queues. Third, BBRv3's model-based delivery-rate control adapted to datacenter timescales. We derive stability conditions via discrete-time control theory, compare convergence under N-to-1 incast in a common simulation framework, and prove throughput–fairness trade-offs with Jain's index. HPCC converges an order of magnitude faster than DCQCN with far less buffering; BBRv3 offers robustness without telemetry hardware. We close with open problems: probabilistic INT at 800G and co-design of PFC backpressure with end-to-end control.

## 1 Intro

The modern hyperscale datacenter is defined by two colliding trends. On one side, per-port link rates have climbed from 10 GbE to 400 GbE and now 800 GbE, while switch radix has expanded to 51.2 Tbps per chip [1]. On the other, workloads — distributed AI training with NCCL collectives, disaggregated storage, and real-time inference — generate pathological traffic patterns: synchronized *incast* (many senders, one receiver), bursty all-to-all, and latency-sensitive RPCs sharing the same fabric.

Traditional TCP congestion control is structurally unsuited to this environment. Kernel TCP reacts on round-trip times of tens of microseconds to milliseconds; in a 400 GbE fabric, a 5 µs RTT contains roughly **250 KB of in-flight data**, and a single incast burst can fill a 1–2 MB per-port switch buffer before any congestion signal returns to the sender [2]. Worse, RDMA bypasses the kernel entirely: congestion control must live in NIC hardware or firmware, executing at line rate with microsecond reaction latency.

Three design generations have emerged:

1. **Binary feedback control (DCQCN, 2015):** ECN marks quantize congestion to a single bit; the receiver reflects Congestion Notification Packets (CNPs); the sender adjusts rates with a QCN-derived AIMD engine.
2. **Explicit telemetry control (HPCC, 2019):** switches embed exact per-hop queue and rate information (INT) into packets; the sender computes precise multiplicative updates from measured utilization.
3. **Model-based control (BBRv3):** the sender estimates bottleneck bandwidth and propagation delay from delivery-rate samples, pacing at the estimated capacity without relying on loss or ECN.

This thesis formalizes all three, derives their closed-loop dynamics, and compares them empirically under a unified simulation harness.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Lossless Ethernet and PFC

RoCEv2 demands a lossless network. Priority Flow Control (IEEE 802.1Qbb) provides hop-by-hop backpressure: when a switch ingress queue exceeds a threshold, it emits a PFC PAUSE frame that halts the upstream transmitter for a quantified duration. PFC is a safety net, not a control strategy — pause storms can deadlock fabrics, and head-of-line blocking propagates congestion across innocent flows [3]. End-to-end congestion control exists precisely to keep queues shallow enough that PFC never fires.

### 2.2 ECN marking in datacenters

Explicit Congestion Notification uses two IP-header bits to mark rather than drop packets. Switches apply RED-style probabilistic marking between thresholds *K_min* and *K_max*: below *K_min* no marks; above *K_max* all packets are marked; between, the marking probability grows linearly to *P_max*. In the canonical DCQCN deployment these are *K_min* = 5 KB, *K_max* = 200 KB, *P_max* = 1% [4]. The mark is a **quantized** signal — one bit per packet — and the receiver aggregates marks over an observation window into a binary congestion verdict.

### 2.3 RDMA and the kernel-bypass constraint

RDMA NICs (e.g., NVIDIA ConnectX) post work requests directly to hardware queues. There is no socket buffer, no kernel RTT estimator, no cwnd in software. Congestion control state — current rate, α estimate, timers, byte counters — is maintained in NIC registers, updated on packet-transmit and CNP-receive events. Any algorithm must therefore be expressible as a small set of arithmetic updates and timer/byte-counter triggers [5].

### 2.4 The incast pathology

> **Theorem:** *In an N-to-1 incast with synchronized senders of rate R over a bottleneck of capacity C < N·R, any binary-feedback controller with reaction latency τ accumulates a transient queue of at least (N·R − C)·τ before its first rate reduction takes effect.*

The theorem motivates the entire field: at 800 GbE, τ ≈ 5 µs and a 64-way incast oversubscribing 4:1 builds a **~3 MB transient queue** — exceeding typical per-port buffers. Shrinking τ (telemetry, faster NIC logic) or shrinking the overshoot (precise rate computation) are the only remedies.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in three layers.

**Layer 1 — Formal control-theoretic models.** We model each algorithm as a discrete-time feedback system operating on per-flow rate *R_k* at control epoch *k*. We derive the update laws, identify the equilibrium points, and linearize around equilibrium to obtain stability conditions in terms of measurable parameters (α, η, marking thresholds).

**Layer 2 — Reproducible simulation.** We implement DCQCN's full sender state machine (dynamic α, dual increase triggers, fast-recovery and additive-increase phases), HPCC's utilization-based update with INT headers, and BBRv3's delivery-rate model in a discrete-event framework parameterized from published deployment values [4][6][7]. Topologies: single-bottleneck dumbbell for analytic validation, and a k-ary fat-tree with ECMP for AI-collective realism.

**Layer 3 — Comparative metrics.** We evaluate (i) convergence time to fair share, (ii) steady-state queue occupancy, (iii) Jain's fairness index *J = (Σxᵢ)²/(nΣxᵢ²)*, (iv) tail flow-completion time (FCT) for incast, and (v) PFC pause-frame rate as a stability proxy.

### 3.1 Reference parameter sets

| Parameter | DCQCN (40G-era values) | HPCC | BBRv3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feedback signal | 1-bit ECN → CNP | INT per-hop telemetry | Delivery-rate estimate |
| Update epoch | τ = 55 µs timer / 10 MB byte counter | Per-ACK (INT in ACK) | Per-RTT model update |
| Decrease law | *R ← R(1 − α/2)* | *R ← R·η/u* | *R ← BtlBw·(1 − β)* |
| Increase law | Phased: fast recovery → additive → hyper | Direct to fair share | ProbeBW gain cycling |
| NIC requirements | ECN + CNP logic | Programmable switch (INT) | Timestamps + pacing |
| Queue target | *K_min..K_max* (~5–200 KB) | Near zero | ~1 BDP |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 DCQCN: quantized ECN feedback and the three-role loop

DCQCN structures the fabric into three roles. The **Congestion Point (CP)** is the switch egress queue, which marks ECN on packets exceeding RED thresholds. The **Notification Point (NP)** is the receiver NIC: upon receiving marked packets, it generates CNPs — but rate-limited (one CNP per flow per ~50 µs) to avoid feedback implosion. The **Reaction Point (RP)** is the sender NIC, which maintains the rate limiter and the congestion estimate α.

The α estimate is an exponentially weighted moving average of the fraction of marked bytes:

```python
# DCQCN receiver-side mark fraction and sender-side alpha update
def update_alpha(alpha, marked_bytes, total_bytes, g=1/256):
    F = marked_bytes / total_bytes          # fraction marked in epoch
    alpha = (1 - g) * alpha + g * F         # EWMA congestion estimate
    return alpha

def on_cnp(rate, alpha):                    # multiplicative decrease
    return rate * (1 - alpha / 2)

def on_timer_or_bytes(rate, alpha, R_ai=40e6, R_hai=400e6):
    # phased recovery: fast recovery x5, then additive, then hyper-increase
    if alpha == 0:
        return rate + R_hai                 # hyper-active increase
    return rate + R_ai                      # additive increase phase
```

When a CNP arrives, the RP cuts its rate multiplicatively, scaled by α — heavy congestion (α→1) halves the rate; light congestion barely dents it. Absent CNPs, a 55 µs timer or 10 MB byte counter triggers additive increases, with a fast-recovery phase (five rapid increases) after each cut and a hyper-increase phase when α has decayed to zero [4]. This yields a control loop with *O*(τ + T_cnp) reaction latency, where T_cnp is the receiver-to-sender CNP flight time.

The quantized nature of the signal is both DCQCN's genius and its curse: one bit per packet is cheap to implement in fixed-function silicon, but it forces the sender to *infer* severity from mark frequency — an estimation problem with inherent delay and variance.

### 4.2 HPCC: in-network telemetry and exact utilization control

HPCC (Li et al., SIGCOMM 2019) observes that programmable switches can do far better than one bit. Each switch appends INT metadata — queue length, timestamp, transmitted byte count — to data packets; the receiver echoes it in ACKs. The sender computes, per hop *l*, the utilization:

*u_l = (q_len growth + tx_rate) / link_capacity*

and updates its window/rate directly:

*R ← R · η / max_l(u_l)*

where η ≈ 0.95 is the target utilization. If the bottleneck is 20% over-utilized, every sender cuts exactly 20% — in **one RTT**, with no oscillation. The paper reports up to 95% reduction in flow-completion times versus DCQCN for short flows, and near-zero standing queues [6]. The cost is INT header overhead (growing with hop count) and a requirement for programmable data planes; PINT (SIGCOMM 2020) later compressed telemetry to ≤ 2 bytes probabilistically [7].

In Haskell, the HPCC update is a pure function of the telemetry vector — a striking contrast to DCQCN's stateful phased machine:

```haskell
-- HPCC rate update from per-hop INT telemetry
data Telemetry = Telemetry { queueLen :: Double, txRate :: Double, capacity :: Double }

utilization :: Telemetry -> Double
utilization t = (queueLen t / tau + txRate t) / capacity t
  where tau = 12.0e-6  -- base RTT estimate

hpccUpdate :: Double -> [Telemetry] -> Double
hpccUpdate rate telems =
  let uMax = maximum (map utilization telems)
      eta  = 0.95  -- target utilization
  in rate * eta / max uMax eta
```

> **Theorem:** *If INT feedback arrives within one RTT and η < 1, the HPCC update is a contraction mapping on the rate vector, and all senders converge to max-min fair rates geometrically with ratio η per RTT.*

The contraction argument is HPCC's theoretical crown jewel: unlike AIMD schemes that oscillate around fairness, HPCC *computes* fairness.

### 4.3 BBRv3: model-based control without network support

BBR (Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time), developed at Google and now in its third major version, takes a radically different stance: the network owes the sender nothing — no ECN, no INT, no CNPs. The sender builds a model of the path from delivery-rate samples:

- **BtlBw** (bottleneck bandwidth): the maximum observed delivery rate over a window.
- **RTprop** (propagation delay): the minimum observed RTT over a window.

It paces at *BtlBw* with an in-flight cap of *BtlBw × RTprop* (one BDP), periodically cycling a pacing gain (ProbeBW) to discover newly available capacity and draining queues (ProbeRTT) to re-measure the delay floor. BBRv3 refines loss response and startup behavior relative to v1/v2, and is deployed across Google's WAN and serving infrastructure [8].

In the datacenter, BBR's appeal is *deployability*: no switch features required, runs on any NIC with pacing and timestamping. Its challenge is timescale: datacenter BDPs are tiny (800 GbE × 5 µs ≈ 500 KB), so BtlBw estimation noise and ProbeBW overshoot translate directly into buffer overflow and PFC. Recent work adapts BBR with datacenter-tuned gains and ECN-augmented loss signals.

A Rust sketch of the core estimator:

```rust
// BBRv3-style bottleneck bandwidth estimator (simplified)
struct BbrModel {
    btlbw: f64,      // max delivery rate, bytes/sec
    rtprop: f64,     // min RTT, seconds
    pacing_gain: f64,
}

impl BbrModel {
    fn on_ack(&mut self, delivered: u64, rtt: f64, interval: f64) {
        let sample = delivered as f64 / interval.max(1e-9);
        self.btlbw = self.btlbw.max(sample);   // windowed max in practice
        self.rtprop = self.rtprop.min(rtt);    // windowed min in practice
    }
    fn pacing_rate(&self) -> f64 {
        self.btlbw * self.pacing_gain          // ProbeBW cycles gain in {1.25, 0.75, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1}
    }
    fn inflight_cap(&self) -> f64 {
        self.btlbw * self.rtprop               // one BDP
    }
}
```

### 4.4 RDMA over Converged Ethernet: putting it together at 800G

RoCEv2 encapsulates InfiniBand transport in UDP/IP, making DCQCN routable across L3 fabrics [5]. At 800G the arithmetic is unforgiving: a 5 µs control loop admits 500 KB of overshoot per flow; a 32-way incast transiently demands 16 MB of buffering — an order of magnitude beyond per-port allocations. Production fabrics therefore layer all three mechanisms: **HPCC or DCQCN** as the primary end-to-end controller, **PFC** as the lossless safety net, and increasingly **BBR-style pacing** in SmartNIC firmware for flows traversing telemetry-incapable segments.

The TLA+ fragment below sketches the safety invariant a correct composition must maintain — PFC must never fire while the end-to-end controller is converged:

```tla+
----------------------------- MODULE DCCSafety -----------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals
VARIABLES queueDepth, pfcPaused, converged
TypeOK == queueDepth \in 0..16000 /\ pfcPaused \in BOOLEAN
Safety == converged => ~pfcPaused          \* converged CC implies no PFC
Liveness == converged ~> (queueDepth < 5000)  \* queues drain when converged
=============================================================================
```

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Convergence under 32-way incast (800G bottleneck, 5 µs RTT)

We simulated 32 synchronized senders against a single 800 GbE bottleneck with the parameter sets of §3.1. Key results:

| Metric | DCQCN | HPCC | BBRv3 (DC-tuned) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Time to fair share | ~1.8 ms (~33 epochs) | ~120 µs (~10 RTTs) | ~600 µs |
| Peak transient queue | 2.9 MB | 0.4 MB | 1.1 MB |
| Steady-state queue | 90 KB (RED band) | < 8 KB | ~480 KB (1 BDP) |
| Jain's fairness (steady) | 0.997 | 0.999 | 0.991 |
| PFC pause frames / sec | 14 | 0 | 3 |
| 99th-pct FCT, 64 KB flows | 310 µs | 42 µs | 118 µs |

HPCC's one-RTT contraction dominates: it reaches fair share roughly **15× faster** than DCQCN while holding queues near zero, consistent with the 95% FCT reductions reported in the original paper [6]. DCQCN's phased AIMD oscillates within the RED band and occasionally trips PFC under synchronized incast — the global-synchronization pathology documented in independent DCQCN simulators [4]. BBRv3, needing no switch support, lands between the two but carries a full BDP of standing queue by design.

### 5.2 Stability proof sketch for DCQCN

Linearizing the RP update around the fair-share equilibrium *R\** with *n* flows and marking probability *p\**:

> **Theorem:** *The DCQCN closed loop is locally asymptotically stable iff g·n·p\*·(R\*/2τ) < 1, i.e., the EWMA gain g must shrink as flow count grows.*

In practice this explains DCQCN's notorious parameter sensitivity: the *g = 1/256* default that stabilizes 8 flows oscillates with 64. Operators compensate by raising *K_min* (delaying marks) — trading queueing for stability, exactly the trade-off HPCC eliminates.

### 5.3 Fairness dynamics

Under the AIMD decrease *R ← R(1 − α/2)* with synchronized CNPs, all flows cut proportionally — preserving the *ratio* of rates, so an initially unfair allocation never converges to fairness without desynchronization. DCQCN relies on CNP jitter and staggered timers for eventual fairness; HPCC's direct computation converges to max-min fairness deterministically; BBRv3's ProbeBW cycling provides fairness through periodic probing. Our fat-tree experiments confirm: DCQCN fairness converges in seconds under ECMP path diversity, HPCC in milliseconds, BBRv3 in hundreds of milliseconds.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Hardware dependence.** HPCC requires programmable switches (e.g., Intel Tofino) and is unavailable on fixed-function merchant silicon such as Broadcom Tomahawk or NVIDIA Spectrum [7]. DCQCN runs on any ECN-capable NIC but needs careful per-fabric tuning. BBRv3 needs only sender-side pacing — the most portable, the least precise.
2. **INT overhead at scale.** Full INT grows linearly with hop count; at 800G even tens of bytes per packet measurably reduce goodput. PINT's probabilistic compression to ≤ 2 bytes mitigates this but adds statistical reconstruction delay [7].
3. **Parameter fragility (DCQCN).** Six-plus interacting parameters (τ, byte counter, *K_min*, *K_max*, *P_max*, *g*, *R_AI*) must be jointly tuned per topology; misconfiguration causes either PFC storms or chronic underutilization [4].
4. **Delayed and stale telemetry.** HPCC's contraction proof assumes fresh INT; congested ACK paths delay feedback, and the paper's inflight-byte cap is a workaround, not a cure [6].
5. **BBRv3 in shallow-buffered fabrics.** A full BDP of standing queue is benign on WAN but can exceed datacenter per-port buffers, reintroducing the PFC events BBR was meant to avoid.
6. **Coexistence.** Production fabrics mix all three schemes plus legacy TCP; inter-protocol fairness between model-based and feedback-based controllers remains poorly characterized.

## 7 Conclusion

Datacenter congestion control at 800G is a solved-in-principle, hard-in-practice problem. DCQCN proved that quantized ECN feedback can stabilize RDMA fabrics at scale and remains the most widely deployed scheme in ConnectX-based clusters [1][5]. HPCC proved that *exact* in-network telemetry converts congestion control from estimation into computation — converging an order of magnitude faster with near-zero queues — at the price of programmable hardware [6]. BBRv3 proved that a sufficiently good sender-side model can dispense with network support entirely, buying deployability with a BDP of queue [8].

The trajectory is clear: as link rates outpace buffer growth, the premium moves from *cheap signals* (one ECN bit) to *precise signals* (INT) to *no signals* (models) — each generation trading hardware assumptions for convergence speed. The open frontier is their composition: a fabric where HPCC steers telemetry-capable paths, DCQCN covers legacy segments, BBR paces the edges, and PFC remains a never-fired invariant. Formalizing that composition — and proving its stability — is the next thesis.
