# Dessins d'Enfants and the Absolute Galois Group: Belyi Maps, Ribbon Graphs, and Grothendieck's Anabelian Vision

## Abstract

We present a unified exposition of Grothendieck's theory of *dessins d'enfants* — children's drawings — and its role as a combinatorial window onto the absolute Galois group $\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})$. Beginning with the Riemann–Hurwitz formula and Belyi's theorem, we show how a meromorphic map to $\mathbb{P}^1$ ramified only over $\{0,1,\infty\}$ encodes a compact Riemann surface defined over $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ as a bipartite ribbon graph, and conversely how every such graph determines a unique Belyi pair. We develop the permutation calculus of dessins via the cartographic group $C_2^+ \cong C_2 * C_3$, the passport invariant, and the profinite completion $\widehat{F}_2 \cong \widehat{\pi}_1(\mathbb{P}^1 \setminus \{0,1,\infty\})$ through which the Galois action factors. We then explain fields of moduli, the faithfulness problem for the Galois action on dessins, and the bridge to the Grothendieck–Teichmüller group $\widehat{GT}$ constructed by Drinfeld and Ihara, which embeds the absolute Galois group into a combinatorially defined profinite group. We close with the anabelian program, the section conjecture, and the principal open problems: the faithfulness of the action, the equality $\widehat{GT} = G_{\mathbb{Q}}$, and the effective computation of Belyi maps in high genus.

## 1 Intro

In January 1984, Alexander Grothendieck circulated a research proposal for a CNRS position that would become one of the most influential mathematical texts of the twentieth century: the *Esquisse d'un Programme* [1]. Among its visions — anabelian geometry, the Teichmüller tower, tame topology — one passage struck readers with particular force. Grothendieck described a theorem of Belyi as *"deep and disconcerting"* and proposed that the absolute Galois group $G_{\mathbb{Q}} = \mathrm{Gal}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})$, arguably the most mysterious object in all of number theory, could be studied through *children's drawings*: bipartite graphs scribbled on Riemann surfaces [1], [5].

The proposal is precise: every compact Riemann surface definable over $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ is the domain of a branched covering of the sphere ramified over exactly three points, and the *preimage of the segment* $[0,1]$ under such a covering is a bipartite ribbon graph — a dessin — that completely determines the arithmetic of the situation. The Galois group acts by acting on coefficients, hence permuting dessins, and the central open question is how much of $G_{\mathbb{Q}}$ can be recovered from this action [3], [5].

This article develops the full arc: from the Riemann–Hurwitz formula through Belyi's theorem [2], [6], the combinatorial encoding of dessins by the cartographic group [7], the profinite fundamental group $\widehat{\pi}_1(\mathbb{P}^1 \setminus \{0,1,\infty\})$ [3], the fields-of-moduli problem, and finally the Grothendieck–Teichmüller group $\widehat{GT}$ of Drinfeld and Ihara [4], which packages the Galois action on the entire Teichmüller tower into a single combinatorially presented profinite group containing $G_{\mathbb{Q}}$.

## 2 Background

### Riemann surfaces, coverings, and ramification

A *compact Riemann surface* $X$ is a one-dimensional compact complex manifold; equivalently, a smooth projective algebraic curve over $\mathbb{C}$. A nonconstant holomorphic map $f: X \to Y$ is a *branched covering* of degree $d$, locally $z \mapsto z^{e_P}$, with *ramification index* $e_P \geq 1$ at each point $P$.

> **Theorem (Riemann–Hurwitz):** For a branched covering $f: X \to Y$ of degree $d$ between compact Riemann surfaces of genera $g_X, g_Y$,
> $$2g_X - 2 = d\,(2g_Y - 2) + \sum_{P \in X} (e_P - 1).$$

The sum $\sum (e_P - 1)$ is the total ramification. When $Y = \mathbb{P}^1$ (genus $0$), this reads $2g_X - 2 = -2d + \sum_P (e_P - 1)$, a formula we will use repeatedly to constrain dessins [6].

### Belyi's theorem

The arithmetic pivot of the whole theory is the following result [2], [6]:

> **Theorem (Belyi, 1979):** A compact Riemann surface $X$ admits a model defined over $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ if and only if there exists a nonconstant meromorphic function $\beta: X \to \mathbb{P}^1$, called a **Belyi map**, whose critical values lie in $\{0, 1, \infty\}$.

The "only if" direction is the difficult one. Grothendieck's proof strategy is a celebrated descent argument: given a curve defined over $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$, one first produces a map $f: X \to \mathbb{P}^1$ whose critical values are algebraic numbers, then composes with rational functions over $\mathbb{Q}$ that shrink the critical-value set, using minimal polynomials of algebraic critical values as raw material [6].

Once $X$ is defined over $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$, the pair $(X, \beta)$ is a **Belyi pair**, and the preimage $\mathcal{D} = \beta^{-1}([0,1])$ inherits a canonical structure: preimages of $0$ are colored black, preimages of $1$ white, and the surface orientation induces a cyclic ordering of half-edges at each vertex. The complement $X \setminus \mathcal{D}$ is a union of open discs (the faces), one per preimage of $\infty$. This is the **dessin d'enfant** [1], [6].

The correspondence is functorial and, by the Riemann existence theorem together with GAGA, an equivalence of categories: dessins $\longleftrightarrow$ Belyi pairs over $\mathbb{C}$, and the arithmetic content is that a dessin "defined over $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$" carries a Galois action obtained by applying $\sigma \in G_{\mathbb{Q}}$ to the coefficients of the defining equations [5], [6].

### The monodromy picture

Topologically, a Belyi map $\beta: X \to \mathbb{P}^1$ is a degree-$d$ covering of the thrice-punctured sphere $\mathbb{P}^1 \setminus \{0,1,\infty\}$, classified by the monodromy representation of $F_2 = \langle x, y \rangle$ (free on loops around $0$ and $1$) into $S_d$. Numbering the $d$ half-edges of the dessin, the loops around $0$ and $1$ induce permutations $\sigma_0, \sigma_1 \in S_d$ recording the cyclic orderings at black and white vertices; the loop around $\infty$ gives $\sigma_\infty = (\sigma_0 \sigma_1)^{-1}$, whose cycles record the faces [6], [7].

| Object | Permutation data | Geometric meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Black vertices | cycles of $\sigma_0$ | fibers over $0$ |
| White vertices | cycles of $\sigma_1$ | fibers over $1$ |
| Faces | cycles of $\sigma_\infty$ | fibers over $\infty$ |
| Passport | $(\lambda_0, \lambda_1, \lambda_\infty)$ | cycle-type triple, a Galois invariant |

The triple of partitions $(\lambda_0, \lambda_1, \lambda_\infty)$ of $d$ is the **passport** of the dessin — a coarse but computable invariant of the Galois orbit [6].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is the standard one of the field: translate arithmetic into combinatorics, compute combinatorially, and interpret the obstructions arithmetically [2], [7].

### The cartographic group

Conjugacy classes of transitive subgroups of $S_d$ generated by an involution and a $3$-cycle thus classify clean dessins [7]. Passing to profinite completions — replacing $F_2$ by $\widehat{F}_2 \cong \widehat{\pi}_1(\mathbb{P}^1_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}} \setminus \{0,1,\infty\})$ — is what lets the Galois group act: $G_{\mathbb{Q}}$ acts on $\widehat{F}_2$ by outer automorphisms, hence on conjugacy classes of open subgroups, i.e., on dessins [3], [5].

### Computing a dessin from permutations

The following routine computes the passport and the genus of a dessin from its monodromy pair, via Riemann–Hurwitz:

```python
from math import gcd
from functools import reduce

def cycles(perm):
    """Cycle decomposition of a permutation given as a dict."""
    seen, out = set(), []
    for x in perm:
        if x in seen:
            continue
        cyc, y = [], x
        while y not in seen:
            seen.add(y)
            cyc.append(y)
            y = perm[y]
        out.append(cyc)
    return out

def passport_and_genus(sigma0, sigma1):
    d = len(sigma0)
    s_inf = {x: sigma0[sigma1[x]] for x in sigma0}
    s_inf = {x: s_inf[x] for x in s_inf}  # (sigma0*sigma1)^{-1} handled by orientation
    lam0 = sorted((len(c) for c in cycles(sigma0)), reverse=True)
    lam1 = sorted((len(c) for c in cycles(sigma1)), reverse=True)
    lamI = sorted((len(c) for c in cycles({x: sigma0[sigma1[x]] for x in sigma0})), reverse=True)
    # Riemann-Hurwitz: 2g - 2 = -2d + sum (e_P - 1)
    ram = sum(e - 1 for e in lam0) + sum(e - 1 for e in lam1) + sum(e - 1 for e in lamI)
    g = (ram - 2 * d + 2) // 2
    return {"passport": (lam0, lam1, lamI), "genus": g, "degree": d}
```

In practice, computing the *Belyi map itself* from a dessin — solving for the coefficients of $\beta$ — is the hard direction, and the survey of Sijsling–Voight [2] classifies the four families of attack:

1. **Direct methods:** write down the ramification constraints as a system of polynomial equations and solve via Gröbner bases; exact but doubly-exponential in the degree.
2. **Complex-analytic methods:** uniformize numerically, then recognize algebraic numbers via LLL lattice reduction.
3. **Modular methods:** exploit covers of modular curves $X(N)$ where Belyi maps are known explicitly.
4. **$p$-adic methods:** deform a known mod-$p$ solution via Hensel lifting.

### Fields of moduli

If $(X, \beta)$ is a Belyi pair, its **field of moduli** $M \subset \overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ is the fixed field of $\{\sigma \in G_{\mathbb{Q}} : (X^\sigma, \beta^\sigma) \cong (X, \beta)\}$. The subtlety: a pair can have field of moduli $M$ without being *definable* over $M$ — the Weil descent obstruction, invisible in the combinatorics and detectable only cohomologically [5], [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Dessins as bipartite ribbon graphs

Classical examples abound. Subdividing any planar polyhedron by placing a white vertex at the midpoint of each edge yields a clean dessin on the sphere; the dodecahedron gives a dessin of degree $60$ whose Belyi function was essentially known to Schwarz and Klein in the nineteenth century [6]. The passport of the tetrahedron dessin is $(3^4 \mid 2^6 \mid 3^4)$, and writing its Belyi map explicitly already exhibits the characteristic tension between combinatorial simplicity and algebraic complexity.

### 4.2 The Galois action and its faithfulness

The absolute Galois group acts on Belyi pairs by acting on coefficients:
$$\sigma \cdot (X, \beta) \;=\; (X^\sigma, \beta^\sigma), \qquad \sigma \in G_{\mathbb{Q}}.$$
Transporting across the dessins–Belyi equivalence, $G_{\mathbb{Q}}$ permutes dessins. Elementary invariants of the orbit are visible combinatorially: the passport, the genus, the automorphism group of the dessin, and the monodromy group are all Galois invariants [6], [7].

Indeed, $G_{\mathbb{Q}}$ acts faithfully on Belyi pairs — an element fixing every Belyi pair fixes every number field — and the equivalence of categories transfers faithfulness to dessins [3], [5]. The *subtle* open problem, and the one Grothendieck actually posed, concerns the induced action on the profinite completion: whether the natural map $G_{\mathbb{Q}} \to \mathrm{Out}(\widehat{F}_2)$ is injective, i.e., whether the outer action on $\widehat{\pi}_1(\mathbb{P}^1 \setminus \{0,1,\infty\})$ is faithful. This remains **open** in general, though the kernel is constrained by the Grothendieck–Teichmüller relations [3], [4], [5].

A concrete shadow of the action: there exist *Galois-conjugate dessins* — pairs of non-isomorphic dessins with the same passport, interchanged by some $\sigma \in G_{\mathbb{Q}}$. The smallest examples occur in degree $7$ and $8$; they demonstrate that the passport, while invariant, does not separate orbits, and that genuinely arithmetic information lives in the combinatorics [6], [7].

### 4.3 Cartographic groups and profinite completions

The cartographic group $\mathcal{U} = C_2 * C_3 \cong \mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ is the *discrete* skeleton; the arithmetic lives in its profinite completion $\widehat{\mathcal{U}}$. Finite-index subgroups of $\mathcal{U}$ correspond to clean dessins, and passing to $\widehat{\mathcal{U}}$ corresponds to the inverse system of all of them. The outer action $G_{\mathbb{Q}} \to \mathrm{Out}(\widehat{F}_2)$ is the precise form of "Galois acts on dessins" [3]. Here $F_2 = \langle x, y \rangle$ is free on loops around $0$ and $1$, and the thrice-punctured line is *anabelian*: its geometry is conjecturally recoverable from this profinite group with its Galois action [5].

### 4.4 The Grothendieck–Teichmüller group

Grothendieck proposed studying $G_{\mathbb{Q}}$ through its action not on a single $\mathcal{M}_{0,4}$ but on the whole **Teichmüller tower**: the inverse system of moduli stacks $\mathcal{M}_{g,n}$ of genus-$g$ curves with $n$ marked points, connected by clutching and forgetful morphisms [1], [5]. Drinfeld made the genus-zero part concrete [4]:

> **Definition (Drinfeld):** The profinite Grothendieck–Teichmüller group $\widehat{GT}$ consists of pairs $(\lambda, f) \in \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}^\times \times \widehat{F}_2$ satisfying the two hexagon and one pentagon relations, together with $f(x,y)f(y,x) = 1$.

These relations encode exactly the compatibilities that the Galois action on the tower $\{\mathcal{M}_{0,n}\}$ must satisfy: the hexagon relations come from the geometry of $\mathcal{M}_{0,4} \cong \mathbb{P}^1 \setminus \{0,1,\infty\}$, and the pentagon relation from the boundary strata of $\mathcal{M}_{0,5}$ [4].

Ihara constructed, using Belyi's theorem and tangential base points, an embedding $G_{\mathbb{Q}} \hookrightarrow \widehat{GT}$, sending $\sigma$ to $(\chi(\sigma), f_\sigma)$, where $\chi$ is the cyclotomic character and $f_\sigma$ records the Galois action on paths. Bar-Natan's work on associators [4] shows that $\widehat{GT}$ acts simply transitively on Drinfeld associators, tying the group to quantum algebra.

The great open question of the subject is Drinfeld's conjecture:

> **Open problem:** Is the Ihara embedding $G_{\mathbb{Q}} \hookrightarrow \widehat{GT}$ an isomorphism?

An affirmative answer would give a purely combinatorial presentation of the absolute Galois group — generators and relations with no reference to number fields. At present, no element of $\widehat{GT}$ outside the image of $G_{\mathbb{Q}}$ has ever been exhibited, and the gap between the two is one of the deepest mysteries in arithmetic geometry [4], [5].

### 4.5 Anabelian geometry and the section conjecture

The dessins story is the genus-zero tip of Grothendieck's **anabelian program**: for "anabelian" varieties — roughly, hyperbolic curves — the étale fundamental group with its Galois action remembers the variety [1], [5]. The flagship prediction is the *section conjecture*: for a hyperbolic curve $X$ over a number field $k$, rational points $X(k)$ should biject with conjugacy classes of sections of $1 \to \pi_1(\overline{X}) \to \pi_1(X) \to G_k \to 1.$ The thrice-punctured line is the minimal anabelian curve, and dessins are its finite combinatorial avatars [3], [5].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### Worked example: the power-map dessin

Consider the Belyi map $\beta(z) = z^n$ on $X = \mathbb{P}^1$. Its critical values are $\{0, \infty\}$ — only two, so strictly speaking it is not Belyi; composing with the degree-$2$ map $p(w) = 4w(1-w)$, ramified over $\{0, 1/2, \infty\}$ with $p(0) = p(1) = 0$, $p(1/2) = 1$, produces a genuine Belyi map $\beta = p \circ z^n$ with critical values in $\{0,1,\infty\}$ [6].

The dessin $\beta^{-1}([0,1])$ is the $n$-armed star: one black vertex at $0$ of valency $n$, one white vertex of valency $2$ on each of the $n$ edges, and a single face. Numbering half-edges $1, \dots, 2n$ cyclically, $\sigma_0$ is the $n$-cycle rotating arms at the black vertex, and $\sigma_1$ is a product of $n$ disjoint transpositions. Riemann–Hurwitz confirms the genus:
$$2g - 2 = -2(2n) + (n-1) + n + (2n-1) = -2,$$
so $g = 0$, as expected. This family illustrates a general principle: *dessins with a single black vertex are exactly the "unicellular" maps*, and their Galois orbits were among the first to be classified [7].

### Passports of small dessins

| Dessin | Degree | Passport $(\lambda_0 \mid \lambda_1 \mid \lambda_\infty)$ | Genus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Segment $[0,1]$ | $1$ | $(1 \mid 1 \mid 1)$ | $0$ |
| $n$-armed star | $2n$ | $(n,1^n \mid 2^n \mid 2n)$ | $0$ |
| Tetrahedron | $12$ | $(3^4 \mid 2^6 \mid 3^4)$ | $0$ |
| Cube | $24$ | $(3^8 \mid 2^{12} \mid 4^6)$ | $0$ |
| Dodecahedron | $60$ | $(3^{20} \mid 2^{30} \mid 5^{12})$ | $0$ |

Each row is Galois-invariant as a triple of partitions, yet rows do not determine orbits: the celebrated degree-$8$ examples of non-conjugate dessins sharing a passport show the invariant is incomplete [6], [7].

### Sketch of Belyi's descent

We outline the hard direction of Belyi's theorem, following [6]. Let $X$ be defined over $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ and choose any nonconstant $f \in \overline{\mathbb{Q}}(X)$; its critical values form a finite set $S \subset \overline{\mathbb{Q}}$. If $|S| > 3$, pick $s \in S$ and let $m_s \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ be its minimal polynomial; then $m_s \circ f$ has strictly smaller critical-set complexity, since $m_s(s) = 0$ collapses one value. Iterating reaches three critical values, and a Möbius transformation sends them to $\{0, 1, \infty\}$. The argument is effective in principle but produces maps of enormous degree — one reason explicit Belyi maps remain hard to compute [2].

## 6 Limitations

The theory's limitations are as instructive as its successes.

- **Computational intractability.** Direct methods solve polynomial systems whose size grows superexponentially with the degree; degrees beyond $\sim 15$–$20$ are heroic, and higher-genus examples are scarce [2]. The $p$-adic and modular methods extend the range but require special structure.
- **The field-of-moduli obstruction.** A dessin may have field of moduli $\mathbb{Q}$ yet admit no model over $\mathbb{Q}$; the Weil cocycle obstruction is invisible in the combinatorics [5], [6].
- **Faithfulness gaps.** The profinite map $G_{\mathbb{Q}} \to \mathrm{Out}(\widehat{F}_2)$ is not known to be injective; the kernel, if nontrivial, would consist of Galois automorphisms invisible to every Belyi cover simultaneously [3], [5].
- **$\widehat{GT}$ vs. $G_{\mathbb{Q}}$.** Drinfeld's conjecture that the Ihara embedding is an isomorphism is wide open; no missing relation beyond the hexagon and pentagon relations has been identified [4].
- **Anabelian conjectures.** The section conjecture remains open over number fields in general, despite spectacular progress in $p$-adic and combinatorial anabelian geometry [5].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Dessins d'enfants realize Grothendieck's most audacious proposal: that the absolute Galois group — the symmetry group of all algebraic numbers — can be seen, drawn, and manipulated as combinatorics. Belyi's theorem [2], [6] converts arithmetic into topology; the cartographic group [7] converts topology into permutations; profinite completion [3] converts permutations into a genuine Galois representation; and the Grothendieck–Teichmüller group [4] organizes the whole tower into a single combinatorial object containing $G_{\mathbb{Q}}$.

The open problems — faithfulness of the profinite action, the equality $\widehat{GT} = G_{\mathbb{Q}}$, the section conjecture, effective computation of Belyi maps — are faces of one question: *how much of arithmetic is combinatorial?* Forty years after the *Esquisse* [1], the children's drawings continue to guard their secrets, and that is precisely why they remain among the most fertile objects in modern mathematics.
