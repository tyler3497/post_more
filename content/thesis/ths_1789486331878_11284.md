# The Navier–Stokes Millennium Problem: Weak Solutions, Partial Regularity, and Convex Integration

## Abstract

The three-dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes equations remain the most consequential unresolved system in mathematical fluid dynamics: the Clay Mathematics Institute's 2000 Millennium formulation asks whether smooth, finite-energy initial data always evolve into globally smooth solutions or whether finite-time singularities can form. This survey traces the three pillars of the modern attack on the problem. First, Leray–Hopf weak solutions guarantee global existence at the cost of uniqueness and regularity, satisfying only an energy inequality in the natural energy class. Second, the Caffarelli–Kohn–Nirenberg partial regularity theory confines any hypothetical singular set of suitable weak solutions to parabolic one-dimensional Hausdorff measure zero. Third, convex integration — imported from Nash's isometric embedding theory through De Lellis and Székelyhidi into fluids — has demolished uniqueness for very weak solutions below the critical regularity threshold, while provably stopping short of the Leray–Hopf class. We weigh these advances against Tao's supercriticality barrier and the unsettled September 2026 machine-assisted claim of finite-time breakdown.

## 1 The Clay formulation: existence versus breakdown

In three spatial dimensions, the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations read

$$
\partial_t u + (u \cdot \nabla) u = -\nabla p + \nu \Delta u, \qquad \nabla \cdot u = 0,
$$

with velocity field $u(x,t)$, pressure $p(x,t)$, and kinematic viscosity $\nu > 0$. Charles Fefferman's official Clay problem statement poses four alternatives. The first pair asks for a proof of *global existence and smoothness*: given smooth, divergence-free, finite-energy initial data with zero forcing, there exist smooth, globally defined solutions on either all of $\mathbb{R}^3$ (alternative A) or the periodic torus $\mathbb{T}^3$ (alternative B). The second pair asks for a proof of *breakdown*: a counterexample — admissible smooth initial data and forcing — for which no smooth, globally defined solution with bounded kinetic energy exists, again on $\mathbb{R}^3$ (alternative C) or $\mathbb{T}^3$ (alternative D) [1].

The heart of the difficulty is the quadratic advection term $(u \cdot \nabla) u$, which can transfer energy from large scales to ever smaller ones through vortex stretching. Unlike the two-dimensional case, where vorticity is conserved along particle trajectories and global regularity is classical, the three-dimensional vorticity equation contains the stretching term $(\omega \cdot \nabla) u$, which has no sign and can amplify vorticity without bound [9].

> **Theorem:** (Scaling invariance.) The system is invariant under $u_\lambda(t,x) = \lambda u(\lambda^2 t, \lambda x)$, $p_\lambda(t,x) = \lambda^2 p(\lambda^2 t, \lambda x)$ for any $\lambda > 0$. A norm is *critical* when it is invariant under this scaling; norms that grow (respectively shrink) under small-scale zoom are *supercritical* (respectively *subcritical*).

The natural energy norm $\|u\|_{L_t^\infty L_x^2}$ is supercritical: it controls large-scale behaviour but says essentially nothing about small scales, which is precisely where a singularity would live. This *supercriticality gap* — that every quantity known to be controlled a priori is weaker than every quantity known to imply regularity — is the structural reason the problem has resisted a century of effort [3].

## 2 Leray–Hopf weak solutions: global existence at a price

In 1934, Jean Leray constructed global-in-time solutions in a *weak* sense by regularizing the equations, obtaining uniform energy bounds, and passing to a limit. Eberhard Hopf extended the construction to bounded domains in 1951. A Leray–Hopf weak solution satisfies the equations in the sense of distributions, belongs to the energy class $u \in L^\infty(0,\infty; L^2) \cap L^2(0,\infty; H^1)$, and — crucially — obeys the *energy inequality*

$$
\frac{1}{2}\|u(t)\|_2^2 + \nu \int_0^t \|\nabla u(\tau)\|_2^2 \, d\tau \le \frac{1}{2}\|u_0\|_2^2,
$$

for all $t > 0$, encoding that kinetic energy is dissipated, never created, by the evolution [10].

This is a remarkable existence theorem, and it is essentially the best unconditional global result available. Its price is steep:

- **No uniqueness.** It remains open whether two Leray–Hopf solutions with the same data must coincide. Weak–strong uniqueness (Prodi–Serrin) says only that a Leray–Hopf solution must equal a strong solution *if one exists* on the same interval — a conditional statement that assumes what the Millennium problem asks.
- **No regularity.** Leray–Hopf solutions are known to be smooth on a union of open time intervals whose complement has Lebesgue measure zero, but nothing rules out a singular set of times.
- **No energy equality.** The inequality may be strict: energy could, in principle, be lost to scales the weak formulation cannot see — the same phenomenon as anomalous dissipation in turbulence.

In short, Leray bought global existence by relaxing the solution concept past the point where the Clay alternatives can even be formulated: alternatives A through D concern *smooth* solutions, and Leray–Hopf theory cannot tell us whether the weak solution it produces is the physical one [1][10].

## 3 Partial regularity: confining the hypothetical singularity

If singularities can form, how bad can they be? Scheffer initiated, and Caffarelli, Kohn, and Nirenberg completed in 1982, a *partial regularity* theory for a refined class of weak solutions. A **suitable weak solution** satisfies a *local* energy inequality — the energy inequality localized to arbitrary spacetime cylinders — which Leray's construction can be shown to produce. For such solutions, Caffarelli–Kohn–Nirenberg proved an $\varepsilon$-regularity criterion: if a scale-invariant energy quantity in a parabolic cylinder $Q_r(z)$ is sufficiently small, the solution is smooth in a smaller cylinder. From this they deduced their celebrated conclusion:

> **Theorem:** (Caffarelli–Kohn–Nirenberg, 1982.) The singular set $\mathcal{S}$ of a suitable weak solution — the set of spacetime points near which the solution is unbounded — has zero one-dimensional parabolic Hausdorff measure, $\mathcal{P}^1(\mathcal{S}) = 0$.

In particular, singularities cannot accumulate along any smooth curve in spacetime; the singular set is, in a precise sense, smaller than a curve. Later refinements (Lin's simplified proof, and work of Escauriaza, Seregin, and Šverák resolving the $L^\infty_t L^3_x$ endpoint) sharpened the criteria but never improved the dimensional bound, suggesting $\mathcal{P}^1(\mathcal{S}) = 0$ may be close to optimal for the method [10].

Complementary to this geometric picture are *conditional regularity criteria* — assumptions under which a weak solution must be smooth. The most important are summarized below.

| Criterion | Assumption on $(0,T)$ | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Ladyzhenskaya–Prodi–Serrin | $u \in L^q_t L^p_x$, $2/q + 3/p = 1$, $p > 3$ | Smooth on $(0,T)$ |
| Escauriaza–Seregin–Šverák | $u \in L^\infty_t L^3_x$ (endpoint) | Smooth on $(0,T)$ |
| Beale–Kato–Majda | $\int_0^T \|\omega(t)\|_\infty \, dt < \infty$ | No blow-up at $T$ |
| Caffarelli–Kohn–Nirenberg | Small scaled energy in $Q_r(z)$ | Regular at $z$ |

Every criterion sits at or above the critical scaling; none reaches into the supercritical energy class where Leray–Hopf solutions live. The pattern is unmistakable: *control implies smoothness only when the control is critical or better*, and the a priori estimates are stubbornly supercritical.

## 4 Convex integration and the collapse of weak uniqueness

The most dramatic development of the last two decades has been the importation of **convex integration** into fluid dynamics — a method originating in John Nash's $C^1$ isometric embedding theorem, transplanted to the Euler equations by De Lellis and Székelyhidi, and sharpened into a precision instrument by a generation of analysts.

### 4.1 From Nash to Onsager: the Euler equations as proving ground

De Lellis and Székelyhidi realized that the Euler equations ($\nu = 0$) admit a *subsolution* formulation analogous to Nash's: one seeks a sequence of approximate solutions carrying a Reynolds stress defect, then adds high-frequency, low-amplitude oscillatory corrections that shrink the defect while barely disturbing the velocity. Iterating this *Nash scheme* produces exact weak solutions with prescribed, often bizarre, behaviour — including solutions that dissipate energy despite the absence of viscosity. This machinery culminated in the resolution of the flexible half of **Onsager's conjecture**: Isett, and independently Buckmaster, De Lellis, Székelyhidi, and Vicol, constructed Hölder-continuous weak solutions of Euler with any prescribed energy profile below the critical exponent $1/3$, while Constantin, E, and Titi had earlier shown energy conservation above it [5].

### 4.2 Buckmaster–Vicol: nonuniqueness reaches Navier–Stokes

The natural question was whether viscosity — the Laplacian's smoothing — would kill the flexibility. Buckmaster and Vicol showed it does not, at least for very weak solutions. Using *intermittent* building blocks (concentrated, anisotropic oscillations that saturate Bernstein inequalities and keep the viscous term subdominant), they proved:

> **Theorem:** (Buckmaster–Vicol, 2019.) For any smooth divergence-free vector field and any $\varepsilon > 0$, there exist infinitely many weak solutions of the Navier–Stokes equations in the class $C_t^0 L_x^{2+} \cap C_t^0 W_x^{1,1+}$, arbitrarily close to the given field, attaining any prescribed smooth energy profile.

Starting from water at rest, one can construct both the trivial solution and a solution that spontaneously erupts and subsides — nonuniqueness from zero initial data. As Quanta Magazine reported at the time, the result is "a warning for the weakest notion of weak solutions," with many stronger layers on which good behaviour may still hold [7][4].

### 4.3 Intermittency and the criticality barrier

Why does convex integration stop where it does? The scheme needs the Reynolds stress to dominate the viscous dissipation at each iteration, which forces the constructed solutions to live *below* the critical regularity $\dot{H}^{1/2}$ — precisely the threshold at which weak–strong uniqueness begins. Crossing $\dot{H}^{1/2}$ would collide with the Prodi–Serrin uniqueness theory, and all known building blocks (Beltrami flows, Mikado flows, intermittent jets) concentrate too much to reach it. The barrier is not an artefact of one construction; it mirrors the supercriticality gap of Section 1. A short computation illustrates the scaling tension at the heart of both obstructions:

```python
def kolmogorov_scale(nu: float, eps: float) -> float:
    """Dissipation length where viscous and inertial terms balance."""
    return (nu**3 / eps) ** 0.25

def scaled_energy(u_norm: float, length: float, exponent: float) -> float:
    """Scale-invariant diagnostic: invariant iff exponent matches criticality."""
    return u_norm * length ** exponent

# Critical exponent for L^3-based diagnostics is 0; energy-class
# diagnostics drift as length -> 0, i.e. they are supercritical.
for L in (1.0, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001):
    print(L, scaled_energy(1.0, L, 0.0), scaled_energy(1.0, L, -0.5))
```

### 4.4 Wild solutions and the Leray–Hopf frontier

Pushing the method further, Buckmaster, Colombo, and Vicol constructed *wild* solutions whose singular set in time is nonempty yet has Hausdorff (indeed box-counting) dimension strictly less than one — the first examples threading between Leray's "almost every time is regular" and genuine nonuniqueness [6]. The outstanding open problem is stark:

> **Open problem.** Are Leray–Hopf weak solutions of the 3D Navier–Stokes equations unique?

Convex integration, in its current form, "seems ill-suited for proving the non-uniqueness of suitable weak solutions," because the local energy inequality — the defining property of suitability — resists the oscillatory constructions [5]. The frontier of the method is exactly the frontier of the Millennium problem.

## 5 Supercriticality, blow-up criteria, and the averaged program

If convex integration attacks from below (weak solutions), the complementary strategy attacks from above: prove that *smooth* solutions can blow up. Terence Tao's 2016 breakthrough formalized the **supercriticality barrier** by constructing finite-time blow-up for an *averaged* Navier–Stokes equation — a modification preserving the energy identity and the coarse harmonic-analysis structure of the nonlinearity, but with the bilinear term averaged over rotations, dilations, and Fourier multipliers of order zero. The averaged system admits a dyadic cascade model in which energy shuttles to ever-higher frequencies faster than viscosity can damp it, producing blow-up in finite time [3].

The moral is double-edged. On one hand, it proves that *no argument using only the energy identity and standard harmonic-analysis estimates can establish global regularity* — any successful proof must exploit finer structure of the true nonlinearity. On the other, it suggests a program: de-average the construction, restoring the true bilinear term step by step, and carry the blow-up along. Whether the true nonlinearity's geometric structure (its null forms, its depletion of nonlinearity through Beltrami-type alignment) defeats the cascade is unknown.

This program gained urgency from parallel developments on the *inviscid* side: Luo and Hou's 2013 numerical evidence for Euler blow-up, and the Córdoba–Martínez-Zoroa framework that produced forced blow-up for hypodissipative Navier–Stokes and unforced blow-up for 3D Euler — results with acknowledged gaps from the Millennium setup, but widely seen as the most plausible route toward a genuine singularity [2].

---

The state of play can be summarized as a squeeze play on the regularity scale. From above, partial regularity and conditional criteria say: *anything critical or better is smooth*. From below, convex integration says: *anything sufficiently weak is wildly nonunique*. Between them lies the energy class — the supercritical no-man's-land where Leray–Hopf solutions live, where uniqueness is open, where singularities are neither proved nor excluded, and where the Clay prize waits.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stressing what is *not* known, and the present moment demands unusual care. On 8 September 2026 — one week before this writing — OpenAI announced that an internal multi-agent system had produced a 166-page manuscript, with a Lean formalization, claiming to establish Fefferman's alternatives (C) and (D): finite-time breakdown of smooth solutions. The announcement arrived amid a priority dispute with Tristan Buckmaster and Levent Alpöge, whose related Euler-equation advances were developed partly with AI assistance, and the Clay Mathematics Institute has not acknowledged any party as having solved the problem [2].

Several caveats apply. First, a Lean formalization certifies *logical validity relative to its axioms and definitions*, not that the formalized statement matches the Clay problem's intended meaning — formalization bugs and specification gaps are a live concern. Second, the mathematical community has not completed independent verification; history is littered with claimed resolutions that collapsed under scrutiny. Third, the dispute over provenance — including questions about training data derived from the mathematicians' own use of AI coding tools — means the epistemic situation is genuinely unsettled, not merely awaiting rubber-stamping [2].

Beyond this episode, structural limitations persist. Convex integration's nonuniqueness lives far below physical regularity and may say nothing about smooth solutions. Partial regularity's dimensional bound has not improved in over four decades. Tao's barrier cuts both ways: it rules out a large class of regularity proofs but its blow-up lives in an averaged world. And even a verified finite-time singularity would leave open the *physical* question — whether real fluids, with molecular cutoffs the continuum model ignores, ever see it.

## 7 Conclusion

Ninety years after Leray, the Navier–Stokes Millennium problem remains open in the precise sense the Clay Institute means: no verified proof of global smooth existence, and no verified finite-time breakdown, for the unforced three-dimensional equations. What has changed is the shape of our ignorance. We now know that global existence is cheap (Leray–Hopf), that singularities — if they exist — are geometrically tiny (Caffarelli–Kohn–Nirenberg), that weak uniqueness fails spectacularly below criticality (convex integration), and that no soft argument can prove regularity (Tao's barrier). Each advance has narrowed the corridor in which the answer must hide: the supercritical energy class, where the equations are simultaneously too rough for our estimates and too rigid for our constructions. Whether the September 2026 machine-assisted claim survives scrutiny or joins the archive of fallen attempts, the problem has entered a new phase — one in which the frontier is patrolled not only by analysts but by the very learning systems whose training data includes their notebooks. The equations, indifferent, continue to flow.
