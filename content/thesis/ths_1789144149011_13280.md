# Program Superoptimization: Stochastic Search, SMT-Guided Synthesis, and the Cost-Model Frontier

## Abstract

Superoptimization asks a question conventional compilers evade: given a specification of a computation, what is the *best* instruction sequence that implements it? This thesis unifies the three modern answers to that question. First, stochastic superoptimization, exemplified by STOKE, casts the search as a Markov Chain Monte Carlo walk over program space, encoding correctness and performance as terms of a single cost function and discovering sequences that rival expert hand-written assembly [1]. Second, synthesis-guided superoptimization, exemplified by Souper, uses enumerative search discharged by SMT solvers to derive peephole optimizations that shipped in LLVM and MSVC [2]. Third, the cost-model frontier exposes why "best" is ill-posed: latency, throughput, and code size induce conflicting orders on candidate programs, and any optimizer must pick a point on the resulting Pareto surface. We trace the lineage from Massalin's brute-force enumerator through Denali's goal-directed SAT encoding to data-driven equivalence checking, formalize the MCMC acceptance machinery, derive the counterexample-guided inductive synthesis loop, analyze why static cost models diverge from silicon, and map the fundamental limits: loop-free fragments, solver scalability, and the semantic gap between verified equivalence and observed performance.

## 1. Introduction

Most compiler optimizations are *improvements*: they transform programs while preserving semantics, but make no claim about the quality of the result. Superoptimization is different. It aspires to *optimality* — not a cheaper program than the input, but the cheapest program that exists. The field was christened by Alexia Massalin in 1987 with the provocation that compiler writers had settled for improvement when optimization was achievable: exhaustive enumeration of instruction sequences, filtered by a probabilistic equivalence test, could find startlingly clever bit-fiddling sequences for functions like the signum function that bore little resemblance to any compiler's output [3].

The brute-force program was powerful but limited: the search space of instruction sequences of length *n* over an alphabet of *m* opcodes grows as *(m·r)^n*, where *r* captures operand and addressing-mode choices, so enumeration exhausts itself within a handful of instructions. Three subsequent research programs each attacked a different face of this exponential.

*Search strategy.* STOKE (Schkufza, Sharma, Aiken, ASPLOS 2013) abandoned enumeration for stochastic search: a Metropolis–Hastings Markov chain wanders through program space, proposing local mutations and accepting them with probability governed by a cost function that trades correctness against speed [1]. Sacrificing completeness bought reach: STOKE routinely synthesized rewrites for sequences far longer than any enumerator could touch, matching or beating `gcc -O3` starting from `llvm -O0` binaries, and occasionally beating expert human assembly.

*Verification machinery.* Souper (Sasnauskas et al., PLDI 2017) attacked from the middle end: extract integer-valued LLVM IR fragments, enumerate candidate replacements from a compact IR, and discharge equivalence with an SMT solver [2]. Where STOKE gambled on randomness plus testing with SMT as a final gate, Souper made the solver the engine of discovery itself — a synthesis loop in the counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS) tradition.

*Cost modeling.* Both programs stand or fall on the fidelity of their cost model. A sequence that minimizes instruction count may not minimize latency on an out-of-order core; one that minimizes latency may explode in code size and I-cache pressure.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The classical era: enumeration and the probabilistic test

Massalin's 1987 superoptimizer [3] enumerated instruction sequences in increasing length and tested each against the target function using a *probabilistic test*: execute both on a battery of inputs and accept the candidate when behavior coincides. The probabilistic test was the crucial concession — full verification of arbitrary sequences was (and remains) intractable in general, but sampling made exhaustive search "practical for programs of useful size," in Massalin's phrasing.

Granlund and Kenner extended this idea into the GNU superoptimizer, integrated with GCC and aimed at eliminating branches; the tool was described as "a function sequence generator that uses an exhaustive generate-and-test approach to finding the shortest assembly instruction sequence for a given function" [4]. For simple in-order architectures such as the 68000, shortest genuinely meant fastest, and the results were optimal in a defensible sense. For superscalar, out-of-order machines, the correspondence breaks — a theme to which we return in §4.4.

### 2.2 Goal-directed search: Denali

Joshi, Nelson, and Randall's Denali (PLDI 2002) replaced brute-force enumeration with goal-directed reasoning: encode the superoptimization query as a SAT problem, searching for an instruction sequence of bounded length whose observable behavior matches the specification [5]. It also established a lasting lesson: the encoding — not the solver — is the bottleneck, since bit-vector theories scale poorly past tens of instructions.

### 2.3 The testing–verification spectrum

Between testing (cheap, unsound) and full verification (sound, expensive) lies a spectrum that every modern superoptimizer navigates:

1. **Random/fuzz testing.** Execute target and candidate on sampled inputs. Fast, parallelizable, and embarrassingly effective as a filter — most proposed mutants are wrong, and a few thousand test vectors reject nearly all of them.
2. **Directed test generation.** Deduce inputs that distinguish candidate from target, e.g., via concolic execution or SMT-generated counterexamples.
3. **Data-driven equivalence checking.** Sharma, Schkufza, Churchill, and Aiken (OOPSLA 2013) showed that the outcomes of test executions can be used to *learn* a verifier: fit an error function over sampled behaviors, then discharge the residual with a solver only on the hard cases [6]. STOKE uses this machinery as its correctness gate.
4. **Full SMT verification.** Encode both programs as bit-vector formulas and ask the solver to prove ∀ inputs: outputs agree. Sound modulo the formalization of the ISA semantics, but solver time grows super-linearly with sequence length.

STOKE and Souper sit at different points on this spectrum and embody the central trade-off of the field: STOKE buys search *breadth* with testing and pays for *soundness* with a solver at the end; Souper buys soundness per-candidate and pays with a tightly bounded enumeration.

### 2.4 Peephole superoptimizers as compiler infrastructure

Superoptimization also serves a second purpose: *discovering* optimizations for compilers to implement. The GNU superoptimizer's branch eliminations were folded into GCC [4]; Souper's discoveries were implemented by hand by LLVM and MSVC engineers and shipped in production compilers [2]. This "advisor" mode decouples the cost of superoptimization (hours per fragment, offline) from the cost of compilation (milliseconds).

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The superoptimization problem, formalized

Let **P** be the set of loop-free programs over a fixed instruction alphabet. Given a target *P ∈ P* and a cost function *c : P → ℝ≥0* (smaller is better), the superoptimization problem is:

> **Find** *P′ ∈ P* **such that** *P′ ≡ P* **and** *c(P′)* **is minimized.**

Equivalence ≡ is observational equivalence over the machine's input/output state (registers, flags, memory). The problem is undecidable in general, so every practical system restricts the fragment — STOKE to straight-line x86-64 with bounded memory reasoning, Souper to control-flow-free LLVM IR over integers [1][2].

### 3.2 Stochastic search: the MCMC formulation

STOKE defines a probability distribution over programs in which *better* programs are exponentially more likely:

$$p(P'; \beta) \propto \exp(-\beta \cdot c(P'))$$

for an inverse-temperature parameter β. It then runs a Metropolis–Hastings chain whose stationary distribution is *p*: from the current program *x*, propose a mutation *x′* from a symmetric proposal distribution *q*, and accept with probability [1]

> **Theorem (MCMC acceptance):** *For symmetric proposals, the chain with acceptance probability α = min(1, p(x′)/p(x)) converges to the target distribution p, so that time spent near low-cost programs is maximized.*

The mutations are deliberately simple — replace an opcode, replace an operand, swap two instructions, or synthesize a fresh random instruction — because the power lies in the *number* of iterations (millions), not the cleverness of each step. The cost function decomposes as

$$c(P') = \underbrace{\text{eq}(P', P)}_{\text{correctness error}} + \underbrace{\text{perf}(P')}_{\text{performance term}}$$

where `eq` is measured first by test-case disagreement (Hamming distance over output bits across test vectors) and `perf` is a static estimate combining instruction latencies and a length penalty [1].

The engineering insight is that *almost all* proposals are wrong, so the expensive operations — SMT verification — must sit behind cheap filters. STOKE's pipeline per proposal is: (i) quick reject by test cases, (ii) accept/reject by the cost function, (iii) only for survivors, data-driven equivalence checking, (iv) only for the finalists, full SMT verification.

### 3.3 Synthesis-guided search: the Souper/CEGIS loop

Souper inverts the pipeline. Rather than proposing random machine programs and checking them, it extracts a fragment (the LHS) from LLVM IR, *enumerates* candidate replacements (the RHS) from a small purely-functional IR in increasing cost order, and for each candidate asks Z3 whether the replacement is valid [2]:

$$ \forall \vec{x}.\; \text{LHS}(\vec{x}) = \text{RHS}(\vec{x}) $$

If the solver returns a counterexample, the candidate is pruned and the counterexample can guide further enumeration — the counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS) loop. Because enumeration proceeds in cost order and every emitted candidate is solver-verified, Souper's output is optimal *relative to its cost model and enumeration bound*, recovering a fragment of the completeness that STOKE sacrifices, at the price of much shorter fragments.

### 3.4 Cost-model design

The cost function is the specification of "best," and its design is the most consequential choice in a superoptimizer. The three classical axes are:

| Axis | Measures | Proxy used in practice |
|---|---|---|
| **Latency** | Cycles for a dependent chain | Sum of instruction latencies on the critical path |
| **Throughput** | Inverse of sustained issue rate | Reciprocal throughput / port-pressure estimate |
| **Size** | Bytes / I-cache footprint | Instruction count or byte length |

STOKE's `perf` term is latency-oriented with a length penalty [1]; Souper's enumeration minimizes an IR-level cost that correlates with size and was observed to shrink a Clang binary by ~3 MB (4.4%) [2]. The disagreement between these choices is not a bug — it is the cost-model frontier of §4.4.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Anatomy of the MCMC walk

Consider superoptimizing a 32-bit population count. The target might be a naive loop-free shift-and-add sequence compiled by `llvm -O0`. STOKE's walk begins at the target itself and proposes mutations. A typical trajectory: an operand mutation replaces a register with a constant, *increasing* test disagreement and hence cost — usually rejected, but occasionally accepted with probability *α*, allowing escape from local minima. An opcode mutation discovers that `lea` can fuse a shift and an add; the cost drops; the chain settles into the new basin. Over millions of iterations the chain performs a form of simulated annealing over the discrete space of programs.

The celebrated result: STOKE discovered a 17-instruction sequence computing population count that matches the throughput of the hardware `popcnt` instruction [1] — a sequence no human wrote and no pattern-based compiler pass would derive, because it exploits non-obvious interactions (multiply-based bit tricks, flag reuse) that lie outside any compiler's rule set. The lesson generalizes: *stochastic search explores the space of what the machine can do, while rule-based compilers explore the space of what the compiler writer imagined.*

Convergence diagnostics matter: the chain is ergodic only in the limit, so STOKE runs are judged by best-so-far traces and independent restarts; there is no certificate of optimality, only a monotonically improving incumbent. This is the price of incompleteness, and it is honest.

### 4.2 Equivalence checking: from tests to data-driven proofs

The correctness term `eq(P′, P)` begins life as test disagreement. STOKE maintains a corpus of test cases — random inputs, boundary values (0, ±1, extrema), and counterexamples harvested from failed verifications — and executes both programs in a sandbox. The Hamming distance between outputs, summed over tests, is a smooth-ish proxy for "how wrong" a candidate is, and crucially it is *differentiable in the MCMC sense*: small mutations produce small changes in disagreement, so the cost landscape is searchable rather than a flat plain of "wrong."

But testing cannot certify. The data-driven equivalence checker [6] bridges the gap: it treats the test executions as samples from the joint input-output behavior and learns a candidate invariant/error model, then invokes the SMT solver on the residual — the cases the model cannot decide. In practice this means the solver sees only the genuinely hard equivalence queries, and solver time stays tractable for the 10–50 instruction fragments STOKE targets. Finalists that survive are verified end-to-end; STOKE's published results carry solver-backed equivalence proofs, not merely test evidence [1].

The failure mode to respect: equivalence is *modulo the ISA formalization*. Bugs in the x86-64 semantics model become bugs in every "verified" output. The PLDI 2016 work on stratified synthesis — automatically learning x86-64 instruction semantics from hardware observations — was motivated precisely by this fragility.

### 4.3 Enumerative synthesis at the IR level

Souper's design decision to work at the LLVM IR level rather than on machine code is a deliberate trade of *precision* for *generality* [2]. A superoptimizer that reasons about x86-64 assembly is hostage to one ISA; one that reasons about LLVM IR discovers optimizations applicable to every backend. The cost is fidelity: the IR cost model cannot see instruction selection, register pressure, or port assignment, so an "optimal" IR fragment may lower to mediocre machine code. Souper accepts this and optimizes for what it can measure — typically code size — with striking results: about 7,900 distinct optimizations discovered while building LLVM itself, applied 85,000 times, and a Clang binary 4.4% smaller [2].

The enumeration itself is the synthesis engine. Candidates are generated in order of increasing cost from a grammar of bit-vector operations; each is checked against the LHS with Z3; counterexamples prune the search. The extension to bitwidth-polymorphic instructions lets Souper discover patterns parameterized over type widths, which is why its findings generalize into compiler rules rather than one-off curiosities.

### 4.4 The cost-model frontier

Here is the geometric heart of the thesis. Fix a target function and consider the set of all equivalent programs, each mapped to a point in **cost space**: (size, latency, throughput⁻¹). The *Pareto frontier* is the set of points not dominated on all axes simultaneously. Every superoptimizer, explicitly or not, selects a point on this frontier by scalarizing — choosing weights *w₁, w₂, w₃* and minimizing *w·cost*.

The frontier explains three persistent phenomena:

1. **Microarchitectural disagreement.** A sequence optimal for latency on Intel Skylake (deep reorder buffer, fast `imul`) may be dominated on AMD Zen (different port mapping, different `imul` latency). Static cost models bake in one microarchitecture's numbers; the frontier moves when the silicon changes. This is why STOKE's cost tables are per-microarchitecture and why "the optimal program" is properly "the optimal program *for this chip*."

2. **Latency–throughput tension.** Two equivalent sequences: A has a 3-cycle dependent chain of 6 instructions; B has a 5-cycle chain of 3 instructions. A latency model prefers A; a throughput model (relevant when the fragment executes in a tight unrolled loop saturating ports) may prefer B, because fewer µops per iteration raises sustained IPC. Scalarizing with the wrong weights yields a "superoptimized" program that is slower in situ — the most common real-world failure of superoptimization.

3. **Size as a proxy and its limits.** Souper's size minimization is a superb proxy for I-cache behavior in large binaries (hence the 4.4% Clang shrink [2]) but a poor proxy for hot-loop performance, where a larger, better-scheduled sequence wins. The frontier makes the trade legible: size-optimal and latency-optimal are different vertices, and no scalarization recovers both.

> **Theorem (informal):** *No single static scalar cost function over syntactic program features is order-isomorphic to observed performance across microarchitectures; any superoptimizer's notion of optimality is relative to its cost model, and the Pareto frontier is the complete description of what was optimized.*

The research frontier, accordingly, is *learned and measured* cost models: llvm-mca-style simulators, predictors trained on hardware counters, and in-the-loop timing — each pushing the scalarization closer to silicon at the cost of evaluation time inside an already expensive search.

### 4.5 Peephole superoptimizers as infrastructure

The advisor mode deserves its own analysis because it changes the economics. In this mode the superoptimizer's incompleteness and slowness are amortized over every future compilation — the search runs once, in a lab, and the rules ship. Souper's trajectory is the proof: discoveries became hand-implemented LLVM and MSVC optimizations [2], outliving the tool's own runtime.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

The empirical record of modern superoptimization is unusually concrete — it is measured in shipped compiler improvements and beaten human experts:

- **STOKE vs. production compilers.** Starting from `llvm -O0` x86-64 binaries, STOKE produced sequences matching or outperforming `gcc -O3` and `icc -O3` across the evaluated kernels, and in several cases outperformed expert hand-written assembly [1]. The population-count result — a 17-instruction sequence matching hardware `popcnt` — remains the iconic demonstration that stochastic search can rediscover what took hardware architects a dedicated instruction.
- **Souper vs. LLVM itself.** Applied as an automated pass while building LLVM/Clang, Souper discovered ~7,900 distinct optimizations, applied them ~85,000 times, and produced a Clang binary ~3 MB (4.4%) smaller than LLVM's own optimizer achieved; the same technique surfaced optimizations that LLVM and Microsoft engineers implemented by hand in shipping compilers [2].
- **Verification strength.** STOKE's finalists carry SMT-backed equivalence proofs [6]; Souper emits only solver-verified replacements by construction [2]. The field's soundness posture has thus moved from Massalin's probabilistic test [3] to machine-checked equivalence — while the *search* remains heuristic.
- **Cost-model sensitivity.** Two sequences with latency order *A < B* and throughput order *B < A* exist for any superscalar machine with non-uniform port assignment. Hence no total order over programs induced by a fixed linear scalarization agrees with both latency-optimal and throughput-optimal selection — the Pareto frontier is irreducible.

## 6. Limitations

**Fragment scope.** Every system discussed here operates on loop-free, bounded fragments — basic blocks or small IR regions. Loops, with their invariants and unbounded iteration, defeat both the MCMC cost landscape (a single wrong iteration poisons all tests) and the SMT encoding (unbounded unrolling). Sound loop superoptimization exists in narrow domains (e.g., sandboxed Native Client code) but remains the exception.

**Solver scalability.** Bit-vector SMT encodings of 50+ instruction x86-64 sequences with memory are routinely solver-hard; timeouts are a fact of life. Souper's IR-level abstraction and STOKE's staged filtering are both, at bottom, strategies for keeping the solver's workload small — the solver is the scarcest resource in the pipeline.

**Cost-model fidelity.** Static models mispredict silicon: undocumented µop decompositions, port contention, and store-forwarding stalls are invisible to instruction tables. A superoptimizer can therefore "optimize" a program into a slower one, and only measurement closes the loop.

**ISA formalization risk.** Verified equivalence is relative to a formal ISA model. Where the model is wrong — and x86-64's dark corners guarantee some wrongness — the proof certifies a fiction. Grounding semantics in hardware observation (stratified synthesis) is the principled fix and remains incomplete.

**Diminishing returns at -O3.** Modern compilers already capture the easy wins; superoptimizers increasingly find improvements only in niche kernels or unusual bit-twiddling. The advisor mode is simultaneously the field's greatest success and the reason its marginal returns shrink: every shipped rule is one fewer missed optimization left to find.

## 7. Conclusion

Program superoptimization has matured from Massalin's audacious enumerator [3] into a disciplined engineering practice with two complementary engines and one unifying geometry. The stochastic engine (STOKE [1]) trades completeness for reach; the synthesis engine (Souper [2]) trades reach for certainty, with its discoveries flowing back into production compilers. Both are governed by the cost-model frontier: latency, throughput, and size admit no common optimum, and every "best program" is best only relative to a scalarization of that Pareto surface.

The open problems are correspondingly clear. *Search*: scale stochastic and enumerative methods to loops without drowning the solver. *Verification*: ground ISA models in measured hardware so proofs certify reality. *Cost models*: replace static tables with learned, microarchitecture-aware predictors — or with silicon itself in the loop. Until then, the field's honest claim stands: not optimality, but the systematic discovery of programs no human and no rule-based compiler would have written.
