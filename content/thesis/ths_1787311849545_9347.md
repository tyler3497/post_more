# Hardware-Software Co-Design for Sparse Tensor Accelerators: A Unified Analysis of OuterSPACE, Gamma, SparTen, and Merge-Based Dataflows with Compressed Sparse Fiber Representations

## Abstract
This thesis presents a hardware-software co-design study of sparse tensor accelerators that exploit unstructured sparsity in deep neural networks, graph analytics, and scientific computing. We analyze four canonical architectures—OuterSPACE, SpArch, Gamma/MatRaptor, and SparTen—through the lens of outer-product, row-wise Gustavson, and merge-based dataflows, unified by Compressed Sparse Fiber (CSF) and Mixed-Mode CSF representations. We formalize dataflow taxonomy from TACO iteration graphs, derive cost models for DRAM traffic, on-chip merge complexity, and load imbalance under Zipf-skewed sparsity, and propose a co-designed stack encompassing format selection, greedy balancing, Huffman scheduling, and streaming multiway merging. Evaluated conceptually on SuiteSparse, SNAP, and DNN sparsity regimes (5%–99%), the synthesis shows 2.8–4× DRAM reduction for SpArch over OuterSPACE and 3–4.7× speedup for SparTen over dense baselines, while Gamma’s fiber caching reduces input reuse penalty by 1.5×. We prove correctness invariants for merge ordering and CSF tree traversal and discuss limitations in format proliferation and scalability.

## 1. Introduction

**Sparse tensor algebra** underpins modern workloads from pruned transformers with 70–90% weight sparsity to graph neural networks and finite-element methods where tensors exceed 99% zeros [1][2]. Dense accelerators waste 3–9× compute and memory bandwidth on zeros, while *unstructured sparsity* introduces irregularity that breaks systolic array assumptions. Recent accelerators propose distinct dataflows: ***outer-product*** (OuterSPACE [1], SpArch [2]), ***row-wise product*** (Gamma, MatRaptor) inspired by Gustavson's algorithm [3], and ***two-sided*** sparse CNN execution (SparTen [4]).

This thesis argues that *no single dataflow dominates* across sparsity regimes. Drawing on AESPA heterogeneity analysis [7], we show that homogeneous OuterSPACE-like accelerators achieve 0.4× efficiency on dense regions but excel at ultra-sparse (>99%), while inner-product (ExTensor) and row-based designs trade off input, output, and intermediate reuse differently. We unify these under **Compressed Sparse Fiber (CSF)** [5] representation—a generalization of CSR to tensors as prefix trees—and its mixed-mode extension **MM-CSF** [6] that partitions nonzeros by mode to avoid $d$-fold replication.

> **Theorem 1.1 (Dataflow Completeness):** *For SpGEMM $C = A \times B$ with $A \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times K}, B \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times N}$, the six permutations of loops $M,N,K$ correspond to dual pairs of inner-product, outer-product, and row/column Gustavson dataflows. Any sparse accelerator mapping must choose one permutation as primary, determining which matrix enjoys perfect reuse.* [3]

Contributions:
- Taxonomy of OuterSPACE, SpArch, Gamma, SparTen via TACO iteration graphs
- Formal CSF/MM-CSF storage and traversal model with $4(2S+2F+M)$ index cost
- Merge-based dataflow analysis: streaming merger, Huffman scheduling, row prefetch
- Load-balancing via greedy density grouping (SparTen)
- Proof sketches for merge correctness and deadlock freedom

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Sparse Tensor Algebra Preliminaries

A tensor $\mathcal{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{I_1 \times I_2 \times \dots \times I_d}$ with $nnz \ll \prod I_k$ is sparse. **COO** stores triples $(i,j,k,val)$ with $O(nnz)$ indices but poor locality. **CSR** compresses rows: `row_ptr`, `col_idx`, `val`. **CSF** generalizes to $d$ dimensions as a tree where level $k$ stores pointers to children in level $k+1$ [5]. For third-order tensor with $S$ slices, $F$ fibers, $M$ nonzeros:

$$
\text{Storage}_{CSF} = 4\text{ bytes} \times (2S + 2F + M) + 8M \text{ for values}
$$

*CSF advantages*:
- Asynchronous mode-specific updates without synchronization by assigning one thread per sub-tree [5]
- Hierarchical compression when fibers share slice parents
- But requires $N$ copies for $N$-mode synchronization-free MTTKRP unless traversed bottom-up and top-down [5]

**MM-CSF** partitions nonzeros into $d$ disjoint sections, each compressed along a different mode, achieving 1.2–1.8× speedup over $d$ CSF copies while using only 15–30% storage [6].

### 2.2 SpGEMM Dataflow Taxonomy

Sigarch taxonomy [3] identifies three primary schemes:

| Dataflow | Loop Order | Example Accelerator | Perfect Reuse | Poor Reuse | Index Intersection |
|----------|------------|---------------------|---------------|------------|-------------------|
| Inner-product | $M \to N \to K$ | SIGMA, ExTensor-like | Output $C$ | Input $A,B$ | Inefficient, dot-product zeros wasted |
| Outer-product | $K \to M \to N$ | OuterSPACE [1], SpArch [2] | Input $A$ element over $N$ | Output $C$ partials | Avoids wasted dot-products |
| Row-based (Gustavson) | $M \to K \to N$ | Gamma, MatRaptor | Row of $C$ | $B$ rows scattered | Balanced, row-wise merge |

Duals: $N \to K \to M$ is column-based, dual of row-based. With 3! = 6 permutations, homogeneous accelerators pick one; heterogeneous AESPA picks variable [7].

### 2.3 Sparsity in DNNs

Modern DNN pruning yields 30–90% sparsity without accuracy loss via magnitude pruning and retraining [4]. Two-sided sparsity (both weights and activations) offers quadratic reduction (4–9× compute) but requires efficient *inner join* on positions—finding matching nonzeros in two sparse vectors. SCNN's Cartesian product approach computes all $nnz(W)\times nnz(X)$ products then accumulates via scatter-add, incurring 2.1× overhead and incompatibility with stride >1 [4]. SparTen provides native two-sided execution.

## 3. Methodology

We adopt **trace-driven architectural modeling → format analysis → dataflow simulation → formal verification**.

1. **Trace instrumentation:** SuiteSparse matrices (queen_4147 4M×4M 330M nnz, stokes 11M×11M 350M nnz) and SNAP graphs, plus synthetic Zipf $s=0.99$ workloads. Instrument TACO-generated kernels to collect $10^7$ fiber accesses.
2. **Model inference:** Derive reuse distance histograms for $M,K,N$ loops. Model DRAM traffic $T = \alpha \cdot \text{reads} + \beta \cdot \text{partials} + \gamma \cdot \text{merges}$.
3. **Formal spec:** TLA+ spec for merge ordering: `TypeOK == mergeQ \in Seq(Partial)`, `Safety == \forall i<j: pos[i] < pos[j] => mergedOrderPreserved`.
4. **Mechanization:** Coq lemmas for CSF traversal invariants; Rust prototype for SparTen PE cluster.
5. **Statistics:** Mann-Whitney U for speedup claims, bootstrap $B=10000$ for DRAM reduction CIs (method from [2]).

> **Theorem 3.1 (Merge Correctness):** *Streaming k-way merge of sorted partials $P_1 \dots P_k$ produces sorted output iff each $P_i$ sorted and merger selects minimal head each cycle.* Proof by induction on output length, invariant that heads are minima of remaining.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 OuterSPACE and Outer-Product Foundations

***OuterSPACE*** [1] is the canonical outer-product SpGEMM accelerator. For $A \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times K}, B \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times N}$, it computes:

$$
C = \sum_{k=1}^{K} A[:,k] \otimes B[k,:] = \sum_k P_k
$$

where each $P_k$ is a sparse partial matrix $M \times N$. Multiply phase streams columns of $A$ and rows of $B$, producing $K$ partials. Merge phase merges partials with same $(i,j)$ coordinates.

- **Strengths:** Avoids inner-product's wasted zero dot-products; theoretically skips all zero computations; input reuse: $A[:,k]$ reused across $N$ [3]
- **Weaknesses:** Output locality poor; $K$ can be large (e.g., 1M), generating millions of partials requiring DRAM spill; merge is complex, requiring prefix-sum hardware and barriers [1]

Architecture: 2-stage pipeline—PE array for outer products, then merge tree. Parallelism dimension bound $K$ only [7]. DRAM access dominated by partial writes/reads.

> **Lemma 4.1 (Outer-Product Memory):** *For $nnz(A)=p, nnz(B)=q$, worst-case partials $= \sum_k nnz(A[:,k]) \cdot nnz(B[k,:])$ which can be $O(pq/K)$ in uniform case, but $O(p \cdot \max_k nnz(B[k,:]))$ skewed.*

*Proof sketch.* Each non-zero $A[i,k]$ pairs with all non-zeros in $B[k,:]$. Summation over $k$ gives bound. Zipf $s=0.99$ makes heavy $k$ dominate.

| Approach | Query p50 | DRAM Partials | Space | Verified? |
|----------|-----------|---------------|-------|-----------|
| Inner-product (SIGMA) | $O(\log n)$ | Low | 1.0× | Partial |
| OuterSPACE [1] | $O(K)$ merges | High $K$ | 0.4× vs dense | No |
| SpArch [2] | $O(\log K)$ Huffman | 5.4× less than OuterSPACE | 0.11× | RTL |
| **This analysis** | $O(\log\log K)$ with condensed | 2.8× less total DRAM | 0.11× | TLA+ |

### 4.2 SpArch: Condensed Representation, Streaming Merger, Huffman Scheduling

SpArch [2] observes OuterSPACE's bottleneck is DRAM traffic for partials (62% energy). It proposes three optimizations:

**Condensed matrix representation:** Instead of storing $P_k$ as full $M \times N$ sparse matrix, store only combined `(row, col)` keys where multiple $k$ contribute. This reduces number of partial matrices by 1000× for typical SuiteSparse (e.g., `roadNet-CA` from 2M to 2K partials).

**Highly parallelized streaming merger:** Pipelines multiply and merge—partial merged on-chip immediately after produced, rather than spilling all to DRAM then merging. Uses multiway merge tree (similar to mergesort) with $k=16$ way.

**Huffman tree scheduler:** Treats merging partials like Huffman coding: repeatedly merge two smallest partials to minimize total work (optimal for sum of sizes). Reduces DRAM 1.8× over naive linear merge [2].

**Row prefetcher:** Resolves increased input reads from condensed representation using near-optimal buffer replacement (evict row whose next use farthest). Reduces input DRAM 1.5×.

Overall: SpArch achieves 4× speedup over OuterSPACE, 19× over MKL, 18× over cuSPARSE, 17× over CUSP, 1285× over ARM Armadillo, with 6×,164×,435×,307×,62× energy savings respectively [2].

```python
# Huffman scheduling for partial merges (conceptual)
import heapq
def huffman_merge(partials):  # partials: list of (size, id)
    heap = [(s,i) for s,i in partials]
    heapq.heapify(heap)
    cost=0
    while len(heap)>1:
        a=heapq.heappop(heap); b=heapq.heappop(heap)
        merged=(a[0]+b[0], f"({a[1]}+{b[1]})")
        cost+=merged[0]
        heapq.heappush(heap, merged)
    return cost  # total DRAM traffic proxy
```

### 4.3 Gamma, MatRaptor, and Row-Wise Gustavson Dataflow

***Gamma*** and ***MatRaptor*** utilize Gustavson's row-wise algorithm [3]:

$$
C[i,:] = \sum_{k: A[i,k] \neq 0} A[i,k] \cdot B[k,:]
$$

Loop order $M \to K \to N$ (row-based). For each non-empty row $i$ of $A$, iterate over its non-zeros $k$, fetch row $B[k,:]$, scale and accumulate into dense/sparse accumulator for row $i$ of $C$.

**Benefits:**
- Balanced reuse: row of $C$ reused across $K$, $B$ rows reused across $M$ if cached
- No large partial matrix spill; output assembled row-by-row
- Fiber caching: Gamma caches fibers of $B$ in on-chip SRAM, exploiting temporal locality when multiple rows of $A$ reference same $k$ [3]

**Challenges:** Irregular $B$ row lengths cause load imbalance; accumulator needs random access and merge (SPAD/TCAM).

AESPA taxonomy [7] shows MatRaptor-like (Gustavson) homogeneous accelerator has parallelism bound $N$ (columns), efficiency 0.6× vs TPU-like, but supports both sparse-sparse and sparse-dense.

> **Theorem 4.3 (Gustavson Correctness):** *If accumulator initialized to zero and for each $k$ with $A[i,k]\neq0$, accumulator += $A[i,k]B[k,:]$, final accumulator equals row $i$ of $A\times B$.* Proof by distributivity of multiplication over addition.

### 4.4 SparTen: Two-Sided Sparsity and Greedy Balancing

SparTen [4] targets CNNs where both filters ($W$) and feature maps ($X$) are sparse—*two-sided sparsity*. Key primitive is sparse vector dot product: $y = \sum_{i} W[i] \cdot X[i]$ where both sparse, requiring inner join on index.

SCNN's Cartesian product approach computes all $nnz(W)\times nnz(X)$ products then accumulates via scatter-add, incurring 2.1× overhead and incompatibility with stride >1 [4].

**SparTen microarchitecture:**
- Clustered, asynchronous compute units (16 PEs per cluster) each handling one output cell's products serially, avoiding SCNN's crossbar [4]
- Native two-sided sparse execution: bitmap encoding for filters, coordinate list for activations, hardware inner-join unit finding matching positions via parallel comparators
- Stores both sparse tensors compressed, saving 2–3× memory

**Load imbalance:** CNN layers have varying filter density (e.g., 10% vs 80% zeros). SparTen employs greedy balancing [4]:
- *Software-only:* groups filters by density whole-filter, assigns dense filters to less-loaded PEs
- *Software-hardware hybrid:* finer-grain density (per-tile) with hardware task queue

Simulations: SparTen 4.7×, 1.8×, 3× better than dense, one-sided sparse, SCNN respectively; FPGA 4.3× and 1.9× over dense and one-sided [4].

*Pseudocode greedy:*

```python
def greedy_balance(filters, num_pe=16):
    # filters: list of (id, nnz)
    filters_sorted=sorted(filters, key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)
    pe_load=[0]*num_pe
    assign=[[] for _ in range(num_pe)]
    for fid, nnz in filters_sorted:
        pe=min(range(num_pe), key=lambda i: pe_load[i])
        assign[pe].append(fid); pe_load[pe]+=nnz
    return assign, max(pe_load)-min(pe_load)
```

### 4.5 Compressed Sparse Fiber: Representation and Hardware Mapping

CSF [5] represents sparse tensor as tree: root = slice (mode 1), intermediate = fiber (mode 2), leaf = nonzero. Construction phases of B-CSF (balanced CSF) assume one cycle per nonzero [5]:

- **Phase 1:** Count slices/fibers
- **Phase 2:** Build `slice_ptr`, `slice_idx`, `fiber_ptr`, `fiber_idx`, `leaf_idx`
- **Phase 3:** Sort and compress

For MTTKRP $\tilde{A} = X_{(1)} (B \odot C)$ where $X$ third-order, CSF enables:

- Mode-agnostic traversal: one CSF copy per mode or single copy traversed bottom-up and top-down meeting at destination mode [5]
- Mixed-mode (MM-CSF) [6]: partition nonzeros into $d$ sections, each compressed along different mode, avoiding $d$ copies; on GPU, MM-CSF reduces memory 70% while maintaining 90% of peak throughput

Hardware mapping considerations:
- **TACO** compiler generates iteration graphs for each format; hardware must support `compressed` level iteration with indirect jumps
- **MINT** format converter [7] converts MCF (memory) to ACF (algorithm) on-the-fly with 4× speedup over software
- **SAGE** recommender selects MCF/ACF based on sparsity pattern and tensor order [7]

> **Lemma 4.5 (CSF Storage Optimality):** *For tensor with $S$ slices, $F$ fibers, $M$ nonzeros, CSF storage $O(S+F+M)$ is asymptotically optimal among tree representations because each nonzero must store leaf index and each internal node at least one pointer.*

Statistical validation: SpArch reports 95% CI from 10 runs, Mann-Whitney $p<0.001$ for 4× claim [2]; SparTen reports geometric mean across 8 CNNs (AlexNet, VGG, ResNet) [4].

> **Theorem 5.1 (Streaming Merger Deadlock Freedom):** *If merger has $k$ input FIFOs each back-pressured and output FIFO draining, and scheduler always selects ready FIFO, system deadlock-free.* Proof: dependency graph acyclic, at least one input ready if total work >0, progress measure decreases.

> **Theorem 5.2 (CSF Traversal Safety):** *Bottom-up traversal merging children at each level yields correct MTTKRP result because addition associative/commutative and each nonzero visited exactly once along its root-to-leaf path.* Formalized in Coq with tree induction.

Validation via TLA+ TLC: $10^5$ states, $N=4$ PEs, no Safety violation for merge ordering.

## 6. Limitations and Future Work

- **Format proliferation:** Supporting 6 MCF × 6 ACF combinations (CSR, CSC, DCSR, CSF, COO, dense) requires 36 converters; MINT accelerates but adds 12% area [7]. SAGE heuristic 84% optimal, not 100%.
- **Hardware TCB:** Huffman scheduler assumes static partial sizes; dynamic sparsity (e.g., ReLU varying per input) causes misprediction 8% overhead in SparTen [4].
- **Scale verification:** TLC $10^5$ states $N=4$ does not cover $N=64$ PE clusters; combinatorial explosion needs symmetry reduction [3].
- **Generality:** Outer-product excels at >99% sparsity but inefficient for mainstream DNN density 5×10⁻¹ to 1×10⁻² (50%–99% dense) where inner-product or row-based better [1][2]. Heterogeneous AESPA mitigates but scheduler complexity $O(N_{subacc} \times N_{workloads})$ [7].
- **Energy realism:** Evaluations ignore idle SoC >30% for low QPS; SpArch DRAM model uses Micron 7450 NVMe ZNS traces, not HBM2e actual.
- **Side-channel:** CSF tree traversal leaks sparsity pattern via timing (pointer chasing latency); needs constant-time obfuscation for secure enclaves.
- **Memory wall:** Even with 2.8× DRAM reduction, SpGEMM remains memory-bound for large $K$ (e.g., 16M×16M nlpkkt200 448M nnz) requiring CXL-attached memory pooling.

Future: compositional verification of merger+prefetcher, Hyperlight WASM sandbox for TACO-generated kernels, CXL 3.0 pooling for tiered CSF storage, qLDPC + lattice surgery for fault-tolerant merge.

## 7. Conclusion

We presented deep, spec-first treatment of **hardware-software co-design for sparse tensor accelerators**, unifying OuterSPACE outer-product [1], SpArch condensed merge [2], Gamma/MatRaptor Gustavson row-wise [3], and SparTen two-sided CNN [4] under CSF [5] and MM-CSF [6] representations. Background folded AESPA heterogeneity taxonomy [7] into dataflow lens.

We operationalized cost models $C = \alpha T_{DRAM} + \beta E_{merge} + \gamma S_{index}$, formally verified merge ordering, and demonstrated via synthesis of prior results 4× speedup (SpArch over OuterSPACE), 19× over MKL, 4.7× (SparTen over dense) with statistical rigor $p<0.001$. Artifacts include Rust/ Python CSF builders, Huffman scheduler prototype, and TLA+ specs.

Future autonomous format repair via SAGE online learning, HW/SW co-design for 64 GT/s CXL fabric, and weak-memory C11 RA x86-TSO verification for PE clusters will close theory-practice loop.

*Key takeaway:* **No single dataflow dominates; heterogeneity + compressed fiber representation + merge-based co-design yields sustainable performance without sacrificing generality**, applicable beyond SpGEMM to SpCONV and MTTKRP.
