# On the Expressivity of Graph Neural Networks: The Weisfeiler–Lehman Hierarchy, Oversmoothing Dynamics, and Spectral versus Message-Passing Architectures

## Abstract

This thesis develops a unified account of the *expressive power* of graph neural networks (GNNs) through three interlocking lenses: the **Weisfeiler–Lehman (WL) hierarchy** of graph isomorphism tests, the **oversmoothing** pathology of deep architectures, and the **spectral versus message-passing** divide in filter design. We prove, following Xu et al., that standard message-passing neural networks (MPNNs) are bounded in distinguishing power by the 1-dimensional WL test, and we characterize how higher-order variants — *k*-GNNs, subgraph GNNs, and the complete subgraph-WL hierarchy — purchase strictly greater expressivity at exponential computational cost. We then analyze oversmoothing as a contraction of node features onto a low-dimensional invariant subspace, deriving the exponential rate *(sλ)^l* of Oono and Suzuki and the Dirichlet-energy formalism of Cai and Wang. Finally, we contrast spectral GNNs, whose expressive strength lies in *function approximation on fixed graphs* rather than *graph distinguishability*, yielding a coherent map of what GNNs can and cannot compute.

## 1 Introduction

Graph neural networks have become the dominant paradigm for learning on relational data, powering molecular property prediction, recommender systems, traffic forecasting, and program analysis [1][6]. Yet beneath their empirical success lies a fundamental theoretical question: **what functions can a GNN actually compute?** Unlike sequence models, where universal approximation is well understood, graph models must contend with the *graph isomorphism* barrier — the requirement that outputs be invariant (graph-level tasks) or equivariant (node-level tasks) under node relabeling — and with the subtle ways in which architectural choices interact with graph structure.

The canonical yardstick for this question is the **Weisfeiler–Lehman (WL) test**, a classical combinatorial algorithm for graph isomorphism testing [2]. Its discovery as an upper bound on message-passing expressivity reshaped the field: it established that the workhorse architectures — GCNs, GraphSAGE, GATs — share a common, surprisingly low ceiling on their ability to distinguish non-isomorphic graphs [1]. The past half-decade has accordingly been defined by three converging programs:

1. **Ascending the WL hierarchy**: higher-order networks (*k*-GNNs [2], subgraph GNNs [3]) that break the 1-WL barrier at exponential computational cost.
2. **Understanding depth limits**: the *oversmoothing* phenomenon, in which deep GNNs collapse node representations into an indistinguishable blur [4][5][6].
3. **Spectral versus spatial design**: Laplacian filters with universal signal approximation but, historically, the same 1-WL discrimination bound — a tension recent full-spectrum constructions resolve [7].

This thesis synthesizes these programs into a single narrative. Our central claim is that *expressivity*, *depth stability*, and *filter parameterization* are three faces of one mathematical object: the operator algebra of message propagation on graphs. A network that is maximally expressive under 1-WL refinement is precisely the one most vulnerable to oversmoothing, because both properties follow from repeated application of the same low-pass propagation operator; spectral designs escape one half of this dilemma only by trading away universality on the other.

The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 fixes notation and reviews the WL test, the MPNN framework, and spectral graph filtering. Section 3 states the formal methodology: definitions of expressivity, the inclusion theorem bounding MPNNs by 1-WL, and the dynamical setup for oversmoothing. Section 4 develops the deep theory across four subsections: the 1-WL ceiling and its injective-aggregation refinements; higher-order and subgraph hierarchies; the spectral/spatial comparison; and oversmoothing as a contraction process. Section 5 presents empirical evidence and proof sketches of the central theorems. Section 6 discusses limitations and open problems. Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Notation

Let *G = (V, E)* be an undirected graph with *n = |V|* nodes, adjacency matrix **A** ∈ {0,1}^(n×n), degree matrix **D** with *D_vv = deg(v)*, and node feature matrix **X** ∈ ℝ^(n×d). The *combinatorial Laplacian* is **L = D − A**; its symmetric normalization is **L̃ = I − D^(−1/2) A D^(−1/2)**, with eigendecomposition **L̃ = UΛUᵀ** and spectrum *0 = λ_1 ≤ λ_2 ≤ … ≤ λ_n ≤ 2*. The *augmented* (renormalized) adjacency **Â = D̃^(−1/2) Ã D̃^(−1/2)** with *Ã = A + I*, *D̃ = D + I* is the propagation operator of the GCN [8].

### 2.2 The 1-Weisfeiler–Lehman Test

The 1-WL test, also called *color refinement* or *naive vertex classification*, assigns initial colors *c_v^(0)* to nodes (uniform, or by label) and iteratively refines:

> *c_v^(t+1) = HASH( c_v^(t), { { c_u^(t) : u ∈ N(v) } } )*

where *{{·}}* denotes a multiset and *HASH* injectively maps the pair to a new color. The process terminates when the color partition stabilizes. Two graphs are declared *possibly isomorphic* if their stabilized color histograms coincide. 1-WL is necessary but not sufficient for isomorphism: it fails on regular graphs with identical degree sequences and on the *Cai–Fürer–Immerman* constructions [2].

The *k-dimensional* WL test (*k*-WL) colors *k*-tuples of vertices and refines using neighborhood structure across coordinates, forming a strict hierarchy 1-WL < 2-WL < … < *n*-WL in distinguishing power [2][3].

### 2.3 Message-Passing Neural Networks

The MPNN framework [1] updates node representations by:

> **h**_v^(l+1) = φ^(l)( **h**_v^(l), AGG^(l)( { { ψ^(l)(**h**_u^(l)) : u ∈ N(v) } } ) )

Instantiations include the GCN **H**^(l+1) = σ(Â**H**^(l)**W**^(l)) [8], GraphSAGE, GAT, and the Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN) **h**_v^(l+1) = MLP^(l)( (1+ε^(l))**h**_v^(l) + Σ_{u∈N(v)} **h**_u^(l) ) [1]. A graph-level readout applies permutation-invariant pooling over final node embeddings.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a *theoretical synthesis*: our methodology is formal analysis of model classes rather than a single new experiment. We adopt three formal lenses, each with its own definition of what it means for a GNN to be "expressive":

**Lens A — Distinguishability expressivity.** A model class ℱ *distinguishes* graphs *G₁, G₂* if ∃f ∈ ℱ with *f(G₁) ≠ f(G₂)*. A model is *at most as expressive as* test *T* when every pair distinguished by the model is also distinguished by *T*. This is the WL-framework of [1][2][3]: expressivity is measured by the equivalence relation induced on the graph space.

**Lens B — Signal-approximation expressivity.** A model class ℱ is *universal* on a fixed graph *G* if it can approximate any continuous equivariant function on node signals arbitrarily well. This is the spectral-framework lens [9][7]: the question is not *which graphs* can be told apart but *which functions on a given graph* can be learned.

**Lens C — Dynamical expressivity.** A deep model preserves *discriminative information* across layers if node embeddings do not collapse onto a low-dimensional subspace. We quantify collapse via the distance *d_ℳ*(**X**) to the *oversmoothing subspace* ℳ [4] and the *Dirichlet energy* *E*(**X**) = tr(**X**ᵀ**L̃X**) [6], and we analyze the per-layer contraction factors of propagation, linear maps, and activations separately.

Our synthesis strategy is to show that Lens A and Lens C are dual views of the same operator: the refined colors of 1-WL are exactly the fixed points toward which repeated low-pass propagation drives embeddings, so that *maximally 1-WL-expressive aggregation* and *maximal oversmoothing* share a common mechanism. Spectral designs (Lens B) escape this duality only partially, trading graph-distinguishing power for approximation universality — until recent full-spectrum models reunite the two [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The 1-WL Ceiling of Message Passing

The foundational result of modern GNN theory is the inclusion theorem of Xu et al. [1]:

> **Theorem (MPNN ⊆ 1-WL).** *Let* ℱ_MPNN *be the class of message-passing GNNs with arbitrary depth, width, and learnable parameters. If two graphs are indistinguishable by the 1-WL test, then every f ∈ ℱ_MPNN assigns them identical outputs.*

The proof is by induction: the WL color *c_v^(t)* refines every possible multiset of neighbor colors, while any MPNN computes a *coarsening* of that refinement, because aggregation functions are multiset functions and the WL hash is injective. Consequently, no choice of width, depth, nonlinearity, or readout can rescue an MPNN from 1-WL's blind spots.

The canonical counterexample is the pair **decalin vs. bicyclopentyl**: two 3-regular graphs on 10 vertices whose color histograms coincide under 1-WL, hence are invisible to every MPNN, yet are non-isomorphic and correspond to chemically distinct molecules (C₁₀H₁₈ isomers) [1]. Message passing cannot count triangles or detect 3-regular structure beyond degree sequences — a concrete consequence with real chemical significance.

Xu et al. further identified the *sharpest* model within the 1-WL bound: the **Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN)** [1]. Because the WL hash is injective on (color, neighbor-multiset) pairs, an MPNN matches 1-WL exactly when its aggregation and update are injective on multisets — which GIN's MLP over *Σ_{u∈N(v)}* **h**_u achieves, making it the theoretical optimum for message passing. Weaker aggregators (mean, max) provably collapse distinct multisets: mean cannot distinguish {1,1} from {1}, and max cannot distinguish {1,2} from {1,2,2}.

> **Key insight.** *The 1-WL bound is not a statement about insufficient depth or data; it is a statement about the algebraic structure of neighborhood aggregation itself. Expressivity beyond it requires changing the computation, not scaling it.*

| Architecture | Aggregation | Expressivity bound | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| GCN [8] | degree-normalized mean | ≤ 1-WL (strictly weaker) | conflates multisets, e.g. {1,1} vs {1} |
| GraphSAGE-mean | mean | ≤ 1-WL (strictly weaker) | same as above |
| GAT | attention-weighted mean | ≤ 1-WL (strictly weaker) | attention is a multiset function |
| GIN [1] | sum + MLP | = 1-WL | blind to regular-graph structure |
| *k*-GNN [2] | *k*-tuple aggregation | = *k*-WL | *O(n^k)* complexity |
| Subgraph GNN [3] | bag of rooted subgraphs | ≤ SSWL hierarchy | gap to 2-FWL persists |

### 4.2 Climbing the Hierarchy: Higher-Order and Subgraph Architectures

Escaping 1-WL requires computation on *higher-order objects*. Morris et al. [2] introduced **k-GNNs**, which maintain representations for *k*-element vertex subsets and aggregate over *k*-subsets sharing *k−1* vertices, proving *k*-GNNs are as expressive as *k*-WL.

A more scalable escape route is the **subgraph GNN** paradigm: extract a bag of subgraphs (e.g., one rooted *k*-hop ego-network per node, or node-marked subgraphs), run an MPNN on each, and pool. Zhang et al. [3] provide the definitive theoretical map: they define **Subgraph WL (SWL)** tests, prove that *every* node-based subgraph GNN falls into one of exactly *six* SWL equivalence classes, identify the maximal class **SSWL**, and establish a tight upper bound relating all SWL algorithms to localized versions of the *Folklore WL (FWL)* test. Their hierarchy is *complete* — no node-based subgraph design can exceed SSWL — yet SSWL itself remains strictly weaker than 2-FWL, exposing an inherent, provable gap.

The practical message of the hierarchy literature is sobering:

1. **Expressivity is monotone in cost.** Every known strict increase in distinguishing power corresponds to an exponential or high-polynomial cost — *O(n^k)* tuples, *O(n)* subgraph bags, or *O(n²)* pairwise terms.
2. **The 2-FWL frontier is stubborn.** Local 2-GNN variants approach but do not reach 2-FWL; the gap concerns global interactions localized models cannot capture [3][7].
3. **Generalization degrades with expressivity.** The number of WL-equivalence classes directly bounds a model's Rademacher complexity: finer colorings yield *weaker* generalization guarantees. Expressivity is not free even statistically.

### 4.3 Spectral versus Message-Passing Architectures

Spectral and message-passing designs answer *different questions*, and conflating them is the source of much confusion in the literature.

**What spectral models do well (Lens B).** On a *fixed* graph, polynomial spectral filters are universal approximators of equivariant signal functions: Wang and Zhang proved that spectral GNNs can approximate any continuous node-signal function arbitrarily well. ChebNet [9] made this practical via Chebyshev polynomials; subsequent designs (APPNP, GPR-GNN, BernNet, ChebNetII) learned *arbitrary* filter shapes — high-pass, band-pass, comb — freeing the model from the low-pass bias of the GCN's first-order filter *g(λ) ≈ 1 − λ*. This matters on **heterophilic** graphs, where neighbors differ in label and low-pass smoothing destroys signal: learned high-pass filters recover performance that MPNNs cannot [7].

**What spectral models do poorly (Lens A).** The same universality says nothing about *graph distinguishability*. Wang and Zhang further proved that standard spectral GNNs — those whose filters depend only on the Laplacian spectrum — are **bounded by 1-WL** for graph-level discrimination: two 1-WL-indistinguishable graphs with identical spectra (e.g., cospectral pairs) defeat them. The spectrum is a coarse invariant; filters of eigenvalues alone cannot see the refined color structure.

**The reunion.** The recent **FSPECGNN** construction [7] resolves the tension by lifting the signal domain: it extends univariate eigenvalue filters *g(λ)* to *bivariate* filters over eigenvalue *pairs* *g(λ_i, λ_j)* on the node-pair domain *V × V*, proving expressivity up to **Local 2-GNN** — strictly beyond 1-WL — while remaining scalable on heterophilic benchmarks. The lesson is architectural: escaping 1-WL requires the model to see *pairs*, whether through *k*-tuples, subgraph bags, or spectral pair-filters. The medium (spatial vs. spectral) matters less than the *order of the objects* the model computes on.

A final contrast concerns **inductivity**. Message-passing models transfer across graph sizes because aggregation is local; spectral models, tied to a specific eigendecomposition, are inherently transductive. Polynomial filters (ChebNet-style) recover partial inductivity through *K*-hop localization — the GCN's *K = 1* filter [8][9] is simultaneously the most inductive and the least expressive spectral design.

### 4.4 Oversmoothing as a Contraction Process

If expressivity asks what GNNs can *distinguish*, oversmoothing asks what deep GNNs can *retain*. Li et al. [5] first observed that GCN propagation **H** ← Â**H** is a form of *Laplacian smoothing*: repeated application drives features toward topology-determined fixed points that ignore input features. Oono and Suzuki [4] made this rigorous:

> **Theorem (Oono–Suzuki).** *Let d_ℳ*(**X**^(l)) *be the Frobenius distance of layer-l embeddings to the oversmoothing subspace ℳ = {* **D̃**^(1/2)**1** ⊗ **w** : **w** ∈ ℝ^d *}. If s = sup_l s_l is the supremum of the products of largest singular values of layer weight matrices and λ is the second-largest eigenvalue of Â (i.e., the spectral gap), then d_ℳ*(**X**^(l)) ≤ (sλ)^l · d_ℳ*(**X**^(0)). *When sλ < 1, embeddings converge to ℳ **exponentially** in depth.*

In words: every layer contracts the component of the features orthogonal to the smoothing subspace by a factor *sλ*; activations (ReLU, being 1-Lipschitz) cannot expand it back. The network's only defense is large weight norms (*s* large) — which training typically penalizes — or a small spectral gap (*λ* close to 1, as in expander graphs). Cai and Wang [6] gave a cleaner formulation via **Dirichlet energy** *E*(**X**) = tr(**X**ᵀ**L̃X**), proving three lemmas: propagation contracts energy by *(1−λ)²* where *λ* is the smallest nonzero Laplacian eigenvalue; linear maps scale it by *‖W‖₂²*; ReLU/leaky-ReLU never increase it. Under the spectral condition on weights, *E* → 0 exponentially, and *E* = 0 means all node embeddings are identical up to the trivial eigenvector — total loss of discriminative power.

This is the **duality** promised in Section 3: the same low-pass operator Â that makes message passing a coarsening of 1-WL refinement (Lens A) is the contraction whose fixed point is ℳ (Lens C). *The 1-WL-optimal GIN and the maximally-oversmoothed deep GCN differ only in whether injectivity is preserved across layers.* Concretely:

- **Oversmoothing** (features become too similar) must be distinguished from **over-squashing** (long-range information compressed through bottlenecks): one is excessive mixing, the other insufficient bandwidth.
## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Proof Sketch: MPNN ⊆ 1-WL

*Induction on layers.* Base: *c_v^(0)* and **h**_v^(0) are functions of the same input features, hence constant on WL-color classes. Inductive step: the WL update computes *c_v^(t+1)* = HASH(*c_v^(t)*, multiset of neighbor colors), an *injective* function of the pair, while the MPNN update computes **h**_v^(t+1) = φ(**h**_v^(t), AGG(multiset of neighbor embeddings)) — a *function* of the same pair. Since HASH is injective, equal WL colors imply equal MPNN outputs; the converse need not hold. Thus the MPNN's partition is coarser than WL's at every layer, and any readout preserves the coarsening. ∎

### 5.2 Proof Sketch: Exponential Oversmoothing

Following [4]: write one GNN layer as *f_l*(**X**) = σ(Â**XW**_l) and decompose **X** = **X**_ℳ + **X**_⊥ relative to ℳ. Â fixes the trivial eigenvector **D̃**^(1/2)**1** and contracts the orthogonal complement by *λ*, the second eigenvalue; right-multiplication by **W**_l scales distances by at most its largest singular value *s_l*; ReLU's 1-Lipschitzness gives *d_ℳ*(σ(·)) ≤ d_ℳ*(·). Chaining: *d_ℳ*(**X**^(l)) ≤ s_l·λ·d_ℳ*(**X**^(l−1)), whence the *(sλ)^l* bound. Cai–Wang's energy version [6] replaces subspace distance with the quadratic form *E* and obtains the same exponential rate from the three lemmas of §4.4. ∎

| Result | Dataset / setting | Finding |

| Xu et al. [1] | synthetic + MUTAG/PTC | GIN matches 1-WL; mean/max aggregators fail on multiset probes; all MPNNs fail the 3-regular pair |
| Morris et al. [2] | QM9, graph classification | *k*-GNNs (k=2,3) separate graphs 1-WL cannot; runtime grows as *O(n^k)* |
| Zhang et al. [3] | ZINC | SSWL-inspired subgraph GNNs beat prior architectures with simpler design; 2-FWL gap remains |
| Oono & Suzuki [4] | Cora/Citeseer/Pubmed | GCN accuracy degrades with depth; measured contraction matches *(sλ)^l* |
| FSPECGNN [7] | heterophilic benchmarks | bivariate spectral filters match Local 2-GNN expressivity; beat 1-WL spectral baselines |
| GPR-GNN / APPNP | heterophilic graphs | learned high-pass filters recover signal where Â-smoothing collapses |

A representative depth-degradation curve (GCN on Cora, after [4][5]): accuracy rises to ~4 layers, then falls monotonically as *E*(**X**^(l)) → 0 — the empirical signature of the contraction theorem.

```python
# Dirichlet energy as an oversmoothing diagnostic (after Cai & Wang [6])
import numpy as np

def dirichlet_energy(X: np.ndarray, L_tilde: np.ndarray) -> float:
    """E(X) = tr(X^T L~ X); E -> 0 signals total oversmoothing."""
    return float(np.trace(X.T @ L_tilde @ X))

def layer_energies(model, X0, A_hat, L_tilde, depth: int):
    energies, X = [dirichlet_energy(X0, L_tilde)], X0
    for l in range(depth):
        X = np.maximum(A_hat @ X @ model.W[l], 0.0)  # GCN layer + ReLU
        energies.append(dirichlet_energy(X, L_tilde))
    return energies  # expect geometric decay ~ (s*lambda)^{2l}
```

## 6 Limitations

1. **WL-distinguishability is not the whole of expressivity.** Real graphs carry continuous features, where WL analysis (defined for discrete colors) is only a coarse proxy; continuous extensions exist but complicate the hierarchy. Moreover, distinguishing *more* graphs is not always useful: the Rademacher-complexity results [cf. 2510.10101] prove that finer WL colorings *weaken* generalization guarantees, so maximal expressivity can be actively harmful on small datasets.

2. **Oversmoothing theory assumes simplified dynamics.** The Oono–Suzuki bound treats ReLU via Lipschitzness and assumes uniform weight norms; architectures with normalization, residuals, attention, and learned diffusion deviate from these assumptions, and recent work argues the classical narrative overstates oversmoothing relative to optimization and over-squashing.

3. **Spectral methods pay in scalability.** Full eigendecomposition is *O(n³)*; polynomial filters avoid it but reintroduce localization limits, and even pair-domain spectral models [7] face quadratic memory. Inductivity across graphs remains weaker than for MPNNs.

4. **The 2-FWL frontier.** No scalable architecture is known to reach full 2-FWL expressivity [3]; whether a practical model can close the SSWL→2-FWL gap without *O(n²)*–*O(n³)* cost is open.

5. **Heterophily and homophily assumptions.** Much of the spectral-vs-spatial debate is benchmark-dependent: low-pass MPNNs are optimal under homophily, high-pass spectral filters under heterophily, and real graphs mix both regimes at different scales.

## 7 Conclusion

We have traced a single mathematical thread through three literatures: the *propagation operator* determines simultaneously what a GNN can distinguish (the 1-WL ceiling and its higher-order escapes), what it retains with depth (exponential oversmoothing toward ℳ), and what it can approximate (spectral universality on fixed graphs versus graph-level blindness). The field's trajectory — from GIN's optimal 1-WL aggregation [1], through the *k*-WL and subgraph hierarchies [2][3], to full-spectrum pair filters [7] — is the story of learning to compute on *higher-order objects* while managing the contraction dynamics that such computation unleashes [4][5][6]. The open frontier is not more expressivity per se, but *expressivity with guarantees*: models that climb the WL hierarchy, resist oversmoothing, and generalize — a three-way trade-off whose Pareto surface is only beginning to be charted.
