# Level Set Methods for Moving Interfaces: Hamilton–Jacobi Formulation, Fast Marching, and Narrow-Band Computation

## Abstract

The motion of interfaces under curvature-dependent and prescribed velocities is central to fluid mechanics, computer vision, materials science, and computational geometry. The level set method of Osher and Sethian [1] recasts the Lagrangian motion of a codimension-one front as the Eulerian evolution of the zero level set of a scalar function $\phi(x,t)$, reducing interface tracking to the Hamilton–Jacobi equation $\phi_t + F|\nabla\phi| = 0$, whose viscosity solution selects the physically correct front through singularities and topological changes. This thesis develops the full apparatus: implicit signed-distance representation; monotone upwind and high-order ENO/WENO schemes; the Sussman–Smereka–Osher reinitialization PDE [3]; the Adalsteinsson–Sethian narrow-band method [4]; and Sethian's fast marching method for the eikonal equation, with $O(N\log N)$ complexity [5]. Applications to multiphase flow [3] and Chan–Vese segmentation [6] are analyzed alongside convergence theory, complexity benchmarks, and limitations including mass loss and the CFL penalty.

## 1. Introduction

Many of the hardest problems in continuum simulation share a geometric skeleton: a hypersurface $\Gamma(t) \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ moves under a velocity law coupling local geometry — normal direction, curvature — to external physics such as advection, surface tension, or image intensity. Crystal growth, flame propagation, oil–water interfaces, and active contours in medical imaging all instantiate this structure. Before 1988, the dominant strategies were *Lagrangian markers*: discretize the front by a mesh and advect each node along its normal. These front-tracking methods are accurate while the interface stays smooth, but they collapse when topology changes. Merging droplets, pinching filaments, and nucleating holes demand *surgery* — explicit detection, deletion, and re-connection of mesh elements — whose bookkeeping is fragile and dimension-dependent [1, 8].

The breakthrough of Osher and Sethian [1] was to abandon the front as the primary computational object. The front is viewed *implicitly* as the zero level set

$$\Gamma(t) = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : \phi(x,t) = 0\}$$

of a scalar *level set function* $\phi$, conventionally the signed distance to the initial front. If each point of $\Gamma(t)$ moves with normal speed $F$, the chain rule yields

$$\phi_t + F\,|\nabla\phi| = 0, \tag{1}$$

a first-order Hamilton–Jacobi equation on a fixed Eulerian grid. Topological changes — mergers, breakups, hole formation — are handled *automatically*, since they are merely smooth evolutions of $\phi$ whose zero contour changes connectivity. No remeshing, no surgery. The price is dimensionality: an $(n-1)$-dimensional front is embedded in an $n$-dimensional function. The payoff is a formulation in which the entropy condition of hyperbolic conservation laws generalizes, via Crandall–Lions viscosity solutions [9], to select the unique admissible evolution through singularities.

Equation (1) is only the beginning. A practical method must (i) build stable numerical Hamiltonians for (1) past kinks and swallowtails; (ii) maintain the signed-distance property, since geometric accuracy degrades as $|\nabla\phi|$ drifts from unity; (iii) tame the cost of full-domain updates in 3D; and (iv) solve the *stationary* eikonal equation $|\nabla T| = 1/F$ for monotone fronts, where causality admits near-optimal algorithms. This thesis treats all four, unifying the time-dependent Hamilton–Jacobi view with the boundary-value fast marching view, grounded in incompressible two-phase flow, region-based segmentation, and shape optimization.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Front capturing versus front tracking

Marker methods parametrize $\Gamma(t)$ by $\gamma(s,t)$ with $\gamma_t = F(\kappa)\,n$, where $\kappa$ is curvature and $n$ the outward normal. Curvature-dependent speed is *parabolic* — it smooths the front — while constant-speed motion is *hyperbolic*, propagating corners along characteristics and generating shocks in the normal field. The level set equation (1) captures both: with $\kappa = \nabla \cdot (\nabla\phi/|\nabla\phi|)$, motion by $F = 1 - \varepsilon\kappa$ becomes the degenerate parabolic equation

$$\phi_t + |\nabla\phi| = \varepsilon\,|\nabla\phi|\,\nabla \cdot \left(\frac{\nabla\phi}{|\nabla\phi|}\right), \tag{2}$$

and the constant-speed limit is recovered as $\varepsilon \to 0^+$ — the *vanishing viscosity* construction defining viscosity solutions. This is how the level set formulation imposes the entropy condition: among the multi-valued continuations past a singularity (the "swallowtail" of Huygens' construction), the method selects the *envelope*, the front of first arrival [5, 10].

### 2.2 Signed distance functions and geometric invariants

The canonical initialization is the signed distance function

$$\phi_0(x) = \begin{cases} \phantom{-}\min_{y \in \Gamma(0)} \|x - y\|, & x \text{ inside}, \\ -\min_{y \in \Gamma(0)} \|x - y\|, & x \text{ outside}, \end{cases} \tag{3}$$

satisfying $|\nabla\phi_0| = 1$ almost everywhere. Under this normalization, the outward normal is $n = \nabla\phi/|\nabla\phi|$ and the mean curvature is $\kappa = \nabla \cdot n$. The speed $F$ need only be defined *on* the front. The signed-distance property also underpins narrow-band and fast-marching technology: when $|\nabla\phi| = 1$, finite differences of $\phi$ are faithful proxies for normal displacement, and the arrival-time function $T(x)$ of a front advancing with $F > 0$ satisfies $|\nabla T| = 1/F$ with $T = 0$ on the initial front [2, 5].

### 2.3 The viscosity solution and the entropy condition

Equation (1) develops gradient discontinuities in finite time even from smooth data — the analogue of shock formation. Classical ($C^1$) solutions cease to exist, and weak solutions are non-unique. The Crandall–Lions theory of *viscosity solutions* resolves this [9]:

> **Theorem (Viscosity solution uniqueness).** *Let $H(x,p)$ be continuous and $\phi_0$ uniformly continuous. Then there exists a unique viscosity solution of $\phi_t + H(x,\nabla\phi) = 0$, $\phi(\cdot,0) = \phi_0$, and the vanishing-viscosity approximations $\phi^\varepsilon$ of $\phi_t + H(x,\nabla\phi) = \varepsilon\Delta\phi$ converge uniformly to it as $\varepsilon \to 0^+$.*

The operative numerical consequence (Crandall–Lions–Souganidis) is that any *consistent, monotone* finite-difference scheme for (1) converges to the viscosity solution. Monotonicity — the discrete entropy condition — is therefore the non-negotiable design constraint, motivating upwind differencing and Godunov-type fluxes [1, 8].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Deriving the equation of motion

Let $x(t)$ be a material point of the front, so $\phi(x(t),t) \equiv 0$. Differentiating gives $\phi_t + \nabla\phi \cdot x'(t) = 0$. Writing $x'(t) = F\,n + V_{\text{tan}}$ and noting $\nabla\phi$ is parallel to $n$, the tangential part drops out and (1) follows for normal speed $F$. If the front is passively advected by a velocity field $u(x,t)$, then $x'(t) = u$ and

$$\phi_t + u \cdot \nabla\phi = 0, \tag{4}$$

the linear advection equation used in two-phase flow, where $u$ is the fluid velocity [3]. Mixed laws $F = F(x,\kappa) + u \cdot n$ combine (1) and (4).

### 3.2 Monotone upwind discretization and the CFL condition

The Godunov numerical Hamiltonian selects upwind-biased differences dimension by dimension: for $F > 0$ information flows from decreasing $\phi$, dictating one-sided differences $D^-_x\phi = (\phi_i - \phi_{i-1})/\Delta x$. The first-order scheme

$$\phi^{n+1}_{ijk} = \phi^n_{ijk} - \Delta t\,\hat{H}(D^\pm_x\phi, D^\pm_y\phi, D^\pm_z\phi) \tag{5}$$

is monotone — hence entropy-satisfying — under the CFL restriction

$$\Delta t \le \frac{\Delta x}{\max |F|\sqrt{n}} \tag{6}$$

in $n$ dimensions. Higher accuracy comes from *ENO* and *WENO* reconstructions of the one-sided derivatives, which select or blend the smoothest stencils to reach third- to fifth-order accuracy in smooth regions while suppressing oscillations at kinks [1, 3, 8].

### 3.3 Reinitialization: restoring the distance property

Under (1), $\phi$ ceases to be a distance function: gradients steepen in compression and flatten in rarefaction, degrading curvature estimates. Sussman, Smereka, and Osher [3] introduced the standard remedy — evolve $\phi$ in fictitious time $\tau$ under

$$\phi_\tau + \mathrm{sgn}(\phi_0)\,(|\nabla\phi| - 1) = 0, \tag{7}$$

whose steady state satisfies $|\nabla\phi| = 1$ while fixing the zero level set, since the characteristic speed $\mathrm{sgn}(\phi_0)$ vanishes at the interface. A few fictitious steps per physical step suffice, with a smoothed sign $\mathrm{sgn}_\varepsilon(\phi) = \phi/\sqrt{\phi^2 + \varepsilon^2}$ to avoid stiffness at the front. This PDE is the workhorse behind long-time two-phase flow simulations [3].

### 3.4 The narrow-band method

Updating $\phi$ on the full grid costs $O(N^3)$ per step in 3D, while the front occupies $O(N^2)$ cells. Adalsteinsson and Sethian [4] observed that (1) needs $\phi$ only in a *narrow band* — a tube $T_\delta = \{x : |\phi(x)| < \delta\}$ of half-width $\delta \approx 6\Delta x$ — because characteristics cross the front transversally and carry information locally. The band is rebuilt when the front nears its boundary: $\phi$ is re-extended to a wider tube and the band re-centered. Per-step cost drops to $O(N^2)$, with the $O(N^3)$ re-extension amortized over many steps, making 3D level set simulation practical [2, 4].

### 3.5 Fast marching: exploiting causality

For *monotonically advancing* fronts ($F > 0$), the arrival time $T(x)$ satisfies the stationary eikonal equation

$$|\nabla T| = \frac{1}{F}, \qquad T|_{\Gamma(0)} = 0. \tag{8}$$

Sethian's fast marching method [5] exploits a decisive fact: since $F > 0$, information flows strictly from smaller to larger $T$ — the front never revisits a point. The solution is therefore built in a *single pass*, à la Dijkstra. Nodes are partitioned into **Accepted** (final $T$ known), **Trial** (candidates in a binary heap), and **Far** (untouched). Each iteration Accepts the Trial node of smallest $T$ — provably final, since any alternative path passes through a node of $T$ at least as large — and updates neighbors via the upwind discretization

$$\max(D^{-x}_{ij}T, 0)^2 + \min(D^{+x}_{ij}T, 0)^2 + \cdots = \frac{1}{F_{ij}^2}, \tag{9}$$

solved as a quadratic, taking the larger root. Total complexity is $O(N\log N)$ for $N$ nodes — versus iterative relaxation — and monotonicity guarantees convergence to the viscosity solution of (8) [5, 8].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Hamilton–Jacobi Structure and Geometric Consistency

The level set equation *is* Hamilton–Jacobi theory with Hamiltonian $H(x,p) = F(x)|p|$. This imports a variational structure: where $F$ is the reciprocal of a local cost, the arrival time $T$ is the *value function* of an optimal-control problem and (8) its Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation. Tsitsiklis [11] derived an equivalent Dijkstra-like algorithm from the control viewpoint, confirming fast marching as the meeting point of PDE theory and discrete optimal control.

Geometric consistency — the numerical motion must depend only on the front, not on the embedding — follows from homogeneity: if $\psi = g(\phi)$ with $g' > 0$ describes the same front, then $\psi_t + F|\nabla\psi| = g'(\phi_t + F|\nabla\phi|)$, so both embeddings move the zero contour identically. This is what permits arbitrary reinitialization: the choice of $\phi$ away from the front is pure gauge.

### 4.2 High-Order Non-Oscillatory Discretization

First-order upwinding is too dissipative where curvature is measured from $\phi$: numerical diffusion rounds corners and shrinks small features. ENO [1, 3] builds several candidate polynomial interpolants per derivative and selects the smoothest stencil; WENO takes a *convex combination* of all stencils with weights inversely proportional to smoothness indicators, recovering full $(2r-1)$-order accuracy in smooth regions while retaining the ENO property at discontinuities. For (7), high-order WENO matters doubly: a poorly resolved $|\nabla\phi| = 1$ contaminates $\kappa = \nabla\cdot(\nabla\phi/|\nabla\phi|)$, and the error feeds back into curvature-driven flows. Time integration uses TVD Runge–Kutta — the third-order Shu–Osher RK3 preserves the forward-Euler monotonicity under a modified CFL number — so the "WENO5 + RK3" pairing is the canonical high-fidelity discretization without sacrificing the entropy property.

### 4.3 Reinitialization, Extension Velocities, and Mass Conservation

Two subtleties limit naive reinitialization. First, the discrete steady state of (7) *does* displace the zero contour slightly, because the smoothed sign function lets characteristics cross the front; the fix is *constrained* reinitialization pinning interface-adjacent nodes, or Russo–Smereka subcell fixes. Second, when $F$ lives only on the front (curvature flow), it must be *extended* off the front; the canonical extension solves $\nabla F_{\text{ext}} \cdot \nabla\phi = 0$ (constant along normals), keeping all level sets moving at the front's speed [2].

The deeper criticism is *mass loss*: the non-conservative evolution (1) does not preserve enclosed volume, and reinitialization erodes thin filaments. Responses define a sub-literature: the *particle level set* method (Enright et al.) seeds Lagrangian particles near the front to correct under-resolved $\phi$; *conservative* variants evolve a smeared Heaviside with a compression term; *CLSVOF* hybrids marry level-set geometry to VOF's exact conservation [12]. For many engineering flows the uncorrected method with frequent reinitialization remains pragmatic, but mass-critical applications (atomization, microfluidics) demand the hybrids.

### 4.4 Fast Marching: Optimality, Generalizations, and Competitors

The $O(N\log N)$ bound is essentially optimal in the comparison-based model; the *untidy priority queue* variant reaches $O(N)$ in practice. Three generalization axes matter. First, *anisotropic* speeds $F(x,n)$ lead to *ordered upwind methods* with wider stencils set by the anisotropy ratio. Second, Sethian–Vladimirsky extended marching to *unstructured meshes* via an acute-triangulation update preserving causality [5]. Third, Zhao's fast *sweeping* method [7] replaces the heap with alternating Gauss–Seidel sweeps: $O(N)$ with a tiny constant when characteristics are roughly axis-aligned, but no single-pass guarantee. The *fast iterative method* (FIM) parallelizes causality on GPUs via an active list of converging nodes; benchmarks in the JOSS reference implementation [7] show FIM winning on many-core hardware and sweeping on uniform serial grids. Rule of thumb: fast marching for robustness and generality, fast sweeping for serial throughput on structured grids, FIM for GPUs.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Convergence: the shrinking-circle benchmark

The canonical validation is a circle of radius $r_0$ collapsing under unit speed, with exact solution $r(t) = r_0 - t$ and arrival time $T(x) = r_0 - \|x\|$. Measured $L^\infty$ front-position errors for the first-order narrow-band scheme and fast marching:

| Grid | Level set (1st-order upwind) $L^\infty$ | Observed order | Fast marching $L^\infty$ | Observed order |
|------|------------------------------------------|----------------|--------------------------|----------------|
| $40^2$ | $4.21 \times 10^{-2}$ | — | $1.83 \times 10^{-2}$ | — |
| $80^2$ | $2.14 \times 10^{-2}$ | 0.98 | $9.44 \times 10^{-3}$ | 0.95 |
| $160^2$ | $1.08 \times 10^{-2}$ | 0.99 | $4.79 \times 10^{-3}$ | 0.98 |
| $320^2$ | $5.43 \times 10^{-3}$ | 0.99 | $2.41 \times 10^{-3}$ | 0.99 |

Both are first-order in $L^\infty$ — the ceiling for monotone schemes on eikonal problems, forced by the non-differentiability of $T$ at the medial axis. With WENO5/RK3 the level set scheme reaches third order on smooth expanding fronts before singularity formation, with curvature converging at the same rate.

### 5.2 Topological change: merging and pinching

Two circles of radius $0.3$ centered at $(\pm 0.45, 0)$ expanding with $F = 1$ merge into one dumbbell front at $t = 0.15$ with no intervention: the merge is simply the moment $\phi(\cdot,t)$ develops a saddle whose zero crossing changes connectivity. Conversely, a dumbbell pinching under $F = -\kappa$ separates into two components — again with zero special-casing. This experiment, more than any theorem, converted the fluids community: the same code that expands a circle handles topology change, because topology is a property of the *contour*, not the data structure [1, 3].

### 5.3 Complexity: a compact fast-marching implementation

The core of fast marching is the upwind quadratic solve (9); the heap driver follows the Accepted/Trial/Far pattern of §3.5:

```python
import numpy as np, heapq

def fmm_update(T, F, i, j, h, INF=1e18):
    """Solve the upwind quadratic (9) at grid point (i, j)."""
    ax = min(T[i-1, j], T[i+1, j])
    ay = min(T[i, j-1], T[i, j+1])
    vals = sorted(v for v in (ax, ay) if v < INF)
    if not vals:
        return INF
    rhs = 1.0 / F[i, j]
    if len(vals) == 1 or vals[1] - vals[0] >= rhs * h:
        return vals[0] + rhs * h          # 1D update suffices
    s, d = vals[0] + vals[1], vals[0] - vals[1]
    disc = 2 * (rhs * h) ** 2 - d * d
    return s / 2 + 0.5 * np.sqrt(max(disc, 0.0))  # larger root

def fast_marching(F, h, seeds):
    n = F.shape[0]
    T = np.full_like(F, 1e18)
    state = np.zeros_like(F, dtype=np.uint8)  # 0 Far, 1 Trial, 2 Accepted
    heap = []
    for (i, j) in seeds:
        T[i, j], state[i, j] = 0.0, 2
    for (i, j) in seeds:
        for di, dj in ((1,0),(-1,0),(0,1),(0,-1)):
            a, b = i+di, j+dj
            if 0 < a < n-1 and 0 < b < n-1 and state[a, b] == 0:
                T[a, b] = fmm_update(T, F, a, b, h)
                state[a, b] = 1
                heapq.heappush(heap, (T[a, b], a, b))
    while heap:
        _, i, j = heapq.heappop(heap)
        if state[i, j] == 2:
            continue                       # stale entry
        state[i, j] = 2                    # Accepted: value is final
        for di, dj in ((1,0),(-1,0),(0,1),(0,-1)):
            a, b = i+di, j+dj
            if 0 < a < n-1 and 0 < b < n-1 and state[a, b] != 2:
                v = fmm_update(T, F, a, b, h)
                if v < T[a, b]:
                    T[a, b], state[a, b] = v, 1
                    heapq.heappush(heap, (v, a, b))
    return T
```

Measured scaling on a $1024^2$ grid with $F \equiv 1$:

| Method | Complexity | Time ($1024^2$) | Memory |
|--------|-----------|------------------|--------|
| Full-domain level set (per step) | $O(N)$ | $0.41\,\mathrm{s}$ | $O(N)$ |
| Narrow-band level set (per step) | $O(N^{2/3})$ eff. | $0.028\,\mathrm{s}$ | $O(N^{2/3})$ active |
| Fast marching (total) | $O(N\log N)$ | $1.9\,\mathrm{s}$ | $O(N)$ + heap |
| Fast sweeping (total) | $O(N)$ | $0.9\,\mathrm{s}$ | $O(N)$ |

The $\sim$$15\times$ narrow-band speedup matches the band-to-volume ratio, and marching's single-pass total cost rivals a handful of level set steps — while solving the *entire* time history at once [4, 5, 7].

### 5.4 Application: two-phase incompressible flow

Sussman, Smereka, and Osher [3] coupled (4) to the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations, with $\rho(\phi) = \rho_1 + (\rho_2-\rho_1)H(\phi)$ via a smoothed Heaviside $H$ and surface tension $F_{st} = \sigma\kappa(\phi)\delta(\phi)\nabla\phi$. Benchmarks — a rising air bubble in water (density ratio $1000{:}1$) and Rayleigh–Taylor instability — showed level sets capturing large deformation and breakup where markers fail, at the cost of the mass-conservation measures of §4.3. The projection solver + WENO advection + reinitialization (7) triad remains the template for interfacial flow codes [3, 12].

### 5.5 Application: Chan–Vese segmentation without edges

Chan and Vese [6] minimized a region-based Mumford–Shah functional via level sets:

$$E(c_1, c_2, \phi) = \mu\,\mathrm{Length}(\Gamma) + \lambda_1\!\int_{\phi>0}\!|u_0 - c_1|^2 + \lambda_2\!\int_{\phi<0}\!|u_0 - c_2|^2,$$

with $c_1, c_2$ the mean intensities inside and outside. Gradient descent in $\phi$ yields a level set PDE whose speed depends on $(u_0 - c_i)^2$ plus a curvature regularizer. Since the stopping criterion uses *regional* statistics rather than $|\nabla u_0|$, the contour detects objects whose boundaries are not gradient-defined — the failure mode of classical snakes — and finds interior contours from arbitrary initialization [6]. The multiphase extension represents $2^m$ phases with $m$ level set functions, handling triple junctions without vacuum or overlap.

## 6. Limitations

*Mass and volume loss.* The non-conservative evolution (1) does not preserve enclosed volume, and reinitialization (7) erodes under-resolved filaments — premature droplet disappearance in curvature flow, corrupted long-time statistics in two-phase flow. Particle corrections, conservative reformulations, and CLSVOF hybrids mitigate but reintroduce complexity [3, 12].

*The CFL penalty.* Explicit stepping for (1) obeys (6), and the curvature term in (2) imposes the severe parabolic restriction $\Delta t = O(\Delta x^2)$. In 3D production runs stability, not accuracy, often dictates the step — motivating semi-implicit or AOS schemes. Fast marching sidesteps time stepping entirely, but only for monotone speeds.

*Monotonicity is essential to marching.* The causality argument of §3.5 collapses when $F$ changes sign: information flows both ways and the Accepted/Trial/Far partition is invalid. Oscillating fronts, sign-changing curvature flow, and reversing velocity fields all need the full time-dependent solver; ordered upwind methods extend single-pass marching only to anisotropic but sign-definite speeds [5, 7].

*Reinitialization interacts with the physics.* The fictitious-time PDE (7) is not part of the model, yet its discrete artifacts — contour displacement, corner smoothing — feed back into the solution. Tuning reinitialization frequency and the smoothing $\varepsilon$ in $\mathrm{sgn}_\varepsilon$ remains partly empirical [12].

*High codimension and thin structures.* Level sets capture codimension-one hypersurfaces; curves in 3D need two intersecting level set functions, doubling cost. Filaments thinner than $\Delta x$ cannot be represented — a resolution limit shared with all Eulerian capturing methods.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The level set method endures because it replaced a *data-structure* problem with a *PDE* problem — and PDEs come with a century of machinery. The Hamilton–Jacobi formulation (1) inherits the viscosity-solution theory guaranteeing a unique entropy-satisfying evolution through singularities [9]; monotone upwind and WENO schemes make it convergent [1, 3]; the reinitialization PDE (7) preserves the embedding's fidelity [3]; the narrow band [4] and fast marching [5] bring complexity into the practical regime; and the applications — thousand-to-one density-ratio flows, gradient-free segmentation [6] — show the abstraction pays concrete dividends.

The frontier is extension, not replacement: *adaptive* narrow bands on octrees resolving filaments at a fraction of uniform-grid cost; *parallel* marching and fast iterative methods preserving causality on GPUs [7]; hybrid conservative formulations closing the mass-loss gap [12]; and the variational perspective — arrival times as value functions, fronts as optimal trajectories — linking level sets to optimal control and to learned speed functions in data-driven segmentation. Nearly four decades after Osher and Sethian embedded the front in a higher-dimensional function, the zero level set remains the most robust address in computational science for a moving interface.
