# Bayesian Program Synthesis with Library Learning: DreamCoder Wake–Sleep, E-Graph Rewriting, and LLM-Guided Inductive Synthesis

## Abstract

Inductive program synthesis — learning programs from input–output examples — is one of the oldest and hardest problems in artificial intelligence: the space of candidate programs grows exponentially with program size while examples are few. Classical approaches either design stronger search algorithms or hand-craft stronger domain-specific languages, but both stall because the prior over programs is fixed *a priori*. This thesis develops a unified account of a third route: **learning the prior itself**. We trace how the DreamCoder wake–sleep framework jointly learns a library of reusable abstractions (reducing search *depth*) and a neural recognition model (reducing search *breadth*) as approximate Bayesian inference over a generative model of programs; how the sleep-phase abstraction step is accelerated by e-graph equality saturation with anti-unification; how large language models enter as proposal distributions and pre-trained world-knowledge priors; and how the Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus (ARC) serves as the proving ground where these strands fuse.

## 1. Introduction

Since the early work on inductive inference, program synthesis has promised a simple and radical interface to computation: describe *what* should happen through examples, and let the machine discover *how*. The **inductive synthesis problem** is conventionally stated as follows [1]:

> **Definition (Inductive program synthesis):** Given a specification $\mathcal{S} = \{(x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_n, y_n)\}$ of input–output pairs and a domain-specific language $\mathcal{L}$ of candidate programs, find $\rho \in \mathcal{L}$ such that $\rho(x_i) = y_i$ for all $i$, i.e., the program is consistent with every example.

The difficulty is immediate. If $\mathcal{L}$ has $k$ primitives and solutions have depth $d$, the search space is $O(k^d)$. Five examples do not pin down $10^{20}$ possibilities; what does is a strong prior, and priors are where all the real work lives. Classical enumerative search, stochastic search, and deductive SyGuS solvers each embody an implicit prior, usually hard-coded by the DSL designer. This creates the notorious **DSL dilemma**: a DSL small enough to search is too weak to express the interesting programs, and a DSL expressive enough to be interesting is too large to search.

*Bayesian* program learning reframes the dilemma. Rather than fixing the DSL once and for all, the system **learns a DSL from its own experience** — it induces libraries of abstractions that compress discovered solutions, and trains a recognition model that amortizes search. The flagship architecture for this idea is **DreamCoder** [1][2], a wake–sleep Bayesian program learner that iterates three phases:

- **Wake** (*solving*): search for programs solving presented tasks, guided by a learned recognition model $Q(\rho \mid x)$.
- **Sleep: abstraction** (*library learning*): refactor solutions into semantically equivalent but syntactically compressed forms, identify recurring substructures via anti-unification, and admit new library primitives that maximize the Bayesian evidence $\log P(\rho) + \log P(x \mid \rho)$.
- **Sleep: dreaming** (*recognition training*): train $Q$ on replays (real solved tasks) and fantasies (tasks sampled from the generative model), thereby amortizing search cost.

This thesis makes three central contributions. First, we give a coherent mathematical treatment of wake–sleep Bayesian library learning, including the minimum-description-length objective that justifies abstraction and the connection between DreamCoder's refactoring search and **e-graph equality saturation** [3]. Second, we show how **large language models** slot into this Bayesian architecture: as highly informative proposal distributions, as priors infused with pre-trained world knowledge, and as generators for the dreaming phase. Third, we evaluate the unified framework against the **Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus (ARC)** [5][6], the benchmark that most stresses exactly this kind of data-efficient abstraction learning.

> **Theorem (Library compression lowers search cost):** Let $\mathcal{L}$ be a DSL with $|L|$ primitives and let solutions require mean depth $d$. Suppose a learned library primitive $f$ of size $s$ occurs $n$ times across the corpus of solutions. Refactoring the corpus through $f$ reduces total corpus description length by $n(s-1) - s$ symbols. Search cost for future tasks, proportional to $|L|^{d}$, is reduced when the *shorter* programs enabled by $f$ outweigh the cost of one additional primitive.

This compression-versus-expressiveness trade-off is the heartbeat of the entire approach: the system literally *grows its own language* to match the domain, and search gets easier as the library gets better. DreamCoder demonstrated this empirically by discovering — from scratch — primitives equivalent to `map`, `fold`, and `unfold` in list programming, vector-algebra operators, and physics primitives [1].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Probabilistic models of programs

A **probabilistic context-free grammar (PCFG)** over a DSL defines a prior $P(\rho \mid L, \theta)$ as the product of production probabilities along the derivation tree. The joint model over tasks and solutions is:

$$
P(L, \theta, \{\rho_t\}, \{x_t\}) = P(L)\, P(\theta \mid L)\, \prod_t P(\rho_t \mid L, \theta)\, P(x_t \mid \rho_t)
$$

where $P(x_t \mid \rho_t)$ is the likelihood: $1$ if $\rho_t$ is consistent with the examples (possibly with noise), and $0$ otherwise. Inference maximizes the evidence for the library. In the **rational rules** tradition, this Bayesian framing elegantly handles noise, continuous parameters, and uncertainty over which tasks share structure.

The recognition model $Q(\rho \mid x)$ is a **neural amortized inference network** trained to approximate the posterior. DreamCoder trains $Q$ with a MAP objective:

$$
\max_Q \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \text{tasks}}\big[\log Q\big(\arg\max_\rho P(\rho \mid x_t, L) \mid x_t\big)\big]
$$

The expectation is taken over a 50/50 mix of *replays* (empirical task distribution) and *fantasies* (samples from the generative model $P(\rho \mid L)P(x \mid \rho)$), in a direct analogy to wake–sleep training of Helmholtz machines [1][2]. A subtle but important effect of the MAP objective is **learned symmetry breaking**: by forcing $Q$ to place all mass on a single canonical member of each semantic equivalence class, it learns to avoid wasting probability mass on syntactic redundancies that hand-coded symmetry breaking used to address.

### 2.2 Library learning via refactoring and anti-unification

The sleep *abstraction* phase asks: given a corpus of solutions $\{\rho_t\}$, what new primitives would make the corpus most probable? DreamCoder approaches this by **refactoring**: for each solution, it searches a space of semantically equivalent rewrites to expose shared syntactic structure, then proposes abstractions by **anti-unification** — the least-general generalization of matching subterms. Concretely, if two solutions contain subexpressions `λz. f(g(z))` and `λz. f(h(z))` in similar contexts, anti-unification proposes the abstraction `λz. λg'. f(g'(z))` with $g$ and $h$ passed as parameters [1].

Each candidate abstraction $f$ is scored by its effect on the Bayesian evidence:

$$
\Delta(f) = \log P(\{\rho_t\}_{ref} \mid L \cup \{f\}) - \log P(\{\rho_t\} \mid L) - \text{cost}(f)
$$

where $\text{cost}(f)$ penalizes the complexity of the new primitive. Only abstractions with $\Delta(f) > 0$ survive. This is exactly a **minimum description length** (MDL) criterion: grow the language when the bits saved by compressing the corpus exceed the bits spent describing the new primitive [1][2].

### 2.3 E-graphs and equality saturation

An **e-graph** is a data structure that compactly represents an exponential number of equivalent terms: e-classes (equivalence classes) contain e-nodes (operator applications whose children are e-class ids). **Equality saturation** iteratively applies rewrite rules to an e-graph until no new equivalences are discovered (saturation) or resources run out, then extracts the optimal term under a cost function [3]. The `egg` library (e-graphs good) made equality saturation practical through *rebuilding* — deferring congruence invariant maintenance to batch at iteration end — and through *e-class analyses* that attach semilattice-valued facts to e-classes, allowing rewrites to cooperate with domain-specific reasoning like constant folding [3].

The connection to library learning is deep and direct: DreamCoder's refactoring search *is* an e-graph problem — the space of semantically equivalent rewrites of a corpus is precisely what equality saturation computes — and modern implementations exploit this correspondence [7].

### 2.4 LLMs as synthesis engines

Large language models changed inductive synthesis in two ways. First, they are astonishingly strong **proposal distributions**: pre-trained on code, they sample plausible programs for a specification with far higher density than any random PCFG [4][6]. Second, they carry **pre-trained world knowledge** — an empirical prior over what programs *humans actually write*, which encodes conventions, idioms, and domain knowledge no small DSL could capture. Approaches like HYSYNTH distill LLM samples into a learned PCFG that then guides exact bottom-up enumerative search [4], while neurosymbolic ARC solvers use LLMs to score and compose symbolic primitives [5][6].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 A unified Bayesian architecture

We now describe the unified architecture that this thesis advocates, integrating the four strands above into a single wake–sleep loop. Notation: $L_k$ is the library at iteration $k$, $\theta_k$ the production probabilities, $Q_k$ the recognition model, and $\mathcal{T}_k$ the set of solved tasks.

1. **Wake (guided search).** For each unsolved task $t$, enumerate candidate programs in order of decreasing $Q_k(\rho \mid x_t)$, testing consistency with the examples. DreamCoder uses a neurally-guided version of enumerative search with *observational equivalence* pruning: two programs equivalent on the task inputs are deduplicated, so the search explores semantics rather than syntax [1]. LLM proposals are incorporated by mixing: $Q_k$ becomes a mixture of the learned recognition net and an LLM-based proposal distribution.

2. **Sleep: abstraction (e-graph rewriting).** Insert all solutions into an e-graph and run equality saturation with a rule set including $\beta$-reduction, inlining, and domain axioms. Anti-unify shared e-nodes across tasks to propose abstractions; score each by $\Delta(f)$; admit winners into $L_{k+1}$. Because the e-graph represents *all* equivalent refactorings simultaneously, the search that DreamCoder originally implemented by hand is subsumed by saturation [3][7].

3. **Sleep: dreaming (amortized training).** Sample programs $\rho \sim P(\cdot \mid L_{k+1}, \theta)$, generate synthetic tasks $x \sim P(\cdot \mid \rho)$, and train $Q_{k+1}$ on the mixed objective of replays and fantasies. Optionally, use an LLM to generate *dreams in the task domain* — e.g., plausible ARC grids — which sharpens $Q$ on in-distribution problems.

4. **Iterate.** Each cycle compresses the corpus (abstraction) and amortizes search (dreaming); empirically, tasks solved per unit search time grows super-linearly across iterations [1].

### 3.2 The anti-unification lattice

The abstraction search can be formalized cleanly. For terms $\rho_1, \rho_2$, the anti-unification $\rho_1 \sqcup \rho_2$ is the *least general generalization* — the most specific term subsuming both. Generalizations form a lattice ordered by subsumption ($\sqsubseteq$); candidate abstractions are maximal elements appearing in multiple tasks, ranked by the Bayesian objective $\Delta(f)$.

### 3.3 LLM integration points

We identify three principled insertion points for LLMs in the Bayesian loop:

| Insertion point | Role | Bayesian interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Wake proposal mixture | Sample candidate programs from LLM conditioned on examples | LLM as proposal $R(\rho \mid x)$ in importance-weighted search |
| Fantasy generation | LLM synthesizes training tasks for $Q$ | Extending the dreaming distribution beyond $P(\rho \mid L)P(x \mid \rho)$ |
| Prior distillation (HYSYNTH-style) | Fit PCFG $\theta$ to LLM-sampled programs | LLM knowledge compiled into the parametric prior $P(\rho \mid L, \theta)$ |

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Wake–sleep as approximate Bayesian inference

The DreamCoder objective can be derived from a variational lower bound. With generative model $P$ and recognition model $Q$, the standard ELBO over tasks and solutions is:

$$
\log P(\{x_t\} \mid L) \;\ge\; \mathbb{E}_{Q(\{\rho_t\} \mid \{x_t\})}\Big[\log P(\{\rho_t\} \mid L) + \log P(\{x_t\} \mid \{\rho_t\}) - \log Q(\{\rho_t\} \mid \{x_t\})\Big]
$$

Wake performs E-step-like inference (finding good $\rho_t$); sleep-abstraction performs an M-step over the library $L$ (structure learning); sleep-dreaming performs an M-step over $Q$ (amortization). The innovation over classical wake–sleep is that *structure* (the library) is itself learned, so the hypothesis class of the generative model grows in expressiveness exactly where data demand it [1][2]. DreamDecompiler [2] strengthens this by letting $Q$'s amortized knowledge directly shape library learning: it *decompiles* the neural policy — querying $Q$ for high-probability subprograms — so the knowledge that reduces search breadth also reduces search depth.

### 4.2 E-graphs as the algebra of refactoring

Why e-graphs? The refactoring problem is: given programs $\rho_1, \dots, \rho_n$, find semantically equivalent forms $\rho_1', \dots, \rho_n'$ maximizing shared substructure. Equality saturation solves the harder, more general problem — it computes *all* equivalents up to the rewrite rules — and extraction then picks the best under a compression cost model. The e-class analysis mechanism [3] is the crucial enabler: analyses can track, per e-class, facts like "which tasks contain a term in this class," so abstraction candidates (shared e-nodes across tasks) emerge directly from the saturated e-graph rather than from pairwise anti-unification. This converts library learning from a quadratic pairwise search into a linear scan over e-classes — at the price of the saturation itself, which `egg`'s rebuilding keeps fast (average 3–30× speedups over upward-merging implementations [3]).

```haskell
-- Abstraction candidate discovery from a saturated e-graph (pseudocode)
discoverAbstractions :: EGraph -> [Task] -> [Abstraction]
discoverAbstractions eg tasks =
  [ antiUnify sharedTerms
  | eclass <- eclasses eg
  , sharedTerms <- subtermsOccurringIn eclass tasks
  , length (taskIds sharedTerms) >= 2        -- appears in >=2 tasks
  , compressionGain sharedTerms > threshold  -- MDL check
  ]
```

### 4.3 Enumerative search with observational equivalence

Even with a learned library and recognition model, the wake phase must search. The state of the art in exact enumerative synthesis is **bottom-up enumeration with observational equivalence**: build programs by size, and whenever two programs agree on all task inputs, keep only one representative. This prunes the search from syntax to semantics — often an exponential reduction, since many syntactically distinct programs compute the same function on the given examples [4]. The learned PCFG or recognition model orders enumeration by decreasing prior probability, so the most plausible programs are tested first. In the unified architecture, this exact core is what makes the system *sound*: no matter how exotic the LLM proposals, the final answer is verified by execution.

```python
def bottom_up_search(spec, library, Q, timeout):
    """Enumerate programs bottom-up, ordered by -log Q(prior), dedup by obs. equivalence."""
    bank = {size: [] for size in range(MAX_SIZE)}
    seen = {}  # signature on spec.inputs -> canonical program
    for size in range(1, MAX_SIZE + 1):
        for prog in generate(library, bank, size, Q):  # prior-ordered
            sig = tuple(prog(x) for x in spec.inputs)
            if sig in seen:  # observationally equivalent: skip
                continue
            seen[sig] = prog
            if all(prog(x) == y for x, y in spec.examples):
                return prog
    return None
```

### 4.4 LLMs as priors: pre-training meets Bayes

The most consequential recent development is treating the LLM as an empirical prior. An LLM trained on billions of lines of code is approximately the distribution of programs humans write — the least arbitrary program prior ever constructed. HYSYNTH [4] operationalizes this: sample programs from the LLM for a task, learn a PCFG from the samples, and run exact bottom-up search guided by that PCFG. The LLM's knowledge is *compiled* into a tractable parametric form, preserving soundness while importing world knowledge. On ARC, LLM-guided symbolic search consistently outperforms both pure LLM guessing and pure symbolic search alone [4][6].

### 4.5 The ARC stress test

The Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus [5] is the natural benchmark for this entire research program: 800+ unique visual reasoning tasks, each solvable by a human from 2–6 examples, with tasks never repeating. ARC is explicitly a *program synthesis benchmark* [5][6]: each task is a tiny program over grids, and the solver must induce it. What makes ARC the right stress test:

1. **Few-shot by construction** — 3–6 examples per task, exactly the regime where learned priors dominate.
2. **Non-repeating tasks** — developer-aware generalization; memorization is useless, so the system must genuinely learn abstractions.
3. **Core-knowledge priors** — objectness, counting, symmetry, topology; a library-learning system should *discover* these as abstractions.
4. **Program-shaped solutions** — grid transformations compose, so abstraction and rewriting apply directly.

DreamCoder was adapted to ARC with the PeARL DSL (Perceptual Abstraction and Reasoning Language); neural guidance exponentially reduced the search space (from $O(n!)$ to $O(n)$ in the analysis of [6]), but without the abstraction phase the library could not grow new primitives. LLM-based approaches solve a *different, partially overlapping* subset of tasks than symbolic solvers [4], which is precisely why the hybrid architecture of Section 3 is the right endpoint: the union of complementary strengths.

We summarize the empirical record across the systems discussed, drawn from their published evaluations [1][2][3][4][6]:

| System | Domain | Key result |

## 6. Limitations

Honesty requires enumerating where the unified architecture still fails.

1. **The abstraction search is fragile.** Anti-unification over refactored programs can propose abstractions that are formally compressive but semantically useless — e.g., generalizing over coincidental syntactic overlap. The MDL criterion filters these, but only after paying the search cost, and pathological corpora can admit large, brittle abstractions that *increase* search depth by bloating $|L|$ [1].

2. **Equality saturation does not scale to rich theories.** Saturation is complete only relative to its rewrite rules; with expressive rule sets (associativity, distributivity) the e-graph explodes before saturation, and extraction under non-local cost functions is NP-hard in general. Rule scheduling and e-class analyses mitigate this [3], but the abstraction phase remains the most expensive part of the loop.

3. **LLM priors are opaque and biased.** An LLM prior concentrates mass on human-idiomatic programs — excellent for ARC-like tasks, but it can *suppress* correct-but-unusual solutions and inherits the biases of its training data. Distilling into a PCFG partially domesticates this [4], but the distilled prior is only as good as the sample set.

4. **ARC is still mostly unsolved.** Even the best hybrid systems solve a minority of ARC tasks; human-level fluid intelligence on novel abstractions remains distant. The benchmark's insistence on *developer-aware generalization* means progress must come from better abstraction learning, not bigger models alone [5][6].

5. **Evaluation is fragmented.** Different papers report on different ARC splits (train, test, hidden, ConceptARC), different time budgets, and different DSLs, making head-to-head comparison hazardous. The field needs standardized, compute-controlled evaluation harnesses for library-learning systems.

## 7. Conclusion

This thesis has argued for a single unifying view: **program synthesis is Bayesian library learning**, and the practical path forward is a wake–sleep architecture in which (i) a learned library compresses solutions and reduces search depth, (ii) a neural recognition model amortizes search and reduces breadth, (iii) e-graph equality saturation provides the algebraic substrate for discovering abstractions, and (iv) large language models supply dense, knowledge-rich proposals and priors that classical Bayesian methods cannot. The empirical record — DreamCoder's discovery of `map` and `fold` from first principles [1], DreamDecompiler's decompilation of amortized knowledge [2], `egg`'s order-of-magnitude acceleration of saturation [3], and the complementary strengths of symbolic and LLM solvers on ARC [4][5][6] — converges on the same lesson: *systems that grow their own languages outperform systems that are given them*.

The research frontier is clear: abstraction search robust to coincidental compression, saturation that scales to richer equational theories, and principled distillation of LLM knowledge into verifiable symbolic priors. ARC will remain the decisive benchmark, because it tests exactly what this architecture promises: learning new concepts from a handful of examples, by writing programs, in a language of the system's own devising.
