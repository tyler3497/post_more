# Worst-Case to Average-Case Reductions for LWE: Regev's Quantum Reduction, Peikert's Classical Reduction, and the Geometry of Ideal Lattices

## Abstract

The security of lattice-based cryptography rests on an unusual and powerful property: *worst-case to average-case* reductions. While RSA and discrete-logarithm schemes base their security on the presumed average-case hardness of randomly chosen instances, Learning With Errors (LWE) derives its security from the worst-case hardness of approximating lattice problems such as the Shortest Vector Problem (GapSVP) and the Shortest Independent Vectors Problem (SIVP). This article gives a rigorous account of the reduction landscape. We begin with Regev's 2005 quantum reduction, which converts an average-case solver for search-LWE into a quantum algorithm for approximating GapSVP and SIVP [1]. We then analyze Peikert's 2009 classical reduction, which removes the quantum requirement at the cost of an exponential modulus [2], and the Brakerski–Langlois–Peikert–Regev–Stehlé modulus-reduction technique of 2013, which recovers classical hardness for polynomial moduli [3]. Finally, we develop the algebraic geometry of ideal lattices via the canonical embedding of number fields, following Lyubashevsky–Peikert–Regev [4], and examine the module-lattice generalization of Langlois–Stehlé [6]. Throughout, we quantify the approximation factors γ, the modulus/dimension tradeoffs, and the cryptographic consequences of each reduction's parameters.

##1 Intro

Most hardness assumptions in cryptography are *average-case*: they assert that a random instance of a problem drawn from some distribution is hard for efficient algorithms. Factoring, discrete logarithms, and learning parity with noise all take this form. Lattice-based cryptography is the striking exception. Since Ajtai's 1996 breakthrough connecting average-case SIS to worst-case lattice problems, and especially since Regev's 2005 definition of Learning With Errors (LWE), the central problems of lattice cryptography have enjoyed reductions from *worst-case* lattice problems: solving LWE on random instances implies solving GapSVP or SIVP on *every* lattice of a given dimension.

> **Thesis statement.** The reduction landscape for LWE forms a ladder of ever-stronger statements: Regev's quantum reduction establishes hardness for exponential and polynomial moduli at the cost of quantum computation; Peikert's classical reduction removes quantumness at the cost of an exponential modulus; Brakerski et al.'s modulus switching makes classical hardness available for polynomial moduli; and the Ring-LWE and Module-LWE extensions carry these guarantees to algebraically structured lattices at the cost of worst-case hardness over ideal and module lattices instead of all lattices.

Why does this matter in practice? The NIST post-quantum standards — Module-LWE-based ML-KEM (Kyber) and ML-DSA (Dilithium) — are instantiated over module lattices. Their security arguments trace back through precisely this ladder of reductions. Understanding what is lost at each rung — approximation factors, modulus constraints, the strength of the worst-case hypothesis — is essential for evaluating concrete security and for choosing parameters that are not merely heuristically safe but *reductively* safe.

**Roadmap.** Section 2 fixes notation and defines GapSVP, SIVP, and the search and decision variants of LWE. Section 3 describes the reduction architecture. Section 4 (Deep Dive) works through the four reductions in technical detail. Section 5 formalizes the theorems and records their parameter regimes, Section 6 confronts the limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

##2 Background

### 2.1 Lattices and worst-case problems

An $n$-dimensional lattice $\mathcal{L} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is a discrete additive subgroup, typically written $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}) = \{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{z} : \mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{Z}^n\}$ for a basis matrix $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$. We denote by $\lambda_1(\mathcal{L})$ the length of a shortest nonzero vector, and by $\eta(\mathcal{L})$ the *smoothing parameter*: the smallest $s > 0$ such that $\rho_{1/s}(\mathcal{L}^*) \leq 1 + \varepsilon$ for the dual lattice $\mathcal{L}^*$, where $\rho_s(\mathbf{x}) = \exp(-\pi \|\mathbf{x}\|^2 / s^2)$.

| Problem | Input | Task | Approximation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GapSVP$_\gamma$** | lattice $\mathcal{L}$, real $d$ | decide $\lambda_1(\mathcal{L}) \leq d$ vs. $> \gamma d$ | factor $\gamma(n)$ |
| **SIVP$_\gamma$** | lattice $\mathcal{L}$ | find $n$ linearly independent vectors, each of length $\leq \gamma \cdot \lambda_n(\mathcal{L})$ | factor $\gamma(n)$ |
| **DGS$_{s,\varepsilon}$** | lattice $\mathcal{L}$, parameter $s$ | sample from the discrete Gaussian $D_{\mathcal{L}, s}$ | — |

The *discrete Gaussian* $D_{\mathcal{L}, s}(\mathbf{x}) \propto \rho_s(\mathbf{x})$ for $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{L}$ is the workhorse of every reduction in this paper. Sampling it is *not* known to be classically easy at small $s$, and its tractability landscape (Babai's nearest-plane at large $s$; quantum sampling at small $s$ via the LWE oracle) is what separates the quantum and classical results.

### 2.2 The Learning With Errors problem

For integers $n, q \geq 2$ and a probability distribution $\chi$ over the torus $\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$, the distribution $A_{\mathbf{s}, \chi}$ on $\mathbb{Z}_q^n \times \mathbb{T}$ is obtained by choosing $\mathbf{a} \gets \mathbb{Z}_q^n$ uniformly, $e \gets \chi$, and outputting $(\mathbf{a}, \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle / q + e)$, where $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n$ is the secret.

- **Search-LWE$_{q, n, \chi}$**: given poly$(n)$ samples from $A_{\mathbf{s}, \chi}$ for a fixed random secret $\mathbf{s}$, recover $\mathbf{s}$.
- **Decision-LWE$_{q, n, \chi}$**: distinguish $A_{\mathbf{s}, \chi}$ (for random $\mathbf{s}$) from the uniform distribution on $\mathbb{Z}_q^n \times \mathbb{T}$ with non-negligible advantage.

The error distribution $\chi$ is typically a continuous Gaussian of standard deviation $\alpha q \geq 2\sqrt{n}$, with $\alpha \in (0, 1)$. A *discrete* variant replaces $\mathbb{T}$ with $\mathbb{Z}_q$ directly; the hardness results transfer with minor parameter changes.

A subtle but crucial point: the modulus $q$ governs the reduction *quality*. Regev's quantum reduction works for $q = \mathrm{poly}(n)$. Peikert's classical reduction demands $q \geq 2^{n/2}$ — exponential. BLP+13's modulus reduction is precisely the technique that turns the exponential-modulus classical reduction into a polynomial-modulus one.

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 The reduction blueprint

Every worst-case to average-case reduction for LWE follows the same skeleton:

1. **Input**: an arbitrary $n$-dimensional lattice $\mathcal{L}$ (the worst-case instance).
2. **Goal**: solve GapSVP$_\gamma$ or SIVP$_\gamma$ on $\mathcal{L}$.
3. **Strategy**: use a hypothetical efficient LWE solver $\mathcal{A}$ as an oracle to sample ever-narrower discrete Gaussians $D_{\mathcal{L}, s}$ over $\mathcal{L}$.
4. **Mechanism**: the LWE oracle "decodes" samples from a wide Gaussian to narrow ones: its answers reveal information that lets us *center* the next round's sampling.
5. **Termination**: when $s$ drops below $\approx \sqrt{n} \cdot \eta(\mathcal{L})$, discrete-Gaussian samples become short enough to directly yield short independent vectors — solving SIVP.

The quantum step in Regev's reduction is needed exactly once: to convert the LWE oracle's classical answers into a quantum state preparation procedure for the discrete Gaussian. Peikert's classical reduction replaces this with a *classical* iterative sampler that works only when $q$ is exponential: the noise must be "roundable" to a coarser grid without revealing the secret.

### 3.2 Proof techniques in play

- **Smoothing parameter analysis**: bounds on $\eta(\mathcal{L})$ via Banaszczyk's transference theorems $\eta(\mathcal{L}) \cdot \lambda_1(\mathcal{L}^*) \leq \tilde{O}(1)$.
- **Noise flooding / smudging**: adding large noise to hide small secret-dependent terms, enabling search-to-decision reductions.
- **Modulus reduction (BLP+13)**: map an LWE instance modulo $p$ to one modulo $q < p$ by random rounding; noise grows by only a factor $\approx q/p \cdot n$, trading dimension against modulus.
- **Canonical embedding**: for Ring-LWE, embed ideals $\mathcal{I}$ of a number field $K$ into $\mathbb{R}^n$ via the Minkowski embedding $\sigma: K \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^{s_1} \times \mathbb{C}^{s_2}$, converting algebraic problems into geometric ones.

```python
# Pseudocode: iterative discrete-Gaussian narrowing (reduction skeleton)
def worst_case_to_average_case(L, lwe_oracle, n):
    s = large_initial_width(L)
    while s > sqrt(n) * smoothing_parameter(L):
        samples = sample_discrete_gaussian(L, s)
        s = lwe_oracle.refine(L, samples, s)   # quantum (Regev) or
                                               # classical w/ exp. modulus (Peikert)
    return short_independent_vectors(samples)
```

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Regev's quantum reduction: from SIVP/GapSVP to search-LWE

Regev's theorem (STOC 2005; journal version 2009 [1]) is the founding result. Formally:

> **Theorem (Regev).** Let $0 < \alpha < 1$, $q \geq 2$, and let $\chi$ be a Gaussian error distribution of standard deviation $\alpha q \geq 2\sqrt{n}$. If there is an efficient algorithm solving search-LWE$_{q,n,\chi}$ on the average, then there is an efficient *quantum* algorithm solving GapSVP$_\gamma$ and SIVP$_\gamma$ on arbitrary $n$-dimensional lattices for $\gamma = \tilde{O}(n/\alpha)$.

The proof is an *iterative quantum reduction* with two nested oracles:

- An outer loop reduces GapSVP to the discrete-Gaussian sampling problem DGS on the dual lattice.
- An inner loop solves DGS using the LWE oracle, bootstrapping from wide to narrow Gaussians.

The quantum ingredient is a procedure that, given classical samples from a discrete Gaussian of width $s$ and access to the LWE oracle, produces *quantum* samples from a narrower Gaussian: the LWE solver tells us how to subtract the "offset" of the wide Gaussian samples, and quantum reversibility turns that subtraction into state preparation. Iterating $\mathrm{poly}(n)$ times drives $s$ from superpolynomial down to $\Theta(\sqrt{n}) \cdot \eta(\mathcal{L})$, at which point short vectors fall out directly.

**Parameter reading.** For polynomial approximation factors $\gamma = \mathrm{poly}(n)$, we need $\alpha = 1/\mathrm{poly}(n)$, i.e., noise of magnitude $\alpha q$ that is an inverse-polynomial fraction of the modulus. The reduction is thus *meaningful* even to quantum skeptics: breaking LWE implies a quantum breakthrough on worst-case lattice problems.

### 4.2 Peikert's classical reduction: removing the quantum at the cost of the modulus

The glaring question Regev left open — explicitly stated in his 2005 abstract — was whether the reduction could be made classical. Peikert (STOC 2009) answered *partially* yes [2]:

> **Theorem (Peikert).** Search-LWE with *exponential* modulus $q \geq 2^{n/2}$ and Gaussian noise of width $\alpha q \geq 2\sqrt{n}$ is classically at least as hard as GapSVP$_\gamma$ with $\gamma = \tilde{O}(n/\alpha)$.

The key innovation is a *classical* procedure for iterative Gaussian narrowing. Instead of Regev's quantum state preparation, Peikert shows that when the modulus is exponential, the LWE oracle's answers can be used to *round* wide-Gaussian samples to a coarser sublattice classically. The exponential modulus buys room for a "noise hierarchy": nested LWE problems at different scales can be embedded in one modulus, so each oracle call leaks information about only one scale while the others stay hidden by noise flooding.

**Why the exponential modulus is the price of classicality.** The classical rounding step needs the error to survive rounding to a coarser grid *without* the secret being recoverable from the rounded value alone. With polynomial $q$, there is no room: the rounding step either destroys too much information (the reduction stalls) or leaks the secret (the oracle becomes useless for the next iteration). Exponential $q$ provides $\Omega(n)$ bits of "headroom" per scale, enough to nest the whole iteration inside one modulus.

### 4.3 BLP+13: modulus reduction and the dimension–modulus tradeoff

Brakerski, Langlois, Peikert, Regev, and Stehlé (STOC 2013) closed the gap [3] with a deceptively simple idea, adapted from techniques in fully homomorphic encryption: *modulus reduction* (also called modulus switching).

Given an LWE instance $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{e}) \bmod p$ with modulus $p$, map it to modulus $q < p$ by randomized rounding:

$$\mathbf{A}' = \left\lfloor \frac{q}{p} \mathbf{A} \right\rceil, \quad \mathbf{b}' = \left\lfloor \frac{q}{p} \mathbf{b} \right\rceil \bmod q.$$

The rounding introduces fresh noise of magnitude $\approx q/p \cdot \|\mathbf{s}\|$, so the new noise width is $\alpha' \approx \alpha + \|\mathbf{s}\|/p$. If the secret is short (or made short via the Hermite-normal-form transformation $\mathbf{s} \gets \mathbf{e}$), this adds only a modest amount of noise. The crucial consequence: **hardness transfers from dimension $n$ with exponential modulus to dimension $n' = \tilde{O}(n \log p / \log q)$ with polynomial modulus**, with only polynomial loss in the approximation factor.

> **Theorem (BLP+13).** LWE with polynomial modulus $q = \mathrm{poly}(n)$ and Gaussian noise of width $\alpha q \geq \omega(\sqrt{\log n})$ is classically at least as hard as GapSVP$_{\tilde{O}(n/\alpha)}$ on arbitrary lattices.

The full parameter picture, combining Peikert + BLP+13:

| Result | Modulus $q$ | Reduction type | Worst-case problem | Approximation $\gamma$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Regev '05 | $\mathrm{poly}(n)$ | quantum | GapSVP, SIVP | $\tilde{O}(n/\alpha)$ |
| Peikert '09 | $\geq 2^{n/2}$ (exponential) | classical | GapSVP | $\tilde{O}(n/\alpha)$ |
| Peikert '09 | $\mathrm{poly}(n)$, product of primes | classical | nonstandard lattice problem | $\tilde{O}(n/\alpha)$ |
| BLP+13 | $\mathrm{poly}(n)$ | classical | GapSVP | $\tilde{O}(n/\alpha)$, dimension blowup |

The BLP+13 paper also resolved the *tradeoff landscape*: there is a smooth curve between dimension and modulus, and the reduction techniques capture it essentially optimally. No fully classical reduction from GapSVP on all lattices to LWE with *small* polynomial modulus and *tiny* noise is known — and there are oracle-separation barriers suggesting the quantum step may be genuinely necessary in the polynomial-modulus regime.

### 4.4 The geometry of ideal lattices: Ring-LWE and the canonical embedding

Plain LWE has a quadratic overhead: keys and ciphertexts are matrices in $\mathbb{Z}_q^{m \times n}$. Ring-LWE (Lyubashevsky–Peikert–Regev, EUROCRYPT 2010 [4]) replaces $\mathbb{Z}_q^n$ with the ring $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(\Phi_m(x))$, typically $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^n + 1)$ for $n$ a power of two, turning matrices into single ring elements — a factor-$n$ efficiency gain.

The reduction, however, can no longer appeal to worst-case problems on *all* lattices: the algebraic structure constrains the geometry. The right worst-case objects are **ideal lattices**: for an ideal $\mathcal{I}$ of the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ of a number field $K$ of degree $n$, the *canonical embedding*

$$\sigma: K \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^{s_1} \times \mathbb{C}^{s_2} \cong \mathbb{R}^n$$

maps $\mathcal{I}$ to a full-rank lattice $\sigma(\mathcal{I}) \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ that is stable under multiplication by $\mathcal{O}_K$. The reduction proves:

> **Theorem (LPR).** Search Ring-LWE over $R_q$ is at least as hard (under a quantum reduction) as approximating SIVP$_\gamma$ on *ideal* lattices in the $m$-th cyclotomic field, for $\gamma = \tilde{O}(\sqrt{n}/\alpha)$.

Two geometric subtleties deserve emphasis:

1. **Coefficient vs. canonical embedding.** Implementations use the *coefficient* embedding (polynomial coefficients as vectors), which is convenient but does not preserve lengths. The reduction works in the *canonical* embedding, where multiplication by ring elements is a rotation-scaling (hence length-distorting in a controlled way). Translating between embeddings multiplies approximation factors by the field's *expansion factor* — polynomial for cyclotomics, potentially exponential for arbitrary fields. This is why Ring-LWE security proofs restrict to cyclotomic fields.

**Module-LWE** (Langlois–Stehlé 2015 [6]) generalizes both: replace the ring $R_q$ by $R_q^k$, so secrets are vectors over the ring. This interpolates between plain LWE ($k = n$, rank-1 modules over $\mathbb{Z}$) and Ring-LWE ($k = 1$). The worst-case hypothesis becomes SIVP on *module lattices* — lattices that are $R$-modules. NIST's ML-KEM and ML-DSA use module rank $k \in \{2,3,4\}$ over the cyclotomic ring of degree 256, precisely the regime where module-lattice reductions are tightest.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 The reduction theorems, precisely stated

We collect the main quantitative statements with their parameter regimes. Let $n$ be the lattice dimension, $q$ the LWE modulus, and $\alpha q \geq 2\sqrt{n}$ the noise width (continuous Gaussian), unless noted.

> **Theorem 5.1 (Regev [1]).** There is a polynomial-time quantum reduction from GapSVP$_{\tilde{O}(n/\alpha)}$ and SIVP$_{\tilde{O}(n/\alpha)}$ on arbitrary $n$-dimensional lattices to search-LWE$_{n,q,\alpha}$ with $q = \mathrm{poly}(n)$.

> **Theorem 5.2 (Peikert [2]).** There is a polynomial-time *classical* reduction from GapSVP$_{\tilde{O}(n/\alpha)}$ on arbitrary $n$-dimensional lattices to search-LWE$_{n,q,\alpha}$ with $q \geq 2^{n/2}$ (exponential modulus).

> **Theorem 5.3 (BLP+13 [3]).** There is a polynomial-time *classical* reduction from GapSVP$_{\tilde{O}(n/\alpha)}$ on arbitrary $n'$-dimensional lattices to search-LWE$_{n,q,\alpha}$ with $q = \mathrm{poly}(n)$, where $n' = \tilde{O}(n \log q_{\text{exp}} / \log q)$.

> **Theorem 5.4 (LPR [4]).** There is a polynomial-time quantum reduction from SIVP$_{\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n}/\alpha)}$ on ideal lattices in the $m$-th cyclotomic number field to search Ring-LWE$_{R_q, \alpha}$.

> **Theorem 5.5 (Langlois–Stehlé [6]).** There is a polynomial-time quantum reduction from SIVP$_\gamma$ on module lattices of rank $k$ over cyclotomic rings to Module-LWE with compatible parameters.

### 5.2 Concrete parameter reading for NIST standards

Consider ML-KEM-768: module rank $k = 3$, ring degree $n = 256$, modulus $q = 3329$, centered binomial noise of width $\approx \sqrt{2}$ per coefficient. The total lattice dimension is $kn = 768$. The reduction ladder applies with $\alpha \approx 2/3329$, giving a worst-case approximation factor $\gamma = \tilde{O}(kn/\alpha) \approx 10^6$ — enormous, far beyond the reach of known algorithms. This enormous gap is the point: the reduction is not tight enough for *concrete* security estimates, but it rules out any attack that would refute worst-case hardness.

| Scheme | LWE variant | Dimension | Modulus | Noise | Reduction basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-KEM (Kyber) | Module-LWE | $256 \times k$, $k \in \{2,3,4\}$ | 3329 | CBD, $\eta \in \{2,3\}$ | Thm 5.5 (module) |
| ML-DSA (Dilithium) | Module-LWE + Module-SIS | $256 \times (k, \ell)$ | $2^{23} - 2^{13} + 1$ | uniform $[- \eta, \eta]$ | Thm 5.5 + Ajtai-type |
| FrodoKEM | Plain LWE | 640–1344 | $2^{15}$–$2^{16}$ | narrow discrete Gaussian | Thm 5.3 (classical) |

Note FrodoKEM deliberately uses *plain* LWE precisely to inherit the strongest reduction basis (Theorem 5.3, classical, arbitrary lattices) at the cost of larger keys — a direct engineering consequence of the reduction landscape.

### 5.3 A worked miniature: the smoothing parameter in the iteration

To make the iterative narrowing concrete, consider the final step of Regev's iteration. We have samples from $D_{\mathcal{L}, s}$ with $s = c\sqrt{n}\,\eta(\mathcal{L})$ for a small constant $c$. By the definition of the smoothing parameter, each sample satisfies $\|\mathbf{v}\| \leq s\sqrt{n}$ with overwhelming probability (Gaussian tail bound), and Banaszczyk's transference gives $\eta(\mathcal{L}) \leq \tilde{O}(1)/\lambda_1(\mathcal{L}^*)$. Collecting $n$ linearly independent such samples solves SIVP with factor $\tilde{O}(n/\alpha)$, closing the loop.

##6 Limitations

**Approximation-factor looseness.** Every reduction in this paper loses a factor of $\tilde{O}(n/\alpha)$ — polynomial but large. In practice, LWE is assumed hard for approximation factors far smaller than anything the reductions justify. There is no known *tight* worst-case to average-case reduction for LWE, and the gap between the reduction's $\gamma \approx n^3$–$n^6$ and the $\gamma \approx 2^{n^{o(1)}}$ relevant to cryptanalysis is a genuine epistemic hole: schemes are secure under the reductions only against adversaries far weaker than the best known ones.

**The quantum question remains open.** Despite Peikert and BLP+13, no classical reduction from GapSVP on *all* lattices to LWE with polynomial modulus and the *standard* noise rate is known. There is evidence the quantum step may be essential: the known classical reductions either pay with exponential modulus (Peikert) or with a dimension blowup plus noise growth (BLP+13). Whether "fully classical" worst-case hardness for standard LWE exists is the most important open problem in the foundations of lattice cryptography.

**Ideal lattices are not all lattices.** Ring-LWE and Module-LWE reductions assume worst-case hardness of problems on *ideal* and *module* lattices — a strict subset of all lattices with extra algebraic structure. While no algorithm is known to exploit this structure for the relevant parameters (the subfield and overstretched-NTRU attacks target different parameter regimes), the hypothesis is strictly weaker than hardness for all lattices. This is the price of efficiency, and it is why conservative designs (FrodoKEM) retain plain LWE.

**The error distribution gap.** Reductions are proved for (continuous or discrete) *Gaussian* noise, but deployed schemes use centered binomial or uniform noise for efficiency and side-channel resistance. Rényi-divergence arguments bridge this gap, but they add another layer of approximation loss that the theorems above do not account for.

##7 Conclusion

The worst-case to average-case reductions for LWE are the crown jewels of lattice cryptography's foundations, and their history is a case study in how a single open problem — Regev's "can this be made classical?" — can drive a decade of progress. We have traced the arc: Regev's quantum reduction establishing the paradigm [1]; Peikert's classical reduction exposing the modulus as the price of classicality [2]; the BLP+13 modulus-reduction technique recovering classical hardness for polynomial moduli via a dimension–modulus tradeoff [3]; and the algebraic extensions to ideal and module lattices that carry these guarantees to the efficient schemes actually being standardized [4, 6].

Three directions stand out. **First**, closing the classicality gap: either a fully classical reduction for standard LWE, or a proof that quantum computation is necessary. **Second**, tightening approximation factors: reductions losing only $\mathrm{polylog}(n)$ would turn qualitative reassurance into quantitative security estimates. **Third**, mapping ideal-lattice hardness: determining exactly which algebraic structures admit attacks, and proving cyclotomic module lattices avoid them, would put the NIST standards on the firmest possible footing. Until then, the reduction ladder stands as both lattice cryptography's greatest theoretical asset and a standing invitation to do better.
