# Succinct Zero-Knowledge Arguments: zk-SNARKs from Arithmetic Circuits to Groth16

## Abstract

Zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (zk-SNARKs) allow a prover to convince a verifier that a computation was executed correctly without revealing private inputs, producing proofs of constant size verifiable in milliseconds. This article develops the complete theoretical pipeline underlying the Groth16 proof system [1]: from high-level programs compiled into arithmetic circuits, through Rank-1 Constraint Systems (R1CS) and Quadratic Arithmetic Programs (QAPs) [2], to the pairing-based argument itself. We present the divisibility characterization A(x)B(x) − C(x) = H(x)Z(x) that reduces circuit satisfiability to a single polynomial identity, derive the Groth16 trusted setup, proving, and verification algorithms, and analyze the powers-of-tau ceremony generating the structured reference string [6]. We discuss the knowledge-of-exponent assumptions and generic group model underpinning knowledge soundness, the blinding technique achieving perfect zero knowledge, empirical prover and verifier costs [8][10], and the scheme's limitations relative to universal and transparent alternatives.

## 1 Introduction

Consider a verifier who wishes to outsource a heavy computation to an untrusted worker, or a blockchain network that must validate thousands of transactions without re-executing each one. In both settings the verifier faces the same dilemma: checking the result should be dramatically cheaper than producing it, and in privacy-sensitive applications the check must leak *nothing* about the private inputs. Zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge — zk-SNARKs — resolve this tension. They compress an arbitrarily long correct execution trace into a proof of a few hundred bytes that anyone can verify in milliseconds [1][9].

The modern lineage of practical zk-SNARKs begins with the quadratic arithmetic programs of Gennaro, Gentry, Parno, and Raykova [2], who showed how to encode circuit satisfiability as a polynomial divisibility problem checkable with bilinear pairings, eliminating the heavy probabilistically checkable proofs of earlier theory. Parno, Gentry, Howell, and Raykova then built Pinocchio [3], the first toolchain compiling C programs into such proofs with only eight group elements and eleven pairings. Groth [1] subsequently reduced this to the information-theoretic minimum for pairing-based arguments: three group elements and three pairings, a construction that now secures shielded transactions in Zcash [9], powers rollup and identity protocols, and underlies toolchains such as Circom and snarkjs.

This article reconstructs that pipeline from first principles. Section 2 fixes notation and security definitions; Section 3 describes our top-down methodology; Section 4 develops the deep theory in five stages — arithmetic circuits and R1CS, QAPs and the divisibility check, the Groth16 proving system, pairings and the trusted setup, and knowledge soundness; Section 5 states the formal completeness and soundness theorems alongside empirical benchmarks; Section 6 examines limitations; and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

We work over a prime field **F**_p and a bilinear group setting. The classical interactive-proof literature — from the knowledge complexity of Goldwasser, Micali, and Rackoff through Kilian's and Micali's succinct arguments — established that interaction and randomness can collapse verification cost. Pairing-based SNARKs make the argument *non-interactive* by replacing the verifier's random challenges with a one-time structured reference string (SRS), also called the common reference string (CRS).

> **Definition:** *SNARG.* A succinct non-interactive argument for an NP relation R is a triple (Setup, Prove, Verify) such that: (i) proofs have size polylogarithmic (in practice constant) in the witness size; (ii) verification runs in time polylogarithmic in the computation, typically linear only in the public input; (iii) no efficient prover can forge a proof for a false statement, except with negligible probability [1].

> **Definition:** *zk-SNARK.* A SNARG is *zero-knowledge* if there exists an efficient simulator producing proofs indistinguishable from honest ones without the witness, and is an *argument of knowledge* if for every efficient prover there exists an extractor recovering a valid witness — knowledge soundness, the property distinguishing arguments of knowledge from mere soundness [4].

The Groth–Sahai proof system [4] provided the first practical non-interactive proofs for bilinear groups and introduced the R1CS-style algebraic language that later work arithmetized. Table 1 places the major pairing-based generations in context.

| Generation | Proof size | Verifier work | Setup |
|---|---|---|---|
| GGPR13 [2] | 8 group elements | 6 pairings | trusted, circuit-specific |
| Pinocchio [3] | 8 group elements | 11 pairings | trusted, circuit-specific |
| Groth16 [1] | **3 group elements** | **3 pairings** | trusted, circuit-specific |
| PLONK-style | ~7 group elements | O(1) pairings | universal, updatable |
| zk-STARKs | ~100 KB | O(log² n) hashes | transparent |

Groth16 sits at the Pareto frontier of *succinctness*: its three-element proofs and single pairing-product equation are optimal among pairing-based arguments [1][5].

## 3 Methodology

Our exposition follows a single top-down compilation pipeline, mirroring how real toolchains (Circom, arkworks, bellman) implement the scheme:

1. **Arithmetization.** A high-level program is compiled into an *arithmetic circuit*: wires carry field elements; gates are additions and multiplications.
2. **Constraint extraction.** Each multiplication gate becomes one rank-1 constraint over a witness vector *w*, forming an R1CS instance (A, B, C).
3. **Polynomial encoding.** R1CS matrices are interpolated into a Quadratic Arithmetic Program; satisfiability becomes polynomial divisibility.
4. **Cryptographic compilation.** A trusted setup samples secret randomness and publishes an SRS encoding encrypted polynomial evaluations; the prover homomorphically evaluates them.
5. **Verification.** The verifier checks one pairing equation that simultaneously enforces divisibility, correct linear combination, and public-input consistency.

We analyze security in the *generic group model* under knowledge-of-exponent style assumptions, the standard idealization in which Groth16 is proved knowledge-sound [1]. Table 2 fixes notation used throughout.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **F**_p | prime field of order p |
| G_1, G_2, G_T | bilinear groups of prime order p |
| e : G_1 × G_2 → G_T | non-degenerate bilinear pairing |
| [x]_1, [x]_2, [x]_T | x·g_1, x·g_2, x·g_T (implicit group notation) |
| n, m | number of wires, number of constraints |
| τ, α, β, γ, δ | trusted-setup trapdoor scalars |
| Z(x) | target polynomial vanishing at constraint points |

A minimal R1CS evaluator illustrates the constraint semantics that everything else builds upon:

```python
def r1cs_check(A, B, C, witness, p):
    # each row: (A row dot w) * (B row dot w) == (C row dot w) mod p
    for i in range(len(A)):
        left = sum(a * w for a, w in zip(A[i], witness)) % p
        right = sum(b * w for b, w in zip(B[i], witness)) % p
        out = sum(c * w for c, w in zip(C[i], witness)) % p
        if (left * right) % p != out:
            return False
    return True
```

```rust
fn dot(u: &[u64], v: &[u64], p: u64) -> u64 {
    u.iter().zip(v.iter()).map(|(a, b)| (a * b) % p).sum::<u64>() % p
}

fn check_constraint(a: &[u64], b: &[u64], c: &[u64], w: &[u64], p: u64) -> bool {
    (dot(a, w, p) * dot(b, w, p)) % p == dot(c, w, p)
}
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Arithmetic Circuits and Rank-1 Constraint Systems

An *arithmetic circuit* over **F**_p is a directed acyclic graph whose wires carry field elements and whose gates compute either sums or products of their inputs [2][3]. Any straight-line program — and hence any bounded computation — can be flattened into such a circuit with only polynomial blowup. The prover's private knowledge is a *witness*: an assignment of field elements to every wire consistent with all gates.

R1CS linearizes this graph. Fix an ordering of the n wires, with w_0 = 1 reserved for the constant term, and let the witness vector be *w* ∈ **F**_p^n. Each multiplication gate yields one constraint of the form

**(A_j · w) · (B_j · w) = (C_j · w),**

where A_j, B_j, C_j are rows of the m × n matrices A, B, C selecting the gate's left input, right input, and output as linear forms. Addition gates are *free*: they are folded into the linear forms of neighboring multiplication gates, so m counts multiplications only. A witness satisfies the circuit exactly when it satisfies every row of the R1CS [2][8].

*Example.* To prove knowledge of x with x³ + x + 5 = 35, introduce wires for x, x², x³ and an intermediate sym_1 = x·x. The R1CS rows encode x·x = x², x²·x = x³, and (x³ + x + 5)·1 = 35 — three constraints over six wires, satisfiable precisely at x = 3. This tiny instance already exhibits the general structure: public outputs are pinned by constraints while private wires remain hidden.

### 4.2 Quadratic Arithmetic Programs and the Divisibility Check

The decisive insight of GGPR [2] is that an entire R1CS — m constraints checked independently — can be compressed into *one* polynomial identity. Choose distinct field points r_1, …, r_m, one per constraint. For each wire i, define polynomials A_i(x), B_i(x), C_i(x) by Lagrange interpolation so that A_i(r_j) equals the (j, i) entry of A, and likewise for B and C. Assemble

**A(x) = Σ_i w_i A_i(x),  B(x) = Σ_i w_i B_i(x),  C(x) = Σ_i w_i C_i(x),**

and let the *target polynomial* be Z(x) = ∏_{j=1}^{m} (x − r_j), which vanishes exactly at the constraint points.

> **Theorem:** *QAP characterization.* A witness *w* satisfies the R1CS if and only if the polynomial P(x) = A(x)B(x) − C(x) is divisible by Z(x); equivalently, there exists H(x) with **A(x)B(x) − C(x) = H(x)Z(x)** [2].

*Proof sketch.* If the R1CS holds, then P(r_j) = 0 at every constraint point, so each (x − r_j) divides P and their product Z(x) divides P. Conversely, divisibility forces P(r_j) = 0 at all m points, recovering each constraint. ∎

This reduces the verifier's burden from re-checking m constraints to checking *one divisibility relation* — but division must be performed *blindly*, on encrypted polynomial evaluations, which is where pairings enter. The Schwartz–Zippel lemma further guarantees that checking the identity at a single random point suffices with overwhelming probability, a fact exploited by the verifier's pairing equation.

### 4.3 The Groth16 Proving System

Groth16 [1] refines the GGPR/Pinocchio blueprint into the smallest possible pairing-based argument. Let the first l wires be the public statement x and the rest the private witness.

**Setup.** Sample uniform trapdoor scalars τ, α, β, γ, δ ∈ **F**_p and *destroy* them — their secrecy is the toxic waste whose compromise permits forgery. Publish the structured reference string:

- G_1 elements: [α]_1, [β]_1, [δ]_1, { [τ^i]_1 }_{i=0}^{n−2}, and { [(β·u_i(τ) + α·v_i(τ) + w_i(τ)) / γ]_1 } for public wires i ≤ l, plus { [(β·u_i(τ) + α·v_i(τ) + w_i(τ)) / δ]_1 } for private wires i > l;
- G_2 elements: [β]_2, [γ]_2, [δ]_2, { [τ^i]_2 }_{i=0}^{n−2}.

**Prove.** Given a satisfying witness, compute A(x), B(x), H(x) as in Section 4.2, sample random blinders r, s, and output the three proof elements

- **A = [α + A(τ) + r·δ]_1,**
- **B = [β + B(τ) + s·δ]_2,**
- **C = [(A(τ) + r·δ)·s + (B(τ) + s·δ)·r − r·s·δ + H(τ)·Z(τ) ... ]_1,**

where C combines the quotient term H(τ)·Z(τ)/δ with the cross-terms needed for the verification identity and the blinding corrections.

**Verify.** Check the single pairing-product equation

**e(A, B) = e([α]_1, [β]_2) · e(X, [γ]_2) · e(C, [δ]_2),**

with X = Σ_{i=0}^{l} x_i·[(β·u_i(τ) + α·v_i(τ) + w_i(τ))/γ]_1 encoding the public input. Expanding the left side via bilinearity reproduces exactly the divisibility identity plus the public-input consistency check; any deviation makes the two sides differ except with negligible probability [1][5]. Table 3 quantifies the improvement over Pinocchio.

| Metric | Pinocchio [3] | Groth16 [1] |
|---|---|---|
| Proof size | 8 group elements | **3 group elements** (~200 bytes on BN254) |
| Verification | 11 pairings | **3 pairings** (one product equation) |
| Prover work | O(n) exponentiations + FFT | O(n log n), dominated by three multi-exponentiations |
| CRS size | 7n + m group elements | n + 2m group elements |

### 4.4 Pairings and the Trusted Setup

A *bilinear pairing* is a map e : G_1 × G_2 → G_T between prime-order groups satisfying bilinearity e(a·P, b·Q) = ab·e(P, Q), non-degeneracy, and efficient computability [7]. It lets the verifier *multiply encrypted values*: given [A(τ)]_1 and [B(τ)]_2, the pairing yields [A(τ)·B(τ)]_T without ever revealing τ. Concretely, the optimal Ate pairing evaluates a Miller loop — accumulating line-function evaluations along the binary expansion of the curve parameter — followed by a final exponentiation raising the result to (p^k − 1)/r, where k is the embedding degree [7]. Deployment curves such as BN254 and BLS12-381 are engineered so that the discrete logarithm problem remains hard simultaneously in G_1, G_2, and G_T.

> **Definition:** *Trusted setup ceremony.* A multi-party protocol generating the SRS such that no participant learns the trapdoor τ. Each contributor multiplies the accumulated powers by their own secret τ_j and publishes a proof of correct update; the final τ = ∏ τ_j remains unknown to everyone provided at least one contributor was honest and deleted their secret [6].

The powers-of-tau ceremony [6] produces the universal backbone {[τ^i]_1, [τ^i]_2} reused across circuits, after which a circuit-specific phase derives the Groth16 CRS. This MPC design replaced the original single-operator setups (as in early Zcash) and is what makes Groth16's per-circuit trusted setup operationally acceptable today.

### 4.5 Knowledge Soundness and Zero Knowledge

Groth16 is proved secure in the *generic group model* under a knowledge-of-exponent style assumption: any adversary producing the proof elements [A]_1, [B]_2 must *know* the linear combination of CRS elements it used, allowing an extractor to recover the witness polynomials and hence the witness itself [1][4]. This is an *argument* (security against computationally bounded provers), not a proof — and the assumption is non-falsifiable, a known price of succinctness without PCPs [2].

Zero knowledge is achieved information-theoretically through the blinders r and s: they randomize A, B, and C subject to the constraint that the verification equation still balances, so the proof distribution is independent of the witness. A simulator can therefore sample valid-looking (A, B, C) triples from the trapdoor alone, and no verifier learns anything beyond the statement's truth [1].

## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

The scheme's correctness rests on two theorems, whose proofs we sketch.

> **Theorem:** *Completeness.* If Setup, Prove, and Verify are executed honestly on a satisfying witness, Verify accepts with probability 1 [1].

*Sketch.* Bilinearity expands e(A, B) into e([α]_1,[β]_2)·e([A(τ)]_1,[B(τ)]_2) times blinding cross-terms; substituting A(τ)B(τ) = H(τ)Z(τ) + C(τ) and collecting terms yields exactly the right-hand side, with the δ-denominated terms absorbing r and s. ∎

> **Theorem:** *Knowledge soundness (generic group model).* For every efficient prover producing an accepting proof, an extractor outputs a witness satisfying the R1CS, except with negligible probability [1][5].

*Sketch.* In the generic group model the prover's group elements are formal linear combinations of CRS elements; the knowledge assumption extracts the coefficients, the verification equation forces the divisibility identity on the extracted polynomials, and the QAP theorem converts divisibility back into a satisfying assignment. ∎

Empirically, Groth16 delivers on its asymptotics. The prover is dominated by FFT-based polynomial multiplication and multi-scalar multiplications — quasi-linear in the constraint count — while verification touches only the public input plus three pairings (~1.5 ms) [8]. Representative measurements on commodity hardware (BN254, libsnark) are summarized in Table 4 [10].

| Constraints | CRS size | Prove time | Verify time | Proof size |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100,000 | ~162 MB | ~4.5 s | ~1.5 ms | ~200 bytes |
| 500,000 | ~810 MB | ~22 s | ~1.5 ms | ~200 bytes |
| 1,250,000 | ~2 GB | ~56 s | ~1.5 ms | ~200 bytes |

The striking feature is the *asymmetry*: proof size and verification time are independent of circuit size, while prover cost and CRS size grow linearly — the precise trade-off that makes verifiable delegation and on-chain verification economical [8][10].

## 6 Limitations

Groth16's optimality in proof size comes with structural costs that practitioners must weigh:

- **Circuit-specific trusted setup.** Every new circuit demands its own ceremony phase; reusing parameters across circuits is impossible, unlike universal setups [6][8].
- **Toxic waste.** Secrecy of τ, α, β, γ, δ is load-bearing: a single leaked trapdoor enables undetectable forgery, so the MPC ceremony's honesty assumption is a genuine trust anchor.
- **Non-falsifiable assumptions.** Knowledge-soundness rests on knowledge-of-exponent assumptions unprovable by reduction to standard falsifiable ones — a principled objection, though no attacks are known [1].
- **Quantum vulnerability.** Security reduces to discrete logarithms in pairing-friendly groups, broken by Shor's algorithm; Groth16 is not post-quantum, unlike hash-based STARKs [8].
- **Prover and CRS scaling.** Linear prover work and gigabyte-scale CRS files bound the feasible circuit size; recursion and aggregation (e.g., folding schemes) are active mitigations.

| Property | Groth16 [1] | PLONK | zk-STARK |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proof size | ~200 bytes | ~400 bytes | ~100 KB |
| Setup | trusted, per-circuit | universal, updatable | transparent |
| Post-quantum | no | no | yes |
| Prover | O(n log n) | O(n log n) | O(n log² n) |

These trade-offs explain Groth16's continued dominance where proof size and verification cost dominate — on-chain verification, embedded verifiers — and its displacement by universal or transparent systems where setup agility or quantum resistance matters [8].

## 7 Conclusion

We have traced the full arc from arithmetic circuits through R1CS and quadratic arithmetic programs to the Groth16 argument: three group elements, one pairing equation, and a proof that a computation was performed correctly while revealing nothing else. The QAP divisibility characterization is the conceptual hinge — everything before it is arithmetization, everything after it is cryptography. Groth16's minimal proof size [1][5], its MPC-based trusted setup [6], and its quasi-linear prover [8][10] make it the reference point against which all later SNARKs are measured.

Open directions include weakening the knowledge assumptions toward falsifiable ones, compressing the prover via hardware acceleration and folding, and recursive composition that lets proofs verify proofs — each extending the reach of succinct zero knowledge without disturbing the elegant core that Groth16 established.
