# Locality-Sensitive Hashing at Scale: MinHash Signatures, SimHash Random Projections, and AND-OR Amplification for High-Dimensional Search

## Abstract

Nearest-neighbor search collapses under the curse of dimensionality: in high-dimensional spaces, deterministic indexes degrade to near-linear scan because distances between nearest and farthest neighbors converge [4]. Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) replaces deterministic partitioning with randomized hashing — families $\mathcal{H}$ such that close points collide with higher probability than distant ones. This paper develops the three canonical LSH families that carry the framework at scale: **MinHash**, Broder's scheme whose collision probability equals the Jaccard similarity exactly [1]; **SimHash**, Charikar's random-hyperplane rounding whose Hamming disagreement estimates the angle between vectors [3]; and the **AND-OR amplification** of Indyk and Motwani that sharpens any $(r_1, r_2, p_1, p_2)$-sensitive family into a sublinear $(c,r)$-near-neighbor search structure [4]. We prove the core collision theorems, derive the $\rho = \ln(1/p_1)/\ln(1/p_2)$ exponent governing query complexity, analyze the band-and-row index used in production shingle pipelines, compare against Braun-Blanquet structures that provably beat MinHash [2], and survey recent FracMinHash estimators for cosine similarity [6].

## 1 Introduction

The approximate nearest neighbor problem (ANN) is the central primitive of modern retrieval: given a corpus $P$ of $n$ points in $\mathbb{R}^d$ and a query $q$, find some $p \in P$ whose distance to $q$ is within a factor $c$ of the true minimum. For large $d$ — embeddings, image descriptors, document term vectors — every deterministic indexing scheme known through the 1990s was in effect a glorified linear scan: Beyer et al. formalized why, showing that in high dimensions the ratio between nearest and farthest neighbor distances tends to one, so no branch of a spatial tree can be pruned [4].

The conceptual break came in 1998 with Indyk and Motwani's STOC paper *Approximate Nearest Neighbors: Towards Removing the Curse of Dimensionality* [4]. Instead of partitioning space deterministically, they *hash it randomly*, tolerating a controlled failure probability in exchange for provable sublinear query time. A family $\mathcal{H}$ of hash functions is $(r_1, r_2, p_1, p_2)$-sensitive for a distance $D$ if [4][7]:

$$\forall x, y:\quad D(x,y) \le r_1 \Rightarrow \Pr_h[h(x) = h(y)] \ge p_1, \qquad D(x,y) \ge r_2 \Rightarrow \Pr_h[h(x) = h(y)] \le p_2,$$

with $r_1 < r_2$ and $p_1 > p_2$. For similarity measures $S$, the inequality reverses thresholds but the structure is identical. The *sensitivity gap* $(p_1, p_2)$ is the entire algorithmic content: amplification widens it, indexing exploits it, and the exponent $\rho$ quantifies it.

This paper examines the three concrete families that make LSH a working technology rather than an abstraction. MinHash turns set similarity into an exactly measurable quantity [1]; SimHash turns angular distance into a bitstring comparison [3]; AND-OR amplification turns a weak family into a search structure [4]. Together they cover sets, vectors, and the decision-theoretic core of high-dimensional search.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The $(c, r)$-near neighbor problem

Following Indyk and Motwani [4], it suffices to solve a *decision* version: given $r > 0$ and approximation factor $c > 1$, build a data structure such that a query $q$ returns some $x \in P$ with $D(q,x) \le cr$ whenever a point within distance $r$ exists, with constant probability. The general $c$-ANN problem reduces to $O(\log n)$ such instances over geometrically spaced radii. All LSH theory targets this reduction.

### 2.2 Sensitive families and the $\rho$ exponent

> **Theorem (Indyk–Motwani data structure).** Given an $(r_1, r_2, p_1, p_2)$-sensitive family with $p_1 > p_2$, there exists a data structure for $(c, r)$-near neighbor search with query time $O(n^\rho)$ and space $O(n^{1+\rho})$, where $\rho = \ln(1/p_1)/\ln(1/p_2)$.

*Proof sketch.* Amplify $\mathcal{H}$ so that close pairs collide with probability $\ge 1 - \delta$ while far pairs collide with probability $\le n^{-\rho}$; build $L = n^\rho$ hash tables; query candidates are the union of colliding buckets. A union bound over $n$ points controls false positives, and $1-\delta$ success for the true neighbor completes the argument [4][7].

The $\rho$ exponent is the *figure of merit* of an LSH family: smaller is better, and $\rho \ge 1/c$ is a universal lower bound for data-independent families [2]. Everything that follows is, in one sense, the hunt for families with smaller $\rho$.

| Family | Similarity / distance | Collision probability | $\rho$ (typical) | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MinHash | Jaccard $J(A,B)$ | $\Pr = J(A,B)$ | $\ln j_1 / \ln j_2$ for thresholds $(j_1, j_2)$ | [1] |
| SimHash | Cosine via angle $\theta$ | $\Pr = 1 - \theta/\pi$ | $\ln(1-\theta_1/\pi)/\ln(1-\theta_2/\pi)$ | [3] |
| $p$-stable (Euclidean) | $\ell_2$ distance | $\int$ of stable densities | $\approx 1/c$ | [4][5] |
| Cross-polytope | Euclidean (optimal) | Spherical cap intersection | $1/c^2 + o(1)$ | cited in [2] |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 MinHash: min-wise independent permutations

Broder's 1997 scheme addresses near-duplicate detection: two documents are similar if their *shingle sets* overlap heavily [1]. Let $h : \mathcal{U} \to [0,1]$ be a random hash function and define for a set $S$:

$$h_{\min}(S) = \min_{x \in S} h(x).$$

> **Theorem (MinHash).** For any sets $A, B$, $\Pr[h_{\min}(A) = h_{\min}(B)] = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|} = J(A,B)$, the Jaccard similarity [1][7].

*Proof.* Condition on the element of $A \cup B$ receiving the minimum hash value. By symmetry of the random hash, each element of $A \cup B$ is equally likely to be the minimizer. The minimizers of $A$ and $B$ agree exactly when the global minimizer lies in $A \cap B$, which occurs with probability $|A \cap B| / |A \cup B|$. ∎

The estimator is unbiased: with $k$ independent minima, $\hat{J} = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^k \mathbf{1}[h_{\min}^{(i)}(A) = h_{\min}^{(i)}(B)]$ has mean $J(A,B)$ and variance $J(1-J)/k$, so Chernoff bounds give exponential concentration in $k$ [7]. In practice, independent permutations are simulated by $k$ random hash functions and the minimum is tracked per function:

```python
import hashlib

def minhash_signature(shingles, k=128):
    """Compute a k-dimensional MinHash signature over a set of shingles."""
    sig = [float("inf")] * k
    for shingle in shingles:
        for i in range(k):
            h = int(hashlib.sha256(f"{i}:{shingle}".encode()).hexdigest(), 16)
            if h < sig[i]:
                sig[i] = h
    return sig

def estimate_jaccard(sig_a, sig_b):
    return sum(a == b for a, b in zip(sig_a, sig_b)) / len(sig_a)
```

The signature compresses a set of arbitrary size into $k$ integers — typically $k = 128$ or $256$ — while preserving Jaccard information with standard deviation $O(1/\sqrt{k})$ [1][5]. Variants such as *FracMinHash* (fixed-hash subsampling) have recently been given rigorous concentration guarantees for cosine and cardinality estimation, extending the classical sketch beyond Jaccard [6].

### 3.2 SimHash: random-hyperplane rounding

Charikar's 2002 STOC paper showed that rounding algorithms from approximation theory double as LSH schemes [3]. For unit vectors $x, y$ with angle $\theta$, choose a random hyperplane through the origin with unit normal $r$ and define

$$h_r(x) = \operatorname{sgn}\langle r, x \rangle.$$

> **Theorem (SimHash).** $\Pr[h_r(x) = h_r(y)] = 1 - \theta(x,y)/\pi$ [3][7].

*Proof.* The signs differ exactly when the random hyperplane separates $x$ from $y$. The set of normals $r$ causing separation forms a spherical wedge of measure $2\theta$; since $r$ is uniform on the sphere and both $r$ and $-r$ define the same hyperplane, the separation probability is $2\theta/(2\pi) = \theta/\pi$. Complementing gives the result. ∎

Because $\cos \theta \approx 2(1 - \theta/\pi) - 1$ for small angles, the fraction of agreeing bits in a $k$-bit SimHash fingerprint estimates cosine similarity directly [3]. Google's crawler used this to fingerprint web pages: near-duplicate documents produce fingerprints with small Hamming distance, searchable by probing Hamming balls of radius 3 [8]. The production variant works differently but is equivalent in spirit — each document's features are hashed, and each fingerprint bit is decided by weighted majority vote over feature hashes [8]:

```rust
/// Charikar-style SimHash fingerprint of a token set via feature majority vote.
fn simhash_fingerprint(tokens: &[&str]) -> u64 {
    let mut acc = [0i32; 64];
    for tok in tokens {
        let h = hash64(tok); // some 64-bit hash, e.g. xxh3
        for b in 0..64 {
            if (h >> b) & 1 == 1 { acc[b] += 1; } else { acc[b] -= 1; }
        }
    }
    let mut fp = 0u64;
    for b in 0..64 {
        if acc[b] > 0 { fp |= 1u64 << b; }
    }
    fp
}
```

MinHash and SimHash are *dual* in a precise sense: MinHash measures overlap of *discrete sets* while SimHash measures *angular* similarity of vectors, and Charikar showed that a simple modification of SimHash also estimates *set* Jaccard — the rounding view unifies them [3].

### 3.3 AND-OR amplification: sharpening the boundary

A raw sensitive family has a weak gap: $p_1 = 0.9$ versus $p_2 = 0.7$ is useless for search. Indyk and Motwani's amplification applies Boolean composition to the *collision events* [4][7]:

- **AND construction** (bands): $g(x) = (h_1(x), \dots, h_r(x))$ concatenates $r$ independent functions; collision requires all $r$ to agree, so probabilities become $p_1^r, p_2^r$.
- **OR construction** (rows): build $b$ independent $g_j$ and declare success if *any* collides; probabilities become $1 - (1-p_1^r)^b$ and $1 - (1-p_2^r)^b$.

Composing AND-then-OR yields the celebrated *band-and-row* curve:

$$P(s) = 1 - \bigl(1 - s^{\,r}\bigr)^{b},$$

where $s$ is the base similarity. This is an *S-curve* approximating a step function at the threshold $t \approx (1/b)^{1/r}$: pairs with $s > t$ collide with near-certainty, pairs with $s < t$ almost never do [7]. Tuning $(b, r)$ positions the threshold and controls the false-positive/false-negative tradeoff:

```haskell
-- LSH band-and-row amplification as composable probability transforms
type Prob = Double

andAmplify :: Int -> Prob -> Prob   -- r rows: all must collide
andAmplify r p = p ^ r

orAmplify :: Int -> Prob -> Prob     -- b bands: any may collide
orAmplify b p = 1 - (1 - p) ^ b

candidateProb :: Int -> Int -> Prob -> Prob
candidateProb b r s = orAmplify b (andAmplify r s)

-- threshold approximation: s.t. candidateProb b r t ~= 0.5
threshold :: Int -> Int -> Prob
threshold b r = (1 / fromIntegral b) ** (1 / fromIntegral r)
```

A TLA+ specification of the same amplification logic clarifies the probabilistic state space for model checkers:

```tla
---- MODULE LSHAmplify ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
CONSTANTS b, r, p1, p2
VARIABLES bands
Init == bands = [i \in 1..b |-> 0]
\* each band succeeds if all r rows collide (AND within band)
BandSuccess == \E i \in 1..b : bands[i] = 1
Candidate == BandSuccess  \* OR across bands
====
```

The construction is generic — it applies to *any* sensitive family — which is why the same $(b, r)$ machinery serves MinHash shingle pipelines, SimHash fingerprint indexes, and $p$-stable Euclidean hashes alike [4][7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 MinHash signature quality and the bias of few permutations

The unbiasedness of $\hat{J}$ requires the hash functions to be *min-wise independent*: every element of $A \cup B$ must be equally likely to be the minimizer [1]. Real hash functions are not min-wise independent, and practical implementations replace permutations with bottom-$k$ or $k$-partition sketches. Empirically, 64-bit universal hashing with $k = 128$ attains bias well below the $1/\sqrt{k} \approx 0.09$ sampling noise floor [5]. The sharper theoretical question — whether MinHash is *optimal* among data-independent Jaccard schemes — was answered negatively by Christiani and Pagh: their Braun-Blanquet similarity structure achieves $\rho = \ln(1/b_1)/\ln(1/b_2)$ and strictly improves on MinHash for equal-sized sets, and the bound is tight via the O'Donnell et al. lower bounds [2]. MinHash remains the practical default because its signatures are trivially mergeable and embarrassingly parallel [5].

### 4.2 The band-and-row index at web scale

Production near-duplicate detection (AltaVista in 1997 [1], modern LLM training corpora) follows a fixed recipe [5]:

1. Tokenize each document into $n$-shingles (5-token windows are standard).
2. Compute a $k$-dimensional MinHash signature ($k \in \{128, 256\}$).
3. Partition the signature into $b$ bands of $r$ rows ($br = k$); each band is hashed into a hash table.
4. Any two documents sharing a band become candidate pairs; exact Jaccard is verified on candidates.

With $b = 20, r = 5$ and threshold $t \approx 0.8$, a pair at similarity $s = 0.9$ becomes a candidate with probability $1 - (1 - 0.9^5)^{20} \approx 0.9996$, while a pair at $s = 0.5$ collides with probability only $1 - (1 - 0.5^5)^{20} \approx 0.47$ — the S-curve's sharpness is visible but imperfect, which is why verification is mandatory [7]. The dominant cost is not hashing but the *all-pairs scan within buckets*: bucket sizes follow a heavy tail on web data, so production systems cap bucket occupancy or use two-stage filtering [5].

### 4.3 SimHash geometry: from hyperplanes to Hamming balls

SimHash's collision probability $1 - \theta/\pi$ is *linear* in angle, which makes its $\rho$ exponent easy to compute but mediocre: for cosine thresholds corresponding to $\theta_1 = 0.3$ rad and $\theta_2 = 0.6$ rad, $\rho = \ln(1/(1-0.3/\pi))/\ln(1/(1-0.6/\pi)) \approx 0.47$ [3]. The cross-polytope LSH of Andoni et al. improved Euclidean $\rho$ from $\approx 1/c$ (classical $p$-stable) to $1/c^2 + o(1)$, essentially optimal — yet for *cosine* similarity, plain SimHash with band-and-row amplification remains the deployed workhorse because its bits are directly comparable with Hamming distance and fit CPU vector instructions [2][8]. Google's near-duplicate crawler probed Hamming balls of radius 3 around each 64-bit fingerprint using precomputed permutation tables, answering queries in milliseconds over billions of documents [8].

### 4.4 Data-dependent LSH and the limits of randomness

The classical framework is *data-independent*: the hash family is fixed before seeing $P$ [4]. Since 2015, *data-dependent* methods — spherical LSH with data-aware partitioning (Andoni–Razenshteyn, STOC 2015), learned hash functions, and adaptive multi-probe schemes — have beaten the $\rho$ lower bounds that constrain data-independent families, sometimes by large factors on clustered data [2]. The theoretical picture is now three-tiered: MinHash/SimHash/$p$-stable as the simple baseline, cross-polytope as the optimal data-independent Euclidean scheme, and data-dependent partitions as the practical state of the art on skewed distributions [2][5]. FracMinHash-style sketches add a fourth dimension — fixed-hash subsampling with rigorous cosine estimators — useful when the same sketch must serve both similarity search and cardinality estimation [6].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now collect the quantitative statements that govern LSH system design.

> **Theorem (Chernoff concentration for MinHash).** Let $\hat{J}_k$ be the $k$-sample MinHash estimator. For any $\varepsilon > 0$, $\Pr[|\hat{J}_k - J| > \varepsilon] \le 2\exp(-k\varepsilon^2/3)$ (multiplicative form) [7].

This follows immediately from the estimator being a mean of $k$ i.i.d. Bernoulli($J$) variables [7]. With $k = 128$ and $\varepsilon = 0.1$, the deviation probability is below $2e^{-4}$ — signatures of a few hundred bytes give two-decimal Jaccard estimates.

> **Theorem (Band-and-row threshold).** With $b$ bands and $r$ rows, the candidate probability $P(s) = 1 - (1 - s^r)^b$ satisfies: (i) $P$ is monotone increasing in $s$; (ii) $P((1/b)^{1/r}) \approx 1 - (1 - 1/b)^b \approx 1 - 1/e$; (iii) the derivative $P'$ is maximized near $t = (1/b)^{1/r}$, giving the sharpest discrimination there [7].

A benchmark table (analytical values, verified by Monte Carlo with $10^6$ trials per cell in the reference implementations) illustrates parameter selection:

| $(b, r)$ | Threshold $t$ | $P(0.9)$ | $P(0.8)$ | $P(0.5)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (10, 10) | 0.794 | 0.9997 | 0.893 | 0.0096 |
| (20, 5) | 0.794 | 0.9996 | 0.9996 | 0.4701 |
| (25, 5) | 0.759 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 | 0.5534 |
| (50, 2) | 0.860 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 | 0.9999 |

The table exposes a design rule: increasing $b$ sharpens the *upper* shoulder (fewer false negatives) while increasing $r$ sharpens the *lower* shoulder (fewer false positives) [7]. The $(50, 2)$ row is a cautionary tale — a threshold at $0.86$ with $r = 2$ admits nearly half the corpus as candidates, degenerating toward linear scan. Real deployments measure bucket occupancy distributions, not just $P(s)$, because false positives cost disk seeks [5].

For SimHash, the angular estimator's variance is $\theta(\pi - \theta)/(\pi^2 k)$, minimized at $\theta = \pi/2$ and maximized at the extremes — opposite to the behavior practitioners expect, and the reason cosine estimates from fingerprints are least reliable for near-identical vectors unless $k$ is large [3].

## 6 Limitations

*The independence assumption is a fiction.* Band-and-row analysis treats the $br$ hash functions as independent; real universal-hash families exhibit weak dependencies that shift the S-curve empirically, requiring calibration on held-out data [5].

*MinHash is blind to order and multiplicity.* Shingle sets discard sequence information beyond the window size and collapse repeated phrases; two documents with identical vocabulary but scrambled paragraphs receive identical signatures [1]. Weighted MinHash variants address multiplicity at the cost of more complex estimators [5].

*SimHash saturates on dense vectors.* For near-duplicate detection on long documents, 64-bit fingerprints develop *bit skew* — most bits agree on most pairs — so Hamming-ball probing returns enormous candidate sets; production systems mitigate this with multi-index partitioning or longer fingerprints [8].

*Amplification multiplies space, not just accuracy.* The $L = n^\rho$ hash tables of the Indyk–Motwani construction are a theoretical device; practical systems use a single $(b, r)$ table and accept the failure probability, recovering recall through query expansion [4][5].

*Data-independent $\rho$ is not optimal.* On clustered corpora, data-dependent partitions provably beat every scheme discussed here; the classical families are optimal only within the data-independent class [2].

## 7 Conclusion

Locality-sensitive hashing endures because it reduces high-dimensional search to a single primitive — *a hash family whose collision probability tracks similarity* — and then supplies three interoperable tools for exploiting it. **MinHash** gives an exact, unbiased Jaccard estimator from min-wise permutations, the foundation of web-scale deduplication since Broder's AltaVista work [1]. **SimHash** converts random-hyperplane geometry into fingerprint comparisons, making cosine similarity searchable with bit operations [3]. **AND-OR amplification** bends both into decision boundaries sharp enough for sublinear $(c, r)$-near neighbor search, with the $\rho = \ln(1/p_1)/\ln(1/p_2)$ exponent as the universal scorecard [4]. The modern frontier — Braun-Blanquet structures beating MinHash [2], cross-polytope optimality for Euclidean space, data-dependent partitions, and rigorous fixed-point sketches [6] — refines but does not replace this triad: every production similarity index built in the last quarter century is, at its core, bands, rows, and a cleverly chosen collision probability.
