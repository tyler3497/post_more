# Code-Based Post-Quantum Cryptography: The McEliece Framework, Information-Set Decoding Hardness, and the BIKE and HQC Key-Encapsulation Designs

## Abstract

Code-based cryptography, inaugurated by McEliece's 1978 public-key system built on binary Goppa codes, is the oldest post-quantum family and the only one whose core assumption — decoding a random linear code — has resisted nearly five decades of cryptanalysis, including the advent of quantum cryptanalytic techniques. This thesis treats the McEliece framework rigorously: its Goppa-code trapdoor construction *G′ = SGP*, Patterson's decoding algorithm, and the Niederreiter dual formulation. It analyzes information-set decoding, the universal attack class, from Prange through Stern, BJMM, and May–Ozerov, including quantum variants based on Grover search and quantum walks, and shows how these attacks calibrate NIST security categories. Finally, it dissects the two modern quasi-cyclic key-encapsulation mechanisms that carried code-based cryptography through NIST's fourth standardization round: BIKE, built on quasi-cyclic moderate-density parity-check codes with iterative bit-flipping decoding, and HQC, which pairs a structured Reed–Muller/Reed–Solomon code with a direct reduction to the quasi-cyclic syndrome decoding problem and was selected by NIST in March 2025 as the fifth post-quantum standard. Comparative parameter, bandwidth, and provable-security analyses are provided throughout.

## 1 Introduction

The prospect of cryptographically relevant quantum computers has forced a wholesale re-examination of public-key infrastructure. Shor's algorithm renders the integer-factorization and discrete-logarithm problems — the foundations of RSA, Diffie–Hellman, and elliptic-curve cryptography — solvable in polynomial time on a sufficiently large quantum device. The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) responded with a standardization process, begun in 2016, that has already produced lattice-based standards (ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA) and, in March 2025, selected HQC (Hamming Quasi-Cyclic) as the fifth algorithm for post-quantum encryption [10].

Among the five mathematical families considered by NIST, code-based cryptography occupies a singular position: it is the *oldest*. Robert J. McEliece proposed his public-key cryptosystem based on algebraic coding theory in 1978, contemporaneously with RSA [1]. Its security rests on the difficulty of decoding a general binary linear code — a problem proven NP-complete in the worst case — while the legitimate receiver exploits the hidden algebraic structure of a Goppa code to decode efficiently. Remarkably, the security level of the McEliece system has remained essentially stable across more than four decades of cryptanalytic effort, a record unmatched by any other public-key proposal, classical or post-quantum [2][3].

This longevity is not accidental. Unlike the number-theoretic problems underlying RSA, the decoding problem admits no known quantum speedup beyond generic techniques: Grover's algorithm, quantum walks, and amplitude amplification apply, but no exponential quantum break analogous to Shor's algorithm is known [6]. NIST's security categories explicitly account for this, demanding, for example, at least 2^143 classical bit operations for Category 1 and 2^272 for Category 5 [9].

The modern code-based landscape is defined by three NIST fourth-round candidates: Classic McEliece, the conservative Goppa-code KEM; BIKE (Bit Flipping Key Encapsulation), based on quasi-cyclic moderate-density parity-check (QC-MDPC) codes; and HQC, which deliberately avoids hiding any trapdoor in its public code so that security reduces more directly to decoding a random quasi-cyclic code [4]. After the isogeny-based candidate SIKE was catastrophically broken in 2022, these three code-based schemes became the principal diversification candidates against the lattice-based ML-KEM. In March 2025, NIST selected HQC for standardization, citing its balance of security and performance [10].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the coding-theoretic background: linear codes, Goppa codes, and the syndrome decoding problem. Section 3 describes the comparative methodology used to evaluate the three schemes. Section 4 presents the deep technical analysis in four parts: the McEliece framework itself, the information-set decoding attack family that determines all concrete security estimates, the BIKE design, and the HQC design. Section 5 gives empirical parameter comparisons and formal security guarantees, Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Linear codes and the syndrome decoding problem

A binary linear code *C* of length *n* and dimension *k* is a *k*-dimensional subspace of **F**₂ⁿ, conventionally specified by a *k × n* generator matrix *G* (so *C = {mG : m ∈ **F**₂ᵏ}*) or equivalently by an *(n−k) × n* parity-check matrix *H* with *C = {c : Hcᵀ = 0}*. The *syndrome* of a received word *y* is *s = Hyᵀ*. The central computational problem of code-based cryptography is the following:

> **Theorem: (Berlekamp–McEliece–van Tilborg, 1978).** The *syndrome decoding problem* — given a parity-check matrix *H ∈ **F**₂^((n−k)×n)*, a syndrome *s ∈ **F**₂^(n−k)*, and an integer *t*, decide whether there exists a vector *e ∈ **F**₂ⁿ* of Hamming weight *wt(e) ≤ t* with *Heᵀ = s* — is NP-complete.

This worst-case hardness result is the bedrock on which code-based cryptography is built. A crucial caveat, revisited in Section 6, is that cryptographic security requires *average-case* hardness over random codes, a strictly stronger and unproven assumption; nevertheless, decades of failed cryptanalysis have made it among the most trusted assumptions in post-quantum cryptography [2].

### 2.2 Goppa codes

Binary Goppa codes, introduced by V. D. Goppa in 1970, are the workhorse of the McEliece system. A binary Goppa code *Γ(L, g)* is defined by a support set *L = {α₁, …, αₙ} ⊆ **F**₂ₘ* of *n* distinct field elements and an irreducible *Goppa polynomial* *g(x) ∈ **F**₂ₘ[x]* of degree *t*. A binary vector *c* is a codeword iff the rational syndrome *Σᵢ cᵢ/(x − αᵢ)* vanishes modulo *g(x)*. The parameters satisfy [5]:

- Length *n ≤ 2ᵐ*, dimension *k ≥ n − mt*, minimum distance *d ≥ 2t + 1*.

The decisive property is *efficient decodability*: Patterson's algorithm corrects any pattern of up to *t* errors in polynomial time using only the secret polynomial *g* and support *L*. To an observer lacking *(g, L)*, the code is computationally indistinguishable from a random linear code — this gap between the trapdoor owner's view and the adversary's view is the entire basis of the system [1][2].

### 2.3 The Fujisaki–Okamoto route to chosen-ciphertext security

McEliece's original construction is a public-key encryption scheme with one-way security under chosen-plaintext attack (OW-CPA). Modern deployments require indistinguishability under adaptive chosen-ciphertext attack (IND-CCA2) as a key-encapsulation mechanism (KEM). The standard route is a variant of the Fujisaki–Okamoto (FO) transform: the deterministic, zero-decryption-failure McEliece PKE is hashed together with the message randomness to derive the session key, with re-encryption during decapsulation to reject invalid ciphertexts. This conversion is *tight* — it preserves the security level — under two assumptions satisfied by the McEliece PKE: determinism (decryption recovers all encryption randomness) and absence of decryption failures for valid ciphertexts; recent work extends comparable tightness to the quantum random-oracle model (QROM) [2].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis employs a comparative analytic methodology across the three principal code-based KEM designs. The evaluation criteria are:

1. **Hardness foundation.** Identify the precise computational problem each scheme reduces to (syndrome decoding, QCSD, QDMPc) and the tightness of the reduction.
2. **Attack calibration.** Survey the information-set decoding (ISD) family — the best known classical and quantum attacks — and record the smallest published work factors against each parameter set, following the comparative methodology of Esser and Bellini [9].
3. **Concrete parameters.** Tabulate key, ciphertext, and shared-secret sizes, error weights, and code dimensions for standardized parameter sets [3][7][8].
4. **Decoding-failure analysis.** Quantify decryption-failure rates (DFR), since non-negligible DFR both invalidates FO-based CCA proofs and enables key-recovery reaction attacks.
5. **Implementation posture.** Consider constant-time implementability and hardware performance evidence where published.

All complexity estimates are stated in the RAM cost model with logarithmic factors suppressed unless noted, consistent with the literature [6][9].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The McEliece Framework and Binary Goppa Codes

**Key generation.** The receiver selects a binary Goppa code *Γ(L, g)* with parameters *(n, k, t)* and computes a *k × n* generator matrix *G*. Two masking transformations are then applied: a random *k × k* invertible *scrambling matrix* *S* and a random *n × n* *permutation matrix* *P*. The public key is the pair *(Ĝ = SGP, t)*, and the private key is *(S, G, P)* [1]. Multiplication by *S* disguises the row space basis while *P* permutes coordinates; jointly they render *Ĝ* computationally indistinguishable from the generator matrix of a random linear code, concealing the Goppa structure that enables efficient decoding.

**Encryption.** To encrypt a *k*-bit message *m*, the sender samples a random error vector *e ∈ **F**₂ⁿ* of exact Hamming weight *t* and computes *c = mĜ + e* [1].

**Decryption.** The receiver computes *ĉ = cP⁻¹ = mSG + eP⁻¹*, applies Patterson's algorithm to correct the *t* errors (permutation preserves weight), recovering *mSG*, and finally multiplies by *S⁻¹* and inverts the Goppa encoding to obtain *m*.

The original 1978 parameters were *n = 1024, k = 524, t = 50*, yielding a public key of *k(n − k) = 262,000* bits in systematic form *Ĝ = (G̃ | I)* [1]. For post-quantum deployment, the Classic McEliece submission to NIST specifies substantially larger parameters [3]:

| Parameter set | n | k | t | Public key (bytes) | NIST category |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| mceliece348864 | 3488 | 2720 | 64 | 261,120 | 1 |
| mcel mece460896 | 4608 | 3360 | 96 | 524,160 | 3 |

| mceliece6960119 | 6960 | 5413 | 119 | 1,046,739 | 5 |

The submission in fact instantiates the *Niederreiter dual* — encryption as *c = Heᵀ* with a parity-check matrix, decryption by syndrome decoding — which offers bandwidth and implementation advantages while preserving security, since the two formulations are equivalent under standard reductions [2]. The Classic McEliece specification is now maintained as an IETF draft, reflecting its status as the conservative, maximally scrutinized option: the submitters emphasize that the system's security level has remained "remarkably stable, despite dozens of attack papers over 45 years" [3].

```haskell
-- Sketch of the McEliece trapdoor in a typed functional style
data GoppaParams = GoppaParams { n :: Int, k :: Int, t :: Int
                               , poly :: IrreduciblePoly, support :: [FieldElem] }
keygen :: GoppaParams -> IO (PublicKey, PrivateKey)
keygen gp = do
  g <- generatorMatrix gp            -- k x n, Goppa structure
  s <- randomInvertible (k gp)        -- scrambling matrix S
  p <- randomPermutation (n gp)       -- permutation matrix P
  let gPub = s `mul` g `mul` p        -- public: SGP, hides structure
  return (PublicKey gPub (t gp), PrivateKey s g p)
-- Security: distinguishing gPub from random is the Goppa-code
-- distinguishing problem; recovering m from m*gPub+e is syndrome decoding.
```

### 4.2 Information-Set Decoding: The Hardness Foundation

Every concrete security estimate for every code-based scheme derives from a single algorithmic family: *information-set decoding* (ISD). Given *H*, *s*, and *t*, an ISD algorithm repeatedly selects a random *information set* *I* of *k* coordinates, inverts the corresponding submatrix, and checks whether the resulting error pattern has weight *t*. Prange's 1962 algorithm is the baseline, with expected complexity governed by the probability *C(n−t, k)/C(n, k)* that a random information set avoids all error positions.

Successive refinements allow a small number *p* of errors inside the information set and recover them combinatorially:

1. **Lee–Brickell (1988):** enumerates weight-*p* patterns within the information set.
2. **Stern (1989):** splits the information set, applies a meet-in-the-middle collision search, and adds a zero-window parameter *ℓ*.
3. **BJMM (Becker–Joux–May–Meurer, 2012):** represents the error vector as a sum of vectors with overlapping support ("representations technique"), achieving the best known asymptotic exponent for high-rate codes.
4. **May–Ozerov (2015):** applies nearest-neighbor search to the representation lists, further reducing the exponent; in the comparative analysis of Esser and Bellini, the May–Ozerov variant yields the *smallest* work factor against every Classic McEliece parameter set [9].

```python
def prange_isd(H, s, t, trials=10**9):
    """Baseline information-set decoding (Prange, 1962)."""
    rows, cols = H.shape          # H is (n-k) x n
    n, k = cols, cols - rows
    for _ in range(trials):
        I = random.sample(range(n), k)          # information set
        HI = H[:, I]
        if not is_invertible(HI):
            continue
        x = solve(HI, s)                        # candidate on I
        e = zeros(n); e[I] = x
        if hamming_weight(e) == t and (H @ e == s).all():
            return e                            # error vector found
    return None
# Success probability per trial ~ C(n-t,k)/C(n,k); all modern ISD
# variants raise this probability at the cost of costlier trials.
```
## 5 Empirical Analysis and Formal Guarantees

### 5.1 Head-to-head comparison

The three designs represent three distinct points in the trade space between conservatism, bandwidth, and reduction tightness:

| Dimension | Classic McEliece | BIKE | HQC |
|---|---|---|---|
| Underlying code | Binary Goppa, hidden trapdoor | QC-MDPC, hidden sparse parity check | Random QC code + public RMRS code, no hidden trapdoor |
| Hard problem | Syndrome decoding + Goppa indistinguishability | QC syndrome decoding (QCMDPC) | QCSD with parity |
| Public key (Level 1) | 261,120 B [3] | ~1,541 B (r = 12323) [7] | 2,241 B [8] |
| Ciphertext (Level 1) | ~240 B [3] | ~1,573 B [7] | 4,433 B [8] |
| Decoder | Patterson (algebraic, deterministic) | Iterative bit-flipping (probabilistic) | RMRS algebraic (deterministic bound) |
| DFR | 0 (exact decoding) | Must be bounded < 2^{−128} heuristically | Closed-form negligible bound [4] |
| KEM security | IND-CCA2, tight ROM/QROM via FO [2] | IND-CCA2 via FO (DFR-dependent) | IND-CCA2 via FO (DFR-dependent) [4] |
| Standardization | 4th-round candidate, not selected | 4th-round candidate, not selected | **Selected March 2025** [10] |

### 5.2 Attack-calibrated security levels

Concrete security is calibrated exclusively against ISD. The progression of the best-known classical exponents — Prange → Lee–Brickell → Stern → BJMM → May–Ozerov — has been gradual: nearly five decades of analysis moved the asymptotic exponent only modestly, which is precisely why parameter scaling is considered reliable [9]. For every Classic McEliece parameter set, the Esser–Bellini comparison finds the May–Ozerov nearest-neighbor ISD variant to give the smallest work factor, and all sets clear their claimed NIST categories (2^143 / 2^207 / 2^272 classical bit operations for Categories 1/3/5) with margin [9].

Quantum cryptanalysis offers no qualitative break: Grover-accelerated Prange and quantum-walk-accelerated Stern/MMT variants reduce the exponent by roughly a square-root factor in the ideal model, and memory-intensive algorithms (BJMM, May–Ozerov) quantize *poorly* because space lower-bounds quantum time [6]. NIST's categories therefore remain satisfiable, and code-based schemes are among the few PQC families whose quantum security story is this conservative [9].

### 5.3 Formal guarantees

Three distinct flavors of provable security are on display:

1. **McEliece:** OW-CPA of the PKE reduces to syndrome decoding plus the assumption that Goppa codes are indistinguishable from random codes; the FO transform lifts this *tightly* to IND-CCA2 in the ROM and QROM because decryption never fails and recovers all randomness [2].
2. **BIKE:** IND-CCA2 rests on the QC-MDPC decoding assumption *and* on a heuristic DFR bound for the bit-flipping decoder — the proof is conditional on decoder behavior that is analyzed empirically and combinatorially rather than derived from the code's minimum distance [4][7].
3. **HQC:** IND-CPA reduces directly to decisional QCSD with parity — no code-indistinguishability assumption — and the FO lift to IND-CCA2 is supported by a closed-form DFR bound derived from the RMRS code's known minimum distance [4].

> **Theorem: (FO lift, Hofheinz–Hövelmanns–Kiltz lineage).** If a deterministic PKE is OW-CPA secure and has zero decryption-failure probability, the FO-transformed KEM is IND-CCA2 secure in the (quantum) random-oracle model with a tight reduction. Schemes with non-zero DFR δ incur a security loss linear in the number of decryption queries times δ, which is why BIKE and HQC must certify δ ≤ 2^{−128} [2][4].

## 6 Limitations

**Key and ciphertext sizes.** Classic McEliece's conservatism is paid for in bandwidth: public keys range from 261 KB to 1.36 MB, which NIST judged impractical for general-purpose deployment — a principal reason it was not selected despite NIST's stated confidence in its security [3][10]. HQC's bandwidth (≈2.9× BIKE's ciphertexts) is modest by comparison but still roughly an order of magnitude above lattice KEMs [4][10].

**Average-case vs. worst-case hardness.** NP-completeness of syndrome decoding is a worst-case statement; cryptography needs random instances to be hard. No reduction from worst-case to average-case decoding is known (unlike lattices, where Ajtai-style worst-to-average reductions exist). The assumption rests on cryptanalytic failure, not on complexity-theoretic bedrock.

**Quasi-cyclic structure.** BIKE and HQC both assume hardness of *structured* (quasi-cyclic) decoding problems, which have received far less scrutiny than unstructured syndrome decoding. The quasi-cyclic setting admits folding and DOOM-style speedups, and the history of structured-code proposals includes breaks (e.g., the 2022 SIKE collapse, though isogeny-based, is a cautionary tale for structured assumptions generally) [10].

**Decoder failures and reaction attacks.** BIKE's probabilistic decoder is its most delicate component: weak keys with elevated DFR exist, and the GJS reaction attack demonstrated that observable decryption failures can leak the sparse secret. Mitigations (key validation, ephemeral keys, DFR bounds) are effective but add implementation complexity absent from McEliece [4][7].

**Side channels and quantum refinements.** Constant-time implementations of Patterson decoding, bit-flipping, and the RMRS decoder are nontrivial; timing and power analysis remain active concerns. On the cryptanalytic side, quantum ISD continues to improve incrementally, and any future non-generic quantum decoding breakthrough would affect all three schemes simultaneously [6].

## 7 Conclusion

Code-based cryptography has traveled from McEliece's 1978 trapdoor — a Goppa code disguised by *S* and *P* — to a diversified family of KEMs evaluated under the most demanding standardization process in the field's history. The McEliece framework endures as the conservative anchor: its hardness assumption, information-set decoding of a hidden Goppa code, has survived 48 years of classical and quantum cryptanalysis with its security estimates essentially intact [1][2][3][9]. BIKE showed that quasi-cyclic MDPC codes with iterative decoding could compress keys by two orders of magnitude, at the cost of a delicate probabilistic decoder and heuristic failure analysis [4][7]. HQC charted a third path — no hidden trapdoor at all, a structured public code, and a direct reduction to quasi-cyclic syndrome decoding — and that combination of provable-security clarity, competitive performance, and manageable bandwidth earned it NIST's selection as the fifth post-quantum standard in March 2025 [4][8][10].

The deeper lesson is methodological. In code-based cryptography, *all* roads lead through information-set decoding: parameter selection, category claims, and quantum security arguments are all ISD work-factor computations [6][9]. That single, well-understood attack surface is both the family's greatest strength — cryptanalysts know exactly where to push — and its concentration of risk. As HQC moves toward final standardization and Classic McEliece remains the hedge against a future structural break, the McEliece framework's founding insight stands vindicated: hide an efficiently decodable code inside a random-looking one, and half a century of mathematics may still not find it.
